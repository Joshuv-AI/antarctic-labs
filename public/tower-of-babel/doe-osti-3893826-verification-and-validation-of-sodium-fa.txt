 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 This report presents the second joint milestone in the Southwest Experimental Fast Oxide Reactor
(SEFOR) benchmark effort, documenting work performed in fiscal year (FY) 2026 to validate the
numerical tools used by the Fast Reactor Program (FRP) and the Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and
Simulation (NEAMS) Program against experimental data from SEFOR. Building on the FY 2023 through
FY 2025 validation activities, this FY 2026 effort extends the ARC suite code and SAS4A/SASSYS-1
code (ARC/SAS workflow) to the SEFOR power-ascending tests and the initial Core I reactivity-insertion
transients, the Griffin/BISON workflow to the Core I-E and Core I-I reflector-worth and isothermal
reactivity feedback measurements, and the Shift tool to the Core I-D material reactivity worth
measurements.
 For the validation performed with the FRP tools, a sensitivity analysis of the SAS power-ascending
model was conducted to evaluate the effects of the assumed helium fill gas thermal conductivity and the
selected mixed oxide (MOX) fuel thermal conductivity correlation on the predicted reactivity feedback
and fuel temperature. Using the OFUEL fuel performance model in the SAS code reduced the uncertainty
associated with the assumed helium conductivity and tracked the measured feedback more closely over
the complete power range than the iterative gap update approach. For the Core I transient tests, calculations
using the dynamic Fast Reactivity Excursion Device (FRED) reactivity worth inferred from the measured
power history reproduced the transient power response more closely than calculations using the nominal
static rod worth. The iterative SAS model systematically underpredicted the measured power peak because
the fuel pin geometry was not updated to account for thermal expansion during the transient. This finding
motivated the extension of the OFUEL treatment to the reactivity-insertion transients. This treatment will
be applied to the Core I reactivity-insertion transients in FY 2027.
 For the validation performed with the NEAMS tools, Griffin k-eigenvalue calculations reproduced the
measured fine reflector cumulative and integral worth curves to within approximately 5% and 8.5%,
respectively, for both Core I-E and Core I-I. For the isothermal-reactivity tests, a one-way BISON-to-
Griffin displacement transfer workflow improved the calculated isothermal feedback relative to that
obtained from a standalone Griffin calculation using prescribed thermal expansion coefficients. A
specialized approach for using the displaced mesh (MOVENODE) was also tested to resolve a Griffin
convergence difficulty encountered at higher-order angular discretizations when the BISON-informed
mesh displacement became large relative to the local mesh size. The NEAMS scope was also extended to
the Core I-D material reactivity worth measurements. These measurements were modeled using the
continuous-energy Monte Carlo code Shift, which reproduced the measured worths reasonably well across
the different rod types.
 Key takeaway: Explicitly tracking the temperature-induced thermal expansion of the fuel and
surrounding structures, whether through the SAS OFUEL treatment on the ARC/SAS side or the BISON-
informed displacement field on the Griffin side, materially improves agreement with the measured SEFOR
reactivity feedback relative to simpler treatments based on assumed or prescribed thermal expansion
properties. The FY 2026 effort also broadened the SEFOR validation basis for both workflows. The
SEFOR model originally developed for modeling power-ascending tests was extended to simulate the
reactivity-insertion transients, and the NEAMS validation was extended to include reflector-worth and
isothermal-feedback calculations (Griffin/BISON) as well as localized material reactivity worth
measurements (Shift). Together, these activities provide a broader technical basis for future sodium-
cooled fast reactor design and licensing analyses.

1 Introduction
 Designers and licensors of sodium-cooled fast reactors (SFRs) rely on computational tools validated
against experimental measurements of reactor behavior. The Fast Reactor Program (FRP), which is part
of the Advanced Reactor Technologies (ART) portfolio within the U.S. Department of Energy Office of
Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE), conducts research and development in support of industry led
commercialization of fast reactors. As part of this effort, the FRP uses data from historical test reactors—
including Experimental Breeder Reactor-II (EBR-II), the Fast Flux Test Facility (FFTF), and the
Southwest Experimental Fast Oxide Reactor (SEFOR) —to expand the validation vases of the tools and
methods used in SFR design and licensing. The FRP has supported validation of the legacy Argonne
Reactor Computation (ARC) code suite [1] and the SAS4A/SASSYS-1 (SAS) safety analysis code [2]
against SEFOR data. Separately, the Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS)
Program has supported validation of the high-fidelity Griffin reactor physics code [3] and the BISON fuel
performance code [4] against the same data.
 SEFOR was a 20 MW SFR located in Washington County, Arkansas, that used mixed plutonium–
uranium oxide fuel. The General Electric Company built and operated the reactor in collaboration with
Germany’s Karlsruhe Laboratory [5, 6]. The experimental program, conducted from 1969 to 1972,
comprised three phases designed to characterize Doppler reactivity feedback: Phase I included reactor
startup, zero-power testing, and isothermal reactivity measurements; Phase II consisted of steady-state
tests conducted at increasing reactor power levels; and Phase III used the Fast Reactivity Excursion Device
(FRED) to rapidly eject a boron carbide (B4C) rod from the center of the core and induce sub-prompt
critical and super-prompt critical power transients [5]. The resulting measurements characterized Doppler
feedback over an unusually broad temperature range, from approximately 350 °F to temperatures
approaching the mixed oxide (MOX) fuel melting point of 5,000 °F [6, 7]. The SEFOR data set therefore
remains a valuable benchmark for reactor physics, thermal-hydraulic, and multiphysics codes.
 Argonne National Laboratory (Argonne) and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) initiated a new
SEFOR benchmarking effort in fiscal year (FY) 2023, jointly funded by the FRP and the NEAMS
Program. Over the following two years, the team developed models of several SEFOR zero-power
configurations and the Phase II power-ascending tests. Monte Carlo calculations used MCNP, Serpent,
and Shift, and deterministic calculations used the ARC code suite and Griffin for neutronics and the SAS
code for thermal hydraulics. The resulting analyses of the Phase I zero-power and Phase II power-
ascending tests are summarized in the project’s first joint FRP/NEAMS milestone report [8]. Calculated
values of criticality, reflector worth, kinetics parameters, and reactivity feedback agree closely with the
experimental data, providing confidence in the ARC/SAS and Griffin modeling approaches.
 This report documents two parallel technical efforts conducted in FY 2026 for the second joint
FRP/NEAMS milestone. For the NEAMS effort, the SEFOR Phase I isothermal tests were modeled using
Griffin and BISON coupled through the Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment
(MOOSE) framework [9]. This work represented the project’s first multiphysics simulation of these tests
and extended the FY 2025 neutronics-only Griffin analyses by incorporating BISON-predicted thermal
expansion of the fuel and surrounding structures into the Griffin reactivity calculations. Additionally, Shift
neutronics-only calculation was used to obtain material reactivity worth measured in one of the core
configurations. For the FRP effort, the ARC/SAS workflow was used to model the familiarization and
sub-prompt critical reactivity-insertion transients performed in SEFOR Core I, extending the single-
channel SAS approach previously validated against the power-ascending tests. The analysis used the
original transient test data and methodology documented in the report GEAP-13837 [5], to simulate the

 1

rapid FRED-driven reactivity insertions and resulting prompt Doppler feedback. Super-prompt critical
transients were outside the FY 2026 scope and are planned for future work.
 The following sections describe the SEFOR reactor design, the experiments conducted during Phases
I–III, and the FRP and NEAMS tools used in the analysis. The report then presents the ARC and SAS
modeling methods and results for the power-ascending tests and reactivity-insertion transients, followed
by the NEAMS modeling and results for reflector-worth calibration, material reactivity worth, and
isothermal tests. The report concludes with a summary of the principal findings and the work remaining
to complete the SEFOR benchmark effort.

2 SEFOR Experiments
 SEFOR reactor started its initial criticality in 1969 and completed all testing in 1972. Among this
period, the project operated with two main core loadings: Core I with a BeO rod as the fuel assembly’s
tightener rod and Core II with a SS304 rod as the tightener rod. For Core I, the core configurations
began with Core I-A through Core I-D, which were used to establish criticality and refine the fuel
loading, culminating in the fully loaded, 108-channel Core I-D. Core I-E introduced guinea pig (GP)
fuel rods with higher fissile plutonium content and supported the first complete series of isothermal
reactivity tests, including reflector-worth calibrations at two coolant temperatures. Core I-I incorporated
two instrumented fuel assemblies (IFAs) containing thermocouples that directly measured fuel
centerline temperature; the reflector-worth and isothermal test programs were then repeated. Core I-J
was created by replacing one B4C absorber rod in Core I-I with a stainless steel rod, thereby adding 36.1
cents of reactivity to compensate for the negative feedback expected at higher power. Core I-J was used
for the later power-ascension measurements, all eight familiarization transients with smaller reactivity
insertions around 0.5 $, and the first three sub-prompt critical tests. Subsequent Core I loadings, I-K
through I-M, supported the remainder of the transient program which involved larger reactivity insertion
from around 0.97 $ to 1.2 $. In Core II, the neutron spectrum is harder which lead to reduced Doppler
coefficient. Comparable transient test programs were performed in both Core I and Core II [5, 6]. Figure
2-1 shows a cross section of a standard SEFOR fuel assembly, including the arrangement of the fuel
rods around the central tightener rod within the hexagonal flow channel.

 2.1 Reflector-Worth Measurements and Isothermal Tests
 The SEFOR zero-power test program measured the reactor physics parameters required to characterize
the core before power operation, including the following:
 • approach to criticality and determination of the minimum critical fuel loading
 • calibration of the movable reflector control system
 • measurement of material reactivity worth for fuel, B4C, and structural rods at selected radial
 positions
 • foil-activation measurements of the fission-rate distribution
 • noise measurements of the kinetics parameter Λ/βeff
 • measurement of reactivity feedback associated with changes in coolant flow, cover-gas pressure,
 and uniform core temperature
 This report focuses on measurements of reflector worth and material reactivity worth and on the
isothermal reactivity tests because these experiments most directly support validation of the reactivity-
feedback models used in this study. Ten movable nickel reflector segments arranged around the core
periphery controlled reactor criticality. Reflectors 3 and 8 were designated “fine” reflectors. Each fine
reflector was calibrated by raising it incrementally, measuring the resulting reactor period, and converting
that period to reactivity worth using the inhour equation. The other eight reflectors were designated
“coarse” reflectors and were calibrated using a rod swap procedure. During this procedure, the coarse
reflector being calibrated was moved from the fully raised to the fully lowered position, a fine reflector
was moved to compensate for most of the resulting reactivity change, and the other fine reflector was
adjusted to restore criticality. The coarse reflector worth was then inferred from the calibrated fine
reflector movements. Because reflector worth depends strongly on the positions of adjacent reflectors,
calibrations were generally performed with the adjacent reflectors fully raised and with the remaining
reflectors in configurations similar to those used during the power tests. Fine reflector calibrations were
performed in Cores I-B, I-D, I-E, and I-I; reflector 8 was also calibrated in Core I-F. Core I-E was
calibrated at both 350 °F and 650 °F to characterize the temperature dependence of reflector worth [6].
 Measurements of material reactivity worth were performed at selected radial locations in Core I-D, as
documented in GEAP-13588 [6]. These measurements characterized the reactivity effects of fissile,
absorbing, fertile, structural, and tightener-rod materials and provided localized integral tests that
complemented the reflector-worth measurements. The Core I-D material-worth measurements used rod
substitution experiments. At each measurement location, the reactor temperature and critical reflector
positions were recorded sequentially with (1) the original fuel rod in place, (2) the original rod removed
and the channel filled with liquid sodium, (3) a test rod inserted into the sodium-filled channel, and (4)
the original fuel rod reinserted. The test rods included standard fuel rods containing 18.7% fissile
plutonium, GP rods containing 25% fissile plutonium, depleted UO2 rods, B4C absorber rods, and stainless
steel rods. The resulting worths were reported relative to the sodium-filled channel. The worths of the
BeO tightener rods were measured separately by replacing pairs of BeO tightener rods at equivalent,
diametrically opposed core positions with special stainless steel tightener rods. Consequently, these
worths were reported relative to stainless steel rather than sodium [6]. Figure 2-4 shows the radial locations
of the material worth measurements in Core I-D.

 Isothermal reactivity tests were performed in Cores I-E and I-I to determine the uniform temperature
coefficient of reactivity. During the Core I-E test, the primary sodium was heated from 350 to 760 °F
using the primary loop trace heating system while reactor power was maintained near zero (at
approximately 600 W). The reflector position adjustments required to maintain criticality were converted
to changes in reactivity as a function of temperature. The sodium temperature was stabilized for 2–3 h at
intermediate plateaus near 450, 550, 650, and 760 °F to establish thermal equilibrium. Inlet and outlet
temperatures were monitored using resistance temperature detectors, and the in-vessel temperature was
monitored using a thermocouple. The measured relationship between reactivity and temperature was
independent of heating rate for rates of approximately 20 °F/h or less, indicating that the feedback
contained no major components with long time constants. For Core I-E, the magnitude of the measured
uniform temperature coefficient decreased with increasing temperature, from approximately −0.67
cents/°F between 350 and 450 °F to approximately −0.57 cents/°F between 650 and 760 °F. Subsequent
Core I-I measurements corroborated the Core I-E results [6].

 2.2 Power-Ascending Tests
 Following the zero-power experiments, the Core I power-ascension program measured reactivity
feedback under conditions representative of normal reactor operation [10, 11]. Initial at-power
measurements were performed in Core I-I, and measurements conducted after August 21, 1970, used Core
I-J. The replacement of a B4C rod with a stainless steel rod in Core I-J provided the additional reactivity
needed for higher-power operation. The Core I tests eventually reached approximately 20 MW, whereas
the data-set modeled in this report spans 0.4–17.5 MW and is represented using the Core I-J geometry.
Reactor power was increased incrementally, and the reflector positions were adjusted at each step to
 6

compensate for the increasingly negative reactivity feedback as the core and fuel temperatures increased.
The core-average coolant temperature was maintained near 760 °F. As power increased, the primary inlet
temperature was reduced, from approximately 745 °F to approximately 400 °F in the selected
measurements, to maintain the specified core-average coolant temperature. Coolant inlet and outlet
temperatures were monitored using resistance temperature detectors, in-vessel temperatures were
measured using thermocouples, and fuel centerline temperatures were measured directly using tungsten-
rhenium thermocouples installed in four fuel rods within two IFAs. Figure 2-5 shows the axial
configurations of the standard SEFOR fuel rod and the IFA rod, including the thermocouple locations
used for the fuel temperature measurements.

 Figure 2-5. Axial configuration of the SEFOR standard fuel rod and IFA rod.

 Flow rates in the main and auxiliary primary sodium cooling systems were also recorded. Together
with the inlet and outlet temperature measurements, these data were used to determine reactor thermal
power from heat balances at each power level. Reactivity feedback was inferred from the changes in
calibrated reflector position required to maintain criticality. This procedure provided an integral
measurement of the combined feedback associated with Doppler broadening, fuel and structural thermal
expansion, and changes in sodium density as a function of reactor power [10, 11].

 2.3 SEFOR Core Transients
 The final stage of the SEFOR experimental program examined the reactor response to rapid, large
reactivity insertions and provided a direct test of the inherent shutdown response produced by Doppler
feedback. Transients were initiated using the FRED, a gas-actuated mechanism that ejected a B4C rod
from the central core channel over approximately 0.14 s. Each transient was terminated by a reactor scram
that inserted substantial negative reactivity by dropping the reflector segments approximately 0.35 s after
transient initiation [5]. Three FRED rods containing different masses of ¹⁰B but having the same nominal
size and weight were used for the familiarization, sub-prompt critical, and super-prompt critical tests. The
insertion magnitude could also be varied by changing the rod’s initial axial position. During each transient,
rod displacement was measured using a photoelectric cell, lift-off contact switches, and magnetic
proximity switches. These position data were combined with independently measured static worth-versus-
position curves to reconstruct the time-dependent reactivity insertion.

 The SEFOR transient tests were classified according to reactivity-insertion magnitude. Familiarization

first measurements of prompt Doppler feedback during a rapid transient. Super-prompt critical transients,
with insertions greater than 1$ but less than approximately 1.3$, produced rapid power excursions that
were reversed by Doppler feedback before the reflector scram. Across the three categories, the tests began
at steady-state power levels ranging from 1 to 10 MW. Table 2-2 summarizes the maximum dynamic
FRED rod worth and the reactivity-versus-energy coefficient measured during sub-prompt critical Tests

was used to test the fission-to-gamma-detector switchover logic, and the gamma-detector data were less

3 Modeling of SEFOR Power-Ascending Tests and Reactivity-Insertion Transient Tests
 Using the ARC/SAS Workflow
 3.1 Modeling of SEFOR Power-Ascending Tests

 3.1.1 Model Description
 The SEFOR power-ascending tests were analyzed using the ARC/SAS workflow shown in Figure 3-1.
MC2-3 [12] generated multigroup (MG) cross sections for the Core I-J model, and DIF3D [13] used those
cross sections to calculate neutron flux and power distributions following the workflow described in Ref.
[14]. PERSENT [15] used the neutronics solutions to calculate spatially dependent reactivity coefficients
for Doppler broadening and changes in fuel and sodium density. The DIF3D power distribution was
supplied to SAS [2], which used a channel model to calculate fuel and cladding temperatures. The
calculated state changes and corresponding feedback contributions were then combined to obtain the total
reactivity feedback for comparison with the experimental feedback inferred from the measured reflector
positions.

 Figure 3-1. ARC/SAS modeling workflow for the SEFOR power-ascending tests.

 Within SAS, the Core I-J fuel assemblies were grouped into six channels corresponding to the SEFOR
orifice zones, with two additional channels representing the IFAs. Each channel was discretized into seven
axial zones, and the active fuel region was represented using nine radial nodes. Fuel and cladding
thermophysical properties were obtained from the correlations recommended by Carbajo et al. [16] and,
where applicable, from material models implemented in BISON [4].
 Heat transfer across the fuel–cladding gap was represented as the sum of three mechanisms:
conduction through the helium fill gas, calculated from an assumed helium thermal conductivity ranging
from 0.15 – 0.25 W/m-K divided by the gap width; solid-to-solid contact conduction; and radiation.
Because the average coolant temperature remained nearly constant at about 760 °F across the full 0.4-17.5
MW power range of the tests, the gap width was governed primarily by the thermal expansion of the fuel
as power increased. This gap width was determined for each test, channel, and axial node through an
iterative procedure: the fuel pin and cladding dimensions specified at 760 °F in the benchmark
specification were used to initialize the SAS model; the resulting average fuel temperatures were then
used to update the fuel outer radius through a thermal expansion correlation; and the updated geometry
was re-entered into SAS for another run. This process was repeated until convergence, typically within
four to five iterations, with the converged fuel outer radius following the axial fission power profile to
within about 2%.

 3.2 Modeling of SEFOR Core Transients

 3.2.1 Model Description
 The SEFOR Core I transient tests were initiated by rapidly ejecting a B4C rod from the central core
channel using the FRED, the gas-actuated mechanism described in Section 2.3. Before each test, the FRED
rod was inserted into the core, and the resulting negative reactivity was compensated by adjusting the fine
reflectors to reestablish the prescribed steady-state power. Ejecting the rod then introduced positive
reactivity over approximately 0.14 s. A reactor scram, initiated approximately 0.35 s after the start of the
transient, terminated each test by dropping the reflectors. The FY 2026 ARC/SAS effort addresses two of
the three Core I transient categories: familiarization transients, with reactivity insertions of 50 cents or
less, and sub-prompt critical transients, with insertions greater than 50 cents but less than 1 dollar.
Modeling of the super-prompt critical transients, which used insertions greater than 1 dollar but less than
1.3 dollars, is planned for future work.
 The Core I transients were modeled using the same DIF3D and SAS neutronic and thermal-hydraulic
model applied to the power-ascending tests in Section 3.1.1. The kinetics parameters were obtained from
Serpent calculations, and the reactivity feedback coefficients for fuel axial expansion, fuel density, radial
core expansion, and fuel Doppler were calculated with PERSENT, using the same coefficients reported
in the FY 2025 milestone report [8]. The SAS thermal-hydraulic model used either the Carbajo or Lucuta
correlation for the fuel thermal conductivity, as described in Section 3.1.2, for the thermal-hydraulic
calculations. A point kinetics model in SAS was used to calculate the time-dependent power during each
reactivity-insertion transient.
 Experimentally, the later sub-prompt critical tests were performed in an updated core configuration,
Core I-K, in which a stainless steel rod present in Core I-J was replaced with a fuel rod; Core I-K was also
used for the first super-prompt critical test, and the remaining super-prompt transients were performed in
Core I-M, a further modification of Core I-K in which a B4C rod was replaced with a stainless steel rod.
Because Core I-K differs from Core I-J by only this one fuel rod, the FY 2026 ARC and SAS models
represent both the familiarization and sub-prompt transients using the Core I-J geometry; the resulting
small differences in core power distribution, reactivity coefficients, and kinetics parameters between Core
I-J and Core I-K were neglected at this initial stage. An updated neutronic model that explicitly represents
these differences is planned for FY 2027 work, to support modeling of the sub-prompt and super-prompt
transients performed in Core I-K and Core I-M.
 For each transient, the time-dependent FRED reactivity insertion is derived from static measurements
of FRED rod worth versus axial position combined with measurements of the FRED rod’s position as a
function of time during the transient. The corresponding measured dynamic reactivity is inferred from the
measured power history using the inverse point kinetics equations, excluding reactivity feedback, as
described in Section 2.3; this method was validated by comparing the inferred dynamic reactivity against
the FRED reactivity curve obtained from static worth measurements, showing agreement to within about
4% [5]. Because of this small but consistent difference between the static and dynamic FRED reactivity
worths, both curves were used as parametric inputs to the SAS transient calculations to bound their effect
on the calculated results.

 3.3 OFUEL Model for SEFOR Experiments

 3.3.1 Model Description
 For FY 2026, the iterative gap update treatment used in the SAS power-ascending model was refined
by activating the OFUEL module in the SAS model, an integrated oxide fuel performance model that
replaces SAS’s older DEFORM-4 treatment. While DEFORM-4 provided a simpler, more empirical
treatment of fuel pin deformation and gap evolution, OFUEL combines a 1.5D axisymmetric mechanical
model with temperature-, burnup-, and composition-dependent fuel and cladding properties to track fuel
and cladding geometry, gap state, composition, porosity, fission gas release, and cladding stress and failure
margin at each time step, providing a more mechanistic prediction of fuel thermal expansion and gap
conductance than the iterative procedure while remaining consistent with it.
 The thermal behavior modeled in OFUEL depends on several composition- and irradiation-dependent
properties. The fuel thermal conductivity is evaluated as a function of temperature, burnup, porosity, and
oxygen-to-metal (O/M) ratio, while the fuel solidus and liquidus temperatures depend on the plutonium
content, burnup, and O/M ratio. The fuel–cladding gap conductance is calculated from the combined
contributions of gas conduction, radiation, and, once fuel and cladding come into contact, a solid-contact
term that depends on the effective interface thickness, surface roughness, and hot radii of the fuel and
cladding. Where applicable, OFUEL also accounts for the joint oxide gap (JOG), a layer of Cs2MoO4
that can precipitate near the fuel–cladding interface under certain burnup, chemistry, and temperature
conditions and that can significantly modify the gap conductance and contribute to inner cladding wastage
through fuel–cladding chemical interaction.

 3.3.2 Results and Discussion
 The SEFOR power-ascending tests were re-evaluated using the SAS/OFUEL model described in
Section 3.3.1, and the results are compared directly against both the measured data and the earlier iterative
gap update approach at HBPAR20 (0.20 W/m-K assumed helium conductivity) described in Section 3.1.1.
The iterative approach results shown below were obtained using the Lucuta correlation for the MOX fuel
thermal conductivity, discussed in Section 3.1.2, rather than the Carbajo et al. (2001) correlation used
elsewhere in this report. Because OFUEL computes the fuel–cladding gap conductance mechanistically
from the evolving fuel and cladding geometry, composition, and porosity, this treatment removes the need
for the assumed helium fill gas thermal conductivity used in the iterative approach, along with the
associated sensitivity to that assumption discussed in Section 3.1.2. Figure 3-13 compares the total
reactivity feedback predicted by the iterative HBPAR20 approach and by the SAS/OFUEL model against
the measured power-ascending test data. Both approaches reproduce the overall trend, but the OFUEL-
based prediction tracks the measured feedback more closely across the full 0.4–17.5 MW power range,
while the iterative HBPAR20 approach shows larger deviations from the data, particularly in the middle
of the power range, where it overpredicts the magnitude of the measured reactivity feedback.

cladding radius predicted by the iterative approach remains constant over the full length of the fuel pin,
reflecting its fixed-cladding-geometry assumption, whereas OFUEL predicts an inner cladding radius that

evolving mechanical, chemical, fission gas, and failure margin behavior of the fuel and cladding. As
OFUEL reduces the uncertainties in specifying the thermophysical properties used in the iterative
approach, and is able to track the fuel pin geometry changes that occur during a transient rather than only
between steady-state power steps, it is well suited to modeling the SEFOR core transient experiments
described in Section 2.3. FY 2027 work will focus on applying the updated SAS model with OFUEL, to

4 Modeling of SEFOR Core Reflector-Worth and Isothermal Tests Using the NEAMS
 Workflow
 Preliminary Griffin modeling of the SEFOR zero-power experiments was performed in FY 2025 for
Core I-E and Core I-I. The calculated criticality was compared with the measurements, as documented in
the first joint FRP/NEAMS milestone report [8]. The FY 2026 effort extended this work by refining the
reflector-worth calculations for both cores, applying Shift to the Core I-D material reactivity worth
measurements, and developing a SEFOR BISON model to calculate temperature-induced deformation of
the fuel and surrounding structures. A one-way BISON-to-Griffin displacement-transfer workflow was
also developed to evaluate the isothermal reactivity tests in Core I-E and Core I-I [19, 20]. The thermal-
expansion treatments and coupling approaches are described in Section 4.3.

 4.1 Reflector-Worth Calibration

 4.1.1 Model Description
 For each core, the fine reflector calibration was modeled using a series of Griffin eigenvalue
calculations with the reflector placed at the heights used in experimental period measurements. All other
reflectors were held at their measured positions. The calculated reactivity changes were used to construct
cumulative reflector-worth curves for comparison with the experimental worths, which were derived from
the measured reactor periods using the inhour equation [6, 19].
 The Core I-E and Core I-I geometries were constructed using the MOOSE Reactor Module [21]. This
module generates finite element meshes at scales ranging from individual rods to the full reactor core.
Defining the geometry within MOOSE allowed the meshes to be reused in the Griffin and BISON
calculations. Volume preservation options were applied where needed to maintain the physical cross-
sectional areas of circular regions during meshing.
 Mesh construction began with two-dimensional representations of each rod type in Core I-E and Core
I-I, including standard fuel, GP, instrumentation, wire, absorber, and central tightener rods. These rod
meshes were combined to construct the required assembly variants, including standard fuel assemblies
and special assemblies containing GP, B₄C absorber, or instrumented rods. The
BatchMeshGeneratorAction capability was tested in this work and used to reduce repetitive mesh
definitions by generating related meshes from a common set of inputs. Mesh model built in last FY 2025
defined each of its fifteen fuel assembly types by hand: a fuel pin, a guinea-pig or absorber pin where the
assembly called for one, each redefined from scratch for every type even though the underlying ring radii
and generator logic repeat exactly. The new rebuilt input generates the fuel, guinea-pig, and absorber pins
from three batch blocks that vary only the ring block IDs across assembly types. Furthermore, the new
mesh input wraps every assembly in its duct and gap layers from two further batch blocks driven by a list
of assembly names, in place of one hand-written FlexiblePatternGenerator block per assembly. Adopting
the action cut the assembly-definition input by nearly half.

 4.2 Material Reactivity Worth

 4.2.1 Model Description
 Unlike the reflector-worth and isothermal-feedback comparisons in Sections 4.1 and 4.3, which are
based on the deterministic code Griffin, the FY 2026 material reactivity worth analysis was performed
only with the CE Monte Carlo code Shift. A three-dimensional, CE Shift Monte Carlo model of Core I-D
was developed for this analysis, building on the Shift model previously validated for the Core I-E
isothermal reactivity measurements described in Section 4.3. Because Core I-D and Core I-E use different
fuel and B4C loading patterns, the underlying geometric description was reused and the rod loading map
was updated to represent the Core I-D configuration; all calculations were performed at a uniform core
temperature of 350 °F [20]. The resulting Shift model is shown in Figure 4-7.

 Figure 4-7. Three-dimensional Shift Monte Carlo model of SEFOR Core I-D.

 For each material worth case, a pair of Shift eigenvalue calculations was performed: for the fuel-
channel substitutions, the reference state was the sodium-filled channel and the perturbed state contained
the test rod, while each BeO case was modeled as the simultaneous substitution of the two tightener rods
reported for that radial location. All other core materials, dimensions, and rod loading locations were held
fixed between the reference and perturbed calculations. The calculated reactivity worth was obtained from
the difference between the perturbed and reference multiplication factors, converted to cents using the
same effective delayed neutron fraction of 325 pcm applied to the reflector-worth and isothermal-feedback
results in Sections 4.1 and 4.3. Calculations were performed with three CE nuclear data libraries—
ENDF/B-VII.1 [22], ENDF/B-VIII.0 [24], and ENDF/B-VIII.1 [25]—to assess the sensitivity of the
calculated worths to the underlying nuclear data [20].

 4.3 Isothermal Tests

 4.3.1 Model Description
 For the isothermal reactivity tests, thermal expansion was treated in two ways in the Griffin
calculations. First, a standalone Griffin model used prescribed, material-specific thermal expansion
coefficients to generate an expanded mesh at each isothermal temperature. Second, a one-way BISON-to-
Griffin workflow was developed in which BISON, a finite-element-based nuclear fuel performance code,
was used to calculate the temperature-induced displacement field of the fuel pin and surrounding structures
[4, 19].
 Because the isothermal tests were conducted at near-zero power, the BISON model did not include
power-driven fuel temperature gradients, burnup evolution, fission gas release, or irradiation-induced
dimensional changes. The BISON model used the same Core I geometry generated for the Griffin
calculations. Starting from the room-temperature reference mesh, BISON increased the temperature to
each target isothermal condition and solved a quasi-static, finite-strain solid mechanics problem
incorporating thermal expansion. Material-specific thermal expansion models were applied to the MOX
fuel, depleted UO2 insulator, B4C, BeO, and stainless steel regions. The spring regions included an
imposed initial eigenstrain, in addition to thermal expansion, to represent the spring-loaded axial
segmentation of the SEFOR fuel rods. Because the coolant and void regions are not load-bearing, mesh
smoothing was applied to these regions to maintain mesh continuity during deformation. The resulting
displacement field was transferred to Griffin as a one-way geometry update. The densities of the solid
regions were adjusted to conserve mass as their volumes changed, whereas the temperature-dependent
sodium density was assigned directly to the coolant [19].
 Two BISON-to-Griffin coupling methods were evaluated during FY 2026. In the file-based coupling
method, BISON calculated the displacement field and stored the results in an Exodus file for each
isothermal temperature. Griffin then used the SolutionUserObject to read the displacement data from the
corresponding BISON solution file and update the neutronics geometry. The second method used in-
memory coupling through the MOOSE MultiApp system. In this method, BISON served as the main
application and controlled the temperature ramp from 350 °F to 760 °F. At each selected temperature,
BISON calculated the displaced mesh and transferred the displacement data directly to Griffin, which
served as the sub-application and calculated the corresponding reactivity feedback [19].

 4.3.2 Results and Discussion
 For Core I-E, the isothermal temperature reactivity feedback was evaluated using three approaches:
an independent Shift Monte Carlo reference calculation, a standalone Griffin calculation using the
prescribed thermal expansion treatment, and the BISON-to-Griffin displacement transfer workflow
described in Section 4.3.1. All three approaches reproduce the nearly linear negative reactivity trend
observed in the measurement over the zero-power heat-up range. At the highest-temperature state, the
standalone Griffin calculation with prescribed thermal expansion predicts an integral feedback of −239.4
cents, compared with the measured value of −253.90 ± 10.20 cents, underpredicting the feedback
magnitude by about 14.5 cents (C/E = 0.943); the Shift result is similar, at −240.80 ± 2.76 cents (C/E =
0.949). When the BISON-informed displacement field is instead transferred to Griffin, the calculated
integral feedback improves to −247.97 cents (C/E = 0.978), indicating that the temperature-induced
geometry change captured by BISON is an important contributor to the calculated feedback [19].

 40

 This convergence behavior was investigated further to isolate its cause. With the P3 scattering
treatment, the Core I-I Griffin model converged when no BISON-informed displacement was applied to
the mesh (i.e., at room temperature); regardless of the values of the number of polar angles per octant (NP)
and the number of azimuthal angles per octant (NAZ) tested, however, the same P3 model failed to
converge once the displaced mesh was introduced at isothermal temperatures above 450 °F, where the
BISON-predicted axial displacement becomes larger. At the highest isothermal temperature considered,
677 K (759 °F), the accumulated axial displacement predicted by BISON reaches a maximum of about 1
cm near the top of the fuel pin, as shown in Figure 4-13, substantially larger than the minimum axial mesh
size used in the Core I-I model. Based on this observation, together with similar experience from other
NEAMS-supported projects, including KRUSTY, Griffin’s SN transport solver appears to become
susceptible to convergence failure when the applied mesh displacement grows large relative to the local
mesh size. To work around this issue, an additional approach was developed in which the node positions
within the mesh file itself are updated to reflect the BISON-predicted displacement, rather than relying on
Griffin to resolve a large relative displacement field superimposed on the original mesh.

 Figure 4-13. BISON-predicted displacement magnitude for the Core I-I fuel pin at 677 K.

 In this alternate “MOVENODE” approach, BISON is used, as before, to compute the temperature-
induced displacement field for the fuel pin and surrounding structure, but rather than transferring this field
to Griffin as a quantity superimposed on the original, undisplaced mesh, the BISON-computed nodal
displacements are read directly by a dedicated preprocessing step and used to relocate the nodes of
Griffin’s own distributed mesh to their deformed positions. This node relocation is carried out using the
MOOSE meshmodifier with a newly developed UserObject MoveNodesByParseExpression, which reads
the three BISON displacement components, disp_x, disp_y, and disp_z, for each node and repositions that
node within Griffin’s mesh accordingly; the same UserObject then calculates the density scaling factor
needed to conserve mass under the resulting deformed geometry directly from these updated node
positions, rather than from a displacement field layered on top of the original mesh, and supplies this
scaling factor to Griffin. This MOVENODE approach resolved the convergence issues observed in the
SEFOR Core I-I calculations as well as the similar convergence issues encountered in the KRUSTY
project.
 41

 To confirm the accuracy of the MOVENODE approach, Figure 4-14 compares the Core I-I isothermal
reactivity feedback calculated with the original BISON-to-Griffin transfer approach (NA = 1, NP = 1,
NAZ = 3) against the results obtained with the MOVENODE approach, run at both the original NA = 1,
NP = 1, NAZ = 3 settings and at the higher-order NA = 3, NP = 3, NAZ = 5 settings that previously failed
to converge. The original BISON-to-Griffin and in-memory coupled results are indistinguishable across
the full temperature range, and the MOVENODE results obtained at NA = 1, NP = 1, NAZ = 3 fall on the
same curve, confirming that relocating the mesh nodes directly does not change the calculated feedback
relative to the original transfer approach. The MOVENODE results obtained at the higher-order NA = 3,
NP = 3, NAZ = 5 settings likewise agree closely with the other three cases across the full heat-up range,
with only a small deviation apparent at the highest temperatures. This agreement demonstrates that the
MOVENODE approach reproduces the SEFOR Core I-I isothermal reactivity feedback accurately while
resolving the convergence difficulty encountered with the original mesh transfer approach at higher-order
angular discretization.

5 Summary and Future Work
 5.1 Summary
 This report presents FY 2026 progress on the joint FRP/NEAMS SEFOR benchmarking effort,
extending the ARC/SAS and Griffin/BISON validation activities begun in FY 2023 to the SEFOR power-
ascending tests, the initial Core I reactivity-insertion transients, and the Core I-E and Core I-I reflector-
worth and isothermal reactivity feedback measurements.
 On the FRP side, work was focused on refining the SAS power-ascending model and beginning to
extend the ARC/SAS workflow to the SEFOR reactivity-insertion transients. An input error in the radial
power distribution assigned within the SAS fuel pin model was identified and corrected, and the corrected
model was used to examine the sensitivity of the predicted reactivity feedback and fuel temperature to the
assumed helium fill gas conductivity and to the choice of MOX fuel thermal conductivity correlation. The
porosity-corrected Lucuta correlation reproduced the measured reactivity feedback and IFA fuel
temperatures more closely than the Carbajo correlation, and using the SAS model OFUEL fuel
performance code removed the ambiguity associated with the assumed helium conductivity altogether,
tracking the measured feedback more closely across the full 0.4–17.5 MW power range than the iterative
gap update approach.
 For the Core I transient tests, the familiarization and sub-prompt critical transients were modeled using
the Core I-J geometry, and the dynamic FRED reactivity worth inferred from the measured power history
reproduced the transient power response more closely than the nominal static rod worth. For these transient
simulations, the SAS model was initialized from the steady-state conditions established in the Phase II
power-ascending tests using the iterative gap update approach; this approach was found to systematically
underpredict the measured power peak because the fuel pin geometry, fixed at its initial steady-state value,
is not updated for thermal expansion during the transient itself, motivating the extension of the
SAS/OFUEL treatment from the power-ascending tests to the reactivity-insertion transients.
 On the NEAMS side, Core I-E and Core I-I geometries were constructed using the MOOSE Reactor
Module, and Griffin k-eigenvalue calculations reproduced the measured fine-reflector cumulative and
integral worth curves to within approximately 5% and 8.5%, respectively, for both cores. For the
isothermal reactivity tests, a one-way BISON-to-Griffin displacement transfer workflow was developed
to represent the temperature-induced thermal expansion of the fuel and structural geometry, and both out-
of-memory and in-memory coupling schemes were implemented and tested. The BISON-informed
geometry improved the calculated isothermal feedback relative to a standalone Griffin calculation using
prescribed thermal expansion coefficients, raising the Core I-E C/E from 0.943 to 0.978, and yielded a
Core I-I C/E of 0.947, with a maximum absolute percent difference of approximately 5.3% across both
cores. At higher-order angular discretization settings, however, the Core I-I Griffin model failed to
converge once the BISON-informed displacement grew large relative to the local mesh size; a
MOVENODE approach, in which the BISON-predicted displacement is used to relocate the mesh nodes
directly rather than being superimposed on the original mesh, resolved this convergence issue and
reproduced the same isothermal-feedback results, consistent with similar experience on the KRUSTY
project. This Griffin convergence issue has since been raised and discussed with the Griffin development
team. Preliminary tests indicate that changing the diffusion closure type to symmetric_lda resolves the
convergence issue when the BISON displacement is superimposed on the original mesh. Further
assessment of whether this will also fix the other convergence issue observed with more SEFOR models
and KRUSTY model is planned for FY 2027.

 43

 The NEAMS scope was also extended in FY 2026 to the Core I-D material reactivity worth
measurements, modeled with the CE Monte Carlo code Shift. The Shift calculations reproduced the
measured worths reasonably well across rod types, with the closest agreement obtained for the standard
fuel and GP rod substitutions and larger, nuclear-data-library-dependent deviations for the stainless steel
and BeO tightener rod cases.
 Main Takeaways
 Explicitly tracking the temperature-induced thermal expansion of the fuel and surrounding
structure is essential for accurately predicting SEFOR reactivity feedback. This conclusion was
reached independently on both sides of the project: the SAS/OFUEL treatment on the ARC/SAS side and
the BISON-informed displacement field on the Griffin side both materially improved agreement with the
measured feedback relative to simpler treatments based on assumed or prescribed thermal expansion
properties.
 Large mesh displacements relative to the local mesh size can cause Griffin SN transport
convergence failures, and node-based mesh updates (MOVENODE) provide a practical solution.
This issue, encountered in the Core I-I isothermal calculations at higher-order angular discretization,
mirrors similar convergence behavior observed on the KRUSTY project and was resolved without
changing the calculated results.
 FY 2026 broadened the SEFOR validation basis for both workflows. The ARC/SAS workflow
was extended from the power-ascending tests to the reactivity-insertion transients, for which the model
captures the shape and timescale of the measured power response but systematically underpredicts the
peak power. The NEAMS effort was extended from reflector-worth and isothermal-feedback calculations
to local material reactivity worth measurements in Core I-D, for which Shift reproduces the measured
trends across rod types, with the closest agreement for the standard fuel and GP rod substitutions and
larger deviations for the stainless steel and BeO tightener rods.

 5.2 Future Work
 For FY 2027, the FRP effort will extend the SAS/OFUEL treatment from the power-ascending tests
to the Core I reactivity-insertion transients and will develop explicit neutronic models of the Core I-K and
Core I-M configurations used in the later sub-prompt and super-prompt critical tests, removing the current
approximation of representing these transients with the Core I-J geometry. The super-prompt critical
transients, not addressed in the FY 2026 scope, are also planned for future modeling.
 For the NEAMS effort, planned FY 2027 work will continue fast reactor multiphysics verification and
validation by developing a coupled Griffin-BISON-SAM model of Core I-J to support multiphysics
validation of the SEFOR steady-state Phase II tests, incorporating improved reactor physics and solid
mechanics models along with a new thermal-hydraulics model.
 Across both programs, continued joint FRP/NEAMS benchmarking is intended to build toward a full
validation of the ARC/SAS and Griffin/BISON workflows against the complete SEFOR experimental
record, including the Phase III reactivity-insertion transients, providing a consistent technical basis for
applying both modeling approaches to future SFR design and licensing analyses.
