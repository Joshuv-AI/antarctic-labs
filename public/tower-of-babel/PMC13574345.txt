Patient-Specific Pessaries After Suboptimal Outcomes With Standard Pessary Use
Authors: Christopher X. Hong, Derek Sham, Shufei Zhang, Cara McDougall, Golafsoun Ameri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Among patients with unsuccessful or suboptimal prior pessary use, patient-specific pessaries achieved high fitting success and continuation.

Vaginal pessaries are an important nonsurgical treatment for symptomatic pelvic organ prolapse (POP), a prevalent and often stigmatized condition that can substantially impair quality of life.1 Despite their general effectiveness, conventional pessaries are manufactured in a limited range of fixed shapes and sizes, and up to one in four patients seeking pessary management is unable to achieve a successful fitting with standard devices.2 Even among those who are successfully fitted, patients may experience discomfort, poor retention, or vaginal irritation, leading to pessary discontinuation, which occurs in approximately one-third of patients within the first 12 months.3 Surveys of urogynecologists similarly report frequent difficulty achieving optimal fit, underscoring the need for more individualized approaches to pessary design and fitting.4

Emerging technologies such as three-dimensional printing and digital modeling now enable fabrication of pessaries tailored to an individual's anatomy and treatment goals. In an early pilot study, patient-specific pessaries created with three-dimensional–printed molds demonstrated feasibility, significant improvement in POPDI-6 (Pelvic Organ Prolapse Distress Inventory-6) scores, and higher patient satisfaction compared with standard pessaries.5 Although these findings provided initial proof of concept, practice-based data evaluating fitting success and patient adoption after regulatory clearance and implementation remain limited.

To address this gap, our objective was to evaluate fitting success, continuation of use, and adoption patterns among patients with prior unsuccessful or suboptimal standard pessary use who selected treatment with a patient-specific pessary as a second-line option. We hypothesized that patient-specific pessaries (Gynethotics) would achieve fitting success rates comparable with those of standard devices, even in this challenging population, and demonstrate high continuation rates at 4 weeks of follow-up after the terminal fitting, the primary outcome of the study.

METHODS

We conducted a retrospective case series of patients who underwent fitting of a patient-specific pessary between February 1, 2024 (the date of introduction into clinical use), and September 25, 2025. Data were obtained from a centralized, secure, cloud-based platform that served as the unified ordering and outcomes repository for patient-specific pessaries. Patients were managed at 18 sites across Canada, including tertiary referral centers, private urogynecology practices, and pelvic floor physical therapy clinics. All data were entered by the ordering clinician into this unified platform, which served as the single data source for analysis. The study was approved by the Advarra IRB (IRB No. PRO0008108).

Patients were eligible for fitting if they had a clinical diagnosis of symptomatic POP and were either current or prior pessary users or had previously experienced unsuccessful fittings. Current pessary users were eligible if they sought improved fit, comfort, or symptom relief. Exclusion criteria included active vaginal erosions, ulcerations, lacerations, or bleeding; urinary or genital tract infection; neurologic disease with impaired pelvic floor sensation; pregnancy; or age younger than 18 years. Those with vaginal erythema or abrasions were eligible for inclusion in accordance with published clinical guidelines.6 All patients who elected to receive a patient-specific pessary provided consent for fitting and research use of deidentified data, obtained locally by the ordering clinician.

All clinicians involved were experienced in standard pessary fitting. Pessary design followed a standardized workflow beginning with subjective assessment of patient-reported issues with prior pessaries (eg, poor retention, excessive movement, or pressure). These reports informed initial design hypotheses. The patient's current pessary or a standard best-fitted pessary was then inserted into the vagina to assess fit. Lateral and anteroposterior dimensions for patient-specific pessaries were assessed by estimating areas of excess space or pressure between the vaginal walls and the standard pessary to guide dimensional adjustments. Position and retention were further assessed during functional maneuvers, including bearing down, coughing, standing, squatting, and voiding.

Quantitative pelvic measurements were obtained, including standardized Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification assessment; vaginal width at the apex, midvagina, and introitus; and evaluation of levator ani tone and strength. These were performed in the dorsal lithotomy position. Suboptimal fit characteristics and prior pessary issues were documented to guide design refinement. Demographic and clinical characteristics (eg, age, parity, surgical history) could also be collected for research purposes, although submission of these data was optional.

Clinicians used a secure, cloud-based platform to design and order patient-specific pessaries (Fig. 1). Available designs included ring and ring-with-support models (each available with or without an anti-incontinence knob), as well as cube, Gellhorn, and Marland pessaries. Using this interface, clinicians tailored pessary parameters, including length, width, height, silicone stiffness, stem angle and length (Gellhorn), elevated platform height (Marland), and anti-incontinence knob size as applicable (Appendix 1, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E717). Dimensions were adjustable in 1-mm increments. Additional features such as a recess in the disk portion of Gellhorn pessaries for improved comfort and a cord on cube pessaries for easier removal could also be incorporated. To guide new clinicians in the process of personalization and ordering, all clinicians were provided with a virtual orientation to the cloud-based platform and a general personalization guide. Although clinicians are given autonomy in the design of the patient-specific pessary to achieve the patient's therapeutic goals, the guide recommends that clinicians first assess the fit and function of the patient's current pessary or best-fitting pessary. These observations then inform targeted modifications to standard designs to achieve the desired therapeutic effect. All designs were clinician approved before fabrication. No modifications were required because of manufacturing constraints; the available range of shapes, sizes, and materials had previously been validated for clinical use. A detailed description of the manufacturing process for patient-specific pessaries has been published previously.5

After the assessment, clinicians submitted orders for up to two patient-specific pessaries through the cloud-based platform. Ordering two designs was encouraged to facilitate patient choice and to inform future predictive modeling of pessary fitting with artificial intelligence, as described further in the Discussion section. At the time of this study, fabrication was typically completed within 1 week of order receipt.

After fabrication and shipment, patients returned 3–6 weeks later for fitting. Unlike standard pessary fitting, which often involves the use of a temporary in-clinic “trial” pessary before a device is prescribed for continued at-home use, patient-specific pessaries serve as both the trial device and the pessary for ongoing use. Fitting success was defined as comfortable pessary retention with patient willingness to proceed with a home trial. If neither pessary was fitted successfully, an additional round of design and fitting could be performed. Patients with a successful fitting took the pessary home for a 4-week trial period and returned for reassessment. For patients fitted with two patient-specific pessaries designs, only the best-fitting device was used for the at-home trial. Outcomes were categorized as 1) successful use, if the patient continued using the pessary (purchase for ongoing use); 2) desiring further fitting, if another round of design and fitting was requested, regardless of whether use was successful; or 3) unsuccessful use, if the patient declined continued use or further fitting.

Patients who desired further fitting, regardless of initial fit or use success, were eligible for a second round of patient-specific pessary designs, following a similar timeline for fabrication, fitting, and follow-up. A reassessment was performed 4 weeks after the final fitting to document the terminal outcome.

The primary outcome was successful patient-specific pessary use, defined as the patient's decision to retain and continue using the patient-specific pessary after up to two rounds of fittings and 4 weeks of follow-up after the terminal fitting. Because the platform was used primarily for clinical ordering and tracking, detailed patient-reported outcome measures and specific outcomes such as retention or complications were not systematically captured.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean±SD or median (interquartile range) as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages with corresponding 95% CIs calculated with the Wilson method, which provides more accurate coverage than the Wald method across most realistic settings. Comparisons of first-round fitting success between single- and multiple-design fittings were performed with the χ2 test. A two-sided value of P<.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed with R 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective study of 113 patients with symptomatic POP and prior unsuccessful or suboptimal pessary use, a patient-specific pessary was successfully fitted in 87 patients (77.0%) at the initial visit. Among these, 69 (79.3%) elected to continue treatment after up to two fittings and a 4-week home trial. Approximately one-quarter of patients who were unsuccessful at the first fitting pursued a second round of design and fitting, with successful fitting and continued use achieved in approximately half of these cases. These findings support the study hypothesis that patient-specific pessaries achieve fitting success rates comparable with those of standard devices (typically 70–80%),7–9 even among patients with prior unsuccessful fittings, and demonstrate high rates of continued use, reflecting improved comfort and satisfaction. Taken together, patient-specific pessaries may provide an acceptable option for patients with prior unsuccessful or suboptimal pessary use.

These results extend an earlier pilot study involving eight participants who underwent iterative, patient-specific pessary design incorporating patient input, clinician assessment, and anatomic measurements.5 Transitioning from a standard to a patient-specific pessary in that study was associated with significant reductions in POPDI-6 scores and improved patient satisfaction on a visual analog scale. However, participants were not permitted to continue long-term use because the device had not yet received regulatory clearance.

The present study builds on early pilot findings by evaluating fitting outcomes and patient adoption of patient-specific pessaries as a second-line option after unsuccessful or suboptimal use of standard pessaries. Although the platform can capture standardized patient-reported outcome measures, they were not systematically recorded because it is used within routine clinical care rather than a structured research environment. Therefore, we selected continued use after a 4-week home trial as a meaningful indicator of patient satisfaction and device performance.

Several features of patient-specific pessaries may underlie the high fitting success rate and clinical adoption observed. Fine-tuned dimensional control (1-mm increments compared with 0.25-in [about 6-mm] increments for many standard pessaries) and individualized geometry may better conform to patient-specific pelvic support needs, thereby improving comfort and retention while reducing irritative symptoms. Specific design elements can also be tailored to patient symptoms. For example, for a patient with occult stress urinary incontinence despite a ring pessary with an anti-incontinence knob, the clinician can design a larger knob or thicker anterior portion to enhance urethral support. For a patient who has difficulty folding a ring pessary, the thickness can be reduced and fabricated with a softer silicone to facilitate insertion. The design platform also enables iterative refinement based on clinician feedback, particularly for patients requiring an additional fitting round.

Beyond clinical fitting success, patterns of patient adoption for use further illustrate the practice-based uptake of patient-specific pessaries. These devices are now cleared by Health Canada and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and are eligible for reimbursement (eg, most Canadian employer health benefit programs); however, they are not yet directly covered by insurance, resulting in initial out-of-pocket costs. During the initial clinical rollout evaluated in this study, patients who elected to continue use typically incurred an upfront cost of approximately $500 (Canadian dollars) before potential reimbursement. Pricing may evolve over time and across regions as manufacturing scale, distribution models, and reimbursement pathways continue to develop. To promote equitable access, participants were offered a risk-free fitting trial, with expenses incurred only if they elected to retain the device, and financial accommodations were available for those with demonstrated need. This distribution model minimized financial barriers while broader reimbursement mechanisms continue to develop. Notably, the decision to retain and purchase the device—the primary end point of the study—despite out-of-pocket cost underscores the perceived clinical and functional benefits that motivated patients to continue use. Continued use in this setting reflects meaningful clinical benefit and willingness to invest in a device that improved comfort and function, reinforcing the real-world effectiveness and acceptability of patient-specific pessaries.

These practice-based outcomes can be better interpreted in the context of prior studies on standard pessaries, which have typically reported initial fitting success rates of 70–80%7–9 and discontinuation rates of 30–40% within the first year.10,11 These findings highlight persistent challenges in achieving both fit and comfort with standard fixed-size devices. In contrast, the present study demonstrated a 77% initial fitting success rate among patients with previously unsuccessful or suboptimal standard pessary use, representing a population typically less likely to achieve satisfactory pessary treatment outcomes. Notably, one-quarter of those with an unsuccessful initial fitting elected to pursue a second round, underscoring both ongoing patient interest in nonsurgical management and the clinical value of offering a personalized pessary as an alternative to standard pessaries.

As with any observational study, perceived benefit may partly reflect novelty associated with a tailor-made device. However, the decision to purchase and continue use after the home trial—particularly among patients who had previously used standard pessaries—suggests that perceived benefit was grounded in tangible improvements in comfort and function rather than novelty alone.

The timing of regulatory clearance coincides with a broader call from clinicians for improved nonsurgical treatment options for POP. In a prior survey of more than 100 urogynecologists and advanced practice clinicians, more than half reported difficulty achieving optimal fit with standard pessaries and more than 95% expressed interest in customized devices.4 Patient-specific pessaries represent a response to this demand for individualized, anatomically adaptable solutions and align with professional society calls for innovation in pessary design and development.6 Together with other recent advances in pessary development such as collapsible pessaries,12 the present study illustrates how patient-specific devices may help meet this ongoing clinical need by expanding effective, patient-centered options for nonsurgical prolapse management.

The integration of patient-specific pessaries into clinical practice represents an important step toward incorporating emerging technologies into prolapse care. Allowing clinicians to design up to two pessaries per fitting round enabled within-patient comparison of design features rather than a binary assessment of success or failure. This approach generates comparative data on relative performance and preference, which can inform the development of future artificial intelligence–based fitting algorithms by linking pessary features to patient-specific anatomy and symptoms. Although fittings in this study were performed by experienced clinicians, decision support tools may eventually aid fitting across a wider range of clinical settings. This work also aligns with ongoing investigations into novel design workflows and archetypes, as well as the biomechanical mechanisms of pessary function.13–16 Although these mechanisms remain incompletely understood, recent magnetic resonance imaging–based studies are beginning to characterize dynamic interactions between pessaries and pelvic floor structures, providing a foundation for future integration of imaging, biomechanical modeling, and artificial intelligence–driven design to optimize patient-specific performance.17 These insights will likely inform the development of novel pessaries incorporating new mechanisms of support that overcome inherent limitations associated with current historical shapes.

Patient-specific pessaries also have practical limitations, including higher manufacturing cost, longer production time, and current reliance on centralized fabrication. As with other customized medical devices such as knee implants or orthotics, individualized design and quality checks increase production costs.18,19 These may decrease with automation and economies of scale as clinical adoption grows. The approximately 3- to 6-week interval from assessment to delivery reflects centralized manufacturing and regulatory logistics. Distributed manufacturing and delivery models in the future, including regional production hubs or in-clinic three-dimensional printing, may shorten turnaround time, enhance customization, and facilitate broader integration into the clinical workflow.

Strengths of this study include the inclusion of patients from multiple and diverse clinical settings, use of a standardized workflow for patient-specific pessary design, and enrollment of individuals who were previously unsuccessful or had suboptimal outcomes with standard pessaries, a population in whom achieving a satisfactory fit is challenging at best.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design, based on clinician-entered data from patients who opted for patient-specific pessary fitting, does not include patients who were approached but declined fitting, which may introduce participation bias. In addition, the retrospective nature of the study introduces the potential for reporting bias and limits the ability to analyze standardized patient-reported outcomes or detailed histories of prior pessary use. Nonetheless, the use of a centralized, cloud-based platform ensured consistent data entry across sites and reflects routine clinical documentation practices. The primary end point, patient continuation of use, serves as a pragmatic proxy for satisfaction and device performance but may not fully reflect symptom improvement or long-term efficacy. The requirement for patients to purchase the device for continued use may also lead to underestimation of successful trial use. This measure, however, captures a meaningful patient-centered outcome that directly reflects willingness to continue use, a key determinant of clinical success in pessary management. In addition, the absence of systematically collected data on adverse events, self-management capacity, retention duration, or anatomic outcomes limits full assessment of safety and efficacy. Future prospective studies with standardized patient-reported outcome measures and longer-term follow-up beyond 4 weeks are needed to validate these findings and to further evaluate the safety, efficacy, and patient experience with patient-specific pessaries. Notably, no adverse safety events were reported through the centralized platform during the study period, suggesting an acceptable short-term safety profile consistent with prior literature on standard pessary use. Finally, unused or ill-fitting patient-specific pessaries are currently discarded in accordance with medical device and infection control regulations, highlighting the need for future work to explore environmentally responsible disposal or recycling pathways that are compliant with regulatory and safety requirements.

In summary, this multisite retrospective evaluation demonstrates that patient-specific pessaries can achieve high fitting success and acceptance among patients with previously unsuccessful or suboptimal outcomes with standard pessaries. These findings highlight the potential of individualized devices to improve comfort, retention, and nonsurgical management options for POP.
