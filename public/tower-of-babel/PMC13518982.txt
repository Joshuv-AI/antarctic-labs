Adaptive quantum kernel selection via leakage-free stacking for clinical diagnostics on NISQ hardware
Authors: Miit Daga, Saransh Naole, Dhriti Parikh, Kundanika Reddy Bommineni, Swarna Priya Ramu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quantum kernel methods map clinical features into exponentially large Hilbert spaces where overlapping biological markers can become more separable than in fixed-dimensional classical feature spaces, but existing work evaluates single quantum feature maps, ignores barren-plateau failure modes, and relies on single train–test splits vulnerable to data leakage. Classical diagnostics for Parkinson’s disease, breast cancer, and diabetes remain limited by the Specificity–Recall trade-off that fixed-dimensional kernels impose on overlapping biomarker distributions. We propose an adaptive hybrid quantum framework routing clinical data through three distinct quantum feature maps, namely Angle, Amplitude, and ZZ-entanglement, computing fidelity-based Gram matrices for Quantum SVM and Quantum KNN classifiers. A Logistic Regression meta-learner, trained on strictly out-of-fold predictions from nested cross-validation (5-fold inner, 10-fold outer), learns which quantum kernel generalizes on each dataset and suppresses those that do not. Evaluated on Parkinson’s (195 patients), Breast Cancer (569), and Diabetes (768) with 1,000-iteration bootstrapping, the ensemble raised Parkinson’s Specificity from 0.585 to 0.813 (p<0.001) while maintaining Recall above 0.95, matched classical RBF-SVM on Breast Cancer (all p>0.05), and improved Diabetes Recall from 0.553 to 0.621 (p=0.004). A standalone Variational Quantum Classifier failed on all datasets (ROC-AUC 0.51 to 0.57), confirming barren plateau limitations. Explainability via SHAP, LIME, and Permutation Importance revealed dataset-dependent kernel trust: the meta-learner suppressed QKNN Amplitude on Diabetes (coefficient -0.353) while amplifying it on Parkinson’s (1.761). PCA-based feature backtracking recovered established biomarkers including Insulin and Glucose for Diabetes, vocal perturbation measures for Parkinson’s, and nucleus geometry for Breast Cancer. Noise simulations confirmed graceful degradation under NISQ conditions. The framework performs data-driven kernel selection, removing the need to pre-specify an encoding strategy.

Introduction

One of the most important applications of machine learning (ML) is still clinical diagnostics of chronic and degenerative diseases. The three diseases considered in this work have an enormous combined global health burden. Parkinson’s disease has over 8.5 million sufferers worldwide, and disability and mortality rates for Parkinson’s increase at a rate faster than for any other neurological disease1. Breast cancer is the most common cancer diagnosed worldwide, with 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths recorded globally in 20202. Diabetes has an estimated 537 million adults suffering from it worldwide, and this figure is expected to increase to 783 million by 2045. The cost of healthcare for diabetes globally is already above $966 billion annually3. For all these three diseases, early and accurate diagnosis has a direct impact on the outcome of the disease. What these diseases share, from a classification standpoint, is that their biomarker distributions overlap heavily between healthy and diseased populations (vocal perturbation measures for Parkinson’s, tumor geometry for breast cancer, metabolic indicators for diabetes), creating decision boundaries that classical fixed-dimensional kernels struggle to resolve.

Classical ML has been applied extensively to all three diagnostic problems. SVMs with RBF kernels, Random Forests, XGBoost, and gradient-boosted ensembles provide strong baselines across these datasets4–6. More recent work has tackled the noise and class imbalance that characterize clinical tabular data through stacked architectures: Zhang et al7. proposed a noise-robust evolving hierarchical configuration that maintains predictive accuracy under up to 30% noise contamination, while Zhang et al8. combined deep ensemble stochastic configuration networks with graph-based intuitionistic fuzzy membership scoring to handle severe class imbalance in depression screening. Both approaches operate in classical feature spaces. The question this paper asks is whether mapping clinical features into quantum Hilbert spaces, where the encoding geometry can be varied and selected adaptively, offers a complementary advantage that fixed-dimensional classical kernels cannot provide. In Pima Diabetes, for instance, glucose distributions of healthy and diabetic patients share a wide overlap region, and classical kernels cannot simultaneously achieve high Recall and high Specificity. Models tuned for sensitivity flag too many healthy patients as diseased; conservative models miss genuinely ill patients who need early intervention.

This is where quantum computing can provide a completely new way of tackling this issue. By encoding the classical patient information in quantum form, quantum feature maps can map the information onto exponentially large Hilbert spaces where the classes can be linearly separable. Havlíček et al9. demonstrated this principle in 2019 by showing that quantum kernel methods on near-term quantum hardware can achieve classification performance comparable to or exceeding classical approaches on structured datasets. Since then, quantum machine learning (QML) has attracted growing interest in healthcare applications, with studies applying quantum kernels and variational circuits to breast cancer10–13, diabetes6,14,15, and Parkinson’s disease5,16–18. Beyond healthcare, quantum kernel methods have also been applied to other structured-data classification tasks such as encrypted network traffic analysis, where hybrid quantum-classical models using Quantum SVM (QSVM) and Quantum k-Nearest Neighbours (QKNN) with angle and amplitude encodings demonstrated competitive performance on cybersecurity benchmarks19. The consistent finding across domains that no single quantum encoding is universally optimal further motivates the heterogeneous multi-kernel framework proposed in this work as illustrated in the Fig. 1.

Despite this growing activity, the current QML literature for clinical diagnostics has three methodological weaknesses that undermine both its scientific credibility and its clinical usefulness. First, the majority of works assess a single quantum feature map and present the optimal result, without considering that various quantum encodings have varying geometrical biases on the data. Rajesh et al10. used Z, ZZ, and Pauli quantum feature maps, but for validation, they used a standard train-test split without nested cross-validation. Jaincy and Pattabiraman11 achieved 98% accuracy for QSVM, but they used a simple hold-out method for evaluation, without any form of bootstrapping or confidence intervals. Second, works that make use of Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQCs) commonly fail to address the notorious barren plateau problem20, in which gradients decay exponentially with circuit depth, resulting in optimization convergence at random chance accuracy. Khan et al14. tested 76 different VQC configurations on the diabetes dataset and observed that, despite using error mitigation techniques, the best VQC achieved only 85% accuracy. Third, the cost of computing quantum Gram matrices, given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(N^2)$$\end{document}, renders rigorous nested cross-validation computationally expensive. As such, most existing research relies on single train-test splits, which are known to be vulnerable to data leakage21 and overfitting. Related works

This section reviews QML research across the three clinical domains evaluated in our experiments. We organize the review by disease because the three datasets differ substantially in feature dimensionality, sample size, class balance, and the geometry of biomarker overlap, so the same quantum encoding can succeed in one domain and fail in another. Within each domain we examine three recurring methodological choices that the recent literature has not resolved simultaneously: the use of a single quantum feature map without comparison against alternative geometries, reliance on hold-out validation rather than nested cross-validation, and deployment of variational quantum circuits without explicit treatment of barren-plateau failures. The closing synthesis at the end of this section consolidates these cross-cutting gaps and connects them to the architectural choices that follow in Section "The Proposed Architecture".

Breast cancer diagnostics

Gurcan4 developed a methodology for breast cancer diagnosis using classical stacked ensemble techniques that combined tree-based algorithms with a deep learning (DL) meta-estimator. Although the initial prediction was made by CatBoost, LightGBM, and Extra Trees, the Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) performed as a meta-estimator to produce the final prediction. Pre-processing of data was performed by applying standard scaling and feature importance ranking by min-max scaling to select the top 11 clinical features. Although the stacked ensemble model produced high results of 97.72% accuracy and 0.9819 for F1 score, it is to be understood that this is a classical approach without any involvement of quantum topological feature mapping to solve overlapping non-linear boundaries, and in this case, using DL on a medical dataset may lead to overfitting without proper out-of-fold validation.

Rajesh et al10. introduced a methodology for the binary classification of breast cancer based on the mapping of classical electronic health records to quantum feature spaces. In the study, the dimensionality reduction of the feature spaces was performed using the Principal Component Analysis method and mapped to 2D, 3D, and 4D spaces. Then the data was encoded using the Z, ZZ, and Pauli feature maps to compute the quantum kernel matrix. A classical SVM was used for the classification task. Dixit and Rajkumar12 presented a methodology for biomedical data classification by using a hybrid quantum-enhanced neural network (QENN) architecture. Even though dropout regularization and randomized deactivation of nodes were applied in classical neural network layers to avoid overfitting, in the quantum layer, ZZFeatureMap was applied for data encoding, and RealAmplitudes was applied to optimize parameters by using the Parameter Shift Rule. Data pre-processing was performed by using Z-score normalization and target encoding. Even though 98.83% accuracy and 100% sensitivity were achieved by using the QENN on the Wisconsin dataset, it is well established that deep parameterized quantum circuits, or ansatz, are notoriously prone to barren plateaus during optimization, which is reflected by their poor performance on noisy and imbalanced datasets.

Yadav et al.13 introduced a methodology for the classification of structured data by iteratively connecting QSVM and Quantum Neural Network (QNN). Although the proposed QSVM model employed fidelity quantum kernels such as Z, ZZ, and Pauli for the identification of high-margin decision boundaries, the results were reduced using PCA and then fed into the Estimator QNN model based on the variational ansatz. The data preprocessing involved Z-score normalization and mutual information-based feature selection. The proposed model has shown results over 95% accuracy and has reduced the computational overhead. However, the use of the variational QNN as a meta-classifier leads to instability and noise in the results because the model is computationally infeasible for robust cross-validation. Diabetes risk prediction

Vimal and Dixit6 proposed a methodology for assessing the predictability power of quantum and classical algorithms on metabolic disorders. Although the classical model was assessed using the Logistic Regression and SVM algorithm, the quantum model was assessed by comparing the VQC and Quantum Support Vector Classifier (QSVC). Although the authors were able to assess the basic metrics and determine that classical SVM (76%) and QSVC (74%) were significantly better than the VQC (61%), the authors failed to provide any architectural solution to improve the performance of the failing quantum neural embeddings and were only able to assess the algorithms as a black box.

Khan et al.14 presented a methodology that predicts diabetes using an optimised VQC with Quantum Error Mitigation (QEM). Although the authors tested the proposed method with 76 different variations based on the number of qubits, layers, and optimizers used (Constrained Optimization BY Linear Approximation (COBYLA), Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA), and Sequential Least SQuares Programming (SLSQP)), the data encoding was based on Pauli, Z, and ZZ feature maps. The data pre-processing included handling missing values using the means of attributes and using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Chi-Square, and Extra Trees as feature selection methods. Although the optimised VQC with the best accuracy of 85% was based on the ZZ feature map, COBYLA optimiser, and 6 layers, the authors empirically proved the severe limitations of the VQC, showing the difficulty that deep quantum circuits face in converging on noisy clinical data due to gradients that vanish, even with the application of the error scale.

Young II15 suggested a methodology based on a classical approach to tackle the problem of risk prediction of diabetes, employing a quantum mechanical emulation approach. Although similarities between patients were taken into account through a self-improving concept of a k-nearest neighbors graph, the expressiveness of features was extended through a trigonometric phase feature map, emulating a quantum amplitude approach. In pre-processing, a severe class imbalance problem was handled through the generation of 2,000 synthetic samples based on a Gaussian Mixture Model approach. Although a stacked ensemble of transformers, CNNs, and tree-based models reported an out-of-fold F1 score of 0.8933, the approach was based on a classical approach only, without utilizing the true Hilbert space approach, thereby missing the topological separability of a true quantum kernel approach.

Kiran et al.22 presented a methodology for the real-time monitoring of diabetes using a hybrid quantum-classical generative adversarial network that is optimized using the Boosted Sooty Tern algorithm. Although the raw Internet of Things (IoT) sensor data was preprocessed using the Iterative Self-Guided Filtering method, the critical features were extracted using the Scale-Aware Modulation Transformer prior to the application of the quantum-classical Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) layers for the categorization of the disease. Although the proposed system achieved an exceptionally high accuracy level of 99.7%, the enormous complexity of the proposed system and the application of the generative adversarial networks for the direct categorization of the clinical data using the proposed system raise severe concerns about the overfitting, poor calibration, and lack of interpretability of the proposed system with respect to the medical professionals. Parkinson’s disease detection

A methodology was proposed by Sha and Rahamathulla5 for the analysis of the speech signal using a Quantum-Classical Convolutional Neural Network (QCCNN). Though the use of the quantum layers was intended for the replacement of the traditional CNN filters for faster feature mapping, the hyperparameters were optimized using a bio-inspired algorithm known as the Gazelle Optimization Algorithm (GOA). The pre-processing of the data heavily involved PCA for the purpose of reducing dimensions before feeding the vectors into the quantum convolution layers. Though the system was able to achieve 98% accuracy in the analysis of the speech signal, the use of CNNs in the analysis of the tabularly extracted features imposes artificial spatial relationships.

Yadav and Vijarania16along with Arpitha et al17. presented methodologies to detect Parkinson’s disease based on ML and quantum computing methods used in voice and acoustic signals. PCA was used as a method of feature selection, while classification was performed through classical models such as KNN, Naïve Bayes, and Decision Trees, along with QNN as a concept. Although the works suggested ensemble methods to improve the accuracy of the models, they were more theoretical in nature, lacking rigorous statistical results such as empirical p-value estimation through bootstrapping methods, while treating quantum models as a black box without understanding the effect of different topologies of quantum feature maps on real-life acoustic noise.

Ahmad et al.18 presented a methodology to deal with the complex temporal dependencies of Parkinson’s speech biomarkers with a Multi-Scale Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) model coupled with a self-attention mechanism. Although the temporal dependencies were successfully addressed with a series of hierarchical GRU models, the problem of feature dimensions was reduced with a Quantum-inspired Feature Selection algorithm, which used a trigonometric score function based on variance and correlation values. The pre-processing of the data was performed with Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), class balancing, and standard scaling methods. Although a remarkable AUC of 99.06% was achieved, the quantum part of the model was only based on a classical mathematical heuristic, meaning the model never actually used a patient’s data in a high-dimensional quantum Hilbert space.

Across all three domains, a consistent pattern emerges: existing QML approaches either rely on a single quantum embedding whose performance depends unpredictably on dataset topology, or deploy variational quantum circuits that encounter barren plateaus during optimization. Beyond the disease-axis organization above, the cited work falls into three methodological families. Kernel-based approaches use quantum circuits to compute fidelity Gram matrices that are then consumed by classical SVMs10,11, restricting quantum computation to inner-product evaluation and avoiding gradient-based optimization entirely. Variational approaches train parameterized quantum circuits end-to-end using classical optimizers6,14, gaining expressive flexibility but exposing the model to barren-plateau failure modes. Hybrid quantum-classical neural networks combine quantum feature extraction with classical CNN or GAN classifiers5,12,13,22, achieving high accuracy at the cost of dual-stack training complexity and reduced interpretability. The framework proposed here sits in the kernel-based family but is distinguished by two architectural choices: it evaluates three quantum encoding geometries in parallel rather than committing to one, and it aggregates their predictions through a leakage-free OOF stacking protocol with explainability-driven kernel selection. No prior work in any of these three families has combined these choices with nested cross-validation and 1,000-iteration bootstrap statistical testing.

Background and fundamentals

In order to fully understand the Hybrid Quantum Stacking Framework, it is first necessary to establish the basic principles of quantum computing and classical ensemble machine learning. This section will discuss the mechanical process in which quantum computers process data, the algorithms they utilize for classification of the data, and the classical systems they utilize for stability and validation in a medical setting.

Quantum computing and hilbert space feature mapping

Traditional ML techniques depend on classical computers, which use bits to process data. A bit is essentially a physical switch that may be in only one of two definitive states, 0 or 1. However, as medical data gets increasingly complicated with thousands of overlapping biological markers, classical computers are forced to sequentially process these variables or use heavy approximations.

Quantum computing represents an entirely different mechanical way of processing data by using quantum bits, or qubits23. Unlike classical bits, which may be in only one of two definitive states, 0 or 1, a qubit may be in a continuous linear combination of these states at any given time by use of a property known as superposition. When a computer operation is performed on a qubit in a state of superposition, it is as if the operation were performed on all of these states at once.

Furthermore, several qubits can be interconnected through a concept known as entanglement. This means that the state of any qubit is mathematically related to the state of any other qubit, irrespective of the distance between the two qubits. In the realm of machine learning, this property is extremely useful. It is rare for medical data to be independent in nature. A patient’s blood pressure and other factors are correlated in complex ways. The entangled qubits would naturally be able to share this complex data and arrive at complex correlations in the data.

For using these characteristics for classification, a technique called Hilbert Space Feature Mapping is employed. In conventional machine learning, SVMs are employed for classification of different data points for different classes (e.g., healthy and diseased patients). In conventional data, the characteristics of healthy and diseased patients are too overlapping, and it is impossible to draw a line between the two classes of data. Conventional SVMs address this problem through a technique called the “Kernel Trick,” where a mathematical function is applied to project the data into a higher dimension where a line can be drawn between the two classes of data.

Quantum computers carry out this process physically. The quantum kernel trick simply takes classical data points and represents them physically on the quantum computer. The mathematical framework for all possible states of any physical system is referred to as a Hilbert space24. Due to the fact that the size of a Hilbert space increases exponentially with every added qubit to the physical system, representing classical data on a quantum computer inherently maps it onto an exponentially massive feature space. In this highly expanded feature space, highly entangled and noisy medical data simply spreads out, making it trivial for the algorithm to create a linear boundary between healthy and diseased patients.

A natural question is how quantum feature spaces differ from the implicit feature spaces of classical kernels, since classical methods like the Gaussian RBF kernel also map data into infinite-dimensional reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. The distinction is threefold. First, classical kernels are restricted to functional forms that can be computed efficiently on classical hardware, which limits the geometric structures they can express. Quantum feature maps access Hilbert spaces whose dimension grows exponentially with qubit count, and the circuit topology (entanglement pattern, gate sequence) determines the geometry in ways that have no efficient classical analog9. Second, classical kernel computations never explicitly construct the feature-space representation; the kernel trick evaluates inner products without materializing the high-dimensional vectors. Quantum circuits, by contrast, physically prepare the feature-space state on the device, so the encoding is not implicit but a real physical process whose structure can be inspected and modified. Third, entanglement between qubits introduces correlations in the feature space that are absent in separable classical kernels. The ZZ-Feature Map, for example, encodes pairwise products of clinical features as entangling gate angles, coupling variables in a way that a product kernel like the RBF cannot replicate without explicit feature engineering. Whether these structural differences translate into classification advantages depends on the dataset, which is precisely why the proposed framework evaluates multiple quantum geometries rather than committing to one.

A practical consequence of these structural differences is that classical kernel methods offer no mechanism to vary the topology of the feature space without changing the kernel function entirely, for example switching from RBF to polynomial. In the quantum case, changing the circuit layout, the entanglement pattern, or the gate angles produces a different Hilbert-space geometry while the kernel evaluation procedure remains identical: prepare two quantum states, measure their fidelity.

This is what enables the proposed framework to route the same patient data through three topologically distinct embeddings and let the meta-learner select the most informative one, an approach that has no natural counterpart in classical kernel learning.

Quantum data encoding strategies

Before any clinical data is fed to the quantum algorithm for processing, the classical data or numbers, such as age, insulin levels, or vocal frequencies, need to be translated or encoded to be in a quantum state. The translation of classical data to quantum data is referred to as data encoding. Due to the effect of encoding on the data geometry in Hilbert space, different encoding techniques produce drastically different results depending on the type of noise in the data set. This study utilizes three different encoding techniques.

Angle encoding

This is one of the most direct approaches to injecting classical data into the quantum circuit. In the Angle Encoding approach, the classical features are scaled to fall within the interval [0, π ]. This value is then used as the angle to physically rotate the qubit along some axis, normally the Y-axis or the Z-axis, using a rotation gate, such as the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_y$$\end{document} gate. In the case of N features in the data set, the number of qubits required to inject the data into the quantum circuit is N. This O(N) scaling makes the Angle Encoding approach computationally inexpensive and fast to simulate. However, the fact that each classical feature is encoded into its own qubit means that Angle Encoding, on its own, is not sufficient to produce the entanglement required to simulate complex relationships between different biological markers.

Amplitude encoding

Unlike rotation angles of individual qubits in Angle Encoding, Amplitude Encoding compresses classical data directly into the probability amplitudes of the quantum state itself. A quantum state with N qubits will have \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^N$$\end{document} probability amplitudes in total. Mathematically, the classical vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}$$\end{document} is normalized to have \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Vert \textbf{x}\Vert ^2 = 1$$\end{document}, and it is then mapped to the quantum state:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\psi _x\rangle = \sum _{i=0}^{2^N - 1} x_i |i\rangle$$\end{document}Thus, it is possible to encode 4 clinical features in a dataset using just 2 qubits, as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^2 = 4$$\end{document}. Hence, it works as a highly dense data compression scheme, which requires only O(łog N) qubits to encode N features in the data. By forcing data to be highly compressed, Amplitude Encoding inherently works as a natural filter for noise in data. In this paper, Amplitude Encoding was found to be extremely useful in extracting the fundamental biomarkers of healthy patients, thereby achieving tremendous improvements in Specificity of diagnosis by eliminating false positives.

ZZ-feature map (instantaneous quantum polynomial-time (IQP) encoding)

The ZZ-Feature Map is a highly non-linear entanglement-heavy quantum encoding strategy. In this approach, a single-qubit rotation operation is performed on the classical data set (similarly to Angle Encoding). Then, pairs of qubits are entangled using two-qubit entanglement gates (ZZ-gates). The rotation angle for these entangled gates is determined by computing the mathematical product of two different classical features being encoded. By forcing a rotation operation on the interaction of two different features in a quantum circuit, the ZZ-Feature Map can naturally encode complex multi-variable interactions in the data set. As a result, this powerful quantum encoding strategy is particularly well-suited for modeling complex interactions in diseases, enabling it to achieve a high Recall rate in identifying severely ill patients.

Hybrid vs. pure quantum machine learning models

Once the clinical data is encoded in the quantum Hilbert space, it needs to be analyzed in order to derive a prediction for the diagnosis. Machine learning algorithms that use quantum mechanics can be broadly classified as hybrid models, in which the feature mapping is performed using a quantum computer and classification is performed using a classical computer, and pure quantum models, in which both feature mapping and classification are performed using a quantum circuit. The algorithms used in this framework are described below.

QSVM

QSVM is a hybrid model. In order to draw a decision boundary for a classical SVM model, a “Gram Matrix” is required. This is a grid where all pairs of kernel values for all pairs of patients in a dataset are stored. QSVM employs a quantum computer for this calculation9. QSVM calculates the quantum state for Patient A and the quantum state for Patient B, and then calculates the “Fidelity” between them. Fidelity is a physical quantity that can be measured by how much these two quantum states overlap, ranging from 0 (completely different) to 1 (completely similar). Fidelity can be expressed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K(\textbf{x}_i, \textbf{x}_j) = \left| \langle \psi (\textbf{x}_i) \mid \psi (\textbf{x}_j) \rangle \right| ^2$$\end{document}The quantum computer computes the fidelity for all pairs of patients and then the Gram Matrix. A classical computer then processes this matrix and determines the support vectors and the decision boundary.

QKNN

Classical K-Nearest Neighbors determines the classification of a new patient based on the known patients near the new patient and the proximity between the patients. Classical KNN computes the proximity between the patients using classical Euclidean distance. QKNN replaces the classical distance metric with Quantum Distance. QKNN measures the fidelity between the two patients’ encoded states in the quantum computer and then computes the Quantum Distance using the following equation25:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_Q(\textbf{x}_i, \textbf{x}_j) = 1 - \left| \langle \psi (\textbf{x}_i) \mid \psi (\textbf{x}_j) \rangle \right| ^2$$\end{document}If the two patients are perfectly identical in the Hilbert space and the fidelity is 1, the Quantum Distance between the two patients is 0. Quantum KNN then determines the K nearest neighbors and the classification based on the Quantum Distance.

VQC and the barren plateau problem

A VQC is a purely quantum neural network26. It has three stages. In the first stage, the classical data is mapped to the quantum circuit. In the second stage, the data goes through the “ansatz,” which is a series of trainable quantum gates with adjustable parameters. In the third stage, the qubits are measured to produce the classical prediction. A classical optimizer is used to determine the error and optimize the parameters of the quantum gates for the next prediction.

However, VQCs suffer from a critical mathematical limitation in the form of the Barren Plateau Problem20. In VQCs, as the parameterized quantum circuit is expanded in depth or the number of qubits is increased, the optimization surface becomes completely flat. In other words, the gradients, which are the mathematical indicators of the direction in which the weights of the model need to be adjusted in order to reduce the error, tend to zero exponentially with an increase in the depth of the circuit. When the gradients are zero, it is impossible for the model to learn anything at all. In VQCs, where medical data is involved, it is expected to be embedded with highly complex features. In such cases, when the Barren Plateau Problem is encountered, the model is expected to stop learning altogether and fail to improve its accuracy beyond random guessing (approximately 50%).

Ensemble learning and OOF stacking

Since no quantum encoding strategy can handle all types of clinical noise optimally, Ensemble Learning is used in this study to combine multiple quantum algorithms in a classical framework. Ensemble learning is a classical framework in which a set of “base models” make predictions on a given problem, and these predictions are combined to make a final prediction that is highly accurate27.

Hard voting vs. meta-learning

The simplest ensemble learner is Hard Voting, in which each base model makes a prediction on a given problem, and a final prediction is made by a majority vote among all base models. However, Hard Voting does not differentiate among base models based on their individual performances. A more complex ensemble learner is Stacking or Meta-Learning28. In a Stacking ensemble learner, a secondary machine learning model, called a Meta-Learner, is used to evaluate all base models’ predictions on a given problem. The Meta-Learner learns dynamically about the strengths and weaknesses of all base models. For instance, the Meta-Learner can learn that QKNN is a very reliable model for diagnosing healthy patients while QSVM is a more reliable model for diagnosing sick patients. The Meta-Learner can then assign different weights to these models for optimal prediction.

The threat of data leakage

Training the Meta-Learner is a delicate task because it has the potential to cause a catastrophic failure known as Data Leakage21. If the underlying quantum models are trained on a dataset and then asked to predict the results on the same dataset, they would be unrealistically optimistic because they were exposed to the answers during the training phase. If such overly optimistic predictions are fed into the Meta-Learner, the latter would be overly reliant on the base models. This would result in severe errors when the model is applied to unseen patients in the real world.

OOF cross-validation

In this framework, Data Leakage can be avoided by utilizing OOF prediction28,29. In this approach, a dataset is divided equally into K folds or subsets. Then, a set of quantum models is trained on K-1 folds of the dataset and asked to make predictions on the “holdout” or “unseen” fold. This approach is repeated K times until all patients in the dataset receive a prediction from a model that has never seen their information during training.

Since these base models never saw the patients they were predicting on during their training phase, these predictions are entirely unbiased. Then these unbiased predictions, referred to as meta-features, are combined and used as inputs for the Meta-Learner model. In this study, Logistic Regression is used as a Meta-Learner model because it is a highly interpretable model that can handle balanced class weights. By using a strict OOF cross-validation approach, the classical scaffolding approach works as a dataset-agnostic safety mechanism that can dynamically harness the topological benefits of quantum models while rigorously proving the genuineness and safety of the diagnostic results for unseen data.

Stage 1: Clinical data compression and circuit feasibility

Medical datasets contain features of varying scales, units, and distributions. The University of California at Irvine (UCI) Parkinson’s dataset, for instance, contains 22 vocal biomarker features ranging from fundamental frequency measurements (Multi-Dimensional Voice Program (MDVP):Fo in Hz) to nonlinear dynamical complexity measures (D2, Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA)). The Wisconsin Breast Cancer dataset contains 30 tumor geometry measurements, and the Pima Diabetes dataset contains 8 metabolic indicators. Feeding all raw features directly into quantum circuits would require one qubit per feature under Angle Encoding, making the simulation of a 30-qubit circuit computationally prohibitive on current NISQ hardware.

To address this, we apply a two-step preprocessing pipeline. First, Z-score standardization is applied to all features to ensure zero mean and unit variance:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x'_i = \frac{x_i - \mu _i}{\sigma _i} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Second, PCA reduces the standardized feature space to d = 8 components. This value was chosen as a compromise between information retention and circuit feasibility. On the Parkinson’s dataset, 8 components retain 95.77% of the total variance. On Breast Cancer, 8 components retain 92.60% despite the original 30-dimensional space. On Diabetes, all 8 original features are preserved, yielding 100% variance retention.

After PCA, a Min-Max rescaling maps all component values to the interval [0, π ]:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x''_i = \frac{x'_i - \min (x'_i)}{\max (x'_i) - \min (x'_i)} \cdot \pi \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This angular rescaling is a physical requirement. Experimental environment

All experiments were conducted within a Kaggle Notebook environment using Python 3.12 on CPU. Quantum circuit simulation was performed using PennyLane v0.44.1 with the lightning.qubit statevector backend for noiseless experiments and the default.mixed density matrix backend for noise robustness analysis. Classical machine learning components used scikit-learn v1.5. Statistical analysis was performed with SciPy v1.14 and statsmodels v0.14. Explainability analysis used SHAP v0.46 and LIME v0.2. All random seeds were fixed to 42 for reproducibility.

In conventional machine learning pipelines, dimensionality reduction is typically applied independently within each cross-validation fold to prevent information leakage. However, quantum kernel evaluation on classical statevector simulators requires computing an N × N Gram matrix with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(N^2)$$\end{document} circuit evaluations. Recomputing these matrices independently within each fold would multiply the already substantial simulation cost by the number of folds. Therefore, following established practice in quantum kernel literature9,24, PCA was applied globally before cross-validation, and the Gram matrices were precomputed once for the full dataset. This is methodologically sound because the quantum kernel function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K(\textbf{x}_i, \textbf{x}_j) = |\langle 0 | U^\dagger (\textbf{x}_j) U(\textbf{x}_i) | 0 \rangle |^2$$\end{document} is a pure function of two input vectors and involves no label information or model fitting. During cross-validation, the appropriate submatrices are sliced for training and testing, ensuring that test samples are only compared against training samples. The actual data leakage risk in ensemble learning arises when base model predictions on training data are generated by models trained on those same samples, which is precisely what the OOF procedure in Stage 3 prevents. In this regard, computing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K(\textbf{x}_i, \textbf{x}_j)$$\end{document} is mathematically equivalent to precomputing a Euclidean distance matrix between all patient pairs before running a classical KNN. Just as a precomputed classical distance matrix does not constitute data leakage, neither does a precomputed quantum fidelity matrix, provided the test samples are strictly isolated during the nested cross-validation and meta-learner training phases. Nevertheless, we recognize that global PCA permits the distribution of the test fold to influence the principal component axes to some extent. To assess this influence, we compared global PCA to a completely ’leak-proof’ approach where PCA is fit independently in each outer fold. The Accuracy difference was found to be small across all three data sets: Parkinson’s: 0.0000, Breast Cancer: 0.0018, Diabetes: 0.0013. This shows that global PCA does not significantly influence cross-validation estimates. This design choice, precomputing the quantum Gram matrix once and slicing submatrices during cross-validation, follows established practice in quantum kernel literature9,24.

Stage 2: Heterogeneous quantum topological mapping

The preprocessed 8-dimensional feature vectors are passed through three structurally distinct quantum encoding circuits. Each encoding imposes a different geometric inductive bias on the data in Hilbert space. The use of multiple encodings is motivated by the finding, confirmed empirically in Section "Experimental Results and Discussion", that no single quantum feature map produces optimal classification boundaries across all clinical datasets.

Angle encoding

Each of the 8 PCA features is mapped to a single-qubit \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_y$$\end{document} rotation on an 8-qubit register:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & |\psi (\textbf{x})\rangle = \bigotimes _{i=1}^{8} R_y(x_i) |0\rangle \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This is a product state with no entanglement between qubits. The encoding is computationally inexpensive (1.685 ms per kernel call) and serves as a baseline that captures individual feature contributions without modeling inter-feature interactions.

Amplitude encoding

The 8-dimensional feature vector is normalized and embedded directly into the probability amplitudes of a 3-qubit state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^3 = 8$$\end{document} amplitudes):4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & |\psi (\textbf{x})\rangle = \sum _{i=0}^{7} \frac{x_i}{\Vert \textbf{x}\Vert } |i\rangle \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This encoding compresses the data into an exponentially smaller qubit register (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lceil \log _2 8 \rceil = 3$$\end{document} qubits instead of 8), creating an information-dense representation where all features are entangled through amplitude interference. The normalization constraint \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Vert \textbf{x}\Vert ^2 = 1$$\end{document} acts as an implicit regularizer, forcing the encoding to prioritize relative feature ratios over absolute magnitudes.

ZZ-feature map (IQP encoding)

The ZZ-Feature Map applies a layer of Hadamard gates and single-qubit \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_z$$\end{document} rotations, followed by two-qubit \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{ZZ}$$\end{document} entangling gates whose rotation angles are the products of adjacent feature pairs:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} U_{ZZ}(\textbf{x}) = \prod _{\langle i,j \rangle } R_{ZZ}(x_i \cdot x_j) \cdot \prod _{i=1}^{8} R_z(x_i) H_i \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle i, j \rangle$$\end{document} denotes nearest-neighbor qubit pairs. By encoding pairwise feature products as entangling gate angles, this circuit naturally captures multiplicative interactions between clinical variables. This is particularly relevant for datasets where diagnostic patterns emerge from feature correlations, for example, the interaction between tumor radius and texture in breast cancer diagnosis.

Stage 3: Leakage-free meta-feature generation via out-of-fold prediction

The central methodological contribution of this work is the OOF stacking procedure that combines the predictions of heterogeneous quantum base models into meta-features for a second-level classifier. The critical challenge in stacking is avoiding data leakage: if a base model’s predictions on the training set are generated by a model that was trained on those same samples, the meta-learner receives inflated confidence estimates and fails on unseen data. The OOF procedure operates as follows. The outer evaluation uses a 10-fold Stratified Cross-Validation loop. Within each outer fold, the training set is further divided using an inner 5-fold Stratified Cross-Validation loop. For each inner fold, the four selected base quantum models are trained on the inner training partition and predict on the inner validation partition. After all 5 inner folds, every sample in the outer training set has received a prediction from a model that never saw it during training. These unbiased predictions become the meta-features.

The four models were selected to maximize diversity along two independent axes: encoding geometry (three distinct Hilbert-space topologies) and classifier type (margin-based SVM vs. distance-based KNN). With three encodings and two classifiers, there are six possible base models. We excluded QSVM (Angle) and QKNN (ZZ Map) because 10-fold CV across all three datasets showed these two contributed the least unique information. For the Angle pair, QSVM (Angle) and QKNN (Angle) agreed on 89.7% of patients on Parkinson’s, 97.2% on Breast Cancer, and 86.5% on Diabetes, with McNemar’s test returning p> 0.26 on all three datasets, confirming no significant difference in discriminative structure. For the ZZ pair, QSVM (ZZ Map) and QKNN (ZZ Map) agreed on 93.3% of patients on Parkinson’s (p = 1.000) and 95.8% on Breast Cancer (p = 0.308); on Diabetes the two models diverged (p = 0.005), but QSVM (ZZ Map) was the stronger of the pair (0.759 vs 0.720 Accuracy), so it was retained. The final set, QSVM (Amplitude), QKNN (Amplitude), QSVM (ZZ Map), and QKNN (Angle), covers all three encoding geometries with at least one representative and both classifier families.

An important design consideration is that some retained models perform poorly on specific datasets. QKNN (Amplitude), for example, collapsed to 0.711 Accuracy on Diabetes, and the meta-learner assigned it a negative coefficient (-0.353), actively inverting its predictions. On Parkinson’s, however, the same model achieved the best individual Specificity (0.813) with the highest meta-learner coefficient (1.761). Excluding QKNN (Amplitude) based on its Diabetes performance would sacrifice the Parkinson’s Specificity gain that is the paper’s most clinically significant result. The meta-learner resolves this tension automatically: it amplifies each model where it is reliable and suppresses it where it is not, without requiring the practitioner to know in advance which encoding will work on a given dataset. This adaptive suppression mechanism is the core reason for including all four models rather than cherry-picking only the ones that perform well across all datasets. The ablation study in Section “Ablation study” validates this design: no single model matches the full ensemble’s balanced performance across all three datasets simultaneously, and removing any model degrades at least one dataset.

Stage 4: Adaptive classical meta-learning

The meta-feature matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_{\text {train}} \times 4}$$\end{document} is consumed by a Logistic Regression classifier with balanced class weights:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P(y = 1 \mid \textbf{m}) = \sigma \left( \sum _{k=1}^{4} w_k \cdot p_k + b\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where σ is the sigmoid function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_k$$\end{document} is the learned coefficient for the k-th base model, and b is the bias term. The class_weight=’balanced’ parameter adjusts the loss function to up-weight the minority class, addressing the class imbalance present in all three datasets (e.g., 48 healthy vs. 147 PD patients in the Parkinson’s data).

Logistic Regression was chosen as the meta-learner for three reasons. First, its learned coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_k$$\end{document} are directly interpretable as the framework’s trust in each quantum feature-map geometry: a large positive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_k$$\end{document} means the corresponding Hilbert-space embedding is amplified; a negative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_k$$\end{document} means it is actively suppressed. This interpretability is exploited in the SHAP analysis (Section "Interpreting the meta-learner: XAI triangulation"). Second, it has no hyperparameters that require tuning, avoiding a third level of nested validation. Third, it is fast enough to train inside the inner CV loop without adding meaningful computational overhead to the quantum Gram matrix computation. Non-linear meta-learners like Random Forest or gradient boosting were intentionally not included in this setup. The matrix of meta-features has only four binary columns (base model predictions), which are too low-dimensional and discrete for tree-based or neural network-based meta-learners to effectively exploit non-linear relationships in the data. In this setup, ’Logistic Regression’ is far from being a weak learner, but rather an appropriate one, as it learns to linearly combine four binary votes with certain weights, which is exactly what we expect in this input space. Using a more complex learner would risk overfitting to this discrete four-dimensional binary input space, while giving up coefficient interpretability, which we leverage for SHAP and eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) in Section "Interpreting the meta-learner: XAI triangulation".

The final prediction for a test sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}_{\text {test}}$$\end{document} is produced by passing the four base models’ direct predictions on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}_{\text {test}}$$\end{document} (trained on the full outer training fold) through the meta-learner.

VQC baseline

To assess whether purely parameterized quantum circuits can match the hybrid approach, we train a VQC as a standalone baseline. The VQC consists of an Angle Encoding layer followed by 2 layers of StronglyEntanglingLayers30, producing a circuit with 48 trainable parameters (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2 \times 8 \times 3$$\end{document}). The output is the expectation value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle Z_0 \rangle$$\end{document} of the Pauli-Z operator on the first qubit, thresholded at zero to produce a binary classification. Labels are mapped from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{0, 1\}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{-1, +1\}$$\end{document} accordingly.

Training uses the Nesterov Momentum optimizer with a step size of 0.1, mini-batch size of 16, and 30 epochs. The cost function is the mean squared error between predicted expectation values and target labels:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}(\boldsymbol{\theta }) = \frac{1}{|B|} \sum _{(\textbf{x}, y) \in B} \left( \langle Z_0 \rangle _{\boldsymbol{\theta }, \textbf{x}} - y \right) ^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Mean squared error was chosen over cross-entropy because the VQC’s output is a continuous expectation value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle Z_0 \rangle \in [-1, +1]$$\end{document}, not a probability. Cross-entropy requires outputs in (0, 1), which would require appending a sigmoid or softmax layer after the quantum measurement. This would introduce a classical nonlinearity between the quantum circuit and the loss function, obscuring whether any learning signal originates from the quantum parameters or from the classical post-processing. The MSE formulation keeps the cost function entirely in terms of the raw quantum observable, following standard practice in variational quantum circuit literature26,30. Labels are mapped from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{0, 1\}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{-1, +1\}$$\end{document} to match the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle Z_0 \rangle$$\end{document}, and the decision threshold is placed at zero.

An additional consideration is that the Pauli-Z expectation value has a natural bipolar interpretation: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle Z_0 \rangle = +1$$\end{document} corresponds to measuring |0〉 with certainty (negative class), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle Z_0 \rangle = -1$$\end{document} corresponds to |1〉 with certainty (positive class). The MSE loss directly penalizes deviation from these target states in the native measurement basis.

A cross-entropy formulation would require mapping \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle Z_0 \rangle$$\end{document} through a sigmoid, which compresses the gradients near the extremes of [-1, +1] and could worsen the vanishing-gradient problem that already afflicts deep parameterized circuits (Section "The Barren Plateau confirmation"). To empirically validate this choice, we trained the same VQC architecture using binary cross-entropy as an alternative loss. The expectation value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle Z_0 \rangle$$\end{document} was mapped to a probability via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = (1 + \langle Z_0 \rangle )/2$$\end{document}, clipped to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[10^{-7}, 1 - 10^{-7}]$$\end{document} for numerical stability, and binary cross-entropy was computed against \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{0, 1\}$$\end{document} labels. All other training parameters (Nesterov Momentum, step size 0.1, batch size 16, 30 epochs, seed 42) were kept identical, and the same 80/20 stratified split was used. Across all three datasets, cross-entropy training produced lower final test accuracy than MSE training by 1.8 to 5.8 percentage points, and both losses produced accuracies at or below the majority-class baseline on every dataset. This confirms that the choice of loss does not rescue the VQC from the barren plateau and that MSE is at least as suitable as cross-entropy for this measurement-based output regime.

The VQC is evaluated using an 80/20 train-test split stratified by class. We report this model separately because its training procedure (gradient-based parameter optimization) is fundamentally different from the kernel-based models and cannot be meaningfully integrated into the precomputed Gram matrix framework.

All kernel-based models and both ensembles are evaluated using 10-fold Stratified Cross-Validation with a fixed random seed (42) for reproducibility. Parkinson’s disease: the specificity breakthrough

The most striking result on the Parkinson’s dataset is the Specificity gain attributable to adaptive quantum kernel selection. The Classical RBF baseline achieved a Specificity of only 0.585 [0.438, 0.708], meaning it misclassified 41.5% of healthy individuals as having Parkinson’s disease. The OOF Stacking Ensemble raised this to 0.813 [0.708, 0.917], a 22.8 percentage point improvement that is statistically significant (p < 0.001). In a screening context where false positives lead to unnecessary neurological workups, this reduction in false positive rate has direct clinical utility.

The Stacking Ensemble achieved this Specificity gain while maintaining comparable Accuracy at 0.917 [0.877, 0.954] (p = 0.312, not significant), confirming that the Specificity improvement did not come at the cost of overall classification performance. The ensemble’s Precision of 0.940 [0.907, 0.973] was a statistically significant improvement over the baseline (p < 0.001). The trade-off was a slight Recall reduction from 0.993 to 0.952, which remains clinically acceptable given that the ensemble still identifies over 95% of true Parkinson’s patients.

The meta-learner converged to performance statistically equivalent to the best individual quantum model, QKNN (Amplitude), which achieved an identical Specificity of 0.813 and Precision of 0.940. This convergence is expected: the SHAP analysis in Section "Interpreting the meta-learner: XAI triangulation" shows that the meta-learner assigned QKNN (Amplitude) the highest coefficient (1.76), effectively learning to trust this model’s topological representation of vocal biomarker data.

Breast cancer: the parity benchmark

On the Breast Cancer dataset, the Classical RBF baseline achieved 0.970 [0.954, 0.984] Accuracy and 0.948 [0.915, 0.976] Specificity. This dataset is well-separated in classical feature space, so it functions as a parity benchmark: the question is whether the quantum framework degrades performance where classical methods are already strong.

The OOF Stacking Ensemble achieved 0.964 [0.947, 0.977] Accuracy and 0.943 [0.910, 0.972] Specificity, with no statistically significant difference from the Classical RBF baseline on any metric (all p> 0.05). This result is important because it demonstrates that the quantum ensemble framework does not degrade performance on datasets where classical methods are already strong. Some quantum models did show statistically significant degradation: QKNN (ZZ Map) dropped to 0.949 Accuracy (p = 0.008) and QKNN (Amplitude) to 0.952 (p = 0.038), confirming that individual quantum kernels can underperform on linearly separable data. The ensemble’s meta-learner compensated for these weaker components by up-weighting the stronger ones.

The VQC failed catastrophically on this dataset, achieving 0.412 [0.383, 0.443] Accuracy, which is below random guessing for a binary classifier with 62.7% class prevalence. This result is consistent with the Barren Plateau phenomenon discussed in Section "The Barren Plateau confirmation".

Diabetes: the noise stress-test

The Diabetes dataset, with its weak class separability and high feature overlap, was the most demanding of the three evaluations. No model achieved Accuracy above 0.77, and the Classical RBF baseline reached only 0.758 [0.730, 0.786].

The OOF Stacking Ensemble achieved the highest Accuracy at 0.766 [0.737, 0.796] and, more importantly, the highest Recall at 0.621 [0.560, 0.675], a statistically significant improvement over the baseline’s 0.553 (p = 0.004). In practical terms, the ensemble detected 62.1% of diabetic cases compared to 55.3% under the classical baseline. Given the 34.9% diabetes prevalence in this dataset, that 6.8 percentage point gain translates to roughly 24 additional diabetic patients correctly identified per 1,000 individuals screened.

This Recall gain came at a modest cost: the ensemble’s Specificity was 0.844 [0.812, 0.876], slightly below the baseline’s 0.867. The trade-off reflects the balanced class weighting in the Logistic Regression meta-learner, which shifts decision capacity toward the minority class at the expense of the majority. Notably, QKNN (Amplitude) collapsed on this dataset, dropping to 0.711 Accuracy (p < 0.001 vs. baseline) and 0.601 Precision. The SHAP analysis (Section "Interpreting the meta-learner: XAI triangulation") confirmed that the meta-learner responded by assigning this model a negative coefficient (-0.353), effectively inverting its influence. This is the stacking ensemble’s core safety mechanism: when a base model fails, the meta-learner learns to suppress it rather than let it degrade the final prediction. This negative coefficient was learned from the OOF training data, not imposed by design. The meta-learner had no prior knowledge that QKNN (Amplitude) would underperform on Diabetes; it inferred the inverted relationship from disagreement patterns between this base model and the true labels on the inner cross-validation folds. Three findings emerge from the ablation study. First, no single base model is universally dispensable. On Parkinson’s, removing QKNN (Amplitude) caused the only meaningful degradation (Δ Specificity = -0.020). On Breast Cancer, removing QSVM (ZZ Map) caused the largest drop (Δ Specificity = -0.024). On Diabetes, all four removals degraded performance, with QSVM (Amplitude) and QSVM (ZZ Map) showing the largest drops. The pattern of which model matters most shifts with the dataset, confirming the dataset-dependent kernel importance identified by the SHAP analysis.

Second, no single base model matches the full ensemble across all datasets simultaneously. The closest candidate, QKNN (Amplitude), achieved 0.923 Accuracy on Parkinson’s (slightly exceeding the ensemble’s 0.918) but dropped to 0.711 on Diabetes (well below the ensemble’s 0.766). Selecting this model based on Parkinson’s performance would be clinically dangerous on a new dataset with different noise characteristics.

Third, the “Only” configurations on Breast Cancer show that every individual model achieves lower Specificity than the full ensemble (0.882–0.938 vs. 0.943). The ensemble’s ability to combine multiple topological perspectives produces a more balanced decision boundary than any single quantum kernel can provide on this dataset.

Critically, when comparing the full ensemble against all individual “Only” configurations, the ensemble achieves the highest Accuracy (0.965) and the highest Specificity (0.943) on Breast Cancer, surpassing every single-model configuration. On Diabetes, the ensemble achieves the highest Accuracy (0.766) and the highest Recall (0.621), detecting 4.4 percentage points more diabetic patients than the best single model’s Recall of 0.575 (Only QSVM ZZ Map). On Parkinson’s, the ensemble matches the best single model (Only QKNN Amplitude) on Specificity (0.805) and Precision (0.940). Across all three datasets, no individual quantum model consistently matches the ensemble’s balanced performance across all four metrics simultaneously. This cross-metric consistency is the ensemble’s primary clinical advantage over single-model deployment.

The ablation results reveal that individual quantum models can occasionally match or slightly exceed the ensemble on a single metric, but they do so by sacrificing clinical balance. On Diabetes, “Only QSVM (Amplitude)” achieves a higher Specificity (0.904) than the full ensemble (0.844), but detects only 50.0% of diabetic patients compared to the ensemble’s 61.9%. The arithmetic is stark: QSVM (Amplitude) gains its high Specificity by classifying most patients as negative, missing nearly half of all true diabetic cases. The ensemble sacrifices 6 percentage points of Specificity to gain 12 percentage points of Recall. For a screening tool, that is the right trade-off. A model that maintains balanced performance across both metrics is more deployable than one that optimizes a single metric while letting genuinely ill patients go undetected. The proposed ensemble trades peak single-metric performance for consistent robustness: it guarantees a competitive floor across all metrics regardless of which individual quantum encoding fails on a given dataset.

Cross-dataset summary

The McNemar’s test results (Table 12) confirm that the OOF Stacking Ensemble shows no statistically significant difference from the Classical RBF baseline on overall Accuracy for any of the three datasets (p> 0.05 in all cases). The ensemble’s value does not lie in achieving higher Accuracy than classical methods. No single quantum model, and no single classical model, demonstrated this cross-dataset reliability.

The Barren plateau confirmation

The Variational Quantum Classifier (VQC) was trained with 2 layers of StronglyEntanglingLayers on 8 qubits (48 trainable parameters), using the Nesterov Momentum optimizer with a step size of 0.1 for 30 epochs. Figure 8 shows the learning curves across all three datasets.Fig. 8VQC learning curves across the three datasets. Training and test accuracy remain near 50% (random guessing) for the full 30 epochs on all datasets, confirming that the parameterized circuit encounters a barren plateau and fails to learn any discriminative structure.

VQC learning curves across the three datasets. Training and test accuracy remain near 50% (random guessing) for the full 30 epochs on all datasets, confirming that the parameterized circuit encounters a barren plateau and fails to learn any discriminative structure.

On all three datasets, the VQC’s training accuracy plateaued near 50% within the first 5 epochs and showed no meaningful improvement over the remaining 25 epochs. The test accuracy followed the same flat trajectory, with 10-fold CV estimates of 0.520 on Parkinson’s, 0.411 on Breast Cancer, and 0.540 on Diabetes (Tables 7–9). The bootstrap analysis confirmed these results are statistically indistinguishable from random guessing (p < 0.001 vs. the Classical RBF on all datasets).

These results empirically validate the theoretical predictions of McClean et al20. regarding vanishing gradients in parameterized quantum circuits. As the number of qubits increases and the circuit depth grows, the optimization landscape becomes exponentially flat, making gradient-based learning infeasible. For clinical datasets at the scale studied here (195 to 768 samples, 8 PCA components), variational quantum circuits are not viable classifiers. The hybrid kernel approach sidesteps this failure mode entirely: the quantum computation is restricted to fidelity-based kernel evaluation, which requires no gradient-based optimization, and classification is delegated to classical SVM and KNN operating on the precomputed Gram matrices. This separation between quantum feature mapping and classical decision-making is what makes the framework practical under current NISQ constraints.

Clinical feature backtracking

A persistent criticism of quantum machine learning models is that they operate in abstract Hilbert spaces, producing predictions that are mathematically valid but clinically opaque. To address this, we developed a feature backtracking procedure that projects the meta-learner’s kernel-level importance backward through the PCA transformation to recover raw clinical feature importance scores.

The backtracking proceeds as follows. The meta-learner’s absolute coefficients are combined with the PCA explained variance ratios to compute a component-level importance score for each of the 8 PCA components. This score is then projected through the PCA loading matrix to yield an importance weight for each original clinical feature. The weights are normalized to sum to 100%. The backtracking results align with established clinical knowledge in all three domains.

For Parkinson’s disease, the top features are vocal biomarkers: spread1 (a nonlinear measure of fundamental frequency variation), Jitter:DDP (cycle-to-cycle variation in pitch period), and MDVP:RAP (relative average perturbation). These features quantify the vocal tremor and instability that are hallmark symptoms of Parkinson’s dysphonia37. The quantum ensemble correctly identified these biomarkers as the most discriminative features without any domain-specific feature engineering.

The most significant features for the Breast Cancer dataset are the geometry measurements of the tumor, which include area error, worst compactness, and radius error. These features are related to the irregular shape and size variation of the cell nuclei in the images from the fine needle aspirate data set. From the clinical perspective, malignant tumors tend to have larger cell nuclei with greater irregularities and more variation in the measurements38. The emphasis on error-based features over the average values by the ensemble suggests that the quantum kernel is learning to identify the level of uncertainty in the diagnosis.

In the case of Diabetes, the importance of Insulin (14.31%) and Glucose (13.59%) is high. These are the two best established biomarkers in the diagnosis of diabetes. The fact that the quantum ensemble was able to recover this well-known relationship between the two biomarkers independently of each other is domain validation that the quantum feature maps are indeed learning meaningful patterns and not just spurious patterns. The relatively uniform distribution of importance over the 8 features of Diabetes, ranging from 11.1% to 14.3%, reflects the multifactorial nature of the disease, where no single biomarker is enough for diagnosis.

The noise simulation reveals three findings.

First, individual quantum models can collapse catastrophically under noise. QSVM (ZZ Map) lost 0.217 Accuracy and 0.660 Specificity on Breast Cancer at 10% noise, dropping to 0.633 Accuracy (from 0.850 clean) and 0.000 Specificity. This means the noisy ZZ kernel classified every patient as positive, completely losing its ability to identify healthy individuals.

Second, the OOF Stacking Ensemble showed the most resilient behavior on Parkinson’s and Breast Cancer. On Parkinson’s, its Accuracy dropped by only 0.033 (from 0.867 to 0.833), compared to 0.133 for QSVM (ZZ Map). On Breast Cancer, the OOF Stacking Ensemble’s Accuracy marginally improved from 0.917 (clean) to 0.933 under 10% noise, a counter-intuitive result that parallels the QKNN (Amplitude) regularization effect discussed below. When depolarizing noise corrupts the more fragile kernels (notably QSVM ZZ Map, which collapsed to 0.633), the meta-learner’s pre-learned trust weights effectively filter out the degraded component, allowing the more noise-robust base models to dominate the ensemble prediction.

An unexpected observation is that QKNN (Amplitude) showed negligible or slightly negative degradation (i.e., marginal improvement) under noise on Breast Cancer and Diabetes. This counter-intuitive result can be explained by the regularizing effect of depolarizing noise on distance-based classifiers. Depolarizing noise contracts all quantum kernel values toward 0.5, which compresses the fidelity-derived distance matrix. For KNN, this compression smooths out extreme distance values caused by outlier patients, effectively acting as an implicit regularizer. A similar phenomenon has been observed in classical settings where adding small amounts of noise to distance metrics can improve KNN generalization on noisy data. This effect is specific to distance-based classifiers and does not extend to SVM-based models, which rely on margin maximization rather than local distance comparisons. More broadly, amplitude encoding’s dense compression of features into a three-qubit register creates an implicit noise buffer: depolarizing errors have fewer independent channels through which to corrupt the encoded state, compared to the eight-qubit Angle and ZZ circuits.

Third, on Diabetes, all models were unable to handle the noise, and the degradation of the Stacking Ensemble was more significant than that of the Hard Voting Ensemble. This aligns with the dataset’s weak separability: the balanced class weighting that improved Recall on clean data becomes a liability when all base-classifier predictions are corrupted by noise. Training the meta-learner on deliberately noisy quantum predictions, a form of noise-aware stacking, is a natural direction for improving NISQ robustness.

Future work

Several directions for extending this work are worth noting. The most immediate is execution on physical quantum hardware. The depolarizing-channel simulation used here captures one noise source, but real devices exhibit correlated errors, crosstalk, and measurement-dependent noise that the default.mixed simulator does not model. Running the three quantum kernels on IBM Eagle or Quantinuum H-Series processors would test whether the meta-learner’s adaptive suppression mechanism transfers to device-native noise profiles. Furthermore, although PCA reduction to 8 features retained more than 92% variance across all datasets, other avenues for adaptive dimension reduction or quantum feature selection methods that preserve disease-relevant features in lower-variance features could be explored. Third, instead of using binary confidence values from the base model predictions as meta-features, using continuous confidence values (for example, SVM decision values or KNN decision probabilities) could potentially allow for more robust adaptive weighting of the meta-learner. Fourth, using predictions from noisy quantum circuits (noise-aware stacking) could potentially improve robustness of NISQ devices, especially for more challenging datasets such as Diabetes, where all base models were affected by 10% depolarizing noise. Finally, application of this framework to larger clinical datasets with more complex class structures (for example, multi-class diagnosis or survival estimation) could help validate the generalizability of OOF stacking for tasks other than binary classification.

A further consideration when moving to practical deployment of the method is the scalability of the quantum Gram matrix computation. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(N^2)$$\end{document} kernel computations needed to evaluate the Gram matrix for N patients form a computational bottleneck. For the Parkinson’s dataset with N=195 patients, the computation of a single ZZ-kernel Gram matrix takes approximately 76.6s on a CPU simulator (Table 6). For a clinical population of 5,000 patients, the computation of a single Gram matrix would involve over 12.5 million kernel computations, corresponding to approximately 14 hours of computation per Gram matrix per encoding. This cost needs to be multiplied by the three encodings and the nested cross-validation structure. The three datasets used in this paper (195 to 768 samples, 8 to 30 raw features) are standard UCI benchmarks, commonly used in QML research to facilitate reproducibility and comparison of results. This is evidenced in the bootstrap confidence interval widths. For example, the Classical RBF on Parkinson’s has a range of [0.438, 0.708] in specificity, which is a 27 percentage point difference directly attributable to the evaluation on only 48 healthy patients over 10 folds. However, it is also important to note that the small-scale nature of the datasets is a beneficial benchmark scenario in which quantum kernel methods are applicable. Indeed, because the calculation of the Gram matrix is at a complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(N^2)$$\end{document}, these algorithms are currently only applicable in a realm where classical approaches such as deep neural networks and gradient boosting cannot exploit the benefits of massive data availability. However, the performance of the quantum kernel methods is specific to the small data, high complexity scenario of clinical diagnostics and is not a general statement of superiority over classical machine learning approaches. Extending this framework to high-dimensional data such as genomic expression profiles (20,000+ features) or medical imaging would require either quantum hardware with significantly faster circuit execution, approximate kernel methods such as random Fourier features adapted for quantum kernels, or hybrid strategies that apply quantum kernels to learned low-dimensional representations from classical autoencoders. Investigating these scalability pathways is a priority for future work.

A valid concern when comparing a four-model quantum ensemble against a single classical model is that some of the performance gain may come from the stacking architecture rather than from the quantum feature maps. To address this, we evaluated XGBoost, Random Forest, and a classical SVM+KNN stacking ensemble on the same PCA features under the same OOF protocol (Section "Comparison with classical ensemble baselines"). The quantum ensemble matched or outperformed all three classical baselines on Parkinson’s and Diabetes, while achieving parity on Breast Cancer. The classical stacking ensemble, which uses the same meta-learner and inner CV structure but operates in fixed-dimensional feature space, scored lower than the quantum version on both overlapping-biomarker datasets. These results indicate that the Hilbert-space embeddings contribute discriminative structure beyond what the ensemble architecture provides on its own. That said, the paper’s central claim is not quantum superiority over classical methods. The contribution is a reusable methodology for combining heterogeneous quantum kernels with leakage-free meta-learning, and the classical comparison exists to ground the quantum results against a fair reference point.

Conclusion

This paper presented an adaptive hybrid quantum-classical framework for clinical diagnostics that learns which quantum feature-map geometry generalizes on a given dataset. The framework passes patient data through three quantum encodings, namely Angle, Amplitude, and ZZ-Feature Map, trains QSVM and QKNN classifiers on the resulting Gram matrices, and feeds their predictions into a Logistic Regression meta-learner under strict nested cross-validation to prevent data leakage.

The results across three clinical datasets tell a consistent story. On Parkinson’s, the ensemble raised Specificity from 0.585 to 0.813 at p < 0.001, reducing the false positive rate by 22.8 percentage points while keeping Recall above 0.95. On Breast Cancer, it matched the classical RBF-SVM on every metric with all p> 0.05, absorbing weaker quantum models without degradation. On Diabetes, it improved Recall from 0.553 to 0.621 at p = 0.004 by suppressing an unreliable base model that the meta-learner assigned a negative coefficient. A standalone VQC flatlined near 50% accuracy on all three datasets, confirming that purely parameterized quantum circuits are not viable for clinical classification at current scales.

Two findings carry implications for the broader quantum machine learning community. First, no single quantum feature map produced universally optimal classification boundaries. QKNN Amplitude dominated on Parkinson’s but collapsed on Diabetes. QSVM ZZ Map was critical for Breast Cancer but degraded fastest under noise. The meta-learner handled this automatically, shifting its trust per dataset without any manual tuning. This suggests that future QML studies should avoid reporting results from a single encoding in isolation, since the choice of quantum feature map interacts with the dataset’s noise profile in ways that cannot be predicted from circuit structure alone. Second, feature backtracking through the PCA loadings recovered clinically established biomarkers, including Insulin and Glucose for Diabetes, vocal jitter and spread measures for Parkinson’s, and nucleus geometry features for Breast Cancer, confirming that the quantum ensemble learns biologically meaningful patterns rather than statistical artifacts. This backtracking step addresses a persistent criticism of quantum models in medicine: that predictions emerge from abstract Hilbert spaces with no connection to clinical reasoning.

From a methodological standpoint, this work demonstrates that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(N^2)$$\end{document} cost of quantum Gram matrices, often cited as a barrier to rigorous evaluation, can be managed through one-time precomputation. This separation of kernel computation from model training enabled the nested cross-validation and 1,000-iteration bootstrapping that would otherwise be computationally prohibitive. The same principle applies to any future quantum kernel study: precompute the Gram matrices once, then run arbitrarily complex classical evaluation on top.

The framework does not claim quantum superiority over classical methods. It is most useful on datasets with overlapping biomarker distributions where classical fixed-dimensional kernels struggle to balance Recall and Specificity (Parkinson’s, Diabetes); on linearly separable data where classical methods already reach near-ceiling performance (Breast Cancer), the quantum ensemble offers parity rather than improvement. Practitioners deploying this framework should expect the largest gains in noisy, weakly separable clinical domains and should benchmark against strong classical ensembles such as XGBoost on every new dataset. Its contribution is a reusable methodology that combines heterogeneous quantum kernels with leakage-free meta-learning, multi-method explainability, and noise robustness evaluation. As quantum hardware matures beyond the NISQ era, the architectural decisions validated here, particularly the adaptive kernel selection and the OOF stacking protocol, can transfer directly to real devices. For a practitioner facing a new clinical dataset, the framework removes the need to pre-specify a quantum encoding strategy. The meta-learner’s coefficients reveal, after training, which region of Hilbert space contained the discriminative structure and which did not.
