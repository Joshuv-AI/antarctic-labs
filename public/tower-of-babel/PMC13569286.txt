Faecal Microbiota Transplantation Reduces Lesion Severity and Medication Use in Canine Atopic Dermatitis: A Randomised, Placebo‐Controlled, Double‐Blinded Clinical Trial
Authors: Vera Felten, Erin Asley West, Franco Martini, Claude Favrot, Stefan Unterer, Jan Suchodolski, Michael Scharl, Harald Renz, Nina Maria Fischer, Ana Rostaher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFaecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is an established therapy for gastrointestinal disease, yet its role in canine atopic dermatitis (cAD) remains unclear.Hypothesis/ObjectivesWe hypothesised that adjunctive FMT improves clinical severity and reduces symptomatic medication use in dogs with cAD. The objective was to evaluate efficacy and safety versus placebo.AnimalsForty‐six client‐owned dogs with naturally occurring cAD were enrolled from a referral hospital population; 40 completed the study (FMT n = 20, placebo n = 20).Materials and MethodsProspective, randomised, placebo‐controlled, double‐blinded clinical trial. Dogs received daily oral lyophilised FMT capsules for 90 days plus three monthly rectal FMT administrations (Day [D]0, D30, D60) or placebo capsules with sham handling. Concomitant symptomatic therapies were permitted. Outcomes included Canine Atopic Dermatitis Extent and Severity Index, fourth iteration (CADESI‐04), pruritus Visual Analog Scale (PVAS), Medication Score (D0–90) and Owner Global Assessment of Treatment Efficacy (OGATE, D90).ResultsCADESI‐04 scores were lower with FMT at month (M) 2 (7 ± 6 vs. 16 ± 12; p = 0.006) and month 3 (8 ± 6 vs. 15 ± 12; p = 0.020). Sustained responders (≥ 50% CADESI‐04 improvement at M2 and M3) were more frequent with FMT (35% vs. 5%; p = 0.044). In the FMT group, the medication scores were lower at M2 (16 ± 10 vs. 23 ± 11; p = 0.033) and M3 (13 ± 10 vs. 24 ± 15; p = 0.007) compared to placebo. PVAS decreased in both groups without between‐group differences. OGATE favoured FMT (p = 0.028). FMT was well tolerated.Conclusions and Clinical RelevanceAdjunctive FMT reduced lesion severity and medication requirements, supporting its use as a safe microbiome‐based add‐on therapy in cAD.

Introduction

Canine atopic dermatitis (cAD) is a chronic, inflammatory and pruritic skin disorder affecting approximately 10% of dogs worldwide [1, 2, 3]. The disease is characterised by persistent pruritus, skin lesions and frequent secondary infections, leading to a marked reduction in quality‐of‐life (QoL) for affected dogs and their owners [4].

Historically, cAD was primarily linked to immunoglobulin (Ig)E antibodies to environmental allergens [3]. Today it is accepted as a hereditary, typically pruritic and predominantly T‐cell‐driven inflammatory skin disease involving skin barrier abnormalities, allergen sensitisation and microbial dysbiosis [5]. Predominantly T helper 2 (Th2)‐skewed immune response contributes to allergic inflammation and IgE‐mediated hypersensitivity, while impairment of the epidermal barrier facilitates allergen penetration and transepidermal water loss (TEWL) [6]. Secondary bacterial and yeast infections commonly exacerbate disease severity [7].

Current treatment strategies for cAD include anti‐inflammatory and immunomodulatory treatments [8, 9], which are widely used. However, they are only symptomatic and may be associated with adverse effects and variable long‐term efficacy [10]. Allergen immunotherapy (AIT) is currently the only treatment option that directly addresses the underlying causes of allergic reactions. However, its effectiveness is limited to a subset of patients, and clinical improvement is often delayed [11, 12, 13]. Therefore, adjunctive strategies targeting disease‐driving mechanisms are warranted.

Growing evidence highlights the significant role of the gut microbiome in the development and progression of immune‐mediated and allergic diseases, including AD [14]. Comparative studies in humans have demonstrated alterations in gut microbiome composition in patients with atopic dermatitis (AD), particularly during early life [15, 16]. Data in dogs are more limited, although reduced microbial diversity and disease‐associated shifts have been reported in allergic dogs compared with healthy controls [17, 18, 19, 20, 21].

A functional gut–skin axis has been proposed in both humans and dogs, linking intestinal microbial communities to skin health through immunological and metabolic pathways [22]. Short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), the essential amino acid tryptophan and microbial antigenic products (pattern‐associated molecular patterns) have been identified as primary components of the gut–skin axis [23]. These findings suggest that modulation of the intestinal microbiome may represent a therapeutic strategy for inflammatory skin diseases [22, 24].

Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), defined as the transfer of faecal material from healthy donors to restore a balanced intestinal microbiota, is an established therapy for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection in humans and is increasingly investigated in several metabolic and immune‐mediated diseases, including skin disorders [25, 26]. Although only a limited number of studies have evaluated FMT in patients with AD, available data suggest potential improvements in clinical signs and immune parameters [27, 28, 29]. In veterinary medicine, FMT has shown clinical benefit in dogs with acute and chronic gastrointestinal disease [30, 31] while evidence in cAD is currently limited to small, uncontrolled studies [20, 31].

The objective of this study was to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of FMT as adjunctive therapy in dogs with cAD in a randomised, placebo‐controlled, double‐blinded trial.

Materials and Methods

This study protocol was approved by the Veterinary Office of Zurich (National No. 35805, Cantonal No. ZH 069/23). All owners signed a written informed consent form before participating in the study.

Study Design

This prospective, randomised, placebo‐controlled, double‐blinded clinical trial evaluated client‐owned dogs with cAD presented to Veterinary Office Canton of Zurich. Dogs were enrolled starting in October 2023, and the study was completed in November 2025, including the follow‐up period. The sample size for this study was calculated based on prior AIT research. We anticipated a 60% improvement in clinical scores for allergic dogs and a 25% improvement for those receiving placebo treatment. The significance level was set at p = 0.05 for a two‐group comparison, and a statistical power of 90% was targeted. Based on these assumptions, a total of 40 dogs (20 per group) was required. To account for potential dropouts or incomplete follow‐up, enrolment of ≤ 50 dogs was planned to ensure that ≥ 40 dogs completed the entire study protocol.

Inclusion Criteria

Client‐owned dogs of any breed, sex, weight and > 12 months of age were eligible for enrolment. The AD diagnosis was established by a board‐certified dermatologist based on the history, clinical signs and exclusion of differential diagnoses and fulfilment of Veterinary Office Canton of Zurich AD clinical criteria [32]. Dogs were required to have non‐seasonal clinical signs for ≥ 12 months before enrolment, a pruritus (PVAS) score [33] ≥ 3 of 10 within the month before inclusion, being sensitised to at least one house dust allergen, as demonstrated by either intradermal and/or serological allergen testing, and having a negative faecal flotation test within 1 month before enrolment.

Continuous ectoparasite control and a stable diet were required throughout the study.

Concomitant Medications

Topical preparations for routine management of cAD were provided if their use remained unchanged throughout the study. Systemic symptomatic therapies, including oclacitinib, prednisolone, lokivetmab and ASIT, were permitted to ensure recruitment feasibility and to reflect real‐world clinical practice. To reduce potential confounding effects, medication use was standardised as far as possible. Owners performed a structured medication withdrawal for ≥ 3 days before each scheduled visit; treatment was resumed immediately if clinical signs worsened. Dogs receiving lokivetmab were enrolled upon recurrence of clinical signs following their previous injection, and administration was intentionally withheld at Month (M)3 to assess drug‐sparing effects. All concomitant treatments were systematically recorded and quantified using a standardised medication score.

Enrolment, Randomisation and Masking

Dogs that met the study inclusion criteria were randomised in a 1 : 1 ratio to receive either FMT or placebo treatment, using prism (Graphpad; https://www.graphpad.com/quickcalcs/randomize1/).

Owners and clinicians responsible for data collection were blinded to treatment allocation.

Stool Donors

Faecal material was obtained from five clinically healthy privately owned donor dogs with no history of disease or antibiotic treatment, a normal body condition and normal Purina Faecal Score (2 of 7). They were fed various commercial diets during the study period.

Donor faeces were screened using suggested guidelines [34]. Donors should have had a normal microbiome as indicated by the quantitative (q)PCR‐based dysbiosis index < 0 [35, 36]. Donor faeces were further screened for parasites, potentially pathogenic bacteria (Salmonella spp., Campylobacter jejuni , Clostridioides difficile, Yersinia, NetF‐encoding Clostridium perfringens ) and carbapenem‐resistant Enterobacteriales. Samples that tested positive were not used for treatment.

Stools were collected and stored at −80°C before lyophilisation.

Faecal Microbiota Transplantation Preparation and Administration

FMT Lyophilisate Preparation

For each FMT batch, material from one to three donors was combined. Frozen faecal samples were lyophilised for 48–72 h at −50°C under vacuum. The resulting powder was sieved, then aliquoted into small plastic containers together with a moisture absorber (Propasil micro; Propagroup) and stored at −80°C until further use.

FMT Capsules

The lyophilised FMT material was encapsulated into cellulose capsules (vegan, size 0, delayed absorption; Kapselwelt) using a semi‐automated capsule machine (MK 100 Pro; Biomation). Finally, each capsule contained 0.4 g of FMT powder. Placebo capsules were filled with pulverised dog food powder (Anallergenic; Royal Canin). Capsules were sealed in standing 150 mL aluminium bags (Standbodenbeutel; Elke Plastic) with a moisture absorber and subsequently refrozen at −80°C until use. Capsules were provided to the owners in a cooling bag with a cooling element. Owners were instructed to store the capsules in a household freezer and to administer them approximately 3 h after the last meal and ≥ 2 h before the next meal. The capsules were dosed based on body weight. Dogs weighing < 10 kg, 10.1–20 kg and > 20.1 kg received one, two or three capsule(s) per day, respectively. This corresponded to an approximate dose of 1.5 g/kg body weight lyophilised material orally per month.

FMT Enema

Fifteen minutes before FMT administration, lyophilised FMT powder, derived from the same preparation used for capsule production (and not from capsule content), was reconstituted with sterile saline at a dosage of 1.5 g/kg body weight to achieve an applesauce‐like consistency. This dosage applies specifically to rectal administration (enema) and differs from the lower, fixed‐dose regimen used for oral capsule administration [32, 33]. A dosage of 1.5 g/kg of lyophilised faeces corresponds to 5–6 g of fresh faeces per kilogram and is the current dosing recommendation for dogs with chronic idiopathic enteropathy. To ensure an optimal FMT retention, food was withheld, and physical activity, including walking, was avoided both for ≥ 4 after administration. Rectal administrations were performed without sedation or analgesia, as the procedure is generally well tolerated in dogs and considered minimally invasive. Dogs were gently restrained, and all efforts were made to minimise stress and discomfort during administration. The placebo group did not receive enema. Placebo dogs underwent identical handling procedures to maintain blinding.

Study Schedule and Clinical Assessments

Dogs were clinically evaluated on Day (D)0, D30, D60 and D90 using Canine Atopic Dermatitis Extent and Severity Index, fourth iteration (CADESI‐04), [37] pruritus Visual Analog Scale (PVAS) [38] and a modified Medication Score [39]. The Medication Score also included lokivetmab, with a dosage of 1–2 mg/kg contributing 30 points and dosages > 2 mg/kg contributing 60 points. Owner Global Assessment of Treatment Efficacy (OGATE) was assessed on D90, and the adverse events were recorded throughout the study. FMT enemas were administered on D0, D30 and D60; capsules were given daily from D0 to D90.

Following completion of the treatment phase, dogs in the FMT group that demonstrated clinical improvement were monitored until the occurrence of an AD flare or for ≤ 1 year after enrolment. An AD flare was defined as a worsening of the PVAS to baseline values or worse, together with the need to re‐institute or increase symptomatic treatment. Time‐to‐relapse was recorded descriptively in months. For this, the owners maintained a daily diary in which they recorded PVAS, faecal scores and administered medications over the treatment period and during the follow‐up. This follow‐up was exploratory and descriptive; no formal statistical comparison between groups was performed.

No predefined quantitative threshold for protocol deviation was applied; decisions regarding continued participation were based on investigator assessment.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using prism (v10.6.0; Graph Pad). A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were summarised as mean and standard deviation (SD), categorical variables as n (%). Baseline variables were compared between the FMT and placebo group using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous data. Because the primary objective was to evaluate between‐group differences at pre‐defined time points, formal within‐group paired analyses and repeated‐measures models were not conducted; within‐group changes over time are presented descriptively.

Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test, while continuous outcomes were analysed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test.

Co‐primary outcome measures were changes from baseline in CADESI‐04, PVAS and Medication Score. Clinical outcomes at each study time point (D0, D30, D60 and D90) were summarised as mean (standard deviation [SD]). Between‐group differences at individual time points were assessed using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests. Efficacy analyses were performed on dogs that completed the study (n = 40), while all randomised dogs were included in safety assessments.

Sustained response (≥ 50% CADESI‐04 reduction at M2 and M3) and OGATE were analysed as secondary outcomes using Fisher's exact test.

The secondary outcome measures were the percentages of dogs having had a flare of AD within 9 months after stopping the treatment and the time to relapse.

Results

Study Population

A total of 46 client‐owned dogs with naturally occurring cAD were enrolled and randomised to receive FMT or placebo. During the study period, six dogs were withdrawn, resulting in a final analysis population of 40 dogs (FMT: n = 20; placebo: n = 20).

Of the six withdrawn dogs, five were allocated to the FMT group and one to the placebo group. Reasons for withdrawal in the FMT group included diarrhoea (n = 2), enucleation owing to an unrelated ophthalmic condition (n = 1), infestation with Echinococcus granulosus (n = 1) and worsening of the pruritus (n = 1), and in the placebo group gastritis in one dog (n = 1).

The final study population consisted of 21 male (53%) and 19 female (48%) dogs, of which 30 dogs (75%) were neutered. The mean age at study inclusion was 5.7 ± 3.4 years, with a mean age of disease onset of 1.9 ± 2.3 years. The mean body weight was 19 ± 10 kg. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced between treatment groups, with no statistically significant differences observed for age (FMT: 6.1 ± 3.3 years; placebo: 5.4 ± 3.5 years; p = 0.4), sex distribution (p = 0.3), neuter status (p = 0.14), body weight (FMT: 21 ± 9 kg; placebo: 18 ± 10 kg; p = 0.3) or age at onset of AD (p = 0.8) (Table S1).

Clinical Disease Severity

Baseline disease severity assessed by CADESI‐04 did not differ significantly between groups (FMT: 17 ± 11; placebo: 19 ± 17; p = 0.9). CADESI‐04 scores were lower in the FMT group at M2 and M3 compared to placebo. Although no statistically significant difference between groups was observed at M1 (FMT: 10 ± 10; placebo: 16 ± 14; p = 0.14), CADESI‐04 scores were significantly lower in the FMT group at M2 (FMT: 7 ± 6; placebo: 16 ± 12; p = 0.006) and remained significantly reduced at M3 (FMT: 8 ± 6; placebo: 15 ± 12; p = 0.020), indicating a sustained improvement (Figure 2a).

Significant differences in CADESI‐04 and medication score were observed at Month (M)2 and M3 between the FMT and placebo group. Significant difference: *p < 0.05.

Pruritus Severity

Baseline pruritus severity assessed by PVAS without medication was comparable between groups (FMT: 6.27 ± 2.42; placebo: 7.23 ± 1.77; p = 0.2). PVAS scores were numerically lower in the FMT group compared to placebo at all post‐baseline time points, although differences were not statistically significant. At M1, PVAS scores were numerically lower in the FMT group compared with placebo (FMT: 4.71 ± 2.50; placebo: 6.15 ± 2.80; p = 0.083). Similar patterns were observed at M2 (FMT: 3.86 ± 2.23; placebo: 5.52 ± 3.16; p = 0.11) and M3 (FMT: 4.23 ± 2.54; placebo: 5.74 ± 2.89; p = 0.078); however, none of these differences reached statistical significance (Figure 2b).

Medication Score

Baseline medication scores did not differ significantly between groups (FMT: 18 ± 16; placebo: 17 ± 16; p > 0.9). The use of concomitant antipruritic or anti‐inflammatory treatments, including oclacitinib, glucocorticoids and ASIT, was comparable between groups at study entry. Medication scores remained similar at M1 (FMT: 21 ± 16; placebo: 24 ± 16; p = 0.4). By contrast, dogs in the FMT group required significantly less medication at M2 (FMT: 16 ± 10; placebo: 23 ± 11; p = 0.033) and M3 (FMT: 13 ± 10); placebo: 24 ± 15; p = 0.007 indicating reduced medication dependency (Figure 2c).

Sustained Responder Analysis

A sustained responder was defined as a dog achieving a ≥ 50% improvement from baseline at both M2 and M3. Using this definition, a significantly higher proportion of dogs in the FMT group achieved sustained improvement in CADESI‐04 scores compared with placebo (35% vs. 5%; p = 0.044, Fisher's exact test). No significant between‐group differences were observed for sustained responders based on PVAS (25% vs. 25%; p > 0.9) or medication score (5.6% vs. 0%; p = 0.5) (Table 1).

Owner Global Assessment Score

Owner global assessment of treatment efficacy at M3 differed significantly between groups (p = 0.028). Dogs in the FMT group were more frequently rated as having a fair or good clinical response, whereas non‐response was more commonly reported in the placebo group (Figure 3).

Safety and Adverse Events

It was found that FMT was generally well tolerated throughout the study period. Transient mildly softened faeces occurred in three dogs and resolved spontaneously. Two dogs developed self‐limited diarrhoea after enema administration. In one of these dogs, this event coincided with a reported deviation from recommended capsule storage conditions at home. These dogs continued with FMT treatment and did not show any adverse effects thereafter. Only one dog was withdrawn from the study as a consequence of gastrointestinal adverse effects. This dog developed severe acute vomiting and diarrhoea following 3 days after the first enema and after FMT capsule intake. At study entry, this dog also had a positive faecal test for Giardia spp. and received concomitant fenbendazole treatment. The clinical signs were resolved under symptomatic treatment by the primary care veterinary surgeon.

In the placebo group, one dog developed mucoid faeces shortly after initiation of capsule administration, which resolved after discontinuation of the capsules. After the capsule reintroduction, the clinical signs did not reappear.

Long‐Term Follow‐Up

Of the 20 dogs in the FMT group, 16 entered the long‐term follow‐up, while four did not owing to owner‐reported lack of treatment response. An AD relapse occurred in nine dogs before the 6‐month follow‐up and in four dogs before the 12‐month follow‐up. Two dogs were lost to follow‐up after M6; one dog remained clinically stable at M12. The median duration of treatment effect among responder dogs was 5.0 ± 3.8 months.

Discussion

In this double‐blinded, placebo‐controlled clinical trial, FMT was safe, well tolerated and associated with significant clinical benefits in dogs with naturally occurring AD. Dogs treated with FMT demonstrated reductions in lesion severity (CADESI‐04), lower medication requirements and improved owner‐reported outcomes. By contrast, dogs receiving placebo exhibited less improvement and stable or increased medication use.

Significant reductions in CADESI‐04 scores were observed at M2 and M3, while placebo‐treated dogs showed limited or inconsistent improvement. These objective findings were supported by owner‐reported outcomes (OGATE). A markedly higher proportion of owners reported a fair or good response in the FMT group compared with placebo.

In contrast to the CADESI‐04 scores, the pruritus severity decreased significantly over time in both treatment groups, yet between‐group differences did not reach statistical significance. A similar dissociation between improvements in skin lesions and pruritus has been found in human studies evaluating FMT for AD [27].

The improvements observed in the placebo group for pruritus and some owner‐reported measures are likely to reflect the impact of regular monthly follow‐up visits, increased attention to symptomatic care, and optimised medication use, all of which are known to influence clinical outcomes in chronic dermatological conditions [40].

However, these supportive effects were present in both groups, while only FMT‐treated dogs demonstrated concurrent improvements in CADESI‐04 and owner global assessment together with a reduction in medication scores. This pattern supports a treatment‐specific effect of FMT.

The present findings are broadly consistent with emerging evidence from human trials investigating FMT for ADs. Studies in humans have shown that FMT can improve skin lesion severity, although pruritus responses have been more variable [27, 28]. This aligns with our observation that lesion severity may respond more consistently than pruritus to microbiome modulation. A similar pattern has been reported in a small non‐blinded pilot study in dogs [20] suggesting that pruritus may be less sensitive when symptomatic therapies are used concurrently.

Differences in FMT protocols are likely to contribute to variability in treatment outcomes. In contrast to our study, where we used lyophilised material, [34] human trials have used capsules containing frozen precipitate of fresh raw faeces in saline and glycerol administered every week for 3 weeks [27] or bi‐weekly for 6 weeks [27, 28]. A prior canine study has used a single dose of oral fresh FMT [20], and another study utilised commercial capsules with lyophilised FMT daily for 1 month [41]. Faecal microbiota transplantation has shown promise in treating cAD, with one study indicating a tendency for improvement in clinical signs; [23] however, the 1‐month observation period may have been insufficient to achieve statistical significance. An open‐label study reported remission in eight dogs [41] although the uncontrolled design limits interpretation. Our study did not demonstrate an excellent response, yet it aligns with data from controlled studies in humans, indicating a significant effect and supporting its value as an adjunct drug‐sparing treatment [30]. Further studies are needed to optimise FMT protocols and identify predictors of response.

This extended and multimodal approach was chosen to enable robust microbial colonisation, potentially explaining the sustained improvement observed in CADESI‐04 and the unique medication‐sparing effect identified in this study.

A key distinction between the present findings and human FMT trials lies in medication use and AD severity. In previous human trials, concomitant medications were typically maintained. A key distinction between the present study and human FMT trials lies in the severity of AD and the concomitant medication use. In our study, cases were predominantly mild, while human trials often include moderate‐to‐severe AD, which may partly explain differences in medication reduction. Further research is warranted to explore the influence of AD severity and medication management on the efficacy of FMT in canine patients.

By actively allowing and monitoring medication adjustments under controlled conditions, the present study demonstrates that FMT may support clinical improvement while reducing the need for symptomatic treatment. This view also was recently adapted for the evaluation of allergen immunotherapy in dogs [42] and also cats [43]. Medication reduction is a particularly important end‐point in chronic allergic diseases, where long‐term pharmacological management is often limited by adverse effects, costs and owner concerns.

Several dogs were withdrawn from the study during the intervention period for medical or safety‐related reasons. The majority of these events were considered unrelated to the investigational treatment and included conditions such as enucleation owing to an independent ophthalmic disease, detection of Echinococcus spp. infection and gastritis. Such withdrawals reflect the heterogeneity of comorbidities commonly encountered in clinical trials involving client‐owned dogs.

One dog in the FMT group developed mild diarrhoea temporally associated with rectal administration of FMT. Transient gastrointestinal adverse events, particularly diarrhoea, are among the most frequently reported adverse effects following FMT in both human and veterinary studies and are generally considered procedure‐related and self‐limiting [20, 27, 44]. These findings are consistent with previous reports and support the overall favourable safety profile of FMT.

Additionally, one dog in the FMT group was withdrawn from the study owing to the development of acute vomiting and diarrhoea 3 days after initiation of FMT therapy. At study entry, this dog also tested positive for Giardia spp. and received concomitant treatment with fenbendazole. Concurrent factors complicate causal interpretation.

Notably, one dog exhibited a marked worsening of pruritus following FMT and was withdrawn from the study. Although a causal relationship cannot be definitively established, a plausible explanation is an adverse reaction related to concomitant food allergy. Dogs with AD frequently present with overlapping food‐responsive disease, and exposure to dietary antigens can exacerbate pruritus even in the absence of gastrointestinal signs. As faecal material contains residual dietary proteins and antigenic components derived from the FMT donor diet, it is conceivable that donor‐associated dietary antigens triggered an allergic response in this dog. Similar considerations have been raised in human medicine, where donor diet composition is increasingly recognised as a factor influencing immune responses following FMT [45, 46] This observation highlights the importance of careful donor selection [34] and consideration of recipient food allergy status in future studies.

Faecal microbiota transfer also is associated with potential safety considerations, including the risk of transmitting infectious agents and antimicrobial‐resistant organisms [47]. In human medicine, such risks have led to the establishment of comprehensive safety guidelines emphasising rigorous donor screening [48]. In the present study, donor selection, faecal material preparation and administration procedures were conducted in accordance with current recommendations and best practices in veterinary medicine [34]. Donor dogs underwent standardised clinical evaluation and screening before faecal donation. No findings suggestive of pathogen transmission were observed. These findings support the importance of standardised safety protocols.

Long‐term durability was limited, with most dogs relapsing within 12 months, consistent with observations in human studies. Further studies should evaluate maintenance protocols and long‐term durability and also may explore preventive applications.

There are several limitations that need to be discussed. Although the sample size was larger than in previous canine FMT studies, it remains modest and may limit the detection of subtle treatment effects on pruritus. Concomitant medications such as glucocorticoids, oclacitinib and lokivetmab were allowed to reflect real‐world clinical practice, which introduces a degree of heterogeneity and may have influenced clinical outcomes—particularly in the placebo group where medication use increased. However, medication reduction occurred exclusively in the FMT group.

The present study was designed to primarily evaluate clinical efficacy and safety, and therefore did not include gut microbiome and metabolomic analyses or immunological assays. This limited mechanistic interpretation yet enabled assessment of clinically relevant outcomes. Future studies should integrate multi‐omics approaches to explore microbial colonisation, metabolic activity and immune modulation in detail.

Owner‐reported outcomes such as PVAS and QoL scores may be subject to subjective bias despite standardised instructions. However, they represent established core outcome measures. Finally, although some dogs were followed up to 12 months, the treatment duration was limited to 3 months, which is shorter than the treatment duration typically required for allergen immunotherapy [13]. Longer treatment and follow‐up periods should be evaluated.

From a clinical perspective, the observed improvement in lesion severity together with reduced medication requirements suggests that FMT may serve as a useful adjunctive therapy in the multimodal management of cAD. The favourable safety profile observed in this study supports its feasibility in clinical practice. FMT should be considered complementary to established therapies; further studies should define optimal protocols and patient selection.

Conclusions

This double‐blinded, placebo‐controlled trial demonstrated that FMT was a safe and well‐tolerated intervention that provided significant clinical benefits, including meaningful reductions in lesion severity and decreased medication requirements for dogs with AD. Further research should clarify long‐term effects, dosing strategies and modes of action.
