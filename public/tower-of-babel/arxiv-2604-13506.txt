A monotone iterative reconstruction method for an inverse drift problem in a two-dimensional parabolic equation
Liuying Zhang, Wenlong Zhang, Zhidong Zhang

Abstract
We study an inverse drift problem for a two-dimensional parabolic equation on the unit square with mixed boundary conditions, where the drift coefficient is recovered from terminal observation data g = u ⁡ ( ⋅ , T ) g=u(\cdot,T) . A monotone operator is constructed whose fixed point coincides with the unknown drift, yielding uniqueness in an admissible class and a constructive iterative reconstruction scheme. Numerical experiments illustrate the monotone convergence and the effectiveness of the proposed method, and show that it remains effective for noisy terminal data under the denoising strategy. Keywords: inverse drift problem, uniqueness, monoticity, iterative algorithm. AMS Subject Classifications: 35R30, 35K20, 65M32

Introduction.

1.1
Mathematical statement.

We consider an inverse drift problem associated with the following parabolic equation:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}-\Delta+q(x)\partial_{x_{1}}+C_{p})u(x,t)&=f(x),&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T];\\
\mathcal{B}u(x,t)&=b(x,t),&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T];\\
u(x,0)&=u_{0}(x),&&x\in\Omega.\\
\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(1.1)

The domain
\Omega
is the unit square
(0,1)\times(0,1)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
x\in\Omega
could be denoted by
(x_{1},x_{2})
.
For the boundary
\partial\Omega
, we denote

l_{1}=[0,1]\times\{0\},\quad l_{2}=\{1\}\times(0,1),\quad l_{3}=[0,1]\times\{1\},\quad l_{4}=\{0\}\times(0,1).

Then the boundary condition
\mathcal{B}u(x,t)=b(x,t)
is set as

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=b_{1}(x,t)\text{ on }l_{1},

\displaystyle u=b_{3}(x,t)\text{ on }l_{3},

(1.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}

\displaystyle=b_{2}(x,t)\text{ on }l_{2},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}=b_{4}\text{ on }l_{4},

where
b_{4}
is a constant. Moreover, the source
f
, the initial condition
u_{0}
and the potential constant
C_{p}
are given; the space dependent drift coefficient
q(x)
w.r.t
x_{1}
direction is unknown.

In the inverse problem of this work, the used measurement is the final time data of the solution, stated as

g(x):=u(x,T).

(1.3)

Hence, the inverse problem investigated in this article could be described as follows:

\text{using data (\ref{data})}\ \text{to recover the drift}\ q(x)\ \text{in equation (\ref{PDE})}.

1.2
Background and literature.

Parabolic equations with drift terms arise naturally in mathematical models describing combined transport and diffusion processes. In statistical physics, such equations appear in Fokker-Planck models, where the drift represents deterministic transport induced by external forces and governs the evolution of probability densities
[
21
]
.
In fluid mechanics and mass transfer theory, convection-diffusion equations describe the interplay between molecular diffusion and advective transport
caused by fluid motion
[
6
]
.
Drift-driven diffusion models also arise in population dynamics and chemotaxis,
where directed movement of organisms is influenced by environmental signals.
Furthermore, in financial mathematics, transformed parabolic equations
such as the Black-Scholes model contain drift terms representing deterministic
trends in asset price dynamics
[
2
]
.
In these applications, the drift coefficient plays a crucial role in the system dynamics
but is often not directly measurable.
This naturally leads to inverse problems concerned with reconstructing the drift from available measurement data.
Inverse problems for convection-diffusion equations, including drift identification problems,
have been widely investigated under various observation settings, for example,
[
15
,
5
,
18
]
.

From an analytical perspective, inverse drift reconstruction is challenging
due to the nonlinear dependence of the solution on the unknown coefficient and
the instability of the problem with respect to data perturbations.
To overcome these difficulties, monotonicity-based operator methods provide a natural framework for inverse drift problems: one constructs a forward operator whose fixed points coincide with the unknown coefficient, and monotonicity derived from maximum principles yields both
uniqueness and a natural iterative reconstruction scheme.
For parabolic inverse problems, early uniqueness results with final-time overdetermination can be traced back to classical works such as
[
16
,
13
,
22
,
14
]
, while the monotonicity-based framework was surveyed in
[
11
,
26
]
.
A fixed-point monotonicity formulation was further developed for
time-dependent coefficients in fractional diffusion equations in

[
29
,
28
,
27
]
, where the unknown coefficient is characterized as a fixed point of a monotone operator, and the same structure leads to an iterative reconstruction scheme.
For inverse drift reconstruction, related terminal-observation results for one dimensional coefficients were obtained in
[
3
]
.
Other inverse problems for drift or convection terms in parabolic equations have also been studied under various observation settings; see, for example,
[
8
,
7
,
1
,
23
]
.
The present work extends this direction to a two-dimensional parabolic equation with mixed boundary conditions.

Main results and outline.

In this work we first construct an operator

)) whose one of fixed points is the unknown drift

. Then we prove several properties for this operator, such as the well-definedness, equivalence and monotonicity. The uniqueness of inverse problem (

The remainder of this article is structured as follows. In Section (

), the maximum principles of elliptic and parabolic equations and the positivity results of equation (

). We prove the uniqueness of inverse problem (

) in Section (

defined in (
3.1
). Finally, several numerical experiments are taken in Section (
4
) and the related numerical results are presented.

Preliminaries.

2.1
Well-posedness of the forward problem.

We now establish the well-posedness of equation (
1.1
), including existence, uniqueness, and regularity of the solution. Our analysis follows the well-posedness framework of Theorem 2.1 in
[
25
]
, which is based on the classical parabolic theory developed in
[
24
]
.
In contrast to
[
25
]
, where a mixed boundary value problem with
time-dependent boundaries is considered under the divergence-free condition

\nabla\cdot(q(x),0)=0
, we restrict ourselves to a fixed spatial domain with
time-independent boundary data and assume only
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Under these assumptions, the well-posedness result remains valid.
For completeness, we sketch the standard lifting and energy argument adapted to the present setting. For a boundary segment
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
, we write

\Gamma_{T}:=\Gamma\times(0,T),\qquad H^{r,s}(\Gamma_{T}):=L^{2}(0,T;H^{r}(\Gamma))\cap H^{s}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma)),

with
r,s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
Q_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T]
. Assume that the source term satisfies
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the initial data
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, and the drift coefficient
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. The boundary data satisfy:

•

b_{1},b_{3}\in H^{3/2,\,3/4}(\Sigma_{D})
, where
\Sigma_{D}=(l_{1}\cup l_{3})\times(0,T)
;

•

b_{2},b_{4}\in H^{1/2,\,1/4}(\Sigma_{N})
, where
\Sigma_{N}=(l_{2}\cup l_{4})\times(0,T)
,

together with the compatibility conditions

u_{0}(x)=b_{1}(x,0)
on
l_{1}
and
u_{0}(x)=b_{3}(x,0)
on
l_{3}
.
Then the forward problem of equation (
1.1
) admits a unique solution
u
such that:

\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}(Q_{T}),\quad u\in C([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega)).

Proof.

We establish the well-posedness via a lifting argument and energy estimates.
To handle the non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, we introduce a
lifting function
U_{D}
that satisfies the prescribed boundary data and
define
w=u-U_{D}
. More precisely, let
U_{D}
be a function such that

•

U_{D}=b_{1}
on
l_{1}
and
U_{D}=b_{3}
on
l_{3}
,

•

U_{D}=0
on
l_{2}\cup l_{4}
,

•

U_{D}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

The existence of such a lifting function follows from standard trace and extension results for parabolic problems;
see, for instance,
[
17
]
.

Then
w
satisfies homogeneous Dirichlet boundary
conditions on
l_{1}\cup l_{3}
and

\partial_{t}w-\Delta w+q(x)\partial_{x_{1}}w+C_{p}w=F\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],

where

F=f-\partial_{t}U_{D}+\Delta U_{D}-q(x)\partial_{x_{1}}U_{D}-C_{p}U_{D}.

On
l_{2}\cup l_{4}
,
w
satisfies the Neumann condition

\frac{\partial w}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}=b_{N}-\frac{\partial U_{D}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\quad b_{N}=b_{2}\,\text{on}\,l_{2},\,b_{N}=b_{4}\,\text{on}\,l_{4}.

Multiplying the equation for
w
by
w
and integrating over
\Omega
yields

\int_{\Omega}w\partial_{t}w\,dx-\int_{\Omega}w\Delta w\,dx+\int_{\Omega}q(x)w\partial_{x_{1}}w\,dx+C_{p}\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}Fw\,dx.

Using integration by parts, the boundary condition, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{p}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}Fw\,dx-\int_{\Omega}q(x)w\partial_{x_{1}}w\,dx+\int_{l_{2}\cup l_{4}}\Big(b_{N}-\frac{\partial U_{D}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big)w\,dS.

The source term is estimated using Young’s inequality:

\left|\int_{\Omega}Fw\,dx\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Since
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, the drift term satisfies

\left|\int_{\Omega}q(x)w\partial_{x_{1}}w\,dx\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\|q\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}}{2}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

The boundary term on
l_{2}\cup l_{4}
is estimated by the trace theorem and Young’s inequality: For any
\varepsilon>0

\left|\int_{l_{2}\cup l_{4}}\Big(b_{N}-\frac{\partial U_{D}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big)w\,dS\right|\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\Big(\|b_{N}\|_{H^{1/2,1/4}(\Sigma_{N})}^{2}+\|U_{D}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\Big),

Combining the above estimates yields

\frac{d}{dt}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{1}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

where
C_{1}>0
depends on
\|q\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
and
C_{p}
.
By Gronwall’s inequality,

\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq e^{C_{1}t}\left(\|w(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|F(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,ds\right),

with
w(0)=u_{0}-U_{D}(0)
.

Higher regularity, in particular
\partial_{t}w\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
, follows by
testing the equation with
\partial_{t}w
and applying standard energy
arguments for parabolic equations. Consequently,

w\in C([0,T];H^{1}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}w\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
.
Since
u=w+U_{D}
, the asserted regularity for
u
follows.
Uniqueness is a direct consequence of linearity and the energy estimate. The proof is complete.
∎

is constant in

(x^{*},t^{*})
; while we could have
C_{p}w(x^{*},t^{*})<0
from the strict positivity of
C_{p}
. These gives that

(\partial_{t}-\Delta+q(x)\partial_{x_{1}}+C_{p})w(x^{*},t^{*})<0,

which contradicts with equation (
2.3
). Hence, we could deduce the nonnegativity of
w=\partial_{t}u
.

For
(d)
, the model of
w=\partial^{2}_{x_{1}t}u
is written as (
2.2
)
With the result
(C_{p}+\partial_{x_{1}}q)\geq 0
, Lemma (
2.5
) gives the nonnegativity of
w=\partial^{2}_{x_{1}t}u
. The proof is complete.
∎

3
Analysis of inverse problem (
1.4
).

Next we would display how to solve inverse problem (
1.4
) by the monotone operator method.

3.1
Operator
K
.

From equation (
1.1
) and data (
1.3
), we define the operator
K
as

K\psi=\frac{f(x)-\partial_{t}u(x,T;\psi)+\Delta g(x)-C_{p}g(x)}{\partial_{x_{1}}g(x)},

(3.1)

with domain

\mathcal{D}_{K}:=\Big\{\psi\in C^{1}(\Omega):\psi\leq\frac{f(x)+\Delta g(x)-C_{p}g(x)}{\partial_{x_{1}}g(x)}\Big\}.

(3.2)

Here the notation
u(x,T;\psi)
means the solution
u
of equation (
1.1
) with drift term
\psi
. The well-definedness of
K
is ensured by Lemma (
2.7
), from which the denominator
\partial_{x_{1}}g(x)
is strictly positive.

From the next lemma, we could solve inverse problem (
1.4
) by considering the fixed point problem of
K
.

Lemma 3.1
.

With (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), for
q\in\mathcal{D}_{K}
,
u(x,T;q)=g(x)
is valid if and only if
Kq=q
.

Proof.

If
u(x,T;q)=g(x)
, it is obvious that
q
is a fixed point of
K
from (
1.1
) and (
3.1
).

Given a fixed point
q\in\mathcal{D}_{K}
of
K
, now we need to show it satisfies
u(x,T;q)=g(x)
.
Setting
w(x)=u(x,T;q)-g(x)
, we have that

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (-\Delta+q(x)\partial_{x_{1}}+C_{p})w(x)&=0,&&x\in\Omega;\\
\mathcal{B}w(x)&=0,&&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

Assume that
w\not\equiv 0
. By Lemma (
2.4
),

w
cannot attain a positive maximum in the interior of
\Omega
.
Since
w=0
on
l_{1}\cup l_{3}
, any positive maximum must be attained at some point
of
l_{2}\cup l_{4}
. Then Lemma (
2.6
) implies

\frac{\partial w}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}>0
at that point, contradicting

\frac{\partial w}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}=0
on
l_{2}\cup l_{4}
.
Hence
w\leq 0
in
\Omega
.
Applying the same argument to
-w
, we obtain
w\geq 0
in
\Omega
.
Therefore
w\equiv 0
in
\Omega
and hence
u(x,T;q)=g(x)
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Monotonicity.

The monotonicity of operator
K
means that
K
could keep the monotone relation on
\mathcal{D}_{K}
, which is stated by the next lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

With operator
K
in (
3.1
) and domain
\mathcal{D}_{K}
in (
3.2
), for
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{K}
, the result
\psi_{1}\leq\psi_{2}
leads to
K\psi_{1}\leq K\psi_{2}
on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Setting
w=\partial_{t}u(x,t;\psi_{1})-\partial_{t}u(x,t;\psi_{2})
, it satisfies

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}-\Delta+\psi_{1}(x)\partial_{x_{1}}+C_{p})w(x,t)&=(\psi_{2}-\psi_{1})\partial^{2}_{x_{1}t}u(x,t;\psi_{2}),&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T];\\
\mathcal{B}w(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T];\\
w(x,0)&=(\psi_{2}-\psi_{1})\partial_{x_{1}}u_{0}(x),&&x\in\Omega.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

From the definition of operator
K
, it is sufficient to show the nonnegativity of
w
.
Assume that

w(x^{*},t^{*}):=\min\{w(x,t):(x,t)\in\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T]\}.

From Lemma (
2.5
) and Assumption (
2.2
), we have that

(x^{*},t^{*})\in\{0,1\}\times(0,1)\times(0,T].

For the case of
x^{*}\in\{0\}\times(0,1)
, the minimum result and the strict negativity yield that

(\partial_{t}-\partial^{2}_{x_{2}x_{2}}u+C_{p})w(x^{*},t^{*})<0;

while the boundary condition on
\{0\}\times(0,1)
together with the minimum gives that

(-\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{1}}+\psi_{1}(x)\partial_{x_{1}})w(x^{*},t^{*})\leq 0.

The above two inequalities lead to the result that
(\psi_{2}-\psi_{1})\partial^{2}_{x_{1}t}u(x,t;\psi_{2})<0
, which contradicts with Lemma (
2.7
) and the fact
\psi_{1}\leq\psi_{2}
.
The proofs for the case of
x^{*}\in\{1\}\times(0,1)
could be deduced analogously. Hence we have that
w\geq 0
on
\Omega\times(0,T]
, which leads to the desired result. The proof is complete.
∎

3.3
The specific iteration and uniqueness theorem.

First we build a uniqueness result under monotone relation.

Lemma 3.3
.

Recalling
K
and
\mathcal{D}_{K}
in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), given
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{K}
with
q_{1}\leq q_{2}
, if
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are both fixed points of
K
, then
q_{1}=q_{2}
on
\Omega
.

Proof.

The model for
w=u(x,t;\psi_{1})-u(x,t;\psi_{2})
could be given as

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}-\Delta+\psi_{1}(x)\partial_{x_{1}}+C_{p})w(x,t)&=(\psi_{2}-\psi_{1})\partial_{x_{1}}u(x,t;\psi_{2}),&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T];\\
\mathcal{B}w(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T];\\
w(x,0)&=w(x,T)=0,&&x\in\Omega.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

The equality
w(x,T)=0
follows from Lemma (
3.1
). The proof of Lemma (
3.2
) gives the nonnegativity of
\partial_{t}w
, which together with
w(x,0)=w(x,T)=0
yields that
w\equiv 0
on
\Omega\times(0,T]
. Then we obtain that
(\psi_{2}-\psi_{1})\partial_{x_{1}}u(x,t;\psi_{2})\equiv 0
, which leads to
\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}
since Lemma (
2.7
) ensures the strict positivity of
\partial_{x_{1}}u
. The proof is complete.
∎

Next we define the iteration from upper bound of
\mathcal{D}_{K}
as follows:

q_{0}=\frac{f(x)+\Delta g(x)-C_{p}g(x)}{\partial_{x_{1}}g(x)},\quad q_{n+1}=Kq_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.3)

Now we could state the uniqueness theorem of inverse problem (
1.4
).

Theorem 1
.

With (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), fixing a fixed point
q\in\mathcal{D}_{K}
of
K
, the sequence
\{q_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
generated by (
3.3
) would converge to
q
decreasingly. Therefore, there is at most one fixed point of
K
in
\mathcal{D}_{K}
. By Lemma (
3.1
), we could obtain the uniqueness of inverse problem (
1.4
).

Proof.

If
q\in\mathcal{D}_{K}
is the solution of inverse problem (
1.4
), it should be one fixed point of
K
by Lemma (
3.1
). From (
3.3
), with the fact that the initial guess
q_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{K}
is the upper bound of the domain
\mathcal{D}_{K}
, we have
q\leq q_{0}
, which leads to
q=Kq\leq Kq_{0}=q_{1}
by Lemma (
3.2
). Also, the nonnegativity of
\partial_{t}u(x,T;q_{0})
ensured by Lemma (
2.7
) leads to
q_{1}\leq q_{0}
, and Lemmas (
2.2
)-(
2.3
) guarantee the required smoothness of
q_{1}
. To sum up, we have
q_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{K}
and
q\leq q_{1}\leq q_{0}
. Using Lemma (
3.2
) again, it holds that

q=Kq\leq Kq_{1}=q_{2}\leq Kq_{0}=q_{1}\leq q_{0}.

Continuing this argument, it gives that
\{q_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a decreasing sequence and has a lower bound
q
, which yields the pointwise convergence of
\{q_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. The limit is denoted by
\tilde{q}
and obviously
q\leq\tilde{q}\leq q_{0}
.

Now the task is to show that
q=\tilde{q}
. From the monotone convergence theorem, we have that
\|q_{n}-\tilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\to 0
. With triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|K\tilde{q}-\tilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}

\displaystyle\leq\|K\tilde{q}-Kq_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\|Kq_{n}-\tilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}

\displaystyle=\|K\tilde{q}-Kq_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\|q_{n+1}-\tilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

It is obvious that
I_{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. For
I_{1}
, with Lemma (
2.7
) we have

\displaystyle I_{1}=\|[\partial_{t}u(x,T;\tilde{q})-\partial_{t}u(x,T;q_{n})]/\partial_{x_{1}}g(x)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\leq C\|\partial_{t}u(x,T;\tilde{q})-\partial_{t}u(x,T;q_{n})\|_{L^{2}(0,1)},

where the constant
C
depends on
m
. Setting
w=\partial_{t}u(x,t;\tilde{q})-\partial_{t}u(x,t;q_{n})
, the proof of Lemma (
3.2
) gives the model of
w
as

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (\partial_{t}-\Delta+\tilde{q}(x)\partial_{x_{1}}+C_{p})w(x,t)&=(q_{n}-\tilde{q})\partial^{2}_{x_{1}t}u(x,t;\psi_{2}),&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T];\\
\mathcal{B}w(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T];\\
w(x,0)&=(q_{n}-\tilde{q})\partial_{x_{1}}u_{0}(x),&&x\in\Omega.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

Testing the equation for
w
with
w
and integrating over
\Omega
,
an application of Young’s inequality and Grönwall’s lemma yields the energy estimate

\|w(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\Big(\|w(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|(q_{n}-\tilde{q})\,\partial^{2}_{x_{1}t}u(\cdot;\tilde{q})\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}\Big),

(3.4)

where
C>0
is a constant.

By the well-posedness and regularity result stated in
Lemma (
2.1
), estimate (
3.4
) yields

\displaystyle\|w(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|w(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|(q_{n}-\tilde{q})\,\partial^{2}_{x_{1}t}u(\cdot;\tilde{q})\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|\partial_{x_{1}}u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+T^{1/2}\|\partial^{2}_{x_{1}t}u\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\Big)\|q_{n}-\tilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(3.5)

By Lemma (
2.3
) and Assumption (
2.2
),
both
\partial_{x_{1}}u_{0}
and the mixed derivative

\partial^{2}_{x_{1}t}u
are bounded.
Consequently, estimate (
3.3
) simplifies to

\|w(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\,\|q_{n}-\tilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(3.6)

where the constant
C>0
depends only on the a priori data of the problem.

So
I_{1}\leq C\|q_{n}-\tilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, which converges to zero as
n\to\infty
. To sum up, we could deduce that
\|K\tilde{q}-\tilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}=0
, which gives that
\tilde{q}
is a fixed point of
K
. Note that the pointwise convergence only ensures that
\tilde{q}\in L^{\infty}(0,1)
.
We further rely on the interior and boundary regularity results established in
Lemmas (
2.2
) and (
2.3
),
which imply

\partial_{t}u(\cdot,T;\psi)\in C^{1}(\Omega).

Consequently,
K\tilde{q}\in C^{1}(\Omega)
.
With
\|K\tilde{q}-\tilde{q}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}=0
, we could pick the smooth version of
\tilde{q}
to make sure
\tilde{q}\in C^{1}
.
Now we have proved that
q
and
\tilde{q}
are both fixed points of
K
in
\mathcal{D}_{K}
and they satisfy
q\leq\tilde{q}
. Applying Lemma (
3.3
), we have
q=\tilde{q}
.

The uniqueness could be deduced straightforwardly. For two solutions
q
and
\hat{q}
in
\mathcal{D}_{K}
of inverse problem (
1.4
), Lemma (
3.1
) implies that they are both the fixed points of
K
. With the above proofs, we obtain that
q_{n}\to q
and
q_{n}\to\hat{q}
simultaneously. This gives
q=\hat{q}
and completes the proof.
∎

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, We report numerical experiments for the proposed reconstruction method.
All computations use uniform Cartesian grids and finite differences for the
forward solver in Algorithm (
1
).

Let
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
be discretized by a uniform grid with steps
h_{x},h_{y}
, where
(x,y)
denotes the spatial variable. Let
t^{n}=n\tau
,
n=0,1,\dots,N_{t}
, partition
[0,T]
.
For a given drift coefficient
q
, the forward problem

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+q(x,y)\,\partial_{x}u+C_{p}u=f(x,y)

(4.1)

is discretized in time by backward Euler,

\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}-\Delta u^{n+1}+q(x,y)\,\partial_{x}u^{n+1}+C_{p}u^{n+1}=f(x,y),\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N_{t}-1.

(4.2)

In space, we use centered finite differences for
\Delta
and
\partial_{x_{1}}u

at interior grid points. Dirichlet conditions are imposed on
y=0
and
y=1

by directly enforcing the boundary values at the corresponding nodes.
Neumann conditions on
x=0
and
x=1
are enforced by replacing the corresponding boundary rows with second-order one-sided finite difference approximations.
Each time step solves

\Big(\frac{1}{\tau}I-\Delta_{h}+Q\,D_{x,h}+C_{p}I\Big)u^{n+1}=\frac{1}{\tau}u^{n}+f,

(4.3)

where
\Delta_{h}
and
D_{x,h}
are the discrete Laplace and first-order
operators, and
Q=\mathrm{diag}(q)
. The solver is called each iteration to
compute the forward solution
u
and a backward-difference approximation of
\partial_{t}u(\cdot,T)
.

Algorithm 1

q

f
, terminal observation
g=u(\cdot,T)
,
initial data
u_{0}
, potential constant
C_{p}
.

0:

Approximate drift term
q_{N}

1:

Set the initial guess

q_{0}=\frac{f+\Delta g-C_{p}g}{\partial_{x}g}.

2:

for

k=1,2,\ldots,N

do

3:

Compute the solution
u(\cdot,t;q_{k-1})
by solving the forward problem ((
1.1
)) with
q_{k-1}
;

4:

Update the drift term by

q_{k}=Kq_{k-1}=\frac{f-\partial_{t}u(\cdot,T;q_{k-1})+\Delta g-C_{p}g}{\partial_{x_{1}}g}.

5:

Check the stopping criterion

\|q_{k}-q_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
for some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
.

6:

end

for

7:

Output
the recovered drift term
q_{N}
.

The experiments are used to show three main points:
(i) the convergence of the proposed iteration for noise-free data,
(ii) its stability under noisy terminal measurements,
and (iii) the monotone improvement of the iterates in the first few steps.

A binary coefficient supported on a Chinese-character-shaped region, namely

The potential constant is fixed as

, the final time is

,

.

on
