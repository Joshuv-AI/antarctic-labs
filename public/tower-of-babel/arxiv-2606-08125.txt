Arithmetic exceptionality of generalized Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind Thanks: This study was supported by The Scientific and Technological Research Council of Türkiye (TÜBİTAK) under Project No. 124F146.
Derya Acar, Metİn Azmaz, Vural Cam, Ömer Küçüksakallı

Abstract
. In this paper, we show that generalized Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind, associated with the root system A 2 A_{2} , are not arithmetically exceptional. We achieve this by studying the norms of certain cyclotomic elements that admit a parametrization of finite fields.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
11T06, 11T22

1.
Introduction

Let
p
be a prime, and let
\mathbf{F}_{p}
denote the finite field with
p
elements. A polynomial

f\in\mathbf{Z}[\mathbf{x}]
in
n
variables is called
arithmetically exceptional
if the induced map

f:\mathbf{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbf{F}_{p}^{n}

is a permutation for infinitely many primes
p
.

The univariate arithmetically exceptional polynomials are classified: they are precisely the compositions of linear polynomials and Dickson polynomials of the first kind
D_{k}(x,a)

[
4
]
. The Dickson polynomials of the first kind are uniquely determined by the functional equation

(1.1)

D_{k}\left(y+\frac{a}{y},a\right)=y^{k}+\frac{a^{k}}{y^{k}},

and admit the explicit formula

(1.2)

D_{k}(x,a)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{k}{k-j}\binom{k-j}{j}(-a)^{j}x^{k-2j}.

See
[
13
, (7.5)]
for details of this computation.

The Dickson polynomials of the first kind are closely related to the Chebyshev polynomials
T_{k}(x)
of the first kind, defined by
T_{k}(\cos\theta)=\cos(k\theta)
. Indeed, equation (
1.1
) implies that

D_{k}(x,1)=2T_{k}(x/2).

Let
q
be a power of
p
, and let
\mathbf{F}_{q}
denote the finite field with
q
elements. It is well known that the polynomial
D_{k}(x,a)
, with
a\neq 0
, permutes the finite field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
if and only if
\gcd(q^{2}-1,k)=1
. Using Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, one can deduce that Dickson polynomials of the first kind are arithmetically exceptional for certain values of
k
.

Another important family consists of the Dickson polynomials of the second kind, denoted by
E_{k}(x,a)
. These are uniquely defined by the functional equation

(1.3)

E_{k}\left(y+\frac{a}{y},a\right)=\frac{y^{k+1}-\dfrac{a^{k+1}}{y^{k+1}}}{y-\dfrac{a}{y}}.

The polynomials
E_{k}(x,1)
are closely related to the Chebyshev polynomials
U_{k}(x)
of the second kind, defined by
U_{k}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta=\sin((k+1)\theta)
. Indeeed, equation (
1.3
) implies that

E_{k}(x,1)=U_{k}(x/2)

Furthermore, using the identity
D_{k}^{\prime}(x,a)=kE_{k-1}(x,a)
, together with (
1.2
), one derives the explicit formula

E_{k}(x,a)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{k-j}{j}(-a)^{j}x^{k-2j}.

Classifying the integers
k
for which
E_{k}(x,1)
permutes
\mathbf{F}_{q}
remains an open problem. Several partial results are known. See
[
6
,
14
]
, and the references therein.

On the other hand, the arithmetic exceptionality of
E_{k}(x,1)
is completely understood. One may possibly use Fried’s characterization
[
4
]
and show that
E_{k}(x,1)
cannot be realized as a compositions of linear polynomials and Dickson polynomials of the first kind. However, this approach appears impractical. Instead, we rely on the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Cohen
[
2
]
, Matthews
[
16
]
)
.

Let
p
be an odd prime. Then the Dickson polynomial
E_{k}(x,1)
of the second kind permutes
\mathbf{F}_{p}
if and only if
k
satisfies the system of congruences

\begin{cases}k+1\equiv\pm 2\pmod{p},\\
k+1\equiv\pm 2\pmod{\frac{p-1}{2}},\\
k+1\equiv\pm 2\pmod{\frac{p+1}{2}}.\end{cases}

The polynomial
E_{1}(x,1)=x
is arithmetically exceptional, trivially. Now let
k\geq 2
be fixed, and let
p
be a prime satisfying
p>k+3
. Then

k+1\not\equiv\pm 2\pmod{p}
. Hence the first congruence condition of the theorem fails, and therefore
E_{k}(x,1):\mathbf{F}_{p}\to\mathbf{F}_{p}
is not a permutation whenever
p>k+3
. Consequently, for every
k\geq 2
, the polynomial
E_{k}(x,1)
is not arithmetically exceptional.

In this paper, we focus on families of polynomial mappings associated with semi-simple Lie algebras that generalize the classical families mentioned above. Indeed, the classical Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind can be realized as a special case of certain polynomial mappings
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}:\mathbf{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}^{n}
, discovered by Veselov
[
18
]
, and somewhat later by Hoffmann and Withers
[
7
]
, following the exponential invariants of Bourbaki
[
1
]
. These polynomial mappings
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}
are called the
generalized Chebyshev polynomials
and they have interesting properties. From their definition, they naturally commute

P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}\circ P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{l}=P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{l}\circ P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}

and it is believed that they exhaust all commuting polynomials under certain additional assumptions
[
19
]
. They are orthogonal with respect to a certain measure and can be extended to a complete set of orthogonal polynomials
[
7
]
.

As a basic example, we may consider the family associated with the simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=A_{1}
. In this case, we recover the first kind classical polynomials as follows:

P_{A_{1}}^{k}(x)=D_{k}(x,1)=2T_{k}(x/2).

The construction of Bourbaki
[
1
]
, is very general, and it also allows us to write the classical Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind as maps suitably acting on the associated group ring of exponential invariants. Guided by the Weyl character formula, we consider the
generalized Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind
, denoted
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}
, associated with the semi-simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

In particular, if
\mathfrak{g}=A_{1}
, then we recover the second kind classical polynomials as follows:

Q_{A_{1}}^{k}(x)=E_{k}(x,1)=2U_{k}(x/2).

To our knowledge, the polynomials
Q^{k}_{\mathfrak{g}}
with
\mathfrak{g}\neq A_{1}
have not been studied in the theory of finite fields before this work. See
[
8
,
\lx@sectionsign
3.3]
for an appearance in numerical analysis.

The polynomial families
P_{A_{1}}^{k}
and
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}
both satisfy the same recurrence relation with different initial conditions
[
3
]
. More precisely, we have

(1.4)

\begin{gathered}P^{k}_{A_{1}}=xP^{k-1}_{A_{1}}-P^{k-2}_{A_{1}},\text{ with }P^{0}_{A_{1}}=2,\ P^{1}_{A_{1}}=x,\\
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}=xQ^{k-1}_{A_{1}}-Q^{k-2}_{A_{1}},\text{ with }Q^{0}_{A_{1}}=1,\ Q^{1}_{A_{1}}=x.\end{gathered}

We emphasize that this small change in the initial conditions leads to a completely different behavior with respect to arithmetic exceptionality.

There is a rich literature about the polynomial mappings
P^{k}_{A_{2}}(x,y)
. For instance, see
[
10
,
12
,
17
]
, and the references therein. We have
P^{k}_{A_{2}}=(g_{k}(y_{1},y_{2}),g_{k}(y_{2},y_{1}))
and the polynomials
g_{k}
satisfy the recurrence relation

(1.5)

g_{k}=y_{1}g_{k-1}-y_{2}g_{k-2}+g_{k-3}\text{ for }k\geq 3.

The first few examples of the polynomials
P^{k}_{A_{2}}
are given below:

\displaystyle P_{A_{2}}^{0}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(3,3),

\displaystyle P_{A_{2}}^{1}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1},y_{2}),

\displaystyle P_{A_{2}}^{2}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1}^{2}-2y_{2},y_{2}^{2}-2y_{1}),

\displaystyle P_{A_{2}}^{3}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1}^{3}-3y_{1}y_{2}+3,y_{2}^{3}-3y_{2}y_{1}+3).

The following result draws an analogy between the polynomial families
P^{k}_{A_{1}}
and
P^{k}_{A_{2}}
in terms of arithmetic exceptionality.

Theorem 2

(
[
12
]
)
.

The bivariate polynomial mappings
P^{\ell}_{A_{2}}
are arithmetically exceptional for prime numbers
\ell\geq 11
.

In this paper, we focus on certain bivariate polynomial mappings. These polynomials are given by
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}=(h_{k}(y_{1},y_{2}),h_{k}(y_{2},y_{1}))
where the polynomials
h_{k}
satisfy the recurrence relation

h_{k}=y_{1}h_{k-1}-y_{2}h_{k-2}+h_{k-3}\text{ for }k\geq 3.

We emphasize that the recurrence relation proved in Theorem
13
coincides with the recurrence relation (
1.5
) satisfied by
P_{A_{2}}^{k}
. However, the initial conditions differ, leading to distinct families. In fact, the first few instances of the polynomials
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}
are given as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{A_{2}}^{0}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(1,1),

\displaystyle Q_{A_{2}}^{1}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1},y_{2}),

\displaystyle Q_{A_{2}}^{2}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1}^{2}-y_{2},y_{2}^{2}-y_{1}),

\displaystyle Q_{A_{2}}^{3}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1}^{3}-2y_{1}y_{2}+1,y_{2}^{3}-2y_{2}y_{1}+1).

In this paper, we prove that the bivariate polynomial mappings
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}
are not exceptional for any integer
k\geq 2
. This result draws an analogy between the polynomial families
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}
and
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}
in terms of arithmetic exceptionality.

The organization of the paper is as follows. In the second section we give the necessary background related to Lie algebras, explaining the polynomial families
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}
and
Q_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}
. In the third section, we revisit Cohen’s theorem and show that the polynomial
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}
does not induce a permutation for all but finitely many primes
p
. In the last section, we prove our main result about
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}
by generalizing the algebraic methods introduced in the third section.

We hope that the ideas of this work can be further generalized to other second kind generalized Chebyshev polynomials associated to simple Lie algebras other than
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
.

2.
Notation and Terminology

In this section, we provide some terminology and notation that will be used throughout the paper. We mainly follow the content of Bourbaki
[
1
, VI,
\lx@sectionsign
3]
while recalling some notation from
[
11
]
.

Let
W
be the Weyl group associated with a semi-simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of rank
n
. Let
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
be a set of simple roots. The Weyl group
W
of
\mathfrak{g}
is a finite reflection group, and each simple root
\alpha_{i}
induces a simple reflection in
W
. To each root
\alpha_{i}
, we associate a coroot

\alpha_{i}^{\vee}=\frac{2\alpha_{i}}{\left(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i}\right)}.

The fundamental weights
\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\}
are then defined by the condition:

\left(\omega_{i},\alpha_{j}^{\vee}\right)=\delta_{ij}

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta function.

Let
\Lambda
be the free abelian group generated by the fundamental weights. The elements of
\Lambda
are called
weights
. Consider the associated group algebra over a unique factorization domain
A
denoted by
A[\Lambda]
. In this paper, it is sufficient to use
A=\mathbf{Z}
. A typical element of
A[\Lambda]
is of the form

x=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}x_{\lambda}e^{\lambda}

with coefficients
x_{\lambda}\in A
. An element
x\in A[\Lambda]
is said to be
invariant
under
W
if

w(x)=x
for all
w\in W
. We denote by
A[\Lambda]^{W}
, the set of
W
-invariant elements of
A[\Lambda]
.

The Weyl group is generated by reflections, and
\det(w)
is
\pm 1
for each element
w\in W
. The following notion is a key property to define the second kind Chebyshev polynomials.

Definition 3
.

An element
x\in A[\Lambda]
is said to be
anti-invariant
under
W
if

w(x)=\det(w)\cdot x

for all
w\in W
.

For any
x\in A[\Lambda]
, define

J(x)=\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)\cdot w(x).

A straightforward calculation shows that
J(x)
is anti-invariant.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the invariant and anti-invariant submodules of
A[\Lambda]
. To exhibit this correspondence, we let
\rho=\omega_{1}+\ldots+\omega_{n}\in\Lambda
. It turns out that for any
\alpha\in\Lambda
, there exists a
W
-invariant element
\beta\in A[\Lambda]^{W}
such that
J(e^{\alpha})=J(e^{\rho})\beta
. Moreover, the multiplication by
J(e^{\rho})
is a bijection from
A[\Lambda]^{W}
to the set of anti-invariant elements of
A[\Lambda]
.

Definition 4
.

Let
x=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}x_{\lambda}e^{\lambda}
be an element of
A[\Lambda]
. We fix an ordering on weights defined by
\mu\geq\lambda
if and only if
\mu-\lambda
is a nonnegative linear combination of simple roots
\alpha_{i}
. A term
x_{\mu}e^{\mu}
is called a
maximal term
of
x
if
\mu\geq\lambda
for all
\lambda
with
x_{\lambda}\neq 0
.

Example 5
.

The element
J(e^{\rho})
has
e^{\rho}
as its unique maximal term.

Example 6

(
S
-type elements)
.

For any
\lambda\in\Lambda
, define

S(e^{\lambda})=\sum_{\mu\in W(\lambda)}e^{\mu}.

Obviously, this is a
W
-invariant element. In particular, we set

y_{i}=S(e^{\omega_{i}})
for each
1\leq i\leq n
. Each element
y_{i}
has
e^{\omega_{i}}
as its unique maximal term.

Example 7

(
J
-type quotients)
.

Let
\chi_{\lambda}
be the character of an irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{g}
with the highest weight
\lambda\in\Lambda
. The Weyl Character Formula
[
5
,
\lx@sectionsign
10.4]
, states that

\chi_{\lambda}=J(e^{\rho+\lambda})/J(e^{\rho}).

In particular, we set

z_{i}=J(e^{\rho+\omega_{i}})/J(e^{\rho})
for each
1\leq i\leq n
. Each element
z_{i}
has
e^{\omega_{i}}
as its unique maximal term.

Now we are ready to state the main result of Bourbaki
[
1
]
. This result enables us to introduce interesting families of polynomials, such as
P^{k}_{\mathfrak{g}}
, and
Q^{k}_{\mathfrak{g}}
, generalizing the classical Chebyshev polynomials,
T_{k}(x)
and
U_{k}(x)
, respectively.

Theorem 8
.

[
1
, VI, §3.4, Th. 1]

Let
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}
be the fundamental weights, and, for
1\leq i\leq n
, let
x_{i}
be an element of
A[\Lambda]^{W}
with
e^{\omega_{i}}
as its unique maximal term. Let

\varphi:A[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]\rightarrow A[\Lambda]^{W}

be the homomorphism from the polynomial algebra
A[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
to
A[\Lambda]^{W}
that takes
X_{i}
to
x_{i}
. Then, the map
\varphi
is an isomorphism.

This theorem has two fundamental consequences, namely the generalized Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind and the second kind. We now give these constructions focusing on the cases associated with
\mathfrak{g}=A_{1}
, and
\mathfrak{g}=A_{2}
.

2.1.
First Kind Generalized Chebyshev Polynomials

These polynomials
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}
were first studied by
[
7
,
18
]
. With each semi-simple complex Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of rank
n
, there is an associated infinite sequence of polynomial mappings
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k},~k\in{\mathbf{N}}
determined from the conditions

\mathbf{y}(k\gamma)=P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}(\mathbf{y}(\gamma))

where
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is the generalized cosine function. Recall that
y_{i}=S(e^{\omega_{i}})
. All coefficients of the polynomials defining
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}
are integers. The formal exponential sums can be considered as complex-valued functions by putting

e^{\lambda}(\gamma)\mapsto e^{-2\pi i(\lambda,\gamma)}

as in
[
7
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Example 9

(
\mathfrak{g}=A_{1}
)
.

In this case, there is a single fundamental weight
\omega=\omega_{1}
. The Weyl group is given by
W=\left\{\text{id},\sigma\right\}
where
\sigma(\omega)=-\omega
. We observe that

S(e^{k\omega})=e^{k\omega}+e^{-k\omega},

and this expression can be written as a polynomial of
y_{1}=e^{\omega}+e^{-\omega}
. In other words,
P^{k}_{A_{1}}
is the polynomial that satisfies the functional equation

(2.1)

P^{k}_{A_{1}}(e^{\omega}+e^{-\omega})=e^{k\omega}+e^{-k\omega}.

We note the similarity between this equation and the equation (
1.1
) defining the Dickson polynomials of the first kind. We have
P_{A_{1}}^{k}(x)=D_{k}(x,1)
. The first few examples of these polynomials are given by

\displaystyle P^{0}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=2,

\displaystyle P^{1}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle P^{2}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{2}-2,

\displaystyle P^{3}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{3}-3x,

\displaystyle P^{4}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{4}-4x^{2}+2.

Example 10

(
\mathfrak{g}=A_{2}
)
.

In this case, the role of the Weyl group is more apparent since there are more symmetries compared to the
A_{1}
case. There is some literature about the polynomial mappings
P^{k}_{A_{2}}
. See
[
10
,
12
,
17
]
, and the references therein for more details.

Let
W
be the Weyl group for the Lie algebra
A_{2}
. The associated Cartan matrix
C
is defined by
[(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}^{\vee})]
. See
[
9
]
for a complete table of Cartan matrices for simple Lie algebras. For the Lie algebra
A_{2}
, we have

C=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\
-1&2\end{bmatrix}.

The Cartan matrix transforms fundamental weights into simple roots. In this case, there are two fundamental weights, namely
\omega_{1}
, and
\omega_{2}
. We have

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=2\omega_{1}-\omega_{2},

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{1}+2\omega_{2}.

The Weyl group has six elements, and each orbit of a fundamental weight has three elements. We have

(2.2)

y_{1}=e^{\omega_{1}}+e^{-\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}+e^{-\omega_{2}},\quad y_{2}=e^{-\omega_{1}}+e^{\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}}+e^{\omega_{2}}.

Recall that
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})
is the generalized cosine function associated with
\mathfrak{g}=A_{2}
. We have
\mathbf{y}(k\gamma)=P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}(\mathbf{y}(\gamma))
. Let us focus on the case
k=2
. A brief computation reveals that

P_{A_{2}}^{2}(y_{1},y_{2})=(y_{1}^{2}-2y_{2},y_{2}^{2}-2y_{1}).

As we have mentioned in the introduction,
P^{k}_{A_{2}}=(g_{k}(y_{1},y_{2}),g_{k}(y_{2},y_{1}))
and the polynomials
g_{k}
satisfy the recurrence relation (
1.5
).
This relation may be established by examining the maximal terms of invariant elements having a minimal number of terms. The recurrence satisfied by
P_{A_{2}}^{k}
is an instance of a much broader and richer theory of polynomial sequences defined recursively. We refer the reader to Withers
[
21
]
for further details.

2.2.
Second Kind Generalized Chebyshev Polynomials

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be an arbitrary semi-simple Lie algebra. For each fundamental weight
\omega_{i}
, we set
z_{i}=\chi_{\omega_{i}}=J(e^{\rho+\omega_{i}})/J(e^{\rho})
. Then, by Theorem
8
,
\chi_{k\omega_{i}}=J(e^{\rho+k\omega_{i}})/J(e^{\rho})
can be written as a polynomial of
z_{i}
’s. The generalized Chebyshev polynomials
Q^{k}_{\mathfrak{g}}
of the second kind are the polynomials that satisfy the functional equation

Q^{k}_{\mathfrak{g}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=\left(\chi_{k\omega_{1}},\dots,\chi_{k\omega_{n}}\right).

To the best of our knowledge, the polynomials
Q^{k}_{\mathfrak{g}}
, for
\mathfrak{g}\neq A_{1}
, have not previously been studied in the context of finite fields. We refer the reader to
[
8
]
, and the references therein for applications of these polynomials in numerical analysis.

Example 11

(
\mathfrak{g}=A_{1}
)
.

In this case, there is a single fundamental weight
\omega=\omega_{1}
. The Weyl group is given by
W=\left\{\text{id},\sigma\right\}
where
\sigma(\omega)=-\omega
. We have

\chi_{k\omega}=\frac{J(e^{\omega+k\omega})}{J(e^{\omega})}=\frac{e^{(k+1)\omega}-e^{-(k+1)\omega}}{e^{\omega}-e^{-\omega}}

which can be written as a polynomial of

z=\chi_{\omega}=\frac{J(e^{\omega+\omega})}{J(e^{\omega})}=\frac{e^{2\omega}-e^{-2\omega}}{e^{\omega}-e^{-\omega}}=e^{\omega}+e^{-\omega}.

In other words,
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}
is the polynomial that satisfies the functional equation

(2.3)

Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(e^{\omega}+e^{-\omega})=\frac{e^{(k+1)\omega}-e^{-(k+1)\omega}}{e^{\omega}-e^{-\omega}}.

We note the similarity between this equation and the equation (
1.3
) defining the Dickson polynomials of the second kind. We have
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}(x)=E_{k}(x,1)
. The first few examples of these polynomials are given by

\displaystyle Q^{0}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle Q^{1}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle Q^{2}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{2}-1,

\displaystyle Q^{3}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{3}-2x,

\displaystyle Q^{4}_{A_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{4}-3x^{2}+1.

Example 12

(
\mathfrak{g}=A_{2}
)
.

Our main result is about the arithmetic exceptionality of the generalized Chebyshev polynomials
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}
of the second kind associated with the Lie algebra
A_{2}
.

Recall that
\chi_{\lambda}
is the character of an irreducible representation of
A_{2}
with highest weight
\lambda
. The polynomial
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}
is defined as the unique bivariate polynomial with integer coefficients that satisfies the following function equation

Q^{k}_{A_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})=(\chi_{k\omega_{1}},\chi_{k\omega_{2}}),

where
z_{1}=\chi_{\omega_{1}}
and
z_{2}=\chi_{\omega_{2}}
. We claim that
z_{1}=y_{1}
and
z_{2}=y_{2}
. We have already computed
y_{i}=S(e^{\omega_{i}})
as in (
2.2
). Now we need to compute
J(e^{\omega_{i}+\rho})
for
i=1,2,
and realize them as a multiple of
J(e^{\rho})
. For this purpose, we may use the following general form of a
J
-type element:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}J(e^{a\omega_{1}+b\omega_{2}})=&e^{(-a-b)\omega_{1}+a\omega_{2}}+e^{b\omega_{1}+(-a-b)\omega_{2}}+e^{a\omega_{1}+b\omega_{2}}\\
&-\left(e^{(a+b)\omega_{1}-b\omega_{2}}+e^{-a\omega_{1}+(a+b)\omega_{2}}+e^{-b\omega_{1}-a\omega_{2}}\right).\end{split}

Using this formula, it is straightforward to verify that

z_{i}=\frac{J(e^{\omega_{i}+\rho})}{J(e^{\rho})}=S(e^{\omega_{i}})=y_{i}.

We will use the variables
y_{i}
, as in (
2.2
), in the rest of the paper, instead of
z_{i}
. We remark that for other Lie algebras such as
B_{2}
, and
G_{2}
, the variables
z_{i}
and
y_{i}
are not the same for each
i
.

The components of the pairs
(S(e^{k\omega_{1}}),S(e^{k\omega_{2}}))
and
(\chi_{k\omega_{1}},\chi_{k\omega_{2}})
are invariant under the action of the Weyl group
W
. Even though they agree when
k=1
, they are distinct for other values of
k
. As a result,
P_{A_{2}}
and
Q_{A_{2}}
are different families of bivariate polynomial mappings. The first few examples of the polynomials
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}
are as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{A_{2}}^{0}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(1,1),

\displaystyle Q_{A_{2}}^{1}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1},y_{2}),

\displaystyle Q_{A_{2}}^{2}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1}^{2}-y_{2},y_{2}^{2}-y_{1}),

\displaystyle Q_{A_{2}}^{3}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1}^{3}-2y_{1}y_{2}+1,y_{2}^{3}-2y_{2}y_{1}+1),

\displaystyle Q_{A_{2}}^{4}(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=(y_{1}^{4}-3y_{1}^{2}y_{2}+y_{2}^{2}+2y_{1},y_{2}^{4}-3y_{2}^{2}y_{1}+y_{1}^{2}+2y_{2}).

We observe that the components of
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}
are symmetric under the change of variables
y_{1}
with
y_{2}
. This is true in general, and it is due to the symmetric nature of
A_{2}
. To ease the notation in further parts, we write

(2.5)

Q_{A_{2}}^{k}(y_{1},y_{2})=(h_{k}(y_{1},y_{2}),h_{k}(y_{2},y_{1}))

for some polynomial
h_{k}(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbf{Z}[\mathbf{y}]
. It turns out that the polynomials
h_{k}
satisfy the recursive relation (
1.5
). More precisely, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 13
.

We have
h_{0}=1
,
h_{1}=y_{1}
, and
h_{2}=y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}
. Moreover

h_{k}=y_{1}h_{k-1}-y_{2}h_{k-2}+h_{k-3},\text{ for }k\geq 3.

Proof.

There are various formulas that convert products of trigonometric functions into sums. For instance, we have

2\sin(\alpha)2\cos(\beta)=2\sin(\alpha+\beta)+2\sin(\alpha-\beta).

Substituting
\alpha=k\theta
and
\beta=\theta
into this equation and dividing through by
2\sin(\theta)
, we obtain, after rearranging the terms, the following relation

\frac{\sin((k+1)\theta)}{\sin(\theta)}=\cos(\theta)\frac{\sin(k\theta)}{\sin(\theta)}-\frac{\sin((k-1)\theta)}{\sin(\theta)}.

Note that the expressions
2\sin(m\theta)
, with
m=k+1,k,k-1
, are closely related with the formal exponential sums
J(e^{m\omega})=e^{m\omega}-e^{-m\omega}
. Thus, the above equation proves the recurrence relation for
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}
.

We want to generalize this idea to the family
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}
. In the
A_{2}
case, the
J
-type elements can be computed by (
2.4
). Note that each such element consists of six exponential terms. However, there may be some cancellations. For instance, if
a=0
or
b=0
, then
J(e^{a\omega_{1}+b\omega_{2}})=0
. For
k\geq 3
, we claim that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}J(e^{(k+1)\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})=&\ S(e^{\omega_{1}})J(e^{k\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})\\
&-S(e^{\omega_{2}})J(e^{(k-1)\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})\\
&+J(e^{(k-2)\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}).\end{split}

This equality can be directly verified by repeatedly applying (
2.4
), and following the straightforward computations in the group ring
\mathbf{Z}[\Lambda]
. It is easier to perform these computations if one focuses on the maximal terms. For instance
J(e^{(k+1)\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})
has a unique maximal term
e^{(k+1)\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}
. Such a term occurs on the right-hand side only within the product
S(e^{\omega_{1}})J(e^{k\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})
, which has
18
terms. Indeed we have

S(e^{\omega_{1}})J(e^{k\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})=J(e^{(k+1)\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})+J(e^{(k-1)\omega_{1}+2\omega_{2}})+J(e^{(k+1)\omega_{1}}).

We observe that the last term on the right-hand side is zero since the coefficient of
\omega_{2}
is zero. We also have

S(e^{\omega_{2}})J(e^{(k-1)\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})=J(e^{(k-1)\omega_{1}+2\omega_{2}})+J(e^{(k-2)\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})+J(e^{k\omega_{1}}).

Again, the last term on the right-hand side is zero since the coefficient of
\omega_{2}
is zero. Using these equalities, we can deduce (
2.6
). Finally, if we divide each term by
J(e^{\rho})=J(e^{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}})
, then we obtain the desired recurrence relation for
h_{k}
.
∎

2.3.
A one-to-one correspondence

While studying the maps
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
, one can use a parametrization of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
obtained by reducing certain algebraic numbers in a suitable number field modulo a prime ideal of that number field. For this purpose, we recall the main ideas in
[
11
]
. The central idea of that paper is the construction of a one-to-one correspondence

\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}\leftrightarrow\operatorname{Fix}(P_{g}^{q}),

between the vector space
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
and the set of complex fixed points of the generalized Chebyshev map
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{q}
. The idea is to prove that there are exactly
q^{n}
fixed points of
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{q}
, distinct fixed points remain distinct modulo
p
, and every reduced fixed point lies in
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}
. This bijection is a key mechanism allowing the dynamics over
\mathbf{C}
to control permutation behavior over finite fields. Below, we combine several results of
[
11
]
into a single statement.

Theorem 14
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semi-simple Lie algebra of rank
n
,
q
be a power of a prime
p>n
,
e
be the exponent of the Weyl group associated with
\mathfrak{g}
, and
\textnormal{Fix}(P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{q})
denote the fixed points of the generalized Chebyshev polynomial
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{q}
of the first kind. Then the number field
\mathbf{Q}(\textnormal{Fix}(P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{q}))
is contained in the cyclotomic field
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{q^{e}-1})
. Moreover, for a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{q^{e}-1})
lying over
p
, the following reduction map is a bijection:

\displaystyle\textnormal{Fix}(P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{q})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}^{n}

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\longmapsto\alpha\pmod{\mathfrak{p}}.

This one-to-one correspondence implies that the multivariate polynomial
P_{\mathfrak{g}}^{l}
in
n
variables is exceptional for every prime
l>e+1
; see
[
11
, Corollary 4]
. In this way, the theory of exceptional polynomial mappings, previously developed for root systems of type
A_{n}
by Lidl and Wells
[
12
]
, is extended to the setting of arbitrary simple Lie algebras.

In this paper, our main purpose is to study
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}
. However, the correspondence
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}\leftrightarrow\operatorname{Fix}(P_{A_{2}}^{q})
is not very useful. Because the dynamics of the first kind and the second kind Chebyshev polynomials are rather different. On the other hand, it is a surprising fact that the correspondence

\mathbf{F}_{q}\leftrightarrow\operatorname{Fix}(P_{A_{1}}^{q})

is useful to study both families
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}
and
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}
.

The Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}=A_{1}
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{Z}_{2}
, and its exponent is
e=2
. We want to utilize Theorem
14
. Let
q
be a power of a prime
p
and
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime ideal of
Q(\zeta_{q^{2}-1})
lying over
p
. We can consider formal exponential sums as complex-valued functions by putting
e^{\lambda}(\gamma)\mapsto e^{-2\pi i(\lambda,\gamma)}
as in
[
7
, Lemma 4.1]
. For the Lie algebra
A_{1}
, there is only one simple root
\alpha
. With
\gamma=u\alpha^{\vee}
,
u\in\mathbf{R}
, the functional equation (
2.1
) becomes

P_{A_{1}}^{q}(2\cos(2\pi u))=2\cos(2\pi qu).

We are interested in characterizing the fixed points of the map
P_{A_{1}}^{q}
by certain cosine values. The cosine function is even and periodic. This implies that
2\pi qu=2\pi u+2\pi c
or
2\pi qu=-2\pi u+2\pi c
for some
c\in\mathbf{Z}
. We define the finite sets

\mathcal{A}_{q}=\left\{2\cos\left(2\pi\frac{c}{q-1}\right)\mid c\in\mathbf{Z}\right\},\quad\mathcal{B}_{q}=\left\{2\cos\left(2\pi\frac{c}{q+1}\right)\mid c\in\mathbf{Z}\right\}.

These sets consist of algebraic numbers in the cyclotomic extension
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{q^{2}-1})
. We remark that these sets
\mathcal{A}_{q}
and
\mathcal{B}_{q}
are very similar to the sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
of
[
2
]
, respectively. Throughout the paper, we shall frequently use the bijection
provided by Theorem
14
. For future reference, we record it as follows

(2.7)

\mathbf{F}_{q}\leftrightarrow\textnormal{Fix}(P_{A_{1}}^{q})=\mathcal{A}_{q}\cup\mathcal{B}_{q}.

Considering formal exponential sums as complex-valued functions, the functional equation (
2.3
) that define the polynomials
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}
becomes

(2.8)

\begin{gathered}Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(2\cos{(2\pi u)})=\frac{\sin{(2\pi(k+1)u)}}{\sin{(2\pi u)}}\text{\quad for }u\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right),\\
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(2)=k+1,\\
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(-2)=(-1)^{k}(k+1).\end{gathered}

We restrict the domain from
\mathbf{R}
to
[0,1/2]
to make the related cosine function, namely
2\cos(2\pi u)
, one-to-one. This will be useful while proving Theorem
17
.

Remark 15
.

Let
q
be a power of an odd prime
p
. Matthews gives a system of congruences for Dickson polynomials of the second kind to be a permutation of a finite field
\mathbf{F}_{q}

[
16
, Theorem 2.5]
. More precisely, he preoves that the polynomial
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(x)
permutes
\mathbf{F}_{q}
if
k
satisfies

\begin{cases}k+1\equiv\pm 2\pmod{p},\\
k+1\equiv\pm 2\pmod{\frac{q-1}{2}},\\
k+1\equiv\pm 2\pmod{\frac{q+1}{2}}.\end{cases}

We emphasize that the use of (
2.7
) in this context is not new. For instance, if
k+1\equiv\pm 2\pmod{\frac{q-1}{2}}
, then
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(x):\mathcal{A}_{q}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{q}
is nothing but the map
x\mapsto\pm 1
. A similar conclusion holds for
\mathcal{B}_{q}
. Matthews employed these ideas in his thesis; however, their origins can be traced to even older results.

3.
Cohen’s Theorem revisited

Recall that Cohen
[
2
]
gives a system of necessary conditions for the Chebyshev polynomial
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}
of the second kind to be a permutation of
\mathbf{F}_{p}
, see Theorem
1
. In this section, we will provide a criterion that concludes
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}
is not a permutation if
p
is sufficiently large compared to a certain function of
k
. If
q=p
, then this result is a partial case of Cohen’s result. However, our method can be generalized to the bivariate case.

Theorem 16
.

Let
k>1
be an integer. Let
q
be a power of an odd prime
p
. If
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}
is a permutation, then
p\leq((k+1)/2)^{2}+1
. Moreover,
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}
is not arithmetically exceptional.

We first observe that the polynomial
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(x)
is even for even
k
and is odd for odd
k
. This can be proved directly by the recursive relation (
1.4
). If
k
is even, then
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(x)=h(x^{2})
for some polynomial
h(x)\in\mathbf{Z}[x]
. It follows that the map
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\to\mathbf{F}_{q}
is not a permutation if
k
is even and
p
is odd. From this point on, we only focus on the odd values of
k
and
p
.

The inverse image of zero under the map
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\to\mathbf{F}_{q}
will play a central role. We adjoin the roots of the polynomial equation
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}(x)=0
to
\mathbf{F}_{q}
, and denote the resulting finite field by
\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{q}}
. We will use the one-to-one correspondence
\textnormal{Fix}(P^{\tilde{q}}_{A_{1}})\leftrightarrow\mathbf{F}_{\tilde{q}}
given by (
2.7
). Since
k
is odd, we already have
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}(0)=0
.

Suppose that
p
divides
(k+1)
then (
2.8
) implies that
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(2)=Q^{k}_{A_{1}}(-2)=0
. In this case, the inverse image of zero under
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\to\mathbf{F}_{q}
is nontrivial since it includes at least three distinct elements, namely
0
, and
\pm 2
. We also note that
2\cos(2\pi u)
is equal to
2
, and
-2
for
u=0
, and
u=1/2
, respectively.

We claim that the solutions of the equation
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}(x)\equiv 0\pmod{\mathfrak{p}}
, with
p\nmid(k+1)
, are in one-to-one correspondence with the following set

\mathcal{Z}_{k}:=\left\{2\cos\left(2\pi\frac{d}{2(k+1)}\right)\mid c\in\mathbf{Z}\right\}\setminus\{-2,2\}.

This set is obviously a subset of
\mathcal{A}_{{\tilde{q}}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{{\tilde{q}}}
by construction. Suppose that
t=e^{2\pi iu}
for some
u\in\mathbf{Q}\setminus\mathbf{Z}
. In the complex setting, we have the following generic equation by (
2.8
)

(3.1)

Q_{A_{1}}^{k}\left(t+\frac{1}{t}\right)=t^{-k}\left(\dfrac{1-t^{2(k+1)}}{1-t^{2}}\right)=0.

The equality on the right-hand side is preserved if the underlying algebraic elements are reduced modulo
\mathfrak{p}
. If
1-t^{2(k+1)}
is congruent to zero modulo
\mathfrak{p}
, then it must be zero as a complex number as well. This is due to the fact that
t=e^{2\pi iu}
is a root of unity for any
u\in\mathbf{Q}
. This proves our claim.

Now, we focus on the intersection
\mathcal{Z}_{k}\cap(\mathcal{A}_{q}\cup\mathcal{B}_{q})
, and obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 17
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an odd integer,
q
be a power of an odd prime
p
with
p\nmid(k+1)
. Consider
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\to\mathbf{F}_{q}
. We have

|(Q_{A_{1}}^{k})^{-1}(\{0\})|=\gcd\left(\frac{q-1}{2},k+1\right)+\gcd\left(\frac{q+1}{2},k+1\right)-2.

Proof.

Suppose that
2\cos(2\pi u)\in\mathcal{A}_{q}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{k}
. This is possible if and only

\frac{c}{q-1}=u=\frac{d}{2(k+1)}

for some unique
u\in(0,1/2)
, and integers
c,d
. We set
t=\gcd\left((q-1)/2,k+1\right)
, and write

\frac{c}{(q-1)/(2t)}=2tu=\frac{d}{(k+1)/t}.

The denominators, namely
(q-1)/(2t)
and
(k+1)/t
, are corpime. Therefore, the above equality holds if and only if
2tu\in\mathbf{Z}
. We have

u\in\left\{\dfrac{1}{2t},\dots,\dfrac{t-1}{2t}\right\}.

Therefore

|\mathcal{A}_{q}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{k}|=t-1=\gcd\left(\frac{q-1}{2},k+1\right)-1.

A similar conclusion holds for
\mathcal{B}_{q}
. Note that
\mathcal{A}_{q}\cap\mathcal{B}_{q}=\{-2,2\}
. Since
p\nmid(k+1)
, then the inverse image of zero does not contain any of these elements. We have

|(Q_{A_{1}}^{k})^{-1}(\{0\})|=|(\mathcal{A}_{q}\cup\mathcal{B}_{q})\cap\mathcal{Z}_{k}|=|\mathcal{A}_{q}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{k}|+|\mathcal{B}_{q}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{k}|.

This finishes the proof.
∎

The inverse image of zero contains a single element if and only if one of the gcd-terms is one, and the other is two. Thus, the following is an immediate consequence of the above theorem.

Corollary 18
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an odd integer,
q
be a power of an odd prime
p
. Suppose that
p\nmid(k+1)
. Then, the inverse image of
\{0\}
under the map
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\to\mathbf{F}_{q}
contains a single element if and only if the following gcd-condition holds

\gcd\left(\frac{q-1}{2},k+1\right)\cdot\gcd\left(\frac{q+1}{2},k+1\right)=2.

We have now seen that, the map
Q^{k}_{A_{1}}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}
is not a permutation (
k\geq 3
is odd and
q
is a power of an odd prime
p
), if
p\mid(k+1)
or the gcd-condition of the above corollary fails. For the remaining cases, we introduce a method that uses the norms of certain elements from cyclotomic extensions.

Let
K
be a number field. Recall that the (field) norm of an element
\alpha\in K
is defined as the product of conjugates of
\alpha
. See
[
15
]
for more details. In general the conjugates may not remain in
K
. However, the cyclotomic extensions are normal extensions, and the conjugate elements are obtained by replacing each root of unity with a suitable power.

Let
\Phi_{n}(x)
denote the
n
-th cyclotomic polynomial. Its roots are primitive
n
-th roots of unity. By definition, we have

\Phi_{n}(x)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\\
\gcd(j,n)=1\end{subarray}}\left(x-\zeta_{n}^{j}\right).

We now state a well-known fact in the theory of cyclotomic fields. To simplify the notation in further statements, we let
\mathbf{Z}^{+}
to be the set of positive integers, and let
\mathbb{P}
be its subset consisting of prime numbers.

Proposition 19
.

Let
n
and
j
be positive integers such that
\gcd(j,n)=1
. Then,

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{n})}\left(1-\zeta_{n}^{j}\right)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }n=1,\\
l&\text{if }n=l^{a}\text{ for some }l\in\mathbb{P},a\in\mathbf{Z}^{+},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We outline the proof for the convenience of the reader. For
n=1
, the result is obvious. If
n>1
, and
\gcd(j,n)=1
, then

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{n})}\left(1-\zeta_{n}^{j}\right)=\Phi_{n}(1).

This is because
\Phi_{n}(x+1)
is the minimal polynomial of the element
1-\zeta_{n}^{j}
, and its constant term gives the norm up to a plus or a minus sign. However, the degree of
\Phi_{n}
is even, and the minus sign never occurs. There is a natural factorization
(x^{n}-1)=\prod_{d|n}\Phi_{d}(x)
.
Dividing both sides by
x-1=\Phi_{1}(x)
, and putting
x=1
, we obtain the equality

n=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d>1\end{subarray}}\Phi_{d}(1).

Let us focus on each factor
\Phi_{d}(1)
. There are two separate cases. If
d=l^{a}
for some
l\in\mathbb{P}
, and
a\in\mathbf{Z}^{+}
, then
\Phi_{l^{a}}(1)=l
. See
[
15
, Chapter 2]
for more details. If
d
has at least two prime factors, then
\Phi_{d}(1)=1
. This is achieved by canceling all the prime factors from
n=\prod\Phi_{d}(1)
by eliminating all
\Phi_{l^{a}}(1)=l
. See
[
20
, Proposition 2.8]
for more details.
∎

We will repeatedly use certain cosine values guided by the one-to-one correspondence (
2.7
). For this purpose, we define

\alpha_{d}:=\zeta_{d}+\zeta_{d}^{-1}=2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{d}\right).

We shall obtain a formula for the norm of
\alpha_{d}
. We achieve this by first calculating the norm in the extension
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})/\mathbf{Q}
and then recovering the norm in the smaller extension
\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{d})/\mathbf{Q}
. We recall that the field
\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{d})
is the maximal real subfield of
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})
and we have
[\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d}):\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{d})]=2
when
d>2
. Following
[
15
, Chapter 2]
, we can relate these norms through different extensions, using the relative degree, as follows

(3.2)

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\alpha_{d})=\left(N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{d})}(\alpha_{d})\right)^{2}.

The following theorem will be helpful while studying certain coefficients of a polynomial whose roots are conjugates of various elements of the form
\alpha_{d}
.

Theorem 20
.

The norm of
\alpha_{d}
is given by:

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{d})}(\alpha_{d})=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }d=1,\\
-2&\text{if }d=2,\\
0&\text{if }d=4,\\
\pm l&\text{if }d=4l^{a}\text{ for some }l\in\mathbb{P},a\in\mathbf{Z}^{+},\\
\pm 1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The cases where
d
equals
1,2,
or
4
are trivial as
\alpha_{1}=2
,
\alpha_{2}=-2
, and
\alpha_{4}=0
, respectively. For the remaining cases, we shall prove

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\alpha_{d})=\begin{cases}l^{2}&\text{if }d=4l^{a}\text{ for some }l\in\mathbb{P},a\in\mathbf{Z}^{+},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

in light of (
3.2
). We note the identity

\alpha_{d}=\zeta_{d}+\zeta_{d}^{-1}=\zeta_{d}^{-1}\left(\frac{1-\zeta_{d}^{4}}{1-\zeta_{d}^{2}}\right).

Using the multiplicativity of the norm, and the fact that
\zeta_{d}^{-1}
is a unit, we have

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\alpha_{d})=\pm\frac{N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}\left(1-\zeta_{d}^{4}\right)}{N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}\left(1-\zeta_{d}^{2}\right)}.

We want to utilize Proposition
19
. In this respect, we analyze the numerator and denominator for all cases of
d
. Starting with non-units, we first consider the cases
d=4l^{a}
where
l
is a prime number and
a
is a positive integer. We further separate this case by the prime’s parity.

If
d=2^{a}
with
a\geq 3
, the numerator is
1-\zeta_{2^{a}}^{4}=1-\zeta_{2^{a-2}}
and the denominator is
1-\zeta_{2^{a}}^{2}=1-\zeta_{2^{a-1}}
. Both have norm
2
in the minimal number fields containing them by Proposition
19
. Moreover, the relative degrees of these extension are

[\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{2^{a}}):\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{2^{a-2}})]=\varphi(2^{a})/\varphi(2^{a-2})=2^{a-1}/2^{a-3}=4

and

[\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{2^{a}}):\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{2^{a-1}})]=\varphi(2^{a})/\varphi(2^{a-1})=2^{a-1}/2^{a-2}=2.

Therefore, we obtain
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\alpha_{d})=2^{4}/2^{2}=2^{2}
by using relative norms.

If
d=4l^{a}
where
l
is an odd prime and
a\geq 1
, the numerator is
1-\zeta_{4l^{a}}^{4}=1-\zeta_{l^{a}}
and the denominator is
1-\zeta_{4l^{a}}^{2}=1-\zeta_{2l^{a}}
. By Proposition
19
, the numerator has norm
l
in the field
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{l^{a}})
. On the other hand, the denominator is a unit in the field
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{2l^{a}})=\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{l^{a}})
. We have

[\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{4l^{a}}):\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{l^{a}})]=\varphi(4l^{a})/\varphi(l^{a})=\varphi(4)=2.

Therefore,
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\alpha_{d})=l^{2}/(\pm 1)^{2}=l^{2}
by using relative norms.

For the remaining cases that yield units, we explain the main ideas and omit the details. If
d=l^{a}
or
d=2l^{a}
where
l
is an odd prime, both
1-\zeta_{d}^{4}
and
1-\zeta_{d}^{2}
have the same norm, namely
l
, in the minimal number field containing them, namely
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{l^{a}})
. If
d=m
or
d=2m
where
m
is an odd integer that is not a prime power, both the numerator and the denominator have norm
1
. This also holds for
d=4m
where
m
is not a prime power, regardless of its parity.
∎

Theorem 23

Let

be an odd integer and

Any element of

\alpha_{d}

The equivalence class of

\prod\limits_{\alpha\in\textnormal{Fix}(P_{A_{1}}^{q})\setminus\{0\}}\alpha=\prod\limits_{d\in S\setminus\{4\}}N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{d})}(\alpha_{d})

4.
The Main Result

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semi-simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of rank
n
. Recall that we use the notation
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathfrak{g}}^{k}:\mathbf{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}^{n}
, for the generalized Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind. These polynomials are defined through exponential invariants of Bourbaki, and have integer coefficients. To our knowledge, these polynomials
Q^{k}_{\mathfrak{g}}
, with
\mathfrak{g}\neq A_{1}
, have not been studied in the theory of finite fields before this work. See
[
8
]
, and the references therein, for some applications in numerical analysis.

The classical Chebyshev polynomials of the first and second kinds, i.e.
P_{A_{1}}^{k}
and
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}
, satisfy the same recursive relation with different initial conditions, see (
1.4
). This slight difference results in distinct behavior in terms of arithmetic exceptionality. The polynomials
P_{A_{1}}^{k}
are examples of exceptional polynomials for infinitely many
k
. On the other hand,
Q_{A_{1}}^{k}
is not exceptional for
k>1
.

In this paper, we show that there is a similar situation in the rank two case. The bivariate polynomial families
P_{A_{2}}^{k}
and
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}
satisfy the same recursive relation with different initial conditions. See (
1.5
), and Theorem
13
. Moreover, the polynomials
P_{A_{2}}^{k}
are examples of exceptional polynomials for infinitely many values of
k
, see Theorem
2
.

In this section, we will provide a criterion that concludes
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}
is not a permutation if
p
is sufficiently large compared to a certain function of
k
. More precisely, we will show the following.

Theorem 24
.

Let
k>1
be an integer. Let
q
be a power of an odd prime
p
. Let
\tau
be the function defined by (
4.2
). If
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}
is a permutation, then
p<|\tau(k)|+1
. Moreover,
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}
is not arithmetically exceptional.

To establish exceptionality, one needs to know if the map
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{p}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{p}^{2}
yields permutations for infinitely many primes
p
. In this section, we use the correspondence
\mathbf{F}_{q}\leftrightarrow\textnormal{Fix}(P_{A_{1}}^{q})
given by (
2.7
). Our main strategy is to reduce the problem to a one-dimensional setting and focus on the diagonal of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}
. For this purpose, we start with the following definition.

Definition 25
.

We define
f_{k}(x):=h_{k}(x,x)
, and call it the
diagonal restriction
of
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}
, by choosing
y_{1}=y_{2}=x
in Example
12
. There is a recursive relation for
f_{k}
given by Theorem
13
. More precisely,
f_{0}=1,f_{1}=x
, and
f_{2}=x^{2}-x
and

f_{k}=xf_{k-1}-xf_{k-2}+f_{k-3},\text{ for }k\geq 3.

Remark 26
.

Consider the diagonal
D=\{(a,a)\mathrel{|}a\in\mathbf{F}_{q}\}
of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}
. We have
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}(D)\subseteq D
by (
2.5
). This allows us to consider the restricted map
f_{k}:D\rightarrow D
. If
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}
permutes
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}
, then its diagonal restriction
f_{k}
permutes
\mathbf{F}_{q}
. Equivalently, if
f_{k}
does not permute
\mathbf{F}_{q}
, then
Q^{k}_{A_{2}}
does not permute
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}
.

We start with collecting some information about the polynomials
f_{k}
.

Theorem 27
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an arbitrary integer. Then we have

\displaystyle f_{k}(0)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }k\equiv 0\pmod{3},\\
0&\text{if }k\equiv 1,2\pmod{3},\end{cases}

\displaystyle f_{k}(1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }k\equiv 0,1\pmod{4},\\
0&\text{if }k\equiv 2,3\pmod{4},\end{cases}

\displaystyle f_{k}(2)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }k\equiv 0,3\pmod{6},\\
2&\text{if }k\equiv 1,2\pmod{6},\\
0&\text{if }k\equiv 4,5\pmod{6}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We start the proof with the first case. Putting
x=0
, we obtain
f_{0}(0)=1
,
f_{1}(0)=0
, and
f_{2}(0)=0^{2}-0=0
. Moreover

f_{k}(0)=f_{k-3}(0),\text{ for }k\geq 3.

The result now follows easily.

Secondly, let us consider the case with
x=1
. We have
f_{0}(1)=1
,
f_{1}(1)=1
, and
f_{2}(1)=1^{2}-1=0
. Moreover

f_{k}(1)=f_{k-1}(1)-f_{k-2}(1)+f_{k-3}(1),\text{ for }k\geq 3.

It follows that

as claimed. It is straightforward to verify that the general pattern holds modulo four.

Finally, we consider the case with
x=2
. We have
f_{0}(2)=1
,
f_{1}(2)=2
, and
f_{2}(2)=2^{2}-2=2
. Moreover

f_{k}(2)=2f_{k-1}(2)-2f_{k-2}(2)+f_{k-3}(2),\text{ for }k\geq 3.

It can be verified that the pattern

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\alpha_{1}&=&2\omega_{1}-\omega_{2},\\
\alpha_{2}&=&-\omega_{1}+2\omega_{2},\end{array}\quad\text{ and }\quad\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega_{1}&=&(2/3)\alpha_{1}+(1/3)\alpha_{2},\\
\omega_{2}&=&(1/3)\alpha_{1}+(2/3)\alpha_{2}.\end{array}

The product formula requires the use of positive simple roots, and we need to make a suitable conversion. Observe that

\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\

. This computation provides a new vector

\tilde{\gamma}=\left(1+\frac{2k}{3}\right)u\alpha_{1}^{\vee}+\left(1+\frac{k}{3}\right)u\alpha_{2}^{\vee}

with the property that
J\left(e^{(k+1)\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}\right)(\gamma)=J\left(e^{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}\right)\left(\tilde{\gamma}\right).

∎

Now, we focus on some other special cases that are not described by Theorem
27
. Suppose that
u
is not congruent to
0
or
1/2
modulo
\mathbf{Z}
. Using
\gamma=u\alpha_{1}^{\vee}+u\alpha_{2}^{\vee}
and (
4.1
), we see that
y_{1}(\gamma)
is equal to
3
and
-1
, respectively. The images of these elements under the polynomial
f_{k}
is given by the following theorem.

Corollary 30
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an arbitrary integer. Then we have

\displaystyle f_{k}(3)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}

\displaystyle f_{k}(-1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(k+2)/2&\text{if }k\equiv 0\pmod{2},\\
-(k+1)/2&\text{if }k\equiv 1\pmod{2}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The values
u=0
, and
u=1/2
correspond to
\beta_{0}=3
, and
\beta_{2}=-1
, respectively. We shall use the expressions of the previous theorem, but the cancellations must be performed before plugging in the values for
u
. In this respect, it is very similar to the computation of the boundary values as in the analogous case (
2.8
).

To make the cancellations more transparent, we substitute
t=e^{2\pi iu}
into the formula of Theorem
29
, and obtain

f_{k}\left(t^{-1}\dfrac{1-t^{3}}{1-t}\right)=t^{-k}\frac{(1-t^{k+1})(1-t^{k+2})}{(1-t)(1-t^{2})}.

The integers
k+1
and
k+2
come with different parity. If
k+1
is even, then we have

\frac{1-t^{k+1}}{1-t^{2}}=1+t^{2}+\ldots+t^{(k+1)/2}\text{ and }\frac{1-t^{k+2}}{1-t}=1+t+\ldots+t^{k+2}.

Similar formulas occur if
k+2
is even. If we put
u=0
, and therefore
t=1
, then we see that
f_{k}(3)=(k+1)(k+2)/2
. In the other case, we have
u=1/2
, and therefore
t=-1
. Doing a case-by-case analysis, we deduce the desired formula for
f_{k}(-1)
.
∎

The values
f_{k}(-1)
depend on the parity of
k
and are not very convenient to express within more complicated formulas. We use the following formula for simplicity

f_{k}(-1)=\frac{1+(-1)^{k}(2k+3)}{4}.

While correct, this formula masks the role played by the integers
k+1
and
k+2
in the values of
f_{k}(-1)
.

Corollary 31
.

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p\geq 5
, and let
k>1
be an integer with
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
. If
p\mid(k+1)(k+2)
, then
f_{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}
is not a permutation.

Proof.

We have
f_{k}(0)=0
by Theorem
27
, and
f_{k}(3)=0
by Corollary
30
. The elements
0
and
3
are distinct in
\mathbf{F}_{q}
since
p\geq 5
.
∎

We make one final restriction by introducing a gcd-condition as in the previous section. This result will be helpful to simplify the proof of Theorem
34
.

Theorem 32
.

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p\geq 5
and let
k>1
be an integer with
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
and
p\nmid(k+1)(k+2)
. Then the inverse image of
\{0\}
under the map
f_{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\to\mathbf{F}_{q}
contains a single element if and only if the following gcd-condition holds

\gcd\left(\frac{(q-1)(q+1)}{3},\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{6}\right)=1.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
17
, we focus on the following sets

\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{i}:=\left\{2\cos\left(2\pi\frac{d}{k+i}\right)\mid c\in\mathbf{Z}\right\},\quad i=1,2,

guided by Theorem
29
.

Since the values of
k
are highly constrained, one can extract useful information about the inverse image of zero. For instance
f_{k}(0)=0
,
f_{k}(1)=1\neq 0
, and
f_{k}(2)=2\neq 0
by Theorem
27
. Moreover,
f_{k}(-1)\neq 0
and
f_{k}(3)\neq 0
by Corollary
30
. We have the following table of values

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr d&1&6&4&3&2\\
\hline\cr u&0&1/6&1/4&1/3&1/2\\
\hline\cr\alpha_{d}=2\cos(2\pi u)&2&1&0&-1&-2\\
\hline\cr\beta_{d}=1+2\cos(2\pi u)&3&2&1&0&-1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Recall the one-to-one correspondence (
2.7
).
Under the hypotheses of the theorem, we shall show that gcd-condition of the theorem is equivalent to the following

(\mathcal{A}_{q}\cup\mathcal{B}_{q})\cap(\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{1}\cup\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{2})=\{-1\}.

Suppose that this intersection contains an element
2\cos(2\pi u)
, other than
\alpha_{3}=-1
, where
u\in(0,1/2)
is a unique rational number. If
u=a/b
is written in its lowest terms, then the denominator
b
cannot be
1,2,3,4
, or
6
by the above table. We further write, for some unique integers
c
and
d
, that

u=\frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{q\pm 1}=\frac{d}{k+i},\quad i=1,2,

using the definitions of the sets within the above intersection.

If
b
is divisible by a prime
l\geq 5
, then the prime
l
divides both
q\pm 1
and
k+i
for some choice of a plus or minus sign and
i\in\{1,2\}
. It follows that the prime
l
divides the expression
\gcd((q-1)(q+1),(k+1)(k+2))
. This implies that the gcd-condition of the theorem fails.

It remains to consider the cases that
b
is divisible by
8
,
9
, or
12
according to the above discussion.

Suppose that
9\mid b
. Then
\gcd((q-1)(q+1),(k+1)(k+2))
is divisible by
9
. This implies that the gcd-condition of the theorem fails. Indeed, we have
f_{k}(\beta_{9})=0
if and only if
9\mid k+1
or
9\mid k+2
. Moreover
\beta_{9}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}}
is an element of
\mathbf{F}_{q}
if and only if
9\mid q-1
or
9\mid q+1
.

The other cases, namely
8\mid b
and
12\mid b
, do not occur. Recall that
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
. It can be verified that
f_{k}(\beta_{8})
and
f_{k}(\beta_{12})
are never zero modulo
\mathfrak{p}
.
∎

Now we shall focus on the norms of the elements
\beta_{d}=1+\zeta_{d}+\zeta_{d}^{-1}
and
f_{k}(\beta)
. We generalize the method in the previous section. Recall that
\mathbf{Z}^{+}
is the set of positive integers, and
\mathbb{P}
is its subset consisting of prime numbers. We start with the following theorem, which is analogous to Theorem
20
.

Theorem 33
.

The norm of
\beta_{d}
is given by:

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\beta_{d})}(\beta_{d})=\begin{cases}3&\text{if }d=1,\\
0&\text{if }d=3,\\
\pm l&\text{if }d=3l^{a}\text{ for some }l\in\mathbb{P},a\in\mathbf{Z}^{+},\\
\pm 1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The cases where
d
equals 1 or 3 are trivial as
\beta_{1}=3
and
\beta_{3}=0
. For the remaining cases, we shall prove

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\beta_{d})=\begin{cases}l^{2}&\text{if }d=3l^{a}\text{ for some }l\in\mathbb{P},a\in\mathbf{Z}^{+},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

in light of (
3.2
). We note the identity

\beta_{d}=1+\zeta_{d}+\zeta_{d}^{-1}=\zeta_{d}^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1-\zeta_{d}^{3}}{1-\zeta_{d}}\right)

Using the multiplicativity of the norm and the fact that
\zeta_{d}^{-1}
is a unit, we have the following:

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\beta_{d})=\pm\frac{N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}\left(1-\zeta_{d}^{3}\right)}{N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}\left(1-\zeta_{d}\right)}.

We want to utilize Proposition
19
. We analyze the numerator and denominator for each case of
d
.

If
3\nmid d
, then the numerator
1-\zeta_{d}^{3}
and denominator
1-\zeta_{d}
have the same norm by Proposition
19
. Hence,
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\beta_{d})=1
.

If
d=3m
with
m>1
not a prime power, then the numerator
1-\zeta_{d}^{3}=1-\zeta_{m}
and the denominator
1-\zeta_{d}
are units by Proposition
19
. Hence,
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\beta_{d})=1
.

If
d=3^{a}
with
a>1
, the numerator is
1-\zeta_{3^{a}}^{3}=1-\zeta_{3^{a-1}}
and the denominator is
1-\zeta_{3^{a}}
. By Proposition
19
, both have norm
3
in the minimal fields containing them. Moreover, we have the following relative degree

[\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{3^{a}}):\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{3^{a-1}})]=\varphi(3^{a})/\varphi(3^{a-1})=3^{a-1}/3^{a-2}=3

Therefore, we have
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\beta)=3^{3}/3=3^{2}
by using relative norms.

If
d=3l^{a}
where
l\neq 3
is a prime and
a\geq 1
, the numerator is
1-\zeta_{3l^{a}}^{3}=1-\zeta_{l^{a}}
and the denominator is
1-\zeta_{3l^{a}}
. By Proposition
19
, the numerator has norm
l
in the field
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{l^{a}})
, and the denominator is a unit in the field
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{3l^{a}})
. Moreover, we have the following relative degree

[\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{3l^{a}}):\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{l^{a}})]=\varphi(3l^{a})/\varphi(l^{a})=\varphi(3)=2.

Therefore, we have
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}(\beta)=l^{2}/1=l^{2}
by using relative norms.
∎

The next theorem follows the ideas introduced in the previous section, see Theorem
22
. For simplicity, we consider only the remaining cases of
k
that may possibly produce permutations of
\mathbf{F}_{q}
. A notable difference from the analogous result is that the gcd-condition depends on
k+2
as well as
k+1
.

Theorem 34
.

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p\geq 5
and let
k>1
be an integer with
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
. Suppose further that
p\nmid(k+1)(k+2)
and

\gcd\left(\frac{(q-1)(q+1)}{3},\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{6}\right)=1.

If
d
is a divisor of
q-1
or
q+1
, then the norm of
f_{k}(\beta_{d})
is given by:

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\beta_{d})}\left(f_{k}(\beta_{d})\right)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}&\text{if }d=1,\\
\dfrac{1+(-1)^{k}(2k+3)}{4}&\text{if }d=2,\\
0&\text{if }d=3,\\
\pm l&\text{if }d=3l^{a}\text{ for some }l\in\mathbb{P},a\in\mathbf{Z}^{+},\\
\pm 1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The cases where
d
equals 1 or 2 are obtained by Corollary
30
. The case
d=3
is obtained by Theorem
27
. For the remaining cases, we shall prove

N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}\left(f_{k}(\beta_{d})\right)=\begin{cases}l^{2}&\text{if }d=3l^{a}\text{ for some }l\in\mathbb{P},a\in\mathbf{Z}^{+},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

in light of (
3.2
). Using Theorem
29
, we obtain the identity

f_{k}(\beta_{d})=\zeta_{d}^{-k}\dfrac{\left(1-\zeta_{d}^{k+1}\right)\left(1-\zeta_{d}^{k+2}\right)}{\left(1-\zeta_{d}\right)\left(1-\zeta_{d}^{2}\right)}

In order to compute the norm of this element, we want to utilize Proposition
19
. For this purpose, we consider various cases of
d
.

Suppose that
3\nmid d
. For some
i
and
j
, with
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
, we assume that
k+i
is odd and
k+j
is even. Note that
k+j
is not divisible by
4
since
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
. The gcd-condition now implies that the following conjugacy relations hold:

1-\zeta_{d}\sim 1-\zeta_{d}^{k+i}\quad\text{ and }\quad 1-\zeta_{d}^{2}\sim 1-\zeta_{d}^{k+j}.

Hence, we conclude that
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}\left(f_{k}(\beta_{d})\right)=1
.

Suppose that
d=3m>3
where
m
is not divisible by three, and
m
is not a prime power. The terms in the denominator, namely
1-\zeta_{d}
and
1-\zeta_{d}^{2}
are units by Proposition
19
. Now, let us focus on the terms in the numerator, namely
1-\zeta_{d}^{k+1}
and
1-\zeta_{d}^{k+2}
. We claim that they are units as well. Assume otherwise, and suppose that
1-\zeta_{d}^{k+i}
is not a unit for some
i=1,2
. Then this means that at least two prime factors are canceled from
d=3m
. Recall that
d
is a divisor of
q-1
or
q+1
, and the gcd-condition puts a severe restriction on
k+1
and
k+2
values. We must have
6\mid\gcd(d,k+i)
. However,
k+i
, for some
i=1,2
, is not divisible by
6
since
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
. Hence, we conclude that
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}\left(f_{k}(\beta_{d})\right)=1
.

Suppose that
d=3^{a}
with
a\geq 2
. The integer
k
is not divisible by three since
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
. For some
i
and
j
, with
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
, we assume that
k+i
is divisible by there and
k+j
is not divisible by three. The gcd-condition implies that the element
1-\zeta_{d}^{k+i}
is conjugate to
1-\zeta_{d}^{3}
. Note that both
1-\zeta_{d}
and
1-\zeta_{d}^{3}=1-\zeta_{3^{a-1}}
have norm
3
in the minimal fields containing them by Proposition
19
. Moreover, we have the following relative degree

[\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{3^{a}}):\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{3^{a-1}})]=\varphi(3^{a})/\varphi(3^{a-1})=3^{a-1}/3^{a-2}=3

On the other hand, the elements

1-\zeta_{d}^{2}
and
1-\zeta_{d}^{k+j}
are conjugates of each other, and their contribution to the norm computation is trivial. Therefore, we have
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}\left(f_{k}(\beta_{d})\right)=3^{3}/3=3^{2}
by using relative norms.

Finally, suppose that
d=3l^{a}
where
l\neq 3
is a prime and
a\geq 1
. The term
1-\zeta_{d}
in the denominator is a unit by Proposition
19
. For some
i
and
j
, with
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
, we assume that
k+i
is divisible by there and
k+j
is not divisible by three. Since
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
, the integer
k+i
is odd and
k+j
is even. We have
1-\zeta_{d}^{k+i}=1-\zeta_{l^{a}}^{(k+i)/3}
. The gcd-condition implies that
1-\zeta_{d}^{k+i}
is a conjugate of
1-\zeta_{l^{a}}
. Here, it is important that
k+i
is odd. By Proposition
19
, the element
1-\zeta_{l^{a}}
has norm
l
in the field
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{l^{a}})
. Moreover, we have the following relative degree

[\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{3l^{a}}):\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{l^{a}})]=\varphi(3l^{a})/\varphi(l^{a})=\varphi(3)=2.

Recall that
k+j
is not divisible by three. We observe that
k+j
is even but not divisible by four. It follows that the elements
1-\zeta_{d}^{2}
and
1-\zeta_{d}^{k+j}
are conjugates of each other, and their contribution to the norm computation is trivial. We finally conclude that
N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{d})}\left(f_{k}(\beta_{d})\right)=l^{2}/1=l^{2}
by using relative norms.
∎

The following theorem is the last step before we prove our main result. To ease the notation, we define the following function

(4.2)

\tau(k)=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)\left(1+(-1)^{k}(2k+3)\right)}{24}.

For a fixed value of
k
, the function
|\tau(k)|+1
gives an upper bound on primes
p
for which our main result, see Theorem
24
, is inconclusive.

Theorem 35
.

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p\geq 5
and let
k>1
be an integer with
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
. Suppose further that
p\nmid(k+1)(k+2)
and

\gcd\left(\frac{(q-1)(q+1)}{3},\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{6}\right)=1.

Let
\mathcal{F}=1+\textnormal{Fix}(P_{A_{1}}^{q})
be the set obtained by translating
\textnormal{Fix}(P_{A_{1}}^{q})
by one. Then we have

\prod\limits_{\beta\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{0\}}f_{k}(\beta)=\pm\tau(k)(q\pm 1).

Moreover, the plus or minus sign within the last term is uniquely determined by the condition
6|(q\pm 1)
.

Proof.

Any element of
\mathcal{F}=1+\textnormal{Fix}(P_{A_{1}}^{q})
is a conjugate of
\beta_{d}=1+\alpha_{d}
for some divisor
d
of
q-1
or
q+1
. Moreover, all conjugates of
\beta_{d}
are included in the number field
\mathbf{Q}(\alpha_{d})=\mathbf{Q}(\beta_{d})
. This is a direct consequence of the one-to-one correspondence given by (
2.7
).

Let
S
be the subset of positive integers
1\leq d\leq q+1
such that
\beta_{d}\in 1+\textnormal{Fix}(P_{A_{1}}^{q})
. The set
S
consists of divisors of
q-1
or
q+1
by (
2.7
). We have the following partition induced by the conjugacy equivalence relation

\mathcal{F}=1+\textnormal{Fix}(P_{A_{1}}^{q})=\bigcup_{d\in S}[\beta_{d}]

The equivalence class of
\beta_{3}=0
has a single element since
\beta_{3}\in\mathbf{Q}
. Symbolically, we have
[\beta_{3}]=\{0\}
. It follows that

\prod\limits_{\beta\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{0\}}\beta=\prod\limits_{d\in S\setminus\{3\}}N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\beta_{d})}\left(\beta_{d}\right).

Repeating a similar computation, we also obtain

\prod\limits_{\beta\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{0\}}f_{k}(\beta)=\prod\limits_{d\in S\setminus\{3\}}N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\beta_{d})}\left(f_{k}(\beta_{d})\right).

The right-hand sides of the above products can be related to each other by using Theorem
33
, and Theorem
34
together. More precisely, we have

\prod\limits_{d\in S\setminus\{3\}}N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\beta_{d})}\left(f_{k}(\beta_{d})\right)=\pm\tau(k)\prod\limits_{d\in S\setminus\{3\}}N_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathbf{Q}(\beta_{d})}\left(\beta_{d}\right).

If
p\neq 3
, then we observe that
P_{A_{1}}^{q}(x-1)=x^{q}+\ldots\pm qx-1
.
The product of nonzero elements of
\mathcal{F}
is equal to
\pm q\pm 1
. Obviously, the term
q\pm 1
is divisible by two. The divisibility by three follows from Remark
21
together with Theorem
33
.
∎

We are ready now ready to exhibit a proof for the main result of this paper.

Proof of Theorem
24
.

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p\geq 5
and let
k>1
be an integer. If
k\not\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
, then the map
f_{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}
is not a permutation by Corollary
28
. If
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
but
p\mid(k+1)(k+1)
, then the map
f_{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}
is not a permutation by Corollary
31
. Suppose otherwise, and consider the gcd-condition of Theorem
32
given below

\gcd\left(\frac{(q-1)(q+1)}{3},\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{6}\right)=1.

If this gcd-condition does not hold, then the map
f_{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}
is not a permutation since the inverse image of
\{0\}
contains more than one element.

It remains to consider the case where
k\equiv 1,8\pmod{12}
,
p\nmid(k+1)(k+1)
, and the gcd-condition above holds. Under these hypotheses, we can use Theorem
35
. We have

\prod\limits_{\beta\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{0\}}f_{k}(\beta)=\pm\tau(k)(q\pm 1).

This computation gives the
x
-coefficient of the following polynomial

\prod\limits_{\beta\in\mathbf{F}_{q}}\left(x-f_{k}(\beta)\right)

up to a plus or a minus sign. Suppose that
p>|\tau(k)|+1
. Finally, we see that

\prod\limits_{\beta\in\mathbf{F}_{q}}\left(x-f_{k}(\beta)\right)\not\equiv x^{q}-x\pmod{p}.

Thus, the map
f_{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}
is not a permutation. We also conclude that the map
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}:\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}
is not a permutation by following Remark
26
.

For a fixed value of
k
, there are only finitely many primes
p
such that the inequality
p\leq|\tau(k)|+1
holds. We conclude that
Q_{A_{2}}^{k}
is not exceptional for
k>1
.
∎

Remark 36
.

We hope that the ideas of this work can be further generalized to other second kind generalized Chebyshev polynomials associated with arbitrary semi-simple Lie algebras. After completing the study of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, one may naturally turn to the next simplest cases, namely
B_{2}
and
G_{2}
. However, there is an immediate difficulty. In those cases, the property (
2.5
) is no longer true. In other words, at first glance, restricting the functions
Q_{B_{2}}^{k}
or
Q_{G_{2}}^{k}
to the diagonal of
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{2}
does not appear to be a useful approach.
