Screened second-order exchange in the uniform electron gas: exact reduction, a single-pole reference model and asymptotic analysis
Fumihiro Imoto

Abstract
We derive an exact reduction of the screened second-order exchange (SOSEX) energy in the uniform electron gas to a triple integral for a specific class of single-pole screened interaction. The reduction proceeds by rescaling the frequency variable to factorize the propagator denominators, applying a Fourier decomposition to separate the two particle-hole blocks, and finally performing a change of integration variables that brings the geometric structure into a tractable form. The reduction to a one-variable integral kernel is possible if and only if the screened interaction belongs to a one-pole class characterized by a single momentum-independent frequency scale μ \mu , which we call the reduction-compatible single-pole (RC-SP) model. The RC-SP model does not approximate plasmon dispersions in real materials, but provides an exactly reducible reference model for analyzing dynamically screened exchange, and gives natural basis elements for approximating more general one-pole screening. We analyze the μ \mu -dependence of the SOSEX energy asymptotically at both small and large μ \mu and establish the leading behaviors at the theorem level. Under a power-law mapping from μ \mu to the density parameter r s r_{s} , this asymptotic structure constrains the analytic form of the screened-exchange correction in r s r_{s} -space, providing a diagrammatically justified basis for beyond-RPA functional construction. Direct numerical integration of the reduced representation confirms the asymptotic behaviors quantitatively.



I
Introduction

The practical success of density functional theory
(DFT)
11
;
13
;
14
;
15

depends on the accuracy of the exchange-correlation energy functional.
The uniform electron gas (UEG) is the most widely used reference system
for constructing exchange-correlation functionals under the local density
approximation (LDA)
16
;
4
.
Most non-empirical LDA functionals are built upon the exchange-correlation
energy of the UEG computed via quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) simulations by
Ceperley and Alder
3
, and fitted using Padé or
rational interpolations constrained to reproduce known asymptotic forms at
high and low density
21
;
19
;
20
.
This strategy works well in practice, but the choice of analytic form is
guided by educated guess rather than by diagrammatic structure.
It is therefore desirable to understand which perturbative contributions
are essential in each density regime and what analytic forms they naturally
produce.

In the high-density regime, the random phase approximation (RPA)
— the simplest resummation of all ring diagrams — captures long-range
screening and collective excitations and serves as a reasonable starting
point
5
;
1
.
However, RPA lacks short-range exchange-like contributions and leaves
systematic errors in absolute correlation
energies
9
;
12
;
6
.
The screened second-order exchange (SOSEX) correction is a natural
candidate to remedy this: it connects the long-range screening of RPA
with the exchange-like correlation that RPA
misses
9
;
12
;
6
.
Full treatments of SOSEX within the adiabatic
connection–fluctuation-dissipation framework are
available
9
;
6
, but the high-dimensional
integrals involved make it difficult to extract the analytic structure
of the correction in closed form.

The goal of this paper is to analyze the dynamically screened exchange
diagram in an analytically controlled setting, with the aim of deriving
a diagram-constrained asymptotic basis for the beyond-RPA
screened-exchange correction.
Our starting point is a well-defined diagrammatic model quantity derived
from the finite-temperature grand potential in the Matsubara formalism,
whose zero-temperature limit captures the topology of the screened
second-order exchange.
We emphasize that this quantity is not the completed correlation energy
of the Coulomb system — the grand potential and the ground-state energy
are related but distinct thermodynamic quantities — and we do not give
it a completed parent approximation in the sense of the
Luttinger–Ward
17
or
Almbladh–Barth–van Leeuwen
2
functional.
Instead, we treat it as a building block for extracting the shape and
density dependence of the screened-exchange correction.

A central technical problem is that reducing this high-dimensional diagram
to a tractable integral form depends critically on the structure of the
screened interaction.
For a general frequency-dependent one-pole screened interaction, the
natural outcome of the reduction is a transform that depends on two
integration variables simultaneously, and this two-variable dependence
cannot be collapsed to a simpler one-variable form.
We prove that the only class of one-pole screening for which exact
reduction to a one-variable kernel is possible is the one where the
characteristic frequency scale is independent of momentum — a condition
we call the reduction-compatible single-pole (RC-SP) model.
This is the minimal reference model in which the dynamically screened
exchange diagram can be analyzed exactly, and it is the model studied
throughout this paper.
Even for general one-pole screening outside this class, the RC-SP kernels
serve as analytically controllable basis elements for a finite-rank
separable approximation, so the results of this paper are not limited to
a single special case.

The exact reduction is carried out by combining three analytical steps in
sequence.
The first is a rescaling of the frequency variable that factorizes the
particle-hole propagator denominators into a form amenable to further
reduction.
The second is a Fourier factorization of the Coulomb exchange kernel,
which separates the two particle-hole blocks and reduces the problem to
a single-block function.
The third is the Schwinger representation for the propagators, followed
by a centered affine transformation of the resulting integration variables.
Together these steps reduce the full diagram to a one-dimensional integral
weighted by a kernel that encodes all the dynamic information, and a
geometric double integral in prolate spheroidal coordinates that involves
only spherical Bessel functions.
The static limit of this construction, in which the dynamic screening is
turned off, reproduces the known bare second-order exchange result, and
we use the Onsager–Mittag–Stephen value
18
for the
unpolarized UEG as the normalization anchor.

The asymptotic behavior of the screened-exchange correction as a function
of the screening parameter is then analyzed via the Mellin–Barnes
representation.
The pole structure of the associated Mellin transform — which is
determined directly by the geometry of the diagram — dictates the
allowed asymptotic forms at both strong and weak screening.
We establish these forms at the theorem level, with explicit justification
of the required analytic steps and quantitative control of the remainders.
The leading behavior at weak screening is linear in the screening
parameter, while the approach to the static limit at strong screening is
governed by a logarithmically modified power law.
Direct numerical evaluation of the exact reduced representation confirms
both asymptotic regimes quantitatively.
When the screening parameter is mapped to the density parameter
r_{s}

through a scale-free power-law ansatz, the Mellin pole structure
translates into a power-log family of basis functions in
r_{s}
-space.
The specific power-law exponent is not fixed from first principles in our
framework, but the diagram topology constrains the admissible analytic
forms of the screened-exchange residual correction, providing a
diagrammatically justified skeleton for beyond-RPA functional construction.

II
Screened second-order exchange and our problem setting

II.1
Definition from finite-temperature perturbation theory

In this article, we define the screened second-order exchange as an exchange-like second-order diagram in which one of two static interaction lines is replaced by a frequency-dependent screened interaction. Hereafter, we use the terms “dynamic” and “static” to denote frequency-dependent and frequency-independent screened interactions, respectively.

Starting from an exchange-like second-order contribution to the grand potential in the Matsubara formalism, we use the following notations. At finite temperature
T=\beta^{-1}
, the bosonic Matsubara frequency is defined by

\nu_{\ell}=\frac{2\pi\ell}{\beta},\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z}.

(1)

We consider a three-dimensional unpolarized uniform electron gas. All momenta are made dimensionless by
k_{F}
, and convention-dependent overall factors such as
(2\pi)^{-3}
, spin degeneracy, volume factor, and the Coulomb constant are absorbed into a single prefactor
A_{0}
. We then define the finite-temperature grand-potential contribution corresponding to the screened second-order exchange as

\Omega_{T}^{\mathrm{scr2x}}[D]=A_{0}\,\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\nu_{\ell}}\int d^{3}q\;D(q,\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})\,X_{T}(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell}),

(2)

where

X_{T}(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})=\int d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\,Q_{T}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})\,Q_{T}(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell}),

(3)

and each particle–hole block is given by

Q_{T}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})=\frac{n_{F}(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}})-n_{F}(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}})}{\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell}-\bigl(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}\bigr)}.

(4)

Here
n_{F}
denotes the Fermi distribution function. If we write the expression keeping the fermionic Matsubara frequencies
\omega_{n}=(2n+1)\pi/\beta
explicit, then the definition Eq. (
2
)–(
4
) corresponds to the exchange-like vacuum diagram

\displaystyle\Omega_{T}^{\mathrm{scr2x}}[D]

\displaystyle\propto\frac{1}{\beta^{3}}\sum_{\nu_{\ell},\omega_{n},\omega_{m}}\int d^{3}q\,d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;D(q,\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})\,\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times G_{0}(\mathbf{p},\mathrm{i}\omega_{n})\,G_{0}(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}+\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})\,G_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathrm{i}\omega_{m})\,G_{0}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\omega_{m}+\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell}),

(5)

in which the screened interaction line
D
carrying bosonic transfer
(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})
and the bare Coulomb line
|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{-2}
carrying momentum difference
(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})
cross each other and connect two particle–hole blocks. Performing the sums over
\omega_{n}
and
\omega_{m}
yields Eq. (
2
)–(
4
). Therefore, our starting expression is not the second-order term of strict bare MBPT but corresponds to the exchange-like second-order diagram of a reorganized perturbation theory that contains one screened interaction line.

The fundamental thermodynamic quantity that appears naturally in the finite-temperature Matsubara formalism is the grand potential
\Omega(T,\mu,V)
, which satisfies

\Omega=E-TS-\mu N

(6)

in general. Therefore, even in the limit
T\to 0
,
\Omega
and
E
differ essentially by
\mu N
. For this reason, we cannot in general identify a contribution to
\Omega
obtained from a finite-temperature diagrammatic expansion directly with the actual zero-temperature correlation energy.

In this paper, we introduce a finite-temperature model quantity
\Omega_{T}^{\mathrm{scr2x}}[D]
corresponding to the screened-exchange topology and use its zero-temperature limit

\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{scr2x}}^{\,\mathrm{UEG}}[D]:=\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{1}{N}\Omega_{T}^{\mathrm{scr2x}}[D]

(7)

as a grand-potential-derived diagrammatic model quantity. The important point is that Eq. (
7
) does not define the completed correlation energy of the actual Coulomb system but gives a building block for extracting the shape and density dependence of the screened-exchange correction.

This standpoint is intentionally distinguished from the completed construction in which RPA correlation energy is derived from the full framework of the adiabatic connection and the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
9
;
6
;
5
. Since we do not yet give a fully conserving parent approximation based on the Luttinger–Ward functional
17
or the Almbladh–Barth–van Leeuwen functional
2
, we do not call Eq. (
7
) a physical exchange-correlation energy. Instead, we regard it as a zero-temperature model quantity for constructing an adiabatic-connection-defined reference correction.

II.2
From Matsubara summation to zero-temperature imaginary-axis integration

In order to take the zero-temperature limit of Eq. (
2
), it is safest to understand the Matsubara summation as a contour integral in the complex plane. We define

\mathcal{F}_{T}(z):=A_{0}\int d^{3}q\;D(q,z)\,X_{T}(\mathbf{q},z),

(8)

so that

\Omega_{T}^{\mathrm{scr2x}}[D]=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\nu_{\ell}}\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell}).

(9)

By the standard contour formula for bosonic Matsubara summation, we can write

\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\nu_{\ell}}\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{C_{B}}dz\;n_{B}(z)\,\mathcal{F}_{T}(z),\qquad n_{B}(z)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta z}-1},

(10)

where
C_{B}
is a contour that encircles the bosonic Matsubara poles
z=\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell}
on the imaginary axis in the counterclockwise direction. The zero-temperature limit used in this paper is therefore based on the standard Matsubara summation argument with a contour encircling the imaginary axis.

Assuming that
\mathcal{F}_{T}(z)
is analytic except on the real axis and that the contribution from the arc at infinity vanishes, we deform the contour
C_{B}
to run just above and below the real axis and obtain

\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\nu_{\ell}}\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,n_{B}(\omega)\,\Bigl[\mathcal{F}_{T}(\omega+\mathrm{i}0)-\mathcal{F}_{T}(\omega-\mathrm{i}0)\Bigr],

(11)

where

\operatorname{Disc}\mathcal{F}_{T}(\omega):=\mathcal{F}_{T}(\omega+\mathrm{i}0)-\mathcal{F}_{T}(\omega-\mathrm{i}0)

denotes the discontinuity across the real axis.

In the zero-temperature limit
T\to 0
, we have

n_{B}(\omega)\to-\Theta(-\omega),

and hence

\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\nu_{\ell}}\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\operatorname{Disc}\mathcal{F}_{0}(\omega),\qquad\mathcal{F}_{0}(z):=\lim_{T\to 0}\mathcal{F}_{T}(z).

(12)

We further assume that
\mathcal{F}_{0}(z)
is analytic in the second and third quadrants and satisfies the decay condition required for the Wick rotation. Rotating the negative real axis onto the imaginary axis gives

-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\operatorname{Disc}\mathcal{F}_{0}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\xi}{2\pi}\Bigl[\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathrm{i}\xi)+\mathcal{F}_{0}(-\mathrm{i}\xi)\Bigr].

(13)

For the three-dimensional unpolarized UEG, the parity relations

\Delta(-\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q};\mathbf{q})=-\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q}),\qquad\delta(-\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q};\mathbf{q})=-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})

(14)

hold, and the substitution
\mathbf{p}\mapsto-\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}
,
\mathbf{k}\mapsto-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}
yields

X(\mathbf{q},-\xi)=X(\mathbf{q},\xi).

(15)

If we further assume for the bosonic screened interaction that

D(q,-\mathrm{i}\xi)=D(q,\mathrm{i}\xi),

(16)

then
\mathcal{F}_{0}(-\mathrm{i}\xi)=\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathrm{i}\xi)
, and therefore

\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\nu_{\ell}}\mathcal{F}_{T}(\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\xi}{\pi}\,\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathrm{i}\xi).

(17)

Equation (
17
) is the precise meaning of the replacement

\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\nu_{\ell}}\;\longrightarrow\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\xi}{\pi}

used in the main text.

For the three-dimensional unpolarized UEG, we make momenta dimensionless by
k_{F}
and measure energies in units of
k_{F}^{2}/m
, so that

\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{q}+\frac{q^{2}}{2}.

(18)

We define

\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q}):=\Theta(1-|\mathbf{p}|)-\Theta(1-|\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}|),\qquad\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q}):=\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{q}+\frac{q^{2}}{2},

(19)

so that in the zero-temperature limit,

Q_{T}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\nu_{\ell})\longrightarrow Q_{\xi}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q}):=\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}{\mathrm{i}\xi-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}.

(20)

We therefore obtain

\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{scr2x}}^{\,\mathrm{UEG}}[D]=A_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\xi}{\pi}\int d^{3}q\;D(q,\mathrm{i}\xi)\,X(\mathbf{q},\xi),

(21)

X(\mathbf{q},\xi)=\int d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}{\mathrm{i}\xi-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})}{\mathrm{i}\xi-\delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})}.

(22)

For brevity, we write

K[D]:=A_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\xi}{\pi}\int d^{3}q\;D(q,\mathrm{i}\xi)\,X(\mathbf{q},\xi)

(23)

and set

\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{scr2x}}^{\,\mathrm{UEG}}[D]=K[D].

(24)

Here
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{scr2x}}^{\,\mathrm{UEG}}[D]
is not a physical correlation energy but a grand-potential-derived zero-temperature model quantity, and
K[D]
is the map that gives it.

II.3
General one-pole screening and RC-SP basis elements

The minimal class of screened interaction we consider in this paper is the one-pole family

D_{1\mathrm{p}}(q,iqz)=D_{q}(q)\,\frac{\Omega_{q}^{2}}{\Omega_{q}^{2}+q^{2}z^{2}}=D_{q}(q)\,\frac{u(q)^{2}}{u(q)^{2}+z^{2}},\qquad u(q):=\frac{\Omega_{q}}{q}.

(25)

The key point of the exact reduction derived below is that the screened interaction separates exactly into
q
-dependence and
z
-dependence in the form

D(q,iqz)=D_{q}(q)\,w(z).

(26)

For the one-pole family (
25
), the necessary and sufficient condition for this one-variable separation to hold is

u(q)=\mu=\mathrm{const.}

(27)

Therefore, the reduction-compatible single-pole (RC-SP) model

D_{\mathrm{RC\text{-}SP}}(q,iqz)=D_{q}(q)\,\frac{\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}+z^{2}}

(28)

treated in this paper is the only class of the one-pole family that preserves exact reduction. We call the RC-SP model an exactly reducible screened-exchange reference model in this sense.

On the other hand, even for a general one-pole screened interaction, we can consider a finite-rank separable approximation using the RC-SP kernel family as a basis:

D_{1\mathrm{p}}(q,iqz)\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}(q)\,\frac{\mu_{m}^{2}}{\mu_{m}^{2}+z^{2}}.

(29)

Since each basis element

w_{m}(z):=\frac{\mu_{m}^{2}}{\mu_{m}^{2}+z^{2}}

(30)

corresponds to an RC-SP kernel, the exact reduction machinery can be applied term by term. The RC-SP model therefore provides analytically controllable basis elements for systematically approximating generic one-pole screening, rather than serving as a crude substitute for generic screening. For general one-pole screening with
u(q)\neq\mathrm{const.}
, the screened interaction

D(q,iqz)=D_{q}(q)\,\frac{u(q)^{2}}{u(q)^{2}+z^{2}}

(31)

does not separate exactly into
q
-dependence and
z
-dependence. In this case, the quantity

\Psi_{D}(s_{1},s_{2};z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\;q\,D(q,iqz)\,[\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})]

(32)

that appears in the reduced equation becomes a two-variable transform depending on
z
, and the exact reduction via a one-variable kernel
K_{\mu}(y)
is lost. The significance of the RC-SP class therefore lies not in being a realistic model for generic screening but in being the minimal reference model in which dynamic screened exchange can be analyzed exactly through a one-variable kernel.

However, it is not appropriate to use a naive Taylor expansion around a finite
\mu
globally for an ordinary plasmon-pole model. In the long-wavelength limit of the three-dimensional UEG, we have
\Omega_{q}\to\omega_{p}
, so that

u(q)=\frac{\Omega_{q}}{q}\sim\frac{\omega_{p}}{q}\qquad(q\to 0),

(33)

and a simple expansion around
\mu
is not uniform in the infrared. A systematic route toward the ordinary plasmon-pole model should therefore be understood as a finite-rank basis expansion of the form (
29
) or as a hybrid approximation with a partitioned momentum domain. This circumstance justifies positioning the RC-SP model not as a substitute for realistic plasmon dispersions but as a reference model and basis element equipped with exact controllability.

The RC-SP model in this paper is a benchmark choice that retains the bare Coulomb radial factor
D_{q}(q)=q^{-2}
in the static limit. As a more screening-like radial model, one can also consider

D_{\mathrm{Y}}(q,iqz)=\frac{1}{q^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\,\frac{\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}+z^{2}},

(34)

which preserves exact one-variable reduction in the same way as the RC-SP model but generally loses the Frullani-type closed form that holds in the Coulomb case. It is therefore natural to regard the Coulomb RC-SP as a benchmark model that maximizes exact reduction and analytic tractability, and the Yukawa-RC-SP as a comparison model that incorporates static screening.

II.4
Adiabatic-connection-defined reference correction

Equation (
7
) is a grand-potential-derived model quantity, and we do not identify it with the actual correlation energy. In order to connect this model quantity to an energy-like correction, we introduce a coupling-strength-dependent path
D_{\lambda}
inside the RC-SP class and define the adiabatic-connection-defined reference correction as

\Delta\varepsilon_{c}^{\mathrm{AC\text{-}RC-SP}}(r_{s}):=\int_{0}^{1}d\lambda\;\lambda\,\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{scr2x}}^{\,\mathrm{UEG}}[D_{\lambda}](r_{s}),

(35)

where
\lambda
is the coupling-strength parameter associated with the explicit interaction line and
D_{\lambda}
is a screening path chosen inside the RC-SP class.

The important point is that the reduced representation is linear in
D
, so that the structure of exact reduction is preserved even when the
\lambda
-integration is introduced. Using the reduced kernel
K_{\mu}(y)
derived below, we can write

\Delta\varepsilon_{c}^{\mathrm{AC\text{-}RC-SP}}(r_{s})=A_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dy}{y}\,\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)\,\overline{K}(y;r_{s}),\qquad\overline{K}(y;r_{s}):=\int_{0}^{1}d\lambda\;\lambda\,K_{\mu_{\lambda}(r_{s})}(y).

(36)

The adiabatic connection therefore gives the most natural prescription for connecting the grand-potential-derived screened-exchange model quantity to a local reference correction while preserving both exact reduction and finite-
\mu
numerical tractability.

The adiabatic connection referred to here is not meant to reproduce the adiabatic connection fluctuation dissipation (ACFD) expression
10
;
8
of RPA but is a restricted adiabatic-connection path defined inside the reference model for the screened-exchange topology. The purpose of this paper is not to give a completed approximation based on the Luttinger–Ward functional
17
or the Almbladh–Barth–van Leeuwen functional
2
but to give a reference framework that controls the mathematical structure of the dynamically screened exchange correction from the two sides of exact reduction and finite-
\mu
numerics.

III
Exact dynamic reduction

III.1
Change of variables to
\xi=qz

Writing
\bm{q}=q\hat{\bm{q}}
, we have

\delta(\bm{p};\bm{q})=q\left(\bm{p}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{q}}+\frac{q}{2}\right).

(37)

We set

\xi=qz,\qquad z>0,

(38)

so that
\mathrm{d}\xi=q\,\mathrm{d}z
and the denominator factorizes as

\mathrm{i}\xi-\delta(\bm{p};\bm{q})=q\left(\mathrm{i}z-\bm{p}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{q}}-\frac{q}{2}\right).

(39)

Each resolvent therefore produces one factor of
1/q
, and we can write

\mathcal{K}[D]=\mathcal{A}_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\pi}\int_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\hat{\bm{q}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\;q\,D(q,\mathrm{i}qz)\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}(q,\hat{\bm{q}},z),

(40)

where

\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}(q,\hat{\bm{q}},z):=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}p\int\mathrm{d}^{3}k\;\frac{1}{|\bm{p}-\bm{k}|^{2}}\frac{\Delta(\bm{p};q\hat{\bm{q}})}{\mathrm{i}z-\bm{p}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{q}}-q/2}\frac{\Delta(\bm{k};q\hat{\bm{q}})}{\mathrm{i}z-\bm{k}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{q}}-q/2}.

(41)

Equation (
39
) gives a denominator of the same form as in the static derivation of Glasser
7
. The subsequent reduction proceeds by applying Fourier factorization and the Schwinger representation in turn to this form.

III.2
Fourier factorization of the Coulomb kernel

We Fourier-factorize the Coulomb exchange kernel as

\frac{1}{|\bm{p}-\bm{k}|^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}r\;\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{r})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\bm{p}-\bm{k})\cdot\bm{r}},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi|\bm{r}|},

(42)

where convention-dependent overall coefficients are absorbed into
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. The integrals over
\bm{p}
and
\bm{k}
then factorize, and using the one-block function

B(\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z):=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}p\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}}\frac{\Delta(\bm{p};q\hat{\bm{q}})}{\mathrm{i}z-\bm{p}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{q}}-q/2},

(43)

we can write

\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}(q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\;\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{r})B(\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)B(-\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z).

(44)

The essential problem therefore reduces to the evaluation of
B
.

III.3
Evaluation of the one-block via the Schwinger representation and the occupied-ball Fourier transform

For
z>0
, the Schwinger representation gives

\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}z-s}=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{e}^{-zt}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ts}.

(45)

Applying (
45
) to (
43
), we obtain

B(\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{e}^{-zt}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}qt/2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}p\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\bm{p}\cdot\hat{\bm{q}}}\Delta(\bm{p};q\hat{\bm{q}}).

(46)

Since

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\bm{p}\cdot\hat{\bm{q}}}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{p}\cdot(\bm{r}-t\hat{\bm{q}})},

we have

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}p\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{p}\cdot(\bm{r}-t\hat{\bm{q}})}\Delta(\bm{p};q\hat{\bm{q}})

\displaystyle=\int_{|\bm{p}|<1}\mathrm{d}^{3}p\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{p}\cdot(\bm{r}-t\hat{\bm{q}})}-\int_{|\bm{p}+q\hat{\bm{q}}|<1}\mathrm{d}^{3}p\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{p}\cdot(\bm{r}-t\hat{\bm{q}})}.

(47)

Substituting
\bm{u}=\bm{p}+q\hat{\bm{q}}
in the second term gives

\int_{|\bm{p}+q\hat{\bm{q}}|<1}\mathrm{d}^{3}p\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{p}\cdot(\bm{r}-t\hat{\bm{q}})}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q(r_{k}-t)}\int_{|\bm{u}|<1}\mathrm{d}^{3}u\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{u}\cdot(\bm{r}-t\hat{\bm{q}})},\qquad r_{k}:=\bm{r}\cdot\hat{\bm{q}}.

(48)

Using the Fourier transform of the unit Fermi ball,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{\rho}):=\int_{|\bm{p}|<1}\mathrm{d}^{3}p\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\rho}},

(49)

we obtain

B(\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{e}^{-zt}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}qt/2}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q(r_{k}-t/2)}\Bigr]\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}-t\hat{\bm{q}}).

(50)

Simplifying the expression in the square brackets yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}qt/2}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q(r_{k}-t/2)}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}qr_{k}/2}\sin\!\left[\frac{q}{2}(r_{k}-t)\right],

(51)

and therefore

B(\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)=2\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}qr_{k}/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{e}^{-zt}\sin\!\left[\frac{q}{2}(r_{k}-t)\right]\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}-t\hat{\bm{q}}).

(52)

Similarly, replacing
\bm{r}\to-\bm{r}
and using the parity
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(-\bm{\rho})=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{\rho})
, we obtain

B(-\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)=-2\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qr_{k}/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{e}^{-zt}\sin\!\left[\frac{q}{2}(r_{k}+t)\right]\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}+t\hat{\bm{q}}).

(53)

We now make
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}
explicit. Writing
\rho=|\bm{\rho}|
and using spherical symmetry, we have

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{\rho})=4\pi\int_{0}^{1}p^{2}\frac{\sin(p\rho)}{p\rho}\,\mathrm{d}p=4\pi\frac{\sin\rho-\rho\cos\rho}{\rho^{3}}=4\pi\frac{j_{1}(\rho)}{\rho}=(2\pi)^{3/2}\frac{J_{3/2}(\rho)}{\rho^{3/2}},

(54)

where the last equality uses the relation
j_{1}(\rho)=\sqrt{\pi/(2\rho)}\,J_{3/2}(\rho)
between the spherical and ordinary Bessel functions.

III.4
Pre-affine exact formula

Multiplying (
52
) and (
53
), we obtain

\displaystyle B(\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)B(-\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)

\displaystyle=-4\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\mathrm{e}^{-z(t_{1}+t_{2})}\sin\!\left[\frac{q}{2}(r_{k}-t_{1})\right]\sin\!\left[\frac{q}{2}(r_{k}+t_{2})\right]

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}-t_{1}\hat{\bm{q}})\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}+t_{2}\hat{\bm{q}}).

(55)

Relabeling the integration variables as
t_{1}\leftrightarrow t_{2}
and exchanging the order of the product gives

\displaystyle B(\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)B(-\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)

\displaystyle=-4\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\mathrm{e}^{-z(t_{1}+t_{2})}\sin\!\left[\frac{q}{2}(r_{k}-t_{2})\right]\sin\!\left[\frac{q}{2}(r_{k}+t_{1})\right]

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}+t_{1}\hat{\bm{q}})\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}-t_{2}\hat{\bm{q}}).

(56)

Setting

\alpha_{1}=\frac{q}{2}(r_{k}+t_{1}),\qquad\alpha_{2}=\frac{q}{2}(r_{k}-t_{2}),

(57)

we have

\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}=q\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2},\qquad\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=q\left(r_{k}+\frac{t_{1}-t_{2}}{2}\right).

(58)

Using the identity

2\sin\alpha_{1}\sin\alpha_{2}=\cos(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})-\cos(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}),

we obtain

\displaystyle B(\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)B(-\bm{r};q,\hat{\bm{q}},z)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\mathrm{e}^{-z(t_{1}+t_{2})}\Biggl[\cos\!\left(q\left(r_{k}+\frac{t_{1}-t_{2}}{2}\right)\right)-\cos\!\left(q\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2}\right)\Biggr]

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}+t_{1}\hat{\bm{q}})\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}-t_{2}\hat{\bm{q}}).

(59)

Substituting this into (
44
) and (
40
) while keeping the
q
-integral unexpanded, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[D]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\int_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\hat{\bm{q}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\;\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{r})\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\mathrm{e}^{-z(t_{1}+t_{2})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\;q\,D(q,\mathrm{i}qz)\Biggl[\cos\!\left(q\left(r_{k}+\frac{t_{1}-t_{2}}{2}\right)\right)-\cos\!\left(q\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2}\right)\Biggr]\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}+t_{1}\hat{\bm{q}})\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}-t_{2}\hat{\bm{q}}).

(60)

We define the
q
-integral that appears only in the cosine-difference form as

\Psi_{D}(s_{1},s_{2};z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\;q\,D(q,\mathrm{i}qz)\bigl[\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})\bigr].

(61)

This definition is important because, even when the individual integrals
\int\mathrm{d}q\,qD(q,\mathrm{i}qz)\cos(qs)
do not converge as in the Coulomb case, the difference integral (
61
) can be well-defined. Using this definition, Eq. (
60
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[D]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\int_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\hat{\bm{q}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\;\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{r})\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\;\mathrm{e}^{-z(t_{1}+t_{2})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Psi_{D}\!\left(r_{k}+\frac{t_{1}-t_{2}}{2},\,\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2};z\right)\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}+t_{1}\hat{\bm{q}})\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}-t_{2}\hat{\bm{q}}).

(62)

This is the exact dynamic formula immediately before the centered affine transformation.

III.5
Centered affine transformation

We introduce

y:=t_{1}+t_{2}>0,\qquad x:=\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{t_{1}+t_{2}}\in[-1,1],\qquad\bm{r}=y\left(\bm{R}+\frac{x}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right).

(63)

The inverse transformation is

t_{1}=\frac{y}{2}(1-x),\qquad t_{2}=\frac{y}{2}(1+x),

(64)

and the Jacobians are

\mathrm{d}t_{1}\,\mathrm{d}t_{2}=\frac{y}{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad\mathrm{d}^{3}r=y^{3}\mathrm{d}^{3}R.

(65)

A direct calculation gives

\bm{r}+t_{1}\hat{\bm{q}}=y\left(\bm{R}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right),\qquad\bm{r}-t_{2}\hat{\bm{q}}=y\left(\bm{R}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right),

(66)

so that, writing

a:=\left|\bm{R}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right|,\qquad b:=\left|\bm{R}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right|,\qquad\xi_{1}:=ya,\qquad\xi_{2}:=yb,

(67)

and noting

r_{k}+\frac{t_{1}-t_{2}}{2}=yR_{k},\qquad\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2}=\frac{y}{2},\qquad R_{k}:=\bm{R}\cdot\hat{\bm{q}},

(68)

we can use (
54
) to write

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}+t_{1}\hat{\bm{q}})\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{B}}(\bm{r}-t_{2}\hat{\bm{q}})=\mathrm{const}\times\frac{J_{3/2}(ay)J_{3/2}(by)}{y^{3}a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}.

(69)

The appearance of the factor
y^{-3}
is crucial: it cancels the
y^{3}
from the Jacobian (
65
), so that the only surviving power of
y
is the factor
y/2
coming from
\mathrm{d}t_{1}\,\mathrm{d}t_{2}
. We therefore obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[D]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\int_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\hat{\bm{q}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}R\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\;y\,\mathrm{e}^{-zy}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathcal{F}_{C}\!\left(y\left(\bm{R}+\frac{x}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right)\right)\Psi_{D}\!\left(yR_{k},\frac{y}{2};z\right)\frac{J_{3/2}(ay)J_{3/2}(by)}{a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}.

(70)

This is the exact dynamic reduction via the centered affine transformation in this paper. The essence of Eq. (
70
) is that the dynamic information is confined to
z
and
\Psi_{D}
, while the geometric part retains the centered affine geometry of the same form as in the static derivation of Glasser
7
. We emphasize, however, that what appears naturally for a general
D(q,\mathrm{i}\xi)
is not a one-variable kernel but the difference transform
\Psi_{D}(s_{1},s_{2};z)
.

IV
Separable model and exact reduction to a one-variable kernel

IV.1
Separable class

If the screened interaction separates as

D(q,\mathrm{i}qz)=D_{q}(q)w(z),

(71)

then (
61
) factorizes into

\Psi_{D}(s_{1},s_{2};z)=w(z)\,\Psi_{D_{q}}(s_{1},s_{2}),

(72)

where

\Psi_{D_{q}}(s_{1},s_{2}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\;qD_{q}(q)\bigl[\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})\bigr].

(73)

Note that the definition is given in cosine-difference form, so that the argument can proceed even when the individual cosine transforms diverge. Substituting (
72
) into (
70
) and defining

K_{w}(y):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\pi}\;w(z)\mathrm{e}^{-zy},

(74)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[D_{q}\otimes w]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{3}\int_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\hat{\bm{q}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}R\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\;yK_{w}(y)

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathcal{F}_{C}\!\left(y\left(\bm{R}+\frac{x}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right)\right)\Psi_{D_{q}}\!\left(yR_{k},\frac{y}{2}\right)\frac{J_{3/2}(ay)J_{3/2}(by)}{a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}.

(75)

This is the exact reduction via a one-variable kernel. If one wishes to use a one-variable kernel
K_{w}(y)
, the problem must therefore be restricted to the separable class (
71
) from the outset.

IV.2
Reduction-compatible single-pole (RC-SP) model

In order to make the one-variable kernel representation exact, the appropriate definition is

D_{\mu}^{\mathrm{RC-SP}}(q,\mathrm{i}\xi):=\frac{\mu^{2}}{\xi^{2}+\mu^{2}q^{2}}=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\,w_{\mu}\!\left(\frac{\xi}{q}\right),\qquad w_{\mu}(z):=\frac{\mu^{2}}{z^{2}+\mu^{2}}.

(76)

We call this the
reduction-compatible single-pole (RC-SP) model
. In this case,

D_{q}(q)=\frac{1}{q^{2}},\qquad w(z)=w_{\mu}(z),

(77)

and the one-variable kernel

K_{\mu}(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\pi}\frac{\mu^{2}}{z^{2}+\mu^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-zy}

(78)

is introduced exactly.

IV.3
Direct
q
-integration for the three-dimensional Coulomb case

In the Coulomb case, Eq. (
73
) becomes

\Psi_{D_{q}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{q}\bigl[\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})\bigr],

(79)

where the individual integrals diverge and must therefore be evaluated while keeping the difference form intact. Using the Frullani-type formula

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(aq)-\cos(bq)}{q}\,\mathrm{d}q=\log\left(\frac{b}{a}\right),\qquad a,b>0,

(80)

and the evenness of
\cos
, we obtain in general

\Psi_{D_{q}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\log\left|\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}}\right|.

(81)

Specializing to the arguments of (
75
) gives

\Psi_{D_{q}}\!\left(yR_{k},\frac{y}{2}\right)=\log\left|\frac{y/2}{yR_{k}}\right|=\log\frac{1}{2|R_{k}|}.

(82)

Substituting this into (
75
), we obtain the specialized formula for the three-dimensional Coulomb RC-SP model:

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{c,\mathrm{RC-SP}}^{\mathrm{scr2x,UEG}}(\mu)

\displaystyle=N_{1}\int_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\hat{\bm{q}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}R\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\;yK_{\mu}(y)

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathcal{F}_{C}\!\left(y\left(\bm{R}+\frac{x}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right)\right)\log\frac{1}{2|R_{k}|}\frac{J_{3/2}(ay)J_{3/2}(by)}{a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}.

(83)

Inserting
\mathcal{F}_{C}(\bm{r})=1/(4\pi|\bm{r}|)
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{C}\!\left(y\left(\bm{R}+\frac{x}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{4\pi y\left|\bm{R}+\frac{x}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right|},

(84)

so that the factor of
y
cancels, and we can also write

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{c,\mathrm{RC-SP}}^{\mathrm{scr2x,UEG}}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{N}_{1}\int_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\hat{\bm{q}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}R\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\;K_{\mu}(y)

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{1}{\left|\bm{R}+\frac{x}{2}\hat{\bm{q}}\right|}\log\frac{1}{2|R_{k}|}\frac{J_{3/2}(ay)J_{3/2}(by)}{a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}.

(85)

By rotational symmetry, we can replace
\hat{\bm{q}}
with a fixed unit vector
\hat{\bm{e}}
to obtain

\tilde{\epsilon}_{c,\mathrm{RC-SP}}^{\mathrm{scr2x,UEG}}(\mu)=\widehat{N}_{1}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}R\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\;K_{\mu}(y)\frac{1}{\left|\bm{R}+\frac{x}{2}\hat{\bm{e}}\right|}\log\frac{1}{2|R_{k}|}\frac{J_{3/2}(ay)J_{3/2}(by)}{a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}.

(86)

This is the mathematically safe specialized formula that serves as the starting point for the single-pole analysis.

V
Derivation of
\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)

V.1
Definition of the corrected reduced block

From Eq. (
86
), we separate the kernel depending only on
y
from the remaining geometric part and write

\tilde{\epsilon}_{c,\mathrm{RC-SP}}^{\mathrm{scr2x,UEG}}(\mu)=N_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\;K_{\mu}(y)\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y),

(87)

where convention-dependent overall numerical factors are absorbed into
N_{0}
and

\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y):=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}R\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\;\frac{\log(1/(2|R_{k}|))}{\left|\bm{R}+\frac{x}{2}\hat{\bm{e}}\right|}\frac{J_{3/2}(ay)J_{3/2}(by)}{a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}.

(88)

In what follows, we reduce this expression to a double-integral representation.

V.2
Cylindrical variables and the
x
integration

We introduce cylindrical variables about the
\hat{\bm{e}}
axis:

\bm{R}=(\rho\cos\phi,\rho\sin\phi,z),\qquad\rho\geq 0,\qquad\phi\in[0,2\pi),\qquad z=R_{k}.

(89)

Then

a=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+\left(z+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}},\qquad b=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+\left(z-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}},

(90)

and the volume element is

\mathrm{d}^{3}R=\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\phi.

(91)

We first carry out the
x
integration directly:

\displaystyle I_{x}(\rho,z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+\left(z+\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2}}}

\displaystyle=2\int_{z-1/2}^{z+1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+s^{2}}}\qquad(s=z+x/2)

\displaystyle=2\Bigl[\operatorname{arsinh}(s/\rho)\Bigr]_{z-1/2}^{z+1/2}

\displaystyle=2\log\frac{z+1/2+\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(z+1/2)^{2}}}{z-1/2+\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(z-1/2)^{2}}}

\displaystyle=2\log\frac{z+1/2+a}{z-1/2+b}.

(92)

We therefore obtain

\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\phi\;2\log\frac{z+1/2+a}{z-1/2+b}\log\frac{1}{2|z|}\frac{J_{3/2}(ay)J_{3/2}(by)}{a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}.

(93)

V.3
Transformation to prolate variables

Following Glasser
7
, we introduce

u:=a+b\in[1,\infty),\qquad v:=a-b\in[-1,1].

(94)

Then

a=\frac{u+v}{2},\qquad b=\frac{u-v}{2},

(95)

and the standard prolate relations give

z=\frac{uv}{2},\qquad\rho=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(u^{2}-1)(1-v^{2})},

(96)

with the volume element

\mathrm{d}^{3}R=\frac{1}{8}(u^{2}-v^{2})\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}\phi.

(97)

We next rewrite the result of the
x
integration (
92
) in terms of
(u,v)
:

\displaystyle z+\frac{1}{2}+a

\displaystyle=\frac{uv}{2}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{u+v}{2}=\frac{uv+u+v+1}{2}=\frac{(u+1)(v+1)}{2},

(98)

\displaystyle z-\frac{1}{2}+b

\displaystyle=\frac{uv}{2}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{u-v}{2}=\frac{uv+u-v-1}{2}=\frac{(u-1)(v+1)}{2}.

(99)

Therefore

I_{x}(\rho,z)=2\log\frac{u+1}{u-1}=:2L(u).

(100)

The other logarithmic factor becomes

\log\frac{1}{2|z|}=\log\frac{1}{|uv|},

(101)

and Eq. (
93
) reduces to

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}v\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\phi\;\frac{1}{4}(u^{2}-v^{2})L(u)\log\frac{1}{|uv|}\frac{J_{3/2}\!\bigl((u+v)y/2\bigr)J_{3/2}\!\bigl((u-v)y/2\bigr)}{a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}.

(102)

V.4
Conversion from
J_{3/2}
to
j_{1}
and cancellation

We write the spherical Bessel function
j_{1}
as

j_{1}(z)=\frac{\sin z}{z^{2}}-\frac{\cos z}{z}.

(103)

Since

J_{3/2}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{2z}{\pi}}\,j_{1}(z),

(104)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{J_{3/2}(ay)J_{3/2}(by)}{a^{3/2}b^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2y}{\pi ab}\,j_{1}(ay)j_{1}(by).

(105)

Using

ab=\frac{u^{2}-v^{2}}{4},

(106)

we substitute (
105
) and (
97
) into (
102
) and obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}v\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\phi\;\frac{1}{4}(u^{2}-v^{2})L(u)\log\frac{1}{|uv|}\frac{2y}{\pi}\frac{4}{u^{2}-v^{2}}j_{1}(ay)j_{1}(by)

\displaystyle=\frac{2y}{\pi}\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}v\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\phi\;L(u)\log\frac{1}{|uv|}j_{1}(ay)j_{1}(by).

(107)

The integrand is an even function of
v
:
L(u)
does not depend on
v
,
\log(1/|uv|)
is even in
v
, and the exchange
a\leftrightarrow b
leaves
j_{1}(ay)j_{1}(by)
invariant. We can therefore write

\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)=\widetilde{C}\,y\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,L(u)\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}v\,\ell_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u+v}{2}y\right)j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u-v}{2}y\right),

(108)

where

\ell_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v):=\log\frac{1}{uv},\qquad u\geq 1,\qquad 0<v\leq 1,

(109)

and all convention-dependent overall constants have been absorbed into
\widetilde{C}
. Reabsorbing this constant into the outer prefactor
N_{0}
so as to be consistent with the original definition (
88
), we finally obtain

\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)=y\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,L(u)\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}v\,\ell_{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u+v}{2}y\right)j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u-v}{2}y\right).

(110)

VI
Static limit and normalization

In Eq. (
87
), the kernel (
78
) satisfies

K_{\mu}(y)\longrightarrow\frac{1}{\pi y}\qquad(\mu\to\infty)

(111)

in the limit
\mu\to\infty
. We therefore set

D_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)}{y}\,\mathrm{d}y,

(112)

so that

\lim_{\mu\to\infty}\tilde{\epsilon}_{c,\mathrm{RC-SP}}^{\mathrm{scr2x,UEG}}(\mu)=\frac{N_{0}}{\pi}D_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}.

(113)

In this paper, we normalize this static limit to match the high-density fix of the bare second-order exchange. We take the Onsager–Mittag–Stephen value
18
for the unpolarized UEG as

a_{2x}^{\mathrm{OMS}}=0.04836\ \mathrm{Ry}

(114)

and fix the normalization constant
N_{0}
by

N_{0}=\pi\,\frac{a_{2x}^{\mathrm{OMS}}}{D_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}}.

(115)

We then introduce the normalized screening factor

S_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(\mu):=\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}_{c,\mathrm{RC-SP}}^{\mathrm{scr2x,UEG}}(\mu)}{a_{2x}^{\mathrm{OMS}}},

(116)

so that

\tilde{\epsilon}_{c,\mathrm{RC-SP}}^{\mathrm{scr2x,UEG}}(\mu)=a_{2x}^{\mathrm{OMS}}\,S_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(\mu).

(117)

Writing

K_{\mu}(y)=\frac{1}{\pi y}\,\Xi(\mu y),

(118)

we have

S_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(\mu)=\frac{1}{D_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)}{y}\,\Xi(\mu y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

(119)

where

\Xi(z)=\pi yK_{\mu}(y)\big|_{z=\mu y}=z\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-zt}}{1+t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(120)

This normalization allows both the finite-
\mu
numerical results and the LDA reference correction to be interpreted as quantities with an absolute energy scale.

VII
Asymptotic analysis via the Mellin–Barnes representation and contour shift

For notational simplicity, we write

\Phi(y):=\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y),\qquad D_{0}:=D_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}},\qquad S(\mu):=S_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(\mu)

throughout this section.

In this section, we give a general asymptotic analysis framework based on the Mellin–Barnes representation and contour shift for the screening factor

S(\mu)=\frac{1}{D_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Phi(y)}{y}\,\Xi(\mu y)\,dy

obtained in Section
VI
. The important point is to make the Fubini exchange needed for deriving the Mellin representation explicit through concrete weighted integrability conditions, rather than leaving it in the vague form of “assumed to be justified.” We also make the residue formula based on a rectangular contour and vanishing horizontal-side conditions explicit for the contour shift, and evaluate the remainder of the small-/
\text{large-}\mu
asymptotics quantitatively.

VII.1
Mellin transform of the kernel

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Xi(z)
be defined by (
120
).
Then, for
-1<\Re s<0
,

\mathcal{M}[\Xi](s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{s-1}\Xi(z)\,dz=-\frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{\Gamma(s+1)}{\sin(\pi s/2)}.

(121)

By Mellin inversion, we therefore have

\Xi(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\Gamma(s+1)}{\sin(\pi s/2)}\right)z^{-s}\,ds,\qquad-1<c<0,

(122)

where
\int_{(c)}
denotes integration upward along the vertical line
\Re s=c
.

Proof.

Write
\sigma:=\Re s
.
From the definition

\Xi(z)=z\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-zt}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt,

we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{z^{s-1}\,z\,e^{-zt}}{1+t^{2}}\right|\,dt\,dz=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{\sigma}e^{-zt}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt\,dz.

Performing the
z
-integration first on the right-hand side gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+t^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{\sigma}e^{-zt}\,dz\right)dt=\Gamma(\sigma+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{-\sigma-1}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt.

For
-1<\sigma<0
, the integrand behaves as
t^{-\sigma-1}
near
t\downarrow 0
, which is integrable, and as
t^{-\sigma-3}
for
t\to\infty
, which is also integrable. The double integral is therefore finite, and Fubini’s theorem applies. We thus obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{s-1}\Xi(z)\,dz=\Gamma(s+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{-s-1}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt.

Setting
u=t^{2}
, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{-s-1}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{-s/2-1}}{1+u}\,du.

Applying the standard formula

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{\alpha-1}}{1+u}\,du=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\alpha)},\qquad 0<\Re\alpha<1

with
\alpha=-s/2
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{-s-1}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt=-\frac{\pi}{2}\csc\frac{\pi s}{2},\qquad-1<\Re s<0,

which gives (
121
).
Equation (
122
) then follows from the Mellin inversion theorem.
∎

VII.2
Mellin–Barnes representation of the screening factor

Definition 2
.

For
\Phi:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
, the Mellin transform of
\Phi
is defined by

F(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{s-1}\Phi(y)\,dy

(123)

for those
s
at which the integral converges absolutely.
In particular, if

D_{0}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Phi(y)}{y}\,dy

(124)

is finite, then
D_{0}=F(0)
.

Theorem 3

(Mellin–Barnes representation of the screening factor)
.

Let
c\in(-1,0)
, and assume that
D_{0}
from (
124
) is finite and nonzero.
Suppose furthermore that

\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Phi(y)|\,y^{-c-1}\,dy<\infty.

(125)

Then the Mellin transform
F(-s)
converges absolutely on the line
\Re s=c
, the Mellin–Barnes integral

\int_{(c)}\mathcal{K}(s)F(-s)\mu^{-s}\,ds

converges absolutely for every
\mu>0
, and the screening factor satisfies

S(\mu)=\frac{1}{2\pi iD_{0}}\int_{(c)}\mathcal{K}(s)F(-s)\mu^{-s}\,ds,\qquad\mathcal{K}(s):=-\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\Gamma(s+1)}{\sin(\pi s/2)}.

(126)

Proof.

Let
s=c+it
.
By (
125
),

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|y^{-s-1}\Phi(y)\right|\,dy=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Phi(y)|\,y^{-c-1}\,dy<\infty,

so
F(-s)
converges absolutely on the line
\Re s=c
, and

|F(-c-it)|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Phi(y)|\,y^{-c-1}\,dy.

(127)

By Proposition
1
,

\Xi(\mu y)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\mathcal{K}(s)(\mu y)^{-s}\,ds\qquad(-1<c<0).

We next justify the exchange of the
y
- and
s
-integrations.
Using (
127
), it is enough to show that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\mathcal{K}(c+it)|\,dt<\infty.

For
|t|\geq 1
, Stirling’s formula gives

|\Gamma(c+1+it)|\leq C_{c}(1+|t|)^{c+1/2}e^{-\pi|t|/2},

and

\left|\sin\frac{\pi(c+it)}{2}\right|^{2}=\sin^{2}\frac{\pi c}{2}+\sinh^{2}\frac{\pi t}{2}\geq c^{\prime}_{c}\,e^{\pi|t|}.

Therefore

|\mathcal{K}(c+it)|\leq C_{c}(1+|t|)^{c+1/2}e^{-\pi|t|}\qquad(|t|\geq 1),

which is integrable on
|t|\geq 1
.
On the bounded interval
|t|\leq 1
,
\mathcal{K}(c+it)
is continuous because
-1<c<0
avoids the poles of both
\Gamma(s+1)
and
\sin(\pi s/2)^{-1}
.
Hence

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\mathcal{K}(c+it)|\,dt<\infty.

(128)

Using (
125
) and (
128
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{\Phi(y)}{y}\mathcal{K}(c+it)(\mu y)^{-c-it}\right|\,dt\,dy=\mu^{-c}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Phi(y)|\,y^{-c-1}\,dy\right)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\mathcal{K}(c+it)|\,dt\right)<\infty.

Tonelli–Fubini’s theorem therefore gives

\displaystyle S(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Phi(y)}{y}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\mathcal{K}(s)(\mu y)^{-s}\,ds\right]dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi iD_{0}}\int_{(c)}\mathcal{K}(s)\mu^{-s}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{-s-1}\Phi(y)\,dy\right)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi iD_{0}}\int_{(c)}\mathcal{K}(s)F(-s)\mu^{-s}\,ds,

which is (
126
).
Finally, absolute convergence of the line integral follows from

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\mathcal{K}(c+it)F(-c-it)\mu^{-c-it}\right|dt\leq\mu^{-c}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\mathcal{K}(c+it)|\,dt\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Phi(y)|\,y^{-c-1}\,dy\right)<\infty.

∎

VII.3
General asymptotic theorem via contour shift

Theorem 4

(General contour-shift asymptotics)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3
, define

I(s;\mu):=\frac{1}{D_{0}}\mathcal{K}(s)F(-s)\mu^{-s}.

Assume further that there exists an open strip

a<\Re s<b

in which
I(s;\mu)
admits a meromorphic continuation.
For any
\sigma\in(a,b)
such that the line
\Re s=\sigma
contains no pole of
I(s;\mu)
, assume that

A(\sigma):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{D_{0}}\mathcal{K}(\sigma+it)F(-\sigma-it)\right|dt<\infty.

(129)

Assume also that for any
\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}
with
[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}]\subset(a,b)
, whenever the horizontal lines
\Im s=\pm T
contain no pole for sufficiently large
|T|
,

\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{\sigma_{1}}^{\sigma_{2}}|I(\sigma+\mathrm{i}T;\mu)|\,d\sigma=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{\sigma_{1}}^{\sigma_{2}}|I(\sigma-\mathrm{i}T;\mu)|\,d\sigma=0.

(130)

Then, choosing
a<\sigma_{-}<c<\sigma_{+}<b
so as to avoid poles, we have

S(\mu)=\sum_{\sigma_{-}<\Re\rho<c}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma_{-})}I(s;\mu)\,ds

(131)

and

S(\mu)=-\sum_{c<\Re\rho<\sigma_{+}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma_{+})}I(s;\mu)\,ds.

(132)

In both formulas, the residue sums are understood as

\lim_{T\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{-}<\Re\rho<c\\
|\Im\rho|<T\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu),\qquad\lim_{T\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c<\Re\rho<\sigma_{+}\\
|\Im\rho|<T\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu),

respectively. The remainder satisfies

\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}I(s;\mu)\,ds\right|\leq\frac{\mu^{-\sigma}}{2\pi}\,A(\sigma)\qquad(\sigma\in(a,b)).

(133)

Therefore, for
\mu\to 0^{+}
, the residue sum from the left-shift poles dominates, while for
\mu\to\infty
, if
b>0
so that
0<\sigma_{+}<b
can be chosen, the contributions from the right-shift poles dominate.

Furthermore, suppose that
\mathcal{K}(s)F(-s)
has a pole of order
m
at
s=\rho
, i.e.,

\mathcal{K}(s)F(-s)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{-j}(s-\rho)^{-j}+O(1)\qquad(s\to\rho).

Then

\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu)=\mu^{-\rho}\,P_{\rho,m-1}(\log\mu),

(134)

where
P_{\rho,m-1}
is a polynomial of degree
m-1
given explicitly by

P_{\rho,m-1}(L)=\frac{1}{D_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{-j}\,\frac{(-L)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}.

(135)

Proof.

By (
126
),

S(\mu)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}I(s;\mu)\,ds.

Right contour shift.

Choose
c<\sigma_{+}<b
so as to avoid poles.
For
T>0
, let

R_{T}^{+}:=\{\,s\in\mathbb{C}:\ c\leq\Re s\leq\sigma_{+},\ |\Im s|\leq T\,\}

and write
\Gamma_{T}^{+}:=\partial R_{T}^{+}
for its positively oriented boundary.
By the residue theorem,

\int_{\Gamma_{T}^{+}}I(s;\mu)\,ds=2\pi i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c<\Re\rho<\sigma_{+}\\
|\Im\rho|<T\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu).

Let
\int_{(\sigma),T}
denote integration
upward
along
\{\,\sigma+it:\ -T\leq t\leq T\,\}
.
Under this convention, the left side
\Re s=c
of the positively oriented boundary is traversed downward, and the right side
\Re s=\sigma_{+}
is traversed upward.
Decomposing the contour integral into four sides therefore gives

-\int_{(c),T}I(s;\mu)\,ds+\int_{(\sigma_{+}),T}I(s;\mu)\,ds+H_{T}^{+}=2\pi i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c<\Re\rho<\sigma_{+}\\
|\Im\rho|<T\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu),

where
H_{T}^{+}
is the sum of the horizontal-side contributions.
By (
130
),
H_{T}^{+}\to 0
as
T\to\infty
.
Since both vertical integrals converge absolutely by (
129
), we can take the limit
T\to\infty
and obtain

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}I(s;\mu)\,ds=-\sum_{c<\Re\rho<\sigma_{+}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma_{+})}I(s;\mu)\,ds,

which is (
132
).

Left contour shift.

Choose
\sigma_{-}<c
so as to avoid poles.
For
T>0
, let

R_{T}^{-}:=\{\,s\in\mathbb{C}:\ \sigma_{-}\leq\Re s\leq c,\ |\Im s|\leq T\,\}

and write
\Gamma_{T}^{-}:=\partial R_{T}^{-}
for its positively oriented boundary.
By the residue theorem,

\int_{\Gamma_{T}^{-}}I(s;\mu)\,ds=2\pi i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{-}<\Re\rho<c\\
|\Im\rho|<T\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu).

Decomposing the contour integral into four sides gives

\int_{(c),T}I(s;\mu)\,ds-\int_{(\sigma_{-}),T}I(s;\mu)\,ds+H_{T}^{-}=2\pi i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{-}<\Re\rho<c\\
|\Im\rho|<T\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu),

where
H_{T}^{-}
is the sum of the horizontal-side contributions.
By (
130
),
H_{T}^{-}\to 0
, and by (
129
) both vertical integrals converge absolutely.
Taking the limit
T\to\infty
gives (
131
).

Remainder estimate.

On the line
\Re s=\sigma
, we have
|\mu^{-s}|=\mu^{-\sigma}
, so that

\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}I(s;\mu)\,ds\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{D_{0}}\mathcal{K}(\sigma+it)F(-\sigma-it)\mu^{-\sigma-it}\right|dt=\frac{\mu^{-\sigma}}{2\pi}A(\sigma),

which is (
133
).

Pole order and
\log\mu
.

In a neighborhood of
s=\rho
,

I(s;\mu)=\frac{\mu^{-\rho}}{D_{0}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{-j}(s-\rho)^{-j}+O(1)\right)e^{-(s-\rho)\log\mu}.

Substituting the Taylor expansion

e^{-(s-\rho)\log\mu}=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-\log\mu)^{n}}{n!}(s-\rho)^{n}+O((s-\rho)^{m})

and extracting the coefficient of
(s-\rho)^{-1}
, we find

\operatorname{Res}_{s=\rho}I(s;\mu)=\frac{\mu^{-\rho}}{D_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{-j}\frac{(-\log\mu)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!},

which gives (
134
) and (
135
).
∎

VIII
Endpoint analysis and Mellin singular structure

The purpose of this section is to make the endpoint singularity of the reduced block

\Phi(y)=\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)

explicit and to provide the concrete input needed for applying the Mellin–Barnes framework of Section VII to the actual
\Phi
.

Although this section contains many technical lemmas, the logical goals are limited to three.

The first goal is to fix the small-
y
endpoint singularity and determine the principal part of the Mellin transform
F(s)
at
s=-1
. The main result for this purpose is Proposition
6
, and Lemma
5
provides the leading coefficient
a_{0}
.

The second goal is to organize the large-
y
endpoint singularity through the exact decomposition

\Phi(y)=T_{v}(y)+T_{u}(y)+R_{3}(y)

and to determine the principal part of
F(s)
at
s=2
. This stage combines the singular decomposition of the
v
-channel (Lemma
8
and Proposition
9
), the endpoint analysis of the
u
-channel (Lemma
10
, Lemma
11
, Proposition
14
), and the endpoint control of the remainder channel (Lemma
13
) to arrive at the final result in Proposition
15
.

Third, we connect the above endpoint analysis with the contour-shift theorem of Section VII. Corollary
16
first fixes the Mellin principal part of
F(s)
from the small-
y
and large-
y
endpoint singularities. Then Definition
17
, Lemma
18
, Proposition
19
, and Corollary
20
verify the assumptions of Theorem
4
for the actual
\Phi
on the first right strip
0<\Re s<2
and rigorously give the leading large-
\mu
asymptotic. After that, Proposition
21
and Corollary
22
rigorously extract the leading small-
\mu
term on the first left strip
-2<\Re s<0
. Furthermore, after Definition
23
, we establish the vertical-growth control of the regular part
H_{2}(s)
on the closed strip
0<\Re s<3
through a sequence of lemmas, and as a consequence, Proposition
31
and Corollary
32
push the second small-
\mu
layer to a result for the actual
\Phi
rather than a conditional statement.

For a first reading, the most transparent approach is to follow Proposition
6
, Proposition
15
, Corollary
16
, Proposition
19
, Corollary
20
, Proposition
21
, Corollary
22
, Proposition
31
, and Corollary
32
in order, and to consult the individual lemmas as the technical input they provide.

VIII.1
Small-
y
asymptotics

Lemma 5

(Spherical Bessel integral)
.

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{j_{1}(x)^{2}}{x}\,dx=\frac{1}{4}.

(136)

Therefore

a_{0}:=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{j_{1}(t/2)^{2}}{t}\,dt=\frac{1}{2}.

(137)

Proof.

Substituting
\ell=1
into the known integral formula for spherical Bessel functions,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{j_{\ell}(x)^{2}}{x}\,dx=\frac{1}{2\ell(\ell+1)},\qquad\ell\geq 1,

gives (
136
). The change of variables
u=t/2
then yields (
137
).
∎

Proposition 6

(Sharpened small-
y
asymptotics)
.

As
y\downarrow 0
,
\Phi(y)
satisfies

\Phi(y)=a_{0}\,y\log y+b_{0}\,y+O\!\bigl(y^{2}|\log y|^{2}\bigr),

(138)

where

b_{0}:=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{j_{1}(t/2)^{2}}{t}\,(1-\log t)\,dt

(139)

is finite. The small-
y
contribution therefore gives the principal part

-\frac{a_{0}}{(s+1)^{2}}+\frac{b_{0}}{s+1}

(140)

near
s=-1
.

Proof.

We start from the defining expression

\Phi(y)=y\int_{1}^{\infty}du\;L(u)\int_{0}^{1}dv\;\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)\,j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u+v}{2}y\right)j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u-v}{2}y\right),

where

L(u)=\log\frac{u+1}{u-1},\qquad\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)=\log\frac{1}{uv}.

We use the standard bounds for
j_{1}
:

|j_{1}(z)|\leq C\min\{z,(1+z)^{-1}\},\qquad|j_{1}^{\prime}(z)|\leq C\begin{cases}1,&0<z\leq 1,\\[2.84526pt]
z^{-1},&z\geq 1.\end{cases}

(141)

Fix
0<y<1
and split the
u
-integration at
y^{-1/3}
:

U_{<}(y):=[1,y^{-1/3}],\qquad U_{>}(y):=[y^{-1/3},\infty),

so that

\Phi(y)=\Phi_{<}(y)+\Phi_{>}(y).

Step 1: Low-
u
region
U_{<}(y)
.

For
u\in U_{<}(y)
, we have
uy\leq y^{2/3}\ll 1
, so that

j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u\pm v}{2}y\right)=O(uy)

holds uniformly. Therefore

\left|j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u+v}{2}y\right)j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u-v}{2}y\right)\right|\leq Cu^{2}y^{2}.

Since

|\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)|\leq C(1+\log u+|\log v|),\qquad\int_{0}^{1}(1+|\log v|)\,dv<\infty,

we obtain

|\Phi_{<}(y)|\leq Cy^{3}\int_{1}^{y^{-1/3}}u^{2}L(u)(1+\log u)\,du.

Using
L(u)\sim 2/u
as
u\to\infty
, we have

\int_{1}^{y^{-1/3}}u^{2}L(u)(1+\log u)\,du=O\!\bigl(y^{-2/3}|\log y|\bigr),

and therefore

\Phi_{<}(y)=O\!\bigl(y^{7/3}|\log y|\bigr).

(142)

Step 2: High-
u
region
U_{>}(y)
.

For
u\in U_{>}(y)
, we substitute
t=uy
, so that

u=\frac{t}{y},\qquad du=\frac{dt}{y},\qquad t\in[y^{2/3},\infty),

and obtain

\Phi_{>}(y)=\int_{y^{2/3}}^{\infty}dt\;L(t/y)\int_{0}^{1}dv\;(\log y-\log t-\log v)\,j_{1}\!\left(\frac{t+vy}{2}\right)j_{1}\!\left(\frac{t-vy}{2}\right).

Since
t/y\geq y^{-1/3}\to\infty
, the expansion

L(t/y)=\frac{2y}{t}+R_{L}(y,t),\qquad|R_{L}(y,t)|\leq C\frac{y^{3}}{t^{3}}

(143)

holds uniformly.

We next write

j_{1}\!\left(\frac{t+vy}{2}\right)j_{1}\!\left(\frac{t-vy}{2}\right)=j_{1}(t/2)^{2}+R_{j}(y,t,v),

where, for
0<t\leq 1
, the smoothness
j_{1}\in C^{2}([0,1])
gives

R_{j}(y,t,v)=O(y^{2}),

while for
t\geq 1
, the mean-value theorem combined with (
141
) gives

R_{j}(y,t,v)=O(yt^{-2}).

Therefore

|R_{j}(y,t,v)|\leq C\begin{cases}y^{2},&0<t\leq 1,\\[2.84526pt]
y\,t^{-2},&t\geq 1.\end{cases}

(144)

Substituting these decompositions, we write

\Phi_{>}(y)=M(y)+E_{L}(y)+E_{j}(y),

where the main term is

M(y)=2y\int_{y^{2/3}}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t}\int_{0}^{1}dv\;(\log y-\log t-\log v)\,j_{1}(t/2)^{2}.

Using

\int_{0}^{1}(\log y-\log t-\log v)\,dv=\log y-\log t+1,

we obtain

M(y)=2y\int_{y^{2/3}}^{\infty}\frac{j_{1}(t/2)^{2}}{t}\,(\log y-\log t+1)\,dt.

The error from extending the lower limit to zero is estimated using
j_{1}(t/2)^{2}/t=O(t)
near
t=0
:

O\!\left(y\int_{0}^{y^{2/3}}t\,(|\log y|+|\log t|+1)\,dt\right)=O\!\bigl(y^{7/3}|\log y|\bigr).

We therefore have

M(y)=a_{0}\,y\log y+b_{0}\,y+O\!\bigl(y^{7/3}|\log y|\bigr).

We also confirm that
b_{0}
is finite. Near
t\downarrow 0
,

j_{1}(t/2)=\frac{t}{6}+O(t^{3}),

so that

\frac{j_{1}(t/2)^{2}}{t}(1-\log t)=O\!\bigl(t(1+|\log t|)\bigr),

while for
t\to\infty
,
j_{1}(t/2)=O(t^{-1})
gives

\frac{j_{1}(t/2)^{2}}{t}(1-\log t)=O\!\bigl(t^{-3}(1+\log t)\bigr).

The integral defining
b_{0}
is therefore absolutely convergent and finite.

For the remainder
E_{L}(y)
, we use (
143
) and (
141
) with
j_{1}(t/2)^{2}=O(t^{2})
for
0<t\leq 1
and
O(t^{-2})
for
t\geq 1
:

|E_{L}(y)|\leq C\int_{y^{2/3}}^{1}\frac{y^{3}}{t^{3}}\,t^{2}\,(|\log y|+|\log t|+1)\,dt+C\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{3}}{t^{3}}\,t^{-2}\,(\log t+1)\,dt=O\!\bigl(y^{3}|\log y|^{2}\bigr).

Similarly, for
E_{j}(y)
, using (
144
):

|E_{j}(y)|\leq Cy\int_{y^{2/3}}^{1}\frac{dt}{t}(|\log y|+|\log t|+1)\,y^{2}+Cy\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t}(\log t+1)\,y\,t^{-2}=O\!\bigl(y^{3}|\log y|^{2}\bigr)+O(y^{2}).

Combining with (
142
), we conclude

\Phi(y)=a_{0}\,y\log y+b_{0}\,y+O\!\bigl(y^{2}|\log y|^{2}\bigr).

Finally, for the Mellin transform, we split

F(s)=\int_{0}^{1}y^{s-1}\Phi(y)\,dy+\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}\Phi(y)\,dy.

The small-
y
part produces

-\frac{a_{0}}{(s+1)^{2}}+\frac{b_{0}}{s+1},

while the Mellin transform of the remainder
O(y^{2}|\log y|^{2})
is regular for
\Re s>-2
. This gives (
140
) near
s=-1
.
∎

VIII.2
Large-
y
asymptotics

Lemma 7

(Exact trigonometric decomposition)
.

Setting

A:=\frac{u+v}{2},\qquad B:=\frac{u-v}{2},\qquad D:=u^{2}-v^{2},

we have

y\,j_{1}(Ay)j_{1}(By)=\frac{2}{Dy}\bigl[\cos(vy)+\cos(uy)\bigr]+\frac{8}{D^{2}y^{3}}\bigl[\cos(vy)-\cos(uy)-uy\sin(uy)+vy\sin(vy)\bigr].

(145)

We can therefore decompose

\Phi(y)=T_{v}(y)+T_{u}(y)+R_{3}(y),

(146)

where

T_{v}(y):=\frac{2}{y}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{A}(v)\cos(vy)\,dv,\qquad\mathcal{A}(v):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\,du,

(147)

T_{u}(y):=\frac{2}{y}\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}(u)\cos(uy)\,du,\qquad\mathcal{B}(u):=L(u)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\,dv,

(148)

R_{3}(y):=\frac{8}{y^{3}}\int_{1}^{\infty}du\int_{0}^{1}dv\;\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\bigl[\cos(vy)-\cos(uy)-uy\sin(uy)+vy\sin(vy)\bigr].

(149)

Proof.

Using
j_{1}(z)=\sin z/z^{2}-\cos z/z
, we expand
y\,j_{1}(Ay)j_{1}(By)
and apply the product-to-sum identities

\sin(Ay)\sin(By)=\tfrac{1}{2}[\cos(vy)-\cos(uy)],\qquad\cos(Ay)\cos(By)=\tfrac{1}{2}[\cos(vy)+\cos(uy)],

\sin(Ay)\cos(By)-\cos(Ay)\sin(By)=\sin(vy),

\sin(Ay)\cos(By)+\cos(Ay)\sin(By)=\sin(uy).

Collecting terms gives (
145
). The decomposition (
146
) is then obtained by substituting (
145
) into the definition of
\Phi(y)
and grouping the terms.
∎

Lemma 8

(The
v
-channel singular decomposition)
.

Let
\chi_{0}\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,1))
be a cutoff satisfying

\chi_{0}(v)=1\quad(0\leq v\leq\delta),\qquad\chi_{0}(v)=0\quad(v\geq 2\delta).

Then
\mathcal{A}(v)
admits the decomposition

\mathcal{A}(v)=2\log 2\,\chi_{0}(v)\log\frac{1}{v}+g(v),\qquad g\in W^{1,1}(0,1).

(150)

Proof.

Since

\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)=\log\frac{1}{u}+\log\frac{1}{v},

we can write

\mathcal{A}(v)=a(v)\log\frac{1}{v}+b(v),

where

a(v):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\,du,\qquad b(v):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\log(1/u)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\,du.

We introduce the constants needed for the local analysis near small
v
:

a_{\mathcal{A},0}:=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{u^{2}}\,du=2\log 2,\qquad b_{\mathcal{A},0}:=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\log(1/u)}{u^{2}}\,du.

Here we note that

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{u^{4}}\,du<\infty,\qquad\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{|L(u)\log(1/u)|}{u^{4}}\,du<\infty.

Indeed, near
u\downarrow 1
,

L(u)=\log\frac{u+1}{u-1}=O\!\left(\log\frac{1}{u-1}\right),\qquad\log\frac{1}{u}=O(u-1),

so that

\frac{L(u)}{u^{4}}=O\!\left(\log\frac{1}{u-1}\right),\qquad\frac{|L(u)\log(1/u)|}{u^{4}}=O\!\left((u-1)\log\frac{1}{u-1}\right),

both of which are integrable near
u=1
. For
u\to\infty
,

L(u)=\frac{2}{u}+O(u^{-3}),

so that

\frac{L(u)}{u^{4}}=O(u^{-5}),\qquad\frac{|L(u)\log(1/u)|}{u^{4}}=O\!\left(\frac{\log u}{u^{5}}\right),

which are integrable at infinity.

For
0<v\leq 1/2
, we have
u^{2}-v^{2}\geq u^{2}/2
for all
u\geq 1
. Using the identity

\frac{1}{u^{2}-v^{2}}=\frac{1}{u^{2}}+\frac{v^{2}}{u^{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})},

we obtain

a(v)-a_{\mathcal{A},0}=v^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{u^{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})}\,du.

Since

\left|\frac{L(u)}{u^{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})}\right|\leq 2\frac{L(u)}{u^{4}},

and the right-hand side is integrable on
(1,\infty)
, we conclude

a(v)=a_{\mathcal{A},0}+O(v^{2}).

Similarly,

b(v)-b_{\mathcal{A},0}=v^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\log(1/u)}{u^{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})}\,du,

and

\left|\frac{L(u)\log(1/u)}{u^{2}(u^{2}-v^{2})}\right|\leq 2\frac{|L(u)\log(1/u)|}{u^{4}},

where the right-hand side is integrable, giving

b(v)=b_{\mathcal{A},0}+O(v^{2}).

It follows that

\mathcal{A}(v)=2\log 2\,\log\frac{1}{v}+b_{\mathcal{A},0}+O\!\bigl(v^{2}\log(1/v)\bigr)\qquad(v\downarrow 0).

We next estimate the derivative near small
v
.
For
0<v\leq 1/2
,

\left|\partial_{v}\frac{1}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\right|=\frac{2v}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\leq 8\,\frac{v}{u^{4}}\qquad(u\geq 1),

so that differentiation under the integral sign is justified by dominated convergence, giving

a^{\prime}(v)=2v\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,du,\qquad b^{\prime}(v)=2v\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\log(1/u)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,du.

Since
(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}\geq u^{4}/4
, we obtain

|a^{\prime}(v)|\leq 8v\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{u^{4}}\,du=O(v),

|b^{\prime}(v)|\leq 8v\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{|L(u)\log(1/u)|}{u^{4}}\,du=O(v).

Since
\chi_{0}(v)=1
on
[0,\delta]
, for
0<v\leq\delta_{0}:=\min\{\delta,1/2\}
we have

g(v):=\mathcal{A}(v)-2\log 2\,\chi_{0}(v)\log\frac{1}{v}=\mathcal{A}(v)-2\log 2\log\frac{1}{v},

and therefore

g(v)=b_{\mathcal{A},0}+O\!\bigl(v^{2}\log(1/v)\bigr).

Moreover,

g^{\prime}(v)=a^{\prime}(v)\log\frac{1}{v}-\frac{a(v)-2\log 2}{v}+b^{\prime}(v),

so that using

a^{\prime}(v)=O(v),\qquad a(v)-2\log 2=O(v^{2}),\qquad b^{\prime}(v)=O(v),

we obtain

g^{\prime}(v)=O\!\bigl(v\log(1/v)\bigr)\qquad(v\downarrow 0).

In particular,

g,\ g^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,\delta_{0}).

Near
v\uparrow 1
, we have
\chi_{0}(v)=0
, so that
g(v)=\mathcal{A}(v)
. Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and decompose

\mathcal{A}(v)=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}(v)+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{far}}(v),

where

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}(v):=\int_{1}^{1+\varepsilon}\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\,du,\qquad\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{far}}(v):=\int_{1+\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\,du.

Since
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{far}}
is
C^{\infty}
on
[1-\varepsilon,1)
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{far}},\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{far}}^{\prime}\in L^{1}(1-\varepsilon,1)
trivially. It therefore suffices to estimate
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}
.

Setting
v=1-\eta
and
u=1+\delta
with
0<\eta,\delta<\varepsilon
, we have

u^{2}-v^{2}=(u-v)(u+v)=(\delta+\eta)(u+v),

and since
u+v\in[1,3]
,

c_{0}(\delta+\eta)\leq u^{2}-v^{2}\leq C_{0}(\delta+\eta).

(151)

Moreover,

L(u)=\log\frac{u+1}{u-1}=\log\frac{2+\delta}{\delta}\leq C\log\frac{e}{\delta},

and for
\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)=\log(1/u)+\log(1/v)
,

\left|\log\frac{1}{u}\right|=\log(1+\delta)\leq C\delta,\qquad\left|\log\frac{1}{v}\right|=-\log(1-\eta)\leq C\eta,

so that

|\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)|\leq C(\delta+\eta).

(152)

Using (
151
) and (
152
), we obtain

\left|\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\right|\leq C\log\frac{e}{\delta},

and therefore

|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}(v)|\leq C\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\log\frac{e}{\delta}\,d\delta<\infty,

which gives
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}\in L^{1}(1-\varepsilon,1)
.

We next estimate the derivative. Differentiating in
v
gives

\partial_{v}\left(\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\right)=L(u)\left[-\frac{1}{v(u^{2}-v^{2})}+\frac{2v\,\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\right].

Using (
151
) and (
152
), we obtain

\left|\partial_{v}\left(\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\right)\right|\leq C\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{\delta+\eta}.

Since this majorant is integrable in
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
, differentiation under the integral sign is justified by dominated convergence, and we obtain

|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\prime}(v)|\leq C\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{\delta+\eta}\,d\delta.

Splitting the right-hand side over
(0,\eta)\cup(\eta,\varepsilon)
, we have

\int_{0}^{\eta}\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{\delta+\eta}\,d\delta\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{0}^{\eta}\log\frac{e}{\delta}\,d\delta=O\!\left(\log\frac{e}{\eta}\right),

\int_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{\delta+\eta}\,d\delta\leq\int_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{\delta}\,d\delta=O\!\left(\log^{2}\frac{e}{\eta}\right).

Therefore

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\prime}(v)=O\!\left(\log^{2}\frac{e}{1-v}\right)\qquad(v\uparrow 1),

and in particular

\int_{1-\varepsilon}^{1}|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\prime}(v)|\,dv<\infty.

We conclude that
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{far}}\in W^{1,1}(1-\varepsilon,1)
. Combining with the estimate near
v\downarrow 0
established above, we obtain
g\in W^{1,1}(0,1)
.
∎

Proposition 9

(Rigorous
T_{v}(y)
asymptotics)
.

We can write

T_{v}(y)=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}+G_{v}(y),

(153)

where
G_{v}(y)
is locally integrable on
[1,\infty)
and its Mellin transform

\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}G_{v}(y)\,dy

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.
In particular,

T_{v}(y)=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}+O(y^{-2})\qquad(y\to\infty).

(154)

Proof.

By Lemma
8
,

T_{v}(y)=\frac{4\log 2}{y}\int_{0}^{1}\chi_{0}(v)\log\frac{1}{v}\,\cos(vy)\,dv+\frac{2}{y}\int_{0}^{1}g(v)\cos(vy)\,dv=:T_{v}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)+T_{v}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y).

Step 1: Representation and
O(y^{-2})
estimate for the regular part.

Since
g\in W^{1,1}(0,1)
, one integration by parts gives

\int_{0}^{1}g(v)\cos(vy)\,dv=\frac{g(1)\sin y}{y}-\frac{1}{y}\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv.

Therefore

T_{v}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y)=\frac{2g(1)\sin y}{y^{2}}-\frac{2}{y^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv.

(155)

Since
g^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,1)
,

\left|\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv\right|\leq\int_{0}^{1}|g^{\prime}(v)|\,dv,

and therefore

T_{v}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y)=O(y^{-2})\qquad(y\to\infty).

(156)

Step 2: Holomorphy of the Mellin transform of the regular part.

We first show that

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\sin y\,dy

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
. Let
K\subset\{\Re s<3\}
be a compact set and set
\sigma_{K}:=\sup_{s\in K}\Re s<3
. One integration by parts gives

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\sin y\,dy=\cos 1+(s-3)\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-4}\cos y\,dy,

where
\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\sigma_{K}-4}dy<\infty
, so that dominated convergence on compact subsets shows

s\mapsto\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\sin y\,dy

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
. The first term of (
155
) therefore contributes

2g(1)\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\sin y\,dy,

which is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.

For the second term, we set for
R>1

I_{R}(s):=\int_{1}^{R}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv\right)dy.

On the finite interval
[1,R]
, Fubini’s theorem gives

I_{R}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\,K_{s,R}(v)\,dv,

where

K_{s,R}(v):=\int_{1}^{R}y^{s-3}\sin(vy)\,dy=v^{2-s}\int_{v}^{vR}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt\qquad(0<v\leq 1).

Fix a compact set
K\subset\{\Re s<3\}
and set

\sigma_{-}:=\inf_{s\in K}\Re s,\qquad\sigma_{+}:=\sup_{s\in K}\Re s<3,\qquad\beta_{K}:=\min\{1,\,2-\sigma_{+}\}>-1.

For
0<v\leq 1
,
R\geq 1
, and
s\in K
, we split

K_{s,R}(v)=v^{2-s}\int_{v}^{\min\{1,vR\}}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt+v^{2-s}\mathbf{1}_{\{vR>1\}}\int_{1}^{vR}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt=:K_{s,R}^{(0,1)}(v)+K_{s,R}^{(1,\infty)}(v).

For the local part, using
|\sin t|\leq t
on
[0,1]
:

|K_{s,R}^{(0,1)}(v)|\leq v^{2-\Re s}\int_{v}^{1}t^{\Re s-2}\,dt.

Writing
\sigma:=\Re s
, for
\sigma\leq 1
we have

v^{2-\sigma}\int_{v}^{1}t^{\sigma-2}\,dt=v\int_{v}^{1}\left(\frac{v}{t}\right)^{1-\sigma}\frac{dt}{t}\leq v\log\frac{1}{v},

while for
1<\sigma<3
,

v^{2-\sigma}\int_{v}^{1}t^{\sigma-2}\,dt=v^{2-\sigma}\frac{1-v^{\sigma-1}}{\sigma-1}\leq v^{2-\sigma}|\log v|\leq v^{2-\sigma_{+}}|\log v|.

Therefore

|K_{s,R}^{(0,1)}(v)|\leq C_{K}\,v^{\beta_{K}}(1+|\log v|).

(157)

For the tail part, one integration by parts gives

\int_{1}^{vR}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt=\cos 1-(vR)^{s-3}\cos(vR)+(s-3)\int_{1}^{vR}t^{s-4}\cos t\,dt.

Since
|(vR)^{s-3}|\leq 1
and
\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{\sigma_{+}-4}dt<\infty
, we have

\left|\int_{1}^{vR}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt\right|\leq C_{K},

and therefore

|K_{s,R}^{(1,\infty)}(v)|\leq C_{K}\,v^{2-\Re s}\leq C_{K}\,v^{2-\sigma_{+}}\leq C_{K}\,v^{\beta_{K}}.

Combining with (
157
), we obtain the master bound

|K_{s,R}(v)|\leq C_{K}\,v^{\beta_{K}}(1+|\log v|)\qquad(0<v\leq 1,\ R\geq 1,\ s\in K).

(158)

As shown in the proof of Lemma
8
, there exists
v_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

|g^{\prime}(v)|\leq C\,v\log\frac{e}{v}\qquad(0<v\leq v_{0}).

Since
\beta_{K}>-1
, we have

|g^{\prime}(v)|\,|K_{s,R}(v)|\leq C_{K}\,v^{1+\beta_{K}}\Bigl(\log\frac{e}{v}\Bigr)\bigl(1+|\log v|\bigr),

which is integrable near
v=0
. On
[v_{0},1]
, the factor
v^{\beta_{K}}(1+|\log v|)
is bounded and
g^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,1)
, so that

\int_{0}^{1}|g^{\prime}(v)|\,v^{\beta_{K}}(1+|\log v|)\,dv<\infty.

By dominated convergence with (
158
) as the majorant, we conclude that as
R\to\infty
,

I_{R}(s)\to I(s):=\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\,K_{s}(v)\,dv,

where

K_{s}(v):=v^{2-s}\int_{v}^{\infty}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt.

This convergence is uniform on compact subsets. Writing

K_{s}(v)=v^{2-s}\int_{v}^{1}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt+v^{2-s}\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt,

we see that the right-hand side is holomorphic in
s
for
\Re s<3
. By dominated convergence on compact subsets (or by Morera’s theorem),

s\mapsto I(s)=\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)K_{s}(v)\,dv

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.

It follows that

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}\left[-\frac{2}{y^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv\right]dy=-2I(s)

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
, and therefore
\mathcal{M}_{>}[T_{v}^{\mathrm{reg}}](s)
is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.

Step 3: Representation and
O(y^{-2})
estimate for the singular part.

We write

a_{\delta}(v):=\chi_{0}(v)\log\frac{1}{v}\qquad(\delta\leq v\leq 2\delta).

Then
a_{\delta}\in W^{1,1}(\delta,2\delta)
with

a_{\delta}(2\delta)=0,\qquad a_{\delta}(\delta)=\log\frac{1}{\delta},

and

T_{v}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)=\frac{4\log 2}{y}\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{v}\cos(vy)\,dv+R_{\delta}(y),

where

R_{\delta}(y):=\frac{4\log 2}{y}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}(v)\cos(vy)\,dv.

One integration by parts gives

R_{\delta}(y)=\frac{4\log 2\,\log\delta}{y^{2}}\sin(\delta y)-\frac{4\log 2}{y^{2}}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv.

(159)

Using the exact formula

\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{v}\cos(vy)\,dv=\frac{\operatorname{Si}(\delta y)-(\log\delta)\sin(\delta y)}{y},

we obtain

\frac{4\log 2}{y}\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{v}\cos(vy)\,dv=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}-\frac{4\log 2\,\log\delta}{y^{2}}\sin(\delta y)+Q_{v}(y),

where

Q_{v}(y):=\frac{4\log 2}{y^{2}}\left(\operatorname{Si}(\delta y)-\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

(160)

Combining with (
159
), the
\sin(\delta y)/y^{2}
terms cancel, giving

T_{v}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}+Q_{v}(y)-\frac{4\log 2}{y^{2}}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv.

We therefore have

G_{v}(y)=\frac{2g(1)\sin y}{y^{2}}-\frac{2}{y^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv-\frac{4\log 2}{y^{2}}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv+Q_{v}(y).

(161)

Since
\operatorname{Si}(x)-\pi/2=-\int_{x}^{\infty}\sin t\,t^{-1}dt
and one integration by parts gives

\operatorname{Si}(x)-\frac{\pi}{2}=O(x^{-1})\qquad(x\to\infty),

we have

Q_{v}(y)=O(y^{-3})\qquad(y\to\infty).

Since
a_{\delta}^{\prime}\in L^{1}(\delta,2\delta)
,

\left|\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv\right|\leq\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}|a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)|\,dv.

Combining with (
156
), we conclude

G_{v}(y)=O(y^{-2})\qquad(y\to\infty),

which gives (
154
).

Step 4: Holomorphy of the Mellin transform of the singular part.

Since
Q_{v}(y)=O(y^{-3})
,

\mathcal{M}_{>}[Q_{v}](s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}Q_{v}(y)\,dy

converges absolutely for
\Re s<3
and is holomorphic there by dominated convergence on compact subsets.

For the remaining term, we set

J_{R}(s):=\int_{1}^{R}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv\right)dy.

Fubini’s theorem on the finite interval gives

J_{R}(s)=\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)K_{s,R}(v)\,dv,

where
K_{s,R}(v)
is as defined above. Since
v\in[\delta,2\delta]
is bounded away from zero, the master bound (
158
) immediately gives

|K_{s,R}(v)|\leq C_{K}\qquad(\delta\leq v\leq 2\delta,\ R\geq 1,\ s\in K).

Since
a_{\delta}^{\prime}\in L^{1}(\delta,2\delta)
, dominated convergence as
R\to\infty
gives

J(s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv\right)dy=\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)K_{s}(v)\,dv.

Since
K_{s}(v)
is holomorphic in
s
for
\Re s<3
on
v\in[\delta,2\delta]
, dominated convergence on compact subsets (or Morera’s theorem) shows that
J(s)
is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.

Each term of (
161
) therefore has a Mellin transform that is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
, and we conclude that

\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s)=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}G_{v}(y)\,dy

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.

Finally,
G_{v}
is continuous on
y\geq 1
by (
161
), hence locally integrable on
[1,\infty)
. This completes the proof of (
153
).
∎

Lemma 10

(Exact formula for
J(u)
and regular decomposition of
\mathcal{B}(u)
)
.

Define

J(u):=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\,dv,\qquad\mathcal{B}(u):=L(u)\,J(u),\qquad u>1.

Then the following hold:

(i)

J(u)=\frac{1}{2u}\left[\operatorname{Li}_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)-\operatorname{Li}_{2}\!\left(-\frac{1}{u}\right)-\log u\,L(u)\right].

(162)

(ii)

As
u\downarrow 1
,

J(u)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}+O\!\bigl((u-1)|\log(u-1)|\bigr),

(163)

and as
u\to\infty
,

J(u)=\frac{1-\log u}{u^{2}}+O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{4}}\right).

(164)

(iii)

Let
\chi_{1}\in C_{c}^{\infty}([1,\infty))
be a cutoff satisfying

\chi_{1}(u)=1\quad(1\leq u\leq 1+\delta),\qquad\chi_{1}(u)=0\quad(u\geq 1+2\delta),

and set

h(u):=\mathcal{B}(u)-\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\chi_{1}(u)L(u).

(165)

Then

h\in W^{1,1}(1,\infty).

(166)

Proof.

Substituting
v=ut
and setting
a:=1/u
, we have
dv=u\,dt
and
u^{2}-v^{2}=u^{2}(1-t^{2})
, so that

J(u)=\frac{1}{u}\int_{0}^{1/u}\frac{-2\log u-\log t}{1-t^{2}}\,dt.

Using the partial fraction decomposition

\frac{1}{1-t^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-t}+\frac{1}{1+t}\right)

and the polylogarithm antiderivatives

\int_{0}^{a}\frac{\log t}{1-t}\,dt=-\operatorname{Li}_{2}(a)-\log a\,\log(1-a),

\int_{0}^{a}\frac{\log t}{1+t}\,dt=\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-a)+\log a\,\log(1+a),

we obtain (
162
).

For
u\downarrow 1
, we set
u=1+\eta
and
1/u=1-\eta+O(\eta^{2})
. Applying the identity

\operatorname{Li}_{2}(x)+\operatorname{Li}_{2}(1-x)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\log x\,\log(1-x)

with
x=1/u
, we obtain

\operatorname{Li}_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{u}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+O(\eta|\log\eta|),\qquad\operatorname{Li}_{2}\!\left(-\frac{1}{u}\right)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}+O(\eta),

\log u\,L(u)=O(\eta|\log\eta|),

from which (
163
) follows.

For
u\to\infty
, we set
x=1/u\to 0
and substitute the expansions

\operatorname{Li}_{2}(x)=x+\frac{x^{2}}{4}+O(x^{3}),\qquad\operatorname{Li}_{2}(-x)=-x+\frac{x^{2}}{4}+O(x^{3}),\qquad L(u)=\frac{2}{u}+\frac{2}{3u^{3}}+O(u^{-5})

into (
162
) to obtain (
164
).

We next estimate
h
. Near
u\downarrow 1
, we have
\chi_{1}=1
, so that

h(u)=L(u)\left(J(u)-\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\right).

By (
163
) and
L(u)=O(|\log(u-1)|)
,

h(u)=O\!\bigl((u-1)|\log(u-1)|^{2}\bigr),

and therefore
h\in L^{1}(1,1+\delta)
.

Differentiating (
162
) gives

J^{\prime}(u)=-\frac{J(u)}{u}-\frac{L(u)}{u^{2}}-\frac{\log u}{2u}L^{\prime}(u).

(167)

Near
u\downarrow 1
,

L(u)=O(|\log(u-1)|),\qquad L^{\prime}(u)=O((u-1)^{-1}),\qquad\log u=O(u-1),

so that

J^{\prime}(u)=O(|\log(u-1)|).

Therefore

h^{\prime}(u)=L^{\prime}(u)\left(J(u)-\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\right)+L(u)J^{\prime}(u)=O(|\log(u-1)|)+O(|\log(u-1)|^{2}),

and

\int_{1}^{1+\delta}|h^{\prime}(u)|\,du<\infty.

For
u\geq 1+2\delta
, we have
\chi_{1}=0
, so that
h(u)=\mathcal{B}(u)
. Using (
164
) and
L(u)=2u^{-1}+O(u^{-3})
, we obtain

h(u)=O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{3}}\right),\qquad h^{\prime}(u)=O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{4}}\right),

so that
h,h^{\prime}\in L^{1}
on
(1+2\delta,\infty)
as well. This gives (
166
).
∎

Lemma 11

(Endpoint asymptotics of the
u
-channel amplitude
D(u)
)
.

Define

D(u):=L(u)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,dv,\qquad u>1.

(168)

Then the following hold.

(i)

Setting
J(u):=\int_{0}^{1}\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)/(u^{2}-v^{2})\,dv
, we have

D(u)=-\frac{L(u)}{2u}J^{\prime}(u)-\frac{L(u)^{2}}{4u^{3}}.

(169)

(ii)

As
u\downarrow 1
,

D(u)=\frac{1}{4}\,L(u)^{2}+O(L(u)),\qquad D^{\prime}(u)=O\!\left(\frac{L(u)}{u-1}\right).

(170)

(iii)

As
u\to\infty
,

D(u)=O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{5}}\right),\qquad D^{\prime}(u)=O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{6}}\right),

(171)

and therefore

D,\ D^{\prime},\ uD,\ (uD)^{\prime}\in L^{1}(1+\delta,\infty)\qquad(\forall\delta>0).

Proof.

Differentiating

J(u)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\,dv

with respect to
u
, we have

\partial_{u}\!\left(\frac{\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{u^{2}-v^{2}}\right)=-\frac{1}{u(u^{2}-v^{2})}-\frac{2u\,\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}},

and therefore

J^{\prime}(u)=-\frac{1}{u}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dv}{u^{2}-v^{2}}-2u\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,dv.

Since

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dv}{u^{2}-v^{2}}=\frac{L(u)}{2u},

we obtain

J^{\prime}(u)=-\frac{L(u)}{2u^{2}}-2u\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,dv,

from which (
169
) follows.

For
u\downarrow 1
, Lemma
10
gives

J(u)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}+O((u-1)|\log(u-1)|).

Using (
167
) and

L^{\prime}(u)=-\frac{2}{u^{2}-1}=-\frac{1}{u-1}+O(1)\qquad(u\downarrow 1),

we obtain

J^{\prime}(u)=-L(u)+O(1).

Substituting this into (
169
) gives

D(u)=-\frac{L(u)}{2u}\bigl[-L(u)+O(1)\bigr]-\frac{L(u)^{2}}{4u^{3}}=\frac{1}{4}L(u)^{2}+O(L(u)),

which is the first part of (
170
).

Differentiating (
169
) gives

D^{\prime}(u)=-\frac{L^{\prime}(u)}{2u}J^{\prime}(u)+\frac{L(u)}{2u^{2}}J^{\prime}(u)-\frac{L(u)}{2u}J^{\prime\prime}(u)-\frac{L(u)L^{\prime}(u)}{2u^{3}}+\frac{3L(u)^{2}}{4u^{4}}.

Near
u\downarrow 1
,

L(u)=O(|\log(u-1)|),\qquad L^{\prime}(u)=O((u-1)^{-1}),\qquad J^{\prime}(u)=O(L(u)).

Differentiating (
167
) once more and using

L^{\prime\prime}(u)=O((u-1)^{-2}),\qquad(\log u)L^{\prime\prime}(u)=O((u-1)^{-1}),

we obtain

J^{\prime\prime}(u)=O((u-1)^{-1}).

Collecting all terms then gives

D^{\prime}(u)=O\!\left(\frac{L(u)}{u-1}\right),

which is the second part of (
170
).

Finally, for
u\to\infty
, substituting

J(u)=\frac{1-\log u}{u^{2}}+O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{4}}\right),\qquad L(u)=\frac{2}{u}+\frac{2}{3u^{3}}+O(u^{-5}),

J^{\prime}(u)=O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{3}}\right),\qquad L^{\prime}(u)=O(u^{-2})

into (
169
) gives

D(u)=O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{5}}\right).

Further differentiation gives

D^{\prime}(u)=O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{6}}\right),

which establishes (
171
) and the tail
L^{1}
integrability.
∎

Lemma 12

(Oscillatory integral with logarithmic endpoint singularity)
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Suppose
g\in C^{1}((0,\delta])
satisfies

|g(t)|\leq C\,\log^{2}\!\frac{e}{t},\qquad|g^{\prime}(t)|\leq C\,\frac{\log(e/t)}{t}\qquad(0<t\leq\delta).

(172)

Then as
y\to\infty
,

\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)e^{\mathrm{i}yt}\,dt=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),

(173)

and in particular,

\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)\cos(yt)\,dt=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),\qquad\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)\sin(yt)\,dt=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

(174)

Proof.

We may assume
y\geq\delta^{-1}
. We split the integral as

\int_{0}^{\delta}=\int_{0}^{1/y}+\int_{1/y}^{\delta}.

For the first part, (
172
) gives

\left|\int_{0}^{1/y}g(t)e^{\mathrm{i}yt}\,dt\right|\leq\int_{0}^{1/y}|g(t)|\,dt\leq\frac{C}{y}\log^{2}(ey)=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

For the second part, we integrate by parts:

\int_{1/y}^{\delta}g(t)e^{\mathrm{i}yt}\,dt=\left[\frac{g(t)e^{\mathrm{i}yt}}{\mathrm{i}y}\right]_{1/y}^{\delta}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}y}\int_{1/y}^{\delta}g^{\prime}(t)e^{\mathrm{i}yt}\,dt.

The boundary terms satisfy

\frac{|g(\delta)|+|g(1/y)|}{y}=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

By (
172
),

\int_{1/y}^{\delta}|g^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\leq C\int_{1/y}^{\delta}\frac{\log(e/t)}{t}\,dt=O((\log y)^{2}),

so that the second part is also

O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

This gives (
173
), and (
174
) follows by taking real and imaginary parts.
∎

Lemma 13

(Endpoint control of the
v
-channel remainder amplitude)
.

Define

C(v):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,du,\qquad 0<v<1.

Then the following hold.

(i)

As
v\downarrow 0
,

C(v)=c_{0}\log\frac{1}{v}+d_{0}+O\!\bigl(v^{2}\log(1/v)\bigr),

where

c_{0}:=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{u^{4}}\,du,\qquad d_{0}:=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\log(1/u)}{u^{4}}\,du.

(ii)

We have

C,\ vC\in L^{1}(0,1).

(iii)

As
y\to\infty
,

\int_{0}^{1}C(v)\cos(vy)\,dv=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),\qquad\int_{0}^{1}vC(v)\sin(vy)\,dv=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

(175)

Proof.

We write

C(v)=a(v)\log\frac{1}{v}+b(v),

where

a(v):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,du,\qquad b(v):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\log(1/u)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,du.

Since

\frac{L(u)}{u^{4}}=O\!\left(\log\frac{1}{u-1}\right)\qquad(u\downarrow 1),\qquad\frac{L(u)}{u^{4}}=O(u^{-5})\qquad(u\to\infty),

and

\frac{L(u)\log(1/u)}{u^{4}}=O\!\left((u-1)\log\frac{1}{u-1}\right)\qquad(u\downarrow 1),\qquad\frac{L(u)\log(1/u)}{u^{4}}=O\!\left(\frac{\log u}{u^{5}}\right)\qquad(u\to\infty),

both defining integrals for
c_{0}
and
d_{0}
converge absolutely.

For
0<v\leq 1/2
, we have
u^{2}-v^{2}\geq u^{2}/2
for all
u\geq 1
, and

\frac{1}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}-\frac{1}{u^{4}}=\frac{v^{2}(2u^{2}-v^{2})}{u^{4}(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}.

Therefore

|a(v)-c_{0}|\leq Cv^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{u^{6}}\,du=O(v^{2}),

and similarly

|b(v)-d_{0}|\leq Cv^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{|L(u)\log(1/u)|}{u^{6}}\,du=O(v^{2}).

This gives the expansion in (i).

We next differentiate near
v=0
.
For
0<v\leq 1/2
,

\partial_{v}\!\left(\frac{1}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\right)=\frac{4v}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{3}},

so that

|a^{\prime}(v)|\leq 32v\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)}{u^{6}}\,du=O(v),\qquad|b^{\prime}(v)|\leq 32v\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{|L(u)\log(1/u)|}{u^{6}}\,du=O(v).

Hence

C^{\prime}(v)=a^{\prime}(v)\log\frac{1}{v}-\frac{a(v)}{v}+b^{\prime}(v)=-\frac{c_{0}}{v}+O\!\bigl(v\log(1/v)\bigr),

(176)

and in particular

|C(v)|\leq C\log^{2}\!\frac{e}{v},\qquad|C^{\prime}(v)|\leq C\,\frac{\log(e/v)}{v}\qquad(0<v\leq v_{0}).

(177)

Also,

|vC(v)|\leq C\,v\log\frac{e}{v},\qquad|(vC(v))^{\prime}|=|C(v)+vC^{\prime}(v)|\leq C\log\frac{e}{v},

so that

|vC(v)|\leq C\log^{2}\!\frac{e}{v},\qquad|(vC(v))^{\prime}|\leq C\,\frac{\log(e/v)}{v}\qquad(0<v\leq v_{0}).

(178)

We next analyze the endpoint
v\uparrow 1
.
Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and write

C(v)=C_{\mathrm{loc}}(v)+C_{\mathrm{far}}(v),

where

C_{\mathrm{loc}}(v):=\int_{1}^{1+\varepsilon}\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,du,\qquad C_{\mathrm{far}}(v):=\int_{1+\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\,du.

Since
C_{\mathrm{far}}
is
C^{\infty}
on
[1-\varepsilon,1)
, it remains to estimate
C_{\mathrm{loc}}
.
Set
v=1-\eta
and
u=1+\delta
with
0<\eta,\delta<\varepsilon
.
As in the proof of Lemma
8
,

c_{\varepsilon}(\delta+\eta)\leq u^{2}-v^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}(\delta+\eta),

L(u)\leq C\log\frac{e}{\delta},\qquad|\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)|\leq C(\delta+\eta).

Therefore

\left|\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\right|\leq C\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{\delta+\eta},

(179)

and hence

|C_{\mathrm{loc}}(v)|\leq C\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{\delta+\eta}\,d\delta=O\!\left(\log^{2}\frac{e}{\eta}\right)\qquad(\eta\downarrow 0).

Thus

|C(v)|\leq C\log^{2}\!\frac{e}{1-v}\qquad(1-\varepsilon<v<1).

(180)

In particular,
C\in L^{1}(1-\varepsilon,1)
and therefore
C\in L^{1}(0,1)
.
Since
0<v\leq 1
, this also gives
vC\in L^{1}(0,1)
.

Differentiating under the integral sign in the local part gives

\partial_{v}\left(\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\right)=L(u)\left[-\frac{1}{v(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}+\frac{4v\,\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{3}}\right].

Using the above local estimates, we obtain

\left|\partial_{v}\left(\frac{L(u)\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)}{(u^{2}-v^{2})^{2}}\right)\right|\leq C\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{(\delta+\eta)^{2}}.

Splitting the
\delta
-integral over
(0,\eta)\cup(\eta,\varepsilon)
gives

|C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{loc}}(v)|\leq C\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\frac{\log(e/\delta)}{(\delta+\eta)^{2}}\,d\delta=O\!\left(\frac{\log(e/\eta)}{\eta}\right)\qquad(\eta\downarrow 0).

Since
C^{\prime}_{\mathrm{far}}
is bounded near
v=1
, we conclude that

|C^{\prime}(v)|\leq C\,\frac{\log(e/(1-v))}{1-v}\qquad(1-\varepsilon<v<1).

(181)

Also,

((1-t)C(1-t))^{\prime}=-C(1-t)-(1-t)C^{\prime}(1-t),

so that (
180
) and (
181
) give

|(1-t)C(1-t)|\leq C\log^{2}\!\frac{e}{t},\qquad\left|((1-t)C(1-t))^{\prime}\right|\leq C\,\frac{\log(e/t)}{t}\qquad(0<t<\varepsilon).

(182)

On any compact interval
[v_{0},1-\varepsilon]\subset(0,1)
, differentiation under the integral sign is justified by dominated convergence, and
C,vC\in W^{1,1}(v_{0},1-\varepsilon)
.
Therefore one integration by parts gives

\int_{v_{0}}^{1-\varepsilon}C(v)e^{ivy}\,dv=O(y^{-1}),\qquad\int_{v_{0}}^{1-\varepsilon}vC(v)e^{ivy}\,dv=O(y^{-1}).

For the endpoint regions, we apply Lemma
12
.
Using (
177
) and (
178
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{v_{0}}C(v)e^{ivy}\,dv=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),\qquad\int_{0}^{v_{0}}vC(v)e^{ivy}\,dv=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

For the endpoint near
v=1
, changing variables
t=1-v
gives

\int_{1-\varepsilon}^{1}C(v)e^{ivy}\,dv=e^{iy}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}C(1-t)e^{-iyt}\,dt=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),

by (
180
) and (
181
), and similarly

\int_{1-\varepsilon}^{1}vC(v)e^{ivy}\,dv=e^{iy}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}(1-t)C(1-t)e^{-iyt}\,dt=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),

by (
182
).
Combining the endpoint pieces with the middle interval estimate and taking real or imaginary parts yields (
175
).
∎

Proposition 14

(Fourier estimates for the
u
-channel remainder amplitude)
.

Let
D(u)
be defined by Lemma
11
.
Then as
y\to\infty
,

\int_{1}^{\infty}D(u)\cos(uy)\,du=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),\qquad\int_{1}^{\infty}uD(u)\sin(uy)\,du=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

(183)

Proof.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
.
For the first integral, we split

\int_{1}^{\infty}D(u)\cos(uy)\,du=\int_{1}^{1+\delta}D(u)\cos(uy)\,du+\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}D(u)\cos(uy)\,du.

Setting
u=1+t
in the local part and using Lemma
11
,

D(1+t)=\frac{1}{4}\,L(1+t)^{2}+O(L(1+t)),\qquad D^{\prime}(1+t)=O\!\left(\frac{L(1+t)}{t}\right)\qquad(0<t\leq\delta).

Since

L(1+t)=\log\frac{2+t}{t}\leq C\log\frac{e}{t},

we obtain

|D(1+t)|\leq C\log^{2}\!\frac{e}{t},\qquad|D^{\prime}(1+t)|\leq C\,\frac{\log(e/t)}{t}\qquad(0<t\leq\delta).

Lemma
12
therefore gives

\int_{1}^{1+\delta}D(u)\cos(uy)\,du=\Re\!\left[e^{iy}\int_{0}^{\delta}D(1+t)e^{iyt}\,dt\right]=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

For the tail part,
D,D^{\prime}\in L^{1}(1+\delta,\infty)
by Lemma
11
, and one integration by parts gives

\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}D(u)\cos(uy)\,du=-\frac{D(1+\delta)\sin((1+\delta)y)}{y}-\frac{1}{y}\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}D^{\prime}(u)\sin(uy)\,du=O(y^{-1}).

This proves the first estimate in (
183
).

For the second integral, we set

g(t):=(1+t)D(1+t)\qquad(0<t\leq\delta).

Then

|g(t)|\leq C\log^{2}\!\frac{e}{t},

and

g^{\prime}(t)=D(1+t)+(1+t)D^{\prime}(1+t).

By the bounds above and Lemma
11
,

|D(1+t)|\leq C\log^{2}\!\frac{e}{t}\leq C\,\frac{\log(e/t)}{t},\qquad|(1+t)D^{\prime}(1+t)|\leq C\,\frac{\log(e/t)}{t},

hence

|g^{\prime}(t)|\leq C\,\frac{\log(e/t)}{t}\qquad(0<t\leq\delta).

Applying Lemma
12
once more, we obtain

\int_{1}^{1+\delta}uD(u)\sin(uy)\,du=\Im\!\left[e^{iy}\int_{0}^{\delta}g(t)e^{iyt}\,dt\right]=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

For the tail,
uD,(uD)^{\prime}\in L^{1}(1+\delta,\infty)
by Lemma
11
, and one integration by parts gives

\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}uD(u)\sin(uy)\,du=\frac{(1+\delta)D(1+\delta)\cos((1+\delta)y)}{y}+\frac{1}{y}\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}(uD)^{\prime}(u)\cos(uy)\,du=O(y^{-1}).

Combining the local and tail parts gives (
183
).
∎

Proposition 15

(Rigorous large-
y
asymptotic structure)
.

For

\Phi(y)=T_{v}(y)+T_{u}(y)+R_{3}(y),

we can write

\Phi(y)=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{\log y}{y^{2}}\sin y+G(y),

(184)

where
G(y)
is locally integrable on
[1,\infty)
and its Mellin transform

\mathcal{M}_{>}[G](s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}G(y)\,dy

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.
In particular,

\Phi(y)=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{\log y}{y^{2}}\sin y+O(y^{-2})\qquad(y\to\infty).

(185)

Proof.

By Lemma
7
,

\Phi(y)=T_{v}(y)+T_{u}(y)+R_{3}(y).

For
T_{v}(y)
, Proposition
9
gives

T_{v}(y)=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}+G_{v}(y),

where
G_{v}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([1,\infty))
and

\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}G_{v}(y)\,dy

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.

Step 1: Regular part of
T_{u}
.

Using the decomposition from Lemma
10
,

\mathcal{B}(u)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\chi_{1}(u)L(u)+h(u),

we write

T_{u}(y)=T_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)+T_{u}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y),

where

T_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y}\int_{1}^{\infty}\chi_{1}(u)L(u)\cos(uy)\,du,\qquad T_{u}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y)=\frac{2}{y}\int_{1}^{\infty}h(u)\cos(uy)\,du.

Since
h\in W^{1,1}(1,\infty)
, one integration by parts gives

T_{u}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y)=\frac{2h(1)\sin y}{y^{2}}-\frac{2}{y^{2}}\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)\sin(uy)\,du.

(186)

Since
h^{\prime}\in L^{1}(1,\infty)
,

T_{u}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y)=O(y^{-2})\qquad(y\to\infty).

We next show that
\mathcal{M}_{>}[T_{u}^{\mathrm{reg}}](s)
is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
. The Mellin transform of the first term,

2h(1)\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\sin y\,dy,

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
: one integration by parts gives

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\sin y\,dy=\cos 1+(s-3)\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-4}\cos y\,dy,

where the right-hand side converges absolutely for
\Re s<3
.

For the second term, we set for
R>1

I_{h,R}(s):=\int_{1}^{R}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)\sin(uy)\,du\right)dy.

Fubini’s theorem on the finite interval
[1,R]
gives

I_{h,R}(s)=\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)L_{s,R}(u)\,du,

where

L_{s,R}(u):=u^{2-s}\int_{u}^{uR}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt.

Fix a compact set
K\subset\{\Re s<3\}
and set
\sigma_{K}:=\sup_{s\in K}\Re s<3
. One integration by parts gives

L_{s,R}(u)=u^{-1}\cos u-u^{-1}R^{s-3}\cos(uR)+(s-3)u^{2-s}\int_{u}^{uR}t^{s-4}\cos t\,dt.

Since
|R^{s-3}|=R^{\Re s-3}\leq 1
and

u^{2-\Re s}\int_{u}^{uR}t^{\Re s-4}\,dt\leq u^{2-\Re s}\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{\Re s-4}\,dt=\frac{u^{-1}}{3-\Re s}\leq\frac{1}{3-\sigma_{K}},

we obtain

|L_{s,R}(u)|\leq C_{K}(1+|s|)\qquad(u\geq 1,\ R\geq 1,\ s\in K).

(187)

Since
h^{\prime}\in L^{1}(1,\infty)
, dominated convergence as
R\to\infty
gives

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)\sin(uy)\,du\right)dy=\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)L_{s}(u)\,du,

where

L_{s}(u):=u^{2-s}\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt.

The same majorant shows that dominated convergence holds on compact subsets, so that

s\longmapsto\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)L_{s}(u)\,du

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
. Therefore
\mathcal{M}_{>}[T_{u}^{\mathrm{reg}}](s)
is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.

Step 2: Decomposition of the singular part of
T_{u}
.

Setting
u=1+t
and writing

\chi_{*}(t):=\chi_{1}(1+t),\qquad L(1+t)=\log\frac{2+t}{t}=\log\frac{1}{t}+r(t),\qquad r(t):=\log(2+t),

we have

T_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y}\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi_{*}(t)\log\frac{1}{t}\,\cos(y(1+t))\,dt+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y}\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi_{*}(t)r(t)\cos(y(1+t))\,dt.

We write the second term as

U_{r}(y):=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y}\int_{0}^{\infty}a(t)\cos(y(1+t))\,dt,\qquad a(t):=\chi_{*}(t)r(t).

Since
a\in W^{1,1}(0,\infty)
with
\operatorname{supp}a\subset[0,2\delta]
, one integration by parts gives

U_{r}(y)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{a(0)\sin y}{y^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{\prime}(t)\sin(y(1+t))\,dt,

(188)

so that
U_{r}(y)=O(y^{-2})
.

Using
\chi_{*}(t)=1
for
0\leq t\leq\delta
, we write

\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi_{*}(t)\log\frac{1}{t}\,\cos(y(1+t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{t}\,\cos(y(1+t))\,dt+H_{\delta}^{(u)}(y),

where

H_{\delta}^{(u)}(y):=\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}b_{\delta}(t)\cos(y(1+t))\,dt,\qquad b_{\delta}(t):=\chi_{*}(t)\log\frac{1}{t}.

Since
b_{\delta}\in W^{1,1}(\delta,2\delta)
with
b_{\delta}(2\delta)=0
and
b_{\delta}(\delta)=\log(1/\delta)
, one integration by parts gives

\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y}H_{\delta}^{(u)}(y)=\frac{\pi^{2}\log\delta}{4}\frac{\sin((1+\delta)y)}{y^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y^{2}}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}b_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)\sin((1+t)y)\,dt.

(189)

Using the exact formulas

\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{t}\cos(yt)\,dt=\frac{\operatorname{Si}(\delta y)-(\log\delta)\sin(\delta y)}{y},

\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{t}\sin(yt)\,dt=\frac{\log y+\gamma-\operatorname{Ci}(\delta y)+(\log\delta)\cos(\delta y)}{y}

together with

\cos(y(1+t))=\cos y\cos(yt)-\sin y\sin(yt),

we obtain

\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y}\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{t}\,\cos(y(1+t))\,dt=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{\log y}{y^{2}}\sin y+V_{\delta}(y),

(190)

where

(191)

with

(192)

Since
\operatorname{Si}(x)-\pi/2=O(x^{-1})
and
\operatorname{Ci}(x)=O(x^{-1})
as

Q_{u}(y)=O(y^{-3})\qquad(y\to\infty).

(193)

Adding (
189
) and (
191

\sin((1+\delta)y)/y^{2}
terms cancel, giving

T_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{\log y}{y^{2}}\sin y+G_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y),

where

\displaystyle G_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{a(0)\sin y}{y^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{\prime}(t)\sin((1+t)y)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y^{2}}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}b_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)\sin((1+t)y)\,dt+\frac{\pi^{3}}{8}\frac{\cos y}{y^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}\gamma}{4}\frac{\sin y}{y^{2}}+Q_{u}(y),

(194)

so that

.

Step 3: Holomorphy of the Mellin transform of
G_{u}
.

For fixed
\alpha>0
, the functions

are holomorphic for

. Indeed, one integration by parts gives

E_{\alpha}^{\sin}(s)=\frac{\cos\alpha}{\alpha}+\frac{s-3}{\alpha}\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-4}\cos(\alpha y)\,dy,

E_{\alpha}^{\cos}(s)=-\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}-\frac{s-3}{\alpha}\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-4}\sin(\alpha y)\,dy,

where the integrals on the right-hand side converge absolutely for
\Re s<3
.

The Mellin transforms of the

and
\cos y/y^{2}
terms in the first term of (
188

\Re s<3
via

and
E_{\alpha}^{\cos}
.

For the second term of (
188

Fubini’s theorem on the finite interval gives

I_{a,R}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{\prime}(t)\widetilde{L}_{s,R}(t)\,dt,

where

\widetilde{L}_{s,R}(t):=(1+t)^{2-s}\int_{1+t}^{(1+t)R}u^{s-3}\sin u\,du.

Fix a compact set
K\subset\{\Re s<3\}
and set
\sigma_{K}:=\sup_{s\in K}\Re s<3
. Since
\operatorname{supp}a\subset[0,2\delta]
, one integration by parts gives

|\widetilde{L}_{s,R}(t)|\leq C_{K}\qquad(0\leq t\leq 2\delta,\ R\geq 1,\ s\in K).

Dominated convergence as

then gives

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{\prime}(t)\sin((1+t)y)\,dt\right)dy=\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{\prime}(t)\widetilde{L}_{s}(t)\,dt,

where

\widetilde{L}_{s}(t):=(1+t)^{2-s}\int_{1+t}^{\infty}u^{s-3}\sin u\,du.

The same majorant shows that the right-hand side is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.

The

-term with support in
[\delta,2\delta]
is treated in exactly the same way:

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}b_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)\sin((1+t)y)\,dt\right)dy=\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}b_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)\widetilde{L}_{s}(t)\,dt,

which is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
.

By (

\mathcal{M}_{>}[Q_{u}](s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}Q_{u}(y)\,dy

converges absolutely for
\Re s<3
and is holomorphic there by dominated convergence on compact subsets.

Setting

G_{u}(y):=T_{u}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y)+G_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y),

we have
G_{u}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([1,\infty))
and

\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{u}](s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}G_{u}(y)\,dy

is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
. We therefore obtain

T_{u}(y)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{\log y}{y^{2}}\sin y+G_{u}(y),\qquad G_{u}(y)=O(y^{-2})\qquad(y\to\infty).

Step 4: Remainder

.

Separating the definition (
149
) from Lemma
7
into
v
-channel and

-channel contributions gives

\displaystyle R_{3}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{y^{3}}\int_{0}^{1}C(v)\cos(vy)\,dv-\frac{8}{y^{3}}\int_{1}^{\infty}D(u)\cos(uy)\,du

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{8}{y^{2}}\int_{1}^{\infty}uD(u)\sin(uy)\,du+\frac{8}{y^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}vC(v)\sin(vy)\,dv,

(195)

where

By (

) from Lemma

and (

) from Proposition

,

\int_{0}^{1}C(v)\cos(vy)\,dv=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),\qquad\int_{0}^{1}vC(v)\sin(vy)\,dv=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),

\int_{1}^{\infty}D(u)\cos(uy)\,du=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right),\qquad\int_{1}^{\infty}uD(u)\sin(uy)\,du=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y}\right).

Therefore

R_{3}(y)=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y^{3}}\right)\qquad(y\to\infty).

Furthermore, by Lemma

Using (

Fix a compact set

and set
\sigma_{K}:=\sup_{s\in K}\Re s<3
. For sufficiently large

Therefore

\mathcal{M}_{>}[R_{3}](s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}R_{3}(y)\,dy

converges absolutely for

Step 5: Conclusion.

Combining the above, we obtain

\Phi(y)=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{\log y}{y^{2}}\sin y+G(y),

Since

This gives (

) and (

Definition 17

\int_{0}^{1}y^{-c}(1+|\log y|)\,dy<\infty,\qquad\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{-\sigma_{-}-3}(1+\log y)\,dy<\infty.

Therefore

\sup_{s\in\Sigma_{\sigma_{-},c}}\int_{0}^{\infty}|y^{-s-1}\Phi(y)|\,dy<\infty.

This shows that (
207
) converges absolutely and that

|F(-s)|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}|y^{-s-1}\Phi(y)|\,dy\leq M_{\sigma_{-},c},

giving (
208
).

Since the integrand
y^{-s-1}\Phi(y)
is entire in
s
and the above integrable majorant allows dominated convergence on compact subsets, Morera’s theorem (or differentiation under the integral sign) shows that
F(-s)
is holomorphic in the strip
\sigma_{-}<\Re s<c
.

We next prove (
209
). Since
\sigma\in\{\sigma_{-},c\}
avoids
-1
,
\mathcal{K}(\sigma+it)
is regular on the line
\Re s=\sigma
. By Stirling’s formula,

|\Gamma(\sigma+1+it)|\leq C_{\sigma}(1+|t|)^{\sigma+1/2}e^{-\pi|t|/2}.

For
\sigma\in\{\sigma_{-},c\}\subset(-2,0)\setminus\{-1\}
,

\left|\sin\frac{\pi(\sigma+it)}{2}\right|^{2}=\sin^{2}\frac{\pi\sigma}{2}+\sinh^{2}\frac{\pi t}{2}.

On the compact interval
|t|\leq 1
,
\sin(\pi(\sigma+it)/2)
is a nonvanishing continuous function, so its absolute value is bounded below by a positive constant. For
|t|\geq 1
,

\left|\sin\frac{\pi(\sigma+it)}{2}\right|\geq\sinh\frac{\pi|t|}{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{\pi|t|/2},

and therefore

\left|\sin\frac{\pi(\sigma+it)}{2}\right|^{-1}\leq C_{\sigma_{-},c}e^{-\pi|t|/2}\qquad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

It follows that

|\mathcal{K}(\sigma+it)|\leq C_{\sigma_{-},c}(1+|t|)^{N}e^{-\pi|t|}.

Combining with (
208
) gives

\left|\frac{1}{D_{0}}\mathcal{K}(\sigma+it)F(-\sigma-it)\right|\leq C^{\prime}_{\sigma_{-},c}(1+|t|)^{N}e^{-\pi|t|},

and the right-hand side is integrable in
t\in\mathbb{R}
, which gives (
209
).

Finally, we prove (
210
). For
\sigma\in[\sigma_{-},c]
and
T>0
, the same Stirling estimate gives

|\mathcal{K}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}T)|\leq C_{\sigma_{-},c}(1+T)^{N}e^{-\pi T}

uniformly. Using (
208
) and

|\mu^{-\sigma-\mathrm{i}T}|=\mu^{-\sigma}\leq\max\{\mu^{-\sigma_{-}},\mu^{-c}\},

we obtain

|I(\sigma+\mathrm{i}T;\mu)|\leq C_{\mu,\sigma_{-},c}(1+T)^{N}e^{-\pi T}

uniformly for
\sigma\in[\sigma_{-},c]
. Therefore

\int_{\sigma_{-}}^{c}|I(\sigma+\mathrm{i}T;\mu)|\,d\sigma\leq(c-\sigma_{-})\,C_{\mu,\sigma_{-},c}(1+T)^{N}e^{-\pi T}\to 0\qquad(T\to\infty).

The lower side is treated in the same way, giving (
210
).

The assumptions (
129
) and (
130
) of Theorem
4
are therefore satisfied for the actual
\Phi
on the strip
\Sigma_{\sigma_{-},c}
.
∎

Corollary 22

(Rigorous leading small-
\mu
asymptotics)
.

Let
-2<\sigma_{-}<-1<c<0
.
Then as
\mu\to 0^{+}
,

S(\mu)=\frac{\pi}{2D_{0}}F(1)\,\mu+R_{\sigma_{-}}(\mu),

(213)

where

|R_{\sigma_{-}}(\mu)|\leq\frac{A(\sigma_{-})}{2\pi}\,\mu^{-\sigma_{-}},

(214)

with

A(\sigma_{-})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{D_{0}}\mathcal{K}(\sigma_{-}+it)F(-\sigma_{-}-it)\right|dt<\infty.

In particular, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, choosing
\sigma_{-}=-2+\varepsilon
gives

S(\mu)=\frac{\pi}{2D_{0}}F(1)\,\mu+O(\mu^{2-\varepsilon})\qquad(\mu\to 0^{+}).

(215)

Proof.

By Proposition
21
, Theorem
4
applies to the contour shift from the line
\Re s=c
to the line
\Re s=\sigma_{-}
for the actual
\Phi
. The only pole in the strip
\sigma_{-}<\Re s<c
is
s=-1
, so that

S(\mu)=\operatorname{Res}_{s=-1}I(s;\mu)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma_{-})}I(s;\mu)\,ds.

The residue of
\mathcal{K}(s)
at
s=-1
is

\operatorname{Res}_{s=-1}\mathcal{K}(s)=\frac{\pi}{2}.

Indeed,

\Gamma(s+1)=\frac{1}{s+1}+O(1),\qquad\sin\frac{\pi s}{2}=-1+O((s+1)^{2})\qquad(s\to-1),

so that

\mathcal{K}(s)=-\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\Gamma(s+1)}{\sin(\pi s/2)}=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{1}{s+1}+O(1).

Since
F(-s)
is regular at
s=-1
with
F(-(-1))=F(1)
,

\operatorname{Res}_{s=-1}I(s;\mu)=\frac{\pi}{2D_{0}}F(1)\,\mu,

which gives (
213
).

For the remainder, applying (
133
) from Theorem
4
with
\sigma=\sigma_{-}
gives

\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma_{-})}I(s;\mu)\,ds\right|\leq\frac{A(\sigma_{-})}{2\pi}\mu^{-\sigma_{-}},

which is (
214
). Choosing
\sigma_{-}=-2+\varepsilon
gives (
215
).
∎

Second-strip verification via the regular part

Lemma 24

(Oscillatory Mellin kernels on a closed strip)
.

Let
0<\sigma_{0}<\sigma_{1}<3
and set

S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}:=\{\,s\in\mathbb{C}:\ \sigma_{0}\leq\Re s\leq\sigma_{1}\,\}.

Define

\beta_{\sigma_{1}}:=\min\{1,\,2-\sigma_{1}\}\in(-1,1].

Then the following hold.

(i)

For any
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}>0
, the functions

E_{\alpha}^{\sin}(s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\sin(\alpha y)\,dy,\qquad E_{\alpha}^{\cos}(s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\cos(\alpha y)\,dy

(217)

are holomorphic in the open strip
\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}
and satisfy

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}\\
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}\end{subarray}}\left(|E_{\alpha}^{\sin}(s)|+|E_{\alpha}^{\cos}(s)|\right)\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\alpha_{0}}(1+|s|).

(218)

(ii)

For
0<v\leq 1
, define

K_{s}(v):=v^{2-s}\int_{v}^{\infty}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt,\qquad M_{s}(v):=v^{3-s}\int_{v}^{\infty}t^{s-4}\cos t\,dt.

(219)

Then
K_{s}(v)
and
M_{s}(v)
are holomorphic in the open strip
\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}
and satisfy

|K_{s}(v)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\,v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}\bigl(1+|\log v|\bigr),

(220)

|M_{s}(v)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|).

(221)

(iii)

For
u\geq 1
, define

L_{s}(u):=u^{2-s}\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt,\qquad N_{s}(u):=u^{3-s}\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{s-4}\cos t\,dt.

(222)

Then
L_{s}(u)
and
N_{s}(u)
are holomorphic in the open strip
\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}
and satisfy

|L_{s}(u)|+|N_{s}(u)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\qquad(u\geq 1).

(223)

Proof.

(i) Estimate of
E_{\alpha}^{\sin}
and
E_{\alpha}^{\cos}
.

Fix
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}>0
. Since
\sigma_{1}<3
, we have
y^{s-3}\to 0
as
y\to\infty
, and one integration by parts gives

E_{\alpha}^{\sin}(s)=\frac{\cos\alpha}{\alpha}+\frac{s-3}{\alpha}\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-4}\cos(\alpha y)\,dy.

(224)

Similarly,

E_{\alpha}^{\cos}(s)=-\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}-\frac{s-3}{\alpha}\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-4}\sin(\alpha y)\,dy.

(225)

For
s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}
,

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\Re s-4}\,dy\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\sigma_{1}-4}\,dy=\frac{1}{3-\sigma_{1}}<\infty.

From (
224
) and (
225
),

|E_{\alpha}^{\sin}(s)|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}+\frac{|s-3|}{\alpha_{0}}\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\sigma_{1}-4}\,dy,

|E_{\alpha}^{\cos}(s)|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}+\frac{|s-3|}{\alpha_{0}}\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\sigma_{1}-4}\,dy,

which gives (
218
).

The right-hand sides of (
224
) and (
225
) are given by absolutely convergent integrals on
\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}
, and dominated convergence applies on compact subsets. Therefore
E_{\alpha}^{\sin}
and
E_{\alpha}^{\cos}
are holomorphic in the open strip
\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}
.

(ii) Estimate of
K_{s}(v)
and
M_{s}(v)
.

We split

K_{s}(v)=v^{2-s}\int_{v}^{1}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt+v^{2-s}\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt=:K_{s}^{(0,1)}(v)+K_{s}^{(1,\infty)}(v).

For the tail part, using (
218
) with
\alpha=1
:

|K_{s}^{(1,\infty)}(v)|=v^{2-\Re s}\,|E_{1}^{\sin}(s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\,v^{2-\sigma_{1}}.

Since
0<v\leq 1
and
\beta_{\sigma_{1}}\leq 2-\sigma_{1}
, we have
v^{2-\sigma_{1}}\leq v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}
, giving

|K_{s}^{(1,\infty)}(v)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\,v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}.

(226)

For the local part, using
|\sin t|\leq t
on
[0,1]
:

|K_{s}^{(0,1)}(v)|\leq v^{2-\Re s}\int_{v}^{1}t^{\Re s-3}|\sin t|\,dt\leq v^{2-\Re s}\int_{v}^{1}t^{\Re s-2}\,dt.

Write
\sigma:=\Re s
.

Case 1:
\sigma\leq 1
.

Then
1-\sigma\geq 0
and

v^{2-\sigma}\int_{v}^{1}t^{\sigma-2}\,dt=v\int_{v}^{1}\left(\frac{v}{t}\right)^{1-\sigma}\frac{dt}{t}\leq v\int_{v}^{1}\frac{dt}{t}=v\log\frac{1}{v}.

Since
\beta_{\sigma_{1}}\leq 1
, we have
v\leq v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}
on
(0,1]
, and therefore

|K_{s}^{(0,1)}(v)|\leq v\log\frac{1}{v}\leq v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}\bigl(1+|\log v|\bigr).

(227)

Case 2:
\sigma>1
.

Then

v^{2-\sigma}\int_{v}^{1}t^{\sigma-2}\,dt=v^{2-\sigma}\frac{1-v^{\sigma-1}}{\sigma-1}.

Using

\frac{1-v^{\sigma-1}}{\sigma-1}=\int_{0}^{1}v^{\theta(\sigma-1)}\,|\log v|\,d\theta\leq|\log v|,

we obtain

|K_{s}^{(0,1)}(v)|\leq v^{2-\sigma}|\log v|\leq v^{2-\sigma_{1}}|\log v|\leq v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}\bigl(1+|\log v|\bigr),

where the last inequality uses
\beta_{\sigma_{1}}\leq 2-\sigma_{1}
.

Combining (
226
) with the two cases gives (
220
).

For
M_{s}(v)
:

|M_{s}(v)|\leq v^{3-\Re s}\int_{v}^{\infty}t^{\Re s-4}\,dt=\frac{1}{3-\Re s}\leq\frac{1}{3-\sigma_{1}},

which gives (
221
).

Since
K_{s}(v)
can be written as

K_{s}(v)=v^{2-s}\int_{v}^{1}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt+v^{2-s}E_{1}^{\sin}(s),

where the first term is an integral of an entire integrand over a finite interval and the second is holomorphic by (i),
K_{s}(v)
is holomorphic in the open strip
\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}
. For
M_{s}(v)
, the integral converges absolutely, and holomorphicity follows from dominated convergence on compact subsets.

(iii) Estimate of
L_{s}(u)
and
N_{s}(u)
.

For
N_{s}(u)
:

|N_{s}(u)|\leq u^{3-\Re s}\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{\Re s-4}\,dt=\frac{1}{3-\Re s}\leq\frac{1}{3-\sigma_{1}},

which gives the
N_{s}
part of (
223
).

For
L_{s}(u)
, one integration by parts gives

\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt=u^{s-3}\cos u+(s-3)\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{s-4}\cos t\,dt,

so that

L_{s}(u)=u^{-1}\cos u+(s-3)\,u^{2-s}\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{s-4}\cos t\,dt.

Since

\left|u^{2-s}\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{s-4}\cos t\,dt\right|\leq u^{2-\Re s}\int_{u}^{\infty}t^{\Re s-4}\,dt=\frac{u^{-1}}{3-\Re s}\leq\frac{1}{3-\sigma_{1}},

we obtain

|L_{s}(u)|\leq u^{-1}+\frac{|s-3|}{3-\sigma_{1}}\,u^{-1}\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|),

using
u\geq 1
. This gives (
223
).

Holomorphicity of
N_{s}(u)
follows from the absolutely convergent integral, and that of
L_{s}(u)
from the integration-by-parts representation above, both via dominated convergence on compact subsets.
∎

Lemma 25

(Growth of the small-
y
contribution and the oscillatory principal term)
.

Let
0<\sigma_{0}<\sigma_{1}<3
and set

S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}:=\{\,s\in\mathbb{C}:\ \sigma_{0}\leq\Re s\leq\sigma_{1}\,\}.

Define

F_{<}(s):=\int_{0}^{1}y^{s-1}\Phi(y)\,dy,\qquad J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s):=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\log y\,\sin y\,dy.

(228)

Then
F_{<}(s)
and
J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)
are holomorphic in the open strip
\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}
and satisfy

|F_{<}(s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}},\qquad|J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\qquad(s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}).

(229)

Proof.

We first treat
F_{<}(s)
.
By Proposition
6
, there exist
y_{0}\in(0,1)
and a constant
C_{0}>0
such that

|\Phi(y)|\leq C_{0}\,y(1+|\log y|)\qquad(0<y\leq y_{0}).

(230)

We next show that
\Phi
is bounded on
[y_{0},1]
. From the defining expression

\Phi(y)=y\int_{1}^{\infty}du\;L(u)\int_{0}^{1}dv\;\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)\,j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u+v}{2}y\right)j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u-v}{2}y\right),

we set

A:=\frac{u+v}{2}y,\qquad B:=\frac{u-v}{2}y.

For
y\in[y_{0},1]
, we have
A\geq y_{0}u/2
, so that by (
141
),

|j_{1}(A)|\leq C(1+A)^{-1}\leq C_{y_{0}}(1+u)^{-1}.

Using the general bound

|j_{1}(z)|\leq C\min\{z,(1+z)^{-1}\}\leq C(1+z)^{-1/2}\qquad(z>0),

we have

|j_{1}(B)|\leq C(1+B)^{-1/2}\leq C_{y_{0}}(1+u-v)^{-1/2}.

Therefore, for
y\in[y_{0},1]
,

|\Phi(y)|\leq C_{y_{0}}\int_{1}^{\infty}du\,L(u)\int_{0}^{1}dv\,(1+\log u+|\log v|)\,(1+u)^{-1}(1+u-v)^{-1/2}.

Since
\int_{0}^{1}(1+|\log v|)\,dv<\infty
and, as
u\to\infty
,
L(u)\sim 2/u
gives

L(u)(1+\log u)(1+u)^{-1}(1+u-v)^{-1/2}=O\!\left(\frac{1+\log u}{u^{5/2}}\right),

while near
u\downarrow 1
,

L(u)=O\!\left(\log\frac{1}{u-1}\right),\qquad(1+u)^{-1}(1+u-v)^{-1/2}=O(1),

which is integrable near
u=1
, the right-hand side is finite. We conclude

\sup_{y\in[y_{0},1]}|\Phi(y)|\leq C_{y_{0}}^{\prime}<\infty.

(231)

For
s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}
,

|F_{<}(s)|\leq\int_{0}^{y_{0}}y^{\sigma_{0}-1}|\Phi(y)|\,dy+\int_{y_{0}}^{1}y^{\sigma_{0}-1}|\Phi(y)|\,dy.

Using (
230
) and (
231
),

|F_{<}(s)|\leq C_{0}\int_{0}^{y_{0}}y^{\sigma_{0}}(1+|\log y|)\,dy+C_{y_{0}}^{\prime}\int_{y_{0}}^{1}y^{\sigma_{0}-1}\,dy\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}},

which gives the
F_{<}
part of (
229
).

For the holomorphicity of
F_{<}(s)
, let
K\subset\{\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}\}
be a compact set and choose
\sigma_{K}>\sigma_{0}
such that
\Re s\geq\sigma_{K}
for all
s\in K
. Then

|y^{s-1}\Phi(y)|\leq y^{\sigma_{K}-1}|\Phi(y)|,

and as above
\int_{0}^{1}y^{\sigma_{K}-1}|\Phi(y)|\,dy<\infty
. Dominated convergence on compact subsets therefore shows that
F_{<}(s)
is holomorphic in the open strip
\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}
.

We next treat
J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)
.
Since
\sigma_{1}<3
, we have
y^{s-3}\log y\to 0
as
y\to\infty
, and since
\log 1=0
, one integration by parts gives

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\log y\,\sin y\,dy=\int_{1}^{\infty}\Bigl[(s-3)y^{s-4}\log y+y^{s-4}\Bigr]\cos y\,dy,

so that

J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\int_{1}^{\infty}\Bigl[(s-3)y^{s-4}\log y+y^{s-4}\Bigr]\cos y\,dy.

(232)

For
s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}
,

\left|\Bigl[(s-3)y^{s-4}\log y+y^{s-4}\Bigr]\cos y\right|\leq\Bigl(|s-3|\log y+1\Bigr)y^{\sigma_{1}-4}.

Since
\sigma_{1}<3
,

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\sigma_{1}-4}(1+\log y)\,dy<\infty,

and from (
232
),

|J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)|\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\left(|s-3|\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\sigma_{1}-4}\log y\,dy+\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\sigma_{1}-4}\,dy\right)\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|),

which gives the
J_{\mathrm{osc}}
part of (
229
).

For holomorphicity, let
K\subset\{\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}\}
be compact and choose
\sigma_{K}<\sigma_{1}
such that
\Re s\leq\sigma_{K}
for all
s\in K
. The integrand of (
232
) is uniformly dominated by

\Bigl(|s-3|\log y+1\Bigr)y^{\sigma_{K}-4},

which is integrable on
[1,\infty)
. Dominated convergence on compact subsets therefore shows that
J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)
is holomorphic in the open strip
\sigma_{0}<\Re s<\sigma_{1}
.
∎

Lemma 26

(Exact decomposition and continuation of
H_{2}(s)
)
.

For
0<\Re s<2
,

H_{2}(s)=F_{<}(s)+J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{u}](s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[R_{3}](s),

(233)

where

F_{<}(s):=\int_{0}^{1}y^{s-1}\Phi(y)\,dy,\qquad J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s):=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\log y\,\sin y\,dy,

\mathcal{M}_{>}[X](s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}X(y)\,dy,

and
G_{v},G_{u},R_{3}
are the remainder functions introduced in the proofs of Proposition
9
and Proposition
15
.

Furthermore, the right-hand side is holomorphic for
0<\Re s<3
, and (
233
) gives a holomorphic continuation of
H_{2}(s)
to
0<\Re s<3
.

Proof.

We first work in the region
0<\Re s<2
. By Proposition
6
and Proposition
15
,

|\Phi(y)|\leq C\,y(1+|\log y|)\qquad(0<y\leq 1),

|\Phi(y)|\leq C\,y^{-2}(1+\log y)\qquad(y\geq 1),

so that

F(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{s-1}\Phi(y)\,dy

converges absolutely and can be split as

F(s)=F_{<}(s)+F_{>}(s),\qquad F_{>}(s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}\Phi(y)\,dy.

(234)

We substitute the exact decomposition from the proof of Proposition
15
,

\Phi(y)=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{\log y}{y^{2}}\sin y+G_{v}(y)+G_{u}(y)+R_{3}(y)\qquad(y\geq 1),

into
F_{>}(s)
in (
234
). For
0<\Re s<2
,

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\,dy<\infty,

and
\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\log y\,\sin y\,dy
converges absolutely. Furthermore, by Proposition
9
and the proof of Proposition
15
,

G_{v}(y)=O(y^{-2}),\qquad G_{u}(y)=O(y^{-2}),\qquad R_{3}(y)=O\!\left(\frac{(\log y)^{2}}{y^{3}}\right)\qquad(y\to\infty),

so that

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}|G_{v}(y)|\,dy,\qquad\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}|G_{u}(y)|\,dy,\qquad\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-1}|R_{3}(y)|\,dy

all converge absolutely. We can therefore integrate
F_{>}(s)
term by term:

F_{>}(s)=2\pi\log 2\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\,dy+J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{u}](s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[R_{3}](s).

Since

\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\,dy=\frac{1}{2-s}\qquad(0<\Re s<2),

we obtain

F(s)=F_{<}(s)+\frac{2\pi\log 2}{2-s}+J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{u}](s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[R_{3}](s).

Using
H_{2}(s):=F(s)-2\pi\log 2/(2-s)
from Definition
23
, we obtain (
233
) for
0<\Re s<2
.

We next show that the right-hand side is holomorphic for
0<\Re s<3
. By Lemma
25
,
F_{<}(s)
and
J_{\mathrm{osc}}(s)
are holomorphic for
0<\Re s<3
. By Proposition
9
,
\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s)
is holomorphic for
\Re s<3
. As shown in the proof of Proposition
15
,
\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{u}](s)
and
\mathcal{M}_{>}[R_{3}](s)
are also holomorphic for
\Re s<3
. The entire right-hand side of (
233
) is therefore holomorphic for
0<\Re s<3
.

Since (
233
) is an identity for
0<\Re s<2
and its right-hand side is holomorphic for
0<\Re s<3
, it gives a holomorphic continuation of
H_{2}(s)
to
0<\Re s<3
.
∎

Lemma 27

(Growth bound for
{\mathcal{M}}_{>}[G_{v}](s)
)
.

Let
0<\sigma_{0}<\sigma_{1}<3
and set

S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}:=\{\,s\in\mathbb{C}:\ \sigma_{0}\leq\Re s\leq\sigma_{1}\,\}.

Then

|\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\qquad(s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}).

(235)

Proof.

We refine the proof of Proposition
9
. Setting

a_{\delta}(v):=\chi_{0}(v)\log\frac{1}{v}\qquad(\delta\leq v\leq 2\delta),

we have
a_{\delta}\in W^{1,1}(\delta,2\delta)
with

a_{\delta}(2\delta)=0,\qquad a_{\delta}(\delta)=\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

In that proof, we decomposed
T_{v}(y)=T_{v}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)+T_{v}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y)
and obtained

T_{v}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y)=\frac{2g(1)\sin y}{y^{2}}-\frac{2}{y^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv.

We also wrote

T_{v}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)=\frac{4\log 2}{y}\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{v}\cos(vy)\,dv+R_{\delta}(y),

where one integration by parts gives

R_{\delta}(y)=\frac{4\log 2\,\log\delta}{y^{2}}\sin(\delta y)-\frac{4\log 2}{y^{2}}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv.

(236)

Using the exact formula

\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{v}\cos(vy)\,dv=\frac{\operatorname{Si}(\delta y)-(\log\delta)\sin(\delta y)}{y},

we obtain

\frac{4\log 2}{y}\int_{0}^{\delta}\log\frac{1}{v}\cos(vy)\,dv=\frac{2\pi\log 2}{y^{2}}-\frac{4\log 2\,\log\delta}{y^{2}}\sin(\delta y)+Q_{v}(y),

where

Q_{v}(y):=\frac{4\log 2}{y^{2}}\left(\operatorname{Si}(\delta y)-\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

(237)

Combining with (
236
), the
\sin(\delta y)/y^{2}
terms cancel, giving
T_{v}(y)=2\pi\log 2/y^{2}+G_{v}(y)
with

G_{v}(y)=\frac{2g(1)\sin y}{y^{2}}-\frac{2}{y^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv-\frac{4\log 2}{y^{2}}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv+Q_{v}(y).

(238)

Step 1: Estimate of
Q_{v}
.

Since
\operatorname{Si}(x)-\pi/2=-\int_{x}^{\infty}\sin t\,t^{-1}\,dt
and one integration by parts gives

\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt=\frac{\cos x}{x}-\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\cos t}{t^{2}}\,dt,

we have
\operatorname{Si}(x)-\pi/2=O(x^{-1})
as
x\to\infty
, and therefore
Q_{v}(y)=O(y^{-3})
as
y\to\infty
. For
s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}
,

|\mathcal{M}_{>}[Q_{v}](s)|\leq C\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{\sigma_{1}-4}\,dy\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}.

Step 2: First oscillatory term.

The Mellin transform of the first term of (
238
) is

2g(1)\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\sin y\,dy=2g(1)\,E_{1}^{\sin}(s).

By Lemma
24
(i),

|E_{1}^{\sin}(s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|),

so this term satisfies (
235
).

Step 3: Estimate of the
g^{\prime}
-term.

We set

I_{g}(s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv\right)dy.

For
R>1
, define

I_{g,R}(s):=\int_{1}^{R}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv\right)dy.

Fubini’s theorem on the finite interval
[1,R]
gives

I_{g,R}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)\,K_{s,R}(v)\,dv,

where

K_{s,R}(v):=v^{2-s}\int_{v}^{vR}t^{s-3}\sin t\,dt.

(239)

By the same argument as in Lemma
24
(ii), for
R\geq 1
,
s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}
, and
0<v\leq 1
,

|K_{s,R}(v)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\,v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}\bigl(1+|\log v|\bigr).

(240)

Indeed, splitting the integration range of (
239
) into
[v,\min\{1,vR\}]
and
[1,vR]
(if
vR>1
), the former is controlled by the same estimate as the local part of
K_{s}(v)
in Lemma
24
, and the latter is uniformly bounded by
C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)
via one integration by parts.

As shown in the proof of Lemma
8
, there exists
v_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

|g^{\prime}(v)|\leq C\,v\log\frac{e}{v}\qquad(0<v\leq v_{0}).

Since
\beta_{\sigma_{1}}>-1
,

|g^{\prime}(v)|\,|K_{s,R}(v)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\,v^{1+\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}\left(\log\frac{e}{v}\right)\bigl(1+|\log v|\bigr),

which is integrable near
v=0
. Since
g^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,1)
and
v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}(1+|\log v|)
is bounded on
[v_{0},1]
,

\int_{0}^{1}|g^{\prime}(v)|\,v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}(1+|\log v|)\,dv<\infty.

By dominated convergence with (
240
) as the majorant, as
R\to\infty
,

I_{g,R}(s)\to\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)K_{s}(v)\,dv,

where
K_{s}(v)
is the kernel from Lemma
24
(ii). Therefore

I_{g}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(v)K_{s}(v)\,dv,

and

|I_{g}(s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\int_{0}^{1}|g^{\prime}(v)|\,v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}(1+|\log v|)\,dv\leq C^{\prime}_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|).

Step 4: Estimate of the
a_{\delta}^{\prime}
-term.

We similarly set

I_{\delta}(s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\sin(vy)\,dv\right)dy.

For
R>1
, Fubini’s theorem on the finite interval gives

I_{\delta,R}(s)=\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)K_{s,R}(v)\,dv.

Since
v\in[\delta,2\delta]
is bounded away from zero,
v^{\beta_{\sigma_{1}}}(1+|\log v|)
is bounded, and (
240
) gives

|K_{s,R}(v)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\qquad(\delta\leq v\leq 2\delta).

Since
a_{\delta}^{\prime}\in L^{1}(\delta,2\delta)
, dominated convergence as
R\to\infty
gives

I_{\delta}(s)=\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)K_{s}(v)\,dv,

and

|I_{\delta}(s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}|a_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)|\,dv\leq C^{\prime}_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|).

Step 5: Conclusion.

Combining the estimates from the above steps,

\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s)=2g(1)E_{1}^{\sin}(s)-2I_{g}(s)-4\log 2\,I_{\delta}(s)+\mathcal{M}_{>}[Q_{v}](s),

and therefore

|\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{v}](s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\qquad(s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}),

which gives (
235
).

Lemma 28

(Growth bound for
\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{u}](s)
)
.

Then

|\mathcal{M}_{>}[G_{u}](s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\qquad(s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}).

Proof.

We refine the proof of Proposition
15
. We set

In the proof of Proposition
15

T_{u}(y)=T_{u}^{\mathrm{reg}}(y)+T_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)

T_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{\log y}{y^{2}}\sin y+G_{u}^{\mathrm{sing}}(y)

(244)

Since

and
\operatorname{Ci}(\delta y)=O(y^{-1})

(245)

Adding (
242
) and (
243

\displaystyle G_{u}(y)

\displaystyle=c_{u,1}\frac{\sin y}{y^{2}}+c_{u,2}\frac{\cos y}{y^{2}}-\frac{2}{y^{2}}\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)\sin(uy)\,du

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{\prime}(t)\sin((1+t)y)\,dt-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4y^{2}}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}b_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)\sin((1+t)y)\,dt+Q_{u}(y),

(246)

where

.

245
), there exists

and

24
(i),

241
).

Step 3: The
h^{\prime}
-term.

We set

I_{h}(s):=\int_{1}^{\infty}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)\sin(uy)\,du\right)dy.

R>1
, define

I_{h,R}(s):=\int_{1}^{R}y^{s-3}\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)\sin(uy)\,du\right)dy.

On
[1,R]\times[1,\infty)
, the integrand is dominated by

, and since

,
u\geq 1
, and

(247)

Since
h^{\prime}\in L^{1}(1,\infty)
, dominated convergence as
R\to\infty
gives

I_{h}(s)=\int_{1}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(u)L_{s}(u)\,du,

where
L_{s}(u)

(iii). Therefore

a^{\prime}
-term.

, since
\operatorname{supp}a\subset[0,2\delta]

Since
(1+t)\in[1,1+2\delta]
, the same integration-by-parts argument as for (

|\widetilde{L}_{s,R}(t)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\qquad(R\geq 1,\ 0\leq t\leq 2\delta,\ s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}).

(248)

,

I_{a}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}a^{\prime}(t)\widetilde{L}_{s}(t)\,dt,\qquad\widetilde{L}_{s}(t):=L_{s}(1+t),

b_{\delta}^{\prime}

248

|I_{b,R}(s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}|b_{\delta}^{\prime}(t)|\,dt.

and

Step 6: Conclusion.

Taking the Mellin transform of (
246

Combining the estimates from the above steps gives

).

Lemma 29

Proof.

\displaystyle R_{3}(y)

Proposition 30

(Rigorous vertical growth of
H_{2}(s)
)
.

For any closed strip

S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}:=\{\,s\in\mathbb{C}:\ \sigma_{0}\leq\Re s\leq\sigma_{1}\,\}\subset\{0<\Re s<3\},

there exists a constant
C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}>0
such that

|H_{2}(s)|\leq C_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}(1+|s|)^{2}\qquad(s\in S_{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}}).

(251)

In particular,
H_{2}(s)
has polynomial vertical growth in
\{0<\Re s<3\}
.

Proof.

X
Summary and outlook

In this paper we developed an analytic framework for the dynamically
screened second-order exchange diagram in the uniform electron gas,
starting from the finite-temperature Matsubara grand potential and
working systematically down to a zero-temperature imaginary-frequency
representation suitable for both exact reduction and asymptotic analysis.
The central message is that the diagram topology itself — rather than
an empirically chosen fitting form — constrains the admissible analytic
structure of the screened-exchange correction, and that this constraint
can be made explicit at the theorem level.

The first main result is the exact dynamic reduction for generic
frequency-dependent one-pole screening. Contrary to what one might
expect from the static case, the natural object that appears after
reduction is not a one-variable kernel but a two-variable cosine-difference
transform. We showed that collapsing this to a one-variable kernel requires
the characteristic frequency scale of the screened interaction to be
independent of momentum, and we proved that this condition is both
necessary and sufficient within the one-pole family. The model satisfying
this condition — the reduction-compatible single-pole (RC-SP) model —
is therefore the minimal reference model in which the dynamically screened
exchange diagram admits exact analytic treatment. For general one-pole
screening outside this class, the RC-SP kernels provide analytically
controllable basis elements for a finite-rank separable approximation,
so the framework is not confined to a single special case.

The second main result concerns the explicit analytic and numerical
content of the RC-SP model. For the three-dimensional Coulomb case,
the exact reduction produces a reduced geometric block expressible as
a double integral in prolate spheroidal coordinates involving only
spherical Bessel functions, multiplied by a one-dimensional kernel
encoding all the dynamic information. We built the numerical
infrastructure to evaluate this representation directly at finite
screening parameter
\mu
, without relying on term-by-term integration
of asymptotic series. The numerical results reveal a clear overshoot
of the screening factor above its static limit at intermediate values
of
\mu
: the dynamic screening in the RC-SP model introduces nontrivial
frequency dependence into the exchange topology, producing contributions
that exceed the static limit before relaxing back as
\mu
grows large.
The static limit is normalized to the Onsager–Mittag–Stephen
value
18
for the bare second-order exchange, giving
the results an absolute energy scale.

The third main result is the rigorous asymptotic analysis of the
screening factor via the Mellin–Barnes representation. The pole structure
of the associated Mellin transform, which is determined directly by the
endpoint behavior of the reduced geometric block, fixes the allowed
asymptotic forms at both weak and strong screening. At weak screening
the leading behavior is linear in
\mu
, while at strong screening the
approach to the static limit is governed by a logarithmically modified
inverse power law. Both the leading large-
\mu
term and the leading and
subleading small-
\mu
terms — the latter involving a

\mu^{2}(\log\mu)^{2}
contribution arising from a pole collision in the
Mellin plane — are established at the theorem level through explicit
contour-shift arguments with quantitative remainder control. Direct
numerical evaluation confirms both asymptotic regimes quantitatively
and is consistent with the analytic predictions throughout.

The physical significance of these results lies not in providing a
complete parametrization of the UEG correlation energy but in
establishing a diagram-derived asymptotic skeleton for at least one
beyond-RPA correction channel. When the screening parameter is mapped
to the density parameter
r_{s}
through a scale-free power-law ansatz,
the Mellin pole structure translates into a constrained power-log family
of basis functions in
r_{s}
-space. The specific exponent of the
power-law mapping is not determined from first principles in our
framework, but the diagram topology fixes the shape of the admissible
basis independently of that exponent. This provides a diagrammatically
justified alternative to the strategy of choosing analytic fitting
forms by educated guess, and it may extend to finite-rank approximations
of more general screened interaction families as described in
Appendix
C
.

Future work proceeds in two directions. The first is to use the RC-SP
model as an independent reference system for dynamically screened
exchange in its own right. As discussed in
Appendix
B
, the RC-SP kernel is
equivalent to a designer fermion–boson model in which a gapless boson
with linear dispersion couples to the electron density. A quantum Monte
Carlo benchmark of this model would give exact energetics as a function
of the single-pole scale
\mu
, in direct analogy with how Ceperley–Alder
QMC
3
provides the correlation energy of the UEG
as a function of
r_{s}
for LDA. The RC-SP model could thereby supply
exact reference conditions that continuously control the strength of
dynamic screening, complementing rather than replacing the existing UEG
reference system. The second direction is to extend the framework to
general pole models: analyzing the convergence of Mittag–Leffler and
Yukawa-rational expansions of the screened interaction, studying the
stability of the principal Mellin poles under such extensions, and
connecting the RC-SP finite-rank approximation systematically to
realistic plasmon-pole models. If this program succeeds, the
diagram-derived asymptotic basis established here for the RC-SP model
will extend to a genuine first-principles constraint on the analytic
form of the screened-exchange correction for generic dynamic screening.

Appendix A
Exact reduced representation and numerical algorithm

A.1
Quantities to be evaluated

For notational simplicity, we write

\Phi(y):=\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y).

The reduced block is defined by

\Phi(y)=y\int_{1}^{\infty}du\,L(u)\int_{0}^{1}dv\,\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)\,j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u+v}{2}y\right)\,j_{1}\!\left(\frac{u-v}{2}y\right),

(284)

where

L(u)=\log\frac{u+1}{u-1},\qquad\ell^{\mathrm{corr}}(u,v)=\log\frac{1}{uv}.

(285)

The screening factor is

S(\mu)=\frac{N(\mu)}{D_{0}},\qquad N(\mu):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Phi(y)}{y}\,\Xi(\mu y)\,dy,\qquad D_{0}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Phi(y)}{y}\,dy,

(286)

and the kernel is given by

\Xi(z)=z\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-zt}}{1+t^{2}}\,dt\qquad(z>0).

(287)

This exact representation is the core of the numerical computation at finite
\mu
. We explain below how to evaluate Eqs. (
284
) and (
286
) in a stable manner.

A.2
Stable evaluation of
j_{1}
and
\Xi

The spherical Bessel function is given in closed form by

j_{1}(z)=\frac{\sin z}{z^{2}}-\frac{\cos z}{z},

(288)

but cancellation occurs as
z\to 0
, so in numerical computation we switch to the small-argument expansion

j_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{3}-\frac{z^{3}}{30}+\frac{z^{5}}{840}+O(z^{7})\qquad(z\to 0).

(289)

The function
j1_stable
in our code implements this hybrid evaluation between the small-argument expansion and the closed form (
288
).

The kernel
\Xi(z)
has the asymptotic forms

\Xi(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}z+z^{2}(\log z+\gamma-1)-\frac{\pi}{4}z^{3}+O(z^{4}\log z)\qquad(z\to 0),

(290)

\Xi(z)=1-2z^{-2}+24z^{-4}-720z^{-6}+O(z^{-8})\qquad(z\to\infty).

(291)

A natural piecewise strategy is therefore to use (
290
) for very small
z
, (
291
) for very large
z
, and the exact integral (
287
) in the intermediate range.

For a more stable exact evaluation, the finite-interval representation

t=\tan\theta,\qquad\Xi(z)=z\int_{0}^{\pi/2}e^{-z\tan\theta}\,d\theta

(292)

is effective. The essential point of the numerical computation is to treat
\Xi
as an exact integral and to use the small-/
\text{large-}z
series only for stabilization.

A.3
Hybrid evaluation of
\Phi(y)

The difficulty of Eq. (
284
) lies in the logarithmic singularity at
u=1
, the logarithmic singularity at
v=0
, and the fact that for small
y
the dominant contribution shifts to
u\sim y^{-1}
. our code uses two representations depending on the magnitude of
y
.

For
y\ll 1
, it is natural to substitute

t=uy,\qquad u=\frac{t}{y},\qquad du=\frac{dt}{y}.

(293)

Setting further

v=e^{-\tau},\qquad dv=e^{-\tau}d\tau,

(294)

we obtain

\Phi(y)=\int_{y}^{\infty}dt\,L(t/y)\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,e^{-\tau}\bigl(\log(y/t)+\tau\bigr)\,j_{1}\!\left(\frac{t+ye^{-\tau}}{2}\right)\,j_{1}\!\left(\frac{t-ye^{-\tau}}{2}\right).

(295)

In this form, the dominant region remains at
t=O(1)
even for small
y
, avoiding the instability associated with Taylor expansion at fixed
u
. The function
phi_corr_scaled
in our code applies Simpson integration to this representation.

For moderate and large
y
, we use the endpoint-absorbing variables

u=\cosh\eta,\qquad du=\sinh\eta\,d\eta,\qquad v=e^{-\tau},\qquad dv=e^{-\tau}d\tau,

(296)

which give

\displaystyle\Phi(y)=

\displaystyle\,y\int_{0}^{\infty}d\eta\,\sinh\eta\,L(\cosh\eta)\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,e^{-\tau}\bigl(\tau-\log(\cosh\eta)\bigr)

\displaystyle\times j_{1}\!\left(\frac{\cosh\eta+e^{-\tau}}{2}y\right)\,j_{1}\!\left(\frac{\cosh\eta-e^{-\tau}}{2}y\right).

(297)

In this representation, the endpoint singularities at
u=1
and
v=0
are both absorbed into integrable weights on semi-infinite intervals. The function
phi_corr_eta_tau
in our code implements this representation.

A.4
Outer
y
-integration:
x=\log y
transformation and tabulation

The outer integrals in (
286
) are transformed via

y=e^{x},\qquad\frac{dy}{y}=dx

(298)

into

D_{0}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(e^{x})\,dx,\qquad N(\mu)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(e^{x})\,\Xi(\mu e^{x})\,dx.

(299)

The numerical computation is therefore organized in two stages:

1.

Tabulate
\Phi(e^{x})
on a uniform grid in
x=\log y
.

2.

Compute
D_{0}
and
N(\mu)
from the same table and obtain
S(\mu)=N(\mu)/D_{0}
.

The advantage of this organization is that the computationally expensive evaluation of
\Phi(y)
needs to be performed only once, after which the
\mu
-dependent evaluation reduces to a one-dimensional integral. Since the numerator and denominator share the same
\Phi(e^{x})
table, interpolation errors also tend to cancel.

Appendix B
Zero-temperature time-ordered Green function perturbation expansion and the screened-exchange transfer kernel

In the main text, we started from the finite-temperature Matsubara formalism to define the screened-exchange model quantity. The purpose of this Appendix is to show that essentially the same transfer kernel appears when starting from the zero-temperature time-ordered Green function perturbation expansion, so that the exact reduction for the current RC-SP and reducible pole models can be applied under the same algebra. We emphasize that what we perform below is not a naive Wick rotation of the raw real-frequency integrand of the time-ordered formulation but rather an analytic continuation and contour deformation of the transfer-frequency representation obtained after first integrating over the internal fermion frequencies.

B.1
Zero-temperature Feynman propagator and the unsummed screened-exchange diagram

We normalize the Fermi wave number to unity and write

\xi_{\mathbf{p}}:=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\varepsilon_{F}

for the single-particle energy measured from the Fermi level. The zero-temperature time-ordered (Feynman) one-particle propagator is

G_{0}^{\mathrm{F}}(\mathbf{p},\omega)=\frac{\Theta(|\mathbf{p}|-1)}{\omega-\xi_{\mathbf{p}}+\mathrm{i}0}+\frac{\Theta(1-|\mathbf{p}|)}{\omega-\xi_{\mathbf{p}}-\mathrm{i}0},

(300)

where
\Theta(1-|\mathbf{p}|)
represents occupied states and
\Theta(|\mathbf{p}|-1)
represents unoccupied states.

The zero-temperature diagrammatic quantity corresponding to the screened-exchange topology with a screened interaction line
D^{\mathrm{F}}(q,\omega)
and a bare Coulomb line
v_{c}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})=1/|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}
is defined, with a convention-dependent overall factor
\mathcal{A}_{0}
, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{scr2x}}^{(0)}[D]:=

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{A}_{0}\int d^{3}q\,d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{d\omega\,d\omega_{1}\,d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{3}}\;D^{\mathrm{F}}(q,\omega)\,

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\,G_{0}^{\mathrm{F}}(\mathbf{p},\omega_{1})\,G_{0}^{\mathrm{F}}(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\omega_{1}+\omega)\,G_{0}^{\mathrm{F}}(\mathbf{k},\omega_{2})\,G_{0}^{\mathrm{F}}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\omega_{2}+\omega).

(301)

Equation (
301
) does not claim to give the completed correlation energy of the actual Coulomb system but provides a diagrammatic reference quantity corresponding to the zero-temperature screened-exchange topology.

B.2
Real-frequency representation of the particle-hole block

We consider the one-block quantity

Q_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},\omega):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\omega_{1}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,G_{0}^{\mathrm{F}}(\mathbf{p},\omega_{1})\,G_{0}^{\mathrm{F}}(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q},\omega_{1}+\omega).

(302)

Poles in the upper and lower half-planes of
\omega_{1}
appear only when
|\mathbf{p}|<1<|\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}|
or
|\mathbf{p}|>1>|\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}|
. A residue calculation gives

Q_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},\omega)=\frac{\Theta(1-|\mathbf{p}|)\Theta(|\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}|-1)}{\omega-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})+\mathrm{i}0}-\frac{\Theta(|\mathbf{p}|-1)\Theta(1-|\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}|)}{\omega-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})-\mathrm{i}0},

(303)

where

\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q}):=\xi_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}-\xi_{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{q}+\frac{q^{2}}{2}.

(304)

Writing

\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q}):=\Theta(1-|\mathbf{p}|)-\Theta(1-|\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}|)\in\{-1,0,1\},

(305)

we can express this compactly as

Q_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},\omega)=\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}{\omega-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})+\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}.

(306)

B.3
Representation as a multiple integral of real functions

Applying the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula

\frac{1}{x\pm\mathrm{i}0}=\operatorname{PV}\frac{1}{x}\mp\mathrm{i}\pi\delta(x)

to (
306
), we obtain

Q_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},\omega)=\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})\,\operatorname{PV}\frac{1}{\omega-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}-\mathrm{i}\pi\,\chi_{\mathrm{ph}}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})\,\delta\!\bigl(\omega-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})\bigr),

(307)

where

\chi_{\mathrm{ph}}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q}):=|\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})|=\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})^{2}.

(308)

The quantity

X^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{q},\omega):=\int d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\,Q_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},\omega)\,Q_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q},\omega)

(309)

therefore decomposes into three parts given entirely by real functions:

X^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=X_{\mathrm{PP}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)-\mathrm{i}\pi\,X_{\mathrm{P}\delta}(\mathbf{q},\omega)-\pi^{2}X_{\delta\delta}(\mathbf{q},\omega),

(310)

where

X_{\mathrm{PP}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\int d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})\Delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\operatorname{PV}\frac{1}{\omega-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}\operatorname{PV}\frac{1}{\omega-\delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})},

(311)

X_{\mathrm{P}\delta}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\int d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\left[\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})\chi_{\mathrm{ph}}(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})\operatorname{PV}\frac{1}{\omega-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}\delta\!\bigl(\omega-\delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})\bigr)+(\mathbf{p}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{k})\right],

(312)

X_{\delta\delta}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\int d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;\frac{\chi_{\mathrm{ph}}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})\chi_{\mathrm{ph}}(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\delta\!\bigl(\omega-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})\bigr)\delta\!\bigl(\omega-\delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})\bigr).

(313)

Equation (
301
) can therefore be written as a multiple integral over real frequencies:

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{scr2x}}^{(0)}[D]=\mathcal{A}_{0}\int d^{3}q\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,D^{\mathrm{F}}(q,\omega)\,X^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{q},\omega).

(314)

B.4
Off-axis transfer kernel and imaginary-axis representation

To reorganize the above real-axis representation, we introduce the off-axis particle-hole kernel for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
:

Q_{0}^{\sharp}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},z):=\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}{z-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})},

(315)

and define the off-axis transfer kernel as

X^{\sharp}(\mathbf{q},z):=\int d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\,Q_{0}^{\sharp}(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q},z)\,Q_{0}^{\sharp}(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q},z).

(316)

This
X^{\sharp}
is analytic away from the real axis, and on the imaginary axis it takes the form

X^{\sharp}(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\xi)=\int d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\,\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}{\mathrm{i}\xi-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}\,\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})}{\mathrm{i}\xi-\delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})},

(317)

which is exactly the same form as the kernel
X(\mathbf{q},\xi)
appearing in Section II.B of the main text.

We also consider an off-axis analytic continuation
D^{\sharp}(q,z)
of the screened interaction and impose the following standard assumptions.

(A1)

D^{\sharp}(q,z)
is analytic away from the real axis, and its real-axis boundary values recover
D^{\mathrm{F}}(q,\omega)
.

(A2)

The product
D^{\sharp}(q,z)\,X^{\sharp}(\mathbf{q},z)
is analytic away from the real axis.

(A3)

D^{\sharp}(q,z)\,X^{\sharp}(\mathbf{q},z)
decays sufficiently fast on large semicircles so that the arc contribution vanishes under contour deformation.

(A4)

The real-axis distributional representation (
314
) is consistent with the transfer-frequency contour representation reconstructed from the boundary values of
D^{\sharp}X^{\sharp}
.

Under these assumptions, we can lift Eq. (
314
) to a transfer-frequency contour for
D^{\sharp}(q,z)\,X^{\sharp}(\mathbf{q},z)
and deform the contour to the imaginary axis. Setting
\omega=\mathrm{i}\xi
, we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{scr2x}}^{(0)}[D]=\mathcal{A}_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\xi}{\pi}\int d^{3}q\;D(q,\mathrm{i}\xi)\,X(\mathbf{q},\xi),

(318)

X(\mathbf{q},\xi)=\int d^{3}p\,d^{3}k\;\frac{1}{|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\,\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}{\mathrm{i}\xi-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q})}\,\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})}{\mathrm{i}\xi-\delta(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{q})}.

(319)

Since

\Delta(-\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q};\mathbf{q})=-\Delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q}),\qquad\delta(-\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q};\mathbf{q})=-\delta(\mathbf{p};\mathbf{q}),

we have
X(\mathbf{q},-\xi)=X(\mathbf{q},\xi)
, so the folding to the half-axis is the same as in Section
II.2
of the main text.

B.5
Specialization to the reducible pole model

For the current RC-SP and reducible pole models, we can take the time-ordered bosonic continuation as

D_{\mu}^{\sharp}(q,z):=\frac{\mu^{2}}{-z^{2}+\mu^{2}q^{2}},

(320)

which on the imaginary axis gives

D_{\mu}^{\sharp}(q,\mathrm{i}\xi)=\frac{\mu^{2}}{\xi^{2}+\mu^{2}q^{2}},

(321)

recovering the RC-SP model used in the main text. Setting
\xi=qz
, we obtain

D_{\mu}(q,\mathrm{i}qz)=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\,\frac{\mu^{2}}{z^{2}+\mu^{2}},

and the
\xi=qz
transformation, Fourier factorization, Schwinger representation, centered affine transformation, and exact one-variable reduction of Section III onward apply line by line.

What we have shown above is not that the thermodynamic meanings of the finite-temperature Matsubara formalism and the zero-temperature time-ordered perturbation theory are completely identical. The claim is that, for the screened-exchange topology, starting from the zero-temperature time-ordered Green-function expansion, integrating over the internal fermion frequencies first, and analytically continuing the transfer-frequency representation off-axis, one arrives at a quantity of the same form as the imaginary-axis kernel
X(\mathbf{q},\xi)
used in the main text. The current RC-SP reduction is therefore not specific to the Matsubara bookkeeping but can be understood as having essentially the same content as an analytic reference problem for the zero-temperature screened-exchange topology.

B.6
Designer fermion–boson reference model and Hubbard–Stratonovich representation

The RC-SP kernel of this paper does not claim to describe an interaction that is directly realized in current standard experiments. However, if it is understood as an auxiliary interacting Fermi model with a retarded density-density interaction as the bare vertex, it can be regarded as an analytic benchmark of a designer fermion–boson Hamiltonian. In this sense, the RC-SP model is not merely a solvable toy kernel but carries physical meaning as a reference system inspired by mediated-interaction platforms.

For simplicity, we consider the Coulomb RC-SP benchmark

V_{\mu}(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\xi_{m}):=\lambda\,\frac{\mu^{2}}{\xi_{m}^{2}+\mu^{2}q^{2}},

(322)

where
\lambda>0
is the interaction strength. The fermion-only Euclidean action with this retarded density-density interaction as the bare vertex is

S_{\mathrm{F}}[\bar{\psi},\psi]=\sum_{\mathbf{k},n,\sigma}\bar{\psi}_{\mathbf{k}n\sigma}(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}})\psi_{\mathbf{k}n\sigma}+\frac{1}{2\beta V}\sum_{\mathbf{q},m}\rho_{-\mathbf{q},-m}\,V_{\mu}(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\xi_{m})\,\rho_{\mathbf{q},m},

(323)

where

\rho_{\mathbf{q},m}:=\sum_{\mathbf{k},n,\sigma}\bar{\psi}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},n+m,\sigma}\,\psi_{\mathbf{k},n,\sigma}.

(324)

Applying a Gaussian Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation to the interaction term gives formally

\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\beta V}\sum_{\mathbf{q},m}\rho_{-\mathbf{q},-m}\,V_{\mu}(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\xi_{m})\,\rho_{\mathbf{q},m}\right]\propto\int\mathcal{D}\phi\;e^{-S_{\mathrm{HS}}[\phi,\rho]},

(325)

with

S_{\mathrm{HS}}[\phi,\rho]=\frac{1}{2\beta V}\sum_{\mathbf{q},m}\phi_{-\mathbf{q},-m}\,V_{\mu}(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\xi_{m})^{-1}\,\phi_{\mathbf{q},m}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\beta V}\sum_{\mathbf{q},m}\phi_{-\mathbf{q},-m}\,\rho_{\mathbf{q},m}.

(326)

For (
322
),

V_{\mu}(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\xi_{m})^{-1}=\frac{\xi_{m}^{2}+\mu^{2}q^{2}}{\lambda\mu^{2}}.

(327)

Rescaling
\phi=\sqrt{\lambda}\mu\,\varphi
, we obtain the equivalent fermion–boson action

S[\bar{\psi},\psi,\varphi]=\sum_{\mathbf{k},n,\sigma}\bar{\psi}_{\mathbf{k}n\sigma}(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}})\psi_{\mathbf{k}n\sigma}+\frac{1}{2\beta V}\sum_{\mathbf{q},m}\varphi_{-\mathbf{q},-m}(\xi_{m}^{2}+\mu^{2}q^{2})\varphi_{\mathbf{q},m}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\lambda}\mu}{\beta V}\sum_{\mathbf{q},m}\varphi_{-\mathbf{q},-m}\rho_{\mathbf{q},m}.

(328)

In the coordinate representation, the bosonic sector becomes

S_{\mathrm{b}}[\varphi]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int d^{3}x\;\left[(\partial_{\tau}\varphi)^{2}+\mu^{2}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}\right],

(329)

so that
\varphi
can be interpreted as a gapless boson with the linear dispersion

\omega_{\mathbf{q}}=\mu|\mathbf{q}|.

(330)

The RC-SP/Coulomb kernel can therefore be understood either as an interacting Fermi model with
V_{\mu}(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{i}\xi_{m})=\lambda\,\mu^{2}/(\xi_{m}^{2}+\mu^{2}q^{2})
as the bare retarded interaction or, equivalently, as a designer fermion–boson model in which a gapless boson with linear dispersion couples to the density.

The advantage of this viewpoint is that the screened second-order exchange quantity analyzed in this paper can be interpreted not as a mere formal model quantity but as an analytic benchmark for the screened-exchange channel of such a retarded Fermi model or fermion–boson model. Furthermore, if a sign-free or mild-sign-problem lattice regularization can be constructed for such a designer model, a QMC exact many-body benchmark becomes possible, and the model is expected to function as a density-functional reference system for the screened-exchange residual channel.

For the general RC-SP kernel
D_{\mu}(q,\mathrm{i}qz)=D_{q}(q)\mu^{2}/(\mu^{2}+z^{2})
, a similar HS representation exists, but when
D_{q}(q)\neq q^{-2}
, the corresponding bosonic quadratic form is generally spatially nonlocal. In this sense, the Coulomb RC-SP benchmark provides the most concise designer reference model with a local gradient boson action (
329
).

Appendix C
The RC-SP basis element as an analytic basis for the general pole model

As described in the previous section, exact one-variable reduction for the general one-pole family

D_{1\mathrm{p}}(q,iqz)=D_{q}(q)\,\frac{u(q)^{2}}{u(q)^{2}+z^{2}},\qquad u(q):=\frac{\Omega_{q}}{q},

(331)

holds only for the RC-SP class
u(q)=\mu=\mathrm{const.}
This fact does not confine the RC-SP model to a solvable toy model but rather provides the motivation for understanding it as an
analytic basis element
for systematically approximating generic one-pole screening. The purpose of this section is to make this viewpoint slightly more explicit mathematically.

The RC-SP basis element

w_{\mu}(z):=\frac{\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}+z^{2}},\qquad\mu>0,\ z>0

(332)

satisfies the following. Setting
x:=\log\mu
, we have

w_{\mu}(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{2(\log z-x)}}=:W(x;z),

so that

\partial_{x}W(x;z)=\frac{2e^{2(\log z-x)}}{\bigl(1+e^{2(\log z-x)}\bigr)^{2}}.

The right-hand side is the derivative of a logistic function, and

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R},\ z>0}|\partial_{x}W(x;z)|=\frac{1}{2}.

(333)

By the mean-value theorem,

|w_{\mu_{1}}(z)-w_{\mu_{2}}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\,|\log\mu_{1}-\log\mu_{2}|\qquad(\forall z>0).

(334)

This estimate shows that when
\mu
-nodes are placed on a
\log\mu
grid, the RC-SP basis element family forms a basis that is uniformly controllable in the
z
-direction. In particular, given a
\log\mu
-grid
\{\mu_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and choosing the nearest
\mu_{m}
to
u(q)
for each
q
, the quantity

\sup_{z>0}\left|\frac{u(q)^{2}}{u(q)^{2}+z^{2}}-\frac{\mu_{m}^{2}}{\mu_{m}^{2}+z^{2}}\right|

is uniformly bounded by the
\log\mu
-grid spacing. In this sense, the RC-SP basis element is mathematically natural as a basis for the
z
-direction of generic one-pole screening.

For the ordinary plasmon-pole model of the three-dimensional UEG,

u(q)=\frac{\Omega_{q}}{q}\sim\frac{\omega_{p}}{q}\qquad(q\to 0),

so that
u(q)\to\infty
. A finite-rank approximation with
\mu
-nodes on a finite interval therefore cannot capture the small-
q
region, and it is natural to treat this region as the static channel

w_{\infty}(z):=\lim_{\mu\to\infty}w_{\mu}(z)=1,

(335)

using finitely many RC-SP basis elements
\{w_{\mu_{m}}\}
only for the remaining
q
-region. The fact that the kernel of this paper converges to
K_{\mu}(y)\to 1/(\pi y)
as
\mu\to\infty
shows that this static channel is naturally included in the current framework.

With this in mind, for generic one-pole screening we first consider

\frac{u(q)^{2}}{u(q)^{2}+z^{2}}\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}(q)\,w_{\mu_{m}}(z)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}(q)\,\frac{\mu_{m}^{2}}{\mu_{m}^{2}+z^{2}},

(336)

so that the screened interaction can be written as

D(q,iqz)\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}F_{m}(q)\,w_{\mu_{m}}(z),\qquad F_{m}(q):=D_{q}(q)c_{m}(q).

(337)

For each
w_{\mu_{m}}
, the exact dynamic reduction and the kernel library
K_{\mu_{m}}(y)
derived in this paper apply directly. The essential problem remaining for the general pole model therefore shifts to how to treat each radial profile
F_{m}(q)
analytically.

The radial transform that is essential in the current reduction is

\Psi_{F}(s_{1},s_{2}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}q\,F(q)\,[\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})]\,dq.

(338)

In the Coulomb case
F(q)=q^{-2}
, we have
qF(q)=1/q
and
\Psi_{F}(s_{1},s_{2})=\log|s_{2}/s_{1}|
, which is the mother kernel producing the current
\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y)
. For a general
F
, if

qF(q)=\frac{A_{F}}{q}+G_{F}(q)

(339)

with

\int_{0}^{1}q^{2}|G_{F}(q)|\,dq<\infty,\qquad\int_{1}^{\infty}|G_{F}(q)|\,dq<\infty,

(340)

then

\Psi_{F}(s_{1},s_{2})=A_{F}\log\left|\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}}\right|+\psi_{F}(s_{1},s_{2}),

(341)

where

\psi_{F}(s_{1},s_{2}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{F}(q)\,[\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})]\,dq.

(342)

Indeed, near
q\to 0
,
|\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})|\leq C(s_{1},s_{2})\,q^{2}
, so the first condition of (
340
) ensures absolute convergence of
\psi_{F}
near
q=0
, and near
q\to\infty
,
|\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})|\leq 2
, so the second condition ensures convergence. The singular part of
\Psi_{F}
is therefore entirely localized in
A_{F}\log|s_{2}/s_{1}|
, and the Coulomb RC-SP model provides a prototype for the singular channel of a general radial profile.

Approximating
F_{m}(q)
by an integer-power series

F_{m}(q)\sim\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{nm}\,q^{n-2}

(343)

leads to the radial transform

\Psi_{n}(s_{1},s_{2}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}q^{n-1}\,[\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})]\,dq

(344)

for each term. For
n=0
, the Frullani formula gives the current Coulomb kernel
\Psi_{0}(s_{1},s_{2})=\log|s_{2}/s_{1}|
, but using Abel regularization for analytic continuation, even
n\geq 2
produces algebraic singular kernels of the form
\Psi_{n}(s_{1},s_{2})\propto s_{1}^{-n}-s_{2}^{-n}
. In the current reduced geometry,
s_{1}=y|R_{k}|
, so this generates
|R_{k}|^{-n}
on the
R_{k}=0
surface, which is locally non-integrable as an ordinary function already at the first nontrivial correction
n=2
. A naive integer-power expansion for the
q
-dependent coefficients is therefore not suited for directly generalizing the current exact reduction within the framework of ordinary functions.

In contrast, approximating
F_{m}(q)
by a rational function of
q^{2}
is essentially compatible with the current reduction. Viewing
\mathcal{F}_{m}(x):=F_{m}(\sqrt{x})
as a function of
x=q^{2}
and assuming that
\mathcal{F}_{m}
is meromorphic, the Mittag–Leffler theorem naturally produces the partial fraction expansion

\mathcal{F}_{m}(x)=P_{m}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{J_{m}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{jm}}\frac{a_{jm\ell}}{(x+\kappa_{j}^{2})^{\ell}}.

(345)

In the current problem,
D_{q}(q)\sim q^{-2}
and
c_{m}(q)
is bounded in a typical situation, so
F_{m}(q)=O(q^{-2})
for large
q
, making the polynomial part
P_{m}
unnecessary. The most natural practical rational approximation is therefore

F_{m}(q)\approx\sum_{j=1}^{J_{m}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{jm}}\frac{a_{jm\ell}}{(q^{2}+\kappa_{j}^{2})^{\ell}}.

(346)

For the
\ell=1
radial transform

\Psi_{\kappa}(s_{1},s_{2}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\;\frac{q}{q^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\,[\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})],

(347)

the splitting

\frac{q}{q^{2}+\kappa^{2}}=\frac{1}{q}-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{q(q^{2}+\kappa^{2})}

(348)

gives

\Psi_{\kappa}(s_{1},s_{2})=\log\left|\frac{s_{2}}{s_{1}}\right|-\kappa^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\;\frac{\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})}{q(q^{2}+\kappa^{2})}.

(349)

The integrand of the second term satisfies
O(q^{2})
as
q\to 0
and
O(q^{-3})
as
q\to\infty
, so it is an absolutely convergent regular correction. In this sense, the Yukawa/rational basis preserves the logarithmic singularity class of
\log|s_{2}/s_{1}|
from the current Coulomb case while adding smooth corrections on top of it. This is the decisive difference from integer-power expansions.

Since

\frac{1}{(q^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{\ell}}=\frac{(-1)^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\frac{\partial^{\ell-1}}{\partial(\kappa^{2})^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{q^{2}+\kappa^{2}},

(350)

the higher-pole radial transforms are generated by
\kappa^{2}
-differentiation:

\Psi_{\kappa,\ell}(s_{1},s_{2}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}dq\;\frac{q}{(q^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{\ell}}\,[\cos(qs_{1})-\cos(qs_{2})]=\frac{(-1)^{\ell-1}}{(\ell-1)!}\frac{\partial^{\ell-1}}{\partial(\kappa^{2})^{\ell-1}}\Psi_{\kappa}(s_{1},s_{2}).

(351)

What is essentially needed for a Mittag–Leffler-type rational expansion is therefore the analysis of the
\ell=1
Yukawa block
\Psi_{\kappa}
, with higher-order poles generated systematically by
\kappa^{2}
-differentiation.

For the general pole model, we decompose

D(q,iqz)=D^{(M,J,L)}(q,iqz)+R^{(M,J,L)}(q,iqz),

(352)

where

D^{(M,J,L)}(q,iqz)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{m}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{jm}}a_{jm\ell}\,\frac{1}{(q^{2}+\kappa_{j}^{2})^{\ell}}\,\frac{\mu_{m}^{2}}{\mu_{m}^{2}+z^{2}}.

(353)

The current exact reduction then gives the corresponding decomposition of the reduced block:

\Phi(y)=\Phi^{(M,J,L)}(y)+\Phi_{R}(y).

If the remainder satisfies

\Phi_{R}(y)=O\!\bigl(y^{1+\delta_{<}}\bigr)\quad(y\to 0),\qquad\Phi_{R}(y)=O\!\bigl(y^{-2-\delta_{>}}\bigr)\quad(y\to\infty),

(354)

then the Mellin transform of
\Phi_{R}
is regular near
s=-1
and
s=2
. In this case, the principal Mellin poles of the general pole model are determined by
\Phi^{(M,J,L)}
alone, and the remainder
\Phi_{R}
is relegated to a subleading regular correction. The finite-rank part consisting of RC-SP basis elements and Yukawa/rational radial blocks therefore carries the principal asymptotic structure of the generic pole model.

The above discussion shows that the Coulomb RC-SP model analyzed in this paper is not merely an exact analysis of the
\kappa=0
benchmark block. Rather, the analysis and finite-
\mu
numerics of

\Phi_{0}^{\mathrm{corr}}(y),\qquad K_{\mu}(y),\qquad\Xi(z)

obtained in this paper provide the first analytic and numerical infrastructure for constructing a double-separable rational approximation for the generic pole model in the future. In other words, the current Coulomb RC-SP model can be understood as providing the
analytic mother block
for the principal asymptotic analysis of general screening based on a Mittag–Leffler-type rational expansion.

This paper does not carry out the concrete construction or convergence analysis of (
345
)–(
354
). However, given that a naive expansion around a constant
\mu
is not uniform in the infrared for the ordinary plasmon-pole model and that integer-power expansions produce algebraic singularities incompatible with the current reduced geometry, the combination of RC-SP basis elements and Mittag–Leffler/Yukawa-rational radial blocks is considered one of the most promising systematic routes toward general screening while preserving the current exact reduction.

Appendix D
Necessity of exact
q
-
z
separability in the one-pole family

Proposition 34

(Necessity of exact
q
-
z
separability in the one-pole family)
.

Consider the one-pole family

D_{1\mathrm{p}}(q,iqz)=D_{q}(q)\,\frac{u(q)^{2}}{u(q)^{2}+z^{2}},\qquad u(q)>0.

(355)

Let
I\subset(0,\infty)
be an interval on which
D_{q}(q)\neq 0
. Suppose that there exist functions
\widetilde{D}_{q}(q)
and
w(z)
such that

D_{1\mathrm{p}}(q,iqz)=\widetilde{D}_{q}(q)\,w(z)\qquad(q\in I,\ z\in J),

(356)

where
J\subset[0,\infty)
contains at least two points. Then

u(q)=\mu=\mathrm{const.}\qquad(q\in I).

(357)

Proof.

Take
q_{1},q_{2}\in I
arbitrarily. By (
356
),

\frac{D_{1\mathrm{p}}(q_{1},iqz)}{D_{1\mathrm{p}}(q_{2},iqz)}=\frac{\widetilde{D}_{q}(q_{1})}{\widetilde{D}_{q}(q_{2})}

is independent of
z
. On the other hand, using (
355
),

\frac{D_{1\mathrm{p}}(q_{1},iqz)}{D_{1\mathrm{p}}(q_{2},iqz)}=\frac{D_{q}(q_{1})}{D_{q}(q_{2})}\frac{u(q_{1})^{2}}{u(q_{2})^{2}}\frac{u(q_{2})^{2}+z^{2}}{u(q_{1})^{2}+z^{2}}.

Therefore

\frac{u(q_{2})^{2}+z^{2}}{u(q_{1})^{2}+z^{2}}

is also independent of
z
. Since
J
contains at least two points, we can choose
z_{1},z_{2}\in J
with
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
, giving

\frac{u(q_{2})^{2}+z_{1}^{2}}{u(q_{1})^{2}+z_{1}^{2}}=\frac{u(q_{2})^{2}+z_{2}^{2}}{u(q_{1})^{2}+z_{2}^{2}}.

Cross-multiplying yields

\bigl(u(q_{2})^{2}-u(q_{1})^{2}\bigr)(z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2})=0.

Since
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
, we have
u(q_{1})^{2}=u(q_{2})^{2}
, and since
u(q)>0
,
u(q_{1})=u(q_{2})
. Since
q_{1},q_{2}\in I
were arbitrary,
u(q)
is constant on
I
.
∎
