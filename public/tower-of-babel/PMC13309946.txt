Beyond resistance: optimizing CAR-T sequencing following bispecific antibody failure in DLBCL
Authors: Wei Zhang, Yuan Fang, Sen Gao, Yao Yao
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the recent study by Li et al. [1], which addresses a burgeoning clinical dilemma: how to manage R/R DLBCL after the failure of bispecific antibodies (BsAbs). Their report of a 100% ORR with CAR-T salvage therapy is both provocative and highly encouraging. However, as clinicians navigating the “post-BsAb era,” we believe several nuanced aspects of their data warrant closer scrutiny to ensure these findings translate robustly into broader practice.

One striking observation in this cohort is the lack of cross-resistance between CD20-targeted BsAbs and CD19-directed CAR-T cells [2]. While Li et al. suggest that switching targets is sufficient, we must question whether the quality of the T-cell graft was compromised by prior BsAb exposure. Recent studies indicate that continuous CD3 stimulation by BsAbs can induce a specific “exhaustion signature” in T-cells [3]. Although the authors’ median washout of 42 days is pragmatic, did they observe any correlation between the duration of prior BsAb therapy and the ex vivo expansion kinetics of the subsequent CAR-T product? This “T-cell fitness” metric is often the silent determinant of long-term durability that mere ORR figures might mask.

Furthermore, the safety profile reported is surprisingly benign given the heavily pre-treated nature of these 12 patients. The low incidence of severe CRS (8.3%) and absence of ICANS are noteworthy. We speculate whether the prior BsAb treatment might have “primed” the patients’ cytokine milieu or reduced tumor burden just enough to dampen the toxicities of subsequent CAR-T infusion—a phenomenon sometimes termed “immune desensitization [4].” Detailed cytokine profiling (e.g., IL-6, IFN-γ) from this cohort would be invaluable to confirm this hypothesis.

Lastly, the intervention of maintenance therapies (PD-1 or XPO1 inhibitors) in 75% of the patients complicates the interpretation of the 12-month PFS. In translational medicine, distinguishing the “booster effect” of checkpoint inhibitors from the primary CAR-T efficacy is crucial.

We congratulate the authors on this timely contribution. Nevertheless, defining the molecular “sweet spot” for transitioning from BsAbs to CAR-T remains a moving target that requires prospective validation.
