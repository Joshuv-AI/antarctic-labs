SAFE: a Mix‐and‐Read Assay for miRNA Detection in Extracellular Vesicles From Unprocessed Plasma Toward Clinical Disease Diagnosis
Authors: Subhra Sulipta Jena, Soo Jin Kim, Hyeonha Jang, Lianhua Chi, Yunji Eom, Kyung‐Oh Doh, JaeJong Lee, Jun Seok Park, Junyeop Lee, Seonki Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRapid and accurate diagnosis of cardiovascular disease (CVD) is essential for timely intervention, yet current workflows often rely on hospital‐based instrumentation that can delay decision‐making. We present SAFE (Sonication‐Assisted liposome Fusion with Extracellular vesicles (EVs)), a rapid, direct‐from‐plasma assay that quantifies microRNAs (miRNAs) encapsulated within EVs in approximately 10 min, providing a molecular signature of myocardial injury. SAFE delivers molecular beacons (MBs) into endogenous EVs through liposome fusion, eliminating the need for EV isolation and RNA extraction while minimizing interference from plasma proteins by confining hybridization within the EV lumen. Brief sonication accelerates fusion kinetics, shortening the assay time to 10 min. In a pilot clinical evaluation involving patients with CVD (n = 20) and healthy controls (n = 15), SAFE detected elevated levels of two cardiac‐associated miRNAs (miR‐133a and miR‐208a), achieving a diagnostic accuracy of 91.4% for each. These findings position SAFE as a rapid and minimally processed platform for the direct quantification of EV‐associated miRNAs, well suited for time‐critical CVD triage and ready for implementation studies across diverse clinical environments.

Introduction

Rapid molecular diagnostics are central to effective clinical care, enabling timely disease detection, early intervention, and improved outcomes. In cardiovascular medicine, diagnostic timeliness is particularly critical, as outcomes for acute presentations such as ST‐elevation myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, and aortic dissection correlate strongly with time‐to‐diagnosis [1, 2, 3]. However, conventional diagnostic workflows—combining electrocardiography (ECG), cardiac troponin assays, and imaging—remain constrained by assay turnaround time, infrastructure requirements, and dependence on specialized personnel. These limitations underscore the urgent need for rapid, accessible molecular tests that can operate directly at the point of care (POC) to enable immediate triage and clinical decision‐making.

Existing point‐of‐care testing (POCT) platforms primarily rely on enzymatic or immunochemical reactions targeting abundant plasma proteins. While effective for analytes such as glucose or troponins, these systems struggle to detect low‐abundance biomarkers within the complex biochemical background of unprocessed biofluids [4, 5]. Their reliance on high‐copy or high‐affinity targets constrains the molecular information accessible at the bedside, underscoring the need for next‐generation POCT modalities that can capture upstream, cell‐level pathophysiological signatures.

Among potential biomarker classes currently underrepresented in POCT, nucleic acids (NAs) such as messenger RNAs and small noncoding RNAs (e.g., microRNAs, miRNAs) offer direct molecular fingerprints of disease states through their regulatory and tissue‐specific expression patterns [6, 7, 8]. Extracellular vesicles (EVs)—nanoscale, membrane‐bound particles actively secreted by cells—have emerged as natural carriers of NAs and other macromolecules for minimally invasive diagnostics [9, 10]. EVs integrate molecular information from heterogeneous cell populations and preserve it within a lipid bilayer that protects enclosed cargo from enzymatic degradation. This structural stability enables reliable handling and storage, making EVs a compelling medium for “ready‐to‐analyze” NAs in plasma or serum. In cardiovascular contexts, EV‐associated miRNAs have demonstrated strong diagnostic potential [11, 12, 13]. For example, it was reported that acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is accompanied by increased circulating “myo‐miRs” (miR‐1, miR‐208, miR‐499), predominantly encapsulated in exosomes, while miR‐133 is enriched in non‐vesicular fractions [14]. Similarly, an exosomal miRNA panel (miR‐155 and miR‐320a) has been identified that is predictive of fulminant myocarditis across independent cohorts [15]. Collectively, these findings establish EV‐associated miRNAs as rich, disease‐relevant biomarkers with promise for real‐time cardiovascular diagnostics.

Despite this promise, the translation of EV‐based assays to POCT has been hindered by workflow bottlenecks and technical complexity [16, 17, 18]. Conventional pipelines require sequential EV isolation (e.g., ultracentrifugation, size‐exclusion chromatography, or polymer precipitation), vesicle lysis, NA extraction, and downstream molecular analysis by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or next‐generation sequencing (NGS)—steps that are time‐consuming, equipment‐intensive, and prone to sample loss and bias. Moreover, purity remains a persistent concern, as non‐EV contaminants such as lipoproteins and protein aggregates often co‐isolate and confound downstream analyses [19, 20, 21]. This challenge is particularly acute for EV‐associated miRNA detection, as circulating miRNAs exist in both vesicular and non‐vesicular forms (e.g., Argonaute complexes and lipoproteins), complicating attribution to bona fide EV cargo and elevating background signal [22, 23]. Consequently, EV‐based assays remain largely confined to research settings rather than clinical use.

Recent advances in bioanalytical chemistry have begun to enable direct interrogation of EVs without prior isolation—leveraging membrane fusion to access their encapsulated contents [24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. These emerging approaches suggest that overcoming the physical barrier imposed by the EV membrane could unlock rapid, in situ molecular readouts of intravesicular cargo. However, conventional fusion‐based assays typically require prolonged incubation (30 min to 2 h) to achieve adequate membrane mixing or additional surface modifications to enhance fusion efficiency, limiting their practical speed and throughput. Building upon this concept, we developed SAFE (Sonication‐Assisted liposome Fusion with EVs), a mix‐and‐read plasma assay that quantifies intravesicular miRNAs within approximately 10 min. In SAFE, molecular beacons (MBs) are delivered into endogenous EVs via controlled liposome–EV fusion, allowing hybridization readouts to occur exclusively within the EV lumen. This confinement minimizes interference from extracellular plasma proteins and eliminates the need for EV isolation or RNA extraction. The integrated sonication dramatically accelerates fusion kinetics, reducing the total assay time by nearly sixfold compared with conventional incubation‐based systems.

In a preliminary evaluation using plasma from patients with CVD (n = 20) and healthy controls (n = 15), SAFE detected elevated levels of cardiac‐associated miRNAs (miR‐133a, miR‐208a) with a diagnostic accuracy of 91.4%. These findings demonstrate that SAFE enables rapid, quantitative detection of EV‐encapsulated NAs directly from unprocessed plasma, achieving both speed and specificity compatible with clinical triage workflows. By integrating molecular‐level sensitivity with point‐of‐care practicality, SAFE establishes a new framework for EV–based molecular diagnostics, paving the way for rapid and decentralized detection of cardiovascular injury and other time‐critical pathologies.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of MB‐encapsulated Liposomes

The streamlined SAFE protocol entails simply mixing native plasma with an aqueous suspension of molecular probes, followed by ∼10 min of brief sonication (Figure 1). The design relies on two key elements: (i) stem‐loop molecular beacons (MBs) that are fluorescence‐quenched until target binding, and (ii) their intravesicular delivery into intact extracellular vesicles (EVs) via vesicle fusion. Selective fusion with lipid‐bounded vesicles enables single‐step delivery of MBs into the intravesicular space of EVs containing target miRNAs, eliminating the need for EV lysis and miRNA isolation. Inside EVs, MB–miRNA hybridization de‐quenches fluorescence, whereas unbound MBs remain quenched, allowing a wash‐free, one‐pot quantitative readout directly in plasma. Intravesicular compartmentalization further concentrates the signal and minimizes dilution into the surrounding medium, ensuring high detection sensitivity. Figure 2a illustrates the architecture of the MB‐loaded fusogenic liposomes that mediate SAFE. The size of the MB‐loaded liposomes targeting model miRNA miR‐21 (Lipo‐21) was comparable to that of EVs derived from the MCF7 cell line, as confirmed by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure 2b).

Free MBs generated fluorescence signals in proportion to the concentration of miRNA mimics. In contrast, MBs encapsulated within liposomes produced no detectable signal upon exposure to miRNA mimics, confirming that the liposomal membrane effectively shields MBs from extracellular interactions (Figure 2c). In the conventional incubation method (60 min), EV‐associated miRNA detection via vesicle‐liposome fusion was strongly dependent on incubation temperature: miR‐21‐specific signals from Lipo‐21 increased with temperature up to 60°C (Figure 2d). However, background signals from liposomes containing a random‐sequence MB (Lipo‐R) also increased at higher temperature, reducing the overall signal‐to‐noise ratio (Lipo‐21/Lipo‐R) at 60°C than 37°C (Figure 2d, yellow dots). Incubation at 37°C yielded EV concentration–dependent fluorescence with sufficiently low background from Lipo‐R, resulting in Lipo‐21/Lipo‐R high around 5.1 at 1010 particles/mL (Figure 2e).

Rationale for SAFE

Low‐frequency sonication (40 kHz) transiently perturbs lipid bilayers by inducing localized pressure oscillations, microscale shear stresses, and incipient cavitation events. These mechanical effects disrupt lipid packing and temporarily increase membrane fluidity, thereby promoting transient curvature fluctuations. Such perturbations are expected to lower the effective energy barrier for the formation of hemifusion stalk intermediates, a critical step in vesicle fusion, consequently accelerating liposome–EV fusion kinetics (Figure 2f) [32, 33, 34]. This mechanism is supported by our experimental observations: fluorescence signals for miR‐21 detection, which typically required over 60 min of incubation at 37°C, were reproduced after only 10 min of sonication (Figure 2g). Importantly, this sixfold reduction in assay time brings the workflow into a practically relevant time frame, a key requirement for translational diagnostic applications in clinical settings. Although high‐energy sonication is known to disrupt lipid vesicles, no detectable damage to liposomes or EVs was observed under our experimental conditions (Figure 2h). Furthermore, control experiments using miRNA mimics showed comparable results between incubation and sonication conditions, confirming that MBs do not escape from liposomes and that free miRNAs (or miRNA mimics) are not taken up by liposomes during sonication (Figure S1). These findings indicate that even if miRNAs are inadvertently released from EVs, they would not contribute to the observed fluorescence signal. Finally, vesicle fusion was monitored by measuring the FRET signal between DiO‐labeled liposomes and DiI‐labeled EVs during SAFE (Figure S2). A simultaneous increase in fluorescence emission at 575 nm was observed, indicating efficient FRET between DiO and DiI and supporting membrane fusion between liposomes and EVs. The negative control (DiO‐labeled liposomes incubated with free DiI in solution without EVs) showed negligible FRET signal, indicating that no detectable leakage of DiO from the liposomes occurred during sonication.

Sonication further yielded EV concentration‐dependent signal increases from Lipo‐21 that were comparable to, or slightly higher than, those obtained by incubation (Figure 2i). Notably, background signals were lower under sonication conditions than incubation (352 vs 448 a.u.), collectively resulting in an improved signal‐to‐noise ratio (Lipo‐21/Lipo‐R = 8.0 at 1010 particles/mL). The reduced background likely arises from the use of an ice bath during sonication, which minimizes heat‐induced biomolecular denaturation, together with the shortened assay duration. Statistical analysis revealed that signal differences between Lipo‐21 and Lipo‐R were not significant at 100 particles/mL (noting that this nominal concentration does not literally correspond to a single vesicle per mL due to inherent variability in NTA‐based quantification), but became significant at 102 particles/mL (** p < 0.01) and highly significant at higher EV concentrations (**** p < 0.0001). Unlike conventional fluorescence assays, where the signal directly correlates with target molecule copy number, the fluorescence signal in this system is influenced not only by miRNA copy number but also by fusion efficiency and subsequent MB delivery. Therefore, while the signal exhibits a clear dependence on EV concentration, it cannot be directly translated into absolute miRNA copy number. Importantly, we confirmed that the detectable EV concentration range of this assay is comparable to that of RT‐qPCR analysis (Figure S3).

Particle size analysis by NTA revealed a slight increase in average particle size after sonication, while the overall size distribution profile remained largely unchanged, indicating that vesicles were not disrupted during the process (Figure 2j). In addition, TEM images showed aggregated structures, likely representing vesicles undergoing fusion. Although fusion between EVs may also occur, the changes in size before and after sonication were less pronounced than those observed in liposome–EV fusion (Figure S4).

Membrane Integrity Preservation After SAFE

To verify that EV membranes remained intact after the SAFE procedure, biotin‐labeled EVs were subjected to analysis, after which the resulting fusion products were captured on bead surfaces and stained with phycoerythrin (PE) via streptavidin–biotin recognition (Figure 3a). In SAFE using Lipo‐21, red PE fluorescence from membrane‐associated biotin co‐localized with green fluorescence from MB–miRNA hybrids, indicating that EV membranes were preserved and that MB–target complexes remained confined within vesicles without leakage (Figure 3b). Line intensity profiling across a representative single bead further revealed a closely aligned spatial co‐distribution of red and green fluorescence along the cross‐sectional axis (Figure 3c). In the Lipo‐R negative control, only red fluorescence was detected, consistent with intact EV membranes in the absence of vesicle lysis during SAFE, while the lack of green fluorescence confirmed the absence of target miRNA hybridization with random‐sequence MBs (Figure 3e,f). Quantitative pixel‐by‐pixel colocalization analysis of enlarged images further supported these observations: the thresholded Pearson correlation coefficient was substantially higher for Lipo‐21 (r = 0.39) than for Lipo‐R (r = 0.17), and similarly elevated thresholded Manders’ overlap coefficients were observed for Lipo‐21 (M1/M2 = 0.56/0.62) compared with Lipo‐R (M1/M2 = 0.36/0.26) (Figure 3d,g).

In a complementary approach, SAFE was applied to EVs pre‐immobilized on beads via antigen–antibody interactions (Figure 3h). After washing, green fluorescence remained concentrated on the bead surfaces, further supporting that MB–target miRNA hybrids were retained within vesicles. This result also indicates that antigen–antibody interactions between EV membrane proteins and bead‐bound antibodies remained stable during the SAFE process.

To evaluate the applicability of SAFE in complex biological matrices, we compared signals obtained from EVs spiked into 50% human plasma with those from purified EVs suspended in aqueous buffer (Figure 3i). Both the specific signals from Lipo‐21 and the non‐specific background from Lipo‐R were comparable between the two conditions, indicating that SAFE can be directly applied to plasma samples without the need for prior EV isolation or purification.

Identification of EV‐associated miRNA Biomarkers in a Cellular Model of Cardiac Damage

To identify EV‐associated miRNA biomarkers for cardiovascular disease (CVD) diagnosis using SAFE, we first selected five representative miRNAs previously reported as potential candidates for CVD liquid biopsy. Calcium ionophores are known to stimulate EV release by increasing intracellular Ca2 + levels, thereby promoting membrane remodeling and vesicle shedding, and can also induce cellular stress responses depending on experimental conditions [35, 36]. Consistent with this, a previous study using A23187‐treated H9C2 cells demonstrated that Ca2 +‐mediated stress is associated with increased miR‐133a levels in EV‐enriched fractions, with levels correlating with the degree of cell viability loss [37]. Based on this rationale, an in vitro model of cell damage was established by treating cells with the calcium ionophore A23187, after which the expression levels of the selected miRNAs were quantified within EVs secreted by the damaged cells using RT‐qPCR, as described previously [37, 38] (Figure 4a). Two cell lines—H9C2 rat cardiomyocytes and MCF7 human breast cancer cells—were employed to determine whether these miRNAs were selectively enriched in EVs from damaged cardiomyocytes, thereby defining a cardiac injury‐specific biomarker panel for SAFE.

As shown in Figure 4b,c, cell viability decreased in both cell lines in a concentration‐dependent manner following A23187 treatment, dropping below 50% at 10 µm A23187, confirming that the treatment effectively induced cellular damage in both cases. However, the analysis of EV‐associated miRNA levels revealed distinct patterns between the two cell types (Figure 4d), allowing the five candidates to be categorized into three groups: 1) miRNAs enriched exclusively in EVs from A23187‐treated H9C2 cells (miR‐208a and miR‐133a), 2) miRNAs unchanged in both cell type after treatment (miR‐1 and miR‐499), and 3) miRNAs elevated in EVs from both cell types (miR‐21).

Consistent with a previous study using the same in vitro model [37], the increased levels of miR‐133a and miR‐208a observed in the EV‐enriched fraction may, at least in part, reflect stress‐ or injury‐associated release, particularly given the reduction in cell viability following A23187 treatment. In contrast, the lack of responsiveness of miR‐1 and miR‐499 was unexpected, as these have previously been reported as biomarkers for acute myocardial infarction (AMI) in both clinical samples and animal models [39, 40]. One possible explanation is the selective incorporation of miRNAs into EVs, a process that has been reported to depend on cellular context and stress conditions [41, 42, 43]. However, the extent to which such mechanisms contribute under A23187‐induced calcium overload remains unclear. Alternatively, these findings may reflect differences in intracellular abundance, release pathways, or stimulus‐specific regulation of individual miRNAs within this in vitro model. In particular, A23187‐induced calcium dysregulation may preferentially trigger release mechanisms distinct from those activated during ischemic injury in vivo. Moreover, certain miRNAs have been reported to exist in non‐vesicular forms, such as protein‐bound complexes, which may further limit their detection in EV‐enriched fractions [44, 45, 46]. In addition, the temporal dynamics of miRNA expression and release are likely to differ substantially between simplified in vitro systems and the complex, time‐dependent milieu of biofluids in patients and animal models. Such biological and temporal variability complicates the identification of specific and consistent biomarkers, underscoring the need for extended screening studies in parallel with analytical method development.

As this study primarily focuses on the development of a simple and robust detection platform, the proposed method may also facilitate future biomarker screening and validation efforts when integrated with additional candidate markers. At the current stage, miR‐208a and miR‐133a were selected as a refined panel serving as a proof‐of‐concept model to validate the feasibility of the SAFE‐based detection platform for cardiomyocyte injury.

To enable SAFE detection, liposomes encapsulating MBs specific to each biomarker were synthesized. The target specificity of each MB was first validated using synthetic miRNA mimics (Figure 4e, right). Subsequently, the analytical performance of MB‐loaded liposomes was evaluated by comparing SAFE measurements with RT‐qPCR results using EVs derived from A23187‐treated cells (Figure 4e, left). The SAFE results were consistent with RT‐qPCR data, showing concentration‐dependent increases in miR‐208a and miR‐133a levels in EVs from H9C2 cells, whereas no significant changes were detected in EVs from MCF7 cells (Figure 4f). These results confirmed that SAFE accurately reproduces qPCR‐based measurements in a cellular model of cardiac injury, supporting its applicability for clinical validation.

Clinical Validation of SAFE in Cardiovascular Disease Diagnosis

The established SAFE platform incorporating two EV‐associated miRNA biomarkers was validated using clinical plasma samples from patients with CVD and healthy controls (a summary of clinical information is provided in Table S1). SAFE offers a simple, mix‐to‐read blood testing workflow in which untreated plasma is mixed 1:1 with MB‐loaded liposomes, followed by a sonication step (Figure 5a). Beyond primary diagnosis, this streamlined approach holds potential for longitudinal monitoring of biomarker dynamics to guide therapeutic decisions, predict prognosis, and support recurrence surveillance. Notably, the temporal kinetics of EV‐associated miRNAs may differ substantially from conventional protein biomarkers such as cardiac troponins, which primarily reflect irreversible cardiomyocyte injury. This difference suggests that SAFE may provide complementary information across distinct phases of disease progression.

Before evaluating EV‐associated miRNAs, we first assessed the size distribution and concentration of EVs in plasma from CVD patients and healthy controls. Although these parameters showed no significant differences between the two groups, substantial inter‐patient variability was observed within each group (Figure 5b,c). The expression levels of the two selected EV‐associated miRNAs were then quantified using SAFE with Lipo‐133a and Lipo‐208a, while Lipo‐R served as a negative control to evaluate background signals (Figure 5d and Figure S5). Even when SAFE was performed under high plasma conditions (final 50% plasma), the background fluorescence measured with Lipo‐R remained negligible across all samples, supporting the direct applicability of SAFE to blood plasma, consistent with the findings in Figure 3i. The use of a high plasma concentration during the assay ensured the presence of abundant target biomarkers, thereby improving detection probability and analytical sensitivity. Notably, the measured signals were normalized to the total input plasma volume rather than to miRNA expression on a per‐EV basis, as the assay directly quantifies EV‐associated miRNAs without prior EV isolation or quantification. Consequently, the signals reflect a composite of both patient‐dependent EV abundance and miRNA content per EV. We consider this integrated readout to be clinically meaningful and diagnostically relevant; however, future incorporation of orthogonal EV quantification methods or internal standards may also improve analytical standardization and biological interpretability. Reproducibility tests using independent SAFE measurements on the same patient samples demonstrated excellent analytical reliability (Figure S6).

Both Lipo‐133a and Lipo‐208a showed consistently elevated signals in CVD patients, while remaining minimal in healthy controls. Statistical analysis confirmed that levels of both miRNAs were significantly higher in CVD patients than in controls (**** p < 0.0001). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis yielded area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.95 for both miRNAs (Figure 5e,f). Based on optimal thresholds determined by the Youden index, SAFE achieved a sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 86.7% for miR‐133a (overall accuracy = 91.4%), and a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 80% for miR‐208a (overall accuracy = 91.4%) (Figure 5g and Table S2). Co‐expression analysis within the patient cohort revealed a moderate correlation, with a thresholded Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.42, suggesting partial co‐occurrence that may reflect shared pathological processes, such as cardiomyocyte injury, rather than identical release pathways (Figure 5h). Overall, these diagnostic performances are in strong agreement with previous clinical studies [47, 48, 49, 50, 51].

These findings validate the capability of SAFE to detect EV‐associated miRNAs directly from unprocessed plasma with high accuracy and reproducibility. The strong performance across patient samples confirms the robustness of SAFE in complex plasma environments. Finally, our comparative analysis positions SAFE as a rapid and low‐complexity alternative to existing EV‐based diagnostic strategies (Table S3).

Conclusion

The goal of this study was to establish a foundation for direct‐from‐plasma analysis of EV‐associated miRNAs. We successfully validated the SAFE platform in a pilot clinical cohort, demonstrating its ability to deliver quantitative readouts within 10 min. Although upstream blood collection and plasma preparation remain necessary, this rapid turnaround suggests that SAFE could serve as a practical complement to existing diagnostic workflows, supporting early risk stratification and timely clinical decision‐making. In resource‐limited or point‐of‐care settings, where access to centralized laboratories or high‐sensitivity assays is limited, this rapid and minimally processed workflow offers particular value for accelerating triage and optimizing clinical resource allocation.

Despite its promise, the present study has limitations that temper generalizability and motivate further clinical investigation. The validation was conducted in a relatively small cohort of 20 patients and 15 controls; expanding the sample size will be essential to confirm and refine diagnostic performance. Moreover, while the two EV‐associated miRNAs identified from our in vitro model achieved an overall diagnostic accuracy of 91.4% for distinguishing CVD patients from healthy controls, inclusion of additional or disease‐specific biomarkers may further enhance precision. In addition, as this pilot study was conducted in clinically stable individuals, the findings may not fully reflect biomarker dynamics during the hyperacute phase of disease onset. Future studies incorporating time‐resolved sampling, particularly within hours of symptom onset, will be essential to define the temporal diagnostic window and clinical utility of EV‐associated miRNAs in CVD.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that SAFE is ready for large‐scale clinical evaluation aimed at refining diagnostic thresholds and identifying clinically actionable biomarkers. Expanding to a broader miRNA panel and integrating machine learning–based multiplex analysis could further improve robustness and discriminatory power across heterogeneous patient populations. Ultimately, such advances may pave the way for incorporating SAFE into standardized diagnostic workflows as a rapid, point‐of‐care molecular testing platform for cardiovascular disease.

Experimental Section

Liposome Synthesis

Liposomes encapsulating MBs were prepared using a modified thin‐film hydration method followed by extrusion, adapted from a previously reported protocol [25]. 1,2‐dioleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (DOPC), 1,2‐dioleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), and cholesterol (Chol) were dissolved in anhydrous ethanol at a mass ratio of 2:1:1 to achieve a total lipid concentration of 4 mg/mL. MBs (100 µm stock in nuclease‐free water) were added to the lipid solution to a final concentration of 100 nm. The sequences of the MBs used in this study are provided in Table S4. The solvent was then evaporated under reduced pressure at 40°C using a rotary evaporator, forming a thin lipid film, which was subsequently dried under high vacuum overnight to ensure complete removal of residual solvent. Next, the dried lipid film was rehydrated in either buffer (10 mm Tris‐HCl, 150 mm NaCl, 1 mm EDTA, pH 8.0) or 1x PBS to reach a final lipid concentration of 4 mg/mL. The resulting suspension was sonicated (POWERSONIC 505, 40 kHz) for 10–20 min, and sequentially extruded through polycarbonate membranes with decreasing pore sizes (800, 400, 200, and 100 nm; 21 passes each) using a mini‐extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids, USA). The obtained liposome suspension was collected and stored at 4°C until further use.

Liposome and EV Characterization

The size distribution and concentration of synthesized liposomes and cell‐derived EVs were characterized using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) with a NanoSight LM10 system (Malvern Panalytical) equipped with an sCMOS camera and a 642 nm laser module. Samples were diluted in 1× PBS (pH 7.4) to achieve an optimal particle concentration of approximately 20 particles per frame. For each sample, five 60 s videos were recorded and analyzed using NanoSight NTA software (version 3.4). To verify measurement consistency, the same samples and dilutions were further analyzed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Malvern Panalytical). For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), liposomes and EVs were placed onto formvar carbon‐coated copper grids and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde. For contrast enhancement, the grids were negatively stained with Uranyless EM stain (Delta Microscopies) for 10 min. Samples were imaged using a Tecnai G2 F20 TWIN TMP electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV.

FRET Experiment

Stock solutions of DiO and DiI were prepared at 1 mg/mL in DMSO and subsequently diluted in PBS to obtain working solutions. Liposomes and EVs were labeled with 20 µm DiO and 20 µm DiI, respectively, by incubation at 37°C for 2 h. Following labeling, excess unbound dye was removed using Vivaspin centrifugal filters (MWCO 10 kDa), with three washes in 1× PBS. The purified DiO‐labeled liposomes and DiI‐labeled EVs were then adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 1010 particles/mL prior to the assay.

For FRET analysis, DiO‐labeled liposomes and DiI‐labeled EVs were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and subjected to sonication. Fluorescence measurements were performed using an excitation wavelength of 475 nm, and emission was recorded at 510 nm and 575 nm.

SAFE Process

Synthesized liposome stocks were diluted 1:10 in buffer (10 mm Tris‐HCl, 150 mm NaCl, 1 mm EDTA, pH 8.0) to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 1010 particles/mL. Separately, cell‐derived EVs were prepared at various concentrations by dilution (greater than 1:10) in the same buffer to obtain an equivalent final particle concentration. The two solutions were then mixed at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio to yield a final volume of 1 mL. The mixture was placed in an ice bath and subjected to sonication (POWERSONIC 505, 40 kHz) for 10 min. In a comparison experiment, the mixture was alternatively incubated at 37°C for 1 h instead of sonication. Fluorescence signals were measured at 488 nm (excitation) and 518 nm (emission) using a multi‐mode microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek).

Alternatively, both liposome and cell‐derived EV stocks were diluted with human plasma (Dukpa Science) and buffer at a 1:5:4 ratio to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 1010 particles/mL each. The resulting solutions were then mixed at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio and sonicated for 10 min.

Clinical Sample Analysis

A total of 100 µL of solution was prepared by mixing 10 µL of synthesized liposomes (1 × 1011 particles/mL), 50 µL of undiluted plasma, and 40 µL of buffer (10 mm Tris‐HCl, 150 mm NaCl, 1 mm EDTA, pH 8.0), followed by sonication for 10 min in an ice bath.

Cell Culture

MCF‐7 cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium (Hyclone), and H9C2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Hyclone), each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Welgene). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, and the culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days to maintain cell viability. Cells were sub‐cultured at 80%–90% confluency using 0.05% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco).

EV Isolation

Cells were incubated for 48 h in fresh medium—either RPMI‐1640 or DMEM—containing 5% exosome‐depleted fetal bovine serum (System Biosciences, SBI) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution (Welgene). Conditioned media were collected and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 30 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 µm membrane and mixed with Total Exosome Isolation Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 2:1 volume ratio, followed by incubation overnight at 2°C–8°C. The mixture was then centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 60 min at 4°C, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of 1× PBS. Samples were stored at −80°C until use.

In Vitro Cardiac Damage Model

Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 48 h. The cells were then treated with various concentrations of A23187 (1–10 µm) dissolved in DMEM (Hyclone) for 1 h [37, 38]. After treatment, the cells were washed with DPBS and incubated with MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL in DPBS; Sigma–Aldrich) for 4 h at 37°C. Untreated cells (vehicle control) and wells without cells (blank control) were included as negative and background controls, respectively. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a multi‐mode microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek Instruments). Relative cell viability was calculated as the percentage of absorbance compared to untreated control cells.

For the Live/Dead assay, cells were seeded in 6‐well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24–48 h prior to the assay. After treatment with 3 µm A23187 for 1 h, cell viability was evaluated using the LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the cells were washed twice with DPBS and incubated with a staining solution containing 2 µm calcein‐AM and 4 µm ethidium homodimer‐1 (EthD‐1) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, the cells were gently washed with DPBS, and fluorescence images were captured using an Olympus CKX53 microscope. Live cells exhibiting green fluorescence (calcein‐AM) and dead cells showing red fluorescence (EthD‐1) were quantified using ImageJ software.

To collect cell‐derived EVs, cells were cultured in 60 mm dishes at a density of 1 × 106 cells per plate and treated with A23187 for 1 h. Following treatment, the medium was replaced with exosome‐depleted conditioned medium, and the cells were further incubated for 48 h.

EV‐associated miRNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR Analysis

EV‐associated miRNAs were extracted using the Total Exosome RNA and Protein Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and purity of the eluted RNA were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and total small RNAs were stored at −80°C until use. For cDNA synthesis, total RNA containing small RNAs was reverse‐transcribed using the TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with miRNA‐specific stem‐loop primers provided in the corresponding TaqMan MicroRNA Assay kits for each target. Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed using the TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the FAM‐labeled probes included in the TaqMan MicroRNA Assays, on a Bio‐Rad real‐time PCR detection system. Expression levels were normalized to U6 small nuclear RNA, detected using the TaqMan MicroRNA Control Assay (Assay ID: 001973). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial enzyme activation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 60 s. All reactions were performed in triplicate.

Capture of SAFE Products on Beads

Isolated MCF‐7‐derived EVs were biotinylated for 30 min in 100 µm sulfo‐NHS‐LC‐biotin (EZ‐Link, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 1× PBS. The biotinylated EVs were then subjected to SAFE process with either Lipo‐21 or Lipo‐R. The resulting SAFE products were captured on 4 µm aldehyde/sulfate latex microbeads (Molecular Probes, 4% w/v), and unbound materials were removed by washing with 1× PBS containing 0.5% BSA. The EV‐captured beads were subsequently incubated with 4 µg/mL streptavidin‐PE (BioLegend) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After final washes, the beads were imaged using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope.

SAFE on Bead‐Captured EVs

Exosome‐depleted conditioned medium collected after 48 h of cell incubation was mixed with the Exosome‐Human CD9 Flow Detection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1 × 107 beads/mL) at a 1:2 (v/v) ratio and incubated overnight at 4°C, according to the manufacturer's protocol. The bead–EV complexes were then washed with 1x PBS containing 0.5% BSA and resuspended in 300 µL of 1x PBS. The EV‐captured beads were subsequently diluted 1:100 and subjected to the SAFE process using either Lipo‐21 or Lipo‐R.

Clinical Sample Collection

Blood plasma samples were collected and processed with approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Asan Medical Center (Seoul, Korea; protocol No. 2021‐1770). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. A total of 15 healthy individuals and 20 patients were enrolled in this study. Patients aged 18–80 years who had been diagnosed with cardiovascular disease within the preceding 30 days at Asan Medical Center and were undergoing treatment were prospectively enrolled. Blood samples were collected during outpatient visits in a clinically stable condition. A summary of the clinical information is provided in Table S1.

Blood samples were drawn in the clinic using BD Vacutainer tube coated with K2EDTA (10.8 mg), and the volume and time of collection were recorded prior to refrigerated storage at 4°C. Within 5 h of blood collection, samples were processed using a standardized protocol: (a) each blood sample was centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 25°C; (b) the resulting plasma was carefully aliquoted into 1.5 mL tubes; (c) to remove residual cells and debris, plasma aliquots were subject to an additional centrifugation step at 1500 × g for 10 min at 25°C. Processed plasma was then stored at −80°C until downstream analysis.

All samples were subsequently re‐labeled to remove personally identifiable information and shipped to DGIST under a material transfer agreement (MTA), using dry ice to prevent thawing. Experiments using de‐identified plasma samples were conducted exclusively at DGIST, where they were exempt from further IRB review (DGISTIRB‐202403‐002; principal investigator: S.H.).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (s.d.) from at least three independent experiments, and exact sample sizes greater than three are specified in the figure legends. Statistical significance was evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test or two‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's or Šidák's post hoc correction for multiple comparisons, as appropriate. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (ns, not significant; * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001).
