Nonarchimedean groups and the axiom of choice 2020 AMS subject classification 03E25, 22F05.
Justin Young, Jindřich Zapletal

Abstract
We prove several novel connections between properties of non-archimedean groups and fragments of the axiom of choice which hold in their associated permutation model.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we prove several theorems connecting properties of non-archimedean Polish groups with fragments of Axiom of Choice which hold in their associated permutation models. Let
X
be a countable set with some relations and operations on it, and let
\mathrm{Aut}(X)\curvearrowright X
be the group of all automorphisms of
X
viewed as a closed subgroup of the full symmetric group acting on the set
X
by application. Consider the ideal

of finite subsets of

is invariant under the action and
\mathrm{Aut}(X)\curvearrowright X,I
is a dynamical ideal in the sense of
[
13
]
. It naturally generates a Fraenkel–Mostowski style permutation model
W[[X]]
of ZFA, the choiceless set theory with atoms, in which
X
is identified with its set of atoms.

Section
2

.

A non-archimedean Polish group

(ZF) The
Axiom of Well-ordered Choice

[

is pliable;

2.

in

In the context of permutation models obtained from the ideal of finite sets, the axiom of well-ordered choice is in fact equivalent to the axiom of countable choice

An extreme case of the theorem occurs when the acting group is abelian. In such a case, in the permutation model every set is a union of well-orderable family of finite sets

Pliable groups

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X
be a countable structure. The following are equivalent:

1.

the group
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
is pliable;

2.

in
W[[X]]
, the Axiom of Well-ordered Choice holds.

Proof.

This is just a diagram-chasing argument using some foundational results of
[
13
]
. Let
X
be a countable structure and
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
act on
X
by application. For each finite set
a\subset X
let
\bar{a}\subset X
be the dynamical closure of
a
, the set
\{x\in X\colon\forall\gamma\in\mathrm{pstab}(a)\ \gamma\cdot x=x\}
. Note that
\mathrm{pstab}(a)=\mathrm{pstab}(\bar{a})
holds. Write
I
for the ideal of finite sets on
X
, and
J
for the ideal generated by sets
\bar{a}
for
a\in I
. Then
J
is the dynamical closure of
I
, and the permutation models derived from
\mathrm{Aut}(X)\curvearrowright X,I
and
\mathrm{Aut}(X)\curvearrowright X,J
coincide
[
13
, Proposition 2.13]
. By
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
, the axiom of well-ordered choice is equivalent to cofinal orbits for
J
, which translates to the statement that for every
a\in I
there is
b\in I
containing
a
such that for every
c\in I
containing
a
, there is
\gamma\in\mathrm{pstab}(a)
such that
c\subset\overline{\gamma b}
.

It will be enough to show that the latter statement is equivalent to the pliability of the group
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
. Suppose first that the group is pliable and
a\subset X
is a finite set. Then
\mathrm{pstab}(a)
is an open subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
; by the pliability assumption, it has an open subgroup
\Delta
such that conjugates of
\Delta
within
\mathrm{pstab}(a)
form a neighborhood basis for
1
. Let
b\supset a
be a finite set such that
\mathrm{pstab}(b)\subset\Delta
; we claim that
b
works as in the restatement of cofinal orbits in the previous paragraph. Indeed, if
c\supset a
is a finite set, then by the choice of
\Delta
there is an element
\gamma\in\mathrm{pstab}(a)
such that
\gamma\Delta\gamma^{-1}\subseteq\mathrm{pstab}(c)
. This means that
\gamma\mathrm{pstab}(b)\gamma^{-1}\subset\mathrm{pstab}(c)
, which turns into
\mathrm{pstab}(\gamma\cdot b)\subset\mathrm{pstab}(c)
and
c\subset\overline{\gamma b}
as desired.

Now suppose that the ideal
J
has cofinal orbits. To argue for pliability of
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
, let
\Delta
be an open subgroup. Let
a\subset X
be a finite set such that
\mathrm{pstab}(a)\subset\Delta
. Let
b
be a finite set witnessing cofinal orbits; we claim that within
\mathrm{pstab}(a)
, the conjugates of
\mathrm{pstab}(b)
form a basis around the unit. To see this, let
c\subset X
be a finite set containing
a
. By the cofinal orbits assumption, there is
\gamma\in\mathrm{pstab}(a)
such that
c\subseteq\overline{\gamma b}
. Unraveling the action, the latter inclusion turns into
\gamma\mathrm{pstab}(b)\gamma^{-1}\subset\mathrm{pstab}(c)
as desired.

As a final contribution to this section, we produce an example of non-discrete pliable group. It is constructed as a closed subgroup of the automorphism group of rationals with their usual ordering. The main tool is the following finite approximation device.

Definition 2.2
.

Define the poset
P
as follows: a condition
p\in P
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{Q}^{3}
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

if
\langle a,s,t\rangle\in p
, then either
a<s<t
or
s=t\leq a
, but not both;

2.

p
is order-preserving. That is, if
\langle a,s_{0},t_{0}\rangle
and
\langle a,s_{1},t_{1}\rangle
are both elements of
p
and
s_{0}<s_{1}
, then
t_{0}<t_{1}
;

3.

there are no disoriented cycles in
p
without immediate cancellation. Here, a
disoriented walk
in
p
is a sequence
\langle a_{i}\colon i\in n,s_{i}\colon i\in n+1\rangle
such that for each
i\in n
, either
\langle a_{i},s_{i},s_{i+1}\rangle\in p
or
\langle a_{i},s_{i+1},s_{i}\rangle\in p
. Such a walk is a
cycle
if
s_{0}=s_{n}
. An immediate cancellation in such a walk is an
i\in n
such that either
s_{i}\leq a_{i}
or
a_{i}=a_{i+1}
and
s_{i}=s_{i+2}
.

P
is ordered by reverse extension.

Note that the last item says that a disoriented cycle trivializes after removing all immediate cancellations from it.

Proposition 2.3
.

The following sets are dense in
P
:

1.

D^{0}_{a,s}=\{p\in P\colon\exists t\ \langle a,s,t\rangle\in p\}
for every
a,s\in\mathbb{Q}
;

2.

D^{1}_{a,t}=\{p\in P\colon\exists s\ \langle a,s,t\rangle\in p\}
for every
a,t\in\mathbb{Q}
;

3.

D^{2}_{a,s,t}=\{p\in P\colon\exists n\ \exists\langle s_{i}\colon i\in n+1\rangle\ s_{0}=s
and
\forall i\in n\ \langle a,s_{i},s_{i+1}\rangle\in p
and
s_{i+1}>t\}
for all
a<s<t
in
\mathbb{Q}
;

4.

D^{3}_{J,s,I}=\{p\in P\colon\exists a\in J\ \exists t\in I\ \langle a,s,t\rangle\in p\}
for any
s\in\mathbb{Q}
and nonempty rational intervals
I,J\subset\mathbb{Q}
such that
J<s<I
.

Proof.

For item (1), given
p\in P
and
a,s\in\mathbb{Q}
, there are three cases. If
s\leq a
then add
\langle a,s,s\rangle
into
p
. If
s>a
and there is

such that
\langle a,s,t\rangle\in p
, then leave

unchanged. In the remaining case, consider the sets
\{a\}\cup\{t^{\prime}\colon\exists s^{\prime}<s\ \langle a,s^{\prime},t^{\prime}\rangle\in p\}
and
\{t^{\prime}\colon\exists s^{\prime}>s\ \langle a,s^{\prime},t^{\prime}\rangle\in p\}

between these two sets which is not mentioned in

, and add
\langle a,s,t\rangle
into

Items (2, 3, 4) are similar and left to the reader.

Now, let

For every

Proposition 2.5

The group

is free over its generators. For every

Proof.

For the freeness, suppose that
n\in\omega
is a number, for each

a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
b_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
, and
\prod_{i}\gamma_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}=1

r_{j+1}>\max_{i}\gamma_{a_{i}}(r_{j})

; note that

holds. Now let

be a condition for which every

\langle a_{i},s_{i},s_{i+1}\rangle

\langle a,s_{i+1},s_{i}\rangle

holds for each

.

Let
p\in G
be a condition such that for each

it contains either
\langle a_{i},s_{i},s_{i+1}\rangle
or
\langle a_{i},s_{i+1},s_{i}\rangle
, and similarly for the

is a disoriented cycle in

; we must show that

is a disoriented cycle in

Corollary 2.6
.

An element

Proof.

The right-to-left implication follows straight from the definitions. For the left-to-right implication, given
\delta
in the closure and

\gamma(q)=\delta(q)

is in the closure of
\Gamma

U\cap\Gamma

for every

Theorem 2.7

The group

Proof.

To see that the group is pliable, suppose that

First of all, every element of
\Gamma

Now, let

\delta\in\Gamma
fixes an initial segment of rationals, and this feature persists to
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
by Corollary
2.6
. Thus, there must be a rational
s<q
such that all elements of
F
fix all rationals smaller than
s
. Then, all elements of
UFU
fix all rationals smaller than
s
, and
\mathrm{Aut}(X)\neq UFU
.
∎

3
Cli groups

Theorem
1.6
relies on a close correspondence between a certain set-theoretic rank and a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
-rank on cli groups recently isolated by Allison and Panagiotopoulos
[
1
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A set
A
has
wo-rank

0
,
\mathrm{wor}(A)=0
, if it is well-orderable. A set
A
has
wo-rank

\leq\alpha
,
\mathrm{wor}(A)\leq\alpha
, if it is a union of a well-ordered collection of sets of rank
<\alpha
. If no ordinal bound can be found, write
\mathrm{wor}(A)=\infty
.

It can be proved by transfinite induction on
\alpha
that
A\subseteq B
implies
\mathrm{wor}(A)\leq\mathrm{wor}(B)
. In consequence, the well-orderable unions in the previous definition can be taken as disjoint well-orderable unions.

Definition 3.2
.

[
1
]

Let
\Gamma
be a Polish non-archimedean group. A pair
(V,U)
of open neigborhoods of the identity in
\Gamma
has
cli-rank

0
,
\mathrm{clir}(V,U)=0
, if
U\subseteq V
, and cli-rank
\leq\alpha
,
\mathrm{clir}(V,U)\leq\alpha
, if there is an open neighborhood
W\subseteq U
of the identity such that for every
\gamma\in U
,
(V,\gamma W\gamma^{-1})
has rank
<\alpha
. If no ordinal bound can be found, write
\mathrm{clir}(U,V)=\infty
.

An immediate transfinite induction argument shows that
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
and
U^{\prime}\subseteq U
together imply
\mathrm{clir}(V^{\prime},U)\geq\mathrm{clir}(V,U^{\prime})
. If
U
happens to be a subgroup of
\Gamma
and
V\subseteq U
, then the evaluation of
\mathrm{clir}(V,U)
does not really depend on
\Gamma
, but only on
U
. We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.6
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X
be a countable structure. The following are equivalent:

1.

the group
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
is cli;

2.

in
W[[X]]
, every set appears at some stage of the well-orderability hierarchy.

Proof.

Let
X
be a countable structure, let
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
be the group of its automorphisms acting on
X
by application, let
I
be the ideal of finite subsets of
X
. Let
W[[X]]
be the permutation model associated with the dynamical ideal
\mathrm{Aut}(X)\curvearrowright X,I
. The parallel between the two ranks is elucidated by the following two propositions.

Claim 3.4
.

Let
B\in W[[X]]
be a set, let
U\subset\mathrm{Aut}(X)
be an open subgroup. If
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{stab}(B),U)\leq\alpha
then
\mathrm{wor}(U\cdot B)\leq\alpha
.

Proof.

By transfinite induction on
\alpha
, for all
B,U
simultaneously. If
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{stab}(B),U)=0
then
U\subseteq\mathrm{stab}(B)
, so
U\cdot B=\{B\}
,
U\cdot B
is therefore well-orderable, and it has wo-rank
0
. Suppose that
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{stab}(B),U)\leq\alpha
and find a subgroup
W\subseteq U
such that for all
\gamma\in U
,
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{stab}(B),\gamma W\gamma^{-1})<\alpha
. Divide
U\cdot B
into
W
-orbits. The set of all
W
-orbits which are subsets of
U\cdot B
is in the model
W[[X]]
and well-orderable there, as each of these orbits is
W
-invariant. So, to show that
\mathrm{wor}(U\cdot B)\leq\alpha
, it is enough to show that each of these
W
-orbits has wo-rank
<\alpha
. Pick
\gamma\in U
. Then,
\mathrm{stab}(\gamma\cdot B)=\gamma\mathrm{stab}(B)\gamma^{-1}
, and
\mathrm{clir}(\gamma\mathrm{stab}(B)\gamma^{-1},W)=\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{stab}(B),\gamma^{-1}W\gamma)<\alpha
. By the induction hypothesis,
\mathrm{wor}(W\cdot\gamma\cdot B)<\alpha
and the proof is complete.
∎

Claim 3.5
.

Let
A\subset X
be a set of wo-rank
\leq\alpha
. There is an open subgroup
U\subset\mathrm{Aut}(X)
such that for every finite set
a\subset A
,
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{pstab}(a),U)\leq\alpha
.

Proof.

By transfinite induction on
\alpha
. If
\alpha=0
, then
A
is well-orderable, the group
U=\mathrm{pstab}(A)
is open, and it works: for each finite set
a\subset A
,
\mathrm{pstab}(A)\subseteq\mathrm{pstab}(a)
, so
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{pstab}(a),U)=0
. Now, suppose that
\mathrm{wor}(A)\leq\alpha
, and let
A=\bigcup B
, where
B
is a well-orderable set of sets of wo-rank
<\alpha
. We claim that
U=\mathrm{pstab}(B)
works. Let
a\subset A
be a finite set. Let
C\subset B
be a finite subset of
B
such that
a\subset\bigcup C
. By the definitions, there is an ordinal
\beta<\alpha
such that
\mathrm{wor}(\bigcup C)\leq\beta
. Let
W\subset U
be an open subgroup witnessing the induction hypothesis for
\bigcup C
; We claim that it witnesses the fact that
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{pstab}(a),U)\leq\alpha
. Let
\gamma\in U
be any element. Then
C
is fixed by
\gamma
, so
\gamma\cdot a\subset\bigcup C
. By the choice of
W
,
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{pstab}(\gamma\cdot a),W)\leq\beta
. However,
\mathrm{pstab}(\gamma\cdot a)=\gamma\mathrm{pstab}(a)\gamma^{-1}
, so
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{pstab}(\gamma\cdot a),W)=\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{pstab}(a),\gamma^{-1}W\gamma)<\alpha
as desired.
∎

The theorem now quickly follows. By
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
, a Polish group
\Gamma
is cli iff for every open subset
U\subset\Gamma
,
\mathrm{clir}(U,\Gamma)\neq\infty
; in fact, in such a case these ranks are all countable. So, suppose first that
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
is cli, and let
A\in W[[X]]
be any set; we must show that
\mathrm{wor}(A)\neq\infty
. By the definition of the model
W[[X]]
, there is a finite set
a\subset X
such that
\mathrm{pstab}(a)\subseteq\mathrm{stab}(A)
. By
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
and the monotonicity properties of cli-rank,
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{stab}(A),\mathrm{pstab}(a))\neq\infty
holds. By Claim
3.4
, the wo-rank of the set
\mathrm{pstab}(a)\cdot A=A
is not infinite.

Suppose on the other hand that for every set
A\in W[[X]]
,
\mathrm{wor}(A)\neq\infty
. In particular,
\mathrm{wor}(X)\neq\infty
, and by Claim
3.5
, there is an open subgroup
U\subset\mathrm{Aut}(X)
such that for every finite set
a\subset X
,
\mathrm{clir}(\mathrm{pstab}(a),U)\leq\alpha
. The monotonicity properties of cli-rank together with
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
show that the open subgroup
U
of
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
is cli. It is easy to argue abstractly that
\Gamma
is cli: if
d
is a complete left-invariant metric on
U
bounded by
1
, then it can be extended to a complete left-invariant metric on
\Gamma
by setting
d(\gamma,\delta)=d(1,\gamma^{-1}\delta)
if
\gamma^{-1}\delta\in U
and
d(\gamma,\delta)=2
otherwise. Thus,
\Gamma
is cli as needed. This completes the argument.
∎

4
Classification of open subgroups

In the main result of this section, we find a necessary and sufficient amalgamation criterion for when a limit of a relational Fraissé class with disjoint amalgamation has classification of open subgroups.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a relational Fraissé class.

1.

Structures
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
are in
amalgamation position
if
A\restriction(\mathrm{dom}(A)\cap\mathrm{dom}(B))=B\restriction(\mathrm{dom}(A)\cap\mathrm{dom}(B)
;

2.

for a pair
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
in amalgamation position, a
minimal amalgamation
of
A
and
B
is a structure
C
on
\mathrm{dom}(A)\cup\mathrm{dom}(B)
such that
C\restriction\mathrm{dom}(A)=A
and
C\restriction\mathrm{dom}(B)=B
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a relational Fraissé class. Let
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
be a pair in amalgamation position and
C,D
be minimal amalgamations of
A
and
B
.

1.

C,D
are
close
if there is a
focus of difference
between
C
and
D
–an element
b\in\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(A)
such that
C\restriction(\mathrm{dom}(A)\cup\mathrm{dom}(B))\setminus\{b\}
is equal to
D\restriction(\mathrm{dom}(A)\cup\mathrm{dom}(B))\setminus\{b\}
;

2.

a
walk
from
C
to
D
is a sequence
\langle C_{i}\colon i\in j+1\rangle
of minimal amalgamations of
A
and
B
such that
C_{0}=C
,
C_{j}=D
, and for each
i\in j
,
C_{i}
and
C_{i+1}
are close;

3.

\mathcal{F}
is a
bootstrapping class
if it has disjoint amalgamation and for any pair
A,B
in amalgamation position and any two minimal amalgamations
C,D
of
A
and
B
there is a walk from
C
to
D
.

Note that the concept of closeness as stated is not symmetric in that the focus of difference is always on the
B
-side. In the walks, the foci are going to change, but they always must remain on the same side. Most proofs showing that a certain relational Fraissé class is a bootstrapping class
are quite natural: among all minimal amalgamations of
A
and
B
, they identify
an optimal one and then show how an arbitrary minimal amalgamation can be
transformed to the optimal one one point at a time.

To kick off the list of examples, recall that a relational Fraissé class
\mathcal{F}
is
hereditary
if it is closed under subsets in the sense that if
A,B
are structures for its relational language with the same domain,
A\in\mathcal{F}
, and all relations in
B
are subsets of the corresponding relations in
A
, then
B\in\mathcal{F}
holds.

Example 4.3
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a relational, hereditary Fraissé class in finite language with disjoint amalgamation, then
\mathcal{F}
is a bootstrapping class.

Proof.

Let
A,B
be a pair of structures in
\mathcal{F}
in amalgamation position. Let
D
be the
minimal disjoint amalgamation of
A
and
B
obtained by taking the union of
the corresponding relations in
A
and
B
. This is indeed a structure in
\mathcal{F}
by the heredity assumption, since its relations are subsets of any minimal disjoint amalgamation of
A
and
B
.
Now, if
C
is any minimal disjoint amalgamation of
A
and
B
, it is possible to
produce a walk from
C
to
D
by setting
C_{0}=C
and then erasing tuples in relations in
C
which are not in
D
one by one; any element of
\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(A)
which is in the tuple being erased will be the focus of difference between two consecutive structures in the walk. It is easy to see that all structures obtained in this walk are in
\mathcal{F}
as
\mathcal{F}
is hereditary. By the finiteness of the language of
\mathcal{F}
, the walk will end in
D
after finitely many steps in the walk. The proof is complete.
∎

Example 4.4
.

The class of all graphs is bootstrapping.

Example 4.5
.

For any number
k>2
, the class of all graphs not containing a
clique of cardinality
k
is bootstrapping.

The non-hereditary bootstrapping classes are much more interesting.

Example 4.6
.

The class of linear orderings is bootstrapping.

Proof.

Let
A
and
B
be a pair of linear orderings in amalgamation position. There is an optimal minimal amalgamation
D
of
A
and
B
, in which if
a\in\mathrm{dom}(A)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(B)
and
b\in\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(A)
are consecutive points, then
b\leq a
holds. There
is only one amalgamation satisfying this property: inside every interval specified by two consecutive points of
A\cap B
, elements of
\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(A)
come before elements of
\mathrm{dom}(A)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(B)
. Now, given an arbitrary minimal disjoint
amalgamation
C
of
A
and
B
, it is possible to produce a walk from
C
to
D
by at each step flipping some pair of two consecutive elements
a\in\mathrm{dom}(A)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(B)
and
b\in\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(A)
for which
a\leq b
holds. Note that
b
will always be the focus of difference of two such amalgamations on the walk.

The walk has to end after finitely many steps, since for every element
a\in A
the set of all elements of
b\in B
which are above
a
is
non-increasing along the walk, and at each step of the walk one of these finite sets actually becomes smaller. The final element of the walk must be the amalgamation
D
.
∎

Example 4.7
.

The class of partial orderings is bootstrapping.

Proof.

Let
A
and
B
be a pair of partial orderings in amalgamation position. There is an optimal amalgamation
D
of
A
and
B
in which elements
a\in A
and
b\in B
are comparable only if there is an element of
a\cap b
between them. This is in fact the inclusion-smallest minimal amalgamation available.

Now, given an arbitrary minimal amalgamation
C
of
A
and
B
, construct a walk from
C
by demanding that at each stage of the walk exactly one pair
\langle a,b\rangle
or
\langle b,a\rangle
is removed from the amalgamation for
a\in A\setminus B
and
b\in B\setminus A
, if at all possible. Successive amalgamations on such a walk are clearly close, because the point
b
will be the focus of difference.

The walk must end after finitely many steps as in each step the cardinality of the amalgamating order relation is decreased. It is enough to show that the final station of the walk must be equal to
D
; this amounts to showing that if
E\neq D
is any amalgamation, then the walk can proceed one more step past
E
. Indeed, if
E\neq D
, then there must be elements
a\in A\setminus B
and
b\in B\setminus A
which are comparable in
E
and such that there is no element of
A\cap B
between them. Selecting such points
a,b
such that the set of elements between them is inclusion-minimal, it becomes clear that such points
a,b
have no element of
A\cup B
strictly between them, and then the pair
\langle a,b\rangle
or
\langle b,a\rangle
can be removed from the relation and the resulting relation will remain a partial ordering and an amalgamation of
A
and
B
.
∎

Example 4.8
.

The class of rational metric spaces is bootstrapping.

Proof.

Let
A
and
B
be a pair of rational metric spaces in amalgamation position, with
metrics
d_{A}
and
d_{B}
. There is an optimal minimal amalgamation
D
of
A
and
B
, in which if
a\in\mathrm{dom}(A)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(B)
and
b\in\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(A)
are points
then the distance of
a
and
b
is the minimum of the set
\{d_{A}(a,c)+d_{B}(c,b)\colon c\in\mathrm{dom}(A)\cap\mathrm{dom}(B)\}
. In view of the triangle inequality, this is the largest metric
available. Now, given an arbitrary minimal amalgamation
C
of
A
and
B
. Construct a walk from
C
such that at each step, exactly one distance between an element of
\mathrm{dom}(A)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(B)
and an element of
\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(A)
is increased as much as possible without violating the triangle inequality.

The walk has to end after finitely many steps. To see this, select a positive rational number
q
such that every distance used in
C
is an integer multiple of
q
. It is easy to prove by induction that all distances in the successive amalgamations on the walk will be integer multiples of
q
. Thus, the sum of all distances used in the amalgamation is increasing each time by at least
q
, and it cannot overtake the sum of all distances in
D
. It will be enough to show that the final amalgamation of the walk must be
D
.

To do this, it is enough to show that if
E
is an amalgamation in which all distances are integer multiples of
q
and at least one distance is smaller than that in
D
, then adding
q
to one of the distances will not violate the triangle inequality.

Find points
a\in\mathrm{dom}(A)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(B)
and
b\in\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(A)
such that
d_{E}(a,b)<d_{D}(a,b)
and
d_{E}(a,b)
is the smallest possible. It will be enough to show that for every
c\in\mathrm{dom}(A)\cup\mathrm{dom}(B)
,
d_{E}(a,b)+q\leq d_{E}(a,c)+d_{E}(c,b)
. Without loss assume that
c\in\mathrm{dom}(A)
. Since all distances in
E
are integer multiples of
q
, it will be enough to derive a contradiction from the assumption
that
d_{E}(a,b)=d_{E}(a,c)+d_{E}(c,b)
.

Now, if
c\in\mathrm{dom}(A)\cap\mathrm{dom}(B)
, this would mean that
d_{E}(a,b)\geq d_{D}(a,b)
, contrary to the choice of the points
a
and
b
. If, on the other hand,
c\in\mathrm{dom}(A)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(B)
holds, then by the minimal choice of the points
a
and
b
it has to be the case that
d_{E}(c,b)=d_{D}(c,b)
. Pick an element
e\in\mathrm{dom}(A)\cap\mathrm{dom}(B)
such that
d_{E}(c,b)=d_{A}(c,e)+d_{B}(e,b)
. By the triangle inequality in
A
,
d_{A}(a,e)\leq d_{A}(a,c)+d_{A}(c,e)
. The strict inequality is impossible here, since
it would violate the triangle inequality in
E
in the triangle with vertices
a,b,e
. However, the equality leads to the conclusion that
d_{E}(a,b)=d_{A}(a,e)+d_{B}(e,b)\geq d_{D}(a,b)
, violating the choice of the points
a,b
.
∎

Example 4.9
.

The class of selectors is bootstrapping.

This is the class of all finite structures
A
equipped with a function
f_{A}
which, to each nonempty subset of
\mathrm{dom}(A)
assigns one of its elements. It is not diffcult to restate this class as one with an infinite relational language.

Proof.

The argument is slightly different from the previous ones in that one does not need to find an optimal amalgamation to streamline the argument. Let

A
and
B
be a pair in amalgamation position and
C
and
D
two of its minimal disjoint
amalgamations. To get a walk from
C
to
D
, at each amalgamation in the walk switch the selector value on a single nonempty set

to

.

Observe that this set

must have nonempty intersection with
\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(A)
(otherwise its value would be dictated by

) and every point of this intersection will be a focus of difference between the two successive amalgamations on the walk. It is clear that the walk must terminate after finitely many steps in the amalgamation
D
.
∎

\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K}

\mathcal{F}*\mathcal{G}
for the

Example 4.10
.

If
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}

, then so is their superposition

Let
A

Example 4.11
.

Example 4.12
.

Let
\mathcal{F}

.

The main result of this section is the following characterization of classification of open subgroups, including Theorem
1.8
as its sub-statement. To set up the notation, let
\mathcal{F}
be a relational Fraissé class with disjoint amalgamation and
X
its limit. For each structure
B\in\mathcal{F}
let
X^{B}
be the set of all injective maps from
B
to
X
which preserve all relations in the language of
\mathcal{F}
. For all structures
A\subseteq B
consider the equivalence relation
E_{A}
connecting two elements of
X^{B}
if they agree on
A
, and a graph
G_{A}
connecting two elements of
X^{B}
if they agree on all elements of
B
except for one point, and this point does not belong to
A
. Note that the group
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
acts on
X^{B}
by composition, and this action preserves both
E_{A}
and
G_{A}
. Note also that
G_{A}\subset E_{A}
holds; the key issue is if
E_{A}
is the connectedness relation of
G_{A}
or not.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a relational Fraissé class with disjoint amalgamation and let
X
be its limit. The following are equivalent:

1.

\mathcal{F}
is bootstrapping;

2.

for all structures
A\subseteq B
in the class
\mathcal{F}
, the connectedness equivalence relation of
G_{A}
is equal to
E_{A}
;

3.

X
admits classification of open subgroups.

Proof.

To show that (1) implies (2), suppose that the class
\mathcal{F}
is bootstrapping. Suppose that
A\subseteq B
are structures in
\mathcal{F}
. First, we introduce some notation. Two
E_{A}
-related maps
\phi,\psi\in X^{B}
are
disjoint over
A
if
\mathrm{rng}(\phi)\cap\mathrm{rng}(\psi)=\mathrm{rng}(\phi\restriction A)
. For any two such maps the symbol
\phi\dot{\cup}\psi
describes a map from two copies

and
B_{1}
of
B
which intersect in
A
; the pullback from
X
by
\phi\dot{\cup}\psi
is then a minimal disjoint amalgamation of
B_{0},B_{1}
.

Claim 4.14
.

For any
\phi\in X^{B}
and any minimal disjoint amalgamation
D
of
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
there is a

-walk from
\phi
to some
\psi\in X^{B}
, disjoint over

with
\phi
, and such that the pullback from

by

is

.

Proof.

First use the universality properties of

to walk from
\phi
to some

which is disjoint over

from
\phi
. Consider the amalgamation

5

In this section, we prove several consequences of classification of open subgroups. We start with a brief argument for Theorem

Theorem 5.1
.

If
X
admits classification of open subgroups, then in
W[[X]]
,
\mathrm{HF}(X)
is a support set.

Proof.

Suppose that
X
is a countable structure which admits classification of open subgroups.

Claim 5.2
.

For every open subgroup
\Delta\subset\mathrm{Aut}(X)
there is a set
y\in\mathrm{HF}(X)
such that
\Delta=\mathrm{stab}(y)
.

Proof.

Use the classification assumption to find a finite set
a\subset X
such that
\mathrm{pstab}(a)\subseteq\Delta\subseteq\mathrm{stab}(a)
. Fix a linear ordering
L
of the set
a
and let
y=\{\delta\cdot L\colon\delta\in\Delta\}
. This set consists of linear orders on the set
a
, therefore it is a finite set. It will be enough to show that
\Delta=\mathrm{stab}(y)
.

The left-to-right inclusion is obvious from the definition of the set
y
. For the opposite inclusion, consider any element
\gamma\in\mathrm{stab}(y)
. Then there must be
\delta\in\Delta
such that
\gamma\cdot L=\delta\cdot L
; in other words,
L=\delta^{-1}\gamma\cdot L
. As finite linear orders have no nontrivial automorphisms, this means that
\delta^{-1}\gamma\in\mathrm{pstab}(a)\subseteq\Delta
holds. Then
\gamma=\delta(\delta^{-1}\gamma)\in\Delta
holds as desired.
∎

In particular,
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
has only countably many open subgroups. Let
\langle\Delta_{n}\colon n\in\omega\rangle
be a list of open subgroups of
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
which contains exactly one group from each conjugacy class. For each
n\in\omega
, use the classification assumption to find
y_{n}\in\mathrm{HF}(X)
such that
\Delta_{n}=\mathrm{stab}(y_{n})
. Replacing
y_{n}
with
\langle y_{n},n\rangle
if necessary, we may assume that the
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
orbits of
y_{n}
are pairwise disjoint.

Let
A\in W[[X]]
be any set and work to find an ordinal
\alpha
and an injection from
A
to
\mathrm{HF}(X)\times\alpha
. Replacing
A
with its superset
\{\gamma\cdot B\colon\gamma\in\mathrm{Aut}(X),B\in A\}
if necessary, assume that
\mathrm{stab}(A)=\mathrm{Aut}(X)
. Observe that for every
B\in A
,
\mathrm{stab}(B)
contains an open subgroup by the definition of

, so it is open itself. In addition, for every
\gamma\in\mathrm{Aut}(X)
,
\mathrm{stab}(\gamma\cdot B)=\gamma\cdot\mathrm{stab}(B)\gamma^{-1}
holds, and so in each
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
-orbit the stabilizers form exactly one conjugacy class of open subgroups of
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
.

This makes it possible, in the surrounding universe
V[[X]]
in which Axiom of Choice holds, to find a set
Z\subset A
which selects from each
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
-orbit exactly one element, and such that for each

there is an

such that
\mathrm{stab}(B)=\Delta_{n}
. For each

and choose an ordinal

and an injection

. Consider the set

. It is clear that

consists of elements of

and it is
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
-invariant; therefore, it belongs to

. It will be enough to prove that

is a function and an injection; for then, it is an injection from

to
\mathrm{HF}(X)\times\alpha
, where

.

To show that

is a function and an injection, it is enough to show that for each set

and group elements

,

is equivalent to

. Since

Now, we exploit the conclusion of Theorem

abstractly to obtain conclusions about the theory of the resulting permutation model. For the first theorem, a

Now, as in the previous cases, we will show that if there is a tournament on

Now, suppose that

Definition 5.8
.

Let

be a set.

1.

A

on

is a set

for some finite set

referred as the base of the cone;

2.

X

has no algebraicity
if every set

closed under intersection either contains a cone or is disjoint from a cone.

Proposition 5.9
.

(ZF or ZFA)

Let
X
be a set with no algebraicity.

1.

every subset of
X
has no algebraicity;

2.

every equivalence relation on
X
with all classes finite has only finitely many non-singleton classes;

3.

there is no injection from
\omega
to
[X]^{<\aleph_{0}}
;

4.

for every infinite set
A\subseteq[X]^{<\aleph_{0}}
there is a set
b\subset X
such that the set
\{x\in X\colon b\cup\{x\}\in A\}
is infinite.

Proof.

For (1), suppose towards a contradiction that
Y\subseteq X
is a set and
A\subseteq[Y]^{<\aleph_{0}}
is a set closed under intersection which does not contain a cone and is not disjoint from a cone. The set
B\subseteq[X]^{<\aleph_{0}}
given by
b\in B\leftrightarrow b\cap Y\in A
is closed under intersection and it does not contain a cone and it is not disjoint from a cone, violating the assumption on
X
.

For (2), let
E
be an equivalence relation on
X
with all classes finite. Let
A\subseteq[X]^{<\aleph_{0}}
be the set of all finite
E
-saturated sets. It is clearly closed under intersections. It cannot be disjoint from a cone because for any finite set
a\subset X
, the
E
-saturation of
a
is a superset of
a
which belongs to
A
. So,
A
must contain the cone of all finite supersets of some finite set
a\subseteq X
. The only way that can occur is when all elements of
X\setminus a
are pairwise inequivalent.

For (3), suppose towards a contradiction that
g\colon\omega\to[X]^{<\aleph_{0}}
is an injection. Manipulating the injection if necessary, we may assume that it is inclusion-increasing, and for each
n\in\omega
,
|g(n+1)\setminus g(n)|\geq 2
. Let
Y=\bigcup_{n}g_{n}
. The set
A\subseteq[X]^{<\aleph_{0}}
defined by
b\in A\leftrightarrow\exists n\ b\cap Y=g(n)
is closed under intersections and it does not contain a cone and it is not disjoint from one, violating the assumption on
X
.

For (4), for each set
b\subset X
write
b^{\prime}=\{x\in X\colon b\cup\{x\}\in A\}
if the latter set is finite, and
b^{\prime}=0
otherwise. Let
B=\{c\in[X]^{\aleph_{0}}\colon\forall b\subseteq c\ b^{\prime}\subseteq c\}
. It is immediate that the set
B
is closed under intersections. First note that
B
cannot be disjoint from a cone. If
a\subset X
was the base of the cone, one can define sets
a_{n}
by recursion by
a_{0}=a
and
a_{n+1}=a_{n}\cup\{b^{\prime}\colon b\subseteq a_{n}\}
. By (3), this sequence of finite sets has to stabilize, and the stable value is then necessarily a superset of
a
in
B
. Thus,
B
contains a cone with a base
a
. Since the set
A
is infinite, it must contain a set

which is not a subset of
a
. Let
x\in c\setminus a
be arbitrary and let
b=c\setminus\{x\}
. The set
a\cup b
belongs to the set

, and this can occur only if

; so, the set
\{x\in X\colon b\cup\{x\}\in A\}
is infinite as desired.
∎

Theorem 5.10
.

(ZF or ZFA)
Suppose that

is a set with no algebraicity such that

is a support set. Then

1.

every set is either well-orderable or it contains an infinite set with no algebraicity, implying
[

2.

[

[

, Form 380]

[

, Form 329]

Prior to further considerations, we must construct a function

such that for every

,

For (1), let

For (2), let

with

is not well-orderable; by (1), there is an infinite set

be the equivalence relation on

For (4), let

In view of Corollary

Claim 5.22

If
