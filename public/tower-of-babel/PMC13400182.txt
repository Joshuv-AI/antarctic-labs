Light-driven thermotropic hydrogel with nanomodulator enables lipid intervention and cavity delivery for postsurgical tumor therapy
Authors: Ziqiao Zhong, Lu Gan, Yihong Gao, Siyuan Peng, Yeqi Huang, Xincheng Lin, Jintao Fu, Ziyi Feng, Zhengwei Huang, Xin Pan, Chuanbin Wu, Wenhao Wang, Ying Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFatty acid-rich milieu is recognized as a potential key factor driving postsurgical tumor recurrence, while insufficient drug delivery to irregular surgical margins further compromises therapeutic effects. We find that this milieu directly drives lipid droplet accumulation in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), promoting M2 polarization, which can be reversed by rapamycin (Ra) via enhanced lipid droplet metabolism. Accordingly, we design a light-driven thermotropic hydrogel (LTG@RL) encapsulating a nanomodulator (R@LOX) containing lipoxygenase and Ra. Upon photothermal irradiation, LTG@RL undergoes reversible gel-sol transition for deep cavity penetration and sustained retention. R@LOX coordinates lipid intervention. It uses tumor-derived fatty acids to generate lipid peroxides, inducing ferroptosis in residual cancer cells, while repolarizing TAMs to the M1 phenotype to restore immune surveillance. In postsurgical melanoma and breast cancer models, LTG@RL suppresses tumor recurrence, prolongs survival, and also exhibits activity in non-surgical melanoma models. This strategy integrates lipid intervention and enhanced cavity delivery, offering precision prevention of postsurgical recurrence.

Introduction

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone for managing malignant solid tumors. However, the persistence of residual tumor cells at surgical margins and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) pose a substantial risk for recurrence.1,2 Current clinical approaches to inhibit recurrence, such as traditional chemotherapy and radiotherapy, are often limited by severe systemic side effects.3 Therefore, the development of effective postsurgical treatments is imperative.

The distinct fatty acid-rich milieu is considered a targetable vulnerability in the TME. Unlike normal cells, cancer cells proliferate uncontrollably, requiring substantial lipid accumulation for membrane and organelle biogenesis.4 Cancer cells also induce adipocyte lipolysis, releasing fatty acids to create a fatty acid-rich milieu.4,5 Directly, this fatty acid-rich milieu fuels tumor progression. Advanced tumors frequently infiltrate fatty acid-rich subcutaneous tissues, which fuels their proliferation and invasion.6 Indirectly, emerging evidence suggests that this same milieu may shape the immune landscape, particularly through its effects on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), although the precise mechanisms remain unclear.7,8,9 Crucially, surgical resection may exacerbate this problem due to tissue disruption. Thus, the fatty acid-rich milieu drives postsurgical tumor recurrence by promoting tumor initiation, progression, and immunosuppression, making it a compelling therapeutic target. Concurrently, residual tumor cells persist at surgical margins.10,11 Due to irregular tumor shapes and poorly defined boundaries, postsurgical cavities often contain microcavities that complicate drug delivery to these high-risk sites.12,13,14 Hence, the fatty acid-rich milieu and suboptimal drug delivery to surgical margins critically limit the efficacy of anti-recurrence therapy.

In this study, we first sought to determine whether and how the fatty acid-rich environment programs TAMs toward an immunosuppressive phenotype. Our findings reveal that lipid droplet (LD) accumulation in macrophages promotes their M2 polarization and suppresses innate anti-tumor immunity. Simultaneously, we found that rapamycin (Ra), an inhibitor of the mTOR signaling pathway, enhances LD metabolism in TAMs, driving their polarization toward the anti-tumor M1 phenotype. Having established that lipid metabolism in TAMs can be therapeutically targeted, we next turned to eliminating residual tumor cells at surgical margins. Notably, the same fatty acid-rich milieu also represents an exploitable resource. The abundant fatty acids could be converted into direct cytotoxic effectors. Lipoxygenase (LOX) offered such a mechanism. It catalyzes free fatty acids to produce lipid peroxides (LPO), which induce ferroptosis in tumor cells.15 Based on these mechanistic insights, we therefore proposed a coordinated lipid intervention strategy that integrates intracellular immunometabolic modulation by Ra with extracellular fatty acid conversion by LOX. Ra was self-assembled with LOX to form nanomodulator R@LOX, enabling simultaneous induction of tumor cell ferroptosis and reprogramming of TAMs for synergistic antitumor and immunomodulatory effects.16,17,18

However, effective delivery of these therapeutic agents to surgical margins remains a challenge. Thus, we developed a light-driven thermotropic hydrogel (LTG) with a controlled upper critical solution temperature. The material remains in a gel state below this temperature and transitions into a sol state above it. This characteristic enables the integration of remote temperature-tuned modules, such as photothermal therapy. By adjusting the proportion of LTG components, the phase transition of LTG can be triggered at the temperature achieved through photothermal therapy, which significantly enhances its fluidity and facilitates the uniform dispersion of drugs to surgical margins and irregular microcavities. As the temperature of LTG returns to body temperature, it reverts to a gel state, functioning as an effective drug reservoir for sustained therapeutic action. Importantly, this sol-gel transition is repeatable and allows it for on-demand, multi-cycle drug delivery, thereby further improving therapeutic efficiency at the surgical site.

Specifically, based on the proposed coordinated lipid intervention strategy, we integrated the R@LOX into LTG to form LTG@RL. The LTG comprised a hyaluronic acid-gelatin matrix to tune the upper critical solution temperature and a tannic acid/Fe3+ metal-polyphenol network to provide photothermal capabilities. Upon injection into the surgical cavity, laser irradiation triggered a phase transition in LTG@RL, increasing its fluidity to penetrate surgical margins and microcavities while simultaneously lysing residual tumor cells to release fatty acids. The R@LOX subsequently oxidized these free fatty acids and tumor cell lysates (CL) to generate abundant LPO, inducing ferroptosis. Furthermore, R@LOX reprogrammed TAMs by enhancing lipid metabolism, polarizing them toward the anti-tumor M1 phenotype to activate immunity. In summary, LTG@RL offers a promising approach for preventing postsurgical tumor recurrence through synergistic microenvironment modulation and adaptive drug delivery.

Results

The impact of fatty acid-rich milieu on TAMs and the modulatory effects of Ra

TME is characterized by a dysregulated metabolic landscape. The uncontrolled rapid growth and proliferation of tumor cells necessitated a substantial intake of nutrients.19 Among these nutrients, fatty acids, which were integral components of the biological membranes and structural units of tumor cells, provided a significant amount of energy for tumor growth and were enriched in the TME.20,21 Following tumor surgery, the levels of free fatty acids were further elevated due to the inevitable rupture of tumor cells. Accumulating evidence has suggested that the lipid-rich environment can profoundly influence tumor progression, yet the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.22,23,24

As an initial step to explore the clinical significance of lipid metabolism in melanoma, we examined its prognostic relevance using 458 samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas-Skin Cutaneous Melanoma (TCGA-SKCM). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that (Figures 1A–1C) dysregulation of lipid-related genes was significantly associated with overall survival. Specifically, low expression of ABCA1 and ABCG1, genes encoding ATP-binding cassette transporters critical for cholesterol efflux, correlated with shorter patient survival. Conversely, high expression of PLIN4, a gene encoding perilipin-4, a structural protein essential for LD formation and stabilization, was also associated with poorer prognosis. Collectively, these findings suggested that aberrant lipid accumulation, driven by imbalanced expression of lipid transport and storage regulators, may contribute to melanoma progression and adverse clinical outcomes. Among the various cell types within the TME, TAMs are particularly noteworthy due to their established plasticity in response to metabolic cues. We, therefore, hypothesized that the enrichment of fatty acids in TME may contribute to an increased presence of immunosuppressive cells, particularly M2-TAMs, thereby hindering the activation of anti-tumor immunity within the TME. This hypothesis is supported by previous reports in colorectal cancer BALB/c models.8,9 Thus, we conducted preliminary investigations into the phenotypic alterations of macrophages under the fatty acid-rich milieu. RAW264.7 is a widely used macrophage cell line with TAM-like characteristics, serving as a model system.25,26,27 We induced lipid accumulation in RAW264.7 macrophages by supplementing the culture medium with linoleic acid (LA) for three generations. Transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between LA-treated and untreated macrophages (Figure S1A). And it revealed 954 significantly differentially expressed genes ([SDEGs], 322 upregulated and 632 downregulated) in LA-treated vs. control cells (Figures 1D and S1B). Gene Ontology (GO) demonstrated significant enrichment of SDEGs in lipid metabolic process, lipid storage, and LD formation (Figures 1E and S1C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis showed activation of fatty acid elongation and biosynthesis pathways (Figure 1F). Encouraged by these results, subsequent analysis of lipid metabolism-related genes revealed upregulated expression of de novo lipogenesis genes (FASN) and LD -associated genes (PLIN2 and PLIN4), coupled with downregulation of lipid efflux transporters genes (ABCA1 and ABCG1) (Figure 1G).28,29 Desaturation-related genes (FADS2, FADS3, and SCD1) exhibited suppressed expression, collectively indicating enhanced lipid synthesis/storage and impaired lipid efflux in LA-treated RAW264.7 (Figure 1G).8

Intriguingly, beyond these metabolic changes, GO analysis also demonstrated SDEG clustering in M2-polarization-associated processes, including ERK1/ERK2 cascade, retinol metabolism, carboxylic acid biosynthesis, and AKT signaling (Figure 1H).8,30 Complementary KEGG analysis confirmed activation of PPAR signaling, retinol metabolism, and AKT pathways (Figure 1I), suggesting enhanced M2-like polarization under the fatty acid-rich milieu.31,32 Immune-related transcriptional profiling revealed downregulation of innate immunity genes (OAS1A, OAS2, OAS3, IRF7, and TLR9) alongside upregulation of M2-associated macrophages genes (MMP9 and CD38), indicative of attenuated anti-tumor immunity (Figure 1J).33,34,35 Additionally, we found that the LD levels in CD206+ macrophages (namely, M2 macrophages) were indeed significantly higher than those in CD206− macrophages (Figures 1K and 1L). This aligned with the transcriptomic data. The fatty acid-rich milieu drove lipid metabolic reprogramming in macrophages, marked by increased synthesis and storage and reduced efflux, while concurrently promoting M2 polarization, thereby linking lipid metabolism to protumoral macrophage function.

Given that, we sought to investigate whether pharmacological intervention targeting this pathway could reverse the phenotype. Ra is known to inhibit mTOR signaling to regulate lipid metabolism (Figure 1M).8 Thus, we investigated whether Ra could reprogram macrophages through the regulation of lipid metabolism. Ra treatment dose-dependently reduced LD accumulation in RAW264.7 cells (Figures S1D, S1E, and 1N–1O). Moreover, in preliminary experiments using bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), Ra effectively upregulated CD86 expression, indicating robust M1-like polarization (Figures S1F and S1G). It also downregulated perilipin 2 (an LD marker) and arginase 1 (an M2 TAM marker, Arg-1), while upregulating the M1 marker inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS or NOS2) (Figures 1P and 1Q). Thus, in the fatty acid-rich milieu, Ra reduced intracellular LDs and drove M2-to-M1 repolarization. In summary, we reveal that the fatty acid-enriched TME induces lipid accumulation in macrophages to drive its M2 polarization, and Ra effectively reduces LD accumulation and drives M2-to-M1 repolarization, suggesting that targeting lipid metabolism may offer a strategy to remodel TAMs.

The synthesis of R@LOX and its synergistic induction of ferroptosis

Having established that Ra modulates macrophage polarization by targeting lipid metabolism, we next designed a coordinated lipid intervention strategy by integrating this immunomodulatory function with a direct tumor cell killing mechanism. LOX has been reported to induce natural cellular ferroptosis in tumor cells through the lipid biocatalytic process, which might exert coordinated lipid intervention effects when co-delivered with Ra.36 Here, our results verified the desired biocatalytic activity of LOX, where the group with LOX successfully converted CL to LPO, establishing its potential to induce ferroptosis in tumor cells (Figure S2A). Using LA as a classical substitute for CL,37 the substrate-dependent cytotoxicity of LOX was revealed in the presence of LA in a concentration-dependent manner, whereas no obvious cytotoxicity was revealed in the absence of LA (Figure S2B). However, Ra exhibited LA-independent cell-killing effects (Figures S2C and S2D). Therefore, these results suggested that LOX exerted its therapeutic potential via biocatalytic reactions, whereas RA did not exhibit such effects.

The hydrophobic Ra was self-assembled with LOX using a one-pot method to enhance its solubility and stability, constructing an R@LOX nanomodulator (Figure 2A). The particle size was measured as 346.43 ± 4.45 nm (Figure 2B), with a zeta potential of −14.27 ± 1.89 mV. The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy results indicated a redshift in the amide A band (3,287 cm−1) and amide I (1,641 cm−1) of R@LOX (Figure S2E), suggesting that Ra and LOX were primarily combined into a nanomodulator through intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces. The synthesized nanomodulator could be effectively delivered into tumor cells (Figures S2F and S2G). R@LOX possessed significant cytotoxicity to B16 cells, and Ra and LOX exhibited a synergistic effect (Figure 2C). Using CompuSyn software to analyze the drug combination, we obtained an effect level (fraction affected) of 0.89 and a combination index (CI) of 0.63 (Figures S2H and S2I), confirming a synergistic interaction according to the Chou-Talalay method (CI<1 indicates synergism). Moreover, R@LOX exhibited a catalytic capacity for producing LPO that was comparable to that of LOX (Figure S2J), and it was still capable of killing over 60% of B16 cells with CL as the substrate (Figure S2K). To identify the death pathway induced by R@LOX, we treated cells with pathway-specific inhibitors and measured viability. The cell death caused by R@LOX was significantly inhibited by ferroptosis inhibitor, antioxidants, and autophagy inhibitor (Figure S2L), suggesting that oxidative stress-induced and autophagy-assisted ferroptosis dominate the R@LOX-induced cell death. The ferroptotic morphology in both LOX + LA- and R@LOX + LA-treated B16 cells was observed with mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae loss (Figure S2M). Notably, substantial LPO accumulation and pronounced plasma membrane roughening, characteristic of lipid peroxidation, were observed specifically in the R@LOX + LA group (Figures 2D and 2E). The observed effect may result from the synergistic effects of LOX and Ra, as cell membranes in the Ra + LA group also exhibited a measurable degree of surface roughening. In conclusion, R@LOX effectively induced ferroptosis and demonstrated a synergistic effect.

Notably, Ra alone elevated LPO both with and without LA, suggesting a distinct, LA-independent mechanism (Figure 2D). While LOX drives LPO production directly via LA catalysis, the LPO induction effects were accompanied by elevated reactive oxygen species (Figures S2M–S2O), indicating an indirect pathway. This observation prompted us to investigate how Ra sensitizes tumor cells to ferroptosis. We found that R@LOX strongly suppressed GPX4 activity (Figure 2F), disabling a key antioxidant defense and thereby promoting ferroptosis. Given that Ra is a typical autophagy inducer that inhibits mTOR,38,39 it was speculated that Ra enhanced ferroptosis by triggering autophagic degradation of antioxidant components, particularly GPX4. Consistent with this, Ra reduced GPX4 expression levels in B16 cells by 64.49% (Figure 2G) and depleted glutathione (Figure S2P), while autophagy inhibitors (CQ or 3-MA) restored GPX4 activity (Figure S2Q). Autophagy activation was further confirmed by lysosomal staining, LC3-II upregulation, and biological transmission electron microscopy (Bio-TEM) (Figures S2R–S2V). Therefore, LOX contributed to the generation of LPO, while Ra impaired cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative damage. This dual effect intensified lipid peroxidation in the tumor cell membrane, leading to more pronounced ferroptosis effects.

Building upon these findings, we performed transcriptomic analysis on R@LOX-treated B16 cells to characterize the molecular mechanisms underlying enhanced ferroptosis. Principal-component analysis revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between untreated and R@LOX-treated B16 cells, with 1,186 SDEGs identified (612 upregulated and 574 downregulated; Figures 2H and S2W). GO enrichment analysis demonstrated SDEGs enriched in biological processes and cellular components central to ferroptosis, particularly glutathione metabolic process, peroxidase activity, and autophagy (Figures 2I and 2J). KEGG pathway analysis further revealed activation of the ferroptosis-associated pathways, including mTOR signaling, autophagy, lysosome, and AMPK signaling (Figure 2K). Detailed analysis of pathway-related genes showed upregulated iron metabolism gene TFRC and downregulated glutathione synthase gene GSS (Figure 2L). Concurrent activation of autophagy-related genes was observed, including autophagosome formation genes GABARAP and lysosomal fusion regulators genes LAMP1/LAMP2, while autophagy inhibition genes AKT (AKT2 and AKT3) related to the mTOR signaling pathway were downregulated.40,41,42 These transcriptional changes align with cellular- and protein-level observations, confirming that R@LOX potentiated ferroptosis by promoting GPX4 autophagy-mediated antioxidant depletion.

Assessment of the immune regulatory capability of R@LOX

In the aforementioned studies, we preliminarily probed into the capacity of Ra to regulate the phenotype of TAMs by enhancing lipid metabolism, while LOX exerts its primary function in inducing tumor ferroptosis. Given the potential of LOX to consume fatty acids and convert them into LPO, which have been reported to selectively sensitize M2-like macrophages to ferroptosis,43,44 we hypothesized that R@LOX might achieve dual immunomodulatory effects by simultaneously generating LPO from extracellular fatty acids and enhancing intracellular lipid metabolism. To test this, we first evaluated the impact of R@LOX on RAW264.7 cells. A time-dependent downregulation of LD levels in RAW264.7 cells upon R@LOX treatment was revealed (Figure S3A). Then, CL was added to the macrophage culture to better mimic the TME with complex compositions. A significant increase in the proportion of CD86+ cells in all CL-supplemented groups compared with the control group was revealed, potentially due to CL’s role in stimulating TAM maturation as a tumor antigen (Figures S3B–S3E). Notably, the introduction of R@LOX exhibited the highest proportion of CD86+ cells, reaching 30.80%, which was 2.9 times that of the CL group alone. Furthermore, the M1/M2 proportion in the R@LOX + CL group was 2.6 times higher than that in the CL group, suggesting that R@LOX could ameliorate the immunosuppressive state of the TME and enhance anti-tumor immunity.

To strengthen the physiological relevance of our findings, we, therefore, complemented functional polarization experiments using BMDMs from wild-type C57BL/6 mice. Although the CL group showed an increased proportion of CD86+F4/80+ cells, a concurrent significant increase in CD206+F4/80+ cells resulted in a modest M1/M2 proportion, indicative of an immunosuppressive TME (Figures 3A, 3B, S3F, and S3G). Conversely, the R@LOX + CL significantly elevated the proportion of CD86+F4/80+ cells, enhancing the anti-tumor capacity of BMDMs, and markedly increased the M1/M2 proportion. The R@LOX + CL group also exhibited a significant upregulation in CD86 mRNA expression, accompanied by a corresponding increase in NOS2 transcript levels (Figures 3C and 3D). These findings confirmed the successful induction of M1 polarization in BMDMs. In contrast, free fatty acids and CL promoted M2 polarization, as evidenced by increased CD206 protein expression in flow cytometry (Figures S3F and S3G) and elevated CD206 mRNA levels in qPCR analysis (Figure 3E). Additionally, a significant increase in Arg-1 transcript levels in the CL group further supported this M2-polarizing effect (Figure 3F). To deeply explore the mechanism of the phenotypic regulation ability of R@LOX, we conducted transcriptomic analysis. Transcriptomic profiling of R@LOX-treated RAW264.7 cells identified 3,987 SDEGs (2,305 upregulated, 1,682 downregulated; Figures 3G and S3H). GO analysis revealed SDEG enrichment in immune-related processes, including immune system process, innate immune response, and T cell proliferation involved in immune response (Figure 3H). Meanwhile, KEGG analysis highlighted activation of pro-inflammatory pathways (IL-17, Notch, and NOD-like receptor) and lipid metabolism pathways (PPAR and mTOR; Figure 3I). These results indicated that after R@LOX treatment, RAW264.7 cells became more active in terms of intrinsic inflammation and lipid metabolism. Gene-level analysis (Figure 3J) revealed that enhanced lipid metabolism resulted from coordinated transcriptional reprogramming: upregulation of lipolytic regulator genes (PNPLA2, SLC27A1, and ATG7) and concurrent downregulation of genes involved in LD fusion (RAB8A and RAB3IL1), de novo lipogenesis (ARID3A and AGPAT5), and LD stabilization (PLIN4).45,46 Additionally, transcripts encoding key mediators of lipid efflux, ABCA1 and ABCG1, were significantly downregulated. Collectively, these changes reflect a metabolic shift away from lipid synthesis, storage, and export, consistent with an overall enhancement of catabolic lipid turnover. Given this, we also analyzed the changes in RAW264.7 phenotype-related genes while lipid metabolism was enhanced. Phenotypic analysis (Figure 3K) revealed an enhanced anti-tumor immune response, including downregulation of MRC1 (CD206) and upregulation of innate immunity genes (OAS1A, OAS2, and OAS3). Additionally, genes related to proinflammatory and induced M1-type polarization of RAW264.7, such as CXCL10, NOS2, TNF, TLR2/4, and NFKB1, were significantly upregulated, confirming enhancement of the immunological function in RAW264.7.47,48,49,50 In conclusion, R@LOX exhibited remarkable regulatory capability on RAW264.7 and BMDMs, significantly enhancing the M1/M2 proportion.

Preparation and characterization of LTG@RL

R@LOX has showcased remarkable anti-tumor efficacy and the ability to regulate macrophage phenotype in vitro. To translate these findings to in vivo application, effective delivery to the irregular postsurgical cavity is essential. We developed an LTG with a controlled upper critical solution temperature as a delivery vehicle for R@LOX (Figure 4A). Photothermal conversion provides the key to enabling this function. The localized heating induces a phase transition that temporarily increases hydrogel fluidity, enabling it to flow into and fill surgical microcavities before reverting to a gel state for sustained drug retention. We selected gelatin and hyaluronic acid as the matrix backbone for tunable mechanical properties and upper critical solution temperature control, while incorporating tannic acid and Fe3+ to form a metal-polyphenol network (MPN) that enables photothermal conversion. Through comprehensive tests, we first determined that LTG with all four components was necessary, which possessed desired viscoelasticity, injectability, and tissue adhesion (Figures 4B–4D and S4A–S4E). We next optimized the gelatin-to-hyaluronic acid ratio to fine-tune the phase transition temperature to within the range of photothermal heating (43°C ± 2°C) enabled light-controlled gel-sol transition (Figures S4F and 4E).

R@LOX was loaded into LTG to construct LTG@RL. Rheological tests on LTG@RL showed that drug loading did not affect its viscoelasticity and dynamic modulus (Figures 4F and S4G). In fact, these properties even improved, possibly due to the incorporation of Ra and LOX groups into the hydrogel scaffold, enhancing complexation. The dynamic viscosity of LTG@RL was largely unaffected by drug loading (Figure 4G). And LTG@RL’s injectability facilitated subsequent in vivo administration studies (Figures S4H and S4I). The analysis of LTG@RL’s structural morphology and elemental distribution revealed a typical porous gel structure with uniform elemental distribution (Figure S4J). In summary, the developed LTG@RL possessed outstanding rheological properties and adhesion, and its injectability was conducive to further in vivo drug delivery research.

The photothermal conversion performance of LTG@RL, enabled by its MPN structure,51 is a critical determinant of its efficacy in targeted delivery applications. In comparison to other groups, LTG@RL demonstrated a swift temperature increase under laser irradiation, surpassing 50°C within 5 min (Figures 4H and 4I). LTG@RL also exhibits inter-batch reproducibility (Figure S4K). It retained its photothermal excellence after “on-off” cycles of heating and cooling, which was crucial for repeated laser treatments in tumor therapy (Figure 4J). Furthermore, it was disclosed that remarkable photothermal properties were sustained in vivo, reaching above 50°C within 4.5 min (Figures 4K and 4L). Given the potential for near-infrared (NIR) irradiation to induce thermal injury in normal skin tissue, we analyzed tissue samples from the irradiated surgical site at 24 h, 3 days, and 7 days after LTG@RL administration and NIR irradiation. Mild temporary tissue changes at 24 h fully recovered by day 7 with no irreversible damage, verifying that the NIR dose is safe and LTG@RL is suitable for postsurgical delivery (Figure 4M). In summary, LTG@RL possesses the rheological properties, injectability, adhesion, and photothermal performance, required for effective postsurgical delivery.

Assessment of the light-driven thermotropic property of LTG@RL

While the preceding studies have optimized the formulation and phase transition temperature of LTG@RL, whether these properties translate into effective drug delivery in the actual surgical setting remains to be demonstrated. To assess light-driven thermotropic capabilities of LTG@RL, detailed in vitro and in vivo studies were conducted. Through tube tests (Figure S4L), LTG underwent a phase transition upon exposure to laser irradiation, transforming into a fluid-like state with flow characteristics. In contrast, the physical states of the other component hydrogels remained unchanged before and after laser exposure. Video S1 further demonstrated that the fluidity of LTG was significantly enhanced under laser irradiation. NIR irradiation sharply reduced LTG@RL’s dynamic modulus (Figure 5A), confirming enhanced fluidity. Upon cooling to 37°C, the modulus fully recovered, and even exceeded its pre-irradiation value, likely due to thermally enhanced molecular mobility promoting stronger intermolecular bonding on re-cooling. Furthermore, the viscoelasticity of LTG@RL post-irradiation also decreased notably, indicating a profound change in fluidity (Figure 5B). Irregularly shaped molds and mouse wound models were employed to visually demonstrate the drug delivery characteristics of LTG@RL. After injection, LTG@RL mostly filled the mold but not its corners, yet seamlessly covered the entire irregular mold after laser irradiation (Figure S4M). Additionally, for irregular surgical margins, LTG@RL could also flawlessly cover the wound post-irradiation (Figures 5C and 5D). To benchmark the drug delivery capability of LTG@RL, we compared it against conventional thermo-responsive hydrogels (TRH) commonly used for postsurgical local therapy. Using Pluronic 188/407 as TRH controls, DID-loaded LTG@RL and TRH were injected into tumor cavities (Figure 5E). As a result, LTG@RL showed stronger deep drug delivery than TRH, which was further boosted by laser irradiation especially with two cycles, due to its photothermally triggered repeated phase transitions rather than TRH’s single passive gelation, while its photothermal effect also enabled tumor cell ablation for antitumor activity. Beyond enhancing drug delivery, the same photothermal conversion capability allowed it to ablate tumor cells via hyperthermia (Figure S4N), conferring antitumor activity. Collectively, these results indicated that LTG possessed superior advantages over TRH for postsurgical tumor drug delivery. In addition, the in vivo release profile of the drug in LTG@RL was investigated. Interestingly, the fluorescence of R@Cy5.5-LOX vanished when it was loaded into LTG (i.e., LTG@RL-Cy5.5) due to light blocking (Figure S4O).52,53 Using this phenomenon, R@Cy5.5-LOX in LTG@RL-Cy5.5 released quickly within the first 10 h and maintained a high concentration for 48 h to sustain the therapeutic effect (Figure S4O).

Additionally, postsurgical treatments must be evaluated for their effects on wound healing. We thus investigated how LTG@RL, alone and with NIR irradiation, influences cutaneous wound repair in vivo. As shown in Figures 5F and 5G, the LTG@RL accelerated wound closure trend relative to the control group, and the addition of NIR further significantly improved closure by day 12. The pathological analysis of H&E staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and CD31 and keratin 10 (CK10) expression collaboratively revealed the effective tissue regeneration and enhanced angiogenesis and re-epithelialization (Figure 5H). Overall, LTG@RL alone or combined with NIR effectively promotes rather than impairs postsurgical wound healing.

The anti-melanoma recurrence effect of LTG@RL

Based on the exceptional rheological, photothermal, and light-triggered delivery properties of LTG@RL demonstrated above, we next evaluated its efficacy in preventing postsurgical tumor recurrence in a postsurgical melanoma mouse model (Figure 6A). As shown in Figures 6B and 6C, despite the removal of tumors in the control group, the rate of tumor recurrence remained rapid and exhibited an uncontrolled trend in the later stages of the experiment. In contrast, the LTG@RL + NIR group exhibited significant inhibition of the process of postsurgical melanoma recurrence. Moreover, the tumor bioluminescence curve for the LTG@RL + NIR group was substantially lower than that of the control group. Owing to the advantages of photothermal therapy, LTG + NIR exhibited a notable capacity to suppress the process of tumor recurrence even without drug loading, and the LTG@RL group demonstrated certain efficacy in eliminating residual tumor cells under the influence of R@LOX. No abnormal changes in body weight were observed in either group (Figure 6D), and comprehensive assessments of major organs revealed no abnormalities (Figure S5A), underscoring the remarkable biocompatibility of LTG@RL. We continuously monitored the survival status of mice following treatments to investigate the long-term effects of LTG@RL. As shown in Figure 6E, all mice in the control group died within 10 days, whereas the survival durations of the treated groups were significantly prolonged. Median survival times,54 were 9.5 days for control, 13.5 days for LTG + NIR, 15.0 days for LTG@RL, and 17.5 days for LTG@RL + NIR, demonstrating that LTG@RL + NIR most effectively extended lifespan. For pathological analysis, H&E staining demonstrated that the tumor tissue became loosened in the LTG@RL + NIR group (Figure 6F). The 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) staining results revealed markedly increased lipid peroxidation in the LTG@RL + NIR group, indicating oxidative stress and enhanced ferroptosis susceptibility. Additionally, a notable increase in LC3 (brown) levels and a significant diminution in GPX4 (brown) levels were observed in the LTG@RL + NIR group, suggesting that the autophagic degradation of GPX4 and the antioxidant capacity of tumor cells were reduced to facilitate tumor ferroptosis (Figure 6F). In summary, LTG@RL, under laser irradiation, exhibited superior efficacy in inhibiting tumor recurrence. Surgical resection induced tumor cell rupture, leading to the release of free fatty acids, which increased the content of free fatty acids (Figure S5B) and may subsequently exacerbate the immunosuppressive TME. To investigate whether LTG@RL could reverse the immunosuppressive TME, we performed a preliminary analysis using immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Figure S5C, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were scarcely detectable in the control group but were more prominently observed in the LTG@RL and LTG@RL + NIR groups. CD86+ cells (M1-like TAMs, red fluorescence) were most abundant in the LTG@RL + NIR group, whereas CD206+ cells (M2-like TAMs, green fluorescence) were least expressed in the same group. These findings provided preliminary evidence that LTG@RL activated anti-tumor immune responses.

In vivo immune regulation of LTG@RL in postsurgical melanoma

Our previous in vitro studies demonstrated that in a fatty acid-enriched milieu, macrophages accumulate LD and undergo M2-like polarization, while the coordinated lipid intervention strategy of R@LOX, which combines LOX-mediated fatty acid consumption with Ra-enhanced lipid metabolism, effectively reduces LD accumulation and promotes M1-like polarization, thereby alleviating immunosuppression. Building on these findings, we next examined the potential of LTG@RL in remodeling the immune microenvironment regulatory in the postsurgical setting. We first determined the transcriptional levels of key cytokines or markers in anti-tumor immunity.55,56 As shown in Figures 7A–7D, the LTG@RL + NIR treatment significantly promoted the transcriptional levels of TNF-α, IFN-γ, and NOS2 (M1 TAMs marker), but reduced the levels of Arg-1 (M2 TAMs marker). To determine whether these molecular changes translated into functional immune responses, we next characterized the major immune cell populations in the TME (Figures S5D and S5E). As a key indicator of effective immune activation, we first examined the T cell response. After treatment, a significant increase in the overall proportion of T cells in the LTG@RL + NIR group, along with a marked elevation in the proportion of CD8+ T cells, was revealed (Figures 7E–7H), suggesting a cornerstone of adaptive anti-tumor immunity.57 Then, we focused on the TAMs in tumor tissues after treatment. The proportion of CD86+ TAMs and CD86+NOS2+ TAMs was significantly improved (Figures 7I–7L), while the CD206+Arg-1+ TAMs showed a significant reduction (Figures 7M–7P). Furthermore, the M1/M2 ratio was significantly elevated (Figure 7Q), confirming that the combination of LTG@RL and NIR treatment effectively ameliorated the immunosuppressive TME. The decrease of PD-L2+ TAMs further corroborated the reduced proportion of TAMs with protumor activity (Figures 7R and 7S). These results collaboratively validated the M2-to-M1 polarization induced by LTG@RL. Additionally, the levels of LD in tumor tissue macrophages (CD11b+F4/80+) were evaluated. The results disclosed a significant reduction in LD levels within macrophages of the LTG@RL + NIR group (Figure 7T), which was consistent with the potential mechanism of macrophage regulation displayed by R@LOX in vitro.

In conclusion, the combination of LTG@RL and NIR showed superior effectiveness in preventing tumor recurrence, significantly delaying the progression of melanoma recurrence. LTG@RL could remodel the immune microenvironment after melanoma surgery, which might be related to its ability to lower the levels of LD within macrophages. This treatment exhibits great potential in the combination of clinical immune checkpoint blockade, representing a promising avenue for clinical translation.

The anti-breast cancer recurrence effect of LTG@RL

Given that our coordinated lipid intervention strategy demonstrated potent efficacy in preventing melanoma recurrence, we next sought to evaluate whether this approach could be extended to other tumor types with lipid-rich microenvironments. Breast cancer is characterized by a lipid-rich stromal environment that supported tumor recurrence and was chosen as another postsurgical model (Figure S6A).58,59,60 As presented in Figures S6B and S6C, treatment with LTG + NIR or LTG@RL delayed tumor recurrence but failed to prevent eventual relapse. In contrast, mice in the LTG@RL + NIR group exhibited significantly suppressed tumor recurrence under the combined action of R@LOX and NIR irradiation, with no evident signs of recurrence observed throughout the 14-day monitoring period. All formulations exhibited remarkable biocompatibility (Figures S6D and S7A). The median survival time of the control group was 37.0 days, whereas over 90% of mice in the LTG@RL + NIR group survived beyond day 45, demonstrating that LTG@RL + NIR treatment significantly prolongs survival in this model (Figure S6E). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed distinct morphological changes in tumor tissues across treatment groups. Tumor cells in the treated groups exhibited a more loosely organized architecture and lighter cytoplasmic staining, particularly in the LTG@RL + NIR group (Figure S6F). Results from 4-HNE and GPX4 immunostaining indicated that tumor cells in the LTG@RL + NIR group experienced severe oxidative damage (Figure S6F) and displayed reduced GPX4 expression, reflecting diminished antioxidant capacity and exacerbated intracellular oxidative stress. This effect may be attributed to robust autophagy induction in these cells, as suggested by strong LC3B immunoreactivity. Furthermore, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were prominently distributed in the LTG@RL + NIR group but were scarcely detectable in the control group (Figure S7B). Additionally, CD86+ cells were more abundant in the LTG@RL + NIR group, whereas CD206+ cells were minimally present (Figure S7B). Collectively, these results indicated that LTG@RL + NIR treatment enhanced anti-tumor immune responses. qPCR results demonstrated a significant increase in the transcriptional level of NOS2 in the LTG@RL + NIR group (Figure S8A). Additionally, the mRNA expression levels of anti-tumor cytokines, including TNF-α and IFN-γ, were markedly elevated compared with the control group (Figures S8B and S8C). These findings were consistent with those observed in the postsurgical melanoma model, collectively indicating that LTG@RL + NIR effectively promoted the activation of anti-tumor immunity.

Both CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells were significantly increased in the LTG@RL + NIR group (Figures S8D–S8G), a profile favorable for suppressing tumor recurrence. In the LTG@RL + NIR group, CD86+ TAMs and CD86+NOS2+ TAMs increased significantly (Figures S8H–S8K), indicating enhanced M1-like polarization. Conversely, CD206+ and CD206+Arg-1+ TAMs decreased significantly (Figures S8L and S8O), reflecting attenuated M2-like polarization and reduced pro-tumorigenic activity. Furthermore, the M1/M2 ratio was significantly increased (Figure S8P), confirming the immunomodulatory capacity of LTG@RL + NIR and its ability to alleviate the immunosuppressive TME. This shift provided a critical immunological basis for the postsurgical inhibition of breast cancer recurrence. The proportion of PD-L2+ TAMs was markedly decreased (Figures S8Q and S8R), further supporting the reprogramming of the TME toward an immunostimulatory state. In summary, the potent efficacy of LTG@RL + NIR in preventing postsurgical breast cancer recurrence was closely linked to its ability to enhance anti-tumor immune activation, which promotes M1-like TAM polarization while suppressing M2-like TAMs, ultimately leading to enhanced T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity.

Evaluation of the antitumor efficacy of LTG@RL in orthotopic melanoma model

The therapeutic strategy of LTG@RL was designed to target the fatty acid-rich TME, a feature not only exacerbated by surgical trauma but also inherently present in tumors that infiltrate adipose tissues like melanoma. To evaluate its efficacy in this native lipid-rich setting without the confounding factors of surgery, we established an orthotopic B16-Luc melanoma model without surgical intervention (Figure S9A). Tumor growth was tracked via bioluminescence (Figures S9B and S9C). LTG@RL + NIR achieved near-complete tumor inhibition and the highest efficacy. This remarkable efficacy was likely attributable to a synergistic mechanism. First, the photothermal effect induced tumor cell lysis and then R@LOX catalyzed the generation of LPO to promote the ferroptosis of tumor cells. Simultaneously, R@LOX may improve the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment through a coordinated lipid intervention strategy, promoting M1-like TAM polarization and alleviating immunosuppression.

LTG@RL + NIR induced the most extensive tissue damage, lowest tumor cell density, robust apoptosis, and suppressed proliferation (Figure S9D). In addition, in the LTG@RL + NIR group, the elevated 4-HNE level, upregulated 4-HNE level, and decreased GPX4 level indicated the GPX4 autophagy-induced ferroptosis. Immunofluorescence showed increased CD8+ T cell infiltration in LTG@RL + NIR, indicating boosted anti-tumor immunity. Blood tests and H&E staining of major organs showed no abnormalities (Figures S10A and S10B), confirming good biosafety. Flow cytometry studies showed that LTG@RL + NIR significantly increased tumor-infiltrating TAMs (CD11b+F4/80+), CD86+ TAMs, mature dendritic cells (CD11c+CD86+), and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (CD3+CD8+) (Figures S10C–S10F). Collectively, LTG@RL + NIR eliminated tumors in vivo by synergistically inducing ferroptosis and activating anti-tumor immunity.

Discussion

Effective prevention of postsurgical tumor recurrence is crucial for patient prognosis. In this study, we first uncovered that the fatty acid-enriched TME drives lipid accumulation in macrophages, promoting their M2-like polarization and contributing to immunosuppression. This finding elucidates the metabolic basis of immune evasion within the surgical margin. Building on this, we developed LTG@RL, an LTG system designed for a coordinated lipid intervention strategy. Unlike conventional thermosensitive hydrogels, LTG@RL possesses a unique upper critical solution temperature, enabling a reversible sol-gel transition. This characteristic allows the material to transition into a sol state under photothermal heating, significantly enhancing its fluidity to penetrate irregular surgical margins and microcavities, thereby improving drug delivery efficiency. Through the coordinated lipid intervention strategy, LTG@RL not only catalyzed the peroxidation of free fatty acids to produce LPO, inducing ferroptosis in residual tumor cells, but also enhanced LD metabolism in TAMs, repolarizing M2 macrophages to the anti-tumor M1 phenotype and activating innate immunity. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that LTG@RL effectively suppressed tumor growth in both melanoma and breast cancer models, delayed postoperative recurrence, and prolonged the survival of tumor-bearing mice. Leveraging the unstained control, compensation control, and viability dye control, we performed in vivo immune cell profiling via flow cytometry and demonstrated that LTG@RL significantly improved the tumor immune microenvironment. To further interrogate immune cell subset differentiation, we consider fluorescence minus one as a complementary strategy for rigorous marker discrimination in the future. Notably, LTG@RL retained potent antitumor activity even in non-surgical orthotopic melanoma models. Overall, the development of LTG@RL represents a promising strategy for postoperative tumor management, offering a synergistic solution that integrates adaptive drug delivery with metabolic reprogramming and immunomodulation.

Limitations of the study

Although LTG@RL demonstrates robust anti-recurrence efficacy in murine melanoma and breast cancer models, some critical limitations must be addressed to support clinical translation. First, current preclinical validation is confined to rodent models. Interspecies disparities in tumor pathophysiology and antitumor immunity necessitate rigorous evaluation in patient-derived xenograft models or physiologically relevant large-animal models to strengthen translational predictivity. Besides, the in vivo pharmacokinetics of the hydrogel were not characterized, limiting the linkage of release profiles to therapeutic efficacy. For example, as a multitargeting molecule, the released concentration of Ra might influence its mechanisms, and off-target effects cannot be ruled out. Comprehensive resolution of these challenges is essential to establish a scientifically rigorous foundation for advancing this synergistic lipid-targeted strategy toward clinical application.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell line and cell culture

B16 melanoma cells (FH0363), B16-Luc melanoma cells (FH1154), 4T1-Luc breast cancer cells (FH1114), and RAW 264.7 macrophage cells (FH0328) were purchased from Fuheng Biology (Shanghai, China). Both B16 melanoma cells, B16-Luc melanoma cells and 4T1-Luc breast cancer cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin. RAW264.7 cells were cultured in high glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin. BMDMs were derived from male C57BL/6 mice (aged 3–5 weeks) and were cultured in a high glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 mg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin and 10 ng/mL M-CSF. The cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cell line identity was validated using STR analysis. All cell lines were regularly tested negative for mycoplasma by PCR.

Animals

Experiments using mice and rats were approved by the ethics committee on animal care at Sun Yat-sen University Laboratory Animal Center and were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal care at Sun Yat-sen University Laboratory Animal Center (approval No. SYSU-IACUC-2023-001676 and SYSU-IACUC-2026-B1337). Male C57BL/6 mice (aged 3–5 weeks), male Sprague-Dawley (SD, weight 250–300 g) and female BALB/c mice (aged 5–8 weeks) were obtained from Sun Yat-sen University Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou China). All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and provided with standard laboratory chow. Based on no study reports indicating sex-specific differences in lipoxygenase and rapamycin responses in this mouse model, sex was not considered as a biological variable.

Method details

The transcriptomic analysis of RAW264.7 and B16 after different treatments

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in a 5-cm cell culture dish (1×106 per dish) and cultured for 24 h. The old culture medium was discarded, and a new medium containing LA at a concentration of 100 μg/mL was added. RAW264.7 cells were subsequently cultivated under these conditions up to the third generation. Subsequently, the cell pellet was collected and washed twice with an appropriate amount of sterile PBS. Then, an appropriate volume of TriZol lysis buffer (purchased from Ambion) was added, and the mixture was repeatedly pipetted until thorough lysis was achieved. Finally, it was transferred and stored at −80°C. The transcriptomics analysis experiment was accomplished by Hangzhou Lianchuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

B16 cells were seeded in a 5-cm cell culture dish (1×106 per dish) and cultured for 24 h. The old culture medium was discarded, and a new medium containing LA and R@LOX was added. The cell pellet was collected by centrifugation following digestion. Subsequently, the cell pellet was washed twice with an appropriate amount of sterile PBS. An appropriate volume of TriZol lysis buffer was added, and the mixture was repeatedly pipetted until thorough lysis was achieved. Finally, it was transferred and stored at −80°C. The transcriptomics analysis experiment was accomplished by Hangzhou Lianchuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

The biocatalytic capacity of LOX

The sterile B16 cell lysate was prepared by freezing-thawing cycles three times in a solution containing 2.0 × 107 B16 cells/mL. In the 96-well plate, 50 μL CL was added to each well, followed by the addition of LOX (25 μg/mL). Then, using BODIPY 581/591 as a fluorescent probe of LPO, the fluorescence intensity of BODIPY 581/591 was detected continuously at 37°C for 30 min.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

B16 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1.2 × 104 per well for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with different drugs at different concentrations. After another 24 h, the cell viability was detected by CCK8 assay.

Preparation and characterization of R@LOX nanomodulator

The R@LOX nanomodulator was prepared by a simple one-pot method. Briefly, Ra was dissolved in ethanol to 50 mM, while LOX was dissolved in distilled water to 5 mg/mL. The LOX (2 mL) solution was added into a small beaker, and 96 μL Ra solution was slowly added at the stirring speed of 1200 r/min, and the system was further stirred for 2 h to obtain the R@LOX nanomodulator.

The particle size and zeta potential of the R@LOX were measured by the Malvern Mastersizer 2000 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). The morphology of R@LOX was detected by Transmission electron microscope (HT7800, HITACHI Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), while the R@LOX were subjected to FTIR analysis by an FTIR spectrometer (UATR Two, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MD).

Cell death form confirmation

To study the specific cell death pathway induced by R@LOX, different cell death pathway inhibitors, including Fer-1 (ferroptosis inhibitor, 2 μM), DFO (ferroptosis inhibitor, 100 μM), NAC (antioxidants, 5 mM), CQ (autophagy inhibitor, 4 μg/mL), Z-VAD-FMK (ZVF, apoptosis inhibitor, 12.5 μM), 3-MA (autophagy inhibitor, 12.5 μM) and Necrostatin-1 (Ne-1, necrosis inhibitor, 40 μM), was employed for the combination of R@LOX to measure the cell viability by CCK8 assay.

The B16 cells’ uptake of R@LOX nanomodulator

The LOX (10 mg) was added to 5 mL NaHCO3 solution (8.4 mg/mL), then 1.9 mg Cy5.5-NHS was dissolved in 250 μL DMSO, and 250 μL Cy5.5-NHS solution was added to the above 5 mL LOX NaHCO3 solution. It was stirred at 4°C overnight. The obtained solution was put into a 30 kD dialysis bag and stirred in 1 L NaHCO3 solution (0.05 mg/mL) for dialysis for 48 h, during which the dialysate was changed five times. Finally, the final product Cy5.5-LOX was obtained by vacuum lyophilization. Cy5.5-LOX was used to prepare R@Cy5.5-LOX nanomodulator.

B16 cells were seeded in glass-bottom dishes at a density of 1.0 × 105 per dish for 24 h. The original culture medium was sucked off and cultured in a serum-free medium containing R@LOX for 1, 2, 4, and 6 h. Then, after discarding the culture medium containing R@LOX, the cells were gently washed 3 times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After 4% paraformaldehyde was discarded, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS to remove the fixative, and the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 staining solution 150 μL at room temperature for 10 min. The Hoechst solution was discarded, washed 3 times with PBS, and finally observed with a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Cellular TEM morphology analysis

B16 cells were seeded in a 5-cm cell culture dish (1 × 106 per dish) and cultured for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with 12 μM Ra + 100 μg/mL LA, 25 μg/mL LOX + 100 μg/mL LA, and 12 μM R@LOX + 100 μg/mL LA for another 24 h, respectively. Then, the cells were washed and harvested gently, which was then fixed with fixed by 2.5% glutaraldehyde. The samples were subjected to Bio-TEM (JEM1400, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Cell surface morphology assay

To investigate the cell surface morphology of B16 cells, the atomic force microscope (AFM) was employed. Briefly, B16 cells were seeded in a 5-cm cell culture dish (1 × 106 per dish) and cultured for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with 12 μM Ra + 100 μg/mL LA, 25 μg/mL LOX + 100 μg/mL LA, and 12 μM R@LOX + 100 μg/mL LA for another 24 h, respectively. The cells were then carefully washed with PBS three times and then fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde. The excessive glutaraldehyde was washed with distilled water carefully and dried naturally overnight. AFM images were obtained in peak force tapping mode at room temperature using a Dimension Fastscan AFM apparatus (Bruker Corp., Germany).

R@LOX oxidation capacity assay

To detect the LPO level produced by R@LOX, B16 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 6 × 105 per well and cultured for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with 12 μM Ra, 25 μg/mL LOX, and 12 μM R@LOX for another 24 h, respectively, in the presence or absence of LA (100 μg/mL). The drug-containing culture medium was removed and the cells were gently washed with PBS 3 times. Medium containing an appropriate volume of 2.5 μM BODIPY 581/591 was added and continued to be cultured in the incubator for 30 min away from light. Then, the cells were gently washed with PBS 3 times, and then digested and centrifuged 3 times. The cells in each group were uniformly suspended with 500 μL PBS for cell precipitation, and the average fluorescence intensity of the cells in each group was detected by flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto, Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA).

B16 cells were seeded in glass-bottom dishes at a density of 1.0 × 105 per dish for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with 12 μM Ra, 25 μg/mL LOX, and 12 μM R@LOX for another 24 h, respectively, in the presence or absence of LA (100 μg/mL). Then, after sucking off the culture medium containing drugs, the cells were gently washed 3 times with PBS and an appropriate volume of medium containing 10 μM DCFH-DA, a reactive oxygen species probe, was added, and the cells were cultured in the incubator for 30 min away from light. Following incubation, the cells were gently washed three times with serum-free medium to completely remove any uninternalized DCFH-DA. The fluorescence of DCF was subsequently observed using CLSM, and the average fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

GPX4 activity and GSH level assay

To investigate the GPX4 activity and GSH level in drug-treated cells, B16 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 6 × 105 per well and cultured for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with 12 μM Ra + 100 μg/mL LA, 25 μg/mL LOX + 100 μg/mL LA, and 12 μM R@LOX + 100 μg/mL LA for 24 h, respectively. Then, the cells were washed with PBS three times and lysed completely. The cell lysate was obtained by centrifugation at 4°C, 12000 g for 10 min. Then, the intracellular GPX4 activity and GSH level were determined following the instructions of the GPX4 activity assay kit and GSH level assay kit, respectively.

GPX4 autophagy assay

To investigate the mechanism of decreased GPX4 activity, Fer-1 (2 μM), DFO (100 μM), CQ (4 μg/mL), and 3-MA (12.5 μM) were employed for the combination of R@LOX to measure the GPX4 activity using the GPX4 activity assay kit.

B16 cells were seeded in glass-bottom dishes at a density of 1.0 × 105 per dish for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with 12 μM Ra + 100 μg/mL LA, 25 μg/mL LOX + 100 μg/mL LA, and 12 μM R@LOX + 100 μg/mL LA for another 24 h, respectively. Then, after discarding the culture medium containing drugs, the cells were gently washed 3 times with PBS and an appropriate volume of medium containing 50 nM LysoTracker red, a lysosome probe, was added, and the cells were cultured in the incubator for 30 min away from light. Following incubation, the cells were gently washed three times with PBS. The fluorescence of LysoTracker red was subsequently observed using CLSM, and the average fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

To visualize the level of autolysosomes in cells, the Bio-TEM method was employed. B16 cells were seeded in a 5-cm cell culture dish (1×106 per dish) and cultured for 24 h. Western blot

The western blot method (WB) was utilized to analyze the specific protein expression level. Briefly, the cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 6 × 105 per well and cultured for 24 h. Effect of intracellular fatty acid level on the polarization of RAW264.7

To investigate the effect of intracellular fatty acid level on the polarization of RAW264.7, the polarization of RAW264.7 into the M2 phenotype was induced by IL-4 (20 ng/mL) and IL-10 (20 ng/mL). Preparation and characterization of LTG

Gelatin, hyaluronic acid, tannic acid, and FCH were dissolved in distilled water to 100 mg/mL, 10 mg/mL, 100 mg/mL, and 120 mg/mL, respectively. Gelatin and hyaluronic acid were evenly mixed, and then tannic acid was added to the above mixing system and fully stirred, and finally FCH was added and stirred evenly to obtain the gel-state formulations. The rheological properties of all hydrogels were investigated at 25°C by HAAKE MARS 40/60 Rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd., Massachusetts, America), using a rotor (P20/Ti/SB). According to gelatin: hyaluronic acid: tannic acid: FCH=0.6:0.4:0.06:0.1, gelatin and hyaluronic acid were mixed evenly to obtain GH, gelatin and hyaluronic acid were mixed evenly and then tannic acid was added to obtain GHT, gelatin and hyaluronic acid were mixed evenly and then FCH was added to obtain GHF. A steady shear-rate sweep was performed as a function of the shear rate from 10−1 to 102 s−1. The viscoelastic tests of GH, GHT, GHF and LTG were evaluated to record both G′ and G'' with an angular strain setup from 10−1% to 103% at the constant frequency of 1 Hz. The frequency-sweep measurement was conducted on GH, GHT, GHF and LTG to record both G′ and G″ with an angular frequency setup from 10−1 to 101 Hz at a constant 1% strain.

Evaluation of light-driven properties of LTG

GH, GHF, GHT, and LTG hydrogels were subjected to irradiation with an 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for a duration of 5 min, during which the temperature increase of each hydrogel in vitro was monitored using the infrared thermal camera (TiS75, Fluke, USA). C57BL/6 mice received subcutaneous injections of LTG with 0.5 mL and were irradiated with different powers (0.5 W/cm2 and 1 W/cm2) lasers, respectively. And the resulting temperature of LTG in vivo was recorded utilizing the same infrared thermal camera.

To detect the “On-off” photothermal effects of LTG, LTG was subjected to irradiation with the 808 nm laser (0.5 W/cm2) for a duration of 5 min, and then the laser was turned off to allow LTG to cool down naturally. This procedure was conducted five times and was documented using the infrared thermal camera.

As presented in Table S1, a series of hydrogels with varying proportions of gelatin and hyaluronic acid were prepared, and the G′ of these hydrogels was evaluated at different temperatures using the rotational rheometer. To investigate the alterations in the rheological properties of LTG before and after laser irradiation (0.5 W/cm2), the variations in G′ and G″ with respect to frequency (10−1 to 101 Hz at constant 1% strain) and strain (10−2% to 103%, at the constant frequency of 1 Hz) were assessed before laser exposure (LTG), following 5 min of continuous laser irradiation (NIRing), and after 1 h of non-irradiation (After NIR).

To evaluate the efficacy of LTG in covering irregular wounds, both in vitro models with irregular shapes and in vivo assessments of irregular wounds were employed. The volume of LTG administered was 0.5 mL.

In vivo antitumor efficiency of LTG@RL

To assess the antitumor efficiency of LTG@RL, a B16-Luc-tumor-bearing mouse model was established. A total of 30 B16-Luc tumor-bearing mice were utilized for comparison of the antitumor effects among different hydrogels, and they were divided into six groups once the tumor volume reached 150 mm3 (n = 5): control, LTG@RL, LTG + near infrared irradiation (NIR), LTG@L + NIR, LTG@R + NIR, and LTG@RL + NIR. The mice were treated with different hydrogels on day 0 and day 2, and the mice tumors were monitored using an In vivo Image System (PerkinElmer, IVIS Lumina Series Ⅲ, America) every 2 days. A solution of luciferin potassium salt (15 mg/mL) was intraperitoneally administered to the mice in doses of 0.2 mL each. After a waiting period of 10 min, the mice were placed in the In vivo Image System chamber in a prone position. The bioluminescence at the tumor lesion was captured and quantified. Then, the mice were sacrificed on day 10, and the tumors were excised, sliced and stained with Ki-67, LC3, 4-HNE, GPX4, H&E, CD8 and Tunel. In addition, the tumors were homogenized to a single-cell suspension for further immune cell analysis.

To investigate whether tumor resection leads to an increase in free fatty acid levels, six melanoma-bearing mice were divided into two groups. In the control group, only the skin layer was incised, followed by rinsing the wound with 1 mL of normal saline and collecting the rinse fluid; this procedure was repeated three times. In the surgical group, the melanoma was partially excised, after which the lesion site was rinsed with 1 mL of normal saline, and the rinse fluid was collected; this procedure was repeated three times. Finally, the concentration of free fatty acids in the collected samples was determined using a free fatty acid detection kit.

We further investigated the differences in drug delivery efficiency between LTG and conventional thermo-responsive hydrogels to postoperative tumor lesions. Specifically, the commonly used thermo-responsive polymers poloxamer 188 (P188) and poloxamer 407 (P407) were selected to formulate a thermosensitive hydrogel (P188:P407 = 1%:18%), which served as the control formulation. Both LTG and the P188/407 hydrogel were loaded with 20 μM of the fluorescent dye DID and administered locally into the surgical tumor bed of melanoma-bearing mice. Mice in the LTG group received no NIR irradiation; those in the LTG+NIR group were exposed to a single NIR treatment on day 1; and animals in the LTG+2×NIR group received NIR on both day 1 and day 2. On day 3, tumor tissues were harvested from all groups, sectioned at a depth of 4 mm from the resection margin, and analyzed under a fluorescence microscope to assess the spatial distribution of fluorescence.

Furthermore, given the potential for NIR treatment to induce thermal damage to murine skin tissue, a biosafety evaluation was conducted to assess the effects of elevated temperatures on skin integrity. Specifically, 0.5 mL of LTG was administered subcutaneously in healthy C57BL/6 mice, followed by NIR at the injection site using a 0.5 W/cm2 laser for 5 min to maintain the intended therapeutic temperature. Skin samples were subsequently collected on day 1, day 3, and day 7 post-treatment for immunohistochemical analyses, including H&E, Ki-67, Masson’s trichrome, TNF-α, and VEGF staining.

In vivo antitumor recurrence effect of LTG@RL

To assess the antitumor recurrence efficiency of LTG@RL, a B16-Luc-tumor postoperative mouse model was established. Briefly, upon reaching a tumor volume of 150 mm3, 90% of the tumor was surgically excised, and the mice with the residual 10% were utilized as models for tumor recurrence. The mice were divided into four groups: control, LTG+NIR, LTG@RL and LTG@RL + NIR. The control group was administered normal saline. The LTG+NIR group received LTG administration combined with NIR irradiation on days 0 and 1. The LTG@RL group was treated with LTG@RL alone, while the LTG@RL + NIR group received LTG@RL in combination with NIR irradiation on day 0 and day 1. The bioluminescence of the mice tumors was monitored using an In vivo Image System every 2 days. Then, the mice were sacrificed on day 11, and the tumors were excised, sliced and stained with LC3, 4-HNE, GPX4, H&E, CD4, CD8, CD86 and CD206. In addition, the tumors were homogenized to a single-cell suspension for further immune cell analysis, and BODIPY 505/515 fluorescent probes were additionally introduced into the single-cell suspension of the tumors to assess the LD levels in TAMs.

Analysis of immune cells

The excised tumors were homogenized to single-cell suspensions. Then, the cells were washed and stained with multiple antibodies: anti-CD4 (Lot: 563151), anti-CD86 (Lot: 105008), anti-CD45 (Lot: 557659), anti-CD16/32 (Lot: 14–0161-81), anti-CD11b (Lot: 550993), anti-CD80 (Lot: 104713), anti-CD3 (Lot: 553061), anti-CD206 (Lot: 17–2061-82), anti-CD8 (Lot: 552877), anti-F4/80 (Lot: 565411), anti-PD-L2 (Lot: 5163202), anti-Arg-1 (Lot: 2892491), anti-NOS2 (Lot: 3234511) and anti-CD11c (Lot: 117306). We employed multiple flow cytometry controls, including the unstained control, compensation control, and viability dye control, to rigorously distinguish positive from negative cell populations and thereby ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the gating strategy used for immune cell separation. For intercellular CD206 analysis, cells were fixed and permeabilized before being stained with antibodies. All cells were tested via flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto, Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA).
