MedBeads: An Agent-Native, Immutable Data Substrate for Trustworthy Medical AI
Takahito Nakajima

Abstract
Background: As of 2026, Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate expert-level performance on medical knowledge benchmarks. However, deploying these models as autonomous “Clinical Agents” in real-world healthcare settings remains fundamentally limited. Current Electronic Medical Records (EMRs) and interoperability standards like FHIR are designed for human review and administrative optimization. This creates a critical structural mismatch: AI agents receive fragmented, context-poor data that forces them to rely on probabilistic inference—such as Vector Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)—to reconstruct patient history. This probabilistic approach is a root cause of “hallucinations” and renders post-hoc auditability nearly impossible. Methods: We propose MedBeads , an agent-native data infrastructure that fundamentally redefines how medical records are structured, stored, and accessed. MedBeads represents clinical events as immutable “Beads”—nodes in a Merkle Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)—that cryptographically reference their causal predecessors. This architecture enforces a “write-once, read-many” paradigm, making data tampering mathematically detectable. We implemented a prototype system consisting of a high-performance Core Engine in Go, a model-agnostic Python middleware for LLM integration, and a React-based clinical visualization interface. Results: We successfully implemented the end-to-end workflow using synthetic patient data. The FHIR-to-DAG conversion transformed flat, reference-based resources into a causally-linked graph structure. The Context Retrieval algorithm, implemented via Breadth-First Search (BFS), traverses relevant patient subgraphs with O ⁡ ( V + E ) O(V+E) complexity where V V and E E denote the nodes and edges within the depth-limited subgraph—enabling efficient real-time clinical decision support. Tamper-evidence is guaranteed by design: any modification to a historical Bead breaks the cryptographic chain of its descendants. The graph visualization is designed to aid clinician understanding of AI reasoning through explicit causal links. Conclusion: MedBeads addresses the fundamental “Context Mismatch” limiting Medical AI. By shifting from probabilistic search to deterministic graph traversal, and from mutable records to immutable chains, it provides the necessary substrate for “Trustworthy Medical AI.” The architecture enforces a clear separation of concerns: MedBeads guarantees what context the AI receives (deterministic, complete, tamper-evident), while the LLM determines how to interpret it. Furthermore, the structured Bead format serves as a token-efficient “AI-native language,” potentially reducing context consumption compared to verbose natural language records. We release MedBeads as open-source software to accelerate the development of agent-native data standards in healthcare.

1
Introduction

1.1
The Promise and Peril of Medical AI Agents

The year 2026 marks a pivotal moment in healthcare informatics. Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4, Gemini, and Claude have achieved—and in many cases surpassed—human expert performance on standardized medical licensing examinations (USMLE, JMLE) and clinical reasoning benchmarks. These models possess an encyclopedic knowledge of pathophysiology, pharmacology, and clinical guidelines that would take a human physician decades to accumulate.

Yet, a paradox emerges: despite this formidable knowledge, deploying LLMs as truly autonomous “Clinical Agents” remains fraught with risk. While recent initiatives like
AIPatient

[
8
]
have demonstrated the potential of agentic workflows using Knowledge Graphs and RAG for medical training, deploying such systems in real-world clinical settings requires a level of robust grounding that current data infrastructures cannot provide. Reports of AI “hallucinations”—confident but factually incorrect statements—continue to surface, eroding clinician trust and raising serious patient safety concerns.

Why does knowledge alone fail to translate into reliable clinical action?

1.2
The “Context Mismatch” Problem

We propose that the core limitation is not the AI’s knowledge, but the
structure of the data it receives
. Current Electronic Medical Records (EMRs) and interoperability standards like HL7 FHIR
[
5
]
are designed with a singular purpose: to facilitate human review and administrative workflows. Data is organized into discrete “Resources” (Patients, Encounters, Observations, etc.) linked by mutable reference strings.

This design works well for human users who can mentally reconstruct context from fragmented information. However, for an AI agent, this structure presents critical challenges:

1.

Context is Implicit, Not Explicit:
When a physician reviews a chart, they subconsciously chain together “Lab result A was abnormal
\rightarrow
Diagnosis B was made
\rightarrow
Medication C was prescribed.” This causal chain is
inferred
from timestamps and narrative notes, not
encoded
in the data structure itself. An AI must guess this chain, introducing uncertainty.

2.

History is Mutable:
Traditional databases are optimized for the “Current State.” Historical data exists as supplementary audit logs that can, in principle, be altered or deleted. This makes it impossible to mathematically prove what an AI “saw” and “reasoned” at a specific point in time—a critical requirement for regulatory compliance and malpractice investigations.

3.

Retrieval is Probabilistic:
The dominant paradigm for providing LLMs with external knowledge is Vector RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)
[
7
]
. RAG embeds documents into a high-dimensional vector space and retrieves semantically similar chunks. While powerful for general Q&A, this probabilistic approach is dangerous in medicine. A query for “chest pain” might retrieve “angina from 2020” and “rib fracture from yesterday” with similar relevance scores, leading the AI to confuse distinct clinical episodes.

We term this collection of issues the
“Context Mismatch”
: the fundamental incompatibility between how medical data is currently structured (for humans) and how AI agents need to consume it (with explicit causality and guaranteed integrity).

Figure 1:
The Context Mismatch visualized. (Left) FHIR’s resource-based model with relational references requires complex querying and provides no inherent causality. (Right) MedBeads’ Merkle DAG model embeds temporal and causal context directly into the data structure, creating an immutable, AI-traversable history.

1.3
A Structural Solution: The Merkle DAG Paradigm

To resolve this mismatch, we turn to a proven technology from distributed systems: the
Merkle Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)
. This data structure, which underpins systems like Git (for version control) and IPFS (for content-addressed storage), possesses three properties essential for trustworthy AI:

1.

Cryptographic Immutability:
Each node (a “Bead” in our terminology) is identified by the SHA-256 hash of its content. As explored by
Zhu et al.

[
17
]
and in the
HealthLink
system
[
1
]
, employing Merkle structures in EMRs ensures that any modification—even a single byte—produces a completely different hash, making tampering instantly detectable. Recent work by
Lakshmanan et al.

[
6
]
further validates this approach for privacy-preserving health data management.

2.

Explicit Causal Linking:
Each Bead contains a list of “parent” hashes, explicitly encoding its causal or temporal dependencies. Instead of inferring that “Observation X led to Diagnosis Y,” this relationship is
physically embedded
in the data.

3.

Token Efficiency as an AI-Native Language:
Natural language (English, Japanese, etc.) consumes substantial tokens when passed to LLMs, especially when including historical context. MedBeads’ structured DAG representation serves as a
compressed semantic language
for AI communication—encoding the same clinical meaning in fewer tokens while preserving causal relationships. This efficiency becomes critical as patient histories grow over time.

1.4
Proposal: MedBeads

We present
MedBeads
(Medical Beads), an agent-native data infrastructure designed from first principles for AI consumption.

Core Thesis:
By restructuring medical records as a Merkle DAG, we transform data from a “Database for Search” into a “Trusted Memory for AI.” The AI no longer
infers
context probabilistically; it
traverses
a graph of explicit causal links. It no longer
hopes
data hasn’t been tampered with; it
verifies
integrity cryptographically.

The contributions of this paper are:

1.

A formal definition of the “Bead” data structure and its properties.

2.

An open-source reference implementation (Go Core, Python Middleware, React UI).

3.

Validation using synthetic FHIR data (Synthea), demonstrating FHIR-to-DAG conversion and efficient context retrieval.

4.

A discussion of the paradigm shift from “Probabilistic Search” to “Deterministic Traversal” and its implications for trustworthy AI.

2
Background: Why Current Approaches Fall Short

Before detailing MedBeads, we analyze the limitations of existing paradigms.

2.1
The FHIR Standard: Interoperability for Humans, Not Agents

HL7 FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) represents a significant advancement in healthcare data exchange. Its RESTful architecture and well-defined resources (Patient, Encounter, Observation, etc.) have become the de facto standard for modern EHR integration.

However, FHIR’s design goals are fundamentally human-centric:

•

Administrative Optimization:
FHIR excels at answering questions like “List all encounters for Patient X” or “Get the latest lab results.” These are queries a human administrator or dashboard would pose.

•

Reference-Based Linking:
Relationships between resources are expressed as string references (e.g.,
"subject": "Patient/123"
). These are mutable pointers, not immutable cryptographic links. As noted by
Mandel et al.

[
10
,
9
]
, while SMART on FHIR enables app portability and recent MCP integrations allow agents to query this data, the underlying storage layer does not inherently prevent “history rewriting” or guarantee the causal integrity required for autonomous agent auditability.

•

Flat Topology:
FHIR resources exist as a relatively flat collection. While references create a relational structure, there is no inherent
graph
that can be efficiently traversed for causal analysis.

For an AI agent needing to understand “Why was this medication prescribed?”, FHIR requires multiple round-trip queries, complex JOIN logic, and ultimately relies on timestamp inference to guess causality.

2.2
Vector RAG: Powerful but Probabilistic

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as the dominant pattern for grounding LLMs in external data. The workflow is:

1.

Embed documents (e.g., clinical notes) into a vector space.

2.

At query time, embed the user’s question and find semantically similar documents.

3.

Inject retrieved documents into the LLM’s prompt as context.

RAG dramatically reduces hallucination compared to zero-shot generation. However, its probabilistic nature introduces critical failure modes in healthcare:

•

Semantic Confusion:
A query about “heart failure” might retrieve documents about both “congestive heart failure” (a chronic condition) and “cardiac arrest” (an acute emergency), conflating distinct clinical concepts.

•

Temporal Blindness:
Vector similarity does not encode time. A note from 10 years ago may be equally (or more) similar to the query than a note from yesterday, leading the AI to confuse historical context with current reality.

•

No Causal Guarantee:
RAG retrieves
related
information, not
causally linked
information. The AI receives a bag of potentially relevant documents, not a chain of reasoning.

2.3
The Auditability Imperative

As AI moves from advisory roles to autonomous decision-making, regulatory and legal frameworks demand robust auditability. Healthcare organizations must be able to answer:

•

“What data did the AI agent see when it made recommendation X?”

•

“Has this data been modified since the AI accessed it?”

•

“Can we reproduce the AI’s reasoning path?”

Traditional databases, even with audit logs, cannot provide cryptographic proof of data integrity at a specific point in time. While blockchain-based solutions and Soul-bound Tokens
[
15
,
13
]
have been proposed to secure audit trails, they often exist as separate layers on top of mutable EMRs. MedBeads integrates this integrity directly into the data structure itself.

3.2
The “Bead” Data Structure

3.2.1
Formal Definition

A Bead is the atomic unit of information in MedBeads. Formally:

B=\{T,\tau,A,P,C,E,\Sigma\}

(1)

Where:

•

T
: ISO 8601 timestamp indicating when the event was recorded.

•

\tau
: Type string categorizing the event (e.g.,
observation
,
encounter
,
reasoning
).

•

A
: Author identifier using Decentralized Identifier (DID) format for provenance tracking.

•

P
: A set of parent Bead IDs
\{h_{1},h_{2},...,h_{n}\}
, establishing causal/temporal dependencies.

•

C
: Content payload (JSON object) containing the semantic data.

•

E
: Evidence array, referencing external binaries (DICOM, PDF) by URI and hash.

•

\Sigma
: Digital signature for author authentication and non-repudiation.

Key design decisions reflected in this schema:

The

3.2.3
Cryptographic Identity

Upon creation, a Bead is serialized to canonical JSON (sorted keys, no whitespace). The unique identifier is computed as:

ID_{B}=\text{SHA256}(\text{CanonicalJSON}(B))

(2)

This hash serves dual purposes:

1.

Addressing:
The Bead’s storage location in CAS is derived from its hash.

2.

Integrity Proof:
Any modification to
B
produces a different hash, breaking all parent references from descendant Beads
[
11
]
.

3.3
Hybrid Storage Model

3.3.1
Content-Addressable Storage (CAS) — The Ground Truth

The CAS layer is the immutable “source of truth.” Each Bead is stored as a flat JSON file named by its hash. This design guarantees:

•

Write-Once, Read-Many:
Files are never modified after creation.

•

Tamper-Evidence:
Verification requires only recomputing the hash and comparing to the filename.

•

Portability:
The entire patient history can be backed up, moved, or replicated as simple files.

3.3.2
Storage Path Structure

To optimize file system performance at scale, Beads are organized using hash-prefix directories:

./objects/{hash_prefix}/{hash_rest}
Example: ./objects/a1/b2c3d4e5f6...

This sharding strategy prevents any single directory from accumulating excessive files, maintaining consistent I/O performance as the dataset grows. The two-character prefix creates up to 256 subdirectories, each containing a manageable subset of Beads.

3.3.3
Metadata Index (SQLite) — Ephemeral Acceleration

While CAS guarantees integrity, graph traversal requires efficient querying. We maintain a parallel SQLite index containing only metadata.

Critical Design Principle:
The SQLite index is
ephemeral
. The system includes a
ReindexStorage()
function that can rebuild the entire database by scanning the CAS directory. This means:

•

Database corruption is recoverable as long as CAS files are intact.

•

The index is a cache, not the truth.

3.5
Context Retrieval Algorithm

The core value proposition of MedBeads is deterministic context retrieval. Unlike probabilistic RAG systems that retrieve semantically similar documents, MedBeads traverses explicit causal links to construct a complete reasoning chain.

3.5.1
Bidirectional Graph Traversal

We implement two complementary traversal functions using Breadth-First Search (BFS):

1.

Ancestor Retrieval (
GetContext
):
Given a target Bead (e.g., a prescription), the algorithm traverses the DAG
upward
through parent links to collect all ancestral Beads. This answers the question: “Why did this event occur?”

2.

Descendant Retrieval (
GetBeadsByParent
):
Given a root Bead (e.g., a patient registration), the algorithm traverses
downward
to collect all child Beads. This answers: “What happened after this event?”

3.5.2
Algorithm Design

The traversal algorithm employs:

•

Depth Limiting:
A configurable
depth
parameter prevents unbounded traversal in large graphs.

•

Visited Set:
A hash map tracks visited Bead IDs to prevent cycles and redundant processing.

•

Queue-Based BFS:
Level-by-level exploration ensures that closer causal relationships are retrieved before distant ones.

•

Lazy Loading:
The AI does not need to scan the entire patient dataset. Instead, it efficiently loads only the relevant subgraph—the Beads causally connected to the query target—reducing both latency and token consumption.

3.5.3
Complexity Analysis

•

Time Complexity:

O(V+E)
where
V
is the number of Beads in the subgraph and
E
is the number of parent-child edges.

•

Space Complexity:

O(V)
for the visited set and result accumulator.

•

Index Optimization:
The
bead_edges
table provides
O(1)
lookup for parent-child relationships, avoiding full table scans.

This deterministic traversal produces a topologically-sorted subgraph containing exactly the information needed for causal reasoning—no more, no less.

3.7
LLM Integration

MedBeads provides the deterministic context infrastructure that AI agents require. While our prototype demonstrates integration with Google’s Gemini model, the architecture is fundamentally
model-agnostic
—any LLM can consume MedBeads data through the standardized API.

3.7.1
Context Injection Workflow

The integration follows a three-step workflow:

1.

Context Retrieval:
The Go Core retrieves the relevant DAG subgraph via BFS traversal.

2.

Serialization:
The Python middleware serializes Beads into a chronologically-ordered, structured format.

3.

Prompt Construction:
The serialized context is injected into the LLM prompt alongside the target event for analysis.

3.7.2
Role of the LLM

The LLM’s role is
not
data retrieval or aggregation—these are handled deterministically by the DAG infrastructure. Instead, the LLM provides:

•

Clinical Reasoning:
Interpreting causal relationships and identifying patterns across the retrieved context.

•

Risk Detection:
Flagging potential contradictions or safety concerns within the patient history.

•

Natural Language Generation:
Translating structured clinical data into human-readable explanations.

3.7.3
Design Principle: Separation of Concerns

This architecture enforces a clear separation: MedBeads guarantees
what
context the AI receives (deterministic, complete, tamper-evident), while the LLM determines
how
to interpret that context. This separation is critical for auditability—the exact input to the LLM is reproducible from the DAG.

3.8
Security and Access Control

To ensure granular privacy control compliant with clinical ethics, we introduced a
Security Clearance
mechanism. Unlike binary access control models, MedBeads implements a role-based “deny-list” embedded directly within each Bead. The system defines nine distinct viewer roles, including Patient, Family, Primary Care, Specialist, Insurance, and Emergency.

This embedding allows for context-aware visibility management; for example, a sensitive psychiatric observation can include a clearance rule that explicitly denies access to the “Insurance” and “Family” roles while maintaining availability for “Specialist” and “Emergency” contexts. Because this rule is part of the immutable Bead, the access policy itself is tamper-evident. The filtering is enforced at the Core retrieval layer (
O(N)
filter complexity), ensuring that both AI agents and human users receive only the data subgraph authorized for their specific role. This capability is essential for multi-stakeholder environments where data transparency must be balanced with patient privacy.

4.3
Theoretical Performance Analysis

The MedBeads architecture is designed for scalability and low-latency access:

1.

Write Operations (Save):

O(1)
. Writing to CAS involves hashing content and saving a file. This is an append-only operation that does not require locking the entire database.

2.

Direct Retrieval (Get):

O(1)
. Fetching a Bead by its hash is a direct file system lookup (or key-value store lookup in distributed implementations).

3.

Context Retrieval (Traversal):

O(V+E)
for the subgraph, where V is the number of relevant beads and E is the number of edges. By limiting depth and using efficient graph traversal (BFS), relevant context can be extracted without scanning unrelated records.

This architectural efficiency suggests that the system can support real-time clinical decision support applications.

4.4
Tamper-Evidence by Design

The security interaction of MedBeads is guaranteed by the properties of the Merkle DAG structure:

•

Cryptographic Dependency:
Because every Bead includes the hash of its parents, the hash of any given Bead effectively sums the entire history leading up to it.

•

Immediate Detection:
Any alteration to a historical record (e.g., changing a glucose value) would alter its hash. This would cause a mismatch with the
parents
field of all subsequent Beads, breaking the chain.

•

Verification:

5.2
Structural Constraint as a Trust Mechanism

We propose that trustworthy AI is not solely about “smarter models” but about
constraining the environment in which models operate
.
MedBeads imposes structural constraints:

1.

Context Constraint:
The LLM receives only the validated ancestral subgraph.

2.

Integrity Constraint:
The data’s authenticity is cryptographically guaranteed.

3.

Traceability Constraint:
Every AI output can be linked to its input DAG.

5.3
Toward an AI-Native Language for Healthcare

Beyond data integrity, MedBeads introduces the concept of
structured clinical data as a language optimized for AI consumption
. Traditional EMRs store information in natural language (clinical notes, reports), which LLMs must tokenize character-by-character. This approach has two fundamental inefficiencies:

1.

Redundant Encoding:
Natural language contains grammatical structures, articles, and verbose descriptions that convey little semantic value to an AI parsing clinical facts.

2.

Context Window Pressure:
As patient histories grow over years or decades, the accumulated text can exceed LLM context limits, forcing lossy summarization or truncation.

MedBeads addresses this by treating the DAG structure itself as a
semantic protocol
—a compressed representation where clinical meaning is encoded in typed fields and explicit relationships rather than prose. This design philosophy aligns with the emerging need for “agent-native” data formats that prioritize machine interpretability alongside human readability.

5.4
Separation of Data and Reasoning

A critical architectural insight from MedBeads is the strict separation between
data infrastructure
and
reasoning engine
. MedBeads does not perform clinical reasoning—it guarantees the
quality and completeness
of context delivered to any reasoning system, whether an LLM, a rule-based engine, or a human clinician.

This separation has practical implications:

•

Model Agnosticism:
Healthcare institutions can upgrade or switch LLM providers without restructuring their data layer.

•

Regulatory Clarity:
The data layer (MedBeads) and reasoning layer (LLM) can be validated and certified independently.

•

Reproducible Audits:
Given the same DAG input, any compliant reasoning system should produce consistent outputs, enabling forensic analysis of AI decisions.

5.5
Limitations

5.5.1
Implementation Challenges

Existing EMR data rarely contains explicit causal links. Migration to MedBeads requires rule-based heuristics or AI-assisted link inference, and the validity of inferred links remains an open question. Additionally, the immutability architecture complicates error correction—if a diagnosis is later found incorrect, the original Bead cannot be deleted; instead, a “Correction” Bead must be appended following Git’s model, requiring careful UX design. Governance of the open format, including Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) for author authentication and consensus mechanisms for multi-institutional DAGs, remains a topic for future investigation.

5.5.2
LLM Performance Evaluation

This paper presents the architectural design and theoretical analysis of MedBeads, but does not include empirical evaluation of LLM performance when consuming MedBeads data. Future work will quantitatively compare: (1) token efficiency between natural language records and structured DAG representations, (2) hallucination rates between RAG-based and DAG-based context retrieval, and (3) clinical reasoning accuracy through expert evaluation. Such empirical validation is planned for subsequent publication.

5.5.3
Standardized Interface and Extensibility

The prototype has been verified to operate as a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server. MCP server implementation enables multi-agent collaboration where specialized AI agents (diagnostic support, prescription checking, image analysis, etc.) share the same DAG. Furthermore, it provides LLM-agnostic API access through a common protocol format, and the combination of tool invocation history with DAG immutability enables complete audit trails. Future work will explore multimodal extensions that position image data within the DAG’s causal context, providing Vision Language Models (VLMs) with integrated pixel data and clinical context.

We believe agent-native data formats cannot be proprietary. Open-sourcing MedBeads aims to establish a common “grammar” for AI-EMR integration, encourage vendor-specific adapters, and enable academic research.

7
Conclusion

We have presented
MedBeads
, a data infrastructure designed for the age of autonomous Medical AI. By restructuring clinical records as a Merkle DAG, we address the fundamental “Context Mismatch” that limits current AI deployment.

Our key contributions are threefold:

1.

Deterministic Context Infrastructure:
MedBeads replaces probabilistic RAG retrieval with deterministic graph traversal. The BFS-based Context Retrieval algorithm guarantees that given the same query, the same context is always returned—a prerequisite for reproducible AI reasoning.

2.

Separation of Data and Reasoning:
The architecture enforces a clear division of responsibilities. MedBeads guarantees
what
context the AI receives (complete, causally-ordered, tamper-evident), while the LLM determines
how
to interpret that context. This separation is critical for auditability: the exact input to any AI inference can be reproduced from the DAG.

3.

Token Efficiency as an AI-Native Language:
The structured Bead format offers a potential path toward more efficient LLM communication. By encoding clinical information in a compact, schema-defined structure rather than verbose natural language, MedBeads may reduce token consumption while preserving semantic completeness.

Our prototype demonstrates technical feasibility across all three architectural layers. As AI moves from advisory to autonomous roles in healthcare, the infrastructure must evolve to match. MedBeads represents a first step toward that future: a world where AI agents are not just intelligent, but
trustworthy
—explainable, auditable, and resistant to hallucination.
