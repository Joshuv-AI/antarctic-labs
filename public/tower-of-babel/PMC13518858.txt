Oligomeric Alpha-Synuclein from neural-derived extracellular vesicles as possible biomarkers of non-motor symptoms in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Mario Meloni, Cristina Agliardi, Anna Salvatore, Francesca Lea Saibene, Massimo Marano, Anna Castagna, Pietro Arcuri, Milena Zanzottera, Franca Rosa Guerini, Mario Clerici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-motor symptoms (NMS) in Parkinson’s disease (PD) are clinically impactful and lack reliable blood-based biomarkers. Neural-derived extracellular vesicles (NDEVs) enriched from serum may reflect central neurodegenerative processes. In this cross-sectional study, we examined associations between NMS burden and NDEV-associated proteins in 68 patients with PD. NDEVs were immunocaptured from serum using an anti-L1CAM approach and characterized according to MISEV guidelines. NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein, SNARE complex proteins (SNAP-25, VAMP2, STX-1A), and neurotrophic factors (BDNF, GDNF, CDNF) were quantified by ELISA and related to NMS burden and PCA-derived non-motor domains, adjusting for age, sex, disease duration, MDS-UPDRS III, and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD). Higher NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein correlated with total NMSS score (partial Spearman’s ρ = 0.252, p = 0.02) and with hallucinations (domain 4) and attention/memory (domain 5). These associations were confirmed by multivariable regression analyses. In domain-specific models, oligomeric α-synuclein was independently associated with worse cognitive and behavioral-psychotic scores, whereas higher STX-1A and lower CDNF were associated with greater affective-motivational symptom severity. No significant associations were observed for autonomic or sleep domains. These findings support NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein as a candidate biomarker of non-motor symptom burden in PD and warrant longitudinal validation.

Introduction

In Parkinson’s disease (PD), the core motor symptoms, tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia, are predominantly attributed to the selective degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the Substantia Nigra pars compacta (SNpc). Conversely, the diverse array of non-motor symptoms (NMS) is believed to result from more widespread neurodegenerative processes, involving multiple neurotransmitter systems beyond the dopaminergic network1. Non-motor manifestations, including olfactory and autonomic dysfunction, neuropsychiatric disturbances, sleep abnormalities, and cognitive impairment constitute a core component throughout the clinical history of the disease. These features not only facilitate early diagnosis but also exert a profound impact on quality of life and functional independence during disease progression, and ultimately emerge as major determinants of mortality in advanced stages2.

Clinical diagnosis of PD remains imperfect and is strongly influenced by the clinician’s expertise. In addition, the marked heterogeneity of the disease adds further complexity in evaluating its progression. Consequently, there is broad consensus on the urgent need for robust biomarkers in PD to enhance diagnostic precision, including during the prodromal phase, to monitor disease evolution and to enable more accurate patient stratification3.

Although motor manifestations in PD are supported by more established symptomatic therapeutic strategies and a broader body of biomarker research, the lack of effective therapeutic options and reliable biomarkers continues to limit the clinical management of NMS. The identification of reliable NMS biomarkers is further hindered by the substantial phenotypic heterogeneity of non-motor manifestations in PD and the broad variability in their clinical presentation4–6.

There is an urgent need to identify and validate serum-based biomarkers that not only enable the early detection of non-motor symptoms, but also allow precise longitudinal monitoring of their progression and objective assessment of treatment response. Such biomarkers should accurately mirror the underlying neurodegenerative and neurotransmitter-related processes driving NMS, thereby providing a reliable surrogate of the disease’s pathophysiological evolution.

Over the past decade, neural-derived extracellular vesicles (NDEVs), enriched from peripheral blood, have emerged as a promising alternative to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in investigating central nervous system (CNS) pathologies7. As NDEVs carry molecular cargo that is thought to reflect the processes occurring in parent neuronal cells, their molecular profiling may provide valuable insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms associated with neurodegeneration. Quantitative analysis of neurodegeneration-associated proteins within NDEVs may thus enable early differential diagnosis, contribute to prognostic stratification, and support the development of targeted therapeutic and rehabilitative strategies.

In this cross-sectional study, we hypothesized that the diffuse neurodegenerative processes underlying NMS in PD are reflected in the protein cargo of NDEVs. To test this hypothesis, we examined the relationship between NMS severity and a panel of NDEV-associated proteins, including oligomeric α-synuclein, the key components of the SNARE complex-synaptosome-associated protein 25 (SNAP-25), vesicle-associated membrane protein 2 (VAMP2), and syntaxin-1A (STX-1A), as well as neurotrophic factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), and cerebral dopamine neurotrophic factor (CDNF). By integrating these molecular measures with concurrent clinical NMS assessments, we explored their potential utility as serum-based biomarkers associated with non-motor symptom burden in PD.

Results

NDEV characterization

Mean protein content in NDEVs lysates was 1.8 ± 0.1 μg/ml. Exocheck (Neuro) results showed the presence of EVs specific markers CD81, CD9, CD63, ICAM1, and TSG101, as well as all neuronal- specific markers L1CAM, NCAM1, MAPT, ENO2, GRIA1, and PLP1 on NDEVs lysates. The absence of the endoplasmic reticulum-associated protein calnexin (CANX), on the other hand, indicated the lack of cellular contamination (Fig. 1A). Western blot analysis of synaptophysin, a protein particularly abundant in neurons (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000102003-SYP), showed the presence of a more intense band of approximately 40 kDa in NDEVs lysate and a weaker band in total EVs, suggesting an enrichment of neuronal extracellular vesicles in NDEVs sample (Fig. 1B). The morphological characteristics of isolated EVs were assessed by TEM. Results showed a heterogeneous population of vesicles, displaying variability in both size and shape. Immunogold labeling using the anti-L1CAM monoclonal antibody confirmed the enrichment of NDEVs, as evidenced by distinct black punctate signals corresponding to gold particles bound to the vesicle membrane surface (Fig. 1C). Quantitative measurements of NDEVs were conducted by NTA in ten representative patients. The mean concentration of NDEVs was 4.9 × 1010 particles/mL, SD: 1.3 × 1010 particles/mL. Cognitive domain (PCA-derived cognitive factor)

PC1 scores were entered as the dependent variable in a multiple linear regression model including age, sex, motor severity (MDS-UPDRS Part III), disease duration, LEDD, and NDEV-associated biomarkers (oligomeric α-synuclein, BDNF, GDNF, CDNF, STX, SNAP-25, and VAMP2). The full model was statistically significant (F (12,51) = 2.60, p = 0.008), explaining 38.0% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.234).

Among the predictors, higher oligomeric α-synuclein concentration was independently associated with lower cognitive scores (β = −0.201, p = 0.027). Age showed a negative trend (β = −0.060, p = 0.067) and higher LEDD was significantly associated with worse cognitive performance (β = −0.002, p = 0.002). Other clinical and biomarker variables were not significant.

Stepwise regression identified a more parsimonious model including age, MDS-UPDRS Part III, LEDD, oligomeric α-synuclein, GDNF, and SNAP-25 (F (6,57) = 4.73, p = 0.0005; adjusted R² = 0.262). Within this model, oligomeric α-synuclein (β = −0.232, p = 0.005) remained a significant independent predictor of cognitive performance. Age showed a borderline association (β = -0.0556, p = 0.077), whereas GDNF (β = +0.009, p = 0.106) and SNAP-25 (β = −0.043, p = 0.104) were retained in the final model but did not reach statistical significance.

Affective-motivational domain (PCA-derived affective factor)

In the full regression model (F (12,49) = 2.65, p = 0.008; adjusted R² = 0.245), STX-1A (β = 0.809, p = 0.004) and LEDD (β = 0.002, p = 0.02) were significantly associated with greater affective-motivational symptom severity.

Stepwise regression identified a reduced model including STX-1A, LEDD, and CDNF (F (3,58) = 7.58, p < 0.001; adjusted R² = 0.245). Within this model, higher STX-1A levels (β = 0.673, p = 0.004) and higher LEDD (β = 0.002, p < 0.001) were independently associated with greater affective-motivational symptom severity, whereas CDNF was inversely associated with affective-motivational symptom severity (β = –0.002, p = 0.049).

Behavioral-psychotic domain (PCA-derived behavioral/psychotic factor)

In the full regression model, oligomeric α-synuclein (β = 0.16, p = 0.038); sex (coded as 0 = male, 1 = female; β = 0.720, p = 0.040) and LEDD (β = 0.004, p = 0.030) were significantly associated with behavioral-psychotic symptom severity. The model explained 37% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.21; p = 0.020).

Stepwise regression retained α-synuclein, sex, LEDD, BDNF, and SNAP25 as predictors (adjusted R² = 0.28; p = 0.0003). In this model, oligomeric α-synuclein (β = 0.180, p = 0.011), LEDD (β = 0.001, p = 0.0007), and sex (β = 0.640, p = 0.045) remained independently associated with behavioral-psychotic symptoms.

Autonomic domain (PCA-derived autonomic factor)

In the multivariable regression model, LEDD emerged as the only significant independent predictor of dysautonomic symptom severity (β = 0.00181, p = 0.015). The overall model was statistically significant (R² = 0.40; adjusted R² = 0.256; p = 0.005). No significant associations were observed between dysautonomic symptoms and NDEV-associated biomarkers, although BDNF (β = 0.114; p = 0.100) and VAMP2 (β = 0.061; p = 0.150) showed trend-level associations.

Sleep domain (PCA-derived sleep factor)

In the multivariable regression model, age was significantly associated with sleep dysfunction (β = −0.063, p = 0.018), whereas LEDD showed a borderline association (p = 0.081). The overall model was statistically significant (R² = 0.424; adjusted R² = 0.288; p = 0.002). No significant associations were detected between sleep dysfunction and NDEV-associated biomarkers. Associations between global non-motor symptom burden and biomarkers

A statistically significant positive correlation was identified between NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein concentrations and the total NMSS score (Spearman’s ρ = 0.250; p = 0.02). This association remained statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounding variables, including age, sex, MDS-UPDRS Part III and disease duration (Spearman’s ρ = 0.252; p = 0.02). In contrast, no significant correlations were observed between NMSS scores and the concentrations of either SNARE complex components or neurotrophic factors. This lack of correlation persisted even after adjusting for the aforementioned clinical variables. Linear regression analysis further confirmed a significant association between NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein concentrations and NMSS total score independently of age, sex, motor impairment severity according to the MDS-UPDRS Part III, disease duration, and total LEDD (t = 2.06; p = 0.044). Considering the NMSS sub-items, the results of the correlation analysis adjusted for age, sex, motor scores (MDS-UPDRS Part III), disease duration and LEDD showed that oligomeric α-synucleinpositively correlates with the score for domain 4 “perceptual problems/hallucinations” (Spearman’s ρ = 0.285, p = 0.01), domain 5 “attention/memory” (Spearman’s ρ = 0.293, p = 0.01) and domain 9 “miscellaneous” (Spearman’s ρ = 0.355, p < 0.01).

Linear regression analysis further confirmed these associations, even independently of age, sex, motor impairment, disease duration and total LEDD (item 4: t = 2.99, p < 0.01; item 5: t = 2.754, p < 0.01; item 9: t = 2.632, p = 0.01).

NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein concentrations were examined across the five levels of non-motor symptom burden (NMSB) as defined by NMSS score ranges. Mean α-synuclein concentrations showed a progressive increase across categories of increasing NMSB severity. The very severe group (NMSS ≥ 71) displayed the highest mean concentration (3.50 pg/mL, SD = 2.36, N = 20), followed by the severe group (NMSS 41–70; mean = 3.09 pg/mL, SD = 2.37, N = 20). Lower mean concentrations were observed in the moderate (2.63 pg/mL, SD = 1.45, N = 17) and mild (2.17 pg/mL, SD = 1.79, N = 11) groups.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated possible relationships between the broad spectrum of non-motor symptoms in PD and a panel of neurodegeneration-related biomarkers isolated from NDEVs. Compared with biomarkers measured in serum, NDEV-associated biomarkers are thought to more specifically reflect the pathological state of neuronal cells, as they originate directly from the central nervous system and are less influenced by peripheral confounding factors8.

Previous studies have demonstrated that NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein can reliably distinguish patients with PD from healthy controls and atypical parkinsonisms; additional results indicated that the concentrations of oligomeric α-synuclein within NDEVs correlate with motor disease severity, as measured by UPDRS motor scores9,10.

However, non-motor symptoms represent a highly heterogeneous and multidimensional clinical domain, and to date, no reliable serum or CSF biomarkers have been identified that adequately capture the severity and clinical heterogeneity of NMS in PD6.

In this context, quantitative profiling of neurodegeneration-related proteins within NDEVs represents a promising approach for tracking disease-related pathological changes in PD. NDEV-based biomarkers may contribute not only to earlier disease detection but also to the stratification of patients according to distinct clinical or biological phenotypes. Importantly, a deeper understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms underlying non-motor symptoms, together with the identification of reliable NDEV-associated biomarkers, is essential for the development of personalized therapeutic and rehabilitative strategies and for improving disease monitoring beyond motor features alone.

One of the main findings of the present study is that higher concentrations of NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein were independently associated with greater impairment in the cognitive and behavioral-psychotic domains. Specifically, increased α-synuclein levels were associated with lower performance on the PCA-derived cognitive factor and with greater severity of behavioral-psychotic symptoms, even after adjustment for relevant demographic and clinical confounders.

These findings are consistent with current pathophysiological models of PD, in which α-synuclein aggregation and propagation play a central role in the progression of neurodegeneration beyond the nigrostriatal system. Cognitive impairment and psychotic symptoms are typically observed in more advanced stages of PD and are thought to reflect a more widespread cortical and limbic involvement, including frontal, temporal, and associative cortical regions11.

In this context, the observed association between NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein and these non-motor domains raises the possibility that NDEV-associated α-synuclein may serve as a peripheral correlate of the cortical α-synuclein pathology underlying cognitive and behavioral manifestations in PD.

Nevertheless, the magnitude of the observed associations was overall moderate, indicating that NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein likely represents only one of multiple biological factors contributing to non-motor symptom heterogeneity in PD. The relatively modest correlation coefficients and explained variance observed across the models suggest that non-motor manifestations arise from complex and multifactorial pathophysiological mechanisms extending beyond α-synuclein-related processes alone. Similarly, other variables retained in the reduced models, including age, dopaminergic treatment load, selected neurotrophic and synaptic markers, and sex in the behavioral-psychotic domain, showed only secondary or trend-level associations, further supporting a multifactorial modulation of non-motor symptom expression in PD.

Interestingly, higher levels of STX-1A in NDEVs were independently associated with greater affective-motivational symptom severity, even after adjustment for clinical covariates, including dopaminergic treatment load. This finding is consistent with emerging evidence suggesting a role for synaptic vesicle release machinery dysfunction in affective symptomatology. STX-1A, a neuronal isoform of syntaxin-1, is a core component of the SNARE complex and plays a critical role in synaptic vesicle docking and neurotransmitter release12,13. In a rat model of depression, chronic stress was associated with presynaptic dysfunction characterized by reduced synaptophysin expression and increased syntaxin levels, suggesting an imbalance of synaptic vesicle cycling mechanisms and impaired synaptic stability14. Similarly, in a learned helplessness model, increased syntaxin-1 expression was observed in vulnerable animals, whereas resilient animals exhibited lower syntaxin-1 levels, further supporting a relationship between presynaptic molecular remodeling and affective behavioral outcomes15. While these studies do not directly address PD, they suggest that alterations in synaptic vesicle release machinery may represent a common molecular substrate linking synaptic dysfunction to affective symptomatology. This hypothesis may be particularly relevant in PD, where non-motor manifestations are thought to arise from widespread dysfunction involving dopaminergic, serotonergic, and noradrenergic systems, as well as limbic and frontostriatal networks, extending beyond the nigrostriatal system. In this context, the interaction between STX-1A and the dopamine transporter (DAT) may be of particular interest. STX-1A has been shown to modulate DAT trafficking, phosphorylation, and transport activity, thereby influencing presynaptic dopaminergic tone independently of protein kinase C signaling16. Given the role of dopaminergic signaling in motivation and mood regulation, alterations in STX-1A may plausibly contribute to affective-motivational symptoms in PD. Consistent with this hypothesis, altered expression of SNARE complex components, including Syntaxin-1, has been reported in limbic and prefrontal regions in experimental models of depression17. These findings suggest that STX-1A may represent a marker of broader synaptic dysfunction relevant to affective manifestations, although this interpretation remains exploratory.

In the present study, lower NDEV-associated CDNF concentrations were independently associated with greater affective-motivational symptom severity, even after accounting for dopaminergic treatment load. Although direct evidence linking CDNF to affective or motivational behavior remains limited, accumulating experimental studies suggest that CDNF exerts biological actions extending beyond classical dopaminergic neuroprotection. In particular, CDNF has been shown to directly interact with α-synuclein, modulate its aggregation and neuronal internalization, and improve behavioral outcomes in α-synuclein-based animal models, independently of overt nigrostriatal neurodegeneration18. These findings, together with broader evidence highlighting CDNF’s role in protein homeostasis, endoplasmic reticulum stress responses, and synuclein-related pathology, support the notion that CDNF may influence widespread neurodegenerative processes relevant to non-motor symptomatology19.

Overall, these findings support the hypothesis that affective-motivational symptoms in PD may arise from multiple interacting biological processes, including synaptic dysfunction, neurotrophic signaling, and dopaminergic treatment-related factors, extending beyond α-synuclein-related pathology alone.

In contrast to the cognitive and behavioral-psychotic domains, no significant associations were observed between NDEV-associated biomarkers and autonomic or sleep-related symptoms. Within the autonomic domain, dopaminergic treatment load (LEDD) emerged as the only independent predictor of symptom severity, suggesting that autonomic dysfunction in PD may be more closely related to disease burden and treatment-related factors than to NDEV-derived molecular signatures. The absence of significant associations between NDEV-associated biomarkers and autonomic dysfunction may also reflect the biological and anatomical compartmentalization of autonomic pathology in PD. Indeed, autonomic impairment in PD is not exclusively mediated by central neurodegenerative processes but also substantially involves peripheral autonomic structures, including enteric, sympathetic, and cardiac autonomic plexuses20. Therefore, biomarkers measured in L1CAM-enriched neuronal extracellular vesicles, which are intended to preferentially reflect CNS-related neuronal cargo, may not fully capture pathological alterations occurring within peripheral autonomic networks. This could partly explain why clinical variables such as LEDD, rather than NDEV-associated molecular markers, emerged as the main predictors of dysautonomic symptom severity in our cohort.

Similarly, in the sleep domain, age was independently associated with sleep dysfunction, while LEDD showed only a borderline association, and no NDEV-associated biomarkers were significantly related to sleep impairment. The inverse association between age and reported sleep dysfunction may reflect age-related differences in symptom perception, reporting, or sleep architecture, rather than a true protective effect. Indeed, alterations in sleep structure and circadian regulation in older individuals may lead to reduced subjective awareness of sleep disturbances, despite objective abnormalities. In addition, sleep dysfunction in PD is highly heterogeneous and likely multifactorial, arising from the interplay of aging, dopaminergic treatment, nocturnal motor symptoms, circadian dysregulation, and non-dopaminergic mechanisms. Accordingly, the lack of significant associations between NDEV-associated biomarkers and sleep dysfunction should not be interpreted as evidence against a biological basis of these symptoms, but rather as suggesting that the selected NDEV cargo may be less sensitive to the mechanisms predominantly underlying sleep-related manifestations in PD.

Consistent with the overall pattern of results observed across the different analytical approaches, linear regression and correlation analyses consistently demonstrated an independent association between NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein concentrations and global non-motor symptom burden, as assessed by the NMSS. Notably, α-synuclein levels were specifically associated with attention/memory dysfunction (NMSS domain 5) and perceptual problems/hallucinations (NMSS domain 4), even after adjustment for relevant demographic and clinical confounders.

These findings are consistent with the PCA-based analyses, which identified the cognitive and behavioral-psychotic domains as those most strongly associated with NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein. Together, these convergent results support the hypothesis that NDEV-associated α-synuclein may serve as a peripheral correlate of the α-synuclein-related pathological processes underlying cognitive and behavioral dysfunction in PD.

Limitations must be acknowledged, the principal one being the cross-sectional design, which precludes any inference regarding the temporal or causal relationships between non-motor symptoms and NDEV-associated biomarkers. Longitudinal investigations will determine whether changes in biomarker levels can track the progression of non-motor symptomatology over time. Furthermore, variability in NDEV isolation and the use of subjective clinical rating scales may introduce measurement bias. Moreover, it should be noted that the use of L1CAM for neuronal EV immunoisolation remains a matter of debate, due to reports describing the presence of soluble L1CAM in plasma/serum that may potentially affect the specificity of immunocapture approaches21. However, subsequent single-vesicle analyses demonstrated the co-localization of L1CAM with both EV and neuronal markers, supporting its association with at least a subset of circulating neuron-derived EVs and reinforcing its utility for neuronal EV enrichment22.

In the present study, this possible limitation was mitigated by combining a preliminary EV enrichment step with stringent washing procedures during immunoaffinity isolation, aimed at reducing the carryover of soluble proteins and enriching for vesicle-associated L1CAM. Moreover, the detection of canonical EV markers in the isolated fraction further supports the vesicular nature of the obtained particles.

Another limitation of the present study is the absence of a healthy control group, which precludes conclusions regarding the disease specificity of the observed biomarker alterations and limits comparisons with physiological baseline concentrations.

Lastly, the findings reported in this study require validation in larger, independent cohorts and confirmation through advanced next-generation ELISA techniques capable of detecting analytes at sub-picogram concentration levels.

In conclusion, this study identifies NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein as a candidate biomarker for multiple clinically relevant non-motor features in PD, including global non- motor symptom burden, cognitive impairment, and specific neuropsychiatric disturbances. These findings underscore the potential utility of NDEV-associated biomarkers for characterizing the heterogeneity of the non-motor phenotype in PD and provide a rationale for future longitudinal studies to validate their prognostic and pathophysiological significance. The observed association between these blood-based biomarkers (particularly NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein) and non-motor symptoms may hold clinical relevance for stratifying PD phenotypes. Specifically, the ability to detect higher non-motor symptom burden through a minimally invasive blood-based biomarker could facilitate earlier identification of patients with a prominent non-motor phenotype, who may benefit from closer monitoring and stratification in future longitudinal studies.

Methods

Study cohort

This study was conducted at the Center for Diagnosis and Rehabilitation of Parkinson’s Disease and Parkinsonisms (DiaRiaPARK) and the Laboratory of Molecular Medicine (LAMMB), IRCCS Fondazione Don Carlo Gnocchi, Milan, Italy. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of IRCCS Fondazione Don Carlo Gnocchi, Milan, Italy (Protocol No. 11_16/04/2020). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, and participation in the study was voluntary. A total of 68 patients with PD were consecutively enrolled and evaluated by movement disorders specialists. The diagnosis of PD was established based on the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) Clinical Diagnostic Criteria for Parkinson’s Disease23.

Patients were excluded in cases of vascular, familial, or drug-induced forms of Parkinsonism, as well as other known or suspected causes of Parkinsonism (including metabolic disorders or brain tumors), clinical features suggestive of atypical Parkinsonism, severe systemic or inflammatory diseases, active malignancies, major psychiatric disorders, or other neurological conditions potentially affecting cognitive or non-motor assessments. At the time of blood collection, demographic and clinical information, including age, sex, disease duration, modified Hoehn and Yahr (mH&Y) stage, and MDS-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale part III (MDS-UPDRS-III) scores, were collectedwhile patients were in the “on” medication state. LEDD was calculated for each patient24.

To comprehensively assess non-motor symptomatology, patients underwent a standardized clinical evaluation using validated rating scales, with symptoms subsequently grouped into five predefined non-motor domains: 1- autonomic domain; 2-sleep domain; 3-affective-motivational domain; 4- cognitive domain; 5-behavioral-psychotic domain. The Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) was administered to quantify the overall burden of non-motor symptoms, and individual NMSS items were subsequently assigned and aggregated into the corresponding non-motor domains for domain-specific analyses25.

The autonomic domain was evaluated using NMSS-derived autonomic items together with the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score-31 (COMPASS-31)26. The sleep domain was assessed using the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)27, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)28, and the NMSS Sleep domain (domain 2)25. The affective-motivational domain was evaluated using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory for Adults (STAI-Y2)29 (The STAI-Y2 was selected to assess trait anxiety, reflecting stable affective characteristics rather than situational anxiety), the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II)30, the Italian version of the Dimensional Apathy Scale (I-DAS)31, and the NMSS Mood/Cognition domain (domain 3)25. The cognitive domain was assessed using the Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB)32, the Mini Mental Parkinson (MMP)33, and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)34, together with the NMSS Attention/Memory domain (domain 5)10. Finally, the behavioral-psychotic domain was evaluated using the Questionnaire for Impulsive-Compulsive Disorders in Parkinson’s Disease-Rating Scale (QUIP-RS)35, the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI-Q)36, the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale-11 (BIS-11)37, and the NMSS Perceptual Problems/Hallucinations domain (domain 4)25.

All study procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with established good clinical practice guidelines.

Serum collection

Six ml of peripheral blood samples were drawn from each patient using serum separator tubes (SST II Advance, BD Vacutainer®, BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Following coagulation and within 30 min after collection, the samples were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min. The resulting serum was then divided into aliquots and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

NDEVs’ enrichment

Neuronal-derived extracellular vesicles (NDEVs) were enriched from 500 µl of peripheral blood (serum) using a two-step protocol previously described38. This method relies on immunocapture employing a biotinylated monoclonal anti-CD171 (L1CAM) antibody (eBio5G3, clone 5G3), which targets the N-terminal region of the L1CAM ectodomain, specifically the Ig-like domains (eBioscience™, Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA). L1CAM is a neuronal surface marker abundantly present in neural tissue and on extracellular vesicles secreted by cultured neurons. The use of L1CAM antibody as target for isolating peripheral blood NDEVs has been extensively supported by numerous findings recently reported22.

Following isolation, 20 µL of the enriched NDEVs were stored at −80 °C for later analysis of intact vesicles. The remaining fraction was lysed using M-PER™ reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in a 1:2.5 ratio. These lysates were also preserved at −80 °C for future use.

NDEV characterization

Characterization of neuronal-derived extracellular vesicles (NDEVs) was carried out in accordance with the MISEV guidelines39. Protein content in NDEVs lysate was measured by Qubit fluorometer and Qubit Protein Broad Range (BR) Assay Kits (Thermofisher scientific).

To evaluate the presence of both specific extracellular vesicle and neuronal markers in NDEV lysates, the Exo-check Exosome Antibody Array (Neuro) kit (System Biosciences, SBI, Palo Alto, CA, USA) was utilized.

For western blot analysis of Synaptophysin, proteins were extracted from NDEVs, total EVs (derived from a NDEVs isolation without biotinylated monoclonal anti-CD171 (L1CAM) antibody) total serum; peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). 40 µg of proteins from each of the described biological samples obtained from the same PD patient were boiled at 95 °C for 10 min, separated by SDS-PAGE using Mini PROTEAN® TGX™ precast gels (Bio-Rad, USA), and transferred to PVDF (polyvinylidene difluoride) membrane with Trans-Blot® instrument (Bio-Rad, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20, membrane was incubated at 4 °C overnight with a primary rabbit monoclonal antibody against Synaptophysin (dil. 1:200 Synaptophysin monoclonal antibody (SP11), #MA5-14532 Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher scientific, USA) followed by incubation at room temperature for 1 h with a goat anti rabbit IgG (H + L) cross absorbed secondary antibody (dil. 1:20,000, #G-21234 Invitrogen Thermo Fisher scientific, USA). Both western blot and Exo-check Exosome Antibody Array (Neuro) membranes were developed with Clarity Max Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, USA) and imaged by ChemiDoc™ Gel Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA).

For immuno-gold labeling and subsequent transmission electron microscopy (TEM), 5 µL of intact NDEVs were deposited onto 200-mesh Formvar/Carbon-coated TEM grids and allowed to adsorb for 10 min. The grids were then incubated for 3 h at room temperature in a humid chamber on 50 µL drops containing the primary anti-L1CAM antibody (clone eBio5G3 (5G3), eBioscience™, Invitrogen, USA), diluted 1:100 in wash buffer. Following this, a 1-h incubation was carried out on 30 µL drops of a 1:50 dilution of anti-mouse IgG conjugated with 10 nm gold particles (ab39619, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), also prepared in a wash buffer.

Subsequent to immunolabeling, the grids underwent sequential washing in five drops of wash buffer, followed by five rinses in distilled water. Negative staining was performed using a 1% aqueous solution of filtered uranyl acetate, applied for 10 s and followed by air-drying. TEM images were acquired using a JEOL JEM 2100Plus Transmission Electron Microscope (JEOL, Japan) operating at 200 kV, equipped with an 8-megapixel Rio CMOS camera (Gatan, Pleasanton, CA, USA).

NDEVs’ size and concentration were evaluated by NTA in ten representative samples using a NanoSight NS300 instrument equipped with a 488 nm blue laser, a flow-cell top plate, and a syringe pump allowing constant-flow analysis (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). Samples were diluted 1:250 in filtered 1× PBS, and three 60 s videos were recorded for each sample. Mean particle size was calculated by integrating data from the three recordings. Analyses were performed using NTA software v3.4 with a detection threshold set at 5 to maximize particle tracking while minimizing background noise.

Quantification of NDEVs’ biomarkers by ELISA

Sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were used following manufacturer’s instructions to measure the concentrations of NDEVs’ associated oligomeric α-synuclein (Human Alpha Synuclein oligomer, MBS730762, MyBioSource, CA, USA), SNAP-25 (Human Synaptosomal Associated Protein 25 kDa, MBS2022159, MyBioSource, CA, USA), VAMP2 (Human vesicle-associated membrane protein 2, MBS062679, MyBioSource, CA, USA), STX-1A (Human syntaxin-1A brain, MBS076045, MyBioSource, CA, USA) and neurotrophic factors BDNF (Human BDNF, EH42RB, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher scientific, MA, USA), GDNF (Human GDNF, EHGDNF, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher scientific, MA, USA) and CDNF (Human CDNF, EH101RBX10, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher scientific, MA, USA). Standard curves and samples were run in duplicate.

Statistical analyses

Associations between global non-motor symptom burden, quantified by the total NMSS score, and NDEV-associated biomarkers were initially examined using partial Spearman’s rank correlation analyses, controlling for potential confounders, including age, sex, motor severity (MDS-UPDRS Part III), disease duration, and LEDD. Subsequently, multiple linear regression analyses were performed with the NMSS total score as the dependent variable and NDEV-associated biomarkers as independent variables, while adjusting for the same set of confounding factors. These models were used to assess the independent contribution of each biomarker to global non-motor symptom burden.

To reduce dimensionality and minimize multiple testing across highly correlated non-motor clinical scales, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied separately to five predefined non-motor domains (cognitive, affective-motivational, behavioral-psychotic, autonomic, and sleep), each comprising clinically related scales assessing the same functional domain. PCA was performed on correlation matrices using eigenvalue decomposition. Component retention was based on eigenvalues > 1.0, inspection of scree plots, and the proportion of variance explained. Variables were considered to load meaningfully on a component when factor loadings were ≥0.40. For each domain, the first principal component (PC1), representing the dominant latent construct, was extracted and used as a composite outcome measure in subsequent regression analyses. Detailed PCA results, including component loadings and domain-specific interpretations, are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Additionally, participants were stratified into five levels of non-motor symptom burden (NMSB) based on total NMSS scores, according to previously established criteria40: no (NMSS = 0), mild (NMSS = 1–20), moderate (NMSS = 21–40), severe (NMSS = 41–70), and very severe (NMSS ≥ 71). NDEV-associated oligomeric α-synuclein concentrations were summarized within each NMSB category using descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation). Differences in mean α-synuclein concentrations across increasing NMSB severity levels were explored descriptively to assess potential trends.

All regression models were systematically checked for compliance with standard assumptions. Linearity was assessed through visual inspection of residuals plotted against predictors, revealing no systematic patterns or curvilinear trends. Normality of residuals was evaluated using Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test, which showed no significant deviation from normality (p = 0.097). Homoscedasticity was verified using residual-versus-fitted plots, which showed stable variance across predicted values. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with all values below 3. Influential observations were evaluated using Cook’s distance, with a maximum observed value of 0.245, well below the conventional threshold of 1.

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
