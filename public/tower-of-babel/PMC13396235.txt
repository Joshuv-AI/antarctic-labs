ABSTRACT
PIK3CA is mutated in ~15% of colorectal cancers (CRC). PI3K regulates immunity, inhibition potentially enhances anti-tumor immunity. We launched a phase 1/2 trial of copanlisib (PIK3CA inhibitor) and nivolumab (anti-PD-1) in metastatic microsatellite stable CRC (NCT03711058): Cohort A: PIK3CAwt (n = 17) and Cohort B: PIK3CAmut (n = 22). Copanlisib/nivolumab is well tolerated with recommended phase 2 dose of nivolumab 480 mg day 1 and copanlisib 60 mg days 1/8/15 of a 28-day cycle. Primary endpoint of objective response rate at 6 months was not met with Cohort A: 0/17 and Cohort B: 2/22 having 6-month treatment response. Secondary endpoints are median progression-free survival (Cohort A: 1.7 months; Cohort B: 1.6 months), median overall survival (Cohort A: 8.5 months; Cohort B: 6.7 months), duration of response (Cohort A: 13.1 months; Cohort B: 17 months) and 6-month disease control rate (Cohort A: 2/17; Cohort B: 3/22). Secondary endpoints were not statistically different between these cohorts.

Introduction

While meaningful progress has been made in the treatment of colorectal cancer (CRC) over the last two decades, CRC still represents the second leading cause of cancer-related death in the United States1,2. Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy has dramatically improved the outcomes of many tumor types, but anti-PD-1 monotherapy is associated with no benefit in the 95% of metastatic CRC cases that are mismatch repair proficient/microsatellite stable (MMRp/MSS)3. Similarly, small molecule inhibitors of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathways have failed to produce significant response rates or survival benefits in patients with MSS CRC4. Despite these failures, recently presented combination approaches have shown promise in the neoadjuvant and metastatic treatment of MSS CRC and provide hope for changing this paradigm5,6.

Mutations of the alpha (α) subunit of PI3K (encoded by PIK3CA) lead to constitutive activation of PI3Kα, resulting in enhanced tumor cell survival and proliferation and a tumor microenvironment (TME) that promotes cellular proliferation and immune evasion7,8. In addition to PI3Kα, there are 3 other isoenzymes within the Class I PI3K family (PI3Kbeta, PI3Kγ, and PI3Kdelta) that function in signaling downstream of growth factor receptor tyrosine kinases9. PI3Kα and PI3Kbeta are the isozymes most active in cancer cells and their inhibition is thought to have direct antitumor activity. In contrast, PI3Kgamma activity is critical for myeloid lineage cells, with myeloid derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) being particularly reliant on signaling through this isoenzyme10. Previous studies have shown that inhibition of PI3Kgamma results in a reduction in MDSC recruitment and slower tumor growth in murine models10.

Based on the 15% prevalence of PIK3CA mutations in MSS CRC, there has been significant enthusiasm for investigating PI3K/mTOR pathway inhibitors for the treatment of this subset of MSS CRC. Unfortunately, PI3K inhibitors alone or in combination with chemotherapy provide minimal benefit in this disease setting11–14. Other currently approved third line options in CRC also exhibit limited efficacy with progression-free survivals (PFS) ranging from 1.9 to 5.6 months and low response rates of 1.0–6.1% across their respective phase 3 trials highlighting the importance of developing better therapeutic options for this patient population15–17.

Preclinical work by Collins et al. identified PIK3CA mutations as mediators of anti-PD-1 resistance in CRC8,18. Using an ORF library of cancer driver mutations, the authors screened CRC cells in immunocompetent and immunodeficient mouse models under anti-PD-1 pressure. Resistant clones were strongly enriched for PIK3CA alterations. Importantly, copanlisib (a pan-PI3K inhibitor) restored anti-PD-1 sensitivity, producing marked tumor regression, whereas neither agent was effective alone. PIK3CA-mutant tumors can upregulate pro-myeloid chemotactic programs that drive MDSC recruitment and reinforce an immunosuppressive microenvironment19.

Copanlisib is a pan-class I PI3K inhibitor with potent activity across all four isoforms (IC50 0.5–6.4 nM). In CRC models, it is ~40-fold more effective at suppressing proliferation in PIK3CA-mutant compared with wild-type cells, suggesting a therapeutic window20. In immunocompetent mouse models, copanlisib also modulates the TME, likely through inhibition of PI3Kdelta/γ in MDSCs, and intermittent dosing reduces regulatory T cell (Treg) suppressive function while enhancing CD8 + T-cell cytotoxicity, collectively augmenting antitumor immunity.10,21,22

Here we report a Phase 1/2 trial of copanlisib in combination with the PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab for patients with metastatic MSS CRC that had progressed on at least two lines of prior therapy. Our trial evaluated this strategy in two cohorts, one that was PIK3CA mutant and the other PIK3CA wildtype.

Serial pre-treatment and on-treatment tumor biopsies were profiled to ascertain the impact of this strategy on the TME. We hypothesized that PIK3CA inhibition with copanlisib may overcome the inherent resistance of MSS CRC to PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade.

Copanlisib and nivolumab combination therapy was safe

In the phase 1 portion of this study, the first 6 patients showed no dose-limiting toxicity, so the trial moved to phase 2. Toxicity was evaluable for all patients who started treatment and no patients discontinued it due to drug-related adverse events (AEs). Hematological and nonhematological toxicities are shown in Table S4. There were 31 grade 3/4 toxicities attributable to copanlisib and nivolumab, most commonly hypertension in 16 (41%) patients and maculopapular rash in 4 (10%) patients. Overall, 38 of the 39 patients experienced at least one treatment-related AE. The most frequent attributable toxicities were diarrhea (11 patients, 28%), nausea (26, 67%), vomiting (13, 33%), fatigue (14, 36%), weight loss (8, 21%), hyperglycemia (21, 54%), maculopapular rash (16, 41%), and hypertension (23, 59%). A summary of grade 3 attributable AEs, all grade 3/4 AEs, and attributable AEs are outlined in Supplementary Data 2, 3, and Supplementary Table 4 respectively.

Copanlisib and nivolumab produced treatment responses in PIK3 altered mCRC with notable durability

Among the 39 patients (including those in the phase 1 portion treated at the same dose) with evaluable response data, 2 of 22 patients in the PIK3CA mutant cohort had partial responses at 6 months. None of the patients in the PIK3CA wild-type group had a partial or complete response at this pre-specified time point, and recruitment of that cohort was therefore stopped early. However, two patients with prolonged stable disease, one in each cohort, developed partial responses after 6 months for a final objective response rate (ORR) of 4/39 across both cohorts. In the patients with PIK3CA mutant CRC, 3/22 patients (13.6%, 95% confidence interval (CI): 4.7–33.3%) responded and in patients with PIK3CA wild-type CRC, 1/17 (5.9%, 95% CI: 1.0–27.0%) responded (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 4). While the experimental arm in PIK3CA mutated patients only had two responders at the 6-month time point, thereby missing the primary endpoint of three or more patients with treatment response, a third patient had a delayed response after 6 months resulting in three total responders out of 22 patients in the PIK3CA mutant group. Thus, the threshold was ultimately met, albeit this must be categorized as an exploratory finding. Interestingly, the lone responder in the PIK3CA wild-type cohort had a PI3K alteration (amplification of the PIK3R1 gene encoding the PI3K p85α subunit) that could potentially activate PI3K signaling23. There were no statistically significant differences in median long-term outcomes between patients with PIK3CA wild-type and mutant tumors with a median PFS of 1.6 months in patients with PIK3CA wild-type disease and 1.7 months in patients with PIK3CA mutant tumors. Median overall survival (OS) was 8.5 months and 6.7 months in these two cohorts, respectively (Supplementary Fig 2). Duration of response was 13.1 months in the patient with PIK3CA wild-type tumor who achieved a PR and median of 17 months [9.40, 20.6] in the patients with PIK3CA mutant tumors. While there was encouraging activity with the copanlisib plus nivolumab combination in PI3K-altered disease, the response rate was modest. However, it is noteworthy that all the patients who achieved a partial response also demonstrated PFS of greater than two years. The three patients with PIK3CA mutant CRC and partial responses had PFSs of 24.2, 26.6, and 29.8 months, respectively. The patient with a partial response in the PIK3CA wild-type cohort (who had a PIK3R1 amplification) is still receiving treatment after 33.7 months of follow up with no disease progression. This contrasts with previous cohorts of patients with MSS CRC treated with anti-PD-1 alone, where the response rates were 0% and 6-month PFS was <5%24. There is limited data with copanlisib alone for CRC, but in the four patients with GI malignancies that received copanlisib on the NCI Match trial, none achieved a partial or complete response25.

As an exploratory analysis, we evaluated whether the type of PIK3CA mutation influenced response to treatment by comparing PIK3CA exon 9 (n = 12, PR:1, SD:1, PD:10) and PIK3CA exon 20 (n = 5, PR:1, SD:1, PD:3) mutations. However, these subgroups did not exhibit a statistical difference in either PFS or OS compared to each other or the PIK3CA wild-type cohort (Supplementary Fig. 2). We then evaluated whether inactivating PTEN mutations or deletions influenced response to treatment. None of the four patients with clinical responses carried genomic PTEN inactivation, although this was not statistically significant. It is important to note that this study was underpowered for these subgroup analyses.

Clinical activity according to site of metastatic disease

Prior literature has suggested that patients with lung-only metastases from CRC respond better to immune modulatory approaches then those with other sites of disease26–28. The majority of patients (n = 23) on this trial were observed to have RECIST liver metastases (as expected for this population), while 13 patients had disease at other anatomic sites including lung, peritoneal, non-thoracic lymph nodes, etc. without evidence of liver metastasis. The remaining three patients had radiographically present liver lesions that were not RECIST eligible. We did not identify any statistically significant differences in response and clinical benefit rate between these subgroups (Fisher’s exact test p = 0.59 and p = 0.45 for response and clinical benefit rate, respectively; n = 36).

To determine if patients with multiple organ sites of metastasis had differential responses to treatment in these locations, RECIST-measured lesions for each patient were categorized as either livers, lung, peritoneal, or other (predominantly adenopathy). We evaluated these lesions at their baseline scan and compared to measurements at their first on-treatment RECIST scan to calculate a “site-specific response”. Our data suggest that liver metastases behaved differently from metastases to other sites. Most liver metastases exhibited progressive disease (PD) as best response (83%) compared to lung (32%) and other sites of metastasis (19%; Fig. 2A). In the subgroup of 14 patients with both liver and lung metastases, liver lesions were generally associated with PD (3 SD, 11 PD) whereas most lung disease was stable (11 SD, 3 PD). This was also true for the subgroup of 10 patients with liver and “other” sites of metastasis: 90% had PD in the liver vs 10% PD (9 SD, 1 PD) in “other” sites (Fig. 2A, C). Considered together, in patients with disease both within and outside of the liver, non-liver metastases had better tumor control compared to liver lesions. However, whether this is reflective of different treatment responses or is reflective of the known tendency for lung metastases to grow more slowly than liver metastases, is unknown. Correlative investigations

Higher pretreatment levels of CD163± and CD3±CD8± cells and lower PD-L1 tumor expression were observed in tumors of responders

To determine how nivolumab and copanlisib treatment alters the TME, we performed Orion multiplexed immunofluorescence imaging (see “Methods”) on tumor biopsy specimens obtained pre-treatment and on-treatment around cycle 2 day 15 (6 weeks into treatment)29. Eighteen patients (PR: n = 3; SD: n = 3; PD: n = 12) had pre-treatment biopsies of sufficient quality to assess baseline immune infiltration status (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Cytotoxic CD3+CD8+ T cells are key immune effector cells of ICI therapy, and we therefore asked whether the prevalence of cytotoxic T cells in pre-treatment biopsies might serve as a marker of nivolumab and copanlisib response. Comparing CD3+CD8+ cell fractions among pre-treatment biopsy samples revealed an enrichment in the CD3+CD8+ cell fraction in partial responders (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 0.039; Fig. 4a). The patient (1887-01) with the highest pre-treatment CD3+CD8+ cell fraction had 41-fold greater pre-treatment CD3+CD8+ cell fraction than the median and went on to achieve a partial response (Fig. 4B, C). Treatment resulted in dynamic remodeling of the immune microenvironment with increased CD3+CD8+ infiltration

To determine if treatment with copanlisib and nivolumab would increase CD3+CD8+ tumor infiltration we compared pre- and on-treatment biopsies for CD3+CD8+ T cell infiltration in the eight patients for whom paired biopsies were available. In all eight patients, CD3+CD8+ infiltration increased following treatment (p = 0.0006; paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Fig. 5A). This finding is in agreement with increases in activated effector CD8+ T cells observed in the peripheral blood in this investigation and the T-cell expansion seen with anti-PD-1 monotherapy in other tumor types30. However, there was no association between treatment response and changes in CD3+CD8+ immune infiltration following treatment. Given the importance of functional T cell states in governing anti-tumor immunity, we applied a high-plex immunofluorescence panel using cyclic immunofluorescence (CyCIF) and a panel designed to define T cell substates. Another patient with a partial response (1887-01) had the single highest pre-treatment infiltration of activated tissue resident T cells (Fig. 5C). In patient 1887-19 (partial clinical response), TRMs increased more than 5-fold in the on-treatment biopsy. A substantial proportion of these TRMs were also positive for granzyme B (pre-treatment: 30%, on-treatment: 28%; Fig. 5D). Although exploratory, these observations raise the possibility that treatment-associated expansion of distinct CD8 + T-cell phenotypes contributes to clinical outcome, a hypothesis that will require validation in expanded patient sets.

We then investigated how cell proliferation signals are impacted by copanlisib/nivolumab treatment. We focused on seven patients (WT: n = 3; PIK3CA mutant: n = 4), with pre- and on-treatment biopsies with sufficient tumor and CyCIF imaging quality for cell state quantification. In all four patients with PI3KCA mutant tumors and paired biopsies, we observed an increase in pERK+ cell fraction following treatment, compared with only one PI3KCA WT tumor, although our statistical power was too limited to draw firm conclusions (Fig. 6E). We observed no consistent difference comparing the pAKT+ tumor cell fraction between pre- and on-treatment biopsies (Fig. 6E). When we examined downstream cell proliferation markers pRB, Ki-67 and p27, there was a trend towards increased RB phosphorylation, increased Ki-67 levels and decreased p27 expression, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, following treatment (Fig. 6F). Although limited by small sample size, these data support the notion that PIK3CA mutant tumor cells increase reliance on ERK/MAPK signaling following copanlisib treatment, and that the tumor cells that survive therapy and are found in on-treatment biopsies tend to be more proliferative.

Discussion

Prior efforts to treat PIK3CA-mutated MSS CRC with PI3K inhibitors or immune checkpoint blockade have yielded limited clinical benefit, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic strategies.11–14 We hypothesized that PI3K inhibition could favorably remodel the TME and enhance susceptibility to PD-1 blockade, particularly in tumors with activating PIK3CA mutations. To test this, we conducted a phase 1/2 trial of copanlisib plus nivolumab in patients with MSS CRC, enrolling separate cohorts of PIK3CA mutant and wild-type disease. All four partial responses occurred in patients with PI3K pathway alterations (three with PIK3CA mutant tumors and one with a PIK3R1 amplification), and three additional PIK3CA-mutant patients achieved stable disease. Although the primary endpoint was not met, these findings are notable given the poor prognosis of this population. Because of the small sample size and non-randomized cohort assignment, however, any comparative interpretations should be made cautiously.

Treatment was generally well tolerated, with no unexpected safety signals despite the immune-modulatory effects of both agents. Hypertension was the most common grade 3/4 toxicity (41%), similar to rates observed with copanlisib monotherapy in CHRONOS-1, while grade 3/4 rash (10%) occurred more frequently than reported with either drug alone, suggesting potential combination-related cutaneous toxicity35,36.

We hypothesize that the responsiveness of PIK3CA-mutant tumors reflects contributions from both tumor-intrinsic biology and the TME. Notably, all three PIK3CA-mutant patients who achieved a partial response experienced PFS exceeding two years, an outcome that is highly unusual in heavily pretreated MSS CRC. Although the number of responders was small, the durability of these responses is striking given the minimal activity of immunotherapy or standard third-line therapies in MSS CRC. The observation that a patient with PIK3R1 amplification achieved a >24-month partial response further suggests that alterations beyond PIK3CA may also confer susceptibility to this therapeutic strategy. We further observed significant delays between treatment initiation and clinical response. We hypothesize that that these represent either ICI-related delayed responses or that PIK3CA inhibition-mediated remodeling of the TME is a slower process than direct anti-tumor effects associated with chemotherapy.

The TME of liver metastases is thought to be immunosuppressive, and liver metastases are associated with worse outcomes to ICI therapy28,37–39. Consistent with these data, we found that patients without liver involvement were twice as likely to achieve a PR as those with liver metastases, although the small sample size limits definitive conclusions. The mechanisms underlying these impaired ICI responses in liver metastases remains incompletely understood. In our separate phase 2 trial of nivolumab and relatlimab in MSS CRC patients, imaging mass cytometry analysis found that lung metastases were associated with higher levels of dendritic cells and a trend towards higher B cells proportion as compared to liver metastases, which were relatively enriched for Tregs40. In a pre-clinical mouse model, copanlisib treatment increased the intratumoral CD8+ T cell to Treg ratio, supporting a model in which PI3K blockade can relieve Treg-mediated immunosuppression22.

Correlative analyses provided several insights into how PI3K inhibition may shape antitumor immunity in MSS CRC. High-plex imaging demonstrated that pathway activity (ERK, AKT, S6 phosphorylation) can be reliably quantified even in small metastatic biopsies, supporting incorporation of imaging-based cell signaling assays such as Orion and CyCIF in future studies. High-plex imaging additionally confirmed expected on-target signaling differences between PIK3CA mutant and wild-type tumors, including higher baseline AKT activation and a trend toward compensatory ERK upregulation with treatment. However, we otherwise did not observe any other consistent trend in how on-treatment samples alter PI3K/AKT or ERK/MAPK signaling. Although this heterogeneity may be attributed to small sample size, complex feedback dynamics and pulsatile activation cycles make ERK and AKT signals difficult to interpret41,42. Given the generally poor results with inhibitors of KRAS G12C and BRAF as monotherapy in CRC compared to other tumor tissue types due to upregulation of parallel growth pathways and improved benefit with combination inhibitor approaches, these data may suggest that a rational approach to improve activity of PIK3CA inhibition in combination with ICI in MSS CRC may be the addition of MAPK pathway inhibitors43,44.

Our finding that patients with higher pre-treatment CD3+CD8+ infiltration were more likely to respond is consistent with longstanding observations that pre-existing cytotoxic T cell immunity predicts benefit from ICI6,45,46. Responders also showed elevated CD163+PD-L1+ macrophages at baseline, which supports preclinical models that PI3K inhibition may relieve myeloid-mediated suppression sufficiently to enable effective PD-1 blockade in a subset of patients8,19. We found that while CD3+CD8+ cells universally increased following treatment, this did not correlate with clinical response. This suggests that while copanlisib/nivolumab may improve T cell trafficking into the TME, this is insufficient in most patients to overcome other barriers to an effective anti-tumor immune response. CD8+ immune cell infiltration also increases in melanoma tumors following immunotherapy, but the extent to which either copanlisib or nivolumab contributed to this effect in our cohort remains unknown47. We also identified treatment-associated increases in tissue resident memory and effector T cells, as well as expansion of TIM3/LAG3 exhausted subsets, consistent with expected immunotherapy-driven phenotypic remodeling48.

The combined use of copanlisib/nivolumab in this patient population limits our ability to discern the contribution of each of these components to observed treatment responses. However, the observation that all of the treatment responses were limited to patients with PI3K-altered disease and the low response rates to nivolumab previously reported in this patient population suggests an important contribution of copanlisib to these favorable responses. Spatial profiling further revealed that PIK3CA-mutated tumors had expected pre-treatment activation of PI3K-AKT signaling and a trend towards compensatory MAPK-ERK activation following treatment, further suggesting on-target effects of copanlisib. However, we could not discern whether alteration of tumor-intrinsic signaling or immune modulation primarily drove the therapeutic response from copanlisib.

Although a subset of patients with PI3K-pathway alterations achieved durable benefit, most experienced primary progression, underscoring the complexity of therapeutically targeting this pathway. One possibility is that copanlisib’s broad PI3K inhibition enhanced CD8+ T cell activation yet simultaneously constrained effector function through on-target inhibition of PI3Kδ/γ in T cells21. More selective PI3Kα inhibitors such as alpelisib or inavolisib, already used clinically in breast cancer, may better exploit tumor-intrinsic PIK3CA activation while minimizing unintended suppression of antitumor immunity. Copanlisib has been withdrawn from the US market after failing to demonstrate an OS benefit in follicular lymphoma. Therefore, to our knowledge, there are no currently active trials further evaluating copanlisib and nivolumab in CRC. However, we anticipate continued discussion on how to best leverage the immune modulatory effects of PI3K inhibition in CRC and other PIK3CA mutated tumors.

Results summary

The combination of copanlisib and nivolumab led to remarkable, durable responses of over 2 years in a subset of patients with MSS metastatic CRC enriched for those with alterations in the PI3K pathway. We found that the presence of higher baseline levels of CD3+CD8+ and CD163+ cells was associated with favorable responses to treatment, which may provide additional biomarkers for selecting patients most likely to benefit from this treatment approach. Paired biopsy pharmacodynamic profiling identified a dynamic increase in infiltrating CD3+CD8+ T cells following copanlisib/nivolumab treatment, and high baseline pAKT levels in the PIK3CA mutant tumors and subsequent rise in pERK activity following treatment. This argues for further exploration of combinatorial approaches involving MAPK pathway inhibitors and immunotherapy in PI3K-altered disease.

These critical biologic insights will help guide future trial design and inform which patients are most likely to benefit from this treatment approach. Additionally, we hope that this trial framework with a focus on obtaining correlative specimens for translational investigations will be increasingly utilized as part of clinical trial design to accelerate our understanding of pharmacodynamic effects and resistance mechanisms to novel treatments.

Limitations

This open-label, multicenter phase 1/2 trial has several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results. Foremost, this is a small study with a limited number of responders (n = 4) and overall patients with PIK3CA mutant (n = 22) and wild-type disease (n = 17) making this study underpowered to draw definitive conclusions on overall activity or predictive biomarkers. This is particularly true with subgroup analysis, such as patients with different PIK3CA mutant subtypes. To dissect how this treatment approach influences the TME, we obtained baseline and on-treatment biopsies. While we obtained biopsies from the same lesion for the two time points, these biopsies only sample a portion of the patient’s lesion, introducing the possibility of sampling bias. Additionally, on-treatment biopsies from some patients, particularly those with favorable treatment responses, had too few neoplastic cells for investigation of MAPK-AKT signaling activity.

Methods

Study design

This trial was an open-label, multicenter phase 1/2 two-arm investigation at the Sidney Kimmel Comprehensive Cancer Center (SKCCC) at Johns Hopkins University (JHU), Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH), and Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI) evaluating the efficacy of combining copanlisib and nivolumab in metastatic MSS CRC (NCT03711058) that had progressed on at least 2 lines of prior therapy, including fluoropyrimidine, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan-containing regimens. Patients were enrolled through referral from their treating physician or self-referral. This may lead to some self-selection bias of patients that are eager to participate in studies. Patients with KRAS/NRAS/BRAF wild-type disease were required to have received or refused anti-EGFR therapy. After an initial phase 1 portion to establish the safety of copanlisib in combination with nivolumab and determine a recommended phase 2 dose, the phase 2 portion of this investigation divided patients with CRC into two cohorts, one for patients with PIK3CA wild-type tumors and one for patients with PIK3CA-mutated tumors. The primary objective of the phase 2 portion was to determine the 6-month ORR of patients treated with copanlisib and nivolumab in each cohort. This is defined as the number of patients with complete or partial response at their 6 months evaluation after cycle 1 day 1. Secondary objectives included determining the 6-month disease control rate (DCR), duration of response (DoR), PFS, and OS in both the overall and PIK3CA-mutant cohorts. We also evaluated changes in PI3K signaling in tumor cells, the composition of the TME, and the states of circulating immune cell populations during treatment with this combination. A copy of the study protocol and statistical analysis plan is available at: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03711058#more-information. There were no major deviations from the original planned analyses.

Enrolled patients were treated with nivolumab 480 mg IV on day 1 and copanlisib IV on days 1, 8, and 15 of a 28-day cycle. For the phase 1 portion, patients were treated with copanlisib at 60 mg IV over 60 min or dose level 1 with plans to perform dose reductions if excessive dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were observed using standard 3 + 3 design. This copanlisib dose was determined based on monotherapy data in hematologic malignancies. Patients were treated until disease progression (allowing treatment beyond progression if suspicion for pseudoprogression), unacceptable adverse events, or withdrawal of consent. The protocol was approved by the institutional review boards (IRBs) at JHU, MGH, and DCFI and complied with the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects and the Declaration of Helsinki. Eligible patients were enrolled centrally at the SKCCC at JHU. All patients provided written informed consent for this study. The trial was registered under ClinicalTrials.gov as (NCT03711058).

Patients

Patients were eligible for this trial if they were 18 years or older with histologically confirmed metastatic CRC with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status ≤1 and adequate organ function as defined by absolute neutrophil count ≥1500/µL, platelets ≥75,000/µL, hemoglobin ≥8.0 g/dL, serum creatinine ≤1.5 x ULN, urine protein:creatinine ratio <3.5 on a random urine sample, total bilirubin ≤1.5 x ULN, AST and ALT ≤ 3 x ULN, Lipase <1.5 x ULN, HbA1c ≤ 7%, Glucose <160 mg/dL (fasting) or <200 mg/dL (non-fasting), International normalized ratio (INR) and partial thromboplastin time (PTT) ≤ 1.5 x ULN unless on anticoagulation, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥ 50%. Patient’s tumors had to be deemed microsatellite stable/mismatch repair proficient by a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendment (CLIA) certified laboratory using either polymerase chain reaction-based microsatellite testing or immunohistochemistry for MMR proteins. Tumor PIK3CA mutation status had to be determined by CLIA certified next generation sequencing. Measurable disease according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 criteria was also required. Sex, race, and ethnicity information were collected by self-report, but because of the small size of these subgroups, no sex specific analysis was performed. For the phase 1 portion, patients were enrolled irrespective of PIK3CA status, in the phase 2 portion, patients were divided into two cohorts based on their PIK3CA mutation status (Cohort A: patients with PIK3CA wild-type CRC, Cohort B: patients with CRC containing a pathogenic PIK3CA mutation). For exploratory mutation analysis, patients were considered to have PTEN inactivation if they possessed at least one PTEN deletion or carried an inactivating frameshift or single-nucleotide mutation.

Mass cytometry (CyTOF) staining

PBMCs were obtained at baseline and cycle 3, day 1. Aliquots of previously frozen PBMCs were thawed with 250 µL of Benzonase media. Cells then underwent repeated washings with 2 mM EDTA diluted in Maxpar PBS. Viability was assessed using 5 μM cis-platinum (Standard Biotools) in Maxpar PBS for 2.5 minutes at RT followed by a media quench. These cells were washed with 2 washes of Maxpar Cell Staining Buffer (CSB), followed by staining with CD45-based barcodes for 25 min at RT utilizing a 7-choose-3 scheme. Samples were then washed x 3 with Maxpar CSB, samples batched, and blocking of the Fc (Invitrogen). Cells were then stained for surface markers using an antibody cocktail targeting chemokine receptors for 10 min at 37 °C and then remaining markers for 30 min at RT (Supplementary Data 1). Cells were then washed and permeabilized/fixed using the Fix/Perm Kit (eBioscience) to facilitate staining with the intracellular antibody cocktail (Supplementary Data 1). Fully stained cells were then fixed with 1.6% PFA (Thermofisher) at RT for 15 min and labeled with 1:500 rhodium (Standard Biotools) at RT for 30 min. Samples were then run on the Helios machine in the Johns Hopkins Mass Cytometry Facility.

CyTOF preprocessing and analysis

To ensure standardized results, data was randomized, beads were used for normalization and then cleared using CyTOF software (v6.7, Standard Biotools). Cells were gated using FlowJo (v10.5, BD Biosciences) based on rhodium and event lengths. Platinum signal was used to gate out dead events. Individual samples were then de-barcoded based on hierarchical gating of 7 CD45 barcode channels. R v4.2.2 was then used for further analysis of processed FCS files. FlowSOM was used to cluster data. Graphpad prism v10 was used for data analysis and display.

Tissue preparation

Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks were cut at 5-µm thickness using a rotary microtome at the Brigham and Women’s Hospital (BWH) Histopathology Core Laboratory. Slides were mounted onto Superfrost Plus microscope glass slides and dried at 37 °C overnight and baked at 59 °C for 1 h.

Orion multiplex immunofluorescence

Orion multiplex immunofluorescence (IF) imaging uses specialized fluorophores with narrow excitation and emission spectra to provide for 18-plex IF in a single imaging run. We employed Orion multiplex IF to provide a basic characterization of the immune microenvironment of the full set of available specimens using a previously standardized antibody panel (Supplementary Table 1) and which have been previously validated29. Unstained 5 µm thick FFPE slides were deparaffinized and stained with a fluorophore-conjugated IF antibodies. Stained slides were then imaged with our Orion instrument (RareCyte). Raw microscopy images were then stitched together with MCMICRO software49.

Cyclic immunofluorescence

Cyclic immunofluorescence (CyCIF) is a multiplex IF method that generates high-plex data by cyclic acquisition; it is slower but more flexible than Orion and we used it in two separate experiments to examine the activation state of the AKT/PI3K signaling cascade and the activation and exhaustion states of T cells29. To determine the influence of copanlisib and nivolumab on activation of the MAPK-AKT pathway, we applied CyCIF using a marker panel designed to capture the activation state of multiple MAPK pathway markers, including phospho-ERK (pERK), phospho-AKT (pAKT), phospho-S6 (pS6; with markers for phosphorylation at both Ser-235 and Ser-240), as well as markers to capture cell cycle state, including phospho-RB (pRB), the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21, cell proliferation markers Ki-67 and PCNA, stromal and tumor markers including smooth muscle actin (SMA), PanCK and CDX2, among others (Supplementary Table 2). To determine the activation and exhaustion state of intratumoral T cells, we applied CyCIF using a marker panel designed to capture key features of T-cell differentiation, effector function, and checkpoint inhibition. This panel included markers of T-cell activation and signaling (SLAMF6, CD134/OX40), cytotoxic effector molecules (granzyme B), lineage and identity markers (CD3d, CD8a, TCF1), transcriptional regulation of effector fate (t-BET), and canonical markers of T-cell exhaustion and dysfunction (LAG3, TIM3), as well as tissue-resident memory markers (CD103) and antigen-presenting cell marker CD11c, among others (Supplementary Table 3). Unstained 5 micron FFPE slides were deparaffinized and stained with cyclic application of four IF antibodies followed by imaging and photobleaching33. Stitching, channel registration, and illumination correction were performed with

MCMICRO software. Antibodies were qualified in multiple FFPE tissues as part of highly multiplexed antibody panels, including multiple clones for some protein targets. Staining patterns were compared to IHC and quantitative analysis of marker correlation in tissues, single cells and sub-cellular features50.

Pathology review and cell quantification

Biopsies were annotated by inspection of adjacent H&E, Orion and CyCIF images to delineate the tumor compartment from the surrounding stroma and to remove from further analysis biopsy fragments containing only normal tissue or acellular debris. For both Orion and CyCIF images, only the tumor compartment was considered for quantitative analysis. Using MCMICRO software, individual cell nuclei were computationally segmented using the nuclear stain (Hoechst) signal. Cytoplasmic and membranous signal was incorporated by modeling a cell as a nuclear mask plus a 5-µm isotropic expansion. The mean marker intensity for each cell and marker was calculated using MCMICRO software. Continuous marker intensities were then converted to binary marker classifications (positive or negative) using a Gaussian mixture model and confirmed by inspection. To ensure immune cell specificity, we required cells to be positive for at least two lineage markers to be included (e.g., cytotoxic T cells as CD45+, CD3+, and CD8+). Overall, we judged the procedure to result in a low level of cell misclassification with 86% of CD8+ cells also scoring as positive for CD3, and 93% of all PD-L1+ cells being either CD45+CD163+ macrophages or PanCK-positive tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Cell fractions and percentages were calculated as the total number of cells positive for the marker combinations under consideration (e.g., CD3+ and CD8+), divided by the total number of cells within the tumor compartment, except as explicitly otherwise described. Statistically testing for unpaired samples was performed using Wilcoxon rank sum test using R (4.3.2) software. Statistical testing comparing paired pre- and on-treatment biopsy samples was performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank test using R (4.3.2).

Statistics and reproducibility

The primary endpoint of the phase 2 portion of this study was objective response at 6 months. The null hypothesis was an ORR of ≤5%, and the alternative hypothesis was an ORR of ≥20%. Each cohort was assessed separately. Cohorts were molecularly defined and no randomization or blinding was utilized. A Simon’s two-stage design was used. A total of 12 patients were enrolled in the first stage, if none of these patients had a partial or complete response then enrollment in that cohort was discontinued. Any cohort that had at least one of the initial 12 patients have a partial or complete response to treatment, an additional 9 patients would be enrolled in that cohort (21 patients total). The trial was designed to have 80% power with a one-sided significance level of 0.1. If ≥3 patients had a response in either cohort, the null hypothesis was rejected and it would be considered an active treatment combination for that cohort. ORR is summarized using proportion along with 95% Wilson confidence interval. Other binary outcomes including DCR at 6 months and immunologic response were analyzed using similar methods. PFS and OS were measured from time of first dose to time of event of interest. Patients without evidence of progression or death were censored at the time of last documented scan or contact respectively up to 4/17/2024. No data were excluded from the analyses. All analyses were performed with R (version 4.3.0). A 2-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
