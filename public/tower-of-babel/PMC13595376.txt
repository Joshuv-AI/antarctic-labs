Socioeconomic Disparities in PFAS Exposure and Susceptibility to Diabetes in Chinese Adults: A Nationwide Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Saisai Ji, Jinghua Wang, Yingli Qu, Zheng Li, Yitao Pan, Yawei Li, Haocan Song, Linna Xie, Wenli Zhang, Jiayi Cai, Feng Zhao, Ying Zhu, Zhaojin Cao, Shilu Tong, Yuebin Lv, Jiayin Dai, Guang Jia, Xiaoming Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence indicates that socioeconomic status (SES) shapes not only patterns of environmental exposure but also the susceptibility to environmental hazards. Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) represent a distinctive case in which socioeconomically disadvantaged populations often exhibit lower exposure levels, raising critical questions about how such exposure patterns translate into health inequalities, particularly for diabetes. Using cross-sectional data from 10 302 adults participating in the China National Human Biomonitoring (CNHBM) program, we examined socioeconomic disparities in serum PFAS concentrations and assessed whether lower SES is associated with stronger PFAS-diabetes associations among Chinese adults. We found that individuals with higher SES exhibited 4–10% higher serum concentrations of PFOA, PFUnDA, PFHxS, PFHpS, PFOS, and 6:2 Cl-PFESA (all p values <0.05). In contrast, associations between PFAS exposure and diabetes were generally stronger among low-SES participants. Across multiple PFAS, odds ratios for diabetes in the low-SES group were 0–17% greater relative to the high-SES group, with statistically significant effect modification observed for PFUnDA, PFOS, and 6:2 Cl-PFESA. These findings indicate that socioeconomic disadvantage may be associated with greater susceptibility, even at lower exposure levels, underscoring the importance of considering differential susceptibility when assessing environmental health risks and advancing health equity.

Introduction

Environmental exposures and their associated health consequences are unevenly distributed across populations, reflecting persistent social and economic inequalities. Socioeconomic status (SES), widely understood as an individual’s position within a hierarchical social structure commonly determined by education, occupation, and income, influences both exposure patterns and susceptibility to environmental hazards. ,

Perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), a class of synthetic chemicals extensively utilized in numerous commercial products such as food packaging, nonstick cookware, textiles, and other consumer items, appear to present a distinct socioeconomic exposure pattern that diverges from that of conventional industrial pollutants. Multiple studies have consistently reported higher body burdens of PFAS among socioeconomically advantaged populations. − While this pattern appears to contradict traditional environmental justice assumptions centered on exposure inequality, it raises a critical concern: whether lower socioeconomic status is associated with greater susceptibility to PFAS-related health effects, thereby contributing to health inequities, despite lower exposure levels.

The health implications of PFAS are of global concern because of their environmental persistence, bioaccumulation potential, and endocrine disrupting properties. Growing epidemiological evidence has linked PFAS exposure to diabetes, − a chronic metabolic disease that represents a major global public health burden and exhibits pronounced socioeconomic gradients. , Our prior research in a nationwide Chinese population similarly identified positive associations between serum PFAS concentrations and diabetes. However, most existing studies have focused on population-average associations, with far less attention paid to whether socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with greater susceptibility to PFAS-associated diabetes. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the elevated diabetes risk among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations may be underestimated when SES-based susceptibility is not considered.

SES is a fundamental determinant of health and a major driver of health inequities worldwide. , Lower SES is associated with a higher prevalence of diabetes and with social and structural conditionsincluding limited access to healthcare, unhealthier behaviors, , a greater burden of chronic comorbid conditions, , and chronic psychosocial stress associated with higher allostatic load , that may increase susceptibility to environmentally related diseases. Previous studies have demonstrated that associations between environmental exposures and diabetes are often stronger among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. For example, in a study from Northern Chile, the association between arsenic exposure and diabetes was stronger among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations than among their higher-SES counterparts. Similarly, in a Danish study, the association between chronic air pollution and type 2 diabetes (T2D) was higher among lower educated participants than among those with high education. These findings suggest that socioeconomic disadvantages may be associated with greater susceptibility to environmental metabolic hazards.

Despite growing recognition of socioeconomic differences in environmental susceptibility, few studies have simultaneously examined socioeconomic disparities in PFAS exposure and SES-based heterogeneity in PFAS-diabetes associations. As a result, it remains unclear whether lower-SES populations experience heightened susceptibility to PFAS-associated diabetes, even when their PFAS exposure levels are lower than those of socioeconomically advantaged groups.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to examine socioeconomic disparities in serum PFAS concentrations and to investigate whether lower SES is associated with stronger PFAS-diabetes associations. We hypothesized that socioeconomically disadvantaged groups would have lower serum PFAS concentrations but stronger associations between PFAS exposure and diabetes than those of socioeconomically advantaged groups. Using data from the China National Human Biomonitoring (CNHBM) program, a nationwide survey covering 31 provinces and populations with diverse demographic, geographic, and environmental backgrounds, this study aims to identify vulnerable socioeconomic groups and inform environmental justice-oriented strategies for reducing PFAS-related health inequalities.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

The CNHBM, designed and conducted by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), is a continuous, population-based cohort study that focuses on the health influence of environmental exposures across the mainland China. Detailed program procedures of CNHBM have been described previously. At the 2017–2018 baseline survey, 21 888 participants between the ages of 3 to 79 were randomly selected from 456 communities across 152 monitoring locations in 31 provinces of China. Participants provided informed consent and underwent structured interviews, physical examinations, and biospecimen collections. Ethical approval was granted from the Ethical Review Committee of the National Institute of Environmental Health, China CDC (no. 201701).

In this cross-sectional study, adults between the ages of 18 and 79 in the 2017–2018 baseline survey were considered for inclusion. We excluded participants with missing data on serum PFAS concentrations (n = 345), those with undefined diabetes status (n = 77), and individuals with missing data on covariates (n = 475). Participants who had self-reported cancer (n = 17) were also excluded, leaving <10 302 participants in the study (Figure S1). The analysis was conducted by using a complete-case approach.

Serum PFAS Measurement

The serum concentrations of 18 targeted PFAS were determined using ultraperformance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) in CNHBM. The names, abbreviations, and limits of quantitation (LOQs) of these 18 PFAS are detailed in Table S1. Rigorous quality control procedures were enforced throughout the PFAS determination process, ensuring that the coefficients of variation of test results for the quality control sample across batches remained below 15%. In this study, nine PFAS with detection rates exceeding 50% were included, including four perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids (PFCAs) (PFOA, PFNA, PFDA, and PFUnDA), three perfluoroalkanesulfonic acids (PFSAs) (PFHxS, PFHpS, and PFOS), and two PFAS alternatives (6:2 Cl-PFESA and 8:2 Cl-PFESA). For concentrations that fell below the LOQ, values were imputed using the LOQ divided by the square root of two.

Assessment of Socioeconomic Status

Education attainment, employment status, and household income were used to assess SES according to previous studies. , Education attainment was classified into primary or below, secondary, and college or above. Employment status was categorized into three groups: not working, working (including full or part-time workers and students), or retired. Annual household income was self-reported and divided into five groups: 30 000 Chinese yuan (CNY), 30 000−<60 000 CNY, 60 000−<100 000 CNY, ≥100 000 CNY, or unknown. The “unknown” category was retained in the latent class analysis to minimize sample loss. The latent class analysis was used to construct an overall SES indicator based on education attainment, employment status, and household income. Two classes of SES (low and high) were identified according to the item-response probabilities. The latent class analysis procedure was described in the Supporting Information (Supplementary Methods, Table S10 and S11).

Assessment of Diabetes

Measurements of fasting serum glucose were conducted using Roche Cobas c702 automated biochemical analyzers with reagents from Roche Diagnostics (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). Diabetes was identified if fasting serum glucose levels reached or exceeded 7.0 mmol/L, if there was a physician-confirmed diagnosis, or if there was a self-reported use of diabetes medications such as oral hypoglycemics or insulin. ,

Covariates Assessment

A variety of socio-demographic and lifestyle-related covariates were assessed through face-to-face interviews, including residential areas, daily activity duration, smoking status, excessive drinking, and dietary habits. Residential area was classified as urban or rural according to the type of residential administrative unit at sampling, with village committees classified as rural and community committees classified as urban. Daily activity duration was self-reported in minutes per day and categorized as <20, 20−<60, or ≥ 60 min. Smoking status was defined based on self-reported current use of any tobacco product (including cigarettes, cigars, and pipes), and participants were categorized as current smokers or noncurrent smokers, with the latter including both former smokers and never smokers. Excessive drinking was defined as reporting four or more drinking episodes per week. Data on food intake was assessed through a food frequency questionnaire. A low-risk diet was defined as meeting at least four out of the following five food behaviors: daily intake of fresh vegetables, daily intake of fresh fruits, red meat consumption between one to six times a week, legumes at least four times a week, and fish consumption weekly. Participants who met at least four of these criteria were classified as having a low-risk diet. Body mass index (BMI) was categorized as underweight, normal, overweight, and obese based on thresholds established for Chinese adults. Hypertriglyceridemia wad defined as triglyceride levels at or above 2.3 mmol/L. Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation. CKD was identified if the GFR was less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a self-reported physician’s diagnosis. The family history of diabetes was defined as self-reported if any of their parents has had diabetes.

Statistical Analysis

PFAS concentrations were skewed distributed and natural log (ln)-transformed. Geometric means (GMs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to describe PFAS concentrations. Pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated among the nine ln-transformed PFAS concentrations, and the results were visualized using a correlation heatmap. Associations between socioeconomic indicators (educational attainment, employment status, household income, and a composite SES index) and PFAS exposure levels were assessed using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with survey sites considered as random effects and participants as fixed effects. Geometric mean ratios (GMRs) were estimated to quantify the relative differences in PFAS concentrations across socioeconomic strata.

Socioeconomic modification of the associations between PFAS exposure and diabetes was evaluated. GLMMs were used to estimate subgroup-specific odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs for diabetes. First, individual PFAS were modeled as categorical variables using quartiles (Q1-Q4) to estimate ORs separately in low- and high-SES groups, as defined by a composite SES index. Second, we performed stratified analyses using PFAS concentrations as continuous variables after natural log transformation, with stratification based on education level, employment status, household income, and the composite SES index. Heterogeneity in ORs across subgroups was assessed through pairwise Z-tests to evaluate interaction. To account for multiple pairwise comparison within each socioeconomic indicator, Bonferroni-adjusted p values were calculated according to the number of comparisons against the reference category.

Covariates were selected a priori based on previous literature and causal considerations informed by directed acyclic graphs (Figures S2 and S3). For the analyses of associations between SES and serum PFAS concentrations, models were adjusted for age, sex, residential area, smoking status, drinking, activity, and dietary habits. For the SES-stratified analyses of associations between PFAS and diabetes, models were adjusted for age, sex, residential area, smoking status, drinking, activity, dietary habits, body mass index, hypertriglyceridemia, and family history of diabetes.

The weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression model was employed to evaluate the relationship of combined exposure to nine PFAS with diabetes in the low- and high-SES subgroups. In the models, a weighted linear index was constructed by categorizing the PFAS into quartiles. This index quantified the cumulative exposure to the nine PFAS, with the weight assigned to each PFAS indicating its individual contribution to the overall WQS index.

Two sensitivity analyses were undertaken to evaluate the robustness of SES effect modification in the relationship between PFAS and diabetes. First, we excluded participants with CKD to eliminate potential confounding effects due to impaired renal function on PFAS excretion. Second, considering seafood as a major source of PFAS exposure, we excluded participants who reported weekly seafood consumption.

All data analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4, except for the latent class analysis, which was conducted using R version 4.5.0. Statistical significance was determined by a two-sided p-value of below 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Population

Overall, 10 302 adults (mean age: 48.01 years, standard deviation [SD]: 15.99 years) from CNHBM were included in the study (Figure S1). Of them, 5172 (50.20%) were men and 5646 (54.80%) resided in urban areas. A total of 5387 were classified into the low-SES group. Compared with the individuals with high SES, participants of low SES tended to be older, women, rural residents, excessive drinkers, less active, and less frequently consume seafood and were less likely to adhere to low-risk dietary habits. Additionally, they exhibited a higher prevalence of CKD and diabetes but a lower prevalence for hypertriglyceridemia and a lower frequency of a family history of diabetes (Table ).

Disparities in Serum PFAS Concentrations by SES

Serum PFAS concentrations exhibited pronounced socioeconomic gradients (Figure and Tables S3–S6). After adjustment for covariates, higher educational attainment was associated with elevated levels of multiple PFAS, including PFOA, PFUnDA, PFHxS, PFHpS, PFOS, and 6:2 Cl-PFESA. Compared with participants with primary education or below, the GMRs for these PFAS among those with secondary education ranged from 1.04 (95% CI: 1.01, 1.07) to 1.08 (95% CI: 1.04, 1.12), whereas the corresponding GMRs among those with college education or above ranged from 1.06 (95% CI: 1.02, 1.11) to 1.11 (95% CI: 1.05, 1.17). Similar patterns were observed across employment status and household income strata, with full estimates shown in Tables S4 and S5. The composite SES indicator revealed pronounced disparities: high-SES individuals exhibited 4–10% greater concentrations of PFOA, PFUnDA, PFHxS, PFHpS, PFOS, and 6:2 Cl-PFESA compared with low-SES participants, with GMRs ranging from 1.04 (95% CI: 1.02, 1.07) to 1.10 (95% CI: 1.06, 1.13).

PFAS concentrations were positively correlated with each other in the total population as well as in the low- and high-SES groups, with all pairwise correlations statistically significant (p < 0.001; Figures S4–S6). Correlations were generally stronger within the same PFAS subclasses, including among PFNA, PFDA, and PFUnDA; among PFHxS, PFHpS, and PFOS; and between 6:2 Cl-PFESA and 8:2 Cl-PFESA. By comparison, PFOA showed weaker correlations with the other PFAS. Overall, the correlation patterns were similar between the low- and high-SES groups.

Overall, ORs for associations of PFAS with diabetes ranged from 0.96 (95% CI: 0.86, 1.06) to 1.18 (95% CI: 1.07, 1.30) in the low-SES populations and from 0.86 (95% CI: 0.78, 0.96) to 1.11 (95% CI: 0.98, 1.25) in the high-SES populations. Compared with individuals of high SES, those with low SES exhibited 0–17% higher ORs for the relationship of PFAS exposure with diabetes, with statistically significant heterogeneity observed for PFUnDA, PFOS, and 6:2 Cl-PFESA (Bonferroni-adjusted p values for heterogeneity = 0.037, 0.017, and 0.023, respectively).

In WQS regression analyses of the nine-PFAS mixture, positive associations with diabetes were observed in both the low-SES (OR = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.09, 1.40) and high-SES groups (OR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.10, 1.46), with no statistically significant heterogeneity between groups (Bonferroni-adjusted p value for heterogeneity = 0.781). The corresponding WQS weights indicated that PFHxS (0.29), PFNA (0.28), and PFHpS (0.22) contributed most strongly in the low-SES group, whereas PFHxS (0.90) contributed predominantly in the high-SES group (Table S7).

Sensitivity Analyses

In sensitivity analyses, the observed effect modification by SES in PFAS-diabetes associations became a little stronger after excluding participants with CKD (Table S8). However, when individuals reporting weekly seafood consumption were excluded, a significant SES-based heterogeneity persisted only for the association between PFOS and diabetes (Table S9).

Discussion

Our analysis of a nationally representative sample of more than 10 000 Chinese adults showed a pronounced socioeconomic gradient in strength of PFAS-diabetes associations. Individuals with higher SES consistently exhibited higher serum concentrations of multiple PFAS. In contrast, associations between PFAS exposure and diabetes were consistently stronger among individuals with lower SES. This inverse patternlower exposure but stronger PFAS-diabetes associationswas robust across multiple PFAS compounds, SES indicators, and sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest that socioeconomic disadvantage may be linked to greater susceptibility to PFAS-associated diabetes. They also underscore the importance of differential susceptibility in environmental justice and health equity research.

Consistent with previous studies conducted in the United States, Europe, and Norway, we observed higher PFAS body burdens among individuals with higher SES. Importantly, the broadly similar inter-PFAS correlation patterns across SES groups suggest that socioeconomic differences in PFAS body burdens were more likely to reflect differences in overall exposure levels rather than substantial differences in the coexposure structure. This socioeconomic gradient in PFAS exposure likely stems from multiple pathways linked to lifestyle and consumption. In the general population, PFAS exposure primarily occurs through contaminated food and drinking water and using fluorinated consumer products. Our data indicate that high-SES individuals report a higher consumption frequency of certain foods, such as seafooda recognized dietary source of PFAS. , Furthermore, higher-SES groups likely have greater purchasing power and consumption frequency of PFAS-containing goods, such as stain-resistant textiles, premium cosmetics, and specialized outdoor gear, all of which are significant contributors to body burden. , For example, fluorinated waxes used in skiing and snowboardingactivities more prevalent among higher-SES populations in Chinahave been identified as significant sources of PFAS exposure. , This consumption pattern is corroborated by a national assessment of landfill leachate in China, which found that waste streams from more affluent economic areas are enriched with PFAS derived from discarded consumer products. Collectively, these findings confirm that PFAS represent a class of consumer-associated chemicals with a socioeconomic exposure pattern distinct from that of traditional industrial contaminants.

This study suggests that socioeconomic disadvantage may be associated with greater susceptibility to PFAS-associated diabetes even in the context of lower exposure levels. Although cumulative PFAS exposure showed broadly similar associations with diabetes in the low- and high-SES groups, the individual PFAS analyses consistently revealed stronger associations among low-SES populations. This discrepancy suggests that the observed SES-related heterogeneity in PFAS-diabetes associations may be more compound-specific than mixture-wide. Moreover, the broadly similar inter-PFAS correlation patterns across SES groups suggest that this heterogeneity is more likely to be explained by differential susceptibility rather than differences in the PFAS mixture structure. Together, these findings underscore an important distinction between exposure inequality and susceptibility inequality, suggesting that environmental health disparities may arise not only from unequal exposure but also from systematic differences in susceptibility to environmental contaminants.

Several interconnected pathways may explain the stronger PFAS-diabetes associations observed among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. First, lower SES is often associated with a higher prevalence of adverse health behaviors. In our analysis, low-SES individuals were more likely to engage in excessive alcohol consumption, exhibit lower levels of activity, and adhere less frequently to low-risk dietary patterns. These factors may contribute to insulin resistance and chronic inflammation, , thereby increasing vulnerability to the metabolic effects of environmental toxicants. Evidence from the Dongfeng-Tongji cohort also found that a healthy lifestyle can modify the relationship of PFAS with T2D. Second, previous studies showed socioeconomically disadvantaged populations are more likely to be related with multiple compounded stressors, including chronic psychosocial stress , and coexposure to other pollutants, which may act synergistically to exacerbate PFAS-related metabolic dysregulation. In particular, chronic psychosocial stress can dysregulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to elevated cortisol levels that interfere with glucose metabolism, activation of the immune system, and an increased risk of T2D. Third, limited access to healthcare, lower health literacy, and delayed disease detection among low-SES populations , may reduce opportunities for early screening, timely risk communication, and appropriate management of metabolic abnormalities, thereby allowing PFAS-related disturbances to progress to clinically diagnosed diabetes. Together, these social and structural factors may help explain why stronger PFAS-diabetes associations were observed among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, even when their exposure levels are lower.

The attenuation of SES-related heterogeneity after excluding participants reporting weekly seafood consumption may reflect both differences in PFAS exposure sources and potential metabolic implications of seafood consumption. Weekly seafood intake was more common among high-SES participants in our study and may be associated with lower diabetes risk, as suggested by previous studies. , Seafood is also an important source of PFAS exposure. , Excluding these individuals may therefore have disproportionately removed a subgroup of high-SES participants with relatively high PFAS exposure but lower underlying metabolic risk, thereby weakening the observed SES-related heterogeneity.

Our findings are consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating socioeconomic modification of associations between environmental exposures and health outcomes. ,,, A study from the ABCD Study in the United States showed that children from lower-income families exhibit more negative brain and cognitive outcomes compared with the children from higher-income families. A prospective study of 1,843,597 persons in Denmark demonstrated weaker associations between air pollution and T2D among highly educated populations compared with low/medium education. A case-control study (N = 1301) in Northern Chile found stronger associations between arsenic and diabetes in people with low SES versus high-SES groups. Together with these studies, our results suggest that PFAS are not unique in exhibiting SES-based heterogeneity in health effects. Rather, PFAS-associated diabetes appears to reflect a broader pattern in which socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with a greater susceptibility to environmentally related diseases.

This pattern points to a critical public health inequity: despite lower PFAS exposure levels, socioeconomically disadvantaged populations appear to bear a disproportionate burden of the PFAS-associated diabetes risk. These findings highlight the need for environmental justice research to move beyond exposure metrics alone and incorporate assessments of health risks and vulnerability. Future research should also move beyond broad SES indicators alone and toward more systematic approaches to characterizing vulnerability in environmental health research. Although a standardized framework has not yet been established for assessing vulnerability in relation to environmental health outcomes, several domains may be considered in future research, including behavioral factors (e.g., diet quality and physical activity), cumulative social and environmental stressors (e.g., psychosocial stress, coexposure to other pollutants, and neighborhood disadvantage), and healthcare- and resource-related factors (e.g., health literacy, access to care, and delayed disease detection).

These findings also have potential implications for public health practice and environmental risk management. Regulatory approaches based primarily on exposure thresholds or population average risk estimates may underestimate health risks in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. Environmental risk assessment frameworks may therefore benefit from considering population vulnerability when evaluating chemicals, such as PFAS. In addition, interventions aimed at reducing PFAS-related health disparities may require a dual-pronged strategy: reducing exposure from relevant environmental and consumer-related sources while also addressing underlying socioeconomic vulnerabilities through strengthened risk communication, improved access to disease screening and preventive care, and interventions targeting modifiable risk factors, such as diet and physical activity.

This study had three key strengths. First, the nationwide program applied a rigorous sampling methodology to select participants across all 31 provinces in mainland China, yielding a large, nationally representative sample, which enhanced the statistical power and the potential for generalizing the findings. Second, stringent quality control measures and standardized methods were implemented throughout each process, ensuring data reliability and accuracy. Finally, we rigorously controlled the major putative confounders and conducted a wide range of sensitivity analyses. Therefore, the main findings from this study were robust; however, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the study’s reliance on a single measurement of PFAS and fasting blood glucose might not fully reflect the variability of these biomarkers and their impact on outcome measures over time. This limitation could lead to misclassification of exposure or outcome, potentially biasing the results. Second, as in any observational study, we could not rule out the possibility of unmeasured and residual confounding. Thus, our results should be interpreted with caution. For example, residual dietary confounding cannot be excluded in the sensitivity analysis because seafood intake was measured only at a relatively coarse level and may not fully capture variation in the quantity, type of seafood, or overall dietary patterns. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the capacity to establish temporality or draw causal inferences regarding PFAS exposure, SES-related heterogeneity, and diabetes. In particular, the observed stronger PFAS-diabetes associations among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups should not be interpreted as causal evidence of an amplified vulnerability. In addition, because chronic disease status may affect PFAS excretion, the possibility of reverse causation cannot be excluded. Prospective cohort studies are warranted to verify the findings from this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this nationwide study provides evidence that socioeconomic disadvantage may be related to greater susceptibility to PFAS-associated diabetes even at lower exposure levels. By demonstrating that socioeconomic disparities in environmental health may be driven by differential susceptibility rather than exposure alone, our findings advance environmental justice research and emphasize the importance of integrating the social context into environmental health risk assessment and prevention strategies.
