Vaccination coverage and inequalities in childhood immunisation in Ethiopia: a secondary analysis of the PMA national longitudinal survey
Authors: Asebe Hagos, Mengistie Kassahun Tariku, Gezahegn Eshetu Mekuriya, Habtamu Wagnew Abuhay, Lidetu Demoze, Gedefaw Abeje Masresha, Mekuriaw Nibret Aweke, Habtamu Abebe Getahun, Samuel Teferi Chanie, Gelila Yitageasu, Gebrie Getu Alemu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to assess vaccination coverage and inequalities in childhood immunisation among children aged 12 months in Ethiopia.DesignPerformance monitoring for action Ethiopia implemented a nationally representative first-cohort longitudinal survey (2019–2021), collecting data at baseline, 6 weeks, 6 months and 1 year post partum to monitor reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health indicators.SettingsThe study was conducted from five Ethiopian regions: three predominantly agrarian (Amhara, Oromia and Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples), one pastoralist (Afar) and one urban (Addis Ababa).ParticipantsThe weighted sample comprised 1888 participants for full vaccine coverage (FVC) and 1677 for both incomplete vaccine coverage (IVC) and dropout rate (DOR) between pentavalent-1 and pentavalent-3 vaccines.Outcome measuresThe vaccine coverage and inequalities were assessed using three indicators: FVC, IVC and DOR (between the first and third doses of the pentavalent vaccine). Key dimensions of inequality included maternal age, economic status, education level, place of residence and subnational regions. The analysis was conducted using Health Equity Assessment Toolkit Plus software, employing seven summary measures of inequalities: Difference, Ratio, Population Attributable Risk, Population Attributable Fraction, Slope Index of Inequality, Relative Index of Inequality and the Theil Index.ResultThe study found that only 29.1% of 12-month-old children in Ethiopia had received full vaccination, whereas 67.2% had incomplete coverage and the DOR between the first and third doses of the pentavalent vaccine was 34.3%. Both simple and complex inequality measures revealed a high level of inequality in childhood immunisation indicators across wealth, education, place of residence and regions. FVC was higher among children of higher socioeconomic status and urban residence, whereas IVC and DOR were disproportionately higher among children from the poorest households, mothers with no education, rural residents and disadvantaged regions.ConclusionsChildhood immunisation coverage in Ethiopia remains low and marked by high inequality. Higher rates of incomplete vaccination and vaccine dropout are disproportionately concentrated among children from low socioeconomic backgrounds, rural residents and disadvantaged regions. These disparities reflect substantial health inequities in childhood vaccinations as they are unfair and avoidable with appropriate interventions. The study underscores that addressing socioeconomic and geographic inequalities could substantially improve the national immunisation coverage and accelerate progress towards universal health coverage targets.Keywords: Vaccination, Ethiopia, HEALTH ECONOMICS, Health Equity, STATISTICS & RESEARCH METHODS

Introduction

Immunisation is one of the safest and most effective public health interventions, preventing infectious diseases and their complications.1 2 Childhood vaccines protect children against illness such as tuberculosis, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, polio, hepatitis B, haemophilus influenzae type b infection, pneumococcal pneumonia, diarrhoea and measles.3 A cornerstone of global health success, immunisation saves millions of lives annually, with vaccines now available for over 20 infectious diseases, improves health and longevity across all age groups.4 In low- and middle-income countries, immunisation has been pivotal in reducing childhood mortality over the past few decades.5 Since the launch of the Expanded Programme on Immunisation in 1974, vaccination has averted an estimated 154 million deaths among under five children worldwide, making it one of the most impactful health interventions in history.2

Ethiopia has made notable strides in expanding childhood immunisation coverage over the past 20 years. However, the country has yet to meet its national targets and the goals set in the 2030 Immunisation Agenda.6 Studies have documented substantial gaps in childhood immunisation services across Ethiopia, with full vaccine coverage (FVC) rates ranging from 32% to 66.4%,7,10 far below the desired targets. Alarmingly, over half of Ethiopian children remain incompletely immunised, while nearly 20% of receive no vaccine at all during their first year of life.9 11 These coverage gaps are especially stark in underserved communities including developing and pastoralist regions, internally displaced populations, newly established administrative regions and conflict-affected areas. A recent study highlights these disparities, showing that these vulnerable groups account for the highest proportion of zero-dose children (33.7%)12 and experience significant pentavalent vaccine dropout rates (DORs) (43.9%).13

Beyond low coverage, studies have identified considerable inequalities in childhood immunisation across Ethiopia.714,17 Health inequalities refer to measurable differences in health outcomes among population subgroups, often defined by demographic, geographic or socioeconomic factors such as age, place of residence, income or education level.18 19 In contrast, health inequities are normative concepts that describe health differences that are avoidable, unfair and unjust.18 19 Health inequalities become inequities when they are both preventable and remediable through policy actions and societal interventions. Such disparities are considered unjust because they could be reduced or eliminated through deliberate efforts, including government programmes, health system reforms or equitable distribution of resources.20 Vaccine access must be equitable across all geographic, socioeconomic or personal characteristics including income, education, ethnicity and disability status.4 20 Ensuring fair access to vaccines is not only a public health imperative but also a fundamental human right and a core component of universal health coverage (UHC).20 However, in Ethiopia, evidence shows that FVC is disproportionately higher among children from wealthier households, urban areas and more advantaged regions.14,1721 Conversely, adverse childhood vaccine indicators such as incomplete vaccine coverage (IVC), high DORs and zero-dose status are more prevalent among disadvantaged children.12 22 Reducing these disparities requires both improving national immunisation coverage and minimising inequality in access.6 A multicounty study across 43 low- and middle-income countries found that low vaccine coverage is strongly associated with high levels of socioeconomic inequality in childhood immunisation status.23 Bridging these gaps demands both higher immunisation coverage and targeted efforts to reduce inequality.

In recent years, reducing health inequalities has become a critical priority for global immunisation programmes, including the 2030 immunisation agenda.6 In alignment with these international goals, the government of Ethiopia is actively working to reduce inequalities in childhood immunisation coverage.24 Tracking inequalities in vaccination coverage helps identify where gaps exist and highlights the status of disadvantaged subgroups in terms of access to and utilisation of immunisation services.18 25 Even though some studies have been conducted on vaccine equity in Ethiopia,14 15 regularly monitoring these disparities over time provides valuable insights into a country’s progress towards promoting health equity.25

In contrast to prior research based on cross-sectional surveys like the Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS), the present study uses the longitudinal design of the performance monitoring for action (PMA). It provides incremental insight by tracking women prospectively from pregnancy to 1 year post partum, which enhances measurement validity. The shorter recall periods inherent in this design mitigate recall bias, a particularly important advantage for mothers with no formal education and children lacking vaccination cards. Moreover, the collection of immunisation status at multiple intervals enables more precise measurement of vaccine initiation, completion and dropout. This study further adds value to the existing literature by employing multiple childhood immunisation indicators and a comprehensive set of summary measures of inequality. Therefore, this study aimed to measure the coverage and level of inequalities in childhood vaccination, using indicators such as FVC, IVC and DOR between pentavalent 1 and pentavalent 3 vaccines in Ethiopia. Insights from this study can support policymakers and health managers in developing evidence-based policies and implementing equity-oriented interventions to improve vaccine coverage and reduce inequalities in childhood immunisation in Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting, design and data source

The study was conducted in Ethiopia, the second most populous country in Africa, with an estimated population of 129.7 million.26 At the time of data collection, Ethiopia was administratively divided into ten regions (later increased to 12): Tigray, Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Somali, Benishangul-Gumuz, Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples (SNNP), Gambella, Harari and Sidama. In addition, there were two city administrations: Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa.

The study used data from PMA Ethiopia, a nationally representative survey designed to track key reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health indicators. The PMA dataset offers robust evidence to inform health policies and programmes at both national and regional levels,27 serving as a vital complement to the DHS by enabling evidence synthesis from multiple sources.

The first cohort (cohort 1) of PMA Ethiopia longitudinal survey was conducted from 2019 to 2021. In this survey, women were followed from pregnancy through 1 year post partum, with data collected at four intervals: a baseline survey, a 6-week postpartum survey, a 6-month postpartum survey and a 1-year postpartum survey. Specifically, the vaccination data used in this study were obtained from the 1-year postpartum survey, conducted between July 2020 and August 2021.27 To construct a comprehensive dataset, we merged information from the 6-week, 6-month and 1-year follow-up surveys with baseline data from the longitudinal survey, using participant identification numbers.28 The PMA dataset is freely available from the Johns Hopkins Research Data Repository online archive doi.org/10.34976/y424-s176.29

The data were collected from five Ethiopian regions: three predominantly agrarian (Amhara, Oromia and SNNP), one pastoralist (Afar) and one urban (Addis Ababa),27 28 which together represent over 80% of the total population.30 A multistage, stratified cluster sampling method was used to recruit study participants. In the first stage, the regions of Amhara, Oromia and SNNP were stratified into urban and rural areas. For Afar and Addis Ababa, the regions themselves served as the rural and urban strata, respectively.27 31 A total of 217 enumeration areas (EAs) were then selected from the six regions using probability proportional to size within each stratum. In the second stage, 35 households were randomly selected from each EA.31

Study population and sample size

Women aged 15–49 years were screened for eligibility, and those who reported being pregnant or having given birth within the past 6 weeks were included in the baseline survey. To be eligible for cohort 1, 1-year follow-up survey, women must have consented to follow-up at baseline, participated in both the 6-week and 6-month follow-up surveys and provided consent for the 1-year follow-up. Women who reported an abortion, had no live birth or did not have surviving infants were excluded from the study.27 28 Missing data for the three vaccine indicators constituted less than 1% of observations. Cases with missing values on these indicators were excluded from the final analysis. Accordingly, weighted sample size was 1888 for FVC and 1677 for both IVC and DOR between pentavalent 1 and pentavalent 3 vaccines.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted electronically using the Open Data Kit platform on mobile tablets. To maintain data quality, PMA Ethiopia organised an extensive 2-week training programme for supervisors and resident enumerators (data collectors). The training covered survey protocols, questionnaire content and interview techniques, and was followed by a 3-day field simulation. Further details are available in the referenced source.31

During the baseline survey, pregnant women provided information on household and socioeconomic characteristics, reproductive and fertility history, and antenatal care services. The 6-week postpartum survey gathered data on maternal and newborn health indicators, including antenatal care, delivery services, postnatal care for mothers, birth outcomes, immediate newborn care, breastfeeding, immunisation and care-seeking for childhood illnesses. At 6 months post partum, information was collected on breastfeeding, infant immunisation, maternal care-seeking for common childhood illnesses and family planning. Finally, the 1 year postpartum survey focused on infant immunisation at 1 year, feeding practices, maternal care-seeking for childhood illnesses and family planning behaviours.27 31

Variables and measurements

The study used three childhood vaccine indicators among children aged 12 months: FVC, IVC and pentavalent 1 to pentavalent 3 vaccine DOR among children aged 12 months.18 20 FVC is defined as the proportion of children who are fully vaccinated by the age of 12 months. A child is considered fully vaccinated if they have received all recommended doses of the following vaccines: BCG, three doses of oral polio vaccine (OPV), three doses of pentavalent vaccine (Penta), three doses of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), two doses of monovalent rotavirus vaccine (Rotarix) and one dose of measles-containing vaccine (MCV). In Ethiopia, the routine immunisation programme includes the Rotarix vaccine and recommends the first dose of the MCV at 9 months of age. Children who receive all these doses are coded as ‘1’ and classified as fully vaccinated, while those who miss one or more doses are coded as ‘0’ and considered not fully vaccinated. IVC indicators refer to the measurement of the proportion of children who have missed one or more of the recommended vaccine doses by the age of 12 months. For this analysis, incomplete vaccination is defined as a child who has started routine immunisation but has missed one or more recommended doses of the following vaccines: BCG, OPV, Penta, PCV, Rotarix and MCV. Children who have received all the recommended doses are coded as ‘0’ and classified as having complete vaccination, while those who have missed one or more doses are coded as ‘1’ and classified as having incomplete vaccination. The pentavalent 1 to pentavalent 3 DOR is defined as the proportion of children who receive the first dose of the pentavalent vaccine but fail to complete the full three-dose vaccination schedule. Children who have received all three doses of the pentavalent vaccine are coded as ‘0’ and labelled as ‘no’, while those who have not completed all three doses are coded as ‘1’ and labelled as ‘yes’. Vaccination status was verified based on documented evidence from vaccination cards, or caregiver recall when vaccination card was not available or not confirmed by the data collectors (onlinesupplemental figures 1 2).

The authors used demographic (maternal age), socioeconomic (economic status, education level) and geographic (place of residence, subnational regions) dimensions of inequality to assess disparities in childhood vaccination coverage across subpopulations.20 These factors are recognised as common sources of discrimination and are frequently used in inequality research across low- and middle-income countries.18 32 Maternal age was grouped into three categories (15–24 years, 25–34 years and 35–49 years), education level was classified as no education, primary, secondary and higher education, place of residence was divided into urban and rural, and subnational regions included Addis Ababa City, Amhara, Oromia, SNNP and Afar. The wealth index was constructed using principal component analysis based on various household characteristics that reflect economic status. These indicators included ownership of assets (such as electricity, television, radio, refrigerator, telephone and watch), the types of vehicles, water and sanitation facilities (including drinking water source and toilet type), housing conditions (construction materials of floors, walls and roofs), as well as ownership of agricultural land and livestock.33 34 Finally, households were categorised into five wealth quintiles: from the poorest (quintile 1) to the richest (quintile 5).

Statistical analysis

In this study, we developed a new database based on the PMA Ethiopia survey and used Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT) Plus software (V.5.0, Geneva, WHO, 2023) to upload the data and explore, analyse and present inequalities in childhood vaccinations. HEAT Plus (upload database edition) is free, open-source software tools developed to assess health inequalities within countries using disaggregated data sources; it allows users to upload their own databases and assess inequalities at the global, national or subnational level for a range of (health) indicators and dimensions of inequality.35 36 To measure the magnitude of inequality in the childhood vaccinations, we employed both simple and complex summary measures of inequality. Recognising that no single measure of inequality can fully capture all dimensions of inequality. Some measures of inequality are developed to capture inequalities between advantaged and disadvantaged groups, while others evaluate inequality across the entire population. Other measures of inequality are appropriate for ordered dimensions (wealth, education), whereas others are better suited for non-ordered dimensions (place of residence, region).35 36 Applying multiple summary measures provides a robust assessment and helps effectively capture inequalities in childhood immunisation.35 37 38

The difference (D) is an absolute measure of inequality that quantifies inequality in childhood vaccination across the three childhood vaccination indicators between population groups. In our study, for example, it compares FVC between the highest (quintile 5) and lowest (quintile 1) wealth quintiles. The calculation of D for FVC is performed as follows: D=y1−y2, where y1 and y2 represent the estimates for quintile 5 and quintile 1, respectively. In contrast, for IVC and DOR, y1 and y2 represent the disadvantaged (quintile 1) and advantaged (quintile 5) subgroup, respectively. An absolute difference greater than 20 percentage points (pp) reflects high inequality, while differences between 5 and 20 pp suggest moderate inequality, and values below 5 pp indicate low inequality in the childhood vaccines uptake.35,37

The ratio (R) represents a relative measure of inequality, calculated as the ratio between two population subgroups (R=y₁/y₂), where y₁ and y₂ correspond to estimates for the richest (quintile 5) and poorest (quintile 1) subgroups respectively. A ratio value of one indicates perfect equality in the three childhood vaccine indicators between subgroups, while values ≤0.5 or ≥2.0 reflect high inequality, and values between 0.5–0.9 or 1.1–2.0 suggest moderate inequality.39

While simple inequality measures (D and R) provide useful initial insight, they suffer from two significant methodological limitations. First, they focus solely on the two extreme subgroups (eg, the poorest and richest quintiles), excluding the intermediate groups (second, third and fourth quintiles) from the analysis. Second, they do not take into account the population size of each subgroup, potentially overlooking the influence of group distribution on overall inequality levels.35 36 38 To address these methodological limitations, we complemented simple summary measures with complex summary measures: Population Attributable Risk (PAR), Population Attributable Fraction (PAF), Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and Relative Index of Inequality (RII), and Theil Index (TI) to comprehensively assess inequality. Unlike simple measures, complex summary measures consider all population subgroups by accounting for weighted population distributions. PAR and PAF can be applied to both ordered and non-ordered dimensions of inequality, while SII and RII are specifically designed for ordered dimensions, and TI is suited for non-ordered dimensions.36 38

Population Attributable Risk is an absolute measure of inequality calculated as the difference between the reference subgroup’s estimate (yref) and the national average of FVC, IVC and DOR (μ). The calculation is represented as PAR=yref−μ, where μ represents the national average of each childhood vaccine indicator including FVC, IVC and DOR. In this study, the reference groups were defined as follows for each inequality dimension: women aged 35–49 for age, urban residents for place of residence, women with higher education for education level, the richest wealth quintile for economic status, and residents of Addis Ababa for subnational regions. On the other hand, PAF represents a relative measure of inequality, calculated as PAF=(PAR/μ)×100. For favourable indicators such as FVC, PAR and PAF take positive values when the reference group has higher coverage than the national average. Conversely, for adverse indicators such as IVC and DOR, PAR and PAF take negative values when the burden is disproportionately lower in the reference group. A value of zero for either PAR or PAF indicates the absence of inequality, while larger absolute values reflect greater inequality.35 36

SII and RII were estimated using a generalised linear model with logit link function, generating the predicted values for each childhood vaccine indicator at the two extremes (rank 1 and rank 0). For favourable health indicators like FVC, the SII represents the absolute difference (SII=V₁−V₀), while the inverse calculation applies for adverse indicators (IVC and DOR), with zero indicating perfect equality. Positive SII values indicate the concentration of favourable outcomes (FVC) among the advantaged groups, while negative values suggest higher prevalence of adverse outcomes (IVC and DOR) among disadvantaged populations. The magnitude of inequality increases with larger absolute SII values. The RII, calculated as the ratio of V1 to V0: RII=V1/V0, provides a relative measure of inequality and always yields positive values. For favourable indicators (FVC), RII>1 suggests a concentration of full vaccination among advantaged children. Conversely, for adverse indicators RII<1 indicates that these unfavourable outcomes are more concentrated among disadvantaged groups.35 36 The TI is a relative inequality measure that incorporates all population subgroups. It is specifically designed for non-ordered dimensions containing more than two subgroups, such as geographic regions. The index ranges from zero (perfect equality) to positive values, with larger magnitudes indicating greater inequality. More detailed information on summary measures of inequality is available in the referenced sources.35,37 To determine the statistical significance of point estimates and identify significant inequality in the three childhood vaccine indicators, 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) were calculated. For D, PAR and SII, significant inequality is indicated when the UIs’ lower and upper bounds do not include zero. In the case of the R, PAF and RII, inequality is considered significant when the UIs do not include one.35 Data management and analysis were conducted using STATA V.17. To account for the complex survey design, we applied sampling weights and used the STATA ‘SVY’ command in our analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

In this study, the mean age of the women was 27.2 years (SD=0.15). The proportion of children whose mother had no formal education was 41.9% in FVC, 39.9% in IVC and 39.6% in DOR. Similarly, the proportion of women children whose mother was in the poorest household was nearly 20% in each group. Regarding place of residence, 78% of children in the FVC group, 75.7% in the IVC group and 75.1% in the DOR groups resided in rural areas. Nearly half (47%) of the children in the three vaccine indicators were from the Oromia region (table 1 and online supplemental table 1).

Childhood vaccination coverage across different dimensions

The present study reported that among 12-month-old children, FVC was 29.1%, IVC 67.2% and DOR 34.3%. The study revealed significant variations in childhood vaccination indicators across subpopulations, demonstrating substantial disparities along multiple inequality dimensions in Ethiopia. FVC was 62.5% among children whose mother attended higher education, compared with 19.6% among those whose mothers had no formal education. On the other hand, the IVC and vaccine DOR was 39.9% and 39.6%, respectively, among children whose mother had no formal education, compared with 11.9% for IVC and 7.9% for vaccine DOR among children whose mothers had attained higher education (table 1 and online supplemental table 1).

In similar pattern, FVC was substantially higher among children of wealthier, urban children, whereas IVC and DORs were more common among children from poorer and rural households. Level of inequalities in childhood vaccination across subpopulations

All inequality measures consistently showed higher rates of full vaccination among advantaged groups and higher rates of incomplete and DORs among disadvantaged groups. For example, this study reported substantial wealth-based inequality across all three childhood vaccine indicators. The simple summary measure of inequality D was 49.9 pp for FVC, 47.3 pp for IVC and 35.9 pp for vaccine DOR. The R values were 4.0 for FVC, 2.5 for IVC and 4.6 for vaccine DOR. Complex summary measures showed similar patterns, the PAR was 37.8 pp for FVC, −35.0 pp for IVC and −24.4 pp for vaccine DOR. Likewise, the SII was 50.8 pp for FVC, while it was −50.5 pp for IVC and −39.7 pp for vaccine DOR. Similarly, we also observed high levels of education, place of residence and regional-based inequality in the immunisation service. Conversely, age-related inequality was inconsistent across the summary measures. For instance, the simple summary measure of D showed mild age-related inequalities in FVC, IVC and vaccine DOR. The D values were −4.6 pp for FVC and −4.7 pp for both IVC and vaccine DOR, suggesting that children born from mothers aged 35–49 years were slightly disadvantaged. The R measure revealed moderate inequality, with a value of 0.8 for FVC, and 0.9 for both IVC and DOR, reinforcing the finding that children of older mothers were less likely to receive full vaccination. In the contrary, the complex summary measures, PAR and PAF indicate 0 for all vaccine indicators, indicating no measurable inequality across maternal age groups for the three childhood vaccine indicators. Discussion

This study investigated coverage and the extent of demographic, socioeconomic and geographic area-related inequalities in the three childhood vaccine indicators among 12-month children using different summary measures of inequalities. The study found that only 29.2% of Ethiopian children were fully vaccinated by 12 months of age, indicating low coverage compared with national targets and previous surveys.7 9 10 Consequently, the rate of IVC was alarmingly high at 67.2%, exceeding the rates reported in prior studies.11 40 41 Furthermore, the study reported a substantial DOR for the first to the third dose of pentavalent vaccine, which was higher than both the national standards and WHO recommendations.10 42 These findings highlight health system weaknesses and barriers within the immunisation programme that hinder children from completing the full vaccination schedule. Effective immunisation service delivery is fundamental to achieving high childhood vaccination coverage. This requires uninterrupted availability of vaccines and supplies, reliable cold chain systems with functional refrigerators and stable power sources, adequately trained and motivated health workers, well-organised service delivery models, accessible vaccination points and good health infrastructures.10 43 44 However, persistent supply-side challenges continue to hinder immunisation efforts in Ethiopia. Studies document critical barriers including geographical and financial inaccessibility of health facilities; irregular service provision with limited operating hours; non-functional health posts and frequent absence of health extension workers; demotivated staff; ineffective service arrangements leading to long wait times; recurrent power outages compromising vaccine storage and frequent stockouts of vaccines.43 44 These systemic challenges have been consistently identified as primary contributors to higher incomplete vaccination rates and DOR across the country. Inadequate vaccine availability in health facilities remains a significant barrier to immunisation service delivery in Ethiopia.45 46 Parental demand for childhood vaccination is associated with multiple behavioural and socioeconomic factors. For example, concerns about vaccine side effects, exposure to misinformation, lack of immunisation awareness, religious or cultural barriers, missed appointments due to competing priorities, and the burden of work obligations can all contribute to reduced vaccine uptake.10 12 16 44 In addition to these demand-side and supply-side barriers, immunisation programmes are highly vulnerable to unexpected public health emergencies and sociopolitical crises at both national and global levels.47 In Ethiopia, the delivery of immunisation services has been significantly affected in recent years by internal conflict and the COVID-19 pandemic.10 48 The conflict has directly undermined immunisation efforts through destruction of health infrastructure, mass displacement, vaccine supply chain interruptions, and mobility restrictions.49 50 Simultaneously, pandemic-related service disruptions resulted in 1.3 million Ethiopian children missing their third dose of the pentavalent vaccine in 2021 alone.51

The summary measures of inequality showed that FVC was disproportionately higher among children from the richest households, suggesting pro-rich inequality. This pattern aligns with findings from studies conducted in Ethiopia7 47 and Indonesia,52 which reported substantial disparities in vaccination coverage between the wealthiest and poorest household groups. Furthermore, other studies in Ethiopia have documented high levels of inequality in the coverage of specific vaccines, with children from the richest households having significantly higher coverage than those from the poorest households.53,56 This study reveals persistent socioeconomic inequalities in vaccination coverage that have remained largely unchanged. This inequality gap has shown only a marginal increase from 40.8 pp in 2016 EDHS47 to 49.6 pp in the current analysis. These findings suggest that existing inequalities have not merely persisted but potentially intensified during this period. Children from the poorest households were less likely to receive all recommended childhood vaccinations compared with their richest counterparts. IVC and DOR were 42.3 and 35.9 pp higher, respectively, among the poorest households than among the richest households. These findings align with other studies reporting disproportionately higher rates of IVC and DOR among economically disadvantaged children.13 16

The SII analysis revealed substantial absolute inequalities in childhood vaccinations. The SII for FVC was 50.8 pp, suggesting that children from the wealthiest households had a significantly higher probability of being fully vaccinated compared with those from the poorest households. Conversely, the negative SII values for IVC (−50.5 pp) and DOR (−39.9 pp) indicate higher rates among children from a poor socioeconomic background, implying pro-poor inequality. Likewise, the measure of PAR was higher in the FCV, highlighting a potential opportunity to improve childhood vaccination rates nationwide. In this research, the PAR estimate suggests if the FVC rates in the four wealth quintiles (ie, quintile 1, quintile 2, quintile 3 and quintile 4) were the same as the richest wealth quintile (quintile 5), the national FVC could potentially increase by 37.8%. Conversely, if IVC and DOR in these four quintiles matched the levels observed in the richest quintile, there could be a potential reduction of 35% in IVC and 30.1% in DOR, respectively, from the current levels. These findings underscore the critical role of addressing wealth-related inequalities in improving vaccination outcomes at the national level. Several justifications can explain why children from poor families fail to complete or drop out of the full vaccination schedule. The first reason is poverty, which is closely linked to socioeconomic determinants and access to healthcare.57 In low- and middle-income countries, poor populations frequently encounter barriers to essential health services, including childhood immunisation.58 59 Although childhood immunisation service in Ethiopia is provided free of charge for all, regardless of economic status, poverty continues to shape socioeconomic conditions in ways that hinder equitable access. It contributes to various disadvantages such as limited access to education, inadequate health information, residence in remote areas or urban slums, and reduced women’s empowerment.60 Economically disadvantaged women often exhibit lower health-seeking behaviours, as they are frequently compelled to prioritise income-generating activities over healthcare to meet their household’s immediate needs.61 62

In line with WHO recommendations, the specific patterns of inequality must also be identified, as they are critical for informing appropriate policy responses. As illustrated in figure 1, wealth-related inequality exhibits a mass deprivation pattern across the three childhood immunisation indicators. Higher FVC and lower IVC and DOR are concentrated in the wealthiest quintile, while the majority of the population (quintiles 1, quintiles 2, quintiles 3, quintiles 4) experiences significantly lower coverage (figure 1, online supplemental figure 3) and higher DOR. Addressing disparities in this pattern requires a comprehensive approach that targets the entire population; ensures equitable resource allocation across all socioeconomic groups, and moves beyond merely providing free immunisation services to tackle the underlying systemic and socioeconomic barriers.19

Our study identified a high level of education-related inequality in childhood vaccinations coverage. Children of highly educated mothers had significantly better vaccination coverage, with FVC being 42.9 pp higher compared with children from no educated or disadvantaged groups. Conversely, children of mothers with no formal education showed 39.2 pp higher rates of IVC and 28.6 pp higher DOR compared with children of highly educated mothers (online supplemental table 2). These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating education-based disparities in vaccination coverage.53 56 The analysis of SII further supports this substantial inequality, indicating children of highly educated mothers had a 40.3 pp higher FVC compared with those of uneducated mothers. The burden of incomplete vaccination (SII: −39.2 pp) and DOR (SII: −32.2 pp) remained disproportionately concentrated among children of uneducated women. The PAR analysis highlights a significant opportunity for improvement by addressing education-related inequalities. If childhood vaccination coverage were equalised across all education levels to match that of mothers with higher education, the national FVC could increase by 33.5%, while IVC and DOR could be reduced by 29.9% and 21.1%, respectively.

A plausible justification for this finding is that higher maternal education increases awareness of the benefits of childhood vaccination in preventing vaccine-preventable morbidity and mortality among their children.15 16 63 Educated women also have better access to health information and can apply it effectively, enabling informed vaccination decisions.64 Furthermore, higher education attainment is frequently linked to improved socioeconomic status, including higher income levels, better job opportunities, financial stability and greater household decision-making autonomy.65 66 Thus, the combination of knowledge, financial capacity and autonomy positions educated women to be more proactive in ensuring their children receive all the recommended vaccines on time. The education-related inequalities observed across all three vaccination indicators demonstrate a linear pattern (figure 1, online supplemental figure 4). This linear gradient suggests that addressing these disparities requires a dual-pronged strategy: population-wide interventions to improve overall coverage combined with targeted approaches for specific subpopulations facing greater disadvantages.19

In this inequality analysis, we observed substantial urban-rural disparities in vaccination outcomes. Children residing in urban areas exhibited significantly higher FVC (D:41.6 pp), while IVC (D:39.2 pp) and DOR (D:29.7 pp) were markedly more prevalent among rural children. These findings align with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia.4753,55 Additionally, the R revealed that FVC was three times higher among urban children compared with their rural counterparts. Conversely, IVC and DOR were 2.5 and 4.6 times higher, respectively, among rural children. The PAR further confirmed substantial inequalities based on residence in childhood vaccinations. This underscores the importance of addressing urban–rural disparities in childhood vaccinations to enhance national immunisation coverage. If rural areas achieved the same levels of FVC, IVC and DOR as urban areas, the national FVC could increase by 32.5%, while IVC and DOR could be reduced by 29.7% and 22.3%, respectively. The variation in childhood immunisation between urban and rural areas can be attributed to various underlying factors. The primary reason is the unequal access to health facilities, immunisation sites and services. In rural and underserved areas, health facilities and vaccination sites are often scarce, forcing parents to travel long distances to reach vaccine delivery sites. Additionally, rural women encounter a lack of transportation and geographical challenges, making it difficult for them to access health facilities and vaccinate their children.67 Moreover, health centres and health posts in the remote areas face critical shortages of supplies such as vaccines and other supplies. As a result, children in disadvantaged areas experience higher levels of incomplete vaccination and DOR compared with urban residents, where health facilities are more accessible and readily available.10 15 43 Other contributing factors to the observed gap may include differences in health literacy, socioeconomic conditions and low women’s decision-making autonomy, all of which further exacerbate the urban–rural disparity in childhood vaccinations.

This study examined subnational regional disparities as a key dimension of inequality in childhood vaccination coverage. The results revealed substantial variations across regions in three critical immunisation indicators: FVC, IVC and DOR. Notably, FVC was 78.8 pp higher in Addis Ababa compared with the Afar region (figure 2). These findings align with those of previous studies conducted in Ethiopia.7 54 55 Additionally, the R demonstrated that FVC was 10.3 times higher in Addis Ababa than in Afar. Conversely, Afar exhibited significantly worse performance on adverse vaccination indicators, with IVC and DOR exceeding Addis Ababa’s rates by 70.2 pp and 50.2 pp, respectively. The disparity was particularly stark for DOR, which was 22.1 times higher in Afar than in Addis Ababa. Further analysis using the TI underscored these disparities, revealing high inequality levels for FVC (TI: 114) and DOR (TI: 65). This suggests that FVC is disproportionately concentrated in advantaged regions, while DOR is more prevalent in disadvantaged ones. This is consistent with prior research documenting pronounced regional inequalities in childhood immunisation services.7 Addressing these regional inequities in FVC, IVC and DOR could substantially enhance national immunisation coverage, as indicated by PAR estimates. If the Afar, Amhara, Oromia and SNNP regions achieved vaccination performance levels comparable to Addis Ababa, the national FVC could increase by 57.7% from current levels. Conversely, IVC and DOR could be reduced by 54.1% and 31.9%, respectively. In Ethiopia, regions are commonly classified into urban, agrarian and pastoralist categories, representing their level of economic development and infrastructure accessibility. Among these, agrarian and particularly pastoralist regions are more disadvantaged, which places children in these areas at a significant disadvantage in accessing childhood immunisation services. Children living in hard-to-reach areas and pastoralist regions often fail to receive vaccinations according to the recommended schedule, primarily due to a combination of supply- and demand-side barriers.7 43 These barriers include disparities in health and physical infrastructure accessibility, such as roads, electricity and health facilities as well as uneven resource allocation, including the distribution of skilled health professionals, availability of medical supplies and budgets.7 17 Furthermore, variations in leadership capacity, including the quality of supportive supervision and mentoring, contribute to regional disparities in vaccine access. Additionally, regional inequalities in vaccination coverage can be attributed to differences in socioeconomic conditions, cultural norms and behavioural factors across regions.7 17 These multidimensional disparities highlight the need for context-specific and equity-focused interventions to improve vaccine access and coverage in underserved regions.

Our analysis revealed inconsistent findings between simple and complex inequality measures when examining childhood vaccination inequality by maternal age. The simple summary measures of inequality D showed mild inequalities between the two age groups: FVC (D: −4.6), IVC (D: −4.8) and DOR (D: −4.7). These values suggest that children born to older mothers had slightly lower FVC and slightly higher rates of IVC and DOR. In contrast, the complex summary measures of inequalities, SII and PAR, did not indicate significant inequality across maternal age groups. The SII results were statistically insignificant for all three indicators, and the PAR values were zero for FVC, IVC and DOR, suggesting no measurable age-related disparity in childhood vaccination. This pattern is consistent with previous studies, in which complex summary measures of inequality did not indicate age-related disparities in childhood immunisation.7 Furthermore, the PAR findings imply that achieving equal coverage across maternal age groups would not lead to any improvement in national vaccination coverage for FVC, nor would it reduce IVC or DOR rates (figure 3). These discrepancies highlight how different inequality measures may capture varying aspects of disparity depending on the population distribution, ordered or nonordered and statistical approach.38

The observed inequalities in childhood immunisation are driven by both proximate and structural determinants. The proximate drivers are immediate factors that influence vaccine uptake, including geographical accessibility to vaccination sites, availability of vaccines and supplies, a functional cold chain system, availability of outreach services, the presence of motivated and skilled healthcare providers, awareness of vaccine schedules and waiting times.68 However, these proximate factors are themselves shaped by deeper structural drivers, such as poverty, low maternal education, gender inequality in decision-making autonomy, regional economic development disparities, residence in remote and underserved areas, poor infrastructure in disadvantaged areas.68 69 This highlights the need to address both proximate barriers and structural drivers to reduce inequality in childhood immunisation.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that Ethiopia remains significantly distant from achieving UHC for childhood immunisations. To attain this goal, the country must strengthen efforts to ensure equitable vaccine access for all children in future years. Importantly, our study suggests that addressing socioeconomic and geographic inequalities could substantially improve the national immunisation coverage and accelerate progress towards UHC targets. The three childhood vaccine indicators revealed considerable potential for improvement across all dimensions of inequality, except age. Eliminating regional disparities could yield the highest gain in national FVC (+57.7 pp), followed by education (+37.8 pp), wealth status (+33.5) and residence (+32.5 pp). Age-based inequality had no attributable impact (figure 3).

Policy implications

This study identified mass deprivation patterns in wealth-based inequalities and linear patterns in education-based inequalities, with important implications for intervention design.70 71 Mass deprivation patterns support population-wide approaches, such as strengthening the routine immunisation services and large-scale supplementary immunisation activities, whereas linear patterns require both universal approaches and targeted catch-up strategies for disadvantaged groups.19 70 Beyond these patterns, both proximal and structural drivers highlight the need for complementary approaches: the gradient-based strategies and targeted interventions for disadvantaged groups.18 71 The gradient approach addresses structural determinants across the socioeconomic spectrum. However, this approach faces technical and political challenges; health gradients are often deeply rooted, complex and costly to address, with improvements requiring a long time to materialise.18 71 72 Despite these challenges, the gradient approach is effective in achieving long-term, sustainable equity. In contrast, targeted interventions focus on disadvantaged groups, such as children from low socioeconomic backgrounds or disadvantaged areas.18 71

Therefore, in the Ethiopian context, a combined approach is essential. In the short term, priority should be given to strengthening routine immunisation services, implementing catch-up vaccination strategies, and expanding supplementary immunisation activities. In remote and underserved areas, particularly pastoralist regions, context-specific vaccine delivery strategies are needed to reach under-immunised children.68 Additionally, strengthening the health system is critical, including improving vaccine availability, cold-chain systems, ensuring adequate and motivated healthcare providers, expanded community-based health education through the Health Extension Programme. Financial support and subsidy mechanisms also should be considered to reduce access barriers for low-income populations.68 73

In the long term, addressing structural drivers such as poverty reduction, investment in women’s education, women’s empowerment and infrastructure development is essential for reducing inequalities in childhood vaccination. These measures align with Ethiopia’s National Expanded Program on Immunisation and the Immunisation Agenda 2030 (IA2030), particularly the goals of reaching zero-dose and under-immunised children through equity-focused strategies and last-mile delivery strategies.6 74

Conclusions

Full vaccination coverage in Ethiopia remains low, with substantial inequalities across socioeconomic and geographic groups. The inequality analysis underscores that addressing socioeconomic and geographic inequalities could substantially improve the national immunisation coverage. Therefore, expanding equitable access to immunisation services is essential for achieving national and global UHC targets.
