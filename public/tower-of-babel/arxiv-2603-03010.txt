Abstract
. Recent advances in Information Retrieval have established transformer-based cross-encoders as a keystone in IR. Recent studies have focused on knowledge distillation and showed that, with the right strategy, traditional cross-encoders could reach the level of effectiveness of LLM re-rankers. Yet, comparisons with previous training strategies, including distillation from strong cross-encoder teachers, remain unclear. In addition, few studies cover a similar range of backbone encoders, while substantial improvements have been made in this area since BERT. This lack of comprehensive studies in controlled environments makes it difficult to identify robust design choices. In this work, we reproduce Schlatt et al. 2025 LLM-based distillation strategy and compare it to Hofstätter et al. 2020 approach based on an ensemble of cross-encoder teachers, as well as other supervised objectives, to fine-tune a large range of cross-encoders, from the original BERT and its follow-ups RoBERTa, ELECTRA and DeBERTa-v3, to the more recent ModernBERT. We evaluate all models on both in-domain (TREC-DL and MS MARCO dev) and out-of-domain datasets (BEIR, LoTTE, and Robust04). Our results show that objectives emphasizing relative comparisons—pairwise MarginMSE and listwise InfoNCE—consistently outperform pointwise baselines across all backbones and evaluation settings, and that objective choice can yield gains comparable to scaling the backbone architecture.

Introduction

The two-stage retrieve-then-re-rank pipeline is a standard pattern in Information Retrieval (IR)
(
Liu 2011
)
.
Nogueira and Cho 2019

(
Devlin et al. 2019

(
Bajaj et al. 2016

(

Zhuang et al. 2023

Over the last years, progress on cross-encoder re-ranking has followed two complementary directions.

First, stronger training strategies have been proposed, especially via knowledge distillation
(
Hinton et al. 2015
)

Hofstätter et al. 2020

2.
Background

Re-ranking setting and notation

We consider pointwise cross-encoder re-rankers that assign a relevance score
S_{\theta}(q,p)
to a query–-passage (or document) pair
(q,p)
. At inference time, the re-ranker scores a candidate set of
n
passages
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
produced by a first-stage retriever. Because re-ranking effectiveness depends on both the scoring model and the candidate set it is applied to, we treat candidate generation as an explicit part of the experimental setup and standardize it across methods in later sections.

Supervised learning-to-rank objectives

We organize supervised training objectives using the pointwise/pairwise/listwise taxonomy described by
Liu 2011
. Pointwise objectives treat each
(q,p)
pair independently (e.g., cross-entropy as in
Nogueira and Cho 2019
). Pairwise objectives optimize relative preferences between a positive and a negative passage for the same query (e.g., hinge losses as used by
MacAvaney et al. 2019
). Listwise objectives optimize over a set of candidates for a query; in this paper we include an InfoNCE-style listwise formulation following
Cao et al. 2007
.

Distillation as supervision for re-ranking

Knowledge distillation
(
Hinton et al. 2015
)
typically replaces hard relevance labels with signals from a stronger teacher. In re-ranking, the supervision can take the form of (i) teacher scores for individual
(q,p)
pairs, (ii) teacher margins between positive and negative candidates, or (iii) a teacher-induced ranking over a list of candidates. The reproduced methods in this paper instantiate (ii) and (iii).

Reproduced method: MarginMSE distillation

Hofstätter et al. 2020
propose to train dual-encoders from an ensemble of 3 cross-encoder teachers, based on BERT-base
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
, BERT-Large and ALBERT
(
Lan et al. 2020
)
– each trained with the RankNET loss
(
Burges et al. 2005
)
– to annotate the top-1000 passages retrieved by BM25
(
Robertson and Zaragoza 2009
)
for the queries of MS MARCO
(
Bajaj et al. 2016
)
. Specifically, they introduce MarginMSE to align the student’s predicted margins with those of a teacher ensemble.

Reproduced method: Rank-DistiLLM distillation

Schlatt et al. 2025
distill RankZephyr
(
Pradeep et al. 2023
)
into an ELECTRA-based
(
Clark et al. 2020
)
cross-encoder by constructing supervision at the level of ranked lists. For each training query, a ColBERTv2
(
Santhanam et al. 2022
)
provides a set of candidates that is re-ranked by the teacher, yielding a teacher ordering over the list. They then train the student to imitate this listwise behavior using distillation objectives such as DistillRankNet, an adaptation of RankNET
(
Burges et al. 2005
)
, and ADR-MSE.

Controlled choices for comparability

Prior work often varies multiple factors simultaneously (candidate generation, backbone choice, training protocol, and benchmark suite), which makes it difficult to isolate the effect of a training objective or supervision signal. In this paper, we therefore separate the reproduced supervision signals and loss formulations (this section) from the controlled experimental environment described next, which includes standardized candidate pools, training protocols, and evaluation benchmarks.

3.
Reproduction Framework

To validate the impact of different distillation strategies, and in particular the benefits of using LLMs against traditional cross-encoders, we reproduce representative distillation-based training strategies for cross-encoder re-rankers from the literature. Notably, we reproduce the results of distilling RankZephyr
(
Pradeep et al. 2023
)
to ELECTRA
(
Clark et al. 2020
)
of
Schlatt et al. 2025
, and apply the MarginMSE objective on the scores from an ensemble of strong cross-encoders from
Hofstätter et al. 2020
to cross-encoders (while originally designed for dual-encoders). To isolate the impact of the training signal/objective from the encoder backbone, we compare these approaches under a controlled experimental environment.

3.1.
Reproducibility Statement

A key tenet of our methodology is the mitigation of initialization bias. Every unique experimental configuration is executed with three random seeds. To ensure a faithful comparison of the tested parameters and architectures, we report the mean performance along with the standard deviation across the seeds.

Every experiment is defined by a standalone YAML configuration file specifying all hyperparameters, model architectures and evaluation datasets. We release these configuration files alongside our newly developed unified codebase, allowing researchers to reproduce our exact results with one command, and use them as templates for benchmarking new training strategies in a comparable environment.
Finally, we release a unified evaluation pipeline and the best-performing model checkpoints and tensorboard for each training strategy on HuggingFace upon publication, to serve as a fully reproducible reference point for future work on cross-encoder training and distillation.

3.2.
Approach

Datasets

Following the literature, we use MS MARCO v1
(
Bajaj et al. 2016
)
to train our models (this remains true even for the distillation strategies). Our evaluation framework assesses performance across both: in-domain (ID), using the official dev set of MS MARCO, TREC-DL’19 and TREC-DL’20
(
Craswell et al. 2020

Craswell et al. 2021

First-stage Retrieval Selection

A critical component of a re-ranking pipeline is the quality of the initial candidate pool. Given
Hofstätter et al. 2020
used BM25
(
Robertson and Zaragoza 2009
)

(

3.4.
Training Strategies

In this section, we detail the six training strategies reproduced in this work, all of which have proven effective for optimizing Cross-Encoders. We restrict our scope to
pointwise cross-encoders
, i.e., models that output one scalar score
S_{\theta}(q,p)
for each query–passage (or document) pair. These models are fine-tuned using
[CLS]
pooling after the final transformer layer. Our analysis compares (i) supervised objectives derived from raw MS MARCO
(
Bajaj et al. 2016
)
labels and (ii) distillation objectives introduced by
Hofstätter et al. 2020
and
Schlatt et al. 2025
.

For a given
query

q
, we consider a set of
n

passages

\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
with associated ground-truth labels
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and predicted logit scores
s_{i}=S_{\theta}(q,p_{i})
where
S_{\theta}
is a cross-encoder. We evaluate training objectives spanning pointwise, pairwise, and listwise formulations to identify the most robust design choices for various model architectures.

3.4.1.
Supervised Objectives

The following losses train directly on MS MARCO
(
Bajaj et al. 2016
)
binary relevance labels.

Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE)

Following monoBERT
(
Nogueira and Cho 2019
)
, this objective treats the ranking task as a pointwise classification. We apply the BCE objective in a
pairwise sampling
setup, where each query is associated with a pair of passages: one positive
(p_{+},s_{+})
and one negative
(p_{-},s_{-})
.

L_{\text{BCE}}=-\log(\sigma(s_{+}))-\log\left(1-\sigma(s_{-})\right)

Hinge Loss

As utilized in CEDR
(
MacAvaney et al. 2019
)
,
this pairwise objective enforces a margin of at least 1 between the positive and
negative scores:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Hinge}}=\max(0,1-(s_{+}-s_{-}))

We use the same random triplet
(q,p_{+},p_{-})
sampling for both BCE and Hinge losses.

InfoNCE

This listwise objective is functionally equivalent to
ListNet
(
Cao et al. 2007
)
and the LCE loss

(
Pradeep et al. 2022
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\log\left(\frac{e^{s_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{s_{j}}}\right)

To sample the negatives, we replicate
Schlatt et al. 2025
training
strategy and randomly sample 7 negatives from the pool of passages retrieved by ColBERTv2

(
Santhanam et al. 2022
)
for each of the 10k query in the dataset they released.

3.4.2.
Distillation Objectives

The following objectives replace binary labels with soft scores from a teacher model
(
Hinton et al. 2015
)
, providing a more fine-grained supervision signal.

MarginMSE

As proposed by
Hofstätter et al. 2020
, this pairwise objective regresses the student’s margin to match the teacher’s margin instead of fitting the logit values.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MarginMSE}}=\text{MSE}(\ s_{+}^{t}-s_{-}^{t},\ s_{+}-s_{-}\ )

We utilize the original triplets and teacher scores released by the authors to ensure exact reproducibility.

The last two objectives use an LLM teacher model, i.e., RankZephyr
(
Pradeep et al. 2023
)
,
to rank a list of passages
p_{1}>\ldots>p_{n}
.

DistillRankNET

Following
Schlatt et al. 2025
, this objective is a teacher-student adaptation of RankNet
(
Burges et al. 2005
)
that considers all the pairs formed by each passage ranked higher in the teacher’s ranking list, and the ones ranked below:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DistillRankNet}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\log(1+\exp(s_{i}-s_{j}))

ADR-MSE (Adaptive Distillation Rank MSE)

Also introduced in

(
Schlatt et al. 2025
)
, this loss applies a weighted
MSE to the scores based on their rank in the teacher’s ranking list, emphasizing
the top-tier results:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ADR-MSE}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\log_{2}(i+1)}\left(i-\mathbf{r}_{i}^{s}\right)^{2}

Using a differentiable approximation of the student rank
\mathbf{r}_{i}^{s}
of passage
p_{i}
, for a given temperature
T
(
T=1
by default following the authors):

\mathbf{r}_{i}^{s}=1+\sum_{j\neq k}\sigma\left(\frac{s_{k}-s_{j}}{T}\right)

For both
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DistillRankNet}}
and

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ADR-MSE}}
, we directly leverage the training data shared
by
Schlatt et al. 2025
but limit ourselves to
the re-ranking by RankZephyr
(
Pradeep et al. 2023
)
of the top 50 passages
retrieved by ColBERTv2 for a subset of 10k queries from MS MARCO, as their
results suggest this depth is the most suitable to fine-tune cross-encoders.

4.
Experimental Setup

4.1.
Base Training Details

To maintain a controlled environment across our diverse backbones and loss functions, we establish a standardized training protocol designed to isolate the impact of architecture and objective function. This framework ensures consistency by fixing several key parameters across all runs:

Training and Evaluation Data
: We utilize the MS MARCO v1 corpus
(
Bajaj et al. 2016
)
for training and evaluate on both In-Domain (MS MARCO dev, TREC-DL’19/20
(
Craswell et al. 2020
;
Craswell et al. 2021
)
) and Out-of-Domain (BEIR
(
Thakur et al. 2021
)
, LoTTE
(
Santhanam et al. 2022
)
, Robust04
(
Voorhees 2004
)
) suites.

Candidate Generation:
We maintain a fixed candidate set size of n=1000 passages per query, retrieved using the SPLADE-v3-DistilBERT first-stage retriever.

Preprocessing
: All input tokenization, maximum sequence lengths, and evaluation scripts remain constant across experiments. Notably, we follow
Schlatt et al. 2025
and truncate all queries and passages respectively to 32 and 256 tokens.

Optimization Framework
: We utilize a standardized optimizer, validation cadence, and checkpoint selection strategy.

Fine-tuning is performed using the AdamW optimizer with
\epsilon=1\texttimes 10^{-8}
. While optimal batch sizes and learning rates are determined per model size class via the preliminary experiments described in Section
4.2.1
, the overall scheduling remains uniform. We set a maximum of 200k optimization steps per training, with a linear warmup for the first 5,000 steps.

Validation Strategy

During training, we perform validation every 50
epochs to monitor convergence. Given the computational cost of validating on
complete datasets, but considering the fact that cross-encoders are prone (even if to a lesser extent than dual embedding models) to overfitting on the training corpus, we follow the sentence-transformers library guidelines
2
2

2

https://sbert.net/index.html
and use nano-BEIR
(
Nan 2026
)
as a proxy for generalization effectiveness. These subsets of the 13 public datasets of BEIR allow for frequent validation without the overhead of using the full datasets.
Note that because we use nano-beir to find our best checkpoints (yet do not directly train on it), we consider BEIR as a
almost-ID
benchmark, and only regard the LoTTE and Robust04 datasets as truly OOD.

Implementation Details

The training pipeline is based
on PyTorch
(
Paszke et al. 2019
)
and
transformers
libraries
3
3

3

https://github.com/huggingface/transformers
. Each training run is conducted using a single NVIDIA H100 (80GB) GPU, with all experiments lasting less than 20 hours (around 10 hours for the
Mini
backbones). Across training strategies, we normalize each batch by the total number of documents to keep the number of samples comparable. Note that this implies that each batch for the DistillRankNET and ADR-MSE losses is composed of a single query along with its ranked list of 50 passages.

4.2.
Phased Experimental Strategy

Given the combinatorial explosion inherent in testing every experimental parameter – encompassing nine backbones, six loss functions, and multiple hyperparameter variants – we adopt a phased optimization strategy. This approach allows us to identify optimal training settings in a controlled environment, without requiring an exhaustive and computationally prohibitive grid search across all configurations.

5.2.
Full Results

We present the aggregated results from Phase II in Figure
1
which summarizes the effectiveness of nine encoder-only backbones across six training objectives, and then detail all values for all setups in
Table

6
. We report the mean nDCG@10 over three random seeds, evaluated across three distinct scenarios: In-Domain (ID), the BEIR-13 benchmark, considered as "semi-OOD" given that we use nano-beir to identify checkpoints, and a true Out-of-Domain (OOD) suite comprising LoTTE Search and Robust04. We note that with only three seeds, fine-grained rankings between close-performing objectives should be interpreted with caution; our analysis focuses on consistent trends that hold across backbones and evaluation settings. We use a Friedman test followed by a Nemenyi post-hoc test
(
Demšar 2006
)
to assess statistical significance. These non-parametric rank-based tests are well-suited to our setting, as they do not assume normality and handle the heterogeneity of evaluation benchmarks by comparing methods through their relative rankings across (backbone, benchmark) or (loss, benchmark) combinations rather than raw scores.

Two primary observations emerge from this large-scale benchmark.

Strategy Consistency
: The choice of training objective exerts a consistent and often substantial impact on performance, regardless of the specific backbone family employed.

Architecture-Objective Interaction
: While scaling the backbone generally improves results, superior training objectives—particularly distillation-based methods—can effectively compensate for smaller model sizes, a trend that is especially pronounced under domain shift.

The following subsections provide a detailed analysis of these interactions, focusing on backbone scaling, objective robustness, and the trade-offs between efficiency and effectiveness.

5.3.
Loss Function Impact

A Friedman test over the 54 (backbone, evaluation setting) combinations confirms highly significant differences among objectives (
\chi^{2}=153.4
,
p<10^{-30}
). The Nemenyi post-hoc test (
\alpha=0.05
, CD
=1.03
) reveals three statistically distinct tiers: a top group of InfoNCE (avg. rank 1.83) and MarginMSE (2.17), a middle tier of DistillRankNet (3.61), ADR-MSE (3.66), and Hinge (3.99), and BCE (5.74) at the bottom, significantly worse than every other objective. This matches
Schlatt et al. 2025
conclusions on the equivalence between the simpler DistillRankNET loss and ADR-MSE.

These statistical groupings align with a clear qualitative pattern visible in
Figure

1
: across virtually all backbones and evaluation settings, the pointwise BCE objective forms a lower bound, consistently dominated by objectives that explicitly compare candidates. This pattern is most pronounced on BEIR-13 and the OOD suite, suggesting that objectives emphasizing relative comparisons between candidates learn more transferable decision boundaries than independent binary classification.

Within the top tier, MarginMSE—which relies on BM25-mined negatives with cross-encoder teacher scores—achieves statistically equivalent performance to InfoNCE, which uses ColBERTv2 hard negatives (see Section
3
). This suggests that the pairwise/listwise formulation itself is a key driver of effectiveness, beyond the quality of the negative sampling strategy. In contrast, the two listwise distillation objectives derived from Rank-DistiLLM
(
Schlatt et al. 2024
)
(DistillRankNET and ADR-MSE) fall in the middle tier under our unified setup: they are competitive for some backbones and datasets but often trail InfoNCE or MarginMSE. This observation is important for practitioners: once candidate generation and training protocols are controlled, the benefits of LLM-derived listwise distillation are not universal and should be evaluated against strong non-LLM baselines. Furthermore, our results suggest that, under a controlled environment, supervised objectives can render cross-encoders competitive with cross-encoders trained under the knowledge distillation framework, which contradicts recent findings on decoder-only cross-encoders
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
.

5.4.
Backbone Effects and Scaling

A Friedman test across the 36 (loss, benchmark) instances confirms highly significant differences among backbones (
\chi^{2}=216.2
,
p<10^{-41}
). The Nemenyi post-hoc test (CD
=2.00
,
\alpha=0.05
) reveals a top group comprising ELECTRA (avg. rank 1.97), Ettin-150M (2.69), MiniLM-L12 (3.11), and BERT-Base (3.79), none of which are significantly different from each other. At the bottom, Ettin-32M (7.94) and Ettin-17M (8.97) are significantly worse than all base-sized models. This also matches recent studies, including
Schlatt et al. 2025
, who switched to ELECTRA as backbone to fine-tune cross-encoders, but further suggests that more recent alternatives such as Ettin-150M may now provide even stronger foundation.

Objective choice can rival a size upgrade.

These rankings confirm a pattern visible in
Figure

1
: the magnitude of objective improvements is often comparable to—and sometimes larger than—the gain from switching to a larger backbone. This is particularly visible in the Ettin scaling suite (17M
\rightarrow
32M
\rightarrow
68M
\rightarrow
150M), where performance increases monotonically with size for nearly all objectives, yet a smaller model trained with a strong objective (e.g., InfoNCE or MarginMSE) can approach or exceed a larger model trained with BCE. Notably, MiniLM-L12 (33M) is statistically indistinguishable from ELECTRA (110M), illustrating that an efficient architecture with good pretraining can match a model three times its size. In deployment scenarios with tight latency or memory budgets, choosing an appropriate objective can provide a substantial fraction of the benefit of scaling the backbone. Conversely, scaling a backbone does not compensate for weak supervision choices, especially under OOD evaluation where the BCE gap remains large.

Backbone-specific anomalies.

While the overall trends are consistent, the results also highlight backbone-specific behavior that cautions against assuming that pretraining advances always translate to re-ranking gains. In particular, DeBERTaV3—despite being among the largest backbones tested (184M) and exhibiting strong effectiveness on NLU tasks
(
He et al. 2023
)
—ranks alongside the much smaller Ettin-68M (avg. ranks 5.63 and 5.76 respectively), systematically weaker than several other base-sized backbones.
This warrants further investigation but reinforces the broader point that re-ranking effectiveness depends on the interaction between pretraining, fine-tuning objectives, and evaluation setups.

5.5.
Robustness on OOD

Across all backbones, MarginMSE and InfoNCE consistently yield the highest
effectiveness on LoTTe-Search, significantly outperforming pointwise losses.
InfoNCE on Ettin-150M reaches a peak nDCG@10 of 0.580, the highest value for
this benchmark in our study. The performance gaps between top-tier objectives
remain relatively narrow, suggesting that multiple pairwise/listwise formulations
can reliably exploit the structured search intent of this benchmark.
Robust04 presents a markedly different picture. With longer documents and older
newswire text, the variance across objectives is considerably wider
(
Table

6
): MiniLM-L12 with DistillRankNET achieves the highest score
(0.520), and smaller Ettin models benefit disproportionately from distillation.
The ranking among top objectives is also less stable, with no single loss
consistently dominating across backbones.

However, as models scale toward the base-sized Ettin-150M, we observe diminishing returns; the gain from Ettin-68M to Ettin-150M on the out-of-domain (OOD) suite is notably narrower, suggesting a performance "sweet spot" at mid-range capacities. 6.
Conclusion

We presented a controlled reproduction and comparison of cross-encoder distillation techniques, training 9 encoder backbones with 6 objectives—including reproductions of MarginMSE
(
Hofstätter et al. 2020
)
and Rank-DistiLLM
(
Schlatt et al. 2025
)
—under a unified setup (MS MARCO, SPLADE-v3-DistilBERT top-1000, shared evaluation suite).

Three findings stand out. First, the choice of objective has a large and statistically significant impact: InfoNCE and MarginMSE form a top tier that consistently outperforms pointwise BCE across all backbones and evaluation settings. Second, distilling LLM knowledge to fine-tune cross-encoder (DistillRankNET or ADR-MSE) doesn’t yield significantly better results than fine-tuning with supervised objectives (InfoNCE) or distilling from strong cross-encoder teachers (MarginMSE). Third, choosing a strong objective can rival or exceed the benefit of scaling to a larger backbone, which is practically relevant for latency-constrained deployments.

By releasing our modular codebase, version-controlled YAML configurations, and complete model checkpoints, we provide a transparent foundation that empowers the community to rigorously test, extend, and improve upon these results, facilitating the development of the next generation of ranking models.

7.
Limitations

Several limitations remain. First, hyperparameters were tuned on proxy models rather than per-backbone due to computational cost. Second, our distillation experiments depend on the specific teacher scores from
Schlatt et al. 2025
and

Hofstätter et al. 2020
; the effect of varying teacher quality is unexplored.
Third, the negative sampling source is not fully controlled: InfoNCE uses ColBERTv2 hard negatives while BCE and Hinge use standard MS MARCO negatives. MarginMSE (trained on BM25 negatives) achieving statistically equivalent performance provides indirect evidence that the loss formulation matters more than the negative pool, but a controlled ablation would strengthen this conclusion.
Finally, all evaluations use a single first-stage retriever (SPLADE-v3 top-1000); results may differ with other retrievers or candidate depths. Extending the benchmark to additional teachers, larger backbone scales, and alternative listwise formulations would further test the generality of these conclusions.
