Germ layer-specific interaction of ion channels and transporters controls membrane potential and left-right patterning in Xenopus tropicalis
Authors: Leonid Tyan, Valentyna Kostiuk, Nicholas Diab, David Zenisek, Mustafa K. Khokha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence indicates that ion channels and transporters are critical for normal embryonic development. They are essential for establishing membrane potential (Vm), a property of all cells. However, it is uncertain if embryos have germ-layer specific regional differences in electrical behavior that play a role in defining local cell fates. In our study, by manipulating ion concentrations and applying a wide spectrum of inhibitors, we demonstrate that various ion channels and transporters differentially determine Vm in specific germ layers of the Xenopus tropicalis embryo. Altering the electrical properties of the embryo leads to localized changes in cell fate and alterations in left-right patterning, which can lead to severe diseases, including heterotaxy and congenital heart disease. Our results indicate that the electrical properties of early embryonic cells are regionally specified and affect local cell fates.

Introduction

All cells in vertebrates exhibit a negative membrane potential (Vm). The interaction of ion channels and transporters on the cell membrane determines its Vm with an essential role of K+ conductance. While a role for Vm in excitable and non-excitable cells is well established, a role for Vm in embryonic development is beginning to emerge1–7. Given the ubiquity of Vm, one could speculate that the electrical properties of cells would provide a permissive rather than an instructive signal. During embryonic development, instructive signals tend to be regionally localized and act to define cell fates or drive morphogenesis in a subset of cells to create the adult body plan. Emerging evidence indicates that the interaction of ion channels and transporters determining Vm can act as an instructive signal for cell fate.

For example, in zebrafish, gain-of-function mutations in kcnk5b, a gene encoding a two-pore domain potassium channel, are responsible for cell hyperpolarization and increased size of the fin8. In planaria, H+/K+ ATPase activity regulates the head-tail axis during regeneration9. Manipulating ion channels and transporters, such as HCN4, H+/K+ ATPase, and H+-V ATPase, can alter cell fate in X. laevis10–12. We previously showed that depleting KCNH6, a member of the ether-a-go-go class of potassium channels, leads to the maintenance of a pluripotent state at the expense of differentiation into ectodermal and lateral mesodermal lineages. These embryos go on to develop defects in left-right (LR) patterning1. Certain internal organs, such as the heart, require LR asymmetry for proper function. Indeed, abnormal development of LR patterning leads to disorders, including situs inversus and heterotaxia. Therefore, understanding the electrical properties of embryonic cells has important implications for human health1,13,14.

However, our study on KCNH6 did not distinguish between a permissive signal that would affect all cells versus a regionally localized instructive signal. We did demonstrate regional changes in cell fate, maintenance of pluripotency in the ectoderm, without a change in the endoderm1. However, we lacked evidence indicating that these germ layers have different electrical properties. Therefore, we could not distinguish whether these changes in cell fate were due to differential Vm across the germ layers or a permissive effect of a global change in Vm.

Interestingly, early studies of Xenopus embryos demonstrated that Vm becomes hyperpolarized during early development, suggesting that the electrical properties of embryonic cells could be dynamic. These studies focused on serial measurements of Vm using electrodes from early cleavage stages to early gastrula in the animal pole, the region of the embryo fated to become ectoderm during gastrulation15,16. However, whether there exist spatial differences by measuring Vm quantitatively in different regions of the embryo (for example, across the germ layers) was unanswered. Quantitative measurements are important as many voltage-sensitive downstream effectors are regulated by precise changes in Vm. In other embryonic contexts, voltage-sensitive dyes indicated that regional differences in Vm may be present in certain embryonic tissues. For example, in Drosophila, Vm is spatiotemporally patterned during development of the wing disc regulating Hedgehog signaling at the compartment boundary7. Alteration of membrane polarization by manipulating the H+/K+ ATPase affects anterior polarity in regenerating planaria9. In X. laevis, neural and eye development can be altered by the expression of various ion channels including potassium (Kv1.5), chloride (GlyR), or a constitutively conductive cation channel (EXP) that lead to changes in Vm17,18. However, whether there are germ-layer specific regional differences in Vm and their implication on cell fate have not been established.

We sought to answer this question by measuring Vm in specific germ layers using intracellular electrodes. Our data indicate that the electrical properties of the germ layers are mediated by different complements of ion channels, indicating that the embryo deploys these channels in a regionally specific manner. Compared to the ectoderm and mesoderm, the Vm of the endoderm differs in its response to extracellular ion concentrations and ion channel and transporter inhibitors. Indeed, based on our findings, we speculated that chloride transport may play a critical role, and blockage of chloride permeation alters cell fate and LR patterning. Our results indicate that temporal and tissue-specific expression of ion channels and transporters may have germ-layer specific regional effects on Vm that translate to changes in cell fate.

Results

Membrane potentials from all three germ layers of X. tropicalis embryos

In X. tropicalis embryos starting from mid-blastula (stage 8) until the end of gastrulation (stage 12), three regions can be distinguished based on morphology and pigmentation: animal pole, marginal zone, and vegetal pole. During blastula stages, the animal pole becomes ectoderm, marginal zone mesoderm, and vegetal pole endoderm. To address whether there is a difference in Vm between these regions from cleavage stages to the end of gastrulation, we performed intracellular microelectrode recordings. Vm was measured from stage 2 to stage 12 in the animal pole, from stage 4 to stage 12 in the vegetal pole, and from stage 8 to stage 12 in the marginal zone (Fig. 1A–C). We found that in the animal pole, there is gradual hyperpolarization of membrane potential from stage 2 to stage 8 followed by gradual depolarization to stage 12. In the animal pole, Vm at stage 2 was -21.9 ± 3.3 mV (n = 12 cells), Vm at stage 8 was −54.6 ± 5.2 mV (n = 14, St.2 vs St.8, p = 0.00002, ANOVA), at stage 12 it was −32.2 ± 3.8 mV (n = 10, St.8 vs St.12, p = 0.04, ANOVA) (Fig. 1A). Similar to the animal pole, the marginal zone exhibited a steady depolarization from mid-blastula to the end of gastrulation. Vm at stage 8 was −52.7 ± 3.5 mV (n = 17), at stage 12 it was -35.9 ± 5.6 mV (n = 10) (Fig. 1B). In the vegetal pole, Vm was not significantly different between stages 4 (-47.1 ± 5.3 mV, n = 11) and 8 (−47.1 ± 5.5 mV, n = 13), but gradually depolarized until the end of gastrulation (stage 12; −28.2 ± 4.9 mV, n = 10) (Fig. 1C). Comparison of Vm between animal and vegetal pole revealed a significant difference in polarization at stage 4 (−29.7 ± 2.8 mV, n = 11 vs −47.1 ± 5.3 mV, n = 11, respectively; p = 0.009, t-test) (Fig. 1D). The Na+/K+ ATPase and potassium channels are necessary for Vm

To address the molecular mechanisms that determine Vm across the germ layers of the embryo, we tested both ion transporters and channels. In all cells, the Na+/K+ ATPase creates chemical gradients such that the sodium concentration is higher outside the cell while the potassium concentration is higher inside the cell. These chemical gradients are essential for establishing the resting Vm in conjunction with ion channels.

Therefore, we postulated that inhibiting the Na+/K+ ATPase should depolarize each of the germ layers given its essential role. We inhibited the Na+/K+ ATPase with its specific blocker ouabain (10 µM)11. Ouabain significantly depolarized Vm in all three regions of the embryo at both stages 8 and 9 (except for the vegetal pole at stage 8) (Fig. 2A–C), although notably a negative membrane potential remained. To further investigate the role of the Na+/K+ ATPase, we tested extracellular sodium replacement with choline. Choline, at high concentrations, can enhance the Na+/K+ ATPase activity19, as well as reduce the Na+ current into cells, as choline is impermeable to Na+ channels20. Previously, it was shown that acute application of choline did not affect Vm in Xenopus embryos21. Consistent with this result, we found that chronic treatment with choline did not affect Vm either (Fig. 2, white vs. blue). In addition, choline could not rescue the membrane depolarization caused by ouabain, again reflecting the essential role for the activity of the Na+/K+ ATPase in all three germ layers (Fig. 2A–C).

Next, we tested the role of potassium channels. The flow of potassium down its electrochemical gradient typically has the largest impact on Vm due to its membrane permeability at resting Vm. Indeed, we previously showed that KCNH6, a voltage-gated potassium channel, is essential for Vm at the animal pole and proper cell fate in the early embryo1. We tested the role of potassium on Vm in all three regions of the embryo. We placed embryos in a high K+ (20 mM KCl) solution to change the gradient for K+ ions. By elevating the extracellular K+ concentration, we reduce the chemical driving force for potassium to leave the cell via potassium channels which depolarizes Vm. Incubating embryos in high K at stage 8 led to a depolarization in the animal pole at stage 9 (control; −42 ± 4 mV, n = 27 vs high K; −20 ± 4.4 mV, n = 14, p = 0.02, ANOVA) but not at stage 8, and in the marginal zone at stages 8 and 9 (stage 8, control; −52.2 ± 3.2 mV, n = 27 vs high K; −23.1 ± 3.3 mV, n = 12, p = 0.0002, ANOVA), (stage 9, control; −47.4 ± 3.9 mV, n = 34 vs high K; −21.8 ± 3.9 mV, n = 15, p = 0.002, ANOVA) (Fig. 2D, E). Surprisingly, the Vm of the vegetal pole was resistant to high K at both stages 8 and 9 (Fig. 2F). This suggests that while potassium channels play an important role in regulating Vm in the animal pole and marginal zone, their impact is significantly less in the vegetal pole.

As described above, choline may repolarize Vm by enhancing the Na+/K+ ATPase activity as well as reduce the Na+ current into cells. While choline could not rescue the depolarization caused by inhibition of the Na+/K+ ATPase (ouabain), we next tested if choline could rescue depolarization due to high K. Indeed, we rescued the effects of high K by substitution of half the amount of Na+ in 1/9 MR solution with choline (choline substitution). While choline substitution did not affect Vm on its own, embryos incubated with high K and choline substitution exhibited normal Vm (Fig. 2D–F), indicating that choline substitution could rescue the depolarizing effect of high K. These results are not easily explained by the expected shift in the Na+ equilibrium potential since a 50% change in extracellular Na+ should have a comparatively smaller effect on membrane potential than the 100-fold change in extracellular K+. We suspect that the high concentrations of choline enhanced Na+/K+ ATPase activity, that in turn repolarized Vm, as previously described19. Indeed, embryos incubated with high K, choline, and ouabain were depolarized indicating that the Na+/K+ ATPase activity was essential for the choline response (Fig. 2D–F).

To narrow in on the molecular mechanism of K+, we next examined voltage-gated K+ channels of the ERG family based on our previous work demonstrating a role for KCNH61. The scorpion peptide ergtoxin (50 nM) is well characterized to specifically block hERG channels22–24. Similar to high K, ergtoxin depolarized the animal pole at stage 9 (control; −62.9 ± 4.2 mV, n = 10 vs ergtoxin; −22.6 ± 3.2 mV, n = 12, p = 0.000001, ANOVA) (Fig. 2G), and in the marginal zone at stage 9 (control; −49.9 ± 6.9 mV, n = 11 vs ergtoxin; −26.3 ± 2.6 mV, n = 12, p = 0.009, ANOVA) (Fig. 2H, J1, J2). Vm in the animal pole and the marginal zone at stage 8 was not significantly affected by ergtoxin, indicating the importance of ERG channels for Vm starting from late blastula but not before. Consistent with our finding with high K, Vm in the vegetal pole was not affected by ergtoxin at any stage (Fig. 2I). Choline rescued the effects of ergtoxin in both the animal pole (ergtoxin; −22.6 ± 3.2 mV, n = 12 vs ergtoxin + choline; −48.2 ± 4.5 mV, n = 15, p = 0.0009, ANOVA) and the marginal zone (ergtoxin; −26.3 ± 2.6 mV, n = 12 vs ergtoxin + choline; −52.4 ± 6.1 mV, n = 10, p = 0.004, ANOVA). The rescue of choline was abolished by incubation of embryos with ergtoxin + choline + ouabain (Fig. 2G, H, J3, J4), similarly as shown for high K.

These results indicate that the germ layers of the early embryo have differential electrical properties during different stages of development. Additionally, the ionic gradients contributing to the electrical properties of the endoderm appear to be distinct from that of the ectoderm and mesoderm.

Na+/K+ ATPase is necessary for LR patterning of the gallbladder

Having investigated the electrical properties of the different germ layers early in development, we next sought to determine if they play any role in development. Since inhibition of the Na+/K+ ATPase depolarized all of the germ layers, we began with ouabain. Treatment with ouabain from stage 2 to stage 25 led to an increased percentage of embryos with abnormal gallbladder placement (control; 4.3 ± 1.1%, N = 336 vs ouabain; 14.3 ± 2.8%, N = 281, p = 0.005, ANOVA) (Fig. 3A, B, F). However, the LR patterning of the cardiac loop was unaffected (Fig. 3A, B, E). Next, we compared the addition of choline to ouabain-treated embryos. Potassium channels are essential for LR patterning

We were surprised that inhibition of the Na+/K+ ATPase did not affect global LR patterning despite the widespread depolarization of the embryo. Previously, we had demonstrated that depolarization by inhibiting potassium channels including KCNH6 led to alterations in global LR patterning (pitx2)1. Our results here indicate that only the animal pole and marginal zone are depolarized by changing the potassium gradient or selectively blocking ERG potassium channels. These results suggest that regional depolarizations may have a greater impact on embryonic development. To address this further, we re-explored the effects of potassium channels on embryonic development.

Embryos incubated in high K until stage 25 exhibited an increased frequency in abnormal heart looping to the left, an L-loop, or to the middle, an A-loop (vs. a normal D-loop to the right) (control; 1.7 ± 0.5%, N = 636 vs high K; 9 ± 2.1%, N = 455, p = 0.002, ANOVA) (Fig. 4A, B, F) and misplacement of the gallbladder on the left (vs a normal positioning of the gallbladder on the right side of the body axis) (control; 4.6 ± 0.9%, N = 546 vs high K; 21.6 ± 3.7%, N = 363, p = 0.000007, ANOVA) (Fig. 4A, B, G). Chloride transport is necessary for Vm and LR patterning

In the above studies, we found that the vegetal pole has a Vm which was resistant to high K or ergtoxin, suggesting that other ions contribute to the establishment of a negative membrane potential in prospective endoderm. This was surprising as K+ permeability driven by the chemical gradient established by the Na+/K+ ATPase generally determines the resting Vm of most cells. Indeed, blocking the Na+/K+ ATPase with ouabain does depolarize the vegetal pole; however, high K+ does not, indicating another class of channels must determine the resting Vm of the vegetal pole. To explore these mechanisms, we next considered chloride channels. DIDS (4,4′-Diisothiocyano-2,2′-stilbenedisulfonic acid) (91 µM) is a broad-spectrum chloride channel and Cl-/HCO3- exchanger blocker25,26. DIDS significantly depolarized the animal pole (stage 9 vehicle; −43.4 ± 6.4 mV, n = 17 vs DIDS; −20.9 ± 3.2 mV, n = 11, p = 0.02, ANOVA), marginal zone (stage 8, vehicle; −48.3 ± 9.4 mV, n = 10 vs DIDS; −20.1 ± 4.9 mV, n = 11, p = 0.01, ANOVA, stage 9, vehicle; −44.9 ± 7.4 mV, n = 11 vs DIDS; −17.8 ± 5.8 mV, n = 12, p = 0.003, ANOVA), and vegetal pole (stage 8, vehicle; −65.3 ± 7.8 mV, n = 11 vs DIDS; −32.3 ± 6.5 mV, n = 14, p = 0.01, ANOVA, stage 9, vehicle; −47.3 ± 6.1 mV, n = 10 vs DIDS; −14 ± 2 mV, n = 13, p = 0.0001, ANOVA) (Fig. 5). Because choline rescued the depolarization in embryos exposed to high K+ and ergtoxin, we tested whether choline would have a similar effect in embryos depolarized by DIDS. Na+/K+ ATPase and chloride transport differentially affect development

The inhibition of both the Na+/K+ ATPase and chloride transport depolarized Vm globally but had different effects on LR patterning. Ouabain mildly affected the LR placement of the gallbladder while DIDS had a major effect on LR patterning of both cardiac loop and gallbladder. Therefore, we compared their temporal requirement during development, studied steps in the LR signaling cascade, and their role in cilia motility.

One advantage of chemical inhibitors is that they can be applied at different times during development to identify temporal requirements. We incubated embryos with ouabain or DIDS during 4 periods: (1) early cleavages – mid-blastula (stages 2-8), (2) late blastula – gastrulation (stages 9-12), (3) neurulation and LRO signaling (stages 13-19), and (4) tailbud (stages 20-28). DIDS led to an abnormal cardiac loop when incubated during the late blastula–gastrulation period (stages 9-12) (DMSO; 0.9 ± 0.4%, N = 813 vs DIDS 20.8 ± 3.5%, N = 250, p = 5.1E-17, ANOVA). Interestingly, DIDS affected gallbladder situs when incubated during both early cleavages—mid-blastula period (stages 2-8) (DMSO; 1.5 ± 0.6%, N = 813 vs DIDS 9 ± 3.8%, N = 216, p = 0.03, ANOVA), and late blastula – gastrulation period (stages 9-12) (DMSO; 1.5 ± 0.6%, N = 813 vs DIDS 21.3 ± 5.4%, N = 250, p = 1.2E-09, ANOVA) (Fig. 8A1, A2). Ouabain affected LR patterning of the gallbladder during early cleavages—mid-blastula period only (control; 0.6 ± 0.3%, N = 742 vs ouabain; 7.5 ± 0.9%, N = 195, p = 2.1E-07, ANOVA) (Fig. 8B1, B2).

We also tested a global marker of LR patterning. The transcription factor, pitx2, is essential for determining LR asymmetry in vertebrates31,32 and is normally expressed in the left lateral plate mesoderm. DIDS dramatically affected pitx2 localization (DMSO; normal (left) 92.5%, bilateral 4.5%, absent 3%, N = 67 vs DIDS; normal (left) 22.6%, bilateral 4.9%, absent 59.8%, right 12.8%, N = 102, p = 2.5E-09, ANOVA). Comparing controls with ouabain-treated embryos, we did not detect any significant alteration in left-sided pitx2 expression (Fig. 8E). Our results indicate that LR defects in DIDS treated embryos are due to events that occur during gastrulation, prior to signaling at the LRO. The LRO is a mesodermal structure formed at the end of gastrulation so the alteration in the pattern of tbxt may be reflected in misorganization of the LRO and consequently LR patterning defects. Interestingly, during the course of our experiments, when we discovered a global LR patterning defect (pitx2c), we examined the multiciliated cells of the embryonic epidermis to assess cilia function. The multiciliated cells of the embryonic epidermis of Xenopus create a brisk extracellular fluid flow. Percentage of embryos with abnormal (slowed or stopped) flow was dramatically increased in DIDS-treated embryos (DMSO; 1.7 ± 1.7%, N = 50 vs DIDS; 72.2 ± 21.5%, N = 49, p = 0.001, ANOVA), while ouabain-treated embryos had normal cilia flow (control; 2.8 ± 1.3%, N = 110 vs ouabain; 1.7 ± 1.7%, N = 61, p = 1, ANOVA) (Fig. 8F). Of note, these cilia do not determine LR patterning and we did not assess LRO cilia because DIDS plays a role prior to the formation of the LRO. Nevertheless, DIDS in addition to depolarizing all three germ layers also affects cilia function, indicating the multiple roles of these channels/transporters in embryonic development.

Discussion

All cells have a negative resting membrane potential that is driven by the ion gradients produced by ATP-driven ion transporters and the relative K+, Cl-, and Na+ conductances down their electrochemical gradients34. In general, K+ channels predominate, leading to a K+ conductance that generates a negative Vm near the Nernst potential for K+. In our previous work, we demonstrated that depletion of KCNH6 depolarized Vm, leading to the activation of voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, which delayed the embryo from exiting a pluripotent state. Therefore, in this context, Vm dictated the cell fates of the early embryo1.

In embryonic development, timing is critical. In the very early embryo, pluripotency is essential which must be extinguished as cells establish the lineages of the different germ layers. Therefore, one might expect that the Vm initially would be depolarized and become hyperpolarized as development proceeds enabling cells to leave a pluripotent state and assume germ layer-specific fates. Indeed, earlier work in X. laevis embryos showed gradual hyperpolarization of Vm from cleavage stages to mid-blastula in the animal pole15,16. Also, it is worth noting a study where Vm was measured in isolated cells from the vegetal pole and partially from the marginal zone, from late blastula stages (8 and 9) of X. laevis embryos35. Here we reproduce the previous experiments in intact and living X. tropicalis and extend it to three regions of the embryo: animal pole (ectoderm), marginal zone (mesoderm), and vegetal pole (endoderm) from stage 2 to stage 12 (final gastrulation stage). Our data confirmed previous studies, showing a gradual hyperpolarization of Vm until stage 8 in the animal pole consistent with a requirement for Vm for initial pluripotency.

Therefore, there is temporal regulation of ion channels, which we presume is due to a combination of changes in the expression, regulation, and modification of these ion channels during development. The role for expression appears to be supported by bulk RNAseq data; however, ion channels are notoriously weakly expressed and so we have been unsuccessful in establishing evidence that the proteins are also temporally regulated in the blastula. However, previously, immunohistochemistry data revealed the temporal localization of some ion channels and transporters in Xenopus, chick, and zebrafish10–12,36.

Importantly, our electrophysiological data would imply that, in addition to temporal regulation, there may also be spatial regulation of ion channels during embryonic development. Multiple avenues of evidence point to this conclusion. The ionic conductances contributing to Vm between the animal and vegetal poles are different. Indeed, we found that ouabain, which inhibits the Na+/K+ ATPase, depolarized all of the germ layers at stage 9 while an elevation of extracellular K+ (“High K”) or blocking K+ channels with ergtoxin depolarized the animal pole and marginal zone without affecting the vegetal pole. Similarly, the H+/K+ ATPase blocker lansoprazole, depolarized the animal pole without affecting other layers. Indeed, a previous study using immunohistochemistry data showed different spatiotemporal localization of H+/K+ATPase during the development of Xenopus, with H+/K+ATPase mRNA expressed only in the animal pole at stage 811. Having investigated K+ conductance, we speculated that chloride channels and transporters may play a role in the vegetal pole, and indeed, the entire embryo, including the vegetal pole, was depolarized with inhibition with DIDS. Thus, our results suggest that during gastrulation the different layers exhibit different dependencies on different ionic conductances and the pharmacological inhibitors provide us tools to differentially manipulate the membrane potential of the prospective endoderm, ectoderm and mesoderm to investigate their influence on patterning during development.

Elevated K+, which depolarizes animal and marginal zones during gastrulation, increased the frequency of both abnormal cardiac looping and gallbladder placement. Following up on our previous findings that reducing Na+ by half and substituting with equimolar choline rescues gastrulation defects induced by KCNH6 CR1, we tested whether Na+ replacement with choline could rescue the effects of elevated K+. Choline rescued both Vm and heterotaxia. Thus, our results are consistent with a relationship between animal/marginal zone membrane potential and LR patterning. The effectiveness of choline is not easily explained by the reduction in Na+ directly on membrane potential. Membrane potential is governed by the GHK equation, which relates membrane potential to the Nernst potentials for permeable ions weighted by their relative permeabilities. The Nernst potential is proportional to the log of the ratio of concentrations of specific ions inside to outside the cell, and thus, reducing Na+ by half should have a minor effect on membrane potential compared to increasing K+ by 100-fold. Nonetheless, our results show a significant rescue of the depolarization, suggesting other mechanisms are involved. Choline has been shown to enhance the activity of the Na+/K+ ATPase when Na+ was reduced19, which could provide a potential explanation for this effect. Consistent with this notion, the Na+/K+ ATPase blocker ouabain eliminated the rescue effect of choline.

In our previous work, inhibition of K+ conductance led to changes in early cell fate in the animal pole and the marginal zone, but the endoderm fates were unaffected. We find here that the endoderm is hyperpolarized like the other layers but resistant to changes in K+ conductance, providing a plausible explanation for it being able to establish endodermal fates even when K+ conductance was inhibited.

In contrast to K+ channel manipulations, the Cl- transport blocker effectively depolarized the vegetal pole in addition to the animal and marginal zones in embryos during blastula stages. This widespread depolarization was accompanied by dramatic changes in LR patterning efficiency, but via a mechanism that differs from KCNH6 depletion. For K+ channel manipulation, the critical period was gastrulation; for DIDS application, it was during early cleavage stages and gastrulation. Moreover, unlike K+ channels, where inhibition caused a reduction in the mesodermal marker tbxt, DIDS enhanced tbxt expression and caused ciliary flow defects implying that cilia function was altered. We envision two potential models to connect these findings with LR patterning. One possibility is that the ciliary flow defects seen on the embryonic epidermis are also occurring in the Left-Right Organizer. Alternatively, the expansion of the mesoderm may alter gastrulation and the formation of the Left-Right Organizer, which is a transient mesodermal structure critically dependent on proper timing and structure.

Interestingly, the depolarization of the endoderm by DIDS could be rescued by choline but not in the mesoderm or ectoderm and this was accompanied by a partial rescue of the LR patterning defects.

Interestingly, the critical period for Na+/K+ ATPase blocker affecting LR patterning was the early cleavage stages. Previously, it was shown that this period is also critical for other ion channels and transporters, whose role in LR patterning has been demonstrated. The potassium channel KCNQ1 and its accessory subunit KCNE1 are asymmetrically localized as maternal proteins during the first few cleavages of X. laevis embryo development in a process dependent on microtubule and actin organization36. The ATP-sensitive K+-channel functions asymmetrically in LR patterning during very early cleavage stages37. Adams et al. revealed an actin-dependent asymmetry of H+-pump H+-V ATPase subunits during the first three cleavages of X. laevis embryo10. These examples and our data point to a complex system where different ion channels and transporters control LR patterning formation in different ways.

Together, our results suggest important interactions between membrane potential and LR patterning, but the roles may vary between layers and timing of depolarization. Adding to this complexity, cells within layers of the gastrula may themselves be heterogeneous and may not express uniform complements of ion channels and transporters. The interactions between membrane potential, cell fate and embryogenesis are complex, and future studies will be necessary to better understand these relationships.

Our results indicate that the embryo has temporal and regional expression of ion channels and transporters that are critical for regional Vm (Fig. 10). Manipulating ion channels and transporters then affects the cell fate of specific germ layers, which can then affect global LR patterning. However, as we explore the role of ion channels and transporters in development, there continue to be unexpected surprises. For example, previously, the role of the chloride channel GlyCl was shown for melanocyte proliferation in Xenopus38. In our study, we uncovered a predominant role for chloride transport in Vm determination of endoderm as well as its role in the function of cilia. The study of ion channels and transporters as necessary components of embryonic development and understanding the complex structure of their interaction will open new horizons in understanding the processes of embryonic and evolutionary dynamics. Methods

Xenopus Husbandry

Adult Xenopus tropicalis were raised and housed according to our established protocols which were approved by the Yale Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. We induced ovulation, performed in vitro fertilization, and raised embryos in 1/9xMR solution (11 NaCl, 0.2 KCl, 0.2 CaCl2 0.11 MgCl2, 0.55 HEPES [all in mM], pH 7.6 with NaOH). All reagents, buffers, and step-by-step instructions of in vitro fertilization are described by del Viso and Khokha39. We staged X. tropicalis embryos according to Nieuwkoop and Faber40.

Organ situs

Stage 45 Xenopus embryos were paralyzed with tricaine and scored with a light stereomicroscope. Cardiac looping was determined by the position of the outflow tract; D-loop: rightward, L-loop: leftward; A-loop: midline. The placement of the gallbladder was scored by its normal position (right-sided) or abnormal (left-sided or midline).

Intracellular Vm recordings

Stage 8 and 9 embryos were mounted into non-toxic clay exposed and covered with 1/9xMR solution. To investigate the resting potential, cells were impaled with a high impedance ( ~ 40 MΩ), sharp microelectrode filled with 3 M KCl for intracellular recordings. Ergtoxin, ouabain, and lansoprazole were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. DIDS was obtained from Tocris. For Vm recordings with ergtoxin, the vitelline membrane was removed by forceps at stage 7. Embryos were incubated with “high K” (1/9xMR except with 20 mM KCl) or ergtoxin from stage 8; with DIDS, ouabain, lansoprazole from stage 2. Concentrations of ergtoxin (50 nM), ouabain (10 µM), and lansoprazole (27 µM) were based on the previously published studies that investigated their role in LR patterning in Xenopus1,11. The concentration of DIDS (91 µM) is based on the study on cardiomyocytes26. The used concentration did not cause general developmental defects. The difference between the control groups in the same conditions is not significant. The recordings were made using a HEKA EPC10 amplifier in current clamp mode. We used HEKA PatchMaster v2x67 software for Windows. All electrodes were zeroed just before entry into the cells and membrane potential was measured after a stable baseline membrane potential was reached.

Whole mount in situ hybridization

Digoxigenin-labeled antisense probes for pitx2c (TNeu083k20), tbxt (TNeu024F07), mixer (TGas105b05), were in vitro transcribed using T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (E2050S) from New England Biolabs. Embryos were collected at the desired stages, fixed in MEMFA for 1–2 h at room temperature (RT), and dehydrated in 100% ethanol. Whole embryos at stage 28 were used to detect the expression of pitx2c. Gastrula stage embryos (stage 10) were bisected to facilitate probe access and visualization of the signal in the prospective endoderm (mixer) or mesoderm (tbxt). Briefly, whole mount in situ hybridization of digoxigenin-labeled antisense probes was performed overnight, the labeled embryos were then washed, incubated with anti-digoxigenin-AP Fab fragments (dilution is 1:3000) (Roche 11093274910), and the signal was detected using BM-purple (Sigma 1442074001)41. To stop the colorimetric reaction, embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde with 0.1% glutaraldehyde in 1x SSC. Embryos were then bleached until pigment was eliminated. Images were taken using Canon EOS camera (DS126201) mounted on a Zeiss Stereomicroscope.

Optical coherence tomography imaging

Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) imaging was performed to assess cilia driven extracellular fluid flow over the surface of the embryonic epidermis. A Thorlabs Ganymede 900 nm spectral domain-OCT Imaging System was used, which allows for 1.4 mm imaging depth in air + water and 2.2 μm Axial Resolution in water. We obtained 2D cross-sectional images by scanning at 36 kHz (36000 A-scans per second). Measurements were obtained with ThorImageOCT software version 4.4.6. Embryos were raised to stages 23–24 and then scanned by OCT to determine the extracellular fluid speed by tracking the endogenous particles within the vitelline envelope29,42. Embryos were categorized qualitatively as either “normal flow” (when compared to stage-matched controls), or “abnormal flow” including “slow flow” which is approximately half of the average flow speed or less, or “no flow,” which is virtually no particle movement43.

Statistical analysis

Data are reported as the mean ± SEM. Determinations of statistical significance were performed using Student’s t-test for comparisons of two means or using ANOVA followed by post hoc Bonferroni test for comparisons of multiple groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (* denotes p ≤ 0.05, ** denotes p ≤ 0.01, and *** denotes p ≤ 0.001. Statistical analysis was performed using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corp.). All measurements were taken from distinct samples. Total cell numbers (n) or embryo numbers (N) are measured over at least 3 independent experiments.
