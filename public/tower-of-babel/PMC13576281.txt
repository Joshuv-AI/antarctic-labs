Long-term evaluation of a comprehensive bullying prevention program starting in preschool: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Antoinette Dewandre, Ophélie Courbet, Quentin Daviot, Marc Gurgand, Elise Huillery, Rebecca Shankland, Marianne Habib, Thomas Villemonteix
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPromoting mental health and psychological well-being from early childhood is a growing priority in global education policy. Bullying has emerged as a major threat to children’s well-being, and while most school-based interventions begin in late childhood or adolescence, research suggests that bullying-related behaviors and peer exclusion emerge as early as preschool. Yet, to date, no bullying prevention program has been rigorously evaluated from the beginning of schooling. This study aims to fill this gap by evaluating Vivre Ensemble—Fri for Mobberi (VE-FFM), a comprehensive, developmentally tailored bullying prevention and social-emotional learning (SEL) program implemented in French primary schools from preschool through elementary school.MethodsWe will conduct a two-arm cluster-randomized controlled trial involving 210 French public schools and approximately 5250 children, recruited at age 3 and followed for 7 years. Schools will be randomly allocated to either the intervention group (VE-FFM) or a wait-list control group continuing with teaching-as-usual. VE-FFM is a universal, whole-school program targeting students, professionals, and families. It focuses on building inclusive peer communities and strengthening children’s social-emotional skills through structured group discussions, well-being activities, and SEL workshops. Primary outcomes include bullying and peer victimization. Secondary outcomes include psychological well-being, mental health difficulties, empathy, social skills, academic performance, teacher–child relationship quality, school belonging, subjective unsafety, perception of school kindness, and teacher job satisfaction. Data will be collected at six timepoints using teacher- and child-rated measures. Implementation fidelity will be monitored through teacher logs and recorded classroom sessions.DiscussionThis trial is the first to evaluate the long-term impact of a bullying prevention and SEL program beginning at preschool level. By embedding the intervention into the full span of early schooling, VE-FFM may produce stronger and more lasting effects than shorter-term interventions implemented at later stages. Results will inform educational and mental health policy by providing evidence on the effectiveness of early, community-based approaches to preventing bullying and promoting mental well-being.Trial registrationDRKS; DRKS00037553. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Over the past two decades, the promotion of child mental health and psychological well-being has become a central objective in global health and education policy [1, 2]. Mental health disorders are among the most prevalent and disabling conditions in childhood and adolescence [3], with estimates suggesting that one in five children worldwide experiences significant psychological difficulties [4]. Childhood mental health difficulties predict long-term impairments in education, employment, and overall quality of life [5, 6].

Developmental research has shown that mental health trajectories are shaped in early childhood, when self-regulation, emotional, and interpersonal skills are still developing [2]. School environments, and the quality of the relationships they foster, play a critical role in these developmental processes [7, 8]. Accordingly, schools are now increasingly viewed as strategic platforms for promoting social-emotional learning (SEL), preventing psychological distress, and fostering inclusive, supportive peer environments [9–11].

Within this broader effort to promote psychological well-being, bullying has emerged as a major risk factor [12]. Bullying undermines victims and bystanders’ sense of safety and belonging—two core components of well-being—[13–15] and has been linked to increased risk of poor health, wealth and social relationship outcomes in adulthood [16]. Peer victimization more broadly is consistently associated with increased symptoms of mental health difficulties [17, 18].

While most bullying prevention programs are introduced in late primary or secondary school [19], growing evidence suggests that bullying-related behaviors emerge as early as preschool, embedded in peer group dynamics [20–22]. From an early age, children engage in asymmetrical peer relationships marked by aggressive behaviors and dominance, especially during resource-related conflicts in nursery and kindergarten settings [23]. They rapidly develop an understanding of social hierarchies [24] and, when group norms allow, may engage in repeated exclusionary and dominance-based behaviors toward specific peers [25]. By the end of kindergarten, some children are already recurrent targets of aggressive behaviors characterized by power imbalance [21, 22].

Despite this, to our knowledge, no intervention aimed at preventing bullying from the earliest years of schooling has undergone formal evaluation. In a meta-analysis of school-based anti-bullying interventions, 69 previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included, yielding positive effects on bullying with a mean effect size of 0.15 and on mental health problems with a mean effect size of 0.20 [19]. Interventions were generally delivered over the course of 1 year, all interventions lasted less than 3 years, and all but one study targeted children or adolescents above 6.

The present study addresses this gap by evaluating the long-term impact of Vivre Ensemble—Fri for Mobberi (VE-FFM; “Learning to live together–Free of Bullying”), a developmentally adapted bullying prevention and SEL program implemented in French primary schools. Grounded in the theoretical framework developed by the Danish Fri for Mobberi (“Free of Bullying” program https://www.freeofbullying.com/), and supported by the sociological findings of the Exploring Bullying in Schools (eXbus) project [26], the intervention is built on the hypothesis that bullying is not merely an individual behavior, but rather a relational and systemic process arising from group norms, emotional insecurity, and cultural patterns of exclusion. The program therefore adopts a universal, group-based approach focused on building inclusive school communities.

In France, there is currently no structured or universal SEL curriculum implemented in preschool settings to promote children’s psychological well-being. In this context, the original Danish Fri for Mobberi program was enriched by integrating a structured SEL curriculum evaluated in a previous randomized controlled trial to create VE-FFM [27]. Importantly, the program engages not only children, but also professionals and families, promoting a whole-community approach that ensures coherence between adult modeling and the values transmitted to children.

By rigorously testing this community-based, developmentally sensitive intervention from the start of formal education, our research will provide critical insights into the potential of early bullying prevention and social-emotional interventions designed to yield greater long-term reductions in bullying compared to interventions implemented at later stages.

Objectives {7}

This trial evaluates the impact of the VE-FFM program, a comprehensive SEL and bullying prevention program implemented in French primary schools over a 7-year period. Our primary objective is to assess the program’s effects on students’ victimization and bullying. We hypothesize that students exposed to the intervention will experience lower rates of victimization and bullying, compared to their peers in the control group.

Our secondary objective is to assess the program’s impact on students’ psychological well-being, mental health difficulties, social skills, empathy, student–teacher relationships, perceptions of school kindness, sense of school belonging, subjective unsafety, academic performance (as measured by national educational assessments), and on teachers’ job satisfaction. We hypothesize that students exposed to the intervention will report greater psychological well-being, and show fewer mental health difficulties and improved social and empathic skills, stronger relationships with teachers, higher academic achievement, a greater perception of their school as a kind environment, a reduced subjective unsafety, and an increased sense of belonging. Additionally, we hypothesize that teachers working in schools delivering the program will report enhanced job satisfaction.

We will also investigate heterogeneous effects based on both school-level and child-level characteristics. Regarding school-level characteristics, we will first examine whether program effects vary according to the school’s social position index (a school-based composite indicator used in the French educational system to assess the social background of students based on the professions and education levels of their parents or guardians), the number of classes in the school, and the school climate prior to study initiation. In terms of child-level characteristics, we will explore heterogeneity based on children’s initial level of mental health difficulties, gender, and age. Consistent with previous findings [27], we expect that children with greater initial difficulties and older children will derive greater benefit from the intervention.

Finally, we will examine potential mechanisms through which the VE-FFM program may reduce bullying and victimization, particularly through changes in children’s socio-emotional competencies, perceptions of the school social environment, and teacher–child relationships.

Trial design {8}

The trial described in this protocol is a two-arm cluster randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the impact of VE-FFM, a teacher-delivered SEL and bullying prevention program, compared to teaching-as-usual. A total of 210 schools will be randomly allocated to either the intervention group or the control group (105 schools per arm). An average of 25 students per school, aged between 3 and 4 years old currently enrolled in Pre-Kindergarten (Pre-K, petite section) will be recruited between September and October 2025, resulting in a total sample of approximately 5250 students. This research cohort will be followed over 8 years, with teacher questionnaire data collected regularly. Student questionnaire data will be collected after 6 and 7 years of intervention (Table 1; participant timeline section). Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

The study will be conducted in French public schools that volunteer to participate. These are either stand-alone primary schools or a pair consisting of a preschool (école maternelle) and an elementary school (école élémentaire) located in the same area, with more than 80% of the preschool students expected to continue their education in the associated elementary school (as confirmed by school principals).

Eligibility criteria {10}

Inclusion criteria: children at pre-K level (petite section, first year of preschool in the French system) attending French public schools taking part in the study.

Exclusion criteria: parent refusal for the child to participate in the study or consent withdrawal during the study.

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

The schools participating in the research commit to taking part through formal agreements covering the period 2025–2028 for preschool years, and 2028–2032 for elementary school years. The agreements are signed and returned in June 2025.

Informed consent to participate in data collection is obtained from children’s parents, before starting the first evaluation session. Information letters with an attached reply form are posted to teachers at the beginning of the school year. Teachers then transmit these letters to parents. Parents who refuse participation for their child send the reply form back to the teacher, who then informs principal investigators of parent refusal [AD, TV]. In the information letter, (1) objectives, contents, and intended benefits of the intervention are described; (2) parents are told that participation of their child is entirely voluntary (i.e., parents can accept or refuse to participate to the study without any consequences); and (3) that they can withdraw their participation at any time by stating it to the teacher and/or returning the refusal reply form. Parents are not paid for their child’s participation in the study. Oral consent will be obtained from children before student-rated data collection.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

N/A. No biological specimens are collected in this trial.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

In this trial, children in schools implementing the VE-FFM program as part of their regular school year are compared to children in wait-list control schools, who follow the “usual” school curriculum. Schools are randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the wait-list control group.

Preschool professional teams assigned to the intervention group receive training between September and December 2025 (first session, 6 h), and between September and December 2026 (second session, 3 h). Those assigned to the wait-list control group for the research cohort (children starting petite section—equivalent to U.S. Pre-K—in September 2025) are offered training during the second half of the 2027–2028 school year, when the cohort completes its final year of preschool (grande section). Elementary professional teams in the intervention group receive training between September and December 2028 (first session, 6 h) and between September and December 2029 (second session, 3 h), aligned with the cohort’s transition into elementary school. Control group teams are offered training during the second half of the 2031–2032 school year, when the cohort completes fourth grade (CM1), which marks the final data collection point.

Primary school teams (combined preschool and elementary schools) assigned to the intervention group are trained on the preschool version of the program (ages 3–6) between September 2025 and December 2026, and on the elementary version (ages 6–10) between September 2028 and December 2029. Control group teams receive training on the preschool version during the second half of the 2027–2028 school year and are asked not to implement it with elementary-level students in subsequent years if they remain in the same school (potential contamination will be monitored annually). They are trained on the elementary school version during the second half of the 2031–2032 school year.

A wait-list control condition was selected for this trial to allow effect size comparisons with prior bullying prevention research, which typically uses this design, before conducting future trials comparing the program to active control conditions to assess specificity of effects. Offering delayed access to the intervention and materials was deemed essential to enhance recruitment and reduce the risk of disengagement from the control group.

Intervention description {11a}

Wait-list control group

Teachers in the control group are instructed to carry on their teaching as they would in any regular school year. In the French context, there is no systematically deployed anti-bullying prevention program in preschools or primary schools. However, the PHARE program (Prévenir le Harcèlement et Agir avec Respect et Empathie–prevent bullying and act with respect and empathy) is a national initiative launched by the French Ministry of Education to prevent and combat bullying in schools. Initially implemented in middle schools starting in 2021, it has since been progressively extended to elementary schools. Beyond response protocols for suspected bullying incidents, PHARE requires ten hours per year to be dedicated to bullying prevention within the elementary curriculum. Schools are free to use these ten hours to address themes such as: raising student awareness about different forms of bullying, including cyberbullying; fostering empathy, respect, and kindness within the classroom; encouraging emotional expression and nonviolent communication; and promoting proactive bystander behaviors. Pedagogical resources to support these activities are provided by the Ministry of Education. Data will be collected from the control group to assess how these hours were used. Regarding the teaching of social and emotional learning, no nationwide program is formally implemented in preschools or primary schools. Nonetheless, in 2024, the Ministry published a booklet of “empathy sessions,” which includes a series of structured pedagogical activities aimed at fostering socio-emotional skills relevant to bullying prevention. Based on these or other resources, some teachers independently choose to incorporate SEL activities into their practice. Within the control group, the use of such resources will also be tracked annually to characterize usual practices against which the program is being evaluated.

Intervention group

The professional teams in the intervention group schools will be trained and supported to implement the VE-FFM program.

VE-FFM is a Danish-originated school bullying primary prevention program available for children aged 0–3, 3–6, and 6–9 years (freeofbullying.com/). In France, the program is implemented by the Ligue de l’enseignement, a French secular organization that promotes access to education, citizenship, and social cohesion through cultural and educational activities. The program is grounded in a community-based approach to bullying prevention, which emphasizes that bullying is not only an individual behavior but a systemic issue that arises from group dynamics and cultural norms. Drawing on research from the eXbus project [26], the program sees bullying as a process of exclusion within groups where children feel insecure or struggle to belong. Instead of targeting individual bullies or victims, VE-FFM works proactively from an early age to build communities marked by tolerance, respect, care, and courage—the four core values of the program.

The French version of the program for the 3–6 age group was developed through a process of cultural adaptation from the original Danish framework, conducted in collaboration with 15 preschools. The original Danish content for the 3–6 age group, which primarily focused on collective dynamics, was adapted to the French context by integrating a structured SEL curriculum previously evaluated in French preschools [27, 28]. The adaptation of the program for the 6–9 age group is currently underway and will be expanded to cover the entire French elementary school age range (6–10 years).

The program’s child-focused activities are structured around three main components: discussion forums, well-being workshops, and SEL workshops. Discussion forums are structured group sessions designed to strengthen class cohesion, foster prosocial behavior, support emotional expression, and prevent conflict. Three types of activities can be implemented within these forums: (1) Group agreements involve collaborative reflection on class rules and their connection to the program’s core values—tolerance, respect, care, and courage—typically conducted at the beginning of the year to establish a shared framework; (2) Illustrated discussion boards depict everyday situations of exclusion and provide guided conversations to help children recognize and respond to social dynamics before issues arise; and (3) Emotion circles, a ritualized activity where children express their emotions related to recent events (e.g., recess), are conducted with a guiding principle of avoiding blame, and the group collaboratively seeks supportive solutions. Well-being workshops focus on helping children regulate their emotions and learn to develop attentional focusing skills through body-based activities such as breathing exercises and gentle movement. A signature activity involves narrated massages, in which children are invited (with consent) to trace a story onto a peer’s back while the story is read aloud. This activity promotes attention, body awareness, and the foundational notions of consent and caring for others in a calm, shared setting. SEL workshops provide a coherent curriculum to teach emotional, social, and cognitive regulation skills over the course of the school year [27]. The emotional competencies addressed include self-awareness, recognition of others’ emotions, and basic emotion regulation strategies. Social competencies focus on developing altruistic behaviors and constructive conflict management. Cognitive competencies emphasize attention regulation. The workshops are organized into thematic sequences, allowing for progressive learning and reinforcement of key socio-emotional skills.

In parallel with the activities dedicated to children, the program also targets professionals and parents. Tools are provided to professionals to carry out team activities aimed at developing a shared professional stance grounded in the program’s four core values. The management team receives specific training lasting 1.5 h, delivered online through a video-based learning pathway, to help them support their staff in adopting a shared educational stance, both in adult-to-adult interactions and in interactions with children. Finally, one key component of the training is dedicated to strengthening partnerships with parents. At the launch of the program, parents are systematically informed and receive a letter outlining the program’s objectives, along with practical guidance on bullying prevention and the promotion of positive peer relationships. Beyond this initial communication, professionals are encouraged to create ongoing opportunities for meaningful engagement with families, through workshops and/or thematic events connected to the program. To support this process, professionals are provided with two sets of structured tools: (1) dilemma cards, that present common social or relational situations (e.g., exclusion, conflicts or bystander positions), which can be used to prompt reflection and discussion; and (2) illustrated discussion boards, depicting everyday scenarios that may occur at home or in children’s peer interactions, helping parents to make connections between school experiences and family life. These tools are intended to be used during dedicated parent sessions at school, for shared reflection and co-construction of strategies.

The implementation of the program is supported by a comprehensive pedagogical toolkit provided to each team and specifically tailored to the developmental needs of each age group. The 3–6 toolkit includes (1) a professional guide detailing the theoretical foundations, implementation strategies, and approaches to engaging both colleagues and families, intended for all professionals working in the school, along with a specific guide for school leadership and coordination teams; (2) a mascot (the “Buddy Bear”) for each class, which serves as an emotional anchor and a symbol of care and friendship for children; (3) a set of thematic guides, including a discussion forum manual, a well-being activities booklet, and a booklet dedicated to the SEL workshops; (4) discussion boards and emotion posters that serve as visual tools to foster emotional literacy and support conflict resolution; (5) “well-being cards” illustrating physical postures and breathing techniques, as well as a narrative massage guide for implementing body-centered well-being activities; (6) dilemma cards for both educators and parents, based on real-life scenarios designed to stimulate professional reflection and parent-teacher dialogue; (7) a letter for parents outlining the program's goals and their role in supporting its implementation; and (8) communication posters to be displayed within the school to promote and sustain the project.

In the context of this research, professional teams are trained in the program through an initial 6-h course during the first year, consisting of two 3-h modules that can be delivered over 1 day or two half-days. The first module focuses on foundational knowledge: the definition and systemic nature of bullying; the theoretical background from the ExBus research; the presentation of the four values and their application in daily life; a concise description of the tools intended for the program’s three target groups (children, parents, professionals); and a more detailed presentation of the principles and objectives of the tools specifically designed for children. The second module emphasizes practical implementation through an experiential approach: videos of program activities (well-being workshops, discussion forums) are presented, followed by hands-on practice in which professionals train to lead pedagogical sessions incorporating these activities in small groups. The training concludes with a discussion of the role of educators in fostering inclusive environments, and strategies to actively engage parents as co-educators. Participants are encouraged to critically reflect on their own professional practices and culture, with an emphasis on the idea that the program’s effectiveness depends on the extent to which adults themselves model inclusive and respectful relationships. The toolkit resources available for this purpose are presented. Finally, the importance of communication with parents and the tools specifically designed for them are introduced. In the second year, teams receive a 3-h training session constituting the third module. During this session, professionals first have the opportunity to reflect on their practice over the past year. Then, the six themes of the psychosocial skills workshop booklet are presented, and professionals are trained to deliver a pedagogical sequence for each of the six themes. In addition to these in-person training modules, professionals have the opportunity to participate freely in monthly online reflective practice sessions held via videoconference.

Training is delivered by certified trainers with expertise in early childhood education. The training and certification process for trainers in the Vivre Ensemble–Fri for Mobberi program is structured in five key stages, designed to ensure a progressive acquisition of skills and a solid understanding of the pedagogical approach. It begins with a reverse shadowing session, where the trainee observes a full training session led by a senior trainer (6 h). This is followed by a self-directed exploration of the educational toolkit, allowing the trainee to become familiar with the program’s pedagogical materials (2–3 h). The core of the training then consists of a 12-h in-person “train-the-trainer” session, focusing on both theoretical foundations and practical facilitation skills. The trainee then conducts five supervised pedagogical sessions with children, each followed by a remote debriefing with a senior trainer to reflect on practice and consolidate learning. Finally, the process concludes with a shadowing phase, where the trainee co-facilitates a training session alongside a senior trainer (6 h). Upon completion of all steps, the trainee is certified as an official program trainer.

In the context of this study, teachers are asked to dedicate an average of one hour per week to implementing the program’s activities with the children. They are free to choose the tools, schedule, and temporal structure of their implementation.

Theory of change

The theory of change explaining the link between the implementation of the program and the expected effects on the variables considered in the protocol is based on a systemic representation of the relationships between adult practices, individual behaviors, and dynamic interactions within groups. This theory of change can be specified with regard to the three main components of the program: activities targeting children, activities targeting professionals, and activities targeting parents. Child-focused activities within the program are designed both to model and to foster spontaneous prosocial behaviors (e.g., helping, comforting, and soothing) among peers. They also aim to encourage prosocial bystander responses when children witness aggressive situations. In addition, the program seeks to promote social norms that reject and discourage aggressive behaviors, particularly those targeting individuals perceived as different. Another core component of the intervention involves teaching emotional regulation and conflict-resolution skills. With regard to the professional-focused components, the theory of change posits that professional training activities, together with collective school-level work and the alignment of practices, will lead to changes in professionals’ relational approaches toward both children and colleagues. These changes are expected to translate into shifts in professional practices, including greater use of emotionally supportive interactions, fewer negative verbal exchanges, increased monitoring of harmful peer interactions, and stronger adult involvement in addressing such situations. These developments are assumed to stem from a deeper understanding of bullying as a group-based and systemic phenomenon, as well as from greater awareness of the central role of relational dynamics within peer groups. Finally, the parent-focused components of the program are expected to strengthen parents’ endorsement of prosocial norms while reducing both their tolerance of and modeling of aggressive behaviors.

The theory of change underlying the program posits that the implementation of the three components promoting the core values of VE-FFM—care, courage, tolerance, and respect—alongside SEL activities, will lead to changes at both the individual level (cognitive, emotional, and behavioral) and the group level, through modifications in children’s peer dynamics. Individual behaviors and group dynamics are conceptualized as mutually influential.

At the individual level, the program aims to promote both cognitive-emotional and behavioral changes in children. At the cognitive and emotional level, it seeks to strengthen children’s awareness of their own emotional states, as well as their cognitive and affective empathy. A central cognitive-emotional mechanism targeted by the program is the progressive internalization of injunctive social norms that disapprove of aggressive behaviors. This shift in perceived norms is expected to create positive peer pressure, thereby helping to regulate individual behavior and reduce the likelihood of aggression. At the behavioral level, the program is expected to reduce the frequency of spontaneous aggressive behaviors, increase the occurrence of spontaneous prosocial behaviors, and promote prosocial bystander responses in situations of aggression. It also aims to increase the use of effective emotion regulation and conflict resolution strategies. These individual-level changes are expected to translate into a reduction in conflictual interactions both among children and between children and adults.

Importantly, the theory of change posits a central feedback loop between changes in relational dynamics and changes in children’s cognitive and emotional processes. The core mechanism underlying this feedback loop is the increase in children’s sense of belonging and psychological safety within peer groups and the broader school environment. This enhanced sense of security is hypothesized to reduce aggressive behaviors, based on the assumption that such behaviors may serve a functional role in securing social status or group belonging for the aggressor.

Finally, improvements in the relational climate—characterized by fewer negative interactions and conflicts, and a higher frequency of prosocial behaviors—combined with an increased sense of belonging and the acquisition of emotion regulation skills, are expected to lead to enhanced emotional well-being among students. In turn, higher levels of day-to-day emotional well-being are hypothesized to reduce reactive aggression, thereby further decreasing the frequency of conflictual interactions and the risk of bullying behaviors.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

The intervention is discontinued for a child only in cases where the child changes school. The protocol does not permit any modification of the assigned intervention.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

No specific strategy will be implemented to improve adherence. However, to control fidelity to intervention, implementation notebooks will be distributed to teachers in the intervention group at the beginning of each school year. Each week, teachers will be asked to write down the VE-FFM activities that have been implemented each day and the duration of activities. In the middle of the second kindergarten and elementary school year, teachers will also be asked to record themselves with a sound recorder while implementing three different curriculum activities: two sessions per activity will be recorded and transferred to the principal investigators [TV, AD, OC] to evaluate implementation. Implementation will be assessed for each record by two independent evaluators following a 4-question list inspired by implementation science frameworks [29, 30]: (1) Objectives: To what extent does the teacher’s delivery of the activity align with the general objectives of the program and the specific objectives of the activity (e.g., follows the key steps, uses key terms and concepts, makes connections with other program concepts or tools)?; (2) Pedagogical stance: to what extent does the teacher’s stance during the activity, and in particular teacher-student interactions, align with the program’s pedagogical approach and view of children?; (3) Quality of delivery: To what extent does the teacher demonstrate effective delivery skills (e.g. clear explanations, enthusiastic and warm tone, positive reinforcement of students’ contributions); (4) Participant responsiveness: To what extent do students show engagement and participation in the activity (e.g., verbal responses, signs of attention and interest, involvement in discussions or proposed tasks)? Evaluators will rate each question on a qualitative scale from 1 (= “Insufficient”) to 5 (= “Very satisfactory”). For each activity, scores for each question in each record for each evaluator will be averaged into a total fidelity score on a 1-to-5 scale.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Treatment of all sorts for children is not controlled in this trial. It is therefore possible for children to begin or stop a drug treatment or a psychological intervention during the trial. At each data collection point, all teachers (intervention group and control group) are asked if they participated in other trainings that aim to develop socio-emotional skills, to promote psychological well-being or to prevent bullying over the course of the year.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

N/A. No provision for ancillary or post-trial care is provided.

Sample size {14}

One study examined the distribution of effect sizes in the context of interventional research on school-based bullying prevention to establish cut-offs for small, medium, and large effect sizes in this field [31]. The results indicated that effect sizes of 0.07, 0.123, and 0.227 correspond to small, medium, and large effects in anti-bullying interventions.

Given our hypothesis that a continuous and consistent early prevention program, implemented from the onset of schooling, would yield greater effects than most programs delivered over shorter durations, we conducted our power calculations based on anticipated effect sizes exceeding 0.15 standard deviations for all our measures.

Accordingly, we performed power calculations to estimate the number of schools required to detect a minimum effect size of 0.15 standard deviations on continuous measures after 7 years, while accounting for an estimated student attrition rate of 20% over this period. A total of 210 schools, each initially enrolling an average of 25 students (5250 students in total), would provide sufficient power to detect an effect size of 0.16 at the 5% significance level, accounting for a compliance rate of 95%, an intra-class correlation coefficient of 0.1, and a coefficient of determination (R2) of 10%.

Our study also includes a binary measure, as elementary school students will be asked whether or not they have experienced bullying, based on the main item of the rBVQ questionnaire. According to data from a report currently under review based on the nationally representative Enabee study sample [32], 16.8% of elementary school students in France are estimated to experience significant levels of victimization. Considering our sample of 210 schools, 25 students per school, an 80% retention rate, an ICC of 0.1, a compliance rate of 95%, and an estimated bullying victimization prevalence of 16.8%, it will be possible to detect an absolute reduction of 5.8%, that is, a relative reduction of 35% (odds-ratio (non victimization):1.64).

Recruitment {15}

Participating schools will be recruited between January 1 st and September 1 st, 2025. To be eligible, schools must either be a primary school (serving students from petite section to CM2, equivalent to Pre-K to 5th grade in the U.S. system), or form a partnership between a preschool (école maternelle) and a geographically close elementary school (école élémentaire), under the condition that more than 80% of the preschool’s students continue their education in the associated elementary school.

Publicity about the study is carried out in partnership with the General Directorate of School Education (Direction Générale de l’Enseignement Scolaire, DGESCO), via academic districts (Académies) and local education authorities through emails and presentation meetings. A dedicated website has been created to present the project and its timeline. Interested school principals contact the research team, which verifies the inclusion criteria before sending a research partnership agreement to be signed.

Children will be recruited in each classroom by the teachers, who will provide information about the study to parents and collect consent to participate. To be eligible, students must be enrolled in petite section (equivalent to Pre-K) during the 2025–2026 school year. School principals will provide a list of students from each class for whom no refusal to participate has been received. The principal investigator will then randomly select 25 children from that school list. If some schools have a small number of classes and cannot reach that number, recruitment will be increased in larger schools to reach an average of 25 children per school.

Randomization will be performed after having recruited 210 schools. Who will be blinded {17a}

Description of the questionnaires used and variables retained (children)

Academic performance

Indicators of school performance are obtained based on national evaluations taking place at the beginning of the 4th grade and 6th grade in France (EVALAIDE). These evaluations target various literacy and math skills. In 4th grade, literacy assessments are designed to evaluate students’ proficiency in reading, writing, and comprehension of both oral and written language. In mathematics, the assessments focus on number sense, mental arithmetic, written calculation, problem-solving skills, and the ability to recognize and analyze geometric figures. In 6th grade, the exercises are designed to assess knowledge and skills related to oral language, reading and comprehension of written texts, and language structure. In mathematics, the evaluation covers the domains of numbers and calculation, measurement, and space and geometry.

Mathematic skills, literacy skills

Based on the test results for each competence, a total score out of 100 will be calculated for each of the two domains for each student.

Empathy questionnaire for children and adolescents

The Empathy Questionnaire for Children and Adolescents (EmQue-CA) is a self-report questionnaire designed to assess empathy in children aged 9 to 16 years. It comprises 14 items divided into three scales, each measuring a distinct component of empathy: affective empathy, cognitive empathy, and intention to comfort. The psychometric properties of the EmQue-CA were evaluated in a previous study, which confirmed its three-factor structure and demonstrated good internal consistency, strong convergent validity, and satisfactory concurrent validity [33]. Although its reliability and validity have been established in other languages [34, 35], no French version has yet been developed or validated. Accordingly, a validation study will be conducted as part of the present research.

EmQue-CA total score; EmQue-CA affective empathy score; EmQue-CA cognitive empathy score; EmQue-CA intention to comfort score: Students are asked to indicate the extent to which each statement applies to them on a three-point Likert scale: 1 = not true, 2 = somewhat true, 3 = true. Raw scores for each scale are calculated by summing the corresponding item scores, with higher scores indicating greater empathy.

Forms of peer victimization scale The Forms of Peer Victimization Scale (FPVS) is a teacher- and student-report scale designed to assess experiences of peer victimization [36]. The original version includes 16 items grouped into four subscales: physical victimization, verbal victimization, social victimization, and property attacks. The instrument has demonstrated promising psychometric properties in a child population around the age of 10, including satisfactory internal consistency, temporal stability, as well as concurrent and convergent validity [36]. To date, no validated French version of the FPVS exists, and its psychometric properties have not yet been established in the French language. A validation study will therefore be conducted as part of the present research. For questionnaires undergoing validation within the context of this study, a cautious and transparent approach will be adopted if any instrument fails to demonstrate satisfactory psychometric properties in the French context. First, we will examine whether the use of specific subscales or a subset of items with adequate psychometric properties is appropriate, based on the results of the validation analyses. Second, analyses involving such measures will be clearly identified as exploratory and interpreted with caution. Third, if the validation process does not yield satisfactory psychometric properties, alternative validated instruments available in French will be used to support the analysis and interpretation of the findings.

FPVS total victimization score; FPVS physical victimization score; FPVS verbal victimization score; FPVS social victimization score; FPVS property attacks score (teacher and student version): Respondents indicate the frequency of victimization experiences over the past several months using a 5-point Likert scale: 1 = not at all, 2 = a little, 3 = sometimes, 4 = a lot, and 5 = a whole lot. Raw scores for each subscale are calculated by summing the corresponding item scores, with higher scores indicating greater levels of peer victimization.

KIDSCREEN‑52:

The European KIDSCREEN-52 is a self-report questionnaire designed to assess health-related quality of life in children and adolescents aged 8 to 18 years [37]. It encompasses 10 dimensions: physical well-being, psychological well-being, moods and emotions, self-perception, autonomy, parent relations and home life, social support and peers, school environment, social acceptance (bullying), and financial resources. The instrument’s psychometric properties have been evaluated across 13 European countries, including France [36]. It demonstrated a robust factor structure, acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.77 to 0.89), good test–retest reliability, and satisfactory convergent validity [37].

KIDSCREEN-52 general psychological well-being score; KIDSCREEN-52 psychological difficulties score; KIDSCREEN-52 relational well-being score; KIDSCREEN-52 school well-being score: A general psychological well-being score will be calculated by averaging the scores of items from subdimension 2 (Your feelings) and items 1 and 2 from subdimension 4 (How you feel). A psychological difficulties score will be computed by averaging the items from subdimension 3 (Your general mood) and items 3, 4, and 5 from subdimension 4 (How you feel). A relational well-being score will be derived by averaging the scores from items 1 and 6 subdimension 8 (Friends), and a school well-being score will be calculated based on the average of items 1, 2, and 5 from subdimension 9 (At school). All items in the selected subdimensions are rated on a 5-point Likert scale reflecting the frequency of the behavior or feeling experienced over the past week.

Preschool and kindergarten behavior scale–social skills

The Preschool and Kindergarten Behavior Scales-Social Skills (PKBS-SS) [38] is a teacher-rated scale assessing preschool and kindergarten children’s social skills. The original scale consists of 34 items divided in 3 subscales: social cooperation, social interaction, and social independence. It displays strong stability as well as adequate content validity and construct validity [38]. The French version of the PKBS-SS displays a five-factor structure: social interaction, agreeableness with peers, autonomy (both parts of the original social independence subscale), compliance, and cooperation with peers (both parts of the original social cooperation subscale) [27]. Adequate construct validity as well as acceptable to excellent internal consistency were reported in a previous study on a French sample of preschool children (total scale: α = 0.94; social interaction: α = 0.90; compliance: α = 0.86; agreeableness: α = 0.90; autonomy: α = 0.76; social cooperation: α = 0.87) [27]. Psychometric properties of the PKBS-SS will be evaluated for elementary school children as part of this study.

PKBSS-SS social interaction score; PKBS-SS cooperation with peers score The 34 items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (= Never) to 3 (= Often). Raw scores for each subscale are calculated by adding scores of each item, with higher scores indicating better social skills.

Revised bully/victim questionnaire

The Revised Bully/Victim Questionnaire (rBVQ) is a 18-item self-report instrument designed to identify children and adolescents’ potential involvement in school bullying [39, 40]. It comprises two subscales of nine items each: victimization and perpetration. The questionnaire begins with a standardized definition of bullying to ensure a shared understanding of the construct. Each subscale includes a general item (“How often have you experienced this type of behavior/engaged in this type of behavior over the past couple of months?”), followed by specific items targeting various forms of bullying. The rBVQ has demonstrated sound psychometric properties across various linguistic and cultural contexts [41, 42]. The French adaptation of the rBVQ maintains the original two-factor structure [43, 44] and exhibits satisfactory internal consistency (α ranging from 0.72 to 0.76) and discriminant validity in populations of children and adolescents aged 9 to 17 [43, 44].

Bullying status Students’ bullying status is determined based on their responses to the general item. Those who report being bullied two to three times a month or more are classified as victims.

School climate questionnaire

The School Climate Questionnaire (SCQ) is a self-report instrument developed to assess students’ subjective perceptions of the school environment. The questionnaire comprises 22 items and captures three dimensions of school climate: deviant behavior, school well-being, and subjective unsafety. The SCQ has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties, including a stable three-factor structure confirmed by confirmatory factor analysis [45]. In the present study, only the subjective unsafety subscale will be used. This subscale assesses students’ perceptions of insecurity and exposure to negative peer interactions within the classroom environment, including behaviors related to teasing, name-calling, aggression, and harassment. The subscale has shown satisfactory internal consistency (α ≈ 0.72) and meaningful associations with related constructs such as victimization, aggression, and lower levels of well-being. As no validated French version of the SCQ is currently available, a validation study of the instrument will be conducted as part of the present research.

SCQ subjective unsafety score The subscale includes 5 items (e.g., “In your class, it is customary to call names” or “In your class, it is customary to interfere with each other, make others nervous, or harass them”). Items are rated on a dichotomous response scale (yes/no). A total score is computed by summing endorsed items, with higher scores indicating higher perceived levels of subjective unsafety within the classroom.

School kindness scale

The School Kindness Scale (SKS) is a brief, unidimensional self-report measure designed to assess students’ perceptions of kindness within the school environment, especially, the perceived frequency of kind behaviors in the classroom and school, as well as the extent to which kindness is encouraged [46]. The original scale displays adequate internal consistency and provides evidence of convergent and discriminant validity in a child and adolescent population (aged 9 to 14 years) [46]. Similarly, the French adaptation retains a unidimensional factor structure, shows acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.71) and suggests convergent and discriminant validity in a population of children and adolescents aged 10 to 15 years [47].

SKS total score The 5 items are rated using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = disagree a lot to 5 = agree a lot. The total score is computed by summing all item responses, with higher scores indicating greater perceived kindness in the school environment.

Simple school belonging scale

The Simple School Belonging Scale (SSBS) is an unidimensional, 10-item student-rating scale designed to evaluate students’ sense of school belonging [48]. In a previous study involving adolescents in junior high school, the scale demonstrated promising psychometric properties, including good internal consistency and preliminary evidence of construct validity [48]. To date, no validated French version of the SSBS exists, and its psychometric properties have not yet been established in the French language or for children in elementary school. We will therefore conduct a validation study of the scale before using it in this study.

SSBS total score Students respond to each item using a 4-point Likert scale (“NO!,” “no,” “yes,” “YES!”). The total score is obtained by summing the scores of all items, with a higher score indicating higher sense of school belonging.

Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire—extended teacher version

The extended teacher version of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) is a standardized instrument designed to assess children’s externalizing symptoms, internalizing symptoms, and prosocial behaviors of 4- to 16-year-old children [49]. It comprises five subscales, each consisting of five items: emotional problems, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship problems, and prosocial behavior. The SDQ displays satisfactory reliability and validity [49] and although its factor structure in French language remains uncertain [50, 51], the SDQ French version shows satisfactory reliability and content validity for the total difficulties score [50].

SDQ total difficulties score The questionnaire includes 25 items rated on a 3-point Likert scale indicating the extent to which each statement applies to the child: 0 = Not true, 1 = Somewhat true, 2 = Certainly true. The total difficulties score is calculated by summing the scores of all subscales except for prosocial behavior. Raw scores for each subscale and the overall difficulties score are computed by adding the item responses. Higher scores reflect greater levels of difficulties.

Student–teacher relationship scale–short form

The Student–Teacher Relationship Scale-Short form (STRS-S) is a brief teacher-rated scale designed to explore teachers’ perceptions of their relationship with a specific child in their classroom [52]. It consists of 15 items grouped into two factors: closeness and conflict. This scale has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties across various samples [52]. It is designed to be used with children aged 3–8. The French adaptation validated in a population of pre-K and kindergarten children retains the original factor structure and shows adequate to excellent internal consistency (closeness: α = 0.77; conflict: α = 0.93), along with satisfactory construct validity [27].

STRS conflict score The 7 items assessing conflict are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = Definitely does not apply to 5 = Definitely applies. Total scores are computed separately for each subscale.

Description of the questionnaires used and variables retained (teachers)

Satisfaction with professional life scale (« Echelle de Satisfaction de Vie Professionnelle»)

The Echelle de Satisfaction de Vie Professionnelle (ESVP) scale is a unidimensional French short self-report measure of general professional life satisfaction derived from the Satisfaction with Life scale from Diener et al. (1985) [53]]. It displays an adequate level of internal consistency (α = 0.87) and temporal stability, and good construct validity [27].

ESVP Total score The 5 items are rated on a seven-point Likert scale (from 1 = “strongly disagree” to 7 = “strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating higher professional life satisfaction.

Data management {19}

The study complies with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), and data management is overseen by the local Data Protection Officer (DPO) of Université Paris 8. Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Child-reported outcomes are assessed at a single post-intervention time point. Therefore, intervention effects on these outcomes will be estimated using regression models including treatment assignment as the main predictor and standard errors clustered at the school level. Models may additionally include pre-specified child- and school-level baseline covariates to improve precision. For binary child-reported outcomes, we will estimate linear probability models with school-clustered standard errors.

To estimate the effects on teacher-rated outcomes listed in Sect. 12, given the longitudinal and hierarchical structure of the data, we will rely on multilevel mixed-effects models. Linear mixed-effects models will be estimated for continuous outcomes. Time will be included as a fixed effect, and the main parameter of interest will be the interaction between time and intervention group. Time will be modelled either as a categorical or continuous variable depending on model fit and the observed developmental trajectories. Random intercepts will be included at the school level to account for clustering, and at the individual level to account for within-individual dependence across repeated measurements. Models may additionally include pre-specified child- and school-level baseline covariates to improve precision. When theoretically and empirically justified, random slopes for time may also be included to account for inter-individual variability in developmental trajectories. Random slopes for time will only be included if supported by model convergence, model fit indices, and sufficient between-individual variability in trajectories. Models will be estimated using maximum likelihood estimation. Standard diagnostic procedures will be used to assess model fit and assumptions. When substantial violations are identified, alternative modeling approaches will be considered in sensitivity analyses. All available data across time points will be used under the assumption of missing at random (MAR). Sensitivity analyses assessing the robustness of findings to departures from the MAR assumption will be conducted as described below (section Methods in analysis to handle protocol non‑adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}).

In addition to the intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses, exploratory treatment-on-the-treated analyses will be conducted using Wald instrumental-variable estimators based on implementation exposure. Treatment-on-the-treated analyses will rely on a measure of cumulative implementation exposure reflecting the frequency and duration of child-focused VE-FFM activities delivered over time. This quantitative indicator will be derived from longitudinal implementation logs completed throughout the study. Assignment to the intervention group at the school level will be used as an instrumental variable for implementation exposure. Because implementation fidelity may be associated with unmeasured school- and teacher-level characteristics, treatment-on-the-treated estimates will be interpreted cautiously. In particular, these analyses rely on strong assumptions regarding the validity of the instrumental-variable approach and will therefore be considered exploratory and complementary to the primary ITT analyses.

To address multiple testing, outcomes will first be grouped into pre-specified families based on construct and informant source. Within each family including multiple related outcomes, we will construct standardized treatment effect indices following Anderson’s approach [54], using the inverse covariance matrix of the outcomes to account for correlations among measures. Family-level standardized indices will constitute the primary confirmatory analyses within each outcome domain, whereas analyses of individual subscales will be considered secondary and interpreted cautiously. For each estimation within each family, we will report both the unadjusted p-value of the coefficient of the treatment variable, and the p value adjusted for control of the false discovery rate using appropriate statistical correction methods (e.g., [55]).

In addition to the primary and secondary outcome analyses, mediation analyses will be conducted to examine the potential mechanisms through which the VE-FFM program may influence bullying and victimization outcomes. These analyses are derived from the theory of change underlying the intervention, according to which improvements in children’s socio-emotional competencies, perceptions of the school social environment, and teacher–child relationships are expected to contribute to reductions in bullying and victimization. More specifically, longitudinal mediation models will be estimated to examine whether early changes in key socio-emotional and relational processes statistically account for part of the intervention effects on later bullying and victimization outcomes. Mediators assessed at earlier time points (e.g., empathy, social skills, teacher–child relationship, school belonging, and perceived school kindness assessed at the end of CE2) will be used to predict subsequent child-reported bullying and victimization outcomes assessed at the end of CM1, thereby strengthening the temporal interpretation of the mediation analyses. In addition, exploratory mediation analyses will examine whether children’s perceptions of subjective unsafety, assessed concurrently with child-reported bullying and victimization outcomes, statistically account for part of the association between the intervention and these outcomes. Because these variables are assessed at the same time point, these analyses will be interpreted cautiously and will not be considered as providing strong evidence for temporal mediation. Given the hierarchical and longitudinal structure of the data, mediation analyses will rely on multilevel modeling approaches accounting for children nested within schools and, when repeated measures are available, repeated observations nested within children. Indirect effects will be estimated using mediation models adapted to clustered and longitudinal data. Overall, results will be interpreted cautiously given the possibility of residual confounding and reciprocal relationships between mediators and outcomes.

Effect estimates will be reported with 95% confidence intervals and standardized effect sizes when appropriate.

Interim analyses {21b}

Not applicable. No interim analyses are planned.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

Exploratory subgroup analyses will be conducted. We will estimate the effects of the intervention on the children’s outcomes listed in Sect. 12 based on the following characteristics: Age of the child; Gender of the child; Initial child’s level of mental health difficulties (SDQ total score); the Social Position Index of the school; the number of classes in the school; the school climate prior to study initiation.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Non-adherence to the assigned intervention condition and variability in implementation fidelity will be monitored through questionnaires completed by teachers at the end of each school year. Primary analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, whereby all participants will be analyzed according to the initial random assignment of their school, regardless of adherence to the assigned condition or level of implementation fidelity.

Attrition rates and baseline characteristics of participants retained versus lost to follow-up will be compared within and across treatment groups to assess potential differential attrition over time.

For child-reported outcomes assessed at a single post-intervention time point, analyses will rely on available observed data. For longitudinal teacher-reported outcomes, primary analyses will rely on mixed-effects models estimated using maximum likelihood, which use all available outcome data under the assumption that missingness can be explained by observed participant characteristics and previously observed outcomes (i.e., missing at random; MAR).

Because this assumption may not fully hold in the context of a long-term school-based intervention, sensitivity analyses will be conducted in both cases to evaluate the robustness of findings to potential attrition-related bias. In particular, inverse probability weights will be estimated based on the predicted probability of remaining observed at follow-up using baseline child- and school-level characteristics, including baseline outcome measures when available, gender, age, baseline mental health difficulties, school social position index, school initial climate, and school size. Weighted analyses will then be compared with the primary analyses.

Because the MAR assumption cannot be empirically verified, additional tipping-point sensitivity analyses will be conducted for the main primary outcomes to examine how large departures from MAR would need to be to alter the conclusions of the primary analyses.

Because missingness is expected to be minimal for baseline covariates, occasional missing values will be handled using simple mean imputation procedures. Missing outcome values will not be explicitly imputed in the primary analyses.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant level-data, and statistical code {31c}

The full study protocol and statistical analysis code will be made publicly available through an open-access repository (e.g., OSF or Zenodo) upon publication of the main results. Regarding participant-level data, access will be granted only to de-identified datasets, in compliance with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Personal data will be pseudonymized and any information that could allow direct identification of individual participants or schools will be removed. All data sharing procedures will be subject to prior approval by the data protection officer and the relevant ethics committee.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {5d}

Data monitoring is conducted by the principal investigators, who oversee the implementation of the research protocol, including randomization, data collection, and the organization of study visits. A research assistant supports the principal investigators by coordinating communication with teachers and evaluators, and by carrying out administrative tasks related to the trial. Data analysis and statistics are carried out by one of the principal investigators [QD]. Evaluators are supervised by a principal investigator [AD] and are regularly in contact by phone and emails with principal investigator during visits periods. Principal investigators meet regularly to ensure and control trial progress, and are in constant contact by emails.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role, and reporting structure {21a}

An independent data monitoring committee has not been formed and no independent auditing will take place.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Due to the intervention nature, serious adverse events are not expected. Any adverse event that occurred will be reported in the manuscript describing trial results if they are directly related to intervention. Those adverse events will be monitored by principal investigators and addressed until resolution.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

Investigators accept to comply with the regulatory requirements of the competent authority for a research audit. Audit may be carried out at any stage of the trial, from the protocol development to the publication of results and archival of data.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g., trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

No important protocol amendments are anticipated. All protocol modifications must be submitted to the Institutional Review Board of the Paris School of Economics prior to implementation, and all participants must be informed of protocol changes.

Dissemination plans {31a}

Regardless of magnitude or direction of effects, all relevant trial results will be submitted to scientific review for publication. No publication restriction is planned. Teachers will be informed through an online conference of the results of the trial, and parents can be informed of overall trial results by principal investigators [OC, TV] after a request by email.

Discussion of practical or operational issues

The most significant operational challenge of this research is its duration, which requires maintaining the commitment of school teams to the research protocol over multiple years, and annually integrating new members of the professional teams into the protocol. To maximize the chances of success, email exchanges and meetings will be conducted with school leadership at the beginning and end of each academic year. Newly arrived staff members will be contacted by phone at the start of each school year to be informed about the research. Partnership agreements will be signed in June 2025 with the schools participating in the study, as well as with the relevant departmental educational authorities.
