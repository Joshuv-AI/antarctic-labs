Extensive bilateral lower limb deep vein thrombosis following uncomplicated open appendectomy in a young healthy male: a case report
Authors: Abdulillah R Khamees, Salaheddin M Abdulhamid, Hassan H Eladl, Yaser Aamer Eisa Alhaibi, Mohammed Emad Mohammed Gafar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep vein thrombosis (DVT) involves blood clots in deep veins, primarily in the lower limbs, with a prevalence of 0.1% annually, more common in elderly men. Open appendectomy, a low-risk procedure for acute appendicitis, typically lasts 30–60 minutes under general anesthesia. Post-surgery, DVT is rare but has been observed in a unique case following an uncomplicated appendectomy, highlighting the need for increased postoperative monitoring for thrombotic complications in low-complexity surgeries.Case presentationA 26-year-old Arab Iraqi male, BMI 22 kg/m2, presented with 1-day asymmetrical bilateral lower limb swelling and pain, more severe in the right leg, following an uncomplicated appendectomy. Introduction

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a health concern where blood clots develop in the deep veins of the lower limbs, including the calf, femoral, and popliteal veins, as well as the pelvic veins. Rarely, it may also be seen in the upper limbs and cerebral veins [1, 2]. DVT’s major concern is the potential to cause pulmonary embolism (PE), an event that occurs when an embolus breaks free and dislodged into the blood vessels of the lungs [2, 3]. The prevalence of DVT is around 0.1% each year, mostly occurring among elderly people, and slightly more among men [4, 5].

Open appendectomy is a traditional surgical intervention aimed at removing an inflamed or infected appendix, usually performed in cases of acute appendicitis due to its relatively lower complexity [6]. The procedure is done under general anesthesia, where an incision of about two to four inches is made on the right side of the abdomen at or near McBurney’s point. The appendix is then located and removed through this incision. Looking at it from an open perspective, open appendectomy is a procedure with low risk, short operative time of approximately 30–60 minutes, and average recovery within 1–2 weeks post-surgery [7, 8]. Serious side effects, including deep vein thrombosis are rare, although wound infection, intra-abdominal abscess, or bleeding are more common [9, 10].

Following an uncomplicated open appendectomy, the development of extensive bilateral lower limb DVT is an uncommon finding, to the best of our knowledge, not reported before in medical literature. Through examination of this singular case, we hope to shed light on thromboembolic risks that have been understudied within low-complexity surgical procedures. The lack of similar cases warrants attention, along with the insights our case may offer surgeons, anesthesiologists, and intensivists in the importance of postoperative monitoring and surveillance for atypical thrombotic complications during seemingly noncomplex surgical procedures.

Case presentation

History

A 26-year-old Arab Iraqi male, with a body mass index (BMI) of 22 kg/m2, not known to be diabetic, with a 6-pack-year history of smoking, presented to our emergency department with a 1-day history of asymmetrical bilateral lower limb swelling extending from thighs to calves, associated with a dull, constant pain. The swelling and pain were more pronounced in the right leg.

Five days prior to presentation, the patient presented to the emergency department with acute appendicitis and underwent an uncomplicated open appendectomy under general anesthesia. The procedure lasted 45 minutes with no intraoperative complications. He was discharged after 1 day, after an uneventful recovery, independent ambulation, and adherence to routine postoperative care. The patient resumed regular activity within 48 hours after discharge. There was no history of prolonged immobilization, fever, wound discharge, or abdominal pain.

The patient had no prior history of previous venous thromboembolic events, malignancy, autoimmune conditions, or inherited coagulopathies. There was no family history of deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), inherited coagulopathies, bleeding disorders, stroke, or autoimmune diseases. The rest of the systematic review showed no clinical features of PE: dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, or syncope.

Physical examination

The patient was conscious, alert, and oriented to time, place, and person. Vitals were stable: blood pressure 115/70 mmHg, pulse 82 beats per minute (bpm), respiratory rate (RR) of 16 bpm, and a temperature of 37 °C. 100% saturation on room air, and the capillary refill time was 2 seconds.

Examination of the lower limb showed bilateral asymmetrical swelling. The right leg circumference was 38 cm at the mid-calf, 3 cm larger than the left leg (35 cm). The thighs and calves were warm and erythematous (livid discoloration) bilaterally; this was more prominent on the right side. There was pitting edema on both lower limbs, spanning the area from the thighs to the ankles. The limbs were tender on palpation, especially in the muscles of the calves and thighs. Pratt’s sign, Homan’s sign, and Moses’ sign were positive bilaterally. The dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses were palpable and symmetric. There were no ulcers or muscle wasting.

Cardiopulmonary examination revealed normal heart sounds with no murmurs, rubs, or loud P2. Jugular venous pressure (JVP) was normal. Both lung fields were clear bilaterally, with no crackles, pleural rub, or reduced breathing sound. Abdominal examination showed a well-healed scar in the right iliac fossa (Fig. 1). There was no tenderness, abdominal distension, or organomegaly. Neurological examination showed intact sensorimotor function in the lower limbs bilaterally.

Investigations

Laboratory investigations

Biochemical analysis revealed mild hyponatremia (sodium: 133 mmol/L; reference: 135–145 mmol/L), with potassium (4.1 mmol/L), chloride (98 mmol/L), and calcium (2.3 mmol/L) within normal ranges. Hematological evaluation demonstrated mild normocytic anemia (hemoglobin: 10.9 g/dL; hematocrit: 30.2%) and neutrophilic leukocytosis (neutrophils: 83.3%; lymphocytes: 9.3%), with a normal platelet count (247 × 103/µL). Urinalysis showed trace red blood cells (4–6/high-power field [HPF]), pus cells (1–3/HPF), few epithelial cells, and amorphous urate crystals. Inflammatory markers were markedly elevated (C-reactive protein [CRP]: 24.88 mg/L; reference: < 5 mg/L; erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR]: 138 mm/hour).

Coagulation studies showed an elevated D-dimer level (4.0 mg/L; reference: 0.0–0.5 mg/L), and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were negative (pANCA: 4.6 U/mL, cANCA: 3.1 U/mL). Thrombophilia screening revealed normal protein C (78%; reference: 70–140%) and protein S (80%; reference: 60–130%) activity, excluding hereditary deficiencies.

Imaging studies

Lower limb venous Doppler ultrasound

Bilateral lower limb venous Doppler studies showed extensive acute deep vein thrombosis (DVT). On the right side, the external iliac vein (EIV), common femoral vein (CFV), and superficial femoral vein (SFV) were non-compressible and filled with hypoechoic thrombus, with absent flow on color Doppler (CDS), and loss of compressibility. The popliteal vein (POPV) showed sluggish flow with minimal patency (Fig. 2). On the left side, the CFV and SFV had similar thrombotic involvement, though less extensive, with preserved patency but non-compressibility of the POPV. Bilaterally, there was a diffuse subcutaneous soft tissue edema, echogenic fat stranding, and reactive inflammatory lymphadenopathy adjacent to thrombosed segments. The great saphenous veins (GSVs) and small saphenous veins (SSVs) were patent but non-compressible, with minimal flow. No sapheno-femoral reflux was observed.

This ultrasound image shows a tubular, non-compressible, hypoechoic structure in the soft tissue, which is highly suggestive of deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

Abdominal ultrasound

Evaluation of the appendectomy site identified a 73 × 11 mm anechoic subcutaneous collection lateral to the surgical scar, consistent with a seroma (Fig. 3). Surrounding echogenic fat suggested early inflammation, though no intra-abdominal organ pathology (liver, gallbladder, pancreas, kidneys, spleen, bladder) was detected.

Arterial assessment

Peripheral arterial systems were normal bilaterally, with intact dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses, ruling out arterial compromise or compartment syndrome.

Treatment

Management involved a multidisciplinary team focused on thrombus stabilization, symptomatic relief, and preventing complications. The patient was admitted with complete bed rest and limb immobilization. The lower limbs were raised above heart level to counteract the edema and improve venous return. Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was started; 1 mg/kg every 12 hours. The patient also received 1 g of paracetamol orally every eight hrs. Prophylactic intravenous ceftriaxone (1 g per day) was given empirically to prevent infection due to concerns regarding the ultrasound findings (a subcutaneous seroma) and inflammatory changes around the scar.

The patient was discharged on direct oral anticoagulants (DOAC); rivaroxaban was started at a dose of 15 mg two times a day for 3 weeks, which was then adjusted to 20 mg once a day, until the patient completed a total duration of 6 months of anticoagulation therapy, as per the current guidelines for provoked venous thromboembolism. Adjunctive therapy included daily clopidogrel (75 mg), which is an institutional protocol for unprovoked DVT, despite limited evidence supporting its use in isolated cases. Daflon (diosmin/hesperidin, 500 mg twice daily) was added to enhance venous microcirculation, and bilateral Class II (30–40 mmHg) graduated compression stockings were prescribed to reduce the risk of post-thrombotic syndrome.

Outcome and follow-up

During follow-up, the patient was regularly assessed for hemorrhagic complications or thrombotic recurrence. The Doppler ultrasound was repeated four 6 seconds after discharge, demonstrating a stable thrombus without extension. Thrombophilia screening was repeated three months after anticoagulation cessation, and confirmed no underlying hereditary condition.

Discussion

This is an unusual case of postoperative DVT as it involves a patient with almost none of the classic risk factors and following a relatively minor surgery. Postoperative VTEs are uncommon after appendectomies compared to other abdominal surgeries [11]. Yet this young, healthy male patient with a normal BMI and no prolonged history of immobilization or hospitalization developed an extensive bilateral DVT.

The patient’s lack of inherited thrombophilia, malignancy, or prolonged immobilization suggests alternative contributors. Inflammation due to acute appendicitis can result in a hypercoagulable state; in this patient, this is supported by the elevated inflammatory markers (CRP: 24.88 mg/L, ESR: 138 mm/hour) and the elevated D-dimer (4.0 mg/L). Inflammation is hypothesized to lead to a hypercoagulable state by causing endothelial cell dysfunction, activating platelets, and promoting thrombin generation and fibrin deposition [12, 13]. Proinflammatory cytokines contribute through several mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, increased platelet activation, stimulation of the coagulation cascade, reduced activity of natural anticoagulants, and the inhibition of fibrinolysis [13].

In this patient, mild hyponatremia (sodium 133 mmol/L) likely reflects a transient, postoperative, stress-related disturbance in water and sodium balance rather than a primary electrolyte disorder. Surgical stress and systemic inflammation from acute appendicitis can trigger antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion, leading to water retention and dilutional hyponatremia. Additionally, perioperative administration of intravenous fluids, even in small volumes, may have contributed to mild dilutional effects [14, 15].

Mild normocytic anemia (hemoglobin 10.9 g/dL) observed postoperatively is consistent with hemodilution and minor intraoperative blood loss, which is typical even in uncomplicated procedures [16, 17]. While the acute-phase response may transiently lower circulating hemoglobin, pro-inflammatory cytokines released during and after surgery can impair erythropoietin signaling and iron metabolism, leading to reduced red blood cell production and the persistence of anemia despite normal iron stores [18].

Urinalysis showed trace hematuria with 4–6 (RBCs)/HPF, minimal pyuria (1–3 WBCs/HPF), few epithelial cells, and the presence of amorphous urate crystals. Reference ranges are red blood cells (RBCs) ≤ 3/HPF, white blood cells (WBCs) 0–4/HPF, and squamous epithelial cells ≤ 15–20/HPF [19]. The mild elevation in RBCs suggests transient, low-grade hematuria, which may be related to minor irritation of the urinary tract. All other findings are within normal limits, indicating the absence of an active urinary tract infection.

Additionally, the postoperative seroma adjacent to the surgical site may have exacerbated the localized inflammation and increased intra-abdominal pressure, which might affect venous return. One of the mechanisms by which laparoscopic techniques may cause venous thromboembolism (VTE) is due to increased intra-abdominal pressure, which reduces venous return and promotes stagnation of blood in the venous system [20, 21]. Yet this case involved open surgery, implying that increased intra-abdominal pressure, tissue trauma, and systemic inflammation may independently contribute to thrombotic risk.

The patient’s smoking history (6 pack-years) likely played some synergistic role with the above toward thrombus formation. Smoking can induce endothelial dysfunction and enhance platelet aggregation, which raises the perioperative thrombotic risk [22]. This demonstrates the need for preoperative counseling about smoking cessation.

Most cases of post-appendectomy VTE involve unilateral DVT or PE in patients with classic risk factors such as obesity, delayed mobilization, or undiagnosed thrombophilia [23, 24]. Bilateral DVT, however, is quite rare and typically associated with systemic hypercoagulability, like malignant tumors [25]. This case is unique because it occurred in a low-risk demographic, suggesting that the inflammation and smoking may together significantly increase the associated risk. Furthermore, the absence of intraoperative complications or prolonged hospitalization shows the importance of not dismissing the potential of VTE even in uncomplicated cases.

Current guidelines for VTE prophylaxis after an appendectomy in low-risk patients are ambiguous, reflecting the perceived low baseline risk. This case shows the need for a more individualized approach. Preoperative assessment should also include modifiable risk factors such as smoking and inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP and D-dimer to identify patients at increased risk. Routine pharmacologic prophylaxis is probably not warranted, but more research is needed to establish the need for selective use in patients with elevated inflammatory markers or a history of smoking.

The multidisciplinary approach, combining anticoagulation (LMWH/DOACs), compression therapy, and adjunctive agents, aligns with guidelines for provoked VTE [26]. However, the addition of clopidogrel lacks robust evidence in isolated DVT and reflects institutional protocols rather than standardized practice. Conversely, Daflon’s use to improve microcirculatory function, though experimental, highlights the need for adjunctive therapies to mitigate post-thrombotic syndrome.

As a single case report, generalizability is limited. Transient thrombophilia or undiagnosed genetic mutations cannot be entirely excluded, though repeated testing was negative. Prospective studies are needed to elucidate the interplay between appendicitis-driven inflammation, surgical trauma, and VTE risk. Biomarker-driven protocols (for example, D-dimer-guided prophylaxis) could refine risk stratification in low-risk surgeries.

Learning points

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) can occur unexpectedly in young, low-risk patients after minor surgery.

Lifestyle factors such as smoking should not be underestimated in preoperative risk assessment.

This case challenges the assumptions that low-risk patients do not require thromboprophylaxis and raises important concerns about the potential limitations of current thromboprophylaxis protocols.

Conclusion

This case of a young, healthy male with few risk factors developing extensive bilateral lower limb deep vein thrombosis (DVT) after an uncomplicated open appendectomy is perplexing. It raises questions about thromboembolic risks associated with low-complexity surgeries. His smoking history, the inflammatory markers, and the postoperative seroma most likely induced a hypercoagulable state, which led to these atypical consequences. The absence of clearly defined guidelines for the provision of venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis in low-risk patients emphasizes the need for tailored multidisciplinary approaches, combining inflammatory stratification using CRP, D-dimer, and modifiable risks such as smoking. This illustrates the need for thorough surveillance for abnormal, frequently overlooked thrombotic events after surgery, regardless of the perceived complexity of the procedure, and prompts the need for further prospective research focused on the preemptive management of these critical yet infrequent incidents.
