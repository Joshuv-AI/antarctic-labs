 SUMMARY
This milestone report documents the development and demonstration of an integrated reactor cost-analysis
workflow linking the Algorithm for Capital Cost Estimation of Reactor Technologies (ACCERT),
International Adjustment Tool (IAT), Cost Reduction Tool (CRT), and Nuclear Energy Cost (NE-COST).
The goal is not simply to compare international construction costs, but to evaluate whether the integrated
ACCERT workflow can explain reactor deployment-cost differences using international cost data and apply
the same framework to tentative U.S. small modular reactor (SMR) cost estimates.
The workflow separates cost differences into country-specific localization effects, first-of-a-kind (FOAK)
to nth-of-a-kind (NOAK) deployment-learning effects, and levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) impacts.
ACCERT provides a U.S. AP1000 well-executed first-of-a-kind (WE-FOAK) overnight capital cost (OCC)
with 2024 USD. IAT adjusts this estimate for country-specific labor, material, equipment, productivity, and
purchasing-power conditions. CRT then estimates cost reductions associated with design completion,
repeat builds, construction proficiency, standardization, and supply-chain learning. NE-COST translates
the resulting capital-cost cases into LCOE estimates.
Starting from an ACCERT AP1000 well-executed first-of-a-kind (WE-FOAK) estimate of approximately
7,200 $/kWe, application of IAT reduced the localized China estimate to approximately 5,000 $/kWe.
Application of CRT further reduced the mature-deployment estimate to approximately 3,000 $/kWe,
compared with a benchmark endpoint of approximately 2,500 $/kWe reconstructed from publicly available
AP1000/CAP1000 deployment data. This decomposition indicates that IAT explains about 47.0% of the
U.S.–China OCC gap, CRT explains about 42.7%, and about 10.3% remains unexplained. The remaining
gap likely reflects construction duration, financing structure, owner-cost treatment, procurement
coordination, project-delivery differences, and economic organization differences not yet fully represented
in the workflow.
The resulting OCC estimates were propagated through NE-COST to evaluate economic impacts. The
analysis demonstrates that reductions in construction cost translate directly into lower projected electricity-
generation costs and provides a consistent framework for evaluating the economic significance of
localization and deployment-learning effects.
The main contribution of this report is the integrated ACCERT workflow itself. It provides a transparent
method for decomposing international reactor cost differences into localization effects, FOAK-to-NOAK
learning effects, and LCOE implications. This capability supports more defensible comparison of
international nuclear construction experience and provides a foundation for evaluating which cost-reduction
mechanisms may be transferable to future U.S. reactor deployment, including future SMR applications.

1. Introduction

1.1 Background and Motivation
International nuclear construction cost outcomes vary widely across countries, reactor designs, and
deployment programs. Observed differences are often attributed to technology choice alone, but the
evidence indicates that lower international costs also reflect localization conditions, deployment learning,
construction duration, financing structures, regulatory stability, and the maturity of domestic supply
chains.
This distinction matters for advanced reactor cost assessment. A cost estimate developed for one country
cannot be transferred directly to another country without accounting for labor productivity, material and
equipment price environments, local construction practice, and the degree to which a deployment
program has already accumulated learning. Similarly, a low observed cost from a mature international
program should not be interpreted as an immediately transferable U.S. cost target unless the underlying
deployment conditions can also be reproduced.
Capital costs for new nuclear power plants with the same reactor design have varied between countries, as
illustrated by the AP1000 in U.S. vs. in China, the EPR in France and Finland vs. in China, and the APR-
1400 in the United Arab Emirates vs. in Korea, and labor costs alone are not enough to explain these
differences.
A connected techno-economic assessment framework is therefore needed to separate the WE-FOAK OCC
from the effects of location, learning, schedule, and full plant economics. The integrated workflow
described in this report connects several tools used or developed within the Systems Analysis and
Integration (SA&I) Campaign. ACCERT provides the reactor cost-account basis for overnight capital cost
(OCC) estimation [1], the Cost Reduction Tool (CRT) represents deployment learning and cost reduction
across build sequences [2], and NE-COST translates capital-cost and operating-cost assumptions into
levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) outcomes [3] [4]. The International Adjustment Tool (IAT) was
initially developed in support of Net Zero World (NZW) international cost-comparison activities and is
incorporated here as the localization module for country-specific labor, material, equipment, and other-
cost adjustment factors [5]. Moving forward, IAT development will be coordinated within SA&I so that it
can support the broader integrated ACCERT workflow.

1.2 Problem Statements
Cost comparisons for nuclear projects are frequently inconsistent between evaluators because they mix
unlike cost bases and do not clearly separate country-specific cost and economic conditions from
deployment-learning effects. This creates high-impact questions for U.S. nuclear deployment: what is the
reasonable OCC of a well-executed FOAK nuclear plant in the United States; why have nuclear power
plants been built at substantially lower cost in China and some other international programs, while recent

U.S. experience (Vogtle 3&4) has been much more expensive; what specific mechanisms could the
United States use to reduce future deployment costs; and how low U.S. costs can reasonably fall under
improved but realistic deployment conditions. Answering these questions requires a framework that can
distinguish localization effects, FOAK-to-NOAK learning, construction-duration effects, financing and
owner-cost treatment, and LCOE impacts.

The fitted polynomial for France exhibits a moderate R², whereas the pre-TMI U.S. and China fits are

Overall, construction duration clearly contributes to time-dependent costs, but duration alone does not

cost driver, but also as a proxy for deeper systemic factors such as rework, regulatory uncertainty, and
project execution efficiency.
Without an integrated framework, these effects can be incorrectly combined into a single adjustment
factor. For example, China, Korea, or UAE cost advantages may reflect both local factor prices and
deployment learning. Treating those advantages as either entirely country-specific or entirely transferable
learning would lead to misleading conclusions for U.S. deployment planning.

1.3 Objective of This Study
The purpose of this report is to document and exercise an integrated ACCERT workflow for reactor
techno-economic assessment. The workflow links ACCERT, IAT, CRT, and NE-COST to evaluate how a
U.S. WE-FOAK capital-cost estimate changes when international localization effects, deployment-
learning effects, and LCOE impacts are represented explicitly. The AP1000/CAP1000 China case is used
as the primary demonstration because it provides sufficient international cost data to test the full sequence
of localization, deployment learning, benchmark comparison, and LCOE evaluation.
Specifically, this study aims to:
 • update the ACCERT AP1000 capital-cost basis and support API-based tool integration;
 • apply IAT to estimate country-specific localization effects for China, South Korea, and the UAE;
 • apply CRT to represent AP1000 deployment-learning effects using the China case and U.S. fast-
 learning diagnostic case;
 • use NE-COST to translate capital-cost and O&M assumptions into LCOE outcomes;
 • identify which portions of international cost differences may be transferable to future U.S.
 deployment and which are country-specific.

1.4 Scope and Structure of the Report
This report focuses on the development and demonstration of the integrated ACCERT workflow for
reactor cost assessment. The main analytical case is the AP1000/CAP1000 China comparison because it
supports the full sequence of ACCERT OCC estimation, IAT localization, CRT deployment-learning
analysis, benchmark comparison, and NE-COST LCOE evaluation. South Korea and the United Arab
Emirates are included only as supporting IAT localization plausibility checks.
The report is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the integrated ACCERT workflow and defines
the cost metrics used in the analysis. Section 3 documents methodology updates for ACCERT, IAT, CRT,
and NE-COST. Section 4 presents the AP1000/CAP1000 case study and transferability discussion.
Section 5 summarizes conclusions, limitations, and future work. Detailed software, input, and cost-
account information are provided in the appendices.

2. Integrated Techno-Economic Assessment Framework

2.1 Overall Workflow
The integrated ACCERT workflow links ACCERT, IAT, CRT, and NE-COST to evaluate reactor cost
outcomes across capital-cost estimation, international localization, deployment learning, and LCOE
evaluation. ACCERT provides the OCC cost-account basis for the reference plant configuration. IAT
adjusts the ACCERT estimate for country-specific labor, material, equipment, productivity, and other-
cost conditions. CRT represents cost evolution across a deployment sequence, including repeat-build
learning, cross-site standardization, transfer efficiency, and proficiency gains. NE-COST then translates
the resulting capital-cost and operating-cost assumptions into LCOE.
Multi-unit deployment is represented mainly through CRT learning and standardization effects rather than
an explicit ACCERT shared-infrastructure model. ACCERT provides the reference OCC cost basis, while
CRT captures repeat-build learning, cross-site standardization, transfer efficiency, and proficiency gains.
Shared infrastructure is included only if it is already embedded in the ACCERT input basis or added
through scenario assumptions

The architecture keeps static localization effects separate from dynamic deployment effects. This
distinction is central to the report: differences in local factor prices should not be treated as equivalent to
learning from repeated deployment.

2.2 Cost Metrics and Definitions
The primary capital metric used in this report is overnight capital cost (OCC), expressed in $/kWe with
2024 USD in this report. OCC excludes construction-period financing and therefore provides a useful
basis for comparing engineering and construction cost structures. Total capital investment (TCI) includes
additional owner, financing, escalation, and project-related costs depending on the accounting convention
used.
ACCERT represents a U.S.-based reactor construction cost basis. In this report, the ACCERT AP1000
result is interpreted as an average U.S. construction-cost reference for a well-executed first-of-a-kind
(WE-FOAK) case, rather than a historically delayed first unit, nor the fully learned NOAK endpoint. IAT
adjusts this WE-FOAK baseline to represent international or localized conditions. CRT then modifies cost
and, where applicable, schedule behavior across a deployment sequence from FOAK through NOAK.
NE-COST evaluates the LCOE consequences of the adjusted capital cost together with O&M, fuel-cycle,
financing, and uncertainty assumptions.

2.3 Demonstration Cases and Analytical Role
The AP1000/CAP1000 China comparison is used as the primary end-to-end demonstration case because
it has sufficient public cost basis and benchmark information to exercise the full ACCERT, IAT, CRT,
and NE-COST workflow. China provides the strongest case in this report for connecting localization
effects, deployment learning, benchmark OCC comparison, and LCOE evaluation.
South Korea and the UAE are included in more limited supporting roles. They do not operate AP1000
units, but they provide relevant experience in large light-water reactor deployment through
APR1000/APR1400 projects, including the four APR1400 units at Barakah [7] [8]. These cases are
therefore used as plausibility checks for LWR localization in IAT rather than as AP1000-specific
validation cases.
Beyond AP1000 projects, cost data for subsequent units of the same design (enabling comparison of first-
of-a-kind, second-of-a-kind, etc.) within the same country are less clear, especially when our view is
limited to NRC-certified designs built in the past 10 years. One instance of costs published for subsequent
units was found for the APR-1400 design: Sauel 1 and 2 (formerly known as Shin Kori 3 and 4) were
under construction in Korea when construction began on another pair of units, Shin Hanul 1 and 2.
Projected costs on an installed capacity basis were nominally 25% higher for the latter plant according to

3. Methodology Updates: ACCERT, IAT, CRT, and NE-COST
This section summarizes the four components of the integrated ACCERT workflow and highlights the
principal FY26 enhancements completed for this milestone. All tools are implemented as complementary
modules that support reactor capital-cost estimation, international localization, deployment-learning
analysis, and LCOE evaluation within the open-source ACCERT framework.

3.1 ACCERT Capital Cost Estimation
ACCERT is the baseline OCC estimation engine in the integrated workflow. It uses an algorithm-based
cost estimation structure organized around reactor technology, plant systems, and standardized cost
accounts [15]. The main update in this activity is the addition of an AP1000 cost basis and the creation of
API-enabled communication with downstream tools [16]. Appendix A provides the supporting technical
description of the FY26 ACCERT software enhancements, AP1000 model development, data-exchange
interfaces, and verification activities. ACCERT was also updated to support API-based communication
with IAT, CRT, and NE-COST, making the AP1000 demonstration the first fully connected
implementation of this workflow.
The AP1000 implementation provides the cost-account detail needed to demonstrate an end-to-end
workflow with a real large light-water reactor reference case. The AP1000 cost basis and benchmarking
context also draw on prior public reactor cost-estimation work, including TIMCAT and the capital-cost
estimation methodology described by Stewart and Shirvan [17] [18, 19]. Appendix A documents the
ACCERT implementation and workflow-coupling enhancements, while Appendix C provides the
complete AP1000 account structure supporting the aggregate OCC result.

3.1.1 Cost Structure
The AP1000 cost structure separates direct costs, indirect costs, and owner costs. Direct costs include the
nuclear island, turbine island, balance-of-plant systems, site improvements, structures, equipment, and
installation labor represented in the cost-account structure. Indirect costs include construction services,
engineering, project management, temporary facilities, field supervision, and other execution support.
Owner costs include owner project management, licensing support, startup, training, and related project-
development expenditures.

3.1.2 Key Assumptions
The current AP1000 baseline uses direct cost mapped from EEDB and TIMCAT, and escalates the
estimate to 2024 dollars. Indirect costs are represented as approximately 60.9 percent of direct costs, and
owner costs are represented as approximately 20 percent, consistent with the current ACCERT
implementation and available source basis. These values should be interpreted as modeling assumptions
rather than final project-budget estimates.

3.1.3 Interpretation of Results
The resulting ACCERT estimate should be interpreted as a well-executed baseline for AP1000
deployment under the defined cost-account assumptions. It provides the starting point for localization,
deployment-learning, and LCOE calculations. The estimate is not intended to reproduce a specific
historical project outcome, especially where that project includes exceptional schedule delay, supply-
chain disruption, or financing escalation.

Appendix C provides the complete AP1000 cost-account table and a summary of the dominant direct-cost
categories. The main text reports the aggregate OCC basis and interprets the result for downstream IAT,

3.1.4 Limitations
The ACCERT AP1000 result should be interpreted only as a WE-FOAK reference cost basis rather than a
project-specific estimate. The current implementation treats AP1000 as a single-unit cost basis before
downstream deployment effects are applied through IAT, CRT, and NE-COST. Some cost accounts rely
on mapping or extrapolation from public and historical sources, so uncertainty remains in aggregated or
incompletely resolved accounts.
Several SMR models have also been developed in ACCERT for first-order cost analysis and to
demonstrate extensibility beyond large light-water reactors. These estimates remain highly uncertain
because public SMR cost-account data, construction sequencing assumptions, modular fabrication costs,
supply-chain maturity, and FOAK-to-NOAK learning evidence are still limited.

3.2 International Adjustments Tool
The International Adjustment Tool (IAT) was initially developed in support of Net Zero World (NZW)
international nuclear cost-comparison activities and is incorporated in this report as the localization
component of the integrated ACCERT workflow [5].

3.2.1 Motivation for Localization
Nuclear construction costs are not transferable across borders at face value because a U.S.-based estimate
embeds assumptions about labor productivity, material and equipment prices, procurement practice, and
regulatory overhead. IAT addresses this by converting a U.S. reference OCC into country-specific cost
environments using transparent adjustment factors.
The International Adjustments Tool (IAT) expresses the OCC of country x as a product of the U.S.
reference cost and dimensionless adjustment factors anchored to public macroeconomic, industrial
sectors, and national accounts and price statistics. It has been initially developed through Net Zero World
initiative [5] .
This structure is intended to keep the IAT transparent. Each factor is tied to a specific cost component and
data source, so changes in the localized estimate can be traced to labor productivity, material prices,
equipment price levels, or other-cost assumptions. The method does not attempt to embed deployment
learning, construction duration, or financing effects; those are treated separately through CRT and NE-
COST.
Labor Costs and Productivity Differentials
Labor cost differences across countries are often summarized through nominal wage comparisons, but
wage levels alone are insufficient for the purpose of cost adjustment. The economic concept that is
relevant here is unit labor cost (ULC): the ratio of total labor compensation to total value added in a given
sector. ULC captures, in a single ratio, both what workers are paid and how much output they generate
per unit of compensation. Two countries with identical nominal wages can have radically different
effective labor costs per unit of produced output if their sectoral productivity levels diverge, which is
precisely the pattern observed in the countries studied here. Korea's OCC labor factor is near parity
because construction-sector advantages are offset by manufacturing-sector disadvantages under the
selected sector weights.

China has lower ULC ratios in both manufacturing and construction, producing a composite OCC labor
factor of 0.785. This indicates a meaningful labor-cost advantage, but one much smaller than a nominal
wage comparison would imply [20] [21].
The UAE represents an imported engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) deployment context
in which low nominal wages do not translate directly into lower productivity-adjusted construction labor
costs.
Supply Chain Maturity and Material Availability
Material costs for nuclear construction are dominated by structural steel and Portland cement. Both
commodities are traded internationally but are also produced locally in most large economies, and their
domestic prices reflect local energy costs, logistics infrastructure, and industrial policy in ways that
diverge substantially from global reference prices. A country with an established, state-supported steel
industry, such as China, will face domestic rebar prices well below Western import parity. A country that
imports most of its steel, as the UAE effectively does for construction-grade products, will face different
price dynamics.
Equipment costs present a different pattern. Major nuclear plant systems, including reactor pressure
vessels, steam generators, turbines, and control instrumentation, are sourced from a limited number of
qualified vendors operating in international markets. Prices for this equipment are therefore more uniform
globally, but not identical: countries with lower capital goods price levels, as measured by the Gross
Fixed Capital Formation (GFCF) price-level index published by the International Comparison Program
[22], will see domestically procured capital goods cheaper to U.S. prices.

3.2.2.2 Limitations and Data Gaps
Several limitations of the current implementation warrant explicit acknowledgment.
Sector weight assumptions. The sector weights applied in the Labor Factor computation (𝑤% = 0.45,
𝑤, = 0.55 for OCC) are calibrated to the EEDB cost account structure and represent engineering
judgment. No independently published source provides nuclear-specific sector labor shares that could be
used to validate or replace these values. The weights have non-trivial effects on the final LFOCC values. A
10-percentage-point reallocation from Construction to Manufacturing (𝑤% = 0.55, 𝑤, = 0.45) would
change the Korea LFOCC from 0.989 to 1.010, crossing the parity threshold and reversing the sign of
Korea's labor cost advantage relative to the U.S.. Under the same reallocation, the UAE LFOCC would
increase from 1.474 to 1.535, and China's LFOCC would decrease modestly from 0.785 to 0.777. The
Korea result is particularly policy-relevant: whether Korean nuclear construction labor is estimated as
cheaper or more expensive than the U.S. baseline depends on an assumption for which no external
validation exists. Sensitivity analysis over the plausible range of weights should therefore be conducted
before using these factors for investment decisions.
Code-of-Account (COA) localization. The adjustment factors in the COA-level IAT factor table are
applied as global multipliers at the top level of the OCC decomposition. The current IAT implementation
does assign cost-component profiles at the COA level (58 accounts), and the aggregate factors reported
here are consistent with those account-level assignments. However, the COA-level localization profiles
themselves, which specify the labor, material, and equipment shares for each account in each country, are
based on engineering judgment and published nuclear construction benchmarks rather than primary cost
data from completed projects in those countries. COA-level international primary data remain the
principal data gap in the methodology.
First-of-a-kind versus nth-of-a-kind considerations. IAT is a localization tool, not a learning or
project-delivery model. It does not represent construction-duration compression, repeat-build proficiency,
state-coordinated procurement, financing terms, or owner-cost differences. The observed cost data
available for validation, particularly for Korea and China, embed learning effects from multi-unit,
sequential deployment programs and therefore represent NOAK costs adjusted by learning by doing
effects. Direct validation against observed costs for these countries is not possible without explicitly
modeling learning curves.
Temporal scope of factor estimates. All adjustment factors are calibrated to 2022 data. Factor values
will change as relative wages, productivity, and commodity prices evolve. In particular, China's
construction-sector ULC has been rising in recent years, consistent with the convergence trajectory

3.3 Cost Reduction Tool (CRT)
3.3.1 Description of CRT
The Cost Reduction Tool (CRT) represents deployment-learning and successive-build improvement
effects within the integrated ACCERT workflow. Whereas the International Adjustments Tool (IAT)
captures country-specific economic conditions such as labor, material, and equipment costs, , CRT
provides a quantitative model that encodes deployment‑related cost‑reduction mechanisms through
explicit equations [37]. These mechanisms, including repeated deployment, workforce experience,
supply chain maturation, standardization, and construction performance improvement, are expressed
through user controlled “levers” that define the deployment scenario and directly influence the underlying
cost calculation models.

July 15, 2026                                                                           17

The purpose of CRT is not to predict future costs through a purely statistical or over-simplified learning
curve. Instead, it provides a structured scenario framework that allows cost-reduction mechanisms to be
quantitatively evaluated by the user, such that decision makers can trace project impacts to each lever
that corresponds to a specific engineering assumption, deployment condition, or process‑improvement
mechanism. These levers include design completion, design maturity, supply-chain proficiency, architect-
engineer (A/E) proficiency, construction proficiency, modular civil construction, cross-site
standardization, cross-site transfer efficiency, commercial balance-of-plant assumptions, non-safety-
related reactor boundary assumptions, orderbook structure, and policy or financing assumptions where
applicable.

For this study, CRT is used to evaluate how deployment-learning effects shape the transition between
WE-FOAK and NOAK cost estimates for the plant of interest, using a Code-of-Accounts-level cost
structure. The CRT works using three main inputs: the WE-FOAK cost estimate, the NOAK cost
estimate, and the lever settings. The WE-FOAK represents a well-executed first plant under realistic first-
unit conditions, rather than a historically delayed or over-budget first unit, and serves as the baseline
starting point. The levers are then applied to the WE-FOAK to produce the expected cost of the actual
first unit, reflecting the specific program conditions at the time of first construction. From there, the
combination of that lever-adjusted first unit cost, the NOAK floor, and the levers together define the cost
trajectory across successive deployments, controlling both the pace of cost reduction and how it is
distributed across the different functions of project execution. The lever descriptions are in the sections
below, while the detailed lever values used in Chapter 4 are provided in Appendix B so that the scenario
assumptions remain traceable to the reported results.

3.3.2 Cost Reduction Levers
The levers available in the current CRT implementation span four functional areas of project execution.
Each lever is set on a defined scale and affects the cost trajectory independently, though interactions
between levers are reflected in the overall output.
Orderbook and Deployment Parameters
These define the scale and structure of the deployment program:
 • Number of firm orders (N): establishes the total length of the deployment sequence under
 consideration.
 • Number of plants to reach NOAK: sets the point at which the cost trajectory is assumed to have
 converged to the NOAK cost floor.
 • Include first plant in orderbook averages (True/False): affects how learning is distributed across
 early units.
Design Levers
These capture the maturity and completeness of the reactor design at the time of first construction:
 • Design Completion (%): reflects the fraction of the detailed design finalized prior to the start of
 construction. Incomplete design at first pour is a well-documented contributor to rework, schedule
 growth, and cost escalation on first-of-a-kind nuclear projects.
 • Design Maturity (0 to 2): captures how well the design has been validated through prior
 construction experience, ranging from a genuinely novel concept to a proven and repeatedly built
 reference design.

18                                                                               July 15, 2026

Standardization and Design Strategy
These levers control the extent to which learning can be captured and carried across successive
deployments:
 • Cross Site Standardization (%): reflects the degree to which the design and construction approach
 is held constant across sites, enabling accumulated experience on one unit to directly inform the
 next.
 • Cross Site Transfer Efficiency (%): reflects what fraction of the learning gains achieved at one site
 can realistically be transferred and applied at a new site, accounting for differences in workforce,
 logistics, and local conditions.
 • Modular Civil Construction (True/False): indicates whether civil construction employs a modular
 approach, which can reduce on-site labor hours and schedule duration.
 • Commercial BOP (True/False): indicates whether the balance of plant uses commercially available,
 non-nuclear-grade components where permissible, reducing procurement cost and lead times.
 • Non-Safety-Related Reactor Boundary (True/False): indicates whether the reactor system boundary
 is defined to minimize the scope of nuclear-grade requirements, potentially reducing cost and
 schedule for systems outside the safety boundary.
Proficiency Levers
These capture the organizational capability of the three primary project execution functions at the time of
the first unit:
 • Supply Chain Proficiency (0 to 2): reflects the extent to which the nuclear-grade supply chain has
 been established, qualified, and integrated into the project delivery structure.
 • Architect/Engineer (A/E) Proficiency (0 to 2): reflects the experience and process maturity of the
 engineering organization responsible for design delivery and construction support.
 • Construction Proficiency (0 to 2): reflects the capability of the construction workforce and
 management organization to execute nuclear-grade field work efficiently.
 • Units to reach NOAK proficiency: Each proficiency lever is paired with a parameter specifying the
 number of units required for that function to reach its NOAK performance level, allowing for
 different learning rates across functions.
Financing
These levers capture the project financing parameters and tax incentives for each plant:
 • Interest Rate (%): accounts for the time value of money in construction-period financing costs when
 computing total capital investment. For OCC comparisons this parameter is typically set to zero.
 • Number of reactors claiming ITC: sets how many units in the deployment sequence are eligible to
 claim the Investment Tax Credit.
 • ITC Amount (%): sets the value of the Investment Tax Credit claimed, directly reducing the
 effective capital cost for eligible units.

3.4 NE-COST Integration for LCOE Evaluation
NE-COST extends the ACCERT-based workflow from overnight capital cost estimation to full levelized
cost of electricity (LCOE) evaluation. In the integrated workflow, NE-COST receives capital-cost outputs
from ACCERT, IAT, and CRT and combines them with O&M, fuel-cycle, financing, plant-performance,
and uncertainty assumptions. This step is necessary because an OCC reduction does not translate directly
into the same proportional LCOE reduction when construction duration, discount rate, capacity factor,
fuel-cycle strategy, or O&M assumptions differ across scenarios.
In this milestone, NE-COST is used primarily as a translation tool from capital-cost scenarios to LCOE
outcomes rather than as a fully country-specific financing model. Financing and schedule effects are
represented only to the extent that they are embedded in the supplied capital-cost and scenario
assumptions. Country-specific discount rates, construction-period financing structures, and schedule-
dependent escalation are not yet modeled consistently across all cases and remain future-work items.

3.4.1 Modeling Capabilities
The NE-COST integration supports Monte Carlo simulation and fuel-cycle modeling, allowing the
workflow to evaluate not only point estimates but also uncertainty ranges around LCOE outcomes. In this
report, uncertainty is applied primarily to capital-cost inputs passed from ACCERT, IAT, and CRT into
NE-COST. The Monte Carlo cases use scenario-specific capital-cost ranges to represent uncertainty
between baseline, localized, and learned deployment-cost conditions. O&M inputs are adjusted using IAT
labor and equipment factors where applicable, while fuel costs are held constant across country cases. The
resulting NE-COST outputs should therefore be interpreted as scenario-based LCOE distributions driven
mainly by capital-cost uncertainty, rather than full probabilistic forecasts of all financing, construction-
duration, fuel-cycle, and operational uncertainties.

3.4.2 Current Status and Limitations
The NE-COST connection within the workflow still requires more work to enable full capabilities. The
AP1000 case demonstrates the intended data flow, but the automation is still under development and has
not yet been generalized to all reactor technologies or all scenario combinations. In addition, financing
and construction-duration effects are not yet represented as fully endogenous cross-tool variables. Future
work will complete the INTEGRATED ACCERT interface, expand the fuel and O&M data basis, and
improve treatment of schedule-dependent financing, owner costs, and construction-duration assumptions.

3.5 Integrated Framework Summary and Validation Need
International nuclear cost differences arise from both localization effects and deployment-learning effects.
The integrated workflow separates these mechanisms so that country-specific factor prices are not
confused with cost reductions from repeated deployment.
IAT represents where the project is built. It adjusts the ACCERT baseline for labor productivity, material
prices, equipment price levels, and other country-specific economic conditions. CRT represents how costs
evolve as a deployment program matures. It captures repeat builds, design completion, standardization,
construction proficiency, supply-chain maturation, modular construction, and transfer of lessons learned
across projects.
This separation is important for transferability analysis. A lower observed cost in China, Korea, or the
UAE may reflect local cost conditions, programmatic learning, financing structure, project-delivery
practice, or some combination of these factors. If all effects are collapsed into one country multiplier, the

model cannot distinguish which cost-reduction mechanisms may be transferable to future U.S.
deployment programs. Figure 3-1 illustrates this conceptual decomposition.

4. AP1000 Case Study Results and Discussion

4.1 AP1000 International Benchmark Context
The AP1000 was selected as the reference reactor for this study because it is one of the few advanced
Generation III+ reactor designs that has been deployed in multiple countries under substantially different
economic, regulatory, and supply-chain conditions. These deployments provide a useful basis for
evaluating how localization effects, deployment learning, and economic assumptions contribute to
observed differences in overnight capital cost (OCC) and levelized cost of electricity (LCOE).
Figure 4-1 shows the integrated workflow, which consists of four stages. First, the OCC of the well-

affect the AP1000 baseline estimate. Third, the Cost Reduction Tool (CRT) is used to represent
deployment-learning effects using the China deployment sequence as the primary calibration case and to
evaluate potential future U.S. deployment scenarios. Finally, the resulting OCC estimates are propagated
through NE-COST to evaluate their impact on LCOE and to assess how much of the observed international
cost difference can be attributed to localization, deployment learning, and residual factors not explicitly
represented within the current framework.

4.2.2 China Deployment Learning Analysis Using CRT
To evaluate the contribution of deployment learning, the Cost Reduction Tool (CRT) was applied to the

deployment sequence. This trend reflects the combined effects of design maturation, workforce
experience, supply-chain development, standardization, and improvements in project execution.
It should be noted that the AP1000-to-CAP1000 transition means this is not a pure, same-design learning
curve. The CRT analysis therefore treats the sequence as deployment-program learning under design
localization rather than replication of an unchanged AP1000 design. Design localization, increasing
domestic content, and deployment experience are all reflected in the observed cost trend and are therefore
treated together within the CRT calibration. Figure 4-2 compares the observed China deployment

allowing lessons learned at Sanmen and Haiyang to be incorporated into later projects rather than

reach NOAK proficiency. The proficiency parameters primarily affect the slope of early learning,

costs across future projects.

range of what should be expected from a domestic plant experience, especially since the CRT was
calibrated and developed using domestic US experience and data. That being said, what the calibrated
lever set reproduces most effectively is the overall rate and direction of cost reduction across the
deployment sequence, rather than the actual cost values.

4.5 Interpretation and Transferability to the U.S.
The China AP1000/CAP1000 deployment program demonstrates that sustained cost reductions are
achievable when learning can accumulate across multiple projects. Several of the mechanisms responsible
for this improvement are potentially transferable to future U.S. deployment programs and the CRT
analysis suggests significant cost reductions if this holds true. These mechanisms include workforce
proficiency, engineering and construction experience, project-management maturity, modular
construction practices, and systematic transfer of lessons learned between projects. Unlike labor rates or

32                                                                               July 15, 2026

macroeconomic conditions, these factors are largely deployment-driven and can improve through
deliberate program execution.
At the same time, not all observed Chinese advantages are directly transferable. China benefited from a
coordinated national deployment strategy, rapid construction schedules, extensive domestic
manufacturing capability, and procurement structures that differ substantially from current U.S. practice
[41]. In addition, portions of the observed cost reduction occurred during the transition from AP1000 to
CAP1000, meaning that design localization and deployment learning evolved simultaneously.
Consequently, the full China deployment trajectory should not be interpreted as a directly achievable
target for future U.S. projects.

the CRT framework. Table 4-5 summarizes the relative applicability of key China deployment levers to

assessed as highly transferable because they can be improved through standardized multi-unit deployment
and organizational learning. Modular construction is considered partially transferable because its
effectiveness depends on project scale, domestic supply-chain capability, and long-term program
continuity.

5. Conclusions and Future Work

5.1 Conclusions
This milestone expanded the Algorithm for the Capital Cost Estimation of Reactor Technologies
(ACCERT) from a standalone reactor capital-cost estimation capability into an integrated techno-
economic assessment workflow capable of evaluating localization effects, deployment learning, and
economic impacts across international deployment environments. The resulting workflow links
ACCERT, the International Adjustment Tool (IAT), the Cost Reduction Tool (CRT), and NE-COST to
provide a consistent methodology connecting reactor design, construction cost estimation, international
deployment conditions, deployment learning, and levelized cost of electricity (LCOE).
The AP1000 was selected as the primary demonstration reactor because it is currently the only reactor
integrated across all components of the workflow and provides publicly available deployment information
from multiple countries. Using the AP1000 case, the workflow was demonstrated through a sequence of
analyses that progressively incorporated localization effects, deployment learning, and economic
performance evaluation.
Under China-specific economic conditions, IAT reduces the ACCERT AP1000 WE-FOAK reference
estimate from approximately 7,200 $/kWe to approximately 5,000 $/kWe. CRT further reduces the
learned-deployment estimate to approximately 3,000 $/kWe, compared with a China benchmark endpoint
of approximately 2,500 $/kWe. These capital-cost estimates are then propagated through NE-COST to
evaluate corresponding electricity-cost impacts.
The results show that international nuclear construction cost differences should not be represented by a
single country multiplier. Under the assumptions used here, localization effects account for approximately
47% of the observed U.S.-China OCC difference, deployment-learning effects account for approximately
43%, and the remaining residual reflects factors not fully represented in the current workflow, including
construction duration, financing structure, owner-cost boundaries, project-delivery practices, procurement
approaches, and uncertainty in public benchmark data.
The international comparison also provides a first-order validation check on the ACCERT AP1000 cost
basis. When the U.S. WE-FOAK reference estimate is adjusted through IAT and CRT, the workflow
reproduces the direction and approximate magnitude of observed international cost trends. This increases
confidence that ACCERT can support future cost-reduction projection studies, while recognizing that the
current analysis is a framework validation and plausibility assessment rather than a formal project-cost
validation.
The main implication for U.S. deployment is that cost reduction depends on coordinated program
execution, not technology selection alone. Repeat builds, stable project delivery, supply-chain readiness,
construction management, and shorter construction duration should be treated as program design
requirements for future nuclear deployment.

5.2 Limitations
Methodological limitations include the use of screening-level localization factors, structured CRT lever
assumptions, and indirect treatment of construction duration and financing. The current workflow does
not endogenously model schedule, owner costs, or project-specific regulatory pathways.

July 15, 2026                                                                           35

Data limitations include heterogeneous public benchmark cost boundaries, limited unit-by-unit
international cost data, engineering judgment in sector and COA-level weighting, and the use of 2022
economic data for IAT factors.

5.3 Implication for Future U.S Deployment
The results suggest that a substantial portion of the observed international cost difference can be
attributed to factors that are potentially transferable to future U.S. deployment programs. While
localization effects are inherently country-specific, many of the deployment-learning mechanisms
represented within the Cost Reduction Tool are linked to deployment strategies that can be influenced in
the United States, including design completion before construction, standardization, modular construction
execution, workforce development, supply-chain readiness, construction management, and transfer of
lessons learned across projects.
This observation suggests that future cost reductions may be achieved not only through technological
innovation, but also through deployment strategies that enable accumulation and transfer of construction
experience across projects. The framework developed in this work provides a structured methodology for
evaluating these opportunities and assessing the relative importance of different deployment strategies
and comparing modeled U.S. cost-reduction pathways against successful international deployment
experience.

5.4 Future work
In FY 2027, the reactor costs estimated using the ACCERT and CRT codes will be utilized to update the
large light water reactors (such as AP1000) of the Cost Basis Report [43] and Annual Technology
Baseline (ATB) Cost [44].
Future work should focus on four areas of recommended improvements. Data improvements include
harmonized benchmark cost boundaries, COA-level international cost data, and better owner-cost and
construction-duration data. Method improvements include endogenous duration treatment, IAT sector-
weight sensitivity analysis, benchmark uncertainty ranges, and stronger coupling among schedule,
financing, owner costs, and LCOE. Reactor-scope improvements include extending the integrated
workflow to SMR and advanced-reactor models, while recognizing that these applications will require
improved cost-account data, modular construction assumptions, supply-chain inputs, and FOAK-to-
NOAK learning evidence. Software improvements include generalized API coupling, automated scenario-
file management, and reproducibility packages for integrated ACCERT workflows.
Future software development will focus on expanding API functionality, improving automation, and
supporting fully integrated techno-economic workflows. In the main text, the API work should be
described as an enabling capability that allows ACCERT outputs to be transferred more consistently to
IAT, CRT, and NE-COST. Appendix A should provide the supporting technical detail, including the
current implementation status, workflow demonstration, and remaining development needs.

developments expanded ACCERT from a standalone reactor capital-cost estimation tool into an

During FY26, ACCERT was updated to support integrated techno-economic assessments through improved
data management, account-level cost handling, and API-based communication with external modules.
Major software enhancements included:
 • Development of the AP1000 reactor model and associated cost-account database.
 • Standardization of account-level cost outputs for downstream analysis.
 • Implementation of API interfaces supporting communication with IAT, CRT, and NE-COST.
 • Improved workflow automation and data exchange between analysis modules.
 • Additional verification testing to support integrated workflow execution.

integrated workflow.

The AP1000 model was developed as the first large light-water reactor implementation within the integrated

analyses. The implementation supports:
 • Detailed direct-cost account representation.
 • Integration with IAT for account-level localization.
 • Integration with CRT for deployment-learning analysis.
 • Integration with NE-COST for economic evaluation.
