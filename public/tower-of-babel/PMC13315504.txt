Computational identification of Ginkgo biloba phytochemicals as dual inhibitors of herpes simplex virus type 1 glycoproteins gB and gD targeting its entry and Alzheimer's disease pathogenesis
Authors: Md Mainuddin Hossain, Sabrina Shahrin Shammi, Golam Sarwar, Sabbir Ahmed, Jannatul Fardous, Md Mahbub Morshed, Mohammed Alamgir Sarker, Mohammad Asaduzzaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) causes severe neurological complications such as herpes simplex encephalitis and Alzheimer's disease (AD). Its entry is mediated by glycoproteins gD and gB, which regulate receptor binding and membrane fusion. Targeting these entry glycoproteins represents a promising therapeutic strategy to limit HSV-1 infection and its contribution to AD pathogenesis. Therefore, this study aimed to identify natural dual inhibitors of HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD from Ginkgo biloba using an integrated computational approach involving molecular docking, pharmacokinetic, toxicity, and bioactivity profiling, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM-GBSA binding free energy analyses to evaluate binding stability and therapeutic potential. A total of 39 phytochemicals were retrieved from IMPPAT 2.0 and PubChem databases and docked against HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. Pharmacokinetics and toxicity were assessed using admetSAR 3.0, SwissADME, Deep-PK, and pKCSM. Molecular docking revealed several phytochemicals with strong binding affinities to HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD, compared to docosanol. Among these, β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A exhibited favorable drug-likeness, acceptable oral bioavailability, no mutagenic and hepatotoxic effects, and biologically active. Molecular dynamic simulation confirmed their greatest binding stability compared to docosanol. MM-GBSA binding free energy analysis further validated their strong and stable interactions within the active sites of HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. These findings identify β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A as promising dual-target inhibitors of HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD in context of HSV-1-associated AD pathogenesis. However, experimental validation is required to confirm their antiviral efficacy and potential relevance to AD.Keywords: Herpes simplex virus type 1, Alzheimer's disease, Glycoproteins gB and gD, Ginkgo biloba, Dual-target inhibitors, Drug-like properties

Introduction

Acute viral infections and chronic neurodegenerative disorders remain major contributors to global morbidity and mortality, posing a sustained challenge to public health worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.1 Among ubiquitous human neurotropic viruses, Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) has gained increasing attention due to its extremely high global prevalence, lifelong persistence, and emerging association with neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's disease (AD).2, 3 HSV-1 is an enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the genus Simplexvirus within the family Herpesviridae.4 Primary infection usually occurs early in life through oral contact, after which the virus establishes latency in sensory and autonomic neurons, with periodic reactivation throughout the host's lifetime.5

While HSV-1 infection is often asymptomatic or limited to mild orolabial lesions, it can progress to severe neurological complications such as herpes simplex encephalitis, which is associated with high mortality and long-term cognitive impairment despite antiviral therapy.6, 7 Beyond acute disease, accumulating epidemiological, molecular, and experimental evidence suggests that recurrent HSV-1 reactivation in the central nervous system may contribute to AD pathogenesis by promoting amyloid-β deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, chronic neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and synaptic dysfunction.8, 9, 10 Notably, HSV-1 DNA has been detected in the brains of elderly individuals, particularly within regions affected by AD pathology, and antiviral treatment has been associated with a reduced risk of dementia in infected populations.11, 12 Given its neurotropic nature, capacity for lifelong persistence, and potential to exacerbate AD-related neuropathological processes, HSV-1 has emerged as a compelling infectious risk factor in AD research. Understanding HSV-1–host interactions and identifying effective strategies to limit viral entry, replication, and reactivation may therefore have important implications not only for controlling HSV-1 infection but also for mitigating viral contributions to AD development and progression.

A critical determinant of HSV-1 infectivity is its envelope glycoproteins gD and gB, which mediate viral attachment and membrane fusion during host cell entry.13, 14, 15 Glycoprotein gD binds neuronal receptors such as nectin-1 and HVEM, triggering conformational changes that activate gB, the primary viral fusogen, enabling fusion of viral and host membranes.16, 17, 18 Both glycoproteins are essential for viral entry and spread, and their inhibition markedly reduces HSV-1 infectivity.19, 20 Targeting gB and gD represents a rational strategy to block early viral entry, limiting neuronal infection and potentially reducing HSV-1–associated contributions to AD pathogenesis.21, 22 Computational approaches such as molecular docking can identify plant derived phytochemicals capable of binding to functional domains of both glycoproteins gB and gD, offering a promising avenue for the development of antivirals with neuroprotective benefits.

Ginkgo biloba (G. biloba), one of the oldest extant tree species, produces standardized extracts (e.g., EGb761) that contain flavonoids, terpenoids, and phenolic compounds with well-documented neuroprotective effects relevant to AD including antioxidant activity, attenuation of amyloid-β-induced toxicity, modulation of neuroinflammation, and enhancement of mitochondrial function.23, 24, 25 These properties support the potential of G. biloba in counteracting key pathological processes associated with neurodegeneration. In addition to neuroprotective effects, G. biloba-derived phytochemicals exhibit antiviral activity against HSV-1. Phenolic compounds such as ginkgolic acid have been shown to inhibit HSV-1 infection by interfering with viral fusion and entry, reducing viral protein synthesis, and suppressing replication in vitro and in vivo, including in acyclovir-resistant strains.26, 27 Phytochemicals constituents (e.g., isorhamnetin and amentoflavone) similarly inhibit early phases of HSV-1 infection, suggesting mechanistic interference with viral attachment or entry processes mediated by HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD.28

Therefore, this study focused on identifying potential therapeutic candidates from G. biloba that could simultaneously inhibit the HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. Phytochemical compounds were obtained from the IMPPAT 2.0 and PubChem databases and subjected to molecular docking analyses to evaluate their binding affinities and interaction patterns with both glycoproteins. Docking, as a structure-based computational method, was employed to predict the preferred binding orientations and strengths of ligand–target interactions. The selected phytochemicals were further evaluated for their pharmacokinetic properties, drug-likeness, and toxicity using admetSAR, SwissADME, pkCSM, and Deep-PK as well as bioactivities of these selected phytochemicals were predicted by Molinspiration tools. To validate the stability and dynamic behavior of the top-ranked protein–ligand complexes, molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the Schrödinger suite. Finally, MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations were conducted to corroborate the binding efficiency and thermodynamic favorability of the interactions.

Materials and methods

Collection of G. biloba-derived phytochemicals (ligands)

Phytochemicals associated with G. biloba were collected from two major databases: the Indian Medicinal Plants, Phytochemistry and Therapeutics database (IMPPAT 2.0) (https://cb.imsc.res.in/imppat/)29 and PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).30 IMPPAT 2.0 was selected as the primary source of phytochemical information because of its curated repository of medicinal plant constituents, while PubChem was used to obtain standardized three-dimensional chemical structures for computational analyses. IMPPAT 2.0 is a manually curated resource compiled through the systematic digitization of data from more than 100 traditional Indian medicine texts, over 7000 peer-reviewed research articles, and additional authoritative sources, making it one of the most comprehensive repositories of phytochemicals derived from Indian medicinal plants. In contrast, PubChem, maintained by the National Institutes of Health (NIH), primarily catalogs small molecules but also encompasses larger chemical entities, including carbohydrates, lipids, peptides, nucleotides, and synthetic macromolecules. Collectively, a total of 39 G. biloba–derived phytochemicals were retrieved from these databases for further analysis (Table S1).

Ligand preparation

Energy minimization of the selected phytochemicals was carried out using SWISS-PdbViewer (version 4.1), a molecular visualization and modeling software developed by the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics (SIB). This tool enables structural optimization and energy minimization of small molecules and ligand conformations prior to downstream computational analyses.31

Target protein retrieval and preprocessing

The three-dimensional crystal structures of the target proteins were obtained from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB),32 a globally recognized repository that archives experimentally determined macromolecular structures, including proteins, DNA, and RNA, to support research and education across the life sciences and biotechnology. The crystal structures of HSV-1 glycoproteins gB (PDB ID: 2GUM; resolution: 2.10 Å) and gD (PDB ID: 1JMA; resolution: 2.65 Å) were retrieved from the RCSB PDB. Prior to downstream analyses, structural preprocessing was conducted using Discovery Studio 4.5, during which all co-crystallized ligands, water molecules, and heteroatoms were removed. The prepared protein structures were subsequently subjected to energy minimization using SWISS-PdbViewer (version 4.1).

Evaluation of binding affinities and interaction patterns using molecular docking

Molecular docking was conducted to identify potential inhibitors by assessing the binding affinities and interaction patterns of G. biloba–derived phytochemicals with HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. Docking simulations and binding energy calculations were performed using the PyRx virtual screening platform integrated with AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.0),33 which predicts optimal ligand–protein binding modes by sampling multiple ligand conformations and orientations within the target binding site.

The predicted ligand-binding pocket of HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD were identified based on the locations of the co-crystallized ligands present in their respective crystal structures. Grid box center coordinates were positioned at the centroid of these experimentally determined binding pockets, while the grid dimensions were adjusted to fully cover the binding cavity and surrounding interacting residues. This strategy ensured that the docking search space encompassed the biologically relevant ligand-binding region. For gB, the grid box dimensions were set to X: 79.6930 Å, Y: 155.7450 Å, and Z: 131.6272 Å, with the grid center positioned at X: 42.3123 Å, Y: 103.7240 Å, and Z: 60.1668 Å. For gD, the grid box dimensions were defined as X: 53.0957 Å, Y: 59.6551 Å, and Z: 42.6670 Å, centered at X: −16.5040 Å, Y: 66.4983 Å, and Z: −1.5304 Å. Among the predicted docking poses, the conformation exhibiting the lowest binding energy was selected as the most energetically favorable and representative ligand–protein complex.

The resulting docked complexes were further examined to analyze binding energies and intermolecular interaction profiles, enabling the identification of promising phytochemical–target associations. To further assess docking reliability, the selected phytochemicals along with the co-crystallized reference ligands—SODIUM ION (NA) for gB and SULFATE ION (SO4) for gD—were re-docked into their respective binding sites using the Mcule 1-Click Docking platform.34 Docking protocol validation was performed by calculating the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the docked poses and their corresponding crystallographic conformations using UCSF Chimera.35

Pharmacokinetics, drug-likeness, and toxicity profiles prediction

The physicochemical characteristics, pharmacokinetic behavior, drug-likeness, and toxicity of the top ten phytochemicals were systematically evaluated using four web-based prediction platforms: ADMET Structure–Activity Relationship 3.0 (admetSAR 3.0) (https://lmmd.ecust.edu.cn/admetsar2),36 SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/),37 Deep-PK (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/deeppk/prediction),38 and pkCSM (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/prediction).39 Canonical Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES) notations of the selected compounds were retrieved from the IMPPAT and PubChem databases and subsequently submitted to these tools for analysis.

admetSAR 3.0 was utilized to predict and assess absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion (ADME) profiles within an integrated computational framework. SwissADME was employed to examine pharmacokinetic parameters, drug-likeness, and medicinal chemistry suitability of the compounds. In addition, Deep-PK, an artificial intelligence–based platform, was used to further evaluate pharmacokinetic properties and drug-likeness of the selected phytochemicals. Lastly, pkCSM was employed to predict pharmacokinetic behavior and toxicity-related parameters based on graph-based modeling of molecular structures.

Prediction of bioactivities of the drug candidates

The bioactivity of the selected phytochemicals was predicted using the online cheminformatics platform “Molinspiration”.40 Several biological activity parameters were predicted using SMILES of our phytochemicals. To evaluate a training set of active structure and compare it to inactive molecules, this tool uses sophisticated Bayesian statistics. The prediction was based on the enzyme inhibition score, which included the G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), an ion channel modulator, a kinase inhibitor, a nuclear receptor ligand, a protease inhibitor, and an enzyme inhibitor. According to previously established guidelines, the results are calculated.41 As a result, it is suggested that if the score is equal to or greater than 0.00, the more active it will be; if the score is between −0.50 and 0.00, the moderately active; and if the score is less than −0.50, the inactive.42, 43

Evaluation of binding stability using molecular dynamics simulations

To evaluate the binding stability of the two selected phytochemicals along with docosanol (control), 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) were conducted on the corresponding protein–ligand complexes. All simulations were performed using the Desmond module implemented in Maestro 2020, employing the OPLS4 force field on a Linux-based computing platform to investigate the dynamic behavior of the complexes.44 The OPLS4 force field was selected because of its improved parameterization for proteins and small molecules and its validated performance in protein–ligand simulations within the Desmond environment. Each system was solvated with the TIP3P water model within an orthorhombic periodic boundary box. Physiological conditions were maintained by setting the temperature to 310 K, ionic strength to 0.15 M NaCl, and pH to 7.0.

Prior to production runs, the solvated systems were subjected to energy minimization to eliminate unfavorable steric interactions and optimize the initial configurations. The systems were then gradually equilibrated and heated to 300 K, with all bonds involving hydrogen atoms constrained using the SHAKE algorithm to ensure numerical stability during integration.45 The application of SHAKE enabled the use of larger integration time steps by restricting high-frequency hydrogen bond vibrations, thereby enhancing computational efficiency while preserving the overall dynamical properties of the systems.46

Post-simulation trajectory analyses were performed to assess structural stability and conformational dynamics by calculating RMSD, root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA).47, 48, 49, 50 Trajectory snapshots were extracted at 100 ps intervals for detailed structural evaluation.

Calculation of binding free energy (MM-GBSA)

The binding free energies (ΔG_bind) of protein–ligand complexes were estimated using the Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) method implemented in the Prime module of the Schrödinger Maestro suite.51 Representative snapshots were extracted from equilibrated molecular dynamics trajectories and used for post-processing energy calculations. The MM/GBSA approach combines molecular mechanics energy terms, including bonded, electrostatic, and van der Waals interactions, with an implicit solvent model (VSGB) to approximate solvation effects. The binding free energy was computed using the single-trajectory scheme according to the equation:

ΔG_bind = E_complex − (E_protein + E_ligand).

where each term represents the total energy of the complex, receptor, and ligand, respectively, including molecular mechanics and solvation contributions. This approach provides a reliable and computationally efficient estimation of relative binding affinities and is widely applied for post-docking refinement and ranking of protein–ligand complexes in structure-based drug design studies. Results

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking analyses were performed to assess the binding potential of the screened phytochemicals against HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD, using docosanol as a reference control. The predicted binding energies of the phytochemicals with HSV-1 glycoprotein gB ranged from −12.3 to −5.8 kcal/mol, whereas docosanol (control) exhibited markedly weaker binding (−4.7 kcal/mol). Similarly, for HSV-1 glycoprotein gD, the binding energies of the phytochemicals ranged from −11.2 to −4.6 kcal/mol, with docosanol (control) again showing reduced affinity (−4.4 kcal/mol). Detailed docking results are presented in Table S2. Docosanol was included as a reference antiviral compound for comparative analysis. Although docosanol is not a validated inhibitor specifically targeting HSV-1 glycoproteins gB or gD, it is an FDA-approved anti-herpetic agent with established clinical efficacy against HSV infections. Given the absence of well-characterized small-molecule inhibitors selectively targeting HSV-1 gB or gD, docosanol was employed as a clinically relevant benchmark to evaluate the relative binding affinity, stability, and interaction profiles of the investigated phytochemicals.

Notably, several phytochemicals demonstrated substantially lower binding energies toward both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD compared with the docosanol (control), suggesting potential dual-target inhibitory activity. Based on binding affinity and interaction profiles, the top ten phytochemicals were selected as β-sitosterol, amentoflavone, bilobetin, 1′-OH-γ-carotene glucoside (carotenoids B–G), ginkgetin, stigmasterol, isoginkgetin, rutin, proanthocyanidin, and ginkgolide A (Table 1). Lower binding energy values are indicative of stronger binding affinities and enhanced stability of ligand–protein interactions.52 Collectively, these findings suggest that the top ten phytochemicals may act as promising dual-target inhibitors of HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD.

To validate the molecular docking protocol, re-docking was performed for the top-ranked phytochemicals selected based on docking scores. The RMSD) between the re-docked and crystallographic ligand conformations was 0 Å, indicating exact pose overlap and confirming that the docking protocol accurately reproduced the experimental binding modes. Re-docking further supported the consistency of binding energies and interaction patterns observed for the top-ranked phytochemicals across both targets. Based on these results, the phytochemicals were selected for subsequent analyses. Overall, the re-docking validation demonstrated the reliability and reproducibility of the docking approach used in this study.

Pharmacokinetics, drug-like properties, and toxicity prediction

The top ten phytochemicals were assessed for their physicochemical characteristics and drug-likeness using lipinski's Rule of Five, including molecular weight, lipophilicity (MLogP), numbers of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, and topological polar surface area (TPSA) (Table 2). Amentoflavone, 1′-hydroxy-γ-carotene glucoside (carotenoids B-G), rutin, and proanthocyanidin exhibited multiple violations of lipinski's criteria (2, 2, 3, and 3 violations, respectively), indicating reduced drug-likeness. In contrast, β-Sitosterol and ginkgolide A fully satisfied all lipinski parameters, suggesting optimal drug-like properties. Bilobetin, ginkgetin, stigmasterol, isoginkgetin, and the docosanol (control) each violated only one lipinski criterion, which is generally considered acceptable and indicative of favorable oral bioavailability. In terms of TPSA, amentoflavone, 1′-hydroxy-γ-carotene glucoside (carotenoids B–G), isoginkgetin, rutin, and proanthocyanidin displayed TPSA values exceeding 140 Å2, a threshold commonly associated with reduced membrane permeability and limited oral bioavailability. Overall, β-sitosterol, bilobetin, ginkgetin, stigmasterol, and ginkgolide A demonstrated favorable physicochemical profiles and drug-likeness, highlighting their potential as promising candidates for further pharmacological and therapeutic investigations.

Furthermore, the selected phytochemicals with favorable physicochemical characteristics and drug-likeness exhibited a moderate predicted oral bioavailability score (0.55), indicating reasonable absorption potential. Central nervous system (CNS) permeability analysis showed that β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, ginkgolide A, and docosanol (control) were predicted to penetrate the blood–brain barrier (BBB), with LogPS values of −1.11, −1.88, −1.26, and − 1.77, respectively, whereas bilobetin and ginkgetin were predicted to be non-penetrant. Consistently, BBB prediction confidence analysis classified bilobetin and ginkgetin as non-penetrant with high confidence, while β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, ginkgolide A, and docosanol (control) were identified as BBB-penetrant with high confidence. Although computational ADME analysis predicted ginkgolide A to be BBB permeable (LogPS = −1.26), this prediction should be interpreted cautiously because the compound possesses several hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor groups that may limit passive diffusion across the blood–brain barrier. As BBB permeability predictions are derived from multivariate computational models, experimental validation will be necessary to confirm the CNS penetration potential of ginkgolide A.

Assessment of cytochrome P450 inhibition revealed that β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A were not predicted to inhibit the major CYP isoforms evaluated, suggesting a lower potential for CYP-mediated drug–drug interactions. In contrast, some phytochemicals, including bilobetin, ginkgetin, stigmasterol, and docosanol (control), were predicted to inhibit specific CYP enzymes, indicating the need for further investigation of their metabolic interaction profiles. Among the evaluated phytochemicals, ginkgetin was predicted to be a P-gp substrate, which may restrict intestinal absorption and tissue distribution. In contrast, β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A were predicted to be non-substrates of P-gp, indicating good intestinal absorption and tissue distribution.

Prediction of bioactivities of the drug candidates

The predicted bioactivity profiles of the selected target phytochemicals, β-Sitosterol and Ginkgolide A, were comparatively evaluated against Docosanol (control) across major pharmacological target classes, including GPCR ligands, ion channel inhibitors, kinase inhibitors, nuclear receptor ligands, protease inhibitors, and general enzyme inhibitors. A bioactivity score > 0 indicates biological activity, whereas −5.0 < score < 0 indicates moderate activity.

β-sitosterol demonstrated superior biological activity compared to the control compound. It showed strong activity as an enzyme inhibitor (1.07) and GPCR ligand (1.05), along with significant ion channel inhibition (0.9), nuclear receptor ligand activity (0.73), kinase inhibition (0.7), and protease inhibition (0.56). All values were positive and markedly higher than those of docosanol (control), indicating robust and broad-spectrum pharmacological potential. Similarly, ginkgolide A exhibited favorable biological activity across all target classes, with prominent GPCR ligand activity (0.85) and enzyme inhibition (0.69). Moderate but positive scores were observed for ion channel inhibition (0.5), nuclear receptor ligand activity (0.51), kinase inhibition (0.2), and protease inhibition (0.12). In comparison, docosanol (control) displayed relatively low bioactivity scores, including weak positive values for ion channel inhibition (0.3), enzyme inhibition (0.33), protease inhibition (0.07), and nuclear receptor ligand activity (0.01), and a negative score for kinase inhibition (−0.5), indicating moderate inactivity in this category (Table 5).

Molecular dynamics simulation study

MDS were performed to evaluate the stability of protein–ligand complexes under conditions that closely mimic the physiological environment.53 This approach enables the assessment of conformational changes within the protein–ligand complexes in a computational setting. To ensure a robust evaluation of stability, two selected protein–ligand complexes, along with the protein–control complex, were subjected to 100 ns MDS to identify the compounds exhibiting the highest structural stability during the analysis.

The MDS revealed distinct stability profiles for HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD complexes with phytochemicals compared to the docosanol (control). For HSV-1 glycoprotein gB, the docosanol (control) complex demonstrated comparatively higher structural deviations throughout the 100 ns simulation, with RMSD values increasing markedly after 70 ns and peaking around 3.0–3.5 Å, indicating reduced conformational stability. Calculation of binding free energy (MM-GBSA)

Ligand binding with the receptor was further confirmed by analysing MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations. The binding free energy (ΔGbind) analysis demonstrated that all phytochemical complexes exhibited substantially stronger interactions with HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD compared to the docosanol (control). For HSV-1 glycoprotein gB, β-sitosterol showed the highest binding affinity with a ΔGbind of −80.77 kcal/mol, followed closely by ginkgolide A (−72.71 kcal/mol), whereas docosanol (control) displayed a significantly lower value of −26.90 kcal/mol. Hydrogen bond contributions (ΔGbind H-bond) were comparable across phytochemicals (ranging from −2.05 to −2.98 kcal/mol) and notably stronger than docosanol (control) (−0.97 kcal/mol). Similarly, for HSV-1 glycoprotein gD, β-sitosterol again exhibited the most favorable binding energy (−71.05 kcal/mol), followed by ginkgolide A (−68.78 kcal/mol), while docosanol (control) remained much weaker at −33.33 kcal/mol. Lipophilic (ΔGbind Lipo) and van der Waals (ΔGbind vdW) interactions dominated the binding contributions for all phytochemicals (Table 6 and Fig. 9), reinforcing their superior stability over the control. These findings indicate that β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A demonstrate significantly stronger and more stable binding affinities with both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD compared to docosanol (control).

Discussion

Based on our comprehensive molecular docking and MDS analyses, β-sitosterol, ginkgolide A demonstrated the highest binding affinity for both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD at their active sites, indicating strong potential for dual inhibition of these HSV-1 glycoproteins. A total of 39 phytochemicals were retrieved from G. biloba for evaluation. Notably, our findings revealed that several phytochemicals exhibited strong binding interactions with both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. Among them, we selected top ten phytochemicals exhibited strong binding interactions with both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD, underscoring their potential as promising dual-target therapeutic agents.

The drug-likeness of these G. biloba-derived top ten phytochemicals was then analyzed using lipinski's rule of 5. In this case, β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A passed 5 of lipinski's rules with no violation, while bilobetin, ginkgetin, stigmasterol, isoginkgetin, and the docosanol (control) each violated only one lipinski criterion, which is generally considered acceptable and indicative of favorable oral bioavailability.54 On the other hand, Amentoflavone, 1′-hydroxy-γ-carotene glucoside (carotenoids B-G), rutin, and proanthocyanidin exhibited multiple violations of lipinski's criteria (2, 2, 3, and 3 violations, respectively), indicating reduced drug-likeness. Molecular weight is a key determinant of a phytochemical's pharmacokinetic behavior, as molecules with a molecular weight below 500 g/mol generally exhibit favorable absorption, distribution, and cellular transport.55 In this study, β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, ginkgolide A, and the docosanol (control) met this criterion, whereas amentoflavone, bilobetin, 1′-hydroxy-γ-carotene glucoside (carotenoids B-G), ginkgetin, isoginkgetin, rutin, and proanthocyanidin did not meet this criterion. In addition, lipophilicity, represented by the MLogP value, is another critical parameter influencing membrane permeability. Phytochemicals with MLogP values below 5 are considered optimal for passive diffusion across the cell membrane, enabling effective interaction with target molecules.56, 57 The predicted MLogP values for most lead phytochemicals were within this acceptable range, except for 1′-hydroxy-γ-carotene glucoside (carotenoids B-G) and docosanol (control). This finding indicates that the remaining nine lead phytochemicals possess suitable lipophilic characteristics for efficient membrane penetration. Moreover, hydrogen bonding capacity also plays an important role in determining drug-likeness, with ideal candidates having no more than 5 hydrogen bond donors and 10 hydrogen bond acceptors.58 In the present analysis, all phytochemicals—except amentoflavone, rutin, and proanthocyanidin—complied with these limits, further supporting their potential as effective drug-like molecules. The TPSA of a drug candidate is generally expected to be ≤140 Å2, as phytochemicals within this range are associated with favorable oral bioavailability and efficient permeability across the intestinal epithelium and the BBB. In contrast, phytochemicals with TPSA values exceeding 140 Å2 are typically characterized by poor absorption.59.59 In the present study, β-sitosterol, bilobetin, ginkgetin, stigmasterol, ginkgolide A, and the docosanol (control), exhibited TPSA values below 140 Å2, suggesting good oral bioavailability. Conversely, amentoflavone, 1′-hydroxy-γ-carotene glucoside (carotenoids B–G), isoginkgetin, rutin, and proanthocyanidin showed TPSA values greater than 140 Å2, indicating a likelihood of poor absorption. Overall, β-sitosterol, bilobetin, ginkgetin, stigmasterol, ginkgolide A, and docosanol (control) demonstrated favorable physicochemical profiles and drug-likeness.

To effectively penetrate the CNS, a phytochemical should exhibit a predicted CNS permeability value greater than −2.00.60 In this study, β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, ginkgolide A, and the docosanol (control) showed CNS permeability values greater than −2, while bilobetin and ginkgetin demonstrated a value less than −2.00. Similarly, β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, ginkgolide A, and the docosanol (control) were predicted to be BBB penetrable with high confidence, whereas bilobetin and ginkgetin were classified as non-permeable to the BBB, also with high confidence. Drug clearance is typically categorized as low (<10 mL/min/kg), moderate (10–50 mL/min/kg), and high (>50 mL/min/kg). β-sitosterol exhibited a clearance value of 10.97 mL/min/kg, indicating moderate clearance, whereas bilobetin, ginkgetin, stigmasterol, ginkgolide A, and docosanol (control) demonstrated clearance values below 10 mL/min/kg, consistent with low clearance. Collectively, β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A emerged as the most promising candidates for their favorable ADME profiles.

Regarding toxicity, β-sitosterol, ginkgolide A, and docosanol (control) were predicted to be non-ames toxic and non-hepatotoxic, suggesting a no mutagenic potential and minimal risk of liver injury. Predicted oral acute toxicity (LD₅₀) values were comparatively high for β-sitosterol (2.326 mol/kg; 2326 mmol/kg) and ginkgolide A (2.833 mol/kg; 2833 mmol/kg), indicating low acute toxicity and a low likelihood of lethality at therapeutic doses. In comparison, docosanol (control) exhibited a lower predicted LD₅₀ value (1.722 mol/kg; 1722 mmol/kg), suggesting relatively higher acute toxicity.61, 62 Furthermore, β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A were not predicted to induce skin sensitization, further supporting their favorable safety profiles, whereas docosanol (control) was predicted to cause skin sensitization. When Bioactivity score > 0, the compound is biologically active. When −5.0 < Bioactivity score < 0, the compound is moderately active. When bioactivity score < 0, the compound is biologically inactive.63 According to Biological activity parameters of drug candidates, β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A showed preeminent GPCR ligand activity, ion channel inhibitor activity, kinase inhibitor activity, nuclear receptor ligand, protease inhibitor, and an enzyme inhibitor. Collectively, these findings indicate that β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A possess favorable molecular characteristics, pharmacokinetic profiles, drug-likeness properties, and biologically active, along with acceptable toxicity profiles, making them promising drug candidates for further iv-vitro evaluation.

Although AutoDock Vina utilizes a rigid-receptor docking protocol and does not explicitly consider induced-fit effects during ligand binding, the stability and dynamic behavior of the top-ranked protein–ligand complexes were further evaluated through MDS. This complementary approach accounts for molecular flexibility and provides additional validation of the docking results under simulated physiological conditions. In this study, β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A derived from G. biloba were compared with docosanol (control) through key simulation parameters, including RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and SASA. RMSD quantifies the deviation of protein structures from a reference conformation throughout the simulation period, serving as an indicator of overall stability. Generally, protein–ligand complexes with RMSD fluctuations within 1–2.5 Å are considered stable during MD simulations.64 Deviations beyond this range often suggest conformational changes in the protein upon ligand binding. Notably, β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A complexes exhibited RMSD values within the acceptable range, whereas the docosanol (control) complexes demonstrated higher fluctuations, reflecting comparatively reduced stability in both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD.65, 66 Similarly, RMSF measures residue-level fluctuations, thereby providing a residue-specific view of protein flexibility during simulations.67 This parameter also captures the dynamic behavior of ligand atoms and the conformational adaptability of amino acid residues during interactions.68 β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A complexes consistently exhibited lower RMSF values compared to docosanol (control) complexes, confirming reduced structural flexibility and greater stabilization across both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD.

The Rg was analyzed to assess the compactness of the protein–ligand complexes. Rg provides a measure of atomic distribution around the protein's axis, thereby reflecting macromolecular folding and stability.69 Lower Rg values indicate a more compact and structurally stable complex. In this study, β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A complexes maintained lower Rg values relative to docosanol (control) complexes, suggesting enhanced compactness and structural feasibility in both gB and gD. SASA is a great indication of protein folding and stability.70 SASA values reflect the accessible surface area of amino acid residues and their interactions with ligands, where higher values generally correspond to increased solvent exposure.71 Hydrophobic residues often contribute to protein folding, whereas hydrophilic residues enhance surface exposure. β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A complexes exhibited comparatively higher SASA values than docosanol (control) complexes in both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD, indicating greater solvent exposure and altered surface accessibility of the protein–ligand complexes.

Ligand–receptor interactions were further assessed using MM-GBSA binding free energy calculations. β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A exhibited more favorable binding energies than docosanol (control), indicating stable and persistent interactions with both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD proteins. Consistent results from molecular docking, MDS, and MM-GBSA analyses revealed that these G. biloba–derived phytochemicals possess enhanced structural stability, compactness, and binding affinities relative to docosanol (control) in complexes with both HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. Collectively, these findings underscore their potential as natural dual inhibitors of HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. Moreover, the results reinforce the significance of plant-derived bioactive compounds in antiviral drug discovery and provide a compelling basis for subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies to validate their therapeutic potential.

Although the present findings offer meaningful insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. A major limitation of the present study is the absence of experimental validation. Although molecular docking, ADMET profiling, MDS, and MM-GBSA analyses consistently suggested favorable interactions of β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A with HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD, these findings remain computational predictions. Therefore, the proposed antiviral potential of these phytochemicals should be considered preliminary until confirmed through in vitro target-binding studies, cell-based antiviral assays, pharmacokinetic investigations, and in vivo efficacy evaluations. Another limitation is that MDS were conducted for 100 ns. Although this duration was sufficient to assess the stability of the protein–ligand complexes and produced stable RMSD, RMSF, Rg, SASA, and interaction profiles, longer simulations and enhanced sampling approaches may better capture slow conformational changes and provide a more comprehensive evaluation of binding stability. In addition, the MDS analyses were based on single 100 ns trajectories for each complex; therefore, error estimation and statistical significance testing across independent simulation replicates were not performed. Future studies employing multiple replicate simulations will enable statistical validation of the observed stability trends. The connection between the present findings and AD is based on the hypothesized contribution of HSV-1 infection to neurodegenerative pathology rather than direct modulation of AD-associated molecular targets. Consequently, the identified compounds should presently be regarded as potential anti-HSV-1 candidates whose relevance to AD remains to be established experimentally. However, integrating computational approaches with rigorous experimental validation could substantially advance the development of G. biloba–derived inhibitors targeting HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. Such a combined framework holds promise for accelerating antiviral drug discovery and enhancing the translational potential of β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A, thereby contributing to improved preparedness against HSV-1 infection.

Conclusion

This study systematically screened 39 phytochemicals derived from G. biloba and identified β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A as potential dual inhibitors targeting HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. These lead phytochemicals exhibited favorable physicochemical properties, drug-likeness characteristics, and pharmacokinetic profiles, along with acceptable in-silico toxicity predictions. MDS and MM-GBSA binding free energy analyses further demonstrated enhanced structural stability, compactness, and stronger binding affinities of these phytochemicals compared with docosanol (control), supporting their potential inhibitory activity against HSV-1 glycoproteins gB and gD. Collectively, the computational findings indicate that β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A may serve as promising natural dual-target therapeutic candidates, consistent with the increasing interest in plant-derived bioactive compounds for antiviral drug discovery. However, these results remain predictive in nature, as experimental validation has not yet been conducted. Future studies should therefore prioritize in vitro and in vivo evaluations, together with structural optimization, to substantiate their efficacy, safety, and pharmacological relevance. The present findings suggest that β-sitosterol and ginkgolide A may act as promising inhibitors of HSV-1 glycoproteins involved in viral entry. Given the proposed association between recurrent HSV-1 infection and AD pathogenesis, inhibition of viral entry may represent a potential strategy for mitigating HSV-1-related neurodegenerative processes. However, the present study did not directly investigate AD-specific molecular pathways, and therefore any implications for AD remain speculative and require dedicated experimental validation.
