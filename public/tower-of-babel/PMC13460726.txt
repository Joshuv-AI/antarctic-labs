Association of Preoperative Inflammatory Markers With Preoperative Anxiety and Emergence Agitation in Children
Authors: Nezahat Merve Ata Soylu, Zehra Hatipoglu, Ersel Gulec, Mediha Turktan, Nurefsan Sadıkoglu, Yusuf Kemal Arslan, Dilek Ozcengiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionEmergence agitation (EA) is a postoperative neurobehavioral complication characterized by a transient disturbance in cognition and perception following anesthesia. Neuroinflammation is thought to play a central role in its pathophysiology. Preoperative anxiety is the “acute preoperative stress response.” This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between hemogram‐derived inflammatory markers and the development of preoperative anxiety and EA in pediatric patients.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 350 pediatric patients aged 5–12 years with ASA grade I‐II. Preoperative anxiety was assessed in the waiting room using the modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale. Demographic and clinical data, anesthesia/surgical data, and hemogram parameters were recorded. EA was evaluated in the recovery room using the Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium scale. Pain was assessed at 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 min using the FLACC scale in children < 7 years and the Visual Analog Scale in those ≥ 7 years.ResultsOf the total 350 patients, 130 (37.1%) developed EA. Compared with those without EA, children with EA had significantly higher leukocyte, monocyte, neutrophil, and platelet counts (p = 0.018, 0.005, 0.001, and 0.001, respectively), lower lymphocyte counts (p = 0.042), and elevated monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio, neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio, platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio, and systemic immune inflammatory index (SII) (all p < 0.001). Regression analysis showed that younger age, higher postoperative pain scores, and elevated SII were independently associated with EA. Preoperative anxiety was observed in 103 children (29.4%). These patients had higher white blood cell counts (p = 0.026), and regression analysis identified that younger age, anxious parents, and elevated SII were independently associated with preoperative anxiety.ConclusionEasily obtainable hemogram‐derived inflammatory markers, particularly SII, may be useful for identifying children at increased risk of preoperative anxiety and EA in children. These findings suggest that SII may assist anesthesiologists in identifying high‐risk patients and implementing preventive strategies to reduce the incidence of EA.Trial RegistrationThe study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06648122)

Introduction

Preoperative anxiety is defined as the distress experienced by individuals before undergoing a surgical procedure and has been documented in up to 65% of pediatric patients [1]. It is also considered a risk factor for the development of emergence agitation (EA) [2]. Affecting 0.25%–90.5% of children, EA manifests within the first 30 min after the end of anesthesia and subsides after 5–15 min. It has been observed in both adults and children and is characterized by symptoms such as restlessness, agitation, non‐purposeful movements, and an inability to calm down in the period shortly after general anesthesia has ended [3].

In recent years, the number of publications demonstrating the role of neuroinflammatory mediators in both preoperative anxiety and postoperative delirium (POD) pathophysiology has increased in adult patients [4, 5, 6]. Preoperative anxiety, an acute stressor, activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPA) and the sympathetic nervous system, disrupts the balance of the systemic inflammatory response, and facilitates neuroinflammation. This stress‐induced immune dysregulation mobilizes phagocytic cells (monocytes and neutrophils) and promotes the release of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin (IL)‐1β, IL‐6, while simultaneously suppressing lymphocyte‐mediated adaptive immunity, a shift that is directly reflected in hemogram‐derived indices such as neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII). Platelets have also been identified as other cellular components that play an active role in the inflammatory process [5, 7]. Elevated stress levels can increase blood–brain barrier permeability, facilitating the passage of inflammatory cells and mediators into the central nervous system, where the resulting neuroinflammation may contribute to neurocognitive impairment [4, 5]. This converging neuroinflammatory mechanism may thus link preoperative anxiety to postoperative neurocognitive dysfunction, including POD and EA. Consistent with this concept, studies in elderly patients have shown that POD is associated with neuroinflammatory markers such as neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and systemic immune inflammatory index (SII), supporting their potential use as accessible surrogate indicators of neuroinflammatory risk [6, 8].

There is a paucity of studies examining the relationship between EA and inflammatory markers obtained from hemogram parameters in children [9]. Furthermore, there is even less evidence linking preoperative anxiety with these markers. In this context, the primary aim of this study was to investigate the association between preoperative hemogram‐derived inflammatory markers and EA. The secondary aim was to evaluate the relationship between these markers and the presence of preoperative anxiety. We hypothesized that inflammatory markers derived from the hemogram, such as NLR, platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and SII, would be associated with EA and preoperative anxiety.

Methods

This prospective observational study was approved by the Cukurova University, Faculty of Medicine Institutional Ethics Committee (No: 147/5, September 06, 2024) and registered at the ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06648122). The study was conducted between October 2024 and July 2025.

Participants

Parents and children received information about the study during the preoperative visit, and written and verbal consent was subsequently obtained from participants. The study included 350 patients between the ages of 5–12 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I to II who were scheduled for elective and/or emergency conditions in the departments of gynecology, ophthalmology, urological surgery, ear, nose and throat surgery, pediatric surgery, orthopedics, plastic and reconstructive surgery, thoracic surgery, and interventional radiology. Exclusion criteria were patients receiving steroid treatment, those with genetic diseases affecting brain development, individuals diagnosed with psychiatric and neurological diseases, patients with attention deficit or intellectual disability, those with hematological and/or immune system disorders, and patients undergoing neurosurgery and cardiovascular surgery.

Study Design

The children were admitted to the preoperative unit before surgery. Their demographic data (age, gender, weight, height, body mass index, ASA physical status) and clinical data (number of children, fasting time, previous hospitalization, emergency/elective surgery, hospitalization status, parental accompaniment, premedication, child and parental anxiety and surgery procedures) were recorded.

The date and parameters of the hemogram were recorded, including white blood cell (WBC) (103/μL), red blood cell (RBC) (106/μL), hematocrit (Hct) (%), monocytes (M) (103/μL), neutrophils (N) (103/μL), lymphocytes (L) (103/μL), platelets (P) (103/μL), platelet distribution width (PDW), mean platelet volume (MPV), M/L ratio (MLR), NLR, N/M ratio (NMR), P/L ratio (PLR), P/M ratio (PMR), P/N ratio (PNR), P/WBC ratio (PWR), and SII (PxN/L). For all patients, including those presenting to the emergency department, hemogram values obtained within the last 30 days, a routine practice in our institution, were used; the time between blood sample collection and surgery was recorded as the preoperative interval. To evaluate the effect of preoperative blood sample collection timing on the discriminatory power of inflammatory markers, the preoperative interval was divided into two periods: ≤ 7 days and > 7 days.

When the patients arrived in the operating room, standard monitoring was performed, and the route of anesthesia induction, the choice of induction and maintenance agents (including opioids), the type of airway device, the postoperative analgesia strategy, the duration of surgery and anesthesia, and the time to discharge were recorded. Anesthesia management was determined by the attending anesthesiologist according to routine clinical practice.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the occurrence of EA, defined as a PAED score ≥ 10 during the first 30 min after anesthesia. The secondary outcome was the presence of preoperative anxiety, defined as an m‐YPAS score ≥ 40 in the preoperative unit. Additionally, preoperative hemogram‐derived inflammatory markers were evaluated for their associations with both outcomes.

In the preoperative unit, children's anxiety levels were assessed using the Turkish version of the modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale (m‐YPAS). The Turkish validity and reliability study of this scale was conducted by Hatipoglu et al. in 2019. This score, which is evaluated across five categories, is calculated as follows: (activity/4 + emotional expressivity/4 + state of arousal/4 + use of parents/4 + vocalization/6) × 100/5. Children with m‐YPAS scores of 40 or higher were classified as anxious [2].

Parents' anxiety levels were measured using a Likert scale (1 = not anxious at all, 2 = a little anxious, 3 = moderately anxious, 4 = very anxious, 5 = extremely anxious). Parents with a Likert scale score of ≥ 3 were considered anxious [10].

After awakening from the operating room, the children were transferred to the recovery room and evaluated for EA and pain during the first 30 min. EA was evaluated using the Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Agitation (PAED) score developed by Sikich and Lerman, and was defined as a PAED score of 10 or higher in children [11, 12]. The Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, and Consolability (FLACC) Scale and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) were utilized for pain assessment. The FLACC scale was used for children under 7 years, whereas the VAS was used for children aged 7 and above. Pain was indicated by the FLACC and VAS scores of 4 or more. Fentanyl (0.5 μg/kg, intravenous bolus) was administered to patients with agitation and/or pain. Children with a Modified Aldrete recovery score ≥ 9 were transferred from the recovery room to the appropriate wards. EA and preoperative anxiety were evaluated by two different researchers.

Statistical Analysis

The primary endpoint of the study was the presence of EA within the first 30 min after anesthesia, defined as PAED score ≥ 10, and its quantified association with preoperative hemogram‐derived indices. Because no closely comparable studies were available in the literature, we based our sample size on a medium effect size (d = 0.40), with 95% power and 5% margin of error (α = 0.05), and assuming a 1:1 ratio between EA‐positive and EA‐negative groups. Under these assumptions, the required total sample size was 328 participants (164 per group). Considering missing data/loss of data, the study was planned to be conducted with 350 participants. G. power 3.1.9.7 was used in the calculations.

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as number (n) and percentage (%). Continuous variables were presented as median with interquartile range (Q1‐Q3). Median differences and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Hodges‐Lehmann estimators. The chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test was used to compare the categorical measurements between groups. The normality of the distribution of continuous variables was confirmed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The Mann–Whitney U test was used if the assumptions were not met in comparing numerical measurements between groups. Logistic regression analyses were used to determine risk factors for both EA and the development of preoperative anxiety. Logistic Regression Models were constructed with variables that were significant in the univariate analysis and unrelated to each other, and final models were obtained using Forward LR method. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of SII for EA, and the Youden index was used to determine appropriate cutoff values; this analysis was also repeated for the ≤ 7 days subgroup. For all analyses, the statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

In total, 419 patients were enrolled in the study. A total of 69 patients were excluded from the study due to malignancy (n = 18), trauma (n = 4), infection (n = 23), unplanned admission to the intensive care unit (n = 2) and unexplained abnormal hemogram values (n = 22) (Figure 1). The mean age of the patients included in the study was 8.3 ± 2.2 years. Of the patients, 139 (39.7%) were female, whereas 211 (60.3%) were male.

Except for lymphocyte count, all parameters (monocyte, neutrophil, and platelet counts, as well as MLR, NLR, PLR, and SII) exhibited varying degrees of discriminatory power for EA. SII demonstrated the highest discriminatory power (AUC: 0.693, 95% CI: 0.634–0.751) (Figure 2).

ROC curve analysis. (A) ROC curve analysis for emergence agitation. (B) ROC curve analysis for the development of emergence agitation in the preoperative interval ≤ 7 days group. (C) ROC curve analysis for the development of emergence agitation in the preoperative interval > 7 days group.

In the preoperative interval ≤ 7 days subgroup, SII demonstrated the highest area under the curve (AUC: 0.713, 95% CI: 0.643–0.783). Furthermore, monocyte, neutrophil, and platelet counts and MLR, NLR, and PLR also demonstrated significant discriminatory power, while lymphocyte count showed the lowest discriminatory power (p = 0.031). The optimal cutoff point for SII was 541, with a sensitivity of 60.9% and a specificity of 80.4% for EA. In the > 7 days subgroup, the discriminatory power of SII decreased (AUC: 0.642, 95% CI: 0.533–0.751), yet remained statistically significant (p = 0.014), along with platelet count (p = 0.023). (Figure 2).

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between inflammatory markers obtained from preoperative hemogram values and both preoperative anxiety and EA in children. We found that elevated leukocyte, neutrophil, monocyte, and platelet counts, as well as decreased lymphocyte counts and ratios derived from these values (MLR, NLR, PLR, and SII), were associated with the development of EA. Notably, SII was independently associated with EA and demonstrated moderate predictive performance. It may also have potential value in identifying patients with preoperative anxiety.

Despite numerous risk factors, the pathophysiological mechanism of EA has not been fully elucidated. However, it is known that surgery, anesthesia, and most importantly, entering a different environment, such as a hospital, are stressful for children and even adults. Stress is perhaps the primary trigger of agitation in children. Acute stress activates the sympathetic nervous system and the HPA axis. The rapid increase in circulating catecholamines mobilizes leukocytes, resulting in hyperacute leukocytosis. Neutrophils are released into the peripheral blood, lymphocytes redistribute to tissues, and platelets are released from the spleen [13, 14]. Activation of the HPA axis increases cortisol levels, which increases neutrophil counts, decreases lymphocyte counts, and enhances megakaryopoiesis, resulting in a higher number of young circulating platelets [15]. This prepares the body for inflammation. Later, with the addition of surgical trauma and anesthesia‐related factors, the process may progress to neuroinflammation, and EA may occur. Given the inclusion of three inflammatory markers in the SII, it is not surprising that the SII has better predictive value for EA than other hematological ratios. Consistent with our findings, previous studies including adults have identified SII as a strong predictor of postoperative cognitive complications. Yang et al. reported an association between high preoperative MLR values and POD in a large cohort of patients undergoing lumbar spinal fusion surgery [6]. Lu et al. reported that SII, NLR, and MLR were correlated with postoperative cognitive dysfunction, and SII was the best predictor of POCD in elderly orthopedic patients [5]. In a retrospective study, Song et al. found that preoperative SII was predictive of the development of POD [8]. Feng et al. investigated the association between preoperative inflammatory markers and EA development after tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy in 94 pediatric patients. They found that EA developed in 25.3% of patients and was associated with low NLR and postoperative pain, while SII values were similar to those who did not develop EA. The authors emphasized that antibiotic treatment in the 2 weeks before surgery in the patients in this study may have increased the lymphocyte counts and led to falsely low NLR and MLR values [9]. In the present study, higher SII was independently associated with EA, while elevated NLR also showed association ‐ consistent with the composite nature of SII (platelet × neutrophil/lymphocyte). The robustness of our findings is supported by the large cohort size, inclusion of diverse surgical procedures, and the high incidence of EA observed.

The timing of preoperative hemogram collection varies across studies. Yang et al. used hemogram values obtained within the last week before surgery, Feng et al. collected blood samples on the morning before surgery, while Song et al. used the most recently available values without specifying a time frame. Unlike these studies, Yaseen et al. collected two samples: one up to 1 month before surgery and another on the day of surgery, and reported that immune cell alterations were more evident in samples collected on the day of surgery. In our study, hemogram values obtained within the last 30 days were used in accordance with institutional routine, and the median preoperative interval in the groups was 5 and 4 days, respectively. When the preoperative interval was divided into two periods, the discriminatory ability of SII appeared higher in the ≤ 7 days group compared to the > 7 days group (AUC: 0.713 vs. 0.642). This observation raises the possibility that hemogram values obtained closer to surgery may more accurately capture the preoperative inflammatory state, although further studies are needed to draw firm conclusions.

In our study, postoperative pain increased the risk of developing EA by 2.5‐fold, which supports its identification as a risk factor consistent with previous literature [3]. In this cohort, there were no significant differences between groups regarding induction route, airway technique, maintenance agents, analgesic use, the type of surgery (emergency vs. elective), or anesthesia/surgery times. Some studies have suggested that opioids may reduce the incidence of EA; however, their effects appear to vary depending on the type of surgery and anesthetic agents used, limiting generalizability [16, 17]. Similarly, the incidence of EA was comparable among opioid preference groups during induction. Additionally, EA has been reported to occur more frequently in certain surgical procedures, particularly ear‐nose‐throat and ophthalmologic surgeries. However, this rate may have been diluted in our study because all surgical specialties were included [3].

In our study, preoperative anxiety did not differ significantly between the EA and non‐EA groups. While some studies support this finding, it contrasts with previous reports that have identified preoperative anxiety as a risk factor for EA [18, 19, 20]. The relatively low anxiety levels in our cohort, the small proportion of preschool‐aged children, and the presence of parents during the preoperative period may have contributed to this result.

The prevalence of preoperative anxiety in this study was 29.4%, which is lower than the 40%–60% reported in the literature [21]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the calming influence of parental presence. Consistent with previous research, anxiety was more common among younger children with low BMI, and parental anxiety emerged as a significant independent predictor of child anxiety, which aligns with previous findings [22, 23]. Furthermore, preoperative anxiety was more frequent in hospitalized than in ambulatory patients, likely due to the uncertainty and environmental changes inherent to hospitalization.

This study is the first to explore the relationship between preoperative anxiety and inflammatory markers in children, revealing that preoperative anxiety is associated with elevated WBC levels, indicative of an acute stress response in this group. Unlike Uzun et al., we found no differences in neutrophil count, NLR, MLR, or PLR between the anxious and non‐anxious groups [7]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the study populations: while their control group consisted of completely healthy children, our comparison involved pediatric patients scheduled for surgery, which may have blurred the distinction between groups due to varying baseline stress levels and preoperative inflammatory status. In adult patients undergoing elective surgery, those with preoperative anxiety exhibit lower leukocyte and lymphocyte counts, but higher neutrophil counts [24]. Consistent with this, higher SII was independently associated with preoperative anxiety in the final model; however, this association should be interpreted with caution. Elevated SII may reflect a broader stress‐related or pro‐inflammatory phenotype rather than serving as a direct measure of preoperative anxiety per se. Moreover, hemogram values were not obtained on the day of surgery, and the wide age range of our cohort (5–12 years) further complicates interpretation, as younger children may not yet be aware of the upcoming procedure at the time of blood collection. Finally, a recent meta‐analysis of nine studies on children and adolescents with anxiety‐related disorders reported no consistent alterations in inflammatory or immune markers [25]. These findings suggest a complex relationship between anxiety and inflammatory pathways in children, warranting further investigation in larger, more homogeneous cohorts.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center, and there was heterogeneity in surgical procedures and anesthesia/analgesia management, which may limit generalizability. Second, hemogram values were obtained within 30 days before surgery, and inflammation was assessed solely using hemogram‐derived biomarkers, excluding markers such as CRP, interleukins, and cortisol. Moreover, the broad age range (5–12 years) and the fact that blood sample collection was not standardized to the day of surgery may limit inferences regarding the temporal relationship between inflammatory indices and preoperative anxiety. Although emergency admissions represented only a small proportion of the cohort and were not independently associated with EA in multivariable analysis, the inclusion of both elective and emergency cases may still constitute a potential source of residual confounding. Third, preoperative anxiety and EA assessments were conducted by two separate observers who were not blinded to the laboratory results, which may have introduced observer bias. Furthermore, hemogram samples were obtained via venipuncture; this procedure can be stressful for many children and may have contributed to preoperative anxiety, potentially affecting the observed associations.

Conclusion

In this prospective observational study involving 350 patients, EA was associated with age, postoperative pain, and hemogram‐derived indices. Given its cost‐effectiveness, ease of application, and availability from routine blood tests, SII may be a useful biomarker for assessing the risk of EA, while its role in preoperative anxiety should be interpreted more cautiously. Future multicenter prospective studies with standardized hemogram timing and validated biomarker panels are warranted to confirm these findings and to establish SII‐based risk stratification protocols in pediatric perioperative care.
