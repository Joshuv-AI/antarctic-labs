Enhanced HTO Separation from H2O by Membrane Distillation: The Crucial Role of Montmorillonite Clay in Gas–liquid Exchange for HTO Capture
Authors: Hirokazu Miyoshi, Masahiro Fujiwara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The removal of tritiated water (HTO) from light water (H2O) is an important process not only for the processing of radioactive materials but also for securing nuclear fusion fuel. We previously reported that HTO can be separated from H2O by using an apparatus with a membrane distillation mechanism, with which H2O-impregnated glass fibers efficiently separate HTO from H2O by gas–liquid exchange. In this study, H2O-impregnated clays were installed instead of H2O-impregnated glass fibers. Montmorillonite, sepiolite, palygorskite, a synthetic zeolite, and a mesoporous silica were employed, and montmorillonite was found to be the most effective for the separation. The tritium (T) ratio in the water that was finally collected in the vessel below the apparatus decreased with the increase in the volume of H2O added to montmorillonite. When 4 mL of H2O was added to 1 g of montmorillonite, the T ratio was reduced by about 10%. X-ray diffraction analysis revealed that the layer structures of montmorillonite significantly varied with the volume of water added. The moderate removal of HTO from H2O was observed in condensed water vapor obtained from the montmorillonite impregnated with HTO-containing water in sealed-vial experiments at room temperature, suggesting that montmorillonite had the specific property of capturing HTO. On the other hand, although the T ratio in the water condensate from the HTO-impregnated montmorillonite remained unchanged during the heating treatment at temperatures higher than 100 °C, the ratio in that from the montmorillonite used for the separation process gradually decreased during the heating treatment. These observations showed that a portion of HTO was tightly captured in specific sites of montmorillonite. In this case, HTO in the vapor was loaded during the separation process by the membrane distillation mechanism. The method of loading HTO in the vapor to H2O-impregnated montmorillonite was found to be an important factor for the efficient removal of HTO from H2O. The improvement in the design of the apparatus for the effective separation of HTO from H2O was achieved using H2O-impregnated montmorillonite.

Introduction

The effective separation and enrichment of nuclear materials are essential for the prevention of environmental contamination and the efficient utilization of such materials. Recently, the separation of the tritium (T) atom, which has a maximal β-particle energy of 15.6 keV and a half-life of 12.3 y, has attracted attention, because securing concentrated T has become increasingly important in connection with the practical applications of deuterium–tritium (D–T) nuclear fusion as a new energy resource. However, the separation of tritiated water (HTO), which is abundant in T, from light water (H2O) is one of the most difficult processes because of the slight differences in the physical properties, such as vapor pressure, between HTO and H2O. Increasing the temperature to promote evaporation further reduces the difference in vapor pressure. For example, the vapor pressure ratio (α) of about 1.1 at 20 °C decreases to 1.03 at 100 °C. On the other hand, the exchange between HTO vapor and H2O liquid, as shown in eq , is also a well-known alternative procedure for HTO separation. , 1 H2O(liquid)+HTO(vapor)↔HTO(liquid)+H2O(vapor)

Large amounts of radioactive waste generated by the accident at the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant (FDNPP) were treated to remove radioactive materials using the advanced liquid processing system (ALPS) until their concentration fell below regulatory limits. , However, ALPS cannot separate HTO in principle. To overcome this problem, methods for separating HTO from H2O have received renewed interest in recent years. For example, Matsumoto et al. reported that HTO molecules can be removed and their levels reduced to about 6% after one month by the isotope exchange reaction between H2O vapor and HTO using a complex system. To improve the HTO separation process, we focused on the application of solar energy to the removal of HTO. Recently, we have developed a system for the efficient separation of HTO from H2O by membrane distillation incorporating gas–liquid exchange. Our system is based on another system for the solar desalination of seawater. In the system for solar desalination, seawater put on a hydrophobic membrane modified with photothermal dyes such as solvent black 5 (SB-5) is exposed to artificial sunlight for desalination by the membrane distillation mechanism previously reported. − By adapting this system to ours, we achieved the efficient separation of HTO from H2O by combining membrane distillation and gas–liquid exchange using four sets of overlaid filters, such as H2O-impregnated glass fiber and PF100 hydrophobic filters. This system enabled the concurrent removal of HTO and radioactive cesium atoms. The exchange between HTO vapor and H2O liquid occurs in the H2O-impregnated glass fiber filter, as expressed in eq . Then, the exchanged vapor permeates into the hydrophobic and H2O-impregnated glass fiber filters. Finally, the HTO-containing water vapor passes through the four sets of H2O-impregnated glass fiber and hydrophobic filters. However, the volume of H2O included in one set of H2O-impregnated glass fiber and hydrophobic filters is limited, and using four sets makes the system thick. Therefore, our previous system was too complex, and simpler systems are required. In addition, since the system for the removal of HTO using the gas–liquid exchange reaction is still insufficient in its performance, some other functionalities must be incorporated.

Clays and analogous porous inorganic materials, which are widely distributed in the world, have unique characteristics in relation to water that are evident especially in the adsorption and desorption of HTO. − For example, Kalinichenko et al. have reported the T accumulation behaviors in the structures of clay minerals (kaolinite, montmorillonite, and palygorskite). These minerals would have limits of water uptake dependent on a combination of their structural OH content and the capability of water to interact with the basal surfaces. As a function of structural OH per mass, kaolin ≫palygorskite/sepiolite > montmorillonite. As a function of water access to basal surfaces for exchange, montmorillonite > palygorskite/sepioloite ≫ kaolinite. Thus, from the results of this study, montmorillonite can achieve the capture of essentially 100% T for H exchange, forming structural OT. The states of water in these clays have characteristic properties and are classified as structural water, cationic interlayer water, interlayer surface water tightly bonded to the cationic interlayer water, interlayer surface water loosely bonded to other interlayers, and excess surface water. The OH content of smectite (montmorillonite) is around 9%. In clay materials, although H+ cation migration occurs similarly to bulk water transfer, the velocity is lower. T cations diffuse through bound layers into mineral lattices. , Rat’ko et al. also reported that after keeping aluminosilicate suspensions in contact with HTO (322.7 Bq/mL) for 3 months, the percentages of T adsorbed on montmorillonite, sepiolite, and palygorskite become 2.85, 3.45, and 6.4%, respectively. Thus, these specific properties of clays and analogous materials are expected to work effectively in our HTO removal system with membrane distillation and gas–liquid exchange mechanisms.

In this study, we examined the effects of porous inorganic materials such as clays and zeolites on the separation of HTO from H2O using our apparatus incorporating both membrane distillation and gas–liquid exchange mechanisms, in which porous inorganic materials were installed instead of H2O-impregnated glass fiber and hydrophobic filters. As porous inorganic materials, montmorillonite, sepiolite, palygorskite, a zeolite, and a mesoporous silica were utilized for the gas–liquid exchange of HTO vapor and H2O liquid. As a result, montmorillonite was found to be the most efficient in removing HTO from H2O. The mechanism of HTO separation using montmorillonite was precisely investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD), and the obtained patterns showed that the absorption and desorption of HTO were enhanced by increasing the temperature.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

3H water (HTO; PerkinElmer NET001B, water, [3H]-, 37 MBq/g) was purchased from Japan Radioisotope Association (JRIA). A photothermal dye, SB-5, was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industries Co., Ltd. and used without further purification. Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane filters (PF100, Advantec), which have 10 μm pore size, 77% porosity, and 1.0 mm thickness and are hydrophobic, were purchased from Toyo Roshi Kaisha, Ltd. Montmorillonite (KUNIPIA-G) was provided by Kunimine Industries. A synthetic zeolite (HS-320) was purchased from Wako Pure Chemicals. Sepiolite (Thermogel) and palygorskite (Minugel) were provided by Tomoe Engineering Co., Ltd. A mesoporous silica, i.e., a cubically ordered MCM-48-type mesoporous silica (PMCM481030R), was purchased from Filgen, Inc. A liquid scintillator (National Diagnostics, Ecoscint Ultra) was purchased from Sowa Trading Co. Distilled water was prepared using a Thermo Scientific Pure RO system.

Process of Separating HTO from H2O by Membrane Distillation with Gas–liquid Exchange

Artificial sunlight was irradiated directly above and through the central hole (42 mm in diameter) of the plastic flange using a solar simulator, HAL-320 (Asahi Spectra Co., Ltd.), as previously reported. The intensity of the light was adjusted to 1000 W/cm2 (1 SUN) using the EKO-CE pyranometer ML-01 (Eiko Instruments Co., Ltd.) connected to the Sanwa digital multimeter PC20. Since the purpose of this study was to separate HTO from H2O, the energy efficiency of the irradiated light for water recovery was not estimated. Figure shows a cross-sectional view of the equipment for the separation of HTO from H2O by membrane distillation using H2O-impregnated montmorillonite (as an example) instead of the H2O-impregnated glass fiber filter. As shown in Figure , a direct-contact membrane distillation was used. HTO vapor was generated from 10 mL HTO (3 kBq/mL)-containing water on the SB-5-modified PTFE membrane filter by the photothermal conversion of SB-5. The generated HTO then penetrated the SB-5-modified PTFE membrane filter with an HTO vapor flux of about 0.025 mL/cm2/h and came in contact with an H2O-absorbed montmorillonite to induce gas–liquid exchange. Subsequently, about 0.4 mL of HTO-containing water was collected in the aluminum foil vessel placed below the non-SB-5-modified PTFE membrane filter after light irradiation for 1.5 h. Two heat sinks and a copper cooling plate were used to promote the condensation of water by heat release. This experiment is referred to as the “separation process” in this paper. Since HTO and distilled water were used in the separation process, the SB-5-modified PTFE membrane filter had no membrane fouling and the porosity did not change in this study. Furthermore, the volume of the generated HTO vapor was linearly proportional to the light irradiation time with r 2 = 0.0065 (volume of generated HTO vapor = 0.3396 × light irradiation time), indicating the stability of the separation process in this study (see Figure S1).

4 mL of only HTO-containing water (control) or 1 g of montmorillonite with 4 mL of HTO-containing water was placed in a 20 mL glass vial sealed with a plastic cap, with filter paper hanging underneath the cap (1 cm × 2 cm), as shown in Figure S2. XRD Patterns of H2O-Impregnated Montmorillonite

XRD patterns of montmorillonite impregnated with various volumes of H2O were measured using a Rigaku Rint II X-ray diffractometer. 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 mL of H2O was added to 1 g of montmorillonite powder and mixed thoroughly in an agate mortar. Each sample was set on the glass plate for the X-ray diffractometer, and XRD patterns were obtained using Cu Kα radiation in the 2-theta range from 2 to 80°. The obtained data were analyzed by phase identification using the powder diffraction software MATCH! (CRYSTAL IMPACT) (LightStone Corp, Japan). The water vapor generated by heating the H2O-impregnated montmorillonite (not used for the separation process) on the hot plate was collected in a 100 mL glass beaker covering the entire H2O-impregnated montmorillonite on the hot plate. The water vapor generated was cooled to condense on the interior surface of the beaker at room temperature. Each beaker with H2O was weighed using an analytical balance before and after the heating experiments at 100 and 160 °C.

Results and Discussion

Previous Results

In our previous study, the removal of HTO from H2O was achieved by the combination of membrane distillation and gas–liquid exchange, where four sets of H2O-impregnated glass and PF100 hydrophobic filters significantly promoted the efficient HTO separation. Generally, HTO-containing water simulating ALPS-treated water set on an SB-5-coated hydrophobic membrane was irradiated with artificial sunlight (1000 W/cm2) to generate heat by the photothermal conversion of SB-5. The vapors of HTO and H2O thus produced penetrated the hydrophobic membrane and came in contact with the H2O-impregnated glass filter directly beneath the membrane. In the H2O-containing glass filter, 3H (tritium; T) of HTO exchanged with 1H of H2O, and the T ratio in water decreased stepwise as HTO passed through the four filter sets during gas–liquid exchange. Consequently, we reduced the ratio of HTO in the water collected below all the filters. The HTO ratio decreased to about 12 ± 0.3% of the initial HTO ratio for 1.5 h of light irradiation. In this study, instead of those filter sets, clays such as montmorillonite, sepiolite, palygorskite, and zeolites (a zeolite and a mesoporous silica) were incorporated into our apparatus system to further reduce the ratio of HTO in the collected water.

Separation of HTO from H2O Using Montmorillonite, Sepiolite, Palygorskite Clays, and Zeolites

For the HTO separation experiments using clays, the volume of H2O impregnated into a clay material was kept constant at 4 mL to investigate the effect of the structure of the clays. For the zeolites (a synthetic zeolite: HS-320, PMCM481030R and a mesoporous silica: PSBA15100120R), their weights were kept constant at 1 g to examine the effect of the volume of H2O. These porous inorganic materials impregnated with H2O were placed below the SB-5-coated hydrophobic PTFE membrane filter. After pouring the HTO-containing water on the membrane filter, artificial sunlight was irradiated directly from above onto the membrane filter. Finally, a certain volume of the water that passed through the membrane as vapor and the layers of inorganic materials was collected below the entire system (collected water). The T ratios in the collected water decreased to about 10% for montmorillonite, 45% for sepiolite, and 43% for palygorskite. Whereas montmorillonite has a layered structure with interlayers, sepiolite and palygorskite have tunnel structures of different sizes. Sepiolite and palygorskite are nonswelling 2:1 phyllosilicate clays that show a fibrous characteristic with a continuous tetrahedral silica sheet and a discontinuity of the octahedral sheet. In the case of using a synthetic zeolite and a mesoporous silica (runs 4 and 5), the volume of the impregnated H2O can be increased from 2 to 4 mL per 1 g of these materials with the channel structure with pore sizes from 0.9 to 2.1 nm. As shown in Figure , the T ratio in the collected water from H2O-impregnated sepiolite, palygorskite, and montmorillonite clays markedly decreased compared with that of the H2O- impregnated glass fiber filter (dashed line). In particular, the reduction in the T ratio was marked when using montmorillonite. In montmorillonite, H2O exists in various states , and is strongly restricted by the matrices of this clay. The H2O–montmorillonite system has many compartments of interlayers containing H2O, through which the HTO vapor is forced to pass. Montmorillonite gels contain pores ranging from micro- (<2 nm) to meso- (2–20 nm) to macropores (>20 nm), which were surface micropores, interlayer micropores, intraparticle slit mesopores, and capillary macropores. Montmorillonite gels at the hydration state greater than 0.72 mL of H2O/1 g contain a similar number of pores in the micro- and small mesopore (adsorptive slit pores) size range of sub-2 to 20 nm regardless of hydration state. Higher water contents (4 mL of H2O/1 g) only increase the numbers of larger adsorptive and capillary meso- and macropores. From the viewpoint of HTO diffusing through the pore system and thus coming into close proximity to the basal surface for exchange, a higher water content would have greater potential to enable exchange than a lower water content because of a higher proportion of adsorptive pores in close proximity to HTO to the basal surfaces. As a simple approach, we focused on montmorillonite swelling, which involved interlayer swelling (surface micropores and interlayer micropores) and osmotic swelling (intraparticle slit mesopores and capillary macropores). Grekov et al. reported that the total water content was determined by thermogravimetric analysis, the pore water content was determined by thermoporometry for humidity-saturated Na-montmorillonite, and the amount of interlayer water was found to be about 43% for interlayer water and about 57% for pore water. It has been reported that the water held in the intraparticle slit mesopores and capillary macropores could be easily removed by heating at 80–90 °C, as determined by thermogravimetric-differential scanning calorimetry tests and specific surface area measurements. Kashif et al. reported that since the interlayer water can be removed at temperatures between 100 and 250 °C depending on the interlayer cations, temperatures of 100 and 150 °C were selected for drying. The evaporation temperature of the adsorbed H2O on montmorillonite was recently reported. The H2O in the interlayers at 130 °C, the bound H2O at 180 °C, and structural hydroxyl groups at 400–500 °C are vaporized. In another paper, it is claimed that the adsorbed H2O desorbs from the external surface at about 150 °C, the interlayer H2O can be removed from 120 to 300 °C, and the hydroxyl groups are eliminated as H2O at about 650 °C at 20 °C/min of the heating rate. , The interlayer H2O of montmorillonite remains even when heated at 100 °C. Furthermore, a complete drying of 40% bentonite (10.4 g), which was mixed with 15.6 g of ground quartz, containing 39 g of tap water without the interlayer cations, was observed at 240 min at 100 °C and 150 min at 150 °C. It will be difficult for static heating to completely segregate interlayer water from other waters (some interlayer water will be lost and some noninterlayer water will remain, depending on pore widths), nor is it optimized.

On the other hand, as listed in Table , the T ratios in the water condensates (C) recovered from the HTO-impregnated montmorillonite clay were lower than those in the control experiments. The T ratio was 10–14% lower than that in the control, indicating that about 14% of HTO was captured in montmorillonite even when liquid HTO was introduced into the clay at room temperature. The ratio is in close agreement with the amount of structural OH in montmorillonite (8–10%). These results illustrate that a portion of HTO (approximately 14%) was strongly immobilized in montmorillonite, which was difficult to liberate from the matrix of the clay under these conditions. Therefore, it is ascertained that HTO loaded into montmorillonite is bound more tightly than H2O, which is advantageous for the separation of HTO from H2O.

The decrease in the T ratio to around 86% was achieved by the simple evaporation of HTO-containing water from the montmorillonite containing HTO and H2O at room temperature for 96 h. It is considered that the surface HTO isotope exchange and transfer to the interlayer H2O in montmorillonite proceeded. Galindo et al. reported that tritium accumulates in clay minerals through the replacement of structural OH– groups by OT– groups. Rat’ko et al. also reported that montmorillonite modified with dodecylpyridinium bromide adsorbed 26% of tritium. Taken together, results suggest that montmorillonite clay can effectively capture HTO at room temperature.

XRD Analysis of the Mechanism of Separation of HTO from H2O by Montmorillonite

The HTO vapor generated by light irradiation penetrated the PTFE membrane filter and came in contact with the H2O-impregnated montmorillonite (4 mL/g). A portion of HTO vapor came in contact with pore water, and others came in contact with layer water randomly. When the HTO vapor came in contact with H2O, gas–liquid exchange between HTO vapor and H2O liquid occurred, and the HTO solution was produced, as shown in eq . The two-stage heterogeneous process has been reported as expressed by the following equations 3 HTOsoln+H2Obon=HTObon+H2Osoln 4 HTObon+(HO−)str=(TO−)str+H2Obon where HTOsoln, HTObon, and (HO−)str represent the HTO solution, the bonded HTO in the interlayer, and the aluminosilicate structure of HO, respectively. It has been reported that the HTO concentration was low near the internal surface, and about 90% of T was located on the montmorillonite surface. In the case of palygorskite, the T concentration near the surface was reported to be the same as that in the external solution. Although the exchange reaction in eq is possible, it needs 300–500 days to complete. The exchange rate is also higher in palygorskite than in montmorillonite. In our separation process (400 wt % H2O in montmorillonite), since HTO vapor, which is not affected by the cohesive force of liquid water, passes through the H2O-impregnated montmorillonite, it comes in contact with H2O on the surface, the bound H2O in the interlayer, and the structural OH- simultaneously. It seems that HTO vapor passes through the pore system and thus comes into close proximity to the basal surface for exchange. The self-diffusion of bulk water was ∼3.3 × 10–9 m2/s, and that of water residing predominantly only in the interlayer of Na-montmorillonite has been measured at 1.17 × 10–9 m2/s or ∼ 1.2 nm2/s. In clays, there was tortuosity of the flow path to consider, which would probably halve the diffusion lengths for interlayer water. Therefore, H of the bound H2O in the interlayer and the structural OH– exchange with T result in the capture of HTO. Finally, the volume of HTO vapor added to H2O-impregnated montmorillonite was recovered and was the same as that of the collected water (see Figure S2).

On the other hand, the distance between the interlayers in montmorillonite clay is changed by the volume of absorbed H2O, which can be estimated using Bragg’s equation from the peak position of the (001) d-spacing. The XRD patterns of the H2O-impregnated montmorillonite are shown in Figure . It has been reported that the basal spacing in the montmorillonite depends on the amount of interlattice water. The XRD pattern of dry montmorillonite shows a peak at about 8° (2θ), whose basal spacing is calculated to be approximately 1.2 nm (Figure a). The 1.2 nm d-value indicates one-layer hydrate (LH). The 1 LH retained in the interlayer of montmorillonite spaces is as much as 10 water molecules per unit cell (depending on its chemistry, layer charge characteristics, and interlayer cation composition). The interlayer distances in montmorillonite with 1 and 2 mL of H2O added increased to approximately 1.7 and 2.2 nm, respectively (Figure b,c). This indicates that two and three more LHs were retained in the interlayer spaces of the montmorillonite. These increases in interlayer spacing were due to the water molecules absorbed in the interlayer. It was found that the peak at less than 10° (2θ) shifted with H2O content, and the peaks became considerably broader when more than 2 mL of H2O was added. When more than 3 mL of H2O was added to montmorillonite (Figure d), the peak became even broader, which was likely to be attributable to osmotic swelling. Finally, when 4 mL of H2O was added, the broader peaks were located at the initial positions when no H2O was added. This shows that the molecules of water distribute to all parts of montmorillonite (Figure e), indicating that the enhanced peak broadening in conjunction with lower intensity, as was observed in the XRD traces, simply means greater heterogeneity of interlayer spacing, that is, less perfect long-range order of the clay fabric.

Massat et al. reported that the specific surface area of Kunipia-G montmorillonite increased from 42 to 660 m2/g in the nitrogen adsorption experiment, as determined using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) equation and water sorption isotherm. It is considered that the interlayer space, the porosity between montmorillonite particles (interparticle porosity), and the porosity between montmorillonite aggregates (interaggregate porosity) contributed to the maximum surface area.

Next, the effects of heating on the H2O-containing interlayer of montmorillonite were also examined on the basis of the XRD patterns.

After heating the montmorillonite with 4 mL of added H2O at 100 °C, the XRD pattern became nearly the same as that of 1 mL-H2O-containing montmorillonite, as shown in Figure f. On the other hand, the interlayer distance decreased to about 1.2 nm when the temperature was raised to 160 °C, as shown in Figure g, which was similar to that of the montmorillonite in Figure a. These findings indicate that whereas the interlayer water did not evaporate at a temperature lower than 100 °C, it was completely vaporized and thereby eliminated from montmorillonite at 160 °C. Finally, it seems that at least two types of water bound in montmorillonite affect the evaporation and capture of HTO. Brochard reported that the behavior of bulk H2O was reproduced in the capillary domain at a spacing larger than 1.9 nm. Furthermore, Li et al. indicated that H2O shows certain bulk-like behaviors in locations away from the clay surface under fully H2O-saturated conditions. These reports might explain our observations. Additionally, on heating wet and swollen montmorillonite, the interlayers collapse, pushing water out from the interlayers, as shown in the XRD traces. Part of the separation simply involves the mass transfer of water from the interlayers, and during this process, the separation of HTO from H2O may occur. HTO is preferentially retained–either by HTO to H2O and OH to OT exchanges, as suggested above, or some other mechanism. In our next study, we will clarify the mechanism of T transfer in H2O-impregnated montmorillonite upon heating after the separation process. During the evaporation of water (desorption) from the interlayers, the clay layers realign (thus, the sharper XRD traces observed upon heating). It could be this realignment that is involved in the trapping of HTO.

Tritium Behaviors in Water Condensate Recovered from H2O-Impregnated Montmorillonite after the Separation Process and Montmorillonite Impregnated with HTO-Containing Water without the Separation Process.

As described above, the placement of H2O-impregnated montmorillonite below the SB5-modified PTFE membrane in the separation process was ascertained to be very effective for the separation of HTO from H2O. This high efficiency of separating HTO from H2O was evaluated on the basis of the desorption performance of H2O from the clay. The bound state of H2O in the montmorillonite clay and the condition of HTO in the water were next investigated by analyzing the behavior of desorption upon heating and the time to the completion of desorption. After the separation process using 1 g of montmorillonite impregnated with 4 mL of H2O, montmorillonite was heated at 100 °C for about 30 min three times without intervals and then further heated at 160 °C.

As shown in Figure , when the montmorillonite simply impregnated with HTO-containing H2O, which was the same as the montmorillonite used in the experiments in sealed vials, was heated, the T ratio in the water condensate (C) in the first heating at 100 °C was about 90.3 ± 0.9 ∼ 93.2 ± 1.0% [=net count rate of water condensate (cpm/0.1 mL) against initial net count rate of HTO (cpm/0.1 mL), see Table S2] compared with that of only HTO-containing H2O (blue bars). The ratio slightly increased to about 96.2 ± 0.8 ∼ 92.4 ± 0.9% by the subsequent heating even at 160 °C. These about 10 to 4% T ratio reductions were likely to be due to failure to capture the entire amount of water vapor in the heating experiments. In the case of the experiments with sealed vials at room temperature, the T ratios in the water condensate recovered from filter papers were lower than the initial T ratio in HTO-containing H2O. There seem to be no clear differences in elimination behaviors between HTO and H2O at temperatures higher than 100 °C. The simple loading of liquid HTO and H2O to montmorillonite is considered modestly effective for the separation of HTO.

On the other hand, when the montmorillonite used for the separation process was heated, the T ratio in the water condensate (C) after the first heating at 100 °C was about 29.5 ± 0.3 ∼ 22.6 ± 0.4%. This low ratio was caused by the mixing of HTO with H2O added to montmorillonite. Note that the ratio decreased after the second and third heating processes at 100 °C, which is different from that in the HTO-impregnated montmorillonite (orange bars). When this montmorillonite was further heated at 160 °C (18.9 ± 0.2 ∼ 20.8 ± 0.3%), the ratio was still lower than that after heating at 100 °C. A portion of HTO that did not strongly bind to montmorillonite was eliminated in the first heating at 100 °C. On the other hand, during the subsequent heatings at 100 and 160 °C, HTO that strongly bound to montmorillonite gradually desorbed, resulting in the T ratio being lower than that in the first heating. HTO was loaded into the H2O-containing montmorillonite as vapor, which is not affected by cohesive force, different from the simple impregnation with liquid H2O, because of the membrane distillation mechanism of our separation process. The HTO vapor generated by the separation process with the membrane distillation mechanism penetrates into the H2O-impregnated montmorillonite clay, which has regions of surface H2O, interlayer H2O, and the aluminosilicate lattice of the montmorillonite clay. Then, the T of HTO vapor was exchanged with the H of H2O solution in the H2O-impregnated montmorillonite by the gas–liquid exchange during the separation process. The HTO vapor, but not HTO-containing water, would be captured in the lattice of the montmorillonite clay during the separation process owing to the difference in surface tension between vapor and liquid. Finally, the HTO vapor may cause T transfer to the lattice OH group of the montmorillonite clay. The self-radiolysis of HTO to HT or T2 is likely to proceed in the interlayer of the montmorillonite clay, resulting in the release of HT or T2 to the air. These observations suggest that introducing HTO vapor (not HTO liquid) into montmorillonite results in the efficient removal of HTO from H2O by our separation process.

Irradiation Time Dependence of Separation of HTO from H2O

The effects of the time of irradiation with artificial sunlight on the separation of HTO from H2O using the membrane distillation mechanism were next investigated. The T ratios in the water evaporated from H2O-impregnated montmorillonite clay were also analyzed simultaneously (see Table S3). Figure a shows the T ratios in the water condensate (solid triangles; C) recovered and the collected water (solid circles; D) as a function of irradiation time. As shown in the figure, the T ratio in the collected water (D) increased with irradiation time. The T ratio in the recovered condensed water (C) at 1.5 h was elevated by the 3 h irradiation and almost became constant after 6 h, indicating that HTO can no longer be captured by the lattice of montmorillonite after 6 h of irradiation. In the entire separation process, the T ratio of the water sampled from the remaining water (B) and the water condensate on the glass plate (A) decreased from 85.7 ± 0.9% to 66.2 ± 0.7% with increasing irradiation time, as shown in Figure b. The HTO used for the separation process was recovered from 92.8 ± 0.9 to 88 ± 1% from the collected water (D), the water condensate (C) recovered from montmorillonite, and other parts (A + B) (see Table S3) after the irradiation for 6 h. These results illustrate that HTO-containing H2O was absorbed in montmorillonite in the separation process after irradiation with artificial sunlight for 1.5 h, which led to the highly efficient separation of HTO. However, an irradiation time longer than 1.5 h reduced the efficiency of montmorillonite in capturing HTO. The volume changes of source water were 0.6, 1.1, and 2.0 g for the collected water of 0.5, 1.0, and 2.1 g before and after the separation process for 1.5, 3, and 6 h, respectively (see Figure S1). In the case of prolonged irradiation, the increase in the T ratio in the collected water means the decrease in separation efficiency, which was also observed in our previous experiment using four sets of overlaid filters, such as H2O-impregnated glass fiber and PF100 hydrophobic filters. Even in the case of using montmorillonite, results suggest that the prolongation of irradiation time reduced the efficiency of the separation of HTO from H2O. Since the maximum volume of H2O that can be absorbed into 1 g of montmorillonite was approximately 4 mL, further improvements in the system components are required in our future study.

In the separation process, the deterioration of SB-5 by decomposition and the PF-100 membrane filter caused by β-particles from T should be considered. We will study the stability of the separation process for long-term use in our next study. The used montmorillonite can be recovered by drying, and the dried montmorillonite can be used for the separation process because only the used montmorillonite absorbs and desorbs water in the separation process. However, the capacity of montmorillonite for HTO adsorption was not clear in the repeated cycles of the separation process. We will clarify the capacity of montmorillonite for HTO adsorption in our next study.

Conclusions

In this paper, we reported the improvement made to our previous process of separating HTO from H2O using an apparatus with a membrane distillation mechanism, with which H2O-impregnated glass fibers remove HTO by gas–liquid exchange. In this study, H2O-impregnated clays were incorporated instead of the H2O-impregnated glass fiber filters. Montmorillonite was found to be the most efficient among the clays employed for the separation process. The T ratio in the water collected in the vessel below the apparatus decreased as the volume of H2O added to montmorillonite increased, and the ratio could be reduced by about 10% when 4 mL of H2O was added to 1 g of montmorillonite. The layer structures of montmorillonite were significantly varied by including H2O, as shown by XRD analysis. The T ratio in the water condensate (C) from the montmorillonite impregnated with liquid HTO was found to moderately decrease in sealed-vial experiments at room temperature. On the other hand, although the T ratio in the water condensate (C) from the H2O-impregnated montmorillonite remained unchanged during the heating treatment at temperatures higher than 100 °C, the ratio in that from the montmorillonite used for the separation process gradually decreased during heating. These observations indicate that a certain volume of HTO was tightly captured in layer structures of montmorillonite, where HTO was loaded in the vapor state during the separation process by membrane distillation. The manner of loading HTO onto montmorillonite was also ascertained to be essential for the efficient removal of HTO from H2O. In our next study, the storage of HTO and its mechanism in the lattice H2O in the montmorillonite clay will be investigated by the self-radiolysis of the stored HTO.
