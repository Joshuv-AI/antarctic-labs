ABSTRACT
This white paper describes ongoing work and portfolios at Sandia National Laboratories that could
be leveraged in AI for electric grid applications. AI for electric grid applications will require
extensive research on trustworthy AI tools for deployment in critical infrastructure applications, and
Sandia has several key areas of expertise that can be leveraged for this challenge. This document
highlights several areas where Sandia has developed capabilities that can be used in future work to
increase the trustworthiness of our AI tools. These areas are human factors, uncertainty
quantification, explainability, and trust maturity framework. This report provides a look at future
collaboration opportunities within the AI for electric grid space at Sandia.

1. INTRODUCTION
Artificial Intelligence (AI) leveraged in the energy domain is increasingly important and plays a
significant role in the energy portfolio at Sandia National Laboratories (SNL). The U.S. Department
of Energy (DOE) has recently developed several roadmaps for how AI can advance energy
applications at a large scale [1], [2].

The Advanced Research Directions on AI for Energy, [1], sponsored by DOE and produced at
Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) with participation across the national laboratory complex
covers five areas, one of which is the Power Grid and will be the focus of this report. Within the
Power Grid Thrust, the report outlines several Grand Challenges including: 1) Realizing proactive,
real-time energy system operations; and 2) Building cyber- and all-hazards resilient and secure energy
systems.

In #1, the report calls out a huge increase in connected smart devices, increases in available data,
and the increasing complexity of control problems, and states that the grid “ . . . requires the latest
advances in AI techniques, which can analyze vast amounts of information, identify patterns, and
create forecasts in real time.” [1].

In #2, the report calls out aging infrastructure challenges combined with the grid’s increasing
complexity and external challenges such as extreme weather and cybersecurity vulnerabilities driving
broad disruptions in the power grid. “Harnessing AI provides the best opportunity to achieve a
cyber- and all-hazards resilient and secure grid by reducing blackouts and brownouts . . .” [1].

Critical infrastructure applications have been slower to adopt AI-based solutions than many other
industries due to the critical nature of the domain and the high cost of mistakes or false positives. A
great deal more research will be required on AI for electric grid topics before widespread adoption
can be achieved. The DOE report states, “While AI holds great promise in this area, grid operations
have a set of very specific requirements that AI, to date, has not been able to satisfy.” [1] Thus,
despite being likely required to process the vast quantities of data and complexities of a modern
electric grid, much of the AI-based technology is not yet suitable for implementation across the grid.

AI-related research that is specifically mentioned in [1] includes foundation models, surrogate
modeling, decision support, explainability, trustworthiness, verifiability, autonomy, orchestration,
continual learning, operator-in-the-loop, and new datasets.

Electric grid applications from [1] include predicting infrastructure vulnerabilities; predicting
equipment degradation; disturbance detection and classification; detecting intrusions; forecasting
weather, generation requirements, and load; state estimation, and control.

Even more recently, the Secretarial Order from Secretary of Energy Chris Wright [3], outlines nine
major objectives for the immediate future. Three of those objectives are directly relevant in this
context:
 1) Unleash American Energy Innovation
 a. AI will certainly play a significant role in the future electric grid
 2) Strengthen Grid Reliability and Security

 7

 a. AI tools are already being developed to enhance grid security and reliability in a
 variety of ways, and they will be important tools going forwards.
 3) Streamline Permitting and Identify Undue Burdens on American Energy
 a. AI tools can be an important way to assist in streamlining the permitting process and
 removing obstacles for modernization the electric grid.

This white paper outlines research directions and opportunities for SNL which directly align with
the strategies and roadmap specified in the DOE report [1] and outline above.

One key area of opportunity for Sandia is in AI trustworthiness. Although trustworthiness for AI is
necessary to be leveraged for critical infrastructure applications, industry is not incentivized to do
research in that area. For utilities it is prohibitively expensive and for major corporations doing AI
research, in many of their application areas, the requirements in terms of trustworthiness or
explainability are not as stringent as in critical infrastructure applications. This provides an
opportunity for the labs to address these critical issues as Sandia has significant experience in
assuring trustworthiness across several domains. Each of the areas outlined above will require the
trustworthiness element. Figure 1 shows the three thrusts focusing on trusted artificial intelligence.

2. OPPORTUNITIES IN AI FOR ELECTRIC GRID APPLICATIONS
 2.1. Introduction
Sandia National Labs has significant expertise in the electric power grid area, as well as applied AI,
but also has core competencies in a variety of other areas that can be leveraged to directly inform the
AI for electric grid research, particularly on the topic of trustworthiness. Figure 3 illustrates one
ontology for grouping topics within energy research. Sandia has expertise in all of these (and more),
and they are all potential applications for AI in energy.

Figure 3 - Topic areas for energy research and AI

The following are summaries of key areas of Sandia expertise with citations, not necessarily related
to energy research, but which could be leveraged for energy applications in the future.

 2.2. Human Factors

Sandia’s Human Systems departments have expertise in cognitive science, human factors, and
human-system interaction. The departments have a long history of research on human-AI teaming,
AI adoption, trust and use, and the development and evaluation of human-in-the-loop systems.
Much of the prior research in this area has been in the context of cyber security. For example, a
prior project used cognitive task analysis to evaluate the balance between automation and human
expertise in cyber incident response [4]. This work identified needs for training and future
technology development to support optimal human-system performance. Another project assessed

 10

how different types of explanations of a machine learning model’s outputs impacted users’ task
performance in a spam detection task [5].

Other projects have investigated the impact of AI/ML tools in domains such as international
nuclear safeguards. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) is seeking to incorporate
AI/ML tools into the workflows of safeguards inspectors and analysts. However, as there is no
tolerance for error in that domain, there is a great deal of interest in studying how AI errors impact
human assessments and performance. To address these questions, Sandia researchers conducted a
series of studies to investigate the impact of AI assistance on visual search and inspection tasks. One
line of experiments tested how different rates and types of AI errors impacted human performance
in an algorithm-assisted visual search task. These studies found that even models with relatively poor
performance could improve human performance relative to an unassisted baseline condition.
However, as the model performance improved, the users’ ability to detect model errors decreased
[6], [7]. The nature of the model errors also impacted human performance. It was much harder for
users to detect false negatives than false positives. A separate line of research examined factors that
influenced user reliance on an AI-based voice user interface in the context of a mock safeguards
inspection task [8].

Several ongoing projects are developing methods for characterizing the maturity and trustworthiness
of AI tools for use in national security

This growing body of research on human-AI interactions in support of high-consequence decision
making can be generalized to multiple domains, including AI for electric grid applications. Even
though AI technology is changing rapidly, the human side of the equation remains relatively
constant. Our research has identified several key factors that influence users’ perceptions about AI
tools and their willingness to rely on those models, as well as factors that influence their ability to
identify situations in which the AI outputs are inaccurate. Since these factors are rooted in common
patterns of human cognition, these findings can be extended to multiple domains and to ever-
evolving AI capabilities.

If AI-based tools will achieve widespread usage throughout the grid, defining and optimizing how
operators interact with those tools will be critical. Already our utility partners are asking us how
much training will be required to use AI tools once they are implemented. Other critical questions
we have heard include: How will an operator know how much trust to put in a tool? How will the
tool enhance decision making, rather than simply increasing the complexity? Human factors
research is required, alongside other technical development, to ensure that the tools created are
suitable to usage alongside human operators.

 2.3. Uncertainty Quantification
Sandia has a long history of research and experience in uncertainty quantification going back
decades. Work in [16] motivates the rigorous usage of uncertainty quantification within the AI
domain. “Providing uncertainty information may yield deeper insight into how a model produced
its results and the trustworthiness of that result.” Uncertainty quantification can help answer a
variety of critical questions such as: What are the sources of uncertainty in this dataset and tool?
What is the worst possible answer that this AI-based tool could give? How could tool developer
reduce the uncertainty in the tool? Could a larger (or different) training dataset reduce the output
uncertainty? Work discussed in [17] covers several ways that ‘credibility’ can be addressed in AI-
based tools, including the role of uncertainty quantification.

Two toolkits developed in-house are the Dakota platform and the UQ Toolkit. Dakota, [18],
enables iterative analysis and interrogation of a particular technology to provide rigorous UQ
metrics. Dakota was started in 1993 and has been continuously improved since then. The UQ
ToolKit (UQTk), [19], provides methods for propagating input uncertainties through computational
models, tools for sensitivity analysis, methods for spare surrogate construction, as well as Bayesian
inference tools.

Probabilistic modeling can be used for more than to quantify uncertainty, it can also quantify the
importance of the uncertain input parameters on the simulated metric. This can be used to help
guide additional characterization or research investments. Sandia has extensively leveraged these
types of performance assessments, for example for the nuclear waste repositories (e.g. Yucca
Mountain and WIPP).

 2.4. Explainability

Establishing confidence in artificial intelligence models is essential for their acceptance within the
scientific community. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) demystifies the model outputs by
illuminating decision-making process, thereby enabling informed trust. This insight can be obtained
through post-mortem model probing or embedded directly into model design.

The inherent design of causal models offers an explicit method for understanding their decision-
making processes. Causal models use directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) to enforce a one-way flow of
information through connected variables. Because DAGs contain no cycles, they identify precursors
to output variables, thereby enabling tasks such as root-cause analysis. Furthermore, when all
variables in the causal model are identified, the model is completely transparent to the users.

 13

Causal models have already been employed at Sandia National Laboratories to identify precursors in
materials science [21], [22] and climate science [23]. In [22], the authors discover key features in
multimodal materials data and a DAG that describes their interrelations, factorizing the joint
probability distribution. In [21] the authors provide a method for learning functional relationships
between variables while preserving a DAG structure, thereby enabling cause-and-effect
analysis. Reference [23] extends DAG model discovery to large space-time systems. Each approach
yields a clearly explainable, human-
interpretable model.

Other methods for explainable AI at Sandia
National Laboratories utilize DAGs to
perform model probing, such as in [24]. In
this work, the authors follow the flow of
information through the AI model to
understand the model's methods for its
computed output. This "chain of thought"
for the AI model highlights low-level causal
explanations for the network's actions.
These methods provide XAI measures in
models that cannot otherwise be reduced to
explicit causal DAG models.

In essence, causal modeling delivers explicit
explainability and robust root-cause
identification, making it well-suited
for high-consequence applications Figure 6 - Explainability in AI-based Tools
such as power grid event analysis. Work in [25] focuses on providing explainability in machine
learning using functional data by providing an explanation in the original data space of how the
model uses variability in the functional data for prediction. Research in [26] develops methods for
symbolic explainability in high-consequence applications. Finally, [27] presents methods Shapley-
based explainability metrics for AI-based tools.

Explainable results from AI models will be a key factor in adoption of these technologies. Human
operators, combined with AI tools, is the future of electric grid operations, and explainability will be
a key aspect in ensuring that AI tools and human operators can work together. The AI tools need to
provide decision support to a human operator and be as transparent as possible in how a result or
recommendation was obtained.

 2.5. Evaluating Trustworthiness of AI Systems for High-Consequence
 Applications

Modern AI systems offer a suite of capabilities ranging from predictive modeling of complex
systems to the acceleration or automation of human workflows. These capabilities include tasks that
in human organizations may require evaluation of the reasoning and evidence underlying a response.
However, the design, scale, and complexity of these systems make determining whether their
outputs should be trusted difficult or even unfeasible.

 14

Definitions of trust range across the literature (e.g. [28], [29], [30], [31]), but we consider trust to be
the user’s willingness to use outputs of the AI system or incorporate them into their decision-
making processes despite the risk of this use [28]. Trustworthiness is the AI system’s ability to
facilitate user trust in relation to a target task and user needs (i.e. a characteristic of the system,
driving how much the user trust should trust it) [30]. Determining the alignment of an AI system’s
capabilities with a user’s needs is necessary for a user to have calibrated trust: The user should trust
the system as much as is appropriate to avoid overuse, disuse, misuse, or abuse [32]. This is currently
a daunting task requiring deep technical familiarity with the AI system in question and expertise
across a range of research areas. At Sandia, we have developed the Trust Calibration Maturity Model
(TCMM) [33] to facilitate well-calibrated trust in AI systems for high consequence settings, which
has begun to be applied to various projects including a Nuclear Regulatory Report on the safety of
sodium purification monitoring software [34].

The TCMM is composed of five dimensions that drive user trust in an AI System: Performance
Characterization, Bias & Robustness, Transparency, Safety & Security, and Usability. Each
dimension is a consolidation of various terms with significant semantic overlap found in a survey of
trust frameworks. An AI system’s maturity along each dimension is scored as 1 (unaddressed), 2
(basic), 3 (systematic), or 4 (comprehensive) based on whether it fulfills the criteria for that
dimension score (see Table 1).

The TCMM is designed to facilitate clear understanding around both system capabilities and user
needs between customers and the developers of an AI system. Individual level criteria can be broken
out and discussed for targeted development as well as the costs associated with that work. For some
tasks and modalities, criteria cannot currently be met for levels 3 or 4 without additional research.
Clarity around current capabilities and mission needs is essential for the safe integration of AI
systems into national security workflows. The TCMM was developed by the Steel Thread venture
(NA-22), Human-Machine Teaming Thrust.

Utility and industry partners want to know how trustworthy a particular AI-based tool is and how
we know it’s trustworthy, but there has not been a defined framework to discuss these issues. The
Trust Calibration Maturity Model, developed at Sandia, provides such a framework. The fact that it

 16

is similar to a TRL framework provides a level of familiarity while simultaneously providing the
dimensions and language to talk about different aspects of maturity and trust for AI tools. This tool
provides an explicit way to discuss different aspects of trustworthiness and their relative maturity.
For example, a tool might be very mature in the ‘performance characterization’ dimension but
lacking in the ‘transparency’ or ‘bias/robustness quantification’ dimensions.

As we have worked with utility partners, questions around trustworthiness of our tools are common,
but often we lack the precise terminology to discuss different axes of trustworthiness and maturity
for AI tools. This type of framework provides both a definition of axes for discussion and more
precise vocabulary to guide these discussions.

3. SUMMARY

AI-based tools will require a significant investment in different aspects of trustworthiness before
they can be deployed in critical infrastructure applications. Sandia has extensive experience in
assuring trustworthiness across many dimensions and applications. Uncertainty quantification,
explainability, human factors, and trust maturity frameworks are just a few examples where Sandia
has expertise that can be leveraged to enhance AI tools for energy applications in future work.
