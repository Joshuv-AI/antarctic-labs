Clear aligner therapy for adult anterior open bite: a systematic review, descriptive evidence map, and certainty appraisal of reported dentoskeletal changes
Authors: Xinqi Huang, Mengling Lei, Juntong Liao, Zhimao Huang, Zhihe Zhao, Xiao Cen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClear aligner therapy is increasingly used for adult anterior open-bite correction, but the certainty of evidence, reported dentoskeletal changes, and clinical boundaries of this approach remain unclear. This systematic review aimed to summarize overbite change and descriptively map the dentoskeletal findings reported in the current clinical literature.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted according to PRISMA. PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library were searched. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also checked, but no additional eligible records were identified. Eligible studies evaluated adult or predominantly adult cohorts with anterior open bite treated with clear aligners and reported overbite change or dentoskeletal outcomes. Risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I and RoB 2. Reported dentoskeletal findings were classified as direct, indirect, or insufficient evidence for mechanistic interpretation. Exploratory single-arm random-effects meta-analyses were performed when extractable completed-treatment data were available. Certainty of evidence was appraised using GRADE principles.ResultsTen clinical studies involving 421 aligner-treated participants were included. Most studies were retrospective and nonrandomized, and nine had serious overall risk of bias. The primary exploratory synthesis of five completed-treatment cohorts showed a pooled overbite increase of 3.07 mm, while an extended sensitivity analysis of seven studies yielded 2.89 mm. Heterogeneity was substantial. Reported dentoskeletal changes most commonly involved anterior dentoalveolar changes, variable posterior vertical change, and limited mandibular plane change. Most studies were not designed to test mechanisms directly; therefore, the dentoskeletal findings were interpreted cautiously. The certainty of evidence was very low.ConclusionsVery low-certainty evidence suggests that selected adult anterior open-bite cases treated with clear aligners may show overbite improvement. However, the available evidence is insufficient to confirm predictable skeletal correction or to validate a single mechanism of bite closure. Future studies should use prospective comparative designs, standardized anterior open-bite phenotyping, direct component-level outcome assessment, blinded evaluation, and long-term retention endpoints.Trial registration CRD420261376480Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09379-z.

Introduction

Anterior open bite is a challenging vertical malocclusion because its diagnosis, treatment planning, and retention are influenced by multiple interacting factors, including skeletal divergence, dentoalveolar compensation, oral habits, airway or soft-tissue influences, and neuromuscular function [1–4]. In adults, treatment may be further complicated by the absence of remaining growth potential. Clinical management therefore depends on the severity and phenotype of the open bite, ranging from dentoalveolar camouflage and posterior vertical control to skeletal anchorage-assisted intrusion or orthognathic surgery in more severe skeletal cases [3, 5–9]. For clear aligner therapy, a central clinical uncertainty is how reported overbite improvement should be interpreted: as dental compensation, posterior vertical control, mandibular autorotation, skeletal correction, or a combination of these changes.

Clear aligner therapy has become increasingly used in adult orthodontics because of its esthetic and removable characteristics. In the context of anterior open-bite correction, aligners may offer several theoretical advantages, including occlusal coverage of the posterior teeth, programmed anterior extrusion, attachment-assisted tooth movement, and the possibility of integrating elastics or staged vertical control into the digital treatment plan [10, 11]. However, movement predictability remains variable. Extrusion is generally considered one of the less predictable aligner movements, and the extent to which posterior vertical control translates into clinically meaningful molar intrusion or mandibular autorotation remains uncertain [11–17].

Several clinical studies have reported anterior open-bite correction with clear aligners in adult or predominantly adult cohorts [18–27]. These studies collectively suggest that overbite improvement may be achievable in selected patients, but they do not describe a uniform biological or biomechanical mechanism. Some investigations emphasize incisor extrusion and incisor inclination changes, others report limited molar intrusion or posterior vertical control, and comparative studies suggest that aligner-based correction may differ from skeletal anchorage-assisted posterior intrusion [18–27]. Therefore, the clinical meaning of a pooled overbite increase cannot be interpreted from the millimetric value alone; the same amount of bite closure may reflect different combinations of anterior dentoalveolar compensation, posterior vertical change, and mandibular autorotation.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis quantified the efficacy of clear aligner therapy for adult anterior open-bite correction and reported an average overbite improvement of approximately 2.76 mm [28]. While this finding supports the clinical effectiveness of aligners in selected cases, a purely efficacy-focused interpretation leaves several clinically important questions unresolved. In particular, clinicians need to understand whether the observed correction is primarily dental or skeletal, which treatment components are most consistently reported, and under what clinical circumstances the pooled treatment effect should or should not be extrapolated.

Accordingly, this review was designed not as a mechanistic validation study, but as an evidence map and certainty appraisal of the current clinical literature. The primary objective was to summarize the available evidence on overbite change and reported dentoskeletal treatment effects in adult anterior open-bite patients treated with clear aligners. The secondary objective was to evaluate how confidently these findings can be interpreted in light of study design, risk of bias, heterogeneity, cephalometric measurement limitations, and contextual evidence from adjacent studies on vertical control and skeletal anchorage.

Materials and methods

Protocol and reporting standards

This systematic review, descriptive evidence map, and exploratory quantitative synthesis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [29]. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420261376480). The review question was structured around adult anterior open-bite patients treated with clear aligners, with overbite increase as the main quantitative outcome and reported dentoskeletal changes as the main descriptive mapping focus.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined according to the PICOS framework (Table 1). Studies were eligible if they evaluated adult patients or predominantly adult cohorts with anterior open bite treated primarily with clear aligner therapy and reported clinical, cephalometric, or cone-beam computed tomography outcomes related to bite closure. For mixed-age cohorts, the available age distribution or study description had to support classification of the population as predominantly adult. Randomized controlled trials and nonrandomized clinical studies were considered eligible if they reported overbite change or dentoskeletal treatment effects. Comparative studies were retained if the clear aligner arm could be evaluated independently.

PICOS eligibility criteria

Studies were excluded if they were case reports, small descriptive case series without analytical design, narrative reviews, scoping reviews, predictability-only studies without clinically usable treatment-effect data, stability-only reports, or mixed malocclusion samples without separable anterior open-bite data. Adolescent first-phase cohorts were excluded because their populations and treatment stages were not considered clinically comparable with adult completed-treatment cohorts.

Information sources and search strategy

Electronic searches of PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library were performed for studies published from January 2010 to April 30, 2026. The final search was conducted on April 30, 2026. The search strategy combined terms related to clear aligner therapy and terms related to anterior open bite or vertical control. The core search concepts included clear aligner, clear aligners, Invisalign, aligner therapy, clear orthodontic appliances, open bite, anterior open bite, vertical control, anterior extrusion, posterior intrusion, and molar intrusion. Complete database-specific search strategies are provided in Additional file 1.

Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were checked manually, but no additional eligible records were identified. No language restriction was applied at the search stage; however, full-text interpretability and availability of extractable clinical or cephalometric data were required for final synthesis.

Study selection

All records identified from database searches were imported into EndNote, and duplicates were removed before screening. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers using Microsoft Excel. Potentially eligible articles were retrieved for full-text assessment. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Reasons for exclusion at full-text assessment were categorized according to study design, population comparability, outcome relevance, and availability of extractable anterior open-bite-specific data.

Data extraction and evidence mapping

Data were extracted using a standardized extraction form. Extracted variables included study characteristics, study design, sample size, age, anterior open-bite sample context, comparator group when present, treatment duration or endpoint, imaging modality, and role in evidence synthesis. For quantitative synthesis, the extracted variables were sample size, mean overbite increase, standard deviation of the change score, and analysis set. When comparative studies were eligible, the clear aligner arm was extracted separately whenever possible.

A second layer of extraction focused on reported dentoskeletal components of anterior open-bite correction. Extracted variables included anterior dentoalveolar changes, incisor extrusion or inclination changes, posterior vertical control or molar intrusion, mandibular autorotation, mandibular plane angle change, skeletal response, and whether the study compared clear aligners with fixed appliances, miniscrew-assisted molar intrusion, miniplate-supported intrusion, or temporary anchorage device-based mechanics.

For the evidence map, reported dentoskeletal findings were classified as direct, indirect, or insufficient for mechanistic interpretation. Direct evidence referred to component-level cephalometric or CBCT measurements reported by the original study. Indirect evidence referred to inference from related measurements, treatment comparisons, or author interpretation. Insufficient evidence referred to studies reporting overbite improvement without adequate component-level data. This classification was used to guide the evidence map and to avoid attributing overbite change to unsupported mechanisms.

All included studies were classified within an evidence-map framework according to their role in the synthesis: completed-treatment quantitative evidence, comparative descriptive evidence, and interim or short-term evidence.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed according to study design. Nonrandomized studies were assessed using ROBINS-I [30]. Because the evidence base included retrospective single-arm and comparative cohort studies, ROBINS-I was applied pragmatically to the nonrandomized evidence. The randomized controlled trial was assessed using RoB 2 [31]. Overall judgments were summarized in the main text, and domain-level judgments are provided in Additional file 2.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for key outcomes was appraised using GRADE principles. Outcomes assessed included overbite increase, incisor extrusion or inclination change, molar intrusion or posterior vertical control, mandibular autorotation or mandibular plane change, and predictable skeletal correction. Certainty judgments considered risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication or selection bias. Because most included studies were retrospective, nonrandomized, and heterogeneous, the quantitative estimates and reported dentoskeletal changes were interpreted as descriptive signals rather than confirmatory evidence.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

All included studies were first summarized qualitatively using the evidence-map framework. Exploratory single-arm meta-analysis was performed only for completed-treatment cohorts that reported extractable mean overbite change and dispersion data. The primary exploratory synthesis included studies judged to provide the most directly comparable completed-treatment data. An extended sensitivity analysis added two further studies with extractable overbite-change data to test the robustness of the pooled estimate.

A random-effects inverse-variance model was used to pool mean overbite increase in millimeters. Heterogeneity was assessed using tau-squared, chi-square, and I² statistics. Studies reporting interim endpoints, stability-only outcomes, or insufficient variance data were excluded from the primary exploratory meta-analysis. Because fewer than 10 studies were pooled, publication bias was not formally assessed. Given the single-arm before-after structure of the pooled data, the estimates were interpreted descriptively. Results

Study selection

The electronic database search identified 252 records. After removal of 121 duplicate records, 131 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 111 records were excluded, and 20 reports identified through database searching underwent full-text assessment. One additional report identified through citation searching was assessed and excluded because eligibility of the predominantly adult population could not be confirmed. Ten clinical studies were ultimately included in the qualitative evidence map, and seven studies provided extractable data for exploratory quantitative synthesis (Fig. 1). Full-text excluded studies and the primary reasons for exclusion are provided in Additional file 3.

Study characteristics and evidence-map classification

The evidence map showed that the available literature addressed several distinct but overlapping questions. Five studies provided completed-treatment data suitable for the primary exploratory quantitative synthesis. Two additional studies provided extractable overbite-change data and were included in the extended sensitivity analysis. Several comparative studies contributed primarily to descriptive interpretation because they contrasted clear aligner therapy with fixed appliances, miniplate-supported posterior intrusion, or temporary anchorage device-based posterior intrusion. Studies that lacked extractable change-score dispersion data, had interim endpoints, or primarily addressed comparative treatment components rather than single-arm treatment effects were retained for qualitative synthesis only.

Descriptive map of reported dentoskeletal changes

Reported dentoskeletal changes are summarized in (Table 4). Because most included studies were retrospective and were not designed to test mechanisms directly, this section describes reported anterior, posterior, and mandibular changes rather than validating mechanisms of bite closure. Across the included studies, anterior open-bite correction with clear aligners was not attributable to a single uniform pattern. Reported findings most commonly involved anterior dentoalveolar changes, variable posterior vertical change or limited molar intrusion, and limited mandibular plane change.

Across the included studies, directly reported anterior dentoalveolar changes were more consistently available than direct measures of posterior vertical change or mandibular rotation. Several studies reported incisor extrusion, incisor inclination change, molar intrusion, or mandibular plane angle reduction, but reporting was heterogeneous and mostly retrospective. Comparative studies suggested that clear aligner therapy and skeletal anchorage-based protocols should not be considered mechanistically equivalent. Overall, the evidence map supports a cautious interpretation: adult anterior open-bite correction with clear aligners appears to involve mixed dentoalveolar changes, whereas predictable skeletal correction has not been established.

Exploratory quantitative synthesis

Exploratory quantitative synthesis was performed only to describe the magnitude of reported overbite change in studies with extractable completed-treatment data; it was not interpreted as confirmatory evidence of treatment effectiveness. The treatment-effect data used for exploratory quantitative synthesis are shown in (Table 2).

The primary exploratory dataset included five completed-treatment cohorts with extractable overbite-change means and standard deviations. Across these studies, the reported mean overbite increase ranged from 2.28 mm to 4.26 mm. The extended sensitivity dataset added Moshiri et al. [18] and Burashed [23], resulting in seven studies with 322 participants available for sensitivity analysis.

The primary exploratory random-effects synthesis included five studies and 206 patients [19–21, 25, 27]. The pooled mean overbite increase was 3.07 mm (95% CI, 2.46 to 3.69 mm), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 93%). This estimate should be interpreted in light of the selected cohorts, single-arm data structure, and substantial heterogeneity. The forest plot for the primary analysis is shown in (Fig. 2).

The certainty of evidence for all key outcomes was very low (Table 5). Publication bias could not be formally assessed because fewer than 10 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis. Therefore, both pooled estimates and reported dentoskeletal changes were interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

This review extends the previous efficacy-focused synthesis by mapping the reported dentoskeletal components of anterior open-bite correction and appraising the certainty of these findings. The pooled overbite increase was 3.07 mm in the primary exploratory analysis and 2.89 mm in the extended sensitivity analysis, close to the 2.76-mm estimate reported previously [28]. However, the included studies were heterogeneous and mostly nonrandomized, and they did not establish a single mechanism of bite closure. These pooled values should therefore be interpreted as descriptive estimates from selected cohorts rather than as reliable predictions of individual treatment response.

The interpretation of a pooled overbite increase is clinically complex because the same numerical change may arise from different dentoskeletal components. A 3-mm improvement in overbite may represent extrusion of anterior teeth, limited intrusion or vertical control of posterior teeth, retroclination of incisors, mandibular counterclockwise autorotation, or a combination of these effects. Across the included studies, a purely skeletal correction pattern was not consistently demonstrated. Instead, the reported findings were more consistent with a composite dentoalveolar correction pattern. This distinction is essential because dentoalveolar compensation may be appropriate in mild-to-moderate adult anterior open bite, whereas severe skeletal open bite may require more direct skeletal vertical correction.

Anterior dentoalveolar change was one of the most consistently reported components of correction. Several studies described incisor extrusion or changes in incisor inclination as contributors to bite closure [18, 20, 21, 27]. This finding is clinically plausible because many adult anterior open-bite cases treated with aligners are managed through nonextraction protocols and digital staging of anterior extrusion. However, extrusion is also recognized as one of the less predictable aligner movements in the general clear aligner literature [10, 16]. Therefore, the presence of incisor extrusion in successful anterior open-bite cohorts should not be interpreted as evidence that programmed extrusion will be fully expressed in all patients. It instead suggests that anterior dentoalveolar compensation is a major pathway through which aligner treatment may improve overbite when case selection and biomechanics are favorable.

Posterior vertical control was another relevant, but less uniform, component of correction. Some studies reported molar intrusion or posterior vertical changes during aligner therapy [18, 20, 27], whereas others suggested that the posterior contribution may depend on the patient’s sagittal and vertical phenotype [21]. The theoretical bite-block effect of full-coverage aligners may contribute to posterior vertical control, but contextual evidence indicates that this should not be overstated. Rask et al. found that available evidence did not support a claim that clear aligner therapy provides better vertical dimension control than fixed appliances in adult nonextraction treatment [32]. Accordingly, posterior vertical control after aligner treatment should be interpreted as possible and protocol-dependent, rather than predictable or equivalent to skeletal anchorage-assisted intrusion.

Mandibular autorotation was reported or inferred in several studies, usually as a secondary response to anterior and posterior dentoalveolar changes rather than as evidence of major skeletal correction [18, 20, 21, 27]. In adult patients, reduction in mandibular plane angle or counterclockwise mandibular rotation may improve overbite and facial proportions, but the magnitude of this change is likely limited when treatment is performed without skeletal anchorage or orthognathic surgery. In addition, interpretation of mandibular autorotation and mandibular plane angle change should be tempered by potential cephalometric superimposition variability. Ovard et al. showed that the same initial and final cephalograms may be interpreted differently according to subjective superimposition, even if strong systematic bias was not detected [33]. Therefore, small angular changes should not be interpreted as robust skeletal treatment effects unless confirmed by standardized blinded assessment or three-dimensional evaluation.

The comparative studies provide important clinical context. Clear aligners and skeletal anchorage-assisted protocols may both improve anterior open bite, but they should not be assumed to represent the same treatment pathway [19, 22, 24, 25]. Miniplate- or temporary anchorage device-supported posterior intrusion is designed to directly reduce posterior dentoalveolar height and promote mandibular counterclockwise rotation. In contrast, aligner-based treatment appears to rely more heavily on dentoalveolar compensation, including incisor extrusion, incisor inclination changes, and limited posterior vertical control. Hamilton’s dissertation is consistent with this distinction by suggesting that clear aligner open-bite closure is primarily associated with incisor extrusion, whereas skeletal anchorage more directly affects skeletal-vertical change and preserves incisor position [34].

The high heterogeneity observed in both exploratory quantitative analyses reinforces the need for cautious interpretation. Heterogeneity likely reflected variation in baseline anterior open-bite severity, skeletal pattern, sagittal classification, extraction status, treatment objectives, attachment protocols, use of elastics, degree of planned molar intrusion, imaging modality, and endpoint definition. For example, a cone-beam computed tomography-based study may detect three-dimensional posterior and incisor changes that are not captured in the same way by two-dimensional cephalometry [27]. Similarly, studies that compare attachment designs or skeletal anchorage protocols address different clinical questions from single-arm completed-treatment cohorts [22, 25].

The risk-of-bias profile also limits the strength of the conclusions. Most included studies were retrospective and nonrandomized, and all nonrandomized studies were judged to have a serious overall risk of bias. Confounding, participant selection, deviations from intended interventions, and selective reporting were the main concerns. These limitations are particularly relevant in clear aligner research because patient compliance, provider experience, case difficulty, attachment protocols, refinement stages, and adjunctive biomechanics may all influence treatment response. The only randomized trial provided useful comparative information but reported an interim 6-month endpoint, limiting direct integration with completed-treatment cohorts.

The main strengths of this review are the separation of completed-treatment quantitative evidence from comparative and interim evidence, the descriptive mapping of reported dentoskeletal changes, and the explicit appraisal of evidence certainty. This framework reduces the risk of interpreting heterogeneous overbite changes as confirmation of a uniform or predictable skeletal mechanism.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The number of eligible studies was small, and publication bias could not be assessed formally because fewer than 10 studies were included in the exploratory meta-analysis. The quantitative synthesis depended on the availability of extractable mean change and dispersion data, which restricted pooling to a subset of the evidence base. The included studies differed in imaging modality, endpoint definition, planned movement protocols, attachment use, and reporting of tooth-level movements. Long-term stability, root resorption, periodontal outcomes, refinement burden, and patient-reported outcomes could not be meta-analyzed. Finally, because most data came from selected adult cohorts, the findings should not be generalized to growing patients, severe skeletal anterior open bite, or complex extraction and surgical cases.

Future research should prioritize prospective comparative studies with standardized anterior open-bite phenotype definitions, completed-treatment endpoints, and long-term retention follow-up. Studies should report dental and skeletal components separately, including incisor extrusion, incisor inclination, molar intrusion, mandibular plane angle, lower anterior facial height, and refinement requirements. Blinded cephalometric superimposition or three-dimensional assessment should be considered when mandibular autorotation or skeletal vertical change is a key outcome. Importantly, future investigations should distinguish between programmed movement, achieved movement, and clinically stable correction.

Conclusions

Very low-certainty evidence indicates an average observed overbite increase of approximately 3 mm in selected adult anterior open-bite cohorts treated with clear aligners. Reported dentoskeletal changes are more consistent with dentoalveolar compensation than with predictable skeletal correction. However, the available studies are few, heterogeneous, and mostly at serious risk of bias; therefore, the pooled estimate should be interpreted as descriptive rather than confirmatory. Future studies should use prospective comparative designs, standardized anterior open-bite phenotyping, direct component-level outcome assessment, blinded cephalometric or three-dimensional evaluation, and long-term retention endpoints.
