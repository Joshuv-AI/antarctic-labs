Idiopathic small bowel volvulus in an adult without prior surgery: a case report
Authors: Sophia Mathew Masha, Ayubu Mathias Kwandikwa, Tatana Museketwa Faustin, Olivier Iryivuze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Small bowel volvulus (SBV) is a rare but potentially life-threatening surgical emergency if not detected and treated on time. Primary or idiopathic SBV, occurring in the absence of any predisposing anatomical lesion, remains an uncommon clinical entity, especially in adults, accounting for a very small percentage of small bowel obstructions (SBOs) in non-volvulus-endemic regions and presenting a diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific symptoms, lack of prior surgical history, or obvious risk factors.Case presentation:We report a case of a 30-year-old African man with 3 days of bowel obstruction symptoms, no prior surgical history, and clinical findings of agitation, fever, tachycardia, mild tachypnea, neutrophilia, leukocytosis, and severe hemoconcentration. Imaging revealed multiple air-fluid levels and a “sigma sign” without free air. Emergency laparotomy identified an ischemic small bowel forming a 40-cm closed loop, twisted clockwise by 360° along the mesentery, 92 cm from the ileo-cecal junction.Discussion:Although idiopathic, primary SBV has been linked to dietary and cultural habits, such as the sudden consumption of large meals after fasting, and lifestyle factors, including heavy alcohol consumption and smoking, which may alter visceral motility. Given the limited literature on SBV in a virgin abdomen, adherence to available guidelines remains crucial.Conclusion:Idiopathic SBV in adults without prior surgery is rare and diagnostically challenging, often presenting with nonspecific symptoms. Clinical vigilance and prompt surgical intervention remain essential, as imaging should not delay treatment in critically ill patients. Systematic documentation of such cases is needed to better define risk factors and strategies for recurrence prevention. In the present case, timely surgery led to a favorable postoperative recovery.

Introduction

Small bowel obstruction (SBO) is the most common form of intestinal obstruction; however, small bowel volvulus (SBV) represents a relatively rare etiology. SBV can be classified into two categories: primary SBV and secondary SBV. Primary SBV is exceedingly rare and often idiopathic. Although its pathophysiology is not fully understood, it has been associated with certain dietary and cultural practices, such as the sudden ingestion of large bulky meals following prolonged fasting, which may precipitate rapid bowel filling and torsion[1]. Chronic constipation and motility disorders have also been implicated, as they can lead to segmental stasis[2]. Lifestyle factors, including heavy alcohol consumption and smoking, have been reported to contribute to altered visceral motility[3].

In contrast, secondary SBV arises from identifiable causes such as Ladd’s bands, postoperative or infectious adhesions, intra-abdominal tumors or cysts[4], or congenital malrotation resulting from abnormal intestinal rotation[5]. It can occur in adults and may present with either chronic or acute symptoms[6].

Both primary and secondary SBV result from torsion of the small bowel around its mesentery, typically ranging from 180° to 720°. This torsion leads to luminal obstruction, venous congestion, arterial compromise, and, in severe cases, peritonitis, sepsis, and death. SBV is relatively uncommon in Western countries but occurs more frequently in Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, accounting for 1–6% of SBO cases, largely due to habitual and cultural practices[3]. Although rare, SBV can cause severe complications if not promptly recognized and treated. Existing guidelines provide a framework for management[7].

We present the case of a 30-year-old African man with no prior surgical history, who developed SBO secondary to SBV. This case has been documented in accordance with the SCARE guidelines[8].

Case presentation

We present a case of a 30-year-old African man who arrived at our rural hospital’s Accident and Emergency department with severe acute abdominal pain, vomiting, and obstipation for 3 days. He had a 3-month history of intermittent constipation with occasional blood-stained stools, which was managed medically. There was no such history among family members. He is a smoker and consumes up to 10 bottles of local beer daily. Generally, he was dehydrated and in severe pain, with a pain scale of 8/10. His respiratory rate was 24 cycles per minute, his blood pressure was 150/96 mm Hg, his body temperature was 39.8°C, and his SPO2 was 96% in room air. His pulse rate was 112 beats per minute, regular, synchronous with normal volume, and there was no radio-femoral delay. There was no obvious peristalsis, pulsations, scars from prior surgeries, or noticeable veins. The abdomen was mildly symmetrically distended and moved with respiration. The umbilicus was inverted. There was no palpable mass, and the abdomen was tense. An empty rectum was discovered on digital rectal examination. Every five bowel sounds per minute were audible. Multiple air-fluid levels and no air beneath the diaphragm were visible on an erect plain abdomen X-ray (Fig. 1). There was no access to other imaging modalities. Correlating the clinical state of the patient with the radiological findings, a diagnosis of bowel obstruction was made, and a decision for laparotomy was taken. Following patient consent, initial resuscitation was performed, including the insertion of a nasogastric tube for decompression and the administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics. An emergency exploratory laparotomy was performed under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, we found that the small bowel formed a 40 cm closed loop, 92 cm from the ileo-cecal junction, and rotated 360° clockwise (Fig. 2). It was dilated, edematous, and ischemic. No congenital bands, adhesions, internal hernia, mesenteric defects, Meckel’s diverticulum, or malrotation were found upon exploration. We also observed a small tear in the outer mesothelium of the mesentery, which was repaired. No bands or malrotation were noted. The patient had an uneventful recovery.

Discussion

An SBO can trigger a dangerous cascade of events, beginning with bowel distension and potentially progressing to life-threatening complications such as ischemia, strangulation, perforation, peritonitis, and sepsis. If untreated, these may culminate in multi-organ failure and death. Epidemiologically, SBV in adults has a higher incidence in Africa, Asia, and the Middle East compared to Western countries, with rates ranging from 24 to 60 per 100 000 population, versus 1.7–5.7 per 100 000 in North America and Western Europe. In some African countries, including Ethiopia, Uganda, and Kenya, SBV accounts for 1–6% of intestinal obstruction cases, often associated with high mortality due to complications[2,3].

We report a case of idiopathic SBV successfully managed surgically. Timely intervention was undertaken to prevent complications. Because of initial ischemic changes, enteropexy was not performed. Secondary SBV typically arises from tumors, hernias, congenital anomalies, or postoperative adhesions[7]. In contrast, our case involved no pre-existing anatomical abnormalities. The nonspecific clinical presentation makes diagnosis challenging[8]. Patients without prior surgical history may present with acute abdominal pain, agitation, tenderness, and even shock[4,7]. Computed tomography of the abdomen is valuable for diagnosis, often revealing dilated small bowel loops and the characteristic “whirl sign,” representing twisted mesenteric vessels and surrounding fat attenuation[9–11]. However, it was not available at our facility, and we relied on the clinical findings and the X-ray.

Constipation, a recognized risk factor for colonic volvulus, may also predispose individuals to SBV, as suggested in our case and supported by previous conjectures[12]. Additional proposed risk factors include the ingestion of a large meal after fasting[1] and the consumption of large amounts of “Kongo,” a local beer reported to contain high serotonin levels. A 1976 case study in northern Uganda suggested that this may precipitate volvulus in approximately 75% of cases. However, due to the rarity of primary SBV and the absence of large case series, these hypotheses remain unsubstantiated.

Management of primary/idiopathic SBV is strictly surgical, in line with the World Society of Emergency Surgery recommendations. Prompt surgery is essential to reduce morbidity and mortality. Preoperative supportive care includes fluid resuscitation and nasogastric decompression[10]. While laparoscopy may be considered, laparotomy remains the gold standard. For non-viable bowel, resection with anastomosis at viable margins is performed. Preventive strategies for recurrence remain controversial. Detorsion alone carries a recurrence risk of approximately 30%. Enteropexy is an option but carries a risk of fistula formation[6]. Preventive mesenteric fixation may increase vascular injury, prolong hospitalization, and raise postoperative morbidity, though some authors advocate for extensive resection[5]. None of these approaches is supported by robust human studies.

In summary, we present a rare case of idiopathic SBV that was successfully treated with prompt surgical devolvulation. Given the critical condition in which most patients present, retrospective studies focusing on clinical history and survival are warranted. Larger sample sizes are needed to clarify the pathophysiology of primary SBV and to establish evidence-based guidelines for effective management and prevention of recurrence, as none currently exist.

Conclusions

Idiopathic SBV in adults, particularly without prior surgery, is rare and poses significant diagnostic challenges, as some patients may present with only mild or nonspecific symptoms. Clinical vigilance is therefore essential, and timely surgical intervention remains the cornerstone of management. While imaging can support diagnosis when available, it should not delay operative treatment in critically ill patients. Further research and systematic case documentation are required to elucidate risk factors and develop strategies for preventing recurrence. The patient described in this report achieved a favorable postoperative recovery.
