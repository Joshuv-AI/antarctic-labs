PRKN mediates the ubiquitination of SCAF8 to reduce the mRNA stability of KLF5 and its transcriptional activation of EFNA3 in colorectal cancer
Authors: Ye Song, Yangsheng Yu, Bo Zhang, Xiao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDysregulation in glycolysis within the tumor microenvironment is a hallmark in the context of colorectal cancer (CRC). We aimed to identify critical drivers of glycolysis in CRC and decipher the underlying mechanism.MethodsThrough combined gene interference in CRC cells using lentivirus and treatment with glycolysis activator DASA-58, we measured glycolytic flux to evaluate how molecular mechanisms influence tumor cell activity via glycolysis. CRC cells were cocultured with NK and CD8+ T cells to analyze the anti-tumor immune response. A patient-derived xenograft mouse model and a CMT93 cell-derived allograft mouse model were developed for analyzing CRC growth, metastasis, and immune evasion.ResultsEFNA3 was elevated in CRC tissues and cell lines. The glycolytic activity, proliferative, migratory, invasive, and pro-angiogenic effects of the CRC cells were reduced, and the anti-tumor response of the CD8+ T and NK cells was enhanced after EFNA3 knockdown in CRC cells. Tumors formed by CMT93 cells with EFNA3 knockdown exhibited reduced metastasis and an increased proportion of anti-tumor immune cells infiltrated. Overexpression of KLF5 promoted EFNA3 transcription, and SCAF8 enhanced KLF5 mRNA stability. Downregulation of PRKN in CRC enhanced the SCAF8 protein expression through ubiquitination. PRKN upregulation blocked glycolysis and suppressed the malignant behavior of CRC cells, whereas the anticancer ability of PRKN was reversed by SCAF8/KLF5/EFNA3 overexpression.ConclusionsActivating the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of PRKN to suppress EFNA3 expression represents a promising therapeutic strategy for the clinical treatment of CRC.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00974-0.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), whose major manifestation is a malignancy derived from the glandular epithelium of the colon (COAD) and rectum (READ), ranks third and second in incidence and cancer-related mortality, respectively, in the world [1]. Metabolic reprogramming is a key hallmark of tumors, and there is a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis, which facilitates tumor immune evasion and the formation of a suppressive tumor immune microenvironment [2]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) includes the surrounding blood vessels, immune cells, fibroblasts, myeloid-derived inflammatory cells, various signaling molecules, the extracellular matrix, and immune cells, mainly encompassing macrophages, neutrophils, natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells, T lymphocytes, and B lymphocytes, which participate in the initiation and progression of tumors [3].

The Eph/Ephrin (EFN) family is key to many developmental processes and an important mediator of cell proliferation, differentiation, adhesion, migration, and death, and EFNA3, a member of this group, was elevated in pan-cancer and negatively correlated with the infiltration of CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils in gastric cancer [4]. Not surprisingly, EFNA3 expression was correlated with overall survival and progression-free survival in patients with lung adenocarcinoma, and it can regulate the glycolysis of lung adenocarcinoma cells [5]. However, there remains a lack of clarity on the function and underlying mechanisms of EFNA3 in CRC. Our preliminary bioinformatics prediction revealed the negative correlation between EFNA3 and the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and NK cells in CRC and the transcriptional regulation of EFNA3 by Krüppel-like factor 5 (KLF5). KLF5, whose expression and activity have been modulated by transcriptional and posttranslational modifications, can regulate the expression of a substantial set of targets and participates in diverse cellular functions, such as stemness, proliferation, apoptosis, and migration [6]. More importantly, KLF5 inactivation reduced basal-like breast tumor growth in a CD8+ T-cell-dependent manner, and KLF5 loss in tumors increased the number and function of T cells [7]. Thus, we hypothesized that EFNA3, controlled by KLF5, regulated the glycolysis and CD8+ T cells- and NK cells-mediated immune evasion of CRC cells.

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), epigenetic regulators that play critical roles in post-transcriptional events, can influence CRC pathogenesis and progression by mediating various components of the TME [8]. SR-related and CTD-associated factor 8 (SCAF8, also known as RNA-binding motif protein 16) is an RNA polymerase II elongation factor whose RNA-binding clusters are usually located throughout the transcript, preventing cleavage of early mRNA transcripts [9]. The effect of SCAF8 on cancers has not been reported, and its protein expression was elevated in CRC samples with little alteration on the transcriptional level. The highly conserved ubiquitination enabled by E3 ubiquitin ligases is another imperative mechanism to control the expression of oncogenes or tumor suppressors in CRC [10]. Parkin (PRKN) was predicted to be the E3 ubiquitin ligase with downregulated expression in CRC. Indeed, the tumor suppressive potential of PRKN is related to its capacity to regulate cell proliferation, cell migration, angiogenesis, and apoptosis, and dysfunction of PRKN contributed to the loss of ubiquitination of cell cycle-related genes, leading to tumor progression [11]. In this study, we demonstrated the roles that EFNA3 played in CRC glycolysis and CD8+ T cells- and NK cells-mediated immune evasion. Importantly, we found that KLF5 induced EFNA3 transcription to accelerate glycolysis, which stimulates tumor immune evasion and metastasis. Furthermore, the tumor suppressor PRKN catalyzed the ubiquitination of SCAF8 to cause the instability of KLF5 mRNA. In summary, our study has provided a novel mechanism for tumor evasion and metastasis in CRC.

Materials and methods

Patient samples and clinical data collection

Primary CRC and paracancerous normal tissues were collected from 34 patients who underwent surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University for etiological research. Among them, 23 were male (14 READ, 9 COAD) and 11 were female (7 READ, 4 COAD). Additionally, fresh tumor tissue from a female patient (T3N1M1, no history of viral hepatitis) was collected for mouse xenograft experiments. All patients were first diagnosed with CRC, and they had complete clinical data and had not received preoperative radiotherapy. All patients provided written informed consent. The research was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (approval no. 2022185, date 11 March 2022) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Immunohistochemical staining (IHC)

After routine deparaffinizing and hydration, paraffin-embedded tumor tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval by citrate buffer. The sections were subsequently incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min at room temperature (RT). After being blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h, the sections were hatched with primary antibodies to EFNA3 (1:200, 12480-1-AP, ProteinTech Group, Chicago, IL, USA), PKM2 (1:400, 15822-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), CD31 (1:4000, 28083-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), CD34 (1:4000, 14486-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), KLF5 (1:200, 21017-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), SCAF8 (1:500, 18893-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), PRKN (1:500, 14060-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), LDHA (1:200, A18574, ABclonal, Wuhan, Hubei, China), CD8A (1:50, A11856, ABclonal), and CD56 (1:200, 82715-1-RR, ProteinTech Group) overnight and with HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (1:500, GB23303, Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) at RT for 1 h. Afterward, immunostaining was assessed against 3,3′-diaminobenzidine and hematoxylin. Mouse tumor samples were quantified by ImageJ to analyze the rate of positive cells stained. IHC staining of clinical samples was analyzed semi-quantitatively by observers who were unaware of the grouping based on the intensity of staining (negative: 0, weak: 1, moderate: 2, strong: 3) and the proportion of positive cells (< 25%: 1, 25–50%: 2, 50–75%: 3, > 75%: 4). The final score was the product of the two scores (0–12 points).

Cell culture and treatment

Human colonic mucosal epithelial cells (HCMECs, CP-H040) and human rectal mucosal epithelial cells (HRMECs, CP-H041) were procured from Procell (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Human COAD cell lines DLD-1 (Serial: TCHu134, RRID: CVCL_0248) and LoVo (Serial: TCHu 82, RRID: CVCL_0399), READ cell line SW1463 (Serial: SCSP-5230, RRID: CVCL_1718), and mouse rectum carcinoma CMT93 (Serial: TCM43, RRID: CVCL_1986) were acquired from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China). Another READ cell line, RCM-1 (Serial: CBP60041, RRID: CVCL_1648), was purchased from Cobioer (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). All cells have been authenticated by STR before use and were free from bacterial, fungal, or mycoplasma contamination. Cells at passage 2–3 were used for the experiment.

The short hairpin RNA (shRNA) lentivirus (based on pHBLV-U6 vector) for the knockdown of EFNA3, KLF5, and SCAF8 and the overexpression lentivirus (based on pHBLV-CMV vector) for PRKN, SCAF8, KLF5, and EFNA3 were obtained from Hanbio (Shanghai, China), and the viral titer was 108 TU/mL. CRC cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5 and screened for stable cell lines by puromycin or G418. The sequence information is provided in Table 1. RNA extraction and RT‑qPCR analysis

Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) was used to extract total RNA from cell samples. Extracted total RNA was analyzed by reverse transcription and fluorescence quantitative PCR using SweScript One-Step RT-PCR Kit (G3335-50, Servicebio). The reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT–qPCR) data were analyzed using the 2−△△Ct method, with β-actin serving as an internal reference. The primer sequences for amplification were as follows: forward 5'- AGTTCTCGGAGAAGTTCCAGCG-3′ and reverse 5′-CAGCAGACGAACACCTTCATCC-3′ for EFNA3; forward: 5′-GGAGAAACGACGCATCCACTAC-3′, reverse: 5′-GAACCTCCAGTCGCAGCCTTC-3′ for KLF5; forward: 5′-AGGAGACAGTGGTCACAACCCA-3′, reverse: 5′-TCGACACAGGAAATGCTGGTGG-3′ for SCAF8, forward: 5′- CCAGAGGAAAGTCACCTGCGAA-3′, reverse: 5′-CTGAGGCTTCAAATACGGCACTG-3′ for PRKN, and forward: 5′-CCACCATGTACCCTGGCATT-3′, reverse: 5′- GTCCTCGGCCACATTGTGAA-3′ for β-actin.

Total protein extraction and Western blot

Cell or tissue samples on ice were lysed with ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After quantification by the BCA method, 30 μg of proteins were separated on 6–10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore Corp, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were sealed in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding primary antibodies to EFNA3 (1:2000, 12480-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), PCNA (1:10000, 10205-2-AP, ProteinTech Group), CD31 (1:4000, 28083-–1-AP, ProteinTech Group), KLF5 (1:5000, 21017-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), PRKN (1:1000, 14060-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), LDHA (1:1000, A0861, ABclonal), PKM2 (1:2000, 15822-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), SCAF8 (1:5000, 18893-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), β-actin (1:3000, GB15003-100, Servicebio), His (1:5000, ab213204, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Ubiquitin (Ub) (1:1000, ab134953, Abcam), and then with secondary antibody HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000, GB23303, Servicebio) at RT for 2 h. Finally, the protein signal was examined with the assistance of a chemiluminescence reagent kit.

Metabolic assays

The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was evaluated on a Seahorse XFe24 analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using the Seahorse XF Glycolytic Stress Test Kit (103020-100, Agilent Technologies). Glucose, oligomycin, and 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) were added to cells (10,000 cells/well) seeded in 24-well plates at the indicated time points. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in the cells was assessed on a Seahorse XFe24 analyzer using the Seahorse XF Cellular Mitochondrial Stress Test Kit (103015-100, Agilent Technologies). Oligomycin, FCCP, Antimycin A, and Rotenone were added to cells (10,000 cells/well) seeded in 24-well plates at the indicated time points.

Glucose uptake levels

The glucose uptake fluorometric assay kit (E-BC-F041, Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China) was used to assess the ability of CRC cells to consume glucose. Briefly, CRC cells were seeded in 96-well cell culture plates (3000 cells/well) and starved in a serum-free cell culture medium overnight. The 2-DG (10 mmol/L) was added to the cell culture plate and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After a 30-min incubation of the working solution at 37 °C to convert the probe into fluorescent material, the fluorescence values were read in a microplate reader (excitation wavelength 530 nm, emission wavelength 590 nm), and the glucose uptake was calculated from the standard curve of the standards.

Analysis of lactic acid content

For CRC cells, 5 × 104 cells were seeded into a 96-well plate and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, cell supernatants were collected for assay. For tumor tissues, after routine homogenization using saline (0.9% NaCl solution), centrifugation was performed at 4 °C, 10,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was placed on ice for measurement. Lactic acid content in tumor tissues or cells was assessed using the l-Lactic Acid Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K044-M, Elabscience) by reading the OD at 530 nm with the microplate reader, and all operations were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Colony formation assay

Stably infected CRC cells were cultured for 2 weeks after inoculation into six-well plates at 500 cells/well. After 2 weeks, the cells were fixed by adding 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min and stained with crystal violet for 30 min. The number of colonies formed was counted after photographing.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

Stable infected CRC cells were seeded at a density of 2000 cells/well into 96-well plates and routinely incubated until designated time points (0 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h). Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (C0038, Beyotime) was added to each well, followed by continued incubation for 2 h. Cell proliferation was assessed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm.

EdU incorporation assay

Cell-Light EdU Apollo643 In Vitro Kit (C10310-2, Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) was used to assess DNA synthesis activity in CRC cells. Briefly, stable CRC cells were seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well), and cells were labeled for 2 h using a 50 μM EdU solution. After fixation in 4% PFA for 30 min, the cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min and stained with Apollo 643 staining solution for 30 min at RT in the dark. The nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was calculated after photographing using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Chicken chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay

To detect the angiogenic effects induced by CRC cells, a culture supernatant of CRC cells routinely cultured for 48 h was collected as a conditioned medium (CM). CAM assay was performed as previously described [13]. Briefly, a window of approximately 1.0 cm in diameter was made in the eggshell on day 6 of fertilization to expose the CAM, and 100 μL of CM from CRC cells was added. Eggs were placed in an incubator at 37 °C and 80–90% relative humidity for 2 days. Subsequently, CAMs were fixed with fixative (methanol:acetone = 1:1) for 15 min and photographed, and the number of blood vessels was counted.

Transwell assays

BeyoGold Transwell Permeable Supports (8 μm pore size, FTW076, Beyotime) were used to analyze the migratory and invasive potential of CRC cells, and Matrix-Gel (C0376, Beyotime) was used to encapsulate the Transwell chambers during the invasion assay. A culture medium (500 µL) containing 10% FBS was added to the 24-well plate, and the Transwell was placed inside the 24-well plate with forceps. CRC cells resuspended in serum-free medium (1 × 106 cells/mL, 200 µL) were seeded in the upper chamber of transwell inserts. After a 48-h incubation, the Transwell chamber was fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min. After being stained with crystal violet for 10 min, migrated and invaded cells were observed and counted under a microscope.

Detection of the killing effects of cytotoxic lymphocytes

Cytotoxic lymphocytes, including CD8+ T cells and NK cells, were isolated from healthy PBMCs using the CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (130-096-495, Miltenyi biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and NK Cell Isolation Kit (130-092-657, Miltenyi Biotec), respectively. CRC cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well, and NK cells and CD8+ T cells were added to CRC cells at an effector cell to target cells (E:T) of 1:1 [14] and 5:1 [15] for 24-h coculture. Harvested supernatants were used to measure the levels of cytokines released by immune cells using Granzyme B (GzmB) ELISA Kit (E-EL-H1617, Elabscience) and interferon gamma (IFN-γ) ELISA Kit (E-EL-H0108, Elabscience). Tumor cells were stained using the LIVE–DEAD Cell Imaging Kit (R37601, Invitrogen Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA), where Calcein-AM stained the live cells in green, and BOBO-3 iodide stained the dead cells in red. The cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope, and the survival rate (%) = green cells/(green cells + red cells) was calculated.

Animal studies

All animal studies were approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (approval no. 2023139, date 6 February 2023) and in accordance with the Basel Declaration. The Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University strictly adheres to the principles and guidelines established by the International Council for Laboratory Animal Science (ICLAS) to ensure that our animal research meets international ethical standards. Female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from HFK Bioscience (Beijing, China). The 16-week-old female huHSC-B-NDG hIL15 mice expressing the human immune system were purchased from Biocytogen (Beijing, China). All the animals involved were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment at appropriate temperatures.

Fresh in situ tumor tissue from patients with CRC was minced into microtissues smaller than 150 μm in diameter and mixed with Matrigel before being implanted subcutaneously into huHSC-B-NDG hIL15 mice [16]. After injection, tumor volume was measured every week: volume = π × length × width × height/6. When tumor volume reached approximately 150 mm3, 15 mice were randomly divided into three groups and received intratumoral injections of either EFNA3-knockdown lentivirus (sh-EFNA3 no. 1, no. 2) or control lentivirus (Scramble-shRNA). Tumor volume was measured weekly thereafter. After 4 weeks, mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg), and tumor tissue was collected.

For tumor growth experiments, 1 × 106 stably infected CMT93 cells [17] were subcutaneously injected into the right ventral side of C57BL/6 mice (n = 5/each group). At 4 weeks after tumor inoculation, mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of excess sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg), and tumor tissues were collected.

A CRC experimental liver metastasis model was constructed by splenic injection [18, 19]. After the C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized by inhalation through 2% isoflurane, a longitudinal incision was made in the left axillary line just below the posterior border of the rib cage to locate the spleen from the abdomen. CMT93 cells (1.5 × 106) suspended in 100 μL of sterile PBS were injected into the spleen. The spleen was excised 5 min after tumor cell injection to prevent splenic tumor formation. Mice were euthanized 21 days after tumor injection, and liver tissue was collected. Liver metastases were detected by hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining using ImageJ software, and the area occupied by metastatic foci was divided by the total surface area.

Flow cytometry

Tumor tissues were minced and digested in PBS containing 0.5 mg/mL collagenase IV and 0.25 mg/mL DNase I for 30 min at 37 °C. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell filter and resuspended in 1% BSA. FITC-coupled anti-mouse CD49b Antibody (11-5971-82, Invitrogen) and APC-coupled anti-mouse NKG2D Antibody (17-5882-82, Invitrogen) were added for 3-h incubation at RT in the dark to detect the content of murine activated NK cells. Human NK cells were detected using FITC-conjugated anti-human CD56 antibody (11-0566-42, Invitrogen) and APC-conjugated anti-human NKG2D antibody (17-5878-42, Invitrogen). To detect infiltrating activated CD8+ T cells in tumors, cells were stained with FITC-coupled CD8 (11-0032-82 or 11-0088-42, Invitrogen) for surface antigen staining. Following fixation and permeabilization, cells were stained with APC-conjugated GzmB antibodies (17-8898-82 or GRB05, Invitrogen). To detect the infiltration of active M1 macrophages, cells were fixed and permeabilized following surface antigen staining with an anti-human CD86 antibody conjugated to an APC (17-0869-42, Invitrogen) and stained with an anti-human CD68 antibody conjugated to FITC (11-0689-42, Invitrogen). To detect the infiltration of CD4+ T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) within them, cells were stained for surface antigens using FITC-conjugated anti-human CD3 antibody (11-0037-42, Invitrogen) and APC-conjugated anti-human CD4 antibody (MHCD0405, Invitrogen). The cells were fixed and permeabilized, followed by staining with PE-conjugated anti-human FOXP3 antibody (12-4777-42, Invitrogen). Staining was immediately detected by CytExpert software.

Immunofluorescence staining

The cells were simultaneously fixed and permeabilized by ice-cold 90% methanol and blocked by 5% goat serum. The cells were incubated with anti-LDHA (1:1000, A18574, ABclonal) and anti-PKM2 (1:2000, 15822-1-AP, ProteinTech Group) overnight at 4 °C, and with Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, GB25303, Servicebio) at RT in the dark for 1 h. The cell nuclei were labeled by incubating with DAPI working solution (1 µg/mL) at RT in the dark for 30 min. Staining was observed under a fluorescence microscope, and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was measured by ImageJ.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

The Pierce Magnetic ChIP Kit (26,157, Thermo Fisher) was used to analyze the binding of KLF5 to the EFNA3 promoter fragment. Briefly, CRC cells were fixed in 1% PFA for 10 min at RT. Chromatin was subsequently fragmented to 200–500 bps by micrococcal nuclease combined with an ultrasonic processor and incubated overnight with a primary antibody to KLF5 (1:20, 21017-1-AP, ProteinTech Group) or IgG at 4 °C. Endogenous DNA protein complexes were precipitated using Protein A/G magnetic beads. Samples were then washed and de-crosslinked. DNA fragments were recovered and purified, and the KLF5-pulled-down EFNA3 promoter fragment was quantified using qPCR.

Dual-luciferase assays

The EFNA3 upstream promoter fragment (chr1: 155078644-155078970) was queried from the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/index.html). The wild-type (WT) EFNA3 promoter fragment or the mutant (MT) promoter fragment lacking the KLF5 binding site was inserted into the pGL6 firefly luciferase plasmid (D2102, Beyotime) in the polyclonal site to construct a WT/MT reporter plasmid driven by the EFNA3 promoter. The above plasmids were transfected into CRC cells by Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher) together with the pRL-TK endogenous gene plasmid (D2760, Beyotime), which carries the Renilla luciferase. After 48 h, the assay was performed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (RG027, Beyotime).

RNA-immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The RIP kit (Bes5101, BersinBio, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) was used to evaluate the binding capacity of SCAF8 protein to KLF5 mRNA. Briefly, 1 × 107 cells were lysed on ice by supplementing the lysis buffer with protease inhibitor and RNase inhibitor. DNA was removed from cell lysates by incubation with DNase salt stock, DNase (10 U) at 37 °C for 10 min. The cells were incubated with anti-SCAF8 (1:20, 18893-1-AP, ProteinTech Group) at 4 °C overnight. Protein A/G beads were added and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h to capture protein–RNA complexes. RNA was eluted from the magnetic beads by polysome elution buffer, and the enriched level of KLF5 mRNA was detected by RT–qPCR after the collection of purified RNA.

mRNA or protein stability assay

CRC cells (3 × 105 cells/well) in six-well plates were treated with 5 μg/mL of Actinomycin D (S8964, Selleck, Houston, TX, USA), and the cells were lysed and RNA extracted at the indicated time points (0 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 12 h). KLF5 mRNA levels were detected by RT–qPCR.

CRC cells (3 × 105 cells/well) in six-well plates were prepared at the indicated time points (0 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 12 h) after the addition of 10 μM cycloheximide (CHX, S7418, Selleck) to prepare protein lysates, and protein expression of SCAF8 was evaluated using western blot analysis.

Co‑immunoprecipitation assay (Co-IP)

SCAF8 mutant protein plasmids (Flag-SCAF8K119R) with K119 mutated to arginine (R119), and recombinant tagged protein plasmids Flag-SCAF8 and His-PRKN were obtained from Servicebio. The above plasmids were cotransfected into 293 T cells (GNHu17, National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures) by Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher), and the Ubiquitin protein (Abcam) was added. After 48 h, the cells were lysed on ice with a lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime). Flag antibody for co-IP (ab205606, Abcam) was added to the samples, and the cell lysate/antibody mixtures were gently spun on a spinner at 4 °C overnight. The protein complexes were captured by rotary incubation at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were rinsed with IP lysis buffer and added to the loading buffer. Finally, the lysed immune complexes were separated by SDS–PAGE and analyzed by western blot.

Quantification and statistical analysis

At least five independent experiments were used for data analysis. Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.2 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Nonlin fit was used to calculate the half-life of the mRNA or protein, and Fisher’s exact test was used for columnar table analysis. The Shapiro–Wilk test or the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to analyze whether the data conforms to a normal distribution or not. For nonparametric tests, the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test was used for the analysis of paired two sets of data, and the Mann–Whitney test for the analysis of data from two unpaired groups. Spearman’s rank correlation was applied to examine the correlation between the two variables. For parametric tests, the unpaired t-tests were employed for comparisons between the two groups, and multiple group comparisons were performed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was denoted by p < 0.05.

Results

EFNA3 is associated with glycolytic activity and immune evasion in tumors of patients with CRC

We first downloaded the top 1000 differential expression genes (DEGs) in COAD and READ (ranked according to adjusted p-value) from GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) and cross-screened them. A total of 772 common DEGs were found ( Additional file 1:Supplementary Fig. S1A). To search for potential therapeutic targets for glycolysis in CRC, we then downloaded glycolysis-related human gene sets from The Molecular Signatures Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp), including WP_AEROBIC_ GLYCOLYSIS.v2023.2.Hs, KEGG_GLYCOLYSIS_GLUCONEOGENESIS.v2023.2.Hs, HALLMARK_GLYCOLYSIS.v2023.2.Hs. Glycolysis-related genes were cross-screened against DEGs in CRC, and there were 17 intersecting genes: ADH1B, MET, CENPA, KIF20A, AURKA, CHPF, HMMR, CDK1, SOX9, POLR3K, EFNA3, KDELR3, SLC2A1, NSDHL, ALG1, DEPDC1, and TGFBI (Additional file 1:Supplementary Fig. S1B). The prognostic significance of the expression of these 17 genes on disease-free survival (DFS) in CRC (COAD + READ) patients was analyzed in GEPIA with p < 0.05 as the cut-off threshold. The expression of CHPF, EFNA3, and SLC2A1 was closely associated with DFS in patients, and all three of them predicted lower DFS in patients ( Additional file 1:Supplementary Fig. S1C). The effects of CHPF [20] and SLC2A1 [21] on CRC glycolysis have been previously reported, whereas it is not clear whether EFNA3 is involved in CRC glycolysis.

Analysis in GEPIA informed that EFNA3 was highly expressed in CRC ( Additional file 1:Supplementary Fig. S1D). The correlation between EFNA3 expression and immune cell infiltration in tumor tissues of patients with CRC was evaluated using the QUANTISEQ algorithm in Sangerbox 3.0 (http://vip.sangerbox.com/). EFNA3 expression was negatively correlated with the infiltration of CD8+ T cells, NK cells, Tregs, and M1 macrophages, and positively correlated with the infiltration of CD4+ T cells in CRC ( Additional file 1:Supplementary Fig. S1E). The presence of cytotoxic lymphocytes is a relevant prognostic factor in CRC [23], and the infiltration of NK cells and CD8+ T lymphocytes in the CRC microenvironment has been shown to improve the prognosis of patients with CRC [24]. However, whether EFNA3 mediates immune evasion by inducing insufficient cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration in CRC through the glycolytic pathway requires further analysis.

KLF5 promotes the EFNA3 transcription

To reveal the cause of elevated transcription of EFNA3 in CRC, we used UniBind: maps of high-confidence direct TF-DNA interactions across species in UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/index.html) to observe the presence of transcription factors with binding sites in the promoter region upstream of EFNA3 (Additional file 2). The correlation between the expression of EFNA3 and the expression of transcription factors in CRC tumor tissues was analyzed by GEPIA, and the transcription factors that were correlated with the expression of EFNA3 were screened at p < 0.05, including HIF1A, KLF5, MXI1, RELA, and SPI1 (Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. S5A). The correlation between KLF5 and EFNA3 expression was the most prominent. Analysis in GEPIA revealed that KLF5 expression was remarkably elevated in CRC (Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. S5B). The presence of multiple binding sites for KLF5 at the EFNA3 promoter was also predicted by JASPAR (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) (Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. S5C). We therefore hypothesized that KLF5 mediated the transcription of EFNA3 in CRC.

IHC detected that the expression of KLF5 was elevated in tumor tissues of patients with CRC (Fig. 4A), and the expression of EFNA3 was significantly and positively correlated with KLF5 in CRC tissues (Fig. 4B). RT–qPCR (Fig. 4C) and Western blot analysis (Fig. 4D) also confirmed the overexpression of KLF5 in CRC cell lines. Stable knockdown of KLF5 expression in CRC cells by shRNAs was observed, and the transcript level of EFNA3 was also significantly reduced (Fig. 4E). The ChIP–qPCR assay confirmed that KLF5 bound to the promoter fragment of EFNA3 (Fig. 4F). By inserting the WT EFNA3 promoter sequence or a mutant promoter sequence lacking the KLF5 binding sequence (BS) into a luciferase reporter construct, we confirmed through dual-luciferase assay that KLF5 knockdown inhibited the transcriptional activity of the WT EFNA3 promoter. PRKN influences the stability of the SCAF8 protein through ubiquitination

Although the protein level of SCAF8 was observed to be significantly elevated in CRC cells, its transcriptional level was not significantly altered ( Additional file 1:Supplementary Fig. S8A), which was consistent with the prediction results in the GEPIA database (Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. S8B). We therefore hypothesized that the elevated protein expression of SCAF8 was caused by post-translational modifications. We predicted ubiquitin ligases (E3) ( Additional file 1:Supplementary Fig. S8C) targeting the SCAF8 protein in UbiBrowser 2.0 (http://ubibrowser.bio-it.cn/ubibrowser_v3/home/index). The expression of these E3 ubiquitin ligases in CRC tissues was analyzed by GEPIA. Among them, only PRKN expression was found to be reduced in CRC (Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. S8D). UbiBrowser 2.0 predicted the lysine site at position 119 (K119) of the SCAF8 protein (UniProt ID: Q9UPN6) as its ubiquitination site (Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. S8E) affected by PRKN.

IHC also confirmed the attenuated expression of PRKN in CRC tissues (Fig. 7A), and a significant negative correlation between PRKN and SCAF8/KLF5/EFNA3 expression was noted (Fig. 7B). The reduction of PRKN expression in CRC cells was verified using both RT–qPCR (Fig. 7C) and western blot analyses (Fig. 7D). CRC cell lines stably overexpressing PRKN were developed, and PRKN overexpression downregulated the protein expression of SCAF8, KLF5, and EFNA3 (Fig. 7E), as well as the mRNA levels of KLF5 and EFNA3, without affecting the mRNA level of SCAF8 (Fig. 7F). This suggests that PRKN regulates the function of SCAF8 through post-translational modification. The protein stability of SCAF8 was reduced after overexpression of PRKN, and the half-life was significantly shortened (Fig. 7G). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, our study holds implications for understanding the multifaceted role of EFNA3 in various aspects of CRC cell behavior, which includes cancer cell proliferation, glycolysis, angiogenesis, and immune evasion. Furthermore, our findings indicate a regulation of transcription factor KLF5 on EFNA3 expression.

EFNA3 was upregulated in most cancer types, and high expression of EFNA3 was associated with tumor progression and worse prognosis in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma [25]. Here, we predicted EFNA3 as a glycolysis-specific gene and observed the higher expression of LDHA and PKM2 in patients with CRC with high EFNA3 expression. The energy obtained from glycolysis is conducive to the proliferation, invasion, migration, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition of cancer cells, as well as the proliferation and function of immune cells [26]. Cancer cells can modify cytokines to inhibit the functioning of cytotoxic T cells, which leads to the suppression of CD8+ T lymphocytes, preventing them from recognizing foreign antigens [27]. Consistent with the predictive results that EFNA3 was negatively correlated with the infiltration of NK cells and CD8+ T cells, we also found the repressed IHC scores of CD8A and CD56 in patients with CRC with higher EFNA3 expression and enhanced levels of IFN-γ and GzmB in the coculture supernatant comprising CRC cells with EFNA3 knockdown. As demonstrated by Husain et al., EFNA3 was frequently upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma, and its overexpression was linked to more aggressive tumor behaviors, including self-renewal, proliferation, and migration in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [28]. Likewise, in vivo evidence here showed that the mice had reduced tumor burden with EFNA3 knockdown.

Among the transcription factors of EFNA3, KLF5 showed the most outstanding correlation coefficient with EFNA3 expression in CRC. It has been recently shown that KLF5 functions as a transcription factor for the leukemia inhibitory factor and bound to its promoter region in CRC [29]. The overexpression of KLF5 has been implicated in the progression of lung cancer and ovarian cancer [30, 31]. More importantly, tumors with KLF5 overexpression had reduced infiltration of CD8+ T cells, and NK cells, CD4+ effector, and CD8+ effector memory T cells were enriched within tumors with KLF5 knockdown [32]. KLF5 has also been reported to regulate the expression of CXCL12 and CXCL5 in CRC to improve the immunosuppressive TME, which contributed to the enhanced efficacy of immunotherapy and reduced metastasis [33, 34]. Our observation here that the repressive effects of KLF5 knockdown on angiogenesis, glycolysis, and immunosuppression on CRC cells were overturned by EFNA3 overexpression might add new insights into the downstream mechanism of KLF5 in CRC. As for its upstream modifiers, Y-box binding protein 1 has been revealed to stabilize KLF5 mRNA in an RNA 5-methylcytosine modification-dependent manner in basal-like breast cancer [35]. In this study, we verified the stabilizing effects of RBP SCAF8 on KLF5 mRNA.

Subsequently, the ubiquitination site of SCAF8 was identified at K119, since the mutation of this site caused the stability of SCAF8. PRKN is a 52 kDa protein with 465 amino acids, and the PRKN gene localizes on human ch6q25‐27, a frequent loss region in cancers [36]. Under the condition of cervical cancer, overexpression of PRKN catalyzed the proteasomal degradation of IGF2BP3 [37]. Regarding its functional role, PRKN acted as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting the proliferation and migration of bladder cancer cells [38]. Intriguingly, the silencing of PRKN almost completely reversed the reduction in OCR and clonogenic capacity of CRC cells caused by USP26 silencing [39]. IHC staining of orthotopic breast tumor tissues showed that PRKN knockout increased the levels of its substrate PHGDH and staining of Ki-67 [40]. In addition, Perales-Linares et al. showed that PRKN deficiency led to aggressive tumor growth, which was linked to poor tumor antigen presentation and constrained antitumor CD8+ T-cell infiltration and activation, and the impact of PRKN inhibition on tumor growth was lost after depletion of CD8+ T cells [41]. Here, by using lentiviruses, we found that overexpression of PRKN repressed the glycolysis, angiogenesis, and immune evasion of CRC, which were all reversed by the overexpression of its direct substrate SCAF8 and the indirect targets KLF5 and EFNA3.

The present study is not without its limitations. First, the findings of this study underscore the pivotal role of EFNA3 as a central regulator of glycolysis and immune modulation in CRC. However, the precise mechanisms linking EFNA3 to LDHA/PKM2 expression and immune-related changes remain to be elucidated, representing a significant direction for future investigation. Additionally, while the present data establish SCAF8 as an upstream regulator of KLF5 mRNA stability, the specific region of KLF5 mRNA bound by SCAF8 remains to be elucidated. Subsequent research endeavors will seek to ascertain the precise binding sites.

Conclusions

Taken together, these findings suggest that PRKN plays a significant role in regulating the SCAF8/KLF5/EFNA3 axis in CRC. Overexpression of PRKN can impair glycolysis and promote antitumor response of NK cells and CD8+ T cells by repressing SCAF8 protein expression and blocking the KLF5/EFNA3 signaling. Moreover, EFNA3 is considered a potential biomarker of glycolysis and a novel immunotherapeutic target in patients with CRC.
