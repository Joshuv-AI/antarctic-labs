Gastroesophageal reflux disease and temporomandibular disorder risk: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Heon-Young Kim, Jung-Hyun Park, Gwang Hyun Leem, Hyo-Jung Lee, Jin-Woo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are multifactorial conditions characterized by complex interactions among biological, psychological, and behavioral factors. GERD is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder with multiple extra esophageal manifestations, while TMD represents a major source of chronic orofacial pain worldwide. This study aims to examine the association between GERD and TMD risk in a large-scale, population-based cohort.MethodsThis nationwide cohort study used data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significantly lower TMD-free survival in the GERD group.ConclusionsGERD was significantly associated with increased TMD risk. Background

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a chronic gastrointestinal disorder caused by the retrograde flow of gastric contents into the esophagus, leading to typical symptoms such as acid regurgitation and heartburn, and potentially serious complications, including esophagitis, Barrett’s esophagus, and esophageal adenocarcinoma [1, 2]. Although GERD is not a life-threatening condition, it can significantly impair health-related quality of life, particularly when accompanied by sleep disturbances, pain, and dietary restrictions [3]. Its global prevalence ranges between 13% and 19%, with notably higher rates reported in Europe (8.8–25.9%) and North America (18.1–27.8%) [3]. Additionally, an increasing trend is observed among East Asian populations [3]. GERD is now increasingly understood as a systemic disorder with multisystemic manifestations rather than a condition confined to the esophagus. It frequently presents with extraesophageal manifestations, including respiratory symptoms (e.g., cough, laryngitis, and asthma), psychological disorders (e.g., anxiety and depression), sleep problems, and oral complications such as dental erosion and mucosal lesions [1, 4]. Although GERD is often attributed to lower esophageal sphincter dysfunction [5], it should be regarded as a systemic condition involving complex interactions among physiological, psychological, and lifestyle-related factors [6].

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are a group of craniofacial pain conditions characterized by dysfunction and pain in the masticatory muscles, temporomandibular joints (TMJs), and surrounding structures [7]. They are considered one of the major causes of non-dental chronic orofacial pain [8, 9]. TMDs affect approximately 15% of adults globally, with a twofold higher prevalence in women [10]. Common clinical manifestations include craniofacial and periauricular pain, noise on joint movement, and restrictions or deviations in mandibular movement, which may interfere with daily functions such as chewing, speaking, and yawning [8]. Historically, the etiology of TMD was primarily considered in mechanical or structural terms; however, TMD is now recognized as a biopsychosocial disorder involving the complex interplay of psychological, biological, and social factors [8]. Contributing factors include structural abnormalities, trauma, muscle dysfunction, genetic predispositions, hormonal fluctuations, systemic diseases, and psychological conditions such as stress, depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances [11]. Additionally, parafunctional oral habits such as bruxism and clenching are known to induce muscular fatigue and dysfunction, further contributing to the development and chronicity of TMD [7, 8].

Accordingly, both GERD and TMD are multifactorial conditions involving complex interactions among biological, psychological, and behavioral factors. Given their shared risk factors, an emerging body of evidence suggests that GERD may be associated with an increased likelihood of developing TMD [12]. In particular, sleep-related mechanisms may represent one potential pathway linking the two conditions, as GERD has been discussed within the framework of dental sleep disorders [13]. However, current evidence remains limited because most previous studies have used cross-sectional or case-control designs, which have limited ability to establish temporal relationships and are susceptible to selection bias and residual confounding. In addition, several studies were conducted in relatively small or selected populations, limiting the generalizability of their findings, and important confounding factors, including sleep disturbances and medication use, were not sufficiently accounted for in prior analyses [12, 14].

To address these limitations, we conducted a large-scale, population-based cohort study using claims data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) to evaluate the association between GERD and TMD. By incorporating propensity score matching (PSM) and multivariable analyses, we aimed to minimize confounding effects and determine whether GERD is a risk factor or indicator of TMD in a nationally representative population. Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that individuals with GERD would have a statistically higher risk of developing TMD than those without GERD, and investigated this association using real-world, population-based cohort data.

Methods

Data source

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (approval number: X-2108-705-901). The requirement for informed consent was waived because the study used anonymized retrospective data. All the procedures adhered to the guidelines and principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. We used the National Sample Cohort, a population-based cohort established by the Korean NHIS [15]. This cohort is a 2% representative sample (approximately 1 million individuals) of the South Korean population as of 2002, selected through stratified random sampling by age, sex, residential region, and insurance type. All enrollees were continuously followed through healthcare claims, eligibility data, and death records up to 2019. The NHIS database contains comprehensive information on demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, medical diagnoses (based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision [ICD-10] codes), prescriptions, procedures, and healthcare utilization. The NHIS is South Korea’s single-payer national health insurance system, covering approximately 97% of the population; the remaining 3% are enrolled in the medical aid program, also managed by the NHIS.

Study population and cohort selection

This study included adults aged ≥ 19 years who underwent at least one general health screening examination conducted by the NHIS between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2011. Because this was a population-based study of the general adult population, younger adults were not excluded a priori. The index date was defined as the most recent health-screening date for each participant during this period.

To identify new TMD cases following GERD diagnosis, we applied the following exclusion criteria: (1) individuals with missing data; (2) those diagnosed with malignant neoplasms (ICD-10 codes: C00–C97) between 2002 and 2011, to minimize the influence of serious systemic diseases; (3) those previously diagnosed with bruxism (F518, F458), TMD (K07.6), or tooth/dental prosthesis fracture (S0252–S0259, T856) before the index date; (4) those who had received a GERD diagnosis but either had no prescription or only a single prescription for proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) or histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs) before the index date; and (5) those who had been prescribed medications known to potentially induce or exacerbate GERD at least twice before the index date, to further minimize confounding [16]. These medications included:

GERD was defined as a diagnosis of GERD with esophagitis (ICD-10: K210) or without esophagitis (K219), accompanied by a same-day prescription of either PPI or H2RA. To improve diagnostic specificity and reduce potential misclassification in claims-based data, individuals were included in the GERD group only if they received prescriptions for a PPI or an H2RA on two or more separate occasions, with a total duration of ≥ 2 weeks between the index date and December 31, 2011. This approach aligns with clinical guidelines recommending a minimum 2-week trial of acid-suppressive therapy in patients with typical GERD symptoms [17]. We excluded those who met the diagnostic code but did not receive any prescriptions, as well as those who received only a single prescription before the index date. This was done to ensure the inclusion of individuals with newly diagnosed or actively treated GERD. All other eligible participants were classified as non-GERD.

GERD-related medications included the following:

PPIs: omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, dexlansoprazole, rabeprazole, esomeprazole, and ilaprazole

H2RAs: cimetidine, famotidine, lafutidine, roxatidine, nizatidine

H2RAs: cimetidine, famotidine, lafutidine, roxatidine, nizatidine

Among the 511,396 adults who underwent general health examinations between 2010 and 2011, 1011 with missing data were first excluded. We then excluded 23,241 individuals who had been prescribed medications known to potentially induce or exacerbate GERD, including calcium channel blockers, benzodiazepines, tricyclic antidepressants, theophylline, and anticholinergics at least twice before the index date. In addition, 154,749 participants who had received a GERD diagnosis but had either no prescription or only a single prescription for a PPI or an H2RA before the index date were excluded according to the washout criteria. Finally, 136,862 individuals with a history of malignancy, tooth or prosthesis fracture, bruxism, or TMD were excluded. After applying all exclusion criteria, a total of 195,533 participants were included in the final study cohort. Among them, 40,769 (20.8%) and 154,764 (79.2%) were categorized into the GERD and non-GERD groups, respectively.

To reduce confounding, we performed 1:3 PSM using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.2. Matching was based on key baseline covariates, including age, sex, household income, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and alcohol consumption. After matching, the final analytic cohort consisted of 40,755 individuals in the GERD group and 115,242 matched controls in the non-GERD group (Fig. 1).

Variable definition and primary outcome

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, economic status, BMI, smoking status, and alcohol consumption, were assessed as of the index date. To account for the multifactorial nature of GERD and its potential systemic impacts, a wide range of comorbidities was evaluated as covariates [5, 6, 18–21]. Comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 codes from insurance claims during the 1-year period preceding the index date. These included hypertension (I10–I15), hyperthyroidism (E058, E059), hypothyroidism (E039), diabetes mellitus (E10, E118, E119, E13, E149), dyslipidemia (E78), myocardial infarction (I21, I22, I25.2), stroke (I60–I63), anemia (D46, D50–D53, D55–D64, D74), asthma (J450–J459), pneumonia (J12–J18), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD; J431, J432, J438, J439, J44), insomnia (F51.0, F51.9, G47.0, G47.9), gastritis (K29.x), and gastrointestinal hemorrhage (K92.2).

The primary outcome was the incidence of TMD, defined as the first outpatient visit with a diagnosis code of K07.6 after the index date, accompanied by a claim for a TMJ examination, identified by electronic data interchange code E9040. This dual-definition approach was used to enhance diagnostic accuracy and specificity. Participants were followed from the index date until the first occurrence of TMD, death, or December 31, 2019. Previous validation work has demonstrated that this dual-criteria definition reliably identifies TMD cases, with a sensitivity of 95.8% [22].

Statistical analyses

The baseline characteristics of the study population were compared between the GERD and non-GERD groups. For continuous variables, Student’s t-test was used, while categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. The incidence rate of TMD was calculated as the number of newly diagnosed TMD cases divided by the total person-years (PYs) of follow-up.

To evaluate the association between GERD and the risk of developing TMD, Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Both crude and multivariable-adjusted models were constructed. The adjusted model included the following covariates: age, sex, household income, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and comorbidities, including hypertension, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, myocardial infarction, stroke, anemia, asthma, pneumonia, COPD, gastritis, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and insomnia.

For the PSM, covariate balance before and after matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD of < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. Cox regression models were re-applied to the matched sample to confirm the robustness of the findings.

In addition, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted to visualize the cumulative incidence of TMD by GERD status, and group differences were tested using the log-rank test. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

In Cox proportional hazards analyses, GERD was associated with an increased risk of incident TMD both before and after matching. Before matching, the crude and adjusted HRs were 1.46 (95% CI: 1.39–1.53, p < .001; Supplementary Table 1) and 1.23 (95% CI: 1.15–1.31, p < .001; Table 2), respectively. After matching, the corresponding crude and adjusted HRs were 1.55 (95% CI: 1.47–1.63, p < .001; Supplementary Table 2) and 1.23 (95% CI: 1.15–1.31, p < .001; Table 2), respectively. These findings indicate that the association remained robust after adjustment for measured covariates and after propensity score matching.

Kaplan–Meier analysis further supported these findings, showing a consistently higher cumulative incidence of TMD in the GERD group throughout the follow-up period. The difference in TMD-free survival between the two groups was significant according to the log-rank test (p < .001) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This nationwide, population-based cohort study demonstrated that individuals diagnosed with GERD had a significantly increased risk of developing TMD compared to matched controls. This association remained robust after adjusting for demographic characteristics and major comorbidities in multivariable analyses. By applying rigorous PSM to balance baseline characteristics between the groups, the study strengthened the epidemiological evidence suggesting that GERD may increase the risk of TMD. Previous studies have also reported an association between GERD and TMD; for example, Li et al. reported an odds ratio (OR) of 2.74 (95% CI: 1.88–3.98) for the presence of chronic TMD among patients with GERD [23], while a bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis conducted in a European population observed a significant causal relationship between GERD and increased TMD risk (OR 1.47, 95% CI: 1.20–1.81) [24]. Our study clearly demonstrates this association based on nationally representative real-world data. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies, but the present study extends the literature in several important ways. First, we used a nationwide population-based cohort, which reduces the selection issues inherent in smaller or clinic-based samples. Second, unlike prior cross-sectional or case-control studies, our longitudinal design allowed evaluation of incident TMD after GERD exposure, providing clearer temporal context for the observed association. Third, the use of PSM and multivariable Cox regression enabled more robust control of measurable confounding factors.

The association between GERD and TMD is more plausibly explained by multifactorial interactions among biological, behavioral, and psychosocial factors, rather than by anatomical or functional abnormalities alone [12, 23, 25]. This multifactorial perspective provides a theoretical basis for understanding the shared symptom profiles, high-risk characteristics, and epidemiological links observed between the two disorders.

One key mechanism potentially linking GERD to TMD is the role of sleep disturbances, particularly those induced by nocturnal acid reflux [4]. GERD has been shown to disrupt normal sleep architecture through recurrent nighttime arousals and micro-awakenings caused by esophageal acid exposure [26, 27]. These disturbances are closely associated with increased rhythmic masticatory muscle activity (RMMA) and sleep bruxism, which impose repetitive strain on the masticatory system and may contribute to the onset or exacerbation of TMD [14, 28, 29]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that esophageal acidification increases RMMA episodes during sleep and elevates masseter muscle activity even during wakefulness [28, 29]. Moreover, reduced salivary flow and impaired esophageal clearance during sleep may enhance acid contact with the oropharyngeal mucosa, potentially aggravating oral and facial discomfort and contributing to muscle overactivation [30]. This link is reinforced by Lobbezoo et al. [13], who proposed that GERD be considered a subtype of dental sleep disorders because of its notable effects on sleep architecture and orofacial health.

Moreover, psychological comorbidities, such as anxiety, depression, and somatization, represent a shared vulnerability pathway between GERD and TMD [23]. These conditions are known to influence both disorders through a shared mechanism of central sensitization [7, 31]. Chronic psychological distress can heighten interoceptive awareness, amplify symptom perception, and lower pain thresholds [23, 25]. In addition, it may lead to maladaptive health behaviors such as sleep disturbance or parafunctional activities (e.g., bruxism), which in turn can exacerbate both GERD and TMD symptoms [32]. Emerging evidence also suggests that these psychosocial factors may be linked to alterations in neuroimmune and pain regulatory pathways, further reinforcing the systemic and multifactorial nature of these disorders [33–35].

Another plausible pathophysiological mechanism linking GERD to TMD involves dental erosion and occlusal alterations. Chronic exposure to gastric acid can demineralize enamel and dentin [14], particularly on the occlusal and palatal surfaces, leading to changes in occlusal morphology, loss of vertical dimension, and occlusal instability [36]. These structural changes may result in masticatory muscle imbalance and increased TMJ loading, thereby facilitating the development or worsening of TMD [1]. Notably, in patients with sleep bruxism, the combination of acid-induced chemical erosion and mechanical stress from grinding may synergistically aggravate TMD symptoms [14, 37]. Previous studies have reported that excessive muscular activity and malocclusion are closely associated with the onset of TMD [38, 39].

Finally, although uncommon, certain procedural factors have been reported to contribute to TMD. For example, during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, excessive mouth opening or repeated retching can impose mechanical stress on the TMJ, potentially leading to condylar dislocation or aggravation of pre-existing dysfunction [40]. In severe cases, repeated vomiting due to GERD can cause TMJ dislocation [41].

Although these mechanisms are biologically plausible and supported by previous laboratory, clinical, and epidemiological observations, they are primarily based on indirect evidence. Most mechanistic pathways linking GERD to TMD have been inferred from smaller experimental studies or observational data, and direct causal pathways have not been definitively established. Therefore, these mechanisms should be interpreted as potential explanatory models rather than confirmed causal processes, highlighting the need for future research to clarify the underlying biological and psychosocial interactions between GERD and TMD.

Nevertheless, while our findings provide important insights into the association between GERD and TMD, some limitations should be considered. First, TMD was identified using the ICD-10 diagnostic code K07.6 in claims data, together with a procedural code for TMJ examination. Because this claims-based definition does not allow reliable differentiation of specific TMD subtypes, we could not distinguish clinically heterogeneous conditions such as myogenous pain, disc displacement, and degenerative joint disease. Accordingly, subtype-specific associations could not be evaluated, which limits the clinical granularity and interpretability of our findings. Second, although we applied PSM and adjusted for a wide range of covariates in multivariable models, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—such as psychosocial stress, pain sensitivity, and autonomic dysfunction—cannot be fully excluded. A prospective cohort study has shown that psychological stress significantly predicted incident TMD, with effects further influenced by genetic susceptibility [42]. In addition, experimental evidence indicates that intra-esophageal acid exposure increases masseter muscle activity and alters autonomic balance [43]. Because these neurobiological mechanisms are not captured in administrative claims data, the observed association between GERD and TMD may partly reflect unmeasured shared vulnerability.

Additionally, chronic pain conditions such as complex regional pain syndrome and fibromyalgia might have influenced the observed association [44]. Moreover, because trauma affecting the TMJ is heterogeneous and the diagnostic granularity of claims data is limited, we were unable to fully account for prior injuries (e.g., whiplash or jaw trauma). Attempting to exclude trauma based solely on diagnostic codes could have led to misclassification or over-exclusion, given the lack of reliable information on trauma timing and severity in administrative datasets. Third, this study identified GERD cases using both ICD-10 diagnostic codes and corresponding prescriptions for PPIs and H2RAs. This definition was established in accordance with clinical guidelines that recommend these agents for the diagnosis and management of GERD [17]. While this approach enhances diagnostic specificity, it may not fully capture the heterogeneous clinical presentation and treatment patterns of GERD. Patients who managed symptoms with over-the-counter medications (e.g., antacids, alginates, or prokinetics) or those receiving on-demand or step-down therapy may not have been identified [45]. Consequently, the prevalence of GERD may have been underestimated, and individuals with mild or non-pharmacologically managed symptoms may have been underrepresented in our cohort. Furthermore, medication adherence could not be assessed from claims data, and important clinical considerations, such as long-term tolerance to H2RAs, were not captured [46]. These limitations may leave residual bias even after PSM, and thus, the results should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, the global prevalence of GERD varies across regions [3], and these differences have been attributed to variations in dietary habits, Helicobacter pylori infection rates, obesity prevalence, and culturally influenced patterns of symptom perception and healthcare-seeking behavior [47, 48]. Similarly, the prevalence and clinical presentation of TMD may vary across populations [10]. Ethnic differences in subtype distribution and psychosocial profiles have also been documented [49]. Accordingly, the findings of the present study may not be fully generalizable to populations with different dietary, genetic, healthcare, and psychosocial contexts. Future prospective studies incorporating disease severity, subtype classification, and non-pharmacological treatment strategies are needed to clarify the mechanisms linking GERD and TMD. In addition, multinational cohort studies are warranted to validate these findings and improve their generalizability across diverse populations.

Conclusions

This nationwide cohort study demonstrated a significant association between GERD and the risk of incident TMD, underscoring the importance of recognizing potential TMD-related symptoms in patients with GERD. A multidisciplinary approach involving gastroenterologists, dentists, and mental health professionals may facilitate early identification and management in this population. Increased clinical awareness of orofacial pain, sleep disturbances, and bruxism, and psychological distress in patients with GERD may help facilitate earlier recognition of TMD.
