Interactive effects of ETc-based drip irrigation scheduling and phytohormones on root architecture, physiological traits and yield of wheat
Authors: Biswajyoti Banik, Santosh Korav, H. T. Sujatha, Vikas Sharma, Munirah Abdullah Al–Dosary, Wasmiah Saud AL Otaibi, Saud Alamri, Manzer H. Siddiqui, Prakash Kumar Jha, Maqsood Ul Hussan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wheat productivity is increasingly threatened by inefficient irrigation practices and declining groundwater resources. Therefore, the identification of efficient irrigation strategies is essential to enhance water productivity without compromising crop yield. A two-year field experiment was conducted using a split-plot design with three replications. The main plot treatments consisted of three crop evapotranspiration (ETc)-based drip irrigation scheduling levels, namely 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 ETc. The sub-plot treatments included foliar application of salicylic acid (SA), gibberellic acid (GA), and abscisic acid (ABA), along with water spray as the control treatment. The results revealed that deficit irrigation at 0.6 ETc significantly increased root length and specific root length compared with 1.0 ETc, indicating adaptive root responses under water-deficit conditions. In contrast, full irrigation at 1.0 ETc maintained superior plant water status, as evidenced by higher relative water content, membrane stability index, chlorophyll index, and lower canopy temperature and electrolyte leakage compared with deficit irrigation treatments. Among the phytohormone treatments, foliar application of SA significantly enhanced root dry weight (18.5–26.2%), antioxidant enzyme activities (catalase and peroxidase), and physiological attributes compared with the water spray control and other phytohormones. The improved antioxidant activity under SA application suggested its role in alleviating drought-induced oxidative stress. The highest grain yield was recorded under 1.0 ETc, showing a 7.9–8.8% increase over 0.6 ETc irrigation. However, irrigation at 0.8 ETc in combination with SA produced grain yield statistically comparable to 1.0 ETc while using comparatively less irrigation water. Foliar application of SA under deficit irrigation conditions improved physiological stability through enhanced photosynthetic pigments and antioxidant defense mechanisms, thereby contributing to improved water-use efficiency. Overall, the findings suggest that ETc-based drip irrigation integrated with salicylic acid application can enhance drought tolerance, sustain wheat productivity, and improve water-use efficiency under limited water availability. The study provides valuable insights for developing climate-resilient and water-efficient wheat production strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-63171-1.

Introduction

Food security is a major global challenge for agriculture. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), by 2050, food production from irrigated lands will have to be raised by over 50% to meet world food needs. At the same time, the total allowable increase in world water extraction is estimated to be only around 10% with ample scope for efficient irrigation and crop production1. Water use for agriculture and other non–agricultural purposes is increasing at a greater rate than the global population over the last five and half decades1. Presently, a large proportion of groundwater is being used for agricultural and non–agricultural purposes far exceeding the recharge potential of most aquifers2. Hence, the present pattern of ground water use for irrigation is highly unsustainable and needs immediate correction through the adoption of more efficient irrigation practices. A major contributing factor to the unsustainable use of water resources in agriculture is the flood method of irrigation (FMI) prevalent in arid and semiarid regions. In this method a large proportion of the water is lost as runoff, deep percolation and transpiration and hence, water use efficiency in FMI has been reported to vary from 30 to 45% in India for many crops3–6. The excess application of water not only has detrimental effects on the aquifer but also requires drastic reduction in order to provide adequate water for crop growth through more efficient irrigation and scheduling practices.

Thus, there is an urgent need to adopt new and improved irrigation technologies with higher water use efficiency and with lesser adverse environmental effects. Efficient water management is essential for meeting the increasing water requirements for sustainable agriculture and hence, the need for finding an alternative method to surface irrigation, especially in water-scarce situations like Punjab, has become critical7. Among various alternatives of surface irrigation, micro irrigation system such as drip irrigation has the potential to effectively meet the global and local water scarcity challenges. It has an irrigation efficiency of 90% as compared to irrigation efficiency of only about 40% with surface irrigation systems8. It minimizes the losses due to evaporation; deep percolation and surface run off to a maximum extent and thus, contaminates the ground water to a lesser extent. Drip irrigation is suitable for a wide range of soil and crop characteristics. At the global level, area under drip-irrigation is estimated to be more than 3.7 mha and this technology is being successfully demonstrated in several horticultural crops as well as some food crops like cotton, maize and wheat9,10. Despite its potential, the adoption of drip irrigation in wheat remains limited, particularly in regions such as northwestern India. In conjunction with drip systems, deficit irrigation (DI) offers a promising strategy for conserving limited water resources in crop production.7 Water stress is the minimal supply of water below the crop’s full evapotranspiration requirement, either throughout the entire growth cycle, termed Classical Deficit Irrigation (CDI), or during specific phenological stages termed Regulated Deficit Irrigation (RDI). The effectiveness of DI strategies depends on crop sensitivity to water stress at different growth stages, as well as the intensity and duration of the imposed deficit11. Strategic implementation of DI under drip systems can therefore enhance water productivity while maintaining acceptable crop yields. Deficit irrigation, wherein crops are exposed to controlled water stress by applying irrigation amounts below the crop evapotranspiration (ET) requirements, is a viable strategy for conserving water without severely compromising yields12. Sharma et al.7 demonstrated that among the various irrigation scheduling strategies evaluated under drip irrigation, the RDI approach wherein 85% of the daily irrigation water requirement (based on daily pan evaporation, Epan) was applied during the initial, development, and late growth stages significantly improved pod yield by 58.1% and WUE by 97.7%, while achieving a 30.7% reduction in irrigation water use compared to conventional flood irrigation in pea cultivation. The level of deficit irrigation (DI) is inherently crop-specific, as it depends on the physiological and phenological responses of individual crops to water stress. Effective implementation of DI requires a detailed understanding of how crops respond to varying degrees of water deficit, particularly with respect to yield performance and quality parameters. A suitable level of deficit irrigation, calibrated to the crop’s sensitivity to water stress and its critical growth stages within the prevailing agro-climatic conditions, is essential for optimizing wheat productivity under water-limited conditions; this need is underscored by the global significance of wheat, with annual production exceeding 790 million tons (USDA FAS, 2024). Under such water-deficit scenarios, phytohormones play major role in mediating plant adaptive responses. These signaling molecules modulate a wide range of physiological and biochemical processes, including nutrient partitioning, activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms, and regulation of stomatal conductance, thereby enhancing plant resilience and maintaining productivity under sub-optimal water availability.13 Among the key phytohormones, abscisic acid (ABA) is known to mediate drought responses by promoting stomatal closure and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD), thereby improving water retention and stress tolerance.14 Exogenous application of ABA has been reported to increase wheat yield by 11% under deficit DI.15 Similarly, SA possess phenolic hormonal properties, which mitigates oxidative stress by ROS activity and enhances chlorophyll content during drought stress.13,16 Gibberellic acid (GA), another critical phytohormone, has been shown to enhance tolerance against abiotic stresses, including drought and salinity, and its application at 250 ppm increased wheat yield by 15.5% over the control through modulation of ROS and antioxidant enzyme dynamics17,18. Despite their proven roles in stress mitigation, limited research exists on the synergistic effects of phytohormone application under drip irrigation, particularly with respect to root development in wheat. Moreover, the interactions between root architecture and water availability under drip-irrigated conditions in northwestern India remain poorly understood.

The study was conducted based on the hypotheses that: (i) moderate deficit irrigation under drip systems can conserve water without substantial yield penalties; (ii) exogenous application of phytohormones can enhance physiological resilience and antioxidant defense under water deficit conditions; and (iii) salicylic acid can partially offset the adverse effects of deficit irrigation through improvements in root development, water relations, and oxidative stress management. In this context, the present two-year field investigation was conducted to assess the interactive effects of irrigation levels and exogenous application of phytohormones on root morphological traits in drip-irrigated wheat. The study further aimed to elucidate the relationship between root characteristics and aboveground biomass under varying water regimes and phytohormonal treatments, with the aim of optimizing water use and improving productivity under deficit-irrigation conditions.

Though salicylic acid has been shown to improve drought tolerance in wheat and other crops, little is known about how it interacts with ETc-based drip irrigation planning under actual field circumstances. Earlier research have concentrated on either irrigation management or phytohormone-mediated stress relief separately, while the combined effect of ETc-based deficit irrigation and extra phytohormones on root architecture qualities still needs more investigation. Moreover, little study has been made of wheat cultivated under drip irrigation systems in northwest India’s root reactions. Therefore, this study sought to assess how well ETc-based drip irrigation levels and phytohormone application interact on root structure, physiological performance, antioxidant activity, production, and economics of wheat. Through physiological intervention under water-limited situations, this integrated strategy offers fresh understanding of the best water-saving irrigation methods.

Materials and methods

Weather of the experimental site

The research trial was conducted at the experimental farm, LPU, Punjab, India (31.25° N, 75° E, ) during the winter (rabi) seasons of 2022-23 and 2023–24. The area has a subtropical climate with hot summers, cold winters, and 1150 mm of rainfall annually. The mean maximum and minimum temperature and RH were 22.2 °C, 10.0 °C and 86.7%, respectively, during 2022-23 and there were 19 °C, 6.7 °C and 92.9% during 2023-24(Fig. 1). The reference crop evapotranspiration (ETc) was highest in 2023-24 (135.7 mm) than in 2022-23 (134.7 mm). An amount of 51.1 and 17.8 mm rainfall was received during the respective study years.

Study details

A split plot design was used with three irrigation levels in main factors (0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 ETc) and four phytohormone levels in sub-factors (SA, GA, ABA and control) with three replications during the rabi seasons of both 2022-23 and 2023-24. Foliar application of SA treatment (150 mg SA L− 1); GA treatment (100 mg GA L− 1); ABA treatment (20 mg ABA L− 1) was done twice per season during tillering and booting stages, randomly distributed in subplots. Foliar sprays were applied using a hand-held sprayer at a volume of approximately 300 L ha⁻¹ without the use of surfactants. Experimental layout

The experimental plot size was 5 m × 2.7 m with row spacing of 22.5 cm and plant spacing of 6 cm. Each subplot consisted of 12 rows. Buffer rows were maintained to prevent lateral water movement between treatments. Standard plant protection measures were uniformly adopted across all treatments to control pests and diseases. Initially, three irrigations were applied at four-day intervals to ensure proper germination and establishment of wheat. Thereafter, irrigation was scheduled as per specific treatments through a drip irrigation system using 16 mm laterals equipped with emitters having a discharge rate of 4 L h− 1. The laterals were placed between crop rows, and control valves were installed to regulate water flow in each treatment.

Root sampling methodology

Using cylindrical polyethylene root observation units (60 cm length by 30 cm diameter), root observations were done and about 42.4 L of soil volume per unit was supplied. Three root observation devices were put in every subplot and filled with homogenized field soil. Within the 0–60 cm soil profile, root counts were taken. One representative unit per subplot was gently dug out at flowering, and roots were retrieved by softly washing the dirt over a fine mesh sieve to reduce root loss. Manual measurement of root length with a meter scale followed total root recovery. Using calibrated electronic scales, root fresh and dry weights were measured. Before data collecting, all instruments were calibrated using manufacturer instructions. Though some constraint of lateral root growth relative to open-field conditions is noted, the restricted root observation system allowed for controlled comparison across treatments.

The use of confined root units enables standardized and non-destructive recovery of intact root systems for precise measurement of root length, biomass, and specific root length. While such systems may restrict lateral root expansion compared to unrestricted field soil, all treatments were subjected to identical root-zone conditions, ensuring a valid comparative assessment among irrigation and phytohormone treatments. It is acknowledged that container-based systems may influence root architecture and soil moisture dynamics. Therefore, root data should be interpreted as relative treatment responses rather than absolute representations of unrestricted field-grown root systems.

At the flowering stage, the following root parameters were examined: Every specimen’s root length (measured in centimetres) was noted using a meter scale. The fresh weight of roots (g) of each specimen was measured by using an electronic weighing balance; roots and shoots were separated at the time of the flowering stage, where plants were cut from the bottom shoot area. After that, the samples were oven dried for 72 h at 70 °C, and root dry weights were measured.

Antioxidant enzyme assay

Leaf samples for antioxidant assays were collected between 9:00 and10:00 AM to minimize diurnal variation. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. A leaf sample size of 0.5 g was drawn from each treatment, crushed, and added to 5 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer in an ice bath. The mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 15 min at 4˚C. The recovered supernatant was then utilized to measure the antioxidant enzyme’s activity. The antioxidant activity of Peroxidase and Catalase was determined by the method of Chance et al.21

Peroxidase (POD) activity was evaluated by guaiacol oxidation and expressed as the rate of increase in absorbance at 470 nm defined as0.01 absorbance change min− 1 mg− 1 protein. The reaction mixture was prepared by mixing 400 µL guaiacol (20 mM), 500 µL H2O2 (40 mM), and 2 mL phosphate (50 mM) in 100 µL enzyme extract. The change in absorbance at 470 nm of the reaction mixture was recorded every 20 s up to 5 min and POD activity was reported as mmol min-1 mg-1 protein.

Catalase (CAT) activity was determined by measuring the rate of H2O2 decomposition at 240 nm. The reaction mixture consisted of 2.0 mL phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0), 1.0 mL H2O2 solution (12.5 mM), and 100 µL enzyme extract. The decrease in absorbance of the reaction mixture was recorded at 240 nm every 20 s for 3 min. CAT activity was expressed as mmol H₂O₂ decomposed min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ protein.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was conducted in a split-plot design with three replications, where irrigation levels were assigned to main plots and phytohormone treatments to subplots. Data was analyzed using a mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA) appropriate for a split-plot design. Prior to analysis, assumptions of ANOVA were verified. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 21.0 IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was examined using Levene’s test. When necessary, data were transformed to meet ANOVA assumptions; however, untransformed means are presented for clarity. where Y = year, I = irrigation level, P = phytohormone treatment, and R = replication nested within year.

In the mixed model, Year and replication were treated as random factors, while irrigation and phytohormone treatments were fixed effects to allow broader inference beyond the experimental seasons. The combined mixed-model analysis demonstrated that although environmental conditions differed between the two growing seasons, the relative superiority of salicylic acid and the 0.8–1.0 ETc irrigation regimes remained generally consistent, indicating reasonable treatment stability across years. Significant year interactions, where present, primarily reflected differences in rainfall and temperature patterns between seasons rather than changes in treatment ranking.

When higher-order interactions (Year × Irrigation × Phytohormone) were significant, simple-effect analyses were performed and treatment means were interpreted within each year to avoid misleading pooled interpretations.

When ANOVA indicated significant treatment effects, mean separation was performed using DMRT at P ≤ 0.05 to compare treatment means while controlling Type I error across multiple comparisons. Main effects and interaction effects were significant at P ≤ 0.05. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate relationships among root morphological, physiological, and yield attributes. Significance levels were determined at P ≤ 0.05 and P ≤ 0.01. In addition to significance testing, percentage change relative to the control treatment was calculated to quantify the magnitude of treatment responses.

Economic analysis

Prevailing market values throughout every growing season formed the basis of cost estimates. Using grain and straw yields together with their corresponding market prices, gross returns were calculated. The Government of India’s Minimum Support Price (MSP) announcement determined grain prices (₹2015 q⁻¹ in 2022–23 and ₹2125 q⁻¹ in 2023–24). Local market prices current during the relevant seasons helped to project straw value. The cost of cultivation covered costs associated with seed, fertilizers, labor, irrigation water application, electricity for pump operation, phytohormone acquisition and application, plant protection strategies, and harvesting operations. Gross returns to total cultivation costs were used to determine benefit-cost ratio; net returns were determined by deducting total cultivation expenses from gross returns.

Results

Root morphological attributes statistically significant by drip irrigation levels, phytohormone application and their interaction during 2022-23 and 2023-24. Compared with 0.6 ETc, drip irrigation (DI) at 1.0 ETc resulted in 40.2 and 37.5% more number of roots during both seasons. Among phytohormone, foliar application of salicylic acid (SA) associated with the maximum root numbers 58.2 and 55.1% as compared to abscisic acid (ABA), 44.0 and 41.1% and gibberellic acid (GA) 42.2 and 40.9% than control (Table 4; Fig. 3a). The minimum root numbers were recorded at 0.6 ETc without any phytohormone application. Data regarding root dry weight of wheat is depicted in Table 4; Fig. 2c which showed maximum root dry weight which was 44.1% and 40.8% higher than the deficit irrigation level of 0.6 ETc during both seasons. Reduced soil moisture under deficit irrigation significantly limited root biomass accumulation. Among phytohormone treatments, foliar application of SA yielded the highest root dry weight (1.55 g in 2022–23 and 1.66 g in 2023–24), relative to GA, ABA, and control treatments. Compared to GA- and ABA-treated plots, salicylic acid enhanced root dry weight by 18.4% and 18.5%, and by 26.2% and 23.8% during 2022–23 and 2023–24, respectively. The lowest root dry weight was observed in control plots (1.02 g in 2022–23 and 1.21 g in 2023–24). Even under moisture-stress conditions at 0.6 ETc, SA application increased root dry weight by 64.3% in 2022–23 and 50.5% in 2023–24 compared with the control, indicating its effectiveness in alleviating drought-induced root growth inhibition. Shoots dry weight was following similar trends (Table 4; Fig. 2d). In deficit-irrigation condition, SA through water spray recorded the maximum shoot dry weight (53.3% in 2022–23 and 47.8% in 2023–24) than water spray.

Root-to-shoot ratio at 90 DAS was statistically significant under various irrigation regimes and phytohormones (Table 4; Fig. 3c). Maximum root-to-shoot ratio was observed at 1.0 ETc i.e. 25.5% higher in 2022–23 and 22.2% higher in 2023–24 than that at 0.6 ETc indicating that root biomass was suppressed more than shoot under severe moisture stress. Maximum root-to-shoot ratio (0.51 in 2022–23 and 0.52 in 2023–24) was observed in SA application and was at par with ABA and GA in 2022–23, while minimum root-to-shoot ratio (0.46) was observed in control. Discussion

Irrigation effects on root architecture and biomass allocation

Water is considered as one of the most important factors influencing root architecture and biomass partitioning in crops. The present investigation confirmed the effect of irrigation levels on root architecture, growth and yield associated physiological attributes of wheat during two consecutive growing seasons. Root system plasticity towards varying water regimes confirmed that root growth of wheat was adjusting with the soil water availability. Under deficit irrigation (0.6 ETc), wheat plants showed more root length and specific root length than under adequately watered circumstances. This answer is often seen as a flexible mechanism that helps plants to search a bigger soil volume for water intake in dry surroundings. However, greater root elongation shouldn’t be interpreted as direct evidence of better plant performance. The simultaneous decreases in root biomass, shoot biomass, relative water content, and grain output suggest that plants were still under significant water stress. Therefore, the reported rise in root length is mostly a drought-induced adaptive reaction and an indication of water stress instead of a functional enhancement in production. Such root changes might help to somewhat increase water intake, but the reduction in grain production under 0.6 ETc shows that root elongation by itself was insufficient to completely offset the degree of water shortage. Drought-induced root elongation is a well-known survival growth response in cereals22,23. The hormonal regulation of this response to moderate stress is not well understood, but a recent study shows that abscisic acid plays a role in the regulation of root auxin transport in root tips under moderate stress24. Growth responses similar to drought-induced root extension are common to many growths under water-limited conditions such as wheat and other crops25,26. Root length increased significantly with deficit irrigation compared to full ETc, but root biomass (both fresh and dry weight) decreased dramatically at 0.6 ETc. The rise in root length seen under 0.6 ETc should be taken mostly as an adaptive reaction to water shortage instead of as direct proof of better plant performance. Drought-stressed plants sometimes devote resources to root development in order to explore more soil volume for water intake. Under extreme water deprivation, however, grain yield and biomass accumulation decreased even if root length improved; this suggests that more root elongation alone was not enough to totally offset the lower water availability. Root development under drought hence should be seen as a stress-adaptive mechanism with little utility under extreme moisture limits27–29. At 1.0 ETc, high water application resulted in more roots, root biomass and shoot growth. Soil water adequate to support plant growth promotes cell elongation, root branching and translocation of assimilates to growing roots and thus root growth30. Higher root to shoot ratio at high water application indicates optimal growth and that plants are allocating biomass for optimal nutrient and water uptake, a phenomenon previously reported31–33, which is an indicator of healthy root growth and increased shoot growth. Seasonal differences in temperature and rainfall influenced the extent of drought stress and the magnitude of between year responses. Water stress under deficit irrigation was more severe in 2023–24 due to a lower rainfall and a cooler temperature regime (lower minimum temperature and larger diurnal range). Inter-annual variability such as this should be considered when planning field experiments examining the effects of irrigation regimes.

Physiological responses to water deficit

The relative water content (RWC), membrane stability index (MSI), canopy temperature and chlorophyll index of the plants were significantly affected by irrigation regime. Dryland irrigation resulted in lower RWC and MSI values and higher canopy temperature and electrolyte leakage.

The leaf relative water content (RWC) is a parameter reflecting the plant water status under drought stress34. A low RWC value (less than 0.6 ETc) indicates that the plant is not absorbing enough water to maintain adequate tissue turgor pressure and cellular development and growth. A low RWC value is often associated with stomatal closure and therefore reduced transpiration and photosynthesis35,36. The strong negative correlation between canopy temperature and RWC further confirms the effect of reduced transpiration due to water stress that results in reduced transpiration cooling, and hence an increase in leaf surface temperature.

Membrane stability index (MSI) declined significantly and consistently with increasing water stress and was related to increased oxidative damage to the cellular membrane. High water stress led to increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in plants such as superoxide radicals (SO) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)37. ROS can cause damage to lipid membrane components which leads to increased electrolyte leakage and decreased membrane stability38,39. Higher electrolyte leakage under deficit irrigation further confirmed membrane destabilization due to oxidative stress40.

Chlorophyll content declined significantly under water limiting conditions. It has been reported that drought stress generally increases the activity of chlorophyllase enzyme, which catalyzes the breakdown of chlorophyll by causing oxidative damage to the chloroplast membrane41,42. The decrease in chlorophyll content decreases the light harvesting efficiency that further reduces the photosynthesis and subsequently biomass and yield of the crop causing yield losses43. The higher chlorophyll index under 1.0 ETc irrigated treatment indicates higher photosynthetic rate due to adequate soil moisture2,13.

Under the 0.6 ETc regime, the activity of the antioxidant enzymes, catalase and peroxidase, was greater than under the normal irrigation regime. Higher antioxidant activity in drought-stressed plants has been attributed to a defense mechanism that regulates cellular redox balance by eliminating ROS44,45. Thus, the higher catalase and peroxidase activities under the 0.6 ETc regime may be indicative of a defense response to neutralize ROS.

Influence of phytohormones on growth and stress mitigation

Foliar application of phytohormones significantly modified root growth, physiological stability, and productivity, with salicylic acid (SA) exhibiting the most consistent positive effect across irrigation regimes.SA was associated with increasedroot length, root biomass, and shoot growth compared to the control and other hormones. Salicylic acid functions as a signalling molecule regulating cell division, elongation, and stress-responsive gene expression46,47. Enhanced root elongation under SA treatment may result from modulation of hormonal crosstalk and stimulation of meristematic activity48. Increased biomass accumulation under SA application is likely associated with improved photosynthetic efficiency and improved soil water extraction, which was reflected in higher relative water content and reduced canopy temperature. These physiological advantages contributed to improved biomass accumulation and ultimately supported higher grain yield.16,49. Physiologically, The improvement in RWC and MSI under SA application likely contributed to better membrane stability, which in turn supported sustained photosynthetic activity and biomass accumulation. Maintenance of higher RWC under SA treatment suggests improved osmotic adjustment and partial regulation of stomatal conductance50,51. SA-induced stabilization of cellular membranes has been attributed to reduced lipid peroxidation and enhanced antioxidant defense52,53. Similar protective effects of SA under drought stress have been reported in wheat, barley, and other crops54,55.

Chlorophyll index increased significantly with SA application, indicating improved preservation of photosynthetic pigments. SA is known to suppress chlorophyll degradation and enhance carotenoid synthesis, thereby protecting chloroplast structure under stress56,57. Enhanced chlorophyll content contributes to sustained photosynthesis and assimilate production, ultimately improving yield performance58,59. Antioxidant enzyme activities were further amplified by SA treatment. Increased catalase and peroxidase activity under SA application suggests reinforcement of the ROS-scavenging system51,60. By enhancing antioxidant capacity, SA reduces oxidative injury and preserves metabolic integrity under water stress61,62. In contrast with the control, exogenous application of GA and ABA also enhanced some stress resistance properties, but to a less extent compared with SA. Except for the regulation of stomatal movement and drought stress signal transduction, ABA is generally involved in plant growth and development processes such as cell expansion and regulation of plant morphology and growth24,63. While SA is involved in multitasking functions in stress conditions including maintaining the antioxidant system, osmotic adjustment and photosynthesis64.

Interaction between irrigation and phytohormones

Irrigation regime × phytohormone interaction was significant for most of the traits, which suggests that exogenous application of plant hormones changed the plant response to various irrigation regimes. Compared with the severe deficit irrigation (0.6 ETc) alone treatment, the application of SA to soil not only partly relieved the growth inhibition caused by drought stress through enhancing the activities of antioxidant defense-related enzymes, maintaining the integrity of membrane structure and relative water content (RC), thereby participating in osmotic adjustment and scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) to strengthen the drought stress resistance of wheat. In addition, SA also exhibited a synergistic effect on water stress in wheat and other crops, such as SA on wheat seedling under water stress65 and SA on barley seedling under drought55. Effect of SA and mild water deficit on spring barley growth and yield Irrigation at 0.8 ETc with SA application resulted in a yield that did not differ significantly from that under full irrigation and was clearly higher than under SA application alone. In total the results suggest that mild water deficit in conjunction with SA application can ensure high water use efficiency without a yield penalty. This is in addition to the alleviation of oxygen stress in the soil. It also suggests that the application of SA can result in more beneficial root growth and plant physiology under mild water deficit.

Yield implications and agronomic significance

Grain and straw yields were increased with full irrigation plus SA treatments, whereas moderate level of deficit irrigation with SA maintained yields at the same level as those under full irrigation. Severe deficit irrigation is known to decrease photosynthetic rates, decrease assimilate translocation, and shorten the grain filling period66,67.

The chlorophyll index, shoot biomass and grain yield showed positive correlation with each other. This indicates that efficient photosynthesis is a prerequisite for maintaining crop yields under different irrigation regimes. There was also positive correlation between root biomass and crop yield. This experiment explored the potential of using supplemental irrigation (ETc) with drip irrigation and foliar applied SA as a tool to enhance wheat stress resistance under water scarcity. Maximum yield was obtained under the fully irrigated (100% ETc) condition while the moderate deficit irrigation (MDI) + SA treatment may provide a water saving alternative with minimal yield reduction. The need to save groundwater resources and in light of changing climate, such irrigation practices are extremely important.

Apparently, the higher profitability with I₁P₃ is due to more moisture, more physiological efficiency because of greater plant growth and increase yield and thus more income. Adequate water supply enables better nutrient uptake and utilization by the plant for biomass production, which is further enhanced by plant growth regulators, thereby making an effective source sink relationship and hence greater yield. Apparently, the lower income with I₃P₁ is due to water stress and insufficient physiological stimulation.

Wheat is a seasonal crop and our experiment is designed to understand its growth and developmental characteristics. Wheat experiments under our study have inherent fluctuations. During the year 2023–24, the lower rainfall and lower minimum temperature is resulting in higher severity of drought under deficit irrigation, thus bringing out more variability among the treatments. Such inter-annual variations are quite common in field experiments and to understand the long term effect of different irrigation and stress management regimes, experiments have to be conducted for multiple years.

One possible shortcoming of this experiment is that root observations were conducted in confined root observation units, which may lead to differential lateral root growth and soil moisture distribution when roots are grown in the open field. In-situ root imaging or soil coring experiments in the field would be needed to verify this prediction.

Considering the insights acquired, the study had several limitations. The experiment was conducted at a single location under specific agro-climatic conditions, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Seasonal variability was observed between the two years, particularly in rainfall and temperature, influencing treatment responses. Furthermore, root observations were performed using confined root units, which may not fully represent field-scale root dynamics. Therefore, caution should be exercised in extrapolating these results to other environments, soil types, or wheat cultivars. To verify the constancy of these responses, multi-location and multi-season studies are necessary.

Conclusion

Though salicylic acid and irrigation at 0.8 ETc cut the amount of irrigation water used by about 20%, it maintained statistically equivalent yields to full irrigation (1.0 ETc) through drip. While maximizing water output, this treatment combination improved root biomass, antioxidant defense, and physiological stability. Therefore, a sensible and commercially viable strategy for wheat cultivated in water-stressed regions might be irrigation at 0.8 ETc taken with salicylic acid treatment. This technique helps to maintain farm profitability and crop productivity under varying climate while also helping to conserve groundwater. The novelty of the study is in the concurrent assessment of phytohormone treatment and ETc-based drip irrigation planning on wheat’s root architecture, physiological characteristics, antioxidant responses, and production under field settings. The findings show that salicylic acid helps to keep physiological stability and productivity while lowering irrigation water use, therefore supplementing deficit irrigation methods. The experiment was based on single location with two growth seasons and should be validated under diverse agro-climatic condition. Root observations were made in pots and therefore results cannot be extrapolated to field conditions. Furthermore, soil moisture dynamics were not continuously monitored throughout the growing season, limiting detailed assessment of soil-water extraction patterns. The study also did not quantify endogenous phytohormone concentrations or molecular responses associated with drought adaptation.

Further research should focus on multi-location multi-year experiments to confirm the current results and test the results using a fully intact field grown root system. Similarly, integrating soil moisture monitoring, hormonal profiling, and molecular analyses would provide deeper mechanistic understanding of plant responses under deficit irrigation. Long-term application of these principles and additional irrigation technologies can lead to the development of irrigation- and crop management practices that are water-use efficient and sustainable in the face of global climate change.
