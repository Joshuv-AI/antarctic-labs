Abstract
. We define a class of algebras, the semilattices of Mal’cev blocks (for short, SMB algebras). In a nutshell, these algebras are semilattices in which each element gets blown up into a Mal’cev algebra. We publish for the first time our old proofs that some SMB algebras induce tractable templates of the reprove that the Constraint Satisfaction Problem. Next, we reprove that, in fact, all SMB algebras induce tractable templates of the Constraint Satisfaction Problem, a result already proved by A. Bulatov. Also, we compare the two general proofs of the CSP Dichotomy and prove they are more similar than initially thought when they are applied to SMB algebras. This paper is the second in the series of papers investigating the SMB algebras and it is a precursor to our further research on the similarities between the proofs of the Dichotomy Theorem.



1.
Introduction, history and motivation

This paper concerns the computational complexity of the
Constraint Satisfaction Problem
(CSP). CSP is a decision problem, and it has several equivalent formulations. The versions we will consider in this paper (the variable-value version and the multisorted version) are defined in Section 2. We prove that certain algebraic restrictions guarantee that the problem is tractable.

1.1.
History

The interest in CSP has its roots in Descriptive Complexity. In the seminal paper by Feder and Vardi
[
21
]
, the complexity of the fixed-template CSP was proved to be computationally equivalent up to random-P reductions to the class of model-checking problems defined by monotone monadic SNP formulae without inequalities (MMSNP). Later the reduction was derandomized by Kun
[
25
]
, so the two classes of problems are computationally equivalent (they traverse the same complexity classes). Feder and Vardi, also in
[
21
]
, conjectured that the CSP can have only two complexities, tractable and NP-complete. Since the reduction by Kun, this Dichotomy Conjecture also implies that model-checking the MMSNP formulae can have only these two complexities.

In
[
24
]
, Jeavons proved that the complexity of the CSP depends on the algebra of compatible operations (polymorphisms) of the template model. Subsequently, this was sharpened by Bulatov, Jeavons and Krokhin
[
17
]
, who proved that, in fact, the pseudovariety generated by the algebra of idempotent polymorphisms of the core controls the complexity. In the same paper, they also proved that if there is no polymorphism which satisfies a set of Taylor equations, then the problem is NP-complete and conjectured that in the converse situation, CSP is tractable. This second conjecture, which would not only imply the Dichotomy Conjecture, but would delineate the bound between P and NP-complete cases, was known as the Algebraic Dichotomy Conjecture. The Algebraic Dichotomy Conjecture was finally settled positively, independently by Bulatov (
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
) and by Zhuk (
[
31
]
and
[
32
]
), thus also confirming the original Dichotomy Conjecture.

Though the Dichotomy Conjecture is resolved, both proofs are very complicated, and it is desirable to find simplifications. This is not just for its own sake, the CSP Dichotomy could lead to major generalizations (e.g. finding “Logic for
P
” in Descriptive Complexity), if only its proof was simpler and more versatile. For a more detailed list of other possible applications of CSP, see, e.g. the final section of
[
15
]
.

In this paper we will look at a special case of finite algebras with a Taylor operation, equivalently, a weak near-unanimity operation (see
[
29
]
). The first and the third author invented these algebras, which we call the SMB algebras, in an unpublished note
[
26
]
. Our hope at the time was to prove CSP over SMB algebras is tractable, and then to generalize from SMB algebras to Taylor algebras. We were only partially successful in
[
26
]
, proving the tractability in the case when the underlying semilattice is either linearly ordered, or a flat semilattice. The second author, in another unpublished note
[
28
]
, improved to the case when the underlying semilattice is ordered as a rooted tree. In the present paper we unite these two notes and publish them for the first time. Subsequently, Bulatov managed to prove in
[
9
]
that CSP over any SMB algebra is tractable, and shortly later used these ideas to prove the Dichotomy Theorem. We fix a gap we discovered in his proof is Sections 5 and 6 in two different ways.

1.2.
Motivation

The reason for our focus on SMB algebras is that they exhibit several “bad” properties, thus being in the “worst case” (in the sense of Tame Congruence Theory) of algebras with a Taylor operation. On the other hand, Barto and Kozik’s famous Absorption Theorem, a major structural property of binary relations compatible with Taylor algebras, is much easier to prove in SMB algebras than in the general Taylor case. Moreover, “binary absorption”, one of the main cases considered by Zhuk in his proof of the Dichotomy, trivially appears all over SMB algebras. So SMB algebras seem to be a good special case to consider before trying to prove any result about Taylor algebras. In a companion paper
[
20
]
(authored by the first, third and fourth author of this paper, and P.
-
D
apić) we prove some universal algebraic results about SMB algebras and some properties of compatible relations which may prove useful for our overall plan.

A few words about relation with other results. The algorithm of Theorem

is inspired by, and generalizes, a special case considered in
[
6

We draw inspiration for defining the SMB algebras from Tame Congruence Theory as developed in the monograph
[

and also from a series of results of Bulatov, in papers
[

Namely, if we assume that every two element subset is a subalgebra, the paper
[
8
]
has distinguished between three types of behavior of the polymorphisms on the two-element subsets of
A

[
10

[

In
[
12

, he proved that, if there are no Mal’cev edges in the resulting graph of the algebra, then the corresponding CSP has
bounded width
and therefore it is tractable, according to
[
4

[

[

(see
[

[
23

As for Tame Congruence Theory, we know that if an idempotent algebra with a weak near-unanimity operation has no Mal’cev factors of subalgebras, then the CSP has bounded width, while the absence of the semilattice type, together with a technical condition (the absence of tails in the minimal sets) means that the algebra of polymorphisms generates a congruence modular variety, and this implies that the corresponding CSP has few subpowers, according to
[
2

Hence, in both Bulatov’s and Tame Congruence Theoretic approaches, absence of either the semilattice or Mal’cev case implies the tractability of the corresponding CSP is easy, either always, or in a major subcase. So, we need to consider algebras which exhibit both the semilattice and Mal’cev local behavior. In that direction, the second author proved in
[
27
]
that if there is a congruence such that its quotient algebra has a Mal’cev operation, while each congruence class, viewed as a subalgebra, contains no Mal’cev factor of a subalgebra, then the CSP is tractable. So the natural case to consider are algebras which have a congruence modulo which the algebra is a semilattice, while each block of that congruence is a Mal’cev algebra. Add a condition that ensures the operations don’t interfere with each other, and we get SMB algebras.

2.
Background and Notation

2.1.
Universal Algebra

We assume that the reader is familiar with the basics of Universal Algebra. The readers who need these facts and definitions are referred to classic textbooks
[
19
]
and
[
30
]
. Moreover, we need some basic results of Tame Congruence Theory, an advanced theory in Universal Algebra which was developed in
[
22
]
.

Following
[
30
]
, we use the notation
{\rm Clo\>{\mathbf{A}}}
and
{\rm Clo_{n}{\mathbf{A}}}
for the clone of term operations and the set of
n
-ary term operations of the algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
, respectively. Also,
{\rm Pol\>{\mathbf{A}}}
and
{\rm Pol_{n}{\mathbf{A}}}
denote the clone of polynomial operations and the set of
n
-ary polynomial operations of the algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
, respectively.

An operation
f
of an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
is said to be idempotent if the identity
f(x,x,\ldots,x)\approx x
holds in
{\mathbf{A}}
. An algebra is idempotent if all of its fundamental operations (equivalently, term operations) are idempotent. We will assume all algebras are idempotent and finite in this paper. Furthermore, we will introduce one more restriction on the algebras we consider and one construction on algebras, which we will use at will. The reason is that we are interested in application of algebras to the Constraint Satisfaction Problem, so any constructions which make the Constraint Satisfaction Problem no easier will be allowed.

The third restriction on the algebras under consideration is that we are interested in algebras which generate varieties that omit type
1
covers (in the language of Tame Congruence Theory). Equivalently, according to
[
29
]
, we may assume for any algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
that there exists a term
w
such that the identities

w(x,x,\ldots,x,y)\approx w(x,x,\ldots,x,y,x)\approx\ldots\approx w(y,x,x,\ldots,x)

hold in
{\mathbf{A}}
. Those identities, plus idempotence (which we need not assume again) make
w
a
weak near-unanimity
term of
{\mathbf{A}}
, or a wnu term for short. We introduce the notation
x\circ_{w}y
for the binary term operation
w(x,x,\ldots,x,y)
. A wnu term
w
of
{\mathbf{A}}
is
special
if
{\mathbf{A}}
satisfies the additional identity
x\circ_{w}(x\circ_{w}y)\approx x\circ_{w}y
. Any finite algebra which has a wnu term must also have a special wnu term obtained by iterated composition of the original wnu term (cf.
[
29
, Lemma 4.7]
).

When we have a finite idempotent algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
with a wnu term
w
and
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
is another algebra on the same set with a wnu term such that each fundamental operation of
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
is a term operation of the algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
, we may start considering
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
instead of
{\mathbf{A}}
when this suits our purpose. We will justify this in the next subsection.

Finally, if
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra,
p\in{\rm Pol_{1}{\mathbf{A}}}
and
p(p(x))=p(x)
for all
x\in A
, then the polynomial
p
is called a retraction. The retraction
p
induces an algebra
p({\mathbf{A}})
on the set
p(A)
with the same similarity type as
{\mathbf{A}}
in the following way: If
f
is an
n
-ary operation symbol then
f^{p({\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}})}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=p(f^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))
. It is easy to verify that, if
{\mathbf{A}}
is idempotent, finite and/or has a wnu term, then
p({\mathbf{A}})
has the same properties. The algebra
p({\mathbf{A}})
is called a
retract
of
{\mathbf{A}}
. We will use retraction in Section 4, but it is not a construction which reduces to an equivalent, or a more difficult, problem, like all the previous ones. We will have to prove under which conditions we can work with retracts and what can we conclude about the original instance when we solve the instance over its retracts.

We proceed with an introduction to the Constraint Satisfaction Problem to justify these three restrictions and the constructions.

2.2.
CSP

The Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP for short) has several equivalent definitions, each offering slightly different language to express the same thing. We find it most convenient to define it similarly as in
[
1
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A relation on the set
A
is a subset of
A^{n}
for some positive integer
n
. Here
n
is the
arity
of that relation. By a
constraint language
we mean a set
\Gamma
of relations (of any arities) on the same nonvoid base set
A
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a constraint language
\Gamma
on the set
A
, we define an instance of
CSP(\Gamma)
as any ordered triple
(V,A,{\mathcal{C}})
where
V
is called the set of variables, while
{\mathcal{C}}
is a set whose elements are called constraints. Each constraint is an ordered pair
(S,R)
, where
S\subseteq V
is the constraint scope, while
R\subseteq A^{S}
is such that there exist an integer
n
and a surjective mapping
\varphi:n\rightarrow S
such that
R\circ\varphi\in\Gamma
. Here
R\circ\varphi=\{g\circ\varphi:g\in R\}
.
R
is the constraint relation of the constraint
(S,R)
. A mapping
f:V\rightarrow A
is a solution to the instance
(V,A,{\mathcal{C}})
of
CSP(\Gamma)
if for every constraint
(R,S)\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
f|_{S}\in R
.

In Section 3, we will assume that the variable set
V
is
n=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, to have a linear order on the variables. We will sometimes write
[n]
instead of
n
to remind the reader that
n
is a set, with elements, subsets etc.

We may assume, without loss of generality, that different constraints have different scopes. This is because we may intersect all constraint relations with the same scope and replace all of those constraints with the single constraint. The way we defined
CSP(\Gamma)
allows us to also assume, without loss of generality, that
\Gamma
is closed under intersection, and also under permutation and identification of coordinates. Otherwise, our definition is the same as in
[
1
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
P
be an instance of CSP
(\Gamma)
. We say that
P
is
(k,l)
-minimal if

•

for any
S\subseteq V
,
|S|\leq l
, there is precisely one
i
such that
S_{i}=S
(
l
-density) and

•

whenever
(S,R)
and
(S^{\prime},R^{\prime})
are constraints such that
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
and
|S^{\prime}|=k
, then
R^{\prime}=R|_{S^{\prime}}
(
k
-consistency).

By using the
(k,l)
-minimality algorithm (see e.g.
[
1
]
), for any given fixed numbers
k\leq l
we can transform, in polynomial time, a given instance of
CSP(\Gamma)
into an equivalent
(k,l)
-minimal instance. Note that the second condition implies that each constraint relation
R
is a subdirect product of
R_{j}
,
j\in S
, where
(\{j\},R_{j})
are constraints in
{\mathcal{C}}
. Whenever an instance is at least
(1,1)
-minimal,
R_{j}
will be fixed and we will denote it by
A_{j}
.

For the last three decades or so, the main focus in the investigation of computational complexity of the Constraint Satisfaction Problem was the Dichotomy Conjecture of Feder and Vardi, stated in
[
21
]
, which said that the complexity of
CSP(\Gamma)
can be either tractable, or NP-complete. Our paper consists of old never-published results, which were partial results aimed at proving the Dichotomy Conjecture and influenced Bulatov’s proof
[
14
]
, and some new results which compare the two proofs of the Dichotomy Conjecture and suggest a direction for their simplification. Note that Feder and Vardi postulated the conjecture only when
\Gamma
is finite, but we do not make that requirement here. On the other hand, we do assume that the domain
A
is finite throughout this paper. According to
[
24
]
, the complexity of the
CSP(\Gamma)
depends on the compatible operations (polymorphisms) of the relational structure
(A,\Gamma)
. Therefore, it suffices to verify the conjecture for
\Gamma={\sf{SP}}_{fin}({\mathbf{A}})
for some finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
. More precisely (to conform to our definitions) we may assume that
\Gamma=\bigcup\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}{\sf{S}}({\mathbf{A}}^{n})
and we will denote such
\Gamma
by
\Gamma({\mathbf{A}})
. The impact of this assumption on
\Gamma
is that now
\Gamma
contains all full finite powers of
A
and that
\Gamma
is closed under intersections and products (along with permutations and identifications of variables assumed earlier), thus
\Gamma
is a
relational clone
.

This justifies our construction where we move from an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
to one of its term reducts
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
. Namely, any relation compatible with all operations of
{\mathbf{A}}
is compatible with all term operations of
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}
, as well. Therefore, any instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
is an instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}}^{\prime})
, so if we can solve any instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}}^{\prime})
in polynomial time, we can do the same with instances of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
.

We may assume that the relational structure
(A,\Gamma)
has no endomorphisms except for automorphisms. Namely, if
\varphi
is an endomorphism which maps
A
onto a proper subset, then the set of instances of
CSP(\Gamma)
which have a solution is precisely the same as the set of instances of
CSP(\Gamma|_{\varphi(A)})
on the domain
\varphi(A)
which has a solution (in the nontrivial direction, just compose the solution of
CSP(\Gamma)
with the endomorphism). Such relational structures for which all endomorphisms are automorphisms are called
cores
.

For
(A,\Gamma)
a core, the complexity of
CSP(\Gamma)
equals the complexity of
CSP(\Gamma^{c})
, which is
\Gamma
augmented with all one-element unary relations. If
\Gamma=\Gamma({\mathbf{A}})
, then
\Gamma^{c}=\Gamma({\mathbf{A}}^{id})
, where
{\mathbf{A}}^{id}
is the idempotent reduct of
{\mathbf{A}}
, i.e.
{\mathbf{A}}^{id}
is an algebra whose operations are all idempotent term operations in
{\rm Clo\>{\mathbf{A}}}
. So, we justified the focus on idempotent finite algebras and assume from now on that all algebras are such. For more details about the reductions in this and the previous paragraph, cf. Theorems 4.4 and 4.7 of
[
17
]
.

As we mentioned in the Introduction, if
{\mathbf{A}}
has no wnu term then, according to
[
17
]
and
[
29
]
,
CSP(\Gamma({\mathbf{A}}))
is NP-complete. In
[
17
]
it is conjectured that in the converse case the
CSP(\Gamma({\mathbf{A}}))
is in P. A proof of this, the Algebraic Dichotomy Conjecture, in the papers
[
32
]
and
[
14
]
thus also confirmed the original Dichotomy Conjecture by Feder and Vardi.

In our paper we will use, as a black box, that
CSP(\Gamma({\mathbf{A}}))
is tractable when
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra with Mal’cev term
d(x,y,z)
, i.e. when the identities
d(x,x,y)\approx y\approx d(y,x,x)
hold in
{\mathbf{A}}
. The proof of this was published in
[
7
]
and a shorter one in
[
16
]
.

2.3.
Multisorted CSP and templates

Already when we reduce an instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
to a
(k,l)
-minimal one, we introduced subuniverses
A_{i}
of
{\mathbf{A}}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Instead of
R\leq A^{S}
, we can consider
R
as a subdirect product of
\{{\mathbf{A}}_{i}:i\in S\}
. For some reductions we will also need
A_{i}
to be homomorphic images of
{\mathbf{A}}
(or of its subalgebra), or a retract of
{\mathbf{A}}
(or of its subalgebra).

Now we define a
template
of CSP.

Definition 2.4
.

A class
{\mathcal{T}}
of isomorphism types of similar finite algebras is called a CSP template if
{\mathcal{T}}
is closed under homomorphic images, subalgebras and unary polynomial retracts.

Note that we do not allow isomorphic algebras to appear more than once in a template, which is why we speak of isomorphism types, rather than algebras themselves. The reason is, if the language of a finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
is finite, then there are only finitely many algebras, up to isomorphism, which can be obtained from
{\mathbf{A}}
by taking subalgebras, homomorphic images and retracts. This will allow us, in Section 4, to reduce a multisorted CSP instance by making the template a smaller set of isomorphism types. Now we define the multisorted CSP instance.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a template
{\mathcal{T}}
, a (multisorted) instance
P
of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
is the triple
(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
. Here
D=\{{\mathbf{A}}_{i}:i\in V\}
, where each
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
is isomorphic to some algebra in
{\mathcal{T}}
, is the tuple of domains, while
{\mathcal{C}}
is the set of constraints. Each constraint is an ordered pair
(S,R)
, where
S\subseteq V
, while
R\leq\prod\limits_{i\in S}{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
is a subdirect product. A mapping
f\in\prod\limits_{i\in V}A_{i}
is a solution to the instance
(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
if for every constraint
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
f|_{S}\in R
.

Of course, we can take as the template
{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}})
, the set of isomorphism types of all finite algebras which can be obtained from a finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
by taking homomorphic images, subalgebras and retracts. Then each
(1,1)
-minimal instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
is an instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
. Moreover, if
{\mathbf{A}}
has a finite language, then
{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}})
is finite, as we said above.

A construction used repeatedly in the
(k,n)
-minimality algorithm, and also in many procedures in our paper, is the
tightening
of an instance
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
of a template
{\mathcal{T}}
. If
D=\{{\mathbf{A}}_{i}:i\in V\}
and
B_{i}\subseteq A_{i}
, we say that the instance
P^{\prime}=(V,D^{\prime},{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime})
is the tightening of
P
to
D^{\prime}=\{B_{i}:i\in V\}
if
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}=\{(S,R^{\prime}):(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}\}
and for each
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
R^{\prime}=R\cap\prod\limits_{i\in S}B_{i}
. To ensure that
P^{\prime}
is also an instance of
{\mathcal{T}}
, it suffices for all
i\in V
to have
B_{i}
as a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
, and indeed, this is what we will always ensure whenever we tighten an instance. Moreover, for any tightening
P^{\prime}
of an instance
P
which will be used in this paper, we will prove that
P^{\prime}
has a solution iff
P
does and in such a case we will say that
P

can be tightened
.

Another construction which we will repeatedly use is be the
restriction

P|_{W}
of an instance
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
to a subset
W\subseteq V
.
P|_{W}
is the instance
(W,D^{\prime},{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime})
, where
D^{\prime}=\{{\mathbf{A}}_{i}:i\in W\}
, while
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}=\{(S^{\prime},R^{\prime}):(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}\}
, where
S^{\prime}=S\cap W
, while
R^{\prime}=R\cap\prod\limits_{i\in S^{\prime}}A_{i}
. Bear in mind that for several

, the intersection of

with

can be the same, so we will actually silently take the intersection of all corresponding

to make

, but this detail will never cause problems so we simply gloss over it.

Another way of encoding a CSP instance was used by Zhuk in his proof of the Dichotomy Conjecture. Take a multisorted instance
(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
over the template
{\mathcal{T}}
. We construct two hypergraphs, one over the set of vertices

(the constraints graph), and the other over the union of the domains
\bigcup D=\bigcup\{A_{i}:i\in V\}
(the microstructure graph). For each constraint
(S,R)
in
{\mathcal{C}}
, we add one hyperedge
S
in the constraints graph, and for each tuple
f:S\rightarrow\bigcup D
in
R
we add a hyperedge
\{f(s):s\in S\}
into the microstructure graph. Each hyperedge of the microstructure graph intersects each

in one point or not at all (depending on whether

).

2.4.
Tame Congruence Theory

We will work on
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
for certain
{\mathbf{A}}
which have a wnu term. The tame congruence theory analyzes finite algebras according to local behavior of the polynomial operations of the algebra. The polynomial operations are term operations in which some of the variables may have been substituted by fixed constants. The tame congruence theory classifies the covers in the congruence lattice of a finite algebra by first finding a minimal image of an unary polynomial which distinguishes the two congruences (this is an
(\alpha,\beta)
-minimal set). The unary polynomial whose image is the minimal set can be assumed to be idempotent, and if
\alpha\prec\beta
in the congruence lattice
{{\rm Con\>}{\mathbf{A}}}
, the set of all
(\alpha,\beta)
-minimal sets is denoted by
M_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\alpha,\beta)
. The structure of the minimal set together with the polynomial operations of the algebra which are compatible with that minimal set depend only on the congruences which constitute the cover. It may be of five types, unary (type
1
), affine (type
2
), Boolean (type
3
), lattice (type
4
) and semilattice (type
5
). We will write
\alpha\prec_{i}\beta
when the congruences
\alpha
and
\beta
of a finite algebra form a covering pair in the congruence lattice, and the type of that cover is
i
. The absence of the unary type not only in the finite algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
, but also in any finite algebra in the variety
{\mathbf{A}}
generates, is equivalent to the existence of the wnu term in
{\mathbf{A}}
. Thus we may assume that
o type
1
cover occurs.

If
\alpha\prec\beta
in
{{\bf{\rm Con\>}}{\mathbf{A}}}
, and the type of that cover is
5
, then in any minimal set
U
, one
\beta
-class restricts to
U
as
B
, which intersects exactly two
\alpha
-classes, while
\alpha
and
\beta
restrict the same way to
U\setminus B
. We call
B
the body of
U
. The polynomials of
{\mathbf{A}}
restricted to
B/(\alpha|_{B})
are those of a two-element semilattice. Moreover, for any
\beta
-class, the transitive closure of the semilattice orders coming from all bodies of minimal sets is a connected partial order
\leq
of
\alpha
-classes inside that
\beta
-class, and
\leq
is compatible with
{\mathbf{A}}/\alpha
(i.e. a subuniverse of
({\mathbf{A}}/\alpha)^{2}
).

We proved in the companion paper
[
20
]
that when
\alpha\prec_{2}\beta\prec_{5}\gamma
in the congruence lattice of a finite algebra with a Taylor (equivalently, wnu) term, then we can find a subalgebra of a term reduct of
{\mathbf{A}}/\alpha
which has a Taylor term and an interesting structure. The subalgebra is contained in one
\gamma
-class and consists of more than one
\beta
-class. We called such algebras semilattices of Mal’cev blocks, SMB algebras for short, and we will define them in the next section.

3.
Semilattices of Mal’cev Blocks

We begin by defining the main object of study of this paper and recalling several results about it from
[

Definition 3.1
.

Let

We say that

We note in passing that we changed the condition

Theorem 3.2

(Theorem 15 and Proposition 17 of
[

.

The class

of all SMB algebras is a variety with a Taylor term.

Definition 3.3

We say that an SMB algebra

(3)

(4)

It can be proved (see

Therefore, in regular SMB algebras, the clone of all terms is generated by

If

Another special property of SMB algebras we will use is the following

Definition 3.5
.

An SMB algebra

over

is

such that for all

It is clear that in an unital SMB algebra

Proposition 3.6

(Proposition 21 of
[

]
)
.

Let

be a finite SMB algebra. Then there are

In view of Proposition

Now we begin our investigation of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
over an SMB algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
:

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}=\langle A;\wedge,d\rangle
be an SMB algebra with respect to
\sim
. When the order of
\sim
-classes is linear, then

is tractable.

Proof.

We assume, as noted above, that
{\mathbf{A}}
is a regular SMB algebra. Let
P=([n],A,{\mathcal{C}})
be a
(1,1)
-minimal instance of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
. We replace
P
with a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
as in Definition
2.5
. Each domain

is the projection to the coordinate

of
R
, where
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
i\in S
. Since
P
is (1,1)-minimal, the domain

(which is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
) does not depend on the choice of the constraint
(S,R)
. We call the new, multisorted instance also

, since it cannot be distinguished computationally from the original one.

We will call a solution

to a multisorted instance of

a least-block solution when for all

,

is in the least
\sim
-class of the SMB algebra

. If we restrict ourselves only to least-block solutions, then

is a Mal’cev operation compatible with all restrictions of

to least blocks, so it is well-known that we can check whether
P
has a least-block solution in polynomial time.

The algorithm
SOLVE(P)
works like this:

Step 1.

Set the working instance
Q:=P
.

Step 2.

Replace
Q
with the
(1,1)
-minimal equivalent instance. If for any
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}(Q)
,
R
is now empty, output that
P
has no solutions and stop.

Step 3.

Set
i:=n
.

Step 4.

Check whether the restriction
Q|_{[i]}
has a least-block solution using the Mal’cev algorithm.

Step 4.a

If ’YES’ and
i=n
, output that
P
has a solution and stop.

Step 4.b

If ’YES’ and
i<n
, tighten
Q
by replacing
A_{i}
with
A_{i}\setminus D
, where
D
is the least
\sim
-class of
A_{i}
and go to Step 2.

Step 4.c

If ’NO’, set

and go to step 4.

It is clear that, for a nonempty
(1,1)
-minimal

always has a least-block solution, so the decrease of

in Step 4.c must stop at some point (we will not get the answer ’NO’ at

). It is also clear that at each application of Step 4.b we tighten the instance and that it can be applied at most

times. By Corollary
3.4
, the tightened instance is still an instance over a regular SMB algebra with a linear order of

-classes. The running time cost of the algorithm is dominated by at most

applications of
(1,1)
-minimality algorithm in Step 2 and at most

applications of the Mal’cev algorithm in Step 4. So, this algorithm works in polynomial time.

To prove that it faithfully computes whether
P
has a solution, it suffices to prove the following claim

Claim 3.8
.

Let
P
be a
(1,1)
-minimal multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
. If
P|_{[i]}
has a least-block solution and
P|_{[i+1]}
does not, then for every solution
f
of
P
,
f(i)\notin\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
.

(Recall that
\{i\}=[i+1]\setminus[i]
.)

Proof of Claim.
Assume not. Let
f
be a solution of
P
such that
f(i)\in\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
. Let
g\in A^{i}
be the least-block solution of
P|_{[i]}
. We will prove that
\overline{f}\in A^{i+1}
, defined by
\overline{f}(i)=f(i)
and for all
0\leq j<i
,
\overline{f}(j)=f(j)\wedge g(j)
is a solution to
P|_{[i+1]}
. This would be a contradiction, since
P|_{[i+1]}
is assumed not to have a least block solution.

Indeed, let
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}(P|_{[i+1]})
. If
i\notin S
, then
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}(P|_{[i]})
, and since both
f|_{[i]}
and
g
are solutions to
P|_{[i]}
, then so is
f|_{[i]}\wedge g
. Therefore,
\overline{f}|_{S}=f|_{S}\wedge g|_{S}\in R
.

On the other hand, let
i\in S
. Let
S^{\prime}=S\setminus\{i\}
and by the definition of
P|_{[i]}
we know that there exists a constraint
(S^{\prime},R^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{C}}(P|_{[i]})
. Therefore,
g|_{S^{\prime}}\in R^{\prime}\subseteq R|_{S^{\prime}}
. Therefore, there must exist some
\overline{g}\in R
such that
\overline{g}|_{S^{\prime}}=g|_{S^{\prime}}
. Since
f|_{S}\in R
as well,
f|_{S}\wedge\overline{g}\in R
. But, since
f(i)\in\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
,
(f|_{S}\wedge\overline{g})(i)=f(i)\wedge\overline{g}(i)=f(i)
by Definition

(2). Hence,
(f|_{S}\wedge\overline{g})(i)=\overline{f}(i)
. On the other hand, for
0\leq j<i
and
j\in S
,
(f|_{S}\wedge\overline{g})(j)=f(j)\wedge g(j)=\overline{f}(j)
. We just proved that
\overline{f}
is a solution of
P|_{[i+1]}
.

Finally, note that for all
0\leq j\leq i
,
\overline{f}(j)\in\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{j})
. Therefore,
\overline{f}
is a least-block solution of

, a contradiction.
∎

We note in passing that this case is a generalization of A. Bulatov’s rectangular case from
[
6
]
and that the algorithm displayed here would work there, as well. In fact, Bulatov’s algorithm would work in our case, too, though it would have a more complicated proof.

We turn to another situation where we can prove tractability. We say that a semilattice is
flat
when it has the least element and all other elements are maximal in the semilattice order.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}=\langle A;w\rangle
be an SMB algebra wrt.
\sim
.
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
is tractable when the order of
\sim
-classes is that of a flat semilattice.

Proof.

We assume, as before, that
{\mathbf{A}}
is a regular SMB algebra and ensure that
P
is
(2,3)
-minimal so we may call
P=([n],D,{\mathcal{C}})
a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
whose domains are subuniverses of
{\mathbf{A}}
. Let
A=O\cup I_{1},\ldots,I_{m}
, where
O
is the least
\sim
-class and
I_{1},\ldots,I_{m}
are the maximal ones.

Because of
(2,3)
-minimality, not only the domains
A_{1}
are fixed by the instance, but also
P
contains all constraints of the form
(\{i,i^{\prime}\},R_{i,i^{\prime}})
, and
R_{i,i^{\prime}}
is the projection to
\{i,i^{\prime}\}
of any constraint relation
R
such that
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
\{i,i^{\prime}\}\subseteq S
. We think of
R_{i,i^{\prime}}
as a bipartite graph between disjoint partitions
A_{i}
and
A_{i^{\prime}}
, and the minimality conditions imply that there are no isolated vertices.

P
defines a preorder
\preceq
on the following set:

S(P):=\{(i,j):0\leq i<n\;\&\;1\leq j\leq m\;\&\;A_{i}\cap I_{j}\neq\emptyset\neq O\cap A_{i}\}.

We say
(i,j)\preceq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
if, whenever
f\in R_{i,i^{\prime}}
and
f(i)\in I_{j}
, then
f(i^{\prime})\in I_{j^{\prime}}
(here
(\{i,i^{\prime}\},R_{i,i^{\prime}})\in{\mathcal{C}}
). We define
(i,j)\cong(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
if
(i,j)\preceq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\preceq(i,j)
. We call
\cong
-classes
strands
.

It is not hard to see that
\preceq
is indeed a preorder: reflexivity is a trivial consequence of the definition of
\preceq
. For transitivity, assume that
(i,j)\preceq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\preceq(i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})
, and let
f\in R_{i,i^{\prime\prime}}
be such that
f(i)\in I_{j}
. By
(2,3)
-minimality, there exists
\overline{f}\in R_{i,i^{\prime},i^{\prime\prime}}
such that
\overline{f}|_{\{i,i^{\prime\prime}\}}=f
. Denote
g=\overline{f}|_{\{i,i^{\prime}\}}
and
h=\overline{f}|_{\{i^{\prime},i^{\prime\prime}\}}
. From
g(i)=\overline{f}(i)=f(i)\in I_{j}
and
(i,j)\preceq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
follows
h(i^{\prime})=\overline{f}(i^{\prime})=g(i^{\prime})\in I_{j^{\prime}}
, similarly, from
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\preceq(i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})
we obtain
f(i^{\prime\prime})=h(i^{\prime\prime})\in I_{j^{\prime\prime}}
.

The properties to remember about strands are (all of these follow from
(2,3)
-minimality of
P
): Firstly, for any strand and
i\leq n
, there is at most one
(i,j)
in this strand (the argument uses that the meet of any two elements of
A_{i}
which are in different
\sim
-classes must be in
O
). Secondly, for any pair
(i,j)\in S(P)
, it is in exactly one strand since
\cong
is an equivalence relation. Finally, whenever
f(i)\in I_{j}
for some solution
f\in A^{n}
of
P
(or of a restriction of
P
to some subset of variables), then for all
i^{\prime}\in[n]
such that
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
is in the same strand as
(i,j)
, then
f(i^{\prime})\in I_{j^{\prime}}
(in case of a restriction of
P
, we need to assume here that
i^{\prime}
is in the scope of this restriction, as well). So, we may refer to a strand as
\{(i,t(i)):i\in F\}
where
F\subseteq[n]
and
t:F\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

Now we describe a procedure
TESTASTRAND(P,\{(i,t(i)):i\in F\})
.

Step 1.

Compute the restriction
Q:=P|_{F}
.

Step 2.

Using the Mal’cev algorithm decide if
Q
has an
O
-valued solution.

Step 2.1.

If ’YES’ output ’Exists an
O
-valued solution’ and stop.

Step 2.2.

If ’NO’, test if
Q
has a solution
f
which for all
i\in F
has
f(i)\in t(i)
. This is again a Mal’cev algorithm. If ’YES’, output ’Exists a strand-valued solution’ and stop, if ’NO’, output ’Neither’ and stop.

We will repeatedly use this procedure. The main algorithm
SOLVE(P)
now looks like this:

Step 1.

Set up the working instance
Q:=P
.

Step 2.

Applying consistency algorithm reduce
Q
to a
(2,3)
-minimal instance. If any
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}(Q)
is such that
R=\emptyset
, output ’
P
has no solution’ and stop.

Step 3.

Compute
S(Q)
and
J=\{i\in n:(\exists j)(i,j)\in S(Q)\}
. Compute
\preceq
and
\cong
on
S(Q)
.

Step 4.

For each strand
\{(i,t(i)):i\in F\}
do

Step 4.1.

If
TESTASTRAND(Q,\{(i,t(i)):i\in F\})
outputs ’Exists an
O
-valued solution’, move on to next strand.

Step 4.2.

If
TESTASTRAND(Q,\{(i,t(i)):i\in F\})
outputs ’Exists a strand-valued solution’, tighten
Q
by replacing each
A_{i}
,
i\in F
, with
I_{t(i)}
and go to Step 2.

Step 4.3.

If
TESTASTRAND(Q,\{(i,t(i)):i\in F\})
outputs ’Neither’, tighten
Q
by replacing each
A_{i}
,
i\in F
, with
A_{i}\setminus I_{t(i)}
and go to Step 2.

Step 5.

Tighten
Q
by replacing each
A_{i}
,
i\in J
, with
A_{i}\cap O
.

Step 6.

Q
is now an instance over Mal’cev algebras, as each
A_{i}
is now within a single
\sim
-class, so solve it. Output that
P
has a solution if
Q
does and that it has no solution if
Q
does not and stop.

We see that the instance always tightens to a smaller one whenever either Step 4.2 or Step 4.3 is applied. Therefore, they can be applied at most
n|A|
many times. Moreover, the number of strands is bounded from above by
nm\leq n|A|
, so at most
n^{2}|A|^{2}
many applications of the TestAStrand procedure can occur during running time of the algorithm. Each application requires at most two applications of the Mal’cev algorithm. Moreover, we need to apply the consistency algorithm in Step 2 at most
n|A|+1
times. Finally, there is one more application of the Mal’cev algorithm in Step 6. So the time-cost of the algorithm is dominated by the number of steps needed to apply
(2,3)
-minimality algorithm
n|A|+1
times, plus the number of steps needed to apply the Mal’cev algorithm
2n^{2}|A|^{2}+1
times. As both of these elementary algorithms run in polynomial time, so does this one.

To study the correctness of our algorithm, we note first that when it outputs that there exists a solution to
P
, then the algorithm really found a solution to a tightening of
P
to certain specified path through
\sim
-classes (since when the Mal’cev algorithm produces ’YES’ it actually finds a generating set for the space of all such solutions). Therefore, this is a solution to
P
, as well.

If the algorithm rejected the instance, on the other hand, it may have done so in the Step 2., or in Step 6. If it rejected
P
in Step 2., it either did it in the initial
2
-consistency check, which is fine as the instance which fails the initial consistency check indeed has no solution, or in application of Step 2. which occurred after a tightening via either Step 4.2. or 4.3.

Both of these tightenings lead to equivalent instances. To see this, fix a strand
\{(i,t(i)):i\in F\}
. If the instance
P|_{F}
has a solution which at some (equivalently, all)
i\in F
takes values in
I_{t(i)}
and another one which at some (equivalently, all)
i\in F
takes values outside the

, then the meet of these two solutions is a solution of
P|_{F}
which is
O
-valued at each variable

(recall that by definition of

, for all
i\in F
,
A_{i}\cap O\neq\emptyset
). Therefore, if there are no
O
-valued solutions to
P|_{F}
, then either
P|_{F}
has no strand-valued solutions, or it has no solutions which are outside the strand at all
i\in F
. Again, by the definition of a strand, these are the only two options. Note that the relation
\cong
is invariant under restriction since
P
is
(2,3)
-minimal, so
\{(i,t(i)):i\in F\}
is a strand for
P|_{F}
, as well as for

. Also note, by Corollary

, that both

and

are subuniverses of
{\mathbf{A}}
, so the tightened instances are still within
CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
.

Finally, the algorithm may have rejected the instance at Step 6. To see that this is a correct rejection, we need to prove the following

Lemma 3.10
.

Assume that
f
is a solution to a
(2,3)
-minimal multisorted instance
P=([n],D,{\mathcal{C}})
of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
. If for all strands
\{(i,t(i)):i\in F\}
such that
f(i)\in I_{t(i)}
there exists an
O
-valued solution of
P|_{F}
, then
P
has a solution
\overline{f}\in A^{n}
such that
\overline{f}\in O
whenever
A_{i}\cap O\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

We first note that, since
f
is a function, for any two different strands
\{(i,t_{1}(i)):i\in F_{1}\}
and
\{(i,t_{2}(i)):i\in F_{2}\}
visited by
f
(i. e. such that
f(i)\in I_{t_{1}(i)}
when
i\in F_{1}
and
f(i)\in I_{t_{2}(i)}
when
i\in F_{2}
), we must have that
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are disjoint. Therefore, we know that the set
J\subseteq n
defined by
J=\{i\in n:f(i)\notin O\;\&\;A_{i}\cap O\neq\emptyset\}
is partitioned into
F_{1}\cup\ldots\cup F_{l}
, which are the sets of variables of all strands visited by
f
. We may define now uniformly
t:J\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that for
i\in F_{j}
,
t(i):=t_{j}(i)
. As the strands are partially ordered by
\preceq
, we assume without loss of generality that when
i\in F_{j}
,
i^{\prime}\in F_{j^{\prime}}
and
1\leq j^{\prime}<j\leq l
, then
\neg(i,t_{j}(i))\preceq(i^{\prime},t_{j^{\prime}}(i^{\prime}))
. Our assumptions give us
O
-valued solutions
h_{j}
to
P|_{F_{j}}
, for all
1\leq j\leq l
. We prove inductively on
s
that there exists a solution
g_{s}
of
Q_{s}=P|_{(n\setminus J)\cup F_{1}\cup\ldots\cup F_{s}}
which is equal to
f
on
n\setminus J
and to
f\wedge h_{j}
(which is
O
-valued) on each
F_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq s
. Clearly for
s=l
this will be the desired
\overline{f}
, as
Q_{l}=P
.

The base case is for
s=1
, where we claim that
g_{1}(i)=f(i)
for
i\in n\setminus J
and
g_{1}(i)=f(i)\wedge h_{1}(i)
is a solution to
Q_{1}
. Indeed, let
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}(Q_{1})
. If
S
is disjoint from
F_{1}
, then
g_{1}|_{S}=f|_{S}
, so
g_{1}|_{S}\in R
. If, on the other hand,
S
is not disjoint from
F_{1}
, then from the fact that
h_{1}
is a solution to
P|_{F_{1}}
, we know that there exists some
h\in R
such that
h|_{S\cap F_{1}}=h_{1}|_{S\cap F_{1}}
. Therefore, where
f^{\prime}=f|_{S}
,
f^{\prime}\wedge h\in R
. Now for
i\in S\setminus F_{1}
,
f^{\prime}(i)\in\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
, so
f^{\prime}(i)\wedge h(i)=f^{\prime}(i)=g_{1}(i)
. On the other hand, where
i\in S\cap F_{1}
,
f^{\prime}(i)\wedge h(i)=f(i)\wedge h_{1}(i)=g_{1}(i)
. Therefore,
g_{1}
is a solution to
Q_{1}
.

Assume that
g_{s}
is a solution to
Q_{s}
, where
g_{s}
is
defined as above. Let
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}(Q_{s+1})
. If
S\cap F_{s+1}=\emptyset
, there is nothing to prove, and if
S\cap(F_{1}\cup\ldots\cup F_{s})=\emptyset
, this is the same as the base case
s=1
. So we assume that
S
intersects both
F_{1}\cup\ldots\cup F_{s}
and
F_{s+1}
.

We need to construct some auxiliary elements of
R
. Firstly, we are guaranteed to have
f^{\prime},g^{\prime},h^{\prime}\in R
such that
f^{\prime}=f|_{S}
,
g^{\prime}|_{S\setminus F_{s+1}}=g_{s}|_{S\setminus F_{s+1}}
, while
h^{\prime}|_{S\cap F_{s+1}}=h_{s+1}|_{S\cap F_{s+1}}
, where the existence of
g^{\prime}
and
h^{\prime}
follows from the definition of the restriction of an instance to a set of coordinates. Secondly, we define
S_{0}=S\setminus(F_{1}\cup\ldots\cup F_{s+1})
and
S_{j}=S\cap F_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq s+1
. Our assumptions ensure that
S_{s+1}\neq\emptyset
and for at least one
j
,
1\leq j\leq s
,
S_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Note also that
S_{0}
consists precisely of those variables
i\in S
where
f(i)\in\mathrm{min}(A_{i})
, so
f(i)
is a left absorbing element for
\wedge
on
A_{i}
(here we use the fact that
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}(Q_{s+1})
, so
S
has no variables in
F_{j}
,
j>s+1
).

Now select some
i_{s+1}\in S_{s+1}
and for any
j
such that
1\leq j\leq s
and
S_{j}\neq\emptyset
a variable
i_{j}\in S_{j}
. Let
(\{i_{j},i_{s+1}\},R_{j})\in{\mathcal{C}}(Q_{s+1})
. As we know that
\neg(i_{s+1},t(i_{s+1}))\preceq(i_{j},t(i_{j}))
and that
(f(i_{j}),f(i))\in R_{j}\cap(I_{t(i_{j})}\times I_{t(i_{s+1})})
, then there must exist also some
q_{j}\in R_{j}
such that
q_{j}(i_{j})\notin I_{t(i_{j})}
, while
q_{j}(i_{s+1})\in I_{t(i_{s+1})}
. Let
q_{j}^{\prime}\in R
be such that
q_{j}^{\prime}|_{\{i_{j},i_{s+1}\}}=q_{j}
and
p_{j}^{\prime}=f^{\prime}\wedge q_{j}^{\prime}
. We know that
q_{j}^{\prime}|_{S_{j}}(i)\notin I_{t(i)}
for all
i\in S_{j}
, as it is so at
i_{j}
and
\{(i,t(i)):i\in S_{j}\}
is the restriction of a strand. Therefore, for all
i\in S_{j}
,
p_{j}^{\prime}(i)\in O
. Moreover, for all
i\in S_{0}\cup S_{s+1}
,
p_{j}^{\prime}(i)=f(i)
. Now define
p^{\prime}=\bigwedge\limits_{S_{j}\neq\emptyset}p_{j}^{\prime}
to be the left-associated meet. We know that
p^{\prime}\in R
, that
p^{\prime}|_{S_{0}\cup S_{s+1}}=f|_{S_{0}\cup S_{s+1}}
and that
p^{\prime}(i)\in O
for all
i\in S_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq s
.

Now we define three tuples
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
in
R
in the following way:

f_{1}=f^{\prime}\wedge(g^{\prime}\wedge h^{\prime})\text{, }f_{2}=p^{\prime}\wedge f_{1}\text{ and }f_{3}=p^{\prime}\wedge h^{\prime}.

We claim that
\overline{f}|_{S}=d(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})
, thus proving
\overline{f}|_{S}\in R
. We break down the proof into three cases:

If
i\in S_{0}
, then
\overline{f}(i)=f(i)=f^{\prime}(i)=p^{\prime}(i)\in\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
, so it is a left absorbing for the meet. Therefore,
f_{1}(i)=f_{2}(i)=f_{3}(i)=\overline{f}(i)
, and the claim follows by the idempotence of
d
.

If
i\in S_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq s
, then
f_{2}(i)=p^{\prime}(i)=f_{3}(i)\in O
, while

\begin{gathered}f_{1}(i)=f(i)\wedge(g^{\prime}(i)\wedge h^{\prime}(i))=f(i)\wedge g^{\prime}(i)=\\
f(i)\wedge(f(i)\wedge h_{j}(i))=f(i)\wedge h_{j}(i)=\overline{f}(i)\in O\end{gathered}

(the second equality follows from
g^{\prime}(i)\in O
, while the fourth equality follows from Definition
3.3
(4)). Therefore,

d(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})(i)=d(f_{1}(i),p^{\prime}(i),p^{\prime}(i))=f_{1}(i)=\overline{f}(i),

since
d
is a Mal’cev operation on
O
.

Finally, if
i\in S_{s+1}
, then
g^{\prime}(i)\wedge h_{s+1}(i)\in O
and

f_{1}(i)=f(i)\wedge(g^{\prime}(i)\wedge h_{s+1}(i)),

while
p^{\prime}(i)=f(i)
and therefore

\begin{gathered}f_{2}(i)=f(i)\wedge f_{1}(i)=f(i)\wedge(f(i)\wedge(g^{\prime}(i)\wedge h_{s+1}(i)))=\\
f(i)\wedge(g^{\prime}(i)\wedge h_{s+1}(i))=f_{1}(i).\end{gathered}

On the other hand,

f_{3}(i)=p^{\prime}(i)\wedge h_{s+1}(i)=f(i)\wedge h_{s+1}(i)=\overline{f}(i)\in O.

Now

d(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})(i)=f_{3}(i)=\overline{f}(i),

since
d
is a Mal’cev operation on
O
. This finishes the inductive proof of the lemma.
∎

We have proved that, if there exists a solution of an instance and if for each strand there exists an
O
-valued solution to the restriction of the instance to the strand variables, then there exists an
O
-valued solution of the whole instance. If the instance
Q
reached Step 6, there is an
O
-valued solution to the restriction of
Q
to the strand variables, for each strand, hence testing
Q
just for
O
-valued solutions determines whether
Q
has a solution.
∎

4.
M-irreducibility and tree-ordered SMB algebras

4.1.
Consistent maps

Definition 4.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template and
(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
. A set
p=\{\,p_{i}\mid i\in V\,\}
of maps is
consistent
with
P
if
for all
i\in V
the map
p_{i}
is a unary polynomial of
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
, and
for every constraint
(S,R)
and tuple
r\in R
the tuple

p|_{S}(r)=\langle p_{i}(r_{i}):i\in S\rangle
is also in
R
.
We say that
p
is
permutational
, if each
p_{i}
is a permutation,
and it is
retractive
, if
p_{i}(x)
is a retraction for all
i\in V
.

Clearly, every consistent set
p=\{\,p_{i}:i\in V\,\}
of maps can be iterated to obtain an retractive one
p^{\prime}=\{\,p^{k}_{i}:i\in V\,\}
where
k=(\max_{i\in V}|A_{i}|)!
, for example.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
P
be an instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
and
p=\{\,p_{i}\mid i\in V\,\}
be a retractive consistent set of maps. The
retraction
of
P
via
p
is the new instance
p(P)
of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
defined as

p(P)=\{\,V,\{p_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})\mid i\in V\,\},\{\ (S,p|_{S}(R)\mid(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}\,\}.

It easily follows from the definitions that for each constraint
(S,R)
, the relation

p|_{S}(R)=\{\,p|_{S}(r)\mid r\in R\,\}=R\cap\prod_{i\in S}p_{i}(A_{i})

is a subuniverse of
\prod_{i\in S}p_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
. Also, if
P
is
(k,l)
-minimal, then so is its retraction
p(P)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
P
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
and
p
be a consistent set of retractions. Then
P
has a solution if and only if
p(P)
does.

Proof.

Since
p_{i}(A_{i})\subseteq A_{i}
and for any constraint
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
p|_{S}(R)\subseteq R
, any solution of
p(P)
is a solution of
P
. Conversely, if
f
is a solution of
P
, then the function
p\circ f=\langle p_{i}(f(i)):i\in V\rangle
is a solution of
p(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

Definition 4.4
.

Let
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
and
t
be a binary term
such that
t(x,t(x,y))=t(x,y)
for all algebras in
{\mathcal{T}}
. For an element
a\in A_{i}
we put
t_{a}(x)=t(a,x)
, which is an idempotent polynomial of
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
.
The
decomposition
of
P
via
t
is the new instance
t(P)
of

CSP({\mathcal{T}})
defined as
t(P)=(V^{\prime},D^{\prime},{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime})
. The variables are

V^{\prime}=\{\,(i,a)\mid i\in V,\ a\in A_{i}\,\}.

The domains are

\begin{gathered}D^{\prime}=\{\,A_{i,a}\mid i\in V,\ a\in A_{i}\,\},\text{ where}\\
A_{i,a}=t_{a}(A_{i})=\{\,t(a,x)\mid x\in A_{i}\,\}.\end{gathered}

Finally, the constraints are

\begin{gathered}{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}=\{\,(S_{r},R_{r})\mid(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}},\ r\in R\,\}\,\cup\,\{\,(S_{i},T_{i})\mid i\in V\,\},\text{ where}\\
S_{r}=\{\,(i,r(i))\mid i\in S\,\}\text{ and }R_{r}=\{\,t(r,x)\mid x\in R\,\},\text{ while}\\
S_{i}=\{\,(i,a)\mid a\in A_{i}\,\}\text{ and }\\
T_{i}={\rm Sg}^{\prod_{a\in A_{i}}t_{a}({\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{A}}}}_{i})}(\{\,\langle t(a,b):a\in A_{i}\rangle\mid b\in A_{i}\,\}).\end{gathered}

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
P
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
and
t
be a binary term
such that
{\mathcal{T}}\models t(x,t(x,y))=t(x,y)
.
If
P
has a solution, then so does
t(P)
.

Proof.

Let
f
be a solution of the instance
P
. We define a solution
g
of
t(P)
as

g((i,a))=t(a,f(i))

for all
(i,a)\in V^{\prime}
. Clearly,
g((i,a))\in A_{i,a}
. Take a constraint of the form
(S_{r},R_{r})
. By definition,

g|_{S_{r}}=\langle t(r(i),f(i)):i\in S\rangle=t(r,f|_{S}).

However,
f
is a solution, so both
r
and
f|_{S}
are in
R
and therefore
t(r,f|_{I})\in R_{r}=\{\,t(r,x)\mid x\in R\,\}
, as well.

Now take a constraint
(S_{i},T_{i})
of the second kind. Here

g|_{S_{i}}=\langle t(a,f(i)):a\in A_{i}\rangle,

that is,
g|_{S_{i}}
is one of the generating elements of the algebra
{\mathbf{T}}_{i}
.
∎

In the next lemma we will try to understand the structure of the relations
{\mathbf{T}}_{i}
in
t(P)
, so we focus on a single algebra
{\mathbf{B}}={\mathbf{A}}_{i}
for the moment.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
{\mathbf{B}}
be an algebra, and
t
be a binary term such that
{\mathbf{B}}\models t(x,t(x,y))=t(x,y)
. For
b\in B
let
{\mathbf{B}}_{b}=t_{b}({\mathbf{B}})
, and put

{\mathbf{B}}^{*}=\prod_{b\in B}{\mathbf{B}}_{b}
. Let

{\mathbf{T}}={\rm Sg}^{{\mathbf{B}}^{*}}\{\,\langle t(b,c):b\in B\rangle\mid c\in B\,\}

and take a tuple
r\in T
. Then the following hold.

(1)

r
viewed as the map
b\mapsto r(b)
is a unary polynomial of
{\mathbf{B}}
.

(2)

Let
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
and
\vartheta
be a congruence of
{\mathbf{B}}
.
If
t(b_{1},x)\equiv_{\vartheta}t(b_{2},x)
for all
x\in B
, then
r(b_{1})\equiv_{\vartheta}r(b_{2})
.

Proof.

Each generator tuple
\langle t(b,c):b\in B\rangle
of
{\mathbf{T}}
is actually a map from
B
to
B
and it is a unary polynomial
{\mathbf{B}}
in the variable
b
where
c
is a constant. When we generate the subalgebra by these vectors, then we take a basic operation
f
of
{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
, some unary polynomials
r_{1}(b),\dots,r_{k}(b)
already generated and generate the tuple
\langle r(b):b\in B\rangle
in
{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
where

r(b)=t(b,f(r_{1}(b),\dots,r_{k}(b))),

so
r(b)
is again a unary polynomial of
{\mathbf{B}}
in the variable
b
.

To prove the second claim it is enough to see that
s(b_{1})\equiv_{\vartheta}s(b_{2})
for each generator tuple
s
and verify that this property is preserved. For
s=\langle t(b,c):b\in B\rangle
a generator of
{\mathbf{T}}
, the claim becomes trivial (what is assumed is the same as the desired conclusion). So assume that the unary polynomials
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
are already generated,

\begin{gathered}r=f^{{\mbox{\scriptsize{\bf{B}}}}^{*}}(r_{1},\dots,r_{k})=\langle t(b,f(r_{1}(b),\dots,r_{k}(b))):b\in B\rangle\text{ and}\\
r_{1}(b_{1})\equiv_{\vartheta}r_{1}(b_{2}),\dots,r_{k}(b_{1})\equiv_{\vartheta}r_{k}(b_{1}).\end{gathered}

Thus
c_{1}=f(p_{1}(b_{1}),\dots,f_{k}(b_{1}))\equiv_{\vartheta}f(p_{1}(b_{2}),\dots,f_{k}(b_{2}))=c_{2}
, and using again our assumption that
t(b_{1},x)\equiv_{\vartheta}t(b_{2},x)
, we get that

r(b_{1})=t(b_{1},c_{1})\equiv_{\vartheta}t(b_{1},c_{2})\equiv_{\vartheta}t(b_{2},c_{2})=r(b_{2})

for the newly generated polynomial
r
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
P
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
and
t
be a binary term
such that
{\mathcal{T}}\models t(x,t(x,y))=t(x,y)
.
If
t(P)
has a solution, then there exists a consistent set
\{\,p_{i}:i\in V\,\}
of unary polynomials for the instance
P

such that each polynomial
p_{i}
of
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
satisfies the conclusion of Lemma
4.6
.

Proof.

Let
g
be a solution of
t(P)
. We define a consistent set
p=\{\,p_{i}\mid i\in V\,\}
of unary maps for
P
as

p_{i}(a)=g((i,a))

for
i\in V
and
a\in A_{i}
.
By Lemma
4.6
, each map
p_{i}:A_{i}\to A_{i}
is a unary polynomial of
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
which also satisfies Lemma
4.6
(2). To see that these polynomial maps are consistent with
P
, take a constraint
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}
and a tuple
r\in R
. Since
g
was a solution to
t(P)
it respects
the constraint
R_{r}
, that is the tuple
\langle g((i,r_{i})):i\in S\rangle
is in
R_{r}\subseteq R
. But this tuple is exactly
p|_{S}
, which shows that
p
is consistent.
∎

Definition 4.8
.

We say that an idempotent algebra
{\mathbf{B}}
can be
eliminated
, if whenever

{\mathcal{T}}
is a template such that
{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{T}}
, and
{\mathcal{T}}\setminus\{{\mathbf{B}}\}
is also a template, and
CSP({\mathcal{T}}\setminus\{{\mathbf{B}}\})
is tractable, then
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
is also tractable.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
{\mathbf{B}}
be an algebra and
t
be a binary term
of
{\mathbf{B}}
such that for each
b\in B
the map
t_{b}(x)=t(b,x)
is a retraction which is not surjective.
Let
C
be the set of elements
c\in B
such that
x\mapsto t(x,c)
is a permutation. If
C
generates a proper subuniverse of
{\mathbf{B}}
, then

{\mathbf{B}}
can be eliminated.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template containing
{\mathbf{B}}
, assume that
{\mathcal{T}}\setminus\{{\mathbf{B}}\}
is also a template, that
CSP({\mathcal{T}}\setminus\{{\mathbf{B}}\})
is tractable, and let
P
be an instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
containing at least one copy of
{\mathbf{B}}
. Replace all occurences of
{\mathbf{B}}
in
P
with the subalgebra generated by the set
C
and restrict the constraints accordingly. Clearly, this new instance is an instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}\setminus\{{\mathbf{B}}\})
so it can be solved in polynomial time. If it has a solution, then we are done, so we can assume that it does not.

Since the maps
t_{b}
are not surjective,
|t_{b}({\mathbf{B}})|<|{\mathbf{B}}|
and therefore the decomposition
t(P)
is an instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}\setminus\{{\mathbf{B}}\})
. Thus it can be solved in polynomial time. If
t(P)
has no solution, then
P
has no solution, either, by Lemma
4.5
. On the other hand, if
t(P)
has a solution, then by Lemma
4.7
we have a consistent set
p=\{\,p_{i}:i\in V\,\}
of unary polynomials for
P
. Let us assume for a moment that
p
is not permutational. Now
p
can be iterated to obtain a retractive non-permutational consistent set
p^{\prime}
of unary polynomials for
P
. By Lemma
4.3
we know that
P
has a solution if and only if
p^{\prime}(P)
does. Also, since
p^{\prime}
is non-permutational, at least one of the domains of
p^{\prime}(P)
is smaller than that of
P
. So by iterating this procedure we will either find out that
P
has no solution, or get to a point when the algebra
{\mathbf{B}}
no longer occurs in the instance
P
.

Now we go back to the problem of making sure that
p
becomes non-permutational. From the first paragraph of the proof, we know that if
P
has a solution
f
, then for at least one
i\in V
,
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\cong{\mathbf{B}}
and
f(i)\not\in C
. Let us iterate through all variables
i\in V
such that
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\cong{\mathbf{B}}
(for simplicity, we assume
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}={\mathbf{B}}
) and all elements
d\in B\setminus C
. For each choice of
i
and
d
we create a new instance from
t(P)
by adding the new unary constraint stating that the solution
g|_{S_{i}}=\langle t(b,d):b\in B_{i}\rangle
. This ensures that
p_{i}(b)=t(b,d)
, that is, it is not permutational. If for any of these choices we find a solution to
t(P)
, then we can reduce the instance
P
as shown in the paragraph above. Otherwise we conculde that the instance has no solution.
∎

4.2.
Applications

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite idempotent algebra,
t(x,y)
a term of
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\sim}\in{{\rm Con\>}{\mathbf{A}}}
such that
(A/{\sim};t)
is a semilattice with more than one maximal element. Then
{\mathbf{A}}
can be eliminated.

Proof.

We can iterate
t
to obtain
t(x,t(x,y))=t(x,y)
on
{\mathbf{A}}
, while
(A/{\sim};t)
is the same semilattice. Since
{\mathbf{A}}/{\sim}
has more than one maximal element, for all
a\in A
the maps
x\mapsto t(a,x)
and
x\mapsto t(x,a)
are not permutations. Thus we can apply Lemma
4.9
with
C=\emptyset
.
∎

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite idempotent algebra,
{\sim}\in{{\bf{\rm Con\>}}{\mathbf{A}}}\setminus\{1_{A}\}
and
t
be a binary term such that
(A/{\sim};t)
is a semilattice with the largest element
B
(
B
is the neutral element in
(A/{\sim};t)
). If
B
contains more than one element and satisfies
t(x,y)=x
, then
{\mathbf{A}}
can be eliminated.

Proof.

We can assume that
t(x,t(x,y))=t(x,y)
on
{\mathbf{A}}
, since we can iterate
t
in the second variable without destroying the required properties stated in the lemma. Suppose that the
{\sim}
-block
B
has more than one element.
Then the maps
t_{a}(x)=t(a,x)
are not permutations for any
a\in A
. Moreover, for any
c\in A
for which

is a permutation we must have
c\in B
. However,
B
is a proper subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}
, thus we can apply Lemma
4.9
to finish the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite SMB algebra over
\sim
.
{\mathbf{A}}
can be eliminated, unless
{\mathbf{A}}/{\sim}
has the largest block
B
with respect to the semilattice order, that largest block satisfies
|B|=1
.

Moreover, in the case when there is the largest
\sim
-block
B
,
B=\{b\}
, and
p(x)=b\wedge x
is not a permutation, then
{\mathbf{A}}
can be eliminated.

Proof.

The first paragraph is a special case of Corollaries
4.10
and
4.11
.

As for the final sentence, the iteration which produces
\wedge^{\prime}
from
\wedge
(used in
[
20
]
in the proof of Proposition
3.6
) will give us a term
t(x,y)=x\wedge^{\prime}y
such that
t(a,x)
is a retraction which is not surjective for any
a\in A
. This is obvious for
a\notin B
, while if
a=b
, then

b\wedge^{\prime}x=b\wedge(b\wedge\dots(b\wedge x)\dots)=p^{|A|!}(x),

and since
p(x)
is not surjective, neither is
b\wedge^{\prime}x
. The only

such that

is a permutation is

, so the conditions of Lemma
4.9
are fulfilled (unless
|A|=1
) and
{\mathbf{A}}
can be eliminated.
∎

Corollary 4.13
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite SMB algebra over
\sim
. Assume that
{\mathbf{A}}/{\sim}
is a tree-ordered meet semilattice (i.e. for any
\sim
-class
B
, the set of all
\sim
-classes below
B
or equal to it is linearly ordered). Then
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
is tractable.

Proof.

According to Proposition
3.6
, we replace
{\mathbf{A}}
with its term reduct to assume that
{\mathbf{A}}
is a regular SMB algebra with tree-ordered
\sim
-classes. Let
{\mathcal{T}}:={\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}})
. Note that any
{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{T}}
in which the order of
{\sim}
-classes is not linear can not be a homomorphic image, a subalgebra, or a retract of any
{\mathbf{C}}\in{\mathcal{T}}
in which the order of
{\sim}
-classes is linear. Hence for any template
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
and any
{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
which has maximal size among algebras in
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
which do not have a linear order of
\sim
-classes, we have that
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}\setminus\{{\mathbf{B}}\}
is also a template. Finally, note that in any
{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathcal{T}}
in which the order of
{\sim}
-classes is not linear, the semilattice
{\mathbf{B}}/{\sim}
has more than one maximal element, and thus can be eliminated by Corollary
4.10
.

So we can make a sequence of templates
{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{T}}_{0},{\mathcal{T}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
so that for all
i\leq k
,
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}={\mathcal{T}}_{i-1}\setminus\{{\mathbf{B}}_{i}\}
,
{\mathbf{B}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{i-1}
has maximal size among algebras in
{\mathcal{T}}
with non-linear order of
{\sim}
-classes, and
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
is a template of regular SMB algebras with linear order of
\sim
-classes. By a multisorted variant of Theorem
3.7
,
CSP({\mathcal{T}}_{k})
is tractable, and thus, by Definition
4.8
and an inductive argument,
CSP({\mathcal{T}}_{0})=CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
is also tractable.
∎

Definition 4.14
.

Let
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
, where
{\mathcal{T}}
consists of finite SMB algebras. We say that
P
is strongly M-irreducible if for all
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\in D
, either
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
is a Mal’cev algebra (i.e.
{\sim}
is the full relation on
A_{i}
), or
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}/{\sim}
has the largest element
B
(with respect to the semilattice order) and

.

We say that

is weakly M-irreducible if for any
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\in D
which is maximal-sized among non-Mal’cev sorts in

,
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
is unital.

The following result is Corollary 1 of A.Bulatov’s paper
[
9
]
, but the argument given there has a gap:

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite SMB algebra. For any instance
P
of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
we can apply a polynomial time algorithm which either solves
CSP(P)
or reduces it to an equivalent strongly M-irreducible multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
.

One is tempted to just apply Corollary
4.12
and say we are done. But this doesn’t quite work. If an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{T}}
is such that
{\mathcal{T}}\setminus\{{\mathbf{A}}_{i}\}
is not a template (e.g. when
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
is a subuniverse, or a factor, or a retract of some larger algebra in
{\mathcal{T}}
), then we cannot just remove
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
. Indeed, it is not hard to imagine such an “algorithm” would end up in an infinite loop, so it might not even be an algorithm. Namely, one would go from an instance
P
to its decomposition
t(P)
, but then the proof of Lemma
4.9
would require one to recursively solve
t(P)
. In the course of this recursion we may run into a strictly bigger instance than the original instance
P
, forcing our algorithm into an infinite loop.

We plug this gap in Bulatov’s paper by proving the above theorem, but in the next Section, after we review some parts of D. Zhuk’s proof of the Dichotomy Conjecture from
[
32
]
.

Another, better proof of tractability of CSP over SMB algebras is just a small variation of the original proof by A. Bulatov. Namely, the original ideas and arguments by Bulatov were sufficient to the task, but the definitions and statements of lemmas need to be amended. We are going to use just the following weaker result:

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite SMB algebra. For any instance
P
of
CSP({\mathbf{A}})
we can apply a polynomial time algorithm which either solves
CSP(P)
or reduces it to an equivalent weakly M-irreducible multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{A}}))
.

Proof.

This is what applying Corollary
4.12
gives us.
∎

So, why do we give the proof in Section 5? As we said, Section 6 demonstrates that Bulatov’s original proof was essentially correct, needing only a minor intervention, while Section 5 uses as a black box a powerful result by Zhuk whose only known proof is inextricable from the whole Dichotomy proof. However, generalizing Bulatov’s proof from SMB algebras to Taylor algebras is the route Bulatov took to the Dichotomy, and it proved a very difficult and complicated task. We hope our ideas from Section 5 can evolve into a simpler proof, merging Zhuk’s and Bulatov’s ideas into an argument simpler than either.

5.
Hypergraph connectivity and Z-irreducibility

Lest we forget, and to avoid complications, we will assume henceforth that for any multisorted CSP instance we will consider, all domains of variables
A_{i}
of the instance are pairwise disjoint.

We remind the reader of some basic facts about hypergraphs.

Definition 5.1
.

A
hypergraph
is an ordered pair
(V,E)
, where
V
is the vertex set and
E\subseteq(P(V)\setminus\{\emptyset\})
is the set of hyperedges.

The usual undirected graph is, therefore, a hypergraph where all hyperedges have two elements. The notions like paths and connectivity can also be generalized to hypergraphs.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a hypergraph. A
path
in
\Gamma
is a finite sequence of the form

p=a_{0},S_{1},a_{1},S_{2},\dots,a_{n-1},S_{n},a_{n},

where each
a_{i}\in V
,
S_{j}\in E
and for all
0<i\leq n
,
a_{i-1},a_{i}\in S_{i}
. If
p
is the path given above, we say that
p
connects the vertices
a_{0}
and
a_{n}
, and we say that vertices
a
and
b
are connected if there exists a path that connects them. A hypergraph
\Gamma=(V,E)
is connected if any pair of vertices in
V
is connected.

A
hypergraph homomorphism
is a map from the vertex set of one hypergraph to the vertex set of another such that each edge maps onto an edge.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template and let
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
. We define two hypergraphs associated with
P
, the
scope graph
of
P
, written
\Gamma_{V}
, and the
microstructure graph
of
P
, written
\Gamma_{P}
, by

\begin{gathered}\Gamma_{V}=(V,\{S:(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}\})\text{ and }\\
\Gamma_{P}=\left(\bigcup\limits_{i\in V}A_{i},E_{P}\right).\end{gathered}

Recall that the domains
A_{i}
are pairwise disjoint.
E_{P}
is defined as the union

E_{P}=\bigcup\limits_{(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}}\{\{a_{1},\dots,a_{|S|}\}:(a_{1},\dots,a_{|S|})\in R\}.

It is clear that the instance
P
has a solution iff there exists a hypergraph homomorphism
f
from
\Gamma_{V}
to
\Gamma_{P}
such that for each
i\in V
,
f(i)\in A_{i}
. The hypergraph variant is just a rephrasing, but it allows us to speak of an instance in terms of hypergraph connectivity. For example, we notice the following easy fact.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template and let
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
. If the microstructure graph
\Gamma_{P}
of
P
is connected, then the scope graph
\Gamma_{V}
of
P
is connected.

Proof.

Let
i,j\in V
. Select arbitrary
a\in A_{i}
and
b\in A_{j}
. Select a path

a=a_{0},E_{1},a_{1},\dots,E_{k},a_{k}=b

in
\Gamma_{P}
connecting
a
with
b
. For each hyperedge
E_{i}
there exists a tuple
\overline{c}_{i}
and a constraint
(S_{i},R_{i})\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\overline{c}_{i}\in R_{i}
and
E_{i}=\{\overline{c}_{i}(1),\dots,\overline{c}_{i}(|S_{i}|)\}
. If
a_{j}\in A_{i_{j}}
, for
j=0,1,\dots,k
, then

i=i_{0},S_{1},i_{1},\dots,S_{k},i_{k}=b

is a path in
\Gamma_{V}
connecting
i
with
j
.
∎

Next we define the notion of cycle consistency.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template and let
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
. We say that
P
is

if for every
i\in V
, every

and every closed path

p=i_{0},S_{1},i_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{k},i_{k}

in
\Gamma_{V}
such that
i_{0}=i_{k}=i
, there exists a closed path

p^{\prime}=a_{0},E_{1},a_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{k},a_{k}

in
\Gamma_{P}
such that
a_{0}=a_{k}=a
and for all
0<j\leq k
,
(S_{j},R_{j})\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
E_{j}
is the set of all coordinates of some tuple in

.

Cycle consistency is a consequence of
(2,3)
-minimality, but may be a weaker property.

In order to express the Z-irreducibility property, we need another notion of an induced smaller instance, which generalizes the restriction of the instance which we introduced in the proof of Theorem
3.7
.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template, let
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
be a multisorted instance of

, and let

if

, while
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{C}}_{1}|_{V^{\prime}}=\{(S^{\prime},R^{\prime}):(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1},

S^{\prime}=S\cap V^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime}=R|_{S^{\prime}}\}
.

Now we are ready to define Z-irreducible instances.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template and let

be a multisorted instance of

. We say that

is

if, for every

and

such that the subinstance

of

.

Next we need the notion of a link partition, which we will also use in the next section.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template and
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
a (1,1)-minimal multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
. We say that
P
has a link partition if for each
i\in V
there exists an equivalence relation
\varepsilon_{i}
on
A_{i}
such that

(1)

There exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 2
, such that for all
i\in V
,
|A_{i}/\varepsilon_{i}|=k

(2)

There exists an ordering of the
\varepsilon_{i}
-classes
A_{i}=A_{i,1}\dot{\cup}\dots\dot{\cup}A_{i,k}
so that for all
(S,R)\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
R=R_{1}\dot{\cup}\dots\dot{\cup}R_{k}
and for all
j\leq k
,
R_{j}\subseteq\prod\limits_{i\in S}A_{i,j}
.

In the above definition,
\dot{\cup}
stands for the disjoint union, of course.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template and
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
a (1,1)-minimal multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
such that
\Gamma_{V}
is connected. Then
P
has a link partition iff
\Gamma_{P}
is disconnected.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma_{P}
be disconnected and let
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k}
be the connected components of
\Gamma_{P}
. For all
i\in V
, we define
A_{i,j}:=A_{i}\cap Q_{j}
. If
(S,R)\in V
, then for any
r\in R
and
i,j\in S
,
r(i)
and
r(j)
must be connected, so all
r(i)
are in the same connected component, say
Q_{j}
. If we define
R_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
to be
\{r\in R:(\forall i\in S)r(i)\in Q_{j}\}
, from the previous sentence it follows that
R=R_{1}\cup\dots\cup R_{k}
. As
Q_{j}
are pairwise disjoint, thus
R=R_{1}\dot{\cup}\dots\dot{\cup}R_{k}
.

Finally, for each
Q_{j}
and
A_{i}
, we need to show
A_{i}\cap Q_{j}\neq\emptyset
to prove property (1) of link partitions. Choose some
i^{\prime}\in V
so that
A_{i^{\prime}}\cap Q_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Since
\Gamma_{V}
is connected, there exists some
i^{\prime}=i_{0},S_{1},i_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{t},i_{t}=i
which is a path from
i^{\prime}
to
i
in
\Gamma_{V}
. Let
(S_{1},R_{1}),\dots,(S_{t},R_{t})\in{\mathcal{C}}
. We select some
a=a_{0}\in A_{i^{\prime}}\cap Q_{j}
. Next, by (1,1)-minimality, there exist some
r_{1}\in R_{1},\dots,r_{t}\in R_{t}
and
a_{1}\in A_{i_{1}},\dots,a_{i_{t}}\in A_{i_{t}}=A_{i}
so that for all
1\leq s\leq t
,
r_{s}(i_{s-1})=a_{s-1}
and
r_{s}(i_{s})=a_{s}
. Inductively it follows that
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{s}\in Q_{j}
, so
a_{t}\in Q_{j}\cap A_{i}
, as desired.

For the other direction, if
P
has a link partition and
j\neq j^{\prime}
for some
j,j^{\prime}\leq k
, then for any
i,i^{\prime}\in V
no edge in
\Gamma_{P}
connects an element of
A_{i,j}
and an element of
A_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
. Thus
\Gamma_{P}
must be disconnected.
∎

Next we prove that the connected components of
\Gamma_{P}
give rise to congruences.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template and
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
a (1,1)-minimal and cycle consistent multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
such that
\Gamma_{V}
is connected. Then, for each
i\in V
,
\{(a,b)\in A_{i}:a
and

are connected in
\Gamma_{P}\}
is a congruence relation of
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
. Consequently, for any connected component
Q_{j}
of
\Gamma_{P}
,
Q_{j}\cap A_{i}
is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}

Sketch of a proof.

For each
a,b\in A_{i}
such that they are connected in
\Gamma_{P}
, let
p_{a,b}
be the path from
a
to
b
in
\Gamma_{P}
and
q_{a,b}
the corresponding closed path from
i
to
i
in
\Gamma_{V}
. By cycle consistency, for any
c\in A_{i}
there is a closed path from
c
to
c
in
\Gamma_{P}
which also corresponds to
q_{a,b}
. Now we define
q
to be the closed path from
i
to
i
in
\Gamma_{V}
obtained by concatenating all paths
q_{a,b}
for any
(a,b)\in A_{i}\times A_{i}
such that
a
is connected to
b
in
\Gamma_{P}
.

We claim that for any
c
and
d
in
A_{i}
which are connected in
\Gamma_{P}
,
c
is connected to
d
by a path in
\Gamma_{P}
corresponding to

. To see this just circle around from
c
to
c
by paths corresponding to various

until
q_{c,d}
comes along. Then move from
c
to
d
and afterwards keep circling from
d
to
d
. Note that

\{(a,b)\in A_{i}\times A_{i}:a\text{ is connected to }b\text{ by a path in }\Gamma_{P}\text{ corresponding to }q\}

is a pp-definable relation, so a compatible relation of the algebra
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
. Moreover, it is obviously an equivalence relation, being the restriction to
A_{i}
of the hypergraph connectedness relation, so it is a congruence. The final sentence follows since congruence classes are subuniverses.
∎

For each induced subinstance with a connected scope graph, but disconnected microstructure graph which is
(1,1)
-minimal and cycle consistent, we just proved that the subinstance splits into disjoint smaller instances. Each of these smaller instances is given by the connected components of the microstructure graph, and the domain of each variable
i
in each of the smaller instances is a nonvoid proper subuniverse of
A_{i}
. Therefore, one can inductively solve each of these smaller instances to ensure there exists a solution through any point. However, there may be exponentially many induced subinstances to consider, so it is not yet obvious we can enforce Z-irreducibility this way.

This issue was resolved by the procedure CHECKIRREDUCIBILITY in
[
32
]
. In a nutshell, D. Zhuk considers one domain of a variable and a maximal congruence on it, assumes the elements inside the same congruence classes are “connected”, and keeps adding constraints and variables for as long as the microstructure graph remains disconnected and the connected components restrict to the fixed domain of variable as a congruence contained in the selected maximal congruence. The maximal such induced subinstance is then solved through any point in the fixed domain of the variable. This is done in polynomial time, since there are only polynomially many maximal subinstances to check. Zhuk proves that any induced subinstance which is cycle consistent must be contained in a maximal subinstance checked by the procedure, hence the procedure correctly forces Z-irreducibility of the instance. Thus one can either resolve an instance or reduce it to an equivalent Z-irreducible instance.

The main tool from Zhuk’s Dichotomy proof we will use in this paper is the following theorem (we will use it as a black box, without proof):

Theorem 5.11

(follows from Theorem 5.5 of
[
32
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template of SMB algebras and let
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
be a multisorted instance of
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
. If
P
is Z-irreducible, (1,1)-minimal, cycle consistent and has a solution, then
P
has a solution
f
such that for each
i\in V
,
f(i)
is in the least
\sim
-class in
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
.

Now we know all prerequisites needed to prove Theorem
4.15
. First we prove some easy, technical lemmas.

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
R\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
be a subdirect product of unital SMB algebras
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
(recall Definition
3.5
). Let
1_{A}
and
1_{B}
be the unit elements of
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}

If
(1_{A},1_{B})\in R

Hence, the path

to

in

.

.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template and let

i
and

and

\Gamma_{P}

Proof.

Let

connecting

. Let
(S_{1},R_{1}),\dots,(S_{k},R_{k})\in{\mathcal{C}}
be the constraints involving the scopes

\begin{gathered}(x,y)\in R\text{ iff }(\exists\overline{z}_{1},\overline{z}_{2},\dots,\overline{z}_{k})(\exists u_{1},\dots,u_{k-1})\\
\left[(x,\overline{z}_{1},u_{1})\in R_{1},(u_{1},\overline{z}_{2},u_{2})\in R_{2},\dots,(u_{k-1},\overline{z}_{k},y)\in R_{k}\right]\end{gathered}

In the above formula, the coordinates of

Lemma 5.14
.

Let

be a subdirect product of regular and unital SMB algebras

be the unit element of

\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
.

Proof.

Since

is a two-sided neutral element with respect to the operation

in

is compatible with

. As for the compatibility with

\begin{gathered}d(\overline{a},\overline{b},\overline{1})=d((\overline{b}\wedge\overline{1})\wedge\overline{a},(\overline{a}\wedge\overline{1})\wedge\overline{b},(\overline{a}\wedge\overline{b})\wedge\overline{1})=\\

(i_{0},a_{0}),Q_{1},(i_{1},a_{1}),\dots,Q_{k},(i_{k},a_{k}),

such that

and let

. Then there exists a corresponding path

in the microstructure graph of

,

such that each hyperedge

is the set of all coordinates of some tuple in the constraint relation

corresponding to the scope

.

Proof of Claim 2.
Let

be the extension of

obtained by adding the hyperedge

to the scope graph. If we extend the path

, but is a path in the scope graph of
P_{1}^{\prime\prime}
. We want to prove that there exists a path
q^{\prime\prime}
in the microstructure graph of

corresponding to the path

whose first vertex is

and last edge is
a_{k}\wedge b,R_{i_{0}},a_{0}\wedge b
. By deleting the last edge from the path

we would obtain the desired path

in the microstructure graph of

.

We have already proved a very similar claim in the course of the proof of cycle consistency of

. The only difference is that we assumed that

, which was more of a convenience than a real requirement. Using an analogous argument as the one we made in the cycle consistency, together with a cyclic path
p
in the microstructure graph of
P_{1}
from

to

, we prove the existence of the corresponding cyclic path

in the microstructure graph of
P_{1}^{\prime\prime}
from

to

. Since the last vertex in the path

is

and the last edge in the path

is

, the last edge in the path

must be

. As we already said, deleting this last edge from

proves the existence of the desired path

and thus, proves Claim 2.

Claim 3.
If the microstructure graph of

is connected and the scope graph of

is connected, then the microstructure graph of

is connected.

Remark
. The contrapositive of the statement of Claim 3 is what we need for Z-irreducibility. We can not prove Claim 3 in general, but we can in our more restrictive setting, recalling that
P
and

are both (1,1)-minimal and cycle consistent.

Proof of Claim 3
. Assume that

and
b_{2}\in A_{j,a_{2}}
. Then there is a path
p
in the microstructure graph of

from

to
b_{2}\in A_{j}
. Let this path

be

where

, for some

,

is the set of all coordinates of a tuple
t_{u}\in R_{u}
, while
(S_{u},R_{u})\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
are constraints such that
j_{u-1},j_{u}\in S_{u}
.

By the definition of
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
, there must exist tuples
r_{u}\in R_{u}
and constraints
(S_{r_{u}},R_{r_{u}})\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{\prime}
. Hence there are hyperedges

in the microstructure graph of

such that

It remains to find paths

in the microstructure graph of

such that

are precisely what is guaranteed by Claim 2. Thus, the microstructure graph of

is connected, so we proved Claim 3.

Now we finish the proof of the lemma. Assume that the subinstance

of

has a connected scope graph, but disconnected microstructure graph. The corresponding subinstance

of

also has a connected scope graph by Claim 1, and by the contrapositive of Claim 3 we obtain that

also has a disconnected microstructure graph.

Let

be any domain of a variable of

and let

be any point in

. We know that

, for any

. Moreover,

is Z-irreducible and Lemma

∎

Now we complete the proof of Theorem
4.15
. As

is either solved or reduced to a (2,3)-minimal and Z-irreducible instance, it follows that

can be assumed to be both (1,1)-minimal and cycle consistent, as both are weaker notions than (2,3)-minimality. So Lemma

implies that the decomposition

is (1,1)-minimal, cycle consistent, Z-irreducible and each domain of a variable in

is a unital SMB algebra (though maybe not regular any longer). Nevertheless, by Theorem

,

has a solution

such that for each

,

is in the least

.

Now we can mimic the proof of Lemma
4.9
to either solve

or reduce it to an equivalent smaller instance. The difference is that in the second paragraph of the proof of Lemma

we recursively invoked

as an instance of a smaller template. Now we are able to, instead, apply the just-proved fact that

6.
A better fix for the gap

We have plugged the gap in A. Bulatov’s paper
[
9
]
, but at a heavy cost. To prove the tractability of SMB algebras, a special case of Taylor algebras, we used Theorem
5.11
, a major part of D. Zhuk’s proof of tractability of Taylor algebras. Even worse, the structure of Zhuk’s proof makes each piece inseparable from the rest of the proof, so if we were to write out all proofs of the facts we used in the previous section, we would be forced to include the full proof of the tractability of Taylor algebras by Zhuk, i.e. of the Dichotomy Conjecture.

Fortunately, a better fix for the same gap can be made using just the ideas in A. Bulatov’s original paper
[
9
]
and tweaking them a little. In order to demonstrate this fix, we proceed to expose definitions and statements of results from
[
9
]
. The issue comes up when we want to solve the CSP over restrictions of the instance
(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
to “coherent sets”
W\subseteq V
. We are able to solve the instance
P|_{W}
using just Theorem
4.16
, instead of Theorem
4.15
.

In the section that follows, we have changed some of the notions and results from
[
9
]
beyond what is needed to solve the reduced instances
P|_{W}
. For example, besides the key notion of block-minimality, we also define a weaker one, block-2-consistency. In effect, after solving
P|_{W}
, we are able to finish the tractability proof even if we enforce less consistency on the projection of
P
to coherent sets than A. Bulatov did. We also include a few easy observations which A. Bulatov uses, though he doesn’t explicitly state them and correct a minor, fairly obvious, error. In all, we feel that our variant of A. Bulatov’s proof is more “user-friendly” than the original. However, we wish to state that what we present here is still only a variant of the proof by A. Bulatov, since the gap in his proof which we found and fixed turned out to be solvable using the ideas in his original paper.

6.1.
Rees congruences

Definition 6.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite regular SMB algebra over
\sim
. If the least block of
{\mathbf{A}}
is
B
, we call the
Rees congruence
of
{\mathbf{A}}
the relation
B^{2}\cup\Delta_{A}
. The Rees congruence is denoted by
\theta_{{\mathbf{A}}}
. Moreover, we will denote the subuniverse
B
as
\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
.

The Rees congruence is a congruence of the regular SMB algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
since
\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
is a strongly absorbing subuniverse (for any term operation, if an element of
\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
is in an essential position, then the result is in
\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
). The term Rees congruence comes from Semigroup Theory.

Lemma 6.2
.

If
{\mathbf{R}}\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\times\dots\times{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
, where all
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
are regular SMB algebras with
\theta_{i}
the Rees congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, then the Rees congruence of
{\mathbf{R}}
is the restriction of the product congruence
\theta_{1}\times\dots\times\theta_{n}
to
R
, while
\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{R}})
is
(\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{1})\times\dots\times\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{n}))\cap R
.

Proof.

Since
R
is subdirect, we can select
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\in R
so that
\mathbf{a}_{i}(i)\in\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
, and

\mathbf{a}:=(\dots(\mathbf{a}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{a}_{2})\wedge\dots)\wedge\mathbf{a}_{n}

must be in
(\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{1})\times\dots\times\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{n}))\cap R
. As
(\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{1})\times\dots\times\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}_{n}))\cap R
is nonempty, it must be the least
\sim
-class of
{\mathbf{R}}
.
∎

Lemma 6.3

(Lemma 11 of
[
9
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a finite regular SMB algebra with the Rees congruence
\theta_{A}
,
0_{A}\leq\alpha\prec\beta\leq\theta_{{\mathbf{A}}}
. Then for any
U\in M_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\alpha,\beta)
we have
U\subseteq\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
.

Proof.

Let
f\in{\rm Pol_{1}{\mathbf{A}}}
be such that
f(A)=U\in M_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\alpha,\beta)
and for all
x\in A
,
f(f(x))=f(x)
. Then
f(\beta)\nsubseteq\alpha
. Select some
(a,b)\in f(\beta)\setminus\alpha
. Hence
a\neq b
. As
f(\beta)\subseteq\beta\subseteq\theta_{{\mathbf{A}}}=0_{A}\cup(\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})\times\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}))
, this means that
(a,b)\in(\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})\times\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}))
. Define
g(x)=f(x)\wedge a
.

We know that for all
x\in U\cap\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
,
f(x)=x\in\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
and therefore

f(g(x))=f(f(x)\wedge a)=f(x\wedge a)=f(x)=x.

On the other hand, since
{\mathbf{A}}
is a regular SMB algebra, any nonconstant unary polynomial maps
\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
into
\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
. Therefore, for any
x\in A
,
g(x)=f(x)\wedge a\in\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
, and therefore

f(g(x))\in U\cap\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}}).

So we have that
f(g(A))=U\cap\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})\subseteq U
and
(f(g(a)),f(g(b)))=(a,b)\in\beta\setminus\alpha
, and thus
f(g(\beta))\nsubseteq\alpha
. Since
U\in M_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\alpha,\beta)
, it follows that
U=U\cap\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
.
∎

Proposition 6.6

Let

{{\rm Con\>}{\mathbf{A}}}

Let

(Lemma 17 of

)

Lemma 6.14
.

(based on Exercise 8.8 (1) of
[
22
]
)

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a regular SMB algebra,
\theta
its Rees congruence and
\alpha\prec\beta\leq\theta
in
{{\rm Con\>}{\mathbf{A}}}
. If
(a,b)\in\beta\setminus\alpha
, then there exist an
(\alpha,\beta)
-trace
N
and
c\in A
such that
\{a,c\}\subseteq N
and
(c,b)\in\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
(a,b)\in\beta\setminus\alpha
and let
V\in M_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\alpha,\beta)
. By Theorem
6.4
(3), there exists
f\in{\rm Pol_{1}{\mathbf{A}}}
such that
f(A)=V
and
(f(a),f(b))\in(\beta|_{V})\setminus(\alpha|_{V})
. Since
\alpha\prec\beta
, we know that
\beta={\rm Cg}(\alpha\cup\{(a,b)\})={\rm Cg}(\alpha\cup\{(f(a),f(b))\})
. We define the relation

\begin{gathered}\beta^{\prime}:=\{(p(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}),p(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n})):n\in\mathbb{N},\,p\in{\rm Pol_{n}{\mathbf{A}}}\text{ and}\\
\text{for all }i\leq n,\,(a_{i},b_{i})\in\alpha\text{ or }(a_{i},b_{i})=(f(a),f(b))\}.\end{gathered}

Obviously
\alpha\subsetneq\beta^{\prime}\subseteq\beta
. We claim that
\beta^{\prime}=\beta
. If we prove that
\beta^{\prime}
is a congruence then
\beta^{\prime}=\beta
would follow from
\alpha\prec\beta
in
{{\rm Con\>}{\mathbf{A}}}
, so we will prove
\beta^{\prime}\in{{\rm Con\>}{\mathbf{A}}}
.

Reflexivity of
\beta^{\prime}
follows from
\alpha\subseteq\beta^{\prime}
, while its compatibility with the fundamental operations follows from the definition of a polynomial. It remains to show symmetry and transitivity.

(S):
Let
(s,t)\in\beta^{\prime}
, where
s=p(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and
t=p(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n})
as in the definition of
\beta^{\prime}
. The polynomial
q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=d(s,p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),t)
witnesses that
(t,s)\in\beta^{\prime}
, since

(t,s)=(d(s,s,t),d(s,t,t))=(q(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}),q(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n})).

(T):
Let
(s,t),(t,u)\in\beta^{\prime}
and let
p,q\in{\rm Pol\>{\mathbf{A}}}
, like in the definition of
\beta^{\prime}
, be such that

\begin{gathered}s=p(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\\
t=p(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})=q(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\\
u=q(d_{1},\dots,d_{n}).\end{gathered}

The polynomial
r(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
given by

d(p(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),t,q(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}))

satisfies

\begin{gathered}s=d(s,t,t)=r(a_{1},\dots,a_{m},c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\\
u=d(t,t,u)=r(b_{1},\dots,b_{m},d_{1},\dots,d_{n}).\end{gathered}

Hence
(s,u)\in\beta^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
is transitive.

Now we have that
\beta^{\prime}=\beta
, and since
(a,b)\in\beta
, it follows that there exists a polynomial
g\in{\rm Pol_{n+1}{\mathbf{A}}}
and
(c_{1},d_{1}),\dots,(c_{n},d_{n})\in\alpha
such that

(a,b)=(g(f(a),c_{1},\dots,c_{n}),g(f(b),d_{1},\dots,d_{n})).

Define
p\in{\rm Pol_{1}{\mathbf{A}}}
by
p(x)=g(x,c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
. Since

p(f(a))=a\not\equiv_{\alpha}b=g(f(b),d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\equiv_{\alpha}g(f(b),c_{1},\dots,c_{n})=p(f(b)),

according to Theorem
6.4
(2),
U:=f(V)\in M_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\alpha,\beta)
. Therefore
U
contains a trace
N
such that
\{p(f(a)),p(f(b))\}\subseteq N
. Since
a=p(f(a))
and
p(f(b))\equiv_{\alpha}b
, we can select
c:=p(f(b))
to prove the lemma.
∎

k-1
compositions, we proceed to inductively prove that for all

,
(f_{j}^{\ell+1}(a),f_{j}^{\ell+1}(b))\in\gamma_{k-\ell}
.

The base case follows from

, i.e.
(f_{j}(a),f_{j}(b))\in\theta_{j}=\gamma_{k}
. Assume that
(f_{j}^{t}(a),f_{j}^{t}(b))\in\gamma_{k-t+1}
and denote

and
b^{\prime}:=f_{j}^{t}(b)
. The assumption of (2) gives that
(\alpha,\beta)
can be separated from
(\gamma_{k-t},\gamma_{k-t+1})
with respect to
{\mathbf{R}}
. Therefore, by Definition
6.13
,
f_{j}(\gamma_{k-t+1}\subseteq\gamma_{k-t}
. Hence

(f_{j}^{t+1}(a),f_{j}^{t+1}(b))=(f_{j}(a^{\prime}),f_{j}(b^{\prime}))\in f_{j}(\gamma_{k-t+1}\subseteq\gamma_{k-t}.

The inductive step is proved, and hence we know that

(f_{j}^{k+1}(a),f_{j}^{k+1}(b))\in\gamma_{k-k}=\gamma_{0}=0_{j}.

In other words,
f_{j}^{k+1}(a)=f_{j}^{k+1}(b)
. But, from idempotence of
f
, it follows that
f_{j}
is also an idempotent polynomial of
{\mathbf{A}}_{j}
, i.e.
f_{j}(f_{j}(x))=f_{j}(x)
. Applying this several times, we get

f_{j}(a)=f_{j}^{k+1}(a)=f_{j}^{k+1}(b)=f_{j}(b).

As

and

were arbitrarily chosen elements of

, we get

lies in

(2).
∎

Corollary 6.30
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a template of regular SMB algebras. Then
CSP({\mathcal{T}})
is tractable.

Proof.

The main algorithm,
SOLVE(P)
, inputs the instance
P=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
of
{\mathcal{T}}
and performs the following steps:

Step 1.

Replace
P
with an equivalent
(2,3)
-minimal and weakly M-irreducible instance, then set
M:=Size(P)
.

Step 2.

Flag all
(i,\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathcal{I}}_{P}
as 0.

Step 3.

For any
(i,\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathcal{I}}_{P}
with flag 0 do

Step 3.1.

Set
W:=\{i\}
and flag
(i,\alpha,\beta)
as 1.

Step 3.2.

For any
(j,\gamma,\delta)\in{\mathcal{I}}_{P}
do

Step 3.2.1.

If
(\alpha,\beta)
can’t be separated from
(\gamma,\delta)
wrt.
P
, then set
W:=W\cup\{j\}
and flag
(j,\gamma,\delta)
as 1.

Step 3.3.

CHKCOHSET(P,W)
.

Step 3.4.

If for any
i,j\in W
,
T_{\{i,j\}}=\emptyset
, output
NO
and stop.

Step 3.5.

If for any
i,j\in W
,
|T_{\{i,j\}}|<|R_{\{i,j\}}|
then

Step 3.5.1.

Update
P
with
R_{\{i,j\}}:=T_{\{i,j\}}
for all
i,j\in W
.

Step 3.5.2.

Perform
(2,3)
-minimality on
P
.

Step 3.5.3.

If
Size(P)<M
, then
SOLVE(P)
and stop.

Step 3.5.4.

Go to Step 2.

Step 3.6.

Go to the next element of
{\mathcal{I}}_{P}
in Step 3.

Step 4.

Output ’
YES
’ and stop.

After initially tightening the instance to a
(2,3)
-minimal and weakly M-irreducible one (which can be done according to Theorem
4.16
), the algorithm
SOLVE
next computes all coherent sets. After applying the procedure
CHKCOHSET
to
P
and a coherent set
W
, the algorithm either concludes there is no solution since even
P|_{W}
has no solution, or the instance
P
is tightened to an instance of smaller size which can recursively be solved, or the instance
P
is tightened to a same-sized one (which thus must still be weakly M-irreducible, while
(2,3)
-minimality has been established in the previous step) and the whole checking has to start over, or the instance is unchanged which means that
P|_{W}
has a solution through any edge. If the procedure reached Step 4, this means that for every coherent set
W
,
P|_{W}
has a solution through any edge, i.e. that
P
is block-2-consistent. Then by Theorem
6.29
,
P
has a solution.

There is a linear number of times an instance can be tightened, and each coherent set is checked exactly once if there are no tightenings, so there is at most a quadratic number of applications of
CHKCOHSET
. Moreover, checking for separation between covering pairs in
{\mathcal{I}}_{P}
takes a constant amount of time, so the running time of the algorithm
SOLVE
is polynomial.

7.
Concluding remarks

We have (re)proved tractability of the Constraint Satisfaction Problem over SMB algebras in two ways. Along the way, we uncovered several similarities between the Dichotomy Theorem proofs by Bulatov and by Zhuk. Firstly, Zhuk’s irreducibility and Bulatov’s block minimality are related properties, they serve the same purpose in the two proofs, and it is easy to find a common generalization. Secondly, there is the benefit one gets from going to a term reduct, like we did by going from an SMB algebra to its reduct which is a regular SMB algebra. This idea was taken to its logical extreme in the paper
[
3
]
by L. Barto, Z. Brady, A.Bulatov, M. Kozik and D. Zhuk, which introduced the idea of minimal Taylor algebras. In minimal Taylor algebras the approaches of Bulatov and Zhuk are connected and there we may find a fertile ground to generalize out idea from Theorem
5.15
. The main drawback is that we still lean on Theorem
5.11
, the only proof of which uses the full proof of the Dichotomy Theorem by Zhuk. So our first open problem is

Problem 7.1
.

Prove Theorem
5.11
without resorting to the full power of Zhuk’s dichotomy proof.

In minimal Taylor algebras, the subset
\mathsf{umax}({\mathbf{A}})
, which is the sole sink strong component of Bulatov’s directed graph of the algebra, is actually a binary absorbing subuniverse. Using Theorem
5.11
, we may assume that in a multisorted instance,
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}=\mathsf{umax}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
for each domain of a variable
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
. To move further in using our proof as a template, it would be beneficial that the subuniverses
R\leq\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
were somehow regular. In our case of regular SMB algebras we have
\mathsf{umax}({\mathbf{A}})=\mathrm{min}({\mathbf{A}})
, and therefore the relations
R
we are interested in are subuniverses of products of Mal’cev algebras, which are very well-behaved. Bulatov proves something similar for
R\leq\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{A}}_{i}
in the case
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}=\mathsf{amax}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
, where
\mathsf{amax}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})\subseteq\mathsf{umax}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
is a well-behaved subset. We thus formulate our second problem, solving which (together with Problem
7.1
) would probably simplify the proof of the Dichotomy Theorem:

Problem 7.2
.

Either prove that the multisorted instances of CSP over minimal Taylor algebras can be reduced to the
\mathsf{amax}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
, or develop a theory of subuniverses of products of minimal Taylor algebras all of which satisfy the condition
{\mathbf{A}}_{i}=\mathsf{umax}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
.
