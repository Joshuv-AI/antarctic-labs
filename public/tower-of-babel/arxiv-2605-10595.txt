Curvature-Dependent Lower Bounds for Frank–Wolfe
Jannis Halbey, Christophe Roux, Sebastian Pokutta

Abstract
The Frank–Wolfe (FW) algorithm achieves a convergence rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / T ) \mathcal{O}(1/T) for smooth convex optimization over compact convex domains, accelerating to 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / T 2 ) \mathcal{O}(1/T^{2}) when both the objective and the feasible set are strongly convex. This acceleration extends beyond strong convexity: 8 proved rates of 𝒪 ( T − p / ( p − 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}(T^{-p/(p-1)}) over p p -uniformly convex feasible sets, a class that interpolates between strongly convex sets and more general curved domains such as ℓ p \ell_{p} balls. In this work, we establish a matching Ω ( T − p / ( p − 1 ) ) \Omega(T^{-p/(p-1)}) lower bound for every p ≥ 3 p\geq 3 under exact line search or short steps, and extend the lower bound to objectives satisfying a Hölderian error bound. The proofs analyze the dynamics of FW iterates on simple instances and hence are not limited to the high-dimensional setting, unlike information-theoretic lower bounds.

Introduction

The
Frank–Wolfe (FW)
algorithm
(
3
)
is designed to solve constrained optimization problems of the form

\min_{x\in\mathcal{C}}f(x),

using only access to a
Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO)
. Here
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth and convex function and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact and convex set.

The classical
\mathcal{O}(1/T)
rate
(
7
)
is tight for general compact convex
\mathcal{C}

(
2
;
10
)
, but stronger structural assumptions yield faster rates.

11
and
15
obtained linear rates under conditions on the position of the unconstrained optimizer, and
4
obtained the global rate
\mathcal{O}(1/T^{2})
when both the set and the objective are strongly convex.

6
recently showed that it is tight for
FW
with exact line search or short steps, and
5
obtained a matching
\Omega(1/T^{2})
bound for a broader class of
LMO
-based methods in the high-dimensional regime.

8
extended the upper bound picture from strongly convex sets to uniformly convex sets, and from strongly convex objectives to objectives satisfying a
Hölderian Error Bound (HEB)
, deriving a family of rates that interpolate between
\mathcal{O}(1/T)
and
\mathcal{O}(1/T^{2})
.
Whether these interpolating rates are tight has remained open.
In this work we resolve the question partially by establishing a matching lower bound for
HEB
objectives with
\theta=\tfrac{1}{2}
on uniformly convex sets, and a looser lower bound for general
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.

Both lower bounds follow from the same recipe. We first minimize a quadratic objective over the
\ell_{p}
ball and exhibit an explicit
slow-start
initialization such that exact-line-search
FW
iterates remain pinned to a particular slow trajectory.
Tracking the contraction along this trajectory recovers the predicted rate.
We then replace this quadratic objective with a richer family of objectives and apply the same argument to obtain the lower bound for the
(\mu,\theta)
-
HEB
regime.

Our lower bound results are closely related to the one of
6
.
We use a similar model setup, replacing the Euclidean ball with the unit
\ell_{p}
ball, and obtain the lower bound by analyzing specific
FW
trajectories.
However, the method for initializing the starting point and the proof technique differ significantly from that used by
6
.
We give a detailed comparison at the end of
Section

2
.

The proof of the main result in this paper (
Theorem

4
) was found using the agentic research framework that we introduced in a companion preprint
(
16
)
, which delegates well-scoped research subtasks to LLM agents under structured human supervision.
Section 4.4 of
16
states
Theorem

4
without proof, as one of the case studies that motivated the framework.
The present paper closes that gap by providing the precise theorem statement, the asymptotic constants, the full proof, the
HEB
extension (
Theorem

5
), and the numerical validation.
Section

4
discusses which steps were agent-led, which were load-bearing for human judgement, and the failure modes we encountered.

Upper bounds.

Lower bounds.

The classical
\Omega(1/T)
bounds
(

10
)
are built on polytopal or general convex constraint sets and do not apply to curved sets.
Until recently, it was open whether the rates obtained by

While
6

Preliminaries.

We work in a finite-dimensional normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|)
.
The support of a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as
\supp(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\{i\in[d]:x_{i}\neq 0\}
.
A differentiable function is
\mu
-strongly convex
if

f(x)\geq f(y)+\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
L
-smooth
if

f(x)\leq f(y)+\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

For a smooth convex objective
f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}
on a compact convex set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote an optimizer by

x^{*}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{C}}f(x)
and the primal suboptimality by
h_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})
.
Further,
B_{p}
denotes the unit
\ell_{p}
ball, i.e.,
B_{p}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|_{p}\leq 1\}
.

1
Uniformly convex set
(
8
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be closed and
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be nondecreasing.
We say that
\mathcal{C}
is
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
-uniformly convex (with respect to
\|\cdot\|
) if
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
, all
\eta\in[0,1]
, and all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|z\|=1
,

\eta x+(1-\eta)y+\eta(1-\eta)\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(\|x-y\|)\,z\in\mathcal{C}.

If there exist
\alpha>0
and
q\geq 2
such that

\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}(r)\geq\alpha r^{q}
for all
r\geq 0
, then
\mathcal{C}
is called

(\alpha,q)
-uniformly convex (or
q
-uniformly convex).

2
Hölderian error bound
(
8
)
.

Let
f
be strictly convex on
\mathcal{C}
, and let
x^{*}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{C}}f(x)
.
We say that
f
is
(\mu,\theta)

2.1
Construction of the slow initialization

To analyze the local behavior near the optimizer
e_{1}
, it is convenient to separate the error along the first coordinate from the transverse component along
e_{2}
.
We therefore define centered coordinates

u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}1-x_{1},\qquad w\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}x_{2}.

Combining the definition of the

We characterize the resulting update in closed form in

The trajectories in

The remaining question is how to scale the transverse coordinate

To disregard sign changes and capture the faster convergence of the transverse coordinate

We will later see that this choice of the exponent

Next we consider the one-step update of the scaled variable

y^{\prime}=\Phi(u,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{|w^{\prime}|}{(u^{\prime})^{1+\alpha}},

(4)

where

Next we derive a fixed point map

2.2
Establishing the lower bound

We now derive the asymptotic convergence rate of the primal gap
h_{t}=f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})
along the explicit slow-start trajectory.
For that we consider the residual distance
r_{t}=\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|
=
\sqrt{h_{t}}
and its one-step contraction factor
s_{t}
, defined as

s_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\frac{r_{t+1}}{r_{t}}.

The key input is the tracking estimate in (
6
), which keeps the iterates in the slow-curve regime where the expansions from the previous subsection are valid with controlled remainders.
In
Proposition

14
we combine these expansions with an exact one-step decrease identity derived from the exact line search, which yields the asymptotic contraction law

1-s_{t}=2C_{p}^{2}\,r_{t}^{\kappa}(1+o(1)).

(7)

With this we can finally assemble the lower bound result.
We consider the problem of minimizing the smooth and strongly convex objective
f(x)=\|x-e_{1}\|^{2}
over the
p
-uniform convex set
B_{p}
.

FW
with an exact line search initialized at the explicit slow-start point
x_{0}^{\mathrm{slow}}(u_{0})
from (
5
) produces a trajectory, for which
Proposition

13
guarantees persistent tracking of the slow curve.
Combined with (
7
), this yields a precise asymptotic recurrence for the primal gap along that trajectory.
The theorem then follows by summing this asymptotic recurrence, yielding the asymptotic constant and rate.

For every
p\geq 3
, there exist a
p
-uniformly convex feasible set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a smooth and strongly convex function
f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for a suitable initialization, exact-line-search
FW
generates iterates
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfying

f(x_{T})-f(x^{*})\;\sim\;\left(\frac{p+1}{p}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{p}{4(p-1)}\right)^{p/(p-1)}T^{-p/(p-1)}\qquad\text{as }T\to\infty.

(8)

In particular, for any
T\geq 0
, we have
f(x_{T})-f(x^{*})=\Omega\left(T^{-p/(p-1)}\right)
.

This result shows that the order of the convergence rate proved by
8

\mathcal{O}(T^{-p/(p-2\theta)})
is tight up to constants in the strongly convex case, i.e., for
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
.
Since the considered quadratic is isotropic, the exact line search and the short step rule coincide.
Thus, the lower bound holds for
FW
with exact line search and for
FW
with short steps.
Moreover, the example is constructed in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
but can be extended to arbitrary dimension due to
Proposition

3
.
So unlike the classical results from
12
or the more recent lower bound by
5
, our result is not limited to the high-dimensional setting.

The above lower bound is closely related to the one by
6
.
Both approaches consider a two-dimensional projection problem onto the Euclidean ball and the unit
\ell_{p}
ball, respectively, and derive a lower bound for a specific initialization.
However, the initialization method as well as the overall proof differ significantly.
For
p=2
, the slow initialization requires very high precision.
Therefore,
6
do not give an explicit formula for the start point
x_{0}
, but instead choose the end point
x_{T}
close to the optimum and then run
FW
backward to obtain a valid slow starting point
x_{0}
.
In contrast, we give an explicit formula for the initialization in (
5
).
Furthermore, we show that the update of the scaled coordinate
y
is contractive in a neighborhood of the fixed-point map
y^{*}(u)
.
Thus we prove the robust initialization, which can be observed in
Figure

3
.

Additionally, the proof of the lower-bound rate differs greatly between the two approaches.

6

r_{t}=\|x_{t}-e_{1}\|_{2}
and its contraction rate

4
Conclusion

We established matching
\Omega\big(T^{-p/(p-1)}\big)
lower bounds on the convergence of
FW
with exact line search or short steps over
p
-uniformly convex feasible sets for every
p\geq 3
, pairing with the upper bounds of
8
.
The arguments follow the dynamics of
FW
on explicit instances on
\ell_{p}
balls and apply in any fixed dimension
d\geq 2
, unlike typical information-theoretic constructions.
We further extended this framework to smooth convex objectives satisfying a Hölderian error bound, recovering the uniformly convex exponent when
\theta=\tfrac{1}{2}
while leaving open whether the known upper bounds are tight for
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.

Open questions.

There are several natural questions left open by our analysis.
First, our lower bound in the
HEB
case does not match the known upper bound by
8
.
It remains open whether one can find a matching lower bound or if one can prove a faster convergence guarantee.
Second, our lower bounds are stated for
FW
with exact line search or short steps.

14
show that open-loop step-size rules can converge faster on uniformly convex sets in some regimes, and a matching lower bound for open-loop
FW
would close this gap.
Finally,
FW
variants such as away-step and pairwise
FW
are known to accelerate the algorithm on polytopes.
Whether away-step and pairwise
FW
can bypass our lower bound on uniformly convex sets is still unknown.

AI-assisted proof discovery.

The framework of
(
16
)
couples a general-purpose command-line coding agent with a sandboxed workspace, a project-specific instruction file, and a set of methodological rules (“commandments”) that enforce single-variable experimentation, staged verification, and structured reporting.
The human researcher steers the agent at the level of subtasks rather than individual edits, and intermediate artefacts (proof drafts, numerical checks, plots) are version-controlled so the trajectory of the project remains inspectable.

We gave the agent the prior strongly convex lower bounds and the matching
p
-uniformly convex upper bound (
Table

1
) and asked it to find a tight lower bound in the uniformly convex regime.
It first attempted to generalize the high-dimensional construction of
5
to
\ell_{p}
balls.
This failed, as the construction relies on decomposing strongly convex sets as intersections of shifted Euclidean balls and the agent did not find a direct analogue for
p>2
.
The agent then pivoted to the dynamics-based approach of
6
, derived the
FW
iteration on
\ell_{p}
balls in closed form, and verified each component numerically with Julia
BigFloat
scripts, totaling more than thirty individual checks.
Experiments across multiple values of
p
revealed that the iterates alternate in sign and settle onto a low-dimensional invariant curve whose shape can be characterized analytically, which suggested the right proof strategy.
The agent first estimated the relevant constants numerically and only then derived them in closed form, before assembling a rigorous proof for
p\geq 3
.
The boundary case
p\in(2,3)
, where the sign alternation is intermittent, was identified empirically as qualitatively different and currently lies outside the reach of our technique.
Once the agent had produced a complete draft, we proofread the proof carefully, improved the presentation, and fixed minor issues.
Finally, the extension to
HEB
functions in
Section

3
was derived by hand.
