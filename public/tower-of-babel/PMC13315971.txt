Yellow fever mimicking dengue and leptospirosis in Nariño, Colombia, 2024/2025
Authors: Ivan Camilo Sanchez-Rojas, Catherin Lorena Solarte-Jimenez, Nery Astrid Pabón-Cadena, Andres Rodriguez-Cardona, Derlis Caicedo-Triana, Lina Cristina Bravo-Muñoz, Darío S. Published: 2026

Yellow fever (YF) remains a reemerging public health concern in South America, with 486 cases and 209 deaths (43%) across seven countries, up to May 27, 2026 [1], with recent outbreaks affecting multiple departments of Colombia [2]. In June 2024/February 2025, a focal outbreak was identified in the municipality of Ipiales, Nariño department, at the Andean–Amazon interface, near the Ecuadorian border, involving rural and indigenous communities exposed to sylvatic transmission cycles (Fig. 1). Notably, although Ipiales is widely recognized as a high-altitude Andean municipality (>2000 m above sea level), the affected area in Sucumbíos lies in a low-altitude Andean–Amazon transitional zone with ecological characteristics more consistent with Amazonian environments, which favor sylvatic transmission and may lead to under-recognition of yellow fever risk. Within this context, we conducted a retrospective review of YF cases in Nariño, highlighting diagnostic challenges and illustrating the clinical spectrum ranging from mild disease to fatal outcomes [3].Fig. 1Yellow fever in Nariño, Colombia, 2024/2025. In June 2024/February 2025, a total of 18 suspected cases were investigated in Nariño, of which 2 were positive (11%). Both positive patients were young males from rural areas of the corregimiento of Sucumbíos (Ipiales municipality) (Brisas del Gavilan and Santa Rosa towns), with occupational and environmental exposure to forested settings (Fig. 1). No YF cases have occurred in other municipalities of the Nariño department during the 2024-2026 outbreak in Colombia. Colombia's 2024-2026 outbreak had cumulated 200 cases and 89 deaths (44.5%) in 11 (out 32) departments, up to June 19, 2026 (www.ins.gov.co). Epidemiological investigations confirmed sylvatic transmission and identified the corregimiento of Sucumbíos as an active focus of YF circulation. Both cases occurred in socioeconomically vulnerable populations, including indigenous communities, consistent with the demographic profile observed in the outbreak.

Case 1 was a 20-year-old male who presented after 7–8 days of febrile illness with headache, persistent vomiting, epigastric abdominal pain, and jaundice (Table 1). Laboratory evaluation showed leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, hyperbilirubinemia, and elevated transaminases. Abdominal ultrasound revealed gallbladder wall edema suggestive of acalculous cholecystitis, a finding typically associated with dengue [4]. The initial clinical impression was dengue with warning signs, and leptospirosis was also considered. Serological tests were positive for dengue IgM (weak/positive) and Leptospira IgM, supporting alternative diagnoses. The patient developed hematemesis and rapidly deteriorated, dying within 24 hours of admission. RT-PCR performed at the national reference laboratory (National Institute of Health, Bogota) confirmed YF infection. Case 2 was an 18-year-old male farmer who presented on day five of illness with fever, headache, retro-orbital pain, vomiting, and abdominal pain (Table 1). Laboratory findings included leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated transaminases (AST 680 U/L, ALT 878 U/L), without evidence of jaundice, coagulopathy, or renal dysfunction. Abdominal ultrasound was normal. He was initially diagnosed with dengue with warning signs; however, given epidemiological risk factors, YF was suspected early, and surveillance protocols were activated. The patient received supportive treatment with intravenous fluids and symptomatic management, resulting in progressive clinical improvement and full recovery. RT-PCR performed at the national reference laboratory (National Institute of Health, Bogota) confirmed YF infection.

These two cases illustrate several clinically relevant points. First, YF may closely mimic dengue across its clinical spectrum, from moderate illness to fatal disease [5]. Both patients presented with classical dengue-like features, including fever, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, abdominal pain, and vomiting. In Case 1, hepatobiliary imaging findings further supported a dengue diagnosis, suggesting that acalculous cholecystitis-like presentations may occur in YF and contribute to misdiagnosis. To our knowledge, acalculous cholecystitis has not been previously reported as a manifestation of yellow fever in the available literature, as occurred in patients with abdominal manifestations of dengue [4].

Second, serological cross-reactivity and concurrent positive results for other endemic infections may obscure the diagnosis [5]. In Case 1, positive dengue and Leptospira IgM results likely delayed suspicion of YF, underscoring the limitations of serological assays in endemic settings and the need for molecular confirmation.

Third, these cases demonstrate marked variability in clinical progression. While Case 2 had a favorable outcome with supportive care, Case 1 progressed rapidly to fatal hemorrhagic disease despite only moderate abnormalities at initial evaluation. This highlights the potential for abrupt deterioration in YF before the full classical laboratory profile is established.

Finally, these cases emphasize the importance of integrating clinical findings with epidemiological context and rapid public health response [2,3,5]. Both occurred within a defined outbreak focus in southern Colombia, where field investigations confirmed ongoing sylvatic transmission and the presence of competent vectors, including Aedes albopictus and Sabethes spp (Fig. 1). In response to these cases, coordinated interventions were implemented by local and national health authorities, including targeted vaccination campaigns, with at least 130 YF vaccine doses administered during field activities and additional prior immunization efforts in the affected communities. Active case finding through community-based surveillance (Búsqueda Activa Comunitaria, BAC) involved the systematic evaluation of households and individuals in the affected areas, with no further cases identified. Additional measures included strengthening of local surveillance networks, training of healthcare personnel, and community education on early symptom recognition and prevention. Vector control strategies were also implemented, including larval surveys, breeding-site interventions, entomological monitoring, and the installation of insecticide-treated mosquito nets in high-risk communities. These coordinated actions highlight the importance of rapid, integrated outbreak response to contain transmission and prevent further cases in rural and indigenous populations [2].

In endemic and outbreak settings, YF should be considered in any patient with acute febrile illness and gastrointestinal or hepatic involvement, even when clinical, laboratory, or imaging findings suggest dengue or leptospirosis, especially during ongoing YF epidemics in South American countries [3,5]. Early suspicion, prompt laboratory confirmation, and appropriate supportive care are critical to improving outcomes.

In conclusion, these two RT-PCR–confirmed cases from Nariño illustrate the diagnostic challenges and broad clinical spectrum of YF in Colombia, reinforcing its ability to mimic other endemic infections and the importance of early recognition in outbreak contexts. These findings underscore the critical role of integrated clinical, laboratory, and public health approaches in the early detection and control of yellow fever outbreaks in endemic regions.
