NiCo2O4/g-C3N4: an efficient heterogeneous photocatalyst for visible-light-driven tandem synthesis of 2,3-disubstituted quinolines

Venkatesan Narasimman, Gunabalan Madhumitha

Abstract

The development of sustainable photocatalytic platforms for heterocyclic scaffolds remains a central challenge in synthetic chemistry. In this work, we report the synthesis of a series of nanocomposites by loading 10, 20, and 30 wt% of nickel cobaltite (NiCo2O4) into graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) nanosheets. Among these nanocomposites, 20 wt% NiCo2O4-loaded g-C3N4 achieves a yield of 82% over five recyclability cycles in the synthesis of 2,3-disubstituted quinolines. The composite was engineered by anchoring spinel NiCo2O4 nanoparticles onto two-dimensional g-C3N4 nanosheets synthesized via a hydrothermal method. Comprehensive characterization by XRD, FT-IR spectroscopy, XPS, FE-SEM, HR-TEM, UV-vis DRS, and PL spectroscopy collectively confirmed the heterostructure formation. The objective is to evaluate the photocatalytic activity of these catalysts for synthesizing 2,3-disubstituted quinolines under 12 W blue LED light. Scavenging experiments confirm the formation of radicals under light irradiation and provide crucial mechanistic insights. The methodology demonstrates broad substrate scope, requires mild reaction conditions, and allows the use of an earth-abundant, eco-friendly catalyst, offering a sustainable alternative to conventional metal-catalysed quinoline syntheses.

Introduction

Quinolines have unique structural scaffolds, and they occur in natural products and pharmacologically important compounds. Several quinoline derivatives exhibit excellent biological activities, including antitubercular, antifungal, anticancer, and neurodegenerative properties. Additionally, many types of quinoline derivatives have been effectively positioned as necessary drugs, such as montelukast, hydroxychloroquine, and tafenoquine. Due to their importance, various synthetic procedures, such as Friedländersynthesis, Gould–Jacob synthesis, and Skraup synthesis, have been developed. While these reactions are generally reliable, most involve the use of pre-functionalized starting materials, which are frequently unstable and unavailable for purchase. Visible light represents the most abundant form of electromagnetic radiation in our natural environment, yet most reactant molecules cannot directly absorb it to initiate chemical reactions. Visible light-mediated reactions require photocatalytic materials to act as agents that transfer energy from light to the chemical substrates. Photocatalysts fall into two broad categories: homogeneous systems, such as ruthenium and iridium complexes along with organic dyes, and heterogeneous systems, which encompass semiconductor materials, plasmonic metal nanoparticles, and emerging photoelectric substances.

Semiconductor-based photocatalysis has experienced substantial growth over recent decades, offering practical solutions for both synthetic chemistry and environmental remediation. Researchers have explored numerous semiconductor materials, focusing on enhancing three key properties: light absorption capacity, charge carrier separation, and surface chemical reactivity.

Modern approaches have demonstrated that engineered nanostructures and composite materials can effectively address the limitations inherent to conventional semiconductors. These strategies not only boost catalytic performance but also broaden the scope of potential applications, ranging from organic synthesis to environmental pollutant removal. Furthermore, investigations into heterojunction architectures and chemical doping methods have provided valuable insights for designing photocatalysts that are both highly active and operationally stable. Collectively, these advancements underscore the potential of semiconductor photocatalysis for developing efficient, environmentally friendly catalytic technologies.

Due to their flexible layered structure and appropriate bandgap (2.7 eV) for visible-light absorption, g-C3N4 nanosheets have attracted a lot of attention. In addition, g-C3N4 has been employed as a major supporting matrix because of its high surface area, strong thermal stability and chemical durability, low cost, and nontoxic nature. Unfortunately, pristine and bulk g-C3N4 exhibits limited visible-light absorption and inefficient charge separation, which result in relatively low photocatalytic performance. Composites of g-C3N4 with additional semiconductor materials were created to increase the photocatalytic activity. NiCo2O4 has a spinel structure and the advantages of being inexpensive, eco-friendly, having good electrical conductivity, and showing rich redox chemistry, which qualify NiCo2O4 as a suitable component of composites with g-C3N4. NiCo2O4 has unique structural properties: Ni occupies octahedral positions, and Co occupies both octahedral and tetrahedral positions. The catalytic activity of NiCo2O4 can be improved by the formation of Co3p/Co2p and Ni3p/Ni2p during redox reactions. The interfacial charge transfer in the NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 heterostructure suppresses recombination and increases the lifetime of the photocarriers. These synergistic processes facilitate the activation of the carbonyl substrate and promote condensation and cyclization in the Friedländer annulation; moreover, because of this activity, NiCo2O4 was incorporated as the primary active component responsible for driving quinoline formation. As illustrated in , traditional approaches for the synthesis of 2,3-disubstituted quinolines have employed catalysts such as Nafion NR50, manganese oxide, and FeCl3, which typically require harsh acidic conditions and high temperatures, often resulting in longer reaction times and limited catalyst reusability. In contrast, the present work introduces a visible-light-driven NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 (20NC) heterojunction photocatalyst that enables the same transformation under mild conditions. This approach offers several advantages over previous methods, including high product yield, an eco-friendly aqueous-organic solvent system, and excellent catalyst reusability. Experimental

Materials

For more information, please refer to the SI document (Text S1).

Synthesis of the g-C3N4 nanosheets

The g-C3N4 synthesis was achieved using thermal polymerization. Initially, 20 g of urea was calcined at 550 °C for 120 min in a furnace under ambient air. After the crucible naturally cooled, the resulting material was thoroughly ground into a powder. The obtained yellow powder was again calcined in a furnace at 500 °C for 180 min to obtain g-C3N4 powder.

Synthesis of NiCo2O4

NiCo2O4 was prepared by a hydrothermal approach. Co(NO3)2·6H2O (2 mmol), Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (1 mmol) and urea (9 mmol) were dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water and stirred for 25 minutes to obtain a homogeneous solution. The mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, which was sealed and heated at 160 °C for 6 hours. After cooling to room temperature naturally, the precipitated precursor was collected, washed several times with deionized water using ultrasonic treatment to remove residual salts, and then dried. The dried material was finely ground and calcined in a furnace at 350 °C for 3 hours to yield the final NiCo2O4 powder, which was allowed to cool to room temperature before characterization.

Synthesis of the NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 composite

The NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 composite material synthesis is shown in . Initially, 500 mg of g-C3N4 and 50 mg of NiCo2O4 were mixed and calcined at 300 °C for 120 min in a tube furnace. The obtained 10% NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 powder was collected and denoted as 10NC. Similarly, NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 hybrids with NiCo2O4 loadings of 10, 20, and 30% were synthesized and denoted as 10NC, 20NC, and 30NC, respectively.

Characterization

For more information, please refer to the SI document (Text S2).

Synthesis of 2,3-disubstituted quinolines

To a magnetically stirred mixture of 2-aminobenzyl aldehyde (1 mmol) and 1,3 cyclohexanediones (1.1 mmol) solubilized in acetonitrile, water (1 : 1) (3 mL) and 20 mg of 20NC photocatalyst were added, and the reaction mixture was taken in a 15 mL borosilicate glass vial and irradiated using a 12 W blue LED lamp (450–495 nm) positioned at a distance of 3 cm for a total irradiation time of 4 h at room temperature. The crude products were then purified by column chromatography, employing a petroleum ether/ethyl acetate (PET : EA) mixture in a 9 : 1 (v/v) ratio as the eluent to obtain pure compounds.

Morphological studies

FESEM was used to examine the morphology of the pristine NiCo2O4 and composite 20NC catalyst. Pristine NiCo2O4 shows a well-defined urchin-like structure with uniformly dispersed features. These structures provide a larger and more effective heterojunction interface (). The NiCo2O4-anchored g-C3N4 composite (20NC) exhibits an irregular, layered flake-like structure (). NiCo2O4 particles are observed decorating the surface of the g-C3N4 nanosheets, consistent with the formation of a NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 heterostructure. The EDS spectrum in shows the uniform distribution of the elements, and confirms the presence of the following elements with weight percentages Co (5.4%), Ni (2.9%), C (31.8%), N (46.0%), O (14.0%). Moreover, elemental mapping () shows those elements are distributed across the sample, indicating good spatial correlation between the oxide particles and the carbon nitride matrix. Together, the morphological and compositional data support successful anchoring of NiCo2O4 onto the g-C3N4 nanosheets and the creation of an intimate interfacial contact that can facilitate charge transfer in photocatalytic applications.

HR-TEM micrograph analysis was also performed to analyse the morphological characteristics and heterojunction formation. and illustrate the strong contact between NiCo2O4 particles and the g-C3N4 nanosheets. illustrates the construction of a heterojunction between the g-C3N4 sheets and NiCo2O4 nanoparticles. The lattice fringes corresponding to NiCo2O4 (0.46 nm) indicate the existence of the (111) plane, which is seen to be directly anchored on the g-C3N4 surface. This close interfacial contact without any gap confirms the formation of a heterojunction rather than a simple physical mixture, and provides the necessary structural pathway for interfacial charge transfer, as shown in (). The nanocomposite SAED pattern is depicted in (); this pattern reveals that the nanocomposite possesses crystalline characteristics. The particle size histogram (), obtained from around 50 particles measured across the TEM micrographs, shows a narrow, near-Gaussian size distribution ranging from 6 to 20 nm, with most particles concentrated between 10 and 14 nm. The average particle size was determined to be 12 nm, indicating that a relatively uniform particle distribution formed during synthesis.

Optical studies

UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) was used to compare the optical properties of pristine NiCo2O4, g-C3N4, and composites containing 10, 20 and 30 wt% NiCo2O4 (10NC, 20NC, 30NC). As shown in , g-C3N4 exhibits an absorption edge near 450 nm, consistent with its limited visible-light response. In contrast, the 20NC composite shows excellent absorption at 380–455 nm, originating from g-C3N4, superimposed on the broad background absorption of NiCo2O4 that extends throughout the visible region. This strong absorption in the blue-light region overlaps well with the emission of the 12 W blue LED (450–460 nm), supporting its selection as the optimal irradiation source for this photocatalytic system. Band-gap energies were estimated from Tauc plots (). The derived Eg values are 1.33 eV for NiCo2O4, 2.73 eV for g-C3N4, and 1.95, 2.19, and 1.87 eV for 10NC, 20NC, and 30NC, respectively. These results indicate that incorporating NiCo2O4 narrows the effective optical band gap of the composites and enhances their visible-light absorption compared with bare g-C3N4.

Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy was used to investigate the electron–hole recombination rate; a high intensity was associated with a high recombination rate and low photocatalytic efficiency. As shown in , the emission peaks were obtained at 475 nm. g-C3N4 shows a highly intense fluorescence emission, whereas NiCo2O4 showed no PL due to rapid non-radiative relaxation pathways. After loading NiCo2O4, the emission intensity decreased. This quenching is indicative of efficient charge transfer across the NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 interface. The excited electrons from g-C3N4 are transferred to NiCo2O4, which confirms heterojunction-driven charge dynamics rather than physical coexistence.

Photocatalytic application of the NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 nanocomposite

The reaction was conducted using 2-aminobenzaldehyde (1 mmol) (1) and 1,3-cyclohexadione (1.1 mmol) (2), catalyzed by the 20NC nanocomposite under optimized conditions under blue light irradiation to obtain the product 3a (). The reaction was optimized by adjusting several parameters, including catalyst, light and solvent. Initially, the reaction was optimized by employing several different photocatalysts, including NiO, Co3O4, and g-C3N4, which gave moderate yields of approximately 36–55% (). The nanocomposite 20NC exhibited a notable yield of 82% within 4 h (, entry 7). The reaction was optimized using several solvent systems, including water (H2O), ethanol (EtOH), methanol (MeOH), dimethyl formamide (DMF), tetrahydrofuran (THF), and acetonitrile (ACN). The combination of acetonitrile and water (1 : 1) showed a higher yield than the other solvents (, entry 8). Moreover, the light source was optimized by conducting the experiment in the dark and under various colours of light. In the dark, a yield of less than 10% was obtained due to the mild intrinsic Lewis acid catalytic.

A substantially higher yield was obtained under 12 W blue light irradiation, which confirms that visible-light-induced charge carrier generation is the primary driving force for efficient product formation (). Various percentages of the NiCo2O4-doped catalysts were used to test the reaction, including 10NC, 20NC, and 30NC. Among all these catalysts, 20NC showed the highest yield (82%). Mechanistic pathway

The photocatalytic mechanism starts with light excitation, which generates electron–hole pairs (h+/e−), as shown in . The holes oxidize the β-dicarbonyl compound (a) into a radical intermediate (b). This radical reacts with 2-aminobenzaldehyde to form the intermediate moiety (c), which subsequently undergoes radical-mediated transformation to form (d). Dehydration of this intermediate leads to the formation of the imine-like structure (e), followed by an intramolecular nucleophilic attack by the amino group on the carbonyl carbon to close the ring. A final dehydration step (f) restores aromaticity to obtain the substituted quinoline derivative (g). This dual-catalyst system enhances efficiency by promoting charge separation, allowing the reaction to proceed under mild conditions.

Trapping study

Radical-trapping experiments prove the photocatalytic mechanism. AgNO3, triethanolamine (TEOA), and 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO) were employed to quench electrons, holes, and radical intermediates, respectively. Addition of AgNO3 or TEOA suppressed product formation, as monitored by TLC monitoring, indicating the involvement of both photogenerated electrons and holes. TEMPO trapped the radical derived from substrate 3b to give a TEMPO-3b adduct, which was confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (Fig. S52), with only trace amounts of product detected.

Reusability study

Reusability tests evaluated the stability of 20NC. The model reaction was repeated for five consecutive cycles using the recovered catalyst (). After the fifth run, the product yield fell to 75%, indicating a small loss of activity. The used sample was analysed by XRD and compared with fresh 20NC (). The main diffraction peaks of NiCo2O4 and g-C3N4 remain present after cycling, with only minor changes in peak intensity and slight broadening, suggesting preserved crystal phases.

Conclusion

The NiCo2O4/g-C3N4 heterojunction nanocomposite was successfully fabricated and demonstrated to be an efficient visible-light photocatalyst for the synthesis of 2,3-disubstituted quinoline derivatives. The optimized 20NC nanocomposite delivered a yield of 82% under 12 W blue LED irradiation within 4 hours. The reaction proceeded through a visible-light-driven condensation followed by a cyclization and aromatization cascade under mild conditions without requiring noble metals or harsh reagents. The earth-abundant nature, broad substrate scope, and operational simplicity of this photocatalytic system make a sustainable and practical platform for the construction of pharmacologically important nitrogen heterocycles.
