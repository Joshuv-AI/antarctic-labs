The Evolving Epidemiology of Invasive Pneumococcal Disease in Australian Children: A Multicentre Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Phoebe C M Williams, Annaleise Howard Jones, Robert Duguid, Linny K Phuong, Amanda Gwee, Niall Johnston, Coen Butters, Shahin Oftadeh, Sharon Chen, Archana Koirala, Nusrat Homaira, Rama Kandasamy, Adam W Bartlett, Adam Jaffe, Brendan McMullan, Pamela Palasanthiran, Priyali Wijeratne, Philip Britton, Parveen Fathima, Thomas N Snelling, Vitali Sintchenko, Sanjay Jayasinghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Australia, high pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) coverage has been achieved, yet invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) continues to cause a significant burden of disease. The epidemiology of IPD was altered by the nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) used to control the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic. We aimed to analyze this impact on the clinical characteristics associated with IPD in Australian children.MethodWe systematically evaluated the clinical and microbiological characteristics of IPD in children (<18 years) presenting to 4 Australian children's hospitals 2 years before (2017–2018: prepandemic) and after (2021–2022: postpandemic) the implementation of NPIs to mitigate COVID-19. Case counts and proportions were used to compare clinical presentations.ResultsWe identified 220 cases of IPD: 116 in 2017–2018 and 104 in 2021–2022. Causative serotypes were available for 176 cases (80%), with serotype 3 most frequently identified (26% of serotyped IPD, 46/176). One-quarter of cases were due to serotypes included in newer PCVs. Postpandemic cases had a higher proportion of meningitis (13.5% vs 3.4%, P = .001) and test-confirmed associated viral infections (63.8% vs 36.2%, P = .008) compared to prepandemic cases. Children with viral co-infections were significantly more likely to have prolonged fevers (>14 days; 13% vs 1%, P = .001).ConclusionsAlternations in the prevalence of respiratory viral transmission influence the epidemiology and clinical presentation of IPD. Newer PCVs and the implementation of immunization programs that target viral pathogens associated with IPD may reduce the ongoing burden of IPD in children.

Lower respiratory tract infections remain a leading cause of death in young children [1], and Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) is responsible for the greatest burden of bacterial pneumonia globally. Alongside pneumonia, pneumococcus can also cause meningitis or bloodstream infections—together known as invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) [2, 3]. Each year, more than 9 million IPD episodes occur globally, resulting in 318 000 deaths in children, despite long-standing recommendations by the World Health Organization (WHO) to fund pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) in childhood immunization programs [2, 3]. Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines have been available for use in children since 2001 [4], yet only two-thirds of the world's children have received the WHO-recommended 3-dose PCV schedule, and significant regional disparities in vaccine coverage are evident: from 26% in the Western Pacific region to 86% in the European region [5].

In Australia, a publicly funded 3-dose PCV program commenced for all children in 2005, which evolved to 10- (2009), 13- (2010) and then 20-v PCV vaccine (2025) programs as these newer vaccines were licensed, with consistently high coverage achieved [6]. Initially, Australia's PCV immunization program was delivered via a “3 + 0” dosing schedule (that is, administered at 2, 4, and 6 months old) and later altered to a “2 + 1” program in 2018 (at ages 2, 4, and 12 months old) to improve the duration of protection [4]. Comprehensive surveillance for IPD in Australia occurs via centralized laboratory-based surveillance systems, which includes identification of causative serotypes [7].

In 2020, following Australia's implementation of strict nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) to mitigate the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic (incorporating border closures, contract tracing, stay-at-home orders, and school closures), Australia's national surveillance system flagged a 50% reduction in IPD notifications in children [7], reflecting epidemiological shifts also observed in other countries [8–11]. Following the relaxation of these orders in October 2021, IPD cases surged to exceed pre-COVID-19 incidence; and by 2023, IPD notification rates in Australian children matched those evident before the rollout of 13vPCV (in 2011) [4, 10, 12].

This shift in the prevalence of IPD during and after the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic occurred concurrent to an almost complete cessation in global circulation of other respiratory pathogens, such as influenza [13, 14]. Following NPI relaxation in Australia, the IPD case surge corresponded with a resurgence of viral infections in children, postulated to be associated with disruption of seasonal trends and acquired immunity [15–17]. The interaction between Streptococcus pneumoniae and common childhood viral pathogens is well documented [18, 19]. Epidemiological and clinical evidence suggests that certain respiratory viruses (particularly influenza, respiratory syncytial virus [RSV], and human metapneumovirus) may predispose to IPD [20, 21]. With an evolving immunization landscape that includes licensing of newer PCVs and the availability of immunizations against viral pathogens (such as RSV), understanding the interplay and association of these pathogens in causing IPD in children is essential.

Although significant gains in reducing IPD have been made through the rollout of PCVs in Australia, the COVID-19 pandemic revealed that many of these gains are precarious. Breakthrough disease in vaccinated individuals, IPD caused by nonvaccine serotypes, and a persistent burden of pneumococcal meningitis necessitates the close monitoring of the clinical and epidemiological burden of IPD in children. Furthermore, serotype 3 disease—for which 13vPCV effectiveness is known to be suboptimal—continues to account for a high proportion of all childhood IPD cases in Australia [22, 23]. Surveillance of IPD cases to understand the potential role for newly licensed (15- and 20-valent) PCVs is therefore necessary to optimize immunization strategies and reduce this high-burden disease.

We have previously described the clinical and epidemiological characteristics of IPD in children presenting to 3 Australian children's hospitals following the relaxation of NPIs to mitigate the COVID-19 pandemic, revealing a significant morbidity and mortality burden [11]. To further explore these epidemiological changes, we established a multicenter research collaboration with 4 tertiary children's hospitals and pathology service providers in Australia's most populous states, New South Wales (NSW) and Victoria (VIC). Our aim was to describe the clinical and microbiological characteristics of IPD in children, and the role of viral co-infections on the clinical characteristics associated with specific pneumococcal serotypes, and how these may have been impacted by the implementation of NPIs utilized to control COVID-19.

METHODS

In Australia, laboratory-confirmed IPD has been nationally notifiable since 2001 and is defined as the detection of S. pneumoniae from a normally sterile site (such as blood, pleural fluid, or cerebrospinal fluid) either by culture or nucleic acid amplification testing. Specimens from all identified cases are serotyped in 1 of 4 national pneumococcal reference laboratories. In NSW and Victoria, the reference laboratories (NSW Health Pathology and Doherty Institute, respectively) serotype IPD-causing S. pneumoniae isolates identified at all hospitals across the states using standardized Quellung methods, with antisera supplied by Statens Serum Institute, Denmark. Molecular serotyping directly on sterile site samples is achieved by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting cpsB gene.

We systematically searched these laboratory databases to identify all patients managed at 4 tertiary children's hospitals in NSW (Children's Hospital at Westmead; Sydney Children's Hospital, Randwick; and John Hunter Hospital) and Victoria (The Royal Children's Hospital, Melbourne). We identified all children (aged < 18 years) presenting with IPD between 1 January 2017–31 December 2018 (the “prepandemic” cohort) and 1 January 2021–31 December 2022 (the “postpandemic” cohort). These years were selected around the peak pandemic period, when a sharp decline in IPD incidence (and viral transmission) occurred globally, to avoid including data that may have biased our analyses. Detailed demographic, clinical, and microbiological data were extracted for each IPD case from electronic medical records and entered into a central REDCap database. Children with IPD who presented with viral respiratory tract symptoms (such as coryza, rhinorrhea, or cough) within 2 weeks of their presentation were grouped as IPD cases “with associated viral infection(s).” Some of these cases also had PCR tests undertaken, which confirmed the presence of a viral pathogen, although this is not a routine investigation for children presenting with viral symptoms to Australian hospitals. The Australian Immunisation Register was reviewed to confirm immunization status.

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to compare pre- and postpandemic cohorts, and IPD cases presenting with and without associated viral infections, to evaluate for differences in disease presentations (including pneumonia, pneumococcal meningitis, bacteremia without a specific focus, and bone or joint infections) and severity (based on requirement for intensive care unit [ICU] admission, number of days with fever [>38.0°C], and peak C-reactive protein [CRP]). For those with associated viral infections, cases with no diagnostic test confirming their viral pathogen were excluded from the statistical tests of comparison (chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for proportions and ANOVA for means). Serotypes were analyzed individually and also grouped into categories, based on whether they are included in a licensed conjugate vaccine (13v-, 15v-, and 20v-PCV). In calculating case proportions by serotype categories, cases where the serotype was unknown were excluded. Statistical Product and Service Solutions statistics software version 22.0, was used for the analysis. The study was approved by the Sydney Children′s Hospital Network Human Research and Ethics Committee (HREC-ETH01567).

RESULTS

We analyzed 220 IPD cases in children over the 4-year study period (Table 1). This included 116 in the prepandemic cohort (2017–2018) and 104 in the postpandemic cohort (2021–2022). The highest proportion of IPD cases occurred in toddlers aged 1 to <2 years (23.2%, 51/220), and the mean age of IPD cases was marginally lower in the postpandemic cohort (3.61 years vs 4.01 years; Tables 1 and 2). Over one-third of IPD cases (38.6%, 85/220) were caused by serotypes included in 13vPCV (Table 1), although only 86.4% of children (190/220) had received 3 or more doses of 13vPCV (defined as “fully vaccinated”) at the time of their IPD presentation (Table 1). Of the 21 IPD cases that occurred in children who had not yet received 3 doses of PCV, serotype data were available for 9 cases, revealing that 5 were due to serotype 19F, 2 due to serotype 3, and a further 2 due to 6C.

The most common IPD presentation was pneumonia (48.1%, 106/220), which incorporated patients with S. pneumoniae detected on either pleural fluid or blood, accompanied by clinical and radiographic features of pulmonary consolidation. This was followed by bacteremia (28.2%, 62/220) and meningitis (8.2%, 18/220; Table 1). There were significantly more cases of meningitis in the postpandemic cohort (13.5% vs 3.4%, P = .001; Table 2). Overall, 53 children (24.1%) required admission to the ICU, and more children required ICU admission in the prepandemic cohort (29.3% vs 18.3%, P = .03). Six children died (2.7%).

Most children (117/220) experienced medical complications secondary to IPD (Table 1). In children with pneumonia, this most frequently included empyema (n = 57), including bronchopulmonary fistula in 9 of these patients. Other serious complications included sepsis, stroke, hemolytic uremic syndrome and sensorineural hearing loss. The duration of fever and peak CRP levels were similar for the pre- and postpandemic cohorts (Supplementary Tables 1–4). One-third of cases (74/220, 33%) had a known underlying risk factor for IPD at presentation (74/220), with proportions being similar across pre- and postpandemic cohorts. The most common underlying risk factor for IPD was a history of prematurity (n = 27), followed by those undergoing treatment for an underlying oncology diagnosis (n = 12). There were 3 IPD cases in children with nephrotic syndrome, while 2 children had inborn errors of immunity (RAG2 heterozygous mutation and common variable immunodeficiency) and 1 child had anatomical asplenia (Table 1). Of the remaining patients, 57 (of 146, 39%) were investigated for immunodeficiency following their IPD episode (assessment of lymphocyte subsets and immunoglobulin levels), of whom 3 (of 57, 5%) had an immunodeficiency identified.

Across both cohorts, one-quarter of all IPD cases (56/220, 25.5%) were diagnosed by molecular methods only. A serotype was identified in the majority (176/220), with serotype 3 responsible for the largest proportion (26%, 46/176) of IPD cases. Excluding serotype 3, the proportion of IPD caused by other 13vPCV serotypes was higher in the postpandemic cohort (27.7% vs 17.2%, P = .068; Table 2). One-quarter of IPD cases (25.5%, 45/176) were caused by 7 serotypes included in 20vPCV, but not 13vPCV (serotypes 8, 10A, 11A, 12F, 15B, 22F, and 33F). Pneumococcal meningitis was most frequently caused by serotype 23B (n = 8), followed by 19F (n = 6). The serotype distribution causative of IPD was broadly similar between the pre- and postpandemic cohorts, aside from a notable decline in the proportion of IPD infections caused by serotype 3 in the postpandemic cohort (Figure 1). Most IPD episodes occurred over the winter months (June to August). The proportion of IPD cases with symptoms of viral co-infection was largely consistent across all months of the year (Supplementary Figure 1).

Over half of all IPD cases (120/220, 54.5%) had symptoms consistent with an associated viral co-infection (antecedent or concurrent). Of these, 57.5% (69/120) had a positive PCR-based test confirming their viral infection, with rhinovirus (n = 26), RSV (n = 21), enterovirus (n = 11), and parainfluenza (n = 10) most frequently identified (Table 2; Supplementary Table 5). Across all cases, the median CRP was 152 mg/L [interquartile range (IQR)] 52–266]; and the median peak temperature was 39.2°C (IQR 38.4°C–39.9°C). The highest fever (42.4°C) and CRP (400 mg/L) recorded were in a child with serotype 3 pneumonia complicated by empyema. Children with serotype 3 IPD were more likely than those with non-serotype 3 IPD to have a prolonged fever, with more than half of all children with serotype 3 disease experiencing fever >38.0°C for more than 1 week (56% for whom clinical data was available, 23/41) and 12% experienced fevers for more than 2 weeks (5/41; Supplementary Table 1). Children with serotype 3 disease also had a higher peak CRP, with 86% (36/42 for whom laboratory results were available) having a peak CRP > 100 mg/L (Supplementary Table 2). By contrast, children with IPD due to serotype 19F were more likely to have a lower CRP (10–100 mg/L; Supplementary Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This multicenter study evaluating IPD before and after the implementation of NPIs to control COVID-19 identifies the changing epidemiology of IPD (and serotypes causative of disease) in Australian children and the important role of viral co-infection in contributing to more severe disease. We also identified that children with serotype 3 disease were more likely to have a more severe clinical presentation.

Less than half the children in our cohort had their associated viral infection confirmed with molecular-based diagnostics, as this is not a routinely requested test in children presenting to hospital in Australia. However, this was more frequently performed in the postpandemic cohort, due to clinician (and infection control staff) preference to identify causative viruses responsible for symptoms in children. Children with IPD who presented with associated viral infections had higher inflammatory markers and were more likely to require a longer hospital admission compared to those without associated viral infections; and in the cohort of children in whom a virus was confirmed on molecular diagnostics, a younger age at presentation and a significantly longer duration of fever (>14 days) were evident (compared to children with IPD who did not have an accompanying viral illness).

Viral co-infections in our cohort commonly occurred in children with IPD presenting as pneumonia. This is congruent with other studies showing that the seasonality of pneumococcal pneumonia mirrors the seasonality of viral respiratory tract infections [24]. The interplay between pneumococcus and viral pathogens, and the synergistic morbidity and mortality they may cause, has been evident since the 1915 influenza pandemic, as viruses modulate the immune response and impair pulmonary mucociliary clearance [25]. Concurrently, S. pneumoniae can promote transmission of viral pathogens [26].

By contrast, other manifestations of IPD (such as meningitis) are less likely to be associated with viral co-infection [24] and may instead be related to colonization with specific pneumococcal serotypes in the nasopharynx that predispose to IPD [27]. While there are no published data pertaining to serotype colonization of children in Australia during the pandemic period, we hypothesize that alteration in colonization serotypes within the country has reflected the changing prevalence of serotypes causative of IPD in children in the postpandemic era and may have contributed to the rise in pneumococcal meningitis evident in the postpandemic cohort.

The polysaccharide capsule of S pneumoniae is a major virulence factor that serves as the target for current vaccine strategies, with more than 100 different capsular serotypes described [28]. Our study identified the high prevalence of serotype 3 IPD, aligning with other studies conducted in Australia [29]. Although there was a substantial decline in the prevalence of serotype 3 disease in the postpandemic cohort, it continued to be the most frequently identified serotype causative of IPD, which may also reflect changes in the colonizing pneumococcal serotypes in Australian children during the pandemic. Studies conducted in Israel [8] and France [9] during the pandemic revealed no change in the proportion of children colonized with pneumococcus during the pandemic (despite the sharp decline in IPD incidence), with a persistent colonization burden with serotypes 24F, 10A, 23B 15B/C, and 11A evident, yet there were low rates of colonization with serotype 3 [8, 9]. Our findings support this observation, indicating that postpandemic viral co-infections, rather than alterations in pneumococcal colonization during and after the period of NPIs, played an important role in the clinical presentation of IPD seen in the postpandemic period.

It is not yet clear if newer (15- and 20-valent) PCVs will provide improved clinical protection against serotype 3 IPD, as limited clinical outcome data for these vaccines are currently available, which were not recommended (or funded) for Australian children over our study period. However, it is hoped that the 2025 alteration to Australia's immunization schedule (utilizing 20vPCV as the recommended, and funded, pneumococcal immunization) may result in a decline in the morbidity and mortality caused by serotype 3 [28, 30]. Furthermore, the increasing availability of immunizations against many of the viruses evident in IPD co-infection may also reduce this clinical impact.

The respiratory viruses most frequently associated with IPD in our cohort included RSV, influenza, rhinovirus, and human metapneumovirus, consistent with the international literature [19, 24, 31]. Many of these viruses typically share seasonality with IPD, and improving immunization coverage—both by enabling access to vaccines and by enhancing vaccine confidence to improve uptake—could significantly reduce the burden of IPD. Seasonal influenza vaccine coverage is low for Australian children [32], and optimizing coverage with the Abryvso® vaccine (licensed for use in pregnant women) and nirsevimab (licensed to prevent RSV in infants) should be a priority [33, 34]. However, from a global perspective, while vaccine inequity persists and many countries fail to have affordable access to influenza, RSV, and even PCV immunization programs, the opportunity to reduce the overall childhood IPD burden remains limited—particularly in countries where mortality in childhood pneumonia is greatest [34].

Our research has several limitations, including its retrospective design and reliance on electronic medical records for data acquisition, which resulted in some missing data. Furthermore, not all episodes of IPD could be serotyped, and our analysis is limited to 2 states in Australia—although these are the most populous states, with the highest absolute number of IPD cases occurring in children. Finally, our comparisons are based on case counts and proportions (rather than disease rates), as an accurate denominator population is difficult to determine; and the limited number of cases included in our analysis restricted the capacity to conduct multivariable analysis. However, the catchment population of the 4 tertiary hospitals included in our study are likely to have remained stable across our study period, and we aim to address the aforementioned limitations by commencing prospective surveillance across a prolonged time period to closely evaluate the impact of important public health interventions (such as vaccine changes) on IPD.

Despite these limitations, our study provides unique and granular insights to a cohort of 220 children with IPD before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. These data have revealed the propensity for IPD to cause a prolonged fever and a significant inflammatory response in children, particularly when this is associated with viral co-infection, and the important interplay of viral co-infections with IPD, particularly for children with pneumonia. However, our data suggest that viral co-infection is less important in the pathogenesis of pneumococcal meningitis, consistent with prior published literature [24]. These important findings can assist clinicians with understanding the natural history of IPD in children.

Future research should focus on prospective surveillance that incorporates enhanced microbiological data for completeness, such as the interplay of IPD causative serotypes with antibiotic resistance and genomic analysis, alongside surveillance of serotypes associated with colonization versus invasive infections. Prospective surveillance is particularly important in countries where changes to immunization programs are likely to influence IPD prevalence, either due to schedule changes (including those described in Australia) [4], the implementation of newer conjugate vaccines, or the rollout of vaccination programs against important viral pathogens. While strong 10vPCV and 13vPCV programs have reduced IPD in children in high-income countries, the potential to reduce the ongoing significant burden of this disease is evident, as new vaccines against both pneumococcus and the viral co-infections that may synergistically cause severe IPD are increasingly available. The inclusion of 15vPCV and 20vPCV into existing pediatric immunization programs in Australia and other high-income healthcare settings may help reduce the burden of IPD in children. Concurrently, improving PCV coverage and identifying and licensing new pneumococcal vaccine technologies (with improved protection against more pathogenic and vaccine-evasive serotypes) are necessary. This will ensure sustainable and equitable progress is made to reduce the global burden of IPD in children.
