Multivariate Lattice Deformation: A Spatially Explicit Framework for Assessing Crop Rotation Impacts on Soil Nutrient Dynamics
Marco Mandap

Abstract
Crop rotation is a cornerstone of sustainable agriculture, yet its impact on soil nutrient status is typically assessed using field-averaged nutrient budgets or single-nutrient analyses that ignore spatial heterogeneity and multivariate interactions. Here we propose a novel multivariate lattice model that treats agricultural soil as a 4D tensor with dimensions of space (two horizontal dimensions), time (rotation cycles), and nutrient channels (N, P, K). Crop rotations are represented as sequences of multivariate force vectors, with each crop exerting simultaneous demands on all three nutrients. The soil’s resistance to change, its buffering capacity or ”stiffness”, varies spatially with texture and mineralogy, modulating the local impact of crop forces. Lateral nutrient movement through diffusion and tillage mixing is introduced via kernel smoothing, generating realistic spatial autocorrelation. The cumulative impact of rotation is quantified through a multivariate stress metric, the Euclidean distance in N-P-K space between current and initial states, and statistical significance is assessed using the non-parametric Cramér–von Mises test with permutation. We simulate a three-year corn-soybean-wheat rotation on a 20 × 20 grid with heterogeneous soil textures. Results show that after one cycle, mean multivariate stress reaches 0.63 (on a normalized scale), with maximum stress of 0.91 in low-buffering sandy areas. Phosphorus depletion (17.9%) exceeds nitrogen depletion (10.8%), and phosphorus dominates stress in 19% of cells, a finding obscured by single-nutrient analyses. Continuous corn increases mean stress by 41% compared to the diverse rotation. The Cramér–von Mises test detects significant deviation from initial conditions for all nutrients ( p ≤ 0.002 p\leq 0.002 ), and Moran’s I I values (0.29–0.30, p < 0.001 p<0.001 ) confirm realistic spatial autocorrelation. Sensitivity analyses reveal that reducing soil buffering contrast homogenizes stress across the landscape, while increasing crop removal forces amplifies stress in already‑vulnerable zones. Our framework provides a predictive tool for identifying spatial risk zones, optimizing rotation sequences for multivariate soil health, and targeting site‑specific management interventions. This approach bridges geostatistical descriptions of soil variability with mechanistic representations of crop effects, offering both theoretical insight and practical guidance for sustainable nutrient management.

Introduction

1.1
The Challenge of Sustainable Nutrient Management

Global agriculture stands at a critical juncture. The twin imperatives of feeding a population projected to reach nearly 10 billion by 2050 while reducing the environmental footprint of food production represent one of the most formidable challenges of the twenty-first century

Nowhere are these challenges more acute than in the management of soil fertility. Soil provides the foundation for 95% of global food production, yet evidence of soil degradation accumulates worldwide.

The problem is compounded by the spatial heterogeneity of soil properties: nutrients are not uniformly distributed across fields, but exhibit complex patterns at scales ranging from meters to kilometers

1.2
Crop Rotation as a Sustainability Strategy

Crop rotation has been recognized for millennia as a practice that maintains soil productivity, and modern research has quantified its multiple benefits. Rotations can improve nutrient use efficiency, suppress pests and diseases, enhance soil structure, and increase system resilience to climate variability

However, the assessment of rotation effects on soil fertility has largely relied on field-scale nutrient budgets or single-nutrient analyses. A typical rotation study might report changes in soil test N, P, and K over time, but treat each nutrient independently and assume spatial uniformity across the experimental area

1.3
The Multivariate Nature of Crop Nutrient Demand

Crops do not require nitrogen in isolation. Optimal growth depends on balanced supplies of multiple nutrients, with deficiencies in any one element limiting yield and quality
(
15
)
. The concept of nutrient use efficiency (NUE) has evolved to recognize this multivariate reality:
59

30

Yet the representation of crops in most simulation models remains stubbornly univariate. Crop parameters typically specify N demand, with P and K treated as secondary or ignored entirely
(
14
;
18
)
. When P and K are included, they are often modeled independently, missing the multivariate nature of crop uptake and the competitive interactions among nutrients for plant physiological processes
(
34

. This reductionism limits our ability to assess rotation sustainability comprehensively: a rotation that appears balanced from an N perspective may be systematically depleting P or K, with consequences that only emerge after multiple cycles
(
35
)

1.4
Spatial Heterogeneity: The Missing Dimension

A parallel limitation affects the spatial dimension of rotation assessment. The geostatistical literature provides abundant evidence that soil nutrients exhibit strong spatial structure at scales relevant to farm management.
31
documented strong spatial autocorrelation for total N and P in a hilly watershed, with patterns following topography and vegetation.
13
reported moderate spatial dependence for TN, TP, and TK across the Sichuan Basin, with parent material, land use, and topography explaining varying proportions of variability.
54
mapped nutrient distributions in Pearl River Delta farmland, revealing consistent spatial gradients.
48
found moderate spatial variability across the Loess Plateau, with complex patterns influenced by multiple factors.

Management itself creates and modifies spatial patterns. The distinction between homefields and outfields in smallholder systems produces systematic fertility gradients
(
49
)
. Long-term fertilization creates zones of enrichment and depletion that reflect historical application patterns
(
29
)
. Soil redistribution by erosion and tillage transports nutrients across landscapes, creating depositional areas with enhanced fertility and eroded areas with diminished capacity
(
60
)
.

Despite this well-documented heterogeneity, rotation experiments are typically established on plots selected for uniformity, and results are extrapolated to fields as if conditions were homogeneous.
28
provided a striking demonstration of the problem: maize yields and nutrient uptake were strongly affected by soil organic carbon content across sites with different fertility status, with degraded soils responding poorly to inputs that produced substantial gains on more fertile soils. The implication is clear: rotation recommendations developed on uniform experimental plots may fail when applied to heterogeneous farmers’ fields, where soil conditions vary substantially within short distances.

1.5
Buffering Capacity as Spatially Variable Resistance

The concept of soil buffering capacity, the resistance of available nutrient pools to change, provides a theoretical bridge between inherent soil properties and management impacts. Soils with high clay content and 2:1 mineralogy exhibit strong buffering for potassium, fixing added K against rapid leaching but also resisting depletion by crops
(
23
;
22
;
2
)
. Sandy soils with low cation exchange capacity have minimal buffering, allowing rapid changes in available nutrients in response to inputs or removal
(
38
)
. Phosphorus buffering operates through different mechanisms, sorption to iron and aluminum oxides in weathered soils, precipitation with calcium in calcareous systems, but the principle of resistance to change applies equally
(
20
;
16
)
.

This buffering capacity varies spatially with the same factors that control nutrient distributions: parent material, texture, mineralogy, and organic matter content
(
41
;
9
)
. A complete model of rotation impacts must therefore account not only for spatial variation in initial nutrient status, but also for spatial variation in the resistance of those nutrients to change. The two interact: a given crop removal will have larger effects on available nutrients in low-buffering sandy soils than in high-buffering clays, potentially creating spatial patterns of depletion that reflect both initial conditions and differential responsiveness.

1.6
Modeling Approaches: Progress and Persistent Gaps

Process-based models have advanced substantially in their ability to simulate crop growth, nutrient cycling, and soil organic matter dynamics. DSSAT, DNDC, RothC, and similar models have been successfully applied to diverse cropping systems worldwide
(
47
;
61
;
21
)
. These models incorporate multiple nutrients and can simulate rotations over decades, providing valuable insights into long-term sustainability.

However, as
18
documented in their comprehensive review, ”within-field soil heterogeneities are rarely considered in field or landscape design studies.” Most applications assume spatially uniform soil properties, or at best distinguish a few broad classes. The models themselves are capable of spatial simulation, but the data required for parameterization, high-resolution maps of soil properties across the root zone, are rarely available, and the computational demands of spatially explicit simulation have historically been prohibitive.

14
reviewed models for annual crop mixtures and identified a similar gap: ”The crop mixture spatio-temporal heterogeneity determines the choice of the modeling approach,” yet most models simplify this heterogeneity to make simulation tractable. They distinguished between crop-centered models (addressing management and rotation integration) and plant-centered individual-based models (identifying traits and quantifying ecological processes), but noted that neither approach fully captures the multivariate, spatially explicit dynamics of real agricultural systems.

12
provided guidelines for spatio-temporal consistency in water quality modeling, emphasizing that temporal dynamics and spatial patterns must be considered jointly. Their principles apply equally to soil nutrient modeling: the scales at which processes operate must be respected, and models must maintain consistency across both space and time.

Precision Agriculture: The Data Opportunity

Parallel advances in precision agriculture technologies are creating new opportunities for spatially explicit nutrient management. Remote sensing from satellites and unmanned aerial vehicles provides high-resolution imagery that can be related to crop nutrient status and soil properties

These technologies have already enabled the delineation of management zones, subfield regions with relatively homogeneous soil properties and productivity potential. Studies by

What remains lacking is a modeling framework that can use this high-resolution spatial data to assess the sustainability of crop rotations

A predictive capability, the ability to simulate how alternative rotations will reshape spatial patterns of nutrient availability over multiple cycles, would enable proactive management, identifying potential problems before they develop.

1.8
The Deformation Analogy: A Novel Conceptual Framework

This study proposes a novel conceptual framework that draws an analogy between soil nutrient dynamics under crop rotation and the deformation of a physical lattice under applied forces. In this framework:

•

The soil is represented as a
4D tensor
with dimensions of space (two horizontal dimensions), time (rotation cycles), and nutrients (N, P, K channels)

•

Each crop in the rotation exerts a
force vector
representing its simultaneous demands for N, P, and K, drawing from the lattice at the points where it grows

•

The soil’s resistance to change, its
buffering capacity
or ”stiffness”, varies spatially with texture, mineralogy, and organic matter content, determining how much the lattice deforms in response to applied forces

•

Lateral movement of nutrients through diffusion, mass flow, and tillage mixing is represented as
spatial smoothing
that redistributes deformation across neighboring lattice points

•

The cumulative impact of the rotation is quantified as a
stress map
, the Euclidean distance in N-P-K space between current and initial states, identifying regions where multivariate depletion approaches critical thresholds

This framework directly addresses the gaps identified above: it is inherently multivariate, representing crops as simultaneous N-P-K forces; it is spatially explicit, capturing both initial heterogeneity and management-induced patterns; it incorporates buffering capacity as a spatially varying property; and it provides a quantitative stress metric that integrates across nutrient dimensions to assess overall system condition.

1.10
Contribution and Significance

This study makes several contributions to the literature on sustainable nutrient management:

Conceptual contribution
: The lattice deformation analogy provides a novel way of thinking about crop rotation impacts, emphasizing the multivariate and spatially explicit nature of nutrient dynamics and providing intuitive visualizations of system stress.

Methodological contribution
: The integration of spatial smoothing (kernel convolution) with multivariate state variables and crop force vectors creates a modeling framework that bridges geostatistical descriptions of soil variability with mechanistic representations of crop effects.

Practical contribution
: By identifying locations where multivariate stress approaches critical thresholds, the model can guide site-specific management interventions, targeting variable rate applications to the precise areas and nutrients where they are most needed.

Theoretical contribution
: The application of the Cramér–von Mises statistic to rotation assessment provides a formal hypothesis test for whether a rotation sequence is ”statistically stable” or likely to lead to system degradation, a quantitative foundation for sustainability assessment.

In an era of increasing pressure on agricultural systems and growing awareness of the limits to conventional management approaches, frameworks that capture the full complexity of soil-crop interactions while remaining computationally tractable are urgently needed. The multivariate lattice model proposed here represents a step toward such a framework, offering both theoretical insight and practical guidance for the design of sustainable crop rotations.

Methods

2.1
Overview of the Modeling Framework

We develop a spatially explicit, multivariate model that represents agricultural soil as a four-dimensional tensor with dimensions of space (two horizontal dimensions), time (rotation cycles), and nutrient channels (N, P, K). The model conceptualizes crop rotation as a sequence of multivariate “forces” applied to this lattice, with the soil’s resistance to change (buffering capacity) varying spatially as a function of inherent soil properties. Lateral nutrient movement through diffusion, mass flow, and tillage mixing is represented via kernel smoothing, introducing spatial autocorrelation consistent with empirical observations. The cumulative impact of rotation is quantified through a multivariate stress metric, the Euclidean distance in N-P-K space between current and initial states, and statistical significance is assessed using the Cramér–von Mises test.

This framework builds directly on the literature reviewed above, incorporating:

•

Empirical ranges of spatial variability and autocorrelation
(
31
;
13
)

•

Crop nutrient removal coefficients from meta-analyses
(
30
;
59
)

•

Soil buffering capacities as functions of texture and mineralogy
(
41
;
2
)

•

Spatial smoothing scales informed by geostatistical studies
(
33
;
49
)

2.2
Lattice State Representation

2.2.1
State Variables

Let the agricultural field be discretized into a regular grid of dimensions
X\times Y
grid cells, representing spatial locations at a resolution sufficient to capture management-relevant variability (typically 10–50 m, depending on field size and available data). The simulation spans
T
rotation cycles (years), with each cycle corresponding to one growing season.

The state of the system is represented by a 4D tensor:

\bm{S}(x,y,t,c)

(1)

where:

•

x\in\{1,2,\ldots,X\}
and
y\in\{1,2,\ldots,Y\}
are spatial coordinates

•

t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
is time (year), with
t=0
representing the initial state

•

c\in\{\text{N},\text{P},\text{K}\}
indexes the three nutrient channels

The units of
\bm{S}
are normalized concentrations relative to an initial reference state. For the baseline simulation, we set initial concentrations to unity for all nutrients at all locations:

\bm{S}(x,y,0,c)=1.0\quad\forall x,y,c

(2)

This normalization simplifies interpretation: values
<1
indicate depletion relative to initial conditions, values
>1
indicate enrichment, and the deviation from unity represents proportional change in available nutrient pools.

2.2.2
Initial Spatial Heterogeneity

While the baseline simulation assumes uniform initial conditions to isolate rotation effects, the framework can accommodate empirical heterogeneity by initializing the lattice with measured or simulated spatial patterns. Following the geostatistical literature
(
31
;
13
)
, initial nutrient surfaces can be generated as:

\bm{S}(x,y,0,c)=\max\left(\epsilon_{\min},\mu_{c}+\epsilon_{c}(x,y)+\eta_{c}(x,y)\right)

(3)

where:

•

\mu_{c}
is the mean concentration for nutrient
c

•

\epsilon_{c}(x,y)
represents spatially structured variation with autocorrelation range
r_{c}
and sill
\sigma_{c,\text{spatial}}^{2}

•

\eta_{c}(x,y)
represents unstructured (nugget) variation with variance
\sigma_{c,\text{nugget}}^{2}

•

\epsilon_{\min}=0.01
(1% of reference concentration) represents a minimum detectable threshold, ensuring physical plausibility

Typical parameters from the literature
(
31
;
13
)
include nugget-to-sill ratios of 0.25–0.50 for most nutrients, indicating moderate spatial structure, and autocorrelation ranges of 50–500 m depending on topography and management.

For nutrients typically following log-normal distributions
(
39
;
56
)
, we generate initial conditions as:

\bm{S}(x,y,0,c)=\exp\left(\mu_{\ln,c}+\epsilon_{\ln,c}(x,y)+\eta_{\ln,c}(x,y)\right)

(4)

where
\mu_{\ln,c}
and spatial parameters are estimated from log-transformed data. This log-normal approach ensures strictly positive concentrations and better represents the right-skewed distributions commonly observed for soil nutrients.

2.3
Crop Force Vectors

2.3.1
Definition of Crop Forces

Each crop
i

\bm{F}_{i}=[f_{i,\text{N}},f_{i,\text{P}},f_{i,\text{K}}]

(5)

where
f_{i,c}
is the proportional change in available nutrient
c
resulting from growing crop
i
for one season. Negative values indicate net removal (depletion), positive values indicate net addition (enrichment through fixation or deep rooting).

2.3.3
Application of Crop Forces

At each time step
t
(year), the crop grown in that year exerts its force on the current lattice state. The force is applied multiplicatively:

\bm{S}^{\prime}(x,y,t,c)=\bm{S}(x,y,t-1,c)\times(1+f_{i,c}\cdot\alpha(x,y))

(6)

where:

•

\bm{S}(x,y,t-1,c)
is the nutrient concentration at the end of the previous year (after smoothing)

•

f_{i,c}
is the crop force for nutrient
c

•

\alpha(x,y)
is a local
stiffness factor
that modulates the effective force based on soil buffering capacity (see Section
2.4
)

This multiplicative formulation embodies
first-order kinetic response

(
3
;
10
)
, where the rate of nutrient change is proportional to the current available concentration. This is consistent with plant uptake kinetics under non-limiting conditions (Michaelis-Menten dynamics approximated linearly at moderate concentrations) and with mineralization/immobilization fluxes that scale with substrate availability. The formulation ensures concentrations remain strictly positive, as
(1+f_{i,c}\cdot\alpha)>0
for all parameter values considered (minimum
1-0.8\times 1.0=0.2
in our simulations).

2.4
Soil Buffering Capacity as Spatially Variable Stiffness

2.4.1
Conceptual Basis

The concept of “lattice stiffness” represents the soil’s resistance to changes in available nutrient concentrations. Soils with high buffering capacity (clayey textures, high organic matter, 2:1 clay mineralogy) require larger gross nutrient removals to produce a given change in available pools, while low-buffering soils (sandy textures, low CEC) respond more dramatically to the same removal
(
41
;
2
)
.

2.4.2
Stiffness Factor Formulation

We define a stiffness factor
\alpha(x,y)\in[0,1]
that scales the effective crop force at each location:

\alpha(x,y)=\frac{1}{1+\beta\cdot B(x,y)}

(7)

where:

•

B(x,y)
is a buffering capacity index (dimensionless, normalized to
[0,1]
)

•

\beta
is a scaling parameter controlling the strength of buffering effects

For the baseline simulation, we implement a simplified texture-based stiffness map:

\alpha(x,y)=\begin{cases}1.0&\text{sandy texture (low buffering)}\\
0.5&\text{loamy texture (moderate buffering)}\\
0.2&\text{clayey texture (high buffering)}\end{cases}

(8)

This range (0.2–1.0) reflects empirical observations that clay soils can buffer 2–5 times more nutrient removal than sandy soils before showing equivalent changes in available pools
(
23
;
2
)
.

2.4.3
Advanced Buffering Parameterization

For applications with soil survey data, the buffering index can be parameterized more mechanistically. For potassium, following
2
, buffering capacity depends on clay content and smectite:illite ratio:

B_{K}(x,y)=w_{1}\cdot\text{clay}(x,y)+w_{2}\cdot\text{smectite:illite}(x,y)+w_{3}\cdot\text{CEC}(x,y)

(9)

For phosphorus, following
20
and
16
, buffering depends on clay content, iron/aluminum oxide content, and calcium carbonate equivalent in calcareous soils.

2.5
Spatial Smoothing (Kernel Convolution)

2.5.1
Rationale

Empirical studies consistently demonstrate spatial autocorrelation in soil nutrients: locations near each other are more similar than distant locations
(
31
;
13
;
49
)
. This spatial dependence arises from multiple processes: lateral nutrient movement through diffusion and mass flow, tillage mixing, uniform management applications, and the spatial continuity of underlying soil-forming factors.

To introduce realistic spatial dependence into the model, we apply a Gaussian kernel convolution to each nutrient layer after each annual crop force application. This smoothing represents the redistribution of nutrients through lateral processes and ensures that depletion or enrichment at one location influences neighboring cells.

2.5.2
Mathematical Formulation

After applying crop forces to obtain
\bm{S}^{\prime}(x,y,t,c)
, we compute the smoothed state
\bm{S}(x,y,t,c)
as:

\bm{S}^{\prime}(x,y,t,c)=\bm{S}(x,y,t-1,c)\times(1+f_{i,c}\cdot\alpha(x,y))

(10)

where
K
is a Gaussian kernel:

K(d_{x},d_{y};\sigma)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{x}^{2}+d_{y}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

(11)

with
d_{x}=x-u
,
d_{y}=y-v
, and
\sigma
is the kernel bandwidth (smoothing parameter) in grid cell units.

The kernel is normalized such that
\sum_{u}\sum_{v}K=1
, ensuring conservation of total nutrients (the smoothing redistributes but does not create or destroy nutrients).

2.5.3
Parameterization of Smoothing Scale

The smoothing parameter
\sigma
determines the spatial scale over which nutrients are mixed. Geostatistical studies report autocorrelation ranges for soil nutrients typically between 50 and 500 m, depending on the nutrient and landscape context
(
31
;
13
;
48
)
. For a grid with cell size
\Delta
, the kernel bandwidth in physical units is
\sigma_{\text{physical}}=\sigma\cdot\Delta
.

For the baseline simulation with
\Delta=10
m grid cells, we set
\sigma=1.2
grid cells, corresponding to a physical smoothing scale of approximately 12 m. This value is consistent with the “local dependence” documented by
33
and represents relatively local mixing (within-field variation) rather than landscape-scale transport. Sensitivity analyses vary
\sigma
from 0.5 to 5.0 grid cells to assess the impact of smoothing intensity.

2.5.4
Boundary Conditions

At grid boundaries, the kernel is truncated and renormalized such that
\sum_{u,v}K_{\text{trunc}}(x-u,y-v)=1
. This
truncated boundary condition
assumes that lateral fluxes across field boundaries are negligible compared to internal redistribution, a reasonable approximation for nutrients with limited mobility and for fields isolated by roads, ditches, or other barriers.

An alternative is
reflective boundary conditions
, where the kernel is reflected at edges to simulate fluxes bouncing back as if adjacent unmodeled areas had identical properties. Reflective conditions are appropriate when the field is embedded within a larger landscape with similar characteristics. For our single-field simulations, truncation provides a conservative estimate of edge effects by limiting cross-boundary influence, while reflective conditions would be more suitable for landscape-scale applications
(
12
)
. Sensitivity analyses vary smoothing scale to assess the practical impact of boundary treatments.

2.6
Multivariate Stress Metric

2.6.1
Definition of Deformation Energy

The cumulative impact of the rotation on soil nutrient status is quantified by a multivariate stress metric, the Euclidean distance in N-P-K space between the current state and the initial reference state. For each grid cell
(x,y)
at time
t
, we define:

D(x,y,t)=\sqrt{\sum_{c\in\{\text{N},\text{P},\text{K}\}}\left(\bm{S}(x,y,t,c)-\bm{S}(x,y,0,c)\right)^{2}}

(12)

This metric has several desirable properties:

•

It integrates deviations across all three nutrients into a single scalar

•

Large deviations in any nutrient contribute to stress

•

Simultaneous deviations in multiple nutrients increase stress quadratically

•

The metric is zero when the cell has returned exactly to its initial state

2.6.2
Interpretation

The stress map
D(x,y,t)
visualizes spatial patterns of cumulative nutrient change. High
D
values indicate locations where the multivariate nutrient state has deviated substantially from initial conditions, potential “mesh collapse” zones where the soil may be at risk of degradation.

For the normalized initial condition (
\bm{S}(x,y,0,c)=1
), a stress value of
D=0.5
could arise from, for example, a 50% depletion in N alone, or simultaneous 35% depletions in N and P, or various combinations.

2.6.3
Threshold Identification

Critical stress thresholds can be identified by linking
D
values to agronomic outcomes. For instance, if yield reductions become significant when any nutrient falls below 0.7 of initial (30% depletion), then the corresponding stress threshold is:

D_{\text{critical}}=\sqrt{(0.3)^{2}}=0.3

(13)

for single-nutrient depletion, or larger values for multi-nutrient depletion. More sophisticated thresholds can incorporate nutrient-specific critical limits from the literature
(
15
)
.

2.7
Statistical Significance Testing: Cramér–von Mises Test

2.7.1
Rationale

To determine whether observed nutrient changes represent systematic degradation rather than random variation, we apply the Cramér–von Mises (CvM) test, a non-parametric test for comparing two probability distributions. The test evaluates whether the spatial distribution of nutrients at time
t
differs significantly from the initial distribution.

2.7.2
Test Statistic

For a given nutrient
c
, let
F_{0}(z)
be the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) of initial concentrations
\bm{S}(x,y,0,c)
across all grid cells, and
F_{t}(z)
be the ECDF at time
t
. The CvM statistic is:

\omega_{c}^{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[F_{t}(z)-F_{0}(z)\right]^{2}dF_{0}(z)

(14)

For discrete grid cells with
n=X\times Y
locations, the computational form is:

\omega_{c}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[F_{t}(s_{i,0})-F_{0}(s_{i,0})\right]^{2}

(15)

where
s_{i,0}
are the initial concentrations sorted in ascending order.

2.7.3
Permutation Test for Significance

The null distribution of
\omega_{c}^{2}
under the hypothesis of no change is generated via permutation:

1.

Randomly permute the time labels (initial vs. final) for each grid cell

2.

Recompute the CvM statistic for the permuted data

3.

Repeat for
R=999
permutations

4.

Calculate the p-value as the proportion of permuted statistics exceeding the observed statistic

This non-parametric approach makes no assumptions about the underlying distribution of nutrient concentrations and accounts for spatial autocorrelation through the permutation structure (maintaining the spatial pattern while randomizing the time label).

2.7.4
Multivariate Extension

For multivariate assessment, we compute a combined CvM statistic:

\omega_{\text{combined}}^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{c\in\{\text{N},\text{P},\text{K}\}}\omega_{c}^{2}

(16)

with p-values obtained through the same permutation procedure applied to the multivariate state vectors.

2.9.1
Pseudocode

The following pseudocode outlines the main simulation workflow as implemented in R:

# Initialize parameters
X <- 20; Y <- 20; T <- 4; C <- 3
S <- array(1.0, dim = c(X, Y, T, C))
alpha <- create_stiffness_map(X, Y) # based on texture classes
crop_forces <- list(Corn = c(-0.6, -0.2, -0.2),
Soybean = c(0.2, -0.1, -0.1),
Wheat = c(-0.2, -0.4, -0.1))
rotation <- c("Corn", "Soybean", "Wheat")

# Run simulation
for (yr in 1:length(rotation)) {
force <- crop_forces[[rotation[yr]]]
for (c in 1:C) {
# Apply crop force with stiffness modulation
S[, , yr+1, c] <- S[, , yr, c] * (1 + force[c] * alpha)
# Apply spatial smoothing
S[, , yr+1, c] <- apply_spatial_smoothing(S[, , yr+1, c], sigma = 1.2)
}
}

# Compute multivariate stress
stress <- sqrt((S[, , T, 1] - 1)^2 + (S[, , T, 2] - 1)^2 + (S[, , T, 3] - 1)^2)

# Statistical validation
cvm_results <- cvm_test(initial = rep(1, X*Y), final = as.vector(S[, , T, 1]))
moran_result <- Moran.I(as.vector(stress), w = distance_weights(X, Y))

The implementation is designed to be modular and extensible, allowing users to modify parameters, add new crop types, or incorporate alternative smoothing kernels with minimal effort.

2.10
Expected Outcomes and Validation Criteria

The simulation is designed to produce outputs that can be evaluated against empirical expectations:

1.

Heterogeneity
: The stress map should exhibit spatial structure (clusters of high and low stress) rather than random noise, indicating that smoothing successfully introduces spatial dependence.

2.

Multivariate effects
: Despite soybean’s N-fixing capability, stress should appear in P and K dimensions, demonstrating the importance of multivariate assessment.

3.

Buffering effects
: Low-stiffness (sandy) areas should show higher stress than high-stiffness (clay) areas under equivalent crop forces.

4.

Statistical significance
: The CvM test should detect significant deviation from initial conditions after three years, with p-values
<0.05
for most scenarios.

These expectations will be assessed quantitatively and visually, with results presented in Section
3
.

2.11
Limitations and Assumptions

The model makes several simplifying assumptions that should be acknowledged:

1.

Multiplicative force application
assumes proportional effects independent of absolute concentration, which may not hold at very low nutrient levels where uptake kinetics change.

2.

Constant crop forces
ignore feedbacks between soil nutrient status and crop growth; in reality, crops growing in depleted soils may take up fewer nutrients.

3.

Linear smoothing
represents lateral movement as a simple diffusion-like process, while real transport mechanisms (tillage, runoff, bioturbation) are more complex.

4.

Normalized units
facilitate interpretation but lose quantitative connection to absolute nutrient concentrations (e.g., mg kg
-1
).

5.

No explicit depth dimension
aggregates the entire root zone into a single layer, missing vertical stratification.

These limitations represent opportunities for future model development and refinement.

3.7
Summary of Key Findings

The simulation results demonstrate that:

1.

Spatial heterogeneity matters
: Stress patterns are strongly modulated by soil buffering capacity, with low-stiffness areas experiencing 2–3 times higher stress than high-stiffness areas under identical management.

2.

Multivariate assessment is essential
: Despite soybean’s N fixation, phosphorus depletion reached 17.9% and dominated stress in 19% of cells, a finding obscured by single-nutrient or field-average analyses.

3.

Rotational diversity reduces risk
: Continuous corn increased mean stress by 41% and maximum stress by 27% compared to the three-year rotation.

4.

Buffering capacity buffers against heterogeneity
: When stiffness contrast is low (uniformly sandy soils), stress becomes spatially uniform and mean stress approaches levels seen under continuous corn, even with diverse rotations.

5.

Force magnitude amplifies existing patterns
: Higher crop removal rates intensify stress in already-vulnerable areas without altering spatial structure, creating extreme hotspots that warrant targeted intervention.

These findings validate the lattice deformation framework as a tool for assessing rotation sustainability and identifying spatial risk zones for site-specific management.

4
Discussion

4.1
Synthesis of Key Findings

This study introduced a multivariate lattice framework for assessing crop rotation impacts on soil nutrients, explicitly integrating spatial heterogeneity, multivariate crop demands, and statistical validation. The simulation results demonstrate that this approach captures dynamics that conventional field-averaged or single-nutrient assessments miss entirely.

The baseline corn-soybean-wheat rotation produced three key findings. First, despite soybean’s nitrogen-fixing capacity, mean phosphorus depletion (17.9%) exceeded that of nitrogen (10.8%), and phosphorus dominated stress in 19% of grid cells. This confirms that rotations appearing balanced from an N perspective can systematically deplete other nutrients, a risk obscured when assessments focus on single elements
(
35
;
15
)
. Second, soil buffering capacity strongly modulated outcomes: low-stiffness sandy areas experienced 2–3 times higher stress than high-stiffness clay areas under identical management, consistent with empirical observations of texture-dependent nutrient dynamics
(
23
;
2
)
. Third, the consistent spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s
I\approx 0.29
,
p<0.001
) across all scenarios validates the kernel smoothing approach as a mechanism for introducing realistic spatial dependence.

The sensitivity analysis revealed that reducing stiffness contrast (S2) produced the most dramatic effect: mean stress increased to 0.84 and the coefficient of variation plummeted to 0.06, indicating near-complete homogenization of stress across the field. This finding has sobering implications for uniformly sandy agricultural landscapes, where the protective effect of high-buffering zones is absent. Under such conditions, even diverse rotations may produce stress levels approaching those observed under continuous corn.

4.2
Spatial Heterogeneity and the ”Poor Get Poorer” Dynamic

The model successfully generated realistic spatial patterns of nutrient depletion, with Moran’s I values falling within the range reported in geostatistical studies of soil nutrients
(
31
;
13
)
. Crucially, the spatial patterns emerged from the interaction of three factors: the inherent stiffness map, the sequence of crop forces, and lateral smoothing. Low-buffering areas consistently developed into stress hotspots, while high-buffering areas remained relatively stable.

The stress decomposition analysis revealed an important nuance: although phosphorus showed greater mean depletion, nitrogen dominated stress in 81% of cells under the baseline rotation. This apparent paradox arises because nitrogen depletion was more spatially variable, creating localized zones where N deviation alone exceeded the combined deviations of P and K elsewhere. This heterogeneity implies that uniform fertilizer recommendations would inevitably misapply nutrients, over-applying some in areas where they are not limiting while under-applying others where they are critical
(
32
)
.

The correlation between initial nutrient status and final stress (not explicitly modeled here but implied by the stiffness contrasts) suggests a “poor get poorer” dynamic: areas with inherently low buffering capacity experience disproportionately greater degradation under the same rotation. This aligns with field observations by
28
, who found that maize yields and nutrient uptake were strongly affected by initial soil organic carbon content, with degraded soils responding poorly to inputs that produced substantial gains on more fertile soils. For precision agriculture, this implies that zones identified as low-fertility through soil testing may require not just higher fertilizer rates but fundamentally different management strategies, perhaps including more frequent cover crops or targeted organic amendments, to prevent further degradation
(
44
;
5
)
.

4.3
Multivariate Stress as an Integrative Metric

The Euclidean distance metric
D(x,y,t)
proved effective as an integrative measure of soil degradation. By combining deviations across N, P, and K into a single scalar, it addresses a long-standing challenge in soil health assessment: how to aggregate multiple indicators into a meaningful composite
(
50
)
. The stress map visually identifies zones where multivariate depletion approaches critical thresholds, enabling targeted interventions.

The threshold concept deserves further elaboration. In our baseline simulation, mean stress of 0.63 indicates that the average cell deviated by 63% from initial conditions in the multivariate space. But what does this mean agronomically? If we assume that yield reductions become significant when any nutrient falls below 70% of initial (i.e., 30% depletion), then the corresponding stress threshold for single-nutrient depletion is
D_{\text{critical}}=0.3
. By this heuristic, 78% of cells in the baseline simulation exceeded the critical threshold, a concerning statistic for a single three-year cycle. However, as
59
emphasized, linking nutrient status to yield outcomes requires empirical calibration that accounts for crop-specific responses and site conditions.

The stress decomposition approach (Table
3
) adds another layer of diagnostic power. Identifying the dominant stressor in each cell enables nutrient-specific interventions. In the baseline simulation, the 19% of cells where phosphorus dominated represent priority zones for P-focused management, perhaps through targeted application of phosphate fertilizer or incorporation of crops with high P-mobilizing capacity
(
15
)
. The fact that potassium never emerged as dominant under any rotation except those with extreme parameters suggests that K depletion, while present, is rarely the primary driver of multivariate stress in these simulations, a hypothesis that warrants field validation.

4.4
Comparison with Existing Modeling Approaches

How does the lattice framework compare to established modeling approaches? Process-based models like DSSAT, DNDC, and RothC simulate crop growth, nutrient cycling, and organic matter dynamics with considerable mechanistic detail
(
47
;
61
;
21
)
. They can incorporate multiple nutrients and simulate rotations over decades. However, as
18
noted, within-field heterogeneities are rarely considered in such applications, and when they are, the data requirements and computational demands become prohibitive.

The lattice model occupies a complementary niche: it sacrifices some mechanistic detail (e.g., no explicit crop growth, simplified nutrient pools, constant crop forces) to achieve full spatial explicitness with minimal data requirements. This trade-off enables rapid assessment of alternative rotations across heterogeneous fields, identifying risk zones that warrant more detailed investigation with process-based models. In this sense, the lattice framework serves as a screening tool, a “first pass” assessment that can guide where to focus scarce modeling and sampling resources
(
12
)
.

Geostatistical models describe spatial patterns but do not simulate management impacts
(
31
;
49
;
54
)
. The lattice model extends geostatistics by incorporating process-based rules for how management modifies spatial structure over time. This bridges the descriptive (geostatistics) and mechanistic (crop modeling) traditions, addressing a gap identified in recent reviews
(
14
;
52
)
.

4.5
Rotation Diversity and System Resilience

The comparison between baseline rotation, two-year rotation, and continuous corn revealed that rotational diversity substantially buffers against extreme nutrient depletion. Continuous corn increased mean stress by 41% and maximum stress by 27% compared to the three-year rotation. The coefficient of variation declined from 0.34 to 0.27, suggesting that aggressive monoculture forces overwhelm local buffering capacities, leading to more homogenized field-wide degradation.

This finding aligns with ecological theory, which posits that diversity enhances ecosystem stability and resilience
(
50
)
. In agricultural contexts, diverse rotations have been shown to improve nutrient use efficiency, suppress pests, and enhance soil structure
(
17
;
25
)
. Our results add a spatial dimension to this understanding: diversity not only affects mean outcomes but also shapes the distribution of risk across the landscape. The three-year rotation maintained higher spatial variance in stress (
CV_{D}=0.34
) than continuous corn (
CV_{D}=0.27
), indicating that diverse rotations allow high-buffering zones to remain relatively protected while concentrating degradation in vulnerable areas, a form of spatial risk stratification.

The two-year corn-soybean rotation produced stress metrics intermediate between baseline and continuous corn (data not shown), suggesting that even modest diversification confers benefits. This is consistent with meta-analytic findings that soybean in rotation increases subsequent corn yields by 5–15% depending on soil and climate conditions
(
45
;
8
)
.

4.6
Implications of Sensitivity Analyses

The sensitivity analyses reveal which parameters most strongly influence model outcomes and, by extension, which factors should be priorities for empirical measurement and management intervention.

Smoothing scale (S1)
: The relative insensitivity of mean stress to smoothing scale (both 0.63 for
\sigma=1.2
and
\sigma=3.0
) suggests that lateral mixing, while important for spatial pattern formation, does not dramatically alter the total nutrient budget. This is reassuring because smoothing parameters are difficult to calibrate empirically; moderate misspecification does not compromise overall conclusions about mean stress levels.

Stiffness contrast (S2)
: The dramatic increase in mean stress (from 0.63 to 0.84) and collapse in spatial variance (
CV_{D}
from 0.34 to 0.06) under low stiffness contrast underscore the critical importance of soil buffering capacity. Landscapes with uniformly low buffering capacity, such as sandy coastal plains or weathered tropical soils
(
38
)
, are fundamentally more vulnerable to nutrient depletion, regardless of rotation diversity. In such systems, external inputs of organic matter or amendments that increase buffering capacity (e.g., biochar, clay addition) may be necessary to achieve sustainability
(
27
)
.

Force magnitude (S3)
: The linear scaling of stress with force magnitude (mean stress 0.63 at 1.0×, 0.90 at 1.5×) confirms that crop removal rates are first-order controls on system degradation. This highlights the importance of accurate crop nutrient removal coefficients
(
30
;
59
)
and suggests that breeding for nutrient-efficient varieties
(
1
;
24
)
could substantially reduce rotation impacts.

Rotation sequence (S4)
: The superiority of the three-year rotation over continuous corn aligns with agronomic wisdom, but the magnitude of the difference (41% higher mean stress) quantifies the benefit in spatially explicit, multivariate terms. This provides a basis for economic valuation of rotation benefits that could inform policy incentives for diversified cropping systems
(
11
)
.

4.7
Limitations and Assumptions

Several limitations of the current model should be acknowledged.

First-order kinetics assumption
: The multiplicative force application (Eq.
10
) assumes that nutrient change is proportional to current concentration. While this is consistent with many soil processes at moderate concentrations, it may break down at very low levels where uptake kinetics become nonlinear or where plants access previously unavailable pools
(
3
;
10
)
. Extending the model to include Michaelis-Menten kinetics or threshold effects could improve realism in severely depleted zones.

Constant crop forces
: The model assumes that crop nutrient removal is fixed regardless of soil nutrient status. In reality, crops growing in depleted soils take up fewer nutrients, and nutrient concentrations in harvested biomass decline
(
30
)
. This feedback loop could moderate the extreme depletion predicted in low-buffering zones. Incorporating dynamic forces that depend on current nutrient levels would add realism at the cost of additional parameters.

Simplified lateral transport
: Gaussian smoothing represents lateral nutrient movement as a diffusion-like process. Actual transport mechanisms, tillage redistribution, runoff, bioturbation, are more complex and anisotropic
(
60
)
. The smoothing parameter
\sigma
lumps multiple processes into a single scale factor; its value was chosen based on geostatistical ranges rather than measured transport rates. Calibrating
\sigma
to field tracer experiments would strengthen the model’s physical basis.

Normalized units
: Working in normalized concentrations (relative to initial) facilitates interpretation and cross-site comparison but loses quantitative connection to absolute nutrient stocks (e.g., kg ha

Single-layer representation
: Aggregating the entire root zone into one layer ignores vertical stratification of nutrients and roots. In reality, surface layers are often enriched while subsoils remain relatively unchanged

. A multi-layer version could capture this, but would require additional parameters for vertical transport and depth-dependent root uptake.

Absence of crop yield feedback
: The model assesses soil nutrient status but does not predict how this affects crop yields. Linking the stress metric to yield functions, for example, through Mitscherlich-Baule response surfaces, would translate nutrient status into agronomic outcomes

)
.

Statistical vs. agronomic significance
: The Cramér–von Mises test detected significant multivariate deviation (
p<0.001

4.8
Future Directions

Several extensions could enhance the model’s utility and realism.

Empirical calibration
: Applying the model to field sites with high-resolution soil data and documented rotation histories would test its predictive accuracy. The CvM statistic provides a natural goodness-of-fit measure: simulated and observed final nutrient distributions can be compared directly. Sites with contrasting soil textures and management histories, such as the long-term experiments reviewed by
32
and
42
, would be ideal testbeds.

Incorporating uncertainty
: The current deterministic framework could be extended to probabilistic simulations by treating crop forces, stiffness factors, and smoothing parameters as random variables. Monte Carlo ensembles would generate confidence intervals around stress maps, helping to make risk-based decisions. This is particularly important for parameters with high uncertainty, such as crop removal coefficients which vary with yield level and management
(
59
)
.

Multi-layer representation
: Adding depth dimensions would capture vertical nutrient stratification and enable assessment of subsoil depletion, a growing concern in intensively managed systems
(
27
)
. This would also allow modeling of deep-rooted crops that access nutrients below the plow layer.

Coupling with crop models
: Integrating the lattice framework with a lightweight crop model (e.g., simplifying DSSAT’s nutrient uptake routines) would replace constant crop forces with dynamic feedbacks. This would increase complexity but improve realism, especially in multi-year simulations where cumulative depletion affects subsequent crop growth. The resulting hybrid model would retain spatial explicitness while gaining mechanistic detail.

Economic optimization
: Linking stress maps to variable rate technology costs and crop yield response functions would enable economic optimization of rotation sequences and fertilizer applications. This moves from “what will happen” to “what should we do”
(
53
)
. The preliminary analysis could identify the marginal cost of reducing stress below critical thresholds, informing incentive structures for sustainable practices.

Integration with remote sensing
: High-resolution imagery can provide initial nutrient surfaces and validate simulated patterns

46

. The model could be inverted to infer soil properties from observed stress patterns, a promising direction for data assimilation. Emerging technologies such as laser-based soil sensing

could provide the high-density data needed for such approaches.

Incorporating biological processes
: The current model focuses on abiotic nutrient dynamics, but biological processes, microbial mineralization, mycorrhizal associations, rhizosphere interactions, strongly influence nutrient availability

4.9
Implications for Sustainable Nutrient Management

Despite these limitations, the lattice framework offers actionable insights for practitioners, policy makers, and researchers.

For practitioners
: Soil buffering capacity, often ignored in rotation planning, is a first-order control on sustainability. Sandy, low-buffering fields require fundamentally different management than clayey, high-buffering fields. The same rotation that is sustainable on clay may rapidly degrade sand. Soil testing should therefore include not only nutrient concentrations but also texture, mineralogy, and organic matter as indicators of buffering capacity. The variable rate technology can then address the heterogeneity within the field, applying more intensive management to vulnerable zones
(
4
)
.

For policymakers
: The stress metric
D
and the Cramér–von Mises statistic provide quantitative benchmarks for sustainability certification. Rotations that produce significant multivariate stress (
p<0.05
) within a defined timeframe could be flagged as potentially unsustainable, triggering management review or incentive adjustments. Programs promoting crop diversification
(

7

could use such metrics to verify that diversity indeed confers the expected benefits.

For researchers
: The framework highlights several understudied areas. First, empirical data on crop-specific nutrient removal vectors under varying soil conditions remain sparse, particularly for P and K
(
59

. Second, the interaction between initial spatial heterogeneity and management outcomes, the “poor get poorer” dynamic, deserves systematic investigation through long-term experiments with spatially explicit sampling
(
33

. Third, the threshold question, ”What level of multivariate stress translates to yield loss?” requires meta-analytic synthesis of existing data and targeted experimentation.

5
Conclusion

The multivariate lattice model demonstrates that crop rotation impacts on soil nutrients are inherently spatial and multivariate. By integrating these dimensions within a single framework, it reveals dynamics, spatial stress patterns, nutrient-specific depletion hotspots, interaction between buffering capacity and management, that conventional assessments miss. The statistical validation via Cramér–von Mises tests and spatial autocorrelation metrics provides a rigorous basis for distinguishing systematic degradation from random variation.

The sensitivity analyses quantify how different factors contribute to system stress. Stiffness contrast emerged as the dominant control on spatial pattern formation; force magnitude as the primary driver of stress intensity; and rotation diversity as a key modulator of both mean stress and its spatial distribution. These findings translate into practical recommendations: prioritize soil buffering assessment, target interventions to vulnerable zones, and diversify rotations to spread risk across the landscape.

In an era of increasing pressure on agricultural systems and growing awareness of the limits to conventional management approaches, frameworks that capture complexity while remaining computationally tractable are urgently needed. The lattice model offers such a framework, bridging geostatistical descriptions of soil variability with mechanistic representations of crop effects. With further development, empirical calibration, uncertainty quantification, economic optimization, it could become a practical tool for designing sustainable rotations and targeting site-specific management interventions. The ultimate goal is not merely to model degradation, but to guide the transition toward agricultural systems that maintain soil health while feeding a growing population.
