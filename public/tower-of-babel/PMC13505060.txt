Patient and family-centered concerns in pediatric cardiac surgery: a Middle Eastern perspective
Authors: Neerod Kumar Jha, Abbas Abdul Hassan, Mohamed Elmahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital heart disease affects approximately 1% of live births worldwide and remains among the most significant contributors to neonatal morbidity and mortality. Approximately half of them require surgical or percutaneous interventions at some stage. Patient care involves critical situations including clinical, psychological, social, regional, and cultural practices. Family and patients go through stressful experiences. Through this review, we aim to highlight issues faced by the patients or their families during management of complex cardiac conditions and hope that medical personnel, health authorities and institutions will benefit from the information provided. This will result in a better patient experience and outcomes.MethodTo identify patient and family concerns, this narrative review is based on daily observations and literature reviews (Google, Google Scholar, PubMed) between 2013 and 2026, and regional perspectives. Key areas included parental and patient stress, communication breakdowns, health care coverage, regional cultural influence, family dynamics, and socio-economic considerations.ResultsPatient-centered concerns in pediatric cardiac surgery include clinical, psychological, socioeconomic, and regional factors. In addition, communication, stress, interruptions in education or employment, funding, and logistics also influence patient’s experience. Effective interprofessional care, psychosocial counseling, finding ways to support the cost of treatment, and appropriate use of technology can foster a better patient experience. This is particularly imperative in the Middle East region, where family cohesion, religious beliefs, language diversity, and healthcare inequities shape the recovery path.ConclusionsOur review shows that pediatric cardiac care requires a multidimensional, culturally attuned model that incorporates interprofessional care, empathy, innovation, community support, and system-level solutions involving various professional agencies in health insurance, finance, hospital management, research, and medical education. In addition, healthcare communication, access, support, cultural and spiritual identity plays a significant role in shaping patient and family experience during medical management.

Introduction

Congenital heart disease affects approximately 1% of live births worldwide and significantly contributes to neonatal morbidity and mortality [1]. Pediatric cardiac surgery is invasive, complex, and associated with risks of infection, bleeding, stroke, organ failure, extracorporeal mechanical support, and the need for staged interventions. Advances in surgical techniques and medical management have significantly reduced complications and improved survival rates. Yet, postoperative recovery is influenced not only by the medical condition or nature of management but also by the emotional, psychosocial, and cultural resilience of the patients and their families [2]. This interdependence highlights the importance of adopting a patient-centered care- a paradigm built upon respect, information-sharing, collaboration, and participation [3–4]. Through this review, we aim to highlight issues faced by the patients or their families during surgical management of complex cardiac conditions and hope that medical personnel, health authorities and institutions will benefit from the information provided. This will result in a better patient experience and outcome.

Materials and methods

This manuscript examines the challenges faced by patients and their families during medical management with particular reference to pediatric cardiac surgery. The review of the literature (search and inclusion criteria) included factors such as psychological and emotional stress, logistics, access to treatment, influence of regional and cultural trends to find key barriers to optimum medical care from the perspective of patients and families [5–6]. This narrative review includes data search (Google, Google Scholar and PubMed) published literature between 2013 and 2026. The search criteria included all discussed factors in this paper as per the theme of the title. The thematic patterns in the published papers in the English literature were the basis of our conclusions.In addition, our day-to-day experience and observations are also included in this review.

Discussion

To identify various compounding factors and discuss the issue, we have stratified following factors related to the patient-related concerns in the context of this narrative review. The age range for the pediatric population concerned is newborns to 16-years old patients with cardiac malformations requiring cardiac surgery.

Psychological issues and family wellbeing

The emotional toll of pediatric cardiac surgery on parents can be immense. A significant number of parents experience chronic anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress symptoms following surgery, with studies reporting prevalence rates as high as 40% [2, 7].

In the Middle Eastern region, cultural expectations exacerbate these effects: mothers often bear deep emotional burdens, while fathers, bound by traditional roles, may suppress emotional expression that further isolates mothers, and increases vulnerability. There is a disruption in education for children due to prolonged hospital stays and multiple hospital visits. In a situation with complications or a need for another medical intervention, the patients are anxious about pain. The parents feel immense pressure, expecting the worst or a delayed recovery [6–7].

The solution to deal with this issue may require integration of mental health specialists within postoperative care teams for early screening and counseling. A family round, enabling parents to observe and participate actively in care discussions, might be helpful [8]. In addition, setting up informal social peer networks (mother-support-groups) or organizing social activities or discussions on topics related to heart diseases and management in children may help emotional processing [9].

Children frequently depend on their parents for emotional support. Invasive treatments and the temporary separation during surgery can heighten separation anxiety, disrupt sleep, and erode trust in their surroundings [10]. A child who does not understands the language spoken by nurses or technicians may perceive clinical actions as threatening, which can heighten fear and lead to oppositional behavior. This presents a unique challenge in tertiary centers staffed by multicultural teams [11]. Children in this region often grow up in very protective family settings. Moreover, families frequently choose to spare the child from stress by avoiding speaking about the procedure, which can backfire and lead to increased uncertainty [12]. Such challenges compound the communication barriers and in association with a geographic isolation leads to delays in diagnosis and specialized care.

Providing Arabic-themed visual aids and allowing a parent to be present during the anesthesia induction process, when it is safe to do so, can also help reduce fear and encourage emotional expression. Family-centered counseling is another approach that can enhance the benefits of collective counseling, involving key family members, addressing cultural guilt, and recognizing both male and female family roles without judgment [13]. These build trust through group decision-making support, with a culturally sensitive psychologist, nurse or social worker playing a crucial role [14].

Health care communication and health literacy

In the Middle East’s multilingual settings, health literacy gaps are widespread. The population includes Arabic and non-Arabic residents, living in a multicultural environment. Various languages are spoken. In this context, they depend on reliable and empathetic communication. A few parents misunderstand surgical outcomes, the need for rehabilitation, or prognosis. This could be a result of inconsistency or poor communication between clinicians and the family [15]. The use of complex medical terminology is found to be confusing from the patient or family perspectives [8]. This may start a conflict between expectations and reality.

A systematic review involving ten published studies by Neubauer ‘et al’ in 2018 has concluded that three main themes regarding physician-parent communication are: amount of information, timing of information exchange, and content provided to parents [16].

To overcome the communication barrier, there is a need to develop educational materials with visuals and narrative formats in Arabic dialects and other local languages [17]. Use of “teach-back” method-asking parents to recount care instructions in their own words to confirm understanding may be helpful [18]. Availability of interpreter services or bilingual clinical staff familiar with cultural nuances is essential and common practice and found to be immensely helpful.

The effective patient-centered care also depends on the clinician’s willingness and capacity to engage empathetically. However, due to the nature of work and stress, the pediatric cardiac teams are frequently emotionally overextended, facing burnout and busy [19–20]. The surgical units are providing a world-class service with good outcomes, but sometimes there is a lack of explanation or communication between clinicians and parents [17]. At times families are bound to get treatment in a center which is not their first choice. This signifies the fact that there is a need to maintain trust between physicians and parents. The continuing medical educational sessions may incorporate patient-centered issues and communication skills for better understanding and avoidance of conflicts.

Healthcare access and socioeconomic barriers

Families from remote or underserved regions confront significant hurdles: travel expenses, dislocation from support networks, and disrupted follow-up [20]. The expatriate populations face systemic barriers to access due to multiple reasons that include socio-economic status, employment, insurance cover, and choice of treatment options in their home countries [21].

Some corrective measures may include provision of accommodation for the patients and families within the vicinity of the hospital during complex surgical procedures or follow-up visits. Similarly, interstage care coordinators may play a role [22]. They can follow the remote patients while coordinating hospital visits or the supply of medications from time to time using phone or internet for exchange of information [23]. However, Miller et al., in their study on the role of communication and information to parents, described that 56% parents felt that information provided was too much, confusing and there was poor agreement between physician and parents on adequacy of communication [15]. Telemedicine is a useful tool during postoperative monitoring and guidance, where feasible. During Covid-19 pandemic, Miller et al., also demonstrated that use of telemedicine was increased significantly especially for older children population in the US [23]. This shows that when there is a need, system may work.

Child health and management are a multifaceted issue that involves not only the family but all around them [24]. Multiple visits to the hospital, long stays, complications, and costly treatments beyond insurance coverage impact working parents and their employers. It is difficult to manage leave at the time of need and at the same time employers must bear the brunt of workforce substitutes. This can strain relationships, loss of jobs and create stress for all involved. However, few expatriate families move to their home countries for treatment due to sustainable social and economic support. Therefore, coordination between government institutions, social organizations, and the hospitals may be considered to find a suitable solution. In some studies, it has been recommended that organizational leaders should foster value adjustment with behavioral expectations of the staff. If there is a malalignment between current and preferred organizational culture, the staff behavioral expectations may not be optimal. It may reflect in poor health care delivery [20].

Cultural and spiritual identity

Faith and spirituality are integral aspects related to illness and healing in this region [15]. Many families perceive illness as spiritually meaningful, drawing strength from religious communities. On the other hand, gender norms can shape hospital interaction, such as preferences for same-sex healthcare interactions, especially involving female caregivers. Moreover, societal stigma around congenital anomalies may prompt families to conceal their child’s condition, limiting access to community support. Similarly, extended family involvement often extends decision-making beyond parents to include grandparents and other relatives, causing delays during urgent communication or providing consent for treatment [25–26].

Religion plays a vital role in coping for families [27]. Prayer can provide comfort for both parents and children. Families may ask for space or permission to engage in religious practices by their child’s bedside. Acknowledging these spiritual needs can significantly ease anxiety and build trust [28]. Considering the above facts, the medical facilities may consider offering spiritual or faith-based counseling as part of holistic care. Also, they can accommodate gender-specific interaction preferences with appropriate staff assignments. Future prospects

Need for interprofessional care

The concept of interprofessional care is to provide multi-specialty care under one roof at the same time [29]. It involves communication, exchange of information, expert opinions, and timely decision- making to solve issues on the spot so that there is no need for separate appointments or visits for individual clinics or expert care. It avoids logistic issues, delays in medical care, availability of medications and advice at the right time and serves as psychological support to the patients and the families. This model of care has already been implemented in wound care on a global scale with remarkable success [30]. Children with multiple congenital malformations may benefit maximally in this context.

Incorporation of new technology

In the current era of technological advancement, there is significant potential for these innovations to enhance patient experience. The institutions in the Middle East already use mobile applications to facilitate access to electronic medical records, results of investigations, and supply of medications. It also includes teleconsultations, appointment scheduling, and reminders [31].

Conclusion

Patient-centered concerns in pediatric cardiac surgery include clinical, psychological, socioeconomic and regional factors. In addition, healthcare communication, access, support, cultural and spiritual identity plays a significant role in shaping patient and family experience during medical management. Therefore, there is a need for a multidimensional, culturally attuned health care model that incorporates interprofessional care, empathy, innovation, community support, and system-level solutions involving various professional agencies in health insurance, finance, hospital management, research, and medical education.
