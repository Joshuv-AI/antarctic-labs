Abstract
We present a new family of information-theoretic generalization bounds within the framework of conditional mutual information (CMI). Most of our results are established based on the leave- m m -out (L m m O) cross-validation error, with m m denoting the number of the hold-out supersamples. Under this setting, we propose a unified CMI-based bound, allowing to envelop and reproduce many known CMI-based bounds and also bridge the gap between the MI- and CMI-based bounds when m m tends to infinity. The proposed framework not only provides a unified description of the existing bounds but also develops new, sharper bounds. We show the benefits of the proposed bounds through several simple examples, where the existing results are either inapplicable or looser. Moreover, under the premise that the loss function is bounded, we tighten the CMI quantities involved in the proposed bounds by reducing the number of conditional terms, thereby enhancing the proposed framework. We show empirically that the resulting new bounds improve upon the previously known ones.



I
Introduction

A fundamental topic of statistical learning theory concerns how well a hypothesis inferred from some training data can generalize to new unseen data drawn from the same distribution as the training data. That is, how can we ensure that the learned hypothesis has captured knowledge that is reflective of the underlying data distribution rather than the training data? It is therefore crucial to understand the generalization error, which measures the discrepancy between the losses that the learning algorithm incurs on training data and unseen test data. Bounding generalization errors for learning algorithms not only provides guarantees whether the produced hypothesis overfits its training data, but also potentially inspires the design of improved learning algorithms. Several information-theoretic quantities such as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, mutual information (MI) and conditional mutual information (CMI), have emerged as promising tools to derive novel information-theoretic bounds. These bounds capture the joint effect of the learning algorithm and data distribution, beyond what conventional algorithm-independent bounds (e.g., those based on the methods of VC dimension and Rademacher complexity) can express
[
22
]
.

Information-theoretic generalization bounds were first initiated in the seminal works
[
20
]
and
[
26
]
, where the generalization error is bounded in terms of the MI between the input training data and the output hypothesis. However, in some learning scenarios where the true generalization error is vanishing, the very first MI bound may yield a vacuous result
[
2
]
. This happens particularly when the input data set and output hypothesis are both continuous random variables and the hypothesis is a deterministic function of the training data, thus leading to an unbounded MI quantity. To mitigate this limitation, the original MI bound was advanced and improved with various techniques, including the individual-sample or random-subset technique
[
2
,
7
,
19
]
, the disintegration technique
[
14
]
and the chaining method
[
1
,
28
]
. Amongst them, the authors of
[
2
]
decomposed the generalization error and bounded each individual term to derive an individual mutual information (IMI) bound, which is proved to be tighter than the MI bound. Remarkably, another major step to improve the MI-based bounds was pushed forward by a series of CMI-based bounds
[
23
,
6
,
27
,
17
,
5
]
, where the training set is randomly selected from a larger supersample set and the generalization behavior is characterized by the CMI quantity between the output hypothesis and the training set selection, conditioned on the supersample set. Notably, based on different construction approaches of the supersample set, CMI-based bounds can be classified into the standard CMI bound and the leave-one-out CMI (LOO-CMI) bound. The former adopts the setting introduced initially in
[
23
]
, where a Bernoulli variable is used to distinguish the training membership from a supersample pair. Furthermore, previous tightening techniques for the original MI bound are also applicable to the standard CMI bound
[
6
,
19
,
27
]
. However, the
n
unused samples in the standard supersample set of size
2n
make it inefficient to compute the CMI quantity. This issue can be alleviated by the LOO-CMI bound, where the introduction of only one supersample brings benefits in terms of lower computational cost, alongside better interpretation and explicit connections with stability theory
[
17
,
5
]
.

Although the CMI-based bounds are always non-vacuous, they still suffer from the challenges arising in cumbersome estimation of MI involving the high-dimensional hypothesis, hindering the bounds from practical application. To alleviate this issue, one line of research employs the functional CMI (f-CMI) bound
[
7
]
, where the high-dimensional hypothesis is replaced by the low-dimensional prediction with respect to the chosen supersample. As a result, the f-CMI bound is applicable to a wide range of methods, including non-parametric approaches, Bayesian algorithms and ensembling algorithms
[
7
]
. Subsequently, the f-CMI bound was advanced in several follow-up works
[
8
,
24
,
3
]
, yielding strengthened bounds evaluated on the loss incurred on supersamples, including evaluated CMI (e-CMI)
[
8
]
, loss-difference based MI (ld-MI)
[
24
]
and binarized-loss based MI (bl-MI)
[
3
]
. In general, the data-processing inequality implies the following progression chain from loosest to tightest: f-CMI
\to
e-CMI
\to
ld-MI
\to
bl-MI. Notably, the bl-MI bound provides an exact characterization of the generalization behavior and is therefore the tightest bound. It should be noted that the loss-based bounds are tailored for the specific loss function that appears in the bound. In contrast, conventional hypothesis-based bounds are compatible with general loss functions as they only require knowledge of the hypothesis itself.

Following the above-mentioned research thread and further motivated by understanding a deeper relationship between various generalization bounds, we establish a unified supersample framework that envelops most of the existing generalization bounds and further facilitates new promising bounds with improved tightness and effectiveness. The main contributions are summarized as follows.

•

Towards the generalization analysis of learning algorithms, we present a new leave-

-out (L

2

27
,

, providing a unified view and derivation of the existing results.

•

The proposed unified setting also allows us to derive new bounds (Corollary

[
2
]
and individually conditional individual mutual information (ICIMI)

•

Inspired by

•

Beyond the regime of conventional hypothesis-based bounds, we further develop the proposed unified supersample setting to establish tighter bounds based on a different discrepancy measure of the generalization error and easy-to-measure bounds based on the predictions for the supersamples (Theorem

II-B

Problem Formulation

Let
{\mathcal{Z}}
denote the data domain, and
{\mathcal{W}}
the hypothesis domain. Let
Z_{[n]}:=\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}
be a collection composed of
n
i.i.d. samples drawn from some unknown distribution
\mu
, where
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Taking the training set
Z_{[n]}
as input, a possibly stochastic learning algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}:={\mathcal{Z}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{W}}
selects a hypothesis
W\in{\mathcal{W}}
as the output, i.e.,
W:={\mathcal{A}}(Z_{[n]})
. This algorithm induces a conditional distribution
P_{W|Z_{[n]}}
which maps from
{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}
to
{\mathcal{W}}
. We denote the joint distribution of the training set
Z_{[n]}
and the hypothesis
W
by
P_{W,Z_{[n]}}:=\mu^{\otimes n}\otimes P_{W|Z_{[n]}}
, and the marginal distribution of
W
by
P_{W}
. Given a loss function
\ell:{\mathcal{W}}\times{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, the learner would find a hypothesis
w\in{\mathcal{W}}
that minimizes the following population loss:

\displaystyle L_{\mu}(w)={\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\mu}\left[\ell(w,Z)\right],

(1)

and the quantity
L_{\mu}:={\mathbb{E}}_{W}[L_{\mu}(W)]
is then defined as the expected population risk. However, it is typically infeasible to compute
L_{\mu}(w)
directly since the underlying distribution
\mu
is usually unknown. One alternate approach is to minimize the empirical loss defined by

\displaystyle L_{Z_{[n]}}(w):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(w,Z_{i}).

(2)

We also define
\widehat{L}_{n}:={\mathbb{E}}[L_{Z_{[n]}}(W)]
as the expected empirical risk, where the expectation is taken over the joint distribution
P_{W,Z_{[n]}}
. To evaluate how the hypothesis selected by the learner performs on unseen samples compared to those that are used for training and thus assess the overfitting effect, the expected generalization error is introduced as

\displaystyle\gen:=L_{\mu}-\widehat{L}_{n}.

(3)

In the sequel, our main efforts focus on bounding the expected generalization error
\gen
.

In particular, if

for all

\displaystyle\gen\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}I(W;Z_{i})}.

III-B

CMI-Based Bounds

The aforementioned MI-based bounds may yield vacuous results, particularly when the hypothesis is a deterministic function of the training data. To alleviate this issue, a new supersample setting was first introduced in

]
to obtain non-vacuous and tightened generalization bounds. We note that all of the supersample settings introduced in this paper serve as purely analytical tools without altering the learning problem stated in Section

Definition 3

(Standard Supersample Setting
[

]
)
.

Define the supersample set

into a training sample and a test sample
1
1

1

. Specifically,
n

.

It is shown in the proof of

]

Theorem 4

(ICIMI Bound

)

Consider the standard supersample setting in Definition

In particular, if
\ell(w,z)\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\gen

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{2I\left(W;R_{i}|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}\right)}.

III-C

Evaluation of the Existing Bounds

In this subsection, we evaluate the tightness of the existing bounds through a specific example, unveiling the important role that the supersample construction plays in developing generalization bounds. For all the considered bounds, the calculations of the involved information measure quantities and decay orders are detailed in Appendix

Example 1

(A Bernoulli Example)

Suppose that all data are sampled from a Bernoulli distribution

\displaystyle W=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}.

Consider a quadratic loss function

The loss function clearly satisfies the boundedness assumption. The calculations of the information measure quantities are detailed in Appendix
E
. We first evaluate the IMI and ICIMI bounds under Example
1
, with the calculation details postponed to Appendices
E-C
and

.

Proposition 1
.

Under Example
1
, the IMI bound in (
7
) and ICIMI bound are both of order
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
.

This proposition aligns with the result in the Gaussian location problem studied in
[

]
. We next evaluate the LOO-CMI bound and arrive at the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Under Example
1
, the LOO-CMI bound in (

) converges to a constant, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textnormal{RHS of \eqref{eq:LOO-CMI}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}H(p)}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

The calculations are detailed in Appendix

. The observations from Propositions

It seems like the adoption of the LOO supersample setting may impair the tightness of the resulting bound. Motivated by this, we introduce in the next sections a new supersample framework, from which all of the aforementioned bounds can be recovered and further improved.

IV
A Unified Framework for CMI-Based Bounds

In this section, we construct the supersample set by a new approach to yield a more general CMI-based bound. We then show that some special cases of the unified CMI-bound can either recover the existing results or provide improved bounds. Finally, we conduct comparisons of all the considered bounds for the case of Example
1
, demonstrating the benefits of the proposed bounds.

IV-A

A Unified CMI-Based Bound

Without loss of generality, we assume that the number of training samples is
n
throughout this paper. Now consider a more general setting where
m
samples are removed from a supersample set of size
n+m
, where
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Definition 5

(Partitioned Leave-
m
-Out Supersample Setting)
.

Define a supersample set
\hat{Z}_{[n+m]}:=\{\hat{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+m}\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{n+m}
as a collection of
n+m
samples drawn i.i.d. from
\mu
. Let
k\in{\mathcal{K}}
be an integer that divides
n
and
m
, where
{\mathcal{K}}:=\{k\in{\mathbb{N}}:n\mod k=0,m\mod k=0\}
. Then, each supersample is mapped to
\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{j}:=\hat{Z}_{\frac{n+m}{k}(i-1)+j}
in an ascending order, where
i\in[k]
and
j\in[\frac{n+m}{k}]
. Let
\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}:=\{\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\frac{n+m}{k}}
denote the
i
-th block of size
\frac{n+m}{k}
, and
\hat{Z}_{[n+m]}=\{\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}\}_{i=1}^{k}
a partition of
\hat{Z}_{[n+m]}
composed of
k
disjoint blocks. For the
i
-th block, we further define
U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}:=\{U^{(i)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\frac{n}{k}}
as the corresponding training membership vector and
\bar{U}^{(i)}_{[\frac{m}{k}]}:=[\frac{n+m}{k}]\backslash U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}
the test membership vector, where
U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}
is a subset of size
\frac{n}{k}
chosen uniformly from
[\frac{n+m}{k}]
(i.e.,
U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}
is distributed uniformly among all
\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{n+m}{k}\\
\frac{n}{k}\end{smallmatrix}\big)
possibilities), such that
\frac{n}{k}
samples are selected as the training subset
Z^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}:=\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}}=\{\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{U^{(i)}_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{\frac{n}{k}}
and the remaining
\frac{m}{k}
supersamples
\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{\bar{U}^{(i)}_{[\frac{m}{k}]}}
constitutes the test subset. The training membership of the partition is then defined by the collection of
U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}
’s, i.e.,
U_{[n]}:=\{U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, and the test membership
\bar{U}_{[m]}
is defined analogously. Accordingly, the whole training set of size
n
is constructed as
\{Z^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}\}_{i=1}^{k}
(or equivalently
\hat{Z}_{U_{[n]}}=\{\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}}\}_{i=1}^{k}
) and the remaining
m
supersamples
\hat{Z}_{\bar{U}_{[m]}}
constitutes the test set. Thus, the output hypothesis is characterized as
W^{\textnormal{L$m$O}}\sim P_{W|Z_{[n]}}(\cdot|\hat{Z}_{U_{[n]}})
.

Remark 1
.

For the benefit of analytical tractability, the setting in Definition
5
divides the supersample set
\hat{Z}_{[n+m]}
into equal-sized blocks. In essence, this setting can be extended by adopting other partitions of
\hat{Z}_{[n+m]}
with variable-sized blocks. In this case, the blocks remain disjoint by the definition of partition, and thus most of our results can be readily extended.

Adopting the convention, we still use
W
instead of
W^{\textnormal{L$m$O}}
whenever it is clear from context. For illustrative purposes, the proposed supersample setting is briefly depicted in Fig.
2
. In addition, a specific example of the proposed setting is provided in Fig.
3
.

We next argue that a certain error incurred on the introduced supersamples is equivalent to
\gen
defined in
(
3
)
, akin to the cases in other supersample settings. Specifically, we define the following L
m
O cross-validation error to measure the difference between the training and test losses:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\textnormal{L$m$O}}\left(w,\hat{z}_{[n+m]},u_{[n]}\right)

\displaystyle:=L_{\hat{z}_{\bar{u}_{[m]}}}(w)-L_{\hat{z}_{u_{[n]}}}(w)

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigg(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j\in\bar{u}^{(i)}_{[\frac{m}{k}]}}\ell(w,\hat{z}^{(i)}_{j})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in u^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}}\ell(w,\hat{z}^{(i)}_{j})\Bigg).

Let define
{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}\left(w,\hat{z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]},u^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}\right):=L_{\hat{z}_{\bar{u}^{(i)}_{[\frac{m}{k}]}}}(w)-L_{\hat{z}_{u^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}}}(w)
as the cross-validation error with respect to the
i
-th block, then (
15
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\textnormal{L$m$O}}\left(w,\hat{z}_{[n+m]},u_{[n]}\right)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}\left(w,\hat{z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]},u^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}\right).

(16)

Hence, the following lemma shows that the L
m
O cross-validation error, when averaged over the joint distribution of the random variables involved, provides an unbiased estimate of the expected generalization error in (
3
).

Lemma 1
.

Consider the partitioned L
m
O supersample setting in Definition
5
. The expected generalization error defined in (
3
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\gen

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{W,\hat{Z}_{[n+m]},U_{[n]}}\left[{\mathcal{E}}_{\textnormal{L$m$O}}(W,\hat{Z}_{[n+m]},U_{[n]})\right]

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}_{W,\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]},U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}}\left[{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}\left(W,\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]},U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}\right)\right].

The proof is given in Appendix
B-A
. Based on this lemma, we are now in a position to derive generalization bounds with new information quantities. The following theorem holds by the linearity of expectation and marginalization, resulting in a new CMI-based bound that is conditioned on an individual block. The complete proof can be found in Appendix
B-B
. We refer to the new bound as the individually partitioned conditional individual mutual information (IPCIMI) bound, which is shown as follows.

Theorem 6

(IPCIMI Bounds)
.

Consider the partitioned L
m
O supersample setting in Definition
5
. Let
(\tilde{W},\tilde{U}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]})
be a decoupled pair of
(W,U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]})
conditioned on
\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}
, i.e.,
P_{\tilde{W},\tilde{U}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}}=P_{W|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}}\otimes P_{U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}}
, where
P_{U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}}=P_{U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}}
as
U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}\perp\!\!\!\perp\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}
. Then,

\displaystyle\gen

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(I^{\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}}\left(W;U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}\right)+\psi_{\mathcal{E}^{(i)}(\tilde{W},\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]},\tilde{U}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]})|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}}(\lambda)\right)\right]

(18)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(I\left(W;U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}\right)+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\psi_{\mathcal{E}^{(i)}(\tilde{W},\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]},\tilde{U}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]})|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}}(\lambda)\right]\right).

(19)

Remark 2
.

It is noted that although the disintegrated bounds are in general tighter than their integrated counterparts (e.g., (
9
) is tighter than (
10
), and (
18
) is tighter than (
19
)), they may suffer from the issue of computational intractability
[
6
,
27
]
. For efficient evaluations and fair comparisons, we mainly focus on the integrated CMI-based bounds (e.g., (
10
) and (
19
)) unless otherwise stated.

The intuition conveyed by the CMI quantity
I(W;U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]})
is whether the output hypothesis
W
can infer the supersamples, from a given block, that contribute to the training, and a negative answer to this question implies a good generalization behavior. Next, we show in the following lemma that the CGF of
\mathcal{E}^{(i)}(\tilde{W},\hat{z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]},\tilde{U}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]})
can be upper bounded when the loss function satisfies some specific assumption.

Lemma 2
.

Consider the partitioned L
m
O supersample setting in Definition
5
. Suppose
\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}}}|\ell(w,z)-\ell(w,z^{\prime})|\leq\Delta
for any
z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, then for all
\hat{z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{\frac{n+m}{k}}
,
i\in[k]
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathcal{E}^{(i)}(\tilde{W},\hat{z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]},\tilde{U}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]})}(\lambda)\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}\Delta^{2}C^{k}_{n,m}\cdot k(n+m)}{8nm},

(20)

where
C^{k}_{n,m}=\begin{cases}\frac{n+m}{\max(n,m)}&\min(n,m)=k,\\
\frac{n+m}{n+m-k}\cdot\frac{nm}{nm-k\min(n,m)}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
.

The proof of the above lemma is provided in Appendix
B-C
. Combining this lemma with Theorem
6
directly yields a specific upper bound for bounded loss functions.

Theorem 7

(IPCIMI Bounds for Bounded Loss Difference)
.

Consider the partitioned L
m
O supersample setting in Definition
5
. Suppose
\sup_{w\in{\mathcal{W}}}|\ell(w,z)-\ell(w,z^{\prime})|\leq\Delta
for all
z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, then

\displaystyle\gen

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^{2}C^{k}_{n,m}\cdot k(n+m)}{2nm}I\left(W;U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}\right)},

(21)

We note that the assumption in Theorem
7
requires the loss difference to be bounded, which is a weaker condition than the bounded loss assumption widely adopted in the previous bounds, e.g., (
8
), (
11
) and (
12
). In particular, suppose that
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)\in[0,1]
, we have
\Delta\leq 1
and (
21
) can be specialized to

\displaystyle\gen\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{\frac{C^{k}_{n,m}\cdot k(n+m)}{2nm}I\left(W;U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}\right)}.

(22)

For clarity, we use the prefix
(m,k)
ahead of the IPCIMI bound to indicate the joint effect of both free parameters
m
and
k
, i.e., (
19
) is referred to as the
(m,k)
-IPCIMI bound. Thus, the strongest result can be achieved by optimizing over
(m,k)
such that

\displaystyle\gen\leq\inf_{m\in{\mathbb{N}},k\in{\mathcal{K}}}\left\{\textnormal{RHS of \eqref{eq:gen-IPCIMI}}\right\}.

(23)

We note that finding the optimal
(m,k)
for the above optimizer is intractable. The challenge arises when the bound is calculated for each possible
(m,k)
through a brute-force search process. However, we will show in the next subsection that some specific
(m,k)
pair values allow to recover most of the classical MI- and CMI-based bounds, implying that (
23
) is at least no looser than the existing results. Moreover, it will be further explored that some other specifications of
(m,k)
are sufficient to derive new bounds even sharper than the existing bounds.

Lemma 3
.

\lim\limits_{m\to\infty}I\left(W;U^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}|\hat{Z}^{(i)}_{[\frac{n+m}{k}]}\right)=I\left(W;Z^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}\right)

The proof of this result is given in Appendix

Corollary 1

((
(\infty,k)
-IPCIMI) IPMI Bound)
.

Consider the partitioned L

\displaystyle\gen\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{\frac{k}{2n}I\left(W;Z^{(i)}_{[\frac{n}{k}]}\right)}.

(24)

1
, the MI bound (
5

A Unified SCMI Bound

Prior to our main results, a few definitions are presented as follows. For any

, the KL divergence between two Bernoulli random variables with parameters

and

is defined as the binary KL divergence
[
8

\displaystyle d_{\textnormal{KL}}(p\|q):=D_{\textnormal{KL}}(\Bernoulli(p)\|\Bernoulli(q))=p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}.

(33)

A relaxed version of the binary KL divergence is further defined as
[
11
]

\displaystyle d_{\gamma}(p\|q):=\gamma q-\log(1-p+pe^{\gamma}),

(34)

which satisfies

\displaystyle d_{\textnormal{KL}}(p\|q)=\sup_{\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}}\{d_{\gamma}(p\|q)\}.

(35)

Note that both
d_{\textnormal{KL}}
and
d_{\gamma}
are jointly convex in their arguments
[
8
]
. Next define a symmetrized and smoothed version of the binary KL divergence, namely the weighted binary Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence, which is given by

\displaystyle d^{\theta}_{\textnormal{JS}}(p\|q):=\theta d_{\textnormal{KL}}(p\|\theta p+(1-\theta)q)+(1-\theta)d_{\textnormal{KL}}(q\|\theta p+(1-\theta)q),

(36)

where
\theta
is the weight for
p
. Note that a special case of
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
can be found in
[
3
]
. Finally, the inverse of the binary JS divergence is defined as

\displaystyle d^{-1}_{\textnormal{JS}}(\theta,p,c):=\sup_{q\in[0,1]}\left\{d^{\theta}_{\textnormal{JS}}(p\|q)\leq c\right\}.

(37)

Built upon the proposed binary JS divergence, an instrumental lemma is introduced to derive a series of information-theoretic generalization bounds later in this paper.

Lemma 4

For a probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},P)

be a random variable,

and we let
\theta=P(T=1)

\displaystyle d^{\theta}_{\textnormal{JS}}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{X|T=1}\left[f(X)\right]\left\|{\mathbb{E}}_{X|T=0}\left[f(X)\right]\right.\right)\leq I(X;T)\leq I(X;Y),

(38)

where the first inequality holds with equality when

and

The lemma is proved through the convexity of the KL divergence and the Donsker–Varadhan inequality, with details relegated to Appendix

VII
Conclusions

This paper proposes a new information-theoretic framework to characterize the generalization behavior of learning algorithms. Particularly, we present a new construction of the supersample set, shedding light on a unified view and derivation of the known MI- and CMI-based bounds. The incorporated free parameters enable reproducing many existing results, facilitating new bounds and a joint optimization over a bunch of bounds. The advantages of the proposed bounds are demonstrated by applying them to various learning scenarios. Additionally, we advance our framework under the premise that the loss function is bounded, establishing potentially sharper bounds. Lastly, our framework is integrated with other methods to yield other advantages in terms of sharpness or effectiveness.

Lemma 20

If
X
is a binomial random variable following
X\sim\Binomial(n,p)
and

, then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log(X+a)\right]=\log(np+a)-\frac{np(1-p)}{2(np+a)^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

(167)

Proof.

By Taylor’s theorem, we expand the function
f(x)=\log(x+a)
at point
x_{0}
such that

\displaystyle f(x)=f(x_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{f^{(i)}(x)}{i!}(x-x_{0})^{i}+\frac{f^{(i)}(\xi)}{4!}(x-x_{0})^{4},

(168)

where
\xi

x_{0}
and
x
. Let

, it can be further calculated that

\displaystyle\log(x+a)=

\displaystyle\log(np+a)+\frac{x-np}{np+a}-\frac{(x-np)^{2}}{2(np+a)^{2}}+\frac{(x-np)^{3}}{3(np+a)^{3}}-\frac{(x-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}.

(169)

Taking expectation on the upper bound of (
169

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log(X+a)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\log(np+a)+\frac{X-np}{np+a}-\frac{(X-np)^{2}}{2(np+a)^{2}}+\frac{(X-np)^{3}}{3(np+a)^{3}}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right]

(170)

\displaystyle=\log(np+a)+\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[X-np\right]}{np+a}-\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-np)^{2}\right]}{2(np+a)^{2}}+\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-np)^{3}\right]}{3(np+a)^{3}}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle=\log(np+a)+0-\frac{np(1-p)}{2(np+a)^{2}}+\frac{np(1-p)(1-2p)}{3(np+a)^{3}}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle=\log(np+a)-\frac{np(1-p)}{2(np+a)^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right].

It suffices to show that the expectation of the remainder term is of order
O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})
. Using Hoeffding’s inequality, we can derive an upper bound for the tail probability that

, which is shown as follows:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[X\leq\frac{1}{2}np\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[X-{\mathbb{E}}\left[X\right]\leq-\frac{1}{2}np\right]

(171)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[X_{i}\right]\leq-\frac{1}{2}np\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2}p^{2}n},

is a Bernoulli random variable for any

. Then, by the tower property of expectation, we rewrite the expectation of the remainder term as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right|X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]+\Pr\left[X\leq\frac{1}{2}np\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right|X\leq\frac{1}{2}np\right].

(172)

Since
X\in[n]
and
\xi
is within
np
and
X
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\leq\frac{\max((np-1)^{4},(n-np)^{4})}{4(1+a)^{4}}=O(n^{4}).

(173)

Due to fact that exponential decay dominates any polynomial growth, we see that the contribution of
X\leq\frac{1}{2}np
to the expectation is negligible, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[X\leq\frac{1}{2}np\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right|X\leq\frac{1}{2}np\right]\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2}p^{2}n}\frac{\max((np-1)^{4},(n-np)^{4})}{4(1+a)^{4}}=o(\frac{1}{n^{2}}).

(174)

In addition, similar to (
172
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-np)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.(X-np)^{4}\right|X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]+\Pr\left[X\leq\frac{1}{2}np\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.(X-np)^{4}\right|X\leq\frac{1}{2}np\right]

(175)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\left[X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.(X-np)^{4}\right|X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}p^{2}n}\right){\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.(X-np)^{4}\right|X>\frac{1}{2}np\right].

Now, by using the results in (
174
) and (
175
), we can bound (
172
) as

\displaystyle 0\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:exp_X_out_range}}{\leq}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\xi+a)^{4}}\right|X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]+o(\frac{1}{n^{2}})

(176)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{(X-np)^{4}}{4(\frac{1}{2}np+a)^{4}}\right|X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]+o(\frac{1}{n^{2}})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:exp_X_in_range}}{\leq}\frac{1}{1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}p^{2}n}}\frac{1}{4(\frac{1}{2}np+a)^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-np)^{4}\right]+o(\frac{1}{n^{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}p^{2}n}}\frac{np(1-p)(1+3(n-2)p(1-p))}{4(\frac{1}{2}np+a)^{4}}+o(\frac{1}{n^{2}})

\displaystyle=\Theta(\frac{1}{n^{2}}),

which implies (
172
) is of order
O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})
. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 22
.

If
X
is a binomial random variable following
X\sim\Binomial(n,p)
, then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[X\log X\right]=np\log np+\frac{1-p}{2}+\frac{(1-p)(5-4p)}{6np}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

(178)

Proof.

Using the Taylor series in (
169
) with
a=0
, for all
x>0
, we have

\displaystyle x\log x=x\left(\log np+\frac{x-np}{np}-\frac{(x-np)^{2}}{2(np)^{2}}+\frac{(x-np)^{3}}{3(np)^{3}}-\frac{(x-np)^{4}}{4\xi^{4}}\right),

(179)

where
\xi
is between
np
and
x
. Taking expectations on both sides yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[X\log X\right]

\displaystyle=np\log np+\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[X(X-np)\right]}{np}-\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[X(X-np)^{2}\right]}{2(np)^{2}}+\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[X(X-np)^{3}\right]}{3(np)^{3}}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{X(X-np)^{4}}{4\xi^{4}}\right].

(180)

Notice
X(X-np)^{i}=(X-np)^{i+1}+np(X-np)^{i}
for any positive integer
i
, then it can be accordingly calculated that

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\left[X(X-np)\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-np)^{2}+np(X-np)\right]=np(1-p),

(181)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\left[X(X-np)^{2}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-np)^{3}+np(X-np)^{2}\right]=np(1-p)(1-2p)+(np)^{2}(1-p),

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\left[X(X-np)^{3}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-np)^{4}+np(X-np)^{3}\right]=np(1-p)(1+3(n-2)p(1-p))+(np)^{2}(1-p)(1-2p).

We plug all of the above expectations into (
180
), yielding

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[X\log X\right]\leq

\displaystyle np\log np+\frac{np(1-p)}{np}-\frac{np(1-p)(1-2p)+(np)^{2}(1-p)}{2(np)^{2}}

(182)

\displaystyle+\frac{np(1-p)(1+3(n-2)p(1-p))+(np)^{2}(1-p)(1-2p)}{3(np)^{3}}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{X(X-np)^{4}}{4\xi^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle np\log np+\frac{1-p}{2}+\frac{(1-p)(5-4p)}{6np}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{X(X-np)^{4}}{4\xi^{4}}\right].

Following a similar argument as proved in Lemma
20
, we are going to show that the expectation term scales as
O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})
. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{X(X-np)^{4}}{4\xi^{4}}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{X(X-np)^{4}}{4\xi^{4}}\right|X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]+\Pr\left[X\leq\frac{1}{2}np\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{X(X-np)^{4}}{4\xi^{4}}\right|X\leq\frac{1}{2}np\right]

(183)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{X(X-np)^{4}}{4\xi^{4}}\right|X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]+o(\frac{1}{n^{2}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{(np)^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.X(X-np)^{4}\right|X>\frac{1}{2}np\right]+o(\frac{1}{n^{2}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}p^{2}n}}\frac{4}{(np)^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[X(X-np)^{4}\right]+o(\frac{1}{n^{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}p^{2}n}}\frac{4}{(np)^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[X(X-np)^{4}\right]+o(\frac{1}{n^{2}})

\displaystyle=\Theta(\frac{1}{n^{2}}),

where

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[X(X-np)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-np)^{5}\right]+np{\mathbb{E}}\left[(X-np)^{4}\right]

(184)

\displaystyle=np(1-p)(1-2p)(1+2p(1-p)(5n-6))+np(1-p)(1+3(n-2)p(1-p))

\displaystyle=\Theta(n^{2}).

Combining this result with (
182
) completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 25
.

If
X
is a binomial random variable following
X\sim\Binomial(n,p)
, then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\begin{pmatrix}n\\
X\end{pmatrix}\right]=nH(p)-\frac{1}{2}\log n+O\left(1\right).

(187)

Proof.

First, by Lemma
24
, the lower bound of the expected binomial coefficient can be calculated as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\begin{pmatrix}n\\
X\end{pmatrix}\right]\geq

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\log n-\log X-\log\bar{X}-\log 8\right)+nH(\frac{X}{n})\right]

(188)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log n-3\log 2-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\log X+\log\bar{X}\right)+X\log\frac{X}{n}+\bar{X}\log\frac{\bar{X}}{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log n-3\log 2-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\log X+\log\bar{X}\right)+X\log X+\bar{X}\log\bar{X}-X\log n-\bar{X}\log n\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log n-3\log 2-\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\log np-\frac{1-p}{2np}+\log n(1-p)-\frac{p}{2n(1-p)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\right.

\displaystyle+n(1-p)\log n(1-p)+\frac{p}{2}+\frac{p(1+4p)}{6n(1-p)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle-np\log n-n(1-p)\log n\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log np(1-p)-3\log 2-\frac{1}{2}-np\log p-n(1-p)\log(1-p)+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle nH(p)-\frac{1}{2}\log n-\frac{1}{2}\log p(1-p)-3\log 2-\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Likewise, the upper bound can be obtained as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\begin{pmatrix}n\\
X\end{pmatrix}\right]\leq nH(p)-\frac{1}{2}\log n-\frac{1}{2}\log p(1-p)-\log 2\pi-\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

(189)

Combining the lower and upper bounds completes the proof. ∎

\displaystyle\gen={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(W-Z\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(W-Z_{i}\right)^{2}\right],

’s. Collecting all the facts that

MI Case

We first evaluate the MI bound in (
5

W=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}

w
and

and
Z_{[n]}

\displaystyle P_{W,Z_{[n]}}(\frac{x}{n},z_{[n]})=p^{x}(1-p)^{n-x},

By definition of mutual information,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{x=0}^{n}B_{n,x}(p)\log B_{n,x}(p).

plugging which back to the standard MI bound yields

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-\frac{1}{2n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\begin{pmatrix}n\\

O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
.

E-E

LOO-CMI Case

Under this case, the learner is simplified as
W=\frac{1}{n}(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\hat{Z}_{i}-\hat{Z}_{U})
, and by Bayes rule we get

\displaystyle P_{U|W,\hat{Z}_{[n+1]}}(u|w,\hat{z}_{[n+1]})=\frac{P_{W|\hat{Z}_{[n+1]},U}(w|\hat{z}_{[n+1]},u)P_{\hat{Z}_{[n+1]}}(\hat{z}_{[n+1]})P_{U}(u)}{\sum_{u}P_{W,\hat{Z}_{[n+1]},U}(w,\hat{z}_{[n+1]},u)}.

(210)

For
w=\frac{x}{n}
, it is easy to verify that the number of
1
’s in
\hat{z}_{[n+1]}
is either
x
when
\hat{z}_{u}=0
or
x+1
when
\hat{z}_{u}=1
since otherwise
P_{W|\hat{Z}_{[n+1]},U}
is zero. When
\hat{z}_{u}=0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\hat{z}_{i}=x
, we have

\displaystyle P_{U|W,\hat{Z}_{[n+1]}}(u|\frac{x}{n},\hat{z}_{[n+1]})

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{n+1}p^{x}(1-p)^{n+1-x}}{\frac{n+1-x}{n+1}p^{x}(1-p)^{n+1-x}}

(211)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1-x}.

Likewise, when
\hat{z}_{u}=1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\hat{z}_{i}=x+1
, it holds that:

\displaystyle P_{U|W,\hat{Z}_{[n+1]}}(u|\frac{x}{n},\hat{z}_{[n+1]})=\frac{1}{x+1}.

(212)

For
w=\frac{x}{n}
, combining (
211
) and (
212
) gives

\displaystyle P_{U|W,\hat{Z}_{[n+1]}}(u|\frac{x}{n},\hat{z}_{[n+1]})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{n+1-x},&\text{if $\hat{z}_{u}=0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\hat{z}_{i}=x$}\\
\frac{1}{x+1},&\text{if $\hat{z}_{u}=1$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\hat{z}_{i}=x+1$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(213)

By definition, we compute the mutual information as

\displaystyle I\left(W;U|\hat{Z}_{[n+1]}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{W,\hat{Z}_{[n+1]},U}\left[\log\frac{P_{U|W,\hat{Z}_{[n+1]}}}{P_{U}}\right]

(214)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{n}\frac{n+1-x}{n+1}B_{n+1,x}(p)\log\frac{n+1}{n+1-x}+\sum_{x=0}^{n}\frac{x+1}{n+1}B_{n+1,x+1}(p)\log\frac{n+1}{x+1}

\displaystyle=\log(n+1)-\sum_{x=0}^{n}B_{n,x}(p)\left((1-p)\log(n+1-x)+p\log(x+1)\right).

Let
X
be a binomial random variable
\Binomial(n,p)
, then the above mutual information can be rewritten as

\displaystyle I\left(W;U|\hat{Z}_{[n+1]}\right)=\log(n+1)-{\mathbb{E}}\left[(1-p)\log(n+1-X)+p\log(X+1)\right].

(215)

Using the approximations in Lemma
20
, we get

\displaystyle I\left(W;U|\hat{Z}_{[n+1]}\right)=

\displaystyle\log(n+1)-p\left(\log(np+1)-\frac{np(1-p)}{2(np+1)^{2}}\right)

(216)

\displaystyle-(1-p)\left(\log(n+1-np)-\frac{np(1-p)}{2(n+1-np)^{2}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\log\frac{n+1}{np+1}+(1-p)\log\frac{n+1}{(1-p)n+1}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}

\displaystyle-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(p)+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

where in
(a)
we apply Lemma
23
. Finally, it is shown that the LOO bound converges to a constant
\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}H(p)}
.

ICIMI Case

In this subsection, we evaluate the ICIMI bound in (

), where the hypothesis is
W=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{Z}_{i+R_{i}n}
. Let
w=\frac{x}{n}
,
r_{[n]}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
and
\hat{z}_{[2n]}=(\hat{z}_{1},\ldots,\hat{z}_{2n})
be the realizations, where

. First, we calculate the conditional probability as

\displaystyle P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(\frac{x}{n}|r_{i},\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n})=

\displaystyle B_{n-1,x-\hat{z}_{i+r_{i}n}}(p).

(217)

By marginalizing out
R_{i}
, we get

\displaystyle P_{W|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(\frac{x}{n}|\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n})=

\displaystyle P_{R_{i}}(0)P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(\frac{x}{n}|0,\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n})+P_{R_{i}}(1)P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(\frac{x}{n}|1,\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n})

(218)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}B_{n-1,x-\hat{z}_{i}}(p)+\frac{1}{2}B_{n-1,x-\hat{z}_{i+n}}(p).

Then, the joint probability is given by

\displaystyle P_{W,R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(\frac{x}{n},r_{i},\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n})=

\displaystyle P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(\frac{x}{n}|r_{i},\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n})P_{R_{i}}(r_{i})P_{\hat{Z}_{i}}(\hat{z}_{i})P_{\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(\hat{z}_{i+n})

(219)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}B_{n-1,x-\hat{z}_{i+r_{i}n}}(p)p^{\hat{z}_{i}}(1-p)^{1-\hat{z}_{i}}p^{\hat{z}_{i+n}}(1-p)^{1-\hat{z}_{i+n}}.

\displaystyle I\left(W;R_{i}|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{w,r_{i},\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n}}P_{W,R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w,r_{i},\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n})\log\frac{P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|r_{i},\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n})}{P_{W|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|\hat{z}_{i},\hat{z}_{i+n})}

\displaystyle\sum_{w}P_{W,R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w,0,0,0)\log\frac{P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|0,0,0)}{P_{W|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|0,0)}+\ldots

\displaystyle+\sum_{w}P_{W,R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w,0,1,1)\log\frac{P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|0,1,1)}{P_{W|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|1,1)}

Notice the following facts:
(a)
whenever

(b)
for

,

\displaystyle\sum_{w}P_{W,R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w,0,0,1)\log\frac{P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|0,0,1)}{P_{W|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|0,1)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}p(1-p)B_{n-1,x}(p)\log\frac{2}{\frac{x}{n-x}\cdot\frac{1-p}{p}+1}

(221)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{w}P_{W,R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w,1,1,0)\log\frac{P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|1,1,0)}{P_{W|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|1,0)},

(c)
for
(R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n})=(0,1,0)
and
(R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n})=(1,0,1)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{w}P_{W,R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w,0,1,0)\log\frac{P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|0,1,0)}{P_{W|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|1,0)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}p(1-p)B_{n-1,x}(p)\log\frac{2}{\frac{n-1-x}{x+1}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}+1}

(222)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{w}P_{W,R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w,1,0,1)\log\frac{P_{W|R_{i},\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|1,0,1)}{P_{W|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}}(w|0,1)}.

\displaystyle I\left(W;R_{i}|\hat{Z}_{i},\hat{Z}_{i+n}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}p(1-p)B_{n-1,x}(p)\log\frac{2}{\frac{x}{n-x}\cdot\frac{1-p}{p}+1}+\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}p(1-p)B_{n-1,x}(p)\log\frac{2}{\frac{n-1-x}{x+1}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}+1}

\displaystyle 2p(1-p)\log 2-\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}p(1-p)B_{n-1,x}(p)\log\left[\left(\frac{x}{n-x}\cdot\frac{1-p}{p}+1\right)\left(\frac{n-1-x}{x+1}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}+1\right)\right]

\displaystyle p(1-p)\left(2\log 2-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\left(\frac{X}{n-X}\cdot\frac{1-p}{p}+1\right)\left(\frac{n-1-X}{X+1}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}+1\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle p(1-p)\left(2\log 2-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\alpha X}{\bar{X}+1}+1\right)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\beta\bar{X}}{X+1}+1\right)\right]\right),

\alpha>1
,
\beta<1

\displaystyle p(1-p)\left(2\log 2-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\frac{(\alpha-1)X+n}{\bar{X}+1}+\log\frac{n-(1-\beta)\bar{X}}{X+1}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\log(1-\beta)+\log\left(\frac{n}{1-\beta}-\bar{X}\right)-\log\left(X+1\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle p(1-p)\left(2\log 2-\log\left((\alpha-1)(1-\beta)\right)-\log\left((n-1)p+\frac{n}{\alpha-1}\right)+\frac{(n-1)(1-p)p}{2\left((n-1)p+\frac{n}{\alpha-1}\right)^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\log\left((n-1)(1-p)+1\right)-\frac{(n-1)(1-p)p}{2\left((n-1)(1-p)+1\right)^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\log\left((n-1)p+1\right)-\frac{(n-1)(1-p)p}{2\left((n-1)p+1\right)^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(1-p)\left(2\log 2-\log\left((\alpha-1)(1-\beta)\right)+\log\frac{(n-1)(1-p)+1}{(n-1)p+\frac{n}{\alpha-1}}+\log\frac{(n-1)p+1}{\frac{n}{1-\beta}-(n-1)(1-p)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}

\displaystyle p(1-p)\left(2\log 2-\log\left((\alpha-1)(1-\beta)\right)+\log\frac{1-p}{p+\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}+\log\frac{p}{\frac{1}{1-\beta}-(1-p)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(1-p)\left(2\log 2+\log\frac{1-p}{p(\alpha-1)+1}+\frac{p}{1-(1-p)(1-\beta)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(1-p)\left(2\log 2+\log\frac{1-p}{2-2p}+\log\frac{p}{2p}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

where
(a)
is obtained by using Lemma
20
and Lemma
21
to each summand,
(b)
is due to Lemma
23
. After taking the square root of

.
