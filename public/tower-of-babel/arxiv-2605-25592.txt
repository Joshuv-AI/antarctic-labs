Optimal Design for Multinomial Logit Model with Applications to Best Assortment Identification
Joongkyu Lee, Min-hwan Oh

Abstract
We study optimal experimental design for multinomial logit (MNL) bandits, where an agent repeatedly selects a subset of K K items from a ground set of size N N and observes single-choice feedback. Unlike linear or generalized linear bandits, MNL bandits have a combinatorial action space, which makes classical optimal design approaches and naive optimization over all subsets computationally intractable. We propose a computationally efficient optimal design framework for MNL models that achieves both statistical efficiency and scalability through two complementary approaches: (i) an exact or certified-approximate reformulation of the design oracle as a 0 0 – 1 1 mixed-integer linear program (MILP) with solver-certified early stopping, and (ii) a fully polynomial-time lifted design that replaces the nonlinear objective with a tractable surrogate. Using the Kiefer–Wolfowitz equivalence theorem, we establish near G-optimality guarantees and characterize the induced statistical–computational trade-offs. As an application, we develop a best assortment identification algorithm for MNL bandits with linear utilities and non-uniform revenues, and prove an instance-dependent sample complexity of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d ​ log ⁡ N Δ 2 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\frac{d\log N}{\Delta^{2}}\big) , where d d is the feature dimension, N N is the number of arms, and Δ \Delta is the minimum revenue gap.

Introduction

We study optimal experimental design in multinomial logit (MNL) models
(
32
)
with linear utilities.
The MNL model is a fundamental discrete choice model widely used in economics, marketing, and machine learning, where outcomes are generated from subset-dependent multinomial distributions parameterized by latent utilities.
For instance, in online advertising, a user selects (e.g., clicks) one item from a subset of
K
ads presented on a page.
Each ad can be represented by high-dimensional features, such as image or text embeddings produced by an off-the-shelf neural network.
This structured feedback setting naturally arises in large-scale applications, where the total number of available items
N
can be very large.

From an experimental design perspective, this setting poses significant challenges.
Unlike linear or generalized linear models—where the design space consists of individual arms—
the design space in MNL models is
combinatorial
: each experiment corresponds to selecting a subset of arms, and the number of feasible experiments grows on the order of
N^{K}
.
Moreover, even for a fixed subset, the information induced by the MNL model is fundamentally different from that in linear or generalized linear models.
In particular, the Fisher information is matrix-valued, depends nonlinearly on the underlying parameter, and is determined by the entire subset through MNL choice probabilities, rather than decomposing additively across individual arms.
As a result, classical optimal design theory
(
25
;
39
)
—which relies on additive, per-arm information contributions—does not directly apply.

In this paper, we develop an optimal design framework for MNL models with combinatorial experiments that addresses the structural and computational challenges described above.
Our approach builds on
locally
optimal design principles to handle the inherent nonlinearity of the MNL model, and leads to a Frank–Wolfe algorithm
(
16
;
11
)
whose main computational challenge is a linear maximization oracle (LMO) over a
combinatorial
collection of feasible subsets.
To address this bottleneck, we propose two complementary approaches:
(i) an
exact

0
–
1
mixed-integer linear program (MILP) reformulation of the LMO, allowing
early stopping
with guaranteed accuracy up to a user-specified tolerance, and
(ii) a
polynomial-time
relaxation based on a Schur-complement lifting of the MNL Fisher information with a bounded approximation error.

As an application of our efficient optimal design framework, we study
best assortment identification
in MNL bandits with linear utilities.
In this fixed-confidence setting, an agent sequentially offers assortments (i.e., subsets of arms) and observes a single MNL choice outcome, with the goal of identifying the unique revenue-maximizing assortment
S^{\star}
with high probability while minimizing the total number of interactions.
Leveraging a
G
-optimal design computed at a nominal parameter, we propose an algorithm,
BSI-MNL
, that combines an initial exploration phase (which ensures the nominal parameter is uniformly close to the true parameter), design-based sampling, and a principled stopping rule.
We prove an instance-dependent sample complexity bound of

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(d\log(N/\delta)\big(\Delta_{\min}^{-2}+(\kappa\Delta_{\min})^{-1}\big)\right)
,
which in the small-gap regime simplifies to the leading-order guarantee

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\big(\frac{d\log N}{\Delta_{\min}^{2}}\big)
.
To the best of our knowledge, this yields the first sample complexity guarantee for best assortment identification in MNL bandits with linear utilities, while remaining computationally efficient via either MILP-based early stopping or the polynomial-time lifted relaxation.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

Exact MILP reformulation:

To address the primary computational bottleneck of the Frank–Wolfe (FW) algorithm—the linear maximization oracle (LMO) defined in (
3
)—we show that the optimization problem admits an
exact
reformulation as a
0
–
1
mixed-integer linear program (Theorem
3.3
).
This reformulation allows the LMO to be solved using off-the-shelf MILP solvers, which either return an exact solution or terminate early with a user-specified, solver-certified optimality gap.
Moreover, we show that the FW algorithm retains its
\epsilon
-optimality guarantee even with certified inexact LMO solutions, at the cost of a small additional number of FW iterations (Proposition
3.4
).

•

Polynomial-time lifted surrogate:

As an alternative approach, we propose a novel lifted surrogate based on a Schur-complement construction.
This relaxation yields a ratio-of-sums objective that can be optimized
exactly
in polynomial time.
The resulting design trades statistical efficiency for computational scalability, while incurring only a bounded and explicitly characterized approximation error (Theorem
3.5
).

•

Best assortment identification in MNL bandits:

Leveraging the optimal design, we propose
BSI-MNL
for best assortment identification in MNL bandits, and establish an instance-dependent sample complexity of

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\big(\frac{d\log N}{\Delta_{\min}^{2}}\big)
,
where
d
is the feature dimension,
N
is the number of arms, and
\Delta_{\min}
is the minimum revenue gap (Theorem
4.4
).
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first sample complexity guarantee for best assortment identification in MNL bandits with linear utilities and non-uniform revenue parameters.
Moreover, the algorithm remains computationally efficient, either via MILP-based early stopping with certified gaps or via a fully polynomial-time lifted relaxation.

3.2
MNL Model and Locally Optimal Design

MNL model and loss function.

We consider the problem of optimal experimental design under the multinomial logit (MNL) model
(
32
)
.
Let
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathbf{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote a finite set of
N
arms, where each arm
i\in[N]
is represented by a
d
-dimensional feature vector
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}
denote the collection of feasible subsets of arms with maximum size
K
.
At each step
t
, the learner selects a subset
S_{t}\in{\mathcal{S}}
with
2\leq|S_{t}|\leq K
.
1
1

1

For a singleton subset, the choice probability is identically
1
; hence, we exclude this trivial case in this section.

Conditional on
S_{t}
, the probability of choosing arm
i\in S_{t}
follows the MNL model:

\displaystyle p(i|S_{t},\bm{\theta}^{\star}):=\frac{\exp\!\big(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{\star}\big)}{\sum_{j\in S_{t}}\exp\!\big(\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{\star}\big)},

(1)

where
\bm{\theta}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
unknown
underlying parameter.
Note that when
K=2
, the MNL model reduces to the Bradley–Terry–Luce (BTL) model
(
6
)
.

At step
t
, let
\mathbf{y}_{t}:=(y_{ti})_{i\in S_{t}}\in\{0,1\}^{|S_{t}|}
denote the one-hot choice feedback vector, where
y_{ti}=1
indicates that arm
i\in S_{t}
is selected. Then

\Pr(y_{ti}=1|S_{t})=p(i|S_{t},\bm{\theta}^{\star}),

where
i\in S_{t},

and

\sum_{i\in S_{t}}y_{ti}=1.

Now, we define the MNL loss function at step
t
as:

\displaystyle\ell_{t}(\bm{\theta}):=-\sum_{i\in S_{t}}y_{ti}\log p(i|S_{t},\bm{\theta}).

Then, the Fisher information matrix
(
15
)
is defined as the expected Hessian of the negative log-likelihood:

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}}(S_{t})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}_{t}}\!\left[\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\bm{\theta})\,\middle|\,S_{t}\right]=\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in S_{t}}p(i|S_{t},\bm{\theta})\,\big(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\bm{\theta}}(S_{t})\big)\big(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\bm{\theta}}(S_{t})\big)^{\top}\!,

where
\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\bm{\theta}}(S_{t})\!:=\!\sum_{j\in S_{t}}p(j|S_{t},\bm{\theta})\,\mathbf{a}_{j}

denotes the mean feature vector under the MNL choice probabilities.
The second equality holds because, under the MNL model, the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood,

\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\bm{\theta})
, does not depend on the
realized
choice feedback.

Throughout the paper, we assume that the pairwise feature differences span the

d
-dimensional space:

Assumption 3.1

(Spanning condition)
.

We assume that
\operatorname{span}\big\{\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{j}:i,j\in[N],\,i\neq j\big\}=\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This assumption holds
without loss of generality
: if the span were a strict subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we could restrict
\bm{\theta}
to the identifiable subspace (and redefine
d
accordingly),
so that Assumption
3.1
holds throughout, without changing the MNL probabilities (see Appendix
A
for more details).

Locally G-optimal design.

We focus on solving the
G-optimal design
problem
(
25
;
39
)
under the MNL model, in which the set of feasible assortments is combinatorial and has cardinality on the order of
\mathcal{O}(N^{K})
.
Let
\pi:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow[0,1]
denote a probability distribution over feasible assortments.
Given any fixed parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
—hence the term
locally
optimal design—our goal is to find

\pi^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\pi}\,g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
, where

\displaystyle g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)=\max_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\,\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\right)\!.

(2)

Here,
\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi}[\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)]
denotes the expected Fisher information matrix induced by the design
\pi
.
Note that, since the objective in (
2
) does not depend on the realized feedback, algorithms based on the optimal design
\pi^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}
are non-adaptive.

It is well known that solutions to the optimal design problem are characterized by the Kiefer–Wolfowitz (KW) equivalence theorem
(
25
;
27
)
.
However, whereas the classical KW theorem is formulated in terms of feature vectors, we consider a matrix-valued information form
\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Therefore, we establish the KW equivalence theorem for the MNL model, building on the matrix-valued KW theorem of
33

Proposition 3.2

(KW equivalence theorem for MNL)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
,
the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\pi^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}
is a minimizer of
g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
, defined in (
2
).

(b)

\pi^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}
is a maximizer of
f_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)=\log\operatorname{det}\!\left(\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)\right)
.

(c)

g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}})=d
.

Furthermore, there exists a minimizer
\pi_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}^{\star}
of
g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
such that
|\operatorname{supp}(\pi_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}^{\star})|\leq d(d+1)/2
.

Proposition
3.2
shows the equivalence between G-optimality and D-optimality under the MNL model, and further guarantees the existence of an optimal design with finite support.
The proof of Proposition
3.2
is provided in Appendix
B.3

Early stopping with
\epsilon_{\mathrm{LMO}}
-optimality.

Although the resulting MILP is NP-hard in general, modern MILP solvers based on

(ii) a provable bound on how good any solution can possibly be.
From these two quantities, the solver reports a

at runtime,
which upper-bounds the remaining suboptimality of the current incumbent solution
(the best feasible solution).
Therefore, the LMO computation can be safely terminated once this solver-certified gap
drops below a user-specified tolerance
\epsilon_{\mathrm{LMO}}
.
This yields an
instance-dependent
and
verifiable

early-stopping
rule:
the returned LMO solution comes with an explicit certificate of near-optimality,
providing a principled trade-off between computational cost and user-specified
optimization accuracy.

When the MILP-based LMO is solved approximately, the solver returns a candidate set

3.5
Polynomial-Time Surrogate LMO via Schur-Complement Lifting

In this subsection, we develop a lifting-based reformulation of the MNL Fisher information that removes the set-dependent centering term
\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{S}(\bm{\theta})
and enables an efficient surrogate linear maximization oracle (LMO).
Throughout, we fix a nominal parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and write
p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\!(i|S):=p(i|S,\bm{\theta}_{0})
for brevity.

Schur-complement lifting.

For each arm
i\in[N]
, define the lifted feature vector
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\!:=\!(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\!,1)^{\top}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and, f
or each assortment

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(i|S)\,\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}.

(4)

Let

\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(i|S)\,\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}
.
Then
\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)
defined in (

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)&\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\\
\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix},

(5)

and the (centered) MNL Fisher information satisfies

\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\!=\!\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\!-\!\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)^{\top}
​,
which is precisely the Schur complement of the bottom-right block of (

3
3

3

For a block matrix

In particular, the dependence of

Given a design

\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi}\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\right]=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)&\bar{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)\\
\bar{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix},

where

\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\!(\pi)\!:=\!\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\big]

\bar{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\!(\pi)\!:=\!\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\big].

Moreover, we define its Schur-complement matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi):=\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)-\bar{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)\bar{\mathbf{b}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

and the associated
design-mismatch
matrix

\Delta_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi):=\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)-\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi).

We call
\Delta_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
a
mismatch
matrix because
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
is the Schur complement of the lifted design matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
, whereas
\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
is the
true
(centered) Fisher design matrix in the original space.
Thus,
\Delta_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
quantifies the discrepancy between the Schur-complement surrogate induced by lifting and the original design matrix.

Lifted G-optimal design.

The lifting in (
4
) naturally induces a
lifted
G-optimal design criterion.
For any design
\pi\in\Delta({\mathcal{S}})
, define

\widetilde{g}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)\;:=\;\max_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\ \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\Big(\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)^{-1}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\Big).

We then define the
lifted optimal design
as any minimizer

\widetilde{\pi}^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\pi\in\Delta({\mathcal{S}})}\widetilde{g}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi).

We solve this lifted design problem
by running Frank–Wolfe with
\widetilde{g}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}
as the objective.
See Algorithm
B.2
for a full description.

Polynomial-time lifted LMO.

At iteration
m
of Algorithm
B.2
, given the current design
\pi_{m}
and

\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{m}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi_{m})
, we compute the next FW atom by solving

S_{m}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\ \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{m}^{-1}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\right).

(6)

This lifted oracle admits a ratio-of-sums representation and can be solved in polynomial time.
Indeed, letting
s_{i}:=\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{m}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}

and
w_{i}:=\exp(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0})
, for every
S\in{\mathcal{S}}
we have

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{m}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\right)=\sum_{i\in S}p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(i\mid S)\,s_{i}=\frac{\sum_{i\in S}w_{i}\,s_{i}}{\sum_{j\in S}w_{j}}.

Consequently, (
6
) is a classical MNL ratio-of-sums assortment optimization
problem and can be reduced to a linear program (LP) and solved in time
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(N))

(e.g.,
40
;
9
).

Let
\pi^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\pi\in\Delta({\mathcal{S}})}g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
be an original
G-optimal design.
Under the Kiefer–Wolfowitz equivalence (Proposition
3.2
),
g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi^{\star}(\bm{\theta}_{0}))=d
.
Similarly, under the lifted criterion, one typically has

g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\widetilde{\pi}^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}})=d+1
(reflecting the lifted dimension).

Theorem 3.5

(Lifted FW guarantee)
.

Fix
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and run Frank–Wolfe (Algorithm
1
) to minimize

\widetilde{g}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi)
using the polynomial-time lifted LMO (
6
).
Let
\hat{\pi}
be the design returned at the stopping time

\widetilde{g}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\ \leq\ (1+\epsilon)(d+1)
, and define

\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}:=\inf\big\{\varepsilon\geq 0:\ \Delta_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\preceq\varepsilon\,\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\big\}
.
Then

g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\ \leq\ 2(1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}})(1+\epsilon)d.

Computational–statistical trade-off.

Using the lifted LMO yields a polynomial-time Frank–Wolfe update.
The trade-off is a potential mismatch between the lifted surrogate and the true Fisher information, quantified by
\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}
.
Accordingly, the
G
-optimality guarantee degrades by at most a factor
(1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}})
.
A crude upper bound is
\mathcal{O}\big(1/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\widetilde{\pi}^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}))\big)
(Proposition
B.4
).
In the standard MNL bandit setting with an outside option (see Equation (
7
)),
we also obtain the bound

\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}\!=\!\mathcal{O}\big(Ke^{B}\!\wedge\!1/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\widetilde{\pi}^{\star}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}))\big),

when
\|\bm{\theta}_{0}\|\leq B
(Proposition
B.5
).
The proof of Theorem
3.5
is provided in Appendix
B.5
.

b

in MNL bandits,
where the abbreviation BSI is used throughout to avoid confusion with best arm identification (BAI).
Following the previous literature
(
5
;
4
;
37
;
7
;
35
;
36
;
38
;
3
;
28
;
29
)
,
we adopt the MNL choice model with an
outside option
(corresponding to not choosing any item in the offered assortment
S
), where the choice probabilities are given by

\displaystyle p(i|S,\bm{\theta}^{\star}):=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{\star})}{1+\sum_{j\in S}\exp(\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{\star})},\qquad\forall i\in S,

(7)

and
p(0|S,\bm{\theta}^{\star}):=1-\sum_{i\in S}p(i|S,\bm{\theta}^{\star})

Here
0

We define the expected revenue of an assortment
S

R(S,\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i\in S}p(i|S,\bm{\theta})r_{i}=\frac{\sum_{i\in S}\exp(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{\star})r_{i}}{1+\sum_{j\in S}\exp(\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{\star})},

where
r_{i}\in[0,1]
is a given revenue parameter.
Let
S^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}R(S,\bm{\theta}^{\star})
denote the optimal assortment and assume the maximizer is unique.
Given a failure probability
\delta>0
,
our goal is to interact with the environment until a (possibly random) stopping time
\tau

\hat{S}
such that

\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}=S^{\star}\wedge\tau<\infty)\geq 1-\delta.

This setting is known as the fixed-confidence setting
(
17
)
,
where the objective is to identify the optimal assortment with as few samples as possible.

We work under the standard boundedness assumption.

Assumption 4.1
.

For all
i\in[N]

\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{2}\!\leq\!1
, and
\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}\|_{2}\!\leq\!B

38

28

\kappa:=\min_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\min_{i\in S}p(i|S,\bm{\theta}^{\star})\,p(0|S,\bm{\theta}^{\star}),

(8)

which measures the degree of deviation from the linear model.
Since
1/\kappa=\Theta(K^{2}e^{3B})
grows exponentially in
B
,
it is crucial to derive bounds that do not depend on
1/\kappa
.

Confidence bound.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{t}\!=\!\{(i_{s},S_{s})\}_{s=1}^{t}

We define the cumulative loss as:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\ell_{s}(\bm{\theta})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\bm{\theta}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\text{where }\,\lambda>0.

We also define Hessian matrix at
\bm{\theta}

\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\nabla^{2}\ell_{s}(\bm{\theta})+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d}.

\sqrt{\log N}

Lemma 4.2

Define

We now describe our algorithm for BSI (Algorithm
2
).

Step 1: Initial exploration.

In Algorithm
2
, we begin with a pure exploration phase that continues until the burn-in condition
of Lemma
4.2
is met.
The purpose of this phase is to ensure that the uncertainty term is uniformly controlled over all arms.
Since the true parameter
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
is unknown, we cannot directly verify the condition
in (
9
).
Instead, we use the fact that

\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})\succeq\kappa\,\mathbf{V}_{t}
, where

\mathbf{V}_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{i\in S_{s}}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d}
.
Therefore, it suffices to ensure that
\kappa^{-1/2}\max_{i\in[N]}\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{V}_{t}^{-1}}
is uniformly bounded:

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[N]}\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{V}_{t}^{-1}}\leq\frac{\kappa^{1/2}}{256}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d\log(N/\delta)}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}\,B}\Big)=:\zeta_{w}.

Step 2: Fixing nominal parameter.

After the initial exploration phase, we estimate a nominal parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
using an

independent
feedback dataset
\mathcal{D}_{w}^{\prime}
.
During the initial exploration, each selected assortment
S_{t}
is offered twice, yielding two independent
choice outcomes
i_{t}
and
i_{t}^{\prime}
.
These outcomes are stored in two separate datasets,

\mathcal{D}_{w}:=\{(S_{s},i_{s})\}_{s=1}^{t_{w}}
and

\mathcal{D}_{w}^{\prime}:=\{(S_{s},i_{s}^{\prime})\}_{s=1}^{t_{w}}
, which are conditionally independent given the offered assortments.
We then compute the nominal parameter as the regularized MLE, i.e.,

\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}_{w}^{\prime}}(\bm{\theta}).

By construction,
\bm{\theta}_{0}
depends only on
\mathcal{D}_{w}^{\prime}
and is therefore independent of the main dataset
\mathcal{D}_{w}

used in the subsequent phase.
This independence is essential for satisfying the conditional independence requirement in
Lemma
4.2
when we later sample assortments according to the design computed at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

Finally, by running the warm-up phase for a sufficient number of rounds, we ensure that the nominal parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is uniformly close to
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
, which in turn implies that
p(i|S,\bm{\theta}_{0})\approx p(i|S,\bm{\theta}^{\star})
for all
S
and
i
(see Equation
C.10
).

Step 3: Computing a
G
-optimal design at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

Given the nominal parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, we compute a (near)
G
-optimal design
\widehat{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}

by running the Frank–Wolfe algorithm (Algorithm
1
).
The design can be computed either using a
MILP solver
(Subsection
3.4
)
or via a relaxed but polynomial-time
lifted G-optimal design
formulation (Subsection
3.5
).

Step 4: Main rounds and stopping rule.

Given the design
\widehat{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}
, we run the main rounds by repeatedly sampling assortments

S_{t}\sim\widehat{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}
and observing the corresponding choice feedback.
At each time
t
, we form the optimistic (
u^{+}_{t,i}
) and pessimistic (
u^{-}_{t,i}
) utilities

u^{\pm}_{t,i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\pm\sqrt{2}\,\beta(\delta)\,\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}},

and define the corresponding optimistic/pessimistic expected revenues for any
S\in{\mathcal{S}}
as

\widetilde{R}_{t}(S):=\frac{\sum_{i\in S}e^{u^{+}_{t,i}}r_{i}}{1+\sum_{j\in S}e^{u^{+}_{t,j}}},\quad\widecheck{R}_{t}(S):=\frac{\sum_{i\in S}e^{u^{-}_{t,i}}r_{i}}{1+\sum_{j\in S}e^{u^{-}_{t,j}}}.

We then select the pessimistic best assortment and the optimistic best alternative as

S^{\mathrm{best}}_{t}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\widecheck{R}_{t}(S),\qquad S^{\mathrm{alt}}_{t}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}:\,S\neq S^{\mathrm{best}}_{t}}\widetilde{R}_{t}(S).

Remark 4.3

(Polynomial-time assortment selection)
.

Note that each of these optimization problems can be solved in polynomial time in
N

(
40
;
9

, analogously to (

The only subtlety is the constraint
S\neq S^{\mathrm{best}}_{t}
in the definition of
S^{\mathrm{alt}}_{t}
.
This can be enforced exactly with at most
K

i\in S^{\mathrm{best}}_{t}
, solve the same maximization problem under the extra constraint
i\notin[N]
, and then take the best solution among these
K

Finally, we stop at the first time
\tau
such that

\widecheck{R}_{\tau}(S^{\mathrm{best}}_{\tau})>\widetilde{R}_{\tau}(S^{\mathrm{alt}}_{\tau}),

(10)

and output
S^{\mathrm{best}}_{\tau}
.
In Lemma
C.7

S^{\mathrm{best}}_{\tau}=S^{\star}

4.2
Sample Complexity

We define the (instance-dependent) expected revenue gap

\Delta_{\min}:=R(S^{\star},\bm{\theta}^{\star})-\max\{R(S,\bm{\theta}^{\star})\!:\!S\in{\mathcal{S}},\ S\neq S^{\star}\}.

We now establish an instance-dependent upper bound on the sample complexity of
Algorithm
2
.

Theorem 4.4

(Sample complexity, MILP solver)
.

Let
\delta\in\!(0,1]
,

\lambda\!=1
, and

T_{0}\!=\!\widetilde{\Omega}\big(\kappa^{-1}d^{2}\log(N/\delta)\big)
.
Suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds.
Then,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,
Algorithm
2
, when implemented with the MILP solver described in Subsection
3.4
, returns the optimal assortment
S^{\star}
,
and
the stopping time
\tau
satisfies

\displaystyle\tau=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(d\log\!\left(N/\delta\right)\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\kappa\,\Delta_{\min}}\right)+T_{0}\right)\!.

Discussion of Theorem
4.4
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first sample complexity bound for best assortment identification in MNL bandits, particularly in the presence of non-uniform revenue parameters.
For sufficiently small
\Delta_{\min}
, the sample complexity is dominated by the
\Delta_{\min}^{-2}
term, yielding the leading-order bound

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\frac{d\log N}{\Delta_{\min}^{2}}\big)
.
This matches the existing lower bound
\Omega\big(\frac{d}{\Delta_{\min}^{2}}\big)
(Theorem 3 of
18
), up to a logarithmic factor in
N
.
In particular, in this small-gap regime, the bound is independent of
1/\kappa
, which can be exponentially large, i.e.,
1/\kappa=\mathcal{O}(K^{2}e^{3B})
.
The proof is provided in Appendix
C
.

Corollary 4.5

(Sample complexity, Lifted G-optimal)
.

Under the setting as in Theorem
4.4
, suppose we employ the lifted G-optimal design described in Subsection
3.5
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\tau=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left((1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}})d\log\!\left(N/\delta\right)\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\kappa\,\Delta_{\min}}\right)+T_{0}\right)\!,

where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}
denotes the approximation error induced by the lifted G-optimal design (see Theorem
3.5
).

Discussion of Corollary
4.5
.

When we employ the lifted
G
-optimal design to improve computational efficiency, particularly in large-scale regimes with large
N
and
K
, the sample complexity incurs an additional multiplicative factor of
(1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}})
.
By Proposition
B.5
, this lifting error satisfies
\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}=\mathcal{O}(Ke^{B})
.
For comparison, a naive application of optimal design based on
\mathbf{V}_{t}
, which ignores local Fisher information and
therefore admits only polynomial-time computation (cf.
18
),
yields a sample complexity of

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\frac{d\log N}{\kappa^{2}\Delta_{\min}^{2}}\big)
.
Since
1/\kappa^{2}=\mathcal{O}(K^{4}e^{6B})
, this bound can be substantially worse.
In contrast, Corollary
4.5
achieves a better worst-case statistical efficiency, by a factor of

K^{3}e^{5B}
, while retaining polynomial-time computation.

Remark 4.6

(Relation to item-level gaps)
.

Our gap
\Delta_{\min}
is an assortment-level global gap between the optimal and second-best assortments, whereas the
\Delta_{i}
quantities in
46
and
24
are item-level separation metrics in MAB settings.
In particular,
46
defines
\Delta_{i}
using the best revenue among assortments constrained to include or exclude item
i
, for which one has
\Delta_{i}\geq\Delta_{\min}
; however, their complexity scales as
\widetilde{O}(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{i}^{-2})
, while ours scales as
\widetilde{O}(\Delta_{\min}^{-2})
, so the two bounds are not directly comparable: theirs uses a finer item-wise hardness measure, whereas ours uses a single global assortment-level gap, and neither uniformly dominates the other based on the gap term alone. Meanwhile,
24
defines
\Delta_{i}
through the advantage score
\eta_{i}=(r_{i}-\theta_{v})v_{i}
and its separation from the top-
K
decision boundary; hence, their gap is not based on constrained assortment revenues, but on item-wise margins relative to the advantage-score boundary, capturing a different notion of hardness from
\Delta_{\min}
and making term-by-term comparison again inappropriate.

5
Numerical Experiments

We empirically evaluate the performance of
BSI-MNL
by measuring the sample complexity up to the stopping time.
All results are averaged over
10
independent problem instances, and we report the mean together with the standard deviation.
In each instance, the true parameter
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
is drawn uniformly from the
d
-dimensional Euclidean ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}(B)
of radius
B
, and the feature vectors
\{\mathbf{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are sampled independently from the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}(1)
.
The revenue parameters are independently sampled as
r_{i}\!\sim\!\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)
for all
i\!\in\![N]
.

To implement the Frank–Wolfe (FW) procedure (Algorithm
1
) within
BSI-MNL
, we consider two variants that differ in how the linear minimization oracle (LMO) is computed.
The first variant, denoted
BSI-MNL-MILP
, solves the LMO by reformulating the original combinatorial optimization problem as a mixed-integer linear program and uses the Gurobi solver
(
19
)
, as described in Subsection
3.4
.
The second variant, denoted
BSI-MNL-Lift
, replaces the original LMO with a Schur-complement–based lifted surrogate, which admits polynomial-time computation; see Subsection
3.5
for details.
Since this work is, to our knowledge, the first to study best assortment identification with provable guarantees, there is no existing algorithm that serves as a direct baseline.
Instead, we include an oracle version of our algorithm that computes the LMO exactly via
brute-force
enumeration, denoted
BSI-MNL-Oracle
.

In the FW procedure, we use a stopping accuracy of
\epsilon=0.1
. For the MILP-based variant,
BSI-MNL-MILP
, the certified optimality gap of the linear minimization oracle is set to
\epsilon_{\mathrm{LMO}}=0.1
.
For the confidence radius
\beta(\delta)
and the warm-up threshold
\zeta_{w}
, we use scaled-down versions of their theoretical expressions by a factor of
10
, as the original theoretical values are overly conservative in practice and lead to unnecessarily long warm-up phases and delayed stopping times.
Throughout, we set
d\!=\!5
,
B\!=\!1
, and
\delta\!=\!0.05
.

Appendix A
Justification of Assumption
3.1
(Without Loss of Generality)

In this appendix, we justify that Assumption
3.1
can be imposed without loss of generality.
Recall that under the MNL model, for any assortment
S\subseteq[N]
and parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the choice probability of item
i\in S
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle p(i|S,\bm{\theta})=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta})}{\sum_{j\in S}\exp(\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta})}.

(A.1)

Identifiable subspace.

Define the subspace generated by all pairwise feature differences as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\;:=\;\operatorname{span}\big\{\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{j}:\ i,j\in[N],\,i\neq j\big\}\;\subseteq\;\mathbb{R}^{d}.

If
\mathcal{U}\neq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then there exists a nontrivial orthogonal complement

\mathcal{U}^{\perp}:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ v^{\top}u=0,\forall v\in\mathcal{U}\}
.
For any
u\in\mathcal{U}^{\perp}
and any
i,j\in[N]
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{j})^{\top}u=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}u=\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}u.

Hence,
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}u
is constant over
i\in[N]
. In particular, fixing any
i_{0}\in[N]
and letting

c(u):=\mathbf{a}_{i_{0}}^{\top}u
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}u=c(u),\qquad\forall i\in[N],\ \forall u\in\mathcal{U}^{\perp}.

(A.2)

Decomposition of the parameter.

Decompose the parameter as
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}}+\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}}
, where

\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}}\in\mathcal{U}^{\perp}
.
By (
A.2
), there exists a scalar
c(\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}})\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}}=c(\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}}),\qquad\forall i\in[N].

Therefore, for any
i\in S
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}}+\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}}=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}}+c(\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}}),

(A.3)

where the additive term
c(\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}})
does not depend on
i
.

Invariance of MNL probabilities.

Plugging (
A.3
) into (
A.1
), we obtain, for any
S
and
i\in S
,

\displaystyle p(i|S,\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}})\exp(c(\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}}))}{\sum_{j\in S}\exp(\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}})\exp(c(\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}})}{\sum_{j\in S}\exp(\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}})}\;=\;p(i|S,\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}}).

(A.4)

Thus, the MNL choice probabilities depend only on the projection
\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}}
onto the
difference-span subspace
\mathcal{U}
, and the component
\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}^{\perp}}
is not identifiable
from choice data.

Without loss of generality.

Let
d^{\prime}:=\dim(\mathcal{U})
and fix any orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{U}
to represent parameters
in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
. Since (
A.4
) shows that replacing
\bm{\theta}
by
\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{U}}

leaves all MNL probabilities unchanged, we may restrict the parameter space to
\mathcal{U}

(and redefine
d\leftarrow d^{\prime}
) without loss of generality.
Under this reparameterization, the spanning condition in Assumption
3.1
holds by construction.

Proof of Theorem

In this subsection, we provide the proof of Theorem

, showing that the LMO optimization problem in (

) reduces to a

Fix the current Frank–Wolfe iterate
\pi_{m}
and let

\mathbf{M}_{m}:=\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\pi_{m})\succ 0
.
Recall the definition of MNL choice probabilities (at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
):

\displaystyle p(i|S,\bm{\theta}_{0}):=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0})}{\sum_{j\in S}\exp(\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0})},\qquad\forall i\in S,

and the corresponding Fisher information matrix:

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}p(i|S,\bm{\theta}_{0})\,\big(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\big)\big(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\big)^{\top},\qquad\text{where }\,\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{0}):=\sum_{j\in S}p(j|S,\bm{\theta}_{0})\,\mathbf{a}_{j}.

Then, the linear maximization oracle (LMO) selects an extreme point supported on a single assortment

\displaystyle S_{m}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\ \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\Big(\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\Big).

Binary encoding.

We represent an assortment
S
by a binary vector
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, where

x_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{i\in S\}
.
This encoding ensures that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}=|S|
, where
2\leq|S|\leq K
.
For a simple presentation, we define

\displaystyle w_{i}:=\exp(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0})>0,\qquad w:=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{N})^{\top},\qquad W(x):=w^{\top}x=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}x_{i}.

Then, for any

, the MNL choice probability can be written as

\displaystyle p(i|S,\bm{\theta}_{0})=\frac{w_{i}x_{i}}{W(x)}.

Moreover, the mean feature vector satisfies

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\sum_{j\in S}p(j|S,\bm{\theta}_{0})\,\mathbf{a}_{j}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}\mathbf{a}_{j}}{W(x)}.

Reformulation as a
0
–
1
QFIP.

Using the definition of

\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\Big(\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in S}p(i|S,\bm{\theta}_{0})\,\underbrace{\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{i}}_{=:s_{i}}\;-\;\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\overbrace{w_{i}s_{i}}^{=:r_{i}}}{W(x)}\;-\;\frac{\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}x_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\Big)^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}\mathbf{a}_{j}\Big)}{W(x)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{\top}x}{W(x)}\;-\;\frac{\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}x_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\Big)^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}\mathbf{a}_{j}\Big)}{W(x)^{2}},

(B.1)

where we defined

\displaystyle s_{i}:=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{i},\qquad r_{i}:=w_{i}s_{i},\qquad r:=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})^{\top}.

Define the symmetric matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
by
G_{ij}:=w_{i}w_{j}\,\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{j}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}x_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\Big)^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}\mathbf{a}_{j}\Big)=x^{\top}Gx.

Moreover, since

) becomes

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\Big(\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\Big)=\frac{(w^{\top}x)(r^{\top}x)-x^{\top}Gx}{(w^{\top}x)^{2}}.

(B.2)

\displaystyle(w^{\top}x)(r^{\top}x)=x^{\top}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}(wr^{\top}+rw^{\top})\Big)x.

Hence, by defining

\displaystyle A:=-\tfrac{1}{2}(wr^{\top}+rw^{\top})+G,\qquad B:=ww^{\top},

we can rewrite (

) as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\Big(\mathbf{M}_{m}^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\Big)=-\frac{x^{\top}Ax}{x^{\top}Bx}.

Therefore, the LMO in (
3

0
–
1

\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\ \,-\frac{x^{\top}Ax}{x^{\top}Bx}=\min_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\ \,\frac{x^{\top}Ax}{x^{\top}Bx},\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad 2\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\leq K.

(B.3)

QFIP
\Rightarrow

Let
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

M_{A}:=\max_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|A_{ij}|,\qquad M_{B}:=\max_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j=1}^{N}B_{ij}.

For any

(Ax)_{i}\in[-M_{A},M_{A}]

\tilde{A}x:=Ax+M_{A}\mathbf{1}\in[0,2M_{A}]^{N}.

Introduce auxiliary vectors

\displaystyle\tilde{A}x=y^{0}+y^{1},\qquad 0\leq y^{0}\leq 2M_{A}(\mathbf{1}-x),\qquad 0\leq y^{1}\leq 2M_{A}x.

These constraints enforce

when

and

\mathbf{1}^{\top}y^{1}=x^{\top}\tilde{A}x=x^{\top}Ax+M_{A}\mathbf{1}^{\top}x\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^{\top}Ax=\mathbf{1}^{\top}y^{1}-M_{A}\mathbf{1}^{\top}x.

Similarly, since

has nonnegative entries, we have
Bx\in[0,M_{B}]^{N}

Introduce

\displaystyle Bx=z^{0}+z^{1},\qquad 0\leq z^{0}\leq M_{B}(\mathbf{1}-x),\qquad 0\leq z^{1}\leq M_{B}x.

Therefore, (

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N},\;y^{0},y^{1},z^{0},z^{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}y^{1}-M_{A}\mathbf{1}^{\top}x}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}z^{1}}

\displaystyle Ax+M_{A}\mathbf{1}=y^{0}+y^{1},

\displaystyle 0\leq y^{0}\leq 2M_{A}(\mathbf{1}-x),

\displaystyle 0\leq y^{1}\leq 2M_{A}x,

\displaystyle Bx=z^{0}+z^{1},

\displaystyle 0\leq z^{0}\leq M_{B}(\mathbf{1}-x),

\displaystyle 0\leq z^{1}\leq M_{B}x,

\displaystyle 2\leq\mathbf{1}^{\top}x\leq K.

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}U^{1}=1,

B.2
Tightening big-
M
constants in (
B.6
)–(
B.7
)

The proof in Appendix
B.1
provides an
exact
reformulation of the LMO subproblem as a
0
–
1
MILP, using the bounds
M_{A}
,
M_{B}
, and
\bar{\alpha}
, which are usually referred to as big-
M
constants
(
44
;
45
)
.
In practice, however, the runtime of MILP solvers—through the strength of the LP relaxation and the effectiveness of branching—is often highly sensitive to how tight these bounds are.
Motivated by this, we derive tighter, problem-specific bounds that retain exact equivalence while typically leading to substantial computational speedups.

Throughout, we only require bounds that are valid over the feasible set

\{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}:2\leq\mathbf{1}^{\top}x\leq K\}
.
Replacing
(M_{A},M_{B},\bar{\alpha})
by any
smaller
constants/vectors that remain valid upper bounds
does not remove any feasible
(x,y^{0},y^{1},z^{0},z^{1})
that corresponds to a feasible
x
of the original QFIP,
and therefore preserves the exact equivalence between (
B.3
), (
B.6
), and (
B.7
).
We now describe concrete tighter choices.

B.2.1
Tighter bounds for
(Ax)_{i}
exploiting
\mathbf{1}^{\top}x\leq K

The coarse bound
M_{A}:=\max_{i}\sum_{j}|A_{ij}|
ignores sparsity.
Since any feasible
x
has at most
K
ones, we can tighten the bound on each coordinate
(Ax)_{i}
.

For each row
i\in[N]
, let
\{|A_{ij}|\}_{j=1}^{N}
be sorted in nonincreasing order:

|A_{i(1)}|\geq|A_{i(2)}|\geq\cdots\geq|A_{i(N)}|.

Define the
K
-aware row bound

M_{A,i}^{(K)}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}|A_{i(\ell)}|\qquad\text{and}\qquad M_{A}^{(K)}:=\max_{i\in[N]}M_{A,i}^{(K)}.

Then for any feasible
x
with
\mathbf{1}^{\top}x\leq K
,

|(Ax)_{i}|=\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}x_{j}\Big|\leq\sum_{j:x_{j}=1}|A_{ij}|\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}|A_{i(\ell)}|=M_{A,i}^{(K)}\leq M_{A}^{(K)}.

Option 1: minimal-change replacement (scalar bound).

One may simply replace
M_{A}
by
M_{A}^{(K)}
in (
B.4
) and everywhere thereafter:

\tilde{A}x:=Ax+M_{A}^{(K)}\mathbf{1}\in[0,2M_{A}^{(K)}]^{N},\qquad 0\leq y^{0}\leq 2M_{A}^{(K)}(\mathbf{1}-x),\quad 0\leq y^{1}\leq 2M_{A}^{(K)}x,

and the identity becomes

x^{\top}Ax=\mathbf{1}^{\top}y^{1}-M_{A}^{(K)}\mathbf{1}^{\top}x.

All subsequent steps remain unchanged with this substitution.

Option 2: Stronger replacement (row-wise bound).

A further tightening uses row-dependent shifts.
Let
m_{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
be given by
(m_{A})_{i}:=M_{A,i}^{(K)}
and define

\tilde{A}x:=Ax+m_{A}\in[0,\,2m_{A}]\quad\text{(componentwise)}.

Then replace (
B.4
) by

\displaystyle\tilde{A}x=y^{0}+y^{1},\qquad 0\leq y^{0}\leq 2\,m_{A}\odot(\mathbf{1}-x),\qquad 0\leq y^{1}\leq 2\,m_{A}\odot x,

(B.8)

where
\odot
denotes elementwise product.
The same logic yields

\mathbf{1}^{\top}y^{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}(\tilde{A}x)_{i}=x^{\top}(Ax+m_{A})=x^{\top}Ax+m_{A}^{\top}x,\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^{\top}Ax=\mathbf{1}^{\top}y^{1}-m_{A}^{\top}x.

Consequently, the LFIP objective in (
B.6
) becomes

\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}y^{1}-m_{A}^{\top}x}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}z^{1}}\quad\text{(instead of $\frac{\mathbf{1}^{\top}y^{1}-M_{A}\mathbf{1}^{\top}x}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}z^{1}}$).}

All other constraints remain identical after replacing
M_{A}
by
m_{A}
componentwise in the upper bounds.

B.2.2
Tighter bounds for
(Bx)_{i}
exploiting
B=ww^{\top}
and
\mathbf{1}^{\top}x\leq K

The coarse bound
M_{B}:=\max_{i}\sum_{j}B_{ij}
is also loose.
Here we can exploit the rank-one structure

(Bx)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{i}w_{j}x_{j}=w_{i}\underbrace{\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}\Big)}_{=:W(x)}=w_{i}\,W(x).

Let
w_{[1]}^{\downarrow}\geq\cdots\geq w_{[N]}^{\downarrow}
be the weights sorted in decreasing order and define

Then for any feasible

0\leq W(x)\leq W_{\max}^{(K)}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad 0\leq(Bx)_{i}=w_{i}W(x)\leq w_{i}W_{\max}^{(K)}.

Define the componentwise bound vector

by

(m_{B})_{i}:=w_{i}W_{\max}^{(K)}.

Then one may replace (
B.5
) by the tighter, componentwise constraints

\displaystyle Bx=z^{0}+z^{1},\qquad 0\leq z^{0}\leq m_{B}\odot(\mathbf{1}-x),\qquad 0\leq z^{1}\leq m_{B}\odot x.

(B.9)

Exactly as before, these enforce
\mathbf{1}^{\top}z^{1}=x^{\top}Bx
.
The same componentwise replacements propagate through the Charnes–Cooper step:
after scaling
(U^{0},U^{1})=\alpha(z^{0},z^{1})
and

, we obtain

0\leq U^{0}\leq m_{B}\odot(\alpha\mathbf{1}-v),\qquad 0\leq U^{1}\leq m_{B}\odot v

M_{B}
.

B.2.3
Tighter upper bound on
\alpha
exploiting
\mathbf{1}^{\top}x\geq 2

In the proof above we used

to deduce

\bar{\alpha}=1/(4w_{\min}^{2})
.
This can be tightened by using the exact minimal possible sum of two weights.

Let
w_{(1)}^{\uparrow}\leq w_{(2)}^{\uparrow}\leq\cdots\leq w_{(N)}^{\uparrow}
be the weights sorted in increasing order and define

W_{\min}^{(2)}:=w_{(1)}^{\uparrow}+w_{(2)}^{\uparrow}.

Then the constraint
\mathbf{1}^{\top}x\geq 2
implies
w^{\top}x\geq W_{\min}^{(2)}
, and therefore

x^{\top}Bx=(w^{\top}x)^{2}\geq\big(W_{\min}^{(2)}\big)^{2},\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\alpha=\frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}z^{1}}=\frac{1}{x^{\top}Bx}\leq\frac{1}{\big(W_{\min}^{(2)}\big)^{2}}.

Hence one may replace the coarse upper bound

\bar{\alpha}=1/(4w_{\min}^{2})
by the tighter

\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{tight}}:=\frac{1}{\big(W_{\min}^{(2)}\big)^{2}}.

This directly strengthens the big-
M
linearization of

v_{i}\leq\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{tight}}\,x_{i},\qquad v_{i}\geq\alpha-\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{tight}}(1-x_{i}),\qquad\forall i\in[N].

,
one can use
W_{\min}^{(L_{\min})}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{\min}}w_{(\ell)}^{\uparrow}
and obtain

\bar{\alpha}_{\mathrm{tight}}=1/\big(W_{\min}^{(L_{\min})}\big)^{2}
.

\pi=\hat{\pi}
and let
\varepsilon=\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}
, where by definition

\Delta_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\preceq\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}\,\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})
.
Applying (
B.18

g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\ \leq\ (1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}})\big(\widetilde{g}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})-1\big).

Finally, the stopping rule
\widetilde{g}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\leq(1+\epsilon)(d+1)
implies

\widetilde{g}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})-1\ \leq\ (1+\epsilon)(d+1)-1\ =\ (1+\epsilon)d+\epsilon,

and hence

g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\ \leq\ (1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}})\big((1+\epsilon)d+\epsilon\big)\ \leq\ 2(1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}})(1+\epsilon)d,

which complete the proof of Theorem

.
∎

B.6

Proposition B.4

(Crude bound for
\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}
)
.

If
\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\succeq\lambda_{0}\mathbf{I}_{d}
for some
\lambda_{0}>0
, then

\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}\|\Delta_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}.

Proof of Proposition
B.4
.

The proof is direct:

\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}\leq\|\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\Delta_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}\,\|\Delta_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\hat{\pi})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition B.5

(Crude bound for
\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}
under the outside-option MNL model)
.

Under the outside-option MNL model at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, the lifting error at the returned design
\hat{\pi}
satisfies

\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}\ \leq\ \sup_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}\frac{1-p_{0}(S)}{p_{0}(S)}\ =\ \sup_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}\sum_{j\in S}\exp(\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}).

In particular, if
|S|\leq K
for all
S\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
\max_{j\in[N]}\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\leq B
, then

\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}\ \leq\ Ke^{B}.

Proof of Proposition
B.5
.

For any
S\in{\mathcal{S}}
, define
w_{i}:=\exp(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0})
and the outside-option probabilities

p_{0}(S):=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j\in S}w_{j}},\qquad p_{i}(S):=\frac{w_{i}}{1+\sum_{j\in S}w_{j}}\quad(i\in S).

Let

\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}p_{i}(S)\mathbf{a}_{i},\qquad\bar{\mathbf{A}}(S):=\sum_{i\in S}p_{i}(S)\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{I}(S):=\bar{\mathbf{A}}(S)-\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)^{\top}\succeq 0.

Moreover, we define

\mathbf{M}(\hat{\pi}):=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}[\mathbf{I}(S)],\qquad\Delta(\hat{\pi}):=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi}\left[\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi}\left[\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)\right]\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\pi}\left[\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)\right]^{\top}\succeq 0,

and

\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}:=\inf\big\{\varepsilon\geq 0:\ \Delta(\hat{\pi})\preceq\varepsilon\,\mathbf{M}(\hat{\pi})\big\}.

Then, by the PSD Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)^{\top}=\Big(\sum_{i\in S}p_{i}(S)\mathbf{a}_{i}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i\in S}p_{i}(S)\mathbf{a}_{i}\Big)^{\top}\preceq\Big(\sum_{i\in S}p_{i}(S)\Big)\Big(\sum_{i\in S}p_{i}(S)\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\Big)=(1-p_{0}(S))\,\bar{\mathbf{A}}(S).

Rearranging yields
\mathbf{I}(S)=\bar{\mathbf{A}}(S)-\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)^{\top}\succeq p_{0}(S)\,\bar{\mathbf{A}}(S)
, hence

\bar{\mathbf{A}}(S)\preceq\frac{1}{p_{0}(S)}\mathbf{I}(S)
and therefore

\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)^{\top}\ \preceq\ (1-p_{0}(S))\bar{\mathbf{A}}(S)\ \preceq\ \frac{1-p_{0}(S)}{p_{0}(S)}\,\mathbf{I}(S).

Since
\Delta(\hat{\pi})=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}[\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}[\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)]\Big(\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}[\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)]\Big)^{\top}\!\!\preceq\,\mathbb{E}[\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)\bar{\mathbf{a}}(S)^{\top}]
, it follows that

\Delta(\hat{\pi})\ \preceq\ \mathbb{E}_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}\Big[\frac{1-p_{0}(S)}{p_{0}(S)}\,\mathbf{I}(S)\Big].

Let
\rho(S):=\frac{1-p_{0}(S)}{p_{0}(S)}
. Then

\Delta(\hat{\pi})\ \preceq\ \mathbb{E}[\rho(S)\mathbf{I}(S)]\ \preceq\ \Big(\sup_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}\rho(S)\Big)\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{I}(S)]=\Big(\sup_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}\rho(S)\Big)\,\mathbf{M}(\hat{\pi}),

which implies
\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}\leq\sup_{S\sim\hat{\pi}}\rho(S)
by the definition of
\epsilon_{\mathrm{lift}}
.
The specialization follows from
\rho(S)=\sum_{j\in S}\exp(\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0})\leq|S|e^{B}\leq Ke^{B}
.
Combining this with Proposition
B.4
, we conclude the proof.
∎

, we have

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\bar{\lambda}_{t}}{t-T_{0}}\max_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\big(\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}})^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\bar{\lambda}_{t}}{t-T_{0}}g_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\widehat{\pi}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}})=\frac{96(1+\epsilon)d\log(2t)}{t-T_{0}},

which proves the claim (i).

To prove the second claim, it suffices to upper bound

\max_{i\in[N]}\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}}^{2}
by an expression in trace form.
Fix any
i\in[N]
, let
S^{\prime}=\{i\}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(i|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{0})p(0|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{0})}{p(i|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{0})p(0|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{0})}\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e\frac{p(i|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{0})p(0|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{0})}{p(i|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}^{\star})p(0|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}^{\star})}\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}}^{2}

(Eqn. (
C.10
))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e}{\kappa}p(i|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{0})p(0|S^{\prime},\bm{\theta}_{0})\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}}^{2}

(Definition of
\kappa
, Eqn. (
8
))

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\kappa}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e}{\kappa}\max_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(S)\right).

Therefore, by applying claim (i) and taking the maximum over
i\in[N]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[N]}\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{\mathbf{H}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{96e(1+\epsilon)d\log(2t)}{\kappa(t-T_{0})}.

Finally, since

\sum_{t=T_{0}+1}^{\infty}\delta_{t}=\frac{6\delta}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}=\delta
,
a union bound over all
t>T_{0}

shows that (i) and (ii) hold simultaneously for all
t>T_{0}
with probability at least

1-\delta
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
C.7
.

We prove the lemma by contradiction.

and suppose that

.
On the event
\mathcal{E}
,

C.10
,
we have

\displaystyle R(S^{\star},\bm{\theta}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{R}_{\tau}(S^{\star})\leq\max_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}:\,S\neq S^{\mathrm{best}}_{\tau}}\widetilde{R}_{\tau}(S)=\widetilde{R}_{\tau}(S^{\mathrm{alt}}_{\tau})

\displaystyle<\widecheck{R}_{\tau}(S^{\mathrm{best}}_{\tau}),

))

Lemma C.14

(Matrix Chernoff bound for independent PSD matrices)
.

Let
\{X_{s}\}_{s=1}^{t}
be independent random matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that

X_{s}\succeq\mathbf{0}

and

\|X_{s}\|\leq L

almost surely
for some constant
L>0
.
Define

\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}
and

\mathbf{H}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}]
.
Fix any
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Assume that
t\geq\frac{6}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}
and choose

\bar{\lambda}=\frac{6L}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

(1-\gamma)\big(\mathbf{H}_{t}+\bar{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{d}\big)\;\preceq\;\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}+\bar{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{d}\;\preceq\;(1+\gamma)\big(\mathbf{H}_{t}+\bar{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{d}\big).

Proof of Lemma
C.14
.

We prove the lemma by reducing the problem to a standard matrix Chernoff bound
for sums of independent positive semidefinite (PSD) matrices with eigenvalues in
[0,1]
.

Define

X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}:=X_{s}+\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{t}\mathbf{I}_{d},\qquad Y:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}=\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}+\bar{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{d},\qquad\mu:=\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbf{H}_{t}+\bar{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{d}.

Then
X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}\succeq\mathbf{0}
and
\mu\succeq\bar{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{d}
, so
\bar{\lambda}_{\min}(\mu)\geq\bar{\lambda}
.
Moreover,

\|X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}\|\leq\|X_{s}\|+\bar{\lambda}/t\leq L+\bar{\lambda}/t\quad\text{a.s.}

Let

R_{\max}:=\max_{s\in[t]}\|X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}\|\leq L+\bar{\lambda}/t.

Since
\mu\succeq\bar{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{d}
, we have
\mathbf{I}_{d}\preceq\mu/\bar{\lambda}
. Hence, for every
s
,

\mathbf{0}\preceq X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}\preceq\|X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}\|\mathbf{I}_{d}\preceq R_{\max}\mathbf{I}_{d}\preceq\frac{R_{\max}}{\bar{\lambda}}\,\mu.

Define the normalized matrices

Z_{s}:=\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\,\mu^{-1/2}X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}\mu^{-1/2}.

Then
Z_{s}\succeq\mathbf{0}
and, using
X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}\preceq(R_{\max}/\bar{\lambda})\mu
,

Z_{s}\preceq\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\,\mu^{-1/2}\Big(\frac{R_{\max}}{\bar{\lambda}}\mu\Big)\mu^{-1/2}=\mathbf{I}_{d},

so
0\preceq Z_{s}\preceq\mathbf{I}_{d}
.
Furthermore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right]=\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\,\mu^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}\right]\mu^{-1/2}=\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\,\mathbf{I}_{d}.

This implies

\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right]\right)=\lambda_{\max}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right]\right)=\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}.

Applying the standard matrix Chernoff bound to the independent matrices
\{Z_{s}\}
with

0\preceq Z_{s}\preceq\mathbf{I}_{d}
, for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\lambda_{\min}\!\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right)\leq(1-\gamma)\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\cdot\exp\!\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\right),

(C.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\lambda_{\max}\!\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\right)\geq(1+\gamma)\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\cdot\exp\!\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{3}\cdot\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\right).

(C.15)

Therefore, if

\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\geq\frac{3}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta},

(C.16)

then each of (
C.14
)–(
C.15
) is at most
\delta/2
,
and by a union bound we obtain, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

(1-\gamma)\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\mathbf{I}_{d}\;\preceq\;\sum_{s=1}^{t}Z_{s}\;\preceq\;(1+\gamma)\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\mathbf{I}_{d}.

(C.17)

Multiply (
C.17
) on both sides by
(R_{\max}/\bar{\lambda})\mu^{1/2}(\cdot)\mu^{1/2}
gives

(1-\gamma)\mu\;\preceq\;\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}\;\preceq\;(1+\gamma)\mu.

Recalling
\sum_{s=1}^{t}X_{s}^{\bar{\lambda}}=\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}+\bar{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{d}
and
\mu=\mathbf{H}_{t}+\bar{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{d}

proves the statement provided (
C.16
) holds.

We now derive a sufficient condition on
t
under which (
C.16
) holds.
Since
R_{\max}\leq L+\bar{\lambda}/t
, it is sufficient to guarantee that

\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}=\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{L+\bar{\lambda}/t}\;\geq\;\frac{3}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}.

Choosing
\bar{\lambda}=\frac{6L}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}
and assuming
t\geq\frac{6}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}
, we obtain

L+\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{t}=L+\frac{\frac{6L}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}}{t}\;\leq\;L+\frac{\frac{6L}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}}{\frac{6}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}}=2L.

Therefore,

\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{R_{\max}}\;\geq\;\frac{\frac{6L}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}}{2L}=\frac{3}{\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2d}{\delta},

which establishes the desired condition.
∎

Lemma C.15

(Variance comparison under bounded likelihood ratios)
.

Let
p=(p_{i})_{i\in\Omega}
and
q=(q_{i})_{i\in\Omega}
be probability mass functions on a finite set
\Omega
,
and assume
q_{i}>0
for all
i
.
Fix
c\geq 1
and suppose that

\frac{1}{c}\;\leq\;\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\;\leq\;c\qquad\text{for all }i\in\Omega.

Then for any real-valued random variable
X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,

\Var\!\!\,_{p}(X)\;\leq\;c\,\Var\!\!\,_{q}(X).

Proof of Lemma
C.15
.

Write
\eta_{i}:=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}
, so that
\eta_{i}\in[1/c,c]
and
p_{i}=q_{i}\eta_{i}
.
Recall the variational characterization of the variance:
for any distribution
\nu
on
\Omega
,

\Var\!\!\,_{\nu}(X)\;=\;\min_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\;\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\big[(X-a)^{2}\big].

(C.18)

(Indeed,
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(X-a)^{2}]=\Var\!\!\,_{\nu}(X)+(a-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X])^{2}
, minimized at
a=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X]
.)

Fix any
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Using
p_{i}=q_{i}\eta_{i}
and
\eta_{i}\leq c
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}\big[(X-a)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\;=\ \sum_{i\in\Omega}p_{i}(X_{i}-a)^{2}\;=\;\sum_{i\in\Omega}q_{i}\eta_{i}(X_{i}-a)^{2}\;\leq\;c\sum_{i\in\Omega}q_{i}(X_{i}-a)^{2}

\displaystyle\;=\;c\,\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[(X-a)^{2}\big].

Taking
\min_{a\in\mathbb{R}}
on both sides and using (
C.18
) yields

\displaystyle\Var\!\!\,_{p}(X)\;=\;\min_{a}\mathbb{E}_{p}[(X-a)^{2}]\;\leq\;c\min_{a}\mathbb{E}_{q}[(X-a)^{2}]\;=\;c\,\Var\!\!\,_{q}(X).

This completes the proof.
∎
