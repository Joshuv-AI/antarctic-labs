Exercise-induced lysosomal adaptations in skeletal muscle
Authors: Anastasiya Kuznyetsova, David A. Hood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The adoption of a regular exercise program has immense benefits for whole body health, and for improving the quality of skeletal muscle. This is important as muscle is involved in metabolism, locomotion, and force production, making it a large contributor to the quality of life. The coordinated behavior of several intracellular organelles is responsible for the maintenance of skeletal muscle health, and these organelles are adaptable in response to both acute and chronic exercise. While the adaptations of mitochondria to exercise are well-established, potential alterations in muscle lysosomes are less appreciated. Lysosomes degrade and recycle debris during the terminal step of various forms of autophagy, such as mitophagy, the pathway involved in the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria. This lysosomal activity is important for the maintenance of cellular protein and organelle homeostasis. Recent work has shown that lysosome biogenesis begins with every acute bout of exercise, driven by the nuclear translocation of regulatory transcription factors such as TFEB and TFE3, which mediate the transcription of autophagy and lysosomal genes. These transcription factors also play a role in other pathways such as chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) and the regeneration of existing lysosomes through the autophagic-lysosome reformation (ALR) pathway. When performed repeatedly, acute bouts of exercise elicit a longer-term adaptive response, leading to the formation of active lysosomes, which increase lysosomal degradative capacity in skeletal muscle. This review addresses the current knowledge surrounding the effects of acute and chronic exercise on lysosomal adaptations in skeletal muscle, highlighting a novel pathway of muscle plasticity.

Introduction

Maintaining an active lifestyle through chronic exercise and physical activity has substantial benefits in promoting health as shown by reductions in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease,1 improvements in body kinetics, cardiovascular function and bone strength.2 Skeletal muscle is involved in exercise-mediated adaptations as it compromises approximately 40% of the total body mass and it mediates fundamental functions such as locomotion, force generation, and metabolic regulation.3 Skeletal muscle is highly adaptable to exercise, demonstrating enhanced mitochondrial bioenergetics when subject to endurance training.4 We are now beginning to recognize that there are many other organelles that contribute to the quality of skeletal muscle. These include lysosomes which are responsible for the degradation of cellular debris.5,6 In particular, lysosomes mediate the terminal step in mitophagy, the selective removal and degradation of mitochondria,6 and they are also involved in the degradation of other organelles as well. Lysosomes can also facilitate retrograde signaling pathways involved in the homeostasis of the cell. Interestingly, research is beginning to show that lysosomes can demonstrate a similar level of adaptive plasticity that is well-known to occur for mitochondria in response to exercise, aging and disuse. This review aims to provide an overview of the current literature surrounding the effects of exercise on lysosomes specifically, evaluating their contribution to skeletal muscle plasticity.

Structure and function of lysosomes

Lysosomes are organelles those are primarily known for their degradative functions in the terminal step of autophagy pathways, making them key regulators of cellular homeostasis. Proteins, lipids, and glycogen are removed via the autophagy system which exists in three distinct forms: macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy. In order to have efficient capacity to degrade and recycle cellular debris, lysosomes must have an intact organellar membrane and an appropriately acidic internal environment to house and facilitate the proper function of various hydrolases which directly breakdown cargo. A schematic of the basic structure of the lysosome is shown in Fig. 1B. The single membrane surrounding the lumen of the lysosome is composed of phospholipids and glycoproteins known as lysosomal membrane-associated proteins-1 and -2 (LAMP1, LAMP2), and lysosomal integral membrane protein-2 (LIMP2). The glycosylation status of these proteins is important to prevent their digestion from the hydrolases within the lysosomal lumen.7 There are more than 50 different enzymatic hydrolases within lysosomes, including proteases, phosphatases, lipases, and glycosidases.8 Proteases, specifically cathepsins, are the most abundant lysosomal hydrolases,9 making their function a major determinant of lysosomal health.10 Cathepsins and other hydrolases within the lysosome require an acidic environment with a pH of ∼4.5 in order to be functionally active. The vacuolar-type ATPase (vATPase) membrane protein pumps protons into the lysosomal lumen to maintain this essential acidic environment. There are many additional ion channels within lysosomal membranes, including the selective calcium ion channel known as mucolipin-1 (MCOLN1) which is involved in mediating signaling pathways, as discussed below (Fig. 1B).

The formation of a lysosome can occur through different pathways making its biosynthesis complex to follow. Lysosomal biogenesis is the formation of newly high functioning lysosomes, and the process is driven by the microphthalmia/transcription factor E (MiTF/TFE) family of transcription factors, including microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MiTF), transcription factor EB (TFEB), TFE3, and TFEC. These transcription factors can homo- or hetero-dimerize and bind to Coordinated Lysosomal Expression and Regulation (CLEAR) motifs in the promoters of genes encoding lysosomal and autophagy-related proteins.11 These gene products must be inserted into or through developing lysosomal membranes which form via maturation from endosomes. These are also vesicular-like organelles, although they have a higher internal pH of around 6 making their lumen unable to house functional lysosomal hydrolases. The process in which endosomes transition to lysosomes occurs through the switch from Rab5 to Rab7, GTPases that are found on the lysosomal membrane responsible for regulating this maturation. Rab5 is localized to the early endosomal membrane with the help of Rabex-5, a Rab5 guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF), promoting the formation and binding of active Rab5 to the lysosomal membrane. In a healthy cell where endosomes undergo maturation to lysosomes, Rab5 GTP hydrolysis occurs with the help of GTPase-activating proteins (GAP) to inactivate Rab5 and allow for recruitment of Rab7 to the membrane.12 Lysosomes can also be recycled and synthesized through the autophagic-lysosomal reformation (ALR) pathway, wherein existing lysosomes use molecular processes to pinch off fragments that ultimately grow and enlarge to form new lysosomes. This pathway is catalyzed by phosphatases and kinases such as inositol polyphosphate 5-phosphatase K (INPP5K) and phosphoinositide kinase, as well as FYVE-type zinc finger containing kinase (PIKfyve) that change the phosphorylation status of phosphatidylinositol's (PIPs), phospholipids that are a major component of the lysosomal membrane.13,14 The change in composition of the lysosomal membrane is then able to recruit fission proteins to the lysosomal membrane such as sorting nexin 2 (SNX2) as well as others to pinch off and form new lysosomes.15

Although lysosomes are primarily known for their role in the degradation of dysfunctional debris, they are also capable of initiating retrograde signaling pathways toward the nucleus to mediate autophagy. For example, the membrane channel MCOLN1 can open and release calcium from the lysosome into the cytosol to ultimately trigger lysosomal biogenesis. The release of calcium activates calcineurin (CaN), which is a phosphatase that dephosphorylates TFEB and TFE3, promoting their nuclear translocation to initiate the transcription of lysosomal-related genes. Mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) acts in an opposing manner, phosphorylating TFEB and TFE3 to cytosolically sequester them.16 An activation site for mTORC1 exists on lysosomes allowing it to sense excess nutrients such as amino acids, lipids and glucose that arise from macromolecular breakdown that activate mTORC1 to supress autophagy by phosphorylating TFEB and TFE3.17 Thus, lysosomes are involved in signaling pathways toward nuclear gene expression separate from their role as a degradative organelle.

Lysosomes within skeletal muscle

The discovery of hydrolytic enzymes led to an appreciation for the existence of abundant lysosomes within skeletal muscle. This occurred in the late 1960s using isolated subcellular fractions from muscle, some of which displayed the enzymatic activity of acid phosphatase and cathepsin D, both specific to the lysosomal lumen.18,19 In the 1970s and 1980s, it became clear that lysosomes played a distinct role in protein catabolism via the measurement of lysosomal enzymatic activity under conditions of muscle denervation or muscular dystrophy.20,21 This revealed that lysosomal activity is upregulated during states of atrophy. Evidence from early literature also showed that high intensity exercise sufficient to induce muscle injury led to changes in lysosomal activity. For example, mice subjected to treadmill running for 4 h and 9 h at a 10.5% incline and 14.5 m⋅min−1 speed, which is high intensity for mice, exhibited increased activities of cathepsin C and β-glucuronidase 3 days post-exercise, which returned back to basal levels after 9 days.22 This study interpreted the role of lysosomes as important for the regeneration of injured muscle. However, subjecting rats to 48 min of a 10% incline and 26.8 m⋅min−1 speed, a moderate intensity for rats, did not alter any lysosomal enzyme activity in the gastrocnemius muscle, indicating that more intense exercise may be required to elicit proteolytic activity.23 Based on these findings, it has been known for some time that lysosomes are able to adapt under different physiological conditions primarily through measurements of whole cell enzymatic activities. One of those physiological conditions involves the response to mechanical stress. As noted above, lysosomes also house the autophagy and protein synthesis regulatory enzyme mTORC1.24 The association of mTORC1 with lysosomal markers may be increased by mechanical stimulation.25 Evidence exists for further localization of mTORC1 to the lysosomal membrane with exercise in human muscle.26,27 These alterations are provoked by resistance exercise and likely contribute to the induction of muscle proteins synthesis, favouring a hypertrophy phenotype. More research on the upstream signaling toward mTOC1-lysosomal interactions appears warranted.

Macroautophagy is the process of removing and degrading large cytosolic cargo, and this is dependent on the presence and activity of lysosomes. When the process refers to the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria, it is termed mitophagy.28 The mitophagy pathway is an important component of overall mitochondrial quality control, which is comprised of biogenesis (i.e., synthesis), degradation (i.e., mitophagy) and “fine-tuning”, as determined by the activity of stress responsive pathways such as the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt).29 Mitochondrial biogenesis is largely driven by the transcription of almost 1 200 nuclear gene products that encode mitochondrial proteins. These are imported into pre-existing mitochondria to produce an expanded mitochondrial network within cells. Inevitably, when cellular conditions exist that promote mitochondrial dysfunction, evident from excessive reactive oxygen species emission or the loss of membrane potential, organellar fragments must undergo fission and encapsulation within autophagosomes for transport to lysosomes and subsequent degradation. Thus, lysosomes play a vital role in maintaining a healthy mitochondrial pool within all cell types. The tagging, removal and subsequent degradation of mitochondria is termed mitophagy flux (Fig. 2) and it can be measured by blocking autophagosome fusion with the lysosome using specific drugs such as Bafilomycin A in cell culture models, as well as colchicine or chloroquine in whole body analyses, and comparing the accumulation of mitophagic markers on mitochondrial membranes to vehicle-treated conditions. This allows for flux to be assessed up to the point of lysosome fusion.30,31 Furthermore, many fluorescent reporter systems exist such as mt-Keima mice in which the fluorescent Keima protein is conjugated to COX8, a protein located in the electron transport chain of mitochondria. When these mitochondria are within physiological pH (∼7), such as in the cytosol, they will fluoresce green, however when localized to the acidic environment of a lysosome (pH ∼4.5), they will fluoresce red indicating they are undergoing mitophagy.32 Immunofluorescence measurements are also useful in diagnosing mitophagy in muscle fibers to assess whether there is colocalization of mitochondria and lysosomes.33,34Fig. 2The role of the lysosome in mitochondrial quality control. Chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) is another form of autophagy where dysfunctional proteins with an exposed KFERQ motif are recognized by heat shock cognate protein 70 (HSC70), delivered to the lysosome, and translocated through the lysosomal membrane through the LAMP2A channel. Immunofluorescence can again be used to assess CMA using the colocalization of lysosomal markers such as LAMP1 with fluorescent proteins that have a KFERQ motif subject to different physiological conditions.35 Furthermore, various lysosomal proteins such as LAMP1, LAMP2, cathepsin B, cathepsin D, vATPase and MCOLN1 are in abundance in skeletal muscle and are detectable through immunoblotting. These measurements have served to identify lysosome plasticity in conditions such as chronic use or disuse.30,36, 37, 38 Recent work from our laboratory has also led to the development of a method to isolate purified lysosomal fractions from skeletal muscle and measure markers of lysosomal function such as cathepsin enzymatic activity and calcium signaling39 which should further advance our understanding of lysosomal function with exercise, aging and disease.

Exercise-mediated lysosomal adaptations

Skeletal muscle exhibits a high level of “plasticity” that can be evident under divergent conditions of exercise, training, disuse and disease.40 Foundational work by Holloszy in 1967 demonstrated that skeletal muscle mitochondria are adaptable under the influence of intense aerobic exercise.5 This discovery led to a cascade of research studying the effects of exercise on many sub-cellular adaptations within muscle, more recently including lysosomes. We now know that acute bouts of exercise lead to the initiation of signals within muscle cells that target lysosomal biogenesis pathways. Exercise produces a rise in cytosolic calcium, an increase in the AMP to ATP ratio as ATP undergoes rapid turnover, and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), both mitochondrially- and cytoplasmically-derived (Fig. 3). Regulators of lysosomal biogenesis: TFEB and TFE3

The CLEAR region within a promoter sequence is responsible for the binding of MiTF/TFE family members, of which there are four, and the subsequent transcription of lysosomal genes. Transcription factor EB (TFEB) and TFE3 are the most highly studied in skeletal muscle. These two transcription factors have typically been discussed together as their roles in lysosomal biogenesis have been perceived to be regulated by the same signaling mechanisms.16 However, research has now shown that TFEB and TFE3 play distinct roles within skeletal muscle, where TFEB is more involved in the biogenesis of autophagy and lysosomal proteins, while TFE3 may be more involved in the regulation of mitochondrial content. However, together, they are both very important for skeletal muscle adaptations.31

Acute exercise and TFEB/TFE3

Acute exercise leads to the nuclear translocation of TFEB and TFE3 within skeletal muscle.31,36,44, 45, 46 This can be initiated via the three acute exercise signaling pathways discussed above. The release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum or from the lysosome through the MCOLN1 channel can activate calcineurin, which dephosphorylates TFEB and TFE3 on Ser211 and Ser321, respectively. Under basal conditions, these amino acids are phosphorylated by mTORC1, thereby leading to their cytosolic sequestration.47 When dephosphorylated, TFEB and TFE3 translocate to the nucleus and upregulate lysosomal genes. The increase in the AMP/ATP ratio leads to the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) which can phosphorylate TFEB and TFE3 identically, on Ser466, Ser467, and Ser469, also promoting their nuclear translocation.48 Furthermore, in contracting muscle AMPK phosphorylates Raptor on Ser792 resulting in mTORC1 inhibition,49 reducing the role of mTORC1 in the cytosolic sequestration of TFEB and TFE3, as discussed above. In addition, the increase in cytosolically-generated ROS under acute exercise conditions50,51 can lead to cysteine oxidation of various proteins within the cell,52 including those involved in TFEB and TFE3 signaling. For example, protein kinase B (Akt) is implicated in the activation of mTORC1, promoting TFEB and TFE3 cellular sequestration. Research has shown that Akt is a major target of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) oxidation on exposed Cys60 and Cys77 residues during cellular stress, which can inhibit its activity.51 This inhibition of Akt can reduce mTORC1 activation, further allowing TFEB and TFE3 nuclear localization. Additionally, the promoter activity and mRNA levels encoding TFEB are increased after a single bout of exercise.36,44 Interestingly, this effect is dependent on the presence of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 alpha (PGC-1α), which is also upregulated during acute exercise.44 It is clear that the well-understood signals generated by acute exercise can upregulate and activate TFEB and TFE3, promoting autophagic and lysosomal biogenesis in skeletal muscle (Fig. 3).

Chronic exercise and TFEB/TFE3

The effects of chronic endurance exercise have not been as extensively studied as the immediate responses brought about by single bouts of exercise. Nonetheless, evidence exists for an adaptive response within muscle. For example, electrical stimulation-induced chronic contractile activity (CCA) in vivo and in vitro has been shown to upregulate TFEB and its downstream targets such as LAMP1, cathepsin D, as well as total lysosomal area in skeletal muscle.31,37,53 Furthermore, TFEB protein expression appears to increase in proportion to the length of time that muscle is subjected to chronic stimulation, representing a positive correlation.37 Chronic endurance exercise such as voluntary wheel running elicits modest increases in TFEB, the mature form of cathepsin D, and vATPase, however the effects are not as large as those shown in CCA studies.30 Additionally, intense treadmill training for 7 weeks increases nuclear TFEB in skeletal muscle.45 Another study demonstrated that chronic exercise using 12 weeks of treadmill training could restore TFEB nuclear translocation as well as LAMP1, cathepsin D, and cathepsin L back to control levels following a high-fat diet-fed induced decrease in lysosomal proteins.54 Other tissue types such as the brain have also been reported to exhibit upregulations in lysosomal markers after 8 weeks of exercise training in mice.55

It has been shown that TFEB and TFE3 are required for exercise adaptations in skeletal muscle. After chronic endurance training, C57BL/6J mice displayed typical adaptations such reduced muscle fatiguability, increases in mitochondrial content and respiration, mitophagy flux, and endurance capacity, as assessed using an acute bout of exercise on a treadmill. These positive outcomes were abolished in a TFE3 knockout (KO) mouse model.30,46 In a C2C12 myotube model of CCA, the knockdown of TFEB using siRNA resulted in a loss of the CCA-induced increase in lysosomal function evaluated using a DQ-BSA assay. Furthermore, a double knockdown of both TFEB and TFE3 reduced the adaptive increases in mitochondrial respiration brought about by CCA.31 Thus, although TFEB and TFE3 may have different roles, it is evident that they are both required and cannot compensate for each other for exercise training adaptations.

Other pathways are involved in the formation of lysosomes which have not yet been fully elucidated in skeletal muscle. Recently, research has shown an important role for the transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf-2) in mediating lysosome biogenesis.56, 57, 58 In addition, expression of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) has been shown to correlate with lysosomal content in skeletal muscle myotubes59 and is required for the age-related increase in lysosomes as seen in muscle specific ATF4 knockout mice (Sanfrancesco & Hood, Unpublished results). ATF4 is largely responsible for the activation of the integrated stress response in an attempt to restore homeostasis within muscle. Notably, acute exercise has been shown to activate signaling pathways leading to the nuclear translocation of ATF4, and over the long-term this would likely help to promote lysosomal biogenesis.60 However, more research in this area is required.

Finally, while lysosomal biogenesis itself is heavily regulated by TFEB and TFE3, the roles of other miTF family members such as MiTF and TFEC that can bind to the CLEAR motif remain unresolved. Research has shown that MiTF is expressed in skeletal muscle and is important for myotube formation,61 however has not been explored in the context of exercise or lysosomal adaptations.

Lysosome functions and adaptations to exercise

Proteolytic activity

Lysosome functionality is determined by how well the enzymes within the lumen are able to breakdown various debris. There are approximately 50 known hydrolases that exist within lysosomes. The abundance of these and their catalytic activities characterize how well the cell is able to degrade and recycle dysfunctional components.62 In particular, the abundance of the cathepsin family of enzymes plays a large role in determining the proteolytic activity of lysosomes.11 There is evidence that 4-5 days after an acute bout of exercise, there are increases in the enzymatic activity of various muscle hydrolases found within lysosomes such as arylsulphatase A, β-glucuronidase, N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase and cathepsin D.63,64 Some studies have also shown that cathepsin B and cathepsin D protein content are increased in skeletal muscle after a period of endurance training,30,65 but the effects of chronic exercise training on lysosomal enzymes are limited to date. However, there is evidence that the digestion of DQ-BSA, a fluorescent-tagged protein that emits green fluorescence when degraded within lysosomes, is increased after 4 days of chronic stimulation in C2C12 myotubes.31 Evidently, lysosomal proteolytic activity is responsive to exercise and further research is required to elucidate the mechanisms by which endurance exercise mediates these adaptations.

Macroautophagy and mitophagy

As noted above, macroautophagy (or more simply “autophagy”) is the general term for the catabolism of various cytosolic cargos, terminating within lysosomes. The levels of LC3-II and p62 within whole-cell or -tissue fractions can be indicative of overall autophagy within the cell. After endurance exercise training either using voluntary wheel running or swimming, basal autophagy flux was found to be upregulated in trained mice.66,67 This increase in basal autophagic activity in trained muscle would rely on higher lysosomal activity in order to maintain and facilitate the proper breakdown of debris targeted by macroautophagy for degradation. With respect to mitophagy, it has been shown that an acute bout of exercise is able to stimulate mitophagy flux.30,31 Exercise leads to an accumulation of various markers such as p62, LC3-II, PINK1 and Parkin in isolated skeletal muscle mitochondrial fractions, indicating that mitophagy flux is occurring.30,68 Lack of a mitophagy inhibitor results in reductions of whole muscle p62 levels due to its degradation within autophagolysosomes after a bout of exercise.36 In trained muscle, there is a higher basal level of Parkin, an essential protein for mitophagy, likely leading to a more responsive mitophagy response when required, resulting in a healthier mitochondrial pool. This aligns with data showing that after an acute bout of exercise, mitophagy flux is blunted in trained muscle as their basal mitophagic flux is upregulated.68,69 As lysosomes are the terminal step in macroautophagy and undergo training-mediated enhancements in their proteolytic activity, this would further improve the capacity for degradation of dysfunctional organelles within the cell.

Autophagic lysosome reformation (ALR)

During autophagy, lysosomes themselves can also be degraded alongside other cellular debris, but these require regeneration to maintain autophagic flux within the cell. Recently, the process of autophagic lysosome reformation (ALR), the generation of new lysosomes from existing autophagolysosomes, has been shown to exist within skeletal muscle. Specifically, the enzymatic conversion of phosphoinositides PI(3)P and PI(4)P into PI(4,5)P2 and PI(3,5)P2 is capable of driving the reformation of autophagolysosomes to create new lysosomes.13,70 Kinases such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), inositol polyphosphate 5-phosphatase (INPP5K), and PIKfyve (FYVE domain-containing phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate 5-kinase) are responsible for the conversion of ALR-related phosphoinositides.13,15 These enzymes are critical for skeletal muscle health, as mutations in INPP5K leading to reductions in its content and activity, have been shown to lead to disruptions in fiber morphology similar to muscular dystrophy.71 PI3K activity has been reported to increase following an acute bout of treadmill running in rat muscle72 and after 7 days of endurance exercise in human subjects.73 Furthermore, it has been shown that PIKfyve is phosphorylated at Ser307 and activated during muscle contraction in vitro by AMPK, leading to the formation of PI(3,5)P2.14,74 The action of INPP5K has not been described with respect to its response to exercise in skeletal muscle, however it is required for lysosome homeostasis and its role cannot be compensated for by the activation of TFEB-dependent lysosome biogenesis.13 Thus, while the study of the ALR pathway is in its infancy, particularly in muscle, its potential adaptation to exercise is clearly important for the regeneration of lysosomal proteolytic activity and the maintenance muscle health.

Chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA)

As noted above, chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) is an important process of removal for individual damaged cytosolic proteins that do not conform to large-scale degradation pathways such as macroautophagy. Acute exercise is capable of inducing CMA, as observed with the localization of a fluorescent protein, Dendra2, labelled with a KFERQ motif, to lysosomes in mouse skeletal muscle.35 Various proteins have been discovered to be CMA substrates that are removed during acute exercise which are primarily involved in muscle functions such as calcium handling, sarcomere organization, and protein folding, such as sarco-endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase 1 (SERCA1) among many others.35 It also appears that CMA is adaptable under conditions of chronic exercise. Using chronic contractile activity to model exercise training in muscle of rats, Kim et al. demonstrated that LAMP2A protein content was elevated after 7 days, although HSC70 content did not change.37 Additionally, in human skeletal muscle after a high-intensity exercise bout, LAMP2A protein levels were elevated, and HSC70 was downregulated, potentially due to its degradation after its role in chaperoning various proteins into the lysosomal lumen.75 Therefore, CMA is an important pathway that is required for skeletal muscle health and can respond to acute and chronic exercise to maintain protein homeostasis in skeletal muscle.

Conclusion

The study of lysosomal plasticity is an exciting new field, particularly in skeletal muscle where variations in lysosomal proteins can occur under seemingly disparate conditions, such as with exercise, disease, aging and disuse. An understanding of lysosome function and abundance under these conditions is highly relevant to the maintenance of muscle health because these organelles serve to clear dysfunctional debris, including mitochondria. It will be important in future work to not only make measurements of lysosomal markers, but also use indicators of lysosomal function, since compensatory changes in lysosomal biogenesis occur to adapt to divergent cellular demands. Much remains to be learned about the pathways that are activated by exercise to maintain lysosomal content and function, potentially serving as a therapeutic to increase lysosomal degradation capacity. However, researchers have begun narrowing the knowledge gaps with a growing appreciation for how TFEB and TFE3 can be activated by specifically through the well-known signals that trigger parallel changes in mitochondria, via increases in AMPK activity and cytosolic calcium, and the generation of ROS. Repeated bouts of acute exercise signaling result in a chronic trained phenotype impacting the downstream consequences of enhanced lysosomal biogenesis resulting in an improved capacity for degradation through increases in proteolytic activity. These adaptations to chronic exercise will be reflected in corresponding enhancements in macroautophagy and mitophagy, along with potential upregulations in CMA- and ALR-related proteins resulting in improvements in debris clearance and lysosomal turnover, thereby maintaining muscle health in aging and disease. Understanding lysosomal biology in skeletal muscle is a promising area for future research in exercise physiology.
