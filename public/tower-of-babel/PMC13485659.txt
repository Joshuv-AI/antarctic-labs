Distal ureteral stump syndrome in an adult female following childhood duplex system surgery
Authors: Joshua Levin, Tamir N. Sholklapper, Samuel Ivan, Joshua A. Cohn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Distal Ureteral Stump Syndrome is a condition causing recurrent urinary tract infections due to a retained ureteral stump post-ureterectomy. We present a 54-year-old female with recurrent urinary tract infections in adulthood following a childhood surgery for a duplex renal collecting system. Cystoscopy revealed mucus and debris within the retained ureteral stump, suspected to be the source of recurrent infections. Stump excision and non-refluxing reimplantation, due to proximity to the functional ureter, resulted in resolution of recurrent infections. This case highlights the need consider adult DUSS as an actionable cause of UTIs in select patients.

Introduction

Distal Ureteral Stump Syndrome (DUSS) is a rare condition characterized by chronic recurrent urinary tract infections and other urinary symptoms due to a retained ureteral stump following surgery. It results from the cytotoxic effects of urine and pre-existing bacteria refluxed into the distal stump without an outlet.1 Affecting approximately 0.8–1.0% of postoperative patients, DUSS is predominantly a pediatric condition and exceptionally rare in adults.2,3 The literature on DUSS is sparse compared to more common urological disorders, primarily limited to case reports and single-center studies. This case of a 54-year-old female with DUSS, following an unknown surgery on a duplex renal collecting system with a complete ureter in the 1970s adds to the limited available literature. Its rarity in adults and the adaptation of pediatric guidelines to an adult with an unclear surgical history address a significant gap in the current literature.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old female presented with vaginal pain, suprapubic pain, left-sided flank pain, worsening urinary leakage relative to her baseline (5 pads per day during this episode), and gross hematuria two days prior. She attributed these symptoms to a urinary tract infection (UTI) episode refractory to culture-directed amoxicillin and nitrofurantoin. Her medical and surgical history included nephrolithiasis, mixed urinary incontinence, and two episodes of pyelonephritis (one in childhood and one in adulthood). Notably, she had chronic recurrent UTIs since childhood, sometimes occurring monthly, and an unspecified major kidney surgery at the age of 3 years for a duplex renal collecting system. Evaluation approximately 15 years prior to her presentation to our team included a CT urogram (CTU), which revealed a duplicated collecting system of the left kidney with fusion of both ureters proximally (Fig. 1). In the months preceding intervention, nuclear medicine renal scan demonstrated a duplicated left renal collecting system with upper-pole moiety hydronephrosis without obstruction; split function was 38% for the left kidney. Cystourethroscopy demonstrated a native left ureteral orifice in the orthotopic position. Cannulation revealed mild purulent drainage. Additionally, using fluorescein dye, a second neo-ureteral orifice was identified inferior and medial to the native orifice. A cystogram revealed a 300 mL bladder capacity with reflux into an approximately 3 cm native ureteral stump from her childhood surgery (Fig. 2). She was diagnosed with Distal Ureteral Stump Syndrome (DUSS), and surgical management was deemed to be potentially curative. A robotic laparoscopic approach was used to identify the functional ureter and the ureteral stump. However, the stump and functional ureter were in direct approximation, making isolated resection of the stump risky due to potential trauma to the functional ureter. Consequently, a section of the bladder wall was removed en bloc, the stump was excised, ureterolysis was performed, and the bladder was closed. Post-procedure, the catheter was removed after a negative cystogram after 1 week, and the stent was removed after 6 weeks. Renal bladder ultrasound obtained 3 months post-procedure demonstrated asymmetric left renal lobulation with mild hydronephrosis, which is grossly similar to pre-surgical axial imaging. The patient has been maintained on a regimen of vaginal estrogen without further urinary tract infections, now 10 months post-procedure. Renal function has remained stable at baseline post-procedure. Symptoms of stress urinary incontinence, which predated the stump excision, persist and are being managed conservatively with pelvic floor physical therapy.

Discussion

Distal Ureteral Stump Syndrome (DUSS) is characterized by chronic recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), hematuria, and flank or abdominal pain due to a retained ureteral stump following surgery.4 Its incidence is reported to be approximately 0.8–1.0% in patients post-heminephrectomy or other procedures leaving a distal ureteral stump.2 Given the rarity of DUSS, the literature is limited, primarily consisting of case reports and single-center retrospective analyses, most of which focus on pediatric patients. This case is notable for occurring in a perimenopausal female who experienced symptoms since childhood, stemming from a surgery at age three for a duplex renal collecting system.

DUSS is attributed to the lack of peristaltic action in the stump, leading to reflux without an outlet, resulting in chronic UTIs.5 Lui et al. identified risk factors for DUSS, including mucus, ureteral debris on ultrasound, and ipsilateral upper pole reflux, with a higher incidence in females than males. This patient exhibited mucus and debris near the ureteral stump, aligning with these risk factors.6 However, the same study also mentioned “long ureteral stumps” as a commonly discussed risk factor without defining what constitutes a long stump. It remains unclear whether any stump length, a specific length beyond a threshold, or a stump-to-ureter length ratio represents a risk factor for DUSS. This is particularly significant, as stump length is a controllable factor with modern robotic-assisted surgery, which permits more straightforward expansion of the operative field and precise dissection. Further research to define stump length parameters could enhance DUSS prevention.

A non-refluxing Lich-Gregoir ureteral reimplantation was chosen due to the patient's recurrent UTIs, with reflux into the stump considered a likely cause. This approach, primarily developed for pediatric patients to prevent calyceal blunting and renal damage, was applied here without knowing that it would be necessary.7 This raises the question of whether non-refluxing reimplantation is necessary in adult DUSS cases where the stump, the source of infection, is removed. This is especially pertinent given that non-refluxing reimplantation tends to be somewhat more technically difficult to execute than a refluxing reimplantation.

Conclusion

This case of Distal Ureteral Stump Syndrome (DUSS) represents a rare presentation of an already uncommon condition. As most literature is limited to case reports and single-center retrospective analyses, this case contributes to a sparse body of research. The occurrence of DUSS in an adult is inherently rare and valuable for understanding the condition; however, this patient's case is particularly unique because her symptoms originated from a pediatric surgery at age three and manifested in adulthood. Beyond its rarity, this case prompts critical analysis of current data, understanding of DUSS risk factors, and the applicability of guidelines primarily designed for pediatric patients to adults. Overall, the patient's diagnosis, surgical management, and recovery reflect innovative surgical approaches that advance patient care and the field of urology.
