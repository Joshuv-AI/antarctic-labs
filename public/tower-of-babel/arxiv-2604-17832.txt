Duality Between Prime Factors and The Prime Number Theorem For Arithmetic Progressions - Higher Order Dualities
Krishnaswami Alladi, Sroyon Sengupta

Abstract




Abstract:

In 1977, the first author observed a duality between the largest and smallest prime factors of integers, and established as a consequence some new results on the Möbius function
\mu(n)
using the Prime Number Theorem for Arithmetic Progressions. In that 1977 paper, higher order dualities were observed involving the
k
-th largest and
k
-th smallest prime factors, facilitated by the Möbius function and
\omega(n)^{k-1}
, where
\omega(n)
is the number of distinct prime factors on
n
. In 2024, the first author and Jason Johnson proved new results involving
\mu(n)
and
\omega(n)
, by exploiting the second order duality identity of Alladi (1977). We establish here extensions to all higher orders
k
, the results of Alladi (1977) and of Alladi-Johnson (2024), by utilizing the
k
-th order duality in Alladi’s 1977 paper. First, we show that for each
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k}}{n}=0,

where
\mu(n)
is the Möbius Function and
\omega(n)
counts the number of distinct prime factors of
n
. Further, using the General Duality Identity and the Prime Number Theorem of Arithmetic Progressions, we prove that for integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=2\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k-1}}{n}=0,

for every
k\geq 3
; this result for
k=1
is due to Alladi (1977) and for
k=2
due to Alladi-Johnson (2024). We also recast this result in the following manner as a density-type theorem: for integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=2\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n){\omega(n)-1\choose k-1}}{n}=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)},

for every
k\geq 3
. All results are established here in quantitative form.

11M06, 11M11, 11N25, 11N37, 11N60

Duality between prime factors, Möbius function, number of prime factors, smallest prime factor,
k^{th}

1
Introduction and Background

Two famous results by Edmund Landau
[
15
]
are that

\displaystyle M(x):=\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}\mu(n)=o(x),\quad\text{as $x\rightarrow\infty$}

(1.1)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}=0

(1.2)

are elementarily equivalent to the Prime Number Theorem (PNT), where
\mu(n)
is the Möbius Function. There are also results equivalent to the Prime Number Theorem in Arithmetic Progressions (PNTAP), with
\mu(n)
is replaced by
\mu(n)\chi(n)
, where
\chi(n)
is the Dirichlet character modulo
k
, when the arithmetic progression considered in context has a common difference
k
.

In 1977
[
3
]
, the first author noticed the following interesting Duality identities involving
the Möbius function that connect the smallest and largest prime factors:

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leq d|n}\mu(d)f(p_{1}(d))=-f(P_{1}(n))\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\sum_{2\leq d|n}\mu(d)f(P_{1}(d))=-f(p_{1}(n)),

(1.3)

where, for integers
n>1
,
p_{1}(n)
and
P_{1}(n)
are the smallest and largest prime factors of
n
respectively, and
f
is ANY function defined on primes. Using the first identity in (1.3) along with properties of the Möbius function, it was proved in
[
3
]
that if
f
is a bounded function on the primes satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x}\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}f(P_{1}(n))=c,

(1.4)

then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)f(p_{1}(n))}{n}=-c,

(1.5)

and vice-versa. This can be realized as a generalization of Landau’s result as follows. We consider the function
f
such that
f(p)=1
for all primes
p
. Then
c
in (1.4) is 1. Further, rewrite (1.2) as

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}=-1.

(1.6)

This is exactly what we have in (1.5) for the chosen
f
.

It was shown in
[
3
]
that PNTAP implies that the sequence
\{P_{1}(n)\}
of the largest prime factors of
n
is uniformly distributed in the reduced residue classes modulo a positive integer
\ell
. In other words, if
f
is chosen to be the characteristic function of the primes in a given arithmetic progression
j\;(mod\;\ell)
, then for such an
f
, equation (1.4) is satisfied with
c=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}
, and therefore, by (1.5), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=2\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}=-\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)},

(1.7)

for ALL positive integers
\ell
and
1\leq j<\ell
satisfying
(j,\ell)=1
. This is quite an intriguing result since the LHS of (1.7) is a subseries of what we have in (1.6), and so each of those
\varphi(\ell)
many slices (due to
\varphi(\ell)
many possible
j
’s) have the exact same value, and these add up to give
-1
. In the past decade, (1.7) has been extended and generalized in the setting of algebraic number theory by several authors
[
6
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
24
]
, and
[
13
]
.

In
[
3
]
, the following general duality identities were also noted: For a positive integer
k
, let
P_{k}(n)
and
p_{k}(n)
denote the
k^{th}
largest and smallest prime factors of
n
, respectively, if
n
has at least
k
distinct prime factors. Let
\omega(n)
denote the number of distinct prime factors of
n
. Then:

\displaystyle\sum_{1<d|n}^{*}\mu(d)f(P_{k}(d))=(-1)^{k}{\omega(n)-1\choose k-1}f(p_{1}(n)),

(1.8)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{1<d|n}^{*}\mu(d)f(p_{k}(d))=(-1)^{k}{\omega(n)-1\choose k-1}f(P_{1}(n)),

(1.9)

where
*
over the summation means that if
n
has fewer than
k
distinct prime factors, then the sum is zero. Now, by Möbius Inversion, it follows from (1.8) and (1.9) that

\displaystyle\sum_{1<d|n}\mu(d){\omega(d)-1\choose k-1}f(p_{1}(d))=(-1)^{k}f(P_{k}(n)),

(1.10)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{1<d|n}\mu(d){\omega(d)-1\choose k-1}f(P_{1}(d))=(-1)^{k}f(p_{k}(n)).

(1.11)

Note here that we have omitted the
*
by adopting the convention that
f(P_{k}(n))=0=f(p_{k}(n))
, whenever
\omega(n)<k
, i.e. if
n
has fewer than
k
distinct prime factors (in that case, the condition represented by
*
earlier stays consistent).

Motivated by the Second Order Duality, namely the case
k=2
in (1.8) and (1.10), the first author along with Jason Johnson proved the following in
[
2
]
: for integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=2\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)}{n}=0.

(1.12)

In the course of proving (1.12), several essential auxiliary results were proved, a crucial one being the uniform distribution of the second largest prime factor
P_{2}(n)
in the reduced residue classes modulo a positive integer
\ell
. It is also proven in
[
2
]
that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)}{n}=0.

(1.13)

Therefore, quite similar to that of the results in
[
3
]
, we see that slicing up the sum in (1.13) into
\varphi(\ell)
many partial sums, we still get that each of them are respectively 0, adding up to 0.

A general theorem later noted in
[
2
]
is that if
f
is a bounded function on the primes such that

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leq n\leq x}f(P_{1}(n))\sim\kappa x

(1.14)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leq n\leq x}f(P_{2}(n))\sim\kappa x

(1.15)

for some constant
\kappa
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)f(p_{1}(n))}{n}=0.

(1.16)

Of course, considering
f
to be the characteristic function of the primes in a given arithmetic progression
j\;(mod\;\ell)
, then for such an
f
,
\kappa=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}
, due to the uniform distribution of the first and the second largest prime factors of
n
in reduced residue classes, and thence, we get the original result (1.12).

In this paper, we establish extensions of all results proved in
[
2
]
for all integers
k\geq 3
. We first start by proving that for any arbitrary positive integer
k\geq 2

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k}}{n}=0.

(1.17)

We actually establish this in strong quantitative form by providing a series expansion for the sum in (1.17) truncated at
x
(see
Theorem 2.1-k
) in §2. We then move on to prove that for any arbitrary positive integer
k
, the sequence of the
k^{th}
largest prime factors
\{P_{k}(n)\}
of
n
is uniformly distributed in reduced residue classes (see
Theorem 5.3
in §5). Using this, and the duality identity (1.10), we establish our main result (
Theorem 6.5
§6):
Given any fixed but arbitrary positive integer
k\geq 3
, and for integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=2\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k-1}}{n}=0.

(1.18)

We also give a density type result that states as follows:
Given any fixed but arbitrary positive integer
k\geq 3
, and for integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1
, we have that

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum^{\infty}_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=2\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(n){\omega(n)-1\choose k-1}}{n}=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}.

(1.19)

All our results are proved in quantitative form.

Remark:
We will first prove (1.18) and (1.19) for
k=3
. The proofs for every
k\geq 4
will follow quite similarly, but the asymptotics get more involved as
k
increases. So we provide the full details for
k=3
, and for
k\geq 4
, we mention only the main steps of the proof.

Notations and Conventions

We denote by
c_{1},c_{2},\cdots
absolute positive constants, whose values will not concern us. The
\ll
and
O
notations are equivalent and will be used interchangeably, as is convenient. We adopt the convention that

\displaystyle f(x)\ll g(x)\implies|f(x)|\leq Kg(x),

(1.20)

with
x
ranging in some domain depending on the context, and
K
a positive constant. Implicit constants are absolute unless otherwise indicated with a subscript. Although our results can be established with uniformity by allowing the modulus
\ell
to grow slowly as a function
x
, we prove the results by fixing an arbitrary
\ell
. The alphabet
n
, whether used as an argument of a function, or in a summation, will always be a positive integer, unless specified otherwise. Further,
p,q,r
and
p_{i}
’s for positive integer subscripts
i
, will always represent prime numbers, whether used in an argument of a function or a summation. The parameter
T=T(x)
, a function that will come up in most of the proofs, will be chosen optimally to get suitable and required bounds in various estimates, and may not be the same in all contexts. We shall use the standard notation
[x]
for the integral part of a real number
x
, and
\{x\}
, where indicated, will denote the fractional part of
x
. Thus,
\{x\}=x-[x]
. Finally, complex numbers will be denoted either by
z
, or by
s=\sigma+it
, the latter while dealing with Dirichlet series. Further notation will be introduced in the sequel as needed.

Note:
Whenever we employ the duality identity of order
k
, we will use
\omega(n)^{k-1}
in view of (1.10). Otherwise, we will simply use
\omega(n)^{k}
.

2
Sums of the Möbius function and
\omega(n)^{k}

The aim of this section is to prove that for all positive integers
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle M_{\omega^{k}}(x):=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k}=o(x),

(2.1)

and

\displaystyle m_{\omega^{k}}(x):=\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k}}{n}=o(1),\quad\text{as $x\to\infty$}.

(2.2)

We obtain strong quantitative versions of (2.1) and (2.2) in the form of an asymptotic estimate given by a series in decreasing powers of
\log x
for
M_{\omega^{k}}(x)
, and a similar asymptotic estimate for
m_{\omega^{k}}(x)
given by a series in decreasing powers of
\log x
as well (see
Theorem 2.1
and
2.1-k
in this section).

For the case
k=1
, Alladi-Johnson established (2.1) and (2.2) by obtaining bounds and communicated this to Tenenbaum. In response to this, Tenenbaum in a letter to Alladi stated that using the Selberg-Delange analytic method, the following sharper estimates hold:
If
\nu
is an arbitrary but fixed positive integer, then there exist constants
\beta_{j}
such that

\displaystyle M_{\omega}(x)=\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}+\frac{\beta_{3}x}{\log^{3}x}+\frac{\beta_{4}x}{\log^{4}x}+\cdots+\frac{\beta_{\nu}x}{\log^{\nu}x}+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x\log\log^{2\nu+2}x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right),

(2.3)

and there exist constants
\gamma_{j}
such that

\displaystyle m_{\omega}(x)=-\frac{1}{\log x}+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\log^{2}x}+\frac{\gamma_{3}}{\log^{3}x}+\cdots+\frac{\gamma_{\nu}x}{\log^{\nu}x}+O\left(\frac{\log\log^{2\nu+4}x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right).

(2.4)

Tenenbaum’s error terms were in a slightly different form, but (2.3) and (2.4) are essentially equivalent to what he stated. In
[
2
]
, Alladi-Johnson proved (2.3) and (2.4) by an elementary method, but using the Prime Number Theorem in the following strong form

\displaystyle\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\ell i(x)+O(xe^{-c\sqrt{\log x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{\log x}+\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}+\frac{2!x}{\log^{3}x}+\cdots+\frac{\log^{\nu}x}{(\nu-1)!x}+O\left(\nu!\frac{x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right),

(2.5)

given by repeated integration-by-parts of
\ell i(x)
. Here, we shall follow the elementary method of Alladi-Johnson to establish
Theorems 2.1
and
2.1-k
below for fixed
k\geq 2
, but we shall rely on strong asymptotic estimates for

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(x):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega(n)=k\end{subarray}}1

in the form of a series similar to that of (2.5).

If
k
is an arbitrary but fixed positive integer, then it can be shown by induction on
k
starting with (2.5), that there exist polynomials
Q_{j,k}(X)
, of degree
\leq k-1
, with
Q_{1,k}(X)
of degree
k-1
and leading coefficient 1, such that

\pi_{k}(x)=\frac{xQ_{1,k}(\log\log x)}{\log x}+\frac{xQ_{2,k(\log\log x)}}{\log^{2}x}+\cdots+\frac{xQ_{\nu,k}(\log\log x)}{\log^{\nu}x}+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k}}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right).

(2.6)

Remark:
For
k
, an arbitrary but fixed positive integer, Landau obtained by induction on
k
, the asymptotic estimate

\displaystyle\pi_{k}(x)\sim\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-1}}{\log x}

(2.7)

as a consequence of the Prime Number Theorem. If
k
varies with
x
, then it becomes cumbersome to keep track of the error terms in the inductive argument, yet Sathe (see
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
) skillfully managed to obtain asymptotic uniform estimates for
\pi_{k}(x)
for
k\leq B\log\log x
, where
B
is an arbitrary constant. Selberg
[
18
]
subsequently showed how to get Sathe’s results painlessly by an analytic method. Delange later improved upon Selberg’s method and the Selberg-Delange analytic method
[
23
]
yields a series representation similar to (2.6) for
k\leq B\log\log x
. Our emphasis here is that, if
k
is arbitrary but fixed, then (2.6) can be derived elementarily by induction on
k
using the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem as given in (2.5).

In the series representation for
M_{\omega^{k}}(x)
and
m_{\omega^{k}}(x)
given below, terms involving
\log\log x
appear only for
k\geq 2
. Thus, the analysis for case
k=2
, although bearing similarity to the case
k=1
in Alladi-Johnson
[
2
]
, is different in a crucial way. The analysis of all cases
k\geq 2
are all quite similar, but the details get more complicated as
k
increases. For these reasons, we shall present here all the details for the case
k=2
, and state the corresponding results for
k\geq 3
sketching only the main ideas in the proof.

We begin by noting that if
\omega(n)=r\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)z^{\omega(d)}=(1-z)^{r}.

(2.8)

So

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\omega(d)z^{\omega(d)}=z\frac{d}{dz}\left(\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)z^{\omega(d)}\right)=-zr(1-z)^{r-1}.

(2.9)

From (2.9), we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\omega^{2}(d)z^{\omega(d)-1}=\frac{d}{dz}\{-zr(1-z)^{r-1}\}=-r(1-z)^{r-1}+zr(r-1)(1-z)^{r-2}.

(2.10)

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\omega^{2}(d)=\left(\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\omega^{2}(d)z^{\omega(d)-1}\right)\Bigg|_{z=1}=-\chi_{1}(n)+2\chi_{2}(n),

(2.11)

where
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are the characteristic functions of integers for which
\omega(n)=1
and
\omega(n)=2
respectively. Therefore, by Möbius inversion, we have

\displaystyle\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}=\sum_{d|n}\left(-\chi_{1}(d)+2\chi_{2}(d)\right)\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right).

(2.12)

In what follows, (2.12) will be used to estimate
M_{\omega^{2}}(x)
asymptotically using the hyperbola method. In order to do this, we need some results on the Möbius function.

The Dirichlet series associated with the Möbius function is

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}=\frac{1}{\zeta(s)},\quad\text{for $\sigma>1$},

(2.13)

where
\zeta(s)
is the Riemann zeta function. By using bounds for
\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}
, the standard analytic approach to obtain the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem can be employed to derive the following bound for
M(x)
- see for instance, Tenenbaum (
[
23
]
, p. 217):

\displaystyle M(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\ll xe^{-c_{1}\sqrt{\log x}}.

(2.14)

The same method also yields

\displaystyle m(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\ll e^{-c_{1}\sqrt{\log x}},

(2.15)

and these are quantitative forms of (1.1) and (1.2). In (2.14) and (2.15), we have used the same constant
c_{1}
, because if we had two different positive constants, we could choose the minimum of these two as
c_{1}
for (2.14) and (2.15), we have for each positive integer j,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\log^{j}n}{n}=\lambda_{j}

(2.16)

is convergent, and its quantitative form

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{\mu(n)\log^{j}n}{n}=\lambda_{j}+O(e^{-c_{1}\sqrt{\log x}}).

(2.17)

The constant
c_{1}=c_{1}(j)
in the exponential of (2.17) depends on
j
, but in what follows, we will use (2.17) only for
j\leq\nu
, where
\nu
will be an arbitrary but fixed positive integer. So, we will use

\displaystyle c_{1}:=\min\{c_{1}(0),c_{1}(1),\cdots,c_{1}(\nu)\}.

(2.18)

Note that (2.17) can be deduced from the method that yields (2.14) or by partial summation using (2.14). It is known that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\log n}{n}=\lambda_{1}=-1.

(2.19)

This is the only value besides
\lambda_{0}=0
that we need. The actual values of
\lambda_{j}
for
j\geq 2
, which can be written in terms of the successive derivative values of
\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}
at
s=1
, will not concern us. We are now ready to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 2.1:

Let
\nu
be an arbitrary but fixed integer. Then there exist linear polynomials
Q_{j,2}^{*}(X)
such that

\displaystyle M_{\omega^{2}}(X)=-\frac{x(2\log\log x+c_{2})}{\log^{2}x}+\sum_{j=3}^{\nu}\frac{xQ_{j,2}^{*}(\log\log x)}{\log^{j}x}+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x\log\log^{2\nu+4}x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right).

Proof:
We shall adapt the method in Alladi-Johnson
[
2
]
to prove
Theorem 2.1
. Begin by using (2.12) to write

\displaystyle M_{\omega^{2}}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}=\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)(2\chi_{2}(d)-\chi_{1}(d)).

(2.20)

Using the hyperbola method, we break up the double sum in (2.20) into

\displaystyle M_{\omega^{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\leq T}\mu(m)\sum_{h\leq\frac{x}{m}}(2\chi_{2}(h)-\chi_{1}(h))+\sum_{h\leq\frac{x}{T}}(2\chi_{2}(h)-\chi_{1}(h))\sum_{T<m\leq\frac{x}{h}}\mu(m)

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2},

(2.21)

where
T
will be chosen optimally below so that

\displaystyle\log T=o(\log x).

(2.22)

We can easily bound
\Sigma_{2}
using (2.14) and the monotone increasing property of

\displaystyle R_{1}(x)=xe^{-c_{1}\sqrt{\log x}},\quad\text{for $x\geq x_{0}$}.

That is, (2.14) yields

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}\ll\sum_{h\leq\frac{x}{T}}\frac{x(2\chi_{2}(h)-\chi_{1}(h))}{he^{c_{1}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{x}{h}\right)}}}\ll\frac{x\log\log x}{e^{c_{1}\sqrt{\log T}}}.

(2.23)

The estimation of
\Sigma_{1}
is more involved. We note that (2.5) and (2.6) yield

\displaystyle\sum_{h\leq x}\{2\chi_{2}(h)-\chi_{1}(h)\}

\displaystyle=2\pi_{2}(x)-\pi(x)+O(\sqrt{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{x(2\log\log x+c_{2})}{\log x}+\frac{xF_{2,2}(\log\log x)}{\log^{2}x}+\cdots+\frac{xF_{\nu,2}(\log\log x)}{\log^{\nu}x}+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x\log\log x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right),

(2.24)

where, for
2\leq j\leq\nu
,

\displaystyle F_{j,2}(\log\log x)=2Q_{j,2}(\log\log x)-(j-1)!.

So, from (2.21) and (2.22) we get

\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{m\leq T}\mu(m)\left(\frac{x\left(2\log\log\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)+c_{2}\right)}{m\log\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)}\right)+\sum_{m\leq T}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{\nu}\frac{\mu(m)xF_{j,2}\left(\log\log\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)\right)}{m\log^{j}\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)}\right)+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x\log\log x\log T}{\log^{\nu+1}\left(\frac{x}{T}\right)}\right).

(2.25)

Denote by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1,j}=x\sum_{m\leq T}\frac{\mu(m)F_{j,2}\left(\log\log\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)\right)}{m\log^{j}\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)},\quad\text{for $j=1,2,\cdots,\nu$},

(2.26)

where
F_{1,2}(\log\log x)=2\log\log x+c_{2}
.

We first estimate
\Sigma_{1,1}
. To this end, we note that since
m\leq T
and (2.22) holds, we have

\displaystyle\log\log\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\log(\log x-\log m)

\displaystyle=\log\log x+\log\left(1-\frac{\log m}{\log x}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\log x-\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}\frac{\log^{j}m}{j\log^{j}x}+O\left(\frac{\log^{\nu+1}m}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right),

(2.27)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log x\left(1-\frac{\log m}{\log x}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\nu}\frac{\log^{j}m}{\log^{j+1}x}+O\left(\frac{\log^{\nu+1}m}{\log^{\nu+2}x}\right).

(2.28)

So, from (2.27) and (2.28), we get

\displaystyle\frac{\log\log\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)}{\log\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)}=\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}+\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}\frac{(\log\log x-H_{j})\log^{j}m}{\log^{j+1}x}+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{\log\log x\log^{\nu+1}}{\log^{\nu+2}x}\right),

(2.29)

where the
H_{j}
are the Harmonic numbers given by

\displaystyle H_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{i}.

Thus, from (2.26) and (2.29), we see that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{x(2\log\log x+c_{2})}{\log x}\sum_{m\leq T}\frac{\mu(m)}{m}+\frac{x(2\log\log x+c_{2}-H_{1})}{\log^{2}x}\sum_{m\leq T}\frac{\mu(m)\log m}{m}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt+\sum_{j=2}^{\nu}\frac{x(\log\log x+c_{2}-H_{j})}{\log^{j+1}x}\sum_{m\leq T}\frac{\mu(m)\log^{j}m}{m}+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x\log\log x\log^{\nu+2}T}{\log^{\nu+2}x}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{x(2\log\log x+c_{2}-H_{1})}{\log^{2}x}+\sum_{j=2}^{\nu}\frac{x(\log\log x+c_{2}-H_{j})}{\log^{j+1}x}

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98866pt+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x\log\log x\log^{\nu+2}T}{\log^{\nu+2}x}\right)+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x\log\log x}{e^{c_{1}\sqrt{\log x}}}\right),

(2.30)

in view of (2.15), (2.17) and (2.19).

Note that the leading term in
\Sigma_{1,1}
is
\frac{x(\log\log x+c_{2})}{\log^{2}x}
. We can treat the sums
\Sigma_{1,j}
for
j\geq 2
in a similar fashion and obtain a series in decreasing powers of
\log x
with leading term

\displaystyle\frac{F_{j,2}(\log\log x)}{\log^{j+1}x}

and error terms as in (2.30), because
\lambda_{0}=0
, where
F_{j,2}(X)
is a linear function of
X
. Finally, if we sum all such expressions for
\Sigma_{1,j}
, we will get

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}=-\frac{x(2\log\log x+c_{2})}{\log^{2}x}+\sum_{j=3}^{\nu}\frac{xQ^{*}_{j,2}(\log\log x)}{\log^{j}x}+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x\log\log^{2\nu+4}x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right)+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{x\log\log x}{e^{c_{1}\sqrt{\log T}}}\right).

(2.31)

At this stage, we choose

\displaystyle T=e^{U^{2}(\log\log x)^{2}},\quad\text{with $c_{1}U=\nu+1$}.

(2.32)

With this choice of
T
,
Theorem 2.1
follows from (2.21), (2.23) and (2.31). ∎

Next, for the purpose of proving
Theorem 2.2
, and also for subsequent use in this paper, and for use in future related work, we will use
Theorem A
below due to Alladi-Johnson
[
2
]
, which is a variant of Axer’s theorem (see Hardy
[
10
]

Theorem A:

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of reals such that

\displaystyle A(x):=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\ll x\eta(x),

(2.33)

where

\eta(x)\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\infty,

(2.34a)

and

x\eta(x)\quad\text{is an increasing function $\to\infty$}\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\infty.

(2.34b)

Suppose also that

\sum_{n\leq x}|a_{n}|\ll x\beta(x),

(2.35)

where

\beta(x)\quad\text{is an increasing function,}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta(x)\eta(x)\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\infty.

(2.36)

Then

\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\left\{\frac{x}{n}\right\}\ll x\sqrt{\eta(x)\beta(x)}=o(x),

(2.37)

where
\{x\}
is the fractional part of
x
.

Remark:
For a proof, see
[
2
]
. Axer
[
5
]
stated his theorem for bounded functions (sequences)
a_{n}
, but the method can be applied with
|a_{n}|
having at most a slowly growing unbounded average as given by (2.35) and (2.36). In the version of Axer’s theorem in Hardy (
[
10
]
, p. 378), the function
\{x\}
is replaced by a more general function
\chi(x)
of bounded variation on finite intervals, but
|a_{n}|
is assumed to have a bounded average.
Theorem A
can be generalized by replacing
\{x\}
with such a function
\chi(x)
, but the version of
Theorem A
given above suffices for our purpose here and in our subsequent work.

Theorem 2.2:

With
\{w\}
denoting the fractional part of
w
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left\{\frac{x}{n}\right\}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\log x}.

Proof:
To prove
Theorem 2.2
, choose
a_{n}=\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}
in
Theorem A
. By
Theorem 2.1
, we can take

\displaystyle\eta(x)=\frac{\log\log x}{\log^{2}x}

in (2.33). Thus, (2.34a) and (2.34b) are satisfied. In view of the well known estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}\omega(n)^{2}\ll x(\log\log x)^{2},

we can take

\displaystyle\beta(x)=(\log\log x)^{2}

in (2.35), since

. Thus, (2.36) is satisfied.
Theorem 2
then follows from (2.37) of

. ∎

Theorem 2.3:

denote the integral part of

\displaystyle+\frac{x(Q_{2,2}(\log\log x)-1)}{\log^{2}x}+\frac{xQ_{2,3}(\log\log x)-2!}{\log^{3}x}+\cdots

\displaystyle+\frac{x(Q_{\nu,2}(\log\log x)-(\nu-1)!)}{\log^{\nu}x}+O\left(\nu!\cdot\frac{x(\log\log x)^{\nu+1}}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right).

Proof:
Note that (2.11) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]=\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\omega^{2}(d)=\sum_{n\leq x}(2\chi_{2}(n)-\chi_{1}(n)),

and from this,

follows using (2.5) and (2.6). We note here that the

We now prove the main result of this section which we state in two parts. The reason for this split will be clear at the end of the proof.

Theorem 2.4:

(i)

We have

\displaystyle m_{\omega^{2}}(x):=\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}}{n}=O\left(\frac{(\log\log x)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\log x}\right).

Consequently

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}}{n}=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}}{n}=0.

(ii)

More precisely, there exist linear polynomials
F_{j,2}^{*}(X)
, such that, for each positive integer
\nu
, we have

\displaystyle m_{\omega^{2}}(x)=\frac{2\log\log x+c_{2}+2}{\log x}+\frac{F_{2,2}^{*}(\log\log x)}{\log^{2}x}+\cdots+\frac{F^{*}_{\nu,2}(\log\log x)}{\log^{\nu}x}+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{\log\log^{2\nu+6}x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right).

Proof of (i):
From
Theorem 2.2
and
2.3
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\frac{x}{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]+\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left\{\frac{x}{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\log x}+\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\log x}.

(2.38)

By canceling
x
on both extremes of (2.38), we get the bound for
m_{\omega^{2}}(x)
in
Theorem 2.4 (i)
. By letting
x\to\infty
in this bound, we get the second assertion of
Theorem 2.4 (i)
. ∎

Proof of (ii):
We start with the representation

m_{\omega^{2}}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}}{n}=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{dM_{\omega^{2}}(t)}{t}.

(2.39)

Note that
M_{\omega^{2}}(t)=m_{\omega^{2}}(t)=0
for
t<2
. Integration-by-parts of the Stieltjes integral in (2.39) gives

\displaystyle m_{\omega^{2}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{\omega^{2}}(t)}{t}\Big|_{1}^{x}+\int_{1}^{x}\frac{M_{\omega^{2}}(t)}{t^{2}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{\omega^{2}}(x)}{x}+\int_{1}^{x}\frac{M_{\omega^{2}}(t)}{t^{2}}dt.

(2.40)

We know from
Theorem 2.4 (i)
that
m_{\omega^{2}}(\infty)=0
, and from
Theorem 2.1
that
M_{\omega^{2}}(x)=o(x)
. So, by letting
x\to\infty
in (2.40), we deduce that

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{M_{\omega^{2}}(t)}{t^{2}}dt=0.

(2.41)

In view of (2.41), we may rewrite (2.40) as

m_{\omega^{2}}(x)=\frac{M_{\omega^{2}}(x)}{x}-\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{M_{\omega^{2}}(t)}{t^{2}}dt.

(2.42)

From
Theorem 2.1
, we see that

\frac{M_{\omega^{2}}(x)}{x}=-\frac{2\log\log x+c_{2}}{\log^{2}x}+\sum_{j=3}^{\nu}\frac{Q_{j,2}^{*}(\log\log x)}{\log^{j}x}+O_{\nu}\left(\frac{\log\log^{2\nu+4}x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}\right).

(2.43)

As for the integral on the right in (2.42), if we substitute the expression in
Theorem 2.1
for
M_{\omega^{2}}(t)
in the integral with
\nu
replaced
\nu+1
, we will get

-\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{M_{\omega^{2}}(t)}{t^{2}}dt=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{(2\log\log t+c_{2})}{t\log^{2}t}dt-\sum_{j=3}^{\nu+1}\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{j,2}^{*}(\log\log t)}{t\log^{j}t}dt+O_{\nu}\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\log\log^{2\nu+4}t}{t\log^{\nu+1}t}dt\right).

(2.44)

In (2.44), the first integral on the right is what gives the leading term in the series expansion for
m_{\omega^{2}}(x)
, and it is

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{2(\log\log t+c_{2})}{t\log^{2}t}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{2\log\log t}{t\log^{2}t}dt+2c_{2}\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t\log^{2}t}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{2\log\log t}{t\log^{2}t}dt+\frac{c_{3}}{\log x},

(2.45)

where
c_{3}=2c_{2}
, another constant. For the integral on the right of (2.45), we use the substitution
v=\log\log t
to get

\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{2\log\log t}{t\log^{2}t}dt=2\int_{\log\log x}^{\infty}ve^{-v}dv=2(-ve^{-v}-e^{-v})\big|_{\log\log x}^{\infty}=\frac{2\log\log x+2}{\log x}.

(2.46)

So, from (2.45) and (2.46), we get

\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{(2\log\log t+c_{2})dt}{t\log^{2}t}=\frac{2\log\log x+c_{3}+2}{\log x}.

(2.47)

The other integrals can be worked out in an analogous fashion. If we denote the generic linear polynomial
Q_{j,2}^{*}(\log\log x)
by
a_{j}\log\log x+b_{j}
, then we get

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{j,2}^{*}(\log\log t)}{\log^{j}t}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}\log\log t}{t\log^{j}t}dt+b_{j}\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t\log^{j}t}

\displaystyle=a_{j}\int_{x}^{\infty}ve^{-(j-1)v}dv+\frac{b_{j}}{(j-1)\log^{j}x}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{j}\log\log x}{(j-1)\log^{j-1}x}+\frac{a_{j}}{(j-1)^{2}\log^{j-1}x}+\frac{b_{j}}{(j-1)\log^{j-1}x},

(2.48)

using the substitution
\log\log t=v
in the integral involving
a_{j}
, and then integrating-by-parts. The terms in (2.48) have to be summed from
j=3
to
j=\nu+1
, and then combined with the terms in (2.44). Finally,

O_{\nu}\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{\log\log^{2\nu+4}t}{t\log^{\nu+1}t}dt\right)\ll\frac{\log\log^{2\nu+6}x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}.

(2.49)

Thus,
Theorem 2.4 (ii)
follows from the equations (2.44) to (2.49). ∎

\nu+1
, we will get

-\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{M_{\omega^{k}}(t)}{t^{2}}dt=-\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{2,k}^{*}(\log\log t)}{t\log^{2}t}dt+\sum_{j=3}^{\nu+1}\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{Q_{j,k}^{*}(\log\log t)}{t\log^{j}t}dt+O_{\nu}\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{(\log\log t)^{k\nu+3k}}{t\log^{\nu+2}t}dt\right).

(2.59)

In (2.59), the first integral on the right is what gives the leading term in the series expansion of
m_{\omega^{k}}(x)
. Since each
Q_{j,k}^{*}(\log\log x)
is a polynomial in
\log\log x
of degree
\leq k-1
, with
Q_{2,k}^{*}(\log\log x)
of degree
k-1
, the integral expressions on the right of (2.59) are linear combinations of integrals of the form

I_{r,j}(x):=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{(\log\log t)^{r}}{t\log^{j}}dt,\quad\text{where $0\leq r\leq k-1$, and $j\geq 2$}.

(2.60)

The substitution
\log\log t=v
converts
I_{r,j}
into

I_{r,j}(x)=\int_{\log\log x}^{\infty}v^{r}e^{-(j-1)v}dv,

(2.61)

which can be evaluated by integrating by parts
r
times to get

\frac{G_{j,r}(\log\log x)}{\log^{j-1}x},

(2.62)

where
G_{j,r}
are polynomials such that

\displaystyle G_{j,r}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\frac{x^{i}}{(j-1)^{r-i+1}}.

Combining all such
I_{r,j}
’s arising from (2.59), we get the expression

\frac{F_{1,k}^{*}(\log\log x)}{\log x}+\frac{F_{2,k}^{*}(\log\log x)}{\log^{2}x}+\cdots+\frac{F_{\nu,k}^{*}(\log\log x)}{\log^{\nu}x}

(2.63)

using (2.60), (2,61) and (2.62) if we have
j\leq\nu+1
. Finally, we have

O_{\nu}\left(\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{(\log\log t)^{k\nu+3k}}{t\log^{\nu+2}t}dt\right)\ll_{\nu}\frac{(\log\log x)^{k\nu+3k}x}{\log^{\nu+1}x}.

(2.64)

Theorem 2.4-k (ii)
follows from (2.57)-(2.64).∎

3
The Third Largest Prime Factor

In what follows, we will provide details of the proofs in the case
k=3
, and sketch how the same methods yield results for all
k>3
. Thus, we start with the discussion of
P_{3}(n)
, the third largest prime factor of
n
, but for this, we need results on
P_{1}(n)
, the largest prime factor of
n
, and
P_{2}(n)
, the second largest prime factor of
n
, and we begin by stating these.

3.1
The counting function for
P_{1}(n)

The fundamental counting function associated with the largest prime factor
P_{1}(n)
is defined by

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(x,y):=\Psi(x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P_{1}(n)\leq y\end{subarray}}1.

(3.1)

We let
\alpha=\frac{\log x}{\log y}
. de Bruijn
[
7
]
has proved that with some
c>0

\displaystyle\Psi(x,y)\ll xe^{-c\alpha}

(3.2)

uniformly for
2\leq y\leq x
. Tenenbaum
[
23
]
has shown that
c=\frac{1}{2}
is admissible in (3.2). de Bruijn
[
8
]
also proved the following improved bound

\displaystyle\Psi(x,y)\ll x(\log^{2}y)e^{-\alpha\log\alpha-\alpha\log\log\alpha+O(\alpha)},

(3.3)

for
y>\log^{2}x
, as well as the asymptotic estimate

\displaystyle\Psi(x,y)\sim x\rho(\alpha),

(3.4)

when
e^{(\log x)^{\frac{3}{5}}}\leq y\leq x
, where
\rho
satisfies the integro-differential equation

\displaystyle\rho(\alpha)=1-\int_{1}^{\alpha}\frac{\rho(u-1)}{u}du,

(3.5)

and

\displaystyle\rho(\alpha)=e^{-\alpha\log\alpha-\alpha\log\log\alpha+O(\alpha)}.

(3.6)

This shows that
\Psi(x,y)
is small in comparison to
x
when
\alpha
is large.

Remark:
Even though (3.4) has been improved substantially in terms of the range of values of
y
(see Hildebrand-Tenenbaum
[
11
]
), the estimates given in (3.3) and (3.4) suffice for our
purpose.

3.2
Ambiguity in the Second and Third Largest Prime Factor

The second largest prime factor
P_{2}(n)
can be defined in two ways, either as
P_{1}(n/P_{1}(n))
, or as the largest prime factor of
n
strictly less than
P_{1}(n)
, when
\omega(n)>1
. It was shown in Alladi-Johnson
[
2
]
, that there is little difference asymptotically between these two definitions because the number
N_{1}(x)
of integers up to
x
for which
P_{1}(n)
repeats is very small in comparison with
x
. More precisely, using (3.3), the following was shown in Alladi-Johnson
[
2
]
:

Lemma 3.1:

The number
N_{1}(x)
of integers
n\leq x
for which the largest prime factor repeats, satisfies

\displaystyle N_{1}(x)\ll\frac{x}{e^{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{\log\,x\log\log\,x}}}.

(3.7)

Thus in Alladi-Johnson
[
2
]
,
P_{2}(n)
was defined to be the largest prime factor of
n
that is strictly less than
P_{1}(n)
if
\omega(n)\geq 2
, and
P_{2}(n)=1
, if
\omega(n)<2
.

Analogously, one could define
P_{3}(n)
, the third largest prime factor of
n
, in two ways:

(i)

using weak inequalities, define
P_{2}(n)=P_{1}(n/P_{1}(n)),
and
P_{3}(n)=P_{1}(n/P_{1}(n)P_{2}(n))
, when
\Omega(n)\geq 3
, and
P_{3}(n)=1
, when
\Omega(n)<3
, or

(ii)

using strict inequalities, define
P_{2}(n)
as the largest prime factor of
n
which is strictly less than
P_{1}(n)
, and
P_{3}(n)
as the largest prime factor of
n
which is strictly less than
P_{2}(n)
, when
\omega(n)\geq 3
, and
P_{3}(n)=1
, when
\omega(n)<3
.

Analogous to
Lemma 3.1
, we now show that there is little difference asymptotically in the two definitions of
P_{3}(n)
:

Lemma 3.2:

Let
N(x)
denote the integers less than or equal to
x
such that either the largest or the second largest prime factors of the integers repeat in their respective prime factorizations. Then

\displaystyle N(x)\ll\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}.

Proof:
Let us partition the set of integers less than or equal to
x
with repeating largest or second largest primes into the following sets
N_{1}(x)
and
N_{2}(x)
: we define them as,

\displaystyle N_{1}(x):=\{n\leq x:\;P_{1}(n)\;\text{repeats}\}\;\;\;\;\;N_{2}(x):=\{n\leq x:\;P_{1}(n)\;\text{does not repeat but $P_{2}(n)$ does}\}.

Here, note that there might be integers in
N(x)
such that
P_{1}(n)
and
P_{2}(n)
both repeat. By the above definitions, it is automatic that such integers will lie in the set
N_{1}(x)
. Of course,
N(x)=N_{1}(x)\cup N_{2}(x)
. By
Lemma 3.1
above, we already have that

\displaystyle N_{1}(x)\ll\frac{x}{e^{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{\log\,x\log\log\,x}}}.

(3.8)

Now, for any arbitrary
n\in N_{2}(x)
, if we denote
P_{1}(n)=q,P_{2}(n)=p
, then we have that
n
is of the order of magnitude
O\left(\frac{x}{p^{2}}\right)
. Further, by Theorem 6 of
[
2
]
, we have that

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(x,T)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P_{2}(n)\leq T\end{subarray}}1\ll\frac{x\log T}{\log x}.

(3.9)

Therefore, using (3.8) and (3.9), we have

\displaystyle N_{2}(x)\ll\Psi_{2}(x,T)+\sum_{p>T}\frac{x}{p^{2}}\ll\frac{x\log T}{\log x}+\frac{x}{T\log^{2}T}.

(3.10)

Now, choosing

\displaystyle T=\frac{\log x}{(\log\log x)^{3}},

we get our required result. ∎

Remark:

Lemma 3.2
shows that the set of integers where the two definitions were inconsistent, is of the order
o(x)
. Therefore, they will be only a part of the error and have no contribution in the main terms of the theorems that follow. So we adopt the definition of
P_{3}(n)
using (ii) above, that is using strict inequalities. However, it is to be noted that the bound for
N_{1}(x)
in
Lemma 3.1
is much smaller compared to the bound for
N(x)
in
Lemma 3.2
, and thus the discussion of asymptotics in this paper is more involved compared to that in Alladi-Johnson
[
2
]
.

The following lemma is very crucial for proving the uniform distribution of the sequence of the third largest prime factors in reduced residue classes. The lemma will imply that
P_{3}(n)
is large for almost all integers, and will also provide a quantitative measure.

Lemma 3.3
(Tenenbaum
[
22
]
) With
\alpha=\frac{\log x}{\log T}
, we have uniformly for
2\leq T\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}

\displaystyle\Psi_{3}(x,T):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P_{3}(n)\leq T\end{subarray}}1\ll\frac{x\log\alpha\log T}{\log x}.

(3.11)

Remark:
Tenenbaum
[
22
]
actually gets a sharper result than what is stated in
Lemma 3.3
and his proof uses intricate analysis. For our purpose, a special case of
Lemma 3.3
namely that

\displaystyle\Psi_{3}(x,T)\ll_{\delta}\frac{x\log\alpha\log T}{\log x},\quad\text{for $T\leq e^{(\log x)^{1-\delta}}$}

(3.12)

suffices, and this can be established elementarily using the bound for
\Psi(x,y)
in (3.2). The elementary derivation of a similar bound, more generally, for
\Psi_{k}(x,T)
, for
k\geq 3
, is given in §5 (
Theorem 5.2
).

3.3
Uniform Distribution of the Third Largest Prime Factor of
n
modulo
\ell

Theorem 3.4:

For each integer
\ell\geq 2
, the sequence
\{P_{3}(n)\}
of the third largest prime factors of
n
, is uniformly distributed in the reduced residue classes modulo
\ell
. More precisely, for each fixed
\ell\geq 2
, and any
1\leq j\leq\ell
satisfying
(j,\ell)=1
, we have that

\displaystyle N_{3}(x;\ell,j):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P_{3}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}1=\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)}+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{3}}{\log x}\right).

(3.13)

Proof:
We note that
P_{3}(n)=1
when
\omega(n)\leq 2
. Now,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega(n)\leq 2\end{subarray}}1=\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{x}{\log x}\right).

(3.14)

Therefore, we can just consider the integers with
\omega(n)\geq 3
in the summation. For a fixed prime
p
, let
S_{3}(x,p)
denote the set of integers less than or equal to
x
with at least 3 distinct prime factors, with square-free first and second largest prime factors and
p
as the third largest prime factor. Therefore, we have, using
Lemma 3.1
and (3.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}|S_{3}(x,p)|=[x]-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\omega(n)\leq 2\end{subarray}}1=x+O\left(\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}\right).

(3.15)

Let
N\in S_{3}(x,p)
. Then, we may write

\displaystyle N=m.pqr\quad\text{where}\quad p<q<r\quad\text{are primes and }P_{1}(m)\leq p.

Thus it is clear that
P_{1}(n)=r
,
P_{2}(n)=q
and
P_{3}(n)=p
. Then, with (3.1), we can rewrite
|S_{3}(x,p)|
as the following sum:

\displaystyle|S_{3}(x,p)|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m<\frac{x}{p^{3}}\\
P_{1}(m)\leq p\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{p<q<\sqrt{\frac{x}{mp}}}\;\;\sum_{q<r\leq\frac{x}{mpq}}1=\sum_{p<r\leq\frac{x}{p^{2}}}\;\;\sum_{p<q<r}\;\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m<\frac{x}{pqr}\\
P_{1}(m)\leq p\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{p<r\leq\frac{x}{p^{2}}}\;\;\sum_{p<q<r}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr};p\right).

(3.16)

Therefore, from (3.15) and (3.16), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}|S_{3}(x,p)|=\sum_{p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{p<r\leq\frac{x}{p^{2}}}\;\;\sum_{p<q<r}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr};p\right)=x+O\left(\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}\right).

(3.17)

Note:
We need to rewrite the triple sum in (3.17) with the innermost sum as a sum over
p
because we later want to consider the sum over
p
with the restriction
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)
. Thus, (3.17) is rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}|S_{3}(x,p)|=\sum_{r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\sum_{q<r}^{*}\sum_{p<q}^{*}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr};p\right),

(3.18)

where * over the summations means the conditions governing
p,q,r
are

\displaystyle p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}},\;p<q<r\leq\frac{x}{6}\quad\text{and}\quad pqr\leq x.

Now, by
Lemma 3.3
, we have for
T\leq e^{\sqrt{\log x}}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq T}|S_{3}(x,p)|=O\left(\frac{x\log\alpha\log T}{\log x}\right).

(3.19)

Therefore,
(3.17) and (3.19) together give us

\displaystyle\Sigma^{T}:=\sum_{T<p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}|S_{3}(x,p)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{T<r\leq\frac{x}{T^{2}}}\sum_{T<q<r}^{*}\sum_{T<p<q}^{*}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr};p\right)

\displaystyle=x+O\left(\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}\right)+O\left(\frac{x\log\alpha\log T}{\log x}\right).

(3.20)

We now split
\Sigma^{T}
as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{T}=\Sigma_{1}^{T}+\Sigma_{2}^{T}+\Sigma_{3}^{T},

(3.21)

where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\;\;\sum_{T<p<q}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right),\hskip 14.22636pt

(3.22)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{T^{2}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<p<\min(q,x/qr)}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right),

(3.23)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{T^{2}}}\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<q<r}\;\;\sum_{T<p\leq\frac{x}{qr}}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right).

(3.24)

We now wish to compare
\Sigma^{T}
with an expression where the inner sum is replaced by an integral. Replace the inner sums of the above triple sums in (3.22)-(3.24) with corresponding integrals as follows: let us define pseudo-integrals
\mathcal{I}_{1}^{T},\mathcal{I}_{2}^{T}
and
\mathcal{I}_{3}^{T}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\;\;\int_{T}^{q}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right)\frac{dt}{\log t},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{T^{2}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\int_{T}^{\min(q,x/qr)}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right)\frac{dt}{\log t},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{T^{2}}}\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<q<r}\;\;\int_{T}^{\frac{x}{qr}}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right)\frac{dt}{\log t}.

For
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, we define
E_{i}=\Sigma_{i}^{T}-\mathcal{I}_{i}^{T}
. Therefore, from (3.21), we have that

\displaystyle\Sigma^{T}=\mathcal{I}_{1}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{2}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{3}^{T}+E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}.

(3.25)

We now move ahead to estimate each of the error terms
E_{i}
.

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\left(\sum_{T<p<q}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right)-\int_{T}^{q}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right)\frac{dt}{\log t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\left(\sum_{T<p<q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq\frac{x}{pqr}\\
P_{1}(n)\leq p\end{subarray}}1-\int_{T}^{q}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq\frac{x}{tqr}\\
P_{1}(n)\leq p\end{subarray}}1\right]\frac{dt}{\log t}\right)\hskip 68.28644pt

\displaystyle=\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tqr}}\left(\sum_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))\leq p\leq\min(\frac{x}{nqr},q)}1-\int_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))}^{\min(\frac{x}{nqr},q)}\frac{dt}{\log t}\right).

(3.26)

The difference between the sum and the integral inside the parenthesis on the right of (3.26) can be bounded using the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem. It is important to observe that the error in the strong form of the PNT that we use, namely
\frac{x}{e^{\sqrt{\log x}}}
, is an increasing function of
x
and therefore, we bound
E_{1}
by simply choosing
\frac{x}{nqr}
to be the upper limit of both the sum and integral and by discarding the lower limit. Thus, we get

\displaystyle|E_{1}|

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tqr}}\frac{x}{nqr\exp\{\sqrt{\log(\frac{x}{nqr})}\}}\ll\frac{x}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\frac{\log x}{qr}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x\log x}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\frac{\log\log x}{r}\ll\frac{x\log x(\log\log x)^{2}}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}.

(3.27)

This gives us an estimate for
E_{1}
. Since the inner sum and the corresponding inner integral in the error
E_{2}
are identical to that of
E_{1}
, we can write
E_{2}
as

\displaystyle E_{2}=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{T<q\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tqr}}\left(\sum_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))\leq p\leq\min(\frac{x}{nqr},q)}1-\int_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))}^{\min(\frac{x}{nqr},q)}\frac{dt}{\log t}\right).

(3.28)

We note here that the upper limit in both the sum and integral over
p
is essentially given by
\min\left(\frac{x}{nqr},q,\frac{x}{qr}\right)
. Since,
n\geq 1
, therefore it suffices to drop
\frac{x}{qr}
. A similar use of the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem therefore yields:

\displaystyle|E_{2}|

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{T<q\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tqr}}\frac{x}{nqr\exp\{\sqrt{\log(\frac{x}{nqr})}\}}\ll\frac{x}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{T<q\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\frac{\log x}{qr}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x\log x}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\frac{\log\log x}{r}\ll\frac{x\log x(\log\log x)^{2}}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}.

(3.29)

A similar treatment of
E_{3}
therefore will lead us to:

\displaystyle E_{3}=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<q<r}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tqr}}\left(\sum_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))\leq p\leq\frac{x}{nqr}}1-\int_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))}^{\frac{x}{nqr}}\frac{dt}{\log t}\right).

(3.30)

Therefore, again by the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem, we get

\displaystyle|E_{3}|

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<q<r}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tqr}}\frac{x}{nqr\exp\{\sqrt{\log(\frac{x}{nqr})}\}}\ll\frac{x}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<q<r}\frac{\log x}{qr}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x\log x}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\frac{\log\log x}{r}\ll\frac{x\log x(\log\log x)^{2}}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}.

(3.31)

Therefore combining all the identities and error estimates from (3.15) to (3.31) , we get that

\displaystyle x=\mathcal{I}_{1}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{2}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{3}^{T}+O\left(\frac{x\log\alpha\log T}{\log x}\right)+O\left(\frac{x\log x(\log\log x)^{2}}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\right).

(3.32)

Choosing
T=\exp\{(2\log\log x)^{2}\}
, we finally get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{2}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{3}^{T}=x+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{3}}{\log x}\right).

(3.33)

We now fix an arbitrary integer
\ell\geq 2
. For any integer
j
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1
, we consider the set
S_{3,\ell,j}(x)
of integers
n\leq x
with non-repeating largest and second largest prime factors with
\omega(n)\geq 2
and
P_{3}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)
. Then, we have, again from
Lemma 3.2
that

\displaystyle N_{3}(x;\ell,j)=|S_{3,\ell,j}(x)|+O\left(\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}\right).

(3.34)

We note here that

\displaystyle|S_{3,\ell,j}(x)|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}|S_{3}(x,p)|.

(3.35)

Of course, again using
Lemma 3.3
, we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq T\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}|S_{3}(x,p)|\leq\sum_{p\leq T}|S_{3}(x,p)|=O\left(\frac{x\log\alpha\log T}{\log x}\right).

(3.36)

We will specify the choice of
T
in the end of the proof. Therefore, from (3.35) and (3.36), we have

\displaystyle|S_{3,\ell,j}(x)|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T<p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}|S_{3}(x,p)|+O\left(\frac{x\log\alpha\log T}{\log x}\right)+O\left(\frac{x\log\log x}{\log x}\right).

(3.37)

We have already set up a summation representation of
|S_{3}(x,p)|
in (3.17). Therefore analogously, as done in (3.21), we can write that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T<p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}|S_{3}(x,p)|=\Sigma_{1,\ell,j}^{T}+\Sigma_{2,l\ell,j}^{T}+\Sigma_{3,\ell,j}^{T},

(3.38)

where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\;\;\sum_{T<p<q;\;p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{T<q\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T<p<\min(q,x/qr)\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<q<r}\;\;\sum_{T<p\leq\frac{x}{qr};\;p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right).

As done previously, we will replace these triple summations with corresponding integrals, with the difference being that the integrals are multiplied by a constant
\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}
owing to the PNTAP. Thus, we define
\mathcal{I}_{1,\ell,j}^{T},\mathcal{I}_{2,\ell,j}^{T}
and
\mathcal{I}_{3,\ell,j}^{T}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\;\;\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{T}^{q}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right)\frac{dt}{\log t},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{T<q\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{T}^{\min(q,x/qr)}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right)\frac{dt}{\log t},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<q<r}\;\;\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{T}^{\frac{x}{qr}}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pqr},p\right)\frac{dt}{\log t}.

We aim to replace
\Sigma_{i,\ell,j}^{T}
with
\mathcal{I}_{i,\ell,j}^{T}
,
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, in (3.38). So we denote the difference by

\displaystyle E_{i,\ell,j}=\Sigma_{i,\ell,j}^{T}-\mathcal{I}_{i,\ell,j}^{T},\quad i=1,2,3.

Thus,

\displaystyle E_{1,\ell,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T<r\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{T<q<r}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tqr}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max(T,P_{1}(n))\leq p\leq\min(\frac{x}{nqr},q)\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}1-\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))}^{\min(\frac{x}{nqr},q)}\frac{dt}{\log t}\right),

\displaystyle E_{2,\ell,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{T<q\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tqr}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max(T,P_{1}(n))\leq p\leq\min(\frac{x}{nqr},q)\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}1-\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))}^{\min(\frac{x}{nqr},q)}\frac{dt}{\log t}\right),

\displaystyle E_{3,\ell,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<r\leq\frac{x}{6}}\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{3}}<q<r}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tqr}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max(T,P_{1}(n))\leq p\leq\frac{x}{nqr}\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}1-\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))}^{\frac{x}{nqr}}\frac{dt}{\log t}\right).

We bound
E_{i,\ell,j}
, for
i=1,2,3
, in exactly the same form as we bounded
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}
above, except that here we use the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem in Arithmetic Progressions, and the fact that the error
\frac{x}{e^{\sqrt{\log x}}}
is an increasing function of
x
. So, we get that for every
i
,

\displaystyle E_{i,\ell,j}\ll\frac{x\log x(\log\log x)^{2}}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}.

Using this above estimate in (3.38), we therefore get that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T<p\leq x^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}|S_{3}(x,p)|=\mathcal{I}_{1,\ell,j}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{2,\ell,j}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{3,\ell,j}^{T}+O\left(\frac{x\log x(\log\log x)^{2}}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\right).

(3.39)

But, note that
\mathcal{I}_{i,\ell,j}^{T}=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\mathcal{I}_{i}^{T}
. Therefore, combining equations (3.34)-(3.39) along with this fact, we get that

\displaystyle N_{3}(x;\ell,j)=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}(\mathcal{I}_{1}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{2}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{3}^{T})+O\left(\frac{x\log\alpha\log T}{\log x}\right)+O\left(\frac{x\log x(\log\log x)^{2}}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\right).

(3.40)

Taking
T=\exp\{(2\log\log x)^{2}\}
, we get from (3.40) that

\displaystyle N_{3}(x;\ell,j)=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}(\mathcal{I}_{1}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{2}^{T}+\mathcal{I}_{3}^{T})+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{3}}{\log x}\right).

(3.41)

Finally, from (3.33) and (3.41), we finally get our desired result:

\displaystyle N_{3}(x;\ell,j)=\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)}+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{3}}{\log x}\right).

∎

4
Main Results for
k=3
using Third Order Duality

We start with the following theorem that give us an estimate of the sum of
\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}
with the condition that the smallest prime factor of
n
is congruent to
j\;(mod\;\ell)
. We need to use the result in
Theorem 3.4
together with the third order duality to establish the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1

For integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1
, we have

\displaystyle M_{\omega^{2}}(x,j,\ell):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{5}}{\log x}.

(4.1)

Proof:
We use the Duality Lemma with
k=3
. This yields

\displaystyle\sum_{1<d|n}\mu(d){\omega(d)-1\choose 2}f(p_{1}(d))=-f(P_{3}(n)).

(4.2)

Next, we apply (4.2) by choosing
f
to be an arithmetic function on primes defined as follows:

\displaystyle f(n)=\begin{cases}1,\;\text{if $n$ is a prime and $n\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)$}\\
0,\;\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(4.3)

Now, using Möbius inversion in (4.2), we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)(\omega(n)-1)(\omega(n)-2)f(p_{1}(n))=-\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)f(P_{3}(d)).

(4.4)

Applying the hyperbola method in the double sum of the RHS of (4.4), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)f(P_{3}(d))=\sum_{m\leq T}\mu(T)\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{m}}f(P_{3}(d))+\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{T}}f(P_{3}(d))\sum_{T<m\leq\frac{x}{d}}\mu(m)=:\mathcal{S}_{1}+\mathcal{S}_{2},

(4.5)

where
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
are respectively the double sums in the RHS of (4.5).
Looking at the double sum
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}=\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{T}}f(P_{3}(d))\sum_{T<m\leq\frac{x}{d}}\mu(m)\ll\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{T}}f(P_{3}(d))\frac{x}{de^{\sqrt{\log(x/d)}}}\ll\frac{x\log x}{e^{\sqrt{\log T}}}.

(4.6)

Further, looking at the inner sum of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
, we observe that using
Theorem 3.4

\displaystyle\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{m}}f(P_{3}(d))=\frac{x}{m\varphi(\ell)}+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{3}}{m\log(x/m)}\right).

Therefore continuing with the estimating of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
using the above, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\leq T}\mu(m)\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{m}}f(P_{3}(d))=\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)}\sum_{m\leq T}\frac{\mu(m)}{m}+O\left(x(\log\log x)^{3}\sum_{m\leq T}\frac{1}{m\log(x/m)}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)e^{\sqrt{\log(T)}}}+O\left(\frac{x\log T(\log\log x)^{3}}{\log(x/T)}\right).

(4.7)

Now, by the following suitable choice of
T=e^{(2\log\log x)^{2}}
, we get from (4.5)-(4.7) that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)f(P_{3}(d))\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{5}}{\log x}.

(4.8)

But, here we note that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)(\omega(n)-1)(\omega(n)-2)f(p_{1}(n))=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}f(p_{1}(n))-3\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)f(p_{1}(n))

\displaystyle+2\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)f(p_{1}(n)),

which gives us that

\displaystyle M_{\omega^{2}}(x,j,\ell)=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)(\omega(n)-1)(\omega(n)-2)f(p_{1}(n))+3\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)f(p_{1}(n))

\displaystyle-2\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)f(p_{1}(n)).

(4.9)

Also, from
Theorem 8
in
[
2
]
and
Theorem 3
in
[
3
]
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)f(p_{1}(n))\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{4}}{\log x}\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)f(p_{1}(n))\ll xe^{-(\log x)^{1/3}}.

(4.10)

Therefore, from (4.4), along with (4.8), (4.9) and (4.10), we finally get our required result. ∎

The importance of
Theorem 3.4
has already been realized in the above theorem. The fact that the average of the third largest prime factors of integers in reduced residue class
j\;(mod\;\ell)
is
\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}
will also prove to be important in one of the upcoming theorems.

Theorem 4.2:

For integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left\{\frac{x}{n}\right\}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{7/2}}{\sqrt{\log x}}.

Proof:
We will prove
Theorem 4.2
using
Theorem A
and therefore, a correct choice of the functions
\eta(x)

and
\beta(x)
will suffice.
We choose the sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n}
such that for all positive integers
n
,
a_{n}=\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}f(n)
, where
f
is defined in (4.3). Then, by
Theorem 4.1
, we choose
\eta(x)
in
Theorem A
to be

\displaystyle\eta(x)=\frac{(\log\log x)^{5}}{\log x}.

(4.11)

Of course,
\eta(x)
satisfies the conditions in (2.34a) and (2.34b). Further, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}|a_{n}|\leq\sum_{n\leq x}\omega(n)^{2}\ll x(\log\log x)^{2}.

(4.12)

Therefore, from (4.12), we choose
\beta(x)
in
Theorem A
to be

\displaystyle\beta(x)=(\log\log x)^{2}.

(4.13)

Again,
\beta(x)
satisfies the conditions in (2.36). Thus, by
Theorem A
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left\{\frac{x}{n}\right\}=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\left\{\frac{x}{n}\right\}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{7/2}}{\sqrt{\log x}},

(4.14)

which proves our theorem. ∎

We next consider the sum with
\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]
replacing
\left\{\frac{x}{n}\right\}
. We then have the following theorem:

Theorem 4.3

For integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{3}}{\log x}.

(4.15)

Proof:
We recall the function
f
defined in (4.3). We replace the condition in the summation using
f
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}f(p_{1}(n))\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]=\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\omega(d)^{2}f(p_{1}(d)).

(4.16)

Rewriting
\omega(d)^{2}
as follows,

\displaystyle\omega(d)^{2}=(\omega(d)-1)(\omega(d)-2)+3(\omega(d)-1)+1,

we get from (4.16) that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]=\sum_{n\leq x}

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)(\omega(d)-1)(\omega(d)-2)f(p_{1}(d))\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]

\displaystyle+3\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)(\omega(d)-1)f(p_{1}(d))\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]+\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)f(p_{1}(d))\left[\frac{x}{n}\right].

(4.17)

Using the Duality Identity in (1.11) for
k=3
,
k=2
and
k=1
respectively, for the inner sums in each of the double sums on the right of (4.17), we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]=-2\sum_{n\leq x}f(P_{3}(n))+3\sum_{n\leq x}f(P_{2}(n))-\sum_{n\leq x}f(P_{1}(n)).

(4.18)

Therefore, using
Theorem 3.4
above,
Theorem 7
in
[
2
]
and
Theorem 4
in
[
3
]
together in (4.18), we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]

\displaystyle=(-2+3-1)\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)}+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{3}}{\log x}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{3}}{\log x}\right).

(4.19)

Hence, we have our theorem proved. ∎

The beauty of
Theorem 4.3
is that the surprising cancellation of the main term that takes place after the use of the Duality Lemma. This is why, to have exact same averages of all the largest, second largest and third largest prime factors in the same reduced residue classes is so significant in this proof. We now go on to state and prove the final theorem of the section, which is our main result for the case
k=3
. We have:

Theorem 4.4:

For integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}}{n}=O\left(\frac{(\log\log x)^{7/2}}{\sqrt{\log x}}\right).

(4.20)

Hence, taking limit
x\to\infty
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq n\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}}{n}=0.

(4.21)

Proof:
Adding inequations (4.14) and (4.15) of
Theorem 4.2
and
Theorem 4.3
, we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\cdot\frac{x}{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left\{\frac{x}{n}\right\}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{7/2}}{\sqrt{\log x}}+\frac{x(\log\log x)^{3}}{\log x}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{7/2}}{\sqrt{\log x}}.

(4.22)

Canceling by a factor of
x
on both sides of (4.22), we therefore have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{2}}{n}=O\left(\frac{(\log\log x)^{7/2}}{\sqrt{\log x}}\right).

Finally, taking the limit
x\to\infty
, we get (4.21). ∎

as its corollary. One can follow the same methods used above to get a quantitative evaluation of the same.

(4.23)

(4.24)

We now move on to get the general form of the results that we have already proved in the

case. It is important to note here that the techniques used to prove results in

were drastically different, and the error estimates were much stronger. We will see, as we continue, that as the value of

5
The
k^{th}
largest Prime Factor and its Uniform Distribution in Reduced Residue Classes
mod\;\ell

As we saw in the case of the third largest prime factor
P_{3}(n)
, we can also define the
k^{th}
largest prime factor, denoted as
P_{k}(n)
, in two ways:

(i)

P_{j}(n):=P_{1}\left(\frac{n}{\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}P_{i}(n)}\right)
,
j=2,\cdots,k
, if
\Omega(n)\geq k
, and
P_{k}(n)=1
, if
\Omega(n)<k
.

(ii)

If
n=p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdot p_{2}^{e^{2}}\cdots p_{r}^{e_{r}}
, with
p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{r}
, then
P_{j}(n):=p_{k}
, for
j=1,\cdots,k
, if
r=\omega(n)\geq k
, and if
r<k
, then
P_{k}(n)=1
.

It is quite clear that the definitions are different for integers
n
whose prime factors would repeat. But, as done before, we bypass this inconsistency with the help of the following lemma, which generalizes
Lemma 3.1
.

Lemma 5.1:

For an integer
k\geq 3
, let
N_{k-1}(x)
denote the integers less than or equal to
x
such at least one of the
P_{i}(n)
’s, for
1\leq i\leq k-1
, repeats in the prime factorization of
n
. Then

\displaystyle N_{k-1}(x)\ll_{k}\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{\log x}.

(5.1)

Proof:
We start by considering the function that counts the number of integers
\leq x
whose
k^{th}
largest prime factor in the sense of (ii) is
\leq T
.

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(x,T):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P_{k}(n)\leq T\end{subarray}}1.

(5.2)

It follows from a result of Tenenbaum
[
22
]
that for

T\leq\exp^{(\log x)^{1-\delta}},
where
\delta>0
, and for an arbitrary fixed integer
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(x,T)\ll_{k,\delta}\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}\log T}{\log x}.

(5.3)

We observe that
Lemma 3.3
in §3.2 is the case
k=3
(also see
Theorem 6
in
[
2
]
for the
k=2
case). So, we now assume that
k\geq 3
.

To bound the number of integers
\leq x
for which at least one of the prime factors
P_{1}(n),P_{2}(n),\cdots,P_{k-1}(n)
repeats, we consider two cases:

Case-I:

P_{k-1}(n)\leq T
.

In this case, the number of such integers is trivially

\displaystyle\leq\Psi_{k-1}(x,T)\ll_{k,\delta}\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-3}\log T}{\log x}.

(5.4)

Case-II:

P_{k-1}>T
.

In this case, each of
P_{1}(n),P_{2}(n),\cdots,P_{k-1}(n)
is
>T
. Now, the number of integers
\leq x
for which a given prime factor
p
repeats is
O\left(\frac{x}{p^{2}}\right)
. Thus, considering the
k-1
cases of
P_{1}(n),P_{2}(n),\cdots,P_{k-1}(n)
repeating, we deduce that the number of integers under Case-II, is

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p>T}(k-1)\cdot\frac{x}{p^{2}}\ll(k-1)\cdot\frac{x}{T\log^{2}T}

(5.5)

Thus,

\displaystyle N_{k-1}(x)\ll_{k,\delta}\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-3}\log T}{\log x}+(k-1)\cdot\frac{x}{T\log^{2}T}.

(5.6)

With the choice of

\displaystyle T=\frac{\log x}{(\log\log x)^{k}},

we have our required bound from (5.6). As the choice of
k
is arbitrary, our lemma is proved. ∎

Remark I:
We see that the
Lemma 5.1
makes it clear that the integers where the above definitions of
P_{k}(n)
are inconsistent, whose size is small as compared to
x
. Henceforth, the
P_{k}(n)
will be defined in a strict sense as in (ii).

Remark II:
Tenenbaum
[
22
]
established a stronger version of (5.3) valid uniformly for

, with

replaced by

Theorem 5.2:

P_{k}(x)\rightarrow\infty
as
x\rightarrow\infty
almost always, for any positive integer
k
. Further, for a fixed positive integer
k\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(x,T)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P_{k}(n)\leq T\end{subarray}}1\ll_{k,\delta}\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}\log T}{\log x},

(5.7)

for all
T\leq e^{(\log x)^{1-\delta}}
, where
\delta>0
.

Proof:
We start with the following routine counting functions:

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(x,T)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P_{k}(n)\leq T\end{subarray}}1\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\Psi_{k}^{(p)}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P_{k}(n)=p\end{subarray}}1.

(5.8)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(x,T)=\sum_{p\leq T}\Psi_{k}^{(p)}(x).

(5.9)

We first estimate
\Psi_{k}^{(p)}(x)
. Let
n=m.pp_{k-1}p_{k-2}\cdots p_{2}p_{1}
and
P_{i}(n)=p_{i}
, where
1\leq i\leq k-1
,
P_{k}(n)=p\leq e^{(\log x)^{1-\delta}}
and
P_{1}(m)\leq p
. Note here that,
p<p_{k-1}<p_{k-2}<\cdots<p_{2}<p_{1}
. For a fixed
p
, we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}^{(p)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq\frac{x}{p^{k}}\\
P_{1}(m)\leq p\end{subarray}}\;\;\sum_{p^{k-1}\leq p_{k-1}p_{k-2}\cdots p_{2}p_{1}\leq\frac{x}{mp}}1

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq\frac{x}{p^{k}}\\
P_{1}(m)\leq p\end{subarray}}\frac{x\left(\log\log(\frac{x}{mp})\right)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!mp\log(\frac{x}{mp})},\;\;\left[\text{since $\pi_{k-1}(x)\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!\log x}$}\right].

(5.10)

We split the summation on the right of (5.10) as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}^{(p)}(x)\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq\exp{(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}\\
P_{1}(m)\leq p\end{subarray}}\frac{x\left(\log\log(\frac{x}{mp})\right)^{k-2}}{mp\log(\frac{x}{mp})}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\exp{(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}\leq m\leq\frac{x}{p^{k}}\\
P_{1}(m)\leq p\end{subarray}}\frac{x\left(\log\log(\frac{x}{mp})\right)^{k-2}}{mp\log(\frac{x}{mp})}\;\;=:\;\mathcal{S}_{1}+\mathcal{S}_{2}.

(5.11)

CASE-I:
Estimating
\mathcal{S}_{1}
:

We observe that
(\log\log(x/mp))^{k-2}\ll(\log\log x)^{k-2}
and
\log(x/mp)\gg\log x
. Using these, we have from
\mathcal{S}_{1}
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{p\log x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq\exp{(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}\\
P_{1}(m)\leq p\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{p\log x}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq p\\
q\;\text{is a prime}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)^{-1}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}\log p}{p\log x}.

(5.12)

CASE-II:
Estimating
\mathcal{S}_{2}
:

Here, using
\log(x/mp)\geq\log p
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}\leq\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{p\log p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\exp{(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}\leq m\\
P_{1}(m)\leq p\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m}<\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{p\log p}\int_{\exp(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}^{\infty}\frac{d\Psi(t,p)}{t}.

(5.13)

Now, looking at the integral above, we use integration by parts to get

\displaystyle\int_{\exp(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}^{\infty}\frac{d\Psi(t,p)}{t}=\frac{\Psi(t,p)}{t}\Big|_{\exp{(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}}^{\infty}+\int_{\exp(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}^{\infty}\frac{\Psi(t,p)}{t^{2}}dt.

(5.14)

Using the estimate (3.2) of
\Psi(x,T)
in (5.14), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\exp(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}^{\infty}\frac{d\Psi(t,p)}{t}\ll e^{-\alpha_{0}/2}+\int_{\exp(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{te^{\alpha(t)/2}},

(5.15)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}>\frac{\log e^{(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}}{\log e^{{(\log x)}^{1-\delta}}}=(\log x)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\alpha(t)=\frac{\log t}{\log p}>\frac{\log e^{(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}}{\log e^{{(\log x)}^{1-\delta}}}=(\log x)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}.

This helps us rewrite (5.15) as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\exp(\log x)^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}^{\infty}\frac{d\Psi(t,p)}{t}\ll\exp(-(\log x)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}).

(5.16)

Hence, combining (5.13) with (5.16), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{\exp((\log x)^{\frac{\delta}{2}})p\log p}.

(5.17)

Thus, using (5.11), (5.12), and (5.17), we get that

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}^{(p)}(x)\ll\mathcal{S}_{1}+\mathcal{S}_{2}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}\log p}{p\log x}+\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{\exp((\log x)^{\frac{\delta}{2}})p\log p}.

Next, by definition, we have that

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(x,T)=\sum_{p\leq T}\Psi_{k}^{(p)}(x)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{\log x}\sum_{p\leq T}\frac{\log p}{p}+\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{\exp(\log x)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}}\sum_{p\leq T}\frac{1}{p\log p}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{\log x}\log T+\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}}{\exp(\log x)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}\log T}{\log x}.

(5.18)

Hence, we have our required result.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{1}\leq\frac{x}{2\cdot 3\cdots q_{k-1}}}\;\;\cdots\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{k-1}\leq p_{k-2}}\;\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T<p<\frac{x}{p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k-1}}\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{pp_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}};p\right).

Similar to what we did earlier, we replace each of these multi-sums with the following expressions denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{i,\ell,j}^{T}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{T<p_{1}\leq x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\;\;\sum_{T<p_{2}<p_{1}}\;\;\cdots\;\;\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{T}^{p_{k-1}}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{tp_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}};t\right)\frac{dt}{\log t};

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{i,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{1}\leq\frac{x}{2\cdot 3\cdots q_{k-1}}}\cdots\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{i-1}\leq p_{i}}\;\;\sum_{T<p_{i}\leq x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\;\;\sum_{T<p_{i+1}<p_{i}}\cdots\;\;\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{T}^{\min(p_{k-1},\frac{x}{p_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}})}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{tp_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}};t\right)\frac{dt}{\log t},

\displaystyle\hskip 355.65944pt\text{when $2\leq i\leq k-1$};

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell,j}^{T}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{1}\leq\frac{x}{2\cdot 3\cdots q_{k-1}}}\;\;\cdots\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{k-1}\leq p_{k-2}}\;\;\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{T}^{\frac{x}{p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k-1}}}\Psi\left(\frac{x}{tp_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}};t\right)\frac{dt}{\log t}.

Next we look at the differences
E_{i,\ell,j}:=\Sigma_{i,\ell,j}^{T}-\mathcal{I}_{i,\ell,j}^{T}
. Using the above replacements in (5.39), we have

\displaystyle N_{k}(x;\ell,j)=\mathcal{I}_{1,\ell,j}^{T}+\cdots+\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell,j}^{T}+E_{1,\ell,j}+\cdots+E_{k,\ell,j}+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}\log T}{\log x}\right),

(5.40)

where the error terms can be written as

\displaystyle E_{1,\ell,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T<p_{1}\leq x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\;\;\sum_{T<p_{2}<p_{1}}\;\;\cdots\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tp_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max(T,P_{1}(n))\leq p\leq\min(\frac{x}{np_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}},p_{k-1})\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}1-\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))}^{\min(\frac{x}{np_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}},p_{k-1})}\frac{dt}{\log t}\right);

\displaystyle E_{i,\ell,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{1}\leq\frac{x}{2\cdot 3\cdots q_{k-1}}}\cdots\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{i-1}\leq p_{i}}\;\;\sum_{T<p_{i}\leq x^{\frac{1}{k}}}\;\;\sum_{T<p_{i+1}<p_{i}}\;\;\cdots

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tp_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max(T,P_{1}(n))\leq p\leq\min(\frac{x}{np_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}},p_{k-1})\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}1-\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))}^{\min(\frac{x}{np_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}},p_{k-1})}\frac{dt}{\log t}\right),\;\text{when $2\leq i\leq k-1$};

\displaystyle E_{k,\ell,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{1}\leq\frac{x}{2\cdot 3\cdots q_{k-1}}}\;\;\cdots\;\;\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{k}}<p_{k-1}\leq p_{k-2}}\;\;\sum_{n\leq\frac{x}{Tp_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max(T,P_{1}(n))\leq p\leq\frac{x}{np_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}}\\
p\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}1-\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\int_{\max(T,P_{1}(n))}^{\frac{x}{np_{k-1}\cdots p_{1}}}\frac{dt}{\log t}\right).

Using the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem in Arithmetic Progressions and the summation estimates, we then deduce that for every
1\leq i\leq k
,

\displaystyle|E_{i,\ell,j}|\ll\frac{x\log x(\log\log x)^{k-1}}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}.

(5.41)

Hence, from (5.40) and (5.41), we get that

\displaystyle N_{k}(x;\ell,j)=\mathcal{I}_{1,\ell,j}^{T}+\cdots+\mathcal{I}_{k,\ell,j}^{T}+O\left(\frac{x\log x(\log\log x)^{k-1}}{\exp\{\sqrt{\log T}\}}\right)+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k-2}\log T}{\log x}\right).

(5.42)

But then, we note that
\mathcal{I}_{i,\ell,j}^{T}=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\mathcal{I}_{i}^{T}
, for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Therefore, using this in (5.42), and choosing
T=\exp\{(2\log\log x)^{2}\}
, we finally get from (5.35) that

\displaystyle N_{k}(x;\ell,j)=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}(\mathcal{I}_{1}^{T}+\cdots+\mathcal{I}_{k}^{T})+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k}}{\log x}\right)=\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)}+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k}}{\log x}\right).

Therefore, we have our required result that the sequence of
k^{th}
largest prime factors of integers is uniformly distributed in the reduced residue classes. ∎

As remarked at the end of §3, it is quite fascinating to see that the averages of any
k^{th}
largest prime factors, where
k
is arbitrarily chosen, is the same in reduced residue classes, and every time it is
\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}
, when we consider the reduced residue classes
mod\;\ell
. This is an extremely important property, due to which our generalizations (see next section) of our previous results are possible. With unequal averages for every sequence
k^{th}
largest prime factors, for different integers
k
, providing a generalization would have been acutely hard. This is another reason why the use of induction is so powerful in the final theorems that are to come in the next section.

The Main Theorems for all orders
k\geq 4

In this section, we extend the results of §4 to all orders
k\geq 4
. We start with the following:

Theorem 6.1

For any fixed integer
k\geq 4
, and integers
j,\ell
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
(j,\ell)=1
, we have that

\displaystyle M_{\omega^{k-1}}(x,j,\ell):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k-1}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k+2}}{\log x}.

(6.1)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle M_{\omega_{k-1}}(x,j,\ell):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega_{k-1}(n)\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k+2}}{\log x},

(6.2)

where
\omega_{k-1}(n)={\omega(n)-1\choose k-1}
.

Proof:
We start by recalling the function that we defined at the start of the proof of
Theorem 4.1
:
f
is an arithmetic function on primes defined as follows:

\displaystyle f(n)=\begin{cases}1,\;\text{if $n$ is a prime and $n\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)$}\\
0,\;\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(6.3)

Let us also recall the following version of the Duality Lemma in (1.11), that we will need in this proof:

\displaystyle\sum_{1<d|n}\mu(d){\omega(d)-1\choose k-1}f(p_{1}(d))=(-1)^{k}f(P_{k}(d)).

(6.4)

To prove
Theorem 6.1
, we will first establish (6.2) for any given
k
and utilize the truth of (6.2) for
t\leq k-1
to deduce (6.1).

Using Möbius Inversion on (6.4), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)(\omega(n)-1)\cdots(\omega(n)-k+1)f(p_{1}(n))=(-1)^{k}(k-1)!\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)f(P_{k}(d)).

(6.5)

Without worrying about the constant in front, we estimate the double sum on the RHS of (6.5) using the hyperbola method. We rewrite the double sum as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)f(P_{k}(d))=\sum_{m\leq T}\mu(m)\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{m}}f(P_{k}(d))+\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{T}}f(P_{k}(d))\sum_{T<m\leq\frac{x}{d}}\mu(m),

(6.6)

for some suitable
T
to be chosen later. Using the estimate of the sum of
\mu(m)
, we get that the second double sum in (6.6) has the following estimate:

\displaystyle\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{T}}f(P_{k}(d))\sum_{T<m\leq\frac{x}{d}}\mu(m)\ll\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{T}}f(P_{k}(d))\frac{x}{de^{\sqrt{\log(x/d)}}}\ll\frac{x\log x}{e^{\sqrt{\log T}}}.

(6.7)

Further, using
Theorem 5.3
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{m}}f(P_{k}(d))=\frac{x}{m\varphi(\ell)}+O\left(\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k}}{m\log(x/m)}\right).

(6.8)

Therefore, using (6.8) in the first double sum of (6.6), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m\leq T}\mu(m)\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{m}}f(P_{t}(d))

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)}\sum_{m\leq T}\frac{\mu(m)}{m}+O\left(x(\log\log x)^{k}\sum_{m\leq T}\frac{1}{m\log(x/m)}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)e^{\sqrt{\log(T)}}}+O\left(\frac{x\log T(\log\log x)^{t}}{\log(x/T)}\right).

(6.9)

Therefore, choosing
T=e^{(2\log\log x)^{2}}
, we have from (6.6)-(6.9) that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)f(P_{k}(d))\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k+2}}{\log x},

(6.10)

and hence, (6.2) follows from (6.5) and (6.10). Next, write

\displaystyle\omega(n)^{k-1}=\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}s_{k-1,t}\omega_{t}(n),

(6.11)

where
s_{k-1,t}
are given in terms of Sterling numbers of the second kind, with
s_{k-1,k-1}=1
(for the definition of Sterling numbers of the second kind, see 6.23 below). Hence,

\displaystyle M_{\omega^{k-1}}(x;j,\ell)=\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}s_{k-1,t}M_{\omega_{t}}(x;j,\ell).

(6.12)

Then (6.1) follows by utilizing in (6.12) the upper bound for

M_{\omega_{t}}(x;j,\ell)
for all
t\leq k-1
. That proves
Theorem 6.1
.
∎

As done earlier, we now estimate the sum with the same summand as that of above in

Theorem 6.2:

For any fixed integer

, and integers

satisfying

and

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\

Proof:
Just like in

from §2 to prove

to be
\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k-1}f(p_{1}(n))

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k+2}}{\log x}.

Theorem 6.3:

For any fixed integer
k\geq 4
, and integers

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\

(6.16)

Proof:
Let us start by looking at the following summation:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)\omega_{t-2}(n)\left[\frac{x}{n}\right],

where
\omega_{t-2}(n)={\omega(n)-1\choose t-2}
, where
t\geq 5
. Using the definition (6.3) of the function

, we have from the above sum that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)\omega_{t-2}(n)\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\omega(n)\omega_{t-2}(n)f(p_{1}(n))\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\omega(d)\omega_{t-2}(d)f(p_{1}(d)).

(6.17)

Now, we notice that

\displaystyle\omega(n)\omega_{t-2}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(t-2)!}\cdot\omega(n)(\omega(n)-1)(\omega(n)-2)\cdots(\omega(n)-t+2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(t-2)!}\left[(\omega(n)-1)\cdots(\omega(n)-t+1)+(t-1)(\omega(n)-1)\cdots(\omega(n)-t+2)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(t-2)!}\left[(t-1)!\cdot{\omega(n)-1\choose t-1}+(t-1)\cdot(t-2)!\cdot{\omega(n)-1\choose(t-1)-1}\right]

\displaystyle=(t-1)\left[{\omega(n)-1\choose t-1}+{\omega(n)-1\choose(t-1)-1}\right].

(6.18)

Now, using (6.18) in the summand of the double sum in the RHS of (6.17), we get by splitting the summation that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)\omega_{t-2}(n)\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]=(t-1)\left[\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu(d){\omega(d)-1\choose t-1}f(p_{1}(d))\right.\hskip 56.9055pt

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{d|n}\mu(d){\omega(d)-1\choose(t-1)-1}f(p_{1}(d))\right].

(6.19)

Applying the higher order duality identity in (6.4) for
k=t
and

respectively in the above two double sums, we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)\omega_{t-2}(n)\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]

\displaystyle=(t-1)\left[\sum_{n\leq x}(-1)^{t}f(P_{t}(n))+\sum_{n\leq x}(-1)^{t-1}f(P_{t-1}(n))\right]

\displaystyle=(t-1)(-1)^{t}\left[\sum_{n\leq x}f(P_{t}(n))-\sum_{n\leq x}f(P_{t-1}(n))\right].

(6.20)

By
Theorem 5.3
, for both the sums inside the brackets in the RHS of (6.20), we have a cancellation of the main terms to deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\mu(n)\omega(n)\omega_{t-2}(n)\left[\frac{x}{n}\right]\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{t}}{\log x}.

(6.21)

It is important to note here that (6.21) has been proved in

and

for both

(

) and

\displaystyle\omega(n)^{k-1}=\sum_{t=2}^{k}C_{t,k-1}\omega(n)\omega_{t-2}(n),

(6.22)

where

are constants. Therefore, (6.21) and (6.22) together prove our required result.∎

Remark:
Note that the case of
k=3
corresponding to
Theorem 6.3
is
Theorem 4.3
in §4. But we have used different methods to deduce these two results. In the proof of
Theorem 4.3
, we have utilized a cancellation, namely
1-3+2=0
, which eliminates the main term of size
\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)}
, thereby giving a bound
o(x)
. This cancellation is due to a property of
the Stirling numbers of the second kind which are defined by

\displaystyle x^{k-1}=\sum^{k}_{j=1}S_{k,j}(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-j+1).

(6.23)

It is known that the Stirling numbers of the second kind satisfy the following
recurrence:

\displaystyle S_{k,j}=jS_{k-1,j}+S_{k-1,j-1},

(6.24)

where
S_{k,j}
is the member in the Stirling’s pyramid in the
j^{th}
position of the
k^{th}
row. We set
S_{k,0}=0=S_{k,k+1}
and note that
S_{k,1}=1=S_{k,k}
. We now claim that the following cancellation holds:

Lemma 6.4:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}(j-1)!S_{k,j}=0.

Remark:
This is the cancellation property that generalizes what he had in (4.19) and
can be used to prove
Theorem 6.3
.

Proof:
We observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}(j-1)!S_{k,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}(j-1)!\left\{jS_{k-1,j}+S_{k-1,j-1}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j}j!S_{k-1,j}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{j}j!S_{k-1,j}.

Using the fact that
S_{k-1,k}=0=S_{k-1,0}
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}(j-1)!S_{k,j}=-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{j}j!S_{k-1,j}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{j}j!S_{k-1,j}=0,

which proves the lemma. ∎

To prove

utilizing this cancellation, we need to write

\displaystyle\omega^{k-1}(n)=\sum^{k}_{j=1}S_{k,j}(\omega(n)-1)(\omega(n)-2)\cdots(\omega(n)-j+1)

and utilize bounds for each of the sums
M_{\omega_{t}}(x;j,\ell)
given by (6.2). The main term of size
\frac{x}{\varphi(\ell)}
will be eliminated because of the cancellation given above, thereby leading to the bound for

given
in Theorem 6.3. The proof of

given above uses a different technique to bypass the use of the sum over
k
terms by the introduction of the new term

, which only requires an estimation of two double sums (see (6.18), (6.19) above).

For any fixed integer
k\geq 4
, and integers satisfying

and

(6.25)

Further, taking

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=2\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k-1}}{n}=0.

Proof:
Combining the estimates (6.13) and (6.16), we have that

\displaystyle x\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
p_{1}(n)\equiv j\;(mod\;\ell)\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(n)\omega(n)^{k-1}}{n}\ll\frac{x(\log\log x)^{k+\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{\log x}}.

(6.26)

Then, canceling
x
on both sides of (6.26), we get (6.25) and hence, our main result is proved. ∎

. The general case

k\geq 3
of (7.3) will be treated in a subsequent paper of ours.

7.2
Algebraic Analogues of Duality between Prime Factors

Forty years after the paper of the first author, spurred by a suggestion of Ken
Ono, Dawsey
[
6
]
extended the results in
[
3
]
to a number field setting.
More precisely, consider a Galois extension
K
of the rationals
\mathbb{Q}
, and
\mathcal{O}_{K}
,
the ring of integers in
K
. If
p
is a rational integer prime, let
\mathfrak{p}
be the
prime ideal contained in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
which lies above
p
. If
p
is unramified in
K
, let

\left[\frac{K/\mathbb{Q}}{p}\right]
denote the Artin symbol. Now consider any conjugacy class
C
in the Galois group
G=Gal(G/Q)
. Then Dawsey’s result is

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 2\\
\left[\frac{K/\mathbb{Q}}{p_{1}(n)}\right]=C\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}=\frac{|C|}{|G|}.

(7.4)

When
K
is an
\ell^{th}-
cyclotomic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
,
G
can be identified with
\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{\ell}
, the set of reduced residues
(mod\,\ell)
for some positive integer
\ell
.
Since
\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{\ell}
is Abelian, each conjugacy class has just one element, and so

\displaystyle\frac{|C|}{|G}=\frac{1}{\phi(\ell)}.

Just as Alladi used the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem for Arithmetic
Progressions, Dawsey used the strong form of the Chebotarev Density Theorem due to Lagarias and Odlyzko
[
14
]
to show

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq n\leq x\\
\left[\frac{K/\mathbb{Q}}{P_{1}(n)}\right]=C\end{subarray}}1\sim\frac{|C|}{|G|}x,

(7.5)

Then by methods involving Duality as in
[
3
]
, Dawsey deduces (7.4) from
(7.5).

Dawsey’s paper led to a flurry of activity on algebraic extensions of the Duality results in
[
3
]
. Sweeting and Woo
[
21
]
extending Dawsey’s results to the case of
L
being a Galois extension of a general number field
K
, and in that process generalized the Möbius function by defining it in terms of products of prime ideals instead of products of primes. The results of Sweeting-Woo were further generalized by Kural et-al
[
13
]
. There was also an extension of the results of
[
3
]

by Wang
[
26
]
, who replaced the Möbius function by the Ramanujan sum
c_{m}(n)
, which is the sum of the
m
-th powers of the primitive
n
-th roots of unity. Note that
c_{1}(n)=\mu(n)
. Subsequently, Duan, Wang, and Yi
[
9
]
, discussed Alladi’s Duality in global function fields. There are also other works of Wang on the first order duality -
[
25
]
, in which
\mu(n)
is replaced by Liouville’s function
\lambda(n)
, and
[
27
]
, where
\mu(n)
is replaced by
\mu(n)\log n
.

While all these algebraic extensions of Alladi’s First Order Duality were being
established, consequences of the Higher Order Duality identities in
[
3
]
remained unexplored until Alladi-Johnson in 2022 explored the Second Order Duality identity (1.10) for
k=2
(see
[
2
]
), and established among other things, their
main result (1.12) in quantitative form. Motivated by this and Dawsey’s theorem
(7.4), Sengupta
[
19
]
extended (1.12) to an algebraic setting, namely in the case of a Galois extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
. More recently, Jagannath Sahoo and Prasanna Nand Jha
[
12
]
have extended
Alladi’s Higher Order Duality Identities (for all
k
) to the function field setting, and
proved analogues of Alladi-Johnson’s result (1.12) in the function field setting, which is the case of Second Order Duality (
k=2
). Similarly, Sengupta
[
20
]

has most recently extended the results of Sweeting and Woo by establishing Higher Order Duality Identities (for all
k
) to the situation of
L
being an
Galois extension of a number field
K
, and obtained analogues of (1.12),
namely the case
k=2
in the case of Galois extensions
L/K
. In view of the results of this paper, the following problems naturally occur for future exploration:
(i)
to extend the results of sections 4 and 6 in the case of Galois extensions
L
of a number field
K
(a paper on this by Sengupta is in preparation), and

(ii)
to extend the results of sections 4 and 6 to the function field setting.
