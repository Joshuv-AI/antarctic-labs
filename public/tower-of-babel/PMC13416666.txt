Causal Relationships Between Oral Microbiota and Inflammatory Skin Diseases
Authors: Xian Zhang, Min Rao, Haibin Wu, Bowen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsThe oral microbiome has been increasingly linked to systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation, but whether specific oral bacteria causally contribute to inflammatory skin diseases remains unclear due to confounding and reverse causation. This study aimed to assess the causal effects of 43 oral microbiota taxa on the risk of five inflammatory skin diseases using a Mendelian randomization (MR) approach.MethodsWe performed a two-sample MR analysis using genetic instruments for oral microbiota derived from publicly available genome-wide association studies and outcome data from the FinnGen consortium. Causal effects of oral taxa on systemic lupus erythematosus, vitiligo, pemphigus, localized scleroderma, and dermatitis herpetiformis were estimated. The inverse-variance weighted method served as the primary analysis, complemented by sensitivity analyses to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy, heterogeneity, and reverse causality.ResultsMR analyses identified several putative causal associations between oral microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases. Genus Granulicatella and an unknown Streptococcus species (ASV0009) showed causal effects on systemic lupus erythematosus. Family Lachnospiraceae_[XIV] and an unknown Rothia species (ASV0016) were associated with vitiligo. Five oral microbiota taxa demonstrated causal associations with pemphigus. Actinomyces species micronuciformis was linked to localized scleroderma. Order Fusobacteriales and an unknown Neisseria species (ASV0004) were associated with dermatitis herpetiformis. No significant heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy was detected in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionThis MR study provides genetic evidence supporting a causal role of specific oral bacteria in the development of several inflammatory skin diseases, highlighting the oral microbiome as a potential contributor to cutaneous autoimmunity and inflammation.Clinical relevanceOur findings highlight the putative role of the oral microbiome as a plausible candidate for mechanistic and clinical investigations into the prevention or adjunctive management of selected inflammatory skin diseases. However, oral hygiene improvement, targeted antimicrobials, and other microbiota-directed interventions were not directly tested in this MR study and remain hypothetical strategies requiring validation in experimental and clinical studies.

Introduction

Inflammatory skin diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE),1 vitiligo,2 pemphigus,3 localized scleroderma,4 and dermatitis herpetiformis,5 represent a significant burden on global health and patient quality of life. These conditions are characterized by dysregulated immune responses, chronic inflammation, and, in many cases, autoimmune pathogenesis. SLE can manifest with various cutaneous lesions, while vitiligo involves autoimmune-mediated melanocyte destruction.6, 7, 8 Pemphigus is an autoimmune blistering disorder, localized scleroderma leads to cutaneous fibrosis, and dermatitis herpetiformis is an intensely itchy blistering disease associated with gluten sensitivity.9 Despite advancements in understanding their immunopathology, the precise etiological factors triggering and propagating these diseases remain incompletely elucidated.

There is growing evidence that the oral microbiome plays a pivotal role in systemic immune regulation and inflammation.10 The oral cavity harbours a complex microbial ecosystem, and its dysbiosis has been linked not only to local oral diseases but also to various systemic conditions. Mechanistic studies suggest that oral bacteria, such as Prevotella spp., can activate innate immune pathways11,12 like Toll-like receptor 2,13 inducing antigen-presenting cells to secrete proinflammatory cytokines,14 including IL-6,15 IL-1,16 and IL-23.17 This cytokine milieu promotes T-helper 17 cells (Th17) cell polarization and a chronic inflammatory state cantered on IL-17, a key cytokine implicated in the pathogenesis of several inflammatory skin diseases.18 Furthermore, oral pathobionts can stimulate epithelial cells to release IL-6, IL-8, and C-C motif chemokine ligand 20, enhancing neutrophil recruitment and amplifying inflammatory cascades.19 These immune activation pathways bear a striking resemblance to the inflammatory signatures observed in psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, and autoimmune skin diseases, suggesting that the oral microbiome may act as a remote trigger or modulator of cutaneous inflammation.

Nevertheless, the majority of evidence linking the oral microbiota to skin diseases stems from observational studies, which are inherently susceptible to confounding factors, reverse causation, and recall bias. It remains difficult to ascertain whether alterations in oral microbial composition are a cause or a consequence of skin inflammation. Mendelian randomization (MR), an analytical method that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) for an exposure, offers a robust approach to infer causal relationships while minimizing confounding. By leveraging the random assortment of alleles at conception, MR simulates a randomized controlled trial design. For instance, recent MR studies have begun to explore the role of the gut microbiome in various diseases. Dai et al20 used a bidirectional two-sample MR design and demonstrated potential causal effects of several gut microbial genera on multiple cardiovascular diseases. Beyond cardio-metabolic and gastrointestinal conditions, MR analyses have also revealed links between the gut microbiota and autoimmune diseases, psychiatric disorders, and several common cancers, underscoring the broad clinical relevance of microbiome-based causal inference.21,22 But a comprehensive MR investigation specifically targeting the causal role of oral microbiota in a spectrum of inflammatory skin diseases is currently lacking.

Therefore, in this study, we performed a two-sample MR analysis to systematically assess the causal effects of 43 oral microbial microbiota on the risk of five major inflammatory skin diseases. We integrated genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for oral microbiota from a European population with corresponding data for SLE, vitiligo, pemphigus, localized scleroderma, and dermatitis herpetiformis from large-scale consortia, including the GWAS Catalog and FinnGen. We employed the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method as our primary analysis and conducted a suite of sensitivity analyses, including tests for heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy, and reverse causality, to validate the robustness of our findings. This study aims to provide genetic evidence regarding the potential causal influence of the oral microbiome on inflammatory skin diseases, thereby illuminating novel preventive and therapeutic targets.

Methods

Study overview

We conducted a two-sample MR analysis to investigate the potential causal effects of oral microbiota on inflammatory skin diseases, including SLE, vitiligo, pemphigus, localized scleroderma, and dermatitis herpetiformis. As shown in the study design (Figure 1), the core assumptions of MR were rigorously applied through a systematic genetic instrument selection process: (1) Selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) must have a strong association with specific oral microbial species; (2) all IVs were confirmed to be independent of known confounders; and (3) the IVs were assumed to influence the risk of inflammatory skin diseases exclusively via the oral microbiota, with no alternative pathways.Fig 1Core assumptions of the MR study. SNPs robustly associated with specific oral microbial taxa (genome-wide significance, independence, and F-statistics >10) were selected as instrumental variables. The exposure was oral microbiota composition, and the outcomes were inflammatory skin diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus, vitiligo, pemphigus, localized scleroderma, and dermatitis herpetiformis. The MR framework relies on three key assumptions: Assumption 1 – Relevance, the selected SNPs are strongly associated with oral microbiota composition; Assumption 2 – Independence, the instrumental variables are independent of confounders; and Assumption 3 – Exclusion restriction, the instrumental variables affect the risk of inflammatory skin diseases exclusively through oral microbiota, with no alternative pathways.Fig 1 dummy alt text

GWAS summary data of oral microbiota

The GWAS summary data for oral microbiota were obtained from the publicly available GWAS Catalog (accession codes: GCST90429799-GCST90429841). This dataset was derived from the analysis of 610 European participants and included 43 different oral microbial species. These species were systematically categorized, providing an overview of the genetic influences on the composition of the oral microbiota and its potential links to systemic inflammatory diseases. Although the sample size is relatively limited, this European oral microbiome GWAS remains one of the key publicly available resources providing species‑level oral microbiota data. In the present study, we further addressed potential power limitations by applying rigorous IV selection and multiple sensitivity analyses to strengthen the reliability of our causal inferences. The taxonomic resolution of the oral microbiome data is constrained by the use of 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which may not allow for precise species- or strain-level annotation in all cases.

GWAS summary data of inflammatory skin diseases

The GWAS summary data for inflammatory skin diseases, including SLE, vitiligo, pemphigus, localized scleroderma, and dermatitis herpetiformis, were sourced from the GWAS Catalog and the FinnGen consortium. The SLE data (accession code: GCST90668067) included 405,811 European participants, with 3686,405 SNPs. The vitiligo dataset (GCST90476174) comprised 450,755 participants, with 1970,938,384 SNPs. Data for pemphigus (GCST90436597) included 402,747 participants with 2829,218,300 SNPs. The FinnGen database provided datasets for localized scleroderma and dermatitis herpetiformis, with 465,673 and 498,266 participants, respectively. Summary statistics for these datasets were obtained from the respective databases.

Genetic IVs selection

To comply with the MR assumptions, genetic instruments were selected through a rigorous process. SNPs associated with oral microbiota were prioritized based on a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10−6). Linkage disequilibrium was pruned using the TwosampleMR package in R (clumping distance = 10,000 kb, r2 < 0.001). We calculated the F-statistic for each SNP to prevent weak instrument bias, excluding SNPs with an F-statistic <10. Additionally, the Steiger test was applied to ensure the correct directionality of causality, verifying that the exposure (oral microbiota) caused the outcome (inflammatory skin diseases), and not the reverse.

Statistical analysis

The IVW method was used as the primary approach to estimate the causal effects of oral microbiota on inflammatory skin diseases. Supplementary analyses were conducted using MR Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods. A causal relationship was considered significant only if the P value for IVW was less than .05, and the directions of the five methods were consistent. To ensure robustness, we performed several sensitivity analyses, including MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode. Additionally, we used the MR-PRESSO method to identify and remove outlier SNPs, assessed heterogeneity through Cochrane’s Q test, and applied the MR-Egger intercept test to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0 with the TwoSample MR, MendelianRandomization, and MRPRESSO packages.

Results

Oral microbiota and SLE

As detailed in Tables S1 and S2, genetic instruments significantly associated with the 43 oral microbial species were identified. MR analysis revealed two microbial species causally linked to the risk of SLE. Using the primary IVW method, Granulicatella was identified as a protective factor (odds ratio [OR] = 0.72, P = .038), while unknown Streptococcus species (ASV0009) was associated with an increased risk of SLE (OR = 1.31, P = .01). Discussion

Inflammatory skin diseases such as SLE, vitiligo, pemphigus, localized scleroderma, and dermatitis herpetiformis are immune-mediated diseases with complex aetiologies involving multiple factors such as genetics, environment, and microorganisms, all of which work together on a common inflammatory pathway. Consistent with the well-established gut-skin axis, one piece of evidence suggests that the oral microbiome is an active immune organ involved in the development and progression of systemic autoimmunity and chronic inflammation.23,24 Therefore, our study aimed to investigate MR-inferred associations between specific oral microbiota and the risk of selected immune-mediated inflammatory skin diseases.

In SLE, we found that Granulicatella and an unknown Streptococcus species (ASV0009) were causally associated with increased disease risk. Granulicatella and Streptococcus are core members of the healthy oral community but can shift into a dysbiotic state under inflammatory conditions.25 The oral microbiota of SLE patients are altered, with changes in alpha diversity and enrichment of proinflammatory genera compared with healthy controls, and the oral community could even be used to build accurate SLE classifiers.26 In parallel, MR analyses of the gut microbiome have identified both deleterious and protective microbial taxa for SLE risk, reinforcing the notion that microbiota-driven immune modulation extends beyond a single mucosal site.27 Mechanistically, oral bacteria may translocate across mucosal barriers or release immunostimulatory microbial products, thereby priming dendritic cells, promoting Th1/Th17-biased T-cell differentiation, and amplifying type I interferon responses, all of which are central to SLE pathogenesis.28 Observational oral microbiome evidence, together with our MR findings in SLE, suggests that oral microbiota dysbiosis may be involved in immune pathways relevant to SLE, although further mechanistic and clinical validation is required.29

Vitiligo is increasingly recognized as a systemic disease. In vitiligo, the gut microbiota affects skin oxidative stress and depigmentation by regulating the expression of reactive oxygen species-related genes and hippuric acid levels.30,31 In the oral cavity, Trichophyceae may participate in nitrate reduction and biofilm formation, while Rothia abundance influences systemic metabolites linked to oxidative stress. These shifts suggest a potential oral–gut–skin immune axis, though direct evidence remains limited.32, 33, 34

Pemphigus, a prototypical autoimmune bullous disease, frequently involves the oral mucosa and is characterized by autoantibodies against desmosomal proteins. Oral microbiota play a critical role in pemphigus vulgaris, exhibiting reduced diversity, enrichment of potentially pathogenic genera, and significant correlations with disease activity.35 A pilot study analysing cutaneous, oral, and intestinal microbiota in pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid found coordinated dysbiosis across body sites, supporting a systemic microbial signature of immunobullous diseases.36 Studies have shown that symbiotic and pathogenic microorganisms in the oral and gut microenvironment can regulate the production of autoantibodies in autoimmune bullous diseases, promote the formation of oral symbiotic biofilms, and inhibit opportunistic pathogens.37,38

Our MR analyses reveal a nuanced microbial pattern in pemphigus, with Firmicutes, Actinomycota, and Prevotellaceae appearing protective, whereas Bacilli increase disease risk. Health-associated oral genera such as Streptococcus, Actinomyces, Rothia, and Prevotella are typically enriched in eubiotic ecosystems and contribute to colonization resistance. These findings suggest that loss of protective microbial communities, rather than the emergence of a single pathogen, may create an immunological milieu permissive to autoantibody generation. In contrast, expansion of proinflammatory Bacilli lineages may exacerbate epithelial damage through enhanced protease activity, reactive oxygen species production, and Th17 polarizing immune signals. Although the current classification resolution of GWAS data is insufficient to identify species- or strain-level drivers, this study highlights the importance of functional traits in explaining microbiome-based autoimmune associations.

Only Species micronuciformis showed a significant causal association with localized scleroderma risk. While direct oral microbiome studies in morphea localized scleroderma are scarce, related evidence from systemic sclerosis documents microbiome alterations and immune fibrosis links, supporting the plausibility that mucosal microbes can modulate fibrotic pathways.39 Taxonomically, ‘micronuciformis’ often refers to Megasphaera micronuciformis in upper aerodigestive tract datasets; this anaerobe has been detected in tongue microbiome metagenomic studies and has been reported in association with upper GI contexts, suggesting it may mark ecological states prone to mucosal inflammation.40

For dermatitis herpetiformis, MR identified Fusobacteriales as a risk factor and an unknown Neisseria (ASV0004) as protective. This directionality is consistent with a broader literature in which Fusobacterium exhibits proinflammatory properties, including epithelial activation and IL-8-linked neutrophil recruitment with barrier impairment, while Neisseria is frequently described as a health-associated oral genus.41,42 Fusobacteriales, particularly Fusobacterium nucleatum, are recognized contributors to mucosal inflammation, barrier disruption, and extraintestinal disease, including periodontal disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and colorectal cancer. Fusobacteriales may amplify gluten-driven immune responses and IgA deposition through heightened mucosal inflammation, whereas preservation of Neisseria-dominated communities may support barrier integrity and limit aberrant immune activation culminating in cutaneous manifestations.43

Methodologically, our study complements and extends a rapidly growing MR literature linking the microbiome to immune-mediated skin diseases. Multiple MR analyses have now demonstrated causal effects of gut microbiota on psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, acne, rosacea, and vitiligo, as well as SLE and other autoimmune conditions.44,45 In parallel, several MR analyses using oral microbiome GWAS data have reported causal links between oral microbiota and cancers and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, underscoring the systemic reach of the oral microbiota.46,47

Actinobacteria, Prevotellaceae, and Neisseria ASV0004 may reflect host immune or inflammatory states associated with inflammatory skin diseases. These taxa may therefore be considered exploratory candidates for future studies of noninvasive risk stratification, early disease-related changes, or longitudinal surveillance, although their temporal stability, reproducibility, and population-level performance require further validation. MR results are consistent with the potential involvement of the oral microbiota in the pathophysiology of diseases such as SLE, vitiligo, and pemphigus. These findings provide biological justification for future mechanistic and exploratory studies to test the hypothesis that oral microbiota modulation, potentially through oral hygiene optimization, probiotics, prebiotics, bacteriophages, or targeted antimicrobial approaches, may influence disease-related pathways. Importantly, these approaches were not directly evaluated in the present MR study and should not be interpreted as validated preventive or therapeutic strategies.

Skin diseases are immunologically heterogeneous and are driven by distinct pathogenic mechanisms. Therefore, we did not assume a shared aetiology across outcomes but instead analysed each skin disease separately in the MR framework, which yielded disease-specific microbial signals consistent with differing immune backgrounds. Notably, the same or closely related oral taxa showed divergent effect directions across diseases, arguing against a nonspecific systemic inflammation explanation and suggesting context-dependent microbiota-immune interactions. Importantly, the systemic inflammatory effects of oral microbiota should not be limited to the skin. Oral microbial antigens, metabolites, and specific inflammatory mediators may theoretically exert multiorgan effects through shared immune pathways.48 The clinical manifestation of skin may depend on tissue-specific susceptibility, host genetic background, and local immune effector programs, such as Th17 activation, autoantibody production, or fibroblast-related inflammation.49,50 Thus, the skin can be regarded as one potential target organ within a broader oral–systemic immune axis. Our MR results were not designed to quantify the relative contribution of oral microbiota to skin diseases compared with other systemic inflammatory disorders. Rather, we showed that the selected oral taxa are causally linked to particular immune-mediated skin diseases. Cross-disease MR analyses and longitudinal multiomics studies are needed to determine whether these oral microbial signals are shared across organs or preferentially enriched in cutaneous inflammatory phenotypes.51,52

Mechanistically, oral dysbiosis may compromise mucosal barriers, enabling microbial components to enter circulation and drive systemic low-grade inflammation.53,54 Concurrently, molecular mimicry and Th17-polarizing signals from oral microbes could amplify cutaneous autoimmune responses.55,56 Additionally, swallowed oral bacteria may alter gut microbiota composition, indirectly modulating skin immunity through the oral–gut–skin axis.57

The relationship between environmental factors, the oral microbiota, and inflammatory skin diseases is complex. Firstly, various common environmental exposures, including oral hygiene levels, smoking habits, dietary patterns, antibiotic use, and socioeconomic status, have been demonstrated to exert long-term and stable influences on the composition, diversity, and functional characteristics of the oral microbial community. For instance, smoking and poor oral hygiene can promote the expansion of proinflammatory bacterial populations, represented by Streptococcus, Fusobacterium, and Prevotella.58 In contrast, high-sugar or high-fat diets, along with repeated antibiotic exposure, can further compromise the stability of the oral microecology. Secondly, the oral cavity serves as a highly vascularized and immunologically active mucosal barrier. Alterations in the oral microbiota can lead to the sustained entry of microbial antigens, lipopolysaccharides, peptidoglycans, and other microbe-associated molecular patterns into the systemic circulation. This process can activate innate immune receptors and induce a state of systemic low-grade inflammation.55

In genetically predisposed individuals, sustained low-grade immune stimulation from oral dysbiosis may break tolerance and sensitize systemic Th1/Th17 responses, facilitating cutaneous inflammation. This mucosal–skin crosstalk, rather than a single pathogenic pathway, likely amplifies or moderates skin disease activity depending on the local immune milieu.59 Consequently, oral microbiota-associated signals observed in different inflammatory skin conditions are more likely to reflect their role in amplifying or buffering inflammatory responses within specific immune contexts, rather than representing a singular, universal pathogenic mechanism.

A key limitation is that the exposure GWAS for the oral microbiome had a relatively small sample size compared with modern microbiome-MR standards, which may reduce power and causal precision. Moreover, partial taxonomic resolution, including ASV-level and unknown species annotations, limits biological interpretation and generalizability. In addition, several taxa were identified based on nominally significant IVW estimates with modest effect sizes; therefore, these associations should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive causal evidence. Although sensitivity analyses did not reveal substantial heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy, the multiple-testing burden and potential weakness of microbiome genetic instruments may still affect the robustness and interpretability of these findings. The exclusive use of European-ancestry data may limit the applicability of these findings to other populations. Although larger oral microbiome GWAS datasets are becoming available, substantial heterogeneity in sampling strategies and profiling platforms currently restricts their direct integration. To minimize confounding, we prioritized ancestry matching and consistency in data quality across datasets. It is necessary to incorporate larger and more ethnically diverse cohorts to validate and extend our findings. We acknowledge that some of the identified causal taxa, designated as unknown species, such as ASV0009 within Streptococcus and ASV0016 within Rothia, reflect the current limitation of 16S rRNA-based classification in public GWAS data. While their precise species identity remains to be resolved, these ASVs represent genetically influenced, distinct microbial units whose association with disease risk emphasizes the importance of specific phylogenetic lineages within their respective genera. Combining methods such as full-length 16S sequencing, metagenomic sequencing, or phylogenetic comparison to precisely characterize these variants and elucidate their biological mechanisms will help better confirm the clinical value of this study. Among the microbial candidates identified in this study, Fusobacteriales and Neisseria ASV0004 may be particularly suitable for follow-up validation. Both were associated with dermatitis herpetiformis with relatively strong statistical support and showed opposite effect direction, making them biologically informative as a putative risk–protective microbial pair. A realistic next step would be a prospective longitudinal cohort including individuals with newly diagnosed dermatitis herpetiformis, patients with celiac disease without cutaneous involvement, and matched controls. Repeated oral sampling, full-length 16S or metagenomic sequencing, serological markers of immunity, and standardized skin activity scores could determine whether these microbial candidates precede disease activity or fluctuate with treatment response. Moreover, the epithelial or immune-cell coculture models could test whether Fusobacteriales-enriched communities promote inflammatory cytokine release, barrier dysfunction, or IgA-related immune activation, and whether Neisseria-dominant communities attenuate these responses. The staged design will help better confirm the clinical value of this study.

Conclusion

Collectively, here, our MR study systematically evaluated the causal relationships between oral microbiota composition and 5 immune-mediated inflammatory skin diseases. Notably, Granulicatella and an unknown Streptococcus species (ASV0009) were causally associated with SLE risk. For vitiligo, Lachnospiraceae_[XIV] and an unknown Rothia species (ASV0016) were identified as risk factors. Analysis of pemphigus revealed protective effects for Firmicutes, Actinomycetales, Prevotellaceae, and Schaalia, while Bacilli was associated with increased risk. Species micronuciformis was causally linked to localized scleroderma, and for dermatitis herpetiformis, Fusobacteriales increased risk, whereas an unknown Neisseria species (ASV0004) exerted a protective effect. Therefore, our study provides MR-based evidence supporting potential involvement of oral microbial dysbiosis in immune-mediated inflammatory dermatoses, suggesting that oral microbiota may represent candidates for future targeted validation.
