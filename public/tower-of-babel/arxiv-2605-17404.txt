Preliminaries

This section introduces algebraic, geometric, and probabilistic foundations used in the following sections.

2.1
Setup and notation

The cyclotomic field
K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
is obtained by adding a primitive
m
-th root of unity
\zeta=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/m}
to the rational number field. Elements of
K
are represented by
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}\zeta^{i}
with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
. The degree of
K
is
[K:\mathbb{Q}]=n=\varphi(m)=2^{k-1}
, because
\zeta
satisfies the minimal polynomial
x^{n}+1
over
\mathbb{Q}
which is valid for
m=2^{k}
with
k\geq 3
. The ring of integers
R=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]
consists of elements with integer coefficients
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The Galois group
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
consists of all field automorphisms of
K
that fix the field
\mathbb{Q}
. Since every such automorphism will map
\zeta
to another primitive
m
-th root of unity,
\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
can be identified with
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, i.e., the group of integers modulo
m
coprime to
m
. For
m=2^{k}
, these are the odd integers
a\in\{1,3,5,\dots,m-1\}
, and the corresponding automorphism
\sigma_{a}
is defined by
\sigma_{a}(\zeta)=\zeta^{a}
. This action is well-defined and can be extended to all of
K
by linearity.

An embedding
\sigma_{j}\colon K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a ring homomorphism from
K
into the complex numbers. Since
K
is complex for
k\geq 3
, all
n
embeddings are mapped into
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. The conjugate pair of embeddings satisfies
\sigma_{m-j}(g)=\overline{\sigma_{j}(g)}
for all
g\in K
, because
\zeta^{m-j}=\overline{\zeta^{j}}
.

For an element
g=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\zeta^{i}\in R
, the
j
-th embedding can be defined by evaluating the polynomial at
\zeta^{j}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}(g)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\zeta^{ij}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\cdot ij/m}.

(1)

This is a discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of the vector
(c_{0},\dots,c_{n-1})
evaluated at the
n
-th roots of
-1
from
\zeta^{n}=e^{\pi\mathrm{i}}=-1
.

Definition 2.1
.

The log-embedding of
g\in R\setminus\{0\}
is the vector of coordinate-wise log-moduli defined by

\displaystyle L(g)=\bigl(\log\lvert\sigma_{j}(g)\rvert\bigr)_{\text{odd }j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2)

This mapping transforms the multiplicative of
R
into additive in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\displaystyle L(g_{1}g_{2})=L(g_{1})+L(g_{2}),

(3)

because
|\sigma_{j}(g_{1}g_{2})|=|\sigma_{j}(g_{1})|\cdot|\sigma_{j}(g_{2})|
. Especially,
g=g_{0}\epsilon
becomes
L(g)=L(g_{0})+L(\epsilon)
for
g_{0}\in R\setminus\{0\}
and
\epsilon\in R^{\times}
.

The trace-zero hyperplane is defined by
H_{0}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{j}x_{j}=0\}
. The orthogonal projection onto
H_{0}
is then defined by

\displaystyle\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))=L(g)-\frac{1}{n}\log\lvert\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(g)\rvert\cdot\mathbf{1},

(4)

where the field norm
\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(g)=\prod_{\text{odd }j}\sigma_{j}(g)
, i,e., the product over all
n
embeddings indexed by
j\in\{1,3,\dots,m-1\}
. Here, conjugate pairs
\sigma_{j}
and
\sigma_{m-j}
satisfy
\sigma_{m-j}(g)=\overline{\sigma_{j}(g)}
.

Denote
J=\{1,3,\dots,n-1\}
as a set representative for all
\tfrac{n}{2}
conjugate pairs. Then
\sigma_{j}(g)\cdot\sigma_{m-j}(g)=|\sigma_{j}(g)|^{2}
for each
j\in J
. So,
N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(g)
is real and positive as

\displaystyle\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(g)=\prod_{j\in J}|\sigma_{j}(g)|^{2}=\prod_{\text{odd }j}|\sigma_{j}(g)|.

(5)

The field norm is a rational integer for
g\in R
.

The log-unit lattice
\Lambda
lies in
H_{0}
since units
\epsilon
satisfy
\lvert\Nm(\epsilon)\rvert=1
and
\sum_{j}\log|\sigma_{j}(\epsilon)|=\log\lvert\Nm(\epsilon)\rvert=0
. So, CVP will be defined in
H_{0}
. Moreover, using the projection (
4
), the CVP distance is independent of the overall scale of
g
.

2.2
The log-unit lattice

By Dirichlet’s Unit Theorem
[
23
]
, the unit group of a number field
K
with
r_{1}
real and
r_{2}
pairs of complex embeddings has unit rank
r_{1}+r_{2}-1
. For
K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2^{k}})
with
k\geq 3
, we have
r_{1}=0
and
r_{2}=\tfrac{n}{2}
. This gives unit rank
r=\tfrac{n}{2}-1
. The log-embedding
L(\epsilon)
for a unit
\epsilon
satisfies
\log|\sigma_{j}(\epsilon)|=\log|\sigma_{m-j}(\epsilon)|
by conjugation, so we have effective dimension
\tfrac{n}{2}
and the lattice rank in
H_{0}

r=\tfrac{n}{2}-1
.

Since
h_{k}^{+}=1
for
k\leq 12
(Part I), the cyclotomic units
\xi_{a}=\frac{\sin(a\pi/m)}{\sin(\pi/m)}
for odd
a\in\{3,5,\dots,n-1\}
generate
R^{\times}
modulo torsion
[
24
,
25
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

The log-unit lattice is defined by

\displaystyle\Lambda=\{(\log|\sigma_{j}(\epsilon)|)_{\text{odd }j}:\epsilon\in R^{\times}\}\subset H_{0}.

(6)

Its rank
r=\tfrac{n}{2}-1
and a basis
\{\mathbf{b}_{a}\}
is indexed by
a\in\{3,\dots,n-1\}
, where
\mathbf{b}_{a}=\Pi_{H_{0}}((\log|\sigma_{j}(\xi_{a})|)_{j})
.

\Lambda
is an
(\tfrac{n}{2}-1)
-dimensional lattice in the
(n-1)
-dimensional hyperplane
H_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Its points correspond to the logarithmic signatures of units. The CVP on
\Lambda
asks: given the log-signature of an arbitrary generator, find the unit whose log-signature is closest.

Definition 2.3
.

The
L^{p}
-cover radius of a lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{p}(\Lambda)=\max_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}/\Lambda}\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda}\lVert\mathbf{t-v}\rVert_{p}.

(7)

This radius is the maximum CVP distance over all possible target vectors. The CDPR method
[
1
]
uses
\mu_{\infty}(\Lambda)=O(\sqrt{n\log n})
. Instead, we show structured targets are much closer than this worst case.

Definition 2.4
.

The Voronoi cell of a lattice point
\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{v})=\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\lVert\mathbf{t-v}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\mathbf{t-w}\rVert_{2}\mbox{ for all }\mathbf{w}\in\Lambda\}.

(8)

A point
\mathbf{t}
lies in
\mathcal{V}(0)
if and only if
2\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{w}\rangle\leq\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}
for every nonzero
\mathbf{w}\in\Lambda
(the Voronoi condition).

2.3
Babai’s nearest-plane algorithm

Babai’s algorithm
[
12
]
is the standard polynomial-time CVP approximation used in Phase 2 of the CDPR attack
[
1
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a basis
B=(\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{r})
of a lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization (GSO) produces orthogonal vectors
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{r}^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{b}_{i}-\sum_{j<i}\mu_{ij}\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\prime},\quad\mu_{ij}=\frac{\langle\mathbf{b}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\prime}\rangle}{\lVert\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

(9)

For the log-unit lattice, these norms are
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
(Table
1
) which is large relative to
O(1)
per-component deviation of structured targets.

The basis matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
admits a QR decomposition
B=QR
, where
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
has orthonormal columns and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is an upper triangular with positive diagonal. The two factorizations are related by

\displaystyle Q_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}}{\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}},\quad R_{ii}=\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2},\quad R_{ji}=\mu_{ij}\lVert\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\quad(j<i).

(10)

Definition 2.6

(Babai’s nearest-plane algorithm
[
12
]
)
.

Given a lattice basis
B=(\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{r})
with GSO
(\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{r}^{\prime})
and a target
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, Babai’s algorithm computes integer coefficients
c_{r},c_{r-1},\dots,c_{1}
as

\displaystyle c_{i}=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\langle\mathbf{t}-\sum_{j>i}c_{j}\mathbf{b}_{j},\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rangle}{\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\Bigr\rceil,\quad i=r,r-1,\dots,1,

(11)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rceil
denotes rounding to the nearest integer. The output lattice vector is given by
\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i}
with Babai residual
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{v}
.

The Babai residual satisfies
\mathbf{e}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\mu_{i}-c_{i})\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}+\mathbf{t}^{\perp}
, where
\mathbf{t}^{\perp}
is the component of
\mathbf{t}
which is orthogonal to
\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{r})
, and
\mu_{i}
is the projection coefficient computed at step
i
. Moreover, the Babai residual satisfies

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{r}}{2}\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{t}^{\perp}\|_{2}.

(12)

When
\Lambda
has full column rank of
m=r
,
\mathbf{t}^{\perp}
vanishes and the bound simplifies into

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{r}}{2}\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}.

(13)

For the log-unit lattice
\Lambda
with rank
r=\frac{n}{2}-1
and GS norms
\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
, the Babai bound gives
\|\mathbf{e}\|_{2}=O(n)
. This is the worst case guarantee over all targets. For structured targets, we will show the projection coefficients
\mu_{i}
are not bounded by
\frac{1}{2}
.

Babai’s algorithm costs
O(r^{2})
arithmetic operations on GS coefficients (or
O(r^{2}m)
if the vectors are in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
). For the log-unit lattice with
r=\frac{n}{2}-1
and
m=n
, the total cost is
O(n^{3})
which might be negligible compared to the PIP step.

Definition 2.7
.

Definition 2.8
.

[
19
]

A centered real random variable

is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sigma^{2}
, denoted as

, if the following condition holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\leq e^{\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2}\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

(14)

Note the sum of independent sub-Gaussian variables is sub-Gaussian, and any bounded variable with
|X|\leq B
is sub-Gaussian with parameter

from the Hoeffding’s Lemma
[
26
]

Definition 2.9
.

If

, then

is the
\chi
-squared distribution (

) with

degrees of freedom.

It satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\chi^{2}_{k}]=k
and
\Var(\chi^{2}_{k})=2k
. The logarithmic moments, which are central to our Trigamma Theorem, involve the digamma and trigamma functions as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log\chi^{2}_{k}]=\psi\bigl(\tfrac{k}{2}\bigr)+\log 2,\quad\Var(\log\chi^{2}_{k})=\psi^{\prime}\bigl(\tfrac{k}{2}\bigr).

(15)

Definition 2.10
.

[
22
]

The digamma function is defined by
\psi(z)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\log\Gamma(z)
and trigamma function is
\psi^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}\log\Gamma(z)

Note

, where

is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Moreover, we have

, which is positive, strictly decreasing, and tends to

for large

The CLT for Embeddings and Variance Identity

In this section, we prove two results for ring-based lattice cryptanalysis: (1) different canonical embeddings of a random ring element are pairwise uncorrelated and asymptotically jointly independent in the large-dimension limit; (2) for a circularly symmetric complex Gaussian random variable
Z\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
, the variance of
\log|Z|
is
\pi^{2}/24
.

3.1
Embedding uncorrelatedness

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
g=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\zeta^{i}
with
c_{i}
i.i.d. real-valued, mean
0
, variance
\sigma_{c}^{2}<\infty
. Then for different odd
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,3,\dots,2n-1\}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{j}(g)\overline{\sigma_{j^{\prime}}(g)}]=0.

(16)

Proof.

By definition, we have
\sigma_{j}(g)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\zeta^{ij}
for the primitive
m
-th root of unity
\zeta=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/m}
with
m=2n
. For real
c_{i^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}
, we get
\overline{\sigma_{j^{\prime}}(g)}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}c_{i^{\prime}}\overline{\zeta^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}}=\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}c_{i^{\prime}}\zeta^{-i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
. We then obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{j}(g)\overline{\sigma_{j^{\prime}}(g)}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\zeta^{ij})(\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}c_{i^{\prime}}\zeta^{-i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[c_{i}c_{i^{\prime}}]\zeta^{ij-i^{\prime}j^{\prime}},

(17)

where the second equality is from the linearity of expectation.

From the i.i.d. zero-mean of
c_{i}
s, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[c_{i}c_{i^{\prime}}]=0
for all
i\neq i^{\prime}
, and
\mathbb{E}[c_{i}^{2}]=\sigma_{c}^{2}
for all
0\leq i\leq n-1
. Using this orthogonality for Eq.(
17
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{j}(g)\overline{\sigma_{j^{\prime}}(g)}]=\sigma_{c}^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\zeta^{i(j-j^{\prime})}.

(18)

Let
c=j-j^{\prime}
. Since
j
and
j^{\prime}
are different odd integers,
c
is a non-zero even integer with
0<|c|<2n=m
. We compute Eq.(
18
) using the equality of
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\zeta^{ic}=\frac{1-\zeta^{nc}}{1-\zeta^{c}}
. This holds because
\zeta^{c}\neq 1
, where
\zeta
is a primitive
m
-th root of unity and
\zeta^{k}=1
if and only if
m
divides
k
. Using the definition of
\zeta
, we have
\zeta^{n}=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\cdot n/m}=e^{\pi\mathrm{i}}=-1
. Note
c
is even,
(\zeta^{n})^{c}=(-1)^{c}=1
. Thus, from Eq.(
18
), we obtain the equality (
16
).
∎

Proposition 3.1
.

Under the conditions of Lemma
3.1
, suppose that
c_{i}
has a finite fourth moment. Then for any fixed different odd indices
j_{1},\dots,j_{s}
, the following result holds

\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{\sigma_{j_{1}}(g)}{\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}},\dots,\frac{\sigma_{j_{s}}(g)}{\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}}\Bigr)\xrightarrow{d}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{s}),\quad Z_{\ell}\overset{\rm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{CN}(0,1).

(19)

Proof.

For each embedding index
\ell\in\{1,\dots,s\}
and coefficient index
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, define
Y_{i,\ell}=c_{i}\zeta^{ij_{\ell}}/\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}
, where
\zeta=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/m}
with
m=2n
. We decompose the normalized embedding as

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{j_{\ell}}(g)}{\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\zeta^{ij_{\ell}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_{i,\ell}.

(20)

For each
i
, define the
s
-dimensional complex random vector as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{i}=(Y_{i,1},Y_{i,2},\dots,Y_{i,s})=\frac{c_{i}}{\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}}(\zeta^{ij_{1}},\zeta^{ij_{2}},\dots,\zeta^{ij_{s}}).

(21)

The factors
\zeta^{ij_{\ell}}
(
\ell=1,\dots,s
) are deterministic complex numbers of modulus 1. So,
\mathbf{Y}_{i}
is a deterministic vector in
\mathbb{C}^{s}
which is scaled by the random scalar
c_{i}/\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}
. All vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{n-1}
are mutually independent.

Since
\mathbb{E}[c_{i}]=0
implies
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}_{i}]=\mathbf{0}
, for the complex covariance
\Cov(U,V)=\mathbb{E}\left[U\overline{V}\right]
with centered random variables
U
and
V
, we get

\displaystyle\Cov\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_{i,\ell},\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_{i,\ell^{\prime}}\Bigr)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,\ell}\overline{Y_{i,\ell^{\prime}}}].

(22)

From Eq.(
22
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,\ell}\overline{Y_{i,\ell^{\prime}}}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{c_{i}\zeta^{ij_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}}\cdot\frac{c_{i}\zeta^{-ij_{\ell^{\prime}}}}{\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[c_{i}^{2}]}{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}\cdot\zeta^{i(j_{\ell}-j_{\ell^{\prime}})}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\zeta^{i(j_{\ell}-j_{\ell^{\prime}})},

(23)

where we have used the equality
\overline{\zeta^{ij_{\ell^{\prime}}}}=\zeta^{-ij_{\ell^{\prime}}}
and
\mathbb{E}[c_{i}^{2}]=\sigma_{c}^{2}
. We then obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,\ell}\overline{Y_{i,\ell^{\prime}}}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\zeta^{i(j_{\ell}-j_{\ell^{\prime}})}.

(24)

In what follows, we evaluate the sum (
24
) in two cases. For
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
, we have
j_{\ell}-j_{\ell^{\prime}}=0
, so
\zeta^{i(j_{\ell}-j_{\ell^{\prime}})}=\zeta^{0}=1
for all
i
s. Moreover, from Eq.(
24
), we get
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}1=1
; For
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
, let
c=j_{\ell}-j_{\ell^{\prime}}
.
c
is a non-zero even integer. Similar to the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\zeta^{ic}=\frac{1-\zeta^{nc}}{1-\zeta^{c}}=\frac{1-(-1)^{c}}{1-\zeta^{c}}=0,

(25)

where the second equality is from
\zeta^{n}=-1
, while the third equality holds as
c
is even.

Combining with two cases, we obtain the covariance matrix of
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{Y}_{i}
as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}=(\Sigma_{n})_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\cdot I_{s},

(26)

where
\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker delta, and
I_{s}
is rank
s
identity matrix.

For the
2s
-dimensional real CLT, the Lindeberg condition
[
17
]
requires for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle L_{n}(\varepsilon):=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{Y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\|\mathbf{Y}_{i}\|_{2}>\varepsilon\}}]\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty,

(27)

where
\|\mathbf{Y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}|Y_{i,\ell}|^{2}
, and
\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\}}
is the indicator function.

Using Eq.(
21
), we firstly simplify the norm as

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{Y}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}|Y_{i,\ell}|^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}\cdot|\zeta^{ij_{\ell}}|^{2}=\frac{sc_{i}^{2}}{n\sigma_{c}^{2}},

(28)

where the final equality is from
|\zeta^{ij_{\ell}}|=1
for all
i,\ell
. From Eq.(
27
), we then get

\displaystyle L_{n}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{sc_{i}^{2}}{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{|c_{i}|>\varepsilon\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}/s}\}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{\sigma_{c}^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Bigl[c_{0}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{|c_{0}|>\varepsilon\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}/s}\}}\Bigr].

(29)

We now prove
L_{n}(\varepsilon)\to 0
when
n\to\infty
. Define
f_{n}(\omega)=c_{0}(\omega)^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{|c_{0}(\omega)|>\varepsilon\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}/s}\}}
. We can show the convergence via the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem, using the following facts:

•

For every outcome
\omega
with finite
c_{0}(\omega)
(which holds almost surely, as
c_{0}
has a finite fourth moment),
\varepsilon\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}/s}\to\infty
when
n\to\infty
.

•

For all
n
, we have
|f_{n}(\omega)|\leq c_{0}(\omega)^{2}
almost surely.

So, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[f_{n}]\to 0
when
n\to\infty
. Since
s
and
\sigma_{c}^{2}
are fixed constants independent of
n
, we have
L_{n}(\varepsilon)=\frac{s}{\sigma_{c}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[f_{n}]\to 0
.

We now use Cramér–Wold Theorem
[
27
,
28
]
. In our setting, we identify
\mathbb{C}^{s}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2s}
via the mapping
Z_{\ell}\mapsto(\Re(Z_{\ell}),\Im(Z_{\ell}))
for
\ell=1,\dots,s
. For any fixed real vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2},\dots,a_{s},b_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{2s}
, define
T_{n}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(a_{\ell}\Re(S_{n,\ell})+b_{\ell}\Im(S_{n,\ell}))
, where
S_{n,\ell}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Y_{i,\ell}
is the
\ell
-th component. Decompose
T_{n}
into a sum of
n
independent real random variables as

\displaystyle T_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}W_{i},\quad W_{i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(a_{\ell}\Re(Y_{i,\ell})+b_{\ell}\Im(Y_{i,\ell})).

(30)

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[W_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{|W_{i}|>\varepsilon\}}]\leq\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot L_{n}(\varepsilon/\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2})\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(31)

This means the one-dimensional Lindeberg condition holds for every linear combination
T_{n}
.

We next compute the variance of
T_{n}
. Using
\Sigma_{n}^{(2s)}=\frac{1}{2}I_{2s}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Var(T_{n})=\mathbf{a}^{T}\Sigma_{n}^{(2s)}\mathbf{a}=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}.

(32)

From the one-dimensional Lindeberg CLT
[
29
]
, we get

\displaystyle T_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\Bigl(0,\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert_{2}^{2}\Bigr)

(33)

when
n\to\infty
.

Now, let
\mathbf{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{s})
be a random vector with i.i.d. components
Z_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
. For the same linear combination
\mathbf{a}
,
T=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(a_{\ell}\Re(Z_{\ell})+b_{\ell}\Im(Z_{\ell}))
has the distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert_{2}^{2})
. Thus,
T_{n}\xrightarrow{d}T
for every fixed
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{2s}
. Using the Cramér–Wold Theorem
[
27
,
28
]
, we get the joint convergence in distribution (
19
).

This implies each component
\Re(Z_{\ell})
and
\Im(Z_{\ell})
are marginally distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{2})
. All
2s
real components are pair-wise uncorrelated. For jointly Gaussian random variables, this implies the uncorrelatedness is equivalent to independence. Therefore, for each
\ell
, we have
\Re(Z_{\ell})
and
\Im(Z_{\ell})
are independent, so
Z_{\ell}=\Re(Z_{\ell})+\mathrm{i}\Im(Z_{\ell})\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
. Moreover, for different
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
,
Z_{\ell}
and
Z_{\ell^{\prime}}
are independent. Thus, the limiting random variables
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{s}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
. This completes the proof.
∎

For the parameter set
n=256
(ML-KEM standard), using Berry–Esséen Theorem
[
30
,
31
]
gives a uniform CDF approximation error of
O(\mathbb{E}[|c_{0}|^{3}]/(\sigma_{c}^{3}\sqrt{n}))
, which evaluates to approximately
0.038
for typical coefficient distributions. This means the Gaussian approximation
\sigma_{j}(g)/\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}\approx Z_{j}
is accurate to within roughly
4\%
in CDF. This is sufficient for the variance and maximum norm computations in Theorems
4.1
and
4.3
.

3.3
The variance identity

The second result is the exact computation of
\Var(\log|Z|)
for a circularly symmetric complex Gaussian random variable
Z\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
Z\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2})
. Then for every
\sigma^{2}>0
we have

(i)

\mathbb{E}[\log|Z|]=\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma^{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}
, where
\gamma\approx 0.5772
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

(ii)

\Var(\log|Z|)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}\approx 0.4112
.

Proof.

For
Z=X+\mathrm{i}Y\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2})
,
X
and
Y
are independent and identically distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}/2)
. Thus,
|Z|^{2}=X^{2}+Y^{2}
has a scaled
\chi
-squared distribution with 2 degrees of freedom.

Define
W:=\frac{|Z|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}
. Note
X^{2}/(\sigma^{2}/2)\sim\chi^{2}_{1}
and
Y^{2}/(\sigma^{2}/2)\sim\chi^{2}_{1}
are independent, so
|Z|^{2}/(\sigma^{2}/2)\sim\chi^{2}_{2}
. The
\chi^{2}_{2}
distribution has density
\frac{1}{2}e^{-t/2}\mathbf{1}_{\{t>0\}}
.

We then express
\log|Z|
in terms of
W
as

\displaystyle\log|Z|=\frac{1}{2}\log|Z|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{2}W)=\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log W.

(34)

The term
\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma^{2}
is a deterministic constant. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log|Z|]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\log W],

(35)

\displaystyle\Var(\log|Z|)

\displaystyle=\Var\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\log W\Bigr)=\frac{1}{4}\Var(\log W).

(36)

For
W\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
and any real
s>-1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W^{s}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}w^{s}e^{-w}dw=\Gamma(s+1).

(37)

For
s>-1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\mathbb{E}[W^{s}]=\frac{d}{ds}\int_{0}^{\infty}w^{s}e^{-w}dw=\mathbb{E}[W^{s}\log W].

(38)

For
s=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log W]=\frac{d}{ds}\Gamma(s+1)\big|_{s=0}=\Gamma^{\prime}(1).

(39)

Differentiating with second time, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\log W)^{2}]=\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\mathbb{E}[W^{s}]\big|_{s=0}=\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\Gamma(s+1)\big|_{s=0}=\Gamma^{\prime\prime}(1).

(40)

The digamma function
\psi(s)
is given as

\displaystyle\psi(s)=\frac{d}{ds}\log\Gamma(s)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(s)}{\Gamma(s)}.

(41)

At
s=1
, we have
\Gamma(1)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-w}dw=1
. So,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}(1)=\Gamma(1)\cdot\psi(1)=\psi(1).

(42)

From the Weierstrass product representation of Gamma functions
[
32
,
33
]
, combining with Eqs.(
42
) and (
40
), we get
\mathbb{E}[\log W]=-\gamma
. From Eq.(
35
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log|Z|]=\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(-\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma^{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}.

(43)

This proves part (i).

To compute the second moment
\mathbb{E}[(\log W)^{2}]=\Gamma^{\prime\prime}(1)
, we use the product rule. In fact, we have

\displaystyle\Var(\log W)=\mathbb{E}[(\log W)^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[\log W])^{2}=\Gamma^{\prime\prime}(1)-(\Gamma^{\prime}(1))^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}.

(44)

From Eq.(
36
), we get

\displaystyle\Var(\log|Z|)=\frac{1}{4}\Var(\log W)\approx 0.4112.

(45)

This completes the proof of part (ii).
∎

4
The CVP Distance Theorems

In this section, we prove three results for the structured closest vector problem (CVP) over the log-unit lattice.

4.1
The exact
L^{2}
distance constant

This subsection proves the precise asymptotic
L^{2}
distance.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
g=\sum c_{i}\zeta^{i}\in R
with
c_{i}
i.i.d., mean
0
, variance
\sigma_{c}^{2}>0
, and finite fourth moment. Conditioned on
g\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\|_{2}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}

(46)

when
n\to\infty
. Equivalently,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\|_{2}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{6}}\approx 0.6413
, where
\xrightarrow{p}
denotes convergence in probability.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in seven steps.

Step 1.
For each odd index
j\in\{1,3,\dots,m-1\}
, define the log-modulus
t_{j}=\log|\sigma_{j}(g)|
, and let
\bar{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\text{ odd}}t_{j}
denote the average of these log-moduli. Using the definition of hyperplane
H_{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))=(t_{j}-\bar{t})_{j\text{ odd}}\in H_{0}.

(47)

From this representation, we get

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j\text{ odd}}(t_{j}-\bar{t})^{2}.

(48)

Step 2.
For 2-power cyclotomic fields, the canonical embedding satisfies the relation
\sigma_{m-j}(g)=\overline{\sigma_{j}(g)}
. Taking moduli on both sides gives
|\sigma_{m-j}(g)|=|\sigma_{j}(g)|
, and hence
t_{m-j}=t_{j}
from the definition of
t_{j}
. This implies the
n
coordinates of
L(g)
form
n/2
identical pairs. So, for each pair
(j,m-j)
its contribution to Eq.(
48
) is

\displaystyle(t_{j}-\bar{t})^{2}+(t_{m-j}-\bar{t})^{2}=2(t_{j}-\bar{t})^{2}.

(49)

Let
J=\{1,3,\dots,n-1\}
with
|J|=n/2
. From Eq.(
48
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j\text{ odd}}(t_{j}-\bar{t})^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}[(t_{j}-\bar{t})^{2}+(t_{m-j}-\bar{t})^{2}]=2\sum_{j\in J}(t_{j}-\bar{t})^{2}.

(50)

Using the same conjugate symmetry, we rewrite
\bar{t}
as

\displaystyle\bar{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\text{ odd}}t_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in J}(t_{j}+t_{m-j})=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in J}t_{j}.

(51)

Step 3.
By Proposition
3.1
,
\sigma_{j}(g)/\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}
converges in distribution to
Z_{j}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
. Applying the continuous mapping theorem to the function
\log|\cdot|:\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\log|\sigma_{j}(g)|=\log\Bigl|\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{j}(g)}{\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}}\Bigr|\xrightarrow{d}\frac{1}{2}\log(n\sigma_{c}^{2})+\log|Z_{j}|.

(52)

From Lemma
3.2
(i), we have
\mathbb{E}[\log|Z_{j}|]=-\gamma/2
. Define the centered random variable as

\displaystyle X_{j}:=t_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\log(n\sigma_{c}^{2})+\frac{\gamma}{2}

(53)

so that
X_{j}\xrightarrow{d}\log|Z_{j}|+\gamma/2
. We compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log|Z_{j}|+\frac{\gamma}{2}]=0,\quad\Var(\log|Z_{j}|+\frac{\gamma}{2})=\Var(\log|Z_{j}|)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24},

(54)

where the second equality is from Lemma
3.2
(ii).

Step 4.
From the definition (
53
), we get
t_{j}=X_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\log(n\sigma_{c}^{2})-\frac{\gamma}{2}
. Combining with Eq.(
51
) implies

\displaystyle\bar{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in J}t_{j}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in J}(X_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\log(n\sigma_{c}^{2})-\frac{\gamma}{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(n\sigma_{c}^{2})-\frac{\gamma}{2}+\bar{X},

(55)

where we have used the sample mean
\bar{X}:=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in J}X_{j}
and
\frac{2}{n}
is from the definition of
\bar{t}
.

From Eq.(
50
), we rewrite the squared projected norm as

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\|_{2}^{2}=2\sum_{j\in J}(X_{j}-\bar{X})^{2}.

(56)

To decompose this sum, we use the standard algebraic identity for sample variance as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}(X_{j}-\bar{X})^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}^{2}-N\bar{X}^{2}.

(57)

Using
N=|J|=n/2
and
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in J}X_{j}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}(X_{j}-\bar{X})^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}X_{j}^{2}-\frac{n}{2}\cdot\bar{X}^{2}.

(58)

Dividing both sides of Eq.(
56
) by
n
and using Eq.(
58
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in J}(X_{j}-\bar{X})^{2}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in J}X_{j}^{2}-\bar{X}^{2}.

(59)

Step 5.
By Proposition
3.1
, for any fixed finite collection of different indices
j_{1},\dots,j_{s}
, the random vector
(X_{j_{1}},\dots,X_{j_{s}})
converges jointly in distribution to a vector of i.i.d. copies of
\log|Z|+\gamma/2
. The vector
(X_{j_{1}}^{2},\dots,X_{j_{s}}^{2})
then converges jointly in distribution to i.i.d. copies of
(\log|Z|+\gamma/2)^{2}
.

We now compute expectation using the eqaulity
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]=\Var(Y)+(\mathbb{E}[Y])^{2}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[(\log|Z|+\frac{\gamma}{2})^{2}\Bigr]=\Var(\log|Z|+\frac{\gamma}{2})+\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[\log|Z|+\frac{\gamma}{2}]\Bigr)^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24},

(60)

where the second equality is from the fact
\log|Z|+\gamma/2
has mean zero and variance
\pi^{2}/24
from Step 3 and Lemma
3.2
.

Note
X_{j}=t_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\log(n\sigma_{c}^{2})+\gamma/2
with
t_{j}=\log|\sigma_{j}(g)|
. Combining the finite fourth moment of
c_{i}
with the Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund Inequality
[
34
,
35
]
, we obtain that
\mathbb{E}[|\sigma_{j}(g)/\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}|^{-4}]
is uniformly bounded over
n
, which further implies
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}^{4}]<\infty
. For uniformly integrable triangular arrays
[
26
]
, we then obtain

\displaystyle S_{n}=\frac{1}{n/2}\sum_{j\in J}X_{j}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[(\log|Z|+\frac{\gamma}{2})^{2}\Bigr]=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}.

(61)

Step 6.
For each
X_{j}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]\to 0
when
n\to\infty
, since
X_{j}\xrightarrow{d}\log|Z|+\gamma/2
and the limiting random variable has mean zero. From the linearity of expectation, we get
\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}]=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in J}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]\to 0
.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\Var(\bar{X})=\Var(\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in J}X_{j})=\frac{4}{n^{2}}\cdot\Var(\sum_{j\in J}X_{j}).

(62)

Decomposing this variance into diagonal and off-diagonal terms as

\displaystyle\Var(\sum_{j\in J}X_{j})=\sum_{j\in J}\Var(X_{j})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\in J,j\neq k\end{subarray}}\Cov(X_{j},X_{k}).

(63)

From the CLT and Lemma
3.2
, we get
\Var(X_{j})\to\pi^{2}/24
as
n\to\infty
. Summing over
n/2
terms in
J
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}\Var(X_{j})=\frac{n}{2}\cdot(\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}+o(1))=\frac{n\pi^{2}}{48}+o(n).

(64)

From Lemma
3.1
, the embeddings
\sigma_{j}(g)
and
\sigma_{k}(g)
are uncorrelated for different
j
and
k
. From Proposition
3.1
, for independent random variables, its covariance is zero. This implies
\Cov(X_{j},X_{k})=o(1)
when
n\to\infty
for each fixed pair
j\neq k
. Note
\Cov(X_{j},X_{k})
depends only on
j-k
modulo
m
by the stationarity of the discrete Fourier transform. We get

\displaystyle|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\in J,j\neq k\end{subarray}}\Cov(X_{j},X_{k})|=O\Bigl(\frac{n^{2}}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)=O(n).

(65)

Using Proposition
3.1
, the total off-diagonal contribution is
o(n)
.

So, from Eqs.(
64
), (
65
), and (
63
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Var(\bar{X})=\frac{4}{n^{2}}\cdot(\frac{n\pi^{2}}{48}+o(n))=\frac{\pi^{2}}{12n}+o(1/n)\to 0

(66)

when
\text{as }n\to\infty
.

Using the Chebyshev’s Inequality
[
26
]
, for any fixed
\delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[|\bar{X}|>\delta]\leq\frac{\Var(\bar{X})+(\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}])^{2}}{\delta^{2}}.

(67)

Since
\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}]\to 0
and
\Var(\bar{X})\to 0
, the right-hand side tends to zero as
n\to\infty
. Hence,
\bar{X}\xrightarrow{p}0
. For the function
f(x)=x^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{X}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}0.

(68)

Step 7.
Combining Steps 5 and 6, using the Slutsky’s Theorem
[
17
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\lVert\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\rVert_{2}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}.

(69)

This further implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\|_{2}\xrightarrow{p}\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}}\approx 0.6413.

(70)

Finally, note the event of
g=0
requires all
c_{i}
s vanish simultaneously, so
\Pr[g=0]=\Pr[c_{0}=0]^{n}
. For any distribution with
\sigma_{c}^{2}>0
, we have
\Pr[c_{0}=0]<1
. Hence,
\Pr[g=0]\leq p_{0}^{n}
for some
p_{0}<1
. Conditional on
g\neq 0
, it changes probabilities by at most
\Pr[g=0]=o(1)
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.2
Voronoi cell containment

Theorem
4.1
computes the
L^{2}
distance from the structured target vector to the origin of the log-unit lattice. This subsection proves that with overwhelming probability the origin is indeed the nearest lattice point to
\mathbf{t}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
k\geq 4
, and
g\in R
be a non-zero ring element with i.i.d. coefficients
c_{i}\in\{-B,\dots,B\}
for some fixed
B\geq 1
. Let
\mathbf{t}=\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\in H_{0}
, and
\Lambda
denote the rank-
(\frac{n}{2}-1)
log-unit lattice. Then the following results hold:

(i)

Gaussian model.
If each non-conjugate embedding is an i.i.d.
\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
random variable, then
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{V}(0)
with probability
1-\exp\Bigl(-\frac{n}{2}\log n+O(n)\Bigr)
.

(ii)

Real ring model.
The output vector
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{V}(0)
holds with a probability
1-o(1)

c_{0}>0
(depending only on
B

\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda
and every
\lambda
with

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v}\rangle}]=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}\lambda^{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}+O(\lambda^{3}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2})+o(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}).

(71)

Consequently, under either model, the exact CVP distance satisfies

\displaystyle d(\mathbf{t},\Lambda)=\lVert\mathbf{t}\rVert_{2}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{n}+o(\sqrt{n}).

(72)

Note the Voronoi cell
\mathcal{V}(0)\subset H_{0}
contains all points in
H_{0}
that are closer to the origin than to any other lattice point in
\Lambda
. By the Euclidean lattice Voronoi characterization,
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{V}(0)
if and only if

\displaystyle 2\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v}\rangle\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}

(73)

for every non-zero
\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda
. For each non-zero
\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda
, we define the bad event
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{v}}=\{2|\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v}\rangle|\geq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}\}

4.2.1
Proof of Part (i)

For any
\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda\subset H_{0}
, we have
\sum_{j\text{ odd}}v_{j}=0
(by definition of
H_{0}
) and conjugate symmetry
v_{j}=v_{m-j}
, and
t_{j}=t_{m-j}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v}\rangle=\sum_{j\text{ odd}}(t_{j}-\bar{t})v_{j}=\sum_{j\text{ odd}}t_{j}v_{j}=2\sum_{j\in J}t_{j}v_{j},

(74)

where
J=\{1,3,\dots,n-1\}
.

Under the Gaussian model, non-conjugate embeddings are i.i.d.
Z_{j}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
. So
t_{j}=\log|Z_{j}|=\frac{1}{2}\log W_{j}
with
W_{j}=|Z_{j}|^{2}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
. From Eq.(
74
) we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v}\rangle=\sum_{j\in J}v_{j}\log W_{j}.

(75)

By independence of
W_{j}
s, the moment generating function (MGF) of
\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v}\rangle
is give by

\displaystyle M_{G}(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v}\rangle}]=\prod_{j\in J}\Gamma(1+\lambda v_{j}).

(76)

For
|\lambda|\leq 1/(2\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty})
, we have
|\lambda v_{j}|\leq 1/2
for all
j
. Using the Taylor expansion
\log\Gamma(1+s)=-\gamma s+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}s^{2}+O(|s|^{3})
for
|s|\leq 1/2
, we get

\displaystyle\log M_{G}(\lambda)=-\gamma\lambda\sum_{j\in J}v_{j}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\lambda^{2}\sum_{j\in J}v_{j}^{2}+O\left(|\lambda|^{3}\sum_{j\in J}|v_{j}|^{3}\right).

(77)

Note
\sum_{j\in J}v_{j}=0
(from
\mathbf{v}\in H_{0}
), so the linear term vanishes. Moreover,
\sum_{j\in J}v_{j}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}
(conjugate symmetry), and
\sum_{j\in J}|v_{j}|^{3}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}
(Hölder’s inequality). These imply

\displaystyle\log M_{G}(\lambda)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}\lambda^{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}+O(\lambda^{3}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}).

(78)

By symmetry, we have
\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{v}}]\leq 2\Pr\left[\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}\right]
. For any
\lambda>0
, Using the Markov’s Inequality
[
26
]
we get

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\exp\left(\log M_{G}(\lambda)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(79)

From Eq.(
78
), choose the optimal
\lambda^{*}=\min\left\{\frac{6}{\pi^{2}},\frac{1}{2\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}}\right\}
to minimize the exponent. For
\lambda^{*}
, we simplfy the exponent into
-C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}{\max\{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty},1\}}
for constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
. We get the unified tail bound as

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{v}}]\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{C^{\prime}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}{\max\{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty},1\}}\right).

(80)

Now, by union bound, we have
\Pr[\mathbf{t}\notin\mathcal{V}(0)]\leq\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{v}}]
. Split this sum into two cases.

•

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. By Eq.(
80
), each term is bounded by
Ce^{-C^{\prime}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}
. The sum equals to
C(\Theta_{\Lambda}(C^{\prime})-1)
, where
\Theta_{\Lambda}(\beta)=\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda}e^{-\beta\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}
is the lattice theta function.

For the log-unit lattice, note
r=\frac{n}{2}-1
,
\log\det(\Lambda)=\frac{n}{2}\log n+O(n)
, and then dual lattice shortest vector
\lambda_{1}(\Lambda^{*})=\Omega(1/\sqrt{n})
. So, we get
\Theta_{\Lambda^{*}}(\pi^{2}/C^{\prime})=O(1)
. Using the Poisson summation, the total contribution from this case is
\exp\left(-\frac{n}{2}\log n+O(n)\right)
.

•

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}>1
. Here
\max\{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty},1\}=\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}
. So, Eq.(
80
) implies
\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{v}}]\leq Ce^{-C^{\prime}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}}
. For short vectors, the Theta function bound gives total contribution
\exp(-\frac{3n}{8}\log n+O(n\log\log n))=o(1)
. For long vectors, each term is bounded by
e^{-\Omega(\sqrt{n}\log n)}
, and the number of such vectors is poly
(n)
. So, the total contribution is
o(1)
.

Combining both cases, the total failure probability for the Gaussian model is
\exp(-\frac{n}{2}\log n+O(n))
. This completes the proof of Part (i).

4.2.2
Proof of Part (ii)

In the real ring model, all embeddings are weakly dependent (not i.i.d.), but the hypothesis (
71
) guarantees the log-MGF matches the Gaussian model (
78
) up to a vanishing
o(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2})
error term.

We repeat the Chernoff argument from Step 3, with optimal
\lambda^{\prime}=\min\left\{\frac{6}{\pi^{2}},\frac{c_{0}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}}\right\}
satisfying the hypothesis. For sufficiently large
n
, the
o(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2})
term is negligible. We get

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{v}}]\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{(C^{\prime}-\epsilon_{n})\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}{\max\{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty},1\}}\right),

(81)

where
\epsilon_{n}=o(1)
. For large enough
n
, we have
C^{\prime}-\epsilon_{n}\geq C^{\prime}/2>0
. So, the bound is the same exponential decay as the Gaussian case.

We then apply the identical union bound from Step 4: For
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, the sum is
\exp\left(-\frac{n}{2}\log n+O(n)\right)=o(1)
; For
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}>1
, the sum is
o(1)
. Thus, the total failure probability is
o(1)
. This completes the proof of Part (ii).

Since
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{V}(0)
with an overwhelming probability, the nearest lattice point to
\mathbf{t}
is the origin. Combining with the asymptotic

norm from Theorem

\displaystyle d(\mathbf{t},\Lambda)=\lVert\mathbf{t}\rVert_{2}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{n}+o(\sqrt{n}),

(82)

4.3
The
L^{\infty}
bound and sub-polynomial SGP approximation factor

The approximation factor for the Short Generator Problem (SGP) in the CDPR attack is controlled by the
L^{\infty}
norm of the CVP residual, i.e., the approximation factor
\gamma=\exp(\rho_{\infty})
, where
\rho_{\infty}=\lVert\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))-\ell^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}
and
\ell^{\prime}
is the nearest lattice point to the target. By Theorem
4.2
,
\ell^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
for short generators with an overwhelming probability, so
\rho_{\infty}=\lVert\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\rVert_{\infty}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
g=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}\zeta^{i}\in R
be a non-zero ring element with i.i.d. bounded coefficients
c_{i}\in\{-B,\dots,B\}
for some fixed
B\geq 1
. Then the following result holds

\displaystyle\lVert\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{2\ln n}+O(\sqrt{\ln\ln n})

(83)

with a probability
1-o(1)
. The corresponding SGP approximation factor satisfies

\displaystyle\gamma=\exp(\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{\ln n}+O(\sqrt{\ln\ln n}))=o(n^{\epsilon})

(84)

for every
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

From the proof of Theorem
4.1
, the projected target vector has coordinates
t_{j}-\bar{t}
for an odd
j
, where
t_{j}=\log|\sigma_{j}(g)|
and
\bar{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\text{ odd}}t_{j}
. By the conjugate-pair relation for 2-power cyclotomic fields, we have
t_{m-j}=t_{j}
and hence
t_{m-j}-\bar{t}=t_{j}-\bar{t}
for all odd
j
s. Let
J=\{1,3,\dots,n-1\}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{j\text{ odd}}|t_{j}-\bar{t}|=\max_{j\in J}|t_{j}-\bar{t}|.

(85)

Define
X_{j}:=t_{j}-\bar{t}
for
j\in J
. Note each
X_{j}=t_{j}-\bar{t}
is a function of the
n
independent bounded coefficients
c_{0},\dots,c_{n-1}
. Fix an index
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, replace
c_{i}
with
c_{i}^{\prime}
such that
|c_{i}|,|c_{i}^{\prime}|\leq B
, while keep all other coefficients fixed. The embedding changes by
\sigma_{k}(g^{\prime})-\sigma_{k}(g)=(c_{i}^{\prime}-c_{i})\zeta^{ik}
, so
|\sigma_{k}(g^{\prime})-\sigma_{k}(g)|\leq|c_{i}^{\prime}-c_{i}|\cdot|\zeta^{ik}|\leq 2B
for all
k
. Using the Central Limit Theorem
[
26
]
,
|\sigma_{k}(g)|=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
with a probability
1-o(1)
. On this high-probability event, using the Mean-value Theorem
[
26
]
, we get

\displaystyle|\log|\sigma_{k}(g^{\prime})|-\log|\sigma_{k}(g)||\leq\frac{|\sigma_{k}(g^{\prime})-\sigma_{k}(g)|}{\min\{|\sigma_{k}(g)|,|\sigma_{k}(g^{\prime})|\}}=O\bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigr).

(86)

We now bound the change in
X_{j}=t_{j}-\bar{t}
. The change in
t_{j}
is
O(1/\sqrt{n})
, and the change in
\bar{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\text{ odd}}t_{k}
is at most
O(1/\sqrt{n})
. Thus, the total change in
X_{j}
satisfies

\displaystyle|X_{j}^{\prime}-X_{j}|\leq|t_{j}^{\prime}-t_{j}|+|\bar{t}^{\prime}-\bar{t}|=O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)=:\Delta_{i},

(87)

where
\Delta_{i}
s are the same for all
i
s by symmetry.

Using the McDiarmid’s Inequality
[
36
,
20
]
,
X_{j}
is a zero-mean sub-Gaussian random variable with the variance

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{subG}}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Delta_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\cdot n\cdot O\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)=O(1).

(88)

The low-probability event (
|\sigma_{k}(g)|
too small) has a total probability
o(1)
.

From Lemma
3.2
and Proposition
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\Var(X_{j})\to\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(89)

For any sub-Gaussian random variable
X
, we get
\Var(X)\leq\sigma_{\mathrm{subG}}^{2}
. So,
\sigma_{\mathrm{subG}}^{2}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}-o(1)
.

From standard extreme-value theory for sub-Gaussian variables
[
37
,
19
]
, we use the variance
\sigma^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}
for the leading-order bound. Now, we use the asymptotic result for the maximum of
m
independent, identically distributed zero-mean sub-Gaussian random variables with variance
\sigma^{2}
as

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq m}|X_{j}|=\sigma\sqrt{2\ln m}+O(\sqrt{\ln\ln m})

(90)

with probability
1-o(1)
. While
X_{j}
s are asymptotically independent by Proposition
3.1
, their weak dependence does not affect the leading asymptotic term or the
O(\sqrt{\ln\ln m})
error bound for sub-Gaussian sequences
[
38
,
39
]
.

In our setting, we have
m=|J|=n/2
and
\sigma=\pi/(2\sqrt{6})
. So, we can get

\displaystyle\max_{j\in J}|X_{j}|

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{2\ln(n/2)}+O(\sqrt{\ln\ln(n/2)})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{2\ln n-2\ln 2}+O(\sqrt{\ln\ln n}).

(91)

Using the Taylor expansion of
\sqrt{a-b}=\sqrt{a}-\frac{b}{2\sqrt{a}}+O(a^{-3/2})
for a fixed
b
and large
a
, the difference between
\sqrt{2\ln n}
and
\sqrt{2\ln(n/2)}
is
O(1/\sqrt{\ln n})
. We thus obtain

\displaystyle\max_{j\in J}|X_{j}|=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{2\ln n}+O(\sqrt{\ln\ln n}),

(92)

which is the
L^{\infty}
bound (
83
).

By definition, the CDPR SGP approximation factor is
\gamma=\exp(\lVert\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))\rVert_{\infty})
, as the nearest lattice point is
\mathbf{0}
with an overwhelming probability by Theorem
4.2
. From Eq. (
92
), we get

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl(\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{6}}\sqrt{2\ln n}+O(\sqrt{\ln\ln n})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl(\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{\ln n}+O(\sqrt{\ln\ln n})\bigr).

(93)

To show
\gamma=o(n^{\epsilon})
for every
\epsilon>0
, we take the logarithm of both sides as

\displaystyle\log\gamma=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{\ln n}+O\Bigl(\sqrt{\ln\ln n}\Bigr)=O(\sqrt{\ln n}).

(94)

For any fixed
\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\log\gamma}{\epsilon\ln n}=\frac{O(\sqrt{\ln n})}{\ln n}=O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ln n}}\Bigr)\to 0

(95)

as
n\to\infty
. This implies
\log\gamma=o(\epsilon\ln n)
for every
\epsilon>0
, or
\gamma=\exp(o(\epsilon\ln n))=o(n^{\epsilon})
. This completes the proof.
∎

For the ML-KEM (standard parameter set
n=256
), we have
\ln 256=8\ln 2\approx 5.545
, so
\sqrt{\ln 256}\approx 2.355
. The leading coefficient
\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}}\approx 0.9069
. The leading term of
\log\gamma
is
0.9069\times 2.355\approx 2.136
. So, the resulting approximation factor is
\gamma\approx\exp(2.136)\approx 8.47\approx 2^{3.1}
.

5
The Coarse Lattice Theorem and Babai Recovery

This section proves two complementary results about Babai’s algorithm on the log-unit lattice. The Coarse Lattice Theorem shows Babai returns a zero vector for all structured targets, an unconditional result that does not require the Voronoi containment of Theorem
4.2
. The Babai Capture Theorem shows that Babai exactly recovers lattice-vector perturbations of arbitrary size, so the CDPR pipeline succeeds whenever the balanced target maps to zero.

5.1
The Coarse Lattice Theorem

The term coarse refers to a scale mismatch: the Gram-Schmidt norms of
\Lambda
are
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
, while the per-component standard deviation of the structured target is
O(1)
. When the lattice space greatly exceeds the target’s fluctuations in every GS direction, the target sits in a tiny neighborhood of the origin, and Babai’s rounding step always gives zero.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be the rank-
r
log-unit lattice with Gram-Schmidt basis
\{\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{r}
,
\mathbf{t}\in H_{0}
be a random vector with independent, zero-mean, sub-Gaussian components with per-component variance
\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}
. Suppose the coarseness condition holds

\displaystyle\frac{\min_{1\leq i\leq r}\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}}=\omega(\log n).

(96)

Then the Babai’s nearest-plane algorithm returns the zero lattice vector with probability
1-o(1)
, and the Babai residual equals
\mathbf{t}
as

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{Babai}}(\mathbf{t},\Lambda)^{2}=\|\mathbf{t}\|_{2}^{2}=n\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}.

(97)

Proof.

From Definition
2.6
, the Babai’s algorithm processes the GS basis in reverse order
i=r,r-1,\dots,1
. At each step
i
, it computes the projection coefficient

\displaystyle c_{i}=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\langle\mathbf{t}-\sum_{j>i}c_{j}\mathbf{b}_{j},\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}\Bigr\rceil.

(98)

The algorithm outputs
\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i}
with residual
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{v}
.

If
c_{j}=0
for all
j>i
, then
\sum_{j>i}c_{j}\mathbf{b}_{j}=\mathbf{0}
, and the projection coefficient at step
i
reduces to

\displaystyle\mu_{i}=\frac{\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}.

(99)

The rounded coefficient is
c_{i}=\lfloor\mu_{i}\rceil
. This equals zero if and only if
|\mu_{i}|<\frac{1}{2}
.

For case
i=r
, there is no sum over
j>r
, so
\mu_{r}
is given by Eq. (
99
).

For
i<r
, assume
c_{j}=0
for all
j>i
. Eq. (
99
) holds for step
i
. So, Babai’s algorithm returns
\mathbf{0}
if and only if
|\mu_{i}|<\frac{1}{2}
for every
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
. This can be verified for each
i
. So, the problem reduces to show
|\mu_{i}|<\frac{1}{2}
simultaneously for all
i
.

We now bound
|\mu_{i}|
using the sub-Gaussian assumption on
\mathbf{t}
. Rewrite the inner product as

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}(\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime})_{j},

(100)

where
t_{j}
s are the components of
\mathbf{t}
and
(\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime})_{j}
s are the components of the
i
-th GS vector. By assumption,
t_{j}
s are independent, zero-mean, sub-Gaussian random variables with common parameter
\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}
. Consider he sub-Gaussian summation property (
[
19
]
): if
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent sub-Gaussian variables with parameters
\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{n}^{2}
, then
\sum_{j}a_{j}X_{j}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}
. So, for
a_{j}=(\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime})_{j}
and
\sigma_{j}^{2}=\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}
for all
j
, we get

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\sim\mathrm{subG}\bigl(0,\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime})_{j}^{2}\bigr)=\mathrm{subG}\bigl(0,\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}\bigr).

(101)

Dividing by
\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
, the projection coefficient is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{i}=\frac{\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rangle}{\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\sim\mathrm{subG}\Bigl(0,\frac{\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}\Bigr).

(102)

The sub-Gaussian parameter of
\mu_{i}
is the given by
\sigma_{\mu_{i}}^{2}=\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}/\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}
, which is the ratio of the target’s variance to the squared GS norm. So, for any
t\geq 0
we obtain the sub-Gaussian tail bound as

\displaystyle\Pr[|\mu_{i}|\geq t]\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mu_{i}}^{2}}\Bigr).

(103)

Setting
t=\frac{1}{2}
(the rounding threshold), we get

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl(|\mu_{i}|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(1/2)^{2}}{2\cdot\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}/\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\Bigr)=2\exp\bigl(-\frac{\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{8\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}}\bigr).

(104)

By the coarseness condition (
96
), we get
\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}/\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}=\omega(\log n)
for every
i
. Hence, the exponent satisfies
\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}/(8\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2})=\omega(\log n)
, and

\displaystyle\Pr[|\mu_{i}|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}]\leq 2\exp(-\omega(\log n))=o(1/n),

(105)

where the last uses
\exp(-\omega(\log n))=o(1/n^{c})
for every constant
c>0
. As there are
r\leq n/2
GS directions, the event that Babai does not return
\mathbf{0}
is
\{\exists i:|\mu_{i}|\geq 1/2\}
. So, by the union bound, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\exists i:|\mu_{i}|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\Pr[|\mu_{i}|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}]\leq r\cdot 2\exp(-\omega(\log n))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2}\cdot o(1/n)=o(1).

(106)

Here, the factor
n/2
is absorbed as the exponential decay is super-polynomial.

Finally, all
c_{i}=0
with a probability
1-o(1)
. The Babai output is given by
\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}0\cdot\mathbf{b}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
, and the residual is
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{t}
. So, we get
d_{\mathrm{Babai}}(\mathbf{t},\Lambda)^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}
. This means for structured targets with i.i.d. sub-Gaussian components of variance
\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}
, we get Eq. (
97
) as the law of large numbers gives
\|\mathbf{t}\|_{2}^{2}\approx n\sigma_{\mathbf{t}}^{2}
.
∎

The proof supposes that the components
t_{j}
of
\mathbf{t}
are independent. For the real structured target
\mathbf{t}=\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))
, the components are not exactly independent, but asymptotically independent by Proposition
3.1
and Theorem
4.3
. The sub-Gaussian tail bound (
104
) holds for this model. Hence, the Coarse Lattice Theorem applies to the real model.

5.2
Infinite capture radius

In the CDPR pipeline, the target is not
L(g_{0})
but
L(g)=L(g_{0})+L(\epsilon)
, where
L(\epsilon)\in\Lambda
can be an arbitrarily large lattice vector. The following theorem shows that Babai’s algorithm can resolve this problem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
have basis
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
with QR decomposition
B=QR
. For any target
\mathbf{t}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and lattice vector
\mathbf{v}=B\mathbf{c}
(
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
), let
\mathbf{v}_{0}=B\mathbf{x}_{0}
be the Babai output for
\mathbf{t}_{0}
, and
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=B\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
for
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{t}_{0}+\mathbf{v}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}_{0}.

(108)

Proof.

It is easy to show the nearest-integer rounding function
\lfloor\cdot\rceil:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}
satisfies

\displaystyle\lfloor a+b\rceil=a+\lfloor b\rceil

(109)

for all
a\in\mathbb{Z},b\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the basis
B=QR
with
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
(orthonormal columns) and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
(upper triangular, positive diagonal). Babai’s algorithm computes

•

\boldsymbol{\beta}=Q^{T}\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}

•

For
i=r,r-1,\dots,1
:

\displaystyle x_{i}=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\beta_{i}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}x_{j}}{R_{ii}}\Bigr\rceil.

(110)

•

Output
\mathbf{v}=B\mathbf{x}=QR\mathbf{x}
.

Here, the columns of
Q
are the normalized GS vectors,
Q_{i}=\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}/\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}
, and
R_{ii}=\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}
,
R_{ji}=\mu_{ij}\lVert\mathbf{b}_{j}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}
for
j<i
. The quantity
\beta_{i}=\mathbf{q}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{t}=\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rangle/\|\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\|_{2}
is the projection of
\mathbf{t}
onto
\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}
, scaled by
\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}
. Equation (
110
) is equivalent to the GS back-substitution in Definition
2.6
.

Let
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{t}_{0}+\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{t}_{0}+B\mathbf{c}
with
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. The projected vector is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}=Q^{T}\mathbf{t}=Q^{T}\mathbf{t}_{0}+Q^{T}B\mathbf{c}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)}+R\mathbf{c},

(111)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)}=Q^{T}\mathbf{t}_{0}
is the projection of the unperturbed target, and we have used
Q^{T}B=R
. Since
R
is upper triangular,
(R\mathbf{c})_{i}=\sum_{j\geq i}R_{ij}c_{j}
depends only on
c_{i},c_{i+1},\dots,c_{r}
.

For
i=r
, the back-substitution at the top step gives

\displaystyle x_{r}^{\prime}=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\beta_{r}}{R_{rr}}\Bigr\rceil.

(112)

From Eq. (
111
), we get
\beta_{r}=\beta_{r}^{(0)}+(R\mathbf{c})_{r}=\beta_{r}^{(0)}+R_{rr}c_{r}
since
R
is upper triangular, where the only term in
(R\mathbf{c})_{r}
involving row
r
is
R_{rr}c_{r}
. Dividing by
R_{rr}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{r}}{R_{rr}}=\frac{\beta_{r}^{(0)}+R_{rr}c_{r}}{R_{rr}}=c_{r}+\frac{\beta_{r}^{(0)}}{R_{rr}}.

(113)

Since
c_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}
, Eq.(
109
) gives

\displaystyle x_{r}^{\prime}=\Bigl\lfloor c_{r}+\frac{\beta_{r}^{(0)}}{R_{rr}}\Bigr\rceil=c_{r}+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\beta_{r}^{(0)}}{R_{rr}}\Bigr\rceil=c_{r}+x_{r}^{(0)}.

(114)

For
i<r
, assume
x_{j}^{\prime}=c_{j}+x_{j}^{(0)}
for all
j>i
. At step
i
, Babai’s algorithm computes

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{\beta_{i}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}x_{j}^{\prime}}{R_{ii}}\Bigr\rceil.

(115)

Using
\beta_{i}=\beta_{i}^{(0)}+(R\mathbf{c})_{i}
from Eq.(
111
) and
x_{j}^{\prime}=c_{j}+x_{j}^{(0)}
, we then get

\displaystyle\beta_{i}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}x_{j}^{\prime}=\beta_{i}^{(0)}+(R\mathbf{c})_{i}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}c_{j}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}x_{j}^{(0)}.

(116)

Note
(R\mathbf{c})_{i}=\sum_{j\geq i}R_{ij}c_{j}=R_{ii}c_{i}+\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}c_{j}
. So, we have

\displaystyle\beta_{i}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}x_{j}^{\prime}=R_{ii}c_{i}+(\beta_{i}^{(0)}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}x_{j}^{(0)}).

(117)

Dividing by
R_{ii}
, we then get

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{i}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}x_{j}^{\prime}}{R_{ii}}=c_{i}+\frac{\beta_{i}^{(0)}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}x_{j}^{(0)}}{R_{ii}}.

(118)

Since
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, Eq.(
109
) gives

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}=\Bigl\lfloor c_{i}+\frac{\beta_{i}^{(0)}-\sum_{j>i}R_{ij}x_{j}^{(0)}}{R_{ii}}\Bigr\rceil=c_{i}+x_{i}^{(0)}.

(119)

This completes the induction.

By induction,
x_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}+x_{i}^{(0)}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Applying the basis matrix gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=B\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=B(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{x}_{0})=B\mathbf{c}+B\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}_{0}.

(120)

Especially, if
\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, then
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\mathbf{v}
, i.e., Babai recovers the lattice vector
\mathbf{v}
. This completes the proof.
∎

5.3
Application to the CDPR pipeline

We combine Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
with the CDPR attack.

(1)

PIP output.
Suppose the quantum PIP algorithm produces a generator
g=g_{0}\epsilon
, where
g_{0}
is a short generator and
\epsilon\in R^{\times}
is a unit. In the log-embedding, it gives

\displaystyle L(g)=L(g_{0})+L(\epsilon),\qquad L(\epsilon)\in\Lambda.

(121)

(2)

Babai’s algorithm on the balanced target
(Theorem
5.1
).
The balanced target is
\mathbf{t}_{0}=\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g_{0}))
. By the Coarse Lattice Theorem
5.1
, Babai’s algorithm returns
\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
for
\mathbf{t}_{0}
with a probability
1-o(1)
.

(3)

Babai’s algorithm on the PIP output
(Theorem
5.2
).
The real target is
\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))=\mathbf{t}_{0}+L(\epsilon)
with
L(\epsilon)\in\Lambda
. By the Capture Theorem
5.2
with
\mathbf{v}=L(\epsilon)
and
\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, Babai’s output is

\displaystyle\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g))=L(\epsilon)+\mathbf{0}=L(\epsilon).

(122)

(4)

Short generator recovery.

From
L(\epsilon)
, the unit
\epsilon
is reconstructed. The short generator is then
g^{\prime}=g\cdot\epsilon^{-1}=g_{0}
. The approximation factor is given by

\displaystyle\gamma=\exp(\lVert\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g_{0}))\rVert_{\infty})=\exp(O(\sqrt{\log n})),

(123)

using Theorem
4.3
for the
L^{\infty}
norm of the

This result is unconditional except for the probabilistic statement in step (2) that the balanced target maps to zero. This holds with probability

by the Coarse Lattice Theorem

, using only the sub-Gaussianity of the log-embedding.

6
The Trigamma Theorem for Module Lattices

We now extend our ideal-lattice analysis to the module lattice setting. The CDPR attack on module lattices reduces the Module Short Generator Problem (Module-SGP) to an ideal-lattice SGP on the module’s determinant ideal (Part II).

6.1
The Trigamma Theorem

ML-KEM deploys module lattices of rank
d\in\{2,3,4\}
over the cyclotomic ring
R=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{256}]
. For a general module with basis matrix
B\in R^{d\times d}
, the CDPR attack reduces the module SGP to an ideal SGP on the determinant ideal
(\det B)\subset R
. The key question reduces to: what is the CVP distance for the shortest generator of this determinant ideal?

From Theorems
4.1
and
4.3
, the CVP distance and resulting SGP approximation factor are controlled by the per-component variance
\sigma_{g_{0}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\lVert\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g_{0}))\rVert_{2}^{2}
, where
g_{0}=\det B
is the shortest generator of the determinant ideal.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
R=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{2^{k}}]
be the
2^{k}
-th cyclotomic ring with
n=2^{k-1}
, and
B=(b_{ij})\in R^{d\times d}
be a module basis matrix whose entries are independent ring elements. Each entry
b_{ij}
has i.i.d. zero-mean coefficients with variance
\sigma_{c}^{2}
and finite fourth moment, with mutual independence across all entries. Define
g_{0}=\det(B)\in R
and the per-component variance
\sigma_{g_{0}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\lVert\Pi_{H_{0}}(L(g_{0}))\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\sigma_{g_{0}}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\psi^{\prime}(j)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

(124)

where
\psi^{\prime}(s)
denotes the trigamma function.

Proof.

Note every canonical embedding
\sigma_{j}:R\to\mathbb{C}
is a ring homomorphism, so it commutes with the determinant operation for matrices over
R
. For any
j
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}(g_{0})=\sigma_{j}(\det B)=\det(\sigma_{j}(B)),

(125)

where
\sigma_{j}(B)\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
denotes the matrix obtained by applying
\sigma_{j}
entry-wise to
B
. Taking the modulus and logarithm of both sides, we obtain

\displaystyle\log|\sigma_{j}(g_{0})|=\log|\det(\sigma_{j}(B))|.

(126)

Note each entry of
B
is a random ring element with i.i.d. zero-mean coefficients of variance
\sigma_{c}^{2}
. Applying Proposition
3.1
entry-wise, for any fixed non-conjugate index
j\in J
, the normalized embedded matrix converges in distribution as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sigma_{j}(B)\xrightarrow{d}M^{(j)},\tau=\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}},

(127)

where
M^{(j)}
is a
d\times d
matrix with i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian entries
\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
. Moreover, from the joint CLT, the limiting matrices
\{M^{(j)}\}_{j\in J}
are mutually independent, as the embeddings for different non-conjugate indices are asymptotically independent by Proposition
3.1
.

Let
M
be a
d\times d
matrix with i.i.d.
\mathcal{CN}(0,\tau^{2})
entries. Consider its QR decomposition
M=QR
. A classical result in random matrix theory gives the exact distribution of the squared diagonal entries of
R

[
40
,
41
]
: the normalized variables
R_{ii}^{2}/\tau^{2}
are independent, and has Gamma distributions as
\frac{R_{ii}^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\sim\Gamma(d-i+1,1)
for
i=1,\dots,d
, where
\Gamma(k,1)
denotes the Gamma distribution with shape parameter
k
and rate parameter 1.

Since
Q
is unitary, we have
|\det Q|=1
, so
|\det M|=\prod_{i=1}^{d}R_{ii}
. Taking the logarithm of both sides, we decompose the log-determinant as

\displaystyle\log|\det M|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log R_{ii}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2}\log\Bigl(R_{ii}^{2}\Bigr)=d\log\tau+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log W_{i},

(128)

where we have defined the independent normalized random variables
W_{i}:=R_{ii}^{2}/\tau^{2}\sim\Gamma(d-i+1,1)
. The term
d\log\tau
is a constant, as it depends only on the scaling factor
\tau
and the module rank
d
.

Note the deterministic term
d\log\tau
does not contribute to the variance of
\log|\det M|
. By the independence of the
W_{i}
, we get the variance as

\displaystyle\Var(\log|\det M|)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Var(\log W_{i}).

(129)

For a Gamma-distributed random variable
W\sim\Gamma(k,1)
, the logarithmic moments are given by:
\mathbb{E}[\log W]=\psi(k)
and
\Var(\log W)=\psi^{\prime}(k)
, where
\psi
is the digamma function and
\psi^{\prime}
is the trigamma function. Using
k_{i}=d-i+1
and re-indexing the sum with
j=d-i+1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Var(\log|\det M|)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\psi^{\prime}(j).

(130)

This variance is independent of
\tau^{2}
, and hence independent of
\sigma_{c}^{2}
and the modulus
q
.

For any fixed finite collection of different indices
j_{1},\dots,j_{s}\in J
, the normalized matrices
\frac{1}{\tau}\sigma_{j_{\ell}}(B)
converge jointly in distribution to independent
d\times d
standard complex Gaussian matrices. We apply the continuous mapping theorem to the function
f(M)=\log|\det(M)|
and obtain

\displaystyle(\log|\sigma_{j_{\ell}}(g_{0})|)_{\ell=1}^{s}\xrightarrow{d}(\log|\det M^{(j_{\ell})}|)_{\ell=1}^{s},

(131)

where the right-hand side consists of i.i.d. copies of
\log|\det M|
.

Finally, applying Theorem
4.1
to the determinant element
g_{0}
, the per-component variance
\sigma_{g_{0}}^{2}
converges in probability to the variance of a single centered log-determinant component. Since the components are asymptotically i.i.d. with the distribution of
\log|\det M|-\mathbb{E}[\log|\det M|]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{g_{0}}^{2}\xrightarrow{p}\Var\Bigl(\log|\det M|\Bigr)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\psi^{\prime}(j),

(132)

which completes the proof.
∎

Across all valid centered distributions, the limit (
124
) depends only on the module rank
d
. Table
2
confirms this numerically:
\sigma_{g_{0}}
is stable across all tested values of
\sigma_{c}
corresponding to moduli
q\in\{3,11,101,3329,65537\}
.

We now combine all results to analyze the security of ML-KEM. ML-KEM is the NIST-standardized post-quantum public-key encryption scheme, whose security relies on the hardness of Module-LWE over the cyclotomic ring
R=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{256}]
with module ranks

Conclusion

We have proved several results for the short generator problem in
2
-power cyclotomic fields. Theorem
4.1
identifies the exact
L^{2}
constant
{\pi}/(2\sqrt{6})
. Theorem
4.3
yields the sub-polynomial SGP factor
\exp(O(\sqrt{\log n}))
. Theorem
4.2
shows the nearest lattice point is the origin. Numerical verification confirms the result for all tested parameters
k\in\{4,\dots,12\}
. Theorem
5.2
strengthens the Babai’s recovery to be unconditional for the cyclotomic-unit basis. The Coarse Lattice Theorem
5.1
explains the reason why
\Lambda
is too coarse for structured targets. The Trigamma Theorem
6.1
resolves the ML-KEM question, i.e.,
\sigma_{g_{0}}=O(1)
for module determinant ideals, independently of
q
.

Part I proved
h_{k}^{+}=1
unconditionally for
k\leq 12
. Part II reduced the module-SVP polynomial factor from
n^{O(d)}
to a universal constant
\alpha_{d}=O(1)
independent of
d
under a balance hypothesis automatic for MLWE inputs. Part III identifies the CDPR exponent as being controlled by
\sigma_{g_{0}}
. For ML-KEM-1024 (
d=4
,
n=256
), the SGP factor is
\gamma_{\mathrm{SGP}}\approx 2^{4-5}
, see Table
2
. The module reduction adds a factor
\alpha_{d}=O(1)
from Part II, independent of
d
. The combined approximation quality is sub-polynomial and sufficient for the attack, so ML-KEM’s security rests on the quantum gate cost of PIP
[
4
]
.
