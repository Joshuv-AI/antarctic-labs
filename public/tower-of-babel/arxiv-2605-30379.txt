Algebraic and FFT-Based Methods for Discrete-Time Matrix Convolutions with Applications to Semi-Markov Models
Leonidas Kordalis, Samis Trevezas

Abstract
We study the convolution product of matrix-valued sequences and its role in the computation of Markov renewal equations. Explicit representations and recursive formulae for the convolutional inverse are derived and used to construct FFT-accelerated convolution and Newton-type inversion schemes, together with a Gauss–Jordan alternative in truncated power-series rings. The proposed framework is also applied to discrete approximations of matrix Stieltjes convolutions, which arise in continuous-time semi-Markov models. These tools are then used for the numerical evaluation of semi-Markov reliability and availability functions. The numerical results show substantial reductions in runtime, while preserving close agreement with exact benchmark solutions, direct computations, and Monte Carlo simulations.

Introduction

Despite their flexibility and simplicity in both practical applications and formula expressions, Markov chains, as a dependence structure in stochastic processes and time-series analysis, rely on relatively restrictive assumptions. In particular, their defining property is memorylessness, which restricts the sojourn time distribution to only two cases:
(i)
the geometric distribution in discrete time and
(ii)
the exponential distribution in continuous time. This limitation motivates the introduction of semi-Markov processes and the corresponding Markov renewal theory, which allow for more general sojourn time distributions, albeit at the cost of increased model complexity. This class of stochastic processes and the associated Markov renewal representation are classical in semi-Markov theory; see, for instance,
[
23
,
7
,
17
]
. In particular, limiting results and theoretical developments were obtained in
[
23
]
, while
[
7
]
further developed Markov renewal theory by introducing the concept of periodicity within the semi-Markov framework.

One of the earliest contributions of semi-Markov processes appeared in queueing theory, where they were used to model non-exponential service times for different types of customers. Related renewal and semi-Markov formulations can be found in
[
7
,
10
,
3
]
. In reliability analysis, numerous studies on continuous-time semi-Markov reliability systems and their computation have been developed, most of which are summarized and cited in
[
17
]
.

On the other hand, the discrete-time case has attracted increased attention in the past 20 years and has been studied extensively in its classical formulation, with many applications in reliability (e.g.,
[
3
,
1
,
11
]
) and seismology (e.g.,
[
22
,
29
,
28
]
). In
[
2
,
26
]
, the authors developed several aspects of the theory of discrete–time Markov renewal chains, focusing on statistical inference via maximum likelihood and on the asymptotic properties of the resulting estimators.

The Stieltjes convolution plays a functional role in continuous-time Markov renewal theory due to its presence in integral equations used to compute important functions such as the renewal function or reliability metrics. However, it is generally not possible to obtain closed-form expressions, except in special cases of the sojourn time distribution. Consequently, the use of numerical methods is required, and several approaches have been proposed in the literature. The first attempts (e.g.,
[
21
]
,
[
4
]
) were based on cubic spline methods, which required complex computations and strong assumptions on the functions. Subsequently, simpler methods were proposed
[
33
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
32
]
based on Newton–Cotes rules, along with corresponding error approximations.

In the semi-Markov framework, the matrix Stieltjes convolution has similar importance. However, its inherent computational complexity requires the development of efficient numerical methods, often based on transform techniques such as generating functions or Laplace transforms
[
16
,
25
,
12
]
as well as algorithmic approaches based on a prescribed error that balance computational cost and accuracy
[
19
,
30
]
.

In the discrete-time case, the essential computations require the discrete-time convolution product, which is a generalization of the usual discrete convolution adapted to matrix-valued sequences. Nonetheless, the available approaches are typically limited to direct methods for computing the convolution product and the theoretical use of generating functions. As a result, there is currently no method that effectively accelerates the computation and analysis of discrete-time semi-Markov systems, and their computational performance remains limited.

This paper aims to present new, faster methods for computing discrete-time convolution products in the semi-Markov setting. Our motivation is both theoretical and practical. On the one hand, there is an important gap in the theoretical development concerning the convolution product of sequences of matrices. From a methodological standpoint, a central mathematical tool in our development is the convolution product of matrix sequences. The convolutional structure plays a fundamental role in describing the state evolution and in solving the governing renewal equations. Furthermore, we explore key algebraic properties of the convolution operator, with particular emphasis on the existence, representation, and efficient computation of the convolutional inverse.

These computational challenges are addressed by proposing and analyzing FFT-based convolution methods
[
9
]
, along with a novel adaptation of the Gauss–Jordan algorithm for matrix sequences. These tools enable the practical resolution of Markov renewal equations, making the proposed approach applicable even for large state-space systems. A typical example arises in the approximation of a semi-Markov process by a discrete-time semi-Markov chain, where the use of small discretization steps is required, as demonstrated in
[
15
]
. However, the computational performance remained poor due to the reliance on direct methods for the convolution product of matrix-valued sequences. Our method significantly improves computational performance, enabling efficient computation over large time horizons while permitting the use of smaller discretization steps.

Another contribution is based on a discrete approximation of the matrix Stieltjes convolution, combining discrete-time convolutions with FFT-based methods to accelerate computation. This method can be used to compute several semi-Markov metrics, such as reliability, availability, and the distribution of first hitting times, especially under heavy-tailed sojourn time distributions.

The contribution of the paper is threefold. First, we formulate the
discrete-time Markov renewal equations as matrix-valued convolution equations and exploit
the associated algebraic inverse. Second, we compute the required convolutions and convolutional inverses by FFT-based algorithms, reducing the computational cost of long-horizon
evaluations. Third, we use a discrete approximation of Stieltjes convolutions to apply the
same framework to continuous-time semi-Markov models, and we illustrate the method on
reliability and availability computations.

The structure of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
develops the algebraic framework and properties of discrete-time matrix convolutions. Section
3
presents the proposed discrete-time approximation of the Stieltjes matrix convolution.
Section
4
recalls the semi-Markov renewal equations to which the methods are applied. Section
5
provides numerical and applied illustrations in semi-Markov theory, covering both discrete-time semi-Markov chains and continuous-time semi-Markov processes, with particular emphasis on reliability and availability modeling.
Finally, the supplementary material contains auxiliary proofs, algorithmic details, and additional numerical results.

2
Discrete time convolution product

This section contains the algebraic definitions and identities needed later by the algorithms. We first define the following sets
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{N}^{*}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
. We work throughout with one discrete time index
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
E=\{1,2,\dots,s\}
be a finite set,
\mathcal{M}_{s}:=\mathbb{C}^{s\times s}
the set of all complex matrices on
E\times E
, and
\mathcal{R}:=\mathcal{M}_{s}^{\mathbb{N}}
the set of all
\mathcal{M}_{s}
-valued sequences defined on
\mathbb{N}
. Each
A\in\mathcal{R}
may be written either as a sequence of matrices
A(k)=(a_{ij}(k))_{i,j\in E}
or, equivalently, as a matrix of scalar sequences
(a_{ij})_{i,j\in E}
with
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
. We write
I_{s}
and
O_{s}
for the identity and zero matrices. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\|A\|_{\infty}(k):=\max_{0\leq n\leq k}\max_{i\in E}\sum_{j\in E}|A_{ij}(n)|.

For a finite computational grid of length
N
, we use the grid Frobenius norm

\|A\|_{G,N}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|A(k)\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

When the infinite tail is involved, we use the corresponding global norm

\|A\|_{G}=\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}\|A(k)\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Here
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius matrix norm. These norms are submultiplicative with respect to pointwise matrix multiplication; convolution estimates are treated separately by Young-type inequalities. In the sequel, the sequences considered on a finite grid are assumed to have finite
\|\cdot\|_{G,N}
norm, while infinite sequences are used under the corresponding summability assumptions.

We identify
A\in\mathcal{R}
with the formal power series

P_{A}(x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}A(k)x^{k},

which transfers convolution into multiplication of matrix-coefficient series. This point of view is useful for inverse formulas and for Newton iteration, and we use it only for these purposes. This tool has long been used in fields such as linear differential and difference equations, of which Markov renewal equations constitute a special case
[
13
]
. More information about polynomial matrices is deferred to the supplementary material.

2.1
Discrete-time convolution product

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{R}
be two matrix-valued sequences. Their convolution product, denoted by
A*B
, is given by

[A*B](k):=\sum_{l+l^{\prime}=k}A(l)\cdot B(l^{\prime}),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(1)

By identifying a sequence
A\in\mathcal{R}
with its formal power series
P_{A}
, one obtains an isomorphism between the ring of all
\mathcal{M}_{s}
-valued sequences on
\mathbb{N}
, endowed with convolution, and the ring
\mathcal{M}_{s}[[x]]
of formal power series with matrix coefficients (e.g.,
[
24
]
). Addition is identified term by term, while multiplication of formal power series corresponds to the convolution product defined above. In this sense, whenever convenient, we will interchange the two representations. We next recall some elementary properties. Since the coefficients are matrices, the convolution ring is generally noncommutative.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},+,*)
be the set of all matrix-valued sequences on
\mathbb{N}
, equipped with the usual addition
+
and the convolution product
*
between matrix sequences. Then
(\mathcal{R},+,*)
is a ring with unity. In particular, the discrete time convolution operator
*
is associative, and the identity element, denoted by
{e_{\mathstrut 0}}
, is given by

e_{\mathstrut 0}(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{s},&\textrm{if $k=0$,}\\
O_{s},&\textrm{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

and
*
is also distributive with respect to the addition of matrix sequences. If there exists
B\in\mathcal{R}
such that

A*B=e_{\mathstrut 0}=B*A,

(2)

then we say that
A
has a
convolutional inverse
and is denoted by
A^{(-1)}
.

Under the correspondence
A\leftrightarrow P_{A}
, the neutral element
e_{\mathstrut 0}
corresponds to the constant series
I_{s}
. Via the correspondence
A\leftrightarrow P_{A}
, one identifies
(\mathcal{R},+,*)
with a ring of formal power series with matrix coefficients.

The
partial-sum operator
can be represented as the convolution of
A
with a specific matrix-valued sequence.
In particular, if we denote by
s_{\mathstrut 0}
the constant identity (summation) sequence, that is
s_{\mathstrut 0}(k)=I_{s}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then

(s_{\mathstrut 0}*A)(k)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}A(\ell),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3)

The
n
-fold convolution power
of a sequence
A
is defined as follows:

A^{(0)}:=e_{\mathstrut 0}\quad\text{and}\quad A^{(n)}:=A*A^{(n-1)},\quad n\geq 1.

In this way,

A^{(n)}(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_{1},\ldots,l_{n}\in\mathbb{N}\\
l_{1}+\ldots+l_{n}=k\end{subarray}}A(l_{1})\cdots A(l_{n}).

(4)

2.2
FFT methods

Evaluating the discrete-time convolution product directly from (
1
) up to time
N
requires
s^{3}N^{2}
operations.
A more efficient approach is to exploit the fast Fourier transform (FFT) of a matrix sequence.
To this end, we first define the Fourier transform of a matrix sequence.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{R}
be such that each scalar sequence
A_{ij}
is absolutely summable. The discrete-time Fourier transform of
A
is defined componentwise by

\mathcal{F}(A)(\omega)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A(k)e^{-\mathrm{i}k\omega},\qquad\omega\in[-\pi,\pi].

(5)

For finite truncations, the discrete Fourier transform is defined without this summability
assumption.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\{A\}
be the DTFT of a sequence
A\in\mathcal{R}
.
The
inverse DTFT
is defined componentwise by

A_{ij}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathcal{F}\{A\}_{ij}(\omega)\,e^{ik\omega}\,d\omega,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},\;i,j\in E.

The numerical computation of the DTFT/IDTFT is approximated by the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)
\widehat{A}
, obtained from a finite sample of the matrix-valued sequence
A
of length
N
. To this end, we define the index set
I_{N}=\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
. The DFT of
A
is given by

\widehat{A}(m)=\sum_{k\in I_{N}}A(k)\,\exp\!\left(-2\pi\mathrm{i}\frac{km}{N}\right),\qquad m\in I_{N},

while the inverse DFT (IDFT) is

A(k)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in I_{N}}\widehat{A}(m)\,\exp\!\left\{2\pi\mathrm{i}\frac{km}{N}\right\},\qquad k\in I_{N}.

This can be interpreted as the evaluation of the DTFT of the truncated sequence at the discrete frequencies
\omega_{m}=2\pi m/N
.

Remark 1
.

For a sequence
A
of length
N
with DFT
\widehat{A}
, the corresponding norms satisfy the Parseval relation

\|A\|_{G,N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\|\widehat{A}\|_{G,N}.

(6)

In practice, the DFT is computed using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Two distinct errors have to be separated: the truncation error caused by replacing an infinite sequence by a finite grid, and the floating-point error introduced by the FFT/IFFT computations.

Let
A^{[N]}
be the truncation of
A
to
\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
, namely

A^{[N]}(k)=\begin{cases}A(k),&0\leq k\leq N-1,\\
0,&k\geq N.\end{cases}

(7)

Then the truncation error is measured by the tail norm

\|A-A^{[N]}\|_{G}=\left(\sum_{k\geq N}\|A(k)\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(8)

The floating-point error produced by the FFT is a separate numerical error and should be
treated separately.

Denoting by
\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}
the computed transform, we write

\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}=\widehat{A}+E_{A},

where
E_{A}
represents the floating-point error matrix-valued sequence.

The discrete Fourier transform of a matrix-valued sequence is computed componentwise. Consequently, it can be efficiently implemented using the classical Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm elementwise, leading to a significant reduction in computational complexity.
To bound the relative error of this procedure, we introduce the unit roundoff
u
and define

\gamma_{4}=\frac{4u}{1-4u},

together with a constant
C
that depends on the numerical method.
The following lemma (see
[
14
]
) provides an error bound for the real case of computing the scalar Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) or its corresponding inverse (IDFT).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{fl}}
be an approximation of the DFT/IDFT sequence
\alpha
up to length
n=2^{t}
, obtained via the Cooley–Tukey FFT/IFFT method.
Then the relative error with respect to the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
satisfies

\frac{\|\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{fl}}-\alpha\|_{2}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}\leq\frac{t\eta}{1-t\eta}:=\sigma,

where
t=\log_{2}(n)
and
\eta=Cu+\gamma_{4}(\sqrt{2}+Cu)
.
In particular, since
\eta=O(u)
, one obtains

\frac{\|\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathrm{fl}}-\alpha\|_{2}}{\|\alpha\|_{2}}=O(u\log n).

A similar error bound in terms of
\|\cdot\|_{G,N}
is presented below.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
N=2^{t}
, let
\widehat{A}
be the exact DFT of the matrix-valued sequence
A
on the grid
I_{N}
, and let
\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}
be its floating-point approximation obtained by the Cooley–Tukey FFT. Then the relative error with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{G,N}
satisfies

\frac{\|\widehat{A}-\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}\|_{G,N}}{\|\widehat{A}\|_{G,N}}\leq\sigma.

(9)

The same componentwise bound holds for the inverse transform.

Proof.

Applying the scalar FFT error bound componentwise to each scalar sequence
A_{ij}
, we obtain

\|\widehat{A}-\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}\|_{G,N}^{2}=\sum_{i,j\in E}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\widehat{A}_{ij}(k)-(\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}})_{ij}(k)|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\sum_{i,j\in E}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\widehat{A}_{ij}(k)|^{2}=\sigma^{2}\|\widehat{A}\|_{G,N}^{2}.

Taking square roots gives (
9
). The proof for the inverse transform is identical.
∎

Consequently, the relative error remains of order
\mathcal{O}(u\log N)
in the matrix-sequence case under the preceding componentwise FFT/IFFT error model.

The convolution theorem for matrix sequences follows from the power series property:

A*B=C\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\widehat{A}(m)\widehat{B}(m)=\widehat{C}(m),\quad m\in I_{N}.

The finite sequences are zero-padded to a length
N
larger than the degree of the desired product, for example
N\geq N_{A}+N_{B}-1
. With this convention, the DFT product gives the coefficients of the linear convolution.
Since this approach requires the computation of the DTFTs of
A
and
B
via FFT, their pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain, and the inverse DTFT via IFFT, we directly obtain the following result regarding the associated number of operations.

Proposition 2.6
.

For two matrix-valued sequences truncated at length
N
, the FFT-based computation of their linear convolution requires

\mathcal{O}(s^{2}N\log N+s^{3}N)

(10)

arithmetic operations after zero-padding.

Proof.

First,
2s^{2}
scalar FFT computations are required for the real sequences involved in the matrix-valued sequences, each requiring
N\log_{2}N
operations. Next, the multiplication of the associated FFT matrices requires
s^{3}
operations at each frequency point. Finally, the convolution product is obtained using
s^{2}
scalar IFFT computations. Therefore, the overall computational complexity is proportional to
(
10
)
∎

The reduction in computational cost achieved using FFT/IFFT methods is significant, while the corresponding relative error estimate follows from Proposition
2.5
, the Parseval relation (
6
), and the convolution bound stated in the supplementary material. The proof of the following corollary is deferred to the supplementary material.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
C=A*B
denote the exact linear convolution, truncated at the required order after zero-padding, and let
\widehat{C}_{\mathrm{fl}}
denote the result obtained by FFT multiplication and inverse FFT in floating-point arithmetic on a grid of length
N=2^{t}
. Assume that
C
is not identically zero. Then, under the preceding componentwise error model,

\frac{\|C-\widehat{C}_{\mathrm{fl}}\|_{G,N}}{\|C\|_{G,N}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{N}\,\|A\|_{G,N}\|B\|_{G,N}}{\|C\|_{G,N}}\left(3\sigma+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{2})\right).

This shows that the relative FFT/IFFT convolution error is of order
\mathcal{O}(u\log N)
under the adopted error model. Absolute errors, however, scale with the size of the quantities being transformed.

2.3
Explicit and recursive representation of the convolutional inverse

In this section, we study the representation and existence of the convolution inverse of a matrix-valued sequence based on specific properties of the convolution product.
In addition, we analyze its computational complexity and propose FFT-based methods to reduce it.

The following lemma provides a useful property of the convolution powers of a matrix-valued sequence
A
under the condition
A(0)=O_{s}
.
This condition is used below to make the Neumann-type series finite coefficientwise.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{R}
be a matrix-valued sequence with
A(0)=O_{s}
.
Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

A^{(n)}(k)=O_{s},\quad\text{for all }n>k.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
A
be a matrix-valued sequence. The convolution inverse of
A
exists if and only if
A(0)
is nonsingular.
Moreover, whenever it exists, the convolution inverse
A^{(-1)}
is given by

A^{(-1)}=A(0)^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(e_{\mathstrut 0}-A_{\mathstrut 0})^{(n)},

(11)

where

A_{\mathstrut 0}(k)=A(k)A(0)^{-1},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(12)

In (
11
) and (
13
), multiplication by
A(0)^{-1}
is understood coefficientwise from the left.
Moreover, each term
A^{(-1)}(k)
in (
11
) can be written as a finite sum:

A^{(-1)}(k)=A(0)^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{k}(e_{\mathstrut 0}-A_{\mathstrut 0})^{(n)}(k),

(13)

Proof.

If
A^{(-1)}
exists, then evaluating
A*A^{(-1)}=e_{\mathstrut 0}
at
0
gives
A(0)A^{(-1)}(0)=I_{s}
; hence
A(0)
is nonsingular. Conversely, suppose that
A(0)
is nonsingular. Let
D\in\mathcal{R}
be defined by
D(0)=A(0)^{-1}
and
D(k)=O_{s}
for
k\geq 1
, and set

\widetilde{A}:=A*D.

Then
\widetilde{A}(k)=A(k)A(0)^{-1}
and
\widetilde{A}(0)=I_{s}
. Put
C:=e_{\mathstrut 0}-\widetilde{A}
. Since
C(0)=O_{s}
, Lemma
2.8
implies that

S:=\sum_{n\geq 0}C^{(n)}

is finite coefficientwise. Moreover,

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-C)*S=S-C*S=e_{\mathstrut 0},\qquad S*(e_{\mathstrut 0}-C)=S-S*C=e_{\mathstrut 0}.

Thus
S=\widetilde{A}^{(-1)}
. Let
D^{-1}
denote the sequence concentrated at zero with value
A(0)
. Since
\widetilde{A}=A*D
, we have
A=\widetilde{A}*D^{-1}
. Hence
D*S
is the convolutional inverse of
A
, because
A*(D*S)=e_{\mathstrut 0}
and
(D*S)*A=D*S*\widetilde{A}*D^{-1}=e_{\mathstrut 0}
. Therefore
A^{(-1)}=D*S
, which gives

A^{(-1)}(k)=A(0)^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{k}(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\widetilde{A})^{(n)}(k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

This is exactly (
13
), because
\widetilde{A}=A_{\mathstrut 0}
.
∎

The determination of the inverse term in (
13
) up to time
k
requires at least

\mathcal{O}\!\left(s^{3}k^{3}\right)
and
\mathcal{O}\!\left(s^{3}k^{2}\log k\right)

operations when computed by direct and FFT-based convolution, respectively, which becomes prohibitive for large grids.

A commonly used approach is the classical recursive method, which provides a practical alternative. The corresponding computational cost, up to time
N
, is proportional to

\mathcal{O}\!\left(s^{3}N^{2}\right)
.

An alternative is the following recursive numerical scheme, based on the identity

A^{(-1)}=e_{\mathstrut 0}+(e_{\mathstrut 0}-A)*A^{(-1)},

which generates the sequence
A^{(-1)}(\cdot,n)
via

A^{(-1)}(\cdot,n)=e_{\mathstrut 0}+(e_{\mathstrut 0}-A)*A^{(-1)}(\cdot,n-1).

This fixed-point iteration is used as a residual-based numerical scheme; its convergence requires additional assumptions, for instance a contraction condition on
e_{\mathstrut 0}-A
in the chosen norm. The iteration is continued until a step
n
such that

\|e_{\mathstrut 0}-A*A^{(-1)}(\cdot,n)\|_{\infty}<\epsilon,

for some prescribed tolerance
\epsilon>0
.

The associated complexity is proportional to

\mathcal{O}\!\left(s^{3}nN\log N\right)
, which remains high and may even exceed that of the previous method.

Nevertheless, the complexity can be substantially reduced by reformulating the problem in terms of power series and applying Newton’s iteration, or alternatively by using a Gauss–Jordan scheme adapted to the convolution product and accelerated via FFT techniques. Both approaches are analyzed in the sequel.

2.4
Acceleration of convolutional inverse computation

Efficient computation of convolutional inverses is essential for the practical applicability of the theory.
We first study Newton’s inversion method, formulated for polynomial matrices modulo truncation ideals, following the classical real-valued setting.
We then develop a Gauss–Jordan elimination scheme adapted to the convolution product by constructing the corresponding theoretical framework.
Both methods lead to substantially faster inversion procedures.

Inversion with Newton’s iteration method

Let
P_{A}
be a matrix-valued formal power series, and seek another power series
P_{B}
such that

P_{A}(x)\cdot P_{B}(x)=I_{s}=P_{B}(x)\cdot P_{A}(x).

(14)

Thus,
B
is the convolutional inverse of
A
. In order to compute
B
efficiently, we apply Newton’s iterative method, which updates the approximation according to

P_{B,N+1}=P_{B,N}+P_{B,N}\cdot(I_{s}-P_{A}\cdot P_{B,N})\mod x^{2^{N+1}}.

(15)

In this iteration scheme,
P_{B,N}
denotes the truncated polynomial approximation of the inverse series
P_{B}
at the
N
-th iteration level. Specifically,
P_{B,N}
contains the coefficients of degrees
0,\ldots,2^{N}-1
, obtained by reducing modulo the ideal
\langle x^{2^{N}}\rangle
. In practice, the truncation length is often padded to the next power of two, where radix-two FFT implementations are especially efficient.

This doubling of the truncation order is expressed by carrying out the next iteration in the quotient ring

\mathcal{M}_{s}[[x]]/\langle x\rangle^{2^{N+1}}.

If
M
denotes the truncation length, direct polynomial multiplication has quadratic cost in
M
, whereas the FFT–Newton implementation has cost of order
\mathcal{O}(s^{3}M\log M)
, up to constants depending on the matrix dimension.
A complete proof of (
15
) is given in Appendix E of the supplementary material.

Inversion with the Gauss–Jordan algorithm

An alternative and often more practical method for computing the convolutional inverse is a convolution-based
Gauss–Jordan algorithm
. To implement this approach, we use the convolution theorem,

A*B=e_{\mathstrut 0}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;P_{A}P_{B}=I_{s}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;P_{B}=P_{A}^{-1},

where
P_{A}
and
P_{B}
denote the polynomial matrix representations of the matrix-valued sequences
A
and
B
, respectively. Thus, the convolutional inverse
B
can be obtained by computing the polynomial matrix inverse
P_{A}^{-1}
via Gauss–Jordan elimination. In the implementation, the elimination is performed over the truncated power-series ring
\mathbb{C}[[x]]/\langle x^{M}\rangle
. All operations are understood modulo
x^{M}
, and a scalar pivot is admissible when its constant coefficient is nonzero, because then it is invertible in the truncated power-series ring. Since polynomial matrix multiplication can be accelerated using FFT-based convolution, this yields an alternative accelerated approach for computing the convolutional inverse.

3
Computation of the Matrix Stieltjes Convolution

This section introduces an efficient computational framework for Stieltjes matrix convolutions, based on discrete mean value–type approximations.
The approach is motivated by applications to continuous-time semi-Markov processes, with particular emphasis on reliability modeling in the presence of heavy-tailed sojourn-time distributions.

Let
f:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be nondecreasing and of bounded variation on compact intervals, and let
g:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be locally bounded. The scalar Stieltjes convolution is defined by

[f\star g](t)=\int_{[0,t]}g(t-x)\,df(x).

An element
A\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is a matrix-valued function, where for a fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

A(t)=\bigl(a_{ij}(t)\bigr)_{i,j\in E}.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Their Stieltjes matrix convolution
A\star B
corresponds to an element
C\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
whose entries are given by

C_{ij}(t)=\sum_{r\in E}\int_{[0,t]}b_{rj}(t-x)\,da_{ir}(x),\quad i,j\in E,\;t\geq 0.

With this convention, the first factor is the Stieltjes integrator. This is the convention used below for the semi-Markov renewal equations.

For any
A\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
n
-fold Stieltjes matrix convolution by

A^{(0)}=\mathbb{I},\quad A^{(n)}=A\star A^{(n-1)},\quad n\geq 1.

Here
A^{(0)}
denotes the identity element for the Stieltjes convolution, namely the matrix-valued distribution concentrated at zero with mass
I_{s}
.

For a Stieltjes integral of the form

\int_{[0,t]}\varphi(u)\,dG(u),

where
G
is of bounded variation, we approximate the smooth term

\varphi
on each subinterval by the average of its endpoint values.
If
0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{n}=t
, this gives

\int_{[0,t]}\varphi(u)\,dG(u)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\varphi(x_{i-1})+\varphi(x_{i})}{2}\bigl[G(x_{i})-G(x_{i-1})\bigr].

As a result, with the preceding convention, we obtain the following discrete approximation of the convolution:

\displaystyle C_{ij}(x_{k})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{r\in E}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{b_{rj}(x_{k}-x_{\ell-1})+b_{rj}(x_{k}-x_{\ell})}{2}\bigl[a_{ir}(x_{\ell})-a_{ir}(x_{\ell-1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in E}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{b_{rj}\bigl(h(k-\ell+1)\bigr)+b_{rj}\bigl(h(k-\ell)\bigr)}{2}\bigl[a_{ir}(h\ell)-a_{ir}(h(\ell-1))\bigr].

For any function
A\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
h>0
, we define its discrete approximation
A_{h}\in\mathcal{R}
by

A_{h}(\ell)=A(h\ell),\quad\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

This enables us to define the matrix sequences
\Delta A
and
B_{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta A_{ir}(\ell)

\displaystyle:=a_{h,ir}(\ell)-a_{h,ir}(\ell-1),\quad\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\qquad\Delta A_{ir}(0):=0,

\displaystyle B_{2,rj}(m)

\displaystyle:=\frac{b_{h,rj}(m+1)+b_{h,rj}(m)}{2},\quad r,j\in E,\;m\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore,
C
can be approximated by the convolution product

C\approx\Delta A*B_{2}.

(16)

In
[
32
]
, a generalized Simpson rule for the computation of the real-valued integral is derived, given by

C_{h}^{S}=\frac{4}{3}C_{h/2}-\frac{1}{3}C_{h}.

(17)

where
C_{h/2}
denotes the mean-value approximation using
\frac{h}{2}
as a discretization step on
[0,t]
, with step size equal to half that of
C_{h}
.

Let
E
denote the deterministic discretization error sequence on the grid, and let
E_{fl,d}
denote the total error sequence after the FFT/IFFT computation of the discrete convolution in (
16
). The following bound separates the discretization error from the floating-point error. The analysis of the deterministic term is deferred to the supplementary material.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A,\,B\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be two matrix-valued functions such that the entries of
A
are of bounded variation and the entries of
B
are continuously differentiable on each compact interval. Then

\|E_{fl,d}\|_{G,N}\leq\|E\|_{G,N}+\sqrt{N}\,\|\Delta A\|_{G,N}\|B_{2}\|_{G,N}\left(3\sigma+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{2})\right).

4
Semi-Markov theory

This section
recalls the semi-Markov equations to which the convolutional methods developed above are
applied.

Consider a random system with finite state space
E=\{1,\dots,s\}
evolving over a general time domain
T=\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, described by a semi-Markov process
Z:=\left(Z_{t}\right)_{t\in T}
. We also assume that the system evolves through jumps on
T
, and that a process
(J_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
records the visited states. The jump times are recorded by the process
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We also define
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as the sequence of sojourn (inter-jump) times, where, by convention,
X_{0}=0
and
X_{n}=S_{n}-S_{n-1}
for all
n\geq 1
, and the counting process of the number of jumps on
T
is given by

N(t):=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S_{n}\leq t\}.

As a result, the semi-Markov process
Z
can be alternatively described by
Z_{t}=J_{N(t)}
,
t\in T
. The process
(J,S)
is called a Markov-renewal process if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
i,j\in E
, and
t\in T
, it satisfies a.s.

\mathbb{P}(J_{n+1}=j,S_{n+1}-S_{n}\leq t\mid J_{0:n},S_{0:n})=\mathbb{P}(J_{n+1}=j,S_{n+1}-S_{n}\leq t\mid J_{n}).

(18)

Besides, if (
18
) is independent of
n
, then
(J,S)
is said to be homogeneous and we set

Q_{ij}(t):=\mathbb{P}(J_{n+1}=j,S_{n+1}-S_{n}\leq t\mid J_{n}=i).

The matrix-valued function
Q(t)
is called the
continuous-time semi-Markov kernel
(ct-smk) when
T=\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and an accumulated semi-Markov kernel when
T=\mathbb{N}
. It satisfies the following two properties:

(i)

Q_{ij}(0)=0,\quad Q_{ij}(t)\in[0,1],\ i,j\in E,\ t\in T
,

(ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{j\in E}Q_{ij}(t)=1,\quad i\in E
.

In the following, each entry of the ct-smk
Q
is assumed to be continuous and of bounded variation on compact intervals. Whenever an error estimate for the Stieltjes approximation is invoked, the smooth term of the corresponding integral is assumed to be continuously differentiable on the intervals under consideration. We now introduce important functions for the conditional distributions given the next visited state.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(J,S)
be a MRP. For any
i,j\in E
and
t\in T
, we introduce the following functions related to the conditional sojourn time distribution of
X_{n+1}
conditional on
J_{n}=i
and
J_{n+1}=j
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

(i)

The transition probability matrix of the embedded Markov chain
J
,
P=(p_{ij})_{i,j\in E}
,

p_{ij}=\lim_{t\to\infty}Q_{ij}(t).

(ii)

For
p_{ij}>0
, the conditional sojourn-time distribution is denoted by
F_{ij}
and satisfies

Q_{ij}(t)=p_{ij}F_{ij}(t),\qquad t\geq 0.

(19)

If
p_{ij}=0
, then
Q_{ij}\equiv 0
.

(iii)

The diagonal matrix-valued function
H:=(H_{i})_{i\in E}\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(T)
whose diagonal entries correspond to the conditional sojourn time distribution in each state:

H_{i}(t):=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\leq t\mid J_{n}=i)=\sum_{j\in E}Q_{ij}(t).

(iv)

The diagonal matrix-valued function
\overline{H}:=(\overline{H}_{i})_{i\in E}\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
whose diagonal entries correspond to the conditional survival function in each state:

\overline{H}_{i}(t)=1-H_{i}(t).

(v)

The vector whose
i
-th component corresponds to the mean sojourn time in state
i
:

m_{i}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}\mid J_{n}=i].

Definition 4.2
.

The transition function of
(Z_{t})_{t\in T}
is denoted by
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(T)
, with entries

P_{ij}(t):=\mathbb{P}(Z_{t}=j\mid Z_{0}=i),\quad i,j\in E,\ t\in T.

We denote by
\alpha
the initial distribution of the semi-Markov process
Z
.
Consider a partition
\{\mathcal{U},\,\mathcal{D}\}
of the state space
E
, where
\mathcal{U}
corresponds to the up-states and
\mathcal{D}
to the down-states. Let
T_{D}
be the random variable corresponding to the first passage time to a down-state. The reliability function is defined as

R(t)=\mathbb{P}(T_{D}>t),\quad t\geq 0.

(20)

The pointwise availability of the system is

A(t)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{t}\in\mathcal{U})=\sum_{i\in E}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}}\alpha_{i}P_{ij}(t),

(21)

where
\alpha
is the initial distribution.

In the following, we consider both semi-Markov chains (
T=\mathbb{N}
) and semi-Markov processes (
T=\mathbb{R}_{+}
).

4.1
Semi-Markov chains

For a discrete-time semi-Markov chain, we define the discrete-time semi-Markov kernel (dt-smk)
q\in\mathcal{R}
by

q_{ij}(k)=\mathbb{P}\big(J_{n+1}=j,\;S_{n+1}-S_{n}=k\mid J_{n}=i\big),\quad i,j\in E,\;k\in\mathbb{N}^{*},

which satisfies the following properties:

(i)

q_{ij}(k)\geq 0
,
i,j\in E
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(ii)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in E}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{ij}(k)=1
,
i\in E
.

Moreover,

p_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q_{ij}(k),\qquad i,j\in E,

so that
q_{ij}(k)=p_{ij}f_{ij}(k)
whenever the conditional sojourn distribution
f_{ij}
is defined.
We set
q_{ij}(0)=0
for all
i,j\in E
.

The sequences of matrices
q,\,Q\in\mathcal{R}
are related through
Q=s_{\mathstrut 0}*q
.
The sequence
e_{\mathstrut 0}-q
is always invertible, since
e_{\mathstrut 0}(0)-q(0)=I_{s}
. Hence, the sequence

\psi:=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-q)^{(-1)}=\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{(n)}

is well-defined. It is called the
sequence of Markov renewal probabilities
and satisfies the discrete-time convolution equation

\psi=e_{\mathstrut 0}+q*\psi,

which is a particular case of the discrete-time Markov renewal equation

M=L+q*M,

(22)

where
L
is a given matrix sequence and
M
is unknown.

The transition sequence of the semi-Markov chain
Z
satisfies the Markov renewal equation

P(k)=\overline{H}(k)+(q*P)(k),\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

and can be computed as
P=\psi*\overline{H}
.

Since
\psi
is defined as the convolutional inverse of
e_{\mathstrut 0}-q
, it can be calculated using the following methods:

(i)

The explicit representation
\sum_{n=0}^{k}q^{(n)}
, which involves computing a finite number of convolution powers.

(ii)

A recursive algorithm.

(iii)

The Gauss–Jordan method.

(iv)

A Newton-based inversion method.

4.2
Semi-Markov processes

The Markov renewal function
\bm{\psi}\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is defined by

\bm{\psi}_{ij}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}Q^{(n)}_{ij}(t),\quad i,j\in E,\ t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

and satisfies the Stieltjes matrix convolution equation

\psi=\mathbb{I}+Q\star\psi,

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity element for the Stieltjes convolution. This equation is a particular case of the Markov renewal equation

\bm{K}(t)=\bm{L}(t)+(Q\star\bm{K})(t),

(23)

where
L
is a given matrix-valued function and
K
is the unknown.

The transition matrix function of the semi-Markov process
Z
satisfies the following Markov renewal equation
[
18
]
:

\bm{P}=\overline{H}+\bm{Q}\star\bm{P},

(24)

and the unique solution is given by
\bm{P}=\bm{\psi}\star\overline{H}
.

In order to compute the matrix-valued function
\bm{P}
, we consider the following two methods:

(i)

A Newton–Cotes method, as described in (
16
).

(ii)

A discretization of step
h
of the semi-Markov process
Z
by a semi-Markov chain
Z_{h}
.

Mean-value approximation and Simpson-type extrapolation

In order to approximate the solution of (
23
), we use (
16
). Let
\Delta Q_{h}(0)=O_{s}
,
\Delta Q_{h}(k)=Q_{h}(k)-Q_{h}(k-1)
for
k\geq 1
, and define the forward increment sequence
\Delta^{+}Q_{h}(k):=\Delta Q_{h}(k+1)
. Set

\overline{Q}_{h}(k):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta Q_{h}(k)+\Delta^{+}Q_{h}(k)\right),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Then the mean-value approximation of
K=L+Q\star K
can be written as

K_{h}=L_{h}+\overline{Q}_{h}*K_{h}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{+}Q_{h}*(K_{h}(0)e_{\mathstrut 0}).

(25)

Since
K_{h}(0)=L_{h}(0)
, (
25
) is equivalent to

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\overline{Q}_{h})*K_{h}=L_{h}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{+}Q_{h}*(L_{h}(0)e_{\mathstrut 0}).

The convolutional inverse exists whenever
I_{s}-\overline{Q}_{h}(0)
is nonsingular. Hence,

K_{h}=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\overline{Q}_{h})^{(-1)}*\left(L_{h}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{+}Q_{h}*(L_{h}(0)e_{\mathstrut 0})\right).

For the Markov renewal function, where
L_{h}=e_{\mathstrut 0}
, this gives the approximation

\widetilde{\psi}_{h}=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\overline{Q}_{h})^{(-1)}*\left(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{+}Q_{h}\right).

Discretization

We approximate a semi-Markov process
\bm{Z}
with ct-smk
\bm{Q}
by a semi-Markov chain
Z_{h}
with discretization step
v>0
and dt-smk
\Delta\bm{Q}_{v}
. This enables us to compute the solution of (
23
) by

\bm{P}_{v}(k)=(\widehat{\psi}_{v}*\overline{H}_{v})(k),

(26)

where
\widehat{\psi}_{v}=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\Delta\bm{Q}_{v})^{(-1)}
.

In
[
31
]
, it is shown that the transition matrix function
\bm{P}
can be approximated efficiently by the sequence
\widetilde{P}_{v}
defined by

\widetilde{P}_{v}(t)=P_{v}(k-1),\quad(k-1)v\leq t<kv.

(27)

Then, the availability function can be approximated by
\widetilde{A}_{v}(t)
, obtained by replacing
\bm{P}
with
\widetilde{P}_{v}
in the definition of
A(t)
.

5.1
Computation of reliability and availability in a semi-Markov chain

The variability of the production output induces randomness in the filling time of the tank, which is therefore modeled as a random variable. Even in the case of deterministic filling dynamics, this can be represented by a Dirac distribution.

6
Discussion

This paper studies the algebraic framework of the convolution product of matrix-valued sequences, with particular emphasis on the convolution inverse, whose role in Markov renewal systems is crucial. Explicit expressions for convolution products are derived, including convolution powers and the convolution inverse. This approach contributes to the solution of Markov renewal equations and advances the current semi-Markov theory and its applications.

However, from a computational perspective, these methods exhibit poor performance and are mainly of theoretical interest, as their direct application requires quadratic-time computations. This motivates the use of FFT/IFFT-based approaches, which lead to a significant reduction in computational complexity, achieving quasi-linear complexity up to logarithmic factors. The floating-point errors remain small in the numerical experiments, whereas the discretization error is controlled by the mesh size. In addition, computing the convolution inverse of a matrix-valued sequence remains challenging, even when FFT/IFFT-based methods are employed. To address this difficulty, we combine Newton-type inversion methods for polynomial matrices or Gauss–Jordan elimination schemes with Cooley–Tukey algorithms, yielding a practical numerical workflow for Markov renewal computations.

Bridging the application perspective with the theoretical framework, several approximations of the Stieltjes convolution product of matrix-valued functions can be reformulated as discrete-time convolution products, thereby enabling flexible and efficient computational approaches. To this end, a mean-value discretization, together with a Simpson-type extrapolation, is employed and adapted to this framework, with particular attention paid to the resulting discretization error.

In addition, the application of the proposed methodology to discrete-time semi-Markov models for the computation of reliability and availability functions demonstrates that the resulting numerical methods significantly accelerate computations, even over large time horizons. This facilitates the analysis of more complex systems or distributions whose forms are difficult to compute.

Finally, the discrete-to-continuous time approximation can be achieved through two approaches:

(i)
the approximation of a semi-Markov process using discrete-time semi-Markov chains, as discussed in
[
31
]
, and

(ii)
the approximation of the matrix Stieltjes convolution product from discrete-time convolution via suitable discretization methods combined with FFT/IFFT-based accelerations. Both approaches are applied to reliability analysis, and their results are found to be in close agreement. Furthermore, they are compared with exact values in the Markov case and with Monte Carlo estimates in the presence of heavy-tailed distributions. The proposed methods are therefore well suited for applications requiring such distributions, including actuarial and insurance science.

Overall, the proposed methods provide an effective and flexible framework for the analysis of complex time-dependent systems, such as those considered in this study. A possible direction for future work is the extension of the present one-dimensional
convolution framework to multidimensional renewal structures, where the notion of time may involve multiple interacting components or scales.

Supplementary Material

This supplementary material contains auxiliary proofs, algorithmic descriptions, error bounds, distributional definitions, parameter values, and additional numerical plots referred to in the main article.
Throughout,
E=\{1,\ldots,s\}
and
\mathcal{M}_{s}=\mathbb{C}^{s\times s}
, as in the main article.

G
Matrix norms

Definition G.1
.

A matrix norm is a function
\|\cdot\|:\mathcal{M}_{s}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
which defines a vector norm on the vector space
\mathcal{M}_{s}
equipped with matrix addition. It is called submultiplicative if it satisfies

\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\,\|B\|,\qquad A,B\in\mathcal{M}_{s}.

The following functions define two submultiplicative matrix norms, namely the row-sum norm and the Frobenius norm,

|A|_{\infty}:=\max_{i}\sum_{j}|A_{ij}|,\qquad\|A\|_{F}:=\left(\sum_{i,j}|A_{ij}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

For matrix-valued sequences, the notation
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
and
\|\cdot\|_{G,N}
is used as in the main text. The submultiplicativity discussed below concerns pointwise matrix multiplication.

Infinity norm

We first consider
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. For subadditivity,

\|A+B\|_{\infty}(n)=\max_{0\leq k\leq n}|A(k)+B(k)|_{\infty}\leq\|A\|_{\infty}(n)+\|B\|_{\infty}(n).

For submultiplicativity with respect to pointwise matrix multiplication,

\|AB\|_{\infty}(n)=\max_{0\leq k\leq n}|A(k)B(k)|_{\infty}\leq\|A\|_{\infty}(n)\,\|B\|_{\infty}(n).

Grid Frobenius norm

The grid Frobenius norm is given by

\|A\|_{G,N}:=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|A(k)\|_{F}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

It coincides with the Euclidean
\ell^{2}
norm of the finite sequence
(\|A(k)\|_{F})_{0\leq k\leq N-1}
. Using the submultiplicativity of the Frobenius norm, we obtain

\|AB\|^{2}_{G,N}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|A(k)B(k)\|_{F}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|A(k)\|_{F}^{2}\|B(k)\|_{F}^{2}.

Consequently,

\|AB\|_{G,N}\leq\|A\|_{G,N}\,\|B\|_{G,N}.

For convolution products one uses a Young-type estimate instead. For example, if

\|A\|_{1,N}:=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|A(k)\|_{F},

then

\|A*B\|_{G,N}\leq\|A\|_{1,N}\,\|B\|_{G,N},

for the corresponding truncated convolution, up to the chosen truncation order.

H
Recursive computing of the convolution inverse

Proposition H.1
.

Let
A
be a convolutionally invertible matrix-valued sequence. Then its convolution inverse
A^{(-1)}
can be computed recursively by

A^{(-1)}(k)=\begin{cases}A(0)^{-1},&\textrm{if }k=0,\\[6.0pt]
-\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}A^{(-1)}(l)A(k-l)A(0)^{-1},&\textrm{if }k\geq 1.\end{cases}

Proof.

The recursion follows from the identity
A^{(-1)}*A=e_{\mathstrut 0}
. For
k=0
, this gives

A^{(-1)}(0)A(0)=I_{s},

and hence
A^{(-1)}(0)=A(0)^{-1}
. For
k\geq 1
, the coefficient of order
k
in
A^{(-1)}*A=e_{\mathstrut 0}
is

\sum_{l=0}^{k}A^{(-1)}(l)A(k-l)=O_{s}.

Solving for
A^{(-1)}(k)
gives

A^{(-1)}(k)=-\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}A^{(-1)}(l)A(k-l)A(0)^{-1}.

∎

I
Fast Fourier transform

This appendix summarizes the Fourier-transform identities used to accelerate convolutions. We distinguish between the discrete-time Fourier transform of an infinite sequence and the finite discrete Fourier transform computed by the FFT. Standard references include
[
8
,
27
]
.

I.1
Notation

Let
\mathrm{i}:=\sqrt{-1}
. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, define

I_{N}:=\{0,\ldots,N-1\}.

I.2
DTFT and convolution

Let
a:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
be absolutely summable. Its discrete-time Fourier transform is the
2\pi
-periodic function

\widehat{a}(\omega):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a(k)e^{-\mathrm{i}k\omega},\qquad\omega\in[-\pi,\pi].

The inversion formula holds in the
\ell^{1}
setting as

a(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\widehat{a}(\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}k\omega}\,d\omega,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

If
c=a*b
denotes the linear convolution on
\mathbb{Z}
,

(a*b)(k):=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}a(l)b(k-l),

then

\widehat{a*b}(\omega)=\widehat{a}(\omega)\widehat{b}(\omega).

I.3
Finite DFT and circular convolution

For numerical computation we work with finite arrays supported on
I_{N}
. Let
a:I_{N}\to\mathbb{C}
. Its DFT is defined by

\widehat{a}(m):=\sum_{k\in I_{N}}a(k)\exp\left\{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\frac{km}{N}\right\},\qquad m\in I_{N}.

The inverse DFT is

a(k)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m\in I_{N}}\widehat{a}(m)\exp\left\{2\pi\mathrm{i}\frac{km}{N}\right\},\qquad k\in I_{N}.

We define circular convolution on
I_{N}
as follows.

Definition I.1
.

For
a,b:I_{N}\to\mathbb{C}
, their circular convolution
a*_{N}b:I_{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is

(a*_{N}b)(k):=\sum_{l\in I_{N}}a(l)b\big((k-l)\bmod N\big),\qquad k\in I_{N}.

With these definitions,

\widehat{a*_{N}b}(m)=\widehat{a}(m)\widehat{b}(m),\qquad m\in I_{N}.

Therefore,
a*_{N}b
can be computed by two forward FFTs, pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain, and one inverse FFT.

I.4
Linear convolution via zero-padding

Let
a
and
b
be finite sequences supported on
\{0,\ldots,N_{a}-1\}
and
\{0,\ldots,N_{b}-1\}
, respectively. To compute their linear convolution on the full support
\{0,\ldots,N_{a}+N_{b}-2\}
, choose a padding length
L
satisfying

L\geq N_{a}+N_{b}-1.

Embedding both sequences into
I_{L}
by zero-padding, the circular convolution on
I_{L}
coincides with the linear convolution on the non-aliased index range.

I.5
Complexity

Let
L
be the FFT grid size. A scalar FFT has computational complexity

\mathcal{O}(L\log L).

For matrix-valued sequences with entries in
\mathcal{M}_{s}
, the DFT is applied entrywise and the product in the frequency domain is a matrix product. Therefore, the cost consists of
s^{2}
scalar FFTs,
s^{2}
scalar inverse FFTs, and
L
matrix multiplications of size
s\times s
. Thus the computational complexity is

\mathcal{O}(s^{2}L\log L+s^{3}L).

For fixed
s
, this is quasi-linear in the truncation length
L
.

J
FFT error bounds

J.1
Relative error of the pointwise matrix product

Proof.

Let
\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}
and
\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{fl}}
denote the computed transforms. We write

\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}=\widehat{A}+E_{A},\qquad\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{fl}}=\widehat{B}+E_{B},

where

\|E_{A}\|_{G,N}\leq\sigma\|\widehat{A}\|_{G,N},\qquad\|E_{B}\|_{G,N}\leq\sigma\|\widehat{B}\|_{G,N}.

Then

\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{fl}}=\widehat{A}\widehat{B}+\widehat{A}E_{B}+E_{A}\widehat{B}+E_{A}E_{B}.

By subadditivity and submultiplicativity of the grid Frobenius norm with respect to pointwise matrix multiplication,

\|\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{fl}}-\widehat{A}\widehat{B}\|_{G,N}\leq\|\widehat{A}\|_{G,N}\|\widehat{B}\|_{G,N}(2\sigma+\sigma^{2}).

∎

J.2
FFT convolution error corollary

Proof.

Let
C=A*B
be the exact linear convolution computed after zero-padding to length
N
, and let
\widehat{C}_{\mathrm{fl}}
denote the sequence obtained by FFT multiplication and inverse FFT in floating-point arithmetic. By Parseval’s identity,

\|\widehat{A}\|_{G,N}=\sqrt{N}\|A\|_{G,N},\qquad\|\widehat{B}\|_{G,N}=\sqrt{N}\|B\|_{G,N}.

The frequency-domain product error satisfies

\|\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{fl}}-\widehat{A}\widehat{B}\|_{G,N}\leq N\|A\|_{G,N}\|B\|_{G,N}(2\sigma+\sigma^{2}).

After applying the inverse DFT and accounting for the inverse FFT error, the aggregate FFT/IFFT error coefficient used in the main article gives

\|C-\widehat{C}_{\mathrm{fl}}\|_{G,N}\leq\sqrt{N}\|A\|_{G,N}\|B\|_{G,N}\left(3\sigma+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{2})\right).

∎

K
Newton’s inversion method: proof of the update

We work in the non-commutative ring of matrix formal power series
\mathcal{M}_{s}[[x]]
and denote by

\mathfrak{m}:=\langle x\rangle

the two-sided ideal of series with vanishing constant term. Recall that
P_{a}
is invertible in
\mathcal{M}_{s}[[x]]
if and only if its constant term
P_{a}(0)
is non-singular.

Assume that at iteration level
N
we have a truncated approximation
P_{b,N}
satisfying

P_{a}P_{b,N}=I_{s}\quad\bmod\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N}}.

(29)

Equivalently, the error term

e_{N}:=I_{s}-P_{a}P_{b,N}

(30)

belongs to
\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N}}
. The proof is written for the right-inverse convention
P_{a}P_{b,N}=I_{s}
. The corresponding left-inverse update is obtained by replacing the iteration with
P_{b,N+1}=(2I_{s}-P_{b,N}P_{a})P_{b,N}
.

Define the Newton update

P_{b,N+1}:=P_{b,N}+P_{b,N}e_{N}=P_{b,N}(2I_{s}-P_{a}P_{b,N})\quad\bmod\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N+1}}.

(31)

The updated approximation satisfies

P_{a}P_{b,N+1}=I_{s}\quad\bmod\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N+1}}.

Proof.

Starting from (
31
) and using (
30
),

\displaystyle P_{a}P_{b,N+1}

\displaystyle=P_{a}(P_{b,N}+P_{b,N}e_{N})

\displaystyle=P_{a}P_{b,N}+(P_{a}P_{b,N})e_{N}

\displaystyle=(I_{s}-e_{N})+(I_{s}-e_{N})e_{N}

\displaystyle=I_{s}-e_{N}^{2}.

Since
e_{N}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N}}
and
\mathfrak{m}^{r}\mathfrak{m}^{s}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{r+s}
, it follows that

e_{N}^{2}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N}}\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N}}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N+1}}.

Thus
P_{a}P_{b,N+1}=I_{s}\bmod\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N+1}}
.
∎

The congruence
P_{a}P_{b,N}=I_{s}\bmod\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N}}
means that
P_{b,N}
coincides with the exact inverse
P_{a}^{-1}
in all coefficients of monomials
x^{k}
with degree
k<2^{N}
. Thus each Newton step doubles the truncation order.

L
Gauss–Jordan method assumptions

The Gauss–Jordan elimination used in the numerical implementation is performed over the truncated power-series ring

\mathbb{C}[[x]]/\langle x^{M}\rangle.

All products and inverses are understood modulo
x^{M}
. A scalar pivot is admissible whenever its constant coefficient is nonzero, since then it is invertible in the truncated power-series ring. Row permutations are allowed when needed in order to select an admissible pivot. Under the assumption that
A(0)
is nonsingular, the associated polynomial matrix is invertible modulo
x^{M}
, for every truncation order
M
.

M
Pseudocodes of the algorithms

M.1
FFT-based convolution product

For the linear convolution product
C=A*B
, choose an FFT length
L
such that
L\geq N_{A}+N_{B}-1
, where
N_{A}
and
N_{B}
are the lengths of
A
and
B
. The sequences are embedded in
I_{L}
by zero-padding.

1.

Compute the discrete Fourier transforms of the zero-padded sequences
A
and
B
.

2.

For each
m\in I_{L}
, compute

\widehat{C}(m)=\widehat{A}(m)\widehat{B}(m).

3.

Compute the inverse FFT of
\widehat{C}
componentwise.

4.

Restrict the resulting sequence to the non-aliased range
0,\ldots,N_{A}+N_{B}-2
.

M.2
Newton inversion

For the convolution inverse of an invertible matrix-valued sequence
A
truncated at length
M
, proceed as follows.

1.

Compute the associated polynomial matrix
P_{A}(x)
modulo
x^{M}
.

2.

Check whether
A(0)
is nonsingular. If
A(0)
is singular, the sequence is not convolutionally invertible.

3.

Set

P_{B}=A(0)^{-1},\qquad n=1.

4.

While
n<M
, replace
n
by
2n
and compute

P_{B}\leftarrow P_{B}+P_{B}(I_{s}-P_{A}P_{B})\mod x^{n},

using FFT-based polynomial products.

5.

Return
P_{B}
modulo
x^{M}
.

M.3
Gauss–Jordan elimination

For the convolution inverse of an invertible matrix-valued sequence
A
truncated at order
M
, proceed as follows.

1.

Form the associated matrix polynomial
P_{A}(x)
modulo
x^{M}
.

2.

Check whether
A(0)
is nonsingular. If
A(0)
is singular, the sequence is not convolutionally invertible.

3.

Perform Gauss–Jordan elimination on
P_{A}(x)
over
\mathbb{C}[[x]]/\langle x^{M}\rangle
, using only pivots with nonzero constant coefficient.

4.

The resulting matrix polynomial is
P_{A}(x)^{-1}
modulo
x^{M}
. Its coefficients give the convolutional inverse of
A
up to order
M-1
.

N
Error bound for the Stieltjes approximation

In
[
32
]
, the truncation error bound for the scalar Stieltjes approximation is obtained under differentiability of the smooth term and monotonicity of the integrator. In the notation of the main text, the Stieltjes convolution is written as

[A\star B]_{ij}(t)=\sum_{r\in E}\int_{[0,t]}B_{rj}(t-x)\,dA_{ir}(x).

Thus
A
is the integrator and
B
is the smooth term in the mean-value approximation.

Proposition N.1
.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{F}_{s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Assume that the entries of
A
are nondecreasing and that the entries of
B
are continuously differentiable on each compact interval. Let
E_{ij}(t_{m})
denote the deterministic discretization error associated with the approximation of
[A\star B]_{ij}(t_{m})
. Then

|E_{ij}(t_{m})|\leq 2h\sum_{r\in E}\sup_{x\in[0,t_{m}]}|b^{\prime}_{rj}(x)|\bigl(a_{ir}(t_{m})-a_{ir}(0)\bigr).

Consequently, if

M_{B}(t_{m})=\max_{r,j}\sup_{x\in[0,t_{m}]}|b^{\prime}_{rj}(x)|,\qquad V_{A}(t_{m})=\max_{i,r}\bigl(a_{ir}(t_{m})-a_{ir}(0)\bigr),

then

\max_{i,j}|E_{ij}(t_{m})|\leq 2hsM_{B}(t_{m})V_{A}(t_{m}).

Moreover,

\|E(t_{m})\|_{F}\leq 2hs^{2}M_{B}(t_{m})V_{A}(t_{m}).

Proof.

For fixed
i,j
and
r\in E
, set
\varphi(x)=B_{rj}(t_{m}-x)
. On each interval
[x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell}]
, the mean value theorem gives

\left|\varphi(x)-\frac{\varphi(x_{\ell-1})+\varphi(x_{\ell})}{2}\right|\leq 2h\sup_{y\in[0,t_{m}]}|b^{\prime}_{rj}(y)|,\qquad x\in[x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell}].

Integrating this inequality with respect to the nondecreasing function
A_{ir}
and summing over
\ell
and
r
yields

|E_{ij}(t_{m})|\leq 2h\sum_{r\in E}\sup_{x\in[0,t_{m}]}|b^{\prime}_{rj}(x)|\bigl(a_{ir}(t_{m})-a_{ir}(0)\bigr).

The estimates in maximum norm and Frobenius norm follow from the definitions of
M_{B}(t_{m})
and
V_{A}(t_{m})
.
∎

O
Heavy-tailed distributions

The following definition is used for the nonnegative distributions appearing in the numerical study.

Definition O.1
.

A nonnegative distribution
F
is said to be heavy-tailed if its moment generating function is infinite for every positive argument, namely

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{tx}\,dF(x)=\infty,\qquad t>0.

For the heavy-tailed families considered below, the survival function decreases more slowly than any exponential rate.

Definition P.1
.

Following
