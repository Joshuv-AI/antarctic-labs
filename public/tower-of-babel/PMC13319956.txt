Deployment pathways for long-duration energy storage
Authors: Todd Levin, W Neal Mann, Jonghwan Kwon, Zhi Zhou, Bo Cheng, Audun Botterud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe apply a least-cost generation expansion model of the continental United States to assess how optimal investments in long-duration energy storage (LDES) technologies are impacted by changes in system generation portfolios and technology costs, assessing 369 capacity expansion scenarios in total. The expansion model considers 8,760 h of chronological operations for the entire target year, 2040. We find that low-cost LDES technologies can reduce generation investments and system costs. Specifically, once the costs for 24- and 100-h storage reach $38/kWh and $14/kWh, respectively, substantial deployments are observed. The distribution of storage investments across durations is strongly influenced by the system generation portfolio. We also demonstrate that a high-fidelity temporal representation is required to capture the value of LDES in generation expansion. Finally, we conduct a regression analysis of our capacity expansion results and find that LDES deployments are positively correlated with the combined wind and solar capacity share and negatively correlated with peaking and baseload shares.Subject areas: energy systems, energy storage, economics

Introduction

Energy storage is playing an increasingly important role in electricity systems by providing a range of services to balance the volatility and uncertainty of weather-dependent wind and solar generation, while also supporting system reliability and resilience more generally as a highly flexible grid asset. In addition to the energy storage systems that are currently being deployed, which today largely consist of lithium-ion batteries with 1–4 h of storage duration, a number of long-duration energy storage (LDES) technologies (defined by the US Department of Energy [DOE] as energy storage resources with 10-h storage capacity or greater) are emerging as potential candidates having an important role in future electricity systems; example technologies are pumped storage hydropower (PSH), compressed air energy storage (CAES), thermal energy storage systems (TESSs), standalone hydrogen energy storage systems (HESSs), iron-air energy storage, flow batteries, and a wide variety of other chemistries and technologies. Yet, significant uncertainty remains regarding the conditions under which LDES will provide the most system value as well as the cost declines that are required before LDES is deployed economically at scale.

Historically, energy storage in the electricity sector has been dominated by PSH, and this is still largely the case; in 2023, PSH accounted for more than 95% of all energy storage capacity globally.1 However, rapid cost declines have led to significant increases in battery energy storage deployments over the last several years. In the US, battery energy storage capacity increased from almost zero in 2011 to 1.1 GWh in 2020, before growing substantially to 19.3 GWh in 2023 and then more than tripling to 71.3 GWh in 2024.2 The installed power capacity of battery energy storage in the US now exceeds 25 GW, which is more than the installed power capacity of PSH. Many more projects are already under development, and this rapid growth is projected to continue over the next decade and beyond.

In recent years, a number of studies have analyzed different future pathways for electricity systems in the US and elsewhere, assessing the role of different technologies under various portfolio, cost, and policy scenarios. Sepulveda et al. evaluated nearly 1,000 expansion scenarios with different policy targets and found that the availability of firm low-carbon technologies, which reliably provide energy across all seasons and for sustained periods, can reduce system costs by 10%–62%. The authors highlighted that least-cost capacity expansion plans are highly sensitive to uncertainty in cost assumptions.3 Denholm et al. identified several different least-cost future pathways for the US electricity system but noted that there may be significant economic, regulatory, market, and policy barriers that can prevent the realization of modeled least-cost pathways.4 A review of 40 studies that examined pathways in different countries and regions with varying policy targets showed both the potential role of variable renewable energy (VRE) resources and the challenges that arise as VRE shares increase.5 The study specifically concluded that either low-carbon firm technologies or energy storage are needed to reduce costs in such scenarios. Other work has asserted that expanded transmission infrastructure is needed to efficiently modernize the electric sector,6 that power-to-gas technologies such as hydrogen electrolysis can play an important role in 100% renewable systems,7 and that dispatchable low- or zero-carbon thermal plants are crucial to achieving net-zero emissions in the power sector.8

Several studies have explicitly analyzed the role of energy storage in future electricity systems with significant contributions from weather-dependent resources. As an early example, de Sisternes et al. found that energy storage is likely to play an important role in future systems with high VRE penetrations but may not be critical under a more diverse generation mix.9 Another study demonstrated how storage can reduce curtailments in systems with high VRE penetrations.10

Other studies have specifically examined the role of LDES in future electricity systems. Dowling et al. found that LDES lowers system costs in low-carbon systems.11 Schill found broad consensus that storage needs increase substantially once renewables account for an 80% share of system generation and that longer durations become increasingly important for seasonal balancing as the renewable share approaches 100%.12 With a focus on Germany, their work further demonstrated that the amount of storage needed and the corresponding distribution across durations depend heavily on the cost and availability of different technologies, as well as other sources of flexibility. Freeman and Agar found that a combination of different LDES technologies is needed to cost effectively maintain reliability and affordability in New England across 44 different weather scenarios.13 Kim et al. demonstrated that storage up to 4 h in duration increases solar value and storage up to 8 h increases wind value but found little evidence for the value of longer storage durations in hybrid configurations.14 Han et al. discussed the status and outlook for LDES in China,15 and Ashfaq et al. quantified the system cost reduction that results from an increase in energy storage duration from 1 to 10 h in California.16

A final set of studies focused more narrowly on identifying LDES cost targets that enable substantial future deployment. Ziegler et al. estimated that storage costs must reach $20/kWh to enable electricity systems to cost competitively serve 100% of demand with renewables, but this cost target increases to $150/kWh if only 95% is served with renewables.17 Albertus et al. reviewed several potential applications for LDES and identified all-in capital cost targets that enable financial viability for LDES technologies with durations from 10 h ($40/kWh) to 100 h ($3/kWh).18 Sepulveda et al. found that energy capacity costs must be very low (less than $1/kWh) in order to fully displace firm low-carbon technologies.19 Jenkins and Sepulveda have asserted that “ultra-low” energy storage costs, in the $1/kWh–$/10 kWh range, must be achieved in order to reduce system costs by 10% or more; to this end, they suggest that research must focus on enabling storage technologies with durations of 100 h or more.20 McPherson et al. similarly found that cost assumptions for energy storage must be “optimistic” in order to achieve large-scale deployment.21 Silva et al. determined a price boundary curve that establishes the maximum cost at which a given quantity of 100-h storage can be deployed to cost effectively decarbonize California’s electricity system by 2050; the curve achieves its maximum value at 17 GW of deployment and a cost of $513/kW ($5.13/kWh).22 Chu et al. found that short-duration storage provides more value for renewable integration than LDES, unless LDES costs are $10/kWh or less.23 Staadecker et al. found that LDES is particularly valuable in wind-heavy regions with limited hydropower and that seasonal storage becomes feasible only when energy capacity costs are $5/kWh or less.24 Finally, a study conducted by the US DOE projects that LDES capital costs will need to be reduced by 45%–55% by 2030 in order to compete against storage technologies with shorter duration that are viable today.25 Notably, these studies collectively offer a broad range of cost targets that vary across contexts and applications.

From an analysis perspective, it has been suggested that capacity expansion models must evolve to properly reflect the value of LDES, particularly by increasing spatiotemporal resolution to capture operational dynamics over longer time horizons. Levin et al. reviewed key challenges related to representing energy storage in long-term capacity expansion modeling and provided a series of recommendations to properly capture the role of energy storage in supporting grid modernization objectives.26 Bistline et al. reviewed national-scale models and provided recommendations for representing storage while balancing tradeoffs, highlighting the importance of capturing high spatiotemporal resolution.27 Similarly, Bistline highlighted that temporal aggregation methods can lead to an underestimation of storage value.28 Gonzato et al. compared temporal aggregation methods that are intended to capture the value of LDES and proposed two preferred approaches.29 An additional study also examined how different spatial and temporal resolutions impact the value of LDES in least-cost power system planning models, establishing that linked representative periods are crucial and also that enhancing temporal resolution is more impactful than enhancing spatial resolution.30

This paper advances the existing literature by analyzing 360 different capacity expansion scenarios to (1) identify the role of LDES of different durations in different future generation portfolios, (2) shed additional light on future cost reductions that are needed for LDES to emerge as a major supporting resource across a wide range of US electricity system pathways, (3) quantify system cost reductions that can be expected if these technology cost targets are achieved, and (4) establish system parameters that closely correlate with LDES deployment across different durations.

In contrast to many other national-scale capacity expansion studies, our analysis prioritizes temporal representation over spatial granularity, considering all 8,760 hourly periods in a future target year. This comprehensive modeling framework enables us to analyze LDES deployment in the US in detail across 180 core LDES cost and generation portfolio pathway scenarios, 90 additional scenarios that how the availability of a low-cost, 4-h storage technology impacts LDES investments, and a further 90 scenarios that explore the implications of considering an LDES investment option with the model-optimized duration. Finally, we also consider three additional scenarios that assess the impacts of utilizing different spatial representations and six more that similarly assess the impacts of utilizing different temporal representations; in total, 369 capacity expansion scenarios are analyzed. We then conduct a rigorous regression analysis across our core LDES cost scenarios and generation pathways to establish correlations between several key system parameters and deployment levels of different LDES technologies. Our goal is, therefore, not to identify a single “optimal” LDES deployment pathway, but rather to broadly assess how the role of LDES changes as the technology costs and generation portfolios evolve.

Results

We first establish how adjusting the temporal and spatial representations in the expansion problem impacts both the quantity and duration of storage resources that are deployed. Specifically, we find that LDES deployment is more limited when shorter representative periods are utilized. We draw upon these results and elect to represent our target year 2040 with a single 365-day period for the remainder of our analysis. We then define five system technology pathways (base, hydrogen, firm, nuclear, and VRE) by adjusting technology costs and which generation technology options are available for expansion; apply six assumptions for the cost and availability of LDES (from no LDES to “breakthrough” costs); and impose different levels of carbon dioxide emission constraints on the system (from unconstrained to full decarbonization). Detailed assumptions are provided in STAR Methods.

Multi-day representative periods are crucial for capturing LDES value

Previous work has suggested that time-series aggregation in electricity system models can lead to suboptimal under-deployment of LDES.31 We, therefore, first assess how storage deployment in the “base” generation technology scenario varies across seven different temporal representations. In five of these cases, a backward scenario reduction algorithm32 is applied to reduce the target year into a set of representative periods; these five cases include (a) 56 one-day periods, (b) 8 seven-day periods, (c) four 14-day periods, (d) 52 seven-day periods, and (e) 26 14-day periods. In all cases, these periods are not temporally linked. We also consider two cases that capture the full year without scenario reduction, one with 365 discrete one-day periods, and a final case with all 8,760 hourly periods represented chronologically in a single 365-day period. The “breakthrough” LDES cost and 100% emission reduction assumptions are selected for this initial analysis as they result in the most LDES deployment, and, therefore, the impacts of temporal representation are most evident.

Figure 1 shows that the choice of temporal representation has a large impact on expansion results. LDES deployment is limited in the two scenarios that consider periods with single representative days. In each case, some 10-h storage is deployed to complement 4-h storage, but no 24- or 100-h storage is developed. In the two scenarios with 7-day periods, 24-h storage is deployed instead of 10-h storage, but no 100-h storage is deployed. The two scenarios with 14-day periods do deploy some 100-h storage in addition to other durations, but the 365-day scenario by far deploys the most 100-h storage. In general, expansion decisions are more impacted by the length of each representative period than by the total number of days that are captured. These results suggest that higher-fidelity temporal representations with longer representative periods are required to capture the full system value that LDES can provide. As noted above, we do not consider temporal linking between our representative periods in this sensitivity analysis, which is an alternative approach that can be considered to improve the fidelity of reduced temporal representation for LDES applications.33,34 We instead elect to prioritize high-fidelity temporal representation and consider a single 365-day period for the remainder of our analysis.

More storage capacity is developed in system representations with greater spatial granularity

We additionally consider a sensitivity analysis to assess how storage investments change as spatial resolution of the model increases. In each case, the system is represented by a certain number of load and resource zones and considers appropriate transmission capacities between each pair of neighboring zones. In addition to our reference system representation that captures the three major US interconnections (used in Figure 1), we also consider (1) a scenario with 18 zones that represent the seven US independent system operators and regional transmission Operators, as well as 11 additional large regional zones, (2) a system with 48 zones, one for each state in the continental US, and 3) a system with 134 zones or balancing authorities following the convention established by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS).35 Similar to the temporal sensitivity analysis, we utilize the “base” generation portfolio with “breakthrough” LDES costs, and 100% emission reduction for all of these spatial sensitivity scenarios. To maintain computational tractability for the high-resolution scenarios, four representative 14-day periods are considered in each case.

Figure 2 shows how storage deployments change with the increasing spatial resolution, the clearest trend being increasing investment in new storage power capacity in successively smaller zones; alternatively, the trend in energy capacity is less monotonic. In particular, when transitioning from ISO-level to state-level representation, LDES deployments shift from 100-h storage to 10- and 24-h storage to meet power capacity needs at lower cost. Deployment of 100-h storage increases again in the scenario with balancing authority-level representation. Notably, we do not model potential transmission capacity expansion due to computational challenges introduced with the higher-fidelity representations. Permitting transmission expansion would likely decrease storage investments to some extent as energy storage and transmission infrastructure can provide similar services and serve as technology substitutes.36,37,38 The remainder of our analysis prioritizes high-temporal resolution and, therefore, utilizes spatial representation with three interconnections to maintain tractability while optimizing over a single 365-day period. However, this sensitivity analysis provides important insights into how model representation may impact the identified least-cost generation and storage portfolio.

Storage investments generally increase as emission levels decrease; however, the distribution of storage capacity across durations is impacted by both the system portfolio and the LDES cost assumptions. LDES expansion is limited under mid-cost LDES scenarios to just some 24-h storage capacity entering the system in the V-100-M scenario. Under low-cost LDES assumptions, some LDES is deployed in the V-90-L and V-95-L scenarios, and some LDES deployment is also observed across all five X-100-L scenarios. LDES deployment continues to expand across scenarios with lower emissions reduction targets under the “advanced-cost” LDES scenarios. Finally, when LDES costs achieve “breakthrough” cost reductions, LDES technologies completely replace 4-h storage, and deployments generally shift toward longer durations as the emission reduction targets become more stringent. More detailed capacity expansion results for other generation resources are summarized in supplemental information. Total energy storage power capacity is fairly consistent within each generation technology scenario for emission reductions up to 95% regardless of cost assumptions; however, the distribution of this capacity across storage durations does shift toward longer durations as the emission target becomes more stringent. The “nuclear” and “firm” scenarios consistently require less new storage capacity than the other generation technology scenarios with the same emission reduction targets. This is partially because new nuclear resources provide substantial baseload energy due to their low fuel cost and are also assumed to have the ability to operate more flexibly than legacy nuclear resources.39 The VRE scenarios generally have more total storage capacity and also relatively more 24- and 100-h resources.

Differences across generation technology scenarios manifest most clearly in the 100% emission-free scenarios. The V-100-L, V-100-A, and V-100-B scenarios develop substantial 100-h storage capacity that is complemented by 24- and 4-h storage but limited 10-h storage. The B-100-L, B-100-A, and B-100-B scenarios follow a similar trend but with less total storage and, in particular, less 100-h storage; this is also generally the case to an even greater extent with the corresponding “nuclear” scenarios. LDES capacity is not developed in the H-100-L scenario, but the H-100-A and H-100-B scenarios both replace increasing quantities of 4-h storage with 24-h storage, however no 100-h storage is developed. This is likely due to the availability of green hydrogen combustion turbines (GHCTs) and combined cycle (GHCC) resources to provide clean, dispatchable energy with low capital costs, which obviates the need for longer-duration storage. Finally, the F-100-X scenarios develop the least LDES capacity overall. In these scenarios, nuclear resources provide low-cost baseload generation, while GHCT resources adjust their generation levels to follow system load during peak demand periods and also support system capacity needs. Some storage capacity is retained to help move low-cost nuclear generation through time and reduce the need for costlier GHCT dispatch.

Figure 4 provides a high-level perspective of these storage expansion pathways by plotting the average storage duration as a function of total storage energy capacity for three LDES cost scenarios (“low,” “advanced,” and “breakthrough”). In general, both the capacity and average duration of energy storage resources tend to increase as emission reduction targets become more stringent. These trends are observed clearly for the “base” and VRE generation technology scenarios; however, there are some exceptions and key differences across other scenarios.

One notable exception is for the “hydrogen” and “firm” scenarios, where total storage decreases or remains fairly consistent within each LDES cost scenario as the emission reduction target increases up to 95%. This is because dispatchable, emission-free GHCT and GHCC capacity is deployed in greater quantity, mitigating the need for LDES to provide similar services. In each cost scenario, total storage capacity then increases sharply when transitioning from 95% to 100% emission reductions, while the average duration decreases moderately. A similar trend is evident in the “nuclear” scenarios. In these scenarios, nuclear resources provide a large majority of system generation, while a small amount of emitting natural gas capacity is retained to help balance net load volatility and serve peak demand, mitigating the need for new storage. However, storage deployment increases substantially in terms of both total capacity and average duration in the 100% emission reduction scenarios, as all emitting gas capacity is retired. Storage resources are, therefore, deployed to replace this gas capacity and support load following and peak capacity requirements. Storage deployments are most pronounced in the VRE scenarios, with consistent increases in both total capacity and average duration as emission constraints become more stringent.

LDES exhibits a strong substitution effect with low-cost, shorter-duration storage

Our primary scenarios all assume the same capital cost profile for the candidate 4-h lithium-ion storage technology. In order to assess the potential impact of declines in shorter-duration storage costs in parallel with LDES cost declines, we also analyze a set of scenarios that consider a 50% reduction in the capital cost of a 4-h lithium-ion energy storage technology, i.e., a reduction in total capital cost from $208/kWh to $104/kWh (Table 1). Figure 5 shows the energy storage investments that result across the 90 scenarios with non-trivial LDES investment (“breakthrough,” “advanced,” and “low” LDES costs). Investment in 4-h storage increases substantially, entirely replacing investments in new 10-h storage capacity across all scenarios. The change is most apparent across the “breakthrough” LDES capital cost scenarios, as the lower-cost 4-h storage replaces almost all LDES capacity, except in a handful of scenarios with high emission reductions. These results suggest that there is a strong substitution effect, as shorter-duration energy storage technologies can be combined to effectively operate as a longer-duration technology. For example, three 4-h storage resources can be charged and discharged in series to mirror the operations of a single 12-h storage resource. Longer-duration technologies may be preferred when non-linear cost scaling results in favorable economics, but parallel declines in the cost of shorter-duration technologies can erode that competitive advantage. Yet, these results also indicate that there are scenarios and grid portfolios that benefit from 24-h and 100-h LDES resources, particularly those with high VRE shares, even when lithium-ion technology costs decline well below the projected levels.

Multiple LDES investment options are needed to capture cost profiles

We further assess an additional LDES investment sensitivity where, instead of three distinct LDES technologies with a fixed duration, 10 h, 24 h, and 100 h, we model a single LDES technology with a duration that is optimized endogenously by the optimization model based on distinct capital costs for the power component and the energy component of the storage system.40 This technology is permitted to assume any duration between 10 and 100 h and has power and energy component costs that are equivalent to our corresponding assumptions for the 24-h technology in each respective LDES cost scenario. Figure 6 demonstrates how this change impacts system average storage duration for the 90 scenarios with non-trivial LDES investment (“breakthrough,” “advanced,” and “low” LDES costs). In the fixed-duration scenarios that are dominated by 10-h LDES investment, the system average storage duration in the corresponding “optimal-duration” scenario is generally comparable or slightly higher. However, in fixed-duration scenarios with investments in 24- and 100-h LDES, the optimal duration approach consistently results in a lower system average storage duration. This is because the storage costs scale non-linearly with duration (Table 1); therefore, assuming constant power and energy component costs across a wide range of durations will generally overestimate the total cost of longer-duration technologies. Furthermore, there may be benefits to having multiple discrete LDES technologies of different durations that can be operated differently, rather than a single uniform technology. One potential alternative modeling approach would be to consider multiple different technologies, each with a narrower range of optimized durations and individual cost characterizations, e.g., 10–20 h, 20–30 h, 30–40 h, etc.; we leave such assessment to future work.

LDES operational patterns depend on generation portfolios

Figure 7 shows the total energy generation provided by each storage technology across the five X-100-B scenarios; these results are normalized by dividing the weekly energy output of each technology by its total energy storage capacity to provide an indication of how frequently each technology is cycling. In the B-100-B and H-100-B scenarios, 10-h storage is operated primarily on a daily cycle, charging and discharging more or less consistently throughout the year. In contrast, in the N-100-B scenario, 10-h storage is primarily utilized during the summer, when the demand is high, to help serve peak load on a daily basis. The 24- and 100-h resources in the N-100-B scenario operate in a similar fashion, remaining unused in the first half of the year but cycling more frequently during the summer, thereby deferring the need for additional capital-intensive nuclear investments to meet peak capacity requirements. 10-h storage in the F-100-B and V-100-B scenarios also exhibits increased utilization during the summer but continues to cycle consistently throughout the remainder of the year as well. Collectively, these results demonstrate that optimal operation of LDES depends on the overall system generation portfolio.

LDES can provide billions in annual cost savings but only under low-cost technology trajectories

Figure 8 (top) illustrates that the marginal cost of emission abatement increases exponentially as the emission reduction target approaches 100%, an effect that has been identified in several other studies.41,42,43 Another study utilized a different modeling approach to considering fixed technology cost scenarios, instead of implementing probabilistic technology cost forecasting and endogenizing technology learning into an integrated assessment model, demonstrating that capturing technology learning can dramatically reduce the modeled renewable deployment costs.44 This exponential cost trend is observed for all generation portfolio scenarios but is less pronounced in the “hydrogen,” “firm,” and “nuclear” scenarios. Figure 8 (bottom) also shows the system cost reduction that LDES enables in each case. This additional value is low in the mid-cost LDES scenarios as little or no LDES is developed. Total annual cost reductions are more significant in the B-100-L (1.9%, $2.5 billion) and N-100-L (2.1%, $2.5 billion) scenarios. In the V-100-L scenario, the presence of LDES reduces the annual system costs by as much as 15.7% ($19.9 billion). The estimated LDES benefits become more significant, also at lower emission reduction levels, in the “breakthrough” cost LDES scenarios. In the extreme case of the VRE-100-B scenario, including LDES as an expansion option reduces the annual system costs by 29.4% or $33.3 billion per year. These findings demonstrate the importance of including LDES as a capacity expansion option in long-term capacity expansion analyses, particularly those that consider systems with substantial emissions reductions.

LDES deployment is positively correlated with VRE capacity share

Finally, we conduct a series of single variable linear regression analyses to establish statistically significant correlations between the system generation portfolio and LDES deployment. To this end, we classify all generation resources into one of three categories: baseload, peaker, or VRE (Table 2), and then calculate the share of the total installed capacity provided by each of these categories. These three capacity shares serve as independent variables (xj) that are individually regressed against each of six different dependent variables (yi), representing total energy storage deployment (MWh), average energy storage duration (h), and the deployed power capacity of each considered storage duration (MW). We, therefore, conduct 18 individual regression analyses, each capturing the correlations between a dependent variable and a single independent variable. This is done to avoid co-linearity between the different capacity shares given that the generation shares sum to 100%. This approach captures the correlation between an increase in the generation share of a single resource type and storage deployment, while implicitly remaining agnostic about which of the other two generation types is decreasing.

We consider expansion results for our core “low,” “advanced,” and “breakthrough” LDES cost scenarios only and individual data points for each of the three transmission zones considered in our regression model. The “mid”- and “high”-cost scenarios are omitted because they did not result in significant LDES deployment and, therefore, fall outside the scope of this regression analysis, which examines correlations between LDES deployment and generation mix within scenarios where LDES is deployed. Our two sensitivity analyses, low 4-h storage costs and optimized storage duration, are also omitted because they would exhibit redundancies with our core scenarios. Each regression analysis, therefore, includes 270 samples. Fixed effects for both the transmission zone and the LDES cost scenario are also included in each regression analysis to control for mean differences between scenarios and zones, as detailed in Equation 1.

The clearest takeaway from the regression results is that the total stored energy and storage duration are negatively correlated with baseload and peaker generation shares and positively correlated with VRE shares. This generally aligns with expectations and provides suggestive evidence that overall storage activity acts as a substitute for baseload and peaker generation and as a complement to VRE.

Although the directional effects between baseload and peaker shares are similar, the magnitude of the impact is greater for baseload capacity when it comes to the total stored energy and storage duration. Furthermore, all the statistically significant estimates for storage investments are negatively correlated with baseload share. This suggests that the increases in baseload share are more consistently associated with the reductions in storage investments in general, likely reflecting a broader decline in the need for all forms of storage as baseload generation rises. This finding is also consistent with the parallel positive correlation that was identified between storage deployment and VRE generation, as increases in VRE generation are typically associated with decreases in baseload generation. In contrast, the relationship between peaker shares and storage is more complex. Capacity of some storage durations, such as those of 24 h, increases with higher peaker shares, while those of others, such as 100-h duration, decline. This suggests that although overall energy storage capacity tends to decrease with the increasing peaker shares, the effect is not uniform across all storage types. In addition to a decrease in energy capacity, increases in peaker shares may be associated with greater re-allocation across different storage resources, reflecting a shift in emphasis from longer-to shorter-duration LDES solutions.

Meanwhile, increases in VRE shares tend to show the opposite pattern: greater total energy storage capacity, longer average duration, and more deployment across 4-, 24-, and 100-h storage. These correlations align with expectations, as higher VRE shares relative to baseload and peaker generation may require more storage to accommodate fluctuations in renewable output.

While these regression results show certain correlations, they do not represent definitive causal relationships between peaker, baseload, and VRE shares and storage activities. The primary aim of this analysis is to leverage the large volume of modeling outputs to summarize general trends in the generation expansion results. The regression results can be viewed as suggestive evidence of how changes in generation mix may be associated with shifts in system storage needs. Although the results align with some expected patterns, they remain exploratory. A more rigorous analysis, with a larger set of simulations and additional controls, would be necessary to confirm these relationships with greater confidence.

Discussion

This paper presents an analysis of 180 core generation expansion scenarios of the US electricity system, with a focus on identifying factors that drive the deployment of LDES resources of different durations and highlighting system conditions where LDES provides substantial system value. We further analyze an additional 180 sensitivity expansion scenarios to assess the impacts of (1) a 50% reduction in the capital cost of 4-h lithium-ion batteries, and (2) considering a single candidate LDES technology with an endogenously optimized duration. We also assess nine scenarios that explore the impacts capturing different temporal and spatial representations. Based on the findings from this spatiotemporal sensitivity analysis, our capacity expansion model was specifically tailored to LDES applications by considering chronological system operations across all 8,760 hourly periods in a single target investment year.

Our results demonstrate that low-cost LDES technologies can reduce generation investments and decrease system costs, primarily in systems with high shares of VRE generation. However, steep cost reductions are needed to enable significant LDES deployment and unlock associated system benefits. Specifically, we find that LDES deployment is very limited until total capital costs for new 10-, 24-, and 100-h storage resources reach levels below $133/kWh, $60/kWh, and $20/kWh, respectively (i.e., the assumptions used in our “low-cost” LDES scenarios). In this case, LDES capacity complements 4-h storage deployment and is developed in greater quantity as the emission levels decrease. Deployment increases substantially when LDES capital costs achieve our “breakthrough” levels, $80/kWh, $38/kWh, and $14/kWh for 10-, 24-, and 100-h, respectively. In these scenarios, little to no new 4-h storage is developed, and, instead, there is significant investment in 24- and 100-h storage. These “breakthrough” cost levels are roughly aligned with the cost targets identified by the US DOE as required to achieve commercial LDES liftoff.25 We also demonstrate that a 50% reduction in the capital cost of 4-h lithium-ion batteries from $208/kWh to $104/kWh increases 4-h storage deployment substantially, substituting for LDES. Some portfolios still maintain LDES deployments, even with low-cost lithium ion, primarily those with high VRE shares. Collectively, these results emphasize that there are concurrent roles for storage resources of multiple different durations in many future system portfolios; however, the distribution of investment across storage technologies of different durations is heavily dependent on the capital costs of each technology.

We further show that (1) least-cost LDES deployments are sensitive to the temporal and spatial representations utilized in the capacity expansion model, (2) 100- and 24-h LDES resources often operate on seasonal dispatch cycles that necessitate multi-month or annual chronological modeling horizons to properly capture their value, (3) the marginal cost of carbon abatement increases substantially as the emission reduction target approaches 100%, (4) LDES provides the most value in terms of cost reduction in systems with high VRE penetrations and low peaking capacity, (5) LDES technologies operate very differently in systems with different generation portfolios, and (6) total storage energy capacity and, particularly, 24- and 100-h storage deployments, correlate significantly with system VRE, while the opposite effect is observed for peaker and baseload capacity share.

This paper clearly demonstrates the importance of appropriately representing and considering LDES technologies in long-term capacity expansion studies. This importance will persist as long as LDES capital cost projections remain highly uncertainty—or in the event that lower costs are realized—and is particularly relevant in systems that are expected to deploy large shares of wind and solar generation resources.

Limitations of the study

A range of modeling simplifications are necessary in any national-scale capacity expansion analysis in order to maintain computational tractability. This study also makes several assumptions that may lead to under- or over-valuation of LDES under different circumstances. Specifically, we do not directly analyze the value of LDES for system resilience, extreme event mitigation, deferral of transmission investments, and hybridization with specific generation resources. We instead prioritize assessing national-scale LDES deployment pathways with very high temporal granularity by considering a complete year with 8,760 hourly periods, rather than employing scenario reduction techniques to ease the computational burden. Future studies may, therefore, complement our work by exploring different tradeoffs, for example, prioritizing spatial granularity over temporal granularity, building on existing work that has explored the complex dynamics of LDES siting,45 exploring the interaction between LDES deployment and transmission expansion, examining different LDES use cases (e.g., storage as a transmission asset36,37,38 and system resilience), or assessing how the consideration of multiple future weather years and periods of extreme operating conditions impacts LDES deployment. Our LDES cost scenarios capture different projected cost reduction rates for different storage durations; however, we do not assess scenarios where a technology of a specific duration achieves a dramatic cost reduction relative to technologies of different durations. In practice, new advances or technologies may emerge that would reduce costs for some durations but not others; for example, a breakthrough, 100-h technology may emerge that is not economic for shorter durations, or vice versa. Such cost dynamics are not captured in our analysis and would shift how storage investments are distributed across durations. Similarly, our cost sensitivity analysis of 4-h storage demonstrates that significant future changes in cost profiles for shorter-duration resources would also impact LDES investments. Importantly, storage technology cost projections remain highly uncertainty but are extremely impactful predictors of technology deployment levels. Future work should, therefore, focus on improving representation and differentiation of the cost and performance characteristics of different specific LDES technologies, including scenarios with differentiated cost trajectories for different LDES technologies and durations, capturing expansion opportunities and limitations for geographically constrained technologies, and linking expansion models with reliability assessments to ensure that the LDES value is fully captured.

Method details

Model structure

Our analysis employs the generation expansion planning (GEP) model in Argonne National Laboratory’s A-LEAF modeling framework. The GEP model formulates a least-cost optimization problem that determines the location, capacity, and type of new generation assets needed to meet future demand reliably at least-cost, while also capturing resource retirements, physical operating constraints, reliability targets, and policy targets and incentives. A-LEAF has been applied to a number of previous analyses36,46,47,48,49 and the full mathematical formulation can be found on the model webpage (https://www.anl.gov/esia/a-leaf ). We now provide an abbreviated formulation of A-LEAF to highlight several components that are particularly relevant for the application presented in this paper.

The objective of the model is to minimize total system costs over the target decision horizon as in Equation 2, where ICy is the amortized investment cost over representative period y, RTCy is retirement cost, FOMy is fixed and operations and maintenance cost, GCy is the energy generation cost including fuel and variable operations and maintenance, RCy is the cost of providing operating reserves, and ωy is the relative weight of the period y. The reduce computational requirements, all investment decisions are formulated as continuous variables representing the total capacity of each technology in the target transmission zone, rather than binary or integer variables that represent individual plants or generation units of fixed size.

Generation and storage expansion decisions are thereby optimized to minimize these system costs while maintaining system reliability. In every considered hour t and in every considered system node n the following demand balance constraint is observed to ensure that all demand is served. Successive terms in Equation 3 ensure that the total power flows into node n (fj,n,t), less the total power flows out of node n (fn,j,t) and associated transmission losses based on the length of the transmission line (Lk)and its loss rate (χk), plus total electricity generation across all generation and storage resources in node n (gi,t), less total energy storage charging (chgi,t) across all energy storage resources in node n (ΨES), equal total electricity demand (Dn,t) in each node n and hour t. As Equation 3 is implemented as an equality constraint, unserved electricity demand is not permitted in the expansion model.

The initial state of charge soctstart,i,y for each energy storage resource in the first hour tstart of each representative period y is also optimized by the model, subject to a constraint (Equation 4) that the SOC returns to at least this initial level in the final hour of the representative period (tend).

System expansion and dispatch decisions are subject to a host of other operational constraints based on operating reserve requirements, intertemporal resource ramping rates for each resource, storage charging and discharging rates and efficiency, relevant state policies and regulations, and renewable resource availability; however this full set of constraints is omitted here for brevity.

This study utilizes a national-scale power system database, which includes all generators operating in the contiguous U.S. Our core study utilizes three transmission zones representing each of the three U.S. interconnections (East, West, Texas), while considering relevant transmission capacities between each pair of interconnections; however, we also consider a set of sensitivity cases exploring different spatial representations. We obtain resource supply curves and hourly generation availability data for VRE from NREL’s Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS) Model. We conduct single-stage capacity expansion with an initial generation portfolio based on 2022 data and 2040 as the target year, considering chronological dispatch across all 8,760 hours in the optimization formulation. In all cases we assume a 1.8% annual load growth rate.

Scenarios

Modeled least-cost system portfolio pathways depend heavily on technoeconomic assumptions, primarily cost and performance parameters for generation and storage resources. These parameters are highly uncertain, particularly for nascent technologies such as LDES. As investment levels are closely correlated with cost projections, this in turn can lead to a high level of uncertainty in modeled projections of future resource portfolios. Our analysis is designed to specifically identify how shifting generation portfolios impact optimal investments in LDES, particularly in terms of the distribution of investments across storage durations.

Therefore, rather than attempting to identify a single least-cost system portfolio pathway, we analyze five fundamentally different possible pathways. Four of these pathways are designed to depend on a reduced set of specific technologies and a baseline case is also considered to capture a more diverse generation mix across a broader range of different technologies. We do not suggest that any one of these specific technology pathways is likely to materialize precisely as defined, but by analyzing them we are able to identify directional impacts as technology portfolios shift over time. The generation portfolios corresponding to each pathway are obtained through constrained least-cost optimization; assumptions for each scenario are outlined Tables 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6.

The new nuclear technology is based on future reactor designs that are able to operate with greater operational flexibility than the current U.S. nuclear fleet. Hydrogen fuel assumptions are based on a cost of $3 per kilogram, the approximate mid-point between current costs and the DOE Hydrogen Shot target of $1 per kilogram. Given the 2040 target year, we do not consider any current federal production or investment tax credits in our investment or operational analysis.

Cost and performance characteristics for candidate new energy storage technologies are based on projections from the U.S. DOE Energy Storage Grand Challenge (ESGC) Cost and Performance Database,50 as further detailed in Tables 1, 5, and 6. For each of the five generation portfolio scenarios, we also analyze six different investment cost scenarios for LDES technologies. These are based on the three cost scenarios from the DOE ESGC Cost and Performance Database50 and two additional Advanced” and “Breakthrough” scenarios that capture the implications of cost declines well beyond current future projections. The Breakthrough scenario roughly corresponds with costs required to achieve “commercial liftoff” as identified by the U.S. DOE, which are $30-$49/kWh for 24-hour storage and $13-$22/kWh for 100-hour storage.25 These costs are based on a 45-55% target reduction from the current cost ranges presented in,25 1,100-1400/kW and 20-30/kWh for 10 to 36-hour storage and 1900-2500/kW and 10-15/kWh for 36 to 160-hour storage. We also consider a sixth scenario where no investments in new LDES technologies are permitted. This final set of scenarios provides a reference point for determining the value and impact of LDES investments under different system conditions.

Because the future cost trajectories of individual LDES technologies are highly uncertain, we model a single generic LDES technology with different durations that is intended to be representative of a range of different technology options. The characteristics of this generic LDES technology are derived from the cost and performance parameter projections for thermal energy storage systems (TESS) with 10-hour, 24-hour, and 100-hour durations.50 We also assess a set of sensitivity scenarios where a single LDES technology is considered with a duration between 10-hours and 100-hours that is endogenously optimized by the model; the characteristics of this technology are based on the cost and performance parameters of 24-hour TESS. TESS was selected as its siting is not geographically constrained in contrast to other LDES technologies such as closed-loop PSH, which requires favorable geography, or compressed air energy storage (CAES) or closed-loop hydrogen energy storage systems (HESS), which require caverns for underground storage. We emphasize that this does not reflect any explicit or implicit assumption that TESS technologies will dominate future LDES investments. Rather we use projections for TESS cost and performance parameters as a starting point to represent generic, technology-agnostic LDES investment options with different durations. To this end, our scenarios and analyses are then structured to consider a wide range of cost trajectories that are representative of potential futures for closed-loop PSH, CAES, HESS, and a variety of other LDES technologies. We therefore primarily focus our analysis on the capacity and duration of the LDES systems that are selected under each scenario, rather than other technological specifications.

We also consider a 4-hour lithium-ion battery with 85% round trip efficiency as a potential investment option. Our reference analysis utilizes Mid case cost projections for this 4-hour technology across all of our LDES cost sensitivity scenarios as it is more mature, and future costs are less uncertain. However, we do also analyze a separate set of sensitivity scenarios where the capital cost of the 4-lithium-ion hour battery is reduced by 50% relative to the ESGC Mid case projection.

Finally, for each combination of generation portfolio scenario and LDES cost scenario, we consider six different scenarios with varying emission reduction targets, ranging from no constraint on emissions to scenarios where national emissions are reduced by 60%-100% relative to 2022 levels (Table 7). These scenarios do not reflect any specific policies or a subjective opinion on how the U.S. electricity system will evolve. Rather they serve to establish a range of different generation portfolios that reflect different ways in which the system may evolve over the expansion horizon due to a variety of factors including potential changes in generation technology costs, state and federal policies, or other factors.
