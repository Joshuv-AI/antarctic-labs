DIG to Heal: Scaling General-purpose Agent Collaboration via Explainable Dynamic Decision Paths
Hanqing Yang, Hyungwoo Lee, Yuhang Yao, Zhiwei Liu, Kay Liu, Jingdi Chen, Carlee Joe-Wong

Abstract
The increasingly popular agentic AI paradigm promises to harness the power of multiple, general-purpose large language model (LLM) agents to collaboratively complete complex tasks. While many agentic AI systems utilize predefined workflows or agent roles in order to reduce complexity, ideally these agents would be truly autonomous, able to achieve emergent collaboration even as the number of collaborating agents increases. Yet in practice, such unstructured interactions can lead to redundant work and cascading failures that are difficult to interpret or correct. In this work, we study multi-agent systems composed of general-purpose LLM agents that operate without predefined roles, control flow, or communication constraints, relying instead on emergent collaboration to solve problems. We introduce the Dynamic Interaction Graph (DIG), which captures emergent collaboration as a time-evolving causal network of agent activations and interactions. DIG makes emergent collaboration observable and explainable for the first time, enabling real-time identification, explanation, and correction of collaboration-induced error patterns directly from agents’ collaboration paths. Thus, DIG fills a critical gap in understanding how general LLM agents solve problems together in truly agentic multi-agent systems. The project webpage can be found at: https://happyeureka.github.io/dig .

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used as general decision-making units due to their broad world knowledge, flexible reasoning abilities, and capacity to interact with external tools and memory. This has led to the emergence of
LLM agents
, which extend LLMs with persistent state, tool access, and environment interaction, enabling them to act autonomously in complex tasks. Recently, significant attention has been devoted to
LLM-powered multi-agent systems

(
Chen et al. 2026
;
Yang et al. 2025
)
, where multiple agents collaborate on tasks such as research, software engineering, robotics, simulation, and question answering
(
Durante et al. 2024
)
.

Despite the potential of allowing LLM agents to act autonomously, in practice unstructured agent collaboration often leads to task failure. Thus, many multi-agent systems utilize
predefined workflows
, with fixed roles, task decompositions, tool usage patterns, and message routing policies, in order to stabilize performance
(
Li et al. 2024
)
. However, such designs are often domain-specific and fundamentally constrain how agents interact.
In contrast, we study LLM-powered multi-agent systems composed of
general-purpose
LLM agents that operate without predefined roles, control flow, or communication constraints. In such systems, coordination, task decomposition, and problem-solving strategies must arise implicitly from local agent decisions and interactions, resembling how human teams dynamically adapt to evolving tasks. Our goal is to characterize analogous forms of cooperative intelligence in general-purpose LLM agents and illuminate the causes of collaboration failures so as to enable more resilient autonomous multi-agent systems.

The Challenges of Emergent Cooperation.

Allowing agents to autonomously interact introduces substantial challenges. Each agent receives information, from other agents and the environment, at arbitrary times while acting concurrently with others. Local agent decisions can thus easily push global execution away from intended behavior, producing redundant work, stalled progress, or cascading failures that are difficult to diagnose or recover from. Simply increasing the number of agents does not guarantee improved performance; instead, interaction complexity grows combinatorially, leading to even more interdependent outputs that may cause task failures or redundancies.
Unlike traditional distributed systems, multi-LLM agent systems are stochastic, heterogeneous, and message-driven, making tracing, debugging, and recovery particularly challenging. Existing approaches typically rely on centralized monitors
(
Gandhi et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
, guard agents
(
Yu et al. 2025
)
, or post hoc failure attribution based on execution logs or curated error datasets
(
Kong et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
. These methods either assume fixed workflows, operate after failures have already occurred, or require large amounts of annotated data, and therefore do not directly address how cooperation emerges or fails during execution.

Our Perspective: Analyzing Agent Interactions.

In this work, we argue that understanding and improving general multi-agent systems requires treating
collaboration itself
as the primary object of analysis. Rather than focusing on individual agents or final outcomes, we model the system in terms of its interaction structure. We introduce the
Dynamic Interaction Graph (DIG)
, a time-evolving directed graph that represents agent activations and interaction events as a causal network. This abstraction makes emergent collaboration observable and explainable, enabling real-time tracing, structural analysis, and principled reasoning about how cooperative behavior forms, propagates, and fails.

Building on DIG, we establish a
topological characterization
of emergent collaboration, which maps interaction graph structures to distinct classes of collaboration-induced error patterns. This result provides a model-agnostic categorization of failure modes, enabling early failure detection at the level of collaboration structure rather than individual agent outputs. Importantly, this structural perspective is designed to be compatible with existing failure detection and attribution methods
(
Cemri et al. 2025
)
. Rather than replacing them, it provides an
error detection and guarding layer
, where topology-level signals can be integrated into semantic, behavioral, and learning-based detectors.
The DIG framework further supports reasoning about effective agent utilization, revealing when additional agents contribute meaningful progress versus when they introduce redundancy, inefficiency, or instability. Together, these capabilities provide a unified and explainable foundation for understanding and debugging truly agentic multi-LLM systems, where cooperation emerges dynamically rather than being imposed by predefined workflows.

Our main
contributions
are:
(1)
Interaction Modeling.
We formalize the interaction structure and cooperative problem-solving process of general LLM agents with DIG, a dynamic directed acyclic graph (DAG) representation that captures both sequential and parallel dependencies, as well as temporal ordering, in unconstrained multi-agent systems.
(2)
Topological Failure Characterization.
We establish a principled mapping from the DIG topology to collaboration-induced error types, yielding a high-level, model-agnostic categorization of failure modes that supports
explainable and hierarchical
error detection and can be naturally integrated with existing error detection and attribution methods.
(3)
General-Agent Execution Traces.
We evaluate DIG on two scalable and verifiable evaluation tasks, allowing us to introduce the first dataset of execution traces that capture the behavior and interaction patterns of general-purpose agents operating without predefined roles or control flow.
(4)
Structure-Driven System Healing.
We propose an automatic system healing mechanism driven by observed agent behavior and interaction structure, enabling early detection and correction of collaboration-induced failures during execution. In experiments, we find that this mechanism yields improved efficiency and fewer errors, particularly on difficult tasks with many agents

After reviewing related work in Section

By contrast, our work provides a lightweight, framework-agnostic tracing system that captures interaction dynamics among general agents, enabling systematic and explainable error attribution and scaling naturally to massively parallel multi-agent systems.

Problem Formulation: Cooperative Problem Solving with General Agents

We consider cooperative problem solving in systems of multiple
general-purpose agents
that interact asynchronously via message passing, with no predefined roles, control flow, or communication protocols. Coordination and task decomposition emerge solely from local agent decisions, as shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
Cooperative problem solving with general-purpose agents. A pool of autonomous agents works on a shared task without predefined roles, control flow, or communication constraints, each operating independently and non-deterministically and interacting through emergent cooperative strategies. We make emergent cooperation analyzable by modeling agent interaction in a protocol-agnostic manner.

System Model.

A cooperative multi-agent system is characterized by the tuple

\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{P},\mathbb{Z}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\}
is a finite set of agents,
\mathcal{E}
is the set of interaction events,
\mathcal{P}
denotes a problem instance, and
\mathbb{Z}
denotes discrete logical time.
The problem instance is encoded as a distinguished initial event
e_{0}\in\mathcal{E}
, delivered at
t=0
to a subset
\mathcal{A}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
. Execution terminates when a terminal event
e_{\infty}
is generated.
Logical time
t\in\mathbb{Z}
advances whenever an interaction occurs, either through agent activation or event delivery. It induces a causal ordering over interactions but may not correspond to wall-clock time.

Each
agent

a\in\mathcal{A}
is modeled as an autonomous decision-making unit that operates asynchronously and has no predefined role or control flow. Thus, we abstract away agent internals and model only observable interaction behavior via a non-deterministic transformation on events

f_{a}:I_{a}\longrightarrow O_{a},\qquad I_{a},\,O_{a}\subseteq\mathcal{E}.

(2)

Each agent
a
maintains a local event
buffer

B_{a}(t)\subseteq\mathcal{E}
. Let
\mathrm{activate}(t,a)\in\{0,1\}
denote whether
a
is activated at time
t
. Activation occurs
(\mathrm{activate}(t,a)\in\{0,1\})
whenever the buffer changes while the agent is idle
((B_{a}(t)\neq B_{a}(t-1))\ \land\ \mathrm{activate}(t-1,a)=0)
. The buffer may change if the agent receives a new event (see the description of events below), or if the agent acts on an event which is then cleared from the buffer. For instance, the event may correspond to a computational task, like sorting a list, which the agent can then complete while it is activated.

Agents interact through
events

e
, which are defined as

e=(p_{e},\,\pi_{e})\in\mathcal{E},\;\pi_{e}\mapsto(R_{t}(e),\,\pi_{e}^{\prime}),\;R_{t}(e)\subseteq\mathcal{A},

(3)

where
p_{e}
is the payload and
\pi_{e}
is a delivery policy. For example,
p_{e}
can be the result of a computation completed by an agent
a
, whose delivery policy is to send the result immediately to all other agents
a^{\prime}\neq a
. At each logical time
t
,
\pi_{e}
determines the set of recipients
R_{t}(e)
and an updated policy
\pi_{e}^{\prime}
. The event is appended to the buffers of agents in
R_{t}(e)
. If
\pi_{e}^{\prime}
is empty, the event expires.

Tracking Agent Dynamics.
The coupled dynamics of agent transformations (
2
) and policy-driven event delivery (
3
) induce a stochastic execution process over logical time. An
execution trace
is then defined as

\mathcal{T}=\{(a_{t},e_{t},t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}},

(4)

where
a_{t}
is the activated agent at time
t
(if any), and
e_{t}
is the event generated or delivered at time
t
.

Due to asynchrony and non-determinism, system behavior is not fully characterized by the terminal output
e_{\infty}
. Failures may arise from intermediate interaction structure, such as stalled activations, lost subproblems, duplicated work, or premature termination.
We therefore study the following
inference problem
: given only an observable execution trace
\mathcal{T}
, infer a system-level diagnosis function

\Phi:\mathcal{T}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F},

(5)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes a space of failure signals and explanations. For example, a failure signal might consist of a computation task (i.e., an event) being forwarded indefinitely between agents, none of whom complete the task and generate the result. The function
\Phi
must operate solely on observable interaction structure, without access to agent internals, semantic task labels, or outcome supervision. To solve this problem, in the next section, we introduce the
Dynamic Interaction Graph (DIG)
, a formal execution representation of agent interactions in cooperative problem solving.

4
Method: Dynamic Interaction Graph (DIG)

We present the
Dynamic Interaction Graph (DIG)
as a system-level representation and inference framework for cooperative problem solving in general multi-agent systems. Concretely, our method takes an execution trace
\mathcal{T}
as input, constructs a labeled causal interaction graph
\Psi(\mathcal{T})
, and performs graph-level inference to detect failures, explain coordination dynamics, and derive interventions using only
observable interaction structure
from
\mathcal{T}
.

4.1
DIG Definition

We represent cooperative multi-agent execution as a directed, dynamic, bipartite causal DIG graph. At logical time
t
, the system induces a graph

G(t)=(\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)\cup\mathcal{V}_{E}(t),\,\mathcal{E}_{G}(t)),

(6)

where
\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)
is the set of
activation nodes
,
\mathcal{V}_{E}(t)
is the set of
event nodes
, and
\mathcal{E}_{G}(t)
is the set of directed edges, as illustrated in Figure
2
(1) and (2). The graph is bipartite: edges connect only activation nodes to event nodes or event nodes to activation nodes.
Each
activation node
corresponds to the activation of an agent
a_{v}\in\mathcal{A}
that lasts from time
t_{v}^{\mathrm{start}}
to
t_{v}^{\mathrm{end}}
:

v=(\mathrm{id}_{v},\,a_{v},\,\tau_{v}),\qquad\tau_{v}=[t_{v}^{\mathrm{start}},\,t_{v}^{\mathrm{end}}].

(7)

Each
event node
corresponds to an interaction event with realized delivery structure:

e_{G}=(e,\,\tau_{e}),\qquad\tau_{e}=\bigl(t_{e}^{\mathrm{gen}},\,\{t_{e}^{\mathrm{recv}}(b)\}_{b\in R_{\infty}(e)}\bigr).

(8)

Edges.

Two edge types exist:
(1)
Generation edges

(v\rightarrow e_{G})\in\mathcal{E}_{G}
, which exist if
v
generates
e
; and
(2)
Delivery edges

(e_{G}\rightarrow v_{b})\in\mathcal{E}_{G}
, which exist if
e
triggers activation
v_{b}
.
Collectively,
\mathcal{E}_{G}
defines a complete
temporal causal trace
of execution. All causal influence propagates exclusively through these edges.

Illustration.

Figures
2
(4) and (5) illustrate the DIG. Each circle denotes an activation node
v\in\mathcal{V}_{A}
, spanning
[t_{v}^{\mathrm{start}},t_{v}^{\mathrm{end}}]
, and each square denotes an event node
e_{G}\in\mathcal{V}_{E}
at generation time
t_{e}^{\mathrm{gen}}
. Edges from circles to squares represent generation
(v\rightarrow e_{G})
, while edges from squares to circles represent delivery
(e_{G}\rightarrow v_{b})
. Figure
2
(4) shows the full real-time DIG, including transient edges, and Figure
2
(5) shows the cleaned graph after removing non-productive edges. In Section
4.2
, we discuss the distinction between transient and productive edges. Together, they illustrate how an execution trace
\mathcal{T}
maps to a time-indexed bipartite causal graph
G(t)
, where coordination structure is encoded by nodes and directed edges. DIG thus provides a complete system-level representation of cooperative multi-agent execution, making coordination dynamics and causal dependencies observable.

4.2
Interaction Primitives as Graph Rewrite Operators

Figure 2
:

Dynamic Interaction Graph (DIG) and local graph rewrite operators. (1-2) Bipartite graph structure with agent activation nodes (circles) and event nodes (rectangles). Each activation node may consume a set of input events and generate a set of output events. Each event has a single source activation and may be delivered to multiple downstream agents. (3) Canonical edge rewrite operators RESPOND, WAIT, REROUTE, DISCARD, and SUBMIT, defining how activations transform incoming delivery edges and produce new events. (4) Raw DIG induced by an execution trace, including all generation and delivery edges. (5) Cleaned DIG after removing non-productive edges corresponding to non-productive interactions, exposing the underlying interaction structure.

The DIG evolves through
local graph rewrite operators
induced by agent activations. When an agent
a
is activated at logical time
t
, the activation is represented by an activation node
v\in\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)
.
Let
B_{a}(t)
denote the local event buffer of agent
a
at time
t
, and let
I_{a}(t)\;\subseteq\;B_{a}(t)

denote the subset of events selected by the agent during the activation. These events correspond exactly to the set of incoming delivery edges of the activation node:

E_{G}^{\mathrm{in}}(v)\;=\;\{(e,v)\in\mathcal{E}_{G}(t)\}.

(9)

We further assign a causal fate to each incoming interaction edge by defining a system-level
edge rewrite semantics
through a mapping

\phi_{v}:E_{G}^{\mathrm{in}}(v)\;\longrightarrow\;\{\textsc{consume},\textsc{delay},\textsc{reroute},\textsc{discard}\}
.
This induces a local graph rewrite operator
\mathcal{R}_{v}:G(t)\;\longrightarrow\;G(t^{+})

that transforms the DIG by modifying, removing, or creating nodes and edges. We elaborate these operators next.

Canonical Rewrite Operators.

Let
O_{a}(t)\;\subseteq\;\mathcal{E}

denote the set of new events generated by the agent
a
during the activation, which correspond to newly created event nodes in the DIG.
The standard interaction primitives correspond to the following canonical edge rewrite patterns:

(1) Respond (R):

The agent acts on all buffered events,

I_{a}(t)=B_{a}(t)
and
B_{a}(t^{+})=\emptyset
,
and labels all incoming edges as consumed,

\phi_{v}(e,v)=\textsc{consume}
.
The DIG is rewritten by removing all input events from the agent buffer and generating new event nodes,
G(t^{+})=G(t)\setminus E_{G}^{\mathrm{in}}(v)\;\cup\;\{(v,e^{\prime})\mid e^{\prime}\in O_{a}(t)\}.

(2) Wait (W):

The agent preserves its buffer,

B_{a}(t^{+})=B_{a}(t)
,
and labels all incoming edges as delayed,

\phi_{v}(e,v)=\textsc{delay}
.
No graph modification is applied:
G(t^{+})=G(t)
.

(3) Reroute (RR):

The agent forwards selected events,

B_{a}(t^{+})=B_{a}(t)\setminus I_{a}(t)
,
and labels their edges as rerouted,

\phi_{v}(e,v)=\textsc{reroute}
.
Each such edge is redirected:
(e,v)\;\mapsto\;(e,v^{\prime}).

(4) Discard (D):

The agent drops selected events,

B_{a}(t^{+})=B_{a}(t)\setminus I_{a}(t)
,
and labels the edges as discarded,

\phi_{v}(e,v)=\textsc{discard}
.
The edges are removed:
(e,v)\;\mapsto\;\emptyset.

(5) Submit (S):

A terminal Respond with

O_{a}(t)=\{e_{\infty}\}
,
yielding
G(t^{+})=G(t)\setminus E_{G}^{\mathrm{in}}(v)\;\cup\;\{(v,e_{\infty})\}.

Global Execution Semantics.

The overall system execution is the composition of local graph rewrite operators induced by activation nodes:

G_{0}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{R}_{v_{1}}}G_{1}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{R}_{v_{2}}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\mathcal{R}_{v_{T}}}G_{T},

(10)

where each
\mathcal{R}{v_{t}}\in{(R),(W),(RR),(D),(S)}
is determined by the edge-action labeling function
\phi{v_{t}}
.

Edge Attribution.
We attribute each edge as either productive or non-productive. Non-productive edges denote interactions that do not advance the cooperative solution at the current step. Edges corresponding to
Wait (W)
and
Discard (D)
are non-productive and drawn as dashed, indicating that no productive work is performed at that time. For
Reroute (RR)
, the edge from the source agent is non-productive and dashed, while the redirected edge to the final recipient is productive and solid. Figure
2
(3) and (4) illustrate this distinction, with solid lines representing productive edges and dashed lines representing non-productive edges.

Illustration.

Thus, cooperative multi-agent execution is formally a
graph rewriting dynamical system
. As illustrated in Figure
2
(3), agents do not coordinate through predefined roles or control flow, but by inducing local transformations on the edges of the DIG. All emergent cooperative structure, system-level behavior, and failure modes arise from the composition of these local edge-level rewrite operators, making DIG the effective state space of cooperative intelligence.

4.3
DIG as a Representation of Cooperative Intelligence

In this section, we argue that our DIG representation
\{G(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is not a visualization artifact but an
intermediate state space
on which we can perform system-level reasoning. We therefore treat the DIG as the representation of cooperative intelligence itself.

DIG as a Labeled Causal Interaction Graph.

For any logical time
t
, let

G(t)=\bigl(\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)\cup\mathcal{V}_{E}(t),\,\mathcal{E}_{G}(t),\,\phi_{t}\bigr),

(11)

where
(\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)\cup\mathcal{V}_{E}(t),\mathcal{E}_{G}(t))
is the directed bipartite causal topology defined in Section
4.1
, and
\phi_{t}:\mathcal{E}_{G}(t)\longrightarrow\{\textsc{consume},\textsc{delay},\textsc{reroute},\textsc{discard}\}

is the edge-action labeling induced by local rewrite operators (Section
4.2
).
Thus, each
G(t)
is a fully labeled causal interaction graph, encoding both
who interacted with whom
and
how each interaction edge was operationally treated
.

We can further use this formalism to characterize agents’ cooperative behavior by defining the cooperative behavior induced by an execution trace
\mathcal{T}
as the equivalence class

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{T})=\Bigl\{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}\ \Big|\ \Psi(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\cong\Psi(\mathcal{T})\Bigr\},

(12)

where
\cong
denotes isomorphism of the time-indexed labeled graphs
\{G(t)\}
, preserving
(i) bipartite node types,
(ii) directed adjacency, and
(iii) edge labels in
\Sigma
.
This defines cooperative behavior purely in terms of observable interaction structure, independent of agent identities, internal states, prompts, or architectures.
Cooperative behavior is therefore encoded in the sequence of graph transformations applied to the DIG:

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{T})\ \equiv\ \{\,\phi_{t}(G(t))\mid t\in\mathbb{Z},\;G(t)=\Psi(\mathcal{T})\,\}.

(13)

That is, cooperation is fully characterized by the temporal evolution of the interaction graph under the edge-level rewrite operators
\{\phi_{t}\}
. Each
\phi_{t}
specifies how interaction edges are locally consumed, delayed, rerouted, or discarded, and therefore makes the system’s coordination dynamics explicitly observable.

DIG as an Explainability Trace.

Consequently, the sequence
\{G(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
constitutes an
explainability trace
of cooperative intelligence.
Any system-level behavior or failure signal
f\in\mathcal{F}
inferred from an execution trace
\mathcal{T}
satisfies

f\;=\;\mathcal{I}\bigl(\{G(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigr)\;=\;\mathcal{I}\bigl(\{\phi_{t}(G(t))\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigr),

(14)

for some graph-level inference operator
\mathcal{I}
.
Thus, explanations depend only on the labeled causal structure
(\mathcal{V}_{A}\cup\mathcal{V}_{E},\mathcal{E}_{G},\phi_{t})
and are invariant to all unobservable agent internals. In this sense, DIG is not merely a representation of execution, but an explicit
causal explanation object
for emergent multi-agent collaboration.

Theorem 4.1

(Topological Inference Reduction)
.

Fix a class
\mathcal{C}
of cooperative multi-agent systems whose observable semantics are defined by asynchronous activations and event passing as in Section
3
. Let
\mathcal{T}
be the space of observable execution traces and let
\Psi:\mathcal{T}\to\{G(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be the DIG construction operator.

For any system-level functional
\Phi:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{F}
that depends only on observable interactions (equivalently, is invariant over the equivalence classes in (
12
)), there exists a graph functional
\mathcal{I}:\{G(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that
\Phi=\mathcal{I}\circ\Psi.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
can be found in Appendix
A
. This result establishes that
all structurally observable system-level reasoning about cooperative intelligence reduces to inference over the DIG
. In other words, diagnosis, explanation, and intervention are not operations over agents, but over the topology and edge-action structure of the evolving causal interaction graph itself.

4.4
Problem-Solving in DIG

We interpret cooperative problem solving as a
structural transformation process
over the DIG. Given an execution trace
\mathcal{T}
and its induced DIG sequence
\{G(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}=\Psi(\mathcal{T})
, task progress is realized through the composition of local graph rewrite operators (Eq. (
10
)), where each activation node
v_{t}\in\mathcal{V}_{A}
induces a rewrite
\mathcal{R}_{v_{t}}
through its edge-action operator
\phi_{v_{t}}
and output event set
O_{a_{t}}(t)
, rather than through access to agent internals.

Each activation node
v\in\mathcal{V}_{A}
consumes an input set of events and produces an output set of events, as recorded by the incident edges in the DIG. Let

\displaystyle I_{v}

\displaystyle=\{\,e\in\mathcal{V}_{E}\mid(e,v)\in\mathcal{E}_{G}\,\},

(15)

\displaystyle O_{v}

\displaystyle=\{\,e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{E}\mid(v,e^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{G}\,\}.

denote the input events delivered to
v
and the output events generated by
v
, respectively. We interpret
v
as a local problem-solving transformation
v:\;I_{v}\;\mapsto\;O_{v}
.

An activation is
problem-generating
if it expands the frontier of unresolved work,
|O_{v}|>|I_{v}|
,
in which case each
p\in O_{v}
is treated as a
subproblem
spawned from context
I_{v}
. An activation is
problem-reducing
if it does not expand the frontier,
|O_{v}|\leq|I_{v}|
,
in which case each
s\in O_{v}
is treated as a
subsolution
produced from context
I_{v}
.

Failure, Detection, and Healing

Figure 3
:
Illustration of the structural failure patterns in DIG defined in Table
1
, showing how coordination invariant violations manifest as interaction structure.

The system defines errors not by the
Internal Reasoning
of the agents, but by observable
Interaction Primitives
(actions taken by agents) and the structural invariants they violate in DIG.

Failure Category

Failure pattern in the DIG

Reachability and Termination

All reachable work in the DIG should persist until some activation consumes it. The system should emit a single
Submit
event, and only after all reachable work has been consumed.

Early termination (ET)
: a
Submit
event is generated while some reachable work remains unconsumed.

Missing termination (MC)
: all reachable work has been consumed, but no
Submit
event is generated within a reasonable time window.

Orphaned event (OE)
: an event is generated but becomes unreachable before any activation can consume it, either because it is discarded or because it is assigned invalid recipients.

Deadlock (DL)
: reachable work remains, but all activated agents wait indefinitely, making progress impossible.

Progress

Generated events should be consumed downstream within a reasonable time; repeated deferral, rerouting, or redundant handling indicates risk.

Failure Taxonomy.
Empirically, cooperative general agent systems exhibit failures that arise not from isolated reasoning mistakes within individual agents, but from breakdowns in interaction structure and execution dynamics. Table
1
summarizes the major classes of failures observed in our experiments with DIG.

The taxonomy is defined in terms of observable execution structure, without access to agent internals, task semantics, or predefined workflows. We consider two distinct classes of violated structural or temporal invariants: failures in preserving and exhausting work (Reachability and Coverage) and warnings indicating inefficient or risky handling of emitted work (Progress). This separation enables precise monitoring, diagnosis, and intervention in emergent cooperative problem solving. Moreover, the detection method remains task- and domain-agnostic, as it operates on the interaction structure.

Failure Detection and Healing.
Whenever a new event
e
is generated at time
t
, the system temporarily blocks its delivery and evaluates the current
G(t)
for error patterns defined in Table
1
and shown in Figure
3
. When an event is blocked due to a failure detection, the system may (i) inject new information into it before delivery, (ii) inject and reroute it to a recipient set, or (iii) create a new event and deliver it to a recipient set in order to
heal
from the failure. We detail how each failure pattern from Table

is detected and healed:

(1) ET.

Detection:
A

event is generated despite unresolved reachable work.

e_{\infty}\in V_{E}(t)
and there exists

without a directed path from

Healing:
apply (ii) by injecting information about unresolved events and rerouting it to the agent that issued
Submit
.

(2) MC.

Detection:
All reachable work is exhausted, but no

Formally,
R(t)=\emptyset
and
e_{\infty}\notin V_{E}(t)
for

exceeding a threshold.
Healing:
apply (i) by injecting information that all reachable work is exhausted.

(3) OE.

Detection:
An event is generated but has no recipients for longer than a reasonable time window.
Formally, there exists
e\in V_{E}(t)

e\neq e_{\infty}

R_{t}(e)=\emptyset
for longer than a reasonable time window.
Healing:
apply (ii) by injecting status information and rerouting the event back to its generating agent.

(4) DL.

Detection:
Reachable work remains, but no activation occurs for longer than a reasonable time window.
Formally,
R(t)\neq\emptyset

We evaluate DIG’s ability to reveal cooperative problem solving in

For each domain, we define

7.2
Case Study with 20 Agents

We conducted a 20-agent case study on the Count Frequency task with 100,000 elements (10 times our “hard” setting) to demonstrate the DIG’s value in highly complex settings. In our case study, the baseline system (MAS) fails to produce any result within 120 seconds without DIG-based healing, whereas our method enables the same 20-agent setup to produce a valid answer within 70 seconds. In this run, our method achieves an RMSE of 5812.69 (approximately 5.8% relative to the array size) with 92 calls, using 435,105 prompt tokens and 10,567 completion tokens.

Figure
6
shows the DIG generated by DIG (top), as well as times at which each agent and the system is activated (bottom). As we would intuitively expect in highly parallel settings, we observe some common interaction failures, including 1 missing completion error, 8 repeated-effort incidents, 2 dependency warnings, 1 orphaned event, and 4 early termination errors. Overall, these observations highlight that
multi-agent collaboration is non-trivial
: as the number of agents increases, the system becomes more susceptible to interaction failures, making scalable reliability a central challenge. DIG addresses this challenge by providing an agent-count-, agent-type-, and problem-domain-agnostic method that supports healing and improves system performance even with 20 agents operating in parallel.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce DIG, which uses the Dynamic Interaction Graph for multi-agent collaboration. DIG models emergent collaboration as a time-evolving causal network of agent activations and interactions, making collaborative dynamics observable and explainable. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first framework enabling real-time identification, interpretation, and correction of collaboration-induced error patterns directly from agents’ interaction paths. We view DIG as an open framework and anticipate continued community contributions to further strengthen and extend it, e.g., further evaluating scalability to more agents and more problem domains.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work aimed at advancing the field of machine learning by improving the interpretability, robustness, and reliability of general-purpose multi-agent systems. By making emergent agent interactions observable and explainable, our approach has the potential to support safer, more transparent, and more controllable deployment of agentic AI systems in practice. We do not identify ethical concerns beyond those commonly associated with large language model–based systems.

Error Taxonomy for Cooperative Multi-Agent Systems

This document defines a structural failure taxonomy for cooperative multi-agent systems in terms of
observable interaction patterns in the Dynamic Interaction Graph (DIG), independent of agent internals
or task semantics.

Reachability and Termination (Failures)

All reachable work in the DIG should persist until some activation consumes it. The system should emit
a single
Submit
event, and only after all reachable work has been consumed.

•

Early termination (ET)
: a
Submit
event is generated while some reachable work remains unconsumed.

•

Missing termination (MC)
: all reachable work has been consumed, but no
Submit
event is generated within a reasonable time window.

•

Orphaned event (OE)
: an event is generated but becomes unreachable before any activation can consume it, either because it is discarded or because it is assigned invalid recipients.

•

Deadlock (DL)
: reachable work remains, but all activated agents wait indefinitely, making progress impossible.

Progress (Warnings)

Generated events should be consumed downstream within a reasonable time; repeated deferral, rerouting,
or redundant handling indicates risk.

•

Excessive rerouting (ER)
: an event is repeatedly
Reroute
d across activations without any activation
Consume
ing it to generate downstream events.

•

Cross-lineage aggregation (CLA)
: an activation
Consume
s events from different lineages.

•

Repeated subproblem solving (RSP)
: multiple problem-reducing activations generate outputs whose coverage overlaps on the same upstream event.

Intended Use

•

Monitoring emergent cooperation

•

Early failure detection

•

Structure-driven system healing

•

Explainable debugging of multi-agent executions
