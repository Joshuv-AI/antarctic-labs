Modulation of the bone marrow microenvironment by acute B-cell lymphoblastic leukemia–derived large oncosomes
Authors: Foruzandeh Samangan, Theresa Krack, Fatima Didar Kerman, Jimena Bravo, Claudius Witzler, Bianca Gregorz, Wolfram Ruf, Daniela S Krause, Valentina R Minciacchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•B-ALL creates a growth-permissive bone marrow microenvironment by secretion of specific large EVs.•MSC conditioned by large EVs modulate the availability of B-ALL growth factor IGF1.

Introduction

Leukemia cells educate the bone marrow (BM) microenvironment (BMM) to support their own survival and resistance to chemotherapy.1 However, although the remodeling of the BMM seems to be a common mechanism shared across different types of leukemia, increasing evidence supports the concept that each leukemia subtype specifically customizes the BMM to its own advantage.2, 3, 4, 5 During disease progression, breakpoint cluster region-Abelson murine leukemia viral oncogene homolog 1 (BCR-ABL1)+ B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) cells interact with several cell populations within the BMM.6,7 Macrophages influence B-ALL progression via the secretion of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13 (CXCL13),8 whereas mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) have been suggested to support both B-ALL progression9 and chemotherapy resistance.10 Similarly, adipocytes contribute to the acquisition of chemotherapy resistance.11 Moreover, osteoclasts stimulated by B-ALL cells reportedly mediate bone loss.12 Consistent with the growing evidence that remodeling of the extracellular space plays an important role in B-ALL progression, we have recently identified the fibrinolytic system, in particular plasmin, as a new player in the remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and release of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1), which contributes to B-ALL progression.4

Due to their ability to simultaneously transfer multiple types of biological mediators, extracellular vesicles (EVs) constitute a major means of communication between cells.13 This communication route is hijacked by leukemia cells and used to condition the microenvironment toward a tumor-supportive phenotype. Nevertheless, the main body of knowledge on EV-mediated communication in leukemia currently stems from studies on acute myeloid leukemia.14 In B-ALL, MSC, upon reduced expression of pleckstrin homology domain family M member 1, release EVs with altered cargo that support a more immature B-cell phenotype in B-ALL.15 Similarly, endothelial cell–derived EVs loaded with angiopoietin-like 2 contribute to B-ALL progression.16 Conversely, B-ALL–derived EVs have been suggested to affect the metabolism of BM stromal cells,17 the quiescent status of normal hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells,18 or the survival of other B-ALL cells.19

Large oncosomes (LO) are a population of atypically large EVs (1-10 μm) released by aggressive tumor cells.20, 21, 22, 23 More precisely, an increase in LO shedding has been associated with the acquisition of an amoeboid-like motility.20,23 These EVs carry a specific cargo24, 25, 26 and contribute to the establishment of a supportive microenvironment in different solid tumors.27,28 Combining the reported ability of leukemia cells to adopt amoeboid-like motility29 with the observation that B-ALL cells can secrete large EVs,30 we hypothesized that B-ALL modulates the BMM via release of LO. Here, we show that BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL cells shed LO capable of reprogramming target stromal cells to their own advantage. Furthermore, MSC primed with B-ALL–derived LO display an increased capability to differentiate into adipocytes upon stimulation. In turn, both LO-primed MSC and their adipocyte-progeny support B-ALL growth. Finally, we suggest that B-ALL–derived LO contribute to reprogramming of the BMM involved in the regulation of the availability of the B-ALL–proproliferative factor IGF1.

Materials and methods

Mice

C57BL/6J mice were bred in the in-house animal facility. C57BL/6J mice, aged 5 to 6 weeks (donors) or 8 to 10 weeks (recipients), were used for all transplants. C57BL/6J mice, aged 8 to 12 weeks were used for the isolation of primary MSC. Animals of different sexes were randomly assigned to experimental groups with an equal number of males and females in each group. All animal studies were approved by the local government (Landesuntersuchungsamt Rheinland-Pfalz, Germany, numbers 23-177-07/G23-1-067 and 23-177-07/G24-1-022).

Cell lines

Information on cell lines used in this study are available in the supplemental Material and Methods.

Induction of B-ALL

BM cells were collected from donor mice, transduced once with murine stem cell virus internal ribosome entry site green fluorescent protein (or red fluorescent protein) BCR-ABL1–expressing retrovirus and transplanted IV (2 × 106 cells per mouse) into nonirradiated mice.4,8,31 Forty-eight hours before B-ALL induction mice had been preconditioned with either vehicle (phosphate-buffered saline) or B-ALL–derived LO. LO were isolated from the long bones of 1 mouse with B-ALL and injected IV into 1 recipient. Disease progression was assessed by analysis of peripheral blood using the Abaxis VetScan HM5 (Zoetis) and flow cytometry. The tumor burden was determined by the percentage of green fluorescent protein (BCR-ABL1)+ leukocytes positive for CD19.

Isolation of primary MSC

Primary mouse MSC and human MSC (hMSC) were isolated as previously described.3,4 For further details, see the supplemental Section. In all in vitro assays MSC were exposed to vehicle or B-ALL–derived LO (10 μg/mL) for 48 hours.

Isolation and characterization of EVs

Cells were cultured in serum-supplemented medium until reaching a total density of ∼2 × 107 cells (∼2 × 106 cells/mL). Afterward, cells were washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline and placed in serum-free medium for 72 hours. The BM supernatant was obtained either via crushing or centrifuging (12 000g for 30 seconds at room temperature) of long bones after cutting off the ends. Large EVs were isolated from EV-containing medium or the BM supernatants via differential centrifugation, and LO-enriched preparations were obtained via density gradient centrifugation of the large EVs.15,24,27 Concentration of isolated EV was measured via tunable resistive pulse–sensing technology, expression of EV markers was characterized via immunoblotting, and analysis of EV surface markers was performed using the MACSPlex EV kit IO, mouse (Miltenyi Biotec). See the supplemental Section for additional details.

MSC differentiation

A total of 1 × 104 hMSC or mouse MSC per well (24-well plate) were differentiated into adipocytes using the adipocyte differentiation medium (Lonza). After 3 days, cells were used in other assays or labeled with oil red O or perilipin 1 (PLIN1) to identify adipocytes.

Immunofluorescence

Once fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin, and incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies to β-catenin (1:100), fatty acid binding protein 4 (1:200), and translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (1:300). Cells were incubated with the secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 647 Invitrogen) for 1 hour at room temperature, and nuclei were counterstained with 5 μg/mL DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). For PLIN1 (1:200) staining permeabilization was performed using 0.1% saponin, which was present during the entire staining process. For bone section imaging after decalcification in 0.5M EDTA (3 weeks), bones were rehydrated in 2% polyvinylpyrrolidone (Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at 4°C, and embedded in Tissue-Tek at optimum cutting temperature (Sakura). A list of all antibodies can be found in supplemental Table 1. The confocal quantitative image cytometer CQ1 (Yokogawa) or the Cytation 5 (Biotek) were used for image acquisition, and quantification was performed using Image J software.

Bulk RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was isolated with the TRIzol-chloroform (5:1) method and recovered by alcohol precipitation in the presence of 50 μg/mL of glycogen (Ambion, Thermo Fisher). Library preparations and next-generation sequencing were performed by Novogene (Munich, Germany). For further details, see the supplemental Section.

FastQC (version 0.12.1) was used to assess the quality of FastQ files. Mapping was conducted with STAR’s “on the fly,” with the GRCm39.112 mouse reference genome. Mapped reads were analyzed with DESeq2.32 Gene set enrichment analysis was performed on the preranked gene lists against the gene sets of the Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes database with clusterProfiler.33

Statistical analysis

Each data set is presented with mean ± standard deviation between independent biological replicates performed in at least 2 repetitions. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (10.4.1). Normal distribution of each data set was assessed to determine whether to use parametric or nonparametric tests. In case of normal distribution, differences between groups were assessed by Student t test. When multiple hypotheses were tested, 1-way or 2-way analysis of variance was used. The Tukey, Šidák, or Fisher least significant difference (each comparison stands alone) tests were used as post hoc tests. When at least 1 data set was not normally distributed, differences were assessed using either the Friedman or the Mann-Whitney test. Survival curves were analyzed by log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. P values ≤ .05 were considered significant.

Results

LO released by BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL cells alter the phenotype of MSC

To characterize LO released by B-ALL cells, we isolated large EVs from the BM of healthy wild-type mice or from mice with BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL by differential centrifugation, as described previously.24,34 A bead-based multiplex assay revealed the presence of a specific CD63+ population of large EVs in the BM of mice with BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL (supplemental Figure 1A-B). These EVs were positive for a number of B-cell markers, including CD19 and CD40,35 as well as for CD2 that has been reported to be expressed on B cells35 (supplemental Figure 1A). In addition, immunoblot analysis detected the LO markers HSPA5 and HSP6024,26,27,34 and the large EV marker Annexin A236 in these EVs (supplemental Figure 1B). The CD63+ CD19+ large EVs were specifically found in the BM of B-ALL mice, but not in healthy controls (Figure 1A). Furthermore, purification via gradient centrifugation24,27,34 led to an enrichment of HSPA5+ larger EVs (LO; supplemental Figure 1C-E) and confirmed previous reports on LO being enriched in the large EV population.34

We next tested the effects of leukemia cell–released LO on primary MSC isolated from the BM of healthy wild-type mice4 as a model of BMM cells. We labeled BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL–derived LO with the membrane dye PKH26 and exposed MSC for various times and demonstrated a time-dependent increase in PKH26-LO+ MSC (Figure 1B). LO uptake was inhibited by performing the experiment at 4°C or by inhibiting dynamin 2 activity with dynasore-OH (supplemental Figure 1F), indicating a specific endosomal uptake as previously suggested.27

We then assessed the effect of BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL–derived LO (from here on referred to as B-ALL–derived LO) uptake on the phenotype of MSC (Figure 1C). After 48 hours of incubation, LO-primed MSC displayed an increased ability to form fibroblast colony forming units (CFU; Figure 1D) and to differentiate into adipocytes (Figure 1E). Further analysis of transcription factors involved in the negative (Tcf4) and positive (Pparg and Cebpa) regulation of adipocyte differentiation37 revealed that exposure of MSC to B-ALL–derived LO led to decreased expression of Tcf4, whereas Pparg and Cebpa transcript levels were increased (Figure 1F; supplemental Figure 1H). Consistent with increased predisposition to adipocyte differentiation we detected higher protein levels of the fatty acid binding protein 4 (Figure 1G), another positive regulator of adipocyte differentiation,11,37 in LO-primed MSC. Finally, analysis of BM sections from mice with BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL showed an increase in PLIN1-positive (PLIN1+) cells, considered cells of adipocytic lineage,38,39 compared with healthy mice (Figure 1H), suggesting changes in adipocyte differentiation in vivo. Notably, the increase in PLIN1+ cells was already visible 12 days after the induction of B-ALL (supplemental Figure 1J-K). These data showed that exposure of MSC to B-ALL–derived LO induced a shift toward a proadipogenic phenotype. However, LO-primed MSC did not spontaneously differentiate into adipocytes but required an additional stimulus for differentiation.

B-ALL–derived LO increase the antioxidant capacity of target cells

To further define the changes induced in target cells, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of MSC exposed to vehicle or B-ALL–derived LO. Pathway analysis revealed the enrichment of a number of pathways associated with cell cycle (Figure 2A-B). This suggested that LO-primed MSC might acquire proliferative advantage over naïve MSC as also indicated by the increased ability to form CFU (Figure 1D). The enrichment of the 1 carbon pool by folate and glutathione metabolism pathways (Figure 2C-D) indicated an increase in antioxidant capability in MSC exposed to B-ALL–derived LO, which may further underline the acquisition of a survival advantage. Additional analysis revealed that LO-primed MSC showed lower levels of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS; Figure 2E) as well as of mitochondrial superoxide (Figure 2F); the latter was not the result of altered mitochondrial function (Figure 2G-H). In parallel, we observed reduced levels of the reduced-to-oxidized glutathione (glutathione/oxidized glutathione) ratio (Figure 2I-J) and increased levels of intracellular lactate (Figure 2K) in LO-primed MSC. Glutathione acts as an important antioxidant agent within the cells. Similarly, lactate has been proposed to contribute to balancing redox levels within cells.40,41 In summary, these results suggest that B-ALL–derived LO increase the antioxidant capacity of target cells, which may increase their ability to survive in a ROS rich environment.42

MSC conditioning by LO regulates IGF1 availability

To further characterize the effects of B-ALL–derived LO on target MSC, we performed secretome profiling of LO-primed MSC. Several chemokines and cytokines were detected at different levels in MSC exposed to B-ALL–derived LO (Figure 3A). These include the chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2) and CCL22 whose concentration was increased in the conditioned medium (CM) of LO-primed MSC (Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 2A for statistical comparison). CCL2 and CCL22 secretion has been reported to be increased in MSC exposed to B-ALL cells43 and it has been associated with the establishment of an immunosuppressive environment.44 LO-primed MSC also secreted higher levels of CCL21 (Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 2A for statistical comparison). The chemokine receptor type 7, the receptor for CCL21, has been reported to be expressed on pre–B-ALL cells and mediate migration.45 Consistently, we observed increased migration of BCR-ABL1+ BA/F3 toward LO-primed MSC (Figure 3B). The effect was reverted when the uptake of the LO was inhibited by dynasore-OH treatment (Figure 3B).

We also tested the secretome of adipocytes after differentiation from MSC primed with vehicle or B-ALL–derived LO. Consistent with an increased ability to differentiate into adipocytes, we detected higher levels of several adipokines in the CM from adipocytes differentiated from LO-primed vs non-primed MSC (Figure 3C; supplemental Figure 2B for statistical comparison). CXCL13 and CXCL16 were also detected at higher levels in the CM of adipocytes originating from LO-primed MSC (Figure 3C; supplemental Figure 2C for statistical comparison). These chemokines have been shown to support BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL progression.8,46

Notably, several members of the IGF-binding protein (IGFBP) family were detected at reduced levels in the CM of MSC exposed to B-ALL LO (Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 2D for statistical comparison). Reduced secretion of IGFBP2 and 6 was maintained upon differentiation into adipocytes (Figure 3C; supplemental Figure 2E for statistical comparison). IGFBP proteins bind and restrict the bioavailability of IGF1 for the IGF1 receptor (IGF1R).47 We have recently shown that IGF1R is specifically upregulated on the surface of BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL cells and that these cells are sensitive to variation in the availability of IGF1.4 We therefore tested the hypothesis that IGFBPs also contribute to the regulation of IGF1/IGF1R-induced proliferation of BCR-ABL1+ BA/F3 cells, which were used as model for BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL.4,48 After 48 hours of culture, we observed that both IGFBP5 and IGFBP6 reverted the stimulation of proliferation by IGF1 (Figure 3D). IGFBP5 and IGFBP6 alone did not affect the growth of BCR-ABL1+ BA/F3 cells (Figure 3D), excluding effects unrelated to IGF1 signaling.31,49 Priming of MSC with B-ALL–derived LO also resulted in reduced secretion of the plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI-1), an effect that was maintained after induction of differentiation into adipocytes (Figure 3A,C,E for statistical comparison). PAI-1 is an inhibitor of the fibrinolytic system, which we recently proposed to be involved in B-ALL progression via regulation of IGF1 availability through remodeling of the ECM.4 Interestingly, gene set enrichment analysis of the transcriptome of LO-primed MSC compared with control MSC revealed an enrichment of the fibrinolytic signature (Figure 3F). Taken together, our findings suggest that conditioning of MSC by B-ALL–derived LO contributes to the regulation of the IGF1 activity within the BMM.

MSC reprogramming by B-ALL–derived LO supports leukemia growth

In a coculture system, we next tested whether MSC primed by B-ALL–derived LO support B-ALL growth. The growth of empty vector–transduced BA/F3 cells on MSC or adipocytes differentiated from primed MSC was not influenced by conditioning with B-ALL–derived LO (Figure 4A). In contrast, BCR-ABL1+ BA/F3 cells showed an increased proliferation rate when cultured on top of MSC previously exposed to B-ALL–derived LO and even more so on adipocytes after induction of differentiation from LO-primed MSC (Figure 4A). The finding that B-ALL–derived LO–primed MSC and adipocytes only stimulated BCR-ABL1+ BA/F3 cells, which show a greater than twofold increase in IGF1R expression,4 further supports the conclusion that LO priming promotes proliferation through the regulation of IGF1 bioavailability. This interpretation is in line with published evidence that the IGF1/IGF1R pathway is a dominant proliferative signaling factor in B-ALL.4 To further test this hypothesis, we added either IGFBP6 or linsitinib, an IGF1R inhibitor that prevents the proproliferative effect of IGF1 (supplemental Figure 3A), to our coculture system. Both treatments reverted the proliferation inducing effects of LO-primed MSC (Figure 4B-C).

Additional experiments showed that MSC preconditioned with B-ALL–derived LO supported the growth of primary BCR-ABL1+ B-cell progenitors cultured in limiting dilution.4,50 Cells cultured on top of conditioned MSC reached the 1 × 106 cell cutoff faster and more frequently than the cells cultured on top of vehicle-treated MSC (supplemental Figure 3B). Competitive proliferation experiments in which empty vector BA/F3 and BCR-ABL1+ BA/F3 were combined at different ratios and cultured on top of adipocytes provided additional evidence that LO conditioning specifically enhanced the growth of BCR-ABL1–transformed B-ALL cells (supplemental Figure 3C-D). Adipocytes have been implicated in disease relapse by maintaining B-ALL cells in a more immature state.11,51 Consistently, BCR-ABL1+ BA/F3 cells cultured on top of adipocytes differentiated from MSC and primed with B-ALL–derived LO yielded more pre-B lymphoid progenitor cell CFU (Figure 4D).

We next evaluated whether BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL–derived LO educated the BMM in vivo.

BM MSC were isolated 48 hours after IV injection of either vehicle or B-ALL–derived LO into healthy mice (Figure 4E). Gene expression analysis revealed that MSC from mice preconditioned with B-ALL–derived LO expressed lower levels of Serpine1 (PAI-1; Figure 4F) and a trend toward reduced levels of Igfbp6 (supplemental Figure 3E). Furthermore, these MSC showed an increased tendency to differentiate into adipocytes (supplemental Figure 3F). Consistent with our in vitro observation, BCR-ABL1+ BA/F3 cells proliferated more when cultured on top of MSC from preconditioned mice and even more so on adipocytes after induction of differentiation (Figure 4G). In agreement with LO-primed MSC being involved in the modulation of IGF1 availability via regulation of the fibrinolytic system, BCR-ABL1+ BA/F3 cells showed an increased proliferation upon plasmin-dependent release of IGF1 from Matrigel containing MSC4 isolated from mice preconditioned with B-ALL–derived LO (Figure 4H).

Given the altered phenotype of MSC exposed to B-ALL–derived LO, we injected healthy mice with B-ALL–derived LO before transplantation with B-ALL–initiating cells 48 hours later. Preconditioning with BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL–derived LO led to acceleration of B-ALL compared with vehicle-treated mice (Figure 4I), although there was only a trend toward increased tumor burden in these mice (Figure 4J). Furthermore, total BM cells isolated from preconditioned mice showed an increased ability to form pre-B lymphoid progenitor cell CFU (Figure 4K).

Interestingly, polymorphonuclear–myeloid derived suppressor cells (MDSC) were increased in the BM of mice preconditioned with BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL–derived LO compared with vehicle-treated mice (Figure 4L). MDSC contribute to tumor progression via suppression of the immune response.52 Therefore, an increase of MDSC is consistent with our observation that MSC primed with B-ALL–derived LO may contribute to establishment of an immunosuppressive environment via release of cytokines such as CCL2 and CCL22 (Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 2A). However, preconditioning with B-ALL–derived LO did not lead to a significant increase in polymorphonuclear-MDSC (supplemental Figure 3G), suggesting that recruitment or expansion of these cells may require the presence of leukemia cells. In summary, our observations indicate that BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL cells condition their microenvironment toward a tumor-supportive phenotype through the release of LO.

LO derived from human BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL cells reprogram hMSC for leukemia subtype-specific support

We next evaluated the applicability of our findings from mouse models to human disease. LO isolated from the BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL cell line SUPB15 were used to prime primary hMSC. We observed that the proadipogenic transcription factors, PPARG and CEBPA, were upregulated in primed hMSC (Figure 5A). Consistently, hMSC exposed to SUPB15-derived LO showed increased ability to differentiate into adipocytes upon stimulation (Figure 5B-C). To test whether SUPB15-derived LO educate the BMM toward a tumor-supportive phenotype, we cocultured different leukemia cell lines with hMSC primed with SUPB15-derived LO and after induction of adipocyte differentiation. Coculture with hMSC differentiated into adipocytes resulted in a trend toward increased proliferation across all the cell lines tested (Figure 5D-E). However, the priming with SUPB15-derived LO further stimulated the growth only of SUPB15 cells (Figure 5E). Conversely, we observed that hMSC, but not adipocytes, primed with LO isolated from the MLL-AF9+ acute myeloid leukemia cell line THP1, specifically supported the growth of THP1 cells (Figure 5F) and not of the other leukemia cell lines (Figure 5F-G). Finally, analysis of BM sections from NOD scid gamma mice injected with SUPB15 showed an increase in PLIN1+ cells compared with healthy mice (Figure 5H). Together, these data show that LO derived from human B-ALL cells reprogram the target cells toward a tumor supportive phenotype. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the effects of LO on target cells may be oncogene and/or leukemia type-specific.

Discussion

In this study, we detected a population of CD63+ CD19+ LO that appears during the development of murine BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL. These EVs were not present in the BM of healthy mice. LO obtained from the BM of mice with B-ALL affected the phenotype of target MSC by increasing their antioxidant capacity and their ability to differentiate into adipocytes. Furthermore, MSC primed with B-ALL–derived LO preferentially supported the growth of B-ALL cells via regulation of IGF1/IGF1R signaling, which is important in BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL cells.4 Consistently, in the B-ALL preclinical model, preconditioning with B-ALL–derived LO led to accelerated disease progression.

In B-ALL, adipocytes support chemotherapy resistance and disease progression.11,53, 54, 55 Although adipocytes contribute to the late stages of B-ALL progression, an early reprogramming of MSC exposed to B-ALL cells toward the adipocyte lineage has also been demonstrated.11 Our data implicate priming of MSC by LO as one of the critical hits that stimulates the differentiation of MSC into adipocytes with a specific secretory profile, which supports B-ALL growth. LO priming of MSC likely contributes to adipocyte differentiation through multiple pathways. Tight regulation of oxidative stress has been implicated in adipocyte differentiation, with moderate levels of ROS being necessary for MSC to differentiate into adipocytes56 and exceedingly elevated levels of ROS being counterproductive.57 Our experiments show that conditioning of MSC with B-ALL–derived LO leads to an increase in their antioxidant capacity, which may provide them with the ability to survive a prooxidative environment.

Several studies reported that adipocytes increase chemotherapy resistance in B-ALL and suggested that adipocytes reduce B-ALL cell proliferation.11,53, 54, 55 On the contrary, our experiments demonstrate that adipocytes originating from MSC exposed to B-ALL–derived LO support both proliferation and CFU ability of B-ALL cells. This might be attributed to the observed increased secretion of CXCL13, which has recently been proposed to support B-ALL progression.8 In summary, the contribution of adipocytes to B-ALL may be more complex than what has been reported and vary depending on the oncogene involved. Notably, according to the leukemia subtype, the changes in the secretory profile of adipocytes rather than in the adipocyte numbers may affect B-ALL progression.

The IGF1/IGF1R axis is an important signal for B-ALL progression. Activation of the fibrinolytic system results in plasmin-mediated remodeling of the ECM and leads to release of IGF1 and specifically supports B-ALL progression.4 The data presented here not only suggest that B-ALL may use LO as a means to potentiate the education of the BMM toward activation of the fibrinolysis-mediated release of IGF1, but our findings identify an additional layer of regulation of IGF1, that is, the modulation of secretion of IGFBP proteins. These proteins are important regulators of the half-life and availability of IGF1 and IGF2, because they bind and sequester IGF1, thereby preventing its interaction with IGF1R.31,49 LO suppress IGFBP secretion in MSC and therefore they may increase the pool of available IGF1. Interestingly, IGFBP5 has been shown to have ECM binding sites,58 suggesting a possible link between the ECM-remodeling dependent and IGFBP-mediated regulation of IGF1 availability. Overall, these data further confirm that IGF1-mediated signaling in BCR-ABL1+ B-ALL is a predominant pathway for cell survival and provide additional understanding of the different strategies used by leukemia cells to activate it. Hence, IGFBP-mediated regulation of IGF1 may act in combination with inhibition of the fibrinolytic system, which remodels the ECM and may successfully hinder B-ALL progression.

In conclusion, these data provide initial evidence that B-ALL cells release LO to educate their microenvironment to benefit themselves. Although there have been a few reports describing a role for EVs in B-ALL communication with the BMM,15, 16, 17, 18, 19,30 our data suggest that LO-mediated reprogramming may affect several aspects of B-ALL progression and increase the appeal of targeting this type of interaction for the development of novel adjuvant therapeutic strategies.
