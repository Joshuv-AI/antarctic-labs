Anti-Leucine-Rich Glioma-Inactivated 1 Encephalitis Presenting with An Atypical, Protracted Course of Cognitive Decline
Authors: Su Min Park, Jung-Ju Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autoimmune encephalitis typically presents with an acute or subacute onset of cognitive decline, altered mental status, and seizures. Here, we report a case of anti-leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 encephalitis characterized by an atypically protracted period of cognitive decline, followed by the delayed onset of seizures. We describe the clinical characteristics, unique neuroimaging findings, and the patient’s response to treatment. By discussing the implications of this unusual clinical trajectory, we aim to enhance diagnostic awareness and prevent potential misdiagnosis in cases with atypically prolonged presentations.

Introduction

Multiple conditions may cause progressive cognitive decline, such as strokes, prion diseases, infections, degenerative dementia, or toxic-metabolic encephalopathy.1–3 Autoimmune encephalitis (AIE) is usually characterized by an acute or subacute onset of cognitive decline, altered mental status, and seizures.4 When AIE presents with isolated cognitive decline, i.e., absent of seizures or altered mental status, it poses a significant diagnostic challenge. This report describes a patient with anti-leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 (LGI1) encephalitis characterized by an atypical, protracted course of cognitive decline followed by the delayed onset of seizures. We aim to highlight the clinical features and implications of such cases to prevent potential misdiagnosis.

Case Report

A 57-year-old male presented to the Emergency Department with cognitive impairment and a seizure. He had a 10-year history of hypertension, managed with amlodipine (5 mg) and valsartan (80 mg) daily, but no other chronic illnesses. On the day of admission, he was found sitting on the toilet with his trousers on, making moaning sounds for 20 minutes, followed by a 1-minute generalized tonic-clonic seizure, accompanied by subsequent post-ictal confusion.

He had insidiously developed cognitive decline about 6 months prior, characterized by worsening memory, confusion, and delusions (e.g., claiming to have lost a wallet he never owned). A month before his visit, he lost the ability to operate a forklift, which was essential to his job; consequently, he was transferred to an office position. He had previously visited other institutions for progressive cognitive decline; however, despite neuroimaging including brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), a definitive diagnosis could not be established. The day before admission, his wife noticed him staring blankly with glassy eyes and moaning for about a minute.

On neurological examination, the patient was alert but disoriented to time, place, and person. No other focal neurological deficits were observed. Laboratory findings showed hyponatremia (serum sodium 125 mmol/L; reference range: 135–145 mmol/L) and hypochloremia (serum chloride 85 mmol/L; reference range: 98–110 mmol/L) and hyperglycemia (serum glucose 260 mg/dL; reference range: 74–106 mg/dL). Initial Korean mini-mental state examination (K-MMSE) score was 20; sub-item analysis revealed impaired performance in memory recall (0/3), time orientation (2/5), place orientation (4/5), and attention and calculation (2/5). The patient also scored 0/3 on cued recall, indicating a significant memory deficit. Laboratory analysis revealed a serum osmolality of 274 mOsm/kg (reference value: 275–295 mOsm/kg) and a urine osmolality of 447 mOsm/kg (reference value: 390–1,090 mOsm/kg). The urinary sodium concentration was measured at 127 mmol/L. Clinical history provided by the patient and caregiver indicated no significant weight fluctuations throughout the course of the illness, suggesting a euvolemic state.

According to these clinical findings, prion disease or slowly progressive encephalitis was suspected and we performed contrast-enhanced brain MRI, including diffusion-weighted imaging, electroencephalography (EEG), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis. Brain MRI revealed hyperintensities in the right hippocampus and bilateral insular cortex on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) imaging, without evidence of diffusion restriction or contrast enhancement. Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) maps showed high signal intensities in the bilateral insular cortex (Fig. 1). The CSF showed an absence of pleocytosis and a slightly elevated protein level (34.4 mg/dL; reference range: 8.0–32.0 mg/dL). EEG revealed no specific abnormality.

Based on the initial findings, high-dose steroid pulse therapy (1.0 g daily methylprednisolone) was initiated. However, due to the onset of steroid-induced severe hyperglycemia (>300 mg/dL) on the second day, the dose was reduced to 500 mg daily and maintained for the remaining 4 days of the course. To control the seizures, a loading dose of 1.0 g levetiracetam was administered, followed by a maintenance dose of 500 mg twice daily. During these treatments, no further generalized seizures occurred; however, brief facial grimacing for several seconds followed by coprolalia (involuntary swearing) persisted with a frequency of more than five episodes per day. The serum sodium concentration was measured at 132 mmol/L following free water restriction.

The patient was discharged against medical advice after receiving initial steroid and anti-seizure treatment due to financial constraints. However, 1 week after discharge, the patient revisited the neurology outpatient clinic due to persistent memory impairment and behavioral symptoms, including delusions and hyperphagia. After discharge, neither facial grimacing nor coprolalia was further observed by the caregivers.

The first follow-up K-MMSE showed an improved score of 24, though it still revealed persistent impairments in memory recall (0/3; cued recall 0/3) and time orientation (2/5). Korean Montreal Cognitive Assessment (K-MoCA) score was 19. Seoul verbal learning test (SVLT) demonstrated significant impairments across multiple domains: immediate recall (raw score: 17; z-score: −1.39), delayed recall (raw score: 0; z-score: −3.40), recognition (raw score: 12; z-score: −5.79), and the recognition discrimination index (raw score: 50; z-score: −5.79). Following readmission, the patient received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy at a dosage of 0.4 g/kg daily for five consecutive days. Brain 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) revealed diffuse hypermetabolism in the left hippocampus and bilateral basal ganglia (Fig. 2). Anti-LGI1 antibodies were detected in both serum and CSF from the samples obtained during the previous evaluation. The 14-3-3 protein was negative. During the readmission period, no clinical seizure nor abnormal movement was observed. Video-EEG monitoring was declined by the patient due to financial constraints. Following the completion of IVIG treatment, the patient was discharged.

Following discharge, the patient’s cognitive function showed no significant improvement; K-MMSE and K-MoCA scores, performed at the 4-week follow-up after discharge, were 23 (memory recall 1/3; time orientation 1/5) and 19, respectively. The SVLT continued to indicate severe impairment across multiple domains: immediate recall (raw score: 16; z-score: −1.63), delayed recall (raw score: 1; z-score: −2.96), recognition (raw score: 13; z-score: −5.20), and the recognition discrimination index (raw score: 54.17; z-score: −5.15). No facial grimacing nor coprolalia was observed during follow-up period. Due to financial constraints, the patient and his caregivers declined further treatment.

Discussion

In presenile patients presenting with progressive cognitive decline, prion diseases such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) must be considered in the differential diagnosis.2 However, as CJD remains untreatable, it is crucial to prioritize the identification of reversible etiologies, such as AIE. In our case, the relatively prolonged clinical course (6-month duration) and the delayed onset of altered consciousness and seizures were atypical for AIE, which consequently posed a diagnostic challenge and led to a delay in diagnosis. The median diagnostic latency, defined as the interval from initial symptoms to diagnosis, in patients with AIE is 50 days (range, 5–150 days).5

While hyponatremia is a well-recognized diagnostic clue for anti-LGI1 encephalitis, it is often a nonspecific finding. Therefore, other potential etiologies must be systematically considered, including underlying medical conditions (e.g., congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease, or polydipsia), syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), and drug-induced effects from diuretics or anti-seizure medications.6 The patient’s laboratory profile-characterized by low serum osmolality, normal to slightly high urine osmolality, and elevated urine sodium in a clinically euvolemic state-is highly suggestive of SIADH.7 This finding aligns with a previous study indicating that hyponatremia in patients with LGI1 encephalitis is primarily attributed to SIADH.8

Neuroimaging is instrumental in the diagnostic process. According to a previous study,9 T2 and/or FLAIR hyperintensities without diffusion restriction or contrast enhancement are key features distinguishing LGI1 encephalitis from other etiologies,10 such as viral encephalitis or CJD. Consistent with these findings, our patient exhibited neither diffusion restriction nor contrast enhancement. Notably, bilateral high signal intensities in the insular cortex were also observed, representing another unique neuroimaging hallmark in this case. The presence of high signal intensity on the ADC map suggests vasogenic edema (VE). Bilateral insular involvement is a rare finding, reported in only 2.5% of LGI1 encephalitis case.11 This VE may be driven by mechanisms similar to posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES). While the association between AIE and PRES remains under-documented, it is plausible that pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 lead to endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress, thereby contributing to the development of VE in this context.12,13

The facial grimacing and coprolalia observed in our patient could be interpreted as faciobrachial dystonic seizures14-a pathognomonic feature of LGI1 encephalitis-or potentially a tic disorder. Unfortunately, these episodes were not captured by video-EEG monitoring. Tic disorders have been documented in AIE, possibly secondary to basal ganglia involvement.15 Following steroid and anti-seizure medication, the clinical symptom resolved completely.

Prompt and exact diagnosis is vital for patients with progressive cognitive decline. Reversible causes like AIE must be excluded first. Diagnostic protocols should include serum electrolyte testing; moreover, MRI and FDG-PET imaging, in conjunction with CSF analysis, are vital for achieving a definitive diagnosis. Despite the atypical and protracted nature of our patient’s disease course, AIE should not be overlooked. It is crucial to maintain a high index of suspicion for AIE in the differential diagnosis, regardless of the duration of symptoms.
