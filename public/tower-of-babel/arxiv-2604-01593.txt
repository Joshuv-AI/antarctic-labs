Nonparametric regression of spatio-temporal data using infinite-dimensional covariates
Subhrajyoty Roy, Soudeep Deb, Sayar Karmakar, Rishideep Roy

Abstract
In spatio-temporal analysis, we often record data at specific time intervals but with varying spatial locations between these timepoints. We propose a conditional model to analyze such spatio-temporal data that accommodates the dependencies alongside second-order stationary explanatory variables, which may be infinite-dimensional and accommodate spatio-temporal covariates. Because of the absence of a mixing-type dependence condition in this case, which is typically required by the existing studies, we consider a weaker polynomially decaying moment contraction (PMC) condition on the covariates. In this paper, we obtain nonparametric point estimates of the mean and covariate functions of such a regression model, which we then show to be statistically consistent. We also obtain a simultaneous confidence interval of the mean function using the central limit theorem for the proposed estimator. Such simultaneous inference tools can be used to test for certain specifications of the mean function. Some simulation studies and two real-data analyses have been illustrated to corroborate the findings.



Keywords:
Simultaneous confidence bands; Spatio-temporal process; Kernel estimation; Infinite dimensional regression

1
Introduction

Spatiotemporal datasets increasingly arise from monitoring systems whose sampling designs evolve over time. At each timepoint, measurements are recorded at a collection of spatial sites that can change as sensors fail, new sensors are deployed, or access constraints vary. At the same time, analysts often observe explanatory information aggregated at the temporal level (e.g., meteorological fields, land-use surfaces, remote-sensing products, GPS tracking, or historical records), whose natural representation is functional or otherwise high or possibly infinite-dimensional. These features, namely irregular time-stamps, time-varying spatial sampling locations, and infinite-dimensional temporal-level covariates, create a setting in which classical spatiotemporal methods and existing functional regression approaches do not directly deliver nonparametric inference on the conditional mean and volatility surfaces.

To better understand this framework, as the first practical use-case, we showcase our inference strategy on an air pollution dataset from New Delhi, the capital of India. This dataset exhibits irregularly spaced observations of
\mathrm{PM}2.5
(airborne particles with a diameter smaller than 2.5 micrometers), with missing data across time due to inactivity of sensors: see
Figure

1
which shows this missingness. Naturally, it demands an infinite-dimensional covariate process due to the growing nature of historical observations and factors like measurements of other pollutants, weather patterns observed in a different set but nearby locations, etc., thus highlighting the need for a model that allows for infinite-dimensional covariates.

:

\mathrm{PM}2.5
observations across locations (columns) and timepoints (rows).

For the second motivating example, we turn our attention to a very different context that stems from the analysis of shots in soccer. In
Figure

2
, the locations of all shots taken in the matches against a specific team (Chelsea) by three different teams (Arsenal, Liverpool, and Manchester City) are illustrated. It is clear that the shots are irregularly distributed over the entire playing field, with no fixed spatial design. One may aim to analyze the quality of a shot, commonly measured through expected goals or xG
(
Mead et al. 2023
)
, based on various contextual variables over the season. These covariates are naturally indexed over time, and accumulate as the season progresses, yielding a high (or infinite) dimensional structure. Together, the irregular spatial sampling of shot locations and the complex temporal evolution of contextual information pose challenges for standard spatial or functional regression methods.

To define the mathematical framework for similar applications, let
Y_{t}(s)\in\mathbb{R}
denote the observed response at time
t
and location
s\in\mathcal{S}
, and let
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
be a second-order stationary covariate taking values in an abstract space
\chi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
. While we only keep the time index in the subscript to focus on the temporal evolution, we emphasize that
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
, being infinite-dimensional, can accommodate spatio-temporal covariates and lagged responses by including explanatory variables across all locations. We formulate the conditional regression model

Y_{t}(s)=\mu(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)+\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)\,\epsilon_{t}(s).

(1)

where the timepoints
t
index the temporal horizon
\mathcal{T}
and locations
s
index the spatial horizon
\mathcal{S}
which is considered to be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In real-life problems,
\mathcal{S}
is often a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, depending on the dataset at hand. Without loss of generality, following the principles of in-fill asymptotics, we can assume
\mathcal{T}=[0,1]
and
\mathcal{S}=[0,1]^{d}
for a fixed
d
after suitably scaling the temporal and spatial domains. The mean function
\mu(\cdot,\cdot)
and the covariance function
\sigma(\cdot,\cdot)
are both defined on
\chi\times\mathcal{S}
. They are assumed to be measurable and sufficiently smooth. The error component
\epsilon_{t}(s)
in (
1
) captures idiosyncratic variation with a nontrivial spatial correlation. Specifically, as temporal dependence is entirely captured by the covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
, we consider
\epsilon_{t}(s)
to be temporally independent and identically distributed (iid) random variables satisfying
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{t}(s))=0
. They are also independent of the past covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}}
for any
t^{\prime}\leqslant t
and for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. These will be discussed in detail in
Section

2.1
.

Although the framework (
1
) models the response variable
Y_{t}(s)
for all timepoints
t\in\mathcal{T}
and spatial points
s\in\mathcal{S}
, in practice, the continuous curves are not typically observed. We assume that the data are collected at some irregularly spaced timepoints and at some specific spatial locations that may differ from one timepoint to another. Keeping that in view, we shall represent the dataset with observations from a total of
n
timepoints as

\mathcal{D}=\left\{\left(t_{i},s_{t_{i}j},\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}},Y_{t_{i}}(s_{t_{i}j})\right):0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{n-1}<t_{n}=1,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n_{i}\}\right\},

(2)

where the
i^{th}
timepoint
t_{i}
observes data from
n_{i}
locations. The goal is to draw nonparametric inference about
\mu(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\sigma(\cdot,\cdot)
given
\mathcal{D}
, and consequently, use the model (
1
) to make predictions of the response function at unknown locations or at a future timepoint.

Our model framework can be regarded as a significant generalization of the conventional stochastic time series regression model
(
Zhao & Wu 2008
, see, e.g.,)
in two ways. First, we incorporate the infinite-dimensional covariate
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
into the model. Second, we extend the time series framework to a spatiotemporal framework, allowing a non-trivial spatial dependency structure. Of course, it provides an extremely general setting, and includes the popular autoregressive setups (e.g. AR, ARCH) by letting
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
to be a spatially aggregated function of the lagged response values. It is easy to note that allowing
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
to be infinite-dimensional automatically includes the finite-dimensional case of the covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some fixed
d>0
, by choosing the coordinates of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
to be equal to
0
beyond the fixed dimension
d
.

It is imperative to note that the existing literature addresses only fragments of our setting. Functional regression in an iid setting may accommodate infinite-dimensional covariates but not temporal dependence; functional time series methods accommodate dependence but typically assume regular time grids and do not treat spatially indexed responses observed on time-varying location sets; and nonparametric spatiotemporal regression focuses on finite-dimensional covariates and regular grids. We bridge this research gap and develop frequentist nonparametric estimation and inference for the mean and volatility surfaces in (
1
) under the irregular design (
2
) with possibly infinite-dimensional temporal-level covariates. Our key contribution in this regard is discussed in more detail next, in light of some existing literature.

1.1
Existing literature and our contribution

In functional data analysis, models similar to (
1
) have been studied to some extent, where an infinite-dimensional covariate process is used to model the response variable. The readers are referred to
Ferraty & Vieu 2003
,
Ferraty & Vieu 2004
for a general treatment of univariate responses, and
Xiang et al. 2013
,
Omar & Wang 2019
for a multivariate response setup. However, all these approaches consider each datapoint to be iid without any regard to the temporal dependence. Recently,
Li & Yang 2023
addressed this gap by considering a temporally dependent functional data setup, but the observations are presented at regular intervals of time and space. We relax that by allowing the dataset (
2
) to have irregularly spaced points in time and varying locations in space.

Nonlinear spatiotemporal regression model is far less studied compared to the extensive literature on the nonlinear analysis of time series or the literature on spatial models
(
Cressie & Wikle 2011
)
. Much success of spatiotemporal modeling has instead come from a Bayesian point of view
(see
Haining & Li 2020
, for a detailed review)
. Among the frequentist viewpoint through nonparametric estimation,
Wang & Wang 2009
solved a related problem of mean (or trend) estimation using a local linear estimation technique. A completely nonparametric estimation was attempted by
Yang & Qiu 2018
for modeling data on incidence rates of diseases. While both studies considered regularly spaced timepoints, the case for irregularly spaced timepoints was investigated by
Al-Sulami et al. 2017
, who proposed a semi-parametric approach. However, these studies did not allow the covariate process to be infinite-dimensional. On that note, perhaps the closest article to our approach is the work by
Hong & Linton 2020
, who considered infinite-dimensional covariates but only in a time series setup.

While the existing works on nonparametric modeling of spatiotemporal data and functional data analysis with infinite-order regression have grown into independent sets of literature, our proposed model (
1
) aims to bring a novel perspective by combining these two ideas. By bridging these, it allows us to efficiently estimate the trend function using even irregularly spaced observations in space and time, which had only limited attention in the past. Furthermore, we completely avoid any mixing-type assumption, which is a standard practice to model data dependence across space and time. In fact,
Hong & Linton 2020
illustrated that, since
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
is infinite-dimensional, it is quite possible that it contains infinite order lags of some exogenous variable, say
\boldsymbol{Z}_{t}
such that
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}\boldsymbol{Z}_{t-j}
for some scalars
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. This is not
\alpha
-mixing in general. To avoid such scenarios, they considered a near-epoch dependence (NED) condition on the covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
, but still require an
\alpha
-mixing condition on
(Y_{t},\boldsymbol{Z}_{t})
jointly. However, we show that it is possible to completely avoid any mixing-type conditions and obtain valid inference under a polynomially-decaying moment contraction (PMC) assumption. It is much weaker than the standard geometric moment contraction (GMC) condition, and encompasses various processes which are not
\alpha
or
\beta
-mixing
(see
Chen et al. 2016
, for a counter-example)
. Our requirement is more general than NED conditions, and can be easier to work with due to lesser restrictions on the moments
(
Shao & Wu 2007
, Remark 5.1,)
. As a result, in addition to allowing stronger than geometric dependence structure, we are able to derive asymptotic validity results of our proposed estimator under only a second-order stationarity condition on the covariate
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
. This improves upon the requirement of the existence of
(2+\delta)
-order moment (
\delta>0
) for
Y_{t}
and fourth order moment condition for
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
required by the analysis of
Hong & Linton 2020
. In summary, the major contributions of our work are threefold:

(a)

We propose a first-of-its-kind nonlinear regression model with infinite-dimensional covariates for spatiotemporally dependent data and its nonparametric estimation from irregularly sampled observations;

(b)

We replace the mixing-type dependence condition with a functional dependence assumption and allow for significantly slow decay of dependence through PMC assumption.

(c)

We reduce the higher-order moment assumptions, which, in turn, allows for a slightly heavier-tailed distribution of covariates.

The methodology and theoretical results are presented in
Section

2
and
Section

3
below. In
Section

4
, we demonstrate the use of the proposed methodology in the real life dataset of air pollution from India. The paper concludes with some important remarks in
Section

5
. Additional theoretical discussions, proofs, another real life example with soccer data, and a simulation study are deferred to the supplementary material in the interest of space.

1.2
Notations

For better understanding of the technical discussions, we collect some notations that will be used throughout the paper. The sets of real numbers, natural numbers, and integers are denoted respectively by
\mathbb{R},\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Z}
. For two sequences
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
,
a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(b_{n})
denotes that for all sufficiently large
n
,
a_{n}
is bounded between
cb_{n}
and
Cb_{n}
for two positive real constants
c<C<\infty
. The notation
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
indicates that
\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}(a_{n}/b_{n})=0
. Correspondingly,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)
and
o_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)
denote the probabilistic versions, where the analytical convergence and boundedness are respectively replaced by convergence in probability and stochastic boundedness.

2
Nonparametric estimation

2.1
Mathematical Framework

Before we discuss the proposed estimator, it is critical to define all components and conditions of model (
1
). We emphasize, and shall explicate properly as required later, that these conditions do not affect the generality of the spatiotemporal model to a large extent.

As the covariate process
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
is allowed to be infinite-dimensional, the abstract space of its range
\chi
must be well-structured to allow necessary mathematical operations to be performed. We assume that
\chi
is an affine subspace of a weighted
L^{2}
Banach space, namely, for any
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\chi
, the difference
(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\in\chi
, and,
\chi\subseteq\boldsymbol{D}\mathcal{L}^{2}
, where

\boldsymbol{D}\mathcal{L}^{2}=\left\{(z_{1},z_{2},\dots)\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}:\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}^{-2}z_{i}^{2}<\infty\right\}

(3)

for some fixed choice of sequence
\{\zeta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. Let us use
\boldsymbol{D}
to denote an infinite-dimensional linear operator that multiplies the
i^{th}
coordinate by
\zeta_{i}
for all
i
. Then, the above restriction allows one to formally define scaled norms for the infinite-dimensional covariates in
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
, and induce a scaled
L^{2}
metric
\left\|\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right\|
for any
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\chi
. In addition, we consider a PMC condition to model the dependence structure of the covariate process
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
. Specifically, there exists iid random variables
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
and measurable functions
G_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\rightarrow\chi
satisfying

\boldsymbol{X}_{t}=G_{t}\left(\dots,Z_{t-1},Z_{t}\right).

For an independent and iid copy
Z_{i}^{\ast}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, assume that the quantity

\theta_{2}(m)=\sup_{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-G_{t}\left(\dots,Z_{t-m-1},Z^{\ast}_{t-m},Z_{t-m+1},\dots,Z_{t}\right)\right)\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(m^{-\tau}),

(4)

for some
\tau>2
. Equation (
4
) controls the temporal dependence structure through the widely used notion of functional dependence measures
(
Wu 2005
,
Wu 2011
,
Wu & Zhou 2011
,
Karmakar & Wu 2020
, see)
. The PMC condition also implies that
\Delta_{2}(m)=\mathcal{O}(m^{-\tau+1})
, where

\Delta_{2}(m)=\sup_{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-G_{t}(\dots,Z^{\ast}_{t-m-2},Z^{\ast}_{t-m-1},Z^{\ast}_{t-m},Z_{t-m+1},\dots,Z_{t})\right)\right\|.

This is a generalization of
\theta_{2}(m)
that considers the changes in
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
due to the change in all historical values up to a time lag of
m
.

Next, assume that the error terms satisfy a temporal homogeneity condition

\text{Cov}(\epsilon_{t_{1}}(s_{1}),\epsilon_{t_{2}}(s_{2}))=\rho(s_{1},s_{2})=\mathcal{O}\left(\|s_{1}-s_{2}\|^{-(d+\delta)}\right),

(5)

for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
, where
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
is a symmetric function satisfying
\rho(s,s)=1
for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
d
is the dimension of the spatial horizon
\mathcal{S}
.
\delta>0
is some small positive constant. Typically,
\rho(s_{1},s_{2})=\rho^{\ast}(\left\|s_{1}-s_{2}\right\|)
with
\rho^{\ast}(0)=1
, and the decay rate in (
5
) simply restricts the
\rho^{\ast}
to be a polynomially decaying function of its argument. This connection immediately simplifies the conditional variance structure of the data
Y_{t}(s)
by separating it into two parts: one involving temporal correlation governed by the covariate
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
and another involving spatial correlation governed by the
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
function, i.e., we can write
\text{Cov}(Y_{t_{1}}(s_{1}),Y_{t_{2}}(s_{2}))=\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{1}},s_{1})\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{2}},s_{2})\rho(s_{1},s_{2}).

We have also indicated that in model (
1
), the mean and covariance functions are sufficiently smooth. To add some mathematical formalism, we assume that for each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
, both
\mu(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)
and
\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)
are elements of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of functions from
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the inner product
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}f(s)g(s)ds
. The space
\mathcal{H}
can be represented as

\mathcal{H}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}b_{i}(s):a_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|a_{i}|<\infty\right\},

where
\{(b_{i}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a set of countable orthonormal basis functions of
\mathcal{H}
. This suggests a representation of the mean and covariance functions in the form

\mu(\boldsymbol{x},s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s),\;\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s),

(6)

such that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})|
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\sigma_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})|
both exist and are finite for each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
. Such representation has appeared before for spatiotemporal modeling
(
Wikle & Cressie 1999
)
, and it is standard in the functional PCA literature due to Karhunen-Loéve expansion
(
Karhunen 1946
,
Loève 1946
)
, where the basis functions are chosen to correspond with the eigenfunctions of covariance-operator
(
Yao et al. 2005
)
. The basis functions in (
6
) have also been extensively used in dependent data modeling, in the form of bi-square functions
(
Cressie & Johannesson 2008
)
, splines
(
Goldsmith et al. 2012
)
, wavelets
(
Chen & Christensen 2015
)
, etc. We refer to
Cressie et al. 2022
and the references therein for a comprehensive exposition. The particular choice of the basis function often has to be paired with the choice of the hyperparameter
K_{\epsilon}
as in Theorem
2
, to maintain a consistent level of accuracy. In a practical setting, one may use any orthogonal set of basis functions for computational convenience, and the corresponding
K_{\epsilon}
is picked out via a cross-validation approach; see Section
4
for our specific choice.

While this takes care of a smoothness restriction over the spatial locations
\mathcal{S}
, to ensure further smoothness over the set
\chi
, we assume that the component functions
\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\sigma_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, are continuous on
\chi
with respect to the scaled distance metric introduced in (
3
). Moreover, these conditions imposed on
\mu(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\sigma(\cdot,\cdot)
in (
6
) are not usually very restrictive. Since
\mathcal{S}=[0,1]^{d}
is a Hausdorff topological space, and is compact, there exist choices of universal kernel functions such that their linear span is able to uniformly approximate any continuous function
(
Micchelli et al. 2006
)
. Hence, by choosing the basis of the corresponding Hilbert space spanned by those universal kernels, it follows that the assumption of existence of such representations given in (
6
) becomes simply a rephrasing of a continuity-type smoothness condition for
\mu(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)
and
\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)
.

2.2
Estimation methodology

We begin with the nonparametric estimation of the mean component for our nonlinear regression model:
Y_{t}(s)=\mu(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)+\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)\,\epsilon_{t}(s)
. Because the mean function has a decomposition as shown in (
6
), it is enough to estimate the component functions
\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In order to proceed further, we shall need two assumptions about the boundedness of the expected covariate and the error components, given as follows.

(A1)

For each
s\in\mathcal{S}
, define
\tilde{\mu}(s)=\mathbb{E}(|\mu(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},s)|)
and
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(s)=\mathbb{E}(\sigma^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},s))
where the expectations are taken over the marginal distribution of the covariate
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
. Assume that both
\tilde{\mu}(s)
and
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(s)
are continuous and are integrable on
\mathcal{S}
.

(A2)

The first and second order moments of the errors
\epsilon_{t}(s)
are uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\sup_{t\in[0,1],s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{t}^{2}(s))<\infty
.

Assumption
(A2)
is a technical assumption that controls the behaviour of the errors and allows us to perform interchangeability of integral and expectation operators later on. It is usually very weak: for example, if the second order moment of
\epsilon_{t}(s)
exists and
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{t}^{2}(s))
is a continuous function of
t
and
s
, then by compactness of
\mathcal{T}=[0,1]
and
\mathcal{S}
, the assumption
(A2)
follows naturally. As a consequence of Assumption
(A2)
, we also have
\sup_{t\in[0,1],s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}(|\epsilon_{t}(s)|)<\infty
. Additionally, note that since
\tilde{\mu}(s)
and
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(s)
as in Assumption
(A1)
are continuous on the compact set
\mathcal{S}
, they are also uniformly bounded. For notational convenience, let us indicate
M
as a generic constant which serves as the uniform bound for all these quantities. Therefore, it follows by an application of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)\epsilon_{t}(s)b_{k}(s)\right\rvert\right]ds\leqslant\int_{\mathcal{S}}M\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)b_{k}(s)\right\rvert\right]ds\leqslant M\left(\int_{\mathcal{S}}b_{k}^{2}(s)ds\right)\left(\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}(\sigma^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s))ds\right).

The first term
\int_{\mathcal{S}}b_{k}^{2}(s)ds
is equal to
1
as
b_{k}(s)
is an orthonormal basis function of
\mathcal{H}
. For the second term, note that due to the second-order stationarity of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
, it is equal to
\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}(\sigma^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},s))ds
, which is also bounded due to Assumption
(A1)
.

Now, starting with the decomposition of the mean function as in (
6
), an application of Fubini’s theorem yields that

\int_{\mathcal{S}}Y_{t}(s)b_{k}(s)\,ds=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})b_{i}(s)b_{k}(s)\,ds+\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)\epsilon_{t}(s)b_{k}(s)\,ds.

Following the orthonormality of the basis functions, the above can be simplified as

\int_{\mathcal{S}}Y_{t}(s)b_{k}(s)ds=\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})+\eta_{tk},

(7)

where
\eta_{tk}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)\epsilon_{t}(s)b_{k}(s)\,ds
. Subsequently, we may write

\mathbb{E}(\eta_{tk})=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)\epsilon_{t}(s)b_{k}(s)\,ds\right)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}b_{k}(s)\mathbb{E}(\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s))\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{t}(s))\,ds=0,

where we use the independence of
\epsilon_{t}(s)
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
. Also, by another application of Fubini’s theorem (valid due to Assumption
(A2)
), we get

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\eta_{t_{1}k},\eta_{t_{2}k})=\mathbb{E}(\eta_{t_{1}k}\eta_{t_{2}k})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{S}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{1}},s)\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{2}},s^{\prime})\epsilon_{t_{1}}(s)\epsilon_{t_{2}}(s^{\prime})b_{k}(s)b_{k}(s^{\prime})\,ds\,ds^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{1}},s)\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{2}},s^{\prime})\right]\rho(s,s^{\prime})b_{k}(s)b_{k}(s^{\prime})\,ds\,ds^{\prime}.

Hence, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we get a reduced temporal model

Y^{\ast}_{tk}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}Y_{t}(s)b_{k}(s)\,ds=\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})+\eta_{tk},

(8)

where
\eta_{tk}
is a zero-mean second-order error process. Now, fix any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The reduced model shown in (
8
) is a nonlinear regression model for dependent data, but with infinite-dimensional covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
. Hence, ideally, one may estimate the mean component functions
\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
using local constant estimators, by suitably replacing the aggregated response
Y^{\ast}_{tk}
(which is unobserved) by an estimate based on the dataset
\mathcal{D}
as detailed below.

It is worthwhile to note that, although the errors
\epsilon_{t}(s)
are independent of the covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
, that is not the case for the aggregated error
\eta_{tk}
, as its variance depends on
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
through the
\sigma(\cdot,\cdot)
function. However, it still follows that

\mathbb{E}\left(\eta_{tk}\mid\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}}:t^{\prime}\leqslant t\}\right)=\int_{\mathcal{S}}b_{k}(s)\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)\mathbb{E}\left(\epsilon_{t}(s)\mid\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}}:t^{\prime}\leqslant t\}\right)\,ds=0,

for any
t\in\mathcal{T}
. As we shall show below, this conditional mean-zero property is usually enough to ensure desirable theoretical properties of the proposed nonparametric estimators. We next focus on estimating each coefficient
\mu_{k}(\cdot)
in (
8
) using standard nonparametric methods. For that, a kernel function of the following form is introduced:

\mathcal{K}^{\ast}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\mathcal{K}\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x})\right)=K\left(\|{\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x})}\|/h_{n}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{H}_{n}
is a bandwidth matrix of the form
h_{n}\boldsymbol{D}
,
\boldsymbol{D}
being a diagonal matrix in line with the linear operator defined in (
3
). Here, we also make use the scaled distance function as in (
3
) to ensure that the above expressions are well-defined. Therefore, it is possible to use a univariate kernel function
K(\cdot)
that acts upon the norm of the scaled distance vector scaled by an appropriate choice of the bandwidth
h_{n}
. The specific conditions on the choice of the kernel function
K(\cdot)
are discussed in
Section

2.3
.

We can now provide an estimator of
\mu_{k}(x)
as

\widehat{\mu}_{k}(x)=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}}))\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}}))}=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}})\right\|\right)\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}})\right\|\right)}

(9)

where
\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}
is an estimate of
Y_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}
based on dataset
\mathcal{D}
. Although the usual Monte Carlo method can be used to estimate the aggregated response as
Y_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}\approx\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}Y_{t_{i}}(s_{t_{i}j})b_{k}(s_{t_{i}j})
, it has one serious drawback. If we assume that the choice of the spatial points
\{s_{t_{i}1},\dots,s_{t_{i}n_{i}}\}
are deterministic, then it is possible that they may concentrate on one region of space for any particular time period. The simple Monte Carlo procedure in this case will provide a biased estimate. On the other hand, if we assume that the choices of spatial points are uniformly at random, then the covariance of the
Y
-values in these locations does not decay sufficiently fast with the distance between two spatial points. Due to the presence of this covariance, the Monte Carlo estimate may end up having infinite variance, thus leading to non-consistency of the estimator. Gathering insights from the functional data analysis, we subdivide the spatial horizon
\mathcal{S}
into small grids, and choose a representative point on each hypercube of the grid. Then, the Monte-Carlo procedure is applied for this reduced set of representative points instead of all the points. This ensures a uniform distribution of the considered spatial points and also allow us to control the covariance between representative points of two distant grids. The final algorithm to obtain
\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}
is illustrated in Algorithm
1
. A correctness guarantee that this procedure produces a consistent estimate of
Y_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}
is given in Proposition
2
.

2.3
Choice of the Kernel and the Bandwidth

Before diving into technical results on the aforementioned estimation procedure, it is meaningful to state the underlying assumptions concerning the choice of the kernel and the bandwidth parameters. We restrict our choice to the type-I kernels as motivated by
Hong & Linton 2020
. The kernel function
K:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is bounded and has bounded support, i.e.,

\int K(u)du=1,\ C_{1}\boldsymbol{1}_{[0,\lambda]}(u)\leqslant K(u)\leqslant C_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{[0,\lambda]}(u),

(11)

for some positive finite constants
C_{1},C_{2}
and
\lambda
. The uniform kernel is one such example of a type-I kernel. Due to this, we can now represent the small ball probability using

\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}u)=\mathbb{P}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x})}\|\leqslant u\right)=P(\|{\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x})}\|\leqslant h_{n}u)=P(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\in B(\boldsymbol{x},h_{n}u)),

(12)

where
B(\boldsymbol{x},h_{n}u)
is the infinite-dimensional hyper ellipsoid centered at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
with different axis lengths
2h_{n}\eta_{j}u
at the
j^{th}
direction. Note that, due to the stationarity of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
, the quantity
\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)
is free of the time index
t
. Furthermore, regarding the choice of the bandwidth, we make the following assumption.

(A3)

The sequence of bandwidth parameter
h_{n}
satisfies
h_{n}\rightarrow 0
,
\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}u)\rightarrow 0
, and
nh_{n}^{2}\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}(h_{n}u)\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
for any fixed
u>0
where
\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}u)
is the small-ball probability defined in (
12
). Additionally, the joint small-ball probability defined below in (
13
) satisfies
\phi_{\boldsymbol{x},|t-t^{\prime}|}(h_{n}u,h_{n}u)=\mathcal{O}(\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}(h_{n}u))
for any
t\neq s
.

Assumption
(A3)
ensures that while the size of the neighborhood and the small-ball probability
\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}u)
diminishes asymptotically, the number of observations in this neighborhood
B(\boldsymbol{x},h_{n}u)
increases at a rate more than
n^{1/2}h_{n}^{-1}
. In the second part of Assumption
(A3)
, we consider the joint small-ball probability for any
t\neq t^{\prime}
,

\phi_{\boldsymbol{x},|t-t^{\prime}|}(h_{n}u,h_{n}v)=\mathbb{P}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x})}\|\leqslant u,\|{\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{x})}\|\leqslant v\right),

(13)

which again, by the second-order stationarity of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
, depends only on the difference in time indices
|t-t^{\prime}|
. By bounding this joint small-ball probability, Assumption
(A3)
ensures only a localized dependence in the joint distribution of
(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}})
for
t\neq t^{\prime}
. This assumption is the same as Assumption B.5 of
Hong & Linton 2020
, which, as we show below, helps to control the cross-product moments of the kernel functions. An obvious consequence of Assumption
(A3)
is that as
n\to\infty
,

n\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}u)=\sqrt{nh^{2}_{n}\phi^{2}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}u)}\times\dfrac{\sqrt{n}}{h_{n}}\rightarrow\infty.

(14)

The condition
(A3)
along with type-I kernel is key to obtaining various insights on the asymptotic nature of the kernel function. Starting with the expected value of the kernel function, note that
E(\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x})))=\mathbb{E}(K(\|\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x})\|))=\int K(u)\,d\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}u)
, which we denote as
\psi_{\boldsymbol{x},1}(h_{n})
below. It is also easy to see that
\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{K}^{2}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}))\right)=\int K^{2}(u)\,d\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}u)
, denoted as
\psi_{\boldsymbol{x},2}(h_{n},0).
Due to the boundedness of the kernel function, the quantity
\psi_{\boldsymbol{x},1}(h_{n})
decays exactly at the order of
\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{j}(h_{n}\lambda)
for
j=1,2
. Therefore, we have,

\dfrac{1}{\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{K}^{j}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}))\right]\rightarrow\xi_{j},

(15)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
for some
\xi_{1}>0,\xi_{2}>0
(cf. Corollary 1 of
Hong & Linton 2020
).

Moving on to the joint distributions, we consider the cross moment of kernel functions

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}))\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{x}))\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(K\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x})\right\|\right)K\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{x})\right\|\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt=\int K(u)K(v)d\phi_{\boldsymbol{x},|t-t^{\prime}|}(h_{n}u,h_{n}v)=\psi_{\boldsymbol{x},2}(h_{n},|t-t^{\prime}|)\leqslant C_{2}^{2}C\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}(h_{n}\lambda)\rightarrow 0,

(16)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
due to Assumption
(A3)
. Here,
C>0
is some arbitrary constant free of
\boldsymbol{x}
. While the above derivation provides a uniform rate of decay for the cross-order moments of the kernel functions, we expect the decay rate to be
\xi_{1}^{2}\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}(h_{n}\lambda)+g(|t-t^{\prime}|)
for some function
g(\cdot)
that controls the covariance between the kernel function evaluated at
(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-x)
and
(\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{x})
as a function of the lag
|t-t^{\prime}|
. In view of the PMC dependence structure as in (
4
), it turns out that a quantitative upper bound to a Martingale difference of any measurable Lipschitz function of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
is possible, as illustrated in the following result.

Proposition 1
.

Let
f:\chi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function such that it is measurable and Lipschitz (under the scaled norm given in (
3
)) with a constant
C
, i.e.,
\left\lvert f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{y})\right\rvert\leqslant C\|{\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})}\|
for any
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\chi
. Then, with
\mathcal{F}_{t}
denoting the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}}:t^{\prime}\leqslant t\}
,

\left|\mathbb{E}\left(f(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}-m}\right)-\mathbb{E}(f(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}}))\right|\leqslant C\Delta_{2}(m)/\sqrt{2}.

(17)

We note that, to make use of Proposition
1
, we need to apply Lipschitz-type smoothness restrictions on the kernel function. This is formalized below.

(A4)

For each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
, the function
f_{\boldsymbol{x}}:\chi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
f_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}))\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})
is Lipschitz over
\chi
in the scaled
L^{2}
-metric given in (
3
), where
\mu_{k}
is the component functions of
\mu
as given in (
6
). The Lipschitz constant is also uniform over
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
. The same holds for the kernel-weighted variance components, i.e., for
g_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}))\sigma_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})
for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and uniformly over any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
.

This results in an improved bound on the autocovariance between the kernel functions, as illustrated in the following corollary, whose proof is provided in the supplementary material.

Corollary 1
.

For sufficiently large
n
, for some constant
C>0
and
C_{2}
as in (
11
), we have

\text{Cov}\left(\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}-\boldsymbol{x})),\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{x}))\right)\leqslant\dfrac{\sqrt{2}CC_{2}^{2}}{9h_{n}^{2}\lambda^{2}}\Delta_{2}(|t-t^{\prime}|)(1-\mathbb{E}\left(\phi_{\boldsymbol{X}_{0}}(3h_{n}\lambda)\right))\\
+C_{2}^{2}(1+C)\phi_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}(h_{n}\lambda)\mathbb{E}\left(\phi_{\boldsymbol{X}_{0}}(3h_{n}\lambda)\right).

(18)

A related inequality can be found in inequality (34) of
Xiao & Wu 2011
. We shall make use of (
15
) and (
18
) repeatedly while deriving the asymptotic properties of the nonparametric estimators of
\mu(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\sigma(\cdot,\cdot)
.

3
Asymptotic properties

3.1
Consistency of the estimator of mean

The first step in establishing various asymptotic properties of the proposed estimator would be to ensure that it is consistent. In this subsection, we derive two key theorems related to the consistency of the mean estimator. However, before proceeding with them, we present a short result that provides correctness guarantees for the Modified Monte-Carlo procedure illustrated in Algorithm
1
and provides a quantitative bound on the error.

Proposition 2
.

Fix any
t_{i}\in\mathcal{T},k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Under assumptions
(A1)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left\lvert\widehat{Y}^{\ast}_{t_{i}k}-Y^{\ast}_{t_{i}k}\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{(\epsilon^{\ast}_{t_{i}})^{d},\sup_{\|s-s^{\prime}\|\leqslant\epsilon^{\ast}_{t_{i}}}|b_{k}(s)-b_{k}(s^{\prime})|,\sup_{\|s-s^{\prime}\|\leqslant\epsilon^{\ast}_{t_{i}}}|\widetilde{\mu}(s)-\widetilde{\mu}(s^{\prime})|\right\}\right),

(19)

where
\epsilon_{t_{i}}^{\ast}
and
\widehat{Y}^{\ast}_{t_{i}k}
is the estimated spatially-aggregated response
Y^{\ast}_{t_{i}k}
using the modified Monte-Carlo procedure (see (
10
)), and
\widetilde{\mu}(s)
is as defined in Assumption
(A1)
. Let us denote the right-hand side of (
19
) as
\delta_{k}(\epsilon_{t_{i}}^{\ast})
.

It is clear that, in addition to Assumption
(A1)
, if we consider
\epsilon_{t_{i}}^{\ast}\rightarrow 0
(which is a proxy for the average gap of data-points in the spatial horizon at timepoint
t_{i}
) in the asymptotic regime, then Proposition
2
establishes
L^{1}
-type convergence for the modified Monte-Carlo estimate
\widehat{Y}^{\ast}_{t_{i}k}
, from which the consistency of the Algorithm
1
readily follows.

Moving on, the first key result presented in this section, establishes the consistency of the estimate
\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
of each component of the mean function to the corresponding component
\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
as in (
6
), pointwise at each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
.

Theorem 1
.

Along with Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A4)
, assume that the effective spatial resolution
\epsilon_{t_{i}}^{\ast}
as defined in Algorithm
1
decays to
0
uniformly over
t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}
. Then, the estimate of the mean components, i.e.,
\widehat{\mu}_{k}(x)
as in (
9
) is pointwise consistent for
\mu_{k}(x)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Mathematically, it means that for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
,
\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
.

While Theorem
1
establishes the pointwise consistency of
\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
for the component functions
\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
, it has limited applicability in practice. Since the unknown mean function
\mu(\boldsymbol{x},s)
may consist of infinitely many component functions as illustrated in (
6
), with a finite amount of computational power, we can estimate it by a truncated series consisting of a large, but finitely many terms. This motivates the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
1
hold. In addition, assume that there exists a function
b_{\infty}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that
|b_{k}(s)|\leqslant b_{\infty}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Then for any fixed
x\in\chi
,
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists sufficiently large
K_{\epsilon}
such that

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\epsilon}}\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s)-\mu(\boldsymbol{x},s)\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(\epsilon),

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover, if
b_{\infty}(s)
is continuous, then as the sample size
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\epsilon}}\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s)-\mu(\boldsymbol{x},s)\right|=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(\epsilon).

3.2
Asymptotic distribution of the estimate of mean

A natural next step is to establish a non-degenerate asymptotic limit of the estimator, after proper normalization. As before, fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Then, for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
, we aim to understand the asymptotics for
v_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})^{-1/2}(\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{b}_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x}))
, where
\tilde{b}_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})
is a bias component and
v_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the suitable normalization factor to obtain a non-degenerate limit, which turns out to be equal to
(n\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda))^{-1}
. However, before describing the result in detail, we present an additional assumption that strengthens the bandwidth restrictions of Assumption
(A3)
by connecting it with both the spatial and temporal dependency patterns.

(A5)

Let
\tau
be the exponent in PMC condition (
4
) and
\delta_{k}(\epsilon^{\ast}_{t_{i}})
be as given in Proposition
2
. Assume that there exists
0<(\tau-1)^{-1}<\beta<\alpha<1
such that the sequence of bandwidth parameter
h_{n}
satisfies

n^{1-2\alpha+2\beta}\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)\to 0
and
\sqrt{n}\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1/2}(h_{n}\lambda)(\sup_{t_{i}}\delta_{k}(\epsilon^{\ast}_{t_{i}}))\to 0
.

Assumption
(A5)
can be thought of as an extension of Assumption
(A3)
that determines the precise rate at which the small-ball probability needs to decay. For example, if
\chi=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
h_{n}=n^{-d/2}
, then the small-ball probability decays at the rate of
\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{d})=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
. If
\tau=3
, one may choose
\beta=x+1/2
and
\alpha=x+5/6
for some
x\in(0,1/6)
. In this case,
\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)n^{1-2(\alpha-\beta)}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/6})
which decays to
0
, satisfying the first part of Assumption
(A5)
. As
n\to\infty
and one has dense samples over the unit square,
\epsilon_{t_{i}}^{\ast}\to 0
, which along with continuity of mean and basis functions also imply
\delta_{k}(\epsilon^{\ast}_{t_{i}})\to 0
. With a precise interplay between the spatial resolution of the sampling and the small-ball probability, the second part of Assumption
(A5)
can be satisfied. The final result on the asymptotic normality of the suitably centered and scaled estimator is obtained below.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
be as defined in (
15
). Under Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A5)
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\dfrac{\sqrt{n\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)}\xi_{1}}{\sqrt{\xi_{2}\sigma_{kk}(\boldsymbol{x})}}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{b}_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})\right),

(20)

is asymptotically distributed as a standard normal random variable, where

\sigma_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{u=1}^{\infty}\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}c_{u,v}(k,l)\sigma_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})\sigma_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ \text{ for any }k,l=1,2,\dots,

(21)

c_{u,v}(k,l)=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\rho(s,s^{\prime})b_{k}(s)b_{u}(s)b_{l}(s^{\prime})b_{v}(s^{\prime})dsds^{\prime}.

(22)

and
\sigma_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})
s are the component functions of
\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},s)
as in (
6
). The bias
\tilde{b}_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})
is given by

\tilde{b}_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})=\dfrac{1}{n\mathbb{E}(K_{0})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(K_{t_{i}}\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}}))-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(23)

It is clear from Assumption
(A4)
that
\tilde{b}_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})=O(h_{n})
, where
h_{n}
is the bandwidth. While
h_{n}\rightarrow 0
asymptotically, justifying the pointwise consistency of the mean estimator, in many practical purposes, we may need a bias-correction procedure to ensure a lower-order bias term. For this, one may consider a jackknife-type procedure and obtain a revised estimator as

\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\ast,(h_{n})}(\boldsymbol{x})=2\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{(h_{n})}(\boldsymbol{x})-\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{(2h_{n})}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(24)

where
\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{(h_{n})}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the estimator of the
k^{th}
mean component with bandwidth
h_{n}
. It is now easy to see that the bias of the corrected estimate
\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\ast,(h_{n})}(\boldsymbol{x})
is
O(h_{n}^{2})
. Another point to note is that the quantity
\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)
appearing in normalizing constant is usually unknown, but one may estimate the entire normalizing constant
n^{1/2}\phi^{1/2}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)
by a plug-in estimate of the square root of the number of points lying in the support of the kernel function
K(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})
centered at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
. Finally, if we were to assume a Karhunen-Loéve-type expansion for the
Y
-value at a location
s
, when covariate is
\boldsymbol{x}
, as

Y(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s),

then the quantity
\sigma_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})
can be viewed as a covariance operator acting on these random coefficients
a_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

The proof of Theorem
3
follows by considering a decomposition of the normalized estimated given in (
20
) as in
Masry 2005
and then follows a big-block small-block decomposition to establish the result. While
Masry 2005
makes use of the
\alpha
-mixing property to establish asymptotic independence between the big blocks to apply central limit theorems, we achieve this through applying the PMC condition on
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
as in Assumption
4
. The technical details of the proof are outlined in the supplementary material. One may also establish a more general result that considers the estimation of multiple components of the mean function simultaneously. We present this as the following corollary.

Corollary 2
.

Under same assumptions as in Theorem
3
, for any finite set of distinct indices
k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and any fixed
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
, as
n\to\infty
,

\sqrt{n\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)}\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\mu}_{k_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu_{k_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{b}_{nk_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\mu}_{k_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu_{k_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{b}_{nk_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})\end{bmatrix}\xrightarrow{d}N\left(\boldsymbol{0},\dfrac{\xi_{2}}{\xi_{1}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{k_{1}k_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\dots&\sigma_{k_{1}k_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\sigma_{k_{1}k_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\dots&\sigma_{k_{m}k_{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\end{bmatrix}\right).

(25)

Additionally, under the setting of Theorem
2
, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists a sufficiently large
K_{\epsilon}
such that, for a standard normal random variable
Z
,

\sqrt{n\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)}\dfrac{\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\epsilon}}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{b}_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)b_{k}(s)-\mu(\boldsymbol{x},s)}{\sqrt{\frac{\xi_{2}}{\xi_{1}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\epsilon}}\sum_{l=1}^{K_{\epsilon}}\sigma_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s)b_{l}(s)}}=Z+\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(\epsilon).

(26)

The proof of this is analogous to the proof of Theorem
3
and hence is omitted for brevity.

3.3
Simultaneous Confidence Interval Estimation

Extending on the point estimators for the mean component, we can obtain an interval estimator to quantify the uncertainty of the estimation. From a direct usage of the asymptotic normality present in (
26
), one can obtain an asymptotic confidence interval for
\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
, provided a valid consistent estimator is available for
\sigma_{kk}(\boldsymbol{x})
. To this end, consider the covariance between
\eta_{t_{1}k}
and
\eta_{t_{2}l}
for any

as given in (

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\eta_{t_{1}k},\eta_{t_{2}l})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{1}},s)\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{2}},s^{\prime})\right]\rho(s,s^{\prime})b_{k}(s)b_{l}(s^{\prime})dsds^{\prime}

where the last line follows from stationarity of

(27)

Connecting the above expression, namely (

\widehat{\sigma}_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\|{\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}})}\|)(\widehat{Y}^{\ast}_{t_{i}k}-\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}))(\widehat{Y}^{\ast}_{t_{i}l}-\widehat{\mu}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}))}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\|{\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}})}\|)}.

Theorem 4
.

Under the same set of assumptions as in Theorem

, for any fixed

and a fixed

\widehat{\sigma}_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})-\sigma_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

.

Guided by Theorems

Under the assumptions of Theorems

\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\ast,(h_{n})}(\boldsymbol{x})

However, to produce a confidence interval for the entire mean function

on the basis functions, as in Theorem

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{1:K}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x},s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\mu}_{k}^{\ast,(h_{n})}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s),\ \boldsymbol{b}_{1:K}(s)=(b_{1}(s),\dots,b_{K})^{\intercal},

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1:K,1:K}(\boldsymbol{x})

It is possible to extend the above pointwise confidence interval for each fixed
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
to a simultaneous confidence interval over a dense subset
\chi_{n}
of
\chi
given by
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}:\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi,\text{ and }B(\boldsymbol{x},2h_{n}\lambda)\cap\chi=\phi\right\}
, where
B(\boldsymbol{x},2h_{n}\lambda)
denotes the infinite-dimensional ball centered at
\boldsymbol{x}
with radius
2h_{n}\lambda
with respect to the scaled distance metric. This means, for any
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\chi_{n}
, the scaled norm
\|{\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}\|_{2}>2h_{n}\lambda
. The following theorem establishes this uniform probabilistic bound over all
x\in\chi_{n}
but for a fixed
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Theorem 5
.

Fix any
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Suppose that the Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A5)
hold. Let
\chi_{n}
be a countable, bounded subset of
\chi
such that for any
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\chi_{n}
, there is a local neighborhood
B(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\delta)
for which the infinite series
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
converges uniformly over all
\boldsymbol{x}\in B(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\delta)
with
\delta
independent of
n
. In addition, assume that there is a dominating function
b_{\infty}(s)
such that
|b_{k}(s)|\leqslant b_{\infty}(s)
for this
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
K_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi_{n}}\dfrac{\xi_{1}\sqrt{n\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)}}{\sqrt{\xi_{2}Q_{K_{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{x},s)}}\;\biggl|\left\lvert\widehat{\mu}^{\ast}_{1:K_{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{x},s)-\mu(\boldsymbol{x},s)\right\rvert-\epsilon b_{\infty}(s)\biggr|<B_{m_{n}}(z)\right)\geqslant e^{-2e^{-z}}

(29)

for any fixed
z>0
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Here,

B_{m_{n}}(z)=\sqrt{2\log(m_{n})}-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\log(m_{n})}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\log(\log(m_{n}))+\log(2\sqrt{\pi})\right]+\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{2\log(m_{n})}},\ m_{n}\geqslant 2,

and
m_{n}=|\chi_{n}|
, the number of elements in the set
\chi_{n}
.

The size of the confidence set is controlled by
m_{n}
, which depends on the properties of the covariate domain
\chi
. For example, if
\chi
is a subset of Euclidean vector space, then

can be approximated by typical covering number metrics, while if
\chi

\mathfrak{F}

\mathfrak{F}
multiplied by appropriate functions of VC-dimension or Dudley’s entropy integral for the function class. We find it imperative to point out that such a Gumbel distribution-based simultaneous confidence interval arises in many situations: see
Wu & Zhao 2007
,
Zhou & Wu 2010

Ma et al. 2012

Deb et al. 2024

\text{NO}_{2}

(
Dutta & Jinsart 2022
)
, we focus on the 38 stations in the national capital region for our analysis. Figure

4.1
Application of the proposed method

To demonstrate the applicability of our estimator described in
Section

2.2
, we model
Y_{t}(s)
using
\{\boldsymbol{Z}_{j}(s):s\in\mathcal{S},\;j<t\}
(this set of covariates is denoted as
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
). As it includes measurements of different pollutants in vastly different scales, we also perform a normalization step for each. Although our theoretical analysis considers
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
to be infinite-dimensional, the number of historical observations is always finite in practice. Hence, we take these finite-dimensional covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\prime}=(X_{t1},X_{t2},\dots,X_{tp})
and augment infinitely many zeros (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\prime}=(X_{t1},\dots,X_{tp},0,0,\dots)
) to make it infinite-dimensional and coherent with the theories derived above. As a distance metric, we choose a discounted
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-metric that discounts past observations with lag
l
by
\phi^{l}
for some
\phi\in(0,1)
. We choose
\phi=0.9
by looking at the partial autocorrelation function of the log-transformed pollutant measurements; however, as discussed below, results remain fairly stable for any
\phi\geqslant 0.6
. Additionally, we use orthogonal Legendre polynomials on
[0,1]^{2}
as basis functions, and make a judicious choice of the number of basis functions via a leave-one-location-out cross-validation method. As illustrated in the introduction, the missingness of the observations brings challenges for standard spatiotemporal techniques with the subset of only complete cases or techniques involving regularly-spaced time series observations. Our approach circumvents these issues by modeling through irregularly-spaced time series data and using infinite-dimensional covariates spanning across multiple locations.

To study the efficacy of our proposed estimator, it may be useful to look at the two different plots given in Figures

and

The map shows that the PM2.5 levels are higher in central Delhi, corresponding to the increasing population density of the city, while the levels are lower in the suburbs. Also, the spatial structure is non-isotropic: the PM2.5 levels are higher in the north-west direction compared to the south-east part of Delhi. We also depict this spatial variation across the first day of three different months and for two times of the day (Morning 10 AM, and evening 8 PM). The intensity of the difference in pollution levels between central Delhi and suburbs are most prominent during colder months and decays as the weather gets warmer.

Conclusion

The main contribution of this work is a nonparametric regression framework for irregularly sampled spatio-temporal observations with infinite-dimensional covariates. Our work establishes a full asymptotic theory, including simultaneous confidence bands, under a polynomially decaying moment contraction (PMC) condition rather than classical mixing assumptions. We start by establishing the consistency of our estimator of the mean part of the model, which also involves a truncated series approximation of the mean surface. Next, we provide asymptotic normality of the properly scaled and centered mean estimator. We finally end with consistency of variance estimators, which yields the confidence interval and simultaneous confidence band constructions. We address the spatial irregularity through a grid approximation, while the infinite dimensionality of the problem is addressed through small-ball probability assumptions.

Some future directions one can consider are to strengthen the inferential theory from the mean to the covariance structure, by deriving asymptotic distributions and uniform confidence statements for
\sigma(x,s)
, complementing the consistency results already obtained for covariance estimation. Another possible direction is to relax the stationarity assumptions on
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\}

and develop an analogous theory under local stationarity or structural breaks, while preserving the PMC-based framework.
One could potentially extend the asymptotic results, which are currently obtained pointwise in
x
, and obtain functional central theorems, which could substantially broaden the scope of simultaneous inference.

S.1
Inference of the Variance Component

In the main paper, we have tackled the problem of estimating the mean function
\mu(\boldsymbol{x},s)
, and the component-wise covariance operator
\sigma_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})
, but a detailed exposition on the estimation of the scale component
\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},s)
in the model (
1
) mentioned in the main manuscript was not included. This section establishes the estimation procedure and the asymptotic properties of the estimator for the variance component. Note that all cross-references in this document may pertain to the contents in the supplement (whenever it has the prefix S.) or the main manuscript (if there is no additional prefix).

S.1.1
Estimation Algorithm

Akin to the case with the mean function, we again start with the reduced model established in equation (
8
) of the main paper and use the decomposition given in (
6
) to estimate the component functions
\sigma_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})
for each
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Note that, by definition of
\sigma_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})
, we know that

\sigma_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{u=1}^{\infty}\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}c_{u,v}(k,l)\sigma_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})\sigma_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ \text{ for any }k,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.

(S.1)

If we substitute
\sigma_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})
by its estimate
\widehat{\sigma}_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})
as in (
28
) for each
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we have a countable set of equations involving the components
\sigma_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})
. If such a system is solvable, then it would let us recover an estimate of the variance component as
\widehat{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x},s)=\sum_{u=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\sigma}_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{u}(s)
where
\widehat{\sigma}_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})
is a solution of these equations.

One approach to solve this system is as follows: Following the decomposition of
\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},s)
as in (
6
), we must have
\sum_{u=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})
to be finitely summable, and hence for any given
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
U_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
\sum_{u=U_{\epsilon}+1}^{\infty}\sigma_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})<\epsilon
for each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi
. Additionally, as
|\rho(s,s^{\prime})|\leqslant 1
, by an application of Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, it follows that
c_{u,v}(k,l)\leqslant 1
for all
u,v,k,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Therefore, we have

\sum_{\max(u,v)>U_{\epsilon}}|c_{u,v}(k,l)\sigma_{u}(x)\sigma_{v}(x)|\leqslant\sum_{u=U_{\epsilon}+1}^{\infty}\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{u}(x)\sigma_{v}(x)+\sum_{u=1}^{\infty}\sum_{v=U_{\epsilon}+1}^{\infty}\sigma_{u}(x)\sigma_{v}(x)=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

As a result, we can truncate the infinite sums present in the right-hand side of (
S.1
) with an error of at most
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. At this point, let us denote the
U_{\epsilon}
-length vector comprising of
\{\sigma_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})\}_{u=1}^{U_{\epsilon}}
as
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1:U_{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Let us denote the corresponding vector of the estimates as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1:U_{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Let us also denote
\boldsymbol{C}_{k,l}
as a
U_{\epsilon}\times U_{\epsilon}
-matrix comprising of the elements
\{c_{u,v}(k,l)\}_{u,v=1}^{U_{\epsilon}}
. Using these notations and the truncation argument, we can then rewrite the system of quadratic equations to be solved as a system of linear equations in the unknown coordinates of
\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1:U_{\epsilon}})
, which yields

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1:U_{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{x})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1:U_{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\intercal}=\text{vec}^{-1}\left(\left[\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{C}_{11}):\dots:\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{C}_{U_{\epsilon},U_{\epsilon}})\right]^{-1}\text{vec}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right)=\boldsymbol{A},

(S.2)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the
U_{\epsilon}\times U_{\epsilon}
-size matrix with entries
\sigma_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x})
for
k,l=1,2,\dots,U_{\epsilon}
, and
\text{vec}(\cdot)
denotes the vectorization operation of a matrix. However, the solution of the right-hand side of (
S.2
) may not result in a rank-one matrix, hence we obtain the estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1:U_{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{x})
by considering the partial eigendecomposition of the output matrix. In summary, we obtain the estimate of the variance component as expressed in Algorithm
2
.

Input:

t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}
,
\{s_{t_{i}j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, Response observations
\{Y_{t_{i}}(s_{t_{i}j})\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}

for

k=1,2,\dots,U_{\epsilon}

do

(A4)
, and a direct application of Proposition
1

4

\displaystyle\sup_{i}\mathbb{E}|U_{i}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{t_{i}}2\mathbb{E}|K(t_{i})\widehat{Y}^{\ast}_{t_{i}k}|,

\displaystyle=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)+\sup_{t_{i}}2C_{2}\mathbb{E}\left|\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}})+\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}},s)\epsilon_{t_{i}}(s)b_{k}(s)ds\right|

. Finally, we choose

Following the same approach as in

\widehat{\mu}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{b}_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})=\dfrac{Q_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{b}_{nk}(\boldsymbol{x})(\widehat{\mu}_{k,1}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\mu}_{k,1}(\boldsymbol{x})))}{\widehat{\mu}_{k,1}(\boldsymbol{x})}

Lemma 4
.

Suppose the Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A5)

S_{n}
is as defined in (

Proof.

Clearly,
\mathbb{E}(S_{n})=0

\displaystyle\text{Var}(S_{n})

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}_{n}^{2}\text{Var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g_{n}}\sum_{i=(j-1)c_{n}+1}^{(j-1)c_{n}+a_{n}}U_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}_{n}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{g_{n}}\text{Var}\left(\sum_{i=(j-1)c_{n}+1}^{(j-1)c_{n}+a_{n}}U_{i}\right)+\tilde{v}_{n}^{2}\sum_{j\neq l}^{g_{n}}\text{Cov}\left(\sum_{i=(j-1)c_{n}+1}^{(j-1)c_{n}+a_{n}}U_{i},\sum_{i=(l-1)c_{n}+1}^{(l-1)c_{n}+a_{n}}U_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}_{n}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{g_{n}}\left[\sum_{i=(j-1)c_{n}+1}^{(j-1)c_{n}+a_{n}}\text{Var}(U_{i})+\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}\text{Cov}(U_{i},U_{i^{\prime}})\right]+\tilde{v}_{n}^{2}\sum_{j\neq l}^{g_{n}}\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}\text{Cov}(U_{i},U_{i}^{\prime})

where the sums over

are taken over the appropriate groups. For the variance term, we have

\displaystyle\text{Var}(U_{i})

\displaystyle=\text{Var}\left(K_{t_{i}}(Y_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\text{Var}\left(K_{t_{i}}(Y_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}))\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}\right)\right]+\text{Var}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[K_{t_{i}}(Y_{t_{i}k}^{\ast}-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}))\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[K_{t_{i}}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}},s)\sigma(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}},s^{\prime})\rho(s,s^{\prime})b_{k}(s)b_{k}(s^{\prime})dsds^{\prime}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[K^{2}_{t_{i}}(\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t_{i}})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}\right]

The last line follows from stationarity of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
. We bound the second term
\mathbb{E}\left[K^{2}_{0}(\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}\right]
by noting that, the random variable inside the expectation is nonzero only when
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}

. Additionally, when
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
is indeed inside the ball
B(\boldsymbol{x},h_{n}\lambda)
, we can apply the Lipschitz condition presented in Assumption
(A4)
to bound the term. In summary, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[K^{2}_{0}(\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}\right]

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that Assumptions
(A1)
-
(A5)

is the big-block as in (

It follows that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}(e^{itB_{n}})-\prod_{j=1}^{g_{n}}\mathbb{E}(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,j}})\right|

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}(e^{itB_{g_{n}c_{n}}})-\prod_{j=1}^{g_{n}}\mathbb{E}(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,j}})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}(e^{itB_{g_{n}c_{n}}})-\mathbb{E}(e^{itB_{(g_{n}-1)c_{n}}})\mathbb{E}\left(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,g_{n}}}\right)\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}(e^{itB_{(g_{n}-1)c_{n}}})\mathbb{E}\left(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,g_{n}}}\right)-\prod_{j=1}^{g_{n}}\mathbb{E}(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,j}})\right|

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}(e^{itB_{(g_{n}-1)c_{n}}})\mathbb{E}\left(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,g_{n}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{(g_{n}-1)c_{n}}}\right)-\mathbb{E}(e^{itB_{(g_{n}-1)c_{n}}})\mathbb{E}\left(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,g_{n}}}\right)\right|+

\displaystyle\qquad\left|\mathbb{E}\left(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,g_{n}}}\right)\right|\left|\mathbb{E}(e^{itB_{(g_{n}-1)c_{n}}})-\prod_{j=1}^{g_{n}-1}\mathbb{E}(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,j}})\right|

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,g_{n}}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{(g_{n}-1)c_{n}}}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,g_{n}}}\right)\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}(e^{itB_{(g_{n}-1)c_{n}}})-\prod_{j=1}^{g_{n}-1}\mathbb{E}(e^{it\tilde{v}_{n}B_{n,j}})\right|

In particular, by combining the inequalities from (

n
. Also,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=(K+1)}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}(\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))b_{k}(s)\right|+\left|\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})b_{k}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant b_{\infty}(s)\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in N_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}\left|\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}(\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))\right|+b_{\infty}(s)\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}|\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})|.

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
is uniformly convergent for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in N_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
, it follows that for any given
\epsilon>0
, there exists
K_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
such that the above bound is less than
\epsilon b_{\infty}(s)
. Taking supremum over both sides yields,

\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\chi_{n}}\left|\sum_{k=(K+1)}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s)\right|\leqslant\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{N}(\delta)}b_{\infty}(s)\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in N_{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}\left|\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}(\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))\right|+\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}|\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})|\right].

Since
\mathcal{N}(\delta)
has finitely many elements, taking
K_{\epsilon}=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{N}(\delta)}K_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
we can ensure that the right-hand side of the above quantity is less than
\epsilon b_{\infty}(s)
.

For ease of notation, let us denote

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,K}=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\xi_{1}\sqrt{n\phi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(h_{n}\lambda)}}{\sqrt{\xi_{2}Q_{K}(\boldsymbol{x},s)}},

\displaystyle\beta_{n,K}=

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{1:K}(\boldsymbol{x},s)-\mu_{1:K}(\boldsymbol{x},s)-b^{\ast}_{n,1:K}(\boldsymbol{x},s),

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,K}=

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{1:K}(\boldsymbol{x},s)-\mu(\boldsymbol{x},s)-b^{\ast}_{n,1:K}(\boldsymbol{x},s)=\beta_{n,K}-\sum_{k=(K+1)}^{\infty}\mu_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})b_{k}(s).

By an application of triangle inequality, it follows that

\alpha_{n,K_{\epsilon}}(|\beta_{n,K_{\epsilon}}|-\epsilon b_{\infty}(s))\leqslant\alpha_{n,K_{\epsilon}}|\gamma_{n,K_{\epsilon}}|\leqslant\alpha_{n,K_{\epsilon}}(|\beta_{n,K_{\epsilon}}|+\epsilon b_{\infty}(s)).

Combining this with (
S.16
) now completes the proof.

S.3
Real-life example: Analyzing Soccer data

As another interesting illustration, we apply our proposed methodology to analyze the shot accuracy patterns in the English Premier League (EPL) across multiple seasons. The dataset is obtained from the
worldfootballR
package
(
Zivkovic 2022
)
. In a soccer match, various players from both teams take shots from different positions on the field, and as mentioned in
Section

1
, xG quantifies the quality of each attempt based on the shot characteristics. The reader is referred to
Anzer & Bauer 2021
for a detailed exposition on this metric. However, it is important to recognize that xG does not explicitly account for team-specific or player-specific effect. We undertake a small-scaled analysis to uncover how this measure varies spatially across the field and to identify systematic effects associated with different teams and seasons. Specifically, we analyze the attacking patterns of Arsenal, Liverpool and Manchester City for three different seasons – 2017/18, 2021/22, and 2024/25. These three teams are chosen for specific reasons: since 2017/18, only Manchester City and Liverpool have managed to win the EPL whereas during the same period Arsenal have been on a path of steady growth and are at the top of the league table in the ongoing season. In the three seasons we consider, Manchester City were the winners in the first two and finished third in the last one. Liverpool won the league in 2024/25 and finished fourth and second in the other two seasons respectively. Arsenal, on the other hand, finished in the sixth, fifth and second position in the league during these three seasons.

For modeling purposes, the response variable
Y_{t}(s)
is taken to be the logit-transformed xG for a shot taken from location
s
in the
t^{th}
match. The covariate vector
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
is defined as team-specific profile at the
t^{th}
match (
t
can be 1 to 38 during a season), including the proportion of matches already played in the season, win–loss ratios, average goals scored and conceded, mean number of shots, and proportion of shots on target, among others. Figure
S.1
displays the estimated mean xG surface – the sigmoid function applied on
\hat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)
– for the three representative teams in the three different seasons as mentioned. For illustration, these estimates are obtained under the setting where the covariates are fixed at an average level.

Figure S.1
:
Estimated Spatial Surface for expected goals (xG) for Arsenal, Liverpool, and Manchester City in different seasons, when playing against an equally strong team.

Typically, we observe that the shots are most effective from central locations close to goal, particularly within the penalty area, and this canonical spatial structure is present across all estimated surfaces, serving as a basic validation that the fitted mean function aligns with domain expectations. The key insight is in the team and season-specific deviations from this baseline geometry. For Arsenal, the last season’s estimated surface exhibits a comparatively broader region of moderate expected xG outside the penalty box, which is consistent with a shift toward taking (or creating) shots from longer range and wider areas. Although this should not be interpreted as claiming that long shots become high-probability outcomes, we find it imperative to highlight that Arsenal’s ranking in the league table improved in 2025. Statistically, our model suggests a relative increase in the conditional expected shot quality associated with those locations, once the match-level context
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
is taken into account.

For Liverpool, we notice a more concentrated pattern of xG in the central locations inside the box. It suggests that the chances created by Liverpool were more impactful in the last season, potentially becoming critical in helping them win the league. One must further observe that it is qualitatively similar to Manchester City’s surfaces from the first two seasons under consideration, where they emerged victorious in the end. Generally, it suggests a stable attacking mechanism that consistently produces high-quality chances from the most dangerous zones. In contrast, Manchester City’s estimated xG during 2024/25 was less pronounced and can directly be connected to them failing to finish in top two.

Overall, the analysis summarizes how the spatial distribution of shot quality varies across teams and seasons: while all teams exhibit the expected concentration of high xG near goal, the extent and spread of moderate-to-high xG regions differ in ways that are consistent with changes in performance across seasons. These patterns provide a compact, interpretable representation of team-specific attacking profiles. With this context in mind, a further advantage of our proposed framework is that these comparisons are not purely descriptive heatmaps: they are conditional spatial surfaces
\hat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},s)
, so differences across teams or seasons can be interpreted as systematic effects beyond what is explained by observable team-level covariates. Finally, the results are obtained by fixing equally strong opponent for both the teams. This setting should be read as a controlled scenario in which opponent-related components of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
are held at comparable levels. Consequently, observed differences in the surfaces are most naturally attributed to team-specific spatial shot-generation profiles rather than imbalances in opponent strength.

S.4
Simulation Studies

To empirically verify the results on the behaviour of the proposed estimator, we consider a simple numerical exercise as follows. Let
n
be the number of timepoints and
p^{2}
be the number of spatial locations at each timepoint. We pick regularly spaced time and location intervals, namely
\mathcal{T}_{n}=\{0,1/(n-1),\dots,1\}
and a regularly spaced grid
\mathcal{S}
on
[0,1]^{2}
given by the points
\{(i/(p-1),j/(p-1)):i,j\in 0,1,\dots,(p-1)\}
. Since,
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we pick a set of polynomial basis functions for the Hilbert space given by

\displaystyle b_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle b_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{12}\left(s_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right),

\displaystyle b_{3}(s)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{12}\left(s_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle b_{4}(s)

\displaystyle=6\sqrt{5}\left(s_{1}^{2}-s_{1}+\frac{1}{6}\right),

\displaystyle b_{5}(s)

\displaystyle=6\sqrt{5}\left(s_{2}^{2}-s_{2}+\frac{1}{6}\right),

\displaystyle b_{6}(s)

\displaystyle=12\left(s_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(s_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right),

where
s=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathcal{S}
. The above set of basis functions is derived from a shifted version of the Legendre polynomials upto degree
2
on
[0,1]^{2}
.

First, we generate the covariate
X_{t}
based on an autoregressive model

X_{t}=1+0.5X_{t-1}+e_{t},\ e_{t}\sim N(0,0.1^{2}).

Then, we generate the response variable as

Y_{t}(s)=X_{t}(s_{1}+s_{2})+\epsilon_{t}(s),(\epsilon_{t}(s_{1}),\dots,\epsilon_{t}(s_{p^{2}}))\sim N\left(0,0.1^{2}((e^{-\|s_{i}-s_{j}\|^{2}}))_{i,j=1}^{p^{2}}\right),

so that
\mu(X_{t},s)=X_{t}(s_{1}+s_{2})
and
\sigma(X_{t},s)=1
. It is clear that, the provided truncated basis functions can exactly express the mean and the covariance terms as a linear combination without requiring the higher order terms. Figure
S.2
showcases one such sample realization.

Figure S.2
:
A sample realization of the simulated data for
n=200
and
p=2
(i.e.,
4
locations)

After we simulate the data from the above model with
n=100
and
p=15
, we use the proposed methodologies to predict the mean function
\hat{\mu}
, and as a result, obtain prediction for
\widehat{Y}_{t}(s)
for the last
10
timepoints (among those
n=100
) for all locations using the first
90
timepoints as observations. About the choice of the covariate, we make three different situations as follows:

(S1)

At timepoint
t
, the original covariate
X_{t}
is known, i.e., we can estimate
\widehat{Y}_{t}(s)
by
\widehat{\mu}(X_{t},s)
.

(S2)

At timepoint
t
, the original covariate
X_{t}
is not known, but only its lagged value
X_{t-1}
is known. Since,
X_{t}=1+0.5X_{t-1}+e_{t}
, it follows that

Y_{t}(s)=(s_{1}+s_{2})+0.5X_{t-1}(s_{1}+s_{2})+\epsilon_{t}(s)+(s_{1}+s_{2})e_{t},

which is also covered by the proposed model in (
1
).

(S3)

At timepoint
t
, only the lagged value of response
Y_{t-1}
is known. Note that, the average of
Y_{t}(s)
over the spatial grid is equal to
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{p}}Y_{t}(s)=X_{t}\times C+\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{p}}\epsilon_{t}(s)
, where
C
is a fixed deterministic constant. Therefore, the knowledge of
Y_{t-1}
can be incorporated into model (
1
) by taking a proxy covariate
X^{\prime}_{t}
to be the average of lagged responses across all locations, which reduces it to the former case (S2).
