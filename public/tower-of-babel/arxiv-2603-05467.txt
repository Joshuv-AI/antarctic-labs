Thresholds for colouring the random Borsuk graph
Álvaro Acitores Montero, Matthias Irlbeck, Tobias Müller, Matěj Stehlík

Abstract
We consider the chromatic number of the random Borsuk graph. The random Borsuk graph is obtained by sampling n n points i.i.d. uniformly at random on the d d -dimensional sphere S d S^{d} , and joining a pair of points by an edge whenever their geodesic distance is > π − α >\pi-\alpha where the parameter α = α ⁡ ( n ) \alpha=\alpha(n) may depend on n n . Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa [ 21 ] have shown that the switch from being ( d + 1 ) (d+1) -colourable to needing ≥ d + 2 \geq d+2 colours occurs in the regime where the average degree is of logarithmic order. We show that for each 2 ≤ k ≤ d 2\leq k\leq d , the switch from being k k -colourable to needing > k >k colours occurs in the regime when the average degree is constant. What is more, we show that for k = 2 k=2 there is a sharp threshold of the form α ( n ) = c ⋅ n − 1 / d \alpha(n)=c\cdot n^{-1/d} , where the constant c c can be expressed in terms of the critical intensity for continuum AB percolation on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . For k = 3 , … , d + 1 k=3,\dots,d+1 we show that there is a sharp threshold for “almost all n n ”.



1
Introduction and statement of results

The
Borsuk graph
with parameters
d,\alpha
has as vertices the points of the
d
-dimensional sphere
S^{d}
and
two points
u,v\in S^{d}
are joined by an edge if and only if
\dist(u,v)>\pi-\alpha
.
Here and in the rest of the paper

\dist(u,v):=\angle u\underline{0}v,

denotes the geodesic distance of the sphere, where
\underline{0}\coloneqq(0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. That is,
\dist(u,v)
is the length of the
shortest curve between
u
and
v
that stays on the sphere, which happens to equal
the angle between the vectors
u,v
.
Typically the parameter
\alpha
is chosen small, so that points that are joined by an edge are “nearly antipodal”.
The Borsuk graph was introduced in 1967 by Erdős and Hajnal
[
11
]
as an explicit example of a graph with
a large chromatic number and without short odd cycles.
In particular, Erdős and Hajnal showed there exists an
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{0}(d)
such that the
Borsuk graph has chromatic number exactly
d+2
for all
0<\alpha<\alpha_{0}
. Some thought shows that
any odd cycle in the Borsuk graph must contain at least
\Omega(1/\alpha)
vertices.

More recently, Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa
[
21
]
introduced the
random Borsuk graph
, which we
will denote by
G_{d}(n,\alpha)
.
It has
n
vertices
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in S^{d}
, chosen i.i.d. uniformly at random, and
an edge
X_{i}X_{j}
if and only if
\dist(X_{i},X_{j})>\pi-\alpha
.
When the dimension
d
is clear from the context, we will suppress the
subscript and just write
G(n,\alpha)
.
Recently the random Borsuk graph featured in a breakthrough result giving an exponential lower bound
for asymmetric Ramsey numbers by Ma et al.
[
26
]
.
(But apparently the authors were not aware that the model has been studied before under the
name random Borsuk graph.)

Figure 1
:
Visualization of the random Borsuk graph with
d=2,n=32,\alpha=1
and
a proper vertex colouring with three colours.

We should perhaps point out that our definition of the (random) Borsuk graph is different from, but equivalent to, the one
used by Erdős and Hajnal
[
11
]
and Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa
[
21
]
.
In those works, instead of the parameter
\alpha
there is a
parameter
\varepsilon>0
and we include an edge between the vertices
u
and
v
iff.
\left\|u-v\right\|>2-\varepsilon
.
We choose a formulation in terms of the geodesic distance instead, that is also used by other
authors (e.g.
[
3
]
).
We find this setup more convenient to work with. What is more, the parameter
\alpha
behaves analogously to the parameter
r
in the
standard definition of the random geometric graph (see e.g.
[
33
]
).
For instance, the average degree scales with
nr^{d}
in the random geometric graph and with
n\alpha^{d}
in the random Borsuk
graph.

Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa have shown that (translated into our setup)
there exist constants
c_{L},c_{U}
satisfying

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\chi\left(G(n,\alpha)\right)>d+1\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $\alpha>c_{U}\cdot(\ln n/n)^{1/d}$, }\\
0&\text{ if $\alpha<c_{L}\cdot(\ln n/n)^{1/d}$. }\end{cases}

(1)

As pointed out by Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa, this also gives that if
\alpha_{0}>0
is the constant
provided by the result of Erdős and Hajnal for the (non-random) Borsuk graph, the chromatic number
of
G(n,\alpha)
is precisely
d+2
with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
whenever

c_{U}(\ln n/n)^{1/d}<\alpha<\alpha_{0}
.

The results of Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa show that the random Borsuk graph transitions from
being
(d+1)
-colourable to needing
\geq d+2
colours in the regime where the average degree is of order
\ln n
.
Our first result shows that the random Borsuk graph achieves chromatic number
\geq d+1
much “sooner”, namely when the
average degree is constant.

Theorem 1

For
d\geq 2
, there is a constant
c=c(d)>0
such that:

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\chi\left(G(n,c\cdot n^{-1/d})\right)>d\right)=1.

So in particular the chromatic number of
G(n,\alpha)
equals
d+1
with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
whenever

c\cdot n^{-1/d}\leq\alpha<c_{L}\cdot(\ln n/n)^{1/d}
.

The regime when the average degree is constant is sometimes called the “thermodynamic regime” in the random (geometric) graphs
literature.
We will show that the switch from being
k
colourable to needing
>k
colours
occurs in the thermodynamic regime, for all
2\leq k\leq d
.
In fact, for
k=2
we can be quite precise:

Theorem 2

For
d\geq 2
, there is a constant
c_{2}=c_{2}(d)>0
such that for all fixed
\varepsilon>0
and all sequences
\alpha=\alpha(n)

it holds that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\chi(G({n},\alpha))>2)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\alpha\geq(c_{2}+\varepsilon)\cdot n^{-1/d},\\
0&\text{if }\alpha\leq(c_{2}-\varepsilon)\cdot n^{-1/d}.\end{cases}

The proof provides an expression for
c_{2}
in terms of the critical intensity for
continuum AB percolation
.

Since having chromatic number one is the same as having no edges, the switch from chromatic number one to
at least two occurs well before the thermodynamic regime.
For completeness we offer the following precise result, that has a relatively straightforward proof
(which we give in Section
6
).

Theorem 3

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\chi(G({n},\alpha))>1)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }n^{2}\alpha^{d}\to\infty,\\
1-e^{-c\cdot\nu}&\text{if }n^{2}\alpha^{d}\to\nu\in(0,\infty),\\
0&\text{if }n^{2}\alpha^{d}\to 0,\end{cases}

where
c:=\Gamma((d+3)/2)/\left(2\cdot(d+1)\cdot\sqrt{\pi}\cdot\Gamma((d+2)/2)\right)
.

The behaviour exhibited by Theorem
2
is a prime example of what is called a
sharp threshold
in the
random graphs community, and Theorem
3
corresponds to what is called a
coarse threshold
.
If
{\mathcal{P}}
is a monotone graph property (formally a family of graphs closed under isomorphism and under the
addition of edges) then we say
\beta=\beta(n)
is a
threshold
for
{\mathcal{P}}
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(G(n,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{P}})=1
for all sequences
\alpha=\alpha(n)

satisfying
\alpha\gg\beta
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(G(n,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{P}})=0
whenever
\alpha\ll\beta
. It is a

sharp threshold
if
{\mathbb{P}}(G(n,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{P}})\to 1
whenever
\alpha\geq(1+\varepsilon)\beta
and

{\mathbb{P}}(G(n,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{P}})\to 0
whenever
\alpha\leq(1-\varepsilon)\beta
for all
\varepsilon>0

(but where we keep
\varepsilon
fixed as we take the limit
n\to\infty
).
A
coarse threshold
is simply a threshold which is not sharp.
The situation in (
1
) is sometimes called a
semi-sharp threshold
.
For
k=2,\dots,d
, the threshold for
k
-colourability is also semi-sharp, as a
consequence of Theorems
1
and
2
.
The semi-sharpness of these thresholds leaves open the possibility that they are in fact sharp.
We conjecture that this is the case.
(See Section
7
for more discussion and more open problems.)
We have not been able to prove our conjecture, but we can offer the following weaker result.

Theorem 4

For
d\geq 2
, here is a set
N\subseteq\mathbb{N}
of density one and
sequences
\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k}(n)
for
k=3,\dots,d+1
, such that for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
every sequence
\alpha=\alpha(n)
and every increasing sequence
n_{1},n_{2},\dots\in N
:

\lim_{i\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\chi\left(G(n_{i},\alpha)\right)>k\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $\alpha>(1+\varepsilon)\alpha_{k}$, }\\
0&\text{ if $\alpha<(1-\varepsilon)\alpha_{k}$. }\end{cases}

Here
N
having density one means that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|N\cap[n]|}{n}=1,

where for a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
One could paraphrase the result as
“the property of having chromatic number
>k
has a sharp threshold for
almost all

n
”.

Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa (
[
21
]
, item 2 in Section 4) asked whether, for each
1\leq k\leq d+1
there exists a
sequence
\alpha=\alpha(n)
such
\chi(G(n,\alpha))=k
with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
.
Our results show that this is true for
k\in\{1,2,d+1\}
:
for
k=1
we can take any
\alpha
satisfying
\alpha\ll n^{-2/d}
;
for
k=2
we can take any
\alpha
satisfying
n^{-2/d}\ll\alpha\ll n^{-1/d}
;
for
k=d+1
we can take any
\alpha
satisfying
n^{-1/d}\ll\alpha\ll(\ln n/n)^{1/d}
.
For the remaining values of
k
our results and proof methods leave us in the dark.
We conjecture that the property of having chromatic number
>k
has a sharp threshold
of the form
c_{k}\cdot n^{-1/d}
for each
3\leq k\leq d
, where
c_{2}<c_{3}<\dots<c_{d}
(with
c_{2}

as provided by Theorem
2
).
This would in particular provide a positive answer to the question of Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa.
As mentioned previously, see Section
7
for more discussion and open problems.

Related work.
For context, we give a very brief and very incomplete overview of related results for
the Erdős-Rényi random graph and random geometric graph model.
The study of thresholds for monotone properties in general has played a
central role in literature on the Erdős-Rényi random graph model.
Landmarks include the work of Bollobás and Thomason
[
7
]
who
observed that every monotone property has a threshold, the work of
Friedgut and Kalai
[
13
]
showed that any monotone property will transition from
holding with probability
o_{n}(1)
to probability
1-o_{n}(1)
inside an interval for the parameter
p

of length
O(1/\ln n)
and Friedgut’s seminal paper
[
14
]
that essentially gave a characterization of the
graph properties that have a sharp threshold.
For more background on thresholds for monotone properties in the Erdős-Rényi model
we refer the reader to
[
15
,
35
]
and the references therein.
For random geometric graphs, McColm
[
28
]
established an analogue of the result of Bollobás and Thomason, but
only in dimension
d=1
. For other dimensions it remains an open problem whether every
monotone graph property has a threshold. Goel et al.
[
16
]
proved a result analogous to the one
of Friedgut and Kalai.

Perhaps even more than monotone properties, the study of the chromatic number of random graphs has had a huge influence
on the development of the field.
It was already discussed in the founding work of Erdős and Rényi
[
12
]
.
In 1975 Grimmett and McDiarmid
[
18
]
gave upper and lower bounds for the case of constant
p
that were a factor two
apart, and in a seminal 1988 paper, Bollobás
[
8
]
was able to show the probability mass
of the chromatic number is concentrated near the lower bound of Grimmett and
McDiarmid. More detailed questions on the
distribution of the chromatic number for the Erdős-Rényi random graph however remained open even for constant
p
, and
the research line is actively pursued to this day. A recent highlight is for instance
[
19
]
.

For
p=p(n)
tending to zero with
n
faster than
n^{-5/6-o_{n}(1)}
, Łuczak
[
38
]
showed that the
distribution of the chromatic number has almost all of its probability mass concentrated on
two consecutive integers
k,k+1
, where
k=k(n,p)
depends on both
n
and
p
. Alon and Krivelevich
[
4
]
subsequently
extended the range of
p
where Łuczak’s result holds.
Achlioptas and Friedgut
[
1
]
showed that for each fixed
k\geq 3
, the property of
being
k
-colourable has a sharp threshold in the regime when the average degree is constant, and
subsequently Achlioptas and Naor
[
2
]
obtained more detailed information on
the shape of the threshold and on the value of the aforementioned
k(n,p)
in the
case when
p=\text{const}\cdot n^{-1}
.
It is widely believed in the community, but remains unproven in general, that the (sharp) threshold for
k
-colourability
of the Erdős-Rényi random graph is of the form
p=\text{const}\cdot n^{-1}
.
In this light, our Conjecture
7
seems (even more) natural.
For random geometric graphs, the chromatic number was studied in
[
29
,
31
,
33
]
.
In particular, the third author of the current paper showed an analogue of Łuczak’s two-point concentration result :
if the average degree is not too large, then the probability mass of the chromatic number is concentrated on
two consecutive integers. However, in the thermodynamic regime (when the average degree is constant) the
value of these two integers tends to infinity with
n
, so that the behaviour is rather different
from what we observe in the current paper for the random Borsuk graph, and the mentioned results
on the Erdős-Rényi graph. Intuitively speaking, the reason is that the chromatic number
for random geometric graphs is “forced” by groups of points that are close together in
the ambient space. In particular, in the thermodynamic regime the random geometric graph even has cliques of
growing size, while the random Borsuk graph does not have cliques of size
>2
.

1.2
Informal description of the proofs.

In this section, we describe some of the main intuitions and strategies guiding the proofs in an informal, non-rigorous
manner in order to aid the reader in digesting the paper more easily.

Theorem
1
.
The strategy for the proof of Theorem
1
is to show that, when the average
degree is a large constant, we can identify a subgraph of the random Borsuk graph that has chromatic number (at least)
d+1
.
More specifically, we will exhibit a subgraph that behaves like a Borsuk graph but in dimension
d-1
rather than
d
.
The technique for lower bounding the chromatic number used by Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa involves showing that
the spherical caps (balls with respect to
\dist
, see Section
2.1
for a detailed definition)
with opening angle
\alpha/2
around the antipodes of the random points cover the sphere, and then apply
a version of the Lyusternik-Shnirelmann theorem. See Lemma
2.4
below.
A candidate subgraph that we might want to try this technique on is
the one induced by all points within a small distance (of order a small constant times
\alpha
, say) of the
equator
S^{d-1}\times\{0\}
.
Unfortunately that will not work however, because in the thermodynamic regime, even when the
average degree is a large constant, there will be lots of “empty patches” on the sphere
with diameter of order
\alpha
and larger that are completely devoid of points.
In particular the equator will not be covered by the aforementioned spherical caps around antipodes of points.

To get around this, we find a suitable embedding
\varphi:S^{d-1}\to S^{d}
that stays close to the equator, avoids
empty patches, is nearly antipodal (satisfies
\varphi(-u)\approx-\varphi(u)
) and is “locally nearly flat”.
To construct this embedding, we find it technically easier to work in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
rather than
S^{d}
. So we
apply stereographic projection to obtain a random set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
construct a map
h:S^{d-1}\to(-1,1)
such that
for every
u\in S^{d-1}
one of the random points is within distance
\varepsilon\cdot\alpha
of
\psi(u):=(1+h(u))\cdot u
, and
moreover
h(u)+h(-u)=0
, and
h
is Lipschitz with an appropriate constant (so that it “looks flat” at the scale
O(\alpha)
).
The construction of
\psi
proceeds iteratively.
Roughly speaking, we order the “bad patches” by scale. We start by avoiding the largest patches.
Each iteration we add a small perturbation in order to also avoid patches on the next smaller scale.
The perturbations are made in such a way that on a local scale the function
h
changes very gradually.

We now consider the subgraph spanned by all (stereographically projected) points within distance
\varepsilon\alpha

of the image of
\psi
.
Because of the careful choice of
h
, it turns out that this subgraph itself contains an induced
subgraph of the
(d-1)
-dimensional Borsuk graph to which the lower bound technique of Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa
(stated as Lemma
2.4
below) applies.

Theorem
2
.

The main intuition behind the proof of Theorem
2
is that locally the random Borsuk graph in the
thermodynamic regime behaves like the continuum AB percolation model.
This model, first introduced by Iyer and Yogeshwaran in
[
20
]
, is defined by taking two
independent, homogeneous Poisson processes
{\mathcal{Z}}_{A}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}_{B}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
first having intensity
\lambda_{A}
and the second
\lambda_{B}
, and joining each point
of
{\mathcal{Z}}_{A}
to any point of
{\mathcal{Z}}_{B}
within distance
1
. (There are no edges with
both endpoints in
{\mathcal{Z}}_{A}
or both endpoints in
{\mathcal{Z}}_{B}
.)
Let us consider a spherical cap
C
of opening angle some large constant
K
times
\alpha
.
In the thermodynamic regime, as
n\to\infty
, up to rescaling by
1/\alpha
, the geometry of
C

behaves like that of a ball of radius
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
And, under the same rescaling, the set
{\mathcal{X}}_{A}
of all random points that fall inside the cap
C
behaves like a constant
intensity Poisson process restricted to this ball of radius
K
.
Now also consider the set of points that fall in
-C
. Their antipodes, which we
shall denote by
{\mathcal{X}}_{B}
, lie in
C
and, similarly to before, under the appropriate rescaling
{\mathcal{X}}_{B}
behaves like
a constant intensity Poisson process, independent of the one describing
{\mathcal{X}}_{A}
and of the same intensity.
What is more, the subgraph of the Borsuk graph spanned by the
points in
C\cup-C
is approximately the same as the graph we get if we join a point of the first
Poisson point process to all points of the second Poisson point process at distance at most one.
That is, the “local picture” of the random Borsuk graph is that of
the continuum AB percolation model with
\lambda_{A}=\lambda_{B}
, restricted to some large ball.
Being a bit more careful, one can see that the intensity in fact scales with
\alpha
(the larger
\alpha
,
the larger the intensity).
The constant
c_{2}
in Theorem
2
corresponds to the
critical value

\lambda_{c}
of the continuum AB percolation.
That is, if
\lambda_{A}=\lambda_{B}<\lambda_{c}
all clusters (connected components) of the model are finite, and for

\lambda_{A}=\lambda_{B}>\lambda_{c}
there is an infinite cluster – both statements holding with probability one
and with respect to the model that is not restricted to a ball but lives on all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Recall that a graph is bipartite (two-colourable) if and only if there is no odd cycle, and that
any odd cycle in the Borsuk graph must have at least
\Omega(1/\alpha)
(which is
\Omega(n^{1/d})

if we are in the thermodynamic regime) vertices.
That the random Borsuk graph is two colourable when we choose
\alpha
in such a way that the corresponding continuum AB percolation
model is subcritical follows relatively easily once we have established that
cluster sizes decay exponentially in the subcritical regime of continuum AB percolation.
Even though continuum AB percolation has been considered in a few papers (e.g.
[
34
,
10
]
)
since the work of Iyer and Yogeshwaran, the literature does not provide the subcritical exponential decay result we need.
(We work in the specific situation when
\lambda_{A}=\lambda_{B}
, but the focus of the cited works is on
other choices of the parameters.) We end up adapting various arguments from the percolation literature
to the continuum AB percolation setting.

Showing that if
\alpha
is such that the corresponding continuum AB percolation model is supercritical
then the random Borsuk graph contains an odd cycle is (even) more involved.
As explained, locally the random Borsuk graph is well-approximated by the continuum AB percolation model.
The challenge is to patch together many of these local pictures into a global one where we are sure
that a (necessarily macroscopic) odd cycle exists with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
.
In order to achieve this we adapt the “sprinkling technique” of
Grimmett and Marstrand
[
17
]
, developed for their famous
“percolation on slabs” result.

Theorem
3
.
As mentioned before, Theorem
3
follows via a relatively straightforward argument.
We consider the range of
\alpha
where the expected number of edges is
constant, and use the “method of moments” to show that the number of edges converges in distribution to a
Poisson random variable.

Theorem
4
.

The proof of Theorem
4
leverages a “sharp thresholds for Boolean functions” result from
[
32
]
, stated as
Proposition
2.2
below.
We use this result as follows. Rather than choosing precisely
n
points uniformly at random on
S^{d}
, we
switch to considering a Poisson point process on
S^{d}
with
\mu=n
points in expectation.
Lemma
2.4
below establishes that it is enough to prove the result for this “Poissonized” version
of the model.
We choose
\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k}(n)
such that the probability that the
(Poissonized) random Borsuk graph with parameter
\mu=n
and
\alpha=\alpha_{k}
has chromatic number
>k
is precisely

1/2
.
We discretize the model, choosing a dissection of
S^{d}
into sets
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
of small diameter, and for
i=1,\dots,m

we take
Z_{i}
to be the indicator variable that at least one of the random points falls inside
C_{i}
.
Having chosen the sets
C_{i}
sufficiently small, the event that the (Poissonized) random Borsuk graph
has chromatic number
>k
is well-approximated by an increasing event defined in terms of
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}
.
The aforementioned sharp thresholds result then allows us to conclude that (the discretized version of) the
event that the chromatic number of the Poissonized random Borsuk graph is
>k
has a sharp threshold
in the parameter
\mu
(the expected total number of points of the Poissonized random graph).
In slightly more detail : the sharp thresholds result implies that if there were no
sharp threshold, then we could (deterministically) include a bounded number of points in specific locations in the
vertex set and increase
\alpha
very slightly to
boost the probability of having chromatic number
>k
to close to one.
Increasing the expected number of points from
\mu
to
(1+\varepsilon)\mu

corresponds to adding another Poisson process with
\varepsilon\mu
points in expectation that is independent of the
original process. Such a
Poisson process will contain a set of points that “looks like” the points in specific locations
provided by the sharp threshold result.

Finally we use (
1
), respectively Theorems
1
and
2
, to show that
the sequence
\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k}(n)
does not behave too “wildly”.
More specifically, for most
n
the ratio of the values
\alpha_{k}(n)
and
\alpha_{k}(\lceil(1+\delta)n\rceil)

is close to one (but we cannot exclude the possibility that occasionally there are “jumps”).
For those values of
n
where the ratio is close to one, we are able to transfer the sharp threshold in terms
of the expected number of points to one in terms of
\alpha
.

Notation and preliminaries

2.1
Geometric preliminaries

We will use
B(x,r):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|x-y\right\|<r\}
to denote the open ball of radius
r
around
x
.
We use
\vol(\cdot)
to denote the
d
-dimensional volume (Lebesgue measure) and
we denote by
\kappa_{d}:=\vol(B(\underline{0},1))
the volume of the
d
-dimensional unit ball.
That is,

\kappa_{d}:=\vol(B(\underline{0},1))=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)},

(2)

where the stated equality is a classical result (see e.g.
[
36
]
, Corollary 15.15, for a proof).
The
spherical cap
around
u\in S^{d}
of opening angle
\alpha
is:

\kap(u,\alpha):=\{x\in S^{d}:\dist(u,x)<\alpha\}.

For all
u,v\in S^{d}
the Euclidean distance and spherical distance are related by the
following identity:

\left\|u-v\right\|=2\cdot\sin\left(\dist(u,v)/2\right).

(As can be seen by considering the triangle with
corners
\underline{0},u,v
.)
For convenient future reference, we point out the following consequence:

(2/\pi)\cdot\dist(u,v)\leq\left\|u-v\right\|\leq\dist(u,v),

(3)

for all
u,v\in S^{d}
.
(We use that
\alpha\geq\sin\alpha\geq(2/\pi)\cdot\alpha
for
0\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2
.)

We denote by
\pi:S^{d}\setminus\{(0,\dots,0,1)\}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
d
-dimensional stereographic projection from the
“north pole”.
It is given explicitly by

\pi(x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1}):=\left(\frac{x_{1}}{1-x_{d+1}},\dots,\frac{x_{d}}{1-x_{d+1}}\right).

As is easily checked,
\pi
is a diffeomorphism (a smooth bijection whose inverse is also smooth).
In several of our arguments below, we will want to compare the geodesic distance between two points
a,b\in S^{d}
with the
Euclidean metric between their images
\pi(a),\pi(b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The following bounds suffice for our purposes.

Lemma 5

(i)

For all
a,b\in S^{d}\setminus\{(0,\dots,0,1)\}
we have

\left\|\pi(a)-\pi(b)\right\|\geq(1/2)\cdot\dist(a,b).

(ii)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\left\|\pi(a)-\pi(b)\right\|\leq(1/2+\varepsilon)\cdot\dist(a,b),

for all
a,b\in\kap((0,\dots,0,-1),\delta)
.

(iii)

For all
a,b\in S^{d}\cap\{x_{d+1}\leq t\}
it holds

\left\|\pi(a)-\pi(b)\right\|\leq\frac{2-t}{(1-t)^{2}}\dist(a,b).

We have not found a convenient reference to the literature.
The proof is relatively straightforward but pedestrian and a bit tedious. In order not to disrupt the flow of the paper
we defer the proof to Appendix
A
.

2.2
Some more ingredients from probability theory

For
\overline{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in(0,1)^{n}
the notation
{\mathbb{P}}_{\overline{p}}(.)
will
signify the situation where
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are independent random variables with

X_{i}\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Be(p_{i})
.
Observe that, for every
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, the probability

{\mathbb{P}}_{\overline{p}}[(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in A]
can be written as a
polynomial in
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
.
In particular, this probability is a continuous function of the

p_{i}
-s and the partial derivatives
\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}{\mathbb{P}}_{\overline{p}}{\big[}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in A{\big]}

exist.
Recall that a set
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
is called an up-set if

a\in A
implies that also
(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},1,a_{i+1},\dots,a_{n})\in A
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
In other words, if we change a zero coordinate into a one then we do not leave the set
A
.
We remark that if
A
is an up-set then
{\mathbb{P}}_{\overline{p}}[(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in A]
is non-decreasing
in each parameter
p_{i}
.

The following result from
[
32
]
can be considered as an asymmetric version
of Bourgain’s powerful sharp threshold result (that appeared in the appendix of Friedgut’s influential paper
[
14
]
).

Proposition 7

[
32
]

For every
C>0
and
0<\varepsilon<1/2
there exist
K=K(C,\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N},c=c(C,\varepsilon)>0
such that
the following holds, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every up-set
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
.

If
\overline{p}\in(0,1)^{n}
is such that
{\mathbb{P}}_{\overline{p}}{\big[}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in A{\big]}\in(\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)

and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(1-p_{i})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}{\mathbb{P}}_{\overline{p}}{\big[}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in A{\big]}\leq C,

then there exist indices

(a)

(b)

For

and

we will write

.

Lemma 8

Assume that for some fixed

2.4
Preliminaries on (random) Borsuk graphs

For
V\subseteq S^{d}
, we denote by
G(V,\alpha)
the subgraph of the Borsuk graph (with parameter
\alpha
) induced
by the vertices
V
.
Throughout the rest of the paper, we let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. uniform on
S^{d}
and
let
N\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Po(n)
be a Poisson distributed random
variable, independent of the
X_{i}
-s.
We consider the Poissonized Borsuk graph
G({\mathcal{X}},\alpha)
where
{\mathcal{X}}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\}
.
It suffices to prove Theorems
1
,
2
and
4
for
G({\mathcal{X}},\alpha)
instead of

G(n,\alpha)=G(\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\},\alpha)
because:

Lemma 11

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(i)

If
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(\chi(G({\mathcal{X}},\alpha))>k)=1-o_{n}(1)
then also

{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(n,\alpha))>k)=1-o_{n}(1)
, and;

(ii)

If
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(\chi(G({\mathcal{X}},\alpha))>k)=o_{n}(1)
then also

{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(n,\alpha))>k)=o_{n}(1)
.

Proof.
By obvious monotonicity, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(\chi(G({\mathcal{X}},\alpha))>k)&=&\sum_{m}{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G({\mathcal{X}},\alpha))>k|N=m){\mathbb{P}}(N=m)\\
&=&\sum_{m}{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(m,\alpha))>k){\mathbb{P}}(N=m)\\
&\leq&{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(n,\alpha))>k)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(N\leq n)+{\mathbb{P}}(N>n)\\
&=&{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(n,\alpha))>k)\cdot(1/2+o_{n}(1))+1/2+o_{N}(1),\end{array}

where we use
{\mathbb{P}}(N\leq n)=1/2+o_{n}(1)
, which follows for instance from the central limit theorem.
The first item follows.
For the second item, we similarly have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(\chi(G({\mathcal{X}},\alpha))>k)&\geq&\sum_{m\geq n}{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(m,\alpha))>k){\mathbb{P}}(N=m)\\
&\geq&{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(n,\alpha))>k)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(N\geq n)\\
&=&{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(n,\alpha))>k)\cdot(1/2+o_{n}(1)).\end{array}

\blacksquare

As a side remark we point out that the lemma and its proof actually hold
if we replace “chromatic number
>k
” with any graph property that is “preserved under taking supergraphs”
(i.e. if we arbitrarily add vertices and/or edges to a graph having the property then the
resulting graph also has the property).

Lemma 12
(

\displaystyle\bigcup_{v\in V}\kap(v,\alpha/2)=S^{d}

\displaystyle\chi(G(V,\alpha))\geq d+2

Proof.

Suppose that
G(V,\alpha)
can be coloured with

Then we can write

such that there are no

3.1

We fix constants

We dissect

A level-1 cube is a hypercube of side length

For each

(4)

Specifically, the set of

bad

For each
i\geq 1

is the union of
(s_{i}/s_{i-1})^{d}=K^{di}

(i-1)

The cube

is called

if at most one of these level-

Otherwise, if at least two of these level-

Lemma 14

For every
K,\delta

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
and the mapping theorem for Poisson processes
(see e.g.
[

{\mathcal{Z}}
is a Poisson point process on

with intensity function

It follows that each level-0 cube

that is contained in

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(\text{$q$ is bad})&\leq&\exp\left[-s_{0}^{d}\cdot n\cdot\frac{1}{(d+1)\kappa_{d+1}}\cdot\left(\frac{2}{10001}\right)^{d}\right]\\[8.61108pt]

(5)

Lemma 21

7
Discussion and suggestions for further work.

Theorem
4
showed that the property of having chromatic number
>k
has a sharp threshold
“for almost all
n
”.
Naturally we conjecture that this last addition is not needed:

Conjecture 55

For every
d\geq 2
and
3\leq k\leq d+1
, the property of having chromatic number
>k

has a sharp threshold.

The proof of Theorem
4
leveraged the semi-sharpness of the threshold to show that the value of

\alpha
where the probability of chromatic number
>k
is exactly 1/2
as a function of
n

can only “jump” relatively rarely. If one could argue that instead of rarely, it does not jump at all (for
large
n
) then one would have proved the conjecture.

We offer the following more detailed conjectures stating also the form of the threshold that
most researchers in the field will probably expect:

Conjecture 56

For every
d\geq 2
there exists a constant
c=c(d)
such that, for every fixed
\varepsilon>0

and sequence
\alpha=\alpha(n)
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(n,\alpha))>d+1)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $\alpha>(1+\varepsilon)\cdot c\cdot(\ln n/n)^{1/d}$, }\\
0&\text{ if $\alpha<(1-\varepsilon)\cdot c\cdot(\ln n/n)^{1/d}$. }\end{cases}.

Conjecture 57

For each
d\geq 2
, there exist constants
c_{3}=c_{3}(d),\dots,c_{d}=c_{d}(d)
such that

c_{2}<c_{3}<\dots<c_{d},

where
c_{2}
is as provided by Theorem
2
and, for every fixed
\varepsilon>0

and
3\leq k\leq d
, and every sequence
\alpha=\alpha(n)
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\chi(G(n,\alpha))>k)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $\alpha>(1+\varepsilon)\cdot c_{k}\cdot n^{-1/d}$, }\\
0&\text{ if $\alpha<(1-\varepsilon)\cdot c_{k}\cdot n^{-1/d}$. }\end{cases}.

The latter conjecture would in particular answer Question 2 of Kahle and Martinez-Figueroa (
[
21
]
, Section 4).

The proof of Theorem
2
(establishing the existence of
c_{2}
) hinged on the
fact that chromatic number
\geq 3
is the same as having an odd cycle and
a comparison with continuum AB percolation.
One might hope that, for
3\leq k\leq d
, the constant
c_{k}
will correspond to the
critical intensity for some sort of “higher order percolation event” in the continuum AB percolation model.
It is however not so clear (at least to the authors) what might be a useful property or characterization of graphs with chromatic
number
>k
that we could exploit in place of existence of odd cycles.

For Conjecture
7
the relevant regime is where the
average degree is of logarithmic order. Here we can imagine that a more careful analysis along the lines
of the arguments in
[
21
]
might work. In particular, perhaps there is an (approximate) correspondence
between chromatic number
\geq d+2
and some
kind of “coverage of the sphere by random caps” event.

Lovász’ pioneering paper
[
25
]
gives a general lower bound for the chromatic number :
if the neighbourhood complex of
G
is
k
-connected then
\chi(G)\geq k+3
.
(See e.g.
[
27
]
for the relevant definitions and much more background.)
It can be shown that the neighbourhood complex of the
subgraph of the random Borsuk graph that we construct in the proof of
Theorem
1
is in fact homotopy equivalent to
S^{d-1}
and hence
(d-2)
-connected
(see e.g. Theorem 4.3.2 in
[
27
]
).
We have chosen not to follow this route in our proof and instead invoke Lemma
2.4
, which is quicker.
It does however suggest the following conjecture.
For
n
fixed, we can imagine increasing
\alpha
from zero to
\pi
while keeping the
random points fixed.
This results in what might be called the “random Borsuk graph
process
” : edges are included one-by-one in
order of decreasing geodesic distance between their endpoints.
Analogously to the
hitting time
for Erdős-Rényi random graph and
hitting radius
for random geometric
graphs, we can define the
hitting angle

\alpha(n,{\mathcal{P}})
for a given monotone property
{\mathcal{P}}
(preserved under the addition of edges but not
necessarily vertices) as the (random) value of
\alpha
where the random Borsuk graph process first

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

The proof of Lemma
2.1
makes use of the following observation, which we separate out
as a lemma.

Lemma 60

Let
f:[0,1]\to S^{d}\setminus\{(0,\dots,0,1)\}
be a continuously differentiable curve, and set

g:=\pi\circ f
.
Then

\left\|g^{\prime}(t)\right\|=\frac{1}{1-f_{d+1}(t)}\cdot\left\|f^{\prime}(t)\right\|,

for all
0\leq t\leq 1
.

Proof.

Writing
f(t)=\left(f_{1}(t),\dots,f_{d+1}(t)\right),g(t)=\left(g_{1}(t),\dots,g_{d}(t)\right)
, the
chain rule tells us that

g_{i}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\left(\partial\pi_{i}/\partial x_{j}\right)_{x=f(t)}\cdot f_{j}^{\prime}(t),

The partial derivatives of
\pi
are given by:

\left(\partial\pi_{i}/\partial x_{j}\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-x_{d+1}}&\text{ if $i=j$, }\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\frac{x_{i}}{(1-x_{d+1})^{2}}&\text{ if $j=d+1$,}\\[8.61108pt]
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

(Note
1\leq i\leq d
while
1\leq j\leq d+1
.)
This translates to:

g_{i}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{f_{i}^{\prime}(t)}{1-f_{d+1}(t)}+\frac{f_{i}(t)\cdot f_{d+1}^{\prime}(t)}{\left(1-f_{d+1}(t)\right)^{2}}=\frac{f_{i}^{\prime}(t)\cdot(1-f_{d+1}(t))+f_{i}(t)\cdot f_{d+1}^{\prime}(t)}{\left(1-f_{d+1}(t)\right)^{2}},

for all
0\leq t\leq 1
and
1\leq i\leq d
.
We can now compute (suppressing the parameter
t
everywhere for conciseness):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left\|g^{\prime}\right\|^{2}&=&\frac{1}{\left(1-f_{d+1}\right)^{4}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(f_{i}^{\prime})^{2}(1-f_{d+1})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}2f_{i}^{\prime}f_{i}f_{d+1}^{\prime}(1-f_{d+1})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}^{2}(f_{d+1}^{\prime})^{2}\right)\\
&=&\frac{1}{\left(1-f_{d+1}\right)^{4}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(f_{i}^{\prime})^{2}(1-f_{d+1})^{2}+\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}^{2}\right]^{\prime}f_{d+1}^{\prime}(1-f_{d+1})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}^{2}(f_{d+1}^{\prime})^{2}\right)\\
&=&\frac{1}{\left(1-f_{d+1}\right)^{4}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(f_{i}^{\prime})^{2}(1-f_{d+1})^{2}+\left[1-f_{d+1}^{2}\right]^{\prime}f_{d+1}^{\prime}(1-f_{d+1})+(1-f_{d+1}^{2})(f_{d+1}^{\prime})^{2}\right)\\
&=&\frac{1}{\left(1-f_{d+1}\right)^{4}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(f_{i}^{\prime})^{2}(1-f_{d+1})^{2}-2f_{d+1}(f_{d+1}^{\prime})^{2}(1-f_{d+1})+(1-f_{d+1}^{2})(f_{d+1}^{\prime})^{2}\right)\\
&=&\frac{1}{\left(1-f_{d+1}\right)^{4}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}(f_{i}^{\prime})^{2}(1-f_{d+1})^{2}+(f_{d+1}^{\prime})^{2}(1-2f_{d+1}+f_{d+1}^{2})\right)\\
&=&\frac{1}{\left(1-f_{d+1}\right)^{4}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}(f_{i}^{\prime})^{2}(1-f_{d+1})^{2}\right)\\
&=&\frac{1}{\left(1-f_{d+1}\right)^{2}}\cdot\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|^{2}.\end{array}

\blacksquare

Proof of Lemma

We use the previous lemma.
