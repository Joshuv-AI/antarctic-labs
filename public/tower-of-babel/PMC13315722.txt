FedSynHAR: a framework based on feature-enhanced adaptive pruning-mutual distillation for federated human activity recognition
Authors: Cheng Wang, Rongze Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep-learning-based human activity recognition (HAR) has been widely studied and applied in recent years, but it raises privacy concerns. Federated learning (FL) enables collaborative training without sharing raw data, thereby protecting user privacy. However, FL for HAR is challenged by three coupled factors: non-IID data across clients, aggregation under heterogeneous local models, and stringent computation and bandwidth budgets on edge hardware. To address these factors, this work introduces FedSynHAR, a lightweight FL framework that combines Gradient-Importance-based Adaptive Pruning (GIAP) with Channel-guided Feature-level Mutual Distillation (CFMD). GIAP prunes both server and client networks based on gradient importance, reducing computational and communication overhead; CFMD uses channel importance to guide mutual distillation between local and proxy models and mitigates pruning-induced degradation to improve robustness under non-IID conditions. Experiments on UCI-HAR and PAMAP2 demonstrate the effectiveness of FedSynHAR for federated HAR. On UCI-HAR, FedSynHAR converges about 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} faster than FedAvg, achieves 94.91% accuracy under non-IID settings, and reduces overhead by up to two orders of magnitude. Results on PAMAP2 further support the robustness of FedSynHAR under stronger heterogeneity.

Introduction

Wearable Internet of Things (IoT) devices commonly incorporate inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensors, which substantially broaden the application scope of sensor-based human activity recognition (HAR)1,2. Recent studies have further shown that multimodal HAR systems can effectively integrate IMU, ambient, GPS, and audio signals to improve robustness in both locomotion and context-aware activity recognition tasks3. In the healthcare sector, particularly in remote monitoring systems, HAR enables precise tracking of patients’ physical activities and can provide decision support for vulnerable populations. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of IoT and machine learning in healthcare, with applications ranging from sedentary behavior detection4 and Internet-enabled assistive signal transmission for individuals with paraplegia5 to smart home technologies designed to support people with disabilities and older adults6. More broadly, recent intelligent sensing systems also indicate that practical deployment requires not only accurate perception but also measurement setups with low invasiveness and minimal reliance on external equipment7. In the fitness sector, HAR systems can monitor athletic performance and integrate physiological signals to derive advanced fitness metrics. In smart-city applications, HAR can support diverse services, such as movement-pattern analytics and location-aware recommendations (e.g., parking guidance)8. Despite diverse technical approaches, many existing solutions still follow a cloud-centric pipeline in which sensor data are centrally collected and used to train high-capacity machine learning (ML) models1. While such centralization can facilitate model training, it raises privacy concerns and often yields suboptimal local performance9. Raw HAR sensor streams may contain sensitive personal information; even with anonymization, fully eliminating the risk of attacks remains challenging, potentially exposing users’ behavioral patterns10. Moreover, a single global model may not adequately capture user-specific behaviors and environmental factors, making it difficult to achieve strong performance on local data11.

Federated learning (FL) is a framework that trains models on client devices and shares only model updates, reducing the risk of data leakage in centralized architectures12. Recent deep architectures for activity and action recognition further suggest that capturing longer-range temporal dependencies is important for improving recognition performance, highlighting the need for effective temporal representation learning in practical HAR systems13. However, applying FL to HAR still faces several coupled challenges14,15. First, activity-sensor data from diverse users are typically non-IID, making it difficult for a single global model to fit all local distributions16. Second, heterogeneous device budgets often necessitate different local model architectures, making parameter-averaging methods such as FedAvg inapplicable17. Third, limited and unstable wireless bandwidth constrains frequent model exchanges18. Together, these factors—data heterogeneity, model heterogeneity, and resource constraints—hinder practical FL deployment for HAR.

To address these challenges, model compression and knowledge distillation have been increasingly explored in resource-constrained intelligent systems and federated learning. In particular, pruning- and quantization-based lightweight network design can reduce model size and computation while preserving discriminative performance19. FedMP20 utilizes pruning techniques to minimize resource overhead, while FedDF21 and FedMD22 leverage knowledge distillation to facilitate knowledge transfer across heterogeneous models. However, most prior work treats pruning and distillation separately and therefore does not fully address their coupled effects under non-IID data, model heterogeneity, and resource constraints in federated HAR. This motivates a unified framework that integrates pruning and distillation to tackle these challenges jointly. We propose an edge-friendly HAR solution that integrates pruning and distillation. First, we implement gradient-importance-based adaptive pruning on both the server and clients. This pruning combines sparsity23 with gradient information, enabling a more informed removal of redundant channels. Consequently, the server obtains a lightweight proxy model and clients obtain heterogeneous local models that match device budgets. Second, we introduce channel-guided feature-level mutual distillation that uses pruning-derived channel scores to guide knowledge transfer between heterogeneous models. In each communication round, the proxy model is distributed to each client and performs feature-level mutual distillation with the local model. Finally, clients upload only the updated proxy model for aggregation. Overall, FedSynHAR reduces computation and communication cost via pruning and improves knowledge transfer via distillation, which helps recover pruning-induced accuracy loss and enhances robustness under both data and model heterogeneity. Related work

Federated learning for HAR

HAR on wearable devices is a critical technology in edge intelligence and is widely used in remote healthcare, fitness, and smart-city applications24,25. Traditional HAR methods generally adopt a centralized architecture, collecting data from multiple users at a central server to train machine learning models1. However, this paradigm introduces challenges such as privacy leakage and limited personalization10,11. Federated learning protects user privacy by training models locally on client devices and sharing only model updates12. In recent years, federated learning for HAR has made notable progress. For example, FedHealth26 combines federated learning with transfer learning to enable wearable-based health monitoring without sharing user data. FLAME27 improves performance in multi-device scenarios by leveraging temporal alignment across devices for the same user. Meta-HAR28 leverages meta-learning to improve adaptation to new users by learning a shared representation. CDFL29 combines contrastive learning and clustering to reduce communication overhead and improve performance under heterogeneous data distributions by selecting representative user data.

However, model updates can still be vulnerable to inference and poisoning attacks. Recent studies therefore integrate privacy-preserving mechanisms30, such as verifiable aggregation for non-IID data31,32 and blockchain-based threshold signatures33, to improve model integrity. In this direction, a robust privacy-preserving federated learning model against model poisoning attacks has been proposed to improve FL reliability when malicious updates may corrupt the global model34. Another related line of work studies hybrid privacy-preserving federated learning against irregular users in next-generation IoT, emphasizing privacy protection and robustness when participating users exhibit unstable or abnormal behaviors35. These studies highlight the importance of privacy protection, robustness, and heterogeneous IoT environments in practical FL systems. FedSynHAR is different from these works: it does not aim to defend against poisoning attacks or irregular-user privacy threats, but focuses on federated human activity recognition under resource-constrained edge settings, especially feature-enhanced adaptive pruning, proxy model construction, and mutual distillation for heterogeneous clients.

Despite progress in privacy protection and model training, federated learning still faces two key challenges in HAR scenarios: (1) data heterogeneity: user-specific behavior patterns lead to non-IID data distributions, and existing methods often fail to adequately address the resulting client drift and global performance degradation36,37; (2) model heterogeneity: differences in edge-device resource budgets lead to diverse model architectures, and much of the existing work assumes homogeneous model structures, making it difficult to accommodate heterogeneous devices38,39. Therefore, jointly handling heterogeneous data and heterogeneous models remains a central challenge in federated HAR14,15.

Federated learning under heterogeneity

Heterogeneity, including data heterogeneity and model heterogeneity, is a key factor limiting federated learning performance. To address non-IID data distributions, one class of methods focuses on improving optimization for existing models. For example, FedProx17 introduces a proximal term to constrain local updates, while SCAFFOLD36 uses control variables to correct local gradient bias. Another class of approaches is heterogeneous federated learning, which aims to train a customized model for each client. For example, FedRep40 learns a shared feature-representation layer and personalized classification heads; Ditto41 balances local training and global consistency by separating the training of local and global models. Knowledge distillation is also commonly used to address model-structure heterogeneity. Pioneering works such as FedMD22 allow multiple models with different architectures to be co-trained by sharing knowledge. More recent studies suggest that richer distillation strategies, such as multi-teacher and relational distillation, can improve knowledge-transfer quality under model mismatch42. This distillation concept has also been extended to parameter-transmission-free federated recommender systems43,44. Instead of directly sharing model parameters, these frameworks use model predictions as knowledge carriers for collaborative learning. Specifically43, proposes “hiding” local models to simultaneously reduce communication overhead and prevent model-privacy leakage, a paradigm that44 further adapts for federated sequential recommendation. In addition, methods such as FedMA45 attempt to aggregate heterogeneous models by matching and averaging different neural-network layers. Meta-learning-based heterogeneous federated HAR methods, such as Meta-HAR, further improve personalization by learning transferable initialization across clients, but they do not explicitly address the joint trade-off among model heterogeneity, non-IID robustness, and communication efficiency. Problem description

Although standard federated learning models perform well in ideal environments, their underlying assumptions often conflict with real-world HAR deployments, resulting in performance degradation14.

Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity is a key challenge for federated learning in HAR scenarios and mainly consists of two aspects15: (1) Data heterogeneity. Due to differences in users’ behavioral habits and living environments, HAR data collected by different clients follow inherently heterogeneous distributions (i.e., local data distributions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i \ne P_j$$\end{document})16. This leads to substantial differences in the optimal solutions of the local loss functions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_i(\omega )$$\end{document} across clients. During local training, each model tends to overfit its local distribution and deviate from the global optimum, a phenomenon known as“client drift”36. Simple averaging in FedAvg cannot effectively mitigate this drift, resulting in limited global performance37. (2) Model heterogeneity. Users’ devices often differ in hardware capabilities38. To meet these constraints, clients may deploy local models with different architectures (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_i \ne M_j$$\end{document})39. However, standard FL methods require identical model structures across clients for parameter averaging; when architectures differ, direct parameter aggregation is invalid.

Constrained resources

In HAR scenarios, constrained resources constitute another significant limiting factor and encompass two aspects18: (1) computational resources. HAR tasks often require complex ML models to achieve high accuracy. However, due to the limited computational capacity of edge devices, full training of large-scale models is often infeasible, leading to slow convergence and high energy consumption48. (2) communication resources. Frequent transmission of large model parameters between clients and the server incurs substantial communication overhead49. In federated learning environments with limited and unstable bandwidth, this issue becomes a critical limiting factor for efficient model training50.

Overall, heterogeneity and constrained resources pose a complex challenge that seriously limits the performance of standard federated learning models for HAR14,15.

Design objectives

To address the above challenges, we propose an efficient and edge-friendly federated learning framework. The main design objectives are as follows:Enable aggregation under heterogeneous local model architectures and mitigate performance degradation under non-IID data. The proposed framework should support training heterogeneous local models, effectively alleviate client drift caused by non-IID data, and enhance each model’s local performance.Reduce computational and communication overhead. The framework aims to reduce resource consumption from two aspects: local computation and communication. On the client side, it reduces the computational load of local training; on the communication side, it compresses transmitted model parameters to alleviate bandwidth burden.Integrate model compression with knowledge transfer. Compression reduces computation and communication overhead, while distillation facilitates knowledge transfer and helps compensate for compression-induced accuracy loss. This design aligns with recent lightweight models and distillation strategies, in which compression reduces deployment cost and distillation alleviates performance loss introduced by model reduction19,42, thereby mitigating heterogeneity and balancing accuracy with efficiency.

Proposed method

This section describes two main components of our framework: (1) Gradient-Importance-based Adaptive Pruning (GIAP), which generates lightweight and efficient client-side local models and a server-side proxy model; and (2) Channel-guided Feature-level Mutual Distillation (CFMD), which enables accurate and efficient knowledge transfer between heterogeneous models.

Gradient-importance-based adaptive pruning

To move beyond sparsity-only criteria and estimate channel importance more reliably, we propose GIAP, a hybrid score-based pruning strategy. GIAP yields lightweight proxy and local models and produces channel scores that are later reused to guide knowledge distillation. In FedSynHAR, GIAP first determines the fixed server-side proxy architecture and the initial client-side local masks at initialization; client-side masks can then be lightly refined at budget-checking rounds, rather than following a frequent iterative prune-and-regrow schedule.

GIAP evaluates each channel from two complementary perspectives: its activation magnitude (intrinsic activity) and its sensitivity to the task loss. We combine these signals into a composite importance score using channel statistics and gradient information. Lightweight adaptive budget-aware and variance-aware pruning refinement

The initial stage of FedSynHAR applies GIAP to obtain a stable server-side proxy architecture and initial client-side local masks. This choice reduces the computational and communication complexity of pruning and, more importantly, keeps the proxy architecture stable during federated training. A stable proxy is useful for both proxy-parameter aggregation and feature-level alignment in CFMD, since all clients interact with a common aggregation carrier even though their paired local models may have heterogeneous widths. Nevertheless, practical edge environments may be dynamic: the battery level, available bandwidth, and computational capacity of a wearable or mobile device can change during the communication rounds. To account for such dynamics while preserving the main FedSynHAR training pipeline, we further incorporate a lightweight adaptive budget-aware and variance-aware pruning refinement.

In the present study, adaptive budget-aware pruning is integrated mainly as a deployment-oriented mechanism within the FedSynHAR pipeline, rather than being treated as a separate dynamic-resource benchmark. Systematic evaluation with real battery and bandwidth traces is left for future work.

Cross-client gradient variance-aware importance

The original GIAP score combines activation-based channel importance and first-order Taylor gradient sensitivity. Under highly non-IID data, however, a channel that is important for one client’s activity pattern may be less active for another client. To better preserve such heterogeneous but potentially useful representations, we add a cross-client gradient variance term to the channel-importance score. For channel c, let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_c$$\end{document} denote the activation-based importance and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{i,c}$$\end{document} denote the gradient sensitivity estimated on client i. The mean gradient sensitivity across the selected client set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_t$$\end{document} is10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \bar{G}_c = \frac{1}{|S_t|}\sum _{i \in S_t} G_{i,c}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and the cross-client gradient variance is defined as11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} V_c = \frac{1}{|S_t|}\sum _{i \in S_t}\left( G_{i,c}-\bar{G}_c\right) ^2. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Eq. The role of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_c$$\end{document} is to identify channels whose importance differs across clients. A high variance indicates that the channel may encode client-specific but globally useful representations, such as activity patterns that appear prominently in only a subset of users. Prioritizing such channels in the proxy model can help retain information that would otherwise be underestimated by an average-only importance score. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_c$$\end{document} is computed from the same client-side gradient statistics already used by GIAP and is updated only during budget-checking rounds, this refinement introduces negligible additional communication and computational overhead.

Channel-guided feature-level mutual distillation

After generating lightweight and heterogeneous local and proxy models via GIAP, it is necessary to transfer knowledge effectively, mitigate pruning-induced accuracy loss, and enable aggregation when local model structures differ. To this end, we propose CFMD, which mitigates both data and model heterogeneity while preserving a simple aggregation procedure.

Under heterogeneous data and model structures, CFMD allows local models to learn shared representations from a proxy model, while enabling the proxy model to absorb client-specific representations during local training. The proxy model follows a server-defined architecture that is identical across clients, although the paired local models may have different widths after pruning. This proxy architecture is determined once by server-side GIAP at initialization and is kept fixed throughout training. The server-side pruning ratio therefore controls proxy capacity: more aggressive pruning reduces communication cost but can increase approximation error, which appears in the convergence term of Theorem 2. Accordingly, the proxy is used as a lightweight aggregation carrier rather than a replacement for heterogeneous local models, improving communication efficiency without overly constraining representation capacity. A unidirectional distillation strategy is insufficient: proxy-to-local distillation regularizes local training but does not return client-specific knowledge to the aggregated representation, whereas local-to-proxy distillation alone provides weaker protection against client drift. Therefore, CFMD adopts bidirectional distillation to achieve two complementary effects: global-to-local regularization and local-to-global knowledge injection. CFMD introduces a proxy model with a shared architecture across clients and performs mutual feature-level distillation between local and proxy models during local training. In addition, the channel-importance scores produced by GIAP weight the transfer so that each model emphasizes salient features in its counterpart. Through mutual distillation between the local and proxy models, the local model can absorb shared knowledge from the proxy model, which helps reduce overfitting to local data. This mutual knowledge transfer supports training across heterogeneous models and helps alleviate the impact of non-IID data.

This method of parameter aggregation through proxy models addresses the challenge of directly aggregating heterogeneous local models. Moreover, since the proxy model is already pruned, its reduced parameter size further decreases the communication overhead in each training round.

CFMD uses GIAP-derived channel scores to weight mutual distillation, thereby improving knowledge transfer across heterogeneous models. Together with proxy-based aggregation, it provides a unified mechanism for handling both data heterogeneity and model heterogeneity.

Through this workflow, FedSynHAR achieves efficient and robust federated learning in resource-constrained, heterogeneous environments. GIAP generates lightweight models on both the server and clients, reducing communication and computation overhead. The adaptive local-mask refinement further allows client-side models to respond to dynamic resource conditions without changing the fixed server-side proxy architecture. CFMD not only addresses model heterogeneity but also mitigates performance degradation under non-IID data and compensates for accuracy loss caused by pruning.

Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the proposed FedSynHAR framework through extensive experiments. We first describe the experimental setup, including the datasets and their federated partitioning, baselines, hyperparameters, evaluation metrics, and task definitions. In the non-IID settings, we adopt Dirichlet-based partitioning with concentration parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in \{2,0.1,0.05\}$$\end{document}. Larger values indicate more balanced client partitions, whereas smaller values yield stronger statistical heterogeneity.

Figure 3 illustrates representative client-level class distributions under the four partition settings. Under IID partitioning, the heatmap is comparatively uniform across clients, indicating balanced class coverage. As the Dirichlet parameter decreases, the label distribution becomes increasingly skewed: some clients concentrate on only a few classes, while others contain sparse or even no samples for those classes. Baselines

To comprehensively evaluate the proposed FedSynHAR framework, we compare it with representative federated learning baselines in two categories: homogeneous federated learning methods (FedAvg (2017)12 and FedProx (2020)17) and heterogeneous federated learning methods (Meta-HAR (2021)28 and FedMD (2019)22).

Homogeneous methods require all clients to use the same model architecture. FedAvg12 updates the global model by taking a weighted average of client parameters. FedProx17 extends FedAvg by adding a proximal term to the local objective, constraining the deviation between local updates and the global model.

Heterogeneous federated learning allows clients and the server to use different architectures. Meta-HAR28 learns an initialization that is distributed to clients for local fine-tuning. FedMD22 performs knowledge distillation on a shared public dataset: the global model guides local training, clients upload only prediction outputs, and the server aggregates these outputs to update the global model.

Complexity analysis

We briefly analyze the dominant per-round communication and computation costs of FedSynHAR. The corresponding empirical overhead results are reported in the overhead comparison below.

Results and analysis

We conduct extensive experiments to evaluate FedSynHAR in terms of average local-model accuracy, total overhead, and convergence speed. All results are averaged over 10 independent runs on client-side local test sets to ensure reliability. The main experiments on UCI-HAR evaluate accuracy, overhead, and convergence behavior in a unified setting, while additional experiments on PAMAP2 validate whether the observed trends generalize to another public HAR benchmark. Overall, FedSynHAR shows the most pronounced advantages under moderate and severe non-IID settings while remaining competitive under IID and mildly non-IID conditions.

Comparison of accuracy

Table 7 reports the average accuracy of all methods on UCI-HAR under four data-partition settings. As data heterogeneity increases, the accuracy of all methods decreases to varying degrees, and the relative ranking also changes with the partition setting. Under IID and mild non-IID conditions, homogeneous federated optimization remains highly effective, whereas under moderate and severe non-IID conditions, the advantage of FedSynHAR becomes increasingly evident.

Under IID and mild non-IID (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =2$$\end{document}) settings, homogeneous methods show the strongest overall performance. FedAvg and FedProx maintain stable accuracy above 92% across most configurations, with FedAvg reaching 96.12%. FedSynHAR also achieves competitive results in this regime, with accuracy ranging from 90.45% to 93.51%. Meanwhile, the heterogeneous baseline FedMD maintains strong accuracy between 90.62% and 93.71%. In contrast, Meta-HAR performs poorly under relatively homogeneous data distributions, with accuracy between 25.97% and 50.31%. These results suggest that when client data distributions are similar, local optimization targets are better aligned, making direct parameter averaging in FedAvg and FedProx an effective aggregation strategy.

Under moderate and severe non-IID settings (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.05$$\end{document}), the performance gap among methods becomes much more pronounced, indicating that stronger statistical heterogeneity places greater demands on knowledge transfer and model coordination. Homogeneous methods degrade substantially: at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.05$$\end{document}, the best FedAvg accuracy is only 79.32%, while FedProx reaches 87.29%. The heterogeneous baseline FedMD is more robust due to knowledge distillation, achieving 92.18% at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.1$$\end{document} and 85.27% at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.05$$\end{document}, suggesting that cross-client knowledge transfer is beneficial when local data distributions become more divergent. In comparison, FedSynHAR remains accurate and stable. At \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.1$$\end{document}, FedSynHAR achieves up to 94.91%, higher than the best results of FedAvg, FedProx, and FedMD. At \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.05$$\end{document}, FedSynHAR maintains accuracy between 82.76% and 88.17%, and its best result remains higher than all baselines. One possible reason is that GIAP retains more task-relevant channels during compression, limiting the loss of discriminative information. Meanwhile, CFMD enables local models to absorb shared knowledge from the proxy model and allows the proxy to incorporate client-specific representations before aggregation. Ablation study

The proposed framework comprises two main components: Gradient-Importance-based Adaptive Pruning (GIAP) and Channel-guided Feature-level Mutual Distillation (CFMD). By comparing representative variants and varying key hyperparameters, we assess the effectiveness of the proposed design under resource-constrained non-IID settings.

Table 10 reports the ablation results. Adding the cross-client gradient variance term leads to a marginal but consistent improvement in average accuracy under both non-IID settings. The gain is modest, which is expected because the original GIAP score already combines activation information and first-order loss sensitivity. Nevertheless, the variance-aware term provides a complementary signal by identifying channels whose importance differs across clients.

The standard deviation is also slightly lower when the variance term is used, suggesting improved robustness across heterogeneous clients. In addition, the corresponding training curves were observed to be smoother in the variance-aware variant, indicating more stable optimization under non-IID data. We therefore interpret the variance term mainly as a stability-oriented refinement rather than a replacement for the original GIAP criterion. The adaptive budget-aware refinement is included to make the pruning strategy responsive to changing device resources, but it is not exhaustively benchmarked under real battery or bandwidth traces in this study. Overall, this result supports the reviewer’s suggestion that considering cross-client importance differences can help preserve channels that are useful for heterogeneous activity patterns.

Discussion

The experimental results support the following observations: (1) FedSynHAR delivers the largest gains as data heterogeneity increases, achieving more than a twofold speedup in convergence over FedAvg under severe non-IID settings, primarily because CFMD mitigates client drift through mutual distillation. (2) The synergy between GIAP and CFMD improves overall performance. The ablation study shows that pruning alone incurs an accuracy loss of about 15%, whereas CFMD limits the loss to within 1% while reducing resource overhead. (3) FedSynHAR reduces communication and computation by roughly two orders of magnitude compared with the baselines, indicating strong practicality for resource-constrained edge devices and confirming the efficiency and robustness of combining pruning with distillation in heterogeneous federated learning.

Limitations and future scope

Although the experimental results are encouraging, several limitations remain. First, our evaluation is limited to two public HAR datasets (UCI-HAR and PAMAP2). While complementary, they cannot fully represent the diversity of real-world HAR scenarios; moreover, our PAMAP2 study is intended as a representative cross-dataset validation rather than a benchmark-scale comparison. Second, FedSynHAR shows its clearest advantages under moderate and severe non-IID settings; under IID or mildly non-IID conditions, simpler homogeneous FL methods can remain competitive. Third, we focus on efficient training under joint data and model heterogeneity and do not explicitly address broader deployment aspects such as large-scale cross-device settings, stronger privacy requirements, or personalization. Future work will expand evaluation to additional HAR benchmarks and more realistic deployments and further investigate adaptations to stricter privacy constraints and personalized federated learning.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we propose FedSynHAR, a federated learning framework that integrates Gradient-Importance-based Adaptive Pruning (GIAP) and Channel-guided Feature-level Mutual Distillation (CFMD) to address data heterogeneity, model heterogeneity, and resource constraints in human activity recognition (HAR). Experiments on the UCI-HAR and PAMAP2 datasets show that FedSynHAR achieves competitive performance across heterogeneous settings, with the clearest advantages under moderate and severe non-IID conditions, while substantially reducing communication and computational overhead on UCI-HAR. These results indicate that FedSynHAR is a practical and effective solution for resource-constrained edge environments. Future work will extend evaluation to more realistic deployments and explore applicability in more complex real-world HAR scenarios.
