A generalist biomedical vision-language model via multi-CLIP knowledge distillation
Authors: Shansong Wang, Zhecheng Jin, Mingzhe Hu, Mojtaba Safari, Yuan Gao, Feng Zhao, Chih-Wei Chang, Richard LJ Qiu, Justin Roper, David S. Yu, James Edward Baciak, Xiaofeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP) models, which are pretrained on natural images with billions of image-text pairs, exhibit strong zero-shot and cross-modal capabilities. However, their application in biomedicine remains challenging due to limited large-scale image-text data and heterogeneous imaging modalities. Here we show that a generalist biomedical foundation model can be effectively built via multimodal medical knowledge distillation. We introduce MMKD-CLIP, which integrates complementary knowledge from nine biomedical CLIP models. Our two-stage pipeline combines CLIP-style pretraining on 2.9 million biomedical image-text pairs across 26 modalities with large-scale feature-level distillation. We evaluate MMKD-CLIP on 58 datasets spanning nine modalities and six tasks, including classification, retrieval, visual question answering, survival prediction, and cancer diagnosis. MMKD-CLIP performs favorably relative to the teacher models, with results supporting its robustness and cross-domain generalization.

Introduction

Foundation models trained with large-scale vision-language data have advanced general-domain applications, including zero-shot classification, cross-modal retrieval, and open-ended visual question answering (VQA)1–5. In parallel, the medical AI community has adapted these foundation models into domain-specific Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP)-style2 architectures to learn visual-semantic correspondences across radiology6–8, pathology9,10, ophthalmology11,12, and other medical specialties. These biomedical vision-language models (VLMs) have shown utility in tasks such as disease classification, outcome prediction, and visual question answering4,13. However, current biomedical CLIP models are usually designed for specific medical domains. As a result, these models often show limited generalization when applied to data from other medical specialties or different image modalities14.

Real-world clinical reasoning rarely confines itself to isolated specialties4. Physicians integrate multimodal information from radiographs, microscopy, endoscopy, molecular imaging, and clinical notes and guidelines15. This interdisciplinary nature of medicine suggests the value of a generalist biomedical foundation model capable of unifying visual and textual concepts across specialties. However, building such a model requires large-scale multimodal datasets from diverse sources, a task hindered by inconsistent data standards across institutions and the fragmented nature of biomedical image-text corpora16. Although several efforts have collected 1.6–6.2 million image-text pairs from PMC-OA17–19, these numbers remain far below the billions used to train natural image vision-language models20, limiting semantic and modality coverage. In natural image domains, distilling knowledge from a single teacher CLIP model pretrained on billions of pairs into a compact student has yielded competitive performance21,22. Inspired by this, we propose training a student biomedical CLIP model by distilling from multiple domain-specific or generalist biomedical CLIP teachers, each pretrained on millions of pairs, with the aim of improving generalization across biomedical CLIP settings and supporting multimodal integration across medical specialties and imaging modalities.

Here, we present MMKD-CLIP, a generalist biomedical foundation model built through Multi-Modal Medical Knowledge Distillation with CLIP. As shown in Fig. 1a–c, MMKD-CLIP follows a two-stage pipeline. The first stage performs CLIP-style pretraining on 2,911,190 biomedical image-text pairs spanning 26 imaging modalities from the PMC-OA database. In the second stage, nine biomedical CLIP models are selected as teachers to extract features offline from the same 2.9 million pairs, producing 16,535,905 image-text feature pairs for large-scale knowledge distillation into a single student model. This design allows the student to integrate complementary information from all teachers and improve generalization across diverse modalities. As shown in Fig. 1d, e, we evaluate the model across 6 task types and 9 imaging modalities using 58 datasets, including 38 classification datasets (zero-shot and linear probe), 2 retrieval datasets, 2 VQA datasets, 12 survival prediction datasets, and 4 cancer diagnosis datasets. Across these evaluations, MMKD-CLIP achieves performance comparable to or better than the teacher models. Results

Curating a large-scale biomedical offline distillation dataset

The training pipeline of MMKD-CLIP consists of two stages: pretraining and offline knowledge distillation. In the pretraining stage, we directly used the “Concept-Filtering” subset of the BIOMEDICA dataset19, which contains about 6 million image-text pairs. To further clean the dataset, we apply an additional filtering step that retains only those pairs annotated with an “image_primary_label” of “Microscopy” or “Clinical Imaging.” In the end, we obtained a total of 2,911,190 image-text pairs, including 1,416,257 single-panel images and 1,494,933 multi-panel images. In Supplementary Table 2, we summarize the distribution of image-text pairs across 26 imaging modalities represented in the filtered subset.

In the offline knowledge distillation stage, we constructed a biomedical offline knowledge distillation dataset containing 16,535,905 ("image”–"text”–"teacher image feature”–"teacher text feature”) quadruplets. The extraction pipeline and rules for these quadruplets are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. This dataset covers 26 imaging modalities and includes feature distributions from 9 biomedical CLIP models. By performing offline knowledge distillation on this dataset, the model can learn joint representations across modalities and CLIP models, which may support generalization across diverse biomedical settings.

MMKD-CLIP attains the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) among the compared models in 7 of 9 imaging modalities (Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Fundus, Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), Computed Tomography (CT), X-ray, Endoscopy, and Pathology), and is ranked among the top 3 models in the remaining 2 modalities. The corresponding dataset-wise ranking distributions for all modalities are provided in Supplementary Fig. 7, which shows the variability and relative ranking of each model across individual datasets.

Specifically, MMKD-CLIP shows AUC improvements in MRI (1.23% vs. the second-best model, P = 0.176), fundus imaging (0.24% vs. the second-best model, P = 0.002), OCT (0.24% vs. the second-best model, P = 0.332), CT (0.90% vs. the second-best model, P = 0.002), X-ray (3.25% vs. the second-best model, P = 0.002), Endoscopy (0.45% vs. the second-best model, P = 0.0679), and Pathology (2.48% vs. the second-best model, P = 0.002). In ultrasound and dermatology, where other general biomedical CLIP models perform better on average, MMKD-CLIP remains among the top 3 models. Across individual datasets, MMKD-CLIP achieves the best or second-best performance in 24 of 38 tasks, as shown in the rank distribution plots. These results suggest that MMKD-CLIP benefits from multimodal knowledge distillation and performs competitively across modality-specific diagnostic tasks.

MMKD-CLIP shows more stable performance across imaging modalities than existing biomedical CLIP variants. As shown in Fig. 2, MMKD-CLIP often ranks higher at the modality level (averaged across datasets) than at the individual dataset level. For example, MMKD-CLIP ranks 1st in the MRI, CT, Fundus, OCT, Endoscopy, and X-ray modalities, while ranking 2nd or 3rd on some constituent datasets (e.g., BreastTumorMRI or SIIMACR). This pattern indicates that its aggregated performance across datasets is less influenced by any single dataset. In contrast, models such as BiomedCLIP and UniMedCLIP show greater variation between dataset-level and modality-level rankings. For instance, BiomedCLIP ranks 1st on specific datasets such as NCT-CRA (Pathology) or WCE (Endoscopy), but its average rank is lower at the modality level. Similarly, UniMedCLIP achieves a top-2 ranking on OCTMNIST but does not maintain the same relative ranking across the broader OCT modality. Overall, MMKD-CLIP maintains relatively consistent performance across diverse imaging modalities, a property that may be relevant for applications involving variation across institutions, devices, and patient populations.

Evaluation was performed under three training data regimes (1%, 10%, and 100%) to examine performance under varying levels of supervision. In the MRI modality, MMKD-CLIP achieves an AUC of 94.44% at 1% training data (95% CI: 92.55-96.09%), exceeding the second-best model, GenMedClip, by 5.46%. In Fundus, MMKD-CLIP achieves an AUC of 79.76% (95% CI: 77.69-81.78%), exceeding UniMedCLIP by 5.69%. These statistically significant differences in high-resolution structural modalities indicate improved performance in these settings. In pathology, MMKD-CLIP achieves an AUC of 88.61% with 1% training data, compared with 86.25% for PLIP, which was trained specifically for pathology. This result indicates that MMKD-CLIP also performs competitively on pathology images.

At higher data availability (10% and 100%), all models improve, and MMKD-CLIP remains among the top-performing models. For example, in X-ray, the AUC increases from 88.03% (1%) to 91.02% (100%), with MMKD-CLIP ranking above the other models at each training proportion. In fundus imaging, MMKD-CLIP achieves an AUC of 89.95% at 100% training data, compared with 88.59% for the next-best model, with minimal overlap between the confidence intervals (95% CI: 88.56–91.16%). These results indicate a statistically significant difference under the full-training setting.

On average across all modalities at the 1% training level, MMKD-CLIP achieves a macro AUC of 84.51%, representing a 0.37% improvement over the strongest baseline model. Similar differences are observed under both low-data and full-data conditions, suggesting that MMKD-CLIP provides competitive generalization and representation performance across training settings. The consistent performance gap across diverse modalities, from cross-sectional imaging (e.g., MRI and CT) to photographic domains such as dermatology and fundus imaging, indicates that MMKD-CLIP performs competitively across a range of diagnostic imaging contexts. Collectively, these results support MMKD-CLIP as a competitive vision-language foundation model for medical image understanding.

Cross-modal retrieval

To evaluate vision-language alignment, we compare MMKD-CLIP with nine baseline medical foundation models on cross-modal retrieval tasks using two diverse datasets: the MedTrinity-25M subset23, a heterogeneous clinical multimodal corpus, and BookSet24, an academic captioning dataset derived from ten academic textbooks (Fig. 3b). The tasks include both text-to-image and image-to-text retrieval, with performance measured by Recall@1, Recall@10, and Recall@50. The quantitative results are in the Source Data.

On the MedTrinity-25M subset, MMKD-CLIP shows generally higher retrieval recall than the compared models across most metrics. For text-to-image retrieval, MMKD-CLIP achieves a Recall@50 of 15.16% (95% CI: 14.15–16.11%), compared with 13.67% for the second-best model, BMCA (95% CI: 12.73–14.66%), corresponding to a difference of 1.49% (P = 0.002). MMKD-CLIP achieves a Recall@10 of 3.76%, compared with 3.74% for UniMedCLIP (P = 0.117). MMKD-CLIP also achieves a Recall@1 of 0.68%, whereas most baseline models remain below 0.5% (P = 0.002). These results suggest that MMKD-CLIP performs well on matching text and images in a heterogeneous medical dataset. In the image-to-text retrieval task on the MedTrinity-25M subset, MMKD-CLIP achieves the highest Recall@50 (16.63%, 95% CI: 15.62–17.72%), compared with 15.06% for BiomedCLIP, a difference of 1.57% (P = 0.002), and achieves similar performance at Recall@10 (4.20%) and Recall@1 (0.38%, P = 0.002). Although some competing models (e.g., BMCA) show similar Recall@10 values (4.19%, P = 0.547), no statistically significant difference was observed between MMKD-CLIP and BMCA at this threshold. Nevertheless, MMKD-CLIP maintains competitive and relatively consistent performance across retrieval directions and recall thresholds.

BookSet contains better-aligned image-text pairs with fewer domain-specific abbreviations and less noise. MMKD-CLIP continues to perform competitively on this dataset. In the text-to-image setting, Recall@50 increases to 52.95% (95% CI: 51.52–54.38%), compared with 52.36% for UniMedCLIP (P = 0.002). For image-to-text retrieval, MMKD-CLIP achieves a Recall@10 of 26.61%, corresponding to a difference of 0.18% relative to UniMedCLIP (P = 0.017), although the absolute difference was small. MMKD-CLIP also achieves the highest Recall@1 of 8.45%, indicating competitive performance in fine-grained image-text matching.

Collectively, these results indicate that MMKD-CLIP performs competitively in retrieval on both noisy, large-scale clinical data (MedTrinity) and more curated academic datasets (BookSet). The observed differences across both retrieval directions, particularly at lower recall thresholds (e.g., Recall@1), suggest improved multimodal alignment relative to existing medical CLIP variants. These findings support the use of MMKD-CLIP as a foundation model for downstream multimodal medical tasks.

Visual question answering

To evaluate the visual question answering performance of MMKD-CLIP, we conduct evaluations on two established VQA benchmarks. The first is SLAKE25, which focuses on multi-organ CT-based anatomical and clinical understanding. The second is VQA-RAD26, a manually curated dataset consisting of 3515 question-answer pairs, in which medical professionals pose natural language questions about radiological images and provide corresponding reference answers. The tasks are categorized into open-ended, closed-form, and overall questions, with accuracy as the main evaluation metric. The quantitative results are presented in the Source Data.

As shown in Fig. 4a, MMKD-CLIP achieves the highest accuracy among the compared models across both datasets and question types. On SLAKE, MMKD-CLIP achieves the highest accuracy on overall questions (80.21%, 95% CI: 77.95–82.56%), compared with 79.83% for the next-best model, PMCCLIP (P = 0.007), and 79.26% for MedCLIP (P = 0.002). In closed questions, MMKD-CLIP attains an accuracy of 83.65%, corresponding to a 0.72% difference relative to PMCCLIP. On VQA-RAD, MMKD-CLIP also ranks highest. For closed questions, MMKD-CLIP achieves 79.34%, the highest value among the evaluated models, and an overall accuracy of 70.07%. To further examine model predictions, we present qualitative visualizations of selected VQA samples (Fig. 4b, c). In SLAKE (Figure b), MMKD-CLIP correctly identifies anatomical structures (e.g., “liver” on the left side) and spatial relations (e.g., “small bowel on the right”) in the examples shown. In contrast, models such as MedCLIP and GenMedClip produce less accurate responses on some samples, particularly for open-ended questions. In VQA-RAD (Figure c), MMKD-CLIP correctly identifies locations (e.g., “anterior mediastinum”) and diagnostic findings (e.g., “calcified atherosclerosis”) in the illustrated cases. While BMCA and UniMedCLIP also provide correct answers for many samples, they produce less accurate responses in some cases involving ambiguous wording or visual occlusion (e.g., “sulcal abnormalities”).

Collectively, these results show that MMKD-CLIP achieves competitive VQA performance across the evaluated settings, including detailed visual recognition and medical question answering. It performs competitively across both quantitative metrics and qualitative examples. These results support its evaluation on multimodal medical tasks.

Survival prediction task

To further evaluate MMKD-CLIP on multimodal survival prediction, we analyze its performance across 12 distinct cancer types from the TCGA dataset27, incorporating pathology patch images, reports, and their fusion. Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves and concordance indices (C-Index) are used to evaluate risk stratification and predictive performance, respectively. The quantitative results are in the Source Data.

As visualized in Fig. 5a, patients were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on model-predicted hazard. Across most cohorts, stratification by MMKD-CLIP is associated with statistically significant separation between the survival curves. For instance, in BRCA (N = 138), the survival curves diverge significantly (P = 0.000752), with a hazard ratio (HR) of 8.22. Similar patterns are observed in LUAD (HR = 6.44, P = 0.000528), HNSC (HR = 5.99, P = 0.000007), and PAAD (HR = 26.22, P = 0.000016). We further compare survival prediction performance using the C-Index metric between MMKD-CLIP and PLIP across unimodal and multimodal inputs (Fig. 5b). Overall, MMKD-CLIP achieves an average C-Index of 0.6873 using both image and report inputs, compared with 0.6675 for PLIP. Across multiple cancers, including STAD, KIRC, and BLCA, the combination of image and report inputs is associated with higher C-Index values than either modality alone. For example, in CESC, MMKD-CLIP achieves a C-Index of 0.7652 with multimodal input, compared with 0.6643 for image-only input and 0.6712 for report-only input.

MMKD-CLIP achieves higher C-Index values than PLIP across both common cancers (e.g., BRCA and LUAD) and lower-sample-size cancers (e.g., SARC and MESO). The standard deviation bars are also smaller for MMKD-CLIP in several settings, particularly under multimodal fusion. These results show that MMKD-CLIP performs competitively for survival risk prediction when visual and textual inputs are modeled jointly.

Ablation of loss components

We conducted an ablation study on the FD and ICL losses used in distillation (Fig. 6b). Across all domains, removing either FD or ICL is associated with reduced performance. Specifically, removing the FD loss results in a 0.09–1.26% decrease across modalities, whereas removing the ICL loss results in a 0.14–0.72% decrease. The full MMKD-CLIP shows the highest accuracy among the evaluated configurations, with an overall accuracy of 84.78% (95% CI: 82.51–86.91%), compared with 84.69% for the second-best configuration (without FD loss) and 84.26% for the pretraining-only baseline. In the X-ray domain, the model without ICL performs below the version without FD loss (82.52% vs. 82.99%). In contrast, the pathology and CT domains show greater differences after removing the FD loss. The MRI domain shows the highest classification scores overall, with MMKD-CLIP reaching 90.09% (95% CI: 87.61–92.30%). The difference between the pretraining-only model (88.90%) and the full MMKD-CLIP (90.09%) is also observed in this modality.

These results show that both loss components are associated with model performance, and that combining ICL loss with FD loss yields higher zero-shot biomedical classification performance across diverse modalities than the ablated settings.

Fidelity of dimensionality-alignment strategies

Alignment of heterogeneous teacher embeddings into a shared representation space is required for feature-level distillation in our framework. Seven of the nine teacher models employ ViT-B encoders and produce 512-dimensional embeddings, whereas BMCA and PMCCLIP are based on ViT-L encoders with 768-dimensional outputs. Because the contrastive, FD, and ICL losses require features of equal dimensionality, all teacher representations are projected to a common 512D space before distillation. We therefore examined whether this alignment step affects information content or alters the modality-specific geometry of the teacher embeddings. The resulting analyses are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 2.

We first assessed the reconstruction error of the shared dual-stream autoencoder used in the AE-Projection strategy. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2a, the mean squared error between the original and reconstructed features is consistently on the order of 10−4 for both image and text embeddings across all teacher models. These results indicate that the reconstructed features are close to the original feature representations, with reconstruction errors on the order of 10−4 across models.

Number and composition of teacher models

To further analyze the influence of teacher composition, we designed three incremental integration strategies based on the number of feature quadruples contributed by each teacher model. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3c, teachers are ordered from left to right by increasing quadruple count; for example, MedCLIP contributes the fewest quadruples, whereas BiomedCLIP contributes the most. In Strategy A, teachers are added from low to high data contribution, simulating the progressive inclusion of larger-scale teachers. In Strategy B, teachers are added from high to low data contribution, testing whether early distillation from data-rich generalists reduces the relative contribution of smaller specialists. In Strategy C, all modality-specific specialist teachers are included first, followed by the progressive addition of generalist teachers (BiomedCLIP, BMCA, GenMedClip and UniMedCLIP).

As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 3a, b, all three strategies show consistent performance gains as the number of teachers increases from one to four, but the improvement saturates beyond six teachers. Strategy C shows a favorable balance between modality coverage and generalization. These results suggest that retaining domain-specific experts while gradually incorporating generalist teachers is associated with complementary knowledge transfer. In contrast, Strategy A shows slower convergence due to the initial reliance on small-scale modality experts, whereas Strategy B shows signs of overfitting to dominant modalities at early stages. These results indicate that the order and heterogeneity of teacher integration appear to play a larger role than the absolute number of teachers, supporting the use of a hybrid ensemble that combines both generalist and specialist biomedical CLIP models.

Effect of generalist and modality-specific teachers

To disentangle the roles of modality-specific and generalist teachers, we performed a controlled ablation study on both X-ray and Pathology benchmarks (Supplementary Fig. 4) under four training settings. In the first setting, we trained a baseline CLIP model using only data from the target modality (X-ray or Pathology) with a standard contrastive objective, corresponding to the first-stage MMKD-CLIP model ("MMKD-CLIP Stage 1 Only”). This pure CLIP-style encoder, trained without teacher supervision, was compared against established modality-specific baselines (MedCLIP for X-ray and PLIP or QuiltNet for Pathology). Although the first-stage model achieved comparable performance, it remained below the best specialized teacher in each domain, suggesting that contrastive pretraining on a single modality alone may be insufficient to capture domain-specific semantics fully.

In the second setting, we kept the training data restricted to the target modality and introduced distillation only from modality-specific teachers (for example, MedCLIP for X-ray and PLIP, QuiltNet, or their combination for Pathology). As shown by the blue bars in Supplementary Fig. 4, these distilled variants consistently reduced the gap to, and in some cases slightly exceeded, the original domain-specific teachers. This finding suggests that within-modality distillation can transfer domain-specific knowledge even without increasing data diversity.

In the third setting, we replaced the domain-specific teachers with generalist models trained on the broader PMC-OA database, including BiomedCLIP, BMCA, GenMedClip and UniMedCLIP, while still restricting student training to target-modality data. As the number of generalist teachers increased, student performance improved further and exceeded both the first-stage CLIP model and the single-modality distillation baselines on both X-ray and Pathology benchmarks. These observations suggest that generalist teachers may provide broader semantic structure learned from heterogeneous data, which remains beneficial even when student training data are modality-restricted.

In the fourth setting, we trained the student on the full PMC-OA database with joint distillation from all nine teachers, including both modality-specific and generalist models. This full MMKD-CLIP configuration achieved the highest AUC among the evaluated settings on both benchmarks and outperformed all single-modality and single-source distillation variants. Relative to the third setting, the additional gains are consistent with a combined contribution from mixed-modality training data and a hybrid teacher ensemble.

Overall, these results suggest that multimodality integration during both data pretraining and multi-teacher distillation contributes to the development of a biomedical foundation model with broader transfer ability. When training is restricted to a single modality, learned representations remain more modality-specific and may capture less cross-domain structure. By contrast, training on the full PMC-OA database with a hybrid teacher ensemble is associated with stronger performance on both benchmarks. This improvement may reflect not only increased data scale but also complementary alignment across modalities: radiological images such as X-ray and CT emphasize macroscopic anatomy and spatial organization, whereas pathology images capture microscopic texture and cellular morphology. Joint distillation may therefore help the model align global and local visual information across domains.

Clinically, this multimodal integration is relevant. Real-world diagnostic workflows often require correlating findings across heterogeneous imaging sources (e.g., CT with histopathology, or ultrasound after MRI). A foundation model exposed to such cross-modal relations may develop modality-invariant yet clinically discriminative features, which may improve its adaptability to unseen datasets and composite diagnostic tasks.

Comparison of modality-specific and generalist ensemble strategies in zero-shot and linear-probing tasks

Following the ensemble formulation, we systematically compared our multi-teacher distilled model (MMKD-CLIP) against direct logit-level ensembles on both zero-shot classification and 10% linear-probing tasks (Supplementary Figs. 5, 6). The distilled model consistently shows performance that is comparable to or higher than ensemble combinations, along with greater stability than individual ensemble configurations.

On MRI, our method shows the highest AUC among the evaluated configurations, outperforming all single-teacher models and multi-teacher ensembles, including the highest-performing baseline formed by PMCCLIP and GenMedClip. A similar trend is observed for Ultrasound, where its accuracy matches the top-performing BiomedCLIP and the BMCA+BiomedCLIP ensemble, while exhibiting more stable performance across different teacher subsets. For X-ray, our model performs higher than domain-specific ensembles, indicating that distilling knowledge from heterogeneous modalities is associated with more balanced and transferable representations than directly merging specialized models. In Pathology, where particularly strong domain-specific models exist, ensembles such as PLIP+QuiltNet achieve the highest accuracy; our distilled model shows slightly lower yet comparable performance within this domain while maintaining consistent results across other modalities-a capability that specialized ensembles do not consistently provide.

Ensemble performance proved unstable and sensitive to teacher composition. For instance, on Ultrasound, different pairings of generalist CLIPs produced divergent outcomes: some combinations yielded moderate gains, while others resulted in substantial performance degradation. This sensitivity indicates that ensemble success depends strongly on the choice of teacher subsets and fusion weights, incurring trial-and-error costs for real-world deployment. In contrast, our unified model integrates complementary knowledge during training, providing stable performance without requiring post-hoc ensemble tuning.

Together, these results suggest that direct logit-level ensembling of multiple CLIP models-whether domain-specific or generalist-does not consistently provide stronger or more practical performance than the multi-teacher distillation approach. While naive ensembling can occasionally exploit complementary cues within a single modality, its benefits are inconsistent and limited in scope. By consolidating diverse visual-textual expertise, the final model shows improved robustness, cross-modality generalization, and scalability, making it suitable for deployment in biomedical imaging applications.

Impact of teacher-selection thresholds and soft weighting on distillation quality

To further examine the effect of the >90% confidence threshold used in our trustworthy teacher selection pipeline, and to assess whether softer weighting schemes might preserve harder examples while mitigating noise, we conducted a series of controlled experiments. The motivation behind this analysis is to clarify whether lowering the confidence cutoff enriches the training signal with informative hard cases.

The distinction between “hard” and “noisy” cases is central to this analysis. In biomedical CLIP models, zero-shot predictions with low confidence are not typically indicative of difficult but meaningful samples. Instead, they often arise from cross-modality leakage, where a teacher produces a superficially correct prediction for a mismatched domain. For example, a pathology model assigning a correct class label to an ultrasound sample by chance, but with a confidence that falls below reliable levels. Lowering the threshold, therefore, increases the number of such mismatches included in the distillation quadruplet, introducing noise into the training signal. To quantify this effect, we varied the confidence threshold from 90% to 60%, and additionally implemented a soft-weighting scheme in which all teacher outputs are retained and weighted proportionally to their softmax confidence. The results, summarized in Supplementary Fig. 8, show that reducing the threshold to 60% consistently degrades zero-shot classification performance across all nine imaging modalities. This behavior suggests that low-confidence “correct” predictions are unlikely to correspond to beneficial hard cases but instead reflect unreliable cross-domain signals that reduce feature quality when incorporated into the student model. Soft weighting, although more flexible than thresholding, performs even worse. By weighting all teacher predictions, including unreliable ones, according to softmax confidence, this scheme does not fully filter out cross-domain mismatches. As a consequence, noise may accumulate throughout the dataset and propagate through the distillation loss, yielding performance that is generally below both threshold-based strategies. In contrast, the 90% threshold reduces such mismatches while retaining informative samples, leading to stable performance across modalities.

Visualizing and interpreting cross-modality alignment in distilled embeddings

To examine how cross-modality distillation contributes to the effectiveness of MMKD-CLIP, we visualized the joint image-text embedding spaces of MMKD-CLIP and nine biomedical CLIP baselines using t-SNE across diverse imaging modalities (Supplementary Fig. 9). MMKD-CLIP consistently produces compact visual-textual clusters with closer alignment, suggesting that heterogeneous clinical cues are represented within a shared semantic manifold. In contrast, several baseline models exhibit fragmented or modality-separated structures in which image and text embeddings show increased separation, as highlighted in red. These geometric discrepancies suggest that single-modality or domain-limited CLIP models tend to form biased or less well-calibrated manifolds, whereas MMKD-CLIP maintains alignment across modalities with varying characteristics. The improved cross-modal embedding consistency provides a potential explanation for improved robustness and generalization across imaging specialties.

Discussion

MMKD-CLIP addresses an important limitation of existing biomedical vision-language models, namely their limited ability to generalize across heterogeneous and fragmented medical data, by introducing a multi-teacher distillation framework that unifies specialist knowledge into a single generalist representation. The proposed two-stage pipeline first performs CLIP-style pretraining on 2.9 million biomedical image-text pairs, followed by distillation from nine domain-specific and generalist biomedical CLIP teachers using a 16.5 million-pair offline multimodal corpus. This design enables aggregation of complementary expertise into a shared embedding space, allowing the student model to acquire modality-agnostic semantic understanding while maintaining domain-specific characteristics. Extensive evaluation across 58 datasets, spanning six task categories and nine imaging modalities, demonstrates the stable performance of this approach. In particular, MMKD-CLIP consistently shows higher performance than individual teacher models in zero-shot classification, surpassing individual teachers in seven of nine modalities. These results help address the tendency of prior biomedical CLIP models to overfit narrowly curated domains and show that integrating knowledge from multiple expert teachers yields a single model capable of handling diverse clinical imaging scenarios, including MRI, CT, X-ray, endoscopy, pathology, OCT, and fundus imaging, without additional fine-tuning. Collectively, these findings support the use of MMKD-CLIP as a foundation model framework that can incorporate future advances in biomedical vision-language modeling.

An advantage of the proposed multi-teacher distillation strategy is its ability to deliver improved downstream performance under limited supervision. In the linear probing experiments (Fig. 3a), particularly under low-data regimes (1% and 10% of training samples), MMKD-CLIP consistently maintains higher AUC values than baseline models (e.g., 5.46% in MRI at 1% data). These results indicate that the multi-teacher distillation strategy allows MMKD-CLIP to leverage complementary strengths from each teacher, such as PUBMEDCLIP’s fine-grained subfigure-subcaption alignments, and QuiltNet and PLIP’s histopathology expertise, which produces a student requiring less task-specific supervision. In practical terms, this means that clinics or resource-constrained settings could deploy MMKD-CLIP for classification tasks with limited labeling effort, an important advantage in low- and middle-income regions where medical annotations are costly and scarce.

Beyond downstream classification, MMKD-CLIP also exhibits improved cross-modal alignment between visual and textual representations. Through cross-modal retrieval experiments (Fig. 3b), the model shows consistent improvements across both noisy and curated datasets. On the MedTrinity-25M subset, MMKD-CLIP achieves a text-to-image Recall@50 of 15.16%, representing a 1.49% improvement over the highest-performing baseline, and an image-to-text Recall@50 of 16.63%, a 1.57% gain. Meanwhile, on the cleaner BookSet, MMKD-CLIP reaches a Recall@50 of 52.95%, exceeding UniMedCLIP by 0.59%. These dual gains suggest the utility of MMKD-CLIP as a backbone architecture for both chart searches and real-time case-query systems, an important capability for a generalist biomedical model operating across varied clinical repositories and textual conventions.

The benefits of unified multimodal representation learning are further reflected in biomedical visual question answering performance. Across both SLAKE and VQA-RAD benchmarks (Fig. 4), MMKD-CLIP shows the highest overall accuracy among evaluated models, indicating consistent recognition of anatomical structures and pathological findings, including spatial relationships and clinically relevant descriptors. Qualitative analyses suggest that the model maintains consistent reasoning even under ambiguous phrasing or partial visual occlusion, suggesting that the distilled joint embedding space captures both local visual cues and broader clinical context. This integration is consistent with patterns observed in clinical reasoning, where diagnostic decisions often require synthesizing information from imaging, pathology, and narrative reports.

Importantly, MMKD-CLIP extends beyond classification and retrieval to prognostic modeling. Survival prediction on TCGA (Fig. 5) further extends MMKD-CLIP’s utility into prognostic modeling. By fusing pathology patches with clinical reports, MMKD-CLIP shows higher C-Index values (e.g., 0.6873 versus PLIP’s 0.6675). This improvement suggests that a unified vision-language representation can capture complementary prognostic signals (morphologic patterns in histology and clinical notes) that neither modality can independently convey. In effect, MMKD-CLIP learns a single model capable of mapping multiple data sources into a risk-score prediction, supporting the development of comprehensive data-driven clinical decision support.

By distilling complementary strengths from multiple pathology teachers, MMKD-CLIP establishes a unified backbone that fine-tunes effectively for cancer diagnosis. In supervised cancer diagnosis tasks (Fig. 6a), MMKD-CLIP’s fine-tuned AUCs, ranging from 87.42% on BRACS-3 to 97.28% on TCGA-NSCLC, suggest that a distilled model can match or exceed specialist teacher performance when provided with a modest amount of labeled data. MMKD-CLIP’s low variance across folds suggests that its pretrained weights are less sensitive to sampling noise, which is a recurring challenge in medical imaging where dataset sizes vary widely. By outperforming domain-specific models (e.g., QuiltNet for histopathology, PLIP for pathology) and generalist variants (e.g., GenMedClip, BMCA), MMKD-CLIP supports the objective of this study: providing a unified foundation that can be consistently fine-tuned for diverse downstream tasks without requiring multiple separate models.

Despite these strengths, MMKD-CLIP remains subject to several important considerations. As with any distillation-based framework, the student model may implicitly inherit biases present in the teacher models, including modality imbalances, institution-specific imaging characteristics, or annotation inconsistencies. While aggregating multiple teachers helps mitigate reliance on any single source, it does not fully eliminate domain-specific biases embedded in their training data. Addressing these issues will require future work on bias-aware distillation strategies, broader inclusion of underrepresented modalities, and prospective evaluation in clinical environments.

In summary, MMKD-CLIP is a generalist biomedical foundation model that unifies diverse imaging modalities and narrative contexts. Through multi-teacher distillation, we show that integrating expert knowledge from multiple domain-specific CLIP models yields a student with higher accuracy and improved robustness compared with individual teachers. Extensive evaluations across 58 datasets and six task categories support that MMKD-CLIP provides strong performance across biomedical vision-language tasks, offering a platform for future clinical and research applications.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study used only publicly available, de-identified benchmark datasets and did not involve direct human participant recruitment or access to identifiable information. Therefore, no additional institutional ethics approval was required for this study. Informed consent was not required for this secondary analysis because all data used in this study were publicly available and de-identified.

Pretraining data curation

For pretraining, we directly utilize the PMC-OA dataset curated by the BMCA19 project. Specifically, we adopt the “Concept-Filtering” subset, which comprises approximately 6 million image-text pairs. To further refine the dataset, we apply an additional filtering step that retains only those pairs annotated with an “image_primary_label” of “Microscopy” or “Clinical Imaging.” This results in a total of 2,911,190 pairs, including 1,416,257 single-panel images and 1,494,933 multi-panel images. In Supplementary Table 2, we summarize the distribution of image-text pairs across 26 imaging modalities represented in the filtered subset.

Model design and pretraining

The pre-training and distillation of MMKD-CLIP is inspired by CLIP-KD21 and consists of two main steps. The first step utilizes approximately 2.9 million image-text pairs collected from the PMC-OA database and applies contrastive learning to align the semantics of the two modalities. In the second step, for each image-text pair, we introduce four additional unpaired text samples and perform zero-shot classification using nine biomedical CLIP models. A CLIP model is deemed a trustworthy teacher if it achieves over 90% confidence in the ground-truth class. This pipeline yields a distillation-ready quadruplet comprising the original image, original text, and the corresponding visual and textual features extracted by the teacher model. Ultimately, this pipeline yields a total of 16,535,905 quadruplet samples. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, knowledge distillation is then performed using both FD loss and ICL loss. The network backbone includes a visual encoder based on the MetaCLIP ViT-B/16 model31, and a text encoder based on the BioMed-BERT text encoder32. Model configurations are specified in Supplementary Table 1.

Multi-teacher dimension alignment

BMCA19 and PMCCLIP18 produce outputs of 768 dimensions, whereas other CLIP models output 512 dimensions, which requires dimensional alignment for distillation. Therefore, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1b, we constructed a dual-stream autoencoder for dimensional alignment. Specifically, we prepared a projection encoder for each CLIP to map their embeddings to the same dimension, with a target dimensionality of 512. The projected features of all CLIP models are then fed into a shared dual-stream autoencoder to learn a joint feature space. Finally, we designed a projection decoder for each CLIP to reconstruct the features. We do not implement contrastive learning loss in the latent space, as this may alter the distribution of the original feature space of each CLIP.

Optimizing the ICL loss can be interpreted as increasing the lower bound on mutual information between student and teacher representations. CLIP-KD21 has proved this theory. This mutual information perspective suggests that as the student learns to align with the teacher’s embeddings, particularly when contrasting against teacher outputs, it gains reduced uncertainty and better representation quality. This dynamic encourages deeper knowledge sharing from teacher to student networks.

Zero-shot and linear probing image classification

We evaluate the zero-shot and linear probing image classification abilities of MMKD-CLIP alongside previously reported medical CLIP models across 38 datasets covering nine imaging modalities. In the zero-shot scenario, ground-truth labels are not used during inference. We follow the zero-shot protocol described in CLIP2, in which labels are combined with prompt words to form sentences, and similarity is computed between the image and the corresponding positive and negative sentence samples. For both positive and negative samples, we generate multiple sentences for each label using various prompts and aggregate these by averaging their embeddings to accommodate descriptive variations. The class corresponding to the highest image-text similarity is then designated as the final classification result. For linear probing, we utilize the frozen MMKD-CLIP to extract embedded features for each image and employ a lightweight linear classifier for classification. The classifier is optimized using the training set of downstream datasets and then evaluated on their test sets. We keep the test set unchanged while varying the proportion of the training set used during optimization to evaluate generalization under limited training data. We use the AUC score for evaluation to reduce dependence on threshold selection.

Cross-modal retrieval

To evaluate whether MMKD-CLIP aligns image and text concepts in the latent space, we perform image-to-text and text-to-image retrieval. In this task, we freeze MMKD-CLIP to compute embedding features for all images and texts, and then use cosine similarity to determine the similarity between each possible pair. For text-to-image retrieval, we retrieve the top-K images closest to a given text query in the aligned latent space. Similarly, image-to-text retrieval follows an analogous procedure. We utilize the Recall@K metric to evaluate retrieval performance. This metric calculates the proportion of test set candidates where the correct match is found within the top-K retrieved samples. We use K values of {1, 10, 50}.

Visual question answering

Visual question answering (VQA) is a common task for evaluating human-computer interaction. Given an image and a natural language question, the model generates an answer to the question. We use the framework provided by PUBMEDCLIP34 to facilitate experiments on the VQA task, which treats VQA as a classification task. We replace the image and text encoders in this framework with frozen MMKD-CLIP or various other biomedical CLIP models, while keeping the rest unchanged. All models were fine-tuned using the QCR34 (question answering via conditional reasoning) framework. Finally, we use closed accuracy, open accuracy, and overall accuracy to evaluate model performance.

Supervised cancer diagnosis

We use frozen MMKD-CLIP to extract visual features of pathology slides offline, which are input into the ABMIL model35 for multiple instance learning (MIL). As shown in Fig. 1d, the output of the model is the probability distribution of cancer categories. We use AUC as the evaluation metric to reduce the impact of threshold selection.

Survival prediction

We evaluated MMKD-CLIP on a survival prediction task using a combination of pathology images and clinical reports. To this end, we used the slice dataset (0.5 μm/pixel) provided by the TCGA-UT27, which involves 32 types of cancer. In addition, following the practice of MUSK36, we matched the TCGA-UT dataset with TCGA-reports37 and eliminated cancer types with limited patient counts, ultimately selecting 12 cancer types: BRCA, LUAD, LUSC, KIRC, UCEC, HNSC, BLCA, STAD, SARC, CESC, PAAD, and MESO. We used ABMIL35 to aggregate image features and trained prognostic models for each cancer type based on multiple instance learning (MIL). Since this task predicts the probability of survival time falling into predefined intervals, it can be regarded as a classification task. We evaluated the model through five-fold cross-validation and used the concordance index (C-Index) as the evaluation metric.

Ablation of loss components

We perform an ablation study on the zero-shot image classification task across four major imaging modalities: X-ray, pathology, CT, and MRI. Furthermore, we compare the overall performance of each component on all 38 datasets ("Overall Dataset”). As shown in Fig. 6b, we compare four settings: (i) pretraining only without distillation, (ii) full model without the FD loss, (iii) full model without the ICL loss, and (iv) the full MMKD-CLIP with all components.

Implementation details

We initialize the image encoder from MetaCLIP Vit-B/1631, and initialize the text encoder from BioMed-BERT32. The size of each input image is 224 × 224 pixels. During the pretraining phase, we use the ADAMW74 optimizer with a learning rate of 5e-5. Training is conducted on two A100 GPUs with a batch size of 512 for 20 epochs, with the first 2000 steps serving as a warm-up phase. In the distillation stage, we ensure that each original image and text is traversed for 20 epochs, and each image-text pair extracts a teacher feature pair from its corresponding trustworthy teacher group for distillation. The batch size at this stage is 384.

For the linear probing image classification task, we used a single-layer fully connected neural network with the input dimension being the CLIP feature dimension and the output dimension being the number of categories in the downstream task dataset. We use the ADAMW optimizer with a learning rate of 5e-5. Training is conducted on one A6000 ADA GPU with a batch size of 128 for 20 epochs. For the VQA task, we kept the same settings as PUBMEDCLIP34. The number of epochs is set to 150. For the survival prediction task, we estimate the survival function using the Kaplan-Meier method, dividing event times into equal probability intervals to obtain time interval cut points that reflect event density. Then, we used ADAM as the optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-4 and performed 50 epochs of training. When visualizing, we grouped individuals into high-risk and low-risk categories based on the median of the predicted risk score.

Metrics

We employ 7 widely-recognized evaluation metrics to assess MMKD-CLIP’s performance across classification, retrieval, VQA, and survival prediction tasks: AUC, Accuracy, Recall, C-Index, C-Indextd, and IBS.

The AUC metric quantifies a classifier’s capability to distinguish between positive and negative classes across various decision thresholds. It is particularly important in medical classification tasks for its threshold-independent nature and ability to summarize diagnostic performance.

Accuracy (ACC) reflects the proportion of correct predictions among all predictions made. It is especially relevant in visual question answering, where it captures the model’s ability to select the correct answer from multiple choices. Recall quantifies the model’s ability to retrieve all relevant items from the total pool of ground-truth positives. In our retrieval setting, we report Recall@K, where K ∈ {1, 10, 50}, indicating the proportion of cases in which the correct match appears within the top-K retrieved results. Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were tailored to the structure of each evaluation task. For evaluations performed on a single test set, including cross-modal retrieval, visual question answering, and supervised classification, uncertainty was quantified using nonparametric bootstrap resampling with replacement. Specifically, 1000 bootstrap resamples were drawn from the corresponding independent test units, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed from the empirical bootstrap distribution. The independent unit of analysis was defined according to the task: individual queries for retrieval, question–answer pairs for VQA, and test samples from the evaluation cohort for supervised classification. For modality-level zero-shot and ablation analyses that aggregate results across multiple datasets, each dataset was treated as one independent experimental unit. Within each dataset, performance was first re-estimated using 1000 bootstrap resamples with replacement. For experiments based on five-fold cross-validation, performance was summarized as the mean across folds, and variability was reported as the standard deviation across folds. These fold-wise results reflect variation across data splits rather than independent biological replicates. When overall comparisons were made across multiple cohorts, each cohort was treated as one independent experimental unit, and statistical significance was assessed across cohorts using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For time-to-event analyses, prognostic performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C-Index). Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to visualize risk stratification, with patients divided into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median predicted risk score within each cohort. Differences between survival curves were assessed using two-sided log-rank tests.

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were determined by the size of the publicly available benchmark test sets used in each task. No data were excluded from the analyses unless exclusion criteria were explicitly defined by the original dataset providers. The experiments were not randomized because this study evaluated fixed retrospective benchmark datasets and predefined data splits. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment because all evaluations were computational and performed on predefined public datasets using fixed protocols.

All experiments were conducted using publicly available datasets with predefined or explicitly described evaluation splits, and all reported results were generated using the same analysis pipeline and fixed evaluation protocols. The code used for model training, evaluation, and statistical analysis is publicly available, enabling reproduction of the reported results.
