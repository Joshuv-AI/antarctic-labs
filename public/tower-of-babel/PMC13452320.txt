Polyethylene liner dissociation and migration in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty leading to vascular injury: a case report
Authors: Kevin Lord, Lucas R Haase, J Michael Wiater
Published: 2026

Introduction

Total shoulder arthroplasty has experienced a rapid expansion in the last decade driven by growing indications for reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA). Initially reserved for rotator cuff tear arthropathy, indications now include glenohumeral osteoarthritis, proximal humerus fractures, and revision arthroplasty.1,2 Despite growing familiarity with the surgery, complications including scapular notching, instability, stress fractures, infection, and glenoid component loosening continue to affect around 9% of patients.3,4

Polyethylene liner dissociation, where the liner separates from the humeral stem, occurs in approximately 0.7% of rTSA cases.5 The precise incidence is uncertain and based on small case series. The proposed mechanism is attributed to impingement between the humeral liner and the scapula during humeral adduction. The generated stress at the locking mechanism of the liner causes deformation of the fixation structure, leading to loss of engagement.6, 7, 8 Open revision is recommended for reduction of the joint, damage control to the prosthesis, and return of shoulder function.5,9

We present a unique case in which the dissociated polyethylene liner migrated into the axilla, leading to an injury to a branch of the axillary artery. The subsequent hematoma formation caused a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and brachial plexopathy. To our knowledge, extra-articular migration causing arterial injury and brachial plexopathy has not been previously described. Our case highlights the importance of expeditious management of polyethylene liner dissociation.

The patient was informed that the details of his case would be submitted for publication, and he provided consent.

Case report

A 70-year-old male presented to clinic for right shoulder pain and instability. He had a history of a right rTSA performed 2 years ago for cuff tear arthropathy, which was complicated by recurrent instability requiring 2 revision surgeries. The most recent revision, performed 6 months prior, was further complicated by polyethylene dissociation, prompting the referral. At initial presentation, he was neurovascularly intact, but with significant pain and dysfunction of the shoulder. Imaging demonstrated eccentric positioning of the humeral component on the glenosphere consistent with polyethylene liner dissociation without evidence of implant loosening (Fig. 1). The patient was indicated for revision rTSA.

Pre-operative radiographs demonstrating eccentric articulation of the reverse total shoulder arthroplasty on (A) the anteroposterior view as well as (B) the axillary view consistent with polyethylene liner dissociation.

Three days prior to his semi-electively scheduled surgery, he presented to the emergency center on a Saturday with acute-onset right upper extremity pain, swelling, ecchymosis, and wrist drop. Physical examination revealed median nerve sensory loss and inability to extend the digits. His hand remained warm and well perfused with a 2+ radial pulse. Plain radiographs of the shoulder were unchanged from prior. A diagnostic work-up in the emergency center included a venous duplex ultrasound that showed a right axillary and brachial vein DVT. A pulmonary embolism protocol computed tomography (CT) angiogram of the thorax demonstrated acute, bilateral pulmonary emboli. The CT angiogram of the thorax also demonstrated extra-articular migration of the polyethylene liner to abut the axillary artery with associated vascular injury and hematoma formation (Fig. 2). On serial physical examination, the neurologic deficit was nonprogressive, and the limb remained well perfused. Given the patient's hemodynamic stability, the patient was admitted to the orthopedic service for observation. Operative intervention was planned for the following Monday when a fully staffed operating room with vascular surgery back-up was available.

Coronal computed-tomographic angiogram of the thorax with intravenous contrast for pulmonary embolism protocol showing the dissociated polyethylene liner in cross-section, as indicated by the red circle.

Revision rTSA was performed with the patient in the beach chair position through the prior deltopectoral incision. Standard dissection was performed to the level of subscapularis, which was managed with a tenotomy. The polyethylene insert was confirmed to be dissociated from the humeral stem, with direct articulation between the glenosphere and metal humeral tray.

Following removal of the glenosphere and humeral tray, the wound was explored to find the polyethylene liner located in the axilla, anterior and medial to the glenohumeral joint. Gentle manipulation to extract the polyethylene insert led to heavy bright red, turbulent bleeding. Digital pressure was applied, and anesthesia staff was notified of the increased bleeding. After further dissection, which included take down of the conjoined tendon, an injury to the axillary artery or a branch thereof was confirmed and vascular surgery was called.

Vascular surgery identified organized hematoma and active arterial bleeding deep within the axillary region. The polyethylene component was removed to allow visualization of the bleeding source. Following liner extraction, the posterior humeral circumflex branch of the axillary artery was isolated as the major source of bleeding. Direct hemostasis was achieved by placing vascular clamps on the vessels, followed by sequential repair with multiple 5-0 Prolene sutures.

Revision arthroplasty was subsequently completed with retention of the humeral stem and glenoid baseplate, which were well fixed and in appropriate position. A new Catalyst OrthoScience 40 mm + 8 mm glenosphere was implanted along with upsizing of the Zimmer Biomet Identity humeral tray to a +6 mm standard neutral humeral tray and a 40 mm + 3 mm retentive vitamin-E polyethylene insert. The shoulder was reduced with excellent stability (Fig. 3). The conjoined tendon was repaired.

Immediate post-operative anteroposterior view radiograph of the right shoulder after revision reverse total shoulder arthroplasty and vascular repair, demonstrating concentric reduction of the shoulder prosthesis.

Post-operatively, the limb remained well perfused and vascular checks were performed every 4 hours. Vascular surgery managed the intravenous heparin drip for the patient’s DVT and pulmonary embolism. The patient was discharged on 5 mg of apixaban twice daily.

At 3-month follow-up, the patient had minimal pain. His radiographs showed a stable, reduced prosthesis (Fig. 4). The right shoulder exhibited improved range of motion, with 85° of active shoulder abduction and 40° of active shoulder external rotation. His radial nerve palsy persists with denervation seen on electromyography testing. Repeat electromyography is planned at 6 months. Additionally, one intraoperative culture was positive for Cutibacterium acnes. He is on on 100 mg of oral doxycycline twice daily following consultation with the infectious disease service.

Post-operative radiographs of the right shoulder at 3-month follow-up. (A) Anteroposterior view showing proper component alignment. (B) Axillary view confirming appropriate, concentric joint reduction.

Discussion

This case demonstrates a unique presentation of polyethylene liner dissociation with migration of the liner leading to arterial injury and a brachial plexopathy. Although polyethylene dissociation is a recognized, albeit rare, complication following rTSA, extra-articular migration into the axilla has not been previously reported to our knowledge. This rare complication highlights the importance of prompt diagnosis and treatment in patients with suspected polyethylene dissociation.

Existing literature explores the mechanical underpinnings of polyethylene liner dissociation. Using a finite element model, Wan et al simulated the adduction impingement between the polyethylene liner and the scapular pillar to demonstrate contraction and deformation of the fixation claws, suggesting adduction impingement may contribute to liner dissociation.6 Nam et al similarly found impingement to cause implant wear leading to polyethylene dissociation, further noting that implant instability may predispose the liner to fatigue wear.7

Several case series have described presentation and diagnosis of polyethylene dissociation without extra-articular migration. Patel et al reported an incidence of 0.7% in a series of 549 patients who underwent rTSA, with presentations including unsuccessful closed reduction attempts, pain, mechanical symptoms, and decreased motion.5 Paynter et al similarly reported an incidence of 0.7%, comprising 15.4% of all dislocations in their cohort, with radiographic signs demonstrating subluxation of the components with less than 100% displacement on CT.10 Additionally, Wren et al reported a subtle presentation of dissociated polyethylene remaining intra-articular, where maintained shoulder motion resulted in delayed initial presentation. Radiographic identification of an “overlap sign,” consisting of decreased space between the glenosphere and humeral tray, can indicate polyethylene dissociation in these cases.11 While these reports highlight various presentations and radiographic clues for polyethylene dissociation, our case uniquely demonstrates extra-articular migration and the ensuant risk of neurovascular injury.

The present case expands current understanding by demonstrating that migration of the dissociated liner can significantly increase symptom complexity and alter the treatment plan. Clinicians must remain vigilant for atypical clinical presentations such as acute-onset increased swelling, ecchymosis, hypoperfusion, limb weakness, numbness, or radiating pain.12 In the case of suspected neurovascular injury, appropriate investigation with plain radiographs and CT angiography is suggested to confirm diagnosis. Urgent revision surgery is required, and the patient should be counseled on expected recovery course associated with the nerve injury, as functional recovery may be lengthy. Salvage procedures such as tendon transfers, free functional muscle transfers, or arthrodesis can be considered for surgical management of brachial plexus injuries.13

Finally, this case highlights the critical steps when encountering heavy arterial bleeding during shoulder surgery. The axillary artery is in close proximity during anterior shoulder surgery, with studies reporting a distance of 5-20 mm from the inferior glenoid rim.14,15 If bleeding is encountered, surgeons should follow a similar sequence as described above to optimize patient outcomes. It is important to stay calm and tamponade the bleeding with direct pressure on the injured vessel. Next, alert anesthesiology to ensure adequate vascular access, order blood products, and draw any necessary laboratories. If available, the vascular surgery team should be called emergently to the operating room for evaluation and assistance. Adequate exposure of the axillary and brachial plexus can be obtained by releasing the conjoined tendon.16 As demonstrated in this case, adopting a composed protocol can improve patient outcomes and minimize the risk of long-term complications.

Conclusion

Extra-articular migration is a rare complication of polyethylene liner dissociation in rTSA. This case brings attention to the potential for neurovascular compromise caused by liner migration, highlighting the need for clinician awareness and prompt surgical management to prevent complications of suspected polyethylene liner dissociation.
