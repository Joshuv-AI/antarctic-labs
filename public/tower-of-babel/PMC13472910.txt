The chemotherapy-induced senescence-associated secretome promotes cell detachment and metastatic dissemination through metabolic reprogramming
Authors: Aidan R Cole, Raquel Buj, Apoorva Uboveja, Evan Levasseur, Alexander Tom, Hui Wang, Katarzyna M Kedziora, Adam Chatoff, Andrea Andress Huacachino, Mariola M Marcinkiewicz, Amandine Amalric, Baixue Yang, Naveen Kumar Tangudu, Emily Noonan, Jeff Danielson, Miho Naruse, Amal Taher Elhaw, Sierra White, Danyang Li, Callen T Wallace, Frederick Keeney, Felicia Lazure, Esther Elishaev, Lauren Borho, Hope A Townsend, Robin D Dowell, Aaron Clauset, Dorota E Jazwinska, Matthew S Laird, Huda Atiya, Denarda Dangaj, Lan G Coffman, George Tseng, Steffi Oesterreich, Andrew Kossenkov, Qin Liu, Ana P Gomes, Aditi U Gurkar, Francisco J Schopfer, Francesmary Modugno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCellular senescence is a consequence of many chemotherapeutics that plays context-dependent roles in cancer. Senescent cells secrete an array of factors collectively known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Here we show that the cisplatin-induced SASP enhances the detachment of high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) cells in vitro and dissemination in vivo. We identify fructose as a metabolic component of the SASP that facilitates cell detachment and show that a high-fructose diet increases HGSOC dissemination in vivo. We identified complex I as the driver of SASP-mediated cell detachment and HGSOC dissemination. Mechanistically, this effect was driven by SASP-mediated inhibition of an NAD+–SIRT–SREBP axis, leading to decreased plasma membrane cholesterol that increased cell detachment. These findings reveal that the SASP reprograms the metabolic microenvironment, promoting metastatic dissemination in a paracrine fashion, and highlight a pro-tumorigenic metabolic effect of fructose in the SASP that may contribute to the high recurrence rate of HGSOC.Subject terms: Senescence, Metabolomics, Cancer, Ageing

Main

Cellular senescence is defined as a stable cell cycle arrest1. In cancer, senescence is a common cell fate induced by chemotherapy (termed therapy-induced senescence (TIS)). TIS was initially thought to be a beneficial therapeutic response1; however, over the past decade this paradigm has shifted, and multiple papers have reported a more nuanced and complex role for TIS in cancer. Recent evidence has shown that TIS paradoxically enhances tumorigenesis, metastasis, and chemoresistance in a paracrine fashion2–7. The pro-tumorigenic role for TIS in cancer is due in part to the unique secretome of senescent cells (termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)). This secretome is composed of a variety of factors and is different depending on both the senescence inducer and cell type1. The SASP is often described as a transcriptional program that alters the inflammatory phenotype of senescent cells and their tumor microenvironment (TME). However, SASP factors go beyond transcriptionally regulated inflammatory mediators, and there is evidence that metabolites such as bioactive lipids, fatty acids, nucleobases, amino acids, and others are SASP components8–10. Moreover, senescent cells reprogram their intracellular metabolism11, and a few publications have pointed to depletion of certain metabolites in the SASP8,12. Yet the metabolic components of the SASP remain underexplored, and it is still unknown how these metabolites influence non-senescent bystander cancer cells within the TME.

One chemotherapy well known to promote TIS is cisplatin13. This is of particular importance in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), in which platinum-based therapies such as cisplatin are standard of care14. While the majority of patients with HGSOC initially respond to platinum-based chemotherapy, disease recurrence is common, accounting for ~90% of HGSOC deaths15. The use of chemotherapy is associated with elevated metastatic dissemination of ovarian cancer16,17, and patients with recurrent HGSOC all have disseminated disease18. Metastatic dissemination of HGSOC occurs most frequently through the transcoelomic route, defined as shedding of cancer cells from a solid tumor (either the primary tumor or peritoneal tumor nodules) into the peritoneal cavity19. The first step in transcoelomic dissemination is detachment of cells from the primary tumor. The role of the cisplatin-induced SASP in detachment and dissemination remains unknown. The use of HGSOC is an ideal model to investigate these important questions in cancer senescence. In this Article, we aimed to investigate the paracrine effects of the cisplatin-induced SASP produced by HGSOC cells on metastatic dissemination.

Results

The cisplatin-induced SASP promotes HGSOC dissemination in vivo

Platinum-based therapies such as cisplatin are standard of care for patients with HGSOC, although the majority of HGSOCs initially treated with platinum will recur with widespread disseminated disease16,17. Using multiple publicly available bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets from matched pre- and post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT)-treated HGSOC tumor samples20,21, we found evidence of robust senescence signatures in patients with HGSOC after platinum chemotherapy (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Tables 1–3). Similar upregulation in senescence-associated genes was also observed in three publicly available NanoString datasets of matched NACT-treated tumors (Extended Data Fig. 1a)16,22–25. Together, these data show that cisplatin induces senescence. To further investigate the intratumoral senescence response to chemotherapy, we applied a senescence score derived from these signatures (see Methods for details) to publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data of matched pre- and post-NACT-treated patient tumors26. As expected, multiple cell types show an increase in the senescence signature, including malignant epithelial cells and other stromal cells (Extended Data Fig. 1b). There was a heterogenous senescence induction in malignant epithelial cell populations (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 4), suggesting a post-chemotherapy TME comprising both senescent malignant cells and proliferative malignant “bystander” cells (Fig. 1c). While TIS has been frequently reported, and the SASP from multiple senescent cell sources is known to affect cancer progression, the contribution of the malignant cell SASP on dissemination and tumor progression of malignant bystander cells remains unclear. Given the complexity of senescence induction in vivo, here we aimed to determine the paracrine effects of the cisplatin-induced SASP from malignant cells on HGSOC bystander cells.

Towards this goal, we first induced senescence in two different HGSOC cell lines using cisplatin (Extended Data Fig. 2a). Senescence in HGSOC cells was confirmed using multiple markers, including decreased proliferation and 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation, increased senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) activity, and decreased lamin B1 expression (Extended Data Fig. 2b–e). We also confirmed high expression of prototypical SASP cytokines using a cytokine array (Extended Data Fig. 2f) and by RNA expression (Extended Data Fig. 2g,h). Finally, we observed increased sensitivity of cisplatin-induced senescent cells to the senolytic navitoclax (ABT-263) (Extended Data Fig. 2i). Together, these data strongly support the notion that cisplatin treatment induces a strong senescent phenotype in these cellular models. To determine how the presence of senescent cells affects HGSOC progression in vivo, we co-injected naive proliferating HGSOC cells with vehicle-treated or cisplatin-induced senescent HGSOC cells (Fig. 1d). It is important to note that we injected the same number of proliferating cells in both groups, and the senescent cells were labeled with GFP (see Methods for details). We observed an increase in tumor burden in mice co-injected with naive proliferating cells + senescent cells compared to naive proliferating cells + vehicle control cells (Fig. 1e). Moreover, we noted a metastatic tropism to the omentum, intestines, visceral fat, and peritoneum (Fig. 1f), while other sites were not markedly affected (Extended Data Fig. 3a). The increase in tumor burden was not a result of senescent cells regaining proliferative capacity in vivo (Extended Data Fig. 3b). To more directly determine the contribution of the SASP to this increase in tumor burden, we used a conditioned media approach. We treated KPCA.B HGSOC cells with vehicle or cisplatin to induce senescence, washed off the drug, and conditioned media for an additional 2 days (Extended Data Fig. 2a). We then injected conditioned media from cisplatin-induced senescent HGSOC cells (SCM) or proliferating controls (PCM) into tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 1g). SCM alone was sufficient to increase tumor burden and metastatic spread (Fig. 1h,i and Extended Data Fig. 3c). There was no marked difference in omental weights or Ki67 staining in either model (Fig. 1j,k), showing that the observed increase in tumor nodules was likely not a result of increased proliferation of cancer cells. SCM did not increase bystander HGSOC cell proliferation in vitro (Extended Data Fig. 3d). In addition, we did not observe differences in terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining in the tumors (Fig. 1l), and SCM did not affect anoikis in vitro (Extended Data Fig. 3e), showing that the SASP does not affect the viability of HGSOC cells in suspension. Together, these data show that the SASP from cisplatin-induced senescent HGSOC cells is sufficient to increase HGSOC dissemination in vivo, and this is not a result of either increased proliferation or decreased cell death.

The cisplatin-induced SASP promotes detachment of bystander HGSOC cells via NDUFA5

We found that the cisplatin-induced SASP increases the number of tumor nodules without corresponding changes in cancer cell proliferation or death (Fig. 1 and Extended Data Fig. 3), suggesting that secreted factors from malignant cells influence metastatic dissemination. During transcoelomic dissemination within the peritoneal cavity, HGSOC cells must detach from the solid tumor by loosening their adhesion to other cells and the extracellular matrix19,27. Thus, we investigated how the cisplatin-induced SASP from HGSOC cells influences detachment and adhesion of bystander HGSOC cells. To measure detachment, we performed time-lapse imaging of spheroids cultured in PCM or SCM. Spheroids cultured in round-bottom ultra-low attachment (ULA) wells were of similar size regardless of whether they were exposed to PCM or SCM, and we did not observe differential compaction between these conditions (Extended Data Fig. 4a), showing that HGSOC cells in SCM remain able to form spheroids of the same size as those in PCM under gravitational constraint. Thus, for live cell imaging experiments, we controlled for cell number and spheroid size by forming spheroids in round-bottom ULA wells before transferring them to flat-bottom ULA wells (Fig. 2a). After transferring spheroids to flat-bottom ULA wells, we observed an increase in the total number of live cell detachment events in both Ovcar8 and KPCA.B cells when bystander cells were cultured in SCM compared to PCM (Fig. 2b,c and Supplementary Video 1), indicating that cells were more loosely adherent. Staining with ethidium homodimer confirmed the detached cells were viable (Fig. 2b). Heat inactivating the SCM to denature proteins also drove detachment of live cells to the same level as regular SCM (Fig. 2c), showing that the observed increase in detachment was likely not a result of protein activity, including extracellular matrix degraders such as matrix metalloproteases. As a complementary approach, we assessed detachment using a controlled trypsinization assay (Fig. 2d), similar to a previous publication28. Consistent with our live cell imaging results in spheroids (Fig. 2c), we found that bystander cells cultured in SCM had increased detachment compared to those cultured in PCM, and the increase in detachment was also retained in heat-inactivated SCM (Fig. 2e). SCM from carboplatin-induced senescent HGSOC cells, cisplatin-induced senescent melanoma cells, and gemcitabine-induced senescent pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells all increased detachment of bystander cells (Extended Data Fig. 4b–d). These data indicate that the effect is not platinum- nor HGSOC cell-specific. Furthermore, SCM from normal fibroblasts undergoing replicative senescence or chemotherapy-induced senescence also increased detachment of bystander HGSOC cells (Extended Data Fig. 4e–g). Together, these data show that the SASP from multiple senescent cell types induces malignant bystander cell detachment. While most previous work has focused on the protein content of the SASP, we and others have shown that the SASP can also have differential metabolite components8–10,12. Therefore, we aimed to determine whether the observed phenotype is driven by a small molecule or metabolite by filtering the SCM to remove most proteins and large extracellular vesicles (>3 kDa), leaving small molecules such as metabolites. Bystander HGSOC cells cultured in filtered SCM also showed increased detachment to the same level as regular SCM (Fig. 2e). Together, these data provide evidence that a metabolic component of the cisplatin-induced SASP may drive the observed increased detachment of bystander HGSOC cells.

Given the observed changes in detachment in heat-inactivated and filtered SCM (Fig. 2e), we reasoned that there is a metabolic component of the SASP that likely has paracrine metabolic effects on bystander cells to decrease their adhesion. To investigate this mechanism, we conducted a CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats) knockout screen using a metabolically focused guide RNA library. We adopted the controlled trypsinization approach to separate the attached and detached cells from SCM and PCM cultured groups. The live cell detachment from spheroids was not optimal for this purpose due to the low numbers of single cell detachment (Fig. 2a–c). Given the consistency in results between live cell imaging and the controlled trypsinization experiments (Fig. 2a–e), we used this assay for our screen. We aimed to identify genes that were negatively enriched in detached bystander cells cultured in SCM but not PCM, indicating these genes are essential for the loosely adhesive phenotype driven by the SASP (Fig. 2f). We found that oxidative phosphorylation genes were negatively enriched in the detached population of SCM-cultured bystander cells compared to adherent cells (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Conversely, cholesterol biosynthesis genes were positively enriched in the detached population of SCM-cultured bystander cells compared to adherent cells (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6), showing that knockout of these genes drives detachment in SCM conditions. This is in stark contrast with the gene pathways identified in PCM cultured condition (Supplementary Table 6), indicating a unique metabolic requirement for detachment driven by the cisplatin-induced SASP. The negative enrichment oxidative phosphorylation signature was mainly driven by complex I subunits (Supplementary Table 7). We confirmed that complex I genes were negatively enriched specifically in detached bystander cells cultured in SCM but not in PCM (Extended Data Fig. 5a). A recent report showed that electron transport chain genes are enriched after patient-derived cells are cultured in ascites collected after chemotherapy29. In addition, analysis of metastatic recurrence in a post-chemotherapy in vivo model identified high Nduf4l2—a complex I subunit—in malignant cells30. To show the potential clinical relevance of our findings, we assessed HGSOC stage based on expression of complex I or respiration genes in which advanced stage acts as a proxy for increased dissemination. Indeed, high expression of complex I or respiration genes corresponds to increased HGSOC stage in treatment-naive patients (Fig. 2h and Extended Data Fig. 5b) even when adjusting for age (P < 0.001). Together with our data, this indicates a key role for complex I in detachment and dissemination.

Next, we aimed to validate our CRISPR screen results. Towards this goal, we used the complex I inhibitor rotenone and confirmed the necessity of functional complex I for the increased detachment phenotype of bystander cells cultured in SCM (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Moreover, we observed an increase in both basal and maximal respiration in bystander cells cultured in SCM compared to PCM (Fig. 2i and Extended Data Fig. 5d), indicating a cisplatin-induced SASP-mediated increase in respiration in bystander HGSOC cells. For further mechanistic experiments, we chose the complex I subunit NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase subunit A5 (NDUFA5), which was one of the top differentially negatively enriched genes in the CRISPR screen (Extended Data Fig. 5e–g) and was required for the increase in SCM-mediated respiration in bystander cells (Extended Data Fig. 5h). We also validated the requirement of NDUFA5 knockdown for decreased detachment in bystander HGSOC cells cultured in SCM in both HGSOC models (Fig. 2j,k). Finally, we found that knockdown of Ndufa5 abrogated the increase in dissemination in both the senescent cell co-injection model and the conditioned media treatment model in vivo (Fig. 2l–n and Extended Data Fig. 5i–k). Together, these data show that the cisplatin-induced SASP from malignant cells regulates HGSOC cell detachment and dissemination in a paracrine manner via NDUFA5.

Finally, we aimed to determine how complex I regulates the increased detachment phenotype. We did not observe an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) following NDUFA5 knockdown in bystander cells cultured in SCM, and scavenging ROS in these cells using N-acetyl-cysteine did not decrease detachment (Extended Data Fig. 5l–m), indicating that the SASP regulates cell adhesion via complex I activity independently of ROS generation. Complex I is also critical for oxidation of NADH to NAD+ (ref. 31) (Fig. 2o). Therefore, we aimed to determine whether replenishing NAD+ using nicotinic acid supplementation (Fig. 2o) would reverse the NDUFA5 knockdown-mediated decrease in detachment of bystander cells in the context of the SASP. Indeed, nicotinic acid supplementation of NDUFA5 knockdown bystander cells cultured in SCM decreased the NAD+/NADH ratio (Extended Data Fig. 5n) and restored the SASP-mediated increase in detachment (Fig. 2p). Finally, to show NADH oxidation is sufficient to drive detachment in bystander cells, we overexpressed the NADH oxidase from Lactobacillus brevis, LbNOX, in NDUFA5 knockout bystander cells (Fig. 2o and Extended Data Fig. 5o). LbNOX expression rescued detachment in SCM-cultured NDUFA5 knockout bystanders (Fig. 2q). These data point to a critical role for complex I-mediated NAD+ generation in the regulation of cell attachment in the context of the SASP.

Bystander cell detachment is increased through inhibition of SREBP1-mediated plasma membrane cholesterol downstream of NDUFA5

We found that the complex I subunit NDUFA5 drives the cisplatin-induced SASP-mediated increased cell detachment in bystander HGSOC cells (Fig. 2). To understand how NDUFA5 regulates cell detachment, we performed RNA-seq on human and murine HGSOC cells with NDUFA5 knockdown (via single guide RNA or short hairpin RNA, respectively) cultured in either SCM or PCM. Pathway analysis of genes consistently upregulated by NDUFA5 knockdown in SCM, but not in PCM (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 8), revealed enrichment of cholesterol-related gene signatures (Supplementary Table 9). More specifically, de novo cholesterol synthesis genes were downregulated in bystander cells cultured in SCM, which was rescued by NDUFA5 knockdown (Extended Data Fig. 6a). We found a negative correlation between the senescence signature and de novo cholesterol synthesis gene signatures in NACT-treated ovarian cancer patients (Extended Data Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table 1). In addition, we used a dataset in which primary ovarian cancer cells were isolated from individual patients and cultured with autologous ascites fluid collected before or after platinum plus taxane chemotherapy29. Gene sets related to cholesterol synthesis were consistently downregulated in cells cultured in ascites after chemotherapy compared to before chemotherapy (Fig. 3b). Collectively, these data support the notion that transcriptional downregulation of de novo cholesterol synthesis is a consequence of the senescent secretome. Consistent with the idea that cholesterol regulates detachment in malignant bystander cells cultured in SCM, our CRISPR knockout screen also identified multiple de novo cholesterol synthesis enzymes to be positively enriched in the detached population (Fig. 2g). In particular, HMGCR and HMGCS1, the first two enzymes of the mevalonate pathway, were both positively enriched in our CRISPR knockout screen and upregulated in SCM-cultured bystander cells following knockdown of NDUFA5 (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). Together, these data indicate that the SCM-driven loss of detachment in bystander cells is a result of transcription downregulation of de novo cholesterol synthesis downstream of NDUFA5.

Cholesterol is an important component of the plasma membrane and has been previously shown to help stabilize integrins, thereby increasing cell adhesion32,33. Thus, we aimed to assess cholesterol content changes downstream of NDUFA5 using filipin III, a fluorescent cholesterol probe, and total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) imaging, which selectively illuminates the plasma membrane in close proximity to the coverslip. We confirmed a decrease in plasma membrane cholesterol of bystander cells cultured in SCM, which was rescued by knockdown of NDUFA5 (Fig. 3c). Supplementation with nicotinic acid increased the plasma membrane cholesterol content of NDUFA5 knockdown cells (Fig. 3c), showing this effect is through NAD+. We confirmed a decrease in non-esterified cholesterol by mass spectrometry (Extended Data Fig. 6e). This was not simply a result of differences in cholesterol trafficking to the plasma membrane as total cholesterol measured by widefield fluorescence filipin III was also similarly altered (Extended Data Fig. 6f,g). In addition, expression of LbNOX to regenerate NAD+ increased whole-cell filipin III staining (Extended Data Fig. 6h,i). To determine whether these changes in cholesterol play a functional role in cell detachment, we depleted cholesterol with either methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD), which specifically depletes plasma membrane cholesterol, or statins to deplete intracellular cholesterol through blocking de novo cholesterol synthesis. Both MβCD and statins abrogated the decreased detachment in NDUFA5 knockdown bystander cells cultured in SCM (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). Conversely, supplementation of cholesterol decreased detachment in bystander cells cultured in SCM but had no effect on NDUFA5 knockdown bystander cells cultured in SCM (Fig. 3e). Cholesterol supplementation also rescued the effects of statins, confirming that detachment was a result of their on-target effects (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). These data show that the cisplatin-induced SASP drives differential cholesterol content in cells via NDUFA5-mediated NAD+, which is critical for the detachment phenotype in bystander cells.

We found that the changes in de novo cholesterol were transcriptionally driven downstream of NDUFA5 (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 6a), and decreased cholesterol drives the increased detachment phenotype of bystander cells cultured in cisplatin-induced SASP (Fig. 3c,d). Transcription of de novo cholesterol synthesis genes is well known to be coordinated via the transcription factor sterol regulatory element-binding transcription factor (SREBP1)34. Next, we sought to determine whether SREBP1 contributes to the downregulation of de novo cholesterol synthesis genes and the increase in cell detachment downstream of NDUFA5 in SCM-cultured bystander cells. Inhibition of SREBP increased detachment of control bystander cells cultured in PCM and NDUFA5 knockdown bystander cells cultured in SCM (Fig. 3f,g). Moreover, SREBP activation through serum starvation rescued detachment in SCM-cultured bystanders (Fig. 3g). Together, these data show the requirement of SREBP1 for detachment. These data are also consistent with results showing that cholesterol depletion increases detachment in NDUFA5 knockdown bystander cells cultured in SCM (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). SREBP1 translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to target genes to regulate their transcription34. We observed a decrease in both nuclear SREBP1 and SREBP1 occupancy on multiple de novo cholesterol synthesis gene promoters in bystander cells cultured in SCM that was rescued by NDUFA5 knockdown (Fig. 3h,i and Extended Data Fig. 7c). The decreased SREBP1 occupancy corresponded to decreased protein expression of HMGCR, HMGCS1, and MVK (Extended Data Fig. 7d). These data are consistent with the observed decrease in de novo cholesterol synthesis gene expression (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 6a). Nicotinic acid supplementation abrogated the NDUFA5 knockdown-mediated decrease in nuclear SREBP1 and occupancy of SREBP1 at de novo cholesterol genes (Fig. 3h–l). This reinforces a role for NAD+ downstream of complex I activity in regulating SREBP activity. In samples from patients with HGSOC, we found that expression of complex I or aerobic respiration genes inversely correlates with cholesterol synthesis genes regulated by the master transcription factor SREBP (Fig. 3m, Extended Data Fig. 7e, and Supplementary Table 1). Together, these data show that downregulation of SREBP1 nuclear localization and activity by SCM decreases de novo cholesterol synthesis gene transcription and adhesion downstream of NDUFA5 (Extended Data Fig. 7f).

NDUFA5 suppresses SREBP1 via increased NAD+-dependent SIRT activity, leading to decreased cholesterol and detachment

We found that SCM decreases SREBP1 activity via the complex I subunit NDUFA5, leading to increased detachment in bystander cells (Fig. 3). In addition, NAD+ pool replenishment with nicotinic acid reversed the decreased detachment caused by NDUFA5 knockdown and restored SREBP1 nuclear localization and promoter occupancy at cholesterol synthesis genes (Fig. 3h–l), altogether showing a pivotal role for NAD+ in this process. Replenishing NAD+ to SREBP-inhibited NDUFA5 knockdown bystander cells cultured in SCM did not change detachment (Fig. 4a), indicating that NAD+ is indeed upstream of SREBP1 activity. Previous reports show that SREBPs can be deactivated by NAD+-dependent deacetylases known as sirtuins35–37 (SIRTs). Thus, we reasoned that SIRT activity driven by NAD+ may increase detachment downstream of NDUFA5 by inhibiting SREBP1-mediated cholesterol synthesis. Indeed, inhibition of SIRTs prevented detachment in SCM-cultured bystander cells (Fig. 4b). This was not a result of a parallel pathway as inhibition of SIRTs did not affect detachment in NDUFA5 knockdown cells cultured in SCM (Fig. 4b). Addition of nicotinic acid to NDUFA5 knockdown cells cultured in SCM increased detachment, which was abrogated by inhibition of SIRTs (Fig. 4b). Together, these data reveal a pathway in which NDUFA5 acts upstream of SIRTs via NAD+. To confirm that SREBP1 activity and SIRT activity are acting in the same pathway, we assessed detachment of bystander cells in response to both SIRT and SREBP inhibition. SIRT inhibition alone decreased detachment of bystander cells cultured in SCM, but not when combined with the SREBP inhibitor (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, we found that inhibition of SIRTs increased nuclear SREBP1 in bystander cells cultured in SCM (Fig. 4d,e) and increased SREBP1 occupancy at de novo cholesterol gene promoters (Fig. 4f). Finally, given the role of cholesterol in our pathway downstream of NDUFA5 and SREBP1 (Fig. 3), we assessed cholesterol content in SCM-cultured bystander cells treated with the SIRT inhibitor. SIRT inhibition increased cholesterol (Fig. 4g). Consistent with the notion that SIRT activity is downstream of NDUFA5, SIRT inhibition, which does not affect detachment of bystander NDUFA5 knockdown cells cultured in SCM (Fig. 4b), also had no effect on nuclear SREBP1 (Fig. 4e) or cholesterol (Fig. 4h). Together, these data support the idea that the cisplatin-induced SASP promotes bystander cell detachment via NAD+-dependent SIRT activity downstream of NDUFA5, which inhibits SREBP1-mediated cholesterol synthesis (Extended Data Fig. 7f).

Increased fructose in the cisplatin-induced SASP drives the paracrine increase in cell detachment

Finally, we aimed to determine the upstream signal in the SASP that regulates detachment in a paracrine fashion. We found that both heat-inactivated and <3 kD-filtered SCM increase detachment (Fig. 2e), indicating that the signal promoting detachment is a small molecule or metabolite. Senescent cells are known to increase glucose uptake8,12,38,39, resulting in a glucose-depleted SASP. Indeed, we observed a significant decrease in glucose abundance in conditioned media from cisplatin-induced senescent cells versus vehicle controls (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 8a). To understand how decreased glucose in the SASP from cisplatin-induced senescent cells affects bystander cells in a paracrine fashion, we performed mass spectrometry and assessed glycolytic intermediates. Although conditioned media from cisplatin-induced senescent cells have decreased glucose (Fig. 5a), we observed an increase in glycolytic intermediates in bystander cells cultured in SCM (Extended Data Fig. 8b,c). One notable metabolite that was upregulated in bystander cells cultured in SCM is dihydroxyacetone phosphate (Extended Data Fig. 8d), which can be derived from fructolysis (Extended Data Fig. 8b). Indeed, we found that conditioned media from multiple senescent cell types and inducers had increased fructose abundance (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 8e), consistent with previous reports showing that high glucose can induce polyol pathway activity, which converts glucose to fructose40–42. To determine how the relative availability of fructose and glucose contribute to bystander cell adhesion, we cultured bystander cells in PCM supplemented with a physiological concentration of fructose or SCM supplemented with glucose. The addition of fructose to PCM cultured bystander cells was sufficient to induce detachment in controls but not NDUFA5 knockdowns (Fig. 5c), showing that fructose is upstream of NDUFA5. Conversely, the addition of glucose inhibited detachment of bystander cells cultured in SCM (Extended Data Fig. 8f), indicating that the balance between fructose and glucose drives the observed detachment. Knocking down the enzymes responsible for the first step of fructolysis, ketohexokinase (KHK) and aldolase B (ALDOB), prevented detachment in SCM-cultured bystander cells (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 8g), supporting the idea that fructolysis promotes detachment. Previous reports have shown that fructolysis can induce aerobic respiration through activating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)43. Indeed, we found that bystander cells cultured in SCM showed increased AMPK phosphorylation upstream of NDUFA5 (Extended Data Fig. 9a). Similarly, fructose supplementation led to increased AMPK phosphorylation in PCM cultured bystander cells, while phosphorylation is inhibited by glucose supplementation in SCM (Extended Data Fig. 9b). We also observed a modest increase in glucose-derived citrate in the presence of fructose (Extended Data Fig. 9c,d), potentially indicating increased glycolytic flux into the mitochondria to support respiration. Finally, we sought to determine whether fructose is upstream of decreased SREBP1 activity and cholesterol abundance. Addition of fructose to PCM, but not to NDUFA5 knockdown cells, was sufficient to decrease nuclear SREBP1 and cholesterol content of bystander cells (Fig. 5e–h). Conversely, the addition of glucose increased SREBP1 nuclear localization and cholesterol content of bystander cells cultured in SCM (Extended Data Fig. 9e–h). In patient samples, high expression of the fructose transporter SLC2A5 after NACT treatment corresponded with low expression of de novo cholesterol synthesis genes (Extended Data Fig. 9i and Supplementary Table 1). Together, these data show that fructose suppresses nuclear SREBP1, corresponding to less cholesterol and increased detachment. These data also show that restoring the balance between glucose and fructose overcomes this phenotype in SCM-cultured bystander cells.

Fructose can have pleotropic systemic effects. Thus, we finally aimed to assess whether the increased fructose-driven dissemination is due in part to cancer cell fructolysis and complex I activity. To this end, we first provided mice with fructose and then implanted control or shKhk HGSOC cells (Fig. 6h). Khk knockdown decreased dissemination in mice with fructose supplementation without affecting omental weights (Fig. 6i,j and Extended Data Fig. 10d), indicating the necessity of malignant cell fructose utilization in driving dissemination. Next, we implanted control and shNdufa5 HGSOC cells into mice on a high-fructose diet (Fig. 6h). Ndufa5 knockdown abrogated dissemination in mice on a high-fructose diet (Fig. 6k,l) but did not affect omental weights (Extended Data Fig. 10e), which is consistent with our previous experiment using Ndufa5 knockdown cells co-injected with senescent cells (Fig. 2l–n). These data show the sufficiency of fructose to drive HGSOC dissemination through both fructolysis and complex I activity in vivo. Together with mechanistic and patient data, these studies point to a previously unrecognized role for fructose in the secretome of senescent cells in promoting cell detachment through a complex I–NAD+–SIRT–SREBP1–cholesterol synthesis axis and suggest that lowering dietary fructose may have beneficial clinical outcomes for patients with HGSOC (Extended Data Fig. 10f).

Discussion

The phenomenon of senescence has gained increasing attention in cancer research owing to its complex role in influencing the TME through its unique secretome. In this study, we investigated the impact of TIS cell metabolism and the malignant cell SASP on HGSOC progression to better understand how chemotherapy-induced metabolic reprogramming affects tumor progression. While the role of SASP proteins in affecting cell adhesion and detachment is well described through direct matrix remodeling or bystander cell signaling effects44–47, our results show that fructose is a part of the SASP from cisplatin-induced senescent HGSOC cells, which is sufficient to increase cell detachment and drive increased metastatic dissemination in HGSOC. These studies have important implications for understanding how chemotherapy alters tumor dynamics in a paracrine fashion, show a mechanism of how senescence impacts bystander cancer cells on a molecular level, and reveal a potential role for diet in modifying HGSOC progression.

We found that the secretome from cisplatin-induced senescent HGSOC cells alone increases detachment of bystander cells and drives metastatic dissemination in a model of HGSOC, although the aggressive nature of this cell line precluded us from determining whether this affects progression-free survival. Another limitation of this study is that the in vivo models were only performed in one cell line. While previous studies have shown that senescent cells promote tumor progression and metastasis through co-injection or inducible models2,4,48,49, these effects reflect the combined influence of senescent cells and their secretome. In our model, by injecting only conditioned media from senescent cancer cells, we specifically isolate the impact of the secretome in the absence of senescent cells, showing that secreted factors alone are sufficient to drive the metastatic phenotype (Fig. 1). While components of the SASP have been linked to metastasis in various cell and cancer types, many of these are pro-inflammatory mediators, and the non-protein, non-cytokine fractions of this secretome are often overlooked. In fact, there are only a handful of reports on the metabolic components of the SASP, such as elevated bioactive lipid production, nucleobases, amino acids, nitric oxide, and ROS8–10,50–52. Moreover, only a small number of studies to date have indicated that specific metabolites are depleted from the SASP8,12. While these reports investigated the metabolite’s role in senescence induction, to our knowledge, no study to date has investigated the paracrine effects of the extracellular metabolite composition on proliferative bystander cells. Here we found that glucose is depleted in the cisplatin-induced SASP (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 8a). This is likely a result of increased glucose uptake and glycolysis of senescent cells, which has been previously reported8,12. High glucose can induce polyol pathway activity, which converts glucose to fructose40–42. How and why this occurs is an interesting point for future studies.

We found that high dietary fructose increases metastatic dissemination in vivo (Fig. 6). While glucose is the most abundant monosaccharide in the body, fructose makes up a large portion of sugar consumed. Fructose is present alone and together with glucose in sucrose in foods such as honey, fruits, vegetables, and high-fructose corn syrup. Resting plasma fructose levels are in the tens of micromolar but can increase 10-fold after consuming fructose53. Although our data implicate a role of fructose acting directly on malignant bystander cells (Fig. 6), fructose metabolism can indirectly support tumor growth through the microbiome or liver lipogenesis54,55, and future studies will determine the contribution of these pathways to metastatic dissemination. Given the dietary and endogenous availability and our findings on fructose driving dissemination, fructose may play an underappreciated role in modulating tumor cell behavior. This raises the possibility that restricting fructose intake could be a promising dietary intervention to assist in treatment response.

Our data reveal a critical role for respiration in reducing cell adhesion downstream of fructose, allowing cells to detach and disseminate (Fig. 2), although additional studies are needed to further interrogate the mechanism of how fructose activates complex I. There is growing evidence that mitochondrial function and efficient respiration supports tumor progression and metastasis56–61. Likewise, our data support the notion that plasma membrane cholesterol depletion in cancer cells can lead to a more disseminated phenotype (Fig. 3), raising questions about the use of statins in cancer patients. Recent mechanistic investigation into statin use in cancer revealed heterogenous responses, including increasing metastatic potential62. Taken together with our data, this highlights the potential context dependency in cholesterol metabolism in malignant cell phenotypes, although the interpretation of the systemic effects of long-term statin treatment can be difficult. The translation of these findings to ovarian cancer could represent a therapeutic strategy to specifically target senescent cells and mitigate the metastatic potential induced by the SASP.

It is interesting to speculate whether these changes in adhesion and detachment observed in bystander cells exposed to SCM could be therapeutically targeted. One such therapeutic route is the use of senolytics, a class of drugs designed to selectively target and eliminate senescent cells63. This is a particularly attractive approach given that we observed an increase in senescence signatures in multiple cell types in addition to malignant epithelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 1b), and the SASP from non-malignant cell types is enriched in fructose (Extended Data Fig. 8e) and induces cell detachment (Extended Data Fig. 4g). Moreover, given our dietary studies (Fig. 6), it is also interesting to speculate that diet interventions could be used in concert with senolytics to attain a greater benefit. This will be an area for the field to explore further. Finally, there is a large body of literature regarding the aging-related decline in NAD+ and surrounding the use of supplements in aging to replenish NAD+ levels64. We found that supplementing cells with nicotinic acid increased detachment in vitro (Fig. 2p), and previous work found that NAD+ promotes the pro-inflammatory SASP65. Thus, our studies herein would indicate that nicotinic acid supplements in cancer patients, regardless of age, may be detrimental, although this needs to be experimentally investigated.

In summary, this study sheds light on a previously unexplored facet of ovarian cancer by revealing how the secretome of cisplatin-induced senescent cells contributes to HGSOC phenotypes. The SASP-induced dissemination, metabolic reprogramming leading to weakened adhesion and increased detachment, and the metabolite driving these changes provide a unique perspective on the complex interplay between senescence and cancer progression. These findings underscore the importance of understanding all components of the SASP and its multifaceted impact on the TME, offering potential therapeutic targets for mitigating ovarian cancer metastasis.

Methods

All experiments with animals were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Pittsburgh (24024583) or The Wistar Institute (201727). Humane end points were >20% change in weight and body condition score of 2 (out of 5). None of these limits were exceeded in any of the experiments. Pretreatment blood was collected from patients with newly diagnosed epithelial ovarian cancer under University of Pittsburgh approved Institutional Review Board protocols 20050019, 19070449, and 19060197. Written, informed consent was obtained from ovarian cancer patients.

Cell lines

Ovcar8 cells were a gift from Dr. Benjamin Bitler (University of Colorado). KPCA.B cells were a gift from Dr. Robert Weinberg (MIT). IMR90 (CCL-186), YUMM5.2 (CRL-3367), and Capan-1 (HTB-79) cells were obtained from ATCC, and HEK293FT cells were obtained from Thermo Fisher (R70007). Ovcar8 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Corning 10-040-CV) supplemented with 5% FBS (BioWest, S1620). KPCA.B cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Fisher Scientific, 12430047) supplemented with 4% heat-inactivated FBS (Sigma, S1620), 2 ng ml−1 hEGF (Sigma, E9644), and Insulin–Transferrin–Selenium (Fisher Scientific, 41400-045). IMR90 cells were cultured in DMEM (Corning, 10-017-CV) supplemented with 10% FBS (BioWest, S1620), L-glutamine (Corning, 25-015-CI), non-essential amino acids (Corning, 25-025-CI), sodium pyruvate (Corning, 25-000-CI), and sodium bicarbonate (Corning, 25-035-CI). YUMM5.2 cells were cultured in DMEM and Ham’s F-12 50/50 media (Corning, 10-092-CV) supplemented with nonessential amino acids (Corning, 25-025-Cl) and 10% FBS (BioWest, S1620). Capan-1 cells were cultured in IMDM (Gibco, 12440053) supplemented with 20% FBS (BioWest, S1620). HEK293FT cells were cultured in DMEM (Corning, 10-013-CV) supplemented with 10% FBS according to ATCC. All cell lines were cultured in 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Fisher Scientific, 15-140-122) at 37 °C under 5% CO2 and atmospheric O2 (except IMR90 cells, cultured at 2% O2) and tested monthly for mycoplasma as described in ref. 66.

Plasmid information and cloning

The following shRNA sequences were purchased from Sigma: mouse shNdufa5-1 (TRCN0000041819) and shNdufa5-2 (TRCN0000041821), human shSLC2A5-1 (TRCN0000043005) and shSLC2A5-2 (TRCN0000043006), human shKHK-1 (TRCN0000037725) and shKHK-2 (TRCN0000037727), human shALDOB-1 (TRCN0000052508) and shALDOB-2 (TRCN0000052512), and mouse shKhk-1 (TRCN0000025511) and shKhk-2 (TRCN0000025512). shGFP control was purchased from Addgene (30323). For overexpression experiments, lentiviral plasmid Flag-LBNOX was synthesized by Twist Bioscience. The Twist Bioscience empty vector was used as the control in overexpressing experiments. Sequences can be found in Supplementary Table 10.

Lentiviral packaging and infection conditions

Lentiviral vectors were packaged as follows: 10 µg of the corresponding lentiviral vector was mixed with 7.5 µg psPAX2 (Addgene, 12260) and 7.5 µg pMD2.G (Addgene, 12259) in 1.5 ml Opti-MEM media (Gibco, 31985070) and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. In parallel, 50 µl Lipofectamine (Invitrogen, 11668019) was diluted in 1.5 ml of Opti-MEM media and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The Lipofectamine mixture was added dropwise to the plasmid mixture and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. The Lipofectamine/DNA complexes were added dropwise to HEK293FT cells. The media was replaced 6–8 h later, and viral supernatants were collected after 72 h, which were filtered through a 0.45 µm filter (Fisherbrand, 09-720-005). Target cells were infected by incubating viral supernatant in antibiotic-free media supplemented with 8 µg ml−1 polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, H9268) for 16 h. Infected cells were selected with 1 µg ml−1 puromycin (ThermoFisher, A1113802) or 3 µg ml−1 blasticidin (InvivoGen, ant-bl-05).

Bioinformatic analysis of transcriptomic data from publicly available ovarian cancer patient data

Information on datasets can be found in the ‘Data availability’ statement.

Analysis of single cell RNA-seq

Downloaded cell type annotated data from the original study were a subject for standard Seurat v2 preprocessing. Epithelial cell clustering was performed using the first 30 principal components with default resolution. Calculation of senescence score relied on SenMayo gene set and DNA replication (GO:0044786) (DNA replication, Supplementary Table 1). Each sample score was calculated as an unweighted linear combination of log2-scaled expression values with positive coefficient from SenMayo and negative coefficient from DNA replication gene set. Calculation of senescence scores was done on per-cell level, and significance of score differences between posttreatment and pretreatment cells within each cluster were compared using Wilcoxon test. Uniform manifold approximation and projection dimensionality reduction was used for data visualization.

Correlation with patient disease stage

Analysis of tumor stage was performed with data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) by splitting patients into quartiles of the average z score of the genes in the indicated gene sets (Supplementary Table 1). For correlative analysis, the average z score of the genes in the indicated gene sets were plotted, and linear regression was performed.

Senescence induction and conditioned media generation

Cells were treated with senescence-inducing drugs for 48 h (see Supplementary Table 11 for doses), after which the drugs were removed, and cells were cultured in fresh media. Twenty-four hours after removal of the drugs, the media was changed and allowed to condition for 48 h. For conditioned media intended for use in vivo, no penicillin/streptomycin was added. Conditioned media was collected and filtered (Fisher Scientific, FB12566506).

Crystal violet and proliferation assays

An equal number of cells were seeded in multiwell plates and cultured for 7–10 days. Cells were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.05% crystal violet, and destained using 10% acetic acid. Absorbance (590 nm) was measured using a spectrophotometer (BioTek Epoch Microplate reader). Proliferation assays were performed using an IncuCyte S3 imaging system (Sartorius); an equal number of cells per condition were seeded in triplicates in a 96-well plate. Cells were imaged for 4–5 days with live cell imaging every 6 h. Cell quantification was performed using the IncuCyte software (Version 2023A).

SA-β-Gal assay

Cells were fixed in 2% formaldehyde/0.2% glutaraldehyde in PBS and stained (40 mM Na2HPO4, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM K3Fe(CN)6, 5 mM K4Fe(CN)6, and 1 mg ml−1 X-gal) overnight at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator. Images were acquired at room temperature using a Nikon Eclipse Ts2 equipped with a Nikon DS-Fi3 camera with a ×20/0.40 objective (Nikon LWD); >300 cells were counted per experiment.

RNA isolation, sequencing, and analysis

RNA was extracted from cells with Trizol (Ambion, 15596018) and DNase treated, cleaned, and concentrated using Zymo columns (Zymo Research, R1013). Complementary DNA libraries, next-generation sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis were performed by Novogene. Raw and processed RNA-seq data are available on Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, GSE305033 and GSE305002).

RT-qPCR

RNA was retrotranscribed with High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, 4368814), and 20 ng of cDNA was amplified using the CFX Connect Real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad) and the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, A25742). Quantification relative to multiple reference genes was determined to ensure reproducibility using the delta-delta CT method. Primers can be found in Supplementary Table 10.

Western blotting

Cell lysates were collected in 1× sample buffer and boiled for 10 min. An equal amount of total protein (Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, 5000006)) was resolved using SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Fisher Scientific). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature, incubated overnight at 4 °C in primary antibodies, washed 4 times in TBS-T for 5 min at room temperature, incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, and washed 4 times in TBS-T for 5 min at room temperature. Proteins were visualized on film after incubation with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (ThermoFisher).

Cytokine array

The human cytokine antibody array C1000 (RayBio, AAH-CYT-1000-2) was used. Individual spot signal densities were obtained using ImageJ software (Version 2.0.2) and normalized to cell number.

Murine ovarian cancer in vivo models

For all experiments, 6- to 8-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (2498079-86). Cohort numbers can be found in the figure legends. All mice were maintained in a HEPA-filtered ventilated rack. Mice were housed up to 5 mice per cage and in a 12 h light/dark cycle. For dietary studies with glucose and fructose, mice were provided 30% w/v sugar in sterile water 10 days before cell implantation and throughout the experiment. For co-injection experiments, one million KPCA.B ovarian cancer cells were injected intraperitoneally alone or together with one million cisplatin therapy-induced senescent KPCA.B cells that stably express GFP by lentiviral transduction. For conditioned media treatment experiments, mice were treated three times per week with intraperitoneal injection of 200 µl of conditioned medias. Mice were imaged by IVIS and analyzed using the Aura Spectral Imaging software (Version 5.0.1) at 5–8 days after injection and weighed weekly. To accurately analyze tumor dissemination, a synchronized end point before predefined humane end points was required. Animals were euthanized at the end of the fourth week, and tumor burden was assessed by counting the number of intraperitoneal nodules.

Immunohistochemistry

After deparaffinization and rehydration, tissues were steamed for 40 min in 1× citrate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, C9999) and then immersed in 3% MeOH for 20 min. Tissues were blocked before overnight incubation in primary antibodies. Mouse and Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB (ABC) Detection IHC kit (Abcam, 64264) was used for visualization of staining. Tissues were incubated in Mayer’s Hematoxylin Solution (Sigma-Aldrich, MHS16). Tissues were dehydrated and mounted in Cytoseal (Sigma-Aldrich, 23-244257). QuPath (Version 0.7.0) was used for analysis.

Non-adherent culture and imaging

Cells were seeded 1,000 (96-well round-bottom ULA, Corning 7007), 10,000 (96-well flat-bottom ULA, Corning 3474), or 300,000 (6-well flat-bottom ULA, Corning 3471) cells per well in a 1:1 ratio of fresh to conditioned media. To assess viability, cells were incubated with 1 mM ethidium homodimer (Sigma Aldrich, 46043) for 30 min. Time-lapse imaging to assess spheroid formation in round-bottom ULA plates was performed using IncuCyte S3 imaging system (Sartorius). Time-lapse imaging of spheroids to assess detachment was performed using a Leica Thunder Imager with an Okolab incubation chamber. Spheroids formed in round-bottom ULA culture with conditioned media were transferred to a flat-bottom ULA plate. Z-stacked images were acquired every 15 min for 12 h.

Control trypsin assay

Cells were cultured in conditioned media for 72 h with inhibitors or supplements (see Supplementary Table 11 for doses). Cells were incubated with trypsin (Corning, MT25053CI) for 90 s, and both adherent and detached cells were counted using a hemocytometer.

CRISPR dropout screen

Ovcar8 cells were infected with pooled CRISPR KO library (Addgene, 110066;) at multiplicity of infection of <0.3 to achieve ∼350-fold library coverage after selection with 1 µg ml−1 puromycin. Cells were cultured in conditioned media for 72 h before the controlled trypsin assay was performed. The detached cell and adherent cell portions were collected separately, and DNA was isolated (Zymo Research, D4069). DNA from each condition were pooled and used for gRNA amplification, ensuring >250-fold coverage. The gRNA region was amplified using an Ex Taq DNA polymerase (Takara, RR001A). PCR amplicons were purified (Promega, A9282) and pooled. Amplicons were sequenced using Next Seq v2.5 High Output 75 cycle on an Illumina NextSeq sequencer. Data were analyzed using the MAGeCK bioinformatics pipeline to identify differentially enriched genes67. See Supplementary Table 10 for all primer sequences.

Bioenergetic analysis of oxygen consumption rate and extracellular acidification rate

The Agilent Seahorse XFp Metabolic Analyzer (Agilent, model S7802A) was used. Cells were evenly seeded and cultured overnight in a Seahorse XFp plate (Agilent, 103022-100). The XFp sensor cartridge was hydrated (Agilent, 103022-100) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator (non-CO2) overnight. On the day of the experiment, cell culture media was replaced with prewarmed media (Agilent,103576-100) supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Agilent 103578-100), 2 mM glutamine (Agilent 103579-100), and 10 mM glucose (Agilent, 103577-100). Cells were then placed into a non-CO2 humidified incubator at 37 °C for 60 min. Three basal rate measurements (3 min measurement time each) were taken.

Immunofluorescence imaging

Cells were seeded on coverslips or in Mattek dishes coated with poly-L-ornithine (Sigma, P4957). For filipin III staining, cells were stained with CellMask deep red (ThermoFisher Scientific, C10046) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Cells were washed four times with PBS and stained with 0.2 mg ml−1 filipin (Cayman Chemicals, 70440). For BrdU incorporation, cells were incubated with 1 μmol l−1 BrdU for 1 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and postfixed with 1% paraformaldehyde + 0.01% Tween-20. Coverslips were DNaseI treated for 10 min, and the DNaseI reaction was stopped using 20 mmol l−1 EDTA. For immunofluorescence, cells were fixed and permeabilized as above. Permeabilized cells were blocked for 5 min with 3% BSA/PBS followed by incubation of corresponding primary antibody (‘Reporting summary’) in 3% BSA/PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were washed three times with 1% Triton X-100 in PBS and incubated with secondary antibody in 3% BSA/PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were counterstained with 0.15 µg ml−1 DAPI and mounted (9 ml glycerol (BP229-1; Fisher Scientific), 1 ml of 1× PBS, and 10 mg p-phenylenediamine (PX0730; EMD Chemicals); pH 8.0–9.0). Images were obtained at room temperature using a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2 microscope equipped with a camera (ORCA-Fusion C14440) with a ×20/0.75 N.A. or ×40/0.95 N.A. dry objective (Nikon DIC N2 Plan Apo). Mean filipin intensity was quantified within segmented regions. The background was defined as the median intensity outside segmented cells and subtracted from the mean filipin signal.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Cells were fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde and quenched with 2.5 M glycine. After washes, cells were lysed in chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) lysis buffer (50 mM Hepes–KOH, pH 7.5, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 1% Triton X-100, and 0.1% deoxycholate (DOC) with 0.1 mM PMSF and EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (P8340; Sigma-Aldrich)). Samples were incubated on ice for 10 min and then centrifuged at 1,800g for 3 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.5 mM EGTA with 0.1 mM PMSF and EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail) and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Samples were centrifuged at 1,800g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 0.1% DOC, and 0.5% N-lauroylsarcosine with 0.1 mM PMSF and EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail). Cells were sonicated on ice (10 s on, 50 s off); 30 µl 10% Triton X-100 was added to each tube, and samples were centrifuged at 21,130×g for 15 min at 4 °C. Samples were precleared for 1 h at 4 °C on a rotator using protein G Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight on a rotator at 4 °C with antibodies conjugated to protein G Dynabeads. The following washes were performed twice for 15 min each by rotating at 4 °C: ChIP lysis buffer, ChIP lysis buffer plus 0.65 M NaCl, wash buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 0.5% NP-30, 0.5% DOC, and mM EDTA, pH 8.0), and 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) and 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0). DNA was eluted by incubating the beads with 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 10 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), and 1% SDS for 30 min at 65 °C. Samples were incubated overnight at 65 °C and then digested (1 mg ml−1 proteinase K at 37 °C for 5 h). DNA was purified (A9282; Promega) and analyzed by qPCR using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, A25742). Primer sets are in Supplementary Table 10.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Transcriptomic profiles of ovarian cancer cells incubated with autologous ascitic fluids from patients before and after chemotherapy was downloaded from GEO (GSE241909)29. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on the differential expression gene list using Metascape (Version V3.x) with the Wikipathways gene set database.

Metabolite measurement

Polar metabolites were measured by liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry adapted from previously published approaches69. Conditioned media samples were snap frozen at −80 °C. Cell samples were quenched in 1 ml prechilled −80 °C 80:20 methanol/water (v/v) and spiked with 50 µl 1 µM isotope-labeled TCA cycle mix (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories MSK-TCA-A) prediluted in 80:20 methanol/water and 50 µl 0.02 ng µl−1 propionyl-L-carnitine-(N-methyl-d3) (Sigma Aldrich 52941). After vortexing for 1 min, samples were returned to −80 °C for 30 min and centrifuged at 18,000g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen gas. Samples were reconstituted in 100 µl of 95:5 water:methanol (v/v), and 2 µl of the sample was injected from a 4 °C autosampler into a ZIC-pHILIC 150 × 2.1 mm 5 µm particle size column (EMD Millipore) with a ZIC-pHILIC 20 × 2.1 guard column in a Vanquish Duo UHPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 25 °C. Chromatography conditions were as follows: buffer A was acetonitrile; buffer B was 20 mM ammonium carbonate, 0.1% (v/v) ammonium hydroxide in water without pH adjustment, with a gradient of 0.5 min at 20% A, then a linear gradient from 20% to 80% A for 20–20.5 min, from 80% to 20% B for 20.5–28 min, and hold at 20% B at a 0.150 ml min−1 flow rate. Column elute was introduced to a Q Exactive Plus with a HESI II probe operating in polarity switching mode with full scans from 70 to 1,000 m/z with an insource fragmentation energy of 1. Instruments were controlled via XCalibur 4.1, and data were analyzed on Tracefinder 5.1 using a 5 ppm window from the predominant ion positive or negative. The area under the curves for each analyte was normalized to the matched internal standard or the nearest surrogate internal standard. For isotope tracing, isotopologue enrichment was calculated using FluxFix (Version v0.1)70.

Cholesterol was measured by liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry after liquid–liquid extraction. Cholesterols were extracted from cells by methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) extraction. Cells were pelleted and resuspended in 80% methanol/water. Samples were sonicated before transferring to a glass tube with the ratio 10:3:2.5 of MTBE/MeOH/0.9% NaCl. Samples were shaken for 30 min and centrifuged for 10 min at 1,000g at 4 °C. The organic layer was transferred to a clean tube and dried overnight under nitrogen. Samples were resuspended in 1:3 MTBE/water. 13C5-cholesterol (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) was used as an internal standard. A Vanquish UPLC system coupled to a Exploris 480 mass spectrometer with a H-ESI source was used for all cholesterol quantifications. Cholesterols were chromatographically separated using an Accucore C30 (Thermo Fisher Scientific 27826-152130) 150 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm partial size column. Solvent A was 10 mM ammonium formate in 6:4 acetonitrile/water and 0.1% formic acid. Solvent B was 10 mM ammonium formate in 88:10:2 isopropanol/acetonitrile/water. The flow rate was 0.26 ml min−1. The method began at 30% B for 3 min, then increased to 43% B over 2 min and further increased to 55% B in 0.1 min. B was further increased to 65% over 10 min then further increased to 85% over 6 min. B was then increased to 100% over 2 min and maintained at 100% for 5 min. B was then decreased to 30% over 0.1 min and maintained at 30% B for 3 min. The mass spectrometer operating conditions were 3,500 V spray voltage, 400 °C ion transfer tube temperature, 200 °C vaporizer temperature, and positive ion polarity. Xcalibur was used for data acquisition and TraceFinder 5.1 was used for quantification.

Patient serum samples and clinical data

Pretreatment blood was collected from patients with newly diagnosed epithelial ovarian cancer (patient characteristics are available in Supplementary Table 12). Blood was collected in red-top tubes and centrifuged at 500g for 10 min, and serum was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. Metabolite levels were assessed by Metabolon using the DiscoveryHD4 platform using three independent platforms: ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS) in the negative ion mode, UPLC–MS/MS in the positive ion mode, and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry after sialylation. Compounds were identified based on chromatographic properties and mass spectra by comparing to a metabolomic library of purified standards. Clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical records by staff trained in medical record abstraction/coding (L.B.) and verified by comparison to the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center Hillman Cancer Center (UPMC/HCC) cancer registry database, which is accredited by the American College of Surgeons Committee on Cancer.

Statistics and reproducibility

GraphPad Prism (version 10.0) was used to perform statistical analysis. Point estimates with standard deviations or standard errors were reported, as indicated, and the appropriate statistical test was performed using all observed experimental data. All statistical tests performed were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. When necessary, outliers were identified and excluded by the Robust regression and Outlier removal (ROUT) method (Q = 0.1%). The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. In vitro experiments were not randomized. For animal studies, the number of animals assigned per condition was selected to account for the variability of the examined phenotypes based on pilot experiment and experience with the animal models. Animals were randomized into groups by random number generator before being assigned to a group for cancer cell implantation, treatment or diet. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation during experiments owing to technical limitations. Animals were excluded from analysis if they reached predetermined ethical end points defined in Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocols, if they did not develop tumors (measured by either IVIS imaging 7 days after implantation or omental weight compared to tumor-free omentum) or if they were determined to be statistical outliers using the ROUT method in GraphPad PRISM (version 10.0).
