GhTTLL12 Coordinates With Transcriptional Regulators GhMML3 and GhMYB86 to Orchestrate Cotton Fibre Development Through Modulating Microtubule Dynamics
Authors: Xiaowei Ma, Xubinmiao Huang, Jinfang Li, Ting Zhao, Zhenfeng Ling, Shengcai Huang, Zesheng Rui, Yue Shi, Yulin Xing, Lei Sun, Mengting Wang, Haoxi Song, Rui Chen, Zequan Chen, Lei Shao, Nangan Sun, Junduo Wang, Juyun Zheng, Lei Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrotubules (MTs) are crucial for cell division, growth, development and morphogenesis in plants. Cotton fibres are single‐celled trichomes that originate from the epidermal cells of the ovule, making them an excellent model for studying plant cell differentiation and rapid elongation. However, the roles of MTs in cotton fibre development remain incompletely understood. In this study, we identified GhTTLL12, a tubulin‐tyrosine ligase‐like protein 12, as a positive regulator of fibre initiation and elongation via Gh‐Gb introgression analysis. GhTTLL12 was preferentially expressed during the rapid elongation stage of cotton fibres. Overexpression of GhTTLL12 enhanced plant height, root length, fibre cell protrusion number and fibre length in cotton. Conversely, CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockout of GhTTLL12 led to opposite phenotypes, thereby significantly reducing fibre quality. MT co‐sedimentation and immunofluorescence assays demonstrated that GhTTLL12 binds directly to MTs and promotes their assembly while facilitating the formation of transverse MT arrays in elongating fibres. Further investigation of the molecular mechanisms revealed that after GhTTLL12 is recruited into the nucleus by GhTUB8, it promotes mitosis in ovule epidermal cells upon activation by GhMML3, increasing the number of fibre cell protrusions. GhMYB86, a negative regulator of cotton fibre elongation, represses GhTTLL12 transcription in the nucleus, leading to attenuated mitotic activity. Cytoplasmic GhTTLL12 modulates fibre cell elongation by regulating MT assembly and ordered arrangement. Collectively, our findings define a GhMML3/GhMYB86‐GhTTLL12‐GhTUB8 regulatory module that links stage‐specific transcriptional regulation to MT remodelling during cotton fibre development, providing new insights into the improvement of fibre quality and yield.

Introduction

Cotton (Gossypium spp.) is a key cash crop worldwide and the primary source of natural textile fibre. Cotton fibres are single‐celled trichomes originating from the epidermal cells of the ovule (Huang et al. 2021; Wen et al. 2023). The development process of cotton fibres includes four distinct but overlapping stages: initiation (−3 to 3 days post anthesis, DPA), elongation (3–25 DPA), secondary cell wall (SCW) thickening (20–40 DPA) and maturation (40–50 DPA) (Fang et al. 2014; Qiao et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2025). These stages are regulated by complex gene networks that involve transcriptional regulation, as well as hormone and sugar signalling, alongside cytoskeletal and membrane dynamics (Huang et al. 2021; Jan et al. 2022; Tian and Zhang 2021; Walford et al. 2011, 2012; Wang et al. 2014; Wen et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2011). Understanding the mechanisms behind these networks is key to enhancing fibre yield and quality.

The initial stage determines the number of cotton fibres, which is a crucial stage in the formation of cotton fibre yield. However, only about 25%–30% of the ovule epidermal cells differentiate into spinnable fibres (Kim and Triplett 2001). An effective way to improve cotton fibre production is to increase the quantity of fibres formed on the ovules. The length and strength of cotton fibres are primarily determined during the elongation and SCW thickening stages, which have a significant impact on their economic value in the textile industry (Sun, Tian, et al. 2024). Therefore, it is important to investigate the potential molecular mechanisms that control the development of cotton fibres. Two transcription factor (TF) families, MYB and HD‐ZIP, are essential for cotton fibre initiation. These include the R2R3‐type MYB TFs, such as GhMYB25 (Machado et al. 2009), GhMYB25‐like (Chen et al. 2025; Walford et al. 2011; Zhao et al. 2024), GhMYB109 (Pu et al. 2008) and HD‐ZIP family TFs, including GhHD‐1 (Walford et al. 2012) and GhHOX3 (Shan et al. 2014). Chen et al. (2025) demonstrated that the duplicated GhMYB25‐like_At (GhMML3_A12) and GhMYB25‐like_Dt (GhMML3_D12) influence fibre initiation in a dose‐dependent way. GhFP1, a basic helix–loop–helix protein (bHLH) TF, acts as a positive regulator for cotton fibre elongation by activating brassinosteroid (BR) biosynthesis and signalling (Liu et al. 2020). GhWRKY16 promotes fibre elongation by directly regulating the expression of downstream genes involved in fibre elongation, such as GhHOX3 and GhMYB109, as well as the expression of the cellulose synthase gene GhCesA6D_D11, which is involved in primary cell wall biosynthesis. It also controls fibre initiation by directly regulating the expression of GhMYB25 (Wang et al. 2021). Plant hormones, such as auxin, also play a significant role in cotton fibre initiation (Zhang et al. 2011). In addition, silencing GhVIN1 results in a fiberless phenotype by using specific promoters for the early development of cotton fibres and seeds (Wang et al. 2014). Furthermore, the role of the cytoskeleton in cotton fibre development has also been demonstrated (Wen et al. 2023). Li et al. (2002) cloned the GhTUB1 gene, which encodes a β‐tubulin subunit that is highly expressed in upland cotton fibres. GhTUB7 influences fibre elongation by modifying MT arrangements (Xu et al. 2024). While these studies identified some relevant genes related to cotton fibre development, the interaction mechanisms among them and other regulatory pathways are still largely unclear.

The TTL protein family consists of 1 TTL protein and 13 TTL‐like (TTLL) proteins in mammals. Among these, the TTL catalyses the re‐addition of a tyrosine residue to the C‐terminus of detyrosinated α‐tubulin (Brants et al. 2012; Sanyal et al. 2023). This modification is reversible, with tubulin tyrosine carboxypeptidase (TTC, also abbreviated to TCP in animal research) removing the terminal tyrosine residue to generate detyrosinated tubulin (Brants et al. 2012). The tyrosination‐detyrosination cycle is crucial for regulating MT dynamics and interactions with microtubule‐associated proteins (MAPs). Generally, tyrosinated MTs are more dynamic and are involved in processes such as cell division and intracellular transport, whereas detyrosinated MTs are more stable and play roles in maintaining cell shape and structure (Nieuwenhuis and Brummelkamp 2019). TTL activity has been linked to the regulation of cell division in animal cells, where it influences spindle assembly and chromosome segregation (Moutin et al. 2021). Additionally, TTL has been implicated in neuronal development and function, with tyrosinated MTs playing a role in axonal transport and synaptic plasticity (Peris et al. 2022). The remaining 13 members, known as TTL‐like (TTLL) proteins, primarily function as glutamylases or glycylases (Janke and Magiera 2020). Of these, TTLL12 was localized to the ciliary base in polarized epithelial cells, where it acts as a novel MT regulator that binds tubulin heterodimers directly to regulate MT dynamics, stability and specific post‐translational modifications (Ceglowski et al. 2024). However, the TTLL12 family is conserved across all eukaryotes and represents the sole TTL family member found in plants (Zhang et al. 2021). To date, only one functional homologue, OsTTLL12, has been characterized in rice. OsTTLL12 regulates the process of α‐tubulin de‐tyrosination/tyrosination, affecting the stability and arrangement of MTs and ultimately influencing rice growth and development (Zhang et al. 2021). Therefore, the role of TTLL12 in plant cells still needs further investigation.

Through Gh‐Gb introgression analysis, we identified a candidate gene, GbTTL (GbTTLL12), which is related to fibre length (FL) and lint percentage (LP) in cotton (Fang et al. 2021). Since the TTLL12 sequence is highly conserved in both G. barbadense and G. hirsutum , and the genetic transformation system of G. hirsutum is relatively mature, facilitating its functional study, we therefore designate the Gh ortholog of GbTTL (GbTTLL12) as GhTTLL12. In this study, we first generated GhTTLL12 transgenic Arabidopsis as well as cotton materials, and found that it could regulate trichome formation in Arabidopsis and cotton fibre initiation and elongation. To dissect the mechanism by which GhTTLL12 regulates fibre development, we investigated its molecular regulatory network. The results showed that the core TF GhMML3, which is related to the initiation of cotton fibre, binds directly to the promoter of GhTTLL12, thereby positively regulating the initiation of cotton fibres. In addition, we identified GhMYB86 as a direct upstream regulator of GhTTLL12, which promotes fibre elongation by modulating MT organization. Furthermore, we demonstrated that GhTTLL12 interacts with the tubulin subunit, GhTUB8, suggesting a direct mechanism for cytoskeletal regulation during fibre development. Overall, our study not only thoroughly analysed the role of GhTTLL12 in the initiation and elongation of cotton fibres but also provided a reference for studying the function of TTLL12 in plants.

Results

Molecular Characteristics of GhTTLL12 in Cotton

In our previous study (Fang et al. 2021), we used the Gh TM‐1 genome as a reference (Hu et al. 2019) and conducted a Gh‐Gb introgression analysis using 229 Gb accessions and 268 Gh accessions. The results revealed that Gh‐i5 exhibited the highest heritability of phenotypic variation and was significantly associated with FL (fibre length) and LP (lint percentage) traits. Within this introgression region, based on an analysis of expression and population variation, we have preliminarily selected GB_A06G0868 and GB_A06G0874, which are expressed during the fibre development stage, as candidate genes (Figures S1 – S3; Table S1). GH_A06G0836 (homologue of GB_A06G0868 in G. hirsutum ) was expressed at all stages of fibre development, whereas GH_A06G0842 (homologue of GB_A06G0874 in G. hirsutum ) was not expressed (Figure S4). GB_A06G0868 was significantly correlated with FL and LP (Figure S5). The sequence of GH_A06G0836 is completely identical to GB_A06G0868, and it is a gene that has been introgressed from G. hirsutum into G. barbadense . Therefore, our subsequent research will focus on GH_A06G0836 (GhTTLL12).

TTLL12 is highly conserved between G. barbadense and G. hirsutum (Figure S6A,B) and encodes a protein containing a TTL domain spanning amino acids 541–802 (Figure S6C). To explore the evolutionary relationships of TTLL12 in cotton, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using 23 TTLL12 protein sequences from five diploid species ( G. arboreum , G. herbaceum , G. thurberi , G. raimondii and G. turneri ) and five allotetraploid cotton species ( G. hirsutum , G. barbadense , G. tomentosum , G. mustelinum and G. darwini ). As shown in Figure S7, the phylogenetic tree was divided into two distinct subfamilies, depending on whether the TTLL12 gene was located in the At‐ or Dt‐subgenome. Notably, the TTLL12_At sequence exhibited high conservation in G. barbadense and G. hirsutum accessions but not in the remaining three allotetraploid cotton species (AD3, AD4 and AD5).

Transcript analysis showed that GhTTLL12 was expressed in roots, stems, leaves, ovules and fibres, with particularly high expression in 10‐DPA fibres (Figure 1A). This indicates that GhTTLL12 may play a significant role in the fibre cell rapid elongation stage. To visualize the spatial expression of GhTTLL12, we fused the GhTTLL12 promoter to GUS by replacing the CaMV 35S promoter in the pBI121 vector and introduced this construct into Arabidopsis and cotton. In Arabidopsis, GUS activity was observed mainly in roots and leaves, with a weaker presence in stems. Interestingly, intensive GUS activity was observed in the root tip meristematic zone (Figure 1B). In cotton, intensive GUS signals were observed in the root tip meristematic zone and the 10‐DPA fibres, and weaker signals in leaves and stems (Figure 1C). These findings indicate that the GhTTLL12 promoter is highly active in the development of cotton fibre cells. In addition, subcellular localization assays showed that GhTTLL12 was localized to the cytoplasm (Figure 1D).

GhTTLL12 Positively Regulates Fibre Elongation in Cotton

The development of Arabidopsis trichomes is known to share similar mechanisms with cotton fibres. To explore the function of GhTTLL12, three GhTTLL12‐OE (overexpression) transgenic Arabidopsis lines were generated using the dipping method. RT‐PCR (Figure S8A) and qRT‐PCR (Figure S8B) analyses confirmed the reliability of the transgenic Arabidopsis plants overexpressing GhTTLL12. To evaluate its role in the development of Arabidopsis trichomes, we counted the number of trichomes on the true leaves and stems, and measured the length of the root hairs in both the wild‐type (WT) plants and the GhTTLL12‐OE lines. The results showed that the trichome numbers on leaves (Figure S8C,D) and stems (Figure S8E,F) of all GhTTLL12‐OE lines were significantly higher than those of the WT plant. There was no significant difference in root hair density in GhTTLL12‐OE lines compared with WT plants, but the length of the root hairs dramatically increased (Figure S8G,H). Therefore, GhTTLL12 positively regulates trichome development in Arabidopsis.

To investigate the function of GhTTLL12 in cotton, we selected three overexpression lines (OE#19, OE#24 and OE#38) and two double‐knockout lines (KO#1 and KO#2) through qRT‐PCR (Figure 2A), Western blot (WB) (Figure 2B), Hi‐Tom and Sanger sequencing (Figure 2C, Figure S9) analysis. The GhTTLL12‐OE plants were taller with higher stem trichome density than the WT plants, while the GhTTLL12‐KO plants exhibited the opposite traits (Figure 2D,E; Figure S10A–C). Furthermore, we would like to explore whether the differences between the GhTTLL12‐transgenic lines and the WT are related to cell elongation. The WT and GhTTLL12‐transgenic seeds were germinated in darkness for 36 h, and the primary root length was measured. The roots of GhTTLL12‐OE seedlings were significantly longer, while the GhTTLL12‐KO roots were shorter than those of the WT (Figure S10D,E). We then stained the meristem region at the root tips with propidium iodide (PI) to observe the cell morphology (Figure 2F). The results showed that the GhTTLL12‐OE lines had larger and longer cells, while the GhTTLL12‐KO lines had smaller cells (Figure 2F–H). These findings suggest that GhTTLL12 positively regulates cell elongation in cotton.

To further analyse the role of GhTTLL12 in cotton fibre elongation, we used an in vitro ovule culture system to measure fibre length at 5, 10 and 15 days. Consistently, fibres from the GhTTLL12‐OE lines were longer than those from the WT, while the GhTTLL12‐KO lines had shorter fibres at all time points (Figure 2I,J). Compared to the WT, the mature fibre length increased by 5.47%, 5.03%, and 4.93% for GhTTLL12‐OE#19, ‐OE#24 and ‐OE#38, respectively, but decreased by 15.05% and 14.57% in GhTTLL12‐KO#1 and ‐KO#2 (Figure 2K,L; Table S2). It is worth noting that the fibre length in single GhTTLL12_At or GhTTLL12_Dt knockout lines was also significantly reduced compared to the WT, but the change was less pronounced than in the GhTTLL12 double‐knockout lines (Figure S11A–C), suggesting that GhTTLL12_At and GhTTLL12_Dt may exhibit a certain degree of functional redundancy. Furthermore, the fibre quality of WT and GhTTLL12‐transgenic plants was detected in two locations over 2 years (Sanya in 2024 and Hangzhou in 2025) (Table S2). Fibre strength of the GhTTLL12‐OE lines was significantly higher than that of the WT, whereas the GhTTLL12‐KO lines showed a significant decrease (Figure 2M). Overall, there was little difference in fibre elongation rate, uniformity, or micronaire (Figure 2N) between the WT and GhTTLL12‐transgenic plants (Table S2). These results demonstrate that GhTTLL12 plays a positive regulatory role in cotton fibre development.

GhTTLL12 Has the Potential to Increase Fibre Yield Components in Cotton

To explore the role of GhTTLL12 in improving cotton fibre yield, we measured the yield traits of the GhTTLL12‐transgenic lines in two locations over 2 years (Table S2). The single boll weight of the GhTTLL12‐OE lines was slightly higher than that of the control, whereas the GhTTLL12‐KO lines showed a dramatic decrease (Figure 3A,D). Overexpression of GhTTLL12 resulted in a slight increase in seed size, whereas knockout led to a significant decrease (Figure 3B,C,E). Interestingly, we found that the LP of GhTTLL12‐OE lines was significantly higher than that of the WT. This indicates that the overexpression of GhTTLL12 increases yield components in cotton. In contrast, GhTTLL12‐KO lines also showed higher LP than the WT (Figure 3F). The reason was that the seeds became significantly smaller, resulting in an increase in LP. In addition, we also measured the lint index of both the GhTTLL12‐transgenic lines and the WT. The lint index rose in GhTTLL12‐OE lines but fell significantly in GhTTLL12‐KO lines (Figure 3G), suggesting that GhTTLL12 has a positive role in lint production.

Notably, the GhTTLL12‐KO lines had significantly less fuzz than the WT after ginning (Figure 3H). Paraffin sections of 5‐DPA ovules indicated greater fuzz protrusion in GhTTLL12‐OE#19, while GhTTLL12‐KO#1 showed a marked reduction in protrusion (Figure 3I). Subsequently, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of ovules at −0.5‐, 0‐ and 1‐DPA showed that GhTTLL12‐OE#19 line already exhibited a few fibre protrusions at −0.5‐DPA, while no fibre protrusion phenotype was observed in the WT and KO#1 line (Figure 3J,K). At 0‐ and 1‐DPA, GhTTLL12‐OE#19 line produced more protrusions, while GhTTLL12‐KO#1 had fewer than WT (Figure 3J,L,M). These results show that GhTTLL12 not only regulates fibre elongation but also participates in the protrusion of both lint and fuzz in cotton.

GhTTLL12 Modulates MT Stability and Orientation During Cotton Fibre Elongation

MTs in cells have a variety of forms and structures, such as the radial distribution in interphase cells, the columnar distribution in polar epithelial cells, the umbrella‐shaped distribution in neuronal growth cones and the spindle distribution during mitosis. These morphological distributions are crucial for the multiple functions of MTs (Ceglowski et al. 2024). Among those, the morphological changes of the spindle during the mitotic phase are relatively easy to observe. The apical meristem is the region of the plant root tip where cell division occurs most vigorously. Therefore, we took the apical meristem from roots of WT and GhTTLL12‐transgenic plants for immunofluorescence staining to observe the MT dynamics of GhTTLL12 during mitosis (Figure 4A). In comparison to WT, interphase cortical MTs in GhTTLL12‐OE roots were more regularly aligned, while GhTTLL12‐KO cells displayed irregular arrays. During metaphase and anaphase, spindles in GhTTLL12‐OE cells were denser than in WT, but GhTTLL12‐KO spindles were sparse and disorganized. At telophase, GhTTLL12‐OE cells had distinct phragmoplast organization, while GhTTLL12‐KO cells showed shorter and disordered MT structures.

Does TTLL12, as a TTL‐like protein, affect the activity of TTL and the level of tyrosination of MTs? We used specific antibodies to detect it in both WT and GhTTLL12‐transgenic plants. Because the expression level of GhTTLL12 is highest in the fibres at 10‐DPA, we isolated the fibres from cotton bolls and extracted total proteins. The protein concentration of all samples was determined using the BCA method, and then the proteins were standardized and subjected to WB detection (Figure S12). Interestingly, TTL and tyrosinated tubulin levels were largely unchanged in GhTTLL12‐OE lines but decreased in GhTTLL12‐KO lines compared to WT. Total tubulin abundance increased in GhTTLL12‐OE lines while it decreased in GhTTLL12‐KO lines, suggesting that GhTTLL12 supports MT assembly or stability.

To evaluate the role of GhTTLL12 on MT stability and dynamics in cotton fibres, we treated the 1‐DPA ovules with Oryzalin (MT destabilizer) and Taxol (MT stabilizer) in the BT culture medium to observe fibre initiation and elongation. As shown in Figure 4B, all ovules were able to grow normally in the BT culture medium at 15 DPA, and fibre initiation and elongation were not inhibited. Following Oryzalin treatment, fibre initiation and elongation were severely inhibited in the WT. In contrast, fibre initiation and elongation were significantly increased in the GhTTLL12‐OE lines. This indicates that GhTTLL12 can alleviate the inhibitory effect of oryzalin on fibre initiation and elongation. Following treatment with Taxol, the fibre initiation and elongation of the GhTTLL12‐KO lines were significantly increased compared to the WT. These results suggest that GhTTLL12 plays a key role in MT stability and dynamics.

The MT organization reflects MT dynamics during growth. We firstly transiently expressed the RFP‐AtMAP65‐1 (MT marker), alongside GhTTLL12, which was driven by the 35S promoter and RFP‐AtMAP65‐1, in tobacco. We then used laser confocal microscopy to observe the MT arrangements (Figure S13). The results showed that the density of MTs significantly increased when 35S: GhTTLL12 was expressed, indicating that GhTTLL12 promotes MT assembly. Then, we used immunofluorescence staining to observe the dynamics of MTs during cotton fibre elongation. In terms of MT fluorescence intensity per unit area, GhTTLL12‐OE lines were higher than the WT plants, whereas the knock‐out plants were remarkably lower (Figure 4C,D). Furthermore, MT fluorescence intensity gradually increased with fibre elongation time. These results indicate that GhTTLL12 promotes MT assembly. As shown in Figure 4C,E, the MT angles mainly fell within the 60°–90° range and exhibited a tendency toward parallel alignment in fibres after 5 and 10 days in vitro culture. Among those, the MT angles of WT and GhTTLL12‐OE lines were primarily found to be between 60° and 90°, whereas the GhTTLL12‐KO plants were predominantly found within the 60°–75° and 105°–120° ranges. Notably, MT angles showed an obvious inclination after 15 days in vitro culture. The WT and GhTTLL12‐OE lines were mainly found between 45° and 75°. In contrast, the GhTTLL12‐KO lines were mostly found in the 60°–75° and 105°–150° ranges. Thus, compared to the WT, the MTs in the fibres of the GhTTLL12‐OE line were almost perpendicular to the direction of fibre elongation and showed a parallel distribution pattern, while the GhTTLL12‐KO lines showed significant deviations.

GhTTLL12 Binds to MTs In Vitro and Interacts With GhTUB8

To assess the relationship of GhTTLL12 and MTs, we purified the GST‐GhTTLL12 protein incubated with preformed Taxol‐stabilized MTs. MTs were only detected in the pellet, while soluble GST‐GhTTLL12 proteins were present only in the supernatant. Furthermore, both MTs and GST‐GhTTLL12 proteins could be detected in the pellet when GST‐GhTTLL12 protein was co‐incubated with MTs, indicating a direct binding between GhTTLL12 and MTs (Figure 5A). Next, we used IP‐MS to investigate which proteins interact with GhTTLL12 (Table S3). The results identified a β‐tubulin subunit, GhTUB8, as a candidate interactor. Y2H assays revealed that yeast cells carrying GhTTLL12‐BD and GhTUB8‐AD exhibited normal growth on an SD‐Trp‐Leu‐His‐Ade medium, while the negative control pairs failed to grow, which confirmed their interaction (Figure 5B). Subsequently, the BiFC assay was used to indicate that GhTTLL12 and GhTUB8 interacted in the nucleus (Figure 5C). This interaction was further verified using a luciferase (LUC) complementation assay, co‐infiltrating GhTTLL12‐nLUC and cLUC‐GhTUB8 into N. benthamiana leaves produced a strong luciferase signal, whereas the negative control showed no signal (Figure 5D). Additionally, a GST pull‐down assay also demonstrated that GST‐GhTTLL12 binds to MBP‐GhTUB8 in vitro (Figure 5E).

GhTUB8 Acts as a Negative Regulator of Fibre Development in Cotton

As shown in Figure S14A, GhTUB8 was expressed at various stages of fibre development, with the greatest level of expression observed at 10‐DPA. Next, we characterized the subcellular localization of GhTUB8 using the tobacco transient transformation system. The results showed that the GFP signal was predominantly found in the cytoplasm, with a small amount present in the nucleus (Figure S14B). To investigate the role of GhTUB8 in the development of cotton fibres, two overexpression lines and two RNAi lines were created (Figure 6A). The analysis of mature fibres shows that overexpressing GhTUB8 reduces fibre length, while RNAi increases it (Figure 6B,C). Furthermore, we employed an in vitro ovule culture system to measure the fibre lengths of the WT and GhTUB8‐transgenic lines at different stages. By the 5th day, significant differences in fibre length had emerged. Specifically, the GhTUB8‐OE lines were significantly shorter than the WT, while the GhTUB8‐RNAi lines were longer than the WT (Figure 6D,E). Immunofluorescence staining of fibres cultured for 10 days indicated that GhTUB8 overexpression leads to disordered MT organization, while RNAi lines maintained ordered MT arrays at angles primarily between 60° and 90° (Figure 6F,H). Additionally, MT fluorescence intensity was slightly lower in GhTUB8‐OE lines but significantly higher in RNAi lines (Figure 6G). SEM of 0‐DPA ovules revealed fewer fibre protrusions in GhTUB8‐OE lines and more in GhTUB8‐RNAi lines compared to the WT (Figure 6I,J). Overall, these results suggest that GhTUB8 negatively impacts fibre initiation and elongation via effects on MT organization.

GhMML3 or GhMYB86 Alternately Regulate the Expression of GhTTLL12 at Different Stages of Fibre Initiation and Elongation in Cotton

The fragment of 1.5 kb promoter of GhTTLL12 was cloned into the pAbAi vector as a bait vector to identify the upstream regulatory factors of GhTTLL12. The results showed that GhMML3A may be a potential regulator of GhTTLL12 (Table S4). Then, we employed RNA‐seq data to analyse the expression levels of GhTTLL12 in WT and GhMML3 knockout lines. The expression level of GhTTLL12 was significantly decreased in the GhMML3 double knockout lines at 1‐ and 3‐DPA (Figure S15), suggesting that GhMML3A and/or GhMML3D may positively regulate the expression of GhTTLL12. In Y1H assays, we observed that yeast strains containing both the bait (proGhTTLL12) and the prey (GhMML3A or GhMML3D) were able to grow normally on SD‐leu medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL AbA. By contrast, the negative control failed to grow (Figure S16A). Furthermore, a luciferase (LUC) reporter assay in N. benthamiana leaves showed that co‐expression of the effector (GhMML3A or GhMML3D) with the reporter (proGhTTLL12) resulted in significantly stronger luc activity compared to the control (Figure S16B–F), indicating that both GhMML3A and/or GhMML3D activate the expression of GhTTLL12. The EMSA results confirmed that GhMML3A and/or GhMML3D could directly bind to the biotin‐labelled probe1, but not to the other three probes (Figure S16G). Collectively, these findings suggest that GhMML3A and/or GhMML3D directly bind to the promoter of GhTTLL12 to activate its expression, thereby regulating the initiation of lint and fuzz fibres.

Unfortunately, we were unable to identify the potential upstream regulatory factors of GhTTLL12 through the Y1H screening method during cotton fibre elongation. However, Xu et al. (2024) demonstrated that GhMYB86 negatively regulates the elongation of cotton fibres by modulating MT arrangement. Does GhMYB86 act as an upstream regulatory factor of GhTTLL12? We therefore performed a series of assays to test this hypothesis. Y1H assays were employed to confirm that GhMYB86 directly binds to the promoter of GhTTLL12 (Figure 7A). Dual luciferase assays showed that co‐expression of the reporter (proGhTTLL12) and the effector (GhMYB86) in N. benthamiana leaves resulted in weaker luminescence compared to the control (Figure 7B–D), suggesting that GhMYB86 inhibited the expression of GhTTLL12. Moreover, EMSA confirmed the direct binding of GhMYB86 to a specific biotin‐labelled probe1 from the GhTTLL12 promoter (Figure 7E). These results indicate that GhMYB86 acts as an upstream regulatory factor for GhTTLL12, negatively regulating its expression.

Discussion

The Importance of MTs in Cotton Fibre Development

Cotton fibre is a single‐cell trichome derived from ovule epidermal cells (Qin et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2025), making it an excellent model for studying cell differentiation and elongation. MTs are hollow cytoskeletal polymers assembled from α/β‐tubulin heterodimers and play central roles in cell division, expansion and morphogenesis (Krtkova et al. 2016; Liang et al. 2016). Multiple tubulin isoforms and MAPs have been implicated in plant development and stress adaptation. For example, loss of TUB4 enhances salt tolerance in Arabidopsis, whereas TUB9 has the opposite effect (Chun et al. 2021). OsTubA1 affects grain size by regulating cell expansion in rice (Liu et al. 2023). In cotton, knockout of GhTUB5 or silencing of GhTUB7 increases fibre length (Ma et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2024), and GhMAP20L5 promotes fibre elongation by interacting with GhTUB13 (Song et al. 2023). GhCLASP2 influences fibre quality by modulating the expression of genes related to tubulin, cellulose synthesis and cell expansion (Zhu et al. 2018). Nevertheless, how tubulin isoforms and MAPs coordinate MT dynamics and organization during cotton fibre growth remains incompletely understood.

Previous studies have demonstrated that post‐translational modifications of tubulin, such as tyrosination and acetylation, could affect the binding of MAPs to MTs, thereby regulating MT arrangement patterns (Cai 2010; Peris et al. 2009). Knockout of GhTTLL12 led to a significant decrease in the tyrosination level of MTs (Figure S12), and the elongation of cotton fibres was severely inhibited. This further demonstrates that post‐translational modifications of tubulin can affect cell elongation. In this study, we found that during the early stage of fibre development, cortical MTs were oriented perpendicular to the direction of fibre cell elongation and exhibited a relatively ordered arrangement. As the rapid elongation phase ceased, MTs gradually reoriented toward a direction parallel to fibre growth, until they became fully aligned with the fibre elongation axis, at which point fibre elongation terminated. Additionally, cotton fibres during the period of rapid growth exhibit a linear growth pattern, encompassing both apical and diffusion growth (Qin and Zhu 2011). However, Yu et al. (2019) revealed that the elongation of cotton fibres exhibits a unique, tip‐biased diffusion growth pattern, which is controlled by the cytoskeleton. In this study, we discovered that MTs were specifically arranged perpendicular to the direction of cell growth during the elongation of cotton fibres, presenting a specific tissue pattern of diffusive growth, and a MT‐deficient area was formed at the tip of the fibre (Figure S17). This spatial organization is consistent with tip‐biased diffuse growth and provides a cytoskeletal framework for understanding how directional elongation is achieved in cotton fibres, with potential implications for improving fibre traits.

GhMML3/GhMYB86‐GhTTLL12‐GhTUB8 Module Regulates Cotton Fibre Development

TTLL12 is a member of the TTLL family, which mediates post‐translational modifications of tubulin and thereby regulates MT function (Ceglowski et al. 2024). In this study, we demonstrate that GhTTLL12 regulates the initiation and elongation of cotton fibres by participating in MT dynamics and organization. Further mechanistic investigations reveal that GhTTLL12 not only directly binds to MTs in vitro but also interacts with the tubulin subunit GhTUB8 in the nucleus. GhMML3 modulates cotton fibre initiation in a dose‐dependent manner by targeting the promoters of genes associated with cotton fibre initiation (Chen et al. 2025). GhMYB86 has been reported to regulate cotton fibre cell elongation by controlling MT organization in fibre cells (Xu et al. 2024). In the present study, we verified that GhMML3 and GhMYB86 specifically target the GhTTLL12 promoter and directly modulate its transcriptional activity. Based on the above results, we propose a working model for GhTTLL12 (Figure 8). During the cotton fibre initiation stage, GhTUB8 recruits GhTTLL12 into the nucleus, and GhMML3 activates GhTTLL12 expression, thereby promoting cell mitosis and driving the proliferation of ovule epidermal cells. Although direct involvement of GhTTLL12 in cell differentiation remains to be demonstrated, the increased density of ovule epidermal cells per unit area gives rise to a greater number of fibre cell protrusions (given that only 25%–30% of ovule epidermal cells differentiate into fibre cells). During the fibre elongation stage, GhTTLL12 is again recruited into the nucleus by GhTUB8, where its expression is repressed by the fibre elongation inhibitor GhMYB86, thus restricting its mitotic regulatory function in the nucleus. In the cytoplasm, GhTTLL12 directly binds to MTs and promotes their ordered assembly and spatial arrangement, which ultimately contributes to enhanced cotton fibre elongation. Collectively, our results establish that GhMML3/GhMYB86–GhTTLL12–GhTUB8 constitutes a novel regulatory module modulating cotton fibre development.

Work model of GhTTLL12 regulating cotton fibre development. GhTTLL12 exhibits preferential expression during the initiation and elongation stages of cotton fibre development. In the cotton fibre initiation stage, GhTTLL12 is recruited into the nucleus by GhTUB8, and GhMML3 activates its expression, promoting cell mitosis and leading to ovule epidermal cell proliferation, and eventually increasing the number of ovule epidermal cells per unit area results in more fibre cell protrusions. During the cotton fibre elongation phase, GhTUB8 recruits GhTTLL12 into the nucleus; the expression of GhTTLL12 is suppressed by the cotton fibre elongation inhibitor GhMYB86, thereby limiting its role in mitosis within the nucleus. In the cytoplasm, GhTTLL12 can directly bind to microtubules, promoting their organized assembly and arrangement, which in turn facilitates cotton fibre elongation.

GhTTLL12 Potentially Contributes to Improving the Cotton Fibre Quality and Yield Components

Cotton is widely cultivated worldwide due to its high economic value. For cotton producers, fibre yield stands as a core breeding objective, whereas textile enterprises prioritize fibre quality as their primary demand. Given the inherent negative correlation between yield‐related traits and fibre quality characteristics in cotton, integrating high yield with superior fibre quality has emerged as the most prominent bottleneck and major challenge confronting current cotton breeding research. The yield and quality of cotton fibres are influenced by various factors (Huang et al. 2021), among which the role of the cell skeleton is particularly critical. Although significant progress has been made in understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying improved cotton fibre yield and quality, research into the cytoskeleton remains limited.

In the present study, we generated GhTTLL12‐transgenic cotton and performed 2‐year phenotypic evaluations under diverse environmental conditions (Table S2). Overexpression of GhTTLL12 significantly enhanced two key fibre quality traits, including fibre length and fibre strength, while simultaneously improving fibre yield‐related indices. In contrast, knockout of GhTTLL12 severely compromised both fibre quality and yield, leading to substantially shorter fibres and inferior overall performance (Figure 2; Figure 3). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GhTTLL12 is indispensable for modulating the formation of cotton fibre yield‐ and quality‐related traits. Interestingly, the GhTTLL12‐OE seeds became slightly larger, but the LP increased significantly (Figure 3). This indicates that GhTTLL12 does not cause the seeds to grow larger and thereby decrease LP, nor does it make the seeds so small as to affect germination. In summary, we not only elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which GhTTLL12 regulates cotton fibre yield and quality, but also provided excellent genetic resources for cotton molecular breeding.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

All of the transgenic cotton used in this study was based on G. hirsutum accession TM‐1 (Texas Marker‐1) as the genetic transformation recipient. Cotton was cultivated in the greenhouses of the Agricultural Experiment Station of Zhejiang University, as well as in fields in Hangzhou and Sanya, China. Transgenic Arabidopsis plants were generated using the Columbia‐0 (Col‐0) ecotype as the receptor material. All Arabidopsis and Nicotiana (N.) benthamiana materials were grown in the light culture room with the temperature set at 22°C, a light/dark cycle of 16/8 h, and humidity of 70%–80%.

Vector Construction, Plant Genetic Transformation

The overexpression or RNAi plasmids of GhTTLL12 and GhTUB8 were constructed using the WMV062 or WMV062:RTM vectors (provided by WIMI Biotechnology (Changzhou) Co. Ltd), both of which are driven by the CaMV 35S promoter. The CRISPR‐Cas9 system was used to edit the genome sequence of GhTTLL12, and two targeting single‐guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed using the CRISPR‐P 2.0 online tool (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/). For cotton transformation, both the overexpression, RNAi, and knockout vectors were introduced into the recipient material TM‐1 via the Agrobacterium (A.) tumefaciens‐mediated transformation method (Ge et al. 2023). For Arabidopsis thaliana , the plasmids were transformed into ecotype Col‐0 using the floral dip method (Ali et al. 2022). All overexpression lines were amplified by PCR using the forward primer of the target gene and the reverse primer of the vector to identify the positive plants. Then, the expression levels of the target gene were further detected by qRT‐PCR. The knockout lines were confirmed by the Hi‐TOM system (Sun, Liu, et al. 2024). All primers were listed in Table S5.

Phylogenetic Analysis of TTLLL2 Family Genes in Cotton

All amino acid sequences of TTLL12 in 5 diploid cotton species ( G. arboreum , G. herbaceum , G. thurberi , G. raimondii and G. thurberi ) and 5 allotetraploid cotton species ( G. hirsutum , G. barbadense , G. tomentosum , G. mustelinum and G. darwini ) were downloaded from CottonMD (https://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/CottonMD.1). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using ClustalW v.1.81, and a phylogenetic tree was then constructed using the neighbour‐joining method in MEGA v.11.

RNA Isolation and Real‐Time Quantitative RT‐PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from different cotton tissues (including roots, stem tips, leaves, ovules and fibres) and from Arabidopsis thaliana leaves using the Easy Plant (Polysaccharide and Polyphenols) RNA Extraction Kit (#DR0407050, Easy‐Do), and then converted into cDNA using the TransScript One‐Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (#AT311‐02, TransGen, China). The qRT‐PCR was performed using the CFX 96 Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The reaction mixture contained 10 μL of 2 × SYBR qPCR Master Mix (#Q711‐02, Vazyme, China), 2 μL of cDNA template and 0.4 μL of forward and reverse gene‐specific primers (10 μM), and ddH2O was then added to reach a total volume of 20 μL. GhHIS3 (Wu et al. 2025) and AtACT2 (Zhang et al. 2020) were used as the reference genes for cotton and Arabidopsis thaliana , respectively. The amplification results were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Each experiment was performed with three biological replicates and technical replicates, and primers are shown in Table S5.

Cotton Ovule Culture In Vitro and Treatment

The cotton ovules were collected from 1‐DPA bolls for culture. Cotton bolls were surface sterilized twice with 75% ethanol for 3 min each time, then once with 95% ethanol for 30 s, and finally washed five times with ddH2O for 1 min each time. Use a scalpel to separate the ovules from the cotton bolls and cultured in a fluid nutrient medium at 30°C in darkness (Beasley and Ting 1973). Fibre length was measured at 5‐, 10‐, and 15‐days, respectively. For treatment assay, ovules were cultured in BT medium supplemented with 100 nM oryzalin or 10 μM taxol for 15 days.

Subcellular Localization

The full‐length CDS without the terminator of GhTTLL12 or GhTUB8 was recombined into the pBinGFP4 vector using the homologous recombination method. Subsequently, constructs were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and infiltrated into the leaves of 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana. The fluorescence signal was detected using a Zeiss LSM780 confocal laser scanning microscope after dark incubation for 48 h. The primers used in this assay are listed in Table S5.

MT Immunofluorescence

The fibres from the ovule cultured in vitro for 5‐, 10‐, or 15‐days were fixed in a PME buffer (50 mM PIPES, 5 mM EGTA and 5 mM MgCl2, pH 6.9) containing 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde and 0.1% (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 1 h. The fixed fibres were carefully washed three times with PME buffer, for 10 min each time, and then digested with 1% cellulose and 1% macerozyme for 1.5 h in a 37°C incubator. Following digestion, the fibres were washed twice with PME buffer and once with PBS buffer, for 10 min each time. Subsequently, the samples were blocked with 1% BSA (Bovine Serum Albumin) at room temperature for 15 min, and then washed with PBS buffer for 10 min. The fibres were incubated with a mouse monoclonal anti‐tubulin primary antibody (#T9026, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO; dilution 1:200) at 4°C overnight, followed by an Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse secondary antibody (#A11001, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA; dilution 1:200) at room temperature for 1 h. After each incubation, the fibres were washed with PBS buffer for 10 min. Finally, the fibres were placed on a glass slide, and fluorescence signals were detected using an ultra‐high‐resolution laser confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM980, Jena, Germany). MT fluorescence intensity and orientation were analysed by ImageJ software (Graham and Haigler 2021; Xu et al. 2024). The method for observing the dynamics of MTs during mitosis in the meristematic region of transgenic cotton root tips is consistent with the procedure for immunofluorescent staining of MTs in fibre cells.

Phenotype Analysis of Transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana and Cotton

The number of trichomes on the leaves and stems of Arabidopsis thaliana was observed using a stereoscope (Zeiss Stemi 305, Jena, Germany). For cotton, a total of 25 mature, naturally self‐pollinated bolls were harvested from the middle and upper parts of 10 individual plants in the field for phenotypic assessment. Subsequently, the fibre yield traits contained single boll weight (SBW), lint percentage (LP) and seed index (SI, weight of 100 seeds) were measured. The fibre quality parameters, including fibre length (FL), fibre strength (FS), fibre elongation (FE) and Micronaire value, were analysed using a high‐volume fibre test system (Premier HFT 9000; Premier Evolvics Pvt. Ltd., Coimbatore, India) at the Cotton Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Anyang, Henan Province, China). Three biological replicates were used to analyse each line.

Histochemical GUS Staining

The 1.5 kb promoter of GhTTLL12 was used to replace the CaMV 35S promoter in the pBI121 vector and transformed it into wild‐type Arabidopsis thaliana and cotton using the Agrobacterium tumefaciens ‐mediated transformation method. For histochemical GUS staining, fresh tissues of Arabidopsis thaliana (roots, stems and rosette leaves) and cotton (roots, stems, true leaves, ovules, and fibres at different developmental stages) were incubated in X‐Gluc solution and incubated overnight at 37°C and then rinsed with 75% ethanol. The GUS staining patterns were observed and photographed using a stereomicroscope (Zeiss Stemi 305, Jena, Germany).

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Paraffin Section Analysis

SEM and paraffin sections were used to observe lint and fuzz initiation of cotton. For SEM, −0.5‐, 0‐ and 1‐DPA ovules of cotton were fixed with 2.5% (v/v) paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C, then further fixed with 1% (v/v) osmium acid. The samples were dehydrated successively through a graded ethanol series of 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95% and 100% (v/v). Subsequently, the dehydrated samples were dried using HCP‐2 (Hitachi). All the ovule samples were coated with gold powder using an E‐1010/E‐1020 ion sputter (Japan), and then observed and photographed using a SEM (Germany) (Chen et al. 2025). For paraffin sectioning, 5‐DPA ovules were fixed in FAA (Formalin‐Aceto‐Alcohol) solution for 24 h and then dehydrated using 70%, 85%, 95% and 100% ethanol, separately. The samples were then embedded in paraffin and cut into thin sections using a microtome (Microm HM 340E, Thermo). Next, they were stained with tolonium chloride for observation under a microscope (ECLIPSE‐Ci, NIKON).

MT Co‐Sedimentation Assay

The MT co‐sedimentation assay was performed as previously described by Qian et al. (2023). In brief, purified GST‐GhTTLL12 protein and paclitaxel‐stabilized MTs were incubated in PME buffer (1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA and 100 mM PIPES‐KOH, pH 6.9) supplemented with 20 μM paclitaxel at room temperature for 30 min. After centrifugation at 100000 × g for 40 min at room temperature, the resulting supernatant (S) and pellet (P) were separated by SDS‐PAGE.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid (Y2H) Assay

The CDS of GhTTLL12 was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector (bait), and the CDS of GhTUB8 was inserted into the pGADT7 vector (prey). Empty vectors were used as the negative controls. The recombinant plasmids were co‐transformed into the Y2HGold chemically competent cell (#CC309, Coolaber) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Yeast cells were then cultured on a SD/‐Trp/‐Leu plate (#PM2221, Coolaber) and SD/‐Ade/‐His/‐Leu/‐Trp plate (#PM2112, Coolaber) for 3–4 days at 30°C. The primers used in this assay are listed in Table S5.

Luciferase Complementation Imaging (LCI) Assay

The full‐length CDSs without the terminator of GhTTLL12 and GhTUB8 were cloned into the pCAMBIA1300‐nLUC and pCAMBIA1300‐cLUC vectors, respectively. Empty vectors served as the negative controls. All constructs were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 for transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves. After 48 h of infiltration, a 1 mM D‐fluorescein potassium salt solution was evenly applied to the abaxial surface of tobacco leaves and incubated in darkness for 5 min. Then, the fluorescence signal was detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon‐5200). The primers used in this experiment are listed in Table S5.

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC) Assay

The CDSs without stop codons of GhTTLL12 and GhTUB8 were respectively fused into pCAMBIA1300‐YNE and pCAMBIA1300‐YCE vectors. All recombinant plasmids were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101, and the bacterial cultures were then co‐infiltrated into the leaves of 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana. The injected tobaccos were grown in a greenhouse under dark conditions for 2 days, and the fluorescence signals were observed using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV1200). The primers used in this assay are listed in Table S5.

Protein Extraction and Western Blot Analysis

10‐DPA fibres harvested from TM‐1 and transgenic lines were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen and suspended in NP‐40 lysis buffer (#P00133F, Beyotime) containing 1 mM PMSF (#ST506, Beyotime). After centrifugation at 4°C, the supernatant was taken. Subsequently, equal amounts of total protein were mixed with 5× SDS‐PAGE Sample Loading Buffer (#P0015, Beyotime) and denatured at 98°C for 10 min. The proteins were loaded on a 10% SDS‐PAGE gel to separate and transferred to a PVDF membrane. Positive transgenic cotton lines were detected by an Anti‐DDDDK tag mouse monoclonal antibody (#ab18230, abcam, 1:5000), followed by a Goat Anti‐Mouse IgG H&L (HRP) antibody (#ab205719, abcam, 1:5000). A monoclonal mouse antibody ATT (#T9028, Sigma‐Aldrich, 1:5000) was used to detect tyrosinated α‐tubulin. The signal was visualized by the chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon‐5200).

Pull‐Down Assay

The CDS of GhTTLL12 was fused with the GST‐tag of the pGEX‐4T‐1 vector. The GhTUB8 CDS was cloned into the pMAL‐c2x vector and fused with the MBP tag. All constructs were expressed in the E. coli BL21 strain. GST‐tag protein was purified by a GST‐tag Protein Purification Kit (#P2262, Beyotime), and MBP‐tag protein was purified using a PurKineTM MBP‐Tag Protein Purification Kit (#KTP2020, Abbkine). A 500 μL reaction mixture containing 400 μL GST pull‐down protein binding buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% (v/v) NP‐40, 1 mM DTT, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM PMSF, pH = 8.0), 50 μL GST‐tag Purification Resin, 25 μg GhTTLL12‐GST and GhTUB8‐MBP fusion proteins and ddH2O was incubated at 4°C overnight. An empty GST‐tag protein served as the negative control. Subsequently, 50 μL of GST pull‐down wash buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, 400 mM NaCl, 50 mM GSH, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM PMSF, pH = 8.0) was used to collect the protein. The proteins were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE gel and transferred to PVDF membrane for antibody (#AF2888, GST Tag Mouse Monoclonal Antibody, Beyotime, 1:5000; #AF2912, MBP Tag Mouse Monoclonal Antibody, Beyotime, 1:5000; #A0216, HRP‐labelled Goat Anti‐Mouse IgG (H+L), Beyotime, 1:5000) incubation. The signal was detected using the chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon‐5200). The primers used in this assay are listed in Table S5.

Yeast One‐Hybrid (Y1H) Assay

The 1500 bp promoter sequence of GhTTLL12 was inserted into the pAbAi vector (bait), and the CDS of GhMML3A, GhMML3D and GhMYB86 were cloned into the pGADT7 vector (prey), respectively. The pAbAi‐proGhTTLL12 vector was transformed into the Y1HGold yeast strain (#CC308, Coolaber) and cultured on the SD/‐Ura (#PM2271, Coolaber) medium with different concentrations of Aureobasidin A (AbA) to determine the optimal concentration for inhibiting the self‐activation growth of the bait vector. Subsequently, the prey plasmid and an empty pGADT7 vector, which was used as a negative control, were transformed into the bait yeast strains and cultured on the SD/‐Leu medium containing the predetermined inhibitory concentration of AbA to confirm the protein‐DNA interaction. The primers used in this assay are listed in Table S5.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

The promoter fragment of GhTTLL12 was inserted into the pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector (reporter), and the CDS of GhMML3A, GhMML3D and GhMYB86 were individually cloned into the pGreen II 62‐SK vector (effector). The effector and reporter plasmids were transformed into the A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and then transiently co‐infiltrated into the leaf epidermal cells of N. benthamiana. The LUC signals were observed by a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200). Additionally, the LUC and REN activities were measured quantitatively using a Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (#DL101‐01, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The primers used in this assay are listed in Table S5.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

The EMSA was performed using the Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (#GS009, Beyotime). In brief, the GhTTLL12 promoter fragment containing the binding sites for GhMML3A, GhMML3D or GhMYB86 was synthesized and labelled with a biotin‐conjugated probe, and non‐labelled probes were used as cold competitors. The CDS of GhMML3 or GhMYB86 was cloned into the pGEX‐4T‐1 vector and expressed in the E. coli Rosetta strain. The recombinant proteins were purified using a GST‐tag Protein Purification Kit (#P2262, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The reaction mixture was separated using a 6% non‐denaturing gel in 0.5 × TBE (Tris‐Borate‐EDTA) buffer and then transferred onto Biodyne B nylon membranes (Pall Corporation, USA). Signals were detected by the chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon‐5200). The primers used in this assay are listed in Table S5.
