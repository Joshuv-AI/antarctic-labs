Unusual coexistence of septic pulmonary embolism and cerebral venous sinus thrombosis in an SLE patient: a case report
Authors: Naimo Awil Dahir, Abdisalam Ismail Hassan, Abdinasir Mohamed Elmi, Mahad Sadik Mukhtar, Jama Farah Abdillahi, Abdirahman Mohamed Hassan Direi, Abdullahi Ali Roble, Nadira Mohamed Ali, Ikran Abdirahman Yusuf, Ahmed Muhammad Bashir, Balqiz Mohamed Kulle, Abdullahi Osman Mohamed, Said Abdi Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Septic pulmonary embolism (SPE) is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of systemic infection. Patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) are particularly vulnerable due to immune dysregulation, chronic inflammation, and a prothrombotic state. The concurrent occurrence of SPE, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST), and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) in an SLE patient is exceptionally uncommon and poses major diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case presentation:We report a 30-year-old woman with known SLE and lupus nephritis, receiving irregular immunosuppressive therapy and undergoing hemodialysis, who presented with fever, pleuritic chest pain, productive cough, and altered mental status. Chest computed tomography demonstrated multiple bilateral cavitating pulmonary nodules and pleural-based wedge-shaped opacities, consistent with septic pulmonary embolism. Blood cultures grew methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus. During hospitalization, she developed acute neurological deterioration with left-sided weakness. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed a large right frontal intraparenchymal hemorrhage, while magnetic resonance venography demonstrated thrombosis of the right transverse sinus and inferior sagittal sinus, confirming CVST complicated by hemorrhagic venous infarction. The patient was treated with targeted intravenous antibiotics and supportive neurocritical care. Anticoagulation was withheld due to active intracerebral bleeding. She showed gradual clinical improvement and was discharged in stable condition.Discussion:This case highlights the complex interaction between infection, autoimmune-mediated vascular injury, and hypercoagulability in SLE, resulting in simultaneous pulmonary and cerebral thrombo-infectious complications.Conclusion:The coexistence of SPE, CVST, and ICH in SLE represents a rare but life-threatening scenario. Early recognition, prompt multimodal imaging, and coordinated multidisciplinary management are crucial for improving outcomes in high-risk patients.

Introduction

Septic pulmonary embolism (SPE) is a serious and potentially life-threatening condition that can arise from a variety of infectious processes and is particularly relevant in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)[1]. SLE is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by multisystem inflammation and a wide spectrum of complications, including thromboembolic events that significantly contribute to morbidity and mortality. CVST is an important neurological complication of SLE and accounts for approximately 11% of stroke cases in this population[3]. It results from thrombosis of the cerebral venous sinuses, leading to impaired venous drainage, elevated intracranial pressure, and, potentially, hemorrhagic complications such as intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH)[4]. Previous reports have shown that CVST progressing to ICH may occur in the setting of infectious triggers, including septic emboli, highlighting the complex interplay between coagulopathy, infection, and inflammation in SLE patients[5].

The simultaneous occurrence of SPE with neurological complications such as CVST and ICH poses substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly in patients with SLE, who are inherently predisposed to vascular pathology because of systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, vasculopathy, and hypercoagulability[2]. Understanding how infections can rapidly precipitate severe vascular and neurological complications in individuals with underlying autoimmune disease is essential for timely diagnosis and appropriate intervention.

Herein, we report a rare and clinically complex case involving the concurrent occurrence of SPE, CVST, and ICH in a patient with systemic lupus erythematosus, emphasizing the importance of early recognition, multimodal imaging, and coordinated multidisciplinary management.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with a several-day history of high-grade fever, productive cough with yellowish sputum, pleuritic right-sided chest pain, progressive fatigue, poor oral intake, and intermittent headaches. Her medical history was significant for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) complicated by lupus nephritis, diagnosed 1 year earlier. She had been prescribed hydroxychloroquine 200 mg twice daily and mycophenolate mofetil 2 g daily, but admitted to poor adherence to treatment. She also had chronic kidney disease requiring hemodialysis twice weekly for the preceding 2 months.

On presentation, the patient appeared acutely ill and febrile. Vital signs revealed a temperature of 39.2°C, heart rate of 118 beats/min, blood pressure of 94/58 mmHg, respiratory rate of 26 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 88% on room air, which improved with supplemental oxygen. Neurologically, she was disoriented to time, place, and person. Respiratory examination demonstrated tachypnea with bilateral coarse crackles. Cardiovascular examination showed tachycardia with normal heart sounds and no audible murmurs. Abdominal examination was unremarkable. Cutaneous examination revealed an ulcerated lesion on the left index finger. Initial neurological examination demonstrated generalized confusion without focal neurological deficits.

Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis, markedly elevated C-reactive protein levels, elevated urea and creatinine consistent with worsening renal dysfunction, hyperbilirubinemia, and metabolic acidosis. Further immunological evaluation demonstrated a positive antinuclear antibody test, elevated anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) titers, and reduced complement levels (C3 and C4), indicating active lupus. Platelet count was within normal limits. Coagulation studies showed mildly prolonged prothrombin time with elevated D-dimer levels. Blood cultures later grew methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no valvular vegetations or evidence of infective endocarditis.

Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules of varying sizes, several with cavitation and associated feeding-vessel signs (Fig. 1A), along with peripheral, pleural-based, wedge-shaped opacities (Fig. 1B), findings highly suggestive of SPE. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit and started on broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics and supportive care. Several days after admission, her neurological condition deteriorated acutely, with the sudden onset of severe headache and left-sided weakness. Neurological examination revealed left-sided hemiparesis with muscle strength graded as 1/5, while pupillary responses remained equal and reactive.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a large right frontal intraparenchymal hematoma (Fig. 2A–B). Subsequent magnetic resonance venography (MRV) revealed loss of flow signal within the right transverse and inferior sagittal sinuses (Fig. 3A–B), confirming CVST complicated by hemorrhagic venous infarction. Based on the combined clinical, radiological, microbiological, and immunological findings, a diagnosis of SPE complicated by CVST and right frontal ICH was established. The patient was transferred to the neurology service for specialized multidisciplinary management.

Following the identification of MSSA in blood cultures, antimicrobial therapy was narrowed to intravenous cefazolin, 2 g every 8 hours. The patient completed a 14-day course of intravenous antibiotics in addition to supportive neurocritical and renal care.

Although anticoagulation is generally recommended in CVST, it was deferred for this patient following a multidisciplinary discussion involving the neurology, hematology, nephrology, and critical care teams because of a large, active intracerebral hematoma, ongoing sepsis, renal impairment requiring hemodialysis, and concern regarding hematoma expansion during the acute phase.

Over the following 2 weeks, the patient demonstrated gradual neurological improvement and recovery of respiratory status. Repeat imaging showed stable intracranial findings, with an interval reduction of the pulmonary lesions. She was subsequently transferred to the medical ward for continued rehabilitation and was discharged in stable condition on hospital day 14.

Discussion

The present case highlights the complex and multifactorial complications that may occur in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), particularly the rare coexistence of SPE, CVST, and ICH. SLE is widely recognized as a prothrombotic autoimmune disorder that predisposes patients to thromboembolic events through several mechanisms, including antiphospholipid antibody–mediated hypercoagulability, endothelial dysfunction, immune-mediated vascular injury, chronic inflammation, and complement activation[4]. Active lupus disease, as demonstrated in our patient by elevated anti-dsDNA antibody levels and reduced complement levels, likely contributed to the increased thrombotic risk.

Infections further amplify the prothrombotic and inflammatory state in patients with SLE. SPE is an uncommon but serious complication caused by infected thrombi embolizing to the pulmonary arterial circulation, resulting in inflammatory pulmonary injury, cavitation, and peripheral infarction[6]. In the present case, methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) bacteremia likely played a central role in precipitating both systemic inflammation and thromboembolic complications.

Several differential diagnoses were considered during the diagnostic process. Severe bacterial pneumonia was initially suspected because of the patient’s fever, productive cough, and pulmonary infiltrates. However, the presence of multiple bilateral peripheral cavitary nodules with feeding-vessel signs and pleural-based wedge-shaped opacities on chest CT strongly favored SPE. Pulmonary vasculitis related to SLE was also considered but was deemed less likely because of the absence of characteristic radiologic findings and additional supportive clinical evidence. Non-septic pulmonary thromboembolism was another diagnostic consideration; however, the cavitary nature of the pulmonary lesions together with positive MSSA blood cultures strongly supported the diagnosis of SPE.

Opportunistic pulmonary infections were also considered because of the patient’s immunocompromised status and irregular immunosuppressive therapy. Nevertheless, the characteristic radiologic findings combined with microbiological confirmation favored SPE rather than an opportunistic infection. Infective endocarditis was additionally evaluated as a potential embolic source but was excluded after transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no valvular vegetations or intracardiac thrombi.

Within this context, CVST represents a recognized but relatively uncommon neurological manifestation of SLE. Previous studies estimate that approximately 11 to 19% of SLE-related cerebrovascular events involve venous sinus thrombosis[2,7]. Antiphospholipid antibodies substantially increase the risk of CVST in SLE patients, although thrombosis may also occur in their absence because of systemic inflammation, endothelial injury, nephrotic-range protein loss, and infection-related hypercoagulability. Clinical manifestations such as headache, seizures, altered mental status, visual disturbances, or focal neurological deficits should therefore raise suspicion for CVST, particularly in patients presenting with concurrent pulmonary thromboembolic findings. The coexistence of pulmonary embolic disease and CVST further increases diagnostic and therapeutic complexity[8–10].

Another major clinical challenge in this case was the development of ICH secondary to hemorrhagic venous infarction. ICH has been reported in approximately 30 to 40% of CVST cases[11,12]. Current guidelines generally recommend anticoagulation even in the presence of hemorrhagic venous infarction because anticoagulation helps prevent thrombus propagation and facilitates venous recanalization. However, therapeutic decisions must be individualized in critically ill patients with a high bleeding risk.

In our patient, anticoagulation was deferred following a multidisciplinary consultation involving the neurology, hematology, nephrology, and critical care teams because of the presence of a large, active intraparenchymal hematoma, ongoing sepsis, renal impairment requiring hemodialysis, and concern about hematoma expansion during the acute phase. A careful balance of the risks of worsening intracranial bleeding and thrombotic progression was, therefore, necessary. This case highlights a significant therapeutic dilemma encountered when managing concurrent thrombosis and hemorrhage in patients with severe systemic inflammatory disease.

Early diagnosis through multimodal imaging was essential for establishing the diagnosis and guiding management. MRI combined with MRV remains the gold standard for identifying venous sinus thrombosis and hemorrhagic venous infarction[2,4,13,14]. Similarly, recognition of characteristic pulmonary CT findings, including cavitary nodules, feeding-vessel signs, and pleural-based wedge-shaped lesions, is critical for the early diagnosis of SPE and prompt initiation of targeted antimicrobial therapy.

Overall, the concurrent occurrence of SPE, CVST, and ICH in a patient with SLE underscores the intricate interaction between autoimmune disease activity, infection, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. This rare but life-threatening clinical constellation requires a coordinated multidisciplinary approach involving infectious disease specialists, neurologists, hematologists, nephrologists, radiologists, and critical care physicians. Early recognition, timely imaging, individualized therapeutic decision-making, and close multidisciplinary collaboration remain essential for improving patient outcomes.

This report has several limitations. As a single-patient case report, the findings cannot be generalized to all patients with SLE. In addition, the relatively short follow-up period limited assessment of long-term neurological and pulmonary outcomes. Nevertheless, this case provides valuable clinical insight into a rare and severe combination of thrombo-infectious complications in SLE and highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with their coexistence.

Conclusion

This case underscores the rare but clinically significant coexistence of SPE, CVST, and ICH in a patient with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). The interplay between infection, autoimmune vascular injury, and hypercoagulability presents substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, especially when treatment of one condition may exacerbate another. Early recognition, prompt imaging, and coordinated multidisciplinary management are essential for optimizing outcomes. Greater clinical awareness and further research are needed to better understand the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these overlapping complications and to guide safe and effective management strategies for patients with SLE.
