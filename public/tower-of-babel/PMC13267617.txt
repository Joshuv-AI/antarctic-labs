Ropeginterferon alfa-2b-njft treatment in essential thrombocythemia across different driver mutations: results from a North American, single-arm, multicentre study (EXCEED-ET)
Authors: Brandi N Reeves, Firas El Chaer, Lynda Foltz, Tsewang Tashi, Ghaith Abu-Zeinah, Albert Qin, Jennifer Lucas, Anna B Halpern, Dawn Maze, Hana Safah, Casey L O’Connell, Swati Goel, Lindsay Rein, Bruno Fang, Joan How, Sunil Babu, Zhuoyan Li, Sonia Cerquozzi, Stephen T Oh, Anthony M Hunter, Nikolai Podoltsev, Pankit Vachhani, Julia M Cunningham, Christopher Hillis, Prithviraj Bose, Abdulraheem Yacoub, John Mascarenhas, Oleh Zagrijtschuk, Henry Castro, Ruben A Mesa, Lucia Masarova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRopeginterferon alfa-2b-njft (ropeg), a mono-PEGylated interferon-α, showed efficacy and safety in patients with hydroxyurea-intolerant/resistant essential thrombocythemia (ET) in the phase 3 SURPASS-ET largely conducted in Asia. Here we report the results from the EXCEED-ET study, which evaluated ropeg in both treatment-naïve and hydroxyurea pre-treated ET in North America.MethodsThe single-arm, multicentre, phase 2b trial EXCEED-ET was conducted at 28 centers in the United States and Canada. Patients with ET enrolled were required to have a platelet count >450 × 109/L at screening. Patients were eligible regardless of prior cytoreductive therapy status and included both treatment-naïve and hydroxyurea-pretreated patients. Ropeg was subcutaneously administered in an accelerated titration schedule at 250 μg at Week 0, 350 μg at Week 2, and 500 μg from Week 4 and beyond. The primary endpoint was durable, modified European LeukemiaNet (ELN) response at both Months 10 and 13 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05482971).FindingsBetween January 17, 2023, and May 15, 2025, 106 patients were screened and 91 were enrolled. At baseline, participant median age was 57 years (range, 22–84) with 57/91 (62.6%) being female and 71/91 (78.0%) being White. Among 91 patients enrolled, durable modified ELN response rate was 60.2% (95% CI, 49.0–71.4). The median time to hematologic response was only 8.4 weeks (95% CI: 8.1–11.9). Splenomegaly and symptom improvement or non progression occurred in 90/91 (98.9%) and 71/91 (78.0%), respectively. Seven of 91 (7.7%) patients with palpable spleen at baseline and all had an improvement in disease-related signs. The rates of molecular response for driver mutations at Month 13 as defined by ELN2009 criteria were 35.0% (7/20) for patients with JAK2V617F, 16.0% (4/25) for CALR, and 25.0% (2/8) for MPL mutations. One (1/1) TP53 mutation-positive patient showed a complete molecular response. Thrombosis and progression occurred in 3.4% (3/91) and 1.1% (1/91), respectively. Most treatment-emergent adverse events were mild, including fatigue (60%) and reversible transaminase elevations (58%). There was no grade 5 TEAE and one (1.1%) grade 4 TEAE (hepatitis toxic) which recovered without sequelae. Grade ≥3 TEAEs occurred in 27.5% (25/91) of the participants, most commonly with alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase increases (4.4%; 4/91), neutrophil count decrease (4.4%; 4/91), fatigue (3.3%; 3/91) and lymphocyte count decrease (3.3%; 3/91). Treatment-related serious adverse events occurred in 5.5% (5/91) of patients. Pneumonia was the most common SAE, occurring in 2/91 (2.2%) patients. Ropeg-related discontinuations occurred in 9/91 (9.9%) patients.InterpretationRopeg showed efficacy and substantial molecular responses with good overall tolerability across a broad ET population.FundingPharmaEssentiaKeywords: Ropeginterferon alfa-2b-njft, Essential thrombocythemia, Treatment-naïve, Pre-treated, Northern American

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Ropeginterferon alfa-2b (ropeg) is a new-generation interferon therapy with a robust pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic profile allowing a flexible biweekly to monthly dosing schedule. Ropeg is approved for the treatment of polycythemia vera (PV) on a global basis and has demonstrated efficacy in the SURPASS ET study for the treatment of ET patients, who were resistant or intolerant to HU, as published recently (Mesa et al., 2025). However, the SURPASS ET study included only few North American patients and the generalizability of the findings to US or Canada patients remained to be shown. In addition, we searched PubMed from July 1, 2016, to January 15, 2023, for clinical trial reports, using the terms “essential thrombocythemia”, “pegylated interferon”, “ropeginterferon alfa-2b”, and “cytoreductive therapies”, with no language restrictions. There was a lack of new disease-modifying, cytoreductive therapies approved for the treatment of ET.

Added value of this study

This single arm, multi-center study investigated the efficacy and safety of ropeg in US and Canadian patients with ET regardless of prior treatment (both naïve and pretreated). The study provided novel data that ropeg therapy could produce durable hematologic responses together with reductions in molecular disease burden in a heterogeneous population that included both treatment-naïve patients and those previously exposed to hydroxyurea. Hematologic responses occurred relatively rapidly, with median time to response of over eight weeks. Another important finding of the study is the demonstration of molecular responses across all three major driver mutations associated with ET. The results provide solid evidence that ropeg treatment leads to deep molecular response across JAK2V617F, CALR and MPL driver mutations. This prospective study adds important evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of ropeg in patients with ET treated in North America, and together with phase 3 SURPASS-ET, indicates that ropeg is an effective and tolerable therapy with a positive benefit-risk profile for both treatment-naïve and HU-treated patients with ET.

Implications of all the available evidence

The results indicate ropeg has strong efficacy and acceptable safety profile for ET patients regardless of driver mutation. Consistency with the observations from the SURPASS ET study supports the generalizability of the latter study to the North American population.

Introduction

Essential thrombocythemia (ET) is a Philadelphia chromosome-negative myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by sustained thrombocytosis, increased risk of thrombosis and hemorrhage, burdensome microvascular symptoms, and a propensity for progression to myelofibrosis or acute myeloid leukemia (AML).1 Most cases of ET are associated with 1 of 3 gain-of-function MPN driver mutations JAK2V617F (approximately 50–60%), CALR (20–25%), and MPL (3%–4%). ET driver mutations confer a differential risk of thrombo-hemorrhagic events, progression, and survival, which can be further modified by co-existing mutations in other genes such as TET2, ASXL1, DNMT3A, and TP53.1,2 Other mutations, including SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, IDH1/2, EZH2, SETBP1, KRAS/NRAS, and SH2B3, could also lead to a high risk of disease progression and a poor prognosis and influence the clinical management in ET.2,3

The primary therapeutic goals in ET are to reduce thrombo-hemorrhagic complications, alleviate symptoms, and, where possible, to prevent disease progression.4 The mainstay of therapy in ET has been cytoreductive agents to normalize peripheral blood cell counts. However recent prospective data for the closely related MPN, polycythemia vera (PV), highlight the importance of achieving a deep driver mutation reduction to diminish neoplastic cells and reduce the risk of thrombosis and disease progression.5, 6, 7 All MPN driver mutations result in constitutively activated JAK-STAT signaling not only in mature blood cells but also in the mutation-carrying stem and progenitor cells leading to elaboration of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic cytokines implicated in disease progression.8

Hydroxyurea (HU) has commonly been used as an off-label, first-line cytoreductive treatment for ET. It non-specifically inhibits DNA synthesis, controls blood counts and reduces thrombotic events.9, 10, 11 Anagrelide inhibits the maturation of platelets from megakaryocytes and has served as a first-line or second-line alternative option in ET treatment. It has also been shown to reduce elevated platelet counts and thrombotic risk.10,11 However, the long-term therapeutic efficacy of both agents is confounded by side effects such as skin ulcers, non-melanoma skin cancers and gastrointestinal disorders for HU or cardiovascular side effects for anagrelide.12,13 Furthermore, neither HU nor anagrelide elicit sustained reductions of MPN driver mutations.14,15

Recombinant human interferon-alfa (IFN-α), a type 1 IFN, has long been used as an off-label therapy for ET and related MPNs.16 Type 1 IFNs, including α and β, can selectively inhibit tumor cell cycle progression, induce tumor cell apoptosis, and stimulate immune-mediated antitumor response.17,18 IFN-α treatment has been shown to induce complete hematologic response (CHR) and be associated with favorable event-free survival in prospective PV studies as well as myelofibrosis-free survival in both PV and ET in retrospective analyses.19, 20, 21, 22 International treatment guidelines have recommended the off-label use of IFNs in ET, particularly for younger patients and those who are intolerant of or refractory to HU.23,24 The guidelines highlight IFN therapy as a preferred option after HU and, in some cases, as a first-line approach for selected patients, reflecting its growing acceptance. Most recently, PEGylated interferon alfa-2a (Pegasys®) received regulatory approval in Europe for the treatment of ET based on the consortium trial MPD-RC112, marking an important milestone and providing formal recognition of the role of IFNs in this disease.25

Ropeginterferon alfa-2b-njft (ropeg) is a novel, mono-PEGylated proline-IFN-α with a robust exposure to response relationship.26 It is administered once every 2 weeks subcutaneously in patients with PV (up to a maximum dose of 500 μg) until hematological parameters are stabilized and can be given every 4 weeks after 1 year of treatment to responding patients. In PV, ropeg induces durable CHR and deep molecular response in JAK2V617F.14,27 Recently, the phase 3 SURPASS-ET study, conducted largely in Asian patients with ET and HU-resistance/intolerance, demonstrated efficacy and safety with ropeg using a higher initial dose and accelerated titration (HIDAT) regimen.15 The current study was designed to complement the SURPASS-ET study and evaluate the efficacy, safety, and molecular response of ropeg in North American patients with ET requiring cytoreductive therapy (including treatment-naïve and HU-treated patients), using the HIDAT regimen and contemporarily modified ELN response criteria.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a phase 2b, open-label, single-arm, multicenter trial conducted at 28 clinical sites across the United States and Canada. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP). Institutional review boards or ethics committees at each participating center approved the study, and all patients provided written informed consent and appropriate privacy authorization. Patients enrolled were required to have ET diagnosed according to WHO 2016 criteria and a platelet count >450 × 109/L at screening. Importantly, patients were eligible regardless of prior cytoreductive therapy status and included both treatment-naïve and hydroxyurea-pretreated patients. The study population reflected the spectrum of patients in whom cytoreduction may be clinically considered in routine practice, including those with elevated platelet counts, prior thrombosis, disease-related symptoms, or physician-determined need for cytoreductive therapy.28 Prior IFN-α was allowed but those who had discontinued IFN-α in the past due to poor efficacy or tolerability were excluded, as were patients with prior use of Janus kinase inhibitors.

Randomization and masking

This is a single-arm, open label study.

Procedures

Following a 28-day screening period, patients received ropeg subcutaneously every 2 weeks, starting at 250 μg at Week 0, escalating to 350 μg at Week 2, and then to 500 μg from Week 4 onward. Dose adjustments depending on safety and tolerability were allowed during treatment. If a patient experienced ≥grade 3 toxicity, or a drop in absolute neutrophil count to below 0.6 × 109/L, ropeg was temporarily interrupted until the adverse event improved to ≤grade 1. Ropeg re-initiation commenced at one dose level lower than the dose that caused the toxicity. In the case of grade 2 toxicity, or a drop in absolute neutrophil count to below 0.75 × 109/L but higher than 0.6 × 109/L, investigator discretion to continue ropeg without interruption at a one dose level reduction was allowed. Dose reductions to previous dose levels or even below the starting dose, i.e., 200, 150, or 100 μg were permitted in cases of toxicity. No concomitant cytoreductive therapies were allowed. The one-year treatment lasted 13 months including the initial 4 weeks of dose up-titrations from 250 μg to 500 μg and was followed by a 28-day follow-up period. Patients continuing to benefit from therapy were allowed to enter a long-term extension phase, lasting up to three years. During the extension phase, quarterly visits were mandatory, while other visits could be conducted in person or by telephone as clinically indicated.

Outcomes

Assessments included platelet and white blood cell (WBC) counts, spleen size evaluation by palpation and ultrasound, patient-reported outcomes (PROs) using the Myeloproliferative Neoplasm Symptom Assessment Form Total Symptom Score (MPN-SAF TSS) and European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 3 Level (EQ-5D-3L) instruments, optional bone marrow histology, and hemorrhagic or thrombotic events. Variant allele frequency (VAF) was assessed for JAK2, CALR, and MPL driver mutations, as well as non-driver mutations in TET2, ASXL1, DNMT3A, TP53, EZH2, SETBP1, KRAS, BCOR, CDKN2A, CUX1, NOTCH1, ZRSR2, SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, IDH1, IDH2, and NRAS genes.1,2,29 Quantitative VAF measurements were performed quarterly via next-generation sequencing by a central laboratory with an assay validated for detection of variants at least 5% VAF (MEDPACE, Cincinnati, Ohio). Safety assessments included vital signs, physical examinations, clinical laboratory tests, electrocardiograms, ECOG performance status, and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Echocardiograms, chest radiographs, and ophthalmologic exams were performed as clinically indicated. Pharmacokinetics (PK) parameters were analyzed using a population PK model and trough concentrations. Immunogenicity assessments measured anti-drug antibodies and neutralizing antibodies.

The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients achieving a durable modified ELN response at both Months 10 and 13. This response required simultaneous peripheral blood count response (platelets ≤400 × 109/L and WBCs <10 × 109/L), improvement or non-progression of spleen size, and absence of thrombotic or hemorrhagic events at both Months 10 and 13. Symptom improvement (MPN-SAF TSS) was initially included but later removed, following regulatory guidance, from the composite endpoint because the study was a single-arm design and symptom endpoints rely heavily on subjective reporting without a comparator group. To preserve interpretability of the primary endpoint, symptoms were instead analyzed as a secondary endpoint. In addition, we also analyzed the response rate with the MPN-SAF TSS component to maintain comparability with the SURPASS-ET study. Key secondary endpoints included the proportion of patients maintaining hematologic normalization over time, longitudinal changes in response rates, molecular burden dynamics, symptomatic improvement, quality-of-life measures, and rates of thromboembolic or progressive disease events. Safety outcomes included the incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs) and laboratory abnormalities.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed according to a pre-specified statistical analysis plan (SAP). Categorical outcomes were summarized with counts and exact 95% CI, and continuous variables with descriptive statistics. Missing data for the primary efficacy endpoint were imputed by assuming missing at random (MAR)30 using multiple imputation (MI) methodology. The MAR assumption is that the outcomes would follow a distribution like those of patients with similar baseline and previous post-baseline values. The imputation model included the baseline key variables (age, sex, country, baseline platelet, baseline WBC, baseline spleen palpable status), ELN response status at Month 4, 7, 10 and 13, and durable ELN response at both Months 10 and 13. The order of variables in the imputed model were specified based on the appropriate order of visits at which variables were measured or assessed. The imputation was performed repeatedly 100 times. The missing data imputation was implemented using PROC MI and combined using SAS PROC MIANALYZE in SAS 9.4. For each of the 100 complete-imputed datasets after MI, the proportion of patients who met the response criteria were estimated with exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95% CI. The 100 sets of results were combined using Rubin’s rule, by PROC MIANALYZE in SAS. The population-level summary is the proportion of patients who met the response criteria with the 95% CI pooled from the 100 sets of results using the MI approach. Sensitivity analyses included the worst-case scenario, for which “non-response” was imputed for missing data by assuming that the mechanism of missing data for all patients was missing not at random. Frequency (counts) and percentage for the patients who met the response criteria and exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95% CIs were presented. Sensitivity analyses also included the available case analysis based on all the cases with complete data. Frequency (counts) and percentage for the patients who met the response criteria and exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95% CIs were presented. Sensitivity analyses were also performed using the Full Analysis set (FAS) population, which included patients who received at least one dose of ropeg and participated in at least one post-treatment assessment. Secondary endpoints were evaluated by using available case analyses in most cases. Time-to-event data were summarized using Kaplan–Meier methods.

Role of the funding source

The funder was involved in trial design, data collection, statistical analysis, and manuscript writing.

Results

The study was initially planned to enroll 64 patients, but 91 eligible patients were rapidly enrolled due to high interests from investigators and patients. Between January 17, 2023, and May 15, 2025, c treatment-naïve or HU-treated patients were screened at 28 centers in the USA and Canada with 91 (85.8%) patients treated and 15 (14.2%) screen failures. Among the 91 patients, 72 (79.1%) patients completed the study as shown in trial profile (Supplemental Fig. S1). Baseline demographics and disease characteristics are shown in Table 1. At baseline, participants had mean body mass index of 26.9 kg/m2, median platelet count of 795 × 109 cells/L (range 440–2082), median WBC count of 7.4 × 109 cells/L (range 2.7–19.2). Most patients had a baseline MPNSAF TSS <10 (53.8%), while 22.0% of patients had a TSS ≥ 20. Majority of patients (67/91, 73.6%) were treatment-naïve and 24/91 (26.4%) received HU treatment previously, with a mean (SD) duration of HU use of 25.3 (32.8) months. The spleen was palpable in 7/91 (7.7%) patients, with a mean (SD) longitudinal diameter of 11.9 (5.0) cm; 1 patient had clinically significant splenomegaly (>17 cm). Driver mutation JAK2V617F was present in 35 (38.5%) of 91 participants with mean VAF of 21.9%; CALR in 36 (39.6%) with mean VAF of 26.3%; and MPL in 9 (9.9%) with mean VAF of 16.6%. No patients received prior IFN treatment. A history of prior thrombosis was noted in 15 (16.5%) of the patients. There were 33/91 (36.3%) high-risk patients with ET according to the revised International Prognostic Score of thrombosis in Essential Thrombocythemia (IPSET-t)31 as shown in Table 1.

The mean (SD) and median (range) biweekly dose of ropeg was 344.4 (112.6) μg and 366.9 (98.3–488.6) μg. The rate of durable modified ELN response at both Months 10 and 13 (primary endpoint) was 60.2% (95% CI, 49.0–71.4) (Fig. 1A). Ropeg showed durable modified ELN responses at both Months 10 and 13 across pre-specified subgroups with similar response rates observed within subgroups for sex, baseline platelet count, and country (Fig. 1B). Durable modified ELN response rates were higher for patients <65 years versus patients ≥65 years: 64.0% (95% CI: 50.5%, 77.4%) versus 52.1% (95% CI: 32.5%, 71.7%). Response was observed in 41/71 (57.2%; 95% CI: 44.6%, 69.7%) of Caucasian, 4/7 (53.0%; 95% CI: 12.8%, 93.2%) of Asian, 5/5 (100%; 95% CI: 47.8%, 100.0%) of Black/African American, and 2/3 (66.7%; 95% CI: 9.4%, 99.2%) of Hispanic patients (Fig. 1B). When evaluating individual response components at both Months 10 and 13, 63.9% of patients (95% CI, 53.1%, 74.7%) achieved peripheral blood count remission, defined as platelet counts ≤400 × 109/L and white blood cell counts <10 × 109/L. Improvement or non-progression in disease-related signs, such as splenomegaly, was observed in 92.8% of patients (95% CI: 85.4%, 100.0%) at Months 10 and 13. For the 7/91 (7.7%) patients with a palpable spleen at baseline, all 7 patients had an improvement in disease-related signs at Month 13. In this study, thromboembolic events occurred in 3/91 (3.4%) of patients over the 13 months of treatment. Absence of hemorrhagic or thrombotic events between Months 10 and 13 was documented in 100.0% of patients (95% CI: 96.0%, 100.0%) (Supplemental Table S1). For the high-risk patients defined by IPSET-t, the rate of the modified ELN response at both Months 10 and 13 was 59.0% (Supplemental Table S1).

Only 1/91 (1.1%) experienced disease progression (moderate myelofibrosis) in the study. Using the response criteria including the MPN-SAF TSS component, 28 patients (31.1%) achieved a durable ELN response at Months 10 and 13 (95% CI 19.3%–42.8%).

Further sensitivity analyses showed that the rate of durable modified ELN response at both Months 10 and 13 under worst-case and available case scenarios were 46.2% (42/91; 95% CI: 35.6%, 56.9%) and 63.6% (42/66; 95% CI, 50.9%, 75.1%), respectively (Supplemental Table S1). For the 24 (26.4%) patients who had previous HU use and available data at Months 10 and 13, the rate of the objective modified ELN response at both Months 10 and 13 was 33.4% (8/24; 95% CI: 12.7%, 54.1%). Among 8 (8.8%) patients who had prior HU use and met the R-IPSET-t high-risk ET criteria at baseline, the rate of the durable modified ELN response rate at both Months 10 and 13 was 41.3% (95% CI: 4.8%, 77.7%).

For secondary endpoints, 49.4% (95% CI, 37.7%–59.1%) maintained simultaneous normalization of platelets and WBCs in ≥80% of assessments, with a median time to hematologic response of 8.4 weeks (95% CI, 8.1–11.9). Ropeg treatment led to a mean (SD) decrease of −519.4 (328.4) x 109/L in platelet and −3.5 (2.5) x 109/L in WBC counts, respectively, at Month 13 from baseline (Fig. 2A and B).

Ropeg treatment led to absolute VAF reductions of JAK2V617F, CALR, and MPL in most molecular response-evaluable patients, i.e., having both baseline and post-treatment measurements available, as shown for individual patients in the waterfall plots (Fig. 3A–C). Mean absolute VAF decreased significantly at Month 13 from baseline for both JAK2V617F (−6.0%, P = 0.0057, 95% CI: −9.97%, −1.96%) and CALR (−2.46%, P = 0.0411, 95% CI: −4.8, −0.1). The mean relative reductions of JAK2V617F and CALR VAFs were 20.5% and 12.0%, respectively (Table 2). Although mean absolute MPL VAF had a reduction of −4.0% with a relative change of 20.6% (Table 2), the change was not significant (P = 0.0874) possibly reflecting relatively small sample numbers. The rates of molecular response, i.e., complete molecular response (CMR) and partial molecular response (PMR), as defined by ELN 2009 criteria at Month 13 were 35.0% (10% CMR + 25%PMR) for JAK2V617F, 16.0% (4% CMR + 12% PMR) for CALR, and 25.0% (12.5% CMR + 12.5% PMR) for MPL (Fig. 3D). We next examined non-driver mutations including TET2, ASXL1, TP53, DNMT3A, SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1, IDH1/2, EZH2, SETBP1, KRAS/NRAS, and SH2B3. Among them, TET2, ASXL1, TP53, DNMT3A, EZH2, SETBP1, and KRAS mutations were identified in patients’ blood samples. The most common non-driver mutations at baseline were TET2 in 6 patients and DNMT3A in 5 patients. All other mutations measured positive at baseline were identified in 1 or 2 patients each. One of 5 (20%) TET2 molecular response-evaluable patients had a PMR in TET2 mutations at Months 4, 7, 10, and 13. At baseline, DNMT3A mutations were detected in 5 patients. During the study period, no new treatment-emergent DNMT3A mutations were identified. Among evaluable patients with baseline DNMT3A mutations, no DNMT3A molecular response was observed and its mean VAF slightly increased, although the small sample size limits interpretation. One patient was found to carry mutated TP53 with a baseline VAF of 17.6% and the patient had a CMR at Months 4, 7, and 10 (Table 2). The patient with mutated TP53 also carried CALR mutations with a baseline VAF of 18.3%. The patient did not have a molecular response in CALR after the treatment but had a durable hematologic response without any thrombotic events at both Months 10 and 13 (the patient missed the spleen assessment at Month 10).

Symptom improvement or stabilization was achieved in 78.0% (71/91) of patients at Months 10 and 13 as measured by the 10-item Myeloproliferative Neoplasm Symptom Assessment Form - Total Symptom Score (MPN-SAF TSS). Symptom improvement in MPN-SAF TSS occurred in 43 (47.3%) patients at Month 4, 36 (39.6%) patients at Month 7, 43 (47.3%) patients at Month 10, and 38 (41.8%) patients at Month 13. The majority of patients (between 70.1% and 98.5% of patients) had no problems at Month 13 for all dimensions of the EQ-5D-3L questionnaire. By Month 13, 61–98% of patients reported no problems across EQ-5D-3L quality-of-life dimensions, with the greatest improvements seen in pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression domains.

All patients experienced at least one treatment-emergent AE (TEAE). TEAEs were mostly grades 1 or 2. Table 3 shows grade 1 or 2 TEAEs occurring in ≥10% of patients and any ≥grade 3 TEAEs. The most common events were reversible fatigue (60.4%), alanine aminotransferase increase (58.2%), aspartate aminotransferase increase (58.2%), headache (36.3%), neutrophil count decrease (31.9%), WBC count decrease (30.8%), and anemia (30.8%) (Table 3). The aminotransferase increases were reversible and manageable without clinical signs and symptoms, and did not correlate with bilirubin increases. There was no grade 5 TEAE and one (1.1%) grade 4 TEAE (hepatitis toxic) which was recovered without sequelae. Grade ≥3 TEAEs occurred in 27.5% (25/91) of the participants, most commonly with alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase increases (4.4%; 4/91), neutrophil count decrease (4.4%; 4/91), fatigue (3.3%; 3/91) and lymphocyte count decrease (3.3%; 3/91) (Table 3). Serious AE (SAEs) occurred in 11.0% (10/91) of patients, most frequently pneumonia, which was reported in 2.2%. Treatment-related SAEs occurred in 5.5% (5/91) of patients. Discontinuations due to TEAEs of any cause occurred in 11 (12.1%) of the 91 patients. Ropeg treatment-related discontinuations in 9/91 (9.9%). Sixty-eight (74.7%) patients had a dose reduction, most commonly for fatigue in 19 (20.9%) and lab abnormalities including alanine aminotransferase increase in 18 (19.8%) and neutrophil count decrease in 17 (18.7%). Forty-eight (52.7%) patients had a dose interruption, most commonly due to alanine aminotransferase increase in 10 (11.0%), aspartate aminotransferase increase in 6 (6.6%), neutrophil count decrease in 6 (6.6%), and anemia and leukopenia in 5 (5.5%) patients each. No grade 4 events led to dose reductions. Grade 3 AEs leading to dose reduction occurred in 6 patients (6.6%), including neutrophil count decrease (2/91, 2.2%), alanine aminotransferase increase (1/91, 1.1%), fatigue (1/91, 1.1%) and anemia (1/91, 1.1%). Grade 3 AEs leading to dose interruption occurred in 19 patients (20.9%), and these included lymphocyte count decrease (3/91, 3.3%), and alanine aminotransferase increase (3/91, 3.3%). In this study, AEs of special interests (AESIs) including vision blurry, depression, and thyroid dysfunction were found in 14/91 (15.4%), 10/91 (11.0%), and 9/91 (5.5%), respectively. Most of the AESIs were grade 1 or 2 and resolved. Overall, the safety findings were consistent with the known safety profile of IFN-α therapy in ET and no unexpected safety signals were identified.

Discussion

This prospective multicentre study provides important evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of ropeg in patients with ET treated in North America. The findings demonstrate that ropeg therapy can produce durable hematologic responses together with reductions in molecular disease burden in a heterogeneous population that included both treatment-naïve patients and those previously exposed to hydroxyurea.

The primary endpoint of durable modified ELN response at Months 10 and 13 was achieved in 60.2% of treated patients. This response rate indicates that ropeg can produce sustained control of peripheral blood counts and clinical disease manifestations over a prolonged treatment period. Importantly, hematologic responses occurred relatively rapidly, with a median time to response of just over eight weeks. These findings suggest that accelerated titration of ropeg can achieve clinically meaningful cytoreduction within a relatively short time frame. Another important finding of the study is the demonstration of molecular responses across all three major driver mutations associated with ET. Our results provide solid evidence that ropeg treatment leads to deep molecular response across JAK2V617F, CALR and MPL driver mutations. Ropeg was well-tolerated overall, with no new safety signals identified. The results of the study, together with the recently completed phase 3 SURPASS-ET study in patients with HU-resistance/intolerance and leukocytosis,15 indicate that ropeg is an effective and tolerable therapy with a positive benefit-risk profile for both treatment-naïve and HU-treated patients with ET. The results offer practice-changing evidence supporting the use of ropeg for North American patients with ET regardless of previous treatment or driver mutation.

Reductions in VAFs for JAK2V617F and CALR mutations were statistically significant and were accompanied by measurable molecular response rates according to established ELN criteria. These results suggest that ropeg therapy influences the underlying clonal biology of ET in addition to controlling peripheral blood counts. The observation of molecular responses across multiple driver mutation subtypes is particularly notable given the biological heterogeneity of ET. JAK2V617F, CALR, and MPL mutations are associated with distinct clinical phenotypes and risks of disease complications. Demonstrating activity across these mutation subtypes supports the concept that interferon-based therapies exert broader antineoplastic effects in MPNs. Although molecular responses were frequently observed alongside hematologic responses, we observed that they were not fully concordant in all patients. For example, some patients achieved durable hematologic response without meeting molecular response criteria. Furthermore, a 25% molecular response rate with a 12.5% CMR rate in MPL-mutated ET was observed in this study. To our knowledge, our study provides the largest molecular response data in MPL-mutated ET. Importantly, we observed evidence of molecular responses even in patients harboring high molecular risk (HMR) mutations such as TP53, underscoring the broad therapeutic activity of ropeg in diverse mutational backgrounds. Together, these findings highlight the potential of ropeg to positively alter the disease biology of ET despite its mutational heterogeneity.

Of note, the patient harboring a TP53 mutation demonstrated a complete molecular response of TP53 while the CALR mutation persisted. This raises the possibility that TP53 may have represented a cooperating mutation within the dominant ET clone, or a separate clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP)-related clone independent of the main disease clone. Because only a single case was observed and no clonal architecture analyses were performed, we cannot definitively distinguish these possibilities.

In this study, thromboembolic and disease progression events were rare, underscoring the favorable safety and disease control potential of ropeg. These findings are consistent with SURPASS-ET, in which treatment with ropeg under the HIDAT regimen was associated with very low rates of major thrombotic complications and no cases of disease progression. The concordance between the present results and those of SURPASS-ET suggests that sustained clinical/hematologic and molecular control achieved with ropeg may translate into meaningful clinical improvements including reduction of vascular complications, which are major contributors to morbidity and mortality in ET. Collectively, these data support the notion that ropeg not only normalizes blood counts but can also modify the underlying disease biology regardless of driver mutation status, and may therefore reduce the long-term risk of thrombosis and disease progression in ET.

SURPASS-ET, having demonstrated efficacy and safety of ropeg in HU-resistant or -intolerant patients with ET, was predominantly conducted in Asian populations. EXCEED-ET specifically enrolled patients with ET in the US and Canada with 78.0% (71/92) being Caucasians and 7.7% (7/91) being of Asian origin. The durable modified ELN response rates were very similar between the two subgroups of the study: 57.2% versus 53.0%. Therefore, the beneficial effects of ropeg do not appear to be influenced by pharmacogenomic differences, suggesting the data from SURPASS-ET and EXCEED-ET are representative of a broad patient population with ET. Clinical outcomes observed in this study are broadly consistent with those reported in the phase 3 SURPASS-ET trial. The magnitude of hematologic responses observed in the present study was similar to that of SURPASS-ET. These findings suggest that the therapeutic activity of ropeg is not limited to specific geographic or ethnic populations and may be generalizable to a broad ET population. It should also be mentioned that the response criteria in the SURPASS-ET used a modified ELN definition including symptoms, whereas EXCEED-ET excluded symptoms from the composite endpoint. In addition, while SURPASS-ET applied a worst-case imputation, EXCEED-ET used multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption, with sensitivity analyses including worst-case and available-case approaches.

In this study, the dosing regimen (HIDAT) was chosen based on the prior experience of SURPASS-ET and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analyses in patients with PV indicating that rapid attainment of therapeutic exposure led to deeper molecular responses and rapid achievement of CHRs.15,26 Ropeg was well tolerated in SURPASS-ET and other studies in patients with PV.15,32 In this study, the most frequent AEs were reversible and manageable laboratory abnormalities. SAEs were uncommon, and no patient deaths occurred. Psychiatric disorders and thyroid dysfunction, areas of particular concern with IFN therapy, were infrequent and manageable. Dose adjustments were primarily due to laboratory abnormalities or fatigue and were manageable without permanent discontinuation in most patients.

The study has some limitations, mainly due to its single-arm, open-label design which may introduce bias in patient enrolment or response assessment. Nonetheless, EXCEED-ET provided data from one of the largest cohorts of patients with ET, demonstrating efficacy and safety of ropeg, consistent with results from the randomized, controlled SURPASS-ET study. The study included both treatment-naïve and HU-treated patients with ET, representative of the real-world disease spectrum, which supports the generalizability of the findings to typical clinical settings. Serial molecular and clinical assessments over 13 months provide a clear relationship between treatment and biological response, strengthening the causal interpretation even without a control arm. The safety findings are consistent with the known pharmacology of ropeg and with prior IFN experience, reducing the likelihood that unrecognized biases meaningfully affect safety conclusions.

In summary, ropeg achieved rapid and sustained disease control and robust molecular improvements in patients with ET, with a manageable safety profile. Major thrombotic events and disease progression were rare. This study, together with SURPASS-ET, supports the use of ropeg as a rational and effective antineoplastic therapy for both treatment-naïve and HU-treated patients with ET. The ongoing extension phase will provide critical information including long-term molecular response rates, the relationship of deeper molecular responses and disease progression, survival, as well as long-term safety.
