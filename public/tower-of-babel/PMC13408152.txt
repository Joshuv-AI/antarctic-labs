Distinguishing intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects by polarization reversal measurements
Authors: Huiyu Dan, Weiqi Qian, Chong Guo, Wang Zhang, Huiqi Zhao, Chris R Bowen, Ya Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPyroelectric devices hold promise for thermal energy harvesting and sensing. While previous studies have focused on the intrinsic pyroelectric response, they neglect the interfacial pyroelectric effect at the electrode-material interface, leading to an overestimation of the intrinsic contribution to pyroelectricity. By leveraging the reversibility of the intrinsic effect and the irreversibility of the interfacial effect, we propose a method that uses polarization reversal measurements to distinguish between them. By calculating the contributions of the two effects for different ferroelectric films, it is observed that the intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects dominate in PbTiO3 and BaTiO3 films with Schottky contacts, respectively, modifying the energy Figure of Merit. Furthermore, a machine learning model is employed to predict the proportion of intrinsic and interfacial effects, offering strategies for material screening. This work provides a rapid optimization approach and guidelines for high-performance pyroelectric devices based on ferroelectric films.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Ferroelectrics and multiferroics

Introduction

Pyroelectric devices exhibit competitiveness comparable to thermoelectric devices for low-grade thermal energy harvesting applications1–3, while also being of value for thermal sensing and imaging. A key difference between these two mechanisms is that the pyroelectric effect depends on temporal temperature changes, whereas the thermoelectric effect depends on spatial temperature gradients. Studies to date have primarily focused on intrinsic pyroelectric effects4–6, where a change in temperature leads to the release of surface charge due to a change in polarization of the material7,8. Recently, the discovery of the interfacial pyroelectric effect has provided a new perspective for research related to pyroelectric devices9,10. In contrast to traditional intrinsic pyroelectric effects, the interfacial pyroelectric effect originates from the temperature dependence of dielectric permittivity, effective dopant density, and the built-in potential within a Schottky barrier at an interface10,11. Ferroelectric pyroelectric materials inherently develop defects such as oxygen vacancies, resulting in weakly semiconducting behavior that facilitates Schottky junction formation at electrodes12–15. However, previous work has overlooked the contribution of this Schottky interfacial pyroelectric effect in pyroelectric devices, leading to an overestimation of the intrinsic pyroelectric effect of materials16–18.

Both intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects are able to coexist in ferroelectric thin-film devices, yet the conventional equation for the pyroelectric energy Figure of Merit (FoM) only accounts for the intrinsic effect, leading to inaccurate results19,20. This is a result of an interfacial pyroelectric effect that originates exclusively within the depletion layer beneath the electrodes, rather than throughout the bulk ferroelectric material9. The pyroelectric energy FoM serves as a crucial reference standard for assessing the thermal energy harvesting capability of ferroelectric devices1,21–23. As a result, to determine the FoM accurately, the contribution and proportions of the intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects need to be determined. There is therefore a need for a method that is able to distinguish between the contributions of both the intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects to the overall response. Materials with a high intrinsic proportion, or contribution, to the overall pyroelectric response are suitable for the Olsen cycle, while those with a high interfacial proportion to the overall response are well-suited for high-temperature conditions8,24,25. In this regard, there is a need to develop an approach that can determine the contribution and proportion from previously reported materials, without undertaking new experiments, since this will enable researchers to control the contribution related to interfacial pyroelectricity, optimize electrode configurations, and screen potential materials of interest. However, there is currently no method for distinguishing the contributions of intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects.

We propose here a method to distinguish between the intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects and determine their relative proportions to the overall pyroelectric response of ferroelectric pyroelectric thin films. In total, 11 types of ferroelectric thin film materials were fabricated using the sol-gel method, where lithium niobate (LNO) and indium tin oxide (ITO) were uniformly used as electrodes. By leveraging the reversibility of the intrinsic effect and the irreversibility of interfacial pyroelectric polarization, we are able to quantify their respective contribution and resulting proportions by determining the pyroelectric coefficients of structures with opposing poled states. The results reveal that the intrinsic pyroelectric effect is dominant in some structures and material combinations, such as ITO/PbTiO3(PTO)/LaNiO3(LNO), while the interfacial pyroelectric effect dominates in other structures, such as ITO/BaTiO3(BTO)/LNO. The detailed temperature dependence of the remanent polarization intensity, dielectric permittivity, effective dopant density, and built-in potential is material specific. An extension of the energy FoM that accounts for the proportions of intrinsic and interfacial contributions has been developed, enhancing the fidelity of the calculation of the FoM. A machine learning model is also established to predict the proportions of the intrinsic and interfacial contributions using only three input parameters, namely the polarization (Pr), permittivity (χ3), and barrier height (Vbi). Using this model, the intrinsic and interfacial proportions of 294 types of ferroelectric thin films reported in the existing literature were predicted. Statistical analysis of results reveal that the selection of optimal electrodes can enhance both the interfacial proportion and the magnitude of the pyroelectric coefficient. This study provides practical guidance for the design and fabrication of high-performance ferroelectric pyroelectric thin film devices.

Results

Conception and distinguishing between intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects

The intrinsic pyroelectric effect and interfacial pyroelectric effect originate from fundamentally different mechanisms that operate in distinct spatial regions9. The intrinsic pyroelectric effect arises from the macroscopic polarization, Pr, of ferroelectric thin films, which forms opposing screening charges at the electrodes, analogous to a parallel-plate capacitor (Fig. 1a)26,27. Temperature variations (ΔT) alter the level of polarization of the material, Pr, thereby driving electron flow and generating a measurable charge ΔQ1, and developing a voltage ΔV1 across the capacitor at open circuit, where the electrode spacing equals the full ferroelectric layer thickness, ensuring that measured values reflect true material responses. In contrast, the interfacial pyroelectric effect originates from the depletion layer beneath the electrodes. Here, the ΔT modifies the Schottky barrier height, producing a charge ΔQ2 and a voltage ΔV2 across the interfacial capacitor. However, in the case of the actual (combined intrinsic and interfacial) pyroelectric response, the experimentally measured voltage ΔV’2 (for the same ΔQ2) diverges from ΔV2 due to the increased effective electrode spacing acting to reduce the device capacitance (Fig. 1b). Practical measurements on ferroelectric thin films typically involve the coexistence of both intrinsic (ΔQ’1, ΔV’1) and interfacial (ΔQ’2, ΔV’2) contributions, yielding composite outputs (ΔQ3, ΔV3) that satisfy ΔQ3 = ΔQ’1 + ΔQ’2; however, they exhibit a non-linear voltage summation due to capacitive coupling. Furthermore, the conventional formulas for calculating the pyroelectric energy FoM in ferroelectric thin-film devices fail to account for interfacial effects19. A comparison between the ‘traditional’ pyroelectric energy FoM and our extended FoM equation in Fig. 1b shows that the traditional energy FoM only accounts for intrinsic pyroelectric effects, whereas the extended equation also incorporates the contribution associated with interfacial pyroelectric effects. The calculated energy values derived from the E and E’ (defined in Fig. 1b) stored on the capacitor were used to verify the energy harvesting efficiency. Detailed equations and derivation processes are provided in the Supplementary Note 1. The intrinsic pyroelectric contribution enables a larger Olsen cycle efficiency, while the interfacial pyroelectric contribution can overcome the thermal operational limitation imposed by the Curie temperature (Fig. 1g). We established a machine learning model that predicts the relative contributions of both the intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects based solely on three input parameters: polarization (Pr), permittivity (χ3), and barrier height (Vbi). Through statistical analysis of a large set of ferroelectric thin-film materials from a wide range of publications, we propose a rapid optimization approach that provides guidelines for pyroelectric device design.

Methods for distinguishing intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric contributions

Size effects significantly influence the pyroelectric performance of ferroelectric thin films28,29. When employing identical electrode configurations and comparable thicknesses, experimental artifacts arising from significant dimensional discrepancies can therefore be excluded in the results (Fig. 2a, b). All ferroelectric thin film materials used in this article share identical electrode configurations and similar thicknesses (Supplementary Fig. 1). All specimens feature identical electrode configurations with statistically comparable thicknesses, centered at ~100 nm. We investigated the pyroelectric properties of PTO and BTO with a different number of layers, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. With an increase in the thickness of the film and more layers, there is a decrease in film quality since the films were annealed layer by layer during the preparation process. A higher number of layers implies a longer annealing time, and this may account for the degradation of performance with increasing thin film thickness and a larger number of layers. For bulk materials, we investigated the pyroelectric properties of BTO ceramics and PMN-PT single crystals, as illustrated in the Supplementary Fig. 3. Due to the excessively high electrical resistance of bulk materials, we were unable to measure their current-voltage (I-V) curves to verify the presence of Schottky junctions within them. Unlike the thin film materials, the pyroelectric coefficients of bulk materials are all zero in their initial unpoled states. By measuring the pyroelectric coefficients when subject to both positive and negative polarization states, we found that the two values were approximately equal. Therefore, we infer that the interfacial pyroelectric effect is extremely weak in bulk ferroelectric materials. The XRD patterns of eleven materials confirm that all samples adopt a perovskite structure with discernible preferred orientation (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). The frequency dependence of dielectric constant and dielectric loss for 11 ferroelectric thin films shows that both parameters exhibit significant frequency dispersion, which is a typical behavior of ferroelectric materials (Supplementary Fig. 6).

During periodic pyroelectric current measurements, a 980 nm wavelength laser was employed as the thermal excitation source. Given that all materials exhibit bandgaps exceeding 3.5 eV, photoconductive contributions are effectively precluded (Supplementary Figs. 7, 8). In the initial state, the PTO ferroelectric generates a negative pyroelectric current upon heating, and this current further increases in the −5.5 V poled state (Fig. 2c). In the +5.5 V poled state, the pyroelectric current produced by PTO reverses polarity and exhibits a greater magnitude compared to that observed when subject to a −5.5 V poled state. In contrast, ferroelectric BTO generates a positive pyroelectric current upon heating in the initial state. The magnitude of the current produced by BTO shows only a marginal variation between the −7 V poled and +7 V poled states (Fig. 2d). When the poling voltage reaches 8 V, the ITO/BTO/LNO is subjected to a degree of dielectric breakdown, which is accompanied by a decrease in the pyroelectric current (Supplementary Fig. 9). These comparative results demonstrate fundamental discrepancies in the polarization-dependent pyroelectric responses between PTO and BTO. It should be noted that the applied voltage here corresponds to the pre-breakdown maximum voltage, which significantly exceeds the coercive voltage, thus ensuring a saturated polarization of the thin film. The current-voltage (I-V) and capacitance-voltage (C-V) characteristics of 11 ferroelectric films confirm the unaltered current direction for Schottky-based interfacial pyroelectric contributions (Supplementary Figs. 10, 11). The current response time between intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects demonstrates closely matched temporal characteristics, revealing concurrent triggering of both mechanisms at the nanoscale regime (Supplementary Fig. 12). As the temperature of the PTO film changes, its polarization intensity, dielectric permittivity χ3, dopant density Nd and apparent built-in potential Vbi,app will also vary, thereby influencing both the intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects. The light intensity affects the current transient, as illustrated in the Supplementary Fig. 13. An analysis of the parameters affecting the current-time constant can be conducted from three aspects: (i) the physical essence of the pyroelectric effect, (ii) the dynamic competition between heat conduction and heat dissipation, and (iii) the influence of the temperature gradient, as illustrated in Supplementary Materials Note 4. The current-temperature responses under bidirectional poled regimes unequivocally exhibit characteristic pyroelectric signatures across all specimens, precluding alternative mechanisms such as thermoelectric effects or structural phase transitions (Supplementary Fig. 14).

In this study, for systems where both the intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects coexist, the pyroelectric coefficient is calculated using the pyroelectric current integration method (Fig. 2e), which is a classical approach for characterizing the macroscopic pyroelectric response. This method directly determines the equivalent pyroelectric coefficient of a thin film by measuring the macroscopic polarization charge flow that is induced by a known temperature change. Its advantage lies in the ability to intuitively reflect the actual pyroelectric response at the device level. Using Eq. 1, the relative contributions of the intrinsic and interfacial effects to the overall pyroelectric response were quantified for each material (Fig. 2f). The quality of the thin film affects both the intrinsic parameters (such as Pr and Ec) and extrinsic parameters (such as defect density and the built-in potential Vbi,app of a junction) simultaneously, thereby making a further impact on both the intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric contribution to the total pyroelectric response.

Mechanism and energy Figure of Merit (FoM)

The intrinsic pyroelectric effect is governed by the remnant polarization (Pr), whereas the interface-induced pyroelectricity depends on the interfacial barrier height (Vbi,app). Lead-based materials typically exhibit a higher remanent polarization, while barium-based materials generally possess larger variations in built-in potential Vbi,app. The remnant polarization, Pr, of ITO/PTO/LNO is significantly larger than that of ITO/BTO/LNO, and it decreases with an increase in temperature (Fig. 3a). In contrast, the remnant polarization, Pr, of ITO/BTO/LNO remains nearly unchanged as the temperature rises. Furthermore, the variation in built-in potential Vbi of ITO/BTO/LNO is much larger than that of ITO/PTO/LNO (Fig.3b). As a result, the pyroelectric effect of barium-based materials is primarily derived from interfacial pyroelectric effects, whereas the pyroelectric response of lead-based materials is dominated by intrinsic pyroelectric contributions. Local PFM phase measurement and switching spectroscopy measurements further confirm the existence of ferroelectricity in the BTO films, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16. We also measured polarization-voltage (P-V) loops at a range of temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 17), and the results show that its remanent polarization decreases with increasing temperature for the ITO/PTO/LNO structure. For the ITO/BTO/LNO structure, the trend of the change in remanent polarization with temperature is not obvious; instead, it shows a slight increase in polarization as the temperature rises. This may be attributed to the increased leakage current at elevated temperatures. The P-V loops exhibit an overall positive shift, showing a distinct imprint effect. Mechanical stress is an important factor contributing to the imprint effect, whose origin lies in the lattice mismatch between the thin film and the electrode or the substrate clamping effect30,31. The current-voltage (I-V) curves at different temperatures demonstrate that the Schottky barrier height varies with temperature, thereby driving carrier flow in the external circuit (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Due to the high volatility of lead, PTO typically exhibits weakly p-type semiconducting behavior32. In contrast, BTO demonstrates weak n-type characteristics due to its propensity for oxygen vacancy formation33. The electron work function of LNO, BTO, PTO, and ITO are 4.9 eV, 4.3 eV, 5.1 eV, and 4.4 eV, respectively. At the ITO/PTO interface, hole injection into ITO induces downward band bending, forming a p-type anti-blocking layer. In contrast, the LNO/PTO interface exhibits a minimal work function difference (ΔΦ <0.2 eV), and is represented by an Ohmic contact in the schematics for enhanced clarity (Fig. 3c). At the BTO/LNO interface, electron injection into LNO induces upward band bending, forming an n-type Schottky barrier layer (Fig. 3d). PTO exhibits a high remnant polarization with minimal barrier modulation, where intrinsic charges dominate compared to interfacial charges. In the -7 V poled states: intrinsic and interfacial currents flow oppositely, inducing compensatory cancellation, while in the +7 V poled state they flow in the same direction. Previous work has observed the asymmetry in pyroelectric coefficients between positive and negative poled, which they attributed to domain pinning effects34,35. The initial polarization direction is consistent with the −5.5 V state of poling. If a pinning effect exists, the pyroelectric coefficient obtained by polarization in the +5.5 V poled state should be smaller than that at −5.5 V poled state. However, the results show that the pyroelectric coefficient in the +5.5 V poled state is significantly larger than the −5.5 V poled state. Moreover, as the coercive voltage derived from the hysteresis loops (Supplementary Fig. 15) is less than 5.5 V, the remnant polarizations for the −5.5 V and +5.5 V poled states are comparable. This is one reason why we attribute the enhancement of the pyroelectric coefficient in the +5.5 V poled state to interfacial pyroelectric effects. Conversely, BTO features a low level of polarization, but a strong barrier modulation with interfacial charge prevalence. Although similar carrier cancellation occurs after negative poling, synergistic transport in the positive poling state continues to lead to a dominant forward current.

The energy FoM values from E, E’ (defined in Fig. 1b) and experimentally measured values across eleven material systems demonstrate consistently closer alignment of the E’ with empirical data, which validates its superior fidelity (Fig. 1e). The largest overestimation of energy FoM values is observed in BNT, reaching a factor of 3.6, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 19. This overestimation factor may be related to the relatively low dielectric constant of BNT, and in the formula for the energy FoM (Supplementary Materials Note 1) a smaller dielectric constant lead to a larger corresponding energy FoM. The computational accuracy enhancement magnitude of E’, relative to E, reveals substantial precision improvements of 19-79% which represents a significant methodological advancement (Fig. 3f). The systematic comparison of energy FoM and energy conversion efficiencies (η) calculated via E, E’, and experimental measurements, demonstrating significantly closer congruence between the revised methodology’s predictions and empirical values that validates its enhanced predictive capability (Supplementary Fig. 20).

Modeling and application

Machine learning models have been effectively applied and promoted in a variety of fields36,37. Although the extensive literature today has demonstrated the modulation of the photoelectric effect by the Schottky barrier, its modulation of the pyroelectric effect has rarely been reported38. We assess five key parameters across eleven distinct ferroelectric thin films: Pr, εr, and Vbi, and the proportional contributions of intrinsic versus interfacial pyroelectric components (Fig. 4a). For each material, data from six independent channels were acquired, yielding a total of 66 channels. These datasets were partitioned into three subsets: 70% for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing. We focus on the parameters (Pr, εr, and Vbi,app) of the fabricated device rather than the fabrication process, as the fabrication process is ultimately reflected in the device properties. With systematically optimized model hyperparameters, the performance metrics are demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 21. Three parameters from 294 literature-sourced materials -Pr, εr, and Vbi were input into the model to predict the proportional contributions of intrinsic versus interfacial pyroelectricity. Subsequent statistical analysis enabled electrode pairing recommendations for optimal performance in each material system.

It has been demonstrated in a number of articles that an asymmetric electrode configuration increases the Schottky barrier height27. The 294 materials were classified into two primary categories: symmetric and asymmetric electrode configurations (Fig. 4b, c). Materials with asymmetric electrodes consistently exhibit higher pyroelectric coefficients and greater interfacial contribution proportions27,39. Further subdivision yielded four material subcategories, each containing both symmetric and asymmetric electrode types. Statistical analysis confirmed that within every subcategory, asymmetric electrode configurations maintained higher pyroelectric coefficients and elevated interfacial contributions, demonstrating consistent behavior across all classifications.

An enhanced remnant polarization facilitates greater intrinsic contributions, while a larger work function, ΔΦ, between the top electrodes and ferroelectric layers promotes an increased interfacial component. A correlation analysis of Pr, ΔΦ between the top electrodes and ferroelectric layers, and the pyroelectric coefficients across all PTO-based material systems, as shown in Fig. 4d. All materials with pyroelectric coefficients exceeding 1200 μC·m⁻²·K⁻¹ exclusively feature asymmetric electrode configurations. Neither an enhanced remnant polarization nor a larger ΔΦ between the top and bottom electrodes demonstrates direct proportionality to the pyroelectric coefficients. Phenomenologically, intermediate ΔΦ values exhibit optimal performance, maximizing the pyroelectric response.

We analysed the statistical relationships between the polarization Pr, pyroelectric coefficients p, the proportional contributions of interfacial pyroelectricity, and the ΔΦ between the top electrodes and ferroelectric layers in PTO-based systems (Fig. 4e). The underlying mechanism that governs how ΔΦ affects the pyroelectric coefficients remains unclear. We investigated the effect of a range of top electrodes on the pyroelectric coefficient, as illustrated in the Supplementary Fig. 22. As the work function difference between the upper electrode and lower electrode decreases from ~0.6 eV to ~ 0 eV, the pyroelectric performance decreases accordingly. We postulate that a Fermi-level misalignment-induced contact potential may constitute a governing factor. In nanoscale metal/ferroelectric/metal heterostructures, Fermi-level alignment during the formation of a contact generates interfacial electric fields that establish thermal equilibrium by counteracting further electron flow. While the potential impact of these fields on intrinsic polarization vectors remains unresolved, empirical statistical analysis of extensive literature data reveals discernible correlations between Fermi-level-alignment-induced contact potentials and modulated pyroelectric coefficients. The temperature-dependent variation in this contact potential constitutes the fundamental operating principle of thermocouples. Furthermore, Schottky junctions formed between ferroelectric thin films and metals are mediated by defects such as oxygen vacancies. Within a certain defect concentration range, elevated oxygen vacancy densities enhance interfacial effects. Paradoxically, excessive vacancies can compromise ferroelectric properties, in particular the remnant polarization, thereby reducing the intrinsic pyroelectric contribution. Consequently, interfacial contribution proportions exhibit non-monotonic optimization, whereby a peak pyroelectric performance emerges within an intermediate range where synergistic coupling between intrinsic and interfacial mechanisms is maximized, rather than at maximal interfacial dominance. Material properties are governed by multiple factors, including crystal growth orientation, grain size, and defect concentration. To enhance the pyroelectric coefficient, we propose a dual-strategy approach: (i) selecting optimal electrode materials as substrates to achieve superior thin-film crystallinity and implement precise dopant concentration control, while preserving a high remnant polarization for intrinsic pyroelectric contributions. (ii) after thin-film deposition, selecting upper electrode materials with a large work function difference of 0.4 - 0.6 eV to establish Schottky junctions, thereby enhancing interfacial pyroelectric effects.

Discussion

We have proposed a method to distinguish between the contributions and proportions of intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric effects in ferroelectric thin films. By leveraging the reversibility of the intrinsic pyroelectric effect and the irreversibility of the interfacial pyroelectric effect, we obtained the pyroelectric coefficients when subject to opposing poled states, and determined the respective intrinsic and interfacial proportions to the overall pyroelectric response using the pyroelectric coefficients in these two states. An extension of the energy figure of merit that accounts for the proportions of intrinsic and interfacial contributions has been developed, augmenting the fidelity of its calculation. A machine learning model is established to predict these contributions using only three input parameters, namely Pr, εr, and Vbi. Statistical analysis of a substantial volume of ferroelectric thin film literature reveals that the optimal selection of electrodes enhances both the interfacial pyroelectric proportion and the overall magnitude of the pyroelectric coefficient. Our findings provide a method to distinguish the intrinsic and interfacial pyroelectric proportions in ferroelectric thin films and improve the accuracy of the thermal energy harvesting figure of merit, thereby offering a rapid approach for material screening and electrode optimization. This research holds promise for the development of interfacial pyroelectric materials and provides guidance for the preparation and optimization of ferroelectric pyroelectric thin film materials for thermal harvesting and sensing.

Methods

Preparation of precursor solutions

All analytical-grade chemical reagents were procured from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The LaNiO3 (LNO) precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 1.4945 g of Ni (CH3COO)2·4H2O and 2.598 g of La (NO3)3·6H2O in 12 mL of acetic acid and 30 mL of ethanol at 353 K, followed by stirring for 12 h. The preparation of ferroelectric film precursor solutions is described in Supplementary Note 5.

Fabrication of indium tin oxide (ITO)/FE film/LNO/mica device

The mica substrate was placed in a homogenizer, and a suitable quantity of lithium niobate (LNO) solution was carefully dripped onto the mica surface using a rubber-tipped dropper. The wet film of ferroelectric (FE) material was obtained by spin coating at 4000 rpm for 30 seconds. Subsequently, the wet film was heated on a hot plate at 180 °C, 400 °C, and 700 °C for 5 minutes. It is essential to note that BTO-based materials require a 5-minute annealing process at 750 °C. This process was repeated six times to achieve a transparent solid LNO film. Following the same procedure, the LNO/FE film was prepared by sequentially dropwise adding the FE solution. Finally, a circular indium tin oxide (ITO) electrode (2 mm diameter) was deposited onto the BTO/LNO/mica sample using RF magnetron sputtering at 150 W for 20 minutes.

Characterization and Measurements

The morphology of the FE film was examined using a Hitachi SUS8020 scanning electron microscope. A low noise laser (CNI, MXL-III-X) with a wavelength of 980 nm served as the light source. The light power density was determined using a calibrated power meter (OPHIR Starlite). The device’s output electric signals were captured using a Keithley 6514 Source Meter. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using a Philips Panalytical X’pert3 instrument with Cu Kα radiation. Ferroelectric hysteresis loops were characterized using a ferroelectric materials test system (RTI-Multi-Ferroic). Ferroelectric domain visualization was carried out using a piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) technique with an MFP-3D-SA instrument. The capacitance-voltage (C-V) data were measured by the Chroma 11022 LCR meter. Elemental analysis via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, ESCALAB 250Xi, USA). The built-in potential Vbi,app and effective space charge density Neff estimated from I-V and C-V measurements via Deep Level Transient (Texas Instruments, FT1230, USA).
