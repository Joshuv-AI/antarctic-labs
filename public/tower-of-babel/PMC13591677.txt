Metabolic phenotype stratification identifies patients at high risk of poor glycemic control in type 2 diabetes mellitus: Insights into hepatic metabolic dysfunction
Authors: Wenxuan Qi, Wenhua Zhao, Li Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo characterize metabolic dphenotypes based on integrated hepatic and metabolic indicators and evaluate their association with poor glycemic control in patients with Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsA total of 320 patients with T2DM were retrospectively enrolled. Data on liver function, blood lipids, insulin resistance, and indicators related to fatty liver were collected. Unsupervised clustering analysis was performed to construct integrated metabolic risk profiles, and glycemic control levels were compared among different groups. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between hepatic metabolic indicators and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent risk factors for poorly controlled blood glucose. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF < 5 considered indicative of acceptable collinearity. Predictive performance of the models was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsTwo metabolic risk profiles were identified, characterized by differences in hepatic enzyme levels, lipid metabolism, and insulin resistance. Patients in the high hepatic metabolic burden group (Cluster 2) exhibited significantly higher HbA1c levels and a greater incidence of poor glycemic control. HbA1c was positively correlated with alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), severity of fatty liver, and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), but negatively correlated with high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Multivariate analysis revealed that longer diabetes duration, elevated AST and TG levels, higher HOMA-IR, and metabolic phenotypes classification (Cluster 2) were independent risk factors for poorly controlled blood glucose. The combined predictive model achieved an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.943.ConclusionMetabolic phenotype stratification based on integrated hepatic and metabolic indicators may facilitate identification of T2DM patients at increased risk of poor glycemic control and provide additional information for metabolic risk assessment.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is one of the most common metabolic disorders worldwide, with a continuously rising prevalence, posing a significant threat to public health [1]. Despite the increasing availability of glucose-lowering therapies in recent years, a substantial proportion of patients with T2DM still fail to achieve optimal glycemic control in clinical practice. Poor glycemic control significantly increases the risk of microvascular and macrovascular complications; it is closely associated with elevated cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality [2, 3]. Therefore, identifying high-risk populations for poor glycemic control and understanding their underlying metabolic basis are of critical clinical importance for optimizing individualized management strategies.

Previous studies have indicated that insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and obesity are important factors affecting glycemic control in T2DM. Among these, the liver, as a central organ of glucose and lipid metabolism, plays a key role in the development of insulin resistance and the regulation of glucose homeostasis [4]. Increasing evidence suggests that hepatic metabolic abnormalities, such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and elevated liver enzymes, are closely associated with the onset and progression of T2DM and may contribute to the pathophysiology of poor glycemic control [5, 6]. However, most existing studies have focused on individual hepatic metabolic indicators, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or liver enzyme abnormalities, or MASLD severity, and have generally evaluated their associations with glycemic control separately. While these studies have provided important insights, they may not fully capture the complex interplay among hepatic function, lipid metabolism, insulin resistance, and hepatic steatosis that collectively contribute to glycemic dysregulation.

Recently, multidimensional metabolic risk stratification based on integrated indicators has emerged as an important approach in metabolic disease research. Data-driven methods, such as unsupervised clustering, enable the identification of patient subgroups with similar metabolic profiles without relying on predefined assumptions, thereby uncovering latent heterogeneity that may be overlooked by conventional classification strategies [7, 8]. Although previous studies have demonstrated associations between individual hepatic metabolic indicators and glycemic control, most have focused on isolated markers rather than integrated metabolic characteristics. Consequently, although individual hepatic and metabolic indicators have been widely investigated, the potential value of integrating hepatic function, lipid metabolism, insulin resistance, and hepatic steatosis into a unified metabolic phenotype framework for risk stratification remains insufficiently explored, particularly in East Asian patients with T2DM [9, 10]. Furthermore, the predictive significance of different metabolic phenotypes for poor glycemic control has not been systematically evaluated, limiting their translation into clinical risk assessment and personalized management.

Against this background, it is necessary to re-examine the role of hepatic metabolic dysfunction in poor glycemic control in T2DM from the perspective of overall metabolic risk features. In this study, we retrospectively integrated multidimensional metabolic indicators, including liver function, lipid metabolism, insulin resistance, and fatty liver, and applied unsupervised clustering analysis to construct liver-related metabolic risk profiles. We systematically compared glycemic control levels among different metabolic profiles. Furthermore, through correlation analysis, multivariate logistic regression, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve assessment, we explored the independent impact and predictive value of hepatic metabolic abnormalities on the risk of poorly controlled blood glucose. This study aims to elucidate whether data-driven metabolic phenotype stratification based on integrated hepatic and metabolic characteristics can improve risk identification for poor glycemic control beyond conventional single-indicator assessment, thereby providing additional evidence for individualized risk stratification and management in patients with T2DM.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was a retrospective observational study. A total of 320 patients with previously diagnosed T2DM who received treatment and completed relevant examinations at our hospital between December 2023 and December 2025 were consecutively enrolled. All study data were obtained from the hospital electronic medical record system and previous laboratory and imaging records, including demographic information, clinical history, laboratory test results, and imaging assessments.

Inclusion criteria were: Definite diagnosis of T2DM; Completion of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), liver function tests, lipid metabolism assessment, insulin resistance-related indicators, and fatty liver evaluation; Complete clinical data.

Exclusion criteria were: Concomitant severe acute infection, malignant tumor, or end-stage organ failure; Presence of other diseases known to significantly affect liver function; Missing key clinical or laboratory data.

Collection of clinical data and laboratory indicators

Demographic information, including age and sex, was collected for all participants. Lifestyle-related factors, including smoking history and alcohol consumption, were recorded. Past medical history, including duration of diabetes, history of hypertension, and coronary heart disease, was also documented.

Laboratory tests included: (1) Liver function indicators: ALT, aspartate aminotransferase (AST); (2) Lipid profile indicators: triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C); (3) Renal function indicators: serum creatinine (Scr) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR); (4) Pancreatic β-cell function and insulin resistance indicators: fasting and postprandial C-peptide levels. Insulin resistance was evaluated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR).

Fatty liver assessment

Fatty liver severity was assessed using abdominal ultrasonography. Hepatic steatosis was classified into four grades (0–3) as follows: 0, no fatty liver; 1, mild; 2, moderate; and 3, severe, based on liver echogenicity, hepatorenal contrast, and the visibility of intrahepatic vessels and diaphragm, according to established criteria [11].

Unsupervised clustering of multidimensional metabolic indicators and definition of metabolic phenotypes

Unsupervised clustering analysis was performed based on multidimensional metabolic indicators, including body mass index (BMI), liver function indicators (ALT and AST), lipid metabolism parameters (TG, LDL, HDL), insulin resistance index (HOMA-IR), and fatty liver severity, to construct comprehensive metabolic risk profiles. Prior to clustering, all continuous variables were standardized using Z-score normalization to eliminate the influence of different measurement scales. Fatty liver severity was treated as an ordinal variable. K-means clustering was performed using R (version 4.4.2), implemented with the kmeans function. The k-means clustering algorithm was performed using the kmeans function in R with multiple random starts (nstart = 25), ensuring stable clustering results. The optimal number of clusters (K) was determined by combining the elbow method and silhouette analysis [12]. Specifically, the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) was calculated for K values ranging from 2 to 5 to identify the inflection point, while the average silhouette coefficient was used to evaluate clustering compactness and separation. Both the elbow method and silhouette analysis consistently supported K = 2 as the optimal number of clusters. Participants were subsequently classified into distinct metabolic phenotypes according to the clustering results for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the R programming environment (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Unsupervised clustering was conducted in R. Continuous variables were Z-score standardized prior to clustering to eliminate scale differences, and clustering results were visualized using heatmaps. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. For normally distributed variables (expressed as mean ± standard deviation [\bar x± s]), comparisons between two groups were conducted using independent samples t-test. For non-normally distributed variables (expressed as median [interquartile range, P25, P75]), comparison between groups were compared using the rank-sum test. Categorical data were presented as counts and percentages [n (%)], and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the chi-square (χ²) test. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate the associations between hepatic metabolic indicators and HbA1c levels. Variables with statistical significance (P < 0.05) in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for poorly controlled blood glucose. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated. ROC curves were used to assess the predictive performance of each significant variable and the combined prediction model, comparing the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Unsupervised clustering of multidimensional metabolic indicators and metabolic phenotype classification

Unsupervised clustering analysis based on hepatic metabolic indicators and systemic metabolic features was performed. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow method and silhouette coefficient. The results showed that when K = 2, the WCSS exhibited a clear inflection point, and the silhouette coefficient reached a relatively high value, indicating an optimal clustering performance (Fig. 1A–B). Accordingly, 320 patients with T2DM were classified into two metabolic phenotypes. The clustering heatmap (Fig. 2) demonstrated distinct distribution patterns across subgroups in liver enzyme levels, lipid metabolism features, insulin resistance-related indices, and fatty liver grades. Specifically, Cluster 1 was characterized by relatively lower levels of BMI, ALT, AST, TG, and HOMA-IR, along with higher HDL levels. In contrast, Cluster 2 exhibited elevated BMI, ALT, and AST levels, accompanied by increased TG and LDL levels, decreased HDL levels, and higher HOMA-IR values. Based on these metabolic characteristics, Cluster 1 was defined as the low hepatic metabolic burden group (LHMB), whereas Cluster 2 was defined as the high hepatic metabolic burden group (HHMB).

Baseline characteristics of different metabolic phenotypes

A total of 320 T2DM patients were divided into two metabolic phenotypes (LHMB and HHMB). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in age, sex distribution, smoking history, drinking history, history of hypertension, or history of CHD (all P > 0.05). In contrast, significant differences were found in metabolic and clinical parameters between the two groups. Specifically, BMI, SBP, DBP, diabetes duration, liver function indicators (ALT and AST), and lipid profiles (TG, LDL-C, HDL-C) differed significantly (all P < 0.05). Renal function indicators also showed significant differences between groups, including Scr and eGFR (Scr: P = 0.03; eGFR: P < 0.001). Additionally, the distribution of fatty liver severity differed significantly between the two groups (χ² = 63.31, P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Differences in glycemic control among metabolic phenotypes

Compared with LHMB group (Cluster 1), the HHMB group (Cluster 2) exhibited significantly higher HbA1c (t = 17.43, P < 0.001). Fasting glucose levels did not differ significantly between the two groups (z = 0.99, P = 0.32). The incidence of poorly controlled glucose (defined as HbA1c ≥ 7%) was significantly higher in the HHMB group (94.56%) compared to the LHMB group (30.06%) (z = 137.41, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Comparison of β-Cell function and insulin resistance indicators

Significant differences were observed between the two groups in β-cell function and insulin resistance indicators. Fasting C-peptide levels were significantly lower in the HHMB group compared to the LHMB group (t = 6.00, P < 0.001). Similarly, postprandial C-peptide levels were significantly reduced in the HHMB group (t = 5.37, P < 0.001). Additionally, HOMA-IR was significantly higher in the HHMB group compared to the LHMB group (t = 8.31, P < 0.001), indicating greater insulin resistance in the more metabolically abnormal phenotype (Table 3).

Correlation between hepatic metabolic indicators and glycemic control

Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between HbA1c and multiple hepatic metabolic indicators. ALT (r = 0.4539, P < 0.001), AST (r = 0.5491, P < 0.001), fatty liver severity (r = 0.4370, P < 0.001), HOMA-IR (r = 0.5129, P < 0.001), and TG (r = 0.5613, P < 0.001) were positively correlated with HbA1c, whereas HDL-C was negatively correlated with HbA1c (r = − 0.5330, P < 0.001) (Table 4). Stratified correlation analyses according to metabolic phenotype clusters demonstrated similar association patterns between HbA1c and hepatic metabolic indicators across clusters (Table 5).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of hepatic metabolic abnormalities and poorly controlled glucose

Patients were divided into poorly controlled glucose (HbA1c ≥ 7%, n = 191) and controlled glucose groups (n = 129). Baseline comparisons are presented in Table 5. Compared with the controlled group, patients with poorly controlled glucose had significantly higher SBP, BMI, diabetes duration, ALT, AST, TG, LDL-C, and HOMA-IR, and lower HDL-C and eGFR (all P < 0.05). The poorly controlled group also had a higher prevalence of hypertension (P = 0.037). No significant differences were observed in age, sex, DBP, smoking or drinking history, CHD prevalence, or Scr (all P > 0.05). Regarding metabolic phenotypes, the poorly controlled group had more severe fatty liver (χ² = 34.25, P < 0.001) and a significantly different distribution of metabolic clusters (χ² = 137.41, P < 0.001) (Table 6). Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed including variables that showed statistical significance in univariate analysis and clinically relevant factors. The results indicated that longer diabetes duration (OR = 1.246, 95% CI: 1.132–1.371), elevated AST (OR = 1.065, 95% CI: 1.028–1.102), higher TG (OR = 2.944, 95% CI: 1.693–5.119), and higher HOMA-IR (OR = 1.440, 95% CI: 1.099–1.889) were independent risk factors for poorly controlled glucose. Additionally, specific metabolic phenotypes were strongly associated with glycemic control; compared to the reference cluster, Cluster 2 was associated with a significantly higher risk of poorly controlled glucose (OR = 10.749, 95% CI: 3.862–29.917). Fatty liver severity also showed a significant association with glucose control (overall P = 0.045), with Grade 1 (OR = 3.008) and Grade 2 (OR = 2.684) showing increased risks compared to Grade 0. Conversely, history of hypertension and eGFR were not found to be independent predictors in the final model (all P > 0.05). Multicollinearity diagnostics showed that all variables included in the final model had VIF values ranging from 1 to 3 (Table 7).

Discussion

In this study, unsupervised clustering based on multidimensional metabolic indicators identified two metabolic risk profiles among T2DM patients and systematically evaluated their associations with poor glycemic control. The results indicate that hepatic metabolic abnormalities are strongly associated with poorly controlled glucose, and likely interact with dyslipidemia and insulin resistance to create a significant metabolic burden on glucose homeostasis [13, 14]. These findings underscore the centrality of liver dysfunction in T2DM pathophysiology, where hepatic abnormalities are intertwined with other metabolic dysfunctions, suggesting a multifactorial origin for poor glycemic control. Importantly, previous studies have largely focused on individual hepatic indicators, such as liver enzymes or hepatic steatosis, when evaluating glycemic control. In contrast, the present study integrated liver function, lipid metabolism, insulin resistance, and MASLD-related features into a unified metabolic phenotype framework, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic heterogeneity among patients with T2DM.

At the metabolic profile level, the LHMB group exhibited low overall metabolic burden, with relatively normal liver function, lipid profiles, and insulin resistance, corresponding to better glycemic control. In contrast, the HHMB group showed higher levels of liver dysfunction, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance, accompanied by significantly elevated HbA1c levels and higher incidence of poorly controlled glucose. Notably, the multivariate regression analysis revealed that the HHMB group (Cluster 2) was a strong independent predictor of poor glycemic control, with an OR exceeding 10. Although previous studies have reported associations between individual hepatic metabolic markers and glycemic control, few have systematically evaluated the predictive utility of integrated metabolic phenotypes. Our findings suggest that metabolic phenotype stratification may provide additional information beyond conventional single-indicator assessment [15].

Although the differences between groups partly reflect variation in overall metabolic burden, the clustering approach integrates multiple metabolic indicators, capturing a composite pattern of metabolic dysfunction rather than relying on a single parameter [16]. Importantly, the association between worsening metabolic abnormalities and glycemic control, including elevated BMI, ALT, AST, TG, and HOMA-IR, indicates that the HHMB group is at a substantially higher risk of poor glycemic control compared to the LHMB group. These findings suggest that hepatic metabolic dysregulation, in particular, may act as an amplifier in glucose imbalance, exacerbating the effects of other metabolic factors like insulin resistance and dyslipidemia. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which these factors interact [17–19].

In addition to hepatic abnormalities, it is clear that insulin resistance and dyslipidemia also contribute to the metabolic burden and poor glucose regulation observed in the HHMB group. Correlation analyses further supported these observations. ALT, AST, fatty liver severity, and HOMA-IR were moderately positively correlated with HbA1c, whereas HDL-C was significantly negatively correlated, indicating close links between liver injury, hepatic lipid deposition, insulin resistance, and long-term glycemic control [20, 21]. Specifically, the negative correlation between HDL-C and HbA1c supports the potential protective role of HDL-C in glycemic control, which deserves further exploration in future studies to better understand its underlying mechanisms [22]. Beyond its role in reverse cholesterol transport, HDL possesses anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties that may directly ameliorate insulin resistance and protect pancreatic β-cell function, thereby contributing to the favorable metabolic profile observed in the LHMB group [23]. These correlations highlight the integrated nature of liver dysfunction and insulin resistance in shaping glycemic outcomes in T2DM patients. Previous studies have confirmed that hepatic insulin resistance contributes to fasting and basal hyperglycemia by enhancing gluconeogenesis and impairing insulin-mediated suppression of hepatic glucose output [24, 25]. Our findings provide clinical evidence supporting this mechanism, further demonstrating the pivotal role of hepatic dysfunction in sustaining chronic hyperglycemia.

Our multivariate logistic regression analysis identified key independent predictors of poorly controlled glucose, including diabetes duration (OR = 1.246), elevated AST (OR = 1.065), higher TG (OR = 2.944), and higher HOMA-IR (OR = 1.440). These findings indicate the substantial role of these metabolic factors in glycemic dysregulation [26]. Importantly, metabolic phenotype classification (Cluster 2) remained a strong independent predictor, emphasizing that the integrated metabolic phenotypes provide additional predictive value beyond single metabolic indicators. In this context, although diabetes duration differed significantly between groups and reflects disease progression, metabolic phenotype remained independently associated with poor glycemic control after adjustment, indicating that the clustering captures additional metabolic heterogeneity beyond disease duration alone. In addition to these findings, the relationship between fatty liver severity and glucose control warrants careful interpretation. In univariate analysis, fatty liver severity was significantly associated with poor glucose control [27]. However, in the multivariate model, fatty liver remained statistically significant overall, although the strength and consistency of this association varied across severity grades. Specifically, mild-to-moderate fatty liver severity were associated with an increased risk of poor glycemic control, whereas this association was not observed in more advanced stages. This suggests that while fatty liver, as a structural indicator, may initially appear predictive, it is the associated metabolic dysfunction (e.g., hepatocellular injury, lipotoxicity, and insulin resistance) that plays a more direct role in glycemic control [28]. These findings indicate that structural steatosis alone may not fully capture the metabolic burden relevant to glycemic regulation, and that functional metabolic alterations may play a more dominant role.

Predictive analyses indicated that individual metabolic indicators had moderate discriminatory ability for poorly controlled glucose, while the combined model showed improved discriminative performance (AUC = 0.943). The strong performance of this combined model underscores an important clinical implication: poor glycemic control in T2DM is not driven by a single metabolic abnormality but arises from multi-system metabolic dysregulation [29]. A model integrating multidimensional metabolic features may provide greater discriminative value than single-indicator assessment for identifying high-risk patients. It should be noted that the predictive performance of the combined model was evaluated using the same dataset without internal validation, which may lead to overestimation of its discriminative ability. Therefore, further validation using independent cohorts or resampling techniques is warranted.

Clinically, these findings support monitoring hepatic metabolic status in addition to routine glycemic measures in T2DM management. Rather than representing entirely distinct biological subtypes, the identified metabolic profiles provide an integrated stratification of metabolic risk, enabling early identification of patients at higher risk of poor glycemic control. By identifying patients with a higher hepatic metabolic burden early, clinicians can implement targeted interventions, such as improving liver function and addressing insulin resistance, potentially improving long-term glycemic control. This study emphasizes the value of multidimensional metabolic risk stratification in guiding individualized T2DM management.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, causality cannot be established, and external validation in multicenter, prospective studies is needed. In addition, the prediction model was not internally or externally validated, which may limit the generalizability of the reported predictive performance. Second, fatty liver assessment relied on imaging grading without quantitative fat content or histological confirmation. Furthermore, due to the retrospective design, detailed information on antidiabetic medications (particularly metformin, pioglitazone, etc.) and lipid-lowering therapies was not consistently available and could not be fully accounted for. As these treatments may affect hepatic steatosis, liver enzymes, and metabolic profiles, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Future prospective studies with comprehensive medication data are warranted to clarify the independent relationships between hepatic metabolic abnormalities and glycemic control. Finally, potential mechanistic indicators such as inflammatory markers or hepatokines were not included, limiting deeper exploration of underlying pathways. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal follow-up and interventional designs to evaluate whether targeted interventions on hepatic metabolic abnormalities improve long-term glycemic control and complication risk.

Conclusion

Hepatic metabolic dysregulation is closely associated with poor glycemic control in T2DM. Greater severity of hepatic metabolic abnormalities corresponds to higher risk of poorly controlled glucose. Integrated assessment based on hepatic metabolic features can facilitate identification of high-risk patients, providing a reference for clinical risk stratification.
