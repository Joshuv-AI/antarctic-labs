Giant left adrenal endothelial cyst presenting as a symptomatic retroperitoneal mass: A case report
Authors: Mohamed Hajri, Mohamed Mehdi Kamoun, Zied Hadrich, Taher Labidi, Lassad Gharbi, Dhouha Bacha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adrenal endothelial cysts are rare lesions that may present as large retroperitoneal masses and mimic other cystic adrenal or retroperitoneal tumors. We report a 54-year-old man with progressive left lumbar pain caused by a giant left retroperitoneal cystic mass measuring 19.2 × 18.1 × 12.0 cm, displacing adjacent organs. Open surgical excision was performed after controlled intraoperative decompression. Histopathology showed a fibrous cyst wall lined by bland flattened CD34-positive endothelium with residual adrenal tissue, confirming an adrenal endothelial cyst. Surgery provided definitive diagnosis and treatment with an uneventful postoperative course.

Introduction

Adrenal cysts are rare entities, reported in approximately 0.06–0.18% of autopsy series, with increased detection in contemporary practice due to widespread use of cross-sectional imaging 1, 2, 3. Their clinical significance derives from (i) the potential for mass effect when lesions become large, (ii) the difficulty of excluding functionality or malignancy preoperatively, and (iii) a broad histopathological spectrum 1, 2, 3.

Historically, adrenal cysts have been categorized into endothelial cysts, pseudocysts, epithelial cysts, and parasitic (most often hydatid) cysts, as reflected in widely used classifications and modern reviews 3, 4, 5, 6. Most lesions are small and asymptomatic; however, “giant” cysts may reach very large dimensions and present with pain, a palpable mass, gastrointestinal symptoms, or complications (hemorrhage, rupture, infection) 1, 2, 3, 4. Current evidence supportssurgical management primarily for adrenal cysts that are symptomatic, hormonally active, radiologically suspicious, enlarging, or when malignancy cannot be excluded. Although giant cysts frequently require surgery because of compressive symptoms and diagnostic uncertainty, size alone should not be considered an absolute indication1,3,7,8.

We report a giant left adrenal endothelial cyst presenting as a symptomatic retroperitoneal mass with significant renal and pancreatic displacement, managed by open excision facilitated by controlled intraoperative decompression. This work has been reported in accordance with the SCARE checklist.9

Case report

A 54-year-old male patient, with no significant past medical history, presented with disabling left lumbar pain evolving over one month, without bowel disturbances or associated urinary symptoms. On clinical examination, a tender palpable mass was noted in the left flank.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated normal adrenal endocrine evaluation. Plasma free metanephrines were within normal limits, excluding pheochromocytoma. Following an overnight 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test, serum cortisol was appropriately suppressed, and no biochemical evidence of autonomous cortisol secretion was identified. The patient was normotensive and normokalemic; therefore, evaluation for primary aldosteronism was not indicated. Tumor markers were also within normal limits.

Abdominal CT scan, complemented by MRI (Fig. 1), revealed a large retroperitoneal cystic mass measuring 19.2 × 18.1 × 12.0 cm in its greatest dimensions. Given these findings, surgical excision was decided for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. The patient underwent a midline laparotomy, which revealed a large, well-encapsulated retroperitoneal cystic mass measuring approximately 20 cm. Due to its size and limited mobility, intraoperative aspiration of the cystic content was required to facilitate mobilization and safe dissection from surrounding structures. After decompression, the mass was carefully freed from its attachments. The lesion was found to originate from the left adrenal gland, displacing the left kidney inferiorly and the pancreas superiorly (Fig. 2). Complete surgical resection of the cystic lesion was achieved.Fig. 2Intraoperative views of the retroperitoneal cystic mass. The postoperative course was uneventful. Macroscopic examination of the adrenalectomy revealed a well-defined paucilocular cystic formation measuring 20 cm and emptied of its contents. The cyst wall was thin and showed focal calcifications. Histological examination indicated that the wall was fibrous and exhibited mild inflammation, along with dystrophic calcifications (Fig. 3). The cyst lining consisted of flattened and bland endothelium, which was stained with the CD34 antibody (Fig. 4). Discussion

Giant adrenal cysts remain exceptional lesions and, despite increasingly frequent incidental detection of adrenal cystic pathology on cross-sectional imaging, they continue to generate substantial diagnostic uncertainty when they reach very large dimensions and present with compressive symptoms. In the present case, a 19.2 × 18.1 × 12.0 cm retroperitoneal cystic mass with a thin wall and scattered calcifications produced marked displacement of the left kidney inferiorly and the pancreatic tail superiorly, a pattern repeatedly observed in large adrenal-region cysts in which the principal clinical expression is mass effect rather than endocrine hypersecretion 1, 2, 3, 4. Contemporary case-based evidence underscores that CT and MRI are invaluable for defining the cystic nature of the lesion, its anatomic relationships and surgical roadmap, yet they are intrinsically limited in determining the histological subtype; notably, calcifications, wall thickening or septations may be encountered across entities ranging from endothelial cysts and pseudocysts to parasitic disease and cystic neoplasms, thereby precluding confident preoperative classification in many giant lesions.1,3,4,10

This limitation is clinically relevant because management is strongly driven by symptom burden, size, and the need to exclude malignancy and functional disease; This limitation is clinically relevant because management is primarily guided by symptoms, hormonal activity, radiological suspicion, interval growth, and the inability to confidently exclude malignancy. Although giant adrenal cysts frequently require surgery because of compressive symptoms and diagnostic uncertainty, lesion size alone should not be regarded as an absolute indication for surgical excision.1,3,10

Our operative strategy (open resection via midline laparotomy) aligns with the prevailing preference for an open approach when the cyst is massive or when oncologic safety cannot be guaranteed with minimally invasive manipulation.2 A salient technical point in our case was the need for controlled intraoperative aspiration to decompress the cyst and enable safe mobilization and dissection from surrounding attachments; while intact removal is generally desirable to minimize spillage, selective decompression has been used pragmatically in giant adrenal-region cysts to facilitate exposure and reduce traction on adjacent organs, provided that careful preoperative assessment has not raised strong suspicion for parasitic disease or malignancy.4,7 This nuance is particularly important because pheochromocytoma must be biochemically excluded before any aspiration or decompression maneuver to avoid potentially life-threatening catecholamine release. In addition, hydatid disease, for which adrenal involvement is exceedingly rare but explicitly emphasized as a diagnostic consideration in cystic adrenal masses in endemic settings. In that context, puncture/aspiration-based strategies are usually discouraged because of potential anaphylaxis and dissemination, and complete excision with appropriate precautions and adjunctive anti-helminthic therapy is often advocated.5

Ultimately, histopathology remains the cornerstone of definitive diagnosis: in contrast to pseudocysts, which are characterized by a fibrous wall without a true epithelial or endothelial lining and may show hemorrhagic contents and dystrophic calcifications, our specimen demonstrated a bland, flattened endothelial lining with CD34 immunoreactivity and entrapped residual adrenal tissue within the wall, establishing the diagnosis of an adrenal endothelial cyst arising from the left adrenal gland.1,3,4 The present findings therefore expand the practical spectrum of giant adrenal cystic disease by illustrating how a clinically and radiologically non-specific mass may correspond to a true endothelial cyst, an entity regarded as common in autopsy classifications yet variably represented in surgical series, while also highlighting that symptom-driven surgery in giant lesions is both diagnostic and therapeutic.1,3

Taken together, this case reinforces three lessons: (i) although endocrine and malignancy-oriented assessment is recommended for all adrenal masses, it is particularly important in giant cystic lesions presenting predominantly with compressive symptoms, as functional tumors and cystic malignancies cannot be reliably excluded on clinical or radiological grounds alone. In the present case, biochemical exclusion of pheochromocytoma was also essential before controlled intraoperative decompression; (ii) imaging guides surgical planning but cannot reliably subtype giant adrenal cysts; and (iii) complete excision with tailored intraoperative tactics, including cautious decompression when appropriate, offers definitive treatment and diagnosis, with follow-up advised to monitor for the uncommon possibility of recurrence described in the broader adrenal cyst literature 1, 2, 3, 4,11.

Conclusion

Giant adrenal endothelial cysts are rare but clinically important causes of symptomatic retroperitoneal masses. Cross-sectional imaging is essential for surgical planning, yet definitive diagnosis requires histopathology with immunohistochemistry. For symptomatic or large lesions, complete surgical excision is both diagnostic and therapeutic; controlled intraoperative decompression may be a useful adjunct to facilitate mobilization in carefully selected giant cysts.
