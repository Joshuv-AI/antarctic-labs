AMPKα Silencing Prevents Kanamycin‐Induced Ototoxicity in an Acute Mouse Model
Authors: Shengyu Zou, Fan Wu, Qiaojun Fang, Zuhong He, Hongguo Su, Shan Xu, Su‐Hua Sha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTKanamycin (KM), a widely used aminoglycoside antibiotic, is limited by its ototoxic side effects, primarily targeting cochlear outer hair cells (OHCs) and leading to irreversible sensorineural hearing loss. Our previous work showed that silencing AMP‐activated protein kinase alpha 1 (AMPKα1) prevents noise‐induced hearing loss through a redox‐sensitive regulatory mechanism. Given that both noise‐induced and aminoglycoside‐induced hearing loss share oxidative stress as a key pathological feature, we tested whether AMPKα1 silencing could mitigate KM‐induced damage using an acute ototoxic model combining KM with furosemide (KM + FU). KM + FU exposure markedly increased AMPKα phosphorylation at Thr172. Targeted siRNA‐mediated silencing via posterior semicircular canal delivery in CBA/J mice significantly reduced AMPKα1 expression in OHCs and effectively prevented KM + FU‐induced OHC loss and hearing impairment. This preventive effect was independent of stria vascularis permeability or cochlear KM uptake. These findings identify AMPKα as a contributor to KM + FU‐induced acute ototoxicity and suggest that its inhibition may be a promising strategy for hearing preservation.

Introduction

Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) represents a significant global health concern affecting millions of people worldwide [1]. Among its various etiologies, ototoxicity caused by aminoglycoside antibiotics, such as kanamycin (KM), contributes to irreversible auditory damage [2]. KM is a potent aminoglycoside antibiotic widely used to treat Gram‐negative bacterial infections. However, its clinical utility is limited by severe side effects, including neurotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and notably, ototoxicity that affects both the cochlear and vestibular systems [3, 4, 5, 6].

Treatment with KM induces ototoxicity affecting both cochlear and vestibular systems. In mammals, cochlear sensory cells lack regenerative capacity, and their damage leads to irreversible hearing loss [7, 8]. Vestibular toxicity, on the other hand, manifests as dizziness, vertigo, and balance disturbances. These pathophysiological effects have been extensively documented in both clinical and experimental settings. Although our understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying KM‐induced ototoxicity has improved significantly, no FDA‐approved pharmacological intervention currently exists to prevent or mitigate this damage [9, 10, 11]. This underscores the urgent need for effective therapeutic strategies to protect auditory and vestibular function during aminoglycoside treatment.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative byproducts, including 4‐hydroxynonenal (4‐HNE) and 3‐nitrotyrosine (3‐NT) [12, 13], are well‐established pathological markers of oxidative stress in cochlear tissues and are consistently elevated following inner ear injury caused by ototoxic drug treatment, acoustic trauma, or age‐related degeneration [14, 15, 16, 17]. In response to these insults, the inner ear activates intrinsic antioxidant defense mechanisms. Importantly, the link between oxidative stress and sensorineural hearing loss is reinforced by studies showing that antioxidant treatments can reduce or prevent aminoglycoside‐ or noise‐induced hearing loss [18].

AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) serves as a master regulator of cellular energy status, ensuring metabolic homeostasis in response to energy stress [19]. Its activation is largely triggered by shifts in the cellular AMP/ATP ratio or by upstream regulator, including liver kinase B1 (LKB1) [20, 21]. The principal activation site of AMPK is the phosphorylation of threonine 172 on its α‐subunit (p‐AMPKα T172). Although intracellular AMP fluctuations are the canonical trigger for AMPK activation, pro‐oxidants such as nitric oxide (NO) and peroxynitrite may also activate AMPK [22, 23, 24]. Under hypoxic conditions, ROS‐dependent AMPK activation has been linked to apoptotic cell death, suggesting a redox‐sensitive regulatory mechanism [25]. Antioxidants, conversely, are expected to suppress AMPK activation.

Consistent with this, our previous studies have demonstrated that the ROS/AMPKα signaling pathway mediates noise‐induced sensory hair cell loss. Specifically, AMPK activation following traumatic noise exposure exacerbates oxidative stress and promotes apoptotic pathways, contributing to hair cell degeneration and cochlear synaptopathy [26, 27]. Moreover, pretreatment with AMPKα siRNA prevents noise‐induced hearing loss [27]. These findings support the notion that modulating AMPK activity could be a viable strategy to mitigate oxidative stress‐induced cochlear damage.

Owing to the mechanistic convergence between noise‐ and aminoglycoside‐induced hearing loss, particularly their shared reliance on oxidative stress [28], we suggest that manipulating AMPKα may represent a promising strategy to prevent aminoglycoside‐induced auditory damage. In this study, we employed an acute ototoxic model using KM + furosemide (KM + FU) co‐administration in CBA/J mice to assess AMPKα activation (Thr172 phosphorylation). We evaluated the efficiency of AMPKα silencing 48 h after siRNA application and assessed its impact on the prevention of KM + FU‐induced ototoxicity by quantifying OHC loss and auditory function.

Our results demonstrate that AMPKα1 silencing via posterior semicircular canal (PSC) delivery effectively prevents KM + FU‐evoked outer hair cell (OHC) damage and subsequent hearing impairment. Moreover, AMPKα1 suppression does not alter stria vascularis permeability or interfere with KM uptake into OHCs via the mechanoelectrical transduction (MET) channel. These findings indicate that transient inhibition of AMPKα1 using siRNA may represent a promising strategy to prevent acute KM + FU‐induced ototoxic damage.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Four‐week‐old male CBA/J mice (stock #00656) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory and kept under pathogen‐free conditions in the animal facility at the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC). Mice were kept under a 12‐h light/dark cycle in a temperature‐controlled environment (22°C), with background noise levels maintained at approximately 50 dB SPL. They had ad libitum access to standard rodent chow (Irradiated Lab Diet #5V75) and water. After a 1‐week acclimation period, baseline auditory brainstem response (ABR) thresholds were recorded. Mice exhibiting baseline thresholds above 40 dB SPL were excluded from the study. At 6 weeks of age, the mice received experimental treatments. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with approval from the MUSC Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, with animal husbandry managed by the Division of Laboratory Animal Resources. Only male CBA/J mice were used in this study due to budgetary constraints.

Kanamycin and Furosemide‐Induced Ototoxicity

An acute ototoxicity model using KM + FU was established in 6‐week‐old CBA/J mice, as described in our recent publication [29]. Each mouse received an 800 mg/kg (base) subcutaneous (SQ) dose of KM, immediately followed by a 200 mg/kg intraperitoneal (IP) dose of FU. This regimen reliably induced significant hearing loss and OHC damage, with minimal inter‐animal variability and no mortality. The sequence of drug administration (KM before FU or vice versa) did not affect outcomes in CBA/J mice. Mice had free access to water throughout the procedure. ABR thresholds were reassessed 14 d post‐treatment. For assessment of phosphorylated AMPKα (Thr172) signaling, cochlear tissues were collected 2 h following KM + FU administration.

Assessment of Auditory Brainstem Response and Distortion Product Otoacoustic Emissions

CBA/J male mice were anesthetized using an IP injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) combined with xylazine (10 mg/kg). After confirming the absence of reflexive responses, each animal was transferred to a sound‐attenuated booth, and core body temperature was maintained at 37°C with a heating pad. Acoustic stimuli were presented to a single ear through a custom‐sealed acoustic coupler.

For ABR acquisition, subdermal needle electrodes were positioned with the active lead at the vertex, the reference electrode beneath the ipsilateral pinna, and the ground electrode placed under the contralateral pinna. Responses were collected at 8, 16, and 32 kHz using a Tucker–Davis Technologies (TDT) system. Tone bursts (15‐ms duration, including 1‐ms rise and fall times) were delivered at decreasing intensity levels, and responses at each level were generated from an average of 1024 stimulus presentations. Hearing thresholds were determined by a trained blinded reviewer as the lowest sound level eliciting a repeatable wave II.

DPOAEs were recorded using a TDT RZ6 system controlled by SigGen software [30]. For each measurement, a probe assembly equipped with an ER‐10B + microphone and two independent sound delivery transducers was gently inserted and acoustically sealed within the external auditory canal to ensure accurate stimulus presentation and response detection.

Two primary tones (f1 and f2) were delivered at levels of 65 dB SPL (L1) and 55 dB SPL (L2), respectively, with an f2/f1 ratio of 1.2. DPOAE responses were collected across frequencies spanning 4–45 kHz. Emission amplitudes were quantified and compared against the corresponding noise floor to assess OHC function.

Intra‐Tympanic siRNA Delivery

To locally silence AMPKα, siRNA targeting AMPKα1 (siAMPKα1; Thermo Fisher, #s98535) or a scrambled control siRNA (siCtrl; Invitrogen, #4390843) was applied to the round window niche (RWN). Under anesthesia, the post‐auricular area of the left ear was shaved and disinfected with 10% povidone–iodine. A retroauricular incision (∼1 cm) was made to expose the otic bulla. A 30‐G needle was used to create a small fenestration in the bulla, which was then enlarged to ∼2 mm to visualize the RWN. Using a sterile micro‐tubing system, 10 μL (0.6 μg) of siRNA solution was slowly delivered directly above the RWN. Although the mouse middle ear cavity holds only about 5 μL, a 10 μL volume of siRNA in RNAse‐free water was delivered to ensure complete filing of the cavity. Overflowing volume was absorbed by adjacent tissues.

The incision site was sealed by repositioning the surrounding muscle and secured with 3M Vetbond tissue adhesive, which was also used to close the skin. Mice were kept in the surgical position for approximately 1 h during anesthetic recovery.

Posterior Semicircular Canal siRNA Delivery

An alternative delivery route involved microinjection into the posterior semicircular canal (PSC). The same siAMPKα1 (Thermo Fisher, #s98535) or siCtrl (Invitrogen, #4390843) was used. Following anesthesia and surgical preparation as described above, the sternocleidomastoid muscle was retracted to expose the PSC. A fine 30‐G needle was used to puncture the canal, allowing perilymph to drain for 2 min to equilibrate pressure. A microinjection system was then used to deliver 1 μL (0.6 μg) of siRNA at a rate of 200 nL/min through customized sterile micro‐medical tubing. The puncture site was sealed with repositioned muscle and Vetbond adhesive, and the skin was closed similarly. Mice were kept warm during recovery. Treatment with KM + FU was administered 48 h after siRNA delivery.

Myosin‐VIIa Immunolabeling and DAB Staining of Cochlear Whole‐Mounts for Hair Cell Analysis

Immediately after euthanasia, temporal bones were collected, and the cochleae were gently perfused through both the round and oval windows with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) prepared in PBS (pH 7.4). Tissues were fixed overnight at 4°C. Decalcification was carried out in 4% neutral sodium EDTA for 2–3 days at 4°C. Following decalcification, the sensory epithelium was isolated and separated into apical, middle, and basal segments, then mounted onto 10‐mm glass coverslips using Cell‐Tak (Corning, #354240).

For immunolabeling, samples were permeabilized in 1% Triton X‐100 and then blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. The tissues were incubated with rabbit anti–myosin VIIa antibody (Proteus Bioscience; 1:500) for 24 h at 4°C. After PBS rinses, a biotinylated goat anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (Vector Laboratories; 1:200) was applied overnight at 4°C. Signal amplification was achieved using the VECTASTAIN ABC kit, followed by a 5‐min reaction with DAB substrate. All incubation steps were separated by three PBS washes on a gentle shaker.

For imaging, approximately 7 μL of mounting medium (Electron Microscopy Sciences) was placed on the coverslip, topped with a second coverslip, and sealed with nail polish. Whole‐mount preparations were examined using a Zeiss microscope at 10× or 20× magnification. The length of the organ of Corti was measured, and outer hair cells (OHCs) were counted across the entire cochlear spiral. As expected for this acute model, inner hair cell (IHC) survival remained intact. OHC loss was quantified in 0.5‐mm intervals to generate cytocochleograms.

Immunocytochemistry of Cochlear Surface Preparations

Cochlear whole‐mounts were prepared and mounted following the same dissection and coverslip–attachment procedures used for the DAB‐labeled samples. After mounting, tissues were incubated in 10% goat serum for 30 min at room temperature to block nonspecific binding. The preparations were then incubated overnight at 4°C (protected from light) to either rabbit monoclonal anti–p‐AMPKα (Thr172) (Cell Signaling Technology; 1:100) or rabbit monoclonal anti‐AMPKα1 (Abcam; 1:100).

Following primary antibody incubation and thorough PBS washes, samples were labeled with Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:200) overnight at 4°C in the dark. Actin filaments were subsequently counterstained using Alexa Fluor 488‐phalloidin (Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature, also protected from light. Negative‐control tissues were processed using identical steps except that the primary antibody was omitted.

Semi‐Quantitative Analysis of Immunofluorescence in Outer Hair Cells

To assess relative expression of p‐AMPKα (Thr172) or AMPKα1, confocal images were captured at 63× magnification using identical acquisition settings across all groups. ImageJ software was used to quantify fluorescence intensity. All samples were processed in parallel using the same reagents and conditions to minimize variability.

Phalloidin staining was used to identify OHCs, and regions of interest were manually outlined using the circle tool. Fluorescence intensity was measured in grayscale units, and background signal was subtracted. Measurements were taken at three defined positions: ∼1 mm from the apex (∼8 kHz), ∼2.4 mm in the middle region (∼16 kHz), and ∼3.9 mm in the basal region (∼32 kHz). Each region was analyzed over a 0.12‐mm segment of the sensory epithelium. For every sample, the measured fluorescence values were normalized to the average intensity obtained from the untreated control group.

Analysis of Stria Vascularis Permeability, Cardiovascular Perfusion, and Stria Vascularis Surface Preparations

Stria vascularis permeability was assessed using a cadaverine–Alexa Fluor 555 tracer assay adapted from previously described methods [31]. Forty‐eight hours following PSC administration of either siCtrl or siAMPKα, mice received an IP dose of FU. After an additional 4 h, lysine‐fixable cadaverine (MW: ∼950 Da) conjugated to Alexa Fluor 555 was delivered via tail vein. Five min later, the animals were anesthetized for vascular perfusion.

For perfusion, mice were positioned insupinely, and a midline thoracic incision was made to expose the heart. A perfusion needle was inserted into the left ventricle, and a small cut was made in the right atrium to allow outflow. The circulatory system was flushed with 5 mL PBS over 3 min, followed by fixation with 20 mL of 4% PFA at ∼2 mL/min.

Upon completion of perfusion, cochleae were isolated and gently perfused with 4% PFA through round and oval windows, then left in the same fixative overnight. Under microscope, the bone capsule was removed to expose the pigmented stria vascularis. The middle turn of the stria vascularis was then carefully dissociated from the spiral ligament and mounted flat onto a Cell‐Tak‐coated coverslip.

Tissues were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated isolectin GS‐IB4 (1:2000; Fisher Scientific, #121411) overnight at 4°C to visualize the vasculature, followed by nuclei counterstaining with DAPI (1:1000) for 30 min at room temperature. Each labeling step was separated by three PBS washes. Finally, the preparations were mounted on round glass coverslips, and lateral wall fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 510 confocal microscope.

Semi‐Quantitative Analysis of Immunofluorescence in Stria Vascularis Preparations

Confocal images of the stria vascularis were collected using a 40× objective. Semi‐quantitative analysis was carried out using ImageJ. Each image was first converted to 8‐bit grayscale, and an identical threshold value was applied across all samples to minimize background variation. The outer vessel region was then manually delineated, and the fluorescence signal within this selected area was measured to determine relative intensity levels.

FM1‐43 FX Uptake Assay

To assess mechano‐electrical transduction (MET) channel function, cochleae were isolated 48 h after PSC administration of siCtrl or siAMPKα and immediately immersed in Hanks' balanced salt solution (HBSS). FM1‐43 FX (Thermo Fisher, #F35355) was prepared at a final concentration of 5 μM in HBSS and gently introduced through the round and oval windows to allow dye entry into hair cells. After brief exposure, the cochleae were rinsed three times with HBSS for 10 s each to remove excess dye.

Tissues were then fixed overnight at 4°C in 4% PFA, followed by decalcification in 4% EDTA for 48 h. Whole‐mount surface preparations were generated using the same procedures described earlier. Fluorescence images were acquired with a Zeiss LSM 510 confocal microscope.

Culture of 3T3 Cells, AMPKa1 Silencing, and Protein Extraction

Mouse embryonic fibroblast 3T3 cells (ATCC CRL‐1658) were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, #11965‐092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, #26140‐079) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, #15140‐122). Cultures were kept at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

For AMPKα1 knockdown, the same AMPKα1 siRNA or scrambled siRNA used in the in vivo experiments were applied to the cell culture system. Cells were plated in 6‐well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and allowed to reach approximately 60%–70% confluency before transfection. Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, #13778150) was used according to the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, 0.6 μg of siAMPKα1 or control siRNA was mixed with Opti‐MEM (Gibco, #31985–070) and combined with RNAiMAX reagent, followed by a 20 min incubation at room temperature. The resulting complexes were then added to the cells, which were incubated for 48 h.

Cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin at 37°C for 5 min, transferred to a 15‐mL centrifuge tube (Corning, #430052), and pelleted at 500 × g for 5 min. After discarding the medium, the pellet was rinsed once with 1 mL PBS (Invitrogen, #20012). Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Sigma‐Aldrich, #R0278) supplemented with a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma‐Aldrich, #04906845001). Finally, total protein lysates were quantified and stored at −80°C until Western blot analysis.

Western Blot Analysis

Protein lysates (30 μg per lane) were separated by SDS‐PAGE and subsequently transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Pierce). The membranes were blocked in 5% non‐fat milk prepared in PBS containing 0.1% Tween‐20 (PBS‐T) for 30 min and then incubated with rabbit monoclonal anti‐AMPKα1 (Abcam; #ab32047, 1:500) antibody at 4°C overnight. After three washes in PBS‐T, the membranes were incubated with an HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:2500) for 1 h at room temperature. Signal was detected using SuperSignal West Dura substrate (Thermo Scientific) following extensive washes. After detecting AMPKα1, membranes were stripped and re‐probed with an anti‐GAPDH antibody (Cell Signaling Tech., # 5174, 1:3000) to verify equal protein loading.

Chemiluminescent bands were imaged using the LI‐COR Odyssey Fc system. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ. Background intensity was subtracted from each band, and AMPKα1 signal was normalized to GAPDH for samples run on the same gel. Normalized values were then compared between experimental and control groups for statistical evaluation.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical evaluations were conducted using SYSTAT 8.0 and GraphPad Prism 6.0 for Windows. Sample sizes were selected based on variability observed in preliminary studies, anticipated effect magnitudes, and prior experience using this experimental model.

For comparisons between two independent groups, unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐tests were applied. One‐sample t‐tests were used to analyze normalized ratio data. When three or more groups were compared, one‐way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc multiple‐comparison testing was performed. Two‐way ANOVA was used to assess factors that varied with frequency (e.g., DPOAE amplitudes) or cochlear position (e.g., OHC loss).

A significance threshold of p‐value (p < 0.05) was applied for all analyses. Data are reported as individual measurements and as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise noted.

Result

Kanamycin–Furosemide Co‐Treatment Significantly Increases p‐AMPKα Immunolabeling in Outer Hair Cells

Building on our previous observation that traumatic noise exposure elevates p‐AMPKα expression in OHCs, we examined whether acute ototoxic injury induced by co‐treatment of KM + FU similarly activates AMPKα in cochlear hair cells.

Six‐week‐old CBA mice received KM (800 mg/kg KM base, subcutaneous) and FU (200 mg/kg, IP), following the dosing protocol established in our recent report [29]. Two hours post‐treatment, cochlear surface preparations from the basal turn were immunolabeled using a monoclonal rabbit anti‐p‐AMPKα antibody targeting Thr172.

Mice treated with KM + FU exhibited markedly increased p‐AMPKα immunolabeling in OHCs compared to age‐matched saline‐treated controls (Figure 1A). Semi‐quantitative analysis confirmed a significant elevation in p‐AMPKα levels in OHCs (Figure 1B, t 6 = 5.457, p = 0.0016). Furthermore, Western blot analysis using whole cochlear tissues collected two hours after KM + FU administration revealed bands corresponding to AMPKα (62 kDa) and LC3‐II (16 kDa); however, no significant differences in total AMPKα or LC3‐II protein levels were observed between control and treated group at this early time point (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). These results indicate that AMPKα is rapidly activated in OHCs under conditions of acute ototoxic injury induced by KM + FU.

Hair cell structure is visualized with phalloidin (green). Scale bar = 10 μm. (B) Semi‐quantitative analysis confirms a significant increase in p‐AMPKα immunolabeling in OHCs following KM + FU treatment. Data are presented as mean + SD (n = 4 per group). **p < 0.01, unpaired Student's t‐tests.

Posterior Semicircular Canal Delivery of siRNA Targeting AMPKα1 Significantly Enhances Silencing Efficiency in Outer Hair Cells

To determine whether modulation of AMPKα1 influences KM + FU‐induced hair cell and hearing loss in vivo, we first assessed silencing efficiency in OHCs. Our previous work demonstrated an approximate 25% reduction in AMPKα1 expression in OHCs following intratympanic delivery of siRNA. More recently, we reported that PSC administration significantly improves silencing efficiency compared with intra‐tympanic injection [29].

Accordingly, we delivered siAMPKα1 or siCtrl via PSC and evaluated AMPKα1 expression 48 h post‐delivery. Immunolabeling for AMPKα1 (red) in OHCs across the entire cochlear spiral was markedly reduced by an average of ∼60% (Figure 2A). Significant reductions were observed in apical (t 6 = 2.816, p = 0.0307; Figure 2B), middle (t 6 = 5.869, p = 0.0011; Figure 2C), and basal turns (t 6 = 6.445, p = 0.0007; Figure 2D) compared with siCtrl. No statistically significant differences were detected among cochlear regions from apex to base in siAMPKα1‐treated mice.

(A) Representative images from apex, middle, and basal turns show reduced AMPKα1 immunolabeling in OHCs 48 h after siAMPKα1 administration via PSC delivery. Scale bar = 10 μm. (B–D) Semi‐quantitative analysis confirms a significant reduction in AMPKα1 expression in OHCs following siAMPKα1 treatment. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 4 per group), analyzed using unpaired Student's t‐tests. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. (E) Silencing efficiency of AMPKα1 (62 kDa) was further validated by Western blot analysis in 3T3 cells. Band intensity was reduced in siAMPKα1‐treated cells compared with siCtrl. GAPDH (37 kDa) served as the loading control. (F) Densitometric analysis of Western blot band intensity confirms a significant reduction in AMPKα1 expression following siRNA treatment. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 4 per group), analyzed using unpaired Student's t‐tests. **p < 0.01.

To validate silencing efficiency, we performed Western blot analysis using homogenates from 3T3 cells. A single band corresponding to AMPKα1 (62 kDa) exhibited reduced density in siAMPKα1‐treated cells compared with siCtrl (Figure 2E). Densitometric analysis confirmed an approximately 50% reduction in AMPKα1 expression following siRNA treatment (t 6 = 4.872, p = 0.0028; Figure 2F).

Collectively, these findings indicate that siAMPKα1 treatment significantly reduces AMPKα1 expression. Consistent with prior studies, PSC delivery of siRNA achieves significantly greater silencing efficiency than intratympanic administration.

Pretreatment With siAMPKα Significantly Prevents KM + FU‐Induced Hearing Loss

To determine whether AMPKα silencing can mitigate KM + FU‐induced hearing loss, we measured ABR thresholds following two siRNA delivery routes: intratympanic and PSC delivery. siAMPKα1 was administered 48 h prior to KM + FU exposure and ABR thresholds and DPOAE amplitudes were assessed 14 d post‐treatment.

Intratympanic delivery of siAMPKα1 significantly attenuated KM + FU‐induced threshold shifts at 32 kHz compared with the siCtrl group (p < 0.01), whereas no significant differences were observed at 8 and 16 kHz (Figure 3A–C). Remarkably, PSC‐delivered siAMPKα1 provided near‐complete prevention of KM + FU‐induced hearing loss across all tested frequencies (8–32 kHz), with threshold shifts remaining within 5 dB of baseline (Figure 3D–F).

Data are presented as mean ± SD, with each dot representing one animal (n = 4–5 per group). **p < 0.01, ns: not significant; analyzed by unpaired Student's t‐tests. (D–F) PSC delivery of siAMPKα confers near‐complete prevention of KM‐FU induced threshold shifts across all frequencies, assessed 14 d post‐lesion. Data are presented as mean ± SD, with each dot representing one animal (n = 6 per group). ****p < 0.0001, analyzed by unpaired Student's t‐tests.

To rule out the potential confounding effects of siRNA treatment alone, we conducted experiments in which siAMPK was injected into the PSC without subsequent KM + FU exposure. Auditory function was monitored for 2 weeks following injection. No significant differences in hearing thresholds were observed before and after siAMPK administration (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2), indicating that siRNA treatment alone does not affect hearing function or cochlear integrity.

Given the robust prevention conferred by PSC‐mediated AMPKα silencing, we further assessed OHC function using DPOAE measurements. The siCtrl group showed no rescue of KM + FU‐induced DPOAE amplitude reductions, with responses falling below the noise floor (> −20 dB SPL) across all tested frequencies (8–45 kHz), indicating severe OHC dysfunction. In contrast, PSC‐delivered siAMPKα completely prevented KM + FU‐induced DPOAE amplitude loss across all frequencies (8–45 kHz, p < 0.0001), maintaining responses comparable to the naïve group (Figure 4A–B).

(A) KM + FU treatment markedly suppresses DPOAE amplitudes, indicating OHC dysfunction. PSC delivery of siAMPKα1 restores DPOAE amplitudes to baseline levels, assessed 14 d post‐treatment. Data are presented as mean ± SD, analyzed by two‐way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. (B) Detailed statistical values for DPOAE amplitude comparisons among the three groups are provided, including F‐values and p‐values for the indicated comparisons.

These findings demonstrate that pretreatment with siAMPKα via PSC delivery effectively prevents KM + FU‐induced acute ototoxicity and preserves OHC function.

Pretreatment With siAMPKα1 via PSC Delivery Effectively Prevents KM + FU‐Induced Outer Hair Cell Loss

To evaluate the protective effect of AMPKα1 silencing on OHC survival, cochleae were harvested for histological analysis. Myosin‐VIIa immunolabeling followed by DAB staining was used to visualize and quantify OHCs along the entire cochlear spiral.

In siCtrl‐treated cochleae following KM + FU exposure, only residual OHCs remained in the apical turn, with complete OHCs loss observed beyond 1.5 mm from the apex. In stark contrast, pretreatment with siAMPKα1 via PSC delivery resulted in near‐complete preservation of OHCs (Figure 5A). Quantitative analysis of OHC counts along the cochlear spiral supported these observations, showing significant OHC loss in the siCtrl group and near‐complete preservation in the siAMPKα1 group (Figure 5B).

Silencing AMPKα1 via PSC Delivery Does Not Alter Cochlear Lateral Wall Permeability or Hair Cell KM Uptake

Because pretreatment with siAMPKα1 nearly completely prevented KM + FU‐induced hearing loss and hair cell damage, we next examined whether this protective effect was associated with changes in stria vascularis permeability or KM uptake into OHCs.

To assess potential alterations in cochlear blood‐labyrinth barrier (BLB) permeability, we performed an Alexa Fluor 555 cadaverine assay. Based on our recent report, peak cadaverine permeability occurs 4 h after FU treatment in CBA/J mice; therefore, we selected this time point. Forty‐eight hours after PSC delivery of siCtrl or siAMPKα1, mice received FU treatment for 4 h, followed by intravenous cadaverine injection. Mice were euthanized 5 min post‐cadaverine administration.

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed no significant differences in cadaverine‐derived red fluorescence intensity within the stria vascularis between groups. Additionally, green fluorescence signals indicated no structural changes in the lateral wall vasculature (Figure 6A). Semi‐quantitative analysis confirmed no significant differences in cadaverine fluorescence intensity (Figure 6B).

Finally, because aminoglycosides primarily enter OHCs through mechano‐electrical transduction (MET) channels, we assessed whether siAMPKα1 pretreatment affects KM entry via MET channels. Based on previous studies [32], we used FM1‐43FX dye uptake to evaluate MET channel activity. Pretreatment with siAMPKα via PSC delivery did not alter FM1‐43FX uptake in OHCs compared with the siCtrl group (Figure 6C). Semi‐quantitative analysis of fluorescent intensity confirmed no significant differences between groups (Figure 6D).

These results indicate that pretreatment with siAMPKα1 via PSC delivery does not affect stria vascularis permeability or hair cell KM uptake, suggesting that the observed protection is mediated by transient AMPKα1 silencing rather than altered drug entry.

Discussion

The salient findings of this study are that AMPKα activation at Thr172 occurs in OHCs following acute ototoxic injury induced by KM + FU exposure, and that targeted silencing of AMPKα1 via PSC delivery nearly completely prevents KM + FU‐induced OHC loss and hearing impairment. Importantly, these preventive effects were achieved without altering stria vascularis permeability or KM uptake into hair cells. These results suggest that AMPKα activation may represent an early molecular response to acute ototoxic damage in the cochlea.

Consistent with our previous work on noise‐induced hearing loss, we observed AMPKα activation in OHCs following KM + FU administration. Although AMPK activation generally promotes cellular energy homeostasis and has demonstrated protective effects in various disease models [33], for example, by enhancing glucose uptake in skeletal muscle during exercise and aging, thereby improving insulin sensitivity [34], it can also exacerbate oxidative stress under certain conditions, contributing to cellular damage [35]. Under acute inner ear injury, both traumatic noise exposure and KM + FU ototoxic insults have been associated with AMPKα activation, suggesting a potentially maladaptive role in this context. This observation links AMPK signaling to cellular stress responses, including oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [18]. Additionally, excessive AMPKα activation may involve CaMKKβ signaling, which operates independently of the AMP/ATP ratio [36, 37]. We previously observed CaMKKβ activation in OHCs under both noise and KM + FU exposure. However, further studies are needed to determine whether this activation is causative or secondary to cellular injury.

Our data also demonstrate that siRNA‐mediated knockdown of AMPKα, particularly via PSC delivery, is associated with improved auditory function and robust preservation of OHCs. Importantly, these effects were not accompanied by alterations in cochlear BLB permeability or changes in KM uptake into hair cells. This supports the conclusion that the observed preventive effects are attributable to modulation of AMPKα signaling rather than changes in drug distribution.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study utilized an acute injury model, and it remains uncertain whether similar preventive effects would be observed in chronic or repeated exposure scenarios that more closely mimic clinical scenarios. Second, although we observed a correlation between AMPKα silencing and improved auditory outcomes, the downstream molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. AMPK regulates diverse cellular processes, including autophagy, apoptosis, and inflammation [38, 39, 40], but we did not detect changes in autophagy marker LC3‐II using whole cochlear homogenates under the current experimental conditions. Reliable detection of AMPKα‐mediated downstream pathways was technically challenging. This limitation may be attributable to the use of whole‐cochlear tissue homogenates, in which changes occurring specifically in basal sensory hair cells could be diluted by contributions from other cochlear cell types. This observation is consistent with our previous findings [27]. AMPK is known to regulate autophagy through the AMPK–mTOR–ULK1 axis, and dysregulation of this pathway has been implicated in auditory degeneration [41]. Importantly, AMPK appears to play dual roles in the auditory system: Its persistent overactivation accelerates hearing loss via mitochondrial deafness models [42], whereas its inhibition may protect cochlear cells from apoptosis via ROS–AMPK–Bcl‐2 dependent mechanisms [43]. As a key cellular energy sensor, AMPK also interacts with SIRT1 to modulate autophagy, mitochondrial function, and antioxidant responses [44, 45]. Through inhibition of mTORC1 and activation of ULK1‐mediated autophagy, and PGC‐1α‐dependent antioxidant pathways, AMPK signaling may exert context‐dependent protective or deleterious effects on cochlear cells. Future studies incorporating optimized time points, increased tissue yield, and phosphorylation‐specific analyses will be necessary to more fully delineate the dynamics of AMPKα activation following KM + FU exposure. Third, the siRNA delivery method warrants careful consideration. Although PSC injection achieved effective gene silencing and conferred prevention from KM + FU‐induced cochlear damage, this approach is technically demanding and may limit clinical applicability. In contrast, intratympanic delivery of siRNA resulted in reduced silencing efficiency and diminished preventive effects, underscoring the need for improved less invasive delivery strategies for potential therapeutic translation [46]. In this context, nanocarrier‐based systems or engineered exosomes, which could be administered via intratympanic injection while enabling sustained release and targeted delivery to the inner ear, represent promising avenues for future studies [47, 48]. Finally, the KM + FU acute ototoxicity model does not fully reflect clinical scenarios; this model tends to produce an all‐or‐none hearing loss response, that is, either near‐complete OHC loss or full preservation, making it challenging to assess graded preventive effects. Thus, the observed preventive effect from AMPKα suppression may reflect increased resistance to a threshold‐based insult rather than modulation of a progressive injury process.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that AMPKα activation is associated with KM + FU‐induced cochlear injury and that targeted silencing of AMPKα1 may offer a preventive strategy in this context. However, further studies are required to clarify the underlying mechanisms, assess long‐term outcomes, and evaluate the feasibility of translating these findings into therapies.
