Progress and challenges of tantalum-containing biomaterials in regenerative medicine applications
Authors: Yang Yang, Jinan Wu, Guanyi Liu, Junsi Luo, Xiaoli Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tissue regeneration is a significant medical challenge in repairing damaged tissues and organs, driving the need for novel biomaterials with both excellent bioactivity and mechanical properties. Tantalum (Ta) is a promising biomedical metal due to its mechanical stability, biocompatibility, and biological functions. However, the high density, cost, and processing difficulty of pure Ta limit its direct application. Consequently, research strategies have shifted to constructing a new generation of Ta-containing biomaterials by compositing Ta with other materials to retain its biological advantages while optimizing overall performance. Despite these advances, a comprehensive review systematically linking the fundamental properties of Ta with diverse material strategies and their clinical applications is still missing. To address this gap, the primary objective of this review is to provide a comprehensive framework that bridges Ta’s material science with its clinical application strategies. Accordingly, we systematically examine Ta’s properties, regeneration mechanisms, and the applications of four types of Ta-containing materials in hard and soft tissue regeneration, followed by an analysis of current challenges and future directions. This work represents the first comprehensive integration from basic properties to clinical applications, providing a systematic overview and a solid theoretical foundation for optimizing design, accelerating translation, and advancing regenerative medicine.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of regenerative medicine offers new hope for repairing tissue defects. However, its clinical translation still faces critical bottlenecks. Significant limitations of existing biomaterials include inadequate bioactivity and dynamic adaptability, mechanical property mismatch leading to stress shielding effects [1], and a lack of active immunomodulatory capacity, all of which substantially compromise tissue healing efficacy [2]. These challenges have spurred the exploration of a new generation of biomaterials. Among various candidates, tantalum (Ta) is transitioning from traditional medical implants to multifunctional platforms for tissue regeneration, owing to its unique physicochemical and biological properties.

Ta is a rare metal characterized by high density, hardness, and melting point, along with outstanding chemical stability and corrosion resistance [3, 4]. It primarily exists in forms such as pure metal, alloys, and tantalum oxide, each offering distinct physicochemical properties suitable for specific biomedical applications [5]. Beyond its fundamental chemical composition, the structural design of Ta is crucial for its biomedical performance. Notably, porous or trabecular Ta structures have served as a cornerstone in orthopedic applications for decades. By mimicking the natural cancellous bone architecture, this highly porous structure provides an optimal three-dimensional microenvironment for cell ingrowth and osseointegration, while its tunable mechanical properties effectively mitigate stress shielding [6]. Concurrently, the engineering of Ta into nanoscale structures offers distinct advantages in promoting cell adhesion and proliferation owing to a high specific surface area [7]. These diverse structural forms provide a broad spectrum of options for material design.

The biological benefits of Ta underpin its molecular mechanisms in tissue regeneration. In osteogenesis, Ta mediates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to enhance the expression of osteogenic transcription factors [8]. It also demonstrates immunomodulatory capabilities by promoting M2 macrophage polarization [9], and upregulates angiogenic genes such as VEGF to enhance neovascularization [10–12]. Furthermore, Ta supports neural and skeletal muscle regeneration by inducing neuron-related genes and activating the integrin-β1/FAK/AKT pathway, respectively [13, 14].

Despite the remarkable clinical success of classical porous and bulk Ta structures, their direct application in complex regenerative scenarios reveals inherent limitations. For instance, classical porous Ta structures lack the capacity for dynamic adaptability and active immunomodulation [2]. To overcome these drawbacks, researchers have increasingly focused on developing Ta-containing composites by combining Ta with organic polymers, inorganic non-metals, or other metals. These composites aim to retain the structural benefits of Ta while endowing it with enhanced bioactivity and multifunctionality. Consequently, Ta-containing biomaterials have demonstrated promising outcomes extending from orthopedics and cartilage repair to skeletal muscle regeneration.

However, current research remains largely confined to specific tissues or mechanisms, lacking a systematic integration of Ta’s role in multi-tissue regeneration. Given the excellent biocompatibility and pleiotropic biological functions of Ta-containing biomaterials, a systematic summary covering the complete framework—from regeneration mechanisms and material classification to clinical applications—is of great significance (Fig. 1). Characteristics of Ta

Tantalum is a rare transition metal [15]. The breadth and depth of its applications in the biomedical field stem from its stable physicochemical properties, bone-matching mechanical properties, excellent biosafety, and diverse biofunctionality. These characteristics collectively ensure that tantalum can function long-term, stably, and safely within the human body.

Stable physicochemical properties

Tantalum exhibits excellent thermal and electrical conductivity, high density, and high hardness. Its surface can form an oxide layer, primarily composed of Ta₂O₅ and TaO₂. This oxide layer is non-conductive and resistant to strong acids and alkalis, endowing tantalum with exceptional corrosion resistance and chemical stability [16–18]. These properties confer excellent application potential in regenerative medicine.

Bone-matching mechanical properties and tribological behavior

Tantalum plays a crucial role in regenerative medicine due to its suitable elastic modulus, high compressive strength, and tunable flexural strength. Tantalum can be fabricated into highly porous structures [19, 20]. By controlling the porosity within 75–80% and the pore size within 500–1000 μm, the elastic modulus of tantalum scaffolds can be adjusted to 1.5–10.0 GPa, matching that of natural bone tissue [21–23], while simultaneously reducing the stress-shielding effect [24–27]. The compressive strength of tantalum scaffolds ranges from 14 to 480 MPa, approximating that of cortical bone, and their toughness and buffering capacity under stress are superior to those of titanium [27–29]. Furthermore, studies have shown that the flexural strength of tantalum increases with decreasing porosity [30], ensuring structural stability in various applications.

In addition to structural mechanical properties, the tribological behavior of tantalum in load-bearing applications presents significant limitations. Although tantalum is generally classified as a hard metal, its hardness is relatively insufficient when compared to conventional orthopedic bearing metals [31]. This inherent hardness deficit makes pure tantalum highly susceptible to surface scratching and plowing, directly resulting in its poor wear resistance [32]. Under continuous mechanical loading, this deficiency leads to the generation of micro- and nano-sized wear particles. Once released, these wear debris can trigger severe local inflammatory responses, activate immune cell cascades, and ultimately lead to catastrophic aseptic loosening, severely compromising the long-term survival of the implant [33]. Paradoxically, while porous tantalum demonstrates a high friction coefficient against bone tissue—which provides excellent initial stability by enhancing interfacial shear strength—this very characteristic can exacerbate surface wear during subsequent physiological micromotion [34]. Therefore, mitigating the friction and wear of tantalum-based implants remains a critical challenge for their clinical translation, underscoring the necessity for surface modification or composite strategies.

Excellent biosafety

Tantalum possesses excellent biocompatibility, allowing cells to survive and grow normally on its surface, thus it is regarded as an outstanding cytophilic material [35–38].

At most tantalum concentrations, cell viability exceeds the 70% non-cytotoxicity standard [39, 40]. At low concentrations, tantalum is not only non-toxic but can also promote osteoblast proliferation, as demonstrated in previous studies culturing mouse osteoblasts with low concentrations of tantalum [41–44]. Zhu et al. confirmed that the release amount of tantalum is extremely low (<0.2 μg/L), it is not prone to accumulation, and low-concentration particles can enhance osteoblast activity [45]. Multiple cytotoxicity assays have further confirmed that 3D-printed tantalum scaffolds, porous tantalum, and tantalum coating materials are non-toxic to mesenchymal stem cells, osteoblasts, fibroblasts, etc., demonstrating good cytocompatibility [38, 46–50].

However, despite the excellent biosafety profile of bulk tantalum, potential adverse effects associated with degradation or wear products warrant careful consideration. In orthopedic applications, mechanical wear can lead to the release of micro- and nanoparticles. Unlike bulk materials, these particles exhibit distinct biological properties and may interact differently with biological systems. Reports have indicated elevated local and systemic tantalum levels in patients with tantalum implants [51]. While generally considered inert, tantalum particles can potentially induce inflammatory responses; for instance, wear debris may interact with immune cells such as macrophages, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines that could contribute to aseptic loosening [33]. Furthermore, nanoparticles possess the capability to cross biological barriers due to their nanoscale dimensions, raising concerns regarding potential systemic distribution and long-term accumulation [52]. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of tantalum’s biosafety should encompass not only the bulk material but also the biological behavior and fate of its degradation products.

Diverse biofunctionality

Tantalum exhibits rich biofunctionality in the field of regenerative medicine. It is widely used in medical devices such as artificial spines, dental implants, artificial joints, and cardiac stents [6, 43, 53, 54]. Both in vivo and vitro studies have shown no significant inflammatory or allergic reactions, providing effective solutions for tissue healing and regeneration.

Tantalum promotes bone tissue regeneration [55, 56]. Osteoblasts can distribute uniformly on its surface [22], and it directionally induces osteogenic differentiation [30, 53]. Tantalum can also synergize with pro-angiogenic factors, such as zinc oxide nanoparticles, to promote the adhesion and proliferation of mesenchymal stem cells, and enhance the tube formation and migration capabilities of vascular endothelial cells in vitro, thereby promoting vascular regeneration [12, 18, 57–59]. In nerve repair, it can support neurite outgrowth and promote the recovery of damaged nerve function [14]. In dental pulp regeneration, tantalum-containing biomaterials such as iRoot BP Plus can promote the proliferation, mineralization, and dentin bridge formation of cells like hDPCs [60]. Furthermore, tantalum possesses antibacterial capabilities, with Ta₂O₅ coatings achieving an antibacterial rate of 87.9% [61, 62].

Currently, there is a growing body of research investigating its mechanisms of action in osteogenesis, cartilage regeneration, angiogenesis, immunomodulation, neuroregeneration, and skeletal muscle formation. Biological mechanisms of tantalum in regulating physiological processes of regenerative medicine

In summary, the mechanisms underlying tantalum’s regenerative efficacy are characterized by remarkable multi-pathway synergy and multi-tissue targeting. As detailed in Table 1, bone regeneration is driven by a highly interconnected signaling network rather than isolated pathways; the Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β/Smad, and Integrin/FAK axes collaboratively orchestrate osteogenic differentiation, matrix synthesis, and metabolic balancing, while chondrogenesis relies on the specific upregulation of key cartilage matrix components. Beyond direct tissue formation, tantalum plays a pivotal role in remodeling the local microenvironment. By suppressing NF-κB/MAPK-mediated oxidative stress and shifting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, it effectively resolves early inflammation. This immunomodulatory effect is deeply intertwined with angiogenesis, as the optimized immune niche synergizes with VEGF/HIF-1α-mediated signaling to establish a robust vascularized network. Furthermore, the upregulation of specific neurotrophic factors (BDNF, NGF) and the activation of the Itgb1/AKT/MAPK pathway extend these regenerative capabilities to neural and skeletal muscle tissues. Collectively, these intricate molecular mechanisms not only elucidate the pleiotropic biological functions of tantalum but also provide precise theoretical targets for the future rational design of multifunctional biomaterials.

Types of tantalum-containing biomaterials

Tantalum has demonstrated profound value in the field of biomaterials due to its unique physical and chemical properties, such as corrosion resistance and excellent biocompatibility. While classical bulk and porous tantalum structures have long served as clinical gold standards in orthopedics due to their excellent bone ingrowth capabilities, they still face inherent limitations such as a single functional profile and lack of active osteoinductive or antibacterial properties. To overcome these bottlenecks, researchers in recent years have utilized various techniques to incorporate different forms of tantalum—including tantalum metal, tantalum nanoparticles, or its oxide—into different matrix materials, thereby developing a variety of functionalized tantalum-containing biomaterials to meet broader clinical needs such as enhanced bone repair, soft tissue regeneration, and antibacterial applications. This article introduces four major categories of these matrix-based materials: organic polymers, inorganic non-metals, metals, and composite materials. Tantalum-containing organic polymeric biomaterials

Polymer materials exhibit favorable biocompatibility and processability. However, they often suffer from limited bioactivity and insufficient mechanical properties. Incorporating tantalum nanoparticles or microparticles into polymers represents an effective strategy to enhance their overall performance.

Tantalum-containing polyaryletherketone (PAEK) biomaterials

Polyaryletherketones (PAEKs), such as polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and polyetherketoneketone (PEKK), demonstrate excellent biocompatibility, bone-matching elastic modulus, and processability, but are relatively bioinert [63]. While classical porous or trabecular tantalum structures have been the undisputed clinical gold standard for osseointegration in orthopedics for decades, their high density, challenging processability, and high cost limit their versatility in certain applications [64, 65]. To bridge this gap, researchers have developed Ta/PAEK composites, aiming to replicate the renowned osteogenic capability of classical porous tantalum within a highly processable and lightweight polymer matrix. This strategic combination yields systems that not only retain the superior mechanical properties of PAEKs but also introduce significantly enhanced bioactivity.

Zhu et al. [66] fabricated a novel composite biomaterial by blending PEEK powder with varying contents of tantalum nanoparticles (ranging from 1 wt% to 9 wt%). It was found that the 3% Ta–PEEK composite exhibited optimal compressive strength and enhanced osteointegration capacity (Fig. 2A). Luo [67] employed cold pressing and sintering to prepare a Ta/PEEK composite (TP) containing 40 vol% Ta. Following sulfuric acid treatment, a microporous PEEK composite (TPS) was formed, which was further loaded with genistein (GS) to yield TPSG. Significant improvements in surface roughness, hydrophilicity, and surface energy were observed in the resulting material. Similarly, using cold pressing and sintering, other researchers developed silicon nitride (SN)-loaded PEKK/Ta composites (STP) [56, 68]. These composites demonstrated markedly enhanced surface characteristics, including roughness, surface energy, hydrophilicity, and protein adsorption. The mechanical properties, such as compressive strength and elastic modulus, of the composite with 40 vol% Ta (PT40) were comparable to those of human cortical bone. Tantalum-containing PCL biomaterials

Polycaprolactone (PCL), a biodegradable polyester, is widely used in tissue engineering due to its favorable biocompatibility [69–73]. Furthermore, its degradation products, CO₂ and H₂O, are non-toxic substances [74]. However, PCL suffers from limitations such as low mechanical strength, insufficient bioactivity, and hydrophobicity, which impede cell adhesion [75]. Recent studies have indicated that the incorporation of tantalum into PCL composites effectively enhances the material’s hydrophilicity and bioactivity, thereby broadening its potential applications.

Xiong et al. mixed PCL particles with tantalum nanoparticles and added the mixture into a solution composed of dichloromethane and anhydrous ethanol. A Ta-PCL scaffold doped with tantalum nanoparticles was then fabricated using a 3D printing mechanical extrusion method. Their research demonstrated improvements in both the mechanical and biological properties of the scaffold [76]. This approach provides a promising strategy for developing PCL-based periosteum substitutes with desirable osteogenic properties.

Tantalum-containing PLA biomaterials

Polylactic acid (PLA) is a biodegradable synthetic polymer characterized by favorable biocompatibility, osteoconductivity, and controllable degradation properties [77]. It demonstrates considerable application potential in various fields, including the promotion of peripheral nerve regeneration, bone tissue regeneration, drug delivery, and tissue engineering [78–80]. However, such polymers are susceptible to partial collapse under stress and exhibit low cell affinity [81, 82]. Relevant studies have indicated that the properties of PLA can be effectively enhanced by combining it with Ta.

For instance, Almansoori et al. [83] fabricated Ta-coated PLA (Ta-PLA) conduits using direct current (DC) magnetron sputtering. Their in vitro and in vivo findings demonstrated that tantalum promoted the growth of Schwann cells, and the Ta-PLA nerve conduits induced peripheral nerve regeneration without the formation of scar tissue. In another study, Hwang et al. [84] prepared Ta-coated electrospun PLA nanofiber membranes via electrospinning and DC sputtering. It was shown that although the mechanical strength of Ta-PLA was reduced compared to that of bare PLA membranes, the Ta coating exhibited minimal delamination from the surface of individual PLA fibers due to its cladding structure. This indicates that the Ta coating possesses high mechanical stability on PLA fibers.

Tantalum-containing hydrogel biomaterials

Hydrogels possess a 3D network structure analogous to the native ECM, providing an excellent microenvironment for cell regeneration, though their standalone mechanical strength is limited [85, 86]. Conversely, classical porous tantalum provides robust mechanical scaffolding but lacks an active cellular microenvironment within its pores. Therefore, filling classic porous Ta scaffolds with hydrogels has emerged as an ideal strategy to combine the mechanical reliability of Ta with the bioactivity of hydrogels.

In this context, Qian et al. utilized laser additive manufacturing to fabricate porous Ta scaffolds. Vancomycin (Van)-loaded polylacticcoglycolicacid (PLGA) microspheres were then incorporated into the porous Ta using GelMA hydrogels at different concentrations, constructing composite Ta/Gel/PLGA/Van scaffolds for the repair of infected bone defects (IBDs). Physicochemical characterization demonstrated that the scaffolds exhibited uniformly distributed pore structures with interconnected pores. The release of Van was sustained for over two weeks. Biological experiments indicated that the composite scaffolds possessed good biocompatibility, as well as antibacterial and osteogenic properties, showing significant potential for clinical application [87]. Furthermore, Zhao et al. [88] fabricated porous tantalum (pTa) using laser additive manufacturing and prepared gelatin nanoparticle (GNPs) hydrogels via a two-step desolvation method. The injectable pTa-GNPs hydrogel scaffold was constructed by filling the pTa with the injectable GNPs hydrogel using a syringe. This material can optimize the compressive strength and elastic modulus of pure tantalum and provide a physiologically relevant microenvironment to promote vascularization and growth, establishing a foundation for the application of Ta in tissue engineering implants (Fig. 2B).

Tantalum-containing inorganic non-metallic biomaterials

Among various material types, calcium phosphate-based ceramics most closely resemble bone. Calcium phosphate serves as the primary inorganic component of bone and exhibits excellent biocompatibility and osteoconductivity. The introduction of tantalum into the calcium phosphate system aims to combine the advantages of both, further enhancing the bioactivity and functionality of the material, thereby providing new strategies for tissue regeneration.

HA

Hydroxyapatite (HA) is highly similar to human bone in terms of its chemical, mineralogical, and crystallographic characteristics. However, HA possesses a limitation, namely its insufficient mechanical strength. This drawback restricts its application in scenarios requiring mechanical load-bearing. In recent years, numerous researchers have explored various techniques, such as plasma electrolytic oxidation (PEO), electrochemical deposition, and magnetron sputtering, to coat HA onto tantalum scaffolds to better meet the requirements of diverse applications.

Lu et al. employed plasma spraying to fabricate Ta incorporated with an HA coating. Their study demonstrated that this tantalum-containing biomaterial possessed higher roughness and wettability. The bonding strength between the Ta/HA coating and the substrate was significantly superior to that of the HA coating alone, and osteogenic activity was enhanced [89]. Antonio et al. utilized the PEO technique to grow an HA coating in situ on a tantalum surface. It was found that the calcium and phosphorus content within the coating gradually increased with rising voltage and processing time. Under specific conditions (e.g., 500 V for over 180 s), crystalline hydroxyapatite was formed. This coating, with its high HA content (reaching up to 80%), endowed the tantalum surface with enhanced bioactivity, which is beneficial for bone formation [90] (Fig. 3A, B).Fig. 3A Schematic diagram of animal experiments [109]. CaP

Calcium phosphate (CaP) is the primary inorganic component of bone. Calcium phosphate-based biomaterials have garnered increasing attention due to their excellent biocompatibility, bioactivity, osteoconductive properties that facilitate bone ingrowth, and similarity to bone mineral composition [91–93]. While classical porous tantalum provides an excellent mechanical scaffold for orthopedic applications, its surface inherently lacks the active triggers required for rapid biological fixation. To bridge this gap, depositing CaP coatings onto tantalum substrates has become a primary strategy to enhance the biomimetic properties of these classic structures, thereby promoting superior osseointegration [94].

CaP is utilized for the surface modification of tantalum-based substrates and for drug delivery. An alendronate-CaP coating, developed by Galib et al., promoted organized bone deposition onto the scaffold surface, addressing the interfacial discrepancy between pTa implants and bone tissue, and effectively filling simulated bone defects [95] (Fig. 3A). Xu et al. applied CaP and magnesium-doped calcium phosphate (Mg-CaP) coatings to the surface of SLM Ta scaffolds using a hydrothermal method. Experimental results indicated that both CaP and Mg-CaP coatings significantly enhanced the osseointegration of the 3D-printed Ta, thereby improving surface bioactivity [96]. Another approach to enhance the bioactivity of tantalum involves depositing amorphous CaP apatite-like particles on the tantalum surface via a biomimetic two-step immersion method in SBF solution (Fig. 3B). This method promoted the initial adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells [97]. In an implantation study conducted by Barrère et al., CaP-coated PTa cylinders were surgically implanted into the femoral diaphyses of 14 adult female goats. Enhanced osseointegration was observed, along with pronounced osteogenic activity [98].

In summary, applying CaP coatings to classic porous and 3D-printed tantalum scaffolds successfully provides these orthopedic implants with the necessary surface bioactivity, significantly improving their bioactivity and osteoconductivity, and substantially expanding their application prospects in the field of bone repair and replacement.

Metal-based biomaterials containing tantalum

Metal biomaterials containing tantalum primarily utilize tantalum in combination with other metals or alloying elements. This approach aims to overcome the limitations associated with single-metal materials, such as elastic modulus mismatch, high processing costs, or insufficient bioactivity, while preserving or enhancing their biocompatibility and osseointegration performance.

Zr

Zirconium (Zr) is chemically similar to titanium and does not exhibit local or systemic toxic effects [99]. Zirconium ions released from implants can enhance the response of bone tissue [100]. Alloys of Ta and Zr possess low elastic moduli, which are similar to the biomechanical properties of human bone. However, any minor defects present in commonly used zirconia materials can potentially lead to ultimately unpredictable fractures, and these materials are susceptible to low-temperature hydrothermal degradation or aging in vivo [101, 102]. The role of alloys formed from tantalum and other metals, such as zirconium, in bone and dental implants has been reported in the literature.

For instance, the addition of a small amount of tantalum (e.g., in Zr₅₈Cu₂₅Al₁₄Ta₃ metallic glass) to zirconium-based metallic glasses (Zr-MGs) improves the material’s surface chemistry and chemical stability. Mg

In recent years, magnesium (Mg) has garnered significant attention due to its remarkable osteogenic and angiogenic capabilitie [108–112]. While classical porous orthopedic implants (such as trabecular Ta or porous Ti) provide excellent structural support for bone conduction, they inherently lack the biological cues to actively trigger rapid vascularization. Therefore, incorporating Mg into these classic porous scaffolds has demonstrated significant potential in accelerating early-stage in vivo vascularization [113, 114]. The combination of magnesium with tantalum-containing systems directly overcomes the insufficient surface bioactivity of traditional bulk or porous metal implants, providing a promising research direction for scenarios requiring robust vascularization and osseointegration.

Zhang et al. deposited a magnesium-containing tantalum oxide (Ta₂O₅) coating onto a classic porous titanium-based scaffold via the sol-gel method. This coating was proven to exhibit favorable hydrophilicity and demonstrated excellent performance in promoting angiogenesis, cell proliferation, osteogenic gene expression, mineralization, and bone ingrowth. Other tantalum-containing metallic biomaterials

Tantalum-containing metallic biomaterials, developed through methods such as alloying, coating, or surface modification, aim to overcome the limitations of single-metal materials and enhance their overall performance. These efforts collectively advance the development of tantalum-containing metallic biomaterials for bone repair and other clinical applications.

To address the biological rigidity of titanium alloys and the high cost of pure tantalum, Lei et al. [116] fabricated Ti6Al4V-based tantalum scaffolds via laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), where tantalum was deposited onto a solid Ti6Al4V substrate. The study showed that the novel scaffold exhibited a compressive modulus and strength conducive to bone ingrowth, and its ultimate interfacial bonding strength was significantly higher than that of human cortical bone, indicating excellent mechanical stability in vivo. This novel scaffold demonstrates considerable potential for clinical application.

Furthermore, doping with metals such as Cu [117, 118] can impart antibacterial properties to tantalum-containing biomaterials. Cui et al. [119] prepared a Ta-5Cu alloy using spark plasma sintering. Tantalum-containing composite biomaterials

Tantalum-containing composite biomaterials have been extensively investigated in fields such as bone regeneration and tissue healing. While classical porous or trabecular tantalum structures have long served as the gold standard for mechanical stability and passive bone conduction in orthopedics, they inherently lack active biological cues such as osteoinduction, angiogenesis, or antibacterial properties. To overcome these limitations of classical bulk and porous Ta, researchers have strategically developed diverse composite systems, utilizing the robust porous Ta scaffold as a structural backbone and upgrading it through surface functionalization or multi-material integration. Numerous studies have explored their properties and applications from various perspectives, revealing the great potential of these materials in the biomedical field.

Utilizing a fused deposition modeling (FDM) system, researchers manufactured 3D-printed PLA/β-TCP scaffolds. A PDA and Ta coating were deposited onto their surface via dopamine self-polymerization, resulting in the composite scaffold PLA/β-TCP/PDA/Ta. This composite biomaterial scaffold possessed a higher swelling ratio, compressive modulus, excellent bioactivity, and enhanced cell proliferation promotion capability, meeting the initial requirements for bone regeneration and tissue healing [120].

Zhou et al. synthesized amorphous CaP nanospheres via a co-precipitation method to prepare a CaP-PLA composite, which was used to coat Ta plates and classic porous Ta scaffolds. Compared to bare Ta plates, the CaP-PLA coated Ta plates exhibited enhanced surface biomineralization, greater hydrophilicity, and the ability to guide bone regeneration in vivo [121]. In subsequent research, Zhou separately prepared ACP nanospheres and HA nanorods and used PLA polymer to coat porous Ta scaffolds. It was confirmed that both ACP-PLA and HA-PLA coatings exhibited strong surface hydrophilicity, and MG63 cells showed good viability, adhesion, and spreading behavior on the correspondingly modified classic porous Ta scaffolds [122].

Other researchers have combined the advantages of Ta, PCL, and Mg to develop high-performance bone repair materials. For instance, a PCL/Ta/MgO nanofiber membrane was prepared via electrospinning. This material possesses both osteogenic and pro-angiogenic capabilities, effectively promoting the formation of vascularized bone tissue and exhibiting potential for coupling effects [11].

For addressing large bone defects, researchers leveraged the adhesive ability of polydopamine. Building upon the classic porous architecture, tantalum scaffolds were manufactured using an SLM 3D printer, and magnesium ions along with polydopamine were deposited via dopamine self-polymerization to form Ta-PDA-Mg scaffolds. Their elastic modulus was close to that of natural bone. In vivo studies demonstrated enhanced osteogenic and angiogenic capabilities of the Ta scaffolds, indicating that magnesium-modified 3D-printed porous tantalum scaffolds represent an effective strategy for developing active, customized implants [123].

Sr is an inherent trace element in the human body that can enhance angiogenesis and bone formation [116, 124–126]. Cheng et al. [12] printed porous Ta scaffolds using an SLM machine and CAD software and deposited a polydopamine and strontium coating via a simple dip-coating method. This novel biological scaffold exhibited an elastic modulus similar to human cancellous bone—a hallmark feature of classical trabecular Ta—potentially avoiding stress shielding, while promoting vascular and bone regeneration.

Ag possesses broad-spectrum and highly efficient antibacterial capabilities [127, 128]. Wu et al. [129] obtained dense and uniform Ag- and Ta-co-doped ACP coating films via radio frequency magnetron sputtering. It was demonstrated that the addition of Ta could inhibit the dissolution of the ACP coating, and the synergistic effect of Ag and Ta could prolong the antibacterial activity.

In summary, tantalum-containing biomaterials have formed diverse material systems by combining tantalum with organic polymers, inorganic non-metals, metals, and other materials. Built upon the reliable structural foundation of classical porous tantalum, these composite designs significantly improve the mechanical properties, surface characteristics, bioactivity, antibacterial performance, and angiogenic capacity of the materials, translating passive metal frameworks into smart, bioactive implants. They provide a rich and promising pool of candidate materials for bone defect repair, as well as for cartilage, skeletal muscle, vascular, and neural applications. Future research could further explore the long-term in vivo behavior, degradation mechanisms, and clinical translation potential of different tantalum-containing biomaterials, and optimize their preparation processes and properties to meet broader and more complex clinical needs.

Application of tantalum-containing biomaterials in regenerative medicine

Regenerative medicine aims to repair damaged tissues, wherein tantalum-containing biomaterials exhibit multifaceted therapeutic capabilities. Structurally, they drive hard tissue regeneration by enhancing osteogenesis and directing dentin-pulp complex restoration. For soft tissue environments, they mimic the chondrocyte matrix to facilitate cartilage healing, modulate immune responses to aid skeletal muscle remodeling, and guide axonal growth for neural repair. Furthermore, they effectively accelerate vascularization by activating endothelial cells. Collectively, these diverse mechanisms underscore the profound potential of tantalum-containing biomaterials as versatile platforms for addressing complex tissue injuries.

Bone regeneration

Various traumas and diseases can lead to bone tissue defects, such as trauma, post-resection of bone tumors, and congenital deformities [130]. Although bone tissue possesses a strong self-healing capacity, severe trauma can result in non-union or persistent bone defects [131]. Traditional bone regeneration treatments, including autografts, allografts, and artificial bone grafts, have limitations such as limited autograft volume, risks of disease transmission and immune rejection associated with allografts, as well as risks of infection and long-term implant failure [132]. In recent years, research on bone repair has gradually shifted towards bioactive materials that can enhance osteoinduction and osteoconduction [133, 134]. Studies have shown that Ta possesses excellent mechanical properties and osteoinductive capabilities, making it a highly promising metal implant material for bone [135]. Recent investigations into tantalum-containing biomaterials for bone regeneration have achieved remarkable success.

For bioinert organic polymers, e.g., PEEK, PCL, the incorporation of Ta nanoparticles or ions effectively overcomes matrix inertia, significantly enhancing BMSC adhesion and osteogenic differentiation while maintaining a bone-matching elastic modulus [11, 136, 137]. For metallic systems, depositing Ta coatings on titanium or alloying Ta into Zr-based metallic glass shifts the biological response from passive integration to active immunomodulation—specifically by regulating macrophage polarization and triggering VEGF/BMP signaling pathways to achieve coupled osteogenesis and angiogenesis [103, 138, 139]. Furthermore, when applied to upgrade classical 3D-printed porous Ta scaffolds, multifunctional modifications (such as the Ta-PDA-Mg system) demonstrate that surface functionalization can further amplify the intrinsic advantages of the porous architecture, ensuring faster and more durable osseointegration [123] (Fig. 7).Fig. 7A Graphical abstract. Cartilage regeneration

Excessive load, trauma, or infection can cause articular cartilage injury. However, due to its unique avascular structure and lack of an effective response to injury, cartilage repair is extremely challenging once damaged. The depth of the injury can progress to full-thickness cartilage defects [140], severely affecting joint function and consequently reducing patients’ quality of life [141]. To promote the repair and regeneration of osteochondral defects, researchers have leveraged the advantages of tantalum, which facilitates BMSC attachment and enables good, stable integration with the host subchondral bone [142, 143]. By implanting tantalum-containing scaffold materials at the defect site, often loaded with seed cells and bioactive factors, new perspectives have been opened for improving and optimizing traditional treatment methods [144–147].

Reviewing recent studies, the design of tantalum-containing materials for cartilage repair generally follows two distinct pathways to address the layered complexity of osteochondral defects (Fig. 8): The first pathway focuses on fabricating composites that directly favor chondrogenesis. For instance, integrating Ta ions or nanoparticles into polymer carriers like fibrin or PLA has been shown to promote the expression of cartilage-specific genes and the formation of lacuna-like structures [148]. Furthermore, modifying Ta surfaces with inorganic phases like CaP-PLA microspheres loaded with growth factors (VEGF/TGF-β) further enhances the ability of these composites to guide deep subchondral bone regeneration [149, 150]. Skeletal muscle regeneration

In recent years, research in skeletal muscle regeneration has gradually shifted from purely mechanical aspects towards bioactive regulation. Studies have confirmed that tantalum-containing biomaterials, due to their unique porous structure, mechanical compatibility, and bioactivity, can provide the volume required for soft tissue growth [152] and facilitate close attachment and growth of soft tissues on the tantalum surface [153]. Tantalum-containing biomaterials demonstrate profound application potential in the field of skeletal muscle and tendon-bone complex tissue repair. Their core advantage lies not only in excellent biocompatibility but also in their ability to significantly promote muscle cell biological behavior and functional tissue regeneration by activating specific signaling pathways.

Vascular regeneration

Vascular regeneration is a critical step in the repair of metabolically active tissues such as bone and myocardium. Single-function biomaterials often fail to meet the dynamic requirements of “structural remodeling-blood supply”. Current research exploits tantalum’s unique surface electronic properties, which can synergize with pro-angiogenic metal ions, significantly enhancing its adsorption capacity for fibronectin and thereby promoting the early adhesion and proliferation of mesenchymal stem cells [18, 57, 58].

To translate this biological potential into practical vascularization, current research consistently focuses on one core strategy: incorporating tantalum with specific pro-angiogenic ions to create synergistic composite systems (Fig. 10). For instance, by co-incorporating Ta with ions like MgO or ZnO into electrospun PCL nanofibers, researchers have successfully developed artificial periosteum substitutes. These composite scaffolds exhibit a remarkable synergistic effect, simultaneously enhancing the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and the angiogenic differentiation of endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) [11, 59]. This principle of ion-assisted synergy is further validated in porous tantalum architectures; Cheng et al. [12] demonstrated that modifying porous Ta scaffolds with polydopamine to load Mg or Sr ions significantly boosts the tube formation and migration capabilities of HUVECs. Neural regeneration

Bone defect repair is a highly complex process that involves stem cells, blood vessels, nerves, and other factors [155]. Neural regeneration still faces profound challenges due to axonal guidance defects and inhibitory signals within the microenvironment. The neurotization of tissue-engineered bone is considered an effective strategy to overcome the challenges of vascularization and neural regeneration that traditional tissue engineering scaffolds cannot adequately address [156–158].

Reviewing current evidence, the mechanism by which tantalum facilitates neural regeneration within bone defects centers on one critical aspect: leveraging material synergy to construct a neurovascular coupled microenvironment (Fig. 11). In electrospun composite membrane systems like PCL/Ta/WH, this synergy significantly upregulates the expression of key neurogenic factors, including BDNF, NGF, and S100 [14]. Dentin-pulp complex regeneration

Regeneration of the dentin-pulp complex requires the activation of odontoblastic differentiation, leading to the formation of dentin tissue [14]. Consequently, novel pulp capping materials with enhanced antibacterial and anti-biofilm capabilities, and the ability to promote hard tissue barrier formation are needed. Tantalum-containing biomaterials have emerged as prominent pulp capping materials due to their potential to enhance dentin formation and exert antibacterial effects.

Reviewing current evidence, the advantage of tantalum-containing materials in pulp regeneration centers on one core aspect: significantly enhancing the biological response of dental pulp stem cells and inducing higher-quality mineralized tissue, consistently outperforming traditional materials (Fig. 12). Antibacterial applications in regenerative medicine

Tantalum-containing biomaterials exhibit broad application potential in regenerative medicine owing to their excellent biocompatibility, favorable mechanical properties, and multifunctional characteristics. However, microbial infection remains a significant factor limiting their clinical application. Therefore, endowing tantalum-containing biomaterials with antibacterial properties through functionalization strategies has become a key research direction for enhancing their therapeutic efficacy and safety.

Reviewing current research, the functionalization of tantalum-containing materials to achieve antibacterial effects predominantly relies on two major strategies (Fig. 13): The first strategy focuses on incorporating antibacterial metal elements (such as Zn, Ag, and Cu) directly into tantalum matrices or coatings. By utilizing surface engineering techniques like magnetron sputtering or micro-arc oxidation, researchers have successfully integrated these metal ions into tantalum surfaces or derived coatings. For instance, the introduction of zinc—whether onto pure tantalum surfaces or within composite artificial periosteum like PCL/Ta/ZnO—imparts excellent antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli [59, 114, 169–171]. Similarly, integrating silver or copper into tantalum-based coatings ensures the sustained release of these active ions. This metallic synergy effectively inhibits the adhesion and proliferation of specific pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as broad-spectrum Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, while concurrently maintaining the osteogenic properties of the implant [129, 172–176]. The second strategy utilizes tantalum-enhanced polymer composites combined with surface engineering or drug loading. In this approach, tantalum is used to upgrade the inherent but often insufficient antibacterial and osteogenic capabilities of traditional polymers like PEEK and polyimide (PI). By constructing microporous Ta-containing structures on PEEK surfaces or co-loading them with antibacterial agents like isoflavone or flavonol, the resulting composites exhibit significantly enhanced bacterial inhibition against E. coli and S. aureus. Limitations and perspectives

Although tantalum-containing biomaterials demonstrate broad application prospects and have achieved remarkable progress in the field of regenerative medicine, their transition towards broader and deeper clinical applications still faces a series of challenges that require urgent resolution. Acknowledging and overcoming these challenges is crucial for promoting the continued development of this field.

Firstly, the practical application of tantalum-containing biomaterials is limited by their inherent physicochemical properties. The high density of tantalum restricts its use in certain lightweight application scenarios, while its difficult processing and complex fabrication techniques further limit its widespread application in complex structures or personalized implants. Furthermore, the complexity of the in vivo environment poses challenges to the long-term stability of the material, including factors such as corrosion by body fluids, mechanical wear, and immune responses, all of which can affect the material’s performance and longevity. More critically, tantalum implants may potentially trigger chronic inflammatory responses, which not only affect the material’s biocompatibility but could also cause secondary damage to surrounding tissues. Additionally, the degradation rate of tantalum is difficult to control; excessively rapid degradation may lead to functional failure of the material, while overly slow degradation might impede the tissue regeneration process. Therefore, balancing the physicochemical properties and biological functions of tantalum-based materials represents the primary challenge for their application in regenerative medicine.

Secondly, the current fabrication of tantalum-containing biomaterials primarily relies on traditional techniques such as powder metallurgy, magnetron sputtering, and anodic oxidation. These methods are not only costly but also difficult to scale up for mass production. Concurrently, microstructural parameters of porous tantalum scaffolds, such as pore architecture, pore size, and porosity, are crucial for cell behavior and tissue regeneration. However, existing technologies struggle to precisely control these structural characteristics, leading to inconsistent material performance. Moreover, surface functionalization techniques for tantalum-containing biomaterials remain underdeveloped, with low loading efficiency and poor stability of antibacterial elements or bioactive molecules, limiting their multifunctional development. The fabrication process also faces challenges in guaranteeing material purity, as the presence of impurities can significantly impact the material’s biocompatibility and mechanical properties. Furthermore, the preparation of tantalum-containing biomaterials requires stringent environmental conditions, such as high temperatures and vacuum, which further increase production costs and technical complexity. Consequently, developing low-cost, high-efficiency, and highly controllable fabrication technologies is key to promoting the widespread application of tantalum-containing biomaterials.

Thirdly, although mechanisms such as the osteoinductive, immunomodulatory, and angiogenic properties of tantalum have been partially revealed, the underlying molecular biological pathways, complex cell-material interactions, and dynamic response mechanisms during different tissue regeneration processes still require more systematic and in-depth investigation. Furthermore, systematic studies on the long-term in vivo mechanisms of tantalum-containing biomaterials and the impact of their degradation products on tissue regeneration are lacking. While the antibacterial properties of tantalum-containing biomaterials have been preliminarily validated, their long-term antibacterial efficacy in complex infection environments, particularly their unique advantages in immunomodulation and their specific targets and signaling networks, are not fully understood, which limits their precise application in this field.

Fourthly, the application of tantalum-containing biomaterials in regenerative medicine is predominantly focused on hard tissue repair, with relatively few studies exploring their use in soft tissue regeneration, nerve regeneration, and vascular regeneration. Their compatibility and functional matching with soft tissues require further exploration. For instance, in bone tissue engineering, while tantalum-containing biomaterials exhibit strong osteoinductive capability, their vascularization potential is relatively weak, making it difficult to meet the requirements for repairing large segmental bone defects. In cartilage regeneration, the rigidity of tantalum materials might adversely affect normal chondrocyte function, leading to suboptimal regeneration outcomes. Additionally, the clinical translation of tantalum-containing biomaterials remains in its early stages; most current research is confined to in vitro experiments or small animal models, lacking support from large animal models and long-term clinical data, which hinders a comprehensive evaluation of their actual performance in complex physiological environments. More critically, the practical application of tantalum-containing biomaterials often necessitates combination with other materials, yet issues such as interfacial compatibility and functional synergy in composite materials have not been effectively resolved. Therefore, optimizing the design and application strategies of tantalum-containing biomaterials according to the specific needs of different tissue regenerations is a vital direction for promoting their clinical translation.

Fifthly, the biosafety profile of tantalum-containing biomaterials is more complex than often perceived, particularly regarding degradation and wear products. While bulk tantalum possesses excellent biocompatibility, metal elements introduced during functionalization, such as zinc, silver, and copper, may pose risks of cytotoxicity or tissue damage. More critically, mechanical wear in orthopedic applications can lead to the release of micro- and nanoparticles. Unlike the inert bulk material, these particles exhibit distinct biological properties and behaviors. Reports have indicated elevated local and systemic tantalum levels in patients with implants. These particles can interact with immune cells such as macrophages, potentially triggering inflammatory cascades or oxidative stress that contribute to aseptic loosening. Furthermore, nanoparticles possess the capability to cross biological barriers due to their nanoscale dimensions, raising concerns regarding potential systemic distribution and long-term accumulation in vital organs. Current research lacks systematic studies on the release kinetics, metabolic pathways, and potential toxicity of these degradation products. Therefore, a comprehensive safety evaluation must encompass not only the bulk material but also the biological behavior and fate of wear debris and degradation products.

Beyond the biological mechanisms discussed above, we must honestly acknowledge that translating these materials from the lab bench to actual clinical use involves very practical, non-biological hurdles. For instance, synthesizing a Ta-polymer composite in a beaker is entirely different from mass-producing it under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions. Ensuring batch-to-batch consistency, especially for complex porous structures or delicate surface modifications, remains technically difficult. Furthermore, the regulatory pathway for these novel multi-material systems is still quite vague. Since they combine metals with polymers or bioactive ions, it is currently unclear how regulatory bodies should evaluate their long-term combined safety. On the clinical side, there is a huge gap between rodent models and human patients. We urgently need large-animal studies that can actually mimic human weight-bearing and physiological environments, though these are often skipped due to high costs and long experimental cycles. Finally, we cannot ignore the economic reality. Tantalum is a rare and expensive metal, and its processing is energy-intensive. Even if a Ta-based composite performs perfectly in the lab, surgeons and hospitals might still prefer cheaper, proven options like titanium alloys or PEEK. Therefore, future research needs to focus not only on biological performance but also on simplifying fabrication processes to make these materials truly cost-competitive.

Looking ahead, the development of tantalum-containing biomaterials in regenerative medicine is promising. To address the challenges, more in-depth research by numerous scholars is required. The immune responses elicited by tantalum in patients need to be clarified through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Furthermore, elucidating the metabolic pathways of wear particles and establishing standardized safety thresholds are essential to mitigate risks associated with long-term implantation. Extensive clinical monitoring and analysis of large case series are necessary to gain deeper insights into the compatibility of tantalum with the human body and to identify any potential safety concerns. Scaffold design should be optimized to minimize material usage, reduce manufacturing costs, and decrease weight, providing tantalum with an ideal internal structure. Surface modification of tantalum can enhance its bioactivity and confer various biological and functional properties, including antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects. Alloying can be employed to improve mechanical strength, reduce elastic modulus, lighten weight, and lower the cost of tantalum. Effectively integrating life sciences, medicine, and engineering is crucial to addressing major challenges in medicine and healthcare, ultimately facilitating clinical translation.

In summary, despite the multitude of challenges, with continuous advancements at the intersection of materials science, biomedical engineering, and regenerative medicine, tantalum-containing biomaterials are expected to overcome existing hurdles and achieve broader and deeper applications in various sub-fields of regenerative medicine. Through sustained fundamental research and technological innovation, it is reasonable to believe that tantalum-containing biomaterials will play an increasingly critical role in repairing damaged tissues and improving patients’ quality of life, injecting new vitality into the development of regenerative medicine, and ultimately benefiting more patients. Continued attention and in-depth research on the progress and challenges of tantalum-containing biomaterials in regenerative medicine will propel this field forward.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this review highlights a paradigm shift in the use of Ta for tissue regeneration: transitioning from the direct application of pure Ta to the strategic integration of Ta as a functional enhancer within composite systems. Through comprehensive analysis, we elucidated the core biological mechanisms driving Ta-mediated tissue healing, specifically its promotion of osteogenesis via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, its regulation of macrophage polarization for immunomodulation, and its stimulation of angiogenesis. Most importantly, we systematically categorized and evaluated the most impactful material strategies—organic polymer, inorganic non-metal, metal matrix, and complex composites. These composite strategies successfully circumvent the inherent limitations of pure Ta, such as high density and processing difficulties, enabling the fine-tuning of mechanical properties (including bone-matching elastic moduli and enhanced tribological behavior) and expanding the application scope from traditional hard tissue repair to emerging soft tissue regeneration (muscle, nerve, and vascular).

Furthermore, our analysis provides a critical perspective on biosafety, emphasizing the significant discrepancy between the inertness of bulk Ta and the potential pro-inflammatory risks and biological barrier penetration associated with micro- and nano-wear debris generated in vivo. Ultimately, realizing the full clinical potential of Ta-containing biomaterials relies on overcoming specific bottlenecks: achieving precise interfacial control in composite fabrication, establishing rigorous safety thresholds for degradation and wear products, and further unraveling the dynamic molecular pathways in multi-tissue regeneration. By addressing these targeted challenges, Ta-containing biomaterials are poised to serve as highly customizable, safe, and multifunctional platforms for the next generation of regenerative medicine.
