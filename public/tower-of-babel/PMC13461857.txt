ALBI Grade is a Determinant of Lenvatinib Pharmacokinetics, Efficacy, and Toxicities in Japanese Patients with Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Kosuke Suzuki, Yumi Otoyama, Natsumi Matsumoto, Yusuke Masuo, Yoko Nakajima, Ikuya Sugiura, Takahiro Fuji, Erika Nomura, Yuki Ichikawa, Yuu Shimozuma, Manabu Uchikoshi, Momoka Hayano, Mayuko Hanano, Remi Murase, Chika Mitomori, Kiyoshi Oka, Masashi Sakaki, Yukio Kato, Hitoshi Yoshida, Ken-ichi Fujita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveThe doses of lenvatinib for patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are determined using the Child-Pugh classification and body weight. Because lenvatinib is highly bound to plasma albumin (98.6%), patients with low plasma albumin levels and hepatic lenvatinib clearance may exhibit a high unbound fraction and/or concentration of lenvatinib in the plasma to alter efficacy and toxicity. This study exploratorily and prospectively examined the effects of baseline albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) on systemic exposure to unbound lenvatinib and lenvatinib clinical responses in patients with HCC.Patients and MethodsPatients with HCC who received lenvatinib were enrolled. Total- and unbound-base dose-normalized area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUCt/dose and AUCu/dose, respectively) were determined. Progression-free survival (PFS) and toxicities were evaluated.ResultsForty-one patients were enrolled between May 2020 and January 2024. AUCu/dose, but not AUCt/dose, was significantly negatively correlated with ALBI score (P = 0.00699). Patients with ALBI grade ≥ 2b had significantly higher AUCu/dose than those with < 2b (mean ± SD, 0.00602 ± 0.00268 vs. 0.00390 ± 0.00168 h/L, P = 0.0131). Child-Pugh class was associated with AUCu/dose (P = 0.0312); however, among Child-Pugh classification components, albumin only was associated with the AUCu/dose. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis identified ALBI grade ≥ 2b as risk factor for shorter PFS (P = 0.0000521; hazard ratio 9.43; 95% confidence interval 3.18–28.0). ALBI grade ≥ 2b, but not Child-Pugh class, showed a significantly higher incidence of lenvatinib-induced toxicity grades ≥ 2 than ALBI grade < 2b (87.0 vs. 55.6%, P = 0.0357).ConclusionThe study exploratorily found that baseline ALBI grade was associated with lenvatinib AUCu/dose and clinical responses.Trial Registration NumberUMIN000057138 (registered on February 27, 2025)Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-026-01662-0.

Key Points

This study examined the association of baseline albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) with systemic exposure to unbound lenvatinib and clinical outcomes in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).Baseline ALBI grade, rather than Child-Pugh classification, was significantly associated with the unbound-base dose-normalized area under the plasma concentration–time curve of lenvatinib, progression-free survival, and the overall toxicity grades of lenvatinib.Baseline ALBI grade-based dosing, rather than Child-Pugh classification-based dosing, may be reasonable to effectively manage the clinical outcomes of patients with HCC receiving lenvatinib.

Introduction

Lenvatinib, an orally administered, small-molecule multi-kinase inhibitor, blocks the activity of protein kinases [1–4]. Lenvatinib showed non-inferiority to sorafenib in overall survival in patients with unresectable advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [5]. It has been approved as a first-line treatment for unresectable advanced HCC and is currently used as a second-line treatment for patients with unresectable advanced HCC who have progressed to combined first-line therapy, including at least one programmed cell death 1 or programmed death-ligand 1 inhibitor [6], or as a first-line option for those who are ineligible to receive immune checkpoint inhibitor-including combined therapy.

Lenvatinib is orally administered once daily at 12 mg to patients with HCC weighing ≥ 60 kg, or 8 mg to those weighing < 60 kg [5]. Patients with HCC generally have impaired liver function. The dosage for patients with mild liver dysfunction (Child-Pugh class A) is the same as that for patients with normal liver function; however, a 4-mg reduction in the daily dosage is recommended for patients with moderate liver dysfunction (Child-Pugh class B) for each category of body weight [7]. Despite this, adverse events occur frequently, necessitating dose reduction or treatment discontinuation.

Lenvatinib is extensively metabolized by liver cytochrome P450, family 3, subfamily A (CYP3A) and aldehyde oxidase in humans [8, 9]. It is also potentially excreted into bile through ATP-binding cassette subfamily B member 1 (ABCB1) and ATP-binding cassette subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2) [8, 10]. Lenvatinib has a high plasma protein binding ratio of 98.6% [11, 12]. The primary lenvatinib-binding protein is albumin (93%), with minor binding to α1-acid glycoprotein (6.1%) [12].

Drug efficacy and toxicity are related to the freely circulating drug that is unbound to plasma proteins such as albumin. Patients with HCC frequently have impaired hepatic function and decreased synthesis of proteins, including albumin, in the liver. The plasma concentration of albumin available for lenvatinib protein binding in these patients may be low, and the unbound fraction of lenvatinib may be high. Furthermore, the hepatic clearance of lenvatinib in patients with impaired liver function may be low, owing to the impaired capacity of drug-metabolizing enzymes. Therefore, decreased plasma protein binding and hepatic clearance may increase the unbound fraction and/or concentration of lenvatinib in the plasma. A previous study investigated the influence of hepatic impairment on lenvatinib pharmacokinetics [12]. Patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class C) showed 2.7-fold higher dose-normalized area under the unbound plasma concentration–time curve (AUCu) of lenvatinib compared to those with normal liver function or mild or moderate hepatic impairment, even though patients with severe hepatic impairment received half dose (5 mg) of lenvatinib as compared to other patients (10 mg). This result is one of the reasons why lenvatinib is not used in patients with severe liver impairment. These results were proposed to be, at least in part, associated with the lower plasma albumin concentrations in patients with severe hepatic impairment (60%) compared with those in other study participants [12]. Besides patients in Child-Pugh class C, some of the participants showed reduced plasma albumin levels even though they were classified into Child-Pugh classes A and B [12], groups that are commonly treated with lenvatinib in clinical practice. We hypothesized that some patients with HCC and mild or moderate hepatic impairment exhibit low plasma albumin levels. These patients may experience high systemic exposure to lenvatinib if they have decreased hepatic clearance, predisposing them to lenvatinib-induced toxicity. Albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score or grade (grade 1: ≤ −2.60; grade 2a: > −2.60 to < −2.27; grade 2b: ≥ −2.27 to ≤ −1.39; grade 3: > −1.39) is clinically used to assess liver function, because it is associated with impaired liver function [13]. Because the ALBI includes plasma albumin and bilirubin levels, the score or grade may be associated with lenvatinib systemic exposure and clinical outcome.

This study prospectively and exploratorily examined the association between baseline Child-Pugh classification and its components (including plasma albumin level) or ALBI (score or grade) and total and unbound plasma concentration-based systemic exposure to lenvatinib, and lenvatinib efficacy and toxicity. The effects of polymorphisms in genes encoding factors associated with lenvatinib pharmacokinetics on systemic exposure to lenvatinib were also examined.

Patients and Methods

Chemicals

Lenvatinib was purchased from LC Laboratories (Woburn, MA, USA). All chemicals and solvents used were of the highest available commercial grade.

Study Design

This prospective study included patients with unresectable advanced HCC who were candidates for lenvatinib administration at Showa Medical University Hospital (Tokyo, Japan). Eligible patients were aged ≥ 20 years with histologically confirmed unresectable advanced HCC and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of ≤ 1. Each patient was confirmed to have adequate bone marrow and organ function within 7 days of lenvatinib treatment initiation.

The primary endpoint was the assessment of the association between dose-normalized AUCu (AUCu/dose) and Child-Pugh class (A and B) or ALBI grade (≥ 2b and < 2b). The secondary endpoint included the examination of the association between dose-normalized area under the total plasma concentration–time curve (AUCt/dose), unbound fraction, progression-free survival (PFS), overall or individual toxicities (≥ 2 and < 2), or relative dose intensity (RDI) and Child-Pugh class and its components, AUCu/dose, AUCt/dose, or ALBI score and/or grade. The exploratory endpoint evaluated the association between genetic polymorphisms and AUCu/dose or AUCt/dose.

This study was conducted following the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of Showa Medical University approved the study protocol (approval number, G305; date, April 16, 2020), and the study was registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network–Clinical Trials Registry (registration number, UMIN000057138; date, February 27, 2025). Written informed consent for the use of peripheral blood samples and medical information for research purposes was obtained from all patients.

Treatment

According to the lenvatinib label approved in Japan, patients with mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A) received oral lenvatinib at 12 mg/day (≥ 60 kg) or 8 mg/day (< 60 kg), and patients with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B) received oral lenvatinib at 8 mg/day (≥ 60 kg) or 4 mg/day (< 60 kg). These dosages were determined based on several clinical trials [7, 14]. In this study, the reduction of the initial dose was allowed. Dose interruptions followed by dose reductions due to lenvatinib-related toxicities (to 8 mg and 4 mg/day or 4 mg every other day) were permitted. Treatment was continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or refusal by the patient.

Efficacy and Toxicity Evaluations

Tumor response was assessed according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) version 1.1 for at least 2 months using computed tomography imaging. Efficacy was evaluated based on overall response rate and PFS. PFS was defined as the interval between the date of the first lenvatinib administration and the date of disease progression as determined with the mRECIST, or the date of death from any cause. The same imaging method was used for baseline tumor measurements and tumor reassessment. Clinical and laboratory adverse events were classified based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0, for 2 months.

Blood Sampling for Pharmacokinetic Analysis

Blood samples for pharmacokinetic analysis of lenvatinib were obtained on day 1 of the first treatment. Blood samples were collected immediately before and at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, and 48 h after lenvatinib administration. Accordingly, the second dose of lenvatinib was omitted. The samples were immediately centrifuged to prepare plasma.

Equilibrium Dialysis of Plasma Samples

The unbound fraction of lenvatinib in plasma was determined with a single-use rapid equilibrium dialysis (RED) device (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using plasma samples collected 4 h after the initial administration of lenvatinib. Briefly, after centrifugations (21,500g, 10 min, 4 °C), 100 µL of the supernatants of the plasma samples and 300 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were placed into the opposing donor and acceptor reservoirs, respectively, and dialyzed at 37 °C for 18 h. Fifty microliters of buffer dialysate was collected from the acceptor reservoir and mixed with 5 μL of blank plasma, whereas 5 μL of plasma dialysate was collected from the donor reservoir and mixed with 50 μL of PBS to match the matrix composition between the two reservoir samples. The dialysis procedure was performed in duplicate for each patient, and the mean values were used for the determination of the unbound fraction, which was calculated by dividing the unbound by the total concentration of lenvatinib. The recovery of lenvatinib in the RED device was experimentally measured when PBS was used in both of the chambers and was 39.2 ± 2.6% (n = 3). Although this suggests substantial adsorption of lenvatinib to the RED apparatus, no further correction of the unbound fraction was performed since both total and unbound concentrations of lenvatinib were directly measured in this analysis.

Determination of Total and Unbound Plasma Concentrations of Lenvatinib

The lenvatinib plasma concentrations were determined using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Thirty microliters of plasma sample were mixed with 10 µL of methanol containing 500 nM erlotinib (internal standard) and 110 µL of methanol, followed by centrifugation at 21,500g for 10 min at 4 °C twice. Fifty microliters of dialysate sample were mixed with 10 µL of 300 nM erlotinib and 290 µL of methanol. After centrifugation at 21,500g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were completely dried up with a centrifugal evaporator and reconstituted in 100 μL of 50% methanol. A 10-µL aliquot of sample solution was injected into a liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry system (LCMS-8050, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Chromatography was carried out at 50 °C with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (solvent B). A 6-min run was carried out on a COSMOSIL 2.5C18-MS-II packed column (2.0 mm I.D. × 100 mm, 3-μm particle size; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) using a linear gradient of 95–35% (0.3–3.3 min), followed by 35–5% (3.3–3.5 min), 5% (3.5–4.5 min), 5–95% (4.5–4.6 min), and 95% (4.6–5.5 min) of solvent A. Multiple reaction monitoring in the positive ion mode was set at 427.1–311.9 for lenvatinib and 394.1–278.1 for erlotinib.

Pharmacokinetic Parameters

Plasma lenvatinib concentration–time data were analyzed using a standard non-compartmental method with WinNonlin, version 8.4, software (Pharsight, Mountain View, CA, USA). The area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC) of lenvatinib from time zero to the last sampling time was calculated using the linear trapezoidal rule (to peak plasma concentration) and the linear-log trapezoidal rule (to the last quantifiable concentration).

Genotyping

Genotyping for CYP3A5*3; ABCG2 421C>A; ABCB1 1236C>T, 2677G>T, A, and 3435C>T; ABCC4 rs3742106 (T>G); and SLCO1B1 388A>G and 521T>C was performed using direct DNA sequencing as described previously [15, 24]. CYP3A4*1G was also analyzed using direct DNA sequencing. The following primers were used for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to amplify the gene fragment containing the polymorphism: forward (5′-CCC AGT GTA CCT CTG AAT TGC-3′) and reverse (5′-CAG AGC CTT CTT ACA TAG-3′). The reaction mixture for amplifying the genomic region consisted of genomic DNA (50 ng), 1×PCR buffer, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM deoxyribonucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), 0.5 μM of each primer, and 0.625 U of AmpliTaq Gold® DNA Polymerase (Roche, Branchburg, NJ, USA) in a final volume of 25 μL. The PCR cycling conditions for amplifying this region were initial denaturation at 94 °C for 15 min, followed by 34 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 40 s, annealing at 51 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The final extension step was performed at 72 °C for 2 min.

Statistical Analysis

Although no a priori sample size calculation was performed, a post hoc calculation was conducted. For the comparison of the primary endpoint (AUCu/dose), the group sizes were 25 and 16 for the Child–Pugh class, and 18 and 23 for the ALBI grade. Under these allocations, the detectable effect sizes (Cohen’s d) at α = 0.05 and β = 0.2 were 0.92 and 0.90, respectively.

The association between AUCu/dose and Child-Pugh class or ALBI grade was analyzed using the Wilcoxon test. Bonferroni correction for these two comparisons of primary endpoints was performed. The correlation or association between AUCu/dose and ALBI score or Child-Pugh class components, and between AUCt/dose and Child-Pugh class or its components, ALBI grade, or ALBI score was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation test or Wilcoxon test. The association between unbound fraction and albumin was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation test. PFS was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Correlations between PFS and categorical variables were assessed using the log-rank tests. The associations between overall or individual toxicities, or RDI, and continuous variables or categorical variables were assessed using Wilcoxon test or Fisher’s exact test. The association between genetic polymorphisms and AUCu/dose or AUCt/dose was analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis test or Wilcoxon test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis was performed for PFS.

All analyses were performed using the JMP software (version 17.0; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Correlations or associations were considered statistically significant when the two-tailed P value was < 0.05.

Results

Forty-one patients were enrolled between May 2020 and January 2024. The observations were performed until the cutoff date, November 30, 2024. Table 1 presents the participants’ characteristics. Twenty-three patients had ALBI grade ≥ 2b, which is associated with impaired liver function [13] and a predictor of poor prognosis [16]. Twenty-eight patients received lenvatinib as first-line therapy.

Efficacy and Toxicity

The median PFS in the 41 patients was 8.0 months (95% confidence interval [CI] 3.0–11) (Fig. 1), slightly longer than that in the REFLECT study (7.4 months; 95% CI 6.9–8.8) [5]. Complete and partial responses and stable disease were observed in three, 13, and 16 patients, respectively. The objective response observed was 41%, which was higher than that observed in the REFLECT study (24.1%) [5]. The toxicities observed in our patients from the initiation of lenvatinib treatment until 2 months are shown in Table 2. Any grade of aspartate aminotransferase increase, thrombocytopenia, hyperbilirubinemia, hypothyroidism, proteinuria, or fatigue was observed at frequencies higher than 50%. Grade 3 and 4 thrombocytopenia was observed in four patients. The frequency of these toxicities was higher than that reported in the REFLECT study [5].

Pharmacokinetics of Lenvatinib

The median AUCt of lenvatinib was 2.52 (0.492–8.13) µM∙h. The median unbound lenvatinib fraction was 0.0290 (0.0165–0.0595). Subsequently, the unbound concentrations of lenvatinib in each patient were calculated by multiplying the total plasma concentrations by the respective unbound fractions measured for the patient. The median AUCu of lenvatinib was 80.6 (18.6–204) nM∙h. Because patients received different doses of lenvatinib, the AUCt/dose and AUCu/dose were calculated. The median AUCt/dose and AUCu/dose values of lenvatinib were 0.154 (0.0525–0.575) h/L and 0.00464 (0.00136–0.0126) h/L, respectively.

Association Between Lenvatinib AUC/Dose Values and Baseline Child-Pugh Classification, Its Components, or ALBI

Patients with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B) showed significantly higher lenvatinib AUCu/dose values than those with mild hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class A) (mean ± SD, 0.00613 ± 0.00245 vs. 0.00442 ± 0.00235 h/L, P = 0.0312; Fig. 2a). Patients with ALBI grade ≥ 2b had significantly higher AUCu/dose than those with < 2b (mean ± SD, 0.00602 ± 0.00268 vs. 0.00390 ± 0.00168 h/L, P = 0.0131; Fig. 2b). The ALBI score was significantly correlated with AUCu/dose (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient 0.415, P = 0.00699; Fig. 2c).

Effects of Child-Pugh Classification, Its Components, Lenvatinib AUC/Dose, or ALBI Grade on PFS

We examined whether PFS was associated with the Child-Pugh classification, its components, lenvatinib AUCt/dose and AUCu/dose values, or ALBI grade ≥ 2b and < 2b. We estimated the PFS in patients with continuous variables—albumin, total bilirubin, prothrombin time–international normalized ratio, and AUCt/dose and AUCu/dose values of lenvatinib—equal to or higher than their respective medians, as well as in those with values lower than the medians. Patients with lower plasma albumin levels showed a significantly shorter median PFS than those with higher plasma albumin levels (3.0 vs. 13 months, P = 0.00133; Fig. 3b). The Child-Pugh classification and other components were not significantly associated with PFS (Fig. 3). AUCt/dose and AUCu/dose values of lenvatinib were not significantly associated with PFS (Fig. 3f and g). Patients with ALBI grade ≥ 2b showed significantly shorter PFS than those with ALBI grade < 2b (3.0 vs. 16 months, P = 0.0000729; Fig. 3h). Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage, a known prognostic factor for HCC [17], was not associated with PFS (see Online Resource 1).

Association Between AUC/Dose Values or ALBI Grade and Lenvatinib-Induced Toxicities

The AUCt/dose and AUCu/dose values in patients with overall toxicity grades ≥ 2 were significantly higher than those observed in other patients (mean ± SD, 0.183 ± 0.096 vs. 0.126 ± 0.051 h/L and 0.00558 ± 0.00261 vs. 0.00376 ± 0.00165 h/L, respectively; P = 0.0284 and 0.0329, respectively) (Table 4). Contrastingly, baseline plasma albumin levels were not associated with toxicity grades ≥ 2, even though plasma albumin was treated as continuous variable or categorical variable with a cutoff value of 3.4 g/dL, which was associated with PFS (Table 4). However, ALBI grade ≥ 2b was significantly associated with a higher incidence of lenvatinib-induced toxicity grades ≥ 2 than ALBI grade < 2b (87.0 vs. 55.6%, P = 0.0357), although the continuous variable of ALBI score was not correlated with toxicity (Table 4). Among lenvatinib-induced individual toxicity grades ≥ 2, constipation was associated with AUCu/dose values, and weakly associated with AUCt/dose (see Online Resource 2).

Association Between AUC/Dose Values, Baseline Albumin, or ALBI Grade and the Relative Dose Intensity of Lenvatinib

The RDI of lenvatinib was evaluated from the initiation of treatment until 2 months, which was the period for monitoring lenvatinib-induced toxicities in this study. RDI was defined as the cumulative (total) dose of lenvatinib divided by the expected total dose during this period. The RDI observed in patients with AUCu/dose values ≥ the median for lenvatinib was significantly lower than that in patients with AUCu/dose values lower than the median (mean ± SD, 71.7 ± 23.9 vs. 85.9 ± 21.8%, P = 0.0230, Fig. 4b). In contrast, significant positive associations were observed between RDI and plasma albumin (mean ± SD, 69.8 ± 23.1 vs. 87.0 ± 21.6%, P = 0.00587, Fig. 4c). Patients with ALBI grade ≥ 2b had significantly lower RDI than those with ALBI grade < 2b (mean ± SD, 71.0 ± 23.3 vs. 88.3 ± 21.1%, P = 0.00600, Fig. 4d). RDI significantly correlated with PFS (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient 0.390, P = 0.0117) (Fig. 4e), and was significantly associated with lenvatinib-induced overall toxicity grades ≥ 2 (P = 0.00188) (Fig. 4f).

Discussion

As initially hypothesized, this study demonstrated that baseline plasma albumin levels were significantly associated with the AUCu/dose, but not with the AUCt/dose of lenvatinib (Fig. 2). Although Child-Pugh classification was also significantly associated with the AUCu/dose, other components of the Child-Pugh classification were not associated with the AUCu/dose. This is probably because ALBI is a function of plasma albumin level, and both ALBI score and grade were significantly correlated and associated with the AUCu/dose, respectively, but not with AUCt/dose of lenvatinib.

Because unbound drug molecules, but not albumin-bound drug molecules, can penetrate cell membranes and exert clinical response, Child-Pugh classification, categorical baseline plasma albumin level, and ALBI grade, associated with unbound plasma levels of lenvatinib, were considered to be associated with lenvatinib clinical response. However, only the ALBI grade ≥ 2b and categorical baseline plasma albumin level, but not Child-Pugh classification, were significantly associated with PFS (Fig. 3). Patients with ALBI grade ≥ 2b or lower baseline albumin levels had reduced systemic and hepatic functions, including decompensation and impaired hepatic reserve capacity, and poor prognosis [13, 16, 19, 20], which may shorten the PFS. In contrast, the RDIs evaluated for 2 months after lenvatinib initiation in patients with ALBI grade ≥ 2b and lower baseline albumin levels were significantly lower than those in other patients (Fig. 4). Additionally, PFS and RDI were significantly positively correlated (Fig. 4e). Therefore, ALBI grade ≥ 2b and lower baseline albumin levels can also cause the shorter PFS by decreasing the RDI, which was at least partly caused by overall toxicity grade ≥ 2. These results indicate that ALBI grade divided by a threshold of 2b and baseline plasma albumin level are predictive markers for PFS in patients treated with lenvatinib.

This study found that patients who had a lenvatinib-induced overall toxicity grade ≥ 2 showed significantly higher AUCu/dose than those with an overall toxicity grade < 2 (Table 4). ALBI grade ≥ 2b, which was associated with significantly higher AUCu/dose of lenvatinib, was associated with a significantly higher incidence of overall toxicity grade ≥ 2 (Table 4 and Fig. 2). The RDI of lenvatinib observed during the same period of toxicity evaluation was significantly lower in patients with an AUCu/dose ≥ the median or with ALBI grade ≥ 2b than in other patients (Fig. 4). Additionally, reduced RDI was directly significantly associated with lenvatinib-induced overall toxicity grades ≥ 2 (Fig. 4). These results suggest that lenvatinib-induced overall toxicity grades ≥ 2 lead to dose reduction or treatment discontinuation of lenvatinib. Additionally, reduced systemic and hepatic functions in patients with ALBI grade ≥ 2b may also be associated with the higher incidence of overall toxicity grades ≥ 2. However, the Child-Pugh classification and its components, including albumin level, were not associated with a lenvatinib-induced overall toxicity grade ≥ 2, indicating that ALBI grade ≥ 2b is a better predictor of lenvatinib-induced toxicities than the Child-Pugh classification and baseline plasma albumin level. Baseline plasma albumin may be indirectly associated with the toxicities via its association with AUCu/dose or RDI (Fig. 2 and 4). Based on these findings, ALBI grade divided by a threshold of 2b rather than baseline plasma albumin level and Child-Pugh classification may be a predictive marker for both lenvatinib efficacy and toxicity, suggesting that ALBI grade-based dosing, rather than the present Child-Pugh classification-based dosing, may be rational. This idea is also supported by the fact that the ALBI grade is a marker of liver function and poor prognosis [19, 20]. However, further investigations are necessary.

Patients with HCC frequently have impaired hepatic function to exhibit (1) reduced plasma albumin levels, resulting in lower lenvatinib protein binding, and (2) reduced hepatic elimination of lenvatinib. Decreased plasma protein binding and hepatic elimination result in an increase in the plasma unbound fraction (fu) and hepatic intrinsic clearance (CLint,h), respectively. Nevertheless, we found that the AUCu/dose, but not the AUCt/dose, was negatively correlated with baseline plasma albumin levels (Fig. 2). According to the pharmacokinetic theory, AUCt/dose is affected by both fu and CLint,h, and therefore, the reason for no correlation of AUCt/dose with fu may be explained by mutual compensation between fu and CLint,h, implying that plasma protein binding of lenvatinib may affect the hepatic elimination potential. This may be through multiple mechanisms, such as the reduced intrahepatic drug delivery during liver dysfunction, direct effects of hypoalbuminemia on drug-metabolizing enzyme expression, and/or simply albumin as a biomarker of overall hepatocellular function. Notably, in this study, the unbound fraction of lenvatinib negatively correlated with baseline plasma albumin levels (Fig. 2). In this study, albumin concentration was associated with AUCu/dose, although theoretically, AUCu/dose is solely affected by CLint,h. Therefore, it can be hypothesized that, when treating patients with HCC with lenvatinib, lower albumin concentrations could be associated with lower CLint,h, leading to higher AUCu/dose. However, directly measuring the CLint,h values of lenvatinib in the patients was difficult, and further studies are required to confirm this hypothesis.

A clear significant association between ABCG2 421C>A and systemic exposure to lenvatinib (see Online Resource 4 and 5) was not observed. Consistent with our finding, a study with Japanese patients with HCC presenting with Child-Pugh A classification showed no significant association between ABCG2 421C>A and lenvatinib systemic exposure on day 1 [21]. In other studies, associations between CYP3A4*1G genotype and mean steady-state dose-adjusted plasma trough concentrations of lenvatinib, and ABCB1 3435C>T and systemic exposure to lenvatinib were observed [22, 23]. However, we did not observe positive associations between these polymorphisms and AUC/dose values. These previous studies involved Japanese patients with thyroid cancer who did not have serious hepatic dysfunction [23] and Chinese healthy participants [22]. Changes in the capacity of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters potentially caused by hepatic impairment in patients with HCC may affect the roles of polymorphisms of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters.

This study had certain limitations. The sample size was relatively small. Additionally, the findings were not validated. Therefore, further authentication with a larger sample size is necessary to substantiate the application of the findings to clinical practice. Because this study was performed in a single Japanese institution, the results apply exclusively to Japanese patients, which might limit the generalizability to non-Asian populations where lenvatinib protein binding characteristics and genetic variant frequencies may differ. A reduction in the initial lenvatinib dose was permitted by physicians according to the patients’ conditions; however, the effects of the initial dose reduction on PFS or lenvatinib-induced toxicities could not be analyzed. Furthermore, the unbound fraction was determined from a plasma sample collected only 4 h after initial administration according to the following considerations: Because the half-life of plasma albumin, which influences the unbound fraction of lenvatinib [12], is approximately 21 days, changes in plasma albumin concentrations might be minimal during the 48 h of the plasma sampling period in patients with good performance status (0 or 1), unless they suffer severe symptoms such as infection. However, plasma protein binding of lenvatinib may possibly change during 48 h. This may be attributed to the fact that because participants of this real-world clinical study were not strictly restricted for food after the administration of lenvatinib, patients probably must have had meals as well as snacks, which may have modified the plasma free fatty acids concentrations to potentially alter plasma protein binding of lenvatinib during 48 h.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed for the first time that the ALBI grades divided by a threshold of 2b, rather than Child-Pugh classification, may be a predictive marker for lenvatinib efficacy and toxicity in Japanese patients with HCC who have impaired hepatic function with Child-Pugh class A or B classification.
