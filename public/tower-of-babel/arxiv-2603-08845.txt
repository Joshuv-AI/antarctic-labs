Time delocalization and causality across temporal quantum reference frames
Veronika Baumann, Maximilian P. E. Lock

Abstract
In relational quantum dynamics, evolution emerges via the correlations between some system of interest and a clock system, which plays the role of a temporal reference frame. Their combined state satisfies a Wheeler-de Witt-like constraint equation, and therefore does not evolve, leading to a “block universe” picture. Here we investigate the interplay of two aspects, namely temporal localization and causal relations, when comparing emergent dynamics with respect to different choices of clock. We first explore the extent to which two clocks can agree on the temporal localization of events. Then, focussing on the operational notion of causality, we require a clearly defined notion of interventions, i.e. quantum operations, and consider two different approaches to modeling these operations within relational dynamics. The first considers their application via the choice of solutions to the constraint equation, i.e. the choice of which “history” is considered. The second approach incorporates the operations into the constraint equation itself and thereby into its solutions, giving a dynamical picture of the interventions. From the perspective of a single clock, both approaches allow for a notion of operational causality in relational dynamics. However, for multiple clocks, only the second approach gives a consistent picture regarding causal relations, while necessarily manifesting some degree of temporal delocalization between frames. Moreover, this second approach, when considering certain cases of temporal delocalization, naturally describes scenarios with indefinite causal order, a well-known quantum feature of operational causality.

I
Introduction

By describing the properties of one quantum system relative to another, treated as a quantum reference frame (QRF), we can construct a relational picture of physics that minimizes reliance on idealized background structures such as classical spacetime coordinates. When applied to time, this perspective offers a compelling route for addressing the “problem of time” in canonical quantum gravity
48
;
33
;
35
. More broadly, QRFs provide a platform for constructing theories in which space and time emerge from the relations among physical systems, breathing new life into the longstanding philosophical debate between the relational and the substantivalist understandings of spacetime
56
;
4
;
1
.

However, the pursuit of this program raises significant challenges. Unlike classical reference frames, different choices of QRF can lead to radically different descriptions of physical systems, which in the context of temporal QRFs is known as the “multiple clock problem”
35
. Crucially, different choices of clock lead to different tensor factorizations of the Hilbert space, and in some cases to the loss of distinction between subsystems
2
. While one may adopt a fully clock-neutral perspective and interpret these problems as simply features of a relational world, the operational comparison of events in different frames remains an open problem
34
, particularly since the joint temporal localizability of events required for such a comparison seems to be ruled out by the gravitational interaction
9
.
Indeed, this issue becomes especially acute in a gravitational context, as spacetime geometry is (up to a conformal factor) determined by the causal relations of the events comprising that spacetime
36
, leading to the hypothesis that quantum gravity extends quantum indeterminacy to causal structure itself
23
;
24
;
12
;
38
;
55
. How, then, can causal structure be understood within in a fully relational quantum framework, and how can it be compared across reference frames, particularly given the lack of joint localizability mentioned above?

A subtlety arises here due to inequivalent but overlapping notions of causality. Relativistic causality is associated with the situation of events in the past and future light-cones of each other, expressed in the context of quantum field theory via microcausality conditions, i.e. the commutation or anti-commutation of observables at spacelike separations. In contrast, information-theoretic approaches define causality in operational terms via the correlations between inputs and outputs of a given process (e.g. the dynamical evolution of some system), for example by considering the effect of interventions on a given input to the process
44
;
3
;
5
. The study of indefinite causal order is situated within a similarly operational setting
12
;
38
. The difference between the two notions of causality is highlighted by the fact that a processes exhibiting indefinite causal order in the operational sense can be embedded in a fixed spacetime
60
;
59
.

Some first steps have been made in the study of causal relations in a relational setting, such as in recent work exploring how the identification of spacetime points becomes frame-dependent in quantum gravity
34
, and how the definite or indefinite character of causal order, defined through proper-time differences between worldline intersections, remains invariant under changes of quantum reference frame
16
. In the context of field theory, a relational description can provide an alternative to
28
, or a means to recover
8
;
26
, relativistic microcausality conditions.

Here, we will focus on the operational notion of causality, specifically its embedding into relational dynamics, and its intersection with the problem of temporal localization. We investigate the extent to which different reference frames can agree about the temporal localization of an event, and how they can disagree about the direction of time, the ordering of operations, and the causal interpretation of interventions. We adopt the relational picture afforded by the Page-Wootters formalism
39
;
62
;
40
, whose equivalence to other formalisms for relational dynamics has been explored in
31
;
29
;
11
. We study how an intervention can be modeled in this picture, and the necessary role of temporal localization as well as its implications for causal explanations across temporal reference frames.
We begin by briefly reviewing the Page-Wootters formalism in Sec.
II
. In Sec.
III
we discuss in more detail the concept of time delocalization when dealing with multiple temporal reference frames and in Sec.
IV
we examine whether the order of operations can, nevertheless, be reference-frame-dependent. In Sec.
V
we review the operational approach to causal relations before presenting a naive attempt to incorporate it into relational dynamics. The latter turns out to be problematic when comparing the perspective of different clocks, as we show in Sec.
V.2
. Consequently we discuss how clock-system interaction terms in the constraint operator can model timed interventions in the Page-Wootters formalism in Sec.
VI

VII
.

II
The Page-Wootters formalism

The Page-Wootters formalism describes the evolution of one or more quantum systems in relation to another one, which acts as a temporal reference frame i.e. a clock
39
;
62
;
40
. Dynamics emerge from a Wheeler-DeWitt-like constraint equation as we explain below. We then discuss important aspects of considering multiple clock systems with respect to which relational dynamics can be obtained, and how the evolution depends on which clock one chooses as the reference frame.

II.1
Evolution relative to a single clock

The Page-Wootters formalism was introduced in response to the problem of time in canonical quantum gravity

;
35

\mathcal{K}

II.2
Relational dynamics with multiple clocks

We now discuss the Page-Wootters formalism with
multiple clocks
, i.e the scenario where there are multiple quantum systems that we may choose as temporal reference frames, and review how to change between these different frames. We therefore consider the kinematical Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}=\bigotimes_{i}\mathcal{H}_{C_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{S}
and constraint operators of the form

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{C_{i}}+\hat{H}_{\rm rest},

(11)

where
\hat{H}_{\rm rest}
can act on systems other than the clocks as well as contain coupling terms. We can now apply the considerations from Sec.
II.1
to each clock individually.
For a given choice of clock, say
C_{i}

1

\displaystyle|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}}\,dt_{i}|t_{i}\rangle_{C_{i}}|\psi_{|C_{i}}(t_{i})\rangle_{\lnot C_{i}},

(12)

is the eigenstate of a time observable associated with clock

3

\bigotimes_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\mathcal{H}_{C_{i}^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{S}

,
which give the evolution of everything but clock

C_{i}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{i}(\tau_{i})=\langle\tau_{i}|_{C_{i}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\lnot C_{i}},

\mathcal{H}_{\rm phys}
to the space of conditional states of clock
C_{i}
at time

\mathcal{H}_{|C_{i}}\simeq\bigotimes_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\mathcal{H}_{C_{i}^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{S}

III
A limit to joint temporal localizability and clock synchronization

We saw above how, in the multiple-clock scenario, the Page-Wootters formalism leads to reference-frame-dependent relational dynamics. Importantly the relational evolution of one clock with respect to another includes some
necessary time delocalization
, even in the absence of clock-system interactions (cf.
9
, which examines the time delocalization induced by clock-system interactions). Consider the simplest example of a multiple-clock scenario, namely two clocks
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
and a third system
S
, i.e.
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}_{C_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{C_{2}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{S}

II.1
, and write an arbitrary kinematical states
|\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle|\phi\rangle=\int_{\mathds{R}}dt_{1}\,dt_{2}\,\sumint_{\;\lambda}\varphi(t_{1},t_{2},\lambda)|t_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|t_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}|\lambda\rangle_{S},

(21)

where

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}}ds\,dt\,dt^{\prime}\,\sumint_{\;\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\varphi^{*}(t^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})\varphi(t,\lambda)\delta^{2}(t+s-t^{\prime})\langle\lambda^{\prime}|e^{is\hat{H}_{S}}|\lambda\rangle\propto\delta(0)\,.

\langle\psi_{|C_{i}}(\tau_{i})|\psi_{|C_{i}}(\tau_{i})\rangle\propto\delta(0)

. Hence, normalizable physical states — and consequently conditional states — require that in the frame of one clock, another clock is necessarily
time-delocalized
to some extent.

As in Sec.
II.1
for the case of a single clock system, we can generate examples of physical states from (non-normalizable) kinematical states of the form
|\phi\rangle=|t_{0}\rangle_{C_{1}}\int dt\,\varphi(t,t_{0})|t\rangle_{C_{2}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}
. Now
\varphi(t,t_{0})
describes the time delocalization of
C_{2}
conditioned on
C_{1}
being in state
|t_{0}\rangle
. We have then “shifted” the necessary time delocalization to clock
C_{2}
only. To simplify things further, we will choose
t_{0}=0
, and denote
\varphi(t)\coloneqq\varphi(t,0)
in what follows. If we then want the clocks to “read the same time at
t=0
”, the function
\varphi(t)
should be sharply peaked around
0
.

Since the divergence in Eq. (
24
) arises due to our assumption of ideal clocks, one may ask if the limit to clock synchronization continues to hold outside of this assumption. Consider for a moment non-ideal clocks, such that
\chi(t-t^{\prime})\coloneqq\langle t|t^{\prime}\rangle\neq\delta(t-t^{\prime})
(see
31
, Sec. III). In the cases where
\chi(0)
is finite, e.g. continuous-spectrum clocks with a doubly-bounded spectrum or discrete-spectrum clocks with a spectrum bounded below, we can normalize the physical states
|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle
in Eq. (
24
). However, in each temporal reference frame, the other clock will still appear delocalized in time, i.e.
|\psi_{|C_{i}}(\tau_{i})\rangle=\langle\tau_{i}|\Psi\rangle\rangle
does not contain a sharp value on clock
C_{j\neq i}
.

IV
Frame-dependent evolution and ordering

We now investigate the extent to which relational dynamics permits disagreement between reference frames about the direction of time and the ordering of operations. We consider a simple example which illustrates the limited extent to which these features are permitted. Consider the above scenario of two ideal clocks and a system
S
, where now the two clocks run in opposite directions, which is equivalent to writing

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\hat{H}_{C_{1}}-\hat{H}_{C_{2}}+\hat{H}_{S}.

(25)

As discussed in Sec.
III
, we can describe the situation where the two clock are approximately synchronized at
t=0
by using Eq. (

|\phi\rangle=|t_{0}=0\rangle_{C_{1}}\int dt\varphi(t)|t\rangle_{C_{2}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}
, where
\varphi(t)
is sharply peaked around

\displaystyle|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}}ds\,dt\,\varphi(t)e^{-is\hat{C}}|0\rangle_{C_{1}}|t\rangle_{C_{2}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}}ds\,dt\,\varphi(t)|s\rangle_{C_{1}}|t-s\rangle_{C_{2}}e^{-is\hat{H}_{S}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle|\psi_{|C_{1}}(\tau_{1})\rangle=\langle\tau_{1}|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=\int dt\,\varphi(t)|t-\tau_{1}\rangle_{C_{2}}e^{-i\tau_{1}\hat{H}_{S}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle|\psi_{|C_{2}}(\tau_{2})\rangle=\langle\tau_{2}|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=\int dt\,\varphi(t)|t-\tau_{2}\rangle_{C_{1}}e^{i(\tau_{2}-t)\hat{H}_{S}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}\,,

However, the temporal correlations between the system and the remaining clock are different in the two reference frames due to the time delocalization. In particular, despite choosing opposing clocks in this manner, we do not obtain frames which are a perfect time-reversal of each other. To understand this better, let us consider the dynamics of the system alone in each temporal reference frame, i.e.

\displaystyle\rho_{S|C_{1}}(\tau_{1})

\displaystyle:=\text{Tr}_{C_{2}}\left(|\psi_{|C_{1}}(\tau_{1})\rangle\langle\psi_{|C_{1}}(\tau_{1})|\right)=e^{-i\tau_{1}\hat{H}_{S}}|\phi_{0}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{0}|_{S}e^{i\tau_{1}\hat{H}_{S}}

(29)

\displaystyle\rho_{S|C_{2}}(\tau_{2})

\displaystyle:=\text{Tr}_{C_{1}}\left(|\psi_{|C_{2}}(\tau_{2})\rangle\langle\psi_{|C_{2}}(\tau_{2})|\right)=\int dt\,|\varphi(t)|^{2}e^{i(\tau_{2}-t)\hat{H}_{S}}|\phi_{0}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{0}|_{S}e^{-i(\tau_{2}-t)\hat{H}_{S}}=e^{i\tau_{2}\hat{H}_{S}}\rho_{S|C_{2}}(0)e^{-i\tau_{2}\hat{H}_{S}}.

(30)

Outside of the chaotic case discussed above, one can ask if it is possible to contrive an example where the two clocks not only see the same initial state evolve in different directions in time, but in fact see events occurring in opposite orders. V
Operational causality and the Page-Wootters formalism

As stated in Sec.
I

quantum operations

After briefly presenting this operational approach to causality in quantum theory, we show how a first, ad hoc attempt of incorporating it into the Page-Wootters formalism breaks down when considering multiple temporal frames, i.e. clock systems.

Interventions in the Page-Wootters formalism

VI.1
Timed interventions via clock-system interaction

In Sec.
V
, the presence or absence of an intervention corresponded to alternative choices of physical state (analogous to different initial conditions in a dynamical theory). Here we show how the subsystem-delocalization problem encountered there can be solved by considering a more refined notion of interventions, namely their encoding as dynamical operations, via the constraint operator. Thus one can maintain subsystem-locality across perspectives by explicitly modeling the temporal locality of the interventions.

To do this, we introduce tailored interaction terms between a clock and the subsystem that a given operation is to be applied to. Let us start by briefly reviewing clock-system interactions within the Page-Wootters formalism
21
;
52
;
9
. In general such interactions may result in non-unitary evolution of the system with respect to the clock
41
;
42
;
25
;
47
. However, as shown in
52
, clock-system interaction terms of the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm int}=\int\,dt\,f(t)|t\rangle\!\langle t|_{C}\otimes\hat{K}_{S}=:\hat{f}(\hat{T})_{C}\otimes\hat{K}_{S}

(48)

where the time states
|t\rangle
are defined as in Sec.
II.1
, can model unitary relational dynamics with an emergent time-dependent Hamiltonian. As we explain in detail in Appendix
C
, using methods from
9
, constraint operators of the form

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\hat{H}_{C}+\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{f}(\hat{T})_{C}\otimes\hat{K}_{S}\;

(49)

lead to well defined physical states. Specifically, the elements of the group generated by
\hat{C}
can be calculated via a Dyson series (cf. Sec. 3.2 in
52
), from which one can obtain physical states via Eq. (
5
). Here we consider
approximately
instantaneous interventions, in the sense that
f(t)
is well-localized around some value
\overline{\tau}
and can be treated as
\delta(t-\overline{\tau})
in the Dyson series governing the relational evolution (see Appendix
C
). We denote this case by

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\hat{H}_{C}+\hat{H}_{S}+\delta(\hat{T}-\overline{\tau})_{C}\otimes\hat{K}_{S}\;

(50)

and obtain physical states

|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\bar{\tau}}dt|t\rangle_{C}\,e^{-it\hat{H}_{S}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\bar{\tau}}^{\infty}dt|t\rangle_{C}\,e^{-i(t-\bar{\tau})\hat{H}_{S}}e^{-i\hat{K}_{S}}\,e^{-i\bar{\tau}\hat{H}_{S}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S},

(51)

which describe the free evolution of the system according to
\hat{H}_{S}
with the application of
e^{-i\hat{K}_{S}}
at time
\overline{\tau}
. We stress, however, that Eq. (
50
) is an abuse of notation, representing the case of non-zero but negligible delocalization of the function
f(t)
; otherwise, since
\delta^{n}(x)
is not a well-defined distribution for
n>1
, the exponential of Eq. (
50
) diverges. Eq. (
51
) should thus be understood as an approximation which neglects the finite width of the function
f(t)
. In
21
the authors used such instantaneous interventions to describe von Neumann measurements
61
;
37
within the Page-Wootters formalism, obtaining Eq. (
51
) in that context (cf. Eq. (34) in
21
).

To consider general quantum operations, we can again let the system
S
be comprised of various subsystems
A,B,\dots X
and ancillary systems
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},\dots
. The subsystems
A,B,\dots
represent the factors to which the operations are to be applied, while
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}\dots
provide the necessary additional space for the Stinespring dilations of the relevant CPTP maps (
A^{\prime}
for dilating the operation on
A
, and so on). The system
X
, included for generality, allows for a purification of the states which one wishes to embed into the Page-Wootters formalism, as well as a Stinespring dilation of the CPTP map whose causal properties we wish to investigate; for simplicity this will be omitted in the following, as we focus here on the modeling of interventions. Letting
N=A,B,\dots
, the unitary operator dilating a given operation on factor
N
will be enacted by including the appropriate generator
K_{NN^{\prime}}
in the constraint. Specifically the operator
U_{NN^{\prime}}\coloneqq e^{-iK_{NN^{\prime}}}
will represent a dilation of the map
\mathcal{M}_{N}
on
N

In the case where the operation is an observable
\mathcal{O}_{N}
, this corresponds to a von Neumann measurement, correlating
N
with its ancilla
N^{\prime}
in the eigenbasis of
\mathcal{O}_{N}
.

VI.2
Timed interventions and operational causality

Incorporating the interventions necessary for investigating causality (see Sec.
V.1
), into the constraint operator, the general form for arbitrarily many clocks and interventions is

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\sum_{j}\hat{H}_{C_{j}}+\hat{H}_{S}+\sum_{jl}\hat{f}(\hat{T}_{j})\otimes K_{A_{l}A^{\prime}_{l}},

(56)

where
j
labels the different clocks, and thus
\hat{T}_{j}
refers to the time operator of clock
C_{j}
. The different subsystems involved in the evolution are labeled
l
and
l^{\prime}
denotes the corresponding ancilla systems, such that
K_{A_{l}A^{\prime}_{l}}
generates the Stinespring dilations of the map to be applied to system
l
. While the approach to investigating causal relations described in Sec.
V.2
entailed a comparison of different solutions to a single constraint equation, here one compares
different constraints
. These constraints differ only in the operators
K_{A_{l}A^{\prime}_{l}}
corresponding to different operations
\mathcal{M}_{A_{l}}
being applied. From this perspective, investigating causal relations within relational dynamics amounts to comparing representatives of a fixed class of constraint equations and their solutions.

Consider the simplest example, where
S
consists of two subsystems
A
and
B
and their respective ancillas
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
, and with two approximately instantaneous interventions, one applied to subsystem
A
followed by the other to subsystem
B
, as timed by a single clock
C
. This corresponds to a constraint operator of the form

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\hat{H}_{C}+\hat{H}_{S}+\delta(\hat{T}-\tau_{i})K_{AA^{\prime}}+\delta(\hat{T}-\tau_{f})K_{BB^{\prime}},

(57)

where
\tau_{i}<\tau_{f}
are the initial and final time according to clock
C
. As before, the Dirac delta notation in Eq. (
57
) represents the case where the interventions have some finite but negligible duration centered on
\tau_{i}
and
\tau_{f}
. The class of constraint operators above describes a scenario where
\mathcal{M}_{A}
is applied to
A
at clock-time
\tau_{i}
(via its dilation
U_{AA^{\prime}}\coloneqq e^{-iK_{AA^{\prime}}}
on
A
and
A^{\prime}
) and
\mathcal{M}_{B}
at final clock-time
\tau_{f}>\tau_{i}
(via its dilation
U_{BB^{\prime}}\coloneqq e^{-iK_{BB^{\prime}}}
on
B
and
B^{\prime}
). Note that, while the evolution due to
\hat{H}_{S}
may allow for a bidirectional causal relationship between
A
and
B
, constraint operators of the form of Eq.(
57
) correspond to the question of whether
A
can causally influence
B
but do not tell us anything about the converse — to interrogate whether
B
can influence
A
, one must invert the timings of the interventions.

We will again consider the case where
\mathcal{M}_{B}=\mathcal{O}_{B}
, and compare the situation where some arbitrary
\mathcal{M}_{A}
is applied to
A
with the situation where no intervention on
A
occurs. The latter case can be obtained by setting
K_{AA^{\prime}}=0
in Eq. (
57
), explicitly:

\hat{C}^{\prime}=\hat{H}_{C}+\hat{H}_{S}+\delta(\hat{T}-\tau_{f})K_{BB^{\prime}}.

(58)

Beginning with the case of an arbitrary intervention on
A
, and recalling that in the case of a single clock, an arbitrary physical state can be obtained via a kinematical state of the form
|t=0\rangle_{C}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}
(see Sec.
II.1
), the method in Appendix
C
can again be applied to find the physical states generated by Eq. (
57
):

\displaystyle|{\Psi^{A}}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\tau_{i}}dt\,|t\rangle_{C}e^{-it\hat{H}_{S}}+\int_{\tau_{i}}^{\tau_{f}}dt\,|t\rangle_{C}e^{-i(t-\tau_{i})\hat{H}_{S}}U_{AA^{\prime}}e^{-i\tau_{i}\hat{H}_{S}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{\tau_{f}}^{\infty}dt\,|t\rangle_{C}e^{-i(t-\tau_{f})\hat{H}_{S}}U_{BB^{\prime}}e^{-i(\tau_{f}-\tau_{i})\hat{H}_{S}}U_{AA^{\prime}}e^{-i\tau_{i}\hat{H}_{S}}\right)|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S},

(59)

where the superscript
A
in
|{\Psi^{A}}\rangle\rangle
again denotes that an intervention is made on
A
, and we recall that
U_{AA^{\prime}}
and
U_{BB^{\prime}}
are the unitary dilations of
\mathcal{M}_{A}
and
\mathcal{O}_{B}
respectively.
We thus obtain the following expression for the conditional system states

\displaystyle|\psi^{A}_{|C}(\tau)\rangle=\langle\tau|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=\mathcal{U}^{A}_{|C}(\tau)|\phi_{0}\rangle,

(60)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{A}_{|C}(\tau)=\begin{cases}e^{-i\tau\hat{H}_{S}}&\tau\leq\tau_{i}\\
e^{-i(\tau-\tau_{i})\hat{H}_{S}}U_{AA^{\prime}}e^{-i\tau_{i}\hat{H}_{S}}&\tau_{i}\leq\tau\leq\tau_{f}\\
e^{-i(\tau-\tau_{f})\hat{H}_{S}}U_{BB^{\prime}}e^{-i(\tau_{f}-\tau_{i})\hat{H}_{S}}U_{AA^{\prime}}e^{-i\tau_{i}\hat{H}_{S}}&\tau_{f}\leq\tau.\end{cases}

(61)

Note that the timed operations are now part of the overall evolution of the system, and when relating this scenario to the one discussed in Sec.
V.1
we identify only the evolution in between these operations, i.e.
e^{-i(\tau_{f}-\tau_{i})\hat{H}_{S}}
in this case, with the CPTP map
\mathcal{E}
in Fig.

VI.3
Relational causality with multiple clocks

We now show how, when multiple clocks are considered, the conditional states relative to different clocks can retain an interpretation of a system-local intervention having occurred, and thus infer causal properties from those interventions. Specifically, we consider two clock systems
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, and distinguish the case where the two interventions of interest are timed by the same clock from those where each clock times one interventions. The latter has the clear advantage that the clock cannot carry a causal influence from
A
to
B
, and also permits examples with an indefinite ordering of quantum operations, as we will show in Sec.
VI.4
.

The case where both interventions are timed by clock
C_{1}

VI.2

\tau_{i}

VII
Discussion and conclusion

In this work we have investigated the modeling of interventions and measurements in the Page-Wootters formalism, and how the resulting understanding of causal relations depends upon the choice of clock, in particular the notions of time-locality and subsystem-locality according to those clocks. First we have elucidated a necessary temporal delocalization when comparing different frames, though in the absence of other factors (for example certain clock-system interactions
9
) one may consider this delocalization to be arbitrarily small for ideal clocks. This exemplifies the necessity of explicitly considering the form of correlations between clocks when comparing them as choices of reference frame, a feature which is absent in the abstract changes of “external” frame usually encountered in physics. To avoid a potential source of confusion, we stress that the delocalization discussed here is different in nature from the time-nonlocal evolution that can result from clock-system interactions
52
.

In light of the fact that two clocks may disagree not only on the specific time of an event, but indeed whether it occurs at a well-defined time at all, we examined the possibility of two clocks disagreeing on the direction of time, showing that this may only occur in an approximate sense. Under this approximation, we found that if those clocks are to disagree about the order of two spontaneous processes (i.e. not triggered by interactions with the clocks), then those processes must be time-symmetric.

Moving on to the relational modeling of interventions, we began with an approach whereby, for example, the difference between the occurrence or not of some intervention was understood as a comparison between two different solutions to the same constraint equation. In this manner, the counterfactual reasoning used to determine causal relations is incorporated as a comparison of different “alternate histories”: one in which the intervention occurred, and one in which it did not. We found that, while this is workable in a particular reference frame, the relativity of subsystems
2
means that the corresponding state-comparison in a different frame does not allow for an interpretation in terms of causal influences from one party to another.

In order to obtain a picture where causal relations are independent of the chosen clock, one must therefore incorporate the interventions at the level of the constraint operator. Adapting methods from
52
;
9
, we have shown how this results in the preservation of the subsystem locality of an intervention or measurement across perspectives. However the extent of its joint localizablity in time is determined by the specific form of correlations between the clocks considered. For states where the necessary temporal delocalization between clocks is negligible, interventions are approximately time-local in both frames, in addition to being subsystem-local. If the temporal delocalization is small (compared to the timescales of the other processes) but non-negligible, however, one finds some temporal smearing of the relevant processes in one or another frame.

Considering then the more extreme case of delocalization between clock states, one can obtain a situation where the ordering of some operations is indefinite, and describe a relational version of the quantum switch in this regime. Note that, while each clock may disagree about the particulars, they do not disagree that the order of operations is indefinite, in keeping with
16
. Further note that, in the relational picture here, the quantum switch arose in part due to a tailored choice of physical state (via a corresponding kinematical state), where the time delocalization
\varphi(t_{2})
plays the role of the control. In
9
, on the other hand, time delocalization between the two clocks is brought about by gravitational interaction with a mass in spatial superposition, which constitutes an actual control system. Hence, the scenario in
9
describes a “physical” quantum switch, while the relational evolution discussed in Sec.
VI.4
describes how a certain choice of “history” (i.e. physical state) allows for this type of indefinite causal order.

The common assumption of ideal clocks, which have the unphysical property that their energy spectrum is unbounded below
43
;
20
, was made throughout this paper except where otherwise stated. If this assumption were relaxed, the “interaction-picture” method described in Appendix
C
would no longer be applicable, at least in its current form, and it is unclear exactly how the conclusions above would change. Indeed in
25
, the authors showed how a measurement described with respect to a non-ideal clock leads to another kind of temporal indefiniteness, an effect which we have not considered here. Moreover, outside of the ideal limit, tensor factorizability itself can become frame-dependent
2
, and thus care must be taken in the relationship between the chosen clock and subsystems in question in order to avoid this.

In addition to clock ideality, Appendix
C
assumed the clock-system couplings to be effectively instantaneous, and relaxing this assumption to account for the necessary duration of the interventions would likewise affect the results above. While it seems that the retention of the subsystem-local nature of interventions between frames was not contingent upon this assumption, the question of time-locality of interventions in a given frame must be reexamined. Furthermore, in the consideration of time-delocalization in Secs.
VI
, we have ignored the potential for overlap between the delocalized interventions and measurements and the dynamics represented by maps of the form
\mathcal{E}_{|C}
, and more refined consideration of this aspect could yield further complications to the modeling of operational causality in a relational setting.

While, in the absence of interactions, the Page-Wootters formalism is one of an equivalent set of approaches to relational dynamics
31
;
29
;
11
, it is unclear to what extent this equivalence is affected by non-zero interactions. If the equilvalence turns out to be broken in this case, then the conclusions obtained here may differ from what would be obtained in the picture of relational Dirac observables
48
;
49
;
50
;
51
;
57
, or in the the relational Heisenberg picture obtained via quantum deparameterization
32
;
30
.

Our results exemplify the necessity of a careful consideration of the joint localizability of clocks, and in particular its limitations. They moreover suggest that operational causality in relational quantum dynamics can be formulated consistently only if interventions and measurements are treated as part of the dynamics rather than via “initial conditions” and abstract operations respectively. The role of joint temporal localizability in such a formulation, together with the results of
9
;
25
, points toward a broader picture in which temporal delocalization is a generic feature in relational quantum settings, and we have seen here how this permits relational realizations of indefinite causal order. At a more fundamental level, there is the question of how synchronization among many distributed clocks can arise under the limited joint temporal localizability discussed here, and with it how a more familiar notion of reference frame, closer to that envisaged in special relativity
19
, and a corresponding notion of causality may emerge.

\displaystyle=\langle+y|\rho^{\prime}_{B}(t_{f})|+y\rangle=\frac{1}{2}=p(-y|\text{no intervention})

(90)

\displaystyle p(+y|\text{intervention})

\displaystyle=\langle+y|\overline{\rho}^{\prime}_{B}(t_{f})|+y\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\big(1-\sin(2t_{f})\big)

(91)

\displaystyle p(-y|\text{intervention})

\displaystyle=\langle-y|\overline{\rho}^{\prime}_{B}(t_{f})|-y\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\big(1+\sin(2t_{f})\big),

(92)

showing that the application of the Hadamard-gate on
A
at time
t_{i}
can be detected by this measurement on
B
at an appropriately chosen time
t_{f}
.

Now we consider this example within the Page-Wootters formalism as described in Sec.
V.2
starting with constraint operator(s)

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\hat{H}_{C}+\hat{H^{(\prime)}}_{S},

(93)

where
\hat{H^{(\prime)}}_{S}
are those in Eqs. (
80
) and (
81
) respectively. Solutions to the constraint equation can be obtained from the kinematical state
|\phi\rangle=|t_{i}\rangle_{C}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}
via

\displaystyle|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int ds\,e^{-is\hat{C}}|t_{i}\rangle_{C}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int ds\,|t_{i}+s\rangle_{C}e^{-is\hat{H}_{S}^{(\prime)}}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}\,,

(94)

which allows for defining the following conditional states for different interventions
\mathcal{M}_{A}

\displaystyle|\psi^{A}_{|C}(\tau)\rangle=e^{-i(\tau-t_{i})\hat{H_{S}^{(\prime)}}}\mathcal{M}_{A}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}\;,

(95)

from which we can reproduce the example above in a straight forward manner. We have
\rho(t_{i})=|\psi_{|C}(t_{i})\rangle\!\langle\psi_{|C}(t_{i})|=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{A}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{B}
and
\overline{\rho}(t_{i})=|\psi^{A}_{|C}(t_{i})\rangle\!\langle\psi^{A}_{|C}(t_{i})|=\mathcal{M}_{A}[|0\rangle\!\langle 0|]_{A}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{B}
and need to compare

\displaystyle\rho_{B}(t_{f})=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{A}\left(|\psi_{|C}(t_{f})\rangle\!\langle\psi_{|C}(t_{f})|\right)

(96)

and

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{B}(t_{f})=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{A}\left(|\psi^{A}_{|C}(t_{f})\rangle\!\langle\psi^{A}_{|C}(t_{f})|\right),

(97)

which are the states in Eqs. (
87
) and (
89
) respectively, since
\exp(-i\tau\hat{H_{S}^{(\prime)}})=U^{(\prime)}_{S}
.

Note that the Hamiltonians
\hat{H}^{(\prime)}_{S}
in this example are symmetric in
A
and
B
and we can equally consider

\displaystyle|\psi^{B}_{|C}(\tau)\rangle=e^{-i(\tau-t_{i})\hat{H}_{S}^{(\prime)}}|0\rangle_{A}\mathcal{M}_{B}|0\rangle_{B},

(98)

and compare
{\rm Tr}_{B}\left(|\psi^{B}_{|C}(t_{f})\rangle\!\langle\psi^{B}_{|C}(t_{f})|\right)
to find signaling from
B
to
A
for the case where the two qubits interact, i.e.
\hat{C}=\hat{H}_{C}+\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}
. In fact, since signaling from
A
to
B
implies signaling from
B
to
A
for any bipartite unitary
7
we would have found the same for non-symmetric Hamiltonians. In the absence of further subsystems, unitary evolution allowing for a causal influence from
A
on
B
equaly allows for
B
to causally influence
A
.

Let us now extend this example to a relational scenario with two clock systems corresponding to constraint operator

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\hat{H}_{C_{1}}+\hat{H}_{C_{2}}+\hat{H}^{(\prime)}_{S},

(99)

where
\hat{H}^{(\prime)}_{S}
are again given by Eqs. (
80
) and (
81
). We can construct a physical state as follows

\displaystyle|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int ds\,e^{-is\hat{C}}\int dt\,|t_{i}\rangle_{C_{1}}\varphi(t)|t\rangle_{C_{2}}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B},

(100)

\displaystyle=\int ds\,dt\,\varphi(t)|t_{i}+s\rangle_{C_{1}}|t+s\rangle_{C_{2}}e^{-is\hat{H}^{(\prime)}_{S}}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B},

where we introduced the necessary time delocalization on clock
C_{2}
and used the same notation as before. From the perspective of clock
C_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\psi^{A}_{|C_{1}}(\tau_{1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\int dt\,\varphi(t)|t+\tau_{1}-t_{i}\rangle_{C_{2}}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(\tau_{1}-t_{i})\mathcal{M}_{A}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B},

(101)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{A}

\displaystyle=M_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B},

(102)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{B}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes O_{B},

(103)

which gives the following probabilities (and signaling behaviors) for measuring
\mathcal{O}_{B}
, i.e.
O_{B}=\sum_{b}b\,\Pi^{b}
at time
t_{f}
.

\displaystyle p_{|C_{1}}(b|\text{intervention})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes\Pi^{b}_{B}|\psi^{A}_{|C_{1}}(t_{f})\rangle\!\langle\psi^{A}_{|C_{1}}(t_{f})|\right)

(104)

\displaystyle=\int dt\,|\varphi(t)|^{2}\langle 0,0|M^{\dagger}_{A}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(t_{i}-t_{f})\Pi^{b}_{B}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(t_{f}-t_{i})M_{A}|0,0\rangle,

(105)

where
b
is the result obtained. If we again consider the Pauli-y measurement, i.e.
O_{B}=|+y\rangle\!\langle+y|-|-y\rangle\!\langle-y|
, and compare
M_{0}=\mathds{1}
and
M_{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}
we recover
p(b|\text{no intervention})
and
p(b|\text{intervention})
from Eqs. (
90
)-(
92
).
In the reference frame of clock
C_{2}
, however, we have

\displaystyle|\psi^{A}_{|C_{2}}(\tau_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=\int dt\,\varphi(t)|\tau_{2}-t+t_{i}\rangle_{C_{2}}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(\tau_{2}-t+t_{i})\mathcal{M}_{A}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B},

(106)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M^{\prime}}_{A}

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha\,|\alpha\rangle\!\langle\alpha|_{C_{1}}\otimes e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}^{(\prime)}_{S}}(M_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})e^{i\alpha\hat{H}^{(\prime)}_{S}},

(107)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{B}

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha\,|\tau_{F}+\alpha\rangle\!\langle\tau_{F}+\alpha|_{C_{1}}\otimes e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}^{(\prime)}_{S}}(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes O_{B})e^{i\alpha\hat{H}^{(\prime)}_{S}}\,.

(108)

While the probabilities remain unchanged under the change of clock perspective

\displaystyle p_{|C_{1}}(b|\text{(no) intervention})

\displaystyle=p_{|C_{2}}(b|\text{(no) intervention})=p(b|\text{(no) intervention})\,,

the operations
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{A}
and
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{B}
now no longer act on single subsystems (see Fig.
4
in the main text). More concretely, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{M^{\prime}}_{A}

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha\,|\alpha\rangle\!\langle\alpha|_{C_{1}}\otimes\left(\cos^{2}(\alpha)M_{A}+\sin^{2}(\alpha)XM_{A}X+i\cos(\alpha)\sin(\alpha)[M_{A},X]\right)\otimes\mathds{1}_{B},

(109)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{B}

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha\,\sum_{b}|\alpha\rangle\!\langle\alpha|_{C_{1}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes\left(\cos^{2}(\alpha)\Pi^{b}_{B}+\sin^{2}(\alpha)X\Pi^{b}_{B}X+i\cos(\alpha)\sin(\alpha)[\Pi^{b}_{B},X]\right),

(110)

for the case where
A
and
B
do not interact, i.e.
\hat{H}_{S}
. Since here
\mathcal{M^{\prime}}_{A}
acts trivially on
B
,
\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{B}
acts trivially on
A
and the probabilities are non-signaling, we can still conclude that there is no causal connection between
A
and
B
. However, when the two qubits interact, i.e. for
\hat{H}^{\prime}_{S}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M^{\prime}}_{A}

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha\,|\alpha\rangle\!\langle\alpha|_{C_{1}}\otimes\left(\cos^{2}(\alpha)M_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}+\sin^{2}(\alpha)XM_{A}X\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}+i\cos(\alpha)\sin(\alpha)[M_{A},X]\otimes X_{B}\right),

(111)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{B}

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha\,\sum_{b}b|\alpha\rangle\!\langle\alpha|_{C_{1}}\otimes\left(\cos^{2}(\alpha)\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes\Pi^{b}_{B}+\sin^{2}(\alpha)\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes X\Pi^{b}_{B}X+i\cos(\alpha)\sin(\alpha)X_{A}\otimes[\Pi^{b}_{B},X]\right),

(112)

meaning that both operations jointly act on all the subsystems,
A
,
B
and
C_{1}
. This means that in the reference frame of
C_{2}
the signaling probabilities can no longer be attributed to a causal influence form
A
to
B
.

Finally, we include the interventions above into the constraint Hamiltonian and show how it gives consistent causal reasoning for all reference frames, as discussed in Sec.
VI
of the main text. Let us consider constraint Hamiltonians

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\hat{H}_{C_{1}}+\hat{H}_{C_{2}}+\hat{H}^{(\prime)}_{S}+\delta(\hat{T}_{1}-t_{i})\hat{K}_{A}+\delta(\hat{T}_{1}-t_{f})\hat{K}_{BB^{\prime}}\,,

(113)

where both interventions are timed by clock
C_{1}
. When considering the example above we need to compare
\hat{K}_{A}=0
corresponding to no intervention to
\hat{K}_{A}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}(X+Z)
describing the application of the Hadamard-gate at time
t_{i}
. The operation at
t_{f}
is the van-Neumann measurement in the Pauli-y basis, i.e.
e^{-iK_{B}}|\pm y\rangle_{B}|b_{0}\rangle_{B^{\prime}}=|\pm y\rangle_{B}|\pm y\rangle_{B^{\prime}}

we obtain

\displaystyle|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{i}}dt_{1}\,\int dt_{2}\,\varphi(t_{2})|t_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|t_{1}+t_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(t_{1})|0,0\rangle_{AB}|a_{0}\rangle_{A^{\prime}}|b_{0}\rangle_{B^{\prime}}

(114)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\tau_{i}}^{\tau_{f}}dt_{1}\,\int dt_{2}\,\varphi(t_{2})|t_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|t_{1}+t_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(t_{1}-t_{i})e^{-i\hat{K}_{A}}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(t_{i})|0,0\rangle_{AB}|a_{0}\rangle_{A^{\prime}}|b_{0}\rangle_{B^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\tau_{f}}^{\infty}dt_{1}\,\int dt_{2}\,\varphi(t_{2})|t_{1}\rangle_{C_{1}}|t_{1}+t_{2}\rangle_{C_{2}}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(t_{1}-t_{f})e^{-i\hat{K}_{B}}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(t_{f}-t_{i})e^{-i\hat{K}_{A}}U^{(\prime)}_{S}(t_{i})|0,0\rangle_{AB}|a_{0}\rangle_{A^{\prime}}|b_{0}\rangle_{B^{\prime}},

which gives the conditional evolution from Eq. (
60
) in the main text and the probabilities form Eqs. (
90
)- (
92
) provided that
\varphi(t_{2})
is peaked around
0
sharply enough, compare Sec.
VI.3
. We have, hence fully incorporated the above example of operational causality into the Page-Wootters formalism via including timed interventions into the constraint operator.

Appendix C
Timed interventions in the Page-Wootters formalism

Here, we show how tailored interaction terms in the constraint operator give rise to physical states with timed interventions. We start with a general form and transform it in a way that allows us to evaluate
e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}}
. We then consider the limiting case of instantaneous interventions.

C.1
An interaction picture for clock-conditioned operations

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{kin}}=\mathcal{H}_{C}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{S}
, and let
\hat{V}(\hat{T})
denote a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{kin}}
of the form

\hat{V}(\hat{T}):=\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,f(t)|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes\hat{K}_{S}.

(115)

Consider the Hamiltonian constraint
\hat{C}|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=0
with

\hat{C}=\hat{H}_{C}+\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{V}(\hat{T}).

(116)

One can then move to an “interaction picture” as follows
10
. Defining
U_{\text{int}}:=e^{i\hat{T}\otimes\hat{H}_{S}}
, and
\hat{C}_{\text{int}}:=U_{\text{int}}\hat{C}U_{\text{int}}^{\dagger}
, and then the constraint equation
\hat{C}|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=0
can be rewritten as

\hat{C}|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=U_{\text{int}}\hat{C}|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=U_{\text{int}}\hat{C}U_{\text{int}}^{\dagger}U_{\text{int}}|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle\equiv\hat{C}_{\text{int}}|{\Psi_{\text{int}}}\rangle\rangle=0,

(117)

where
|{\Psi_{\text{int}}}\rangle\rangle:=U_{\text{int}}|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle
. We thus have a new set of physical states
\{|{\Psi_{\text{int}}}\rangle\rangle\,:\,\hat{C}_{\text{int}}|{\Psi_{\text{int}}}\rangle\rangle=0\}
, which are unitarily equivalent to the original set. On can then construct a projector from any element of the kinematical Hilbert space
|\phi\rangle
to the corresponding “interaction picture” physical state in an analogous manner to Eq. (
5
), i.e.

|{\Psi_{\text{int}}}\rangle\rangle=\delta(\hat{C}_{\text{int}})|\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}}ds\,e^{-is\hat{C}_{\text{int}}}|\phi\rangle

(118)

The form of
\hat{C}_{\text{int}}
can be obtained via the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula:

\hat{C}_{\text{int}}=\hat{H}_{C}+\hat{V}_{\mathrm{int}}(\hat{T}),

(119)

where

\hat{V}_{\mathrm{int}}(\hat{T}):=U_{\text{int}}\hat{V}(\hat{T})U_{\text{int}}^{\dagger}=\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,f(t)|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes e^{it\hat{H}_{S}}\hat{K}_{S}e^{-it\hat{H}_{S}}\equiv\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,f(t)|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes\hat{K}_{S}(t),

(120)

with
\hat{K}_{S}(t):=e^{it\hat{H}_{S}}\hat{K}_{S}e^{-it\hat{H}_{S}}
.

C.2
Exponentiating the interacting constraint Hamiltonian

Now, note that we can use the “interaction picture” to write

e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}}=U_{\text{int}}^{\dagger}e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}_{\mathrm{int}}}U_{\text{int}}.

(121)

We now follow the Supplemental Material of Ref.
9
to obtain an expression of the middle factor in terms of an time-ordered exponential. First, using Eq. (
119
) and the Trotter-Suzuki formula, we have

e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}_{\mathrm{int}}}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{H}_{C}}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{V}_{\mathrm{int}}(\hat{T})}\right)^{N}.

(122)

Recalling that the eigenstates
\{|t\rangle\}_{t\in\mathds{R}}
of
\hat{T}
form a basis of
\mathcal{H}_{C}
, and taking the tensor product with an arbitrary basis
\{|r\rangle\}_{r}
of
\mathcal{H}_{S}
, one can consider the action of the base on the right-hand side of Eq. (
122
) on an arbitrary basis element
|t\rangle_{C}|r\rangle_{S}
of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{kin}}
:

e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{H}_{C}}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{V}_{\mathrm{int}}(\hat{T})}|t\rangle_{C}|r\rangle_{S}=e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}f(t)\hat{K}_{S}(t)}|t+\frac{\alpha}{N}\rangle_{C}|r\rangle_{S},

(123)

where we have used the fact that

e^{-i\alpha\hat{V}_{\mathrm{int}}(\hat{T})}=\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes e^{-i\alpha f(t)\hat{K}_{S}(t)}.

(124)

Repeating the process, one finds that

\left(e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{H}_{C}}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{V}_{\mathrm{int}}(\hat{T})}\right)^{2}|t\rangle_{C}|r\rangle_{S}=e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}f(t+\frac{\alpha}{N})\hat{K}_{S}(t+\frac{\alpha}{N})}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}f(t)\hat{K}_{S}(t)}|t+2\frac{\alpha}{N}\rangle_{C}|r\rangle_{S}.

(125)

and thus that the
N^{\text{th}}
power satisfies

\displaystyle\left(e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{H}_{C}}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{V}_{\mathrm{int}}(\hat{T})}\right)^{N}|t\rangle_{C}|r\rangle_{S}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}f(t+\frac{n}{N}\alpha)\hat{K}_{S}(t+\frac{n}{N}\alpha)}|t+\alpha\rangle_{C}|r\rangle_{S}

(126)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}f(t+\frac{n}{N}\alpha)\hat{K}_{S}(t+\frac{n}{N}\alpha)}|t\rangle_{C}|r\rangle_{S}

(127)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}\int_{\mathds{R}}dt^{\prime}\,|t^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle t^{\prime}|\otimes\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}f(t^{\prime}+\frac{n}{N}\alpha)\hat{K}_{S}(t^{\prime}+\frac{n}{N}\alpha)}|t\rangle_{C}|r\rangle_{S},

(128)

where
\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}
should be understood as successive left-multiplication.
Since Eq. (
128
) holds for all
t
and
r
, we can simply equate the operators themselves, i.e.

\left(e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{H}_{C}}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{V}_{\mathrm{int}}(\hat{T})}\right)^{N}=e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}f(t+\frac{n}{N}\alpha)\hat{K}_{S}(t+\frac{n}{N}\alpha)}.

(129)

Then, as
N\to\infty
in Eq, (
122
), the time-dependent Trotter-Suzuki formula (see e.g.
54
) applied to the product in Eq. (
129
) gives

e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}_{\mathrm{int}}}=e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes\mathcal{T}\exp\left[-i\int_{t}^{t+\alpha}ds\,f(s)\hat{K}_{S}(s)\right],

(130)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the time-ordered exponential and we have performed a change of integration variables from
s\to s-t
(which, being monotone increasing in
s
, does not affect the time-ordering). The time-ordered exponential can be written in terms of the Dyson series:

\displaystyle e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}_{\mathrm{int}}}=e^{-i\frac{\alpha}{N}\hat{H}_{C}}\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes\left\{\mathds{1}-i\int_{0}^{\alpha}ds_{1}\,f(s_{1}+t)\hat{K}_{S}(s_{1}+t)\right.

(131)

\displaystyle\left.-\int_{0}^{\alpha}ds_{2}\,\int_{0}^{s_{2}}ds_{1}\,f(s_{2}+t)f(s_{1}+t)\hat{K}_{S}(s_{2}+t)\hat{K}_{S}(s_{1}+t)+\ldots\right\}.

Note that, this expression can also be obtained by the methods presented in
52
by equating
\hat{H}_{\rm int}
therein with Eq. (
115
) above (cf. Eqs. (17)-(19) in
52
).

C.3
Instantaneous interventions

A perfectly well-timed intervention, such as
f(t)=\delta(t)
, is strictly speaking ill-defined, since the exponential of Eq. (
116
) will in that case lead to powers of
\delta(0)
, and therefore diverge. However, we may consider the case where
f(t)
is arbitrarily well localized around some value, and we can approximate this situation by using the Dirac delta distribution in the Dyson series in Eq. (
131
). For example, considering such a perfectly-timed intervention occurring at clock time
\bar{\tau}
, we have
f(t)=\delta(t-\bar{\tau})
, and then Eq. (
131
) gives

\displaystyle e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}_{\mathrm{int}}}=e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes\left\{\mathds{1}-i\int_{0}^{\alpha}ds_{1}\,\delta(s_{1}+t-\bar{\tau})\hat{K}_{S}(s_{1}+t)\right.

(132)

\displaystyle\left.-\int_{0}^{\alpha}ds_{2}\int_{0}^{s_{2}}ds_{1}\,\delta(s_{2}+t-\bar{\tau})\delta(s_{1}+t-\bar{\tau})\hat{K}_{S}(s_{2}+t)\hat{K}_{S}(s_{1}+t)+\ldots\right\}.

Considering the terms in the series individually, we have

\int_{0}^{\alpha}ds_{1}\,\delta(s_{1}+t-\bar{\tau})\hat{K}_{S}(s_{1}+t)=\begin{cases}\hat{K}_{S}(\bar{\tau})&\text{if }\bar{\tau}\in[t,t+\alpha],\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(133)

and for the second-order term, noting that
\delta(s_{2}+t-\bar{\tau})\delta(s_{1}+t-\bar{\tau})=0
if
s_{1}\neq s_{2}
, we find that

\int_{0}^{\alpha}ds_{2}\int_{0}^{s_{2}}ds_{1}\,\delta(s_{2}+t-\bar{\tau})\delta(s_{1}+t-\bar{\tau})\hat{K}_{S}(s_{2}+t)\hat{K}_{S}(s_{1}+t)=\begin{cases}\hat{K}_{S}(\bar{\tau})^{2}&\text{if }\bar{\tau}\in[t,t+\alpha],\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(134)

and so on for each term, and thus Eq. (
132
) becomes

e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}_{\mathrm{int}}}=e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes e^{-i\,\mathrm{rect}_{\alpha}(\bar{\tau}-t)\hat{K}_{S}(\bar{\tau})},

(135)

where
\mathrm{rect}_{\alpha}(s)
is a rectangular function defined such as

\mathrm{rect}_{\alpha}(s)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }s\in[0,\alpha],\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(136)

Then

e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}_{\mathrm{int}}}=e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}\int_{\mathds{R}}dt\,|t\rangle\!\langle t|\otimes e^{-i\,\mathrm{rect}_{\alpha}(\bar{\tau}-t)\hat{K}_{S}(\bar{\tau})},

(137)

We will now show how this can give a Page-Wootters picture with the usual evolution under
\hat{H}_{C}+\hat{H}_{S}
, interspersed with an intervention occurring at time
\bar{\tau}
. Recalling that an arbitrary physical state can be obtained via a kinematical state of the form
|t=0\rangle_{C}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}
(see Sec.
II.1
) we consider the action of
e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}_{\mathrm{int}}}
on such a state. Assuming for simplicity that
\bar{\tau}>0
,

e^{-i\alpha\hat{C}_{\mathrm{int}}}|t=0\rangle_{C}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}=\begin{cases}e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}|t=0\rangle_{C}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S},&\text{if }\alpha<\bar{\tau}\\
\left(e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}\otimes e^{-i\hat{K}_{S}(\bar{\tau})}\right)|t=0\rangle_{C}\,|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S},&\text{if }\alpha\geq\bar{\tau}.\end{cases}

(138)

Recalling that the interaction-picture physical state corresonding to
|t=0\rangle_{C}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}
is given by
\delta(\hat{C}_{\text{int}})|t=0\rangle_{C}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}
, and that a physical state can be obtained from the interaction picture via
|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle\equiv U_{\text{int}}^{-1}|{\Psi_{\text{int}}}\rangle\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv U_{\text{int}}^{-1}\,\delta(\hat{C}_{\text{int}})|t=0\rangle_{C}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}=e^{-i\hat{T}\otimes\hat{H}_{S}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathds{R}}d\alpha\,e^{-i\alpha\hat{H}_{C}}|t=0\rangle_{C}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}

(139)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\hat{T}\otimes\hat{H}_{S}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\bar{\tau}}dt|t\rangle_{C}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\bar{\tau}}^{\infty}dt|t\rangle_{C}\,e^{-i\hat{K}_{S}(\bar{\tau})}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}\right\}.

Noting that, as in the usual interaction picture,
\hat{K}_{S}(\bar{\tau})^{n}=e^{i\bar{\tau}\hat{H}_{S}}\hat{K}_{S}^{n}e^{-i\bar{\tau}\hat{H}_{S}}
, one can write the physical state
|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle
above as

|{\Psi}\rangle\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\bar{\tau}}dt|t\rangle_{C}\,e^{-it\hat{H}_{S}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\bar{\tau}}^{\infty}dt|t\rangle_{C}\,e^{-i(t-\bar{\tau})\hat{H}_{S}}e^{-i\hat{K}_{S}}\,e^{-i\bar{\tau}\hat{H}_{S}}|\phi_{0}\rangle_{S}.

(140)
