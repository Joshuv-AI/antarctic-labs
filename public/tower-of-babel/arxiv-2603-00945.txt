Non-Rectangular Average-Reward Robust MDPs: Optimal Policies and Their Transient Values
Shengbo Wang, Nian Si

Abstract
We study non-rectangular robust Markov decision processes under the average-reward criterion, where the ambiguity set couples transition probabilities across states and the adversary commits to a stationary kernel for the entire horizon. We show that any history-dependent policy achieving sublinear expected regret uniformly over the ambiguity set is robust-optimal, and that the robust value admits a minimax representation as the infimum over the ambiguity set of the classical optimal gains, without requiring any form of rectangularity or robust dynamic programming principle. Under the weak communication assumption, we establish the existence of such policies by converting high-probability regret bounds from the average-reward reinforcement learning literature into the expected-regret criterion. We then introduce a transient-value framework to evaluate finite-time performance of robust optimal policies, proving that average-reward optimality alone can mask arbitrarily poor transients and deriving regret-based lower bounds on transient values. Finally, we construct an epoch-based policy that combines an optimal stationary policy for the worst-case model with an anytime-valid sequential test and an online learning fallback, achieving a constant-order transient value.

Introduction

Robust Markov decision processes (MDPs) provide a foundational framework for reliable sequential decision-making under model ambiguity by requiring the controller to optimize performance against the worst-case transition kernel within a specified ambiguity set. This framework has become increasingly important in contemporary stochastic control, reinforcement learning, and operations contexts, with applications in systems governed by partially specified physical parameters, environments shaped by policy-dependent feedback effects, and data-driven transition models subject to correlated estimation errors. In such settings, the modeler may have difficulty committing to a particular transition dynamic that could be misspecified, making robustness a central design consideration.

The predominant literature on robust MDPs focuses on ambiguity sets that satisfy some form of rectangularity, such as
SA-rectangularity
[
10
,
5
,
17
]
,
S-rectangularity
[
29
,
28
]
,

r
-rectangularity
[
6
]
, and

k
-rectangularity
[
16
]
. The primary motivation for imposing rectangularity is tractability: it restores an appropriate dynamic programming principle and reduces the original robust control problem to solving a Bellman-type fixed-point equation. Under rectangularity, nature’s choices decompose across states or state–action pairs, so the adversary’s optimization can be performed locally at each decision epoch.

However, in many data-driven and structurally motivated ambiguity models, the rectangularity assumption is restrictive. Non-rectangular ambiguity sets, in which ambiguity is intrinsically coupled across states, arise naturally in applications: transition probabilities may be linked through shared parameters, joint statistical constraints, or global structural conditions, and thus cannot be perturbed independently on a state-by-state basis. We illustrate this point with two representative examples.

Example 1:
(MLE-based confidence regions).
Suppose the transition kernel is estimated from data via maximum likelihood, and ambiguity is modeled through a joint confidence region around the MLE. Such regions are typically defined through likelihood-ratio or score-based constraints that couple all transition probabilities simultaneously. For instance, an asymptotic

\chi^{2}
-type confidence set constrains the entire transition matrix through a single quadratic form. As a result, perturbations to one state–action transition must be compensated elsewhere to remain within the global confidence region. The resulting ambiguity set is inherently non-rectangular. We refer the reader to the construction in
Wiesemann et al., 2013
.

Example 2:
(factorized MDPs). In many applications, transition kernels depend on a set of shared underlying latent factors. For instance, in healthcare settings, patient transitions may be influenced by common factors such as genetic background, demographic characteristics, and physiological attributes associated with particular diseases
[
7
]
. In such models, ambiguity arises at the level of these latent factors rather than at individual state–action transitions.
Importantly, the model ambiguity across factors is typically not independent, and the dependence of transition probabilities on these factors may be nonlinear. As a result, perturbations in one component of the factor space simultaneously affect multiple state–action transitions. The induced ambiguity set is therefore inherently non-rectangular and, in general, does not satisfy

r
-rectangularity either.

In contrast to the substantial progress and tractability achieved under rectangular MDPs, the non-rectangular setting is fundamentally more intricate, as the Bellman-type fixed-point equation typically fails to hold. In particular, optimal policies can no longer be expected to be Markovian or to admit a characterization through standard robust Bellman equations. Moreover, it is generally unclear whether the robust value admits any tractable dynamic programming representation.

These issues become even more delicate under the average-reward criterion, which is natural for ongoing systems without an intrinsic horizon
[
23
]
. Compared with discounted formulations, average-reward control is already technically more subtle even in the classical, non-robust setting. Optimality conditions and policy structure depend sensitively on the communicating structure of the underlying Markov chain (e.g., multichain, weakly communicating, or unichain models), and the gain–bias decomposition separates steady-state performance from transient effects, introducing additional analytical complexity
[
18
]
. Moreover, long-run optimality alone does not address transient performance, raising the additional question of how to evaluate and control finite-time behavior in such models.
While these challenges are largely understood in the classical (non-robust) theory, they become substantially more delicate in robust models.

This paper addresses these challenges in non-rectangular average-reward robust MDPs. We impose no structural assumptions on the ambiguity set beyond weak communication, which guarantees that, in the non-robust case, the optimal average reward is well defined and independent of the initial state. Recognizing that Markovian policies may fail to be optimal under non-rectangular ambiguity, we adopt the standard formulation in
Grand-Clément et al., 2026
: the controller may employ general history-dependent policies, while the adversary is stationary and commits to a single transition kernel
p\in\mathcal{P}
for the entire horizon. This non-rectangular model encompasses a broad class of statistically natural ambiguity models, including those induced by joint confidence regions, maximum-likelihood estimators, or nonlinear factor models, while lying outside the classical robust dynamic programming framework that relies on rectangularity to ensure stagewise decomposability.

Under this framework we establish a crucial message: robust optimality under the average-reward criterion is fundamentally linked to learnability. Specifically, we introduce an abstraction of online RL algorithms as a class of history-dependent policies that achieve sublinear regret, and we show that such policies are robust-optimal for (non-)rectangular average-reward problem. This connection offers a different perspective: rather than restoring tractability through structural assumptions on the ambiguity set, robust optimality fundamentally emerges from the ability to achieve online RL over all transition models in the ambiguity set.

While optimal, these online RL policies typically have poor finite-time performance: the transient value (TV) must tend to negative infinity as the horizon increases due to the need for persistent exploration. Motivated by this issue, we go beyond long-run optimality and study finite-time behavior, asking whether one can achieve a uniformly lower bounded TV over any finite horizon. We show that this is indeed possible by proposing a hybrid, epoch-based policy that, perhaps surprisingly, achieves a uniformly lower bounded TV of the same order as the span of the value function.

To obtain this finite-time performance, we design Policy
1
, which alternates between exploitation and learning. The policy follows an average-reward optimal stationary policy for a candidate worst-case model while simultaneously running a Markov chain sequential probability ratio test (SPRT)
[
21
]
. If the observed data are inconsistent with the model currently being followed, the policy falls back to a standard online RL routine for the remainder of that stage. By scheduling stages to grow over time and making “false alarms” increasingly rare, the policy adapts quickly when the model is wrong while preserving robust optimality.

Rectangular robust MDPs:
The discounted-reward theory for robust MDPs under rectangular ambiguity sets is now well developed:

Non-rectangular robust MDPs:
Recent progress on robust MDPs beyond rectangularity has focused on algorithmic methods that bypass the breakdown of robust Bellman recursions under coupled ambiguity.
Li et al., 2026
develop policy-gradient algorithms for robust infinite-horizon MDPs with non-rectangular transition ambiguity sets, including methods that achieve globally optimal robust policy evaluation, albeit at significant computational cost. For the average-reward criterion,
Wang et al., 2025a
propose a projected policy-gradient method for ergodic tabular robust average-reward MDPs with general (non-rectangular) ambiguity and analyze convergence of both the policy updates and the inner worst-case transition evaluation. Complementing gradient-based approaches,

provide provable efficiency guarantees for robust MDPs with general policy parameterizations under non-rectangular uncertainty by reducing average-reward problems to entropy-regularized discounted formulations amenable to first-order methods. In a related but distinct setting,
Gadot et al., 2024
study non-rectangular
reward

In contrast to the above work, which primarily targets computational methods and convergence guarantees for computing robust policies, our paper takes a structural viewpoint in the stationary-adversary, non-rectangular average-reward setting: we characterize robust optimality through the existence of online-RL policies, culminating in a robust-optimal policy with constant-order transient value via sequential testing.

Online RL for average-reward MDPs:
The online RL literature for average-reward MDPs has developed along several threads.
Jaksch et al., 2010

Our work leverages these advances by showing that any such algorithm with sublinear regret can be converted into a robust-optimal policy for non-rectangular average-reward robust MDPs.

1.2
Contributions and Organization

Our contributions and main results are organized as follows.

First, we connect online average-reward RL to the non-rectangular robust average-reward MDP. In Theorem
1
, we show that any online RL algorithm with uniform sublinear expected or high-probability regret can be converted into a robust optimal policy.

We also clarify that such policies need not exist in full generality. Without additional structural conditions on the ambiguity set, we construct an instance in Proposition
3.1
in which every policy incurs linear regret. Nevertheless, Proposition
3.2
shows that under weak communication, standard high-probability regret guarantees for average-reward RL can be converted into an expected regret bound, which in turn implies the existence of robust optimal online RL policies.

We then study finite-time behavior through a new performance notion, the transient value (TV), which can be viewed as a refined “negative regret” term measuring cumulative deviation from the robust average reward. In Proposition
4.1
, we establish a general upper bound on TV in terms of the span of worst-case bias functions, showing that one cannot accumulate an indefinitely growing expected deviation from the optimal robust average reward. We further relate transient performance to learning speed: for an RL policy with a given regret rate, Proposition
4.2
provides a corresponding lower bound on its transient value, making explicit how typical regret growth (e.g.,
\sqrt{T}
) translates into the corresponding TV scaling (e.g.,
-\sqrt{T}
).

Finally, Section
5.2
gives an explicit construction of a robust-optimal policy with TV uniformly lower bounded by the span of the worst-case bias function. The policy operates in epochs: it executes a candidate worst-case stationary policy while simultaneously running an anytime-valid Markovian composite sequential probability ratio test (SPRT)
[
21
]
. Upon rejection, it switches to a learning-based procedure within the same epoch. In Theorem
3
, we show that this design yields a constant-order lower bound on TV while maintaining robust optimality.

A key technical ingredient behind this result is the design and analysis of the composite SPRT for Markov chains, with carefully scheduled type-I error control and logarithmic expected detection delay under alternatives (Theorem
2
), which allows the policy to adapt quickly when the current model is misspecified. The second ingredient is the scheduling and use of the online RL policy after rejection (whether accidental or correct), ensuring that the controller can adequately explore and exploit when the test indicates a model mismatch.

Canonical Construction and the Optimal Robust Control Problem

In this section, we present a brief but self-contained canonical construction of the probability space, the processes of interest, and the controller’s and adversary’s policy classes. We adopt the notation and framework of
Wang et al., 2023a
, to which we refer the reader for additional details.

Let

be finite state and action spaces, each equipped with the discrete Borel
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
, respectively. Define the underlying measurable space

with

and
\mathcal{F}

\sigma
-field. The process

is defined by point evaluation, i.e.,
X_{t}(\omega)=s_{t}
and
A_{t}(\omega)=a_{t}
for all

and any
\omega=(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},a_{1},\ldots)\in\Omega

The history set

at time

contains all

\mathbf{H}_{t}:=\left\{{h_{t}=(s_{0},a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1},s_{t}):\omega=(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1}\dots)\in\Omega}\right\}.

We also define the random element
H_{t}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbf{H}_{t}
by point evaluation
H_{t}(\omega)=h_{t}
, and the
\sigma

\mathcal{H}_{t}:=\sigma(H_{t})

Motivated by applications in which the controller must be restricted to a subset of randomized decisions (e.g., to deterministic actions), we consider the constraint set as a prescribed subset
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
. Let
\delta_{a}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
denote the point mass measure at

. For the compatibility with the RL literature, we will frequently require that

A controller policy

is a sequence of decision rules

where each

is a measure-valued function

\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q}):=\{\pi=(\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\ldots):\pi_{t}\in\{\mathbf{H}_{t}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}\},\ \forall t\geq 0\}.

A controller policy

Dynamic Programming for Classical Average-Reward MDPs

and the same holds for

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}(s)

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in A}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\alpha_{p}(s^{\prime})

(2.2)

\displaystyle v_{p}(s)

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in A}\left\{{r(s,a)-\alpha_{p}(s)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)v_{p}(s^{\prime})}\right\}

Robust Optimality of Online RL Policies

3.1
Online RL Algorithms and Expected Regret

In this section, we define regret under the average-reward criterion and provide an abstraction of online RL algorithms viewed as history-dependent policies.

Regret for an online RL policy measures the deviation of a policy’s realized value from the optimal long-run average reward over a finite horizon
T
. Define

R_{r,p}(T):=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[\alpha_{p}(X_{0})-r(X_{t},A_{t})\right],

(3.1)

where
\alpha_{p}
is the unique solution to (
2.2
). Then the expected regret of a policy
\pi
is given by
E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}R_{r,p}(T)
. Because
S
and
A
are finite, any history-dependent policy has expected regret
E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}R_{r,p}(T)=O(T)
as
T\to\infty
.

An online RL algorithm can be interpreted as a systematic procedure that selects (possibly randomized) actions based on the observed history of states and actions and thus induces a history-dependent policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}
. The key distinction between a reasonable online RL procedure and an arbitrary history-dependent policy is
sublinear expected regret
. Intuitively, achieving sublinear expected regret requires the induced policy to approximate optimal decisions with arbitrarily small error as
T\to\infty
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 1

(Online RL Policies)
.

We say that a policy
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
achieves online RL over the class of transition kernels
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\left\{{S\times A\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(S)}\right\}
if for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(S)
, and
r:S\times A\rightarrow[0,1]
,

\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}R_{r,p}(T)=0.

We denote policies
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
that achieve online RL over
\mathcal{P}
by
{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
.

We note that in the literature, online RL algorithms often assume a fixed initial distribution, such as the uniform distribution or a specific starting state. However, most approaches readily extend to arbitrary initial distributions.

3.2
Optimality of Online RL Policies

We define the robust optimal average reward as the worst-case (over the ambiguity set) classical optimal gain, weighted by the initial distribution:

\alpha^{*}(\mu):=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s),\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha^{*}(s):=\alpha^{*}(\delta_{s})

(3.2)

for all
s\in S
, where

.

\displaystyle\underline{\alpha}(\mu,\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q}),\mathcal{P})

1

with the same inequalities holding for
\overline{\alpha}

Therefore, if we can show that for all

\underline{\alpha}(\mu,{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p)=\overline{\alpha}(\mu,{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p)=\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s),

To show (

1

{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
satisfies that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}R_{r,p}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s)-\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t},A_{t})\right]

3.3
Weak Communication and the Existence of Online RL Policies

While algorithms for online RL under the general gain setting remain (justifiably, to be discussed in Example
1
) underexplored, there has been substantial interest and progress in the RL literature under various notions of communicating structures. Among these, one of the most general settings that has been actively studied and has yielded several positive results is that of weakly communicating MDPs.

In this section, we first present a simple example showing that, without additional assumptions on
\mathcal{P}

{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\varnothing
. In such a case, Theorem
1
becomes vacuous. However, we demonstrate that Theorem
1
remains meaningful–namely, that there exists
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
–when we restrict attention to weakly communicating
\mathcal{P}
as defined in Definition
2
. To establish the existence of such a
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}
, we introduce a principled method to convert almost any online RL algorithm for weakly communicating MDPs that satisfies a sublinear high-probability regret upper bound into a policy that achieves online RL in the sense of Definition
1
.

Example 1
.

Consider the following setting with 3 states
\left\{{i,e_{1},e_{2}}\right\}
(one initial state and two ending states that are absorbing) and 2 actions
\left\{{a_{1},a_{2}}\right\}
. We consider
\mathcal{P}=\left\{{p^{(1)},p^{(2)}}\right\}
with

p^{(1)}(\cdot|\cdot,a_{1})=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad p^{(1)}(\cdot|\cdot,a_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},

p^{(2)}(\cdot|\cdot,a_{1})=p^{(1)}(\cdot|\cdot,a_{2})
, and
p^{(2)}(\cdot|\cdot,a_{2})=p^{(1)}(\cdot|\cdot,a_{1})
. So, both
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are absorbing states under
p^{(i)}
,
i=1,2
regardless which action is taken. The reward function is defined by
r(i,\cdot)=0
,
r(e_{1},\cdot)=1
, and
r(e_{2},\cdot)=0
.

We consider any policy
\pi:=(\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\dots)\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
. Choose the initial distribution as the point mass measure at state
i
and write
E_{i}^{\pi,p}
as a shorthand for
E_{\delta_{i}}^{\pi,p}
. Let
b:=\min\left\{{\pi_{0}(a_{1}|i),\pi_{0}(a_{2}|i)}\right\}
. In this setting, we show that the following Proposition holds

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
, the regret for any policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
satisfies

\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}E_{i}^{\pi,p}R_{r,p}(T)=1-b\geq\frac{1}{2}.

In particular,
{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\varnothing
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

First, note that for
k=1,2
, under kernel
p^{(k)}
and starting from state
i
, taking action
a_{k}
always leads to
e_{1}
as the next state. Since
e_{1}
is an absorbing state with reward
1
, the long-run average reward under this action is
1
. Consequently,
\alpha_{p}(i)=1
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
.

So, we have that for
k=1,2

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}E_{i}^{\pi,p^{(k)}}R_{r,p}(T)

\displaystyle=1-E^{\pi,p^{(k)}}_{i}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t},A_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=1-E_{i}^{\pi,p^{(k)}}\left[\frac{T-1}{T}\mathds{1}\left\{{X_{1}=e_{1}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=1-\frac{T-1}{T}\pi_{0}(a_{k}|i)

where the last equality follows from the observation under kernel
p^{(k)}
and starting from state
i
, taking action
a_{k}
always leads to
e_{1}
as the next state; but taking action
a_{(k\text{ mod }2)+1}
always leads to
e_{2}
. Taking
T\rightarrow\infty
and
\max
over
k=1,2
and noting that
b\leq 1/2
, we obtain the statement of the proposition.
∎

While Example
1
shows a negative result in which no policy can achieve online RL, this failure arises because state
i
is visited only once, regardless of the controller’s policy. Consequently, the controller has no opportunity to learn the correct action at
i
before the process is absorbed into either
e_{1}
or
e_{2}
.

In contrast, if an MDP possesses some form of “irreducibility”–in particular, if all states can be visited infinitely often under some control policy–then achieving online RL becomes feasible. This observation motivates the consideration of a weak form of irreducibility, known as weakly communicating in the classical MDP and RL literature, which serves as a sufficient condition to guarantee the learnability of the optimal policy.

We proceed by first introducing the following notation. For
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
\Delta:S\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
, denote

p_{\Delta}(s^{\prime}|s):=\sum_{a\in A}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\Delta(a|s).

Also, let
p_{\Delta}^{n}(s^{\prime}|s)
be the
(s,s^{\prime})
entry of the
n
-th power of the matrix
\left\{{p_{\Delta}(s^{\prime}|s):s,s^{\prime}\in S}\right\}
. Moreover, for
C\subseteq S
we denote the complement of
C
is
S
by
C^{c}:=S\setminus C
.

Definition 2

(Weak Communication)
.

Consider arbitrary controller and adversary action sets
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{P}
. A transition kernel
p\in\mathcal{P}
is said to be weakly communicating if there is a communicating class
C_{p}\subseteq S
s.t. for any
s,s^{\prime}\in C_{p}
, there exists
\Delta:S\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
and
N\geq 1
s.t.
p_{\Delta}^{N}(s^{\prime}|s)>0
. Moreover, for all
s\in C_{p}^{c}
,
s
is transient under any stationary controller policy.

The ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
is weakly communicating if every
p\in\mathcal{P}
is weakly communicating.

Weak communication has been a standard assumption in the classical MDP setting that guarantees the optimal average reward is initial state independent
[
18
]
. In particular, if
\mathcal{P}
is weakly communicating and
\left\{{\delta_{a}:a\in A}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
, then for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
, the classical dynamic programming theory simplifies (cf. (
2.1
)) to

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})}\overline{\alpha}(\mu,\pi,p)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathcal{Q})}\overline{\alpha}(\mu,\pi,p)=\alpha_{p},

(3.4)

where
\alpha_{p}\in[0,1]
is a part of a solution pair
(\alpha_{p},v_{p})
to the constant-gain Bellman equation (cf. (
2.2
))

v_{p}(s)=\max_{a\in A}\left\{{r(s,a)-\alpha_{p}+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)v_{p}(s^{\prime})}\right\},

(3.5)

for all
s\in S
. Moreover,
\alpha_{p}
is unique in the sense that if
(\alpha^{\prime},v^{\prime})
also solves (
3.5
), then
\alpha_{p}=\alpha^{\prime}
. But in general,
v_{p}
could be non-unique.

In this setting, since
\alpha_{p}
is state-independent, the definition of
\alpha^{*}(\mu)
in (
3.2
) also simplifies. In particular, we write
\alpha^{*}=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\alpha_{p}
.

With this definition of weakly communicating
\mathcal{P}
, we now discuss the existence of policies that achieve online RL within weakly communicating models. There is already a substantial body of work on online RL algorithms for the average-reward setting
[
11
,
1
,
3
,
30
]
. However, most of this literature under weak communication has focused on establishing
high-probability
regret bounds rather than expected regret, as defined in Definition
1
. To connect these results with our framework, we show in the following proposition that a policy satisfying the online RL criterion in Definition
1
can be constructed from algorithms that guarantee high-probability regret bounds.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is weakly communicating. Assume further that there exist a sequence of policies
\{\pi(T):T\geq 0\}\subseteq\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
with the following property. There exist parameters
\eta\in(0,1)
,
K\geq 1
, and
\beta>0
such that for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
, reward function
r:S\times A\to[0,1]
, initial distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(S)
, and
T\geq K
, there exists

f_{\mu,r,p}\geq 0
such that

P_{\mu}^{\pi(T),p}\left(\frac{R_{r,p}(T)}{f_{\mu,r,p}T^{\eta}}\leq 1\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{T^{\beta}}.

Then, there exists a policy
\pi\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

We construct a policy
\pi\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
using an
epoch-based
procedure.
The
n
th epoch spans time steps
t=2^{n}K,\ldots,2^{n+1}K-1
.
At the beginning of each epoch, all previously collected data are discarded, and a fresh instance of the online RL algorithm
\pi(2^{n}K)
is executed for the duration of that epoch.
This construction ensures that the regret accumulated across epochs can be bounded in terms of the regret guarantees of
\pi(T)
provided in the assumption of Proposition
3.2
.

We start by bounding the expected regret within one epoch. We first note that since
\mathcal{P}
is weakly communicating, by (
3.4
) and (
3.5
),
\alpha_{p}
is state independent. By the high-probability bound assumption in Proposition
3.2
and the fact that
R_{r,p}(T)\leq T
almost surely, we have

E_{\mu}^{\pi(T),p}R_{r,p}(T)\leq\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{T^{\beta}}\Bigr)T^{\eta}f_{\mu,r,p}+T^{1-\beta}\leq T^{\max\{\eta,1-\beta\}}\bigl(f_{\mu,r,p}+1\bigr),

for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(S)
,
r:S\times A\to[0,1]
, and weakly communicating
p
.
This inequality bounds the regret of
\pi(T)
over any horizon of length
T
.

Let
\mu_{2^{n}K}
denote the distribution of
X_{2^{n}K}
under
E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}
.
Since each epoch begins by resetting the history, the expected regret accumulated during the
n
th epoch can be expressed as

\displaystyle E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\!\left[\sum_{t=2^{n}K}^{2^{n+1}K-1}\!\!\bigl(\alpha_{p}-r(X_{t},A_{t})\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle=E_{\mu_{2^{n}K}}^{\pi(2^{n}K)}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{2^{n}K-1}\!\!\bigl(\alpha_{p}-r(X_{t},A_{t})\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle\leq(f_{\mu,r,p}+1)(2^{n}K)^{\max\{\eta,1-\beta\}}

\displaystyle\leq(f_{\mu,r,p}+1)2^{n\max\{\eta,1-\beta\}}K.

This bound follows from substituting
T=2^{n}K
into the previous inequality and noting that
\eta<1
and
\beta>0
, giving us the expected regret bound within epoch
n
.

We first establish the bound at epoch boundaries. Summing the epoch regret bounds over completed epochs
n=0,\ldots,j-1
, the expected regret at the epoch boundary
T^{\prime}=2^{j}K
satisfies

E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}R_{r,p}(T^{\prime})\leq\sum_{0\leq n\leq j-1}(f_{\mu,r,p}+1)2^{n\max\{\eta,1-\beta\}}K\leq(f_{\mu,r,p}+1)\frac{2^{j\max\{\eta,1-\beta\}}-1}{2^{\max\{\eta,1-\beta\}}-1}K.

(3.6)

Next, we extend this bound to an arbitrary horizon
T
. For any
T
with
2^{j-1}K\leq T\leq 2^{j}K
, let
T^{\prime}=2^{j}K
be the next epoch boundary. By the average-reward optimality equation (
3.5
), for every action
a
and state
s
,

r(s,a)-\alpha_{p}\leq v_{p}^{*}(s)-\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)v_{p}^{*}(s^{\prime})
.
Taking expectations and summing from
t=T
to
T^{\prime}-1
, a telescoping argument yields

E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\!\left[\sum_{t=T}^{T^{\prime}-1}\bigl(\alpha_{p}-r(X_{t},A_{t})\bigr)\right]\geq-\left|v_{p}^{*}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}.

Therefore,

E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}R_{r,p}(T)\leq E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}R_{r,p}(T^{\prime})+\left|v_{p}^{*}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}.

Since
\eta>0
and
1-\beta<1
, define a finite normalization constant

f^{\prime}_{\mu,r,p}:=\frac{4}{2^{\max\{\eta,1-\beta\}}-1}\bigl(f_{\mu,r,p}+1\bigr)<\infty.

Combining the epoch-boundary bound (
3.6
) with the extension above, for any
2^{j-1}K\leq T\leq 2^{j}K
,

\frac{1}{T}E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}R_{r,p}(T)\leq f^{\prime}_{\mu,r,p}2^{j\max\{\eta-1,-\beta\}}+\frac{\left|v_{p}^{*}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}}{T},

which converges to
0
as
j\to\infty
because
\eta<1
and
\beta>0
. This implies that
\pi\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
.
∎

Remark
.

The bound in Proposition
3.2
can be refined by incorporating logarithmic terms following
T^{\eta}
. However, since this paper primarily focuses on policy optimality rather than regret analysis, we omit this generalization for the clarity of the current presentation.

We provide the following Example
2
where Proposition
3.2
is applied to turn an RL algorithm (viewed as a sequence of history-dependent policies
\left\{{\pi(T):T\geq 0}\right\}
) in
Zhang and Xie, 2023
with high-probability regret bound to an online RL policy
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
under Definition
1
.

Example 2

(The UCB-AVG Algorithm in
Zhang and Xie, 2023
)
.

Suppose
\left\{{\delta_{a}:a\in A}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{P}
is weakly communicating. Then
Zhang and Xie, 2023
shows that the UCB-AVG algorithm, identified as
\pi_{\text{ZX}}^{\delta}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
, which only uses deterministic actions, achieves

P_{\mu}^{\pi_{\text{ZX}}^{\delta},p}\left(R_{r,p}(T)\leq C\left|v_{p}^{*}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}\sqrt{T}\log\left(\frac{T}{\delta}\right)\right)\geq 1-\delta

for all initial distributions
\mu
and all
T\geq 1
. Here
C
is a constant that only depends on
|S|
and
|A|
, and
v_{p}^{*}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is one of the solutions to (
3.5
), which depends on
r
and
p
.

Choosing
\delta=1/\sqrt{T}
and writing
\pi_{\text{ZX}}(T):=\pi_{\text{ZX}}^{1/\sqrt{T}}
, we have that

P_{\mu}^{\pi_{\text{ZX}}(T),p}\left(R_{r,p}(T)\leq 2C\left|v_{p}^{*}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}\sqrt{T}\log T\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{T^{1/2}}.

Then, we choose
\beta=1/2
and
\eta=1/2+\epsilon
for any
0<\epsilon<1/2
. Hence, we see that
\left\{{\pi_{\text{ZX}}(T):T\geq 0}\right\}
satisfies Proposition
3.2
, with
f_{\mu,r,p}=\left|v_{p}^{*}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}<\infty
and any
K
large enough so that

\frac{2C\sqrt{K}\log K}{K^{1/2+\epsilon}}\leq 1.

Hence, by Proposition
3.2
, one can construct
\pi_{\mathrm{ZX}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
. This shows the following corollary.

Corollary 1.1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is weakly communicating and
\left\{{\delta_{a}:a\in A}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
, then
\pi_{\mathrm{ZX}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
which achieves the optimal robust average reward

\underline{\alpha}(\mu,\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q}),\mathcal{P})=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\underline{\alpha}(\mu,\pi_{\mathrm{ZX}},p)=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\alpha_{p},

where
\alpha_{p}
is the unique solution to (
3.5
).

4
Transient Values for Optimal Robust Policies

By Theorem
1
, if
{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\neq\varnothing
, then starting from any initial distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
, every optimal policy under the liminf average-reward criterion achieves a long-run liminf average reward of
\alpha^{*}(\mu)
. In practice, even when a robust MDP is formulated under the long-run average criterion, good transient performance–where the system attains high expected stage-wise rewards while converging to its steady-state behavior–is highly desirable. In this section, we investigate this aspect of optimal robust policies by defining their transient values and constructing efficient robust optimal policies that enjoy (perhaps surprisingly) strong transient performance guarantees.

4.1
Transient Values and Their Upper Bounds

We begin by defining the transient value of a history-dependent policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
against the optimal average reward
\alpha^{*}(\mu)
defined in (
3.2
).

Definition 3

(Transient Value)
.

Consider a weight function
w:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow(0,1]
that is non-increasing. Given an initial distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
,
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
, we define the
w
-weighted transient value against
\alpha^{*}(\mu)

\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi):=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}w(T)E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)].

(4.1)

When
w(\cdot)\equiv 1
, let
\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\pi)
denote the (unweighted) transient value.

The transient value captures the long-run cumulative deviation of the expected rewards under
(\mu,\pi)
from the optimal average reward. First, we show in Proposition
4.1
that history-dependent policies cannot attain an infinite unweighted transient value; that is,
\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\pi)<\infty
. This, in turn, implies that
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi)\leq 0
for any weighting function
w(T)\to 0
. However, in general, history-dependent optimal policies can exhibit arbitrarily poor transient performance.

Before we state Proposition
4.1
, we introduce the following technical assumption.

Assumption 1
.

Given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
, assume that there exists
p\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\alpha^{*}(\mu)=\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s)
. In this case, we define
\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}:=\left\{{p\in\mathcal{P}:\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s)=\alpha^{*}(\mu)}\right\}
.

We note that Assumption
1
serves as the minimal condition that makes the definition (
4.1
) meaningful when
w(T)
is not
O(1/T)
. Indeed, if there is no
p\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\alpha^{*}(\mu)=\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s)
, then for any
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}
,

E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)]=E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha_{p}(X_{0})]+T\left(\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s)-\alpha^{*}(\mu)\right)=\Omega(T)

as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

On the other hand, even if
\mathcal{P}
is compact and convex, a worst-case kernel need not exist
[
7
, Proposition 3.2]
. However, existence can be guaranteed when
\mathcal{P}
is weakly communicating. We summarize this fact in the following lemma as a side result. To streamline the discussion of transient values, we defer the proof of Lemma
1
to Appendix
A.1
.

Lemma 1

(Existence of Worst-Case Kernels)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{P}
is compact and weakly communicating. Then Assumption
1
holds.

With a well-defined finite transient value under Assumption
1
, we establish some important properties.

Proposition 4.1

(Properties of Transient Values)
.

Fix
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
and suppose that Assumption
1
is in force. Let

V^{*}:=\left\{{v\in\left\{{S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}\right\}:(\alpha_{p^{*}},v)\text{ solves \eqref{eqn:multichain_eqn} with $p=p^{*}$ for some }p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}}\right\}.

Then, for any history-dependent policy
\pi
,

\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\pi)\leq\inf_{v\in V^{*}}\left|v\right|_{\mathrm{span}}.

(4.2)

In particular,
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi)\leq 0
for any weight function such that
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}w(T)=0
.

In contrast, the transient values of optimal policies are, in general, not lower bounded. Specifically, there exists an MDP instance that satisfies the following. For any weight function
w
such that
Tw(T)\rightarrow\infty
as
T\rightarrow\infty
, there exists a policy
\pi_{w}
that is optimal in both the liminf and limsup average-reward criteria with
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi_{w})=-\infty
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

We first prove the upper bound (
4.2
). Note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})}\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})}\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}}\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})}\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)\right]

By Assumption
1
, we have that for all
{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}}
,
\alpha^{*}(\mu)=\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p^{*}}(s)=E_{\mu}[\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{0})]
, where
X_{0}\sim\mu
. Therefore, we further have

\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\pi)\leq\inf_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}}\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})}\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{0})\right].

For any fixed
p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu}^{*}
, let
V_{p^{*}}=\left\{{v\in\left\{{S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}\right\}:(\alpha_{p^{*}},v)\text{ solves \eqref{eqn:multichain_eqn} with $p=p^{*}$}}\right\}
. Notice that

\inf_{v\in V^{*}}\left|v\right|_{\mathrm{span}}=\inf_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}}\inf_{v\in V_{p^{*}}}\left|v\right|_{\mathrm{span}}.

Therefore, to conclude (
4.2
), it suffices to show that for any
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
,

\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{0})\right]\leq\inf_{v\in V_{p^{*}}}\left|v\right|_{\mathrm{span}}.

(4.3)

To this end, we recall the multichain Bellman equation (
2.2
). In particular, with
p
replaced by
p^{*}
and
(\alpha_{p^{*}},v_{p^{*}})
as a solution pair, we have that

\displaystyle\alpha_{p^{*}}(s)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}p^{*}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\alpha_{p^{*}}(s^{\prime}),

\displaystyle v_{p^{*}}(s)

\displaystyle\geq r(s,a)-\alpha_{p^{*}}(s)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}p^{*}(s^{\prime}|s,a)v_{p^{*}}(s^{\prime})

for all
a\in A
. In particular, we have that for any
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathcal{Q})
,

\displaystyle E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}[\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{t+1})-\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{t})|\mathcal{H}_{t}]\leq 0,

\displaystyle E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{t})+v_{p^{*}}(X_{t+1})-v_{p^{*}}(X_{t})|\mathcal{H}_{t}]\leq 0.

Summing over
t=0,\dots,T-1
and take expectation, we have that

0\geq E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{t+1})-\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{t})\right]=E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{T})-E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{0}),

(4.4)

and hence

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{t})+v_{p^{*}}(X_{t+1})-v_{p^{*}}(X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{t})\right]+E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\left[v_{p^{*}}(X_{T})-v_{p^{*}}(X_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\geq E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{0}))\right]-\left|v_{p^{*}}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}

where the last inequality follows from (
4.4
), implying that
-E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{t})\geq-E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
, together with
v_{p^{*}}(X_{T})-v_{p^{*}}(X_{0})\geq-\left|v_{p^{*}}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}
. Since
(\alpha_{p^{*}},v_{p^{*}})
is an arbitrary solution pair to (
2.2
) and
\alpha_{p^{*}}
is unique,

E_{\mu}^{\pi,p^{*}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha_{p^{*}}(X_{0})\right]\leq\inf_{v\in V_{p^{*}}}\left|v\right|_{\mathrm{span}}.

(4.5)

This implies (
4.3
) and hence (
4.2
).

Next, we show that
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi)\leq 0
for any weight function satisfying
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}w(T)=0
.
The argument relies on the following elementary lemma, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
A.2
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\left\{{g(T):{T\geq 1}}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
w:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow(0,1]
with
w(T)\to 0
as
T\to\infty
.
Assume that
\liminf_{T\to\infty}g(T)\leq C
for some
C<\infty
.
Then,
\liminf_{T\to\infty}w(T)g(T)\leq 0.

To apply Lemma
2
, fix
p\in\mathcal{P}
and define

g(\mu,\pi,p,T):=E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)\right].

By (
4.5
), we have
\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}g(\mu,\pi,p^{*},T)<\infty
.
Then, for any
w:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow(0,1]
with
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}w(T)=0
, applying Lemma
2
yields

\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi)=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\liminf_{T\to\infty}w(T)g(\mu,\pi,p,T)\leq\liminf_{T\to\infty}w(T)g(\mu,\pi,p^{*},T)\leq 0.

This shows that the weighted transient value is always non-positive whenever the weighting function
w(T)
vanishes as
T\to\infty
.

To show the final statement, we construct a simple robust MDP instance with
S=\left\{{s}\right\}
and
A=\left\{{a_{0},a_{1}}\right\}
. In this setting, there is only one possible transition kernel
p
, defined by
p(s|s,a_{i})=1
for
i=0,1
. Therefore, the only possible ambiguity set is
\mathcal{P}=\left\{{p}\right\}
. We let
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
or
\left\{{\delta_{a_{0}},\delta_{a_{1}}}\right\}
, and consider a reward function
r(s,a_{i})=r(a_{i})=i
for
i=0,1
. It is immediate that the optimal average reward is
1
, achieved by the policy that always plays action
a_{1}
.

For any fixed weight function
w
, we construct an optimal deterministic Markovian but non-stationary policy
\pi_{w}=(\pi_{w,0},\pi_{w,1},\pi_{w,2},\dots)
, where
\pi_{w,t}(a_{i}|s)\in\left\{{0,1}\right\}
, and show that
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi_{w})=-\infty
.

To achieve this, consider a sequence of time points
\left\{{T_{n}:n\geq 1}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
satisfying
T_{1}=1
,

T_{n+1}\geq 2T_{n},\quad\text{and}\quad T_{n}w(T_{n})\geq n^{2}+n.

(4.6)

Such a sequence exists because
Tw(T)
can be made arbitrarily large as
T
increases.
Next, define a sequence
\left\{{B_{n}\in\mathbb{N}:n\geq 1}\right\}
, to be specified later, satisfying
0\leq B_{n+1}-B_{n}\leq T_{n+1}-T_{n}
. Intuitively,
B_{n}
represents the number of “bad” actions played before time
T_{n}
.

Specifically, we define the policy as follows. For
t=T_{n},\dots,T_{n+1}-(B_{n+1}-B_{n})-1
, the policy plays the good action, i.e.,
\pi_{w,t}(a_{1}|s)=1
. For the remaining time steps
t=T_{n+1}-(B_{n+1}-B_{n}),\dots,T_{n+1}-1
, the policy plays the bad action, i.e.,
\pi_{w,t}(a_{0}|s)=1
.

For
T_{n}\leq T\leq T_{n+1}-1
, since
\pi_{w}
first plays the good action and then the bad action, the fraction of bad actions played satisfies

0\leq 1-\frac{1}{T}E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t},A_{t})\leq\max\left\{{\frac{B_{n}}{T_{n}},\frac{B_{n+1}}{T_{n+1}}}\right\}.

Therefore, if
T_{n}^{-1}B_{n}\to 0
, then
\pi_{w}
is an optimal policy under both the limsup and liminf average-reward criteria. On the other hand,

w(T_{n})E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\sum_{t=0}^{T_{n}-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-1]=-w(T_{n})B_{n},

so
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi_{w})=-\infty
if
\pi_{w}
is optimal and
w(T_{n})B_{n}\to\infty
.

Hence, to finish the proof, it suffices to find
B_{n}
satisfying
B_{n+1}-B_{n}\leq T_{n+1}-T_{n}
,
T_{n}^{-1}B_{n}\to 0
, and
w(T_{n})B_{n}\to\infty
.
Let
B_{n}:=\lfloor T_{n}/n\rfloor
. Then,

B_{n+1}-B_{n}\leq\left\lfloor{\frac{T_{n+1}}{n+1}}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor{\frac{T_{n+1}}{2}}\right\rfloor\leq T_{n+1}-T_{n},

where the last inequality follows from (
4.6
). Moreover, by the same conditions,

\frac{B_{n}}{T_{n}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\to 0,\quad\text{and}\quad w(T_{n})B_{n}\geq\frac{T_{n}w(T_{n})}{n}-1\geq n\to\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

From Proposition
4.1
, if
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi)>-\infty
, then
1/w(T)
can be interpreted as the growth rate of the deviation between the cumulative reward up to time
T
and
T
times the optimal average reward. In particular, when
w(T)\geq\Omega(1/T)
(for example,
w(T)=1/\sqrt{T}
), the condition
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi)>-\infty
provides a stronger form of optimality certificate than the long-run average criterion, formally suggesting that

\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{1}{T}E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t},A_{t})=\alpha^{*}(\mu)-O\left(\cfrac{1}{Tw(T)}\right).

On the other hand, when
w(T)=\frac{1}{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left[\frac{1}{T}E_{\mu}^{\pi,p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t},A_{t})\right]-\alpha^{*}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\underline{\alpha}(\mu,\pi,p)-\alpha^{*}(\mu).

Hence, when
{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\neq\varnothing
, by Theorem
1
, any
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
satisfies
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}})=0
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
.
In contrast, any suboptimal policy
\pi
under the liminf average-reward criterion satisfies
-1\leq\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi)<0
.
Therefore, suboptimal policies have negative transient values, with magnitude growing at rate
O(1/w(T))=O(T)
.

4.2
Transient Value Lower Bounds for RL Policies

We have shown that finite lower bounds on
\mathrm{TV}_{w}
translate into transient performance guarantees for an optimal policy. We now derive such lower bounds for RL policies
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
by leveraging expected regret upper bounds. Recall that, by Definition
1
, any
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
satisfies
E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}R_{r,p}(T)=o(T)
. The proposition below quantifies how sublinear regret rates yield a corresponding lower bound on
\mathrm{TV}_{w}
.

Proposition 4.2

(Transient Value Lower Bound for RL Policies)
.

Fix
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
and
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
. Suppose Assumption
1
holds. Moreover, assume that for any
p\in\mathcal{P}
, there exists a constant
c_{p}>0
and a weight function
w_{p}
such that
Tw_{p}(T)\rightarrow\infty
and

E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}R_{r,p}(T)\leq\frac{c_{p}}{w_{p}(T)}

(4.7)

for all
T\geq 1
. Then, for all weight functions
w
such that
Tw(T)\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}})\geq-\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}}\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{c_{p^{*}}w(T)}{w_{p^{*}}(T)}.

Remark 1
.

Before presenting the proof, we note that Proposition
4.2
identifies how the decay rate of
w(T)
needed to ensure
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}})>-\infty
is governed by the regret growth rate of
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}
against the
worst-case kernels

p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}
that attain the robust optimal average reward
\alpha^{*}(\mu)
. Importantly, this dependence is through the quantities
w_{p^{*}}(T)
, and therefore can be less stringent than what would be suggested by the worst-case rate
\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}}w_{p}(T)
.

In particular, a typical minimax-optimal RL policy achieves
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})

[
11
,
30
]
regret for all weakly communicating
p\in\mathcal{P}
, with a common growth rate but instance-dependent constants. In this case, one may take
w_{p}(T)=\widetilde{\Omega}(T^{-1/2})
for all such
p
. For instance, applying Proposition
4.2
to the RL policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ZX}}
in Example
2
, we obtain that under the assumptions of Corollary
1.1
, for any weight function
w(T)=O(T^{-1/2-\epsilon})
, we have
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi_{\mathrm{ZX}})=0
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
.

On the other hand, if a policy
\pi
is designed to adapt to the sub-optimal
p\in\mathcal{P}
and, in addition, achieves a
slower
regret growth rate (ideally
O(1)
) against the particular kernels
p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}
, then Proposition
4.2
permits
w(T)
to decay more slowly while still ensuring
\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,\pi)>-\infty
. This highlights that transient values of optimal policies in robust MDPs can behave fundamentally differently from minimax regret in standard online RL. We leverage this observation in the next section to construct robust optimal policies with constant-order transient values.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

We note that for any
p\in\mathcal{P}
, by the definition of the regret in (
3.1
),

\displaystyle E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)]

\displaystyle=E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r(X_{t},A_{t})\right]-T\alpha^{*}(\mu)

\displaystyle=-E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}R_{r,p}(T)+T\left[\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s)-\alpha^{*}(\mu)\right].

Therefore, by (
4.7
), we have that for any
p\in\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)]

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{c_{p}}{w_{p}(T)}+T\left[\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s)-\alpha^{*}(\mu)\right].

In particular, if
p\notin\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}
, then
\sum_{s\in S}\mu(s)\alpha_{p}(s)-\alpha^{*}(\mu)>0
, and hence for any weight function
Tw(T)\rightarrow\infty
,

\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}w(T)E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)]=\infty,

while for
p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}w(T)E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p^{*}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)]\geq\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}-\frac{c_{p^{*}}w(T)}{w_{p^{*}}(T)}.

Therefore, by the definition of the transient value,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}_{w}(\mu,{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}})

\displaystyle=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}w(T)E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}}\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}w(T)E_{\mu}^{{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}},p}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[r(X_{t},A_{t})-\alpha^{*}(\mu)]

\displaystyle\geq-\sup_{p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}}\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{c_{p^{*}}w(T)}{w_{p^{*}}(T)}.

where
(i)
follows from the fact that for any
p\notin\mathcal{P}^{*}_{\mu}
, the corresponding limit is positive infinity.
∎

5
A Policy with Constant Order Transient Value

This section develops and analyzes an optimal policy with constant-order transient value (
w\equiv 1
) of the same order as the bias span of an average-reward optimal model, rather than diverging with time.

Assuming a weakly communicating scenario, the design of this policy draws inspiration from the observation in Remark
1
, as well as the fact that the controller “knows” the optimal adversarial response, i.e.,
p^{*}
such that
\alpha_{p^{*}}=\alpha^{*}
, so long as its policy achieves online RL.

Specifically, we construct a policy that combines (i) an optimal stationary policy
\Delta^{*}
for the worst-case kernel
p^{*}
with (ii) an anytime-valid sequential test that detects when the observed trajectory is inconsistent with the induced optimal Markov kernel
p^{*}_{\Delta^{*}}
and (iii) a reference online RL policy
{\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}\in{\Pi_{\mathrm{RL}}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
.

This policy starts by drawing decisions from
\Delta^{*}
while computing the test statistics along the way. If the test never rejects and we keep using
\Delta^{*}
, then the transition dynamics behave essentially like the Markov chain under
(\Delta^{*},p^{*})
. By the characterization (
3.5
), this yields the optimal average reward
\alpha^{*}
with
-O(\left|v_{p^{*}}\right|_{\mathrm{span}})
transient value. On the other hand, if the test rightfully rejects, then the policy switches to a reference online RL policy
\pi_{\mathrm{RL}}
, ensuring that under any strictly suboptimal kernel the transient value grows without bound.

Central to the design of this policy is the calibration of the sequential test. We need to ensure that false rejections, i.e., type-I error, occur with sufficiently small probability
\rho
, while true rejections occur quickly, with expected delay
O(\log(1/\rho))
whenever the alternative differs from
p^{*}_{\Delta^{*}}
; see Theorem
2
. These ingredients are then assembled into the epoch-based policy
\pi^{*}
and analyzed to yield an explicit constant-order lower bound on
\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\pi^{*})
; see Theorem
3
.

5.1
A Brief Introduction to Sequential Probability Ratio Tests for Markov Chains

A key component of our construction is a sequential likelihood-ratio-type statistic that continuously tests the null hypothesis that the trajectory is generated by the kernel
P_{0}:=p^{*}_{\Delta^{*}}
against a composite alternative in which the trajectory is generated by some other kernel. This is in the spirit of Wald’s sequential probability ratio test
[
21
]
, adapted to Markovian dependence and to a composite alternative via a mixture likelihood ratio.

We proceed with defining the mixture likelihood ratio process and the rejection time.

Definition 4

(Mixture Likelihood Ratio Process and Rejection Time)
.

Let
P_{0}
be the null transition kernel and let
\Pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{|S|\times|S|}))
be a prior on transition kernels. For
n\geq 1
, define the mixture likelihood ratio process

\Lambda_{n}:=\int\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}\frac{Q(X_{t+1}|X_{t})}{P_{0}(X_{t+1}|X_{t})}\Pi(dQ),

(5.1)

with the convention
q/0=+\infty
for
q>0
.
Define for
\rho\in(0,1)
the rejection time

\tau_{\rho}:=\inf\left\{{n\geq 1:P_{0}(X_{n}|X_{n-1})=0\ \text{or}\ \Lambda_{n}\geq\frac{1}{\rho}}\right\}.

(5.2)

Definition 5

(Product Dirichlet Prior)
.

Fix parameters
\left\{{\gamma(s^{\prime}|s)>0:s,s^{\prime}\in S}\right\}
.
Let
\Pi
be the
product Dirichlet prior
, defined as the law of random matrix
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|\times|S|}
with independent rows, where for each
s\in S
,
\Phi(\cdot|s)\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}\left(\gamma(\cdot|s)\right)

and
\Phi(\cdot|s)
is independent of
\Phi(\cdot|s^{\prime})
for all
s\neq s^{\prime}
.

Before we state the main theorem of this section, we define the following notation and terminology. For a transition kernel
Q
, viewed as a matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{S\times S}
, let
Q|_{C}
denote the sub-kernel on
C\subseteq S
, i.e., the principal submatrix of
Q
indexed by
(s,s^{\prime})\in C\times C
. Also, let
\mathcal{C}_{Q}
denote the set of closed communicating classes of
Q
. We say that a transition kernel
Q
is unichain if
\mathcal{C}_{Q}=\left\{{C_{0}}\right\}
for some
C_{0}\subseteq S
; i.e.,
Q
has only one closed communicating class, possibly with some transient states.

Theorem 2

(Properties of the Rejection Time)
.

Let
\Pi
be the product Dirichlet prior with parameter
\gamma
in Definition
5
. Let
P_{0}
be unichain with
C_{0}\subseteq S
as its only closed communicating class. Then the following properties hold for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
:

1.

The type-I-error of ever rejecting under
P_{0}
is bounded by
\rho
; i.e.,

P_{\mu}^{P_{0}}(\tau_{\rho}<\infty)\leq\rho

(5.3)

2.

Let
P
be an alternative transition kernel. Suppose
P|_{C_{0}}\neq P_{0}|_{C_{0}}
, then there exists some constant
K<\infty
that only depend on
(P,P_{0},\gamma)
such that

E_{\mu}^{P}\tau_{\rho}\leq K\left(\log\frac{1}{\rho}+1\right)

(5.4)

The proof of this result is presented in Section
5.1.1
. We note that the expected rejection time scales with
\log(1/\rho)
instead of
1/\rho
, an important observation that enables the design of our policy in Section
5.2
.

5.1.1
Proof of Theorem
2

Proof.

For clarity, we prove the two statements of Theorem
2
separately, each via a stand-alone proposition. The proofs of these propositions are presented in Section
5.1.2
.

We show a more general version of (
5.3
) summarized in the following Proposition
5.1
.

Proposition 5.1

(Type-I error control)
.

Let
P_{0}
be any transition kernel on
S
and let
\Pi
be any prior. For every
\rho\in(0,1)
, the type-I error of ever rejecting under
P_{0}
is bounded by
\rho
; i.e.,
P_{\mu}^{P_{0}}\left(\tau_{\rho}<\infty\right)\leq\rho.

This proposition immediately implies the first statement of Theorem
2
.

Next, we prove the second statement of Theorem
2
. We first present Lemma
3
, which states an important property of the product Dirichlet prior in Definition
5
. This property underlies our analysis by turning the mixture likelihood ratio process
\Lambda_{n}
into a likelihood ratio process for testing against a simple alternative
P
. The proof of Lemma
3
is deferred to
A.3
.

Lemma 3

(Uniformity of Product Dirichlet Prior)
.

Let
\Pi
be the product Dirichlet prior with parameter
\gamma
in Definition
5
. Then, for any transition kernel
P
and every
\epsilon>0
, the set

U(\epsilon,P):=\left\{{Q:\ Q(s^{\prime}|s)\geq e^{-\epsilon}P(s^{\prime}|s)\ \ \forall s,s^{\prime}\in S}\right\}

(5.5)

satisfies
\Pi(U(\epsilon,P))>0
.

We define the following parameter

\beta(\rho,\epsilon):=\log\frac{1}{\rho\Pi(U(\epsilon,P))}.

(5.6)

By Lemma
3
,
\beta(\rho,\epsilon)<\infty
for any
\epsilon>0
and
\rho\in(0,1)
.

Let
P
be a transition kernel on a finite state space
S
and
C\subseteq S
be one of its closed communicating classes. We denote
\nu_{P,C}
as the unique stationary distribution of a Markov chain with transition kernel
P
restricted to a closed communicating class
C
. Moreover, for any
C\in\mathcal{C}_{P}
, we define the following
\nu_{P,C}
-weighted KL-divergence.

Definition 6

(KL-Divergence between Transition Kernels)
.

Consider two transition kernels
P
and
P_{0}
on

. Let
C
be a closed communicating class of
P
. We define the stationary distribution weighted KL-divergence as

\displaystyle I_{C}(P\|P_{0})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{s\in C}\nu_{P,C}(s)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}P(s^{\prime}|s)\log\left(\frac{P(s^{\prime}|s)}{P_{0}(s^{\prime}|s)}\right)

(5.7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in C}\nu_{P,C}(s)\mathrm{KL}(P(\cdot|s)\|P_{0}(\cdot|s)).

where
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\|\cdot)
is the KL divergence, with
\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu)=\infty
if
\mu(s)>0
and
\nu(s)=0
for some

.

Then, the second statement of Theorem
2
will follow from the following proposition, whose proof is deferred to the next section.

Proposition 5.2

(Bounds on Expected Rejection Time)
.

Fix an alternative transition kernel
P
on
S
and
C\in\mathcal{C}_{P}
, let
T_{C}:=\inf\left\{{t\geq 0:X_{t}\in C}\right\}
and write
\mathds{1}_{C}:=\mathds{1}\left\{{T_{C}<\infty}\right\}.

If
I_{C}(P\|P_{0})=\infty
, then there exists
K_{C}<\infty
, depending only on
(P,P_{0},C)
, such that for all
\rho\in(0,1)

E_{\mu}^{P}\tau_{\rho}\mathds{1}_{C}\leq K_{C}.

(5.8)

If
0<I_{C}(P\|P_{0})<\infty
, let
\epsilon:=\frac{1}{2}I_{C}(P\|P_{0})
and let
\beta(\rho,\epsilon)
be as in (
5.6
).
There exists
K_{C}<\infty
, depending only on
(P,P_{0},C)
, such that for all
\rho\in(0,1)
,

E_{\mu}^{P}\tau_{\rho}\mathds{1}_{C}\leq K_{C}(1+\beta(\rho,\epsilon)).

(5.9)

To apply Proposition
5.2
, we verify that if
P|_{C_{0}}\neq P_{0}|_{C_{0}}
,

for all
C\in\mathcal{C}_{P}
. We show the contrapositive statement that, if

, then
P|_{C_{0}}=P_{0}|_{C_{0}}
.

Since
C
is a closed communicating class of

,
\nu_{P,C}(s)>0
for all
s\in C
. So, if
I_{C}(P\|P_{0})=0
,

\mathrm{KL}(P(\cdot|s)\|P_{0}(\cdot|s))=0

for every
s\in C
. Hence
P(\cdot|s)=P_{0}(\cdot|s)
for all

.

Because
C

s\in C

Therefore, under the assumptions of the second statement of Theorem

,

. Hence, Proposition

K:=\left(\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{P}}K_{C}\right)\cdot\left(1+\max_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{P},\ I_{C}(P\|P_{0})<\infty}\log\left[\frac{1}{\Pi\left(U(\frac{1}{2}I_{C}(P\|P_{0}),P))\right)}\right]\right)

E_{\mu}^{P}\tau_{\rho}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{P}}E_{\mu}^{P}\tau_{\rho}\mathds{1}\left\{{T_{C}<\infty}\right\}\leq K\left(\log\frac{1}{\rho}+1\right).

.

Proof of Propositions

and

Proof of Proposition

We first note that

Lemma 4

(Ville’s Inequality)

Let

With these preparations, we construct the policy

, we define

as follows. For

Theorem 3

(Transient value bound for fixed-length epochs)
.

Suppose Assumption
2

\mathcal{P}^{*}=\left\{{p^{*}}\right\}
is a singleton. Construct
\pi^{*}
as in (

1

•

with

,

.

•

Then, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(S)
and

\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\pi^{*})\geq-\frac{2^{\zeta}}{2^{\zeta}-1}\left|v^{*}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}-\frac{1}{2^{\zeta-1}-1},

(5.28)

where

is the unique (up-to a shift) solution to

Remark 3
.

It is possible to achieve an

transient value without the identifiability or irreducibility assumptions. In this case, one can consider a more complex policy with an additional test statistic that rejects when the chain is not absorbed into
C_{0}
quickly enough. However, without the identifiability or irreducibility assumptions, we believe it may not be possible to obtain a clean
-O(\left|v^{*}\right|_{\mathrm{span}})
lower bound on the transient value, since weakly communicating alternative kernels with
C_{0}
as their only closed communicating class under
\Delta^{*}
can have arbitrarily large hitting times to

. So, the maximum expected hitting time could also enter the lower bound.

Therefore, in this paper, we opt to introduce these additional assumptions in order to obtain the clean result in Theorem

, with explicit bounds and clear algorithm design intuition.

The assumption on the uniqueness of
p^{*}
could potentially be removed, while maintaining an

transient value, if

and we tailor the policy design to the specific adversarial environments

is finite, there exists a policy that achieves

regret. However, this cannot be generalized to more general multi-arm bandit settings with continuum

, as evidenced by the classical

regret lower bound established by
Lai and Robbins, 1985
; see also Theorems 6 and 8 in
Bubeck et al., 2013
.

\ell_{s^{\prime}}(y_{x,s^{\prime}})=\ell_{s^{\prime}}(x)-1,\qquad p(y_{x,s^{\prime}}|x,a_{x,s^{\prime}})>0.

Define a stationary deterministic policy
\pi^{s^{\prime}}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{SD}}
by
\pi^{s^{\prime}}(x)=a_{x,s^{\prime}}
for
x\neq s^{\prime}
and take arbitrary action at
s^{\prime}
. Under
\pi^{s^{\prime}}
, from every
x\in S
there is a path to
s^{\prime}
of length at most

m_{p}(s^{\prime}):=\max_{x\in S}\ell_{s^{\prime}}(x)<\infty

and with strictly positive probability under
(\pi^{s^{\prime}},p)
. Since there are only finitely many such designated paths, their probabilities admit a strictly positive minimum:

\eta_{p}(s^{\prime}):=\min_{x\in S}P_{x}^{\pi^{s^{\prime}},p}(\tau_{s^{\prime}}\leq m_{p}(s^{\prime}))>0.

By continuity of hitting probabilities in
q
, there exists
\delta_{p}^{(2)}(s^{\prime})>0
such that, whenever
d(p,q)\leq\delta_{p}^{(2)}(s^{\prime})
,

P_{x}^{\pi^{s^{\prime}},q}(\tau_{s^{\prime}}\leq m_{p}(s^{\prime}))\geq\eta_{p}(s^{\prime})/2,

for all
x\in S.

By the Markov property, this implies the geometric tail bound

P_{x}^{\pi^{s^{\prime}},q}(\tau_{s^{\prime}}>km_{p}(s^{\prime}))\leq(1-\eta_{p}(s^{\prime})/2)^{k},\qquad k\geq 0.

Therefore, let
\delta^{(2)}_{p}:=\min_{s^{\prime}\in C_{p}}\delta_{p}^{(2)}(s^{\prime})

\sup_{q:d(p,q)\leq\delta_{p}^{(2)}}\max_{s\in S,s^{\prime}\in C_{p}}E_{s}^{\pi^{s^{\prime}},q}\tau_{s^{\prime}}\leq\max_{s^{\prime}\in C_{p}}\frac{2m_{p}(s^{\prime})}{\eta_{p}(s^{\prime})}=:L_{p}.

(A.3)

Now fix
q\in\mathcal{P}
with

d(p,q)\leq\delta_{p}:=\min\{\delta_{p}^{(1)},\delta_{p}^{(2)}\},

and choose

\bar{s}=\bar{s}(q)\in\arg\max_{s\in C_{p}}v_{q}(s).

Let
\pi_{q}^{*}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{SD}}
be a stationary deterministic policy attaining the maximum in the Bellman equation for
q
. Since
0\leq r\leq 1
, we also have
0\leq\alpha_{q}\leq 1
, hence
-1\leq r-\alpha_{q}\leq 1
. Moreover the Bellman equation implies that

G_{n}:=v_{q}(X_{n})+\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}[r(X_{t},\pi_{q}^{*}(X_{t}))-\alpha_{q}]

is a
P_{s}^{\pi^{*}_{q},q}
martingale.

Since
\delta_{p}\leq\delta_{p}^{(1)}
,
P_{s}^{\pi^{*}_{q},q}(\tau_{C_{p}}<\infty)=1
. Therefore, stopping at
\tau_{C_{p}}\wedge n
and letting
n\uparrow\infty
, we obtain for every
s\in S
,

v_{q}(s)=E_{s}^{\pi_{q}^{*},q}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\tau_{C_{p}}-1}\bigl(r(X_{t},\pi_{q}^{*}(X_{t}))-\alpha_{q}\bigr)+v_{q}(X_{\tau_{C_{p}}})\right]\leq v_{q}(\bar{s})+E_{s}^{\pi_{q}^{*},q}\tau_{C_{p}},

because
X_{\tau_{C_{p}}}\in C_{p}
and
v_{q}(X_{\tau_{C_{p}}})\leq v_{q}(\bar{s})
. Therefore, by (
A.2
),

\max_{s\in S}v_{q}(s)\leq v_{q}(\bar{s})+M_{p}.

(A.4)

On the other hand, for any
s\in S
, apply the same arguments to the Bellman inequality

v_{q}(s)\geq r(s,\pi^{\bar{s}}(s))-\alpha_{q}+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}q(s^{\prime}|s,\pi^{\bar{s}}(s))v_{q}(s^{\prime})

and the stopping time
\tau_{\bar{s}}\wedge n
and letting
n\uparrow\infty
yields

v_{q}(s)\geq E_{s}^{\pi^{\bar{s}},q}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\tau_{\bar{s}}-1}\bigl(r(X_{t},\pi^{\bar{s}}(X_{t}))-\alpha_{q}\bigr)+v_{q}(\bar{s})\right]\geq v_{q}(\bar{s})-E_{s}^{\pi^{\bar{s}},q}\tau_{\bar{s}}.

Hence, by (
A.3
),

v_{q}(\bar{s})-\min_{s\in S}v_{q}(s)\leq\max_{s\in S}E_{s}^{\pi^{\bar{s}},q}\tau_{\bar{s}}\leq L_{p}.

(A.5)

Combining (
A.4
) and (
A.5
), we conclude that

\sup_{q:d(p,q)\leq\delta_{p}}\left|v_{q}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}\leq M_{p}+L_{p}<\infty.

Returning to (
A.1
), for
d(p,q)\leq\delta_{p}
,

|\alpha_{q}-\alpha_{p}|\leq d(p,q)\bigl(\left|v_{p}\right|_{\mathrm{span}}+M_{p}+L_{p}\bigr).

Thus
q\to p
implies
\alpha_{q}\to\alpha_{p}
. Therefore
p\rightarrow\alpha_{p}
is continuous on
\mathcal{P}
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is compact, there exists
p^{*}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\alpha_{p^{*}}=\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\alpha_{p}=\alpha^{*}.

Therefore, Assumption
1
holds.
∎

A.7
Proof of Lemma
7

Proof.

Since
p
is weakly communicating, let
C_{p}\subseteq S
be as in Definition
2
. It is standard for finite weakly communicating average-reward MDPs that there exists an average-reward optimal deterministic stationary policy
[
18
]
; fix one and denote it by
\bar{\Delta}
, and write
\bar{P}:=p_{\bar{\Delta}}
.

Let
\bar{C}
be any closed communicating class of
\bar{P}
. Since every
s\in C_{p}^{c}
is transient under every stationary policy, no recurrent class can intersect
C_{p}^{c}
, hence
\bar{C}\subseteq C_{p}
.

Define a directed graph on the vertex set
C_{p}
by putting an edge
s\to s^{\prime}
if there exists an action
a\in A
with
p(s^{\prime}|s,a)>0
. The weak communication property implies that this graph is strongly connected; in particular, for every
s\in C_{p}
there exists a directed path from
s
to some state in
\bar{C}
.

For
s\in C_{p}
, let
d(s)
denote the graph distance of
s
from
\bar{C}
, i.e.,

d(s):=\min\{n\geq 0:\exists\,s^{\prime}\in\bar{C}\ \text{s.t. there is a directed path of length $n$ from $s$ to $s^{\prime}$}\}.

Then
d(s)=0
if and only if
s\in\bar{C}
. Moreover, since
\bar{C}\subseteq C_{p}
and the graph on
C_{p}
is strongly connected,
d(s)<\infty
for all
s\in C_{p}
.

For each
s\in C_{p}\setminus\bar{C}
, choose a state
\mathrm{nxt}(s)\in C_{p}
and an action
a_{s}\in A
such that

d(\mathrm{nxt}(s))=d(s)-1,\quad\text{and}\quad p(\mathrm{nxt}(s)\mid s,a_{s})>0.

Now define a deterministic stationary policy
\Delta
by

\Delta(\cdot|s)=\begin{cases}\bar{\Delta}(\cdot|s),&s\in\bar{C},\\
\delta_{a_{s}},&s\in C_{p}\setminus\bar{C},\\
\bar{\Delta}(\cdot|s),&s\in C_{p}^{c}.\end{cases}

Let
P:=p_{\Delta}
.

Since
\Delta
agrees with
\bar{\Delta}
on
\bar{C}
, we have
P(\cdot\mid s)=\bar{P}(\cdot\mid s)
for all
s\in\bar{C}
, and therefore
\bar{C}
remains a closed communicating class of
P
.

Since all states in
C_{p}^{c}
are transient under every stationary policy (in particular under
\Delta
),
P
cannot have any closed communicating class contained in
C_{p}^{c}
.

Next, fix any
s\in C_{p}\setminus\bar{C}
. By construction, iterating
\mathrm{nxt}(\cdot)
produces a directed path of length

that stays in
C_{p}\setminus\bar{C}
until it reaches
\bar{C}
, and each transition along this path has strictly positive probability under
\Delta
. Hence

P^{d(s)}(\bar{C}\mid s)>0,\qquad\forall s\in C_{p}.

In particular, no closed communicating class of
P
contained in
C_{p}
can be disjoint from
\bar{C}
.

Combining the previous observations, any closed communicating class of
P
must lie in
C_{p}
and must intersect
\bar{C}

\bar{C}
is itself closed, it follows that

\bar{C}
as its unique closed communicating class; i.e.,

coincides with the optimal policy
\bar{\Delta}

\bar{C}

\bar{C}

. Therefore, the long-run average reward under

\bar{C}

\bar{\Delta}
, which is
\alpha_{p}
. Hence,
\Delta
is optimal.
