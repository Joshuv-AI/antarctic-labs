Psychopathological correlates of early childhood sleep terror: associations with nutritional biomarkers, behavioral dysregulation, and sleep disturbance
Authors: Makbule Esen Öksüzoğlu, Hilmi Onur Kabukçu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep terror (ST) is a common NREM parasomnia in early childhood and may be associated with broader emotional and behavioral dysregulation. However, its links with psychopathological symptoms and nutritional biomarkers remain unclear. This study compared children with ST and healthy controls (HC) in terms of psychopathology, sleep features, and biochemical markers, and explored short-term changes in a subgroup of children with ST receiving supplementation for documented micronutrient deficiencies.MethodsForty-eight children aged 24–60 months with ST and 48 HC were evaluated for sociodemographic, sleep, biochemical, anthropometric, and standardized scale data obtained from electronic medical records. A subset of 25 children with ST with documented micronutrient deficiencies received guideline-based supplementation and had biochemical and scale reassessments after a mean interval of 90.6 days, as part of routine clinical follow-up.ResultsChildren with ST showed shorter sleep duration and higher rates of bedtime resistance at the nominal level, but these differences did not survive FDR correction (FDR p = .092 and 0.069, respectively). Only nocturnal awakenings remained significant after FDR correction (FDR p = .028). In contrast, total anxiety, behavioral dysregulation, and sleep disturbance scores were markedly higher in the ST group and remained significant after FDR correction (all FDR p < .001). Vitamin D (20.4 vs. 25.8 ng/mL; p = .015, FDR p = .026), vitamin B12 (231.7 vs. 309.4 pg/mL; p = .002, FDR p = .003), and folate (11.3 vs. 13.1 ng/mL; p = .016, FDR p = .028) levels were lower in the ST group. At follow-up, vitamin B12 increased significantly (201.8→281.1 pg/mL; p = .006, FDR p = .009; r = .631, 95% CI [0.237, 0.926]), whereas other biomarkers did not change significantly after FDR correction. Anxiety, behavioral dysregulation, and sleep disturbance scores decreased substantially; however, change-score analyses did not show statistically robust associations between vitamin B12 change and symptom change after FDR correction.ConclusionST in early childhood was associated with a multidimensional profile of anxiety, behavioral dysregulation, parent-rated sleep disturbance, and lower vitamin D, vitamin B12, and folate levels. Clinical symptom differences remained robust after FDR correction. During naturalistic follow-up, vitamin B12 increased and symptom scores decreased, but change-score analyses did not support a direct biomarker–symptom association. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Larger prospective, controlled studies are needed to clarify the role of micronutrient status in early childhood ST.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08298-1.Keywords: Sleep terror, NREM parasomnia, Psychopathology, Behavioral dysregulation, Anxiety, Micronutrients

Introduction

Sleep terror (ST) is a parasomnia characterized by sudden arousals from non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, accompanied by behaviors such as abrupt sitting or rising from bed, inconsolable screaming, and pronounced autonomic activation, including tachycardia, tachypnea, and diaphoresis. During an episode, affected children typically show minimal responsiveness to external stimuli, and retrograde amnesia for the event is common upon full awakening [6]. In childhood, the etiology of ST is considered multifactorial, involving a combination of biological, environmental, and psychological factors. Genetic predisposition—particularly the HLA DQB105:01 and DQB104 alleles—has been consistently associated with parasomnias. Additional contributing factors include insufficient or irregular sleep, psychosocial stress, anxiety, febrile illnesses, obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, excessive caffeine consumption, and the use of certain medications such as antihistamines, antidepressants, and stimulants [6, 11, 17, 28].

Beyond these established risk factors, an emerging body of evidence suggests that nutritional deficiencies may also play a role in the pathophysiology of sleep-related disorders, including STs. Zhu and Yao [33] proposed that vitamin D deficiency may disrupt melatonin synthesis and circadian rhythm regulation, thereby contributing to sleep fragmentation and parasomnias. Similarly, Al-Shawwa et al. [4] reported that children with lower serum vitamin D levels exhibited shorter total sleep duration and reduced sleep efficiency. Although vitamin D has garnered the most attention, other micronutrients—particularly vitamin B12, folate, and iron—have also been implicated in sleep–wake regulation.

Vitamin B12 and folate are essential cofactors for neurochemical pathways underlying circadian rhythm stability and sleep–wake transitions. Geng et al. [10] demonstrated that low levels of vitamin B12 and folate were associated with an increased risk of narcolepsy, potentially via impaired regulation of the sleep–wake cycle. Additional research suggests that vitamin B12 deficiency may disturb melatonin synthesis, thereby contributing to sleep onset difficulties and altered circadian timing [13]. Folate deficiency has similarly been linked to sleep disturbances, given its involvement in the synthesis of serotonin and melatonin—two neurotransmitters central to sleep maintenance [7].

Iron status has also emerged as a key nutritional determinant of sleep health. In a Brazilian cohort, infants who experienced iron-deficiency anemia within the first year of life were found to have a fourfold increased likelihood of shortened sleep duration compared with iron-sufficient peers, and each 1 g/dL increase in hemoglobin level corresponded to an additional 16 min of sleep [22]. Interventional data further support this association, with iron supplementation shown to improve nocturnal sleep duration and quality in iron-deficient children [18].

Given this background, the present retrospective cohort study aims to evaluate the associations between STs and nutritional parameters—including serum vitamin D, vitamin B12, folate, and iron indices—in children aged 24 to 60 months. A secondary aim is to determine whether deficiencies in these micronutrients influence clinical characteristics such as sleep duration, sleep quality, and sleep latency among children diagnosed with STs.

Methods

Participant selection

This naturalistic observational study with retrospective data extraction included children aged 24–60 months who presented to the Pediatric Outpatient Clinic of Kastamonu Training and Research Hospital between January 1, 2024 and April 1, 2025 with symptoms suggestive of ST and were referred to Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, where diagnosis and follow-up were initiated. Children with chronic endocrinological, cardiological, neurological, metabolic, genetic, or inflammatory disorders were excluded.

ST was diagnosed by a child and adolescent psychiatrist according to ICSD-3 criteria based on detailed caregiver interviews, characterization of nocturnal episodes, and review of home video recordings when available. Psychiatric comorbidities were assessed using developmentally informed clinical interviews within DC:0–5 and DSM-5 frameworks. Polysomnography was not routinely performed, consistent with standard clinical practice for typical parasomnia presentations; diagnoses therefore reflect clinical rather than electrophysiological confirmation.

The healthy control (HC) group comprised age-matched children evaluated in the same clinic during the same period for routine care and without developmental, neurological, psychiatric, or systemic medical disorders. Clinical, laboratory, and anthropometric data were extracted from electronic medical records. Standardized behavioral and sleep scales were administered to parents during routine clinical evaluations and subsequently extracted from electronic medical records for research purposes; for the HC group, only baseline assessments were available, whereas 3-month reassessments were documented for children with ST who returned for routine follow-up care.

Among 48 children with ST, complete 3-month laboratory and scale data were available for 25 (52.1%); missing follow-up resulted from non-attendance (n = 15), incomplete laboratory testing (n = 5), or incomplete scale administration (n = 3). Among children with complete longitudinal data, the mean interval between baseline and follow-up assessments was 90.6 days, with a median of 90 days and a range of 82–97 days. Follow-up assessments reflected usual clinical care rather than a research-driven protocol. In total, 48 children with ST and 48 HC met inclusion criteria. The study was approved by the Kastamonu University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2025-02; July 14, 2025) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki; informed consent was waived due to retrospective analysis of anonymized routine clinical data.

Demographic and clinical variables

Demographic data included age, sex, height, weight, and BMI. Parental variables comprised maternal and paternal age, educational level, and family structure. Medical history encompassed past and current medical and psychiatric conditions, psychiatric medication use, and family psychiatric or medical history.

For the ST group, additional sleep variables included average nightly sleep duration, sleep latency, bedtime resistance, frequency of nighttime awakenings, and clinician-determined psychiatric comorbidities (e.g., ADHD, speech sound disorder, tic disorder, separation anxiety disorder, developmental coordination disorder).

Biochemical measures

Laboratory parameters included serum vitamin D, vitamin B12, folate, hemoglobin, ferritin, and iron indices. Deficiency thresholds were defined as vitamin D < 20 ng/mL, vitamin B12 < 200 pg/mL, folate < 5 ng/mL, and anemia according to age- and sex-adjusted hemoglobin and iron reference ranges [14]. Ferritin < 12 ng/mL (≥ 12 months) was considered low, and ferritin values were interpreted only when CRP < 5 mg/L to minimize inflammatory confounding [14].

Children with documented deficiencies received guideline-based supplementation for three months: vitamin D (2000 IU/day), vitamin B12 (sublingual 1000 mcg/day), ferrous sulfate (4–6 mg/kg/day), or folic acid (5 mg/day) [14]. In the longitudinal subgroup (n = 25), vitamin D supplementation was provided to 20 children (80.0%), vitamin B12 supplementation to 17 (68.0%), folic acid to 5 (20.0%), and iron supplementation to 10 (40.0%); 17 children (68.0%) received two or more types simultaneously (see Supplementary Table S5). Participants lacking relevant baseline laboratory data were excluded from specific analyses.

Psychiatric and sleep assessments

Sleep and associated psychopathology were assessed using parent-rated Turkish versions of the Aberrant Behavior Checklist (ABC; [5, 26]), the Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC; [1, 9]), and the Preschool Anxiety Scale (PASC; [25, 27]). The Turkish version of the PASC was validated in a preschool sample and demonstrated a four-factor structure with high internal consistency. The Turkish form of the ABC was examined psychometrically by Sucuoğlu, supporting its reliability and validity for assessing maladaptive/problem behaviors in Turkish samples. The Turkish SDSC was validated as a parent-rated measure of pediatric sleep disturbances and includes T-score conversion for standardized interpretation. Higher scores on all three instruments indicate greater symptom severity; SDSC T-scores were used for standardized comparisons. Psychiatric diagnoses were established via clinician-administered interviews based on DC:0–5 and DSM-5 criteria.

Follow-up occurred within routine clinical pathways. Children receiving psychotropic or targeted sleep medications were excluded; those receiving nutritional supplementation alone constituted the longitudinal analytic subgroup. To preserve ecological validity, participants with variable supplementation adherence or incomplete biochemical normalization were retained. Baseline assessments were conducted for all participants, with repeat evaluations planned at approximately 3 months; complete follow-up laboratory and scale data were available for 25 children.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS (version 26.0) and R (version 4.6.0). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and median (interquartile range); categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection. Between-group comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square, Fisher’s exact, or simulated chi-square tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. Within-subject changes from baseline to follow-up were examined using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Effect sizes for continuous between-group comparisons were reported as rank-biserial correlations (r); within-subject changes as matched-pairs rank-biserial correlations; and categorical comparisons as Cramér’s V. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were calculated for rank-biserial effect estimates. To address multiple testing, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied as a sensitivity analysis across all comparisons. The principal outcomes were total anxiety symptoms (PASC total), total behavioral problems (ABC total), total sleep disturbance (SDSC total), and the main nutritional biomarkers (vitamin D, vitamin B12, folate, and ferritin); subscale-level analyses were considered exploratory. Additional sensitivity analyses used rank-transformed regression models to examine whether group differences in principal outcomes remained after adjustment for age, sex, and baseline psychiatric comorbidity. Within the longitudinal ST subgroup, Spearman correlations and adjusted linear regression models were used to examine whether biomarker changes—particularly vitamin B12 change—were associated with changes in total anxiety, behavioral dysregulation, and sleep disturbance, after controlling for baseline symptom severity, age, sex, and psychiatric comorbidity. Two-tailed p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant; FDR-adjusted p values were used to evaluate robustness.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

No statistically significant differences were found between the ST and HC groups with respect to sociodemographic features (see Table 1). The mean age of children with ST was 38.6 months (SD = 12.0), compared with 39.9 months (SD = 12.8) in the HC group (p = .609). Gender distribution was comparable (female: 50.0% vs. 58.3%; χ² = 0.671, p = .413). Similarly, no group differences were observed in family structure (χ² = 1.111, p = .759), maternal age (p = .944), paternal age (p = .125), or parental medical and psychiatric history (all p > .05). Supplementary analyses comparing children with and without 3-month follow-up demonstrated no significant differences in age, sex, family structure, parental health characteristics, or baseline sleep variables (Supplementary Table S1); however, follow-up completers showed a markedly higher prevalence of psychiatric diagnoses and lower baseline vitamin D and B12 levels, indicating that this subgroup represented a more clinically severe and nutritionally vulnerable subset rather than a cross-section of the full ST sample.

Sleep characteristics and psychiatric comorbidities

At the unadjusted level, children with ST had shorter nightly sleep duration than HC (9.17 ± 1.29 vs. 9.67 ± 1.04 h; p = .048, FDR p = .092, r = − .222, 95% CI [− 0.421, 0.001]). Bedtime resistance (47.9% vs. 27.1%; p = .035, FDR p = .069, Cramér’s V = 0.215) and nocturnal awakenings (39.6% vs. 16.7%; p = .013, FDR p = .028, Cramér’s V = 0.255) were also more frequent at the nominal level. However, sleep duration and bedtime resistance did not remain significant after FDR correction; nocturnal awakenings reached FDR significance. Sleep onset delay did not differ between groups (p = .331). Among children with ST, 58.3% met criteria for at least one psychiatric comorbidity. The most common diagnoses were separation anxiety disorder (43.8%), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (22.9%), and speech sound disorders (20.8%), followed by tic disorders (8.3%) and developmental coordination disorder (2.1%). Supplementary analyses indicated that children who completed follow-up had a substantially higher baseline prevalence of any psychiatric diagnosis (80.0% vs. 34.8%) and separation anxiety disorder (76.0% vs. 8.7%) compared with those lost to follow-up (Supplementary Table S1), confirming that the longitudinal subsample represented a more clinically severe subgroup rather than a random subset of the ST cohort.

Baseline psychopathology and behavioral profiles

Group differences in standardized symptom scales were robust and remained significant after FDR correction. Children with ST had substantially higher total PASC scores (r = .653, 95% CI [0.470, 0.807], FDR p < .001), ABC total scores (r = .695, 95% CI [0.516, 0.836], FDR p < .001), and SDSC total T-scores (r = .884, 95% CI [0.776, 0.962], FDR p < .001). Across all major domains, children with ST exhibited significantly elevated anxiety, behavioral dysregulation, and sleep-related difficulties compared with HC (see Table 2). The ST group had higher total anxiety scores (p < .001) and elevated subscale scores, including: general anxiety (p < .001), social anxiety (p < .001), fear of physical injury (p < .001), separation anxiety (p < .001), obsessive–compulsive symptoms (p < .001). Children with ST showed significantly higher scores for total behavior problems (p < .001), irritability (p < .001), lethargy (p < .001), stereotyped behaviors (p = .001), and hyperactivity (p < .001). No significant difference was observed for speech-related behaviors (p = .200). Marked group differences were found across most SDSC domains. Children with ST had higher total SDSC scores (p < .001) and greater impairments in: sleep initiation/maintenance (p < .001), sleep breathing disorders (p < .001), sleep–wake transition disorders (p < .001), excessive somnolence (p < .001), sleep hyperhidrosis (p < .001). Arousal disorder scores did not differ significantly (p = .223).

Supplementary Table S2 showed that follow-up completers had higher baseline PASC total scores and elevated levels across all anxiety subscales (general, social, physical injury fears, separation anxiety, and obsessive–compulsive symptoms). They also had higher ABC total scores, with notable increases in lethargy, stereotyped behavior, and hyperactivity. Likewise, SDSC total scores and several sleep domains—including sleep initiation/maintenance, breathing-related problems, sleep–wake transitions, and excessive somnolence—were higher among children who completed follow-up. In rank-transformed sensitivity models adjusted for age, sex, and baseline psychiatric comorbidity, group differences in total PASC (FDR p < .001), ABC total (FDR p < .001), and SDSC total T-scores (FDR p < .001) remained significant, indicating that the elevated symptom burden in the ST group was not fully explained by baseline psychiatric comorbidity (see Supplementary Table S6).

Baseline nutritional and laboratory parameters

Children with ST showed lower serum vitamin D (20.43 ± 13.42 vs. 25.77 ± 10.53 ng/mL; p = .015, FDR p = .026, r = − .291, 95% CI [− 0.511, − 0.059]), vitamin B12 (231.67 ± 107.70 vs. 309.35 ± 139.24 pg/mL; p = .002, FDR p = .003, r = − .375, 95% CI [− 0.585, − 0.164]), and folate levels (11.25 ± 6.61 vs. 13.14 ± 5.38 ng/mL; p = .016, FDR p = .028, r = − .284, 95% CI [− 0.511, − 0.055]) compared with HC, with all differences surviving FDR correction (see Table 3). No significant between-group differences were found for ferritin, hemoglobin, serum iron, TSH, free T4, height, weight, BMI percentile, or BMI z-score (all p > .05). Supplementary Table S3 showed that children in the follow-up-available group (n = 25) had significantly lower baseline 25(OH)D (p = .002, FDR p = .008, r = − .536) and vitamin B12 (p = .012, FDR p = .030, r = − .421) compared with non-completers, whereas other laboratory markers did not differ. However, in rank-transformed sensitivity models adjusted for age, sex, and psychiatric comorbidity, group differences in vitamin D, vitamin B12, folate, and ferritin were attenuated and no longer statistically significant after FDR correction (all adjusted FDR p > .05; see Supplementary Table S6). Baseline nutritional differences should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as they may partly reflect the broader clinical profile and healthcare-related selection factors of the ST group.

Changes in nutritional and anthropometric measures over 3 months

Follow-up analyses were restricted to the 25 children with complete longitudinal laboratory and scale data. The mean interval between baseline and follow-up assessments was 90.6 days, with a median of 90 days and a range of 82–97 days. Serum vitamin B12 increased significantly from baseline to follow-up (201.76 ± 101.54 to 281.08 ± 80.35 pg/mL; Wilcoxon p = .006, FDR p = .009; matched-pairs r = .631, 95% CI [0.237, 0.926]). In contrast, vitamin D, folate, ferritin, hemoglobin, and serum iron did not show significant changes after FDR correction (all FDR p > .05). Height (p < .001, FDR p = .003, matched-pairs r = .831) and weight (p < .001, FDR p < .001, matched-pairs r = 1.000) increased significantly, consistent with expected developmental growth. BMI percentile (FDR p = .065) and BMI z-score (FDR p = .054) showed nominal increases that did not survive FDR correction. Associations between vitamin B12 change and symptom change

Within-subject change-score analyses were conducted to examine whether biomarker changes were associated with symptom changes (see Supplementary Table S7). Although vitamin B12 increased over follow-up and total symptom scores decreased in parallel, vitamin B12 change was not significantly associated with change in total PASC score (Spearman ρ = −0.354, p = .083, FDR p = .546), ABC total score (ρ = −0.025, p = .907, FDR p = .952), or SDSC total T-score (ρ = −0.339, p = .097, FDR p = .546). In adjusted regression models controlling for baseline symptom severity, age, sex, and psychiatric comorbidity, vitamin B12 change did not significantly predict changes in total PASC, ABC, or SDSC scores (all FDR p > .05). These findings indicate that the parallel increase in vitamin B12 and reduction in symptoms should be interpreted as temporal co-occurrence rather than evidence of a direct biomarker–symptom association.

Discussion

In this naturalistic observational study with retrospective data extraction, early childhood ST was associated with a robust multidimensional symptom profile characterized by elevated anxiety, behavioral dysregulation, and parent-rated sleep disturbance, all of which remained significant after FDR correction. Baseline vitamin D, vitamin B12, and folate levels were lower in children with ST in both unadjusted and FDR-adjusted comparisons, although these biomarker differences were attenuated after adjustment for age, sex, and psychiatric comorbidity. Longitudinally, vitamin B12 increased and symptoms decreased in a selected follow-up subgroup; however, change-score and adjusted regression analyses did not support a direct association between vitamin B12 change and symptom change.

Consistent with prior literature, ST was not associated with basic sociodemographic markers, including age, sex, family structure, parental age, education, or parental medical/psychiatric history [16, 17], suggesting that although socioeconomic factors may influence pediatric sleep outcomes [29], they do not appear to account for ST occurrence in our cohort. Basic sleep-history variables, including sleep duration and bedtime resistance, differed only at the nominal level and did not remain significant after FDR correction; nocturnal awakenings did survive FDR correction. By contrast, standardized SDSC scores showed robust group differences that were highly significant after FDR correction. Thus, the strongest sleep-related signal in this study involves multidimensional parent-rated sleep disturbance rather than isolated sleep-history indicators, aligning with evidence implicating NREM stage 3–4 instability and incomplete arousal as core mechanisms [2, 16, 20].

Cross-sectionally, children with ST exhibited lower vitamin D, vitamin B12, and folate levels despite comparable hemoglobin and iron parameters. The association between vitamin D and sleep disturbance has been reported across age groups, including improved sleep complaints following supplementation [24], links between low vitamin D and pediatric sleep disorders [21], and shorter sleep duration among vitamin D–deficient preschoolers [32], with proposed mechanisms involving melatonin synthesis, circadian regulation, and inflammatory pathways [33]. Our findings extend these observations to clinically diagnosed ST in early childhood, supporting vitamin D assessment as a reasonable—though not causal—component of evaluation. Importantly, after adjustment for age, sex, and psychiatric comorbidity, the baseline differences in vitamin D, vitamin B12, and folate were attenuated and no longer statistically significant, suggesting that micronutrient differences may be partly linked to the broader clinical profile of the ST group, including psychiatric comorbidity and healthcare-related selection factors, rather than representing independent biomarkers of ST.

Evidence regarding vitamin B12 is mixed: lower B12 has been associated with shorter sleep duration and initiation difficulties [15, 30], whereas randomized and population-based analyses have not consistently demonstrated robust associations or universal supplementation benefits [8, 12], and most prior work has not focused on parasomnias or early childhood. In this context, the combination of lower baseline B12, subsequent B12 increase, and concurrent symptom reduction in our cohort is clinically notable yet remains hypothesis-generating, as spontaneous remission, developmental maturation, regression to the mean, improved sleep routines, and nonspecific follow-up effects cannot be excluded.

Similarly, lower folate levels in the ST group align with evidence linking folate to melatonin synthesis and circadian stability, and to sleep onset latency and duration in adult and pediatric samples [7, 34], although we observed no significant longitudinal folate change, likely reflecting supplementation patterns. In contrast, iron indices did not differ, despite prior data associating early iron deficiency anemia with altered REM architecture and persistent sleep disturbance [19] and with poorer sleep quality in children [3], suggesting that in this cohort any iron-related contribution was less pronounced than that observed for vitamin D, B12, or folate, possibly due to limited power or low anemia prevalence.

Over three months, vitamin B12 increased significantly in the ST subgroup with complete follow-up, whereas vitamin D, folate, ferritin, and other laboratory parameters remained stable; concurrently, anxiety, behavioral dysregulation, and sleep disturbances improved markedly. The temporal co-occurrence of increased vitamin B12 and reduced symptom scores is clinically noteworthy, but change-score analyses did not demonstrate a statistically robust association between vitamin B12 change and symptom change after FDR correction. Therefore, these longitudinal findings should not be interpreted as evidence that vitamin B12 correction directly produced symptom improvement; spontaneous remission, developmental maturation, regression to the mean, improved sleep routines, and nonspecific follow-up effects remain plausible explanations. Among biomarkers examined, only B12 changed meaningfully, making it the most prominent signal observed and a candidate for future controlled investigation. Small increases in anthropometric indices are best understood as expected developmental growth rather than treatment effects.

At baseline, children with ST displayed substantially elevated anxiety (except PTSD), behavioral dysregulation (except speech-related behaviors), and sleep disturbance across most SDSC domains, underscoring that ST rarely occurs in isolation but rather within a broader internalizing and sleep dysregulation profile. Bidirectional interactions are likely: anxiety and behavioral dysregulation may exacerbate sleep fragmentation and arousal instability, while recurrent parasomnias may amplify emotional reactivity. High rates of psychiatric comorbidity—particularly separation anxiety disorder, ADHD, and developmental conditions—further complicate causal attribution. The concurrent presence of lower vitamin D, B12, and folate raises the possibility that micronutrient deficiencies modulate sleep–emotion interactions via effects on neurotransmitter synthesis, melatonin regulation, and neuroinflammatory pathways [3, 7, 8, 12, 15, 19, 23, 24, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34], supporting a multifactorial model in which psychological, neurobiological, and nutritional influences interact.

Children with available follow-up exhibited greater baseline symptom severity and lower baseline 25(OH)D and B12, indicating that longitudinal improvements reflect change within a more clinically impaired and nutritionally vulnerable subgroup rather than the full ST sample; this pattern clarifies the interpretive scope and highlights real-world engagement dynamics in pediatric sleep medicine.

Strengths include a clinically well-characterized preschool sample, exclusion of psychotropic confounding, and multi-instrument assessment of anxiety, behavior, and sleep, alongside novel documentation of a B12–ST association and parallel symptom change. Limitations include single-center design, modest sample size, and the large number of exploratory comparisons that require cautious interpretation despite FDR correction. Additional limitations are substantial attrition, retrospective and naturalistic follow-up, high psychiatric comorbidity, absence of systematic dietary and sleep-hygiene assessment, lack of polysomnographic confirmation, and the inability to infer causality. The adjusted analyses were necessarily parsimonious given the modest sample size, and residual confounding by psychiatric comorbidity, diet, sunlight exposure, seasonal variation, sleep hygiene, and family-level behavioral changes remains possible. Furthermore, biomarker change was not statistically associated with symptom change after FDR correction, limiting causal interpretation of the longitudinal vitamin B12 finding. Non-significant findings for ferritin and other iron-related indices should not be interpreted as evidence of absence, given the limited statistical power of this study.

These findings support further investigation of nutritional status in children with ST, but they do not justify treatment recommendations based on the present observational data. The temporal co-occurrence of vitamin B12 increase and symptom reduction is hypothesis-generating; establishing any therapeutic relevance requires larger, prospective, controlled, and ideally randomized studies incorporating polysomnography, circadian measures, and detailed nutritional profiling.

Conclusion

Early childhood ST was associated with a multidimensional profile of anxiety, behavioral dysregulation, parent-rated sleep disturbance, and lower vitamin D, vitamin B12, and folate levels. Clinical symptom differences remained robust after FDR correction and after adjustment for psychiatric comorbidity, whereas nutritional biomarker differences were attenuated in adjusted analyses. During naturalistic follow-up, vitamin B12 increased and symptom scores decreased, but change-score analyses did not support a direct biomarker–symptom association. These findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and non-causal. Larger prospective, controlled studies are needed to clarify whether micronutrient abnormalities contribute independently to the clinical profile of early childhood ST.
