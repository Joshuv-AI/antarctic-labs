First variation of the fractional k k -dimensional measure: extending the concept of nonlocal curvature to submanifolds
Cornelia Mihaila, Brian Seguin

Abstract
The fractional k k -dimensional measure of a submanifold of ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} is a generalization of the fractional perimeter and fractional length appearing in the literature and depends on a parameter σ \sigma between 0 0 and 1 1 . Here its first variation is computed. The resulting formula is used to define a nonlocal version of the mean-curvature vector for embedded submanifolds. It is shown that in the case where k = n − 1 k=n-1 , this agrees with the nonlocal mean-curvature that has been widely studied.



1
Introduction

The nonlocal or fractional perimeter of a bounded set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
\sigma\in(0,1)
was introduced by Caffarelli, Roquejoffre, and Savin in
[
CRS10
]
, and is defined by

\text{Per}_{\sigma}(E)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\alpha_{n-1}}\int_{E}\int_{E^{c}}|x-y|^{-n-\sigma}dxdy,

(1.1)

where
\alpha_{n-1}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. When
E
is unbounded, the above quantity is generally infinity. Thus, in the unbounded case one needs to consider the fractional perimeter of
E
relative to a bounded, open set
\Omega
:

\displaystyle\text{Per}_{\sigma}(E,\Omega)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{n-1}}\Big(\int_{E\cap\Omega}\int_{E^{c}}+\int_{E\cap\Omega^{c}}\int_{E^{c}\cap\Omega}\Big)|x-y|^{-n-\sigma}dxdy

(1.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{n-1}}\int_{E}\int_{E^{c}}\frac{\max\{\chi_{\Omega}(x),\chi_{\Omega}(y)\}}{|x-y|^{n+\sigma}}dxdy.

(1.3)

Caffarelli and Valdinoci
[
CV11
]
proved that when
E
has smooth boundary, for almost every
r>0
,

\lim_{\sigma\uparrow 1}(1-\sigma)\text{Per}_{\sigma}(E,B_{r})={\cal H}^{n-1}(\partial E\cap B_{r})

(1.4)

which motivates calling it a perimeter function. With this perimeter function, mathematicians have been interested in many of the classical questions from minimal surface theory, including characterizations of minimizers in Plateau’s problem or under fixed volume constraints. For instance, Dipierro, Savin, and Valdinoci
[
DSV17
,
DSV20
]
considered a nonlocal Plateau-type problem and considered the irregularities induced by the nonlocal setting, and Cabré, Fall, and Weth
[
CFW16
]
and Ciraolo, Figalli, Maggi, Novaga
[
CFMN16
]
considered properties of critical numbers for constant mean curvature sets with fixed mass. See also the work of Dipierro and Valdinoci
[
DV18
]
and the references therein.

By taking the first variation of the nonlocal perimeter functional, Abatangelo and Valdinoci
[
AV14
]
derived the nonlocal mean-curvature equation for
E
which has a
C^{1,\alpha}
boundary with
\alpha>\sigma
:

H_{\sigma}(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\omega_{n-2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)\,dx}{|z-y|^{n+\sigma}},\qquad z\in\partial E,

(1.5)

where
\omega_{n-2}
is the surface area of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
\tilde{\chi}_{E}=\chi_{E}-\chi_{E^{c}}
with
\chi_{S}
denoting the characteristic function of a set
S
, and the integral is computed as a principle value. The nonlocal mean-curvature converges to the classical notion in the sense that

\lim\limits_{\sigma\uparrow 1}(1-\sigma)H_{\sigma}(z)=H(z).

Moreover, in the case of a point
z\in\partial E
where most of the nearby points are in
E^{c}
, like on the sphere, the nonlocal mean curvature is negative. Similarly the nonlocal mean curvature is positive if most of the points near
z
are in
E
. This is consistent with the sign of the classical mean-curvature when an exterior unit-normal is used.

A limitation of the nonlocal perimeter is that it only describes the perimeter of
(n-1)
-dimensional sets that are the boundaries of
n
-dimensional sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Paroni, Podio-Guidugli, and Seguin extended this idea to
(n-1)
-dimensional hypersurfaces in
[
PPGS18
]
. By defining
{\cal X}(\partial E)
to be the set of pairs
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the line segment connecting
x
and
y
intersects
\partial E
an odd number of times, they noticed that the definition from Caffarelli, Roquejoffre, and Savin is equivalent to

\text{Per}_{\sigma}(E,\Omega)=\frac{1}{2\alpha_{n-1}}\int_{{\cal X}(\partial E)}\frac{\max\{\chi_{\Omega}(x),\chi_{\Omega}(y)\}}{|x-y|^{n+\sigma}}dxdy.

(1.6)

Indeed, almost every pair of points on opposite sides of the boundary
\partial E
will cross it an odd number of times. With this perspective they defined

\text{Area}_{\sigma}({\cal S},\Omega)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\alpha_{n-1}}\int_{{\cal X}({\cal S})}\frac{\max\{\chi_{\Omega}(x),\chi_{\Omega}(y)\}}{|x-y|^{n+\sigma}}dxdy

(1.7)

to be the fractional area for a hypersurface
{\cal S}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Under the assumption that
{\cal S}\subseteq\Omega
, they showed that

\lim_{\sigma\uparrow 1}(1-\sigma)\text{Area}_{\sigma}({\cal S},\Omega)={\cal H}^{n-1}({\cal S}).

(1.8)

Moreover, they computed its first variation and showed that the first variation is zero if and only if

\lim_{\varepsilon\uparrow 0}\left(\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm ext}(z,\varepsilon)}-\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm int}(z,\varepsilon)}\right)\frac{1}{|z-y|^{n+\sigma}}\,dy=0,\qquad z\in{\cal S},

(1.9)

where

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{\rm ext}(z,\varepsilon)\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:

\displaystyle\big((y,z)\in{\cal X}_{\varepsilon}({\cal S})\text{ and }(z-y)\cdot{\bf n}(z)>0\big)

\displaystyle\text{or }\big((y,z)\in{\cal X}_{\varepsilon}({\cal S})^{c}\text{ and }(z-y)\cdot{\bf n}(z)<0\big)\},

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{\rm int}(z,\varepsilon)\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:

\displaystyle\big((y,z)\in{\cal X}_{\varepsilon}({\cal S})^{c}\text{ and }(z-y)\cdot{\bf n}(z)>0\big)

\displaystyle\text{or }\big((y,z)\in{\cal X}_{\varepsilon}({\cal S})\text{ and }(z-y)\cdot{\bf n}(z)<0\big)\},

with
{\cal X}_{\varepsilon}({\cal S})
being the set of all pairs of points
(y,z)
such that the segment between
y
and
z
crosses
{\cal S}
an odd number of times and
|z-y|>\varepsilon
and
{\bf n}
an orientation for
{\cal S}
. This led them to define the nonlocal mean-curvature of
{\cal S}
at
z
by

H_{\sigma}(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\omega_{n-2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\hat{\chi}_{\cal S}(y)}{|z-y|^{n+\sigma}}\,dy,\qquad\text{ for }z\in{\cal S}

(1.10)

where

\hat{\chi}_{\cal S}(z,y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&y\in{\cal A}_{i}(z),\\
0&y\notin{\cal A}_{i}(z)\cup{\cal A}_{e}(z)\\
-1&y\in{\cal A}_{e}(z),\\
\end{cases}

and the integral is computed as a principle value. This agrees with (
1.5
) in the case where
{\cal S}
is the boundary of a set
E
.

In
[
Seg20
]
, Seguin introduced a notion of fractional length. (See Seguin
[
Seg
]
for an updated, corrected version of this work.) His motivation came from considering the
n=2
case, where a curve is a hypersurface. In this setting, a line segment can be viewed as a one-dimensional disk. Thus, rather than considering a pairs of points
(x,y)
that describe line segments, he used disks described by a center, a unit vector determining the orientation, and a radius. This led to the following definition of fractional length:

\text{Len}_{\sigma}({\cal C},\Omega)\coloneqq\int_{{\cal D}({\cal C})}\frac{\sup\{\chi_{\Omega}(p+r{\bf v}):{\bf v}\in{\cal U}(\{{\bf u}\}^{\perp})\}}{r^{1+\sigma}}d{\cal H}^{2n}(p,{\bf u},r),

(1.11)

where
{\cal D}({\cal C})
consists of all disks, described by a center
p
, unit normal vector
{\bf u}
, and radius
r
, that intersect the curve
{\cal C}
an odd number of times. Analogous to (
1.8
), it was shown that

\lim_{\sigma\uparrow 1}(1-\sigma)\text{Len}_{\sigma}({\cal C},\Omega)=\frac{4\pi^{n-1}}{\Gamma(\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{n+1}{2}$}})\Gamma(\text{\scalebox{.8}{$\frac{n-1}{2}$}})(n-1)}{\cal H}^{1}({\cal C}),

(1.12)

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function. The presence of the factor on the right-hand side is due to the fact that the definition of the fractional length does not involve a normalization factor. Assuming that
{\cal C}
has
C^{1,\alpha}
-regularity with
\alpha>\sigma
, the first variation of
\text{Len}_{\sigma}({\cal C},\Omega)
vanishing is equivalent to

\lim_{\varepsilon\uparrow 0}\Big(\int_{{\cal A}_{\rm o}^{+}(z,\varepsilon)}-\int_{{\cal A}_{\rm e}^{+}(z,\varepsilon)}\Big)\frac{({\bf b}\wedge{\bf a}){\bf t}(z)}{r^{1+\sigma}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2n-2}({\bf a},{\bf b},r)=\textbf{0},\qquad z\in{\cal C},

where
{\bf t}(z)
is a unit tangent to
{\cal C}
at
z
and

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{+}_{\rm o}(z,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(\mathbf{a},{\bf b},r\}\in{\cal U}^{2}_{\perp}\times(\varepsilon,\infty):\mathcal{H}^{0}\big(D(z+r{\bf a},{\bf b},r)\cap{\cal C})\text{ is odd},{\bf b}\cdot{\bf t}(z)>0\},

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{+}_{\rm e}(z,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(\mathbf{a},{\bf b},r\}\in{\cal U}^{2}_{\perp}\times(\varepsilon,\infty):\mathcal{H}^{0}\big(D(z+r{\bf a},{\bf b},r)\cap{\cal C})\text{ is even},{\bf b}\cdot{\bf t}(z)>0\}.

Using this, Seguin defined the nonlocal curvature-vector
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\sigma}
at
z\in{\cal C}
by

\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\sigma}(z)\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\Big(\int_{{\cal A}_{\rm e}^{+}(z,\varepsilon)}-\int_{{\cal A}_{\rm o}^{+}(z,\varepsilon)}\Big)\frac{({\bf b}\wedge{\bf a}){\bf t}(z)}{r^{1+\sigma}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2n-2}({\bf a},{\bf b},r).

(1.13)

The interest in understanding fractional measure or mass in higher codimensions has been demonstrated in recent works. Employing the Fourier transform of a measure, Cicalese, Heilmann, Kubin, Onoue, and Ponsiglione
[
CHK+25
]
introduced the notion of
s
-fractional mass for one-currents in higher codimension. Caselli, Freguglia, and Picenni
[
CFP24
]
introduced a notion of fractional
s
-mass on (
n-2
)-dimensional closed orientable surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
using the concepts of linking number and fractional Sobolev spaces. In Mihaila and Seguin
[
MS25
]
, the authors extended the notions of fractional length and area for curves and hypersurfaces described above so as to work for any
k
-dimensional manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. They defined the
k
-dimensional
\sigma
-measure of a
k
-dimensional manifold
{\cal M}
relative to a bounded, open set
\Omega
by

\text{Meas}_{\sigma}^{k}(\mathcal{M},\Omega)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{M})}r^{k-n-\sigma}\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in{\cal U}([\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp})}\chi_{\Omega}(p+r\mathbf{u})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n+k(n-k)+1}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r),

(1.14)

where
{\cal D}({\cal M})
is the collection of
(n-k)
-dimensional disks that intersect
{\cal M}
an odd number of times. In this setting a disk is described by a center point
p
, orientation
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, which is a simple
k
-vector of unit length describing the space orthogonal to the disk, and radius
r
. They proved that if
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Omega
, then

\lim_{\sigma\uparrow 1}(1-\sigma)\text{\rm Meas}_{\sigma}^{k}(\mathcal{M},\Omega)=\frac{4\pi^{\frac{(n+2)(k+1)}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{n-k+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})(n-k)}\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{i}{2})}{\pi^{i}\Gamma(\frac{n-i+1}{2})}\mathcal{H}^{k}(\mathcal{M}).

(1.15)

This constant is consistent with the constants in the
k=1
and
k=n-1
cases. See (
1.12
) and (
1.8
), respectively. The result holds for all integers
0\leq k\leq n-1
.

We use the term nonlocal or fractional measure for
\text{Meas}_{\sigma}^{k}
since it generalizes the concepts of fractional perimeter, fractional area, and fractional length. However, the
\sigma
-measure relative to a bounded set
\Omega
is not a measure because it is not additive. This was shown explicitly in
[
MS25
]
. In this work, we complete the natural next step of finding necessary conditions for the first variation to be zero and use this to define a nonlocal mean curvature-vector. In particular, we show that a necessary and sufficient condition for the vanishing of the first variation of
\text{\rm Meas}^{k}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{M},\Omega)
with respect to compact,
k
-dimensional manifolds with the same boundary as
\mathcal{M}
is that for all
z\in\mathcal{M}
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\left(\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm e}^{+}(z,\varepsilon)}-\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\rm o}^{+}(z,\varepsilon)}\right)\frac{(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\llcorner\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)}{r^{1+\sigma}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{w+1}(\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)={\bf 0},

(1.16)

where
w=n-1+k(n-1-k)
,
vol
is a volume form for
{\cal M}
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rm o}^{+}(z,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{({\bf a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{W}\times(\varepsilon,\infty):\mathcal{H}^{0}(D(z+r\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\mathcal{M})\mbox{ is odd},\ \boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)>0\},

(1.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rm e}^{+}(z,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{({\bf a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{W}\times(\varepsilon,\infty):\mathcal{H}^{0}(D(z+r\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\mathcal{M})\mbox{ is even},\ \boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)>0\}.

(1.18)

Notice that unlike the nonlocal mean-curvature for hypersurfaces, but similar to the nonlocal curvature for a curve, this quantity is not normalized. Now that the first variation is understood, the next step would be to consider properties of surfaces where the first variation is equal to zero. This work also opens up the discussion of nonlocal mean curvature flow, as in the work of Chambolle, Marini, and Ponsiglione
[
CMP15
]
, to a wider variety of surfaces.

-vectors. For a detailed introduction to

2
Notation and an introduction to multivectors

Let
\mathbb{R}^{+}\coloneqq(0,\infty)
denote the set of positive numbers not including zero and
\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\coloneqq[0,\infty)
the set of positive numbers including zero. Given an inner-product space
{\cal V}
, let
{\cal U}({\cal V})
denote the set of all unit vectors in
{\cal V}
. If
{\cal Z}
is a subset of
{\cal V}
, let
{\cal Z}^{\perp}
denote those vectors in
{\cal V}
that are orthogonal to every vector in
{\cal Z}
.

There are different ways to define multivectors. Here, we take the approach of viewing them as skew multilinear mappings. While this is not the definition used in the book by Rosén
[
Ros19
]
, it is equivalent, and we recommend Rosén’s book for an introduction to multivectors. The definition presented here can be found in Federer’s book
[
Fed69
]
. The overview of multivectors presented here is mostly taken from our work
[
MS25
]
, with some alterations and additions.

Fix a natural number
k
such that
1\leq k\leq n
. Given
k
vectors
{\bf w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, define the
k
-vector
{\bf w}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf w}_{k}
to be the multilinear map from the
k
-fold product
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}
to
\mathbb{R}
by

({\bf w}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf w}_{k})({\bf a}_{1},\dots,{\bf a}_{k})\coloneqq\text{det}\ \begin{array}[]{cccc}{\bf w}_{1}\cdot{\bf a}_{1}&{\bf w}_{1}\cdot{\bf a}_{2}&...&{\bf w}_{1}\cdot{\bf a}_{k}\\
{\bf w}_{2}\cdot{\bf a}_{1}&{\bf w}_{2}\cdot{\bf a}_{2}&...&{\bf w}_{2}\cdot{\bf a}_{k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\bf w}_{k}\cdot{\bf a}_{1}&{\bf w}_{k}\cdot{\bf a}_{2}&...&{\bf w}_{k}\cdot{\bf a}_{k}\\
\end{array}\,,\qquad({\bf a}_{1},\dots,{\bf a}_{k})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}.

(2.1)

Notice that this mapping is skew in the sense that switching any pair of
{\bf a}_{i}
results in changing the sign of the output. The set of all
k
-vectors, denoted by
\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, is the linear span of all linear mappings of the form
{\bf w}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf w}_{k}
. It is true that
\text{dim}\,\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})={n\choose k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
.

The collection of
k
-vectors of the form (
2.1
) are called simple
k
-vectors, and are denoted by
\hat{\Lambda}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. While
\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a vector space,
\hat{\Lambda}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is not. However, it is a manifold. Moreover, simple
k
-vectors span the set of all
k
-vectors. For a simple
k
-vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf w}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf w}_{k}
, we denote by
[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
spanned by the
{\bf w}_{i}
. Let
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
and
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}
be the orthogonal projections of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into
[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
and
[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}
, respectively, so that
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}+{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\textbf{1}_{n}
, where
\textbf{1}_{n}
is the identity map on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We define several linear mappings on
k
-vectors and pairs of
k
-vectors. To do so, we first define them on simple
k
-vectors and then extend them to all
k
-vectors by linearity. The reason that this process works is because
k
-vectors satisfy a universality property. See Rosén
[
Ros19
]
for the details. As a first example of this, consider a linear mapping
{\bf L}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This induces a linear mapping from
\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to itself that we will also denote by
{\bf L}
such that it acts on a simple
k
-vector
{\bf u}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf u}_{k}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by

{\bf L}({\bf u}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf u}_{k})\coloneqq{\bf L}{\bf u}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf L}{\bf u}_{k}.

(2.2)

Given two simple
k
-vectors
{\bf u}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf u}_{k},{\bf v}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf v}_{k}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, their inner-product is defined by

({\bf u}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf u}_{k})\cdot({\bf v}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf v}_{k})\coloneqq\text{det}\ \begin{array}[]{cccc}{\bf u}_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{1}&{\bf u}_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{2}&...&{\bf u}_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{k}\\
{\bf u}_{2}\cdot{\bf v}_{1}&{\bf u}_{2}\cdot{\bf v}_{2}&...&{\bf u}_{2}\cdot{\bf v}_{k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\bf u}_{k}\cdot{\bf v}_{1}&{\bf u}_{k}\cdot{\bf v}_{2}&...&{\bf u}_{k}\cdot{\bf v}_{k}\\
\end{array}\,.

(2.3)

Orthogonal, simple
k
-vectors have the following geometric interpretation: given
\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}=0
, there is an
{\bf a}\in[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
such that
{\bf a}\in[\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{\perp}
.

We will denote the set of simple
k
-vectors of unit length by
\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Elements of
\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be viewed as oriented
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus,
\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be used as a double cover for the Grassmannian
\textbf{Gr}(k,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. It follows that
\text{dim}\,\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=k(n-k)
. Given a subspace
{\cal V}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we will use the notation
\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{k}({\cal V})
for those unit simple
k
-vectors
\boldsymbol{\omega}
such that
[\boldsymbol{\omega}]\subseteq{\cal V}
.

The exterior product
\wedge:\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\Lambda^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\Lambda^{k+\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the bilinear map such that given simple multivectors
{\bf u}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf u}_{k}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
{\bf v}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf v}_{\ell}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

({\bf u}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf u}_{k})\wedge({\bf v}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf v}_{\ell})\coloneqq{\bf u}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf u}_{k}\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf v}_{\ell}.

(2.4)

We will also make use of the interior product of multivectors. For
k\geq\ell
, the left interior-product
\lrcorner:\Lambda^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\Lambda^{k-\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the bilinear map such that for
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\Lambda^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is the unique
(k-\ell)
-vector satisfying

\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\nu}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\lambda})=(\boldsymbol{\nu}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda},\qquad\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Lambda^{k-\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(2.5)

and the right interior-product
\llcorner:\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\Lambda^{\ell}\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\Lambda^{k-\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the bilinear map such that for
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\Lambda^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\llcorner\boldsymbol{\nu}
is the unique
(k-\ell)
-vector satisfying

\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\llcorner\boldsymbol{\nu})=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\wedge\boldsymbol{\nu})\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda},\qquad\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Lambda^{k-\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(2.6)

These two interior products differ at most by a sign in that

\boldsymbol{\nu}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(-1)^{\ell(k-\ell)}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\llcorner\boldsymbol{\nu},\quad\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\Lambda^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(2.7)

It transpires that if
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then

{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\bf u}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner{\bf u})\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega},\qquad{\bf u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.8)

Given an orientation for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the form of a top dimensional multivector
\boldsymbol{\iota}\in\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, one can then define the (left) Hodge star map
*:\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\Lambda^{n-k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by

*\boldsymbol{\omega}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\iota}\llcorner\boldsymbol{\omega},\qquad\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(2.9)

The Hodge star is an isometry on multvectors and satisfies the identity

*(\boldsymbol{\nu}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\lambda})=(*\boldsymbol{\lambda})\wedge\boldsymbol{\nu},\quad\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\Lambda^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(2.10)

A useful relation involving the left interior-product is

(\boldsymbol{\omega}\wedge\boldsymbol{\lambda})\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\lrcorner(\boldsymbol{\omega}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu})

(2.11)

for all
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\Lambda^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
where
k+\ell\leq m
. There is also a varient on Lagrange’s identity: if
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then

|{\bf a}|^{2}|\boldsymbol{\omega}|^{2}=|{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}|^{2}+|{\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega}|^{2}.

(2.12)

There is also the anticommutation relation:

{\bf a}\lrcorner({\bf b}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})+{\bf b}\wedge({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega})=({\bf a}\cdot{\bf b})\boldsymbol{\omega},

(2.13)

which holds for all
{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. It can be shown that for a simple
k
-vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
,

[\boldsymbol{\omega}]=\{{\bf a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ :\ {\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega}=\textbf{0}\}\quad\text{and}\quad[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}=\{{\bf a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ :\ {\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}=\textbf{0}\}.

(2.14)

Analogous facts are true involving the right interior-product.

We will illustrate the left interior-product by mentioning a fact that will be of use later. Let
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. It follows that there are orthonormal vectors
{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2},{\bf w}_{3}
, and
{\bf w}_{4}
such that
\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf w}_{1}\wedge{\bf w}_{2}\wedge{\bf w}_{3}\wedge{\bf w}_{4}
. From the definitions above, for
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}=({\bf a}\cdot{\bf w}_{1}){\bf u}_{2}\wedge{\bf w}_{3}\wedge{\bf w}_{4}-({\bf a}\cdot{\bf w}_{2}){\bf w}_{1}\wedge{\bf u}_{3}\wedge{\bf w}_{4}+({\bf a}\cdot{\bf w}_{3}){\bf w}_{1}\wedge{\bf w}_{2}\wedge{\bf w}_{4}-({\bf a}\cdot{\bf w}_{4}){\bf w}_{1}\wedge{\bf w}_{2}\wedge{\bf w}_{3}.

(2.15)

Thus,

{\bf w}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}=-{\bf w}_{1}\wedge{\bf w}_{3}\wedge{\bf w}_{4},

(2.16)

and for
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

{\bf b}\wedge({\bf w}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega})={\bf w}_{1}\wedge{\bf b}\wedge{\bf w}_{3}\wedge{\bf w}_{4}.

(2.17)

This kind of identity generalizes. Namely, if
\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf w}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf w}_{k}\in\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
{\bf b}\wedge({\bf w}_{i}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega})
is the
k
-vector obtained by replacing
{\bf w}_{i}
with
{\bf b}
in
\boldsymbol{\omega}
—that is,

{\bf b}\wedge({\bf w}_{i}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega})={\bf w}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf w}_{i-1}\wedge{\bf b}\wedge{\bf w}_{i+1}\wedge\dots{\bf w}_{k}.

(2.18)

We now establish several facts involving multivectors that will be of use in this work that are not present in Rosén
[
Ros19
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in{\cal U}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. If
\mathbf{a}\in[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}
, then

(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{\omega}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mu}.

Proof.

As
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are simple
k
-vectors, they can be written as
\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf w}_{1}\wedge...\wedge{\bf w}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}={\bf m}_{1}\wedge...\wedge{\bf m}_{k}
, where
\{{\bf w}_{1},\dots,{\bf w}_{k}\}
and
\{{\bf m}_{1},\dots{\bf m}_{k}\}
are orthonormal basis for
[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
and
[\boldsymbol{\mu}]
, respectively. From the definition of the inner-product of multivectors (
2.3
), we have

(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mu})=\det\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}&\mathbf{a}\cdot{\bf m}_{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{a}\cdot{\bf m}_{k}\\
{\bf w}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{a}&{\bf w}_{1}\cdot{\bf m}_{1}&\ldots&{\bf w}_{1}\cdot{\bf m}_{k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\bf w}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}&{\bf w}_{k}\cdot{\bf m}_{1}&\ldots&{\bf w}_{k}\cdot{\bf m}_{k}\,\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\mathbf{a}\in[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}
, we know
{\bf w}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a}=0
for
1\leq i\leq k
, and because
\mathbf{a}
is a unit vector, it follows that

(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mu})=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf{a}\cdot{\bf m}_{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{a}\cdot{\bf m}_{k}\\
0&{\bf w}_{1}\cdot{\bf m}_{1}&\ldots&{\bf w}_{1}\cdot{\bf m}_{k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&{\bf w}_{k}\cdot{\bf m}_{1}&\ldots&{\bf w}_{k}\cdot{\bf m}_{k}\,\end{pmatrix}.

Computing the determinant using minors gives the desired result.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Consider
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
{\bf a}\in[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}
. For any
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\Lambda^{k-1}([\boldsymbol{\omega}])
, we have

{\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\nu}\in T_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(2.19)

meaning that
{\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\nu}
is in the tangent space of the manifold
\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
at
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for a collection of
\boldsymbol{\nu}
that span
\Lambda^{k-1}([\boldsymbol{\omega}])
. Thus, we need only consider the case where
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{k-1}([\boldsymbol{\omega}])
, so that
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is a simple
(k-1)
-vector of unit magnitude. Similarly, we may assume that
{\bf a}
is a unit vector. As
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
are both simple multivectors and
[\boldsymbol{\nu}]\subseteq[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
, there is a
{\bf b}\in[\boldsymbol{\nu}]^{\perp}
such that
\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf b}\wedge\boldsymbol{\nu}
. Moreover, as
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
have unit magnitude,
{\bf b}
must have unit magnitude. Consider the curve
\hat{\bf a}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by
\hat{\bf a}(s)\coloneqq\cos(s){\bf b}+\sin(s){\bf a}
and the curve
\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(s)=\hat{\bf a}(s)\wedge\boldsymbol{\nu}
. One can readily check that
|\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(s)|=1
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(s)
is simple for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, so
\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is a curve in
\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. As
\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(0)=\boldsymbol{\omega}
, it follows that

\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime}(0)={\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\nu}\in T_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(2.20)

which establishes the claim.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

If
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, with
k\leq\ell
, then
|\boldsymbol{\mu}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}|=|\boldsymbol{\omega}||{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\mu}|
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
k
. The
k=1
case was established by Mihaila and Seguin
[
MS25
]
[Lemma 1]. Now assume that

|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}|=|\boldsymbol{\omega}||{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\quad\text{for}\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(2.21)

Given
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, find
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\lambda}={\bf 0}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}={\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\lambda}.

(2.22)

It follows from (
2.11
), the induction hypothesis (
2.21
), (
2.22
), and Lagrange’s identity (
2.12
) that

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\mu}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|({\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\lambda})\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\lrcorner({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega})|^{2}

\displaystyle=|{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}|^{2}|{\bf P}_{{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{\omega}|^{2}||{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\bf a}|^{2}|{\bf P}_{{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{\omega}|^{2}[|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\bf a}\wedge{\bf P}_{{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}|^{2}+|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\bf a}\lrcorner{\bf P}_{{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}|^{2}].

One can find an orthonormal basis
\{{\bf w}_{1},\dots,{\bf w}_{\ell}\}
of
[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
such that
\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf w}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf w}_{\ell}
and
{\bf a}\cdot{\bf w}_{i}=0
for
i>1
. It follows that

{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\bf a}=({\bf a}\cdot{\bf w}_{1}){\bf w}_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf P}_{{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k-1}([{\bf w}_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf w}_{k}]).

(2.23)

Thus,
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\bf a}\lrcorner{\bf P}_{{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}={\bf 0}
. Moreover, from (
2.22
)
1
,

{\bf P}_{{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}={\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}.

(2.24)

This means that
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\bf a}\wedge{\bf P}_{{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}={\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\bf a}\wedge{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}={\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}({\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

For
{\bf a}\in{\cal U}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf 0}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it is true that

|({\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\llcorner\boldsymbol{\mu}|^{2}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu})^{2}+|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu})|^{2}.

(2.25)

Proof.

Notice that for any
{\bf u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by the definition of the right and left interior-products and (
2.13
),

\displaystyle({\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\llcorner\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot{\bf u}

\displaystyle={\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot{\bf u}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mu}

(2.26)

\displaystyle={\bf u}\lrcorner({\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}

(2.27)

\displaystyle=[({\bf u}\cdot{\bf a})\boldsymbol{\omega}-{\bf a}\wedge({\bf u}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega})]\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}

(2.28)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu})({\bf a}\cdot{\bf u})-\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot{\bf u}\wedge({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu})

(2.29)

\displaystyle=[(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}){\bf a}-\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu})]\cdot{\bf u}.

(2.30)

It follows that

({\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\llcorner\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}){\bf a}-\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu}).

(2.31)

Notice that the two terms on the right-hand side of (
2.31
) are orthogonal since from the assumption
{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf 0}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu})\cdot{\bf a}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot{\bf a}\wedge({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu})

(2.32)

\displaystyle={\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot{\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu}

(2.33)

\displaystyle=0.

(2.34)

It then follows from Proposition
2.3
and (
2.7
) that

|({\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\llcorner\boldsymbol{\mu}|^{2}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu})^{2}+|\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu})|^{2}=(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu})^{2}+|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}({\bf a}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\mu})|^{2}.

(2.35)

∎

3
Set of disks

In this section we discuss the set of
(n-k)
-dimensional disks in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
used to define the fractional
k
-dimensional measure. Moreover, several measure theoretic properties of these disks will be established that will be of use in this work. We find it convenient to work with oriented disks, so for the rest of the paper a disk refers to an oriented disk.

Each
(n-k)
-dimensional disk can be represented by its center
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, radius
r>0
, and perpendicular
k
-dimensional (oriented) subspace represented by
\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The disk with center
p
, radius
r
, and orientation
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is denoted by

D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\coloneqq\{p+\xi\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathbf{v}\in{\cal U}([\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}),\xi\in[0,r)\}.

Thus, the set of all disks can be described by

\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{+}.

The dimension of this set is denoted by
d\coloneqq\text{dim}\,{\cal D}=n+k(n-k)+1
. By the boundary
\partial D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)
of a disk
D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)
we mean the
(n-k-1)
-dimensional manifold

\partial D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\coloneqq\{p+r\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathbf{v}\in{\cal U}([\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp})\}.

We denote by
\overline{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)
the closure of the disk
D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)
.

Consider a
k
-dimensional
C^{1}
manifold with boundary, where
1\leq k\leq n-1
, which we denote by
\overline{{\cal M}}
.
{\cal M}
will denote the interior of the manifold and
\partial{\cal M}
its boundary, so that
\overline{{\cal M}}={\cal M}\cup\partial{\cal M}
. Let
vol
denote a volume form for
\overline{{\cal M}}
, meaning that for each
z\in\overline{{\cal M}}
,
\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
[\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)]=T_{z}\overline{{\cal M}}
. It is not assumed that
\overline{{\cal M}}
is orientable, so it is possible that
vol
is not continuous. Define the following subsets of
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\partial\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{H}^{0}(\overline{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\partial\mathcal{M})\neq 0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\tan}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}:\text{there is }z\in\overline{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\overline{\mathcal{M}}\text{ such that }\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)=0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{H}^{0}(\overline{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\overline{\mathcal{M}})=\infty\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\partial D}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{H}^{0}(\partial D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\mathcal{M})\neq 0\}.

Roughly speaking, the interpretation of these sets is as follows:
{\cal D}_{\partial{\cal M}}
consists of those disks whose closure intersect
\partial{\cal M}
,
{\cal D}_{\text{tan}}
consists of those disks that are tangent—that is, not transversal—to the manifold,
{\cal D}_{\infty}
is the collection of disks that intersect
\overline{{\cal M}}
an infinite number of times, and
{\cal D}_{\partial D}
consists of those disks whose boundary intersects
{\cal M}
.

Mihaila and Seguin
[
MS25
]
[Lemma 2] established the following facts about these sets.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given an open, bounded set
{\cal E}\subseteq{\cal D}
, the following results hold:

1.

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}({\cal D}_{\partial\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{E})<\infty
,

2.

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}({\cal D}_{\rm{tan}}\cap\mathcal{E})<\infty
,

3.

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}({\cal D}_{\partial D}\cap\mathcal{E})<\infty
,

4.

\mathcal{D}_{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\text{\rm tan}}
.

In this work we require several additional subsets of disks and results about them. Namely, consider the sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\partial\mathcal{M}1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{H}^{0}(\overline{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\partial\mathcal{M})=1\},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\partial\mathcal{M}2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{H}^{0}(\overline{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\partial\mathcal{M})\geq 2\},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\partial D1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{H}^{0}(\partial D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\mathcal{M})=1\},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\partial D2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{H}^{0}(\partial D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\mathcal{M})\geq 2\},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\rm odd}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}\setminus({\cal D}_{\partial{\cal M}}\cup{\cal D}_{\partial D}\cup{\cal D}_{\rm{tan}}):\mathcal{H}^{0}(D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\mathcal{M})\ \text{is odd}\},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{D}\setminus({\cal D}_{\partial{\cal M}}\cup{\cal D}_{\partial D}\cup{\cal D}_{\rm{tan}}):\mathcal{H}^{0}(D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\mathcal{M})\ \text{is even}\}.

The set

is exactly the set

Each element in

\mathbf{a}

Lemma 3.2
.

The following results hold:

1.

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{D}_{\partial D2})=0
,

2.

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{D}_{\partial{\cal M}2})=0
,

Proof.

Item 1.
To get the control on the measure of
{\cal D}_{\partial D2}
, we subdivide it into two subsets. Define

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in{\cal D}_{\partial D2}:p=\tfrac{z_{1}+z_{2}}{2}\mbox{ for some }z_{1},z_{2}\in{\cal M}\cap\partial D(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\},

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\cal D}_{\partial D2}\setminus S_{1}.

The set
S_{1}
consists of those disks in
{\cal D}_{\partial D2}
with center the midpoint between two points of intersection of

and
{\cal M}
, and

Now consider the set

is

\mathbf{a}
is a unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(n-1)

and
(z_{2}-z_{1})\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}=\bf 0

Proposition 3.5

(3.23)

(3.24)

Proof.

By Proposition
3.4

{\cal D}_{\text{odd}}

First, we show that
{\bf m}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)
is orthogonal to

since

({\bf c},0,0)\cdot{\bf m}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)=({\bf c}\wedge\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z))\cdot(-r{\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega}).

Find an orthonormal basis
\{{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2},\dots,{\bf w}_{k}\}

(3.26)

which is zero since the first row is entirely made out of zeros due to the properties of

. Next, consider any

and set

\displaystyle(r{\bf b},-{\bf a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi},0)\cdot{\bf m}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)

\displaystyle=-r{\bf b}\cdot\big((r\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})\llcorner\textbf{\rm{vol}}\big)-(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi})\cdot\big(r\mathbf{a}\wedge\big({\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(r\mathbf{a}\lrcorner\textbf{\rm{vol}})\big)\big)

Now we use the fact that

(3.27)

\displaystyle=-r^{2}\big(-({\bf b}\wedge\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z))\cdot({\bf b}\wedge\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi})+(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi})\cdot\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)\big)

\displaystyle=-r^{2}\big(-\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)\cdot(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi})+(\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi})\cdot\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)\big)=0.

=-(\mathbf{a}\wedge\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z))\cdot(r\mathbf{a}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega})+r\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}=0.

(3.28)

Next, we show that
{\bf m}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)
is in the tangent space
T_{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)}{\cal D}
. Since

T_{(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)}{\cal D}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times T_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R},

(3.29)

it suffices to show that the second component of
{\bf m}(p,\boldsymbol{\omega},r)
is in
T_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\hat{\Lambda}_{u}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. As we know

2.2
.

We now show

and

. The second of these conditions implies that the third component of

is positive. This means that the radius of the disk

is an increasing function of

for small

. Moreover, as there is a unique point

, it follows that for small negative

The next three results are related and together show that the integral of
r^{-1-\sigma}
over a certain collection of disks all of whose radius are less than
\eta
goes to zero as
\eta
goes to zero. This fact is useful in computing the first variation of the
\sigma
-measure in the next section.

Lemma 3.6
.

Given
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\hat{\Lambda}^{k}_{u}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
K>0
, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, define the region

{\cal R}\coloneqq\{z+{\bf t}+{\bf h}:\mathbf{t}\in[\boldsymbol{\mu}],{\bf h}\in[\boldsymbol{\mu}]^{\perp},\,\text{and}\ |{\bf h}|<K|\mathbf{t}|^{1+\alpha}\}.

(3.30)

If

{\cal D}_{\cal R}\coloneqq\{(\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in{\cal W}\times(0,\infty):\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}>0\mbox{ and }D(z+r\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap{\cal R}\neq\emptyset\},

(3.31)

then it follows that

{\cal D}_{\cal R}\subseteq\Big\{(\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in{\cal W}\times(0,\infty):r\geq\frac{|\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}|^{1/\alpha}}{2K^{1/\alpha}}\Big\}.

(3.32)

Proof.

Let
(\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in{\cal D}_{\cal R}
. Notice that
|\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}|\neq 1
, as otherwise
D(z+r\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap{\cal R}=\emptyset
. The main idea of this proof is to first consider the
(n-k)
-dimensional plane
z+[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}
and show that the closest point to
z
in the intersection of this plane with
{\cal R}
has distance at least
\frac{|\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}|^{1/\alpha}}{K^{1/\alpha}}
. Thus, for the disk
D(z+r{\bf a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)
to intersect
{\cal R}
would require
2r
to be larger than this distance.

To minimize the distance between
z
and the intersection of the plane
z+[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}
and
{\cal R}
, it suffices to minimize the distance to the intersection
(z+[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp})\cap\partial{\cal R}
not including
z
. Thus, we consider the minimization problem

\inf|p-z|^{2},\qquad p\in(z+[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp})\cap\partial{\cal R}\setminus\{z\}.

(3.33)

To analyze this problem, we employ the method of Lagrange multipliers. The constraints for
p
can be formulated as

\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner(p-z)={\bf 0}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\perp}(p-z)|^{2}=K^{2}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)|^{2+2\alpha},

(3.34)

the first of these being associated with the plane
z+[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}
and the second with
\partial{\cal R}
. It follows that there are Lagrange multipliers
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}\in\Lambda^{k-1}([\boldsymbol{\omega}])
such that

2(p-z)=2\lambda_{1}\big((1+\alpha)K^{2}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)|^{2\alpha}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)-{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)\big)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}.

Using the identity
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\perp}(p-z)=(p-z)-{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)
and rearranging the previous equation yields

2(1+\lambda_{1})(p-z)=2\lambda_{1}\big((1+\alpha)K^{2}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)|^{2\alpha}+1\big){\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}.

(3.35)

The goal is to use this equality to obtain a lower bound for
|p-z|
. To do so, we need to handle three degenerate cases before getting to the fourth, main case.

Case 1: Suppose
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)={\bf 0}
. Then, by (
3.34
)
2
,
{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)={\bf 0}
and, so,
p=z
, which is not allowed in (
3.33
).

Thus, for the rest of the cases, we can use the unit vector

\textbf{u}\coloneqq\frac{{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)}{|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)|}.

(3.36)

Case 2: Suppose
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)\neq{\bf 0}
and
\lambda_{1}=-1
. Using

\overline{\lambda}_{1}\coloneqq 2\big[(1+\alpha)K^{2}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)|^{2\alpha}+1\big]|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)|,

(
3.35
) can be written as

{\bf 0}=\overline{\lambda}_{1}\textbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}.

As
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}\in[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
, this implies
\textbf{u}\in[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
. We know
(p-z)\in[\boldsymbol{\omega}]^{\perp}
, so

0=\textbf{u}\cdot(p-z)={\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\textbf{u}\cdot(p-z)=\textbf{u}\cdot{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)=|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)|,

which is a contradiction.

Case 3: Suppose
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)\neq{\bf 0}
,
\lambda_{1}\neq-1
, and
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}={\bf 0}
. Upon defining

\beta_{1}\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{1}[(1+\alpha)K^{2}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)|^{2\alpha}+1]|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)|}{1+\lambda_{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\coloneqq\frac{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}}{2(1+\lambda_{1})},

(3.37)

(
3.35
) becomes

p-z=\beta_{1}\textbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}.

(3.38)

Utilizing (
3.34
)
1
, we find that

{\bf 0}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner(\beta_{1}\textbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega})=\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner\textbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega}).

Since
[\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}]\subseteq[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
, it is true that
[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}]\subseteq[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
, so it follows that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega})
. Therefore, the previous equation implies that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}=-\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner\textbf{u}
.
Hence, going back to (
3.38
), using (
2.8
) yields

p-z=\beta_{1}(\textbf{u}-(\boldsymbol{\omega}\llcorner\textbf{u})\lrcorner\boldsymbol{\omega})=\beta_{1}(\textbf{u}-{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\textbf{u})=\beta_{1}{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\textbf{u}.

Plugging this into (
3.34
)
2
gives

|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\perp}{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\textbf{u}|^{2}=\beta_{1}^{2\alpha}K^{2}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\textbf{u}|^{2+2\alpha}\,.

Since the right-hand side is 0 in this case, we conclude
{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\textbf{u}={\bf 0}
, so
\textbf{u}\in[\boldsymbol{\omega}].
As in Case 2, this gives a contradiction.

Case 4: We are now ready to handle the main case, in which
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p-z)\neq{\bf 0}
,
\lambda_{1}\neq-1
, and
{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}\neq{\bf 0}
. Define
\beta_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}
as in (
3.37
). As in Case 3,

|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\perp}{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\textbf{u}|^{2}=\beta_{1}^{2\alpha}K^{2}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\textbf{u}|^{2+2\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad p-z=\beta_{1}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}.

It follows that

\beta_{1}=\frac{|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\perp}{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\textbf{u}|^{1/\alpha}}{K^{1/\alpha}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\bf P}^{\perp}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\textbf{u}|^{1+1/\alpha}}

and, so,

|p-z|=\frac{|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\perp}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}\textbf{u}|^{1/\alpha}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}\textbf{u}|}{K^{1/\alpha}|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}|^{1+1/\alpha}}.

(3.39)

To help simplify the right-hand side of the previous equation, find an orthonormal basis
\{{\bf m}_{1},{\bf m}_{2},\dots,{\bf m}_{k}\}
for
[\boldsymbol{\mu}]
and
\{{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2},\dots,{\bf w}_{k}\}
for
[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
such that

{\bf m}_{i}\cdot{\bf w}_{j}=0\mbox{ if }i\neq j\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad{\bf m}_{i}\cdot{\bf w}_{i}=\cos\theta_{i},

(3.40)

where
0\leq\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2}\leq...\leq\theta_{k}\leq\pi/2
are the principal angles between the subspaces
[\boldsymbol{\mu}]
and
[\boldsymbol{\omega}]
; see Jordan
[
Jor75
]
. Moreover, the order of these vectors can be chosen so that
\boldsymbol{\mu}={\bf m}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf m}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf w}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge{\bf w}_{k}
so that from (
2.3
),

\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\cos\theta_{i}.

(3.41)

Since
\textbf{u}\in[\boldsymbol{\mu}]
, we can write
\textbf{u}=u_{1}{\bf m}_{1}+...+u_{k}{\bf m}_{k}
for real numbers
u_{1},...,u_{k}
. It follows that

{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}={\bf u}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}({\bf w}_{i}\otimes{\bf w}_{i}){\bf u}={\bf u}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}\cos\theta_{i}{\bf w}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(u_{i}({\bf m}_{i}-\cos\theta_{i}{\bf w}_{i}))

and, so,

|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(u_{i}^{2}|{\bf m}_{i}-\cos\theta_{i}{\bf w}_{i}|^{2})}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}}.

(3.42)

We also have

{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big[({\bf m}_{j}\otimes{\bf m}_{j})\sum_{i=1}^{k}(u_{i}({\bf m}_{i}-\cos\theta_{i}{\bf w}_{i}))\Big]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}{\bf m}_{i}

and, hence,

|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\sin^{4}\theta_{i}}.

(3.43)

Moreover, this yields

{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\perp}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}={\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}-{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}(\cos^{2}\theta_{i}{\bf m}_{i}-\cos\theta_{i}{\bf w}_{i})

and

|{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\perp}{\bf P}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\perp}{\bf u}|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{i}|\cos\theta_{i}{\bf m}_{i}-{\bf w}_{i}|^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}}.

(3.44)

Inserting (
3.42
)–(
3.44
) into (
3.39
) yields

|p-z|=\left[\frac{\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}\Big)^{\alpha}}{K^{2}\Big(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\sin^{4}\theta_{i}\Big)^{\alpha+1}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\geq\left[\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}}{K^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}.

(3.45)

Now set

\xi_{i}\coloneqq\frac{u_{i}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}},

and notice that
0\leq\xi_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}=1
. By the generalized arithmetic-geometric mean inequality and (
3.41
)

|p-z|\geq K^{-1/\alpha}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\right]^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\geq K^{-1/\alpha}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\cos^{2}\theta_{i})^{\xi_{i}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\\
\geq K^{-1/\alpha}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\right]^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}=K^{-1/\alpha}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\cos\theta_{i}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}=\frac{(\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega})^{1/\alpha}}{K^{1/\alpha}}.

4
First variation of the fractional measure

In this section we compute the first variation of the fractional

[

(4.1)

where

Theorem 4.1
.

\mathcal{M}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{({\bf a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\in\mathcal{W}\times(\varepsilon,\infty):\mathcal{H}^{0}(D(z+r\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\omega},r)\cap\mathcal{M})\mbox{ is odd},\ \boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\textbf{\rm{vol}}(z)>0\},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rm e}^{+}(z,\varepsilon)

(4.4)

\sigma

(4.5)

By the dominated convergence theorem, this truncated version converges to
\text{Meas}_{\sigma}^{k}({\cal M},\Omega)
as
\eta\downarrow 0
. To establish the desired result, we will compute the first variation of this truncated
\sigma
-measure and then send

Consider

Due to the regularity of
