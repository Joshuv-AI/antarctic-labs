Six-Month Real-World Data in Macular Edema Due to Retinal Vein Occlusion Treated with Faricimab: Swiss Retina Research Network Report
Authors: Katharina Heck, Katja Hatz, Marco Cattaneo, Isabel Pfister, Simon Magnin, Ludovico Ruscitti, Isabel Stasik, Anne Tillmann, Marat Shamshiev, Joseph Fang, Andreas Weinberger, Sandrine A Zweifel, Justus G Garweg, Chiara M Eandi, Gabriela Grimaldi, Marion R Munk, Gabor M Somfai, Aude Ambresin, Nicolas Feltgen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFaricimab has been shown to be effective for treatment of macular edema (ME) due to retinal vein occlusion (RVO). So far, there is only limited evidence in real-life settings and hard-to-treat cases.MethodsThis multicenter, retrospective study evaluated patients with branch or central (including hemi-central) RVO treated with a faricimab treat-and-extend regimen with at least 6 months of follow-up (FU). Data included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), center-point retinal thickness (CRT), central subfield thickness (CST), presence of intra- and subretinal fluid in the 1, 3, and 6 mm Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid zones, number of injections, and adverse events at baseline and at 1, 3–4, and 6 months of faricimab therapy. Pretreated eyes were additionally evaluated at predefined time points of the pretreatment period.ResultsA total of 112 eyes from 111 patients, including 13 treatment-naive and 99 pretreated eyes, were included. BCVA improved in treatment-naïve eyes from 0.46 ± 0.28 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) at baseline to 0.32 ± 0.28 logMAR 6 months later (p = 0.041). BCVA remained stable in pretreated eyes (0.20 ± 0.25 vs. 0.21 ± 0.29 logMAR; p = 0.7). CRT and CST improved in both groups: in treatment-naïve eyes, CRT from 522 to 267 µm (p < 0.001) and CST from 467 to 301 µm (p = 0.032); in pretreated eyes, CRT from 275 to 253 µm (p = 0.009) and CST from 315 to 293 µm (p = 0.001). Significant resolution in retinal fluid was shown in the central 1 mm (p ≤ 0.014), 3 mm (p ≤ 0.013), and 6 mm zones (p ≤ 0.013) of the ETDRS grid in both groups. Over 6 months, treatment-naïve and pretreated eyes received a mean of 5 ± 1 injections. One systemic adverse event and no intraocular inflammation were recorded during FU.ConclusionThis study demonstrates good real-world efficacy with significant drying and promising safety profile of faricimab in eyes with ME due to RVO, including a high proportion of hard-to-treat cases, over a 6-month period.Keywords: Anti-VEGF, Treatment-naïve, Pretreated, Intravitreal injection, Switching

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Treatment of patients with macular edema after retinal vein occlusion using vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors is effective, but stable improvements even in hard-to-treat cases would be desirable.Faricimab, a new drug that combines anti-VEGF and angiopoietin-2 for intravitreal injection, has demonstrated treatment efficacy in phase III trials.In this study, we examined real-world outcomes in a multicenter, retrospective study including switchers and treatment-naïve patients.What was learned from the study?Faricimab was effective and safe in cystoid macular edema after retinal vein occlusion with a high proportion of hard-to-treat cases.Best-corrected visual acuity showed moderate improvement in treatment-naïve eyes and stabilization after switching therapySignificant decrease in macular thickness was observed in switchers and treatment-naïve eyes.

Introduction

Retinal vein occlusion (RVO) is the second-leading cause of retinal vascular disease after diabetic retinopathy and represents a frequent cause of vision loss [1, 2]. With RVO reportedly affecting approximately 28.1 million people globally in 2015, and increasing in prevalence with advancing age, the treatment of macular edema (ME), the leading cause of vision loss in RVO, has become an increasingly important aspect of ophthalmic care [3, 4]. The gold standard therapy for ME management in RVO entails intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy that reduces VEGF-driven vascular leakage and facilitates more resilient retinal vasculature [5]. In addition to VEGF, angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) has been found to play an essential role in vessel homeostasis and neovascularization, promoting vascular permeability and abnormal vessel structure [6]. Elevated vitreous Ang-2 levels have been reported in ocular vascular diseases, notably among the highest in patients with RVO [7]. This might indicate that Ang-2 inhibition could be a particularly effective therapeutic target in these patients.

Faricimab represents a new treatment approach that effectively targets not only VEGF but also Ang-2 pathways. The BALATON and COMINO randomized controlled phase III trials (RCTs) have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of faricimab in treating ME secondary to RVO [8]. However, real-world applicability has proven difficult due to discrepancies in monitoring and injection frequency resulting from reduced treatment compliance, as well as reduced therapeutic responses compared with strictly controlled clinical trials [9].

Due to the very recent approval of faricimab for ME secondary to RVO, and consequent restrictions on cost reimbursement by health systems, real-world data have been limited so far, and the first reports have only recently begun to emerge. To date, only a few small real-world studies have reported favorable short-term outcomes regarding visual acuity and safety in patients switched from other anti-VEGF agents to faricimab and in treatment-naive patients, respectively [10–18]. One prospective study showed significantly improved visual acuity and decreased central retinal thickness after a mean follow-up (FU) of 11.7 weeks after switch to faricimab in patients with ME due to RVO refractory to prior anti-VEGF therapy [11]. RVOSTAR, an ongoing prospective study of 72 planned treatment-naïve patients with ME due to RVO treated with faricimab in a modified treat-and-extend (T&E) regimen, with a FU of 72 weeks, is currently recruiting [19]. Lastly, recently published global population-based data from 65 countries assessed safety signals and reported a favorable ocular safety profile for faricimab comparable to that of aflibercept 2 and 8 mg [20].

Due to early approval of faricimab for ME due to RVO in Switzerland and the application of a largely identical treatment scheme in the centers of the Swiss Retina Research Network (SRRN), an analysis of the pooled SRRN data is ideally suited to systematically gain knowledge regarding the real-life outcomes of faricimab for ME due to RVO in larger populations. Our retrospective study presents real-world data for least 6 months of FU from 10 Swiss medical retina centers, with a high proportion of hard-to-treat ME. The treatment protocol across all participating centers was a standardized T&E regimen, as it has been shown to provide superior morphological outcomes in ME secondary to RVO compared with a PRN injection protocol [21]. Evaluation primarily included functional and morphological retinal parameters, as well as potential treatment side effects. Treatment-naive patients and patients switched from other anti-VEGF therapies were included in the analysis.

Methods

This retrospective, longitudinal, observational multicenter case study was conducted by reviewing the electronic patient data from 10 major Swiss medical retina centers from the Swiss Retina Research Network (SRRN) (Vista Augenklinik Binningen, Binningen; Berner Augenklinik, Bern; Jules Gonin Eye Hospital, Lausanne; Institute of Clinical Neurosciences of Southern Switzerland, Lugano; Department of Ophthalmology, Universitätsspital Basel, Basel; Augenärzte Bern- Zentrum Marktgasse Gutblick AG, Bern; Department of Ophthalmology, Stadtspital Zürich, Zürich; Swiss Visio Retina Research Center, Lausanne; Pallas Kliniken AG, Olten; Department of Ophthalmology, Universitätsspital Zürich, University of Zürich, Zürich). Data were collected from July 1, 2024, to September 30, 2025.

The study was approved by all participating ethics committees, led by the Ethics Committee of the canton of Berne (registration no. 2024–01026). Coded patient data were included based on general or specific informed consent, in line with the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) E6 guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki, and Swiss law.

Patients were included in the analysis if they were at least 18 years of age, presented with ME secondary to treatment-naïve or pretreated RVO, and received intravitreal faricimab therapy for at least 6 months.

Exclusion criteria for the study were the following: patient’s refusal to grant consent for the use of their coded data; FU time of less than 6 months; intravitreal corticosteroids within 6 months prior to the first faricimab injection; preexisting macular structural damage without functional potential; intraocular surgery or laser treatment within 3 months prior to the first faricimab injection except for YAG laser capsulotomy; clinically relevant opacifications of the optical axis; Snellen visual acuity below 0.05 at diagnosis or switch to faricimab; history of amblyopia with relevant reduction in VA before the occurrence of RVO; systemic comorbidities which could potentially confound interpretation of treatment outcomes, i.e., local or systemic rheumatoid diseases and/or vasculitis requiring treatment. Additionally, patients who discontinued faricimab treatment within the first 6 months of FU were excluded from the data analysis. If a patient underwent cataract surgery during that period, their BCVA values were also excluded from the data analysis.

Electronic patient data were evaluated at certain designated time points: prior to initiation of faricimab treatment (baseline), and 1, 3–4, and 6 months after initiating faricimab therapy. Data from pretreated eyes were additionally assessed at the start of the first intravitreal treatment (IVT) in general, 3–4 months later, 6 months before switch to faricimab, and at the time of the last injection before switch to faricimab.

At each time point, Snellen refracted visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP) and the following optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging data were collected: center point retinal thickness (CRT), central subfield thickness (CST), and the presence of intraretinal fluid (IRF), subretinal fluid (SRF), or both, in the central 1 mm, 3 mm, and 6 mm zones defined by the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid. Baseline and follow-up OCT measurements were obtained at all sites via the Heidelberg Spectralis® device (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Snellen refracted BCVA values were converted to corresponding logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) values; in order to estimate the potential changes in ETDRS letters, the raw values before estimation were converted into ETDRS letter score [22–24].

At all sites, the treatment protocol followed a T&E regimen (with or without 3× monthly loading dose) in which treatment intervals were shortened or extended by 2-week increments within a scope of 4 to 16 weeks based on disease activity based on OCT findings, not visual acuity or hemorrhages as sole factors. If retinal fluid was newly detected within the 6 mm circle of the ETDRS grid, or if fluid at any location increased, disease activity was assumed and the treatment interval adapted accordingly, the decision for reinjection were at the discretion of the investigators.

Primary outcomes included mean changes in visual acuity (ETDRS letters), CST, and CRT (μm).

Secondary outcomes included the number of eyes with complete resolution of retinal fluid in the central 1, 3, and 6 mm ETDRS subfields after a single injection of faricimab, at 3 to 4 months, and at 6 months. The proportion of eyes with IRF and/or SRF at these time points, the number of injections administered, and incidence of adverse events (AEs) were also evaluated.

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical data are presented as counts and percentages and compared using McNemar’s chi-square test. Numerical variables were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and as median and interquartile range (IQR: 25–75%). Missing data were handled by available case analyses (the number of missing values is reported for each variable). No corrections for multiple testing or for the correlation between the eyes of the same patient were applied. The level of significance was set at a p-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Demographics

A total of 170 eyes (169 patients) received faricimab treatment during the study period; 58 eyes did not meet the inclusion criteria and were therefore excluded from analysis. The final dataset included 112 eyes from 111 patients (69 male and 42 female) with macular edema secondary to RVO who received faricimab injection therapy during a FU period of 6 ± 1 months. The eyes were subdivided into two groups: 13 treatment-naïve eyes and 99 eyes pretreated with other anti-VEGF agents (switchers). None of the eyes had received prior intravitreal corticosteroid therapy. One patient was treated bilaterally with faricimab. A total of 58 eyes presented with a BRVO, including 54 eyes with a CRVO (including hemi-CRVO).

Visual Outcomes

In treatment-naïve eyes, mean BCVA improved significantly from 0.46 ± 0.28 logMAR letters (median: 0.40, IQR: 0.30–0.70) at baseline to 0.32 ± 0.28 logMAR (median: 0.20, IQR: 0.10–0.49) 6 months later (p = 0.041), which corresponds to approximately +7 ETDRS letters. In pretreated eyes, no significant difference in mean BCVA from 0.20 ± 0.25 logMAR (median: 0.10, IQR: 0.00–0.30) at baseline to 0.21 ± 0.29 logMAR (median: 0.20, IQR: 0.00–0.30) 6 months later (p = 0.7) was recorded (Fig. 1). For pretreated eyes, eyes with CRVO (including HCRVO) versus BRVO showed significant differences in BCVA, with much better mean BCVA in BRVO at baseline as well at 6 months (0.31 ± 0.28 vs. 0.13 ± 0.19 logMAR, p < 0.001; 0.31 ± 0.27 vs. 0.14 ± 0.30 logMAR, p < 0.001), but for both groups with no significant differences between baseline and 6-month values (CRVO p = 0.08, BRVO p > 0.9). There were no significant differences in BCVA between female and male patients at any time point. BCVA values from five pretreated eyes and one naïve eye that received cataract surgery during the FU period were excluded from our data analysis to minimize potential bias of results.

Safety Outcomes

One mild case of vitreous floaters and posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) in association with faricimab IVT was reported during the 6-month FU period. No cases of intraocular inflammation (IOI) or vasculitis were reported. One systemic adverse event—a non-ST-elevation Myocardial Infarction (NSTEMI)—was recorded.

Discussion

In this multicenter study, we demonstrated the efficacy and safety of faricimab using a T&E regimen on patients with ME secondary to RVO over a 6-month FU period in a real-world Swiss population with a high proportion of hard-to-treat ME. This study uniquely reflects a healthcare setting with early access to therapy, resulting in relatively high baseline BCVA and substantial pretreatment exposure, thereby representing a particularly challenging and clinically relevant population. Race and ethnicity data were not collected because the patients were predominantly Swiss citizens, and we do not expect any differences in the analysis.

Analysis of functional outcomes in our study showed stabilization in BCVA, consistent with expectations, from 0.20 logMAR at switch to 0.21 logMAR 6 months later in the pretreated group, with no changes in BCVA in the CRVO and BRVO subgroups. In comparison, the naïve group demonstrated a moderate improvement in mean BCVA from 0.46 logMAR at baseline to 0.32 logMAR at 6-month FU (approximately +7 ETDRS letters). This fell short of expectations considering the BALATON/COMINO phase III clinical trials reported mean visual acuity gains of approximately −0.34 logMAR (+ 16.9 ETDRS letters) from baseline at week 24 [8]. This smaller increase in VA might be explained by a ceiling effect due to much higher baseline BCVA in our study (0.46 logMAR vs. approx. 0.6 and 0.7 in BALATON and COMINO, respectively) and by differences in the populations, comparing the strictly selected patients in RCTs and the more heterogeneous real-world populations with varying levels of disease severity and comorbid conditions. Our high baseline BCVA could be a result of early diagnosis and broad access to therapy in our Swiss study population.

Real-world data on the effects of faricimab on RVO-related ME have been limited so far, with analyses of a small number of patients and predominantly over short observational periods. Limited numbers of patients with RVO were included in mixed-patient analyses: While the FARTURK study did not perform a separate analysis of the 21 included patients with RVO, 29 treatment-naïve patients with CRVO in the FARI-LATAM study showed significantly higher visual acuity improvements after a mean follow-up of 26 weeks (1.31–0.83 logMAR) than in our study (0.46 logMAR to 0.32 logMAR at 6 months) [13, 14]. In FARI-LATAM, visual acuity gains were also achieved in 21 pretreated patients with CRVO, in contrast to the stability observed in our patients, which may be explained by more intensive pretreatment in our patients with correspondingly higher visual acuity at the time of switch (0.46 logMAR vs. 1.02 logMAR), with a consequent ceiling effect [14]. Short-term retrospective and prospective analyses in small numbers (19–52 patients) of treatment-naïve and pretreated patients showed significant BCVA improvements after 3 to 9 months [10–12, 15–18]. Within these, the smaller studies [10, 11, 15–17] with 19–30 patients included a population with similar baseline BCVA while the only larger one [12] (52 patients) included a completely different population with a baseline BCVA of about 0.9 logMAR and thick retinae (mean CST > 500 µm). Therefore, comparison of our analysis—to date the largest evaluation of faricimab in ME due to RVO—to the aforementioned analyses might be limited.

In our study, we observed significant thinning of central macular thickness in both the naïve and pretreated groups. This is in accordance with the BALATON and COMINO trials, which showed an even greater decrease in CST at week 24 (BALATON −311 µm, COMINO −461 µm) in a population with more severe baseline ME (mean baseline CST 558 µm and 702 µm, respectively) than ours (315 µm pretreated, 467 µm treatment naïve) [8]. In the RELATE study, there was no significant benefit observed from increasing the ranibizumab standard dosage of 0.5–2.0 mg, demonstrating the limitations of VEGF monotherapy in treating ME due to RVO [25]. Our findings of significantly decreased CST/CRT not only in naïve eyes but, most importantly, in eyes pretreated with other anti-VEGF medications might suggest a possible further effect of faricimab as an anti-VEGF and anti-Ang-2 combination therapy, which was mainly driven by eyes with CRVO. Patients with RVO have been reported to have among the highest vitreous Ang-2 levels in ocular vascular diseases, and high Ang-2 expression has been implicated in the pathogenic mechanism of RVO, which might be especially pronounced in eyes with CRVO [7, 26]. A short-term CST-reducing effect of faricimab even in anti-VEGF pretreated eyes was shown in a small, recently published prospective study and a small retrospective case series [12, 15–18]. We were able to confirm this in this large real-life population with RVO and strict pretreatment in a health care system with early and continuous access to intravitreal therapies.

In BALATON and COMINO, a greater proportion of patients achieved complete resolution of macular leakage after 6 months with monthly faricimab injections than with monthly aflibercept 2 mg injections [8]. In our study population, we found that considerably more—effectively 73%—of treatment-naïve patients demonstrated macular dryness in the 1 mm, 3 mm, and 6 mm ETDRS grid zones at 6 months. The anatomic outcome exceeded expectations by far, but should be interpreted with caution given our small treatment-naïve patient cohort (n = 11) and the associated lack of statistical power. Even more interesting, and statistically more robust, is the fact that in our large, intensively pretreated population, significantly more eyes achieved retinal dryness in the 1 mm, 3 mm, and 6 mm ETDRS grid zones at 6 months compared with the switching time point. In the small—but with regard to BCVA and CST comparable—pretreated subgroups of Hafner et al. (n = 14), Inokuchi et al. (n = 18), and Hirakata et al. (n = 13), similar effects seemed to appear [11, 15, 17]. Unfortunately, the multicenter FARI-LATAM analysis did not evaluate OCT dryness data in the 41 pretreated RVO eyes [14].

At all sites, intravitreal therapy followed a T&E regimen in which injection intervals were adapted based exclusively on the retinal fluid status determined by OCT data, and the interval was shortened or extended by 2-week increments within a range of 4–16 weeks [27]. In the BALATON and COMINO trials, the initial treatment phase consisted of six initial intravitreal injections of faricimab administered at 4-week intervals [8]. This loading protocol was set to establish strong disease control before interval extension. In contrast, we observed an initial loading phase in only 48% of our patients but demonstrated strong anatomical and comparable functional outcomes, challenging the necessity of a loading phase for faricimab in general instead of early adoption of this treatment according to individual needs. To reduce patient burden and health insurance costs, a pivotal focus at present is extending injection intervals in T&E regimens without compromising efficacy in real-world clinical practice. However, this study’s FU period of 6 months, even though it was the longest FU period in a real-world study published on the efficacy of faricimab in treating ME secondary to RVO, was too short to detect and analyze significant changes in treatment intervals [10, 12, 13]. Further research is needed to validate the long-term efficacy, safety, and injection interval extension of faricimab in a real-world setting.

In addition to individualization of injection intervals, treatment decisions are affected by factors such as health insurance coverage and regulatory approval. Swissmedic, Switzerland’s national authority for therapeutic products, officially approved the use of faricimab for the treatment of ME due to RVO in July 2024, but health insurance reimbursement is not yet confirmed, and numerous local health insurance providers still do not cover the costs for intravitreal therapy with faricimab to treat RVO to date (early 2026). Since some of our analyzed Swiss real-world patients had received faricimab through samples from the manufacturer, lack of health insurance coverage led four of our analyzed patients to switch from faricimab to an alternative anti-VEGF, specifically aflibercept 2 mg. This was also identified as one of the main reasons for faricimab treatment discontinuity in our analysis.

One of the key strengths of this study is its multicentric design with a relatively large population in a real-world setting, representing a diverse patient population with variability in demographics, comorbidities, treatment histories, and disease duration, with less center-specific bias leading to improved generalizability of the findings and real-world relevance regarding everyday clinical practice. Furthermore, this analysis has not been sponsored and was carried out independently of any pharmaceutical company.

However, this study also has several limitations, such as its retrospective and observational design, which might lead to selection bias, as well as the absence of a control group, limiting causal inference, and the follow-up time of only 6 months. For the limited number of treatment-naïve eyes, a subgroup analysis of CRVO versus BRVO did not appear meaningful and was therefore not conducted. The group of eyes with Snellen BCVA < 0.05 was not studied, and therefore no conclusion can be drawn for probably severely ischemic or highly refractory cases. Baseline lifestyle and health-related variables related to RVO risk, such as smoking status, body mass index, presence of concurrent arterial hypertension, diabetes, sleep apnea, or known blood disorders, could not be systematically collected due to the multicentric structure of the analysis. While this limits adjustment for potential confounding variables in treatment effects, it reflects real-world clinical practice across various centers. Since the analysis was performed exclusively on a Swiss population, the generalizability of the results may be limited due to Switzerland’s well-developed health care system, with mandatory, affordable health insurance and generally early access to medication and health care. The discontinuation of faricimab by 11 patients during the 6-month FU period represents another limitation of our study. These patients were not excluded from our analysis, thus introducing attrition bias and limiting the significance of treatment effects. It should be noted, however, that this better reflects real-world IVT conditions, where treatment discontinuation is common in everyday clinical practice.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this real-world analysis of faricimab in patients with ME secondary to RVO represents one of the earliest contributions to an expanding evidence base and may help optimize its application in routine clinical practice. Favorable visual and, in particular, anatomical outcomes in both treatment-naïve and pretreated eyes, the latter including a high proportion of hard-to-treat ME, might suggest additional benefits of faricimab’s dual inhibition pathway in patients with RVO. The low number of AEs demonstrates a promising risk–benefit profile. Further multicenter studies with larger patient populations and extended follow-up are required to validate these findings and improve treatment approaches for retinal vascular disorders such as RVO.
