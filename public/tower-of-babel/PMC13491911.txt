Case report on resistant postpartum hemorrhage during cesarean section treated with a modified quadruple uterine belt suture technique for uterus preservation
Authors: Yanyan Bai, Lizhu Guo, Fuling Wang, Kehua Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere postpartum hemorrhage during cesarean section is a critical obstetric emergency; delayed hemostasis may lead to hemorrhagic shock, hysterectomy, and even maternal mortality. Individualized uterine-preserving hemostatic strategies represent the central focus and greatest challenge in current clinical management.Case presentationThis article reports a case of a patient at 40 + 2 weeks of gestation with concurrent gestational diabetes mellitus and prenatal fever. During cesarean section, the patient developed severe postpartum hemorrhage (with blood loss reaching 2,500 mL) due to complete dense adhesion between placenta and membranes, partial placental implantation on the posterior uterine wall, extensive intrauterine adhesions with pseudocyst formation, and uterine atony. Despite multiple hemostatic measures—including manual placental detachment, forceps removal of implanted tissue, suture closure with braided sutures, administration of uterotonic agents, ligation of bilateral ascending uterine arteries, local figure-of-eight sutures, and intrauterine gauze packing—all proved ineffective. Emergency quadruple modified uterine belt suturing was subsequently performed, successfully preserving the uterus. Postoperatively, multidisciplinary comprehensive management was implemented, encompassing anti-infection therapy, antithrombotic treatment, prevention of uterine necrosis, and correction of anemia and hypoalbuminemia, effectively preventing complications such as infection, uterine necrosis, and venous thrombosis. Follow-up imaging at 3 months showed no abnormalities, with favorable outcomes for both mother and fetus. This study delineates key procedural principles for this technique, providing a reproducible emergency management protocol for clinical practice.ConclusionsThe quadruple modified uterine dorsal suture technique represents a variant of traditional compression suturing, enabling uniform compression of the entire uterine segment and rapid hemostasis. Combined with multimodal interventions, it effectively addresses refractory postpartum hemorrhage of complex etiology. Strict control of suture tension and postoperative dynamic monitoring can balance the benefits of uterine preservation with surgical risks.Keywords: Cesarean section, Severe postpartum hemorrhage, Modified quadruple uterine belt suture, Multi-modal hemostasis, Uterine necrosis, Uterine preservation

Background

Postpartum hemorrhage is one of the leading causes of maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for 13% to 38% of all maternal deaths, with this proportion inversely correlated with a country’s development level and healthcare service quality [1]. Even in high-income countries, the incidence of postpartum hemorrhage continues to rise, closely associated with abnormal placental attachment and increased cesarean delivery rates [2, 3]. Severe postpartum hemorrhage during cesarean section (blood loss ≥ 1000 mL) manifests acutely and progresses rapidly, posing significant challenges to clinical hemostatic management. Uterine atony and placental factors are the primary etiologies, while the combination of multiple contributing factors markedly complicates hemostasis.

The modified B-Lynch suture is a commonly used conservative hemostatic technique in clinical practice; however, traditional double-needle suturing demonstrates limited efficacy in managing bleeding caused by complex etiologies. Current research supports the clinical application of various modified suture techniques. While combined approaches may improve uterine preservation rates, they increase risks of uterine ischemia and infection, thereby posing challenges for clinical decision-making between uterine preservation and hysterectomy.

This case employed a quadruple modified uterine belt suture technique to treat refractory postpartum hemorrhage with successful uterine preservation. By analyzing its technical characteristics and safety profile from the literature, this study provides a reference for managing similar clinical cases.

Case presentation

General conditions

The patient, a 27-year-old female with no prior pregnancies (2 pregnancies and 0 deliveries), was admitted due to gestational diabetes mellitus at 40 + 2 weeks of gestation. She had no history of chronic conditions such as hypertension or cardiac disease, no prior surgical history, and one history of induced abortion.

Admission examination: Body temperature 36.5 °C, pulse 93 beats/min, respiratory rate 19 breaths/min, blood pressure 125/77 mmHg; height 165 cm, weight 80 kg, body mass index 29.38 kg/m². Obstetric examination: Uterine height 36 cm, abdominal circumference 102 cm, cephalic presentation, fetal heart rate 139 beats/min, no uterine contractions; cervical Bishop score 2, pelvic measurements normal. Auxiliary examinations: Obstetric color Doppler ultrasound revealed a single live fetus in cephalic position with biparietal diameter approximately 9.4 cm, head circumference approximately 33.2 cm, femur length approximately 7.2 cm, abdominal circumference approximately 35.0 cm, amniotic fluid volume 10.9 cm; hemoglobin level 109 g/L (mild anemia).

Hospital admission diagnosis: 40 + weeks of gestation, pregnancy stage 2, delivery stage 0, fetal position; gestational diabetes mellitus (type 1); gestational anemia.

Disease progression and surgical indications

Upon admission, the patient underwent balloon-induced labor induction. The following day, artificial membrane rupture combined with intravenous oxytocin infusion was performed for labor induction. During the procedure, prenatal fever (body temperature 38.1 °C) and abnormal fetal heart rate monitoring were observed. Given the suspicion of intrauterine infection and fetal distress, an emergency lower uterine segment cesarean section was performed, with cefazolin sodium administered 30 min prior to surgery for infection prophylaxis.

Surgical procedure and stepwise hemostasis process

A cesarean section of the lower uterine segment was performed under combined spinal-epidural anesthesia, resulting in the delivery of a live male infant (weight 3500 g, Apgar score 10). The intrauterine temperature was elevated; fetal membrane tissue was obtained for routine bacterial culture and drug sensitivity testing. During surgery, dense adhesions between placenta and membranes, partial implantation of the posterior uterine wall, extensive intrauterine adhesions with pseudocysts formation, and uterine atony were observed, prompting the initiation of stepwise hemostasis.

Initial hemostasis: Oxytocin 10 U administered intramaterially and 10 U via intravenous infusion; manual placental separation; suturing of bleeding sites with braided sutures. Additional uterotonic agents were administered: intravenous push of carbetocin 100 µg and intramaterial injection of karprost 250 µg. After 15 min of observation, no hemostatic effect was achieved. Bilateral ascending branches of the uterine arteries were ligated followed by local figure-of-eight sutures, yet bleeding remained uncontrolled.

Intermittent hemostasis: Intrauterine gauze rolls (12 cm × 300 cm) were inserted for 30 min, but the hemostatic effect was poor; conventional double-needle modified uterine belt suturing was performed, followed by repeated intrauterine injection of carpoxanolol 250 µg and intravenous infusion of tranexamic acid 1.0 g. Active bleeding persisted after 30 min of observation.

Modified quadruple uterine support suture technique (1) Sutures and instruments: 2 strands of No.1 Coated VICRYL™ Plus antibacterial sutures, a 40 mm curved needle, and 90 cm of suture length; (2) Procedure steps: Extract the uterine support from the abdominal cavity; the right insertion site is located 2 cm below the inferior margin and 3 cm medial to the lateral border of the uterine incision, with needle insertion through the anterior wall → penetration into the uterine cavity → exit through the posterior wall; wrap the sutures around the uterine fundus and secure them at 4 cm medial to the lateral border of the fundus; the assistant compresses the uterus while the operator tightens and knots the sutures at the anterior superior part of the fundus; perform symmetrical placement of the second suture on the left side, adding one additional longitudinal suture between each original suture line on both sides to create four parallel longitudinal compression lines (Fig. 1. After observation for 30 min, bleeding ceased, and the uterus was successfully preserved. Two pelvic drainage tubes were placed to monitor bleeding and drain pelvic effusion.

Intraoperative parameters and management

The total blood loss was 2,500 mL, with transfusions including 8 U of type O leukocyte-depleted suspended red blood cells, 600 mL of virus-inactivated frozen plasma, and 12 U of cold precipitated coagulation factors; intravenous fluid replacement amounted to 4,000 mL, with intraoperative urine output of 400 mL. Vital signs remained stable after blood transfusion and fluid replacement. Prothrombin time, lactate levels, and alkaline residual were all within normal ranges; post-transfusion hemoglobin concentration was 94 g/L. The patient was subsequently transferred to the ICU for monitoring for 3 days.

Postoperative management and follow-up

Postoperative management

Antimicrobial therapy: Stepwise administration of antibiotics. Postoperatively, intravenous infusion of cefoperazone sodium and sulbactam sodium was initiated for anti-infective treatment, with dynamic monitoring of complete blood count (CBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and bacterial cultures of drainage fluid and amniotic fluid. On postoperative day 2, body temperature rose to 39.1 °C, CRP level reached 132.3 mg/L, and PCT level was 0.353 ng/mL; the regimen was adjusted to meropenem 1 g administered via intravenous infusion every 8 h. By postoperative day 5, body temperature normalized, inflammatory markers declined, bacterial cultures turned negative, and the antibiotic regimen was reduced to cefoperazone sodium and sulbactam sodium, accompanied by removal of the drainage tube. Intravenous antibiotics were discontinued on postoperative day 8.

Complication prevention and control: Prevent uterine necrosis and thrombosis. Postoperatively, monitor the fundal height and lochia characteristics daily; avoid excessive use of uterotonic agents to prevent exacerbation of myometrial ischemia due to rigid uterine contractions; administer subcutaneous enoxaparin sodium 4000 IU once daily on postoperative day 1, along with guidance for bed activity, ankle pump exercises, and limb pressure therapy; initiate ambulation on postoperative day 3; dynamically monitor coagulation function, D-dimer levels, and vital signs to rule out thrombosis.

Drainage and hospitalization: The drainage tube was left in place for 5 days, with a total drainage volume of 1,110 ml; postoperative hospitalization lasted 8 days.

Follow-up results

At 42 days postoperatively: Follow-up gynecological color Doppler ultrasound showed gradual restoration of echogenicity in the myometrium and absorption of iliac fossa effusion (FIG2.B). At 3 months postoperatively: Pelvic MRI confirmed no uterine ischemia, necrosis, or myometrial defects, nor chronic abdominal pain, late postpartum hemorrhage, pelvic organ prolapse, or urinary incontinence; menstruation had not yet resumed (likely related to breastfeeding and duration of postpartum recovery); neonatal growth and development were normal.

Discussion

The modified B-Lynch suture is a commonly employed conservative hemostatic technique in clinical practice. Current research supports the clinical application of various modified suturing techniques; while combined approaches may improve uterine preservation rates, they increase risks of uterine ischemia and infection, thereby posing challenges to clinical decision-making between uterine preservation and hysterectomy [4–7].

This case presented with refractory postpartum hemorrhage caused by complex etiologies during cesarean section. After failed stepwise hemostasis, a modified quadruple uterine support suture technique was successfully employed to preserve the uterus, effectively preventing complications such as infection, uterine necrosis, and thrombosis postoperatively. The key issues are discussed as follows:

Causes of Failure in Conventional Hemostasis

Causes of Failure in Conventional Hemostasis

Conventional hemostatic measures have significant limitations: traditional uterine compression suturing provides limited pressure coverage and fails to adequately control bleeding from the uterine fundus or lower uterine segment; ligation of only the ascending branch of the uterine artery cannot block collateral blood supply at the placental implantation site; intrauterine packing merely provides temporary compression without improving uterine atony, and rebleeding often occurs after removal of the packing.

2.Successful Reasons for the Modified Four-Row Suture Technique

Successful Reasons for the Modified Four-Row Suture Technique

This surgical technique represents an optimized variant of the traditional uterine compression suture method. The key to hemostasis lies in: uniform and comprehensive compression, more rational protection of blood supply; four longitudinal sutures covering the fundus, body, and lower segment of the uterus, with complete closure of blood sinuses for more thorough hemostasis; longitudinal suturing aligns with the physiological contraction characteristics of the uterus, reducing vascular occlusion and lowering the risk of uterine necrosis, with a hemostatic mechanism consistent with modified B-Lynch suture; reliable fixation: puncture anchoring at the fundus prevents suture slippage; minimal trauma: no incision is required in the uterine cavity, reducing risks of infection and intrauterine adhesions; rapid procedure: proficient execution takes only 5–6 min, making it suitable for emergency scenarios.

3.Risks and Trade-offs of Hemostatic Combination Regimens

Risks and Trade-offs of Hemostatic Combination Regimens

The combination of multiple surgical techniques can improve the success rate of uterine preservation, but complications must be carefully monitored: Uterine ischemic necrosis: Associated with suture tension and vascular ligation. The BLynch “double reinforcement” uterine compression suture technique reported in 1997 is a conservative surgical approach for controlling postpartum hemorrhage, where suture tension may be a critical factor for surgical success. Excessive suture tension (particularly when combined with uterine artery/intra-iliac artery ligation) can compromise uterine blood supply and lead to ischemic necrosis [7–10]. In this case, this risk was effectively mitigated by intraoperative control of suture tension and monitoring of uterine coloration; Infection: Related to repeated intrauterine interventions and prolonged operative time, with a postoperative incision infection rate of 3%–15% following cesarean section; emergency cesarean section, blood transfusion due to hemorrhage, and extended operative duration are major risk factors [1, 11]. In this case, infection was successfully controlled through preoperative prophylactic medication, graded postoperative anti-infective therapy, and adequate drainage; Thrombosis: Can be effectively prevented through combined physical and pharmacological measures; Long-term risks include potential failure of conservative treatment, delayed hysterectomy, or intrauterine adhesions; as this technique does not involve uterine incision, long-term risks are further reduced.

Clinical practice should prioritize life safety as the primary principle. When uterine-preserving therapy for postpartum hemorrhage proves ineffective, hysterectomy should be considered [4–7, 12–14].

4.Uterine Protection and Pelvic Floor & Reproductive Health

Uterine Protection and Pelvic Floor & Reproductive Health

The uterus serves as the core supportive structure of the pelvic floor. Preserving the uterus reduces the risks of pelvic organ prolapse and stress urinary incontinence, which is of significant importance for women’s quality of life and reproductive function, and also lays the foundation for future reproductive reconstruction techniques such as uterine transplantation [15].

Clinical significance

The core management of complex etiologies of refractory postpartum hemorrhage involves precise etiological identification, dynamic adjustment of hemostatic strategies, enhanced complication prevention and control, as well as rational decision-making regarding uterine preservation or hysterectomy. Preoperative screening for high-risk factors, intraoperative identification of bleeding sources, stepwise progression of surgical approaches, and postoperative individualized management can significantly improve treatment success rates while preserving patient fertility. The successful management experience in this case provides valuable practical reference for the clinical management of similar hemorrhagic conditions in obstetrics.

Conclusion

For refractory postpartum hemorrhage caused by complex etiologies during cesarean section, when stepwise hemostatic measures prove ineffective, the modified quadruple uterine band ligation can serve as a safe and effective salvage procedure. Multimodal surgical intervention is crucial for uterine preservation; standardized techniques and precise control of suture tension are essential during operation, with postoperative focus on preventing infections, uterine necrosis, and thrombotic complications. The decision between uterine preservation and hysterectomy should be based on comprehensive evaluation of bleeding status, vital signs, and fertility requirements, with contingency plans established in advance. Postoperative management through multidisciplinary targeted care, imaging monitoring, and dynamic complication follow-up ensures patient safety. This technique is simple to perform and highly effective in hemostasis, demonstrating clinical value, though its long-term efficacy and safety require further validation through multicenter studies.
