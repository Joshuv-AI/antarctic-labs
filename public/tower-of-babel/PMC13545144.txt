The Effect of Food and the Proton Pump Inhibitor Rabeprazole on Binimetinib Pharmacokinetics in Healthy Participants
Authors: Erik Hahn, Joseph Piscitelli, Dustin Huynh, Renae Chavira, Lance Wollenberg, Micaela B Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBinimetinib is a selective and potent MEK1/MEK2 inhibitor approved in combination with encorafenib for patients with BRAF-mutant melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer. Binimetinib is a basic amine with pH-dependent solubility in vitro, indicating that binimetinib may be susceptible to drug–drug interactions when coadministered with a proton pump inhibitor.ObjectiveThe primary objective of this study was to evaluate the effect of a low-fat meal and high-fat meal and, separately, of multiple doses of rabeprazole 20 mg, a proton pump inhibitor, on the pharmacokinetics of a single oral dose of binimetinib 45 mg in healthy subjects.MethodsThe food effect study was an open-label, randomized, three-treatment, three-period, six sequence, crossover study, and the proton pump inhibitor effect study was an open-label, two-period, two-treatment, fixed-sequence study. Both studies were conducted in healthy subjects.ResultsA total of 12 participants were enrolled in the study evaluating the effect of food on binimetinib pharmacokinetics and 15 participants were enrolled in the study evaluating the effect of concomitant proton pump inhibitor use on binimetinib pharmacokinetics. The area under the plasma concentration–time curve from 0 extrapolated to infinity for binimetinib did not change in the presence of either a low-fat meal or high-fat meal compared to in the fasted state. The geometric mean ratio peak plasma concentration increased 29% with a low-fat meal and decreased by 17.2% with a high-fat meal. The area under the plasma concentration–time curve from 0 extrapolated to infinity and maximum concentration geometric mean ratio values for binimetinib in the presence of oral rabeprazole versus binimetinib alone increased by 4% and decreased by 17.4%, respectively. There were no serious adverse events or patient discontinuations in either study.ConclusionsThe results indicate that there were no clinically significant changes in pharmacokinetics or tolerability resulting from meals or proton pump inhibitor use during concomitant administration with binimetinib.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40262-026-01676-8.

Key Points

Changes in binimetinib exposure were minimal in participants regardless of whether they received a meal or were administered a proton pump inhibitor.Although technically classified as a Biopharmaceutics Classification System Class 4 compound, the results of these two clinical studies show that binimetinib behaves more like a Biopharmaceutics Classification System Class 1 drug.No dosing adjustments are necessary for binimetinib in the presence of food or an acid-reducing agent.

Introduction

Binimetinib is an orally bioavailable, selective, and potent inhibitor of the serine-threonine protein kinases MEK1 and MEK2. As an MEK inhibitor, this compound has the potential to benefit patients with advanced cancers by inhibiting the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway. Binimetinib 45 mg orally twice daily (BID), in combination with encorafenib (also known as LGX818), has received marketing approval in several jurisdictions for the treatment of patients with BRAF V600E or V600K mutant melanoma and for non-small cell lung cancer [1]. In addition, binimetinib in combination with encorafenib and cetuximab is approved in Japan for the treatment of unresectable advanced or recurrent colorectal cancer with BRAF V600E mutations progressing after chemotherapy [2]. Binimetinib is currently being investigated as a single agent in combination with multiple antineoplastic agents (chemotherapy and/or targeted agents) in patients with selected advanced or metastatic solid tumors, including melanoma, biliary, colorectal, ovarian, and non-small cell lung cancers [3–6].

Binimetinib is an orally bioavailable, rapidly absorbed (median time to maximum concentration [Tmax] of 1.6 h) drug with at least 50% of the dose being absorbed [1]. Binimetinib is a weak base (pKa = 2.5) with a clogP value of 2.87. It exhibits a pH-dependent solubility profile in vitro, and across the range of physiologically relevant pH values would not be completely soluble in 240 mL of water at the currently labeled dose (45 mg) [7]. Additionally, the cellular permeability of binimetinib was evaluated in vitro by measuring its transit across human epithelial cell lines used for permeability measurements with results showing binimetinib has moderate-to-high permeability. Binimetinib was also determined to be a substrate of both P-glycoprotein and BCRP [1]. The contribution of direct glucuronidation to the clearance of binimetinib was estimated to be 61.2% and represented the majority of the clearance [1]. Following a single radiolabeled dose of binimetinib 45 mg, 62% of the administered dose was recovered in feces (32% unchanged) while 31% was recovered in urine (6.5% unchanged) [8].

Administration of an oral investigational drug product with food may change the bioavailability of the drug by various means, including a delay in gastric emptying, stimulation of bile flow, a change in gastrointestinal pH, a change in luminal metabolism, and a physical or chemical interaction with the dosage form or drug substance [9]. Additionally, many patients with cancer are taking acid-reducing agents (ARAs) such as proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). These agents can cause the solubility and dissolution of compounds that are weak bases to be altered due to elevation of gastric pH [10–12]. Because of the low solubility of binimetinib, a positive food effect may be more likely as food could increase drug exposure. Therefore, it was important to assess these drug–drug interactions (DDIs) and food–drug interactions in order to provide appropriate binimetinib dosing recommendations in these settings.

Rabeprazole was selected as the PPI for this study as it has limited DDI potential both as a victim and as a perpetrator. As a victim, DDI risk is limited by its mechanism of elimination [13]. The major metabolic pathway for rabeprazole is nonenzymatic reduction to a thioether compound [14]. Additionally, among other commercially available PPIs, rabeprazole showed the lowest inhibitory potency on cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2C9, 2C19, and 3A4 compared to other PPIs [15, 16]. The metabolism of binimetinib, while mainly through direct glucuronidation mediated by UGT1A1 and UGT2B7, also has some CYP1A2- and CYP2C19-mediated metabolism. Choosing rabeprazole as the PPI for this study resulted in confidence that the DDI from its effects on stomach acid will be assessed (as intended), without contribution from inhibition of CYP2C19. In addition, a meta-analysis of multiple studies shows that rabeprazole has relatively high anti-secretory potency among PPIs tested [17]. As acid control by PPIs is a sustained effect, PPIs are commonly given daily despite the short elimination half-life (about 2 h or less) [18]. Rabeprazole is primarily dosed at 20 mg once daily for many of its indications (healing of erosive or ulcerative gastroesophageal reflux disease, treatment of symptomatic gastroesophageal reflux disease in adults) [14]. Additionally, the findings from two clinical studies indicate that the rabeprazole dosing regimen utilized in this study will ensure an intragastric pH > 4, thus allowing the evaluation of the maximum effect of gastric acid suppression on binimetinib pharmacokinetics (PK) [19, 20].

To characterize the effect of food or a PPI on the PK of binimetinib, two separate studies were conducted. The purpose of Study 1 (food effect) was to compare the relative bioavailability of binimetinib administered with a low-fat low-calorie meal (LFM) or a high-fat high-calorie meal (HFM) compared to the fasted state (FS). The purpose of Study 2 (PPI effect) was to evaluate the PK of binimetinib with the concomitant use of a PPI, rabeprazole, compared to when no rabeprazole was coadministered. Both studies evaluated a single oral dose of binimetinib 45 mg in healthy participants. The aim was to inform appropriate clinical use and dosage of binimetinib for patients with cancer with regard to food and/or PPI.

Methods

Clinical Study Design

Study 1 was an open-label, randomized, three-treatment, three-period, six sequence, crossover study conducted at a single clinical site (PPD Development, Austin, TX, USA) in the USA to determine the effect of food on the PK of binimetinib in healthy subjects. Study 2 was an open-label, two-period, two-treatment, fixed-sequence study conducted at a single clinical site (Celerion, Lincoln, NE, USA) in the USA to investigate the effect of oral rabeprazole, a PPI, on the PK of binimetinib in healthy subjects. The trial protocols were reviewed and approved by the respective institutional review boards. These studies were conducted in compliance with the ethical principles originating in or derived from the Declaration of Helsinki and in compliance with all International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice Guidelines.

Eligibility of Participants

Eligibility of participants between the two studies differed slightly. Eligible participants for Study 1 were female of non-childbearing potential (post-menopausal or permanently infertile) or male, aged 18–65 years, and with a body mass index ≥ 18 and ≤ 29.9 kg/m2 and a body weight > 50 kg and < 100 kg. Eligible participants for Study 2 were female of non-childbearing potential or male, aged 19–60 years, and with a body mass index ≥ 18 and ≤ 32.0 kg/m2 and a total body weight > 50 kg and < 113.6 kg. Participants must have been medically healthy with no clinically significant medical history, physical examination, laboratory profiles, vital signs, or electrocardiograms (ECGs), as deemed by the principal investigator. Lastly, only continuous nonsmokers who had not used nicotine-containing products for at least 4 weeks (Study 1) and at least 3 months (Study 2) prior to the first dose of the study drug could be enrolled.

Key exclusion criteria for both studies included (1) a history or presence of hypersensitivity or idiosyncratic reaction to the study drugs or related compounds; (2) any condition possibly affecting drug absorption (e.g., malabsorption syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, gastrectomy, gastric bleeding, gastric bypass); (3) the inability to refrain from or anticipate the use of any drug, including prescription and nonprescription medications, including vitamins, PPI, H2-antagonists or antacid preparations, herbal and dietary supplements, or grapefruit/grapefruit-containing products beginning 14 days prior to the first dose of the study drug and throughout the study; and (4) a diet incompatible with the on-study diet, in the opinion of the principal investigator, within the 28 days prior to the first dose of the study drug, and throughout the study.

Study Drug Administration

In Study 1, two participants were randomized into each of the six treatment sequences and underwent three treatment periods, with an 8-day washout period between each period (Table S1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). For the high-fat and low-fat meal treatment arms, participants followed an overnight fast of at least 10 h and were instructed to finish the morning meal in 25 min or less. A 45-mg dose of binimetinib was administered 30 min after the start of the meal with approximately 240 mL of non-carbonated water. No food was allowed for at least 4 h post-dose. Water was allowed as desired except for 1 h before and 1 h after drug administration. For the fasted treatment arm, participants followed an overnight fast of at least 10 h. They were then administered a 45-mg dose of binimetinib on an empty stomach with approximately 240 mL of non-carbonated water. No food was allowed for at least 4 h post-dose. Water was allowed as desired except for 1 h before and 1 h after drug administration.

In Study 2, each subject received a single 45-mg dose of binimetinib in the morning under two different conditions (Treatment Period 1 and 2). Subjects reported to the clinic on day -1 and commenced an overnight fast (10 h prior to study drug administration). On day 1 of Period 1, subjects received a single oral dose of binimetinib 45 mg. Prior to check-out on day 4 of Period 1 (start of Period 2), subjects received rabeprazole (oral 20 mg once daily) after breakfast and were instructed to self-administer rabeprazole at the same dose on each of the next 2 days at home (days 5 and 6) after breakfast at approximately the same time each day. Subjects returned to the clinic on the morning of day 7 (the fourth day of rabeprazole dosing), whereby a standard breakfast was served prior to the rabeprazole dose. Subjects were released from the site and then returned to the clinic on the afternoon of day 7 and commenced an overnight fast. On day 8, approximately 1 h after an oral dose of rabeprazole 20 mg, subjects received a single oral dose of binimetinib 45 mg. No food was allowed for at least 4 h post-dose. Water was allowed as desired except for 1 h before and 1 h after drug administration. Treatment Periods 1 and 2 were separated by a 7-day washout period.

Participants were instructed to swallow the study drug entirely. Crushing, splitting, or chewing of the study drug was prohibited. Participants in both studies were required to remain seated upright for the first 4 h following binimetinib administration, except when they were supine or semi-reclined for study procedures.

Evaluation of Pharmacokinetics (PK) and Safety

Blood samples of approximately 3–4 mL were collected into appropriately labeled tubes containing the anticoagulant dipotassium ethylenediamine tetra-acetic acid (K2-EDTA) at the specified timepoints to measure plasma concentrations of binimetinib. Serial blood samples for PK were collected in Study 1 pre-dose (0 h), 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24, 36, 72, 96, 120, and 144 h post-binimetinib dosing on binimetinib treatment days. For Study 2, blood samples for PK were collected pre-dose (0 h), 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h post-binimetinib dosing on binimetinib treatment days.

Plasma concentrations of binimetinib were determined using the high-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry method validated with respect to accuracy, precision, linearity, sensitivity, and specificity at WuXi AppTec Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The assay met the performance criteria specified in guidance from the US Food and Drug Administration. Binimetinib was extracted from samples using solid-phase extraction. Reconstituted samples were analyzed using an AB Sciex API 4000 liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry system in MRM positive mode using Electro Spray ionization. The lower and upper limits of quantification for the assay were 1.00 and 1000 ng/mL, respectively, using a sample volume of 100 μL. The between-run precision for all levels of quality-control samples was characterized by < 10% coefficient of variation with accuracy and recovery centered on 100%. No analytical interferences or ion suppression effects were observed in the assays. All samples were analyzed within the documented stability period of 246 days. Additionally, there was no significant matrix interference observed from co-administered drugs, and an incurred sample reanalysis demonstrated reproducibility, with ≥93.8% of reanalyzed samples meeting the ±20% acceptance criterion across both studies.

Using Phoenix WinNonlin (Version 6.3; Certara USA Inc., Princeton, NJ, USA), the pharmacokinetic parameters determined for binimetinib using a noncompartmental analysis of the observed plasma concentration–time data included: area under the plasma concentration–time curve from 0 extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf), AUC from 0 to the last concentration timepoint (AUClast), maximum concentration (Cmax), apparent total body clearance, Tmax, volume of distribution during the apparent terminal phase, and apparent first-order terminal elimination half-life. Parameter values of AUCinf, apparent first-order terminal elimination half-life, apparent total body clearance, and volume of distribution during the apparent terminal phase were only reported when a well-characterized terminal phase was observed, defined as one with at least three data points and r2 ≥ 0.8. Maximum concentration and Tmax were estimated from the observed data. The AUClast and AUCinf were calculated using the linear-up log-down method.

The overall safety profile was characterized by laboratory test abnormalities, physical examination, vital signs, ECGs, and adverse events (AEs). Single measurements of blood pressure and heart rate were obtained in both studies, as well as triplicate ECGs, which were measured at screening, prior to, and approximately 1 h post-binimetinib dosing for Study 2. In Study 1, triplicate ECGs were measured at screening, baseline, and approximately 1-h post-binimetinib dosing. Triplicate ECGs were also measured at the end of studies or upon early termination.

Statistics

The sample size was calculated for Study 1 to obtain a target precision of about 20–25% of the 90% confidence interval (CI) for the ratio of geometric means. To assess the food effect in Study 1, log transformed binimetinib pharmacokinetic parameters (AUClast, AUCinf, and Cmax) were analyzed by a linear mixed model with sequence, period, and treatment (HFM, LFM, or FS) as fixed effects, and subject nested in sequence as a random effect. An estimator for the difference between test (HFM or LFM and reference [FS]) and also for the difference between HFM and LFM, including a two-sided 90% CI were obtained based upon the log-transformed observations. The same statistical methods were used for Study 2; however, the linear mixed-effects models only included treatment (binimetinib + rabeprazole 20 mg vs binimetinib alone) as a fixed effect and subject as a random effect. The sample size for Study 2 was based on feasibility and targeted an adequate precision of at least 20% in estimation of treatment differences between binimetinib administered alone and in combination with rabeprazole.

The results of both analyses were back-transformed to obtain the geometric mean ratios (GMRs) and corresponding 90% CIs on the original scale. The lack of effect was to be concluded if the 90% CIs for the GMRs of the natural log-transformed, AUClast, AUCinf, and Cmax of binimetinib fell between 80% and 125%. Treatment differences with regard to Tmax for binimetinib in both studies were provided by the median difference and associated ranges for the comparisons of interest.

Following a single 45-mg oral dose of binimetinib in the fed state (LFM and HFM), the extent of exposure (area under the plasma concentration–time curve [AUC]) to binimetinib did not change compared to a single oral 45-mg dose of binimetinib in the FS. The AUCinf and AUClast were very similar for all three feeding conditions tested and display low-to-moderate variability with coefficients of variation (CV% geo-mean) not exceeding 35%. The rate of absorption of binimetinib when administered with a HFM, LFM, or in the FS was generally similar, with median Tmax of 2.03, 1.25, and 0.875 h, respectively. Geometric mean ratio for binimetinib in the presence of either a LFM or HFM versus binimetinib alone was 1.00 (90% CI 0.935–1.07) and 0.993 (90% CI 0.929–1.06) for AUCinf, respectively. For Cmax, GMR was 1.29 (90% CI 1.10–1.50) with LFM and 0.828 (90% CI 0.713–0.962) with HFM. The Cmax for binimetinib with a HFM decreased by 17% whereas with a LFM, it increased by 29%.

The exposure to binimetinib 45 mg following coadministration with multiple oral once-daily doses of rabeprazole was similar to that measured following binimetinib alone. The rate of absorption of binimetinib was also similar following the co-administration of binimetinib 45 mg with multiple oral once-daily doses of rabeprazole 20 mg compared with that following binimetinib alone, with comparable median Tmax of approximately 1 h and a similar AUC but 17% lower Cmax in the presence of rabeprazole. The GMR for binimetinib in the presence of oral rabeprazole versus binimetinib alone was 1.04 (90% CI 0.930–1.17) for AUCinf and 0.826 (90% CI 0.692–0.984) for Cmax.

Safety

A single oral 45-mg dose of binimetinib was generally safe and well tolerated by the healthy participants enrolled in both studies. In Study 1, AEs were reported by 4 of 12 participants (33.3%, all mild in severity). The most commonly affected primary system organ classes were gastrointestinal disorders (three subjects, 25.0%) and eye disorders (two subjects, 16.7%). Nausea, reported by two participants (16.7%), was the most frequently reported AE. Other AEs occurred in single subjects (each 8.3%). No participants discontinued the study early because of reported AEs. All primary hematology and biochemical variables normalized by the end of Study 1, except for one participant who newly reported a low white blood cell count of Grade 1 at the time of the last laboratory evaluation. In addition, a different participant had blood pressure values indicative of pre-hypertension at baseline and showed persistent Grade 3 systolic blood pressure during the three study periods. Aside from these two participants, no clinically significant treatment-related trends were observed in clinical laboratory, vital sign, ECG, echocardiogram, physical examinations, and ophthalmic examinations for this study.

For Study 2, musculoskeletal pain was reported by 1 of 15 participants (7%) who received binimetinib alone. The reported AE was Grade 1 (mild) in severity and deemed possibly related to the study drug. No other participants reported AEs after receiving rabeprazole and binimetinib. No clinically significant changes in vital signs, laboratory values, or ECG measurements were observed during Study 2, and no participants withdrew from the study because of reported AEs.

Discussion

Comparison with Previous Binimetinib PK

The binimetinib pharmacokinetic profiles observed in participants from Study 1 and Study 2 were generally consistent with previous studies. Fasted state pharmacokinetic parameters from both Study 1 and Study 2 for binimetinib were similar with what was reported for healthy volunteers who participated in the human ADME study [8]. When compared to the single-dose pharmacokinetic parameters calculated from the studies with patients with cancer, Study 1 and Study 2 have slightly higher AUC values and a longer half-life [21, 22]. A shorter, less rich sampling period in cancer studies (eight samples up to 8 h) compared to a longer, more rich sampling period in Study 1 and Study 2 (at least 15 samples up to 72 h) may explain the differences in pharmacokinetic parameters. Some variability was observed in the fasted reference populations between the two studies. This may be attributed to the overall variability of binimetinib PK and slight differences in pharmacokinetic sampling around the Tmax of binimetinib.

Potential Study Limitations

Study 1 and Study 2 were conducted in healthy participants, consistent with current guidance and standard practice for clinical pharmacology studies of oral oncology agents [23, 24]. Healthy participants are the preferred population for these clinical pharmacology assessments because they minimize confounding from comorbidities, concomitant medications, and disease-related variability. This allows for a more precise characterization of the food or PPI effect on drug absorption [25]. The effects of food and gastric pH on oral drug absorption are driven primarily by the physiochemical properties of the drug and gastrointestinal physiology (e.g., gastric emptying, pH, bile salt concentrations) rather than by the underlying disease state. Therefore, the effects observed in healthy volunteers are expected to be comparable to those in patients with cancer. This approach is well established within drug development, as food effect and PPI studies for many approved oral kinase inhibitors (such as dacomitinib and encorafenib) were conducted in healthy volunteers and informed the drug label [11, 12].

To try and ease the burden on the healthy subjects of having to spend more days at the clinical sites, they were instructed to self-administer rabeprazole at home on days 5 and 6 (two total doses), even though this increased the risk for participant non-compliance with the PPI. Although the inhibitory effect of rabeprazole on 24-h stomach acid is 88% of the maximum after the first dose, additional methods to reduce the risk of non-compliance were implemented for this study [14]. Subjects recorded when they took their dose (date and time), and phone calls from the sites were made to subjects reminding them to take the rabeprazole. None of the subjects in Study 2 had to be removed from the pharmacokinetic analysis because of non-compliance of rabeprazole.

Interpretation of Data

Studies 1 and 2 were designed with the intent to assess whether the PK of binimetinib was modified by concomitant intake of food or a gastric pH-altering agent, such as a PPI. Despite a mild effect of food on the Cmax of binimetinib (Table 2), GMR values of AUCs remained unchanged between conditions. Time to maximum concentration was also observed to increase in both fed states (more notably with a HFM). This increase suggests that food delayed binimetinib absorption into systemic circulation, which is thought to be from delayed gastric emptying and increased stomach pH (which occur in the fed state) [26]. These findings show that the effect of food was limited to the absorption rate. The magnitude of changes in Cmax with fed conditions was similar to or smaller than the variability observed in all treatment conditions. Variability of Cmax and AUC was slightly lower with food intake versus the FS. Furthermore, the predicted increase in Cmax under the LFM conditions (29%) is less than the magnitude of change between the labeled dose (45 mg BID) and the maximum tolerated dose (60 mg BID) [33%], supporting the lack of clinical significance from a safety perspective [21]. Taken together, these results support the conclusion that the effect of food on the absorption of binimetinib is not clinically relevant.

Similarly, co-administration of multiple oral once-daily doses of rabeprazole 20 mg had no effect on the overall extent of exposure (AUC) of binimetinib absorption. There was a reduction in Cmax (~17% lower), but binimetinib Tmax was similar between the two treatment arms. Additionally, given that the efficacy of binimetinib is not solely driven by Cmax, the observed changes are not considered clinically relevant [27, 28]. These results indicate that there is no overall effect on the binimetinib extent of exposure when administered with rabeprazole; thus binimetinib can be taken without regard to concomitant PPI use at the approved dose without restriction.

From a mechanistic perspective, the slight decrease in Cmax when binimetinib was given in combination with rabeprazole may be due to the pH-dependent solubility of binimetinib. Binimetinib has a pKa of 2.5 [7]. In simulated gastric fluid, binimetinib showed solubility of 1.15 mg/mL (pH 1.2); however, in simulated intestinal fluid (pH 6.8), solubility was lower (0.023 mg/L) [data on file]. The solubility of binimetinib in the stomach during fasted conditions without rabeprazole coadministration (pH = 1–2) is sufficiently high to drive complete dissolution of the dose (45 mg) and facilitate rapid absorption. The super-saturation of the drug dissolved in the stomach as it moves into the intestines is expected to aid absorption [1, 29–31]. However, in pH values > 4.5 due to ARA administration, binimetinib is expected to have slower dissolution based on its lower solubility characteristics in the higher pH of the stomach, impacting the shape of the concentration–time curve. The effect of the PPI, however, was mild, with no delay of Tmax and a small reduction of Cmax. Ultimately, although Cmax was slightly reduced, the similar AUC values despite PPI administration (and fed state) indicate that the extent of absorption is similar for the FS and fed state and when coadministered with rabeprazole. Additionally, binimetinib demonstrates moderate-to-high permeability. These biopharmaceutics properties support equivalent AUCs across all scenarios (fed, fasted, ARA conditions), suggesting complete absorption for all three scenarios, while demonstrating differing dissolution kinetics that modulate some PK parameters (e.g., Cmax) [7]. Results from the human ADME study for binimetinib supported the conclusion that a lower limit on the extent of absorption for binimetinib is 50%, but absorption is thought to have been much higher (i.e., if the binimetinib and M4 metabolite in the feces were from direct glucuronide conjugates falling apart in the gut to reform binimetinib and then some going on to form the M4 metabolite) [8].

The likelihood of a clinically relevant food or ARA effect depends on the properties of the compound. Drugs with a Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) Class of 2 or 4 are more likely to have a positive food effect compared to those that are 1 or 3, which can sometimes have negative food effects [32, 33]. In vitro evaluations of binimetinib show moderate-to-high permeability and pH-dependent solubility that is low at some pH values, and so technically binimetinib is classified a BCS Class 4 molecule. However, the results of these two clinical studies show that binimetinib behaves more like a BCS Class 1 drug, which are expected to have good absorption because of their high solubility and permeability. Binimetinib behaving more like a BCS Class 1 compound is further supported by the lack of apparent dose-limited absorption in the phase I dose escalation study in oncology patients, as linear PK was observed up to doses as high as 80 mg BID [21].

Conclusions

In summary, two separate studies were conducted to assess the effect of a LFM and HFM on the absorption of binimetinib and the effect of a PPI on the PK of binimetinib. There was no clinically meaningful change in binimetinib exposure in participants when taken in the fed state with either a HFM or LFM or when taken with an PPI. Based on the findings of these studies, binimetinib can be taken in both the fed state or FS at the clinically recommended doses, and no dosing adjustment is needed when binimetinib is coadministered with an ARA [1, 34]. The results of these two studies allow patients to have more flexibility with binimetinib dosing (e.g., no need to schedule their dosing around meals) and allow for patients in need of an ARA the freedom to use any drug class without restriction to timing of administration.
