CNS involvement by EBV-positive extranodal T-cell lymphoma harboring STAT3 Y640F mutation following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation: A case report
Authors: Tausif Ahmed Siddiqui, Mohammed Usman Syed, Zahin Alam, Pavan Kottamasu, Kashfia Nayeem, Akm A. Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Secondary central nervous system involvement by extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma is rare and presents significant diagnostic challenges, particularly in immune compromised patients. We report a case of a 29-year-old man with chronic active Epstein–Barr virus infection and prior extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma status post allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation who presented with a new-onset seizure and a ring-enhancing intracranial lesion. Initial imaging findings closely mimicked cerebral toxoplasmosis, leading to empiric antimicrobial therapy and delayed diagnosis. Despite treatment, the lesion demonstrated rapid progression, prompting stereotactic biopsy, which revealed Epstein–Barr virus-positive extranodal T-cell lymphoma with a pathogenic STAT3 p.Y640F activating mutation. This case highlights the substantial radiographic overlap between central nervous system lymphoma and infectious etiologies in immunosuppressed patients, where conventional imaging modalities may lack specificity. The presence of an aggressive molecular profile, including STAT3 activation, likely contributed to rapid disease progression and poor prognosis. This report underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for lymphoma in patients with progressive intracranial lesions unresponsive to empiric therapy and emphasizes the critical role of early tissue diagnosis to guide appropriate management.

Introduction

Malignant lymphoma involving the central nervous system (CNS) is a rare but clinically significant manifestation of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, occurring either as a primary neoplasm or as secondary involvement in the setting of systemic disease [1,2]. The World Health Organization defines primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL) as primary diffuse large B-cell lymphoma arising in immunocompetent patients, which accounts for fewer than 1% of all NHLs and approximately 2% of brain tumors [1,3]. Approximately 90% of PCNSL cases demonstrate diffuse large B-cell lymphoma histology with an angiocentric growth pattern, while the remaining 10% comprise T-cell lymphomas, Burkitt lymphomas, and other low-grade subtypes [2,3]. Secondary CNS involvement occurs in 5%-29% of systemic NHLs and is often associated with aggressive disease and poor survival [4].

Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL) is an aggressive subtype of peripheral T-cell lymphoma strongly associated with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection [5,6]. It is characterized by distinctive pathological features including angiocentric growth, a cytotoxic immunophenotype, and extensive necrosis [6]. Chronic active EBV disease is defined by persistent EBV infection of T or NK cells and is recognized as a precursor condition that can progress to overt ENKTL or aggressive NK-cell leukemia [7,8]. EBV-encoded oncogenes, such as latent membrane protein 1, activate oncogenic signaling pathways including the JAK-STAT pathway, while somatic mutations in driver genes—including STAT3, DDX3X, and TP53—promote malignant transformation [9,10]. Gain-of-function mutations in STAT3, particularly the Y640F variant, lead to constitutive STAT3 phosphorylation and downstream pro-survival signaling and have been identified in 15%-21% of ENKTL cases [[10], [11], [12]]. CNS involvement by ENKTL is exceedingly rare, occurring in approximately 1.4%-6% of cases, and carries a poor prognosis with a median overall survival of fewer than 10 months [[13], [14], [15]]. EBV-driven lymphoproliferative disorders in the setting of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) may develop due to impaired immune surveillance; however, T-cell post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorders remain exceptionally rare [7,16].

Radiological evaluation of intracranial lesions in immunocompromised patients presents considerable diagnostic challenges. In immunocompetent patients, PCNSL typically demonstrates homogeneous contrast enhancement, moderate peritumoral edema, and restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging in approximately 90% of cases [17,18]. In contrast, CNS lymphoma in immunocompromised patients may present as ring-enhancing lesions with central necrosis, closely mimicking infectious etiologies such as cerebral toxoplasmosis [[17], [18], [19]]. The eccentric target sign on T1-weighted postcontrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and the concentric target sign on T2-weighted sequences are relatively specific for toxoplasmosis; however, CNS lymphoma can mimic these findings, and imaging overlap occurs in 50%-80% of cases [[20], [21], [22]]. Advanced modalities such as 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET), magnetic resonance (MR) spectroscopy, and diffusion-weighted imaging may provide additional discriminatory information; however, no single modality is entirely specific, and histopathological confirmation via stereotactic biopsy remains the gold standard [18,23].

Herein, we report a case of secondary CNS involvement by EBV-positive extranodal T-cell lymphoma harboring a STAT3 Y640F activating mutation in a young man with chronic active EBV following allogeneic HSCT. The intracranial lesion presented as a ring-enhancing mass on MRI that closely mimicked cerebral toxoplasmosis, resulting in empiric antimicrobial therapy and delayed diagnosis. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first reported cases of secondary CNS T-cell lymphoma harboring a STAT3 Y640F mutation following HSCT. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for secondary CNS lymphoma when empiric therapy fails to produce radiographic improvement in immunocompromised patients.

Case report

A 29-year-old man with chronic active EBV infection and extranodal EBV-positive NK/T-cell lymphoma, status post chemotherapy and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, presented with a new-onset generalized tonic–clonic seizure. His posttransplant course had been complicated by prior ischemic strokes and intracranial hemorrhage requiring placement of a right frontal Ommaya reservoir. On presentation, he was postictal without focal neurologic deficits.

Noncontrast head computed tomography (CT) demonstrated subtle hypoattenuation in the anterior left frontal lobe without acute hemorrhage (Fig. 1A). Brain MRI revealed a 1.3 cm ring-enhancing intra-axial lesion in the parasagittal left frontal lobe with surrounding vasogenic edema and mild mass effect (Fig. 1B). No central diffusion restriction was identified on diffusion-weighted imaging/apparent diffusion coefficient sequences. FDG PET/MR demonstrated low metabolic activity within the lesion (Standardized Uptake Valuemax 2.9) with a mildly hypermetabolic peripheral rim (Fig. 1C), favoring an infectious etiology [23]. Cerebrospinal fluid cytology and flow cytometry were negative for malignant cells. Empiric intravenous trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and meropenem were initiated for presumed toxoplasmosis [19].

Follow-up MRI at 3 weeks demonstrated interval enlargement of the lesion with worsening vasogenic edema and increasing mass effect (Fig. 1D). Subsequent imaging showed progression to approximately 4 cm with up to 8 mm of rightward midline shift and effacement of the adjacent frontal horn (Fig. 1E). Given radiographic progression despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, stereotactic biopsy was performed.

Histopathologic evaluation revealed extensive necrosis with infiltration by large atypical lymphoid cells. Immunohistochemistry was positive for CD3, diffuse CD7, CD30, TIA-1, and granzyme B [6,24]. In situ hybridization for Epstein–Barr encoded small RNA was positive, confirming EBV association [5,6]. Tumor cells were negative for CD20 and CD56. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 80%. Molecular analysis identified a pathogenic STAT3 p.Y640F mutation [10,12,25,26]. These findings established a diagnosis of EBV-positive extranodal T-cell lymphoma with secondary CNS involvement.

The patient underwent stereotactic radiosurgery followed by whole-brain radiation therapy [24,27,28]. Interval MRI following whole-brain radiation therapy demonstrated a partial radiographic response, with a slight decrease in lesion size, decreased surrounding vasogenic edema, and reduced mass effect, including improvement of the rightward midline shift from 8 mm to 6 mm (Fig. 1F). However, systemic disease progressed, including development of a malignant right pleural effusion confirmed by flow cytometry to represent gamma-delta T-cell lymphoma. Despite salvage therapy, the patient experienced continued clinical decline and died in October 2024, approximately 10 months after alloHSCT.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this represents one of the first reported cases of secondary CNS involvement by EBV-positive extranodal T-cell lymphoma harboring a STAT3 Y640F activating mutation following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. This case highlights 3 important diagnostic and therapeutic challenges: (1) secondary CNS involvement by EBV-positive T-cell lymphoma following HSCT, (2) radiologic mimicry of cerebral toxoplasmosis resulting in delayed diagnosis, and (3) the presence of an activating STAT3 Y640F mutation associated with aggressive tumor biology.

This patient developed a left frontal lobe lesion following allogeneic HSCT. Initial empiric treatment for cerebral toxoplasmosis was administered; however, interval radiographic progression prompted stereotactic biopsy, which confirmed secondary EBV-positive extranodal T-cell lymphoma with the STAT3 Y640F driver mutation. In the post-alloHSCT setting, EBV latency programs may drive post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder, most commonly of B-cell origin; T-cell post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorders remains exceptionally rare [16]. Despite partial response following stereotactic radiosurgery and whole-brain radiation therapy, this patient experienced systemic disease progression and died approximately 10 months after transplantation. The combination of delayed diagnosis, aggressive molecular profile, and continued systemic progression contributed to the patient's clinical decline.

Cerebral toxoplasmosis presents as T1-hypointense and T2-hyper-to-mixed intensity ring enhancing lesions on MRI. Both CNS lymphoma and cerebral toxoplasmosis may present as ring-enhancing intracranial lesions in immunocompromised patients [19,20]. The eccentric target sign (95% specificity and <30% sensitivity) on T1-post contrast MRI and concentric target sign on T2-sequences may aid in differentiating cerebral toxoplasmosis from CNS lymphoma [[20], [21], [22]]. In this case, the extensive necrosis noted on biopsy likely contributed to the centrally hypointense region with peripheral enhancement resulting in the ring-enhancing appearance mimicking cerebral toxoplasmosis (Fig. 1B). Imaging features of secondary CNS lymphoma (SCNSL) include leptomeningeal, parenchymal, and subependymal involvement, while PCNSL typically exhibit a solid, homogenous MRI enhancement pattern [28,29]. However, imaging features may vary and overlap and differentiating PCNSL from SCNSL is typically not feasible by MRI alone [29]. Further assessment of the patient’s history of systemic lymphoma, PET/CT, and biopsy is typically required to differentiate PCNSL from SCNSL [29].

The tumor’s positivity for CD3 and CD7 confirm a T-cell origin. The absence of CD20 rules out B-cell origin, while the absence of CD56 rules out NK cell origin [6,24,30]. The TIA-1 and granzyme B markers indicate a cytotoxic phenotype, resulting in the extensive necrosis seen on biopsy and the centrally hypodense region on T1 post-contrast imaging, mimicking cerebral toxoplasmosis (Fig. 1B) [31]. Positive in situ hybridization for Epstein-Barr encoded small RNA confirms the tumor is EBV+ [5,6,8]. The Y640F mutation in STAT3 activates the JAK-STAT cell-growth promoting pathway, which contributes to the high Ki-67 proliferation index of 80% and results in aggressive tumor growth [26,30].

Treatment for SCNSL consists of high-dose methotrexate-based chemotherapy with radiation therapy, but overall survival (OS) for PCNSL is 9 months and OS for SCNSL is 4-5 months, indicating poor prognosis even with treatment [28]. The aggressive molecular profile of this patient’s CNS tumor likely contributed to the interval progression upon administration of intravenous trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and meropenem for presumed cerebral toxoplasmosis (Fig. 1D and E) [25,26]. The combination of the delayed treatment owing to the cerebral toxoplasmosis imaging mimic along with the aggressive nature of this SCNSL driven by the STAT3 Y640F oncogenic mutation as well as the pulmonary complications of systemic T-cell lymphoma progression led to this patient’s clinical decline and eventual demise [14,15,32].

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by CNS lesions in immunocompromised patients, particularly when imaging findings mimic opportunistic infections such as cerebral toxoplasmosis. Secondary CNS involvement by EBV-positive T-cell lymphoma is rare and may require tissue biopsy for definitive diagnosis when empiric antimicrobial therapy fails to produce radiographic improvement. Recognition of aggressive molecular alterations such as STAT3 Y640F may provide additional insight into tumor biology and prognosis. Early consideration of lymphoma in the differential diagnosis of progressive intracranial lesions in immunocompromised patients may facilitate timely diagnosis and management.
