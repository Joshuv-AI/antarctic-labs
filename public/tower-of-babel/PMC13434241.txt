The hippocampus becomes topographically and functionally specialized along the longitudinal axis with development
Authors: Jonah Kember, Ying He, Zeus Gracia-Tabuenca, Alexander Barnett, Xiaoqian J. Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human hippocampus exhibits distinct genetic, cellular, and connectivity profiles along its anterior–posterior long-axis, resulting in a differential sensitivity to visuospatial information. Long-axis development, therefore, may contribute to developmental increases in visuospatial memory capacity. To test this, we developed and applied a precision functional mapping technique to identify functional systems in the hippocampus of single subjects using BOLD-fMRI (N = 471, aged 5–21). We discovered considerable developmental remodeling of the hippocampal long-axis, particularly for the posterior functional system. With age, surface-area (mm2) of the posterior system decreased by 39.4%, while BOLD activity became increasingly independent and showed a sharper topographic boundary with the rest of the hippocampus. Notably, the posterior system showed strong preferential connectivity with medial-parietal regions which correlated with both age and age-adjusted memory scores. These results indicate the hippocampal long-axis becomes topographically and functionally specialized with development, potentially contributing to developmental increases in memory capacity.

Introduction

In the human hippocampus, differences in gene-expression, cell-type composition, and anatomical connectivity exist along the anterior–posterior longitudinal axis, promoting differences in neuronal activity and circuit function1–3. In the posterior hippocampus, anatomical, effective and functional connectivity from regions in medial-parietal and occipital cortex promote a specialization towards the processing of salient visuo-spatial information2,4–8. This is in contrast to the anterior hippocampus, which exhibits a suppressed hemodynamic response when processing salient visuo-spatial information, as well as increased anatomical and functional connectivity with default-network regions of the medial-parietal cortex4,5,9. This functional dissociation may help explain why the posterior hippocampus is more strongly related to visuo-spatial forms of memory7, and why maturation of the posterior hippocampus is more strongly related to developmental increases in visuo-spatial memory10,11.

In addition to its role in memory development, maturation of the hippocampal long-axis is disrupted across multiple neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders (e.g., autism-spectrum disorder, schizophrenia), with some evidence indicating a delay in development of the posterior hippocampus specifically12–14. Thus, understanding how the hippocampal long-axis becomes functionally specialized with development may be critical in promoting health and cognitive outcomes in children, and in clinical translation15,16.

The hippocampal long-axis is often segmented heuristically through anatomical landmarks, or automated segmentation protocols trained on these landmarks17–19. However, this approach does not capture the individual variability in the spatial extent and location of functional systems in the brain, which stems from individual differences in genetic and activity-dependent mechanisms (e.g., BOLD-fMRI resting-state networks20). Recently, precision-mapping techniques have been developed that are capable of capturing individual differences in this functional-system topography through within-individual mapping of extended acquisition-time fMRI data. These techniques are unique in that they enable the investigation of spatial topography, including the surface area of cortical tissue dedicated to functional systems at the single-subject level. These measures of spatial topography have demonstrated high sensitivity to individual variation related to disease, and thereby hold great promise for clinical translation21–23 and for understanding individual differences in cognitive abilities during development20,24–26.

We reasoned that a similar technique might be sensitive to individual variation that arises during development of the anterior–posterior functional systems that exist in the hippocampus. To test this, we developed a precision technique to map hippocampal tissue to the posterior, body and anterior systems in single subjects and examined how the topography and functional activity of these systems change with age across a sample of 471 subjects aged 5 to 21 years. In brief, this precision technique involved subdividing a group-average hippocampus into systems based on their connectivity to the neocortex, then mapping out the exact location of these template systems in each subject using their unique functional and anatomical data. We discovered that the functional systems along the hippocampal long-axis undergo considerable remodeling with development in terms of spatial topography and functional activity. Notably, the surface area (mm2) of hippocampal tissue dedicated to a functionally defined posterior system decreases by 39.4% with age, while BOLD-fMRI activity of the region becomes increasingly independent and shows a sharper topographic boundary with the rest of the hippocampus. We also discovered that the posterior hippocampal system shows strong and unique functional connectivity to a network located in the medial-parietal cortex (Yeo-17 control_c network27), particularly a part of this network located in the parieto-occipital sulcus. This region showed near-perfect overlap with area POS2, which prior work indicates has high anatomical and effective connectivity to the posterior hippocampus5,8. Crucially, we found that the preferential connectivity of the posterior relative to the anterior hippocampal system with this region increases with age and memory, and that this pattern of connectivity mediates the relationship between age and memory. These findings represent an important contribution to our understanding of how the hippocampus changes throughout child development.

Results

Precision-functional mapping of the long-axis systems of the hippocampus

To perform precision functional mapping of the hippocampus, we developed a “template-matching” technique, similar to that used in cortical-mapping26, which involved identifying the functional systems that exist in the group-average hippocampus, then mapping these systems onto the hippocampus of each subject using their own anatomical and functional data. An overview of this workflow is shown in Fig. 1, with further details provided in the Methods. First, the hippocampal surface was constructed from the 0.8 mm T1-weighted image, and preprocessed resting-state BOLD signals were projected to each vertex on this surface. These signals were then correlated with those on the neocortical surface to create a group-average [hippocampal-vertex, neocortical-vertex] correlation matrix (shape = 7262 × 32,492 vertices). To identify template systems, the primary spatial patterns of neocortical connectivity across the hippocampus (gradients) were identified through diffusion-map embedding28, and hippocampal vertices were clustered into three systems based on these gradient loadings using a Gaussian mixture model. Once these systems had been identified, their mean neocortical connectivity profiles were used as templates. The templates identified from the group-average primarily divided the hippocampus along the longitudinal axis, resulting in an anterior, body, and posterior system that closely resembled prior tripartite models of functional systems in the hippocampus29. This division was identifiable in all participants and was highly consistent across left and right hemispheres (supplementary Fig. 1). To contextualize the anatomical location of these systems, we inspected their spatial overlap with the ROIs in the FreeSurfer hippocampal atlas17. In brief, we found that the posterior hippocampal system had the highest spatial overlap with the Hippocampal_tail (dice = 0.90), subiculum-body (dice = 0.55), and presubiculum-body (dice = 0.43). The body hippocampal system had the highest spatial overlap with the molecular_layer_HP-body (dice = 0.56), CA1-body (dice = 0.56), and subiculum-body (dice = 0.48). The anterior hippocampal system had the highest spatial overlap with the molecular_layer_HP-head (dice = 0.83), CA1-head (dice = 0.74), and GC-ML-DG-head (.60). Spatial overlap between each hippocampal system and FreeSurfer ROIs is presented in the supplementary material.

Neocortical connectivity differs between functional systems along the hippocampal long axis

After mapping the precise location of the hippocampal systems in each subject, we examined how these systems functionally connected to the neocortex. We correlated the mean BOLD signal of each hippocampal system with the mean BOLD signal of each cortical network in the Yeo-17 parcellation27, which we had individually mapped to each subject’s functional data using a precision template-matching technique. The posterior hippocampal system showed the highest connectivity with the control_c network (mean z-scored FC across subjects: .71 ± .44, Fig. 2A). This network is often referred to as the medial-parietal memory network30; is closely related to the salience network31; and consists primarily of functional regions RSC, POS2, 7Pm, and PCV32. We next tested which patterns of connectivity uniquely characterize each hippocampal system by training one-versus-rest logistic regression classifiers (e.g., posterior versus anterior/body). The posterior hippocampal system was uniquely characterized by increased connectivity with the control_c (odds-ratio = 14.4) followed by the salience network (odds-ratio = 10.7). The body hippocampal system was uniquely characterized by increased connectivity with the default_b network (odds-ratio: 26.1; see supplementary materials for odds-ratios of all hippocampal system/cortical network pairs). To visualize these effects, we examined vertex-wise connectivity of each hippocampal system with the cortex (Fig. 2).

The posterior hippocampal system spatially contracts with development while the anterior hippocampal system expands

We next tested whether the surface area of hippocampal tissue dedicated to each hippocampal system changes with development. We discovered a considerable decrease in the surface area of the hippocampus dedicated to the posterior system (r = −0.48, p = 1e-28; Fig. 3A), indicating a developmental contraction. Indeed, the posterior system went from occupying 650 ± 200 mm2 in subjects under 10 years of age, to 394 ± 125 mm2 in subjects over 18, a 39.4% decrease. Concomitantly, we observed an increase in the surface area of the hippocampus dedicated to the anterior system with age (r = 0.31, p = 1e-12; Fig. 3B), indicating a developmental expansion. We additionally tested this effect in an independent set of typically developing children from the Healthy Brain Network (HBN) dataset (N = 95; 13.5 ± 5 min of 2.5 mm resolution BOLD-fMRI). We treated the HBN data as a completely independent test set, with the templates used in mapping hippocampal systems being those identified in the HCP-D. We found that this effect replicated, despite a reduced spatial resolution and a lower number of minutes. Specifically, there was a significant decrease in the surface area of the posterior system with age (r = −0.25, p = 0.02; N = 95), as well as significant increases in surface area of both the anterior (r = 0.21, p = 0.04) and body (r = 0.23, p = 0.03) systems with age.

To anatomically contextualize this finding, we examined how the anatomical location of each hippocampal system differed with age. In general, we found evidence that each hippocampal system exhibited an anatomical anterior-to-posterior shift, with the posterior system anatomically retracting into the hippocampal-tail, and the body/anterior systems expanding into more posterior regions. Indeed, with age, the posterior hippocampal system increased in %-overlap with the anatomical ROI in the tail (Hippocampal_tail: r = 0.23, p = 5e-7) and primarily decreased in %-overlap with ROIs in the body (e.g., CA1-body: r = −0.30, p = 2e-11). The body system decreased in %-overlap with anatomical ROIs in the head (e.g., CA1-head: r = −0.20, p = 1e-5). While the anterior system primarily increased in %-overlap with anatomical ROIs in the body and tail (e.g., CA1-body: r = 0.18, p = 1e-4, Hippocampal_tail: r = 0.14, p = 5e-3). All correlations are provided in the Supplementary material.

The posterior hippocampal system becomes functionally specialized with development

To further understand the development of hippocampal long-axis systems, we tested for age-related changes in functional specialization, which we examined through two related yet distinct metrics: segregation and boundary-sharpness. In brief, segregation quantifies how dissimilar BOLD activity in each hippocampal system is with BOLD activity in the rest of the hippocampus, while boundary-sharpness quantifies the degree to which the boundaries of a given system can be clearly delineated (i.e., show independent BOLD activity on either side of the system boundary). Both of these functional tissue properties are driven in part by activity-dependent refinement of neuronal circuits.

Independent of age, the posterior hippocampal system was the most segregated system (segregation: d = 2.33 ± 0.25), showing significantly stronger segregation than the body (t(470) = 9.56, p < 1e-20, d = 0.58) and anterior systems (t(470) = 7.71, p < 1e-13, d = 0.55). With age, we discovered that the posterior hippocampal system also became increasingly segregated from the rest of the hippocampus (r = 0.48, p < 2e-28; shown in Fig. 4A). The body showed a significant, albeit weaker effect with age (r = 0.13, p = 0.004), while the anteriorhippocampus showed a slight decrease in segregation with age (r = −0.15, p = 0.001). A similar pattern emerged when inspecting boundary sharpness. Independent of age, the posterior hippocampal system showed the sharpest boundary (d = 3.35 ± 0.77), significantly sharper than both the body (t(470) = 7.39, p = 6.6e-13), and anterior systems (t(470) = 11.34, p < 1.5e-26). With age, the boundary of the posterior system increased in sharpness (r = 0.27, p < 1e-9, Fig. 4B), along with the boundary sharpness of the body system (r = 0.24, p < 1e-8). In contrast, the boundary sharpness of the anterior system did not change with age (r = 0.04, p = 0.34). Our measures of topographic- and functional-specialization (surface-area, segregation, and boundary-sharpness) are collinear with one another. We therefore assessed whether they accounted for unique variance in age by using them as features in a linear regression predicting age. Indeed, we found that each feature was a significant predictor (surface_area: p < 2.6e-6, boundary-sharpness: p = 0.006; segregation p = 0.007), with the full model accounting for 27% of the variance in age (R2 = 0.27, p < 1e-16). This indicates that surface area, segregation, and boundary sharpness of the posterior hippocampal system show developmental effects that are significantly independent of one another.

Posterior hippocampal system shows increased connectivity to the control-c/medial parietal network with age and memory

Our above analysis revealed that the posterior hippocampal system shows high and specific connectivity with the control_c/medial-parietal and salience networks (Fig. 2A). We therefore examined whether connectivity between the posterior hippocampal system and control_c/salience networks increased with development, and whether this connectivity became more specific to the posterior hippocampal system, relative to the rest of the hippocampus, with development. Given the control-c/medial-parietal network has been previously implicated in memory processes8,30,33, we also tested for correlations with memory capacity using scores on the NIH Picture-Sequence memory test, which a subset of subjects had completed (N = 346)34,35. The false-discovery rate of these tests was controlled for at FDR-p = 0.05 (12 tests: 3 behavioral measures [age, raw-memory, age-adjusted memory], 2 networks [control_c, salience], 2 contrasts [posterior FC, posterior versus rest FC]).

We found that higher connectivity between the posterior hippocampal system and the control_c network was associated with higher memory scores (Picture-sequence raw-scores: r = 0.13, FDR-p = 0.03) and higher age-adjusted memory scores (Picture-sequence age-adjusted scores: r = 0.13, FDR-p = 0.03). The relative connectivity of the posterior hippocampal system to the control_c network when compared to the rest of the hippocampus (e.g., the difference in connectivity of the posterior-system versus the mean of the anterior and body systems) increased with age (r = 0.18, FDR-p = 0.009), memory (r = 0.14, FDR-p = 0.004), and age-adjusted memory (r = 0.12, p = FDR-p = 0.03), while the absolute connectivity between the posterior hippocampal system and the control_c network remained stable with age (r = 0.03, FDR-p = 0.56). For the salience-network, we found that posterior hippocampal connectivity showed a marginally significant correlation with age (r = 0.09, FDR-p = 0.07), and a significant correlation with raw memory scores (r = 0.14, FDR-p = 0.02), although this did not reach significance in its correlation with age-adjusted memory scores (r = 0.09, FDR-p = 0.12).

To better understand this effect, whereby preferential connectivity of the posterior hippocampal system to the control_c network increased with age and was associated with higher memory scores, we conducted post-hoc tests examining the four regions with high spatial overlap of the control_c network: POS2 (located in the parieto-occipital sulcus), 7Pm (located in the medial-parietal portion of Brodmann area 7), RSC (located in the retrosplenial cortex), and PCV (Precuneus visual area). We also conducted post-hoc tests correlating memory scores with connectivity of each hippocampal system to the control_c network. We found that this effect was primarily driven by increased connectivity of region POS2 to the posterior relative to anterior hippocampal systems (age: r = 0.15, p = 8e-04; memory: r = 0.17, p = 2e-03; age-adjusted memory: r = 0.17, p = 2e-03), which can be seen in Fig. 5B. This was attributable to the increased control_c network connectivity of the posterior system, as reported above (r = 0.13, p = 0.03), as well as a marginally significant decrease in control_c network connectivity of the anterior system (r = −0.09, p = 0.073). Together, these findings indicate that connectivity of the posterior hippocampal system to the control_c network is related to memory capacity. Moreover, they indicate that the degree to which hippocampal connectivity with the control_c network is specific to the posterior hippocampal system (i.e., a relative increase in connectivity of the posterior-hippocampus relative to the anterior/body-hippocampus) is correlated with both age and age-adjusted memory scores.

Moreover, a mediation model revealed that this measure of posterior-hippocampal connectivity significantly mediated the relationship between age and age-adjusted memory scores (indirect effect: β = 0.092, p = 0.012). This effect was also notable given that our results in Fig. 2A (showing cortex-wide connectivity of the posterior hippocampal system) exhibited a near-perfect spatial overlap with region POS2, which we have highlighted in Fig. 5A. We also found that region RSC showed a similar, although slightly weaker relationship with age (r = 0.12, p = 0.01) and memory (r = 0.11, p = 0.039) that did not reach nominal significance for age-adjusted memory (r = 0.10, p = 0.058). Region 7Pm showed a decrease with age (r = −0.11, p = 0.01) and a marginally significant decrease with memory (r = −0.10, p = 0.05). Region PCV showed a significant negative correlation with age, but not raw (r = −0.06, p = 0.20) or age-adjusted memory scores (r = −0.01, p = 0.79).

Discussion

Here, we developed and applied a technique to perform within-individual precision mapping of the functional systems in the hippocampus. We discovered that the hippocampal long axis undergoes considerable spatial remodeling throughout childhood and adolescent development. The posterior functional system exhibits a 39.4% decrease in surface area from early/mid-childhood (ages 5-10) to late adolescence (ages 18–21); while the anterior functional system expands at a similar rate, becoming anatomically concentrated in more posterior regions. This topographic reorganization indicates the hippocampal long axis may be becoming more functionally specialized during development. This specialization is further supported by our finding of an increase in the independence of hemodynamic activity within the posterior hippocampus, as well as an increase in the sharpness of the boundary dividing the posterior hippocampus from the rest of the hippocampal formation during development.

We also found that the posterior hippocampus shows strong and unique connectivity with a well-known network located in the medial parietal cortex (control-c from Yeo 17, also referred to as the medial-parietal memory network4,9,23,24,31). Moreover, the strength of this connectivity increased with both age and age-adjusted measures of episodic memory, indicating it may play a mediating role in the development of children’s memory capacity. Together, these changes in topography, local hemodynamic activity, and network integration indicate that the posterior hippocampus is becoming functionally specialized during development, and that these functional changes may have consequences for memory capacity.

We also found connectivity between the posterior hippocampal system and control_c network was the strongest for a specific area in the parieto-occipital sulcus (POS2) previously identified by Glasser et al. (2016) in their multimodal parcellation of the neocortex32. Notably, the connectivity profile of the posterior hippocampal system showed near-perfect overlap with this region (Fig. 5A). This aligns with prior work examining the structural and functional connectivity of the posterior hippocampus in adults. Indeed, non-invasive tract-tracing in humans indicates the posterior hippocampus has strong and preferential anatomical connectivity with the same areas in the parieto-occipital sulcus and retrosplenial cortex (Bonferroni-corrected significance for POS2 and RSC)5. Similarly, BOLD-fMRI in adults shows the posterior third of the hippocampus has strong and preferential functional connectivity to both POS2 and RSC (Zheng et al., 2021; see supplementary material in Angeli et al., 2025 for clear connectivity of the posterior hippocampus with areas POS2 and RSC at the individual level)4,9.

We found that increases in connectivity between the posterior hippocampus and medial-parietal/control-c network mediate age-related increases in memory capacity. Prior studies investigating the mechanisms that underlie hippocampal-dependent forms of memory through transcranial magnetic stimulation have shown that causally increasing connectivity between the hippocampus and medial-parietal regions by targeting lateral parietal cortex, a site which is amenable to perturbation non-invasively, directly increases memory performance33,36,37. Crucially, these memory increases are mediated by connectivity between the hippocampus and RSC36. While the mechanistic pathway is unclear, recent work indicates the control-c/medial-parietal network is highly sensitive to salient/novel/unexpected stimuli4,30. With this in mind, one possible explanation for our findings is that developmental increases in connectivity to medial-parietal regions increase the sensitivity of the posterior hippocampus to salient visuo-spatial information, which in turn may facilitate visual forms of memory8.

We opted to examine a level of analysis where the hippocampus is subdivided into three parts along the anterior–posterior axis, into a head, body, and tail. This was motivated by several factors. First, prior work in both rodents and humans indicates the tripartite division is an appropriate level of analysis: in rodents, sharp boundaries between gene-expression domains divide the hippocampus into dorsal, intermediate, and ventral parts; in humans, 3-cluster divisions of the hippocampus based on functional connectivity result in a clear head, body, and tail parcellation3,29. Second, further subdivisions of the hippocampus are much less commonly examined in prior work, and would therefore be more exploratory and integrate less readily with previous findings29. Finally, it is unclear whether further subdivisions of the long-axis can be reliably captured at the single-subject level without further spatial resolution (e.g., ≥ 7 T MR field strength) or enhanced SNR (e.g., ≥ 1 h scan-times). Indeed, while the posterior, body, and anterior systems could be identified in every subject we examined, we found in testing that this was not the case for divisions of the hippocampus into four systems or more. Furthermore, with this division of the hippocampus into three systems, we found that the posterior system was most distinct at the template level, while the anterior and body systems showed higher relative similarity. This could help explain why, on average across subjects, the functional segregation and boundary-sharpness of the anterior and body systems tended to be lower than that of the posterior system. Indeed, if we were to treat the anterior and body as a single system (which might be reasonable given the similarity of their connectivity profiles relative to the posterior hippocampus), we could conclude that the anterior/body system becomes functionally segregated, as opposed to simply the posterior system becoming segregated. To more precisely test the specificity of this developmental specialization of function to the posterior system, future work may benefit from utilizing task data capable of selectively evoking BOLD activity in the posterior hippocampus (i.e., an oddball detection task4).

Altogether, our analyses show that the posterior hippocampus becomes an increasingly specialized functional region over the course of child and adolescent development, segregating from the rest of the hippocampal formation in terms of its spatial topography and functional activity. This specialization co-occurs with increased integration of the posterior hippocampus into a circuit involving regions in the posterior medial-parietal cortex– regions which are directly involved in processing and storing visuo-spatial information into memory. These results, therefore, help explain how developmental changes in the neurobiology of the hippocampus relate to developmental increases in memory capacity.

Methods

Participants

Data were obtained from the development cohort of the Human Connectome Project (HCP-D), which we accessed after obtaining an NIMH data-archive data-use certificate from the McGill University Research Ethics Board. Informed consent was obtained during data collection. Subjects were all healthy and were subjected to extensive exclusion criteria that included prior head injuries, psychiatric illness, the reception of special services at school, and more (see Somerville et al. for full exclusion criteria)38. Analyses were conducted on 471 subjects (230 Males) aged 5-21 years (mean = 14.9 ± 4.0), who were acquired from four different neuroimaging sites: Harvard (N = 152), UCLA (N = 95), UMinn (N = 116), and WashU (N = 108)38.

MRI data

Structural and functional imaging data were acquired with a Siemens 3 T Prisma scanner with a Siemens 32-channel Prisma head coil. T1-weighted and T2-weighted images were collected with 0.8 mm isotropic voxels (sagittal FOV = 256 × 240 × 166 mm, matrix size = 320 × 300 × 208 slices, slice oversampling of 7.7%, 2-fold in-place acceleration in phase encode direction, bandwidth = 744 Hz/pixel). The T1-weighted image had the following parameters: TR/TI = 2500/1000, TE = 1.8/3.6/5.4/7.2 ms, flip angle = 8 degrees, and up to 30 TRs. The T2-weighted image had the following parameters: TR/TE = 3200/564 ms, turbo-factor = 314, and up to 25 TRs for motion-induced reacquisition.

Resting-state fMRI time-series were obtained from the NIMH Data Archive. A total of 26 min of 2.0 mm resting-state fMRI (four 6.5-min concatenated runs) were collected for subjects over 8 years old, while 21 min were collected (six 3.5-min concatenated runs) for subjects under 8 years old. These data were cleaned with the HCP preprocessed-recommended pipeline: fMRIVolume, which performs volume alignment, removes spatial distortions, and registers data to the structural image with minimal smoothing due to interpolation (no overt smoothing); spatial-ICA [sICA + FIX], which regresses out ICA components related to subject motion, cardiac-related artifacts, and scanner-related artifacts; and, for surface time-series, fMRISurface, which maps gray-matter voxels to the cortical ribbon and registers them to fs_LR 32k space32,39.

Precision mapping of hippocampal functional systems

A schematic of the pipeline we used to perform precision mapping of the hippocampus is shown in Fig. 1. First, the archicortical ribbon of the hippocampus was estimated in each subject using HippUnfold with T1-weighted and T2-weighted images6. This outputs a mid-thickness surface file of the hippocampal body (Subiculum, CA1, CA2, CA3, CA4) with 0.5 mm vertex-spacing in both native-space and unfolded-space. Preprocessed resting-state BOLD signals were then projected to each vertex on the mid-thickness file using trilinear interpolation (“wb_command -volume-to-surface-mapping -trilinear”) and smoothed with a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum of 2 mm (“wb_command -metric_smoothing -fwhm 2”).

Next, BOLD signals at each hippocampal vertex were correlated with BOLD signals at each vertex on the neocortical surface to create a [hippocampal-vertex, neocortical-vertex] correlation matrix (shape = 7262 × 32,492 vertices) for each subject. A group-average [hippocampal-vertex, neocortical-vertex] correlation matrix was then created by averaging across subjects. To reduce the dimensionality of this group-average [hippocampal-vertex, neocortical-vertex] connectivity matrix to consist solely of the most prominent spatial patterns that exist within the hippocampus, we identified the first 100 functional gradients using BrainSpace for Python28. This involved performing diffusion map embedding of a [hippocampal vertex, hippocampal_vertex] affinity matrix, which was created with a “normalized_angle” kernel. This kernel was selected based on prior work investigating hippocampal functional gradients3,28,40. These gradients, shown in Fig. 1, were remarkably similar to the gradients identified in prior work (see Fig. 2 in Xie et al.; see Fig. 2 in Katsumi et al.; see Fig. 1 in Nordin et al.)7,40,41.

Hippocampal vertices were then clustered into distinct functional systems based on their loadings onto the first 100 functional gradients, which are sorted by eigenvalue. This was done with a Gaussian mixture model (n_mixtures=3), fitted in scikit-learn v1.6.142. We found that the first two gradients clearly separated the posterior, body and anterior hippocampus. While we included the first 100 gradients in clustering for completeness, we found in testing that the inclusion of more than ~5 gradients had little to no effect on the clustering outcome. The resulting clusters were manually annotated as posterior, body, and anterior based on their anatomical location.

Once these systems had been identified, their mean neocortical connectivity profile was calculated and taken as templates (3 templates, each a neocortical-vertex vector of length [32492]). These templates were then used in the precision-mapping of each individual subject’s functional data. Specifically, for each subject, the neocortical connectivity profile of each vertex in the hippocampus was correlated with each system’s template, and that hippocampal vertex was assigned to the system with which it was maximally correlated. Compared to other leading precision-mapping techniques (e.g., infomap, non-negative matrix factorization), this “template-matching” variant has been shown to produce solutions that are more reliable and require less scan-time26. This pipeline was implemented completely separately for right and left hemisphere data.

Quality control

All subjects in the HCP-D Release 2.0 were initially included in analyses (N = 652). Three stringent quality-control procedures were applied (described in greater detail below), resulting in the removal of 181 subjects. Subjects were removed if: (1) they had fewer than 10 min of clean low-motion fMRI data (TRs were defined as low-motion if their framewise displacement was ≤0.25 mm), (2) the reconstruction of their hippocampal surface was low-quality, (indexed by a Dice coefficient less than 0.7, as recommended by HippUnfold), or (3) the interpolation of their fMRI data to the reconstructed hippocampal surface was low-quality (≥20mm2 on the hippocampal surface was not properly interpolated, which resulted when HippUnfold surfaces did not sufficiently overlap with the gray-matter voxels defined by the preprocessed fMRI). Together, quality control criteria resulted in a total of 471 subjects entering analyses with an average of 21.78 ± 3.67 low-motion minutes. Of these subjects, 436 had at least 15 min or more of low-motion data.

Neocortical connectivity of the posterior hippocampus

For each subject, vertex-wise neocortical connectivity of the posterior hippocampal system was calculated by correlating the mean BOLD signal of vertices within each subject’s posterior hippocampus with the BOLD signals at each vertex on the neocortical surface. These surface maps were then z-scored to remove individual variation related to the strength of signal correlation (which can be influenced by noise) and instead capture the distribution of connectivity across the cortex. To quantify the mean connectivity of each subject’s posterior hippocampus system with each of the Yeo-17 networks, we took the average connectivity of vertices within each subject’s individually defined Yeo network parcellation27. These were created using a template-matching approach, where the spatial co-colocalization of each vertex’s connectivity profile (thresholded at 95%) was assigned to the Yeo network with which it showed the highest spatial overlap (measured via the Dice coefficient; for code, see: https://github.com/JonahKember/precision_mapping).

We trained a one-versus-rest logistic regression model to classify the posterior hippocampus from the body and anterior hippocampus using scikit-learn. The coefficients of the classifiers were converted to odds ratios, which are easily interpretable. For example, if an unlabeled hippocampal system shows a one standard-deviation increase in connectivity with the “control_c” network, that hippocampal cluster is 18.1 times more likely to be classified as “posterior” relative to either “anterior”, or “body.”

To measure connectivity between the posterior hippocampus and the HCP-MMP-1.0 ROIs within the control_c network (POS2, 7Pm, RSC), we took the mean BOLD signal of vertices within each ROI and correlated (Pearson’s r) these with the mean BOLD signal of vertices within each subject’s posterior hippocampus (Python package pingouin)43. Correlations with memory scores used age-adjusted and raw scores from the National Institute of Health’s Picture-Sequence memory test, a clinical measure of episodic memory function with good test-retest reliability44.

Mediation analyses were conducted with the Python package pingouin43, which implements a bias-correct, non-parametric bootstrap method (see: [https://pingouin-stats.org/build/html/generated/pingouin.mediation_analysis.html] for details).

Quantifying topographic and functional specialization

To quantify the surface area of a hippocampal system, the surface area of each vertex within the hippocampal system, output from HippUnfold, was summed. This was divided by the sum of all vertex surface areas in calculations involving the portion of the hippocampus.

To quantify functional segregation, we correlated the BOLD activity of all vertices in the hippocampus with the average BOLD signal in the posterior hippocampus, then compared the distribution of vertices belonging to the posterior hippocampus with the distribution of vertices not belonging to the posterior hippocampus using an effect-size measure (Cohen’s d), calculated as the mean difference normalized by the pooled standard deviation. This measure was selected as opposed to related metrics (e.g., t-statistics) because it is independent of the number of observations and therefore not directly structurally collinear with surface area (see Fig. 4 for an example).

To quantify boundary sharpness, we first created a vertex-wise map correlating all vertices in the hippocampus with the mean BOLD signal of the posterior hippocampus, identical to segregation. Next, we identified each vertex that exists on the boundary of the posterior hippocampus. For each of these boundary vertices, we extracted all the correlation values of vertices that fall within ± 4.0 mm of this border-vertex, then took the Cohen’s d effect size of correlations within the posterior hippocampus and not within the anterior hippocampus. Note that this is identical to our measure of segregation, localized to a specific vertex on the boundary. We then averaged these measures across all border-vertices. This defines the boundary sharpness and can be interpreted as the average effect-size difference of correlations on each side of a border.

Replication across data acquisition sites and datasets

To assess the replicability of our results across sites (Harvard, UCLA, UMinn, WashU), we used a leave-one-site-out strategy where we re-ran analyses after removing an acquisition site. We fully replicated our results, indicating the posterior hippocampus becomes topographically and functionally specialized with age. Correlations between age and: surface-area (SA), segregation (S), and boundary sharpness (BS) were as follows: removal of WashU (SA: r = −0.48, p < 1e-22; S: r = 0.48, p < 1e-23; BS: r = 0.25, p < 1e-6), removal of Harvard (SA: r = −0.49, p < 1e-20; S: r = 0.48, p < 1e-20, BS: r = 0.28, p < 1e-7), removal of UCLA (SA: r = −0.47, p < 1e-22, S: r = 0.48, p < 1e-23, BS: r = .26, p < 1e-7), removal of UMinn (SA: r = −0.48, p < 1e-22, S: r = 0.45, p < 1e-17, BS: r = 0.30, p < 1e-9). All effects were highly significant after the removal of any given acquisition site.

We also replicated our result that connectivity to the control_c network becomes more specific to the posterior hippocampus versus the rest of the hippocampus with memory. Correlations with memory and age-corrected memory remained significant after the removal of all "four sites: removal of WashU (raw-memory: r = 0.20, p = 0.001; age-adjusted memory: r = 0.18, p = 0.002); removal of UCLA (raw-memory: r = 0.13, p = 0.034, age-adjusted memory: r = 0.15, p = 0.01); removal of Harvard (raw-memory: r = 0.20, p = 0.003, age-adjusted memory: r = 0.24, p < 1e-4); removal of UMinn (raw-memory: r = 0.22, p < 1e-4, age-adjusted memory: r = 0.22, p < 1e-4).

We partially replicated our results that connectivity to the control_c network becomes more specific to the posterior hippocampus versus the rest of the hippocampus with age: results were significant after the removal of Harvard (r = 0.16, p = 0.005) and UCLA (r = 0.10, p = 0.04), but not after the removal of WashU (r = 0.06, p = 0.23) or UMinn (r = 0.04, p = 0.36).

To assess the replicability of our analyses, we conducted these analyses in a set of typically developing children in the Healthy Brain Network (HBN) dataset for which multiple resting-state/movie fMRI runs had been acquired (see Supplementary methods for processing details). We treated the HBN data as a completely independent test set: the templates used in mapping hippocampal systems were those identified in the HCP-D. We found a significant negative correlation between posterior-system surface area and age in the HBN dataset (r = −0.25, p = 0.02; N = 95), replicating our primary result. Similarly, we found positive correlations between age and surface area for both the anterior (r = 0.21, p = 0.04) and body (r = 0.23, p = 0.03) systems. However, the age effects in functional segregation and boundary-sharpness of the posterior hippocampal system were not significant (segregation: r = −0.13, p = 0.22; sharpness: r = −0.01, p = 0.86). The absolute values of segregation in the posterior hippocampus were considerably lower than those in the HCP-D (HBN: d = 1.36 ± .30; HCP-D: 3.35 ± .77), perhaps indicative of a reduced signal-to-noise. The lack of replication with respect to these effects may be due to the reduced amount of fMRI data (HBN: 13 ± 5 min; HCP-D: 22 ± 4 min), the reduced spatial resolution (HBN: 2.5 mm; HCP-D 2.0 mm), or the nature of the task (HBN consists of both movie-watching and resting-state runs).

Sensitivity analyses

We tested whether our primary results were dependent on sex or head-motion (amount of clean data, defined as the number of TRs with a framewise-displacement <0.25 mm). We did not find any of our primary analyses to be dependent on these confounds. Specifically, controlling for sex and head-motion did not impact: the correlation between posterior system surface-area and age (controlling for sex: r = −0.40, p < 1e-10, controlling for head motion: r = −0.40, p < 1e-10); the correlation between posterior system segregation and age (controlling for sex: r = 0.48, p < 1e-10, controlling for head motion: r = 0.40, p < 1e-10); the correlation between posterior system boundary-sharpness and age (controlling for sex: r = 0.27, p < 1e-9, controlling for head motion: r = 0.25, p < 1e-8); the correlation between age-adjusted memory scores and posterior-control_c connectivity (controlling for sex: r = 0.15, p < 1e3, controlling for head motion: r = 0.11, p = 0.01).

Our technique for parcellating the hippocampus into functional systems is dependent on hippocampal-cortical connectivity. Functional specialization of the posterior hippocampus may therefore be driven by the differentiation of the cortical systems with which it is coupled. To test whether the topographic segregation of the posterior hippocampus with age was sensitive to this cortical differentiation, we quantified the degree to which posterior, body, and anterior-coupled cortical regions were differentiated from the rest of the cortex through the segregation index [(within-FC/between-FC)/within-FC], then tested whether individual variation in this differentiation explained differences in the surface area of the posterior hippocampal system. Overall, we found that each system showed a significant increase in segregation with age (anterior: r = 0.32, p = 1e-13; body: r = 0.33, p = 1e-13; posterior: r = 0.35, p = 1e15). However, we found that including measures of cortical-system differentiation as covariates in a model predicting age from hippocampal system surface area from age did not modify the significance of our reported effects. Specifically, for the model predicting age from posterior-system surface area with cortical system segregation as covariates, the regression statistics were: surface-area: β = −0.40, p = 1e-17; anterior-segregation: β = 0.74, p = 0.08; body- segregation: β= −0.75, p = 0.12; posterior- segregation: β = 0.19, p = 0.12. For the model predicting age from anterior-system surface area with cortical system segregation as covariates, the regression statistics were: surface-area: β = 0.35, p = 1e-15; anterior-segregation: β = 0.53, p = 0.22; body- segregation: β = −0.22, p = 0.65; posterior- segregation: β = 0.07, p = 0.57.
