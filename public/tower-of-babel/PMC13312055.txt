Global, Regional, and National Burden of Neck Pain Among Adolescents and Young Adults, 1990–2021, and Projection to 2035: A Trend and Cross-Country Inequality Study
Authors: Fei Wang, Hao Lu, Yu Cao, Dilu Li, Lingjie Cao, Hanqing Zhang, Zhiming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNeck pain (NP) as a common public disease results in significant suffering, handicap, and financial burden. We aim to investigate the trends as well as cross-country disparities of NP burden worldwide among adolescents and young adults (aged 15–39 years).MethodsData of NP were gained from the Global Burden of Diseases Study (GBD) 2021. We investigated trends of NP burden in 1990–2021 with global, regional, and national levels, and explored the NP burden by sociodemographic index (SDI), age distribution, and gender differences. Additionally, we assessed cross-country inequality and projected trends of NP burden through 2035.ResultsAccording to GBD 2021, the worldwide NP burden among adolescents and young adults showed an ascending trend, with a continuously high global period prevalence from 1990 to 2021. South Asia reported the maximal number of prevalent, incident, as well as disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) cases, meanwhile Tropical Latin America presented the highest age-standardized rates (ASR) of prevalence and DALYs, while North Africa and the Middle East reported the highest ASR of incidence. Decomposition analysis indicated that women accounted for 57.68% of DALYs cases of NP, while the Middle-SDI quintile contributed 32.57% DALYs cases. Projections suggest a continuous increase for case number in 2022–2035, while a slight rise is expected in most age groups for rate, except for a predicted decrease in the 35–39 age group.ConclusionOverall, NP global burden has demonstrated an upward trend in 1990–2021 and SDI-related inequalities worsening with time. This research underscores an increasing number of cases and distributive inequalities globally. These discoveries can provide an valuable insights for improving public health policies.Keywords: neck pain, incidence, age-period-cohort, health inequalities, adolescents and young adults

Introduction

Neck pain (NP) is a prevalent and clinically heterogeneous musculoskeletal disorder that results in significant pain, progressive disability, and substantial economic costs globally, ranking among the leading causes of activity limitation and years lived with disability.1 Within the GBD framework, NP is defined as pain in the cervical region with or without referred pain in the arms, lasting for at least one day.2 In 2021, the global burden included roughly 62.8 million existing cases and 15.4 million new cases of incidence, resulting in considerable health and economic impacts, including direct and indirect costs, as well as intangible factors like fatigue and reduced social engagement.3 With the global population’s growth and the increasing use of smartphones and participation in work-related and sports activities, there is a pressing need to address the NP burden for adolescents and young adults. Focusing on the 15–39 age group is particularly crucial, as these represent the prime educational and working years, during which NP significantly impairs productivity and establishes chronic trajectories.

The disease’s high burden is often linked to socio-demographic challenges, limited access to healthcare, and inadequate system performance.4 In contrast, high-income countries also face substantial healthcare costs due to NP. Epidemiological reports have typically characterized NP based on common clinical features such as pressing pain and activity limitations.5 However, it is crucial to recognize NP as a clinically heterogeneous condition. Projections suggest that NP could become one of the most prevalent disorders among adolescents and young adults in high-income nations in the coming decades, largely driven by an increase in sedentary lifestyles, prolonged screen time, and occupational ergonomic hazards. The global demographic increase and changes in epidemiology have influenced the prevalence of non-communicable diseases, like diabetes and chronic kidney diseases,6 potentially impacting NP epidemiology as well.

Although previous several studies have explored NP burden, the data they used was relatively old,2 analyzed a single metric,7 and focused on specific populations.8 Furthermore, this study explicitly distinguishes itself from prior Global Burden of Disease (GBD)-based analyses by utilizing the updated GBD 2021 dataset and focusing specifically on the adolescent and young adult demographic, providing a more current and targeted evaluation.9

NP can be understood as a chronic, progressive disorder influenced by multiple factors, including muscle strain (from poor posture, sleep issues, repetitive activities, or injuries), inactivity, inflammatory or degenerative alters in spinal structures, and non-catastrophic injuries from various incidents. Thus, early and proactive management is crucial. However, limited public awareness and underperforming healthcare systems, particularly in low-resource settings, impede effective prevention and management of NP.

To enhance the update understanding of NP epidemiology globally, especially for adolescents and young adults, this study leverages database from the GBD 2021. It is aimed at providing a systematic evaluation to the trends as well as inequalities of NP, including: (1) explanatory analysis of NP epidemiology with national, regional, as well as global dimensions; (2) trends study across local, whole, as well as multi-dimensional visions; (3) heatmap analysis of NP burden by age and location; (4) cross-country inequality analysis following WHO-recommended methods; (5) predictive analysis to 2035 about the global NP burden among individuals aged 15 to 39 years. This systematic analysis seeks to update projection of prevalence, incidence, and disability - adjusted life-years (DALYs) related with NP in adolescents as well as young adults.

Methods

Data Acquisition

Data were obtained from the GBD 2021 study, which provides systematic estimates of health loss due to 369 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories, stratified by age and sex. The study integrates a wide range of data sources from the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx), which were analyzed using spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression to ensure smoothing across time, age, and geographic locations, especially in regions with sparse data. To address variations in case definitions and study methods across countries, the Meta-Regression Bayesian, Regularized, Trimmed (MR-BRT) tool was applied for bias adjustment. Detailed methodological descriptions are available in prior publications.10 From these datasets, we extracted estimates—including 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs)—of the prevalence, incidence, and DALYs for NP, expressed per 100,000 population.11 We also incorporated the sociodemographic index (SDI), a composite measure of income, education, and fertility, used to categorize regions into five quintiles.12 All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1), the Joinpoint Regression Program, and the INLA package for predictive modeling.

Ethical Statement

The data used in this study were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database, which is publicly available and consists of de-identified, aggregated data. Ethical approval for the GBD study and waiver of informed consent were granted by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board.

The present study was reviewed by the Department of Spine Surgery, The Ninth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital, Beijing, China, which determined that no additional Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was required, as the study does not involve identifiable human participants.

Descriptive Analysis

We summarized the NP burden at global, regional, and national levels from 1990 to 2021. Metrics included counts and age-standardized rates (ASRs) for prevalence, incidence, and DALYs, disaggregated by sex. Comparative analyses were performed across 21 GBD regions, 204 countries and territories, and five SDI groups for the years 1990 and 2021.

Trend and Age-Period-Cohort Analysis

Temporal trends in ASRs were evaluated using estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), derived from a linear regression model of the natural logarithm of ASR over time. An increasing or decreasing trend was determined based on whether the EAPC and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were both above or below zero, respectively.13

To identify localized trends, we applied segmented regression using Joinpoint software, calculating the annual percentage change (APC) and average APC (AAPC) for each segment. The Monte Carlo permutation test (with 4499 replicates) was used to identify inflection points, with significance levels adjusted via Bonferroni correction.14

We further employed an APC model to estimate independent effects of age, period, and birth cohort on NP trends. Data were grouped into 5-year intervals for age (15–19 to 35–39 years), period (1990–1994 to 2015–2021), and birth cohort (1955–1959 to 2000–2004). The model produced relative risks (RRs) for each dimension using an iterative fitting algorithm.

Heatmap Analysis

Heatmaps were generated to visualize incidence, prevalence, and DALY rates across age groups (15–19 to 35–39 years) and the 21 GBD regions. Values were aggregated and normalized to allow cross-regional and cross-age comparison.

SDI Association Analysis

The SDI, ranging from 0 (lowest development) to 1 (highest), combines income per capita, educational attainment, and total fertility rate. We examined the association between SDI and ASRs of NP across geographic regions and time.15

Cross-Country Inequality Analysis

Health inequality in NP burden was assessed using the concentration index and slope index of inequality, based on DALY rates for the 15–39 age group. These measures evaluate socioeconomic-related inequality across nations ranked by SDI.4

Predictive Analysis

Future trends in NP burden were projected to 2035 using a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model, implemented via integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) for improved accuracy and computational efficiency compared to conventional Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods.16

Decomposition Analysis

We performed decomposition analysis using a three-factor framework (population size, age structure, and age-specific rates). Uncertainty intervals were estimated using Monte Carlo simulation (1000 draws), assuming normal distributions based on reported 95% uncertainty intervals. Decomposition was repeated across draws, and 95% uncertainty intervals were derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

Results

Explanatory Analysis to NP Burden with Global, Regional, National Perspectives

Globally, the incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs of NP showed significant increases, while the ASR and crude rates of these metrics initially declined, then rose, and ultimately exhibited a slight decrease from 1990 to 2021. Notably, the values were consistently higher in females compared to males (Figures S1–S3). Tables 1–3 present the case numbers as well as ASR of NP incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs in 1990 and 2021. Regionally, South Asia reported the maximal number of incident, prevalent, as well as DALYs cases, while North Africa and the Middle East showed the maximal ASR for incidence (Table 1 and s Table 1). Meanwhile, Tropical Latin America presented the maximal ASR for prevalence and DALYs (Tables 2 and 3; s Table 1). To provide context on the magnitude of between-region differences rather than focusing on rank alone, it is important to note that these top-ranking regions demonstrated substantial absolute differences in ASRs compared to both the global averages and lower-burden regions. Nationally, NP incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs changed significantly among different countries, with China presenting the highest total cases, while Iran exhibited the highest ASR for all three metrics (Figure 1; s Tables 2 and 3). Furthermore, when considering the SDI quintiles, Middle-SDI quintile had the maximal cases number of incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs in 2021. Conversely, High-SDI quintile showed the maximal ASR for prevalence, incidence, as well as DALYs (Tables 1–3; s Table 1).

General Trends of NP Burden with Comprehensive Estimation Approach

Globally, the ASR for incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs of NP experienced annual declines of 0.140% (95% CI: 0.233–0.047), 0.143% (95% CI: 0.235–0.051), as well as 0.137% (95% CI: 0.228–0.046) in 1990–2021, severally (Tables 1–3; s Table 4). Regionally, the maximal increments of EAPC for ASR of incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs were found in Western sub-Saharan Africa, whereas the most remarkable decreases occurred in High-Income North America (Tables 1–3; s Table 4). Nationally, the NP burden trends changed widely among the 204 countries as well as territories (Figure 1), in which Kuwait showing the maximal EAPC for ASR of prevalence, incidence, as well as DALYs, and Spain showing the minimum (s Table 5). Additionally, examining the 5 SDI quintiles revealed that the maximum increments of EAPC for ASR of prevalence, incidence, as well as DALYs were found in Low-SDI quintile. Although most EAPC values of ASR demonstrated decreasing trends among the 5 SDI quintiles in 1990–2021, the EAPC of ASR in Low-middle SDI quintile was closest to stability during this period (Tables 1–3; s Table 4).

Local Trends of Segmented Regression Analysis for NP Burden

The findings from the segmented regression analysis for NP burden were illustrated in Figure S4. From 1990 to 2021, there was a notable rise in the cases’ number for incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs, with the most significant growth occurring between 2003 and 2009 (Figure S4). While the ASR generally showed slight declines, local trends varied across segments. Specifically, the ASR for incidence had two periods of increase (2003–2009 and 2009–2015), alongside decreases during 1997–2003 and stable rates from 1990 to 1997 and 2015 to 2021. The ASR for prevalence experienced three increasing segments (2003–2009, 2009–2015, and 2015–2021), with a notable decrease from 1997 to 2003 and stability from 1990 to 1997. Similarly, the ASR for DALYs also reflected two increasing segments (2003–2009 and 2009–2015) and a rapid decline during 1997–2003, with minimal changes from 1990 to 1997 and 2015 to 2021 (Figure S4). Furthermore, analyzing the data with a ten-year gap revealed that the NP burden measures exhibited their most rapid changes during 1993–2003 (Figure S4).

Age-Period-Cohort Analysis of NP Burden

The findings from the APC analysis regarding the incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs of NP are presented in Figure S5. Controlling for birth cohort and period effects, age was found to significantly influence the risks of NP incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs. The deviation of rate of incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs initially rose and then declined, peaking among individuals aged 20–24 years (Figure S5). When eliminating age as well as birth cohort effects, noteworthy period effects to the risks of NP incidence, prevalence, as well as DALYs were noted. These period effects exhibited a trend of increasing, then declining, and rising again, with the highest risks recorded during the period from 1995 to 1999, and the lowest risks during 2005 to 2009 (Figure S5). Furthermore, after eliminating the period and age effects, the birth cohort effect was found to significantly influence the risk of NP incidence, prevalence, and DALYs. This effect indicated higher risks in later birth cohorts compared to earlier ones, with deviations rising, then falling, and rising again from the 1955–1959 cohort to the 2005–2009 cohort (Figure S5).

Discussion

The current investigation provides a comprehensive and updated analysis of the global, regional, and national burden of NP from 1990 to 2021, based on the GBD study. Our findings indicate a consistent increase in the global prevalence, incidence, and DALYs associated with NP among adolescents and young adults over the past three decades, with projections suggesting this trend will persist. Specifically, while the total number of incident cases rose by 36.6% between 1990 and 2021, the ASR of incidence saw a slight decrease of 2.5%, indicating that population growth and aging are the major drivers behind the increasing absolute burden. A notable strength of this study is its utilization of the most recent GBD 2021 data and its specific, targeted focus on adolescents and young adults, providing an updated and highly relevant epidemiological perspective.

A key finding from this study is the persistent sex-based disparity. Across most regions and age groups, females exhibited higher incidence, prevalence, and DALYs rates compared to males (Figures S1–S3), a trend projected to continue. This disparity may be attributed to anatomical differences in cervical vertebrae and paraspinal muscles, which are generally weaker in females. Furthermore, genetic factors are believed to play a significant role in differential pain perception between sexes.17 The underlying causes of these observed disparities warrant further in-depth investigation.

Despite NP being a major cause of global disability since 1990, effective management and prevention strategies remain inadequate. The condition is not uniform; it is a symptom with diverse underlying causes, including muscle strain from poor posture or repetitive activities, degenerative changes in spinal structures, and non-catastrophic injuries from events such as motor vehicle accidents or sports-related incidents, which are particularly common in younger populations. The clinical presentation of NP is highly individualized, likely resulting from a complex interaction between tissue-related and stress-related factors, including pain history, genetic predisposition, life events, and cultural influences. This complexity makes it essential to move beyond traditional academic boundaries to develop effective, personalized strategies.

Of particular concern is that the highest NP burden occurs during prime working years, specifically among those aged 25–39. NP significantly impairs employee productivity, leading to substantial financial costs for individuals, employers, and healthcare systems, and often results in presenteeism. Recommended early interventions include targeted exercises to relieve muscle tension, ergonomic adjustments such as proper workstation setup, and varied work tasks. While workplace wellness programs can help, the overall effectiveness of these strategies in reducing the global NP burden remains unclear. There is a critical need to develop robust causal interpretations and functional prevention and treatment strategies to alleviate individual suffering and reduce the overall condition burden.

Significant regional disparities were observed. Tropical Latin America exhibited the highest ASR of prevalence and DALYs, while North Africa and the Middle East had the highest ASR of incidence. These variations may reflect cultural and lifestyle differences. In some regions, higher rates are associated with physical labor such as carrying heavy loads on the head, particularly among women. In wealthier countries, increased use of electronic devices like smartphones and computers, coupled with sedentary lifestyles and poor ergonomic practices during remote work—exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic—are hypothesized to be associated with the high burden, though these remain contextual interpretations as they were not directly tested in our analysis. This raises important questions about whether increased healthcare access drives detection or merely responds to the burden, especially since many interventions do not always adhere to evidence-based practices.18 Overreliance on imaging can lead to increased costs and potential iatrogenic harm,19,20 highlighting the need for clinicians and patients to prioritize evidence-based, informed treatment decisions. Furthermore, the conclusion that higher burden is concentrated in high-SDI countries requires nuanced interpretation, as this may partly reflect better diagnostic detection, comprehensive disease reporting, and advanced healthcare access rather than a true excess in biological risk.

Our study has several limitations. A primary inadequacy is the lack of original data, particularly from low-income countries, which increases the risk of compositional bias. In many poor regions, NP is likely underreported despite being common in populations engaged in physical labor. We advocate for increased investment in standardized data collection across diverse ethnic, educational, and rural–urban populations. The heterogeneity of available NP data also introduces indeterminacy, and limited input data may obscure true disparities in burden. There is an ongoing need to develop and use reasonable musculoskeletal questionnaires to standardize investigation methods and improve global comparability.21,22

Previous studies indicate that about 20% of individuals with NP do not achieve full recovery, often due to factors such as high initial pain intensity, concurrent headaches, psychological distress, or coexisting low back pain. Focusing solely on pain relief without addressing underlying physical impairments can lead to recurrence. Furthermore, delayed access to evidence-based treatment can result in chronicity and markedly reduced quality of life.

To improve the accuracy of future estimates, it is essential to develop methods that account for variations in NP severity by country, sex, and age, and to prioritize comprehensive global data collection. Our model was limited by the absence of data on key risk factors such as smoking, obesity, sedentary behavior, psychopathology, and trauma,23 which diminishes its precision. Future research should aim to identify modifiable risk factors, especially in data-scarce regions. Additionally, our model did not account for the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have decreased access to treatment and increased NP prevalence and DALYs. As our analyses are primarily descriptive, causal interpretations regarding SDI-related inequalities and cohort effects should be avoided.

Conclusion

In conclusion, no significant reduction in the NP burden was observed from 1990 to 2021, and projections indicate a continued rise until 2035. However, the inherent uncertainty of long-term projections must be explicitly acknowledged, as they inherently assume the continuity of past trends and may not fully account for future policy changes, technological shifts, or global disruptions. Addressing this challenge requires interdisciplinary collaboration and policies that engage patients at the primary healthcare level. A standardized definition of NP, along with the collection of high-quality data on NP and its risk factors, is essential for accurate burden estimation. Policymakers must strengthen health systems by promoting early examination, timely management, and continuous care, with considerations for age and sex as biological variables. Explicitly linking these key findings to targeted intervention strategies, such as implementing robust ergonomic education and workplace wellness programs in high-risk regions, is crucial to mitigating the future impact of NP globally.
