Poster 174. Association of Sport Specialization with Squat Performance in Youth Athletes: A Novel Functional Classification System
Authors: Omar Abdalla, Alexa Jaume, Ameen Suhrawardy, Batoul Baydoun, Haytham Alqasmi, Ahmad Almaat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Early single-sport specialization has been hypothesized to increase overuse injuries due to year-round repetitive strain on the same muscle groups and joints. Currently, there is no widely adopted clinical screening tool analogous to a cholesterol panel for cardiovascular health to assess overall musculoskeletal health or injury risk before athletes sustain an acute injury. The squat, often used informally in clinics, is a foundational compound movement that engages multiple joints and associated soft tissue structures. To perform a full flexion squat, a patient must demonstrate (1) adequate mobility of the hips, knees, and ankles, (2) suppleness of ligaments and tendons, (3) Lower Extremity (LE) proprioception, (4) sufficient muscle strength and balance, particularly involving the extensor mechanism, and (5) a non-restrictive body weight. This makes it a powerful surrogate for comprehensive LE function that can serve as a rehabilitative benchmark. A novel classification system was developed to stratify LE function through squatting ability. The system is divided into two components. Firstly, depth assessment at the lowest point of descent, described as grade 1-4, reflects eccentric strength, presence of musculoskeletal pain, and range of motion (ROM). The second component, graded as A-C, assesses rising from maximal squat depth, primarily reflecting the concentric strength of the extensor mechanism. This study serves to determine whether multi-sport athletes are more likely than single-sport athletes to achieve higher squat classification grades and whether superior squat performance could serve as a potential preseason injury risk indicator.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted during school pre-participation physicals in Detroit Public Schools. Athletes (K-12, N = 136; 72 males, 64 females) reported sports played and were categorized as single-sport (one sport, n = 63) or multi-sport (>=2 sports, n = 73). Each performed a maximal-depth squat and was classified using our two-component system shown in Table 1. The optimal classification is 4C. We compared the proportion of athletes achieving 4C between groups using chi-square tests and calculated effect sizes (risk difference [RD], relative risk [RR]). A Cochran-Armitage trend test was performed to assess whether the probability of achieving a 4C classification increased with the number of sports played.Results:Multi-sport athletes were significantly more likely to achieve the optimal 4C squat classification than single-sport athletes (33.3% single-sport vs 54.8% multi-sport, Chi-Squared p = 0.0186). The RD was 20.1% (95% CI [3.8%-36.4%]), indicating ~20 additional 4C classifications per 100 athletes in multi-sport compared to single-sport athletes. The RR of achieving 4C for multi-sport vs single-sport was 1.60 (95% CI [1.06-2.41]), and the OR was 2.29 (95% CI [1.14-4.61]), reflecting more than double the odds of 4C in multi-sport athletes. A positive dose-response trend was observed: 4C rates increased from 33.3% in single-sport athletes to 46.3% in two-sport athletes and 62.5% in those playing >=3 sports. A one-sided Cochran-Armitage trend test confirmed this positive trend (one-sided p = 0.0032), demonstrating a significant relationship between increasing levels of sport participation and squat performance.Conclusions:Multi-sport athletes were significantly more likely to achieve optimal 4C squat classification than single-sport athletes, suggesting that diversified sports participation may foster better overall LE function, as evidenced by superior squatting ability. The squat classification system has practical implications as a preseason screening tool. It is simple, quick, and equipment-free, yet integrates multiple aspects of orthopaedic health like strength, range of motion, balance, and body weight. If an athlete cannot perform a full 4C squat, it may indicate limitations such as tight ankles, a weak extensor mechanism, or pain that warrant targeted interventions before the season. Our novel squat classification system proved feasible for quantifying lower-extremity functions in a field setting. With further validation, squat performance grading could be incorporated into preseason evaluations to identify athletes with modifiable deficits and guide preventive conditioning. However, additional research, including prospective injury tracking and reliability studies, is needed before widespread adoption.
