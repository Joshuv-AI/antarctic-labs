Experimental simulation investigation on fracture behavior and bolt anchoring mechanism in a circular tunnel under support in layered rock mass
Authors: Yulong Chen, Duo Xu, Junyang Teng, Kaiyuan Deng, Honghua Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper aims to explore the fracture behavior and bolt anchoring mechanism in circular tunnel under support in layered rock mass. Seven support methods, horizontal and vertical bedding planes are set up, and cubic rock-like material samples with a prefabricated circular hole and bedding planes are tested under uniaxial compression. During the compression, the failure of the hole sidewall is monitored by a camera. The fracture behavior in tunnels under support in layered rock mass is reproduced. After uniaxial compression failure, the fracture behavior of specimens is analyzed via CT scanning, and the bolt anchoring mechanism is analyzed. The results demonstrate that the system bolt and steel floral pipe provide higher improvement. The shotcrete and steel arch can provide supporting pressure for the tunnel, which effectively prevents the surrounding rock from spalling. The strength increment for the vertical bedding specimen is larger than that for the horizontal bedding specimen. CT observations combined with theoretical analysis indicate that the anchor bolt generates anchoring effect by improving the stress state and the mechanical properties of the surrounding rock in the anchorage zone where the fractures are compacted, arrested, and deflected, which could increase the bearing capacity of the surrounding rock.Keywords: Circular tunnel, Support, Anchor bolt, Anchoring mechanism, BeddingSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Excavation Damaged Zone (EDZ) occurs at all types of excavation, including tunnel, hydraulic, mining and other underground projects. The formation of the EDZ is related to the excavation methods and the stress redistribution of the surrounding rock caused by excavation unloading. In the EDZ, initiation, propagation and coalescence of fractures appear, and trigger damage, spalling, buckling, slippage, and rockburst on the excavation face1–3. After these disasters occur during the tunnel construction, it is impossible to devote much time to investigate the failure mechanism behind. Engineers usually apply the I-beam arches for primary support. After the deformation of the surrounding rock diminishes, the I-beam arches are removed section by section and the secondary lining is implemented in time, which would significantly increase the project cost and delay the construction4–9. Therefore, it is of great significance to reveal the fracture initiation and propagation mechanism for controlling the deformation and failure during underground excavation.

The researches on the fracturing mechanism induced by underground excavation in the rock mass with holes are conducted from two viewpoints: one regards the holes as cavern excavation10–17, and studies the fracture growth around the holes from the perspective of excavation damage; the other one regards the holes as the initial damage inside the rock mass18–20, and analyzes the damage accumulation process, fracture propagation path, and the strength and deformation characteristics of the rock mass with holes from the perspective of damage and fracture mechanics. Theoretically, the former should consider the effects of excavation unloading, blasting vibration, mechanical damage on the surrounding rock. Its fracture evolution of the surrounding rock is different from the latter. However, due to the difficulty of experimental operation and observation, there are few relevant studies in the literature considering excavation effect on the initial damage in the form of holes.

Since the mechanical response of a material is usually governed by the weakest portion, a fracture is more prone to occur surrounding the hole and stimulates much more complexity of the stress state in the vicinity of the hole. The complex fracture initiation and propagation result in the surrounding rock becoming the most likely zone where fractures may first initiate and propagate.

There are three types of fractures associated with cylindrical cavities loaded in compression: primary fractures nucleating at the tensile stress concentration of the perimeter, remote fractures at points remote to the perimeter where a combination of tensile and compressive stresses creates favorable conditions for fracture, and slabbing fractures that form at the compressive stress concentrations of the perimeter. Not all three fracture types necessarily develop simultaneously. Their formation depends on loading conditions and the size of the cavity21,22. Subsequently, numerous researchers have studied initiation and propagation, as well as influencing factors of the three types of fractures in the rock with holes, mainly focusing on the stress level12,15,23,24, shape11, size15,24, number and distribution15 of holes, specimen size15,24 and the rock material anisotropy24.

Rock bolt has a relatively strong reinforcement effect on jointed rock mass and has been widely used in mining and tunnelling as well as coal pillar dam support of underground reservoir25–31. The anchoring effect and mechanism of rock mass have attracted much attention, especially for the jointed rock mass. However, the existing studies have primarily focused on rock type22, joint roughness32,33, bolt inclination34,35, and mechanical properties of bolt material36,37 and rock38, failure mode38,39, bending and shear strengths38,39 of bolted rocks. Little attention has been paid to fracture-arresting effect of the anchor bolt.

Actually, the EDZ not only occurs during the underground excavation, the instability and failure of the surrounding rock are often encountered even after the support is completed. Therefore, understanding the fracture growth in the surrounding rock of tunnel under support is a key issue in relation to underground excavations for support system design and long-term safety of tunnel40. In this paper, the fracture behavior and failure characteristics in the circular tunnel are effectively reproduced by experimental simulations. The circular tunnel is simulated by a cubic rock specimen with a circular hole. A rock-like material is used to simulate the surrounding rock mass incorporating horizontal and vertical bedding orientations. A series of uniaxial compression tests is carried out on the rock-like material specimens with a circular hole under seven support methods. The damage to the hole is monitored in real-time under uniaxial loading. Besides, the internal damage characteristics of the anchored rock after uniaxial compression failure are analyzed based on X-ray CT scanning. Furthermore, the analytical solutions for stress and displacement of the surrounding rock in anchorage zone and the non-anchorage zone are derived. Finally, the bolt anchoring mechanism is revealed by combining analytical solutions and CT observations.

Materials and methods

Table 1 shows the program of experiments. Seven support methods are considered, that is, (1) no-support, (2) system bolt, (3) steel floral pipe, (4) ‘system bolt + shotcrete’, (5) ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete’, (6) ‘system bolt + shotcrete + steel arch’, (7) ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete + steel arch’. Experiment for each support method involves a horizontal and a vertical bedding-plane direction. The bedding angle is defined to be horizontal when the loading direction is perpendicular to the bedding-plane direction. Similarly, the bedding angle is defined to be vertical when the loading direction is parallel to the bedding-plane direction.

Characteristics of experiments case.

The anchor bolt used in this study included system bolt and steel floral pipe. Screw-thread bolts (strength grade: 8.8) composed of 45# steel were used as the system bolts. The yield strength, tensile strength, and diameter of the screw-thread bolts were 640 MPa, 800 MPa, and 5 mm, respectively. Steel floral pipes were fabricated from 45# carbon steel using a cutting process; their outer diameter was 6 mm and wall thickness was 1 mm.

The shotcrete was simulated by a layer of epoxy resin with a thickness of 2 mm. and the steel arch was simulated by stainless pipe #304 with an inner diameter of 20 mm. This pipe was inserted into the hole and the gap between the pipe and surrounding rock was filled by the epoxy resin.

Cubic specimens with horizontal and vertical bedding planes were prepared for uniaxial compression testing. The specimens were prepared using the rock-like material composed of C15 cement, river sand and quick lime at mass ratio of 1:3:1.5, respectively. Slaked lime was used as a coagulant, and its amount was the same as that of cement. A specified mold with dimension of 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm was prepared. The hole was created by inserting a steel pipe with an outer diameter of 22 mm in the center of the mold. Thus the cubic samples with the dimension of 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm and a circular hole of diameter 22 mm were casted. The mixed synthetic rock-like materials were poured into the mold in four layers, i.e., 20 mm, 30 mm, 30 mm, and 20 mm in sequence. After the accomplishment of each layer, five bolts with dimension of Φ 5 mm × 25 mm were arranged radially on the top part. Thus, 15 bolts were arranged in three layers, and every two bolts on each layer were arranged at 72°. Figure 1 shows a schematic view of a typical experiment.

Schematic diagram of a typical experiment and position of hole and bolts.

To simulate the effect of layered strata, four layers of 0.2 mm thick sheet mica with a spacing of 20 mm were evenly laid as the bedding plane. Horizontal and vertical bedding planes were considered for each support method. Three specimens were tested in each group to reduce experimental variance. The uniaxial compression tests were conducted on the specimens after being maintained at room temperature for 28 days.

The MTS815 rock mechanics testing system was used to conduct the uniaxial compression experiments. A displacement rate of 0.1 mm/min was applied for loading on the specimens. A Nikon camera D5200 (resolution 6000 × 4000 pixels) was placed in front of the specimen to record the fracture evolution, and an industrial endoscope camera (resolution 1280 × 720 pixels) was used to monitor the sidewall damage characteristics of the hole. The experimental setup for a sample is illustrated in Fig. 2.

The uniaxial compression test setup.

Before and after the experiment, the SOMATOM Scope X-ray spiral CT machine (Fig. 3) was used to perform nondestructive scanning to test the fracture development and distribution in the specimens.

Results

Stress-strain curve

The stress-strain curves of the rock-like specimens under support are presented in Fig. 4. The rock-like material has better homogeneity, resulting in consistent stress-strain curve of each group. Both of the support method and the bedding angle have significant influence on the stress-strain curves.

Stress-strain curves of the rocks under each support method.

All support methods are able to enhance the uniaxial compressive strength of the specimens. Additionally, the bedding angle could affect the uniaxial compressive strength. The uniaxial compressive strengths of the specimens under different support methods are shown in Fig. 5. For the horizontal bedding specimens, the uniaxial compressive strength is 4.01 MPa for those under no-support and 7.13 MPa for those under system bolt support, which causes the strength to be increased by 77.80%, indicating that the system bolt can significantly improve the strength of the specimens. The uniaxial compressive strength of the specimens under ‘system bolt + shotcrete’ and ‘system bolt + shotcrete + steel arch’ is 7.28 MPa and 7.68 MPa, respectively. Compared to the system-bolt supported specimens, the strength of those under the support with ‘system bolt + shotcrete’ and ‘system bolt + shotcrete + steel arch’ is increased by 2.13% and 7.71%, respectively. Such small increment in strength indicates that the shotcrete and steel arch are rather less effective in improving the strength of surrounding rock than the system bolt. The uniaxial compressive strength of the specimens under the support with steel floral pipe, ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete’ and ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete + steel arch’ is 6.76 MPa, 6.96 MPa and 7.33 MPa, respectively. The specimen strength under the support with steel floral pipe is increased by 68.61% compared to that under no-support, indicating that the steel floral pipe also has significant effect on the strength of the surrounding rock, but it is slightly less than that under the system bolt in strength improvement. The strength of the specimen under the support with ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete’ and ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete + steel arch’ is increased by 2.87% and 8.40%, respectively, as compared with the strength of the specimen under the support of steel floral pipe, indicating that the shotcrete and steel arch are less effective in improving the strength of the surrounding rock than the steel floral pipe. When the bedding planes are vertical, the strength reinforcement under the support structure follows similar behavior to that with horizontal bedding planes. The uniaxial compressive strength of specimens under no-support is 2.71 MPa. The strength with support of system bolt, ‘system bolt + shotcrete’ and ‘system bolt + shotcrete + steel arch’ is 5.38 MPa, 5.62 MPa and 5.89 MPa, respectively. The strength is increased by 98.55%, 107.55% and 117.47%, respectively, for the specimens under support with system bolt, ‘system bolt + shotcrete’ and ‘system bolt + shotcrete + steel arch’. The strength with support of steel floral pipe, ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete’ and ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete + steel arch’ is 5.14 MPa, 5.62 MPa and 5.89 MPa, respectively. The strength is increased by 89.87%, 97.92% and 114.38%, respectively, for the specimens under support with steel floral pipe, ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete’ and ‘steel floral pipe + shotcrete + steel arch’. It can be seen that the increment magnitude of strength for the vertical bedding is larger than the horizontal bedding under the same support method, indicating that the specimens with vertical bedding planes is more sensitive to the support than horizontal bedding specimens.

Uniaxial compressive strength of the rocks under each support method.

The uniaxial compressive strength values for horizontal bedding specimens are located above these with vertical bedding planes, regardless of the support method. As the horizontal bedding planes are perpendicular to the axial loading, the bedding planes are compacted and closed under axial load. As a result, the horizontal bedding specimens have longer initial compaction stage than vertical bedding ones. By contrast, the vertical bedding planes that are paralleled to the axial loading cause a small stress drop in the initial loading stage.

There are many small fluctuations near the peak stress of the stress–strain curve. As shown in Fig. 6, such stress fluctuations are attributed to the fracture initiation and propagation surrounding the hole or along the bedding planes of the specimens. For the specimens under no-support, the stress fluctuations mainly occur in the pre-peak region, as a result of spalling damage on the sidewall of the hole. For the specimens under support with system bolt and steel floral pipe, there are obvious stress fluctuations in the pre and post peak stress regions. Such stress fluctuations are related not only to the spalling damage on the sidewall, but also to the local fracturing and plastic hardening in the post peak region caused by anchoring effect of the system bolt and steel floral pipe. Under the support of ‘system bolt/steel floral pipe + shotcrete’ and ‘system bolt/steel floral pipe + shotcrete + steel arch’, the damage is visibly mitigated. As shown in Fig. 7a, deformation occurs in shotcrete without fragment for the specimen under support with ‘system bolt/steel floral pipe + shotcrete’. Even there is no spalling damage observed for the specimen under support with ‘system bolt/steel floral pipe + shotcrete + steel arch’ (Fig. 7b). Under such support methods, the stress fluctuations are caused by the fracture initiation and propagation in the plastic hardening stage after the peak stress as a result of given support, rather than by the spalling damage on the sidewall. Figure 4 shows that the stress-strain curves of the specimen under support with ‘system bolt/steel floral pipe + shotcrete + steel arch’ present longer plastic hardening stage and more drastic stress fluctuations than those under support with ‘system bolt/steel floral pipe + shotcrete’ and ‘system bolt/steel floral pipe’. The specimen under support with ‘system bolt/steel floral pipe’ has the shortest plastic hardening stage and smallest stress fluctuations among these support methods. In other words, the system bolt/steel floral pipe, shotcrete and steel arch could prolong the span of plastic hardening stage, and stimulate the stress fluctuations. This observation can demonstrate that the stress fluctuations are related to the plastic hardening of surrounding rock as a result of support.

Failure mode

Figure 8 shows the failure modes of specimens under each support method at which S_fracture, T_fracture, and R_fracture refer to shear, tensile and remote fractures, respectively. Failure modes are schematically summarized in Fig. 9. It is noted that the failure mode depends on the bedding angle of the specimens. The failure mode exhibits shear failure for the specimens with horizontal bedding planes, and tensile failure for those with vertical bedding.

Failure modes of rocks under each support method.

Schematic failure modes.

For the horizontal bedding specimens, the fractures initiate from the spalling of the sidewall of the hole and propagate towards the corners. Three shear failure modes are observed, that is, single, conjugated ‘X’ and ‘T’ shear plane failure. For specimens of P1-3 and PC1-1, the fractures initiate from the spalling of the sidewall of the hole and propagate towards the diagonal corners, and finally form a single shear plane. For specimens of M1-1 and BC1 -2, the shear fractures initiate from both sides of the hole propagate towards both upper and lower corners to form a conjugated ‘X’ shear plane. For specimens of B1-3, BC1-2, PCG1-2 and BCG1-3, shear fractures propagate towards both upper and lower corners on one side of the hole, and on the other side shear fractures propagate towards the lower corner or no fractures are generated, which shows a conjugated ‘T’ pattern.

For vertical bedding specimens, their failures are mainly controlled by the vertical bedding planes. The vertical bedding specimens undergo tension splitting along the bedding planes. Fractures initiates from the spalling of the sidewall of the hole, and propagate towards one end (BC2-3) or both ends (M2-1, B2-3, BCG2-2, P2-1, PC2-1, PCG2-1) along the vertical bedding planes.

According to different fracture formation mechanisms, fractures can be divided into three types: tensile fracture (T_fracture), shear fracture (S_fracture) and remote fracture (R_fracture). Tensile fracture which develops along the vertical bedding are caused by the tension of the bedding planes. For vertical bedding specimens, the tensile fracture dominates the failure mode. Shear fracture initiates from both sides of the hole where compressive stress concentrates. In horizontal bedding specimens, shear fracture controls the failure.

Hole damage

Different support methods result in various extents of spalling damage of the hole (Fig. 10). The specimens under ‘System bolt + shotcrete + steel arch’ (BCG1-3, BCG2-3) do not have obvious spalling damage of the hole. But the vertical bedding specimens under no-support (M2-3) have the most serious spalling damage. System bolt and steel floral pipe could effectively improve the rock strength and mitigate the extent of spalling damage. The specimens under support with system bolt and steel floral pipe exhibit slighter spalling damage of the hole compared to those under no-support.

Sidewall damage of the hole of the rocks under each support method.

The specimens under support of ‘system bolt + shotcrete’ (BC1-3, BC2-3) just present lateral deformation instead of detachment, indicating that the shotcrete enhances the supporting and bonding effects. This is because the shotcrete is made of the epoxy resin with the nature of high plastic deformability. Even under a certain of lateral deformation, the hole can maintain its integrity.

For both horizontal and vertical bedding specimens, both sidewalls of the hole exhibit the severest damage. This is due to the concentration of the compressive stress on the sidewalls under vertical compression.

It should be noted that although the damage and fracture behaviors around the hole of the rocks with horizontal and vertical bedding planes are consistent, the role bedding orientation plays in the stress distribution around the hole could not be neglected. In these experimental tests, the bedding distance of 20 mm is selected, which is equal to the hole diameter. In actual engineering, various bedding angles and distances may be encountered. If the bedding distance exceeds the hole diameter, surrounding rocks of the hole could be considered intact. That is, the effect of the bedding plane on the surrounding rock stability of the hole is small. On the contrary, when the bedding distance is less than the hole diameter, the excavation of tunnel would encounter the bedding plane. As a result, the buckling, separation, and slippage are prone to occur along the bedding plane in the surrounding rock, which pose a major safety problem to surrounding rock stability. Therefore, understanding the fracture behavior and failure characteristics concerning the effects of bedding angle and distance has received widespread attention.

CT observations

In the present research, X-ray CT scanning of specimens is carried out after uniaxial compression test to analyze the effect of anchor bolt in the surrounding rock on fracture propagation. The scanning sequence is from front to back for the front vertical cross-section images. 17 slice images are obtained in the X-ray CT testing of each specimen. Herein the CT images of horizontal bedding rock under the support with steel floral pipe, i.e., P1-2, are used as an example to further illustrate the fracture characteristics of surrounding rock of a hole under support.

17 CT images are captured from front to back for specimen P1-2 and marked as (a)~(q) in sequence, as shown in Fig. 12. As shown in Fig. 12a, tensile fractures b and d initiate from the tensile stress concentration zone in the top and bottom of the hole. The shear fractures a, c, and e initiate from the sidewall. With the propagation of fractures a and c, secondary fractures f and g are produced (Fig. 12b).

Front vertical cross-sections with different depths after uniaxial compression failure.

After entering the anchorage zone formed by the first row of pipes, fractures c and g disappear (Fig. 12c and d), the fracture b shrinks (Fig. 12e-g), and the fracture a branches into two, i.e., fractures a_1 and a_2 (Fig. 12e). This specimen has three rows of steel floral pipe inside. For each row of steel floral pipe, the compacting and arresting effects on the fractures in the anchorage zone are observed. Through the second row of pipes, the tensile fractures b and d become compacted and disappear (Fig. 12j). Similarly, fractures e_1 and j compact and disappear (Fig. 12o), and fracture ahi deflects and branches off (Fig. 12o) after crossing third row of pipes. This phenomenon indicates that the anchor bolt could form an anchorage zone within a certain range inside the anchored rock mass, and effectively constrain penetrating fractures to develop further.

Although the anchor bolt-induced anchorage zone could restrain fracture propagation, new fractures h and i are generated. Fractures h and i propagate forwards and connect with each other to coalesce into the fracture hi (Fig. 12g). The fracture hi disappears after crossing the second row of pipes (Fig. 12h and i). Fractures hi and a_1 gradually change propagation paths (Fig. 12j and k), and coalesce into a penetrating fracture ahi (Fig. 12l), near which a secondary fracture j grows. The fracture j disappears when passing the third row of pipes (Fig. 12m and n). A new fracture k is created with shear fracture e_1 and penetrating fracture ahi to be compacted (Fig. 12o). With being far away from the bolt, fractures ahi, k, and e_1 develop to form a damaged zone ((Fig. 12p and q).

Thus, it is clear that the initiation and propagation of fractures in the anchored rock are controlled by the stress state in the surrounding rock of the hole, as well as the anchor bolt that exerts the compacting and arresting effects on fractures in the anchorage zone. The initiation, development, compaction, coalescence, and disappearance of fractures are reflected by the fluctuations of the stress-strain curves.

It should be emphasized that the above analytical solutions are derived under elastic assumption and uniformly arranged bolts, which belong to an idealized mechanical model. In actual experiments, the surrounding rock exhibits obvious elastoplastic deformation, discrete fracture propagation, and non-uniform bolt–rock debonding, leading to a certain discrepancy between the theoretical predictions and test observations. Such simplification is intended to capture the fundamental anchoring mechanism rather than achieve precise quantitative matching. The analytical results qualitatively reflect that bolts can optimize the stress state, improve mechanical properties, and reduce the plastic zone range of the surrounding rock, which is consistent with the experimental phenomenon that fractures are compacted, arrested, and deflected in the anchorage zone.

Discussion

Homogeneity of rock-like specimens and bolt installation accuracy

In this study, rock-like materials are used to simulate the layered rock mass due to its high homogeneity and small resultant discreteness of the tests. To minimize the discreteness of the test results, the anchor bolts should be installed as evenly as possible. The specimens are casted layer wise. During pouring process, the bolts may be dislocated (Fig. 14a and b). Thus CT scanning is recommended to detect the material homogeneity and bolt arrangement before test.

Dislocated anchor bolt before the test.

Unloading effect on post-test CT observations

CT observation of the fracture on the fractured specimens may arise a discrepancy, as the unloading effects after compression may cause differences in specimen deformation and the spatial morphology of the fracture, compared to in situ observations. Thus, experimentally obtained interior structure information may result in incorrect understanding of fracture characteristics. A novel servo-controlled loading and CT scanning system which facilitates laboratory in situ observation of the continuous evolution of 3D fractures their evolution inside a rock specimen subject to loading without removing it out of the loading system or altering its original observation condition will provide an ideal solution.

Simulation difference between epoxy resin and field shotcrete

The epoxy resin applied in this experimental investigation to simulate the shotcrete is capable of sustaining high plastic deformation. After tests, the specimens were broken carefully to extract the shotcrete. The extracted shotcrete presents evident plastic deformation (Fig. 15), which explains why no cracking or spalling of the simulated shotcrete was observed in the tests.

Deformation of shotcrete after the test.

It should be clarified that epoxy resin cannot represent conventional brittle shotcrete, but its deformable characteristic is reasonably similar to modern high‑ductility shotcrete materials such as fiber‑reinforced shotcrete and engineered cementitious composites (ECC). Traditional shotcrete is brittle and prone to fracture and peeling under large tunnel deformation, whereas fiber‑reinforced shotcrete and ECC exhibit obvious strain‑hardening and multi‑cracking characteristics, which greatly improve toughness and crack resistance. In this sense, using epoxy resin to simulate shotcrete can qualitatively reflect the supporting and anti‑spalling effect of ductile support layers in layered rock tunnels.

In field engineering, especially for high‑stress soft rock tunnels, the application of traditional shotcrete is limited by its brittleness. Therefore, it is strongly recommended to prioritize the use of fiber‑reinforced shotcrete or ECC with high ductility to replace conventional shotcrete. Such advanced materials can better coordinate with large deformation of surrounding rock, inhibit crack propagation, reduce spalling damage, and improve the integrity and long‑term stability of tunnel support systems. Future model tests should adopt more realistic cement‑based fiber‑reinforced materials to further improve the similarity between laboratory simulations and field conditions.

Analytical solution simplification and engineering applicability

Combining the analytical solutions for stress and displacement of the surrounding rock in the anchorage zone and non-anchorage zone with CT observations, it can be concluded that the anchor bolt generates an anchoring effect by improving the stress state and mechanical properties of the rock mass in the anchorage zone. Within the anchorage zone, fractures are compacted, arrested, and deflected, which significantly increases the bearing capacity of the surrounding rock.

It should be emphasized that the analytical solutions in Sect. 4 are established under ideal elastic assumptions and uniformly arranged bolts, which inevitably leads to a certain discrepancy with the actual experimental phenomena. In the tests, the surrounding rock presents obvious elastoplastic deformation, discrete fracture propagation, and non-uniform debonding between bolts and rock mass, which are highly consistent with the typical failure characteristics of layered rock masses.

Such simplification is reasonable and acceptable in rock mechanics research. The analytical model is designed to qualitatively reveal the fundamental anchoring mechanism rather than quantitatively match the experimental data. Although there are deviations in the quantitative calculation of stress and displacement, the analytical results correctly reflect the core mechanism that bolts optimize the stress distribution and enhance the mechanical performance of the anchorage zone, which is consistent with the fracture control effect observed in the experiments.

Taking bolt installation as an example, the analytical model assumes that bolts are uniformly arranged in the same plane. However, in both the experiments and practical engineering, bolt installation is affected by manual operation, making it difficult to guarantee uniform installation angle and ideal grouting quality. After testing, the specimens were broken to disassemble the support structure (Fig. 16). The anchoring performance of the anchor bolt is different. The bonded and anchored rock mass exhibits fan-shape (Fig. 16a), square (Fig. 16b), polygon (Fig. 16c). Some anchor bolts and rock are debonded (Fig. 16d). These non-uniform characteristics further lead to differences between the ideal analytical model and actual failure behavior.

Strain gauge interference and internal deformation measurement

An attempt is made to attach strain gauge to the anchor bolt to measure the deformation of surrounding rock and anchor bolt (Fig. 17). But the wire of the strain gauge affects the results significantly. Fractures initiate and propagate along the wire. Thus the strain gauge is no longer used in this experiment for reducing error. Therefore, the analytical solutions could not be validated. A large-scale experiment to diminish the wire effect is necessary. Understanding the internal deformation evolution characteristics of the rock and support structures, as well as fracturing, is beneficial for rational design and stability analysis of surrounding rock in tunnelling applications.

Limitations of uniaxial compression and future triaxial tests

The uniaxial compression test greatly deviates from the triaxial test, although it may be of interest in a follow-up study to validate the results of this study. Triaxial tests will be conducted, allowing the fracture evolution in circular tunnel under real stress condition to be investigated precisely. Additionally, the findings in this paper are only attained from the results of horizontal and vertical bedded rocks. More experimental studies on bedding orientations should be involved for the comprehensive studies of the failure mechanism and mechanical behavior of tunnels in inclined bedded strata. Even so, the experimental studies presented herein provide important guidance. The research of these aspects is still in need of being continued.

Scale effect and engineering applicability

The present study adopts small-scale specimens to reveal the fundamental fracture behavior and bolt anchoring mechanism. Although the specimen size (100 mm cube with a 22 mm hole) is much smaller than that of field tunnels, the test follows the similarity principle to ensure the consistency of mechanical mechanism. The scale effect affects the absolute values of stress, displacement, and fracture dimension, but does not change the intrinsic mechanism that bolts improve the stress state and mechanical properties of the anchorage zone, leading to fracture compaction, arrest, and deflection.

Owing to the scale effect, the test results reveal the fundamental mechanism of bolt anchoring and fracture control, which can provide a mechanical basis for field support design of layered rock tunnels but cannot be directly quantitatively applied to actual tunnel engineering. The support parameters in the field should be further optimized according to in-situ stress, rock mass conditions, and engineering standards.

Conclusion

This paper explores the fracture behavior and bolt anchoring mechanism of layered rock with a prefabricated circular hole under seven support methods using a series of uniaxial compression experiments and CT observations. Based on the experimental and theoretical analyses presented, some probable useful conclusions have been made as follows:

Support structures can improve the strength of the specimen. Among them, the system bolt and steel floral pipe provide higher improvement.

The improvement in the strength of the anchored specimens under the same support method is closely related to bedding orientation. The strength increment for the specimen with vertical bedding planes is higher than that with horizontal bedding planes.

Analytical solutions for stress and displacement of the surrounding rock in anchorage zone and the non-anchorage zone are derived. Combination with the CT observations enables to indicate that the anchor bolt generates anchoring effect by means of improving the stress state and the mechanical properties of the surrounding rock in the anchorage zone. In the anchorage zone, the fractures are compacted, arrested, and deflected, which could increase the bearing capacity of the surrounding rock.
