Neural network as low-cost surrogates for impurity solvers in quantum embedding methods
Rohan Nain, Philip M. Dee, Kipton Barros, Steven Johnston, Thomas A. Maier

Abstract
A promising application of machine learning (ML) is in creating low-cost surrogate models to replace computational bottlenecks in quantum many-body simulations. Here, we explore whether a neural network (NN) can be trained in the low-data regime, with one to two orders of magnitude fewer training examples than previous works, as an efficient substitute for the impurity solver in dynamical mean field theory (DMFT) simulations of correlated electron models. We show that the NN solver achieves accuracy comparable to popular continuous-time QMC (CT-QMC) impurity solvers when interpolating between samples within the training set. While the NN ’s performance decreases notably when extrapolating to lower temperatures outside the training distribution, its output still provides an excellent initial guess for input to more accurate CT-QMC impurity solvers, thus accelerating the time to solution up to a factor of five. We discuss our results in the context of rapid phase-space exploration.

The

The computationally expensive part is the “impurity solver,” which calculates

In general, these prior studies have found that appropriately trained
ML
surrogates could indeed replace more expensive numerically exact impurity solvers and reduce the time to solution. However, in all cases they used relatively large training data sets that are expensive to generate. This aspect poses challenges for generalizing this approach to multi-orbital models and, in particular, cluster extensions of
DMFT
, where generating sufficient amounts of high-quality training data will be computationally demanding.

Motivated by this, here we examine the extent to which a
NN
can be
efficiently
trained as a low-cost but accurate surrogate for the impurity problem in a
DMFT
calculation. Focusing on the half-filled Hubbard model, we show that a well-trained
NN
, trained on a fairly feasible 500 samples synthetic data set, can predict the impurity self-energy
\Sigma^{(n)}(\tau)
(for a given iteration
n
), from which the impurity Green’s function
G^{(n)}(\tau)
is obtained via Dyson’s equation, using Hubbard
U
, inverse temperature
\beta=1/T
(
k_{\mathrm{B}}=1
), and
G_{0}^{(n)}(\tau)
as input features.

Within the parameter regime that is well covered by the training data, the ML surrogates are sufficiently accurate to fully replace traditional
CT-QMC
impurity solvers, which can yield dramatic acceleration. Crucially, this level of performance can be achieved with a relatively limited training data set. We also show that the model extrapolates well for a range of temperatures below the training set coverage. As expected, model errors can become significant with extrapolation; nonetheless, we find that imperfect model predictions can be useful as an initial guess that accelerates the convergence to self-consistency within traditional
DMFT
solvers.

II
Methods

Throughout this work, we consider the half-filled Hubbard model

\hat{H}=-t\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle,\sigma}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{j,\sigma}+U\sum_{i}\left(\hat{n}_{i,\uparrow}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\left(\hat{n}_{i,\downarrow}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right),

(1)

defined on the Bethe lattice within
DMFT
(Fig.
1
).
Here,
\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}
(
\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{i,\sigma}
) creates (annihilates) a spin-
\sigma
electron on site
i
,
\hat{n}_{i,\sigma}=\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}\hat{c}^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{i,\sigma}
is the number operator,
t
is the nearest-neighbor hopping, and
U
is the Hubbard repulsion.

In the first iteration, the Weiss mean-field
G_{0}^{(n)}(i\omega_{n})
is given by an integral

G^{(n=0)}_{0}(i\omega_{n})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\rho(\varepsilon)}{i\omega_{n}+\mu-\varepsilon}d\varepsilon\,,

(2)

where
\rho(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2\pi t^{2}}\sqrt{4t^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}
is the semi-circular density of states of the Bethe lattice.
For a given
G_{0}^{(n)}(i\omega_{n})
, each
DMFT
iteration calculates the self-energy
\Sigma^{(n)}(i\omega_{n})
, which is a functional of
G_{0}^{(n)}(i\omega_{n})
,
\beta
, and
U
, by solving the
AIM
.
The local Green’s function
G^{(n)}(i\omega_{n})
is then updated using the Dyson equation

G^{(n)}(i\omega_{n})=\left[\left[G_{0}^{(n)}(i\omega_{n})\right]^{-1}-\Sigma^{(n)}(i\omega_{n})\right]^{-1}.

(3)

The updated Weiss field for the next iteration is determined by the self-consistency condition, which, for the Bethe-lattice, simplifies to
Georges et al. 1996

\left[G_{0}^{(n+1)}(i\omega_{n})\right]^{-1}=i\omega_{n}+\mu-t^{2}\,G^{(n)}(i\omega_{n}).

(4)

This process is iterated until the
root-mean-square error (RMSE)

\varepsilon_{G}^{(n+1)}=\left\|G^{(n+1)}(\tau)-G^{(n)}(\tau)\right\|_{\rm RMS}<\varepsilon_{\rm tol}.

(5)

falls below a solver-dependent tolerance
\varepsilon_{\rm tol}
.

We used the
Toolbox for Research on Interacting Quantum Systems (TRIQS)
library
Parcollet et al. 2015
with the
continuous-time hybridization-expansion (CTHYB)

QMC
solver to solve the
AIM
in the
DMFT
loop. For each simulation, we use
n_{\rm warmup}=5\times 10^{4}
thermalization sweeps followed by
n_{\rm cycles}=2\times 10^{5}
measurement sweeps
with a cycle length of
\ell_{\rm cyc}=1{,}200
. The cycle length was chosen to maintain a measured autocorrelation time between 0.5 and 1.5, as described in the
TRIQS
documentation
Parcollet et al. 2015
.

The Matsubara Green’s functions
G_{0}(i\omega_{n})
and
G(i\omega_{n})
and self-energy
\Sigma(i\omega_{n})
are obtained through Fourier-transform from the corresponding functions on the imaginary time (
\tau
) axis.
We adopt a compact Legendre polynomial basis
Boehnke et al. 2011
to represent all imaginary-time quantities

F(\tau)=\sum_{l\,\mathrm{even}}^{l_{\rm max}}f_{l}\,P_{l}\!\left(\frac{2\tau}{\beta}-1\right),

(6)

where
F(\tau)
can be the Green’s function
G(\tau)
or the self-energy
\Sigma(\tau)
,
P_{l}(\tau)
are the
l
th
order Legendre polynomials defined on the interval
[-1,1]
,
f_{l}
are the expansion coefficients, and
l_{\mathrm{max}}=60
is a cut-off in the expansion order. Working at half-filling, particle-hole symmetry demands that
F(\tau)=F(\beta-\tau)
, which results in vanishing coefficients
f_{l}
for odd
l
in the expansion. Thus, both
G_{0}(\tau)
and
\Sigma(\tau)
are fully encoded by their 30 even-indexed coefficients
\{f_{0},f_{2},\ldots,f_{58}\}
. In practice, we have found that taking
l_{\mathrm{max}}=60
is sufficient to obtain reliable results for all values of
U
and
\beta
considered here.

The architecture of our
NN
is shown in Fig.
1
. It accepts the Hubbard interaction strength
U
, the inverse temperature
\beta
, and the 30 even Legendre coefficients of the
Weiss field
{G}_{0}(\tau)
as a 32-dimensional input vector, and returns the 30 even Legendre coefficients of the self-energy
\Sigma(\tau)
as an output vector. The network is comprised of four fully-connected layers of neurons with GELU activations and trained to minimize the physics-informed loss function

\mathcal{L}=\left\langle\sum_{l\,\mathrm{even}}w_{l}\bigl(f_{l}-\hat{f}_{l}\bigr)^{2}+\lambda_{\rm dec}\sum_{l\,\mathrm{even}}l^{2}\,\hat{f}_{l}^{2}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{batch}}.

(7)

Here
\hat{f}_{l}
denotes the predicted coefficient,
\lambda_{\rm dec}=10^{-4}
controls the Legendre-decay regularization, and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{batch}}
denotes the batch average. The per-coefficient weights
w_{l}
are chosen to strongly penalize errors in the lower order coefficients, which carry the dominant spectral weight and were determined empirically through iterative refinement by comparing single-step
NN
predictions against
CT-QMC
self-energies
\Sigma(\tau)
across a range of weight configurations. The Legendre-decay term acts as a mild regularizer, penalizing unphysically large high-
l
coefficients, enforcing the expected physical suppression of the higher order Legendre models in smooth imaginary-time functions.

To impose the analytically exact constraint that the self-energy vanishes for
U=0
, the training set is augmented with 100 synthetic samples at
U=0
with all target coefficients set to zero.
(These training examples can be generated with no computational cost.) The network is optimized with AdamW
Loshchilov and Hutter 2019
with a cosine learning rate schedule decaying from
\eta_{0}=10^{-3}
over
10^{4}
steps and weight decay
5\times 10^{-4}
, for up to 2000 epochs with a batch size of
12
and with early stopping whenever the validation loss fails to improve for
25
consecutive epochs.

To generate the training data, we use synthetic data for the Weiss mean-field
G_{0}(\tau)
and, together with
U
and
\beta
as inputs, compute the corresponding self-energy
\Sigma(\tau)
using the
CT-QMC
impurity solver. To ensure generalization across arbitrary bath geometries, we construct the Weiss field
G_{0}(i\omega_{n})=[i\omega_{n}+\mu-\Delta(i\omega_{n})]^{-1}
by finite pole expansion of the corresponding hybridization function

\Delta(i\omega_{n})=\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{\sigma=\pm 1}\frac{|V_{p}|^{2}}{i\omega_{n}+\sigma\epsilon_{p}},

(8)

with the number of poles
N_{p}\in[1,10]
, pole positions
\epsilon_{p}/t\in[0.05,4.0]
, and hybridization strengths
V_{p}/t\in[0.1,0.5]
drawn randomly from uniform distributions. Eq. (
8
) explicitly respects particle-hole symmetry; generalization to doped models will require removing the symmetric sum over
\pm\epsilon_{p}
. The resulting
\Sigma(\tau)
from
CT-QMC
solver defined by its Legendre coefficients
\Sigma^{l=0,2,\dots 58}
constitutes the training target.

The training samples were generated on a grid of
(U,\beta)
values, with
10
Weiss fields drawn at each grid point via the bath parametrization Eq. (
8
).
[The
(U,\beta)
grid is indicated by the white squares in Fig.
5
.]
Samples for which the Legendre-reconstructed
\Sigma(\tau)
exhibited unphysical oscillations or sign changes were discarded, as these were taken to indicate insufficient Monte Carlo statistics, yielding a data set of 500 training examples. In future studies, the training data may be more expensive to generate and collect; accordingly, we intentionally limit the model here to 500 samples as a test of data efficiency.

We have found that the
NN
predicts impurity Green’s functions very accurately for parameters within the coverage of the training data, where it is effectively interpolating between training examples. However, we also examine the network’s performance in regions of parameter space where it must extrapolate to lower temperatures than those represented in the training set. In the following sections, we refer to these two regions of parameter space as the “interpolation” and “extrapolation” regions of the phase diagram.

IV
Discussion & Conclusions

We have shown that a compact
NN
-based surrogate trained on a relatively small synthetic dataset can provide an accurate solution for a
DMFT
simulation of the half-filled single-band Hubbard model.
In particular, our
NN
model reproduces the behavior of a
CT-QMC
impurity-solver on an iteration-by-iteration basis, accurately capturing the converged
DMFT
solutions in the interpolation regime, and provides viable warm starts for exact solvers beyond that regime.
The
NN
-surrogate predictions for the
DMFT
self-energy reproduce the impurity Green’s function
G(\tau)
in the metallic and insulating regions of the phase diagram, capturing the correct behavior for metallic and insulating initial seeds of the
DMFT
loop.
The model also performs qualitatively well in the extrapolative regime for temperatures close to the training set boundary. This observation naturally led to the finding that the trained model can be used to initialize new simulations and accelerate their convergence. However, our relatively compact and efficient
ML
framework yields small discrepancies in some measured quantities, such as double occupancy, highlighting the sensitivity of the correlation functions stemming from our model prediction of the self-energy.

Several aspects of the physical problem we study here and our workflow help to explain the accuracy of the
ML
model predictions. Starting from the half-filled Hubbard model on the Bethe lattice, we leveraged particle-hole symmetry to constrain the learned mapping and maintain the correct density, thereby removing potential complications arising from chemical potential tuning. Moreover, the Bethe lattice admits a particularly simple self-consistency condition,
\Delta(i\omega_{n})=t^{2}G_{\mathrm{loc}}(i\omega_{n})
, making the
DMFT
mapping smoother and plausibly easier for the
ML
-surrogate to learn. The Legendre polynomial basis parameterization is known
Boehnke et al. 2011
to be an efficient representation of many-body Green’s functions, and we find it to be similarly efficient for the self-energy. We note that our approach of using the Green’s function
G_{0}(\tau)
as an input, which efficiently encodes Hamiltonian parameters such as the lattice type, filling or chemical potential, bandwidth, and symmetries in a single function, allows for straightforward extensions to other impurity Hamiltonians and may have improved universality and transferability compared to other approaches which use the Hamiltonian parameters directly.

It is important to emphasize that the
ML
-surrogate contains enough expressivity to capture essential features of the Mott transition, as exemplified by Fig.
4
. The surrogate tracks the hysteresis curves arising from the choice of a metallic or insulating seed at the beginning of the simulation. Although some quantitative discrepancies in the double occupancy are clearly visible, we believe these are tunable with more training data and architectural design tweaks.

The interpolative power of
ML
-based approaches is readily apparent in our predictions of
U_{\mathrm{c}1}
and
U_{\mathrm{c}2}
in Fig.
6
. High-fidelity results are achievable in this region, enabling high-density predictions in phase diagrams that are well-represented by the training data distribution. However, as is well known, high-dimensional
NN
-based solution manifolds are often unconstrained outside the training manifold, and extrapolations of these models to out-of-distribution regimes are typically unreliable. In our case, the
NN
model exhibits enough extrapolation power to capture schematic aspects of the unseen region, suggesting that the model could be fine-tuned by partially freezing weights and training on additional examples in this region. Moreover, the model can accelerate convergence by passing off the NN-converged solution to the exact impurity solver (as shown in Fig.
7
), yielding trustworthy predictions in the extrapolative region of the phase diagram. In all cases, extra care is needed where additional competing phases and broken-symmetry channels may enter between or outside the grid of training examples.

Compared to earlier
ML
studies of impurity solvers and
DMFT

Arsenault et al. 2014
;
Sheridan et al. 2021
;
Agapov et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2025
, our work deliberately targets compactness and economy for the application at hand. In future efforts on more complex models and solvers beyond the single-impurity site, acquiring training data will become inherently more expensive. Therefore, beyond feasibility, representations, error-correction, and modern
ML
architectures, one should expect that the order of magnitude of training examples may be small. Our results suggest this route is not only feasible but also sufficiently accurate for real exploration of challenging regimes of the correlated electron problem. While preparing this manuscript, Ref.
Valenti et al. 2026
appeared as a preprint. While this work is broader in scope and more materials-facing, our work uses roughly two orders of magnitude fewer training examples.

Taken together, our results suggest a new paradigm for exploring many-body Hamiltonians with
ML
-based quantum impurity solvers. As mentioned above, carefully constructed models on small synthetic datasets can enable essentially continuous phase-diagram predictions, and their utility can be extended to impurity solver accelerators. The newly acquired exact results may be reused for further self-improving training, thereby improving model accuracy and expanding its mapping to new physical regions of important model Hamiltonians.
