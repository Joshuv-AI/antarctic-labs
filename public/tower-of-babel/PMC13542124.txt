Characterization and evaluation of Moringa oleifera seed powder as a flocculating agent in biofloc culture of Clarias magur catfish
Authors: Reshmi Debbarma, Soibam Khogen Singh, Soibam Ngasotter, Tanmoy Gon Choudhury, Pradyut Biswas, Gusheinzed Waikhom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlocculation is key to the performance of biofloc systems, and largely achieved using synthetic flocculants, which are not cost-effective. In this study, we investigate the potential of de-oiled Moringa oleifera seed powder (DMSP) as a natural bio-flocculant in biofloc system to enhance floc formation and water quality. DMSP was prepared through drying, husk removal, grinding, and oil extraction, then characterized for particle size, charge, and surface features. Laboratory tests confirmed strong flocculation efficiency, removing over 90% suspended solids at optimal conditions. A 45-day biofloc trial with catfish, Clarias magur (avg. wt. = 0.36 ± 0.02 g) stocked at 80 individuals per tank revealed that 70 mg L−1 DMSP significantly reduced ammonia and nitrite levels, increased nitrate concentrations, and improved microbial nitrification (p < 0.05) in the fish culture system. Fish performance in terms of biomass gain, survival, and feed utilization also improved markedly in the same dosage group. These results highlight DMSP as a eco-friendly alternative to synthetic flocculants, offering practical benefits for sustainable aquaculture by improving both system stability and fish productivity.Keywords: Biofloc, De-oiled Moringa seed powder, Flocculating activity, Water quality, Floc quality

Introduction

Aquaculture plays a vital role in global food security, contributing 15% of the world’s animal protein supply1. As production intensifies to meet rising demand, maintaining water quality and fish health becomes increasingly challenging due to nitrogen-rich waste and its environmental impacts, such as eutrophication and pollution2. Biofloc technology (BFT) offers a sustainable solution to these challenges. BFT allows high-density production while minimizing environmental impact3. It shifts from traditional water exchange methods to in situ waste recycling by fostering heterotrophic microbial flocs through external carbon sources4. Biofloc consist of diverse microbial communities, including bacteria, protozoa, fungi, and microalgae, that aggregate with organic waste particles5. This system enhances protein production for farmed species, improving growth and immune function, and reducing reliance on supplementary feeding4,6.

Developing floc is crucial for stabilizing biofloc systems, involving complex interactions of physical, chemical, and biological processes7. Robust floc development is essential for water quality, as nitrogen removal is linked to floc formation8,9. Establishing a stable biofloc system takes several weeks due to these complex interactions5. Maintaining consistent floc quality and preventing system crashes remain challenging even after initial setup. To overcome these delays and risks, flocculating agents that can speed up floc formation is essential toimprove system stability, making biofloc aquaculture more reliable and efficient. Flocculating agents enhance floc formation by agglomerating suspended particles and colloids, typically by reducing the ionic double layer or neutralizing surface charges. Conventional flocculants include mineral additives such as poly‑aluminium chloride and synthetic polymers like polyacrylamide10–12. While these agents can be effective, they present several problems: residues from metals and synthetic polymers may pose health risks to fish, farmers, and consumers; they persist in the environment without breaking down, contributing to long-term pollution; and their continued use increases costs, making less sustainable. These concerns have driven interest in natural, non‑toxic, and biodegradable alternatives that can deliver the same benefits of floc formation while ensuring safety, environmental compatibility, and cost effectiveness.

Moringa oleifera (MO) is recognized as one of the most important natural flocculants for water and wastewater treatment, with numerous studies confirming its effectiveness across different types of effluents13–16. More recently, MO seed extracts have been applied in aquaculture wastewater treatment, showing strong potential to improve water quality and fish health. For example, catfish juveniles reared in MO-treated wastewater performed as well as those cultured in clean well water17, while other studies reported significant improvements in hematological parameters18, survivability, and immune responses19. These findings demonstrate that MO is not only effective in removing suspended solids and harmful compounds but also safe and beneficial for aquaculture species.

The coagulation properties of M. oleifera are attributed to an active protein component that functions as a cationic polyelectrolyte. This protein binds to negatively charged particles in suspension, facilitating the formation of large flocs in water. Additionally, M. oleifera seeds contain 19–45% w/w oil20,21, and their de-oiled form enhances flocculation by removing lipids that may hinder particle binding. Previous studies have shown that oil extraction improves the coagulant activity of M. oleifera for wastewater treatment22,23, enhances turbidity and dye removal24,25, and increases microbial clearance such as E. coli removal26. Compared to other plant-based flocculants, M. oleifera offers distinct advantages for biofloc systems. Its cationic proteins are highly effective in aggregating suspended solids, while its de-oiled form promotes clearer water and more stable floc formation. Moreover, its natural abundance, biodegradability, non-toxicity, and compatibility with microbial processes make it especially well-suited for biofloc aquaculture,

Environmental parameters such as pH, mixing speed, flocculant dosage, and turbidity affect the coagulation and flocculation process of M. oleifera27–29. Studies have shown that the flocculating activity of MO increases with increase in pH levels from 3 to 9, with an optimal range between 7 and 930,31. The iso-electric pH value is typically between 9 and 10 or higher32–34. The optimal dosage of Moringa seed powder depends on the water’s initial turbidity. Mangale et al.35 found that effective turbidity removal is achieved with dosages of 100–150 mg L−1, while Adelodun et al.36 reported an effective dosage of 182.74 mg L−1. For fish farm wastewater treatment, a dosage of 400 mg L−1 is effective37, while 70 mg L−1 of seed extract is suitable for oil refinery wastewater treatment38. Mixing speed and duration are also crucial for floc formation, which traps and removes suspended particles from water. Higher stirring speeds (100–200 rpm) are usedduring coagulation to rapidly mix the coagulant with water, while lower speeds (20–50 rpm) are used during flocculation to allow flocs to grow without breaking apart36,39.

The search for cheaper and more reliable flocculant can boost the biofloc industry in a more sustainable way. To date, the application of flocculant is very much limited to general aquaculture wastewater treatment, while no studies to date have focussed precisely on biofloc aquaculture systems. Overlooking the specific ecological dynamics of biofloc systems, this gap is critical because biofloc aquaculture requires floc formation not only for water clarification but also to stabilize organic matter, support microbial aggregation, and recycle nutrients. DMSP is uniquely suited to this context as it delivers high flocculating efficiency at practical dosages while remaining stable across typical aquaculture pH (6.5–8.5) and temperature ranges. Unlike other flocculants that are sensitive to water chemistry.

Furthermore, the potential of de-oiled Moringa oleifera seed powder (DMSP) to enhance flocculation efficiency in biofloc environments remains unexplored. Addressing this gap, the present study used DMSP as a novel bio-flocculant specifically for biofloc aquaculture systems. The flocculant is characterized based on key variables in biofloc landscape, such as particle size, surface charge, and functional groups. Additionally, the efficiency offlocculantswill be assessed under different environmental conditions and dosages to determine its practical application in biofloc systems. This research represents one of the first attempts to improve sustainable water quality management through efficient waste management techniques, utilizing DMSP bio-flocculant in biofloc landscapes. The novelty of the study lies in demonstrating the practical viability of DMSP as a bio-flocculant in biofloc-based aquaculture, a domain where its use has not been previously reported.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics and care

The work adhered strictly to the guidelines for animal welfare and experimentation established by the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals, Ministry of Environment & Forests, Government of India. Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) further approved the experiment and the protocols vide letter No. CAU-CF/48/IAEC/2018/06b, dated 30 October 2023. Further, plant material was used in accordance with all relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

Materials

Mature seeds of Moringa oleifera were collected from local cultivars at Lembucherra, West Tripura, India (23.8861° N, 91.2792° E), and identified by a botanist at ICAR Tripura Centre based on seed morphology (size, shape, and color). Seed use complied with all relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines. Dry pods were manually de-shelled to obtain mature seeds, which were dried for 24 h at 40–50 °C. The dried seeds were ground using a laboratory mill, and the powder was sieved through a 0.15 mm mesh to achieve uniform particle size. The powdered coagulant was stored in airtight, light-protected containers until further use.

Preparation of DMSP

Ethanol-based oil extraction was performed on fine M. oleifera seed kernel powder following the procedure of Kwaambwa and Maikokera40. Seed powder was suspended in 95% ethanol at a 1:10 ratio (1 g powder: 10 mL ethanol) and mixed with a magnetic stirrer for 10 min. The suspension was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min (Eppendorf 5910 Ri), and the supernatant was collected. The residual de-oiled seed powder was dried at room temperature for 24 h and stored under refrigeration until further use (Fig. 1).

Flow chart for the representation of the preparation steps of DMSP flocculant.

Effects of environmental parameters on flocculating activity

The effect of DMSP dosage on flocculating activity was evaluated using a completely randomized design with concentrations of 60, 70, 80, and 90 mg L−1 at room temperature, each tested in triplicate. Upon identifying 80 mg L−1 as the optimal dose, further experiments were conducted to assess the effects of pH and stirring speed. For pH evaluation, levels were adjusted from 6 to 9 using 0.5 N NaOH or HCl and measured with a calibrated pH/Ion meter (LAQUA PH1500). DMSP was added at 80 mg L−1 in triplicate, with experiments conducted at room temperature and 150 rpm. Stirring speed effects were tested at 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 rpm using 80 mg L−1 DMSP at constant pH 7 and 30 °C, also in triplicate.

The estimation of flocculating efficiency of DMSP was done using a modified kaolin clay method41. Instead of a kaolin suspension, actual biofloc water was used as the test medium. DMSP was added at varying concentrations (0, 60, 70, 80 and 90 mg L−1) to 500 mL of biofloc water. The mixture was stirred at 150 rpm for 2 min, followed by 80 rpm for 1 min, and then allowed to settle for 30 min. The transmittance of the supernatant, measured 3 cm below the surface at 550 nm, was used to assess flocculation performance. A control flocculation activity assay was also conducted using the same method, with the biofloc sample water replaced by deionized water.

Practical application in biofloc system

A 45-day biofloc experiment was carried out with varied concentrations of 60, 70, 80 and 90 mg L−1, and a control group without any flocculating agent, to assess the practical application of DMSP (Fig. 2). The design of the experiment was completely randomizeddesigned with triplicated each. Biofloc was developed in aquarium tanks at the wet laboratory of the College of Fisheries, Lembucherra, Tripura. Each tank was filled with 200 L of water and provided with continuous vigorous aeration. Sugarcane molasses, bought from the local market, was utilized to develop the floc inoculum following the method described by Avnimelech42. Briefly, 20 g/L of pond soil (taken from fish pond), 10 mg L−1 of ammonium sulphate, and 200 mg L−1 of fermented sugarcane molasses (fermented for 24 h before use) were used to prepare the inoculum. After that, the inoculums were made and applied to the experimental tanks at a ratio of 1:50 (inoculum to culture volume). The biofloc was kept suspended by vigorous aeration. DMSP was added at concentrations of 60, 70, 80, and 90 mg L−1 after a 72-h periodin triplicate. The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio was maintained at 15:1 in accordance with Avnimelech42, utilizing a carbon source (fermented molasses) with a carbon content of 42%. This was administered bi-weekly based on the feed quantity and the protein content of the feed used43. Using Na2HCO3, the pH and alkalinity were maintained at the initial development stage.

Schematic diagram of the experimental design.

Animal stocking and feeding management

This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https:// arriveguidelines.org). Following a two-week biofloc generation period, 25-day-old C. magur fry weighing approximately 0.36 ± 0.02 g, were stocked in all experimental tanks at a density of 80 individuals per tank. To ensure optimal growth, feeding was initiated at 10% of body weight for the first 15 days, reduced to 7% for the subsequent 15 days, and further adjusted to 5% for the final 15 days. A high-protein diet containing 37% crude protein (CP) was provided. Feed quantities were continuously modified based on biomass estimations conducted at 15-day intervals.

Characterization of DMSP Flocculant

The prepared DMSP flocculant was characterized using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). FTIR spectra of the samples were recorded under ambient conditions using an FTIR spectrometer (Alpha, FTIR spectrophotometer, Bruker, Germany) in ATR (Attenuated Total Reflectance) mode, within the spectral range of 4000–600 cm−1, at a resolution of 4 cm−1, with a total of 32 scans. The zeta potential of the 0.1% (w/v) DMSP suspensions was determined using a dynamic light scattering instrument (Litesizer DLS 500, Anton Paar). SEM was performed using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope with EDS & Sputter coater (Model-Sigma 300, Carl Zeiss) at an accelerator voltage 15 kV.

Water quality analysis

Water quality parameters were measured in duplicate for each tank, and mean values were used for analysis. Temperature and dissolved oxygen (DO) levels were measured using an optical dissolved oxygen meter (YSI, ProODO). The pH levels were determined with a digital pH meter-335 (Systronics). Alkalinity and hardness were analyzed using titrimetric and EDTA methods as described by Aljerf44. Total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) was quantified using the phenate method at an absorbance of 630 nm, while nitrite-N and nitrate–N were measured at 543 nm following spectrophotometric methods outlined by APHA45. Absorbance readings were taken using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV1800-Pharmaspcc, Shimadzu).

Floc parameters

Floc volume estimation

Floc volume in each tank was measured at 10 days interval. 1 L of water sample was collected with an Imhoff cone and allowed to settle for 30 min5. The floc volume was expressed as mL/L. Flocs were harvested on the 45th day and stored at −20 °C until analysed for proximate content.

Proximate composition estimation

The proximate composition of the harvested flocs, including moisture, crude protein, lipid, and ash content, was determined following standard AOAC methods46. Moisture content was measured by oven-drying samples at 105 °C until constant weight was achieved. Crude protein was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method, in which nitrogen content was determined and multiplied by the conversion factor (N × 6.25). Lipid content was assessed by Soxhlet extraction with petroleum ether as the solvent, while ash content was quantified by incinerating samples in a muffle furnace at 550 °C until a constant weight was obtained.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were collected in triplicate and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test was used to determine significant differences among treatment groups, with a threshold of P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. To explore the interrelationships among water quality parameters and identify dominant influencing factors, factor analysis followed by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted. Factor loadings were interpreted based on magnitude and algebraic sign. Only the two highest loadings (positive and negative) were considered for interpretation.

Results and discussion

Structural characterization of DMSP

The analysis of various functional groups, present in prepared DMSP, was done using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis47. The IR spectra of DMSP revealed several significant peaks (Fig. 3) that indicate various functional groups48. A moderately band at 3285 cm−1 corresponds to the O–H stretching vibration, indicating the presence of hydroxyl groups. Intense peaks at 2921 cm−1 and 2851 cm−1 are associated with symmetrical and asymmetrical stretching of CH and CH2 methyl groups present in the fatty acids, respectively49,50, which are commonly found in these types of compounds51. A peak at 1742 cm−1 suggests the presence of ester groups (C–O–C). The moderately peak at 1645 cm−1 is attributed to amide groups in the protein. A peak at 1536 cm−1 points towards the presence of aliphatic nitro groups (−NO2). The 1458 cm−1 peak indicates the stretching of aromatic rings (aryl groups). The 1231 cm−1 peak is indicative of ether groups. The peak at 1153 cm−1 corresponds to C–N groups, and finally, the peak at 716 cm−1 is associated with aromatic 1, 2 disubstitution (ortho)49. The presence of hydroxyl, ester, amide, nitro, aromatic, ether, and C–N groups allows the DMSP to interact with various impurities in water. These functional groups enhance the ability of the seed powder to act as a natural coagulant, facilitating the aggregation and settling of suspended particles.

FTIR spectra of the prepared DMSP samples showing the most significant peaks.

The presence of hydroxyl, ester, amide, nitro, aromatic, ether, and C–N groups enables DMSP to interact effectively with various impurities in water52. These functional groups contribute to the seed powder’s role as a natural coagulant by promoting charge neutralization, bridging between particles, and enhancing hydrophobic interactions. This facilitates the aggregation of colloidal particles into larger flocs, which can then settle out of suspension more readily—an essential step in the flocculation mechanism for water purification.

The SEM images of DMSP, as shown in the Fig. 4, highlight the surface morphology. The SEM image of DMSP presents a highly rough and heterogeneous surface, marked by numerous cavities and interstices. Additionally, small pores observed around the surface suggest a porous structure that may significantly influence its adsorption and interaction properties49,53. Further characterization indicated that the particle size and zeta potential of the DMSP sample were approximately 0.4 µm and 31.5 mV, respectively (Fig. 5a and b). The particle size of DMSP, measured at approximately 0.4 µm, is significantly smaller than the typical ranges observed for both natural and synthetic flocculants. Natural flocculants, such as those derived from M. oleifera, chitosan, or starch, generally exhibit particle sizes ranging from 1 to 100 µm, while synthetic flocculants like poly-acrylamides and poly-aluminum chloride are often used in granular or powdered forms with particle sizes between 100 µm and 2 mm. The submicron size of DMSP offers a distinct advantage by providing a higher surface area, which enhances adsorption capacity and interaction with suspended particles. This fine particle size also improves dispersion in aqueous systems, increasing the likelihood of contact with contaminants and promoting efficient floc formation. When combined with its positive zeta potential, the small particle size of DMSP contributes to superior performance in water treatment applications, particularly in biofloc systems where rapid and stable flocculation is essential.

This positive charge is likely attributable to DMSPprotein, which inherently carries a positive charge15. Small particle size increase surface area for adsorption interaction and positive zeta potential value denotes a stable colloidal dispersion in water. These physicochemical characteristics with rough and porous surface morphology, small particle size, and positive zeta potential play a crucial role in the flocculation mechanism. These properties strengthen DMSP’s function as a natural coagulant by facilitating electrostatic neutralization, inter-particle bridging, and boosting the likelihood of particle collisions54. As a result, larger and more compact flocs are formed, which settle more efficiently—ultimately enhancing the performance of water quality in biofloc systems.

Factors affecting the operational parameters

Effect of different dosage on flocculating efficiency at laboratory condition

The study investigated the effect of varying dosages of DMSP on flocculating activity and floc volume using biofloc water at laboratory condition. The dosage requirement of DMSP depends on factors such as water type, initial turbidity, pH, and chemical composition14,30,55,56. Previous studies have reported that floc volume is directly correlated with flocculating activity57. The present results (Fig. 6a) show that increasing DMSP dosage significantly enhances flocculating activity and floc volume up to 80 mg L−1 (p < 0.05), identifying this concentration as optimal for biofloc water.

Factor affecting the operational parameters: (a) Dosage, (b) pH, (c) stirring speed.Values represent the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). The different letters (a, b, c, d and e) in a graph under each dosage represents significant difference (p < 0.05).

However, lower flocculation activity and reduced floc volume can result from the insufficient dosage of flocculant, which is unable to effectively coagulate all the colloidal particles present in the water. Beyond the optimal concentration (70–80 mg L−1), excess coagulant may cause particle re-stabilization through charge reversal, in which colloid surface charges become overly neutralized or reversed. This reintroduces electrostatic repulsion and prevents aggregation36,39. Additionally, oversaturation of binding sites hinders inter-particle bridging, producing smaller or weaker flocs. These fragile, loosely bound flocs settle poorly, reducing overall floc volume and efficiency36,39. These findings are crucial for optimizing the use of DMSP as a flocculant in biofloc systems, indicating that careful dosage control is essential to maximize flocculating efficiency and floc volume without surpassing the optimal concentration.

Further, the present study confirms that DMSP is a highly effective natural flocculant, achieving superior performance within a practical dosage range compared with other plant- and waste-derived materials (Table 1). While cactus–banana peel composites58, bitter gourd seed57, cactus59, castor oil cake60, chicken eggshell61, and tannin-based coagulants62 have demonstrated flocculating potential, their efficiencies are generally lower or require substantially higher dosages. In contrast, DMSP maintained strong activity under moderate operating conditions, underscoring its robustness and suitability for aquaculture systems.

When compared with synthetic flocculants, DMSP was competitive (Table 1). Aluminum sulfate38, ferric chloride57, iron (III) chloride hexahydrate59, and ferric sulfate trihydrate63 can match or slightly exceed efficiency, but they often require higher dosages or pose risks of residual chemical contamination. Industrial adoption will require advances in production technology, reliable biomass sourcing, and regulatory approval. Effect of pH on flocculating efficiency at laboratory condition

pH is one of the crucial parameters in the coagulation and flocculation process. Previous studies reported that the optimum pH for these processes not only depends on the types of flocculants but also on the types of water used14,56,64,65. In our study, we found that flocculating activity and floc volume were significantly higher at pH levels between 6 and 9 (p < 0.05), with flocculating activity exceeding 90% and floc volume above 23 ml/L (Fig. 6b). This indicates that the optimal pH range for using DMSP as a coagulant is between 6 and 9 for application in biofloc system.

The enhanced flocculation within this pH range can be explained by the interaction between the charges of colloidal particles and DMSP proteins. The iso-electric point of the active protein in moringa seeds is 1033. This means when pH is below 10, DMSP ionizes and produces carboxylate and H+ charges that attract and neutralize these negatively charged colloidal particles of water, forming flocs that settle out of the water50. Similar results have been reported by Ndabigengesere and Narasiah66, Desta and Bote30 and Sánchez-Martín et al.27 where DMSP effectively flocculate over a broad pH range. However, at pH levels below 6 and above 9, both flocculating activity and floc volume decrease significantly. This reduction occurs because the ionization state of the flocculant and colloidal particles changes, resulting in weaker interactions and less effective floc formation30. In acidic conditions (pH < 6), excessive hydrogen ions may interfere with protein ionization, while in more alkaline conditions (pH > 9), the reduced positive charge on DMSP proteins weakens their ability to neutralize colloids. Overall, the results demonstrate that DMSP powder can be efficiently utilized a in for biofloc systems at neutral pH, for sustainable floc formation and management.

Effect of stirring speed on flocculating efficiency at laboratory condition

The results from the study indicate a significant impact of flocculating stirring speed on both the flocculating activity of DMSP and the resultant floc volume. As illustrated in Fig. 6c, the flocculating activity observed to be significantly higher (> 96%) at stirring speeds of 100 rpm and 150 rpm, compared to lower speeds. This finding suggests that higher stirring speeds enhance the interaction between the DMSP and the suspended particles, thereby promoting more effective flocculation67. Moreover, the floc volume reached its peak value of 24 ml/L at 150 rpm, indicating that the optimal stirring speed for maximizing floc volume lies within the higher range of the speeds tested. This increased floc volume at higher speeds could be attributed to the formation of larger and more robust flocs, which are likely a result of better particle collision and aggregation dynamics facilitated by the increased turbulence at these speeds68,69. Conversely, the significantly lowest flocculating activity and floc volume were recorded at 50 rpm. The reduced performance at these lower speeds may be due to insufficient particle interaction and collision frequency, which are critical for the formation of stable flocs. However, at higher stirring speed above 200 rpm leading to lower flocculating activity and smaller floc volume could be link with more turbulence breaking the fragile flocs27,70,71. The statistical significance of these findings (p < 0.05) highlights the crucial role of optimizing stirring speed to achieve efficient flocculation using DMSP. These results are consistent with the general understanding that appropriate agitation levels are essential for maximizing the effectiveness of flocculants in water treatment processes.

Application in biofloc system

Water quality parameters

TAN, NO2–N and NO3–N

The early rise in TAN across treatments (Fig. 7a) reflects microbial establishment and organic matter breakdown during biofloc initiation3,72. The most effective reduction occurred in the 70 mg L−1 group where consistently low TAN indicates stable floc formation and efficient microbial assimilation through heterotrophic and nitrification pathways3,9,73,74. Nitrite accumulation was minimized in this group (Fig. 7b), suggesting enhanced colonization of nitrifying bacteria within well-structured flocs75–77. Steady nitrate increased across treatments (Fig. 7c) confirmed effective nitrification78,79 but higher nitrite levels in the 60 and 90 mg L−1 groups point to weaker floc aggregation or microbial imbalance75,76,80. These results highlight dosage-dependent nitrogen removal and with 70 mg L−1 providing the most favorable balance.

Water quality trend during the experimental period. (a) TAN; (b) Nitrite–N; (c) Nitrate–N; (d) Dissolved oxygen; (e) pH; (f) Alkalinity; (g) Floc volume; (h) TSS. Values represent the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3) for the treatment and for the control at each sampling day.

pH and alkalinity

The pH and total alkalinity of water are critical to the nitrification process, as they significantly influence the activities of nitrifying bacteria80. Biofloc treatment units depend on ample alkalinity reserves to sustain an optimal pH level. Observations from a 45-day experiment revealed a pH variation ranging from 7.65 to 6.97 (Fig. 7e and f). Declines in pH during early biofloc development were linked to alkalinity consumption by microbial activity3,74,76. Additionally, the addition of a carbon source and the constant release of acids due to respiration activity contributed to the decline in pH. It is important to note that a lower pH significantly decreases the rate of the nitrification process3. To mitigate this issue, a liming agent, Na2HCO3, was added from day 20 onwards to maintain the optimum pH level in the culture system, keeping it above 73,81.

Dissolved oxygen

Dissolved oxygen (DO) is the important factors in biofloc systems, directly influencing microbial activity and animal health. In our study, DO levels declined initially across treatments due to organic matter degradation during system start-up3,82. In the control, 60, and 70 mg L−1 groups, DO remain above 4 mg L−1 throughout the experiment (Fig. 7d), which is considered optimal for biofloc technology7. In contrast, higher dosages (80 and 90 mg L−1) led to significant DO depletion and reflecting increased organic load from excess flocculant addition83.

This imbalance arises because overdosing promotes residue accumulation and intensified microbial respiration which was elevating biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) beyond aeration capacity84. The resulting oxygen deficit disrupts system stability and compromises biofloc efficiency. These findings suggest that DMSP dosages below 80 mg L−1 are optimal for maintaining adequate DO and ensuring stable biofloc performance.

TSS and floc volume

Effective management of total suspended solids (TSS) is critical in biofloc systems, as excessive levels impair gill function, promote harmful bacteria, and reduce oxygen availability85–87. In biofloc system, it is implied that a TSS of > 1000 mg L−1 would impact the health and well-being C. magur fry health88. All treatments in this study remained below this limit (Fig. 7h), indicating safe operating conditions. The highest TSS was observed in the 90 mg L−1 group, reflecting organic overload from excess flocculant addition that can cause residue accumulation and microbial imbalance83,84.

Floc volume (FV) showed clear dosage-dependent differences (Fig. 7g). The 70 and 80 mg L−1 treatments achieved the greatest FV, demonstrating efficient aggregation and stable biofloc formation. In contrast, the 90 mg L−1 group exhibited high TSS but lower FV, suggesting that overdosing disrupted floc structure despite increased particulate matter. These findings highlight that optimal DMSP concentrations enhance microbial aggregation while preventing excessive solids buildup, thereby supporting water quality and fish health.

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed clear relationships among water quality variables under different DMSP dosages (Fig. 8, Table 2). Factor 1 (42.63% variance) was dominated by positive loadings for TSS, floc volume, and nitrate–N. This reflects the synergy between microbial aggregation and nitrogen transformation, where increased floc volume supports microbial biomass and enhances nitrification, leading to stable nitrate accumulation3. Treatments with 70–80 mg L−1 DMSP clustered along this axis, indicating that these dosages promoted mature biofloc communities with efficient nutrient recycling.

Factor 2 (16.84% variance) highlighted the trade-off between dissolved oxygen (DO) and nitrite-N. Negative loadings for DO and positive loadings for nitrite suggest that oxygen limitation disrupts complete nitrification, resulting in nitrite buildup89. This pattern was most evident in the 80–90 mg L−1 groups, where excess flocculant increased organic load and microbial respiration, elevating biochemical oxygen demand and reducing oxygen availability.

Together, the first two factors explained nearly 60% of the dataset variability, underscoring their relevance for system management. Factor 1 represents structural and biochemical stability, while Factor 2 captures operational sensitivity to oxygen dynamics. Practically, these findings show that optimal DMSP dosages (70–80 mg L−1) enhance floc formation and nutrient cycling, whereas overdosing (> 80 mg L−1) risks oxygen depletion and nitrite accumulation. Thus, PCA serves not only as a statistical tool but also as a diagnostic framework for aquaculture management, guiding interventions that balance microbial aggregation, nutrient recycling, and oxygen supply.

Further, future research should explore integrating PCA with advanced machine learning to enhance predictive capabilities. Studies highlight the promise of stacked and active learning for complex property prediction (104), optimization via the Grey Wolf Optimizer (106), transfer learning in composites (107), and biomedical applications for treatment evaluation (105). Building on these advances, predictive models for aquaculture biofloc systems could anticipate water quality shifts, optimize natural flocculant dosages (e.g., Moringa), and reduce reliance on trial-and-error experimentation.

Correlation analysis

The Pearson correlation analysis, illustrated by the dot map of “r” values in Fig. 9, provides a complementary perspective to PCA by highlighting direct pair-wise relationships among water quality parameters. Strong positive correlations were observed between TSS and floc volume, as well as between nitrate–N and floc volume. These associations highlight the functional role of floc formation in biofloc systems, where increased suspended solids and floc volume reflect active microbial aggregation and the rise in nitrate–N signals efficient nitrification. Together, these linkages suggest that floc development stabilizes organic matter and enhances nutrient recycling, thereby reinforcing system maturity and ecological integrity 89.

Correlation plot showing associations among water quality variables in the biofloc system.

Conversely, strong negative correlations were detected between TAN and nitrate–N, and DO and nitrite-N. The inverse relationship between TAN and nitrate–N reflects the sequential nature of nitrogen transformation, where effective nitrification reduces TAN levels while increasing nitrate concentrations. Similarly, the negative correlation between DO and nitrite-N highlights the sensitivity of nitrification to oxygen availability. Insufficient oxygen hinders complete ammonia oxidation and leads to nitrite accumulation, which is toxic to aquatic organisms3. Overall, the correlation analysis highlights the interdependence of physical, chemical, and biological parameters in biofloc systems. It demonstrates that changes in one variable can cascade through the system, influencing multiple aspects of water quality. These findings underscore the importance of integrated monitoring and management practices, where parameters are evaluated collectively to ensure microbial balance, nutrient recycling, and oxygen stability.

Proximate composition analysis

The analysis of the proximate composition of biofloc generated from different dosages of DMSP treated groups is presented in Table 5. Nutritional value of biofloc is dynamic and micro-biota is likely to affects the nutritional value of biofloc90. Moreover, several parameters affected the nutritional value of biofloc such as types of carbon source, feed and nutrient inputs and environmental parameters. Addition of flocculant additives also reported to affect the proximate composition of bio-flocs91. In the present study, the moisture content of generated biofloc did not show statistically significant (p < 0.05). However, the crude protein content, which was significantly higher in the 70 and 80 mg L−1 dosage groups as compared to others. This increase in protein content can be attributed to enhanced flocculation, thereby creating a more suitable environment for microorganisms and other organisms7, and the protein content in DMSP itself (shown in Table 4)83. However, at a higher dosage of 90 mg L−1, the protein level did not significantly increase, which could be linked to lower flocculation at this concentration. The crude lipid content increased in the treatment groups, with the highest significant increase recorded at the 90 mg L−1 dosage. This suggests that higher dosages of DMSP elevate lipid levels in generated flocs. This can be attributed to the lipid content in DMSP. Despite the de-oiling process, lipids were still observed in the flocculant powder, and the presence of lipids in the DMSP flocculant was confirmed by FTIR analysis and proximate composition analysis shown in Table 4. Similarly, the ash content increased in the treated groups, with the highest significant values at the 80 and 90 mg L−1 dosages, indicating an impact on the mineral composition of the biofloc. This could be linked to the mineral content of DMSP92,93. Therefore, the addition of DMSP flocculant increases the mineral content of the biofloc. Overall, these findings suggest that specific dosages of DMSP can significantly influence the proximate composition of biofloc, particularly in terms of protein, lipid, and ash content, thereby potentially enhancing its nutritional value and efficiency for aquaculture systems.

Growth performance and feed utilization

The growth performance and feed utilization of C. magur under varying DMSP dosages are summarized in Table 3. Biomass gain and survivability were significantly higher in the groups treated with 70 mg L−1 of DMSP (p < 0.05), indicating this concentration as optimal for biofloc-based culture of C. magur. However, no significant differences were observed in individual weight gain, specific growth rate (SGR), or length increment across treatments. This variation may be attributed to differential growth patterns commonly observed within populations of the same age and rearing conditions94, which can influence overall biomass gain59,95,96. Conversely, the control group and those treated with 60 mg L−1 and 90 mg L−1 of DMSP exhibited lower survival rates and biomass gain. These outcomes may be linked to suboptimal floc formation and compromised water quality management, resulting from insufficient or excessive DMSP flocculant levels that hinder effective flocculation97 (Table 4).

Regarding feed utilization, the feed conversion ratio (FCR) was significantly lower in the 70 mg L−1 group, suggesting that microbial flocs enhanced with DMSP were efficiently consumed and assimilated by the fish. The protein efficiency ratio (PER) was also significantly higher in these groups, with the highest values recorded in the 70 mg L−1 treatment and the lowest in the control group (p < 0.05). These improvements in growth, FCR, and PER can be attributed to the protein-rich microbial floc (as shown in Table 5), which serves as a continuous and natural feed source throughout the culture period.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that a DMSP dosage of 70 mg L−1 significantly enhances biomass yield, survivability, and feed utilization efficiency in C. magur cultured under biofloc conditions. These results support the potential of DMSP as a sustainable and effective flocculant for improving aquaculture productivity.

Conclusions

The findings from this study indicate that the DMSP-based bio-flocculant exhibits considerable potential for sustainable aquaculture as well as extensive wastewater management. It demonstrated remarkable flocculation efficiency as indicated by the removal of over 90% of TSS at an optimal concentration of 80 mg Lv. Consistent effective flocculation was noted across a pH range of 6–9, with stirring speeds between 100 and 150 rpm, and under moderate temperature conditions. Notably, careful dosage optimization is essential, since concentrations above 90 mg L−1 did not yield further advantages and, in fact, resulted in lowered DO levels and increased TSS. Subsequently, utilizing this bio-flocculant within catfish aquaculture systems led to enhanced water stability, improved growth metrics, and superior feed efficiency, underscoring its beneficial impact on the health and productivity of cultured fishes in intensive farming landscape. However, future work should include cost analysis and environmental impact assessment to fully establish the advantages of DMSP.
