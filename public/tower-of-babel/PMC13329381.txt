Interaction mechanism between tetramethylpyrazine and pumpkin seed protein: binding characteristics and functional property changes
Authors: Jianyu Huang, Dengmi Wang, Na Zhang, Hui Xu, Zhizhi Yang, Peichao Zhang, Jicheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTetramethylpyrazine (TTMP) is a bioactive aroma compound from fermented foods, but its high volatility and short half-life limit application. This study investigated pumpkin seed protein (PSP) as a carrier for TTMP due to its nutritional and functional properties. Multiple characterization techniques and functional assessments were combined to examine the PSP–TTMP system. Results showed that TTMP bound to PSP in a concentration-dependent manner, causing conformational changes. Fluorescence analysis indicated a static quenching process, with spontaneous and exothermic binding mainly driven by hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces. Structural analyses confirmed that TTMP affected PSP secondary structure, aggregation, surface hydrophobicity, and microstructure, promoting a more ordered and dispersed state at suitable concentrations. Simulations confirmed stable binding between PSP and TTMP through key residues such as ASP, ARG, HIS, and GLN. TTMP also improved PSP antioxidant activity, α-glucosidase inhibition, and emulsion stability, highlighting PSP as a promising volatile flavor carrier.Keywords: Pumpkin seed protein, Tetramethylpyrazine, Molecular interaction, Anti-glycation activity, Emulsifying stability

Introduction

Food flavor is a core indicator determining food quality and consumer acceptability (Spence, 2020). Currently, consumers' demand for natural and safe food keeps rising, while artificially synthesized flavorings fail to meet the quality requirements of high-end food processing due to such issues as insufficient flavor authenticity and potential safety risks. Endogenous fermentation-derived aroma compounds are favored in fermented dairy production for clean-label flavor formation that satisfies consumer demands for naturalness, health, and safety (Tian, Xiong, Yu, Chen, & Lou, 2023). Accordingly, research on the development and application of related resources has emerged as one of the core research directions in the field of food flavor regulation.

Tetramethylpyrazine (TTMP) is a characteristic flavor compound naturally present in fermented soybean products such as Douchiba, possessing a nutty aroma with a low odor threshold, and can be significantly increased through enhanced fermentation with high-yielding strains, thereby improving the flavor quality of the product (Yang et al., 2025). Beyond its flavor-enhancing effect, TTMP exhibits favorable disease-modulating properties. For instance, Long et al. found that TTMP can treat pulmonary thromboembolism by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and suppressing hypoxia-induced excessive autophagy (Long et al., 2025). Furthermore, Zou et al. demonstrated that the combined therapy of TMP and paclitaxel (PTX) inhibits angiogenesis and promotes the apoptosis of tumor cells by blocking the ERK1/2 and Akt pathways (Zou et al., 2019). However, its inherent application limitations, such as high volatility and a short metabolic half-life, result in inherent drawbacks for its practical application in the food industry.

With the rise of the plant-based food industry, pumpkin seeds serve as an agricultural by-product with abundant reserves and controllable costs. The pumpkin seed protein (PSP) extracted therefrom features a well-balanced amino acid composition and excellent nutritional value, which can meet the food industry's demand for high-quality protein raw materials (Vinayashree & Vasu, 2021). By virtue of its core properties that adapt to the requirements of modern food processing, PSP has emerged as one of the research hotspots in the field of food science. Studies have demonstrated that PSP also exhibits superior processing functional properties including emulsibility and foaming capacity, rendering it suitable for the processing technological scenarios of various food products. For instance, Mehvish Habib et al. treated pumpkin seed protein isolate (PSPI) with high-intensity ultrasound, which improved its color, solubility, emulsifying capacity and stability (Habib et al., 2025). In addition, PSP possesses favorable delivery potential; Yang et al. performed covalent cross-linking between PSPI and various polyphenols, which enhanced the antioxidant activity, storage stability and digestive stability of functional foods (Yang et al., 2023). Besides, studies have shown that interactions exist between proteins and flavor compounds; for example, Guo et al. demonstrated that the binding of pea protein to pyrazine compounds was dependent on both pyrazine concentration and alkyl chain length, thereby affecting flavor retention (Guo et al., 2024). However, research on the synergistic effects between PSP and TTMP remains limited. This gap highlights an important opportunity for the innovative combination and high-value application of natural food ingredients.

To fill the gaps in existing research and meet the industrial needs of natural food ingredient innovation and high-value utilization of plant proteins, this study systematically investigated the interaction between TTMP and PSP, as well as its effects on the conformation and functional properties of PSP. Multispectral techniques, structural characterization, and molecular docking were employed to further study the concentration-dependent effects of TTMP on the conformational rearrangement, micromorphology, and surface properties of PSP. In addition, the regulatory effects of TTMP on the functional properties of PSP were evaluated by assessing its antioxidant activity, anti-glycation activity, and emulsifying stability. This study bridged the theoretical gap in the interaction between natural flavor compounds and plant proteins.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pumpkin seed protein (PSP, purity ≥90%) was provided by Xi'an Ruisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; The edible vinegar and soybean oil were purchased from a local supermarket; 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS, ≥95%) was acquired from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China); α-glucosidase were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biological Co., Ltd.; 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazine (DPPH) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.; All other chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade.

Extraction of TTMP

This method was slightly modified based on previous studies (Chen et al., 2010). Briefly, 100.0 g of vinegar powder sieved through a 60-mesh sieve was accurately weighed, and 80% ethanol solution was added at a solid-liquid ratio of 1:10 (w/v), followed by ultrasonic-assisted extraction at 45 °C for 45 min. The extract was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was collected, and the extraction process was repeated twice. After combining all the extracts, ethanol was removed by rotary evaporation under reduced pressure at 40 °C, and the obtained aqueous phase was reserved for subsequent separation. The extract was centrifuged again for 5 min at a relative centrifugal force of 5000g, and the supernatant was filtered for further use. The pH of the aqueous phase was adjusted to 8.5 with 1.0 mol/L NaOH solution. The organic phases were combined, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and then filtered, followed by concentration under reduced pressure to obtain a TTMP-enriched extract. The TTMP content in the extract was determined to be 99.1 ± 0.2% through detection.

Preparation of solutions

Pumpkin seed protein (PSP; major subunits, 20–36 kDa) was dissolved in PBS buffer (0.01 M, pH 7.4) to obtain a stock solution at 2 mg/mL. After vortex mixing, the solution was kept at 4 °C for 2 h to ensure complete hydration. Tetramethylpyrazine (TTMP, MW 136.2 g/mol) was dissolved in absolute ethanol to prepare a 100 mM stock solution. Aliquots of the TTMP stock were then added to the PSP solution, followed by vortex mixing. The final TTMP concentrations were 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 150, 200, and 300 μM. To minimize the potential effect of ethanol on protein structure and function, the final ethanol concentration in all samples was maintained at ≤1% (v/v). The resulting PSP–TTMP complexes were designated PT.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

The changes in functional groups of different samples were slightly modified based on previous methods (Yue, Xu, Luo, & Zhao, 2024). Briefly, 5 mg of PT powders with different concentrations and 200 mg of KBr powder were ground uniformly in an agate mortar, compressed into thin slices under a pressure of 50 MPa. Structural characterization of the interaction between PSP and TTMP

Particle size and zeta potential

The average particle size and zeta potential of PT with different concentrations (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 150, 200, and 300 μM) were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS, Anton Paar Litesizer 500, Anton Paar GmbH). Briefly, 1 mL of samples with different concentrations was placed in a 10 mm optical path cuvette. The dynamic light scattering mode was selected and scanned 8 times. The equilibration time was 60 s, the temperature was 25 °C, and the measurement was repeated 3 times.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

This method was slightly modified based on previous research methods (Zhou et al., 2024). Briefly, 0.1 g of PT powders with different concentrations (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 150, 200, and 300 μM) was sprinkled into the sample cell, ensuring that the loose sample powder was slightly higher than the surface of the sample holder; then a ground glass slide was used to gently press the surface of the sample. Detection was performed using an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Avance), and the specific test conditions were as follows: accelerating voltage = 40 kV; current = 40 mA; test speed = 0.05°/s; angle scanning range = 4°–40°.

Quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring (QCM-D)

This method was slightly modified based on previous studies (Yang et al., 2025). Briefly, the adsorption behavior between PSP and TTMP was investigated using a Q-Sense Omni (Biolin Scientific AB, Sweden). Before spin-coating, the gold sensor (QSX 301, 5 MHz) was cleaned with a “piranha” solution composed of Millipore water, 25% ammonia, and 30% hydrogen peroxide at a ratio of 5:1:1 at 75 °C for 10 min. The sensor was rinsed with deionized water and dried under a stream of nitrogen. Deionized water was used to establish a signal baseline. PSP (0.5 mg/mL) and TTMP ethanol solution (0.5 mg/mL) were applied for adsorption. At 25 °C, the changes in frequency (Δf) and energy dissipation (ΔD) over time were observed at a flow rate of 100 μL/min for approximately 45 min. The data were analyzed using Q-Sense modeling software (QTools). The thickness (h) results were evaluated based on 3–9 overtones using the Voigt model.

Surface hydrophobicity

This method was slightly modified based on previous studies (Wu et al., 2019). Briefly, 20 μL of ANS solution (20 mM, PBS) was added to 5 mL of PT solutions with different concentrations (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 150, 200, and 300 μM), vortexed to mix uniformly, and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Full-wavelength scanning was performed using a fluorescence spectrometer (RF-6000), and the fluorescence parameters included: excitation wavelength of 390 nm, emission wavelength scanning range of 400–600 nm, and slit width of 5 nm.

Intermolecular interaction between PSP and TTMP

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed based on previously reported methods with minor modifications. The crystal structure of PSP (PDB ID: 2E9Q; resolution, 2.20 Å) was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/). and the three-dimensional structure of TTMP was generated using ChemDraw 2023.1. The protein structure was preprocessed in PyMOL 2.6 by removing water molecules and phosphate groups. Receptor and ligand files were then prepared using AutoDockTools 1.5.6 (Wei et al., 2025) including hydrogen addition, ligand torsion assignment, and docking box setup. Docking was carried out using AutoDock Vina, and the optimal binding pose was selected according to the docking score. The docking results were visualized using Discovery Studio 2019 and PyMOL 2.6.

Molecular dynamics (MD)

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022. The protein was parameterized with the CHARMM36 force field, while ligand parameters were generated using the GAFF2 force field via Antechamber with RESP charges (Abraham et al., 2015). The protein–ligand complex was solvated in a TIP3P water box with a 1.0 nm buffer, and Na+ and Cl− ions were added to a final concentration of 0.15 M. Long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method, and bond constraints were applied using the LINCS algorithm. After energy minimization by the steepest descent method, a 100 ns MD simulation was conducted under the NPT ensemble with a 2 fs time step at 310 K and 1 bar, controlled by the Nosé–Hoover thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat, respectively (Wang et al., 2022). Trajectory analysis included root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen bonds, radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA). Binding free energy was further estimated using the MM-PBSA method.

Results and discussion

Multispectral analysis of the interaction between PSP and TTMP

UV–vis spectroscopy analysis

UV–Vis spectrophotometry reveals protein conformational changes and binding effects with small molecules via shifts in peak position and intensity. As shown in the Fig. 1A, with the gradual increase of TMP concentration gradient, the UV absorption spectrum of PSP exhibited significant concentration-dependent changes. In the wavelength range of 200–220 nm, the intensity of the characteristic peak showed a decreasing trend. This may be due to the induced protein conformational compaction after TMP binds to PSP, which reduces the exposure of peptide bonds in polar aqueous solution. Meanwhile, the reconstruction of the hydrogen bond network decreases the molar absorptivity of π → π* transition of peptide bonds, indicating the rearrangement of the protein secondary structure (Wang, Li, Wei, Yin, & Ding, 2022). In addition, in the absorption region of aromatic amino acid residues (250–300 nm), the characteristic peak of pure PSP at 261 nm gradually redshifted to 278 nm with the increase of TMP concentration, suggesting a significant increase in the hydrophobicity of the microenvironment where aromatic residues such as tyrosine and tryptophan are located, and the residues are encapsulated into the hydrophobic interior of the protein (Xu, Liu, Wang, Xu, & Han, 2026). At the same time, the absorption intensity in this region increased, suggesting that TMP binding changed the microenvironment of aromatic residues in PSP. Moreover, a charge-transfer complex may form between TMP and PSP, further enhancing the absorption contribution. These results demonstrate that the synergistic changes in peak redshift and absorption intensity confirm the specific binding between TMP and PSP, which induces the conformational rearrangement of the protein toward a more compact state with a concentration-dependent binding effect.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy analysis

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy uses Trp/Tyr residues as probes to reflect binding-site microenvironments and protein conformational changes via shifts in fluorescence intensity and emission wavelength. As shown in Fig. 2A–C, when the concentration of TTMP increased from 0 to 300 μM, the fluorescence intensity of PSP was significantly quenched in a concentration-dependent manner. These results indicate that TTMP interacts with PSP and alters the microenvironment surrounding the tryptophan residues, resulting in fluorescence quenching. At different temperatures (298, 304, and 310 K), the maximum fluorescence intensity showed a decreasing trend, suggesting that temperature affected the conformation of PSP and its interaction with TTMP. Temperature-induced structural rearrangement may alter the exposure and local environment of tryptophan residues, thereby influencing the fluorescence response. As shown in Fig. 2D–F, the maximum emission wavelength of PSP exhibited a significant blueshift with increasing temperature, indicating that the microenvironment of tryptophan residues became less polar and more hydrophobic. This result suggests that temperature induced conformational rearrangement of PSP, although the detailed structural basis requires further verification (Zhang, Wang, Song, Yu, & Wang, 2022).

Fluorescence quenching mechanism and binding constants

In this study, the fluorescence quenching behavior of TTMP and PSP at 298 K, 304 K, and 310 K was systematically analyzed by fitting the Stern-Volmer equation and double logarithmic equation. As shown in the Stern-Volmer curve (Fig. S1A), F0/F exhibited a good linear relationship with TTMP concentration at all tested temperatures (R2 > 0.99), indicating that the quenching behavior within the studied concentration range could be well described by the Stern–Volmer model. As summarized in Table 1, with increasing temperature from 298 to 310 K, the Ksv decreased from 1.24 ± 0.02 × 104 M−1 to 0.96 ± 0.03 × 104 M−1, while the Kq decreased correspondingly from 1.24 ± 0.02 × 1012 to 0.96 ± 0.03 × 1012 M−1 s−1. At all temperatures, the Kq values were much higher than the maximum diffusion-controlled quenching constant (2 × 1010 M−1 s−1). Together with the decrease in Ksv with increasing temperature, these results suggest that the fluorescence quenching of PSP by TTMP was predominantly governed by a static quenching mechanism, likely involving the formation of a ground-state non-fluorescent complex rather than collisional deactivation in the excited state. In addition, the binding constant (Ka) decreased from 3.04 ± 0.62 × 105 M−1 to 1.82 ± 0.55 × 105 M−1 as the temperature increased, suggesting that the binding process was exothermic and that elevated temperature reduced complex stability, possibly by weakening hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding (Liu et al., 2021). The calculated binding site number (n) ranged from 1.34 to 1.41, suggesting the presence of one predominant binding site and an approximately 1:1 binding stoichiometry between PSP and TTMP. Moreover, the double-logarithmic plots showed good linearity (R2 > 0.98; Fig. S1B), supporting the reliability of the estimated Ka and n values.

Thermodynamic parameter analysis

Thermodynamic parameters (ΔH, ΔS, ΔG) reveal the spontaneity, driving forces, and binding modes of protein–small molecule interactions. As shown in Table 1, ΔG was negative at all tested temperatures (298, 304, and 310 K), indicating that the binding of TTMP to PSP was spontaneous. In addition, the absolute value of ΔG decreased with increasing temperature, suggesting that higher temperatures made the binding less thermodynamically favorable, which was consistent with the observed decrease in Ka. Based on commonly used thermodynamic criteria, the negative values of both ΔH and ΔS suggest that hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions likely played dominant roles in the binding process (Yong, Huang, Wei, Xu, & Yi, 2020). The negative ΔH indicates that the binding was exothermic, whereas the negative ΔS may be associated with increased conformational ordering of the protein–ligand complex and solvent reorganization during binding (Semenova et al., 2020). Therefore, the interaction between PSP and TTMP can be considered a spontaneous and exothermic process in which hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions are likely the major contributors. The decrease in binding favorability with increasing temperature is also consistent with a static quenching mechanism.

FTIR spectroscopy detects intermolecular interactions and protein conformational changes through peak shifts and intensity variations. As shown in Fig. S2, pure TTMP exhibited characteristic peaks at 3223.91 cm−1, 2994.90 cm−1, 1642.08 cm−1, 1363.91 cm−1, 1228.91 cm−1, and 988.33 cm−1, corresponding to O—H stretching, asymmetric C—H stretching, C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="20.666667pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 20.666667 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.019444,-0.019444)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> N stretching, symmetric C—H bending, C—N stretching, and out-of-plane bending of the pyrazine ring, respectively (Yin, Chen, Luo, & Sun, 2021). These peaks reflect the characteristic vibrational features of TTMP. When PSP interacted with TTMP at different concentrations (Fig. 1B), the amide A (3272.60 cm−1), amide I (1629.55 cm−1), and amide II (1515.29 cm−1) bands of PSP shifted to different extents, indicating that specific non-covalent interactions occurred between TTMP and the peptide bond region of PSP. These spectral changes suggest that hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions may be involved, thereby altering the local microenvironment of the peptide bonds and inducing conformational rearrangement of PSP. Therefore, the FTIR results support the formation of a PSP–TTMP complex through non-covalent interactions.

To further understand the effect of these interactions on the secondary structure of PSP, Gaussian fitting of the amide I region (1600–1700 cm−1) was performed. As shown in Fig. 1C, when the concentration of TTMP increased from 20 to 80 μM, the α-helix content fluctuated between 24.32% and 26.72%, while the β-sheet content varied from 27.68% to 30.41%, showing a slight overall decrease compared with pure PSP. This suggests that low concentrations of TTMP had a limited effect on PSP secondary structure. When the TTMP concentration increased from 100 to 150 μM, the α-helix content increased from 25.17% to 27.91%, whereas the β-sheet content decreased from 29.65% to 26.17%, suggesting a secondary-structure rearrangement toward a more ordered conformation. At higher TTMP concentrations (200–300 μM), the α-helix content decreased slightly from 27.16% to 26.37% but remained higher than that of pure PSP, while the β-sheet content increased from 27.12% to 28.16% but remained lower than that of the control. These results indicate that excessive TTMP may induce dynamic conformational adjustments in PSP, leading to a new structural balance.

Structural characterization analysis of the interaction between PSP and TTMP

Particle size and zeta potential

Particle size and zeta potential reflect the formation, aggregation behavior, and colloidal stability of protein–small-molecule complexes. As shown in Fig. 1D and E, when the TTMP concentration ranged from 20 to 80 μM, the particle size of PT-20 was slightly smaller than that of pure PSP, and then gradually increased, reaching a maximum at PT-60 (236.48 ± 16.43 nm), accompanied by an obvious rightward shift in the particle size distribution peak. In addition, the absolute zeta potential value of the complexes first increased and then decreased relative to pure PSP (Fig. 1F), suggesting an increase in surface negative charge and enhanced electrostatic effects. The concomitant increase in particle size implies that, in this concentration range, attractive intermolecular interactions may still dominate, thereby promoting moderate aggregation of PSP. When the TTMP concentration was between 100 and 150 μM, the particle size of the complexes remained in the range of 240–260 nm, and the particle size distribution peak became narrower, indicating improved aggregate uniformity and a relatively stable aggregation state. Consistent with this, the absolute zeta potential values in this range were still significantly higher than those of pure PSP (Fig. 1F), suggesting that the balance between intermolecular attraction and surface electrostatic repulsion may contribute to the maintenance of aggregate stability. When the TTMP concentration further increased to 200–300 μM, the particle size of the complexes significantly decreased to below 211 nm (Fig. 1D), and the particle size distribution peak shifted leftward and became narrower, suggesting partial disassembly or reduced aggregation of the complexes at high TTMP concentrations. Meanwhile, the absolute zeta potential value of the PT-300 group significantly increased to 26.33 ± 4.05 mV, indicating that excessive TTMP enhanced the electrostatic repulsion at the complex surface, inhibited re-aggregation, and thereby improved the dispersion stability of the colloidal system (Xu et al., 2025).

XRD analysis

XRD reveals changes in molecular packing, crystallinity, and ordered structure of proteins upon interaction with bioactive small molecules. As shown in Fig. S3, pure TTMP exhibited sharp characteristic diffraction peaks at 10.12°, 13.02°, 15.29°, 26.70°, and 41.02°, indicating its well-defined crystalline structure. In contrast, pure PSP showed broad diffuse peaks (Fig. 3A), suggesting a predominantly amorphous nature. QCM-D analysis

QCM-D is a highly sensitive technique for characterizing interfacial interactions between biomacromolecules and bioactive small molecules. It enables real-time monitoring of changes in Δf and ΔD during the adsorption process, thereby reflecting the mass, thickness, and viscoelastic properties of the adsorption layer and providing dynamic evidence for analyzing the binding behavior between TTMP and PSP (Ye, Zhang, & Meng, 2022). In this experiment, a stable baseline was first established using deionized water [38]. As shown in Fig. 3B, the fluctuations in Δf and ΔD were close to zero, indicating good baseline stability. After the introduction of TTMP and PSP solutions, Δf decreased rapidly (blue curve), while ΔD increased synchronously (red curve), and the rapid adsorption stage (Phase I) was completed within 600 s. This result indicates that the interaction between TTMP and PSP on the gold sensor surface was initiated quickly. In Phase II (600–3600 s), the decreasing rate of Δf gradually slowed and approached a plateau, while ΔD remained at a relatively high level, suggesting that the mass of the adsorption layer continued to increase and that its structure gradually stabilized. In Phase III (after 3600 s), after rinsing with deionized water, no significant fluctuations in Δf or ΔD were observed, indicating that the remaining adsorbed molecules were mainly strongly bound, whereas loosely bound components had been effectively removed. As shown in Table 2, the Δf of the PT system was −19.08 ± 0.008 Hz, corresponding to an increase in the mass of the adsorption layer. The ΔD value was 0.43 ± 0.003 × 10−6, indicating that the adsorption layer possessed a certain degree of viscoelasticity. The thickness of the adsorption layer was 2.55 ± 0.006 nm, further confirming the formation of a stable adsorption layer. The negative shift in Δf and the positive change in ΔD suggest that TTMP interacted with PSP and formed an interfacial composite layer with both mass and viscoelastic characteristics. Combined with conformational analyses such as XRD and FTIR, these results further elucidate the dynamic binding behavior between TTMP and PSP.

Surface hydrophobicity analysis

Surface hydrophobicity is an important indicator of protein conformational changes and intermolecular interactions, as it reflects the exposure of hydrophobic residues upon binding with bioactive small molecules. As shown in Fig. 3C, TTMP had a clear concentration-dependent effect on the surface hydrophobicity of PSP. As the TTMP concentration increased from 0 to 300 μM, the fluorescence intensity associated with surface hydrophobicity gradually decreased, suggesting that TTMP binding induced the burial or shielding of hydrophobic residues in PSP. This trend is consistent with the general behavior of bioactive small molecules in modulating protein hydrophobicity. At low concentrations (0–80 μM), the decrease in hydrophobicity may be mainly attributed to hydrophobic interactions between TTMP and the hydrophobic regions of PSP. At medium concentrations (100–150 μM), the further reduction in hydrophobicity may indicate the involvement of additional non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, in stabilizing the PSP–TTMP complex. At high concentrations (200–300 μM), the more pronounced decrease may be related to the further shielding of hydrophobic sites or steric effects caused by excess TTMP, which may contribute to reduced protein aggregation.

Morphological characterization analysis of the interaction between PSP and TTMP

SEM analysis

SEM visualizes the micromorphology and concentration-dependent structural changes of protein–small-molecule complexes. As shown in Fig. 4A, pure PSP exhibited irregular block-like aggregates with a rough surface and small protrusions, suggesting a compact and disordered molecular arrangement. At a low TTMP concentration, PT-20 changed from the initial aggregated state to a lamellar structure with slightly curled edges (Fig. 4B), which may reflect weak interactions between TTMP and PSP and slight conformational adjustments of the protein. At a moderately low concentration, the lamellar structure of PT-60 gradually fractured and showed fragmentation with particle attachment (Fig. 4C), indicating the onset of structural rearrangement and mild aggregation. At the intermediate concentration, PT-100 exhibited a further reduction in lamellar structures, accompanied by an increase in fragmented and aggregated particles (Fig. 4D), suggesting enhanced PSP–TTMP interactions and more pronounced conformational reorganization. At the highest concentration, the lamellar structure of PT-300 was markedly disrupted and replaced by a porous network-like morphology (Fig. 4E), implying that excessive TTMP promoted the breakdown of compact aggregates and the reorganization of the protein into a looser structure.

AFM analysis

AFM revealed TTMP-induced changes in PSP surface morphology via 2D and 3D imaging. As shown in Fig. 4F and K, pure PSP exhibited a peak height of 12.0 nm and an Rq value of 3.53 ± 0.04 nm, indicating a relatively uniform surface with mild undulations. This feature may reflect a balance between electrostatic repulsion and weak intermolecular interactions among PSP molecules. After treatment with a low concentration of TTMP (20 μL; Fig. 4G and L), the peak height increased to 16.5 nm, whereas the Rq value slightly decreased to 3.43 ± 0.06 nm. This change may be associated with partial binding of TTMP to PSP, which induced conformational rearrangement and localized aggregation, resulting in protruded structures while slightly reducing overall surface roughness. At a moderately low TTMP concentration (60 μL; Fig. 4H and M), the peak height decreased markedly to 10.4 nm and the Rq value dropped significantly to 2.79 ± 0.05 nm, indicating a flatter surface. This may be attributed to more extensive PSP–TTMP interactions, which possibly weakened PSP self-association driven by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions and improved molecular dispersion. At a medium TTMP concentration (100 μL; Fig. 4I and N), the peak height slightly rebounded to 12.4 nm, while the Rq value further decreased to 2.61 ± 0.07 nm, suggesting a dynamic balance between intermolecular attraction and repulsive interactions, with dispersibility being further improved. At a high TTMP concentration (300 μL; Fig. 4J and O), the peak height remained at 10.4 nm and the Rq value reached its minimum, indicating the formation of a more homogeneous and flatter surface. These results suggest that TTMP progressively suppressed PSP aggregation and enhanced surface uniformity. Such changes may have weakened intermolecular interactions among PSP molecules, thereby favoring a more extended conformation and greater surface exposure (Hui Wang et al., 2022).

Analysis of intermolecular interactions between PSP and TTMP

Molecular docking

Molecular docking reveals the interaction mechanism between PSP and TTMP at the molecular level. As shown in the Fig. 5A, the docking results of 11S and TTMP revealed a stable and highly specific binding complex with an optimal binding energy of −3.5 kcal/mol, indicating that the complex had strong affinity and a synergistic effect formed by non-covalent interactions, which together constructed a dense interaction network (Lu et al., 2024). From the binding mode (Fig. 5B), it can be seen that the ASP164 and ASP162 residues on the 11S receptor formed hydrogen bond interactions with TTMP, and the ARG321, VAL166, ASN165, THR401, ALA61, and HIS344 residues on the 11S receptor formed van der Waals interactions with TTMP. The partial enlarged drawing showed that two hydrogen bonds were formed between the side chains of ARG-A:164 and ASP-162 and TTMP, with bond lengths of 2.4 Å and 2.3 Å, respectively.

Molecular dynamics (MD)

MD simulation reveals dynamic interactions and conformational changes in protein–ligand complexes beyond the static picture provided by docking. RMSD is commonly used to evaluate the conformational stability of proteins and ligands and to measure the deviation of atomic positions from their initial coordinates (Sargsyan, Grauffel, & Lim, 2017). As shown in Fig. 5C, the complex reached a relatively stable state between 60 and 90 ns and displayed moderate fluctuations after 90 ns; however, the RMSD remained below 4 Å throughout the simulation, suggesting the absence of major conformational drift and overall structural stability of the PSP–TTMP complex.

In addition, the Rg reflects changes in the overall structure and compactness of the protein (Zeng et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 5D, the Rg of the complex fluctuated within a narrow range of 23–24 Å throughout the 100 ns simulation, indicating that the overall compactness of the complex was well maintained. Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) is an indicator of the solvent-exposed surface area of a protein (Zhang et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 5E, the SASA value of the PSP–TTMP complex remained around 20,000 Å2, indicating that TTMP binding did not induce substantial rearrangement of the protein surface. RMSF reflects the flexibility of individual amino acid residues (Yingcong Lu, Zhao, Wang, Zhang, & Wang, 2022). As shown in Fig. 5F, most residues exhibited relatively small fluctuations, supporting the overall stability of the complex, whereas residues around position 100 showed higher flexibility, with RMSF values approaching 6 Å. This suggests the presence of a locally flexible region that may participate in ligand-induced conformational adaptation. The FEL, constructed using RMSD and Rg as reaction coordinates, showed a concentrated low-energy basin (Fig. 5G), indicating that the complex predominantly adopted a thermodynamically favorable conformational state during the simulation (Li & Minkara, 2024). Based on the representative binding conformation, the binding free energy between TTMP and PSP was further calculated using the MM/PBSA method (Fig. S4A and B). The calculated binding free energy was −5.77 kcal/mol, indicating that the interaction was energetically favorable. Residue decomposition analysis further identified CYS27, CYS60, HIS344, and GLN381 as major contributors to ligand binding (Fig. 4B), suggesting that these residues play important roles in stabilizing the PSP–TTMP complex.

Analysis of functional characteristics of the interaction between PSP and TTMP

Antioxidant activity

Antioxidant activity is an important indicator for evaluating the functional enhancement of the PT system. As shown in Fig. 6A, pure PSP exhibited relatively low antioxidant activity, while the antioxidant activity of the PT system gradually increased with increasing TTMP concentration. At low TTMP concentrations, TTMP may have induced slight conformational changes in PSP, thereby exposing some previously buried antioxidant-related groups, such as thiol groups and tyrosine residues, which partially improved the antioxidant activity. At medium and high TTMP concentrations, the antioxidant activity significantly increased to 31.09 ± 1.34%. This result suggests that TTMP may enhance the antioxidant activity of the PT system through two possible mechanisms. On the one hand, the pyrazine ring of TTMP may directly contribute to free radical scavenging by donating electrons or hydrogen atoms, while its conjugated structure may stabilize the resulting radical species through resonance, thereby improving its antioxidant efficiency. On the other hand, TTMP may interact with PSP through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, leading to conformational changes in PSP and further exposing active groups such as thiol groups and tyrosine residues.

Anti-glycation activity

α-Glucosidase inhibition reflects the hypoglycemic potential of protein–bioactive complexes and indicates bioactive-induced conformational changes at functional sites. As shown in Fig. 6B, the α-glucosidase inhibitory activity of pure PSP was only 9.43 ± 1.59%. With increasing TTMP concentration, the inhibitory activity of the PT system showed a concentration-dependent increasing trend. Specifically, the inhibitory activity of the PT-300 group reached 26.69 ± 2.03%, which was approximately 2.8-fold that of pure PSP. This result suggests that, at low TTMP concentrations, TTMP may partially bind to PSP and induce preliminary conformational rearrangement of the protein, thereby exposing some functional groups associated with enzyme inhibition. Meanwhile, TTMP itself may also interact weakly with the enzyme through its pyrazine ring, thus contributing to the overall inhibitory effect. As the TTMP concentration further increased, the binding of TTMP to PSP likely approached saturation, and the protein structure may have become more extended, exposing more inhibitory-related sites. At the same time, the interaction between TTMP and the enzyme may also have been strengthened, resulting in an enhanced combined effect and a continuous increase in inhibitory activity. Under high-concentration conditions, as the available binding sites gradually approached saturation, the increase in inhibitory activity tended to level off.

Emulsifying stability

EAI measures protein adsorption at the oil–water interface, and ESI reflects emulsion long-term stability. As shown in Fig. 6C, pure PSP exhibited the highest interfacial adsorption capacity. With increasing TTMP concentration, the EAI gradually decreased, reaching 32.95 ± 2.55 m2/g in the PT-300 group. This result suggests that the interaction between TTMP and PSP may have altered the protein conformation, molecular flexibility, and interfacial adsorption behavior, thereby weakening its ability to rapidly adsorb at the oil–water interface (Kan et al., 2024). In terms of ESI, pure PSP showed a value of 73.38 ± 0.73%, indicating relatively good emulsion stability. At low TTMP concentrations (20–80 μL), the ESI showed some fluctuations but exhibited an overall downward trend, suggesting that the effect of TTMP on emulsion stability was relatively complex at this stage. This may be due to the partial disturbance of the original interfacial behavior of PSP, while the amount of TTMP was still insufficient to form a stable interfacial network. However, at medium (100–150 μL) and high (200–300 μL) TTMP concentrations, the ESI gradually increased and reached 92.93 ± 2.77% in the PT-300 group. This finding indicates that, at higher TTMP levels, the PT complex may form a denser and more stable interfacial film, which helps prevent droplet aggregation and thus significantly improves emulsion stability.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive picture of the interaction between TTMP and pumpkin seed protein and its effect on the structural and functional behavior of the protein.The combined evidence from UV–Vis, fluorescence spectroscopy, FTIR, XRD, QCM-D, particle characterization, and microscopic observations demonstrated that TTMP does not simply coexist with PSP, but forms a relatively stable complex through specific non-covalent interactions. The fluorescence quenching data and thermodynamic analysis indicated that the binding process was spontaneous, exothermic, and mainly governed by hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces, with static quenching as the dominant mechanism. These findings were further supported by molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation, which identified several key residues involved in ligand stabilization and confirmed the conformational stability of the PSP-TTMP complex during dynamic evolution.

More importantly, TTMP induced a concentration-dependent conformational reorganization of PSP. The observed changes in secondary structure, surface hydrophobicity, particle size distribution, and interfacial adsorption behavior collectively indicate that PSP transitioned from a native aggregated state toward a more rearranged and, at higher TTMP levels, more dispersed configuration. This structural modulation was closely coupled to functional alterations. The PT complexes exhibited markedly enhanced antioxidant capacity and stronger α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, suggesting that TTMP binding may expose or reorganize active sites within PSP while also contributing inherent bioactivity. Although the emulsifying activity index declined upon TTMP addition, the substantially improved emulsion stability at medium-to-high TTMP concentrations indicates that the remodeled interfacial architecture more effectively resisted droplet coalescence.

These findings demonstrate a viable strategy to expand the application of TTMP in food systems by binding it to a plant protein matrix, which may help overcome its inherent volatility, while also extending the utility of pumpkin seed protein beyond its conventional nutritional role and highlighting its potential as a multifunctional ingredient for flavor retention, colloidal stabilization, and functional enhancement. Overall, this study advances the mechanistic understanding of natural aroma compound–plant protein interactions and provides a theoretical basis for designing cleaner-label, plant-based formulations that combine desirable flavor with enhanced bioactivity.
