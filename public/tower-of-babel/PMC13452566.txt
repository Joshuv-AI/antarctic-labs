A Lipid‐Conjugation Strategy for Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species Control in Hepatic Cells
Authors: Olav Vestrheim, Huichao Zhao, Anders Bodholt Nielsen, Nino Wili, Niels Chr Nielsen, Joseph A Lyons, Rajeshwar Prosad Mookerjee, Brigitte Städler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSynthetic catalytic antioxidants offer attractive alternatives to enzymatic redox regulators, yet their biological application is frequently limited by poor stability, formulation challenges, and insufficient control over intracellular localization. Here, we report a lipid conjugation strategy that enables the covalent integration of manganese Salen (EUK) catalysts into phospholipid membranes. A modular synthetic route is established in which a carboxylate‐functionalized EUK derivative is coupled to an amine‐terminated phospholipid tail, yielding a structurally defined phospholipid‐EUK conjugate that retains catalytic activity upon liposome formulation, with controlled catalyst loading and long‐term colloidal integrity. The resulting liposomes exhibit efficient catalytic degradation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) with activity scaling with conjugate content. Importantly, covalent anchoring of the catalyst within the lipid bilayer prevents aggregation and precipitation observed for non‐conjugated analogues. Using an acetaminophen challenged steatotic HepaRG cell model, we demonstrate that lipid conjugation enables intracellular delivery of the catalyst and sustained reduction of elevated ROS levels without inducing cytotoxicity. This effort establishes a chemically precise approach for positioning organometallic catalysts within biomimetic membranes and highlights these conjugates as versatile platforms for controlled intracellular redox modulation.Keywords: intracellular ROS, lipid conjugates, Salen manganese complexes, steatotic HepaRG cells

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide (O2 − ·), and hydroxyl radical (HO·) are naturally formed through many biological processes. Overproduction of these can lead to oxidative stress and cellular damage making them a major contributor to many medical conditions including cancer, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinsons disease, cardiovascular, and liver diseases among others [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Superoxide dismutase and catalase are enzymes which catalyze dismutation reactions reducing these damaging species to innocuous products (oxygen and water). However, when these enzymes together with other antioxidant compounds such a glutathione are unable to remove a sufficient amount of ROS, oxidative stress occur [8, 9]. Many antioxidant therapies have been considered to treat these disorders with varying degrees of success. An example is the use of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase, where the main issues impacting therapeutic efficacy include difficulties with delivery, protein stability and immunogenic responses [8, 10, 11]. Stoichiometric amounts of antioxidants such as vitamin C and E, flavonoids and curcumin are another avenue, which have been explored, but require large doses for the treatment to be effective [5, 6]. Synthetic catalytic antioxidants represent a third alternative, offering unique design features such as tunable structures and smaller sizes, which make them easier to adapt and deliver compared to natural enzymes [8, 12, 13, 14]. There are multiple reports of catalytic antioxidants, most of which are metal complexes containing either iron, copper or manganese. Common examples of these are metalloporphyrins [15, 16, 17] and salen complexes [13, 14, 15] as well as a large variety of nanozymes [18, 19, 20]. The manganese versions of these can perform catalytic reductions of both superoxide and hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, which provides benefits over other compounds that can only reduce one of these oxidative species [8, 21]. The manganese Salen complexes (often referred to as EUKs) [13, 22, 23, 24] are of particular interest as their relatively simple synthesis makes them widely accessible [8, 13, 14]. EUKs have demonstrated protective effects in a wide range of oxidative stress‐related disease models [13, 25, 26, 27, 28]. EUK‐8 have shown neuroprotection in models of Parkinson's disease [29] and suppressed the development of paralysis in mice with multiple sclerosis [30]. EUK‐134 has proven effective in rodent stroke and seizure models [22]. EUK‐189 and EUK‐207 have been used to prevent cell death in bovine adrenal endothelial cells exposed to radiation [31]. Multiple other EUKs have also been employed in studies focusing on modulating redox signaling and preventing cell injury caused by oxidative stress [13, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Despite their promising therapeutic profile, the potential of EUKs remains limited by formulation challenges. Most EUKs are hydrophobic and lack functional chemical handles, hindering their conjugation to delivery vehicles or targeting ligands [8]. Their administration as free compounds compromises stability, biodistribution, and intracellular efficacy. Attempts to encapsulate EUKs in nanocarriers such as liposomes have shown only some improvements [32], suggesting that the potential for developing more advanced delivery strategies could radically improve their catalytic potential in biologically relevant environments.

Over recent years, we aimed to chemically diversify EUK compounds with focus on formulation opportunities. For instance, EUK was embedded in alginate‐based microgels and extracellular protection from ROS related stress in peroxide‐challenged hepatic cells was demonstrated [26, 28]. From a different perspective, we have previously observed that tail‐modified fluorescent lipids could be shuttled to the lipid droplets in steatotic HepG2 cells, while head‐modified fluorescent lipids remained trapped in the lysosomes [27]. Therefore, we have formulated an alkene functionalized EUK into liposomes followed by crosslinking to an acrylate functionalized lipid. When these formulations were internalized by steatotic HepG2 cells, the transition metal catalysts have been shuttled to intracellular lipid droplets using the cell's native transport mechanism for protection against ROS without compromising the short‐term cell viability [27]. In this past effort, the fact that we coupled the EUK to the tail of the lipid after being formulated into liposomes, is a substantial limitation. This approach offered limited control over both the type and quantity of the delivered product. On the one hand, unintended side products could be co‐encapsulated within the liposomes. On the other hand, the conjugation efficiency was unlikely to reach completion and was dependent on experimental parameters and equipment (e.g., the UV lamp), which resulted in final composition variations between the individual liposomes in a sample. In addition, although widely used, HepG2 cells provide only a limited representation of hepatic physiology, as they exhibit an immature metabolic phenotype, reduced expression of key drug‐metabolizing enzymes, and altered lipid handling compared to primary human hepatocytes.

Therefore, we report a new synthetic strategy for generating phospholipid‐EUK conjugates and evaluate their capacity to reduce intracellular ROS in steatotic HepaRG cells, an in vitro model that more closely recapitulates human liver function, when formulated as liposomes (Scheme 1). Specifically, we (i) identifed a suitable synthesis strategy to conjugate an EUK derivative to the tail of phospholipids, (ii) determined the catalytic activity of liposomes equipped with these EUK‐lipid conjugates, (iii) established a 2D steatotic HepaRG cell model to evaluate the intracellular fate of fluorescent lipids, and (iv) illustrated that the intracellular elevated ROS levels were lowered for cells with internalized EUK‐lipid conjugates.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis

Phospholipids represent a fundamental class of amphiphilic molecules that constitute the structural basis of biological membranes and play an important role in cellular signaling, metabolism, and molecular transport. The most common structural features contain a glycerol backbone esterified with two fatty acid chains and a phosphate‐containing headgroup, typically choline, ethanolamine, or serine [33]. The synthetic access to phospholipid structures, particularly lysophosphatidylcholines and mixed‐chain phosphatidylcholines, remains a longstanding challenge due to the susceptibility of acyl migration and the amphiphilic nature of the molecules, which complicates purification [34, 35, 36]. Several non‐enzymatic routes have been developed to circumvent these limitations, including regioselective strategies based on orthogonal protection of glycerol precursors [37] and late‐stage acylation in addition to dibutyltin oxide‐mediated acylation of glycerophosphorylcholine [38] have enabled regioselective access to mixed‐chain phosphatidylcholines. These strategies enable precise structural control to support biochemical and therapeutic applications. By coupling transition metal complexes to engineered lipids the position of the catalyst is more defined, which can increase the spatial control over catalysis in mammalian cells. Inspired by our previous effort that highlighted the importance of a native lipid head group for the intracellular fate of the phospholipids in steatotic liver cells [27], we focused the current effort on attaching the EUK to the tail of the phospholipid. Consequently, we explored three different EUK derivatives and functional tail groups for efficient and easy coupling (Figure 1). EUK‐B and EUK‐A have previously been reported showing much higher catalytic activity toward degrading H2O2 compared to the commercially available EUK‐134 [24].

Pairs of manganese Salen complexes (EUKs) and phospholipids with a functional tail group considered for coupling. (a) EUK‐B and an acid functionalized lipid couples via a Steglich esterification at the phenolic hydroxyl group. (b) The alkyne functionalized EUK‐E and an azide functionalized lipid which can couple via a CuAAC. (c) The carboxylic acid functionalized EUK‐A can be attached to an amine functionalized lipid via a DCC coupling between the carboxylic acid on the EUK and the primary amine on the lipid tail.

The most successful of the three approaches (Figure 1) proved to be the coupling between the EUK‐A and the amine functionalized lipid (Figure 1c). The synthesis of the other two approaches is described in the supporting information. The synthesis of EUK‐A started with a simple imine condensation between the commercially available 3,4‐diaminobenzoic acid (1) and 3‐methoxy salicylaldehyde (2) to form the EUK‐A ligand (3) in 60% yield (Scheme 2a). The uncharged ligand was used in the coupling to the phospholipid in order to confirm the successful reaction via 1H‐NMR (as it is difficult to obtain the 1H‐NMR spectrum of the paramagnetic manganese charged species). To form the lipid, lysophosphatidylcholine (5) was coupled with the commercially available boc‐protected undecanoic acid (6) via a Steglich esterification as a protected amine handle for coupling the EUK ligand to the phospholipid. This reaction gave a yield of 56% when using the method with the glass beads [39] described in the supplementary information. The amine was then deprotected by using trifluoroacetic acid, and the removal of the boc‐group was confirmed by 1H‐NMR and mass spectrometry. The obtained product (8) was used without further purification. A DCC coupling between the deprotected amine of 8 and the carboxylic acid of the EUK‐A ligand (3) was then performed. During this step, utmost caution was used to avoid any moisture in the reaction to protect the imine bonds from hydrolysis. Specifically, the glassware was flame dried and 3Å molecular sieves were added. Due to the instability of the imine bonds we were unable purify and isolate the intermediate. After ∼2 h, manganese acetate was added to charge the EUK‐A ligand, and the reaction was left overnight, producing the final product 9 in 35% yield over two steps as a dark sticky solid referred to as EUK‐PC (Scheme 2b). We could observe the mass of the compound in mass spectrometry, and TLC indicated that the isolated compound was not the same as the precursors, 3 and 8.

Third synthesis route for the phospholipid‐EUK conjugate. (a) Synthesis of the EUK‐A ligand. (b) Synthesis of the amine functionalized lipid and the conjugation with the EUK‐A resulting in EUK‐PC (9).

The mass of the final compound (9) was detected by mass spectrometry, with the quadruply charged adduct with three sodium ions being the major peak in the mass spectrum with a mass to charge ratio of 304 Da. The singly charged ion was visible, although at very low intensity (Figures S1 and S2). In addition, the difference in the UV–vis absorbance spectra of the different species indicated conjugation and not only mixing (Figure S3). Specifically, the UV–vis absorbance spectra of EUK‐PC differed markedly from those of both the EUK‐A complex and the corresponding unconjugated ligand species. The spectral features of EUK‐PC cannot be reproduced by a linear combination of the precursor spectra, indicating that the observed absorbance profile did not arise from a simple mixture of starting materials. Instead, the shifts in absorbance bands were consistent with changes in the electronic structure of the Salen chromophore upon lipid conjugation. Importantly, analogous spectral differences were observed when comparing the uncharged ligand and its lipid‐coupled counterpart, suggesting that these changes originated from covalent modification of the ligand framework rather than solely from manganese coordination. While these data did not allow quantification of conversion, they provide supporting evidence for the formation of a chemically distinct EUK–lipid conjugate.

While 1H NMR is challenged by the presence of a paramagnetic centre, which can lead to paramagnetic shifts and/or linebroadening, we managed to record a 1H NMR spectrum of 9 at 1 mM concentration (Figure S4). The broadening effects appearing around the aromatic region of the Salen complex indicate the presence of the EUK‐conjugated lipid. It should be noted that we could not obtain a stable, purified uncharged intermediate for analysis by 1H NMR.

Taken together, the observed mass, the 1H NMR, the UV–vis spectral data, and the successful formation of stable liposomes (see below) provide strong evidence that the target compound was successfully obtained.

Catalytic Activity of the EUK‐PC in Liposome Formulations

EUK‐PC was incorporated into liposomes to facilitate its delivery into mammalian cells. The liposomes were formed via the rehydration method followed by extrusion through 400 and 200 nm filters, denoted as Lx_EUK where x represents the molar ratio between DOPC and EUK‐PC. The increased amount of EUK‐PC in the formulation was evident from the progressively darker brown coloration of the samples (Figure 2a). Additionally, samples containing liposomes in which EUK‐A was simply trapped in the membrane (L50_free EUK‐A) rather than lipid‐conjugated exhibited substantial precipitation, likely corresponding to aggregated EUK‐A, within 24 h of assemble illustrating the limited stability of this formulation. Liposome samples made with EUK‐PC did not show post‐assembly precipitation for up to 84 days, demonstrating the benefit of the lipid conjugation over physical entrapment. In addition, the formulations were analyzed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Table S2). All samples exhibited similar hydrodynamic diameters between 158 and 204 nm and displayed low polydispersity indices (0.08–0.17), indicative of a narrow liposome size distributions. In addition, the two formulations L20_EUK and L50_EUK were visualized by cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo‐EM) (Figure 2b). Finally, the ability of the different liposomes to degrade hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was assessed within 30 min and 3 h. Increasing amount of EUK‐PC in the liposomes resulting in lower amounts of remaining H2O2 (Figure 2c). Specifically, L20_EUK removed ∼20% of H2O2 after 30 min and ∼60% after 3 h, whereas L50_EUK removed ∼40% after 30 min and ∼70% after 3 h, whereas L0_EUK showed no detectable H2O2 removal ability.

Induction and Characterization of Steatotic HepaRG Cells

HepaRG cell line is a human bipotent progenitor cell line that is, capable of proliferating and differentiating into biliary cells and mature hepatocytes. Supplementation of 2% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to the culture medium triggers the differentiation of progenitor HepaRG cells into bile canaliculus‐like structures and more granular cells closely resembling typical adult primary hepatocytes [40]. Once differentiated, HepaRG cells exhibit diverse functions, including the expression of cytochrome C enzymes (CYPs) at levels comparable to those in primary hepatocytes [41]. This positions HepaRG cells as a viable model of primary human hepatocytes to study xenobiotic metabolism and toxicity. Free fatty acids (FFAs), including oleic acid and palmitic acid, which are the most abundant FFAs found in the human diet, are the most commonly used to induce steatosis in hepatocytes [42]. Alternative paradigms, such as insulin resistance and liver X receptor agonists that activate de novo lipogenesis [43], sugar overload (e.g., high fructose/glucose) [44], and drug‐induced lipid accumulation (e.g., amiodarone) [45], also can be used to mimic the development of steatosis. Notably, a recent study demonstrated that FFA treatment stimulated mitochondrial respiration and drove lipotoxicity in HepaRG cells, but not in HepG2 cells, suggesting that HepaRG cells are more suitable for studying mitochondrial respiratory adaptations for the early stage of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis [46].

Here, we established a 2D steatotic HepaRG model by first culturing the HepaRG cells in the progenitor state for one week, followed by a 2‐week treatment with 2% (DMSO) for differentiation. During the differentiation period, morphological changes were observed using a brightfield microscope, where the HepaRG cells transitioned from an elongated shape to a round cluster, which represented hepatocyte‐like cells. First, differentiation was confirmed by immunostaining of two protein markers. Compared to non‐differentiated HepaRG cells, HepaRG cells exposed to DMSO for two weeks showed robust expression of cytokeratin 19 (CK19, biliary cell marker) and hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4α, mature hepatocyte marker) (Figure S5). Steatosis was induced in the HepaRG cells by treating the differentiating HepaRG cells with FFA for either two or seven days (Figure 3a). Specifically, we used an FFA mixture of oleic acid and palmitic acid in a ratio of 3:1 [27]. First, the HepaRG cell viability was assessed when exposed to FFA compared to untreated HepaRG cells. The results indicated that FFA did not induce significant cytotoxicity in differentiated HepaRG cells for 2‐day FFA incubation for concentrations up to 2.4 mM. However, the HepaRG cell viability was significantly reduced after 7‐day FFA exposure for concentrations 1.2 mM and above (Figure 3b).

Differentiated HepaRG cells express a range of cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYPs), including CYP1A2, 2B6, 2C9, 2E1, and 3A4 at levels comparable to those observed in cultured primary human hepatocytes, which represent liver‐specific functions [40]. Therefore, the CYP1A2 activity in differentiated HepaRG cells was assessed by monitoring the dealkylation of non‐fluorescent resorufin ethyl ether (REE) into the fluorescent resorufin and compared to non‐differentiated HepaRG cells and differentiated steatotic HepaRG cells. As expected, all the differentiated HepaRG cells showed increasingly higher fluorescence intensity originating from resorufin over time compared to the non‐differentiated HepaRG cells (Figure 3ci). However, the treatment with FFA slowed the catalytic conversion, which represented partly impaired CYP1A2 activity, aligning with previous research that showed that oxidative stress and inflammation suppressed CYP1A2 expression in steatotic HepaRG cells [47]. In addition, the total amount of produced resorufin was calculated after 8 h of incubation with REE (Figure 3cii). The differentiated native HepaRG cells had an ∼2× higher resorufin production compared to differentiated steatotic HepaRG cells. Non‐differentiated HepaRG cells had a resorufin production below the detection limit. Consequently, it was concluded that a 2D steatotic, metablically active HepaRG cell model was obtained.

Subsequently, the level of steatosis of differentiated HepaRG cells was investigated by staining the lipid droplets and visualizing them using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). We compared an acute high‐dose FFA exposure (two days, 1.2 mM) with prolonged exposure to lower FFA concentrations (seven days, up to 1 mM). Treatment with 1.2 mM FFA for two and seven days incubation with 1 mM FFA successfully induced a marked intracellular lipid droplet accumulation compared to the untreated HepaRG cells, as indicated by the strong yellow fluorescence observed in the CLSM images (Figure 3di/ii). The ratio of yellow (lipid droplets) to the blue (nuclei) pixels in the images was calculated to obtain a more quantitative measure of the level of lipid droplet formation. This relative ratio significantly increased after seven days treatment with 1 mM FFA and two days treatment with 1.2 mM FFA compared to either the untreated HepaRG cells or the HepaRG cells exposed to only 0.6 mM FFA for seven days (Figure 3diii). Additionally, HepaRG cells were co‐stained with LipiBright SMCy5.5 and BODIPY to visualize lipid droplets simultaneously (Figure S6a). Interestingly, the CLSM images revealed incomplete co‐localization of these two dyes (Figure S6bi/ii), which suggested that lipid droplet staining can be probe‐dependent because of the heterogeneity in composition and function of lipid droplets. This is particularly evident when comparing conventional neutral‐lipid stains (e.g., BODIPY 493/503) with fluorogenic/solvatochromic probes (e.g., SMCy5.5), which preferentially light up in highly hydrophobic environments [48, 49].

Aerobic organisms have developed robust antioxidant defense systems of enzymatic and non‐enzymatic antioxidants against ROS to preserve cellular redox homeostasis [50]. In conditions of fat and added inflammation such as some forms of metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), decreased antioxidant defense is a driver that promotes the disease progression. Therefore, one characteristic of MASLD is elevated oxidative stress, marked by a disrupted redox environment and elevated ROS production [7]. HepaRG cells were stained with an intracellular ROS probe and a nuclear marker for visualization using CLSM (Figure 3e). The CLSM images showed that HepaRG cells treated with FFA exhibited increased intracellular ROS levels in all cases as indicated by the increased fluorescent signal originating from the ROS probe compared to untreated HepaRG cells (Figure 3ei/ii). Quantitative image analysis revealed an increasing ratio of green (ROS probe) to blue (nuclei) pixels, consistent with elevated ROS production in FFA‐treated HepaRG cells (Figure 3eiii). However, this increase was only statistically significant for HepaRG cells that were treated for seven days with 1 mM FFA.

In addition to the intracellular ROS levels, the impaired expression of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutases (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidases (GPx), and glutathione reductase (GSR), has important clinical implications [51, 52]. Therefore, the expression level of SOD2, CAT, GSR, and GPx4 was determined using real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) (Figure 3f). Two and seven days FFA treatment showed opposite results. A two days 1.2 mM FFA treatment (acute exposure) induced increased expression of all four genes, with SOD2 showing a significant upregulation. In contrast, a seven days 1 mM FFA treatment (prolonged exposure) induced modest downregulation of these genes, notably CAT, GSR, and GPx4. In addition, the nuclear factor erythroid 2 (NFE2)‐related factor 2 (Nrf2), which controls the basal and induced expression of antioxidant enzymes to regulate the physiological and pathophysiological outcomes of oxidant exposure [53], was also investigated, but no significant changes were observed for the FFA treatments. FFA treatment in hepatocytes triggers a time‐dependent remodeling of antioxidant defenses. After acute and short exposure to FFA, oxidative stress typically leads to a compensatory increase in mitochondrial and NFR2‐related antioxidants, such as SOD2, indicating the activation of cytoprotective programs [54]. However, as lipotoxic stress continues (prolonged FFA exposure), both preclinical and clinical results indicated a reduction in NRF2 expression, along with the downregulation of CAT, GPx4, and components of the glutathione system, including GSR [55]. In the progression of MASLD, this “early‐up, late‐down” pattern corresponds with rising oxidative damage and signaling related to ferroptosis [56].

Lastly, the mitochondrial transcript expression level of electron transport chain subunits in the steatotic HepaRG cells was quantified by RT‐qPCR with focus on NADH‐ubiquinone oxidoreductase chain one (ND1, a subunit of complex I), cytochrome c oxidase I and II (COXI and COXII) (Figure S7). However, 1 mM FFA treatment for one week did not result in statistically significant changes in gene expression compared to non‐steatotic HepaRG cells.

Inherent Lipid Transport in Steatotic HepaRG Cells

We have previously observed that formulated lipids could be transported from lysosomes to lipid droplets exclusively when the head group of the phospholipids was unmodified in steatotic HepG2 cells [27]. To further investigate the intracellular fate of lipids in differentiated and steatotic HepaRG cells, liposomes were formulated through the rehydration method with phospholipids labelled either on the tail or head group with fluorescent markers (Figure S8a). The resulting liposomes were denoted as Lyx, where x indicates the labelled lipid (e.g., DOPC for 2‐dioleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine, DOPE for 1,2‐dioleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine, and DPPC for 1,2‐dipalmitoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine), and y refers to the position of modification and the name of the fluorescent markers (e.g., tail‐NBD for nitrobenzodiazole on the tail group, head‐Rho for rhodamine on the head group) (Table S3). Specifically, xy represents tail‐labelled phospholipid, and xy represents head‐labelled phospholipids (e.g., DOPENBD for NBD‐labelled DOPE on the tail group, and DOPERho for Rho‐labelled DOPE on the head group). Additionally, none of the formulations induced significant cytotoxicity in steatotic HepaRG cells (Figure S11).

Subsequently, steatotic HepaRG cells with seven days FFA treatment were exposed to the fluorescent liposomes for 24 h before the intracellular fate of the fluorescent lipids was determined by lysosomal staining and imaging by CLSM. In all cases involving NBD tail‐labelled lipids (both Ltail-NBDDOPC and Ltail-NBDDPPC), these lipids were observed to escape the lysosomes, shown as minimal overlapping of lysosomal (white) and NBD (green) signals (Figure 4ai/ii), regardless of the lipid type used. Moreover, in healthy cells without FFA treatment, it was found that most Ltail-NBDDPPC were cleared from the cells, as indicated by the absence of green fluorescence (Figure S12), suggesting that cells without lipid droplet accumulation may selectively remove these phospholipids. Interestingly, when Ltail-R6GDPPC were used, the signal originating from DPPCR6G remained co‐localized in the lysosome, shown as the overlapping of white fluorescence (lysosome) and red fluorescence from the R6G on the tail group (Figure 4aiii). We speculate that the size of the fluorophore on the tail group may affect the behavior of the phospholipids, with the larger R6G moiety impairing lysosome escape. In addition, for formulation Ltail-NBD, head-RhoDOPE, a distinct pattern was observed where the DOPENBD successfully escaped the lysosomes, whereas the DOPERho remained trapped in the lysosomes (Figure 4bi). In contrast, for Lhead-NBD, head-RhoDOPE with only head‐labelled phospholipds, both DOPENBD and DOPERho were co‐localized with lysosomes (Figure 4bii), which suggested that an unmodified head group is essential for lysosomal escape, rather than the molecular size of the fluorophore on the head group affected the behavior. The visual observations in the CLSM images were supported by Pearson's correlation coefficient results, where only tail‐labelled phospholipids were able to be transported outside the lysosome, while head‐labelled phospholipids were largely trapped inside the lysosome (Figure 4c). It should be noted that steatotic HepaRG cells with two days FFA treatment showed comparable behavior (for details, see Figures S8–S10 and the related text).

ROS Scavenging of Liposome Formulations With EUK‐PC in Steatotic HepaRG Cells

Finally, we determined the ability Lx_EUK to lower the intracellular ROS levels in steatotic HepaRG cells with focus on the seven days FFA treatment. First, the short‐term cytotoxicity assessment of Lx_EUK in these steatotic HepaRG cells showed no statistically significant negative effect on their viability (Figure S13). However, L50_freeEUK‐A induced a modest reduction in cell viability, likely due to the precipitation of unencapsulated EUK‐A and was therefore, not further considered.

We next evaluated the intracellular ROS‐scavenging capacity of L20_EUK and L50_EUK compared to the pristine liposomes (L0_EUK). The intracellular ROS levels were assessed by CLSM using the same ROS probe as above. No change in the fluorescent signal was observed for steatotic HepaRG cells exposed to L0_EUK, indicating the same levels of ROS, whereas the fluorescent signal dropped for steatotic HepaRG cells exposed to L20_EUK and L50_EUK, illustrating an increased ROS scavenging effect for higher amounts of EUK‐PC in the formulation (Figure 5ai). The ratio of green (carboxy‐DCF) to blue (nuclei) pixels was calculated from the CLSM images and normalized to pristine HepaRG cells. A significant decrease in intracellular ROS levels was observed in L50_EUK‐treated (p = 0.003) and L20_EUK‐treated (p = 0.003) steatotic HepaRG cells compared to untreated steatotic HepaRG cells, whereas L0_EUK treatment did not show a protective effect (p = 0.154) (Figure 5aii).

In addition, we aimed to determine the time‐course of intracellular ROS level reduction in steatotic HepaRG cells following their exposure to either L0_EUK or L50_EUK. As expected, steatotic HepaRG cells incubated with L0_EUK had similarly high intracellular ROS levels over 48 h (Figure 5bi). In contrast, steatotic HepaRG cells treated with L50_EUK showed a progressive decrease in intracellular ROS levels after 6 and 24 h, which remained consistently low for at least 48 h. This finding was also quantitatively validated by the decrease of the relative ratio of green fluorescence to blue fluorescence, indicating the robust and durable scavenging ability of the L50_EUK formulation (Figure 5bii).

Acetaminophen (APAP), widely used for the management of pain (paracetamol) and hyperthermia in clinical settings, has been shown to exacerbate liver injury in steatotic conditions through CYP450‐mediated overproduction of the toxic metabolite N‐acetyl‐p‐benzoquinone imine (NAPQI), leading to GSH depletion, impaired redox homeostasis, and increased ROS accumulation. Therefore, steatotic HepaRG cells (1 mM FFA for seven days) were further challenged with APAP. The dose response analysis of APAP‐induced cytotoxicity in these cells yielded an IC50 of 9 mM (Figure S14a). The expression levels of mitochondrial‐encoded transcripts (COXI, COXII, and ND1) were subsequently assessed (Figure S7). Under steatotic conditions (1 mM FFA), exposure to 1 or 2.5 mM APAP did not result in significant changes in transcript levels. In contrast, treatment with 5 mM APAP led to a marked reduction in the expression of all three genes, indicating compromised mitochondrial function and suggesting mitochondrial dysfunction as a consequence of acute APAP toxicity in steatotic HepaRG cells.

In addition, non‐treated steatotic cells exposed to 1, 2.5, and 5 mM APAP exhibited significantly increased intracellular ROS levels compared with healthy cells (no FFA), with 5 mM APAP inducing the strongest ROS increase (Figure 6a). In comparison, treatment with L50_EUK reduced ROS levels compared to L0_EUK in the challenged HepaRG cells. Following on, the ratio of green (intracellular ROS) to blue (nuclei) fluorescence intensity was determined from the CLSM images and normalized to healthy cells for quantitative analysis (Figure 6b). In steatotic HepaRG cells exposed to 1 mM APAP, L50_EUK incubation showed a trend toward reduced ROS levels compared with the untreated group (p = 0.06). In steatotic HepaRG cells exposed to 2.5 and 5 mM APAP, L50_EUK exposure significantly decreased the intracellular ROS levels (p = 0.02 and p = 0.03). By contrast, L0_EUK did not provide any detectable protection against FFA‐ and APAP‐induced oxidative stress.

Furthermore, the intracellular GSH/GSSG ratio, a marker of redox status, showed a slight decline with increasing APAP concentration, consistent with GSH depletion and oxidative stress. Treatment with L50_EUK partially restored the ratio, although not significantly (Figure S14b). Consistently, L50_EUK induced an upward trend in mitochondrial transcript levels (COXI, COXII, ND1, Cytb1, ATP6/8) in cells exposed to 1 and 2.5 mM APAP, suggesting a protective effect on mitochondrial function compared to L0_EUK or untreated controls (Figure S15). No improvement was observed at 5 mM APAP, probably due to too low cell viability.

Conclusion

We established a chemically defined strategy for the covalent integration of manganese Salen catalysts into phospholipid membranes. A well‐defined lipid conjugate was obtained by coupling a carboxylate‐functionalized EUK derivative to an amine‐terminated phospholipid tail that exhibited catalytic activity following metal coordination and liposome formulation. The resulting liposomes catalytically decomposed intracellular ROS in a concentration‐dependent manner in APAP challenged steatotic HepaRG cells. Collectively, these results demonstrated that, precise lipid engineering provided a powerful chemical handle to control the localization, stability, and activity of organometallic redox catalysts in complex biological environments.

The presented synthesis strategy has the potential to offer a generalizable chemical platform for positioning small molecular functionalities within biomimetic membranes. Beyond manganese Salen complexes, this approach could be extended to other transition‐metal catalysts, redox‐active cofactors, or probably even artificial metalloenzymes, enabling spatially controlled catalysis in cells. Systematic variation of the lipid anchor, linker length, and membrane composition might further tune catalyst orientation, accessibility, and reaction kinetics. In addition, integration of targeting lipids or responsive motifs may enable cell‐ or organelle‐specific redox modulation. More broadly, lipid‐anchored catalysts provide a bridge between synthetic organometallic chemistry and cellular systems, opening opportunities for the development of artificial organelles, catalytic therapeutics, and biorthogonal reaction platforms that operate with enzyme‐like selectivity while retaining the modularity of small‐molecule design.
