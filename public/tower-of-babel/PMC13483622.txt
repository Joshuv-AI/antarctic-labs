Evidence-based behaviour change techniques to aid smoking cessation for pregnant women with mental illness: a systematic review
Authors: Ellie Jones, Amelia Casey, Christine MacArthur, Beck Taylor, Tim Coleman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmoking during pregnancy is the leading preventable cause of maternal and infant morbidity and mortality in high income countries, disproportionately affecting women with mental illness. While behavioural smoking cessation interventions are effective for pregnant women and non-pregnant people with mental illness separately, evidence for specific smoking cessation interventions for pregnant women with mental illness is lacking. This review aimed to identify and map BCTs from effective general mental health smoking cessation trials to determine which may be helpful for pregnant women experiencing mental illness.MethodsA systematic review identified effective smoking cessation RCTs among people with mental illness. MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, HMIC and CENTRAL databases were searched through April 2026. BCTs were coded using BCT Taxonomy; those in at least two effective interventions classified as ‘promising’ and compared against BCTs identified within National Centre for Smoking Cessation Training Standard Treatment Programme for pregnant women.ResultsOf 12,403 records, 195 full texts were screened and 16 RCTs met the inclusion criteria. Most interventions combined psychosocial and pharmacological components; motivational interviewing was the most common. Of 39 distinct BCTs identified, 26 were ‘promising’. Frequent BCTs included goal setting (1.1), problem solving (1.2), action planning (1.4), social support (unspecified) (3.1), instruction on how to perform the behaviour (4.1) and pharmacological support (11.1). High overlap exists; most promising BCTs for mental health populations are already embedded within the standard UK pregnancy care pathway.ConclusionsThis study shows that behavioural components of effective smoking cessation interventions for people experiencing mental illness align with what is available women accessing NHS smoking in pregnancy cessation services. However, persistently high smoking rates in pregnant women with mental illness suggest that the presence of these techniques alone is insufficient. These findings imply that service improvements should prioritise adapting the delivery of existing BCTs, such as goal setting and problem-solving, to address specific psychological barriers and social contexts of this group to improve engagement and clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09347-z.Keywords: Smoking cessation, Perinatal mental health, Behaviour change, Pregnancy

Background

Smoking in pregnancy remains a leading preventable cause of maternal and infant mortality and morbidity in the UK and other developed countries [1–3] and remains a major public health concern [4]. Pregnant women who smoke are twice as likely to experience a stillbirth, miscarriage and sudden infant death syndrome [5–7]. Smoking during pregnancy is associated with increased risk of low birthweight, children developing respiratory conditions, attention and hyperactivity disorders, learning disabilities, ear, nose and throat problems, obesity, diabetes and becoming smokers themselves [8–18].

Smoking in pregnancy is a significant health inequality [19], including for women with mental illness. Cohort data from England suggests nearly one in four pregnant smokers has a diagnosed mental disorder [20] and whilst these women are more likely to accept cessation referrals than those without mental illness (69% vs. 56%), they are significantly less likely to successfully quit by time of birth [20], a pattern also observed beyond the UK. Studies from the United States of America report smoking prevalence rates of 27–33% among pregnant women with depression or anxiety compared to 10–13% without [21, 22], with almost half of pregnant smokers meeting criteria for at least one mental disorder [21]. Consistently lower quit rates have also been observed among pregnant women with mental illness who are smokers (48% compared to 62%) [23], suggesting unmet need and additional barriers beyond motivation and support that may contribute to persistence of smoking.

A Cochrane review of non-pharmacological interventions for smoking cessation during pregnancy (any pregnant women) has shown several methods are effective [24]. Compared with usual care, counselling increased smoking cessation in late pregnancy (risk ratio (RR) 1.44, 95% CI 1.19–1.73), incentive-based interventions were effective when compared with non-contingent incentive (RR 2.36, 95% CI 1.36–4.09), feedback provided in conjunction with counselling increased smoking cessation when compared with usual care (RR 4.39, 95% CI 1.89–10.21). Similarly, non-pharmacological interventions were also effective in supporting smoking cessation for people (not specifically pregnant) with mental illness. A recent review found evidence of effectiveness for bespoke face-to-face interventions compared with usual care at (medium-term (up to 6 months) RR 2.29, 95% CI 1.38,3.81; long-term (> 6 months): RR 1.58, 95% CI 1.09,2.30) [25].

Although non-pharmacological interventions for smoking cessation have been shown to be effective for both non pregnant people experiencing mental illness and for pregnant women as separate populations, no evidence exists on which intervention components may be most beneficial for pregnant women who have mental illness. Trials of smoking cessation interventions for people with mental illness typically exclude pregnant women [26, 27] while pregnancy specific trials rarely provide subgroup analyses for this population [24]. Consequently, it remains unclear which behavioural components and behaviour change techniques are most effective or appropriate for use during pregnancy among women with mental illness.

Currently, the National Centre for Smoking Cessation and Training (NCSCT) Standard Treatment Programme (STP) for pregnant women represents the standard behavioural support available in the UK. However, this standard programme does not currently include specific components tailored to the unique barriers faced by pregnant women with mental illness. Given that trials specifically targeting pregnant women with mental illness are lacking, the next logical next step was to examine the wider evidence base for smoking cessation in people with mental illness as potentially effective BCTs identified in this wider population could be adapted and added to the existing NCSCT STP. Hence, this systematic review aimed to identify behavioural intervention components and BCTs used in effective smoking cessation trials for people with mental illness. These were then compared to the NCSCT STP to determine their potential for adaptation and integration into standard pregnancy care to better support women with mental illness.

Methods

The protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO 1030965) and is based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocol (PRISMA-P) guideline [28]. Covidence systematic review software [29] was used for the title and abstract screening and Microsoft Excel [30] used for data extraction and analysis.

Behavioural interventions are typically multifaceted, so distinguishing their active ingredients requires a standardised, theory-driven framework. Michie and colleagues developed the BCT taxonomy v1 [31, 32] and proposed that by identifying individual BCTs within interventions, it is possible to specify how interventions operate and therefore what components might be incorporated into new and more effective interventions [31]. This methodology has been used to specify evidence-based BCTs within smoking cessation interventions for the general population [33–35], preventing relapse following stay in smoke free settings [36, 37], during pregnancy [38, 39] and reducing environmental tobacco smoke exposure [40].

Eligibility criteria

Participant types

Eligible studies included adults with mental illness who smoke including people with schizophrenia or other psychotic disorders, depression, anxiety (including post-traumatic stress disorder and obsessive compulsive disorder), personality disorders and eating disorders.

Intervention types

Eligible studies included those that described or evaluated a behavioural intervention to support smoking cessation. The definition of behavioural interventions was based on Spanakis et al. [25]: a structured program or strategy designed to influence or change specific behaviours through psychological, social, or environmental means rather than by pharmacological methods. Interventions could be delivered face-to-face or remotely, digital or group based. Trials that used electronic cigarettes or adjunctive pharmacotherapy alongside a behavioural programme were eligible for inclusion. Solely pharmacotherapeutic trials were excluded as the focus was to identify components of effective psychosocial interventions which may be beneficial for pregnant women.

Comparator types

Eligible studies were those with an alternative smoking cessation intervention, usual care or no intervention.

Outcomes

Eligible studies were those that reported a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05) in biochemically verified 7-day point prevalence abstinence (including carbon monoxide, salivary cotinine, urinary cotinine or serum cotinine) at any time point.

Study types

Randomised Controlled Trials RCTs (including individually randomised and cluster RCTS.

Exclusion criteria

Trials were excluded if they did not show a statistically significant between-group difference on biochemically 7-day point prevalence abstinence. Studies not in English language were also excluded due to detailed intervention information required to label the BCTs using the BCT taxonomy and high precision needed for this. Non-randomised studies were also excluded to risk of performance and selection bias [41].

Search strategy

The search strategy was adapted from an existing review of behavioural smoking cessation interventions for people with mental illness [25] (Additional File 1). Spanakis et al. [25] provided important evidence on intervention effectiveness but did not examine the specific BCT components. The present review extends this work by applying the BCT Taxonomy v1 to identify and categorise the active ingredients of effective interventions. To ensure relevance to perinatal populations, additional terms were added to include other mental health conditions commonly experienced by women who use perinatal mental health services but excluded from Spanakis et al. [25], such as anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder. These additions reflect qualitative evidence highlighting the mental health illnesses most relevant to pregnant women who smoke [42]. MEDLINE, EMBASE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Health Management Information Consortium and CENTRAL databases were searched on February 2025. Reference lists of all eligible studies, existing reviews and trial registries were also checked and supplemented by grey literature searches for relevant studies. Conference abstracts were subsequently checked to see if full papers had been published. Two researchers screened titles and abstracts independently. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus, consulting two additional authors where necessary. The searches were updated in April 2026 were reviewed by one researcher and discussed with all authors.

Data extraction

Relevant primary study characteristics data were extracted independently by two researchers who were trained in the BCT taxonomy using a pre-defined data extraction form (Additional File 2). To ensure any relevant BCTs were captured, the following coding principles were followed: BCTs which were definitely coded were given (++) and probably coded were given (+) (www.bct-taxonomy.com). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. An artificial intelligence (AI) programme, ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT-4, 2025) was also used as exploratory tool for extracting and labelling behaviour change techniques (BCTs). Prompts were designed to elicit standardised BCT labels according to the BCT Taxonomy v1. The use of AI assistance was exploratory and supplemented standard manual coding processes by the two researchers.

Data analysis

Primary studies were categorised into three groups: behavioural intervention only, behavioural intervention with pharmacotherapy commonly used for smoking cessation in pregnancy and behavioural intervention with pharmacotherapy not used in pregnancy. Frequency counts of BCTs present across trials were conducted. A list of ‘promising’ BCTs most likely to be effective in supporting smoking cessation in people with mental illness was created. A BCT was classified as ‘promising’ if it featured in at least two interventions included in the review, to identify techniques that work across different settings and populations, as described in other studies using similar methods [38, 40, 43]. To compare the list of promising BCTs to the NCSCT standard treatment programme (STP) for pregnant women, firstly the STP was screened for BCTs using the methods described above. The list of promising BCTs from primary studies were then compared to the BCTs identified in the NCSCT STP for pregnant women.

One researcher independently assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane risk of bias 2 tool [44]. Risk of bias was categorised as high, low, or unclear for the following domains: ‘random sequence generation’, ‘allocation concealment’, ‘blinding of participants and personnel’, ‘incomplete outcome data’, ‘blinding of outcome assessment’, and for any other bias (e.g., funding) in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [45].

Results

The search identified 12,403 records, of which 195 full texts were screened for eligibility. Of these, 16 studies were discussed with two additional authors to determine whether they met criteria for inclusion (Fig. 1). A total of 16 studies met the inclusion criteria (Table 1). All studies were individually randomised controlled trials, although three studies were pilot randomised controlled trials [46–48]. Eleven studies were undertaken in United States of America [47–57], three in Australia [58–60] and two in the United Kingdom [27, 46].

Four studies were conducted within inpatient psychiatric hospitals for people with SMI [57, 59, 61, 62]. All other studies included people accessing mental health treatment within the community. Four studies included only those accessing treatment for depression [52, 53], depression and anxiety [46] and depression or bipolar disorder [51].

The included studies targeted adult participants > 18 years, with smoking levels ranging from occasional use [58] to more than 15 cigarettes per day [60]. Seven studies included individuals regardless of their motivation to quit [49, 57, 61–65], while three required interest in quitting within a defined timeframe [46, 51, 66]. Two studies specifically recruited those who were not motivated to quit as the intervention was designed to improve motivation to quit or engage in smoking cessation treatment [48, 67]. Two studies were gender-specific, recruiting only men [56] or women [47].

All but one primary study interventions included both psychosocial and pharmacological components. Thirteen studies included a prescription of nicotine replacement therapy in addition to a psychosocial intervention [46, 47, 49, 51, 56, 57, 60–64, 67, 68]. Two studies also offered other pharmacological treatment including varenicline and bupropion in addition to a psychosocial intervention [66, 69]. Only one study did not include a pharmacological element as the focus of the intervention was to increase levels of motivation to quit smoking [48].

Motivational interviewing techniques were the most common psychosocial approach [48, 56, 57, 60–63, 65–67], with five studies combining this with smoking cessation counselling or support [56, 57, 62, 65, 70]. Other approaches included an exercise programme [47], behavioural activation therapy [64], contingency management [71], and adaptations of the National Centre for Smoking Cessation and Training (NCSCT) Standard Treatment Programme [72], the evidence-based framework used in UK stop smoking services to guide structured behavioural support for tobacco dependence (not pregnant specific). Adaptations involved either embedding the NCSCT Programme within a cognitive behavioural therapy framework for understanding the nicotine withdrawal cycle and its impact on mental health [46], or by delivering the programme in a more flexible and supportive manner [69]. One study used proactive outreach calls rather than waiting for patients to initiate contact.

The intervention was delivered by a range of professionals, most commonly qualified counsellors [27, 46, 52, 53, 60, 73], followed by research staff with unspecified qualifications [49, 66], smoking cessation counsellors [59, 61],social workers [57], peer researchers [67] and a health/exercise coach [47]. The person delivering the intervention was unclear in two studies [51, 62].

Behaviour change techniques content of the interventions

Of the 93 BCTs in the BCT taxonomy v1, 39 were present across included effective trials, 26 of which were identified across more than one included study and therefore classified as ‘promising’ BCTs (Table 2). The most common promising BCTs identified in 10 or more included studies were: goal setting (1.1), problem solving (1.2), action planning (1.4), social support (unspecified) (3.1), Instruction on how to perform the behaviour (4.1), credible source (9.1) and pharmacological support (11.1). Less common BCTs identified in 5–9 included trials were: Self-monitoring of behaviour (2.3), social support (practical) (3.2), social support (emotional) (3.3), information about antecedents (4.2), information about health consequences (5.1), pros and Cons (9.2). Those only identified in one included study were: focus on past success (15.3), mental rehearsal of successful performance (15.2), distraction (12.4), restructuring the social environment (12.2), social reward (10.4), material incentive (10.1), graded tasks (8.7), demonstration of the behaviour (6.1), information about emotional consequences (5.6), feedback on outcome of behaviour (2.7), self-monitoring of outcome(s) of behaviour (2.4), commitment (1.9), discrepancy between current behaviour and goal (1.6) and monitoring of behaviour by others without feedback (2.1).

Comparison to BCTs in the National Centre for Smoking Cessation Training (NCSCT) Programme for pregnant women

The promising BCTs identified in this review were mapped against the current NCSCT Standard Treatment Programme (STP) for pregnant women. The analysis revealed a high degree of overlap with the majority of promising BCTs for mental health populations are already embedded within the standard UK pregnancy care pathway. Only one promising BCT identified in this review was absent from the current NCSCT STP for pregnant women: ‘material incentive (behaviour)’ (BCT 10.1). This technique is defined as informing the participant that money, vouchers, or other valued objects will be delivered contingent upon effort and/or progress in performing the behaviour [32].

Risk of bias

The majority of studies were judged to be at low risk in most domains. Most studies employed adequate random sequence generation methods [46, 56, 57, 61, 63, 65, 66, 69, 74], although for some other studies, the random sequence generation was unclear [47, 48, 51, 60, 64, 67] (Fig. 2). The majority of studies were rated low risk for allocation concealment [46–48, 51, 54, 56, 57, 61, 62, 68, 69, 74] however, in four studies, it was not clear whether staff and personnel were aware of group assignment during enrolment of participants in the trial [60, 63, 64, 67]. It was not possible to blind participants due to the nature of the intervention and therefore all studies had an ‘unclear’ risk of bias for this domain. One study blinded staff to group assignment [61]. All studies included an objective biochemical measure of point prevalence abstinence and therefore considered to be at low risk of detection bias.

Risk of bias

In terms of attrition bias, most studies had a low rate of incomplete outcome data although two studies had high dropout rates [61, 68]. Two studies had differential attrition between the two arms adequately [46, 48]. One study had high dropout prior to attending any treatment arm [64]. All studies performed an intention to treat analysis with missing assumed to be smoking which is consistent with the approach in other smoking cessation trials.

Most studies reported on the outcomes specific in the methods section and seven studies published a protocol or registered the study prior to publishing the results of the trial [47, 51, 57, 61, 66, 68, 69]. Rogers et al. [74] described a discrepancy between cotinine verified abstinence and self-reported abstinence and yet odds ratios were based only on self-reported abstinence resulting in an unclear rating for selective outcome reporting.

Discussion

This is the first known study to identify and specify behaviour change techniques from psychosocial smoking cessation interventions for people with mental illness, with the specific aim of identifying which components may help inform or enhance interventions for pregnant women with mental illness. By mapping the active components of effective interventions, 39 of the 93 BCTs in the BCT Taxonomy v1 were identified, 26 of which were considered promising as they appeared in more than one included study. The most consistently observed were pharmacological support (providing or encouraging the use of or adherence to drugs to facilitate behaviour change), instruction on how to perform the behaviour (advising or agreeing on how to perform the behaviour) and social support (unspecified) (advising on, agreeing or providing social support or non-contingent praise or reward for performance of the behaviour). These findings suggest that combining structured behavioural support with credible, relationship-based communication and pharmacological support may be key to promoting engagement and sustained cessation among pregnant women with mental illness. These results provide a foundation for adapting cessation support within perinatal mental health settings, where women may face unique motivational and psychological challenges. Identifying which BCTs are most consistently associated with effectiveness in broader mental health populations offers a practical starting point for tailoring future pregnancy-specific interventions.

Most BCTs identified in this review align with the NCSCT Standard Treatment Programme for pregnant women which underpins behavioural smoking cessation support delivered by NHS professionals in England within maternity services. This suggests that the core behavioural framework used by NHS services is comprehensive. Only one BCT within the promising category was not included in the NCSCT Programme: material incentive (behaviour) (10.1) although this is recommended as part of standard care as part of the National Smoke Free Pregnancy Incentive Scheme [75] with a large proportion of maternity trusts currently delivering the scheme across England and the vast majority of pregnant women who smoke are now able to access up to £400 for remaining smoke free throughout pregnancy and 3 months postpartum [75] Understanding how BCTs are operationalised and adapted for pregnant women with mental illness however is likely to be crucial to improving their effectiveness. BCTs may require adaptation in terms of delivery intensity, practitioner support and sensitivity to psychiatry symptoms to achieve the same efficacy seen in the general population.

Evidence suggests that people with mental illness are often as motivated to quit as those without [69, 76] yet they face additional psychological, social and structural barriers to cessation, including high nicotine dependence, fear of symptom exacerbation and inconsistent clinical support [77, 78]. Interventions that are individualised, flexible and integrated within mental health care pathways have been shown to improve engagement and abstinence in outcomes in people with severe mental illness [69]. Recent NCSCT guidance for tobacco dependence treatment for people with severe mental illness acknowledges a need for contextual tailoring, recommending approaches that maximise engagement and mitigate barriers among people with severe mental illness who smoke [79]. We do not know how smoking cessation treatment could be optimally tailored for pregnant women with mental illness although some suggestions for treatment tailoring in the above guidance might be appropriate [79]. These include offering both abrupt-quit and gradual-reduction pathways, providing person-centred support that is responsive to mental health fluctuations and incorporating relapse management within treatment plans. Future research should explore how to better support pregnant women with mental illness to stop smoking with a focus on developing and testing interventions which incorporate the promising BCTs identified in this review in ways that are acceptable to women. In terms of clinical practice, findings suggest that smoking cessation support for this group may benefit from greater flexibility, tailoring to both mental health and pregnancy-specific needs and consistent provision of pharmacological support.

Our review extends the existing literature by focusing specifically on people with mental illness, aiming to identify which BCTs may be most helpful components to incorporate in interventions for supporting pregnant women with mental illness. It bridges evidence on smoking cessation for those with mental illness with research on cessation support for pregnant women, highlighting BCTs that may be particularly useful for this population. Unlike previous reviews of psychosocial interventions that focus narrowly on severe mental illness [25], this review also includes conditions commonly seen within perinatal mental health services such as post-traumatic stress disorder. The use of the BCT Taxonomy v1 [32] enabled detailed specification of intervention components, providing insights into which techniques may be most useful. Overall, risk of bias of primary trials was low, with biochemical verification of abstinence in all trials, although some studies reported challenges with blinding and attrition.

In terms of limitations, this review only included published trials showing effectiveness and it is possible that there were BCTs embedded within complex interventions that overall were not found to be effective but may have been helpful. This is however an established methodology used in several studies for pregnancy smoking cessation and relapse prevention [38, 40, 43] In this review, BCTs were classified as ‘promising’ when they appeared in more than one included trial. While this provides a transparent and replicable approach to identifying commonly used techniques within effective interventions, it does not account for contextual factors, such as population characteristics, delivery format or person or setting that may influence their relevance to pregnant women with mental illness. Alternative approaches, such as weighting BCTs according to effect size, fidelity or contextual fit could provide a more nuanced understanding of which components are most promising for adaptation for pregnant women with mental illness. Finally, no studies specifically targeting pregnant women with mental illness were identified and therefore the relevance of our findings to this population is based on inference from broader mental health populations. However, this review provides an important foundation for identifying, testing, and refining behavioural components in future pregnancy-specific interventions.

Conclusion

Smoking in pregnancy remains a major preventable cause of maternal and infant mortality and morbidity and disproportionately affects women with mental illness. This review demonstrates that the BCTs effective for smoking cessation in general mental health populations largely align with the current National Centre for Smoking Cessation and Training programme for pregnant women which guides best clinical practice for smoking cessation for pregnant women in the UK. However, how these BCTs are adapted, delivered and perceived by women with mental illness is likely to be critical to their effectiveness. The next phase of our research will use these findings to adapt and further develop an existing intervention specifically for pregnant women who use perinatal mental health services for subsequent evaluation.
