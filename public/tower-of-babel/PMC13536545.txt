Barriers to women’s access to healthcare in Sudan: insights into male influence and structural constraints

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWomen in Sudan face significant challenges in access to healthcare due to financial hardship, male decision-making influence and geographic limitations, contributing to delayed care-seeking barriers to maternal and reproductive healthcare, and persistent health inequities.MethodologyA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 760 Sudanese women aged 18 years and above using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews across urban and rural areas, and associations with healthcare accessibility were examined using binary logistic regression analysis.ResultsFinancial limitations were a major barrier, with 24.8% of women reporting rare or no financial ability to access healthcare. Additionally, 32.1% always required male permission to seek care and 7.6% experienced healthcare delays due to male family members’ decisions. Women living more than 10 km from a healthcare facility were 7.1 times more likely to report inadequate access. Nearly half of the participants 49.6% strongly disagreed that male family members understood the importance of timely healthcare.ConclusionWomen’s access to healthcare in Sudan remains constrained by interconnected economic, sociocultural, and geographic barriers. The findings highlight that urgent policy reforms are needed to expand affordable and geographically accessible healthcare services, strengthen women’s financial and decision-making autonomy, and promote community-based interventions that engage men in supportive healthcare practices.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14992-3.

Introduction

Access to healthcare is a fundamental human right, yet women worldwide continue to face significant barriers in obtaining essential medical services. Previous studies in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have demonstrated persistent barriers to women’s healthcare access, including financial and sociocultural constraints [1–3]. Gender disparities in healthcare are not only a public health concern but also an economic issue. Studies estimate that closing the women’s health gap could contribute up to $1 trillion to the global economy [4].While these challenges are widely documented globally, their interaction within the Sudanese context remains insufficiently explored.

In Sudan, women’s access to healthcare is shaped by socioeconomic inequalities, male-dominated decision-making and inadequate healthcare infrastructure [5–7]. These challenges are reflected in the country’s persistently high maternal mortality burden, estimated at approximately 295 deaths per 100,000 live births. Many women rely on male family members for financial support, limiting their autonomy in seeking care [2]. Additionally, geographic disparities pose a major challenge as rural women often face long distances to healthcare facilities, a factor linked to delays in maternal and reproductive healthcare services [8, 9].

Sociocultural norms further exacerbate these barriers with traditional gender roles influencing decision-making and access to healthcare [10, 11]. Although global efforts have aimed to address these challenges, many interventions fail to consider the combined economic, cultural, and geographic constraints that uniquely affect Sudanese women [1, 3].

Despite existing research describing individual barriers to women’s access to healthcare, there is limited community-based quantitative evidence from Sudan examining how financial, sociocultural, and geographic factors jointly influence healthcare accessibility among women [12].

In particular, the role of male decision-making as an interconnected structural and sociocultural determinant of healthcare access remains insufficiently explored in the Sudanese context. Addressing this gap is essential for informing context specific policies and interventions aimed at improving equitable access to healthcare for Sudanese women.

Methodology

Study participants, sampling and setting

A community-based descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among Sudanese women aged 18 years and above. Women who were married or residing in households where a male family member was identified as the primary decision-maker were eligible to participate during the period 15th November 2024 till 30th December 2024 based on study timeline and logistical feasibility of data collection.

The study targeted women from both urban and rural areas across Sudan to capture diverse perspectives on healthcare accessibility and the influence of male decision-making. To ensure diversity and avoid intra-household bias data were collected from only one woman per family.

A multistage sampling approach was used. Three states were selected to reflect diverse geographic and sociodemographic contexts, from which six regions were included representing both urban and rural settings. Communities and households were then selected and eligible participants were identified within households. Proportional allocation was applied based on population size to ensure appropriate representation. This approach enhanced the representativeness and generalizability of the findings.

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula at a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, yielding a minimum required sample of 385 participants. A total of 760 women were recruited to increase statistical power.

Research instrument

A semi-structured questionnaire was used for data collection. The questionnaire was designed by the authors (supplementary file 1) to gather information about participants’ demographics, healthcare accessibility and the role of male decision-makers. It was piloted with 25 women who shared similar characteristics with the target population and modifications were made based on their feedback to enhance clarity and cultural appropriateness.

Composite scores were calculated for the study scales according to predefined scoring criteria. Healthcare accessibility levels were categorized using Bloom’s cut-off criteria. Detailed scoring procedures, item coding, score transformations and classification thresholds are presented in (supplementary file 1).

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by a trained data collectors. The interviews were held in a variety of locations including participants’ homes, community centers, healthcare facilities and other accessible public spaces to ensure convenience and inclusivity. Data collectors ensured that only one woman from each family participated in the study to maintain independence of responses. For participants with literacy challenges the questionnaire was read aloud and responses were recorded by the data collectors.

Data management and analysis

The data were cleaned and entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet and analyzed using SPSS version 27 software. The percentage of missing data across all variables was zero with a response rate of 100%. Descriptive statistics including frequencies and proportions for categorical data and means with standard deviations for continuous data were used to explore the data. Pearson’s correlation test was used to assess the relationships between the accessibility scores and both male impact scores and women’s perception scores after checking the distribution of scores using the Shapiro-Wilk test, skewness, kurtosis, and histograms. The Chi-Square test was employed to analyze the association between health accessibility and various socio-economic factors. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed on variables that showed significance (p ≤ 0.025) in the bivariate analysis. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values ≤ 0.05 were reported for all statistically significant results.

Reliability testing

The reliability of the subscales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients which yielded values of 0.519, 0.680, and 0.518 for the subscales of Women’s health accessibility, male impact on women’s health accessibility, and women’s perception of male role in health access respectively. These values exceed the acceptable threshold of (0.5), Additionally, the mean inter-item correlation values were 0.153, 0.346, and 0.192, respectively, indicating satisfactory internal consistency for these subscales.

Results

A total of 760 women participated in the study with a mean age of 33 ± 12.4 years. Most participants had higher education (59.2%) while smaller proportions had primary or secondary education(31.8%) or no formal education (8.9%). Half of the participants had no children and approximately one-third had between one and four children. Regarding marital status, nearly half were single, followed by married women while a smaller proportion were widowed, divorced, or separated. Employment status was varied, with approximately one-third employed or self employed, one third unemployed and the remainder homemakers. Most participants reported moderate income, and about two-thirds lived within 10 km of a healthcare facility. Participants were relatively evenly distributed between urban and rural areas [Table 1].

Access to healthcare services appeared generally available as nearly two-thirds of the participants reported consistent access to healthcare services in their area. However, financial capacity to access care was more limited with a considerable proportion reporting only occasional or rare financial means.

Access to special healthcare was also restricted for many participants with a substantial proportion reporting rare or no access. While over half of the women felt comfortable making healthcare decisions independently, a smaller group reported limited autonomy in decision-making. Healthcare utilization varied with most participants reporting occasional visits to healthcare facilities, while a smaller proportion reported infrequent visits. Approximately one-third of participants reported that their last healthcare visit occurred more than one year ago[Table 2]. Overall, these findings suggest that financial and sociocultural factors remain major barriers despite general availability of healthcare services.

Pearson correlation test

The Pearson correlation test showed a weak negative correlation between the total score for women’s health accessibility and male impact on women’s health access (r = -0.233, p < 0.001). This suggests that as the score for accessibility increases, the male impact score decreases indicating a significant association. Additionally, there was a weak negative correlation between the total score for women’s health access and women’s perception of the male role in health access (r = -0.387, p < 0.001). This indicates that as accessibility scores increase women’s perception scores decrease, reflecting a positive view of the male role in health accessibility.

Multiple binary logistic regressions

Binary logistic regression analysis identified several significant predictors of inadequate healthcare accessibility with corresponding odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals presented in Table 6. Unemployment was the strongest predictor as unemployed women had higher odds of inadequate access to healthcare compared with employed participants. Living more than 10 km from a healthcare facility was also significantly associated with inadequate access. In addition, higher levels of male influence on healthcare decisions and more negative perceptions of male roles in healthcare access were significantly associated with increased odds of inadequate healthcare accessibility.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the structural and cultural barriers limiting Sudanese women’s access to healthcare. Findings reveal that financial constraints, male decision-making, geographic challenges and restrictive gender norms significantly impact healthcare accessibility. These results align with previous research demonstrating the interplay of economic, sociocultural and systemic factors in shaping women’s health outcomes globally [1, 3].

These economic barriers likely reflect broader structural gender inequalities that limit women’s economic autonomy and reduce their ability to independently seek healthcare. In this study, 24.8% of participants reported rarely or never have the financial means to access healthcare. This aligns with global trends where economic disparities disproportionately affect women’s healthcare-seeking behavior [13, 14]. The McKinsey Global Institute estimates that closing the women’s health gap could contribute $1 trillion to the global economy underscoring the broader economic implications of inadequate healthcare access [4]. In Sudan, economic instability and gendered financial dependency exacerbate healthcare inaccessibility. Many women rely on male family members for financial support which further limits their ability to seek care independently [5]. Similar associations between male decision-making and delayed care have been reported in Pakistan [2] and South Sudan [15].

The strong association between unemployment and reduced healthcare accessibility may limit women’s economic independence. Financial dependence on spouses or family members can restrict women’s ability to afford healthcare-related expenses and reduce their autonomy in seeking care. In settings affected by economic instability, unemployment may therefore exacerbate existing barriers to healthcare access and contribute to delayed healthcare utilization [2, 5, 7]. The study highlights the influence of male family members on women’s healthcare decisions with 32.1% of women always needing permission to seek care and 9.7% facing outright refusal. Research confirms that in many societies, patriarchal norms dictate women’s health autonomy limiting their ability to make independent decisions [9, 11]. In Sudan, restrictive gender norms are reinforced by social and cultural expectations that position men as primary decision-makers in families [5]. Similar challenges have been reported in South Sudan and Morocco, where male gatekeeping significantly delays women’s access to medical care [15].

Community-based programs that engage men in gender-sensitive health education have been successful in shifting attitudes and improving healthcare access for women in other regions [9]. Expanding these initiatives in Sudan could help increase women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making.

A notable finding was that 49.6% of women strongly disagreed that male family members understand the importance of timely healthcare. Moreover, 46.1% reported feeling very uncomfortable discussing their healthcare needs with male relatives. These results highlight how deep rooted cultural norms shape women’s health-seeking behavior [10]. Similar trends are observed in other patriarchal societies where stigma, privacy concerns and gender dynamics prevent women from openly discussing health issues [2, 14]. Studies in Morocco and Burundi show that improving health literacy and gender-sensitive healthcare training can enhance women’s confidence in accessing care independently [9, 14].

The study found that women living more than 10 km from a healthcare facility were 7.1 times more likely to experience inadequate healthcare access. This aligns with findings from other LMICs where poor infrastructure, lack of transportation and healthcare shortages make healthcare inaccessible for women [6]. Geographic distance is a well-documented barrier to maternal and reproductive healthcare access especially in rural areas where healthcare facilities are scarce [3, 9]. A study in Bangladesh found that rural women faced significantly higher risks of pregnancy complications due to healthcare inaccessibility [3]. In Sudan, limited rural healthcare infrastructure and physician shortages further exacerbate this issue [5].

The findings should be interpreted within the context of ongoing conflict and instability in Sudan which have significantly disrupted healthcare systems and reduced access to essential services. Conflict settings are associated with damage to health infrastructure, displacement of populations and increased barriers to healthcare access [7, 8]. Women are particularly affected, facing additional challenges related to safety, mobility, and exposure to gender-based violence [5, 16].

These conditions further compound existing financial and sociocultural barriers identified in this study and highlight the need for resilient healthcare strategies that can maintain access for vulnerable populations during periods of crisis, while not all participants in this study were directly affected by the conflict, these broader systemic challenges may contribute to the healthcare disparities observed.

The findings of this study have important implications for health policy and practice in Sudan. Interventions aimed at improving women’s access to healthcare should prioritize financial protection mechanisms, such as subsidized services or targeted support for low-income women. Expanding the availability of primary healthcare services in underserved and rural areas is also critical to reducing geographic barriers particularly through the implementation of mobile clinics and outreach services. In addition, community-based interventions that promote women’s autonomy and actively engage men in supportive healthcare decision-making may help address sociocultural constraints. Integrating gender-sensitive approaches into national health policies and programs is essential to ensure equitable access to care.

Future interventions aimed at improving women’s healthcare access in Sudan should adopt multisectoral approaches that address economic, sociocultural, and geographic barriers simultaneously. Future research should explore regional differences in women’s healthcare access across Sudan and include displaced and conflict-affected populations that may experience additional barriers.

Limitations

This study provides a large scale, community-based analysis of women’s healthcare barriers in Sudan. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study relied on self-reported data which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. The study sample didn’t specifically include displaced or highly vulnerable populations or women primarily from male headed households and a relatively high proportion of participants with higher education may limit generalizability. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. In addition, the study was not designed to assess regional differences. Furthermore, although mean inter-item correlation analyses supported the internal consistency of the study scales, two subscales demonstrated Cronbach’s alpha values below the conventional threshold and the related findings should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Conclusion

Women’s access to healthcare in Sudan remains constrained by financial barriers, geographic limitations and sociocultural factors, especially male influence on decision-making. Without targeted efforts to promote women’s financial empowerment, expand accessible healthcare infrastructure in rural and underserved areas and implement community-based programs that engage men in supporting equitable healthcare decisions, substantial inequities in women’s healthcare access are likely to persist. Such approaches are essential to improving healthcare access and advancing gender equity in health. Future research should explore these issues through longitudinal studies and qualitative investigations that include the perspectives of male family members and other key decision-makers.
