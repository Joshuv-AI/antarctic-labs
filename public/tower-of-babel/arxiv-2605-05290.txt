Krylov Dynamics and Operator Growth in Time-Dependent Systems via Lie Algebras
András Grabarits, E. Medina-Guerra, Adolfo del Campo

Abstract
We study quantum dynamics generated by time-dependent Hamiltonians in Krylov space, the minimal subspace in which the evolution takes place. We establish a direct link between dynamics in the time-dependent Krylov subspace and the underlying Lie-algebraic structure of the Hamiltonian. Exploiting the geometry of the root system, we develop a general framework in which the dynamics in the time-dependent Krylov subspace is generated by ladder operators of the associated Lie algebra. In particular, we identify the minimal conditions under which the exact time-dependent Krylov dynamics is naturally determined by the interaction-picture Hamiltonian and governed by an embedded 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 2 , ℂ ) \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C}) subalgebra. We further show that an exact single-exponential representation of the time-evolution operator gives rise to a distinct time-independent Krylov dynamics in a unitarily related basis, from which the exact time-dependent Krylov dynamics can nevertheless be recovered. We also extend the framework to the oscillator algebra as the simplest extension of the nilpotent Heisenberg–Weyl algebra, and provide further examples, including the translated and dilated harmonic oscillator, systems governed by closed Virasoro subalgebras, a spin in a rotating magnetic field, and higher-dimensional generalizations for multi-level systems. In addition, we introduce a new quantum speed limit to the complexity growth rate generated by a time-dependent generator and show that, for evolutions governed by a Lie algebra, it retains the same functional form as in the time-independent case. Remarkably, saturation of this bound is strongly affected by temporal driving and persists only when the Hamiltonian commutes with itself at different times. These results establish a unified framework for characterizing operator growth and Krylov complexity in time-dependent quantum systems with underlying Lie-algebraic structures.

Understanding the complexity of quantum dynamics remains a central challenge in modern physics. The exponential growth of Hilbert space makes the description of quantum evolution increasingly difficult as the number of degrees of freedom increases. Despite this progress, most existing formulations of the Krylov framework are restricted to time-independent generators. They are thus concerned with the preparation of an initial stationary state and its subsequent time-evolution, i.e., following a sudden quantum quench. In this setting, Krylov methods have also led to quantum speed limits for the growth of Krylov complexity

Evolutions saturating the Krylov complexity growth are described in terms of generalized coherent states. By contrast, for explicitly time-dependent generators, the only available speed limit in Krylov space to date addresses the non-escape probability in the Krylov lattice

Extending the ideas above to explicitly time-dependent systems remains considerably more challenging. In general, Hamiltonians at different times do not commute, and the resulting evolution operator involves time ordering, obscuring the simple geometric picture underlying the Krylov construction.
Developing such a framework is crucial for uncovering how complexity builds up in driven quantum systems and for characterizing the spread of chaos and scrambling of information in nonequilibrium dynamics.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Krylov subspaces for
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
representations in time-independent dynamics

In this subsection, we recall the standard time-independent Krylov construction for operator dynamics
56
;
41
;
47
;
11
;
27
. Operators are denoted by round kets
\lvert\mathcal{O})
, while ordinary Dirac kets
\ket{\psi}
are used for quantum states. The corresponding operator-space Krylov basis is denoted by
\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n})
. In the Lie-algebraic formalism developed below, the same tridiagonal Krylov structure is written in vectorized Liouville space notation as
\ket{K_n}
.

The dynamics of an operator generated by a time-independent Liouvillian is

i\partial_{t}\lvert\mathcal{O}(t))=\mathcal{L}\lvert\mathcal{O}(t)).

(1)

Starting from an initial operator
\lvert\mathcal{O}_{0})\equiv\lvert\mathcal{O}(0))
, the Krylov subspace is generated by the set
\{\mathcal{L}^{n}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{0})\}_{n\geq 0}
. Given an inner product, one constructs an orthonormal Krylov basis
\{\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n})\}
by the Lanczos recursion

b_{n+1}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n+1})=\mathcal{L}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n})-a_{n}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n})-b_{n}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n-1}),

(2)

with
\lvert\mathcal{O}_{-1})=0
,
b_{0}=0
, and
(\mathcal{O}_{n}|\mathcal{O}_{m})=\delta_{nm}
. The recursion terminates when
b_{d_{K}}=0
, where
d_{K}
is the Krylov dimension.

The time-independent Lanczos coefficients
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
determine a tridiagonal representation of the Liouvillian in the Krylov basis. In this basis, operator dynamics is mapped to a nearest-neighbor hopping problem on an emergent one-dimensional chain.

Expanding the evolved operator in this basis as

\lvert\mathcal{O}(t))=\sum_{n=0}^{d_{K}-1}\varphi_{n}(t)\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n}),

(3)

one finds that the Krylov amplitudes
\varphi_{n}(t)=(\mathcal{O}_{n}\lvert\mathcal{O}(t))
obey

i\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)=b_{n}\varphi_{n-1}(t)+a_{n}\varphi_{n}(t)+b_{n+1}\varphi_{n+1}(t),\qquad\varphi_{n}(0)=\delta_{n,0}.

(4)

Thus, the dynamics is mapped to the propagation of a particle on a one-dimensional Krylov chain.

For systems with an underlying Lie-algebraic structure, this chain structure is often not merely an abstract numerical construction but can be identified directly with the action of ladder operators in a representation of the corresponding algebra. This observation is especially useful when the generator belongs to a rank-one algebra, as the resulting Krylov chain can be constructed exactly from the ladder structure itself.
In particular, in Ref.
11
, it was shown that when the evolution is generated by elements of a Lie algebra, the corresponding Krylov dynamics can be related to generalized coherent-state evolution, thereby providing a geometric description of operator growth; see also Ref.
27
. This observation motivates the Lie-algebraic viewpoint adopted in the remainder of this work.

Let us now specialize to the case in which the Liouvillian belongs to a representation of

\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\mathcal{L}_{+},\mathcal{L}_{-},\mathcal{L}_{0}\},

(5)

with commutation relations

[\mathcal{L}_{+},\mathcal{L}_{-}]=2\mathcal{L}_{0},\qquad[\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{\pm}]=\pm\mathcal{L}_{\pm}.

(6)

The relevant algebra is selected by the choice of
*
-structure. The compact
case corresponds to the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
choice

\mathcal{L}_{+}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{L}_{-}
, while the non-compact case corresponds
to the
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
choice

\mathcal{L}_{+}^{\dagger}=-\mathcal{L}_{-}
.

For representing Hermitian generators, it is convenient to absorb this sign
into the definition of the lowering operator. We therefore introduce

L_{+}=\mathcal{L}_{+},\qquad L_{-}=\sigma\mathcal{L}_{-},\qquad L_{0}=\mathcal{L}_{0},

(7)

with
\sigma=+1
for
\mathfrak{su}(2)
and
\sigma=-1
for

\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
. Then
L_{+}^{\dagger}=L_{-}
, and the generators obey

[L_{+},L_{-}]=2\sigma L_{0},\qquad[L_{0},L_{\pm}]=\pm L_{\pm}.

(8)

With this convention, both cases can be treated in parallel through the sign

\sigma
. If the Liouvillian is of the form

\mathcal{L}=\alpha(L_{-}+L_{+}),

(9)

then it acts on the basis states as

L_{+}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n})=b_{n+1}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n+1}),\qquad L_{-}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n})=b_{n}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n-1}).

(10)

As a result, from Eq. (
1
) one finds

\lvert\mathcal{O}(t))=e^{-it\alpha(L_{+}+L_{-})}\lvert\mathcal{O}(0)).

(11)

Thus, the Krylov basis is identical to the orbit generated by the ladder operators acting on a lowest-weight state, and the dynamics takes the form of a generalized coherent state.

More generally, whenever the operator Krylov subspace generated by the Liouvillian admits a realization in an ordinary Hilbert-space representation of the same Lie algebra, the vectorized operator basis
\{\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n})\}
may be put in one-to-one correspondence with a state basis
\{\ket{K_n}\}
. Under this identification, the tridiagonal Liouvillian action

\mathcal{L}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n})=b_{n}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n-1})+b_{n+1}\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n+1})

(12)

is represented equivalently as

\mathcal{L}\ket{K_n}=b_{n}\ket{K_{n-1}}+b_{n+1}\ket{K_{n+1}},

(13)

where, without notational abuse, we use the same symbol
\mathcal{L}
for the generator in the Liouville
representation.
In this way, the Krylov structure is preserved, but the discussion can be carried out in a language reminiscent of that for ordinary quantum states.

2.2
Krylov subspace methods for time-dependent problems

We next recall the extension of Krylov subspace methods to explicitly time-dependent generators introduced in Ref.
51
. Since the standard Lanczos construction is formulated for a time-independent generator, the basic idea is to embed the Schrödinger dynamics into the extended Hilbert space in which time evolution is generated by a time-non-local operator.
For a quantum state obeying the Schrödinger equation

i\partial_{t}\ket{\psi(t)}=H(t)\ket{\psi(t)},

(14)

the dynamics can be embedded into an extended space through the effective generator

H(t)-i\partial_{t}.

(15)

In this formalism, the time-evolved state may be written as

\ket{\psi(t)}=e^{-it(H(t)-i\partial_{t})}\ket{\psi(0)}.

(16)

Treating
H(t)-i\partial_{t}
as the time-dependent analogue of the usual Krylov generator, the Krylov basis
\{\ket{K_n(t)}\}
can be constructed starting from the initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}
by a generalized Lanczos algorithm. In direct analogy with the time-independent case, the associated coefficients
a_{n}(t)
and
b_{n}(t)
play the role of on-site terms and nearest-neighbor hoppings on the emergent Krylov lattice,

\displaystyle b_{n+1}(t)\ket{K_{n+1}(t)}

\displaystyle=\left(H(t)-i\partial_{t}\right)\ket{K_n(t)}-a_{n}(t)\ket{K_n(t)}-b_{n}(t)\ket{K_{n-1}(t)},

(17)

\displaystyle a_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\bra{K_n(t)}\left(H(t)-i\partial_{t}\right)\ket{K_n(t)},

(18)

\displaystyle b_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\bra{K_{n-1}(t)}\left(H(t)-i\partial_{t}\right)\ket{K_n(t)}.

(19)

The resulting dynamics is therefore mapped to motion on a one-dimensional Krylov lattice with time-dependent nearest-neighbor hopping amplitudes and on-site terms.

Expanding the state on this basis as

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{d_{K}-1}\varphi_{n}(t)\ket{K_n(t)},

(20)

the Krylov wavefunction is given by

\varphi_{n}(t)=\bra{K_n(t)}\ket{\psi(t)},

(21)

and the corresponding Krylov complexity is

K(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{d_{K}-1}n\,|\varphi_{n}(t)|^{2},

(22)

where
d_{K}
denotes the dimension of the Krylov subspace.

Although this construction provides a conceptually natural extension of the usual Krylov framework, its practical implementation can be challenging. In particular, the explicit determination of the time-dependent Lanczos coefficients and of the associated Krylov wavefunction is typically difficult, and exact results are presently available only in very limited settings
51
. One of the main goals of the present work is precisely to identify a broad class of time-dependent Hamiltonians for which this generalized Krylov dynamics can be obtained exactly from the underlying Lie-algebraic structure.

3
Time-dependent Krylov subspace from Lie algebra

We now turn to time-dependent Hamiltonians with an underlying Lie-algebraic structure. The required background on the latter can be found in standard expositions
29
;
18
. We provide the general construction under which the Hamiltonian in a properly chosen interaction picture can be written solely in terms of a single pair of ladder operators, and thus its action is tridiagonal in the corresponding lowest-weight representation and directly generates the Krylov basis. We also show below that the exact propagator admits an exponential representation, leading to an effective time-independent Krylov evolution in which the physical time enters only as a fixed parameter.

3.1
Simple Lie algebras with one-dimensional Krylov lattice

We first consider the case in which the interaction-picture Hamiltonian contains only the ladder operators of an embedded rank-one subalgebra and no Cartan term. The corresponding dynamics is then mapped to nearest-neighbor motion on a one-dimensional Krylov lattice.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a simple Lie algebra of rank
r

\mathfrak{h}

\Delta

\Delta_{+}\subset\Delta

H_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}

E_{\alpha}

\displaystyle[H_{i},H_{j}]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle[H_{i},E_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\alpha(H_{i})E_{\alpha},

\displaystyle[E_{\alpha},E_{-\alpha}]

\displaystyle=H_{\alpha},

\displaystyle[H_{\alpha},E_{\pm\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\pm 2E_{\pm\alpha},

(26)

The constants

Upon separating the selected ladder pair from the remaining generators, we write

\displaystyle H_{I,C}(t)

\displaystyle=U_{C}^{\dagger}(t)\left[f(t)L_{+}+f^{*}(t)L_{-}+H_{E}\bigl(\{E_{\beta}\}_{\beta\neq\alpha},t\bigr)\right]U_{C}(t)

and by
\bra{K_n}L_{\pm}\ket{K_n}\propto\bra{K_0}L^{n}_{-}L_{\pm}L^{n}_{+}\ket{K_0}=0
. As a result, the second line is equivalent to Eq. (
51
) with
b_{n}
given in Eq. (
49
).

Finally, we note that the lowest-weight construction directly reproduces the
complexity algebra of Refs.
27
;
11
;
51
. Denoting the Krylov Liouvillian as
\hat{L}=L_{+}+L_{-}
and
\hat{B}=L_{+}-L_{-}
, their commutator gives

\displaystyle\hat{\widetilde{K}}\coloneqq[\hat{L},\hat{B}]=-4\sigma L_{0}=-4\sigma(\hat{\mathcal{K}}+\lambda_{\alpha})=A\hat{\mathcal{K}}+G,\quad A=-4\sigma,\quad G=-4\sigma\lambda_{\alpha}.

(54)

Here, the Krylov complexity operator is defined as
27

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}=\sum_{n}^{d_{K}-1}n\ket{K_n}\bra{K_n},\quad\hat{\mathcal{K}}\ket{K_n}=n\ket{K_n},

(55)

which is equivalent to the Cartan generator up to a constant shift,
\hat{\mathcal{K}}=L_{0}-\lambda_{\alpha}
.
The constant
G
is positive
with the lowest-weight convention: for
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
one has

\sigma=-1
and
\lambda_{\alpha}=\kappa>0
, while for
\mathfrak{su}(2)
one has

\sigma=+1
and
\lambda_{\alpha}=-j
. In the latter finite representation,

d_{K}=2j+1
, this gives

A=-4=-\frac{2G}{d_{K}-1}
,
whereas in the infinite-dimensional non-compact representation
A=4\geq 0
.
Thus, the shift in
\hat{\mathcal{K}}=L_{0}-\lambda_{\alpha}
is precisely the constant shift relating the Cartan generator of the embedded rank-one
algebra to the complexity-algebra generator.

3.2
Krylov-wave function and spread complexity

Expanding

\ket{\psi_I(t)}=U_{E}^{(I)\dagger}(t)U_{C}^{\dagger}(t)\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{d_{K}-1}\varphi_{n}(t)\ket{K_n},\qquad\varphi_{n}(t)=\bra{K_n}\ket{\psi_I(t)},

(56)

the interaction-picture Schrödinger equation

i\partial_{t}\ket{\psi_I(t)}=H_{I}(t)\ket{\psi_I(t)}

(57)

reduces to

i\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)=\gamma(t)b_{n}\,\varphi_{n-1}(t)+\gamma^{*}(t)b_{n+1}\,\varphi_{n+1}(t).

(58)

Thus, the dynamics is mapped to a one-dimensional Krylov lattice with nearest-neighbor hopping only. The corresponding Krylov spread complexity is

K(t)\coloneqq\sum_{n}n\,|\varphi_{n}(t)|^{2}.

(59)

As shown in App.
B
, this complexity is invariant under the transformation to the interaction picture, and the Schrödinger-picture amplitudes are recovered by the inverse unitary transformation.

The Krylov wavefunction follows from the Wei–Norman disentangling of
U_{I}(t)
. Writing

H_{I}(t)=\gamma(t)L_{+}+\gamma^{*}(t)L_{-},

(60)

the corresponding evolution operator admits the Wei–Norman factorization

U_{I}(t)\coloneqq\mathcal{T}\exp\!\left[-i\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,H_{I}(t^{\prime})\right]=e^{z(t)L_{+}}e^{\eta(t)L_{0}}e^{w(t)L_{-}},

(61)

where the Wei–Norman coefficients satisfy
13

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\gamma(t)+i\sigma\gamma^{*}(t)z^{2}(t),\qquad\dot{\eta}(t)=2i\sigma\gamma^{*}(t)z(t),

(62)

\displaystyle\dot{w}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\gamma^{*}(t)e^{\eta(t)},\qquad\qquad\qquad z(0)=\eta(0)=w(0)=0.

(63)

For the lowest-weight initial state

\ket{\psi_I(0)}=\ket{\lambda}\equiv\ket{0},\qquad L_{-}\ket{0}=0,\qquad L_{0}\ket{0}=\lambda_{\alpha}\ket{0},

(64)

one obtains

\ket{\psi_I(t)}=U_{I}(t)\ket{0}=e^{z(t)L_{+}}e^{\eta(t)L_{0}}\ket{0}=e^{\lambda_{\alpha}\eta(t)}e^{z(t)L_{+}}\ket{0}.

(65)

Expanding the exponential gives

\ket{\psi_I(t)}=e^{\lambda_{\alpha}\eta(t)}\sum_{n=0}^{d_{K}-1}\frac{z^{n}(t)}{n!}L_{+}^{n}\ket{0}=e^{\lambda_{\alpha}\eta(t)}\sum_{n=0}^{d_{K}-1}z^{n}(t)\,\frac{\sqrt{\bra{0}L_{-}^{n}L_{+}^{n}\ket{0}}}{n!}\,\ket{K_n}.

(66)

For
\sigma=1
, the algebra is compact and
d_{K}=2j+1
, with
\lambda_{\alpha}=-j
. This yields

\displaystyle L_{+}^{n}\lvert j,-j\rangle

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\lvert j,n-j\rangle,\quad n=0,1,\dots,2j,

(67)

\displaystyle\bra{0}L_{-}^{n}L_{+}^{n}\ket{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{m=1}^{n}m(2j-m+1)=n!\,\frac{(2j)!}{(2j-n)!},

(68)

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\bra{0}L_{-}^{n}L_{+}^{n}\ket{0}}}{n!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{(2j)!}{n!\,(2j-n)!}}=\sqrt{\binom{2j}{n}}.

(69)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_I(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-j\eta(t)}\sum_{n=0}^{2j}z^{n}(t)\sqrt{\binom{2j}{n}}\,\ket{K_n},

(70)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-j\eta(t)}z^{n}(t)\sqrt{\binom{2j}{n}},

(71)

with
n=0,1,\dots,2j
.
Using unitarity,
e^{-\eta(t)-\eta^{*}(t)}=\bigl(1+|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{-2}
, and so the Krylov probabilities depend only on
z(t)
,

P_{n}(t)\coloneqq|\varphi_{n}(t)|^{2}=\binom{2j}{n}\,\frac{|z(t)|^{2n}}{\bigl(1+|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{2j}},\qquad n=0,1,\dots,2j.

(72)

The complexity is then

K(t)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{2j}n\,P_{n}(t)=\frac{2j\,|z(t)|^{2}}{1+|z(t)|^{2}}=\frac{-2\lambda_{\alpha}\,|z(t)|^{2}}{1+|z(t)|^{2}}.

(73)

For
\sigma=-1
, writing
\kappa=\lambda_{\alpha}>0
, one has
b_{n}^{2}=n(2\kappa+n-1)
and

\bra{0}L_{-}^{n}L_{+}^{n}\ket{0}=\prod_{m=1}^{n}m(2\kappa+m-1)=n!\,\frac{\Gamma(2\kappa+n)}{\Gamma(2\kappa)},\quad\frac{\sqrt{\bra{0}L_{-}^{n}L_{+}^{n}\ket{0}}}{n!}=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(2\kappa+n)}{n!\,\Gamma(2\kappa)}}.

(74)

Hence,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_I(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\kappa\eta(t)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}(t)\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(2\kappa+n)}{n!\,\Gamma(2\kappa)}}\,\ket{K_n},

(75)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\kappa\eta(t)}z^{n}(t)\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(2\kappa+n)}{n!\,\Gamma(2\kappa)}},\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots,

(76)

and by unitarity

e^{\eta(t)+\eta^{*}(t)}=\bigl(1-|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{2},\qquad|z(t)|<1.

(77)

The Krylov occupation probabilities become

P_{n}(t)\coloneqq|\varphi_{n}(t)|^{2}=\frac{\Gamma(2\kappa+n)}{n!\,\Gamma(2\kappa)}\bigl(1-|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{2\kappa}|z(t)|^{2n},\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots

(78)

which is a negative-binomial distribution, yielding the following expression for the Krylov complexity:

K(t)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\,P_{n}(t)=\frac{2\kappa\,|z(t)|^{2}}{1-|z(t)|^{2}}=\frac{2\kappa\,|z(t)|^{2}}{1-|z(t)|^{2}}.

(79)

In a unified form, the complexity reads

K(t)=\frac{-2\sigma\,\lambda_{\alpha}\,|z(t)|^{2}}{1+\sigma|z(t)|^{2}},

(80)

where
z(t)
denotes the solution of Eq. (
62
) for the given
\sigma=\pm 1
.

Finally, we compare this construction with the standard Krylov approach based on the exponential expression of the time-evolution operator,

U_{I}(t)=e^{-iG(t)},\qquad G(t)=\Theta_{0}(t)\,L_{0}+\Theta_{+}(t)\,L_{+}+\Theta_{+}^{*}(t)\,L_{-},\qquad\Theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

(81)

where
G(t)
remains in the same rank-one algebra. The parameters
(\Theta_{0},\Theta_{+})
are determined exactly from the Wei–Norman variables
(z,\eta)
by the inversion formulas derived in App.
A
. Defining
A\coloneqq e^{-\eta/2}
and
B\coloneqq z\,e^{-\eta/2}
, one finds

\Theta_{0}=\frac{2\chi\,\operatorname{Im}A}{\sqrt{(\operatorname{Im}A)^{2}+\sigma|B|^{2}}}\,,\quad\Theta_{+}=\frac{i\chi\,B}{\sqrt{(\operatorname{Im}A)^{2}+\sigma|B|^{2}}}\,,\quad\chi=\arctan\frac{\sqrt{(\operatorname{Im}A)^{2}+\sigma|B|^{2}}}{\operatorname{Re}A}\,,

(82)

with the
\arctan
branch fixed by continuity from
\chi(0)=0
.
For
\Theta_{+}(t)\neq 0
, this generator
G(t)
induces the same ladder Krylov basis
{\ket{K_n}}
,

\displaystyle L_{0}\ket{K_n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\lambda_{\alpha}+n)\ket{K_n},\quad L_{+}\ket{K_n}=b_{n+1}\ket{K_{n+1}},\quad L_{-}\ket{K_n}=b_{n}\ket{K_{n-1}},

(83)

\displaystyle G(t)\ket{K_n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{n}(t)\ket{K_n}+\tilde{b}_{n+1}(t)\ket{K_{n+1}}+\tilde{b}_{n}^{*}(t)\ket{K_{n-1}},

(84)

with

a_{n}(t)=\Theta_{0}(t)(\lambda_{\alpha}+n),\qquad\tilde{b}_{n}(t)=\Theta_{+}(t)\,b_{n},\qquad b_{n}=\sqrt{-\sigma\,n\bigl(2\lambda_{\alpha}+n-1\bigr)}.

(85)

If
\Theta_{+}(t)=0
, the auxiliary problem is diagonal and the Krylov dynamics is trivial.

This complementary construction differs from the interaction-picture dynamics (
58
) in two ways: it has a diagonal term
a_{n}(t)
, and its off-diagonal coefficients carry the phase of
\Theta_{+}(t)
. More importantly, it is time-independent in an auxiliary parameter
s
, while the physical time
t
enters only parametrically through
a_{n}(t)
and
\tilde{b}_{n}(t)
. The corresponding amplitudes satisfy

i\partial_{s}\psi_{n}(s)=a_{n}\,\psi_{n}(s)+\tilde{b}_{n+1}\,\psi_{n+1}(s)+\tilde{b}_{n}^{*}\,\psi_{n-1}(s),\qquad\psi_{n}(0)=\delta_{n,0},

(86)

with solution

\psi_{n}(s)=\bra{K_n}e^{-isG}\ket{0}.

(87)

Since
e^{-iG(t)}=U_{I}(t)
, evaluating at
s=1
gives

\psi_{n}(1)=\innerproduct{K_n}{U_I(t)|0}=\varphi_{n}(t),

(88)

so the effective time-independent evolution reproduces the exact physical Krylov wavefunction at
s=1
.

The evolution remains purely off-diagonal only when
\gamma(t)=e^{i\delta}r(t)
with constant phase
\delta
. In that case, time ordering is trivial,
\Theta_{0}=0
, and

\Theta_{+}=\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\gamma(s)=e^{i\delta}\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}s\,r(s),

(89)

so that

G(t)=\left(e^{i\delta}L_{+}+e^{-i\delta}L_{-}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}s\,r(s).

(90)

Then
a_{n}=0
and
\tilde{b}_{n}=b_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\gamma(s)
. The generator thus contains the time-integrated hopping amplitudes of the physical Krylov dynamics (
58
). For a time-dependent phase of
\gamma(t)
, one generically has
\Theta_{0}\neq 0
, and the diagonal term cannot be removed without reintroducing a time-dependent phase in the hopping; see App.
A
.

4
Demonstration on exactly solvable models

In this section, we provide examples of the general Lie-algebraic framework in exactly solvable models.

4.1
Multi-level systems with
\mathfrak{su}(4)

To illustrate the general construction, we consider the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(4)
, which provides one of the simplest examples containing two commuting simple-root sectors. Since
\mathfrak{su}(4)
is of type
A_{3}
with rank
r=3
, its Dynkin diagram is

\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{3}

and the full set of positive roots is

\Delta_{+}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3},\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}\}.

(91)

The Cartan matrix and the corresponding commutation relations between the Cartan elements
H_{k}
,
k=1,2,3
, and the simple-root generators are

A_{jk}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\
-1&2&-1\\
0&-1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad[H_{k},H_{\ell}]=0,\qquad[H_{k},E_{\alpha_{j}}]=A_{jk}E_{\alpha_{j}}.

(92)

Equivalently, the rows of the Cartan matrix encode the Cartan action on the simple-root generators,

\alpha_{1}(H_{k})=(2,-1,0),\qquad\alpha_{2}(H_{k})=(-1,2,-1),\qquad\alpha_{3}(H_{k})=(0,-1,2).

(93)

Each simple-root pair closes an
\mathfrak{su}(2)
subalgebra,

[E_{\alpha_{i}},E_{-\alpha_{i}}]=H_{i},\qquad i=1,2,3,

(94)

where
H_{i}\equiv H_{\alpha_{i}}
are the simple coroots. Thus, the rows of
A_{jk}
determine the Cartan phases of the root generators, while the embedded rank-one Cartan generator for the
\alpha_{i}
sector is
H_{i}/2
.
Among the positive-root generators, the only non-vanishing commutators are

\displaystyle[E_{\alpha_{1}},E_{\alpha_{2}}]\propto E_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}},

(95)

\displaystyle[E_{\alpha_{2}},E_{\alpha_{3}}]\propto E_{\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}},

(96)

\displaystyle[E_{\alpha_{1}},E_{\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}}]\propto E_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}},

(97)

\displaystyle[E_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}},E_{\alpha_{3}}]\propto E_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}}.

(98)

In particular, since neither
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}
nor
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{3}
is a root, the simple-root sectors generated by
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{3}
commute.

We now apply the general construction with
L_{\pm}\coloneqq E_{\pm\alpha_{1}},\,L_{0}=H_{1}/2
, and consider the Hamiltonian

H(t)=f(t)L_{+}+f^{*}(t)L_{-}+f_{\alpha_{3}}(t)E_{\alpha_{3}}+f_{\alpha_{3}}^{*}(t)E_{-\alpha_{3}}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}g_{i}(t)H_{i}.

(99)

Because the
\alpha_{3}
sector commutes with the embedded
\alpha_{1}
subalgebra, it factors from the
\alpha_{1}
dynamics into the commuting operator
U_{E}^{(I)}(t)
. The interaction-picture Hamiltonian governing the Krylov dynamics in the
\alpha_{1}
sector is therefore

H_{I}^{(\alpha_{1})}(t)=\gamma(t)L_{+}+\gamma^{*}(t)L_{-},\qquad\gamma(t)=f(t)\exp\!\left(i\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,[2g_{1}(t^{\prime})-g_{2}(t^{\prime})]\right).

(100)

From a physical perspective, this choice is also natural in view of reductions of multilevel driven dynamics to independent effective two-state sectors in the sense of Morris–Shore
33
, together with hyperbolic-secant pulses familiar from exactly solvable two-level driving protocols such as the Allen–Eberly and Rosen–Zener schemes
1
.
A convenient choice that renders
\gamma(t)
real and simultaneously keeps the commuting
\alpha_{3}
sector analytically tractable is

f(t)=f_{\alpha_{3}}(t)=\Omega_{0}\,\sech\!\left(\frac{t}{T}\right),\qquad g_{1}(t)=g_{3}(t)=\Delta(t),\qquad g_{2}(t)=2\Delta(t),

(101)

with arbitrary real
\Delta(t)
. For this choice, the phases from the Cartan parts cancel, so that

\gamma(t)=f(t)=\Omega_{0}\,\sech\!\left(\frac{t}{T}\right).

(102)

The
z(t)
solution of the Wei–Norman equations in Eq. (
62
) with
\sigma=1
acquires the exact form

z(t)=-i\tan\Theta(t),\qquad\eta(t)=-2\ln\!\bigl[\cos\Theta(t)\bigr],\qquad w(t)=-i\tan\Theta(t),

(103)

where

\Theta(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,\gamma(t^{\prime})=2\Omega_{0}T\,\arctan\!\left[\tanh\!\left(\frac{t}{2T}\right)\right].

(104)

For the simplest non-trivial representation of the
\alpha_{1}
subalgebra, the lowest weight is
\lambda_{\alpha_{1}}=-1
, corresponding to
j=1
. The Krylov space is then three-dimensional, with
n=0,1,2
. The Lanczos coefficients follow from the general expression, Eq. (
49
), as

b_{n}=\sqrt{n(3-n)},\qquad n=0,1,2.

(105)

The Krylov wavefunction is obtained from the general
\mathfrak{su}(2)
result (
70
),

\varphi_{n}(t)=(-i)^{n}\sqrt{\binom{2}{n}}\,\tan^{n}\Theta(t)\,\cos^{2}\Theta(t),\qquad n=0,1,2,

(106)

and the Krylov complexity is

K(t)=2\sin^{2}\Theta(t)=2\sin^{2}\!\left(2\Omega_{0}T\,\arctan\!\left[\tanh\!\left(\frac{t}{2T}\right)\right]\right).

(107)

Figure 1:
(a) Krylov occupation probabilities in the
\mathfrak{su}(4)
example for
T=1
and
\Omega_{0}=1
, showing transfer of weight to the highest Krylov state at long times, with
P_{2}\to 1
. (b) Krylov complexity for
T=0.5,1,2
and
\Omega_{0}=1
, approaching the corresponding long-time values
1
,

, and
0
, respectively.

The corresponding occupation probabilities,
P_{n}(t)=|\varphi_{n}(t)|^{2}
, are shown in Fig.

a for
T=1
. At long times, the probability weight is transferred to the highest Krylov state, consistent with
P_{2}\to 1
. The Krylov complexity is displayed in Fig.

b for
T=0.5,1,2
. The asymptotic value is controlled by the pulse area
\Theta(\infty)=\pi\Omega_{0}T/2
, leading to the corresponding final complexities shown in Fig.
1
b as well.

4.2
Harmonic oscillator with dilation

We next consider the dilated harmonic oscillator
54
;
31
;
35
;
16
, which provides a standard realization of the non-compact case,

H(t)=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}(t)\,x^{2}=g(t)\,a^{\dagger}a+f(t)\bigl[a^{2}+(a^{\dagger})^{2}\bigr]+E_{0}(t),

(108)

where

a=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega_{0}}{2}}\!\left(x+\frac{i}{m\omega_{0}}p\right),\quad g(t)=\frac{\omega^{2}(t)+\omega_{0}^{2}}{2\omega_{0}},\quad f(t)=\frac{\omega^{2}(t)-\omega_{0}^{2}}{4\omega_{0}},\quad E_{0}(t)=\frac{g(t)}{2},

(109)

with
\omega_{0}\equiv\omega(0)
. The squeezing sector is governed by an embedded
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
subalgebra generated by

L_{+}=\tfrac{1}{2}(a^{\dagger})^{2},\qquad L_{-}=\tfrac{1}{2}a^{2},\qquad L_{0}=\tfrac{1}{4}(2a^{\dagger}a+1),

(110)

satisfying
[L_{+},L_{-}]=-2L_{0}
,
[L_{0},L_{\pm}]=\pm L_{\pm}
, i.e.,
\sigma=-1
. Without loss of generality, we choose the lowest-weight state to be the vacuum annihilated by
a
. Hence,
L_{-}\ket{0}=0
and
L_{0}\ket{0}=\frac{1}{4}\ket{0}
, so that
\lambda_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{4}
.
Passing to the interaction picture with respect to
H_{0}(t)=g(t)(a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{2})
and discarding the scalar
E_{0}(t)
, one obtains

H_{I}(t)=\gamma(t)\,L_{+}+\gamma^{*}(t)\,L_{-},\qquad\gamma(t)=2f(t)\,e^{2i\mathcal{G}(t)},\qquad\mathcal{G}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,g(t^{\prime}).

(111)

The Krylov basis and the Lanczos coefficients are

\ket{K_n}=\frac{(a^{\dagger})^{2n}}{\sqrt{\bra{0}a^{2n}(a^{\dagger})^{2n}\ket{0}}}\ket{0}=\ket{2n},\qquad b_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{n(2n-1)}{2}},

(112)

respectively, where
\ket{2n}
denotes the even-parity harmonic-oscillator eigenstates. The Krylov wavefunction obeys

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(t)\,b_{n}\,\varphi_{n-1}(t)+\gamma^{*}(t)\,b_{n+1}\,\varphi_{n+1}(t),

(113)

and the Wei–Norman equations (
62
) with
\sigma=-1
read explicitly

\dot{z}=-i\gamma(t)-i\gamma^{*}(t)\,z^{2},\qquad\dot{\eta}=-2i\gamma^{*}(t)\,z,\qquad z(0)=\eta(0)=0.

(114)

From the general
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
solution with
\lambda_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{4}
, the Krylov probabilities and complexity are

\displaystyle P_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}(n!)^{2}}\,\bigl(1-|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\,|z(t)|^{2n},

(115)

\displaystyle K(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|z(t)|^{2}}{2\bigl(1-|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)},

(116)

respectively.

As an explicit example, we consider the sudden frequency quench
15

\omega(t)=\begin{cases}\omega_{0},&t<0,\\
\omega_{1},&0<t<\tau,\\
\omega_{0},&t>\tau,\end{cases}

(117)

for which
f
and
g
are piecewise constant. During the quench (
0<t<\tau
), the coupling reads
\gamma(t)=2f_{0}\,e^{2ig_{0}t}
with

f_{0}=\frac{\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}}{4\omega_{0}},\qquad g_{0}=\frac{\omega_{1}^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}{2\omega_{0}}\,.

(118)

Since
|\gamma|=2|f_{0}|
is constant but the phase rotates at rate
2g_{0}
, the Wei–Norman equations (
114
) are reduced to constant coefficients by the substitution
z(t)=e^{2ig_{0}t}\zeta(t)
, giving

\dot{\zeta}=-2if_{0}(1+\zeta^{2})-2ig_{0}\,\zeta,\qquad\zeta(0)=0.

(119)

Linearizing via
\zeta=\dot{u}/(2if_{0}\,u)
yields
\ddot{u}+2ig_{0}\,\dot{u}-4f_{0}^{2}\,u=0
with characteristic roots
\mu=i(-g_{0}\pm\omega_{1})
, where we used the identity
4f_{0}^{2}-g_{0}^{2}=-\omega_{1}^{2}
. The solution satisfying
u(0)=1
,
\dot{u}(0)=0
is

u(t)=e^{-ig_{0}t}\!\left[\cos\omega_{1}t+i\frac{g_{0}}{\omega_{1}}\sin\omega_{1}t\right],

(120)

from which we obtain

z(t)=\frac{-2if_{0}\,e^{2ig_{0}t}\sin\omega_{1}t}{\omega_{1}\cos\omega_{1}t+ig_{0}\sin\omega_{1}t}\,,\qquad|z(t)|^{2}=\frac{4f_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}\omega_{1}t}{\omega_{1}^{2}+4f_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}\omega_{1}t}\,.

(121)

The equation involving
\eta
integrates to
\eta=-2\ln u
, giving

\eta(t)=2ig_{0}\,t-2\ln\!\left[\cos\omega_{1}t+i\frac{g_{0}}{\omega_{1}}\sin\omega_{1}t\right].

(122)

From the general
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
result with
\lambda_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{4}
, the Krylov wavefunction and occupation probabilities are

\varphi_{n}(t)=e^{\eta(t)/4}\,z^{n}(t)\sqrt{\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}(n!)^{2}}}\,,\qquad P_{n}(t)=\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}(n!)^{2}}\,\bigl(1-|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\,|z(t)|^{2n},

(123)

respectively. Therefore, the time-dependent Krylov complexity becomes

K(t)=\frac{2f_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}\omega_{1}t}{\omega_{1}^{2}}=\frac{(\omega_{1}^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2})^{2}}{8\omega_{0}^{2}\omega_{1}^{2}}\sin^{2}\omega_{1}t,\qquad 0<t<\tau,

(124)

oscillating at the quenched frequency
\omega_{1}
with an amplitude set by the frequency mismatch. For
t>\tau
the coupling vanishes,
z
freezes at
z(\tau)
, and the complexity saturates at
K(t>\tau)=K(\tau)
.
The real and imaginary parts of the Krylov wavefunction are shown in Fig.
2
a and Fig.
2
b for a representative quench protocol oscillating during the interval
0<t<\tau
and converging to constant values once the quench is switched off. In Fig.
3
a and Fig.
3
b, we show, respectively, the Krylov complexity for different values of
\omega_{1}
at fixed
\omega_{0}
and the corresponding Krylov occupation probabilities.

4.3
Virasoro subalgebra

As a further non-compact example, we consider the rank-one subalgebras obtained by restricting the Virasoro algebra

[L_{n},L_{m}]=(n-m)L_{n+m}+\frac{c}{12}n(n^{2}-1)\delta_{n+m,0}

(125)

to the modes
\{L_{-k},L_{0},L_{k}\}
with fixed
k>0
, with commutators given by

[L_{0},L_{\pm k}]=\mp kL_{\pm k},\qquad[L_{-k},L_{k}]=-2kL_{0}-\frac{c}{12}k(k^{2}-1).

(126)

Introducing the rescaled generators

J_{+}=\frac{L_{-k}}{k},\qquad J_{-}=\frac{L_{k}}{k},\qquad J_{0}=\frac{L_{0}}{k}+\frac{c}{24k}(k^{2}-1),

(127)

one obtains an embedded
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
algebra,

[J_{+},J_{-}]=-2J_{0},\qquad[J_{0},J_{\pm}]=\pm J_{\pm}.

(128)

A Hamiltonian confined to this sector may be written as

H(t)=kg(t)J_{0}+kf(t)J_{+}+kf^{*}(t)J_{-}.

(129)

Passing to the interaction picture with respect to the Cartan part gives

H_{I}(t)=\gamma(t)J_{+}+\gamma^{*}(t)J_{-},\qquad\gamma(t)\coloneqq k\,f(t)\exp\!\left(ik\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,g(t^{\prime})\right).

(130)

Denoting a Virasoro primary state representing the lowest-weight state by
\ket{h}
,

J_{-}\ket{h}=0,\qquad J_{0}\ket{h}=\lambda_{k}\ket{h},\qquad\lambda_{k}=\frac{h}{k}+\frac{c}{24k}(k^{2}-1).

(131)

The associated Krylov states and Lanczos coefficients become

\ket{K_n}=\frac{J_{+}^{n}}{\sqrt{\bra{h}J_{-}^{n}J_{+}^{n}\ket{h}}}\ket{h}=\frac{L_{-k}^{n}}{\sqrt{\bra{h}L_{k}^{n}L_{-k}^{n}\ket{h}}}\ket{h},\qquad b_{n}=\sqrt{n(2\lambda_{k}+n-1)},

(132)

by which the Krylov amplitudes satisfy

i\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)=\gamma(t)b_{n}\,\varphi_{n-1}(t)+\gamma^{*}(t)b_{n+1}\,\varphi_{n+1}(t).

(133)

The exact solution follows directly from the general
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
Wei–Norman construction discussed in the main text. Writing

U_{I}(t)=e^{z(t)J_{+}}e^{\eta(t)J_{0}}e^{w(t)J_{-}},

(134)

with
z,\eta,w
determined by the Wei–Norman equations (
62
) for
\sigma=-1
, one finds for the Krylov wavefunction

\varphi_{n}(t)=e^{\lambda_{k}\eta(t)}z^{n}(t)\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(2\lambda_{k}+n)}{n!\,\Gamma(2\lambda_{k})}}.

(135)

Finally, the Krylov occupation probability and complexity become

P_{n}(t)=\frac{\Gamma(2\lambda_{k}+n)}{n!\,\Gamma(2\lambda_{k})}\bigl(1-|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{2\lambda_{k}}|z(t)|^{2n},\quad K(t)=\frac{2\lambda_{k}|z(t)|^{2}}{1-|z(t)|^{2}},

(136)

respectively.

4.4
Spin in rotating magnetic field

As a compact example, we consider a spin in a rotating magnetic field, which realizes the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
case in a particularly familiar setting,

H(t)=h(t)\,\mathbf{n}(t)\cdot\bm{S}=\frac{h(t)}{2}\sin\theta_{2}(t)\,e^{-i\theta_{1}(t)}\,S_{+}+\frac{h(t)}{2}\sin\theta_{2}(t)\,e^{i\theta_{1}(t)}\,S_{-}+h(t)\cos\theta_{2}(t)\,S_{z},

(137)

with
\mathbf{n}=(\sin\theta_{2}\cos\theta_{1},\,\sin\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{1},\,\cos\theta_{2})
.
Comparing with the general formulation (
29
), one identifies

f(t)=\frac{h(t)}{2}\sin\theta_{2}(t)\,e^{-i\theta_{1}(t)},\qquad H_{C}(t)=h(t)\cos\theta_{2}(t)\,S_{z}.

(138)

Passing to the interaction picture with respect to
H_{C}(t)
and using
[S_{z},S_{+}]=S_{+}
yields

H_{I}(t)=\gamma(t)\,S_{+}+\gamma^{*}(t)\,S_{-},\qquad\gamma(t)=f(t)\,\exp\!\left(i\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,h(t^{\prime})\cos\theta_{2}(t^{\prime})\right).

(139)

In the lowest-weight representation with spin
j
, the time-independent Krylov states are
\ket{K_n}=\ket{j,-j+n}
,
n=0,1,\dots,2j
, and the Krylov probabilities and complexity are given by

P_{n}(t)=\binom{2j}{n}\frac{|z(t)|^{2n}}{(1+|z(t)|^{2})^{2j}},\qquad K(t)=\frac{2j\,|z(t)|^{2}}{1+|z(t)|^{2}},

(140)

respectively.

As a solvable example, we set

\theta_{2}(t)=\theta_{0},\qquad\theta_{1}(t)=\Omega\,t,\qquad h(t)=1,

(141)

so that
\gamma(t)=\frac{\sin\theta_{0}}{2}\,e^{-i\delta t}
with the detuning
\delta\coloneqq\Omega-\cos\theta_{0}
.

Then the Wei–Norman equations (
62
) with
\sigma=1
are solved exactly by

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-i\sin\theta_{0}\,e^{-i\delta t}\,\sin(\Omega_R t/2)}{\Omega_{R}\cos(\Omega_R t/2)-i\delta\,\sin(\Omega_R t/2)},

(142)

\displaystyle\eta(t)

\displaystyle=-i\delta\,t-2\ln\!\left[\cos(\Omega_R t/2)-i\frac{\delta}{\Omega_{R}}\,\sin(\Omega_R t/2)\right],

(143)

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-i\sin\theta_{0}\,\sin(\Omega_R t/2)}{\Omega_{R}\cos(\Omega_R t/2)-i\delta\,\sin(\Omega_R t/2)},

(144)

where

\Omega_{R}\coloneqq\sqrt{\delta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta_{0}}

(145)

is the generalized Rabi frequency.
For the numerical demonstration below, we choose the spin-
1
representation, so that the Krylov space consists of three states
n=0,1,2
, and we consider
\theta_{0}=\pi/2
, for which
\delta=\Omega
,
\Omega_{R}=\sqrt{\Omega^{2}+1}
, and the only relevant parameter simplifies to

z(t)=\dfrac{-ie^{-i\Omega t}\sin(\sqrt{\Omega^2+1}t/2)}{\sqrt{\Omega^{2}+1}\cos(\sqrt{\Omega^2+1}\,t/2)-i\Omega\sin(\sqrt{\Omega^2+1}\,t/2)}.

(146)

The resulting Krylov occupation probabilities and Krylov complexity are shown in Fig.
4
for different values of
\Omega
. For
\Omega=1
, the probabilities
P_{n}(t)
with
n=0,1,2
exhibit the expected oscillatory behaviour, while the complexity shows the corresponding periodic response with a frequency that depends on
\Omega
.

4.5
Heisenberg–Weyl and oscillator algebra

The above construction can also be extended beyond simple Lie algebras.
As a representative example, we consider the
\mathfrak{h}_{3}
Heisenberg–Weyl algebra as the simplest example for nilpotent algebras for which the commutator is central and the dynamics reduces to the familiar displacement of the harmonic oscillator. To this end, we consider the general Hamiltonian

H(t)=\gamma^{*}(t)a+\gamma(t)a^{\dagger},

(147)

with
[a,a^{\dagger}]=1
. This immediately identifies the Krylov states and Lanczos coefficients as

\displaystyle\ket{K_n}=\frac{(a^{\dagger})^{n}}{\sqrt{\bra{0}a^{n}(a^{\dagger})^{n}\ket{0}}}\ket{0}=\ket{n},\qquad b_{n}=\sqrt{n},

(148)

respectively. Accordingly, the Krylov wavefunction satisfies

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)=\gamma(t)b_{n}\varphi_{n-1}(t)+\gamma^{*}(t)b_{n+1}\varphi_{n+1}(t).

(149)

Since

\displaystyle[H(t_{1}),H(t_{2})]=\gamma^{*}(t_{1})\gamma(t_{2})-\gamma(t_{1})\gamma^{*}(t_{2})

(150)

is a constant, the time-evolution operator can be written exactly as

\displaystyle U_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\Phi(t)}e^{-i\alpha(t)a^{\dagger}-i\alpha^{*}(t)a}=e^{i\Phi(t)-|\alpha(t)|^{2}/2}e^{-i\alpha(t)a^{\dagger}}e^{-i\alpha^{*}(t)a},

(151)

where

\displaystyle\alpha(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,\gamma(t^{\prime}),\qquad\Phi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\differential t_{2}\,\Im\!\bigl[\gamma(t_{1})\gamma^{*}(t_{2})\bigr].

(152)

For the vacuum initial state,
a\ket{0}=0
, this gives

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_I(t)}=U_{I}(t)\ket{0}=e^{i\Phi(t)-|\alpha(t)|^{2}/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}\alpha^{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{K_n},

(153)

so that

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t)=e^{i\Phi(t)-|\alpha(t)|^{2}/2}\frac{(-i)^{n}\alpha^{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n!}},\qquad|\varphi_{n}(t)|^{2}=e^{-|\alpha(t)|^{2}}\frac{|\alpha(t)|^{2n}}{n!}.

(154)

Hence, the Krylov complexity is

\displaystyle K(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}n\,|\varphi_{n}(t)|^{2}=|\alpha(t)|^{2}.

(155)

Equivalently, since the commutator of
a
and
a^{\dagger}
is central, the time-evolution operator can be written in a simpler exponential form,

U_{I}(t)=e^{-iG(t)},\qquad G(t)=-\Phi(t)+\alpha(t)\,a^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*}(t)\,a.

(156)

Its action on the Krylov basis states defined in Eq. (
148
) is

G(t)\ket{K_n}=-\Phi(t)\ket{K_n}+\alpha(t)\sqrt{n+1}\ket{K_{n+1}}+\alpha^{*}(t)\sqrt{n}\ket{K_{n-1}},

(157)

so that

a_{n}(t)=-\Phi(t),\qquad\tilde{b}_{n}(t)=\alpha(t)\sqrt{n}.

(158)

Similar to the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
and
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
simple Lie algebras in Sec.
3.2
,
G(t)
is defined only locally at each physical time
t
, and acts as a time-independent Krylov Liouvillian along a fictitious-time evolution with respect to
s
,

i\partial_{s}\psi_{n}(s)=a_{n}\,\psi_{n}+\tilde{b}_{n+1}\,\psi_{n+1}+\tilde{b}_{n}^{*}\,\psi_{n-1},\qquad\psi_{n}(0)=\delta_{n,0}.

Since
G
is
s
-independent, the solution is
\ket{\psi(s)}=e^{-isG}\ket{0}
, which by the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff disentangling gives

\psi_{n}(s)=e^{is\Phi-s^{2}|\alpha|^{2}/2}\,\frac{(-is\alpha)^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\,.

(159)

At
s=1
, this reproduces the exact physical Krylov wavefunction,
\psi_{n}(1)=\varphi_{n}(t)
, confirming the equivalence of the time-independent generator approach with the full time-dependent dynamics, as in the rank-one case.

If
\gamma(t)=e^{i\delta}r(t)
with
r(t)\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\Im\!\bigl[\gamma(t_{1})\gamma^{*}(t_{2})\bigr]=0,\qquad\Phi(t)=0,

(160)

and the single-exponential generator becomes purely off-diagonal,

G(t)=\alpha(t)\,a^{\dagger}+\alpha^{*}(t)\,a,\qquad\alpha(t)=e^{i\delta}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,r(t^{\prime}).

(161)

Thus, for a fixed phase of
\gamma(t)
, the generator
G(t)
leads to the same Krylov subspace and the same
\sqrt{n}
structure as the direct construction, but with off-diagonal coefficients involving the time integral of
\gamma(t)
rather than
\gamma(t)
itself.

As an example, we consider the translated harmonic oscillator

H(t)=\omega\,a^{\dagger}a-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega^{3}}{2}}\,x_{0}(t)\bigl(a+a^{\dagger}\bigr)+E_{0}(t),\qquad E_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}x_{0}^{2}(t)+\frac{\omega}{2}\,.

(162)

Although this system is described by the oscillator algebra extending
\mathfrak{h}_{3}
with the number operator
a^{\dagger}a
, passing to the interaction picture with respect to the latter gets back to
\mathfrak{h}_{3}
, with the interaction-picture Hamiltonian

H_{I}(t)=\gamma^{*}(t)\,a+\gamma(t)\,a^{\dagger}+E_{0}(t),\qquad\gamma(t)=f(t)\,e^{i\omega t},\qquad f(t)=-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega^{3}}{2}}\,x_{0}(t).

(163)

The scalar term
E_{0}(t)
contributes only an overall phase and therefore does not affect the Krylov probabilities or the complexity. Compared to the general Heisenberg–Weyl Hamiltonian (
147
), the Krylov basis remains the Fock basis,

\ket{K_n}=\ket{n},\qquad\gamma(t)\sqrt{n}=-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega^{3}}{2}}\,x_{0}(t)\,e^{i\omega t}\sqrt{n}\,,

(164)

and

\alpha(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,\gamma(t^{\prime})=-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega^{3}}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,x_{0}(t^{\prime})\,e^{i\omega t^{\prime}}.

(165)

From the general Heisenberg–Weyl solution (
154
), the Krylov amplitudes and occupation probabilities become

\varphi_{n}(t)=e^{i\Phi(t)-|\alpha(t)|^{2}/2}\frac{(-i)^{n}\alpha^{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n!}},\quad|\varphi_{n}(t)|^{2}=e^{-|\alpha(t)|^{2}}\frac{|\alpha(t)|^{2n}}{n!}\,,

(166)

and the Krylov complexity is given by

K(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}n\,|\varphi_{n}(t)|^{2}=|\alpha(t)|^{2}=\frac{m\omega^{3}}{2}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\,x_{0}(t^{\prime})\,e^{i\omega t^{\prime}}\right|^{2}.

(167)

To illustrate these results, we consider a harmonically dragged oscillator with
x_{0}(t)=x_{0}\cos(\omega t)
. One finds

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-x_{0}\sqrt{\frac{m\omega^{3}}{2}}\left[\frac{t}{2}+\frac{e^{2i\omega t}-1}{4i\omega}\right],

(168)

\displaystyle K(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m\omega^{3}x_{0}^{2}}{8}\left[t^{2}+\frac{t}{\omega}\sin(2\omega t)+\frac{\sin^{2}(\omega t)}{\omega^{2}}\right].

(169)

The leading
t^{2}
growth reflects the secular energy injection by the resonant component of the drive.
Since
\gamma(t)=f(t)\,e^{i\omega t}
carries a time-dependent phase, the equivalent single-exponential generator acquires the additional scalar term
-\Phi(t)
, although this does not affect the Krylov basis or the complexity.
Numerical results for the real and imaginary parts of the Krylov wavefunction are shown in Fig.
5
. The real and imaginary parts exhibit an exponential decay with additional oscillations on top of it, as is obvious from the Poissonian form, Eq. (
166
). The Krylov complexities are plotted in Fig.
6
, showing quadratic
\sim t^{2}
and cubic
\sim\omega^{3}
growth for fixed frequencies and instantaneous times, respectively.

x_{0}=0.5,\,\omega=2
).

4.6
Higher-dimensional generalization

The construction developed above extends naturally to the case of several mutually commuting ladder sectors. Let
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\subset\Delta_{+}
be a set of positive roots such that, for
i\neq k
, none of
\pm\alpha_{i}\pm\alpha_{k}
is a root. Then the corresponding embedded rank-one subalgebras commute, and the interaction-picture reduction factorizes exactly as in the one-dimensional case.

For each selected root
\alpha_{k}
, we introduce

L_{+,k}=E_{\alpha_{k}},\qquad L_{-,k}=\sigma_{k}E_{-\alpha_{k}},\qquad L_{0,k}=\frac{1}{2}H_{\alpha_{k}}.

(170)

For selected simple roots,
H_{\alpha_{k}}
is the corresponding simple coroot. Thus, if
\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{j}
, then
H_{\alpha_{k}}=H_{j}
and
L_{0,k}=H_{j}/2
. The Cartan phases are instead determined by the rows
A_{j\ell}=\alpha_{j}(H_{\ell})
. The corresponding commutation relations read

[L_{+,k},L_{-,k}]=2\sigma_{k}L_{0,k},\qquad[L_{0,k},L_{\pm,k}]=\pm L_{\pm,k},

(171)

and all generators belonging to different sectors commute with one another. Here
\sigma_{k}=+1
corresponds to a compact
\mathfrak{su}(2)
sector, while
\sigma_{k}=-1
corresponds to a non-compact
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
sector.

Under the same closure conditions as before, the Hamiltonian can be written as

H(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Bigl(f_{k}(t)E_{\alpha_{k}}+\sigma_{k}f_{k}^{*}(t)E_{-\alpha_{k}}\Bigr)+H_{E}\!\left(\{E_{\beta}\}_{\beta\notin\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}},t\right)+H_{C}\!\left(\{H_{i}\},t\right),

(172)

with

H_{C}\!\left(\{H_{i}\},t\right)=\sum_{i}g_{i}(t)H_{i}.

(173)

After removing the Cartan sector and the commuting remainder exactly as in the rank-one construction, the final interaction-picture Hamiltonian becomes

H_{I}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Bigl(\gamma_{k}(t)L_{+,k}+\gamma_{k}^{*}(t)L_{-,k}\Bigr),\qquad\gamma_{k}(t)=e^{\,i\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\alpha_{k}(H_{\ell})\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,g_{\ell}(t^{\prime})}f_{k}(t).

(174)

Let
\ket{\lambda}
be a common lowest-weight state satisfying

L_{-,k}\ket{\lambda}=0,\qquad L_{0,k}\ket{\lambda}=\lambda_{\alpha_{k}}\ket{\lambda},\qquad k=1,\dots,N.

(175)

Since the sectors commute, the Krylov basis factorizes as

\ket{K_{n_1,\dots,n_N}}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\frac{L_{+,k}^{\,n_{k}}}{\sqrt{\bra{\lambda}L_{-,k}^{\,n_{k}}L_{+,k}^{\,n_{k}}\ket{\lambda}}}\ket{\lambda}.

(176)

For each direction
k
, the corresponding Lanczos coefficients are

\bigl(b_{n_{k}}^{(k)}\bigr)^{2}=-\sigma_{k}\,n_{k}\bigl(2\lambda_{\alpha_{k}}+n_{k}-1\bigr),

(177)

so that

L_{+,k}\ket{K_{n_1,\dots,n_k,\dots,n_N}}=b_{n_{k}+1}^{(k)}\ket{K_{n_1,\dots,n_k+1,\dots,n_N}},

(178)

and

L_{-,k}\ket{K_{n_1,\dots,n_k,\dots,n_N}}=b_{n_{k}}^{(k)}\ket{K_{n_1,\dots,n_k-1,\dots,n_N}}.

(179)

Expanding the interaction-picture state as

\ket{\psi_I(t)}=\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}}\varphi_{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}}(t)\ket{K_{n_1,\dots,n_N}},

(180)

the Schrödinger equation reduces to

i\partial_{t}\varphi_{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\gamma_{k}(t)b_{n_{k}}^{(k)}\,\varphi_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}-1,\dots,n_{N}}(t)+\gamma_{k}^{*}(t)b_{n_{k}+1}^{(k)}\,\varphi_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}+1,\dots,n_{N}}(t)\right].

(181)

Because the sectors commute, the full-time evolution operator and hence the Krylov wavefunction also factorize in a product form,

\varphi_{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}}(t)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\varphi_{n_{k}}^{(k)}(t),

(182)

where each
\varphi_{n_{k}}^{(k)}(t)
is determined by the same one-dimensional Wei–Norman analysis as in the rank-one case.

Accordingly, the occupation probabilities factorize as

P_{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}}(t)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}P_{n_{k}}^{(k)}(t),\qquad P^{(k)}_{n_{k}}=|\varphi^{(k)}_{n_{k}}|^{2}.

(183)

For a compact sector
\sigma_{k}=+1
, writing
j_{k}=-\lambda_{\alpha_{k}}>0
, one obtains

P_{n_{k}}^{(k)}(t)=\binom{2j_{k}}{n_{k}}\frac{|z_{k}(t)|^{2n_{k}}}{\bigl(1+|z_{k}(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{2j_{k}}},\qquad n_{k}=0,1,\dots,2j_{k},

(184)

whereas for a non-compact sector
\sigma_{k}=-1
, writing
\kappa_{k}=\lambda_{\alpha_{k}}>0
, one finds

P_{n_{k}}^{(k)}(t)=\frac{\Gamma(2\kappa_{k}+n_{k})}{n_{k}!\,\Gamma(2\kappa_{k})}\bigl(1-|z_{k}(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{2\kappa_{k}}|z_{k}(t)|^{2n_{k}},\qquad n_{k}=0,1,2,\dots.

(185)

The total Krylov complexity therefore decomposes additively,

K(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}K_{k}(t),\quad K_{k}(t)=\frac{-2\sigma_{k}\lambda_{\alpha_{k}}|z_{k}(t)|^{2}}{1+\sigma_{k}|z_{k}(t)|^{2}}.

(186)

As a minimal illustration of the multidimensional construction, consider the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(7,\mathbb{C})
of type
B_{3}
, whose Dynkin diagram is

\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{3}

In the present convention
A_{jk}=\alpha_{j}(H_{k})
, the Cartan action of the simple roots is encoded by the rows

\alpha_{1}(H_{k})=(2,-1,0),\qquad\alpha_{2}(H_{k})=(-1,2,-2),\qquad\alpha_{3}(H_{k})=(0,-1,2).

(187)

Thus the Cartan matrix is

A_{jk}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\
-1&2&-2\\
0&-1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad[H_{k},E_{\alpha_{j}}]=A_{jk}E_{\alpha_{j}}.

(188)

The simple-root pairs close as

[E_{\alpha_{i}},E_{-\alpha_{i}}]=H_{i},

(189)

where
H_{i}\equiv H_{\alpha_{i}}
are the simple coroots.
Since
A_{13}=A_{31}=0
, the two end-root sectors
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{3}
commute, providing a simple realization of two independent rank-one Krylov directions
18
.
For these two simple-root sectors, the embedded generators are

L_{+,1}=E_{\alpha_{1}},\qquad L_{-,1}=\sigma_{1}E_{-\alpha_{1}},\qquad L_{0,1}=\frac{1}{2}H_{1},

(190)

and

L_{+,3}=E_{\alpha_{3}},\qquad L_{-,3}=\sigma_{3}E_{-\alpha_{3}},\qquad L_{0,3}=\frac{1}{2}H_{3}.

(191)

We therefore consider the Hamiltonian

H(t)=f_{\alpha_{1}}(t)E_{\alpha_{1}}+\sigma_{1}f_{\alpha_{1}}^{*}(t)E_{-\alpha_{1}}+f_{\alpha_{3}}(t)E_{\alpha_{3}}+\sigma_{3}f_{\alpha_{3}}^{*}(t)E_{-\alpha_{3}}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}g_{i}(t)H_{i}.

(192)

After removing the Cartan sector, the interaction-picture Hamiltonian becomes

H_{I}(t)=\gamma_{1}(t)L_{+,1}+\gamma_{1}^{*}(t)L_{-,1}+\gamma_{3}(t)L_{+,3}+\gamma_{3}^{*}(t)L_{-,3},

(193)

where

\gamma_{1}(t)=e^{\,i\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,[\,2g_{1}(t^{\prime})-g_{2}(t^{\prime})\,]}\,f_{\alpha_{1}}(t),\qquad\gamma_{3}(t)=e^{\,i\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,[\,-g_{2}(t^{\prime})+2g_{3}(t^{\prime})\,]}\,f_{\alpha_{3}}(t).

(194)

Because the two sectors commute, the time-evolution operator factorizes,

U_{I}(t)=U_{I,1}(t)U_{I,3}(t),

(195)

and each factor is governed by the same one-dimensional Wei–Norman problem discussed above.

For a common lowest-weight state
\ket{\lambda}
satisfying

L_{-,1}\ket{\lambda}=0,\qquad L_{-,3}\ket{\lambda}=0,

(196)

and
H_{i}\ket{\lambda}=\lambda_{i}\ket{\lambda}
, the corresponding lowest weights, defined with respect to
L_{0,1}=H_{1}/2
and
L_{0,3}=H_{3}/2
, are

\lambda_{\alpha_{1}}=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2},\qquad\lambda_{\alpha_{3}}=\frac{\lambda_{3}}{2}.

(197)

The Krylov basis therefore factorizes as
\ket{K_{n,m}}=\ket{K_n^{(1)}}\otimes\ket{K_m^{(3)}}
, so that

\varphi_{n,m}(t)=\varphi_{n}^{(1)}(t)\varphi_{m}^{(3)}(t),\qquad P_{n,m}(t)=P_{n}^{(1)}(t)P_{m}^{(3)}(t),

(198)

and the total Krylov complexity is additive,

K(t)=K_{1}(t)+K_{3}(t),\qquad K_{j}(t)=\frac{-2\sigma_{j}\lambda_{\alpha_{j}}|z_{j}(t)|^{2}}{1+\sigma_{j}|z_{j}(t)|^{2}},\qquad j=1,3.

(199)

Thus,
\mathfrak{so}(7,\mathbb{C})
provides a minimal explicit application of the higher-dimensional factorized Krylov dynamics described above.

5
Quantum speed limits to operator growth and saturation in time-dependent Krylov dynamics

Quantum speed limits refer to various inequalities that set the minimum time for a process to unfold
23
;
12
. Several works have explored quantum speed limits for the Krylov complexity growth in the time-independent setting
27
;
21
;
28
;
12
;
22
;
51
;
36
.
In this section, we derive a quantum speed limit (QSL) to the rate of change of Krylov complexity in the time-dependent Lie-algebraic setting. The bound follows from the same Robertson-type argument as in the time-independent case
27
, but its saturation properties are substantially modified by the driving. In particular, while the lowest-weight coherent-state dynamics saturates the bound identically for time-independent generators, time-dependent driving generally impedes this saturation unless a specific phase-locking condition is satisfied.

5.1
Time-independent case

We first recall the time-independent bound for the rate of change of the Krylov complexity
27
;
12
with the same notation as in Sec.
2.1
.
For

i\partial_{t}\lvert\mathcal{O}(t))=\mathcal{L}\lvert\mathcal{O}(t)),\qquad\lvert\mathcal{O}(t))=\sum_{n}\varphi_{n}(t)\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n}),

(200)

with

\hat{\mathcal{K}}=\sum_{n}n\,\lvert\mathcal{O}_{n})(\mathcal{O}_{n}\rvert,\qquad K(t)=(\mathcal{O}(t)\rvert\hat{\mathcal{K}}\lvert\mathcal{O}(t)),

(201)

the Heisenberg equation and the Robertson inequality give

\left|\partial_{t}K(t)\right|\leq 2\,\Delta\mathcal{L}\,\Delta\mathcal{K}(t).

(202)

Since
\mathcal{L}
is time independent,
\Delta\mathcal{L}
is conserved. For the Krylov initial state
\lvert\mathcal{O}_{0})
, this gives

\Delta\mathcal{L}=b_{1},

(203)

and therefore

\left|\partial_{t}K(t)\right|\leq 2b_{1}\,\Delta\mathcal{K}(t).

(204)

For the algebraic Liouvillian

\mathcal{L}=\alpha(L_{+}+L_{-}),

(205)

acting on the lowest-weight Krylov sector, the coherent-state evolution saturates this bound
27
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\text{ Heisenberg-Weyl}:\qquad

\displaystyle K(t)=\alpha^{2}t^{2},

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{K}(t)=|\alpha t|,\,

\displaystyle b_{1}=\alpha,

(206)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{su}(2):\qquad

\displaystyle K(t)=2j\sin^{2}(\alpha t),

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{K}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{j}{2}}\,|\sin(2\alpha t)|,\,

\displaystyle b_{1}=\alpha\sqrt{2j},

(207)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{su}(1,1):\qquad

\displaystyle K(t)=2\kappa\sinh^{2}(\alpha t),

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{K}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\,|\sinh(2\alpha t)|,\,

\displaystyle b_{1}=\alpha\sqrt{2\kappa},

(208)

with the lowest weights given by
-j
for
\mathfrak{su}(2)
,
\sigma=1
and
\kappa
for
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
,
\sigma=-1
.
In all three cases,

\left|\partial_{t}K(t)\right|=2b_{1}\,\Delta\mathcal{K}(t),

(209)

so the QSL is saturated identically, provided the initial Krylov state is a lowest-weight state, or more generally, as formulated in Ref.
27
, when the Krylov complexity operator can be embedded into a rank-
1
complexity algebra.

5.2
Time-dependent case with simple Lie-algebras

In the time-dependent case, first we consider the same Hamiltonian as in Eq. (
40
),
H_{I}(t)=\gamma(t)L_{+}+\gamma^{*}(t)L_{-}
, where
L_{\pm}
generate a representation of either
\mathfrak{su}(2)
or
\mathfrak{su}(1,1),\,
and the initial Krylov state is the lowest weight state,
\lvert K_{0}\rangle=\lvert\lambda\rangle
.

The Krylov complexity operator is the shifted Cartan generator satisfying the commutation relations

[\hat{\mathcal{K}},L_{\pm}]=\pm L_{\pm},\qquad[H_{I}(t),\hat{\mathcal{K}}]=-\gamma(t)L_{+}+\gamma^{*}(t)L_{-}.

(210)

The Heisenberg equation in the interaction picture yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}K(t)=i\bra{\psi_I(t)}[H_{I}(t),\hat{\mathcal{K}}]\ket{\psi_I(t)}=2\,\Im[\gamma(t)\,C(t)],\qquad C(t)\coloneqq\bra{\psi_I(t)}L_{+}\ket{\psi_I(t)},\qquad

(211)

and the Robertson inequality applied to
H_{I}(t)
and
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
gives the time-dependent dispersion bound

\left|\partial_{t}K(t)\right|\leq 2\,\Delta H_{I}(t)\,\Delta\mathcal{K}(t).

(212)

The complexity variance
(\Delta\mathcal{K})^{2}(t)=\langle\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{2}\rangle_{t}-\langle\hat{\mathcal{K}}\rangle^{2}_{t}
follows directly from the occupation probabilities of Sec.
3.1
. Here,
\langle\dots\rangle_{t}
denotes the averaging over the time-evolved state,
\ket{\psi_I(t)}
. For
\mathfrak{su}(2)
with lowest weight
-j
, the probabilities
P_{n}(t)
correspond to a binomial distribution with parameters
N=2j
and
p=|z|^{2}/(1+|z|^{2})
, giving

(\Delta\mathcal{K})^{2}(t)=Np(1-p)=\frac{2j\,|z(t)|^{2}}{\bigl(1+|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{2}}.

(213)

For
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
with lowest weight
\kappa
, the probabilities follow a negative-binomial distribution with
r=2\kappa
and
p=|z|^{2}
, giving

(\Delta\mathcal{K})^{2}(t)=\frac{rp}{(1-p)^{2}}=\frac{2\kappa\,|z(t)|^{2}}{\bigl(1-|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{2}}.

(214)

Thus, the two cases unify into a single expression

(\Delta\mathcal{K})^{2}(t)=\frac{-2\sigma\lambda_{\alpha}\,|z(t)|^{2}}{\bigl(1+\sigma|z(t)|^{2}\bigr)^{2}},

(215)

with
z(t)
governed by the Riccati equation
\dot{z}=-i\gamma+i\sigma\gamma^{*}z^{2}
for
\sigma=\pm 1
. A similar analysis can be performed for the Hamiltonian variance. From
\langle H_{I}^{2}\rangle_{t}=|\gamma|^{2}\langle\{L_{+},L_{-}\}\rangle_{t}+2\Re[\gamma^{2}\langle L_{+}^{2}\rangle_{t}]
and
\langle H_{I}\rangle_{t}=2\Re[\gamma(t)C(t)]
, the anti-commutator and the second moment of
L_{+}
give

\langle\{L_{+},L_{-}\}\rangle_{t}=-2\sigma\lambda_{\alpha}+\frac{4\lambda_{\alpha}(2\lambda_{\alpha}+1)\,|z(t)|^{2}}{(1+\sigma|z(t)|^{2})^{2}},\qquad\langle L_{+}^{2}\rangle_{t}=\frac{2\lambda_{\alpha}(2\lambda_{\alpha}+1)\,(z^{*}(t))^{2}}{(1+\sigma|z(t)|^{2})^{2}}.

(216)

Using the identity
|\gamma(t)|^{2}|z(t)|^{2}+\Re[\gamma^{2}(t)(z^{*}(t))^{2}]=2\Re^{2}[\gamma(t)z^{*}(t)]
, all contributions collect into

(\Delta H_{I})^{2}(t)=-2\sigma\lambda_{\alpha}|\gamma(t)|^{2}+\frac{8\lambda_{\alpha}\,\Re^{2}[\gamma(t)z^{*}(t)]}{(1+\sigma|z(t)|^{2})^{2}}.

(217)

Next, we analyze the saturation condition. A time-independent phase of
\gamma(t)=e^{i\delta}|\gamma(t)|
makes
H_{I}(t)
proportional to a fixed algebra element, and hence
[H_{I}(t),H_{I}(t^{\prime})]=0
. When the phase of
\gamma(t)
varies in time, the Hamiltonians are generically non-commuting, and saturation can occur only under additional instantaneous phase-locking conditions.
For the coherent states considered here, direct evaluation of the two sides of the Schrödinger–Robertson relation gives

4(\Delta H_{I})^{2}(\Delta\mathcal{K})^{2}=|\partial_{t}K|^{2}+\langle\{\widetilde{H}_{I},\hat{\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}}\}\rangle^{2},\qquad\hat{\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}}\coloneqq\hat{\mathcal{K}}-\langle\hat{\mathcal{K}}\rangle,\quad\widetilde{H}_{I}\coloneqq H_{I}-\langle H_{I}\rangle.

(218)

To evaluate the symmetric covariance, one uses
\langle L_{+}\hat{\mathcal{K}}\rangle=\sum_{n}\varphi_{n+1}^{*}\varphi_{n}b_{n+1}n
, yielding from the exact expression for the wave-function, Eq. (
70
),

\langle L_{+}\hat{\mathcal{K}}\rangle=\frac{2\lambda_{\alpha}(2\lambda_{\alpha}+1)\,|z(t)|^{2}\,z^{*}(t)}{(1+\sigma|z(t)|^{2})^{2}}.

(219)

Inserting (
219
) and the coherent-state value of
C(t)
into
\langle\{\widetilde{H}_{I},\hat{\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}}\}\rangle_{t}=4\Re[\gamma\langle L_{+}\hat{\mathcal{K}}\rangle]+2\Re[\gamma(t)C(t)](1-2K)
, one finds upon simplifying that

\langle\{\widetilde{H}_{I},\hat{\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}}\}\rangle=\frac{4\lambda_{\alpha}(|z(t)|^{2}-\sigma)\,\Re[\gamma(t)z^{*}(t)]}{(1+\sigma|z(t)|^{2})^{2}}.

(220)

The Schrödinger–Robertson identity therefore gives

4(\Delta H_{I})^{2}(\Delta\mathcal{K})^{2}-|\partial_{t}K|^{2}=\frac{16\lambda_{\alpha}^{2}(|z(t)|^{2}-\sigma)^{2}\,\Re^{2}[\gamma(t)z^{*}(t)]}{(1+\sigma|z(t)|^{2})^{4}}\;\geq\;0.

(221)

The condition
\Re[\gamma(t)z^{*}(t)]=0
identifies the generic saturation criterion at a given time. At a single time this condition may be satisfied accidentally, even when the Hamiltonians at different times do not commute. Requiring it to hold persistently over a time interval is much more restrictive. Writing

\displaystyle\gamma(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\delta(t)}|\gamma(t)|,\qquad z(t)=-ie^{i\delta(t)}\zeta(t),\qquad\zeta(t)\in\mathbb{R},

(222)

and inserting this ansatz into the Riccati equation gives

\displaystyle\dot{\delta}(t)\,\zeta(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\qquad\dot{\zeta}(t)=|\gamma(t)|\bigl(1+\sigma\zeta^{2}(t)\bigr).

(223)

Hence, on any interval where the drive is nonzero and
z(t)\neq 0
, persistent phase locking forces
\dot{\delta}(t)=0
. Thus, persistent saturation through this generic evolution requires a time-independent phase of
\gamma(t)
on that interval. Equivalently,

\displaystyle[H_{I}(t),H_{I}(t^{\prime})]=2\sigma\!\left[\gamma(t)\gamma^{*}(t^{\prime})-\gamma^{*}(t)\gamma(t^{\prime})\right]L_{0},

(224)

so a constant phase of
\gamma(t)
makes the Hamiltonian commute with itself at different times. Conversely, when the phase of
\gamma(t)
varies in time, the Hamiltonians are generically non-commuting and the phase-locking condition can survive only at isolated accidental times, not as a persistent saturation mechanism.
In addition, the compact
\mathfrak{su}(2)
case admits an exceptional instantaneous saturation at all times. From Eq. (
221
), for
\sigma=+1
the right-hand side also vanishes when
|z(t)|=1
, independently of the value of
\Re[\gamma(t)z^{*}(t)]
. This is a special compact-sector effect and should be understood as an accidental saturation at isolated times rather than as a persistent saturation mechanism.

In the commuting case, the dispersion entering the bound reduces to the instantaneous hopping scale. Indeed, for the lowest-weight initial state one has

(\Delta H_{I})^{2}(t)=\langle\left(H_{I}-\langle H_{I}\rangle_{t}\right)^{2}\rangle_{t}=\langle\left(H_{I}-\langle H_{I}\rangle_{0}\right)^{2}\rangle_{t}=|\gamma(t)|^{2}b_{1}^{2},

so that the time-dependent bound is saturated as

|\partial_{t}K(t)|=2|\gamma(t)|b_{1}\,\Delta\mathcal{K}(t),

which is the natural time-dependent analogue of the time-independent
b_{1}
bound.

5.3
Heisenberg–Weyl algebra

Finally, we consider the Heisenberg–Weyl algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{3}
, with Hamiltonian

H_{I}(t)=\gamma^{*}(t)a+\gamma(t)a^{\dagger},\qquad[a,a^{\dagger}]=1.

(225)

For the vacuum initial state,
a\ket{0}=0
, the Krylov wave-function is a coherent state

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(t)=e^{i\Phi(t)-|\alpha(t)|^{2}/2}\frac{(-i)^{n}\alpha^{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n!}},

(226)

leading to a Poisson distribution over the Krylov basis with the Krylov complexity variance equaling the complexity itself

\displaystyle K(t)=\langle\hat{\mathcal{K}}\rangle_{t}=(\Delta\mathcal{K})^{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\alpha(t)|^{2}.

(227)

The variance of the Hamiltonian can be obtained from the coherent states as

\displaystyle(\Delta H_{I})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\gamma|^{2}\langle\{a,a^{\dagger}\}\rangle+\gamma^{2}\langle a^{\dagger 2}\rangle+(\gamma^{*})^{2}\langle a^{2}\rangle-\bigl(\gamma^{*}\langle a\rangle+\gamma\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle\bigr)^{2},

(228)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\gamma|^{2}\bigl(\langle\{a,a^{\dagger}\}\rangle-2|\langle a\rangle|^{2}\bigr)=|\gamma|^{2}\langle[a,a^{\dagger}]\rangle=|\gamma|^{2}.

(229)

On the other hand, the growth rate of the Krylov complexity is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}K(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}|\alpha(t)|^{2}=\dot{\alpha}(t)\alpha^{*}(t)+\alpha(t)\dot{\alpha}^{*}(t),

(230)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(t)\alpha^{*}(t)+\gamma^{*}(t)\alpha(t)=2\,\Re\!\left[\gamma(t)\alpha^{*}(t)\right],

(231)

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}K(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\,\Re^{2}\!\left[\gamma(t)\alpha^{*}(t)\right].

(232)

Thus, the bound reads

|\partial_{t}K(t)|^{2}\leq 4\,(\Delta H_{I})^{2}(t)\,(\Delta\mathcal{K})^{2}(t)=4\,|\gamma(t)|^{2}\,|\alpha(t)|^{2}.

(233)

As a result, the bound is saturated at a given time when

\gamma(t)\alpha^{*}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e., when the instantaneous
drive is phase-aligned with the accumulated displacement. Saturation for
all times, starting from
\alpha(0)=0
, requires this phase alignment
to persist throughout the evolution. This is obtained when

\gamma(t)=e^{i\delta}r(t)
, with constant
\delta
and real

r(t)
, in which case all Hamiltonians are proportional to the same
fixed quadrature and therefore commute at different times,

[H_{I}(t),H_{I}(t^{\prime})]=0
. For a genuinely time-dependent phase of

\gamma(t)
, this persistent alignment is generically lost, although
isolated accidental saturation times are not excluded.

Similarly to the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
and
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
cases,
in this commuting case the dispersion reduces to the instantaneous
hopping scale,

\displaystyle\Delta H_{I}(t)=|\gamma(t)|,

(234)

and the bound is saturated in the instantaneous form

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}K(t)|=2|\gamma(t)|\,\Delta\mathcal{K}(t),

(235)

with
b_{1}=1
for the Heisenberg–Weyl algebra.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we developed a Lie-algebraic framework for describing the quantum dynamics induced by a time-dependent generator in a time-dependent Krylov subspace. Our main result is that, for a broad class of driven Hamiltonians with an underlying algebraic structure, the exact time-dependent dynamics formulated in Krylov space can be organized directly in terms of the ladder operators and the root structure of the corresponding Lie algebra. In particular, we identified the minimal conditions under which the interaction-picture Hamiltonian reduces to an embedded rank-one algebra, yielding an exact one-dimensional Krylov chain governed by an effective
\mathfrak{su}(2)
or
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
structure. This yields closed expressions for the Krylov basis, the Lanczos coefficients, the Krylov wavefunction, and the Krylov complexity. We further showed that the Lanczos algorithm applied to the exact exponential representation of the time-evolution operator defines a distinct, effective time-independent Krylov dynamics. In this formulation, the temporal non-locality of the exact generator is no longer manifest at the level of the Krylov recursion, since it is encoded implicitly in the effective exponentiated operator. The resulting evolution in the same Krylov subspace therefore proceeds in a fictitious time, while the physical time enters only parametrically through the effective generator. Nevertheless, the exact time-dependent Krylov wavefunction is recovered by evaluating this auxiliary evolution at unit fictitious time.
The construction also extends naturally to higher-rank algebras with several commuting ladder pairs, where the dynamics acquires the structure of a higher-dimensional Krylov lattice. In that setting, the evolution factorizes into independent algebraic sectors, and the Krylov wavefunction and complexity decompose accordingly. This provides a geometric picture of how time-dependent operator growth is constrained by the root-space structure of the underlying algebra.

Beyond simple Lie algebras, we show that the same framework also applies to nilpotent settings, in particular to the Heisenberg–Weyl algebra and its oscillator extension, as realized for instance in the translated harmonic oscillator.
We illustrate the formalism in several representative physical settings, including the translated and dilated harmonic oscillator, a spin in a rotating magnetic field, closed Virasoro subalgebras, and higher-dimensional multilevel examples. These examples show that the formalism is not only algebraically natural, but also directly applicable to physically relevant driven systems. In each case, the Lie-algebraic structure determines the corresponding Krylov hierarchy and enables an exact characterization of the associated complexity growth.

Finally, we generalized the quantum speed limit to Krylov complexity to time-dependent generators and analyzed it in the algebraic setting for the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
,
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
, and Heisenberg–Weyl cases. We found that the bound retains the same Robertson form as in the time-independent case, while its saturation is strongly constrained by the driving. Persistent saturation occurs only under the corresponding phase-locking condition, which in the commuting case is realized when the Hamiltonian is proportional to a fixed algebra element at all times.

Overall, our results provide a unified algebraic perspective on dynamics in time-dependent Krylov subspaces and establish a direct link between Krylov complexity and the symmetry structure of the Hamiltonian. They further show that exact nonequilibrium Krylov dynamics is accessible in broad classes of systems whenever the interaction-picture evolution can be organized into closed ladder structures. A natural direction for future work is to relax the present commutativity and closure conditions in order to describe more general interacting many-body systems, non-factorizable higher-rank dynamics, and genuinely non-integrable regimes. It would also be interesting to combine the present framework with approximate algebraic truncation schemes, with the aim of capturing complexity growth in systems where exact Lie closure is only weakly broken, and to extend the construction to more general higher-rank simple Lie algebras associated with non-orthogonal higher-dimensional Krylov lattices.

i\partial_{s}\ket{\psi_I(s)}=G(t)\ket{\psi_I(s)},\qquad\ket{\psi_I(0)}=\ket{\lambda}\equiv\ket{0},\qquad s\in[0,1].

(261)

Expanding

\ket{\psi_I(s)}=\sum_{n}\psi_{n}(s)\ket{K_n},

(262)

and using the tridiagonal action of
G
, one obtains

i\partial_{s}\psi_{n}(s)=a_{n}\,\psi_{n}(s)+\tilde{b}_{n+1}\,\psi_{n+1}(s)+\tilde{b}_{n}^{*}\,\psi_{n-1}(s),\qquad\psi_{n}(0)=\delta_{n,0},

(263)

where
a_{n}=\Theta_{0}(\lambda+n)
and
\tilde{b}_{n}=\Theta_{+}b_{n}
are constant in
s
. Since
G(t)
is
s
-independent, the solution is formally

\ket{\psi_I(s)}=e^{-isG(t)}\ket{0}.

(264)

The disentangling formula (
254
) then applies at any
s
,

e^{-isG(t)}=e^{Z(s)L_{+}}e^{\mathcal{E}(s)L_{0}}e^{W(s)L_{-}}.

(265)

Acting on
\ket{0}
, the rightmost factor drops out because
L_{-}\ket{0}=0
, while the middle factor contributes
e^{\lambda\mathcal{E}(s)}
. Hence

\psi_{n}(s)=\bra{K_n}\ket{\psi_I(s)}=e^{\lambda\mathcal{E}(s)}\,\frac{Z(s)^{n}}{n!}\sqrt{\bra{0}L_{-}^{n}L_{+}^{n}\ket{0}}\,.

(266)

To identify the solution at
s=1
with the physical Krylov wavefunction, recall that by definition

U_{I}(t)=e^{-iG(t)},

(267)

while the main text factorizes the same operator as

U_{I}(t)=e^{z(t)L_{+}}e^{\eta(t)L_{0}}e^{w(t)L_{-}}.

(268)

Setting
s=1
in the auxiliary disentangling formula gives

e^{-iG(t)}=e^{Z(1)L_{+}}e^{\mathcal{E}(1)L_{0}}e^{W(1)L_{-}}.

(269)

Since both factorizations represent the same operator with the unique Wei–Norman decomposition, the corresponding coefficients can be identified,

Z(1)=z(t),\qquad\mathcal{E}(1)=\eta(t),\qquad W(1)=w(t).

(270)

Substituting this into the expression for
\psi_{n}(s)
at
s=1
yields

\psi_{n}(1)=e^{\lambda\eta(t)}\,\frac{z(t)^{n}}{n!}\sqrt{\bra{0}L_{-}^{n}L_{+}^{n}\ket{0}}\,,

(271)

which is precisely
\varphi_{n}(t)=\innerproduct{K_n}{U_I(t)\ket{0}}
obtained from the Wei–Norman solution in the main text. Thus, the auxiliary tridiagonal recursion (
263
), whose coefficients are determined by the Lanczos algorithm applied to
G(t)
, reproduces the exact interaction-picture Krylov wavefunction at
s=1
.

As a consistency check, consider the constant-phase case

\gamma(t)=e^{i\delta}r(t),

(272)

with
r(t)
real. Then

H_{I}(t)=r(t)\bigl(e^{i\delta}L_{+}+e^{-i\delta}L_{-}\bigr)

(273)

is proportional at all times to a fixed algebra element, so time ordering is trivial, and one immediately reads off

\Theta_{0}=0,\qquad\Theta_{+}=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(274)

The same result follows from the inversion formulas (
258
). Indeed, the Wei–Norman equations

\partial_{t}z=-i\gamma+i\sigma\gamma^{*}z^{2},\qquad\partial_{t}\eta=2i\sigma\gamma^{*}z,

(275)

are solved by the ansatz
z=e^{i\delta}\zeta
with
\zeta
purely imaginary, which reduces the Riccati equation to

\partial_{t}\zeta=ir(\sigma\zeta^{2}-1).

(276)

Writing

R(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}r(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

(277)

the solution is

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+1:\quad\zeta=-i\tan R,\quad\eta=-2\ln\cos R;

(278)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1:\quad\zeta=-i\tanh R,\quad\eta=-2\ln\cosh R.

(279)

For
\sigma=+1
, the formula is understood on intervals where
\cos R\neq 0
, with the operator relation continued by continuity.
In both cases
\eta
is real, so
A=e^{-\eta/2}
is real and

B=ze^{-\eta/2}=-ie^{i\delta}\sin_{\sigma}R,

(280)

where
\sin_{+1}\equiv\sin
and
\sin_{-1}\equiv\sinh
. Equation (
258
) then gives

\chi=\begin{cases}\;R&(\sigma=+1),\\
\;iR&(\sigma=-1),\end{cases}\qquad\Theta_{0}=0,\qquad\Theta_{+}=e^{i\delta}R=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

(281)

confirming the expected result in both the compact and non-compact cases. When the phase of
\gamma(t)
varies in time,
\eta
can acquire an imaginary part,
\operatorname{Im}[e^{-\eta/2}]\neq 0
, and a non-vanishing Cartan component
\Theta_{0}
is then generated generically.

Appendix B
Krylov basis in the Schrödinger picture

In this section, we show that the time-dependent Krylov dynamics is invariant under the transformation back to the Schrödinger picture. More precisely, the same Krylov amplitudes are obtained in a suitably chosen moving basis, provided the ladder operators are dressed by the time-dependent Cartan phases.
Let

W(t)=U_{C}(t)U_{E}^{(I)}(t),\qquad\ket{\psi(t)}=W(t)\ket{\psi_I(t)}.

(282)

We define the Schrödinger-picture moving Krylov basis by transporting the interaction-picture basis with
W(t)
,

\ket{\mathcal K_n(t)}\coloneqq W(t)\ket{K_n}.

(283)

With this choice, the Schrödinger-picture state admits the expansion

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{d_{K}-1}\varphi_{n}(t)\ket{\mathcal K_n(t)},

(284)

so that the expansion coefficients are exactly the same
\varphi_{n}(t)
as in the interaction picture.

The effective generator governing the amplitudes in this moving basis is

W^{\dagger}(t)H(t)W(t)-iW^{\dagger}(t)\partial_{t}W(t)=H_{I}(t).

(285)

Therefore,

i\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{d_{K}-1}\bra{K_n}H_{I}(t)\ket{K_m}\varphi_{m}(t),

(286)

and, since
H_{I}(t)
is tridiagonal in the basis
\{\ket{K_n}\}
, one recovers the same off-diagonal Krylov equation,

i\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)=\gamma(t)b_{n}\,\varphi_{n-1}(t)+\gamma^{*}(t)b_{n+1}\,\varphi_{n+1}(t).

(287)

As a result, the Krylov wavefunction satisfies the same nearest-neighbor tridiagonal recursion even though the state is expressed in the Schrödinger picture.

It is convenient to rewrite the moving basis in terms of a moving lowest-weight state and dressed ladder operators. Defining

\ket{\lambda_S(t)}\coloneqq W(t)\ket{\lambda}=U_{C}(t)U_{E}^{(I)}(t)\ket{\lambda},

(288)

and

\mathcal{L}_{\pm}(t)\coloneqq W(t)L_{\pm}W^{\dagger}(t),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)\coloneqq W(t)L_{0}W^{\dagger}(t),

(289)

one finds, using
[U_{E}^{(I)}(t),L_{\pm,0}]=0
together with

U_{C}^{\dagger}(t)L_{+}U_{C}(t)=e^{i\phi_{\alpha}(t)}L_{+},\qquad U_{C}^{\dagger}(t)L_{-}U_{C}(t)=e^{-i\phi_{\alpha}(t)}L_{-},

(290)

that

\mathcal{L}_{+}(t)=e^{-i\phi_{\alpha}(t)}L_{+},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{-}(t)=e^{i\phi_{\alpha}(t)}L_{-},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)=L_{0}.

(291)

Moreover,

\mathcal{L}_{-}(t)\ket{\lambda_S(t)}=0,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{0}(t)\ket{\lambda_S(t)}=\lambda_{\alpha}\ket{\lambda_S(t)},

(292)

so that
\ket{\lambda_S(t)}
remains a lowest-weight state for the dressed ladder structure. The moving Krylov states may therefore be written as

\ket{\mathcal K_n(t)}=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{+}^{n}(t)}{\sqrt{\bra{\lambda_S(t)}\mathcal{L}_{-}^{n}(t)\mathcal{L}_{+}^{n}(t)\ket{\lambda_S(t)}}}\ket{\lambda_S(t)}=e^{-in\phi_{\alpha}(t)}\frac{L_{+}^{n}}{\sqrt{\bra{\lambda}L_{-}^{n}L_{+}^{n}\ket{\lambda}}}\ket{\lambda_S(t)}.

(293)

Thus, the Schrödinger-picture basis that preserves the off-diagonal Krylov form is still generated by repeated ladder action, but now with the dressed raising operator
\mathcal{L}_{+}(t)
, or equivalently with the bare raising operator supplemented by the compensating phase
e^{-in\phi_{\alpha}(t)}
.

For comparison, suppose instead that one defines the more naive moving basis

\ket*{\hat K_n(t)}\coloneqq\frac{L_{+}^{n}}{\sqrt{\bra{\lambda}L_{-}^{n}L_{+}^{n}\ket{\lambda}}}\ket{\lambda_S(t)}.

(294)

Then

\ket*{\hat K_n(t)}=e^{in\phi_{\alpha}(t)}\ket{\mathcal K_n(t)}.

(295)

Expanding the Schrödinger-picture state as

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{d_{K}-1}\hat{\varphi}_{n}(t)\ket*{\hat K_n(t)},

(296)

the amplitudes are related to the ones in the interaction picture by

\hat{\varphi}_{n}(t)=e^{-in\phi_{\alpha}(t)}\varphi_{n}(t).

(297)

Using the relation
\gamma(t)=e^{i\phi_{\alpha}(t)}f(t)
, inherited from the interaction-picture construction in the main text, these amplitudes satisfy

i\partial_{t}\hat{\varphi}_{n}(t)=n\,\dot{\phi}_{\alpha}(t)\,\hat{\varphi}_{n}(t)+f(t)b_{n}\,\hat{\varphi}_{n-1}(t)+f^{*}(t)b_{n+1}\,\hat{\varphi}_{n+1}(t).

(298)

The diagonal contribution
n\,\dot{\phi}_{\alpha}(t)
is thus a gauge term induced by working in the naive moving basis
\{\ket*{\hat K_n(t)}\}
rather than in the compensated basis
\{\ket{\mathcal K_n(t)}\}
.

In this way, the same off-diagonal Krylov dynamics may be formulated either in the final interaction picture with the time-independent basis
\{\ket*{K_n}\}
, or equivalently in the Schrödinger picture with the moving basis
\{\ket{\mathcal K_n(t)}\}
. The two descriptions are completely equivalent and differ only in how the Cartan phase is distributed between basis states and amplitudes.
