Impact of cannabis use and tobacco smoking on outcomes of open carpal tunnel release surgery: a nationwide study in the United States
Authors: Amir Human Hoveidaei, Sina Esmaeili, Ruby Gilmor, Zhongming Chen, Janet D Conway, John V Ingari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the impact of cannabis use, tobacco smoking, and their concurrent use on postoperative complications following open carpal tunnel release (CTR) using a nationwide database.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the PearlDiver database (2010–2022) to identify adult patients who underwent open CTR. Patients were categorized into four groups: cannabis-only users (n = 800), tobacco-only users (n = 167,803), concurrent users (n = 3529), and non-users (n = 167,803 matched controls). Postoperative complications, surgical site infection, wound disruption, joint stiffness, and complex regional pain syndrome, were assessed at 6 weeks and 3 months postoperatively.ResultsAt 6 weeks and 3 months, surgical site infection was more common among concurrent cannabis and tobacco users, cannabis-only users, and tobacco-only users. Wound disruption occurred more frequently in concurrent users and tobacco-only users, but not in cannabis-only users. Complex regional pain syndrome was associated only with tobacco use. No association was found between any substance use and joint stiffness.ConclusionCannabis use, tobacco smoking, and especially concurrent use increase the risk of surgical site infection and wound disruption following open CTR. Tobacco use is independently associated with complex regional pain syndrome, while cannabis may have a protective effect. These findings emphasize the need for preoperative screening and patient education.Type of study/level of evidenceRetrospective cohort study/level III.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00402-026-06391-4.Keywords: Carpal tunnel release, Cannabis, Tobacco, Surgical site infection, Outcomes

Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), with a prevalence of 4–5% globally, occurs when the median nerve is compressed as it passes through the carpal tunnel [1, 2]. Clinical symptoms such as paresthesias, discomfort, and numbness can progress to hand weakness, impaired fine motor function, and thenar muscle atrophy that can lead to diminished quality of life for the patient [3, 4]. Carpal tunnel release (CTR) is a highly effective and common procedure for treating carpal tunnel syndrome, but it can lead to various complications, including surgical site infection (SSI), nerve lacerations, joint stiffness, complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), and wound disruption [5–8].

In 2020, around 209 million people globally (4%) aged 15–64 used cannabis, while in the United States, 52.4 million individuals aged 12 and older (18.7%) reported cannabis use in 2021 [9]. A 2024 study showed that from 2008 to 2022, annual cannabis use per capita in the United States rose by 120%, and usage days increased by 218% [10]. However, tobacco use in the United States among 18 to24-year-olds, smoking rates decreased from 19.2% in 2011 to 4.9% in 2022 [11]. Cannabis use raises perioperative risks, including slight increases in morbidity and mortality after major surgery [12]. Similarly, tobacco use before surgery raises the risk of wound complications, infections, pulmonary issues, and overall morbidity [13].

After orthopaedic procedures, cannabis use, especially after total joint arthroplasty, is linked to increased revision surgeries, fractures, and dislocations [14]. Smoking tobacco also leads to wound complications, delayed fracture healing, and periprosthetic joint infections (PJI) [15, 16]. Two studies investigated how using both cannabis and tobacco affects orthopaedic surgery outcomes [17, 18]. Concurrent users had higher risks of complications such as nonunion, malunion, and SSI after distal radius fracture surgery, and increased risks of periprosthetic fracture, dislocation, and infection after shoulder arthroplasty, which is generally associated with low complication rates [17–20].

This study aims to evaluate how cannabis use and tobacco smoking, independently or combined, affect outcomes of open CTR surgery, using nationwide data from 2010 to 2022.

Methods

Data source

A retrospective cohort methodology was utilized to analyze data from the PearlDiver Insurance Database (PearlDiver Technologies, Colorado Springs, CO, USA), which contains patient information from 2010 to 2022. For this investigation, the M170rtho dataset, which contains about 170 million anonymized patient records, was used. All patient data were de-identified in accordance with HIPAA requirements. Furthermore, audit rules and internal assessments were applied to claims data to assure its authenticity and dependability. Patient data, including features, diagnosis, and medical procedures, were queried using International Classification of Diseases 9th and 10th (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes or current practice terms (CPT). Due to the nature of this research, no institutional review board approval was necessary.

Patient selection

The study included adult patients aged 18 years and older who underwent primary open CTR surgery, identified by CPT code 64,721, for carpal tunnel syndrome. Patients were required to have a minimum of 90 days of active postoperative follow-up. A total of 1,205,246 patients met these inclusion criteria. Smoking-related substance use was assessed based on ICD codes recorded within one year prior to surgery (see Appendix Table S1 for all codes used to query the PearlDiver database). Patients were classified into four groups according to their substance use: those who used only tobacco (n = 167,803), only cannabis (n = 800), both substances concurrently (n = 3529), or neither (n = 888,058). To ensure balanced group sizes for comparison, the PearlDiver system randomly selected sample of 167,803 from the large pool of non-users, matching the size of the tobacco-only group; this group was designated as the control group. This sampling approach was intended to improve the accuracy and robustness of the multivariable regression analyses (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were postoperative complications occurring at two time intervals following open CTR: at 6 weeks and at 3 months postoperatively. The complications of interest included surgical site infection (SSI), wound disruption, joint stiffness (patients with preoperative records of stiffness were excluded), and Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS). These outcomes were identified using corresponding ICD codes within the database (Appendix Table S1).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using the R statistical software (version 4.1.0; R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) integrated within the PearlDiver platform. Continuous variables, including patient age and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), were reported as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables such as sex, obesity, diabetes, and hypertension were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group differences in baseline characteristics were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Logistic regression models were applied to evaluate the association between substance use and each postoperative complication, adjusting for potential confounders including age, gender, CCI, diabetes, obesity, hypertension, opioid use, and alcohol abuse. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05.

Patient characteristics

There were significant differences across all demographics among the four groups (Table 1). The control group had the highest mean age (58.88 ± 13.41 years), while concurrent tobacco and cannabis users were the youngest (46.77 ± 11.93 years) (p < 0.01). The proportion of male patients was lowest in the control group (35.6%) and highest among cannabis-only users (44.9%) (p < 0.01). Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores were lowest in the control group (1.22 ± 1.78) and highest in concurrent users (3.02 ± 2.35) (p < 0.01). Obesity was most prevalent among tobacco-only users (56.7%), followed closely by concurrent users (56.0%) and cannabis-only users (54.3%), with the lowest prevalence in the control group (46.5%) (p < 0.01). The highest rates of diabetes mellitus and hypertension were observed in the tobacco-only group (43.4% and 78.7%, respectively), while the lowest were among cannabis-only users (32.6% and 66.9%, respectively) (p < 0.01).

Results

Postoperative complications (bivariate analysis)

Rates of postoperative complications differed significantly across groups for most outcomes (Table 2). At both 6 weeks and 3 months postoperatively, SSIs were significantly more frequent in substance users compared to controls. By 3 months, SSI was observed in 2.10% of concurrent users, 1.25% of cannabis-only users, and 1.05% of tobacco-only users, compared to just 0.42% in the control group (p < 0.01). Wound disruption followed a similar pattern. At 3 months, wound disruption occurred in 1.87% of concurrent users, 0.65% of tobacco-only users, and 0.50% of cannabis-only users, compared to 0.17% of controls (p < 0.01). Joint stiffness was the most common complication overall. While there were no significant differences in joint stiffness at 6 weeks (p = 0.18), 3-month rates were higher in cannabis-only (2.88%) and concurrent users (2.83%) compared to controls (2.18%; p = 0.03).

Multivariable analysis at 6 weeks

After adjusting for potential cofounders, substance use remained significantly associated with several postoperative complications at 6 weeks. Compared to controls, patients in the cannabis-only group had increased odds of SSI (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.09–3.90), while tobacco-only users also demonstrated elevated risk (OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.61–1.98). The greatest risk was observed in concurrent users (OR 2.73, 95% CI 2.07–3.55). Wound disruption was significantly associated with tobacco-only use (OR 2.77, 95% CI 2.39–3.23) and concurrent use (OR 4.55, 95% CI 3.26–6.24), though not cannabis use alone (OR 2.02, 95% CI 0.62–4.79). CRPS was significantly associated with tobacco use (OR 2.34, 95% CI 1.47–3.80), while elevated but nonsignificant odds were observed in the cannabis-only and concurrent use groups. None of the substance use groups showed a statistically significant association with joint stiffness at this time point (Table 3).

Multivariable analysis at 3 months

By 3 months postoperatively, substance use continued to be associated with increased odds of infection and wound complications. Cannabis-only users remained at elevated risk of SSI (OR 1.77, 95% CI 1.08–3.16), while both tobacco-only (OR 1.82, 95% CI 1.66–2.00) and concurrent users (OR 2.47, 95% CI 1.91–3.16) also had significantly increased odds. Wound disruption risk was again highest among concurrent users (OR 5.14, 95% CI 3.84–6.79), followed by tobacco-only (OR 2.68, 95% CI 2.34–3.08); cannabis-only use did not reach statistical significance (OR 1.70, 95% CI 0.52–4.02). CRPS remained significantly associated with tobacco use (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.56–2.82). As with the 6-week analysis, no substance use group demonstrated a significant association with joint stiffness (Table 3).

Discussion

This study shows that cannabis use, tobacco smoking, and co-use of cannabis and tobacco increased the SSI rate after open CTR at both 6-week and 3-month intervals. Cannabis usage, whether therapeutic or recreational, increases the risk of bacterial, viral, parasitic, and fungal infections, presumably via altering the immune system [21]. Cannabidiol may act as an immune suppressor by inhibiting the activation of certain immune cell types, inducing apoptosis, and promoting regulatory cells, all of which affect other immune cell targets [22]. Cannabis has also been shown to decrease the production of interleukin levels and certain cytokines, as well as immune cells’ chemotaxis capabilities [23, 24]. Cannabis use has been connected to a number of detrimental social, economic, and health consequences, with the latter affecting almost every physiological and biochemical system, which may contribute to the increased SSI rate [22, 25]. Tobacco use leads to SSIs by impeding wound healing through a variety of ways. Nicotine-induced vasoconstriction and carbon monoxide exposure reduce tissue oxygenation, while smoking also weakens the immune response by impairing inflammatory cell function and disrupting the balance of healing-related enzymes [26]. Our data showed that co-using cannabis and tobacco increased the risk of SSI with an OR of 2.47, expected to have a synergistic effect because of its combined effects on the immune system and wound healing processes. Tobacco and cannabis users have been found to have significantly higher levels of biomarkers for toxicant exposure, such as acrylonitrile and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, which are known to impede cellular activities necessary for wound healing [27]. This increased toxicant burden can lead to higher rates of postoperative complications, including SSI [28, 29].

Our findings support previous research demonstrating that cannabis usage and tobacco smoking enhance the risk of postoperative SSI. Cannabis usage in orthopaedic surgeries, including total knee and shoulder arthroplasty, has been linked to an increase in SSI [18, 28]. A meta-analysis by Liu et al. discovered that smoking is a significant risk factor for SSIs after orthopaedic surgery, with an OR of 2.87 [30]. A recent study discovered that concurrent users had a higher risk of SSI after distal radius fracture surgery [17].

Our data show that tobacco smoking alone and cannabis and tobacco smoking together have an effect on the rate of wound disruption following open CTR at both 6-week and 3-month intervals. Tobacco smoking decreases tissue oxygenation and aerobic metabolism, which are critical for wound healing. It also attenuates the inflammatory response by reducing the chemotactic responsiveness and migratory function of inflammatory cells, and it impairs the proliferative response by reducing fibroblast migration and collagen synthesis [26]. Furthermore, smoking has been shown to increase the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which can contribute to delayed wound healing [31]. A study by Cho et al. reported higher rates of wound healing issues in tobacco smokers undergoing hand surgery [32]. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that tobacco users had significantly greater risks of wound complications with OR of 1.78, following total hip and knee arthroplasty [15], and a study by Duchman et al. showed that current smokers had the highest wound complication rates compared to former smokers and nonsmokers [33]. Patients who smoke tobacco and/or cannabis should be made aware of their potentially increased risk via patient education materials as well as by their treating physician directly [34].

One of the most important findings from our study was that tobacco smoking alone increases risk of developing CRPS, whereas cannabis use and co-use do not, following open CTR at 6-week and 3-month intervals. Chronic nicotine exposure and subsequent withdrawal alter pain sensitivity, leading to hyperalgesia due to changes in pain-related neural pathways, including alterations in glutamate decarboxylase, µ-opioid receptors, and GABA expression [35]. Additionally, nicotine withdrawal has been linked to hyperalgesia through downregulation of the descending serotonergic pathway in the nucleus raphe magnus [36]. Smoking is also associated with heightened inflammatory responses; chronic nicotine exposure increases levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNFα and COX-2, along with a greater density of neuro-immune cells in the spinal cord, further contributing to pain sensitization [37]. Studies show smokers are more likely to develop CRPS and report higher pain levels [38, 39]. Smoking also reduces the effectiveness of spinal cord stimulation as well as peripheral nerve stimulation, potentially limiting their utility in this population [40–43]. A bidirectional relationship exists, where pain increases smoking and smoking worsens pain, creating a cycle that exacerbates CRPS severity and complicates pain management [44, 45].

However, according to our data analysis, cannabis consumption has a protective effect relative to CRPS. Cannabis has numerous analgesic effects [46]. Tetrahydrocannabinol activates CB1 and CB2 receptors, limiting neurotransmitter release through lower cAMP and calcium influx, whereas CBD modifies TRPV1 channels and increases anandamide signaling [46–48]. These interventions reduce central nociceptive transmission and neuroinflammation [49]. Supporting our findings, clinical trials and meta-analyses have shown modest benefits of cannabinoids in chronic pain, particularly with oral formulations. For instance, a meta-analysis found small improvements in pain and sleep [50–52]. It is noteworthy that, the cannabis-only cohort (n = 800) was substantially smaller than the tobacco-only and control groups, reflecting the lower prevalence of formally coded cannabis use in administrative claims data. This imbalance limits the statistical power to detect associations between cannabis-only use and less frequent outcomes such as wound disruption and CRPS, and contributes to the notably wider confidence intervals observed for this group. Accordingly, findings in the cannabis-only cohort should be interpreted with caution due to limited sample size and the observational nature of the study.

Our study was the first national-scale investigation into how cannabis use and tobacco smoking affects open CTR outcomes, and it assessed multiple items. Using PearlDiver database permits a diverse group of patients to be included in the study, boosting the findings’ generalizability. Conversely, similar to all retrospective studies using insurance-based databases, this dataset was not designed specifically for research purposes. As a result, certain exposures may be misclassified or incompletely captured. Cannabis and tobacco use were identified using ICD diagnostic codes and are therefore subject to underreporting, particularly for cannabis use. Patients with undocumented substance use may have been misclassified as non-users. Because substance-use status was recorded before surgery and before the occurrence of postoperative complications, such misclassification is likely non-differential and would be expected to bias effect estimates toward the null, potentially underestimating the true associations between substance use and postoperative complications. To maximize identification of substance users, we included all relevant ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes documented within one year prior to surgery. Also, the database does not distinguish between recreational and medical cannabis use and lacks information regarding dose, frequency, route of administration, and cannabis formulation. Consequently, all documented cannabis users were analyzed as a single cohort, which may have introduced residual confounding. Additionally, the database does not include a distinct ICD code for vaping use or dependence, preventing us from analyzing vaping as a separate exposure category. Furthermore, smoking status could not be definitively differentiated between current and former smokers. Although we defined smoking based on codes documented within one year prior to surgery to approximate recent or active use, some misclassification is still possible. Regarding outcome ascertainment, both SSI and CRPS were identified using ICD diagnosis codes documented by treating clinicians within the database, rather than through prospectively verified clinical criteria. SSI identification was not confirmed against standardized definitions. Similarly, CRPS diagnoses were captured through ICD codes and we are unable to verify whether the standardized criteria were formally applied at the point of care. A prospective clinical study evaluating detailed substance-use history and applying standardized diagnostic criteria for both SSI and CRPS would allow for more precise classification and improve the accuracy and interpretability of future findings.

In conclusions, this study demonstrates that both cannabis and tobacco use, particularly co-use, are associated with increased rates of SSI and wound complications following open CTR. Tobacco use alone was additionally linked to a higher risk of CRPS, whereas cannabis use showed a potentially protective effect against CRPS. These findings highlight the need for careful preoperative screening of substance use and patient counseling regarding potential surgical risks. Further prospective studies are warranted to better understand the underlying mechanisms and to guide perioperative management in substance-using patients.
