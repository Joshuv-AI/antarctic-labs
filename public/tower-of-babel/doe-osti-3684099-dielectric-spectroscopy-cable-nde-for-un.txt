 SUMMARY
 The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory assessed the effect of unequal aging over different lengths
of cable using dielectric spectroscopy (DS) bulk impedance measurements from the cable end. DS
measurements can indicate the aged condition of insulated cables; however, most benchmark tests are
based on accelerated aging of the entire cable. If only a portion of the cable is exposed to a harsh
environment (as is frequently the case), the DS measurement will indicate a significantly less-aged cable
than if the entire cable were exposed to a uniform environmental stress. The DS measurement is primarily
related to the intrinsic insulation material relative permittivity—with values typically between 1.5 and 3.0.
Insulation aging increases permittivity and, correspondingly, the overall capacitance of the cable
measured from the cable end. To judge the health of the overall cable system, operators are primarily
interested in the condition of the most severely aged section since that is where a failure is most likely to
occur. This can be expressed as the dimensionless relative permittivity of the severely aged section
divided by the corresponding permittivity of a pristine cable. This relative permittivity is an intrinsic
material parameter independent of cable length and can serve as a quantitative indication of the insulation
condition. If the relative percentages of cable length exposed to a harsh and benign environment are
known, the DS measurement of the entire cable length, including both pristine and aged sections, can be
compensated to predict the relative permittivity (relative to pristine permittivity) of the most severely
aged segment.
 A test was performed on three 50 ft (15 m) cables with 10%, 50%, and 90% of each cable inside a
thermal aging oven. Predictably, the aging effect on the DS response was greatest in the 90% sample,
followed by the 50% sample, and then the 10% sample. The more interesting result is the ratio of the aged
insulation permittivity to the pristine insulation permittivity. Based on prior work, an end-of-life cable has
a relative permittivity value of approximately 1.35. If the relative percentages of pristine and severely
aged cable lengths are known, and the total cable-length capacitance of the initial pristine cable and the
combined pristine plus aged cable are known, the relative aged permittivity (ratio of aged section
permittivity / pristine section permittivity) can be estimated. The relative cable lengths exposed to a harsh
environment may be known or estimated based on plant layout drawings or cable reflectometry tests that
can locate the oven entry and exit points. The compensation was verified using a lumped-parameter
model to predict the aged-segment permittivity ε2/ε1, where ε2 is the aged-segment permittivity and ε1 is
the pristine-segment permittivity. For all three percentages of aged cable lengths, the ratio was quite
similar, as would be expected since all cable segments were exposed to the same aging environment. This
ratio of aged permittivity to pristine permittivity can be compared to other damage indicating tests,
including elongation at break, to enable cable aging management decisions.

 1. OBJECTIVE
 The primary objective of this milestone report from Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) is
to rigorously evaluate how dielectric spectroscopy (DS) measurements are influenced when only portions
of the total length of a low‑voltage cable have undergone accelerated global aging. Because DS is a bulk
electrical property measurement, its response reflects the combined condition of all dielectric materials
within the energized test domain. When a cable contains sections with differing degradation profiles, the
measured spectrum becomes a convolution of pristine and aged segments, making interpretation
non‑trivial. The work described in this report aims to understand, quantify, and compensate for these
effects so that DS can be used reliably for condition monitoring of field‑deployed cables where aging is
rarely uniform.

 2. BACKGROUND AND LITERATURE REVIEW
 As nuclear power plants (NPPs) apply for second or subsequent license renewals to extend their
operating period from 60 to 80 years and beyond, it is important to understand how materials installed in
plant systems and components will age during that time and to develop aging management programs
(AMPs) to ensure continued safe operation under normal operation and design-basis events (DBEs).
Degradation of cable insulation is known to occur as a function of age, temperature, radiation, and other
environmental factors. Although system tests verify cable function under normal loads, concerns remain
for cable performance under exceptional loads associated with DBEs. The ability of cables to perform
safely over the initial 40-year planned and licensed life has generally been demonstrated, and there have
been very few age-related cable failures [1]. With greater than 1000 km of power, control,
instrumentation, and other cables typically found in an NPP, replacing all the cables would be a severe
cost and logistical burden. License renewal to 60 years and subsequent license renewal to 80 years,
therefore, requires a cable AMP in accordance with regulatory guidance [2] to justify cable performance
under normal operation as well as accident conditions.
 Acceptance criteria that define the threshold for degradation below which cables may continue to be
used are a challenge, because it is impractical to subject each cable system to loss-of-coolant accident
(LOCA) or seismic simulation events following 40+ years of service. The Initial Acceptance Criteria
Concepts and Data for Assessing Longevity of Low-Voltage Cable Insulations and Jackets report [3]
develops a basis for acceptance criteria and evaluates the aging profiles for many commonly used cable
jackets and polymers. The report describes 50% elongation at break (EAB) as a conservative practical
end-of-life threshold for cables that may be stressed during maintenance or subjected to LOCA exposure.
The report also discusses the basis for cautious continued use of cables beyond the 50% EAB threshold.
EAB inherently compromises the in-service cable use as it is a destructive ex situ test, so the challenge is
to develop nondestructive evaluation (NDE) methods that can reasonably be correlated with EAB.
Reliable in situ NDE approaches are needed to objectively determine the suitability of installed cables for
continued service. One NDE test that has generally been shown to relate to EAB is a direct or indirect
measurement of permittivity. A variety of tests are available to assess various aspects of electrical and
mechanical cable performance, but none of the available tests are suitable for all cable configurations nor
does any single test assess all features of interest. Direct current (DC) withstand tests at or above the rated
cable voltage and low frequency dielectric dissipation factor (tan δ) tests are widely applied, particularly
during commissioning to confirm quality installations, but these tests stress the polymers, which may
reduce the life of the cable system and should not be used excessively.
 Low voltage DS has been used for decades to characterize the electrical behavior of polymers and
other dielectric materials without introducing any significant damage to the dielectric material [4]. Several
researchers have applied this general method to cable tests as indicated below.
 2.1 Technical Reports on Dielectric Spectroscopy of Cables
2.1.1 EPRI Technical Report 1022969 Plant Engineering: Electrical Cable Test
 Applicability Matrix for Nuclear Power Plants
 Toman [5] describes the state of the art for in situ NPP cable tests and characterizes advantages and
weaknesses of each test, with particular focus on aged unshielded power cables for instrumentation and
control (I&C) (600V–5kV) from Canadian nuclear and thermal generation plants. That report describes a
DS test as a current passing through the insulation in response to a step change in voltage that is analyzed
by transforming the acquired current data into a function of frequency to arrive at dielectric impedance as
a function of frequency. The variation in dielectric impedance is primarily due to bulk cable capacitance
and conductance. The real and imaginary parts of the permittivity, the change in the real permittivity, and
the tan δ are evaluated with respect to voltage and frequency change. At each applied voltage, a frequency
sweep is performed, and the permittivity and tan δ parameters are evaluated.
 The following four electrical response behaviors have been defined:

 3

• Low-loss, linear permittivity: The permittivity response for new or non-water-tree-deteriorated cables
 are nearly frequency independent, the loss is low and has a very weak frequency dependence, and the
 real and imaginary parts of the permittivity are independent of applied voltage.
• Voltage-dependent permittivity: An indication of water treeing that is significant but has not yet
 penetrated the whole of the insulation, in which the real and imaginary parts of the permittivity are
 voltage dependent.
• Transition to leakage current: An indication that the water trees penetrate through the wall of the
 insulation and that the breakdown strength of the insulation is significantly reduced. The transition is
 indicated by an increased loss at the time of applied voltage. In other words, the second measurement
 at a specific voltage level has a higher loss than the initial measurement.
• Leakage current: An indication that water trees penetrate the whole of the insulation and the cable has
 very low breakdown strength. Leakage current is observed at low voltages; loss increases with
 decreasing frequency, and the real part of the permittivity shows a voltage-dependent response.
 The study concluded that none of the conventional diagnostic measurements (DC insulation
resistance, polarization index, high voltage partial discharge, capacitance, dissipation factor) utilized were
sensitive to unshielded cable degradation. Moreover, the value of testing at frequencies other than power
frequencies was highlighted.
2.1.2 U.S. NRC Report NUREG/CR-6904: Evaluation of the Broadband
 Impedance Spectroscopy Prognostic/Diagnostic Technique for Electric
 Cables Used in Nuclear Power Plants
 Rogovin and Lofaro [6] focused on the application of broadband impedance spectroscopy as a
condition monitoring tool for low-voltage (LV) I&C cables found in NPPs. Samples of a two-conductor
I&C cable were subjected to thermal aging over their entire length or at discrete locations to simulate the
effect of hot spots. The resulting sample population included cables with different levels of aging, ranging
in length from 10 to 100 m. The presence of mechanical defects, such as abrasion and racks, was also
investigated. Samples were tested with a broadband impedance analyzer over a frequency range of 100
Hz to 100 MHz. Impedance magnitude and phase were extracted from the complex impedance spectra to
determine the sensitivity of the method to thermal aging. Analytical models were developed to simulate
the test response and interpret the experimental data. The technique was found to be sensitive to global
thermal aging in the frequency range below 1 MHz, with the measured impedance phase spectra showing
an increase in the impedance phase angle for higher levels of aging. In the frequency range above 1 MHz,
the impedance magnitude and phase spectra were downshifted to lower frequencies with increasing level
of aging. These findings indicated that the method could be used as an indicator of thermal degradation on
the cable.
2.1.3 European ADVANCE - Ageing Diagnostics and Prognostics of Low-
 Voltage l&C Cables - Project 269983
 The ADVANCE project [7] was a European-led collaborative project involving partners from a
number of industries, research and engineering institutes, and universities. The main objectives of the
project were to evaluate and optimize promising nondestructive condition monitoring methods to assess
the condition of safety-related LV cables over their entire length, and to develop acceptance criteria for
these methods.
 The first stage of the project established the potential application of state-of-the-art condition
monitoring methods for LV cables in NPPs. The research looked at the advantages and disadvantages of
laboratory and field techniques based on the physical/mechanical characterization of cable insulation and
jacket materials. The use of electrical techniques was also investigated, and it was concluded that
information about the effect of aging on electrical properties of LV cable insulation is still lacking. Based
on this information a research program was developed to investigate and improve the most promising
techniques. The program included long and short cable samples subjected to combined thermal and

 4

radiation aging characterized at regular intervals with selected condition monitoring methods. The long
cable samples also went through DBE testing to correlate condition indicators measured near the cable
end of life with cable survivability.
 No single-condition monitoring method was shown to be sufficient to track degradation at all stages;
therefore, it was recommended to use a combination of techniques until aging mechanisms are
sufficiently identified. From tests on longer samples (about 20 m), no general behavior was observed and
it was hypothesized that each cable type (or group) may require the most appropriate condition
monitoring method/marker to be defined or at least tuned. Among electrical techniques investigated in the
work, low frequency dielectric spectroscopy (LFDS) (obtained using frequency domain techniques)
showed the most promising results. In particular, between 10-2 Hz and ~106 Hz, test results on short
samples showed that both real (ε') and imaginary (ε") parts of permittivity of cross-linked polyethylene
(XLPE), ethylene propylene rubber (EPR)-based, and ethylene propylene diene monomer rubber
(EPDM)-based insulations increased over the whole frequency range. The variation of ε' was more
evident at low frequency, while ε'' increased markedly also in the high frequency range. The change in
electrical properties appeared to correlate well with traditional material-based characterization techniques.
 Other techniques, such as frequency domain reflectometry (FDR), were noted as being able to locate
hot-spots or locations for the greatest potential for damage. Defect location is not provided by DS
measurements. The importance of measurement techniques and environmental controls, particularly of
temperature, were also noted.
2.1.4 IAEA Coordinated Research Project Condition Monitoring and
 Management of Ageing of Low Voltage Cables in Nuclear Power Plants
 The intent of this large collaborative project [8] between 17 participating countries was to provide
comprehensive information and guidelines on how to monitor the condition of LV cables in NPPs. To
achieve this goal, a benchmarking program assessed both well-established and new condition monitoring
techniques on cables exposed to accelerated thermal and radiation aging. Fourteen different methods,
material-based and electrical, were used to test 12 different types of cables. The various participants used
the selected methods and results were compared to determine their respective efficacy and accuracy to
measure the level of aging for each cable. Among the material-based techniques, EAB and indenter
modulus were found to be the most sensitive to degradation by aging for most materials. Oxidation
induction time (OIT) and oxidation induction temperature also yielded trendable results for several cable
formulations. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), ultrasonic velocity, and density were found
to be promising in the early stage of aging and have trendable potential for some materials.
 LFDS and FDR were found to be the only electrical tests with the potential to trend the degradation of
the insulation materials. While FDR was determined to be most useful to detect local areas of
degradation, LFDS was found to be able to track changes in the bulk properties of the insulation, more so
for irradiated samples than for thermally aged samples. The LFDS tests were conducted between 10-2 and
106 Hz on short cable samples (up to 1 m) by the University of Bologna. The measurements were made
between individual wires or between shield and individual wires for shielded cables. Only a few cables
were tested as part of this work but the influence of stray capacitances on the repeatability of results for
unshielded cables was noted. It was also reported that testing at higher temperatures enhances the
sensitivity of the method to detect material degradation induced by aging.
2.1.5 EPRI Technical Report 3002010403: Long-Term Operations: Initial
 Findings on Use of Dielectric Spectroscopy for Condition Monitoring of
 Low Voltage Nuclear Power Plant Cables
 The Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) [9] sought to address the question: Is there a diagnostic
test that can be applied to a wide range of LV cable insulation types and configurations that provide a
global indication of thermal degradation of a cable circuit, whether it is shielded or unshielded? The
research applied LFDS and polarization/depolarization current (PDC) methods that focused on both time-
domain (for very low frequencies [0.01–10 Hz]) and frequency-domain (for >10 Hz) methods to assess
thermal aging effects on several cables, including:

 5

• a 3-conductor control cable
• a 3-conductor power cable
• a conductor instrument and control cable.
 DS tests were performed in various configurations (e.g., electrode grounding, external mandrel
grounding, applied voltage level, ambient temperature) and were compared to common physical and
chemical cable aging tests of indenter modulus, EAB, near infrared (NIR), infrared (FTIR), etc. Key
findings included the following:
• LFDS and PDC measurements can be sensitive enough to detect bulk thermal aging effects (e.g.,
 structural changes in polymer, depletion of antioxidants) in a variety of shielded and unshielded LV
 cable insulation types and geometric configurations.
• A variety of diagnostic “metrics” derived from the variable frequency and time dependent dielectric
 response data showed quantifiable trends versus increasing levels of thermal aging. The trends varied
 depending on the analyzed parameter, and temperature.
• Experimental parameters and test configurations specific to the cable under test must be carefully
 considered to obtain optimal dielectric response measurement data.
• The results from the study highlighted the unique (cable specific) frequency and time dependent
 dielectric response characteristics of LV multi-conductor cables with different insulation types to bulk
 thermal aging.

2.1.6 PNNL-27982 Dielectric Spectroscopy for Bulk Condition Assessment of
 Low Voltage Cable – Interim Report
 Glass et al. [10] investigated nondestructive test methods focusing on bulk cable insulation testing
using a DS approach. That report addressed relevant literature coupled with a discussion of the theory of
DS measurements and use of modeling to appreciate the influence of damage/defect profile on the bulk
total cable measurement.
2.1.7 PNNL-35504 Radiation Aging of Cable Insulation Systems to Support
 Extension of Cable Electrical Assessment Techniques
 Murphy [11] and Elen et al [12], investigated the condition monitoring of electrical cables used in
NPPs using electrical diagnostic techniques such as LFDS, PDC techniques, along with other
reflectometry techniques such as FDR and time domain reflectometry (TDR), on XLPE and EPR
insulated cables, subjected to gamma irradiation and thermal aging. LFDS showed trends of increasing
tan δ at frequencies above 0.1 Hz across the “single conductor” samples and 30-conductor “mandrels.” A
larger increase of tan δ is noted for samples subjected to progressive thermal plus gamma radiation aging
than for radiation aging alone. It was reported that additional analysis metrics and condition indicators,
especially for the more complex responses observed with the cable mandrels, will require more in-depth
analysis of the data.
 2.2 Journal and Conference Proceedings
 Various research groups have published journal and conference articles on the research work
performed using DS on NPP cable materials.
 A high-voltage DS system was developed under Werelius et al. [13] for diagnostics of water tree
deteriorated extruded medium voltage cable-based on measurements of non-linear dielectric response in
the frequency domain to measure relatively low loss and small variation of permittivity as a function of
frequency and voltage. Experience from more than 200 field measurements was combined with laboratory
investigations of accelerated aged samples. The dielectric response of water tree deteriorated XLPE
cables was shown to correlate with aging and breakdown strength. Two different field studies of

 6

implementation of this diagnostic method were also presented. The field studies showed the fault rate
decreased significantly when replacement strategy was based on the diagnostic criteria formulated.
 Linde et al. [14] evaluated DS as a condition monitoring technique for aged XLPE electrical
insulation in NPPs. Bare core XLPE samples were aged at 55°C and 85°C and under exposure to 60Co
γ-radiation at different dose rates (0.42–1.06 kGy h-1). Samples were assessed using a variety of methods
including DS, tensile testing, crystallinity, density, and FTIR. Results suggest that DS can be used for in
situ measurements of the degree of oxidation of XLPE cables to assess cable condition and remaining
useful life.
 Imperatore [15] examined low voltage DS responses of polyvinyl chloride/ethylene propylene rubber
(PVC/EPR) multi-conductor cables aged at PNNL compared to EAB and other cable condition metrics.
Specific capacitance and dissipation factor measurements showed a monotonic trend that correlated well
with aging at 10 Hz, 1 kHz, and 100 kHz. For these samples, the 1 kHz response was the best DS
diagnostic marker. The DS change preceded any significant change in EAB, thereby indicating that this
measurement could be an earlier indicator of the onset of damage than mechanical measurements.
 Fabiani et al. [16] examined combined radiation and thermal aging effects on permittivity. They
found an imaginary part of the permittivity at 100 kHz to correlate well with mechanical properties, such
as EAB. It was further demonstrated that a post-irradiation effect occurs even years after aging is stopped,
increasing the imaginary permittivity and worsening mechanical properties due to the slow conversion of
radicals into oxidized species. One consequence is that when cable insulation is subjected to a nuclear
accident, the health of cable insulation must also be followed for a long time after the accident occurred,
since aging due to oxidation progresses even when the radiation source is switched off.
 Cable insulation performance was studied by Mustafa et al. [17] through nondestructive DS and
hardness techniques, along with destructive techniques such as OIT and EAB . The use of three
parameters, antioxidant depletion factor (ADF), degradation index (DI), and degradation rate (DR),
provided a thorough representation of the state of degradation of cable insulation. It was observed that the
presence of antioxidants deeply influenced XLPE degradation, slowing down the degradation process in
the initial phases of aging. The cable gradually reached its end-of-life criterion, the 50% value of EAB,
which was also observed by the DI and DR trends. The tan δ and DI correlated highly with the chemical
and mechanical parameters under study, establishing them as reliable aging indicators of NPP cables, and
demonstrating the effectiveness of dielectric spectroscopy as a viable non-destructive condition
monitoring technique.
 Suraci et al. [18] explored both DS and TDR. Ten-meter-long coaxial cables were thermally aged and
characterized using non-destructive methods (i.e., DS and TDR). Both techniques allowed changes of
electrical properties to be revealed with aging; however, the coupling of these two techniques permitted
an effective cable aging assessment including recognition of local defects. Indeed, it has been
demonstrated that DS is more sensitive when the cable is globally aged, while TDR, in addition to a
global investigation, can also single out aging occurring in a limited portion of cable (local aging).

 3.2 Cable System Capacitance and Relative Permittivity
 The concept of measuring the capacitance and impedance of a cable system including conductors and
dielectric material can be extended to either co-axial cables or multi-conductor cables by considering the
conductors as analogous to parallel plates. The measured cable capacitance relation to the insulation
permittivity is more complex than for the parallel plate case, but the idea is the same. The nominal
capacitances and open-circuit impedances can be calculated from standard formulas [24]. Configurations
and formulas for calculating nominal cable capacitance for a co-axial case and a single conductor pair are
shown in Figure 3-2 . Formulas for distributed line capacitance for the two cases illustrated in Figure 3-2
are shown below the images.

 If CL [F/m] and cable geometry are known or measured, and cable aging is uniform over the full cable
length, the absolute and relative permittivity can be calculated as indicated in Figure 3-2. Frequently
however, cable aging is far from uniform. As the cable approaches the reactor with higher temperatures
and radiation levels, or as it passes near a hot steam pipe, or as it goes through successive rooms with
substantially different temperatures or radiation levels, cable aging/damage occurs at different rates. The
overall cable health is governed by the cable segment that is the most aged. A simple lumped parameter
cable model as shown in Figure 3-3 can be used to estimate the ratio of the aged section permittivity to a
pristine cable section based on the total measured cable capacitance (Ct) after aging, the pristine
capacitance (Cpristine), and the relative cable length that is pristine/unaged, and the length that is aged.

 4. TEST SETUP AND DS MEASUREMENTS
 4.1 Direct Measurement of DS Response Compared to EAB
 A 62-day thermal aging study was conducted on unshielded general cable (14AWG-3/C) with EPR
insulation to evaluate measures of EPR degradation. EPR-insulated cable was selected for the study
because EPR, along with XLPE, is one of the two most common cable insulation types found in U.S.
reactors and because accelerated thermal degradation of EPR (months) was more compatible with the
research study timeline than that of XLPE (years). A 30-ft section of the 100-ft cable was subjected to
140°C aging inside a mechanical convection oven. During the aging period, witness samples consisting of
insulation straws extracted from the cables and short cable segments were aged in the oven in parallel
with a 30-ft section and collected at regular intervals (see Figure 4-1). Straw specimens were evaluated
for their EAB, and DS was performed on aged segments using the Spectano 100 instrument. These
samples were silver-painted on the insulation—with the inner conductor used as an inner electrode, and
silver paint serving as the outer electrode—and the measurement was performed similar to the coaxial
cable geometry (cylindrical electrodes). Complex permittivity, capacitance, and tan δ values were
measured and analyzed.

 Figure 4-1. Thermal aging of witness samples at 140°C in the thermal oven
 As shown in Figure 4-2, the permittivity values measured through DS were compared with the EAB
values, and it can be observed that the permittivity values trend fairly linearly while EAB values remain
fairly constant (up to 37 days of aging) before dropping rapidly (37 to 55 days of aging). The permittivity-
to-EAB correlation coefficient is -0.82. Both the instrusty standard EAB method and permittitvity track
with aging of the insulation, but permittivity is seen to track monotonically over the entire aging range
investigated, whereas EAB has an induction period of little change with age before onsite of rapid
property loss. While identification of an endpoint for cable aging from EAB is more straightforward as
the point of significant loss, permittivity is demonstrated to be a more useful overall monitor of cable
aging since it is nondestructive and increasing steadily over the entire aging period (see Figure 4-2).
Furthermore, permittivity may more directly correspond to the critical functional property of interest to
maintain for aging cables.

 5. CONCLUSIONS
 PNNL performed an experimental study to evaluate the condition of cable health affected by thermal
aging conditions using dielectric spectroscopy. An experiment was conducted by thermally aging
different lengths of the cable and measuring the dielectric response. The primary focus was on cable with
only a portion subjected to severe aging. Conclusions and observations of the investigation are as follows:

• A substantial body of work suggests feasibility to measure cable insulation damage using various forms
 of DS.
• There are differences in specific capacitance and inferred real and relative permittivity measured on
 pristine cables versus those with a thermally aged cable.
• Prior studies showed permittivity (both real and complex parts) to correlate with damage metrics such
 as obtained using EAB, indenter modulus, and FTIR analysis. This study affirmed prior work by
 showing an EPR insulated multi-conductor cable exhibited permittivity increases that generally
 correlate with EAB (correlation coefficient = -0.77) as a damage indicator but there are notable
 differences in the indicator trend shape. Permittivity is revealed as a preferred metric for installed cable
 aging compared to EAB as it is non-destructive and trends steadily with aging rather than changing
 abruptly following a long induction period of little change.
• The DS measurement represents an average of the dielectric condition over the entire cable. If only a
 small percentage of the cable is damaged, this should be considered when assessing the DS
 measurement. The metric of interest to predict if a cable will fail is the most damaged segment, not an
 average. An assumption that the damage is distributed along the entire cable length is a non-
 conservative assumption.
• If the cable is exposed to two separate environments and the relative cable length in the harsh and
 benign environments are known, the ratio of ε2/ε1 can be estimated (where ε2 is the permittivity of the
 aged cable section and ε1 is the permittivity of the pristine cable segment. The cable lengths may be
 known either based on design drawings, or by reflectometry measurements where transitions from
 benign to harsh environments produce detectable reflectometry peaks.
