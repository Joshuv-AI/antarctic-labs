NANOS3-YTHDF2 drives aberrant P-body accumulation to impair folliculogenesis in offspring of maternal aristolochic acid I exposure
Authors: Jianwei Geng, Yan Zhang, Junlin He, Fei He, Yunlong Xiang, Yongheng Wang, Yanqing Geng, Yidan Ma, Yuhan Dai, Xuemei Chen, Fangfang Li, Yingxiong Wang, Xinyi Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Processing-bodies (PBs) are cytoplasmic membraneless condensates essential for RNA regulation. They share structural and functional similarities with germ granules and are critical for primordial folliculogenesis, a developmental process highly susceptible to exogenous insults. Aristolochic acid I (AAⅠ), a pervasive environmental toxin from Aristolochiaceae plants, is known to cross the placental barrier and induce fetal ovarian oxidative stress. Here, using a mouse model of maternal AAⅠ exposure (16.5 to 18.5 days post-coitus), we demonstrate that this prenatal insult causes profound defects in offspring folliculogenesis. AAⅠ induces PB enlargement and protein accumulation, causing these condensates to acquire germ granule-like properties. Specifically, AAⅠ upregulates the germ granule component NANOS3 to drive condensate enlargement. Multi-omic profiling further revealed that these aberrant condensates recruit germ granule-associated proteins and exhibit altered RNA profiles. Concurrently, neonatal oocytes exhibited enlarged Balbiani body-like structures alongside intermitochondrial cement, a feature not previously reported in oocytes. Furthermore, AAⅠ promotes the recruitment of N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-related factors into PBs, accompanied by elevated global m6A levels and the upregulation of YTHDF2. We show that YTHDF2 is essential for recruiting NANOS3 to PBs; truncating the intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) or RNA-binding domains of either protein impairs their interaction. These perinatal perturbations manifest in adulthood as primary ovarian insufficiency (POI)-like dysfunction, including reduced ovarian reserve, compromised oocyte quality, and hormonal deficits. Overall, our work uncovers a NANOS3-YTHDF2 axis that drives germ granule-like PB adaptations, illustrating how fetal environmental insults disrupt folliculogenesis and serve as an etiological driver for adult-onset POI.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02991-0.

Introduction

Ovarian follicles, comprising an an oocyte surrounded by supportive granulosa cells, serve as the fundamental functional units for producing healthy oocytes and ovarian hormones. They originate as “primordial follicles,” which lie dormant in the ovary until a small subset is recruited to develop into growing follicles during each reproductive cycle. The total number of primordial follicles, termed the ovarian reserve, is fixed after birth and thus determines a woman’s fertility potential [1]. The assembly of primordial follicles occurs through the breakdown of germ cell cysts, wherein interconnected oocytes separate and become encapsulated by a single layer of pregranulosa cells. This process establishes a proper ovarian reserve, meaning any defects during this window may serve as a potential cause of primary ovarian insufficiency (POI). POI is an ovarian dysfunction affecting 3.7% of women younger than 40 years old, characterized by a declined ovarian function. It impairs fertility and reduces ovarian hormone production, thereby inducing premature aging across multiple systems [2]. While POI has a strong genetic basis, supported by identified mutations in candidate genes such as NANOS3, NR5A1, and GDF9, the underlying causes remain poorly understood [3]. Emerging evidence reveals that fetal oogenesis is susceptible to maternal environmental exposures, which can disrupt early folliculogenesis and increase subsequent POI risk in adulthood [4].

Aristolochic acid I (AAⅠ) is the predominant component of aristolochic acids, a class of nitrophenanthrene carboxylic acids commonly found in plants of the Aristolochiaceae family. Despite being a severe nephrotoxin and a designated Group 1 human carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), AAⅠ remains a concerning ingredient in various traditional herbal remedies [5, 6]. Although stringently restricted in many countries, human exposure persists through the consumption of AAⅠ-containing herbal products, weight-loss pills, and even common foods (e.g., Houttuynia cordata, a popular vegetable in parts of Asia), as well as through environmental routes including AAⅠ-contaminated soil and groundwater [7, 8]. In addition to its carcinogenicity, AAⅠ exerts significant reproductive toxicity in both males and females. In males, AAⅠ disrupts testicular function and reduces sperm count [9]. In females, it impairs oocyte maturation and quality through mitochondrial dysfunction [10]. Critically, AAⅠ can cross the placenta barrier, therefore posing potential risks to the developing fetus [11]. We have previously demonstrated that maternal exposure to AAⅠ induces DNA damage, oxidative stress and defective homologous recombination in fetal oocytes, thereby impairing meiotic prophase I progression in the fetal mice [12]. We also found that AAⅠ inhibits primordial folliculogenesis via upregulation of NANOS3, a candidate gene for POI, leading to impaired fertility in adulthood [13]. However, the mechanisms by which AAⅠ disrupts early folliculogenesis, particularly through dysregulation of RNA metabolism, remain poorly understood.

Processing bodies (PBs) are dynamic, membraneless organelles composed of ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes, which consist of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and their associated RNAs. They serve as critical hubs for post-transcriptional regulation, including mRNA storage, degradation and translational repression [14, 15]. PB assembly is highly stress-responsive; under oxidative stress, translation-arrested mRNAs are recruited into PBs, leading to an increase in PB size and number [16–18]. Once the stress is resolved, PBs disassemble, releasing these mRNA back to the cytoplasm to resume translation [19]. In the germline, PBs exhibit structural and functional overlap with germ granules, which are germline-specific condensates enriched in gamete-specific RBPs. For instance, NANOS proteins, highly conserved components of germ granule [20], frequently colocalize with PBs in germline [21, 22]. Critically, these condensates are essential for the establishment of the primordial follicle pool. This is evidenced by the defective primordial follicle formation following the loss of PB components like Ddx6 or Lsm14b [23, 24]. Furthermore, both germ granules and PB-like structures in oocytes contribute to maternal mRNA accumulation and storage, a process initiated during primordial folliculogenesis to preserve transcripts required for subsequent oocyte maturation [24, 25]. Considering their roles in RNA processing and maternal mRNA regulation, we hypothesized that PB/germ granule dysregulation during early folliculogenesis disrupts RNA homeostasis, leading to lasting consequences for oocyte quality and female fertility.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the most prevalent RNA modification in eukaryotes, is highly responsive to environmental stimuli and plays a key roles in post-transcriptional regulation, follicle development, and fertility [26, 27]. The m6A modification is deposited by methyltransferases (m6A writer), primarily METTL3 and METTL14, removed by demethylases (m6A eraser) such as ALKBH5 and FTO, and recognized by m6A -binding proteins (m6A reader), including YTHDC1-2, YTHDF1-3, which determine the fate of the modified transcript [28]. Notably, the regulation of m6A-mediated RNA metabolism often converges on the assembly and function of PBs [28]. YTHDF2, in particular, has emerged as a key regulator of PB dynamics; by selectively binding to m6A-modified transcripts, it facilitates their recruitment to PBs, modulating RNA degradation or storage under specific cellular conditions [29]. Additionally, YTHDF2 is abundantly expressed in oocytes, where it plays a critical role in the programmed degradation of maternal mRNA [30]. It also interacts with the PB component LSM14B in oocytes [31]. Thus, the distribution of YTHDF2 within PBs likely coordinates m6A signaling with PB dynamics to regulate RNA homeostasis in the oocyte.

Despite the established roles of m6A and PBs in oogenesis, the impact of environmental toxins like AAⅠ on this delicate RNA regulatory network remains entirely unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that maternal AAⅠ exposure impairs offspring folliculogenesis by altering the components of PBs, driving these condensates to acquire germ granule-like properties. These alterations involve the aberrant recruitment of germ granule components and changes in the transcripts within these condensates. Crucially, we provide the first ultrastructural evidence of intermitochondrial cement (IMC), a typically male-specific germ granule, in neonatal oocytes, alongside enlarged Balbiani body-like structure. Concurrently, AAⅠ elevates global mA levels and induces significant upregulation and recruitment of YTHDF2 into PBs, where YTHDF2 serves as an essential mediator for recruiting NANOS3. These disruptions cause long-term consequences for ovarian function and fertility in adulthood. Overall, our study defines a YTHDF2-NANOS3 regulatory mechanism that alters PB dynamics in neonatal oocytes following maternal AAⅠ exposure. These findings provide new insights into the regulatory networks underlying of primordial folliculogenesis and support the ovarian dysgenesis syndrome hypothesis, illustrating how exogenous insults during early oogenesis can lead to adult-onset ovarian disorders [4].

Materials and methods

Mice

The CD1 mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) [Certificate No.: SCXK (Beijing) 2016-0006]. Mice were housed in the Chongqing Medical University Animal Care Facility with free access to water and food. Female mice were caged with fertile adult males, and the day with the presence of a vaginal plug was designated as 0.5 days post-coitus (dpc). Birth usually occurs at 19.5 dpc and is designated as postnatal day 0 (P0).

AAⅠ Delivery and ovary collection

Pregnant mice were administrated PBS or AAⅠ (0.5 or 1.0 mg/kg.bw/day) on the mornings of the 16.5, 17.5 and 18.5 dpc by oral gavage. AAⅠ was dissolved in PBS and diluted to the final concentrations. The mice in the control group were given an equal volume of PBS. Neonatal (F1) ovaries were isolated under a dissection microscope on P1. Meanwhile, the F1 female mice were maintained under the same conditions until reaching 8 weeks of age.Vaginal smears were taken daily to monitor estrus for 10 days; the analysis standard is provided in Figure S3A. Then, their oocytes, ovaries and serum were collected.

Ovary cultures

The ovaries at 16.5 dpc were dissected and then placed in 6-well plate with 1 mL serum-free DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, California, USA) for a 4-day culture (equal to P1) at 37℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. AAⅠ dissolved in DMSO was added to the medium with a final concentration of 0.5 µM. Control wells received an equal volume of DMSO. The concentrations of AAⅠ were based on our previous study [13].

The Nanos3-shRNA lentivirus (Santa Cruz, Dallas, USA; cat. No.sc-75869-v) and Ythdf2-shRNA lentivirus (customized and constructed by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd., China) were introduced into the 16.5 dpc ovary cultures along with 0.5 µM AAⅠ. The medium was replaced after 24 h to remove the lentivirus, and the cultures were continued for an additional 3 days. Control ovaries were incubated with scrambled shRNA lentivirus. The ovaries were collected on the 4th day (equal to P1). The sequence for Ythdf2-shRNA: 5’-GCTCCAGGCATGAATACTATA-3’.

Plasmid construction

Full-length cDNAs of mouse Nanos3, Ythdf2 and Ddx6 were amplified by PCR; these were then used as templates to generate truncated fragments for each gene. The coding regions of Nanos3 and Ythdf2 were cloned into the pEX1-GFP (GFP tag at C-terminus) and pEX7-RFP (RFP tag at N-terminus) vectors, respectively. These plasmids were used to generate GFP-tagged NANOS3 and RFP-tagged YTHDF2 deletion mutants, including the following: NANOS3 with deletions of amino acids 25–50 (lacking IDR1; N-ΔIDR1), 56–110 (lacking the zinc finger domain; N-ΔZinc) and 131–178 (lacking IDR2; N-ΔIDR2); YTHDF2 with deletions of amino acids 1–45 (lacking IDR1; Y-ΔIDR1) and 247–387 (lacking IDR2; Y-ΔIDR2). The coding region of Ddx6 was labeled with an HA-tag at its C-terminus and then cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector. Plasmids containing the three full-length coding sequences were synthesized by GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and the five truncation constructs were generated by Youbio Co., Ltd. (Changsha, China). All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Cell culture and transfection

HeLa and 293T cells were purchased from the cell bank of the Type Culture Collection of Chinese Academy of Sciences (TCCCAS, Beijing, China). Cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, California, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Lonsera, Suzhou, China), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 ℃. For co-transfections, cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 36 h according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Live-cell imaging

HeLa cells were plated in a 35 mm glass-bottom dish and were transfected with GFP-NANOS3, RFP-YTHDF2, and HA-DDX6 constructs. After 36 h, live cells were observed using a Ti2-E inverted confocal microscope (C2, Nikon, Japan). GFP and RFP were excited at 488 and 555 nm, respectively, and images were acquired every 30 s for 9 min. The images were analyzed using ImageJ software to identify fusion and fission events of cytoplasmic droplets.

Histological analysis and oocyte quantification

Neonatal ovaries were collected and fixed in 4% PFA for 4 to 6 h, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. The whole ovary was sequentially sectioned at 5 μm. After deparaffinization and rehydration, slides were stained with Hematoxylin according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The slides were observed under a microscope (BX43, Olympus, Japan) and images were captured using the BX2-BSW 03.04a (Olympus, Japan) software. A primordial follicle was defined as an individual oocyte surrounded by a single layer of pregranulosa cells. Cyst-oocytes were oocytes within clustered cysts, and single oocytes were separated oocytes without surrounding pregranulosa cells. Follicles were counted on every fifth section of the whole ovary. The cumulative oocyte counts were multiplied by five, because four-fifths of the ovary was not analyzed.

For adult ovaries, only follicles containing oocytes with visible nuclei were counted and classified as primordial, primary, secondary, and antral (Figure S3B). Follicles were counted on every fifth section of the ovary with complete ovarian structure. The follicle count per section was calculated as the total follicle count divided by the number of sections analyzed. Corpus luteum numbers were recorded separately. The maximum cross-sectional area of each ovary was measured using iViewer (Unic Technologies, Beijing, China).

Immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

ovary sections were subjected to antigen retrieval by microwaving in 0.01 M Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) for 15 min. Then, the sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum (NGS) and incubated with the primary antibody at 4 ℃ overnight. After washing in PBS, sections were incubated with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at room temperature (RT) for 1 h. For cell cultures, the cells on coverslips were fixed using 4% PFA for 15 min at RT. Fixed cells were permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, then incubated with primary antibodies at 4℃ overnight. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with a Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at RT for 1 h. Confocal fluorescence images were captured using a Nikon Ti2-E inverted confocal microscope (C2, Nikon, Japan). The fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ.

For IHC, after incubation with primary antibodies, the slides were processed with a Polymer-HRP sheep anti-mouse/rabbit secondary antibody kit (AFIHC001, AiFang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hunan, China) as the manufacturer’s instruction. All antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Multiple immunofluorescence (mIF)

Multiple immunofluorescence of the paraffin sections was carried out using the TSA Fluorescence Triple Staining Kit (AFIHC024, AiFang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after antigen retrieval by microwaving in 0.01 M Tris-EDTA, slides were incubated with peroxidase blocking solution for 15 min, then blocked with 5% NGS, and incubated with the primary antibody at 4 ℃ overnight. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies at RT for 1 h, then stained with fluorescent dyes. Slides were observed using a Ti2-E inverted confocal microscope (C2, Nikon, Japan).

Western blotting (WB)

Total protein was extracted from 12 ovaries using cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with PMSF (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and dithiothreitol (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The supernatant of the tissue lysates was boiled with SDS for 10 min before separation by SDS-PAGE. After transferring to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, California, MA, USA), the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies, and subsequently incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using a Chemiluminescent Substrate kit (Yoshi, Wuhan, China) and imaged using a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The band intensity was analyzed using the ImageJ. The relative protein expression level was calculated as the intensity of the target protein divided by the intensity of β-actin. All antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

RNA extraction and m6A dot blot assay

Total RNA was extracted from 12 ovaries using TRI reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louise, USA). The isolated RNA was then washed with 75% ethanol and dissolved in RNase-free water. OD260/OD280: 1.8-2.0. The RNA was denatured at 95 ℃ for 5 min. Then RNA of 600 and 800 ng were loaded to nylon membranes and allowed to air dry. The membranes were exposed to UV for 30 min for crosslinking and washed with PBST. Then, RNA dots were stained with 0.02% Methylene blue (Solarbio, Beijing, China) to indicate the total content of input RNA. These RNA-dots were then blocked with 5% BSA and incubated with the anti-m6A antibody overnight at 4 ℃ and secondary antibody for 1 h. The m6A-RNA dots were visualized using a Chemiluminescent Substrate kit (Yoshi, Wuhan, China) and imaged using a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The antibody is listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and IP-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

The IP assay was carried out using Co-IP Toolkit (KTI1020-CN, Abbkine, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, mouse ovaries or 293T cells were homogenized in lysis buffer for 30 min on ice, and lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was incubated with protein A/G beads, which were pre-incubated with primary antibody at 4 ℃ overnight. After being washed 3 times with cold washing buffer, IP pellets were boiled in SDS-loading buffer at 100 ℃ for 5 min before SDS-PAGE. Then, the following procedures were performed as we described in WB. All antibodies used were listed in Supplementary Table 1. For DDX6-IP-MS, the proteins in the SDS-PAGE were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to collect proteins associated with DDX6. The proteome analysis was performed by LianChuan-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Briefly, peptides obtained from trypsin digestion and HPLC fractionation were analyzed by MS. The resulting data were processed using Proteome Discoverer (Version 2.1), and tandem mass spectra were searched against the Mus_musculus_10090_SP_20200509.fasta database (17,229 sequences) from UniProt Mus musculus database (https://www.uniprot.org). The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/).

RIP

The RIP assay was carried out using the Magna-RIP Kit (17–700, Merck Millipore, Burlington, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 P1 ovaries per replicate (three replicates total) were homogenized in IP buffer. The supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with protein G -conjugated magnetic beads pre-incubated with an anti‐DDX6 antibody. The IP and RNA extraction were performed as described above, and the RNA quality was evaluated using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent).

For RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq), libraries were constructed using the Smart-seq2 protocol. Purified RNA was mixed with oligo-dT primer (10 µM) and dNTPs, incubated at 72 °C for 3 min, and then subjected to reverse transcription and pre-amplification for 10 cycles. cDNA was purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (N411-03, Vazyme) and used for library preparation with commercial kits (TD202 and TD502, Vazyme). Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Raw sequencing reads were quality-trimmed with Trim Galore (version 0.6.10) and mapped to the mouse mm10 genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) [32]. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM with StringTie (v2.0.6) based on the refFlat database [33]. To identify differentially RIP-enriched genes, a linear model was employed to test the interaction effect between the treatment (Control vs. AAⅠ) and the library type (IP vs. Input). This interaction analysis accounts for baseline variations in the corresponding input RNA levels. Different bound transcripts were defined as those with an IP/Input fold change > 1.5 and an interaction P value < 0.05.

RNA-seq and motif analysis

Neonatal ovaries were collected at P1. RNA isolation, library construction, RNA-seq, and standard bioinformatics analysis were carried out by Personal Biotechnology Cp., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The library products underwent Sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The differential expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using criteria of fold change > 2 and adjusted P value < 0.05.

The motif analysis followed an adapted method based on a previous study [30]. Briefly, the R package biomaRt was used to extract 400 bp upstream and downstream of the start and stop codons for both the identified DEGs and a set of 60 baseline transcripts with minimal expression variation, from the reference genome file (https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release110/fasta/mus_musculus/dna/Mus_musculus.GRCm39.dna.primary_assembly.fa.gz.). The occurrences of GACT or GACA motifs within these sequences were tallied. The data were visualized using R/ggplot2, with motif occurrence plotted against position. A smoothed curve was generated using LOESS (locally weighted regression) to highlight motif distribution trends across the regions.

Embryo culture

F1 females aged 8–10 weeks were caged with fertile males. Following the observation of a vaginal plug, zygotes were harvested from the ampulla of the oviduct at noon and then cultured in KSOM medium (Millipore, MA, USA) at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. The development of the zygotes was monitored and recorded every 24 h.

Hormone assay

Blood was collected on the day of vaginal plug observation (to synchronize estrous stage). After clotting for 1 h at RT, the serum was isolated by centrifuging at 3,000 g for 15 min. The estradiol (E2), Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) and progesterone (P4) levels were detected by ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Ruixin Biotech, Quanzhou, China). Briefly, the serum was added to 96-well plates along with the working solution and incubated for 1 h at 37 ℃. After washing the plates, the reaction solution was added, and the plates were incubated for an additional 15 min at 37℃. The OD values at 450 nm were then measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, CA, USA) after the stop solution was added.

Oocytes collection Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

F1 females at 8–10 weeks of age were intraperitoneally injected with 10 IU pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG) followed by an injection of 10 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) 48 h later. The oocytes were collected from the ampulla of oviduct 12 h post-hCG injection and immediately fixed in 4% cold glutaraldehyde.

The TEM was carried out in the TEM platform of Chongqing Medical University. Briefly, freshly collected neonatal ovaries or MⅡ oocytes were fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde and post-fixed in 1% Osmium tetroxide (Sevenbio, Beijing, China). After dehydration and infiltration, samples were embedded with agarose. Ultrafine sections of 70 nm were made and stained with 3% uranium acetate and lead citrate. Slices were observed using a TEM (Hitachi-7500, Tokyo, Japan) with an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out at least three times. The data were analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism software (v8.3.0, La Jolla, CA, USA). All data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) or standard deviation. The statistical significance between two groups was analyzed by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests. One-way and two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, were used for multiple comparisons as appropriate. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

AAⅠ induces NANOS3-mediated P-body accumulation in neonatal ovaries

The fetal stage is a susceptible window for folliculogenesis. To comprehensively evaluate the impact of AAⅠ on this process, we utilized both in vivo (maternal oral administration) and in vitro (ovary culture) models. Consistent with previous findings [13], AAⅠ exposure delayed primordial folliculogenesis, characterized by large germ-cell cyst remnants and a reduction in neonatal ovaries at P1 (equivalent to 20.5 dpc) (Fig. 1A, B and Supplementary Figure S1A).

Notably, IF and WB analyses revealed that AAⅠ upregulated ovarian NANOS3 expression and triggered the formation of large, intensely stained NANOS3 condensates in both models (Fig. 1C, D and Supplementary Figure S1B). Meanwhile, NANOS3 expression remains constant across developmental stages (16.5 dpc, 19.5 dpc, and 3 dpp; Supplementary Figure S1C). Given that NANOS3 is known to interact with and function within PBs [22], we next investigated PBs formation in the exposed ovaries. IF staining showed that AAⅠ increased both the number and size of distinct cytoplasmic DDX6 foci in oocytes within germ-cell cysts and primordial follicles. Strikingly, these enlarged DDX6 foci exhibited strong co-localization with the NANOS3 condensates (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Figure S1D). Consistently, AAⅠ exposure significantly increased global DDX6 protein levels in neonatal ovaries (Fig. 1F).

To determine whether NANOS3 is required for this PB accumulation, we performed lentiviral-mediated shNanos3 knockdown in 16.5 dpc ovary cultures. We found that, under AAI exposure, shNanos3-knockdown markedly decreased DDX6 expression and subsequently attenuated the formation of large DDX6 foci; furthermore, in DMSO-treated control ovaries, silencing Nanos3 reduced the formation of large DDX6 foci without altering the overall DDX6 fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1G-H). Collectively, these data suggest that AAⅠ exposure induces the accumulation of PBs through the upregulation and condensation of NANOS3.

AAⅠ exposure triggers P-body adopting germ granule properties in neonatal ovaries

As the indispensable central scaffold of PBs, DDX6 orchestrates RBP interactions to drive PB formation and RNA metabolism. To identify its potential interacting partners, we performed DDX6-IP-MS on P1 ovaries. A total of 648 and 684 putative DDX6 interaction proteins were identified in the control and AAI-exposed (0.5 mg/kg) group, respectively, with 126 proteins exclusively recruited following AAⅠ exposure (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2). Remarkably, AAI triggered the specific recruitment of a subset of RBPs, including the gametogenesis-related factors DAZL, PIWIL2, YTHDC2, and TDRD3, which were absent in controls (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 3). These RBPs predominantly function as germ granule components, suggesting that AAI exposure drives PBs to acquire germ granule-like properties via the alteration of their protein composition. Intriguingly, multiple m6A readers, including YTHDC1/2, YTHDF2, HNRNPC, HNRNPA2B1, IGF2BP1-3, were identified as DDX6 interactors, suggesting m6A readers may collaborate with DDX6 to regulate mRNA processing. GO analysis of the 126 differential proteins revealed significant enrichment in biological processes such as RNA splicing, ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, and regulation of translation, alongside enrichment in cellular components like the mitochondrial membrane and secretory granule membrane (Fig. 2C). While NANOS3 was not detected by untargeted MS, potentially because of its highly dynamic behavior within condensates, several proteins from its known interaction partners were enriched. These included PUM1, PUM2, and components of the CCR4-NOT deadenylase complex (CNOT1, CNOT2, and CNOT9), which were enriched in the AAI-exposed ovaries. This strongly supports an AAI-induced recruitment of the NANOS3-associated complex to DDX6.

To identify mRNA targets enriched within the RNP condensates, we performed DDX6-RIP-seq on P1 ovaries. Using stringent criteria (IP/Input > 2, adjusted P < 0.05), we identified 894 putative DDX6-associated mRNAs in control ovaries (Supplementary Table 4). Go analysis revealed that these transcripts are involved in the regulation of fertilization, cell cycle, cytoskeleton organization, DNA repair, and autophagy (Fig. 2D). Manual curation highlighted key transcripts known to play essential roles in oogenesis or oocyte maturation, such as Aurka/b, Gdf9, Bmp15, Btg4, Kit, Lin28a, Mapk6, Nlrp5, Sohlh2, Wee2, and Zp1. The binding of these critical factors confirms DDX6 as an essential core for coordinating RNA-protein networks during primordial folliculogenesis. Notably, AAⅠ exposure significantly altered the pool of DDX6-associated RNAs. Specifically, 53 transcripts exhibited differential binding (fold change > 1.5, interaction P < 0.05), including 30 that showed markedly increased enrichment in the AAI group (Supplementary Table 5). These altered transcripts are involved in regulation of organelle organization, nuclear transport, cell cycle, transcription, and retrograde transport endosome to Golgi, with 8 representing novel transcripts implicated in embryo development (Fig. 2E). Very significantly, we found a negative correlation between changes in DDX6 binding (RIP enrichment) and the corresponding levels in the input for the 53 differentially bound transcripts (Pearson r = -0.8973, p = 9.48e-20, Fig. 2F). This suggests that DDX6-mediated sequestration may be a key event in the post-transcriptional silencing of these targets.

Subsequently, TEM analysis was performed to examine the subcellular changes in P1 ovaries. AAⅠ increased the frequency of Balbiani body-like structures within oocytes and promoted their expansion. These structures featured spherically arranged Golgi complexes with expanded, stacked cisternae and dense vesicle clusters (Fig. 2G). Unexpectedly, AAI-exposed neonatal oocytes exhibited the formation of intermitochondrial cement (IMC), a membraneless germ granules typically restricted to spermatogenic cells [34]. This electron-dense structure nestles among mitochondrial clusters and serves as a central platform for piRNA processing [34]. Its aberrant appearance aligns with our multi-omics results, which demonstrated the AAI-induced recruitment of the piRNA effector PIWIL2 and mitochondria-associated transcripts (Ciapin1, Dgkz, Ecsit, Enox2) to DDX6 condensates. To our knowledge, this is the first report of the male-specific IMC emerging in oocytes following AAⅠ exposure, highlighting a novel inter-germline plasticity of RNP granule in response to environmental insults.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that AAI exposure drives PBs to adopt germ granule-like properties.

AAⅠ elevates m6A modification and YTHDF2 accumulation in neonatal ovaries

Given that AAⅠ-induced recruitment of m6A-associated proteins to DDX6, we hypothesized that the m6A modification is actively involved. Indeed, IHC staining revealed more intense signals within AAⅠ-exposed oocytes, which was corroborated by dot blot analysis showing a significant increase in global m6A levels in P1 ovaries (Fig. 3A, B). Consistently, WB analysis showed that AAⅠ upregulated the m6A writer METTL3, while the eraser ALKBH5 remained constant. Furthermore, the m6A reader YTHDF2 was most sensitive to AAI exposure, increasing significantly at both doses, whereas YTHDC1 and YTHDC2 only showed significant elevation at the 1.0 mg/kg dose (Fig. 3C). IF staining further revealed that AAⅠ exposure induced the formation of enlarged, high-intensity YTHDF2 foci (Fig. 3D, E). Given its known role in promoting PB formation by localizing to these condensates [29], this finding implies an active role for YTHDF2 in driving AAⅠ-induced PB accumulation and compositional alteration.

Since m6A modification is a determinant of RNA fate, we next performed global RNA-seq on P1 ovaries exposed to 0.5 mg/kg AAⅠ. Unexpectedly, only 50 DEGs were identified (Supplementary Table 6), none of which overlapped with the differentially bound transcripts identified in our DDX6-RIP-seq. This discrepancy likely occurs because AAⅠ triggers the sequestration of specific RNAs into PBs. Since these transcripts are being stored or translationally repressed rather than degraded, their overall abundance in the global RNA-seq remains unchanged. GO analysis of these 50 DEGs revealed enrichment in terms of regulation of gene expression, cellular response to stimuli, and regulation of transcription, rather than gametogenesis-specific pathways (Fig. 3F). To explore whether these DEGs are potential targets of YTHDF2, we analyzed the enrichment of the GACU/A consensus sequence, the primary RNA binding motif for YTHDF2 [30], within ± 400 bp of their start and stop codons, where m6A modifications are highly enriched [35, 36]. This analysis revealed distinct motif enrichment patterns between the upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 3G). Together, these results suggest that while DDX6 orchestrates the local sequestration of RNAs, the global transcriptomic changes are primarily mediated by m6A modification and YTHDF2 binding.

YTHDF2 promotes P-body accumulation in AAⅠ-exposed ovary

Subsequently, interactions among NANOS3, YTHDF2 and DDX6 was further investigated. The mIF analysis revealed that AAⅠ exposure increased co-localization of NANOS3, YTHDF2 and DDX6 (Fig. 4A). The Co-IP assay further confirmed an enhanced NANOS3-YTHDF2-DDX6 interaction following maternal treatment with 0.5 mg/kg AAI (Fig. 4B), with consistent alterations observed in fetal ovary cultures (Fig. 4C). Given that DDX6 can also partition into stress granules [16], we evaluated the formation of these structures using the canonical marker G3BP1. Although AAⅠ notably increased G3BP1 foci formation and expression in neonatal ovaries, these condensates exhibited minimal co-localization with either NANOS3 or YTHDF2 (Figure S2A, B). This spatial separation indicates that while AAⅠ upregulates multiple membraneless organelles, the recruitment of NANOS3 and YTHDF2 is strictly specific to PBs.

To functionally validate the role of YTHDF2 in this process, we performed lentiviral shYthdf2 knockdown in 16.5 dpc ovary cultures. Following AAⅠ exposure, Ythdf2 knockdown significantly reduced both the overall fluorescence intensities and condensate formation of NANOS3 and DDX6; however, in the control (DMSO) ovaries, it disrupted condensate assembly but left the total fluorescence intensities of these proteins unchanged (Fig. 4D, E). Analysis of primordial follicle formation indicated that Ythdf2 knockdown efficiently reversed the inhibitory effects of AAⅠ, with a significant increase in the proportion of oocytes within primordial follicles and a corresponding decrease in oocytes remaining in cysts (Fig. 4F, G, and S2C). The total oocyte pool was unaffected by Ythdf2 knockdown (Figure S2D). Collectively, these findings establish YTHDF2 as a specific and critical driver of AAⅠ-induced PB accumulation and subsequent primordial follicle assembly defects.

YTHDF2 recruits NANOS3 to facilitate P-body formation

To investigate the interplay between NANOS3 and YTHDF2 within DDX6-RNP granules, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with GFP-NANOS3, RFP-YTHDF2 and HA-DDX6 constructs. As expected, we observed the formation of enlarged condensates where all three proteins colocalized (Fig. 5A). Co-IP assays further confirmed the tripartite interaction among these proteins (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, live-cell imaging in HeLa cells revealed highly dynamic fusion and fission events of these NANOS3- and YTHDF2-positive foci (Fig. 5C), highlighting the liquid-like properties characteristic of phase-separated condensates.

To further dissect their interrelationship, pairwise transfections were performed. Cells co-expressing YTHDF2 with either NANOS3 or DDX6 exhibited prominent co-localized condensates. However, in the absence of YTHDF2, NANOS3 failed to colocalize with DDX6 or assemble into large condensates (Fig. 5D). Co-IP assays validated these observations: while YTHDF2 interacted with both NANOS3 and DDX6 (Fig. 5E, F), no interaction was detected between DDX6 and NANOS3 in the absence of YTHDF2 (Fig. 5G). This demonstrated YTHDF2 serves as a bridging factor that mediates the functional association between DDX6 and NANOS3.

The assembly of RNP granule depends on liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), a process largely driven by intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). NANOS3 features an N-terminal NOT1-interacting motif (NIM) and two C-terminal IDRs flanking a zinc-finger domain responsible for RNA binding and interactions with PUM proteins [37]. YTHDF2 contains two N-terminal IDRs, including a P/Q/N-rich domain in IDR2 responsible for localizing YTHDF2-mRNA complexes to PBs, and a C-terminal YTH domain that binds m6A-RNA [38]. To map the specific domains responsible for the NANOS3-YTHDF2 interaction, we constructed a series of deletion mutants targeting their functional modules (Fig. 6A, B) and co-expressed them with their full-length binding partners in 293T cells. IF and Co-IP assays revealed that deleting any single domain did not completely abolish the NANOS3-YTHDF2 interaction (Fig. 6C-E). However, domain truncations exerted distinct effects on condensate assembly. For NANOS3, only the N-ΔZinc significantly reduced the formation of large condensates, although all three NANOS3 mutants exhibited impaired binding to YTHDF2 (Fig. 6F, G). Conversely, both YTHDF2 deletion mutants unexpectedly enhanced the formation of large foci, yet their ability to associate with NANOS3 was markedly reduced (Fig. 6H, I). Together, these findings indicate that IDR-mediated multivalent interactions between NANOS3 and YTHDF2 directly drive their co-condensation and specific RNP granule assembly.

Maternal AAⅠ compromises adult offspring fertility through persistent ovarian defects

Given that the accumulation of maternal mRNA, essential for oocyte maturation and early embryogenesis, is initiated in PB-like granules during early oogenesis [23, 39], we hypothesized that disruption of RNP granule dynamics during primordial folliculogenesis would exert lasting effects on subsequent oogenesis and adult female fertility. To test this, we evaluated the ovarian function of 8-week-old F1 female offspring. Maternal AAI exposure induced notable estrous cycle irregularities, with a significantly prolonged estrus stage (Fig. 7A, B). Alongside a decreased ovarian coefficient (Fig. 7C), serum hormone analysis revealed significantly reduced E2 levels across both AAI-exposed groups and a marked decline in AMH (an indicator of ovarian reserve) in the 0.5 mg/kg group, while P4 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 7D). Consistently, morphological analyses demonstrated a reduction in the maximum cross-sectional area of the ovaries, which was statistically significant in the 0.5 mg/kg group (Fig. 7E, F). Crucially, the total follicle pool was significantly depleted in both AAI groups (Fig. 7G), with the most pronounced reduction observed in primordial follicles (Fig. 7H). However, the morphology and number of corpora lutea remained unaltered, suggesting that ovulation was unaffected (Figure S3C).

The m6A machinery, including METTL3 and YTHDF2, governs maternal RNA maintenance in oocytes and orchestrates its timely clearance after fertilization. This precise RNA turnover is essential for zygotic genome activation and subsequent embryo development [40]. To evaluate the developmental competence of F1 oocytes, we collected zygotes and monitored their in vitro development. While the number of ovulated oocytes (Fig. 7I) and the cleavage rates (2-cell, 4-cell, 8-cell, and morula stages) remained comparable across groups, the blastulation rate was significantly reduced in the 1.0 mg/kg AAI group (Fig. 7J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that compromised oocyte quality, rather than reduced ovulation quantity, is the primary driver of fertility decline in F1 females. Importantly, this developmental defect effectively provides a direct cellular basis for our previously reported breeding data [13], explaining why the decline in F2 litter sizes initiates as early as the first parity (equivalent to 9–10 weeks of age of F1 mice). Strikingly, TEM analysis revealed the aberrant presence of ectopic RNP granules in the subcortical cytoplasm of MII oocytes from the 1.0 mg/kg AAI group. These structures appeared as large, spherical, electron-dense bodies composed of intertwined RNP fibrils and granular clusters (Fig. 7K), which were absent in the control and 0.5 mg/kg groups (Figure S3D).

In summary, maternal AAI exposure compromises adult offspring fertility by not only depleting the primordial follicle reserve but also impairing the developmental competence of MII oocytes, alongside endocrine disruption. This highlights that early-life AAI exposure exerts long-lasting detriments to female reproductive capacity.

Discussion

PBs and germ granules are evolutionarily conserved cytoplasmic condensates that serve as hubs for RNA metabolism, orchestrating mRNA storage, degradation, and translation. While PBs dynamically respond to environmental stress, germ granules are specialized for gametogenesis, playing critical roles in maternal mRNA management during oogenesis. Here, we demonstrate that maternal exposure to AAⅠ, a plant-derived toxin, disrupts primordial folliculogenesis by triggering aberrant RNP granule dynamics. Specifically, we reveal that YTHDF2 acts as an essential factor coordinating the association of NANOS3 and DDX6, a process that is highly responsive to AAⅠ. Consequently, this early-life disruption is closely associated with persistent ovarian dysfunction in adult offspring, manifesting as a dual impairment: the severe depletion of the ovarian reserve and a profound decline in mature oocyte quality. Our study uncovers an RNP granule regulatory mechanism in early oogenesis, highlighting that perturbations during the critical window of primordial folliculogenesis underlie early-onset and long-lasting female fertility impairment.

AAⅠ exerts reproductive toxicity primarily through oxidative stress, consistent with studies linking AAⅠ to mitochondrial damage. In male mice, AAⅠ alters amino acid metabolism and impairs the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle in the testis, resulting in decreased sperm count [9]. In female mice, AAⅠ-induced oxidative stress activates the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, disrupts mitochondrial dynamics, and impairs ovarian function [10]; it also undermines oocyte mitochondrial function and ATP production, compromising oocyte maturation and quality [41]. Our previous study showed that maternal oral administration of AAⅠ induces oxidative stress in fetal ovaries, including increased Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), Malondialdehyde (MDA), Catalase (CAT), Superoxide Dismutase 1 (SOD1) levels, and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential [12], supporting mitochondria as a major target of AAⅠ. Here, the observation of aberrant IMC-like structures amidst mitochondrial clusters further indicates that AAⅠ-induced redox imbalance may affect the organization of mitochondria-associated germ granules, thereby dysregulating RNA metabolic processes.

Maternal RNA accumulation in RNP granules and mitochondria-associated ribonucleoprotein domains (MARDO) is crucial for oocyte maturation and early embryonic development [42]. As full-grown oocytes (FGOs) undergo transcriptional silencing, they rely on the transcripts preserved within these structures to sustain the protein synthesis necessary for subsequent developmental events. Meanwhile, the timely degradation of these maternal RNAs is crucial for proper embryonic development [31]. The delicate balance between these two opposing processes is directly orchestrated by specific RBPs, such as the NANOS family and the m6A reader YTHDF2. In spermatocytes, NANOS proteins localize to germ granules to facilitate the degradation of specific mRNAs (e.g., Dazl) [43, 44], with NANOS2 relying on PB components to support spermatogenesis [45]. Notably, NANOS3 is more closely linked to female fertility, with molecular epidemiology studies identifying associations between Nanos3 mutations and POI [46]. Our previous work also showed that NANOS3 upregulation mediates AAⅠ-induced disruption of primordial follicle formation [13]. Meanwhile, YTHDF2 directs m6A-RNAs to distinct condensates depending on the cellular state: under normal conditions, it targets them to PBs for degradation [47–49]; whereas under oxidative stress, it sequesters them into stress granules for protection [50]. This regulation targets m6A-modified transcripts, rather than causing a non-specific, global translational alteration. Furthermore, the presence of aberrant RNP aggregates in adult MII oocytes indicates a sustained defect in RNP homeostasis. This suggests that the initial AAI-induced upregulation of NANOS3 and YTHDF2 fundamentally alters RNP dynamics, leaving a lasting defect that impairs oocyte developmental competence in adulthood.

Our findings indicate that AAⅠ disrupts PB dynamics in oocytes through the dysregulation of NANOS3 and YTHDF2. Notably, while RIP-seq revealed a significant negative correlation between DDX6 binding and input transcript abundance, RNA-seq identified very few DEGs. This discrepancy suggests that transcripts recruited to DDX6-associated complexes are not actively degraded. Instead, we propose that AAⅠ induces the sequestration of key maternal transcripts into aberrant reservoirs, such as the atypical IMC-like structures and enlarged Balbiani body-like structures. The sequestration of these transcripts likely limits the functional RNA pool required for proper oocyte maturation. This proposed model is consistent with our morphological and developmental observations, specifically the retained large RNP granules in MII oocytes and the decline in blastulation rates. The dysregulation of PB components may also be interlinked with the observation of aberrant membraneless organelles. Balbiani body assembly is a highly coordinated process requiring Golgi rearrangement, vesicle trafficking, and mitochondrial transport [51]. DDX6-IP-MS revealed an enrichment of proteins involved in COPII-coated vesicle budding and secretory granule membranes, while RIP-seq identified increased DDX6 binding to genes involves in retrograde transport endosome to Golgi. With their subtle downregulation in input likely drives the expanded Golgi morphology and the corresponding abnormal appearance of the Balbiani body. Additionally, as RBPs like DDX6 and LSM14B are essential for maintaining oocyte prophase arrest [52], their sequestration within aberrant condensates may impair their regulatory functions, thereby contributing to meiotic defects and linking granule dysfunction to fertility impairment.

It should be noted that the molecular mechanisms governing PB dynamics under AAⅠ exposure are not yet fully elucidated. For instance, the upstream signaling pathways linking AAⅠ-induced oxidative stress to the upregulation of NANOS3 and YTHDF2 remain undefined. Future research should focus on how oxidative stress sensors interface with the RNP granule assembly machinery. Additionally, given YTHDF2’s rapid stress response, its potential cooperative interactions with other RBPs, such as LSM14B and the CCR4–NOT complex, warrant further investigation to clarify how RNP granules dictate RNA fate during oogenesis. Furthermore, exploring the interplay between m6A modification and RNP granule dynamics will deepen our understanding of RNA stability and translational control during embryo development. Finally, targeting RNP granule dynamics for therapeutic intervention represents a promising avenue for fertility preservation in patients exposed to environmental toxins, positioning these condensates as critical biomarkers in reproductive medicine.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study sheds light on the dynamic reorganization of RNP granules in oocytes during primordial folliculogenesis, particularly in response to environmental insults. Our findings demonstrate that maternal AAI exposure triggers PBs to adopt germ granule-like properties, a process mediated by the enhanced interaction between NANOS3 and YTHDF2. These molecular events culminate in persistent impairments to oocyte quality and female fertility, highlighting the importance of RNP granule integrity for germline protection during early oogenesis.
