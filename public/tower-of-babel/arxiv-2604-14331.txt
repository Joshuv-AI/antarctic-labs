Heat and Matérn Kernels on Matchings
Dmitry Eremeev dimaeremeev2002@gmail.com, Salem Said salem.said@univ-grenoble-alpes.fr, Viacheslav Borovitskiy viacheslav.borovitskiy@gmail.com

Abstract
Applying kernel methods to matchings is challenging due to their discrete, non-Euclidean nature. In this paper, we develop a principled framework for constructing geometric kernels that respect the natural geometry of the space of matchings. To this end, we first provide a complete characterization of stationary kernels, i.e. kernels that respect the inherent symmetries of this space. Because the class of stationary kernels is too broad, we specifically focus on the heat and Matérn kernel families, adding an appropriate inductive bias of smoothness to stationarity. While these families successfully extend widely popular Euclidean kernels to matchings, evaluating them naively incurs a prohibitive super-exponential computational cost. To overcome this difficulty, we introduce and analyze a novel, sub-exponential algorithm leveraging zonal polynomials for efficient kernel evaluation. Finally, motivated by the known bijective correspondence between matchings and phylogenetic trees—a crucial data modality in biology—we explore whether our framework can be seamlessly transferred to the space of trees, establishing novel negative results and identifying a significant open problem.

Introduction

Matchings—partitions of a set of
2n
elements into
n
pairs—are fundamental combinatorial structures that model paired relationships across diverse domains.
In sports analytics, for instance, a matching represents a single round of a tournament schedule, determining which teams play against each other.
Similarly, in logistics and planning, matchings model the assignment of students to dormitory rooms or the pairing of participants in networking activities.
Despite the practical importance of these structures, applying machine learning techniques to tasks involving them remains challenging.
Standard machine learning models are typically designed for continuous Euclidean spaces (
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), whereas matchings are discrete, combinatorial objects that cannot be naturally treated as vectors without destroying their inherent geometric structure.

While we do not address these in the paper, our investigation is motivated by potential applications such as Bayesian optimization
(
19
)
and kernel hypothesis testing
(
20
)
.
For instance, one might wish to optimize a tournament schedule to maximize fairness, or to statistically detect latent biases in student housing allocations.
These scenarios illustrate the need to model complex functions and distributions over the non-Euclidean domain of matchings, underscoring the potential utility of learning thereon.

In this purely theoretical paper, we develop the foundations for kernel methods on matchings.
To enable this, one could technically define a kernel by embedding matchings into a high-dimensional vector space (e.g., via one-hot encoding of a matching’s edges), but such an ad-hoc approach would fail to respect the natural geometry of the space of matchings.
A more principled approach is to use a true
geometric
kernel
(
31
)
: a kernel that respects the natural symmetries of the space—i.e. is
stationary
under the relabeling of the underlying items—and varies smoothly with respect to a meaningful distance.

This geometric perspective has driven recent progress in machine learning, building practical generalizations of standard kernels---such as the heat

(

Adopting this paradigm, we present a principled framework for constructing kernels on matchings.

We begin by formalizing the geometry of the space of matchings in

S_{2n}/H_{n}
and identifying the pair
(S_{2n},H_{n})
as a
Gelfand pair
.
This algebraic structure enables us to provide a complete characterization of all
stationary
kernels on matchings.
However, stationarity alone is insufficient in practice, as it is too permissive, encompassing kernels such as the delta kernel (
k(x,x^{\prime})=\mathbbold{1}_{x=x^{\prime}}
) that preclude any meaningful generalization.
To address this, we additionally view the set of matchings as vertices of a weighted quotient of the
Cayley graph
of the symmetric group
S_{2n}
.
This construction induces a natural distance and a discrete notion of smoothness governed by the spectral theory of the graph Laplacian.
Using this spectral perspective, we identify the heat and Matérn kernels as the specific stationary kernels that generalize the smoothness properties of their Euclidean counterparts (
Section

However, this spectral characterization encounters an immediate computational barrier: the cardinality of the matching space

Constructing the full graph Laplacian, let alone diagonalizing it—which is required for naive kernel computation—is intractable for all but the smallest cases (

To overcome this, we utilize the expansion of stationary kernels into

This effectively reduces the task of kernel evaluation (or approximation) to the computation of these functions.
Crucially, we propose a novel algorithm for this task based on

This algorithmic advance allows us to perform exact or highly accurate approximate kernel computations for much larger problem sizes (e.g.,

Finally, in

Although there is a known bijection between them, we prove it does not preserve geometry.
Consequently, the
“push-forward”

1.1
Background

Intuitively,
kernels
are similarity measures.
Formally, a function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
on some set
\mathcal{X}
is a kernel if it satisfies the technical condition of being
positive semi-definite
.
That is, if the matrix
\mathbf{K}_{nn}=[k(x_{i},x_{j})]_{i,j}
is positive semi-definite for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Designing functions that represent a useful notion of similarity on a given
\mathcal{X}
and, at the same time, satisfy this technical condition is far from trivial.

When
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the family of
heat
kernels (also known as
RBF
,
squared expoential
,
Gaussian
, or
diffusion
kernels), and their extension known as the
Matérn
kernels, are perhaps the most popular
(
34
;
33
)
.
A key reason for this is they are essentially the simplest kernels satisfying:

•

Stationarity
.
This means that shifting both arguments of the kernel by the same vector
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
leaves the kernel value unchanged:
k(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mathbf{s})=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
.
In many settings, e.g. in kernel ridge regression or Gaussian process regression, this translates into the following: training on a shifted dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{s}}=(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\mathbf{s},y_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}+\mathbf{s},y_{n})
yields a model
f_{\mathbf{s}}(\cdot)
related to the original model
f
by the same shift
f_{\mathbf{s}}(\cdot)=f(\cdot+\mathbf{s})
.
This is a property so natural that it hardly requires further explanation.

•

Smoothness
.
In its simplest form, this can be formalized as continuity:
k(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\approx k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
for small
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
More generally, in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this is formalized by differentiability—that is, we assume
k
has a certain number of derivatives.
2
2

2

For Matérn kernels, the degree of differentiability can be non-integer and is controlled by a parameter
\nu\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
See
34
and
33
for more details on Matérn kernels.

Assuming some smoothness is crucial for
generalization
: if
k
could change arbitrarily even for nearby points, generalization to unseen data would be impossible.

These two properties—stationarity and smoothness—admit far-reaching generalizations, enabling the construction of useful kernels for input domains
\mathcal{X}
far beyond
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(
2
;
3
;
7
)
.
Notably, such generalizations include extensions of the heat and Matérn kernels
(
8
;
6
)
.
3
3

3

Interestingly, generalizations based on stationarity and smoothness yield more successful kernel constructions than extending explicit formulas for the heat and Matérn kernels from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to other domains
(
17
;
12
)
.

To illustrate the generalized notions of stationarity and smoothness in non-Euclidean settings, and how their interplay gives rise to generalized heat and Matérn kernels, we now discuss an example setting.
Specifically, let us assume
\mathcal{X}
is the node set of a graph—the setting that will turn out to be of key importance throughout the rest of the paper.
Formally, let
\mathcal{X}=V
be the set of nodes of an undirected graph without loops
G=(V,E)
with edges
E\subseteq V\times V
and with
V=\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
For simplicity, we assume
G
is unweighted.

For a graph
G
, the analog of translations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
automorphisms
of
G
, i.e., the set of all permutations
\sigma:V\to V
preserving edge structure, i.e. such that

(\sigma(x),\sigma(x^{\prime}))\in E
if and only if
(x,x^{\prime})\in E
.
Therefore, a kernel
k:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
is called stationary, if and only if
k(\sigma(x),\sigma(x^{\prime}))=k(x,x^{\prime})
for all such
\sigma
.
Intuitively, this reflects the fact that labeling nodes by indices
1,\ldots,d
is arbitrary: for many tasks such as regression, relabeling the data should simply transform the model according to the same relabeling.
This is a concrete example of the general concept of being
stationary under a group action
, which we revisit in
Section

2.1
.

Smoothness on graphs is more subtle.
While it is possible to define proximity via the shortest path distance, this does not yield a quantitative smoothness scale analogous to the number of (generalized) derivatives.
Instead, smoothness can be characterized spectrally: the decay of coefficients in the basis of graph Laplacian eigenvectors generalizes the role of smoothness and Fourier decay in classical settings (see, e.g.,
11
).

26
and
6
introduced general-purpose versions of the heat and Matérn kernels which respect the above-mentioned notions of stationarity and smoothness on graphs.
Let the connectivity of
G
be encoded in the
d\times d
adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
, and define the graph Laplacian as
\mathbf{\Delta}=\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{A}
, where
\mathbf{D}
is the diagonal degree matrix with entries
\mathbf{D}_{ii}=\sum_{j}\mathbf{A}_{ij}
.
It is well-known that
\mathbf{\Delta}
is a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix.
Let
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
be an orthonormal basis of
eigenvectors
of
\mathbf{\Delta}
, with corresponding
eigenvalues

\lambda_{i}\geq 0
.
We can reinterpret them as
eigenfunctions

u_{i}:V\to\mathbb{R}
with
u_{i}(x)=\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\right)_{x}
.
We denote the heat kernel on
V
by
k_{\infty,\kappa}(\cdot,\cdot)
and the Matérn kernel by
k_{\nu,\kappa}(\cdot,\cdot)
, where
\kappa,\nu>0
.

Then, according to
6
, both can be computed via

\displaystyle k_{\nu,\kappa}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{C_{\nu,\kappa}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\Phi_{\nu,\kappa}(\lambda_{\ell})u_{\ell}(x)u_{\ell}(x^{\prime}),\qquad\Phi_{\nu,\kappa}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\lambda\right)&\text{if }\nu=\infty,\\[4.26773pt]
\left(\frac{2\nu}{\kappa^{2}}+\lambda\right)^{-\nu}&\text{if }\nu<\infty.\end{cases}

(1)

Here,
x,x^{\prime}
are nodes of the graph, and
C_{\nu,\kappa}>0
is a normalization constant ensuring
\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{X}\right|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}k_{\nu,\kappa}(x,x)=1
.

Stationary Kernels

Intuitively, since the specific labeling
1,\ldots,2n
of elements in a matching is arbitrary, a kernel on matchings should remain unchanged when both of its arguments are relabeled in the same way.
We formalize this intuition in
Section

2.1
using the framework of stationarity under a group action.
Examining the relevant group action further in
Section

2.2
, we naturally identify the structure of matchings as a homogeneous space.
This structure allows us to explicitly characterize the class of stationary kernels on matchings in
Section

2.3
.

2.1
Group Action and Stationarity under a Group Action

As mentioned above, the labeling
1,\ldots,2n
of elements in a matching is arbitrary.
This arbitrariness is an example of a
symmetry
.
More generally, a symmetry of a set
\mathcal{X}
is any bijective map
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
that preserves its relevant structure.
The choice of structure determines what transformations qualify as symmetries.

Formally, we represent the symmetries of
\mathcal{X}
by considering the action of a group
G
on
\mathcal{X}
, expressed as a
group action

\triangleright:G\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
.
In typical notation,
g\triangleright x
denotes the action of
g\in G
on
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Given a group
G
acting on
\mathcal{X}
via
\triangleright
, a kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
stationary under the group action
if and only if

k(g\triangleright x,g\triangleright x^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})\qquad\text{for all }x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},\ g\in G.

(2)

For the set
\mathcal{X}_{n}
of matchings of size
n
, the relevant symmetries are permutations
\sigma:\{1,\ldots,2n\}\to\{1,\ldots,2n\}

(
10
)
.
The collection of all such permutations is called the
symmetric group

S_{2n}
of degree
2n
.
For
\sigma\in S_{2n}
and a matching
x\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, we define the action by

\sigma\triangleright x:=\{\{\sigma(i_{1}),\sigma(i_{2})\},\ldots,\{\sigma(i_{2n-1}),\sigma(i_{2n})\}\},

(3)

where
x=\{\{i_{1},i_{2}\},\ldots,\{i_{2n-1},i_{2n}\}\}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
.

Combining this group action with the invariance condition (
2
) defines stationarity for kernels on matchings.
We now describe how this group action offers an alternative, algebraic perspective on
\mathcal{X}_{n}
: the space of matchings is a
homogeneous space
.
This perspective is key to the explicit characterization of stationary kernels in
Section

2.3
.

2.2
The Set of Matchings
\mathcal{X}_{n}
as a Homogeneous Space

Recall that the group action is said to be
transitive
if, for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists
g\in G
such that
g\triangleright x=x^{\prime}
.
Intuitively, this means the action is rich enough to transform any point into any other point.
In this case,
\mathcal{X}
is called a
homogeneous space
, and it may be identified with the
coset space

G/H
, where
H
is the
isotropy subgroup
fixing some basepoint
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
:

H=\left\{h\in G:h\triangleright x_{0}=x_{0}\right\}.

(4)

The elements of
G/H
are
cosets

gH=\{gh:h\in H\}
.
The natural bijective correspondence between
G/H
and
\mathcal{X}
maps the coset
gH
to
the element
g\triangleright x_{0}
.
Here,
G
acts on
G/H
by left multiplication
g\triangleright(g^{\prime}H)=(gg^{\prime})H
.

In the case of matchings
\mathcal{X}_{n}
with the action (
3
), let us fix the basepoint as
x_{0}=\{\{1,2\},\ldots,\{2n-1,2n\}\}
.
Any permutation
h
that leaves
x_{0}
unchanged (that is,
h\triangleright x_{0}=x_{0}
) can either swap the elements within a given pair (that is, map
(i,i+1)
to
(i+1,i)
for some odd
i
) or permute entire pairs among themselves (that is, send the
j
th pair
(2j-1,2j)
to the
k
th pair
(2k-1,2k)
).
Together, these generate the isotropy subgroup
H
, which is isomorphic to the
hyperoctahedral group

H_{n}
, the symmetry group of the
n
-dimensional hypercube
(
10
)
.
Thus, the set of matchings can be identified with the coset space
S_{2n}/H_{n}
, and this perspective enables the application of general theory of stationary kernels on homogeneous spaces.

Functions on
G/H
and functions on
G
.

An important consequence of viewing a homogeneous space as a quotient
G/H
is that any function
\phi:G/H\to\mathbb{R}
can be
lifted
to a function
\widetilde{\phi}:G\to\mathbb{R}
on the group
G
, constant on cosets, by defining

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}(g)=\phi(gH),

\displaystyle g\in G.

(5)

Conversely, any function
\widetilde{\psi}:G\to\mathbb{R}
can be
projected
down to a function on
\psi:G/H\to\mathbb{R}
via averaging:

\displaystyle\psi(gH)=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}\widetilde{\psi}(gh),

\displaystyle g\in G.

(6)

Notably, the lifting from
\phi
to
\widetilde{\phi}
is information-preserving, while the projection from
\widetilde{\psi}
to
\psi
typically loses information due to averaging.
The constructions in
Equations

5
and
6
extend naturally to kernels: any kernel
k:G/H\times G/H\to\mathbb{R}
can be lifted to a kernel
\widetilde{k}:G\times G\to\mathbb{R}
, and, conversely, any kernel on the group can be projected to the quotient.
Importantly, positive semidefiniteness is preserved in both directions.

2.3
The Class of Stationary Kernels

As discussed in
Section

2.2
, the set of matchings
\mathcal{X}_{n}
of size
n
can be naturally regarded as the homogeneous space
S_{2n}/H_{n}
, where
S_{2n}
is the symmetric group of degree
2n
and
H_{n}
is the hyperoctahedral group.
Thanks to this identification, we can apply the general theory of stationary kernels on homogeneous spaces as developed in
43
.
Moreover, in our case, the pair
(S_{2n},H_{n})
satisfies the technical assumptions of a
Gelfand pair

(
10
)
—we omit the discussion of this notion here—further simplifying the general structure of stationary kernels on
\mathcal{X}_{n}
.
This leads to the following description.

Theorem 1
.

A kernel
k
on the homogeneous space
\mathcal{X}_{n}=S_{2n}/H_{n}
is stationary
(
in the sense of
Equation

2
)
if and only if it can be written as

k(\sigma H_{n},\pi H_{n})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\vdash n}a_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\phi_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\pi^{-1}\sigma),

(7)

where the sum runs over
\boldsymbol{\mu}\vdash n
, all
integer partitions

\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{s})
of
n
;
a_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
; and
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}:S_{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
are the
zonal spherical functions
on the homogeneous space
S_{2n}/H_{n}
described in
Proposition

1
below.

Proof.

This is a version of the Bochner–Godement theorem (see, for example, Section 1.1, Theorem 1.2 in
16
), with the general representation-theoretic indexing of zonal spherical functions specialized to the combinatorial partition-based indexing for matchings.
Please see
Section

A.1
for details.
∎

Theorem

1
generalizes the classical Bochner’s theorem, which states that every continuous positive semidefinite function is proportional to the inverse Fourier transform of a unique probability measure.
Here, the expansion into a linear combination of zonal spherical functions plays the role of inverse Fourier transform, while specifying a probability measure amounts to assigning the non-negative coefficients
a_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
in the expansion.

The

from

admit the following description in terms of

which, in their turn, posses well-known analytical expressions.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{s})
be a partition of

.
For the zonal spherical function

\phi_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{|H_{n}|^{2}}\sum_{\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in H_{n}}\chi^{(2\boldsymbol{\rho})}(\pi_{1}\sigma\pi_{2}),

(8)

where
2\boldsymbol{\rho}=(2\rho_{1},\ldots,2\rho_{s})
is a partition of

\chi^{(2\boldsymbol{\rho})}
is the corresponding

of the group

.

Proof.

See

for the proof and further discussion on zonal spherical functions.

Thus, zonal spherical functions can be viewed as two-sided

.
Analytic expressions for these characters are available, for example, via the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (see Theorem 7.17.1 in

However, zonal spherical function computation using averaging and these analytic expressions is computationally expensive—the issue we return to in

Here, a kernel

3
Heat and Matérn Kernels

The class of stationary kernels described in the previous section is far too broad for practical applications.
For instance, setting
a_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=1
for all
\boldsymbol{\mu}
yields the delta kernel
k(x,x^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}_{x=x^{\prime}}
which precludes any generalization.
To define useful kernels, it is necessary to also impose a degree of smoothness.
This is precisely where the heat and Matérn kernels become relevant.
As discussed in
Section

1.1
, constructing such kernels on discrete spaces is subtle, as it is difficult to define a quantitative scale of differentiability on such spaces.

Here, we address this challenge indirectly, circumventing it.
We first describe heat and Matérn kernels on the group
S_{2n}
where an appropriate notion of smoothness can be obtained by regarding it as its
Caley graph
.
We then obtain kernels on the set of matchings by canonically projecting these group kernels onto the matchings space, as described at the end of
Section

2.2
.
Finally, we show that these projected kernels can also be interpreted as heat and Matérn kernels defined directly on a certain associated
quotient graph
—see details in
Section

A.3
.

3.1
Symmetric Group

We begin by introducing a notion of distance between two elements
g,g^{\prime}
, of a finite abstract group
G
.
Intuitively, this distance can be understood as the minimum number of elementary operations needed to transform
g
into
g^{\prime}
.
Formally, let
\mathcal{S}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}
be a set of
generators
for the group
G
—i.e, such a set that any
g\in G
can be written as
g=g_{i_{1}}\cdots g_{i_{\ell}}
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and some sequence
g_{i_{1}},\ldots,g_{i_{\ell}}\in\mathcal{S}
(repetitions allowed).
Given a set
\mathcal{S}
, we can define the distance
d(g,g^{\prime})
as the minimal length
\ell
needed to express
g^{-1}g^{\prime}
as a product of generators from
\mathcal{S}
.
It turns out
(
27
)
that this is the shortest-path distance between
g
and
g^{\prime}
in the
Cayley graph
of group
G
generated by
\mathcal{S}
;
that is, the graph
\Gamma(G,\mathcal{S})
whose nodes are the group elements
V=G
and whose edges are pairs of the form
(g,g_{i}g)
where
g\in G
and
g_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Now, consider the specific case
G=S_{2n}
of the symmetric group of permutations of
2n
elements, which acts as the group of symmetries of matchings.
There are several possible choices for the generating set
\mathcal{S}
.
However, not all of them behave equally well—see Corollary 5.2.2 and the subsequent discussion in
27
.
A particularly appealing choice is the set
\mathcal{S}
that consists of all transpositions, i.e., all permutations that swap exactly two elements
1\leq i,j\leq 2n
.
With this choice, the resulting heat and Matérn kernels on
\Gamma(G,\mathcal{S})
—as defined by
Equation

1
—are bi-invariant, meaning

k(\pi\sigma,\pi\sigma^{\prime})=k(\sigma\pi,\sigma^{\prime}\pi)=k(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\qquad\text{for all }\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\pi\in S_{2n}.

(9)

This is shown for heat kernels in
27
.
However, the exact same argument applies to Matérn kernels.

The argument above uses the definition of heat and Matérn kernels in terms of eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of the graph Laplacian associated with the Cayley graph, as described in
Section

1.1
.
Importantly, by leveraging the algebraic structure of
S_{2n}
, one can reformulate the standard expansion by grouping together eigenfunctions corresponding to the same eigenvalue, thereby dramatically reducing the number of terms and making it simpler.
This leads to the character-theoretic expansion for kernels on
S_{2n}
, as presented below.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, e.g.,
\Phi(\lambda)=e^{-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\lambda}
or
\Phi(\lambda)=\left(\frac{2\nu}{\kappa^{2}}+\lambda\right)^{-\nu}
.
Then, for any kernel of the form

\displaystyle k(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{C(\Phi)}\sum_{\pi\in S_{2n}}\Phi(\lambda_{\pi})u_{\pi}(\sigma)\overline{u_{\pi}(\sigma^{\prime})},

\displaystyle\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in S_{2n},

4
Computation

Here, we develop algorithms for computing the heat and Matérn kernels introduced above.
We begin in
Section

4.1
by showing that kernel evaluation—either exact or approximate—ultimately reduces to computing zonal spherical functions.
In
Section

4.2

In
Section

4.3
, we present our own sub-exponential algorithm, which enables kernel computations for much larger values of

.
The key insight is that each zonal spherical function depends only on a generalized distance to a fixed basepoint, and the number of distinct generalized distance values is sub-exponential in
n
.
A discussion, including computational complexity estimates, practical considerations, and numerical experiments, is deferred to the next section.

4.1
From Kernels to Zonal Spherical Functions

As observed in
Section

3
, the heat and Matérn kernels can be interpreted as kernels on the node set of a certain graph.
In principle, this would allow for evaluation via
Equation

1

However, the graph Laplacian matrix has size

This motivates the search for alternative approaches.

Alternatively, we may exploit the expansion in

Here, the number of terms is equal to the number of integer partitions of

This is a classical combinatorial quantity, and it is well known that

\exp(an)

4.2
Zonal Spherical Functions: Existing Algorithms

The most direct method for computing zonal spherical functions is to use
Proposition

1

S_{2n}

Approximation quality.

A truncated kernel is an approximation to the exact kernel.
It is therefore natural to ask how good this approximation is in practice.
We analyze this question as follows.
Recall that the truncated kernel is defined as

\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{R}}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{R}}\Phi(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\frac{d_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{|S_{2n}|}\phi_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{1}),

\displaystyle x=\sigma_{1}H_{n},\quad x^{\prime}=\sigma_{2}H_{n},

\displaystyle\frac{\|k-k_{\mathcal{R}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{n})}^{2}}{\|k\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{n})}^{2}},

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{n})}^{2}=\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\left|f(x,x^{\prime})\right|^{2}.

5.3
Hyperparameters

Besides choosing the set of representations for truncation, in practice we also need to specify two hyperparameters for the kernel: the lengthscale
\kappa
and the smoothness parameter
\nu
.

For a fixed smoothness parameter
\nu
, we choose the lengthscale
\kappa
using a simple heuristic.
We consider the expansion in
Equation

12
, and in particular the coefficients corresponding to the partitions
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(n)
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(n-1,1)
.
We then choose
\kappa
so that the ratio of these two coefficients is equal to
2
.
This heuristic is intended to avoid two degenerate regimes.
If
\kappa
is too large, the kernel approaches a constant kernel and therefore treats all inputs as almost equally similar.
If
\kappa
is too small, the kernel approaches a delta kernel and therefore treats all inputs as almost completely dissimilar.
The choice of partitions is motivated by spectral considerations.
Ideally, we want this ratio condition to involve the partitions associated with the largest and second-largest eigenvalues.
In the set
\mathcal{R}
, however, partitions are selected according to the size of
\rho_{1}
rather than directly by their eigenvalues.
It is therefore natural to use the partitions with the largest and second-largest values of
\rho_{1}
, namely
(n)
and
(n-1,1)
.

For the
smoothness parameter
, it can be empirically observed that values of
\nu
popular in the Euclidean case, such as
\nu=2.5
, do not yield reasonable kernels in our setting.
For example, such choices tend to result in overly “rough” kernels, as the coefficients in
Equation

12
end up amplifying high frequencies more than low frequencies.
We hypothesize that this is due to the need to adjust kernels for the effective “dimensionality” of the domain.
Indeed, while the original definition of Matérn kernels in the Euclidean case incorporated a dimension correction, this adjustment is omitted in the standard graph-based definition
(
6
)
, since the notion of dimension is not well-defined for a general graph:

\text{No dimension correction: }\quad\Phi(\lambda)=\left(\frac{2\nu}{\kappa^{2}}+\mathbf{\Delta}\right)^{-\nu}\qquad\text{Dimension correction: }\quad\Phi(\lambda)=\left(\frac{2\nu}{\kappa^{2}}+\mathbf{\Delta}\right)^{-(\nu+\frac{d}{2})}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!.

(22)

However, since the graphs we consider are regular with constant degree, interpreting the degree as the effective dimension is natural.
This idea has appeared in previous work
(
9
;
7
)
.
In our case, the degree is
n(n-1)
—see derivation in
Section

A.3
—which is a relatively large value.
Therefore, accounting for this correction may be crucial in practice.
Empirically, with this correction, kernels behave as expected, and values such as
\nu\in\{0.5,1.5,2.5,\infty\}
serve as good defaults—please refer to
Section

C.2
for examples with and without correction.
We also note that, in this case, it is straightforward to differentiate the (approximate) kernel with respect to the hyperparameter
\nu
, as for other topologically compact domains
(
8
;
6
)
.
This allows, in settings such as Gaussian process regression, for
\nu
to be optimized over jointly with
\kappa
, rather than selected manually.
Importantly, in this case, the dimension correction may still be important for choosing a reasonable initialization of
\nu
.

Does there exist an efficiently computable bijection between matchings and phylogenetic trees such that “small” changes in one space correspond to “small” changes in the other and vice versa?

Some hope may be drawn from the following argument.

A.3
Quotient Cayley Graph

In this section, we will define quotient Cayley graph and discuss two related questions.
First, we will prove that defining the kernel on quotient Cayley graph is equivalent to averaging the kernel on Cayley graph, therefore justifying our definition through averaging.
Second, we will compute degree of the corresponding quotient Cayley graph, which we will further use to employ degree correction for hyperparameter selection.

Let

Degree

As we will further see, the quotient Cayley graph is regular, so all nodes have the same degree.
We will further need the exact value of degree in order to make a degree correction to one of the hyperparameters of the defined kernel.
So, here we will compute the (unweighted) degree.

Consider a node in the Cayley quotient graph, i.e., a coset
\sigma H
or, equivalently, a matching
x\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
.
There will be an edge between
x
and
y\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
if and only if there is transposition
\pi\in W
such that
y=\pi\triangleright x
.
Let us analyze how
\pi\triangleright x
looks like for all transpositions
\pi
.

Note that if
\pi=(i\,j)
such that the pair
\{i,j\}\in x
, then
\pi\triangleright x=x
.
Otherwise, there are two pairs
\{i,k\},\{j,l\}\in x
, and the generalized distance (see
Section

4.2
for definition)
d(x,\pi\triangleright x)=(2,1\ldots,1)
since
\pi\triangleright x
has exactly the same pairs as
x
except
\{i,k\},\{j,l\}
, which are replaced with
\{j,k\},\{i,l\}
.
And vice versa, if
d(x,y)=(2,1,\ldots,1)
, then there is transposition
\pi
such that
y=\pi\triangleright x
.
So, degree of
x
is given by
|\{y\in X\mid d(x,y)=(2,1,\ldots,1)\}|

It is easy to see that
|\{y\in X\mid d(x,y)=(2,1,\ldots,1)\}|=n(n-1)
since one should select two pairs in

So, there are

General algorithm

As discussed in the main text, our algorithm is grounded in the relationship between zonal spherical functions and zonal polynomials.

C.1
Derivation of the approximation error formula

In this section, we will derive a formula that will allow us to estimate the approximation error of truncated kernels.
Remember that the relative approximation error is given by

\frac{\|k-k_{\mathcal{R}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{n})}}{\|k\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{n})}}

Our first step here is to replace the space
L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{n})
by
L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n})
, which is easier to analyze.

Lemma 4
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{n})
be a bi-invariant function.
Then,

\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{n})}=|\mathcal{X}_{n}|\cdot\|f(\bullet,x_{0})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n})}

(42)

Proof.

The following holds,

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}_{n})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}|f(x,y)|^{2}=\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|^{2}}{|S_{2n}|^{2}}\sum_{\sigma,\pi\in S_{2n}}|f(\sigma x_{0},\pi x_{0})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|^{2}}{|S_{2n}|^{2}}\sum_{\sigma,\pi\in S_{2n}}|f(\pi^{-1}\sigma x_{0},x_{0})|^{2}=\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|^{2}}{|S_{2n}|}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{2n}}|f(\sigma x_{0},x_{0})|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{X}_{n}|\cdot\|f(\bullet,x_{0})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n})}.

as required in the lemma.
∎

According to this lemma, we are interested in computing

\frac{\|k(\bullet,x_{0})-k_{\mathcal{R}}(\bullet,x_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n})}}{\|k(\bullet,x_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n})}}

However, since both

are of the form

for some

(this is

for

with an arbitrary
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\{\boldsymbol{\rho}\vdash n\}
.
This is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

For arbitrary
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\{\boldsymbol{\rho}\vdash n\}
, and truncated kernel

k_{\mathcal{R}}(\sigma H_{n},\sigma^{\prime}H_{n})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{R}}\Phi(\lambda_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}})\,\frac{d_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{|S_{2n}|}\,\phi_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}((\sigma^{\prime})^{-1}\sigma),

(44)

Proof.

Remember that

\displaystyle\|k_{\mathcal{R}}(\bullet,x_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{R}}\left(\Phi(\lambda_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}})\frac{d_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{|S_{2n}|}\right)^{2}\|\phi_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{n})}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{R}}\left(\Phi(\lambda_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}})\frac{d_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{|S_{2n}|}\right)^{2}\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|}{d_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}}}

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{X}_{n}|\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\left(\Phi(\lambda_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}})\frac{d_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{|S_{2n}|}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{d_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}}}=|\mathcal{X}_{n}|\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\frac{\Phi(\lambda_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}})^{2}d_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{|S_{2n}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{X}_{n}|}{|S_{2n}|^{2}}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\Phi(\lambda_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}})^{2}d_{2\boldsymbol{\rho}}

Recall the Diaconis–Holmes correspondence between matchings in
\mathcal{X}_{n}
and rooted binary phylogenetic trees
with
n+1
leaves.

We use the following simple observation: a single NNI move changes the height of a rooted binary phylogenetic tree by at most
1
.
Consequently, for any two trees, NNI-distance is lower bounded by a difference of heights of corresponding trees.

We now construct, for every
n\geq 9
, two matchings that differ by a single transposition but whose trees
have heights differing by at least
\frac{n}{2}-2
.

Define the matching
x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
by

x_{1}=\bigl((3,4),(5,n+2),(6,n+3),\ldots,(n+1,2n-2),(2,2n-1),(1,2n)\bigr).

(47)

Let
\sigma
be the transposition swapping
1
and
2n-1
.
Then
\sigma\triangleright x_{1}
differs from
x_{1}
only in the last two pairs, and we obtain

x_{2}:=\sigma\triangleright x_{1}=\bigl((1,2),(3,4),(5,n+2),(6,n+3),\ldots,(n+1,2n-2),(2n-1,2n)\bigr).

(48)

Let
T_{1},T_{2}
be the rooted phylogenetic trees corresponding to
x_{1},x_{2}
under the Diaconis–Holmes map.

Height of
T_{1}
.

In the tree reconstruction procedure, the first eligible pair among labels
\{1,\dots,n+1\}
is
(3,4)
, so the
first-created internal node
n+2
has children
3
and
4
.
Next, among labels
\{1,\dots,n+2\}
, the smallest eligible pair is
(5,n+2)
, so the next internal node

n+3
has children
5
and
n+2
.
Continuing inductively, we create a caterpillar chain: each new internal node has one new leaf as one child
and the previously created internal node as the other child, until the pair
(2,2n-1)

(1,2n)

Proof.

By analyzing the embedding proposed by
35
, we establish the following two properties:

1.

Locality of internal labels.

The label of an internal node depends only on the labels of leaves in its subtree.
More precisely, the label equals
-k
, where
k
is the largest leaf label
j
in the subtree such that no
internal node in the same subtree has label
-j
.

2.

Labels present in a subtree.

Let a subtree have leaf labels
0\leq a_{1}<\dots<a_{k}
. Then the set of labels appearing on
all

nodes of this subtree is
\{-a_{2},\dots,-a_{k}\}\ \cup\ \{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
.

In particular, unlike the Diaconis–Holmes labeling, the label of a fixed internal node does not depend on
the global topology of the tree.

Now consider an NNI move performed on an internal edge
(u,v)
inside a rooted subtree
T
(see

Figure

8
).
Assume that
u
has children
v
and a subtree
C
, while
v
has children subtrees
A
and
B
.
After the NNI move we obtain a subtree
\widetilde{T}
in which
v
has children
A
and
u
, and
u
has
children
B
and
C
.

Let
\mathrm{label}_{T}(\cdot)
and
\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(\cdot)
denote the node labels before and after the move.
By the locality property above, the labelings
within
each of the subtrees
A
,
B
, and
C
are unchanged,
and therefore all matching pairs internal to
A
, internal to
B
, and internal to
C
are unchanged as well.
Hence, the only pairs within subtree
T
that may change are those created at the two internal parents
v
and
u
.

Denote by
\mathrm{root}(\cdot)
the root node of a subtree. In
T
the two relevant pairs are

\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(A)),\,\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(B))\bigr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{T}(v),\,\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(C))\bigr),

(51)

while in
\widetilde{T}
they become

\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(\mathrm{root}(A)),\,\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u)\bigr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(\mathrm{root}(B)),\,\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(\mathrm{root}(C))\bigr).

(52)

Since
\mathrm{root}(A),\mathrm{root}(B),\mathrm{root}(C)
belong to unchanged subtrees, we may recognize

\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(A))=\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(\mathrm{root}(A))
and similarly for
B,C
. Thus the change is

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(A)),\,\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(B))\bigr)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(A)),\,\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u)\bigr),

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{T}(v),\,\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(C))\bigr)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(B)),\,\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(C))\bigr).

We claim that these transformations can be realized by at most two transpositions of labels.
Define

\sigma_{1}\;=\;\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(B)),\,\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u)\bigr).

(53)

Applying
\sigma_{1}
swaps the two labels
\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(B))
and
\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u)
and therefore
turns the first pair into the desired one. If additionally

\mathrm{label}_{T}(v)\neq\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u)
, we also apply

\sigma_{2}\;=\;\bigl(\mathrm{label}_{T}(v),\,\mathrm{label}_{T}(\mathrm{root}(B))\bigr),

(54)

which corrects the second pair as well. Thus, inside the affected part of the matching, at most two
transpositions suffice.

It remains to check that no additional transpositions are needed to fix the pair involving the root of
T
.
By the subtree label-set property, the set of labels appearing in the subtree rooted at
u
is unchanged by
NNI. In particular,

\{\mathrm{label}_{T}(u),\mathrm{label}_{T}(v)\}\;=\;\{\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u),\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(v)\}.

(55)

Hence there are only two cases:

1.

If
\mathrm{label}_{T}(v)=\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u)
, then necessarily
\mathrm{label}_{T}(u)=\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(v)
.
In this case the root label of the considered subtree is unchanged, and applying
\sigma_{1}
does not affect
the pair containing
\mathrm{label}_{T}(u)
.

2.

If
\mathrm{label}_{T}(v)\neq\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u)
, then
\mathrm{label}_{T}(u)=\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u)
and

\mathrm{label}_{T}(v)=\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(v)
.
In this case, after applying
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, the pair incident to
\mathrm{label}_{T}(u)
is transformed
into the pair incident to
\mathrm{label}_{\widetilde{T}}(u)
, as required.

Therefore the pair involving the root of the modified subtree remains consistent after the same
(at most two) transpositions.

Finally, since the set of labels appearing in the modified subtree is unchanged, all labels of internal nodes

outside
this subtree (which depend only on the label sets of their descendant subtrees) remain unchanged.
Thus no other matching pairs are affected.

We conclude that the matchings associated with
T
and
\widetilde{T}
differ by at most two transpositions,
so their distance in the quotient Cayley graph is at most
2
.
