Gallbladder torsion in multimorbid older adults: a structured narrative review and case report
Authors: Ji Liu, Zhi-Feng Cheng, Min-Zhi Li, Yi Wu, Zi-Yang Tang, Wen-Tao He, Fu-Kang Xiong, Xiao-Qin Liang, Wei Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGallbladder torsion (GT) is a rare, life-threatening surgical emergency that disproportionately affects older adults. Diagnosis is frequently complicated by multimorbidity and age-related anatomical changes, which mask typical symptoms and mimic acute cholecystitis. We report a complex case of GT and present a structured narrative review of 180 cases to provide evidence-based strategies for early recognition and surgical management.Case presentationWe report the case of an 84-year-old male with sudden-onset severe right upper quadrant pain, nausea, and vomiting. Preoperative computed tomography revealed a markedly distended gallbladder with gallstones and pericholecystic fluid, and torsion was highly suspected but not definitively confirmed until laparoscopic exploration. Laparoscopic exploration confirmed 360° clockwise torsion of the gallbladder around the cystic duct and artery. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy was successfully performed, and the patient recovered uneventfully. Pathological examination confirmed transmural hemorrhagic necrosis and acute inflammation, consistent with gangrenous cholecystitis secondary to torsion.ConclusionGT is a critical differential diagnosis for acute abdomen in older adults. Age-related anatomical changes require a high index of suspicion, as clinical presentations often overlap with routine cholecystitis. Early recognition of specific imaging markers and immediate laparoscopic intervention are vital to prevent gangrene, perforation, and mortality in this vulnerable population.

Core tip

Gallbladder torsion masquerading as acute calculous cholecystitis is a dangerous diagnostic pitfall. Lack of response to initial antibiotic and analgesic therapy should raise immediate suspicion. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion in thin, older patients presenting with sudden severe pain and a markedly distended gallbladder, even when imaging shows stones. When in doubt, prompt laparoscopic intervention is essential to prevent ischemic complications.

Background

Gallbladder torsion (GT) is a rare condition. Since its first description by Wendel in 1898, our understanding of this condition has gradually improved [1]. Although precise epidemiological data are lacking, published reports suggest that slightly more than 500 cases have been documented. GT occurs predominantly in women aged > 60 years, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 3:1 [2]. The typical presenting symptoms include abdominal pain, vomiting, fever, and occasionally a palpable abdominal mass. Because these features overlap significantly with other acute abdominal conditions such as acute calculous or acalculous cholecystitis, and even acute appendicitis, misdiagnosis is common. If not promptly treated, torsion compromises the blood supply via twisting of the cystic artery, leading to ischemia, necrosis, and potentially severe complications, such as biliary peritonitis or gangrenous gallbladder perforation [3, 4]. Consequently, early recognition and timely surgical intervention are essential to achieve favorable outcomes. Previous narrative reviews of GT have predominantly summarized the general clinical features of this rare disease across all age groups, with few studies providing dedicated, quantitative analysis of GT in multimorbid older adults - the population with the highest reported incidence of the disease. Furthermore, prior reviews have rarely performed a dedicated analysis of the associations between torsion severity, surgical timing, and clinical outcomes specifically in older adult patients. To address this gap in the existing literature, we present a rare case of GT in an 84-year-old multimorbid male patient, alongside a structured narrative review of 180 published GT cases from 1989 to 2025, with a dedicated focus on the unique diagnostic challenges and surgical management strategies for GT in older adults. This report describes the case of an older male patient with gallbladder torsion who underwent successful laparoscopic cholecystectomy and provides a structured narrative review of GT to support clinical practice.

Case presentation

Chief complaints

An 84-year-old man presented to our emergency department with a half-day history of persistent right upper quadrant (RUQ) abdominal pain and vomiting.

History of present illness

The pain began abruptly without an identifiable trigger. It was exacerbated by the ingestion of fatty food and radiated to the right shoulder and lumbar region. The episode was accompanied by nausea and multiple episodes of vomiting gastric contents. The patient reported no fever, chills, or jaundice prior to admission.

History of past illness

The patient had a significant medical history, including coronary artery disease status post percutaneous coronary intervention (March 2023), paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and cerebral infarction. He had undergone partial small-bowel resection and hernia repair for a strangulated left inguinal hernia several years prior. A history of cholecystitis was also noted. His regular medications included atorvastatin, bisoprolol fumarate, indobufen, and sustained-release isosorbide dinitrate.

Personal and family history

The patient was a retired office worker with no history of smoking or alcohol consumption. He denied any family history of malignant tumors or hereditary diseases.

Physical examination

Upon admission, vital signs were stable: temperature 37.2℃, heart rate 88 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, blood pressure 145/84 mmHg. The patient was thin with a BMI of 18.5 kg/m². Abdominal examination revealed a flat abdomen with marked tenderness and rebound tenderness localized to the RUQ. Murphy’s sign was positive. No palpable mass or organomegaly was detected. Bowel sounds were normoactive.

Laboratory examinations

Blood analysis revealed an elevated white blood cell count of 11.23 × 10⁹/L [reference range: (3.5–9.5) × 10⁹/L] and a neutrophil percentage of 85.4%. C-reactive protein was elevated at 45 mg/L (reference: < 10 mg/L). Liver function tests, including alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and total bilirubin levels, were within normal limits.

Imaging examinations

Abdominal CT revealed folding of the gallbladder (Fig. 1A), significant distension (Fig. 1B), gallstones with features suggestive of acute cholecystitis (Fig. 1C), and fluid surrounding the gallbladder and the hepatic hilum (Fig. 1D). A clear separation was observed between the gallbladder and the liver, giving the appearance of a ‘floating gallbladder’ (Fig. 1E). A complex configuration between the cystic duct and the common bile duct demonstrated abrupt tapering resembling a ‘beak sign’ (Fig. 1F). No significant intrahepatic or extrahepatic bile duct dilations were observed.

Based on these findings and the acute presentation, an initial trial of conservative management was instituted. Although imaging signs suggestive of torsion were present in retrospect, the initial clinical picture overlapped heavily with acute calculous cholecystitis. Given the patient’s high cardiovascular risk (recent percutaneous coronary intervention) and concurrent antiplatelet therapy, a brief trial of conservative management was initially prioritized to avoid prohibitive perioperative morbidity. Strict monitoring for any signs of deterioration was maintained. The patient received intravenous anisodamine hydrobromide (10 mg) for spasmolysis and cefoperazone-sulbactam (3.0 g) as empiric antibiotic therapy. However, his right upper quadrant pain showed no improvement. His condition subsequently deteriorated, with the development of fever (38.6℃), tachycardia, and a decrease in blood pressure to 105/75 mmHg. Due to the failure of conservative treatment and signs of systemic deterioration, a decision for emergency surgical intervention was made.

Final diagnosis

Preoperative diagnosis

Cholelithiasis, acute cholecystitis, and suspected gallbladder torsion, based on clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and imaging features.

Intraoperative diagnosis

Complete clockwise torsion of a floating gallbladder, resulting in ischemic necrosis.

Postoperative pathological diagnosis

Gangrenous cholecystitis with transmural hemorrhagic necrosis and acute inflammation; cholelithiasis present. No evidence of malignancy.

Treatment

Emergency laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed. The key procedural steps were as follows:

Exploration

A 10-mm infraumbilical trocar and two additional 5-mm ports were placed. The gallbladder appeared markedly distended, dark purplish, and was suspended from the liver bed by a thin, elongated mesentery.

Identification of torsion

The cystic duct and vascular pedicle were found to be twisted 360° clockwise (Fig. 2A).

Detorsion and assessment

The gallbladder was carefully rotated counterclockwise to restore anatomical alignment. No recovery of blood flow to the gallbladder wall was observed after detorsion, confirming established ischemia (Fig. 2B).

Dissection and ligation

The cystic artery and cystic duct were meticulously dissected, doubly clipped with non-absorbable polymer clips, and transected.

Specimen retrieval

The gangrenous gallbladder was placed into a retrieval bag and extracted through the umbilical port. Purulent bile was aspirated and sent for culture.

Outcome and follow-up

Postoperative course

The patient’s recovery was unremarkable. He was started on clear fluids on postoperative day 1 and advanced to a regular diet by day 2. Intraoperative bile culture later grew Escherichia coli, and targeted antibiotic therapy was completed. His abdominal pain resolved promptly after surgery. A timeline of the patient’s clinical course is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Discharge

He was discharged on postoperative day 4 in stable condition, with normal vital signs and laboratory parameters.

Follow-up

At the 1 month follow-up visit, the patient was asymptomatic and had resumed all normal activities. Physical examination and routine blood tests were within normal limits.

Histopathological findings

The resected specimen measured 9.5 × 4.0 × 3.5 cm. The serosal surface was congested and fibrinous. Cross-section revealed a necrotic, hemorrhagic wall and multiple mixed cholesterol stones (Fig. 4A). Histology confirmed transmural necrosis, acute inflammatory infiltrate, and vascular congestion, consistent with gangrenous cholecystitis secondary to torsion (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

Pathophysiology and anatomical predisposition

Gallbladder torsion is a rare but severe surgical emergency caused by rotation of the gallbladder along the axis of the cystic duct and cystic artery, typically due to an excessively long or short mesentery [5]. Although its precise etiology remains unclear, three major mechanisms are widely recognized. First, torsion may result from congenital anatomical variations such as a ‘floating gallbladder’, in which the organ is attached to the liver only by the cystic duct and neck. This configuration increases gallbladder mobility and predisposes the gallbladder to twisting under external forces. Second, decreased visceral fat, altered gastrointestinal motility (colonic, duodenal, or gastric), and spinal kyphosis may contribute to this condition. In older individuals with hunchback deformities or visceral ptosis, organ atrophy and weakened perivisceral support can lead to gallbladder descent and increased susceptibility to torsion [6]. Third, torsion may be triggered by abrupt changes in body position or intense visceral peristalsis, especially when combined with gallbladder enlargement caused by large stones or bile stasis [7]. In the present case, several contributing factors—prior small bowel resection and hernia repair, altered intra-abdominal pressure, reduced visceral fat, and a short gallbladder mesentery—collectively predisposed the patient to torsion.

Study design & reporting guidelines

This study comprises a retrospective case report compliant with the SCARE 2023 criteria, alongside a structured narrative review. Since GT is extremely rare and the existing literature consists almost entirely of individual case reports, we opted for a narrative synthesis rather than a quantitative meta-analysis. The term “structured narrative review” denotes our systematic approach to literature retrieval and data extraction without the formal risk of bias assessments or multi-database searches typical of a full systematic review. Literature search and data extraction were conducted following the applicable PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A search of the PubMed database was performed using the following strategy: (‘gallbladder‘[Title/Abstract]) AND (‘torsion‘[Title/Abstract] OR ‘volvulus‘[Title/Abstract] OR ‘strangulation‘[Title/Abstract]) AND (“1989/01/01“[PDAT] : “2025/12/31“[PDAT]). Two researchers independently conducted the study screening and data extraction, with any disagreements resolved through discussion with a third senior author. Articles containing ‘torsion’ or ‘strangulation’ were cross-referenced with those containing ‘gallbladder’, and additional case reports were identified by screening the reference lists of the retrieved studies. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Clinical case reports or case series of histologically or intraoperatively confirmed gallbladder torsion (GT); (2) Detailed clinical, imaging, surgical, and follow-up data available; (3) Published between 1989 and 2025. Exclusion criteria: (1) Reviews, letters, editorials, conference abstracts; (2) Animal experiments; (3) Non-English articles without reliable translation; (4) Studies lacking key clinical or surgical data. All other data for all patients were analyzed (Fig. 5).

After rigorous screening, 180 case reports met the inclusion criteria (Table 1). The median patient age was 79 years (range, 2-100 years), and the detailed age distribution is presented in Fig. 6. Of the included patients, 133 were female and 47 were male, yielding a female-to-male ratio of 2.8:1. Interestingly, gallstones were identified in 39 patients (21.6%). With respect to the torsion direction, 66 patients (36.7%) exhibited clockwise rotation, 61 (33.8%) showed counterclockwise rotation, and 53 (29.5%) had an undetermined direction. Complete torsion was present in 159 cases (88.3%), with no significant correlation found between the torsion direction and completeness. Furthermore, the majority of complete torsion cases in our review presented with established ischemia or transmural necrosis, underscoring the rapid clinical progression of this subtype.

Limitations

Several limitations of this structured narrative review and case report must be acknowledged. First, this review is based exclusively on published case reports and small case series, which are subject to inherent publication bias, as cases with atypical presentations or unfavorable outcomes may be under-reported in the literature. This limits our ability to estimate the true population incidence of GT. Consequently, the descriptive statistics and percentages calculated herein reflect only the characteristics of this specific published cohort and cannot be extrapolated as true population-based epidemiological estimates. Second, our literature search was limited to the PubMed database and English-language full-text articles, which may introduce selection and language bias, potentially missing eligible studies published in other databases or non-English languages. Third, there was inherent heterogeneity in the reporting of clinical data across included case reports, particularly regarding preoperative imaging findings and long-term follow-up data, which limited our ability to perform more detailed subgroup analyses for some variables. Fourth, the quality of included case reports varied, as is inherent to retrospective observational data, which may affect the reliability of some extracted variables. Finally, some of the pathophysiological hypotheses discussed are based on limited preclinical and anecdotal clinical evidence, and require further validation in large, prospective studies.

Risk factors in older adults

Previous studies have shown that gallbladder torsion occurs most frequently in older women, and is usually acalculous. Key predisposing factors in older adults include hepatic atrophy, reduced intra-abdominal fat, and degenerative laxity of the gallbladder mesentery or hepatic bed attachment, which further increase gallbladder mobility and torsion risk [8]. Age-related reductions in connective tissue elasticity and weakened mesenteric support further increase the likelihood of torsion during postural changes or gastrointestinal motility [9].

Estrogen and pregnancy-related hormones are known to modulate collagen metabolism and promote general connective tissue laxity [10, 11]. Extrapolating from these physiological mechanisms, cumulative remodeling of abdominal support structures following repeated pregnancies is frequently proposed to explain the female predominance of GT. Although sporadic cases during pregnancy provide anecdotal support [12], robust epidemiological data linking these specific hormonal changes to GT are currently lacking. Consequently, the higher incidence in older women is likely attributable to anatomical and degenerative predispositions rather than isolated hormonal effects.

Although gallstone-associated torsion is relatively uncommon, gallstones may contribute to torsion through several mechanisms [13]. First, gallstones increase the weight and tension of the gallbladder—particularly when stones are large or numerous—which enhances oscillatory movement during postural changes, gastrointestinal peristalsis, or fluctuations in intra-abdominal pressure. This increased mobility predisposes the suspended gallbladder to tilt and axial rotation. Consistent with this, many case reports of calculous torsion describe the coexistence of gallstones and necrotic gallbladder wall tissue at surgery [14]. Second, chronic irritation from gallstones may lead to gallbladder wall thickening and remodeling of connective tissue within the mesentery or cystic neck, reducing structural stability [15]. Additionally, stones can cause asymmetric gallbladder distension or deformation, shifting the axis of rotation and further increasing torsion susceptibility [16, 17].

The lower incidence of GT in males may be attributed to a lower prevalence of anatomical predispositions such as a floating gallbladder or lax mesentery [18], as well as a higher volume of intra-abdominal fat, which provides additional structural stabilization of the gallbladder and counteracts torsional forces [19–21]. Calculous GT is particularly rare in males, likely due to the need for stronger mechanical triggers to induce rotation in the presence of gallstones, though this hypothesis requires further validation [22].

In our patient, a combination of established risk factors likely facilitated the torsion. His thin habitus and reduced visceral fat provided the anatomical basis, while his long-standing history of cholelithiasis served as the mechanical trigger, a condition potentially exacerbated by altered gastrointestinal motility following his prior bowel resection [23]. Chronic irritation from gallstones led to gallbladder wall thickening, and the weight of the stones further decreased the organ’s resistance to rotational forces.

Clinically, GT typically presents as persistent biliary colic closely resembling acute cholecystitis. Symptom severity generally correlates with the degree of rotation: incomplete torsion (≤ 180°) often results from transient fluctuations in intra-abdominal pressure and may cause intermittent, milder symptoms [24, 25]. In contrast, complete torsion (> 180°) produces fixed rotation of the vascular pedicle, leading to rapid ischemia and necrosis [14, 26]. While the classic triad of vomiting, sudden abdominal pain, and a palpable RUQ mass is often cited [27], a key clinical red flag is the failure of conservative therapy. Worsening pain despite appropriate antibiotic and antispasmodic treatment strongly indicates that the underlying pathology is mechanical rather than purely inflammatory.

GT may occur in either a clockwise or a counterclockwise direction. While some authors propose that the direction of torsion is influenced by gastrointestinal peristalsis [28], mesentery deviation [29], or the shifting center of gravity caused by gallstones [30, 31], these theories lack robust validation. Ultimately, the orientation is likely shaped by unpredictable anatomical variations and dynamic interactions among adjacent organs.

Diagnostic challenges and imaging indicators

The diagnosis of gallbladder torsion typically relies on ultrasound and CT imaging; however, most cases are identified only during surgery. Several factors contribute to the diagnostic challenge. First, the rarity of the condition results in a low clinical suspicion. Because gallbladder torsion is an exceptionally uncommon cause of acute abdomen, clinicians and radiologists often lack experience. Imaging findings such as gallbladder distension or wall thickening in older patients with sudden right upper quadrant pain, are frequently attributed to acute cholecystitis or gallstone obstruction, leading to missed diagnoses. Reported preoperative misdiagnosis rates can be as high as 90% [14, 32].

Second, imaging findings are nonspecific and may overlap with those of acute cholecystitis. Features such as pericholecystic fluid or wall edema appear under both conditions. Even more specific signs—including the “floating gallbladder” and ‘beak sign’—are present in only 30–40% of cases and can be obscured by bowel gas, fluid accumulation, or limited visualization in thin patients with low visceral fat [5]. Third, the anatomic complexity of the cystic duct–vascular axis limits imaging accuracy. Ultrasound and CT have poor sensitivity for detecting torsion of the cystic duct, distortion of the vascular pedicle, and mesenteric twist. Advanced imaging techniques, such as 3D reconstruction or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are rarely feasible in emergency settings. Prior abdominal surgery may also produce adhesions that distort the anatomy and further mask torsion-related findings.

Data from our review of 180 cases highlight why gallbladder torsion (GT) remains a challenging preoperative diagnosis. A correct diagnosis was made before surgery in only 23.9% of cases, meaning over three-quarters of patients (76.1%) were initially misdiagnosed, most commonly as standard acute cholecystitis (Table 1). This is largely driven by overlapping clinical presentations. While right upper quadrant tenderness was universal (100%), the classic palpable, mobile mass was documented in less than half of the patients (49.5%; Table 1). Furthermore, our analysis confirms a strong somatic archetype: thin patients accounted for 57.8% of cases, whereas obese patients made up only 5.0% (Table 1). In thin older individuals, the marked loss of visceral fat pads allows the gallbladder to descend from its liver bed, increasing mobility and creating a mechanical environment highly prone to axial twisting. Clinicians should not rely on textbook triads; instead, a high index of suspicion is required whenever a thin older patient presents with an acute onset of RUQ pain that fails to respond to conventional management.

Given these diagnostic challenges, gallbladder torsion evaluation should follow a stepwise approach, including initial screening, precise differentiation, and intraoperative confirmation. Optimal imaging selection enhances diagnostic efficiency. As a foundational emergency examination, ultrasound rapidly assesses gallbladder size, wall thickness, and stone presence while evaluating wall perfusion via blood flow signals. Findings such as wall thickening (> 3 mm), surrounding fluid, and reduced blood flow warrant the suspicion of torsion. However, this modality is susceptible to intestinal gas and body shape, limiting its utility to preliminary screening [14]. Abdominal CT (plain scan + contrast-enhanced) serves as a crucial supplement to ultrasound, clearly displaying specific findings such as a ‘floating gallbladder’ and the ‘beak sign’ at the gallbladder neck [33]. Contrast-enhanced scanning further identifies ischemic areas in the gallbladder wall and excludes other acute abdominal conditions like liver abscesses or bile duct stones. For suspected cases, thin-slice scanning of the gallbladder region with coronal reconstruction is recommended to more clearly visualize the torsion relationship between the cystic duct and artery, thus enhancing diagnostic accuracy. When ultrasound and CT remain inconclusive, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) serves as a precise differential diagnostic tool [34]. It clearly delineates the anatomical structures of the cystic duct and common bile duct, confirming torsion or obstruction. This method is particularly suitable for radiation-sensitive patients. However, owing to its extended examination time, its use in emergency patients requires careful consideration of the urgency of their condition. For older patients with sudden severe right upper quadrant pain, poor response to conservative treatment, and inconclusive imaging findings, prompt surgical exploration is required to confirm the diagnosis and avoid disease progression.

Management and surgical intervention

Management of GT primarily relies on surgical intervention, supported by symptomatic therapy tailored to the patient’s overall condition. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the treatment of choice in most cases because of its minimally invasive nature and rapid postoperative recovery [9].

The management trends and outcomes from this expanded cohort reveal a clear relationship between diagnostic delay and tissue ischemia. Surgical intervention occurred more than 24 h after symptom onset in 60.6% of patients. Notably, this delayed intervention paralleled the 92.7% rate of established ischemia or transmural necrosis observed intraoperatively (Table 1). Although pooled case data preclude establishing definitive causality, this strong descriptive association highlights the unforgiving timeline of GT. Unlike routine calculous cholecystitis, where inflammatory changes may fluctuate, prolonged axial rotation provides a direct mechanical basis for vascular strangulation and rapid gangrenous progression. Regarding surgical management, open laparotomy historically remains the most frequent approach (63.9%). However, laparoscopic cholecystectomy was successfully utilized in 31.6% of cases, with a conversion rate of just 1.1% (Table 1). These data suggest that despite advanced age, severe gangrenous changes, and multiple comorbidities, a minimally invasive approach is highly feasible in experienced hands and provides a safe, definitive treatment pathway for this vulnerable patient population.

Intraoperatively, the direction of torsion can be visualized and reduced, allowing assessment of gallbladder perfusion. Although preservation may be considered if the gallbladder appears viable, cholecystectomy is generally recommended to prevent recurrence [35]. Conversion to open surgery may be required in the presence of gallbladder necrosis or extensive intra-abdominal adhesions [36]. Conservative treatment is reserved for rare cases of very mild incomplete torsion in patients who are unable to tolerate surgery. Management consists of antibiotics, analgesics, antispasmodics, and gastrointestinal decompression. However, close monitoring is essential, and surgical intervention should be pursued promptly if the symptoms progress [37]. Postoperative care includes continued anti-infective therapy, dietary transition guidance, and regular follow-up. Patients who retain their gallbladder require extended follow-up. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is not recommended as standard treatment for GT; it may only be used as a temporary bridging decompression measure in critically ill patients unable to tolerate general anesthesia, with definitive surgical cholecystectomy required once the patient’s condition stabilizes [38].

Conclusion

Gallbladder torsion, a rare yet critical acute abdominal emergency, requires vigilant recognition and prompt intervention. It should be strongly suspected in older patients presenting with sudden right upper quadrant pain, marked gallbladder enlargement, a poor response to initial conservative therapy, and imaging findings such as a ‘floating gallbladder’ or ‘beak sign’, even in the presence of gallstones. When imaging findings are inconclusive in older patients, delayed intervention increases the risk of severe morbidity. Early laparoscopic exploration offers decisive diagnostic and therapeutic value, serving as a definitive intervention to prevent gallbladder gangrene and perforation in vulnerable multimorbid populations. This case highlights the synergistic role of gallstones, mesenteric laxity, and history of abdominal surgery, providing supplementary evidence for identifying such atypical patients. Heightened vigilance and prompt surgical intervention are key to preventing gangrene and reducing mortality in this vulnerable population.
