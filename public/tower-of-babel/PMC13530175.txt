Research on a high-reliability-guided hierarchical dynamic optimal scheduling method for photovoltaic systems
Authors: Xiaohan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In hierarchical scheduling of active distribution networks (ADNs), conventional methods often struggle to maintain the consistency of nodal power fluctuation characteristics while enforcing hierarchical forecast coherence. To address this issue, this paper proposes a high-reliability-guided hierarchical dynamic optimal scheduling method for photovoltaic (PV) power. First, Crossformer is employed to construct a nodal power forecasting model, and frequency-domain features are introduced to construct a forecast reliability proxy. On this basis, a dynamic hybrid reconciliation strategy is designed, which adaptively switches between bottom-up aggregation and top-down decomposition according to the reliability assessment results. In the aggregation path, high-reliability nodes are used to correct forecast deviations. In the decomposition path, a constraint-based neural network, namely ConsNN, is introduced to reconstruct nodal forecasts while strictly satisfying the aggregation-consistency constraint between system-level and node-level forecasts. Simulation results on the IEEE 33-bus system show that the proposed method can effectively balance system security and scheduling efficiency.

Introduction

Under the background of the global energy transition, renewable energy sources represented by photovoltaic (PV) generation are being integrated into active distribution networks (ADNs) on a large scale. The uncertainty of PV output and forecasting errors introduce additional challenges to the day-ahead scheduling and operation of active distribution networks (ADNs)1. Hierarchical PV forecasting requires forecasts at both aggregate and disaggregate levels. Forecast reconciliation is then used to enforce aggregation consistency, so that lower-level PV forecasts are coherent with the corresponding system-level forecast2–4. However, simple top-down and proportional allocation strategies usually enforce aggregate consistency through fixed allocation rules, which may overlook the heterogeneous output characteristics of distributed PV nodes. Simple top-down or proportional allocation methods usually rely on fixed allocation ratios and may therefore fail to fully capture heterogeneous node-level PV variability and cross-node dependence2,3. Therefore, developing a hierarchical PV power forecasting method that can dynamically coordinate multi-level forecasting information while considering both nodal forecasting accuracy and physical constraints is of great significance for improving renewable energy accommodation and ensuring grid security5,6.

In this paper, traditional hierarchical methods mainly refer to bottom-up aggregation (BU), top-down decomposition (TD), proportional allocation, and statistical forecast reconciliation methods such as ordinary least squares (OLS) and minimum trace (MinT). BU methods obtain the aggregate forecast by summing lower-level forecasts, while TD methods first generate the aggregate forecast and then distribute it to lower-level nodes according to predefined proportions or allocation rules. OLS and MinT reconciliation methods adjust base forecasts through linear reconciliation mappings to obtain coherent forecasts across hierarchical levels. These methods provide useful baselines for hierarchical forecasting, but they usually rely on fixed reconciliation structures or static allocation relationships, and therefore may be insufficient when the relative reliability of system-level and node-level PV forecasts changes over time.

Among them, bottom-up aggregation can fully preserve the independent characteristics of each node. Reference7 focuses on refined node-level management to minimize operating costs, while8 constructs a bottom-up energy coordination mechanism based on underlying consensus from the perspective of network architecture, effectively addressing data interaction and trust among transmission and distribution network nodes. However, this simple superposition often ignores the error accumulation effect between hierarchical levels, making it difficult to satisfy system consistency requirements9,10. In contrast, top-down decomposition gives priority to maintaining aggregate-level consistency by distributing the system-level forecast to lower-level nodes. Reference11 considers system nonstationarity and multi-scale chaotic characteristics in decomposition-based forecasting. However, when top-down methods rely on fixed proportional allocation, the resulting lower-level forecasts may fail to preserve the heterogeneous output characteristics of distributed nodes12.

Compared with traditional empirical strategies that rely mainly on single-level information for unidirectional transmission, statistical forecast reconciliation methods, such as ordinary least squares (OLS)3 and minimum trace (MinT)4, construct reconciliation mappings to adjust base forecasts across hierarchical levels. These methods improve forecast coherence by projecting or transforming the base forecasts into an aggregation-consistent forecast space, while MinT further accounts for the forecast-error covariance structure through trace minimization. Reference13 further extends the perspective to physical coordination and, for flexible resources such as 5G base stations and electric vehicles, establishes a closed-loop control strategy integrating bottom-up assessment and top-down dispatch, achieving higher accuracy than a single aggregation or decomposition strategy. However, such methods usually adopt fixed coordination paths and ignore the dynamic changes in forecast reliability at different hierarchical levels under different operating conditions, making it difficult to effectively balance accuracy and constraints14–16. In addition, although purely data-driven neural network methods can significantly improve nonlinear fitting capability, their black-box nature may cause forecasting results to violate hierarchical aggregation consistency, which may further affect the feasibility of subsequent scheduling schemes in practical implementation17–19.

To address the above challenges, this paper proposes a hierarchical dynamic optimal scheduling method for distribution networks guided by high-reliability PV forecasting. The proposed method aims to overcome the conflict between the accuracy and constraints of multi-level forecasts in hierarchical scheduling of ADNs. It should be clarified that the primary methodological focus of this paper is hierarchical PV forecast reconciliation. The equality between the system-level forecast and the sum of node-level forecasts is treated as a forecast coherence or aggregation-consistency constraint, rather than as the real-time generation–load balance constraint in ADN operation. Operational dispatch balance and network feasibility involve controllable generation, energy storage, demand response, renewable curtailment, grid exchange, and power-flow constraints. In this study, these operational factors are considered in the subsequent scheduling simulation to evaluate the usefulness of the reconciled forecasts, but they are distinguished from the forecast coherence constraint addressed by the proposed reconciliation strategy. Framework of hierarchical dynamic optimal scheduling for PV power

The proposed framework mainly consists of three core components: baseline forecasting, reliability assessment, and dynamic reconciliation decision-making. Hierarchical dynamic optimal scheduling framework for PV power.

To meet the sub-hourly refined scheduling requirements of active distribution networks (ADNs), the time resolution of both power forecasting and optimal scheduling is set to 15 minutes in this paper. First, the input feature matrix of the forecasting model is constructed based on multi-source heterogeneous data. The specific input features include: 1) historical active-power time-series data of each PV node; and 2) local meteorological data collected by micro weather stations, mainly including strongly correlated weather conditions such as global horizontal irradiance and ambient temperature. On this basis, the Crossformer model is used to generate initial power forecasting baselines at the lower node level and the upper system level in parallel. Furthermore, a frequency-domain feature analysis module is introduced to quantify the predictability and forecast reliability proxies of forecasting results at each hierarchical level through the discrete Fourier transform. Based on this, a dynamic hybrid reconciliation mechanism is constructed to adaptively switch the reconciliation path according to the relative reliability between the upper and lower hierarchical levels.

When node-level reliability dominates, a neighborhood high-reliability-guided bottom-up aggregation strategy is adopted, in which spatiotemporal correlations are used to correct the deviations of low-confidence nodes. When system-level reliability dominates, the framework switches to a top-down decomposition strategy based on a constraint-based neural network, namely ConsNN, to reconstruct nodal forecasts under the premise of strictly satisfying the aggregation-consistency equality constraint. The framework aims to overcome the limitation of a single reconciliation direction through dynamic path optimization, thereby achieving coordinated optimality among distribution-network operating constraints, forecast coherence, and nodal accuracy.

Reliability-guided crossformer-based PV forecasting model

Construction of the crossformer-based hierarchical PV forecasting model

Crossformer embeds multivariate time series (MTS) into two-dimensional representations using a dimension segment-wise embedding (DSW) layer to preserve temporal and dimensional information. It then uses a two-stage attention (TSA) mechanism to capture cross-time and cross-dimension dependencies. Finally, a hierarchical encoder–decoder (HED) aggregates multi-scale information for forecasting.

Although the original Crossformer has strong spatiotemporal feature extraction capability, the reliability of its direct forecasting outputs still fluctuates dynamically under complex and variable meteorological conditions. Therefore, instead of blindly modifying its model structure, this paper introduces a forecast reliability assessment module based on frequency-domain analysis to quantify and support the original forecasting results of Crossformer.

Forecast reliability assessment

System predictability describes the extent to which the future state of a time series can be accurately forecast. We apply discrete Fourier analysis to forecast–observation pairs within a rolling calibration window. At each scheduling update, x(l) denotes the observed PV power sequence in the latest available window, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{x}(l)$$\end{document} denotes the corresponding forecast sequence generated by the trained Crossformer model for the same timestamps. Both sequences have length L. Thus, the reliability proxy is updated using historical forecast–observation pairs rather than unknown future observations.

The relative error is defined to evaluate the prediction accuracy of each component:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta \gamma (k)=\frac{\sum _{l=0}^{L-1}\left| X_{\Gamma }[k](l)-\hat{X}_{\Gamma }[k](l)\right| }{\sum _{l=0}^{L-1}\left| X_{\Gamma }[k](l)\right| }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \gamma (k)$$\end{document} measures the relative discrepancy of the k-th frequency component.

In practice, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \gamma (k)$$\end{document} decreases rapidly for low-frequency components, while higher-frequency components require more training epochs to converge. This reflects spectral bias in deep neural networks: low-frequency components are fitted first, and high-frequency components converge later. Because high-frequency components of PV sequences typically correspond to random noise, forcing the model to fit high-frequency information in late training can induce overfitting and reduce generalization.

Specifically, the core criterion for determining the cutoff frequency index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{cutoff}}$$\end{document} is to compare the difference between the reduction amounts of relative error for two adjacent frequency indices, k and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k{+}1$$\end{document}, over the training interval from epoch E to epoch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E{+}E_{\textrm{ITV}}$$\end{document}. Here, the reduction amount of the k-th frequency component is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \gamma (k)_E-\Delta \gamma (k)_{E+E_{\textrm{ITV}}}$$\end{document}. The frequency index that maximizes this difference indicates the new frequency boundary that the model has just started to fit over this interval, and is therefore defined as the cutoff frequency index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{cutoff}}$$\end{document}. In this way, informative components can be quantitatively distinguished from noise-like components.

Moreover, after determining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{cutoff}}$$\end{document}, an additional constraint is imposed on the reduction amount to enhance the logical consistency of the predictability assessment. Specifically, for frequency indices satisfying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k<k_{\textrm{cutoff}}$$\end{document}, the reduction amount \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \gamma (k)_E-\Delta \gamma (k)_{E+E_{\textrm{ITV}}}$$\end{document} is constrained to be positive so as to accurately retain effective low-frequency signals. In contrast, frequency components with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k>k_{\textrm{cutoff}}$$\end{document} are directly treated as unpredictable random noise and are no longer included in the final predictability assessment.

After obtaining the cutoff frequency index, we further normalize it into a DFT-based reliability proxy. For the system-level forecast and the forecast of node i, the corresponding cutoff frequency indices are denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{cutoff}}^{\textrm{sys}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{cutoff},i}$$\end{document}, respectively. A larger value indicates that more frequency components can be effectively captured by the forecasting model before overfitting, implying higher relative predictability of the corresponding PV sequence. Conversely, a smaller value indicates that only a narrower low-frequency range can be reliably captured, and the sequence contains stronger noise-like or rapidly varying components, leading to lower forecast reliability.

It should be noted that Ω and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega _i$$\end{document} are used as frequency-domain reliability proxies rather than direct physical reliability measurements. They are computed using forecast–observation pairs in a rolling calibration window and updated when new observation data become available. Therefore, the proposed reliability assessment does not require unknown future observations during online scheduling. For strongly non-stationary PV profiles, standard DFT may not fully characterize time-varying spectral patterns, and future work will further investigate sliding-window DFT, wavelet analysis, or adaptive time–frequency methods.

Predictability-oriented dynamic hybrid reconciliation strategy

The core challenge of hierarchical forecasting is to improve nodal accuracy while maintaining system-level coherence under aggregation-consistency constraints. Due to the stochastic variability of PV generation, the forecast reliability of the system-level and node-level models varies with operating conditions. Therefore, a fixed reconciliation path is often suboptimal. In this section, we develop a dynamic hybrid reconciliation strategy based on predictability-oriented reliability proxies. According to the relative reliability across hierarchical levels, the proposed strategy adaptively switches between bottom-up aggregation and top-down decomposition, and uses information from high-reliability levels to effectively correct the forecast deviations of low-reliability levels.

High-reliability-guided bottom-up aggregation

When the BU-Agg path is selected, the node-level forecasts are considered overall reliable. However, direct summation can accumulate instantaneous errors from a subset of nodes. We therefore introduce a high-reliability-guided correction mechanism that exploits the spatiotemporal correlations among nodes to correct low-reliability node forecasts before aggregation.

We define a dynamic threshold \varepsilon and identify the low-reliability node set11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S_L=\{\,i \mid \Omega _i < \bar{\Omega }_w-\varepsilon \,\}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{\Omega }_w$$\end{document} is the installed-capacity-weighted average node-level reliability and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega _i$$\end{document} is the reliability of node i. For each low-reliability node \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i\in S_L$$\end{document}, we select a set of nearby high-reliability neighbors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{H,i}$$\end{document} such that each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j\in S_{H,i}$$\end{document} satisfies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega _j>\bar{\Omega }_w$$\end{document} and is spatially close to node i. Building on the Crossformer model, we further establish a dynamic correction model to characterize the mutual influence between node i and its neighbor set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{H,i}$$\end{document}. Using the real-time outputs of high-reliability nodes, the model updates the initial forecast \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{P}_i$$\end{document} of node i and yields the corrected nodal forecast \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{P}_i$$\end{document}.

Finally, the reconciled system-level forecast is obtained by aggregating all nodal forecasts:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{P}_{\textrm{sys}}=\sum _{i\in S_L}\tilde{P}_i+\sum _{i\notin S_L}\hat{P}_i, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which maximizes the preservation of nodal fidelity while suppressing local bias via group-level high-reliability information.

Top-down decomposition with constraint-based neural network

When the TD-Dec path is selected, the system-level forecast \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{P}_{\textrm{sys}}$$\end{document} is regarded as a highly reliable baseline, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{P}_{\textrm{sys}}=\hat{P}_{\textrm{sys}}$$\end{document}. We then decompose \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{P}_{\textrm{sys}}$$\end{document} into nodal forecasts to obtain reconciled nodal predictions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{P}_i$$\end{document}. To strictly satisfy the forecast aggregation-consistency constraint while accounting for nodal accuracy and distribution-network operating constraints, we introduce a constraint-based neural network (ConsNN) to reconstruct the nodal forecasts.

ConsNN architecture and hard constraint embedding

ConsNN aims to learn an optimal decomposition strategy. To strictly satisfy the forecast aggregation-consistency equality constraint:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{i=1}^{N}\tilde{P}_i=\tilde{P}_{\textrm{sys}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This equality represents the coherence relationship between the reconciled node-level forecasts and the system-level forecast, rather than the operational generation–load balance constraint of the ADN. ConsNN designs a specific structure in the output layer to guarantee this constraint. The network body first learns the complex relationship between the input features and the nodal power allocation ratios. Here, on the basis of the original historical power sequences and meteorological condition data, the input features further incorporate the initial forecasts of the node-level model, so as to realize deep feature interaction among multi-level information. The output layer uses the Softmax function to generate a normalized allocation ratio vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\beta }=[\beta _1,\ldots ,\beta _N]$$\end{document}, ensuring that the sum of all ratios is equal to 1. Finally, the ratio vector is multiplied by the total system power \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{P}_{\textrm{sys}}$$\end{document} to obtain the final reconciled nodal forecasts:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{P}_i=\beta _i\,\tilde{P}_{\textrm{sys}},\quad \sum _{i=1}^{N}\beta _i=1. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This structural design ensures that the aggregation-consistency constraint is always satisfied regardless of how the network parameters change, thereby avoiding coherence violations that may be caused by the use of penalty terms.

Loss function combining reliability and physical constraints

ConsNN is trained by minimizing the total loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_T$$\end{document}. Since the aggregation-consistency constraint is enforced by the architecture in (14), the loss mainly consists of a reconstruction term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_r$$\end{document} and a physical-constraint term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_p$$\end{document}:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_T = L_r + \lambda L_p, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where λ is a balancing factor.

(1) Weighted reconstruction loss. The reconstruction term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_r$$\end{document} encourages the reconciled nodal forecasts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{P}_i$$\end{document} to stay close to the initial forecasts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{P}_i$$\end{document}. To incorporate reliability guidance, we weight the deviation by the nodal reliability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega _i$$\end{document}:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_r = \sum _{i=1}^{N} \Omega _i \left( \tilde{P}_i - \hat{P}_i\right) ^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which preferentially preserves information from high-reliability nodes while allowing larger adjustments for low-reliability nodes under the aggregation-consistency constraint imposed by the system-level forecast.

(2) Physical-constraint loss. The physical-constraint term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_p$$\end{document} penalizes decomposition solutions that violate distribution network operating constraints. Using an embedded power-flow model (e.g., a linearized DistFlow model), we compute line flows \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_l(\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}})$$\end{document} and nodal voltages \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_i(\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}})$$\end{document}, and impose line-capacity and voltage limits via:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} L_p&= \sum _{l\in \Gamma } \operatorname {ReLU}\!\left( \left| F_l(\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}})\right| -F_l^{\max }\right) + \sum _{i=1}^{N} \operatorname {ReLU}\!\left( \left| V_i(\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}})\right| -V_i^{\textrm{limit}}\right) , \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where Γ denotes the set of distribution lines; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_l^{\max }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_i^{\textrm{limit}}$$\end{document} are the line-capacity and nodal-voltage limits, respectively; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}=[\tilde{P}_1,\ldots ,\tilde{P}_N]^\top$$\end{document} is the reconciled nodal power-injection vector; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\operatorname {ReLU}(\cdot )$$\end{document} applies a penalty only when the corresponding constraint is violated.

By training ConsNN to minimize \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_T$$\end{document}, the model can achieve a reconciled nodal decomposition that jointly considers nodal accuracy, forecast aggregation consistency, and distribution-network operating constraints.

Combined with the performance comparison in Table 4, the influence of different coordination strategies on the system operating state is further analyzed from the perspective of physical mechanisms. The data show that, compared with BU-Agg, TD-Dec increases the renewable energy utilization rate by approximately 10.9%, but its network active power loss increases from 537.2 kW to 545.0 kW. The underlying mechanism is that TD-Dec gives priority to maintaining system-level forecast consistency and forces the lower-level distribution network to absorb the renewable energy output predicted at the aggregate level during the decomposition process. Although this improves the total accommodation amount at the macro level, strict boundary constraints force the total power to be allocated proportionally, which destroys the inherent output characteristics of distributed nodes and the voltage sensitivity of the local distribution network. Such physically unaware forced injection can easily cause local reverse power flow and reactive power circulation in the distribution network, thereby explaining why TD-Dec improves renewable energy utilization but fails to effectively reduce system network loss.

By contrast, the proposed Dyn-Mix method achieves the best performance in all indicators, with the lowest network loss of 490.7 kW and the highest renewable energy utilization rate of 89.4%. This mainly benefits from the effective coupling between the dynamic reliability mechanism and system operating behavior. With the reliability proxies extracted from frequency-domain features, Dyn-Mix can adaptively switch paths according to the fluctuation intensity of meteorological conditions and power output. When the weather system is stable, the bottom-level node model can more accurately characterize local features, and the system adopts the BU path to avoid the network-loss penalty caused by upper-level decomposition. When PV output fluctuates strongly due to cloud shading and similar conditions, the system-level forecast becomes more reliable because of the spatial smoothing effect. In this case, the TD path embedded with ConsNN physical constraints is adopted, and the physical-constraint loss function is used to avoid violation risks while enforcing forecast aggregation consistency, thereby effectively avoiding the passive state of “error averaging”. It should be noted that the performance improvement of Dyn-Mix has a certain scenario dependence. Its marginal benefit is most significant during high-load periods when PV output fluctuates sharply and the source-load mismatch is severe. In terms of scheduling cost, Dyn-Mix performs similarly to TD-Dec and is significantly better than BU-Agg. Although its computation time increases slightly to 35.18 s, it still fully satisfies the real-time requirements of minute-level scheduling in distribution networks.

In summary, the proposed dynamic hybrid reconciliation strategy performs well in key indicators such as voltage stability, network loss, renewable energy utilization rate, and scheduling cost, reflecting the dual improvement of grid security and economy brought by the technical optimization.

To support the engineering deployment of the proposed dynamic hybrid reconciliation strategy, namely Dyn-Mix, in practical active distribution networks, the system needs to establish a collaborative architecture covering measurement, communication, and multi-time-scale updating. In terms of measurement equipment, advanced micro phasor measurement units (μPMUs) should be deployed at distributed source-load nodes to collect real-time electrical quantities such as voltage and power at high frequency, and micro weather stations should be deployed in a gridded manner within the region to provide environmental data support for the multi-level forecasting model. In terms of communication architecture, since the strategy requires frequent data interaction between the bottom-up and top-down paths, a cloud–edge–terminal collaborative architecture combined with 5G network slicing and a dedicated optical-fiber network is recommended. Lightweight protocols such as IEC 61850 or MQTT can be used to strictly control the bidirectional communication delay within the hundred-millisecond level. In terms of update-interval configuration, considering that the single solution time of the proposed algorithm is approximately 35 s and that it has good online operation capability, practical deployment can set a rolling update of meteorological data and hierarchical forecast reliability every 15 min. The dispatch center can adaptively switch the coordination path and issue global optimization commands every 5–15 min. Combined with bottom-level second-level local control, an efficient closed loop integrating forecasting, communication, and physical regulation can finally be achieved.

Robustness and sensitivity analysis

To further verify the robustness of the proposed high-reliability-guided dynamic hybrid reconciliation strategy, namely Dyn-Mix, under complex and even extreme operating conditions, this section conducts an in-depth analysis from two aspects: high prediction-error disturbance, which is used to simulate extreme weather, and key-parameter sensitivity.

(1) Robustness analysis of prediction errors under extreme meteorological conditions.

In practical operation, the initial forecasts of PV and wind power often contain relatively large deviations. Therefore, in this case study, random Gaussian noise following a normal distribution is artificially injected into the original meteorological and power test sets. The noise levels are set to 10%, 20%, and 30%, respectively, to simulate extreme fluctuation conditions of different degrees. The system voltage deviation and network active power loss of BU-Agg, TD-Dec, and the proposed Dyn-Mix method are compared under different noise levels.Fig. 15Comparison of robustness of prediction errors for different methods.

Comparison of robustness of prediction errors for different methods.

The simulation results show that, as environmental noise and prediction errors increase, the performance indicators of all three methods deteriorate to varying degrees. However, because BU-Agg directly accumulates bottom-level errors, under the 30% noise level, some nodes experience serious voltage violations and the system network loss increases sharply. Although TD-Dec can maintain macro-level forecast consistency, local reconstruction errors are significantly amplified due to forced proportional decomposition.

By contrast, the proposed Dyn-Mix method exhibits strong disturbance resistance. Under the 30% noise level, the cumulative voltage-deviation metric of Dyn-Mix only slightly increases to 0.435 p.u., and no voltage-violation node occurs throughout the process. This mainly benefits from two aspects. First, the real-time reliability perception mechanism decisively switches to the TD path with the spatial smoothing effect under extreme fluctuations. Second, during bottom-level reconstruction, the introduced ConsNN uses a physical-constraint loss function combining soft and hard constraints to actively transfer error absorption to non-sensitive nodes, thereby maintaining the system security boundary even under extreme operating conditions.

(2) Sensitivity analysis of the switching-threshold parameter.

A switching threshold δ is introduced into the dynamic path adaptive selection mechanism to avoid strategy oscillation caused by frequent switching. To investigate the influence of this parameter on the scheduling results, δ is varied within the interval [0.01, 0.15], and the number of path switches and the daily average total scheduling cost of the system are observed.Fig. 16Sensitivity analysis of the switching threshold.

Sensitivity analysis of the switching threshold.

The analysis shows that when δ is too small, for example 0.01, the system becomes overly sensitive to slight differences in reliability between the upper and lower levels, resulting in excessively frequent switching between the bottom-up and top-down paths throughout the day. Such frequent changes in control commands not only increase the burden of edge computation and communication, but also cause power-command oscillations in inverters and energy storage devices, leading to an increase rather than a decrease in the actual scheduling cost. Conversely, when δ is too large, for example greater than 0.1, the algorithm can hardly satisfy the switching condition. In this case, the system easily falls into a single aggregation or decomposition state and degenerates into a traditional fixed-path method, thus failing to exploit the advantages of dynamic reconciliation.

The test results show that when δ is set within the interval [0.03, 0.06], the number of system path switches remains within a reasonable range, approximately 4–6 times per day, and both the daily average scheduling cost and network loss remain close to the minimum level. This demonstrates that the proposed algorithm has excellent parameter robustness within a reasonable parameter range and can achieve stable operation without strict parameter tuning for different typical days.

(3) Sensitivity analysis of the low-reliability threshold \varepsilon

To further investigate the influence of the low-reliability node identification threshold \varepsilon on the performance of the dynamic hybrid reconciliation strategy, a sensitivity test is conducted while keeping the other parameters unchanged. Therefore, the low-reliability threshold is set to \varepsilon =0.05 in this paper, which provides a good balance between avoiding over-correction and preventing missed identification of low-reliability nodes.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a high-reliability-guided hierarchical dynamic coordination forecasting method for PV power. Based on simulations and analysis, the following conclusions are obtained: Compared with traditional fixed-path methods, the proposed dynamic hybrid reconciliation strategy provides a feasible approach for handling the contradiction between system-level forecast coherence and node-level forecasting accuracy in hierarchical PV forecasting. Under the test conditions set in this paper, the strategy shows certain potential in coordinating objectives at different hierarchical levels.For the standard test case used in this paper, the results show that the proposed method has positive effects on reducing voltage deviation, increasing renewable energy utilization, and reducing daily network loss. During periods with severe voltage fluctuations and concentrated renewable energy output, compared with the top-down decomposition method, the voltage deviation in the test case is reduced by 1.98%, and the renewable energy utilization rate reaches 89.4%. This indicates that the proposed method has certain application value in considering both the security constraints and economic indicators of system operation.For the nodal power reconstruction problem, the introduced constraint-based neural network overcomes the defect that purely data-driven methods can easily violate the physical laws of the power grid. It ensures that the reconciled nodal forecasts strictly satisfy the forecast aggregation-consistency equality constraint while not violating line-capacity and nodal-voltage limits in the subsequent scheduling evaluation.
