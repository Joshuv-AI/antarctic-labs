Translating With Feeling: Centering Translator Perspectives within Translation Technologies
Daniel Chechelnitsky, Sireesh Gururaja, Seyi Olojo, Wesley Hanwen Deng, Giuseppe Attanasio, Chrysoula Zerva, Maarten Sap

Abstract
. Rapid development of Large Language Models (LLMs) and similar automated approaches for translation tasks is increasingly affecting the landscape of translation technologies. As concerns about the outsourcing of translator work to these automated translation tools grow, it becomes increasingly crucial to gather insights from the translation community directly. To this end, we conduct an interview study with 19 professional translators working across 11 languages and 11 domains to understand their perspectives, experiences, and concerns with using translation technologies in their work. We find that translators are cautious when incorporating new tools into their workflow, with several expressing concerns machine translation (MT) and LLMs are infringing on the necessary human aspects and verification steps of translation, worried that these tools have potential for harmful downstream effects due to compromising the human aspect of translation work. These findings demonstrate the need to develop translation technologies that directly serve translators’ needs rather than replacing human translation. This can be done by focusing more on the assistive, rather than the automating aspects of these tools.



1.
Introduction

Translation technologies
are tools that assist users with translation tasks
(
1
)
. However, as technologies like Computer Assisted Translation (CAT) tools and Large Language Models (LLMs) have become more widely used in professional translation tasks
(
45
;
47
)
, the question emerges of whether these tools introduce any biases. Given the ongoing rapid development and lower barriers for entry of these tools, translation is currently emerging as one area at risk of labor outsourcing, ultimately hindering professional translators from continuing work, similar to professional artists and musicians
(
23
;
10
)
. Furthermore, there is a significant possibility that by automating away translation tasks, human verification will no longer be a requirement of translation, leading to potentially disastrous downstream consequences in domains like medicine and law
(
36
;
12
)
.

Even the most advanced state-of-the-art CAT tools are still limited to hyper-specialized translation tasks
(
2
)
. This is due to how translation tasks vary in regards to source-target language pairing, domain, and intended audience.
However, one core issue is that translation goes beyond simple mapping of words in one language to the other; it is an inherently context-dependent task.
Many existing LLM-based machine translation approaches have over-simplified this task, leading to loss in quality and possibly even harms from biases when the source language does not have grammatical gender it introduces gender bias in the target language that does have grammatical gender
(
37
)
. Further, current CAT tools are often specialized for certain sub-tasks, for example, incorporating translation memories that prioritize lexical consistency and preciseness, whereas LLMs provide a more generalizable translation ability but show decreased performance and increased risk of hallucinations
(
30
;
48
;
39
)
.

In this work, we investigate professional translators’ experiences with and attitudes about translation technologies and their affordances, a first of its kind study, investigating the following general research threads:

•

The use-cases and affordances of current translation technologies.

•

The translator attitudes towards translation tools.

•

The decisions and stakeholders translators keep in mind when utilizing technologies into their workflow.

To address these points, we performed semi-structured interviews with 19 trained translation professionals across 11 domains and 11 languages.
Through semantic analysis, our results show that overall, translators in our study overwhelmingly encourage using both CAT and AI tools, with one important caveat: the tool must be used only as an addition to a professionally trained human for translating the specific task and domain (Section
6.1
).
This ensures that translators have access and can validate any errors the tools may have made.
Contrastingly, there is a growing distrust of LLMs and AI tools among professional translators due to ethical and personal reasons such as AI infringing on individual’s work (Section
6.2
).
Lastly, there is a growing concern as the translation technology landscape keeps changing, the role of a translator will be forced to adapt with the added risk of the creative and human role of a translator being automated away. So even with the exciting developments of AI tools, it is crucial to emphasize the required role of human translators within the translation technology.
The assistive and guiding nature of the tools should be prioritized over simply automating the entire translation process.
Building on these findings, we discuss these possible futures for the field of translation and what it means for translators’ career longevity (Section
6.3
).

2.
Background

In this section, we scope the problem within the context of translation technology, largely based on the human-centered machine translation tools described in the work of
25
. To do this, we describe terminology, provide concrete examples, and discuss current benefits and oversights for translation technologies and tools.

2.1.
The Current Landscape of Translation Tools

As stated above, we broadly define translation technologies as tools that assist users with translation tasks. These users might be lay or professional, and the types of tasks they perform with these technologies can vary significantly. Generally, however, we focus on three types of translation technology in this work, in ascending order of specificity. The first are general-purpose tools aimed at a broad audience, in which translation is one function of many. This category is exemplified by LLM-based systems such as (1) ChatGPT, (2) translation-specific tools like Google Translate or DeepL that are built for machine translation (MT), and (3) tools that are built for professional translators which constitute the category of computer-aided translation (CAT) tools. These tools are often designed to center the needs of large-scale translation projects, prioritizing translation consistency.

2.2.
History of Translation Technologies

The goal of translation aided or performed by computers has a long history. The archives of the European Association for Machine Translation, for instance, lists correspondence from as early as 1947 discussing the possibility of translating text by computer to aid with the mission of UNESCO
(
42
)
. Machine translation was also the primary subject of the infamous 1966 ALPAC report
(
3
)
, which found that the quality of both machine-only and post-edited translation insufficient, and thereby in many accounts led to the the first AI winter, in which funding for MT work in the U.S. was severely curtailed
(
22
)
. The report itself, however, notes that while full automatic translation was infeasible at the time, computer-aided translation merited further development, with two case studies of computer-aided translation in Europe whose function mirrors the translation memories of CAT tools today. MT has since recovered and retained a strong research community, leading to the development of foundational AI techniques like attention
(
5
)
and the transformer architecture
(
41
)
.

3.
Related Work

Here, we discuss ongoing parallel work in human-AI interaction, specifically emphasizing the expert experiences. Our work within the translation technology space similarly provides novel insights into the field.

3.1.
Characterizing Expert Work Processes

Prior to attempting to understand the role of AI in reshaping work practice, we first situate this research in the tradition that examines how experts conceptualize and perform that work.
7
, for instance, describe the work processes of professionals who produce audio descriptions of video content, and
20
characterize the processes of knowledge workers engaged in research in document corpora. Recent work from Human-Computer Interaction has also examined how machine translation is being used in high-stakes scenarios such as medical domains
(
11
;
27
;
35
)
, as well as providing guidelines for designing more
human-centered machine translation systems
(
25
;
35
)
. Studies like these further our understanding of the fields that new technologies seek to disrupt, and whether these technologies can in fact address the concrete needs of expert users.

3.2.
Expert Perceptions of AI Tools

As AI becomes ubiquitously available, it is important to understand how expert workers perceive the technology, and how may or may not assist with their work.
44
, for instance, find that while knowledge workers see some utility in AI tools, they largely see them as tools to "perform menial work, under human review", and view them as exacerbating existing trends like deskilling in their industries.

46
focus on when and why decision makers in organizations might choose to use AI or not, finding that AI tool use is often impacted by factors outside of its task performance, including the legal or reputational consequences for its use.
More specifically to translation, projects like Translators Against the Machine
(
29
)
and Blood in the Machine
(
28
)
have collected stories from translation professionals whose work has been impacted by translation technologies, showing that while translators’ opinions of AI as a technology vary, their perception of AI as an instrument of social change focuses on its use to de-value their work, with some translators finding it economically infeasible to continue in the profession.

Individual translators’ perceptions of AI as a harbinger of precarity also reflect concerns about the broader impacts of the technology. A U.K. survey of 787 members of trade organization for translators, for instance, found that 36% of them reported job losses from the use of AI
(
31
)
;
32
argue that the naïve use of primarily English-centric translation technologies reproduces colonial structures of harm.

We therefore orient our work towards understanding how translators approach their work, what they consider valuable in it, how machines may or may not be in conversation with those values, and the broader implications for both translators themselves and the users of translated language.

For this study, we conducted semi-structured interviews to help provide more nuance and acute understandings of technological ways that these translation technologies should be contextualized
(
20
;
32
)
. This was done in order to document more fine-grained details and perspectives of translators, often contextualized for each translator’s specific domain and languages. We first conduct our interviews, then perform three independent rounds of open coding to conduct thematic analysis to extract commonalities and broader themes from our interviews
(
8
)
.

Recruitment

To represent as many possible translators viewpoints as possible, recruiting was done more broadly, regardless of specific translation language pair or translation domain. With an IRB obtained from the institution of the first-author, recruitment of interviewees was conducted using convenience and snowball sampling stemming from the first author’s academic and personal network
(
13
;
17
)
. The first author additionally recruited through social media posts on LinkedIn, X, Bluesky, Facebook, and Reddit (see Appendix
A
). All participants had self-selected as translation experts prior to completing the consent script, and participants were recruited until experimental saturation was met
(
19
)
. Interviews lasted approximately 45 minutes, where upon completion the interviews were de-identified for any participant and interviewer personal information.

Participants

The translators covered a range of 11 different languages, and 11 different domains (see Table
1
and Table
2
). These interviewees represented translators who have been translating for over 60 years, to those who were relatively new and have only been translating after LLMs became widespread in 2022. These languages include high resourced languages like Spanish, Arabic, and Chinese, as well as lower-resourced languages like Swahili and Bengali. In total, 19 interviews were conducted by three of the authors between January and February 2026 over Zoom.

Qualitative Analysis Procedure

For our results, we wanted to extract commonalities of what the interviewees expressed, while also providing nuance across the translators’ specific domains and languages. Therefore to evaluate this, we used a inductive interpretivist approach
(
40
)
to synthesize our findings within existing literature for our research questions, as well as a grounded theory approach where we extracted themes from our on participant responses
(
9
)
. We do both of these in order to obtain rich descriptive information of how these tools are used
(
32
;
20
)
. The themes were drawn from codes which were obtained using a thematic analysis approach consisting of closed coding, open coding, and axial coding of the specific, in-vivo examples from the translation tasks described by the interviewees
(
8
)
. Specifically, three authors completed three rounds of coding:

R1

Closed Coding
: Each interview is coded individually by separate authors, with each author keeping an individual codebook.

R2

Open Coding
: The three authors who were closed coding met and discussed the common codes that emerged, and began merging existing codes. The final list included a codebook of 63 codes.

R3

Axial Coding
: Two of the three authors from the first two rounds and an additional author performed axial coding by sorting the codes into sub-themes and themes. This follows the approach in thematic analysis
(
8
)
. At the end, each research question was directly addressed using two to three themes.

In addition to these codes, the first-author wrote down major takeaways of each participant as personal memos. These, in tandem with the codes and themes, provided organization and structure for discussing the results. Our findings therefore incorporate both broader motifs, as well as rich context-specific details all based on the experiences of our interviewees.

5.
Findings

Overall,
based on our themes extracted from the qualitative open-coding
(
8
)
, we found translators have strong personal opinions of current translation. In this section, we first describe the translators themselves, the translation process and the human component of translation. We then discuss the tools they use, and translators incorporate the tools within their workflow; we also hear more broadly how the affordances of these tools impact the ways translators conduct their work, including what current limitations and restrictions they face. Finally, we discuss what this means for the translator profession as a whole, and how the recent development of AI tools is infringing on translators’ careers more broadly.

5.1.
Emergent Translator Profiles

The 19 translators interviewed spanned domain expertise in academic literary translation, to document translation for a translation agency in medical, legal, and design domains, to freelance work (Table
1
). For instance,
P6
describes how their approach to translation varies depending on which language pair they are translating for. They describe how for them certain maneuvers are highly language specific, like how for example it is generally bad grammar to have dangling particles when translating into Russian, but not for into English. Similarly,
P18
describes how in English you can stack nouns, but in Spanish you cannot. Different languages are structured differently and therefore a deeper linguistic expertise is often required beyond simply that of a fluent speaker.

Furthermore, translators who are completely fluent in both source and target languages often prefer translating in a specific direction, either due to expertise or training. For example,
P18
expressed how, as a non-fluent speaker of Spanish, they only feel comfortable translating Spanish into English and consider themselves not qualified to translate English into Spanish.
P6
and
P16
also echo similar sentiments, both explaining how the target language, being English and Arabic respectively, is more important to have a deep understanding for.

All translators, regardless of age, have concrete viewpoints on what translation ‘should’ be, and how it should be conducted. Whereas older translators might be more hesitant to use ML and AI tools, newer translators who still have a more flexible workflows are able to experiment and potentially incorporate newer translation technologies in their work. To more helpfully characterize these translator experiences, we identify three main translator demographics from the pool we interviewed:

•

Traditionals (P2, P6, P8, P13, P17, P19, P20):
Translators who became translators through academic or more traditional training. Preferred tools include dictionaries, pen and paper, and prior knowledge of a specific domain. Their work is also done more directly with clients.

•

Operationalists (P3, P4, P5, P7, P11, P14, P15, P18):
Individuals who still have a more traditional training for translation, often involving extensive schooling, but have used a wide variety of tools in their training including CAT tools, MT, and even more recently LLMs. Their work is typically in collaboration with other translators or as part of an agency or corporation. Similarly, their work is often bounded, quite strictly, to CAT or other tools specifically prepared for them.

•

Free Agents (P1, P9, P12, P16):
These translators have come to translation from a wide variety of domains, but are not working under a corporation or doing the job for anyone else except themselves. Typically have some sort of formal training via a degree or certificate, but are also willing to pick up new skills along the way. Often times they are online translators, or literary translators who work independently. The tools they use fully is due to individual preference.

Using these categories, we help communicate why certain translators tend to have certain preferences when it comes to using translation tools, going beyond just describing the translator’s individual language pair and translation domain.

5.1.1.
Translator Domains

Though all of our participants identified as translators, the actual tasks they performed as part of their work varied in both the primary intent of the translation, as well as the issues that these translators saw as being important to their work. Notably, the pertinent issues to work cut across domains: translators who worked with legal and medical languages, for instance, shared a deep concern for the consistency of their translation work with translators who worked on the localization of software user interfaces (
P13
,
P14
); translators working on marketing copy and literary translation both felt strongly about the effect their work had on the reader in addition to whether the meaning of the text was conveyed:
‘if you’re writing about meditation, it has to be meditative’
(quote from
P1
,
P6
). These differences show that while translation can be conceptualized in the way that machine translation tools often do: as words in a source language to words in a target language. The tasks that translators take on involve diverse goals that are prioritized based on domain.

5.2.
Translation as a Craft

5.2.1.
Translators want to do a good job.

Through our interviews, our participants communicated to us repeatedly the degree of care they exercised in translation in the pursuit of translation quality. Translators across domains and regardless of demographics often spoke about prioritizing the experience of the reader, in order to convey to them particular aspects of the text. Translators often balanced competing priorities in order to achieve what they saw as a high quality translation: sacrificing tone and register in legal documents
(P2)
, or syntax from grammatically different languages
(P6)
, for instance, in order to be legible at the expense of being entirely faithful. Similar to how different translation domains reflect different needs and communicative intents, the definition of quality varied from translator to translator, though the focus on achieving it did not.

5.2.2.
Translation requires preparation and cultural understanding.

A common practice among our participants across categories was a preparatory read-through of a text before beginning translation. P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P12, and P19 all described their process of familiarizing themselves with a text before beginning a translation, in order to understand the aspects of the text: technical terms or idiomatic constructions, the register and tone of the language, or even the "feel" of the language.

P19
states that research and background knowledge is necessary to consider author style when translating. They describe their process for doing research as such:

‘I will do some searching online to familiarize myself with the author, with their context. If there are any particularities about their own writing style that I should take into account while trying to translate the text.’

—P19, Traditional

P1
also notes that on top of familiarity with the text, it is important to know how it fits into the surrounding culture, and describes language and culture as inherently linked.
P20
agrees and adds the translator’s role in translating is not to simply facilitate translation, but to include context to more accurately engage with the culture, place, and time as important components to have in consideration before proceeding with a translation.

Finally,
P3
mentions that part of preparation for structured translation work requires knowing the audience.
P19
similarly echoes how for academic contexts this is critical because often times the language used is context specific, where one French word in one historic setting can mean a completely different thing in another.

5.2.3.
Translators translate feeling and style, not just words.

Broadly based on our interviews, more than just accuracy is needed to makes a ‘good’ translation. Ultimately, this answer does not have one definitive answer, as we saw among translator responses.

As mentioned previously, we did observe that translators want to do a good job (Section
5.2.1
), i.e., make a translation that is the best for the specific task that is comprehensive in the target language.
P7
describes how translating overall meaning is more important that simply translating word by word from English to French.
P6
puts it more deliberately that in literary translation ‘style should not be sacrificed for accuracy or idiomaticity’. This is in reference to certain authors like Dostoevsky or Dickens having a unique sound, a sound which can easily become distilled when translated.

Ultimately, translation is not just finding adjacent words in a target language. It requires a more comprehensive level of understanding so that deeper meanings, and nuances can be translated as well through more implicative linguistic and literary cues. As
P17
puts it:

‘You have to think about the feelings. You’re translating feelings, not just words. So that the people there can understand, if they’re gonna read it’

—P17, Traditional

This is the part of translation that draws many translators to translate for their specific domains.
P7
describes how what interested them in translation was ‘the challenge of mediating meaning across languages and cultures, rather than simply just transferring words.’
P18
similarly refers to this as the ‘fun’ part of translating, where the task, albeit difficult, can have many creative solutions.

5.2.4.
Translation is a creative process, requiring translators to make stylistic and artistic decisions.

As discussed in Section
5.2.2
, translation is not something to be taken lightly and without consideration for both the original texts, as well as the audience. Through these interviews we see how translation is a highly individualized task not just specific to domain, language pairing, and approach used
(
14
;
43
)
, but also personal styles, decisions, and approaches.

Given this, translators have a certain creative freedom of which they can use their expertise, frameworks and approaches. Still, translators are often faced with difficult decisions. One of these decisions is in finding the balance, or the tradeoff between idiomaticity and accuracy.
P4
describes how in Swahili, a highly idiomatic language, a lot of the sentences meaning if translated literally into English is lost, so they instead prioritize translating so the English audience can understand the intended meaning. In literature,
P17
describes how when working with an author it is similarly important to consider not just the best translation, but also consider what the author intent carefully.

P19 & P20
, both literary translators, shared similar sentiments that often times translating not only meant providing the text in the target language, but also potentially simplifying or adding cultural explanations to help the audience better understand the original material. Outside of literature and in prose, this is also a common issue.
P15
describes how in translating menus for an event based in the United States they often ran into situations where they had to make executive decisions in translating:

‘Hot Pocket, there was no equivalent in Chinese, and I didn’t know what it was until I searched for it. So somehow, I have to come up with either an explanation of what this is, or something similar to the item. So, I’ll have to make the decision. And sometimes I can’t really find a similar item in Chinese for a Hot Pocket, so I’ll just explain. This is a stuffed pastry with probably meat inside.’

— P15, Advertisement

These decisions are even more so evident in translating dialectal languages. For instance,
P1
describes how they had to make decisions for which dialect of Norwegian to translate into based on context, whether it is academic or more formal, or more informal and similar to ones found in advertisements. This is similarly expressed by those who translate into Arabic, Chinese, and French (P3, P11, P15, P16), also highly dialectal languages with a written ’Standard’
(
34
)
.

Ultimately, the role of a translator requires the ability to make thoughtful and careful decisions. Even with the rapid development of translation technology, several translators including
P5
feel that their role as the translator is critical:

‘I don’t see tools as replacing the translator, but more as supporting better decision making.’

— P5, Operationalist

5.2.5.
Translation expertise is acquired through time and experience.

Besides just having access to a translation tool, the tools themselves must be used in tandem with the experience and rationale of the person translating. Therefore, the key component here is not the tool but the translator does themself.

P17
describes that although ‘there are many people out there who say they’re translators’, there is potential room for error when the person doesn’t have the required experience. This emphasis on translators not only being qualified, but also practicing with care and attention to detail that one gains from experience. A translator’s reputation is inherently tied to their work. Therefore, there is a higher risk that a translator with a history of carelessness or lack of contextual awareness ‘will produce something that’s inferior’ (
P17
).
P18
puts a more positive spin on this, saying that a translator’s skill-set is valuable and that translation is inherently a difficult task, the precise reason it should be done by a human. They further add that ‘there are so many things we interact with daily that are translations that we don’t realize are translations’, emphasizing the benefits everyday speakers of language have from well done human translations.

5.3.
Current Landscape of Translation Technologies

To inquire about what translation tools are commonly used, we asked translators directly as part of our interview:
‘What is a translation technology?’
, where the responses to this question provided both scale as well as depth into the variety and specificity of different translation technologies currently being used.

Several participants listed several specific translation tools like dictionaries (P1, P6, P19), Google Translate (P1), MemoQ (P3, P4), and ChatGPT (P1, P7, P14). This breakdown between physical and non-physical tools is echoed by
P13
, who describes tools as ranging from physical mediums like the ones immediately available to them: ‘you may see dictionaries behind me [referring to bookshelves in background]’ to describing non-physical tools as ‘the compendium of software or platforms that allow you to get a translation that you can edit or customize for your own use’.

However, even these specific examples cover only a fraction of different ways of assisting in translating
(
26
)
.
P16
states more broadly:

‘Well, technology is as simple as paper and pen. And as sophisticated as large language models now, or translation memories, or software like Trados. So technology does encompass all of those layers…’

—P16, Free Agent

Since tools are used for different contexts and domains, the tools afford different outcomes.
P15
breaks down the different categories of tools:

•

CAT tools: (ex. MemoQ)
These are online datastores of previous translations, with instant lookup. These are useful for structured translation tasks where consistency matters.

•

MT tools: (ex. DeepL)
Translating models that are specifically for translation tasks. Mostly accurate, but limited to specific language pairings and domains.

•

AI tools: (ex. ChatGPT)
Helpful for not just translating, but also more high-level aspects (paraphrasing, rewording, etc.), also better at colloquial language than either CAT or MT tools.

This overall approach is echoed by
P19
who also adds ‘(web)sites that kind of crowdsource as well, like Linguee’, acknowledging the various types of databases and search tools that translators use for research.

5.3.1.
Translation tools do not translate, they assist in translation.

Translation technologies are not intended to offer an end all be all solution to translation, instead adhering to ‘aid in the task of translation’ (
P3
). They do so in various ways, with main emphasis on speed and efficiency.

For many translators like
P4
, speed and efficiency is one of the main reasons they use tools like LLMs, which they then organize and cross-reference with a CAT tool like MemoQ. CAT tools often have a translation memory component, but they can still differ in specific function. For example WordFast used by
P12
is a paid CAT tool with access to a larger database of translations, while others like SmartCat, used by
P15
, are free to use in a web-browser.

There is also a difference overall between effectiveness depending on tool type. For CAT tools, an efficient CAT tool was one that prioritized ‘consistency’ and ‘ease of use’ (
P18
).

P13
, who does legal translation, refers to CAT tools as a ‘clearinghouse’ of vocabulary, a collection of readily available terminology for a given use case. For translators that use ML and AI tools in addition to CAT tools like
P2
, they mention how CAT tools are often more restricted in usage and how AI tools like LLMs offer more creative ways to use them. For MT tools and AI tools, effectiveness is often attributed to speed, as already mentioned, as well as better capturing ‘tone’ and ‘context’ of longer texts (
P15
). Unlike CAT tools, MT and AI tools are often models trained on much larger data stores, and as such have the ability to generate a larger variety of outputs and process a wider variety of requests. According to
P2
, for AI tools like GPT this comes with both the good and the bad depending on context. For example
P14
found success in instructing LLMs to adapt the translation in specific ways:

‘I can just simply ask the LLM to: Hey, could you make it shorter? Could you like be more concise?’

—P14, Operationalist

Whereas others, like
P16
who tried using LLMs like ChatGPT for religious translations found the outputs to be unsatisfactory:

‘So, ChatGPT for me now is as bland as they come. The output is messy, sometimes inaccurate, and, at best, below average.’

—P16, Free Agent

Overall, we see the differences in tools and the affordances they lend can vary, but they never replace the translator or the translation task. While some, like LLMs, have come close, there are still user discernments about the output quality.

5.3.2.
LLMs compliment, but do not replace existing tools.

If translation tools have changed how translation works, it therefore follows that the newest development of AI translation tools will similarly alter the translation landscape.

However, what we see is that translators, even when adapting emerging technologies like AI tools into their workflow, are still prioritizing what is important for them: the human aspect. For instance,
P2
uses different LLMs like ChatGPT and Claude in tandem until they ‘converge’ to a ubiquitous answer. While this is not the intended use cases of either of these LLMs, P2 has creatively used them as a tool in a way that more directly benefits their use case. We see how similarly,
P7
uses LLMs that are present within certain CAT tools for starting point, but then proceeds to do quality checks and translation on top of it.
P17
, a longtime translator who has observed several ‘waves’ of translation tools, discusses how the introduction of AI tools poses a new, much bigger, obstacle for translators:

‘I mean, every generation does this so it’s like, yes, there are rules, but humans are still in control. And so that’s (AI tools) where you lose that. You lose that factor when you hand it over to some program that can’t necessarily make judgment calls on certain things.’

—P17, Traditional

P20
and
P4
express similar concerns, and as such only use LLM on small sentence fragments, and avoid using it on larger texts because they feel they do not have ‘as much control’.

However, translators have found workaround for various tools even before the introduction of LLMs.
P8
will default to translating by hand and only use tools when ‘necessary’, usually via an agency when collaborating with other translators. Others, like
P16
, come up with their own individual alternatives. Here, P16 simply keeps their own translation memory via an Excel spreadsheet instead of using a CAT tool. They describe this as more straight forward, as their context of translating literary texts in Arabic is very specific and CAT tools do not have the supporting structures to story elements in a organized way to their liking.

5.3.3.
LLMs as a search tool.

Recently, the development of LLMs has increased in both speed and accuracy for translating across many languages
(
38
)
. We see how for direct translation tasks, there are often mixed results. For example,
P11
describes their experience using LLMs to translate into Standard French as fair, but translating into Quebecois or colloquial French as inadequate.

However, when instead used simply as a search tool as opposed to a full translation tool, LLMs have shown to be quite useful for finding and incorporating cultural and linguistic information. For instance,
P8
found success in using Google’s AI Studio for finding new vocabulary, and
P20
found that for Sanskrit, Prakrit, and other older Indian languages LLMs are actually better than dictionaries for providing accurate definitions.

In addition to using an LLM as a search tool,
P2
finds it most helpful when LLMs also provide references with clickable links to specific sources.
P2
also recommends using multiple LLMs in parallel for searching to ‘maximize search breadth’. These experiences of using LLMs mostly for search tools instead of a full translation pipeline reflects similarly across all translators who have experience using LLMs for translation (P1, P4, P5, P7, P9, P11, P12, P14, 15).

5.4.
Tool & Translator Misalignments

5.4.1.
Translators prioritize their audience understanding, tools prioritize efficiency.

Throughout our interviews, there is a clear misalignment between goals of translator and translator tools. From our translators, based on the type of decisions they make (Section
5.2.4
), it is clear that they prioritize the
human
feedback component. However, tools often afford efficiency and time
5.3.1
. This major misalignment between translator and tool outcome goals results in friction when these differing end-goals transpire in tangential outcomes. This is usually evident in the translation quality from the translators’ perspectives.

P3
, when doing translation in the website UI design domain, described centering their client needs, and their audience, as one of the main goals for their translation. Similarly, P5, P6, P8, P12, P15, P17, and P18 all expressed similar sentiments of translating for an ‘audience’, and therefore would make translation decisions to prioritize and ensure the audience’s understanding. For instance,
P14
, who translates UX design interfaces, describes how when translating for a Gen Z audience in particular they would purposefully block, or ‘ban’ words from the available translations within their CAT tool to focus on language that demographic would typically use.

On the other hand,
P13
, who translates legal documents, often times ignoring the CAT tool that translates short phrases and simply going through existing translations or entire documents to see if it can be ‘recycled’, because in law consistency of language is important. In both these scenarios the tools merely are there as an aid, if need be. They are not required to be used in any given instance.

5.4.2.
Translation tools reduce communicative intent.

One commonality expressed by translators about tools is their inherent ability to dilute language. While CAT tools are by design implementing consistency, for example some agency specific CAT tools even have a AI tools like ChatGPT have a tendency to sound ‘bland’ and ‘messy’ (
P16
). Both
P2
and
P14
similarly agree that ChatGPT often is ‘too creative’ and is often adding words that are not necessary to be there. Even for current topics or slang,
P8
mentions how they often favor Google search instead of ChatGPT since ‘something that is trending on TikTok, YouTube, or something, is going to be easier to reach it, via Google than ChatGPT’.

Outside of AI tools and LLMs, these sentiments of translation technology outputs being mundane or bland are seen in CAT tools as well. For instance,
P14
, who works in UX translation, cannot use certain words that are ‘banned words’ in CAT tools used in their agency, because those words are not part of the agency’s brand voice. Similar restrictions exist in other CAT tools used by agencies
P15
where the tools must be specialized in order to match the tone of specific phrases and sayings used in product marketing.

Lastly,
P18
and
P20
both touch upon the chauvinism, specifically within an Anglo-centric context, that often protrudes into translation tools, and as a result, into the translations themselves.

P18
discusses how this reflects the translation profession as a while where you are more less likely to interact with translated content in English mediums:

‘I think that people… especially Anglophone people, are like: Oh, but that sullies the language somehow to translate it, that makes it worse… And I think it just makes it different.’

—P18, Operationalist

These are similarly reflected in the outputs of tools, like for instance when
P16
describes their experience using LLMs to translate Arabic into English as adequate, but translating from English into Arabic as subpar.

All of these are examples of how translation tools, while with intent to help, can actually compromise intended meaning and present obstacles for translators to have to address inaccuracies in their work.

5.4.3.
Translators do not trust AI tools.

Overall, LLMs and AI tools like ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and more have become popular methods for translation rivaling traditional online MT tools like Google Translate and DeepL. Many translators, like
P17
, feel that this is ‘overhyped’, and that these technologies have underlying harms that aren’t being openly discussed upfont.

The first of which is simply: translators do not know the underlying mechanisms for how LLMs work.
P1
comments that they do not use LLMs because they are scared of them and are worried they are collecting their data.
P13
, a legal translator, shares similar concerns and states that they will not use LLMs at any point with translation to avoid any legal confidentiality issues.

Similarly, other translators have already highly productive and established workflows and adding to it or updating it is more of a nuisance than an aid (P8, P6, P16, P19). Others are barred from using anything besides the designated CAT tools provided by their agencies (P4, P14), or are limited by access to internet and digital media (P8).

P15
describes that the main problem of LLMs, over tools like CAT, is that they have an ‘increased risk’ to be incorrect.
P3
and
P13
both similarly express encountering occurrences where LLMs have hallucinated or translated terms entirely incorrectly.

Still, others cite outside factors.
P18
mentions that they don’t feel comfortable using LLMs for ethical reasons, citing the harms LLMs are not environmentally friendly.
P17
even goes further to say that LLM outputs being used for translation can cause spread of misinformation, which if used by the wrong hands can have disaterous consequences if used in domains like law:

‘If AI makes a mistake, or DeepL makes a mistake, or Google makes a mistake, you can’t hold it accountable.‘

—P17, Traditional

If AI tools were to be utilized more broadly in translation tasks, this type of work would thus require more caution. Instead of translators only having to account for problems like preparation mention in
5.2.2
, they would also need to be constantly making sure their translation is not doing something unexpected.
P13
puts this concern as more inhibitory to their work:

‘Ultimately, you’re responsible for whatever you render [using AI tools] in that other language, so… you could use it to draft something, but it’s like… boiling something on the stove. You can’t turn your back to it.’

—P13, Traditional

This distrust of AI tools stems from valid translator concerns about lack of transparency and systemic push of these technologies onto them, without acknowledging or addressing translator needs. As these tools grow increasingly widespread, translators will likely grow more hesitant in using these tools.

5.4.4.
Translators work within complex labor arrangements.

Across our participants, the impacts of AI have been felt very differently, in part because those impacts are felt very differently based on the terms and power dynamics in their employment. Even in our sample, we heard directly from translators in a variety of arrangements, both freelance and in house; we also heard secondhand about other arrangements: the disappearing market for one-off translation of academic manuscripts into English in Egypt, for instance
(P16)
, or the subcontracting of in-house translation work to freelance agencies in the pursuit of scale
(P3)
. Freelance translators varied widely in the degree of power they were able to exercise in their work, with some (perhaps not coincidentally traditionalists with long careers) like
P6
and
P13
being able to refuse work that did not meet their standards, especially out of concern for their reputation; by contrast, even some in-house translators, like
P18
, felt the pressure from their employers to embrace AI:
“Their perspective is, this is the future, this is here, we need to use it so that we can continue to be relevant…couldn’t you go so much faster?”

This push to adopt AI was also a concern for
P3
, though in different form.
P3
is an in-house translator, but described how their company outsourced much of its work to freelance translators, and how recently, the agencies that they contract to have been submitting identifiably AI-generated text. They then described how LLMs were seen to "solve" the consistency problem created by a freelance, rather than in-house model:
”we don’t know who they’re using as freelancers. Sometimes we get, like, really good translations, and sometimes they would be really bad, and we couldn’t ask them to keep using the same, good freelancer. So, at least with the LLM, it’s consistent.”

Through our interviews, we saw repeatedly how AI translation was often framed as a necessity to keep pace with growing expectations, while translators themselves often saw it as being of an unacceptably low quality that required significant oversight, consistent with the findings in
44
.

6.
Discussion

Our findings illustrate how the work of translation is indelibly human. Participants discuss the importance of the familiarity with texts, notably for the translation of literary texts (Section
5.2.2
). Translation work is skill deeply rooted in continuous and iterative practices that define the unique stylistic approach of a translator. For many of the participants we interviewed, their stylistic approaches were refined over time and protected through a fidelity to the craft of translation. In translation, the process of communicating meaning is embodied through the perspective of the translator. Translatability, the ability to communicate meaning within a text, reflects the vision of the person completing the translation. Our participants purport, in alignment with the humanistic nature of translation work, that translation is a human-centered practice. Our findings trace how translators contend with the growing influence of translation tools. They highlight how the dual utility of translation technologies as powerful machines that can refine the skills needed to produce consistent translations while simultaneously challenging the control that translators have over their decision-making processes.

In this discussion section, we outline three key elements that illustrate how translation technologies transform the nature of translation work. First, we argue that the introduction of translation tools in translation work further highlights just how dependent translation work is on
craft
, the situated and tacit knowledge-based skills of translators. Secondly, we discuss how translation tools, if used incorrectly, contribute to the erasure of the craft of translation. This process decontextualizes translations, a key trademark of translation as described by our study participants. Finally, we discuss the friction that translation technologies present in the division of labor as it relates to the professional field of translation. Ultimately, translation technologies disrupt normative approaches to translation that for many are deeply personal, subjective, and until recently, avoided an essentialist understanding of what it means to translate.

6.1.
Translation Practice as Humanistic Craft

Translation practices can be seen as a type of creative skill that reflects the interpretive style of a translator. Our participants described their translation work as experiential, where judgment calls on providing the communicative meaning of translation relies on their creative interpretations. Recall
P17
describes how translation is not simply a practice of translating words but feelings as well:
‘your translating feelings, not just words’
. The importance of research on the context and domain of the translation illustrates just how influential and iterative translation work can be. Philosopher Walter Benjamin argued that the point translation is not replicate the meaning of the original text but rather instead it must
‘lovingly and in detail incorporate the original’s way of meaning’
in consequence creating a dyadic relationship between the original and the translation
(
6
)
. Translation is more than a replication of words from a source language to a target language. It is a stylistic practice that attends to the meaning behind those words while considering the linguistic features of the source language. Translators, in turn, consider the problem of translatability and creatively work towards alleviating it. Such judgement calls require them to describe concepts that cannot be readily defined in another language. For example,
P15
discussed improvising the description of a ‘Hot Pocket’ as it would be described in Chinese. Improvision and the will to find connotative meaning beyond literalness illustrate the humanistic nature of translation work, a feature of a translator’s expertise that was widely discussed in our study.

The perspective of translators is also deeply situated and can be described as co-produced from the lived-experiences of the translators. This kind of situated knowledge
(
21
)
is a key component of a human-centered translation process. Our participants recognized the work of contextualization as a creative practice that relies on the perspective of the translator. Furthermore, a ‘good’ translation requires creative interventions that have long been recognized by scholars in the field of translation studies
(
18
)
. The incorporation of translation tools into translation work offers a reminder of how the most important components of translation work require the subjectivity of human reasoning.

6.2.
Translation Tools and the Erasure of Craft

Participants discussed a distrust of translation tools. While some tools, namely LLMs, assisted in the creative practice of translators there was a general sense that translation tools could fundamentally change the nature of translation work. We see that translators position translation tools as assistive technologies as a way to exert control over when and how translation tools intervene in their work. Yet, technologies marketed as assistive are still able to hide the true human costs of work
(
15
)
. This not only poses the risk of decontextualization and but it can make opaque the decision-making behind how to translate. Given that so much of that decision-making relies on the judgement of the translator, the use of AI for translation communicates a devaluation of subjective human judgement within translation work. This poses an epistemological threat for translators who see their work as reliant on humanistic analysis. In 2025, the American Translators Association released a statement of the use of AI for translation. They state that while incorporating AI into translator workflows can assist in productivity they warn that without careful supervision AI can create mistranslations that would be difficult for lay users to recognize
(
4
)
. A lack of transparency regarding where data is sourced also threatens the quality of AI-mediated translation outputs. Within our own study we see a similar friction between translators and translation technologies. Participants’ distrust for the tools but forewarn a response to threats to agency on a larger scale. The rapid adoption of AI in translation work pressures professional translators to have to contend with what this means their employability. Additionally, they consider unmitigated AI as infringing on their craft. At a larger scale, unmitigated AI use for translation not only poses threats to professional translation jobs but it also asserts that the humanistic nature of translation can be replaced. This threat is both material and epistemological.

Nonetheless, we see moments of translator resistance, where strict boundaries for how translation technologies will be incorporated into workflows still reflect the importance of human expertise. Recall that participants consider using LLMs for more creative tasks as opposed to CAT tools. The critical use of translation technologies and the identification of the limitations of these tools, illustrate moments where translators assert their control over their workflow. Moments of refusal and redirecting by translators also illustrate the small yet intentional ways translators assert their agency and their power. Yet these individual acts of refusal are not enough to protect against the professional field of translation’s general adoption of translation tools. As we see in the findings, a top-down approach in agencies’ requesting the AI-assisted workflows depict how translation tools are seen as business solutions.

6.3.
Technologically-Mediated Divisions of Translation Work

Negotiations in how and where these technologies intervene within the translation process reveal the inherent definitions that translators have on what it means to translate. Although translation technologies assist with translation tasks, the decision-making process remains with translators. This retention of their agency over what it means to engage in translation work shows the tacit nature of translation work. Furthermore, translators learn about translation technologies to best situate them within their work while also maintaining a great deal of control over the creative process. Given that translation work often reflects the positionality of the translator, this study shows how professional translators seek to maintain the craft of their work despite using technologies to assist with their process. Yet, we see a transformation in the nature of translation work. We argue that contextualization and integration of translation technologies into translation work shifts the distribution of cognitive labor among translators as individual workers, but also highlights a distrust in translation technologies as a form of automation that makes the subjectivity of translation work fairly opaque. Technologies have long transformed the nature of work and created new dynamics of labor reorganization. Mathematician Charles Babbage theorized two ways in which technologies reorganize labor. We see in translators’ negotiations with translation technologies, how automation not only de-centers the influence of the worker but also centers macro-economic level influence on the division of labor
(
33
)
. Babbage’s labor theory of the machine states that machines replace manual labor as a way of eliminating human error. His principle of labor calculation argues that various types of labor can be distilled into tasks that can be quantified and tracked to minimize the costs of human labor. As a result, the workers become alienated from their labor. We observe a similar kind of labor alienation in this study, where the creative process and individual autonomy of translators come into question with the introduction of translation tools. Translators respond with significant distrust and emergent work practices that situate translation tools as secondary, mechanistic practices that should not threaten their own tacit knowledges. In this discussion section, we therefore discuss how translators’ commitment to protecting their tacit knowledge of translation practice reveals how translation technologies infringe on the epistemic authority of translators and how this raises anxieties for the translation field and the future of their work.

The confluence of pressure to adopt translation technologies and to become knowledgeable about how to use them not only changes the nature of translation work but it presents a conflict in how translators maintain close proximity to the intentionality of their work. We see this occur in ways that are beneficial in the immediacy of translators’ workflows but we see translators hold that in tandem with more existential fears that reflect uncertainty about the future of translation work within the era of generative AI.

6.4.
Limitations

This study by no means represents the viewpoints of all professional translators. Since we recruited with convenience and snowball sampling, our sample of participants was inherently scoped to those close to or adjacent to academia. Similarly, our pool of languages and domains covered was limited to those seen in Tables
2
and
1
. Any other languages or domains were not represented in these findings, especially those from low-resourced linguistic backgrounds. The 19 interviewees instead serve as a glimpse into what perspectives and conversations exist around translation technologies.

In this work, translators self-identified as professional translators. This is because there does not exist just one standardized ‘professional translator’ test or evaluation
(
16
;
24
)
. We determined if they were eligible simply by their own discretion, as well as their responses to the form questions ‘Yes’ to the question:‘Do you have translation certification (a degree, certificate, or other proof of translation training)?’ (see Appendix
B
).
