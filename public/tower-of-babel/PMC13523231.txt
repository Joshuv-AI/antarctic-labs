Incorporating dietary information to enhance polygenic prediction models with applications to body mass index and type 2 diabetes
Authors: Eunice Y Lee, Bryan L Dinh, Ji Tang, Samantha A Streicher, Xinran Wang, Subarna Biswas, He Tian, Xian Yu, Kekoa Taparra, Take Naseri, Satupa’itea Viali, Daniel E Weeks, Jenna C Carlson, Christopher A Haiman, Loïc Le Marchand, Gertraud Maskarinec, Lynne R Wilkens, Song-Yi Park, Charleston W K Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolygenic predictors can enhance screening for metabolism-related traits such as body mass index (BMI) and type 2 diabetes (T2D). However, these predictors explain limited phenotypic variance and face implementation challenges in non-European populations. Dietary patterns are well-established metabolic risk factors that remain under-investigated in quantitative risk stratification models.MethodsWe developed and evaluated risk stratification models combining polygenic predictors and data-driven dietary scores (DDS) for BMI and T2D in 14,346 Native Hawaiians from the Multiethnic Cohort (MEC-NH), a population with high prevalence of obesity and T2D. Using 5,374 participants with genetic data, we integrated publicly available large-scale GWAS summary statistics to develop cross-ancestry polygenic score (PGS) models using PRS-CSx. We developed DDS using machine learning algorithms on 520 dietary variables and evaluated model performance in held-out test sets.ResultsTrans-ancestry PGS achieved better prediction accuracy than single-ancestry models for both phenotypes (partial R² [SE] = 0.12 [0.04] vs. 0.03–0.09 for BMI; liability R² [SE] = 0.09 [0.04] vs. 0.01–0.07 for T2D). The best-performing DDS was based on a Random Forest model and substantially explained BMI variation (partial R² [SE] = 0.12 [0.01]), comparable to genetic prediction. Combined models integrating PGS and DDS significantly outperformed single-predictor models for BMI (adjusted R² = 0.29 vs. 0.21, P < 10⁻¹¹⁷). For T2D, combined models showed marginal but significant improvement over PGS alone. The BMI dietary score additionally associated with multiple chronic diseases, with effects partially mediated through inflammatory and lipid pathways.ConclusionTrans-ancestry polygenic scores and data-driven dietary scores provide complementary information for metabolic trait prediction. Combined genetic-dietary models significantly outperform single-predictor approaches, with improvement being most pronounced for BMI. In Native Hawaiians, systematic integration of dietary information substantially improved BMI prediction, demonstrating the value of incorporating modifiable environmental factors alongside genetic information.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12263-026-00811-1.Keywords: PGS, Diet score, T2D, BMI, Risk prediction model

Introduction

The excessive global burden of obesity (measured by body mass index, BMI) and risk of type-2 diabetes (T2D) necessitates the development of risk stratification models for clinical applications. In particular, BMI and T2D are two of the most widely studied conditions through genetic approaches. They are also connected to the broader set of metabolic syndrome and other co-occurring diseases (such as cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, potentially leading to premature death [1–3]). Currently, nearly 2.5 billion adults world-wide (43% of the world’s population) are overweight or obese [4]. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), obesity and T2D prevalence in both adults and children in the United States continue to increase and is projected to rise further [5, 6]. Early identification of risk factors can help prevent future incidence and reduce disease prevalence.

Both BMI and T2D have strong genetic components. The within-family heritability estimates, which are relatively free of bias due to shared environment, are 0.43 for T2D and 0.49 for BMI across major ethnic groups in the United States [7]. Hundreds to thousands of genetic risk variants discovered in recent large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of metabolic phenotypes across diverse populations demonstrate that these traits are polygenic. Rather than focusing on single genes or small gene sets, aggregating cumulative effects across the genome into a polygenic score (PGS) provides better risk prediction and stratification, particularly when implemented alongside other clinical factors [8–10]. However, PGS face transferability issues across populations because their performance depends heavily on the underlying GWAS, which have been predominantly conducted in European-ancestry individuals [11–13]. This results in low prediction accuracy for non-European populations, hindering clinical implementation and presenting an ongoing challenge [13–15].

Besides genetic contributions, multiple environmental factors can also contribute to the variation of BMI or risk of T2D, with diet as one of the primary contributors, among others. Research highlights that chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and adipose tissue dysfunction are key pathogenic mechanisms in T2D, and the larger spectrum of metabolic syndromes [16]. Unhealthy dietary habits such as consuming excess energy, sugar, saturated fats, and salt are major sources of low-density lipoprotein accumulation, promoting inflammation and altering glucose metabolism, thereby increasing T2D risk and atherosclerotic plaque formation that can lead to cardiovascular conditions [17–19]. A meta-analysis of 50 prospective and randomized controlled studies demonstrated that Mediterranean diet adherence reduced metabolic syndrome incidence by 50%, indicating that healthy dietary patterns such as Mediterranean, Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), vegetarian, or high-Healthy Eating Index diets can improve immune and cardiometabolic function while reducing chronic disease risk [20–22].

Similar to PGS, rather than examining individual food items or nutrients, risk stratification models could be developed to provide a composite score that were trained to illuminate the association of dietary patterns with specific traits or diseases. Such a model could be used to augment genetic risk stratification, at least when extensive dietary information is available, but has not been widely developed [23]. While dietary quality indices exist, they were developed to quantify overall dietary quality and pattern. These dietary indices tend to be developed based on specific dietary recommendations to capture the overall health and well-being, rather than focusing on any specific disease or conditions [24, 25]. Therefore, there is still a need for developing dietary models with respect to specific clinical outcomes.

In this study, we explored the role of genetic and non-genetic risk factors for BMI and T2D by developing PGS and data-driven diet scores (DDS) in Native Hawaiians, a population with high prevalence of obesity and T2D [26, 27]. Moreover, because there are limited GWAS data available in this population, polygenic predictions are more challenging. Therefore, it stands to reason that non-genetic information such as dietary information could be more pertinent in risk stratification in a clinical setting. For both PGS and DDS developments, we applied a data-driven approach. Unlike traditional methods such as factor and cluster analysis, our approach considers both linear and non-linear relationships without making arbitrary decisions about factor or cluster structure. Combining both genetic and dietary information, we focus on evaluating the gains in risk stratification of an integrative framework. While we developed this framework using BMI and T2D in a Native Hawaiian cohort, in principle our framework can be applied to other populations and/or traits and thus has significant implications for disease prevention and precision medicine in general [23, 28].

Methods

Overview of the methods, input data, and phenotypes for this study are described in Fig. 1.

Overview for constructing and evaluating trans-ethnic polygenic score (PGS) and diet score prediction models. The estimations of trans-ethnic PGS for BMI and T2D were based on the GWAS from three ethnic groups including East Asian (EAS, representing largely EAS-like ancestry), European (EUR, representing largely EUR-like ancestry), and Native Hawaiians (NH, representing largely Polynesian, or PNS-like, ancestry) using Bayesian approaches. In this case, MEC-NH individuals genotyped on MEGA array (N = 4,148) were used to train the PGS, while individuals genotyped on the GDA array (N = 1,226) were used as validation dataset. DDS was trained and validated for each phenotype using three different machine learning-based algorithms, with all individuals with survey data only (N = 8,372) used as training dataset, and individuals with survey and genetic data (N = 5,222) were used as validation dataset

Multiethnic cohort: study participants and dietary data

The Multiethnic Cohort (MEC) is a large-scale cohort designed to study diet and cancer and other chronic diseases among ethnic minorities in the United States. Study participants comprise 215,251 adult men and women aged 45 to 75 years at baseline who resided in Hawaii and California (primarily Los Angeles County). The cohort includes five ethnic groups: Japanese Americans (28.5%), Whites (24.8%), Latinos/Hispanic (22.7%), African Americans (16.8%), and Native Hawaiians (7.2%). Recruitment occurred from 1993 to 1996. Blood and/or oral samples were collected 5–10 years after cohort entry from which genetic data were assessed. The subset with genetic data was slightly younger, less likely to smoke, and slightly more educated, but otherwise similar to the subset who did not donate a biospecimen for genetics (Table 1). More information regarding the study design, questionnaire, and exclusion and inclusion criteria can be found elsewhere [29–31].

MEC individual labels and groupings are based on baseline self-reports from the early 1990s. The survey allowed participants to select multiple options: “Black or African-American”, “Chinese”, “Filipino”, “Hawaiian”, “Japanese (includes Okinawan)”, “Korean”, “Mexican or other Hispanic”, “White or Caucasian”, or “Other”. Most MEC analyses categorize participants into one of five targeted groups using a strict priority order: “African-American”, “Hawaiian”, “Latino”, “Japanese”, and “White”. Consequently, individuals reporting “Chinese” and “Hawaiian,” or “White” and “Hawaiian,” are classified as Native Hawaiian. For the 0.3% of participants with missing data, race/ethnicity was derived from parental information if available; otherwise, they were labeled “Other.” While these classifications encompass multiple layers of self-identity, nationality, and genetic ancestry, we retain them for consistency with prior MEC literature and to accurately contextualize historical health disparities, including the utility of polygenic risk predictions. We recognize these labels are not direct proxies for genetic ancestry or lifestyle. However, they are deeply substantive for the Native Hawaiian community that prioritizes self-identity and genealogical records over estimated genetic ancestry, and for individuals who encompass complex, multi-ancestry continua that are unethical to arbitrarily delineate. Thus, we define our participants as self-reported Native Hawaiians without imposing genetic ancestry thresholds. By convention, we append the suffix “-like” when referring to genetic ancestry (e.g., Polynesian-like [PNS-like] ancestry) and omit the suffix when referring to specific populations of individuals (e.g., Native Hawaiians, Samoans, MEC-NH).

Each MEC participant completed a self-administered 26-page questionnaire at baseline. A main portion of the questionnaire was a quantitative food frequency questionnaire (QFFQ) with more than 180 food items to assess habitual diet during the previous year [29]. For each food item, the QFFQ queried consumption frequency using eight categories (“never or hardly ever”, “once/month”, “2–3 times/month”, “once/week”, “2–3 times/week”, “4 to 6 times/week”, “”once/day”, “≥2 times/day”) and portion size (two or three serving size choices). A calibration study demonstrated a good validity of the QFFQ against three 24-hour dietary recalls based on correlations in nutrient estimates [30]. Based on the QFFQ responses, participants’ food and nutrient intakes were computed using comprehensive food composition database developed and maintained for the MEC, including a large recipe database [29]. MEC dietary data includes nutrients (absolute intake), individual foods (grams), food groups (grams), MyPyramid Equivalents Database (MPED), and dietary patterns (component scores and total scores), which resulted in a total of 520 diet variables that were used to train and compute DDS.

Definition of metabolic health phenotypes

Obesity/BMI

BMI (kg/m2) was calculated using self-reported height and weight at cohort entry for each participant. A sex-normalized BMI was used in this study. The raw BMI was first stratified by sex and regressed against age. The residuals of the sex stratified regression models were inverse normalized and combined across sexes for the analyses. This residualized and inverse-normalized BMI is referred to as BMI for brevity.

Type 2 diabetes

T2D status was ascertained from several major sources of information for the MEC. First, self-reports were available baseline and five follow-up questionnaires, in response to the question “Has your doctor ever told you that you had diabetes?” Note that MEC questionnaires do not differentiate type-1 vs. type-2 diabetes, but given the age of the participants at cohort entry (> 45 years old), the proportion of misclassification due to type-1 diabetes is expected to be low. Second, as part of the biorepository, cohort members who reported T2D medication at time of blood draw were classified as cases. Third, a one-time linkage to Hawai‘i insurance databases as of Jan. 1, 2008, also indicated T2D cases for participants recruited in Hawai‘i. Finally, a linkage to Medicare Chronic Condition Warehouse was used to identify diabetes for Medicare claims between the years 1999–2019 (the latest linkage). Individuals indicated on any of these sources are considered a case, with the remaining individuals considered as controls. Prevalent diabetes is based on self-reports on the baseline questionnaire, and cases who did not report diabetes in the baseline questionnaire are considered to have incident T2D. For Native Hawaiians, 2,024 T2D cases were used in building PGS models and 2,294 incident T2D cases were used to train DDS (see Fig. 1 for detailed study design).

Genotype data generation, processing, and quality control

In total, 5,374 self-identified Native Hawaiians from the MEC were genotyped on one of two separate GWAS arrays: Illumina MEGA (n = 4,148) and Illumina Global Diversity Array (GDA; n = 1,226). Each dataset was processed through the same quality control pipeline: we removed variants that were genotyped in fewer than 95% of people in the sample, as well as variants out of Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p < 10− 6). We also applied a filter for people with more than 2% missing genotypes but removed no one with this filter. The datasets were then lifted to hg38 using triple-LiftOver (v.1.33) [32] to ensure that alleles in inverted sequences between reference genome builds were properly lifted. We phased the genotypes with EAGLE (v.2.4.1) [33] using a Native-Hawaiian specific genetic map [34], and then imputed using the TOPMed imputation server against the TOPMed reference panel (release 3) [35].

We performed GWAS for BMI and T2D (prevalent + incident T2D cases) based on the subset of 4,148 MEC-NH individuals genotyped on the MEGA array, herein designated the genetic “training dataset”. The remaining 1,226 individuals genotyped on the GDA array are designated as the genetic “validation dataset” (Fig. 1). Specifically, we implemented the UW-GAC TOPMed GENESIS analysis pipeline (https://github.com/UW-GAC/analysis_pipeline) and inferred a Genetic Relationship Matrix (GRM) and Principal Components (PCs) using array data for MEC-NH [36]. In brief, this pipeline filtered variants (MAF < 1%, missingness > 1%) before LD pruning (R2 > 0.1, 10 Mb window). Initial kinship and population divergence estimates are inferred with KING [37]. These estimates were used as input for PC-AiR to infer PCs that in turn served as input into PC-Relate to infer a genetic-relatedness matrix [38, 39]. A second iteration of PC-AiR and PC-Relate was run with the PC-Relate GRM as the input. Association testing is done with the GENESIS package [40]. The null model is fit using the phenotype, GRM, and 20 PCs. Association testing is done with imputed dosages.

As an external validation set from a cohort with substantial PNS-like ancestry, a dataset of Samoans was downloaded from dbGAP (accession number: phs000914.v1.p1), containing 3,501 individuals and genotyped at over 900,000 SNPs on the Affymetrix 6.0 array. The Samoan participants are much less admixed than the MEC-NH [41]. The same quality control, phasing, and imputation procedures were followed.

Trans-ethnic polygenic score (PGS)

The PGS models were developed for each phenotype applying two Bayesian approaches, namely PRS-CSx and BridgePRS, which are described below [42–44]. For the development of BMI PGS, GWAS summary statistics conducted in individuals from the Taiwan Biobank and Biobank Japan (n = 256,450) representing EAS-like ancestry, Genetic Investigation of Anthropometric Traits (GIANT) consortium and U.K. BioBank (n = ~ 700,000) representing EUR-like ancestry, and the MEC-NH genetic training set in MEC study (n = 4,148 genotyped on MEGA array only) representing largely PNS-like ancestry (Supplementary Figure S1) were used. For the development of T2D PGS, we used ancestry-specific GWAS summary statistics from EAS-like populations (n = 427,504) and EUR-like ancestry populations (n = 1,812,017) as released by prior trans-ancestry GWAS efforts for T2D [45]. We conducted in-house GWAS for T2D in the MEC-NH training set. Note that the in-house GWAS for BMI and T2D in MEC-NH training set is necessarily somewhat underpowered, as a subset of individuals (n = 1,227) were reserved as validation dataset. A GWAS using the entire cohort is reported elsewhere [41].

We applied PRS-CSx to jointly model GWAS summary statistics from two ancestries or all three ancestries, and applied BridgePRS to construct pairwise two-stage models. Given each integrated PGS model, individual-level PGS were calculated for individuals in the MEC-NH validation set (those genotyped on GDA array). Scores were standardized to have mean = 0 and standard deviation = 1. Following a previous study [14], we measured performance of a PGS model based on the partial R2 (for continuous outcome BMI, calculated by R package rsq v2.5) or the Nagelkerke pseudo-R2 (calculated for T2D by R package DescTools v0.99.43) transformed to the liability scale assuming an estimated lifetime risk of 60% [15, 46]. Depending on the relative performance between PRS-CSx and BridgePRS models, the better-performing PGS by these metrics was then used in the downstream analyses for the respective phenotype.

PRS-CS and PRS-CSx

Polygenic scores were constructed using PRS-CS for single-ancestry models and PRS-CSx for trans-ancestry models [42–44]. Both methods use Bayesian continuous shrinkage priors to estimate SNP effect sizes from GWAS summary statistics while accounting for population-specific LD structure. PRS-CS operates on individual GWAS independently, while PRS-CSx extends this framework through a high-dimensional Bayesian regression approach that integrates GWAS summary statistics from multiple populations, allowing information sharing across ancestries while maintaining population-specific estimates.

Both PRS-CS and PRS-CSx require population-specific LD reference panels. We used pre-compiled 1000 Genomes references provided by PRS-CSx for EAS and EUR ancestry GWAS summary statistics and constructed an in-house Native Hawaiian reference conforming to the pre-compiled format. For the Native Hawaiian reference, we extracted imputed genotypes at HapMap3 variants from a randomly selected subset of 500 Native Hawaiian samples, restricted to variants passing imputation quality filtering (R² > 0.8) and MAF filtering (> 1%), and back-converted the genome build to hg19 for consistency with pre-compiled 1000 Genomes references. LD correlation matrices were computed using PLINK (v1.9) and organized into LD blocks using Asian population boundaries derived from population-specific LD breakpoints estimated by LDetect [47]. The final reference panel includes population-specific allele frequencies and strand-flipping indicators integrated into the standard PRS-CSx multi-population framework, with LD matrices stored in compressed HDF5 format for computational efficiency. The Native Hawaiian LD reference panel is publicly available (DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17822659).

For single-ancestry polygenic scores, PRS-CS was run independently on each ancestry-specific GWAS (EAS, EUR, Native Hawaiian) using the ancestry-matched LD reference panels described above and default parameters (phi="auto”, 1,000 MCMC iterations, 500 burn-in). PRS-CS estimates posterior SNP effect sizes by applying continuous shrinkage priors that account for population-specific LD structure. PGS weights were calculated as posterior means across MCMC samples. The polygenic score for each study participant was calculated using PLINK (--score command with sum modifier to combine chromosome-specific scores into genome-wide scores).

For trans-ancestry polygenic scores, PRS-CSx jointly modeled GWAS summary statistics from all three ancestries (EAS, EUR, Native Hawaiian). PRS-CSx performs cross-population polygenic prediction by sharing information between summary statistics while considering genetic diversity through population-specific allele frequencies and LD patterns. The method applies a shared continuous shrinkage prior, allowing varying degrees of shrinkage to be applied to SNP effect sizes based on the strength of their GWAS associations.

PRS-CSx was run in auto mode with default Strawderman-Berger prior parameters (a = 1, b = 0.5) for local shrinkage, and the global shrinkage parameter (φ) learned from data using a fully Bayesian approach (1,000 MCMC iterations, 500 burn-in). Summary statistics and ancestry-matched LD reference panels were used to estimate population-specific posterior SNP effect sizes. The population-specific posterior effect estimates were meta-analyzed using inverse-variance weighting within the Gibbs sampler to produce combined SNP effects. Polygenic scores were calculated for each study participant using PLINK (--score command with sum modifier).

BridgePRS

While PRS-CSx constructs PGS using continuous shrinkage priors applied at individual causal SNPs, BridgePRS operates at the locus level, combining SNP effects within genomic loci through Bayesian posterior mean estimation rather than simple averaging [44]. SNP effects within each locus are modeled by a multivariate Gaussian distribution, where a locus is defined as variants with R² > 0.01 and within 1 Mb of a lead variant (the SNP with the lowest p-value in that genomic region). We constructed three different trans-ancestry models for each phenotype: (1) EAS-like population as discovery and Native Hawaiian as target, (2) EUR-like population as discovery and Native Hawaiian as target, and (3) EAS-like population as discovery and EUR-like population as target. Single-ancestry or population-specific PGS models were trained and optimized using default parameters (α = 0.5 for allele frequency dependency, λ⁽⁰⁾ = 0.5 for baseline shrinkage), with zero-centered Gaussian prior distributions assigned to SNP effects at each locus. In the two-stage cross-ancestry models, posterior SNP effect sizes estimated in the discovery populations were used as informative priors for the target population (τ = 100 for cross-ancestry shrinkage). For EAS-like and EUR-like populations, we used LD information based on the EAS and EUR populations from 1000 Genomes, respectively, using variants with MAF > 5%. For Native Hawaiians, we computed the LD information based on 500 randomly selected individuals from the MEC-NH (the same set of individuals used above to construct the reference panel for PRS-CSx models). BridgePRS ensemble weights were optimized using the MEGA array training dataset (n = 4,148 and 4,131 for BMI and T2D, respectively), where ridge regression was used to determine optimal weights for combining single-ancestry and cross-ancestry models. The performance of the final ensemble PGS model was then evaluated in the GDA array validation dataset (n = 1,226 and 1,218 for BMI and T2D, respectively).

Dietary variables and phenotype-specific DDS

We split MEC-NH into a set of up to n = 8,972 individuals with diet information from survey but without genetic data as the MEC-NH dietary “training dataset”, holding out the remaining up to n = 5,374 individuals with both diet and genetic data as the MEC-NH dietary “validation dataset” (Fig. 1). Depending on the phenotype availability, the number of individuals in the training and validation datasets differed. We developed diet scores for BMI and T2D by applying three machine learning algorithms (elastic net regularization, random forest, and neural network) to all 520 dietary variables. For each phenotype, diet scores from all three algorithms were computed and standardized to have a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1, then evaluated to identify the best-performing model.

First, an elastic net regularization was implemented with a two-step nested cross-validation approach (5-fold cross validation) that identified the optimal L2 penalty parameter by evaluating cross-validation error across the lambda2 grid which ranged from 0.00001 to 0.01 with increments of 0.001, then the corresponding optimal L1 parameter for the selected L2 value was determined. After training the elastic net model on BMI using the MEC-NH dietary training dataset, a single composite score was calculated for each participant in the MEC-NH dietary validation dataset based on the learned coefficients of the selected dietary variables. For the prediction of the diabetes outcome, a cross-validated elastic net logistic regression model was developed using the optimal lambda value identified through cross-validation, keeping the alpha fixed at 0.3 (blend of L1 and L2 penalties: 30% Lasso penalty for variable selection and 70% Ridge penalty for handling correlated predictors) in the training dataset. Individual dietary scores were computed based on the elastic net model coefficients for the validation dataset. We used the ‘gcdnet’ (v.1.0.6) in R for the elastic net models.

Second, a random forest regression model was trained to predict BMI in the MEC-NH dietary training dataset, configured with 500 decision trees. The trained random forest model was then applied to predict BMI values for each participant in the dietary validation dataset. For diabetes status, a random forest classification model was trained to predict incident T2D status using all dietary variables in the dietary training dataset. We applied the trained random forest model to the dietary validation dataset to predict the probability of each individual having diabetes rather than providing binary classifications, and used the predicted probabilities as diabetes risk scores. For the random forest models, we used randomForest (v.4.7–1.2) in R.

Third, we applied a neural network diet score model. Multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) possess sufficient representational capacity to model complex nonlinear relationships in terms of the Universal Approximation Theorem [48], and it is common practice to use MLPs to fit classifiers and regressors in the machine learning field. The predictions of T2D and BMI based on dietary variables are technically a classification task and a regression task, respectively. Therefore, we employed MLPs for predicting T2D and BMI. We used grid search to find the optimal values of the hyperparameters. Specifically, we defined multiple candidate values for the hyperparameters (Supplementary Table S1; the combination of these candidate values resulted in more than 1,500 candidate models for predicting BMI and T2D, respectively). We trained all candidate models in parallel and chose the ones with the lowest validation loss as the final models. The key details for the training include: (a) 80% of the training data was used to optimize the weights and bias in the neurons, and the remaining 20% of the training data was used as a validation set to evaluate the validation loss; (b) The initial learning rate was 0.01, and was multiplied by 0.5 if validation loss did not improve for 10 epochs; (c) Early stopping was used to stop training after no improvement in validation loss for 15 epochs; (d) At most 200 epochs of training were executed; (e) Batch Normalization was used to normalize the outputs of each hidden layer; (f) Mini-batch training was used; (g) Dropout regularization with a rate of 0.1 was applied to hidden layers. Techniques (c), (e), and (g) work together to effectively avoid overfitting, making the use of L1 and L2 regularizations unnecessary. Keras within TensorFlow [49] was used to implement the building and training of the models.

Mediation analysis between BMI-related diet score and chronic disease conditions

We screened a set of chronic diseases to explore the associations of the BMI diet score to other diseases that may not be related to adiposity. We tested the association of the best-performing BMI diet score with the incidence of different chronic diseases ascertained from linkage of ICD codes from Medicare Claims with the Chronic Condition Warehouse in MEC-NH [46], adjusting for age, sex, BMI, total energy intake (kcal/day), and the first 10 genetic PCs. These chronic conditions included Alzheimer’s disease, anemia, asthma, atrial fibrillation, cataracts, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), depression, gout, hypertension and related phenotypes, ischemic heart disease, osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and stroke. For disease outcomes that were significantly associated with DDS, we conducted causal mediation analysis using two complementary sets of mediators: (i) BMI as a proximal mediator to quantify how much of the diet score effect on disease operates through adiposity, and (ii) four biomarkers reflecting downstream biological pathways (C-reactive protein (CRP), low-density and high-density lipoproteins (LDL and HDL), and triglyceride). We employed a two-stage regression approach within the counterfactual framework for causal mediation analysis using the mediation R package (version 4.5.1). In the first stage, we fitted a linear regression model to estimate the mediator model. For BMI mediation, we regressed BMI on diet score while controlling for age, sex, daily energy intake, and the first 10 PCs. For biomarker mediation, we regressed each biomarker on diet score while controlling for BMI, daily energy intake, and the first 10 PCs.

In the second stage, we fitted a probit regression model for the binary disease outcome, regressing disease phenotype on both the mediator (BMI or biomarker) and diet score (treatment). For BMI mediation, covariates included age, sex, daily energy intake, and the first 10 PCs. For biomarker mediation, covariates additionally included BMI. We used nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 100 simulations to generate confidence intervals for the mediation effects. The analysis estimated the Average Causal Mediation Effect (ACME), Average Direct Effect (ADE), and total effect, representing the indirect effect through the biomarker, the direct effect of diet score, and the overall effect, respectively. Biomarkers measured in a subset of MEC-NH individuals included C-reactive protein (n = 1,929), low-density lipoprotein (n = 1,886), high-density lipoprotein (n = 1,898), and triglycerides (n = 1,894). Phenotype transformation for these biomarkers were performed as previously described [50].

Results

Baseline characteristics for the Native Hawaiian participants in MEC (MEC-NH) are shown in Table 1. A total of 14,346 MEC-NH participants have dietary data from the baseline questionnaire, and a subset (n = 5,374) of these individuals had their DNA samples genotyped. All study participants had BMI information. The incidence of T2D from enrollment to the latest follow-up was 4,380 and the participants with genetics data had significantly higher prevalence of T2D (36.2%).

Developing the cross-ancestry PGS for BMI and T2D

To improve upon single-population PGS models previously developed for Native Hawaiians [14], we used two approaches (PRS-CSx and BridgePRS) to develop cross-ancestry PGS for BMI and T2D based on pairwise combinations of GWAS summary statistics from three continental populations: East Asian (n = 256,450 for BMI, 427,504 for T2D), European (n = 700,000 for BMI, 1,812,017 for T2D), and Native Hawaiians (n = 4,148 for BMI, 4,131 for T2D) (Methods).

For BMI, the best-performing trans-ancestry model with the highest accuracy in held-out MEC genetic validation dataset (n = 1,226) is one derived by PRS-CSx, combining GWAS summary statistics from EUR-like and EAS-like populations (927,557 common SNPs included in the model, with partial R2 = 0.119; Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2). This model outperformed single-population models previously constructed using pruning-and-threshold or LDPred2 [14], publicly available models [14], or estimated by PRS-CSx in single population mode here (partial R2 = 0.029–0.093; Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2). However, because this cross-ancestry PGS model did not use information from Native Hawaiian GWAS, we also found that this model continue to show the disparity in performance accuracy across populations as previously observed for single-population PGS models [14] (Supplementary Figure S2).

Notably, single-population PGS models using Native Hawaiian GWAS did not perform better than single-population PGS using East-Asian or European GWAS (partial R2 = 0.029 vs. 0.074–0.093; Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2), likely due to the small size of Native Hawaiian GWAS data. In cross-ancestry PGS models, using Native Hawaiian GWAS in combination with East-Asian or European GWAS likely introduced more noise and lowered the accuracy of PGS models, compared to single-population PGS using either GWAS (partial R2 = 0.036–0.065 vs. 0.074–0.093; Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2). In fact, when we compared the two-population cross-ancestry PGS models to a three-population model, we found that the inclusion of Native Hawaiian GWAS negatively impacted the efficacy of the model (partial R2 = 0.034; Supplementary Figure S3).

For T2D, we observed a similar trend. A cross-ancestry PGS model combining information from EAS and EUR GWAS tended to perform better (liability R2 = 0.092) than single-population PGS (liability R2 = 0.014–0.074) model or cross-ancestry models including Native Hawaiians (liability R2 = 0.011–0.039), though in this case BridgePRS model produced slightly higher liability R2 and AUC than the PRS-CSx model (liability R2 = 0.092 vs. 0.090; AUC = 0.65 vs. 0.64; Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2). But because the differences are marginal and made no difference in downstream analysis, we elected to use PRS-CSx model for both BMI and T2D below.

Developing a risk stratification model based on dietary information for BMI and T2D in Native Hawaiians

The MEC collected complete dietary data across a total of 520 variables, which we used to train our data-driven diet score (DDS) model. The dietary pattern differed by sampling location of the MEC (Hawai‘i vs. Los Angeles), though for Native Hawaiians they are nearly entirely sampled from Hawai‘i (Supplementary Figure S4). The dietary variables are also highly correlated (Supplementary Figure S5; see Supplementary Data S1 for a full list of dietary variables), which were taken into account by the algorithms to derive the DDS. We used three different machine learning algorithms (Methods) to derive DDS in the Native Hawaiian dietary training set (n = 8,372 with survey data but no genetic data; Fig. 1). Performance of these DDS were evaluated for association with BMI and T2D in the Native Hawaiian dietary validation dataset (individuals with both survey and genetic data; n = 5,222 for BMI, n = 4,633 for T2D). Diet score models applying random forest algorithm outperformed score models trained by elastic net and neural network for both BMI and T2D (Table 2).

For BMI, we found that a standard deviation (sd) increase in BMI DDS was significantly associated with 0.38 sd (s.e of ± 0.01) increase in BMI and explained approximately 12% of the variability after adjusting for daily energy intake (kcal/day) (Table 2). This is approximately similar level of variability explained for BMI in MEC-NH as the BMI PGS (12%, Fig. 2). We also found that the DDS are significantly associated with future BMI, taken approximately 10 years after baseline (n = 3,492; partial R2 [95%CI] = 0.12 [0.10 to 0.14]), though we note that adult BMI tends to be reasonably stable over time.

For T2D, a sd unit increase in DDS was significantly associated with 21% increase in odds of having the disease after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and daily energy intake (kcal/day). However, T2D DDS estimated based on these three algorithms had relatively poor predictability (AUC ranged from 0.57 to 0.58) and explained very small, possibly negligible, amount of variance of T2D, regardless of whether it is broadly defined incident T2D (Liability R2 ranged from 6.04 × 10− 3 to 0.012; Table 2), or incident T2D in 5 years from baseline (Liability R2 = 4.30 × 10− 3; AUC = 0.54; not significantly associated with incident T2D in 5 years) (Supplementary Table S3).

Combining Native Hawaiian PGS and diet score in risk stratification model

As we expect the genetic and non-genetic predictors to capture complementary information in the variation of the phenotype of interest, we fitted regression models with the best-performing PGS (from PRS-CSx) and DDS (from Random Forest) as the main predictors on each of BMI and T2D to assess their individual and joint effects adjusting for potential confounding variables. This joint evaluation is made in the subset of MEC-NH with both genetic and dietary data, so that both PGS and diet scores can be calculated (Fig. 1). Note that because the best-performing cross-ancestry PGS was developed without using MEC-NH information (Fig. 2), this joint analysis using both MEC-NH genetic training and validation dataset would not lead to over-fitting.

We first evaluate the joint effect of PGS and DDS for BMI (Table 3.a). The multivariate model containing both PGS and DDS (along with other covariates; Model 3) is a significantly better predictor than models with either of the scores alone (adjusted R2 in Model 3 = 0.29 vs. adjusted R2 in Model 1 or 2 = 0.21 Table 3.a; P = 4.95 × 10− 117 and P = 4.89 × 10− 120, respectively by likelihood ratio test; Table 3.b), suggesting that adding DDS to PGS model significantly improved the model fit. Together, PGS and DDS capture complementary and nearly additive information, as the partial R2 barely diminished in the joint model (0.12 or 0.11 in Models 1 or 2, respectively, to 0.10 in Model 3). We next tested for interaction effects but did not observe a significant interaction between PGS and DDS (Model 4, P = 0.93; Table 3.a). Sensitivity analyses treating diet score as quartiles yielded consistent results (Supplementary Table S4).

Association of BMI diet score with other chronic diseases in Native Hawaiians

Because BMI is a risk factor for a number of chronic diseases, we tested the association of the BMI DDS on the incidence of multiple chronic diseases and conditions in MEC-NH participants (n = 5,374; the subset of MEC-NH with genetic data that was not used to develop diet score). We found that the BMI DDS was associated with 21% increase in odds of developing T2D, 20% increase in odds of having stroke, 20% increase in odds of having chronic kidney disease, 17% higher odds of having ischemic heart disease, and 12% increase in having anemia (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S6). To formally quantify the role of adiposity in these associations, we conducted causal mediation analysis with BMI as the primary mediator. BMI was a significant mediator for four of the five diseases (all p < 0.0001; Table 5, Supplementary Table S7), with the proportion of the total diet score effect mediated through BMI ranging from 32% for chronic kidney disease (ACME = 0.0242 [0.0172, 0.0317]) to 52% for T2D (ACME = 0.0500 [0.0433, 0.0567]) (Supplementary Table S6). Anemia (34%; ACME = 0.0143 [0.0074, 0.0209]) and ischemic heart disease (36%; ACME = 0.0201 [0.0135, 0.0267]) also showed significant BMI-mediated effects. Stroke was the exception, with a non-significant indirect effect through BMI (proportion mediated = 13%, p = 0.188), indicating that the DDS–stroke association is largely BMI-independent. Across all five diseases, substantial direct effects (ADE) of diet score remained after accounting for BMI, suggesting that the diet score additionally operates through adiposity-independent biological pathways.

To further characterize the BMI-independent pathways through which DDS influences disease risk, we conducted mediation analysis for each of these five diseases with four biomarkers available in the cohort: CRP (as an inflammatory marker), HDL, LDL, and triglycerides (reflecting different lipid pathways). After correcting for a total of 24 tests we did not generally find significant results (Bonferroni p-value threshold = 0.0021), we did observe some trends suggestive for mediation effects (Table 5, Supplementary Table S7). For example, the average causal mediation effect (ACME) of CRP on T2D (ACME = 0.0022, p-value = 0.06) suggests that the BMI-related diet could lead to incident T2D through the inflammatory pathway. We also found that BMI-related diet may affect the levels of healthy lipids (HDL) in the system that could play a role in developing chronic kidney disease (ACME = 0.0028, p-value = 0.02) or stroke (ACME = 0.0020, p-value = 0.06). For T2D, triglycerides partially mediated the effect of the BMI-associated diet score on incident T2D. The indirect effect through triglycerides (ACME = 0.0042, p-value = 0.02) accounted for 13% of the total effect (proportion mediated = 0.13, p-value = 0.04). Each unit increase in triglycerides was associated with 23% increased odds of T2D (OR = 1.23, p-value = 4.35 × 10− 10; Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

In the present study we developed comprehensive risk stratification models integrating genetic and dietary predictors for BMI and T2D in Native Hawaiians. Our cross-ancestry polygenic score (PGS) approach showed improved prediction accuracy over previous single-ancestry models for Native Hawaiians [14], demonstrating that leveraging large-scale cross-ancestry GWAS summary statistics can improve prediction accuracy in populations with limited genetic studies. Beyond genetic factors, dietary intake emerged as a powerful complementary predictor, with our data-driven diet scores (DDS) explaining substantial variance in BMI. The combination of PGS and DDS achieved markedly improved prediction performance compared to either approach alone [23]. Furthermore, our best-performing BMI DDS showed associations with multiple chronic diseases, even when BMI was included as a covariate. Even though we are limited in available biomarker data for a systematic mediation analysis, our results suggest that BMI-associated dietary patterns may influence broader health outcomes through inflammatory and lipid-mediated pathways.

Comparative predictive power of diet versus genetics

Building on these predictive capabilities, our analysis revealed that dietary intake explains phenotypic variation beyond genetic factors. Our best-performing BMI DDS accounted for approximately 12% of adult BMI variance in MEC-NH. As diet is imperfectly recalled or measured, diet may explain even greater BMI variance than estimated. In contrast, the best-performing DDS trained on T2D incidence, though significantly associated with the outcome in held-out validation cohort, explained only ~ 1% of phenotypic variation on the liability scale. This limited predictive ability for T2D likely reflects several factors. For instance, dietary patterns vary considerably over time, making diet at baseline a poor predictor of incident T2D. We may have substantial mis-classification issues with a disease like T2D that requires medical access. Moreover, individuals with pre-diabetic conditions may have already modified their diets toward healthier patterns, confounding the diet-disease relationship.

Interpreting the major contributing factors for a machine learning-based prediction model is a general challenge [51, 52]. In our best performing approach (Random Forest), we can obtain features of importance, but it lacks directionality. ElasticNet models, while they did not attain as high accuracy, would provide a list of variables and direction of effects in association with T2D incidence or BMI. We should take the magnitude and direction of the effect estimates with caution, as ElasticNet shrinks the effect estimates and direction of effect in multivariate analysis may depend on the variables included in the analysis [53]. Nonetheless, in our DDS models for Native Hawaiians, we found that the effect sizes of ElasticNet-selected dietary variables were generally relatively small for T2D incidence compared to BMI (Supplementary Data S1). We observed shared and distinct dietary factors across BMI and incident T2D outcomes in Native Hawaiians. Diet soda intake and roasted or baked chicken with skin were positively associated with both BMI and T2D [54, 55], consistent with their roles in energy balance and metabolic dysfunction. Meat-related factors showed outcome-specific patterns: non-processed red meat consumption was positively associated with BMI, likely reflecting its caloric density, while nitrite—a biomarker of processed meat consumption—showed a positive association with incident T2D, aligning with established evidence linking processed meat to diabetes risk through nitrosamines and advanced glycation end products [56, 57]. Interestingly, we also observed that more non-Western dietary patterns (ramen/saimin, refried beans, enchiladas) were associated with reduced T2D, which also had suggestive association previously in literature [58]. Taken together, population-specific dietary-metabolic relationships warrant further investigation.

Mechanisms linking diet to chronic disease risk

Despite the challenges of using DDS to establish causality between diet variables and outcome, we found BMI diet score to be strongly associated with the incidence of several chronic diseases in Native Hawaiians, such as T2D, chronic kidney disease, ischemic heart disease and stroke (Table 5). Mediation analysis revealed that BMI itself is a major proximate mediator of these associations, accounting for 32–52% of the total diet score effect across four diseases (T2D: 52%, ischemic heart disease: 36%, anemia: 34%, chronic kidney disease: 32%). Stroke was the notable exception, where the indirect effect through BMI was non-significant (proportion mediated = 13%, p = 0.188), suggesting that the diet–stroke association operates primarily through BMI-independent pathways. Across all five outcomes, substantial direct effects of diet score persisted after accounting for BMI, indicating that adiposity alone does not fully explain how dietary patterns influence chronic disease risk. We used mediation analysis to begin probing the mechanism through which BMI-related diet is associated with incident chronic diseases. Our analysis revealed that among the residual BMI-independent effects, the biomarkers we tested (CRP, HDL, LDL, triglycerides) explain less than 13% of the total diet score–disease association, suggesting they capture only a fraction of the non-adiposity pathways. While it is possible that diet’s health effects additionally operate through other molecular markers in the inflammatory or lipid metabolism pathways, or through other unmeasured biological pathways, it is also likely that we are limited by sample size for which these biomarkers are available (generally approximately 2,000 individuals out of 5,000 MEC-NH). Most importantly, currently available biomarker data are from a cross-sectional one-time measurement. These measures do not adequately reflect the effect of long-term exposure and alone cannot establish causality. Therefore, a more complete and thorough analysis, particularly in longitudinal cohort, is warranted in the future.

Future studies could also consider other pathways such as gut microbiome interactions, metabolomic alterations, epigenetic mechanisms, and direct cellular mechanisms (including mitochondrial and endothelial function) to capture the full impact of diet on chronic disease risk [59–62]. Population-specific mechanisms should also be investigated, such as genetic variants affecting metabolic enzyme function, population-specific genetic adaptations in Native Hawaiians, and traditional versus Western diet interactions [63–66]. Nevertheless, due to this apparent ‘pleiotropic’ effect of dietary patterns associated with BMI, these BMI-prone diet scores could be incorporated into clinical risk assessment tools to improve chronic disease risk prediction.

Limitations and future directions

One current limitation of our DDS models is that it is dependent on variable availability, which differs across cohorts. Unlike genetic data which is more standardized or can be imputed to the same set of genetic markers for calculating the polygenic scores, our diet score models currently require having the same set of diet information available to calculate the score. For example, we have examined the possibility of harmonizing the available diet phenotype in the MEC with that from UK Biobank, which employed a two-tiered dietary assessment including different levels of food questions and different time frames of recalls [67]. Indeed, manual curation found that variables can only match ~ 30–57% of the times depending on the strictness of interpretation (Supplementary Data S1). Additionally, dietary patterns are likely specific to each ethnicity, thereby limiting transferability of models from one population to another even if dietary information is shared. Therefore, utilizing diet scores in risk stratification in different cohorts will require extensive and systematic variable harmonization of dietary surveys or assessment tools, as well as future evaluation of transferability of our DDS to other ethnic groups.

Another limitation is that while our composite scores effectively predict metabolic phenotypes, establishing a causal role of diet remains challenging. Unlike polygenic scores, which are determined at conception and remain constant throughout life, dietary information in the MEC was collected in adulthood, when participants may already have been in the preliminary stages of disease but not yet diagnosed. As a step toward establishing causality, we tested whether our best-performing BMI diet score could predict BMI measured approximately 10 years after baseline, finding significant predictive ability (n = 3492; partial R2 [95%CI] = 0.12 [0.10 to 0.14]), although we should note that adult BMI is generally stable and our two measurements of BMI, 10 years apart, are highly correlated (R2 = 0.736). Future longitudinal study designs that collect detailed dietary data, particularly in adolescent or young adults, could greatly benefit efforts to establish causal relationships between diet and metabolic conditions [68–71].

Implications for genetic studies and clinical practice

Irrespective of the clinical utilities of the diet score models, at least for BMI and to a lesser extent for T2D, it is clear that diet information explains part of the phenotypic variation that is not captured by the best available polygenic predictors. This has important implications for genetic research: when diet information is available for a cohort in a genetic study, computing and adjusting for diet scores will help remove a part of the phenotypic variation explained by the non-genetic factors, thereby improving the statistical power of the genetic mapping study. Such an increase in power will be particularly valuable when mapping population-specific associations in a relatively small GWAS cohort, as is often the case for underrepresented populations like Native Hawaiians.

Conclusion

For populations with limited genetic data, integrating large-scale GWAS data from other ancestries substantially improved polygenic score performance compared to single-ancestry approaches in Native Hawaiians. Importantly, diet scores trained on shared risk factors such as BMI can be applied across different chronic diseases, though effectiveness may vary by the disease, the population, and dietary patterns. Our findings demonstrate that personalized metabolic disease risk assessment should integrate both genetic and dietary information, accounting for population-specific genetic variants and dietary patterns to develop effective prevention and management strategies for diverse populations.
