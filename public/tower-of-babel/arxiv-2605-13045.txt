Large Language Models Lack Temporal Awareness of Medical Knowledge
Zihan Guan, Qiao Jin, Guangzhi Xiong, Fangyuan Chen, Mengxuan Hu, Qingyu Chen, Yifan Peng, Zhiyong Lu, Anil Vullikanti

Abstract
The existing methods for evaluating the medical knowledge of Large Language Models (LLMs) are largely based on atemporal examination-style benchmarks, while in reality, medical knowledge is inherently dynamic and continuously evolves as new evidence emerges and treatments are approved. Consequently, evaluating medical knowledge without a temporal context may provide an incomplete assessment of whether LLMs can accurately reason about time-specific medical knowledge. Moreover, most medical data are historical, requiring the models not only to recall the correct knowledge, but also to know when that knowledge is correct. To bridge the gap, we built TempoMed-Bench, the first-of-its-kind benchmark for evaluating the temporal awareness of the LLMs in the medical domain through evolving guideline knowledge. Based on the TempoMed-Bench, our evaluation analysis first reveals that LLMs lack temporal awareness in medical knowledge through the key findings: (1) model performance on up-to-date medical knowledge exhibits a gradual linear decline over time rather than a sharp knowledge-cutoff behavior, suggesting that parametric medical knowledge is not strictly bounded by knowledge cutoffs; (2) LLMs consistently struggle more with recalling outdated historical medical knowledge than with up-to-date recommendations: accuracy of historical knowledge is only 25.37% - 53.89% of up-to-date knowledge, indicating potential knowledge forgetting effects during training; and (3) LLMs often exhibit temporally inconsistent behaviors, where predictions fluctuate irregularly across neighboring years. We also show that the temporal awareness problem is a challenge that cannot be easily solved when integrated with agentic search tools (-3.15% - 14.14%). This work highlights an important yet underexplored challenge and motivates future research on developing LLMs that can better encode time-specific medical knowledge 1 1 1 The code and data are available at https://github.com/GuanZihan/TempoMed-Bench .

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable performance in various medical tasks, including clinical question answering, diagnosis, and specialty-specific knowledge asssessment
barile2024diagnostic
;
rydzewski2024comparative
;
mihalache2023performance
. These capabilities are often due to their strong ability to encode large amounts of medical knowledge during pre-training and post-training
singhal2023large
. Because clinical applications are high-stakes, the LLMs’ internal medical knowledge must be evaluated rigorously.

Most existing evaluations, however, assess medical knowledge in an atemporal way. Medical Benchmarks
jin2021disease
;
pal2022medmcqa
;
zuo2025medxpertqa
typically test factual and reasoning ability using exam-style questions, but they overlook a critical dimension:
temporal validity
. Real-world medical knowledge is inherently dynamic and continuously evolves with new evidence, guidelines, and treatment approvals. For example, obesity treatment guidelines issued by the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity have changed over time as new anti-obesity medications became available
seo20192018
;
kim2023evaluation
;
bae20252024
. A treatment that was appropriate according to a 2018 guideline may no longer be recommended in a 2024 guideline (Figure
1
). Conversely, a current recommendation may not have been valid in an earlier clinical context. As a result, atemporal benchmarks may be insufficient to assess whether LLMs can reason about time-specific knowledge.

Figure 1
:
An example of the evolving medical knowledge.

To address this limitation, we study a crucial yet underexplored problem through the lens of
temporal awareness
: the ability of LLMs to correctly recall and align medical knowledge with a specified point in time.
Temporal awareness is strongly grounded in real-world clinical practice: Much of medical data is inherently historical and longitudinal
goodwin2022timing
;
cui2025timer
, as they are recorded from past patient encounters and clinical trials. This requires the models not only to recall the correct knowledge, but also to know
when
that knowledge is correct.
This need arises in multiple scenarios. First, users may ask LLMs health-related questions whose correct answers depend on recently updated evidence. Second, clinicians and researchers may need to retrospectively interpret prior studies, historical recommendations, or past treatment decisions.
In both cases, the model must distinguish between current and outdated knowledge.

To evaluate LLMs’ temporal awareness in the medical domain, we introduce TempoMed-Bench, a benchmark built from evolving clinical guidelines. Guideline knowledge is continuously released and updated by official organizations, and can therefore be naturally seen as snapshots of evolving medical knowledge. We collect over 20,000 guideline articles from the PubMed Central (PMC) database and organize them into 3,411 trajectories of guideline versions issued by the same organization on the same topic. For each trajectory, we extract differences between the up-to-date version and each of the outdated version, yielding 721 version-to-version difference pairs. Finally, for each pair, we construct a multiple-choice question (MCQ) to evaluate whether LLMs’ knowledge aligns more closely with the current or outdated guidelines.

TempoMed-Bench aims to address four research questions.
RQ1
: Have LLMs already acquired up-to-date medical knowledge?
RQ2
: Can LLMs correctly recall historical (outdated) medical knowledge, and how consistent are their behaviors over time?

RQ3
: Can LLMs maintain temporal consistency across different time periods?

RQ4
: Can agentic RAG systems mitigate these failures?

We evaluate more than 10 frontier LLMs on TempoMed-Bench. Our evaluation results first reveal that LLMs lack temporal awareness in medical knowledge, which is supported by the key findings below.
In RQ1, we empirically observe that, across different guideline trajectories, strong models such as GPT-5 are not oracles in recalling all detailed up-to-date knowledge (Table
1
); moreover, model performance on up-to-date medical knowledge exhibits a gradual linear decline over time (Figure
6
), rather than a sharp knowledge-cutoff behavior. In RQ2, we found that, in a given guideline trajectory, LLMs consistently struggle more with recalling historical medical knowledge than with up-to-date recommendations: accuracy of historical knowledge is only 25.37% - 53.89% of up-to-date knowledge (Figure
7
), indicating potential knowledge forgetting effects during training.
In RQ3, we show that LLMs exhibit temporally inconsistent behaviors, where predictions fluctuate irregularly across neighboring years without coherent temporal transitions. We also show that the temporal awareness problem is a non-trivial challenge that cannot be easily solved when integrated with popular agentic search tools, such as ToolUniverse
gao2025democratizing
and Biomni
huang2025biomni
, which only bring limited improvements (-3.15% - 14.14%; see Table
3
).

To sum up, our contributions are:
(1)
Temporal-Aware Medical Knowledge Evaluation
. We propose temporal context as a necessary dimension for evaluating medical LLMs, motivated by the inherently evolving nature of real-world medical knowledge.
(2)
TempoMed-Bench
. We construct a large-scale collection of evolving clinical guideline trajectories and a benchmark of 721 temporally grounded medical MCQs.
(3)
Empirical Evidence of Limited Temporal Awareness in LLMs
. We empirically show that current LLMs lack temporal awareness of medical knowledge and that current agentic RAG systems only partially address the problem, through four research questions.

Related Works

Medical Knowledge Evaluation.
Prior work on medical knowledge evaluation has largely concentrated on atemporal QA benchmarks

, including MedQA

, PubMedQA

, and MedMCQA

, and HealthBench
arora2025healthbench
. However, these evaluations still mostly assume that medical knowledge is static. In contrast, medical knowledge is inherently evolving, with clinically important changes frequently formalized through updated guidelines.

Temporal Knowledge Evaluation.
Most knowledge in the real world evolves over time. Temporal awareness has recently been a popular dimension in evaluating the evolving knowledge of LLMs
lin2025temporal
;
wang2024large
;
chen2024temporal
;
zhu2025evolvebench
;
tang2025evowiki
;
nakshatri2025facts
;
mousavi2024dyknow
. However, the existing works only focus on the general knowledge (e.g., “who served as the president of the United States from 2001 to 2009?”) from sources such as Wikidata
tang2025evowiki
;
zhu2025evolvebench
;
mousavi2024dyknow
;
chen2024temporal
or industry documents
nakshatri2025facts
. In this paper, we particularly focus on the temporal knowledge evaluation in the medical domain. The problem remains largely unexplored, mainly due to the intrinsic challenges of collecting and curating temporally evolving medical knowledge, which is often unstructured and distributed across various clinical trial reports and guideline papers.
wu2025assessing
focuses on evaluating whether LLMs can endorse current and reject pseudo-outdated medical recommendations when some synthesized conflicting information is present in the context. Although we share a similar setting in evaluations with medical guidelines, the differences are also significant: (1) we focus on evaluating LLMs’ temporal awareness in the medical domains, which is fundamentally different; (2) their outdated knowledge is constructed using manually created pseudo-outdated versions, while ours focuses on the real evolution of medical guidelines.

3
TempoMed-Bench

In this section, we introduce TempoMed-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating how LLMs handle evolving medical knowledge. Specifically, we construct a dataset grounded in clinical guideline documents, which naturally capture snapshots of medical knowledge at different points in time and are regularly revised to reflect new evidence. Based on these guidelines, we identify differences across versions and construct multiple-choice questions (MCQs) that explicitly probe models’ preferences between up-to-date and outdated medical knowledge.

In the following sections, we describe the dataset construction pipeline in detail and provide a comprehensive statistical analysis of the resulting benchmark.

3.1
Dataset Preparation Pipeline

Guideline Collection & Filtering.
We collect the guideline papers from the PubMed Central (PMC)

database, which contains over 7.5 million life science and biomedical papers of various types, including reviews, clinical studies, guidelines, and research articles. In this step, we aim to build a rough classifier to minimize the risk of missing guideline papers. To this end, we adopt a relaxed filtering strategy that combines metadata from PubMed with naïve keyword matching. Specifically, for each article, if the term “guideline” appears in the title or in the
<article-type>
tag, we classify it as a guideline paper. Figure

The articles in the PMC database are organized into three subsets:

Guideline Trajectory Building.
The above process introduces many noisy papers that might not actually be a guideline paper. For example, the paper “Tuberculosis in childhood: a systematic review of national and international guidelines”
berti2014tuberculosis
is classified as a guideline paper simply because ‘guideline’ appears in the title. Therefore, a more fine-grained filter is required.

We adopt an LLM to analyze the article texts and determine whether the given article is a guideline paper or not, where a guideline paper is defined as a guideline issued by a professional society (e.g., the American College of Rheumatology). If the guideline checking is passed, then the LLM proceeds to analyze whether the guideline paper has cited any prior versions of the current guideline paper, where the prior guideline is formally defined as an official guidance document issued by the
same organization
before the year of the current guideline and focuses on the
same topic
as the current guideline. The overall prompt for the LLM is present in Listing
13
. This process enables us to extract at least one prior guideline for 1,134 papers in the
comm
subset, 1,586 papers in the
noncomm
subset, and 200 papers in the
other
subset, resulting in a total of 2,920 guideline trajectories. We name these trajectories as candidate guideline trajectories.

We notice that the above candidate trajectory might not be complete either because the current guideline may not mention all the prior guidelines, or the LLMs might omit some by hallucination. Therefore, we apply necessary post-processing steps to revise and augment the trajectory data (with details shown in Appendix
A.1
). After the post-processing, there is at least one prior guideline for 1,496 papers in the
comm
subset, 1,691 papers in the
noncomm

other

TempoMed-Traj

Trajectory Difference Extraction.
Given the guideline trajectories, the next step is to extract the evolving changes between the up-to-date guideline and each of its previous outdated versions. To achieve this, we prompt LLMs to identify these differences, and the extracted changes are subsequently verified by a separate, strict verifier LLM.
Listing

16
present the prompt to the LLM. As seen, the extracted difference pair between each up-to-date guideline and outdated guideline must represent a direct head-to-head comparison and be meaningfully different. Then, a verifier LLM scores the extracted difference pair with detailed rubrics.
Note that in this step, we only consider the trajectory where all the guideline nodes are in the guideline set we prepared in {Guideline Collection & Filtering}, since otherwise we would not be guaranteed to get the full text of the paper. The process is visualized in Figure
3

C.1

This process results in 721 difference pairs; each is an extracted difference between an up-to-date guideline and an outdated guideline.

MCQ Generation.
For each of the extracted differences, we construct a corresponding evaluation question that aims to test whether the LLMs’ knowledge is closer to the up-to-date knowledge or to the outdated knowledge. To align with the traditional exam style, we design QAs according to the NBME (National Board of Medical Examiners) guideline
3
3

3

https://www.nbme.org/sites/default/files/2021-02/NBME_Item%20Writing%20Guide_R_6.pdf

Specifically, for each extracted difference, we prompt the LLM as in Listing
17

The option set contains five options, which contain an option that aligns with the knowledge in the up-to-date guideline, an option that aligns with the knowledge in the outdated guideline, and two clinically plausible distractor options that align with neither of the two. We also added a ‘I don’t know’ (IDK) option to investigate the LLM’s abstention ability following
madhusudhan2025llms

Experimental Setup.
To investigate this problem, we evaluate multiple LLMs on each question instance in the TempoMed-Bench. Each question is designed to assess whether a model’s response aligns with the most up-to-date medical guidelines in a trajectory, as opposed to outdated guidelines or distractor options that reflect incorrect or irrelevant recommendations. To ensure a robust evaluation, for each original question, we generate two additional variants by perturbing the positional order of the answer options. This augmentation allows us to reduce the influence of positional bias

4.3
RQ3: Can LLMs maintain temporal consistency when recalling medical knowledge across different time periods?

Temporal Consistency.
We define
temporal consistency
as a qualitative concept that characterizes whether an LLM exhibits coherent and stable behavioral patterns when recalling medical knowledge across time. Specifically, temporally consistent behavior does not necessarily require the model to provide factually correct answers for every year; rather, it requires the model’s predictions to evolve in a logically consistent manner over time. In contrast, temporally inconsistent behavior refers to irregular or contradictory prediction patterns that fluctuate unpredictably across neighboring years without coherent temporal transitions. Such behaviors suggest unstable or weakly grounded temporal knowledge representations rather than coherent medical reasoning, thereby undermining the trustworthiness of medical LLMs.

Experimental Setup.
To evaluate temporal consistency in recalling the time-specific medical knowledge, we conduct a fine-grained analysis of LLM predictions using the time-specific question templates. Specifically, for each difference pair extracted in the TempoMed-Bench benchmark, we construct queries using the following template:

Diversified Consistency Patterns.
For each question instance, this analysis probes the model’s encoded knowledge across different years, resulting in a two-row heatmap where each column represents the varying years and each row represents the up-to-date statement and the outdated statement, respectively. Ideally, the model’s responses should reflect the temporal validity of the statements. For the up-to-date statement, the model should answer “No” before the up-to-date guideline year while answering “Yes” thereafter; Conversely, for the outdated statement, the model should answer “Yes” before the update and “No” afterwards. This results in a zig-zag transition pattern centered around the guideline update year (named as
Correct-Transition-Point
). Empirically, however, this ideal pattern rarely occurs. Instead, we observe several iconic temporal consistency patterns in the probing heatmaps:

•

All-True
: the model answers “Yes” for both statements across all years.

•

All-False
: the model answers “No” for both statements across all years.

•

Only-Know-Latest
: the model consistently answers “Yes” for the up-to-date statement and “No” for the outdated statement, regardless of the year.

•

Only-Know-Prior
: the model consistently answers “No” for the up-to-date statement and “Yes” for the outdated statement, regardless of the year.

•

Wrong-Transition-Point
: the zig-zag transition pattern appears, but the transition occurs at a year that deviates significantly from the true guideline update year.

All other heatmaps that do not belong to the above categories are deemed as
Inconsistency
. We present examples of each error pattern in Appendix
E.3.1
. Each error pattern reveals a distinct limitation in how LLMs encode and utilize temporally evolving medical knowledge:
All-True
indicates that the model exhibits a strong affirmative bias, accepting both outdated and up-to-date statements regardless of their temporal validity.
All-False
suggests that the model systematically rejects both statements, indicating a lack of confidence or failure to recognize valid medical knowledge.
Only-Know-Latest
shows that the model consistently favors the up-to-date statement across all years, ignoring temporal constraints.
Only-Know-Prior
indicates that the model consistently relies on outdated knowledge, failing to incorporate more recent updates.
Wrong-Transition-Point
reflects cases where the model captures the existence of a knowledge shift but misplaces its timing.

Pattern Distributions.
To analyze each LLM’s internal preference towards the temporal medical knowledge, we conduct a statistical analysis on the distributions of these patterns for each LLM model, with the results shown in Table

All-True

4.5
Limitations and Future Work

While our study provides a first-of-its-kind dataset for evaluating the temporal medical knowledge of LLMs, several limitations remain and could be addressed in future work: (1) We apply strict criteria when extracting guideline differences, resulting in a relatively limited dataset containing only 721 instances; (2) Although we observe limited improvements when using off-the-shelf agentic RAG tools, we have not yet explored how to further improve performance through techniques such as fine-tuning or more advanced agentic systems; (3) Due to limited full-text availability in the PubMed database (only the PMC provides full-text access), our trajectory dataset remains incomplete.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce TempoMed-Bench, the first benchmark for evaluating the temporal awareness of LLMs in the medical domain. Our empirical results demonstrate that even state-of-the-art models exhibit limited temporal awareness when handling evolving medical knowledge. Furthermore, integrating agentic RAG systems does not fully resolve this limitation. We hope this work highlights an important yet underexplored challenge and motivates future research on developing LLMs that can more effectively encode and reason over time-specific medical knowledge.

A.2
Extracting Differences from the Guideline Trajectories

Given the guideline trajectories, the next step is to extract the evolving changes between the up-to-date guideline and each of its previous outdated versions. To achieve this, we prompt LLMs to identify these differences, and the extracted changes are subsequently verified by a separate, strict verifier LLM.
Listing
15
and Listing
16
present the prompt to the LLM. As seen, the extracted difference pair between each up-to-date guideline and outdated guideline must represent a direct head-to-head comparison and be meaningfully different. Then, a verifier LLM scores the extracted difference pair with detailed rubrics.

Note that in this step, we only consider the trajectory where all the guideline nodes are in the guideline set we filtered from the PMC database, since otherwise, we would not be guaranteed to get the body text of the paper.

C.1
Examples of the Extracted Guideline Trajectory and Differences

Each difference file is formulated as a dictionary object. The field ‘clinical_focus’ provides a short description of the clinical topic or decision point under comparison. The fields ‘current_recommendation’ and ‘prior_recommendation’ contain normalized summaries of the recommendation in the current and prior guidelines, respectively, while ‘current_recommendation_raw_text’ and ‘prior_recommendation_raw_text’ preserve the original supporting passages from the source documents. The field ‘change_summary’ summarizes how the recommendation evolved across guideline versions. Finally, ‘score’ records the score from the verifier LLM (0=Not Good, 0.5=Okay but not good, and 1=Good), and ‘rationale’ provides a justification for why the paired recommendations are considered a valid and meaningful change instance. This format allows both structured downstream processing and direct inspection of the underlying textual evidence.

D.2
Positional Perturbation

We applied positional perturbations to each 5-option MCQ at the evaluation stage. Starting from the original item, we generated perturbed variants under three schemes.

•

Reorder-only
permutes the displayed option order while preserving the original label–content bindings

•

Label Shuffling
keeps the displayed slots fixed but permutes the option contents across positions, thereby changing the location of the correct, outdated, and distractor options.

•

Alternate Labels
preserves the option contents but replaces the canonical option labels with an alternative label set (e.g., X, Y, Z, W).

Appendix F
Broader impacts

Our evaluation on TempoMed-Bench shows that even state-of-the-art LLMs lack temporal awareness in medical knowledge, and this limitation persists even when integrating agentic retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems. These findings suggest that current LLMs are still far from being reliable assistants for time-specific medical decision-making.

It should be emphasized that strong performance on TempoMed-Bench alone does not guarantee the safe deployment of LLMs in clinical settings. However, failure on this benchmark indicates a critical limitation in temporal medical reasoning that must be addressed before such systems can be trusted in real-world healthcare applications. In this sense, we believe that passing TempoMed-Bench should be considered a necessary—although not sufficient—condition for deploying LLMs in evolving medical environments.
