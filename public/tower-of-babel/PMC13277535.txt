Sirt1 transgene delivery improves diabetes-impaired wound healing
Authors: Ruyue Luo, Huifeng Wang, Chongwen Duan, Michelle Jen, Hsin-Yu Kuo, Xinlong Wang, Milan Mrksich, Guillermo Ameer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus severely impairs wound healing, in part due to persistent inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, and dysregulated cellular responses. Sirtuin-1 (Sirt1), an NAD+-dependent deacetylase, has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for restoring regenerative capacity in diabetic wounds. However, effective and localized gene delivery remains a major challenge. Here, we report an injectable thermoresponsive poly(polyethylene glycol citrate-co-N-isopropylacrylamide) (PPCN) hydrogel as a localized delivery platform for lentiviruses encoding Sirt1 (LV-Sirt1) to promote sustained gene expression and tissue regeneration in diabetic full-thickness dermal wounds. Encapsulation of LV-Sirt1 within PPCN enabled in situ gelation and significantly accelerated wound closure compared with hydrogel or virus alone. Histological analyses revealed enhanced re-epithelialization, increased granulation tissue formation, and improved epidermal barrier restoration. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated elevated fibroblast activity, reduced inflammatory response, and a facilitated polarization of macrophage from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward a regenerative M2 phenotype. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that Sirt1 modulates key genes associated with immune regulation (Spp1, Ccl24, Ggt1), epithelial migration (Krt8), vascular stabilization (Aspn, Plscr4), and nerve regeneration (Nefm). Collectively, these results demonstrate that thermoresponsive PPCN hydrogels enable effective localized lentiviral gene delivery, and that sustained Sirt1 expression promotes angiogenesis, immune modulation, and tissue regeneration in diabetic wounds. This platform provides a promising strategy for controlled gene therapy in chronic wound management and other regenerative applications.

Introduction

Impaired wound healing is a prevalent and severe complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), frequently manifesting as chronic ulcers that are slow to heal and highly susceptible to infection [1,2], which significantly increases the risk for limb amputations. Globally, approximately 18.6 million individuals develop diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) each year, including 1.6 million cases in the United States [3], where 9.9% of adults over 40 have diabetes, with 30% affected by lower-limb complications [4]. Among these patients, 17% require amputation [5], and the five-year mortality rate for those with major amputations exceeds 70% [6,7]. For those who heal with conservative treatment, more than half will experience recurrence within five years [8], and the five-year mortality rate reaches up to 50% [[9], [10], [11]].

Diabetic wounds are characterized by delayed closure [12], inadequate angiogenesis [13], sustained inflammation [14], elevated oxidative stress [15], and increased tissue senescence [16]. These pathological features reflect profound molecular dysregulation within the wound microenvironment, underscoring the urgent need for therapeutic strategies that restore coordinated regenerative signaling rather than addressing isolated symptoms.

Sirtuin-1 (Sirt1), an NAD + -dependent lysine deacetylase, plays a critical role in regulating inflammation [17], oxidative stress [18], metabolism [19], cell cycle, and the inhibition of senescence [20]. Dysregulation of these processes is closely linked to diabetes pathogenesis, positioning Sirt1 as a promising therapeutic target for diabetic wound repair [21,22]. Previous studies have demonstrated that Sirt1 deficiency leads to vascular formation defects and impaired ischemia-induced neovascularization, whereas Sirt1 overexpression enhances endothelial cell survival and angiogenic capacity [22,23]. Despite these observations, the mechanisms by which sustained Sirt1 activity contributes to diabetic wound healing, particularly within the complex wound microenvironment, remain incompletely understood.

In recent studies, Sirt1 overexpression was originally achieved using chemical activators. These strategies have several limitations. Small-molecule activators such as resveratrol often lack specificity, interacting with other sirtuin family members or unrelated targets, which can result in off-target effects [24,25]. In addition, poor bioavailability and dose-limiting toxicity, as reported for SRT2104, have hindered their clinical translation [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. These challenges highlight the need for alternative approaches capable of achieving robust and sustained Sirt1 activity with improved spatial and temporal control.

Lentiviral vectors offer a powerful alternative for Sirt1 overexpression. Lentiviral-mediated gene transfer enables stable, long-term transgene expression through genomic integration [30,31], a feature particularly advantageous for chronic conditions such as diabetic wounds. Moreover, lentiviral vectors exhibit relatively low immunogenicity compared with adenoviral vectors [32] and allow tissue- or cell-specific targeting, thereby reducing systemic exposure and off-target effects [33]. Importantly, lentiviral-mediated Sirt1 overexpression has been shown to enhance endothelial cell survival and angiogenic function, promoting tissue repair in multiple pathological contexts [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. However, direct delivery of lentiviral vectors to open wounds remains challenging due to rapid diffusion, enzymatic degradation, and limited retention at the target site.

To overcome these limitations, we incorporated lentiviruses encoding Sirt1 into a thermoresponsive poly(polyethylene glycol citrate-co-N-isopropylacrylamide) (PPCN) hydrogel. PPCN undergoes a sol-gel transition at physiological temperature, enabling minimally invasive administration and localized, sustained delivery of therapeutic payloads directly to the wound site [38,39]. This delivery strategy minimizes systemic exposure while maintaining effective local concentrations of lentivirus over time.

Beyond its role as a localized therapeutic delivery vehicle, PPCN possesses intrinsic bioactive properties that are highly relevant to diabetic wound repair. PPCN exhibits antioxidant activity through reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, thereby mitigating oxidative stress within the wound microenvironment [38]. The hydrogel also maintains a moist environment that supports re-epithelialization and granulation tissue formation. Furthermore, PPCN can be functionalized with cell-adhesive peptides to promote cellular infiltration and migration, further enhancing tissue regeneration [39,40].

By combining localized, sustained lentiviral-mediated Sirt1 overexpression with the intrinsic antioxidative and regenerative properties of PPCN, this strategy aims to establish a pro-regenerative wound microenvironment that promotes angiogenesis, modulates inflammation, and suppresses cellular senescence. Building on prior studies that have elucidated aspects of Sirt1 function in wound healing, the present work seeks to define the therapeutic potential and mechanisms of Sirt1 in the context of controlled, localized gene delivery. Simultaneously, we aim to delineate the distinct and synergistic contributions of PPCN to wound repair. Together, this approach advances the development of an integrated biomaterial-based gene therapy platform for diabetic foot ulcers and other chronic wound conditions associated with diabetes. In this work we show that Sirt1 expression and deacetylase activity are decreased in the skin of diabetic mice, PPCN can achieve localized, sustained release of a lentiviral vector and, specifically, the overexpression of Sirt1 via PPCN accelerates wound closure in a splinted skin excision wound diabetic mouse model.

Materials and methods

Materials

Citric acid, polyethylene glycol (PEG), glycerol 1,3-diglycerolate diacrylate (GDD) and N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAAm) were purchased from MilliporeSigma (Burlington, MA, USA). Human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were purchased from Zen-Bio (Durham, NC, USA). The antibodies used included Anti-Sirt1 (#9475) and Anti-β-actin (#4970) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), and Anti-Sirt1 (sc-15404) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). Key reagents and kits were sourced as follows: the BCA Protein Assay Kit from Pierce Thermo Scientific (Rockford, IL, USA); the qPCR Lentivirus Titration Kit from Applied Biological Materials Inc. (Richmond, BC, Canada); the Lenti-X Maxi Purification Kit and Concentrator from ClonTech/Takara Bio (Mountain View, CA, USA); the Cellular Senescence Staining Kit from Cell Biolabs (San Diego, CA, USA); the RNeasy Mini Kit from Qiagen (Hilden, Germany); and the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA, USA). The core plasmids pWPI, pWPI-Sirt1, and pWPI-Sirt1 mutant were procured from Addgene (Watertown, MA, USA). Common chemical reagents such as Polybrene and Hoechst 33342 were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA); D-Luciferin was from Biovision (Milpitas, CA, USA); FuGENE HD Transfection Reagent was from Promega (Madison, WI, USA); and Opti-MEM medium and Pierce RIPA Lysis Buffer were from Life Technologies/Thermo Fisher Scientific (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Modeling and dressing instruments included a 6 mm biopsy punch from Acuderm (Fort Lauderdale, FL, USA), McPherson-Vannas microscissors from World Precision Instruments (Sarasota, FL, USA), and tissue adhesive (Vetbond) and occlusive film (Tegaderm) from 3M (Saint Paul, MN, USA).

Sirt1 expression and deacetylase activity in dermal tissue

Dermal tissues from male Leprdb mice and c57bl/6J mice of age 9-12 weeks old were homogenized in buffer D (20 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 20% glycerol, 0.1 M KCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, and 0.1 mM PMSF) using a dounce tissue homogenizer. Total protein was quantified using BCA assay kit (Pierce Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL). For immunoblotting, cellular proteins were fractionated using Bolt® 4-12% Bis-Tris gel (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA), electro-transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane, and probed using anti-Sirt1 (#9475, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA) and anti-β actin antibodies (#4970, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA). To measure deactylase activity, a method referred to as self-assembled monolayer desorption ionization (SAMDI) mass spectrometry was employed. Briefly, tissue lysates were diluted to 1.1 mg/mL protein concentration and were incubated at 37°C for 3 h or 16 h with nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, trichostatin A, and Ac-GRKAcHYC-NH2 peptides at a final concentration of 1 mM, 50 μM, and 17.4 μM. The reaction mixtures were then quenched with 50 mM nicotinamide and transferred to a 384-gold spotted array plate with maleimide-terminated self-assembled monolayers for peptide immobilization. The plates were subsequently washed with deionized water and ethanol, dried using nitrogen, and treated with matrix (2,4,6-trihydroxyacetophenone, 20 mg/mL in acetone) and analyzed using MALDI-TOF MS. Lysine deacetylase activities were calculated from each spectrum based on the percentage conversion as previous described.

PPCN synthesis and characterization

PPCN (poly (polyethyleneglycol citrate co N-isopropylacrylamide)) was synthesized as previously described. First, citric acid, polyethylene glycol (PEG), and glycerol 1,3-diglycerolate diacrylate (GDD) in a 5:9:1 M ratio was reacted in a polycondensation reaction at 140°C for 45 min by melting under constant stirring to produce poly (polyethyleneglycol citrate) acrylate prepolymer (PPCac). Subsequently, PPCac and N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAAm) were added to a three-necked flask in a 1:1 wt to weight ratio and dissolved in 1,4-dioxane. AIBN radical initiator was added to the PPCac and NIPAAm mixture (final concentration: 6.5 × 10−3 M) and reacted for overnight at 65°C in a nitrogen atmosphere. The resulting PPCN copolymer was dissolved in 1,4-dioxane and purified by precipitation in diethyl ether and vacuum dried. PPCN were gas sterilized using ethylene oxide. Attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectra were obtained from Thermo Nicolet Nexus 870 spectrometer equipped with a Pike horizontal ATR accessory with a covered sample trough, using 8 cm−1 spectral resolution and accumulation of 64 scans, the reflectance element was a ZnSe crystal with ten internal reflections. The viscoelastic properties of neutralized PPCN hydrogels with 100 mg/ml in PBS have been studied in a Discovery® Hybrid DHR-3 (TA, New Castle, DE) rheometer. The analyses were conducted at a frequency of 1.5 Hz and a heating rate of 2 °C/min, using a 1.5% strain and a 50 rad/s angular frequency, in the temperature range from 20°C up to 40°C. Storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) changes of PPCN solutions were recorded.

Production of LV-Sirt1 and LV-Sirt1 mutant

Sirt1 and Sirt1 mutant (Addgene plasmid 1791 and 1792, respectively) were cloned into a lentiviral transfer vector, pWPI. Lentiviral packaging vectors, pMD2.g and psPAX2, were co-transfected with pWPI, pWPI-Sirt1, or pWPI-Sirt1 mutant (H363Y) (mass ratio 1:3:4, respectively) into HEK-293FT cells using Fugene HD at 3:1 total DNA mass to Fugene HD (Promega, Madison, WI) volume ratio complexed in Opti-MEM (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). After 48 h of transfection, the supernatant was collected and purified using Lenti-X Maxi Purification Kit (ClonTech, Mountain View, CA) and subsequently concentrated using Lenti-X concentrator (ClonTech, Mountain View, CA). The lentivirus titer was determined using a qPCR lentivirus titration kit (Applied Biological Materials, Richmond, BC, Canada).

Lentivirus entrapment and release from PPCN

Lentiviruses (2 × 108 particles) were suspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and mixed with sterile PPCN pre-dissolved in PBS (pH 7.4, 100 mg/mL). A volume of 50 μL of the PPCN solution containing lentivirus was dispensed into each well of a 96-well plate. The plate was placed in a 37°C incubator for 30 min for gelation. Gels were subsequently incubated in DMEM plus 10% FBS at 37°C and 5% CO2 to quantify the number of lentiviruses released. At different time points, the medium was removed (supernatant), stored at −80°C, and fresh medium was added to the well. The amount of lentivirus released was determined by a qPCR lentivirus titration kit (Applied Biological Materials, Richmond, BC, Canada). To assess activity of released lentiviruses, lentiviruses encoding for Sirt1 (LV-Sirt1) were mixed with dissolved sterile PPCN (100 mg/mL, pH 7.4 in PBS). Media was collected daily and added to HDFs cultured on tissue culture plastic. HDFs were stained for Sirt1 expression using a fluorescence microscope (TE2000, NIKON) after three days of transduction.

Lentivirus transduction and characterization

HEK-293T, human epithelial keratinocytes and human dermal fibroblasts were transduced with empty vector lentiviruses or lentiviruses encoding for Sirt1 or Sirt1 mutant for 3 days with the presence of 1:1000 polybrene. For immunoblotting, cellular proteins were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA). Proteins were fractionated using Bolt® 4-12% Bis-Tris gel (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA), electro-transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane, and probed using anti-Sirt1 (#9475, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA) and anti-β actin antibodies (#4970, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA). For immunofluorescence, cells were fixed with cold methanol and incubated with anti-Sirt1 antibody (sc-15404, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX). Nuclei were stained with Hoescht 33342 (Sigma, St Louis, MO).

In vivo gene expression from lentivirus-loaded PPCN

In vivo gene expression following subcutaneous injection of lentivirus loaded PPCN (100 μL/injection site) was determined through bioluminescence imaging. PPCN at a concentration of 125 mg/mL was diluted to 100 mg/mL using either 20 μL of lentiviruses encoding for luciferase (LV-Luc, 1 × 106 particles/μL) or 20 μL of PBS. Under deep isoflurane–O2 general anesthesia, each rat received four subcutaneous injections randomly assigned to receive PPCN + LV-Luc or PPCN. Before imaging, animals were anesthetized and received an intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg of body weight of d-luciferin (Biovision, Milpitas, CA). Animals were monitored over time for transgene expression using IVIS imaging system (Caliper Life Sciences, Hopkinton, MA). Constant-size regions of interest over the implant site were used for radiance quantification (photons per second per centimeter square per steradian). Animals were cared for in compliance with the regulations established by the Northwestern University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Evaluation of cell senescence

After transduction, 200 μM hydrogen peroxide was applied to HDFs for 24 h. Cells were processed for senescence assessment using Cellular Senescence Staining Kit (Cell Biolabs, San Diego, CA) following manufacturer's protocol. Cell migration

Cells were seeded on 24-well plates. After transduction, a p200 pipette tip was used to make the scratch by pressing the tip perpendicularly onto the substrate of the well and moving slowly and straightly. Each well was rinsed with 1xPBS 3 times, and the pre-warmed medium was added to each well followed by incubation at 37°C in the incubator. The time point for making scratch was labeled as 0 h and the time point for taking images was labeled as x hours. Images were taken by Cytation-5, areas were measured using ImageJ. Full-thickness excisional dorsal skin wounds creation and treatment

Two dorsal splinted excisional wounds were created in 8-10 weeks old male Leprdb mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, Maine) as previously described by Galiano et al. Briefly, mice were anesthetized and dorsal fur was removed by shaving with an electric clipper followed by application of a depilatory cream. The skin was sterilized by alternating applications of betadine and alcohol swabs. Mice were subcutaneously injected with buprenorphine (0.5 mg/kg). A 6 mm punch biopsy (Acuderm, Fort Lauderdale, FL) was marked with a surgical marker and was used to gently outline two wounds on each side of the mouse's midline. Following the outline, full-thickness wounds were made using a McPherson-Vannas Micro Scissor (World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL). Each mouse received two ethylene oxide gas-sterilized, donut-shaped splints that were positioned such that the wounds were centered within the splint. The splints (OD 16 mm, ID 10 mm) were fabricated from a 1 mm-thick acrylic tape (3M, Saint Paul, MN). Tissue adhesive (Vetbond, 3M, Saint Paul, MN) and interrupted 6-0 nylon sutures were used to fix the splint to the skin. Each wound was randomly assigned to receive either 40 μL of following treatment groups: (1) Saline; (2) PPCN (100 mg/mL, pH 7.4 in PBS); (3) pWPI (LV-control, 2 × 107 particles); (4) Sirt1 (LV-sirt1, 2 × 107 particles); (5) PPCN-pWPI; or (6) PPCN + Sirt1 (100 mg/mL, pH 7.4 in PBS; 2 × 107 particles). After the material gels, an occlusive film (Tegaderm, 3M, Saint Paul, MN) was used to cover the wounds and the animals were placed singly caged and allowed to recover from isoflurane anesthesia. Treatments were reapplied 3 days after initial wounding. Animals were cared for in compliance with the regulations established by the Northwestern University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Wound healing assessment

Digital photographs were taken on the day of the surgery and every 3 days thereafter. Wound area was analyzed by tracing the wound margin and calculating pixel area using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). The wound area at each time point was normalized to the fixed inner area of the splint. The wound closure rate was calculated by the following formula:%woundclosure=100×(1−(woundareasplintarea)Dayx(woundareasplintarea)Day0)

Tissue processing and histology

After complete wound closure, approximately 28 days post-surgery, regenerated dermal tissue was excised and fixed in Neutral Buffered formalin (10%). Tissue samples were embedded in 10% neutral buffered formalin, sections of 5 μm thickness were cut for histological analysis. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded alcohol series, and stained with hematoxylin for 8 min to visualize nuclei, followed by eosin for 2 min to stain cytoplasmic components. This stain was used to assess general tissue architecture and cellular details of the wound healing process. Sections were stained with Weigert's iron hematoxylin for nuclei, Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin for cytoplasm, and aniline blue for collagen. The stain differentiates between collagen and non-collagenous components, useful for evaluating fibrosis.

Assessment of the inflammatory response

After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies against Vimentin (1:100, BD Biosciences), Integrin α3 (1:100, BD Biosciences), Arg-1 (1:100, BD Biosciences), IL-10 (1:100, Abcam), TNF-α (1:100, Abcam), IL-6 (1:100, BD Biosciences), CD86 (1:100, BD Biosciences), and CD163 (1:100, BD Biosciences) overnight at 4°C, respectively. Fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. This protocol allowed visualization of blood vessels and myofibroblasts, indicators of angiogenesis and wound contraction.

Image acquisition and analysis

Stained sections were examined under a light microscope or a fluorescence microscope. Images were captured using a digital camera system attached to the microscope. Quantitative analysis of the stained sections was conducted using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD), images were semi-quantitatively scored (0 = one, 1 = scant, 2 = moderate, and 3 = abundant) by a blinded dermatopathologist for the extent of dermal fibrosis, angiogenesis, and inflammation. Parameters such as epidermal thickness, collagen density, inflammatory cell count, fibroblast presence, blood vessel density, and cellular proliferation were quantitatively assessed and compared across different treatment groups.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from wound tissue samples collected on Day 28 post-treatment using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA quality and integrity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), and samples with RNA integrity numbers (RIN) above 7.0 were selected for library preparation. Libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) and quantified using Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150 bp paired end reads. Raw sequencing reads were trimmed for quality and adapters using Trimmomatic and aligned to the mouse genome (GRCm38) using HISAT2. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2, with a threshold of |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted p-value <0.05 for significance. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment and pathway analyses were performed using the ClusterProfiler package in R, providing insights into biological processes and pathways enriched in PPCN-Sirt1-treated samples. All sequencing data were deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number [to be provided].

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± SEM or mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (La Jolla, CA). Statistical significance between two groups was determined using unpaired Student t tests. For three or more groups, statistical significance was assessed using a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey posthoc test. A level of p ≤ 0.05 was accepted as significant.

PPCN effectively entraps and enables sustained release of bioactive lentiviruses

We first evaluated whether the thermoresponsive PPCN hydrogel could effectively encapsulate lentiviruses and support localized, sustained transgene delivery. PPCN was synthesized by reacting citric acid, PEG and GDD via thermal condensation and followed by free radical polymerization with NIPAAM (Fig. S1a). FT-IR spectroscopy confirmed its chemical structure (Fig. S1b). The rheological analysis at different temperature revealed its lower critical solution temperature (LCST) is 30°C (Fig. S1c). Quantitative analysis of viral release revealed that approximately 50% of encapsulated lentiviruses were released within the first 24 h, followed by a sustained release over the subsequent two days (Fig. 1d). Lentiviruses released during this period retained functional infectivity, with viruses collected on day 1 producing the highest proportion of Sirt1-positive cells following transduction(Fig. S2). These data indicate that PPCN preserves lentiviral bioactivity while enabling controlled release from the polymer network.

To assess sustained and localized transgene expression in vivo, lentiviruses encoding luciferase (LV-Luc) were encapsulated in PPCN and administered via subcutaneous injection, resulting in in situ hydrogel formation at the injection site. Bioluminescence imaging demonstrated robust and localized luciferase expression for at least six weeks, which remained significantly higher than that observed in PPCN only controls at all measured time points (Fig. 1e). Transgene expression peaked at approximately two weeks post-injection and gradually declined, which is consistent with sustained but temporally regulated lentiviral-mediated gene expression (Fig. 1f).

Given the association between diabetes, accelerated cellular senescence, and impaired wound healing, we next evaluated whether Sirt1 overexpression modulates senescence in fibroblasts. Oxidative stress–induced senescence was triggered using hydrogen peroxide, with 200 μM identified as an optimal concentration based on cell viability analysis (Fig. S3). Following lentiviral transduction, cells were exposed to hydrogen peroxide for 24 h and assessed for senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity. Senescent cells exhibited characteristic blue staining and were quantified across groups (Fig. 3e–g). Sirt1-transduced fibroblasts displayed a significantly lower proportion of senescent cells compared with pWPI and Sirt1 mutant groups, demonstrating that Sirt1 overexpression effectively suppresses stress-induced cellular senescence.

PPCN-mediated Sirt1 delivery accelerates wound closure and enhances tissue regeneration in diabetic mice

Full-thickness excisional wounds (6 mm) were created on the dorsal skin of diabetic mice on day 0. Wounds were treated topically with saline, PPCN, Sirt1, or PPCN-Sirt1 (40 μL per wound) on days 0, 3, and 6, with routine wound care maintained throughout the study period. Histological evaluation further demonstrated enhanced tissue regeneration in the PPCN-Sirt1-treated wounds. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed a mean epidermal thickness of approximately 136 μm in the PPCN-Sirt1 group, which was significantly greater than that observed in saline-treated wounds (38 μm) and substantially higher than in wounds treated with Sirt1 alone (81 μm) or PPCN alone (78 μm) (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4d and e). In addition, granulation tissue thickness in the PPCN-Sirt1 group reached approximately 503 μm, representing a 5.1-fold increase compared with saline-treated controls (98 μm) (Fig. 4d–f). Masson's trichrome staining revealed increased collagen deposition and improved collagen organization in PPCN-Sirt1-treated wounds relative to all other groups, indicating enhanced extracellular matrix remodeling. Together, these findings demonstrate that localized delivery of Sirt1 via PPCN significantly promotes re-epithelialization, granulation tissue formation, and matrix remodeling, resulting in accelerated healing of diabetic wounds.

To further assess the therapeutic effect, immunofluorescence staining was performed on wound tissues harvested on day 28 to assess markers associated with fibroblast activation, epidermal migration, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling. Vimentin and integrin α3 were selected as representative markers of activated fibroblasts and keratinocyte migration, respectively. Wounds treated with PPCN-Sirt1 exhibited markedly increased expression of both markers compared with control groups (Fig. 4d). Quantitative analysis revealed that vimentin fluorescence intensity in the PPCN-Sirt1 group was approximately 7.5-fold higher than saline-treated wounds, 3.3-fold higher than PPCN alone, and 2.0-fold higher than Sirt1 alone (Fig. 4g). Similarly, integrin α3 expression was significantly elevated following PPCN-Sirt1 treatment, with fluorescence intensity approximately 33.5-fold higher than saline, 4.5-fold higher than PPCN, and 1.9-fold higher than Sirt1 alone (Fig. 4h).

To distinguish the therapeutic contribution of Sirt1 expression from nonspecific effects of lentiviral exposure or hydrogel-mediated vector delivery, empty-vector controls were further evaluated in parallel. Wounds treated with pWPI alone or PPCN-pWPI did not reproduce the accelerated wound closure, enhanced epidermal regeneration, increased granulation tissue formation, or elevated vimentin and integrin α3 expression observed in the PPCN-Sirt1 group (Fig. S4). These comparisons indicate that the enhanced regenerative response was not attributable to the empty lentiviral backbone or PPCN-assisted vector delivery alone, but was associated with local Sirt1 transgene expression.

To further assess the inflammatory milieu, expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (Arg-1 and IL-10) was examined (Fig. 5c and d). PPCN-Sirt1 treatment resulted in substantially reduced TNF-α and IL-6 staining compared with saline, PPCN, and Sirt1 groups, indicating effective suppression of persistent inflammation (Fig. 5e and f). In contrast, expression of the M2-associated markers Arg-1 and IL-10 was significantly elevated following PPCN-Sirt1 treatment, exceeding levels observed in all control groups (Fig. 5g and h).

Empty-vector controls showed a similar pattern. Neither pWPI nor PPCN-pWPI induced the pronounced increase in the CD163/CD86 ratio observed with PPCN-Sirt1, nor did they reproduce the same degree of pro-inflammatory cytokine suppression or anti-inflammatory marker upregulation (Fig. S5). These data further support that the immunomodulatory effect was driven by functional Sirt1 expression rather than by nonspecific lentiviral delivery or hydrogel exposure.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that localized delivery of Sirt1 via PPCN not only promotes macrophage polarization toward a regenerative M2 phenotype but also reshapes the wound inflammatory environment by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines while enhancing anti-inflammatory signaling. This immunomodulatory shift provides mechanistic support for the accelerated tissue regeneration and improved wound healing observed following PPCN-Sirt1 treatment in diabetic wounds.

Sirt1 treatment upregulated genes involved in immune modulation and oxidative stress regulation, including Spp1, Ccl24, and Ggt1, which are associated with immune cell recruitment and glutathione metabolism. Genes related to epithelial migration and tissue regeneration, such as Krt8, Hpd, Gprc5a, and Myh4, were also elevated, indicating enhanced re-epithelialization and metabolic support within the wound tissue. In addition, Sirt1 modulated remodeling-associated genes, including downregulation of Aspn and Plscr4, which are implicated in fibrosis and vascular dysregulation, and upregulation of Nefm, suggesting a role in maintaining neuronal stability during tissue repair (Fig. 6a). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further revealed that Sirt1-regulated genes were significantly associated with pathways related to response to stimulus, vasculature development, epithelial morphogenesis, and epidermis development (Fig. 6c–S3), supporting a broad regulatory role for Sirt1 across multiple stages of wound healing.

Compared with Sirt1 alone, PPCN-Sirt1 treatment induced a more pronounced and coordinated transcriptional response. PPCN-mediated delivery further amplified the expression of genes involved in intracellular transport, immune regulation, and vascular repair, including Rab23, Cxcl2, and Calcrl (Fig. 6b). GO pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of developmental process, cellular process, sarcomere organization, and epidermis development pathways following PPCN-Sirt1 treatment (Fig. 6d–S6-7). Notably, PPCN-Sirt1 more effectively regulated key genes associated with tissue stability and functional recovery, including Myh6, Nefm, and Rab23, compared with Sirt1 alone.

Collectively, these transcriptomic data demonstrate that Sirt1 orchestrates gene programs critical for inflammation resolution, epithelial regeneration, vascular stability, and tissue remodeling, and that PPCN-mediated delivery further enhances and stabilizes these regulatory effects. This amplified and coordinated gene modulation provides a molecular basis for the accelerated wound closure, improved tissue architecture, and enhanced functional recovery observed following PPCN-Sirt1 treatment.

To further validate selected transcriptomic findings, qPCR analysis was performed for representative Sirt1-responsive genes associated with epithelial repair, immune regulation, metabolic remodeling, and extracellular matrix organization. Compared to pWPI controls, Sirt1 overexpression significantly increased Krt8, Hpd, Ccl24, and Myh4 expression, and markedly reduced Aspn and Plscr4 expression (Fig. S8). Although these assays were performed as targeted transcript-level validation rather than early-time-point in vivo profiling, the results support the RNA-seq-associated interpretation that Sirt1 modulates selected repair-related and inflammatory gene programs.

Discussion

Previous studies have identified SIRT1 as a promising therapeutic target in diabetic wound healing because of its roles in regulating inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial survival, and cellular senescence [[21], [22], [23]]. However, previous studies explored SIRT1 activation using pharmacological modulators, such as resveratrol or SRT compounds, primarily as experimental tools rather than clinically adopted therapies, and these approaches remain limited by poor bioavailability, incomplete specificity, and lack of localized control [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. In parallel, although lentiviral gene delivery offers the possibility of sustained SIRT1 expression, direct administration of viral vectors to open wounds remains challenging because of rapid diffusion, limited retention, and poor spatial confinement [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. In this context, we evaluated a thermoresponsive PPCN hydrogel as a localized delivery platform for sustained Sirt1 transgene expression within the diabetic wound microenvironment. The central contribution of this study is therefore not SIRT1 or PPCN as isolated components, but their integration into a biomaterial-assisted gene delivery platform for diabetic wound repair. By combining the sustained local delivery capacity of PPCN with the pleiotropic regulatory functions of Sirt1, the platform was associated with coordinated improvements in wound closure, inflammation resolution, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling. The consistency of outcomes across in vitro, in vivo, histological, and transcriptomic analyses supports the interpretation that this platform enables a coordinated reparative response in diabetic wound healing.

Because impaired fibroblast function and cellular senescence are major cellular barriers in diabetic wound repair, we first considered whether localized Sirt1 overexpression could improve reparative cell behavior within a biomaterial-supported environment. While previous studies have linked SIRT1 activation to improved oxidative stress resistance and endothelial survival [[21], [22], [23],35], its effect on diabetic fibroblast behavior within a localized biomaterial-assisted delivery system has been less clearly defined. At the cellular level, lentiviral-mediated Sirt1 overexpression significantly enhanced fibroblast migration and suppressed stress-induced senescence in diabetic human dermal fibroblasts. These findings align with Sirt1's established role in regulating p53 acetylation, oxidative stress responses, and cell-cycle control under diabetic or injury-related stress conditions [[41], [42], [43]]. Importantly, these in vitro findings were accompanied by in vivo evidence of improved tissue repair, including increased vimentin expression, elevated integrin α3 levels, enhanced re-epithelialization, and increased granulation tissue formation in PPCN-Sirt1-treated wounds. Transcriptomic analysis further supported these observations through upregulation of genes such as Krt8 and Aspn. Krt8-positive transitional epithelial cells have been shown to facilitate epithelial repair and cellular plasticity during tissue regeneration, while also conferring resistance to oxidative stress through enhanced mitophagy [44,45]. Concurrently, Aspn modulation was consistent with changes in extracellular matrix remodeling and tissue organization during wound repair [44]. Together, these data suggest that PPCN-Sirt1 restores key cellular behaviors essential for effective re-epithelialization and matrix organization in diabetic wounds. Although these findings do not establish a direct causal role for each downstream transcript, they support the view that localized Sirt1 overexpression helps restore fibroblast-associated reparative functions in the diabetic wound environment.

In parallel with cellular repair, restoration of vascularization is essential because impaired angiogenesis is a defining feature of diabetic wounds and a major reason why healing stalls despite infection control or supportive dressings [12,13,23]. Previous studies have attempted to restore vascularization using pro-angiogenic growth factors or stem cell-based interventions, but these approaches often face major limitations, including short half-life, poor retention, and incomplete control over the hostile inflammatory microenvironment [13,35,37]. This is particularly relevant in diabetic wounds, where angiogenic insufficiency rarely occurs in isolation and is typically accompanied by persistent oxidative stress and inflammatory suppression. SIRT1 itself has been implicated in endothelial protection and ischemia-induced neovascularization [23,35,36], yet prior studies have largely examined these effects in simplified cellular or vascular settings rather than within a chronic diabetic wound environment. In our study, PPCN-Sirt1 treatment enhanced vascularization together with improved tissue architecture, suggesting that the therapeutic effect extended beyond endothelial stimulation alone and occurred in parallel with broader wound microenvironment remodeling. Transcriptomic upregulation of Calcrl and Rab23, together with increased granulation tissue formation, supports the view that PPCN-Sirt1 promotes a functionally favorable angiogenic program rather than a transient or disorganized vascular response. Calcrl has been shown to support endothelial barrier integrity and balanced vascular remodeling, while Rab23 regulates trafficking of receptors such as VEGFR2, thereby facilitating endothelial migration and tube formation [[44], [45], [46], [47]]. These observations are consistent with prior reports that Sirt1 promotes angiogenesis via activation of the eNOS/NO pathway and enhancement of VEGF signaling [48,49]. PPCN may have further supported these effects by improving local retention of lentiviral cargo and by providing an antioxidant microenvironment, which is consistent with previous reports showing that antioxidant hydrogels can benefit diabetic wound repair [38].

Although SIRT1 is widely recognized as a regulator of inflammatory signaling, including NF-κB suppression and macrophage polarization [17,19,50], few wound-healing studies have demonstrated that localized SIRT1 gene delivery can reshape the immune microenvironment in vivo in a coordinated and sustained manner. In addition to promoting angiogenesis, PPCN-Sirt1 exerted a pronounced immunomodulatory effect. Macrophage polarization analysis revealed a robust shift toward an M2, pro-regenerative phenotype, accompanied by increased expression of Arg-1 and IL-10 and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6. These findings are consistent with Sirt1's known ability to inhibit NF-κB signaling and promote inflammation resolution [51]. The supplementary pWPI and PPCN-pWPI controls further support this interpretation by distinguishing the effects of viral delivery and hydrogel-assisted delivery from functional Sirt1 overexpression. Compared with PPCN-Sirt1, the pWPI-containing controls did not reproduce the same degree of wound closure, tissue regeneration, fibroblast activation, macrophage polarization, or inflammatory cytokine modulation, indicating that the observed effects require functional Sirt1 expression rather than nonspecific vector or hydrogel exposure. These controls do not resolve every downstream mechanism, but it provides stronger support that the observed therapeutic effects are linked to functional Sirt1 activity rather than nonspecific hydrogel placement or viral exposure alone. Together, these results indicate that PPCN-Sirt1 not only suppresses chronic inflammation but actively reshapes the immune microenvironment to favor tissue repair. Thus, an important aspect of PPCN-Sirt1 is that it not only accelerates wound closure, but also supports measurable changes in the diabetic wound immune microenvironment through sustained local gene delivery.

The role of PPCN itself is also central to the therapeutic design. PPCN is not presented here as an entirely new biomaterial concept; rather, its significance in this study lies in its function as a supportive and bioactive delivery platform. Through thermoresponsive gelation, PPCN helps localize the lentiviral payload at the wound site, reduces rapid loss of vector from the wound bed, and provides a material environment compatible with tissue repair. The added physicochemical characterization further supports this role of PPCN. FT-IR analysis confirmed the successful formation of the PPCN polymer, while rheological testing demonstrated its thermoresponsive sol-gel transition, with an LCST near physiological temperature. These material properties provide a basis for in situ gelation after topical application and help explain how PPCN improves local vector retention and sustained transgene delivery within the wound microenvironment. Many previous SIRT1-related wound studies have focused on isolated aspects of repair, such as endothelial protection or inflammation suppression alone [[21], [22], [23],35]. In contrast, this material context was associated with a coordinated response involving fibroblast activation, macrophage polarization, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling.

This broader response was also reflected in the transcriptomic analysis, including modulation of genes associated with matrix organization such as Aspn (Asporin), a regulator of collagen fibrillogenesis and TGF-β-related signaling that has been implicated in fibrotic remodeling and extracellular matrix organization [[52], [53], [54]]. The regulation of Aspn and related pathways suggests that Sirt1 overexpression in the PPCN scaffold does not simply accelerate matrix deposition but may promote a more organized and regenerative extracellular matrix architecture during diabetic wound healing. This distinction is important because it suggests that the benefit of PPCN-Sirt1 is not limited to faster wound closure, but may also involve improved quality and organization of tissue repair.

Beyond the histological and immunological findings, transcriptomic analysis suggested that PPCN-Sirt1 was associated with coordinated regulation of multiple repair-related programs rather than a single regenerative endpoint. The targeted qPCR validation of selected genes, including Krt8, Ccl24, Hpd, Myh4, Aspn, and Plscr4, further supports the transcriptomic trends associated with Sirt1-mediated regulation of epithelial repair, immune signaling, metabolic remodeling, and extracellular matrix organization. However, these data should be interpreted as supportive validation of selected gene-level changes rather than definitive evidence of early causal pathway activation. By integrating biomaterial-based delivery with molecular regulation, PPCN-Sirt1 represents a synergistic therapeutic platform that addresses the multifactorial nature of diabetic wound healing. However, because this dataset was generated at a late healing stage, it should be interpreted primarily as an integrated endpoint readout rather than a direct map of early mechanistic events. Genes such as Ggt1 and Nefm further suggest possible contributions to oxidative homeostasis and tissue functional recovery [55,56]. These data suggest that PPCN-Sirt1 may also influence tissue functional recovery, although the relevance of neural repair pathways will require further validation.

Despite these strengths, we recognize that the mechanistic depth of this study remains incomplete, particularly regarding how specific immune and vascular pathways are causally linked downstream of Sirt1 overexpression. While our data suggest coordinated regulation of macrophage polarization, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling, the precise signaling networks linking these processes require further investigation. This is particularly relevant because the platform relies on an integrating lentiviral vector, for which local retention alone does not address broader concerns related to systemic exposure, off-target transduction, local toxicity, immune safety, and integration-related risk. In addition, although the db/db mouse model recapitulates several key features of diabetic wound pathology, including impaired angiogenesis and chronic inflammation, differences in skin structure and immune responses between mice and humans remain. Future studies incorporating large-animal models and advanced functional assessments, such as perfusion imaging and single-cell transcriptomics, may further clarify the mechanisms underlying PPCN-Sirt1–mediated regeneration and strengthen the translational relevance of this therapeutic strategy.

This work establishes PPCN-Sirt1 as a biomaterial-assisted gene delivery strategy that coordinates angiogenesis, immune modulation, and tissue regeneration in diabetic wounds. By integrating sustained local Sirt1 transgene delivery with a thermoresponsive bioactive hydrogel platform, this work provides a more complete framework for further preclinical investigation of localized Sirt1 gene delivery in chronic wound repair.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that localized and sustained Sirt1 delivery via a thermoresponsive antioxidant hydrogel (PPCN-Sirt1) is an effective strategy for promoting diabetic wound repair. By integrating controlled gene delivery with a bioactive hydrogel platform, PPCN-Sirt1 accelerated wound closure, enhanced tissue regeneration, and restored key regenerative processes disrupted in diabetic wounds. This approach promoted fibroblast migration, suppressed cellular senescence, increased angiogenesis and collagen remodeling, and reshaped the wound immune microenvironment by favoring anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization. These functional outcomes were accompanied by gene expression changes related to inflammation resolution, epithelial migration, vascular stability, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Although important questions remain regarding wound-site biodistribution, safety, and early pathway-level causality, these findings support PPCN-Sirt1 as a biomaterial-assisted gene delivery platform for diabetic wound repair and provide a basis for further preclinical evaluation in chronic wound regeneration.
