In-hospital cardiac arrest in a Singaporean tertiary hospital: Epidemiology, outcomes, and potential suboptimal end-of-life planning
Authors: Gurangad Singh Chandok, Daryl Jones, Peng Soon Yoon, King Jane Ong, Lip Hoe Koh, Sandra Li Yan Hui, Sumi Marium Saj, Mashithah Mansor, Xiqin Wang, Bryan Choo, Roshni Sadashiv Gokhale, Augustine Tee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the characteristics, antecedents, and outcomes of in-hospital cardiac arrests (IHCAs) in a Singaporean tertiary hospital.DesignWe conducted a retrospective review of electronic medical records of all IHCAs that occurred in the general ward from January to December 2022 at Changi General Hospital. Based on pre-arrest characteristics, IHCAs were categorised as “potential suboptimal end-of-life planning (SELP)”, “potentially predictable”, or “sudden and unexpected”.Main outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were IHCA incidence and survival to hospital discharge. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of potential SELP cases and the frequency of pre-arrest extent of care (EOC) documentation.ResultsThere were 50 IHCAs among 52 617 admissions (0.95/1000 admissions). The median age was 72.5 (interquartile range [IQR], 63–81) years, and 82% had a Clinical Frailty Scale score of ≥ 4. Thirty (60%) arrests fulfilled criteria for potential SELP, while EOC documentation was present in 30% of patients. Five patients (10%) survived to hospital discharge. Factors associated with survival included shorter cardiopulmonary resuscitation duration (p = 0.004), witnessed arrests (p = 0.019), and sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) (p = 0.005).ConclusionsOur data corroborate that witnessed arrests, shorter downtime, and sustained ROSC are associated with better outcomes. However, the high prevalence of potential SELP and low rates of documented EOC reveal critical gaps in end-of-life planning within a frail inpatient population. Earlier, routine EOC discussions may help align care with patient goals, optimising resource utilisation and reducing potentially avoidable IHCAs.

Introduction

In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) is a major clinical event associated with high morbidity and mortality. Globally, the incidence of IHCA ranges from 0.62 to 10 events per 1000 hospital admissions, depending on population characteristics, healthcare system infrastructure, and reporting methodologies.1,2 Despite advances in resuscitation science and the implementation of rapid response systems (RRSs), survival outcomes remain poor, with in-hospital survival rates to discharge varying between 12% and 25% in large registry studies from Japan, Australia, and North America.[2], [3], [4] Moreover, among survivors, a substantial proportion experience long-term neurological deficits or poor functional recovery.[5], [6], [7]

Singapore presents a distinct and paradoxical context for the study of IHCA. As a high-income nation and city-state with one of the fastest-ageing populations globally, where one in four citizens will be aged 65 or above by 2030, up from 1 in 10 in 2010,8 Singapore operates a highly centralised public healthcare system with acute care capabilities comparable to those of Australia, New Zealand, and North America. Yet this advanced medical infrastructure exists within a multiethnic Asian society where end-of-life decision-making is fundamentally shaped by values that diverge from those underpinning Western resuscitation practice. Unlike Western healthcare models, where individual patient autonomy and early extent of care (EOC) documentation are routine, clinical practice in Singapore frequently encounters a paradigm of “relational autonomy”, in which medical decisions are a collective family responsibility rather than an individual choice.9 Cultural attitudes towards death as a taboo, particularly within Chinese, Malay, and Indian communities, frequently result in the avoidance or deferral of advance care planning (ACP) discussions. Deeply rooted values of filial piety may further compel families to demand maximal interventions, including cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), even for elderly patients with advanced, irreversible comorbidities, as withdrawing treatment may be perceived as the abandonment of a parental duty.10,11 Despite the “Living Matters” National ACP programme, launched in 2011, uptake remains inconsistent across care settings, and clinicians report persistent barriers to initiating EOC conversations.12 These sociocultural challenges are not unique to Singapore, but reflect patterns observed across East Asian healthcare systems, including Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan, where Confucian values and family-centred paradigms similarly delay timely EOC documentation.13 Taken together, these structural and cultural features create conditions in which potentially avoidable IHCAs may occur in patients for whom resuscitation is neither beneficial nor concordant with their values or prognosis.

Despite this, local IHCA data remain scarce. Existing Singaporean studies have examined resuscitation outcomes in specific settings14,15 but none have simultaneously characterised frailty, EOC documentation rates and potential suboptimal end-of-life planning (SELP) in a single cohort. A recent Australian multicentre study described a classification system categorising IHCAs into three groups – “possible SELP”, “potentially predictable”, and “sudden and unexpected”, based on pre-arrest clinical characteristics, reporting that approximately one-third of IHCAs in hospitals with mature medical emergency teams (METs) were classified as possible SELP, suggesting that SELP may be a contributing factor in a substantial proportion of arrests.16 Applying this categorisation to a Singaporean tertiary hospital allows us to examine whether the association between SELP and IHCA outcomes, first described in an Australian context, is similarly observed in a non-Western cultural environment.

This study aimed to evaluate the epidemiology, arrest characteristics, and outcomes of IHCAs at Changi General Hospital (CGH), a 1000-bed tertiary public hospital in Singapore, including the prevalence of SELP, pre-arrest EOC documentation, and factors associated with survival to discharge.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study to review all IHCAs that occurred from 1st January to 31st December 2022 at CGH.

CGH is a 1000-bed tertiary referral hospital and academic medical institution, providing over 23 medical and surgical specialties to a catchment population of close to one million people in eastern Singapore. As a general tertiary hospital, CGH serves as a high-volume acute centre, managing a comprehensive range of acute internal medicine, oncology, and common surgical subspecialties with on-site intensive care and high-dependency units. Select quaternary services (e.g., cardiac surgery, complex neurosurgery, and transplantation) are referred to co-located national specialty centres within the SingHealth cluster, such as the National Heart Centre Singapore and National Neuroscience Institute. This structure, alongside its mature, hospital-wide rapid response system, is broadly comparable to large metropolitan tertiary public teaching hospitals in Australia and New Zealand, which similarly centralise certain quaternary services at designated national or state centres and remain entirely distinct from low-acuity community or intermediate-care facilities.

In Singapore and at CGH, ACP is a document that formally documents a patient's preferences for care in the event of deterioration, and this is generally documented prior to hospital admission. In contrast, an EOC documentation is a documented discussion that a hospital staff member has had with the patient or their loved ones regarding escalation and goals of care after hospital admission. Finally, do not resuscitate (DNR) status is a specific term that stipulates that the patient should not receive CPR in the event of cardiac arrest.

All code-blue activations during 2022 were reviewed, focusing strictly on patients with true IHCA who received CPR. All patients with true IHCA in the general ward were included regardless of age or admitting discipline if the patient received CPR. Patients were excluded if (1) IHCA occurred outside the general ward setting in areas of pre-existing higher level of monitoring (emergency department [ED], operating theatre [OT], intensive care unit [ICU], high dependency [HD] unit); (2) no CPR was performed; (3) “cardiac arrest” was attributed to mimics such as with hypoglycaemia, stroke, or hypotension with a pulse; or (4) a DNR order was present. For patients with multiple IHCAs, only the first IHCA was reviewed and included in the study.

Medical emergency team system

CGH operates a hospital-wide rapid response system (RRS) served by the medical rmergency team (MET) available to all non-ICU wards, which can be activated by any clinical staff member, including allied health professionals and doctors, upon the fulfilment of one or more predefined criteria. These criteria encompass airway compromise (threatened airway, noisy breathing, or stridor); respiratory deterioration (acute change in respiratory rate to less than 8 or greater than 30 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation below 90% despite supplemental oxygen, or inspired oxygen fraction requirements exceeding 50%); circulatory instability (acute change in heart rate to below 40 or above 130 beats per minute, or an acute drop in systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg); and neurological deterioration (acute decline in Glasgow Coma Scale score of greater than 2 points, or repeated or prolonged seizure activity exceeding 5 min). MET activation could additionally be triggered by clinical staff concern regarding a patient's overall status, irrespective of the above criteria.

Details of data collected

We collected data on patient demographics (age, gender, ethnicity, Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI],17 Clinical Frailty Scale [CFS], and Hospital Frailty Risk Score [HFRS]18), the presence of metastatic cancer and any chronic end-stage disease, the date, and time of admission, recent hospitalisation in last 30 days (Yes/No), place of residence, employment status, and admitting discipline (Medical or Surgical). We also recorded whether the patient had an established ACP or EOC documentation during prior admissions and during the current admission prior to IHCA.

Details pertaining to the cardiac arrest included the date, time and day of the week, location of arrest, the days between admission and arrest, whether the arrest was witnessed or monitored, initial rhythm (asystole, pulseless electrical activity [PEA], ventricular tachycardia [VT], ventricular fibrillation [VF], other), number of defibrillations, number of adrenaline doses, duration of CPR, and return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) details. Sustained ROSC was defined as the first instance of ROSC maintained for more than 20 min. Intermittent ROSC was defined as recurrent arrest within 20 min of initial ROSC.

In addition, we conducted a detailed assessment to evaluate objective warning signs and markers of instability in the 24 h prior to the IHCA. We reviewed the time of the last vital sign recorded prior to arrest, whether the patient breached any MET activation criteria in the 24 h before arrest and whether MET was activated, details of the exact MET criteria breached, the number of MET calls during admission, the presence of serum potassium and bicarbonate measurements in the 24 h prior to arrest and any major anomalies in their levels, the presence of oxygen supplementation and the lowest oxygen saturation prior to arrest. Lastly, we collected outcome data on hospital and ICU length of stay (LOS), final outcome (died during arrest, died in hospital/ICU, discharged home, transferred to another facility), and functional status at the time of discharge. Functional status was adjudicated via a comprehensive retrospective review of serial multidisciplinary clinical documentation recorded by medical, nursing, physiotherapy, and occupational therapy teams at admission and prior to hospital discharge.

Characterisation and classification of IHCAs

To investigate mechanisms by which IHCAs in Singaporean hospitals could be further reduced, we assessed the proportion of IHCAs within each of the following three categories: (i) potential SELP; (ii) potentially predictable; and (iii) sudden and unexpected. These three categories were chosen and defined, based on a previous IHCA study by Jones et al16 The criteria for SELP included: admission from high-level residential care (nursing home); a CCI >5; or the presence of at least two of the following: age >75 years, CCI >3, metastatic cancer, documented limitation of medical treatment, dependency in activities of daily living, or admission from low-level residential care or supported accommodation. The criteria for SELP were intended to highlight clinical conditions that might have prompted the need for discussion and documentation of EOC. Similarly, the criteria for potentially predictable IHCAs reflected either suboptimal activation of or response to the MET when objective deterioration was present, or suboptimal correction of electrolyte changes that are known to be associated with IHCA.

Data analysis

Data were analysed according to an analysis plan that was decided upon prior to the study. Each patient's electronic medical record was reviewed manually to collect data for the study. Numerical data are presented as numbers and percentages. All continuous data were non-normally distributed and are presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR). Differences in non-normally distributed data were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and differences in categorical data were analysed with the Fisher exact test. A two-sided p < 0.05 was taken to indicate statistical significance.

Ethical approval

This project was reviewed and approved as an audit aimed at quality assurance and service improvement, and as such an IRB waiver was granted by the research office of CGH. Informed consent was not required as this was a retrospective chart review that required no patient contact.

Features and outcomes of arrest

Among the 50 IHCAs, 22 (44%) occurred during the daytime (08:00 to 17:00 h), 14 (28%) occurred during the evening (17:00 to 00:00 h), and another 14 (28%) occurred during the overnight period (00:00 to 08:00 h). Nineteen (38%) of IHCAs occurred during the weekend and 31 (62%) occurred during weekdays (Table 3). The most common initial rhythm was nonshockable (PEA in 56% and asystole in 30% of patients), and a small proportion (10%) had an initial shockable rhythm (Table 4). The median duration of CPR was 30 min among nonsurvivors vs 13 min for those who survived (p = 0.004). ROSC was achieved and sustained in 23 out of 50 (46%) patients with (Table 4), and five patients (10%) survived to discharge. All five patients who survived to discharge had a witnessed cardiac arrest (p = 0.019) and achieved sustained ROSC (p = 0.012). In the group of patients who died only 40% had a witnessed IHCA (Table 4). Patients who survived had their last vital signs recorded a median of 5 min prior to arrest, whereas those who did not survive had vital signs recorded a median of 73 min prior to arrest (Table 4). Among the 45 patients (90%) who died, on 20 (40%) occasions death occurred in the ICU. Among the five survivors, functional status at discharge was lower than their prearrest status in all cases. Discussion

Summary of findings

We conducted a retrospective review of IHCAs at a Singaporean tertiary hospital. There were 50 IHCAs in the general ward among 52,617 admissions in 2022. Most patients were admitted under a medical team and had a nonshockable rhythm. A substantial proportion were elderly, with comorbidities and frailty. Despite 60% of the patients fulfilling criteria for SELP, EOC documentation and ACP uptake were infrequent.

Comparison with previous studies

Our IHCA incidence of 0.95 per 1000 admissions aligns with the 0.88–1.94 per 1000 range reported for Singaporean tertiary centres,14,19 yet sits below the broader 1.31–6.11 per 1000 range pooled in large Australian and New Zealand systematic reviews, such as that by Fennesy et al.20 This represents an apparent paradox: given that only 30% of the cohort had pre-arrest EOC documentation, a higher incidence of IHCA might be expected in this frail, multimorbid population. This variance is largely methodological. A substantial proportion of the higher incidence rates reported in historical Australian and New Zealand registries stem from broad case definitions that capture hospital-wide events. Conversely, when case definitions are strictly limited to multiday general ward admissions, excluding patients with active DNR orders or those arresting in specialised areas such as the HD, ED, OT, contemporary Australia and New Zealand data demonstrate a significantly lower true ward incidence of 0.62 per 1000 admissions.2 Because our cohort applied these identical selective inclusion criteria and isolated true code-blue activations outside the ICU, our numerator was naturally narrowed relative to registries utilising all-encompassing institutional definitions.

Structurally, we hypothesise that, in the absence of timely EOC discussions, clinicians may lower their threshold for transferring deteriorating patients to monitored or intensive care settings earlier in their clinical decline. This early escalation may, in part, be influenced by family expectations of maximal intervention rooted in filial piety. Consequently, some potentially avoidable arrests may occur within the ICU rather than on general wards, excluding them from our cohort. While we lacked the data to test this hypothesised dynamic directly, it reinforces our central conclusion: a lower ward-based IHCA incidence does not necessarily reflect optimal end-of-life planning. Rather, it suggests that the clinical burden of delayed EOC discussions is redistributed elsewhere within the acute care pathway.

Among the IHCAs in our cohort, the predominant initial rhythm was a nonshockable rhythm (90%), consistent with international registry data.21 The median CPR duration of 30 min in nonsurvivors, whilst longer than the 21-min median reported in North American registries,22 is highly congruent with resuscitation practices observed in East Asian healthcare settings. For instance, a Taiwanese study of 1525 IHCA cases reported a matching median CPR duration of 30 min when the family was absent, attributing prolonged resuscitation efforts to deep-seated Confucian values of filial piety and family-centred models of care.23 Within these sociocultural frameworks, terminating resuscitation early can precipitate severe familial conflict, create profound moral distress for clinicians or be perceived by relatives as the premature abandonment of care. In Singapore's acute hospitals, 30 min operates as an implicit institutional threshold beyond which further resuscitation is universally deemed futile, with cessation following emergent discussion between the responding team and the family. This clinical pattern, while pragmatic under acute duress, further underscores the urgent mandate for proactive, pre-arrest EOC discussions and timely documentation on general wards.

Interpretation of findings

Despite the presence of frailty, advanced age, and life-limiting illnesses, EOC documentation was only apparent in 30% of patients, and only 1 out of the 50 patients had an ACP. Our data suggest that 60% of the patients who arrested had potential SELP. This proportion is in keeping with the existing evidence of delayed end-of-life discussions and low ACP documentation in Asia.24 Numerically, there was no difference in survival (10%) between the three categories of IHCA in our cohort, likely reflecting the limited statistical power of this single-centre cohort. In addition, ESRF was the most common comorbidity in the overall population and was more prominent among the patients who had potential SELP. Patients with ESRF are known to have poor outcomes after cardiac arrest, with lower survival rates, higher in-hospital mortality, and likelihood of discharge to skilled nursing facilities compared to the general population.[25], [26], [27] Given the poor postarrest survival in ESRF and patients with SELP, the low rate of EOC discussions in our cohort may represent missed opportunities to better align care with prognosis and patients’ values.

Early warning systems and timely MET activation may help identify and manage deteriorating patients, potentially reducing the risk of some IHCAs. However, not all ward arrests are preventable, and MET activation alone does not guarantee improved outcomes. Our data showed that although MET activation criteria were breached nine times in the 24 h prior to IHCAs in our cohort, only one patient had a MET activation in the 24 h prior to arrest. Notably, there were 807 MET calls during 2022 (15.3/1000 admissions), providing context for this finding. A relative underuse of the MET may result in fewer escalation of care and a potentially higher risk of deterioration and a greater likelihood of unmonitored and unwitnessed IHCAs, which are associated with poorer survival and neurological outcomes.28 In our study, witnessed cardiac arrest was a significant prognostic factor for survival, as all five survivors had a witnessed cardiac arrest.

It is well-recognised that the likelihood of survival and favourable functional outcomes exponentially declines exponentially with longer periods of CPR before ROSC.29 Our study is consistent with these findings – the median CPR duration was 30 min in nonsurvivors vs 13 min in survivors. Although survival following IHCA was achieved in five patients, none returned to their prearrest functional baseline prior to discharge. All survivors experienced some degree of functional decline, with previously independent patients becoming partially dependent and previously partially dependent patients becoming fully dependent. These findings highlight the morbidity of IHCA and emphasise the importance of evaluating outcomes beyond survival alone and underscore the need for postarrest rehabilitation.

Implications for clinicians and policy makers

Our data underscore the need to routinely screen and engage patients with potential SELP in early EOC discussions. When establishing goals of care, clinicians should consider patients’ values, comorbidities, frailty, and family needs, recognising that resuscitation may not be beneficial or concordant with patient preferences in a significant proportion of patients.1 Early goals-of-care discussions are among the steps recommended by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) to improve IHCA outcomes.1

Secondly, our data highlight the need to create and sustain awareness regarding the presence and utility of the MET and to encourage healthcare teams to activate it for early deterioration in the inpatient population so that patients at risk can be closely monitored. While earlier activation of the MET does not guarantee the prevention of an arrest, it provides an operational window to optimise clinical monitoring, refine goals of care discussions, or facilitate a structured escalation to a higher level of care. In Asian healthcare settings, hierarchical workplace dynamics and a high-power-distance culture have been associated with reluctance among junior doctors and nursing staff to escalate deteriorating patients to the MET, even when clinically indicated.[30], [31], [32] This cultural barrier to timely escalation is particularly relevant in Singapore and represents a modifiable systemic factor that should be addressed through structured escalation protocols and the cultivation of a culture that supports early escalation.

Study strengths and limitations

Strengths include the detailed characterisation of patient demographics, frailty, comorbidities, and arrest circumstances within a single cohort. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to apply the ANZ-CODE SELP categorisation outside Australia, contributing to its external validation in an Asian sociocultural context.

Limitations include the single-centre retrospective design and small sample size, which restricted statistical power and generalisability. SELP classification was performed retrospectively after the arrest, introducing potential hindsight bias – patients with poor outcomes may appear more predictably inappropriate for CPR than they would have at the time of clinical decision-making. This is an inherent limitation of retrospective SELP classification and has been acknowledged in the original ANZ-CODE methodology. Adjudication of SELP retains inherent subjectivity, as evidenced by three patients classified as potential SELP who survived to hospital discharge. Cultural and institutional factors specific to CGH may further limit generalisability to other Asian healthcare settings. Furthermore, our strict exclusion of patients with pre-existing DNR orders significantly deflates our reported IHCA incidence relative to major international registries, which typically include all IHCA regardless of code status. By restricting our cohort strictly to patients who received active CPR, we acknowledge the inherent circularity when evaluating end-of-life planning failures. Finally, we acknowledge that our focus on prearrest MET compliance addresses only the afferent limb of the hospital's Rapid Response System. IHCA is a complex multisystem event, while a MET call represents the critical pivot between identifying deterioration and deploying a specialised resuscitation team, it is not a panacea. Not all clinical deterioration or ward-based arrests are inherently predictable or preventable, and afferent limb failure is merely one component of a multifaceted system. Given our final sample size of 50 arrests and 5 survivors, all statistical evaluations are descriptive and associative, and no direct causality should be inferred from our data.

Conclusion

In our institution, the incidence of true general ward-based IHCA was approximately one per 1000 hospital admissions. Standard resuscitation metrics, including a witnessed arrest, shorter CPR duration, and sustained return of spontaneous circulation, remain strongly associated with survival to hospital discharge. Our findings highlight a high prevalence of potential suboptimal end-of-life planning and persistently low EOC documentation within a frail, multimorbid inpatient population, reflecting deep-seated socio-cultural barriers to early end-of-life discussions in Singapore. Earlier and more routine extent of care discussions, alongside targeted efforts to address cultural and systemic barriers to MET activation, are crucial to align acute care with patient values and potentially reduce the frequency of avoidable ward-based cardiac arrests in Singapore and similar healthcare settings.
