Terminal Matters: Kinodynamic Planning with a Terminal Cost and Learned Uncertainty in Belief State-Cost Space
Zhuoyun Zhong, Seyedali Golestaneh, Constantinos Chamzas

Abstract
In many real-world robotic tasks, robots must generate dynamically feasible motions that reliably reach desired goals even under uncertainty. Yet existing sampling-based kinodynamic planners typically optimize running costs accumulated along trajectories and treat goal reaching as a feasibility check, rather than explicitly optimizing terminal-state quality, such as goal preference or goal-reaching reliability. In this work, we introduce Kinodynamic planning with a Terminal cost, termed KiTe . It augments AO-RRT with a terminal-cost objective to optimize terminal-state quality alongside trajectory running cost. We provide a rigorous proof that this augmented AO-RRT formulation preserves asymptotic optimality. Furthermore, we extend the formulation to belief space and prove that minimizing the Wasserstein distance between the terminal belief and the goal improves a lower bound on the probability of reaching the goal region. To support systems without analytical uncertainty models, we learn dynamics and process uncertainty directly from data and integrate the learned belief dynamics into planning. We validate the proposed formulation with experiments on Flappy Bird, Car Parking, and Planar Pushing with learned belief dynamics in both simulation and the real world. Compared with baselines without terminal costs, KiTe better satisfies goal preferences and consistently achieves higher goal-reaching success under uncertainty. Source code is available at https://github.com/elpis-lab/KiTe .

II
Related Work

Motion planning under uncertainty for dynamical systems has been studied through sampling-based kinodynamic planning, uncertainty-aware motion planning, and learning-based dynamics and uncertainty modeling.
Our work lies at the intersection of these directions.

II-A

Kinodynamic Motion Planning

Kinodynamic motion planning addresses the problem of generating dynamically feasible trajectories that satisfy both geometric constraints and system dynamics.
These methods are suitable for robotic systems with complex dynamics including mobile robots
[
10
]
, drones
[
24
]
, and other systems with nontrivial constraints
[
9
,
42
]
.
Sampling-based methods, such as Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) and their variants, have become a dominant paradigm due to their probabilistic completeness and scalability in high-dimensional spaces
[
7
]
.

To improve solution quality, asymptotically optimal kinodynamic planners have been developed. Methods such as SST
[
23
]
and DIRT
[
24
]
achieve asymptotic optimality without requiring a steering function by maintaining a sparsified tree structure and pruning suboptimal nodes.
More generally, the AO-
x
framework
[
9
]
reformulates optimal kinodynamic planning as a sequence of feasible planning problems in an augmented state-cost space. Building on this,
[
16
]
formally proves asymptotic optimality of AO-RRT with running cost objectives.

Despite these advances, existing optimal planners primarily consider objectives defined as running costs along trajectories.
This limitation restricts the ability of planners to explicitly encode preferences over terminal states, such as goal quality, downstream task cost, or preferences among multiple feasible goal regions.
This gap motivates our extension of AO-RRT to incorporate terminal cost with formal guarantees.

II-B

Uncertainty-Aware Motion Planning

Uncertainty-aware planning aims to explicitly reason about uncertainty in system dynamics, sensing, and the environment.
Sampling-based approaches have been successfully adapted to this setting by planning in belief space, where a belief represents a probability distribution over the underlying state.
Belief-space planners maintain a search tree of belief nodes under uncertainty
[
4
]
, and Gaussian belief tree
[
10
]
extends it to kinodynamic planning with chance constraints for partially observable systems.
A key challenge in belief-space planning is defining a valid metric, for which metrics such as the 2-Wasserstein distance have been proposed
[
25
]
.
POMDP-based methods further address long-horizon and online decision making under uncertainty
[
19
,
20
,
12
]
.

A parallel line of work focuses on incorporating risk and robustness into the planning process.
For example, chance-constrained planners
[
27
,
37
]
enforce safety constraints to avoid collisions and terminal constraints to reach the goal region with high probability.
Similarly, risk-aware planners incorporate uncertainty through different representations, including risk maps for perception uncertainty
[
15
]
, ambiguity sets for distributionally robust planning
[
36
]
, and Monte Carlo estimation for trajectory optimization
[
14
]
.
Other work provides robustness for uncertain nonlinear systems
[
41
]
, optimizes collision probability under environment uncertainty
[
26
]
, and reasons about stochastic robustness for temporal-logic specifications
[
13
]
.

However, existing sampling-based planners focus on feasibility, chance constraints, or trajectory-wise costs in belief space, such as cumulative length or risk.
The role of terminal cost in belief space, which captures the quality of the final belief state, remains underexplored with theoretical guarantees.

II-C

Learning Dynamics and Uncertainty Models

Accurate modeling of system dynamics and uncertainty is critical for effective planning under uncertainty, particularly in complex and contact-rich domains.
Learning-based approaches provide a flexible alternative to analytical models by fitting both dynamics and uncertainty directly from data
[
1
,
42
]
.
Gaussian process (GP) models, as used in learning-based task and motion planning
[
40
]
, provide uncertainty estimates and enable sampling of actions with high success rate.
In deep learning settings, aleatoric uncertainty can be learned through probabilistic loss such as negative log-likelihood (NLL) or its variants, e.g.,
\beta
-NLL
[
33
]
.
In low-data regimes, modeling epistemic uncertainty is also important for safe and informative planning. This can be achieved using approximate Bayesian methods such as Monte Carlo dropout
[
6
]
, with learned embeddings
[
11
]
, or through learned uncertainty estimators
[
28
]
.
More recently, evidential regression
[
2
]
has been proposed to jointly capture both uncertainties within a unified framework.

Learning-based models have been integrated with planning frameworks to enable uncertainty-aware decision making
[
42
,
40
]
.
However, such integrations use learned uncertainty only to bias action selections, rather than being tightly integrated with belief-space planners to explicitly propagate the belief dynamics.
In this work, we show how to train a neural network with a standard NLL objective to acquire the required belief dynamics model suitable for belief-space planning.

III
Problem Formulation

In this work, we consider kinodynamic planning problems under uncertainty.
Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{U}
be the state space and control space respectively. The state space
\mathcal{X}
is partitioned into two disjoint subsets

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{\text{obs}}\;\cup\;\mathcal{X}_{\text{free}}

, where
\mathcal{X}_{\text{obs}}
is the obstacle region and
\mathcal{X}_{\text{free}}
is the collision-free region.
The system dynamics is given by the ordinary differential equation:

\dot{x}(t)=f\big(x(t),u(t)\big),

(1)

where
t\in[0,{\mathcal{T}}]
denotes time over the horizon
\mathcal{T}
,
x(t)\in\mathcal{X}
is the system state and
u(t)\in\mathcal{U}
is the corresponding control
[
29
]
.

III-A

Kinodynamic Planning:

The
kinodynamic planning problem
is to determine a control function
\boldsymbol{u}:[0,{\mathcal{T}}]\to\mathcal{U}
over a time horizon
{\mathcal{T}}
such that the generated state trajectory
\boldsymbol{x}:[0,{\mathcal{T}}]\to\mathcal{X}
satisfies:

\begin{gathered}x(0)=x_{\text{start}},\;x({\mathcal{T}})\in\mathcal{G},\\
x(t)\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{free}},\;u(t)\in\mathcal{U},\;\dot{x}(t)=f\big(x(t),u(t)\big),\;\forall t\in[0,{\mathcal{T}}].\end{gathered}

(2)

where
x_{\text{start}}
denotes the starting state, and
\mathcal{G}
denotes the goal region of the problem. For better readability, we use
x(\cdot)\equiv\boldsymbol{x}(\cdot)
and
u(\cdot)\equiv\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot)
.

In optimal kinodynamic motion planning, the planner also tries to optimize an objective function
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})
while searching for a feasible trajectory.
This objective typically measures the quality of a trajectory, which is expressed as an integral cost
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
accumulated along the trajectory:

\mathcal{C}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})=\int_{0}^{{\mathcal{T}}}\ell\big(x(t),u(t)\big)\,dt,

(3)

where
\ell:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the running cost (e.g. path length or energy consumption).

In this work, we further consider a
terminal cost
term
\phi
to optimize terminal-state quality, and consider the total cost
\mathcal{C}_{T}
:

\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})=\int_{0}^{{\mathcal{T}}}\ell\big(x(t),u(t)\big)\,dt+\phi\big(x({\mathcal{T}})\big),

(4)

where
\phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a terminal cost function that is evaluated based on the endpoint
x({\mathcal{T}})
.

III-B

Kinodynamic Planning in Belief Space

Under uncertainty, the system evolution is no longer fully deterministic.
Instead of planning directly in the state space
\mathcal{X}
, we plan in the
belief space

\mathcal{B}
, where each belief
b\in\mathcal{B}
represents a probability distribution over
\mathcal{X}
that captures the system’s uncertainty about its true state.
We assume that the belief over the true (but unknown) state can be approximated by a Gaussian distribution
b=\mathcal{N}(x,P)
, parameterized by its mean
x\in\mathcal{X}
and covariance
P\in{\mathbb{S}}
, where
{\mathbb{S}}
denotes the space of symmetric positive definite matrices.

The belief evolves according to Gaussian propagation under process and observation noise
[
39
]
. In this work, we consider open-loop planning and focus on process uncertainty, omitting the observation update, such that the belief is propagated purely through the belief dynamics model:

\dot{b}(t)=f_{b}(b(t),u(t)),

(5)

where
f_{b}
represents the combined effect of motion dynamics with process uncertainty.
This form is analogous to the deterministic system dynamics in
Eq. 1
, but now the state variable
x(t)
is replaced by the belief
b(t)
, which encodes both the expected state and its associated uncertainty.
The specific belief propagation models used in this work are introduced later in
Sec. V-C
.
Additionally, we demonstrate in
Sec. VII
that such a model can be directly learned from interaction data.

The
belief-space kinodynamic planning problem
seeks a belief trajectory
\boldsymbol{b}:[0,{\mathcal{T}}]\!\to\!\mathcal{B}
generated by a control function
\boldsymbol{u}
such that, with high probability, the true trajectory
\boldsymbol{x}
induced by
\boldsymbol{b}
satisfies the kinodynamic feasibility conditions in
Eq. 2
.
In particular, we require the control and dynamics constraints to hold as in the deterministic setting, while collision avoidance and goal satisfaction are expressed probabilistically
[
27
,
10
]
:

\begin{gathered}x(0)=x_{\text{start}},\\
u(t)\in\mathcal{U},\;\dot{x}(t)=f\big(x(t),u(t)\big),\;\forall t\in[0,{\mathcal{T}}],\\
\Pr\!\big(x(t)\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{free}}\big)\geq p_{\text{free}},\;\forall t\in[0,{\mathcal{T}}],\\
\Pr\!\big(x({\mathcal{T}})\in\mathcal{G}\big)\geq p_{\text{goal}}.\end{gathered}

(6)

where
p_{\text{free}}
and
p_{\text{goal}}
are the thresholds for collision-free execution and goal-reaching success, respectively.
Intuitively, the planner searches for a belief trajectory whose induced distribution over true trajectories remains largely within the feasible region of the deterministic problem.

Similar to
Eq. 4
, the belief-space objective evaluates trajectory quality at the distribution level, through both a running cost and a terminal cost:

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{u})=\int_{0}^{{\mathcal{T}}}{w}\big(b(t),u(t)\big)\,dt+\psi\big(b({\mathcal{T}})\big),

(7)

where
{w}:\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the running cost in belief space and
\psi:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the terminal belief cost.
For readability, we use
b(\cdot)\equiv\boldsymbol{b}(\cdot)
.
A key contribution of this work is to show that AO-RRT can optimize objectives with an explicit terminal cost (
Eq. 4
and
Eq. 7
), while preserving asymptotic optimality.

IV
KiTe
: AO-RRT with A Terminal Cost

In this section, we first introduce the core planning algorithm for
KiTe
, AO-RRT, originating from the AO-x family
[
9
]
.
Although the original AO-x framework defines an optimization objective that includes a terminal cost, its asymptotic optimality analysis relies on the assumption that the underlying RRT is well-behaved, yet no rigorous proof is given when a terminal cost is present.
Moreover, this term is not incorporated or evaluated in any of its experiments.
A later extension
[
16
]
establishes a formal asymptotic optimality proof for AO-RRT, but only for objectives consisting of an integrable running cost.

We extend previous theoretical analyses
[
9
,
16
]
by demonstrating that a terminal cost can be incorporated into AO-RRT while preserving asymptotic optimality.
Our analysis provides a complete and rigorous proof for optimizing both running and terminal costs under mild regularity assumptions.

IV-B

Proof Preliminary

For simplicity and clarity, in this proof, we adopt the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
when measuring differences in state, control, and dynamics, and the absolute value
|\cdot|
when measuring differences in cost. The results naturally extend to the weighted norm or other valid metric
[
16
]
that satisfies the Lipschitz continuity assumptions below.
For any measurable set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mathcal{V}\!\left[S\right]
denote its Lebesgue measure, i.e., its
d
-dimensional volume.
For any state
x\in\mathcal{X}
and radius
r>0
, we denote the
r
-radius closed ball centered at
x
as:

B_{r}(x)=\{\,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\mid\|x-x^{\prime}\|\leq r\,\}.

(10)

We begin by stating the assumptions required for the optimality proof. Specifically, we assume Lipschitz continuity for the system dynamics and objective functions.

Assumption 1.
(Lipschitz Continuity of the System Dynamics)
:

The system dynamics
f
is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous in both the state and control variables. Formally,
\exists K^{f}_{x},K^{f}_{u}\geq 0
such that,
\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
:

\begin{gathered}\|f(x,u)-f(x^{\prime},u)\|\leq K^{f}_{x}\|x-x^{\prime}\|,\\
\|f(x,u)-f(x,u^{\prime})\|\leq K^{f}_{u}\|u-u^{\prime}\|.\end{gathered}

(11)

This assumption ensures that small perturbations in the state or control lead to proportionally bounded variations in the system dynamics.

Assumption 2.
(Lipschitz Continuity of the Running Cost)
:

The running cost
\ell(x,u)
is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous, that is,

\exists K^{\ell}_{x},K^{\ell}_{u}\geq 0
such that
\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
:

\begin{gathered}|\ell(x,u)-\ell(x^{\prime},u)|\leq K^{\ell}_{x}\|x-x^{\prime}\|,\\
|\ell(x,u)-\ell(x,u^{\prime})|\leq K^{\ell}_{u}\|u-u^{\prime}\|.\end{gathered}

(12)

This assumption guarantees that the cost rate varies smoothly along nearby trajectories.

If the system dynamics
f
and running cost
\ell
both satisfy Lipschitz continuity, it is shown in
[
16
]
that its corresponding augmented system
z
also satisfies Lipschitz continuity with some constants
K_{y},K_{u}\geq 0
:

\begin{gathered}\|z(y,u)-z(y^{\prime},u)\|\leq K_{y}\|y-y^{\prime}\|,\\
\|z(y,u)-z(y,u^{\prime})\|\leq K_{u}\|u-u^{\prime}\|.\end{gathered}

(13)

Assumption 3.
(Lipschitz Continuity of the Terminal Cost)
:

Finally, the terminal cost introduced in this work is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous in a neighborhood of the goal region
\mathcal{G}
. Specifically,

\exists K_{\phi}\geq 0
such that
\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
:

|\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})|\leq K_{\phi}\|x-x^{\prime}\|.

(14)

This implies that small deviations in the terminal state result in proportionally bounded differences in terminal cost.

To support the theoretical analysis, we introduce several definitions and notations, following prior work on optimal kinodynamic planning
[
23
,
16
]
. Sampling-based kinodynamic planners are typically implemented such that the control function is piecewise-constant:

Definition 1.
(Piecewise-constant Control)
:

A control function
\boldsymbol{u}
is
piecewise-constant
with resolution
\tau>0
if it is represented as a finite concatenation of constant control segments. Each segment
i
is defined by a function:
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}:[0,k_{i}\cdot\tau]\rightarrow u_{i}
, where
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
is fixed and
k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

Definition 2.
(Valid Trajectory)
:

A trajectory
\boldsymbol{x}
generated by a piecewise-constant control function
\boldsymbol{u}
is said to be
valid
if it satisfies the kinodynamic constraints in
Eq. 2
.

Definition 3.
(Obstacle and Goal Clearance)
:

The
obstacle clearance

\delta_{o}
of a valid trajectory
\boldsymbol{x}
is the minimum distance from obstacles over all states in
\boldsymbol{x}
, i.e., maximum value
\delta_{o}
such that:

B_{\delta_{o}}(x)\subset\mathcal{X}_{\text{free}},\forall x\in\boldsymbol{x}.

(15)

Similarly, the
goal clearance

\delta_{g}
of a valid trajectory
\boldsymbol{x}
is the minimum distance from its terminal state
x({\mathcal{T}})
to the boundary of goal region
\mathcal{G}
, that is, the maximum value
\delta_{g}
such that:

B_{\delta_{g}}(x({\mathcal{T}}))\subset\mathcal{G}.

(16)

Definition 4.
(Robust and
\delta
-robust Trajectory)
:

A valid trajectory is
robust
if there exists a clearance
\delta>0
such that both its obstacle clearance
\delta_{o}
and goal clearance
\delta_{g}
are at least
\delta
.
When the value of
\delta
is specified, the trajectory
\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta}
is called
\delta
-robust
.

Definition 5.
(Asymptotic Optimality)
:

Given a problem, let
c^{\ast}
denote the minimum achievable cost among all robust trajectories. Let
C^{\mathtt{ALG}}_{n}
denote a random variable that represents the best cost value among all solutions from algorithm
\mathtt{ALG}
at iteration
n
.
\mathtt{ALG}
is
asymptotically optimal
if

\Pr\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}C^{\mathtt{ALG}}_{n}=c^{\ast}\right)=1,

(17)

that is,
C^{\mathtt{ALG}}_{n}
converges to
c^{\ast}

almost surely
.

, near the reference pair

, such that the propagated state

Illustrations of AO-RRT Lemmas

We now establish the asymptotic optimality of
KiTe
i.e., AO-RRT with a terminal cost.
The proof follows the general structure used in prior analysis of asymptotically optimal kinodynamic planners
[
23
,
17
,
16
,
41
]
, while incorporating a new terminal-cost argument specific to our setting.
As illustrated in
Fig. 2
, the key idea is to place a finite sequence of balls with increasing radii along any
\delta
-robust reference trajectory in the augmented space
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
We then show that AO-RRT can, with positive probability, repeatedly select a vertex from the current ball (
Lemma 1
) and propagate it into the next ball (
Lemma 2
).
Repeating this argument for all balls yields a positive probability of reaching the final ball, thereby producing a trajectory whose total cost is arbitrarily close to the reference one (
Lemma 3
).
We then state the main theorem.

Theorem 1.
(Asymptotic Optimality)
:

Assume that the system dynamics, running cost, and terminal cost are all Lipschitz continuous and let
\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta}
be any robust trajectory generated with piecewise-constant control
\boldsymbol{u}_{\delta}
.
Then
\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\exists a,b>0
,
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, s.t.
\forall n>n_{0}
:

\Pr\big(\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n})>(1+\varepsilon)\,\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta},\boldsymbol{u}_{\delta})\big)\leq a\cdot e^{-bn},

(18)

where (
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
) is the best solution returned by AO-RRT after
n
iterations.

Consequently, let
c^{\ast}
denote the minimum achievable cost among all such trajectories
\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta}
, then AO-RRT is (almost surely) asymptotically optimal with respect to cost
\mathcal{C}_{T}
, i.e.,

\Pr\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n})=c^{\ast}\right)=1.

(19)

The proof of
Theorem 1
relies on the following three lemmas, illustrated in
Fig. 3
.
We adapt
Lemma 1
and
Lemma 2
from
[
17
,
23
]
and provide proofs that are refined to improve precision and clarity.
We then introduce a new lemma
Lemma 3
that captures the effect of the terminal cost.

Lemma 1.

Suppose the tree contains a vertex
y_{k}\in B_{kr_{i}}(y_{i})
for some
k\in(0,1)
.
Given a random sample
y_{\text{rand}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, the probability that AO-RRT selects a vertex
y_{\text{near}}
as the nearest neighbor for propagation inside
B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})
is positive and satisfies

p_{i}^{\text{sel}}\coloneq\Pr\big(y_{\text{near}}\in B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})\big)\geq\frac{\mathcal{V}\!\left[B_{(1-k)r_{i}/2}(y_{i})\right]}{\mathcal{V}\!\left[{\mathcal{Y}}\right]}>0.

(20)

If the tree already contains a vertex
y_{k}
close to
y_{i}
, then any random sample
y_{\text{rand}}
falling in a sufficiently small neighborhood around
y_{i}
will not select a vertex outside
B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})
as the nearest neighbor.
Thus, selection from the ball
B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})
occurs with nonzero probability.
A full proof is given in
Appendix
A-A
.

Lemma 2.

Consider a trajectory segment from
y_{i}
to
y_{i+1}
, generated by applying a constant control
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
for a duration
\tau_{i}\leq{\mathcal{T}}_{p}
, where
{\mathcal{T}}_{p}
is the maximum propagation time.
Let
K_{y}
be the Lipschitz constant of the augmented dynamics with respect to the state.
Let
B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})
and
B_{r_{i+1}}(y_{i+1})
be two balls centered at
y_{i}
and
y_{i+1}
, respectively, with increasing radii satisfying
r_{i+1}=r_{i}(e^{K_{y}{\mathcal{T}}_{p}}+\beta)/\,k
for some
\beta>0
and
k\in(0,1)
.

Then for any
y_{i}^{\prime}\in B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})
, there exist a control neighborhood
B_{\Delta u_{i}}(u_{i})\subset\mathcal{U}
with
\Delta u_{i}>0
and a time interval
I_{s}\subset(0,{\mathcal{T}}_{p}]
of nonzero measure such that, for all

u_{i}^{\prime}\in B_{\Delta u_{i}}(u_{i})
and

\tau_{i}^{\prime}\in I_{s},

the propagated state
y_{i+1}^{\prime}
obtained from
y_{i}^{\prime}
under
(u_{i}^{\prime},\tau_{i}^{\prime})

lies in
B_{kr_{i+1}}(y_{i+1})
.
Consequently, AO-RRT can sample a control-duration pair and reach
B_{kr_{i+1}}(y_{i+1})
with positive probability:

\displaystyle p_{i}^{\text{ext}}

\displaystyle\coloneq\Pr\!\left(y_{i+1}^{\prime}\in B_{kr_{i+1}}(y_{i+1})\right)

(21)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathcal{V}\!\left[I_{s}\right]}{\mathcal{V}\!\left[(0,{\mathcal{T}}_{p}]\right]}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{V}\!\left[B_{\Delta u_{i}}(u_{i})\right]}{\mathcal{V}\!\left[\mathcal{U}\right]}

\displaystyle>0.

Because the dynamics and running cost are Lipschitz continuous, small perturbations in the initial state, control, and its duration induce bounded deviations in the resulting trajectory.
Starting from a vertex
y_{i}^{\prime}
inside the ball
B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})
, AO-RRT can reach the next ball with nonzero probability by sampling a sufficiently close control-duration pair
(u_{i}^{\prime},\tau_{i}^{\prime})
to the original pair
(u_{i},\tau_{i})
.
The full proof is provided in
Appendix
A-B
.

Lemma 3.

Let
y_{m}=(x_{m},c_{m})
be the terminal state of a
\delta
-robust trajectory with total cost
c_{T}=c_{m}+\phi_{m}
, where
\phi_{m}=\phi(x_{m})
. Let
K_{\phi}
be the Lipschitz constant of the terminal cost function. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, define

r_{m}=\min(\delta,\dfrac{\varepsilon\,c_{T}}{1+K_{\phi}}).

(22)

For any other robust trajectory whose terminal state is

y_{m}^{\prime}=(x_{m}^{\prime},c_{m}^{\prime})\in B_{r_{m}}(y_{m})
with terminal cost

\phi_{m}^{\prime}=\phi(x_{m}^{\prime})
and total cost
c_{T}^{\prime}=c_{m}^{\prime}+\phi_{m}^{\prime}
, we always have:

c_{T}^{\prime}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\,c_{T}.

(23)

This lemma captures the key intuition behind AO-RRT.
Once the planner reaches a sufficiently small neighborhood of the reference terminal in the augmented space, it not only reaches the goal region, but also attains a cost close to that of the reference.
In our new setting with terminal cost, we show that by choosing the terminal radius
r_{m}
in a particular way, any robust trajectory that ends inside the terminal ball
B_{r_{m}}(y_{m})
has a total cost within a factor
(1+\varepsilon)
of the reference cost
c_{T}
.
Similarly, the full proof of this lemma is provided in
Appendix
A-C
.

Now we prove
Theorem 1
with these lemmas.

Proof.

As shown in
Fig. 2
, fix any
\delta
-robust trajectory
\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta}
with piece-wise constant controls
\boldsymbol{u}_{\delta}
and total cost
\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta},\boldsymbol{u}_{\delta})
.
Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{\delta}
denote its corresponding augmented trajectory with start
y_{0}=(x_{0},0)
and terminal
y_{m}=(x_{m},c_{m})
.

Pick any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and choose
r_{m}
according to
Lemma 3
, i.e.,
r_{m}=\min(\delta,\tfrac{\varepsilon\,c_{T}}{1+K_{\phi}})
.
Starting from this terminal ball
B_{r_{m}}(y_{m})
, we place a finite sequence of balls
B_{r_{0}}(y_{0}),\dots,B_{r_{i}}(y_{i}),\dots,B_{r_{m}}(y_{m})
backward along the trajectory
\boldsymbol{y}_{\delta}
.
Each consecutive pair corresponds to one constant-control segment, and their radii are chosen to satisfy the condition required by
Lemma 2
, i.e.
r_{i+1}=r_{i}(e^{K_{y}{\mathcal{T}}_{p}}+\beta)/\,k
.
Since each radius is obtained from the next one by a positive multiplicative factor, all balls have nonzero volume.

Now consider any stage
i\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
. By
Lemma 1
, if the tree contains a vertex in
B_{kr_{i}}(y_{i})
, then in one iteration, AO-RRT selects a vertex in
B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})
with probability
p_{i}^{\text{sel}}>0
.
Conditioned on such a selection,
Lemma 2
implies that, with probability
p_{i}^{\text{ext}}>0
, the extension can reach
B_{kr_{i+1}}(y_{i+1})
.
Therefore, the probability of successfully advancing from stage
i
to stange
i+1
in one iteration is lower bounded by

p_{i}\coloneqq p_{i}^{\text{sel}}\cdot p_{i}^{\text{ext}}\;>\;0.

(24)

The induction is initialized automatically since
y_{0}\in B_{kr_{0}}(y_{0})
. Thus, the above argument applies in every stage, yielding a sequence of transitions from
B_{r_{0}}(y_{0})
to
B_{r_{m}}(y_{m})
, where each succeeds with probability at least
p\coloneqq\min_{i=0,\dots,m-1}p_{i}>0
.

We consider each iteration as a Bernoulli trial that attempts to make progress from stage
i
to the next stage
i+1
. To traverse the entire sequence, AO-RRT needs
m
such successful transitions.
Let
S_{n}
denote the number of successful transitions over
n
iterations.
Then the event “AO-RRT has not reached
B_{r_{m}}(y_{m})
after
n
iterations” implies
S_{n}<m
.
Since each iteration has success probability at least
p_{i}
, the number of successful transitions can be stochastically lower bounded by a binomial random variable with parameters
(n,p)
.
Standard tail bounds then imply that there exist constants
a,b>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n>n_{0}
:

\Pr\left(S_{n}<m\right)\leq ae^{-bn}.

(25)

If
S_{n}\geq m
, then the tree has successfully traversed the entire sequence of balls, and contains a terminal state
y_{m}^{\prime}\in B_{r_{m}}(y_{m})
.
By
Lemma 3
, the corresponding trajectory has total cost:

\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\,\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta},\boldsymbol{u}_{\delta}).

(26)

By contraposition, if the cost of the best solution
\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n})
still exceeds
(1+\varepsilon)\,\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta},\boldsymbol{u}_{\delta})
,
then AO-RRT cannot have completed all
m
successful transitions, i.e.,
S_{n}<m
.
Therefore,
\exists a,b>0
,
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, s.t.
\forall n>n_{0}
:

\Pr\big(\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n})>(1+\varepsilon)\,\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta},\boldsymbol{u}_{\delta})\big)\leq ae^{-bn},

(27)

which proves the first part of
Theorem 1
.

Let
c^{\ast}
denote the minimal achievable total cost over all robust trajectories, and select a robust trajectory
(\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{u}^{\ast})
achieving this minimum, i.e.,

\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{u}^{\ast})=c^{\ast}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, the argument above shows that there exist constants
a,b>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n>n_{0}
:

\Pr\!\big(\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n})>(1+\varepsilon)\,c^{\ast}\big)\leq ae^{-bn}.

(28)

Thus, as
n\to\infty
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\!\left(\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n})>(1+\varepsilon)\,c^{\ast}\right)=0,

(29)

which establishes asymptotic optimality in probability
[
7
]
.

Since
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}ae^{-bn}<\infty
, the Borel-Cantelli lemma implies that the event
\{\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n})>(1+\varepsilon)\,c^{\ast}\}

can occur only finitely many times. Thus:

\Pr\!\left(\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n})>(1+\varepsilon)\,c^{\ast}\text{ infinitely often}\right)=0.

(30)

Hence, AO-RRT with a total cost
\mathcal{C}_{T}
is (almost-surely) asymptotically optimal
[
7
]
:

\Pr\!\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}_{n})=c^{\ast}\right)=1.

(31)

∎

V
KiTe
in Belief Space

In practical robotic systems, execution is subject to process uncertainty arising from modeling errors, perception noise, and stochastic disturbances. As a result, the system evolution is no longer deterministic, and planning only in the state space may produce trajectories that are difficult to execute reliably.

A principled solution is to perform planning in the belief space, where each belief represents a distribution over possible system states. Accordingly, rather than planning in the state-cost space
{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
KiTe
can also plans in the belief state-cost space
{\mathcal{Y}_{b}}=\mathcal{B}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
\mathcal{B}
denotes the space of probability measures over
\mathcal{X}
.
The asymptotic optimality of AO-RRT with terminal cost naturally carries over to
{\mathcal{Y}_{b}}
if the Lipschitz continuity conditions required by the analysis continue to hold in the belief space.
We prove that, under mild assumptions on the stochastic dynamics, these conditions are satisfied when
\mathcal{B}
is equipped with the Wasserstein metric.

V-A

Belief-space Metric

Following prior work on belief-space metrics
[
10
,
25
]
, we adopt the 2-Wasserstein distance (also known as earth mover’s distance) as the proper distance metric in
\mathcal{B}
.
For simplicity, we will refer to it as Wasserstein distance.
The Wasserstein distance measures the minimum transport cost required to transform one probability distribution into another. Given a valid metric
D_{\mathcal{X}}
in space
\mathcal{X}
, the Wasserstein distance is defined as:

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(b,b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(b,b^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(X,X^{\prime})^{2}]

(32)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(b,b^{\prime})}\int D_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})^{2}\,d\gamma(x,x^{\prime}),

where
\Gamma(b,b^{\prime})
denotes the set of all couplings (joint distributions)
\gamma
whose marginal distributions are
b
and
b^{\prime}
respectively.
Let
(X,X^{\prime})\sim\gamma
be random variables drawn from such a coupling so that
X\sim b,X^{\prime}\sim b^{\prime}
.
The infimum selects the optimal coupling
\gamma*
that minimizes the expected transform cost, as shown in
Fig. 4
.

Fig. 4
:
Illustration of Wasserstein distance between two Gaussian beliefs. Line segments denote pairings between representative locations of probabilistic mass in the state space. The optimal coupling minimizes the expected squared transport cost.

For Gaussian beliefs, Wasserstein distance
W_{2}
captures both changes in the mean and covariance of the state distribution, and admits a proper metric with closed-form expression.
Given two Gaussian beliefs
b=\mathcal{N}(x,P)
and
b^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}(x^{\prime},P^{\prime})
:

W_{2}^{2}(b,b^{\prime})=\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}\,\big(P+P^{\prime}-2\,(P^{\prime\frac{1}{2}}PP^{\prime\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\big),

(33)

where
\mathrm{Tr}(\cdot)
is the trace of a matrix and
P^{\frac{1}{2}}
denotes the principal square root of
P
. The second term is also known as Bures metric between
P
and
P^{\prime}
.

V-B

Belief-space Dynamics and Costs

Since controls are applied in piecewise-constant segments, we consider a general stochastic system of discrete-time form:

\begin{gathered}x_{i+1}=F(x_{i},u_{i})\diamond\eta_{i},\\
\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x_{i},u_{i})\big).\end{gathered}

(34)

where
F:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{X}
is a deterministic transition map,
\eta_{i}
is a random Gaussian noise term, and
\diamond
specifies how noise is injected (e.g.
\diamond
is addition
+
in Euclidean space).
The process uncertainty may be heteroscedastic, i.e., the noise covariance
Q(x_{i},u_{i})
may depend on the current state and applied control.
We approximate the belief state by a Gaussian distribution
b=\mathcal{N}(x,P)
.
Under the model in
Eq. 34
, the belief evolution is described by a discrete-time Markov process
[
10
]
:

b_{i+1}=F_{b}(b_{i},u_{i}),

(35)

where
F_{b}
jointly propagates the mean and covariance of the belief state.
The cost function is then expressed as:

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{u})=\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{m-1}{w}\big(b_{i},F_{b}(b_{i},u_{i})\big)+\psi\big(b_{m}\big),

(36)

where
{w}:\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\psi:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
are the belief-space running cost and terminal cost in discrete form, and
m
is the number of steps in the trajectory.

We aim to show that the belief dynamics (
Eq. 35
), belief-space running cost and terminal cost (
Eq. 36
) are Lipschitz continuous with respect to the Wasserstein metric.
These properties are sufficient to ensure that the theoretical guarantees of AO-RRT in
Sec. V
extend to the belief state-cost space.

Let
D_{\mathcal{X}}
be a valid metric in state space
\mathcal{X}
.
With discrete-time representation, the Lipschitz conditions of the system dynamics in
Assumption 1
takes the form:

\begin{gathered}D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(x,u),\,F(x^{\prime},u)\big)\leq K_{x}^{F}\,D_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime}),\\
D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(x,u),\,F(x,u^{\prime})\big)\leq K_{u}^{F}\,\|u-u^{\prime}\|,\end{gathered}

(37)

for some constants
K_{x}^{F},K_{u}^{F}\geq 0
.

Since the system includes stochastic process uncertainty whose covariance may depend on the state and the control input, we impose two mild regularity assumptions to ensure that the noise term behaves in a Lipschitz manner.

Assumption 4.
(Lipschitz continuity of process uncertainty)
:

The process uncertainty is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous with Wasserstein distance. Formally,
\exists K_{x}^{Q},K_{u}^{Q}\geq 0
s.t.
\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},\forall u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
:

\begin{gathered}W_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x,u)\big),\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x^{\prime},u)\big)\bigr)\leq K_{x}^{Q}\,D_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime}),\\
W_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x,u)\big),\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x,u^{\prime})\big)\bigr)\leq K_{u}^{Q}\,\|u-u^{\prime}\|.\end{gathered}

(38)

This assumption ensures that changing the state or control does not change the process covariance arbitrarily fast.

Assumption 5.
(Lipschitz continuity of noise injection)
:

The system in
Eq. 37
preserves Lipschitz continuity with injected noise.
Specifically,
\exists K_{x}^{F\eta},K_{u}^{F\eta},K_{\eta}^{F\eta}\geq 0
such that
\forall x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
u\in\mathcal{U}
, and noise realizations
\eta,\eta^{\prime}
:

\begin{gathered}D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(x,u)\diamond\eta,F(x^{\prime},u)\diamond\eta\big)\leq K_{x}^{F\eta}D_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime}),\\
D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(x,u)\diamond\eta,F(x,u^{\prime})\diamond\eta\big)\leq K_{u}^{F\eta}\|u-u^{\prime}\|,\\
D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(x,u)\diamond\eta,F(x,u)\diamond\eta^{\prime}\big)\leq K_{\eta}^{F\eta}D_{\mathcal{X}}(\eta,\eta^{\prime}).\end{gathered}

(39)

This assumption guarantees that variations in the injected noise lead to proportionally bounded deviations in the resulting state.

Under these additional assumptions, we can establish that the belief dynamics and the associated cost functions inherit Lipschitz continuity with respect to the Wasserstein metric.

Proposition 1.

Consider a system with Gaussian noise that satisfies the assumptions in
Eq. 37
,
Eq. 38
and
Eq. 39
. Then
\exists K_{x}^{F_{b}},K_{u}^{F_{b}}\geq 0
s.t.
\forall b,b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
and
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
:

\begin{gathered}W_{2}\big(F_{b}(b,u),F_{b}(b^{\prime},u)\big)\leq K_{x}^{F_{b}}\,W_{2}(b,b^{\prime}),\\
W_{2}\big(F_{b}(b,u),F_{b}(b,u^{\prime})\big)\leq K_{u}^{F_{b}}\,\|u-u^{\prime}\|.\end{gathered}

(40)

Proposition 2.

Consider a system with Gaussian noise that satisfies assumptions in
Eq. 37
,
Eq. 38
and
Eq. 39
. Then
\exists K_{x}^{w},K_{u}^{w},K_{x}^{\psi}\geq 0
s.t.
\forall b,b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
and
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
:

\begin{gathered}|{w}\big(b,F_{b}(b,u)\big)-{w}\big(b^{\prime},F_{b}(b^{\prime},u)\big)|\leq K_{x}^{w}\,W_{2}(b,b^{\prime}),\\
|{w}\big(b,F_{b}(b,u)\big)-{w}\big(b,F_{b}(b,u^{\prime})\big)|\leq K_{u}^{w}\,\|u-u^{\prime}\|,\\
|\psi(b)-\psi(b^{\prime})|\leq K_{x}^{\psi}\,W_{2}(b,b^{\prime}),\end{gathered}

(41)

if running cost function
{w}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Wasserstein distance
W_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
and terminal cost
\psi(\cdot)
is the Wasserstein distance between the terminal belief and some constant belief
b_{c}
.

The detailed proofs of
Proposition 1
and
Proposition 2
are provided in
Appendix
B-A
and
Appendix
B-B
, respectively.
These propositions imply that, as long as the underlying system dynamics
F
satisfy the Lipschitz continuity conditions in
Eq. 37
(
Assumption 1
), the corresponding belief dynamics and cost functions remain Lipschitz continuous in the belief space when equipped with the Wasserstein metric.
As a result, AO-RRT with terminal costs preserves asymptotic optimality when operating in the belief state-cost space.

V-C

Belief Formulation for Common Systems

We next illustrate how the proposed belief-space formulation is instantiated for two common classes of systems: 1) linear or linearized systems in Euclidean space, and 2) rigid-body systems evolving on matrix Lie groups.

V-C
1
Linear or Linearized System

Consider a linear or linearized system with additive Gaussian noise in Euclidean space:

\begin{gathered}x_{i+1}=F(x_{i},u_{i})\diamond\eta_{i}=Ax_{i}+Bu_{i}+\eta_{i},\\
\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x_{i},u_{i})\big),\end{gathered}

(42)

where
A
and
B
are constant matrices.
We represent the belief state as a Gaussian distribution
b_{i}=\mathcal{N}(x_{i},P_{i})
.
Under open-loop propagation, the belief dynamics
F_{b}
updates the mean and covariance as
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle x_{i+1}

\displaystyle=Ax_{i}+Bu_{i},

(43)

\displaystyle P_{i+1}

\displaystyle=AP_{i}A^{\top}+Q(x_{i},u_{i}).

Since the state space is Euclidean, the Wasserstein distance between Gaussian beliefs admits a closed form directly.
Therefore, the running cost
{w}(\cdot,\cdot)
and terminal cost
\psi(\cdot)
introduced earlier can be evaluated naturally in this setting using the Gaussian belief representation.

V-C
2
Rigid-body System on Lie Groups

Rigid-body configurations often evolve on a matrix Lie group
G
, such as
SE(2)
or
SE(3)
.
Since
G
is not a vector space, Gaussian uncertainty is more naturally defined in its associated Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, which is the tangent space at the identity and admits a linear structure.
The exponential and logarithm maps,
\mathrm{Exp}:\mathfrak{g}\to G
and
\mathrm{Log}:G\to\mathfrak{g}
, provide the local correspondence between the manifold and tangent space
[
34
]
.
A state
x
near a reference
x_{\text{ref}}\in G
can be expressed as
x=x_{\text{ref}}\,\mathrm{Exp}(\xi)
with
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
.

We model the dynamics with multiplicative noise as:

\begin{gathered}x_{i+1}=F(x_{i},u_{i})\diamond\eta_{i}=F(x_{i},u_{i})\,\mathrm{Exp}(\eta_{i}),\\
\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x_{i},u_{i})\big),\end{gathered}

(44)

where
F:G\times\mathcal{U}\to G
is the nominal rigid-body update. Gaussian noise
\eta_{i}
is defined in the tangent space.
A belief state is represented as a Lie-Gaussian belief
b=\mathcal{N}_{L}(x,P)
, where
x
is the nominal pose and
P
is the covariance in the tangent space. A random sample is given by:

X\sim\mathcal{N}_{L}(x,P)=x\,\mathrm{Exp}(\mathcal{N}(0,P)).

(45)

Under
Eq. 44
, the belief dynamics
F_{b}
evolves according to
[
34
]
:

\begin{gathered}x_{i+1}=F(x_{i},u_{i}),\\
\xi_{i}=\mathrm{Log}(x_{i}^{-1}x_{i+1}),\\
P_{i+1}=\mathrm{Ad}_{\mathrm{Exp}(-\xi_{i})}\,P_{i}\,\mathrm{Ad}_{\mathrm{Exp}(-\xi_{i})}^{\top}+Q(x_{i},u_{i}),\end{gathered}

(46)

where adjoint action
\mathrm{Ad}
transports covariances between tangent spaces.

A common global metric for a Lie-group system is the double geodesic distance
[
18
]
:

D_{G}(x,x^{\prime})=\|p-p^{\prime}\|+w_{r}\,\|\mathrm{Log}(R^{-1}R^{\prime})\|,

(47)

where
p,p^{\prime}
and
R,R^{\prime}
denote the positional and rotational components of
x
and
x^{\prime}
, respectively, and
w_{r}
is a weight.
However, since it is not induced by a global linear structure, the corresponding Wasserstein distance does not admit a simple closed form.

To obtain a tractable Gaussian Wasserstein distance on a Lie group between beliefs
b=\mathcal{N}_{L}(x,P)
and
b^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}_{L}(x^{\prime},P^{\prime})
, we linearize both beliefs in a common tangent space. A natural choice is to use one of the means
x
as the reference point.
In this frame, the relative means and covariances are:

\begin{gathered}\hat{x}=I,\quad\hat{x}^{\prime}=x^{-1}x^{\prime},\\
\hat{P}=P,\quad\hat{P}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Ad}_{x^{-1}x^{\prime}}\,P^{\prime}\,\mathrm{Ad}_{x^{-1}x^{\prime}}^{\top}.\end{gathered}

(48)

This yields a local metric:

D_{G}(x,x^{\prime})=\|\mathrm{Log}(\hat{x})-\mathrm{Log}(\hat{x}^{\prime})\|=\|\mathrm{Log}(x^{-1}x^{\prime})\|,

(49)

which matches the true geodesic up to second order near the reference state
x
. Under this linearization, the two beliefs become Gaussian in the vector space
\mathfrak{g}
, namely
\mathcal{N}(\hat{x},\hat{P})
and
\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}^{\prime},\hat{P}^{\prime})
.
We then approximate the Wasserstein distance by the closed-form Gaussian expression in the same tangent space:

W_{2}^{2}(b,b^{\prime})=\|\mathrm{Log}(\hat{x}^{\prime})\|^{2}+\mathrm{Tr}\big(P+\hat{P}^{\prime}-2(\hat{P}^{\prime\frac{1}{2}}P\,\hat{P}^{\prime\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\big).

(50)

In summary, rigid-body systems on
G
admit intrinsic belief propagation via
Eq. 46
and a local Gaussian Wasserstein metric approximation
Eq. 50
.
With the required Lipschitz conditions satisfied, these systems can fit directly into the AO-RRT belief-space framework.

VI
Goal-reaching Success Lower Bound

As discussed earlier, in belief-space kinodynamic planning, the system state is modeled as a random variable rather than a deterministic quantity.
This allows the planner to reason explicitly about uncertainty, enabling chance-constrained formulations
[
27
,
10
]
that reject states with high collision risk and terminal states that fail to reach the goal region with sufficiently high probability:

\begin{gathered}\Pr\!\left(x\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{free}}\right)<p_{\text{free}},\quad\Pr\!\left(x_{\text{end}}\in\mathcal{G}\right)<p_{\text{goal}},\end{gathered}

(51)

where
p_{\text{free}}
and
p_{\text{goal}}
denote the minimum required probabilities of collision-free validity and goal reaching, respectively.

In practice, however, identifying appropriate threshold values across different environments and tasks is not trivial.
For collision avoidance, a conservative threshold (high
p_{\text{free}}
) is often acceptable, since many alternative actions may still remain available during sampling.
By contrast,
p_{\text{goal}}
can be difficult to specify a priori for goal reaching. If it is set too high, the planner may fail to find a feasible solution, whereas if it is too low, it may accept solutions with weak goal-reaching probability. In fact, as shown in
Sec. VIII
, optimizing only the running cost may lead the planner to favor paths with low goal-reaching success.
Ideally, rather than enforcing a hard threshold, the planner should use the available time budget to continuously improve the goal-reaching probability.

In this work, instead of imposing a hard goal-reaching threshold, we derive a lower bound on the probability of reaching the goal region using the Wasserstein distance between the terminal belief state and the goal.
This bound can be incorporated as a terminal cost in the optimization objective, allowing AO-RRT to favor trajectories with higher goal-reaching success without requiring manual threshold tuning.

VI-A

Lower Bound on Goal-reaching Probability

To incorporate goal-reaching probability into the planning objective, we relate it to a geometric quantity in belief space.
Specifically, the probability of reaching a ball-shaped or ellipsoidal goal region at the goal state
g
can be lower-bounded using the Wasserstein distance between the terminal belief state
b
and the Dirac measure
\nu_{g}
at
g
.
Intuitively, the Dirac measure
\nu_{g}
represents a deterministic belief concentrated entirely at the single state
g
.

We now state the formal theorem and provide its proof.

Theorem 2.
(Goal-reaching Lower Bound)
:

Let
b
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
with finite second moment, and let
\nu_{g}
denote the Dirac measure at a goal state
g\in\mathcal{X}
. For any radius
r>0
, define the goal region as the ball
\mathcal{G}=B_{r}(g)
. If
X\sim b
, then the probability of reaching the goal region
\mathcal{G}
satisfies

\Pr\left(X\in\mathcal{G}\right)\geq 1-\frac{W_{2}^{2}\left(b,\nu_{g}\right)}{r^{2}}.

(52)

Proof.

Let
X\sim b
and define the nonnegative random variable
J:=\|X-g\|^{2}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{G}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\in\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\|X-g\|\leq r\right)

(53)

\displaystyle=1-\Pr\left(\|X-g\|>r\right)

\displaystyle=1-\Pr\left(J>r^{2}\right).

Applying Markov’s inequality to
J
gives:

\Pr\left(J>r^{2}\right)\leq\Pr\left(J\geq r^{2}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[J]}{r^{2}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|X-g\|^{2}]}{r^{2}}.

(54)

We now relate
\mathbb{E}[\|X-g\|^{2}]
to the Wasserstein distance between
b
and
\nu_{g}
:

W_{2}^{2}(b,\nu_{g})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(b,\nu_{g})}\int\|x-v\|^{2}\,d\gamma(x,v).

(55)

Since
\nu_{g}
is a Dirac measure at
g
, we have
v=g
almost surely under every coupling. Thus, the integral in
Eq. 55
reduces to:

\displaystyle\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(b,\nu_{g})}\int\|x-v\|^{2}\,d\gamma(x,v)

\displaystyle=\int\|x-g\|^{2}\,db(x)

(56)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|X-g\|^{2}].

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\in\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\Pr\left(J>r^{2}\right)

(57)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|X-g\|^{2}]}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{W_{2}^{2}\left(b,\nu_{g}\right)}{r^{2}}.

∎

If the belief state
b
is Gaussian,
b=\mathcal{N}(x,P)
, then its squared Wasserstein distance to the Dirac measure
\nu_{g}
admits the closed form:

W_{2}^{2}(b,\nu_{g})=\|x-g\|^{2}+\mathrm{tr}(P).

(58)

The following corollary is immediate from
Theorem 2
.

Corollary 1.

If the belief state
b
is Gaussian, i.e.,
X\sim\mathcal{N}(x,P)
, and
\mathcal{G}=B_{r}(g)
, the probability of reaching the goal region satisfies

\Pr\left(X\in\mathcal{G}\right)\geq 1-\frac{\|x-g\|^{2}+\mathrm{tr}(P)}{r^{2}}.

(59)

Note that
Eq. 59
contains the distance between the belief mean and the goal,
\|x-g\|
. This motivates using only the state distance to the goal,
\|x-g\|
, without the uncertainty term, as a simple surrogate in practice.
We evaluate this empirically in
Sec. VIII
.
However, we emphasize that
\|x-g\|
alone does not in general induce a valid lower bound on
\Pr\left(X\in\mathcal{G}\right)
, and should only be viewed as a computationally convenient heuristic.
A belief with mean close to
g
but large covariance may still assign little probability mass to the goal region.

A parallel result applies when the goal region is an ellipsoid.
Let
E
be a symmetric positive definite matrix and define the squared
E
-norm:

\|q\|_{E}^{2}:=q^{\top}E^{-1}q.

(60)

For
r>0
, we define an ellipsoidal goal region as:

\mathcal{G}_{E}=\{\,(x-g)^{\top}E^{-1}(x-g)\leq r^{2}\,\}=\{\,\|x-g\|_{E}\leq r\,\}.

(61)

We then define the weighted Wasserstein distance to
\nu_{g}
:

W_{2,E}^{2}(b,\nu_{g}):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(b,\nu_{g})}\int\|x-v\|_{E}^{2}\,d\gamma(x,v).

(62)

The next corollary follows the same argument as
Theorem 2
, replacing the Euclidean norm by the weighted norm
\|\cdot\|_{E}
.

Corollary 2.

If
X\sim b
, the probability of
X
lying in an ellipsoidal goal region
\mathcal{G}_{E}
is lower-bounded by:

\Pr\left(X\in\mathcal{G}_{E}\right)\geq 1-\frac{W_{2,E}^{2}(b,\nu_{g})}{r^{2}}.

(63)

VI-B

Goal-reaching Success Lower Bound as Terminal Cost

Since Wasserstein distance defines a Lipschitz-continuous terminal cost in
\mathcal{B}
(
Proposition 2
), the lower bound in
Theorem 2
can be incorporated directly into the planning objective.
Rather than enforcing a hard goal-reaching threshold, we use the Wasserstein distance from the final belief
b_{m}
to the Dirac measure of the goal
\nu_{g}
as a terminal cost. For simplicity, we call it Wasserstein distance to the goal:

\psi(b_{m})=w_{g}\,W_{2}(b_{m},\nu_{g}),

(64)

where
w_{g}
is a weight.
By
Theorem 2
, reducing
W_{2}(b_{m},\nu_{g})
directly improves a lower bound on the probability of reaching the goal region.
For Gaussian beliefs,
Corollary 1
shows that this terminal cost jointly penalizes mean deviation from the goal and terminal uncertainty.
Combined with the running cost, this yields the belief-space objective:

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{u})=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}W_{2}(b_{i},F_{b}(b_{i},u_{i}))+w_{g}\,W_{2}(b_{m},\nu_{g}).

(65)

We provide two examples to highlight the role of the terminal cost in belief-space planning.

Fig. 5
:
Example where running cost alone is insufficient. Although
\boldsymbol{b}_{2}
has a larger running cost due to its longest state-space path, it reaches the goal with a smaller Wasserstein distance to
\nu_{g}
, giving it the highest lower bound on goal-reaching probability.

Fig. 6
:
Example where terminal cost is necessary. Although extending the belief
b_{1}
always increases accumulated running cost, a successor such as
b_{3}
can still reduce the terminal Wasserstein distance to
\nu_{g}
and improve the lower bound on goal-reaching probability.

The first example is shown in
Fig. 5
.
The vertical axis represents covariance magnitude, quantified by its trace, while the horizontal plane is the state space.
With process uncertainty, each propagation step increases uncertainty.
Consider two trajectories that start from the same belief and end at beliefs whose means are equally distant from
g
.
Although
\boldsymbol{b}_{2}
has a larger running cost than that of
\boldsymbol{b}_{1}
, it ends with a smaller terminal covariance.
Therefore,
\boldsymbol{b}_{2}
has the highest lower bound on goal-reaching probability (
Eq. 59
).
This shows that running cost may loosely correlate with terminal quality, but it does not optimize it directly and can rank trajectories incorrectly.

A second example is shown in
Fig. 6
.
Suppose the tree reaches a belief
b_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{1},P_{1})
whose mean is already inside the goal region.
In standard AO-RRT with only running costs, extending from
b_{1}
is unattractive because every successor increases accumulated cost.
However, terminal quality can still improve.
A noisy action to
b_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{2},P_{2})
worsens the terminal Wasserstein distance, even though
x_{2}
is closer to
g
in state space.
But a precise action to
b_{3}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{3},P_{3})
moves the mean closer to
g
while adding little uncertainty, giving
b_{3}
a smaller terminal Wasserstein distance than
b_{1}
.
Thus, although both successors have a higher running cost,
b_{3}
is better in terms of goal-reaching probability.
Without a terminal term, AO-RRT would not favor such beneficial extensions.

Together, these examples show that the Wasserstein running cost in belief space is only a loose surrogate for goal-reaching probability and may even conflict with it.
This motivates augmenting the running-cost objective with an explicit terminal Wasserstein cost.
The running cost remains important for guiding the search toward efficient trajectories that avoid unnecessary drift in the mean or covariance.
The terminal cost complements it by improving a lower bound on the goal-reaching probability and correctly ranking terminal beliefs.

VII
Learning Dynamics and Uncertainty

For many robotic systems, the dynamics and process uncertainty are difficult to model in closed form
[
40
,
1
]
. Even when an approximate analytical model is available, its parameters may still be difficult to identify accurately
[
42
,
8
]
.
In such settings, we train a probabilistic forward model from interaction data and use it to propagate beliefs during planning.

Rather than directly predicting the next state, we learn a local stochastic transition model.
Specifically, let
\oplus
denote a system-dependent composition operator such that

x_{i+1}=x_{i}\oplus\Delta x_{i},

(66)

where
\Delta x_{i}
denotes the local transition variable associated with applying control
u_{i}
at state
x_{i}
.
We model this local transition as a Gaussian random variable:

\Delta X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\Delta x_{i},Q_{i}),

(67)

where
\Delta x_{i}
is the mean local transition and
Q_{i}
is the associated process covariance.
In this work, for simplicity, we use a diagonal covariance model for learning process uncertainty:

Q_{i}=\mathrm{Diag}(\sigma_{i}^{2}),

(68)

where
\sigma_{i}^{2}
denotes the variance vector.
Thus, the learned model aims to predict both the mean local transition
\Delta x_{i}
and its associated process covariance parameterized by
\sigma_{i}^{2}
, which are then combined with the current belief
(x_{i},P_{i})
to obtain the next belief state
(x_{i+1},P_{i+1})
.

Fig. 7
:
Neural network model for learning dynamics and uncertainty. The model predicts a mean local transition
\Delta x_{i}
and a diagonal process covariance parameterized by
\sigma_{i}^{2}
. Combined with the current belief
(x_{i},P_{i})
, these predictions are used to propagate and obtain the next belief
(x_{i+1},P_{i+1})
.

The specific form of
\oplus
and the corresponding covariance propagation depends on the geometric of the state space.
For linear Euclidean systems, as in
Eq. 43
,
\oplus
reduces to vector addition, so the state update becomes

x_{i+1}=x_{i}+\Delta x_{i}.

(69)

Its covariance is then propagated with first-order linearization:

\begin{gathered}P_{i+1}=A_{i}P_{i}A_{i}^{\top}+Q_{i},\quad A_{i}=I+\frac{\partial\Delta x_{i}}{\partial x_{i}},\end{gathered}

(70)

where
I
is the identity matrix and
A_{i}
is the Jacobian of the mean transition with respect to the current state.
For Lie-group systems,
\oplus
corresponds to composition through the exponential map, so the mean transition becomes

x_{i+1}=x_{i}\,\mathrm{Exp}(\Delta x_{i}).

(71)

In this case, the covariance is propagated in the tangent space:

P_{i+1}=\mathrm{Ad}_{\mathrm{Exp}(-\Delta x_{i})}\,P_{i}\,\mathrm{Ad}_{\mathrm{Exp}(-\Delta x_{i})}^{\top}+Q_{i},

(72)

which is consistent with the belief update in
Eq. 46
.

The overall neural network structure is shown in
Fig. 7
.
Given the current state
x_{i}
and control
u_{i}
, the network first extracts a shared feature representation and then branches into a mean head and an uncertainty head.
The mean head predicts the mean local transition
\Delta x_{i}
, while the uncertainty head provides the corresponding variance
\sigma_{i}^{2}
.
For numerical stability, the uncertainty head predicts the log-variance in practice.
In this work, the forward model is implemented as a simple multilayer perceptron (MLP).

The model is trained in a supervised manner using tuples
(x_{i},u_{i},x_{i+1})
.
Several probabilistic regression losses can be used to jointly learn dynamics and uncertainty, such as evidential regression
[
2
]
and
\beta
-NLL
[
33
]
.
In this work, we use the standard Gaussian negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NLL}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{(\Delta x_{i}^{\mathrm{gt}}-\Delta x_{i})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\log\sigma_{i}^{2}\right),

(73)

where
\Delta x_{i}^{\mathrm{gt}}
denotes the ground-truth local transition.
This objective encourages accurate transition prediction while calibrating the predicted uncertainty according to the observed variability in the data.
The learned uncertainty primarily captures aleatoric effects arising from stochasticity, model mismatch, and unmodeled interactions.

VIII
Experiments

In this section, we evaluate three aspects of the proposed framework:
(1) whether the terminal cost allows the planner to encode goal desirability,
(2) whether it improves goal-reaching probability under uncertainty, and
(3) whether learned belief dynamics can be integrated into AO planners.
The experiments are conducted on three tasks with increasing complexity: Flappy Bird, Car Parking, and Planar Pushing.
The last two tasks are run in Mujoco simulation
[
38
]
, while the Planar Pushing task is also evaluated in the real world.
All planning and learning experiments are run on a computer with an i7-14700KF CPU, 32GB of RAM and a RTX 4070 Ti Super GPU.

VIII-A

Flappy Bird Task

We begin with a Flappy Bird benchmark adapted from
[
9
]
to isolate the effect of terminal cost in a deterministic setting.
The bird moves from left to right with constant horizontal speed and can optionally apply an upward thrust.
Its state is defined as

x=[p_{x},\ p_{y},\ v_{y}]^{\top}
,
where
p_{x}
and
p_{y}
denote the horizontal and vertical positions in pixels (px), and
v_{y}
denotes the vertical velocity.
The control is binary,
u\in\{0,1\}
, indicating whether an upward thrust is applied.
The frame-based dynamics are

\begin{bmatrix}p_{x,i+1}\\
p_{y,i+1}\\
v_{y,i+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{x,i}\\
p_{y,i}\\
v_{y,i}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}v_{x}\\
v_{y,i}\\
g_{p}+u_{i}f_{p}\end{bmatrix}.

(74)

where
v_{x}=5
px/frame,
g_{p}=-1.0
px/frame
2
, and
f_{p}=9
px/frame
2
.
The screen size is
720\times 480
pixels, and
v_{y}
is clipped to
[-10,10]
px/frame.

A trajectory is feasible if it stays within the screen, avoids all pillars, and reaches the goal region after the last pillar, as shown in
Fig. 9
.
We define the running cost with accumulated obstacle clearance, i.e., the distance from the bird to the nearest pillar, so that safer trajectories are preferred.
To encode goal desirability, we define a terminal cost that penalizes deviation from the center of the final gap.
This corresponds to the setting where the next planning starts from the current terminal state, so ending near the center provides a more desired initial condition.

We compare
KiTe
with vanilla AO-RRT
[
9
]
and SST
[
23
]
, without terminal costs. All methods are implemented in OMPL
[
35
]
.
We denote the three methods as Base (AO-RRT), Base (SST), and
KiTe
(AO-RRT,
w_{g}
), where

is the terminal weight. Note that SST is not theoretically justified for incorporating a terminal cost.
We generate 20 random pillar layouts and run each planner 5 times per environment.
The results are averaged across environments, with standard deviations computed over repeated runs.

As shown in
Fig. 8
, all planners improve solution quality as more planning time is provided.
However,
KiTe
consistently returns trajectories whose terminal states lie closer to the center of the final gap.
This comes with only a modest increase in running cost, which reflects the intended trade-off.

Fig. 8
:
Comparison of running cost and terminal distance to the goal center.
KiTe
reduces terminal distance to the goal center while incurring a modest increase in running cost.

Fig. 9
:
Representative planning process in the Flappy Bird task.
KiTe
first finds a feasible trajectory (
KiTe
First), then improves the terminal state toward the center (
KiTe
Intermediate), and eventually achieves both low running and terminal costs (
KiTe
Final). In contrast, other methods have no incentive to improve further once the goal region is reached.

Fig. 9
shows a representative planning process.
At early iterations,
KiTe
finds a feasible path that merely enters the goal region.
Given more time, it identifies alternative trajectories that terminate closer to the center of the gap.
Eventually, it finds trajectories that jointly achieve low running cost and low terminal cost.
In contrast, when only running cost is optimized, extending farther within the set is usually undesired because any additional motion increases the accumulated path cost.
The terminal term changes this behavior by making improvement inside the goal region explicitly beneficial.

One alternative to achieve goal desirability is to shrink the goal region towards the desired state.
However, doing so makes the feasible planning problem harder under a fixed time budget.
In contrast, the terminal-cost formulation preserves the original feasible region and allows the planner to progressively improve terminal quality after feasibility has already been achieved.

Overall, this experiment shows that incorporating terminal cost allows the planner to preserve the original feasible region and progressively improve the terminal state to satisfy the specified goal desirability.

VIII-B

Car Parking Task

We next consider a car parking task in
SE(2)
with nonholonomic dynamics.
We model the car by the simplified kinematic bicycle model
[
32
]
with state

x=[p_{x},\ p_{y},\ \theta]^{\top}
and control

u=[u_{v},\ u_{\phi}]^{\top}
:

f(x,u)=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{p}_{x}\\
\dot{p}_{y}\\
\dot{\theta}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{v}\cos\theta\\
u_{v}\sin\theta\\
\dfrac{u_{v}}{L_{\text{car}}}\tan u_{\phi}\end{bmatrix},

(75)

where
(p_{x},p_{y})
is the position,
\theta
is the heading angle,

u_{v}
is the commanded longitudinal velocity,
u_{\phi}
is the steering angle, and
L_{\text{car}}
is the wheelbase of the vehicle.
To obtain belief propagation as in
Eq. 43
, we linearize the dynamics around state
x_{i}
to compute the state-transition Jacobian
A_{i}

IX
Limitations and Future Work

We conclude by discussing limitations of the proposed method and outlining potential future directions.
First, the current formulation considers only open-loop propagation. Incorporating observation updates and closed-loop feedback policies would more directly connect planning to execution-time robustness, but would require additional modeling and algorithmic extensions.
Second, introducing a terminal cost adds design degrees of freedom such as the terminal weight. While the ablation study in
Sec. VIII
provides guidance for choosing it, a user still must select weights to reflect task preferences and the desired balance.
Third, the learned uncertainty model is diagonal, i.e., variance-only. Although this is simple and effective, diagonal covariances may miss important correlated errors, which can lead to inaccurate belief evolution.

Future work naturally follows these limitations.
The first direction is closing the loop: integrating observation updates or execution-time feedback to adapt the planned belief trajectory during execution
[
10
,
31
]
.
Because the framework involves multiple objectives, a second direction is to reduce manual weight selection by adopting multi-objective formulations that explicitly characterize the trade-off, e.g., via Pareto front
[
3
]
.
A third direction is richer uncertainty learning. We can learn correlated covariances or hybrid residual-physics models that incorporate structured priors more deeply
[
42
]
.
Finally, we plan to evaluate scalability on more complex, higher-dimensional kinodynamic systems, including tasks with longer horizons, tighter dynamic constraints, and increased state dimensionality.

(X,X^{\prime})
is drawn from an optimal coupling of
b
and
b^{\prime}
, the propagated pair
(X^{+},X_{x}^{\prime+})
may not remain an optimal coupling of
F_{b}(b,u)
and
F_{b}(b^{\prime},u)
. From the definition of Wasserstein distance (
Eq. 32
):

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(F_{b}(b,u),F_{b}(b^{\prime},u))

(93)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(X^{+},X_{x}^{\prime+})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(X,u)\diamond H,\;F(X^{\prime},u)\diamond H^{\prime}\big)^{2}].

With the triangle inequality of
D_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot,\cdot)
:

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(F_{b}(b,u),F_{b}(b^{\prime},u))

(94)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\bigl(D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(X,u)\diamond H,\;F(X^{\prime},u)\diamond H\big)

\displaystyle+D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(X^{\prime},u)\diamond H,\;F(X^{\prime},u)\diamond H^{\prime}\big)\bigr)^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\bigl(K_{x}^{F\eta}D_{\mathcal{X}}(X,X^{\prime})

\displaystyle+K_{\eta}^{F\eta}D_{\mathcal{X}}(H,H^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}\bigr]\quad\text{(\autoref{as:lip-injection})}

\displaystyle\leq 2(K_{x}^{F\eta})^{2}\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(X,X^{\prime})^{2}]+2(K_{\eta}^{F\eta})^{2}\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(H,H^{\prime})^{2}].

Now, conditioning on
(X,X^{\prime})
, the coupling
(H,H^{\prime})
is optimal for
\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(X,u)\big)
and
\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(X^{\prime},u)\big)
, thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(H,H^{\prime})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(H,H^{\prime})^{2}\mid X,X^{\prime}]\right]

(95)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{2}^{2}\big(\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(X,u)\big),\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(X^{\prime},u)\big)\big)\right]

\displaystyle\leq(K_{x}^{Q})^{2}\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(X,X^{\prime})^{2}].\quad\text{(\autoref{as:lip-noise})}

Substituting this into the previous bound gives

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(F_{b}(b,u),F_{b}(b^{\prime},u))

(96)

\displaystyle\leq\left(2(K_{x}^{F\eta})^{2}+2(K_{\eta}^{F\eta}K_{x}^{Q})^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(X,X^{\prime})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\left(2(K_{x}^{F\eta})^{2}+2(K_{\eta}^{F\eta}K_{x}^{Q})^{2}\right)W_{2}^{2}(b,b^{\prime}).

Taking square roots yields the first Lipschitz constant:

K_{x}^{F_{b}}=\left(2(K_{x}^{F\eta})^{2}+2(K_{\eta}^{F\eta}K_{x}^{Q})^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

For the control argument, let
X\sim b
. For each realization
X=x
, let

\tilde{\gamma}_{\eta}^{*}(x)\in\Gamma\bigl(\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x,u)\big),\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x,u^{\prime})\big)\bigr)

be an optimal coupling for the noise, and let
(H,H^{\prime})\mid(X=x)\sim\tilde{\gamma}_{\eta}^{*}(x)
.
Then, conditioned on
X=x
, the marginals satisfy

H\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x,u)\big)
and
H^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(x,u^{\prime})\big)
.

Define
X^{+}=F(X,u)\diamond H
and
X_{u}^{\prime+}=F(X,u^{\prime})\diamond H^{\prime}
. Then
X^{+}\sim F_{b}(b,u)
and
X_{u}^{\prime+}\sim F_{b}(b,u^{\prime})
, and
(X^{+},X_{u}^{\prime+})
is a valid coupling of
F_{b}(b,u)
and
F_{b}(b,u^{\prime})
. Similarly:

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(F_{b}(b,u),F_{b}(b,u^{\prime}))

(97)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(X^{+},X_{u}^{\prime+})^{2}]\quad\text{(definition of \autoref{eq:wasserstein})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(X,u)\diamond H,\;F(X,u^{\prime})\diamond H^{\prime}\big)^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\bigl(D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(X,u)\diamond H,\;F(X,u^{\prime})\diamond H\big)\;\;\text{(triangle inequality)}

\displaystyle+D_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(X,u^{\prime})\diamond H,\;F(X,u^{\prime})\diamond H^{\prime}\big)\bigr)^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl(K_{u}^{F\eta}\|u-u^{\prime}\|+K_{\eta}^{F\eta}D_{\mathcal{X}}(H,H^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}\right]\;\;\text{(\autoref{as:lip-injection})}

\displaystyle\leq 2(K_{u}^{F\eta})^{2}\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{2}+2(K_{\eta}^{F\eta})^{2}\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(H,H^{\prime})^{2}].

Again, conditioning on
X
, the coupling
(H,H^{\prime})
is optimal for
\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(X,u)\big)
and
\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(X,u^{\prime})\big)
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(H,H^{\prime})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{X}}(H,H^{\prime})^{2}\mid X]\right]

(98)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{2}^{2}\bigl(\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(X,u)\big),\mathcal{N}\big(0,Q(X,u^{\prime})\big)\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle\leq(K_{u}^{Q})^{2}\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{2}.\quad\text{(\autoref{as:lip-noise})}

Therefore,

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(F_{b}(b,u),F_{b}(b,u^{\prime}))

(99)

\displaystyle\leq\left(2(K_{u}^{F\eta})^{2}+2(K_{\eta}^{F\eta}K_{u}^{Q})^{2}\right)\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{2}.

Taking square roots yields the second Lipschitz constant with

K_{u}^{F_{b}}=\left(2(K_{u}^{F\eta})^{2}+2(K_{\eta}^{F\eta}K_{u}^{Q})^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Thus, the belief dynamics
F_{b}
is Lipschitz continuous in both arguments with respect to the Wasserstein distance.
∎

Proof of
Proposition 2

Proof.

Since the Wasserstein distance is a valid metric in
\mathcal{B}

[
25
]
, it satisfies the reverse triangle inequality:

\begin{gathered}|W_{2}(b,b^{\prime\prime})-W_{2}(b^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime})|\leq W_{2}(b,b^{\prime}),\end{gathered}

(100)

where
b,b^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
.
If the running cost is the Wasserstein distance between the current belief and the next belief, then:

\displaystyle|{w}\big(b,F_{b}(b,u)\big)-{w}\big(b^{\prime},F_{b}(b^{\prime},u)\big)|

(101)

\displaystyle=|W_{2}\big(b,F_{b}(b,u)\big)-W_{2}\big(b^{\prime},F_{b}(b^{\prime},u)\big)|

\displaystyle\leq|W_{2}\big(b,F_{b}(b,u)\big)-W_{2}\big(b^{\prime},F_{b}(b,u)\big)|

\displaystyle+|W_{2}\big(b^{\prime},F_{b}(b,u)\big)-W_{2}\big(b^{\prime},F_{b}(b^{\prime},u)\big)|

\displaystyle\leq W_{2}(b,b^{\prime})+W_{2}\big(F_{b}(b,u),F_{b}(b^{\prime},u)\big)\quad\text{(\autoref{eq:reverse_tri_ineq})}

\displaystyle\leq(1+K_{x}^{F_{b}})W_{2}(b,b^{\prime}).\quad\text{(\autoref{pr:belief-lip-dyn})}

Also for the control argument,

\displaystyle|{w}(b,F_{b}(b,u))-{w}(b,F_{b}(b,u^{\prime}))|

(102)

\displaystyle=|W_{2}\big(b,F_{b}(b,u)\big)-W_{2}\big(b,F_{b}(b,u^{\prime})\big)|

\displaystyle\leq W_{2}\big(F_{b}(b,u),F_{b}(b,u^{\prime})\big)\quad\text{(\autoref{eq:reverse_tri_ineq})}

\displaystyle\leq K_{u}^{F_{b}}\|u-u^{\prime}\|.\quad\text{(\autoref{pr:belief-lip-dyn})}

Given a constant belief
b_{c}
, by
Eq. 100
, the terminal cost with Wasserstein distance satisfies:

|\psi(b)-\psi(b^{\prime})|=|W_{2}(b,b_{c})-W_{2}(b^{\prime},b_{c})|\leq W_{2}(b,b^{\prime}).

(103)

In summary, both cost functions are Lipschitz continuous with constants
K_{x}^{w}=1+K_{x}^{F_{b}}
,
K_{u}^{w}=K_{u}^{F_{b}}
and
K_{x}^{\psi}=1
.
∎
