School-based mental health promotion and behavioral modification in pubertal adolescents: A controlled intervention study
Authors: Madhurima Chongder, Suchismita Pahantasingh, Ritarani Nayak, Jhunilata Pradhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Adolescence represents a period of rapid physical, emotional, and cognitive transition. Early pubertal changes, hormonal fluctuations, and psychosocial pressures increase vulnerability to behavioral disturbances. School-based mental health programs have been shown to enhance coping ability, emotional regulation, and adaptive behavior among adolescents. To evaluate the effect of a school-based mental health program on behavioral modifications during puberty among school students in Bhubaneswar.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A quasi-experimental pre-test post-test control group design was adopted among 92 adolescents aged 9–15 years selected through purposive sampling. A socio-demographic proforma and a 30-item Behavioral Modification Scale were used. The experimental group received a structured five-session mental health program; the control group received no intervention. Data were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and Chi-square statistics.RESULTS:Pre-test maladaptive behavior was high in both groups (experimental 82.6%, control 87%). Post-intervention, adaptive behavior increased to 76.1% in the experimental group, with negligible change in the control group. Between-group differences were statistically significant (U = 147.5, P < 0.001). Behavioral improvement within the experimental group was significant (Z = –5.9, P < 0.001). Behavior was significantly associated with age, gender, pubertal stage, educational level, and family type.CONCLUSION:The school-based mental health program effectively improved behavioral modification among early adolescents. Findings highlight the necessity of integrating structured mental health education into school curricula.

Introduction

Adolescence is widely recognized as a transitional life stage marked by rapid physical maturation, cognitive restructuring, and evolving psychosocial demands. During this period, individuals experience heightened emotional sensitivity and increased vulnerability to behavioral dysregulation, which often emerges in response to pubertal changes and environmental stressors.[123] Puberty, driven by complex hormonal and neurobiological processes, contributes to fluctuations in mood, impulsivity, and social reactivity, making young adolescents particularly susceptible to both internalizing and externalizing behavioral patterns.[45] These behaviors, if left unaddressed, may extend into adulthood and influence long-term mental health trajectories.[6]

Globally, behavioral and emotional problems among adolescents have become a significant public health concern, with school-aged children increasingly demonstrating challenges such as irritability, aggression, withdrawal, and declining academic engagement.[78] Evidence suggests that such difficulties arise from the dynamic interaction between pubertal development, psychosocial influences, peer pressure, family relationships, and socio-environmental stress.[910] Although adolescence is often characterized as a period of resilience, many students lack the skills required to effectively manage emotional distress or navigate interpersonal challenges.[11]

School settings offer a unique platform for early mental health intervention due to continuous student engagement, structured learning environments, and opportunities for preventive health initiatives. Over the past decade, school-based mental health programs have shown promising results in improving self-awareness, emotional regulation, communication skills, and adaptive behavioral responses.[12131415] Such interventions not only support psychological well-being but also enhance academic performance, social competence, and overall quality of life.[1617]

In India, increasing concerns regarding early puberty, behavioral instability, and mental health stigma highlight the need for accessible school-level programs that equip adolescents with essential life skills. Despite the growing evidence supporting school-based mental health interventions, their implementation remains limited, particularly in urban government schools. Addressing this gap is crucial to supporting adolescents during a sensitive developmental phase.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured school-based mental health program on behavioral modifications during puberty among students in selected schools of Bhubaneswar.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A quasi-experimental pre-test post-test control group design was employed to examine the effectiveness of a structured school-based mental health program on behavioral modifications among adolescents experiencing pubertal transition. This design was chosen because it allows evaluation of the intervention effect while maintaining comparability between groups in natural school settings.[12] The study was conducted in two selected schools in Bhubaneswar, Odisha, following institutional approval and informed consent from parents and participants.

Study participants and sampling

This study was conducted in two selected schools in Bhubaneswar, Odisha, following institutional approval and informed consent from parents and participants. A total of 92 students, aged 9–15 years were selected using purposive sampling and divided equally into experimental and control groups (n = 46 each).

Data collection tools and technique

Data were collected using (a) a structured socio-demographic questionnaire and (b) a validated 30-item Behavioral Modification Scale. Content validity was established by experts in mental health and child development, and reliability was supported by pilot testing procedures.

Intervention

A five-session School-Based Mental Health Program delivered over one week, where sessions were on:

Understanding puberty and physical changes

Understanding puberty and physical changes

Emotional development and self-awareness

Emotional development and self-awareness

Behavioral regulation and discipline

Behavioral regulation and discipline

Stress and anger management

Stress and anger management

Self-esteem and peer relationship skills.

Self-esteem and peer relationship skills.

Methods included interactive discussion, role-play, reflection, and audio-visual materials.

Pre-test data were collected before the intervention, and post-test data were obtained one week after program completion. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic characteristics. Mann–Whitney U test compared behavioral scores between groups, Wilcoxon signed-rank test assessed within-group changes, and Chi-square tested associations with demographic variables.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics. Frequency and percentage distribution were used to describe socio-demographic characteristics. The Wilcoxon Signed Rank test assessed pre- and post-test differences within groups. The Mann–Whitney U test compared post-test behavioral modification scores between the experimental and control groups. Chi-square tests were used to determine associations between behavioral modification levels and selected socio-demographic variables. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrated a substantial improvement in behavioral modification among adolescents who participated in the school-based mental health program. The significant increase in adaptive behavior after the intervention aligns with the existing body of literature suggesting that structured psychosocial education can positively influence emotional and behavioral outcomes in school-going adolescents.[121418] The improvement observed in the experimental group underscores the importance of providing age-appropriate mental health resources that address the developmental challenges associated with puberty.

Pubertal transition is often accompanied by hormonal changes that contribute to emotional volatility, impulsivity, and fluctuations in self-esteem.[34] Prior studies have shown that adolescents in early puberty may experience heightened sensitivity to social cues, increased peer influence, and decreased ability to regulate emotions.[1920] The program’s focus on emotional coping, behavioral regulation, and self-awareness likely contributed to the decline in maladaptive behaviors and improvement in interpersonal interactions noted among participants.

The absence of behavioral improvement in the control group further reinforces the value of school-based interventions. This finding is consistent with research indicating that behavioral difficulties may persist or worsen without targeted support.[21] Additionally, the significant associations observed between behavioral outcomes and variables such as age, gender, pubertal stage, and family type support earlier evidence that developmental and environmental factors strongly influence adolescent behavior.[222324] Adolescents from supportive family environments and advanced educational levels generally exhibit better adaptive skills, as documented by previous studies.[25]

The strong effect size observed in the experimental group suggests that the intervention had a meaningful and sustained impact, consistent with global reports that life-skills education enhances resilience, peer relationships, and problem-solving abilities.[1516] The interactive design of the program—using discussions, role-play, and reflection activities—may have contributed to improved learning engagement and practical application of skills.

Overall, the findings emphasize the need for routine integration of mental health programs within school curricula, particularly during puberty when students experience significant emotional and behavioral transitions. Implementing such programs across educational institutions may reduce behavioral challenges, promote psychological well-being, and support adolescents in developing healthier patterns of adjustment.

Limitations

The only limitation of this study was it conducted in particular schools in Bhubaneswar, which restricts generalization of findings to other regions, school settings, or adolescent populations with different socio-cultural backgrounds.

Conclusion

The structured school-based mental health program demonstrated significant effectiveness in improving behavioral outcomes among adolescents experiencing pubertal changes. The intervention enhanced adaptive behavior, emotional understanding, and interpersonal skills. Incorporating such programs within school curricula can provide essential support for students navigating developmental challenges.
