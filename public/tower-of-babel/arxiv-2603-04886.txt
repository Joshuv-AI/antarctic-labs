Global versus local internal–external field separation on the sphere: a Hardy–Hodge perspective
X. Huang, C. Gerhards, Z. Ren

Abstract
Internal–external field separation is crucial for many aspects of geomagnetism, aiming at distinguishing contributions of the magnetic field generated within the Earth (or any other planet) from those produced in the exterior. When data is available on a full spherical observation surface, this separation is a standard, stable, and widely used procedure dating back to Gauss. However, when data is only available in a subdomain of the observation surface (as is the case for aeromagnetic and ground-based surveys), the situation changes. Here we show that, without prior assumptions, an internal–external field separation is not uniquely possible. Given the geophysically reasonable assumption that the exterior sources (e.g., ionospheric current systems) are located above a source-free spherical shell, we show that a unique separation becomes possible but that it is highly unstable. The results are based on a Hardy-Hodge decomposition of spherical vector fields and provide an explanation of the intrinsic difficulties of regional data-based internal-external field separation.

Introduction

Geomagnetic measurements contain contributions from various sources. The separation of internal and external fields is a fundamental problem of both mathematical and geophysical interest. In geomagnetism it is frequently used to separate the geomagnetic field into contributions originating from sources within the domain enclosed by the observation surface (e.g., a satellite’s orbit) and from sources in the exterior of the observation surface (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
12
,
24
,
26
,
27
]
). This problem is mathematically closely related to the characterization of silent magnetizations (i.e., magnetizations whose corresponding magnetic fields vanish inside or outside of the source layer), although the geophysical interpretation is slightly different. The latter has been investigated quite intensively, e.g.,
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
14
,
17
,
21
]
in the context of inverse magnetization problems. Disentangling such mixed-source signals is essential for interpretation, yet, generally a challenging inverse problem. However, internal-external field separation in geomagnetism is a comparatively tractable exception: it follows from a canonical decomposition of vector fields on the observation surface, the so-called Hardy-Hodge decomposition.

If the observation surface is a sphere and data is available on the full sphere, the internal-external field separation is unique and stable. In fact, it coincides with the expansion in terms of well-known (Gauss) vector spherical harmonics. This expansion in terms of spherical harmonics underpins almost all global geomagnetic field models, e.g.,
[
2
,
11
,
22
,
23
,
34
]
.

Satellite observations provide a near-global data coverage at a given altitude, making global internal-external field separations directly applicable. However, due to their altitude, sources like ionospheric current systems lie within the satellite’s orbit, such that the internal magnetic field contributions do not only contain the often sought-after contributions due to processes inside the Earth but also those due to processes outside the Earth (a better separation of these processes can be valuable, e.g., in geomagnetic depth-sounding and magnetotellurics, where transfer functions play a crucial role; see, e.g.,
[
16
,
20
,
31
,
32
]
). Ground-based and aeromagnetic measurements provide a remedy to this, due to their location below these ionospheric sources. Furthermore, ground-based measurement can additionally provide continuous time series at fixed locations and are particularly valuable for capturing temporal variations and induction responses over a broad frequency band (although temporal variations will not be considered in the paper at hand). In recent years, efforts of continental-scale arrays (e.g., EMScope in the United States
[
35
]
, AusLAMP in Australia
[
36
]
, and SinoProbe in China
[
10
]
) have substantially improved spatial resolution over land. However, coverage remains geographically heterogeneous and incomplete (notably over oceanic regions). Therefore, a natural question is whether internal-external field separation (in particular, global spherical harmonic-based approaches) can be meaningfully adapted to geographically incomplete observations, i.e., geomagnetic measurements available only on a patch
U\subset\mathbb{S}
of the observation sphere
\mathbb{S}
. This motivates to ask what remains true and what necessarily fails when global separation is restricted to local data.

A convenient language for analyzing the effect of patch restriction is provided by the Hardy-Hodge framework,e.g., from
[
5
,
15
]
, which formulates internal-external separation in terms of function spaces and extends beyond the spherical-harmonic setting. In this framework, one models external-source contributions by the Hardy space
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and internal-source contributions by the Hardy space
\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
. All other contributions are collected in the space of tangential, divergence-free vector fields
\mathcal{H}_{df}(\mathbb{S})
. That is, any vector field in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}
can be decomposed into these three contributions. For brevity, we only consider the decomposition into the two Hardy-components and drop the tangential divergence-free contribution. From a geophysical point of view, tangential divergence-free contributions are indeed present in the magnetic field at satellite altitude (since source currents are present across the orbit), but in aeromagnetic and ground-based data (which are the main aim of our study on regional data) such contributions are negligible. From a mathematical point of view, tangential divergence-free contributions do add to the nonuniqueness of the studied problem, but they do not contribute to the instability. Furthermore, the nonuniqueness due to them plays only a minor role in the overall nonuniqueness characterization.

Therefore, the localized separation question that we study in this paper reads as follows: given
\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})\oplus\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}\subset L^{2}(U)^{3}
on a subset (which we call “patch“)
U\subset\mathbb{S}
, can one determine
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
,
\mathbf{f}_{-}\in\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
such that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})\big|_{U}=\mathbf{d}\,?

(1)

We investigate the two basic issues of uniqueness and stability. The aim is to clarify the
structural limitations
of localized internal-external field separation rather than to propose corresponding reconstruction algorithms. Our results can be summarized as follows:

(i)
Non-uniqueness on patches.
Without additional assumptions, localized measurements do not determine internal and external contributions uniquely: there exist nontrivial internal and external fields whose sum vanishes on the observation patch.

(ii)
Uniqueness under a geophysically motivated altitude/analyticity condition (source-free shell).

Uniqueness can be restored if one imposes the following condition: external contributions are assumed to be generated only above some fixed altitude, which is equivalent to saying there exists a source-free shell bordering the observation surface.

(iii)
Ill-posedness persists.
Even under such an altitude/analyticity condition, the problem remains ill-posed: arbitrarily small perturbations of the data on the patch may produce large changes in the separated components.

(iv)
Conditional stability.
Assuming appropriate bounds on the internal and external contributions, we can derive a logarithmic conditional stability estimate. In particular, we can quantify the influence of the thickness of the source-free shell on the stability.

The paper is organized as follows: Section
2
recalls the Hardy-Hodge decomposition on closed Lipschitz surfaces and its spherical specialization. Section
3
studies the uniqueness of localized separation on spherical patches, establishing non-uniqueness under general conditions, and uniqueness under the altitude/analyticity condition. The corresponding instability and conditional stability is investigated in Section
4
.

Convention (sources vs. domains).

Throughout,
internal
(resp.
external
) refers to the
location of sources
relative to the observation surface: internal sources lie below the surface and external sources above it. We reserve
interior
and
exterior
for the corresponding potential-theoretic regions (inside vs. outside the observation surface). Accordingly, an external-source potential is harmonic in the interior region enclosed by the observation surface, while an internal-source potential is harmonic in the exterior region and decays at infinity. This matches the standard Gauss interpretation (regular vs. decaying solid spherical harmonics) and avoids ambiguity when switching between physical and potential-theoretic language.

2
Hardy–Hodge decomposition as a general framework for internal–external source separation

In this section, we recall the Hardy–Hodge decomposition as a functional-analytic framework for global internal–external source separation. For more precise properties of the topics mentioned in this section, we refer the reader to
[
5
,
15
]
. We summarize the layer-potential characterization of the Hardy-Hodge decomposition on general Lipschitz surfaces, which provides the key preliminaries needed for the proofs in Sections
3
and
4
]. We also briefly indicate how, in the spherical setting, this framework recovers the classical (Gauss) vector spherical harmonic separation.

2.1
Hardy–Hodge decomposition on closed Lipschitz surfaces

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a Lipschitz domain with
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
its closed boundary, oriented with respect to the outward unit normal

\eta
. For a vector field
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}
we use the normal–tangential splitting

\mathbf{f}=\eta f_{\eta}+\mathbf{f}_{T},\qquad f_{\eta}=\mathbf{f}\cdot\eta\in L^{2}(\Gamma),\quad\mathbf{f}_{T}=\mathbf{f}-(\mathbf{f}\cdot\eta)\eta\in T(\Gamma),

(2)

where
T(\Gamma)
denotes the space of square-integrable tangential vector fields on
\Gamma
.

We recall the boundary integral operators for the Laplacian on
\Gamma
: the boundary single-layer operator
S
, the boundary double-layer operator
K
, and the tangential gradient operator
\nabla_{T}S
together with its adjoint
(\nabla_{T}S)^{\ast}
. We refer to
[
5
]
for precise definitions, mapping properties on Lipschitz surfaces, and the associated trace/jump relations. In what follows, we only use the identities introduced below and the fact that these operators are bounded on the natural
L^{2}
- and trace spaces.

The
B
-operators and Hardy spaces.

Following
[
5
, Defs. 3.2 and 3.15]
, define the scalar operators
B_{o},B_{i}:L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}\to L^{2}(\Gamma)
by

B_{o}\mathbf{f}=-\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}-K\Big)f_{\eta}+(\nabla_{T}S)^{\ast}\mathbf{f}_{T},\qquad B_{i}\mathbf{f}=\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}+K\Big)f_{\eta}+(\nabla_{T}S)^{\ast}\mathbf{f}_{T}.

(3)

Their adjoints
B_{o}^{\ast},B_{i}^{\ast}:L^{2}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}
are given explicitly by

[
5
, Lemmas 3.5 and 3.18]
:

B_{o}^{\ast}g=-\eta\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}-K^{\ast}\Big)g+\nabla_{T}S\,g,\qquad B_{i}^{\ast}g=\eta\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}+K^{\ast}\Big)g+\nabla_{T}S\,g.

(4)

We define the Hardy-type subspaces by

\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)=\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(B_{o}^{\ast})}^{\,L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)=\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(B_{i}^{\ast})}^{\,L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}}.

(5)

Potential-theoretic interpretation (physical meaning).

Although
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}(\Gamma)
are introduced via the boundary operators
B_{o}^{\ast},B_{i}^{\ast}
, they admit a direct potential-theoretic characterization. Elements of
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)
coincide with
L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}
-traces of gradient fields
\nabla u
generated by harmonic scalar potentials
u
in the interior domain
\Omega
. Elements of
\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)
coincide with traces of gradients
\nabla u
of harmonic potentials
u
in the exterior domain
\Omega^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
that satisfy a suitable decay condition at infinity. The equivalence between this interpretation and the
operator-based definitions (
5
) follows from the layer-potential analysis in
[
5
]
. More precisely:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\{\nabla u|_{\Gamma}:\Delta u=0\textrm{ in }\Omega,\|(\nabla u)_{i}^{M}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}}<\infty\},

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\{\nabla u|_{\Gamma}:\Delta u=0\textrm{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega},\lim_{x\to\infty}u(x)=0,\|(\nabla u)_{o}^{M}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}}<\infty\},

(7)

where
(\nabla u)_{i}^{M}
and
(\nabla u)_{o}^{M}
denote the inner and outer notangential maximal function, respectively (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
18
]
). In geomagnetic potential-field applications, it is convenient to read this characterization in terms of source location:

external sources (outside
\Gamma
):

u
harmonic in
\Omega

\Rightarrow

\nabla u|_{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)

internal sources (inside
\Gamma
):

u
harmonic in
\Omega^{c}
,
u(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty

\Rightarrow

\nabla u|_{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)

The divergence-free component.

For the sake of completeness, we introduce the third contribution

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\Gamma)=\Big\{\,\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}:\mathbf{f}\cdot\eta=0\ \text{and}\ \operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{f}=0\,\Big\},

(8)

where
\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}
denotes the (weak) surface-divergence on
\Gamma
.
This space is
L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}
-orthogonal to
\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)+\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)
(see
[
5
]
for the precise functional-analytic formulation).
In the present paper,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\Gamma)
will not play an explicit role in the localized analysis of
Sections
3
and
4
. It is included here only to state the full decomposition of vector fields.

Hardy–Hodge decomposition as a topological direct sum (global stability).

The main Hardy–Hodge result of
[
5
]
yields a continuous (topological) direct-sum decomposition of
L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}
into the above components:

L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}=\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)+\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)+\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\Gamma).

(9)

That is, every
\mathbf{f}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}
admits a unique decomposition
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{df}}
with
\mathbf{f}_{\pm}\in\mathcal{H}_{\pm}(\Gamma)
and
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{df}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\Gamma)
. The mapping
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)\times\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)\times\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}
,
(\mathbf{f}_{+},\mathbf{f}_{-},\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{df}})\mapsto\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{df}}
is injective. Its inverse
L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)\times\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)\times\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\Gamma)
,
\mathbf{f}\mapsto(\mathbf{f}_{+},\mathbf{f}_{-},\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{df}})
is Lipschitz-continuous (equivalently, the associated projections are bounded). On a general Lipschitz surface this splitting is typically not orthogonal, in the sense that
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)
and
\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)
need not be orthogonal to each other, although
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\Gamma)
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\Gamma)+\mathcal{H}_{-}(\Gamma)
with respect to the
L^{2}(\Gamma)^{3}
-inner product. Nevertheless, when data are available on the full closed surface
\Gamma
, the induced internal-external separation is
stable
in the sense of continuous dependence of
\mathbf{f}_{\pm}
on the data
\mathbf{f}
. This contrasts sharply with the localized setting studied in Sections
3
and
4
. Orthogonality of the function spaces is recovered in the spherical setting, which we review in Section
2.2
.

2.2
Spherical Hardy–Hodge decomposition and (Gauss) vector spherical harmonics

On the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|=1\}
we write
\eta(x)=x
for the outward unit normal. Let
\{Y_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\;k=-n,\dots,n}
be an orthonormal basis of scalar spherical harmonics (see, e.g.,
[
13
,
25
]
). We recall the standard vector spherical harmonic families aligned with with the spherical Helmholtz decomposition:

\mathbf{Y}_{n,k}=\eta\,Y_{n,k},\qquad\mathbf{\Psi}_{n,k}=\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Y_{n,k},\qquad\mathbf{\Phi}_{n,k}=\eta\times\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Y_{n,k}.

(10)

The fields
\mathbf{\Phi}_{n,k}
are tangential and spherical-divergence free (also known as the spherical curl fields or toroidal fields), hence they span the tangential divergence-free subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\mathbb{S})=\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{\mathbf{\Phi}_{n,k}:n\geq 1,\ |k|\leq n\}
.

(Gauss) internal/external poloidal fields.

We now consider the solid harmonics, with
r=|x|
and
\xi=\tfrac{x}{|x|}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
,

V^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n,k}(x)=r^{n}Y_{n,k}(\xi),\quad r<1\qquad\text{and}\qquad V^{\mathrm{int}}_{n,k}(x)=r^{-(n+1)}Y_{n,k}(\xi),\quad r>1

which represent harmonic functions inside and outside the unit ball
\mathbb{B}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|<1\}
, respectively.
Using
\nabla=\eta\,\partial_{r}+r^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}
, their gradients restricted to
\mathbb{S}
are

\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n,k}=\left.\nabla V^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n,k}\right|_{r\to 1^{-}}=\mathbf{\Psi}_{n,k}+n\mathbf{Y}_{n,k},\qquad\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{int}}_{n,k}=\left.\nabla V^{\mathrm{int}}_{n,k}\right|_{r\to 1^{+}}=\mathbf{\Psi}_{n,k}-(n+1)\mathbf{Y}_{n,k}.

(11)

We follow the source-location convention stated in the introduction:external and internal refer to the location of the sources relative to the observation sphere.
Consequently, external-source fields correspond to potentials that are harmonic inside the unit ball (regular solid harmonics), whereas internal-source fields correspond to potentials that are harmonic in the exterior of the unit ball (decaying solid harmonics). With this convention, the (Gauss) vector spherical harmonics
\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n,k}
represent external-source contributions and
\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{int}}_{n,k}
represent internal-source contributions.

Connection to the
B
-operators.

On the sphere, we recall the definition of
B
-operators (note that
K
is self-adjoint on the sphere)

B_{i}^{\ast}=\eta\Bigl(K^{*}+\tfrac{1}{2}I\Bigr)+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S,\qquad B_{o}^{\ast}=\eta\Bigl(K^{*}-\tfrac{1}{2}I\Bigr)+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S,

(12)

where
S
and
K
denote the (boundary) single- and double-layer operators (notice that, on the sphere, the double layer potential is self-adjoint, i.e.,
K^{\ast}=K
holds). Both layer potential operators diagonalize in the scalar spherical harmonic basis. In particular, one has (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
)

S\,Y_{n,k}=-\tfrac{1}{2n+1}\,Y_{n,k},\qquad K\,Y_{n,k}=\tfrac{1}{2(2n+1)}\,Y_{n,k}.

(13)

Consequently, for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle B_{o}^{\ast}(Y_{n,k})=-\tfrac{1}{2n+1}\Bigl(\mathbf{\Psi}_{n,k}+n\mathbf{Y}_{n,k}\Bigr)=-\tfrac{1}{2n+1}\,\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n,k},

(14)

\displaystyle B_{i}^{\ast}(Y_{n,k})=-\tfrac{1}{2n+1}\Bigl(\mathbf{\Psi}_{n,k}-(n+1)\mathbf{Y}_{n,k}\Bigr)=-\tfrac{1}{2n+1}\,\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{int}}_{n,k}.

(15)

Identification of the spherical Hardy spaces.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S})
denote the spherical Hardy spaces (poloidal components) in the sense of the Hardy–Hodge decomposition. Under the above source-location convention, we identify

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n,k}:n\geq 1,\ |k|\leq n\}}^{\,L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}=\overline{\mathrm{Ran}(B_{o}^{\ast})}^{\,L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{int}}_{n,k}:n\geq 0,\ |k|\leq n\}}^{\,L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}=\overline{\mathrm{Ran}(B_{i}^{\ast})}^{\,L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}.

(17)

Together with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\mathbb{S})
, this yields the spherical Hardy–Hodge decomposition

L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}=\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{df}}(\mathbb{S}),

(18)

and, in contrast to the general Lipschitz-surface setting of Section
2.1
, the three subspaces in (
18
) are mutually orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}
.

Remark 1

(Zero degree case)
.

For
n=0
one has
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Y_{0,0}=0
and
(K-\tfrac{1}{2})Y_{0,0}=0
. Thus, only internal-source fields contain the degree-zero (monopole) term. Accordingly,
B_{o}^{\ast}
is invertible only on the zero-mean subspace
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})/\langle 1\rangle
.

For later reference, we denote by
\mathbb{S}_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|=r\}
and
\mathbb{B}_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|<r\}
the sphere and ball of radius
r>0
, respectively.

3
Nonuniqueness and uniqueness for patches

Let
U
be a fixed subregion of
\mathbb{S}
(a “surface patch”). Throughout the paper, we assume
U
has a connected Lipschitz boundary, and both
U
and
U^{c}=\mathbb{S}\setminus\overline{U}
have proper interiors on the sphere. We study internal-external field separation from data that are only available on
U
. That is, we are confronted with the question (
1
) from the introduction.

3.1
Localized separation is non-unique in general

The following theorem shows that, without additional assumptions, the answer to uniqueness is negative.

Theorem 2

(Non-uniqueness for patch data)
.

The restriction operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{U}:\;\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})\times\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})\longrightarrow L^{2}(U)^{3},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{U}(\mathbf{f}_{+},\mathbf{f}_{-})=(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U},

(19)

has the nontrivial null space

\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{A}_{U})=\big\{(B_{o}^{\ast}f,B_{i}^{\ast}g):\,

\displaystyle f=[(K^{\ast}+1/2)S^{-1}h_{1}]|_{U}+h_{2},\,g=S^{-1}h_{1}-f,

(20)

\displaystyle\textnormal{for any }h_{1}\in\tilde{H}^{1}(U^{c}),h_{2}\in L^{2}(U^{c})\big\},

with
\tilde{H}^{1}(U^{c})=\{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{S}):f=\textnormal{const. on }\,U\}
and
L^{2}(U^{c})=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}):f=0\textnormal{ on }\,U\}
.

Proof.

The proof is based on layer-potential representations of Hardy components and on the characterization of
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S})
via the
B
-operators from Section
2.2
. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{+}

\displaystyle=B_{o}^{\ast}f=\eta(K^{\ast}-\tfrac{1}{2})f+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf,

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{-}

\displaystyle=B_{i}^{\ast}g=\eta(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})g+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sg.

(22)

Then,
(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U}=0
if and only if

\displaystyle(K^{\ast}-\tfrac{1}{2})f+(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})g=(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})(f+g)-f=0\quad\text{on}\quad U,

(23)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sf+\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}Sg=\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}S(f+g)=0\quad\text{on}\quad U,

(24)

i.e., the tangential part and normal part of
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}
vanish on
U
. Condition (
24
) implies that
S(f+g)
is constant on
U
. Thus,

\displaystyle f+g=S^{-1}h

(25)

for some
h\in\tilde{H}^{1}(U^{c})
, since
S
is bounded and invertible from
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
to
H^{1}(\mathbb{S})
. From (
23
) we then obtain

\displaystyle f|_{U}=[(K^{\ast}+1/2)S^{-1}h]|_{U}.

(26)

On
U^{c}
,
f
can be chosen to be any square-integrable function. Combining the observations from above, we eventually obtain the representation of the nullspace as provided in (
20
).
∎

Remark 3

(Geophysical interpretation)
.

In particular, Theorem
2
states that there exist nonzero pairs
(\mathbf{f}_{+},\mathbf{f}_{-})\in\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})\times\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
such that
(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U}=0
and, hence, internal-external field separation from information restricted to
U
is
not
unique in general.

This illustrates a basic limitation of localized geomagnetic observations: when data are only available on a restricted subregion of the sphere (e.g. a continental-scale array footprint), there may exist internal and external contributions that annihilate each other on the observation domain and thus do not contribute to the measured data.

3.2
An altitude/analyticity condition restores uniqueness

The non-uniqueness in Theorem
2
shows that patch data alone are insufficient for unconditional internal-external separation within the full classes
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
. In many geophysical settings, however, the external current systems are not arbitrarily close to the Earth’s surface. Near-surface air is typically treated as an effective insulator at the frequencies of interest, and the dominant external sources of the geo-electromagnetic system are associated with ionospheric and magnetospheric current systems (and plasma dynamics), which are located at altitudes typically
60\,\mathrm{km}
above the surface
[
9
]
. With
a
denoting the Earth’s radius, an altitude
h
above the Earth’s surface corresponds to
r=1+h/a
in our unit-sphere normalization (i.e., for
a\approx 6371\,\mathrm{km}
,
h\approx 60\,\mathrm{km}
gives
r\approx 1.01
). This motivates imposing an altitude-constraint (or, equivalently, an analyticity-constraint) on the external-source component, meaning that the annulus
A_{a,a+h}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:a<|x|<a+h\}
(or the annulus
A_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:1<|x|<r\}
in our normalized setting) is considered source-free. Internal sources may still be arbitrarily close to the measurement surface. That is, our results hold true for measurements at the Earth’s surface. We show that such a constraint restores uniqueness of the localized internal-external field separation. But it does not remove the fundamental instability of the inverse problem.

Definition 4

(External-source altitude class)
.

For a fixed
r>1
, we define
\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})\subset\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
as the class of external-source fields on
\mathbb{S}
whose associated scalar potential extends harmonically to the entire ball
\mathbb{B}_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|<r\}
of radius
r
. More precisely,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle=\{\nabla u|_{\mathbb{S}}:\Delta u=0\textrm{ in }\mathbb{B}_{r},\|(\nabla u)_{i}^{M}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}_{r})^{3}}<\infty\}.

(27)

Any
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})
has an associated scalar potential
u_{+}
that is harmonic in the unit ball and extends harmonically (hence, real-analytically) to the ball
\mathbb{B}_{r}
of radius
r>1
. In the spherical harmonic expansion of
u_{+}
and
\mathbf{f}_{+}
, the expansion coefficients satisfy a geometric (exponential-type) decay governed by
r
. In particular, each external (Gauss) vector spherical harmonic
\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n,k}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})
for every
r>1
.

Under the above condition on the external-source component, localized internal-external field separation becomes unique.

Theorem 5

(Uniqueness for patch data under an altitude/analyticity constraint)
.

Let
r>1
be fixed and denote by
\mathcal{A}_{U}^{(r)}:\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})\times\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})\to L^{2}(U)^{3}
the restriction of
\mathcal{A}_{U}
to
\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})\times\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
. Then,
\mathcal{A}_{U}^{(r)}
has only the trivial nullspace

\displaystyle\ker(\mathcal{A}_{U}^{(r)})=\big\{(0,0)\big\}.

Proof.

Take
(\mathbf{f}_{+},\mathbf{f}_{-})\in\mathcal{H}^{(r)}_{+}(\mathbb{S})\times\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
such that
(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U}=0
. By the definition of the underlying spaces, there exist functions
u_{+}
and
u_{-}
that are harmonic in
\mathbb{B}_{r}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}}
, respectively, such that
\mathbf{f}_{+}=\nabla u_{+}|_{\mathbb{S}}
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}=\nabla u_{-}|_{\mathbb{S}}
. Therefore, the function
w=u_{+}+u_{-}
is harmonic in the annulus
A_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:1<|x|<r\}
. Due to the assumption
(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U}=0
, its trace additionally satisfies
\nabla w|_{U}=0
and, hence,
\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}w=0
and
\partial_{\eta}^{+}w=0
on
U
. The former implies that
w
is constant on
U
and leads to the following Cauchy data on the patch
U
:

\displaystyle w=\text{const. on }U,\qquad\partial_{\eta}^{+}w=0\ \text{ on }U.

(28)

From a Holmgren/analyticity-type uniqueness argument as in
[
3
, Lemma 1]
, it follows that
w
is constant in
A_{r}
and, hence,
\nabla w=0
in the annulus
A_{r}
. Taking the trace of
\nabla w
on
\mathbb{S}
yields
\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-}|_{\mathbb{S}}=\nabla w|_{\mathbb{S}}=0
. Thus,
\mathbf{f}_{+}=-\mathbf{f}_{-}
on
\mathbb{S}
. Since
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})\cap\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})=\{0\}
due to their orthogonality, we eventually obtain
\mathbf{f}_{+}=\mathbf{f}_{-}=0
.
∎

Remark 6
.

In particular, Theorem
5
states that there exist
no
nonzero pairs
(\mathbf{f}_{+},\mathbf{f}_{-})\in\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})\times\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
such that
(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U}=0
and, hence, internal-external field separation from information restricted to
U
is unique in these spaces.

In planetary-scale geomagnetic modelling, imposing a spherical bandlimit is standard. In such a setting, Theorem
5
applies directly and yields the following corollary.

Corollary 7

(Bandlimited external-source fields)
.

If the external-source component
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
is bandlimited with respect to its spherical harmonic expansion, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{+}\in\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n,k}:n\leq N,\,|k|\leq n\}

for some fixed integer
N
, then it satisfies the condition of Theorem
5
. Hence, the restriction of
\mathcal{A}_{U}
to
\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n,k}:n\leq N,\,|k|\leq n\}\times\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
is injective.

Remark 8

(Geophysical interpretation)
.

The above illustrates that geophysically reasonable assumptions suffice to (theoretically) enable internal-external field separation from regional data. Furthermore, bandlimited signals in terms of vector spherical harmonics, as typically assumed in global geomagnetic modeling, yield a unique separation. However, for the latter it should be said that real geomagnetic fields are not bandlimited, so that the separation in terms of finitely many vector spherical harmonics does not solve the actual underlying nonuniqueness (it could rather be considered an initial step for regularization).

4
Instability and conditional stability for patches

Uniqueness does not imply stability. Even under the altitude/analyticity condition, the inverse problem remains ill-posed on a patch. The separation problem suffers from the following local density property:
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}\cap\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}
with respect to
L^{2}(U)^{3}
-norm. In particular, the same approximation property applies to
\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}
since
\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})\subset\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
. Therefore, the inverse map of
\mathcal{A}_{U}^{(r)}
cannot be bounded, even under the altitude/analyticity condition. However, appropriate source conditions on
\mathbf{f}_{\pm}
allow at least logarithmic stability estimate.

4.1
Instability despite uniqueness

The density of
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}\cap\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}
in
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}
follows from the following analysis of the nullspace
\ker(\mathcal{A}_{U})
.

Theorem 9
.

The space
\mathrm{proj}_{\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})}(\ker(\mathcal{A}_{U}))
is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
. That is, for any
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{\pm}\in\mathcal{H}_{\pm}(\mathbb{S})
such that

\displaystyle(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{+}+\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{-})|_{U}=0

and

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{+}-\mathbf{f}_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}\leq\varepsilon.

(29)

Proof.

Equation (
29
) is a direct consequence of the mentioned density. Thus, we only need to prove that
\mathrm{proj}_{\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})}(\ker(\mathcal{A}_{U}))
is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
. Since the operator
B_{o}^{\ast}
is bounded from
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
to
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
, it suffices to show that the scalar functions
f
satisfying conditions (
25
) and (
26
) form a dense subset of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
.

Again, note that the two equations (
25
) and (
26
) impose no particular constraints on
f|_{U^{c}}
. This means that we can choose
f
freely on
U^{c}
, and
h
can be chosen freely in
\tilde{H}^{1}(U^{c})
. Via the choice
g=S^{-1}h-f
, equation (
25
) is then always satisfied. Therefore, it suffices to show that the restrictions
f|_{U}
, for
f
satisfying (
26
), are dense in
L^{2}(U)
. We denote this set by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\left\{\,\big[(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})S^{-1}h\big]\big|_{U}:h\in\tilde{H}^{1}(U^{c})\,\right\}\subset L^{2}(U).

(30)

We prove its density by showing that
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}=\{0\}
. For any
f^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}^{\perp}\subset L^{2}(U)
, we denote by
\bar{f^{\ast}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
the zero extension of
f^{\ast}
on the full sphere
\mathbb{S}
. Then, it holds for any
f\in\mathcal{M}
, that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left\langle f|_{U},f^{\ast}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}=\left\langle f,\bar{f^{\ast}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}=\left\langle(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})S^{-1}h,\bar{f^{\ast}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}

\displaystyle=\left\langle h,(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})S^{-1}\bar{f^{\ast}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})},

(31)

for
h\in\tilde{H}^{1}(U^{c})
corresponding to
f
. In the last equation we have used that
\bigl(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)S^{-1}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, which holds for general smooth surfaces by the Plemelj’s symmetrization principle
[
19
, Lemma 2.5]
.

Since
{H}^{1}(U^{c})=\{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{S}):f=0\textnormal{ on }\,U\}\subset\tilde{H}^{1}(U^{c})
is dense in
L^{2}(U^{c})
, this means
(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})S^{-1}\bar{f^{\ast}}
must vanish on
U^{c}
. Moreover,
\bar{f^{\ast}}
vanishes on
U^{c}
by definition. Thus,
\bar{f^{\ast}}=0
on the full sphere, which implies
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}=\{0\}
and, therefore, the statement of the theorem. However, the conclusion
\bar{f^{\ast}}=0
follows from the uniqueness of a Cauchy-Laplace problem that requires some more precise argumentation. This we do in the remainder of the proof.

Since
\bar{f}^{\ast}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
, we only have
\mu=S^{-1}\bar{f}^{\ast}\in H^{-1}(\mathbb{S})
. To be able to apply the Cauchy-Laplace uniqueness argument for smooth functions, let
T_{\varepsilon}
be a spherical mollifier

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}k(x\cdot y)g(y)d\sigma(y)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}),

(32)

with kernel
k_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}([-1,1])
and
\textnormal{supp}(k_{\varepsilon})\subset[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
, such that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|T_{\varepsilon}g-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}=0
for all
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S})
. Now, set
\bar{f}^{\ast}_{\varepsilon}=T_{\varepsilon}\bar{f}^{\ast}
and

\mu_{\varepsilon}=S^{-1}\bar{f}^{\ast}_{\varepsilon}
.
Because
T_{\varepsilon}
commutes with
S
and
K^{\ast}
, we have

\displaystyle(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})\mu_{\varepsilon}=(K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})S^{-1}\bar{f}^{\ast}_{\varepsilon}=T_{\varepsilon}\big((K^{\ast}+\tfrac{1}{2})S^{-1}\bar{f}^{\ast}\big).

(33)

Moreover, since
\bar{f}^{\ast}=0
on
U^{c}
, we have
\bar{f}^{\ast}_{\varepsilon}=0
on the shrunken open patch
U^{c}_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in U^{c}:\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{S}}(x,\partial U)>\varepsilon\}
, and similarly
(K^{\ast}+1/2)\mu_{\varepsilon}=0
on
U^{c}_{\varepsilon}
. Next, set
u_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{S}\mu_{\varepsilon}
to be the single-layer potential of
\mu_{\varepsilon}
, in the sense

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\mathcal{S}\mu_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{1}{|x-y|}\mu_{\varepsilon}(y)\,d\sigma(y),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}}.

(34)

Then, the jump relations for the single-layer potential imply
u_{\varepsilon}=\bar{f}^{\ast}_{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{\eta}^{-}u_{\varepsilon}=(K^{\ast}+1/2)\mu_{\varepsilon}
on
\mathbb{S}
(see, e.g.,
[
5
]
). The previous observations then lead to
u_{\varepsilon}=0
and
\partial_{\eta}^{-}u_{\varepsilon}=0
on
U^{c}_{\varepsilon}
. Since
\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}}
and
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}u_{\varepsilon}(x)=0
, we have exactly the desired Cauchy-Laplace problem. The Holmgren/analyticity argument from
[
3
, Lemma 1]
then implies that
u_{\varepsilon}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{B}}
. Taking the trace, we get
\bar{f}^{\ast}_{\varepsilon}=0
on
\mathbb{S}
. Observing
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\bar{f}^{\ast}_{\varepsilon}-\bar{f}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}=0
, we finally obtain
\bar{f}^{\ast}=0
on
\mathbb{S}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 10
.

The set
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}\cap\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}
is a dense subset of
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})|_{U}
. That is, for any
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\mathbf{f}_{-}\in\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
, such that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{f}_{+}-\mathbf{f}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}\leq\varepsilon.

(35)

Proof.

We can simply choose
\mathbf{f}_{-}=-\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{-}
where
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{+}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{-}
indicate the functions stated in (
29
). Then it holds

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{f}_{+}-\mathbf{f}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{f}_{+}+\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}\leq\|\mathbf{f}_{+}-\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{+}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}+\|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{+}+\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{+}-\mathbf{f}_{+}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}\leq\|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{+}-\mathbf{f}_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}\leq\varepsilon,

which is the desired statement.
∎

Since
\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})\subset\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{S})
, equation (
35
) holds in particular for any
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})
. This means that, even under the altitude/analyticity condition, internal-external field separation cannot be stable.

Corollary 11

(Instability on patches)
.

There exists
no
continuous function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Phi(\varepsilon)=0
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{f}_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}+\|\mathbf{f}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}\leq\Phi\Big(\|(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}\Big),\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{f}_{+}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S}),\ \mathbf{f}_{-}\in\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S}).

(36)

Remark 12

(Implications for practice)
.

Theorem
5
and Corollary
10
show that localized internal-external separation remains challenging in practice. An altitude-type assumption can remove that ambiguity of non-uniqueness, but small perturbations of patch data may still cause large changes in the separated components. The combination of additional prior information or regularization methods are, therefore, typically necessary. Among others, a fairly common regularization method in planetary geomagnetism are generalized Slepian functions as indicated, e.g., in
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
, which also comply with the often used assumption of bandlimitedness.

4.2
A quantitative conditional estimate

Instability of harmonic continuation has long been known and quantitative estimates have been supplied, e.g., in
[
1
,
8
,
33
]
. Since harmonic continuation is also the underlying driver of the internal-external field separation problem, we can make use of some of these insights in our setup. Assuming appropriate bounds on the involved Hardy-components, we can derive the upcoming logarithmic stability estimate. For that purpose, we note that, for every
\mathbf{f}_{+}=\nabla u_{+}|_{\mathbb{S}}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})
, the underlying potential can be expressed in the form
u_{+}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}\tfrac{\varphi_{+}(y)}{|x-y|}d\sigma(y)
,
x\in\mathbb{B}_{r}
, for some
\varphi_{+}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}_{r})
.

Theorem 13

(Conditional logarithmic stability on patches)
.

Let
M>0
be fixed and
1<r<R
, for some sufficiently small
R>1
. Then, for every
\mathbf{f}_{+}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})
,
\mathbf{f}_{-}\in\mathcal{H}_{-}(\mathbb{S})
with
\|\mathbf{f}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}\leq M
,
\|\varphi_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}_{r})}\leq M
, it holds

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{f}_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}+\|\mathbf{f}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}\leq\Phi\Big(\|(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}\Big),

(37)

for
\|(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}
sufficiently small, with

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=\frac{C_{1}(r)}{(r-1)^{2}}\left|\ln\left(\frac{C_{2}(r)}{t}\right)\right|^{-a(r)},\quad t>0,

(38)

and constants
c>0
,
C(r)\geq 1
,

\displaystyle C_{2}(r)=\frac{cM}{(r-1)^{3}},\quad a(r)=e^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}.

(39)

Apart from the indicated dependence on
r
, all constants depend only on the patch
U
and
R
.

Before we can prove the theorem above, we require the following lemma.

Lemma 14
.

Let
w\in H^{1}(A_{r})
be harmonic in the annulus
A_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:1<|x|<r\}
, for some sufficiently small
r>1
. Furthermore, let
s=\tfrac{r-1}{4}
, so that
A_{1+s}\subset A_{r}
. Then it holds

\displaystyle\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}\leq\frac{C(r)}{(r-1)^{2}}\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}\left|\ln\left(\frac{\frac{c}{r-1}\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}}{\|w\|_{H^{1}(U)}+\|\partial_{\eta}^{+}w\|_{L^{2}(U)}}\right)\right|^{-a(r)},

(40)

for
\|w\|_{H^{1}(U)}+\|\partial_{\eta}^{+}w\|_{L^{2}(U)}
sufficiently small, with constants
C(r)\geq 1
,
a(r)=e^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}
, and
c>0
only depending on the patch
U
(and the indicated dependence on
r
).

Proof.

From application of Caccioppoli’s inequality, we get
\|w\|_{H^{2}(A_{1+s/2})}\leq cs^{-1}\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}
, for some constant
c
. And for brevity, we set
\varepsilon=\|w\|_{H^{1}(U)}+\|\partial_{\eta}^{+}w\|_{L^{2}(U)}
. Inserting this into
[
8
, Cor. 2.1]
, we directly get the interior estimate

\displaystyle\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s/2})}\leq Ccs^{-1}\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}\left|\ln\left(\frac{cs^{-1}\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|^{-a},

(41)

for some constants
C>0
and
a\in(0,1)
. Joining
Cc
into a generic constant
C>0
, with
c>0
being generic as well (possibly depending on the patch
U
), and observing
s=\frac{r-1}{4}
, we get after application of a trace estimate and Caccioppoli’s inequality that

\displaystyle\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s/4})^{3}}\leq\tfrac{C}{s}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(A_{1+s/2})^{3}}\leq\tfrac{C}{s}\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s/2})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{(r-1)^{2}}\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}\left|\ln\left(\frac{\tfrac{c}{r-1}\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right|^{-a}.

(42)

Thus, it remains to justify the exponential expressions of
C
and
a
with respect to
r
.
[
8
, Cor. 2.1]
states that
a
can be chosen arbitrarily in
(0,1)
, with
C
possibly depending on the choice of
a
. We make the choice
a(r)=e^{-\frac{1}{r-1}}
. A closer look at the proof of
[
8
, Cor. 2.1]
guarantees that
C(r)\geq 1
, for all choices of
a
, i.e., for all
r>0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
13
.

Let
\mathbf{f}_{+}=\nabla u_{+}|_{\mathbb{S}}
and
\mathbf{f}_{-}=\nabla u_{-}|_{\mathbb{S}}
. As in the proof of Theorem
5
,
w=u_{+}+u_{-}
is harmonic in the annulus
A_{r}
. For convenience, we set

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}=\|(\mathbf{f}_{+}+\mathbf{f}_{-})|_{U}\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}.

(43)

Since a constant vanishes under application of the gradient, we may assume
\int_{U}w(y)d\sigma(y)=0
without altering the validity of our arguments. Splitting the gradient into its tangential and normal components yields
\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}w\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}\leq\varepsilon
and
\|\partial_{\eta}^{+}w\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq\varepsilon
. From Poincaré inequality, we get that that
\|w\|_{H^{1}(U)}\leq C\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}w\|_{L^{2}(U)^{3}}\leq C\varepsilon
. This yields

\displaystyle\|w\|_{H^{1}(U)}\leq C\varepsilon,\qquad\|\partial_{\eta}^{+}w\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq\varepsilon.

(44)

We consider
C>0
to be a generic constant that may depend on the patch
U
.

Next, we derive estimates on the Sobolev norm of
w
in the annulus
A_{1+s}\subset A_{r}
, for
s=\tfrac{r-1}{4}
. For any
x\in A_{r}
and
y\in A_{1+s}
it holds
|x-y|\geq r-(1-s)=\tfrac{3}{4}(r-1)=\tilde{s}
. Observing that the surface area of the sphere
\mathbb{S}_{r}
is
|\mathbb{S}_{r}|=4\pi r
, Cauchy’s inequality and the assumption on
\varphi_{+}
yield that, for
x\in A_{1+s}
,

\displaystyle|u_{+}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}\frac{|\varphi_{p}(y)|}{|x-y|}d\sigma(y)\leq\frac{\sqrt{4\pi r}}{s}\|\varphi_{+}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}_{r})}\leq\frac{\sqrt{4\pi r}}{\tilde{s}}M,

(45)

\displaystyle|\nabla u_{+}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{S}_{r}}\frac{|\varphi_{p}(y)|}{|x-y|^{2}}d\sigma(y)\leq\frac{\sqrt{4\pi r}}{\tilde{s}^{2}}M.

(46)

Integrating over the annulus
A_{1+s}
yields

\displaystyle\|u_{+}\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}\leq C\sqrt{s}\sqrt{4\pi r}M\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{s}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{s}^{2}}\right)\leq CM\frac{1}{(r-1)^{2}},

(47)

where we have used that
r
is assumed to be bounded from above by some
R
(thus,
C
may additionally depend on
R
). Similarly, we proceed for
u_{-}
, again assuming without restriction that
\int_{\mathbb{S}}u_{-}(y)d\sigma(y)=0
. Poincaré inequality provides that
\|u_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq C\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}u_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}
. Furthermore, the assumption on the bound of the norm of
\mathbf{f}_{-}
yields
\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}\leq\|\mathbf{f}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})^{3}}\leq M
. Furthermore, it holds, due to the harmonicity of
u_{-}
, that
\|u_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\geq\|u_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}_{\rho})}
, for all
\rho>1
. Therefore, by integration over
A_{1+s}
, we get
\|u_{-}\|_{L^{2}(A_{1+s})}\leq\sqrt{s}\|u_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}
. Combing the previous estimates and applying Caccioppoli’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|{u}_{-}\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}\leq\frac{C}{s}\|{u}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(A_{1+2s})}\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{s}}\|{u}_{-}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}\leq CM\frac{1}{\sqrt{r-1}}.

(48)

The inequalities (
47
) and (
48
) eventually yield

\displaystyle\|w\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}\leq\|u_{+}\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}+\|{u}_{-}\|_{H^{1}(A_{1+s})}\leq CM\frac{1}{(r-1)^{2}}.

(49)

Using (
44
) and (
49
) in Lemma
14
, the desired estimate (
37
)-(
39
) follows immediately.
∎

Remark 15

(Influence of thickness of source-free shell)
.

Theorem
13
illustrates the influence of the altitutde/analyticity condition on the conditional stability estimate. The larger the thickness
r-1
of the source-free shell, the better is the conditional stability. Opposed to that, a vanishing source-free shell, i.e.,
r\to 1
, results in a blow-up of the function
\Phi
, complying with the loss of uniqueness discussed in the previous section.

5
Discussion: implications for geomagnetic modelling

The results of Section
3
sharpen a basic distinction between satellite and ground-based observations from the viewpoint of internal-external field separation. For global data on a closed surface, the Hardy-Hodge decomposition provides a stable separation into external-source, internal-source, and tangential divergence-free components. In contrast, if measurements are available only on a proper surface patch (as is the case, e.g., for continental-scale arrays), Theorem
2
shows that internal and external contributions need not be determined uniquely from the local data.

A physically natural way to reduce this ambiguity is to incorporate prior information on the location of the external sources. This motivates the altitude/analyticity constraint encoded by the class
\mathcal{H}_{+}^{(r)}(\mathbb{S})
in Definition
4
. In the unit-sphere normalization,
r-1
measures the radial gap between the observation surface and the nearest allowed external sources. Under such a constraint, Theorem
5

Consequently, any practical separation workflow for patch data necessarily relies on additional priors or regularization. Common choices in geomagnetic practice include spectral truncation/bandlimiting, parametric external-field models, minimum-energy or smoothness penalties, and spatio-spectral localization.

We emphasize a point of interpretation: throughout this paper, “internal/external” refers to source location relative to the observation surface, not to a classification by physical processes. In particular, for time-varying geomagnetic fields, the internal component may largely represent the induced (secondary) field generated within the conducting Earth in response to primary external current systems. Conversely, the same current system may be classified differently depending on the observation altitude: for example, ionospheric currents are often treated as external with respect to ground observations but as internal with respect to low-Earth-orbit satellite measurements. Combining such complementary observation modalities is an appealing topic, but it lies beyond the scope of the present analysis.
