Quantum-Enhanced Zero-Error Communication and Storage under Positional Uncertainty
A. Diebra 1 , D. González-Lociga 1 , M. Hillery 2,3 , J. Calsamiglia 1 , and E. Bagan 1

Abstract
Permutation channels model communication and storage scenarios in which the positional identity of the physical carriers is partially or completely lost, so that the transmitted information is only accessible up to an unknown reordering. Here we show that quantum mechanics can dramatically enhance zero-error communication through such channels. For cyclic reorderings of n n d d -level systems, and in the absence of positional metadata, the number of classical zero-error messages scales asymptotically as d n / n d^{n}/n , whereas quantum protocols can fully recover the identity-channel value d n d^{n} . Ancilla-assisted protocols further increase this number to d 2 ​ n / n d^{2n}/n , enabling dense coding under positional uncertainty. We also analyze dihedral permutation channels and derive general Pólya-like formulas for the number of distinguishable messages in a broad class of permutation groups. Finally, for the symmetric group S n S_{n} , corresponding to complete scrambling of the information carriers, the number of distinguishable messages scales as n d − 1 n^{d-1} classically, compared with n d ⁡ ( d + 1 ) / 2 − 1 n^{d(d+1)/2-1} for quantum protocols and n d 2 − 1 n^{d^{2}-1} in the ancilla-assisted setting. Our results establish a fundamental quantum advantage for communication and storage under positional uncertainty.

In many communication and storage systems, information is not only vulnerable to noise and losses, but also to a distinct form of uncertainty: the loss of positional identity. Messages may be fragmented, shuffled, displaced, or retrieved in an order unrelated to the one in which they were originally encoded. These situations can be naturally modeled by
permutation channels

27
;
15
;
14
, where the transmitted data is only accessible up to an unknown reordering of its constituent parts. As modern technologies increasingly rely on large collections of mobile, distributed, or indistinguishable carriers, understanding how much information can still be reliably transmitted or stored under such uncertainty becomes both a practical and theoretical challenge.

Permutation channels arise naturally whenever positional information is degraded or lost. A particularly important example is DNA-based archival storage
12
;
20
, where data is encoded into a large collection of short DNA strands that are later retrieved in an essentially unordered fashion through sequencing, requiring substantial resources to recover their original arrangement. Similar issues also appear in packet-based networks
8
;
16
, asynchronous transmission protocols
25
, and molecular communication
17
;
10
. Such scenarios naturally raise questions about the ultimate limits of communication under positional uncertainty, particularly in the form of permutation uncertainty.

In practice, positional uncertainty is typically mitigated by incorporating additional metadata, such as sequence numbers, timestamps, synchronization markers, or explicit addressing information
22
. These mechanisms effectively restore ordering information, but inevitably consume communication or storage resources and thereby reduce the effective transmission rate. In DNA-based storage, for instance, a non-negligible fraction of each strand must be devoted to indexing information in order to reconstruct the original ordering after sequencing
12
. More generally, the need to recover positional identity introduces an intrinsic overhead in communication protocols operating under permutation uncertainty.

These limitations naturally motivate the exploration of genuinely quantum communication strategies for permutation channels. Throughout this work, the carriers are not supplemented with positional metadata, since this would enlarge the local dimension and partially break the symmetry underlying the permutation channels themselves. Under the same assumptions, quantum mechanics nonetheless allows such reference information to be encoded into coherent superpositions and relative phases, opening the possibility of qualitatively different communication protocols. It is then natural to ask whether these quantum effects can provide an advantage for zero-error communication and storage in such constrained scenarios.

For permutation uncertainty, the possible reorderings naturally define a permutation group acting on the set of length-
n
strings over an alphabet of size
d
used to encode messages. Classical messages are then identified up to the corresponding group orbits. Consequently, the number of perfectly distinguishable classical messages grows much more slowly than the total number
d^{n}
of possible strings, often leading to vanishing asymptotic zero-error rates
21
.

Quantum mechanics does not, in general, remove this obstruction or restore a finite asymptotic rate. But coherent superpositions can substantially enlarge the number of messages that can be retrieved with certainty. For example, under a cyclic permutation channel, the classical number scales asymptotically as
d^{n}/n
, whereas quantum encodings can recover the full identity-channel value
d^{n}
. Ancilla-assisted strategies increase this further to
d^{2n}/n
. Under the full symmetric group
S_{n}
, the corresponding classical number grows only polynomially, as
n^{d-1}
, while the numbers achievable with quantum and ancilla-assisted encodings scale respectively as
n^{d(d+1)/2-1}
and
n^{d^{2}-1}
.

Accordingly, our focus is not on asymptotic transmission rates, but on the single-shot zero-error number of messages that can be transmitted or stored with certainty, and on the symmetry-based mechanisms that make this possible. The loss of positional identity is not merely ordinary noise: it endows the set of possible messages with an underlying symmetry structure. For certain permutation groups, these mechanisms can be realized through quantum Fourier-type encodings over the corresponding orbits, making the effect both conceptually transparent and potentially accessible to current experimental platforms
7
.

Related questions concerning communication under incomplete or missing reference information have previously appeared, for example, in the context of quantum communication without a shared reference frame
4
;
5
;
3
. Permutations have also been considered as controlled encoding resources in entanglement-assisted communication protocols
28
. Here, however, we focus on the fundamentally different setting in which the ordering of the physical carriers is itself inaccessible.

This Letter is organized as follows. We first introduce a general framework for communication and storage under permutation uncertainty. We then specialize to cyclic positional uncertainty, illustrated by a ring of
n
cold atoms with
d
internal states used as a memory device, where the atoms may collectively rotate around the ring. In this setting, the classical problem reduces to necklace counting
26
;
11
. We next allow the ring to flip over, leading to the dihedral group
D_{n}
, and derive the corresponding classical and quantum asymptotic behaviors. The analysis is then extended to other physically motivated permutation groups. Finally, we consider full permutation uncertainty, corresponding to the symmetric group
S_{n}
.

General framework.
As mentioned in the introduction, messages are encoded into strings of symbols, which may be pictured as balls with
d
possible colors. In the quantum setting, the symbols are replaced by quantum systems with
d
orthonormal internal states. A permutation group
\mathcal{G}\leq S_{n}
(i.e., a subgroup of the symmetric group
S_{n}
) acts on the classical strings
\bm{x}=x_{0}x_{1}\dots x_{n-1}
by permuting their positions according to
\sigma\bm{x}:=x_{\sigma^{-1}(0)}x_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\cdots x_{\sigma^{-1}(n-1)}
, for
\sigma\in\mathcal{G}
. Likewise, in the quantum setting, the action of
\sigma
defines a unitary representation through
U(\sigma)|\bm{x}\rangle:=|\sigma\bm{x}\rangle
, where
|\bm{x}\rangle
is shorthand for the computational basis state
|x_{0}\rangle\otimes|x_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|x_{n-1}\rangle
. We denote by
X
the set of all such strings or, by a slight abuse of notation, the corresponding computational-basis vectors.

Recall that the
\mathcal{G}
-orbit, or simply orbit, of a string
\bm{x}\in X
is the set
O_{\bm{x}}=\{\sigma\bm{x}:\sigma\in\mathcal{G}\}
, namely the strings obtained from
\bm{x}
through the action of the group. In the quantum setting, we use the same notation for the set
\{U(\sigma)|\bm{x}\rangle:\sigma\in\mathcal{G}\}
. Strings belonging to the same orbit therefore cannot encode distinct classical messages through the
\mathcal{G}
-channel. The number of classical messages that can be transmitted with certainty is thus simply the number
N_{\rm c}
of orbits in
X
, namely
N_{\rm c}=|X/\mathcal{G}|
in standard mathematical notation. Since orbits are equivalence classes, they partition
X
into disjoint subsets.

Burnside’s lemma
26
;
2
states that

N_{\rm c}={1\over|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}|X_{\sigma}|={1\over|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}d^{c(\sigma)},

(1)

where
X_{\sigma}
denotes the fixed-point set of
\sigma
, i.e., the set of strings in
X
left invariant by
\sigma
. The second equality is the simplest instance of Pólya’s enumeration theorem
11
, where
c(\sigma)
denotes the number of cycles in the disjoint cycle decomposition of
\sigma
. It follows immediately from the identity
|X_{\sigma}|=d^{c(\sigma)}
, since a string is fixed by
\sigma
if and only if all positions belonging to the same cycle of
\sigma
carry the same symbol. One may therefore choose the symbol associated with each cycle independently, yielding
d^{c(\sigma)}
invariant strings.

In the quantum setting, the Hilbert space of the information carriers decomposes into orthogonal subspaces associated with the different orbits,

(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}=\bigoplus_{j}\operatorname{span}(O_{\bm{x}_{j}}),

(2)

where
\{\bm{x}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N_{\rm c}-1}
is a set of representatives, one from each orbit.

Every orbit subspace further decomposes into irreducible representations
(irreps) of
\mathcal{G}

23
,

\operatorname{span}(O_{\bm{x}_{j}})=\bigoplus_{\mu}\mathscr{H}_{\mu}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m_{j,\mu}},

(3)

where
\mathscr{H}_{\mu}
denotes the irrep labeled by
\mu
, appearing with
multiplicity
m_{j,\mu}
. The spaces
\mathbb{C}^{m_{j,\mu}}
are the
multiplicity spaces, on which
\mathcal{G}
acts trivially. By definition of
irreducibility, no nontrivial proper subspace of
\mathscr{H}_{\mu}
is invariant under
the action of
\mathcal{G}
. Thus, within each of the
m_{j,\mu}
copies of

\mathscr{H}_{\mu}
, one cannot select two or more linearly independent states that
remain perfectly distinguishable under the unknown group action. Such a copy
therefore encodes exactly one perfectly distinguishable message, which can be
decoded by a projective measurement onto the corresponding copy of
\mathscr{H}_{\mu}
.
We denote by
\{|u^{\mu}_{\alpha}\rangle\}_{\alpha=0}^{m_{\mu}-1}
the orthogonal
encoding states gathered from the different orbit subspaces and call the
subspace they generate the “message space”. Hence, the number of perfectly
distinguishable messages that can be encoded without ancillary systems is

N_{\rm q}=\sum_{\mu}\Bigg(\sum_{j}m_{j,\mu}\Bigg)=\sum_{\mu}m_{\mu},

(4)

i.e., the sum of all multiplicities.

If an ancillary system of dimension
m_{\mu}
is available and is not affected by the permutation channel, this message space can be used for dense coding
6
;
29
. Let
\{|u^{\mu}_{\alpha}\rangle\}_{\alpha=0}^{m_{\mu}-1}
be the orthonormal set of encoding vectors, and let
\{|\varphi^{\mu}_{\alpha}\rangle\}_{\alpha=0}^{m_{\mu}-1}
be an orthonormal basis of the ancilla. The sender and receiver may share the maximally entangled state

|\Phi_{\mu}\rangle={1\over\sqrt{m_{\mu}}}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{m_{\mu}-1}|u^{\mu}_{\alpha}\rangle\otimes|\varphi^{\mu}_{\alpha}\rangle.

(5)

By applying a complete set of
m_{\mu}^{2}
orthogonal unitary transformations on the message space, the sender can generate
m_{\mu}^{2}
mutually orthogonal maximally entangled states. Since the ancillary system is unaffected by the permutation channel, these states remain perfectly distinguishable at the receiver. Thus, the sector associated with
\mu
contributes
m_{\mu}^{2}
distinguishable messages, and the ancilla-assisted number of messages is

N_{\rm a}=\sum_{\mu}m_{\mu}^{2}={1\over|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}\left|\chi(\sigma)\right|^{2},

(6)

where
\chi(\sigma)
is the character of the representation
U(\sigma)
on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
, and we have used the standard representation-theoretic identity relating the squared multiplicities to the absolute value of the character. Moreover,
\chi(\sigma)=\sum_{\bm{x}\in X}\langle\bm{x}|U(\sigma)|\bm{x}\rangle=|X_{\sigma}|=d^{c(\sigma)}
, and we therefore obtain the compact expression

N_{\rm a}={1\over|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}d^{2c(\sigma)}.

(7)

Hence, dense coding over a permutation channel allows the transmission of
N_{\rm a}
mutually distinguishable messages. This is the ultimate limit permitted by quantum mechanics under permutation uncertainty.

Cyclic group.
To illustrate the above formalism, we consider the simplest nontrivial setting: a cyclic permutation group. As a concrete example, we revisit the ring of cold atoms introduced above as a memory device. In this scenario, the carriers of information are arranged on a circle, and their relative ordering is well defined, but there is no distinguished reference position. In other words, the stored message is defined only up to an unknown cyclic shift of the atomic positions.

Since the symmetry group
\mathcal{G}
in this example is cyclic, and therefore abelian, all its irreps are one-dimensional
23
, i.e.,
\dim(\mathscr{H}_{\mu})=1
for all
\mu
. Using
\sum_{\mu}m_{\mu}\dim(\mathscr{H}_{\mu})=\dim[(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}]=d^{n}
, Eq. (
4
) immediately gives

N_{\rm q}=d^{n}.

(8)

The corresponding classical and ancilla-assisted expressions follow from the
cycle structure of the powers of the permutation

r=(0,1,\dots,n-1)
, representing a one-step shift and written in
standard cycle notation, which generates

\mathcal{G}=\langle r\rangle\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. The powers of
r
take the
form
r^{k}=(0,k,2k,\dots)
, with indices understood modulo
n
. Thus, if
p

is the smallest positive integer such that
pk=0\pmod{n}
, then
r^{k}

decomposes into
n/p
cycles of length
p
. Elementary modular arithmetic
gives
p=n/\gcd(k,n)
, where
\gcd
denotes the greatest common divisor.
Hence,
c(r^{k})=\gcd(k,n)
(see
2
for a worked-out example).
Equations (
1
) and (
7
) then yield

N_{\rm c}={1\over n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d^{\gcd(k,n)};\qquad N_{\rm a}={1\over n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d^{2\gcd(k,n)}.

(9)

The first quantity coincides with the number of distinct necklaces that can be formed with
n
beads of
d
colors, a well-known problem in classical combinatorics.
In the asymptotic regime
n\to\infty
, the dominant contribution comes from the term
k=0
, for which
\gcd(0,n)=n
, leading to

N_{\rm c}\sim{d^{n}\over n};\qquad N_{\rm a}\sim{d^{2n}\over n}.

(10)

In other words, without ancillary systems, quantum encoding allows a factor-
n
increase in the number of perfectly distinguishable classical messages compared with the corresponding classical protocol under the same symmetry constraints, fully recovering the identity-channel value. Ancillary systems further give an additional factor-
d^{n}
enhancement.

The orthonormal basis
\{|u^{\mu}_{\alpha}\rangle\}_{\alpha=0}^{m_{\mu}-1}
of the message space, consisting of vectors that transform under
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
according to its characters [i.e., phases
(\omega_{n})^{\mu}
, with
\omega_{n}={\rm e}^{2\pi i/n}
], can be constructed by applying the quantum Fourier transform (QFT)
24
;
18
along the orbits. To this end, we first determine a set of orbit representatives
\bm{x}_{j}
, for instance using the Fredricksen–Kessler–Maiorana (FKM) algorithm
9
, which generates them efficiently in lexicographical order. Denoting by
n_{j}=|O_{\bm{x}_{j}}|
the size of the orbit of
\bm{x}_{j}
, we define the Fourier basis within each orbit as

|\tilde{u}^{k}_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{j}}}\sum_{l=0}^{n_{j}-1}(\omega_{n_{j}})^{-kl}U(r^{l})|\bm{x}_{j}\rangle,

(11)

where
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n_{j}-1\}
. These states diagonalize the action of
r
,

U(r)|\tilde{u}^{k}_{j}\rangle=(\omega_{n_{j}})^{k}|\tilde{u}^{k}_{j}\rangle.

By the orbit–stabilizer theorem
11
;
2
,
n_{j}
divides
n
, so that
n=n_{j}p_{j}
for some integer
p_{j}
. Then,

\omega_{n_{j}}=(\omega_{n})^{p_{j}},

and we obtain

U(r)|\tilde{u}^{k}_{j}\rangle=(\omega_{n})^{p_{j}k}|\tilde{u}^{k}_{j}\rangle.

Each
|\tilde{u}^{k}_{j}\rangle
therefore transforms according to a one-dimensional irreducible representation (character) of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, namely the one labeled by
\mu=p_{j}k
. Collecting, from all
N_{\rm c}
orbits, the states with the same value of
\mu
yields the desired basis
\{|u^{\mu}_{\alpha}\rangle\}_{\alpha=0}^{m_{\mu}-1}
.

Dihedral group
D_{n}
.
Assume now that the ring of
n
cold atoms not only drifts rigidly but can also be flipped. This enlarges the symmetry group, which is now generated by two elements:
r
, with
r^{n}=e
, and
s
, with
s^{2}=e
and
srs=r^{-1}
. The resulting group,
D_{n}=\{e,r,r^{2},\dots,r^{n-1},s,sr,sr^{2},\dots,sr^{n-1}\}
, of order
2n
, is the dihedral group: the group of symmetries of a regular polygon with
n
sides. The elements
sr^{k}
correspond to reflections. For even
n
, these reflections occur along axes passing through opposite vertices or opposite sides, whereas for odd
n
each reflection axis passes through one vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.

The number of distinguishable classical messages (the bracelet-counting problem
26
;
11
) and its ancilla-assisted counterpart can be computed from Eqs. (
1
) and (
7
):

N_{\rm c}={1\over 2n}\left[\,\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d^{\gcd(k,n)}+f(n)\,\right],

(12)

with

f(n)=\begin{cases}{n\over 2}d^{{n\over 2}}(d+1),&n~\mbox{even},\\[3.99994pt]
nd^{{n+1\over 2}},&n~\mbox{odd}.\end{cases}

(13)

The contribution
f(n)
arises from the reflections, while the sum accounts for the cyclic subgroup
\langle r\rangle\trianglelefteq D_{n}
, as in Eq. (
9
). The corresponding ancilla-assisted result,
N_{\rm a}
, is obtained by replacing
d
with
d^{2}
. For asymptotically large
n
, the contribution of
f(n)
is subdominant, and

N_{\rm c}\sim{d^{n}\over 2n};\qquad N_{\rm a}\sim{d^{2n}\over 2n}.

(14)

Before deriving the corresponding ancilla-free expression, we reformulate Eq. (
4
) for an important family of groups that includes
D_{n}
and the symmetric group
S_{n}
.

Message counting within ancilla-free quantum protocols for totally orthogonal groups.
Except for special cases such as cyclic groups, the computation of
N_{\rm q}
becomes much more involved. However, if
all
irreps of
\mathcal{G}
are realizable over the real numbers, one obtains Pólya-like formulas analogous to Eqs. (

) and (

N_{\rm q}={1\over|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}d^{c(\sigma^{2})}.

(15)

Such groups, known as totally orthogonal, include many physically relevant examples, notably the dihedral group
D_{n}
and the symmetric group
S_{n}
.

The proof of Eq. (
15
) can be found in
2
. It exploits the fact that, for every irrep
\mu
, the Frobenius–Schur indicator of totally orthogonal groups is equal to
+1

13
:

\nu_{\mu}:=|\mathcal{G}|^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}\chi_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})=1
,
where
\chi_{\mu}(\sigma)
is the character of the irrep
\mu
of
\mathcal{G}
.

For the dihedral group, Eq. (
15
) gives

N_{\rm q}={d^{n}\over 2}+{1\over 2n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d^{\gcd(b_{n}k,n)},

(16)

where
b_{n}=1
for odd
n
and
b_{n}=2
for even
n
. The first term arises from reflections, since
(sr^{k})^{2}=e
, whose cycle decomposition consists of
n

1
-cycles. The second term comes from rotations, for which
\sigma^{2}=r^{2k}
. Asymptotically, the reflection contribution dominates, and therefore

N_{\rm q}\sim{d^{n}\over 2}.

(17)

Comparing with Eq. (
14
), we recover the expected hierarchy
N_{\rm c}<N_{\rm q}<N_{\rm a}
for

, providing a consistency check for the expressions above.

As in the cyclic case, the quantum encoding for the dihedral group can be implemented using the QFT

The symmetric group
S_{n}
.
As a concrete example, let us assume that messages are transmitted asynchronously, for instance due to diffusion, as in molecular communication, or because of routing processes in communication networks. We model this situation with a full permutation channel, i.e., a channel whose symmetry is described by the symmetric group
S_{n}
.

The number
N_{\rm c}
of messages that can be transmitted in the classical setting can be computed from Eq. (
1
). However, a more direct derivation uses the fact that under arbitrary permutations only the occupation numbers of the different symbols are preserved. A straightforward stars-and-bars combinatorial argument yields

N_{\rm c}={n+d-1\choose n},\qquad N_{\rm a}={n+d^{2}-1\choose n},

(18)

where the ancilla-assisted expression is obtained from the classical one by the replacement
d\mapsto d^{2}
. Asymptotically,

N_{\rm c}\sim{n^{d-1}\over(d-1)!},\qquad N_{\rm a}\sim{n^{d^{2}-1}\over(d^{2}-1)!}.

(19)

We note that the exponential scaling found for cyclic and dihedral symmetries is lost under such a demanding channel, becoming merely polynomial. Even ancilla-assisted encoding cannot restore the exponential behavior, although it still provides a significant improvement over the classical setting.

Without ancillas, the number of perfectly distinguishable messages is given by Eq. (
15
). The value of
c(\sigma^{2})
depends on the parity of the cycle lengths: a
k
-cycle of
\sigma
remains a
k
-cycle under squaring when
k
is odd, whereas for even
k
it splits into two cycles of length
k/2
. Therefore,

N_{\rm q}={1\over n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\;\prod_{k~{\rm odd}}d^{c_{k}(\sigma)}\prod_{k~{\rm even}}d^{2c_{k}(\sigma)},

(20)

where
c_{k}(\sigma)
is the number of
k
-cycles in the decomposition of
\sigma
. This expression can be written equivalently as

N_{\rm q}=Z_{S_{n}}(d,d^{2},d,d^{2},\dots),

where

Z_{S_{n}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}):={1\over n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{c_{k}(\sigma)}

(21)

is the cycle index function of
S_{n}

19
in the independent variables
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}
. Its generating function, commonly known as the cycle index series, has the compact form

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^{n}Z_{S_{n}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})=\exp\!\left(\,\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{a_{k}x^{k}\over k}\right).

(22)

For the case of interest here,
a_{2j+1}=d
and
a_{2j}=d^{2}
, the series in the exponent can be evaluated in closed form, yielding

N_{\rm q}=[x^{n}]\!\left[(1-x)^{-{d(d+1)\over 2}}(1+x)^{-{d(d-1)\over 2}}\right],

(23)

where
[x^{n}]f(x)
denotes the coefficient of
x^{n}
in the Taylor expansion of
f(x)
. The asymptotic behavior is then obtained from the dominant singularity at
x=1
:

N_{\rm q}\sim{n^{{d(d+1)\over 2}-1}\over 2^{d(d-1)\over 2}\left[{d(d+1)\over 2}-1\right]!}.

(24)

Together with Eq. (
19
), this reveals a clear quantum advantage over the classical encoding for the most general scrambling of information carriers.

The explicit construction of the encoding states and additional details can be found in

, which also addresses the transmission and storage of quantum information.

Summary and outlook.
We have shown that quantum mechanics can substantially enhance zero-error communication and storage when the positional identity of the information carriers is lost. Without additional positional information, classical protocols suffer a severe reduction in the number of distinguishable messages, whereas quantum coherence and entanglement allow information to be encoded into collective states of the carriers. For cyclic rearrangements, this restores the communication and storage capabilities of an ideal ordered array. In addition, we demonstrated a clear quantum advantage for both dihedral and fully random scrambling. In the cyclic and dihedral cases, the corresponding protocols can be implemented through QFT, making them particularly appealing for near-term experimental realizations. Dense-coding-like enhancements remain possible even under positional uncertainty.

Future work should address the robustness of these protocols under realistic noise and the development of concrete proof-of-principle experimental implementations. In particular, recent advances in coherent transport of neutral-atom arrays while preserving internal quantum states suggest that these protocols may already be realizable in current programmable quantum platforms
7
. More broadly, our results suggest a new paradigm for communication and storage in architectures where the ordering of the carriers is intrinsically uncertain or dynamically lost, including mobile quantum platforms and molecular or DNA-based information systems.

Equivalence classes and quotient sets

Let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on a set

. The

of

is the subset

[x]=\{y\in X\;|\;y\sim x\}.

The element

. The

can be chosen
as a representative of

The set of equivalence classes of

.
Any equivalence relation partitions the set

Therefore, if
X
is finite,

|X|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}|[x_{j}]|,

where
|Y|
denotes the number of elements (cardinality) of a set
Y
,

N=|X/\sim|
, and
x_{j}
is a representative of the
j
th equivalence class.

Left and right cosets. Normal subgroups

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a subgroup of a group
\mathcal{G}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{H}\leq\mathcal{G}
.
We define a relation on
\mathcal{G}
:
g\sim g^{\prime}
if
g^{\prime}=gh
for some

h\in\mathcal{H}
. This is an equivalence relation.

All elements equivalent to
g
form the
left coset
of
g
:

g\kern-0.50003pt\mathcal{H}=\{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}:g^{\prime}\sim g\}
. Hence, left cosets are a special case of
equivalence classes in which the underlying set,
\mathcal{G}
, carries a group
structure. As before, we call
g
a representative of the coset, although
any element of the coset can equally well serve as a representative.
The notation
g\kern-0.50003pt\mathcal{H}
reflects the fact that its elements are obtained by
multiplying
g
on the left by elements of
\mathcal{H}
. In particular, if
\mathcal{G}

is finite, every left coset contains the same number of elements as
\mathcal{H}
.
As for any equivalence relation, the one we have defined partitions the
group
\mathcal{G}
into disjoint left cosets. Therefore, if
\mathcal{G}
is finite,

|\mathcal{H}|
divides
|\mathcal{G}|
, i.e.,

|\mathcal{G}|/|\mathcal{H}|\in\mathbb{N}.

One can similarly define right cosets of the form
\mathcal{H}g
, with analogous
properties. In general,
\mathcal{H}g\neq g\kern-0.50003pt\mathcal{H}
unless
\mathcal{H}
is a normal subgroup.

By definition, a subgroup
\mathcal{H}\leq\mathcal{G}
is a
normal subgroup
, denoted

\mathcal{H}\trianglelefteq\mathcal{G}
, if it is invariant under conjugation, meaning that
for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
and every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, one has
ghg^{-1}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Equivalently, for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists
h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}

such that
gh=h^{\prime}g
. In particular, for normal subgroups one has

\mathcal{H}g=g\kern-0.50003pt\mathcal{H}
.

In general, the quotient set, denoted
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{H}
(instead of
\mathcal{G}/\sim
), is
not a group unless
\mathcal{H}
is normal. In that case, one can define the product
of cosets by

(g\kern-0.50003pt\mathcal{H})(g^{\prime}\kern-0.50003pt\mathcal{H})=(gg^{\prime})\kern-0.50003pt\mathcal{H}.

This operation is well defined precisely because
\mathcal{H}
is normal.

Action of a group on a set

An action of a group
\mathcal{G}
on a set
X
is a mapping

\varphi:\mathcal{G}\times X\rightarrow X,

satisfying

(a)

\varphi(e,x)=x
for all
x\in X
,

(b)

\varphi(g^{\prime},\varphi(g,x))=\varphi(g^{\prime}g,x)
for all

g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
and
x\in X
.

It is convenient to introduce the shorthand notation

gx:=\varphi(g,x)
. The above conditions then become

(a)

ex=x
for all
x\in X
,

(b)

(g^{\prime}g)x=g^{\prime}(gx)
for all
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
and
x\in X
.

The concept of a group action is ubiquitous in Physics. Space-time
transformations, permutations of particles, and time evolution are all
examples of group actions. In these examples, as well as in many other
applications, the set
X
carries additional mathematical structure beyond
that of a simple set, such as that of a vector space, group, or algebra.

The stabilizer of
x\in X
is defined as

\operatorname{stab}(x)=\left\{g\in\mathcal{G}\;|\;gx=x\right\}.

In words, it is the set of group elements that leave
x
invariant.
It is convenient to introduce the shorthand notation

\mathcal{G}_{x}:=\operatorname{stab}(x)
. We will use both notations interchangeably throughout
these notes. One can easily check that the stabilizer is a subgroup of

\mathcal{G}
.

The fixed-point set of
g\in\mathcal{G}
is the subset

X_{g}\subset X
of elements fixed by
g
, namely

X_{g}=\{x\in X\;|\;gx=x\}.

The
\mathcal{G}
-orbit of
x\in X
(or simply orbit of
x
if no ambiguity arises) is defined as the set

O_{x}=\left\{x^{\prime}\in X\;|\;x^{\prime}=gx,\;g\in\mathcal{G}\right\},

or simply

O_{x}=\left\{gx\;|\;g\in\mathcal{G}\right\},

of all elements of
X
that can be reached from
x
by the action of
elements of the group.
The orbits
O_{x}
are precisely the equivalence classes of the relation

x_{1}\sim x_{2}\;\Leftrightarrow\;x_{2}=gx_{1}\ \text{for some }g\in\mathcal{G}.

Hence,

[x_{1}]=\left\{x_{2}\in X\;|\;x_{2}=gx_{1}\ \text{for some }g\in\mathcal{G}\right\}=O_{x_{1}},

and the set of all these equivalence classes, or orbits, is denoted by

X/\mathcal{G}
. This implies that

(a)

Any two orbits are either identical or disjoint.

(b)

For a finite set
X
,

|X|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}|O_{x_{j}}|,

where
O_{x_{j}}
,
j=1,2,\dots,N
, are the

N=|X/\mathcal{G}|
distinct orbits of
X
. The elements
x_{j}
are
representatives of the corresponding orbits.

Orbit-stabilizer theorem

Theorem 1

Let the group
\mathcal{G}
act on the set
X
. Then the mapping

\phi:\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{G}_{x}\rightarrow O_{x},

defined by

\phi(g\mathcal{G}_{x})=gx,

is a bijection.

Here,
g\mathcal{G}_{x}=\{gk\;|\;k\in\mathcal{G}_{x}\}
denotes a left coset.
The quotient set
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{G}_{x}
is not necessarily a group, since
\mathcal{G}_{x}

need not be a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

(a)

The mapping
\phi
is well defined: if
g^{\prime}\in g\mathcal{G}_{x}
(that is,
if
g^{\prime}
is another representative of the same coset
g\mathcal{G}_{x}
), then

g^{\prime}x=gx
. Indeed, since
g^{\prime}=gk
for some
k\in\mathcal{G}_{x}
,

g^{\prime}x=(gk)x=g(kx)=gx.

(b)

\phi
is injective. If
\phi(g\mathcal{G}_{x})=\phi(g^{\prime}\mathcal{G}_{x})
, then

g^{\prime}x=gx\;\Rightarrow\;(g^{-1}g^{\prime})x=x\;\Rightarrow\;g^{-1}g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{x}.

Hence
g^{\prime}=gk
for some
k\in\mathcal{G}_{x}
, and therefore

g\mathcal{G}_{x}=g^{\prime}\mathcal{G}_{x}
.

(c)

\phi
is surjective. If
x^{\prime}\in O_{x}
, then there exists

g\in\mathcal{G}
such that
x^{\prime}=gx
. Thus,

\phi(g\mathcal{G}_{x})=gx=x^{\prime}.

\blacksquare

Corollary 1
(Orbit-stabilizer theorem)

If the finite group
\mathcal{G}
acts on the finite set
X
, then for any

x\in X
,

|\mathcal{G}|=|\mathcal{G}_{x}||O_{x}|.

Hence
|O_{x}|
divides
|\mathcal{G}|
, denoted by

|O_{x}|\mid|\mathcal{G}|
.

Indeed, for fixed
x\in X
, Theorem
1
implies that

|\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{G}_{x}|=|O_{x}|
, while

|\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{G}_{x}|={|\mathcal{G}|\over|\mathcal{G}_{x}|}.

Burnside lemma

Lemma 1

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a group acting on a set
X
. The stabilizers of all elements
belonging to the same orbit have the same order (that is, the same number of
elements if
\mathcal{G}
is finite). In other words, if
x_{2}\in O_{x_{1}}
, then

|\mathcal{G}_{x_{1}}|=|\mathcal{G}_{x_{2}}|.

Proof.
If
x_{2}\in O_{x_{1}}
, there exists
g\in\mathcal{G}
such that
x_{2}=gx_{1}
.
Assume
h\in\mathcal{G}_{x_{1}}
. Then
ghg^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}_{x_{2}}
, since

(ghg^{-1})x_{2}=ghg^{-1}(gx_{1})=gh(g^{-1}g)x_{1}=(gh)x_{1}=g(hx_{1})=gx_{1}=x_{2},

i.e.,
ghg^{-1}
fixes
x_{2}
.
This naturally leads us to define a map

\psi:\mathcal{G}_{x_{1}}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}_{x_{2}}

as

\psi(h)=ghg^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}_{x_{2}}\quad\mbox{for all $h\in\mathcal{G}_{x_{1}}$.}

We now prove that
\psi
is a bijective group homomorphism, i.e., a map that
preserves the group operation:

(a)

\psi
is a homomorphism. Indeed, if
h,k\in\mathcal{G}_{x_{1}}
,

\psi(hk)=g(hk)g^{-1}=gh(g^{-1}g)kg^{-1}=(ghg^{-1})(gkg^{-1})=\psi(h)\psi(k).

(b)

\psi
is injective. Since
\psi
is a group homomorphism, it suffices to prove that
\ker\psi=\{e\}
. Indeed, if
h\in\ker\psi
, then

\psi(h)=e\quad\Rightarrow\quad ghg^{-1}=e\quad\Rightarrow\quad h=e.

(c)

\psi
is surjective. Take any
k\in\mathcal{G}_{x_{2}}
, and consider
h=g^{-1}kg
. Then
h\in\mathcal{G}_{x_{1}}
, since

hx_{1}=g^{-1}kg(g^{-1}x_{2})=g^{-1}x_{2}=x_{1},

and

\psi(h)=g(g^{-1}kg)g^{-1}=(gg^{-1})k(gg^{-1})=k.

Since
\psi
is a bijection, we have
|\mathcal{G}_{x_{1}}|=|\mathcal{G}_{x_{2}}|
.
\blacksquare

Theorem 2
(Burnside)

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite group acting on a finite set
X
. Then the number
of distinct orbits is given by

N=\left|X/\mathcal{G}\right|={1\over|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}|X_{g}|.

Proof.

Let
x_{j}
,
j=1,2,\dots,N
, be representatives of the
N
distinct orbits.
Then,

\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}|X_{g}|=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{x\in X}\delta_{gx,x}=\sum_{x\in X}|\mathcal{G}_{x}|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{x\in O_{x_{j}}}|\mathcal{G}_{x}|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}|O_{x_{j}}||\mathcal{G}_{x_{j}}|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\mathcal{G}|=N|\mathcal{G}|.

In the second equality, the order of summation over
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
x\in X

is exchanged, and the Kronecker delta is used to express the cardinalities
of the fixed-point set and stabilizer as

|X_{g}|=\sum_{x\in X}\delta_{gx,x}
and

|\mathcal{G}_{x}|=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\delta_{gx,x}
.
The third equality follows from the fact that distinct orbits partition

X
, the fourth from Lemma
1
, and the fifth from Corollary
1
.
\blacksquare

II
Conjugation and conjugacy classes

II.1
Conjugation in arbitrary groups

Let
\mathcal{G}
act on itself by
conjugation
:

\varphi(g,h)=ghg^{-1}.

In this section, we avoid the shorthand notation
gh:=ghg^{-1}

introduced in Sec.
I
, since it would be misleading, or even
inconsistent, in the present context. Although both
g
and
h
are group
elements, they play different roles in this expression:
g
labels the group
element generating the action, whereas
h
denotes the point in the domain on
which the action acts.

Conjugation defines a group action since:

(a)

\varphi(e,h)=ehe^{-1}=h
, for all
h\in\mathcal{G}
.

(b)

\varphi(g_{1},\varphi(g_{2},h))=\varphi(g_{1},g_{2}hg_{2}^{-1})=g_{1}(g_{2}hg_{2}^{-1})g_{1}^{-1}=(g_{1}g_{2})h(g_{1}g_{2})^{-1}=\varphi(g_{1}g_{2},h)
,
for all
g_{1},g_{2},h\in\mathcal{G}
.

The orbits
O_{h}
,
h\in\mathcal{G}
, under conjugation are called

conjugacy classes
and are denoted
C_{h}
instead of
O_{h}
.
Theorem
2
relates their sizes to the stabilizers

\operatorname{stab}(h)=\mathcal{G}_{h}
, known in this context as
centralizers
,

\operatorname{cen}(h)=\{g\in\mathcal{G}:gh=hg\}=\{g\in\mathcal{G}:\varphi(g,h)=h\}=\mathcal{G}_{h}.

In particular,
\operatorname{cen}(h)
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
. Therefore,

|C_{h}|={|\mathcal{G}|\over|\operatorname{cen}(h)|}.

(S1)

II.2
Conjugacy classes in
S_{n}

The
symmetric group

S_{n}
is the group of all permutations of
n

objects. Its elements can be represented as bijections

\sigma:\{0,1,2,\dots,n-1\}\to\{0,1,2,\dots,n-1\}
, with group operation
given by composition. Applying first
\sigma
and then
\tau
to

x\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
yields the permutation
\tau\sigma
, defined by

(\tau\sigma)(x)=\tau(\sigma(x))
. The order of
S_{n}
is

|S_{n}|=n!
.

Cycle and disjoint-cycle structure of
S_{n}

A
cycle

(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1})
, with

a_{j}\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, permutes the elements cyclically:

\mbox{$a_{0}\to a_{1}$, $a_{1}\to a_{2}$, $\dots$,
$a_{k-1}\to a_{0}$},

leaving all other elements fixed. More precisely,
its action is

(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1})(x)=\begin{cases}a_{j+1\!\!\!\pmod{k}}&\mbox{if $x=a_{j}$ for some $j\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}$,}\\
x&\mbox{if $x\notin\{a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1}\}$.}\end{cases}

Proposition 1

Any permutation can be written uniquely (up to order) as a product of disjoint cycles, i.e., cycles with no elements in common.

Proof:

Let
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Consider the sequence

0,\,\sigma(0),\,\sigma^{2}(0),\,\ldots

Since
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
is finite, there exist
i<j
such that
\sigma^{i}(0)=\sigma^{j}(0)
. Applying
\sigma^{-i}
, we obtain
\sigma^{j-i}(0)=0
. Let
r
be the smallest positive integer such that
\sigma^{r}(0)=0
. Then

(0,\,\sigma(0),\,\ldots,\,\sigma^{r-1}(0))

is a cycle.

Now choose
a\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
not belonging to this cycle and repeat the construction:

a,\sigma(a),\sigma^{2}(a),\,\dots

Since orbits are disjoint, so is the resulting cycle
(a,\sigma(a),\,\dots,\sigma^{s-1}(a))
. Since the set is finite, the process terminates after finitely many steps. Thus
\sigma
can be written as a product of disjoint cycles.
\blacksquare

Hereafter, we will assume that
\sigma
is composed of
m_{1}

cycles of length
\lambda_{1}
,
m_{2}
cycles of length
\lambda_{2}
, and so on.
This cycle structure can be represented graphically by a Young diagram: a
left-justified arrangement of boxes consisting of
m_{p}
rows of length

\lambda_{p}
, for each
p
.

Characterizing the conjugacy classes of
S_{n}

We first recall the following elementary fact.

Proposition 2

Let
\pi\in S_{n}
and let
\tau=(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1})
,
k\leq n
. Then

\pi\tau\pi^{-1}=\left(\pi(a_{0}),\pi(a_{1}),\dots,\pi(a_{k-1})\right).

(S2)

Proof:
Let
x\in\{0,1,\cdots,n-1\}
. We need to prove that

(\pi\tau\pi^{-1})(x)=\left(\pi(a_{0}),\pi(a_{1}),\dots,\pi(a_{k-1})\right)(x).

(a)

Assume first that

x\notin\{\pi(a_{0}),\dots,\pi(a_{k-1})\}
, so that

(\pi(a_{0}),\pi(a_{1}),\dots,\pi(a_{k-1}))(x)=x
.
Under this assumption,

\pi^{-1}(x)\notin\{a_{0},\dots,a_{k-1}\}
, and hence

\tau(\pi^{-1}(x))=\pi^{-1}(x),

so

(\pi\tau\pi^{-1})(x)=\pi(\tau(\pi^{-1}(x)))=\pi(\pi^{-1}(x))=x.

(b)

If
x=\pi(a_{j})
for some
j
, then
\pi^{-1}(x)=a_{j}
, and

(\pi\tau\pi^{-1})(x)=\pi(\tau(a_{j}))=\pi(a_{j+1}),

where indices are understood modulo
k
. This coincides with

(\pi(a_{0}),\dots,\pi(a_{k-1}))(x)
.
\blacksquare

It follows that if a permutation has the form
\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\cdots\tau_{s}
, where the
\tau_{i}
are disjoint cycles, then

\displaystyle\pi\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\cdots\tau_{s}\pi^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\pi\tau_{1}\pi^{-1})(\pi\tau_{2}\pi^{-1})\cdots(\pi\tau_{s}\pi^{-1})

\displaystyle=\tau^{\prime}_{1}\tau^{\prime}_{2}\cdots\tau^{\prime}_{s},

(S3)

where each
\tau^{\prime}_{j}
is a cycle of the same length as
\tau_{j}
(up to relabeling). Therefore,
conjugation preserves the cycle structure of a permutation
.

The converse is also true:
if two permutations have the same cycle structure, they are conjugate to one another
. To prove this statement, it suffices to consider two cycles of the same length
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})
and
(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{k})
, and define
\pi
by
\pi(a_{i})=b_{i}
. Then Eq. (
S2
) shows that
\pi\tau\pi^{-1}=\tau^{\prime}
. Since permutations decompose into disjoint cycles and conjugation acts multiplicatively [cf. Eq. (
S3
)], the claim follows.

The above shows that

Proposition 3

Conjugacy classes of
S_{n}
are uniquely determined by a partition
(equivalently, a Young diagram)

\lambda=[\lambda_{1}^{m_{1}},\lambda_{2}^{m_{2}},\dots,\lambda_{s}^{m_{s}}]
,
where
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{s}
and

\sum_{p}m_{p}\lambda_{p}=n
. We denote this by
\lambda\vdash n
.

Size of conjugacy classes of
S_{n}

For the symmetric group
S_{n}
, Eq. (
S1
) yields

|C_{\sigma}|={n!\over|\operatorname{cen}(\sigma)|},\quad\sigma\in S_{n}.

Now let us compute
|\operatorname{cen}(\sigma)|
. Assume
\pi\in\operatorname{cen}(\sigma)
and write

\sigma=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\cdots\tau_{s}
, where the cycles
\tau_{p}
are disjoint.
The condition
\pi\sigma\pi^{-1}=\sigma
implies

(\pi\tau_{1}\pi^{-1})(\pi\tau_{2}\pi^{-1})\cdots(\pi\tau_{s}\pi^{-1})=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\cdots\tau_{s}.

Thus conjugation by
\pi
must permute cycles of equal length. If the distinct
cycle lengths are
\lambda_{j}
, with multiplicities
m_{j}
, then cycles of
length
\lambda_{j}
can be permuted in
m_{j}!
ways, and each such cycle admits

\lambda_{j}
cyclic rotations. Hence

|\operatorname{cen}(\sigma)|=\prod_{j}m_{j}!\,\lambda_{j}^{m_{j}}.

Finally,

|C_{\lambda}|:=|C_{\sigma}|={n!\over\prod_{j}m_{j}!\,\lambda_{j}^{m_{j}}}.

(S4)

IV
Proof of Eq. (
15
)

In this section we prove Eq. (
15
), which gives the number

N_{\rm q}
of classical messages that can be transmitted or stored using
ancilla-free quantum encoding when the symmetry group
\mathcal{G}
is totally
orthogonal. The starting point is Eq. (
4
), valid in full generality:

N_{\rm q}=\sum_{\mu}m_{\mu},

(S5)

where
m_{\mu}
denotes the multiplicity of irrep
\mu
in the decomposition of
the total Hilbert space
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
. From character theory,
each multiplicity is given by

m_{\mu}=\langle\chi,\chi_{\mu}\rangle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}\chi(\sigma)\,\overline{\chi_{\mu}(\sigma)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}|X_{\sigma}|\,\overline{\chi_{\mu}(\sigma)},

where the bar denotes complex conjugation. The second equality follows from
the definition of the inner product of
characters
\langle\chi,\chi_{\mu}\rangle
. In the third equality we used

\chi(\sigma)=\operatorname{tr}U(\sigma)=\sum_{\bm{x}\in X}\langle\bm{x}|U(\sigma)|\bm{x}\rangle=|X_{\sigma}|,

since the trace counts the basis states left invariant by the action of

\sigma
.
Equation (
S5
) then becomes

N_{\rm q}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}|X_{\sigma}|\sum_{\mu}\chi_{\mu}(\sigma),

(S6)

where we used that totally orthogonal groups have all irreps realizable
over
\mathbb{R}
, and exchanged the sums over
\mu
and
\sigma
. The inner sum admits a simple
interpretation given by the following lemma.

Lemma 2

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite totally orthogonal group. Then

\sum_{\mu}\chi_{\mu}(\sigma)=n(\sigma),

where
n(\sigma)
denotes the number of square roots of
\sigma
, and the sum runs over all irreducible characters
\chi_{\mu}
of
\mathcal{G}
.

In other words,

n(\sigma)=|\mathscr{S}_{\sigma}|,

where

\mathscr{S}_{\sigma}:=\{\tau\in\mathcal{G}\;|\;\tau^{2}=\sigma\}

is the set of square roots of
\sigma
.
The proof of this lemma uses the following proposition.

Proposition 4

The number of square roots is a class function. Namely, if
\sigma
and

\sigma^{\prime}
belong to the same conjugacy class, then

n(\sigma)=n(\sigma^{\prime}).

Proof.

Assume
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
belong to the same conjugacy class, so that

\sigma^{\prime}=\pi\sigma\pi^{-1}
for some
\pi\in\mathcal{G}
. Define

\psi:\mathscr{S}_{\sigma}\longrightarrow\mathscr{S}_{\sigma^{\prime}}

by

\psi(\tau)=\pi\tau\pi^{-1}.

This map is well defined since, for every
\tau\in\mathscr{S}_{\sigma}
,

\psi(\tau)^{2}=(\pi\tau\pi^{-1})^{2}=\pi\tau^{2}\pi^{-1}=\pi\sigma\pi^{-1}=\sigma^{\prime}.

(S7)

Hence
\psi(\tau)\in\mathscr{S}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
.
Moreover,
\psi
is bijective. Indeed, its inverse is

\psi^{-1}(\tau^{\prime})=\pi^{-1}\tau^{\prime}\pi,

which maps
\mathscr{S}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
back to
\mathscr{S}_{\sigma}
by the same argument used in
Eq. (
S7
). Hence,

|\mathscr{S}_{\sigma}|=|\mathscr{S}_{\sigma^{\prime}}|,

or equivalently,

n(\sigma)=n(\sigma^{\prime}).

\blacksquare

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Since
n(\sigma)
is a class function, it can be expanded in the basis of irreducible characters:

n(\sigma)=\sum_{\mu}\langle n,\chi_{\mu}\rangle\chi_{\mu}(\sigma).

The coefficients of this expansion are

\langle n,\chi_{\mu}\rangle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}n(\sigma)\chi_{\mu}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{G}}\delta_{\sigma,\tau^{2}}\chi_{\mu}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{G}}\chi_{\mu}(\tau^{2})=\nu_{\mu},

where
\nu_{\mu}
denotes the Frobenius–Schur indicator of the irrep
\mu

13
, and we used the Kronecker delta to represent
n(\sigma)
as

n(\sigma)=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{G}}\delta_{\sigma,\tau^{2}}.

(S8)

Since
\mathcal{G}
is totally orthogonal, all irreducible representations are realizable over
\mathbb{R}
, and therefore
\nu_{\mu}=+1
for all
\mu
. Hence,

\langle n,\chi_{\mu}\rangle=1,

which proves the lemma.
\blacksquare

We have used the well-known fact
13
that the
Frobenius–Schur indicator
\nu_{\mu}
takes the values
+1
,
0
, or
-1

depending on whether the irreducible representation
\mu
is respectively
realizable over
\mathbb{R}
, genuinely complex, or quaternionic
(pseudoreal).

Lemma
2
allows Eq. (
S6
) to be rewritten as

N_{\rm q}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}|X_{\sigma}|\,n(\sigma).

Using again the representation of Eq. (
S8
),
we obtain

N_{\rm q}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}|X_{\sigma}|\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{G}}\delta_{\sigma,\tau^{2}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{G}}|X_{\tau^{2}}|.

Equivalently,

N_{\rm q}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}|X_{\sigma^{2}}|={1\over|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}d^{c(\sigma^{2})}.

The last step follows from the fact that a configuration remains invariant
under
\sigma
precisely when all positions belonging to the same cycle of

\sigma
contain the same symbol. Since each cycle can be assigned any of the

d
symbols independently, the number of fixed strings is

|X_{\sigma}|=d^{c(\sigma)}.

This expression should be compared with its classical counterpart, Eq. (
1
):

N_{\rm c}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}d^{c(\sigma)}.

Since squaring group elements may increase the number of cycles in their
disjoint cycle decomposition, the sum may become
larger. In particular, elements satisfying
\sigma^{2}=e
contribute the maximal
value
d^{n}
. Hence, one generally has

N_{\rm q}\geq N_{\rm c}.

V
The cycle index function and the cycle index series of
S_{n}

The
cycle index function of
\mathcal{G}\leq S_{n}

26
;
19
;
23
is a polynomial in the variables
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}
defined as

Z_{\mathcal{G}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}):={1\over|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{c_{k}(g)},

where
c_{k}(g)
is the number of cycles of length
k
(
k
-cycles) of
g\in\mathcal{G}
.

Let us consider the cycle index function of the symmetric group
S_{n}
.
If we allow
m_{k}
to take the value zero, we can write the partition

\lambda=[\lambda_{1}^{m_{1}},\lambda_{2}^{m_{2}},\dots,\lambda_{s}^{m_{s}}]
as

\lambda=[1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,n^{m_{n}}],

and the condition
\lambda\vdash n
can be expressed as

\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}k=n,\quad m_{k}\geq 0.

(S9)

Then,

Z_{S_{n}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})={1\over n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{m_{k}(\sigma)}=\\
{1\over n!}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in C_{\lambda}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{m_{k}(\sigma)}\right)={1\over n!}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}|C_{\lambda}|\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{m_{k}}=\\
{1\over n!}\!\!\sum_{\begin{array}[]{c}\\[-15.00002pt]
\scriptstyle m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{n}\geq 0\\[-3.00003pt]
\scriptstyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}k=n\end{array}}{n!\over\prod_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}!k^{m_{k}}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{m_{k}},

where we have used Eq. (
S4
).

We next introduce the generating function of
Z_{S_{n}}
, known as the
cycle index series of
S_{n}
:

\sum_{n\geq 0}x^{n}Z_{S_{n}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}{a_{k}x^{k}\over k}\right).

(S10)

This equation must be understood in the following sense: the coefficient of
x^{n}
in the formal power expansion of the right-hand side is precisely
Z_{S_{n}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})
. This is a purely algebraic relation, as is usual in combinatorics; issues of convergence do not arise here.

Proof of Eq. (
S10
)

x^{n}Z_{S_{n}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})=\hskip-10.00002pt\sum_{\begin{array}[]{c}\\[-15.00002pt]
\scriptstyle m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{n}\geq 0\\[-3.00003pt]
\scriptstyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}k=n\end{array}}{x^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{m_{k}}\over\prod_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}!k^{m_{k}}}=\hskip-10.00002pt\sum_{\begin{array}[]{c}\\[-15.00002pt]
\scriptstyle m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{n}\geq 0\\[-3.00003pt]
\scriptstyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}k=n\end{array}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}{1\over m_{k}!}\left({a_{k}x^{k}\over k}\right)^{m_{k}},

(S11)

where the last equality is justified by Eq. (
S9
):

x^{n}=x^{\sum_{k=1}^{n}km_{k}}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(x^{k})^{m_{k}}.

Now consider

\prod_{k\geq 1}\sum_{m_{k}\geq 0}{1\over m_{k}!}\left({a_{k}x^{k}\over k}\right)^{m_{k}}.

(S12)

For any integer
n\geq 0
, the term of order
x^{n}
is obtained from those choices of
(m_{k})_{k\geq 1}
such that
\sum_{k\geq 1}km_{k}=n
. Since
k\geq 1
, necessarily
m_{k}=0
for all
k>n
. Hence, at fixed order
n
, the condition reduces to
\sum_{k=1}^{n}km_{k}=n
, and the product
\prod_{k\geq 1}
reduces to
\prod_{k=1}^{n}
. Therefore, the contribution of order
x^{n}
in Eq. (
S12
) coincides with Eq. (
S11
). Therefore,

\sum_{n\geq 0}x^{n}Z_{S_{n}}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})=\prod_{k\geq 1}\sum_{m_{k}\geq 0}{1\over m_{k}!}\left({a_{k}x^{k}\over k}\right)^{m_{k}}=\prod_{k\geq 1}\exp\left({a_{k}x^{k}\over k}\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}{a_{k}x^{k}\over k}\right).

This completes the proof.
\blacksquare
