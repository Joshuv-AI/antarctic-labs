Back on track: a case study on sustaining and reducing stroke treatment times in a medium-sized Norwegian hospital
Authors: Are Fjermeros, Geir Vegard Berg, Jos Benders
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSustaining continuous improvement (CI) in healthcare organizations remains challenging, particularly in settings characterized by disciplinary silos. Although many initiatives achieve initial improvements, less is known about how improvement efforts are sustained, decline over time, or become revitalized. This study examined the long-term development of a cross-disciplinary stroke care improvement initiative in a Norwegian general hospital.MethodsWe conducted a diagnostic single-case study of a cross-disciplinary improvement initiative in stroke care. Data were collected over an extended period (2013–2024) and included participant observations, internal documents, and performance data (door-to-needle times), as well as six semi-structured interviews with members of the interdisciplinary improvement group. Data was analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis, supported by triangulation across data sources and iterative discussions among the authors.ResultsInitial reductions in treatment times were associated with the development of cross-disciplinary structures, including a shared care pathway, continuous performance feedback, and team-based organization. A subsequent decline in performance appeared to be linked to personnel changes and reduced improvement activity. Improvement efforts were later revitalized through simulation-based training and renewed practitioner engagement. The analysis identified three interrelated themes: (1) development of practitioner ownership, (2) establishment of cross-disciplinary structures, and (3) the role of these structures in enabling the revitalization of improvement efforts.ConclusionsThis study suggests that sustaining continuous improvement in cross-disciplinary healthcare processes depends on the interaction between practitioner engagement and organizational support structures. Declining performance was caused by reduced improvement activity, personnel changes, and weakening practitioner ownership. At the same time, established cross-disciplinary structures - such as performance feedback, shared care pathways, and team-based work - enabled recognition of these problems and supported subsequent revitalization efforts. While the empirical findings are case-specific, the study highlights the potential value of a diagnostic approach for identifying and addressing breakdowns in continuous improvement processes in and beyond healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14929-w.Keywords: Cross-disciplinary improvement, Enhanced coordination in hospitals, Sustainable quality improvement, Stroke care

Introduction

Sustaining continuous improvement (CI) has long been recognized as troublesome and remains so for organizations [1–3]. This is particularly true in settings with organizational silos, such as most general hospitals [4–8]. Implementing changes within an organization where silos proliferate is difficult, as each unit sees the transformation from its own perspective and interests [9]. When the organizational structures around medical disciplines are rigid, much greater effort is required to achieve improvements involving the work of several medical specialties [10]. This is relevant for hospitals that intend to develop strategies to improve cross-disciplinary work processes to ease pressure on healthcare systems. CI is a potentially highly effective and low-cost way to improve the quality of care, enabling care professionals’ voices to be heard. Their in-depth knowledge of their own tasks can help ensure that the improvements are workable. We define CI as “a systematic and continuous approach to challenge and improve existing ways of working by formulating and revising operational standards. Employees are expected to identify issues for improvement, investigate these issues, and design novel working standards. Once established, these working standards are the subject of continued scrutiny, as yet better ways of working are likely to be developed” [11].

This study describes and explores one instance of interdisciplinary improvement work aimed at improving stroke treatment in a small Norwegian hospital. In this case, the improvement work continued for a period of more than ten years, ultimately resulting in stroke treatment at high national standards. We approach the case as a diagnostic study of an improvement trajectory that includes phases of success, decline, and recovery. Accordingly, we posed the following research question: How did the hospital succeed in maintaining the improvement work over such a long period of time, and how can periods of decline and recovery be understood?

Design and methods

The case described and explored in this study represents one of many initiatives to introduce ‘lean thinking’ in this Norwegian hospital, a process that started in 2012. The hospital’s lean approach aims to develop its ability to continuously improve work processes by designing them with coherent flows leading to the least amount of waste [12, 13]. To understand the underlying mechanisms of how improvement efforts evolved, declined, and were revitalized over time in a Norwegian general hospital, we applied a single-case design. This allowed an in-depth analysis of complex organizational processes using multiple data sources. The case was selected because it contains comprehensive data relevant to the topic of the study, namely the complexity of quality improvement and dilemmas encountered, and deliberations made by the involved actors. Individual and collective stories provided an opportunity to gain a comprehensive understanding of the case [14]. Utilizing multiple data sources is advisable in case studies because it allows diverse perspectives to be captured [15].

The use of multiple data sources is crucial to obtaining opportunities for obtaining complex knowledge and achieving a comprehensive understanding [16–18]. The present case includes documentation of the work of an interdisciplinary group (IDG) aimed at improving stroke treatment in a Norwegian general hospital. It comprises participant observations, individual interviews, documents, and statistics on the duration of treatment. The information collected through the interviews with IDG participants proved to be highly important. These interviews provide access to reflections on what triggered a commitment to carry out improvement work.

Setting and participants

In October 2013, eight clinicians met to create a vision of how to organize a future workflow for the treatment of stroke patients at a typical small Norwegian hospital. The problem they faced was that it took too long to provide effective treatment to patients suffering from thrombotic stroke.

The eight participants represented all disciplinary units involved in stroke care delivery: a local manager in the ambulance service, two nurses from the emergency department and the intensive care unit, two medical doctors from the neurological and radiological departments, a medical doctor (MD) in an educational position from the Department of Internal Medicine, a biomedical laboratory scientist and a radiographer. The improvement work was led by the hospital’s quality advisor (QA). Over the next few years, participants implemented several changes in stroke treatment task allocation and responsibilities. The changes involved many of the nearly three hundred professionals who worked daily in diagnosing and treating stroke patients. In this period, there was considerable variation in treatment times for stroke patients.

Data collection

We present data from two different time periods. The first dataset primarily consists of the original project documentation of the activities conducted in the IDG to improve stroke treatment from August 2013-December 2017. The key documents are minutes of meetings, various versions of flowcharts, procedures, and statistics (door-to-needle times). The first author’s observations and field notes from this period are also included in the dataset.

The second dataset consists of six in-depth interviews with the experienced clinicians who participated in the improvement group mentioned above. The informants include all available employees that participated in the IDG Group from its inception in 2012 and the following years. Two participants from the original IDG group were not interviewed. One had taken up work outside the health region, and face-to-face interview was difficult to arrange. The other was a doctor who rarely participated in activities in the IDG group and therefore had limited information to offer.

An interview guide (supplemental file 1- Interview guide Nov.2022) was developed in collaboration with the first and second authors. Follow-up interviews were conducted with two of the participants who were responsible for facilitating monthly training with the hospital’s stroke team from 2016 to 2020. The first author conducted face‒to-face interviews from November 2022–January 2024. The interviews lasted between 54 and 74 min and were recorded and transcribed verbatim by the first author. Employees voluntarily consented to participate in this study and completed an informed written consent form. SIKT (Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research) approved the research project in a decision by the Data Protection Officer at Hospital Inland (Protocol no. 19449967).

Analysis

The data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis, conducted in six steps: familiarization, coding, preliminary theme development, review and refinement, theme definition, and production of the analytical narrative. Documents and field notes were organized chronologically to map the initiative, while interview transcripts provided retrospective insights through the trajectory to coherently comprehend the case`s story [19]. All data were assessed as a complete set and read several times in search of patterns and themes [20]. Initially, descriptive and analytical codes were generated. Subsequently, subthemes and preliminary themes represent the data that were generated. Development of the main themes was guided by the research question. The entire analytical process alternated between the various data sources to investigate, justify, supplement or expand the insight and understanding of the data material along with the study`s objective [21].

Reflexivity

The first author held a dual role as both an internal facilitator of the quality improvement initiative and a researcher conducting the case study. This embedded position provided in-depth contextual knowledge and access to processes and stakeholders that might otherwise have been difficult to obtain. At the same time, it introduced potential methodological challenges related to researcher positionality, including risks of confirmation bias, role conflict, and taken-for-granted assumptions. To address these challenges, we adopted a reflexive approach throughout the study, explicitly acknowledging the researcher’s dual role and its potential influence on data generation and interpretation. First, preliminary findings from interviews conducted in 2022 and 2023 were presented to hospital staff. This was done through follow-up discussions with key personnel who had played central roles in revitalizing the improvement efforts during the period 2016–2020, as well as through a joint professional meeting attended by approximately 70 hospital employees. Feedback from these engagements contributed to refining the analysis. In particular, it highlighted the importance of an organizational restructuring implemented in 2017, which assigned a single clinical department clear responsibility for the overall organization of stroke care. The discussions also provided a deeper understanding of how cross-functional structures established in 2013–2014 enabled the subsequent revitalization of improvement efforts in 2016–2017. Of particular importance were the continuous feedback of performance data (door-to-needle times) and the transition to team-based work. This team organization allowed staff to recognize how weaker coordination between professional groups in 2015 had contributed to delays in treatment.

Second, the analytical process was developed through iterative engagement with co-authors who had not been directly involved in the improvement initiative. These discussions focused on the relative emphasis of emerging themes and the relationships between them. For example, we critically examined whether the strong ownership among staff for the new stroke protocol could have been sufficient to revitalize the improvement efforts in late 2016 if robust cross-departmental structures established during the initial phase, such as team-based organization and continuous performance measurement.

Third, insights from these feedback processes informed repeated refinement of the analysis throughout the period 2023–2024. This iterative approach contributed to enhancing the robustness, transparency, and trustworthiness of the findings.

Results

Every year, 10 000–11 000 people suffering from stroke are admitted to Norwegian hospitals (Norwegian Stroke Registry). In addition to being a frequent cause of death, stroke is a dominant cause of severe disability with considerable impacts on patients, their relatives, and society. Providing fast treatment to dissolve a blood clot in the brain has a great influence on patients’ chances of recovery [22, 23]. Every minute that the condition remains untreated, two million brain cells are damaged [24]. While managers and professionals in hospitals are aware of this, it seems challenging to develop work processes across disciplines that can provide rapid treatment on an ongoing basis (Norwegian Stroke Registry | National Service Environment for Medical Quality Registries).

Phase one: planning for change

On October 1, 2013, eight clinicians and the first author as internal quality advisor (QA) met to create a vision of how to organize the future workflow for treating stroke patients. At that time, it took the hospital too long to provide effective treatment to stroke patients (the average time for the last 15 patients at this point was 62 min). In addition, there was considerable variation from 38 min to 2 h.

Before this meeting, the practitioners mapped the actual work process together via the value stream mapping (VSM) tool. During this process, they discovered that the present stroke workflow was poorly coordinated, resulting in a significant delay in providing effective treatment. Among other things, they reported that the specialist units conducted similar tasks several times (e.g., diagnosis and documentation in medical records), and that a lack of communication between professional units created simultaneous conflicts and delays. The practitioners also understood that the dysfunctionalities in the workflow had emerged over time, in the absence of coordination and formal authority over the entire process [25]. The participants used these insights to formulate a future vision for the stroke workflow. The new proposal entailed the following organizational changes.

From the assembly line to the team-based assembly line

The VSM survey revealed a complex care process consisting of just over 70 subtasks. All specialist units had more than five tasks, and four departments conducted more than 10 main tasks. The fundamental organizational principle was that each specialist completed her/his subtasks in relative isolation before handing over to the next department. The medical professionals had only limited insight into each other’s activities, resulting in sub-optimal coordination and double work. To enable faster treatment times, the practitioners found it efficient to organize the entire process as teamwork. Therefore, all disciplines should gather around patients to solve subtasks in collaboration with other units.

Merging tasks and moving them forward in the action chain

The VSM survey revealed delays in treatment because of rework (repeated diagnosis and documentation), for instance the existing workflow included three (sometimes four) separate transport stages before treatment was provided. Finally, professional competence (e.g., doctor) and equipment (X-ray and lab) were often occupied because the practitioners were not aware that a stroke patient was on her/his way to the hospital. Facing these problems, the stroke group decided that the prehospital service was to carry out all preliminary diagnoses of the patient’s condition. They should consult the neurologist (team leader on the new stroke team) at the hospital before triggering a stroke alarm. The ambulance then provided transport from the patient’s home directly to the X-ray room where the stroke team was to complete diagnostics and provide treatment. This change replaced four separate transport stages.

The practitioners in the IDG proposed that a revised stroke standard be realized at the hospital within six weeks. They also agreed on a common goal: all stroke patients should receive treatment within 20 min of arrival. The next IDG meeting started with the division QA, posing one question: What will it take to realize the vision we have now created?

During the next six weeks, the participants in the IDG completed approximately forty interventions, and each participant completed at least four improvement measures. Typically, the group members worked in pairs. The measures entailed extensive tasks such as creating one procedure for the entire stroke workflow and archiving several procedures describing tasks for each subunit. The QA had administrative responsibility for the hospital’s quality system and was a member of the hospital’s management team. His position made it possible to provide resources to speed up the pace of improvement work. To ensure sufficient speed, he agreed with all managers that ongoing changes in the stroke procedures could be implemented without their formal approval. In this way, changes proposed by employees were discussed in the stroke group and then implemented in a revised stroke protocol.

A system was developed to continuously record results. An ambulance crew, a neurologist, and an intensive care nurse were asked to develop a checklist for the entire patient pathway, which was included in the patient’s medical records and documented as follows:

Time of onset of symptoms:

Arrival in Acute Admissions:

Start time of X-ray examination and The start time of effective treatment.

A copy of the checklist was placed on the desk of a secretary, who entered the times into an Excel sheet that could provide “door-to-needle times”.

Phase two: Interdisciplinary structures and improvement activity

On 13 November 2013, at 08:00, the new standard for stroke treatment was formally implemented. In the following two years, the participants in the improvement group carried out more than 50 additional measures to ensure fast treatment. During this period, the hospital reduced the average time to treatment from 62 to 29 min and reduced the variation in treatment times (see Fig. 1- Door-to-needle times).

The improvement work was structured through meetings in the interdisciplinary group during which action lists were used (illustrated in Table 1, examples of improvement actions undertaken during the implementation phase.). As the improvement group participants regularly worked in the stroke pathway, they constantly discovered new bottlenecks. Therefore, the stroke procedure was revised several times over the following two years. Each change required the participants in the improvement group to implement new instructions in their professional unit. The VSM process created a better understanding across disciplines of other specialist units’ tasks and what kind of bottlenecks they experienced. This enabled them to understand how to improve task solving for other departments. For example, the doctors made an on-call function available for ambulance drivers, and paramedics took over tasks previously performed at the hospital (e.g., inserting a cannula for intravenous drug treatment).

“Door-to-needle times” were announced regularly, which inspired many employees to suggest measures to ensure maximum speed. To ensure a better understanding among all employees, one doctor in the improvement group took the initiative to make a movie that illustrated the revised stroke process. Staff from specialist units were the actors, and the film was later presented at a large meeting for hospital employees and later used for educational purposes.

However, towards the end of 2015 and during the following year, the treatment time increased to 43 min, as did the variation in “door to needle time” (see Fig. 1- Door-to-needle times). The systematic improvement program had stopped, and the new stroke treatment standards were no longer fully adhered to. At the beginning of this period, four of the experienced participants left the IDG. The hospital’s QA, who had been responsible for convening and preparing meetings, was assigned to lead other IDGs to improve treatment for patients with lung cancer and sepsis; one doctor moved to another hospital to complete her education, the ambulance service representative changed his place of work, and the biomedical laboratory scientist was given a new area of responsibility. The participants from the specialist units were replaced by other clinicians. Attempts were made to hold meetings, but employees did not always prioritize attending. From December 2015and the next year, there was a significant reduction in the level of action taken to improve the stroke workflow. During this period, several doctors developed practices other than those prescribed in the revised stroke procedure.

Phase 3: Revitalizing improvement efforts

At the end of 2016, the neurology department took over the professional responsibility for stroke treatment, which had previously been organized by the medical department. An IDG meeting was held, in which the problem of significant variation in “door-to-needle times” was highlighted. To reduce the time spent, it was decided to start simulation exercises for the stroke team on a regular basis. Just over 200 employees may be members of a stroke team. Every team consists of an ambulance employee, a reception nurse, a neurologist, an internal medicine doctor, a radiographer, a radiological doctor, an intensive care nurse and a biomedical laboratory technician. Each day these eight employees carry the on-call function for stroke alarms on behalf of their specialist unit. During a normal year the stroke alarm will be triggered about 500 times, and no team will be composed of the exact same group of employees. Two experienced clinicians who had participated in IDG from the start of the project in 2013 undertook the task of facilitating similar exercises for the stroke team. From the beginning of 2017, monthly exercises were convened. Each exercise was prepared by sending a notice to relevant employees. The facilitators created patient cases for each session, including fictitious patient records and CT images. From the beginning of 2017 and the following years, the average time was once again reduced to 30 min, with less variation. Statistics from the National Stroke Registry for the period from 2018 to 2022 confirm that the results have been stable and place hospitals among the best at the national level between 2021 and 2022 (Norwegian Stroke Registry | National Service Environment for Medical Quality Registries).

Key contributing factors

The analysis reveals three key themes to understand the evolution of the improvement process and its results. First, some practitioners developed strong ownership of cross-disciplinary organizational change. Ownership was developed through participation in value stream mapping, participation in the development of a redesigned workflow, and efforts to implement the redesigned stroke workflow in the hospital’s daily practice. Second, interdisciplinary structures were established in the first phase of the improvement project. This involved a system for continuous feedback of results, a common procedure including all specialties, and teamwork. Finally, engaged practitioners use cross-disciplinary structures to revitalize cross-disciplinary improvement work.

Theme 1: Strong ownership

Through their involvement in planning and implementing a better quality of stroke treatment, the IDG participants developed a strong sense of ownership of the rationale behind the comprehensive cross-disciplinary organizational change. Ownership was developed through three processes.

The VMS process developed an understanding of the need for change

First, when the entire stroke workflow was mapped and assessed, practitioners realized that the poor quality of stroke treatment in hospitals was caused by an ill-considered division of labor and weak coordination between seven autonomous professional units [25]. These insights underscore the necessity of changing the distribution of tasks and interactions between professional units. An experienced doctor who initiated the stroke improvement work stated, “I have learned a lot from the work that we have done. It made me understand that there’s no point in getting someone to change if they do not understand why” (MD, December 2022). Another practitioner in the stroke improvement group commented, “It was interesting to see the entire workflow. When we understood other units’ task performance and what kind of bottlenecks they experienced, it was easier to understand how we could contribute to better flow” (Ambulance employe, November 2022).

Participation in redesigning workflow developed ownership of the rationale for a revised stroke procedure

The practitioners in the IDG took advantage of their new organizational understanding to determine a workflow that could provide faster treatment for stroke patients. During the first year of this improvement, work practitioners continually discovered new areas to improve, as they observed the practice field from their new perspective: “We started with a flowchart, and then we changed this again and again (MD, January 2024). In a presentation of this improvement work for nearby industrial companies in 2017, a participant in the IDG made the following statement: “The entire treatment chain was consulted and provided input. Together, we found the problem areas… there could be small details that turned out to take minutes we could not afford (quotation from a presentation by participants in the IDG, in Lean Forum Inland 13.03.2017). One of the initiators stated; “It is when employees feel like they’re actually participating and influencing a process that the outcome is good … The participants became very much in favor of the need for improvement. … I think the work we conducted in the start-up period was essential for maintaining the changes over time.” (MD, November 2022).

Efforts to implement a redesigned stroke workflow developed an understanding of why the participants need to engage to make changes happen

The participants realized that the revised stroke procedure represented extensive changes in the task solution for nearly 300 employees. Therefore, they considered their involvement to be a crucial factor for implementing the proposed changes in the hospital. In a conversation between the QA and a participant in the IDG, the employee stated, “No one, including you, would have had the opportunity to implement these solutions in our department without me convincing staff. Once my colleagues understood how the new solution would increase the quality for patients, there was less resistance to implementing the changes … they discovered the overall picture” (Radiographer, November2022. The participants in the IDG spent much of their time convincing colleges that the revised stroke procedure included sensible and necessary changes. Several experienced resistances among colleagues to implementing these changes, especially in departments that had to allocate more resources: “I felt like there was a tremendous commitment on my part… I spent a lot of time motivating and training staff to understand that this is perhaps one of the most important things we do because it has such great significance for the patient.” (Nurse, January 2023). The representative from the ambulance service expressed something similar: “We (IDG) changed the procedure a lot of times in the first years. In each case, it was necessary to provide instructions to the employees. We spent a lot of time in morning meetings reviewing the new adjustments” (Ambulance employe, December 2022).

Theme 2: Establishing interdisciplinary structures

The first six months of the improvement project developed some cross-disciplinary structures. These included a system for continuous feedback of results, a common procedure including all specialties, and teamwork. An examination of documents in the hospital’s quality system and interviews with employees show that the basic elements of these systems were maintained in this period.

System for continuous feedback of results

The system for feedback of results made “door-to-needle times” available to all employees from January 2014. A secretary working in the emergency medicine department was given documentation of the results as a permanent task. Several of the IDG participants believed that they and other employees were highly motivated because the aim of the improvement work was to increase the quality of patient treatment. “It became a kind of competition, where everyone became very concerned about what we could do to make this go as quickly as possible.” (MD, December 2022).

The documentation revealed that patients received faster treatment from the autumn of 2013 to the following two years. These results strengthened the position of the IDG as an effective change agent for stroke treatment. Therefore, employees informed representatives of the IDG about behavior that deviated from the new procedure. For example, such information revealed occasions where patients had arrived at the emergency department, without the stroke team being notified. This caused significant delays in treatment, as it took time to release personnel and equipment in the specialist units. The IDG investigated such matters to understand why this happened and introduced measurements to increase the quality of training for personnel. A review of treatment times was also an item on the agenda of IDG meetings during this period. In cases where the hospital had spent more than 30 min administering treatment, a doctor carried out a medical record review. A discussion of these findings in the IDG ensured a common understanding of why the delays occurred. The IDG implemented several measures based on this information. The systematic feedback of the results made it clear to all employees that the quality of the treatment deteriorated from the end of 2015 to the following year.

A common procedure including all specialties

The new stroke procedure described task solutions for all seven professional units and how these should be coordinated on a timeline. The action cards that described each professional unit’s task performance in detail were attached to the procedure. A participant in the IDG group made the following headline on a presentation for regional industrial companies that were invited to the hospital: “Treatment of stroke: From doing what each individual thinks is wise to creating a recipe for everyone to follow” (excerpt from presentation at the network gathering for Lean Forum Inland 13 March 2017).

Teamwork

The redesigned stroke procedure instructed all specialists to gather at the CT lab in response to the stroke alarm. In this room, each specialist should conduct tasks as part of coordinated teamwork. The team arena made it possible to observe other units’ task performance and struggles. It also made it possible for members of the team to evaluate the quality of coordination.

Theme 3 Practitioners used cross-disciplinary structures to revitalize improvement work

Interestingly, some specialists revitalized the systematic improvement work almost four years after the start of an improvement project. What enabled them to act?

First, the continuous feedback of results made it clear to everyone that the achievements gained were about to be lost. This makes it obvious that some people need to act to maintain high-quality stroke treatment.

Some specialists involved in the IDG from the beginning developed a strong sense of ownership of the cross-disciplinary change and were strongly motivated to maintain good results. They realized that the IDG gradually lost its role as a driving force in improving the stroke process. Four of the group’s members had left the group and were assigned to solve other tasks inside health services. No one had replaced the QAs’ role in convening meetings and ensuring that they were well prepared. The loss of structure in the IDG resulted in a great reduction in the number of improvement measures implemented, and the systematic follow-up results vanished. The employees who replaced the former participants in the IDG lacked the deep organizational insights that the previous members had developed through participation in VSM and redesign of the stroke workflow. Without such ownership, they were unable to convince new employees that faster treatment was a result of a better-coordinated workflow. IDG was no longer considered as a suitable arena in which to conduct further improvement work. An IDG participant stated, “None of us practitioners feel mandated to run such a group across disciplines in the hospital” (quotation from an interview in January 2023 with a member of the IDG, who later became one of the facilitators that led exercises for the stroke team).

In addition to the continuous feedback of results, two other interdisciplinary structures established three years earlier still exist. The stroke procedure described the tasks of all the specialist units. It had a high status among the specialists and outlined a clear recipe for task solving and coordination in the entire workflow. The third cross-disciplinary structure was team organizing, which provided a good vantage point for practitioners with strong ownership of the new stroke procedure. They were frustrated by what they observed; ‘I felt very often—and I said that repeatedly to the neurologists—that it is their training that is lacking…and we saw that time and time again—the doctors found it difficult to be team leaders” (Radiographer, January 2024). The doctors who were supposed to lead the team by coordinating task solutions were sometimes inexperienced in treating patients with stroke and faced challenging medical assessments. This may explain why they struggled to coordinate activities. A nurse reported, ‘I remember that the biomedical laboratory scientist had to wait to take blood samples because everyone was so concerned about carrying out their own tasks. We had to let them know that it was important to have a quick test result, so the lab could start the analysis.” (Nurse, January 2023).

In addition to being an arena for observing the quality of task solutions and coordination, the stroke team provided an opportunity to conduct improvement work. Simulation exercises enabled team members to gain insight into the importance of coordination. “We sent out notifications about exercises in advance to prepare for simulation. They included a description of the tasks – you should be able to do this…. here is your action card. … In addition, then it became obvious when someone did not know their tasks (Facilitator, January 2024). With respect to the learning effect, a facilitator stated, “And when we practiced, everybody understood why we should have this order… Why it is important that the lab comes first and takes blood samples—so that they can initiate the analysis… Why does the neurologist have to come in as No. 2… How can one utilize the time while the patient is being X-rayed…. Then, the work flowed much better”. (Facilitator, January 2024). In this way, engaged practitioners were able to revitalize systematic improvement work as part of the ordinary operating structure at the hospital.

However, the results indicate how and why it was difficult to retain the shorter treatment times and thus maintain the gains achieved in the first phase of improvement work. The good quality of care gained in the early phase of this improvement work was almost lost from 2015 to 2016. This shows that continuous improvement in interdisciplinary processes is about more than finding ever better and more effective solutions (cf. definition in the introduction section). The most experienced facilitators who conducted training with the stroke team expressed this as follows: “I think continuous improvement depends on enthusiasts, but what happens when the enthusiasts no longer show up?“. Emergency treatment in hospitals is largely performed by younger doctors who have not completed their training as specialists. These doctors are frequently replaced by new doctors, as in the present case. Therefore, replication success will depend on good systems for repetitive training. Such training will be very important for maintaining the positive effects of organizational changes.

Discussion

This diagnostic case study shows that the organizational changes developed in 2013 and 2014 have largely been retained (until now), while also revealing how and why performance temporarily deteriorated before being restored. This is believed to be due to employees across disciplines developing a common understanding of (a) why better coordination is needed and (b) why they need to play an active role in implementing new solutions.

These results correspond to studies reporting that affective commitment is important for achieving successful change in healthcare [26, 27] and elsewhere [28]. Similarly, a Canadian study showed that hospital staff did not support a lean strategy because it failed to activate sense-making among employees when they implemented the strategy [29]. The findings also correspond to a study arguing that sustainable continuous improvement is a result of employees’ desire to participate in improvement efforts [30]. This study indicates that employees’ motivation to participate is strengthened if each employee considers that her/his participation is important for achieving results. Several of the doctors were heavily involved in work to achieve change. Doctors’ involvement has previously been found to be an important factor in establishing permanent improvement efforts [31]. Another study revealed that the use of VSM is important not only in the implementation phase but also for establishing sustainable systems for continuous improvement [6]. The exercises established in this case represent continuous training in assessing and improving the holistic treatment chain. From a diagnostic perspective, such practices also function as mechanisms for identifying and addressing emerging coordination problems in real time. Several studies highlight that such structures can promote sustainable improvements [32, 33] and [34].

However, this does not guarantee that the CI will survive in the future because it is dependent on enthusiasts, who constitute the driving force. The maintenance might be better protected by introducing organizational functions that clarify responsibilities for cross-disciplinary processes. This could involve designating a permanent leader’s function responsible for the coordination of the cross-disciplinary process. The leader could be a professional employee or a leader from the silo with the clearest medical professional responsibility. A far more revolutionary alternative is process organization. This requires ensuring specialist professional expertise through means other than hierarchical organization in professional silos. Framing the study as diagnostic clarifies its contribution: while the empirical findings are context-specific, the process of systematically identifying breakdowns and enabling recovery in improvement work may be applicable across similar healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This diagnostic case study examines the sustainability of a cross-disciplinary quality improvement initiative by tracing phases of improvement, decline, and recovery over time. The analysis shows that sustaining continuous improvement depends less on initial process redesign than on the ongoing alignment between organizational structures and practitioner engagement. Consistent with sustainability theory, decline was associated with staff turnover, reduced improvement activity, and erosion of shared ownership. However, institutionalized coordination mechanisms—such as performance feedback, shared care pathways, and team-based work—enabled the organization to detect breakdowns and reinitiate improvement.

By adopting a diagnostic perspective, the study highlights sustainability as a dynamic capability involving continuous monitoring, sensemaking, and adaptive response. The findings suggest that sustaining improvement in professionally fragmented settings requires organizational capacity for recurrent diagnosis and learning, rather than stability or formalization alone.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, as a single-case study, the findings from the diagnosis are case-specific. As stated above, however, this does invalidate the importance of diagnosing the causes of hampering CI-projects. Second, the first author’s dual role as both facilitator of the improvement initiative and researcher introduces potential sources of bias, including interpretive and confirmation bias [31]. As outlined in the reflexive considerations, this challenge was addressed through an explicitly reflexive approach, including iterative discussions with co-authors not involved in the initiative and validation of preliminary findings through feedback from participants. Nevertheless, the author’s embedded position may have influenced both data generation and interpretation. Third, parts of the empirical material are retrospective and may be subject to recall bias. In addition, as participants were directly involved in the improvement work, their accounts may reflect elements of social desirability. Finally, although the use of multiple data sources strengthens the study through triangulation, the findings remain shaped by the researchers’ analytical perspectives, and alternative interpretations are possible.
