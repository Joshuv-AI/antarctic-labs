Real-time phase-dependent occupational radiation exposure analysis in full-endoscopic spine surgery
Authors: Takayuki Kitahara, Takafumi Ohshima, Daiki Nakajima, Seiya Watanabe, Saori Soeda, Makoto Takeuchi, Hiroshi Kageyama, Junzo Fujitani, Masatoshi Morimoto, Hiroaki Manabe, Fumitake Tezuka, Koichi Sairyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Full-endoscopic spine surgery performed under local anesthesia offers clinical advantages, particularly for elderly patients. However, its reliance on fluoroscopy raises concerns about occupational radiation exposure. Although several studies have evaluated cumulative radiation dose, real-time data identifying specific high-risk phases of surgery remain limited. This study aimed to clarify phase-dependent scatter radiation patterns and identify targets for effective protection. We analyzed real-time personal dose equivalent, Hp(10), measured over the protective apron (over-apron Hp(10)) in 28 FESS cases using RaySafe i3 dosimeters across four staff roles: operator, assistant, supervisor, and scrub nurse. Surgical procedures were divided into three phases: Phase 1 (Marking), Phase 2 (Setup), and Phase 3 (Check). The operator showed the highest median total over-apron Hp(10) per case (0.244 mSv), significantly exceeding other staff (p < 0.001), with an operator-to-nurse over-apron Hp(10) ratio of approximately 51.9:1. Phase-based analysis demonstrated that the operator’s over-apron Hp(10) was heavily concentrated in Phase 2 (Setup) (median 0.194 mSv), significantly higher than Phase 1 and Phase 3 (p < 0.001). No significant difference in total over-apron Hp(10) was found among surgical approaches. In conclusion, although FESS is minimally invasive for patients, the Setup phase represents a critical target for radiation safety. These anatomical and spatial observations suggest that further investigation into behavioral modifications, such as the use of single-shot fluoroscopy and maximizing operator distance during sheath placement, is warranted to reduce occupational radiation risk.

Introduction

Full-endoscopic spine surgery (FESS), particularly when performed via the transforaminal approach under local anesthesia, has emerged as a transformative technique in minimally invasive spine surgery. As established by Sairyo et al., this "awake and aware" strategy offers profound clinical advantages, including the ability to perform decompression in elderly or medically compromised patients who are unfit for general anesthesia1,2. Furthermore, the continuous feedback from the awake patient allows surgeons to monitor nerve root status intraoperatively, thereby minimizing the risk of severe iatrogenic neural injury3,4. However, the minimally invasive nature of FESS paradoxically introduces a significant occupational hazard: a heavy reliance on fluoroscopic guidance to navigate the endoscopic portal safely. Consequently, while patients benefit from reduced invasiveness, the surgical team faces the risk of increased radiation exposure, a concern that has been increasingly documented in recent endoscopic literature5,6. Moreover, recent comparative studies have highlighted the distinct radiation exposure risks associated with various intraoperative imaging modalities in spinal and orthopedic surgery7,8. Previous reports have shown that occupational radiation exposure in minimally invasive spine surgery is not trivial. Reported surgeon chest doses include 0.1718 mSv per operated level during percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy9 and approximately 0.10 mSv per case outside the apron during minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion6.

However, most previous studies have relied on cumulative dose measurements using passive dosimeters. These studies have provided valuable baseline data regarding total occupational exposure per case or the anatomical distribution of radiation dose6,9, but they do not identify which surgical phase is primarily responsible for exposure. Moreover, they do not elucidate when or why peak exposures occur within the FESS workflow. This lack of real-time, phase-dependent data leaves a critical gap in safety management, making it difficult to identify specific high-risk maneuvers and hindering the development of targeted behavioral interventions for the surgical team.

To address this critical gap, this study aimed to elucidate the real-time, phase-dependent scatter radiation dynamics during FESS performed under local anesthesia. Using a high-sensitivity real-time dosimetry system, we continuously monitored the personal dose equivalent, Hp(10), by placing the dosimeters outside the protective lead aprons of the entire surgical team. This over-apron Hp(10) measurement was specifically utilized to evaluate the relative scatter radiation density in the operating field, rather than estimating the whole-body effective dose, across three distinct surgical phases. This study was designed to clarify phase-dependent exposure patterns and to identify practical targets for occupational radiation protection during FESS.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethics

This prospective observational study was conducted at Tokushima University Hospital between November 2025 and February 2026. The study protocol was formally reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tokushima University Hospital (approval number: 3642). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Informed consent was also obtained for the publication of identifying images/information in an online open access publication. We analyzed 28 consecutive cases of FESS performed under local anesthesia. As an exploratory study designed to establish real-time phase-dependent exposure dynamics, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed; rather, a convenience sample of 28 cases was included to provide initial benchmark data. The surgical procedures included Full-Endoscopic Discectomy (FED), Thermal Annuloplasty (TA) using radiofrequency ablation, Full-Endoscopic Lumbar Foraminotomy (FELF), and Full-Endoscopic Ventral Facetectomy (FEVF) (Fig. 1A–D). The surgical team consisted of five mid-career orthopaedic spinal surgeons who joined the department as clinical fellows to master full-endoscopic spine surgery. Radiation dosimetry and equipment

All radiographic images were acquired using a mobile C-arm fluoroscopy system (Zenition 70, Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands). To minimize occupational radiation exposure, the system was operated predominantly in single-shot fluoroscopy mode. However, during technically demanding steps in the Setup phase, continuous fluoroscopy was occasionally required to confirm needle trajectory and sheath placement under lateral imaging. The exposure parameters were dynamically regulated by an automatic exposure control system, with representative values of approximately 77 kVp and 3.7 mA. The system utilized standard inherent and added beam filtration. Image magnification was not used, and collimation was appropriately adjusted to the region of interest to restrict the primary radiation field and minimize secondary scatter.

Real-time radiation exposure was monitored using the RaySafe i3 system (Unfors RaySafe, Hovås, Sweden). Personal dosimeters were worn by four key surgical staff members: the Operator (Op1), Assistant (Op2), Supervisor, and Scrub Nurse. All staff members wore standard 0.35 mm lead-equivalent protective aprons and thyroid collars. Importantly, supplemental shielding devices such as table-suspended lead curtains or ceiling-suspended screens were not utilized for the operating staff due to the spatial constraints and the necessity for frequent C-arm maneuvers inherent to FESS. Mobile lead shields were solely provided for circulating observers. The RaySafe dosimeters were attached over the lead apron at the chest level to measure the unshielded personal dose equivalent, Hp(10). This over-apron measurement was deliberately chosen not to estimate the whole-body effective dose, but to accurately evaluate the relative exposure differences among staff members and surgical phases. Data were wirelessly transmitted to a dedicated monitor in the operating room, providing immediate visualization of exposure rates.

Definition of surgical phases

To identify high-risk maneuvers, the continuous dosimetry data for each surgical procedure was divided into three distinct phases. Phase segmentation was performed retrospectively by the primary author based on timestamps recorded by the RaySafe system. Distinct phases were clearly identifiable because of standardized non-fluoroscopic intervals inherent to the surgical workflow. Specifically, a non-fluoroscopic interval of at least 10 min invariably occurs between Phase 1 and Phase 2 for patient disinfection and sterile draping. Similarly, another gap exceeding 10 min reliably separates Phase 2 and Phase 3, corresponding to the main endoscopic decompression procedure where fluoroscopy is routinely paused. Phase 1 (Marking) involved skin marking of the entry point and trajectory planning using postero-anterior and lateral fluoroscopic views (Fig. 2A, B). Phase 2 (Setup) included the infiltration of local anesthesia, needle puncture, guidewire insertion, dilation, and placement of the endoscopic sheath, following the standardized step-by-step protocol described elsewhere10. This phase typically involved frequent lateral fluoroscopic guidance, with occasional use of continuous fluoroscopy when necessary for accurate needle and sheath placement while the operator is positioned on the X-ray tube side (Fig. 2C-E). Phase 3 (Check) consisted of intraoperative confirmation of the decompression level or instrument position. Data processing and statistical analysis

Over-apron Hp(10) data were synchronized with surgical timestamps to calculate the total over-apron Hp(10) per case. For multi-level cases (e.g., TA involving two levels), where skin marking (Phase 1) is performed once for multiple levels, the over-apron Hp(10) and duration from Phase 1 were mathematically imputed to subsequent levels to ensure accurate per-case comparison.

We evaluated five parameters: (1) phase-dependent over-apron Hp(10) for the operator; (2) dosimeter-defined exposure-active time (defined as the cumulative duration of non-zero radiation detection per phase); (3) total over-apron Hp(10) comparison among surgical procedures (FELF, FEVF, FED, and TA); (4) exposure levels among the four staff roles; and (5) the protection factor relative to the operator.

Continuous variables are presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)] due to non-normal distribution. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare over-apron Hp(10) among phases and surgical procedures. The Friedman test was employed for paired comparisons of over-apron Hp(10) among the four staff roles within the same surgery. Post-hoc analyses were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction or the Nemenyi test where appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Python (pandas, scipy, statsmodels).

Results

Surgical demographics and total over-apron Hp(10)

A total of 28 consecutive FESS cases were analyzed. The distribution of surgical procedures was as follows: TA (n = 10), FED (n = 7), FELF (n = 6), and FEVF (n = 5). The median total over-apron Hp(10) for the operator per case was 0.244 mSv [IQR: 0.175–0.413].

Phase-dependent over-apron Hp(10) (operator)

Analysis of the operator’s exposure by surgical phase revealed a distinct concentration of radiation during the Setup phase (Phase 2). The median over-apron Hp(10) in Phase 2 was 0.194 mSv [0.130–0.395], which was significantly higher than that in Phase 1 (Marking) (0.065 mSv [0.030–0.082], p < 0.001) and Phase 3 (Check) (0.002 mSv [0.001–0.005], p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Consistent with the over-apron Hp(10) distribution, the cumulative exposure-active time also differed significantly across the three phases. The median exposure-active time was 1.16 min [IQR: 1.00–1.33] in Phase 1, 2.38 min [2.04–2.98] in Phase 2, and 0.25 min [0.10–0.53] in Phase 3 (p < 0.001). Role-dependent over-apron Hp(10) and protection factors

A significant disparity in over-apron Hp(10) was observed among the surgical team members (p < 0.001). The operator (Op1) received the highest median over-apron Hp(10) (0.244 mSv), followed by the assistant, supervisor, and scrub nurse.

Notably, despite standing adjacent to the operator within the sterile field, the Assistant (Op2) received a significantly lower median over-apron Hp(10) of 0.081 mSv [0.046–0.133] (p < 0.001 vs. Operator). The over-apron Hp(10) ratio indicated that the operator received approximately 3.3 times (median ratio: 3.3) the over-apron Hp(10) of the assistant. This finding highlights that even a slight difference in position relative to the X-ray source and the primary scatter field has a substantial impact on radiation safety.

The Scrub Nurse, positioned farther from the radiation source, received a negligible median over-apron Hp(10) of 0.004 mSv [0.003–0.009], which was significantly lower than that of all other staff (p < 0.001). Discussion

Principal findings and study significance

This prospective observational study using real-time dosimetry demonstrated that the primary surgeon in FESS faces a disproportionate radiation risk compared with the rest of the surgical team, a phenomenon driven almost exclusively by the Setup phase. Our phase-dependent analysis revealed that maneuvers involving local anesthesia and endoscopic sheath placement (Phase 2) resulted in significantly higher over-apron Hp(10) than other surgical phases, acting as the primary determinant of total occupational over-apron Hp(10) regardless of the specific endoscopic procedure performed. Furthermore, the operator received approximately 50 times the over-apron Hp(10) of the scrub nurse and over 3 times that of the adjacent assistant. These findings underscore that the critical control point for radiation safety lies not in the surgical technique itself, but in the geometric relationship and fluoroscopic mode employed during the initial setup.

The setup phase: a critical control point

The first major finding is that the operator’s over-apron Hp(10) is heavily concentrated in the Setup phase. While previous studies have established that minimally invasive surgery procedures generally incur higher over-apron Hp(10) than open surgery due to reliance on fluoroscopy11, our real-time data pinpoint the exact timing of this risk. This observation refines the understanding of occupational risk in endoscopic spine surgery, where high cumulative doses to the surgeon’s hands and body have been reported9. The disproportionate over-apron Hp(10) during the Setup phase is attributed to the confluence of unfavorable positioning and fluoroscopic modality. During this phase, the surgeon typically stands on the X-ray tube side to manipulate the needle under lateral fluoroscopy. Recent spatial dosimetry analysis visualized that the zone immediately adjacent to the X-ray tube during lateral imaging represents the area of highest scattered radiation density12. Furthermore, the need for precise trajectory control often compels surgeons to utilize continuous fluoroscopy. As demonstrated in cadaveric studies, continuous fluoroscopy significantly elevates the dose rate compared to pulsed fluoroscopy13. Consequently, the Setup phase creates a localized high-risk environment in which the surgeon is exposed to the highest scatter intensity during the most fluoroscopy-intensive portion of the procedure. This likely reflects a combination of repeated lateral imaging, occasional continuous fluoroscopy during trajectory adjustment, and close proximity to the X-ray tube side.

Interpretation of dosimetry and shielding limitations

When interpreting these results, a critical distinction must be made regarding the operational quantity measured. In this study, dosimeters were placed over the lead apron to record Hp(10). As noted in radiation safety literature, over-apron readings do not represent the actual whole-body effective dose received by the worker, as 0.35 mm lead-equivalent aprons typically attenuate 80% to 90% of the incident scatter radiation14. Therefore, the nominal effective dose to the staff’s body is realistically 10–20% of the reported readings15. The utility of these over-apron measurements lies specifically in identifying the relative differences in scatter radiation density across distinct surgical phases and physical locations within the operating room. Furthermore, the high relative over-apron Hp(10) observed in the Operator must be contextualized by the absence of supplemental shielding. Due to the physical space constraints of FESS, table-suspended lead curtains or ceiling screens were not utilized. The absence of these established dose-reduction tools likely contributed significantly to the operator’s over-apron Hp(10)6,16, highlighting a practical dilemma in current FESS workflows where ergonomic limitations preclude the use of optimal radiation shielding.

Structural risks and disparity among the surgical team members

The second critical finding is the stark disparity in over-apron Hp(10) among the surgical team, identifying the operator’s position as a unique structural risk. Consistent with findings in minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF) procedures where the primary surgeon received significantly higher doses than assistants or technicians6, our study confirmed a similar trend in FESS. Notably, while the assistant stood adjacent to the operator within the sterile field, their over-apron Hp(10) was approximately one-third of the operator’s, and the scrub nurse’s over-apron Hp(10) was negligible. This steep over-apron Hp(10) gradient validates the profound protective effect of distance and positioning (Fig. 6). A recent study in an orthopedic trauma center demonstrated that radiation exposure drops precipitously with distance, becoming negligible at just 2 m from the source17. Additionally, phantom studies verified that increasing the distance from the source significantly reduces scatter radiation exposure, adhering to the inverse square law18. Cadaveric simulations further clarified that the operator, positioned on the X-ray tube side, stands in the area of maximum scatter intensity, whereas staff on the detector side or slightly removed are shielded from the primary backscatter19 (Fig. 6). Mesbahi and Rouhani also reported that scatter dose rates drop significantly just 20 cm outside the primary beam path20. Proposed behavioral modifications

Given the structural constraints where the X-ray tube must face the surgeon during lateral imaging, physical shielding alone is often insufficient. Although not directly investigated in our current dataset, extrapolating from our phase-dependent over-apron Hp(10) results and existing literature allows us to propose a theoretical, two-pronged behavioral modification strategy for future evaluation: "Pulse and Step-back". First, transitioning from continuous to pulsed fluoroscopy is paramount. Yamashita et al. demonstrated that utilizing pulsed fluoroscopy (8 pulses/sec) reduced radiation exposure to the surgeon’s hands and thyroid by approximately 70% compared to continuous mode13. Second, the conscious maintenance of distance must be rigorously enforced; even a momentary retreat of one step during image acquisition can drastically lower over-apron Hp(10)21. These findings support future evaluation of technique-focused behavioral interventions during local anesthesia infiltration and sheath placement, including minimizing fluoroscopy use and increasing operator distance when feasible. Recent evidence also suggests that real-time auditory and visual feedback significantly contributes to dose reduction22, supporting the importance of such behavioral interventions. Because the operator’s technique affects the radiation exposure of the entire team, disciplined fluoroscopic practice represents a shared professional responsibility.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (28 cases), and the data were collected from a single center, although multiple surgeons participated. Second, radiation exposure was measured only at the chest level outside the lead apron. While extremity (hand) and lens doses are critical concerns in endoscopic surgery due to the surgeon’s proximity to the primary beam9, the high cost of real-time dosimetry systems restricted our capacity to monitor multiple body sites simultaneously. Consequently, our data may underestimate the specific local exposure to the surgeon’s hands. Nevertheless, the chest dose serves as a valid real-time proxy for establishing the relative risk profile of surgical maneuvers and identifying high-risk phases.

Third, the significantly higher over-apron Hp(10) observed in Phase 2 is likely attributable to a combination of factors, including a high overall frequency of image acquisition, occasional use of continuous fluoroscopy during dynamic needle manipulation and sheath placement, and the operator’s proximity to the scatter source. While the cumulative exposure-active time was quantified, the dosimetry system could not retrospectively distinguish continuous fluoroscopy from intermittent single-shot acquisitions on a frame-by-frame basis. Therefore, the specific dosimetric contribution of different fluoroscopy modes remains to be elucidated.

Furthermore, the technical specifications of the dosimetry system must be acknowledged. The RaySafe i3 dosimeters may exhibit inherent angular and energy dependence, particularly during lateral fluoroscopic projections, which could marginally influence the over-apron Hp(10) measurements. Additionally, because actual pulse-rate settings and console-derived beam-on fluoroscopy times were unavailable for retrospective extraction, the ‘exposure-active time’ utilized in this study serves as a dosimeter-derived surrogate. While this metric provides informative relative comparisons among surgical phases, it should be interpreted cautiously as an approximation rather than a direct measurement of the actual fluoroscopy beam-on time.

Conclusion

FESS offers significant benefits to patients as a minimally invasive procedure. However, it presents a unique occupational radiation hazard specifically for the primary operator. Our real-time dosimetric analysis identified the Setup phase—characterized by frequent lateral fluoroscopic guidance, occasional use of continuous fluoroscopy, and proximity to the X-ray tube—as the primary contributing factor to elevated over-apron Hp(10). Recognizing these inherent positional risk differences among the surgical team is essential. Future efforts to mitigate this hazard should evaluate the efficacy of targeted behavioral modifications, such as strictly adhering to pulsed fluoroscopy and consciously maximizing distance during initial sheath placement.
