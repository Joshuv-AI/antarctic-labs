The prevalence of refractive errors among patients with keratoconus attending Damascus hospital, Syria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rami Akwan, Ahmad Nabil Alhouri, Hasan Nabil Alhouri, Raghad Aljaramani, Hany Olabi, Sameh Issa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKeratoconus (KC) is a progressive ectatic corneal disease characterized by corneal thinning and cone-like protrusion, leading to significant refractive errors. Despite its global impact, local data for the Syrian population remains scarce. This study aimed to investigate refractive error patterns, prevalence, and disease grading among a sample of Syrian patients with KC.MethodsThis prospective cross-sectional study evaluated 173 eyes from 93 patients at Damascus Hospital. Corneal Tomography was performed using the Pentacam Scheimpflug system. Refractive status was determined via subjective refraction, and KC severity was graded using the Buxton keratometric classification. Statistical analysis included Chi-square tests and Pearson correlations.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 29.7 ± 10.9 years, with most patients (34.4%) aged 19–30 years. Bilateral involvement was observed in 92.5% of patients. Mild and moderate disease were the most frequent grades, accounting for 45.1% and 43.9% of eyes, respectively. A significant association between KC grading and gender was identified (P = 0.04), whereas no significant association was found between grading and age. Oblique compound myopic astigmatism was the most prevalent refractive error (25.4%). Kmax showed a strong positive correlation with absolute spherical equivalent (r = 0.669, P < 0.001), whereas corneal thickness at the thinnest location showed a weak negative correlation (r = -0.393, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe clinical profile of KC in Syrian patients is comparable to reports from other regional and global populations, manifesting as bilateral mild-to-moderate disease in young adults, with compound myopic astigmatism as the hallmark refractive error. Future research should prioritize multicenter genetic and environmental studies within the Syrian population to optimize disease management strategies and early detection.Keywords: Corneal tomography, Keratoconus, Pentacam, Refractive errors, Syria

Background

Abnormalities in corneal curvature are a primary cause of refractive errors that reduce visual acuity [1]. When a patient has difficulty focusing on distant objects, they are classified as myopic; difficulty focusing on near objects indicates hyperopia, while blurred vision at all distances typically signifies astigmatism [2]. One significant contributor to abnormal corneal curvature and the development of these refractive errors is Keratoconus KC.

KC is a progressive ectatic corneal disease characterized by central or paracentral corneal thinning and steepening, which results in a cone-like protrusion of the cornea. These structural changes reduce visual acuity and are typically accompanied by progressive myopia and astigmatism [3–5]. Although the etiology of KC remains unclear, various genetic, environmental, and inflammatory factors are considered to be associated with the condition [6, 7]. The disease generally develops in the early twenties and affects both eyes asymmetrically, often manifesting in one eye before the other. Progression usually stabilizes by the fourth decade of life, although earlier or later onset has been documented [1, 6, 7]. Previous studies report that approximately 50% of patients with unilateral involvement will experience progression in the fellow eye within a few years, with the highest risk occurring during the first six years after onset [8].

Gender predominance in keratoconus remains unclear, as studies have shown variability across populations, settings, and diagnostic methods [9]. Its prevalence in the general population is estimated at approximately 1 in 2,000, though this varies considerably depending on ethnicity, age, geography, and diagnostic criteria [1, 10]. For example, the prevalence in Denmark has been reported as low as 4 per 100,000 persons, whereas in the Middle East, rates as high as 22 per 100,000 have been observed [7].

Currently, Scheimpflug-based corneal imaging systems, such as the Pentacam, are widely utilized to identify and monitor KC progression by evaluating the anterior and posterior surfaces, pachymetry, keratometry, and elevation maps [5, 6, 11]. Despite the established global understanding of keratoconus, local data on Syrian patients with keratoconus remain limited. A study conducted on a university-student population in Syria in 2019–2020 reported a keratoconus prevalence of 1.43% among 839 participants [12], while another study of patients seeking refractive surgery found prevalence rates of 18.19% for keratoconus and 13.52% for keratoconus suspects among 1479 participants [13]. However, data regarding refractive error patterns and disease grading among keratoconus patients in the general Syrian population attending hospitals remain scarce. Without such information, clinicians in the region lack a tailored, evidence-based framework for early screening and management. This knowledge gap may contribute to delayed diagnosis and suboptimal care, significantly impacting visual function and quality of life in young adults.

Consequently, this study was conducted to characterize a sample of Syrian patients with keratoconus who attend the Damascus Hospital, with a particular focus on refractive error patterns, their prevalence, and the grading of keratoconus among affected patients.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Damascus Hospital (Al-Mujtahid Hospital), a major tertiary care center under the Syrian Ministry of Health. Data collection occurred between September 2021 and April 2022. The study cohort included both newly diagnosed patients and those attending follow-up examinations for previously diagnosed Keratoconus (KC).

Clinical protocol and imaging

All participants were evaluated by a single experienced ophthalmologist to ensure diagnostic consistency. Ocular examination was performed under standardized darkroom conditions. Corneal tomographic analysis was conducted using the Pentacam system (Wavelight Allegro Oculyzer). During imaging acquisition, patients were instructed to maintain a wide-open gaze and fixate on the central black spot within the instrument’s blue fixation beam to minimize blink-related artifacts.

Keratoconus diagnosis

Keratoconus detection was based on the following tomographic parameters collectively:

Maximum Keratometry (Kmax): The steepest point on the anterior surface of the cornea.

Maximum Keratometry (Kmax): The steepest point on the anterior surface of the cornea.

Pachymetry values: Thinnest Pachymetry (TP), which is the thickness at the thinnest location, and Apical Pachymetry, which is the thickness at the corneal apex.

Pachymetry values: Thinnest Pachymetry (TP), which is the thickness at the thinnest location, and Apical Pachymetry, which is the thickness at the corneal apex.

Elevation Maps: Maximum anterior and posterior elevation measured using the Best-Fit Toric Ellipsoid (BFTE) reference body within an 8-mm diameter zone.

Elevation Maps: Maximum anterior and posterior elevation measured using the Best-Fit Toric Ellipsoid (BFTE) reference body within an 8-mm diameter zone.

In addition, the Kmax2/TL index was applied, which has been reported to demonstrate high diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 99.5% and a specificity of 95.7% for differentiating keratoconic eyes from normal eyes in clinical settings at a cutoff value of ≥ 4.1 [14].

Keratoconus severity was graded according to the mean central keratometry value (Km) obtained from corneal imaging, based on the Buxton Keratometric classification as follows: mild ≤ 45 D, moderate (45.1–52.0 D), and advanced to severe ≥ 52.1 D. This simplified classification conceptually aligns with guidelines of the Contact Lens Association of Ophthalmologists (CLAO) [7, 15] and is routinely used at Damascus Hospital as a practical approach in busy clinical settings. It was also applied, with minor modifications, in previous similar cross-sectional studies [1, 16].

Refraction and visual acuity assessment

Uncorrected Distance Visual Acuity (UDVA) and Best Corrected Visual Acuity (BCVA) with spectacles were measured using an E-tumbling chart (decimal scale) and then converted to Log MAR (Logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution) using the standard formula [LogMAR = - Log(decimal acuity)] for statistical analysis. For eyes with uncorrected visual acuity worse than the top line (the largest letter) of the eye chart at the standard testing distance, vision was recorded as counting fingers (CF) without conversion to LogMAR. Therefore, BCVA with spectacles was measured separately for these eyes and subsequently converted to LogMAR using the standard formula.

Subjective refraction was performed using a phoropter without cycloplegia. Refractive errors were defined based on the Manifest Refraction Spherical Equivalent (MRSE), calculated by adding spherical power to half of the cylindrical power. Myopia was defined as a spherical power of − 0.50 diopters or less, while hyperopia was defined as a spherical power of + 0.50 diopters or greater. Astigmatism was defined as a cylindrical power of at least − 0.50 D.

Astigmatism was further classified based on the axis orientation and the focal relationship of the principal meridians. Based on axis orientation, it was categorized as: With the Rule (WTR), if the axis of a positive cylinder lay within 30 ° on either side of the vertical meridian; Against the Rule (ATR), if it lay within 30 ° on either side of the horizontal meridian; and oblique if the axis lay between 1200 ° – 1500 ° and 300 ° − 60°. Based on the location of the principal meridian foci relative to the retina, simple myopic astigmatism was defined as plano spherical power with a negative cylindrical component, whereas simple hyperopic astigmatism was defined as plano spherical power with a positive cylindrical component. Compound astigmatism was determined when both principal meridians are either myopic or hyperopic. As for mixed astigmatism, it was determined when one meridian is myopic, and the other is hyperopic.

Exclusion criteria

For the purpose of this study, exclusion criteria were applied at both eye and patient levels. Eyes were excluded if they had a history of ocular surgery (corneal collagen cross-link (CXL), corneal implants, and other eye procedures), co-existing ocular pathologies (e.g., glaucoma, uveitis), corneal hydrops, recent ocular trauma, or poor-quality tomographic maps (indicated as “invalid” by the Pentacam software). Consequently, patients were excluded if any bilateral criteria were met.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Data were initially organized in Microsoft Excel 2016. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while quantitative variables were expressed as means and standard deviations. The association between KC severity and demographics such as age and sex, as well as refractive error types, was evaluated using the Chi-square test. When expected cell counts were less than five, Fisher’s exact test was applied.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between Kmax and the absolute value of MRSE, and between the thinnest pachymetry and the absolute value of MRSE. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Syrian Private University Institutional Review Board (IRB) (reference number: PH-22023022-82). Additionally, official permission to conduct the study at Damascus Hospital was obtained from the hospital’s review board through the appropriate administrative channels, as the hospital is affiliated with the Syrian Private University for clinical training. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Damascus Hospital Review Board and the Declaration of Helsinki [17]. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Strict measures were taken to ensure patient anonymity and data confidentiality.

Results

A total of 99 patients were initially examined. Six patients were excluded: one due to severe corneal hydrops in one eye and poor fixation in the fellow eye, and five due to inability to complete the required examinations. The final cohort consisted of 93 patients; of these, seven patients (7.5%) exhibited unilateral keratoconus, contributing a single affected eye each, while 86 patients (92.5%) exhibited bilateral disease of varying severity. Among the bilateral cases, six eyes were excluded due to previous corneal cross-linking (CXL) or intracorneal implants. Consequently, a total of 173 eyes were included in the final analysis.

The mean age of the participants was 29.6 ± 10.6 years, with 41 males (44.1%) and 52 females (55.9%), as illustrated in (Fig. 1). Participants were stratified into four age groups: ≤ 18 years (n = 19, 20.4%), 19–30 years (n = 32, 34.4%), 31–40 years (n = 30, 32.3%), and over 40 years (n = 12, 12.9%), as shown in (Fig. 2).

Regarding tomographic parameters, the mean Kmax was 52.7 ± 6.07 D [range: 43–74.5]. The mean thinnest pachymetry measured 473.3 ± 43.1 μm [range: 343–609], while apex pachymetry averaged 486.05 ± 41.17 μm [range: 369–612]. Mean maximum anterior and posterior elevation values were 14.53 ± 10.08 μm [range: 2–48], and 31.05 ± 19.36 μm [range: 5–93], respectively. In terms of refractive status, the mean spherical power was − 1.87 ± 3.69 [range: -17.00 to 10.25], and the mean cylindrical refraction was − 2.85 ± 1.9 D [range: -10.00 to 0.00]. The mean Manifest Refraction Spherical Equivalent (MRSE) was − 3.30 ± 3.98 [range: −20.50 to 8.75 D].

For 149 eyes (86.1% of the total), the mean uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) was 0.5 ± 0.4 logMAR [range: 0 to 1.30], while the mean best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) with spectacles was 0.14 ± 0.19 logMAR [range: 0 to 1.00]. The remaining 24 eyes (13.9%) had unrecordable visual acuity and were assessed using finger counting (CF). The BCVA for these eyes was independently evaluated, with a mean of 0.5 ± 0.31 logMAR [range: 0.10 to 1.30].

As shown in (Table 1 and Table 2), the distribution of KC grades among 173 eyes was 45.1% mild, 43.9% moderate, and 11% advanced to severe. Moderate KC was more frequent in females (29.5%), while mild KC was more frequent in males (26.0%). While no significant difference was found between KC grade and age groups (P = 0.299), mild and moderate grades were most frequent in patients aged 19–30 years, at 17.9% and 14.5%, respectively, whereas severe KC was most frequent in patients aged 31–40 years, at 5.2%. Notably, a significant difference was observed between gender and KC grading (P = 0.04).

Discussion

Keratoconus (KC) is a progressive ectatic corneal disease characterized by corneal thinning and steepening, leading to various refractive errors, most notably complex variants of astigmatism. As the condition progresses and corneal steepening increases, the patient’s visual acuity typically undergoes significant deterioration [16]. This study was conducted to address the lack of epidemiological data regarding KC grading and refractive error patterns within the Syrian population.

In our analysis of 173 eyes from 93 patients, unilateral involvement was observed in 7.5% of patients, while 92.5% had bilateral involvement. These findings closely align with the study by Elbedewy et al. in Egypt, which reported 5.5% unilateral and 94.5% bilateral cases [18]. In contrast, studies in Nepal [19] and Sudan [20] reported significantly higher unilateral rates, at 27.3% and 22.1%, respectively. These variations may be attributed to the different diagnostic technologies utilized or regional genetic and environmental factors influencing disease progression and manifestation. The mean age in our study was 29.6 ± 10.6 years, comparable to results from Mexico (28.14 ± 10.30 years) [1] and Egypt (29.40 ± 9.79 years) [18]. However, studies in Tehran [21], Iraq [16], and Sudan [20] observed lower mean ages, ranging from 21.4 to 24.9 years. Regarding age groups, patients aged 19–30 years (group 2) were the most frequent (34.4%), while those over 40 (group 4) were the least frequent (12.9%). Similar trends were observed in the Tehran study, which applied the same age groups; group 2 was predominant (61.7%), and group 4 was the least frequent (4.8%). Interestingly, the Iraqi study found their youngest group (≤ 18 years) to be the most prevalent [16], suggesting potential regional differences in disease onset, early detection, or healthcare-seeking behavior.

Regarding gender distribution, KC was more frequent in females (55.9%) than in males (44.1%) in our study, consistent with findings from Iraq (females 61.1%, males 38.9%) and Egypt (females 54.9%, males 45.1%) [16, 18]. However, this contrasts with results from studies conducted in Mexico (males: 55.8%), Tehran (males: 62%), and Sudan (males: 55.8%), which reported a male predominance [1, 20, 21]. These conflicting trends highlight the ongoing debate regarding gender as a risk factor for keratoconus, with current evidence suggesting a complex interplay of genetic and hormonal factors that warrant further research [9].

In interpreting our visual acuity outcomes, uncorrected and best-corrected distance visual acuity were available for 149 eyes, while the remaining 24 eyes had vision limited to counting fingers and were therefore considered separately. Among eyes with recordable acuity, the mean uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) was 0.5 ± 0.4 LogMAR [range: 0 to 1.30], improving to a mean best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 0.14 ± 0.19 LogMAR [range: 0 to 1.00]. For the 24 eyes with counting fingers vision, spectacle correction resulted in a mean BCVA of 0.5 ± 0.31 logMAR [range: 0.10 to 1.30], indicating that visual improvement may also be attainable in more severely affected eyes. When compared with other studies, differences in visual acuity outcomes should be interpreted in light of the measurement scales used. In the Sudanese study by Mustafa Abdu et al. [20], visual acuity was reported using decimal notation, with a mean uncorrected visual acuity of 0.19 ± 0.18 [range: 0.01-1.00] and a mean spectacle-corrected visual acuity of 0.43 ± 0.27 [range: 0.01-1.00]. In the Nepalese study [19], the mean uncorrected visual acuity was 0.80 ± 0.41 log MAR [range: 0 -1.78], and the mean BCVA was 0.49 ± 0.35 logMAR [range: 0.30–1.78]. This may reflect a predominance of more advanced cases of keratoconus in these samples compared with early-stage cases in ours. In contrast, the Malaysian cohort [22] had a mean BCVA of 0.75 ± 0.25 in decimal notation, properly indicating a less advanced presentation. These differences can be attributed to both disease severity at presentation and methodological variations across studies concerning the visual acuity scales and charts used.

Refractive error analysis further supports this interpretation. The mean manifest refractive spherical equivalent in our cohort for myopic eyes was − 3.69 ± 3.8 D [range: -0.50 to -20.50], which is less severe than the values reported in Malaysia (-6.19 D) and Sudan (-6.93 D) [20, 22]. The majority of keratoconic eyes in our cohort were myopic, with compound myopic astigmatism being the predominant pattern (57.2%). Among axis orientations, oblique astigmatism was the most frequent subtype (25.4%). Hyperopic astigmatism was observed in only 4% of keratoconic eyes. Comparable regional studies showed similar refractive distributions in patients with keratoconus. In Iraq [16], compound myopic astigmatism was the most frequent pattern (69.7%) with no cases of hyperopic astigmatism recorded. Likewise, the Mexican study [1] showed a marked predominance of compound myopic astigmatism (87.5%), while hyperopia and hyperopic astigmatism were rarely reported. However, both these studies found that with-the-rule astigmatism was the most common axis orientation. This discrepancy in the predominant axis orientation compared with our study may be attributed to differences in cone location, disease-stage distribution, and other population-specific corneal biomechanics.

Concerning disease severity, mild (45.1%) and moderate (43.9%) KC grades were the most prevalent in our analysis. This is consistent with the Iranian study [21], in which stages one (mild) and two (moderate) were more prevalent across age groups, with no significant association between disease severity and age (P = 0.149). The Sudanese study [20] also reported 35.68% of subjects as mild, although they observed a high prevalence of severe to advanced stages (34.59%). Additionally, the Iraqi study [16] found severe forms in 37% of cases, while in India [23], severe grade accounted for 49% of cases, with only 14.4% classified as mild. These differences across populations probably reflect variability in referral patterns, timing of diagnosis, and access to ophthalmic care, with some populations presenting later and therefore exhibiting a higher proportion of severe keratoconus.

Correlation analysis demonstrated a strong, statistically significant positive correlation between Kmax and the absolute value of the manifested refractive spherical equivalent (r = 0.669, P < 0.001), reinforcing the clinical understanding that progressive corneal steepening is accompanied by increasing refractive error. Additionally, a weak negative correlation was observed between the manifest refractive spherical equivalent and the thinnest corneal pachymetry, suggesting that thinner corneas may be associated with more severe refractive errors.

Strengths and limitations

This study is one of the few to systematically characterize keratoconus in a Syrian cohort, using corneal tomography and refractive data, providing insight into the epidemiological and clinical profile of keratoconus. The inclusion of both uncorrected and best corrected visual acuity with spectacle data allowed a comprehensive assessment of visual function and the potential for visual improvement even at advanced stages.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted in a non-ophthalmology specialized hospital with a relatively small sample size, which may limit its generalizability to the broader Syrian population. Second, although our study relied primarily on corneal tomography and refraction data, we believe slit-lamp examinations would have provided additional diagnostic information, especially in the more advanced stages of KC. Additionally, we recognise that using a simplified keratometry-based classification rather than more detailed systems, such as the Amsler-Krumeich or ABCD classification, might be a limitation. However, these comprehensive grading systems are employed in clinical practice at Damascus Hospital for surgical planning, contact lens fitting, and monitoring disease progression. For this cross-sectional study, the simplified keratometry-based classification was deemed suitable and is routinely implemented in busy clinical settings, using central readings from corneal tomography alone. Finally, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to evaluate disease progression and to infer relationships between the disease, visual acuity, and refractive errors.

Future large-scale multicenter studies are warranted to evaluate disease progression and explore potential genetic, environmental, and demographic risk factors specific to the Syrian population.

Conclusion

Our study provides a systematic characterization of the clinical profile of keratoconus in Syrian patients, addressing regional data gaps. Syrian patients typically present with bilateral disease and most commonly have compound myopic astigmatism as the primary refractive error, a pattern consistent with regional and global data. Additionally, mild and moderate keratoconus were the most frequent grades in our cohort, indicating that Syrian patients may be presenting earlier in the disease course and likely reflecting better healthcare-seeking behaviors compared to regional populations. These insights lay the foundation for future research and clinical practice, emphasising the importance of early detection, accurate grading, and tailored visual rehabilitation to enhance patient outcomes and quality of life.
