Analysis of the impact of contemporary subgingival debridement techniques on immunological biomarkers in gingival crevicular fluid
Authors: Safak Necati Donertas, Selcen Ozcan Bulut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimIn non-surgical periodontal therapy, periodontal curettes are used for scaling and root planing, which is considered a standard approach for the management of mild to moderate periodontitis. This prospective clinical study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of two distinct non-surgical periodontal therapy modalities in patients diagnosed with Stage 1 and Stage 2 periodontitis.MethodThe study comprised 80 nonsmoking, systemically healthy volunteers. Patients were divided into two groups at random: the Guided Biofilm Therapy (GBT) group and the conventional therapy group (CT), which included Gracey curette and an ultrasonic device. All patients were assessed before and one and three months after therapy for clinical characteristics like pocket depth (PD), Gingival Index (GI), Plaque Index (PI), Bleeding on Probing Index (BOP), and the levels of IL-1β, IL-10, TNF-α, and MMP-8 in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF).ResultsOver time, both treatment approaches significantly improved all indicators (p < 0.001). At 1 and 3 months, the GBT group showed a more substantial decrease in GI, PI, and IL-1β levels than the standard treatment group (p < 0.05). However, no significant differences were found between the groups in TNF-α, IL-10, and MMP-8 levels.ConclusionThe information gathered shows that guided biofilm therapy is a viable choice, especially for managing plaque and lowering the inflammatory response. These findings emphasize the value of contemporary methods for treating periodontal disease that incorporate patient motivation.Clinical relevanceTo date, no studies have evaluated the impact of current subgingival debridement techniques on immunological biomarkers in gingival crevicular fluid. Our findings suggest that guided biofilm therapy may be more effective in reducing gingival inflammation compared to conventional approaches. Studies involving more participants and longer follow-up durations are necessary to support these conclusions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08748-y.Keywords: Cytokine, Guided biofilm therapy, Periodontal treatment, Periodontitis

Introduction

When biofilm turns into plaque and becomes pathogenic, it causes irritation in the gingiva. Biofilm is formed in the gingival pockets and sticks to the teeth. The main cause o f periodontal disease is dental plaque, and when gingival inflammation spreads to the alveolar bone due to poor oral hygiene, periodontitis can develop [1–3]. Periodontitis, which exhibits a high prevalence worldwide, is defined as a chronic infectious inflammatory disease characterized by phases of active destruction and remission. It originates as inflammation of the gingiva due to the effects of bacterial pathogens, which subsequently extends to the supporting structures of the teeth, leading to the destruction of gingival fibers, resorption of the alveolar bone, and ultimately, tooth loss [2, 4, 5].

The composition of gingival crevicular fluid (GCF), which aids in the diagnosis of periodontal diseases, arises from the interaction between periodontal tissue cells and the bacterial biofilm adhering to the tooth surface. It consists of serum and tissue breakdown products, inflammatory mediators, and biologic active substances produced in response to dental plaque bacteria. An individual’s periodontal health condition and current occurrences in periodontal tissues are reflected in the quantitative biochemical indicator of GCF analysis of particular components. There may be diagnostic and therapeutic implications for qualitative alterations in the makeup of these biochemical indicators in the GCF of patients with periodontitis [6, 7]. MMPs play a major role in destructive periodontal diseases; the most well-known example of the undesired tissue destruction linked to the presence and activity of MMPs at disease sites is the role of collagenases, especially MMP-8, in periodontitis and periimplantitis. MMPs are responsible for the degradation of extracellular matrix proteins during normal tissue turnover, growth, or organogenesis. Although their expression and activity are normally very low in tissues, they become significantly more active in a variety of pathological conditions, including inflammatory diseases, tumor growth, and metastasis [8]. Interleukin 1 (IL-1) and other mediators can frequently be thought of as host cell components. In reaction to bacterial products, immune complexes, or other cytokines, macrophages in inflamed gingival tissues primarily release the inflammatory cytokine IL-1. Through the activation of osteoclasts and the stimulation of prostaglandin E2 synthesis, IL-1β can regulate bone resorption. These results suggest that IL-1β plays a role in the development and course of periodontitis [9]. Pro-inflammatory indicators, such as neutrophil buildup in the gingival crevicular fluid and the subsequent chemotaxis of T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, and plasma cells, are triggered by pro-inflammatory cytokines such interleukin (IL)-1β, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and IL-6. Consequently, they constitute the foundation of periodontitis pathogenesis, the clinical expression of gingivitis, characterized by the degradation of gingival connective tissue collagen or attachment loss resulting from alveolar bone resorption [10].

The foundation of periodontitis treatment is the removal of plaque and calculus accumulated on the tooth and root surfaces. The treatment begins with supragingival instrumentation to control plaque and calculus and reduce gingival inflammation, followed by subgingival instrumentation, which constitutes the second step of non-surgical therapy. Subgingival debridement aims to reduce probing pocket depth, control gingival inflammation, and improve clinical attachment levels. These procedures, defined as scaling and root planing, are considered the gold standard treatment approach for mild to moderate periodontitis [1, 6, 7, 11–13]. Subgingival instrumentation forms the core of therapy in the presence of periodontal pockets. Hand instruments, such as Gracey curettes, play a central role, particularly in deep and narrow pockets and areas with complex root anatomy [12, 14]. Sonic and ultrasonic devices are also commonly used, with no significant difference in outcomes compared to hand instruments; choice depends on operator experience, clinical conditions, and patient factors, with combined use often preferred [12, 13, 15]. Subgingival instrumentation is especially indicated for pockets ≥ 4 mm and may be repeated during surgical therapy if residual deep pockets (≥ 6 mm) persist after non-surgical treatment [13].

Debridement therapies have recently been supplemented with guided biofilm therapy. This technique involves giving the patient a plaque dye and letting them see the biofilm on the tooth—a crucial step in encouraging good oral hygiene. Next, air-flow (an EMS device) and perio-flow (an ultrasonic debridement device, or EMS) are employed. The idea behind this technique is to use water and air to spray an abrasive substance onto the tooth and root surface. The stained biofilm (which becomes visible) is then removed until a clean surface is revealed. The patient is given plaque dye during control sessions. There is evidence of decreased biofilm staining [1, 6, 7, 16, 17]. Although these techniques are commonly employed to treat periodontitis, it is unknown which is more successful. There aren’t many studies in the literature that compare various therapy approaches [1, 6, 7, 16]. Additionally, no research has been done to precisely assess how plaque staining affects the guided biofilm therapy approach. Our study compared the subgingival debridement techniques currently employed in the treatment of patients with Stage 1 and Stage 2 periodontitis in order to fill this gap in the literature.

In order to treat mild to moderate periodontitis, patients got non-surgical periodontal therapies. Our study group comprised these two distinct groups, and we compared two widely utilized current treatments (patient allocation was determined using the coin approach). Written consent was obtained from the patients participating in the study.

Conventional Treatment (CT) Group: Ultrasonic devices and Gracey curettes, which are regularly used in routine practice, were utilized to accomplish subgingival debridement.

Conventional Treatment (CT) Group: Ultrasonic devices and Gracey curettes, which are regularly used in routine practice, were utilized to accomplish subgingival debridement.

Guided Biofilm Therapy (GBT) Group [17]: The GBT protocol (Guided Biofilm Therapy, GBT + Airabrasion) was followed when performing subgingival debridement. The most recent approach is this one. A plaque dye is used to show the patient the biofilm, and perioflow (the ultrasonic device in the EMS device) and air abrasion are used to remove the biofilm. We used information from the patient’s examinations during the first and third month follow-ups, as well as before and after the initial scaling session (supra- and subgingival debridement).

All patients were given the same oral hygiene instructions and were asked to brush their teeth twice a day. All participants received standardized oral hygiene motivation, with the Modified Bass technique demonstrated on a model. They were instructed to immediately practice the brushing technique demonstrated during the training in their own mouths. To standardize oral hygiene equipment, all participants were provided with the same soft-bristled manual toothbrush (Professional Gum Care Soft Bristle, Oral-B, China) and a fluoride-containing standard toothpaste (Colgate Total, Colgate, China). Participants were asked not to use mouthwash or any additional oral hygiene aids during the study period.

Periodontal clinical and radiological examination data

All clinical evaluations were performed by a single calibrated specialist (SND) who was blinded to the group allocation, using a Williams probe to assess the parameters listed below. Non-surgical periodontal therapy was carried out by another specialist (SOB); however, the examiner responsible for the clinical measurements remained blinded throughout the study.

Gingival Index (GI) [8].

All teeth are evaluated on 4 surfaces (distal, buccal, mesial, lingual) and the average is calculated.

b. Plaque Index (PI) [8].

Plaque Index (PI) [8].

All teeth are evaluated on 4 surfaces (distal, buccal, mesial, lingual) and the average is calculated.

0 = No plaque in the pocket. The probe surface is completely clean.

1 = No plaque visible on the tooth surface; but when the probe is moved over the tooth surface, plaque builds up on the probe surface as a thin layer that resembles paint.

Bleeding on Probing (BOP) [8].

Periodontal Pocket Depth (PD)

Gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) evaluation

A single specialist physician (SND) took GCF measurements at baseline, one month, and three months from the tooth with the deepest pocket identified at baseline before treatment in patients with periodontitis. In short, the region was isolated using cotton pads, and the plaque was removed using cotton pellets. After that, 20 cm of mild air drying was applied. Strips of absorbent paper (Periopaper®, Proflow Inc., Amityville, NY, USA) were used to obtain GCF samples. The strips were positioned for 30 s after being carefully inserted into the gingival sulcus/periodontal pocket until a small amount of resistance was felt. Strips tainted with blood were thrown away. Before being used for laboratory testing, each subject’s strips were gathered, combined, and kept at -80 °C (Ultra Low-Temperature Freezer, New Brunswick Scientific).

As directed by the manufacturer, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were used to measure the levels of IL-1β, TNF- α, IL-10, and MMP-8 in GCF patients.

At baseline, the first month, and the third month, all data—aside from demographic data—were assessed.

Statistical analysis

Prior to the start of the study, a power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to determine the required sample size. The analysis indicated that 40 participants per group would be sufficient to detect a medium effect size with 80% power at a 5% significance level.

The software IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26 was used to analyze the data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was utilized to assess adherence to the normal distribution. Frequency, percentage, median, mean, and standard deviation are the ways that descriptive statistics are displayed. GI, PI, BOP, and pocket depth scores, together with those for two distinct treatment approaches, were assessed, and variations over time within and between groups were looked at. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare treatment groups, and the Friedman test was used to examine changes over time within each treatment group. At p < 0.05, all results were deemed statistically significant.

Discussion

At present, mild to moderate periodontitis can be effectively treated with nonsurgical periodontal therapies. Eliminating dental plaque, the main contributing cause, is the aim of these therapies. Traditionally, scaling root planing, curettes, and/or ultrasonic portable subgingival debridement are used to remove dental plaque and calculus. One current treatment option is guided biofilm treatment, which uses air-abrasion (EMS) equipment and ultrasonic portable devices to clean teeth and roots, and employs plaque staining to visualize the biofilm [17]. There is not much research that compare the two methods, and there aren’t any that use GCF to compare cytokine parameters. We examined two nonsurgical periodontal therapies that are presently in use using both clinical and GCF factors. At follow-up, both treatment approaches showed statistically significant improvements in clinical and GCF indicators. At three months, the GBT method produced the lowest mean GI and PI levels as well as the lowest IL-1 levels.

There is limited research in the literature that compare the two current methods. In their study comparing GBT with SRP clinically in 31 patients with stage 3 and stage 4 periodontitis, Mensi et al. found that both therapy groups significantly improved periodontal clinical indicators [18]. Additionally, at the post-treatment follow-up, they did not find any differences in PD, BOP, or PI across the treatment groups. In their trial comparing GBT with conventional treatment, Cyris et al. found that at follow-up, the BOP in the GBT group was 14.3%, while in the control group, it was 15.9% (p = 0.037) [19]. The GI index was not examined in either study [19]. Similar to previous research, our study demonstrated significant differences in GI and PI at the 3-month follow-up, but no variations in the clinical parameters PD and BOP. With the exception of PI, no changes were noted between baseline and the 3-month follow-up.

Toxins from microorganisms are known to cause connective tissue epithelial cells to release a variety of inflammatory mediators, especially TNF-α and IL-1β, in cases of infection and inflammation [20]. Moreover, these mediators have the ability to penetrate the gingival crevicular fluid and the connective tissue epithelium. Vascular alterations and the migration of immune system cells, including neutrophils, to the periodontium are caused by cytokines necessary for the inflammatory response [21]. The main mediators of periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease, are TNF-α and IL-1, which can lead to bone loss and tissue destruction in periodontal diseases [22, 23]. In a 2024 study by Raza et al., patients previously diagnosed with chronic periodontitis were predominantly reclassified as Stage II–III, mostly Grade B, while those with aggressive periodontitis were almost entirely reclassified as Stage III–IV, Grade C [24]. Ertugrul et al. reported that the highest levels of IL-1 and TNF-α were observed in generalized aggressive periodontitis (110.23+-9.20 pg/30s, 1284.46+/-86.32 pg/30s), chronic periodontitis (423.65+-35.24 pg/30s, 82.64+-9.12 pg/30s), gingivitis (52.10+-7.15 pg/30s, 824.244+-44.68 pg/30s), and periodontally healthy (36.44+-8.86 pg/30s, 628.26+-34.61 pg/30s) in their GCF study comparing periodontal health, gingivitis, chronic periodontitis, and generalized aggressive periodontitis [25]. This study demonstrates that severe inflammation and bone destruction are associated with much higher levels of TNF-α and IL-1β [25]. Our study comprised patients with mild to moderate periodontitis. After baseline, IL-1β levels were 143.99 ± 72.91 pg/mL in the conventional treatment group and 151.55 ± 39.75 pg/mL in the GBT group; after three months after treatment, they were 121.75 ± 54.88 pg and 86.67 ± 21.04 pg, respectively (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that periodontal healing was successful for both treatment groups. GBT had the lowest IL-1β level and also exhibited the lowest GI level. (p < 0.05) As another proinflammatory mediator, TNF-α was investigated in our study; like IL-1, it showed a decline from baseline to three months.

Tsang et al. conducted a split-mouth design study in 27 chronic periodontitis patients in the only study in the literature comparing GBT and conventional treatment using IL-1β and IL-1ra markers in GCF [26]. No differences were found between the groups in terms of IL-1 and IL-1ra [26]. In our study, IL-10 was used as an anti-inflammatory mediator. Although the literature is still unclear regarding IL-10 levels following periodontal treatment, interleukin-10 (IL-10) is a powerful anti-inflammatory cytokine, and recent research has demonstrated that IL-10 may help preserve bone structure by suppressing osteoclastic bone resorption and controlling osteoblastic bone formation [27]. According to Bostanci et al., both before and after periodontal therapy, patients with chronic periodontitis (CP) had higher GCF IL-10 levels than healthy controls [28]. According to the scientists, this rise in IL-10 may help CP sufferers avoid inflammation [29]. Although there was no discernible difference between the treatment groups in our study, both treatment groups experienced a comparable rise in GCF IL-10 levels following treatment.

MMPs are known to rise in the presence of inflammatory diseases and induce tissue damage, despite having extremely low expression and activity in healthy tissues. Among the MMPs, MMP-8 is particularly important in inflammatory diseases chracterized by progressive bone loss, such as peri-implantitis and periodontitis. The literature indicated a maximum value of 3997 ± 3126 ng/mL and a minimum concentration of 2.00 ± 1.60 ng/mL [30]. The baseline readings in our study were 48.2 ± 7.4 ng/ml and 49.3 ± 7.2 ng/ml. The degree of periodontal disease and destruction, as well as laboratory and research techniques, may be factors in the variations amongst studies. The literature generally agrees that GCF MMP-8 levels will decrease with periodontal treatment. As anticipated, the periodontal treatment in our study resulted in a decrease in GCF MMP-8 levels; nevertheless, there was no discernible difference between the two groups.

In our study, when the changes in periodontal and GCF parameters were assessed between the 0–3 month findings, a significant difference was identified only in PI. Although both approaches had comparable effects on inflammation, GBT was better in terms of PI alterations, according to our study and the literature. The observed discrepancy between PI measurements may be attributed to the influence of plaque disclosing agents on patient motivation for oral hygiene.

Limitations

Medication, systemic conditions, and patient hygiene habits are just a few of the variables that can affect inflammatory markers. Patients’ self-reports were used to determine the presence of systemic disease; additionally, the use of antibiotics or anti-inflammatory drugs in the last three months was based on these reports. The use of antibiotics and anti-inflammatory drugs within the last month may influence the results. Although standart oral hygiene motivation was provided to patients within the study, it remains unclear whether their oral hygiene practices were standardized. In addition, the absence of clinical attachment level measurements represents a limitation of this study, as it does not allow a comprehensive evaluation of periodontal attachment changes and healing patterns.

More patients could result in more significant findings, and adding blood measures would give the study even more importance.

Conclusion

Clinical and biochemical data were used to assess the effectiveness of GBT and conventional treatment in individuals with Stage 1 and Stage 2 periodontitis. With notable improvements in important clinical indicators such as the Gingival Index, Plaque Index, Bleeding on Probing Index, and pocket depth, both treatment approaches were found to be successful in enhancing periodontal health.

The significant reduction in IL-1β levels, together with the decrease in plaque index observed at the first- and third-month follow up visits in the guided biofilm therapy group, suggests that this approach may confer superior efficacy in mitigating inflammation and enhance patient motivation for oral hygiene, potentially attributable to the adjunctive use of plaque disclosing agents, which may facilitate more effective biofilm removal and greater patient engagement.

Other biochemical markers, however, did not show any statistically significant variations between the groups. In conclusion, guided biofilm therapy can be utilized as an alternative to traditional procedures and provides an important contribution to periodontal treatment, especially with its patient education and motivational features. Larger sample sizes and longer-term follow-up are needed to validate these findings.
