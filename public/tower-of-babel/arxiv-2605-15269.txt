Kerroll black holes
Florian Ecker, Daniel Grumiller, Lucas Hörl, Mattéo Leturcq–Daligaux, and Alfredo Pérez

Abstract
We construct rotating black holes in Carroll gravity using two distinct approaches. In one of them, we exploit the freedom in the Carroll compatible connection to encode rotation. In particular, we construct rotating solutions in magnetic Carroll gravity by dressing the Carroll–Schwarzschild black hole with a rotational charge. This solution is intrinsically Carrollian and has no Lorentzian analog. In the other approach, we construct an extension of magnetic Carroll gravity from general relativity in an odd-power expansion in the speed of light. This theory contains magnetic Carroll gravity as a subsector but has in general more physical degrees of freedom. We show that this theory admits a Carroll analog of the Kerr black hole as a solution, which we refer to as the “Kerroll black hole”. Its rotation appears as an intrinsically odd-power effect in the Carroll data. We compute the corresponding conserved charges for both theories.

Rotating geometries in magnetic Carroll gravity

Magnetic Carroll gravity admits massive solutions

In this Section, we reconsider this issue and point out a loophole in the above no-go result. In general, for theories of gravity rotation may be described by the presence of boundary charges that form a representation of some kinematical Lie algebra (see, e.g.,

3.4

3.5.1
Geodesics

Since rotational charges affect only the connection in the bulk it becomes natural to ask about the behavior of geodesics. Consider a curve

\displaystyle=\frac{F}{1-\frac{r_{\mathrm{s}}}{r}}+\frac{4J\ell}{9\pi^{2}r^{2}(r-r_{\mathrm{s}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{r}^{2}}{2}+V_{\textrm{\tiny eff}}(r)

\rho^{ij}
to zero. The same is true for the associated momentum constraints. However, note that the “electric” coupling constant
G_{\mathrm{E}}
features in (

), not the magnetic counterpart
G_{\mathrm{M}}
.
The full set of constraints featuring in
I_{\mathrm{exM}}
closes under Poisson brackets and realizes an algebra reminiscent of the Carrollian surface deformation algebra.
In particular, the NNLO Hamiltonian constraint commutes with itself

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(2)}(x),\,\mathcal{H}^{(2)}(y)\}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(79a)

For the remaining Poisson brackets one obtains

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(2)}(x),\,\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{i}(y)\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{(2)}(y)\,\partial_{i}\delta(x-y)

(79b)

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{i}(x),\,\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{j}(y)\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{i}(y)\,\partial_{j}\delta(x-y)+\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{j}(x)\,\partial_{i}\delta(x-y)

(79c)

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{i}(x),\,\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{j}(y)\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{i}(y)\,\partial_{j}\delta(x-y)+\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{j}(x)\,\partial_{i}\delta(x-y)

(79d)

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(2)}(x),\,\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{i}(y)\}

\displaystyle=0

(79e)

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{i}(x),\,\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{j}(y)\}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(79f)

This reduces to the magnetic Carroll algebra in the limit of vanishing odd-power fields
49
;
24
;
64
. By means of their closure under the Poisson bracket the constraints
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{(2)}
may be thought of as generating spatial diffeomorphisms, as expected from the discussion around (
71
).
The equations of motion derived from
I_{\mathrm{exM}}
,

\displaystyle\dot{h}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{N_{(0)}}h_{ij}

(80a)

\displaystyle\dot{m}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{32\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}}{\sqrt{h}}\,N_{(0)}\big(\rho_{ij}-\frac{1}{d-1}h_{ij}\rho\big)+\mathcal{L}_{N_{(1)}}h_{ij}+\mathcal{L}_{N_{(0)}}m_{ij}

(80b)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}^{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{N_{(0)}}\rho^{ij}

(80c)

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}^{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{h}}{16\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}}\Big[-N_{(0)}\Big(R^{ij}-\frac{1}{2}h^{ij}R\Big)+\nabla^{i}\nabla^{j}N_{(0)}-h^{ij}\nabla^{2}N_{(0)}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{16\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}N_{(0)}}{\sqrt{h}}\Big[2\rho^{i}{}_{k}\rho^{jk}-\frac{2}{d-1}\rho\,\rho^{ij}-\frac{1}{2}h^{ij}\Big(\rho^{kl}\rho_{kl}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{d-1}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{L}_{N_{(1)}}\rho^{ij}+\mathcal{L}_{N_{(0)}}\sigma^{ij}

(80d)

are of first-order in time derivatives by construction. Here
\rho^{ij}
and
\sigma^{ij}
are again tensor densities of weight
+1
and we defined
\rho=h^{ij}\rho_{ij}
.

The first equation implements the constraint that the leading order extrinsic curvature vanishes,
K_{ij}=0
, which survives this extension of magnetic Carroll gravity. The remaining equations govern the dynamics of the additional odd-power fields
(m_{ij},\rho^{ij},\sigma^{ij})
. The degrees of freedom of extended magnetic Carroll gravity can now be counted as follows. The canonical variables consist of two symmetric tensors
h_{ij}
and
m_{ij}
and their conjugate momenta
\sigma^{ij}
and
\rho^{ij}
, giving
2D(D-1)
phase space variables, where
D=d+1
denotes the number of spacetime dimensions. The full set of constraints comprises one scalar constraint
\mathcal{H}^{(2)}
and two sets of
d
momentum constraints
\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{i}
and
\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{i}
, i.e., a total of
2D-1
first-class constraints, each removing two degrees of freedom in the phase space. Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{d.o.f.}=\frac{1}{2}\big(2D(D-1)-2(2D-1)\big)=D(D-3)+1.

(81)

Evaluating this for
D=3,\,4,\,5
spacetime dimensions yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{d.o.f.}\big|_{D=3}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\mathrm{d.o.f.}\big|_{D=4}

\displaystyle=5

\displaystyle\mathrm{d.o.f.}\big|_{D=5}

\displaystyle=11\,.

(82)

As expected, the introduction of the additional field
m_{ij}
and its momentum
\rho^{ij}
increases the number of propagating modes compared to magnetic Carroll gravity. In
D=4
the number of degrees of freedom curiously coincides with the number of massive graviton polarizations. In
D=3
the theory acquires a single local degree of freedom.

4.2
Kerroll black hole

We now show that a
c\to 0
expansion of the Kerr black hole leads to a solution of extended magnetic Carroll gravity, which we refer to as the
Kerroll black hole
. Let us start with the Kerr metric (
2
) in ADM variables,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-N^{2}c^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{2}+g_{ij}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{i}+N^{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t)(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{j}+N^{j}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t)~.

(83)

We work in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates
(t,r,\theta,\varphi)
and again use the definitions
\Delta=r^{2}-r_{\mathrm{s}}r+a^{2}
and
\Sigma=r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta
where
r_{\mathrm{s}}=2G_{\mathrm{N}}M/c^{2}
. The spatial metric
g_{ij}
is given by

\displaystyle g_{ij}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{i}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x^{j}=\frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\Sigma\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\bigg(r^{2}+a^{2}+\frac{r_{\mathrm{s}}ra^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}\bigg)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi^{2}

(84)

while lapse and shift are

\displaystyle N=\sqrt{\frac{\Sigma\Delta}{(r^{2}+a^{2})\Sigma+r_{\mathrm{s}}ra^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}\qquad\qquad N^{i}=-\frac{c\,r_{\mathrm{s}}ra}{(r^{2}+a^{2})\Sigma+r_{\mathrm{s}}ra^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\,\delta^{i}_{\varphi}\,.

(85)

Finally, the ADM equations of motion determine the canonical momentum
\pi_{ij}
. It may be expressed in terms of the extrinsic curvature
\mathcal{K}_{ij}
as
\pi_{ij}=\frac{\sqrt{g}}{16\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}}(\mathcal{K}_{ij}-g_{ij}\mathcal{K})
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2N}\big(\dot{g}_{ij}-\nabla_{i}N_{j}-\nabla_{j}N_{i}\big)\,.

(86)

Consistently with the expansion set up in (
57
), we hold the electric combination
G_{\mathrm{E}}=G_{\mathrm{N}}c^{-2}
fixed such that
r_{\mathrm{s}}=2G_{\mathrm{E}}M
. The parameters
M
and
a
are assumed to not scale with
c
.
Using the expansions of the various fields (
58
) we then find

\displaystyle h_{ij}

\displaystyle=g_{ij}

\displaystyle m_{ij}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle l_{ij}

\displaystyle=0

(87a)

\displaystyle\Pi^{ij}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\rho^{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{h}}{16\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}}\,\big(S^{ij}-h^{ij}S\big)

\displaystyle\sigma^{ij}

\displaystyle=0

(87b)

\displaystyle N_{(0)}

\displaystyle=N

\displaystyle N_{(1)}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle N_{(2)}

\displaystyle=0

(87c)

\displaystyle N^{i}_{(0)}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle N^{i}_{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{r_{\mathrm{s}}ra}{(r^{2}+a^{2})\Sigma+r_{\mathrm{s}}ra^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\delta^{i}_{\varphi}

\displaystyle N^{i}_{(2)}

\displaystyle=0

(87d)

where
S_{ij}
is a shorthand for the NLO term in the expansion of
\mathcal{K}_{ij}=K_{ij}+cS_{ij}+\mathcal{O}(c^{2})
,

\displaystyle S_{ij}=-\frac{1}{2N_{(0)}}\,\big(\nabla_{i}N_{(1)j}+\nabla_{j}N_{(1)i}\big)~.

(88)

We therefore obtain the Carroll metric data directly from the expansion of the relativistic Kerr black hole metric data. When expanding lapse and shift, only the leading order lapse contributes, whereas the shift is again of order
c
. Crucially, the leading order canonical momentum and hence the leading order extrinsic curvature vanish with nontrivial contributions only of
\mathcal{O}(c)
. This ensures compatibility with the constraint
\Pi^{ij}=0
imposed to define extended magnetic Carroll gravity. One can show that all the constraints as well as the evolution equations of the theory (
77
) are satisfied. The fields (
87
) thus define a two-parameter family of solutions to extended magnetic Carroll gravity.

In the context of Carroll geometry it is often convenient to work with the second order PUL parametrization as a starting point for the Carroll expansion
47
. The corresponding PUL vector field can then be expressed in terms of the lapse and shift as

\displaystyle V^{\mu}=\frac{1}{N}\,\big(-1,N^{i}\big)\,.

(89)

From Eq. (
87
) one concludes that expanding
V^{\mu}
of the Kerr black hole in powers of
c
not only yields the Carroll vector field
v
, but also a non-zero spatial subleading field
u^{i}=N^{i}_{(1)}/N_{(0)}
, stemming from the expanded term linear in
c
. This field encodes the rotational features of the Kerroll black hole as an odd-power effect in
c
.

The metric formalism also results in higher order contributions to the PUL spatial metric
\Pi_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}+c^{2}T_{\mu}T_{\nu}
, where, after fixing the Carroll clock one-form
T_{\mu}=\tau_{\mu}
, one similarly obtains non-vanishing subleading fields
m_{\mu\nu}
and
l_{\mu\nu}
in the expansion
\Pi_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}+c\,m_{\mu\nu}+c^{2}\,l_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})
, which is also evident from Eq. (
83
). See Appendix
C
for more details on the odd-power expansion in the second order formalism.

Representing the Kerroll black hole in the Carrollian ADM-like variables is more natural than in second order language, since one does not obtain multiple contributions in different orders of
c
from the same relativistic parent field, as can be seen in Eq. (
87
). The significance of including odd-powers in the expansion explains the difficulties in constructing the Carrollian analog of rotating Lorentzian black holes in standard magnetic Carroll gravity
32
;
52
, which does not include contributions from odd-powers in
c
.

The Carroll extremal surface

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{r_{\mathrm{s}}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{r_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}{4}-a^{2}}

(90)

coincides with the event horizon of the relativistic Kerr black hole. This is analogous to the non-rotating case
32
. We define a Carroll extremal surface like in the Introduction, as a locus where the Carroll vector field
v
diverges, which corresponds to solving
\Delta=0
here. For a more precise definition in 2d, see
32
.

4.3
Boundary charges

One can determine the physical charges of the Kerroll black hole by examining which boundary terms are required to make the canonical generators associated with bulk symmetries functionally differentiable. Although we do not analyze the full space of asymptotic symmetries here, we can restrict attention to a minimal set that is certainly contained within it — namely, the vector fields
\partial_{t}
and
-\partial_{\varphi}
.

Let us start by considering the canonical generator of a diffeomorphism along
\partial_{\varphi}
. From the discussion around Eq. (
71
) we know that diffeomorphisms in this theory should be generated by
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{(2)}
, so we investigate whether
-\partial_{\varphi}
is associated to a non-vanishing boundary charge of this generator. Indeed, keeping all boundary terms we may write the variation of
D^{(2)}[\xi]
as

\displaystyle\delta D^{(2)}[\xi]

\displaystyle=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\xi^{i}\delta\mathcal{H}_{i}^{(2)}=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\big[(...)\delta h_{ij}+(...)\delta\rho^{ij}+\partial_{i}B^{i}\big]

(91)

where we used that
\delta m_{ij}=0
and
\delta\sigma^{ij}=0
for the family of solutions we are looking at. The total derivative term reads

\displaystyle B^{i}=-2\xi^{(j}\sigma^{k)i}\delta h_{jk}-2\xi^{(j}m_{jk}\delta\rho^{k)i}+\xi^{i}\sigma^{jk}\delta h_{jk}

(92)

and we know that a non-zero boundary charge only exists if
B^{i}
is non-zero for the considered field configuration and transformation. However, since
\sigma^{ij}=0=m_{ij}
for the Kerroll black hole, the diffeomorphism charge vanishes for any
\xi^{i}
.

We may instead look at a similar quantity for the generator of subleading diffeomorphisms
D^{(1)}[\xi]
to find

\displaystyle\delta D^{(1)}[\xi]

\displaystyle=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\xi^{i}\delta\mathcal{H}_{i}^{(1)}=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\big[(...)\delta h_{ij}+(...)\delta\rho^{ij}+\partial_{i}B^{i}\big]

(93)

with

\displaystyle B^{i}=-2\xi^{(j}\rho^{k)i}\delta h_{jk}-2\xi_{j}\delta\rho^{ji}+\xi^{i}\rho^{jk}\delta h_{jk}\,.

(94)

In this case, we obtain a non-vanishing boundary contribution and to render the generator functionally differentiable we have to cancel it by adding a boundary term
\delta Q^{(1)}_{\xi}
to
\delta D^{(1)}[\xi]
. On-shell this boundary term is just given by

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(1)}_{\xi}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\oint\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S_{i}\,\big(2\xi^{(j}\rho^{k)i}\delta h_{jk}+2\xi_{j}\delta\rho^{ji}-\xi^{i}\rho^{jk}\delta h_{jk}\big)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\delta Q^{(1)}_{-\partial_{\varphi}}=\frac{\delta(r_{\mathrm{s}}a)}{2G_{\mathrm{E}}}

(95)

We therefore find a non-zero charge,
Q_{-\partial_{\varphi}}=Ma=:J^{(1)}
associated to
-\partial_{\varphi}
which sits in the subleading diffeomorphisms. We interpret this as another way of seeing that taking the strict Carroll limit of the Kerr metric cannot reproduce angular momentum. Instead, angular momentum is a subleading effect: Starting from the relativistic angular momentum
J
in the Kerr metric (
2
) one equivalently obtains
J^{(1)}
by extracting the
\mathcal{O}(c)
-coefficient,
J=aE/c=J^{(1)}c
.

Finally, the boundary charge associated to the Killing vector of time translations
\partial_{t}
may be computed by considering the generator associated to the scalar constraint

\displaystyle\delta G[\xi=\partial_{t}]=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,N_{(0)}\delta\mathcal{H}^{(2)}=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\big[(...)\delta h_{ij}+(...)\delta\rho^{ij}+\partial_{i}B^{i}\big]

(96)

where we used that the canonical transformation parameters related with
\mathcal{H}^{(2)}
and
\mathcal{H}_{i}^{(2)}
associated to a given diffeomorphism generated by
\xi^{\mu}
are given by
N_{(0)}\xi^{t}
and
\xi^{i}+N_{(0)}^{i}\xi^{t}
, respectively. The boundary term then reads

\displaystyle B^{i}=-\frac{\sqrt{h}}{16\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}}\Big(2N_{(0)}h^{j[i}\nabla^{k]}\delta h_{jk}-2\nabla^{[i}N_{(0)}h^{j]k}\delta h_{jk}\Big)

(97)

which implies the boundary charge

\displaystyle\delta Q_{\partial_{t}}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{16\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}}\oint\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S_{i}\sqrt{h}\Big(2N_{(0)}h^{j[i}\nabla^{k]}\delta h_{jk}-2\nabla^{[i}N_{(0)}h^{j]k}\delta h_{jk}\Big)=\frac{\delta r_{\mathrm{s}}}{2G_{\mathrm{E}}}

(98)

confirming the expected relation between the mass parameter and energy,
Q_{\partial_{t}}=M
. Here we used that
r_{\mathrm{s}}=2G_{\mathrm{E}}M
. From the perspective of the relativistic parent theory this charge appears at
\mathcal{O}(c^{2})
, i.e. the relativistic energy expands as
E=E^{(2)}c^{2}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})
. With
E^{(2)}=M
this is consistent with keeping the mass parameter fixed.

5
Conclusion and Outlook

In this article, we have presented two complementary constructions of rotating black holes in Carrollian gravity. The first resides in magnetic Carroll gravity, where we have shown that the Carrollian connection can be used to dress Carroll–Schwarzschild black holes with a rotational charge. This construction exploits genuinely Carrollian features of the theory and has no direct analog in the relativistic setting. The second construction is based on an odd-power expansion in the speed of light of general relativity, which allows us to identify a Carrollian analog of the Kerr black hole, the Kerroll black hole. In this case, rotation arises from subleading contributions in odd powers of the speed of light.

We finish with a few open points that, we believe, are interesting for further investigations.

Given the definition of Carroll black holes
32
there is a vital aspect that we did not address so far: The thermodynamic properties of these solutions. Indeed, in both cases it is not obvious whether a first law may be derived from first principles. For the rotating Carroll–Schwarzschild solution derived in Section
3.4
we found boundary charges for energy and angular momentum but for establishing a first law relation an independent derivation of the temperature of these geometries is needed. In analogy to the two-dimensional case one may attempt this by demanding the absence of conical singularities in a Wick rotated geometry, i.e., impose
t\sim t+\beta
and compute the four-dimensional Carroll analog of the Euler character (see
34
for a definition of quadratic curvature invariants). Analogously to the Euclidean case
54
;
55
demanding
\chi=\chi(D^{2})\times\chi(S^{2})=2
should yield a relation between
\beta
and the solution parameters
r_{\mathrm{s}}
and
J
.

As far as the thermodynamics of the Kerroll black hole of Subsection
4.2
is concerned, the first law has to follow from expanding the Lorentzian counterpart: Since we are extracting the theory at
\mathcal{O}(c^{2})
in an expansion around the electric limit we also expect the thermodynamic quantities to obey a relation consistent with that order. In other words, at
\mathcal{O}(c^{2})
we should have

\displaystyle\delta E^{(2)}=(T\delta S)^{(2)}+\Omega^{(1)}\delta J^{(1)}

(99)

where
E^{(2)}=M
and
J^{(1)}=aM
are the boundary charges associated to time translations and rotations (see Subsection
4.3
). The expansions of
S
,
T
and
\Omega
are not fixed a priory but we may at least check for consistency with the expansions of the Lorentzian quantities associated to the Kerr family. This yields

\displaystyle S=c^{2}S^{(2)}=c^{2}\Big(\frac{A_{\mathrm{c}}}{4G_{\mathrm{E}}\hbar c}\Big),

\displaystyle T=T^{(0)}=\frac{\hbar c}{2\pi}\frac{r_{\mathrm{c}}-r_{-}}{2(r_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+a^{2})},

(100)

\displaystyle\Omega=c\Omega^{(1)}=c\frac{a}{r_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+a^{2}},

\displaystyle J=cJ^{(1)}=caM

(101)

where
A_{\mathrm{c}}=4\pi(r_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+a^{2})
is the area of the Carroll extremal surface at
r=r_{\mathrm{c}}
and
r_{-}=r_{\mathrm{s}}/2-\sqrt{(r_{\mathrm{s}}/2)^{2}-a^{2}}
. Additionally, we assumed here that the combination
\hbar c
is held constant, similarly to the tantum gravity limit
31
. While these expansions are indeed consistent with (
99
) it would be interesting to see whether the expressions for
S^{(2)}
,
T^{(0)}
and
\Omega^{(1)}
admit an independent derivation within extended magnetic Carroll gravity.

Interestingly, there is a tantum gravity-like
31
multi scaling limit of Kerr black hole thermodynamics that leads to finite energy
E=Mc^{2}
, temperature
T
, entropy
S
, horizon radii
r_{\pm}
, Kerr parameter
a
, and (rescaled) horizon velocity
\Omega
/angular momentum
J
,

G_{\mathrm{N}}\to G_{\mathrm{M}}\,c^{4}\qquad\qquad\hbar\to\frac{\hbar}{c}\qquad\qquad\Omega\to\frac{\Omega}{c}\qquad\qquad J\to J\,c\qquad\qquad c\to 0\,.

(102)

On the level of the metric this limit corresponds to the second option discussed in the Introduction after (
2
). It could be rewarding to find a Carroll theory where the scaling above appears naturally. Given that the scaling (
102
) keeps fixed the magnetic Carroll coupling
G_{\mathrm{M}}=G_{\mathrm{N}}/c^{4}
rather than the electric Carroll coupling
G_{\mathrm{E}}=G_{\mathrm{N}}/c^{2}
, the extended magnetic Carroll gravity theory of Section
4
is not a candidate for such a theory. Neither is magnetic Carroll gravity because of the constraint violation
R[g_{ij}]\neq 0
.

In general, it would also be worthwhile to study Carroll extremal surfaces
32
in higher spacetime dimensions
D>2
in more detail, particularly in the context of rotating black holes and extended magnetic Carroll gravity. Additionally, it would be gratifying to investigate whether there exists an extension or modification of general relativity for which extended magnetic Carroll gravity arises as a direct
c\to 0
limit.

Finally, we have only considered the rotating Carroll–Schwarzschild black hole and the Carroll analog of the Kerr black hole in this article. It could be rewarding to study the possibly rich class of rotating solutions in magnetic Carroll gravity and to construct other Carroll analogs of Lorentzian black holes such as higher dimensional black holes, for instance Myers–Perry black holes
60
in extended magnetic Carroll gravity.

\displaystyle\{\rho^{ij},\,D^{(1)}[\xi]\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Pi^{ij}

\displaystyle\{\sigma^{ij},\,D^{(1)}[\xi]\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\rho^{ij}

(115d)

\displaystyle\{h_{ij},\,D^{(2)}[\xi]\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}h_{ij}

\displaystyle\{m_{ij},\,D^{(2)}[\xi]\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}m_{ij}

\displaystyle\{l_{ij},\,D^{(2)}[\xi]\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}l_{ij}

(115e)

\displaystyle\{\Pi^{ij},\,D^{(2)}[\xi]\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\Pi^{ij}

\displaystyle\{\rho^{ij},\,D^{(2)}[\xi]\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\rho^{ij}

\displaystyle\{\sigma^{ij},\,D^{(2)}[\xi]\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}\sigma^{ij}\,.

(115f)

We can directly obtain the Poisson brackets of extended magnetic Carroll gravity by setting
\Pi^{ij}=0
, which essentially means that we can omit all appearances of
l_{ij}
and
\Pi^{ij}
in the Poisson brackets. The diffeomorphism generators change accordingly.

Appendix C
Odd-power Carroll gravity expansion in second-order formalism

Here, we briefly sketch how to perform an odd-power expansion of general relativity along the lines of
47
. In order to generalize the Carroll expansion of Einstein gravity to include odd powers in
c
, we start from the established PUL decomposition

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mu\nu}-c^{2}T_{\mu}T_{\nu}

\displaystyle g^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\Pi^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}V^{\mu}V^{\nu}

(116)

with

\displaystyle V^{\mu}T_{\mu}

\displaystyle=-1

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}V^{\nu}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}T_{\nu}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=-V^{\mu}T_{\nu}+\Pi^{\mu\rho}\Pi_{\rho\nu}\,.

(117)

In the context of PUL variables
\Pi^{\mu\nu}
denotes the (inverse) spatial metric, not the leading order canonical momentum, which has spatial indices
\Pi^{ij}
.
We then consider a more general expansion than in
47
, including subleading contributions in odd powers of
c

\displaystyle T_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\tau_{\mu}+ct_{\mu}+c^{2}s_{\mu}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=h_{\mu\nu}+cm_{\mu\nu}+c^{2}l_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})

(118)

\displaystyle V^{\mu}

\displaystyle=v^{\mu}+cu^{\mu}+c^{2}w^{\mu}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=h^{\mu\nu}+cm^{\mu\nu}+c^{2}l^{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})\,.

(119)

By setting the odd-power fields to zero, we recover the even-power expansion considered in
47
. The kinematic relations between these fields are fixed by expanding (
117
)
order by order, which yields constraints for the subleading fields, similarly to those for Carroll geometry

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}v^{\mu}

\displaystyle=-1

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle h^{\mu\nu}\tau_{\nu}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=-v^{\mu}\tau_{\nu}+h^{\mu\rho}h_{\rho\nu}\,.

(120)

Including subleading odd-power contribution necessitates an expansion of the extrinsic curvature beyond the leading order quantity
K_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{v}h_{\mu\nu}
as follows

\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{V}\Pi_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{v+cu+c^{2}w+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})}\left(h_{\mu\nu}+cm_{\mu\nu}+c^{2}l_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})\right)\\
=K_{\mu\nu}+cS_{\mu\nu}+c^{2}L_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})

(121)

From
V^{\mu}\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}=0
we obtain

\displaystyle v^{\mu}K_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle v^{\mu}S_{\mu\nu}+u^{\mu}K_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle w^{\mu}K_{\mu\nu}+u^{\mu}S_{\mu\nu}+v^{\mu}L_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(122)

In general
v^{\mu}S_{\mu\nu}\neq 0
and
v^{\mu}L_{\mu\nu}\neq 0
, which means that we cannot use
h^{\mu\nu}
as a metric-like object to move the indices of
S_{\mu\nu}
and
L_{\mu\nu}
. Instead, it is more convenient to separately expand

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{K}_{\rho\sigma}\Pi^{\rho\mu}\Pi^{\sigma\nu}=K^{\mu\nu}+cS^{\mu\nu}+c^{2}L^{\mu\nu}\,.

(123)

Moreover, we write
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}\Pi^{\mu\nu}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=K+cS+c^{2}L\,.

(124)

Let us now turn to the odd-power expansion of the Einstein–Hilbert action in PUL variables

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{EH}}=\frac{c^{2}}{16\pi G}\int_{M}\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d+1}xE\,\big[\big(\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}-\mathcal{K}^{2}\big)+c^{2}\Pi^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{R}_{\mu\nu}+\frac{c^{4}}{4}\Pi^{\mu\nu}\Pi^{\rho\sigma}\left(dT\right)_{\mu\rho}\left(dT\right)_{\nu\sigma}\big]

(125)

where
E=\sqrt{\det\left(T_{\mu}T_{\nu}+\Pi_{\mu\nu}\right)}
denotes the PUL vielbein determinant. We note that in a systematic expansion in
c
, the equations of motion of the lower order action is always encoded in the higher order action
46
. Writing

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{EH}}=I_{0}+cI_{1}+c^{2}I_{2}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})

(126)

we find

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{LO}}=\frac{1}{16\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d+1}x\mathfrak{e}\,\big(K^{\mu\nu}K_{\mu\nu}-K^{2}\big)

(127a)

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{NLO}}=\frac{1}{16\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d+1}x\mathfrak{e}\,\Big(S_{\mu\nu}K^{\mu\nu}+S^{\mu\nu}K_{\mu\nu}-2KS+\frac{\mathfrak{e}^{(1)}}{\mathfrak{e}}\big(K^{\mu\nu}K_{\mu\nu}-K^{2}\big)\Big)

(127b)

I_{\mathrm{NNLO}}=\frac{1}{16\pi G_{\mathrm{E}}}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{d+1}x\mathfrak{e}\,\Big(h^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}+S^{\mu\nu}S_{\mu\nu}+L_{\mu\nu}K^{\mu\nu}+L^{\mu\nu}K_{\mu\nu}-S^{2}-2KL\\
+\frac{\mathfrak{e}^{(1)}}{\mathfrak{e}}\left(S^{\mu\nu}K_{\mu\nu}+S_{\mu\nu}K^{\mu\nu}-2KS\right)+\frac{\mathfrak{e}^{(2)}}{\mathfrak{e}}\big(K^{\mu\nu}K_{\mu\nu}-K^{2}\big)\Big)

(127c)

where
\mathfrak{e}^{(1)}
and
\mathfrak{e}^{(2)}
stem from the expansion of the vielbein determinant
E=\mathfrak{e}+c\mathfrak{e}^{(1)}+c^{2}\mathfrak{e}^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})
. We observe that the leading order action again reproduces electric Carroll gravity, whereas the odd-power NLO term is new and by construction does not feature in the even power expansion at all. The NNLO term picks up additional contributions from the odd power fields compared to
47
, which are crucial to realize the rotating Kerroll black hole solution.
