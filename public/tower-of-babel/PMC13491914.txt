Congenital bronchobiliary fistula in a newborn: a case report
Authors: Yujuan Wang, Ping Zha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital bronchobiliary fistula (CBBF) is a developmental structural abnormality between the trachea or bronchus and the biliary tree, with non-specific clinical manifestations that mainly present as repeated cough and dyspnea, but it is often misdiagnosed as pneumonia. If not promptly diagnosed and effectively treated, CBBF can be fatal. Currently, radical surgery is the only treatment option for CBBF.Case presentationAn 8-day-old full-term Han Chinese female infant presented with coughing accompanied by sputum and shortness of breath with worsening dyspnea for 4 days. Yellowish green sputum was seen during invasive ventilation. A follow-up chest radiograph suggested a solid lesion in the right upper lung. Airway malformations and an abnormal opening in the middle of the tracheal eminence were observed by fiberoptic bronchoscopy. Chest computed tomography (CT) and tracheal imaging showed a fistula below the tracheal bulla traveling towards the liver and communicating with Glisson's capsule at the hepatic hilum, suggesting the presence of a tracheobiliary fistula. Intraoperatively, a fistula was seen extending from the tracheal eminence to the esophageal hiatus, and trans-fistula imaging showed that the contrast agent entered the intestines through the biliary tree, confirming the diagnosis of a CBBF, which was removed. The pathology was consistent with a bronchial fistula. The ventilator was withdrawn on postoperative day 10. Following discharge, the patient recovered well with good growth and development, and no symptoms of lung or digestive tract discomfort.ConclusionCBBF is frequently misdiagnosed. Bronchoscopy remains the preferred diagnostic modality for definitive confirmation.

Introduction

Congenital bronchobiliary fistula (CBBF) is a developmental structural abnormality between the trachea or bronchus and the biliary tree, with non-specific clinical manifestations that mainly present as repeated cough and dyspnea. A systematic review of 23 neonatal CBBF cases found that 14 (60.9%) were initially misdiagnosed, with 11 (78.6%) of these misdiagnoses being pneumonia. However, anti-infection agents are not effective for treatment of CBBF and death may occur if prompt diagnosis and effective treatment are delayed. To date, only three cases of neonatal CBBF have been reported in China [1–3]. In this report, the clinical data of a newborn with CBBF treated in our hospital are summarized and relevant literature was reviewed to improve clinical diagnosis and treatment of CBBF.

Case presentation

A newborn Han Chinese female aged 8 days (primigravida/para 1; 392 weeks; spontaneous labor; birth weight, 2800 g; Apgar score, 9–10) was transferred to our institution with progressive respiratory distress. Both parents were healthy with no family history of congenital malformations or genetic disease. The mother had no complications during pregnancy, such as gestational diabetes, hypertension, or infection. Prenatal ultrasonography showed no abnormalities. Following exposure to a respiratory pathogen on postnatal day 4, she developed perioral cyanosis, cough, and tachypnea that eventually required invasive mechanical ventilation. Despite treatment with antibiotics, oxygen supplementation, and airway management at a local hospital, there was no symptom improvement.

An examination upon admission to our hospital revealed tachypnea (62 bpm), intercostal retractions, diminished right upper lobe breath sounds, and bilateral basal crackles. Chest radiography demonstrated consolidation of the right upper lobe of the lung. Despite antibiotic therapy and mechanical ventilation, respiratory distress progressed with new bilious endotracheal secretions. Persistent respiratory distress and unclear pulmonary lesions detected by chest radiography prompted further bronchoscopic evaluation, which identified an abnormal orifice at the tracheal carina that discharged bile-stained fluid, while electron fiberoscopy showed marked hyperemia of the tracheal mucosa with attached bile-like fluid (Fig. 1). Biochemical analysis confirmed bilirubin-positive secretions. These findings raised suspicion of a CBBF. Subsequent diagnostic evaluations confirmed these findings. Chest computed tomography (CT) delineated a fistulous tract connecting the tracheal carina to Glisson's capsule (Fig. 2), corroborating the bronchoscopic findings. No bile-like sputum was found postoperatively in the tracheal cannula under mechanical ventilation. So, mechanical ventilation was withdrawn on postadmission day 16 and low-flow oxygen inhalation was initiated. After withdrawal, symptoms of milk choking, hoarseness, and cough were present. Chest and tracheal CT on postadmission day 20 showed no reopening of the fistula after airway reconstruction. During the course of treatment, cough symptoms were accompanied by difficulty in oxygen delivery upon withdrawal of oral feeding. On postadmission day 26, fiberoptic bronchoscopy revealed type 1 (mild) laryngomalacia. Aerosol sputum suction, vitamin A and D supplementation, and nasal feeding were administered.

Oxygen supplementation was discontinued and oral feeding was successfully established at 1 month postoperatively. A follow-up examination conducted 3 months after surgery found no recurrence of respiratory symptoms or abnormalities in the digestive system, while growth and development were normal. Neurodevelopmental follow-ups using the Gesell Developmental Scale (GDS) at 6 and 12 months revealed no abnormalities. Discussion

Clinical characteristics of neonatal CBBF

CBBF is a rare condition characterized by abnormal passages between the trachea or bronchus and the biliary tree. In 1850, Peacock first described CBBF as a hydatid cyst of the liver similar to pulmonary hydatid disease. Neuhauser et al. first reported this disease in 1952 [4]. CBBF occurs less frequently than acquired BBF, accounting for fewer than 6% of all BBF cases [27]. To definitively characterize the clinical presentation of this condition, the present study aggregated data from 23 neonates diagnosed with congenital tracheobiliary fistulas between 1850 and 2024 (Table 2). Among the 23 neonates, females predominated (69.6%, 16/23) over males (30.4%, 7/23), with a mean age at diagnosis of 12.6 ± 9 days. The most common location for fistula openings was the tracheal carina (47.8%, 11/23). Preoperative examinations primarily relied on bronchoscopy alone or in combination with CT (combined 60.87%, 14/23). In these cases, a high proportion (56.5%, 13/23) exhibited concomitant organ developmental anomalies, with a marked tendency toward biliary tract abnormalities, the most prevalent type, accounting for 84.61% (11/13) of all associated anomalies. Mortality of CBBF is mainly associated with severe concurrent congenital or perinatal abnormalities. Among the 23 cases summarized in Table 2, 3 patients died (13%), all of whom had severe combined malformations or severe perinatal pulmonary complications. Of these 3 patients, one had a tracheoesophageal fistula with esophageal atresia, while another had meconium aspiration syndrome; both died of postoperative sepsis. The third case had common bile duct agenesis and died of pneumothorax due to poor healing of the bronchial anastomosis. In contrast, the present case had no severe associated malformations and recovered smoothly. Notably, many previously reported CBBF patients with mild or surgically manageable malformations also achieved favorable outcomes. These observations indicate that severe, multisystem or critical structural coexisting malformations, or severe perinatal pulmonary complications, are the main causes of mortality associated with neonatal CBBF. These malformations increase the risk of death by elevating surgical complexity, causing superimposed organ injury, and inducing fatal complications, such as refractory sepsis and anastomotic failure. In contrast, CBBF cases without malformations or with mild/repairable associated malformations can have an excellent prognosis with early diagnosis, targeted surgery, and standardized perioperative care. This emphasizes that preoperative evaluation should focus not only on the presence of coexisting malformations, but also on the severity and scope (especially of the biliary tract, airway and digestive tract) to guide risk stratification and surgical planning to ultimately reduce mortality. Clinical manifestations of neonatal CBBF include repeated cough, progressive dyspnea, bile-like sputum, aspiration pneumonia, atelectasis or emphysema, and growth retardation. Most reported cases of neonatal CBBF present with coughing or vomiting of bile-like fluid, with development of progressive dyspnea. The severity of symptoms depends on whether bile drains normally through the duodenum or only through fistulas [7]. Less obstruction of abdominal bile drainage is correlated to fewer obvious clinical symptoms. Among patients with no obvious symptoms, CBBF might not be detected until adulthood, thus CBBF is less reported in adolescents and adults [5]. Delayed onset and diagnosis are the main reasons for later detection in adulthood. CBBF should be differentially diagnosed from an esophagotracheal fistula, esophageal atresia, and gastrointestinal obstruction [8]. CBBF should be considered when a large amount of bile-like secretion is observed in the trachea. Detection of bilirubin and bile acid in tracheal secretions can assist in diagnosis.

Pathogenesis of congenital bronchobiliary fistula

Embryological developmental abnormalities of the bronchobiliary system. Human embryological development reveals that the digestive and respiratory systems originate from a common source within the endodermal foregut. Between embryonic weeks 4 and 6, the ventral endoderm of the foregut differentiates into respiratory pouches (lung buds) and hepatic pouches. The former develop into the trachea and lungs, while the latter form the liver and biliary tract system [28, 29]. CBBF is an abnormal anatomical structure formed between the bronchial tree and the intrahepatic or extrahepatic bile ducts during embryonic development. However, the pathophysiological mechanism remains unclear. Two main hypotheses regarding the pathogenesis of CBBF have been proposed: (1) duplication of the upper digestive tract and (2) confluence of an abnormal bronchial bud and abnormal biliary tree, with pseudostratified ciliated epithelium and cartilage surrounded by mucous glands found in pathological specimens [5], consistent with the pathological results of the present case. However, these traditional hypotheses merely describe the pathogenesis from a morphological perspective without elucidating the regulatory mechanisms at the molecular level. Moreover, the genetic basis of CBBF remains largely unexplored. Genetic testing of the patient in this case revealed no abnormalities.

The Shh/GLI signaling pathway hypothesis for CBBF pathogenesis. As shown in Table 2, patients with CBBF exhibit the same clinical phenotype as patients with tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF)—a condition characterized by the formation of an abnormal fistula between the trachea and esophagus—which is a classic example of foregut developmental abnormalities. Drawing on previous studies of the molecular mechanisms underlying foregut malformations, including tracheoesophageal fistula and esophageal atresia, we can reasonably infer the potential pathogenesis of congenital CBBF. The differentiation and morphogenesis of the foregut are precisely regulated by a series of conserved cellular signaling pathways. The highly conserved Hedgehog gene family is closely associated with human embryonic development and tissue remodeling, serving as a crucial signaling pathway in embryonic development and cell differentiation, where the core functions include regulation of cell fate determination, organogenesis, and tissue morphogenesis. The encoded proteins have been demonstrated to be indispensable morphogenetic factors during embryonic development and adult tissue remodeling. Sonic hedgehog (Shh) is the central member of the Hedgehog gene family and plays a vital role in foregut development [30]. Animal model experiments have demonstrated that embryos with Shh gene deletion (Shh-/) exhibit foregut malformations as early as gestational day 9.5 (the onset of tracheal diverticulum development) [31]. Additionally, studies have revealed that the GLi family—key factors in the Shh pathway—are highly expressed in biliary atresia, consistent with the abnormal bile duct development observed in CBBF patients [30]. Based on these findings, it is hypothesized that abnormal Shh signaling may promote abnormal connections between the trachea and biliary tree in CBBF. This could lead to a loss of “spatial specificity” during differentiation of precursor cells in the respiratory and hepatobiliary systems, thereby causing erroneous connections between the tracheal and biliary epithelium, leading to fistula formation. This mechanism aligns with the pathological features of CBBF and may represent a potential underlying pathogenesis. Although biologically plausible, this hypothesis remains theoretical. Future advancements in molecular biology and genetic diagnostic technologies may elucidate the underlying mechanisms of this disorder.

Pathophysiological changes induced by CBBF formation. Pathophysiologically, bile cannot flow smoothly into the duodenum owing to physiological obstruction of the distal biliary tree, causing compression and bile reflux, resulting in congenital dysplasia of the biliary tree. The bile flowing out of the biliary tree irritates the diaphragm, triggering inflammation, irritation, and tissue necrosis. This irritation then erodes the bronchi and creates a passage between the lungs and liver. A differential pressure is created by positive pressure in the biliary tree and negative pressure in the bronchi, perpetuating the fistula [6].

Diagnostic strategies for neonatal CBBF

As shown in Table 2, bronchoscopy was performed for diagnosis of > 60% of patients. As an essential tool for the diagnosis of CBBF, bronchoscopy is employed to not only assess the severity of pulmonary lesions but also allows direct visualization of the fistula location and bile-stained secretions, thus facilitating early determination of surgical intervention plans. This technique is also widely applied for the diagnosis of congenital pulmonary and lower airway developmental anomalies in children [32]. However, this strategy poses risks for severe cases, especially patients receiving invasive ventilation and those with high ventilator parameters. As a non-invasive, convenient, and highly feasible imaging method, chest CT and tracheal imaging can display the origin, pathway, and termination of the fistula, thereby providing critical information for surgical planning and is recommended in combination with bronchoscopy. Hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scanning with the tracer technetium-99m not only displays radioactive distribution in the trachea, bronchi, and gallbladder, but also effectively visualizes the liver and distal biliary tree to evaluate the patency of distal bile drainage and predict the probability of abnormal liver function [9]. For cases with suspected biliary tract abnormalities (such as jaundice or impaired bile drainage), additional HIDA or MRCP studies are recommended to comprehensively evaluate complications. Hepatobiliary ultrasonography to visualize the liver or gallbladder pneumatosis shadow also has a certain diagnostic value. In the present case, liver ultrasonography revealed portal vein pneumatosis.

Surgical management of neonatal CBBF

Radical thoracotomy, thoracoscopy, or thoracoabdominal surgery is the main approach for treatment of CBBF. As a potentially safer choice, traditional thoracotomy can be applied to resect the fistula and close the trachea and biliary tree to address the problem of bile drainage, although administration of anesthesia, fistula dissection, and identification of biliary tree obstruction can be challenging.

Among the retrieved reports, 12 cases underwent exploratory surgery through the right thorax. With this approach, an incision to the 4th–6th intercostal space is recommended, as thoracotomy through the 6th right intercostal space can better expose the operative field [10]. The fistula is completely dissected from below and through the diaphragmatic hiatus, while avoiding separating or ligating only the fistula and preventing postoperative reopening of the fistula [11]. The presence or absence of biliary tree obstruction was not explored in 50% (4/8) of cases requiring a second operation (Table 2). One case had a patent biliary tree, as determined by intraoperative exploration, but obstructive jaundice still occurred postoperatively, which was likely due to intraoperative passive dilation of the biliary tree caused by the contrast agent, which adversely affected intraoperative judgment. One case had no defined abdominal fistula. Therefore, the risk of a second operation was effectively reduced by identifying the presence or absence of biliary tree dysplasia pre- or intraoperatively, while evaluating the path of the fistula with complete dissection. Surgical drainage of the biliary tree depends on the status of the hepatobiliary ducts. In case of unobstructed drainage of the common and intrahepatic biliary tree, the fistula must be resected and ligated. For cases with obstruction of the biliary tree, inhibiting drainage, hepatectomy, and biliary tree drainage are required, including Roux-en-Y fistula-intestinal or fistula-gallbladder anastomosis [8].

Perioperative care and neurodevelopmental follow-up of the present case

In the present case, bile-like liquid was intermittently seen in the tracheal cannula, which progressively worsened following treatment. Bronchoscopy showed that a large amount of bile-like liquid was intermittently released from the fistula opening. In clinical practice, therefore, it is necessary to consider to the possibility of CBBF for children with refractory pneumonia accompanied by bile-like secretions, and priority should be given to detection of bilirubin in secretions as a preliminary screening examination. At the onset of CBBF, active mechanical ventilation, airway management, and anti-infection effects were not effective, and the ventilator parameters were high accompanied by significant carbon dioxide retention (63.3–106.4 mmHg). Conventional and high frequency ventilation modes were adopted but not ideal to improve dyspnea and hypercapnia, which were likely due to ventilation dysfunction resulting from pulmonary chemical inflammation caused by bile reflux. Ventilation–perfusion mismatch can also lead to neonatal hypoventilation, which is characterized by a decrease in the total tidal volume, resulting in reduced ventilation and hypoxemia. The presence of a fistula allows oxygen to flow directly into the biliary tree under mechanical ventilation, resulting in further decreases in effective ventilation and hypoxemia. Although hypercapnia can stimulate cerebral vasodilation and increase cerebral perfusion, neonatal cerebrovascular autoregulation is limited and hypoxemia can further cause cerebral hypoxia. To address this situation, cerebral oxygen saturation can be monitored to evaluate cerebral blood flow perfusion, while monitoring blood oxygen pre- and intraoperatively. Research suggests that a baseline cerebral perfusion value of 25% or an area under the curve exceeding 250 minutes in cerebral oxygen saturation monitoring has clinical value to predict the risk of neurological complications [12], which also provides a scientific basis for the balance of cerebral oxygen supply and demand in such children under hypoxic conditions. In the present case, ventilator parameters gradually decreased on postoperative day 3 and blood gas analysis showed that hypercapnia was improved during the follow-up. After withdrawal of the ventilator, milk choking occurred. Subsequent re-examination by fiberoptic bronchoscopy showed laryngomalacia and normal vocal cord mobility, excluding intraoperative recurrent laryngeal nerve injury. Aerosol sputum suction, nasal feeding, oral feeding training, and other treatments were given. One month postoperatively, oxygen supply was withdrawn, choking symptoms were relieved, and oral feeding was established. As compared to previous reports, this case had two advantages: (1) early multimodal diagnosis was completed upon admission, thereby shortening the waiting time for surgery. The focus was on evaluating biliary tract development to avoid the risk of secondary surgery; and (2) postoperative neurological development follow-up was conducted, a point rarely reported in the literature. The GDS is a validated, widely adopted tool to evaluate the neurodevelopment of infants aged 0–3 years based on five core developmental domains: adaptive, gross motor, fine motor, language, and personal-social. According to standardized norms, a GDS score ≥ 85 is defined as age-appropriate neurodevelopment of newborns. The GDS scores of our patient at both 6 and 12 months post-surgery were within the normal threshold (≥ 85) for all domains, confirming the absence of neurodevelopmental delay. This further underscores the critical importance of early diagnosis and targeted perioperative monitoring for favorable outcomes.

Conclusion

Neonatal congenital tracheobiliary fistula is frequently misdiagnosed. Bronchoscopy remains the preferred diagnostic modality for definitive confirmation.
