Adenylate kinase 5 autoimmune encephalitis following craniopharyngioma radiotherapy
Authors: Moyuan Quan, Fuyun Ren, Tengfei Sun, Xiaojing Chang, Liping Chen, Liang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnti-AK5 encephalitis is a rare, non-paraneoplastic autoimmune disorder, typically characterized by anterograde amnesia as a core clinical manifestation. Although seizures are infrequent in the early disease phase, some patients may develop seizures as the condition progresses.Case presentationWe report a rare case of anti–adenylate kinase 5 (AK5) autoimmune encephalitis following surgical resection and radiotherapy for craniopharyngioma. Background

AK5 encephalitis is a rare subtype of autoimmune encephalitis (AE), typically exhibiting a non-paraneoplastic phenotype. It predominantly affects elderly males and is characterized primarily by episodic anterograde amnesia [1]. Weight loss, fatigue, and anorexia are widely recognized as highly specific clinical manifestations of this entity [1]. Although seizures are infrequent in the early disease phase, some patients may develop seizures as the condition progresses [1], which are associated with an unfavorable prognosis [2]. Cytotoxic T cells are the primary mediators of neuronal damage and loss in anti-AK5 encephalitis, and they play a central role in the underlying immune-mediated pathogenic mechanisms of the disease [2]. Bilateral temporal lobe hyperintensities are almost uniformly detected at disease onset, and these lesions nearly invariably progress to significant atrophy on follow-up magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans [1]. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis may reveal mild to moderate lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal or slightly elevated protein concentrations, and positive detection of disease-specific antibodies [3].

Case presentations

A 52-year-old male was admitted with a six-year history of personality changes and progressive cognitive decline, along with one day of new-onset seizures. His past medical history included craniopharyngioma (Fig. 1A), with craniotomy and tumor resection performed in February 2019. Postoperatively, he developed right-sided vision loss and hypopituitarism, requiring long-term hormone replacement therapy (levothyroxine and prednisone). A follow-up cranial MRI approximately six weeks after surgery (April 2019) showed persistent abnormal signal in the sellar region (Fig. 1B), prompting adjuvant cranial radiotherapy. From April 15 to May 25, 2019, he received fractionated intracranial radiotherapy (Fig. 1C total dose 54 Gy in 30 fractions, the cumulative radiation dose to the hippocampi averaged 25 Gy over the 30 fractions of radiotherapy, while the dose to the bilateral temporal lobes was less than 15 Gy). A post-radiotherapy scan (May 2019) showed normal hippocampal signal and morphology (Fig. 1D). About 1–2 weeks after radiotherapy, he exhibited progressive neuropsychiatric symptoms, including irritability, logorrhea, memory impairment, and psychomotor slowing. Over the following years, he gradually developed social withdrawal, which was not medically evaluated.

In March 2025, the patient was hospitalized following a generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Neurological examination on admission revealed deficits in higher cortical functions, mainly characterized by slowed responses and impaired recent memory; no other significant neurological abnormalities were noted. Routine blood tests were unremarkable. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed normal opening pressure and white blood cell count (2 × 10⁶/L; reference: 0–8 × 10⁶/L). CSF chloride (125.8 mmol/L; reference: 120–130 mmol/L) and glucose (4.29 mmol/L; reference: 2.5–4.5 mmol/L) were within normal ranges, while CSF protein was mildly elevated (0.66 g/L; reference: 0.15–0.45 g/L). Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of CSF detected no pathogens. Autoimmune encephalitis antibodies were tested simultaneously in both serum and cerebrospinal fluid specimens, Serum and CSF samples were both subjected to cell-based assay (CBA) and tissue-based assay (TBA) (Supplementary Material 1). The titer of anti-AK5 antibody was 1:100 in serum and 1:1 in cerebrospinal fluid.

Electroencephalography (EEG) revealed seizure onset characterized by low-amplitude fast activity originating from the right anterior-midtemporal region and sphenoidal electrodes, or alternatively from the right frontopolar region. This evolved into medium-to-high amplitude slow waves over the right frontotemporal area, spreading to the right hemisphere as theta slow waves, and ultimately progressing to theta and delta slow waves in the right anterior-midtemporal, sphenoidal, frontopolar, and frontal regions. Cranial MRI during hospitalization demonstrated bilateral hippocampal atrophy, abnormal signal intensity in the right hippocampus (Fig. 1E), with mild linear enhancement in the same region (Fig. 1F).

Hospital course: The patient received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG; 0.4 g/kg/day, five days) combined with intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP; 500 mg/day for 3 days, then 250 mg/day for 3 days, followed by 125 mg/day for 3 days).

Discharge regimen: Oral methylprednisolone (60 mg/day) and mycophenolate mofetil (500 mg twice daily) were prescribed, with a planned glucocorticoid taper of 8 mg per week.

Antiepileptic management: The patient experienced two generalized tonic-clonic seizures only on the day before admission, with no recurrence thereafter. Antiepileptic therapy was transitioned from intravenous valproate sodium to oral extended-release valproate sodium (0.5 g twice daily) plus levetiracetam (0.5 g twice daily). However, follow-up complete blood count at discharge indicated thrombocytopenia, leading to regimen adjustment to extended-release valproate sodium (0.5 g twice daily), levetiracetam (0.5 g twice daily), and lacosamide (50 mg twice daily). Lacosamide was scheduled to increase by 50 mg weekly to a target of 100 mg twice daily; upon reaching this target, valproate sodium was to be reduced by 0.25 g every 3 days.

On admission, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score was 24 and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was 26. Clinical features included euphoria, inappropriate familiarity with strangers, logorrhea, pressured speech, compulsive shopping urges, slowed responses, and impaired recent memory. At the one-month follow-up, MMSE and MoCA scores were both 30. Euphoria and logorrhea had resolved, and the patient could perform daily shopping tasks appropriately. Meanwhile, the titer of anti-AK5 antibody in the repeated serum specimen decreased to 1:10. The patient underwent follow-up brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at one month (April 2025) and five months (August 2025) following the diagnosis of AK5 autoimmune encephalitis and the completion of standard therapy. The MRI from April 2025 showed bilateral hippocampal atrophy and a reduced abnormal signal in the right hippocampus relative to the pretreatment scan (Fig. 1H). No significant contrast enhancement was noted in the hippocampus (Fig. 1I). The subsequent MRI in August 2025 revealed progressive bilateral hippocampal atrophy and a further reduction in the extent of the right hippocampal signal abnormality (Fig. 1J). At the three-month follow-up, the family reported that the patient’s cognition, logical thinking, memory, and language had returned to his pre-radiotherapy (2019) baseline.

Discussion

In the present case, the patient did not initially present with the typical severe amnestic syndrome. Instead, the earliest manifestations were subtle and insidious personality alterations, including irritability and logorrhea, and the diagnosis was only confirmed subsequent to the development of overt epileptic seizures. Previous studies have demonstrated that subacute anterograde amnesia is not the sole clinical presentation of AK5 encephalitis [3]; the disease may also manifest with neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression, delusions, and hallucinations, which typically have an insidious and progressive onset. In our case, these insidious symptoms emerged shortly after radiotherapy, which might represent the early clinical phase of autoimmune encephalitis (AE). However, the lack of timely specialist evaluation and follow-up until the onset of seizures in 2025 resulted in a prolonged diagnostic delay.

A critical diagnostic challenge raised by the clinical course is differentiating between postictal MRI changes and primary autoimmune inflammation. Radiographic abnormalities in the hippocampus can be transiently induced by seizure activity. However, several features in this patient strongly support a primary inflammatory etiology: the presence of mild linear enhancement on the initial MRI, the high titer of anti-AK5 antibodies (1:100 in serum), and the progressive nature of the bilateral hippocampal atrophy observed during follow-up despite the absence of recurring seizures. While postictal edema might have contributed to the initial signal intensity in March 2025, the overall clinical-radiological evolution aligns more closely with a sustained AK5-mediated autoimmune response.

Furthermore, the patient’s complex neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as euphoria and pressured speech, likely resulted from the combined impact of the underlying inflammatory process and potential seizures. Although generalized seizures were controlled with antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), these behavioral abnormalities only fully resolved following the initiation of intensive immunotherapy. This sequence of recovery underscores that addressing the autoimmune trigger is as essential as seizure control for restoring the patient’s cognitive and behavioral baseline.

In contrast, limbic encephalitis associated with other well-characterized antibodies is often subacute in onset (developing over a period of days to weeks), and patients typically seek medical attention due to short-term memory impairment, epileptic seizures, and neuropsychiatric abnormalities (e.g., hallucinations, irritability, depression). Additionally, each antibody subtype may be associated with distinct clinical phenotypes: for instance, anti-LGI1 antibody encephalitis may occasionally present with faciobrachial dystonic seizures and hyponatremia [4]; anti-NMDAR antibody encephalitis is characterized by severe neuropsychiatric disorders, movement disorders, and autonomic instability [5]; and anti-GABABR antibody encephalitis may be complicated by intractable epileptic seizures [6].

This case is reported herein based on our hypothesis regarding a potential association between AK5 autoimmune encephalitis and cranial radiotherapy. Before proposing the hypothesis, we first clarify that radiation encephalopathy is defined by a definite history of radiotherapy, typically developing months to years after irradiation in a chronic or delayed manner. The lesion location is highly consistent with the radiation field, and clinical manifestations may include focal neurological deficits, cognitive decline, and epilepsy. On neuroimaging, lesions align with the irradiated area and may present with characteristic features such as a cheese-like, soap bubble-like appearance, or the cut green pepper sign [7]. The CSF usually shows no inflammatory changes. However, this case does not exhibit the aforementioned characteristics.

We elaborate on the potential association between these two conditions from the following perspectives: the patient underwent surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy for the treatment of craniopharyngioma, which resulted in a certain degree of blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption—an underlying factor that predisposes to immune response activation and infection [8]. Furthermore, the AK5 antigen is an intracellular antigen, and AK5 antibodies may not be directly pathogenic but rather byproducts secondary to an immune response. Radiotherapy is highly likely to be the trigger for the imbalance of the intracranial immune inflammatory response [9] in this patient. Additionally, literature has demonstrated that the incidence of pathogen-mediated infectious encephalitis following radiotherapy, such as herpes simplex virus (HSV) and cytomegalovirus encephalitis [10–12], is elevated. Although the patient presented no evidence of central nervous system (CNS) infection from post-radiotherapy in May 2019 until the onset of epilepsy in March 2025, the possibility of an occult viral or other pathogen-mediated CNS infection during this interval cannot be excluded. It is plausible that the autoimmune encephalitis represents a infection-associated immune imbalance. The above represents the potential mechanisms we proposed by integrating relevant literature and the clinical data of this case.

Finally, we acknowledge certain limitations. Due to the lack of documented clinical follow-up between 2019 and 2025, the exact temporal evolution of the autoimmune process remains speculative. Additionally, while MMSE and MoCA scores demonstrated significant improvement, these screening tools provide only a limited evaluation of cognitive domains. A more comprehensive neuropsychological battery would have been valuable to characterize the patient’s long-term recovery more precisely. Nonetheless, this first reported case of AK5 encephalitis following radiotherapy highlights the need for clinical vigilance when neuropsychiatric symptoms emerge after intracranial radiation.
