What can, and cannot, be known about the avian-type arch?
Authors: Tuna Aras, Julia Khabyuk, Martin Scaal, Payman Majd
Published: 2026

We thank Professor Takagi for his generous and stimulating commentary and for distilling, in his title, the question that our case report1 also provoked: what can we know about the avian-type aortic arch? His estimated summary prevalence of 0.022% is a welcome refinement, and we share his suggestion that the elevated inner-curvature wall shear stress implicated in bovine-arch dissection2 may also prove relevant to this variant as well.

When we first encountered this case, we searched the literature using purely morphologic terms: a bicarotid trunk and a common subclavian trunk. This search led us to the term “avian arch.” However, the variant is not anatomically identical to the avian arch. Birds typically possess paired brachiocephalic trunks, each dividing into carotid and subclavian arteries, whereas the human “avian-type” variant consists of a bicarotid trunk and a separate common subclavian trunk. The established term is therefore useful but, strictly speaking, a partial misnomer.

Comparative anatomy places this observation within a broader vertebrate framework. Among aquatic vertebrates, multiple aortic arches persist and remain functional for gill-based oxygenation. In amphibians, metamorphosis is supported by a three-chambered heart, retained fourth-arch systemic arches, and a third-arch bicarotid trunk; selective shunting redirects blood between pulmonary and systemic circuits during the aquatic-to-terrestrial transition.3 Crocodilians are unique intermediates: despite a fully septated four-chambered heart, they retain both systemic aortic arches, which are connected by the foramen of Panizza.4 This arrangement permits selective pulmonary bypass during diving or digestion, supporting prolonged apnea while maintaining systemic perfusion.5 In birds, flight-related aerobic demands favor a single systemic outflow from the right fourth aortic arch. This forms paired brachiocephalic trunks, each dividing into carotid and subclavian arteries to supply the brain and wings in parallel, minimizing turbulence, ensuring balanced perfusion, and supporting the high metabolic and aerodynamic demands of flight.6

This matters because the variant raises a broader question: are such rare arch configurations merely coincidental developmental variants, or do they represent evolutionary echoes of a conserved vascular blueprint? Haeckel’s recapitulation theory is no longer accepted in its strict form; human embryos do not literally pass through ancestral adult forms. Nevertheless, shared embryologic mechanisms are real. Comparative developmental studies show that reptiles, birds, and mammals begin from related pharyngeal arch artery architecture but remodel it differently, through differential persistence and regression of arch arteries, neural crest contribution, second heart field-derived flow dividers, apoptosis, and hemodynamic influences.4 In this limited sense, rare human aortic arch variants may reveal the latent plasticity of a deeply conserved vertebrate pharyngeal arch artery scaffold.

The current number of reported cases and imaging studies is far too small to allow any conclusions regarding natural history, geographic distribution, or anthropological or evolutionary significance. This configuration may ultimately prove to be a coincidental developmental variant without specific evolutionary or clinical implications beyond its procedural relevance.

At present, we believe that what remains unknown about the avian-type arch still exceeds what can currently be inferred. What can be stated, however, is that this rare configuration is clinically relevant, anatomically fascinating, and a powerful stimulus for the curiosity that drives scientific inquiry.
