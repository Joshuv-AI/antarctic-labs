 Analysis of Natural Gas Pipeline Link from
 San Juan to Palo Seco
Background
The Department of Energy Office of Electricity tasked Argonne National Laboratory with
evaluating fuel supply options for power plants in San Juan and Palo Seco, Puerto Rico.

The analysis provides results that determine economics, emissions, and safety comparisons
between a base case scenario with the current fuel mix and two additional scenarios, where Fuel
Oil 2 is replaced with liquefied natural gas (LNG) and all the required fuel is replaced with
natural gas delivered by pipeline from San Juan’s port to Palo Seco. The pipeline link is expected
to be 3.75 miles.

Argonne National Laboratory (Argonne) estimated criteria pollutant emissions and CO₂
emissions for each of the scenarios using technology- and fuel-specific emissions factors from
EPA, unless provided by Genera PR. Emissions associated with fuel transport were also
included.

Using information obtained from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), Argonne
evaluated the economics based on price curves for LNG delivered to San Juan, delivered cost of
diesel and fuel oil to Palo Seco, and $250 per truck to deliver LNG from San Juan’s port to Palo
Seco.

Safety comparisons were calculated using both pipeline incident data and truck transportation
incident data as reported by the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration
(PHMSA).

Figures 1 and 2 show the proximity of the LNG terminal to the Palo Seco and the Central San
Juan power plants. Central San Juan has four main operating units – two steam turbines running
on fuel oil and two combined-cycle units that can run on either diesel fuel or natural gas. Palo
Seco has two main operating steam turbine units (totaling 602 MW capacity) running on
pipeline-supplied heavy fuel oil. The proposed 20-inch, 3.9-mile natural gas pipeline from the
LNG terminal would run overland. Both San Juan and Palo Seco also have several small-
capacity, FEMA-provided emergency generators. In total, the plants have a generating capacity
of 1,433 MW.

Scenarios
Argonne considered three scenarios for this analysis:

 1. Baseline: Powering the existing generating units at the Central San Juan and Palo Seco
 sites with today’s fuel supply (fuel oil 6 and 2 and natural gas).
 2. Scenario 1: Converting to natural gas all units that can do so. Central San Juan would be
 supplied directly from the port; Palo Seco would be supplied with truck-transported LNG
 except for the large steam-turbine units, which consume too much fuel for truck
 shipments. They would remain dependent on fuel oil.
 3. Scenario 2: Converting remainder units and powering all units with natural gas. Palo
 Seco, including the large steam-turbine units, would be supplied with pipeline-supplied
 pressurized natural gas.

Table 1 lists the Central San Juan and Palo Seco generating units and the fueling assumptions
made for each of the cases. Information on the units was provided to Argonne by Genera PR, the

company contracted to operate Puerto Rico’s power plants. The cases do not consider a proposed
new 450 MW natural gas combined cycle unit at Palo Seco.

The analysis looked at the following:

 1. Changes in criteria emissions:
 a. CO2
 b. SOx
 c. NOx
 d. Total particulate matter
 e. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)
 f. CO
 2. Changes in fuel and transportation costs.
 3. Changes in public and worker safety.

The emission calculations looked only at emissions of the power plants and the fuel trucks. Other
life-cycle emission sources, such as those from pipeline construction or leakage during storage
and transportation, were not considered.

While the economic analysis includes fuel cost differentials and transport cost differentials it
does not include differences in capital expenditure (e.g., pipeline construction and new LNG
gasification units), operations, on-site fuel storage, maintenance, and lifetime replacement – only
the cost of getting fuel to the power plants.

The safety assessment looked at the incident rates associated with fuel transport, either by
pipeline or by truck. Argonne did not assess e.g., accident consequences, health risks due to
changes in criteria emissions, or hazards in industrial construction and operations.

Methodology
Monthly generation and fuel consumption for the Central San Juan and Palo Seco units were
collected from EIA Form 923 for January 2024 through November 2025. EIA does not report
values for smaller units such as the FEMA emergency units and mobile packs. These values were
provided by Genera PR.

Fixed prices for fuel oil No. 2 and fuel oil No. 6 were provided by LBNL. Prices as delivered to
Palo seco were $13/MMBtu and $17/MMBtu respectively. Natural gas prices vary seasonally.
The economic analysis looked at monthly Henry Hub natural gas prices from January 2024
through November 2025 and were adjusted for San Juan (using the equation $7.6 + 1.15×Henry
Hub Price) with additional truck delivery costs of $250 per truck when LNG is used. Thus, fuel
delivery costs could be estimated from the monthly fuel consumption data.

Likewise, monthly fuel consumption was translated to annualized emissions using appropriate
EPA AP-42 emission factors by fuel type, when plant specific emission factors were not
provided by Genera PR. Note that the Central San Juan and Palo Seco steam turbines currently
use electrostatic precipitators (ESPs) and selective catalytic reduction (SCR) for, respectively,
particulate matter and nitrogen oxide control. The plants do not employ sulfur dioxide scrubbers.

Truck emissions for annual fuel deliveries (and return trips) for Scenario 1 were based on
standard LNG truck capacities, loaded and unloaded fuel economies, and relevant emission
factors. 1

Results: Fuel and Transport Costs
Figure 3 shows the annualized costs for fuel and delivery for the three cases. A 4% and 11%
reduction in annual cost (from $222 million to $213 million to $198 million) can be achieved in
the alternative scenarios. It is important to note that these are just delivered-fuel costs and that
the analysis did not consider capital or operational expenditures to burn pipeline-supplied natural
gas.

Figure 3. Annual fuel and fuel transport costs for the three cases.

Results: Criteria Emissions
Switching from fuel oil to natural gas combustion reduces carbon dioxide and criteria emissions
(Figures 4 – 9). This is especially true for sulfur dioxide, which would be essentially eliminated
by moving away from fuel oil (Figure 5). Likewise, total particulate matter emissions would be
significantly reduced (Figure 7).

In contrast, carbon monoxide emissions would increase by 31% under scenario 1 and by 57%
under scenario 2 when using pipeline natural gas to run the large Palo Seco steam-turbine units
(Figure 9). This increase in CO emissions is consistent with emission factors from EPA’s AP-42
and the larger CO emissions industry experiences with natural gas versus fuel oil.

Results: Safety Assessment

Argonne analyzed and compared incident rates for the LNG trucks and pipeline transport (Figure
10). The route between the San Juan port and Palo Seco poses significant risks to trucks and
truck drivers, as well as nearby communities in the event of an accident; the risk increases with
the number of daily LNG trucks. The incident rate for the pipeline is a small fraction of that for
LNG trucks between the port and Palo Seco.

Figure 10. Estimate of monthly incident rates for LNG truck transport and pressurized natural
gas pipeline transport.
