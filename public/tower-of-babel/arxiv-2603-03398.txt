Zero-Knowledge Federated Learning with Lattice-Based Hybrid Encryption for Quantum-Resilient Medical AI
Edouard Lansiaux

Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative training of medical AI models across hospitals without centralizing patient data. However, the exchange of model updates exposes critical vulnerabilities: gradient inversion attacks can reconstruct patient information, Byzantine clients can poison the global model, and the Harvest Now, Decrypt Later (HNDL) threat renders today’s encrypted traffic vulnerable to future quantum adversaries. We introduce ZKFL-PQ ( Zero-Knowledge Federated Learning, Post-Quantum ), a three-tiered cryptographic protocol that hybridizes (i) ML-KEM (FIPS 203) for quantum-resistant key encapsulation, (ii) lattice-based Zero-Knowledge Proofs for verifiable norm-constrained gradient integrity, and (iii) BFV homomorphic encryption for privacy-preserving aggregation. We formalize the security model and prove correctness and zero-knowledge properties under the Module-LWE, Ring-LWE, and SIS assumptions in the classical random oracle model . We evaluate ZKFL-PQ on synthetic medical imaging data across 5 federated clients over 10 training rounds. Our protocol achieves 100% rejection of norm-violating updates while maintaining model accuracy at 100%, compared to a catastrophic drop to 23% under standard FL. The computational overhead (factor ∼ \sim 20 × \times ) is analyzed and shown to be compatible with clinical research workflows operating on daily or weekly training cycles. We emphasize that the current defense guarantees rejection of large-norm malicious updates; robustness against subtle low-norm or directional poisoning remains future work.



Keywords:
Post-Quantum Cryptography, Federated Learning, Zero-Knowledge Proofs, Homomorphic Encryption, Medical AI, ML-KEM, Lattice-Based Cryptography

1
Introduction

The digitization of healthcare generates enormous volumes of sensitive data—medical imaging, genomic sequences, electronic health records—which are ideal substrates for training powerful AI models. Privacy regulations (GDPR, HIPAA) and ethical constraints, however, prevent centralized data aggregation across institutions. Federated Learning (FL)
[
1
]
offers an elegant solution: clients (hospitals) train models locally and share only parameter updates (gradients) with a central aggregation server.

Despite its privacy-preserving premise, FL suffers from three fundamental vulnerabilities. First,
gradient inversion attacks

[
2
]
can reconstruct raw patient data from shared model updates with alarming fidelity. Second,
Byzantine attacks
allow malicious or compromised clients to poison the global model by submitting adversarial gradients
[
3
]
. Third, and most critically for long-lived medical data, the
Harvest Now, Decrypt Later
(HNDL) strategy
[
4
]
enables a quantum-capable adversary to record today’s encrypted traffic and retroactively decrypt it once a sufficiently powerful quantum computer is available.

Medical data requires confidentiality guarantees spanning patient lifetimes. An MRI scan encrypted with RSA-2048 today may be decryptable within 15–20 years
[
5
]
, well within the period during which the data must remain protected. This creates an urgent need for
quantum-resilient
cryptographic protocols for FL.

Contributions.

We propose
ZKFL-PQ
, a three-tiered cryptographic framework for federated learning that addresses all three vulnerabilities simultaneously:

(i)

Quantum-resistant channels
via ML-KEM (FIPS 203)
[
6
]
, ensuring that key exchange and data encapsulation resist both classical and quantum attacks.

(ii)

Verifiable gradient integrity
via lattice-based Zero-Knowledge Proofs, enabling each client to prove that its update satisfies predefined constraints (e.g., bounded
\ell_{2}
-norm) without revealing the update itself.

(iii)

Privacy-preserving aggregation
via BFV homomorphic encryption
[
7
]
, allowing the server to compute the average of encrypted gradients without accessing individual contributions.

We formalize the protocol, prove its security properties under standard lattice hardness assumptions, and validate it experimentally on synthetic medical imaging data with 5 federated clients over 10 rounds, including a Byzantine adversary that is detected with 100% accuracy.

Zero-Knowledge Proofs

Definition 2.4

(Zero-Knowledge Proof System)
.

A ZKP system for a language

1.

Completeness:
If

2.

If

.

3.

There exists a simulator
\mathcal{S}

3
Threat Model

We consider a federated learning system with
N
clients (hospitals) and one aggregation server. The adversary
\mathcal{A}
has the following capabilities:

T1

Passive eavesdropping:

\mathcal{A}
can observe all network traffic between clients and the server.

T2

Byzantine clients:

\mathcal{A}
can corrupt up to
f<N/2
clients, which may submit arbitrary (malicious) model updates.

T3

HNDL adversary:

\mathcal{A}
records all encrypted traffic and possesses a future quantum computer capable of solving the factoring and discrete logarithm problems in polynomial time.

T4

Honest-but-curious server:
The server follows the protocol but attempts to learn information from client updates.

Definition 3.1

(Security Goals)
.

The protocol must provide:

1.

Post-quantum confidentiality
: No quantum adversary can recover the shared secrets or individual gradients from recorded traffic.

2.

Norm-constrained Byzantine rejection
: Gradient updates with
\left\lVert\Delta w_{i}\right\rVert_{2}>\tau
are detected and rejected with high probability.

3.

Gradient privacy
: The server learns only the aggregate update
\sum\Delta w_{i}
, not individual
\Delta w_{i}
.

4.

Verifiability
: Each client’s update is certified to satisfy protocol constraints.

4.3
ZKP for Gradient Norm Bounds

We construct a non-interactive ZKP (via the Fiat-Shamir heuristic) based on a
\Sigma
-protocol with lattice-based commitments. The language is:

\mathcal{L}_{\tau}=\left\{\Delta w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\lVert\Delta w\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\tau^{2}\right\}

(5)

Definition 4.2

(Lattice Commitment)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m\times(d+\ell)}
be a public matrix, where
d
is the gradient dimension and
\ell
is the randomness dimension. For message
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and randomness
\mathbf{r}\xleftarrow{\$}D_{\mathbb{Z}^{\ell},\sigma_{r}}
:

\mathsf{Commit}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{A}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\mathbf{r}\end{pmatrix}\bmod q

(6)

Binding relies on the SIS (Short Integer Solution) assumption; hiding is statistical when
\sigma_{r}
is sufficiently large.

Protocol.

The
\Sigma
-protocol for proving
\left\lVert\Delta w\right\rVert_{2}\leq\tau
proceeds as follows:

1.

Commitment.
Let
\tilde{w}=\text{Quantize}(\Delta w)
. The prover computes
C=\mathsf{Commit}(\tilde{w};\mathbf{r})
. The prover samples masking vector
\mathbf{y}\leftarrow D_{\mathbb{Z}^{d},\sigma_{y}}
with
\sigma_{y}=\tau\cdot\beta
for rejection parameter
\beta
, and computes
T=\mathsf{Commit}(\mathbf{y};\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
.

2.

Challenge.

c=H(C\|T\|\tau)\bmod 2^{\kappa}
via the Fiat-Shamir transform, where
H
is SHA3-256 and
\kappa
controls the challenge space.

3.

Response.
Compute
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{y}+c\cdot\tilde{w}
and
\mathbf{r}_{z}=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+c\cdot\mathbf{r}\pmod{q}
. Accept (output
(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{r}_{z})
) with probability according to rejection sampling.

4.

Verification.
The verifier checks:

(a)

\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}\leq B
for bound
B=\sigma_{y}\sqrt{d}\cdot 1.5
;

(b)

c=H(C\|T\|\tau)
(challenge consistency);

(c)

\mathbf{A}\cdot(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{r}_{z})^{\top}\equiv T+c\cdot C\pmod{q}

(algebraic consistency)
.

The algebraic verification (c) is
critical
for soundness: it ensures that the prover cannot fabricate a valid transcript without actually possessing a valid commitment opening.

Theorem 4.3

(Security of the ZKP)
.

Under the SIS
{}_{m,d+\ell,q,\beta^{\prime}}
assumption, the non-interactive ZKP described above satisfies:

1.

Completeness:
An honest prover with
\left\lVert\Delta w\right\rVert_{2}\leq\tau
produces a valid proof with probability
\geq 1-2^{-\lambda}
.

2.

Soundness:
No efficient prover can produce a valid proof for
\left\lVert\Delta w\right\rVert_{2}>\tau
except with probability
\text{negl}(\lambda)
.

3.

Honest-Verifier Zero-Knowledge:
There exists a polynomial-time simulator
\mathcal{S}
that produces transcripts indistinguishable from real proofs.

Proof.

We provide a rigorous separation between the statistical and computational arguments.

Completeness
follows from the Gaussian rejection sampling lemma
[
10
]
. When
\sigma_{y}\geq\beta\cdot\tau
with
\beta=12
, the expected number of rejection sampling rounds is
O(1)
, so the prover succeeds with overwhelming probability. The algebraic verification passes because:

\mathbf{A}\cdot(\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{r}_{z})^{\top}=\mathbf{A}\cdot((\mathbf{y}+c\tilde{w})\|(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+c\mathbf{r}))^{\top}=T+c\cdot C\pmod{q}.

Soundness
relies on two components:

(i) Statistical norm gap:
The response bound
B=1.5\sigma_{y}\sqrt{d}
is calibrated such that honest provers (with
\left\lVert\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\tau
) pass the norm check with probability
\geq 1-2^{-\lambda}
via Gaussian tail bounds. For a cheating prover with
\left\lVert\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}>\tau\cdot\gamma
(for gap
\gamma>1
), the response
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{y}+c\cdot\tilde{w}
has expected norm
\sqrt{\sigma_{y}^{2}d+c^{2}\left\lVert\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}
. When
c\cdot\left\lVert\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}\gg\sigma_{y}\sqrt{d}
, the norm check fails with high probability.

(ii) Computational binding via SIS:
The algebraic verification ensures that a valid proof
(C,T,\mathbf{z},\mathbf{r}_{z},c)
implies knowledge of a valid opening. Specifically, if a malicious prover produces valid proofs for two distinct challenges
c\neq c^{\prime}
on the same commitment
C
, then by subtracting the verification equations:

\mathbf{A}\cdot((\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\|(\mathbf{r}_{z}-\mathbf{r}_{z}^{\prime}))^{\top}=(c-c^{\prime})\cdot C\pmod{q}.

This yields a short vector in the kernel of
\mathbf{A}
, breaking the SIS assumption. Thus, for each
C
, at most one challenge can yield a valid response, and soundness error is bounded by
2^{-\kappa}
.

Zero-Knowledge
is shown by constructing a simulator that samples
\mathbf{z}\leftarrow D_{\mathbb{Z}^{d},\sigma_{y}}
, chooses random
c
, and programs the random oracle
H
so that
C
and
T
are consistent. The statistical distance between simulated and real transcripts is bounded by
2^{-\lambda}
when
\sigma_{y}/\max(c\cdot\left\lVert\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2})\geq\beta
.
∎

4.4
Correctness of Homomorphic Aggregation

Theorem 4.4

(Aggregation Correctness)
.

Let
\Delta w_{1},\ldots,\Delta w_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be gradient updates encoded as plaintexts
m_{1},\ldots,m_{N}\in R_{t}
via quantization with scale factor
s
. Under the BFV scheme with parameters
(n,q,t)
, the homomorphic sum satisfies:

\mathsf{Dec}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{Enc}(m_{i})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\pmod{t}

(7)

provided
N\cdot(2\sigma+1)\cdot n<q/(2t)
, where
\sigma
is the error standard deviation.

Proof.

Each ciphertext
\mathsf{Enc}(m_{i})=(c_{0}^{(i)},c_{1}^{(i)})
satisfies
c_{0}^{(i)}+c_{1}^{(i)}\cdot s=\Delta\cdot m_{i}+e_{i}\pmod{q}
where
\left\lVert e_{i}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq(2\sigma+1)\cdot n
. After summing
N
ciphertexts, the accumulated noise is bounded by
\left\lVert\sum e_{i}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq N\cdot(2\sigma+1)\cdot n
. Correct decryption requires this to be less than
q/(2t)=\Delta/2
. Given our parameters (
N=5
,
\sigma=3.2
,
n=512
,
q=2^{32}-5
,
t=2^{16}
), we have
5\cdot 7.4\cdot 512=18{,}944\ll 32{,}768=q/(2t)
, confirming correctness.
∎

4.5
End-to-End Security

Theorem 4.5

(ZKFL-PQ Security)
.

Under the MLWE
{}_{256,3,3329,\text{CBD}_{2}}
, RLWE
{}_{512,2^{32}-5,D_{3.2}}
, and SIS
256,d+128,7681,B
assumptions, the ZKFL-PQ protocol achieves:

1.

IND-CCA2 security of the key encapsulation (ML-KEM-768);

2.

Semantic security of gradient encryption (BFV);

3.

Soundness and zero-knowledge of gradient verification;

4.

Byzantine resilience: malicious updates with
\left\lVert\Delta w\right\rVert_{2}>\tau
are rejected with probability
\geq 1-\text{negl}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Property (1) follows from the IND-CCA2 security of ML-KEM-768 under MLWE
[
6
]
.
Property (2) follows from the semantic security of BFV under RLWE
[
7
]
.
Properties (3) and (4) follow from Theorem
4.3
.

The protocol is constructed as a layered composition. Each layer operates on independent randomness:

•

ML-KEM uses fresh randomness per encapsulation.

•

BFV uses fresh error polynomials per encryption.

•

The ZKP uses fresh masking vectors per proof.

No information leaks between layers beyond the intended outputs. Under correct parameterization and assuming no side-channel leakage, the overall security reduces to the conjunction of the underlying hardness assumptions.

We note that this argument is modular; a full UC-style composition proof is outside the scope of this work.
∎

4.5.1
Fiat–Shamir and Random Oracle Considerations

The non-interactive transformation of the
\Sigma
-protocol relies on the Fiat–Shamir heuristic in the Random Oracle Model (ROM).
Since ZKFL-PQ aims at post-quantum security, it is important to consider adversaries with quantum access to the hash function.

Our proof assumes classical ROM security. A full treatment in the Quantum Random Oracle Model (QROM) would require additional analysis, as rewinding-based arguments do not directly apply in the presence of quantum queries to the oracle.

While lattice-based identification and signature schemes have been studied under QROM assumptions
[
11
]
, a tight QROM reduction for the present norm-bound proof system remains future work. Consequently, our post-quantum claim applies fully to the underlying hardness assumptions (MLWE, RLWE, SIS), whereas the Fiat–Shamir transform inherits standard ROM caveats.

7
Discussion

Clinical Applicability.

The
\sim
20
\times
overhead is acceptable for medical FL scenarios where training occurs daily, weekly, or on-demand rather than in real-time. A training pipeline that takes 1 minute under standard FL would require
\sim
20 minutes under ZKFL-PQ—well within a nightly batch window.

Scalability.

The current bottleneck is the schoolbook polynomial multiplication (
O(n^{2})
) used in ML-KEM and BFV. NTT-based implementations achieve
O(n\log n)
, which would reduce ML-KEM overhead by an order of magnitude. GPU-accelerated HE libraries (e.g., OpenFHE, SEAL) would further improve the HE encryption phase.

Threshold Calibration.

The norm threshold
\tau
must be carefully calibrated: too low rejects legitimate updates from clients with unusual data distributions; too high allows subtle poisoning. We propose an adaptive scheme where
\tau^{(t)}
is set to
\mu^{(t-1)}+k\cdot\sigma^{(t-1)}
based on the empirical distribution of norms from round
t-1
, with
k
as a tunable sensitivity parameter.

Limitations.

•

Synthetic data only:
Our evaluation uses synthetic medical imaging data. While this suffices for a proof of concept, validation on real multi-centric datasets (e.g., MRI, CT scans) is essential before clinical deployment.

•

Partial HE coverage:
To keep computational costs manageable, we encrypted only 512 out of 108,996 parameters. A full-parameter HE implementation would increase communication by
\sim
100
\times
, necessitating optimized libraries.

•

Limited adversarial coverage:
The current ZKP enforces only an
\ell_{2}
-norm bound. While this guarantees rejection of large-magnitude malicious updates, it does not prevent subtle low-norm, directional, or backdoor-style poisoning attacks.

•

Multiple cryptographic assumptions:
The security of ZKFL-PQ relies on three distinct hardness assumptions (MLWE, RLWE, SIS). While each is well-studied, their combination broadens the attack surface.

•

Trusted decryptor:
The current protocol assumes a trusted party holds the BFV secret key. Integrating distributed threshold decryption would eliminate this single point of failure.

•

ROM vs. QROM:
As noted in §
4.3
, the Fiat–Shamir transform is analyzed in the classical ROM; a full QROM treatment remains future work.

8
Conclusion

We have presented ZKFL-PQ, a three-tiered cryptographic protocol for federated learning that provides quantum-resistant communication (ML-KEM), verifiable gradient integrity (lattice-based ZKPs), and privacy-preserving aggregation (BFV homomorphic encryption). Experimental evaluation demonstrates that ZKFL-PQ achieves 100% Byzantine detection while preserving model accuracy, at a computational overhead of
\sim
20
\times
that is compatible with clinical research workflows.

Our security analysis is conducted under standard lattice assumptions (MLWE, RLWE, SIS) in the
classical random oracle model
. The underlying hardness assumptions are conjectured to be post-quantum secure; the Fiat–Shamir transform inherits standard ROM caveats, and a full QROM analysis remains future work.

Future work will focus on: (1) NTT-accelerated implementations for order-of-magnitude speedups; (2) extending the ZKP to richer constraint languages (e.g., per-layer norm bounds, directional consistency); (3) integrating distributed threshold decryption to eliminate the trusted decryptor assumption; (4) validation on real-world multi-centric medical imaging datasets; and (5) QROM-secure Fiat–Shamir instantiation.
