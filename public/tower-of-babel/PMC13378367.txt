Adaptive hybrid deep learning framework for medium-term wind speed forecasting
Authors: Fu-Kwun Wang, Fantahun Tsegaw, William Gomez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAccurate wind speed forecasting is essential for stable wind farm operation but is challenged by nonlinear, stochastic, and multi-scale spatiotemporal dynamics. An adaptive hybrid forecasting framework is proposed that integrates complementary feature extraction with sequence modeling. Evaluations across the Pacific Grove weather station and the VALHALL_A dataset show that the model achieves coefficients of determination above 0.97 and reduces root-mean-square error by over 20% relative to benchmark models while maintaining low maximum error. These results indicate that the integration of these components facilitates the effective modeling of both short-term variations and long-term dependencies in complex wind dynamics. The framework incorporates transparency mechanisms that enable interpretability and, together with its predictive performance, provide accurate, reliable, and scalable wind forecasting for renewable energy integration.Keywords: Adaptive hybrid model, ConvsLSTM, Medium-term forecasting, Wind speed

Introduction

Due to increasing environmental concerns related to traditional energy sources, the use of green energy technologies has become a sustainable alternative.1 Successfully addressing this urgent issue requires focusing on renewable energy sources that are both widely available and economically feasible. A comprehensive overhaul of the global power system is essential to reach high levels of renewable energy integration needed for decarbonization. Projections show that by 2050, about 91% of worldwide electricity will come from renewable sources, with solar photovoltaics (PVs) and wind power making up roughly 70%. Achieving even higher shares of renewables is possible if current technological, regulatory, and compliance challenges are effectively tackled.2 Among renewable options, wind energy remains a key focus due to its vast potential and scalability. However, its technical and operational viability is limited by the inherent variability and unpredictability of wind resources.3 These issues become especially critical for medium- to long-term forecasts. Extending forecasting models beyond traditional seven-day limits offers significant opportunities to support large-scale wind power deployment. Major challenges in analyzing long-term data can be reduced by extracting periodicity and volatility features across different time scales, with a 15-min sampling interval generally needed for accurate wind power forecasts.4

Several models have been developed for wind speed forecasting, and they fall into one of the three main classes: statistics-driven, physics-driven, and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven models.5 Statistical models remain among the most extensively applied approaches for wind speed forecasting, as they establish functional relationships between historical wind data and future trends. For example, the performance of the fractional autoregressive integrated moving average (f-ARIMA) model for day-ahead wind speed prediction has been rigorously evaluated, demonstrating substantial improvements in forecasting accuracy compared with the conventional persistence method.6 The Hammerstein autoregressive model has also proven effective for short-term power scheduling applications.7 Li et al.8 proposed a genetic algorithm that was utilized to optimize the parameters of a gray model for the prediction of both wind and hydropower outputs. Likewise, an autoregressive spatial model has been employed to forecast wind power across multiple wind stations.9 Although these statistical methods successfully capture seasonal regularities and consistent temporal patterns in wind speed data, their predictive accuracy is often constrained by the inherent nonlinear and stochastic characteristics of wind dynamics.10

In physical modeling approaches, input data such as meteorological variables are integrated into complex numerical frameworks like numerical weather prediction models to estimate wind speeds around turbine blades, requiring substantial computational resources.5 Recently, advanced and flexible AI-based models have gained increasing attention for their superior ability to capture nonlinear relationships and enhance forecasting accuracy. For instance, Chen et al.11 developed a hybrid long short-term memory (LSTM) model that demonstrated superior performance on large-scale wind datasets. Similarly, an improved extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) algorithm optimized via Bayesian hyperparameter tuning outperformed competing models in short-term wind power prediction under extreme weather conditions.12 The use of a gated recurrent unit (GRU) model with skip connections enabled more accurate determination of wind power prediction intervals.13 A hybrid model combining a graph convolutional network and LSTM can effectively capture spatial and temporal dependencies in wind power data.14 Furthermore, a model integrating a quaternion convolutional neural network and bi-directional LSTM (BiLSTM) architectures achieved higher precision and stability in wind speed prediction.15 In another study, graph attention networks and gated temporal convolutional networks were employed to extract spatial and temporal correlations, respectively, with an adaptive fusion module used to generate the final wind speed predictions.16

Accurate wind speed prediction is vital for improving economic efficiency and ensuring the stability of grid-integrated wind power. However, existing models often fail to capture both local and long-range spatial correlations, limiting their predictive performance. To address this issue, a local and long-range convolutional LSTM network can achieve lower multi-step forecasting errors.17 Similarly, Dahal et al.18 combined a convolutional LSTM (ConvLSTM) for modeling wind generation dynamics with a graph neural network for power flow analysis, yielding a high coefficient of determination. Sun et al.19 enhanced sea surface wind speed prediction for marine applications using a hybrid model that integrates ensemble empirical mode decomposition and ConvLSTM, effectively capturing two-dimensional spatial features. Despite the improved sequence handling of the scalar LSTM (sLSTM), it still struggles to retain long-term dependencies, underscoring the need for computationally efficient models capable of sustained long-term forecasting.20

Owing to their ability to model long-range temporal dependencies, Transformer-based architectures have been increasingly adopted in time series forecasting (TSF).21 For example, the Informer model improves efficiency and scalability through ProbSparse self-attention, a generative-style decoder, and a hybrid multi-head attention design, reducing the computational and memory complexity of long-sequence forecasting to O(LlogL), where L denotes the input sequence length.22 Similarly, autoformer captures periodic and seasonal patterns using an autocorrelation-based mechanism that models long-range dependencies via time-delay aggregation.23 By using patch-based tokenization to shorten input sequences and improve attention efficiency, PatchTST enhances long-horizon TSF performance.24 As reported by Liu et al.,25 its effectiveness may drop for highly volatile signals due to reliance on aggregated patch-based representations. The iTransformer model compresses temporal information into a single variate-level representation, which may weaken explicit modeling of fine-grained intra-variable temporal dynamics, as noted in recent work such as Pfformer.26 Patro and Agneeswaran27 reviewed state space models (SSMs) as transformer alternatives for long-sequence modeling, showing that Mamba-style attention scales nearly linearly with sequence length, unlike the quadratic cost of transformers. However, SSMs still lag behind state-of-the-art transformers on some tasks and modalities. TimesNet captures multi-scale temporal patterns by transforming time series into 2D representations for convolutional modeling without explicit sequential memory.28

Hyperparameter settings critically influence the performance of hybrid models. While empirical tuning remains common, recent studies increasingly employ heuristic optimization algorithms such as genetic algorithms (GAs) and differential evolution to automatically identify optimal configurations.29 For example, Yang et al.30 optimized a multi-input LSTM for wind speed prediction using Bayesian optimization (BO). Similarly, Gomez et al.31 developed a hybrid framework for forecasting renewable and non-renewable energy signals, using BO for hyperparameter tuning. Their approach combined support vector regression for intrinsic mode functions with a BiLSTM-attention model for residual prediction, effectively capturing temporal dependencies and improving peak-valley accuracy.

Selecting informative features not only mitigates overfitting but also enhances model interpretability. The comprehensive relative importance analysis (CRIA) is an advanced feature selection framework that builds on least-squares orthonormal similarity theory using a systematic, stepwise approach.32 This approach enables robust feature identification in high-dimensional datasets with strongly correlated variables, where conventional methods often fail. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that CRIA consistently outperforms benchmark techniques under comparable conditions, while maintaining interpretability through its complete mathematical derivation. Zeng et al.10 compared CRIA with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, XGBoost, mutual information, and self-feature selection models, reporting superior performance across metrics and reinforcing the value of incorporating CRIA in feature selection pipelines.

Explainability and interpretability are essential for building trust and ensuring practical use of data-driven models in power systems. Explainability goes beyond interpretability by providing human-understandable insights into a model’s internal reasoning, while interpretability focuses on measuring how input features influence model outputs.33 Addressing both is important for transparent and dependable decision-making in power system applications.34 Post-hoc methods like Shapley additive explanations (SHAPs) and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIMEs) are commonly used to enhance interpretability.35 These methods modify input features near the original data and assess their effect on predictions to determine feature importance. For instance, Fu et al.36 used SHAP to interpret CNN-based photovoltaic power predictions, and Serra et al.37 employed LIME to explain heating load forecasts from various machine learning models. In wind power prediction, Liao et al.38 compared four explainable AI techniques SHAP, permutation feature importance, partial dependence plots, and LIME, highlighting their complementary strengths in both global and local interpretability, an aspect often overlooked in previous research.

Despite significant progress in deep learning-based wind speed forecasting, fundamental research gaps persist. Existing sLSTM-based models still struggle to effectively capture both short-term spatial correlations and long-term temporal dependencies, which are essential for reliable medium-term prediction. In addition, most prior studies adopt independent optimization strategies, with only a limited number exploring joint learning of short- and long-horizon forecasting tasks. Such fragmented training approaches tend to suffer from cumulative errors and instability over extended forecasting horizons, thereby constraining their practical applicability. Furthermore, many current methods do not provide a single, comprehensible framework for capturing both local and global dependencies from shared modules. For medium-term wind speed forecasting under dynamic and variable environmental conditions, these limitations drive the need for a comprehensive, jointly optimized forecasting architecture that may improve prediction accuracy, temporal coherence, robustness, and interpretability.

In this study, we proposed the channel independence and channel mixing modules combining convolutional scalar LSTM and scalar LSTM with a patching method (CIM-ConvsLSTM-P-sLSTM) framework for the medium-term wind speed forecasting. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.

A comprehensive set of comparative experiments is conducted using real-world wind speed datasets and advanced benchmark models to systematically examine the effectiveness, robustness, and cross-dataset generalization capability of the proposed framework. The evaluation focuses on several key aspects, including seasonal generalization performance, predictive accuracy, and the extraction of both feature-specific and cross-feature interaction patterns. Model performance is quantitatively assessed using standard error metrics, while additional validation procedures are applied to ensure the reliability and trustworthiness of the AI system. Data description and visualization

The experiment in this study is done using different datasets with a 15-min and 1-h interval. The first dataset was obtained from the Pacific Grove weather station in Monterey Bay, California, USA, and is publicly available through the California Irrigation Management Information System (https://cimis.water.ca.gov). It covers August 2023 through July 2024, with detailed descriptions of the meteorological variables and the precise geographic coordinates.10 The temporal variation of wind speed, which serves as the target variable, is shown in Figure 2 (a). This dataset originally comprised ten variables; three additional calendar features, such as month, day, and hour, were incorporated to enrich the temporal context, resulting in a total of 12 input features. Missing values were handled using a combined forward- and backward-mean imputation strategy to preserve temporal continuity, which is critical for time-series analysis. The final dataset contained 8,784 samples. To further assess performance for multiple operating conditions, the full Pacific Grove dataset is partitioned into four seasonal subsets, and the proposed model is evaluated against a benchmark model.

The second dataset, referred to as VALHALL_A, was used to further assess the generalization capability of the proposed model. It covers the period from January 2023 to March 2024, with comprehensive detail.42 Its temporal variation of wind speed is shown in Figure 2 (b). To stabilize model training, all variables were normalized using the min-max normalization method, scaling their values to the range [0, 1]. It was then compared with the benchmark model on the same dataset using hourly intervals across four seasons. Finally, it was compared with the four state-of-the-art models and one baseline model using hourly intervals on the VALHALL_A station dataset for 240 h-ahead forecasting.

The concatenation of the channel independence, channel mixing, and raw data components with ConvsLSTM, termed CIM-ConvsLSTM, demonstrates the most substantial gains among the ablation variants. As indicated in Table 5, compared with ConvsLSTM, the proposed model reduces RMSE by 59.69% and 45.25%, and sMAPE by 63.24% and 22.96%, while increasing R2 by 5.07% and 5.55% for the two prediction lengths, respectively. These improvements can be attributed to the richer, more informative concatenated representations produced by combining all three components.

The P-sLSTM model also surpasses the baseline ConvsLSTM, achieving RMSE reductions of 2.62% and 0.39%, sMAPE reductions of 1.90% and 1.93%, for the two prediction lengths. However, its performance gains are less pronounced than those of the other variants, likely due to a lack of feature extraction and the capability of capturing short-term fluctuation.

The proposed model, which integrates all modules and employs an ensemble forecasting strategy, achieves the most significant performance improvement. These dramatic improvements stem from both architectural and methodological advances. Architecturally, the model captures short-range spatiotemporal dependencies through the ConvsLSTM, enhanced by the two pivotal information-extraction modules, and long-range temporal dependencies through the P-sLSTM, facilitated by the patching technique. The patching method effectively bridges long look-back windows by segmenting them into shorter, more manageable sequences, enabling the sLSTM, which is naturally adept at short-term memory, to retain longer-term information. Methodologically, the concurrent extraction of both short-and long-range dependencies across two parallel models enhances the model’s competitiveness and robustness. Furthermore, the adopted ensemble strategy leverages the strengths of both forecasting paradigms while maintaining a balanced and weighted approach, contributing to the superior overall performance.

In summary, by integrating enriched inputs from the pre-model CI and CM modules, the model gains a context-aware understanding of feature relevance, enhancing its ability to distinguish informative from non-informative features and adaptively adjust its focus over time.

The in-model evaluation clarifies both the condition of the feature and how it contributes to memory updating and spatial dependency. The temporal dependency describes how the model balances past and present information when updating its internal memory. It is quantified by the per-feature temporal importance score, indicating how strongly each feature contributes to the cell-state update over time, and the temporal dependency score, representing the ratio of retained to newly introduced information. For the former one, a positive change in consecutive timesteps means a feature gains importance at the later timestep as shown in Figure 7A. As a result, the model updates the cell memory accordingly. For example, sol rad scored a +0.01, implying that its relevance has increased from t1 to t2 whereas precip’s importance has decreased by a value of −0.003. For the latter one, a dependency score of 0.793 (on a scale from 0 to 1) confirms that the update depends on both retained memory and short-term information, implying that both shorter and relatively longer lookbacks are needed to update the cell.

On the other hand, the spatial dependency captures how convolutional operations enable each feature to integrate contextual information from its neighbors. In Figure 7B, the soil temperature feature is centered, while L–1 is wind direction positioned one step to the left, and R+1 is air temperature to the right of the soil temperature. With two timesteps and a kernel size of three, the neighbor influence analysis shows that soil temperature exhibits a moderate self-contribution, sharing a smaller portion of the candidate update value of 0.07 proposed by air temperature and a larger portion of 0.84 proposed by wind direction, both admitted by i_1. New information attributable to neighboring features is further reflected in state S_2. The spatial-neighbor contribution score measures the extent to which a neighboring feature influences the target feature’s update, thereby revealing localized spatial interactions. Collectively, these metrics characterize the model’s spatiotemporal learning dynamics.

The physics-aware nonlinear model is employed as a secondary post-model technique under the assumption that the user seeks local interpretability for a specific test sample, consistent with the log-law assumptions. The purpose of this demonstration is to apply a transparent and physics-aware rule that can produce approximately close predictions to those of the AI model, prioritizing interpretability over optimality. With this aim, the recursive model’s prediction was analyzed using a log law to understand the AI model’s behavior through physically consistent parameters. At a fixed height of 2 m, the log law reproduced the AI prediction with approximated parameters u∗ at 0.71 mph and z0 at 0.03 m. The first parameter reflects the magnitude of surface-air momentum exchange and turbulent shear, while the second specifies the type of surface where the station is located. These values indicate moderate surface stress and limited drag, implying that the AI model predicted this sample’s wind speed under conditions of low roughness and moderate shear. Nevertheless, if the u∗ value is too high, then it implies that the turbine may be damaged, and preventive maintenance should be done.

The approximation was performed using 200 perturbations, generated from the sample with a measured wind speed of 7.40 mph at 2 m height. For this specific sample, the AI model predicted 7.54 mph, while the log law iteratively estimated the above parameters and yielded a final log-law prediction of 7.44 mph. This approach not only helps technical users to gain trust in the black-box AI model but also assists non-technical stakeholders, such as managers and decision-makers, in understanding the wind-speed behavior at a fixed weather station or in evaluating the feasibility of potential sites from different perspectives.

Statistical test

To further prove the superiority and robustness of the proposed model, the study conducts a Diebold-Mariano (DM) test comparing the forecasting accuracy of the proposed model against several benchmark models under two experimental settings: 10-day forecasting at the VALHALL-A station and 7-day forecasting on the Pacific Grove full dataset. The results are shown in Table 7. Real-time performance and complexity trade-off

Regarding the performance-complexity trade-off, the CIM-ConvsLSTM component comprises 131,603 trainable parameters and requires approximately 5 min for offline training. In contrast, the P-sLSTM component is considerably lighter, with 20,450 trainable parameters and a training time of 38.44 s. By comparison, the Informer model contains 738,311 trainable parameters and requires about 3 min and 58.0 s for offline training, while PatchTST includes 267,393 parameters and completes training in 1 min and 11.1 s.

More importantly, the proposed framework demonstrates practical suitability for near-real-time and real-time deployment. The ensemble components exhibit low inference latency, with average single-sample inference times of 238.75 μs for P-sLSTM and 339.5 μs for CIM-ConvsLSTM. Informer requires an average of 8.79 ms per prediction step, whereas PatchTST achieves 1.97 ms. Despite architectural optimizations aimed at improving computational efficiency, both transformer-based models exhibit higher inference latency than the proposed ensemble components, due to attention mechanisms and patch aggregation operations. This comparison demonstrates that the proposed framework, leveraging compact recurrent and ensemble architectures, achieves significantly higher accuracy while maintaining lower inference time, which is critical for real-time wind energy forecasting and control.

The ensemble inference latency is governed by the slower of the two parallel model components. All reported inference-time results on the VALHALL_A dataset correspond to a 240-h prediction horizon.

Discussion

Accurate and adaptive wind speed forecasting is essential for reliable wind speed prediction and efficient grid operation. This study developed a medium-term forecasting model, CIM-ConvsLSTM-P-sLSTM, which demonstrates superior performance across multiple datasets and forecasting horizons compared with existing models. The proposed architecture integrates a concatenated channel independence and channel mixing module for extracting informative features, a ConvsLSTM network for capturing complex spatiotemporal dynamics, and a P-sLSTM component for learning global dependencies across distant temporal segments within a unified hybrid framework. This integration enables the model to simultaneously capture local fluctuations and long-range temporal dependencies, which are critical for stable medium-term forecasting.

The ablation and benchmarking experiments confirm that each architectural component contributes meaningfully to the overall predictive capability of the framework. Across multi-day forecasting horizons, the proposed model achieved reductions exceeding 40% in RMSE and sMAPE compared with its component models, while maintaining a maximum prediction error below 2%. On the Pacific Grove dataset with a prediction horizon of 672 min, the proposed model achieved performance improvements of up to 62% over CNN, nearly 50% over ConvLSTM, and approximately 32% over PatchTST. It consistently outperformed benchmark methods in cross-dataset evaluations. These results indicate that the hybrid design effectively mitigates error accumulation, which is a common limitation of conventional deep learning models for longer forecasting windows, and demonstrates strong robustness and generalization across different climatic conditions.

The proposed methodology extends beyond predictive accuracy toward deployment-oriented forecasting by integrating pre-model analysis, in-model transparency, and post-model interpretability through realistic surrogate and user-specific models. Explicit evaluations of model complexity and real-time inference performance demonstrate an effective balance between expressiveness and computational efficiency. Its robustness across datasets and low-latency inference support reliable integration into smart wind farm operations and energy management systems, enabling timely decision-making for wind farm dispatch, grid scheduling, reserve allocation, and renewable energy integration.

Limitations of the study

Despite the strong predictive accuracy achieved by the proposed model, the study is subject to certain limitations. One notable limitation is its reliance on carefully calibrated hyperparameter settings. In addition, recurrent neural-based architectures inherently exhibit limited parallelization capability. Taken together, these factors indicate that further improvements to the model remain a promising direction for future research.

Future research will focus on integrating advanced architectures, such as transformer variants, to further enhance long-term forecasting capabilities and on applying improved decomposition techniques to better suppress noise under highly volatile atmospheric conditions. In addition, developing globally interpretable and in-model explanation techniques that enable real-time reasoning during decision-making will further strengthen the trustworthiness and practical adoption of intelligent forecasting models in renewable wind energy planning and management.

Proposed model

The proposed parallel hybrid architecture integrates the channel independence and channel mixing modules with ConvsLSTM (the upper path) and then ensembles it with the P-sLSTM (the lower path) (see Figure S5). Firstly, to get a richer feature map, the original data is concatenated with the channel independence and channel mixing modules’ outputs. Subsequently, deep learning models can be ensembled to make a better prediction using the combination of forecasting strategies. A recursive strategy is considered in this study. It models the data as a multiple-input single-output function in which the model uses the value just forecasted as part of the input values for forecasting the next timestep using the same one-step-ahead model while discarding the oldest value. Unlike the recursive strategy, multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) prevents the accumulation of errors as a single model produces all the predictions in one shot. Convolutional sLSTM network

The sLSTM includes exponential gating to easily and dynamically decide what information is to keep or discard as new data, and a new memory mixing mechanism to simultaneously integrate recent fluctuations with long-term patterns from different timesteps. Additionally, the building blocks and equations about it are provided in Beck et al.48 The structure of ConvsLSTM is depicted in Figure S4. Its mathematical formulation is shown in METHODS S1.

ConvLSTM49 has the capability to extract spatiotemporal information. Yu et al.44 enhanced its capability by adding feature fusion and feature attention blocks. However, in our study, the standard LSTM is replaced with the sLSTM. In the LSTM architecture, temporal relationships in the data are captured through a gating mechanism implemented by fully connected layers, where the input is represented as one-dimensional data. Compared to LSTM, ConvsLSTM also employs a gating mechanism to manage sequence dependencies but replaces fully connected layers with convolutional operations. This enables it to process two-dimensional inputs and capture spatial features within multidimensional data, while the remaining components stay consistent with those of LSTM.

Channel independence (CI) module

This module assumes that analyzing each feature individually enables the model to prioritize which features are more informative to the output than others. In this way, features are assigned a dynamic weight at each timestep, saving the main predictive model in downstream tasks greatly from treating all features equally. These intelligently learned weights using the module’s internal mechanisms can be averaged to get a global view of which one it pays attention to over time. Hence, the predictive engine takes this importance score into account during training.

The module utilizes a 1D convolutional layer that filters each feature based on its input requirements. Subsequently, two fully connected layers are used to enhance the capability of complex pattern extraction. To facilitate variable ranking by quantifying each variable’s contribution at individual time steps, well-established variable selection networks, such as those employed in TFT, assign softmax-normalized importance weights and aggregate per-variable representations accordingly.10 Unlike attention mechanisms, which globally mix features via query–key–value similarity and may be unstable,50 the CI module uses independent 1D convolutions to preserve feature independence before temporal modeling. Structurally, it combines 1D convolutions, lightweight fully connected layers, and sigmoid gating to capture temporal patterns and nonlinear relationships, producing channel-wise diagonal weights in [0,1] rather than using full matrix weighting correspondingly. This design enables independent learning of each channel’s temporal history.

Most feature fusion approaches do not explicitly model interaction weights, limiting interpretability of cross-variable interactions and relying on latent representations. For example, MFF-Mixer51 adopts representation-level fusion, where temporal features are learned in parallel via a Transformer for global dependencies and a 1D convolution for local patterns, then combined using a gated fusion mechanism. In contrast, the proposed CM module applies 2D convolutions directly to the original multivariate inputs to capture localized inter-feature interactions via shared kernels, followed by lightweight fully connected layers for nonlinear transformation and sigmoid gating for weight computation. This design enforces a structured bias toward localized interactions, functioning as an interaction extraction mechanism rather than a feature fusion operator, while enabling spatially localized and parameter-efficient learning.

P-sLSTM

Relying solely on models that can only capture short-term information is not always advantageous. Medium to long-range predictions may require long-term memories. Therefore, the patching method is used in the proposed hybrid model along with sLSTM, called P-sLSTM. The theoretical foundation and analysis of the memory properties of sLSTM and patching are discussed in.20 P-sLSTM, which forms the lower part of the proposed model, begins by separating the multivariable time series into independent channels (M), after which the patching operator divides each channel into smaller patches (N), thereby making long-range information with lookback size (L) manageable for independent processing. Mathematically, the batch (b) of samples X∈RL×M with a total of b×L×M is transformed to b×M×L initially to undertake channel independence patching for the length of P to get (b·M)×L. Applying a patching operator on this, resulted in (b·M)×N×P. A projection using a linear layer transforms P-length raw features into an Embedding (E) length feature vector (b·M)×N×E. The sLSTM runs across patches inside the sLSTM block, wherein the value adding task of extracting different patterns over patches is done. The detailed mechanics of the sLSTM block can be found in the literature.48 To get the global information by combining information from different patches, flattening must be done to convert it into (b·M)×D, where, D=(N·E). Overall, this study’s primary contribution is the systematic integration of sLSTM in both convolution-augmented and patch-based forms to address persistent challenges in medium-term wind speed forecasting under dynamic conditions, by providing downstream spatiotemporal models with feature-specific and interaction-aware representations that offer explicit interpretability. First, channel-wise temporal features and inter-channel interactions are extracted via structured 1D and 2D convolutions, then fused in a ConvsLSTM to capture local spatiotemporal dependencies through convolutional and gated operations. This enables more parameter-efficient and physically interpretable spatiotemporal learning compared to non-convolutional temporal models that rely on flattening and dense recurrence, thereby improving generalizability. Second, a patching strategy enhances the same sLSTM module by expanding its temporal receptive field to better capture long-range dependencies. Third, the framework adopts a unified ensemble learning strategy that jointly optimizes short- and long-horizon predictions, thereby mitigating error accumulation and instability commonly associated with independent training. Lastly, by integrating pre-model, in-model, and post-model interpretability mechanisms, the proposed approach establishes a coherent and reliable forecasting pipeline that is largely lacking in existing deep learning–based wind speed prediction methods.
