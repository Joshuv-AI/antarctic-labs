Pseudo-Labeling for Unsupervised Domain Adaptation with Kernel GLMs
Nathan Weill Kaizheng Wang

Abstract
We propose a principled framework for unsupervised domain adaptation under covariate shift in kernel Generalized Linear Models (GLMs), encompassing kernelized linear, logistic, and Poisson regression with ridge regularization. Our goal is to minimize prediction error in the target domain by leveraging labeled source data and unlabeled target data, despite differences in covariate distributions. We partition the labeled source data into two batches: one for training a family of candidate models, and the other for building an imputation model. This imputation model generates pseudo-labels for the target data, enabling robust model selection. We establish non-asymptotic excess-risk bounds that characterize adaptation performance through an “effective labeled sample size,” explicitly accounting for the unknown covariate shift. Experiments on synthetic and real datasets demonstrate consistent performance gains over source-only baselines.



Keywords:
Covariate shift, kernel GLMs, pseudo-labeling, unsupervised domain adaptation.

1
Introduction

The standard machine learning framework relies on the fundamental assumption that training and test datasets are drawn from the same underlying distribution
(
27
)
. However, in realistic deployment scenarios, this assumption is frequently violated. A canonical instance of distribution shift is
covariate shift

(
23
;
3
)
, where the marginal distribution of features varies between the source and target domains, while the conditional distribution of the output label remains stable. This phenomenon is pervasive in high-stakes applications, from personalized medicine to computer vision. It is well-documented that covariate shift can severely degrade predictive performance
(
12
)
.

In many applications, obtaining labels for the target population is prohibitively expensive, whereas collecting unlabeled covariates is straightforward. This motivates the study of unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA), where the learning algorithm must generalize to the target domain using labeled source data and only unlabeled target covariates. We investigate UDA for ridge-regularized Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) and their kernel extensions. In this regime, simply applying a model trained on the source is often suboptimal, even if the model is well-specified. Standard learning algorithms tune regularization hyperparameters to balance bias and variance on the
source
distribution, ignoring regions of the covariate space where the target density is high but source data is scarce. Consequently, achieving optimal target performance requires an adaptive strategy that leverages unlabeled target data to recalibrate the estimator.

Contributions

We address this challenge by developing a pseudo-labeling framework tailored for kernel GLMs. The approach enables model selection for the target domain using only unlabeled data. Our contributions are two-fold:

•

Methodology:
We propose a procedure that partitions the labeled source data into two batches. The first batch is used to train a family of candidate models, while the second trains an imputation model to generate pseudo-labels for the target covariates. These pseudo-labels serve as a proxy for ground truth, allowing us to select the candidate model that minimizes the estimated target risk. We provide precise guidance for tuning the imputation model to optimize the final selection.

•

Theory:
We derive non-asymptotic excess risk bounds for ridge-regularized kernel GLMs in the UDA setting. Our bounds quantify the information transfer via an “effective labeled sample size” that accounts for the discrepancy between source and target spectral properties. This theoretical result demonstrates that our method automatically adapts to the unknown covariate shift.

Related Work

The theoretical landscape of transfer learning largely focuses on settings where the learner has access to labeled target data or a known target covariate distribution
(
3
;
13
)
. Such assumptions simplify adaptation by allowing for direct risk estimation or precise density ratio calculation. In contrast, we address the strictly unsupervised regime, where the target distribution is unknown and observed only through a finite set of unlabeled covariates. This necessitates inferring the structure of the shift and adapting the estimator solely from the geometry of the unlabeled data, without the guidance of target labels.

The necessity of adaptation under covariate shift depends on the modeling regime. In the
parametric
setting, if the model is well-specified, standard Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) on the source is asymptotically efficient for the target, rendering adaptation unnecessary
(
9
)
. However, if the parametric model is mis-specified, the source MLE is biased for the target; the standard remedy is importance weighting
(
23
;
25
)
or its doubly robust variants utilizing pseudo-outcomes
(
16
)
. While statistically consistent, these methods often suffer from high variance and the difficulty of estimating density ratios in high dimensions.

In the
nonparametric
regime we consider, the situation differs. Even if the model is well-specified (e.g., the true function lies in an RKHS), regularization is required to ensure finite-sample generalization. Recent works have established theoretical guarantees for reweighting-based adaptation in kernel ridge regression (KRR)
(
17
)
and kernel methods with more general losses
(
8
)
. However, these approaches remain dependent on the existence and accurate estimation of density ratios. An alternative that avoids density estimation is to adapt the regularization directly.
29
addressed this for KRR using pseudo-labels to estimate the target error for selecting the optimal regularization. Our work operates in this same nonparametric regime, extending the pseudo-labeling paradigm to the broader and technically more demanding class of kernel GLMs.

The strategy of filling missing labels with model predictions has driven empirical success in deep learning
(
14
;
24
)
. Theoretical analysis of pseudo-labeling has mostly focused on classification under cluster assumptions or margin conditions
(
15
;
30
;
4
)
, which do not translate to the continuous responses covered by our GLM framework.

Problem Setup

2.1
Generalized linear models under covariate shift

Let
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{(\bm{x}_{0i},y_{0i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}}
denote samples drawn from the source and target distributions, respectively. Here,
\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{0i}\in\mathcal{X}
are covariates in a compact metric space
\mathcal{X}
, and
y_{i},y_{0i}\in\mathbb{R}
are real-valued responses. In the Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) setting, the target labels
\{y_{0i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}}
are unobserved. Our goal is to learn a predictive model using the labeled source data
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and the unlabeled target covariates
\{\bm{x}_{0i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}}
that achieves low error on the target population.

We assume that both domains share a common Generalized Linear Model (GLM)
(
20
)
in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
\mathcal{F}
. Specifically, conditioned on
\bm{x}
, the response
y
is drawn from an exponential family distribution whose density function has the form:

p(y|\bm{x})=h(y,\tau)\exp\bigg(\frac{yf^{*}(\bm{x})-a(f^{*}(\bm{x}))}{d(\tau)}\bigg),

(2.1)

where
a:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a known, strictly convex log-partition function;
h
and
d
are known functions;
\tau>0
is a (possibly unknown) dispersion parameter; and
f^{*}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{F}
.
The conditional mean of the response is given by the canonical link:

\mathbb{E}[y\mid\bm{x}]=a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x})).

(2.2)

Up to an affine transform,
the corresponding negative log-likelihood function is:

\ell(f(\bm{x}),y):=a(f(\bm{x}))-yf(\bm{x}),\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{F}.

(2.3)

Classical examples include
linear regression
[
a(u)=u^{2}/2
],
logistic regression
[
a(u)=\log(1+e^{u})
], and
Poisson regression
[
a(u)=e^{u}
].

The RKHS
\mathcal{F}
is generated by a continuous, symmetric, positive semi-definite kernel
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. By the Moore-Aronszajn Theorem
(
2
)
, there exists a feature space (Hilbert space)
\mathbb{H}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
and a mapping
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
such that
K(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\langle\Phi(\bm{x}),\Phi(\bm{x}^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
for all
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. The RKHS is defined as the class of linear functionals on the feature space:

\mathcal{F}=\left\{f_{\bm{\theta}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\mid f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\langle\Phi(\bm{x}),\bm{\theta}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},\,\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{H}\right\}.

(2.4)

Equipped with the norm
\|f_{\bm{\theta}}\|_{\mathcal{F}}:=\|\bm{\theta}\|_{\mathbb{H}}
,
\mathcal{F}
is a Hilbert space isometric to
\mathbb{H}
.

Below are some frequently used kernels and associated RKHS’s
(
28
)
.

Example 2.1

(Linear and affine kernels)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The linear kernel
K(\bm{z},\bm{w})=\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{w}
and the affine kernel
K(\bm{z},\bm{w})=1+\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{w}
yield the RKHS
\mathcal{F}
of all linear and affine functions respectively, of dimension
D=d
and
D=d+1
respectively.

Example 2.2

(Polynomial kernels)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The inhomogeneous polynomial kernel of degree
m\geq 2
is
K(\bm{z},\bm{w})=(1+\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{w})^{m}
. It yields the RKHS
\mathcal{F}
of all polynomials of degree
m
or less, of dimension
D=\binom{d+m}{m}
.

The function spaces in
Examples

2.1
and
2.2
are finite-dimensional. The following example is infinite-dimensional.

Example 2.3

(First-order Sobolev kernel)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
and
K(\bm{z},\bm{w})=\min\{\bm{z},\bm{w}\}
. Then the first-order Sobolev space is

\mathcal{F}=\left\{f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\mid f(0)=0,\int_{0}^{1}\left|f^{\prime}(x)\right|^{2}dx<\infty\right\}.

We adopt the following standard assumptions for the UDA setting:

Assumption 2.1

(GLM in RKHS and Random Design)
.

The source dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and target dataset
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\{(\bm{x}_{0i},y_{0i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}}
are independent, with samples drawn i.i.d. within each domain.

•

Covariate Shift:
The covariate distributions for source and target are
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, respectively. We assume
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(\mathcal{Q})\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(\mathcal{P})
. The second-moment operators
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{P}}[\Phi(\bm{x})\otimes\Phi(\bm{x})]
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{Q}}[\Phi(\bm{x})\otimes\Phi(\bm{x})]
are trace-class.

•

Well-Specified Model:
There exists a true function
f^{*}=f_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\in\mathcal{F}
that generates the responses in both domains according to the distribution in
Eq.

2.1
.

•

Finite Noise Variance:
Define noise variables
\varepsilon_{i}=-y_{i}+a^{\prime}\left(f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\right)
and
\varepsilon_{0i}=-y_{0i}+a^{\prime}\left(f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0i})\right)
. We assume that conditioned on the covariates,
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{\varepsilon_{0i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}}
have finite second moments.

2.2
Ridge regularization for kernel GLMs

For any predictive model
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we evaluate its performance via the expected negative log-likelihood on the target population:

\displaystyle R(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{x},y)\sim\mathcal{Q}}[a(f(\bm{x}))-yf(\bm{x})]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{Q}}[a(f(\bm{x}))-f(\bm{x})a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}))].

(2.5)

Under Assumption
2.1
,
R(f)
is minimized by
f^{*}
. We define the
excess risk
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f)=R(f)-R(f^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{Q}}\Big[a(f(\bm{x}))-a(f^{*}(\bm{x}))-a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}))(f(\bm{x})-f^{*}(\bm{x}))\Big].

(2.6)

This quantity, which is the expected Bregman divergence defined by
a
, measures the discrepancy between the predicted conditional distribution and the truth.

A standard approach to estimating
f^{*}
is to minimize the regularized empirical risk on the source data:

\hat{f}_{\lambda}=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(a(f(\bm{x}_{i}))-y_{i}f(\bm{x}_{i})\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\right\},

(2.7)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularization hyperparameter balancing data fit and smoothness. By the Representer Theorem
(
22
)
, the solution lies in the span of the source data:
\hat{f}_{\lambda}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}K(\bm{x}_{i},\cdot)
. The coefficient vector
\hat{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the solution to the convex optimization problem:

\min_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{a}(\bm{\alpha})-\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{\alpha}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{\alpha}\right\},

(2.8)

where
\bm{K}_{ij}=K(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})
,
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\top}
, and
\bm{a}(\bm{\alpha})=(a([\bm{K}\bm{\alpha}]_{1}),\dots,a([\bm{K}\bm{\alpha}]_{n}))^{\top}
.

For source-only learning,
\lambda
is typically selected to minimize the source validation error. However, under covariate shift, an optimal penalty for the source may be suboptimal for the target, particularly if the target distribution is concentrated in directions where the source provides weak coverage.
Ideally, if we had access to target labels
\{y_{0i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}}
, we would select the parameter
\lambda
minimizing the empirical target risk:

\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\left(a(\hat{f}_{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0i}))-y_{0i}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{0i})\right).

(2.9)

Due to missing labels, we require a strategy to estimate the target performance using only the unlabeled covariates.

Methodology

We propose an adaptive method for unsupervised domain adaptation with kernel ridge GLM (KRGLM). The core idea is to construct a computable proxy for the empirical risk
Eq.

2.9

\mathbb{E}(y_{0i}|\bm{x}_{0i})=a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{0i}))
is given by the true model
f^{*}
. We will build an auxiliary “imputation model”
\tilde{f}
trained on part of the labeled source data and then use the estimated conditional mean
\tilde{y}_{0i}=a^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{0i}))
to replace
y_{0i}
in
Eq.

2.9
. The full procedure is detailed in Algorithm

Pseudo-Labeling for Kernel GLM

Soft vs. Hard Labeling.

For classification tasks such as logistic regression, our method utilizes “soft” pseudo-labels
\tilde{y}_{0i}=1/(1+e^{-\widetilde{f}(\bm{x}_{0i})})\in(0,1)
—representing the estimated conditional probability—rather than “hard” labels
\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{f}(\bm{x}_{0i})\geq 0)\in\{0,1\}
. This distinction is critical. While hard pseudo-labeling is common in semi-supervised learning to enforce low-entropy decision boundaries
(
14
;
24
;
15
;
30
;
4
)
, our objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood to recover the true conditional distribution. Using hard labels would destroy the calibration information necessary for minimizing the log-likelihood, introducing severe bias into the risk proxy
Eq.

3.1
by forcing candidate models to fit extreme probabilities (0 or 1) rather than the true underlying uncertainty.

Implementation and Tuning Strategy.

The procedure separates the role of model training (Step 2a) from validation (Step 2b & 4), ensuring that the data used to score the models (
\mathcal{D}_{2}
) is independent of the data used to train them (
\mathcal{D}_{1}
). The computational cost is dominated by solving the kernel GLM optimization, which is efficient for standard losses.
Our theoretical analysis (presented in
Section

4
) provides specific guidance on parameter choices:

•

Split Ratio
: We typically set
n_{1}=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, using half the source data for training candidates and half for imputation. This balanced split is standard in split-sample validation methods.

•

Candidate Grid (
\Lambda
)
: The grid should be sufficiently dense to cover the bias-variance spectrum. We typically set
\Lambda
as a geometric sequence ranging from
O(n^{-1})
(low bias, high variance) to
O(1)
(high bias, low variance), with a cardinality of roughly
O(\log n)
.

•

Imputation Penalty (
\tilde{\lambda}
)
: This is the critical hyperparameter. Unlike standard supervised learning, where
\tilde{\lambda}
would be tuned to minimize source prediction error, here it must be chosen to optimize the

capability of the pseudo-labels. Our theory suggests setting
\tilde{\lambda}\asymp n^{-1}
up to logarithmic factors. This rate typically corresponds to “undersmoothing” the imputation model—prioritizing low bias in the pseudo-labels over low variance—which is crucial for accurately ranking the candidate models on the target domain.

4.1
Assumptions

Our analysis of kernel ridge GLM under covariate shift (
2.1
) necessitates only mild regularity conditions, that one can verify are satisfied in most common scenarios.

First, we need some assumptions on the kernel function
K
. Mercer’s theorem (see for instance

Assumption 4.1

(Kernel regularity)
.

We assume Mercer’s theorem holds under both source and target distributions, impose a uniform boundedness assumptions on the eigenfunctions, and a polynomial decay of the eigenvalues:

•

For any
\bm{x},\bm{z}\in\mathcal{X}
,

can be expanded as

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{j}\phi_{j}(\bm{x})\phi_{j}(\bm{z})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{0j}\phi_{0j}(\bm{x})\phi_{0j}(\bm{z})

where
\left(\mu_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}
and
\left(\mu_{0j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}
are sequences of positive eigenvalues
1
1

1

we order them as
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots>0
and
\mu_{01}\geq\mu_{02}\geq\cdots>0
. Note that they are also the eigenvalues of the second moment operators
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{0}
respectively.
,
\left(\phi_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal sequence of eigenfunctions in

, and
\left(\phi_{0j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal sequence of eigenfunctions in

•

\exists M_{1}<\infty,\forall j,\left\lVert\phi_{j}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq M_{1},\left\lVert\phi_{0j}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq M_{1}

•

\exists A,A^{\prime}>0,\,\exists\alpha\geq 1,\,\forall j\geq 1,\mu_{j}\leq Aj^{-2},\,\mu_{0j}\leq A^{\prime}j^{-2\alpha}

The uniform boundedness of eigenfunctions holds for linear and polynomial kernels on compact domains, as well as for the first-order Sobolev kernel, whose eigenfunctions are sinusoidal.

Remark 4.3

(Interpretation of Effective Sample Size)
.

The quantity

•

If the source dominates the target (i.e.,

•

If the target

Proof Sketch

Our main result hinges on two ingredients.

selection via pseudo-labels under the GLM loss, characterizing how the
imperfection of the pseudo-labels propagates into the excess risk of the
selected model. This result is
generic and independent of the RKHS structure.
Section

5.2

then shows how, in our kernel GLM setting, each term of this oracle inequality
— the imputation error decomposition and the candidate excess risk bounds
— can be controlled via a Taylor expansion of the regularized empirical
loss’s gradient around

Theorem

5.2
as a key intermediate result.
Section

explains how these two ingredients are combined to yield

Theorem

5.1
Generic model selection analysis with GLM loss

At the core of our analysis, we need to understand how the pseudo-labeler impacts the model selection. We consider the task of selecting an optimal candidate from a set of
m
deterministic models
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
to approximate an unknown regression function
f^{*}
, whose conditional mean is characterized through the canonical link
a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}))
. We work under a fixed design of unlabeled samples
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
: by treating the candidate models and design points as deterministic and the imputer
\tilde{f}
as the sole source of randomness, we isolate the effect of pseudo-label errors from the finite-sample fluctuations of the target covariates, which are handled separately as described in
Section

5.3
.

We decompose the pseudo-labels as

\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i}):=f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})+B_{i}+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i},

(5.1)

where
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}=(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i})_{i}
is a centered random vector and
\bm{B}=(B_{i})_{i}
is a deterministic term. They represent the random error and bias of the imputation model.

Recall that the in-sample risk in a GLM framework is given by the negative log-likelihood:

R_{in}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a(f(\bm{x}_{i}))-a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))f(\bm{x}_{i})),

(5.2)

and the in-sample excess risk is thus given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{in}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ \Big[a(f(\bm{x}_{i}))-a(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))-a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))[f(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})]\Big].

(5.3)

Since the true function
f^{*}
is unknown, we will use as in
Eq.

3.1
a proxy for the in-sample excess risk, a pseudo excess risk for model selection purposes:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{in}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ \Big[a(f(\bm{x}_{i}))-a(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i}))-a^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i}))[f(\bm{x}_{i})-\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})]\Big].

(5.4)

The pseudo-labeling strategy is thus to choose

\hat{j}=\argmin_{j\in[m]}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{in}(f_{j}),

(5.5)

and we denote the selected model
\hat{f}=f_{\hat{j}}
.

We will only impose some regularity conditions, that are verified by the covariate shift setting of the previous section, as shown in the appendix.

Assumption 5.1
.

We make the following assumptions:

1.

The image of covariates by the true model are bounded almost surely, i.e. there exists a constant
C>0
such that
|f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})|\leq C
,
\forall i
.

2.

a
is twice differentiable, locally strongly convex and
a^{\prime\prime}
is locally upper bounded and Lipschitz, i.e. there exist finite constants
k>0,K>0,L>0
such that
\forall x,y\in[-2C,2C]
,
K\geq a^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq k
and
|a^{\prime\prime}(x)-a^{\prime\prime}(y)|\leq L|x-y|
.

3.

With probability at least
1-\delta/2
,
\max_{i\in[n]}|\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})|\leq\eta
, for a constant
\eta<\min\{k/L,C,\sqrt{kK}/L\}
.

4.

The imputation random error
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}=(\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{n})^{\top}
is a zero-mean sub-exponential random vector:
\exists\tilde{\sigma}>0
,
\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\leq\tilde{\sigma}
.

Now the objective is to bound the excess risk of the selected model
\hat{f}
, relative to the excess risk of the model selected by an oracle that knows the true function
f^{*}
. We denote by
f_{j^{*}}
this oracle model, with

j^{*}=\argmin_{j\in[m]}\mathcal{R}_{in}(f_{j}).

The following theorem is an oracle inequality for the in-sample excess risk of the selected model:

Theorem 5.1

(Oracle inequality)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then there exists a universal constant
C_{1}>0
such that under
5.1
, the following holds with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{in}(\hat{f})\leq\inf_{\epsilon>0}\left\{(1+\epsilon)\mathcal{R}_{in}(f_{j^{*}})+\frac{C_{1}K}{nk}\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\xi(\tilde{\sigma},\bm{B},\eta,\delta)\right\},\end{split}

(5.6)

with

\xi(\tilde{\sigma},\bm{B},\eta,\delta)=K\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\log^{2}(\frac{m}{\delta})+K\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{L^{2}n\eta^{4}}{k}.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
Appendix

E
. It proceeds by applying the law of cosines for Bregman divergences, which decomposes the true excess risk of any candidate into its pseudo excess risk plus a cross term involving the imputation error
\tilde{f}-f^{*}
, controlled by the local curvature of
a
from
5.1
. The minimality of the selected model’s pseudo risk then transfers to the true risk up to this cross term — a strategy that mirrors the square loss case, but where the Bregman
structure of the GLM loss requires additional care to obtain a sharp bound.

The additive overhead in the oracle inequality
Eq.

5.6
corresponds to the error of the pseudo-labels. It comprises a bias term proportional to
\|\bm{B}\|^{2}/n
, a variance term proportional to
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\log^{2}(m)/n
, and a perturbation term proportional to
L^{2}\eta^{4}
, which arises from the nonlinearity of the GLM loss and is shown to be negligible compared to the other two terms in
Section

5.3
. Note that in the case of the square loss,
L=0
, so the perturbation term vanishes and the third item in
5.1
is no longer necessary. The key asymmetry between the bias and variance terms is that
\tilde{\sigma}
is a scalar sub-exponential proxy for the noise of the imputer, whereas
\|\bm{B}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}^{2}
accumulates the squared pointwise biases over all
n
design points. Consequently, as long as
\log^{2}(m)/n
is small, even a moderate pointwise bias of the imputation model can dominate the variance contribution, making bias the critical quantity to control.

Together, that answers our bias-variance trade-off for the imputation model: we should prioritize an
undersmoothed
imputation model, i.e. with small bias, in order for the pseudo-labels to select a more faithful model.
In the case of kernel GLM, the imputation model should thus be trained with a small regularization parameter.

5.2
Analysis of the KRGLM estimator

We now show how the oracle inequality of
Theorem

5.1
can be applied in our kernel GLM setting by bounding each of its
terms — the imputation error decomposition and the candidate excess risk
bounds — via a Taylor expansion of the regularized
empirical loss’s gradient.

For any positive
\lambda
, the estimator
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
of
Eq.

2.7
is only defined implicitly as the minimizer of a nonlinear objective, unlike the closed-form solution available in kernel ridge regression. For ease of presentation, we derive the following expansion in the finite-dimensional setting with
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the linear kernel, writing
\bm{X}=(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
for the source design matrix,
\bm{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n})^{\top}
for the noise vector, and
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{x}
. The KRGLM minimization problem
Eq.

2.7
becomes

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(a(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta})-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\bm{\theta}\|^{2}\right\}.

(5.7)

Setting the gradient of the associated loss to zero at
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}
and Taylor-expanding around
\bm{\theta}^{*}
yields:

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{*}=-\bm{H}^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\varepsilon}+\lambda\bm{\theta}^{*}\right),

(5.8)

where the data-dependent Hessian is

\bm{H}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{ii}\,\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}+\lambda\bm{I},\qquad D_{ii}=\int_{0}^{1}a^{\prime\prime}(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{\theta}^{*}+t(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{*})))\,dt.

(5.9)

To isolate the dependence on
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}
, we introduce the deterministic Hessian
\bar{\bm{H}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a^{\prime\prime}(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{*})\,\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}+\lambda\bm{I}
, obtained by evaluating
a^{\prime\prime}
at
\bm{\theta}^{*}
rather than along the path. Splitting
Eq.

5.8
accordingly gives the decomposition:

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{*}=-\bigg[\underbrace{\lambda\bm{H}^{-1}\bm{\theta}^{*}}_{\text{bias}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\bar{\bm{H}}^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\text{variance}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}(\bm{H}^{-1}-\bar{\bm{H}}^{-1})\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\text{perturbation}}\bigg].

(5.10)

An equivalent decomposition holds in the general setting via the isometry between the RKHS
\mathcal{F}
and the feature space
\mathbb{H}

Eq.

2.4
, with
\bm{X}
interpreted as a bounded linear operator; see
Section

A.1
.

Recall that our

Kernel logistic regression specifics.

In the following experiments, we focus on kernel logistic regression, for which the log-partition function is
a(u)=\log(1+e^{u})
, and its first derivative is the sigmoid function

that maps real values to
[0,1]
. If the true underlying function is
f^{*}
, then
y_{i}\mid\bm{x}_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\sigma(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})))
. We can thus denote the conditional mean as a probability
p^{*}_{i}:=\sigma(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))
. Equivalently,
f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})=\log(\frac{p^{*}_{i}}{1-p^{*}_{i}})=\text{logit}(p^{*}_{i})
. As such, for any estimator
f
of
f^{*}
, if we denote for each sample
p_{i}=\sigma(f(\bm{x}_{i}))
, the in-sample version of the GLM risk
Eq.

2.5
recovers the usual log-loss with "soft labels" or cross-entropy between the true distribution
p^{*}
and the estimate
p

R_{in}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[a(f(\bm{x}_{i}))-a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))f(\bm{x}_{i})\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-(1-p^{*}_{i})\log(1-p_{i})-p^{*}_{i}\log(p_{i})\right].

7
Conclusion

We presented a principled framework for unsupervised domain adaptation in kernel GLMs, utilizing a split-and-fit strategy to perform target-aware model selection via pseudo-labeling. Our theoretical contributions include non-asymptotic excess risk bounds that characterize adaptation through a novel “effective labeled sample size,” while our empirical results confirm consistent gains over source-only baselines.
Several future directions are worth exploring. Extending our framework to broader function classes and alternative learning procedures beyond ridge regularization would significantly expand its applicability. Also, our current analysis focuses on a one-step imputation strategy. Developing theoretical insights for iterative refinement, where pseudo-labels are progressively updated in multiple rounds (akin to self-training heuristics), could bridge the gap between rigorous statistical bounds and the empirical success of iterative self-training methods.

Remark A.3

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we use

, the excess risk is bounded by:

We analyze the term inside the sum. Observe that for any

Substituting this bound and using the decay assumption
\mu_{0j}\leq cj^{-2\alpha}
, we split the sum at an integer index
J
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{0j}}{k\mu_{0j}+\rho}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{J}1+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}\frac{kcj^{-2\alpha}}{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{k}+\frac{c}{\rho}\sum_{j=J+1}^{\infty}j^{-2\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{J}{k}+\frac{J^{1-2\alpha}}{\rho}.

Substituting this back into

A(\rho)\lesssim\rho+\frac{1}{n_{\text{eff}}}\left(\frac{J}{k}+\frac{J^{1-2\alpha}}{\rho}\right).

.

and constants for the rate):

•

\rho=n_{\text{eff}}^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}
.

•

•

\left\lVert\bm{X}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{*})\right\rVert_{\infty}\left(1-\frac{cC_{1}\log n}{\sqrt{n\lambda^{1/\alpha}}}M_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\log(\frac{8}{\delta})}\right)\leq cM_{1}\left(\sigma\log(2/\delta^{\prime})\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\lambda^{-1/4\alpha}+\lambda^{1/4}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rVert\right).

Thus, if
\frac{cC_{1}\log n}{\sqrt{n\lambda^{1/\alpha}}}M_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\log(\frac{8}{\delta})}\leq\frac{1}{2}
,

\left\lVert\bm{X}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{*})\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2cM_{1}\left(\sigma\log(2/\delta^{\prime})\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\lambda^{-1/4\alpha}+\lambda^{1/4}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rVert\right).

(D.10)

With
\lambda\gtrsim\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2\alpha}\frac{1}{g(n)^{\alpha/2}}
,

\frac{cC_{1}\log n}{\sqrt{n\lambda^{1/\alpha}}}\lesssim cC_{1}g(n)^{1/4}\to 0

so for
n
large enough, we do have
\frac{cC_{1}M_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\log(\frac{8}{\delta})}}{\sqrt{n\lambda^{1/\alpha}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

Finally, we can plug back
Eq.

D.10
into
Eq.

D.9
with probability larger than
1-(n+1)\delta^{\prime}
conditionally on
\mathcal{B}
:

\left|\left\langle\Phi(\bm{x}_{0}),\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rangle\right|\leq c\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n\lambda^{1/\alpha}}}2cC_{1}M_{1}^{2}\sigma\sqrt{\log(\frac{8}{\delta})}\left[\sigma\log(2/\delta^{\prime})\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\lambda^{-1/4\alpha}+\lambda^{1/4}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rVert\right]\right.\\
\left.+\sigma\log(2/\delta^{\prime})\frac{M_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\lambda^{-1/4\alpha}+M_{1}\lambda^{1/4}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rVert\right),

i.e. there is a constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that, for
n
large enough and
\delta^{\prime}=\frac{\delta}{2(n+1)}\leq\frac{\delta}{6}\in(0,1)
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\langle\Phi(\bm{x}_{0}),\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rangle\right|\leq c^{\prime}M_{1}\left(\sigma\log(n/\delta)\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\lambda^{-1/4\alpha}+\lambda^{1/4}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rVert\right)\Bigm|\mathcal{B}\right)\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{2},

(D.11)

and thus

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\langle\Phi(\bm{x}_{0}),\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rangle\right|\leq c^{\prime}M_{1}\left(\sigma\log(n/\delta)\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\lambda^{-1/4\alpha}+\lambda^{1/4}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rVert\right)\right)\geq(1-\frac{\delta}{2})^{2}\geq 1-\delta.

(D.12)

If we set

\lambda=c_{2}\left(\frac{M_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\log(8/\delta)}\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2\alpha}\frac{1}{g(n)^{\alpha/2}}=c_{2}\left(M_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\log(8/\delta)}\right)^{2\alpha}\left(\log(n)\right)^{\alpha(2+\beta/2)}n^{-\alpha},

the probabilistic bound in
Eq.

D.12
becomes:

M_{1}\sigma\log(n/\delta)\frac{n^{-1/4}}{\log(n)^{1/2+\beta/8}}\left(M_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\log(8/\delta)}\right)^{-1/2}+\left(M_{1}\sigma\sqrt{\log(8/\delta)}\right)^{\alpha/2}\frac{\log(n)^{\alpha(1/2+\beta/8)}}{n^{\alpha/4}}M_{1}\left\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rVert.

(D.13)

With
\alpha=1
and
\beta\in(0,8)
, it yields, with probability at least
1-\delta

\left|\left\langle\Phi(\bm{x}_{0}),\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\rangle\right|\lesssim\sqrt{M_{1}^{3}\sigma}n^{-1/4}(\log(\frac{n}{\delta}))^{3/2}\lesssim\sqrt{M_{1}^{3}\sigma}n^{-1/4}(\log(\frac{n}{\delta}))^{3}.

(We only care about the
n
exponent, so we chose
3
as an upper bound to the true
3/2
for the logarithm exponent to lighten the expression.)

∎

Appendix E
Proof of
Section

5.1
’s in-sample oracle inequality

See
Theorem

5.1

Remark E.1
.

Under that event, we also have this intermediate inequality that holds with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}}\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{j^{*}}}+\frac{C_{2}\sqrt{K}}{\sqrt{nk}}\left(\sqrt{K}\tilde{\sigma}\log(\frac{m}{\delta})+\sqrt{K}\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert+\frac{\sqrt{n}\eta^{2}}{\sqrt{k}}\right),

where
C_{2}>0
.

Proof.

We start by introducing some notations for the rest of the proof.
For any function
f
, let
\bm{f}=(f(\bm{x}_{1}),\cdots,f(\bm{x}_{n}))
be the evaluation vector over the samples. We also denote for all
j
,
\mathcal{R}_{j}:=\mathcal{R}_{in}(f_{j})
and
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{j}:=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{in}(f_{j})
.

We use the definition of the selected index
j^{*}=\argmin_{j\in[m]}\mathcal{R}_{j}
. We first need to establish a decomposition result between
\mathcal{R}
and
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
:

Fact E.1
.

Let
f
be any candidate model. Then

\mathcal{R}_{in}(f)=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{in}(f)-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{in}(f^{*})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i}))-a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})))(f(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})).

Proof.

We simply apply the law of cosines of Bregman divergence since

\mathcal{R}_{in}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{a}(f(\bm{x}_{i}),f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))\,,\quad\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{in}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{a}(f(\bm{x}_{i}),\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})).

∎

Applying fact
E.1
to
\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}
, it follows that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\hat{j}}-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(f^{*})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i}))-a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})))(f_{\hat{j}}(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{j}-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(f^{*})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i}))-a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})))(f_{\hat{j}}(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))\,,\quad\forall j\in[m]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{j}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i}))-a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})))(f_{\hat{j}}(\bm{x}_{i})-f_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})),

where the first inequality stems from the minimality of
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\hat{j}}
, and the last equality is obtained by re-applying fact
E.1
to
\mathcal{R}_{j}
.

By the Mean Value Theorem, we can write

a^{\prime}(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i}))-a^{\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))=a^{\prime\prime}(q_{i})(\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))

for some
q_{i}
between
f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})
and
\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})
. Denote
\widetilde{\bm{D}}
the positive diagonal matrix of entries
a^{\prime\prime}(q_{i})
that induces an inner product and a (weighted) norm so that the previous inequality becomes:

\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}\leq\mathcal{R}_{j}+\frac{1}{n}\left\langle\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j},\bm{B}+\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}.

(E.1)

We cannot directly use a sub-exponential tail bound for the inner product because
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{D}}
are correlated. Thus we introduce the intermediate weight matrix
\overline{\bm{D}}
with components
\overline{D}_{ii}=a^{\prime\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))
and use the fact that the perturbation introduced is small thanks to the consistency rate assumed.

In the remainder of this proof, we will use repeatedly the following inequalities linking the
\widetilde{\bm{D}}
-weighted norm, the
\overline{\bm{D}}
-weighted norm, and the standard Euclidean norm:

Fact E.2
.

For all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

1.

\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}\,,\quad\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}\leq\sqrt{K}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}
.

2.

\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\overline{\bm{D}}}\leq\sqrt{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}
.

Proof.

Since, we assume that the
\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{f}}-\bm{f}^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\eta\leq C
, and
\left\lVert\bm{f}^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C
, we can leverage our assumptions on the local properties of the log-partition function
a
in
[-2C,2C]
.
Part 1 directly follows from the definition of the weighted norm and boundedness of
a^{\prime\prime}
. For part 2, we obtain a stronger bound by leveraging the Lipschitzness assumption on
a^{\prime\prime}
and the uniform consistency of the imputation model:

\left|\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\overline{\bm{D}}}^{2}-\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}^{2}\right|\leq\sum_{i}z_{i}^{2}\left|a^{\prime\prime}(q_{i})-a^{\prime\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))\right|\leq\frac{L\eta}{k}\sum_{i}kz_{i}^{2},

hence, via the strong convexity assumption,

\left|\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\overline{\bm{D}}}^{2}-\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{L\eta}{k}\sum_{i}a^{\prime\prime}(q_{i})z_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{L\eta}{k}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}^{2}.

Therefore, since
\frac{L\eta}{k}<1
according to the assumptions,

\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\overline{\bm{D}}}^{2}\leq 2\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}^{2}.

∎

To make progress on inequality
Eq.

E.1
, we decompose the inner product into three terms that we can tackle independently:

\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}\leq\mathcal{R}_{j}+\frac{1}{n}\left[\left\langle\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j},\bm{B}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}+\left\langle\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j},\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\overline{\bm{D}}}+\left\langle\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j},\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}-\overline{\bm{D}}}\right].

•

Cauchy-Schwarz for the first term yields:

\left\langle\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j},\bm{B}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}\leq\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}\leq\sqrt{K}\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert_{2}.

•

Cauchy-Schwarz for the third perturbation term gives:

\left\langle\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j},\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}-\bar{\bm{D}}}\leq\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{D}}-\bar{\bm{D}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{L}{\sqrt{k}}\max_{i\in[n]}\left|\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})\right|\cdot\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}},

where we used
\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{D}}-\bar{\bm{D}}\right\rVert=\max_{i}\left|a^{\prime\prime}(q_{i})-a^{\prime\prime}(f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))\right|\leq L\max_{i}\left|\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})\right|
.
We will see that the consistency rate of our estimator is good enough to make this term of the same order as the two others.

We can improve the
\max_{i\in[n]}\left|\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})\right|\cdot\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{2}
factor by noting that:

By assumption,
\forall i\in[n],\left|B_{i}+\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{i}\right|\leq\eta
, hence
\left|\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{i}\right|\leq\eta+\left|B_{i}\right|
and
\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2(n\eta^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert_{2}^{2})
.

Therefore,

\max_{i\in[n]}\left|\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})\right|^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2(n\eta^{4}+\eta^{2}\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}).

(E.2)

•

For the second term, first bound its sub-exponential norm:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left\langle\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j},\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\overline{\bm{D}}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\overline{\bm{D}}(\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\left\langle\frac{\overline{\bm{D}}(\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j})}{\left\lVert\overline{\bm{D}}(\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j})\right\rVert_{2}},\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\overline{\bm{D}}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{K}\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\overline{\bm{D}}}\left\lVert\left\langle\frac{\overline{\bm{D}}(\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j})}{\left\lVert\overline{\bm{D}}(\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j})\right\rVert_{2}},\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\overline{\bm{D}}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\sigma}\sqrt{K}\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\overline{\bm{D}}}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\sigma}\sqrt{2K}\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}.

Hence, the sub-exponential property and union bound gives that the event

\mathcal{A}=\left\{\forall j\in[m],\left\langle\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j},\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\overline{\bm{D}}}\leq\sqrt{2K}\tilde{\sigma}\log(4m/\delta)\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\widetilde{\bm{D}}}\right\}

holds with probability at least
1-\delta/2
.
We place ourselves under
\mathcal{A}
.

So we obtain with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}\leq\mathcal{R}_{j}+\frac{2\xi}{n}\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\tilde{\bm{D}}},

(E.3)

where
\xi=(\sqrt{2K}\tilde{\sigma}\log(4m/\delta)+\sqrt{K}\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert+\frac{L\max_{i\in[n]}\left|\tilde{f}(\bm{x}_{i})-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})\right|\cdot\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{k}})/2
.

Then using the properties of the energy norm and the boundedness of
a^{\prime\prime}
we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\tilde{\bm{D}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{K}{k}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{\bar{\bm{D}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{K}{k}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}^{*}\right\rVert_{\bar{\bm{D}}}+\left\lVert\bm{f}_{j}-\bm{f}^{*}\right\rVert_{\bar{\bm{D}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{K}{k}}\frac{\sqrt{K}}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\left\lVert\bm{f}_{\hat{j}}-\bm{f}^{*}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\bm{f}_{j}-\bm{f}^{*}\right\rVert\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K}{k}\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{j}}+\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}}\right),

where the last inequality follows from
Eq.

A.2
.

Hence, with
\tilde{\xi}=\frac{K}{k}\xi/\sqrt{n}
, we get:

\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}\leq\mathcal{R}_{j}+2\tilde{\xi}\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{j}}+\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}}\right),

and we can now complete the square:

•

Rearranging:

\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}-2\tilde{\xi}\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}}\leq\mathcal{R}_{j}+2\tilde{\xi}\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{j}}\Leftrightarrow(\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}}-\tilde{\xi})^{2}-\tilde{\xi}^{2}\leq(\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{j}}+\tilde{\xi})^{2}-\tilde{\xi}^{2}.

Thus,

\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}}\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{j}}+2\tilde{\xi}.

Since this is valid for all
j
, an intermediate result is thus:

\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}}\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{j^{*}}}+2\tilde{\xi}.

•

Squaring on both sides, we obtain:

\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}\leq\mathcal{R}_{j^{*}}+2\sqrt{4\tilde{\xi}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{j^{*}}}+4\tilde{\xi}^{2}.

Using the simple inequality
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
and the "Rob Peter to pay Paul" trick with any
\epsilon>0
, i.e.
2ab=2\frac{a}{\epsilon}\epsilon b\leq\frac{a^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\epsilon^{2}b^{2}
, we get:

\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathcal{R}_{j^{*}}+4\tilde{\xi}^{2}(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}).

Finally, to get the final formula of the theorem, we leverage
Eq.

E.2
to bound
\tilde{\xi}^{2}
as:

\tilde{\xi}^{2}\lesssim\frac{K}{kn}(K\tilde{\sigma}\log^{2}(m/\delta)+\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}(K+2L^{2}\eta^{2}/k)+\frac{L^{2}n\eta^{4}}{k}).

Using
\eta\leq\sqrt{kK}/L
and taking the infimum on
\epsilon
yields the final result.

∎

We deduce a bound on the expectation:

Corollary E.1
.

Under the same assumptions as
Theorem

5.1
, we deduce:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}\right]\leq\inf_{\epsilon>0}\left\{(1+\epsilon)\mathcal{R}_{j^{*}}+\frac{C_{1}K}{nk}\left(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\left(K\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{L^{2}n\eta^{4}}{k}+K\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(1+(\log(m)+1)^{2})\right)\right\}.

Proof.

Denoting
W=[\mathcal{R}_{\hat{j}}-(1+\epsilon)\mathcal{R}_{j^{*}}-\frac{C_{1}K}{nk}(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon})(K\left\lVert\bm{B}\right\rVert^{2}+L^{2}n\eta^{4}/k)]/\frac{C_{1}K}{nk}(1+\frac{1}{\epsilon})K\tilde{\sigma}^{2}
, we have from the theorem:

\mathbb{P}\left(W\geq\log^{2}(m/\delta)\right)\leq\delta,\forall\delta\in(0,1).

Let
t=\log^{2}(m/\delta)
such that:

\mathbb{P}\left(W\geq t\right)\leq me^{-\sqrt{t}},\forall t\geq\log^{2}(m).

Integrating to get the expectation yields:

\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(W>t\right)dt\leq\int_{0}^{\log^{2}(m)}1dt+\int_{\log^{2}(m)}^{\infty}me^{-\sqrt{t}}dt=(1+\log(m))^{2}+1.

Rearranging the terms and taking the infimum gives the desired result.
∎

F.1
Lemmas for the GLM estimator

Lemma F.1.1
.

Let the kernel regularity assumption
4.1
hold, with spectral eigendecay of the form
\mu_{j}\leq Aj^{-2\alpha},\alpha\geq 1
, and let
\lambda\in(0,1]
. Then the effective dimension
\displaystyle r(\lambda):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}
satisfies for a universal constant
C>0
:

r(\lambda)\leq C\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}.

In particular, if the trace class operator
\bm{\Sigma}
has eigenvalues
\mu_{j}
, then
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma})=r(\lambda)\lesssim\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}
.

Likewise, for a vector
\Phi(\bm{x})
of the RKHS with basis decomposition
\Phi(\bm{x})=(\sqrt{\mu_{1}}\phi_{1}(\bm{x}),\sqrt{\mu_{2}}\phi_{2}(\bm{x})...)
, where the basis functions are uniformly bounded by
M_{1}
, the quadratic form
\left\langle\Phi(\bm{x}),(\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\Phi(\bm{x})\right\rangle\leq M_{1}^{2}r(\lambda)\lesssim M_{1}^{2}\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}
.

Proof.

Split the sum before and after
j=\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}
and use the asymptotic tail of the series. Without loss of generality, we assume
A=1
(for
A\neq 1
, use
\lambda=\lambda/A
in the formula).

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\leq\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}}\frac{1}{1+\lambda j^{2\alpha}}+\sum_{j>\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}}\frac{1}{1+\lambda j^{2\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\leq\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}}1+\sum_{j>\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}}\frac{1}{\lambda j^{2\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}+\frac{1}{\lambda(2\alpha-1)\lambda^{-(2\alpha-1)/2\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha-1}\lambda^{-1/2\alpha}.

We find that
C=\frac{2\alpha A^{1/2\alpha}}{2\alpha-1}
in general.
∎

Remark F.1
.

By the eigendecay for the source distribution, applying
Lemma

F.1.1
with
\alpha=1
directly gives for
\lambda\in(0,1)

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(\bm{C}_{\lambda})\lesssim\lambda^{-1/2}.

F.2
Technical lemmas

Lemma F.2.1
.

Let
\bm{x}
be a centered sub-exponential vector with
\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert_{\psi_{1}}\leq 1,\forall i\in[n]
,
\bm{A}\succeq 0
a fixed PSD matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then there exists a universal constant
C>0
, such that
\forall\delta\in(0,\frac{2}{e}]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}\leq C\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(\bm{A})\log^{2}(n)\log(\frac{2}{\delta})

when
n\geq\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)^{1/3}
.

Proof.

We will truncate the sub-exponential variables to get sub-gaussian variables and bound the gap between the quadratic forms. Then use lemma D.1 in
(
29
)
.

•

First by definition of sub-exponential,

\forall i,\mathbb{P}\left(\left|x_{i}\right|>C\log n\right)\leq 2n^{-C}

for any constant
C>0
. Hence if we define the truncated version
\bar{\bm{x}}
of components
\bar{x}_{i}:=x_{i}\mathds{1}(\left|x_{i}\right|\leq C\log n)
, by union bound,
\bm{x}=\bar{\bm{x}}
with probability larger than
1-2n^{1-C}
. Denote this event
\mathcal{A}
. Also, by definition of
\bar{\bm{x}}
, we have immediately that
\left\lVert\bar{\bm{x}}\right\rVert\leq C\sqrt{n}\log n
almost surely.

•

\bar{\bm{x}}
is not centered anymore so let its mean be
\bm{\mu}
. Then
\forall i,\left|\mu_{i}\right|=\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}\mathds{1}(\left|x_{i}\right|\leq C\log n)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}\mathds{1}(\left|x_{i}\right|>C\log n)\right]
since
\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}\right]=0
. So Cauchy-Schwarz yields:

\left|\mu_{i}\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|x_{i}\right|^{2}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(\left|x_{i}\right|>C\log n\right)}\leq 2\sqrt{2}n^{-C/2}\lesssim n^{-C/2},

where the first factor is bounded by a constant by the sub-exponential property, and the second obeys the tail bound mentioned before. Hence:

\left\lVert\bm{\mu}\right\rVert\lesssim\sqrt{n}n^{-C/2}=n^{(1-C)/2}.

•

Next define the centered version
\tilde{\bm{x}}:=\bar{\bm{x}}-\bm{\mu}
. It is centered and bounded almost surely:
\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2C\log n
hence it is sub-gaussian with
\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim C\log n
. Thus its quadratic form satisfies a tail bound as proposed in lemma D.1 of
(
29
)
for a universal constant
C^{\prime}>0
,
\forall\delta\in(0,\frac{2}{e}]
, with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
:

\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{x}}\leq C^{\prime}\left\lVert\tilde{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(\bm{A})\log(2/\delta)\lesssim\log^{2}(n)\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(\bm{A})\log(2/\delta).

Denote this event
\mathcal{B}
.

•

Finally, we can bound the difference
\left|\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{x}}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}\bar{\bm{x}}\right|=\left|\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{A}(2\bar{\bm{x}}-\bm{\mu})\right|\leq\left\lVert\bm{A}\right\rVert(2\left\lVert\bm{\mu}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\bm{\mu}\right\rVert^{2})\leq\left\lVert\bm{A}\right\rVert(n^{1-C}+n^{1-C/2}\log n)
.

Putting everything together, under the event
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}
which holds with probability larger than
1-2n^{1-C}-\frac{\delta}{2}
by union bound, we get:

\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}=\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{x}}+(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}\bar{\bm{x}})+(\bar{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}\bar{\bm{x}}-\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\top}\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{x}})\lesssim\left\lVert\bm{A}\right\rVert(n^{1-C}+n^{1-C/2}\log n)+\log^{2}n\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(\bm{A})\log(2/\delta)
.

Choosing
C=4
, since
\log(2/\delta)\geq 1
for
\delta\leq 2/e
, and
\left\lVert\bm{A}\right\rVert\leq\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(\bm{A})
, yields:

\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}\lesssim\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(\bm{A})\log(\frac{2}{\delta})\left(\log^{2}(n)+\frac{1}{n^{3}}+\frac{\log(n)}{n}\right)

with probability larger than
1-\frac{2}{n^{3}}-\frac{\delta}{2}
.
For
n\geq\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)^{1/3}
, the probability is larger than
1-\delta
as wanted. For
\delta=0.05
, this threshold is just
n\geq 5
.
Removing the negligible terms yields the desired result.

∎

Lemma F.2.2
.

Let
2.1
and
4.1
hold, with
M_{2}
the bound on the covariates
\Phi(\bm{x}_{i})
. Choose
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\delta\in(0,1/e]
, and
\lambda\geq\frac{4M_{2}^{2}\log(14n/\delta)}{\gamma^{2}n}
. Then the following event holds with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\mathbb{P}\left((1-\gamma)(\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})\preceq\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\lambda\bm{I}\preceq(1+\gamma)(\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Under that event, the following inequalities are true:

1.

For any vector
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{H}
,
\left\langle\bm{a},(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{a}\right\rangle\leq(1-\gamma)^{-1}\left\langle\bm{a},(\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{a}\right\rangle
.

2.

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}})\leq M_{1}^{2}(1-\gamma)^{-1}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma})
, where
M_{1}>0
is the uniform bound on the eigenfunctions.

Proof.

The first part is just a repetition of corollary D.1 in
(
29
)
. Then, the first inequality is a direct corollary from the main sandwich inequality, by definition of the PSD order.
Finally, for the second inequality, observe that the sandwich inequality implies:

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}})\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}).

Define
\tilde{\Phi}_{i}:=(\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1/2}\Phi(\bm{x}_{i})
such that

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}})=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1/2}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}(\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1/2})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}_{i}\right\rVert^{2}.

Since by uniform boundedness of the eigenfunctions,

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}_{i}\right\rVert^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}\left|\phi_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})\right|^{2}\leq M_{1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\lambda}=M_{1}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}),

we get:

\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}})\leq M_{1}^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}((\bm{\Sigma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}),

and the result follows.

∎

Through the effective dimension defined as
n_{\text{eff}}=\sup\bigg\{t\leq n|t\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\preceq n\bm{\Sigma}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{k}\bm{I}\bigg\}

, we can relate expressions depending on

G.1
Implementation details

We optimize the ridge-regularized kernel GLM
Eq.

2.7
in function space
\textbf{f}=\bm{K}\bm{\alpha}
via Fisher scoring
(
19
)
, i.e. Newton’s method with the Hessian replaced by the expected Hessian (Fisher information). At each iteration, with current evaluations
\eta_{i}=f(\bm{x}_{i})
, mean
\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[y_{i}\mid\eta_{i}]
, and variance
V_{i}=\text{Var}[y_{i}\mid\eta_{i}]
, we compute the standard GLM weights
\bm{W}={\rm diag}((\frac{d\mu_{i}}{d\eta_{i}})^{2}/V_{i})
and pseudo-responses
z_{i}=\eta_{i}+(y_{i}-\mu_{i})/\frac{d\mu_{i}}{d\eta_{i}}
. The resulting iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) update requires solving
(\bm{K}\bm{W}+n\lambda\bm{I})\textbf{f}=\bm{K}\bm{W}\bm{z}
.
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the kernel matrix introduced in equation
Eq.

2.8
.

To ensure stable convergence of our conjugate gradient
(
10
)
(CG) solver, we avoid extreme weight scalings by defining
\bm{S}=\bm{W}^{1/2}
and substituting
\bm{u}=\bm{S}\textbf{f}
to solve the equivalent symmetric positive definite system:

(\bm{S}\bm{K}\bm{S}+n\lambda\bm{I})\bm{u}=\bm{S}\bm{K}\bm{W}\bm{z}

We then recover
\textbf{f}=\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{u}
.

For large-scale problems, forming the dense
n\times n
matrix
\bm{K}
introduces an
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
memory bottleneck. We circumvent this by implementing the inner CG solver using KeOps
(
6
)
for on-the-fly GPU kernel-vector products. This strategy reduces the memory footprint to
\mathcal{O}(nd)
without altering the exact IRLS update equations. Full algorithmic details and solvers are available at
https://github.com/nathanweill/KRGLM/
.

Let us explain in more details the experiments of

S

where
t(x)\in\{0,1\}
yields standard log-loss and
t(x)\in[0,1]
yields the corresponding
soft-label
