Abstract
. Multi-attribution learning (MAL), which enhances model performance by learning from conversion labels yielded by multiple attribution mechanisms, has emerged as a promising learning paradigm for conversion rate (CVR) prediction. However, the conversion labels in public CVR datasets are generated by a single attribution mechanism, hindering the development of MAL approaches. To address this data gap, we establish the M ulti- A ttribution Ben C hmark ( MAC ), the first public CVR dataset featuring labels from multiple attribution mechanisms. Besides, to promote reproducible research on MAL, we develop PyMAL , an open-source library covering a wide array of baseline methods. We conduct comprehensive experimental analyses on MAC and reveal three key insights: (1) MAL brings consistent performance gains across different attribution settings, especially for users featuring long conversion paths. (2) The performance growth scales up with objective complexity in most settings; however, when predicting first-click conversion targets, simply adding auxiliary objectives is counterproductive, underscoring the necessity of careful selection of auxiliary objectives. (3) Two architectural design principles are paramount: first, to fully learn the multi-attribution knowledge, and second, to fully leverage this knowledge to serve the main task. Motivated by these findings, we propose M ixture o f A symmetric E xperts ( MoAE ), an effective MAL approach incorporating multi-attribution knowledge learning and main task-centric knowledge utilization. Experiments on MAC show that MoAE substantially surpasses the existing state-of-the-art MAL method. We believe that our benchmark and insights will foster future research in the MAL field. Our MAC benchmark and the PyMAL algorithm library are publicly available at https://github.com/alimama-tech/PyMAL . 0 0 footnotetext: ∗ Equal contribution. † Corresponding Author.

Background and Related Works

2.1.
CVR Prediction and Attribution Mechanism

Conversion rate (CVR) prediction
(
Chapelle 2014
;
Lu et al. 2017
;
Ma et al. 2018a
;
Chan et al. 2023
)
plays a vital role in online advertising.
Researchers have pursued numerous avenues to improve CVR prediction models, such as joint learning with other tasks
(
Ma et al. 2018a
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Luo et al. 2026
)
, model structure
(
Zhuang et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
)
, and delayed feedback modeling
(
Yang et al. 2021
;
Gu et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
.
Although
attribution mechanisms

(
Shao and Li 2011
;
Yao et al. 2022
)
, namely the rules for allocating conversion credits to ad clicks, directly determine the learning goals of CVR prediction models, they are overlooked in existing studies, which all rely on a single attribution mechanism.

Chen et al. 2025
proposes multi-attribution learning (MAL) and achieves significant performance gains on in-house data, highlighting the potential of the MAL paradigm.
Nevertheless, existing public CVR datasets
(
Chapelle 2014
;
Ma et al. 2018a
;
Zhu et al. 2018
)
all use a single attribution mechanism to generate labels, hindering advances in MAL.
To the best of our knowledge, we release the first CVR dataset with multi-attribution labels, and further contribute the first algorithmic codebase and in-depth analysis for MAL approaches.

2.2.
Multi-Task Learning for Recommendation

Multi-task learning (MTL)
(
Zhang and Yang 2021
;
Caruana 1997
;
Misra et al. 2016
;
Sheng et al. 2021
)
seeks to train a single model for predicting multiple targets simultaneously.
Researchers have made extensive efforts to apply MTL to recommendation tasks, ranging from general structure design
(
Ma et al. 2018b
;
Tang et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
to specific task grouping
(
Ma et al. 2018a
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2026
)
.

Chen et al. 2025
regard MAL as an MTL problem and apply classical MTL models to MAL, such as MMoE
(
Ma et al. 2018b
)
and PLE
(
Tang et al. 2020
)
, where they underperform the NATAL model tailored for MAL.
In this study, we will delve deeper into the strengths and weaknesses of the MTL methods within the MAL context, aiming to collect insights for future architectural design.

3.
The
MAC
Benchmark

3.1.
The MAL Problem Setup

In CVR prediction, the conventional paradigm assumes a
single attribution mechanism

\mathcal{A}
(e.g., last-click or linear) that assigns a binary label
y^{\mathcal{A}}\in\{0,1\}
to each ad click, indicating whether the click is credited for a subsequent conversion within a fixed attribution window. However, this view is inherently limited: different attribution mechanisms encode distinct perspectives on user intent and touchpoint contribution, and no single rule fully captures the true conversion causality.
To overcome this limitation, we formalize
multi-attribution learning (MAL)
as follows. Let
\mathcal{M}=\{\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{K}\}
be a set of
K
attribution mechanisms (e.g., last-click, first-click, linear, and data-driven attribution (DDA)).
For each click instance
\mathbf{x}
, there is a set of
continuous attribution weights

\{w^{\mathcal{A}_{1}},w^{\mathcal{A}_{2}},\dots,w^{\mathcal{A}_{K}}\}
, where
w^{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
denotes the contribution of the click to related conversions under the mechanism
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, computed over a fixed attribution window.

In MAL, one attribution mechanism
\mathcal{A}_{t}\in\mathcal{M}
is designated as the
target attribution mechanism
according to business requirements.
Predicting the conversion labels under
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is the
primary task
, while the remaining
K-1
mechanisms serve as
auxiliary attribution mechanisms
, and the labels yielded by them are
auxiliary targets
. The goal is to train a prediction model
f_{\theta}(\cdot)
such that the estimate
\hat{y}^{\mathcal{A}_{t}}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})
accurately approximates the true attribution weight
w^{\mathcal{A}_{t}}
by jointly leveraging supervision signals from all
K
attribution views. This setup enables the model to acquire a more holistic understanding of post-click conversion behavior.

3.2.2.
Data Sampling Criteria

MAC
covers 21 consecutive days of traffic on Taobao, one of the world’s largest e-commerce platforms.
To ensure that the dataset size is suitable for academic research, we performed stratified sampling, where we assigned higher
sampling rates to highly active users and lower rates to less active ones.
The resulting
MAC
dataset includes 0.8 million users, 79 million clicks, and 9.6 million distinct items.
As shown in Table
1
,
MAC
is comparable to or larger than existing datasets
(
Chapelle 2014
;
Ma et al. 2018a
;
Zhu et al. 2018
)
.

3.2.3.
Feature and Label Schema

Each ad click sample in
MAC
contains three groups of signals:

•

Categorical features
: 7 user features including user ID and profile features, 10 item features such as item, shop, category, and adgroup IDs, and 3 context features such as the ad scenario ID.

•

Behavior sequence features
: the user purchase item sequence with a maximum truncated length of 20.
For each item, we provide the item, shop, and category IDs along with its visual similarity score to the target advertised item, using the features from an image encoder pre-trained through contrastive learning
(
Sheng et al. 2024
)
.

•

Multi-attribution conversion labels
:
MAC
provides continuous attribution weights for each ad click under all four attribution mechanisms as stated in §
3.2.1
, derived from the conversion paths within a fixed, confidential attribution window.
We use the CausalMTA model
(
Yao et al. 2022
)
as the DDA model and use direct conversion attribution, which means attributing conversions only to clicks on ads within the same item.

3.2.4.
Data Compliance and Availability

Our
MAC
benchmark has undergone rigorous anonymization to remove personally sensitive information.
All personally identifiable information and business details, such as user, shop, and adgroup identifiers, are irreversibly hashed.
The dataset is constructed from historical traffic logs and does not contain real-time operational metrics or business conditions due to the user sampling procedure.
We release
MAC
as a public benchmark to support future research on CVR prediction on
https://huggingface.co/datasets/alimamaTech/MAC
.

3.3.
Evaluation Metrics

Following industrial practice
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, we regard CVR prediction as a weighted binary classification problem with the sample definition:

•

Positive Samples.
Samples with positive attribution weights are positive samples, where attribution weights serve as sample importance weights.

•

Negative Samples.
Samples receiving zero attribution weights are treated as negative samples.

For evaluating model performance, we report the widely-adopted
AUC
and Group AUC (
GAUC
) metrics
(
Schütze et al. 2008
;
Zhou et al. 2018
;
Sheng et al. 2023
;
Bian et al. 2022
)
of the primary task.
AUC is a widely used ranking metric that evaluates a model’s ability to distinguish positive and negative samples, making it well-suited for CVR prediction.
The definition of AUC is given in Eq. (
1
)

(1)

\displaystyle\text{AUC}=\frac{1}{n_{+}n_{-}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{-}}\left[\mathbb{I}(y_{i}^{+}>y_{j}^{-})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}(y_{i}^{+}=y_{j}^{-})\right]

Here
n_{+}
and
n_{-}
denote the numbers of positive and negative samples, respectively,
y_{i}^{+}
and
y_{j}^{-}
stand for the predicted conversion probabilities for the
i
-th positive and
j
-th negative sample, respectively, and
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function.

Moreover, we report the GAUC metric, which captures the model’s ability to rank ad clicks within individual user groups and serves as the primary evaluation criterion in our production system.
It has been empirically shown to align more closely with online performance than AUC.
The definition of GAUC is given in Eq. (
2
):

(2)

\displaystyle\textrm{GAUC}=\frac{\sum_{u=1}^{U}\#\textrm{click}(u)\times\textrm{AUC}_{u}}{\sum_{u=1}^{U}\#\textrm{click}(u)}

Here
U
represents the number of users,
\#\textrm{click}(u)
denotes the number of clicks for the
u
-th user, and
\textrm{AUC}_{u}
is the AUC computed on the samples from the
u
-th user.

The PyMAL Library

To foster reproducible and comparable research on multi-attribution learning (MAL) for CVR prediction, we establish
PyMAL
, an open-source Python library built on top of PyTorch
(
Paszke et al. 2019
)
available at
https://github.com/alimama-tech/PyMAL
.
It provides a unified, modular, and extensible framework that implements a comprehensive suite of MAL baselines, and it can be easily extended to incorporate ongoing efforts in the MAL field.
The library supports rapid prototyping and fair comparison of MAL approaches, which paves the way
for studies on MAL in the community.

We categorize the implemented models in
PyMAL
into three representative families as follows, reflecting the evolution of modeling paradigms in MAL.
Figure

First, the single-attribution learning baseline.

The
BASE

Regarding model architecture, it comprises target attention
(
Zhou et al. 2018
)
for sequence modeling, SimTier for multimodal modeling
(
Sheng et al. 2024
)
, and an MLP predictor for outputting the predicted CVR value, following prior industrial practice
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
.

Second, the multi-task learning (MTL) models adapted for MAL.

These models jointly learn the primary task together with one or more auxiliary attribution tasks, using shared or structured representations to capture cross-task knowledge:

•

Shared-Bottom

(
Caruana 1997
)
: A classic architecture featuring a shared bottom embedding layer followed by task-specific output towers.

•

MMoE

(
Ma et al. 2018b
)
: A mixture-of-experts model that uses task-specific gating networks to dynamically combine shared expert subnetworks, enabling flexible knowledge sharing.

•

PLE

(
Tang et al. 2020
)
: A progressive layered extraction architecture that explicitly separates shared and task-specific experts to reduce negative transfer and improve task cooperation.

•

HoME

(
Wang et al. 2025
)
: An enhanced MoE variant that introduces Self-Gate and Feature-Gate mechanisms to adaptively modulate expert selection based on input feature granularity, coupled with Swish activation for better nonlinearity modeling.

Last, the state-of-the-art NATAL model tailored for MAL.

NATAL
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, short for k

Concretely, it incorporates three key components: (1)

Primary Target Predictor

5.
Method: Mixture of Assymetric Experts

5.1.
Motivation: Structure Design Principles

To understand the pros and cons of prior methods, we record their performance under both the target and auxiliary attribution mechanisms.
As shown in Table
3
, the advanced MTL model equipped with the MoE structure, e.g., PLE and HoME, not only raises the primary task performance, but also improvese the auxiliary task metrics compared with Shared-Bottom.
This is reasonable, as it is known that MoE can capture task commonalities and reduce gradient conflicts, thereby improving the performance of all subtasks in MTL
(
Ma et al. 2018b
;
Tang et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
.
In contrast, NATAL discards the MoE structure and introduces asymmetric knowledge transfer from auxiliary towers to the main task tower for enhancing main task-prioritized knowledge utilization
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
.
As a result, although NATAL gives the best main task performance among baselines, it underperforms PLE and HoME in terms of auxiliary task performance.
Therefore, we contend that a successful MAL model should meet two principles:

Structure Design Principles

1.

Fully learn multi-attribution knowledge underlying multiple attribution mechanisms, as done in PLE and HoME;

2.

Fully leverage multi-attribution knowledge in a main-task-prioritized manner, as done in NATAL.

.

6.1.2.
Objective Formulation

The CVR prediction tasks under all attribution mechanisms are formulated as binary classification, where the loss is defined as:

(3)

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[y_{i}\log p_{i}+(1-y_{i})\log(1-p_{i})\Big],

where
N
is the batch size,
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is the ground-truth label of the
i
-th instance, and
p_{i}\in(0,1)
is the predicted conversion probability.
The overall training objective combines the primary task loss under the target attribution mechanism with auxiliary losses from other attribution mechanisms:

(4)

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{primary}}+\lambda\sum_{k}\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}^{(k)},

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{primary}}
denotes the primary task loss, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}^{(k)}
denotes the loss for the
k
-th auxiliary task.
Each loss term is instantiated as the binary cross-entropy loss in Eq. (
3
).
To find the best
\lambda
value for each model, we search over the set
\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4\}
based on validation performance.
We also test an automatic method GCS
(
Du et al. 2020
)
dynamically setting different weights for each task, but we find it does not make significant contributions, which will be shown in §
7.2
.

6.1.3.
The Choice of Auxiliary Tasks

For each target attribution setting, we further search over the combination of auxiliary attribution objectives and report the results under the best setting.

6.2.1.
The Generality of MAL

Considering that
Chen et al. 2025
only demonstrate the contribution of MAL under last-click and DDA attribution mechanisms, it remains unknown whether MAL brings stable performance gains across different target attribution mechanisms.
It is an important question as other attribution mechanisms, e.g., linear attribution, are also widely adopted in recommendation systems.
As shown in Table
4
, the cutting-edge MAL models, e.g., NATAL
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
and our
MoAE
, achieve substantial improvements under all four target attribution settings: last-click, first-click, DDA, and linear.
The performance lifts across diverse attribution mechanisms demonstrate that
MAL is a general, robust, and highly promising learning paradigm for conversion modeling
.

Furthermore, regarding the improvements under different target attribution mechanisms, we observe that
last-click and DDA settings see the most significant GAUC
increases at 2.12pt and 1.74pt, respectively.
In contrast, in the
linear attribution
setting where MAL does not increase the number of positive samples, we observe
a smaller GAUC uplift
at 0.80pt, which suggests that the causal relationships in DDA labels and the positional information underlying last-click and first-click labels also make contributions.

Notably, in the
first-click
setting,
MAL yields the smallest GAUC improvement
at 0.34pt.
We attribute this more modest improvement to the inherently noisy nature of first-click labels.
Specifically, the extended time gap between the initial click and the final conversion introduces many confounding factors (e.g., subsequent promotions) unknown to the model.
This weakens the label’s correlation with the user’s original intent, thereby constraining the potential improvement from MAL under the first-click setting.
The fact that the first-click setting yields the lowest absolute AUC and GAUC scores, as shown in Table
4
, underscores the noisy property of the first-click labels, a claim we will further substantiate with empirical evidence in the Appendix
B
.

6.2.2.
Impact Analysis on User Groups

To answer
RQ2
, we present the AUC lift brought by our
MoAE
over the single-attribution baseline across

in Figure

Specifically, the conversion path complexity is proxied by the ratio of the number of positive samples under the linear-click setting to that under the last-click setting.
A higher value of this ratio indicates that users click more ads on average before conversion, signifying a longer conversion path.

We observe that
multi-attribution learning delivers greater AUC improvements for users with more complex conversion paths
, a trend that holds across most of the evaluated target attribution mechanisms: last-click, linear, and DDA.
This correlation suggests that
MAL is particularly beneficial for users with complex multi-touch conversion journeys
,
where richer information gains underlie multi-attribution labels.
In contrast, when first-click is the target attribution mechanism, the AUC improvements brought by MAL do not exhibit a clear monotonic trend with respect to conversion path complexity, which is likely caused by the inherently noisy nature of first-click labels.

6.2.3.
The Effect of Auxiliary Objective Choice

We progressively add different auxiliary targets to
MoAE
under each target attribution mechanism and report the resulting model performance in Table
5
, where we make two key observations as follows.

First, the model’s performance lift scales with the number of auxiliary targets
under most of target attribution mechanisms, and the Cartesian product auxiliary target (CAT)
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
yields a further significant improvement, demonstrating
the potential of incorporating more sophisticated auxiliary targets
.

Second, in contrast, the first-click CVR prediction model is sensitive to the choice of auxiliary objectives
.
Concretely, only the last-click auxiliary target yields significant performance gains, while other choices of auxiliary targets undermine model performance.
We speculate that this phenomenon is due to the unique position of the last-click target.
Because it is temporally closest to the conversion, it offers the greatest discrepancy from the first-click signal, which in turn helps the model learn the user’s intrinsic intent more effectively.

6.2.4.
The Superiority of Our
MoAE
Model

As shown in Table
4
,
MoAE consistently outperforms all baselines under all four target attribution mechanisms.

Compared with the existing state-of-the-art model NATAL
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
,
MoAE
achieves GAUC gains of +0.23pt, +0.13pt, +0.27pt, and +0.39pt under last-click, first-click, DDA, and linear settings, respectively.
Note that a GAUC increase of 0.10pt can be regarded as a confidently significant improvement on large-scale benchmarks like our
MAC

The superiority of
MoAE
substantiates the power of combining the two architecture designs principles in §
6.2.4
, namely
(1) fully learning multi-attribution knowledge
underlying multi-attribution targets and
(2) fully leveraging multi-attribution knowledge
to maximize the main task performance.
We are confident that these findings will serve as valuable guidelines for future MAL architectural designs.

7.1.
Effect of Parameter Growth

Given that MAL models such as NATAL
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
and our
MoAE
introduce more parameters for fitting multiple attribution labels compared to the single-attribution baseline, the performance growth of MAL models may be attributed to
parameter scaling
rather than
multi-attribution knowledge utilization
.
To verify the source of performance gains brought by MAL, we disentangle the effect of multi-attribution labels (
mal
for short) from parameter scaling by setting the auxiliary tasks weights to zeros, and list the resulting model metrics in Table
6
.

We observe that
vanilla parameter scaling yields no or marginal GAUC improvements
, and
the performance growth brought by MAL mainly results from multi-attribution supervision signals.

Specifically, when the auxiliary task weights are set to zero, namely the “w/o mal” variants in Table
6
, although the parameter scales of resulting models are larger than the single-attribution Base and equal to corresponding MAL models, they significantly underperform the MAL counterparts learning from auxiliary labels, and perform almost on par with the Base model.
The trend is consistent across the multi-task family (Shared-Bottom, MMoE, PLE, and HoME), NATAL, and our
MoAE
model, suggesting that harnessing multi-attribution knowledge underlying diverse attribution mechanisms contributes to the performance advantage of MAL models over the single-attribution baseline.

7.2.
Effect of Auxiliary-Task Learning Methods

Auxiliary-task learning (ATL)
(
Du et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. [n. d.]
;
Yu et al. 2020
)
aims to maximize the target task performance by leveraging the knowledge underlying auxiliary tasks, which aligns with our MAL problem setup.
ATL is a developed research area with various approaches, such as gradient manipulation
(
Yu et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. [n. d.]
)
, auxiliary loss weight adjustment
(
Lin et al. 2019
;
Du et al. 2020
;
Shi et al. 2020
)
, and auxiliary task grouping
(
Fifty et al. 2021
;
Song et al. 2022
)
, and we believe that incorporating ATL techniques is promising for future work on multi-attribution learning.
In this study, we take the first step to investigate the effect of ATL techniques on multi-attribution learning models.
Concretely, we implement the classic GCS
(
Du et al. 2020
)
and PCGrad
(
Yu et al. 2020
)
approaches as
optional training features in our PyMAL
library, and examine their effects on MAL models.
GCS adjusts auxiliary loss weights using gradient similarities between target and auxiliary targets, and PCGrad eliminates gradient conflicts through gradient projection.
Their details are given in Appendix
C
.

We present the results in Table
7
and make two key observations.

First
, GCS and PCGrad yield small performance gains when attached to weak baselines such as Shared-Bottom and MMoE, showing their effectiveness in mitigating negative transfer, namely the negative effects of auxiliary tasks on the target task.

Second
, when applied to strong MAL models such as NATAL and our
MoAE
tailored for maximizing the target task performance, GCS and PCGrad show marginal negative effects.
Overall, our
MoAE
without GCS or PCGrad achieves the best performance among all model variants.
These results indicate that
well-designed architectures are more effective than ATL techniques based on gradient projection and loss weight adjustment in multi-attribution learning for CVR prediction
.
We believe that it is easy to incorporate more advanced ATL approaches to our
PyMAL
library, which are promising for future study.

8.
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
MAC
, the first public CVR prediction benchmark providing
conversion labels derived from multiple attribution mechanisms
.
This dataset enables systematic investigation into
multi-attribution learning (MAL)
, a promising paradigm previously hindered by the unavailability of multi-attribution conversion labels.
Through comprehensive experiments on
MAC
using our open-source
PyMAL
library, we gather three key insights.

First
, MAL consistently enhances model performance across diverse target attribution settings, with the most significant gains observed for users exhibiting long and complex conversion paths.

Second
, the choice of auxiliary objectives in MAL is crucial and task-dependent.

Third
, effective MAL architectures must satisfy two core design principles.(i) fully learn the knowledge embedded in multi-attribution labels, and (ii) effectively leverage this knowledge in a main-task-prioritized manner.
Motivated by these insights, we propose
MoAE
, an effective MAL model that integrates an MoE backbone for knowledge acquisition with an asymmetric transfer module for main-task-centric utilization, whose superiority is demonstrated by extensive empirical evaluation.

We believe the
MAC
benchmark, the
PyMAL
toolkit, and the gathered actionable insights will serve as valuable resources for the research community, accelerating future innovation in multi-attribution learning and more holistic conversion modeling.
For future work, we believe that incorporating stronger foundation model capabilities, such as chain-of-thought reasoning
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, and more comprehensive conversion objectives
(
Zeng et al. 2025
)
holds great promise.

Appendix A
Details of Auxiliary Objective Search

For each model under each attribution setting, we start from the primary objective alone and greedily add auxiliary objectives in a forward-selection manner: (1) at each step, we temporarily add one candidate auxiliary objective, train the model, and measure the GAUC improvement on the validation day; (2) we then select the auxiliary objective that brings the largest positive gain and permanently include it into the auxiliary objective set; (3) this process is repeated on the remaining candidates until either all auxiliary objectives have been used or no further GAUC improvement is observed. This greedy search is conducted independently under each target attribution mechanism.

In our experiments, this greedy search yields consistent patterns across settings. For all models under the last-click, linear, and DDA attribution mechanisms, the optimal configuration includes all candidate auxiliary objectives. Under the first-click attribution mechanism, however, the best configuration for all models uses only the last-click objective as an auxiliary signal.

Appendix C
Details of ATL Methods

Here we provide the detailed formulations of the two auxiliary-task learning (ATL) techniques used in our experiments, namely Gradient Cosine Similarity (GCS)
(
Du et al. 2020
)
and PCGrad
(
Yu et al. 2020
)
. Both methods aim to better exploit auxiliary tasks by manipulating gradients during optimization, thereby alleviating negative transfer and enhancing the primary task performance.

Throughout this section, we consider a generic learning setup with one primary task and multiple auxiliary tasks. Let
\theta
denote the shared model parameters,
L_{\text{primary}}
the loss of the primary task, and
\{L_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
the losses of
K
auxiliary tasks.

C.1.
Gradient Cosine Similarity (GCS)

GCS dynamically adjusts the weights of auxiliary losses by measuring the alignment between the primary task gradient and each auxiliary task gradient. The core idea is that auxiliary tasks whose gradients are positively aligned with the primary task gradient should receive higher weights, while those that conflict with the primary task should be down-weighted or suppressed.

C.1.1.
Basic Formulation

At each training step, we first compute the gradients of the primary and auxiliary losses with respect to shared parameters:

(5)

\displaystyle g_{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}L_{\text{primary}},

(6)

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}L_{k},\quad k=1,\dots,K.

For each auxiliary task
k
, we compute the cosine similarity between
g_{\text{p}}
and
g_{k}
:

(7)

s_{k}=\cos\left(g_{\text{p}},g_{k}\right)=\frac{\langle g_{\text{p}},g_{k}\rangle}{\|g_{\text{p}}\|\;\|g_{k}\|+\varepsilon},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product and
\varepsilon
is a small constant to avoid division by zero.

C.1.2.
Auxiliary Loss Reweighting

In our implementation, we adopt a simpler reweighting scheme based directly on the cosine similarities
\{s_{k}\}
. Instead of clamping and normalizing them, we pass each similarity through a sigmoid function to obtain a bounded weight in
(0,1)
:

(8)

\alpha_{k}=\sigma(s_{k})=\frac{1}{1+e^{-s_{k}}},\quad k=1,\dots,K.

Intuitively, auxiliary tasks whose gradients are more strongly aligned with the primary gradient (larger
s_{k}
) receive larger weights, while conflicting tasks (smaller or negative
s_{k}
) are down-weighted but never completely discarded.

With this choice, the total loss used in training is

(9)

L_{\text{GCS}}=L_{\text{primary}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}L_{k},

where we no longer apply the global auxiliary coefficient
\lambda
in Eq. (4) of the main text.

C.2.
PCGrad: Gradient Surgery for Multi-Task Learning

PCGrad
(
Yu et al. 2020
)
is a gradient manipulation method designed to reduce gradient conflicts among tasks. Instead of reweighting losses, PCGrad directly projects task gradients to avoid negative interference, while preserving as much task-specific information as possible.

C.2.1.
Per-Task Gradient Computation

As in GCS, we first compute gradients for the primary and auxiliary tasks:

(10)

\displaystyle g_{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}L_{\text{primary}},

(11)

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}L_{k},\quad k=1,\dots,K.

For notational convenience, we collect all task gradients into a set:

(12)

\mathcal{G}=\{g_{\text{p}},g_{1},\dots,g_{K}\}.

PCGrad processes each task gradient sequentially to remove components that conflict with others.

C.2.2.
Gradient Projection Procedure

For each gradient
g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}
, PCGrad randomly samples another gradient
g_{j}\in\mathcal{G}
,
j\neq i
, and checks their inner product. If the inner product is negative, indicating that
g_{j}
is pushing parameters in a direction that harms task
i
, PCGrad projects
g_{i}
onto the normal plane of
g_{j}
:

(13)

\tilde{g}_{i}=\begin{cases}g_{i}-\displaystyle\frac{\langle g_{i},g_{j}\rangle}{\|g_{j}\|^{2}+\varepsilon}g_{j},&\text{if }\langle g_{i},g_{j}\rangle<0,\\
g_{i},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This process is repeated for multiple randomly sampled
g_{j}
(often looping over all other tasks in random order), yielding a modified gradient
\tilde{g}_{i}
in which mutually conflicting components are suppressed.

After the projection procedure, we obtain a set of conflict-reduced gradients
\{\tilde{g}_{\text{p}},\tilde{g}_{1},\dots,\tilde{g}_{K}\}
.

C.2.3.
Aggregated Update Direction

PCGrad then aggregates the modified gradients by simple averaging:

(14)

g_{\text{PCGrad}}=\tilde{g}_{\text{p}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{g}_{k},

and uses
g_{\text{PCGrad}}
as the final update direction for shared parameters
\theta
:

(15)

\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,g_{\text{PCGrad}},

where
\eta
is the learning rate.

In practice, we only apply PCGrad to the shared parameters, while keeping the gradients of task-specific towers unchanged, following standard practice in multi-task learning.

Appendix D
Details of CAT Label Construction

The Cartesian-style Auxiliary Target (CAT)
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
transforms the set of
N
binary attribution labels into a single multi-class label by enumerating all possible joint outcomes across attribution mechanisms.

Concretely, for each sample we start from the binary labels

\mathbf{A}=\big(A_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{N-1}\big),\quad A_{i}\in\{0,1\},

where
A_{i}
is the conversion indicator under the
i
-th attribution mechanism (e.g., last-click, first-click, DDA, linear). The CAT label is then defined as the integer encoding of
\mathbf{A}
in little-endian binary form:

(16)

\mathbf{O}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}A_{i}\cdot 2^{i},\quad\mathbf{O}\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{N}-1\}.

Algorithm
1
gives the corresponding pseudo-code. In our experiments with four attribution mechanisms (last-click, first-click, DDA, linear), CAT becomes a 16-class classification problem. For instance, the combination
(1,0,1,0)
is mapped to class

\mathbf{O}=1\cdot 2^{0}+0\cdot 2^{1}+1\cdot 2^{2}+0\cdot 2^{3}=5.

Computation of the
CAT
Label

Array
\mathbf{A}
of length
N
with elements in
\{0,1\}
