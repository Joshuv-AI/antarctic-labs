Evaluating the role of silica fume and Alccofine in concrete mechanical and durability strength using machine learning models
Authors: Siva Shanmukha Anjaneya Babu Padavala, Ashok Amgoth, Siva Avudaiappan, Deepak Kothuri, Abraham Mengistu Gashe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCement manufacturing is a significant source of CO2 emissions in the world, with approximately 8% of the global emissions being due to cement manufacturing, mainly through clinker manufacturing. In order to reduce its environmental effects, this paper examines the use of silica fume (SF) and Alccofine (AF) as additional cementitious materials (SCMs) to improve the mechanical and durability characteristics of concrete. The first stage involved the addition of SF at 5, 7.5 and 10% replacement levels to establish the optimum dosage and the second stage involved the addition of AF at 5, 10 and 15% with the optimum SF content maintained. Mechanical properties such as compressive strength, flexural strength, and split tensile strength and durability properties such as water sorptivity and rapid chloride penetrability (RCP) were tested. The best combination of 7.5% SF and 10% AF was 34.7% increase in compressive strength, 16.1% increase in flexural strength and 16% increase in split tensile strength over the control mix. This blend showed a 43.7% decrease in water sorptivity and a 42.8% decrease in RCP in terms of durability, which is significant in terms of resistance to water ingress and chloride penetration. Compressive strength was well predicted by machine learning models, with the most significant variables being cement content and AF. Life cycle assessment (LCA) indicated that the optimum blend had a global warming potential that was reduced by a factor of about 10% relative to OPC. These findings indicate that the integration of SF and AF does not only improve the performance of concrete but also minimizes its environmental impact, which is a promising avenue to sustainable and high-performance construction materials.Keywords: Silica fume, Alccofine, Microstructure, Life cycle assessment, Machine learningSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Cement manufacturing is a significant contributor to global CO2 emissions and climate change since it emits high levels of CO2 during the chemical process of cement manufacturing and during the energy consumption in the manufacturing process. Cement is the third-largest anthropogenic source of CO2 emissions after fossil fuels and land-use change, contributing approximately 7–8% of global CO2 emissions. The chemical reaction (calcination) of limestone (calcium carbonate) into clinker is the main source of CO2, and the byproduct is CO2. This process itself contributes to a large portion of cement production emissions1–3. Fossil fuels are burnt in large quantities to heat kilns to high temperatures, which further contributes to CO2 emissions. The two highest sources of emissions in the cement industry are fuel burning and electricity consumption4–6. The production of cement has increased at a high rate particularly in the emerging economies and this has caused a drastic rise in emissions. Cumulative cement-related CO2 emissions have increased more than 70% since 1990, and the global process emissions are approximately 1.5 Gt CO2/year, or about 4% of all fossil fuel emissions1,2,7. The second-largest industrial CO2 emitter is the cement industry, which contributes approximately 25% of the global industrial CO2 emissions7. The growth of infrastructure in developing areas is accelerating, and the future decarbonization is becoming more difficult3,4,7. The CO2 emissions of cement production are a major contributor to global warming and climate change, and thus it is a key area of focus in terms of emissions reduction1,2,5,8. Although cement can recapture part of the CO2 throughout its lifecycle (the so-called sponge effect), this is only compensated by a small portion of the emissions, and significant technological and efficiency gains are needed to achieve net reductions5,6,9.

SCMs are substances, usually industrial or agricultural byproducts, which are incorporated into concrete to partially substitute Portland cement. SCMs are important in minimizing the environmental impact of cement production through the reduction of CO2 emissions, natural resources conservation, and waste reduction. SCMs consist of fly ash, SF, ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), and rice husk ash. These are usually byproducts of other industries (e.g., power plants, steel manufacturing, agriculture) and are mixed with cement to create concrete10–13. The direct substitution of a part of cement with SCMs will result in a direct decrease in the quantity of clinker (the primary source of CO2 in cement) required, which will result in substantial decreases in greenhouse gas emissions10–12,14–16. SCMs can be used to save non-renewable raw materials by replacing some of the cement content with recycled or waste materials12,13. The use of industrial and agricultural byproducts as SCMs will divert waste to landfills and minimize environmental pollution12,13. SCMs are practical in structural applications because they can sustain or even enhance the strength and durability of concrete at the optimum replacement levels10,14–16. The environmental advantages are determined by the nature and percentage of SCM applied, transportation and processing effects14,17,18.

The fine particle size, high reactivity, and high pozzolanic properties make SF and AF highly desirable as SCMs in concrete. The two materials are highly sought after in high-performance and sustainable construction due to their ability to increase the mechanical strength and durability of concrete. Amorphous silica is highly abundant in SF and reacts with calcium hydroxide during cement hydration to produce more calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H), the primary strength-providing substance in concrete. This pozzolanic reaction results in a more dense microstructure and enhanced durability19–23. AF is composed of ultra-fine particles that are rich in calcium and silica, which facilitates quick hydration and pozzolanic reactions. Although its pozzolanic activity can be a little less than SF, AF remains an important contributor to strength gain and microstructural refinement24–27. Both SF and AF enhance compressive, tensile, and flexural strength. At low replacement levels (approximately 5%), SF is especially effective, whereas AF exhibits steady strength gains to 15% replacement, particularly under ideal curing conditions19,20,22–27. AF enhances early strength because of its quick hydration, which is appropriate in high-strength and fast-track construction25–27. AF enhances the elastic modulus and energy absorption capacity of concrete, which leads to improved mechanical performance24. Both SF and AF reduce the permeability of concrete, increasing resistance to water intrusion, chloride, and sulfate attack20–23,25,26. SF, specifically, enhances resistance to aggressive environments (sulfates, chlorides), increasing the service life of concrete structures2021. The pozzolanic reactions of SF and AF lead to a denser, less porous matrix, which is central to enhanced durability19–23.

In order to fully evaluate the performance of SF and AF blended concretes, both mechanical and durability properties should be evaluated. These properties define the strength, resilience, and serviceability of the concrete in different environments. Both SF and AF have a strong positive effect on compressive strength, particularly at the optimum replacement levels (e.g., 5–10% in SF, up to 15% in AF)19,28–34. The split tensile strength of SF and AF blends is better, which means that they have better crack resistance28–34. Both SCMs enhance flexural strength, which is essential in structural applications19,28–33. SF and AF decrease water uptake, resulting in reduced permeability and enhanced durability19,30,33,35. Both SCMs increase chloride resistance, minimizing the chances of corrosion of steel reinforcement30,34,35. SF, specifically, enhances sulfate and acid attack resistance, increasing concrete life19,34.

Machine learning (ML) is changing the way concrete properties are predicted and mixture designs are optimized, providing faster, more accurate, and cost-effective alternatives to conventional laboratory testing. ML models are able to quickly estimate important concrete properties and inform optimal mix designs, eliminating the necessity of large-scale physical experiments. ML algorithms are capable of forecasting such properties as compressive strength, flexural strength, workability, and durability based on available datasets of mix proportions and test outcomes, which reduces the time-consuming and expensive lab tests36–42. Even complex or novel mixtures36,38,39,41. Advanced ML models (e.g., XGBoost, neural networks, adaptive boosting) are highly accurate (often R2 > 0.9 and mean absolute percentage error < 5%), even in predicting concrete properties. ML is superior to traditional empirical or statistical models in modeling nonlinear and multi-variable relationships between mix components and concrete performance37–40. ML, together with metaheuristic algorithms (e.g., particle swarm optimization, genetic algorithms), allows the optimization of several goals simultaneously, including strength, workability, cost, and environmental impact36,42–45. ML-based optimization can find mix designs that achieve high performance and low carbon emissions and material costs, which can be used to support sustainable construction36,43,45. Pareto fronts and optimization frameworks generated by ML assist engineers in choosing the optimal mix design to use in a particular project based on the project requirements prior to the commencement of construction42–44.

Recent studies on SF and AF as SCMs in concrete demonstrate that there are encouraging gains in strength and durability, yet there are a number of gaps and limitations in knowledge. The number of studies that specifically examine the combined use of SF and AF is limited, and there are still uncertainties about their microstructural mechanisms and long-term performance. The majority of the research is on SF or AF alone, and few studies have investigated their interaction in concrete or mortar mixes46,47. The existing literature on their combination is frequently restricted to particular mechanical characteristics (e.g., compressive strength), and less focus is on overall durability or microstructural examination46,47. Recent reviews note that there is a general dearth of research on new SCM combinations, such as SF and AF, and that more research is needed in this field48,49. Although the pozzolanic reaction of SF and its effect on pore structure are well known, the specific effect of SF and AF on hydration products, pore refinement, and long-term microstructural development remains poorly understood49. The relationship between SF and AF, particularly their joint influence on the development of calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and other hydration products, is under-researched. More sophisticated microstructural investigations (e.g., SEM, XRD) are required to elucidate these mechanisms and their consequences on durability49. The majority of the research is on short-term mechanical properties, and little information is available on long-term durability, environmental exposure, and lifecycle performance49. Standardized mix design techniques and optimization strategies of SF and AF blends are lacking, and it is hard to generalize results48,49. There is limited literature that assesses the environmental and economic effects of the use of both SF and AF49.

The primary goal and importance of the recent research on SF and AF in concrete are to fill the gap in the knowledge of their combined effects, maximize their application to achieve better performance, and close the gaps in the knowledge of both mechanical and durability properties. The literature on the use of SF and AF together in concrete is limited. The recent studies are intended to study the effect of varying ratios of SF and AF in combination on strength, durability, and microstructure in a systematic manner, which has not been studied in detail previously. Research is aimed at testing a broad spectrum of compressive, tensile, and flexural strength, water absorption, and durability of different curing periods and mix ratios. This assists in developing a more comprehensive picture of the interaction of SF and AF in concrete. Research aims to determine the best replacement levels of SF and AF that will maximize strength and durability and minimize adverse effects such as dilution or loss of workability. Experimental data is used in some studies to develop empirical or machine learning models that can effectively predict concrete properties based on SF and AF content, eliminating the necessity of large-scale laboratory testing. The microstructural mechanisms of the observed improvements, including denser particle packing and improved hydration, are not fully understood and are the subject of investigations.

Despite the extensive research available on SF and AF individually as supplementary cementitious materials, studies investigating their synergistic interaction in ternary blended concrete systems remain limited, particularly in the context of simultaneously evaluating mechanical performance, durability behaviour, microstructural characteristics, environmental sustainability, and machine learning-based prediction frameworks within a single integrated study. Most previous investigations primarily focused on isolated strength characteristics or durability indicators without establishing a comprehensive relationship between microstructural densification, statistical validation, sustainability assessment, and predictive modelling.

The novelty of the present study lies in the development and evaluation of an integrated ternary concrete system incorporating optimized combinations of SF and AF, coupled with a multi-dimensional assessment framework involving experimental testing, SEM-based microstructural interpretation, statistical significance analysis using ANOVA and Tukey HSD methods, life cycle assessment using SimaPro, and machine learning-based compressive strength prediction. Unlike previous studies that mainly reported conventional strength enhancement trends, the present research establishes quantitative relationships between supplementary cementitious material interactions, pore refinement mechanisms, chloride resistance, environmental impact reduction, and data-driven predictive modelling.

Fine aggregate

Fine aggregate was locally available river sand that met the requirements of IS 383 (Zone II). The sand was of a fineness modulus of 2.83 and water absorption of 1.04%. It was not clayey, silty, or organic.

Coarse aggregate

Coarse aggregate was crushed stone of a maximum size of 20 mm and meeting the requirements of IS 2386. The aggregate was of good grade, clean and devoid of deleterious materials.

Water

Both mixing and curing were done using potable water that was free of sediments or other impurities.

Mix proportions

The concrete mix design was developed in accordance with IS 10262:2019 guidelines for concrete mix proportioning51. Ordinary Portland cement was used as 100% of the control mixture. Cement was then substituted with SF by 5, 7.5 and 10% weight. The optimum SF substitution mixture was then taken as a base to add AF at 5, 10 and 15% by weight of cement. The SF replacement level of 7.5% was selected as the base mixture for subsequent incorporation of AF because preliminary experimental results demonstrated that this dosage provided the optimum balance between workability and mechanical performance among the SF-only mixes. The SF7.5 mix exhibited the highest compressive strength compared to SF5 and SF10 mixes, indicating enhanced pozzolanic activity and matrix densification at this replacement level. Therefore, 7.5% SF was considered the optimum binary blend and was subsequently used as the reference mixture for evaluating the combined synergistic effect of SF and AF in ternary blended concrete systems. Specimen preparation

The raw materials were measured and combined in a mechanical mixer according to the IS 516. Water was added slowly to obtain the desired workability and homogeneity. The newly prepared concrete was then poured into standard molds (150 mm cubes compressive strength, 100 × 300 mm cylinders for split tensile strength and 100 × 500 mm beams for flexural strength). The specimens were tamped with a tamping rod and stored under normal curing conditions (at 27 °C and 90% RH) over 24 h. The specimens were then demoulded and left in water to cure until the corresponding testing ages of 7 and 28 days.

Rapid chloride penetrability

The RCP was tested according to ASTM C 1202 (2005) to determine the resistance of concrete to chloride ion penetration. The specimens (100 mm in diameter and 50 mm thick) were put in a special cell, and the two sides of the specimen were exposed to sodium chloride (NaCl) solution and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution respectively. The cell was subjected to 60 volts DC over a period of 6 h. The amount of charge transferred (in coulomb) in this time was measured, which is the diffusivity of chloride ions through the concrete.

Results and discussions

Workability

Workability is a very important parameter in determining the performance of fresh concrete because it directly determines the ease of mixing, placing, compacting and finishing operations. It indicates the fluidity and cohesiveness of the mix and is usually measured by the slump test. This paper evaluated the workability of different concrete mixtures to determine the impact of using SF and AF as partial substitutes of Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC). The experimental findings are depicted in Fig. 1, which shows the slump values of all mixes. The findings clearly show that the addition of SF alone had a significant positive effect on the workability of concrete relative to the control OPC mix. The mix with 5% SF had an increase in slump of 7.4% and the mixes with 7.5% and 10% SF had an increase of 14.8% and 21.3% respectively. Two important mechanisms can be identified as the cause of this progressive increase in slump with increasing SF content. To begin with, the micro-filler effect of SF allows smaller particles to occupy the spaces between cement grains, which enhances the density of the particle packing and decreases the internal friction of the mix. Secondly, the round shape of SF particles serves as miniature ball bearings in the mix, which makes it easier to flow and less resistant to movement. The combination of these effects leads to a more homogeneous and cohesive matrix with enhanced flowability.

Workability of OPC, SF, and SF-AF concrete mixes.

Additional workability gains were realized when AF was added to SF. The highest workability was observed in the mix with 7.5% SF and 5% AF, and the slump was increased by 28.7% relative to the OPC control. This significant enhancement is explained by the ultrafine particle size and smooth surface texture of AF, which offers better lubrication in the mix. The observed enhancement in workability due to SF incorporation is consistent with previous studies reporting improved particle packing and rheological modification in SCM-based concrete systems. Similar observations regarding the influence of SF on fresh concrete behavior and matrix refinement were reported in recent investigations on sustainable cementitious composites54,55. The particles of AF are dispersing agents, which decrease the thickness of the water film between particles and permit easier compaction and less entrapped air. The synergistic effect between SF and AF at these levels improves the rheological behavior and the particle packing density of the mix resulting in optimum workability, especially in low water-to-binder ratio systems. The workability gains were however not maintained at higher AF dosages. The slump values were reduced significantly when the AF content was raised to 10% and 15% with only 5.5% and 1.8% improvements, respectively, over the control mix. This reduction is attributed to the large surface area and reactivity of excess ultrafine particles, which elevates the total water requirement of the mix. The higher the dosage of AF, the denser and more viscous the mix becomes as the free water is rapidly absorbed by the ultrafine particles, and the slump decreases. When this happens, the resulting mix viscosity reduces flowability, and the fresh concrete becomes harder to place and compact without extra water or admixtures. These results highlight the significance of maximizing the ratios of SCMs in concrete. Although moderate concentrations of SF and AF enhance workability by increasing physical and rheological properties, high doses may be detrimental because of the high water requirement. Thus, it is necessary to balance the proportions to obtain a balanced mix that provides workability and mechanical performance without reducing the efficiency of placement.

Compressive strength

One of the most important mechanical properties that determine the structural performance of concrete is compressive strength. It gives an understanding of the capacity of the material to resist the axial loads and the general quality of hydration, microstructural integrity, and material packing. The effect of SF and AF on the compressive strength of concrete after 28 days was systematically tested in this study. As shown in Fig. 2, the findings indicate a significant increase in strength of all the modified mixes relative to the control mix that had Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) alone. Out of all the combinations tested, the ternary mixture with 7.5% SF and 10% AF was the most effective mix design, with a 34.7% increase in compressive strength compared to OPC at 28 days. This dramatic improvement is credited to the synergistic action of the pozzolanic reactivity and particle refinement provided by both SCMs. Other ternary blends, namely 7.5% SF and 5% AF and 7.5% SF and 15% AF, also showed significant strength gains of 28.4% and 25.7, respectively. These combinations were slightly below the optimum mix, but they still performed better than the OPC and binary blends, which proves the effectiveness of using AF in combination with SF. The binary mixes with SF alone exhibited moderate but consistent gains in compressive strength. The best binary dosage of 7.5% SF gave a strength gain of 12.6% with 5% and 10% SF dosages also playing a positive role. These mixes have been improved mainly because of the high pozzolanic activity of SF, which reacts with calcium hydroxide (CH), a byproduct of cement hydration, to produce more calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H). The compressive strength enhancement observed in the present study is in agreement with previous research demonstrating that SF contributes to secondary C-S-H formation, pore refinement, and improved interfacial transition zone characteristics in high-performance concrete systems54,55.

Compressive strength of OPC, SF, and SF-AF concrete mixes.

The high performance of the SF-AF ternary mixes can be attributed to both chemical and physical processes. As an ultrafine, low-calcium silicate material, AF not only participates in further C-S-H formation through pozzolanic reactions but also is an important part of the filler effect. It has a very fine particle size that enables it to fill microvoids in the matrix, enhancing the packing density and decreasing the connectivity of capillary pores. This results in a more compact and less permeable microstructure, which is desirable in terms of strength and durability. Moreover, the synergistic action of SF and AF increases the overall reactivity and efficiency of binder use, which guarantees the improved consumption of CH and the maximum strength-yielding compounds. Nevertheless, the results also indicate that the strength gains do not grow indefinitely as more ultrafine materials are added. At increased AF contents (15%), strength was still greater than OPC, but the percentage increase was a little less than that of the 10% AF mix. This is explained by the fact that the water demand is higher and the workability is lower at higher fine contents, which can disrupt proper compaction and hydration. The high viscosity due to high fines may also impede the even distribution of hydration products resulting in localized weaknesses and poor performance of the binder. Thus, although both SF and AF have a positive effect on the development of strength, their doses should be optimized to prevent the decrease in their effect. To conclude, the addition of 7.5% SF and 10% AF in concrete gives the best balance between pozzolanic reactivity, microstructural refinement, and workability, which leads to the maximum compressive strength in this study. The results highlight the importance of developing ternary cementitious systems to attain high performance and still have realistic fresh-state characteristics.

Flexural strength

Flexural strength is an important mechanical property that indicates the capacity of the material to withstand tensile stresses caused by bending. It is especially significant in structural components like beams, slabs, and pavements where tension is formed at the lower fiber levels. To gain insight into the effect of these SCMs on the post-crack performance and fracture resistance of concrete, flexural strength of concrete mixes with different proportions of SF and AF was tested at 28 days in this study. Figure 3 shows the results graphically. The experimental results showed a tendency that was similar to compressive strength. The ternary blend with 7.5% SF and 10% AF had the best flexural strength with a 16.1% increment compared to the control OPC mix at 28 days. Such a significant enhancement is a sign of an optimized balance between the densification of the matrix and microstructural reinforcement. Other ternary combinations also demonstrated significant improvements: the combinations with 7.5% SF + 5% AF and 7.5% SF + 15% AF gave 13.4% and 12.3% improvements, respectively, which proves that the combination of both SCMs has a positive effect on flexural performance. Nevertheless, of these, the 10% AF mixture proved to be the most effective in terms of strength and consistency. Blends with SF alone also showed progressive increases in flexural strength with increase in SF content, although to a smaller degree than the ternary systems. These improvements in these binary mixes are explained by the pozzolanic activity and micro-filler effect of SF that enhance the internal bonding and decrease the creation of voids. The ultrafine and spherical characteristics of SF particles allow them to be more dispersed in the cement matrix, resulting in a decrease in pore volume and the quality of the interface between the cement paste and the aggregates. Similar improvements in flexural behavior associated with SF incorporation have also been reported in previous studies due to enhanced matrix densification and crack resistance mechanisms54,55.

Flexural strength of OPC, SF, and SF-AF concrete mixes.

The improved flexural behavior of the ternary mixes can be largely explained by the improvements in the Interfacial Transition Zone (ITZ) and the general densification of the matrix. The use of SF and AF is advantageous to the ITZ, which is generally the weakest zone in concrete. The high surface area and reactivity of SF occupy micro-pores and enhance the cement-aggregate interface by facilitating the development of more calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H). This leads to a stronger and crack resistant matrix. This effect is further enhanced by AF because of its ultrafine size and increased calcium content, which not only leads to further C-S-H formation but also to the homogenization of the pore structure. The synergistic effect of SF and AF therefore greatly increases crack-bridging capacity and tensile stress resistance, which are essential in better flexural performance. Nevertheless, the consequences of excessive dosage of ultrafine materials should also be taken into account. High levels of SF or AF may cause an overly dense matrix with lower internal porosity, which, though advantageous to strength, may cause brittle behavior at flexural loads. A very dense matrix may reduce the energy absorption capacity of concrete, decreasing its ductility and making it more prone to sudden failure during bending. These tendencies were noted in this study in the 15% AF mix where the strength gain was a little less than that of the 10% AF mix indicating diminishing returns beyond the optimum dosage. Finally, 7.5% SF and 10% AF is the best balance of strength, ductility, and matrix refinement, which offers the best flexural strength without reducing the toughness of the composite. These results support the possibility of blended SCM systems to improve the mechanical behavior of concrete, especially in structural use where tensile and bending resistances are important.

Split tensile strength

Split tensile strength is a very important mechanical property of concrete that indicates its capacity to withstand indirect tensile stresses. Although concrete is weak in tension, its tensile loading behavior is critical to the study of its cracking resistance and general structural behavior, especially in pavements, tanks, and prestressed elements. The split tensile strength test gives information on the cohesiveness of the matrix, the bond strength at the interfacial transition zone (ITZ), and the quality of microstructure. The 28-day split tensile strength of concrete mixes with SF and AF was compared in this study and the findings are shown in Fig. 4. The results show that there is a significant improvement in split tensile strength when SCMs are added. The ternary mixture with 7.5% SF and 10% AF had the best tensile strength, which was 16% higher than the control OPC mix. This high performance highlights the good synergy between the two SCMs in enhancing the tensile properties of the concrete. Other ternary blends with 5% and 15% AF, respectively, with 7.5% SF also demonstrated considerable improvements, with 13% and 12% increases, respectively. These values are a little below the optimum mix, but they are a significant improvement over the control. Concrete mixes with SF alone showed moderate yet steady increases in split tensile strength. The tensile performance increased with the increase in dosage of SF between 5% and 10%. This is mostly due to the pozzolanic activity and ultrafine particle size of SF that leads to refinement of pores and interfacial bonding. The spherical shape of SF also contributes to the uniform distribution of particles in the matrix, reducing stress concentrations that normally cause crack formation during tensile loading.

Split tensile strength of OPC, SF and AF mixes.

The increase in split tensile strength of the ternary mixes is mainly due to the increase in the microstructure and interfacial transition zone (ITZ). The ITZ, the space between the coarse aggregates and the cement paste, is usually less dense and porous than the bulk matrix. This is the area where crack formation under tensile stress usually begins. Because of its high surface area and pozzolanic properties, SF occupies microvoids and reacts with calcium hydroxide to form more calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H), which decreases porosity and enhances the bond strength between paste and aggregates. AF also enhances this process by adding to the filler effect and secondary C-S-H formation, which leads to a denser and more uniform ITZ. These microstructural enhancements enable a more homogeneous distribution of stress in the matrix, which in effect postpones the formation of microcracks and retards their growth during tensile loading. As a result, the concrete has a better crack resistance and tensile capacity, which is manifested in the strength gains. However, it is worth mentioning that the advantages of SCM incorporation have optimal dosage levels. Beyond some threshold, particularly when using highly reactive and ultrafine materials such as AF, the concrete can have lower workability and higher mix viscosity. Such conditions may impede effective compaction, resulting in trapped air and micro-defects, which eventually counterbalance the mechanical advantages of enhanced binder chemistry. This is demonstrated by the slightly poorer performance of the 15% AF mix than the 10% AF mix. Overall, the findings indicate clearly that the combination of SF and AF can be used judiciously to achieve substantial improvements in split tensile strength through the increase in matrix density and ITZ quality. The 7.5% SF and 10% AF mix provides a good combination that balances strength improvement and fresh concrete workability. These results highlight the strategic importance of SCMs in the customization of tensile behavior of concrete in structural applications where cracking and deformation resistance under indirect tension is critical.

Water sorptivity

Water sorptivity is a very important parameter that shows the ability of concrete to resist the entry of water by capillary action. It is a good indicator of the permeability of the material and is directly related to the long-term durability of the material. The high water sorptivity is normally linked with high vulnerability to harsh environmental factors, such as freeze-thaw cycles, chloride penetration, sulfate attack, and reinforcement corrosion. In this regard, the analysis of the water absorption characteristics of concrete with SF and AF can be regarded as an important contribution to the understanding of the possibility of the concrete to have a longer service life. Figure 5 shows the 28-day sorptivity of all mixes tested. The findings indicate that there is a great enhancement in water absorption resistance when SCMs are added. The ternary mixture of 7.5% SF and 10% AF had the lowest water sorptivity value, which was 43.7% lower than the control mixture prepared using Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) only. Other ternary mixtures, with 5% and 15% AF and 7.5% SF, also showed significant sorptivity reductions-37.5% and 40.0, respectively. These values emphasize the high performance of SCM-based mixes in reducing the water transport by the cement matrix. Blends with SF alone also registered an increase in water sorptivity, but the degree of decrease was not as high as in the ternary systems. With the increase in dosage of SF, there was a gradual decrease in sorptivity, which validated the contribution of SF in the refinement of the pore structure and decrease in permeability. Nevertheless, the performance improvements were much higher when AF was added to SF, which suggests a synergistic effect of the two SCMs. The observed decreases in water sorptivity are mainly explained by various microstructural improvements that are enabled by the synergistic effect of SF and AF. The two materials are very reactive and have ultrafine particle sizes, which allow them to fill the voids and refine the capillary pore network in the concrete matrix. The pozzolanic reactions cause SF and AF to absorb calcium hydroxide (CH) that is emitted during cement hydration, resulting in the production of more calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H). This extra C-S-H gel helps in densifying the matrix, decreasing the connectivity of the pores and breaking the continuity of the capillaries- hindering the flow of water in the concrete.

Specifically, AF is crucial in increasing the impermeability of concrete because of its very fine particle size and high calcium content. It serves as a reactive pozzolan and as a micro-filler, which enhances the packing of the particles and removes weak areas in the microstructure to a great extent. Combined with SF, it leads to a finer pore structure with reduced interconnected capillaries, which leads to lower sorptivity values and enhanced barrier properties. It is worth mentioning that the decrease in water sorptivity due to the incorporation of SCM is not only useful in terms of water resistance but also has a wider scope of application in terms of durability performance. Reduced rate of water absorption means that fewer deleterious ions like chlorides and sulfates, which cause corrosion and chemical degradation of steel reinforcement, are transported. As a result, the concrete will be less susceptible to the deterioration processes caused by moisture, which will eventually increase its durability and minimize the maintenance needs in hostile conditions. To sum up, the findings in Fig. 5 affirm that the synergistic effect of SF and AF is significantly superior to the effect of either of the two materials in the reduction of water sorptivity. The best combination of 7.5% SF and 10% AF gave the best reduction, which means that there was an optimal balance between pozzolanic reactivity and filler efficiency. The reduction in water sorptivity observed in the ternary blends is consistent with earlier findings that SF significantly reduces pore connectivity and capillary absorption through microstructural densification54,55. These results support the use of optimized ternary binder systems in the production of high-performance, low-permeability concrete that can be used in the construction of long-lasting infrastructure.

Rapid chloride penetrability

RCP is a standard test to determine the resistance of concrete to ingress of chloride ions, which is a key parameter in determining the durability of concrete, especially in environments where chloride ingress is prevalent, such as marine environments or de-icing salt treatment. One of the major causes of reinforcement corrosion is the penetration of chloride ions, which causes serious degradation of concrete structures. Thus, to increase the long-term performance and service life of concrete, it is necessary to enhance its resistance to chloride penetration. Figure 6 illustrates the RCP outcomes of the different concrete mixes with SF and AF at 28 days. The findings are clear that there is a significant decrease in the chloride ion penetration of the modified concrete mixes, especially those that have a mixture of SF and AF. The most effective mix, which was 7.5% SF and 10% AF, showed a 42.8% decrease in the values of RCP relative to the control mix (OPC), indicating the great influence of these additional materials on enhancing the resistance of concrete to chloride infiltration. Other ternary combinations of 5% AF and 15% AF also showed significant improvements, with 35.7% and 39.2% decreases, respectively, further confirming the increased protective effect of the combination of both SF and AF. Conversely, blends with SF alone also registered gains in chloride resistance, albeit less than the ternary blends. The decreased performance of SF can be explained by the fact that it can enhance the densification of the matrix and the pozzolanic reactivity but without the additional advantages of the ultra-fine particle size and high filler capacity of AF. Although SF continues to add to the total strength and impermeability of the concrete, the mixture with AF leads to a more sophisticated pore structure and better chloride ion resistance. Previous studies have similarly reported that SF incorporation enhances chloride penetration resistance by refining the pore structure and reducing ionic transport pathways within the cementitious matrix54,55.

RCP of OPC, SF and AF mixes.

The decrease in the RCP values of the modified mixes is mainly attributed to the refinement of the concrete microstructure. Both SF and AF are ultrafine materials that when added to the concrete mix, they add to the filler effect, which fills the micro-pores in the cement paste. Also, these materials are involved in pozzolanic reactions with calcium hydroxide (CH) generated during cement hydration, resulting in the creation of more calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) gel. The C-S-H formation not only enhances the strength of the matrix but also decreases the overall porosity of the concrete, which in effect seals the routes through which the chloride ions would otherwise enter the concrete. The synergistic action of SF and AF is particularly noticeable in the fact that they can collaborate to create a denser and more impermeable matrix, which greatly restricts the movement of chloride ions. The fine particle size of AF also contributes to the better packing of the particles, and the reduced capillary pore space, and the pozzolanic activity of the two materials results in the increased C-S-H formation. This mixture produces a concrete mix that is not only stronger but also more resistant to environmental degradation, which makes it very appropriate to be used in aggressive environments that are likely to be exposed to chloride. These results highlight the significance of applying both SF and AF together because their two effects, which are to increase impermeability and decrease ion transport, give concrete better durability than traditional OPC-based mixes. These modified mixes have a longer service life and higher structural integrity by greatly enhancing the resistance to chloride penetration, and are therefore the best candidates to infrastructure that is exposed to harsh environments, including coastal and de-icing salt environments. To sum up, the RCP outcomes in Fig. 6 clearly show the synergistic advantages of using SF and AF in concrete. The ternary mixes are better than the binary SF mixes, which proves that the addition of the two materials results in increased resistance to chloride ion penetration, which enhances the durability and sustainability of concrete structures.

SEM is among the most efficient methods of studying the microstructure of SCM based concrete because it generates high-resolution images of electrically conductive samples. The improved microstructure is due to the pozzolanic reactions between the SCMs (SF and AF) and the calcium hydroxide (CH) that is emitted during cement hydration. These reactions use CH and produce more C-S-H and other hydration products, polishing the pore structure and densifying the matrix. CH crystals in the SF7.5AF10 mix are small and sparse relative to the OPC matrix, which implies a high level of pozzolanic activity. The resulting C-S-H effectively seals the existing pores, decreasing the pore size and connectivity. This fined microstructure is the reason why compressive strength increased by 34.7% and water sorptivity and chloride penetrability decreased significantly in SF7.5AF10 relative to OPC.

Statistical variability and replication

The most important thing in any scientific study is to ensure that the results of the experiment are reliable and reproducible. The impact of different SCMs on mechanical and durability characteristics of concrete was evaluated in this research by rigorous replication and statistical analysis. To reduce uncontrolled variability, all tests such as compressive strength, flexural strength, split tensile strength, water sorptivity, and RCP were conducted on three replicate specimens of each of the seven concrete mix designs. These mix designs were the control mix (OPC) and binary mixes that used SF alone and ternary mixes of SF and AF in various ratios. The three specimens of each mix guaranteed a high degree of accuracy, which gave a strong basis of statistical analysis and interpretation.

To minimize further the variability of materials, all specimens were cast using the same batch of materials, mixed, cast, cured, and tested under the same conditions. This standardization was necessary to remove any variation due to differences in material properties, mixing methods, or casting methods. The study was in a position to isolate the effects of the SCMs on the concrete properties by making sure that all the samples were subjected to the same experimental conditions. The concrete mixes were OPC as the control, SF content of 5–10%, and AF content of 5–15% in the ternary mixes. The control mix was developed to have 100% OPC, whereas the binary and ternary mixes were used to investigate the influence of SF and AF on different concrete properties.

All specimens were cured in the normal laboratory conditions to maintain uniform hydration and reduce external effects on the concrete properties. Curing was done in water tanks with a temperature of 27 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of more than 95% as per the IS/ASTM standards. This controlled environment guaranteed that the specimens would be properly cured, which is a critical consideration in attaining the desired strength and durability properties of concrete. The specimens were subjected to compressive strength, flexural strength, split tensile strength, sorptivity, and RCP after 28 days of curing. Calibrated equipment was used to perform these tests to guarantee the accuracy and reliability of the measurements. The 28 days testing period is a norm in concrete research, since it is the time when concrete normally attains its desired strength and durability.

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA for compressive strength

One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate whether statistically significant differences existed among the compressive strength values of the investigated concrete mix designs56,57. ANOVA is a widely used statistical technique for comparing the means of multiple groups and determining whether observed variations are statistically meaningful. One-way ANOVA is a strong statistical test that is applied to compare the means of two or more groups. It assists in determining whether the differences in group means that are observed are random or whether they are real differences that are brought about by the factors that are being considered. Table 4 shows the F-statistic and p-value of the one-way ANOVA test of compressive strength. The findings show that the group means are statistically different as the p-value of 0.00023 is lower than the generally accepted level of significance of 0.05. This finding validates the fact that at least one concrete mix design is significantly different to the rest in compressive strength. The F-statistic of 9.91 indicates that the between-group variance is significantly higher than the within-group variance, which also confirms the argument that the mix designs possess varying compressive strengths. Since the p-value is large, it is evident that the differences in the effects of the different SCMs (SF and AF) on compressive strength are not insignificant and should be investigated further. In addition to the F-statistic and p-value, the coefficient of determination (R²) was estimated to evaluate the proportion of variability in compressive strength explained by the concrete mix design variations. The calculated R² value was 0.81, indicating that nearly 81% of the variability in compressive strength could be attributed to differences in SF and AF proportions among the investigated concrete mixes. This result demonstrates a strong relationship between mix composition and compressive strength performance within the investigated experimental conditions. However, considering the relatively limited sample size, the R² value should be interpreted cautiously and within the scope of the present laboratory-scale experimental investigation.

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)

A detailed LCA was conducted on the SimaPro software to determine the environmental sustainability of different concrete mix designs. This LCA was aimed at quantifying and comparing the environmental effects of OPC, binary blended concrete containing 7.5% SF (SF7.5), and ternary blended concrete containing 7.5% SF and 10% AF (SF7.5AF10). The approach used in this study is the gate-to-gate approach, which only considers the environmental effects of the concrete production stage, beginning with the processing of the raw material and concluding with the production of the ready-mix concrete. This does not include upstream (e.g., extraction of raw materials) or downstream (e.g., demolition or recycling) stages.

Goal and scope definition

The main objective of this LCA is to assess the environmental trade-offs of the partial replacement of OPC with SF and AF SCMs. This assessment will determine whether the use of alternative materials will lead to reduced environmental burdens and to what degree these advantages can be measured by examining several categories of environmental impacts. The functional unit was determined as 1 cubic meter of concrete and the evaluation was based on five major environmental indicators, Global Warming Potential (GWP), Ozone Formation (Human Health), Fine Particulate Matter Formation, Ozone Formation (Terrestrial Ecosystems), Terrestrial Acidification.

Life cycle inventory and method

The life cycle inventory (LCI) was assembled based on the data that is present in the SimaPro database of cement, SF, AF, and admixtures. There was also transport, energy consumption, and water use during the mixing process. The mixes were modeled with the same workability and strength and the ratios of substitution were used according to the real experimental designs. The gate-to-gate boundary condition restricted the system to include only the batching, mixing, and material input phases, which is consistent with realistic construction-phase assessment objectives.

Figure 10 presents the Environmental load which compares the percentage contribution of each mix to five categories of environmental impact. As indicated in the Fig. 10, SF7.5AF10 had lower environmental loads in all the impact categories than OPC. In particular, the GWP of SF7.5AF10 was lower by about 10% than that of OPC. Equally, significant decreases were noted in the formation of ozone (human health and terrestrial ecosystems), fine particulate matter formation, and terrestrial acidification. SCMs like SF and AF decrease the amount of clinker, the most carbon-intensive constituent of concrete, and thus decrease emissions and other pollutants.

Interpretation

The findings are clear that the replacement of OPC by SCMs like SF and AF results in measurable environmental improvements. The ternary blend (SF7.5AF10) was better than the OPC and binary mix in all the categories evaluated. This is due to the decreased cement content, increased pozzolanic reactivity, and the general decreased embodied energy of the supplementary materials. Also, the gate-to-gate analysis is only concerned with the production phase, and therefore, the results are very applicable to construction decision-makers who want to find greener material alternatives at the mix design level. These lessons can be applied to green certification (e.g., LEED), policy-making, and sustainable procurement.

The LCA performed with the help of SimaPro and the gate-to-gate approach proves that the introduction of SF and AF can moderately reduce environmental impacts associated with cement consumption of concrete. The ternary blend SF7.5AF10 turns out to be the environmentally preferable mix within the investigated scope, which has reached the reduction of the global warming potential, the formation of air pollutants, and acidification. These results justify the shift to low-carbon construction materials and show the promise of high-tech SCM-based concrete to achieve performance and sustainability objectives in the built environment.

It should also be noted that the present environmental assessment was conducted using a gate-to-gate system boundary, which primarily considers material production and concrete manufacturing stages. Consequently, upstream and downstream processes such as raw material extraction, transportation variability, construction practices, service-life extension benefits, demolition, and end-of-life recycling were not included in the present assessment. Therefore, the environmental improvements reported in this study should be interpreted within the defined assessment boundary and not as a complete cradle-to-grave sustainability evaluation.

In addition, environmental impact results may vary depending on regional electricity mixes, transportation distances, industrial processing efficiencies, and allocation methodologies associated with supplementary cementitious materials. Although a detailed sensitivity or uncertainty analysis was outside the scope of the present study, future investigations should incorporate uncertainty quantification and scenario-based sensitivity analysis to further improve the robustness and generalizability of environmental sustainability assessments for ternary blended concrete systems.

Machine learning models

The 28-day compressive strength of concrete is an important predictor of concrete mix formulations and optimization of concrete mixes. Three popular machine learning (ML) models Support Vector Machine (SVM), Decision Tree, and Random Forest were used in this research to estimate the compressive strength of concrete using the actual mix proportions. The aim was to test the capability of the models to predict the concrete strength accurately using different input features including cement, SF, AF, fine aggregate, coarse aggregate, and water contents. The data was composed of 7 experimental mixes and 210 samples were obtained through laboratory tests. These samples were divided into training and testing sets, 80% of which were used to train the models and 20% to test their performance.

Model data preprocessing and model inputs

The machine learning dataset was developed using experimentally obtained results from all concrete mix combinations and corresponding replicate measurements generated during laboratory testing. Multiple observations were collected from repeated experimental measurements associated with compressive strength testing and related mix parameter variations, resulting in a dataset comprising 210 data instances. The dataset therefore represents experimentally derived observations corresponding to the different concrete mix proportions and testing repetitions rather than artificially generated synthetic data.

Although the dataset size is relatively limited compared to large public concrete databases, the primary objective of the machine learning analysis in the present study was to establish a preliminary predictive framework demonstrating the feasibility of estimating compressive strength from ternary blended concrete mix parameters. The ML investigation should therefore be interpreted as an exploratory and proof-of-concept analysis integrated with the experimental program rather than a generalized universal predictive model.

Model evaluation metrics

In order to compare the performance of both models, a number of regression measures were employed, such as the R2 Score, Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). These measures give information on the predictive power of each model and the extent of prediction errors. R2 Score is used to determine the percentage of the variance in the dependent variable (compressive strength) that can be predicted by the independent variables (input features). The higher the R2, the better the model fit. RMSE is the square root of the mean squared errors between the predicted and actual values, which gives an indication of the accuracy of prediction in terms of concrete compressive strength (in MPa). The smaller the RMSE, the better the model. MAE is the mean of the absolute errors between the predicted and actual values, which indicates the size of the average errors in predictions.

Table 6 summarizes the model performance metrics, with the Random Forest model performing better than the other models. It had the lowest R2 (0.88), the lowest RMSE (1.72 MPa) and the lowest MAE (1.49 MPa), which means that it is the most accurate in predicting compressive strength. The Decision Tree model also fared well with an R2 of 0.85, but with a little more errors than the Random Forest. The SVM model was the least performing of the three models with an R2 of 0.82, and greater RMSE (2.15 MPa) and MAE (1.85 MPa) values.

Feature importance analysis

Figures 18 and 19 show the Feature Importance analysis of the Decision Tree and the Random Forest models, which determine the most significant input features in the prediction of compressive strength. The importance of features is calculated by evaluating the contribution of each feature to the decrease in the impurity (variance) of the predictions. Decision Tree (Fig. 18) shows that cement content and AF are the most significant features, then SF and water content. This implies that the composition of the binder is a dominant factor in compressive strength. Similar trends are supported by the Random Forest (Fig. 19), where cement and AF have the greatest impact on the predictions of the model, then SF and water content. The reduced significance of fine and coarse aggregates is consistent with the relatively fixed proportions of the mixes, which probably did not significantly affect compressive strength. These importance results of features indicate that the binder materials (cement, SF, and AF), and the water-to-binder ratio are the most important factors that affect the compressive strength of concrete. This validates the fact that microstructural properties of the paste are much more important than aggregate properties in determining the strength of concrete.

This paper shows that machine learning models, especially the Random Forest, can be used to predict the 28 day compressive strength of concrete mixes. The findings indicate that the most significant factors in predicting strength are cement content, AF, and SF. Random Forest model was the best model as it had the highest accuracy and lowest error measures. These results demonstrate the possibility of applying machine learning methods to optimize concrete mix design and predict its performance, which can be a useful resource to researchers and engineers in the construction industry. The feature importance analysis performed in the present study provides preliminary interpretability regarding the influence of binder constituents on compressive strength prediction. However, advanced explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques such as SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations), permutation importance, and partial dependence analysis could further improve model interpretability by quantifying both global and local feature interactions. Future studies involving larger experimental datasets should incorporate these advanced interpretability approaches to achieve more robust understanding of nonlinear parameter interactions in ternary blended concrete systems.

Study limitations and future scope

Although the present study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the mechanical performance, durability behaviour, statistical significance, environmental impact, and machine learning-based prediction of SF–AF blended concrete, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The experimental investigation was primarily focused on 28-day properties in order to establish the short-term performance characteristics and optimum ternary blend proportions. Long-term durability aspects such as carbonation resistance, sulfate attack resistance, shrinkage behaviour, freeze-thaw durability, and extended curing-age performance were not included within the present scope and require further investigation.

In addition, the microstructural evaluation in the current study was primarily based on SEM observations to qualitatively examine matrix densification and hydration morphology. Although SEM analysis provided valuable insights into pore refinement and C-S-H development, advanced quantitative characterization techniques such as XRD, TGA, MIP, and BET surface analysis could provide deeper understanding of hydration kinetics, phase assemblage evolution, and pore structure refinement. Therefore, future studies should integrate these advanced characterization methods together with long-term durability assessments to further validate the synergistic mechanisms of SF and AF in sustainable high-performance concrete systems.

However, the machine learning investigation in the present work should be interpreted as an exploratory predictive framework based on experimentally generated datasets, and future studies should focus on larger databases, external validation, and advanced explainable AI techniques to improve generalization capability and model interpretability. Future studies should also incorporate cradle-to-grave assessment boundaries, transportation sensitivity analysis, service-life extension benefits, and uncertainty quantification to provide more comprehensive sustainability evaluation of SCM-based concrete systems. Although the statistical analyses demonstrated consistent trends regarding the influence of SF and AF on concrete performance, future studies involving larger datasets and broader experimental conditions are necessary to further strengthen statistical generalization and effect-size reliability.

Conclusions

The present study contributes to the existing literature by establishing a comprehensive experimental-statistical-machine learning framework for evaluating ternary blended concrete containing SF and AF. Unlike previous studies focused primarily on isolated strength enhancement, this research integrates durability assessment, SEM-based microstructural interpretation, statistical significance validation, environmental life cycle assessment, and predictive modeling to identify an optimized sustainable concrete mixture.
