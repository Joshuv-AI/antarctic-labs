Adsorption of methylene blue and tetracycline on nitrogen-doped cotton stalk-based biochar and mechanism research
Authors: Sining Li, Qiushuang Cui, Yong Huang, Ke Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the pressing issue of tetracycline (TC) and methylene blue (MB) contamination in water, most prior research on cotton stalk-based biochar has concentrated on optimizing process parameters (e.g., carbonization temperature and activator ratio), with less systematic investigation into regulating the material’s surface chemistry via heteroatom doping. In this study, nitrogen-doped biochars (NKBC-10, NKBC-5, and NKBC-1) with different melamine/KOH/cotton straw mass ratios (1:10:10, 1:5:5, and 1:1:1) were prepared by co-pyrolysis using cotton straw as the raw material and melamine as the nitrogen source, and the synergistic mechanism of N doping on the structural properties and adsorption performance of the materials was systematically elucidated. The results indicated that NKBC-5 possessed an excellent microporous-mesoporous structure (average pore size of 2.006 nm) and abundant surface nitrogen-containing functional groups, achieving maximum adsorption capacities of 1328.636 mg/g for TC and 917.170 mg/g for MB at 308.15 K. The adsorption process aligned with the quasi-secondary kinetics and Langmuir monolayer adsorption models. The mechanism involved a synergistic mix of Lewis acid-base interactions, π-π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions. The regeneration experiments revealed that the adsorption capacity of NKBC-5 for TC and MB remained 91.26% and 93.54% of the initial time after four cycles of recycling, indicating good stability. This study employs an N-doping strategy to synergistically optimize the pore structure and surface chemistry of cotton stalk-based biochar, offering a novel material design concept and technical foundation for the efficient and sustainable remediation of water contaminated with dyes and antibiotics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01860-8.Keywords: N-doped biochar, Tetracycline, Methylene blue, Adsorption mechanism

Introduction

Since the twenty-first century, with the rapid development of businesses such as animal husbandry and textile printing and dyeing, human activities’ impact on the natural environment is now greater [1]. Investigations have shown that discharging untreated wastewater containing antibiotics or dyes [2], directly into water bodies threatens aquatic life and poses risks to water environment safety [3]. As a result, effective dye and antibiotic removal from bodies of water has emerged as a pressing issue that must be addressed [4, 5]. Currently, major techniques for removing antibiotic residues from wastewater [6] include advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) [7], biodegradation [8], Fenton oxidation [9], photocatalytic degradation [10], and adsorption [11]. Adsorption offers several advantages, including simple operation, low cost, high removal efficiency, and no secondary contaminants [12].

Agricultural waste contains abundant organic components that can be transformed into porous materials through pyrolysis treatment to absorb contaminants in wastewater [13]. However, unaltered biochar contains fewer surface active sites, insufficient specific surface area (SSA), and a low quantity of carbon defects, resulting in poor adsorption efficiency [14]. In order to enhance the efficacy of biochar in removing organic contaminants from water bodies, researchers conducted a number of modification tests [15]. Shi et al. [16] discovered that the SSA of walnut shell biochar treated with KOH pyrolysed at 900 °C (KWS900) was greatly raised to 1713 ± 37 m2/g compared to pristine walnut shells. The highest adsorption of TC by KWS900 was 607 ± 31 m2/g. Qu et al. [17]discovered that biochar generated from corn stover treated with KOH had an SSA of 2183 m2/g and many micropores with an average pore size of 2.75 nm. Cheng et al. [18]discovered that after activating grapefruit peel with KOH, the TC adsorption capacity increased to 476.19 m2/g. Ma et al. [19] found that modifying cow dung biochar (CDBC-VC) with vitamin C increased the adsorption capacity by 60.46% to 50.90 mg/g before and after modification. Zhao et al. [20] conducted a study to research and found that KOH-activated reed biochar (965 m2/g) exhibited a total adsorption capacity of 127 mg/g. Jang et al. [21]found that the adsorption capacity of pine biochar (959 m2/g) increased to 274 mg/g via hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking. Researchers have conducted substantial study to investigate various modification approaches for improving the adsorption properties of biochars. The Sumita team [22] presented Fe-modified biochar, within 25 min, the results showed that ciprofloxacin and amoxicillin were removed at 87% and 83%, respectively. Mechanistic studies demonstrated the formation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and singlet oxygen (1O2), as well as the activation of hydrogen peroxide in the system. Similarly, Yud et al. [23]discovered that dihydroxide-modified poplar wood powder could increase the ciprofloxacin adsorption rate to 84.7%. The N-doped graded biochar prepared by Luo et al. via two-step pyrolysis of kelp had a SSA of 771 m2/g, and its intermediary pore structure was formed by the self-templating effect of the inorganic salts in the kelp, resulting in a high methyl blue adsorption capacity of 379.8 mg/g [24]. During the pyrolysis process, naturally enriched N elements in biomass can be degraded and recombined to produce in-situ N doping [25, 26], increasing biochar’s hydrophilicity, surface polarity, and pollutant removal effectiveness. All of these strategies were employed to significantly enhance the adsorption performance of virgin biochar.

To improve the practical usefulness of biochar research, incorporating heteroatoms into the carbon structure is an effective method for enhancing adsorption capacity, raising functional group density, and altering surface features. As a result, modifying heteroatoms (B, N, S, and etc. ), is an effective technique for adding chemisorption sites onto biochar surfaces [27]. Wang et al. [28, 29] created reed (PA)-based N-doped biochars (PAB-AC and PAB-AA) using phosphoric acid activation and microwave-assisted treatment with ammonium chloride and ammonium acetate as N sources. The unmodified biochar had almost negligible adsorption of acid magenta (AR18), whereas the N-doped biochar had a higher adsorption of AR18 (134.17 mg/g for PAB-AA and 111.94 mg/g for PAB-AC) than the phosphoric acid-activated biochar (95.22 mg/g).

In aqueous solutions, methylene blue (MB) and tetracycline (TC) are typical cationic and complex antibiotic pollutants, respectively [30]. Comparing their adsorption behaviours reveals the prominent importance of electrostatic interaction, and numerous investigations have verified the important effect of N on adsorption qualities. Wang et al. [31] found that N-doped garlic peel hydrochar was an effective adsorbent for TC removal, with a maximum tetracycline adsorption capacity of 1304.5 mg/g. Similarly, Ganje et al. [32] found that the greatest adsorption capacity of NaNH2-activated, N-doped porous carbon material ATC-800 produced from discarded tea leaves was 581.39 mg/g for MB under optimal conditions. This is because N doping introduces additional defects and active sites to anchor TC molecules, whilst different nitrogen sources, when assisted by activators, also exhibit dual functionality in promoting the formation of porous structures [33]. China, one of the world’s leading cotton producers, produces more than 100 million tonnes of cotton stalks each year [34]. Zhang et al. [35] investigated that biochar derived from cotton stalks exhibits a distinctive selective adsorption capacity for Congo red, achieving a maximum adsorption capacity of 997.1 mg/g. However, previous research has focused on maximising process parameters (such as carbonisation temperature and KOH activation ratio), ignoring the key opportunity to control surface chemistry via heteroatomic doping (N, S, P). Current research should focus on doping techniques [36] and clarifying the mechanism of action in order to fully realise the sustainable potential of cotton straw-derived biochar for wastewater treatment of cationic and complicated antibiotic-based contaminants.

The present research uses melamine as a nitrogen source to synthesise N-doped porous carbon materials (NKBC) via a co-pyrolysis technique. By altering the melamine-to-cotton stalk ratio, nitrogen-doped biochar with a high specific surface area and multi-porous structure was created. Investigate the effects of adsorption temperature, solution pH, and adsorption time on the adsorption effectiveness of TC and MB to determine the best adsorption conditions. This study uses an N-doping strategy to synergistically optimise the pore structure and surface chemistry of biochar, with the goal of developing a biomass adsorbent capable of rapidly and easily adsorbing TC and MB from aqueous solutions.

Materials and methods

Materials

The cotton stalks were obtained from Kashgar Farm in Xinjiang, and the major components of the cotton stalks are listed in Table S1. TC and MB both with purity ≥ 98%, were purchased from General Reagent Co., Ltd. Tianjin Zhiyuan Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. provides anhydrous ethanol with a purity of ≥ 99.7%. Deionised water is produced in-house by the laboratory. Jinhongshan Company supplies N2 with a purity of at least 99.99%. KOH and melamine were provided by Adamas-beta. Other reagents, such as HCl and NaOH, were acquired from General Reagent Company and were of analytical quality.

Preparation of nitrogen-doped biochar

Melamine monomer, KOH, and cotton stalk powder were ground in a mortar at mass ratios of 1:10:10, 1:5:5, and 1:1:1. The mixture was then placed in an alumina boat. The mixture was then transferred to a tube furnace (Anhui Kemi, TFH-1200-50-②-200) and heated at a rate of 5 ℃/min to 900 ℃ under a nitrogen atmosphere. After 2 h of pyrolysis, the resultant compounds were identified as NKBC-10, NKBC-5, and NKBC-1. The resulting biochar was washed sequentially with distilled water, 0.1 mol/L HCl, and hot water (80 °C) until the rinse reached neutral pH, followed by drying at 105 °C for 12 h.

Characterization of biochar

NKBC accomplished characterisation testing with SEM, FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, BET, and XPS. SEM(Hitachi S4800, Hitachi, Japan) testing uses a field emission scanning electron microscope. During testing, the sample is subjected to two gold spray operations and microstructural analysis at 2000× magnification. The KBr pellet method was used to analyse BC functional groups via Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy(FTIR, Great EMB 10, China), with a measurement range of 4000 cm− 1 to 400 cm− 1 and a scan number of 32. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher, USA) determines an element’s valence state. N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms, along with specific surface area and pore size distribution data, were obtained using a gas adsorption analyser (BET, Mettler, USA). Confocal Raman spectroscopy(Raman, HORIBA, France) was used to evaluate the defect density and graphitisation degree of biochar, with a laser wavelength of 532 nm and a spectral range of 800 cm− 1 to 2000 cm− 1.

Results and discussion

Characterisation analysis of NKBC

SEM images in Fig. 1a, b, c reveal that NKBC-10, NKBC-5, and NKBC-1 exhibit irregular morphologies. The pore diameter decreases and the surface roughness increases with increasing nitrogen doping concentration. Figure 1c demonstrates that NKBC-1 has unique wrinkled and flaky structures without big blocky or porous characteristics. This phenomenon can be attributed to the production of volatile gases from melamine during carbonization, which exfoliates carbon layers and facilitates the incorporation of N into the carbon lattice. This technique causes more wrinkling and produces a significant amount of faults [37]. Elemental mapping images (Fig. 1d–f) show a uniform distribution of C, O, and N throughout NKBC-10, NKBC-5, and NKBC-1. The abundance of nitrogen atoms increases gradually with the melamine percentage, confirming successful nitrogen doping into the biochar.

SEM images of NKBC, a NKBC-10, b NKBC-5, c NKBC-1, elemental mapping images d NKBC-10, e NKBC-5, f NKBC-1

Table S2 shows that, at the same pyrolysis circumstances, NKBC produced with progressively increasing nitrogen concentration (from NKBC-10 to NKBC-1) exhibits significant differences in specific surface area, pore size distribution, and volume. NKBC’s specific surface area grew from 2228 m2/g (NKBC-10) to 3440 m2/g (NKBC-1), while pore volume increased from 1.210 cm3/g to 2.392 cm3/g. The average pore diameter decreased and subsequently increased (2.176 nm→2.006 nm →2.781 nm). Figure 2a shows that all NKBCs adhere to I-type curves, showing that they are mostly microporous but also display mesoporous features. The isotherms of NKBC-1 show H4-type hysteresis loops in the high-pressure region (P/P₀ > 0.4), suggesting a mesoporous structure with non-slit pores [38]. Although such holes increase total pore capacity, their narrow openings and poor connectivity limit the diffusion rates of TC and MB molecules. In comparison, NKBC-5’s pore size (2.006 nm) is closer to the microporous-mesoporous transition region, presumably indicating higher pore connectivity and thus improved adsorption ability.

a N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm of NKBC, and b pore size distribution of NKBC

As shown in Fig. 3a, the absorption peak at 3447 cm− 1 is attributed to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations. The intensity increases significantly with rising N doping concentration, owing to the enhanced formation of functional groups such as -NH2 and =NH2 [39]. This indicates that N doping effectively enriches polar functional groups on the NKBC surface. The modest signal at 2834 cm− 1 is due to the symmetric stretching vibration of the C-H bond. Between 2364 cm− 1 and 1363 cm− 1, C ≡ N stretching and -NO2 antisymmetric stretching peaks were found, confirming the existence of nitrogen atoms in the carbon skeleton in the forms of -CN and -NO2. This is similar to the nitrogen-containing intermediates produced during the heat breakdown of melamine [40]. The strong absorption peak at 1137 cm− 1 is attributed to the C-O stretching vibrations of ether (C-O-C) and ester (C = O-O-C) groups, indicating the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups on the material surface. The absorption decreases slightly with increasing nitrogen doping concentration, possibly due to the introduction of nitrogen atoms inhibiting the formation of some oxygen-containing groups [41]. The peak at 1000 –650 cm− 1 is the out-of-plane bending vibration (γ-CH) of the aromatic ring’s C-H bonds, while the characteristic peak at 1595 cm− 1 is the stretching vibration of the aromatic ring’s C = C bond. It is worth noting that as nitrogen content increases, the C = C peak location gradually changes to higher frequencies (to the right). This behaviour may be linked to N atoms’ stronger electronegativity in comparison to C atoms [42], as well as their electron-drawing action, which reduces the electron density of the C = C bond within the aromatic ring. This increases the bond constant, which raises the vibrational frequency [43]. Furthermore, N-doping induces nitrogen-containing conjugated structures such as pyridine and pyrazine rings (e.g., C = N-C bonds), which enhance the double bond character of neighbouring C = C bonds via the conjugation effect, raising the vibrational frequency even further.

Spectra of NKBC-10, 5, and 1, a FTIR spectra, b Raman spectra

Figure 3b reveals that the ID/IG values for NKBC-10, NKBC-5, and NKBC-1 are 0.86, 1.00, and 1.07, respectively. The ID/IG ratio of NKBC gradually rises as the N-doping concentration increases, due to the formation of aromatic conjugated rings through dehydration condensation of small-molecule organic compounds during high-temperature pyrolysis of cotton stalks, thereby constructing the carbon skeleton. However, at the border of the carbon layer and at grain boundaries, incompletely condensed carbon atoms easily form unsaturated bonds, resulting in inherent defects centred on carbon atoms. At low nitrogen doping levels, nitrogen atoms are primarily dispersed as isolated sites within the carbon matrix, causing little disruption to the graphitic framework. As nitrogen doping increases, nitrogen atoms form continuous nitrogen-containing conjugated structures within the carbon network, intensifying lattice distortion and resulting in a significant increase in defect density. This defect structure has a dual effect on material properties: on the one hand, defect sites provide more adsorption sites for TC/MB; and the other hand, excessive flaws can disturb the orderliness of the pore structure, reducing pollutant diffusion and transport [44].

Figure 4 shows the XPS spectrum of NKBC, which is predominantly constituted of C, O, and N atoms. Figure 4a depicts the C1s spectrum of NKBC, which is divided into four peaks: C-C/C = C at about 284 eV, C-N at around 285 eV, C = O at around 287 eV, and a π-π* peak at around 290 eV. As nitrogen doping increases from NKBC-10 to NKBC-1, the proportion of the C-C/C = C peak rises and subsequently falls. This is because nitrogen-containing radicals produced by melamine decomposition increase the C-C/C = C peak proportion, whereas excessive melamine introduces a large number of nitrogen atoms, partially replacing C-C bonds with C-N bonds, resulting in a decrease in the intensity of the C-C/C = C peak.

XPS spectra of NKBC-10, 5, and 1, a C1s, b N1s

The N1s peak is typically resolved into pyridine N (398.6 eV): formed at lower temperatures or under milder conditions, representing nitrogen at edge or defect sites; pyrrole N (400.2 eV): commonly found in five-membered ring structures, unstable at elevated temperatures and prone to conversion into other forms [45]; and graphite N (401 eV): N embedded within graphite layers, typically requiring elevated pyrolysis temperatures or extended reaction times for formation. From Fig. 4b, the proportion of pyridine N increases and then decreases with increasing N doping levels. This happens because the breakdown of melamine at low ratios gives a more abundant nitrogen supply, encouraging N doping at edges. When N doping increases, elevated temperatures induce some of the pyridine N to convert into more stable graphitic N, resulting in a decrease in its proportion. The pyrrole nitrogen fraction falls dramatically because it easily decomposes or rearranges into pyridine nitrogen/graphitic nitrogen after high-temperature activation [46]. The extreme alkalinity of KOH may disturb the five-membered ring structure, hastening the conversion of pyrrole nitrogen; the high concentration of melamine supplies extra nitrogen atoms, promoting nitrogen entry into graphite layers and so increasing the amount of graphite nitrogen [47].

Adsorption performance analysis

Figure 5 shows that the equilibrium adsorption capacity of NKBC steadily increases with increasing initial TC and MB concentrations. While the starting concentration increased from 20 mg/L to 200 mg/L, the equilibrium adsorption capacities of NKBC-10, NKBC-5, and NKBC-1 increased from 199.987 mg/g, 197.421 mg/g, and 199.978 mg/g to 888.460 mg/g, 1237.336 mg/g, and 881.94 mg/g, respectively. When the initial MB concentration increased from 30 mg/L to 100 mg/L, the equilibrium adsorption capacities for MB increased from 299.998 mg/g, 300 mg/g, and 300 mg/g to 935.221 mg/g, 991.268 mg/g, and 847.092 mg/g, respectively. Primarily, as pollutant concentrations grow, so do collisions with biochar, increasing adsorption capacity. Whenever the N doping concentration increases, the adsorption capacity for TC and MB initially rises and then falls, with NKBC-5 demonstrating the highest adsorption performance. This is because the high-content melamine-derived C3N4 generates a dense carbon coating on the microporous surface [48], making it difficult for TC and MB to reach the active areas. The extra nitrogen partially converts C-N bonds into inert graphitic nitrogen or N = C=O structures, decreasing interactions with TC and MB and limiting adsorption capability. In contrast, a suitable number of nitrogen atoms incorporated in the carbon framework can produce pyridine nitrogen (C-N-C) or -NH2 groups. These groups interact with TC/MB’s -OH or -NH2 groups via hydrogen bonding, improving adsorbent capacity.

pH influence on the adsorption of TC and MB in NKBC-5

Figure 6 shows the Zeta potential and adsorption properties of MB at various pH values for NKBC-5. The pHpzc of NKBC-5 is about 7.40. When pH < 3.3, NKBC-5’s positively charged surface experiences electrostatic repulsion with the cation TCH3+. When pH is 3.3–7.4, TC is mostly polymeric and uncharged [49], reducing adsorption ability. However, NKBC-5 shows an initial rise followed by a decrease in TC adsorption, suggesting the presence of additional interactions (hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking). When the pH surpasses pHpzc, the NKBC-5 surface becomes negatively charged, resulting in electrostatic repulsion with the anion TC2−, slowing the adsorption process and lowering adsorption capacity. When the pH is below the pHpzc, NKBC-5 carries a positive charge, and MB also carries a positive charge, resulting in electrostatic repulsion. The adsorption tendency of NKBC-5 towards MB at different pH values increases with rising pH, possibly due to hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking interactions between NKBC-5 and MB, which overcome the electrostatic repulsion [50]. When the pH exceeds the adsorbent’s pHpzc, the NKBC surface becomes negatively charged, resulting in electrostatic attraction with MB, which enhances the adsorption reaction and gradually increases the adsorption quantity.

Adsorption kinetics of NKBC-5

Figure 7 shows that NKBC-5’s adsorption capacity for TC and MB first increases dramatically before stabilising. At an adsorption time of 30 min, NKBC-5 achieved adsorption capacities of 276.358 mg/g for TC and 385.520 mg/g for MB; When the adsorption time reached 180 min, the adsorption capacities of NKBC-5 for TC and MB reached 472.967 mg/g and 496.186 mg/g respectively, indicating excellent removal efficiency for both compounds; this is attributed to NKBC-5’s large specific surface area and abundant C-N and oxygen-containing active functional groups, which facilitate rapid adsorption rates for TC and MB. The parameters generated from the adsorption kinetic curves are shown in Table S3. The pseudo-second-order kinetic model (R2 = 0.994, 0.990) performed better than the pseudo-first-order kinetic model (R2 = 0.984, 0.943) in describing TC and MB adsorption processes on NKBC-5. Furthermore, the equilibrium adsorption capacities of TC and MB fitted by the pseudo-second-order kinetic model (494.079 mg/g, 498.731 mg/g) were closer to the actual observed values (497.421 mg/g, 499.967 mg/g), showing that chemical adsorption is the dominant adsorption mechanism.

Adsorption thermodynamics of NKBC-5

Figure 9 depicts the NKBC-5 adsorption isotherm, and Table S5 lists the derived parameter values. At various temperatures, the determination coefficient (R2) for the Langmuir model of TC (or MB) ranged from 0.990 to 0.997 (or 0.992 to 0.995), which was greater than that of the Freundlich model (0.741–0.906 or 0.859–0.974, respectively), indicating superior fitting performance for the Langmuir model [19], implying that NKBC-5 adsorption for TC and MB is single-layer adsorption. RL can be used to determine if an adsorption process is unfavourable (RL>1), linear (RL=1), favourable (0 < RL< 1), or spontaneous (RL=0). Figure 9c and d show that the RL ranges for NKBC-5 adsorption of TC and MB are both between 0 and 1, implying that NKBC-5 has advantageous adsorption properties for both TC and MB.

Langmuir and Freundlich adsorption isotherms for NKBC-5, a TC, b MB, Plot of separation factor (RL) versus initial concentration, c TC, d MB, D-R model fit, e TC, f MB

The Freundlich model, with nF>1, shows the presence of a physical adsorption process [51]. Furthermore, the ratio 1/nF is strongly related to surface heterogeneity, so as the heterogeneity of the NKBC-5 surface rises, its value approaches zero even more. The adsorption 1/nF values of NKBC-5 for TC at various temperatures ranged from 0.119 to 0.180, while those for MB ranged from 0.142 to 0.220, revealing some heterogeneity in NKBC-5.

Figure 9a and b show that the adsorption capacity of NKBC-5 for TC and MB increases with increasing adsorption temperature. The maximum adsorption capacity for TC increased by a factor of 1.10 when the temperature increased from 288.15 K (1205.003 mg/g) to 308.15 K (1328.636 mg/g). The adsorption of MB also gradually increased with rising temperature (900.021 mg/g-917.170 mg/g), though the increase in MB adsorption was relatively minor, indicating that adsorption temperature has a greater influence on TC adsorption. The D-R model calculates an adsorption energy E of 2.162 KJ/mol for TC adsorption at 288.15 K, which is less than 8 KJ/mol. This suggests that physical adsorption mechanisms, such as pore filling or van der Waals forces, play a primary role in the process. As the adsorption temperature rises, E progressively increases to 9.466 KJ/mol (308.15 K), and the adsorption mechanism shifts to ion exchange. This process is closely linked to alterations in the material’s surface chemical environment: elevated temperatures induce Lewis acid-base interactions between the amide group (-CONH-) in TC molecules and nitrogen-containing functional groups such as pyridine N and pyrrole N on the NKBC-5 surface, or facilitate the formation of charge-transfer complexes through π-π conjugation effects. The adsorption energies E for MB surpass 8 KJ/mol in all cases (E = 8.626 KJ/mol at 288.15 K and E = 10.530 KJ/mol at 308.15 K), showing that ion exchange is the dominant adsorption mechanism. This is due to the cationic nature of MB molecules (bearing negatively charged sulfonate groups) and the electrostatic attraction to N functional groups on the NKBC-5 surface (such as the positively charged sites of pyridine N), as well as the π-π stacking interactions between the aromatic rings of MB and the graphitic structure of carbon materials. Differences in thermodynamic behaviour further highlight the differential adsorption processes of NKBC-5 against diverse pollutants: TC adsorption has a synergistic effect between physical adsorption and ion exchange, with pollutants captured quickly at low temperatures via physical interactions such as pore filling, and deep adsorption achieved at high temperatures through specific chemical interactions involving nitrogen-containing functional groups. While MB adsorption relies on strong chemical interactions (electrostatic and π-π forces), temperature influences mostly affect the kinetic level rather than the underlying mechanism. This conclusion complements the FTIR distribution of polar functional groups and the Raman spectroscopy defect structure (ID/IG values), demonstrating that N-doping endows NKBC-5 with temperature-responsive adsorption capacity towards polar organic compounds by regulating surface chemistry and pore structure.

Table S6 shows the thermodynamic constants for the adsorption of TC and MB onto NKBC-5, with the results shown in Fig. 10. The absorption of TC and MB results in positive enthalpy increases (ΔH0 = 9.277 and 89.402 KJ/mol), indicating an endothermic reaction where higher temperatures favour the process [52]. The significant entropy change (ΔS0 = 63.110 and 368.944 J/(mol·K)) indicates an spontaneous adsorption process that leads to increased system disorder. Moreover, ΔG0 < 0 demonstrates that the adsorption process is spontaneous. The value of ΔG0 reduces as the adsorption temperature increases, showing that higher temperatures are more favourable for the adsorption process [53].

Recycling performance of NKBC-5

Figure 11 shows the recycling performance of NKBC-5. After the first to fourth cycles, NKBC-5 attained adsorption rates of 97.01%, 95.44%, 93.81%, and 88.53% for TC, respectively, whereas adsorption rates for MB were 99.23%, 97.89%, 96.46%, and 92.82%. After four cycles, the NKBC-5 adsorption capacities for TC and MB were 442.644 mg/g and 464.103 mg/g, respectively, representing 91.26% and 93.54% of the starting cycle values. Compared to KOH-activated biochar [11], N-doping improves BC stability and regeneration performance substantially.

Adsorption mechanism of NKBC-5 on TC and MB

Pore structure is an important factor in biochar adsorption efficacy, and during the adsorption of TC and MB by NKBC-5, the pore filling effect and evolution of pore architecture were directly verified using BET analysis (Table S2). Prior to adsorption, NKBC-5 had a specific surface area of 3156 m2/g, a pore volume of 1.583 cm3/g, and an average pore diameter of 2.006 nm, indicating a typical microporous-mesoporous multi-level pore structure with numerous adsorption sites for pollutants [54]. The absorption of TC and MB reduced specific surface areas to 1190 m2/g and 1492 m2/g, respectively, whereas pore volume fell to 0.769 cm3/g and 0.953 cm3/g. After adsorption, the average pore width rose significantly to 2.583 nm (NKBC-5-TC) and 2.555 nm (NKBC-5-MB) [55]. This is due to micropore filling and mesopore expansion, with TC and MB preferentially occupying micropores less than 2 nm, decreasing micropore counts. Higher MB molecules adsorb on mesoporous walls (2–50 nm) by π-π stacking interactions [56]. Their planar aromatic ring structure has a’stretching impact’ on the pore channels, altering the mesoporous pore size distribution to 2.5–3.0 nm. The dynamic equilibrium between micropore depletion and mesopore expansion causes a rise in average pore diameter while decreasing total pore volume.

Figure 12 shows a considerable increase in the O-H/N-H stretching vibration peak at 3434 cm− 1 after TC absorption. These demonstrates the creation of hydrogen bonds between the TC molecule’s -OH and -NH2 groups and the polar functional groups on the NKBC-5 surface. The -NH2 group of TC acts as a proton donor, conducting a protonation reaction with the pyridine N (Lewis base site) on the material surface (-NH2+N+→-NH3+-N), establishing an ionic connection and strengthening the N-H vibrational dipole moment. The TC molecule’s β-lactam lactone group (C = O) forms a hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl group (-OH) on the NKBC-5 surface, increasing the intensity of the C-O vibrational peak at 1363 cm− 1. The broadening of the aromatic ring C = C vibrational peak at 1595 cm− 1 is attributable to the π-π conjugation between the TC benzene ring and the NKBC-5 graphitised carbon skeleton, which raises the vibrational energy levels of the bonded area through electron delocalisation effects. Conversely, after MB adsorption, peak intensities at 3434 cm− 1 and 1595 cm− 1 decrease. The MB molecule’s sulfonic acid group establishes ionic connections (-SO3⁻···N⁺) with the protonated pyridine N (N⁺) on the NKBC-5 membrane. This immediately covers the O-H/N-H active sites and attenuates the polar group vibration signals. MB’s hydrophobic aromatic ring binds closely to the graphitised carbon layer via π-π stacking, physically protecting the vibrational paths of surface hydroxyl groups. Concurrently, its cationic nature (-N+(CH3)2) occupies the hydrogen-bonding sites of pyrrole N, preventing C-N vibrational coupling

FTIR spectra of NKBC-5 before and after adsorption

Figure 13 shows that the C1s and N1s peak spectra of NKBC-5 alter significantly after TC and MB adsorption. The percentage of C-C bonds fell dramatically from 55.02% to 42.72% and 38.21%, respectively, showing that π-π coupling occurred to facilitate the adsorption of TC and MB. Protonation of TC (or MB) -NH2 groups may form hydrogen bonds with oxygen-containing functional groups on the biochar surface (e.g., -COOH, -OH) or covalent bonding with defect sites (e.g., sp2 carbon edges), resulting in increased C-N and C = O percentages. For N1s, the graphitic N and pyridinic N content in NKBC-5 reduced significantly compared to pre-adsorption levels, demonstrating that both graphitic N and pyridinic N are important in the TC and MB adsorption processes. Pyrrole nitrogen content increased significantly following TC and MB adsorption, reaching 62.02% and 71.45%, respectively. Pyridine nitrogen (398.6 eV) was changed to pyrrole nitrogen (399.9 eV), implying that NKBC-5 may facilitate TC and MB adsorption through Lewis acid-base interactions. In contrast, the fraction of graphite N fell from 22.34% to 19.24% and 11.22%, respectively, due to improved π-π stacking that strengthened adsorption on TC and MB. Graphite N’s strong electronegativity increases the π-π interaction between the aromatic rings of NKBC-5 (π electron acceptor) and TC with MB (π electron donor) [38]. Figure 14 shows that adsorption of TC and MB onto NKBC-5 leads to an increase in nitrogen content. This is likely owing to the aromatic structure of NKBC-5 generating π-π stacking with the aromatic rings of TC/MB, exposing nitrogen atoms from the adsorbate to the surface. Thus, the adsorption processes for TC and MB include Lewis acid-base interactions, π-π stacking, and hydrogen bonds.

Conclusion

This study successfully prepared nitrogen-doped biochar (NKBC) using cotton stalks as a feedstock via a melamine-assisted KOH co-pyrolysis method, systematically investigating the effects of varying doping ratios on material structure and adsorption properties. The results indicate that moderate nitrogen doping (NKBC-5) synergistically optimises the biochar’s pore structure and surface chemistry, forming abundant micro-mesoporous structures and nitrogen-containing functional groups, which significantly enhance its adsorption capacity for TC and MB. At 308.15 K, NKBC-5 achieved maximum adsorption capacities of 1328.636 mg/g for TC and 917.170 mg/g for MB. The adsorption process conformed to pseudo-second-order kinetics and the Langmuir model, with mechanisms involving Lewis acid-base interactions, π-π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions. Furthermore, NKBC-5 exhibits excellent regenerative properties and cycling stability, retaining 91.26% and 93.54% of its initial adsorption capacities for TC and MB, respectively, after four adsorption-desorption cycles. This study achieved the synergistic regulation of the pore structure and surface chemistry of cotton stalk-derived biochar through nitrogen doping. It provides novel design concepts and a technical foundation for developing highly efficient and sustainable remediation materials for dye- and antibiotic-contaminated water, holding significant theoretical and practical value.

Challenges and prospects

Although nitrogen-doped biochar has showed great promise for dye and antibiotic adsorption in water, it still faces numerous substantial hurdles in practical applications. Firstly, the regulatory mechanism of the nitrogen doping process has yet to be fully understood, particularly the effects of the synergistic interaction between different nitrogen sources and activators on the microstructure, surface chemistry, and adsorption properties of the materials, which require further systematic investigation. Second, the majority of previous research focus on static adsorption in laboratory conditions, with no systematic evaluation of the materials’ long-term stability, interference resistance, and regeneration efficacy in dynamic flow systems (e.g., fixed bed). Furthermore, biochar’s environmental safety and ecotoxicity, as well as its selective adsorption behaviour in complicated aqueous media, require further investigation.

In the future, research should focus on the following directions: first, establishing intelligent design and controllable synthesis methodologies, and second, revealing the conformational link between doping-structure-properties using multi-scale modelling and in-situ characterisation approaches. The second goal is to support the transition of materials from laboratory to real-world water treatment situations, conduct pilot scale experiments, and build process parameter optimisation models. Third, we will investigate biochar recycling and resourcing options, as well as promote environmental sustainability through life cycle evaluation. Fourth, we will broaden the use of functionalised biochar in composite pollution systems, membrane separation coupling processes, and catalytic degradation in order to significantly increase its overall performance in water treatment.
