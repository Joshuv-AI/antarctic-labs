Magnetohydrodynamic drag on an oscillating sphere in a rotating cavity
David Cébron 1, Paolo Personnettaz 1,2

Abstract
We analyse the magnetohydrodynamic drag on a sphere undergoing small-amplitude translational oscillations in a rotating spherical cavity. This provides a canonical model for oscillatory flows in confined rotating magnetohydrodynamic systems, where dissipation arises from the poorly constrained coupling between magnetic fields, rotation and viscosity. Such flows occur in planetary interiors, notably driven by the translational oscillations of the Earth’s inner core along linear or circular trajectories (the polar and equatorial Slichter modes). They may also arise in the thin subsurface oceans of icy moons where strong confinement is expected. Previous theoretical studies considered only simplified limits, restricted to the polar mode: 82 solved the viscous bounded problem without rotation or magnetic effects, revealing the importance of pressure, whereas 9 examined magnetic tension in a non-rotating inviscid unbounded fluid, neglecting magnetic pressure and confinement. We develop a unified boundary-layer framework for magnetic and viscous effects in a fluid shell bounded by two solid regions with possibly different electromagnetic properties. Large electromagnetic contrasts arise even in simple laboratory configurations, such as an iron sphere oscillating in a liquid-metal (e.g. Galinstan). We derive boundary layers and obtain the magnetohydrodynamic drag from Alfvén-wave radiation, viscous effects and Ohmic dissipation (accounting for pressure effects). The theory is extended to non-axisymmetric equatorial modes and to rotation perturbations of the polar mode, with stronger rotation treated through magnetohydrodynamic Stokes-Ekman boundary layers and a corrected inviscid solution of 11 . Our magnetohydrodynamic simulations validate our theory, providing a quantitative framework for planetary interiors.

Introduction

1.1
Geophysical background

The Earth’s solid inner core can slightly translate within the liquid outer core relative to the planetary centre. Such motions may arise when seismic or asteroid impact energies are comparable to the gravitational energy of the interior
(
65
)
. A force balance shows that the inner-core centre of mass behaves as a damped harmonic oscillator, where the Archimedean force is the dominant restoring term. Earth’s rotation splits these oscillations into one polar and two equatorial Slichter modes
(
75
)
, the latter being an orbital-like motion in the equatorial plane. Their dynamics remain less well constrained than rotational modes driven by external interactions
(
44
, e.g.)
.

The oscillation period follows from Newton’s second law applied to the inner core, including the Archimedean restoring force and the fluid added mass
(
75
)
. Rotational, inertial and viscous effects were examined by
90
. Moving beyond lumped models,
11
, noted B74 hereafter, solved the Euler equations in a rotating cavity, showing that confinement and fluid Coriolis effects nearly compensate, yielding only small corrections to previous period estimates; he also derived a closed-form inviscid velocity field for polar oscillations. The linear hydrodynamic problem was later investigated numerically by
62
, who formulated a coupled eigenvalue problem for a slightly oscillating inner core in a rotating, isentropic and viscous spherical fluid shell.

Viscous and Ohmic dissipation have received less attention. Neglecting magnetic-pressure effects,
9
used a boundary-layer model to derive the magnetic-tension contribution to the Lorentz force for polar oscillations in an inviscid, non-rotating and unbounded fluid.
78
analysed the three modes in a non-magnetic, bounded, viscous and rotating fluid, constructing the basic flow from products of Legendre functions
(
30
, inertial-mode approaches of)
and including Stokes-Ekman boundary layers. They obtained the inviscid pressure force and viscous tangential stresses, but neglected viscous corrections to the pressure required to recover the exact solution of
82
. This formulation was used by
77
to infer an unrealistically large viscosity near the inner core
(
76
, see also)
. Despite these efforts, the relative importance of dissipation mechanisms remains uncertain
(
65
)
.

After more than two decades of limited attention, the dissipation of Earth’s core Slichter modes was revisited by
56
. Combining magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations with scaling arguments, they estimated both Ohmic and viscous dissipation and inferred quality factors and decay times for Earth’s core. Beyond its geophysical motivation, however, the problem is attractive for its simple geometry, which is amenable to analytical approaches such as potential-flow theory and boundary-layer analysis. In the following, we review the relevant contributions from both fluid-dynamical and MHD perspectives.

1.2
Fluid dynamics context

The flow and forces generated by an oscillating sphere form a canonical problem in fluid mechanics, relevant from laboratory experiments to planetary interiors. Classical solutions describe viscous effects in simple configurations, whereas many geophysical systems additionally involve rotation, confinement and magnetic fields. In such environments, oscillations of electrically conducting fluids generate coupled viscous, rotational and magnetic boundary layers, which have not yet been addressed within a unified framework.

The drag on a sphere moving in a fluid has been studied for more than two centuries. Early work was motivated by pendulum experiments aimed at determining the Earth’s figure, requiring accurate estimates of fluid resistance

Extensions to non-spherical geometries followed. Using the potential theory of

(e.g. pp. 141, 144 of

The influence of rotation on the flow generated by a moving sphere was first examined by

Electrical properties may differ between fluid and solid domains: metallic cores are comparably conducting in both phases, outer solid regions may be insulating or conducting, and subsurface-ocean boundaries are typically insulating. Six ingredients govern the problem: the oscillation type (polar or equatorial), confinement (bounded or unbounded), fluid viscosity (inviscid or viscous), rotation, electrical properties of the three domains (inner solid sphere, fluid shell, outer solid shell), and the imposed magnetic field. As reviewed below, no previous study incorporates all these effects simultaneously. We therefore examine their coupled influence on the drag force, filling this gap.

Without magnetic field, the viscous force on a sphere arises from both pressure and viscous stresses. For stress-free and no-slip spheres,

The formulation is finally extended to arbitrary conductivity and permeability contrasts across solid interfaces.

2.2
Forces from pressure, viscous and magnetic stresses

Several forces act on the sphere, whose outward unit normal is
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}
. The viscous stress yields
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{v}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{vn}=\int_{S}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{v}\cdot\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{S}
, where
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{v}
and
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{vn}
denote tangential and normal components, with
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{S}=\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\mathrm{d}S
. The pressure force is
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{p}=\int_{S}\rho_{f}{\varPi}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{S}
, so the total normal force is
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}={\boldsymbol{F}}_{p}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{vn}
and the total viscous contribution
\boldsymbol{F}_{\nu}=\boldsymbol{F}_{v}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{F}_{n}(\nu=0)
. Hence
\boldsymbol{F}_{\nu}
, unlike
\boldsymbol{F}_{v}
, can remain non-zero for stress-free viscous boundaries (but vanishes for inviscid flow). The Lorentz force
\mu_{f}^{-1}(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{B})\times\boldsymbol{B}=-\mu_{f}^{-1}\nabla(\boldsymbol{B}^{2}/2)+\mu_{f}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{B}
produces a magnetic force
\boldsymbol{F}_{\eta}=\mu_{f}^{-1}\int_{V}(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{B})\times\boldsymbol{B}\,\mathrm{d}V={\boldsymbol{F}}_{mp}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{mt}
, with magnetic pressure
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{mp}=-(2\mu_{f})^{-1}\int_{S}\boldsymbol{B}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{S}
and magnetic tension
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{mt}=\mu_{f}^{-1}\int_{S}\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{S})
.

Neglecting magnetic and rotational effects, the oscillating no-slip sphere was analysed by
82
, who showed that
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{v}
is the sum of a viscous drag proportional to
{\boldsymbol{V}}_{s}
(vanishing as
\nu\to 0
) and an inertial contribution proportional to
\mathrm{d}_{t}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{s}
, written
-C_{a}m_{f}\mathrm{d}_{t}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{s}
, where
C_{a}\to C_{a}^{i}\neq 0
in the inviscid limit owing to
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{p}
. Although the steady result
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}+{\boldsymbol{F}}_{v}=-6\pi\rho_{f}\nu a_{s}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{s}
for
\omega_{s}\to 0
is well known, the relative roles of pressure and viscous stresses, and the rapidly oscillating regime, are less known. Moreover,
82
also considered an outer spherical boundary, deriving implicit bounded-flow solutions and an explicit force on the sphere. Revisiting these results, we obtained an explicit flow expression (appendix
A.1
) and asymptotic expansions of the force for slow and rapid oscillations, highlighting confinement effects (appendix
A.2
).

For stress-free boundary conditions, the force differs but remains relevant to the no-slip configuration, as it directly reflects viscous dissipation in the fluid bulk. This contribution becomes significant when viscosity is not negligible; we therefore recall the corresponding stress-free bulk-force expressions (appendix
A.3
).

In the presence of rotation or magnetic effects, the inviscid-fluid force does not necessarily retain the form
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{p}=-C_{a}^{i}{m}_{f}\mathrm{d}_{t}{\boldsymbol{V}}_{s}
; in general
{\boldsymbol{F}}_{p}
is not even opposite to the acceleration (B74). However, for the three Slichter modes, B74 showed that this property persists when rotation is included (without magnetic or viscous effects). In this inviscid rotating limit, the pressure force is parallel to
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
for the polar mode and directed along
-\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}
for equatorial modes, with
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}=-\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\times(\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{r}})
the cylindrical-radius unit vector.

2.3
Ohmic and Viscous dissipation

The Ohmic and viscous dissipations are
{\mathcal{D}}_{\eta}=\eta_{f}\mu_{f}^{-1}\int_{{V}_{f}}(\nabla\times{\boldsymbol{B}})^{2}\mathrm{d}V

{\mathcal{D}}_{\nu}=\int_{{V}_{f}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{v}\boldsymbol{:}\nabla{\boldsymbol{V}}\mathrm{d}V

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{v}=\rho_{f}\nu[\nabla{\boldsymbol{V}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{V}})^{\top}]

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{V}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{\nu}=\int_{{V}_{f}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{v}\boldsymbol{:}\nabla{\boldsymbol{V}}\mathrm{d}V=\rho_{f}\nu\int_{V_{f}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}^{2}\mathrm{d}V+2\rho_{f}\nu\int_{{V_{f}}}\nabla\cdot(\mathrm{D}_{t}\boldsymbol{V})\mathrm{d}V,

(7)

\mathrm{D}_{t}\boldsymbol{V}=\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{V}+(\boldsymbol{V}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{V}

\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{V}=0
,

\displaystyle\int_{{V_{f}}}\nabla\cdot(\mathrm{D}_{t}\boldsymbol{V})\mathrm{d}V=\int_{\partial{V_{f}}}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot(\mathrm{D}_{t}\boldsymbol{V})\mathrm{d}S=\int_{\partial{V_{f}}}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}\times\boldsymbol{V}+\nabla(\boldsymbol{V}^{2}/2)\right]\mathrm{d}S,

where

Analytical simplifications follow from the BG95 assumptions: (i) retaining only the homogeneous solution, valid for sufficiently thin boundary layers, and (ii) neglecting spatial variations of boundary-layer thickness, i.e. assuming a uniform layer. The latter is exact in specific limits (e.g. no rotation with a monopole field at
r=a_{s}
), and otherwise relies on a characteristic thickness. This approach is hereafter termed
huBL
(homogeneous uniform Boundary Layer).

4

Potential flows, added mass and weak rotational perturbations

For rapid oscillations at

4.3
Large rotation effects: basic flow and added mass coefficient

The regime of arbitrary
\gamma
is analytically challenging, notably because inertial modes may be directly forced when
\gamma>1

\gamma<1

Using cylindrical coordinates and a stream function

\displaystyle C_{a}^{i}=\frac{\mathcal{H}(\zeta_{s})-\mathcal{H}(\zeta_{m})+\zeta_{s}^{-1}+\zeta_{s}(\zeta_{s}^{2}-1)^{-1}}{\mathcal{H}(\zeta_{m})-\mathcal{H}(\zeta_{s})},

We have reassessed these studies using MHD simulations and extended them to include confinement, electromagnetic contrasts between solid domains, equatorial modes, and rotational corrections to the polar mode. The resulting fluid response yields forces decomposed into in-phase and out-of-phase components, given by the real and imaginary parts of the detuning vector
\boldsymbol{\Delta\omega}
. The in-phase contribution defines the added-mass coefficient, whereas viscous and Ohmic effects appear in the out-of-phase component, equivalently measured by the volume dissipation. The lower part of table
2
summarises the resulting analytical expressions and their validity ranges.

The unified boundary-layer framework shows that MHD drag arises from three coupled mechanisms: Alfvén-wave radiation into the fluid bulk, viscous stresses within thin shear layers, and Ohmic dissipation associated with electromagnetic coupling across interfaces. Confinement and electromagnetic contrasts modify both amplitude and phase of the MHD loading, while rotation reorganises the response through MHD Stokes-Ekman boundary layers and, for the polar mode, a rotation-corrected flow derived from
11
. The theory captures both axisymmetric polar oscillations and non-axisymmetric equatorial modes within a single asymptotic description.

These results provide a quantitative framework for oscillatory flows in confined rotating MHD systems. In planetary settings, they quantify how translational oscillations of solid regions dissipate energy through coupled viscous and electromagnetic processes, with relevance to Earth’s Slichter modes and to strongly confined subsurface oceans of icy moons. More broadly, the present formulation establishes a canonical benchmark for oscillatory MHD coupling in spherical geometry, amenable to laboratory realisation and numerical validation, and providing a consistent basis for incorporating rotation, magnetic fields and confinement in future studies of planetary interior dynamics.

7.2
Perspective

A first limitation concerns equatorial modes at non-negligible rotation, for which no analytical base flow is available. Further developments are therefore required to obtain a tractable description of rotation-modified equatorial modes. A promising route is to build upon the rotation-corrected basic flow of
78
, allowing consistent evaluation of detuning including rotational, magnetic and viscous effects. Their formulation, close to an inertial-mode basis, could enable exploration of strong-rotation regimes (
\gamma>1
), although inviscid inertial modes in a spherical shell are singular and only regularised by viscosity.

Another limitation stems from the small-amplitude assumption used in both the theory and the approximate boundary condition implemented in
xshells
, which requires amplitudes smaller than the boundary layer. While appropriate for planets, this is limiting in laboratory configurations. As noted in
56
, the approximate boundary condition overestimates dissipation at larger amplitudes. Using
comsol
simulations, where the Arbitrary-Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) method allows a truly moving inner sphere, we have checked that the agreement remains good for modest deformations. Further work is needed, particularly regarding Ohmic dissipation and the transition to finite-amplitude regimes.

Experimental validation is both feasible and desirable. Table
3
compares the typical ranges relevant for planetary applications to the ones that can are typically achieved in laboratory experiments. Liquid-metal experiments readily access regimes where Alfvén-wave radiation becomes significant (
\Lambda\gtrsim 1
), for instance in a
50
cm Galinstan-filled cavity with an oscillating iron or titanium sphere. Moderate to large electromagnetic contrasts arise then naturally in laboratory configurations. Our theoretical framework also accommodates differing electromagnetic properties in the surrounding outer domain, providing additional experimental flexibility.

Further extensions include non-spherical inner bodies, beginning with spheroids using the potential-flow of
42
, and boundary layers along spheroidal interfaces
(
14
, e.g.)
. Incorporating density stratification and gravity-wave coupling
(
88
)
would also be relevant for planetary interiors such as Mercury’s stratified core
(
72
)
. Finally, the present model treats Alfvén-wave radiation without wave-wave interactions or reflections
(
70
)
; including these effects would provide a more complete description of wave-mediated dissipation.

The theory also predicts zero torques, whereas simulations exhibit finite mean torques in the presence of rotation effects, indicating higher-order effects in oscillation amplitude. Rapid oscillations generate steady streaming at order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, producing mean zonal flows and modified dissipation
(e.g.
14
, for rapid rotation regimes)
. Extending this study to include such nonlinear corrections would clarify the role of oscillations and rotation induced mean secondary flows.
