Impact of manual and mechanical glide path preparation on surface defects of nickel-titanium rotary instruments
Authors: Merve Gökyar, İdil Özden, Ecem Azgari, Hesna Sazak Öveçoğlu
Published: 2026

Introduction

Nickel-titanium (NiTi) rotary instruments are widely regarded as the gold standard for root canal preparation (RCP). Their use has been shown to facilitate preparation procedures in a safe, effective, and time-efficient manner. Moreover, compared with stainless steel instruments, NiTi files provide superior flexibility and adaptability to complex canal anatomies, thereby reducing operator fatigue and minimizing procedural errors. Such advantages have been consistently supported by clinical evidence and have contributed to the routine adoption of NiTi rotary instruments in contemporary endodontic practice (1).

The glide path is defined as a biomechanical tunnel that allows small-sized endodontic instruments to progress continuously from the canal orifice to the physiological terminus. Establishing a glide path contributes to the preservation of the original canal anatomy and significantly reduces the risk of deformation and fracture of instruments used during subsequent RCP (2). NiTi instruments improve the safety and predictability of glide path creation. Their flexibility and shape memory allow better preservation of the natural canal anatomy compared with stainless steel files. This advantage reduces the risk of procedural errors during subsequent RCP (3). In the literature, a glide path is defined as the ability of a stainless steel #10 file to advance freely along the canal walls and reach the apical terminus without resistance. This movement should be repeatable and predictable (4).

The PathFile system (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland), consisting of three files with tip sizes ISO 13, 16, and 19 and a constant 2% taper, was introduced as one of the first standardized NiTi systems designed specifically for glide path preparation (GPP) and to facilitate safer RCP procedures (4). Berutti et al. (5) reported that the PathFile system resulted in less alteration of canal curvature and a lower incidence of canal aberrations compared with K-files.

Numerous studies (6, 7) have highlighted the role of GPP in enhancing the safety and predictability of RCP by reducing mechanical stresses and minimizing procedural errors. Nevertheless, most investigations have focused on specific outcomes such as instrument fracture rates (8), screw-in effect, or torque generation (9), rather than directly assessing surface defects of NiTi rotary instruments. Although similar studies exist, they remain limited in number. For example, Patel et al. (10) examined the effects of manual and mechanical GPP on surface deformations of NiTi rotary instruments using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), but their study did not include a no–glide path control group. Because they performed an in vivo study, a no–glide path control group could not be ethically included. In contrast, the ex vivo design of the present study allowed controlled comparison with a no–glide path group, providing complementary rather than corrective evidence to existing clinical findings.

The aim of this study was to compare the effects of manual and mechanical GPP on surface defects of NiTi rotary instruments. In addition, a control group was included in which RCP was performed solely with a 10.04 NiTi rotary file, without prior GPP. This design allowed for a comparative evaluation of the alterations induced on the instrument surface by different glide path strategies, as well as by the absence of glide path creation. This study hypothesized that manual and mechanical GPP would mitigate surface defects in NiTi rotary instruments relative to no GPP, without significant differences between the two approaches.

Materials and Methods

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Non-Interventional Clinical Research, Faculty of Health Sciences, Marmara University (Istanbul, Turkey) with the approval number 62, dated 27.03.2025.

Selection and allocation of the teeth to the groups

Human mandibular molars with fully formed apices, a single patent mesiobuccal canal, and a clinically moderate degree of curvature were included. Teeth had been extracted within the past month and stored in 0.9% saline solution at room temperature. All specimens were examined under a dental operating microscope (Zumax OMS series; Zumax Medical Co., Suzhou, China) at 12x–16x magnification to ensure accurate detection of cracks and structural defects. Teeth with calcified canals, internal or external resorption, immature apices, previous root canal treatment, visible cracks, fractures, or any structural defects were excluded.

To ensure anatomical comparability among specimens, canal curvature was evaluated visually and radiographically by three experienced endodontists using the Schneider classification (11) as a reference framework (MG, EA, IO). Curvature values were not calculated numerically; instead, only teeth that clearly exhibited the characteristic morphology of moderately curved canals (approximately 10°–25°) were included based on examiner consensus.

The teeth were then assigned to one of three groups (n=20) using simple randomization. The sample size was determined with reference to a previous study (10) and calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany), assuming a large effect size (Cohen's w = 0.40), a power of 80%, and an alpha level of 0.05.

Root canal preparation

Access cavities were prepared under constant water cooling using an access opening bur #3 (Dentsply Tulsa Dental, York, PA, USA) (MG, EA). In all specimens, only the mesiobuccal canal of the mesial root was included. Coronal shaping was performed with an SX orifice opener (Action Gold SX, İnci Dental, Türkiye) to standardize coronal enlargement across groups. Following coronal enlargement, all canals were irrigated with 1 mL of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (Vensons India, Bangalore, India) delivered with a 27-gauge side-vented needle (Unolok; Hindustan Syringes and Medical Devices Ltd., Faridabad, India) for 1 minute. Intermittent irrigation during RCP was also performed using 1 mL of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite under the same conditions.

Patency was confirmed with a size #10 stainless steel K-file. Working length was determined by advancing the #10 K-file until its tip reached the apical foramen, then subtracting 1 mm, and confirmed radiographically using a paralleling technique. Group 1 (Mechanical Glide Path): GPP was performed with a #13.02 rotary glide path file (C Path File, Fanta Dental, Guangdong, China), Group 2 (Manual Glide Path): GPP was performed manually with a #10.02 stainless steel K-File (EndoArt K Files, İnci Dental, Türkiye), Group 3 (Control – No Glide Path): No GPP was performed.

Preparation and allocation of the files to the groups

A total of 60 unused 10.04 NiTi rotary files (Smart Gold, 25 mm; İnci Dental, Türkiye) were individually numbered and sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C and 15 psi for 15 minutes. The files were then randomly allocated into three experimental groups described above (n = 20).

SEM imaging of rotary files

For standardization, each file was mounted with the "10.04" marking on the shank facing upward and at 250×, 500×, and 1000× magnifications using a Thermo Scientific Quattro S scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at the Surface Science and Technology Center, Boğaziçi University (Istanbul, Türkiye) before and after RCP. The imaging procedures were performed by an experienced laboratory technician who was blinded to the group allocation.

Assessment of SEM images

The SEM images were assessed by two calibrated endodontists (IO, EA). Prior to the evaluation, both assessors independently reviewed the relevant literature defining the SEM criteria for instrument defects and reached a common understanding of the assessment parameters. In cases of disagreement, a third senior endodontist provided consensus (HSO). Inter-rater reliability for the baseline assessment demonstrated excellent agreement, with Cohen's kappa value of κ = 0.827. Flute disruption, microcracks, tip deformation, blade distortion, surface pitting, and fractures were scored dichotomously as "0" (absent) or "1" (present) and recorded in a Microsoft Excel database (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Root Canal Shaping

Each mesiobuccal canal was shaped to the full working length using previously sterilized and imaged 10.04 NiTi rotary files (Smart Gold, 25 mm; İnci Dental, Türkiye), as described above. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test and Pearson's Chi-square test, with the level of significance set at 5% (α = 0.05) for all analyses.

Results

A total of 60 SX files, 20 stainless steel #10.02 K-files, 20 #13.02 rotary glide path files, and 60 #10.04 NiTi rotary files were used in the study. No files were lost due to procedural effects.

Before RCP, a total of 22 defects were observed, with tip deformation being the most common baseline defect (n = 11). After RCP, the total number of observed defects increased substantially, with flute disruption (n = 46), blade distortion (n = 31), surface pitting (n = 30), and tip deformation (n = 29) being the most prevalent. In addition, newly detected defects, including microcracks (n = 16) and fractures (n = 10), were observed (Table 1, Figure 2).

Flute disruption was frequently observed after RCP (Figure 3). Microcracks were significantly more frequent in the control group (No GPP; 50.0%) compared with both the manual GPP (15.0%) and mechanical GPP (15.0%) groups (p = 0.031). Significant intergroup differences were also observed for blade distortion, which was most prevalent in the control group (85.0%), followed by the manual GPP (40.0%) and mechanical GPP (30.0%) groups (p < 0.001) (Figure 4). Similarly, surface pitting occurred most frequently in the control group (80.0%), compared with the mechanical GPP (40.0%) and manual GPP (30.0%) groups (p = 0.005). Fracture formation was significantly higher in the control group (40.0%) than in the mechanical GPP group (10.0%), while no fractures were observed in the manual GPP group (p = 0.002) (Table 1).

Discussion

Glide path creation is considered one of the critical steps in the RCP phase of root canal treatment. This procedure enables larger taper motor-driven NiTi instruments to advance within the canal without undue resistance, thereby making RCP safer and more predictable (12). The findings of the present study further support this concept, demonstrating that GPP reduces surface defects and structural alterations in NiTi files, thereby reinforcing its clinical significance. In line with our hypothesis, both manual and mechanical GPP significantly decreased the incidence of surface defects compared with no glide path, and no significant differences were observed between the two methods, thus confirming our initial hypothesis.

In the present study, extracted mandibular molars were used instead of resin blocks to better simulate clinical conditions (13). Furthermore, mesial roots of moderately curved mandibular molars were selected, as GPP in such canals is known to present greater clinical challenges (14). Moderately curved canals were intentionally selected because they provide an optimal balance between anatomical complexity and methodological standardization. Severely curved canals, although clinically relevant, occur less frequently and present wide morphological variability, which may introduce confounding factors and compromise reproducibility. In contrast, straight or minimally curved canals do not generate sufficient mechanical stress on instruments to meaningfully differentiate the effects of GPP on the development of surface defects. By focusing on moderately curved canals, the present study ensured anatomically representative and consistently reproducible conditions under which potential differences among glide path strategies could be more reliably detected. This approach ensured that all specimens presented similar anatomical conditions relevant to stress generation during root canal preparation. The findings indicated that both manual and mechanical GPP reduced the incidence of defects such as tip deformation, blade distortion, surface pitting, and fracture compared with the absence of a glide path. These observations are consistent with previous studies highlighting the role of GPP in minimizing torsional stresses and reducing surface defects in NiTi instruments during RCP (6, 7). From a methodological perspective, teeth were used within one month after extraction and stored in saline solution to minimize dehydration-related changes in dentin structure, which could otherwise influence the mechanical behavior of instruments during canal preparation. Similarly, color-coding of teeth was used solely to facilitate accurate group identification during the experimental procedures and was not intended to influence the outcomes. In addition, the operator performing access cavity and root canal preparation (MG) was blinded to the group allocation and worked exclusively with the color-coded specimens to minimize potential operator-related bias.

In endodontic treatment, procedural complications such as cyclic fatigue and torsional fractures are well-recognized modes of NiTi instrument failure. One of the primary objectives of GPP is to prevent taper lock in motor-driven NiTi RCP instruments, thereby reducing the risk of instrument fracture due to torsional fatigue (8, 9). By reducing mechanical stresses, GPP minimizes structural deformation and lowers the likelihood of instrument failure. Previous studies (8, 9, 10) have shown that mechanically assisted GPP influences screw-in effect, torque generation, surface topography, and fracture resistance. These findings are consistent with the results of the present study, in which both manual and mechanical GPP significantly reduced the incidence of defects compared with the no-glide-path group. Similarly, Berutti et al. (6) emphasized that manual GPP up to size 20 K-file is necessary to ensure the safe use of ProTaper instruments. Their findings obtained in simulated resin canals using Endo-Training-Blocks are consistent with the results of the present study conducted using extracted human teeth, indicating that the protective effect of manual GPP is maintained under conditions closer to the clinical setting.

The standardized use of an SX orifice opener in all groups ensured straight-line coronal access, reduced coronal interferences, and facilitated more controlled instrument progression. This uniform coronal shaping approach minimized torsional stress on the shaping instruments and ensured that differences in surface defects were attributable to the glide path technique rather than variations in coronal resistance.

Additionally, a standardized 1-minute NaOCl irrigation period was used after coronal shaping to ensure uniform chemical action on canal walls before glide path procedures. This duration provided consistent soft-tissue dissolution and debris removal while avoiding prolonged exposure that could alter dentin surface properties or confound comparisons among groups. The controlled waiting time therefore contributed to methodological standardization rather than representing a variable intended for outcome assessment.

Consistent with the findings of the present study, Ha and Park (9) demonstrated that GPP with ISO #20 K-files significantly reduced the screw-in effect and torque generation during simulated RCP in resin root canals using Endo-Training Blocks. This suggests that GPP not only preserves the surface integrity of NiTi files but also minimizes biomechanical stresses, thereby reinforcing its clinical relevance. Although the evaluated outcomes differed—surface defects in the present study versus torque and screw-in effect in Ha and Park's study—both investigations converge on the broader implication that GPP reduces mechanical stresses and enhances the safety of RCP. Importantly, the markedly higher incidence of fractures observed in the no-glide-path control group of the present study further supports this interpretation, as it highlights the vulnerability of NiTi files to stress accumulation in the absence of GPP.

Similarly, Patel et al. (10) conducted an in vivo comparison of manual and mechanical GPP and reported no statistically significant differences in the surface topography of subsequent RCP instruments between the two methods. Since their study was performed under clinical conditions, including a no–glide path control group would not have been ethically feasible. In contrast, the ex vivo design of the present study allowed controlled comparison with a no–glide-path group, providing complementary rather than corrective evidence to the existing clinical data. Likewise, Jonker et al. (8) found no significant difference between manual and mechanical GPP regarding the number of simulated blocks prepared before fracture of the reciprocating WaveOne instrument (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). Consistent with these findings, the present study also revealed no significant difference between manual and mechanical GPP with respect to defect incidence, although both approaches were superior to the no-glide-path group. Moreover, no significant differences were found among the groups with respect to flute disruption and tip deformation. These non-significant findings may indicate that such defects represent early-stage or minor surface changes, which could occur regardless of GPP. It is therefore possible that the progression of these defects into more severe alterations, such as blade distortion or fractures, is more strongly influenced by the presence or absence of a glide path.

The absence of baseline SEM imaging for each instrument was acknowledged as a limitation, since manufacturing-related defects could not be excluded (15). To address this concern, the present study obtained baseline SEM images for all NiTi rotary files before clinical simulation, allowing subsequent comparisons to be based on changes from the initial condition. This approach minimized the potential influence of pre-existing defects on the evaluation of alterations after RCP. Importantly, baseline SEM examinations revealed that certain surface irregularities were already present in unused instruments, indicating that not all detected defects originated from canal shaping. This distinction between manufacturing-related imperfections and shaping-induced alterations strengthened the interpretive validity of the findings and underscores the importance of careful handling and single-use recommendations for NiTi rotary instruments.

Autoclave sterilization was performed before the baseline SEM evaluation and again after RCP to reproduce standard clinical reprocessing protocols. Previous studies (16, 17) evaluating surface characteristics of NiTi instruments have indicated that the effects of sterilization are cycle-dependent, with minimal or no detectable surface alteration after one or a few cycles, whereas more pronounced roughness changes occur only after multiple repeated sterilization cycles. For example, Alex et al. (16) reported no statistically significant change in surface characteristics of HyFlex EDM (Coltene/Whaledent, Switzerland) and WaveOne Gold (Dentsply Maillefer, Switzerland) files after repeated autoclave cycles. Similarly, Yılmaz et al. (17) demonstrated that noticeable increases in surface alterations occurred only after 5–10 autoclave cycles, not after a single cycle. Considering that all instruments in the present study underwent the same limited and standardized sterilization protocol, any potential influence of autoclaving would have been uniformly distributed across groups, and therefore would not bias the comparative interpretation of surface defects. Because all instruments were subjected to an identical single-cycle sterilization procedure prior to imaging, autoclave-related alterations—if present—would have been minimal and consistent across groups.

Both manual and mechanical GPP demonstrated comparable protection against surface defects. From a clinical perspective, manual GPP may be advantageous in narrow, highly curved, or calcified canals, where enhanced tactile control allows gradual canal negotiation and reduces the risk of apical transportation. In contrast, mechanical GPP may be preferred in canals with sufficient initial patency, as it can decrease chair time, reduce operator fatigue, and provide consistent glide path enlargement. Technique selection may be guided by root canal morphology, the clinician's familiarity with the system, and the availability of instruments.

Future studies should explore whether the protective effect of GPP is consistent across different NiTi rotary systems, alloy compositions, and kinematic motions. Incorporating quantitative surface evaluation techniques such as atomic force microscopy or 3D profilometry may allow more precise measurement of defect severity and progression. Additionally, experimental models that simulate dynamic intracanal conditions—such as periodontal ligament support, real-time torque and force monitoring, and irrigation at body temperature—may provide a more clinically relevant representation of mechanical stresses. Ultimately, well-designed clinical studies are needed to clarify whether the reduction in surface defects observed under ex vivo conditions translates into lower rates of instrument deformation or fracture in routine practice.

In the present study, conductive coating was not required prior to SEM imaging because the NiTi instruments were metallic and inherently conductive. This eliminated the need for sputter coating procedures commonly used for non-conductive samples and allowed direct surface evaluation without introducing coating-related artifacts.

This study has some limitations. First, only one type of NiTi rotary instrument was tested, which may restrict the generalizability of the results to other file systems or alloys. Second, surface defects were classified dichotomously as present or absent, without quantifying their extent or severity. Third, GPP was limited to a single file size in both manual and mechanical groups, whereas alternative instruments or sizes might yield different outcomes. Fourth, for standardization purposes, only one surface of each instrument was examined under SEM, potentially overlooking defects on other surfaces. Moreover, although baseline SEM imaging allowed identification of pre-existing surface irregularities, these defects were not evenly distributed across the experimental groups. This imbalance may have partially confounded the interpretation of whether certain alterations were induced by instrumentation or were already present prior to RCP. Additionally, the mechanical glide path file (#13/.02) provided slightly greater apical enlargement compared to the manual #10/.02 file. Although RCP was standardized using the same #10/.04 NiTi rotary instrument in all groups, this difference in initial canal diameter may have influenced stress distribution during RCP and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, while autoclaving was standardized across groups, the possibility that sterilization contributed minimally to surface alteration cannot be completely excluded. Moreover, this was an ex vivo study conducted on extracted human teeth under controlled laboratory conditions; therefore, factors present in vivo—such as periapical tissue resistance, dynamic irrigation flow, and patient-related anatomical variability—were not replicated. These considerations may limit the direct clinical generalizability of the results.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, GPP significantly reduced blade distortion, surface pitting, microcrack formation, and fractures, with no significant differences observed between manual and mechanical techniques. By minimizing file damage regardless of the technique used, GPP may reduce the risk of unexpected instrument fracture, enhance clinical efficiency, and contribute to safer and more predictable root canal procedures.
