From Intensive Care Unit Prediction to Intensive Care Unit Accountability: A Global Readiness Passport for Machine-learning Prognostication
Authors: Mulavagili Vijayasimha, Mulavagili Srikanth, Ravinder Kaur, Bhawana Thukral, Kanakala P Malini, Simranpreet Kaur
Published: 2026

The Editor,

We read with great interest the timely article by Samavedam, “Beyond Conventional Severity Scores: Machine Learning and the Future of Intensive Care Unit Prognostication,” published in Indian Journal of Critical Care Medicine.1 The article appropriately points out the potential of machine learning to enhance the prognosis in the intensive care unit (ICU). However, the next scientific challenge is not merely whether machine-learning models can predict mortality better than conventional scores, but whether such models can be implemented safely, equitably, and accountably across heterogeneous ICUs.

A prediction model that performs well in a development dataset may fail when transferred to a different ICU because of case-mix variation, missing data, changing treatment protocols, resource limitations, or altered clinician behavior. Therefore, ICU artificial intelligence should not move from publication to bedside use on the basis of discrimination statistics alone. Calibration, external validation, subgroup performance, clinical utility, interpretability, and post-deployment monitoring are equally important. The TRIPOD+AI statement has emphasized transparent reporting for prediction models using regression or machine-learning methods.2 For critical care, this transparency must be extended into implementation governance.

We propose an ICU-AI Readiness Passport as a pragmatic framework before machine-learning prognostic tools are adopted in clinical practice. This passport may include five domains. First, dataset legitimacy, including source population, ICU type, illness severity, missing data handling, outcome definition, and representation of low- and middle-income settings. Second, performance beyond the area under the curve, including calibration, decision-curve analysis, external validation, and subgroup fairness. Third, workflow accountability, which defines the timing, recipients, and expected actions for the alert and the final clinical accountability. Fourth, equity-by-design, where models are not underperforming for elderly patients, women, rural referrals, sepsis phenotypes, socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, or hospitals with different nurse-to-patient ratios. Fifth, post-deployment surveillance, including calibration drift, alert fatigue, automation bias, unintended limitation of care, and patient outcome displacement.

This resonates with the DECIDE-AI framework, which aims to consider early clinical evaluation, human factors, and implementation context for AI-based decision-support systems.3 It also highlights the recent consensus recommendations from critical care experts that AI in critical care should be human-centered, clinically interpretable, a priori tested, and monitored continuously.4 In addition, the literature on human-AI teaming indicates that there may be disagreements between clinicians and data scientists regarding the level of automation that is appropriate, and that intensivists, nurses, data scientists, ethicists, administrators, and representatives of the patients should be engaged in co-designing ICU-AI governance.5

This is a significant international matter. There is no uniformity in electronic health records, data engineers, or in real-time model monitoring infrastructure in many ICUs in Asia, Africa, Latin America, and resource-variable health systems. Machine learning could actually exacerbate critical-care inequity if medical standards for using it apply only to digitally advanced hospitals. If critical care standards for machine learning are developed to only apply to digitally sophisticated hospitals, then the technology could actually worsen critical care inequality. On the other hand, if journals and professional societies encourage transparent validation and equity audits and promote implementation passports, resource-variable settings can be champions of responsible ICU-AI translation.

In summary, the use of machine learning in ICU prognostication should be embraced, but adopting this technology at the bedside needs more than just predictive power. Prediction with accountability, innovation with equity, and automation with clinical responsibility is the future of ICU-AI. The Indian Journal of Critical Care Medicine is uniquely poised to encourage this next milestone in worldwide responsible critical-care innovation.
