Intermittent fasting rewires tissue-specific gene–transposable element regulatory networks
Authors: Xiangru Cheng, Yibo Fan, Xiangyuan Peng, Nishat I Tabassum, Dong-Gyu Jo, Terrance G Johns, Sureshkumar Balasubramanian, Sridevi Sureshkumar, Thiruma V Arumugam, Martha Field
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntermittent fasting (IF) is a dietary intervention known to promote systemic health benefits, yet its impact on genome-wide transcriptional regulatory networks, particularly those involving transposable elements (TEs), remains poorly understood. This study investigates the multitissue transcriptomic response to chronic IF in mice, focusing on TE regulation and its integration with host gene networks. We subjected C57BL/6 mice to 16 h of daily fasting for 4 months and performed RNA-seq on liver, skeletal muscle, and cortex tissues. Using locus-specific TE quantification, we found that IF induces profound, tissue-specific changes in TE expression, with the liver showing the strongest response (5,359 differentially expressed TEs), followed by skeletal muscle (620), while minimal changes were observed in the cortex. Integrated co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) in the liver and muscle revealed IF-responsive TEs that co-vary with nearby genes, forming distinct co-expression modules. Functional enrichment of genes proximal to co-expressed TEs within these modules highlighted clear tissue-specific regulatory programs. In the liver, the enriched terms were predominantly associated with translation and metabolism, whereas in skeletal muscle, the enriched pathways were involved in muscle contraction, mitochondrial organization, and chromatin modification. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed strong, significant co-expression between TEs and their proximal genes within these modules, suggesting that TEs may exert potential cis-regulatory effects on adjacent genes. Taken together, our results provide a high-resolution atlas of TE regulation under IF and demonstrate that TEs are integral components of tissue-specific transcriptional networks reshaped by fasting. These findings offer new insights into how dietary interventions influence gene regulatory systems.Keywords: intermittent fasting, transposable elements, gene regulation, transcriptional regulatory networks, cis-regulatory effects

This study reveals that intermittent fasting (IF) induces widespread, tissue-specific reprogramming of transposable elements (TEs), repetitive genomic sequences traditionally viewed as “junk DNA.” Using multitissue transcriptomics in mice, we show that IF dramatically alters TE expression in the liver and skeletal muscle but not brain and that these TEs are tightly integrated into co-expression networks with metabolic genes. TE expression significantly correlates with proximal genes involved in lipid metabolism and muscle contraction, suggesting TEs actively contribute to the physiological benefits of IF. These findings establish TEs as previously unrecognized participants in dietary adaptation.

Introduction

Dietary restriction (DR), including caloric restriction and intermittent fasting (IF), is widely recognized as one of the most robust nongenetic interventions for improving metabolic health and extending health span across diverse species. In humans, DR interventions have been associated with enhanced insulin sensitivity, improved cardiovascular function, reduced systemic inflammation, and greater metabolic flexibility (1). Despite these beneficial effects, the physiological responses to DR are highly context dependent, varying according to factors such as age, sex, nutritional status, and duration or severity of restriction. Together, these findings underscore the importance of elucidating the molecular mechanisms through which DR exerts tissue-specific effects and influences organismal health. IF known to extend health span, improve metabolic function, and enhance stress resilience across multiple organisms (2). Its systemic benefits, including autophagy induction, mitochondrial biogenesis, and metabolic switching, are well documented (3). Over the past decade, we have investigated the genome-wide impacts of IF through transcriptomic and proteomic analyses and found that IF substantially alters gene expression and reshapes the proteome (4–12). Our work, together with findings from other groups, demonstrates that IF remodels the epigenetic landscape, leading to changes in gene expression and protein profiles that confer protection against neurological disease conditions (4, 7, 9, 12). Despite these advances, the role of IF-responsive transposable elements (TEs) in shaping gene expression programs remains unresolved.

TEs are major contributors to epigenetic and transcriptional changes in response to environmental cues across diverse organisms (13). TEs are increasingly recognized as important regulators of gene expression and genome function. A vast portion of the mammalian genome consists of TEs, which are repetitive DNA sequences that can move within the genome and influence genomic architecture and gene expression (14). While the evolutionary influence of TEs is well established (15, 16), their role in regulating gene expression and chromatin state, particularly in response to environmental and metabolic stress in mammals, is increasingly recognized (17, 18). Beyond their repetitive presence in the genome, TE-derived sequences can dictate enhancer/promoter activity and transcription factor binding sites, thereby influencing the transcription of nearby genes in a cis-regulatory manner (19, 20). Emerging evidence suggests that TE-mediated regulatory networks contribute to tissue-specific transcriptional programs thereby enabling metabolic adaptation (21, 22). In this context, understanding how IF influences TE activity could provide crucial insights into the mechanisms driving systemic transcriptomic remodeling during metabolic stress. Metabolic challenges, such as high-fat diet, have recently been shown to perturb the epigenetic silencing of TEs, leading to their transcriptional reactivation, which may subsequently impact nearby gene regulatory networks (23). This raises the possibility that TEs act as molecular conduits through which dietary interventions exert wide-ranging effects. However, whether dietary interventions such as IF lead to genomic reprogramming of TEs remains an open question. IF impacts differ between tissues, as reflected in tissue-dependent gene expression and epigenomic profiles (10, 11). These observations raise a fundamental question: does IF modulate TE activity in a tissue-specific manner? Despite growing interest in the regulatory roles of TEs, a comprehensive multitissue understanding of how IF reshapes the TE transcriptome and its integration with host gene regulatory networks remains lacking. We hypothesize that IF induces tissue-specific, genome-wide reprogramming of TE activity, thereby contributing to the transcriptional and physiological adaptations associated with fasting.

We tested a murine model of 16-h daily IF over 4 months to profile transcriptomic changes in the liver, skeletal muscle, and cortex. We show that IF exerts substantial genome-wide impacts on TEs in addition to its effects on protein-coding genes. Using locus-specific TE quantification and integrated gene–TE co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), we mapped the landscape of IF-responsive TEs and their associated gene networks. Our results demonstrate that IF causes significant changes in TE expression that differ between tissues. These tissue-dependent changes are embedded within co-expression modules controlling metabolism and stress response. By examining the correlation between IF-induced changes in TE expression and the expression of their proximal genes (TPGs), we reveal potential cis-regulatory relationships.

Materials and methods

Animal handling and intermittent fasting protocols

All in vivo procedures were reviewed and approved by the La Trobe University Animal Care and Use Committee (Ethics ID: AEC21047) and were carried out in compliance with the Australian Code for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes (8th edition) as well as the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Measures were taken throughout the study to limit animal discomfort and to use the minimum number of animals required. All experimental reporting adhered to ARRIVE guidelines. Male C57BL/6 mice were obtained at 4 weeks of age from the Animal Resources Centre, Australia, and maintained in the La Trobe University animal facility. Animals were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark schedule, with lights on from 07:00 to 19:00. Mice were provided a standard Barastoc Mice Maintenance Cube diet supplied by Ridley. This fixed-formulation diet contains cereal grains, including wheat and dehulled oats; animal protein sources, including meat and bone meal and fishmeal; vegetable protein meal, including soybean meal; skim milk powder; wheat-derived cereal by-products; molasses; salt; canola-origin vegetable oil; amino acids; vitamins; and trace minerals. The typical nutrient composition of the diet includes 20% crude protein, 8.5% crude fat, 3.2% crude fiber, 13.2 MJ/kg digestible energy, 1.1% calcium, 0.96% phosphorus, 0.35% sodium, 0.89% potassium, 0.57% chlorine, 0.25% magnesium, 1.22% lysine, 0.38% methionine, 3.11% linoleic acid, and 25% starch. The diet also contains added vitamins and trace minerals, including vitamins A, D3, E, K3, B1, B2, B6, and B12, niacin, pantothenic acid, biotin, folic acid, iron, zinc, manganese, copper, selenium, molybdenum, cobalt, and iodine. Water was supplied ad libitum (AL) for all groups. At 6 weeks of age, mice were randomly allocated to dietary intervention groups: a 16-h IF regimen (n = 8) or AL feeding (n = 8). IF mice underwent daily fasting from 16:00 to 08:00 for a duration of 4 months, whereas AL mice had unrestricted access to food. The tissues analyzed in this study were obtained from the parental animals of the cohort previously reported by Fan et al. (24), in which the physiological and metabolic effects of IF were characterized. While the previous study focused on parental metabolic phenotypes and offspring outcomes, the present study examines transcriptomic and TE regulatory changes in tissues collected from the parental mice. As reported in Fan et al. (24), AL-fed parental mice exhibited significantly greater body weight gain than IF parental mice. In contrast, IF parental mice showed significantly lower blood glucose levels, significantly higher ketone levels, and significantly reduced HbA1c levels relative to AL-fed controls. All animals were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation between 07:00 and 12:00. At the time of sacrifice, mice in the IF group remained within their fasting period, whereas AL-fed control mice continued to have unrestricted access to food. Thus, tissue collection from IF mice was performed under fasting conditions, whereas AL mice were collected under normal feeding conditions. Following euthanasia, mice were perfused with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and tissues were collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

RNA extraction and transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was isolated from liver, skeletal muscle, and cortical tissues collected from mice subjected to different feeding regimens, either AL or IF. Whole cerebral cortex tissue was dissected according to standard anatomical landmarks rather than isolating specific cortical subregions. RNA-seq was carried out by Novogene using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. A 150-bp paired-end, strand-specific sequencing was carried out to obtain 12 Gb of quality-controlled raw sequence output for each of the samples. After read cleaning using fastp (25), these clean reads were further processed for quantifying transcripts. To quantify TEs, we used the SQuIRE pipeline (26), which integrates reference genome retrieval, read alignment, TE annotation, and quantitative TE expression analysis. Briefly, reads were aligned to the mm39 mouse reference genome using STAR (27). Read mapping statistics were provided in Table S1. TEs were annotated with the Repeatmasker and TE features overlapping with annotated exons of genes were removed using BEDtools (28). TE expression was quantified at the level of individual genomic locus and as aggregated TE subfamilies. Differentially expressed TEs (DETEs) and genes were identified using DESeq2 (29), with a significance threshold of Padj < 0.05. To characterize organ-specific TE expression under AL conditions, we first identified TEs expressed in the liver, cortex, and muscle. Within each tissue, a TE was classified as expressed if it showed a raw count >10 in at least four of the eight AL control samples. These expressed TEs were then matched to the annotation database to retrieve their corresponding Clade and Family classifications. Categories contributing <1% of the total TE pool were consolidated into an “Other (<1%)” group. Tissue-specific TE distributions were visualized using ggplot2 (30), generating pie charts at the Clade level and stacked bar plots at the Family level.

Data preprocessing and feature selection

Independent co-expression networks were generated for liver and muscle datasets. Raw RNA-seq counts were processed using a standardized workflow for weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Each experimental group (IF and AL) included eight biological replicates, and genes and TEs were retained only if they showed raw counts >10 in at least four samples (50% of replicates) within either group. A variance-stabilizing transformation was then applied. From the transformed dataset, the 8,000 genes and 8,000 TEs with the highest coefficients of variation (CV) across all 16 samples were selected to retain the most informative and dynamically regulated features for network construction. Different feature cutoffs above and below 8,000 were evaluated, and 8,000 was selected as an intermediate threshold that balanced capturing biologically relevant variation with minimizing the inclusion of low-variance noise features that could reduce network robustness. These filtered and normalized expression matrices were subsequently used for network construction.

Weighted co-expression network construction and module characterization

Signed co-expression networks for liver and muscle were independently generated using the WGCNA R package (31). For each tissue, the soft-thresholding power (β) was determined with the pickSoftThreshold function to approximate scale-free topology. Networks were constructed using biweight midcorrelation (bicor) with the following settings: minModuleSize = 30, deepSplit = 2, mergeCutHeight = 0.25, and pamStage = TRUE. Module–trait relationships were evaluated by correlating module eigengenes with binary vectors representing IF and AL treatment groups. To identify modules of biological relevance, feature significance (FS) and module membership (MM) were computed for all genes and TEs. FS was defined as the bicor correlation between each feature's expression and the treatment vector, while MM represented the correlation between the feature and its module eigengene. Features were classified as significant TEs when |FS| > 0.5 and MM > 0.7, and as significant genes when |FS| > 0.6 and MM > 0.8. Analyses focused on three modules, blue, turquoise, and brown, that exhibited the strongest association with the IF condition in both liver and muscle. FS–MM scatterplots for these modules were generated using ggplot2, with genes and TEs differentiated by point shape, and linear regression lines added to illustrate overall trends. For network visualization, a subset of 30 features per module was selected based on intramodular connectivity (kWithin). Graphs were constructed using the igraph package and rendered with ggraph. Within each module, the seven features with the highest kWithin values were placed at the center as hub nodes, with remaining features arranged in an outer ring. Node size reflected kWithin, and genes and TEs were distinguished by both shape and color. All visualizations were produced in R using the tidyverse framework for data handling. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed independently on gene and TE expression datasets using the variance-stabilizing transformation (VST)-normalized, CV-filtered matrices. PCA was computed with the prcomp function in R with scaling enabled. Sample distributions were visualized on the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), with IF samples shown in red and AL samples in blue. The R code used for WGCNA analysis (including data preprocessing, network construction, module detection, and visualization), demonstrated using liver tissue data as an example, is provided in Table S2; the same analysis was applied separately to muscle tissue in this study.

Definition of transposable element–proximal genes

TE–gene proximity was defined using two spatial criteria: (i) intragenic proximity, where a TE was located within the annotated boundaries of a gene, and (ii) extragenic proximal proximity, where a TE overlapped a 5-kb window either upstream of the transcription start site or downstream of the transcription end site (32, 33). Genes meeting either of these positional relationships were included in all downstream TE functional enrichment analyses. These same TE–gene pairs were also used in subsequent TE–gene correlation analyses.

Module functional enrichment analysis

For TE focused enrichment, significant TEs (|FS| > 0.5 and MM > 0.7) from each co-expression module were first filtered to retain only those that were differentially expressed between AL and IF groups (Padj < 0.05). The resulting DETEs were then used to identify their corresponding TPGs, which were subsequently subjected to GO and KEGG pathway analysis. For gene-based enrichment, significant genes (|FS| > 0.6 and MM > 0.8) were directly filtered by differential expression (Padj < 0.05) prior to enrichment testing. All enrichment analyses were conducted using clusterProfiler (34) with Fisher's exact test and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment (Padj < 0.05).

Module GO term overlap analysis

Overlap among significant GO terms (Padj < 0.05) was assessed across six analyses performed for each tissue. These analyses included: (i) three gene-based sets derived directly from the blue, turquoise, and brown co-expression modules, containing significant genes (|FS| > 0.6 and MM > 0.8), and (ii) three TPG sets generated from significant TEs (|FS| > 0.5 and MM > 0.7) within the same modules. Shared and unique GO terms across these six sets were visualized using the UpSetR package (35).

Correlation analysis between TEs and proximal genes

To evaluate potential regulatory interactions, TE–gene pairs from the three IF-responsive co-expression modules (blue, turquoise, and brown) were examined in the liver and muscle datasets (16 samples per tissue: eight IF and eight AL). Significant TEs (|FS| > 0.5 and MM > 0.7) were matched with their previously defined proximal genes. Following the linear regression framework similar to that implemented in the TEffectR package (36), expression values were normalized using the voom transformation. For each TE–gene pair, a linear model of the form gene expression ∼ TE expression + Treatment was fitted, with the TE effect represented by the regression coefficient (β). Statistical significance was assessed using t tests followed by false discovery rate (FDR) correction, applying thresholds of nominal P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.05. Results were visualized using volcano plots and scatterplots, and the same analytical workflow was applied to both liver and muscle tissues. BAM files were visualized in Integrative Genomics Viewer to generate coverage plots for visual inspection of RNA-seq read coverage at representative TE–gene loci (37).

GO term–gene–TE relationship visualization

A three-layer network was generated to illustrate potential regulatory relationships in the liver tissue. For each co-expression module, significant TEs (|FS| > 0.5 and MM > 0.7) were identified, and their expression correlations with proximal genes were evaluated. All TE–gene pairs showing significant correlations (P < 0.05) across the three modules were combined into a single dataset. The gene components of these pairs were then subjected to GO enrichment analysis, and four representative GO terms meeting the significance threshold (Padj < 0.05) were selected to form the functional layer of the network.

Results

Intermittent fasting induces differential expression of transposable elements

To investigate the impact of time-restricted feeding on TEs, we performed RNA-seq on liver, skeletal muscle, and cortex from mice subjected to 4 months of IF or unrestricted feeding (AL) and compared their expression profiles using a defined workflow created with BioRender (Fig. 1A and B). We identified a substantial number of DETEs in response to IF (Fig. 1C–E). Interestingly, we observed clear differences between the tissues in the magnitude of the IF impact on TE expression (Figs. 1C–E and S1). The liver showed the strongest response, with 2,065 significantly up-regulated and 3,065 down-regulated TE loci (Padj < 0.05, |log2FC| > 1) in IF compared with AL (Fig. 1C). Skeletal muscle showed a less pronounced yet significant response, with 301 up-regulated and 107 down-regulated DETEs (Fig. 1D), while the cortex remained largely unaffected, with only 10 significant DETEs (Fig. 1E). An analysis in which we aggregated TEs at the levels of subfamilies also corroborated these findings, highlighting robust changes in the liver and muscle but limited shifts in cortex (Fig. S2). These findings suggested that IF has a significant impact on TE expression and this effect is highly tissue dependent. We also observed that the baseline TE expression under AL conditions also varied between tissues, suggesting a basal tissue dependence in TE landscape (Fig. S3). Consistent with this, PCA of highly variable TEs confirmed clear separation by treatment (IF) group in the liver and muscle, but not in cortex (Fig. S4). There was minimal overlap between DETEs across liver, muscle, and cortex, which suggested that the effects of IF on TE are tissue specific (Fig. S5; for TE identities, see Table S3). These findings underscore the organ-selective nature of TE regulation under IF with the liver showing the most distinct and diverse TE expression profile.

Differential expression analysis revealed marked tissue-specific changes in TE expression following IF (Fig. 2). In the liver, a substantially larger number of DETEs were detected compared with skeletal muscle, indicating a stronger TE transcriptional response in this tissue. As shown in Fig. 2A, short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) were the most abundant class of DETEs in the liver, followed by long terminal repeats (LTRs) and long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), whereas DNA transposons showed relatively few changes. Both up-regulated and down-regulated SINE elements were highly represented, although the down-regulated TEs showed modest predominance. Similarly, skeletal muscle also displayed enrichment of differentially expressed SINEs and LTRs, although the overall number of altered TEs was considerably lower than in the liver (Fig. 2B). Analysis of TE families further demonstrated distinct tissue-specific patterns. In the liver, the B2 and Alu SINE families were the most prominently represented DETE families, followed by the LINE family L1 and the LTR family endogenous retrovirus-like (ERVL) and mammalian apparent LTR-retrotransposon (MaLR) (ERVL-MaLR) (Fig. 2C). Additional enriched families included B4 and ERVK elements. In skeletal muscle, Alu and ERVL-MaLR families exhibited the greatest number of DETEs, followed by L1, B2, and ERVK families (Fig. 2D). Together, these findings demonstrate that IF induces broad yet tissue-dependent remodeling of TE expression, with SINE-associated elements constituting the dominant responsive TE group across metabolically active tissues.

One important methodological consideration in TE transcriptomics is the potential overestimation of locus-specific TE expression arising from intronic reads and intron retention artifacts (38). To assess intron retention bias, we used IRFinder (39), a pipeline to assess intron retention, and calculated the mean IRratio per intron (n = 16/tissue). In the liver, 2,936 of 5,359 DETEs (54.8%) were intronic, of which only 603 (20.5% of intronic DETEs, 11.3% of all DETEs) fell into introns with IRratio > 0.2. In muscle, 334 of 620 DETEs (53.9%) were intronic, of which only 56 (16.8% of intronic DETEs, 9.0% of all DETEs) fell into introns with IRratio > 0.2. Thus, intron retention does not systematically bias our TE analysis (see Table S4).

Integrated gene–TE co-expression networks reveal IF-responsive modules in metabolic tissues

To investigate the system-level coordination between genes and TEs under IF, we constructed independent signed co-expression networks for liver and skeletal muscle applying WGCNA (Fig. 3). For each tissue, the same filtering and normalization procedures were applied independently. The top 8,000 genes and top 8,000 TEs with the highest CV were then selected and combined for network construction. In the liver dataset, the optimal soft-thresholding power (β = 11) was selected using the pickSoftThreshold function from WGCNA package to ensure scale-free topology (Fig. 3A). This yielded four distinct co-expression modules (Fig. 3B and C). Module–trait correlation analysis identified three modules: blue, turquoise, and brown, as most strongly associated with the IF condition (Fig. 3D), each exhibiting high correlation coefficients and statistically significant P-values, indicative of robust transcriptional responses to IF. A parallel WGCNA in skeletal muscle, using a soft-thresholding power of β = 9 (Fig. 3E), revealed a more complex modular architecture comprising 21 modules (Fig. 3F and G). It is also notable that, in muscle tissue, the modules with matching color labels (blue, turquoise, and brown), despite having different profiles, showed the strongest correlations with IF treatment (Fig. 3H). Within these IF-associated modules, genes and TEs displayed tightly correlated expression profiles, reinforcing their potential roles in metabolic adaptation. These modules were prioritized for downstream analyses, including functional enrichment, TE–gene co-expression mapping, and network visualization.

IF-associated modules are enriched for distinct biological processes linked to TE-proximal genes

Having identified three modules (blue, turquoise, and brown) in both liver and muscle, we next asked whether the genes and TEs contributing to these modules were also associated with IF (Figs. 4A–I and 5A–I). We looked at the relationship between FS for the IF trait and MM in the liver (Fig. 4A, D, and G) and muscle (Fig. 5A, D, and G), which revealed strong correlations suggesting that IF is indeed a key component driving these modules. To visualize these interactions, we selected the top 30 features with the highest intramodular connectivity (kWithin) in each module, where the TEs (triangles) were integrated alongside protein-coding genes (circles) in the liver (Fig. 4B, E, and H) and muscle (Fig. 5B, E, and H). We observed even among the top 30 interactions, the hubs often included TEs, suggestive of the strong influence of TEs in reprogramming the transcriptome. We also observed a module (Fig. 4H, liver brown), which is primarily driven by a network of TEs.

To infer which biological processes these co-expressed TEs might influence, we performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on genes located proximally to TEs that were significant within each module (|FS| > 0.5, MM > 0.7) in the liver (Fig. 4C, F, and I) and muscle (Fig. 5C, F, and I). In the liver, TPGs in each module were enriched for distinct biological processes. TPGs in the blue module were enriched for terms related to metabolic processes, catabolic processes, and biosynthetic processes (Fig. 4C). TPGs in the turquoise module were strongly associated with small-molecule catabolic processes and carboxylic acid and oxoacid metabolism (Fig. 4F), consistent with the liver's central metabolic functions. TPGs in the brown module also showed enrichment for terms involving metabolic processes and catabolic processes (Fig. 4I). Direct enrichment analysis of the highly connected genes (|FS| > 0.6, MM > 0.8) within these modules yielded highly similar GO terms (Fig. S6), indicating convergence between TPGs and overall module functions. UpSet analysis further confirmed substantial overlap between GO terms derived from direct gene sets and those from TPG sets in the liver (Fig. S7), demonstrating a nonrandom grouping of TEs in the modules and functionally relevant connections among the TE–gene networks in the liver.

In skeletal muscle, a parallel approach revealed distinct enrichments. The blue module (Fig. 5A and B) TPGs were enriched for muscle system processes, response to ketone, cell differentiation, and cell development (Fig. 5C). The turquoise module (Fig. 5D and E) TPGs displayed an enrichment for neuromuscular processes, muscle contraction, development, and ion transport (Fig. 5F). The brown module (Fig. 5G and H) showed enrichment for metabolic and catabolic processes (Fig. 5I). Similar to liver, when the gene lists of IF-impacted genes were used for enrichment analysis, they resulted in similar enrichment as that of the TPGs (Fig. S8), which was further supported by an UpSet analysis (Fig. S9). Taken together, these analyses further support a nonrandom grouping of TEs with functional connections, raising the possibility that TE-mediated gene expression regulation could mediate some of these functional impacts.

TE expression significantly correlates with the expression levels of TPGs in IF-responsive modules

We next probed potential regulatory relationships by performing linear modeling to assess expression correlations between significantly IF-responsive TEs (|FS| > 0.5, MM > 0.7) within the three key WGCNA modules (blue, turquoise, and brown) and their proximally located genes (within gene boundaries or ±5 kb). Using linear modeling, we identified significant TE–gene pairs whose expression levels were significantly correlated, with significance defined as both raw P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.05 (Fig. 6A). Most associations were positive, indicating that when a TE was up-regulated by IF, its neighboring gene tended to show a similar pattern. For example, strong positive correlations were observed between the IF-responsive RSINE1 elements (at specific genomic loci) and the expression levels of genes Acot4 and Angptl8, both showing high correlation coefficients and tight linear fits across samples (Fig. 6B). Likewise, ORR1A3-INT and L2B elements were positively associated with the expression levels of genes Apoa5 (apolipoprotein A5) and Pygl (glycogen phosphorylase), respectively. Apoa5 and Pygl further link TE activation with hepatic lipid and glycogen metabolism (Fig. 6B). A similar picture emerged in skeletal muscle, where we detected several significant TE–gene pairs, again dominated by positive correlations between TE and proximal gene expression (Fig. 6C). For instance, B4A elements correlated with Fam13a and Lipe, and L1MDA_III and ORR1D2 elements correlated with Hacd1 and Ucp3, respectively, each exhibiting strong linear fits across AL and IF groups (Fig. 6D).

To understand how these relationships fit into broader biological programs, we constructed a three-layer liver network, from outer to inner: TE, gene, and biological process, linking significantly correlated TE–gene pairs from all three modules to representative enriched GO terms (Padj < 0.05). The resulting network strikingly converged on “cellular ketone metabolic process,” “fatty acid metabolic process,” and “regulation of lipid metabolic process” (Fig. 6E). This network could potentially explain how IF-responsive TEs are embedded within coherent metabolic pathways, suggesting that TE modulation under IF is closely coordinated with transcriptional programs that control lipid and energy metabolism. To further visualize representative TE–gene relationships identified in the correlation analyses, we examined RNA-seq coverage tracks at selected genomic loci in the liver tissue (Fig. S10). These browsers revealed coordinated IF-associated changes in expression between specific TE loci, such as RSINE1, ORR1A3-INT, and L2B elements, and their nearby metabolic genes including Acot4, Angptl8, Apoa5, and Pygl, supporting the observed positive correlations between IF-responsive TEs and proximal gene expression.

Discussion

Our study provides the first comprehensive, multitissue analysis of how IF reshapes transcriptional dynamics of TEs. We have demonstrated that IF is a potent regulator of the TE expression. TEs have been previously suggested and shown to be responsive to environmental stressors (13, 40, 41). However, there has not been an exploration of whether cells/organisms perceive IF as an environmental perturbation and reprogram their transcriptomes. Our studies suggest that IF may be perceived as an environmental perturbation, leading to significant changes in TE expression that reprogram the cellular transcriptome and alter physiological programs. The physiological impacts of IF clearly differ between organs and tissues. We have shown here that the effect of IF on TEs is strikingly tissue dependent, which suggests that it is not a general random environmental impact on TEs but a deeply integrated co-expression architecture of protein-coding genes with functional consequences. These findings position TEs as one of the active, coordinated participants in the organism-wide transcriptional reprogramming induced by DR.

The liver, a central metabolic organ, displayed the most pronounced TE response, while the cortex remained largely unchanged. Skeletal muscle showed an intermediate response. This hierarchy of responsiveness aligns with the known metabolic intensity of these tissues and the central role of the liver in nutrient sensing and adaptation (42, 43). The sheer number of DETEs in the liver suggests that metabolic stress from IF may transiently relax epigenetic silencing of TEs by DNA methylation and histone modifications (44, 45). The relative stability of the cortical TE transcriptome could reflect the brain's privileged metabolic status and/or more robust epigenetic safeguarding of neuronal genomes, where somatic TE mobilization poses a particular risk to genomic integrity (46, 47). The clear differences in baseline TE expression landscapes across tissues further suggest that tissue-specific epigenetic environments predetermine which TE loci are poised to respond to environmental/metabolic cues like IF.

A central advance of our study is the application of integrated co-expression network analysis, which revealed that IF-responsive TEs are not expressed randomly but are embedded within highly organized, functionally meaningful, gene modules. In the liver, the TEs association modules were linked to translation and core metabolism, consistent with a potential role in fine-tuning anabolic and catabolic processes during feeding-fasting cycles. In muscle, the module enrichments point toward mitochondrial functions, and muscle contraction directly connects TE activity to known improvements in oxidative metabolism and physical performance, hallmarks of the IF-adapted state (2).

The significant positive correlations we discovered between TEs and TPGs within these modules strengthen the possibility for a cis-regulatory interaction between TE activation and host gene expression. These relationships suggest that the transcriptional activation or repression of specific TE loci may directly influence the expression of neighboring genes, a mechanism well documented in developmental and disease contexts (48, 49). For instance, TE-derived sequences can provide transcription factor binding sites or function as enhancers or promoters (19, 20). In this context, IF-induced changes in TE expression could mechanistically contribute to the up- or down-regulation of critical metabolic or stress-response genes. Notably, our data reveal that this regulation frequently involves multiple distinct TEs converging on a single gene. Correlation analyses within IF-responsive WGCNA modules revealed that IF-responsive TEs were significantly associated with the expression of nearby genes in both liver and skeletal muscle, with the majority of significant correlations being positive. These multi-TE correlations suggest that coordinated action, rather than isolated cis effects, may orchestrate the fine-tuning of key metabolic genes in response to IF, consistent with the emerging concept that TEs can function as “coordinated regulatory platforms” enabling concerted control of host gene expression (50). However, further functional studies will be required to establish direct causal regulatory relationships.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. A key methodological challenge in TE transcriptomics is the potential overestimation of locus-specific TE expression resulting from intronic reads (38). To evaluate whether our dataset was affected by this bias, we used IRFinder to assess the contribution of intron retention to the observed TE expression profiles. Our results suggest that intron retention does not systematically confound the analysis of TE expression in our dataset. Nevertheless, because short-read RNA-seq has inherent limitations in resolving repetitive elements at single-locus resolution, we cannot fully exclude some contribution from intronic transcription or mapping ambiguity. Future studies incorporating complementary approaches, including long-read sequencing and alternative TE quantification frameworks, will be important to further validate locus-specific TE regulation under IF.

Our analysis is focused on transcription and does not confirm whether TE-derived RNAs are translated, processed, and mobilized within the genome through retrotransposition events. The correlative nature of the TE–gene relationships requires functional validation (e.g. CRISPR-based epigenetic editing to activate and inactivate specific TEs). Additionally, we rely on poly-A–selected RNA, which may not capture all noncoding TE-derived transcripts. Future work incorporating long-read sequencing, chromatin state assays (assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing [ATAC]-seq and chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing [ChIP-seq]), and single-cell resolution will be crucial to dissect the precise epigenetic mechanisms and cellular heterogeneity underlying these observations. Our analyses are primarily based on transcriptomic profiling, and, therefore, the predicted biological effects were not validated at the protein level. Although the observed TE-associated gene networks suggest functional impacts on metabolic and stress-response pathways, future studies incorporating proteomic analyses and targeted protein validation will be important to confirm the translational and biological significance of these transcriptomic changes. Another limitation of our study is that all experiments were performed using male mice only, and, therefore, we could not assess potential sex-specific effects of IF on TE regulation. Given the well-documented sex differences in metabolic, endocrine, and transcriptional responses to DR and IF, it is plausible that TE expression and TE-associated regulatory networks may also differ between males and females. Future studies incorporating both sexes will be important to determine whether IF-mediated TE reprogramming exhibits sex-dependent patterns and contributes differently to metabolic adaptation and aging-related processes.

The relevance of these findings may extend beyond metabolic adaptation to broader mechanisms of aging biology. Retrotransposons have been increasingly implicated in genomic instability, chronic inflammation, cellular senescence, and the progression of age-related diseases (51). In this context, the IF-mediated remodeling of retrotransposon expression observed in our study may represent an additional molecular pathway through which DR contributes to improved health span and lifespan. Given that DR is widely recognized as one of the most robust interventions promoting healthy aging, our findings raise the possibility that modulation of the “repeatome” forms an integral part of the adaptive genomic response to metabolic stress. However, an important consideration is that IF was initiated in relatively young mice (6 weeks of age), and, therefore, our findings primarily reflect early-life adaptive responses to DR. Because aging is associated with substantial alterations in metabolism, chromatin organization, epigenetic regulation, and TE activity, the impact of IF on TE regulation may differ in older animals, particularly in the context of age-associated TE derepression and genomic instability. It is therefore possible that IF may exert either more pronounced or diminished effects on TE activity during aging. Furthermore, because our study is based on RNA-seq data, we cannot determine whether altered retrotransposon transcription reflects changes in retrotransposition activity, genomic mobilization, or chromatin state. Future studies incorporating aged cohorts together with long-read sequencing, chromatin accessibility profiling, and direct assays of retrotransposition activity will be required to determine whether IF functionally suppresses or remodels retrotransposon activity during aging.

In conclusion, our findings support a model in which IF acts as an environmental perturbation that induces metabolic stress that alters the silenced state of TEs, which in turn influence the expression of genes in a nonrandom manner to reprogram cellular physiology. Our study reveals a tight coupling between the repetitive and nonrepetitive genome. The tissue-specific reprogramming of TE expression and its integration into distinct co-expression networks represent a novel layer of transcriptional regulation underlying the adaptive response to DR. Understanding how nutritional signals regulate this genomic “repeatome” may open new avenues for harnessing TEs not as genomic liabilities but as dynamic contributors to metabolic health and longevity.
