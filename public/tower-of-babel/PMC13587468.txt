Maintaining personhood within family life: a qualitative study of home-based palliative care for patients with advanced cancer in urban China
Authors: Shan Zhang, Xiaotian Zhang, Yuan Qin, Minghui Wang, Zhaoyu Li, Lijie Xu, Xiaohong Ou, Zidan Wang, Yao Xiao, Lingling Wu, Peng Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, several patients prefer home as the place of death, making home-based palliative care (HBPC) an important component of holistic palliative care delivery. In China, where many patients receive palliative care at home, HBPC is constrained by limited resources, fragmented services and death-avoidant cultural norms. This study aimed to explore how urban patients with advanced cancer experience HBPC within family and cultural contexts in China.MethodsWe conducted a focused ethnographic study in Beijing, China. Twelve urban patients with advanced cancer receiving HBPC were purposively recruited via two outpatient departments and four community health centers and all participated in the study. Data were generated through semi-structured interviews, participant observation, and field notes. Data were analyzed using content analysis with iterative coding and constant comparison to refine themes; team discussion and analytic memos were used to enhance rigor.ResultsContent analysis generated four interrelated themes: (1) Being the focus of the family – patients sought to regain visibility in family life and embed care in daily routines; (2) Guarding my boundaries – patients defined personal limits, managed social exposure and articulated autonomy to preserve dignity; (3) The final safety net – patients sought external care and institutional support as illness progressed to enhance comfort and reduce family burden; (4) Preserving the value of life – patients affirmed meaning by reflecting on their life course, staying connected with family and aspiring to be remembered.ConclusionsFor urban Chinese patients with advanced cancer, HBPC is experienced as a process of maintaining personhood within intersecting domains of care, identity and family ethics. Background

Globally, most individuals express a preference to spend their final days at home, and home deaths are increasingly recognized as an indicator of high-quality end-of-life care [1]. However, the ability to die at home depends on multiple factors, including healthcare system capacity, cultural norms, and the availability of family and professional support. In China, an estimated 71.5% of adults die at home, underscoring the importance of understanding the home context in end-of-life experiences and care planning [2]. Cancer remains one of the leading causes of terminal illness in China, and individuals with advanced cancer often suffer from intense pain, functional decline, fear of death, and emotional distress, all of which severely compromise their quality of life [3].

Home-based palliative care (HBPC) provides physical, psychological, social, and spiritual support to patients and families in their own homes [4]. Although “home” is often broadly defined to include community or institutional settings such as nursing homes or assisted living facilities [5], this study refers specifically to private residences, excluding institutional environments. This focus allows a closer examination of care practices within personally inhabited spaces. HBPC is a person- and family-centered approach that seeks to maintain dignity, relieve suffering, and support both patients and caregivers at the end-of-life [6]. Despite regional variations, its core goals remain consistent—to alleviate distress, enhance family well-being, and promote a peaceful and dignified dying process [7–9]. In this study, home-based palliative care is used as the primary term. Consistent with a recent consensus definition, palliative care is the active holistic care of people with serious health-related suffering due to severe illness, especially near the end of life [10]. The present study focuses on patients with advanced cancer receiving palliative care at home, whose care experiences often overlapped with care near the end of life.

Despite its potential benefits, the implementation of HBPC in China remains limited [11]. Most services are delivered by community health centers, which often lack sufficient training, multidisciplinary teams, and essential resources [12, 13]. These issues are exacerbated by the country’s vast geographic scale and large population, resulting in a significant mismatch between the availability of palliative care resources and the actual demand for end-of-life care [13]. Even patients who are connected to tertiary hospitals or community health centers and receive basic HBPC often experience substantial unmet physical, psychosocial and spiritual needs, and limited professional guidance for family caregivers, fragmented volunteer services, low insurance reimbursement rates, and a lack of non-profit organiational support further constrain the development of HBPC [14–16].

Beyond structural barriers, the cultural context also shapes how HBPC is practiced and perceived in China. Palliative care is deeply influenced by family-centered decision-making, filial piety, and collective values that prioritize family harmony over individual autonomy [17, 18]. Discussions about death are often avoided to protect patients from emotional distress, delaying communication and hindering advance care planning [19, 20]. In this family-oriented setting, caregiving responsibilities fall primarily on relatives, whose burden is compounded by moral expectations and limited community support [18–22]. These intertwined structural and cultural constraints underscore the need for HBPC models that are culturally attuned and contextually feasible, rather than direct adaptations of Western frameworks.

Given these challenges, there is a critical need to deepen understanding of the lived experiences and care expectations of individuals with advanced cancer receiving care in the home environment in China. This study therefore adopts a qualitative approach to explore the perspectives of patients with advanced cancer on home-based palliative care, aiming to inform the development of culturally sensitive and contextually appropriate service models that can better meet the needs of patients and families living with advanced illness at home.

Methods

Aim

To explore how urban patients with advanced cancer receiving home-based care experience palliative care within family and cultural contexts in urban China.

Design

This study adopted a focused ethnographic approach [23] to explore the needs of individuals with advanced cancer receiving home-based palliative care (HBPC). This approach enabled an in-depth understanding of participants’ real-life experiences and care preferences within their natural home environments. The study followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) to ensure methodological rigor and transparency [24].

Setting

This study was conducted in Beijing, China, and focused on patients with advanced cancer receiving home-based palliative care. Participants were recruited via two tertiary hospital outpatient departments and four community health centers that provided basic HBPC services. Although recruitment occurred through these institutions, all participants received daily care at home.

Participants and recruitment

Purposive sampling was employed to recruit adults with advanced cancer receiving home-based palliative care (HBPC).

Inclusion criteria included: (a) age ≥ 18 years; (b) diagnosis of advanced cancer; (c) clinical estimation of survival ≤ 6 months; (d) residence in a private home for ≥ 3 months after diagnosis; and (e) capacity for verbal communication and informed consent. Individuals unable to participate meaningfully because of severe physical deterioration were excluded.

Eligible participants were identified through gatekeepers, including directors of two tertiary hospital palliative outpatient departments and four community health centers. To protect privacy and autonomy, gatekeepers first introduced the study to potentially eligible patients and asked whether they were willing to be contacted by the research team. Only patients who agreed to be approached were then contacted by the research team and given a full explanation of the study.

Written informed consent was obtained before participation in the study. Participants could choose to take part in interviews only, observations only, or both. When participant observation was undertaken in home or outpatient settings, additional verbal confirmation was sought immediately before the observation session, even when written consent had already been obtained, to ensure ongoing willingness to participate in that specific component. Verbal confirmation was documented in the field notes. Confidentiality was assured, and participants were informed that participation was voluntary, that they could decline any component of the study, and that withdrawal would not affect the care they received.

Data generation

Between March 2022 and April 2023, data were generated through semi-structured interviews, participant observation, field notes, and supplementary documents voluntarily shared by participants or family members, such as caregiving diaries, memory albums, and advance care planning or end-of-life preference documents. All data were generated in Mandarin Chinese. Home interviews, conducted by a female nursing graduate student with formal training in qualitative research, followed a guide refined through expert consultation and pilot testing. Six open-ended questions explored multidimensional support needs. Observations examined caregiving interactions and spatial contexts, with field notes documenting key details while maintaining privacy. During the observation phase, the researcher adopted an observer-as-participant stance [25]. Participants were aware of the researcher’s role, and the researcher engaged in limited, purposeful interaction while primarily observing naturally occurring interactions, routines, and care practices in participants’ usual environments. Participants could choose to take part in interviews only, observations only, or both, and consent for each component was sought separately. The patient interview guide and observation guide were developed specifically for this study by the research team, informed by a review of relevant literature on the needs and experiences of patients with advanced cancer receiving care at home [14–27], and refined through iterative team discussions and pilot interviews. Two pilot interviews were conducted to assess the feasibility and sensitivity of the interview guide in practice. Feedback from these pilot interviews was subsequently discussed among qualitative research experts, clinical experts, and the main interviewer. Based on both the pilot feedback and the expert discussion, only minor revisions were made to the guide, mainly rephrasing the opening question to reduce distress when participants described their illness trajectory. The pilot interviews were not included in the final analysis. The full guides, including the Chinese originals and English translations, are provided as Supplementary Table 1 (Patient interview guide) and Supplementary Table 2 (Observation guide).

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted concurrently using content analysis [28], informed by an interpretivist research paradigm. The analysis was led by the two first authors, who repeatedly reviewed the transcripts and field notes, identified meaning units, assigned initial codes, and grouped similar codes into categories and higher-order themes. NVivo 11 (QSR International) supported data organization and retrieval.

The two first authors repeatedly read the interview transcripts and field notes to gain an overall understanding of the data. Meaning units were then identified within and across data sources, condensed, and assigned initial codes. In the next step, codes derived from interviews and field notes were compared and grouped according to similarities and differences, and then organized into categories and higher-order themes. Observational data were not analyzed separately; rather, they were integrated with interview data throughout the analytic process to provide contextual detail, clarify caregiving interactions and spatial arrangements, and refine interpretation of participants’ accounts. Team discussions and analytic memos were used throughout the process to challenge interpretations and strengthen analytic consistency.

Data generation and analysis were conducted in Mandarin Chinese. Translation into English occurred after coding and theme development for the purposes of manuscript preparation, consistent with a late-phase translation approach in cross-language qualitative research [29]. Repeated reference to the original Chinese data was maintained to preserve meaning as closely as possible. Selected excerpts were translated into English using a forward–backward process by the first and corresponding authors, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. Direct quotations are shown in quotation marks, and contextual remarks from field notes are presented without them.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (Approval: Z2019SY068) on October 15, 2019, and informed consent was obtained from the participants. All methods involving human participants were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants had the right to withdraw at any time, and their palliative care services will not be affected, nor will they be held accountable.

Trustworthiness and positionality

Research trustworthiness was ensured through triangulation, audit trails, and sustained reflexivity. Semi-structured interviews, observations, and supplementary documents voluntarily shared by participants or family members were integrated to enhance credibility and identify convergent themes. Transcript verification within 48 h allowed factual accuracy without overburdening participants. An audit trail systematically documented coding processes, theme refinement, and team discussions to ensure transparency.

Prolonged engagement was achieved through sustained involvement across the fieldwork period from March 2022 to April 2023, including repeated contact with participants in home and outpatient settings and informal pre-interview visits that helped build rapport and deepen contextual understanding. These pre-interview visits occurred after patients had agreed to be contacted and before the formal interviews. They were used mainly for rapport-building and familiarization with the care context, rather than formal data generation. This prolonged engagement supported trustworthiness by allowing the researcher to develop familiarity with participants’ care contexts, everyday routines, and relational dynamics over time. Participants were reminded throughout the study that participation was voluntary, that they could decline any question or withdraw at any time, and that their care would not be affected by their decision.

Positionality was addressed explicitly throughout the study. The two first authors, both female nursing graduate students, were involved in data generation and analysis. Neither of the authors was part of the participants’ formal care team nor did they provide direct medical or nursing care during the study period. Reflexive journals were maintained throughout the study to document evolving insights, positionality, and assumptions related to the topic. During analysis, the journals were used to bracket preconceptions and support repeated return to the data, helping to ensure that interpretations remained grounded in participants’ accounts. Weekly peer debriefings provided critical feedback, promoting analytical consistency and minimizing potential bias.

Main findings

Through the analysis of interview transcripts and field observation notes, this study developed an interpretive framework that elucidates how participants maintain personhood within family life. The framework is organized around a core concept—maintaining personhood—and comprises four interrelated themes: (1) Being the focus of the family; (2) Guarding my boundaries; (3) The final safety net; and (4) Preserving the value of life (Table 3). Rather than serving as discrete descriptive categories, these four themes represent distinct yet interconnected dimensions of a dynamic psychosocial process.

Overview of the findings

Theme 1: Being the focus of the family

Patients wished to be the focus of their family’s attention, both emotionally and spatially. They sought to remain visible and included in everyday family life, while also hoping to feel heard, acknowledged, and emotionally connected within the home.

Reclaiming visibility in the family space

The living room is an open and communal space within the home, and its use as a care environment reflects patients’ desire for visibility, interaction, and inclusion in family life. During home visits, researchers observed that most patients had positioned their beds in the living room.

“I told my daughter, ‘Just put me in the living room. I want to see you all moving around, hear the TV, and know I’m still part of this family’”(P01).

“By placing the bed in the living room, I can always keep an eye on my family members. It’s convenient for them to take care of me, and I feel much more at ease“(P03).

For participants, this spatial arrangement was not merely a practical caregiving decision, but a deliberate effort to remain socially present—to be seen, to overhear conversations, and to feel that they still belonged to the rhythms of family life.

“When I’m out here, I don’t feel so alone. I can hear everyone talking, and they can check on me without even thinking about it. That means everything” (P02).

Embedding care into everyday rhythms

Rather than being separate from daily life, care was integrated into the home environment, aligning with everyday routines and helping patients maintain a sense of autonomy and continuity despite illness.

Field notes from P02 described a home environment that was well-equipped for both comfort and medical care. The patient’s family had prepared various herbal remedies tailored to her condition and purchased multiple treatment and monitoring devices, including a pulse oximeter, a frequency therapy device, pain relief medications, and nutritional supplements. A remote-controlled adjustable bed and a bedside table stocked with medications, ensure nutritional drinks, and snacks were observed to comprehensively meet the patient’s daily physiological needs.

This dense arrangement of care resources served a dual purpose: it ensured that the patient’s physical needs could be met efficiently, while also creating an environment in which the patient could remain in the center of family life—surrounded, cared for, yet still present.

Securing emotional responsiveness

More than a place for physical care, the home was an emotional space where patients sought recognition, connection, and responsiveness from their families.

Field notes from P08 recorded that the patient repeatedly expressed a desire to be more involved in conversations. During a home visit, while the family was gathered and chatting in the living room, the patient stated:

“I wanted to participate more actively rather than being a passive listener. I also made frequent requests for my children to contact friends and relatives to come visit me” (P08).

Theme 2: Guarding my boundaries

In times of illness, patients were not only receiving care but also actively defining their own limits in terms of what to accept, reveal, or control. “Guarding my boundaries” reflects their quiet efforts to preserve a personal space, physical, emotional, and symbolic, where they could retain dignity and negotiate how much of themselves to make visible. The home thus became more than a care setting; it was a space for balancing exposure and protection, dependence and selfhood.

Asserting identity through self-reliance

Despite physical decline, patients used small acts of self-reliance to maintain control and express their identity.

Field notes from P10 described that, despite her poor mental state and unsteady gait, the patient declined assistance and insisted on walking unaided to her bedside. “I can still do this myself—let me at least try,” she said. She struggled to remove her shoes without support, reflecting a strong desire to maintain independence.

Managing social exposure to safeguard self-image

To preserve dignity, patients managed their public presence by choosing when and how to be seen in order to protect their self-image during illness.

Observations showed that patients were often reluctant to disclose their illness in public, as doing so threatened their sense of privacy and normalcy. For example, field notes from P03 recorded an episode in which her son invited her to go downstairs to enjoy the fresh air. The patient looked at herself in the mirror and expressed that.

“My appearance was unsuitable for being seen. I feared others would easily recognize me as unwell and declined to go outside. I didn’t want people to see me like that—I wanted them to remember me the way I was before”(P03).

Claiming interpretive and decisional authority over my body

Patients sought to stay in control of their bodies by interpreting their own symptoms and making personal choices about eating, discomfort, and treatment.

Field notes from P09 indicated that the patient frequently resisted family members’ efforts to provide semi-liquid meals. When pressured to eat, the patient would respond with visible frustration or plead in discomfort, once exclaiming,

“Please don’t make me eat; my stomach is so bloated, I can’t swallow. I often thought about stopping dialysis because I didn’t want to continue anymore. But every time, I pushed myself to go on. The nausea, vomiting, and fatigue became unbearable, and I felt these were signs that death was near. Only then did I finally decide to stop” (P11).

Articulating and formalizing end-of-life autonomy

Some patients took proactive steps to express and document their palliative care wishes, working with family members to ensure their values would be respected in future care decisions.

Field notes from P03 documented that when introduced to an advance directive (“Living Will”) booklet, the patient chose her son to serve as both witness and designated surrogate.

“If I were in a coma or brain dead, I would not want blood transfusions, antibiotics, or feeding tubes”(P03).

Theme 3: The final safety net

As illness worsened and patients became weaker, family care could no longer meet all their needs. To stay comfortable and reduce the burden on loved ones, patients sought external support including symptom management from healthcare professionals, practical daily care from paid home caregivers, and hospital-based support when needed. They did not view such support as replacing family care, but as helping to make life at home more manageable. Even when patients felt uncertain about seeking external help, their choices reflected a desire to preserve dignity, reduce pressure on family members, and respond to the realities of progressive illness and dying.

Seeking external care to improve quality of life

Some patients found that professional and practical support beyond the family helped with daily needs and made them feel more at ease at home.

Field notes from P08 indicated that a nurse visited each evening to assist the patient with managing incontinence, supporting limb mobility, and patiently addressed symptoms associated with delirium.

Field notes from P03 indicated that, after a paid home caregiver hired by the family joined, the patient appeared more settled during daily care interactions. The caregiver attended carefully to everyday needs and explained each action before providing assistance.

“With you here, I truly feel blessed”(P03).

Seeking support to relieve the burden on family caregivers

Some patients expressed a preference to be transferred to the hospital when approaching the final stage of dying, not because they rejected home care, but because they knew hospitals were better equipped to handle death-related procedures. They worried that if death occurred at home, their families, who were unfamiliar with such processes, would be left with heavy burdens.

“I would not consider hospitalization unless I became unconscious and unresponsive. I was unfamiliar with post-death procedures and did not want to burden my children, who also lacked knowledge in this regard”(P09).

Theme 4: Preserving the value of life

As life neared its end, many patients sought to preserve meaning through family and legacy. They described wanting to continue contributing to family life, pass on experiences, and leave something of themselves behind.

Reflecting on one’s life journey

Patients reflected on their past achievements and the selfless contributions they made in their work and personal lives, drawing from their memories of hardships and joys to express the value of their lives.

Field notes from P05 noted that the patient enthusiastically shared stories from her youth. She used nursing pads and chopsticks to recreate scenes of underground mining. Her pride and vivid memories of those times were clearly felt, even if not every detail was fully understood.

“When I was young, I was chosen to be the school broadcaster. My teacher thought my voice was clear, so she asked me to try. I was nervous at first, but later the whole school could hear me reading announcements, and I felt very proud. It wasn’t just broadcasting—I also used to take the lead in many things in my family. I was like that from a young age”(P10).

Passing on personal experiences

Terminal patients accumulate a lifetime of skills and hope to pass them on to those around them during their final days.

Field notes from P10 documented a moment when the patient watched a video brought by her daughter on making preserved plums and pickled mustard greens.

“This is not how we do it in Zhejiang. For preserved plums, you rub them with salt first and then dry them in the sun. For meigancai, you steam and dry the xuelihong greens three times, until they turn shiny black”(P10).

Staying connected with family

Some patients sought to stay connected with family members even in limited physical states. Maintaining small but meaningful routines allowed them to express care and stay connected with their loved ones.

“My partner is in a nursing home, and I call him every evening at 7 o’clock. He worries about my health and always asks me to call. When he hears my voice, he feels at ease” (P01).

Being remembered by others

Some patients feared being forgotten and took steps to stay connected with friends and family near the end of life.

Field notes from P08 described how the patient called each of his old friends to say goodbye. Several friends or their family members later came to visit him at home.

“I want to call them while I still can. We have known each other for so many years, and I want to say goodbye properly. I do not want to disappear quietly, as if no one remembers me” (P08).

Discussion

Main findings

This study explored how patients with advanced cancer in urban China experienced home-based palliative care within family and cultural contexts Overall, our findings showed that patients understood palliative care at home not only as symptom control, but as an ongoing process of maintaining personhood and dignity within family life. Emotional connection, relational autonomy and the search for legacy and continuity were central to how participants made sense of care at the end of life.

Our findings indicate that emotional connection and the desire for acknowledgment within the family were crucial to how patients sustained a sense of self. Participants used shared spaces and everyday interactions to remain visible and to resist the “social death” often associated with terminal illness. This resonates with cross-cultural evidence that emotional recognition preserves identity and autonomy [30, 31], and that family presence and shared domestic space are key to a dignified death [32]. Studies from Japan, Hong Kong and the UK similarly show that proximity, affirmation and touch help alleviate suffering and affirm social identity [33–35]. In collectivist societies such as China, these practices are embedded in intergenerational duty [36, 37]. Our findings suggest that such emotional practices constitute a relational form of care in which identity, care and agency are continuously negotiated within family life.

Despite increasing physical dependence, participants actively worked to maintain autonomy and personal boundaries. Through choices such as declining assistance, controlling social exposure and making treatment decisions, they exercised “quiet” forms of agency that challenge purely individualistic notions of autonomy. Drawing on de Certeau’s concept of “tactics” [38], these actions can be viewed as everyday strategies of self-determination within constrained care environments. Relational accounts of autonomy emphasize emotional interdependence, caregiving networks and shared moral responsibility [39, 40]. Our data illustrate how autonomy is embedded in trust and reciprocity, rather than detached independence. In the Chinese context, where family obligation and collective well-being are core values, decision-making often reflects “family-determination” rather than purely individual choice [17, 41]. Some patients found comfort in family involvement, whereas others navigated tensions between dependence and self-expression, highlighting the need for communication that honors both autonomy and relational ethics.

The study further highlights that, beyond symptom relief, participants sought to sustain meaning, identity and continuity at the end of life. They engaged in legacy-building activities, such as sharing stories, passing on skills and reconnecting with others, to affirm their value and belonging within family and community. Meaning-centered interventions, including dignity therapy, have been shown to enhance well-being by reinforcing self-worth and alleviating distress [42, 43], echoing Becker’s view [44] that constructing personal meaning helps individuals confront mortality. Our study extends this understanding by illustrating that meaning-making in China is deeply embedded in everyday family practices. Acts of caregiving, teaching and farewell reflect moral duty and relational continuity, and may carry more significance for patients than formalized therapeutic programmers.

What this study adds to the literature

This study extends research on home-based palliative care by centering personhood in patients’ accounts, rather than symptom control alone. While most work in China has highlighted service gaps, symptom burden and caregiver strain, our findings show how emotional connection, acknowledgment and everyday shared spaces function as relational care through which patients resist “social death” and sustain dignity, situating home-based palliative care within ordinary family life rather than as a purely clinical intervention.

The study also offers empirical evidence on relational autonomy in collectivist settings. Participants’ ways of maintaining boundaries, controlling social exposure and taking part in treatment decisions indicate that autonomy is negotiated through trust, reciprocity and shared moral responsibility rather than individual independence alone. This extends theories of autonomy and person-centered care by showing that family involvement can both support and constrain patients’ agency in urban China.

Finally, our findings link work on dignity therapy, legacy-building and meaning-centered care with ethnographic insight into everyday practice. Patients’ efforts to share stories, pass on skills and sustain family roles show that meaning and legacy are embedded in routine family interactions as well as formal interventions, refining what culturally sensitive, holistic home-based palliative care should aim to support.

Strengths and limitations

This study combined interviews and participant observation to provide an in-depth understanding of patients’ experiences with home-based palliative care. The use of multiple qualitative methods captured emotional, relational, and spatial dimensions of care within the Chinese cultural context, informing culturally responsive models.

However, the small sample size and recruitment through hospitals and communities linked to HBPC services may limit transferability, as participants potentially had greater access to support than those outside such systems. Patients with severe impairments were underrepresented, and caregiver and provider perspectives were not included. While the concept of maintaining personhood is central to our findings, the study does not claim to advance philosophical theories of personhood. Future studies should recruit more diverse participants and incorporate multiple viewpoints to further validate and expand these findings.

In addition, most participants were female and middle-aged or older adults. This sample composition may have shaped the findings, particularly in relation to family roles, everyday care practices, and relational expectations, and caution is therefore needed when considering the transferability of the findings to younger patients or to male patients with advanced cancer receiving home-based palliative care.

Recommendations for practice and policy

The findings suggest that home-based palliative care in urban China should prioritize supporting personhood within family life rather than symptom control alone. Clinical assessment and care planning should consider how patients remain visible and recognized in the household, how they set and protect personal boundaries, how they wish to participate in decisions, and how everyday legacy-building (such as storytelling, passing on skills and maintaining family roles) contributes to meaning. Community teams should explore patients’ preferred ways of engaging in family routines and treat emotional connection, acknowledgment and legacy work as core components of care. Training should focus on working with families to negotiate roles, support patients’ “quiet” autonomy and manage tensions between protection and control in culturally sensitive ways, while also creating opportunities for patients to share memories or advice with relatives to sustain continuity and dignity.

At a policy level in China, strengthening home-based palliative care requires greater investment in community health centers, better integration of home-based services into referral and reimbursement systems, and formal recognition of family caregivers as partners in care. Policies should embed HBPC within broader aging and chronic disease strategies and adopt standards that reflect relational understandings of autonomy and personhood. Internationally, especially where home deaths are common and family caregiving is central, our findings support policies that prioritize community-based palliative care, expand workforce training and provide structured support for family caregivers.

Conclusion

This study qualitatively explored how patients with advanced cancer in China experienced home-based palliative care within their cultural and family contexts. Using interviews and participant observation, it generated four interrelated domains: emotional connection and recognition, relational autonomy in care and decision-making, and preservation of meaning and identity. The findings highlight the culturally embedded, relational nature of end-of-life experiences at home and the need for care models that address emotional, psychosocial and existential dimensions alongside symptom control, showing how patients negotiate visibility, autonomy and continuity within domestic spaces to inform culturally responsive, context-specific home-based palliative care that honors both personal dignity and family values.
