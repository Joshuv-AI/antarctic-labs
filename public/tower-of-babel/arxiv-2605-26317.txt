A Jacobi-like algorithm for normal matrices by the skew-symmetric part Thanks: Submitted to the editors.
Simon Mataigne, P.-A. Absil

Abstract
We present a fast Jacobi-like algorithm for computing the eigenvalues, and optionally the eigenvectors, of a real normal matrix. The method gains a computational advantage by using Paardekooper’s method for skew-symmetric matrices The method is most efficient for matrices where most eigenvalues are complex, such as random orthogonal matrices arising in the context of statistics on manifolds. In this case, the method is faster than the other Jacobi-like algorithms. In the last section of this paper, we also give explicit formulas for the nearest symmetric skew-Hamiltonian and the nearest ortho-symplectic matrix. These problems arise in the design and the analysis of the algorithm.



keywords

Normal matrices, orthogonal matrices, Jacobi, eigenvalues.

Introduction

Jacobi’s algorithm is a famous and one of the oldest algorithms
[
24
]
for solving the symmetric eigenvalue problem (EVP)
[
19
,
18
,
34
]
. Over the years, many variants have been proposed for solving other EVPs, subsumed under the name
Jacobi-like algorithms
. While the operation count of these methods is higher than that of competitors such as the QR algorithm, Jacobi-like algorithms have regained popularity because of their high parallelizability. The shared idea of many Jacobi-like algorithms is to repeatedly solve small-size problems obtained from submatrices of the original matrix. In particular, the classical algorithm for symmetric matrices relies on the explicit solution to the
2\times 2
symmetric EVP. In 1971, Paardekooper
[
30
,
20
]
proposed an algorithm for (real) skew-symmetric matrices based on the explicit solution to the
4\times 4
skew-symmetric EVP. This algorithm converges to the real Schur form. For normal matrices, this form is the real-valued analog of the eigenvalue decomposition. Later on, other specialized algorithms were proposed, e.g., for Hamiltonian and skew-Hamiltonian matrices
[
15
]
.

In 1959, a generalization of Jacobi’s algorithm was proposed by Goldstine and Horwitz to solve the normal eigenvalue problem
[
17
]
. Instead of diagonalizing submatrices, it focuses on decreasing the norm of off-diagonal elements. This efficient algorithm converges quadratically
[
33
]
but relies on complex-valued arithmetic. If the input matrix
A
is real-valued, relying on complex transformations ignores the additional structure of real matrices and yields complex arithmetic, which is more costly than real arithmetic. It is well-known that the symmetric and skew-symmetric parts of a real normal matrix commute. Therefore, the real Schur form can also be computed by the procedure of Bunse-Gerstner et al.
[
5
, Algorithm 2]
for the simultaneous diagonalization of commuting matrices. Moreover, in 2003, an algorithm using only real arithmetic was also proposed by Zhou and Brent
[
42
]
. It relies on the assumption that a subroutine can compute the real Schur form of an arbitrary
4\times 4
matrix. This method is essentially equivalent to
[
5
, Algorithm 2]
and the idea outlined in
[
39
, p. 178]
.

Finally, other Jacobi-like algorithms, based on non-unitary transformations, were proposed for arbitrary matrices, either real
[
14
]
or complex
[
12
,
13
]
. For real matrices with complex eigenvalues, the convergence of
[
14
]
is slow
[
38
]
. This lead to the development of the quadratically convergent method of Veselić
[
40
]
for arbitrary real matrices with complex eigenvalues. Early ideas of taking advantage of the skew-symmetric part for arbitrary matrices can be found in Veselić’s method.

In this paper, we extend the idea of exploiting the skew-symmetric part for real normal matrices, introduced in
[
27
]
, to develop a Jacobi-like algorithm for the real normal EVP. The method is based on the sequential application of modified versions of Paardekooper’s method, Jacobi’s algorithm for symmetric skew-Hamiltonian matrices
[
15
]
, and related techniques. The fast convergence of Paardekooper’s method
[
32
]
allows this new method to outperform other Jacobi-like algorithms for normal matrices. We analyze the behavior of the algorithm through a theoretical perturbation analysis building on that of
[
27
]
. Finally, we demonstrate the speed, accuracy, and robustness of the proposed method through a set of numerical experiments.

The real normal eigenvalue problem plays a central role in computational applications. It arises in statistical inference and optimization on the special orthogonal group and Stiefel manifolds, where preserving orthogonality and exploiting normal structure are essential for stable and geometry-preserving computations
[
7
,
43
,
31
,
28
,
25
]
. It also appears as a fundamental building block in algorithms for generalized eigenvalue problems, where reductions to structured normal forms can improve numerical robustness and efficiency
[
8
,
6
]
. In signal processing, the real normal EVP underlies the computation of Pisarenko frequency estimates, a classical technique for high-resolution spectral estimation and harmonic retrieval
[
1
,
10
]
.

Notation

I_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix. The orthogonal group and the special orthogonal group of
n\times n
matrices are denoted, respectively, by
\mathrm{O}(n)
and
\mathrm{SO}(n)
. For
n\geq k
, the Stiefel manifold of orthonormal
k
-frames in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathrm{St}(n,k)\coloneq\{V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\ |\ V^{\top}V=I_{k}\}
.
A\otimes B
refers to the Kronecker product of two matrices
A
and
B
.
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
and
\mathrm{Skew}(n)
denote, respectively, the sets of symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices of size
n
. We use
\mathrm{sym}(A)=\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{\top})
and
\mathrm{skew}(A)=\frac{1}{2}(A-A^{\top})
for, respectively, the symmetric and the skew-symmetric part of the matrix
A
. Finally, an even-odd permutation matrix
P_{\mathrm{eo}}\in\mathrm{O}(n)
is a permutation matrix such that for every vector
[a_{1}\ a_{2}\ ...\ a_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
, we have

[a_{1}\ a_{2}\ ...\ a_{n}]P_{\mathrm{eo}}=[a_{1}\ a_{3}\ ...\ a_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}\ a_{2}\ a_{4}\ ...\ a_{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}].

2
The normal eigenvalue problem by the skew-symmetric part

The normal matrices are the matrices that can be diagonalized under complex unitary similarity transformation. However, if we consider
real
matrices only, it is natural to restrict the similarity transformation to
orthogonal
matrices, and thus real arithmetic. Then, the corresponding canonical form is the real Schur form, i.e., a block diagonal matrix with
1\times 1
and/or
2\times 2
diagonal blocks. The
2\times 2
blocks have the form of (
2.1
), which is easily related to its eigenvalues:

\lambda\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\theta)&-\sin(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
i&-i\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda e^{-i\theta}&0\\
0&\lambda e^{i\theta}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&-i\\
1&i\end{bmatrix}.

(2.1)

The real Schur form is sometimes called Murnaghan’s form
[
29
]
. For normal matrices, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 1
(
[
23
, Thm. 2.5.8]
).

A matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is normal if and only if it admits a
real Schur decomposition (RSD)
by an orthogonal transformation
Q\in\mathrm{O}(n)
, i.e.,

A=QSQ^{\top},

where
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is block diagonal with
r\geq 0
blocks of size
1\times 1
and
p\coloneq\frac{n-r}{2}
blocks of size
2\times 2
of the form (
2.1
). The columns of
Q
are termed
Schur vectors
. W.l.o.g., the first subdiagonal of
S
is imposed nonnegative.

Letting
I_{2}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
J_{2}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, there are positive diagonal matrices
\Lambda,\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and a diagonal matrix
\breve{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
with
2p+r=n
such that

S=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda\cos(\Theta)\otimes I_{2}+\Lambda\sin(\Theta)\otimes J_{2}&0\\
0&\breve{\Lambda}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.2)

In
[
27
, Alg. 4.1]
a QR-type algorithm is proposed for computing the RSD of a normal matrix
A
. In particular, the method gets a computational advantage by computing the RSD of the skew-symmetric part

\Omega\coloneq\mathrm{skew}(A).

In the present paper, we further exploit the properties of the skew-symmetric part to design a Jacobi-like algorithm. Let us recall relevant results from
[
27
]
.

2.1
Eigenvalues with distinct nonzero imaginary parts

Consider the real Schur decomposition of
\Omega=\mathrm{skew}(A)
:

\Omega=Q_{\Omega}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda\sin(\Theta)\otimes J_{2}&0\\
0&0_{r}\end{bmatrix}Q_{\Omega}^{\top}.

Let
V\in\mathrm{St}(n,2k)
gather the
2k
columns of
Q_{\Omega}
corresponding to the
2\times 2
blocks of
\Lambda\sin(\Theta)\otimes J_{2}
with simple (i.e., non-repeated) eigenvalues of
\Omega
. Let
\Lambda_{k}\sin(\Theta_{k})\otimes J_{2}
be the block-diagonal matrix containing these
2\times 2
blocks. Then,

\Omega V=V(\Lambda_{k}\sin(\Theta_{k})\otimes J_{2}),

and by
[
27
, Theorem 3.3]
,

\displaystyle AV=V(\Lambda_{k}\cos(\Theta_{k})\otimes I_{2}+\Lambda_{k}\sin(\Theta_{k})\otimes J_{2}).

(2.3)

A consequence of (
2.3
) is that the matrix
V^{\top}AV
is block-diagonal and
V^{\top}AV
is a submatrix of the Schur form
S
of
A
.

2.2
Eigenvalues with repeated nonzero imaginary parts

Now, assume that exactly
m>1
eigenvalues have the repeated imaginary part
\sigma>0
. Then there is a permuted subset
V\in\mathrm{St}(n,2m)
of the columns of
Q_{\Omega}
verifying

\Omega V=V(J_{2}\otimes\sigma I_{m})=V\begin{bmatrix}0&-\sigma I_{m}\\
\sigma I_{m}&0\end{bmatrix},

and, by
[
27
, Thm. 3.4]
, there are
\widetilde{H}\in\mathrm{Sym}(m)
and
\widetilde{\Omega}\in\mathrm{Skew}(m)
such that

AV=V(I_{2}\otimes\widetilde{H}+J_{2}\otimes(\widetilde{\Omega}+\sigma I_{m}))=V\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{H}&-\widetilde{\Omega}-\sigma I_{m}\\
\widetilde{\Omega}+\sigma I_{m}&\widetilde{H}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.4)

The matrix
M=I_{2}\otimes\widetilde{H}+J_{2}\otimes\widetilde{\Omega}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\widetilde{H}&-\widetilde{\Omega}\\
\widetilde{\Omega}&\widetilde{H}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
belongs to the set
\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)
of
2m\times 2m

symmetric skew-Hamiltonian (SSkH)
matrices
[
15
]
:

\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)\coloneq\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{H}&-\widetilde{\Omega}\\
\widetilde{\Omega}&\widetilde{H}\end{bmatrix}\ |\ \widetilde{H}\in\mathrm{Sym}(m),\ \widetilde{\Omega}\in\mathrm{Skew}(m)\right\}.

The eigenvalue decomposition of the matrix
M\in\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)
admits a form (see, e.g.,
[
27
, Lem. B.1]
)

M=R(I_{2}\otimes D_{m})R^{\top}=R\begin{bmatrix}D_{m}&0\\
0&D_{m}\end{bmatrix}R^{\top},

where
D_{m}
is diagonal and
R
belongs to the set
\mathrm{OSp}(2m)
of
2m\times 2m

ortho-symplectic
matrices
[
11
, Eq. 5.1]
, i.e.,
R
is orthogonal and commutes with
J_{2}\otimes I_{m}
. This property yields the following structure:

\mathrm{OSp}(2m)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}U_{\mathrm{r}}&-U_{\mathrm{i}}\\
U_{\mathrm{i}}&U_{\mathrm{r}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{O}(2m)\right\}.

Moreover, the commutativity of
R
and
J_{2}\otimes I_{m}
yields the commutativity of
R
and
\sigma J_{2}\otimes I_{m}
, and thus

A(VR)=(VR)R^{\top}(M+\sigma J_{2}\otimes I_{m})R=(VR)\begin{bmatrix}D_{m}&-\sigma I_{m}\\
\sigma I_{m}&D_{m}\end{bmatrix}.

Using an even-odd permutation
P_{\mathrm{eo}}
, we obtain the identity

P_{\mathrm{eo}}\begin{bmatrix}D_{m}&-\sigma I_{m}\\
\sigma I_{m}&D_{m}\end{bmatrix}P_{\mathrm{eo}}^{\top}=D_{m}\otimes I_{2}+\sigma I_{m}\otimes J_{2}.

Since
D_{m}\otimes I_{2}+\sigma I_{m}\otimes J_{2}
is block diagonal, we conclude that it is a submatrix of the Schur form
S
of
A
.

2.3
Real eigenvalues

Finally, a similar result can be obtained for the
r
real eigenvalues of
A
. Assume the matrix
V\in\mathrm{St}(n,r)
gathers all columns of
Q_{\Omega}
spanning the nullspace of
\Omega
, i.e.,

\Omega V=0_{r}.

Then, by
[
27
, Thm. 3.5]
, there is a symmetric matrix
H\in\mathrm{Sym}(r)
such that

AV=VH.

(2.5)

By (
2.5
), if
V
is known and the real eigenvectors of
A
are desired, computing the eigenvalue decomposition
H=\breve{R}\breve{\Lambda}\breve{R}^{\top}
yields
A(V\breve{R})=(V\breve{R})\breve{\Lambda}
.

3
Paardekooper’s algorithm for skew-symmetric matrices

The starting point of the method proposed in this paper is computing the RSD of the skew-symmetric part
\Omega
of the real normal matrix
A
.
The Jacobi-like algorithm for solving this problem is called Paardekooper’s method for skew-symmetric matrices
[
30
]
. Since this method is not commonly known, this section recalls the essential ideas of Paardekooper’s method. It converges globally
[
30
]
, and locally at least quadratically
[
32
]
to the real Schur form.

3.1
The
2\times 2
Singular Value Decomposition

Paardekooper’s method relies on the explicit solution to the
2\times 2
Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). Given a
2\times 2
real matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, there is a pair of Givens rotations with angles
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\alpha_{1})&\sin(\alpha_{1})\\
-\sin(\alpha_{1})&\cos(\alpha_{1})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\alpha_{2})&-\sin(\alpha_{2})\\
\sin(\alpha_{2})&\cos(\alpha_{2})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}&0\\
0&d_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(3.1)

with
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Details on how to compute the angles
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
can be found, e.g., in
[
30
, Section 1]
.

Remark 2
.

The transformation in (
3.1
) uses a pair of
rotations
(i.e., as opposed to
reflections
) which does not allow enforcing the nonnegativity of
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
.

We use the following notation for Givens rotations of size larger than
2\times 2
. For integers
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
and an angle
\alpha
, the Givens rotation
G(\alpha,i,j)\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
is

G(\alpha,i,j)_{k,l}=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if}\quad k=l\notin\{i,j\},\\
\cos(\alpha)\quad&\text{if}\quad k=l\in\{i,j\},\\
\sin(\alpha)\quad&\text{if}\quad k=j\ \text{and}\ l=i,\\
-\sin(\alpha)\quad&\text{if}\quad k=i\ \text{and}\ l=j,\\
0\quad&\text{ortherwise}.\end{cases}

3.2
Paardekooper’s step for a
4\times 4
skew-symmetric submatrix

Based on the decomposition (
3.1
), Paardekooper
[
30
]
computes the real Schur form of a
4\times 4
skew-symmetric matrix
\Omega
in finitely many arithmetic operations. First, it requires computing rotations from (
3.1
) for the submatrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\omega_{21}&-\omega_{32}\\
\omega_{41}&\omega_{43}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
:

\displaystyle G(\alpha_{1},2,4)^{\top}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&-\omega_{21}&-\omega_{31}&-\omega_{41}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{21}}&0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}-\omega_{32}}&-\omega_{42}\\
\omega_{31}&\omega_{32}&0&\omega_{43}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{41}}&\omega_{42}&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{43}}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{=\Omega}G(\alpha_{2},1,3)=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\omega_{21}&-\omega_{31}^{\prime}&-\omega_{41}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}d_{1}}&0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}0}&-\omega_{42}^{\prime}\\
\omega_{31}^{\prime}&\omega_{32}&0&\omega_{43}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}0}&\omega_{42}^{\prime}&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}d_{2}}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Notice that
G(\alpha_{1},2,4)
and
G(\alpha_{2},1,3)
commute and, hence, the eigenvalues are restored by completing the similarity transformation with

\displaystyle G(\alpha_{2},1,3)^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}-\omega_{21}}&-\omega_{31}^{\prime}&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}-\omega_{41}}\\
d_{1}&0&0&-\omega_{42}^{\prime}\\
\omega_{31}^{\prime}&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{32}}&0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{43}}\\
0&\omega_{42}^{\prime}&d_{2}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}G(\alpha_{1},2,4)=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}-d_{1}}&-\omega_{31}^{\prime\prime}&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}0}\\
d_{1}&0&0&-\omega_{42}^{\prime\prime}\\
\omega_{31}^{\prime\prime}&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}0}&0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}-d_{2}}\\
0&\omega_{42}^{\prime\prime}&d_{2}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqcolon\Omega^{(1)}}.

Zeros have been introduced on the anti-diagonal of
\Omega^{(1)}
. A second pair of rotations gives the real Schur form of
\Omega
. The angles
\widehat{\alpha}_{1},\widehat{\alpha}_{2}
are obtained from the submatrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}d_{1}&-\omega_{42}^{\prime\prime}\\
\omega_{31}^{\prime\prime}&-d_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
of
\Omega^{(1)}
. Let
G\coloneq G(\widehat{\alpha}_{1},2,3)G(\widehat{\alpha}_{2},1,4)
.
Readily applying the full similarity transformation to
\Omega^{(1)}
yields

\displaystyle G^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}0&-d_{1}&-\omega_{31}^{\prime\prime}&0\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}d_{1}}&0&0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}-\omega_{42}^{\prime\prime}}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{31}^{\prime\prime}}&0&0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}-d_{2}}\\
0&\omega_{42}^{\prime\prime}&d_{2}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}G=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&-d_{1}^{\prime}&0&0\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}d_{1}^{\prime}}&0&0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}0}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}0}&0&0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}d_{2}^{\prime}}\\
0&0&-d_{2}^{\prime}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqcolon\Omega^{(2)}}.

Finally, the similarity transformation by
D\coloneq\mathrm{diag}(1,\mathrm{sign}(d_{1}^{\prime}),1,-\mathrm{sign}(d_{2}^{\prime}))
enforces the non-negativity of the subdiagonal:

D\begin{bmatrix}0&-d_{1}^{\prime}&0&0\\
d_{1}^{\prime}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&d_{2}^{\prime}\\
0&0&-d_{2}^{\prime}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}D=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\sigma_{1}&0&0\\
\sigma_{1}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&-\sigma_{2}\\
0&0&\sigma_{2}&0\\
\end{bmatrix},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{with}\ \sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\geq 0.

This concludes Paardekooper’s solution to the
4\times 4
skew-symmetric EVP. Observe that
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are the singular values of
\Omega
,
\pm i\sigma_{1}
and
\pm i\sigma_{2}
are the eigenvalues of
\Omega
.

Remark 3
.

If
n
is odd, then either the
3\times 3
skew-symmetric EVP must be solved (see
section

B.1
), or a row and a column of zeros can be appended to the matrix to make
n
even.

3.3
Sweeping strategy and pseudo-code

All Jacobi-like algorithms require a
sweeping strategy
, i.e., an order in which submatrices are diagonalized or block-diagonalized. Sweeping strategies have been extensively studied, in particular for parallel implementations, see, e.g.
[
35
,
4
,
26
,
36
]
. The standard and cheapest sweeping strategy is the
cyclic strategy

[
16
,
41
]
, where submatrices are selected in a cyclic order. This strategy is used in the pseudo-codes of this paper.

Finally, convergence is defined by a stopping criterion. Paardekooper’s method enforces convergence toward a block-diagonal matrix with (possibly zero)
2\times 2
diagonal blocks. In this manuscript, the function “
\mathrm{offschur}
” is defined by

\mathrm{offschur}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}:A\mapsto\sqrt{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\ \text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{n-2}\sum_{j=i+2}^{n}a_{i,j}^{2}+a_{i+1,j}^{2}+a_{j,i}^{2}+a_{j,i+1}^{2}}.

algorithm

3.1
gives the pseudo-code of the cyclic Paardekooper method for skew-symmetric matrices. Operations specific to Paardekooper’s method are written in
blue
and marked with (i). In this paper, however, our goal is to obtain the RSD of a general normal matrix
A
. Therefore, we use a variant of this algorithm computing the transformations from the skew-symmetric part
\Omega\coloneq\mathrm{skew}(A)
but applying these transformations to
A
. The sweeps are
implicitly
applied on
\Omega
by linearity since
\mathrm{skew}(Q^{\top}AQ)=Q^{\top}\mathrm{skew}(A)Q
. We term this variant the
Implicit Paardekooper’s method
. The pseudo-code of
algorithm

3.1
includes this variant in
green
and marked with (ii).

6
Perturbation analysis

This section presents a perturbation analysis yielding analogs of the results of
section

2
that take into account the effect of inexactness. It quantifies how the matrix structures, such as symmetry in (
2.5
), are perturbed in practice. This section explains the behavior and justifies the design of
algorithm

5.1
. More precisely, by means of perturbation bounds, it identifies matrices that may trigger the different steps of the algorithm. Indeed, the results of
section

2
hold if and only if the real Schur decomposition of
\Omega
is known exactly. In practice, the inexactness comes from (i) floating-point arithmetic and (ii) the stopping criteria in
algorithm

5.1
. If the stopping criteria are not smaller than machine precision, we claim that their influence dominates that of floating-point arithmetic, such that the numerical behavior of
algorithm

5.1
can be anticipated by considering
exact
arithmetic. This claim is supported by the following facts.

1.

All matrix transformations involve orthogonal matrices, in particular, Givens rotations. These are well-known to be backward stable: given a matrix
\widehat{Q}
obtained from the floating-point product of
s
sets of
N
disjoint Givens rotations and
Q
the exact product,
\|Q-\widehat{Q}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}f(s)
where
f(s)
is a moderately growing function of
s

[
22
, Lem. 19.9]
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}
is the unit roundoff..

2.

The effect of floating-point arithmetic is captured by the stopping criterion. Indeed, given a stopping criterion
\widehat{\rho}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}
in floating-point arithmetic, there is an effective stopping criterion
\rho
of equivalent magnitude in exact arithmetic. Indeed, assume the approximate RSD
\widehat{Q}^{\top}A\widehat{Q}-\widehat{S}=\widehat{E}
with
\|\widehat{E}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}
. Let
S
be an exact Schur form of
A
that is nearest to
\widehat{S}
and assume
\widehat{Q}
is approximately orthogonal such that
\widehat{Q}=Q+E_{Q}
for
Q\in\mathrm{O}(n)
with
\|E_{Q}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}
, then

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}^{\top}A\widehat{Q}-\widehat{S}=\widehat{E}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(Q+E_{Q})^{\top}A(Q+E_{Q})-\widehat{S}=\widehat{E}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle Q^{\top}AQ-S=E\eqcolon\widehat{E}-E_{Q}^{\top}AQ-Q^{\top}AE_{Q}-E_{Q}^{\top}AE_{Q}+(\widehat{S}-S).

This yields an effective error bound
\|E\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\tau\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}
with
\tau\geq 1
for exact arithmetic.

Therefore, this section assumes that operations are performed in exact arithmetic with inexactness only induced by the stopping criteria.

Consider the output of step I.1 where the stopping criterion
\rho=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}
is used. Then, by
[
27
, Lem. 4.3]
, step I.1 returns
Q\in\mathrm{O}(n)
such that

\Omega Q-Q\mathrm{skew}(S)=E,\ \text{where}\ \|E\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\tau\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\text{ and $S$ is a Schur form of $A$.}

(6.1)

with
\tau\geq 1
. For the rest of this section, the short-hand notation
\cos(\theta_{i})\eqcolon c_{i}
and
\sin(\theta_{i})\eqcolon s_{i}
is used.

6.1
Eigenvalues with distinct nonzero imaginary parts

Assume first that all eigenvalues of
A
have distinct nonzero imaginary parts. The eigenvalues are denoted by
\{\lambda_{j}e^{\pm i\theta_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{p}
. Then, if and only if (
6.1
) holds, it is shown in
[
27
, Thm. 4.4]
that

\|AQ-QS\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\tau\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\left(1+\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1,...,p\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\lambda_{i}c_{i}-\lambda_{j}c_{j}}{\lambda_{i}s_{i}-\lambda_{j}s_{j}}\right|\right).

(6.2)

By (
6.2
), small distances between the imaginary parts of the eigenvalues of
A
result in non-negligible off-diagonal blocks in
A
after step I.1. However, unless these off-diagonal blocks are large enough, they are ignored by the clustering procedure of step I.2, and thus not considered at step II. Step III is designed for solving the loss of accuracy induced by (
6.2
).
A similar result can be obtained for eigenvalues with repeated nonzero imaginary parts and real eigenvalues.

6.2
Eigenvalues with repeated nonzero imaginary parts

Assume there is multiplicity in the imaginary parts of the eigenvalues such that for all
j\in[j]\subseteq\{1,...,p\}
, we have
\lambda_{j}s_{j}=\sigma
. Let
m\coloneq|[j]|
and
[i]\coloneq\{1,...,p\}\setminus[j]
. Then, by (
6.1
), there is subset
V\in\mathrm{St}(n,2m)
of the columns of
Q
such that
\Omega V-V(J_{2}\otimes\sigma I_{m})=E^{\prime}
with
\|E^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\tau\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}
. By
theorem

10
given in appendix, we have

\displaystyle\min_{R\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\|A(VR)-(VR)\left[\begin{smallmatrix}D_{m}&-\sigma I_{m}\\
\sigma I_{m}&D_{m}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\|_{\mathrm{F}}

(6.3)

\displaystyle\leq\tau\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\left(1+\frac{\max\limits_{i\in[i],j\in[j]}|\lambda_{i}c_{i}-\lambda_{j}c_{j}|}{\min\limits_{i\in[i]}|\lambda_{i}s_{i}-\sigma|}+\frac{\max\limits_{j_{1},j_{2}\in[j]}|\lambda_{j_{1}}c_{j_{1}}-\lambda_{j_{2}}c_{j_{2}}|}{\sigma}\right)+\mathrm{o}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}),

where
D_{m}
is an
m\times m
diagonal matrix containing the real parts of the eigenvalues. Thus, after step I.1, there is a
2m\times 2m
submatrix
\widehat{M}=V^{\top}AV-\sigma J_{2}\otimes I_{m}
and

\displaystyle\min_{M\in\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)}\|\widehat{M}-M\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{R\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\|\widehat{M}-R\left[\begin{smallmatrix}D_{m}&0\\
0&D_{m}\end{smallmatrix}\right]R^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\min_{R\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\|\widehat{M}+\sigma J_{2}\otimes I_{m}-R\left[\begin{smallmatrix}D_{m}&-\sigma I_{m}\\
\sigma I_{m}&D_{m}\end{smallmatrix}\right]R^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\breve{R}\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\|AV-V(R\left[\begin{smallmatrix}D_{m}&-\sigma I_{m}\\
\sigma I_{m}&D_{m}\end{smallmatrix}\right]R^{\top})\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Combining the latter inequality with (
6.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\widehat{M}-\mathrm{sskh}(\widehat{M})\|_{\mathrm{F}}

(6.4)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\tau\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\left(1+\frac{\max\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I},j\in[j]}|\lambda_{i}c_{i}-\lambda_{j}c_{j}|}{\min\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}}|\lambda_{i}s_{i}-\sigma|}+\frac{\max\limits_{j_{1},j_{2}\in[j]}|\lambda_{j_{1}}c_{j_{1}}-\lambda_{j_{2}}c_{j_{2}}|}{\sigma}\right)+\mathrm{o}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}).

In conclusion,
\widehat{M}=\mathrm{sskh}(\widehat{M})+\Delta\widehat{M}
where the error bound on
\Delta\widehat{M}
is amplified, either by eigenvalues with imaginary parts close to
\sigma
or if
\sigma
is very small. The consequence for
algorithm

5.1
is that the specialized Jacobi algorithm for SSkH matrices of step II.1 is only triggered if
\Delta\widehat{M}
is small enough. Otherwise, the method is redirected to step II.3 and then to step III.

6.3
Real eigenvalues

Finally, assume the matrix
A
has
r
real eigenvalues and define the sets
[j]\coloneq\{1,...,p\}
and
[r]\coloneq\{1,...,r\}
. Then, by (
6.1
), there is a subset
V\in\mathrm{St}(n,r)
of columns of
Q
such that
\Omega V=E^{\prime\prime}
with
\|E^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\tau\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}
. By
[
27
, Thm. 4.7]
, it holds that

\min_{\breve{R}\in\mathrm{O}(r)}\|A(V\breve{R})-(V\breve{R})\breve{\Lambda}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\tau\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\left(1+\frac{\max\limits_{j\in[j],\ k\in[r]}|\lambda_{j}c_{j}-\breve{\lambda}_{k}|}{\min\limits_{j=1,....,p}|\lambda_{j}s_{j}|}\right)+\mathrm{o}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}).

(6.5)

Similarly, the error bound on the real eigenspace is amplified by complex eigenvalues with small imaginary parts. The result from (
6.5
) is the perturbed analog of (
2.5
).

At the end of step I.1, an
r\times r
submatrix of
A
is equal to
\widehat{H}=V^{\top}AV
. This submatrix
\widehat{H}
satisfies

\min_{H\in\mathrm{Sym}(r)}\|\widehat{H}-H\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\min_{\breve{R}\in\mathrm{O}(r)}\|\widehat{H}-\breve{R}\breve{\Lambda}\breve{R}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\min_{\breve{R}\in\mathrm{O}(r)}\|AV-V(\breve{R}\breve{\Lambda}\breve{R}^{\top})\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Moreover, it is well known that
\argmin_{H\in\mathrm{Sym}(r)}\|\widehat{H}-H\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\mathrm{sym}(\widehat{H})
. Therefore, by (
6.5
), it follows that

\|\widehat{H}-\mathrm{sym}(\widehat{H})\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\tau\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\left(1+\frac{\max\limits_{j\in[j],k\in[r]}|\lambda_{j}c_{j}-\breve{\lambda}_{k}|}{\min\limits_{j\in[j]}|\lambda_{j}s_{j}|}\right)+\mathrm{o}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{m}}).

(6.6)

In conclusion,
\widehat{H}=\mathrm{sym}(\widehat{H})+\Delta\widehat{H}
where the error bound on
\Delta\widehat{H}=\mathrm{skew}(\widehat{H})
is amplified by eigenvalues with small imaginary parts. If these imaginary parts are large enough, then
\Delta\widehat{H}
is small enough for
algorithm

5.1
to trigger step II.2. Otherwise, the real eigenvalues are approximated at step II.3 and refined at step III.

8
Nearest symmetric skew-Hamiltonian and ortho-symplectic matrices

The design of
algorithm

5.1
and the perturbation analysis of
section

6
required fundamental results on symmetric skew-Hamiltoninan and ortho-symplectic matrices. This section proves these results. Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
, let us consider the following partition in
m\times m
blocks:

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}.

8.1
Nearest symmetric skew-Hamiltonian matrix

We consider the set
\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)
of
2m\times 2m
symmetric skew-Hamiltonian matrices. It can be identified with
m\times m
Hermitian matrices. Indeed,

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{H}&-\widetilde{\Omega}\\
\widetilde{\Omega}&\widetilde{H}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)\iff\widetilde{H}+i\widetilde{\Omega}=(\widetilde{H}+i\widetilde{\Omega})^{*}.

(8.1)

However, since
\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
can not be identified with
\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, the nearest symmetric skew-Hamiltonian matrix is not equivalent to the nearest Hermitian matrix.

Theorem 6
.

For every matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
, the nearest symmetric skew-Hamiltonian matrix to
A
exists, is unique and is given by

\argmin_{M\in\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)}\|A-M\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\mathrm{sskh}(A)\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{sym}(A_{11}+A_{22})&-\mathrm{skew}(A_{21}-A_{12})\\
\mathrm{skew}(A_{21}-A_{12})&\mathrm{sym}(A_{11}+A_{22})\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

Proving the statement first requires splitting the matrix
A
as follows:

\displaystyle 2A

\displaystyle=(A+J_{2m}AJ_{2m}^{\top})+(A-J_{2m}AJ_{2m}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}+A_{22}&A_{12}-A_{21}\\
A_{21}-A_{12}&A_{11}+A_{22}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}-A_{22}&A_{12}+A_{21}\\
A_{21}+A_{12}&A_{22}-A_{11}\end{bmatrix}.

Moreover, a second splitting in symmetric and skew-symmetric parts yields

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}+A_{22}&A_{12}-A_{21}\\
A_{21}-A_{12}&A_{11}+A_{22}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{sym}(A_{11}+A_{22})&-\mathrm{skew}(A_{21}-A_{12})\\
\mathrm{skew}(A_{21}-A_{12})&\mathrm{sym}(A_{11}+A_{22})\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqcolon 2\mathrm{sskh}(A)}

\displaystyle+\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{skew}(A_{11}+A_{22})&-\mathrm{sym}(A_{21}-A_{12})\\
\mathrm{sym}(A_{21}-A_{12})&\mathrm{skew}(A_{11}+A_{22})\end{bmatrix}.

The matrix
\mathrm{sskh}(A)
is termed the
symmetric skew-Hamiltonian part
of
A
. Since
\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
,
\mathrm{sskh}(A)
is the nearest SSH matrix to
A
if and only if
E\coloneq A-\mathrm{sskh}(A)
is orthogonal to
\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)
. Notice that

\displaystyle 2E

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{skew}(A_{11}+A_{22})&-\mathrm{sym}(A_{21}-A_{12})\\
\mathrm{sym}(A_{21}-A_{12})&\mathrm{skew}(A_{11}+A_{22})\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqcolon 2E_{1}}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}-A_{22}&A_{12}+A_{21}\\
A_{21}+A_{12}&A_{22}-A_{11}\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqcolon 2E_{2}}.

For all
M\in\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)
, by linearity of the inner product, we have

\displaystyle\langle M,2E\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\langle M,2E_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}+\langle M,2E_{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}.

Let
M=I_{2}\otimes\widetilde{H}+J_{2}\otimes\widetilde{\Omega}
with
\widetilde{H}\in\mathrm{Sym}(m)
and
\widetilde{\Omega}\in\mathrm{Skew}(m)
. This yields

\displaystyle\langle M,2E_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Tr}\left(\widetilde{H}\mathrm{skew}(A_{11}+A_{22})-\widetilde{\Omega}\mathrm{sym}(A_{21}-A_{12})\right)=2(0-0)=0.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\langle M,2E_{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{H}(A_{11}-A_{22})-\widetilde{\Omega}(A_{21}+A_{12})+\widetilde{H}(A_{22}-A_{11})+\widetilde{\Omega}(A_{21}+A_{12}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\widetilde{H}0-\widetilde{\Omega}0)

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore, for all
M\in\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)
,
\langle\underbrace{\mathrm{sskh}(A)-M}_{\in\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)},E\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=0
. Thus, it holds that

\displaystyle\argmin_{M\in\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)}\|A-M\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{M\in\mathrm{SSkH}(2m)}\|\mathrm{sskh}(A)-M\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|E\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sskh}(A).

8.2
Nearest ortho-symplectic matrix

Consider the set
\mathrm{OSp}(2m)
of
2m\times 2m
ortho-symplectic matrices. It is known that
\mathrm{OSp}(2m)
can be identified with
\mathrm{U}(m)
, the set of
m\times m
unitary matrices:

\begin{bmatrix}U_{\mathrm{r}}&-U_{\mathrm{i}}\\
U_{\mathrm{i}}&U_{\mathrm{r}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)\iff U_{\mathrm{r}}+iU_{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathrm{U}(m).

Again, the nearest ortho-symplectic matrix does not readily derive from this identification because the set
\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
is larger than
\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
. In
theorem

7
, an explicit expression for the nearest ortho-symplectic matrix is given. From the result of
theorem

7
, an easily computable upper bound on the distance to the ortho-symplectic group is derived in
theorem

8
. In the absence of ambiguity, we let
I\coloneq I_{2m}
and
J\coloneq J_{2}\otimes I_{m}
.

Theorem 7
.

For every matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
,
R^{\star}
is a nearest ortho-symplectic matrix, i.e., such that

R^{\star}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{\mathrm{r}}^{\star}&-U_{\mathrm{i}}^{\star}\\
U_{\mathrm{i}}^{\star}&U_{\mathrm{r}}^{\star}\end{bmatrix}\in\argmin_{R\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\|A-R\|_{\mathrm{F}},

if and only if
U_{\mathrm{r}}^{\star}+iU_{\mathrm{i}}^{\star}=UV^{*}
is a polar factor of
\left(\frac{A_{11}+A_{22}}{2}\right)+i\left(\frac{A_{21}-A_{12}}{2}\right)=U\Sigma V^{*}
. Moreover,

\min_{R\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\|A-R\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=2\|\Sigma-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|AJ-JA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

(8.2)

Proof.

The result follows from the following sequence of identities, where, in particular, the first identity follows from
\langle A+JAJ^{\top}-R,A-JAJ^{\top}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=0
.

\displaystyle\min_{R\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\|A-R\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\small\min_{R\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\left\|\frac{A+JAJ^{\top}}{2}-R\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|\frac{A-JAJ^{\top}}{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{U_{\mathrm{r}}+iU_{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathrm{U}(m)}\left\|\frac{1}{2}\small\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}+A_{22}&A_{12}-A_{21}\\
A_{21}-A_{12}&A_{11}+A_{22}\end{bmatrix}-\small\begin{bmatrix}U_{\mathrm{r}}&-U_{\mathrm{i}}\\
U_{\mathrm{i}}&U_{\mathrm{r}}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\left\|AJ-JA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{U_{\mathrm{r}}+iU_{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathrm{U}(m)}2\left\|\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}+A_{22}\\
A_{21}-A_{12}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}U_{\mathrm{r}}\\
U_{\mathrm{i}}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|AJ-JA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{U_{\mathrm{r}}+iU_{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathrm{U}(m)}2\left\|\left(\frac{A_{11}+A_{22}}{2}\right)+i\left(\frac{A_{21}-A_{12}}{2}\right)-(U_{\mathrm{r}}+iU_{\mathrm{i}})\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\left\|AJ-JA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Finally, it is well known that a matrix is a nearest unitary matrix if and only if it is a polar factor of
\left(\frac{A_{11}+A_{22}}{2}\right)+i\left(\frac{A_{21}-A_{12}}{2}\right)
. Moreover, the distance to the nearest unitary matrix is equal to
\|U\Sigma V^{*}-UV^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|\Sigma-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}
. This concludes the proof.

The proof of
theorem

10
requires an explicit upper bound in terms of
A
on the distance to a nearest ortho-symplectic matrix. We give this bound in
theorem

8
.

Theorem 8
.

For every matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{R\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\|A-R\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\|A^{\top}A-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|A^{\top}JA-J\|_{\mathrm{F}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|AJ-JA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Proof.

Let us define
B=\left(\frac{A_{11}+A_{22}}{2}\right)+i\left(\frac{A_{21}-A_{12}}{2}\right)=U\Sigma V^{*}
. Then it follows that

\displaystyle 2\|\Sigma-I_{m}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\Sigma^{2}-I_{m}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\|B^{*}B-I_{m}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\frac{1}{4}(A+JAJ^{\top})^{\top}(A+JAJ^{\top})-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\frac{1}{4}(A^{\top}A+JA^{\top}AJ^{\top}+A^{\top}JAJ^{\top}+JA^{\top}J^{\top}A)-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{16}\left(2\|A^{\top}A-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}+2\|A^{\top}JAJ^{\top}-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\|A^{\top}A-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|A^{\top}JA-J\|_{\mathrm{F}}\right)^{2}.

By inserting the latter inequality in (
8.2
), the claim follows.

The following
corollary

9
derives directly from
theorem

8
. It is used in the proof of
theorem

10

Corollary 9
.

For every matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
, we have

\min_{R\in\mathrm{OSp}(2m)}\|A-R\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\|A^{\top}A-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\|A^{\top}JA-J\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\left\|AJ-JA\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\right).

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we have designed a new Jacobi-like algorithm for computing the real Schur decomposition of a real normal matrix. We have shown in the numerical experiments of
section

7
that
algorithm

5.1
is accurate and faster than the other Jacobi-like algorithms for normal matrices. An important contribution to this line of work would include an open-source multithreaded implementation of
algorithm

5.1
.

\sigma&0\end{bmatrix}=(P_{\mathrm{l}}^{\top}\widetilde{E}P_{\mathrm{r}})_{ij}.

(A.1)

B.1
The Schur form of a
3\times 3
skew-symmetric matrix

Paardekooper’s method with
n
odd requires the solution to the
3\times 3
skew-symmetric EVP. Solving the problem requires two similarity transformations by Givens rotations. The first Givens rotation is obtained from the elements
\omega_{21}
and
\omega_{31}
:

\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&c_{1}&s_{1}\\
0&-s_{1}&c_{1}\end{bmatrix}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&-\omega_{21}&-\omega_{31}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{21}}&0&-\omega_{32}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{31}}&\omega_{32}&0\end{bmatrix}}_{=\Omega}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&c_{1}&-s_{1}\\
0&s_{1}&c_{1}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&-\omega_{21}^{\prime}&0\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{21}^{\prime}}&0&-\omega_{32}^{\prime}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}0}&\omega_{32}^{\prime}&0\end{bmatrix}}_{=\Omega^{(1)}},

where
c_{1}=\frac{\omega_{21}}{\sqrt{\omega_{21}^{2}+\omega_{31}^{2}}}
and
s_{1}=\frac{\omega_{31}}{\sqrt{\omega_{21}^{2}+\omega_{31}^{2}}}
define the Givens rotation. The second transformation is obtained from
-\omega_{21}^{\prime}
and
\omega_{32}^{\prime}
of
\Omega^{(1)}
:

\small\begin{bmatrix}c_{2}&0&s_{2}\\
0&1&0\\
-s_{2}&0&c_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}-\omega_{21}^{\prime}}&0\\
\omega_{21}^{\prime}&0&-\omega_{32}^{\prime}\\
0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}\omega_{32}^{\prime}}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{2}&0&-s_{2}\\
0&1&0\\
s_{2}&0&c_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}-\sigma}&0\\
\sigma&0&0\\
0&{\color[rgb]{0.4,0.4,1}0}&0\end{bmatrix},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{with,}\hskip 9.24994pt\sigma\geq 0,

where
c_{2}=\frac{\omega_{21}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega_{21}^{\prime 2}+\omega_{32}^{\prime 2}}}
and
s_{2}=\frac{-\omega_{32}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega_{21}^{\prime 2}+\omega_{32}^{\prime 2}}}
.
These two steps give a simple, explicit solution to the

skew-symmetric eigenvalue problem. Notice that

B.3
A Jacobi-like algorithm based on
RandDiag

The randomized method
RandDiag
for computing the eigenvalue decomposition of a complex normal matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is proposed in
[
21
]
. It relies on the diagonalization of the Hermitian matrix
\widetilde{H}=\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}(A+A^{*})+i\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}(A-A^{*})=U\Lambda U^{*}
where
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The authors show that
U
diagonalizes
A
with probability
1

[
21
, Thm. 1]
. An implicit Jacobi-like algorithm can be derived from
RandDiag
. Its pseudo-code is given in
algorithm

B.2
. It consists of applying Jacobi’s algorithm for Hermitian matrices
implicitly
to the Hermitian matrix by applying the rotations on
A
.

Algorithm B.2

RandDiag
algorithm

A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
normal,
Q\in\mathrm{O}(n)
and
\rho>0
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

while

\mathrm{offdiag}(\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}(A+A^{*})+i\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}(A-A^{*}))>\rho\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}

for

i=1,...,n-1

do

for

j=i+1,...,n

\mathtt{l}=\{i,j\}
