Nearly Optimal Attention Coresets
Edo Liberty, Alexandr Andoni, Eldar Kleiner

Abstract
We consider the problem of estimating the Attention mechanism in small space, and prove the existence of coresets for it of nearly optimal size. Specifically, we show that for any set of unit-norm keys and values ( K , V ) (K,V) in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , there exists a subset ( K ′ , V ′ ) (K^{\prime},V^{\prime}) of size at most O ⁡ ( d ​ e ρ + o ⁡ ( ρ ) / ϵ ) O({\sqrt{d}e^{\rho+o(\rho)}/\epsilon}) such that ‖ Attn ⁡ ( q , K , V ) − Attn ⁡ ( q , K ′ , V ′ ) ‖ ≤ ε \left\|\Attn(q,K,V)-\Attn(q,K^{\prime},V^{\prime})\right\|\leq\varepsilon simultaneously for all queries whose norm is bounded by ρ \rho . This outperforms the best known results for this problem. We also offer an improved lower bound showing that ε \varepsilon -coresets must have size Ω ⁡ ( d ​ e ρ / ϵ ) \Omega({\sqrt{d}e^{\rho}/\epsilon}) .

We study the question of optimal KV-cache compression with error guarantees.

Perhaps surprisingly, the optimal size of the coreset for the attention problem has not been known yet, and our goal here is to establish such a bound. Building on the above line of work, we construct a subset of key-value pairs that uniformly approximates the attention output for every query of bounded norm, with a coreset size that nearly matches our information-theoretic lower bound.

Problem definition.

In the attention computation problem, we are given a set of
n
keys and values (
k_{i},v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
) denoted by
K,V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume keys are centered and both keys and values are at most norm one.
For a query vector
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and we need to output the “attention vector”
\Attn(q,K,V)=A(q,K,V)/B(q,K)
, where

A(q,K,V)=\sum_{i}e^{q^{T}k_{i}}v_{i}\mbox{ \;\; and \;\; }B(q,K)=\sum_{i}e^{q^{T}k_{i}}.

To simplify the presentation we remove a customary (but superfluous)
1/\sqrt{d}
factor from the exponents (see Section
2
for precise setup).

A
coreset
is a subset
K^{\prime},V^{\prime}
of
K,V
respectively, of size
S
such that, for some error tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, and for any query
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or some more restricted class):

\|\Attn(q,K,V)-\Attn(q,K^{\prime},V^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon.

(1)

Limits of compression and on the need to bound the norm of
q
.

Without any constraints on or relaxations of the attention problem, no compression is possible.
The attention mechanism approximates retrieval; the very terminology of “keys” and “values” is borrowed from associative lookup.
Concretely, attention can be made to reproduce any single value vector almost exactly: place a query in the direction of the corresponding key and scale its norm to make that key dominate the softmax.
Any sketch that supports arbitrary queries must therefore essentially store the full set of values, which seems to rule out any lossy compression.

The escape from this impossibility is to bound the query norm.
A query that “cherry-picks” a specific value is necessarily one of large norm: the larger the desired separation in softmax weights, the larger the query norm required.
We therefore seek to compress the cache so that attention is approximated for
all
queries below a fixed norm threshold
\rho
.
Without such a constraint, the lower bounds such as
30
or our improved Theorem
2
rule out any compression.

Discussion of the approach to Theorem
1
.

Our main technical contribution is to design a coreset for the
A(q,K,V)
vector;
optimal coresets for
B(q,K)
are already known and are a direct outcome of
28
.

The result in
28
produces small corsets for general sums of all analytic functions of the shape
\sum_{i}f(q^{T}k_{i})
or
\sum_{i}f(\|q-k_{i}\|)
.
It relies heavily on deep result in discrepancy theory called Banaszczyk’s vector balancing theorem
4
.
The result in
28
does not naturally extend to vector valued functions (like
A(q,K,V)
). Indeed, such an extension is the main focus of this paper.

Very recent work gave a streaming coreset contraction
30
based on discrepancy minimization that achieves size
S
roughly
\tfrac{\sqrt{d}\log n}{\varepsilon}e^{2\rho}
(when mapped to our setup).
They design an efficient algorithm by applying a breakthrough algorithm for online discrepancy minimization
3
.
They use a linearization of the exponential kernel
e^{z_{1}^{T}z_{2}}=\psi(z_{1})^{T}\psi(z_{2})
and run Algorithm 1 in
3
on
\psi(k_{i})
.
As is common for operating on infinite-dimensional kernel embeddings
\psi
, the authors use the kernel trick to sidestep it.
The crux of their proof uses Banaszczyk’s theorem to show that
\sum_{i}\psi(k_{i})
is contained in an appropriately small ball.

In this paper, we surgically modify (and simplify) the result in
28
to prove the existence of smaller coresets.
We too use Banaszczyk’s theorem but in a different way. Specifically, we craft a convex body that more tightly bounds certain tensor norms.
As a result, this paper improves the best known upper bound by a factor of
\log(n)\cdot e^{\rho-o(\rho)}
. We show our bound is a near-optimal coreset size
by proving a matching coreset lower bound.

Related work

Work on compressing the KV-cache has, broadly, taken four directions:

•

Floating point compression:

The first is to keep all key-value pairs but represent each entry using fewer bits.
These include a large number of techniques which are out of scope for this manuscript.
Notable examples include
13
;
26
;
21
;
50
.
These methods reduce per-entry storage but leave the number of cached tokens unchanged.

•

Distillation:

A second line replaces the original keys and values with a different, smaller set that mimics the same attention behavior.
Examples include clustering keys and values to a small set of centroids
52
, learning compact representations of long contexts
18
, prefix and prompt tuning
33
, and attention-matching distillation
54
. The substituted set need not be a subset of the original vectors.
The approaches require access to the query distribution to avoid degrading quality.

•

Linearization:

A third line abandons the explicit cache altogether and replaces the attention mechanism with a linear-time surrogate based on random or learned feature maps
38
;
29
;
14
;
47
;
40
, or with state-space and recurrent alternatives
24
;
8
. Such models trade exact softmax attention for fixed-size state but require architectural changes and retraining.

•

Coresets selection:

A fourth direction, and the one taken in this paper, is
coreset
construction: keep a small subset of the original key-value pairs and discard the rest.
Heuristics in this family include attention-sink retention
49
, accumulated-attention scoring
53
;
53
;
34
;
35
;
35
, layer- and head-adaptive budget allocation
11
;
20
;
48
, query-aware sparsity
43
, model-guided eviction
22
, sparse long-context patterns
9
, and short-window memorization
10
. A recent line gives provable guarantees for coreset-style attention approximation via discrepancy theory
30
;
52
;
51
;
25
.

Computing attention for all queries.

A related but distinct line studies the cost of computing the full attention matrix-vector product for
n
queries, rather than compressing a fixed KV-cache for future queries.
Exact implementations such as FlashAttention optimize memory traffic and hardware utilization but still compute the dense attention pattern
17
.
On the theory side, subquadratic or near-linear algorithms are possible under additional smoothness, bounded-entry, or approximation assumptions, often via kernel-density estimation, sampling, or kernel data structures
51
;
25
;
12
;
40
.

We highlight the work of
2
who parametrize the complexity of the problem as a function of the size of the entries of
K,V,
and
q
’s, similar to our bound on norm
\rho
.
They
identify a sharp bounded-entry transition: for
d=O(\log n)
and entries below
\sqrt{\log n}
, attention can be approximated in near-linear time, while entries of order
\sqrt{\log n}
lead to SETH-based barriers against truly subquadratic algorithms.
Our setting is complementary: we ask how many key-value pairs must be retained so that a compressed cache answers every bounded-norm query, independent of the number of future queries.

Setup and notation

Unit normed values:
We assume that
\|v_{i}\|\leq 1
, without loss of generality.
This is achieved by scaling
v
by
\max_{i}\|v_{i}\|_{2}
. The attention vector, and the eventual norm of the error, scales linearly with the same factor.
The results from
30
obtain bounds that depend on
\sum_{i}\|v_{i}\|_{2}
which is could be more general.
They split the
K,V
set in logarithmic number of bins whose value norms are at most a constant factor away from one another.
This obviously incurs an additional logarithmic factor in the coreset size. For brevity and simplicity, we do not add this discussion to this paper.

Nearly centered keys:
We assume
\|\sum_{i}k_{i}\|\leq c\sqrt{d}\log(n)
without loss of generality.
Translating keys by any vector does not change the attention expression.
Specifically, subtracting
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}k_{i}
from each key yields
\|\sum_{i}k_{i}\|=0
and minimizes
\sum_{i}\|k_{i}\|^{2}
which should heuristically work in our favor.
In the worst case, it can could double the norm of some keys.

Unit normed keys:
We also assume
\|k_{i}\|\leq 1
without loss of generality, since the attention is a function of
q^{T}k_{i}
.
Hence, dividing all key vectors by
\max_{i}{\|k_{i}\|}
and multiplying queries by the same factor changes nothing.

Query norms:
Throughout,
\rho
denotes the maximal radius
\|q\|\leq\rho
to which the bounds hold.
The coreset size and discrepancy bounds will depend on a slightly larger radius
\zeta:=\rho+\frac{1}{2}\log(\rho)+o(\log\log{\rho})
.

No temperature
\sqrt{d}
rescaling:
The standard notation uses weights
e^{q^{T}k_{i}/\sqrt{d}}
.
For the same reason as above, we can absorb the
\sqrt{d}
factor in the exponent into the norms of
q
. This only simplifies the notation.
Therefore, readers who are used to the standard notation should think of our query vectors
q
as
q\cdot\max_{i}\|k_{i}\|/\sqrt{d}
.
As a practical note,
k_{i}
are usually initialized to be random Gaussians.
So
\|k_{i}\|
are typically
\sqrt{d}
and
\max_{i}\|k_{i}\|/\sqrt{d}
can roughly be thought of as a constant.

Tensor notations:

We use
X=x^{\otimes m}
to denote
d
-dimensional
m
-tensor of
x
outer-producted with itself
m
-times
X[i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m}]=x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{m}}
.
By convention
x^{\otimes 0}=1
is a scalar or a zero-dimensional tensor.
We use
\operatorname{flat}(X)
to denote a
d^{m}
long vector containing the individual entires of
X
. Finally, we use
\langle X,Y\rangle:=\operatorname{flat}(X)^{T}\operatorname{flat}(Y)
.

Constants:
We use
c
to indicate universal constants but reuse the same symbol for different constants.

Bounding the numerator

The halving trick.

There is a standard “trick" to halve the number of items.
Create the error function
E(q,K,V):=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}e^{q^{T}k_{i}}v_{i}^{T}

by multiplying every summand above with a sign
\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
.
Let
(K^{\prime},V^{\prime})
be the set associated with indexes whose
\sigma_{i}=1
. Then:

\displaystyle A(q,K,V)+E(q,K,V)=2A(q,K^{\prime},V^{\prime}).

Note that
(K^{\prime},V^{\prime})
is at most half the size of
(K,V)
. If that’s not the case, flip the signs of
\sigma_{i}
.
At this point, we can now pick arbitrary signs
\sigma
specifically to minimize
\|E(q)\|
.

Discrepancy argument.

Here, we use a discrepancy argument to show that there is always some signs
\sigma
for which
\|E(q)\|
is small.
We use a technique similar to
28
;
30
.
We surgically modify and simplify their arguments to work specifically for this case.

Below we use the Taylor expansion
e^{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x^{m}/m!
,
the fact that
||v||=\max_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}v^{T}u
,
that
(q^{T}k)^{m}=\langle q^{\otimes m},k^{\otimes m}\rangle
, and
q^{\otimes m}=\|q\|^{m}\cdot\hat{q}^{\otimes m}
where
\hat{q}=q/\|q\|
.
We also define
z(m):=\sqrt{3(m+2)}\log(m+2)
and
S_{m}:=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\frac{k_{i}^{\otimes m}\otimes v_{i}}{z(m)}
.

\displaystyle\|E(q)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{u,q}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}e^{q^{T}k_{i}}v_{i}^{T}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{q,u}\sum_{i}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}(q^{T}k_{i})^{m}v_{i}^{T}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{q,u}\sum_{i}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\left\langle q^{\otimes m},k_{i}^{\otimes m}\right\rangle v_{i}^{T}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{q,u}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\left\langle q^{\otimes m}\otimes u,\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}k_{i}^{\otimes m}\otimes v_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{z(m)\|q\|^{m}}{m!}\max_{\hat{q},u}\left\langle\hat{q}^{\otimes m}\otimes u,\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\frac{k_{i}^{\otimes m}\otimes v_{i}}{z(m)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{z(m)\|q\|^{m}}{m!}\|S_{m}\|_{\star}

\displaystyle\|E(q)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\sqrt{d}\sum_{m}\frac{z(m)\rho^{m}\sqrt{\log(m)}}{m!}

5
Simultaneous Balancing

Here we prove Facts

,
2
.

Proof of Fact
1
.

This is straight forward because
\|k_{i}\|\leq 1
. Banaszczyk’s vector balancing theorem applies directly.
We can define the convex set
\mathcal{K}
for the Gaussians as the ball of radius
\sqrt{10d}
.
Applying Markov to the squared norm of
g
, a vector of
d
Gaussians, we get
\Pr[\|g\|^{2}\geq 10d]\leq\mathbb{E}[\sum g_{i}^{2}]/10d\leq 1/10
.
There is always a set
K^{\prime}
of at most half the key such that
\|\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}k_{i}\|=\|\sum_{i}k_{i}-2\sum_{i}k^{\prime}_{i}\|\leq 5\sqrt{10d}

Proof of Corrolary
1
.

While a coreset for

Set
V
such that all

For the proof above to work, however, we need to achieve all three objectives

Theorem 5

(simultaneous balancing)
.

Let there be
s

n

. Let the Gaussian measure of

be at least

\sum_{j}\delta_{j}<1/2
then there exist signs

\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}x_{i}^{s}\in\sqrt{s}5\mathcal{K}^{s}

Proof.

This is a trivial extension of Banaszczyk’s vector balancing theorem.
Let

be a concatenation of

\|x_{i}\|\leq 1
.
Since
\sum_{j}\delta_{j}\leq 1/2
, by the union bound, the gaussian measure of

.
So, there exist

\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}x_{i}\in 5\mathcal{K}

Since

are dimension disjoint, this also means that, for all

\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}x_{i}^{j}\in 5\mathcal{K}^{j}

∎

In our case, use

6
Lower Bounds

We now prove the following two lower bounds.
Essentially, the first theorem proves a bound of
\Omega(de^{\rho}/\varepsilon)

bits
required to store a sketch for the Attention computation. The second theorem proves a bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{d}e^{\rho}/\varepsilon)
on the size of the coreset (Theorem
2
from the introduction).

We use the following standard spherical code fact
15
: there is an absolute constant
a>0
such that, for every
0<\eta\leq 1/2
and every
m\leq\exp(a\eta^{2}d_{k})
, there are unit vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
with
\langle u_{i},u_{h}\rangle\leq\eta
for every
i\neq h
.

Theorem 6
.

Fix dimensions
d,d_{k}>1
,
\rho\geq 2
, let
L=e^{\rho}
, and let
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
for a sufficiently small absolute constant
\varepsilon_{0}
. Suppose there is a sketching algorithm that, for any input
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}},V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|k_{i}\|_{2},\|v_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1
, produces a sketch
\Attn^{\prime}(\cdot)
of size
S
so that for any
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
of norm at most
\rho
, with constant probability,

\|\Attn(q,K,V)-\Attn^{\prime}(q)\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon.

Then

S=\Omega\!\left(d\cdot\min\{L/\varepsilon,\;L^{2},\;\exp(ad_{k}/\log^{2}L)\}\right).

In particular, it is enough to take
d_{k}=\Omega(\log^{2}L\cdot\log(L/\varepsilon))
for the key dimension not to be the bottleneck.

Proof.

We reduce from one-way
Indexing
. By repeating the sketch a constant number of times, we may assume its success probability is at least
0.99
, changing
S
by only a constant factor. Set
\eta=1/\log L
. Since
\rho=\log L\geq 2
, we have
\eta\leq 1/2
. Let

m=\left\lfloor c\min\{L/\varepsilon,\;L^{2},\;\exp(ad_{k}/\log^{2}L)\}\right\rfloor

for a sufficiently small absolute constant
c
, and consider an
Index
instance
x\in\{0,1\}^{md}
. Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
be the codewords from the coding fact above. We identify a bit position with a pair
(i,j)\in[m]\times[d]
. Using public randomness, Alice and Bob draw signs
b_{ij}\in\{\pm 1\}
. Alice constructs
m
key-value pairs

k_{i}=u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}},\qquad v_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}(b_{i1}x_{i1},\ldots,b_{id}x_{id})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Clearly
\|v_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1
. Alice feeds these pairs to the sketching algorithm and sends the resulting sketch to Bob.

To recover bit
(i,j)
, Bob asks the query
q=\rho u_{i}
, whose norm is
\rho
. Write

w_{ih}=\exp(\rho\langle u_{i},u_{h}\rangle).

Then
w_{ii}=L
and
w_{ih}\leq e
for
h\neq i
. Let

B=\sum_{h=1}^{m}w_{ih}

be the softmax denominator for this query. The
j
th coordinate of the true attention, after multiplying by the public sign
b_{ij}
, is

b_{ij}\Attn(q,K,V)_{j}=\frac{Lx_{ij}/\sqrt{d}+N_{ij}}{B},\qquad N_{ij}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{h\neq i}w_{ih}b_{ij}b_{hj}x_{hj}.

The first term is the signal. The second term is noise from all other keys. Over the public signs,
\mathbb{E}N_{ij}=0
and
\mathbb{E}N_{ij}^{2}\leq e^{2}m/d
, so Chebyshev gives

\Pr\left[|N_{ij}|>L/(10\sqrt{d})\right]\leq 100e^{2}m/L^{2}.

Our choice of
m\leq cL^{2}
makes this probability a small constant.

On the event
|N_{ij}|\leq L/(10\sqrt{d})
, the two cases are separated by a gap: if
x_{ij}=1
then

b_{ij}\Attn(q,K,V)_{j}\geq\frac{9L}{10\sqrt{d}\,B},

whereas if
x_{ij}=0
then

\left|b_{ij}\Attn(q,K,V)_{j}\right|\leq\frac{L}{10\sqrt{d}\,B}.

Thus thresholding at
L/(2\sqrt{d}\,B)
recovers the bit as long as the sketch error in coordinate
j
is at most
L/(5\sqrt{d}\,B)
.

It remains to check that the promised
\ell_{2}
error gives such a coordinate bound for a random
Index
query. Conditioned on the sketch succeeding, if
(i,j)
is uniform in
[m]\times[d]
, then
j
is uniform in
[d]
, and Markov’s inequality gives

\Pr_{j}\left[\left(\Attn^{\prime}(q)_{j}-\Attn(q,K,V)_{j}\right)^{2}>(10\varepsilon)^{2}/d\right]\leq 1/100.

Finally, since
B\leq L+em
,
m\leq cL/\varepsilon
,
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, and
c,\varepsilon_{0}
are sufficiently small, we have
B\leq L/(50\varepsilon)
, and hence

10\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}\leq L/(5\sqrt{d}\,B).

Therefore Bob recovers a uniformly random bit of
x
with constant probability. A sketch of size
o(md)
would give a one-way protocol for
Index
on
md
bits using
o(md)
communication, contradicting the standard
\Omega(md)
lower bound. Hence
S=\Omega(md)
, which is the claimed bound.
∎

Theorem 7
.

Fix dimensions
d,d_{k}>1
,
\rho\geq 2
, let
L=e^{\rho}
, and let
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}/\sqrt{d}
for a sufficiently small absolute constant
\varepsilon_{0}
. Suppose there is a coreset algorithm that, for any input
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}},V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|k_{i}\|_{2},\|v_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1
, produces a subset
K^{\prime},V^{\prime}
of size
S
, so that for any
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
of norm at most
\rho
, with constant probability,

\|\Attn(q,K,V)-\Attn(q,K^{\prime},V^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon.

Then

S=\Omega\!\left(d\cdot\min\{L/(\varepsilon\sqrt{d}),\;L^{2},\;\exp(ad_{k}/\log^{2}L)\}\right).

In particular, it is enough to take
d_{k}=\Omega(\log^{2}L\cdot\log(L/(\varepsilon\sqrt{d})))
for the key dimension not to be the bottleneck.

Proof.

The reduction is again from one-way
Indexing
. By constant repetition we may assume the coreset succeeds with probability at least
0.99
. Set
\eta=1/\log L
. Since
\rho=\log L\geq 2
, we have
\eta\leq 1/2
. Let

m=\left\lfloor c\min\{L/(\varepsilon\sqrt{d}),\;L^{2},\;\exp(ad_{k}/\log^{2}L)\}\right\rfloor

for a sufficiently small absolute constant
c
, and consider an
Indexing
instance
x\in\{0,1\}^{md}
. Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
be the codewords from the coding fact. We identify a bit position with a pair
(i,j)\in[m]\times[d]
. Using public randomness, Alice and Bob draw signs
b_{ij}\in\{\pm 1\}
. Alice constructs
md
key-value pairs indexed
(i,j)\in[m]\times[d]
:

k_{i,j}=u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}},\qquad v_{i,j}=b_{ij}x_{ij}e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Clearly
\|v_{i,j}\|_{2}\leq 1
. Alice runs the coreset algorithm and sends the resulting coreset to Bob.

To recover bit
(i,j)
, Bob asks the query
q=\rho u_{i}
. As above, write
w_{ih}=\exp(\rho\langle u_{i},u_{h}\rangle)
, so
w_{ii}=L
and
w_{ih}\leq e
for
h\neq i
. The softmax denominator for the original instance is now

B=d\sum_{h=1}^{m}w_{ih},

because there are
d
pairs for each codeword. After multiplying coordinate
j
by the public sign
b_{ij}
, the true attention value is

b_{ij}\Attn(q,K,V)_{j}=\frac{Lx_{ij}+N_{ij}}{B},\qquad N_{ij}:=\sum_{h\neq i}w_{ih}b_{ij}b_{hj}x_{hj}.

Over the public signs,
\mathbb{E}N_{ij}=0
and
\mathbb{E}N_{ij}^{2}\leq e^{2}m
, so

\Pr\left[|N_{ij}|>L/10\right]\leq 100e^{2}m/L^{2}.

Since
m\leq cL^{2}
, this failure probability is a small constant.

On the event
|N_{ij}|\leq L/10
, the two bit values are separated: if
x_{ij}=1
then

b_{ij}\Attn(q,K,V)_{j}\geq\frac{9L}{10B},

whereas if
x_{ij}=0
then

\left|b_{ij}\Attn(q,K,V)_{j}\right|\leq\frac{L}{10B}.

Thus thresholding at
L/(2B)
recovers the bit provided the coreset approximation has coordinate error at most
L/(5B)
.

As before, the
\ell_{2}
approximation guarantee implies the desired coordinate guarantee for a random
Indexing
query. Conditioned on the coreset succeeding, if
(i,j)
is uniform in
[m]\times[d]
, then
j
is uniform in
[d]
, so Markov’s inequality gives

\Pr_{j}\left[\left|\Attn(q,K,V)_{j}-\Attn(q,K^{\prime},V^{\prime})_{j}\right|^{2}>(10\varepsilon)^{2}/d\right]\leq 1/100.

Finally, since
B\leq d(L+em)
,
m\leq cL/(\varepsilon\sqrt{d})
, and
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}/\sqrt{d}
, for
c,\varepsilon_{0}
sufficiently small,

B\leq\frac{L\sqrt{d}}{50\varepsilon},

and therefore

10\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}\leq L/(5B).

Bob can therefore recover a uniformly random bit of
x
with constant probability from the coreset. If the coreset had size
o(md)
, then Alice could send the indices of the selected pairs and corresponding
x_{i,j}
’s (using
o(md)
retained pairs of information), and Bob could run the above construction and the decoder. This contradicts the standard
\Omega(md)
lower bound for
Indexing
. Hence
S=\Omega(md)
, which is the claimed bound.
∎

7
Discussion

In this paper we prove the best possible coreset size is essentially
\tfrac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}e^{\zeta}
.
The upper bound is off by a factor of
O(e^{\frac{1}{2}\log(\rho)+O(\log\log(\rho))})=O(\rho^{1/2+o(1)})
.
To qualify how “nearly optimal" our results is, consider the following.
Based on the lower bound, coreset are only viable when
\zeta\leq\log(\varepsilon n/\sqrt{d})

because otherwise
c\sqrt{d}e^{\zeta}/\varepsilon>n
which mean our coreset is larger than the data.
So, in the worst case, the gap between the upper and lower bound is roughly
\sqrt{\log(n)}
.
We conjecture that closing this gap will require completely different approaches.
