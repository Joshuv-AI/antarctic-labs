Spoiler Alert: Narrative Forecasting as a Metric for Tension in LLM Storytelling
Peiqi Sui, Yutong Zhu , Tianyi Cheng, Peter West, Richard Jean So, Hoyt Long, Ari Holtzman

Abstract
LLMs have so far failed both to generate consistently compelling stories and to recognize this failure—on the leading creative-writing benchmark (EQ-Bench), LLM judges rank zero-shot AI stories above New Yorker short stories, a gold standard for literary fiction. We argue that existing rubrics overlook a key dimension of compelling human stories: narrative tension. We introduce the 100-Endings metric, which walks through a story sentence by sentence: at each position, a model predicts how the story will end 100 times given only the text so far, and we measure tension as how often predictions fail to match the ground truth. Beyond the mismatch rate, the sentence-level curve yields complementary statistics, such as inflection rate , a geometric measure of how frequently the curve reverses direction, tracking twists and revelations. Unlike rubric-based judges, 100-Endings correctly ranks New Yorker stories far above LLM outputs. Grounded in narratological principles, we design a story-generation pipeline using structural constraints, including analysis of story templates, idea formulation, and narrative scaffolding. Our pipeline significantly increases narrative tension as measured by the 100-Endings metric, while maintaining performance on the EQ-Bench leaderboard.

1
Introduction

Even the largest and strongest LLMs have trouble with creativity:
AI writers struggle to avoid clichés
(
Chakrabarty et al. 2024
)
, produce diverse plot and narrative arcs
(
Padmakumar & He 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
)
, or maintain long-range narrative coherence
(
Gurung & Lapata 2025
;
Pham et al. 2025
)
. This failure extends to evaluation, with strong, widely-adopted model judges rating professional human-written stories below low-quality AI generations (Section
3.2
). Increasing model scale does not resolve these shortcomings, which indicates that core aspects of compelling stories may not be naturally understood by zero-shot LLMs. We investigate how these attributes can be explicitly incorporated into current LLM evaluators and writers, focusing on
narrative tension
as a key signpost for quality in human-written stories.

On their own, LLMs regularly fail to recognize their lack of narrative tension in writing. Humans often note that LLM stories are flat and trite
(
Chakrabarty et al. 2025
)
, while model judges typically focus on other aspects of their writing. Most rubrics simply omit tension as an evaluation criterion, but even when it is included, model evaluators tend to reward surface markers of drama like urgent language or explicit emotional labels, rather than verifying that information is genuinely withheld across the narrative arc. More fundamentally, rubric-based evaluation assumes that story quality is an average over item scores, yet this is not how human readers experience fiction: readers do not consult a checklist; they read to be immersed, to feel uncertain and compelled, to arrive at the ending slightly transformed. This visceral sense of tension is immediate and holistic, and a key component of what makes a story worth reading—yet no rubric item, however well-worded, gives a model judge access to it. Reliable measurement therefore requires an approach that operationalizes tension structurally rather than delegating it to a judge’s proxy.

To address this gap, we introduce the
100-Endings
metric that directly targets narrative tension
by design
. Following insights from literary theory (Section
2.1
), we operationalize tension as unpredictability: after every sentence of a story, a model is asked to infer 100 possible endings, and we use their divergence from the ground truth ending as a measurement for tension.
100-Endings
flags predictable stories when the model successfully anticipates their endings, while stories with genuine tension would exhibit narrative recalcitrance that resists easy predictions. Unlike the baseline of standard evaluation practices, our
100-Endings
correctly identifies the high tension of high-quality stories from
The New Yorker
, widely regarded as a gold standard of contemporary literary fiction (Section
3
).

Using
100-Endings
as a north star, we investigate what needs to be injected into LLMs to write high-tension stories. At a high level, the answer seems to be the complexity of inputs and structural constraints. We design a generation pipeline grounded in literary theory that can take a flexible range of story ideas and transform them into tension-rich stories through a thick intermediate plan: a beat-level scaffold specifying narrative function, information withholding, and tension mechanics, derived from an analysis of professional fiction (Section
4
). This pipeline closes roughly half the gap to professional
New Yorker
fiction on our metric while achieving the top score on the EQ-Bench Creative Writing leaderboard (Section
5
). Our contributions are as follows:

1.

A new metric for narrative tension.
We introduce the
100-Endings Metric
, which operationalizes tension as unpredictability of story endings at the sentence level—capturing a crucial dimension of story quality missed by existing rubrics (Section
3.1
).

2.

Evidence that LLMs systematically cannot hold tension.
Using 100-Endings, we show that top LLMs produce stories with the lowest narrative tension of any corpus we evaluate, even as rubric-based judges rate them above professional human fiction (Section
3.2
).

3.

A structured pipeline improves tension.
We propose a story generation pipeline grounded in literary theory and show that structured constraints improve narrative tension (Section
5
).

This work highlights the need for deliberate, literary theory-driven solutions for LLM creativity: scale alone has failed to capture fundamental narrative elements such as tension, while explicitly modeling it creates significant and demonstrable improvements. Tension is by no means the only missing element in LLM writers, and explicit, narratology-driven work will be needed to map out and implement the full space of key narrative elements needed for true narrative machines.

2
Related Work

2.1
Literary and Narrative Theories of Tension.

Narratology has long considered tension, an affective state driven by information asymmetry and delayed resolution, as a key component of storytelling. Tension is the direct result of the structural divide between chronological events of a story (
fabula
) and reconfigured order in which they are disclosed to the reader (
syuzhet
)
(
Propp 1928
)
. Maintaining this divide requires prolonged restricted focalization, establishing specific perspectives that deliberately limit what is known to the reader at any given moment
(
Genette 1980
)
. Tension operates through the active management of narrative interest and expectations, such as suspense generated by forward-looking uncertainty about unresolved outcomes
(
Sternberg 2003
)
. Structurally, tension is maintained through open, high-stakes questions posed early in the narrative that remain unanswered until the ending, forming causal chains whose strength depends on the viability of competing possible answers
(
Carroll 1990
)
—conversely, if the resolution is disclosed prematurely, this question-and-answer dynamic collapses and tension vanishes. As an affect, tension integrates uncertainty with anticipation, which requires compelling plots to continuously deviate from the expected norm to generate “eventfulness”
(
Baroni 2007
)
.

These narratological formulations find empirical support in cognitive science: narrative structure reliably produces distinct affective states by temporally separating initiating events from outcomes, forcing readers into sustained predictive inference
(
Brewer & Lichtenstein 1982
;
Tobin 2018
)
; the greater the divergence between possible fictional outcomes, the higher the reader’s experience of tension
(
Lehne et al. 2015
)
. Beyond the literary domain, this friction is shown to have broader social affordances as a reaction to cognitive dissonance
(
Lehne & Koelsch 2015
)
, where tension is more generally formalized as the variance in beliefs about future outcomes
(
Ely et al. 2015
)
.

Computational approaches to suspense have a long history but have not produced a generalizable, automated metric for evaluating tension during a story’s unfolding.
O’Neill & Riedl 2014
quantify suspense by counting a protagonist’s available escape plans and success probabilities, but require a symbolic planner with hand-engineered action spaces.
Wilmot & Keller 2020
model suspense as uncertainty reduction over neural story representations, correlating strongly with human ratings but requiring human annotations. More recently,
Tian et al. 2024
proxy tension via word-level arousal curves,
Xie & Riedl 2024
use human pairwise judgments, and
Matlin et al. 2025
replicate psychology experiments showing LLMs cannot reliably track suspense. Building on these accounts, we formalize narrative tension (Section
3.1
) and operationalize it through
predictability of endings
—a structural prerequisite for tension rather than a proxy of emotional vocabulary or self-reported ratings.

2.2
Evaluation of LLM Creative Writing.

Scalable evaluation of LLM-generated fiction has largely converged on the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm
(
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2024
)
, utilizing frontier models to score outputs against multi-criteria rubrics. The widely adopted EQ-Bench
(
Paech 2023
)
, for example, evaluates 22 criteria like prose quality and character authenticity. While these rubrics effectively separate polished prose from careless writing, they struggle to capture deep structural mechanics that are representative of more nuanced aspects of literary quality
(
Chakrabarty et al. 2024
;
Feuer et al. 2025
)
. Crucially, direct measures of narrative tension are absent from EQ-Bench and similar frameworks. This gap is further exacerbated by the self-preference bias of LLM judges, which favor the resolution-oriented, affectively flat styles that aligned models tend to produce
(
Panickssery et al. 2024
)
.

Beyond rubric-based evaluation, automated metrics for narrative quality have progressed from reference-dependent overlap measures, which are largely inapplicable to open-ended creative generation
(
Guan & Huang 2020
;
Chhun et al. 2022
)
, toward structural approaches like coherence-oriented frameworks
(
Wang et al. 2025
;
Yi et al. 2025
)
or information-theoretic methods that quantify the predictability gap between human and LLM writing
(
Xu et al. 2025
;
Sui 2026
)
. However, these metrics evaluate predictability as an artifact of raw continuation, capturing whether the immediate next sentence is surprising but not whether the story’s ultimate resolution remains uncertain throughout; a story may exhibit high local novelty on the sentence or paragraph-level while revealing its ending very early on, producing superficial surprise without sustained tension. Our
100-Endings
metric targets this gap, shifting the unit of evaluation from immediate next-step continuation to the story’s resolution and measuring forecasting difficulty at multiple positions across the full narrative arc.

The
100-Endings
Metric

Example: “Snowing in Greenwich Village”

Richard walks Rebecca home through the snow. She invites him up to her tiny apartment under a slanting roof. He sees the bed, the stove on the fridge; they look out the window together…

Sentence 198 (91.2% revealed):

✓ Matched:

“The quiet between them thickened, neither stepping forward nor back, and just as the snow seemed to settle over the city like a layer of pure silence, Richard reached out—not to embrace or undo, but merely to touch the curtain, letting the folds fall gently back into place between them.”

✗ Diverged:

“They stood a moment longer in the windowed silence of her small room, snow falling beyond the pane, and then Richard reached out, gently, and pulled her to him. She did not resist, but neither did she respond.”

We introduce the
100-Endings
metric (Figure
2
), which measures how predictable a story’s ending is at each point in its progression. At every sentence boundary, we prompt a language model to predict how the story will end given only the text so far, then compare those predictions against the held-out ground truth ending. The core intuition is that tension is operationally linked to predictability: a story that sustains information asymmetry, keeps possibilities open, and delays resolution should resist easy prediction and allow divergent trajectories that depart from the actual ending; a story that lacks tension will be easy to anticipate even from partial text. Unlike adjacent approaches that measure the predictability of the immediate next sentence
(
Xu et al. 2025
;
Sui 2026
)
, our metric evaluates the forward-looking narrative trajectory with a focus on how the story ends.

3.1
Evaluation Procedure

Ending Generation.

Given a story of
N
sentences, we evaluate sentence ending positions
P
spanning 10%--99% of the text of the story.
1
1

1

Positions below 10% provide too little context for meaningful prediction; sentences under 10 words are concatenated to the next.
At each position
i\in P
, we provide sentences
1\ldots i
as context to prompt a generation model (
Qwen3-32B
,
T=1.2
) to bypass intermediate plot developments and explicitly produce 100 candidate endings.

LLM-as-a-Judge Ending Evaluations.

A separate judge model (
Qwen3-8B
,
T=0
) then evaluates each of the 100 generated endings against the entirety of the held-out ground truth (sentences
i{+}1\ldots N
of the original story). For each ending, the judge determines whether the plot, events, or actions of the generated ending overlap with the actual ending, following a strict standard where vague thematic similarity is not sufficient.

The judge returns a binary verdict (Y/N) for all 100 endings in a single pass. We define the
no-rate
at position
i
as the fraction of 100
endings that do
not
match the actual continuation:

\text{no-rate}(i)=\frac{1}{100}\sum_{j=1}^{100}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,\text{ending}_{j}\text{ does not match true
continuation}\,\right]

(1)

A no-rate of
0.85
means that 85 of 100 generated endings fail to predict what actually happens. In other words, the story up to this point is highly unpredictable.

Mean no-rate.

We compute the story-level no-rate by averaging across all evaluated
positions, then report the average across all stories in a group:

\overline{\text{no-rate}}=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s\in S}\left(\frac{1}{|P_{s}|}\sum_{i\in P_{s}}\text{no-rate}_{s}(i)\right)

(2)

where
S
is the set of stories and
P_{s}
is the set of evaluated positions in story
s
. Mean no-rate is our primary metric throughout this paper.

Inflection rate.

Because no-rate is computed at every sentence boundary, it also produces a
curve
over the arc of the story. A complementary statistic is the
inflection rate
: the fraction of evaluated positions at which the smoothed no-rate curve sharply reverses direction. Because the vertex angle is a geometric measurement, it depends on the aspect ratio of the coordinate space. Before computing angles, we rescale each story’s curve onto a unit square: the
x
-axis (% story revealed) is mapped to
[0,1]
using the story’s position range, and the
y
-axis (smoothed no-rate) is independently mapped to
[0,1]
using the story’s own min and max. This fixes a
1{:}1
aspect ratio for angle computation, ensuring thresholds are comparable across stories of different lengths and no-rate ranges. We report results at three thresholds (
30°
,
60°
,
120°
):

\text{inflection-rate}_{\alpha}(s)=\frac{|\{i:\theta_{i}\leq\alpha\}|}{|P_{s}|}

(3)

While mean no-rate summarizes overall unpredictability, inflection rate tracks narrative manipulation—the frequency with which a story reverses reader expectations. Figure
3
illustrates this with two
New Yorker
stories. Petrushevskaya’s “Poor Girl”, a realist story with a clinical and distanced exteriority, has high mean no-rate (
0.925
) but low inflection rate (
0.048
). “The Fellow” (Joy Williams), a surrealist story in which a guest at a writers’ residency vanishes in a flood and a dog begins to speak, has low mean no-rate (
0.403
) because the plot is minimal, but very high inflection rate (
0.339
): each small revelation briefly makes the ending guessable before the next twist displaces it. Whether these two statistics capture genuinely distinct dimensions of craft is an open question, but the contrast suggests that the no-rate curve contains information beyond its mean.

Post-spike retention.

For each local peak in the no-rate curve (a position exceeding both neighbors), we measure how much of the peak value is retained at the minimum within the next 10 positions. The story-level
retention
is the mean across all peaks. Higher retention means tension peaks are sustained rather than immediately resolved.

3.2
Why a New Metric?

EQ-Bench Creative Writing v3
(
Paech 2023
)
is one of the most widely used benchmark for evaluating LLM-generated fiction. Its rubric scores stories on 22 criteria (e.g., adherence to instructions, prose quality, emotional complexity, etc.) using Claude Sonnet 4 as judge, producing a composite score on a 0--100 scale. Notably, none of the 22 directly evaluate for tension.
2
2

2

While EQ-Bench also employs an Elo rating system based on pairwise matchups, we evaluate only the rubric scores. The leaderboard recently transitioned to Sonnet 4 as a judge (previously Sonnet-3.7). Examination of the repository indicates that while rubric scores have been updated, most Elo scores have not, making pairwise comparisons inconsistent. Furthermore, because the Elo ranking criteria closely mirror the initial rubric and entirely omit narrative tension, the same evaluative limitations apply to both.

New Yorker

It is implausible that a single-pass LLM could genuinely surpass this level of craft; if taken at face value, it would mean the craft of literary fiction has been solved—a claim directly contradicted by various human validation results from recent NLP research in this area
(

;
Ismayilzada et al. 2025

. The more likely explanation is that the rubric misses something important, and EQ-Bench rewards dimensions where LLMs already excel (e.g., prose fluency, structural completeness, and instruction-following), while omitting narrative tension, the dimension where professional fiction most clearly surpasses LLM output.

Our
100-Endings
metric provides confirmation of our central hypothesis: LLMs cannot hold tension.
100-Endings
orders the five groups consistently with intuition:
New Yorker
(0.765) exceeds EQ-Bench’s top-10 LLMs (0.63) by
+
0.14, and all three amateur corpora fall in between (Table
1
). The gap is especially stark in late-stage unpredictability:
New Yorker
stories maintain a late-stage no-rate of 0.607, nearly triple that of LLMs (0.215), which means that professional fiction preserves narrative tension even as it approaches its ending while LLM stories become predictable well before they conclude. Post-spike retention reinforces this:
New Yorker
stories retain 52% of local peaks on average, while LLMs retain only 23%. Professional authors raise narrative questions and leave them partially open; LLMs resolve them almost immediately. We argue that this is the missing dimension in EQ-Bench—the sustained management of what the reader does and does not yet know.

4
Pipeline

Story Idea Primer.

The pipeline takes a story idea of any length as its sole input. These “seed” ideas can be user-supplied, drawn from existing benchmarks (e.g., the 32 genre-specific prompts from EQ-Bench that we use in Section
5
), or produced by a custom LLM-based idea generation module that might combine genre profiles, tension-rich narrative tropes, or other mechanisms for combinatorial creativity to automatically produce diverse premises (see Appendix
D.3
).

Given a story idea, the pipeline (Figure
1
) produces a full story in three sequential steps, threading a shared context through each stage so that every step can condition on all prior outputs.

Figure 4:
Effect of our narrative pipeline on rubric scores and tension metrics (32 EQ-Bench prompts). Left: EQ-Bench rubric leaderboard with our pipeline at #1 (85.03). Right:
100
-Ending
on the same 32 prompts. The pipeline improves tension over vanilla models.

Step 0: Warmup Analysis.

We randomly select a reference story from one of two sources: full-text stories from our
New Yorker
dataset, or a curated pool of canonical short stories. For the latter, we provide only the title to leverage the model’s parametric knowledge of the story. An LLM (Claude Sonnet,
T{=}0.7
) reads (or recalls) the reference story and extracts 7 scene-level structural beats, each annotated with (1) the concrete action, (2) the specific narrative technique employed, and (3) what made it memorable. This step serves as a “warmup link,” priming the model with the structural logic of high-quality literary fiction before it encounters the target story idea.

Step 1: Beat Adaptation (The Thick Intermediate Layer).

The extracted beat sheet template is adapted to the target story idea. The LLM produces a detailed intermediate plan, a structural “to-do list” that specifies the following for each beat: the narrative function, what information is revealed or withheld, the tension mechanism in play, and how stakes escalate. This intermediate representation is deliberately
thick
: it carries enough structural information to constrain the generated story away from default narrative templates while leaving stylistic-level decisions to the next step. When the warmup step is ablated, beats are generated from scratch, prompted with the story idea alone.

Step 2: Story Generation.

The LLM generates the final story conditioned on the story idea and the thick intermediate plan from Step 1. The model is instructed to follow the structural to-do list closely, with a system-prompt emphasis on sustaining tension. When both planning steps are ablated, the pipeline reduces to single-shot (vanilla) generation, providing a controlled baseline.

Results

3

4
) to three frontier models (Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.6, and GPT-5.2) across the standard 32 EQ-Bench prompts, and evaluate the outputs on both the EQ-Bench rubric and
100-Endings
.

6.1
Case Study: What is the Shape of Stories With Tension?

The central finding of this work is that zero-shot LLMs tend to collapse narrative tension through premature resolution, a shortcoming that a thick, structured generation pipeline can mitigate. To illustrate the problem and the remedy, we examine the tension curves of three romance stories that share a single source of suspense: the unspoken chemistry between a man and a woman alone together. One is from our
New Yorker
dataset, while the other two are generated by
Claude Sonnet 4.6
from identical prompts—one zero-shot, and one using structured pipeline. Because the genre, subject, and emotional register are held constant, the resulting differences in the no-rate curves isolate how effectively each story manages the gap between possibility and resolution.

The New Yorker
.

In Updike’s “Snowing in Greenwich Village” (1956), a husband walks an attractive female guest home through the snow while his wife stays behind with a cold. The moment introduces immediate stakes: will the underlying attraction culminate in an affair, or will nothing happen? As Figure
5
illustrates, the no-rate spikes once the pair is alone and stays elevated throughout their ambiguous parting at her door. Although the affair never materializes, the narrative sustains the possibility of transgression into the final sentence. Updike maintains the tension to the very end by never allowing the narrative to prematurely settle; the story remains suspended between seduction and restraint, making ambiguity the focal point.

The zero-shot failure.

Figure
6
(left) illustrates the typical failure mode of an unguided LLM attempting a comparable romance, which exhibits a decisive turning point at 64% progression. Up to that mark, the banter sustains uncertainty as the owner initially rebuffs the actor. However, the moment she invites him to the back room for tea and acknowledges his honesty, their mutual attraction is explicitly confirmed. With the romantic trajectory resolved, the no-rate drops to
0.29
and completely flatlines by the final sentences. Unlike Updike, the vanilla model prematurely forecloses the narrative pathway and collapses tension the very moment the characters are alone together.

The pipeline recovery.

Figure
6
(right) shows our pipeline’s version of the same prompt. The interaction is driven entirely by subtext, as the characters part without ever acknowledging their underlying chemistry after exchanging only oblique vulnerabilities. This deliberate ambiguity culminates in the story’s conclusion, as the distracted owner corrects her ledger: “She sat with the mistake for a moment before she fixed it.” Here,
mistake
and
fixed
operate on two levels, referring simultaneously to a mundane accounting error and the unacted-upon romantic spark. Consequently, the no-rate remains above 0.85 through 85% of the story. Like Updike, our pipeline sustains the tension between attraction without resolving into either acceptance or rejection. This highlights that the zero-shot failure is not an issue of prose fluency (both LLM versions are fluent), but a fundamental inability to
defer closure
—a deficit our pipeline avoids by design through thick planning.

6.2
What
100-Endings
Measures

100-Endings
is a measure of predictability, which we argue is a necessary (but not sufficient) component of tension. A story can be unpredictable and incoherent, since unpredictability without stakes, investment, or logic is simply chaos. The metric should therefore be used alongside mainstream benchmarks of generic writing quality. Yet, the metric’s ability to distinguish professional fiction from LLM outputs (unlike EQ-Bench) suggests it captures an essential element of narrative craft: the deliberate management of reader expectations through information asymmetry, delayed resolution, and structural surprise.

While our four statistics (Section
3
) primarily evaluate
plot-level
predictability, the generated endings offer a rich artifact for further analysis. Future work could develop metrics analyzing the
style
of these endings to capture sub-event forms of tension, such as thematic irony and psychological interiority.

The metric’s reliance on LLMs as generator raises a circularity concern: are we measuring the properties of the stories, or the idiosyncratic preferences of the judge? We mitigate this by strictly separating the generator and evaluator models (employing
Qwen3
exclusively for evaluation) and by validating that the metric correctly orders human corpora of known quality (details see Appendix
A
).

The Gap to Professional Fiction

Our best pipeline configuration achieves a mean no-rate of
0.702
on the EQ-Bench prompts, closing roughly half the gap between vanilla Sonnet 4.6 (
0.577
) and
New Yorker
fiction (
0.765
), but still below. The remaining gap likely reflects aspects of professional craft that our pipeline does not yet address: tacit knowledge based on cultural contexts, subversion of genre through deep familiarity with their conventions, and the kind of sentence-level surprise that emerges from a distinctive authorial voice rather than structural planning.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

Zero-shot LLMs struggle to hold narrative tension, resolving uncertainty prematurely rather than sustaining the gap between reader knowledge and narrative withholding. Because tension is downstream of hidden information—secrets, delayed revelations, chilling endings—this tendency to prematurely reveal produces stories that are polished but flat. The
100-Endings
metric makes this deficit measurable, operationalizing tension as unpredictability of endings at the sentence level. Our results reveal that stories generated by top LLMs exhibit the lowest tension of any evaluated corpus, even when rubric-based judges rate them above professional fiction. However, our structured generation pipeline demonstrates that this deficit is not immutable: literary theory-driven constraints can significantly improve long-range tension. While tension is a vital aspect of the craft of storytelling, it is only one element of the broader space of key narrative attributes required to build true narrative machines.

This paper provides a framework for quantifying and improving narrative tension, and represents an initial step toward a comprehensive computational narratology
(
Piper et al. 2021
)
that opens up several interesting directions: our immediate subsequent work will expand on various components of our pipeline, particularly the generation of synthetic story primers at scale; we also plan to conduct systematic ablations across every stage of the pipeline to isolate which structural constraints contribute most to tension—specifically, whether the key factor is the thickness of intermediate steps or the combinatorial novelty of the constraints themselves; aside from measuring tension, the
100-Endings
framework also produces a rich object (100 candidate endings at every sentence boundary) from which further metrics could be derived, particularly literary theory-driven measures of stylistic or thematic aspects of quality that operate below the level of overall tension.

Beyond the pipeline, tension is only one dimension of narrative craft. Good stories do not emerge from a vacuum, and our future work aims to explore the tacit knowledge and contextual frameworks behind narrative artifacts like tension—if multi-agent architectures can simulate the editorial processes, social dynamics, and cultural ecosystems from which high-quality literature originates. Modeling these environments may prove essential for closing the remaining gap to professional fiction.

Appendix C
The 100-Ending Metric

This section formalizes the 100-ending metric and provides complete implementation details, including exact prompt templates and all hyperparameters.

C.1
Overview

The 100-ending metric measures
narrative predictability
at the sentence level. The intuition is simple: at each point in a story, we ask a language model to predict how the story ends, repeating this 100 times, then check how many predictions match what actually happens. If the true ending is easy to predict, most predictions will match; if it is surprising, few will.

Crucially, the model is asked to
describe
how the story ends—a plot-level prediction—rather than to write creative prose. This frames the task as forecasting rather than generation, measuring whether the model can anticipate the narrative trajectory.

Formally, let
S=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{N})
be a story decomposed into
N
sentences. At each sentence boundary
i\in\{1,\ldots,N{-}1\}
, we:

1.

Predict
100 independent ending predictions
\hat{e}_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,\hat{e}_{100}^{(i)}
conditioned on the prefix
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{i})
, using a generation model
\mathcal{G}
.

2.

Judge
each prediction against the true remainder
(s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{N})
using a judge model
\mathcal{J}
, which returns a binary verdict
j_{k}^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}
indicating whether
\hat{e}_{k}^{(i)}
captures the “general direction” of the true continuation.

3.

Aggregate
into a position-level
no-rate
:

\text{no-rate}(i)=1-\frac{1}{100}\sum_{k=1}^{100}j_{k}^{(i)}

(4)

A no-rate of 0.85 means 85 of 100 predicted endings fail to match what actually happens—the story is highly unpredictable at that point. The
story-level no-rate
is the mean across all evaluated positions:

\overline{\text{no-rate}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}}\text{no-rate}(i)

(5)

where
\mathcal{P}
is the set of retained positions (see Section
C.3
).

C.2
Sentence Splitting

Stories are split into sentences using a rule-based boundary detector. The algorithm identifies boundaries where terminal punctuation (
.
,
!
,
?
), optionally followed by closing quotation marks or parentheses, is followed by whitespace and then an uppercase letter or opening punctuation. The regex pattern is:

[.!?][""\u2019)]*\s+(?=[A-Z\u201c"(\[])

This heuristic correctly handles most English prose, including quoted dialogue and parenthetical asides. Both the generation and judge pipelines use an identical implementation of this splitter.

C.3
Position Selection

Not all sentence boundaries are evaluated. We apply two filters:

1.

Minimum context
: Position
i
is skipped if the preceding sentence
s_{i-1}
contains fewer than 10 words (
MIN_SENT_WORDS = 10
). This excludes positions following very short sentences (e.g., single-line dialogue tags) that provide minimal narrative context.

2.

Token percentage window
: We compute the fraction of story tokens revealed at each position using the tokenizer of the generation model. Only positions where 10%–99% of tokens have been revealed are retained. The lower bound excludes positions with insufficient context; the upper bound excludes trivial positions near the final sentence.

Stories exceeding 5,000 words are excluded entirely to ensure the full context fits within the generation model’s context window at every position.

C.4
Stage 1: Ending Generation

Model.

We use
Qwen3-32B
as the generation model
\mathcal{G}
, served via vLLM with offline batch inference on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU. The vLLM engine is configured with
dtype=bfloat16
,
max_model_len=8192
,
enable_prefix_caching=True
, and
gpu_memory_utilization=0.92
. The model’s chain-of-thought mode is disabled (
enable_thinking=False
).

Prompt template.

At each retained position
i
, we construct a chat-formatted prompt with a system message and user message:

System:

You are a fiction writer. Write only story text.

User:

In 2--3 sentences, describe how you think this story ends. No commentary, no explanation, no preamble.

STORY SO FAR:

{
s_{1}\;\|\;s_{2}\;\|\;\cdots\;\|\;s_{i}
}

where
\|
denotes string concatenation with a space separator. The prompt asks the model to
describe
the ending rather than write it as prose. This frames the task as plot-level forecasting: the model predicts what events occur, not how they would be narrated.

Both the actual continuation and the predicted ending are prefixed with the same

The judge’s output is parsed by checking whether the first character (after stripping whitespace and uppercasing) is

j_{k}=0

We judge each prediction independently rather than presenting all 100 in a single prompt for two reasons: (1) context length—100 predictions plus the actual continuation would exceed most context windows; (2) independence—presenting multiple predictions in a single prompt introduces ordering effects and cross-contamination between judgments.

C.6
Aggregation and Reporting

The 100-ending framework produces a position-level no-rate curve for each story. We derive four story-level statistics from this curve.

1. Mean no-rate.

The primary metric. At each position
i
, the no-rate is the fraction of 100 predictions the judge rejects:

\text{no-rate}(i)=1-\frac{1}{100}\sum_{k=1}^{100}j_{k}^{(i)}

(6)

The story-level mean no-rate averages across all retained positions:

\overline{\text{no-rate}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}}\text{no-rate}(i),\qquad\mathcal{P}=\{i:10\%\leq\text{pct}(i)\leq 99\%\}

(7)

2. Late-stage no-rate.

The mean no-rate restricted to positions where
\geq
80% of the story has been revealed. This measures whether the story remains unpredictable as it approaches its ending, or whether the conclusion becomes obvious once most of the narrative has been read.

3. Post-spike convergence.

For each local peak in the no-rate curve (a position where no-rate exceeds both neighbors), we compute the percentage drop from the peak to the minimum within the next 10 positions. The story-level metric is the mean of these drops across all peaks. More negative values indicate that tension peaks resolve more sharply—the story answers narrative questions decisively after raising them.

4. Inflection rate.

The fraction of positions at which the smoothed no-rate curve sharply reverses direction, as described in Section
3
. Before computing vertex angles, both axes are rescaled to
[0,1]
(using each story’s own range) to fix a 1:1 aspect ratio. We report at three thresholds:
\alpha\in\{30°,60°,120°\}
.

Group-level reporting.

For a group of
n
stories, we report the mean of story-level values. Confidence intervals are
\bar{x}\pm 1.96\cdot\sigma/\sqrt{n}
.

Appendix D
Pipeline Details

D.1
Pipeline Architecture

The narrative generation pipeline consists of up to three stages, each independently ablatable:

1.

Step 0 — Warmup
: Extract a structural beat sheet from a reference story (either a famous title or a full New Yorker story text).

2.

Step 1 — Beat Adaptation
: Adapt the warmup beat sheet to the target story idea. When warmup is absent, generates beats from scratch.

3.

Step 2 — Story Writing
: Write the final story from the adapted beats, or directly from the idea in vanilla mode.

All API calls use the OpenRouter endpoint. Reasoning/thinking is disabled for all calls. Stories shorter than 200 characters are automatically discarded and regenerated.

D.3
Pipeline Input

Our structured generation pipeline is designed to take an initial “seed” idea and autonomously handle all subsequent structural planning and narrative execution. This seed input can be highly flexible in length. The pipeline accommodates anything from a single-sentence premise to a detailed outline. For readers and researchers interested in utilizing or adapting this pipeline, seed ideas typically originate from one of three sources:

•

Existing Benchmark Prompts:
Prompts from established writing benchmarks, such as the EQ-Bench prompts utilized in our evaluation. They provide a baseline for comparative analysis.

•

User-Generated Ideas:
Human authors can supply their own story premises. While this allows for maximum creative control, the qualitative depth and structural viability of the user’s initial input will naturally impact the quality of the pipeline’s final output—a variable that remains difficult to control or standardize in evaluations.

•

Synthetically Generated Ideas:
Seed premises can also be generated by LLMs using structured, theory-driven constraints. For instance, to automate genre blending, an LLM can be prompted with a combinatorial profile sampled from the
Screenwriters Taxonomy
(e.g., macro-genre, voice, narrative pathway, and POV) alongside narrative building blocks curated from a extensive list of tropes (such as
TV Tropes
). This approach reliably produce structurally sound, 3–4 sentence “pitch”-like ideas that lean into unusual combinatorial spaces. While this approach can yield diverse premises, finding the optimal configuration for AI-driven seed generation remains an open question for future work.

F.2
Vanilla Zero-Shot Generation (Claude Sonnet 4.6)

The bell above the door didn’t so much ring as shriek, and Meredith looked up from her inventory sheet to find a man pressed flat against the glass, peering through the gap in the window display like a hunted animal checking for predators.

He was, she noticed with some irritation, absurdly handsome.

“We’re open,” she said. “If that’s what you’re wondering.”

He turned. The face resolved itself into something she recognised from cinema screens and magazine covers — Callum Drake, whose last film had apparently broken three box office records and whose jaw could, according to a review she’d skimmed, “stop traffic on the M4.” He looked at her with the particular expression of someone accustomed to being recognised, waiting for the machinery of that recognition to produce its usual effect.

She looked back down at her inventory sheet.

“Brilliant,” he said, moving away from the door. He was breathing slightly hard. “I’ll just — browse, shall I?”

“That is generally what one does in a bookshop.”

He laughed — a short, surprised sound. “Right. Yes.”

She could feel him hovering. She added three copies of a Patrick O’Brian to the spreadsheet.

“There are about six of them out there,” he said, from somewhere near Military History. “Photographers. Someone must have tipped them off I was in Abergavenny.”

“Mm.”

“It’s a nice town.”

“I know. I live here.”

Another pause. She heard him actually pull a book from the shelf, which she hadn’t expected.

“
The Worst Journey in the World
,” he read aloud. “Cheerful.”

“Cherry-Garrard. Antarctic expedition. Three men walked into the polar winter in complete darkness to collect penguin eggs for science.” She set down her pen. “Two of them nearly died. All three of them suffered what we’d now recognise as severe psychological trauma. Cherry-Garrard spent the rest of his life wondering if he could have saved Scott.”

Callum Drake looked at the book, then at her. “You’re quite intense for a Tuesday.”

“It’s Wednesday.”

He smiled then — the smile, she suspected, the one that had its own fan accounts. It was genuinely devastating, which annoyed her considerably.

“I’m Callum,” he said.

“I know who you are.”

“And you are?”

“The person who owns this shop.”

He set the book down and leaned against the shelf with the ease of someone who had never once worried about taking up space. “Come on. I’m hiding from the paparazzi in your establishment. The least you could do is tell me your name.”

“Meredith.” She picked up her pen again. “And I’d argue the least I could do is nothing at all, which is what I was doing before you came in.”

“Meredith.” He said it as though testing the weight of it. “Do you treat all your customers like this?”

“Only the ones who use my shop as a bolt-hole without asking.”

“Would you have said yes if I’d asked?”

She considered this honestly. “Probably. But you didn’t.”

He had the grace to look slightly abashed, which she hadn’t anticipated. The charm, she was realising, had a kind of autopilot quality — it ran continuously, like background software — but underneath it something else flickered when it failed to land.

“Fair point,” he said quietly.

She softened, fractionally, against her better judgement. “There’s tea in the back. If you want to wait properly.”

He followed her through the curtain into the small room behind the counter — two chairs, an electric kettle, towers of unshelved stock. She filled the kettle without looking at him.

“Do you ever get tired of it?” she asked. “People recognising you everywhere.”

“Tired of it?” He sat down in one of the chairs. “God, no. I’d be terrified if they stopped.” He said it lightly, but the lightness had a careful quality, like a lid on something.

She turned to look at him properly for the first time. “That’s an honest answer.”

“Don’t tell anyone. It’ll ruin the mystique.” He looked at his hands. “My dad was an actor. Fringe theatre, mostly — never made it. I used to watch him at the dinner table, just
performing
, even at home. Telling stories, making everyone laugh. And I thought that was just — that was just who he was.” He stopped. “Took me until I was about thirty to understand he was terrified of the room going quiet. That if nobody was watching, he didn’t quite know what he was.”

The kettle began to build toward boiling.

“And you?” Meredith said.

“Me?”

“Are you terrified of the room going quiet?”

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, distantly, she heard a car pass on the high street.

“I haven’t let it go quiet long enough to find out,” he said.

She poured the water. She was very aware, suddenly, of the smallness of the room, the particular quality of the afternoon light coming through the single high window, the fact that her hands were not entirely steady.

“Milk?” she asked.

“Please.” Then: “You’re not going to tell me I’m brilliant and it must be so wonderful, are you.”

“No.”

“Most people do.”

“I’m sure they do.”

“It’s—” He stopped again. Started differently. “It’s quite refreshing, actually. Talking to someone who’s not—”

“Performing back at you?”

He looked startled. Then he laughed — not the polished one, but something rougher and more real. “Yeah,” he said. “Exactly that.”

She handed him his tea and sat down in the opposite chair with her own. Through the curtain, the shop waited — its shelves of difficult journeys and long winters and people who had nearly not survived the thing they’d chosen.

“The photographers will probably give up in an hour,” she said.

“Probably,” he agreed.

Neither of them suggested he leave sooner than that.

F.3
Narrative Pipeline Generation (Claude Sonnet 4.6)

The bell didn’t ring; it clattered, a harsh, broken sound that made Elara look up from her ledger.

The man who shoved his way inside was soaked, his trench coat plastered to his shoulders. He threw his weight against the door, pressing his forehead against the frosted glass. Outside, the flash of cameras strobed through the rain, turning the quiet Welsh street into a silent, frantic disco.

“Sorry,” the man said, breathless.

“We’re closed.”

“Just five minutes.” He didn’t turn around. He was still watching the street, his shoulders tense.

Elara didn’t move from the counter. She watched a drop of water fall from the hem of his coat and soak into the threadbare rug. “You’re dripping on the folios.”

He finally turned. The ambient light from the streetlamp caught his face. It was a face built for fifty-foot screens, all sharp angles and calculated symmetry, currently ruined by exhaustion and the frantic energy of a hunted animal. He reached up to push his wet hair back and stopped.

His fingers came away red. He stared at the blood, then touched a scratch on his cheek, right below his eye.

“It’s raining,” he said, as if that explained everything.

“I hadn’t noticed.”

He let out a short, hollow laugh and wiped the blood on his coat sleeve. He started to pace the narrow aisle, running a hand over the spines of the books. His movements were too large for the space, too dramatic, as if he were trying to hit a mark on a stage.

“Do you have any poetry? Contemporary?”

“Second aisle, back wall.”

He stopped pacing and pulled a slim volume from the shelf. He didn’t open it. He just held it, staring at the cover.

“I don’t read poetry,” he said to the spine of a Doris Lessing novel. “But someone told me I should start.”

“Usually people start with the classics.”

“I don’t have time for the classics.”

“I don’t think they mind.”

He looked at her then, really looked at her. The manic energy drained out of him, leaving something brittle in its place.

“Do you know what it’s like,” he said, “to spend six months pretending to be someone else, and then when you stop, you aren’t sure if you exist when no one is watching?”

The silence stretched. Outside, the flashes stopped. The paparazzi were moving on.

Elara closed her ledger. She picked up a pen and began to trace the grain of the wood on the counter.

“I had a cat,” she said. “A ginger tom. He died three years ago. Sometimes I still step over the place where he used to sleep on the rug.”

He didn’t move. He didn’t say anything. He just watched her trace the wood grain.

“Did you get another cat?”

“No.”

He nodded, slowly. He walked to the counter and set the book down. “I’ll take the poetry.”

“That’s £4.50.”

He pulled a damp ten-pound note from his pocket and laid it on the wood. “Keep the change.”

“We don’t do tips.” She slid the change across the counter.

He didn’t take it immediately. His hand hovered over the coins, close to hers. The air in the shop felt suddenly thin. Then he scooped the money up, put the book in his pocket, and walked out into the rain.

Elara watched him go. She picked up her pen and opened the ledger. She wrote the sale in the wrong column, putting the £4.50 under expenses instead of income.

She sat with the mistake for a moment before she fixed it.
