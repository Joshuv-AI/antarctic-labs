A Characterization of the Cumulants as Continuous Moment-Based Statistics
Sofia de la Cerda

Abstract
Cumulants are classical statistics associated with a random variable, defined as polynomial functions of its moments and distinguished by their additivity under convolution of distributions. A statistic is the name given to a function of a random variable, and a moment-based statistic is one that depends only on the moments ( 𝔼 ⁡ [ X n ] ) n ∈ ℕ (\mathbb{E}[X^{n}])_{n\in\mathbb{N}} . We prove a converse: any statistic depending continuously on finitely many moments and additive for independent sums must be a linear combination of cumulants. The proof uses an algebraic reformulation of the problem via the Hurwitz product and a linearizing change of coordinates. This result also follows from the more general theorem of Mattner [ 4 ] , but our approach is elementary and self-contained.



1
Introduction

The cumulants
(\kappa_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are real functions defined in the space of random variables with finite
n
-th moment, i.e.,
\kappa_{n}:L^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. They are so named because they are additive in the sense that
\kappa_{n}(X+Y)=\kappa_{n}(X)+\kappa_{n}(Y)
for independent random variables
X,Y
. They are defined by the relation

\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{zX}\right]\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{z^{\ell}}{\ell!}\kappa_{\ell}(X)+o(z^{n})\quad\textup{as }z\to 0

for
z\in i\mathbb{R}
. Note that this does not necessarily hold for
z\in\mathbb{C}
. For concreteness, we note that
\kappa_{1}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X]
coincides with the expectation,
\kappa_{2}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}
is the variance, and
\kappa_{3}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^{3}]-3\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X]+2\mathbb{E}[X]^{3}
is finally a new function, and

\kappa_{4}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^{4}]-4\mathbb{E}[X^{3}]\mathbb{E}[X]-3\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]^{2}+12\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}-6\mathbb{E}[X]^{4}.

For a normal distribution, the cumulants of order
3
and higher all vanish, a property that characterizes normality
[
3
]
. This vanishing property motivates thinking of higher-order cumulants as measures of deviation from normality. In particular
\kappa_{3}
is a measure of asymmetry, and
\kappa_{4}
is a measure of tailedness. The dilation-invariant quotient
\kappa_{3}/\kappa_{2}^{3/2}
is known as skewness, and
\kappa_{4}/\kappa_{2}^{2}
is known as kurtosis (in some texts excess kurtosis), from the Greek κυρτ´ς

meaning convex or curved.

Additivity follows from
X,Y
independent implies
e^{zX},e^{zY}
independent implies

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{z^{\ell}}{\ell!}\kappa_{\ell}(X+Y)+o(z^{n})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{z(X+Y)}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{zX}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[e^{zY}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{zX}\right]\right)+\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{zY}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{z^{\ell}}{\ell!}(\kappa_{\ell}(X)+\kappa_{\ell}(Y))+o(z^{n})

thus
\kappa_{\ell}(X+Y)=\kappa_{\ell}(X)+\kappa_{\ell}(Y)
for every
\ell\in[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
.

To relate the cumulants to the moments, recall Faà di Bruno’s formula

\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}f(g(x))=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f^{(\ell)}(g(x))B_{n,\ell}(g^{\prime}(x),\dots,g^{(n-\ell+1)}(x))

where
B_{n,\ell}
is an incomplete Bell polynomial — defined as

\displaystyle B_{n,\ell}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-\ell+1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\dots,j_{n-\ell+1}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+\dots+j_{n-\ell+1}=\ell\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\dots+(n-\ell+1)j_{n-\ell+1}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{j_{1}!\dots j_{n-\ell+1}!}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{1!}\right)^{j_{1}}\left(\frac{x_{2}}{2!}\right)^{j_{2}}\dots

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x_{n-\ell+1}}{(n-\ell+1)!}\right)^{j_{n-\ell+1}}.

An application of the formula for
f=\exp
and
g(z)=\log(\mathbb{E}[e^{zX}])
gives

\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\Bigg\rvert_{z=0}\mathbb{E}[e^{zX}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}e^{g(0)}B_{n,\ell}(\kappa_{1}(X),\dots,\kappa_{n-\ell+1}(X))

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}B_{n,\ell}(\kappa_{1}(X),\dots,\kappa_{n-\ell+1}(X)),

i.e., the moments of
X
can be expressed as a polynomial in its cumulants. The polynomials
B_{n}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}B_{n,\ell}
thus give the moments as functions of the cumulants, and they are called exponential Bell polynomials.

Another application of the formula for
f=\log
and
g(z)=\mathbb{E}[e^{zX}]
gives

\displaystyle\frac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\Bigg\rvert_{z=0}\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{zX}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(-1)^{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!\frac{1}{g(0)^{\ell}}B_{n,\ell}(\mathbb{E}[X],\dots,\mathbb{E}[X^{n-\ell+1}])

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(-1)^{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!B_{n,\ell}(\mathbb{E}[X],\dots,\mathbb{E}[X^{n-\ell+1}]),

i.e., the cumulants are expressed as a polynomial in the moments via the logarithmic Bell polynomials
\overline{B}_{n}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(-1)^{\ell-1}(\ell-1)!B_{n,\ell}
. This is a particular case of the relation of formal power series

\log\left(1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{x_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\overline{B}_{\ell}(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})}{\ell!}z^{\ell}+O(z^{n+1}).

(1)

It is remarkable that the function
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto(B_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,B_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))
is (entry-wise) polynomial with a polynomial inverse
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto(\overline{B}_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,\overline{B}_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))
.

We are now ready to state the main theorem. Formally, it is not an original result as it follows from
[
4
, Theorem 1.11]
, however, to the best of the author’s knowledge, this specific formulation and its proof are new. The historical background is further discussed in
section 2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
F:L^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that there exists a continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with
F(X)=f(\mathbb{E}[X],\dots,\mathbb{E}[X^{n}])
. Suppose that
F
is additive, i.e., for all
X,Y\in L^{n}
independent,
F(X+Y)=F(X)+F(Y)
. Then
F
is a linear combination of cumulants of order at most
n
, equivalently
f
is (in the appropriate domain) a linear combination of logarithmic Bell polynomials.

Requiring that
F
depend only on the moments is not that significant a restriction, since under mild conditions a distribution is uniquely determined by its moments. Moreover, the hypotheses of the theorem are weak — continuity and additivity — and its consequences are strong — polynomial and even more rigidly a finite family given explicitly.

The fundamental observation in the proof is that addition of independent random variables, at the level of moments, is an algebraic operation (the Hurwitz product) involving binomial expansions. After encoding additivity in these terms, we find a system of coordinates that transforms this product into ordinary addition in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The problem then reduces to solving a Cauchy functional equation under hypotheses that imply linearity. The cumulants appear as a basis associated to this linearization.

Before proceeding, define the set of moment sequences

\Omega=\big\{(\mathbb{E}[X],\dots,\mathbb{E}[X^{n}]):X\in L^{n}\big\}

so we can outline the proof:

1.

Prove that
\Omega
has non-empty interior;

2.

Define the Hurwitz product
*
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and observe that the additivity hypothesis of the main theorem is equivalent to
f(x*y)=f(x)+f(y)
;

3.

The Hurwitz product gives
\mathbb{R}^{n}
the structure of an abelian group which is isomorphic to the usual
\mathbb{R}^{n}
via the function
\varphi
, i.e.,
\varphi(x+y)=\varphi(x)*\varphi(y)
;

4.

f\circ\varphi
extends to a linear functional on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

5.

The compositions
\overline{B}_{n}\circ\varphi
span the space of linear functionals.

2
Previous Work

The cumulants were introduced by Thiele, and the reader can find a translated version of his paper in
[
1
]
. Rota
[
5
]
also credits him with the following characterization of the cumulants:

Sometime in the last [19th] century, the Danish statistician Thiele observed that the variance of a random variable, namely
\text{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}
, possesses notable properties, to wit:

1.

It is invariant under translation:
\text{Var}(X+c)=\text{Var}(X)
for any constant
c
.

2.

If
X
and
Y
are independent random variables, then
\text{Var}(X+Y)=\text{Var}(X)+\text{Var}(Y)
.

3.

\text{Var}(X)
is a polynomial in the moments of the random variable
X
.

He then proceeded to determine all nonlinear functionals of a random variable which have the same properties.

Thiele’s works predate modern notation, and we could not find a modern statement of the polynomial characterization in the literature. His characterization (as described by Rota) is similar to ours, with the main difference being that he considers only functions of the form
f(\mathbb{E}[X],\dots,\mathbb{E}[X^{n}])
for polynomial
f
, where we considered any continuous
f
.

Another far-reaching characterization was found by Mattner
[
4
]
. He gives the space of probability measures with all moments finite a topology, and proves that functions that are additive and continuous with respect to that topology are linear combinations of cumulants. He chooses a strong topology, so continuity is a weak assumption of
f
, and in fact our result can be obtained as a particular case of his. His proof uses functional and Fourier analytic techniques. The present work may thus be viewed as an intermediate result between Thiele’s and Mattner’s characterizations.

The Hamburger Moment Problem

The Hamburger moment problem is: which sequences
x=(x_{\ell})_{\ell\in[n]}
are moment sequences, insofar as there exists a random variable
X
such that
\mathbb{E}[X^{\ell}]=x_{\ell}\>\forall\>\ell\in[n]
, equivalently
x\in\Omega
? Is this random variable unique up to distribution?

The first question has a rather simple answer, and the second one is beyond the scope of this paper, but very interesting. Its answer is negative in general, but positive under mild restrictions (e.g. Carleman’s condition) under which the corresponding random variables are characterized by their moments. In this context, the hypothesis of the main theorem that
F
may only depend on the moments appears less restrictive than at first glance.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
6
, Theorem 3.8]
)
.

A sequence
(x_{\ell})_{\ell\in[n]}
is a moment sequence if and only if the Hankel matrix associated with it

\begin{bmatrix}1&x_{1}&x_{2}&\cdots&x_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&\cdots&\cdots&x_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}\\
x_{2}&\vdots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}&x_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}&\cdots&\cdots&x_{2\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\end{bmatrix}

is positive semidefinite.

For example, a length two sequence
(x_{1},x_{2})
is a moment sequence if and only if
x_{2}-x_{1}^{2}\geq 0
, that is if and only if
\textup{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}\geq 0
. This can always be achieved by, e.g., a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(x_{1},x_{2}-x_{1}^{2})
.

Knowing this theorem, we can prove the main result of this section.

Proposition 3.2
.

The set
\Omega
contains a neighborhood of
x=(\ell!)_{\ell\in[n]}
.

Proof.

To apply Sylvester’s criterion, compute the determinant of the leading minors

\det\begin{bmatrix}1&1!&\cdots&k!\\
1!&2!&\cdots&(k+1)!\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
k!&(k+1)!&\cdots&(2k)!\end{bmatrix}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\ell!^{2}

[
2
, Example 2]
. These determinants are all strictly positive, so by continuity of
\det
there is a neighborhood of
x
where they are all positive. For any element of this neighborhood, we can apply Sylvester’s criterion to prove that the associated Hankel matrix is positive semidefinite, thus there exists a random variable with the corresponding moments, thus it is an element of
\Omega
.
∎

This vector
(\ell!)_{\ell\in[n]}
is a natural choice as the moment sequence of a standard exponential distribution.

4
The Hurwitz Product

In the previous section, we discussed the map that takes a random variable to its moment sequence. We now define the Hurwitz product
*
, which corresponds to the sum of independent random variables under this map. More precisely, let
x=(\mathbb{E}[X^{\ell}])_{\ell\in[n]}
and
y=(\mathbb{E}[Y^{\ell}])_{\ell\in[n]}
, then
x*y
is the moment sequence of
X+Y
.

We might think of the Cauchy product of two sequences
\left(\sum_{k}x_{k}y_{\ell-k}\right)_{\ell\in[n]}
as the operation that gives the coefficients of the product of two generating functions
\sum_{\ell}x_{\ell}z^{\ell}\sum_{\ell}y_{\ell}z^{\ell}
. We are taking
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
x_{0}=1
for convenience, and similarly for all other sequences. Likewise, the Hurwitz product

x*y=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}x_{k}y_{\ell-k}\right)_{\ell\in[n]}

gives the sequence of coefficients of the product of exponential generating functions

\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{x_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{y_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{(x*y)_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}+O(z^{n+1}).

(2)

For example,

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})*(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})=\left(x_{1}+y_{1},x_{2}+2x_{1}y_{1}+y_{2},x_{3}+3x_{2}y_{1}+3x_{1}y_{2}+y_{3}\right).

This operation is commutative despite its name.

Recall the additivity hypothesis of the main theorem:
F(X+Y)=F(X)+F(Y)
. It is now clear that it is equivalent to
f(x*y)=f(x)+f(y)
for all
x,y\in\Omega
.

In fact, the Hurwitz product gives
\mathbb{R}^{n}
the structure of an abelian Lie group, but we will not need that fact. What we will need is that
\Omega
is a sub-semigroup of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},*)
, which is easy to verify since for
x,y\in\Omega
we can find random variables
X,Y
(which can be taken to be independent) with moment sequences
x,y
then
X+Y
is a random variable whose moments are
x*y
which is an element of
\Omega
.

5
Linearization

William Stein said that Mathematics is the art of reducing any problem to linear algebra, and this paper is no exception. We now define a function
\varphi
such that
f\circ\varphi
is linear.

\displaystyle\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto u\quad\textup{where }\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+z^{k})^{x_{k}}=1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{u_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}.

We omit terms of order
O(z^{n+1})
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The function
\varphi
is a group isomorphism to
(\mathbb{R}^{n},*)
from the usual
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Indeed, let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varphi(x)=u
and
\varphi(y)=v
, then

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\varphi(x+y)_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+z^{k})^{x_{k}+y_{k}}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+z^{k})^{x_{k}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+z^{k})^{y_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{u_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}\right)\left(1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{v_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}\right)

by
Equation 2
,
\varphi(x+y)=\varphi(x)*\varphi(y)
.

Furthermore if
\varphi(x)=\varphi(y)
we have

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+z^{k})^{x_{k}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+z^{k})^{y_{k}}

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+z^{k})^{x_{k}-y_{k}}

\displaystyle=1+O(z^{n+1})

from which we can extract the
z
coefficient, implying
x_{1}=y_{1}
then the
z^{2}
coefficient implies
x_{2}=y_{2}
, etc. Inductively
x=y
, i.e.,
\varphi
is injective.

Finally, observe that

\varphi(x)=\left(x_{1},\binom{x_{1}}{2}+2x_{2},\binom{x_{1}}{2}+6x_{1}x_{2}+6x_{3},\dots\right)

in particular the
\ell
-th coordinate
\varphi(x)_{\ell}
is something that only depends on
x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell-1}
plus
\ell!x_{\ell}
. So for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we can find its pre-image by recursion on the coordinates:
x_{1}=u_{1}
, and
x_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{2}-\binom{x_{1}}{2}\right)
,
x_{3}=\frac{1}{3!}(u_{3}-\dots)
, etc. That is,
\varphi
is surjective.
∎

The reason why we need this isomorphism is to state this double equality which is key to the entire proof

f\circ\varphi(x+y)=f(\varphi(x)*\varphi(y))=f\circ\varphi(x)+f\circ\varphi(y)\quad\textup{for all }x,y\in\varphi^{-1}(\Omega)!

In words:
f\circ\varphi
is additive. Now, since
\varphi
is continuous, the inverse image of a ball
\varphi^{-1}(B)
is open, and since it is surjective, it is not empty so a standard result from algebra proves linearity. The additivity condition
g(x+y)=g(x)+g(y)
is equivalent to the Cauchy functional equation, which under these conditions has a unique linear solution.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a sub-semigroup with non-empty interior and
g:S\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous additive function, i.e.,
g(x+y)=g(x)+g(y)
for all
x,y\in S
. Then
g
extends to a unique linear functional on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Define
\overline{g}:S-S\to\mathbb{R}
by
\overline{g}(x-y)=g(x)-g(y)
, where
S-S=\{x-y:x,y\in S\}
. To verify that this is well-defined, consider two representatives of the same point
x_{1}-y_{1}=x_{2}-y_{2}
then

\displaystyle x_{1}+y_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{2}+y_{1}

\displaystyle g(x_{1}+y_{2})

\displaystyle=g(x_{2}+y_{1})

\displaystyle g(x_{1})+g(y_{2})

\displaystyle=g(x_{2})+g(y_{1})

\displaystyle g(x_{1})-g(y_{1})

\displaystyle=g(x_{2})-g(y_{2}).

Furthermore, for any point
p\in S-S
write it as
p=x-y
thus
p+y=x
which implies that any additive extension
g^{\prime}
in
S-S
satisfies
g^{\prime}(p+y)=g^{\prime}(x)
thus
g^{\prime}(p)=g^{\prime}(x)-g^{\prime}(y)=g(x)-g(y)=\overline{g}(p)
, that is,
\overline{g}
is the unique extension of
g
to an additive function in
S-S
.
Since
S
contains an open ball
B_{\epsilon}(v)
,
S-S
contains
B_{\epsilon}(v)-B_{\epsilon}(v)=B_{2\epsilon}(0)
, so we can finally define an extension
\overline{\overline{g}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\overline{\overline{g}}(x)=\lambda\overline{g}(x/\lambda)
for a sufficiently large
\lambda
. By additivity, this extension is unique and well-defined.
∎

From now on, we will consider
f
to be its linear extension to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We must now prove that the functions
\overline{B}_{\ell}\circ\varphi
are linear and find their closed form. Let
u=\varphi(x)
and compute

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{u_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+z^{k})^{x_{k}}+O(z^{n+1})

\displaystyle\log\left(1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{u_{\ell}}{\ell!}z^{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\log(1+z^{k})+O(z^{n+1})

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\overline{B}_{\ell}(u)}{\ell!}z^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\log(1+z^{k})+O(z^{n+1}).

Wherein we used the definition of
\varphi
, took a logarithm, and applied
Equation 1
. Now we use the power series for the logarithm

[z^{n}]\log(1+z^{d})=\begin{cases}\frac{(-1)^{n/d+1}}{n/d}&d\mid n\\
0&d\nmid n.\end{cases}

Where prepending
[z^{k}]
to a power series denotes the operation that gives its
z^{k}
coefficient. Finally extract the
z^{n}
coefficient,

\displaystyle\overline{B}_{n}(u)=\overline{B}_{n}\circ\varphi(x)

\displaystyle=n![z^{n}]\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\log(1+z^{k})

(3)

\displaystyle=n!\sum_{d\mid n}x_{d}[z^{n}]\log(1+z^{d})

(4)

\displaystyle=(n-1)!\sum_{d\mid n}(-1)^{n/d+1}dx_{d}.

(5)

The attentive reader will recognize the right-hand side as a Dirichlet convolution, a remark we will make no use of.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

Proof.

Consider the stacking
\left(\overline{B}_{1}\circ\varphi,\dots,\overline{B}_{n}\circ\varphi\right)
which by
Equation 5
is represented by an
n\times n
triangular matrix with non-vanishing diagonal entries, and in particular is invertible. Therefore
\left(\overline{B}_{\ell}\circ\varphi\right)_{\ell\in[n]}
is linearly independent, so their span is the entire space of linear functionals. Hence, there exist constants
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
such that

f\circ\varphi=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell}\cdot\overline{B}_{\ell}\circ\varphi.

Now apply
\varphi^{-1}
on the right to obtain

f=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}c_{\ell}\cdot\overline{B}_{\ell},

i.e.,
F=\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}\kappa_{\ell}
.
∎
