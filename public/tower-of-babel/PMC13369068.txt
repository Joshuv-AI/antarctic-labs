Neuromodulation Techniques Alleviating Neuropathic Pain by Targeting Microglia: A Narrative Review
Authors: Qinghua Mu, Xiaojin Li, Long Li, Shujun Sun, Juanjuan Xu, Guobin Song, Yifei Chen, Yang Lv, Zheng Gan, Dong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropathic pain (NP) has attracted growing attention in recent years; however, its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, and therapeutic options remain limited. Neuromodulation techniques have demonstrated considerable promise in managing NP, offering advantages including noninvasiveness, minimal discomfort, and precise anatomical targeting. Nevertheless, therapeutic parameters have yet to be standardized as a result of variability across multiple factors such as modality type, stimulation site, and treatment duration. Microglia, as the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, play a pivotal role in the development and maintenance of NP. Following nerve injury, microglia become activated and release various inflammatory mediators and signaling molecules that modulate pain transmission pathways. Importantly, emerging evidence indicates that neuromodulation can regulate microglial activation states and associated inflammatory cascades through either indirect or direct pathways. Indirect mechanisms involve modulating neuronal activity and downstream signaling, thereby influencing microglial functional phenotypes. Direct mechanisms act via microglia-specific properties such as electrophysiological sensitivity, receptor expression, or local field potential interactions. This review synthesizes current evidence on the mechanisms and recent advances in microglia-targeted neuromodulation strategies for NP relief, distinguishing between preclinical and clinically validated findings, and aiming to provide a theoretical foundation for future research.Keywords: Neuropathic pain, Microglia, Neuromodulation, Neuroinflammation, Pain modulation

Key Summary Points

Neuropathic pain (NP) is a chronic condition in which microglia-driven neuroinflammation and synaptic remodeling play a central role.Neuromodulation techniques (SCS, TENS, PNS, rTMS, FUS) show promise for NP, but their mechanisms of action on microglia are incompletely understood and stimulation parameters lack standardization.Preclinical evidence suggests neuromodulation regulates microglial function via indirect (neuronal modulation) and direct (e.g., Kaiso–P2X7 axis, electrophysiological sensitivity) pathways, whereas direct human in vivo confirmation is extremely scarce.Rigorous, standardized studies with long-term follow-up, real-time cell-specific monitoring, and NP subtype differentiation are urgently needed to advance the field.Future closed-loop systems, multimodal imaging of microglial markers, and combination therapies may improve clinical outcomes for patients with refractory NP.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is a chronic condition arising from damage or disease affecting the somatosensory nervous system, characterized by complex pathophysiological mechanisms. It commonly develops from diverse etiologies including diabetes, alcoholism, or trauma [1, 2]. Following peripheral nerve injury, local inflammatory responses and activation of specific ion channels (e.g., transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 TRPA1), can exacerbate pain and contribute to hyperalgesia [2]. Additionally, NP involves maladaptive central nervous system changes, including aberrant pain signal processing in the spinal cord and brain [3]. Recent research indicates that neuroplastic changes, encompassing peripheral sensitization in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and central sensitization at spinal and supraspinal levels, underlie NP development and maintenance. Furthermore, non-neuronal cells, particularly astrocytes and microglia, play critical roles in both initiating and sustaining NP [4].

From an epidemiological perspective, the prevalence of chronic pain and related disorders is increasing, driven by population aging, psychosocial stressors, and environmental changes. Approximately one-third of the general population experiences acute or chronic pain, with NP prevalence estimated at 3.0–17.0% [5]. Multiple risk factors, including diabetes, metabolic syndrome, lifestyle factors, alcohol consumption, and certain medications, are associated with NP development [1, 2, 6, 7]. Current clinical outcomes remain suboptimal. Standard treatments include pharmacotherapy, minimally invasive procedures (e.g., nerve blocks, pulsed radiofrequency, nerve ablation), and increasingly, neuromodulation techniques [8]. However, conventional pharmacological approaches are limited by modest efficacy, adverse effects, and dependence potential.

Neuromodulation technology encompasses a range of biomedical engineering approaches that utilize electrical, magnetic, optical, ultrasound, or chemical modalities, delivered via implantable or non-implantable methods, to modulate neuronal activity within the central, peripheral, and autonomic nervous systems [9]. Common techniques include spinal cord stimulation, transcranial magnetic stimulation, focused ultrasound, and optogenetics, each offering distinct clinical advantages. For instance, ultrasound and optogenetic approaches are non-pharmacological, avoid systemic drug administration, and carry minimal risk of addiction or metabolic side effects. Image-guided transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) enables precise targeting of pain-related brain regions while sparing non-target tissues, and clinical evidence indicates that properly delivered tFUS does not cause structural brain damage or systemic adverse effects [10]. Neuromodulation technologies are further characterized by cell-type specificity, sustained efficacy, and reversibility. Optogenetics achieves cellular specificity through genetic targeting, and certain implementations can produce prolonged pain suppression lasting over 30 min after a single intervention, with duration adjustable via parameter tuning [10, 11]. Implantable systems allow dynamic parameter adjustment based on individual patient response, supporting personalized treatment [10]. However, most existing neuromodulation strategies primarily target neuronal activity, while microglia-focused approaches for NP remain underexplored.

This review summarizes recent advances in microglia-targeted neuromodulation for NP relief, discussing the role of microglia in pain pathophysiology, clinical applications, emerging findings, and future challenges, to provide updated insights for advancing NP management. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Methods

This narrative review synthesizes published evidence on neuromodulation techniques that alleviate NP by targeting microglia, covering multiple research domains including preclinical neurobiology, molecular signaling pathways, and clinical translational studies. A systematic literature search was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, and CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure) databases to identify relevant articles published up to January 1, 2026.

The search strategy employed combinations of the following keywords: “neuropathic pain”, “microglia”, “neuromodulation”, “spinal cord stimulation”, “transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation”, “peripheral nerve stimulation”, “repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation”, “focused ultrasound”, “neuroinflammation” and “microglial polarization”. Both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text searches were used to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Studies were selected on the basis of their relevance to the scope of this review, with priority given to peer-reviewed original research articles, preclinical studies (including rodent models of NP such as spared nerve injury, chronic constriction injury, spinal nerve ligation, and painful diabetic neuropathy), and clinical trials or observational studies in human patients. Review articles, editorials, conference abstracts, and studies unrelated to pain mechanisms or microglial function were excluded.

Additional relevant publications were identified by manually screening the reference lists of key articles and previous systematic reviews. A wide range of study designs were considered, including mechanistic studies of microglial signaling pathways, electrophysiological investigations of neuromodulation, neuroimaging studies, and randomized controlled trials evaluating analgesic efficacy.

The identified literature was organized and narratively synthesized across key thematic areas: (1) the role of microglia in NP pathophysiology; (2) electrical stimulation techniques (spinal cord stimulation, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, peripheral nerve stimulation); (3) transcranial magnetic stimulation; and (4) focused ultrasound technology. Particular emphasis was placed on distinguishing between indirect effects (neuromodulation primarily modulating neuronal activity, which secondarily alters microglial function) and direct effects (neuromodulation acting on microglia-specific properties such as electrophysiological sensitivity or receptor expression), as well as clearly differentiating preclinical evidence (derived from animal models) from clinically validated evidence (derived from human studies).

Role of Microglia in NP

Microglia, as the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), exhibit significant morphological and functional plasticity in response to environmental changes, injury, and disease, playing a central role in neural development, homeostatic maintenance, and pathological processes [12, 13]. In NP, peripheral nerve injury or inflammation triggers microglial activation and proliferation in the spinal dorsal horn, a hallmark of chronic pain [12, 14]. Activated microglia regulate central sensitization and synaptic plasticity by releasing inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin-6, IL-6; tumor necrosis factor alpha, TNFα) and signaling molecules (e.g., ATP) [14–16]. For example, ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF) activates the STAT3 pathway, driving the expression of pro-inflammatory factors that promote pain development [15]. Additionally, activated microglia release ATP through volume-regulated anion channels, which then act on purinergic receptors (e.g., P2X4R) to enhance pain signal transmission via the P2X4R–BDNF–TrkB–KCC2 pathway (Fig. 1, pathway 1) [16].

Recent studies have revealed several microglia-dependent signaling pathways in NP. In peripheral nerve injury models, upregulation of transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) in spinal microglia promotes calcium influx, leading to microglial activation and subsequent release of lipocalin-2 (LCN2). LCN2 acts on spinal excitatory neurons to enhance structural and functional plasticity, driving central sensitization. Thus, TRPV4 serves as a key regulator of the glial neuroimmune axis and a potential therapeutic target for chronic pain [12]. Jeon et al. reported that the LCN2 receptor 24p3R is expressed on spinal neurons, suggesting that LCN2–24p3R binding promotes synaptic plasticity through glia–neuron crosstalk in NP [17]. The aforementioned process is shown as pathway 2 in Fig. 1. Furthermore, fractalkine (CX3CL1) signaling between neurons and microglia via CX3CR1 regulates synaptic pruning and exacerbates pain-related synaptic remodeling [14]. High concentrations of ATP activate microglial P2X7 receptors, triggering the release of cathepsin S (CatS), which cleaves fractalkine to produce soluble CX3CL1. This in turn activates microglial p38 MAPK signaling and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, thereby enhancing neuronal excitability and NMDA receptor-mediated synaptic transmission (Fig. 1, pathway 3) [14, 16, 18, 19].

Neuromodulation Technology Targeting Microglia

Electrical Stimulation

Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS)

SCS delivers low-frequency (40–60 Hz) or high-frequency (10 kHz) electrical pulses via epidural electrodes to inhibit pain signal transmission in the spinal dorsal horn and activate descending inhibitory pathways, thereby relieving pain. The traditional low-frequency SCS (40–100 Hz) has been routinely applied to failed back surgery syndrome (FBSS), complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), diabetic peripheral neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, chemotherapy- and radiotherapy-induced painful neuropathy, and peripheral nerve injury pain. At the same time, high-frequency 10 kHz SCS did not produce sensory abnormalities, and the 6-month pain relief rate could reach 76–78%, and showed advantages in improving neurological function (such as sensory recovery), but the follow-up data of more than 5 years were limited. Burst SCS (burst SCS) is formed by clusters of short pulses. Every five spikes with a frequency of 500 Hz form a cluster. The analgesic effect is better than that of traditional low-frequency SCS, but the mechanism and long-term efficacy still need to be verified. DRG stimulation has limited scope and is less affected by cerebrospinal fluid. It is suitable for focal pain, such as lower limb CRPS. Closed-loop SCS is based on using physiological signals (such as cortical EEG and heart rate) to adjust stimulation parameters in real time, which in theory can be done on demand, but the existing control algorithm has the problems of high false positive and low capture rate [9, 20, 21]. Although frontier methods such as high-frequency 10 kHz, burst, DRG stimulation, and closed-loop systems show advantages such as better analgesic effect and no sensory abnormalities, the long-term safety and adaptability to the population still need to be further verified. However, it still has certain guiding significance for clinical practice. For example, patients should be strictly screened according to the etiology and drug reactivity in the treatment to avoid invalid implantation for patients with poor response such as pain after spinal cord injury. At the nursing level, we should focus on the prevention of perioperative infection in patients with diabetes, electrode displacement and pulse generator maintenance, and realize long-term management with the help of remote program control and pain diary.

Studies have shown that the analgesic mechanism of traditional spinal cord electrical stimulation includes (i) activation of Aβ fibers and excitation of inhibitory interneurons to close the spinal “pain gate”; (ii) modulation of inhibitory neurotransmitter release (e.g., GABA, acetylcholine, serotonin); (iii) regulation of pain-related neural circuits; and (iv) neural remodeling [9]. At the same time, preclinical studies (mainly based on animal models) demonstrate that SCS exerts anti-neuroinflammatory and analgesic effects by regulating microglial activation states and their molecular signaling networks.

Following peripheral nerve injury in preclinical models, both microglia and astrocytes become activated in the spinal dorsal horn, evidenced by enhanced CD11b (OX-42) immunoreactivity. In spared nerve injury (SNI) models, SCS at 4 Hz and 60 Hz attenuates this activation, with the SCS group showing significantly reduced OX-42 staining compared to sham stimulation. This effect may be an indirect effect on microglia, likely via reduced neuronal hyperexcitability. Additionally, preclinical data indicate SCS decreases levels of pronociceptive molecules including p-ERK1/2, BDNF, PKCγ, p-NR1, and p-GluR1 in nerve-injured rats [13, 22]. These changes reflect downstream consequences of SCS, primarily indirect.

The anti-inflammatory effects of SCS involve multitarget modulation of signaling pathways, as demonstrated in preclinical studies. In chronic constriction injury (CCI) and painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN) animal models, SCS reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (IL-6, IL-1β, TNFα) via suppression of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, alleviating NP [23, 24]. This suppression is largely indirect, as SCS modulates neuronal input to microglia. Furthermore, following nerve injury, DRG neurons release colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1), which activates spinal microglia to produce cytokines, chemokines, and free radicals that drive pain progression [25, 26]. In a preclinical chronic contusion injury model, Sun et al. demonstrated that SCS reduces CSF1 levels in the spinal cord, thereby decreasing glial activation and alleviating NP in a chronic contusion injury model [27]. This is an indirect effect on microglia, mediated by reducing neuron-derived CSF1.

High-frequency SCS (1–10 kHz) provides unique analgesic advantages over traditional low-frequency modes by precisely modulating the neuroimmune system. Preclinical evidence shows that HF10-SCS specifically inhibits the Kaiso–P2X7R signaling axis in spinal dorsal horn microglia. P2X7R is predominantly expressed on microglia, and HF10-SCS directly alters microglial electrophysiological properties. Following nerve injury, the transcription factor Kaiso promotes a pro-inflammatory microglial phenotype (marked by CD86 and release of factors such as CXCL2, C1qa, and Plac8) by upregulating the P2X7 receptor—a key ATP-gated ion channel on microglia [28, 29]. HF10-SCS blocks this cascade by reducing Kaiso expression and P2X7R activity while altering microglial electrophysiological properties (membrane potential, calcium signaling), thereby reshaping microglial phenotype. Consequently, HF10-SCS shifts microglia toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype (marked by CD206) and increases release of IL-10 and TGFβ (Fig. 2, pathway 1). Single-cell RNA sequencing from preclinical studies confirms significant microglial reduction post-treatment, with genome-wide analysis revealing suppression of pro-inflammatory pathways and activation of anti-inflammatory repair pathways [28]. By targeting the Kaiso–P2X7R axis to reprogram the spinal inflammatory microenvironment, HF10-SCS achieves sustained analgesia beyond conventional neurotransmission blockade, offering an innovative strategy for chronic pain management. It should be noted that all of the above evidence regarding HF10-SCS directly targeting microglia comes from preclinical animal models; direct human clinical validation is currently lacking.

Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family members, including extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), p38, and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), are core regulators of NP. Following nerve injury, rapid activation of these kinases in the DRG and spinal dorsal horn drives neural plasticity, central sensitization, and neuroinflammation, thereby promoting pain chronicity [30]. Preclinical studies confirm that in spinal nerve ligation models, ERK and p38 are specifically activated in spinal microglia, inducing release of pro-inflammatory factors (e.g.,IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα) and upregulating COX-2 and iNOS expression, which sustain hyperalgesia (Fig. 2, pathway 2) [31, 32].

In the SNI model, HF-SCS administered early after injury significantly attenuates mechanical hyperalgesia and cold allodynia. Mechanistically, HF-SCS selectively inhibits p38 MAPK activation in the DRG and spinal cord, and reduces the injury-induced upregulation of p-ERK1/2 in both regions, indicating that modulation of ERK hyperphosphorylation is a key anti-hyperalgesic mechanism (Fig. 2, pathway 2) [30]. The inhibition of microglial MAPK pathways by HF-SCS is considered an indirect effect, as SCS primarily acts on neuronal circuits, and the suppression of p38/ERK/JNK in microglia is likely secondary to reduced neuronal injury signals or direct modulation of upstream microglial receptors such as P2X7R. In summary, HF10-SCS directly targets the Kaiso–P2X7R signal axis on microglia and changes the electrophysiological characteristics and phenotype of microglia. SCS inhibits the TLR4/NF-κB pathway and MAPK pathway (p38, ERK, JNK) in microglia by reducing neuron-derived csf1 and regulating neuronal excitability. These effects depend on the regulation of SCS on neural circuits or neuronal signals, rather than directly acting on the initial target on microglia.

The above mechanisms of SCS regulating microglia, including Kaiso–P2X7R axis, TLR4/NF-κB pathway, MAPK pathway, and CSF1 signal, are all derived from rodent models (SNI, CCI, PDN, spinal nerve ligation, chronic contusion, etc.). Thus far, there are limited human clinical studies directly verifying that SCS can relieve NP by regulating microglia. The existing clinical evidence mainly focuses on the analgesic effect of SCS itself, and the microglial mechanism behind it is mostly inferred from preclinical studies. Therefore, it is necessary to be careful when translating the above mechanism into clinical application to avoid overinterpretation.

Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation (TENS)

TENS is a noninvasive neuromodulation technique that delivers electrical current via skin-surface electrodes to stimulate peripheral nerves, thereby inhibiting nociceptive input and modulating pain signal transmission [33]. Percutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (PENS) combines principles of TENS and electroacupuncture, delivering current through fine needles inserted near target tissues (muscle or nerve) to modulate nerve activity with greater precision [34]. TENS and PENS are routinely used in clinical practice for conditions such as chronic low back pain, neck pain, myofascial pain, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, postoperative pain, and exercise-related pain, offering advantages such as the absence of drug toxicity and low cost. However, systematic reviews have clearly indicated that, whilst the pooled effect of TENS compared with a sham stimulation group shows a reduction in pain intensity scores, the quality of evidence has been rated as “very low” because of a high risk of bias in the included studies, small sample sizes, and imprecise results. The actual efficacy is likely to differ from the estimated values, and the effectiveness of TENS in the treatment of NP in clinical practice requires further validation. Closed-loop SCS stimulation, transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation, and high-frequency/burst modes remain in the exploratory phase, with a current lack of high-quality validation [33–35]. On the basis of the aforementioned evidence, TENS may serve as a low-risk adjunctive treatment in clinical practice for patients who do not respond well to medication or cannot tolerate it, provided that patients are clearly informed of the uncertainty regarding its efficacy. In terms of patient care, electrode placement must be strictly standardized, avoiding the anterior neck, eyes and cardiac regions; stimulation intensity should be adjusted according to guidelines, whilst monitoring for skin irritation. Individualized treatment strategies should select frequency modes on the basis of the type of pain; conventional high-frequency, low-intensity stimulation is suitable for most patients, whilst acupuncture-like low-frequency, high-intensity stimulation is appropriate for those requiring muscle contraction. Patients should be encouraged to keep a pain diary and attend regular follow-up appointments to optimize parameters. If outcomes are unsatisfactory, escalation to transcutaneous needle electrode stimulation or implantable peripheral nerve stimulation may be considered, thereby achieving safe and effective pain management despite the very limited evidence available.

The analgesic mechanism involves the gate control theory of Melzack and Wall, which proposes that activation of low-threshold, large-diameter Aβ fibers by non-painful stimuli excites inhibitory interneurons in the spinal dorsal horn. These interneurons subsequently inhibit signal transmission from small-diameter Aδ and C nociceptive afferent fibers, effectively closing the pain gate to higher CNS centers and blocking ascending pain pathways [36].

Although TENS has been widely used for analgesia, its underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Emerging preclinical evidence indicates that TENS alleviates NP by targeting spinal microglia indirectly. First, TENS indirectly suppresses microglial pro-inflammatory polarization by inhibiting spinal dorsal horn neuronal activity and reducing excitatory neurotransmitter release (e.g., glutamate) [37]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that high-frequency TENS (HF-TENS) increases spinal GABA while decreasing glutamate under pathological conditions such as osteoarthritis. GABAB receptor activation may suppress microglial interleukin release, while reduced glutamate may decrease TNFα release via group II metabotropic glutamate receptors, collectively reducing spinal neuron hyperexcitability [38]. Both pathways are indirect, as they rely on prior changes in neuronal neurotransmitter levels. Second, in preclinical models, early TENS application effectively inhibits microglial proliferation (BrdU-positive) in the superficial spinal dorsal horn and decreases the number of activated microglia. It also reduces levels of key pro-inflammatory and pronociceptive signaling molecules, including phosphorylated p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p-p38), protein kinase Cγ (PKCγ), and phosphorylated cyclic AMP response element-binding protein (p-CREB), while downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokine release by suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling pathways [39, 40]. These effects are indirect, resulting from TENS-induced reduction in neuronal excitability and neurotransmitter release. Additionally, animal studies show that TENS upregulates spinal opioid receptor expression, thereby enhancing endogenous opioid system activity. Pre-administration of naloxone blocks TENS-mediated inhibition of hyperalgesia and glial activation, indicating that early TENS acts via opioid receptors to prevent neurotransmitter release (glutamate and substance P), thereby indirectly inhibiting glial cell activation [41].

Peripheral Nerve Stimulation (PNS)

PNS is a neuromodulation technique that treats pain by implanting small electrodes near target peripheral nerves, which are connected to subcutaneously implanted or external pulse generators to deliver electrical pulses. Its core principle is precise transmission of stimulation to specific peripheral nerves or branches to modulate nerve signal transduction [42]. PNS has now been clinically validated and is routinely used for focal NP (such as median, ulnar, and radial nerve neuropathies, occipital neuralgia, and intercostal neuralgia), acute postoperative pain (following total knee replacement, anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, rotator cuff repair, and amputation), as well as chronic migraine, shoulder pain, FBSS, and phantom limb pain. Meanwhile, cutting-edge research is focused on the development of magnetic PNS, which utilizes alternating magnetic fields to noninvasively stimulate peripheral nerves. A retrospective case series has demonstrated that it can reduce patients’ pain scores and opioid usage. However, the level of evidence remains low [33, 43]. Of course, it still brings us a lot of enlightenment in clinical practice and patient care. For example, we need to focus on the problem of electrode displacement, the incidence of which varies according to the location and wire design, and the new barbed or spiral wire can reduce the risk. At the same time, we should also pay attention to patients’ wound infection, bleeding, acupuncture discomfort, skin irritation, and other issues.

Regarding mechanisms, while not fully elucidated, several theories exist. The classic gate control theory proposes that stimulation of large-diameter, non-nociceptive afferent fibers activates spinal inhibitory interneurons, thereby blocking pain signal transmission from small-diameter nociceptive fibers. At the central level, PNS can inhibit wide dynamic range (WDR) neurons in the spinal dorsal horn, interfere with spinothalamic tract transmission, and modulate pain signaling through regulation of neurotransmitters (serotonin, dopamine, GABA, glycine) and specific proteins (Arc, GluA1) [43–45]. At the peripheral level, PNS significantly downregulates pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-2, IL-17A, TNFα) while upregulating anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-10), thereby reducing local neuroinflammation, a key mechanism underlying its sustained analgesic effect [46]. These mechanisms are largely derived from preclinical animal studies and some human experimental models; direct clinical evidence remains to be fully established.

Additionally, preclinical studies in animal models demonstrate that PNS alleviates pain through microglial regulation. Wong et al. investigated sciatic nerve stimulation in an L5 nerve root ligation (NRL) rat model. At post-injury day 7, the ipsilateral spinal cord dorsal horn of the NRL+ SES group exhibited significantly enhanced ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1)-labeled microglial activation (increased Iba1-positive area and integral density) compared with sham controls, along with elevated spinal expression of IL-1β, IL-6, NF-κB, and TNFα. Following sciatic nerve electrical stimulation at 2 Hz and 20 Hz, Iba1 signaling in the ipsilateral dorsal horn decreased, accompanied by increased paw withdrawal thresholds and latencies. These findings indicate that peripheral nerve stimulation reduces microglial activation, effectively alleviating neuropathic hyperalgesia following nerve injury in this rat model [47].

Kilohertz high-frequency electrical stimulation (KHES) demonstrates unique advantages in NP treatment. In preclinical animal models, specifically the CCI models, sciatic nerve damage significantly upregulates transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) and N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor 2B (NMDAR2B) expression in the spinal dorsal horn, promoting pain signal transmission and inducing mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia. KHES reverses these effects by suppressing TRPV1 and NMDAR2B expression at both protein and mRNA levels. Its analgesic effects are closely associated with regulating microglial activation through modulation of TRPV1 and NMDAR2B signaling pathways in the spinal cord [48].

Further preclinical evidence confirms that TRPV1/NMDAR2B pathway activation promotes downstream activation of glial cells, particularly microglia and astrocytes [49]. In the CCI model, expression of the microglial marker Iba1 and the astrocytic marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is significantly elevated, while KHES treatment effectively suppresses activation of both cell types. Pharmacological experiments provide direct evidence for TRPV1’s central role in KHES analgesia: the TRPV1 agonist capsaicin partially reverses KHES-induced pain threshold elevation accompanied by spinal microglia reactivation, whereas the TRPV1 inhibitor SB-705498 mimics KHES analgesic effects with no significant difference in microglia activation inhibition [48, 50]. In summary, KHES may exert therapeutic effects in NP by inhibiting excessive TRPV1/NMDAR2B pathway activation in the spinal cord, thereby suppressing downstream neuroinflammatory responses in glial cells. This provides robust experimental evidence supporting KHES as a novel non-pharmacological analgesic strategy. However, there is no clinical study to directly verify the regulation of microglia induced by khes, so it is necessary to be cautious in translating these results into clinical application.

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS)

TMS uses an electromagnetic coil placed on the scalp to generate a strong, time-varying magnetic field that penetrates the skull and induces electric currents in targeted brain regions, thereby directly regulating neuronal excitability. Studies suggest that rTMS can alleviate pain by regulating microglial phenotypic polarization, inflammatory factor balance, synaptic pruning, and molecular signaling pathways [51]. rTMS has been extensively investigated for neurological and psychiatric conditions including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, stroke, and multiple sclerosis [52]. For NP, rTMS shows efficacy in treating central post-stroke pain, spinal cord injury-related NP, peripheral NP, and generalized NP [53]. A number of randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews have consistently shown that high-frequency, high-intensity, multisequence rTMS stimulation of the contralateral primary motor cortex (M1) has a definite analgesic effect on drug-resistant peripheral NP (such as diabetic polyneuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, post-traumatic neuralgia). Its short-term efficacy is significantly better than that of the sham stimulation group, and the efficacy accumulates with the increase of stimulation times, lasting up to 25 weeks, which is recommended as class A evidence. In contrast, the analgesic effect of low-frequency (≤ 1 Hz) stimulation and dorsolateral prefrontal target has not been confirmed. At present, the frontier directions still in the research stage include closed-loop/adaptive rTMS, deep H-coil stimulation, and transcutaneous magnetic stimulation. Although they initially show potential, they still need high-quality clinical verification. In clinical practice, high-frequency M1 rTMS is preferred for patients with peripheral NP, and the cumulative treatment course and long-term maintenance program for at least 8–10 times are planned. At the same time, the expected management and safety monitoring of patients’ efficacy are strengthened to avoid patients’ self interruption of treatment [52–56].

Microglia Phenotypic Polarization and Inflammatory Regulation

High-frequency repetitive TMS (HF-rTMS, ≥ 5 Hz) not only exerts therapeutic effects by enhancing neuronal excitability but also indirectly drives microglial polarization from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby rebalancing neuroinflammation. In a preclinical animal study, Yang et al. reported that in CCI models, pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα) in the left prefrontal cortex (PFC) and sciatic nerve region were significantly elevated, while anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels were also increased compared with sham controls. Following 2 weeks of 10-Hz rTMS treatment, pro-inflammatory cytokines decreased whereas IL-10 levels were further elevated. Furthermore, anti-inflammatory marker CD206 expression increased while pro-inflammatory marker CD86 expression decreased in both the left PFC and sciatic nerve region [57]. These findings demonstrate that HF-rTMS suppresses M1 marker expression while upregulating anti-inflammatory markers, inhibiting pro-inflammatory factor release, and enhancing anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels. These effects are all indirect, resulting from rTMS modulation of neuronal circuits. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) represents the primary target for HF-rTMS interventions in NP. The DLPFC inhibits pain signaling through endogenous pain modulation pathways that interact with downstream inhibitory neural circuits (e.g., thalamocortical limbic system) [58]. Clinical studies demonstrate that high-frequency stimulation targeting the left DLPFC significantly reduces pain scores in spinal cord injury patients, with mechanisms involving reversal of excessive microglial activation in the prefrontal cortex, regulation of glutamatergic neurotransmitter balance, and suppression of neuroinflammatory responses [57, 59, 60]. It should be noted, however, that the microglial mechanisms in human patients are inferred from preclinical findings.

HF-rTMS exhibits robust regulatory capacity over microglial pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory condition across multiple disease models, but these effects are indirect. In preclinical animal models of rat cerebral ischemia (MCAO, OGD/R) and traumatic brain injury (TBI), HF-rTMS suppressed pro-inflammatory markers (iNOS, CD16/32, IL-6, TNFα) while promoting anti-inflammatory markers (CD206, Arg1, IL-10). This phenotypic shift reduces pro-inflammatory factor release while increasing anti-inflammatory and neurotrophic factor secretion, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation and promoting neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity. The NF-κB signaling pathway serves as a key downstream target of rTMS action, with its activation consistently suppressed across multiple models [61–65]. In TBI models, rTMS demonstrates unique indirect inhibitory effects on microglial pyroptosis. Following TBI, the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis pathway is strongly activated, whereas rTMS significantly reduces expression of key pathway proteins, thereby decreasing IL-1β and IL-18 maturation and release [62]. In ischemic stroke models, rTMS regulation involves the let-7b-5p/HMGA2 axis. Studies confirm that 10-Hz rTMS specifically upregulates let-7b-5p expression in microglia, which inhibits the downstream NF-κB pathway by targeting HMGA2, ultimately curbing pro-inflammatory polarization [65]. Although let-7b-5p is upregulated in microglia, this is an indirect consequence of rTMS-induced neuronal activity changes; no evidence supports direct rTMS action on microglial gene expression. Collectively, these preclinical findings demonstrate that rTMS effectively modulates microglial phenotypes and neuroinflammation. Whether similar rTMS mechanisms operate in NP-induced neuroinflammation in humans requires further investigation.

Synaptic Remodeling and Neural Circuit Regulation

The analgesic mechanism of TMS comprises not a single pathway but a three-dimensional network integrating neurophysiological, neuroimmune, and neurotrophic regulation. In a preclinical animal study, Andreou et al. demonstrated that single-pulse TMS (sTMS) administered immediately after induction of cortical spreading depression (CSD) significantly inhibits CSD in rats. sTMS suppresses spontaneous firing of third-order thalamic neurons (VPM) and C-fiber afferents while blocking ascending trigeminothalamic transmission, thereby exerting antimigraine effects. This study identifies the thalamus, rather than the traditionally implicated trigeminocervical complex, as a key sTMS target in migraine treatment [66].

In a clinical study involving human patients with centrally sensitized chronic pain, such as diabetic peripheral neuropathy, Xu et al. used TMS combined with EEG and fMRI to assess motor-evoked potentials, short-interval cortical inhibition, cortical excitability, and motor pathway integrity, thereby enhancing early neuropathy detection sensitivity and specificity [67]. rTMS upregulates brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression, activates the BDNF-TrkB-NMDA receptor pathway, and promotes neural regeneration and synaptic plasticity. Concurrently, stimulation of the primary motor or prefrontal cortex inhibits spinothalamic pain transmission via corticofugal projections, modulating blood flow and metabolism in pain-related brain regions (thalamus, anterior cingulate cortex), reducing hyperactivity and restoring inhibitory balance to alleviate pain in diabetic neuropathy. These therapeutic effects can persist for weeks [67]. High-frequency stimulation (e.g., 15 Hz) has proven most effective for NP relief [53].

In a preclinical animal model of sciatic nerve branch injury, overexcitation of the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) enhanced synaptic input to parvalbumin-positive (PV+) inhibitory neurons in the primary motor cortex (M1), excessively suppressing M1 glutamatergic pyramidal neuron activity and weakening output. High-frequency rTMS directly augmented M1 pyramidal neuron excitability while suppressing M1 PV+ neuron activity, thereby correcting the S1–M1 excitation–inhibition imbalance. The reinforced M1 pyramidal neurons project to PV+ neurons in the lateral hypothalamus (LH). This M1 → LH-PV pathway is weakened in NP, whereas rTMS enhances M1 output to activate LH-PV neurons, which subsequently excite glutamatergic neurons in the ventrolateral periaqueductal gray (vlPAG). Ultimately, through the rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM)-5-HT descending pathway, this strongly inhibits nociceptive input in the spinal dorsal horn, representing the core mechanism of top-down pain regulation [68]. This mechanistic pathway was elucidated in an animal model.

At the cortical level, preclinical studies in animals show that rTMS modulates inflammation in regions such as the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and anterior cingulate gyrus by stimulating the primary motor cortex (M1). This reduces TNFα, IL-1β, and IL-6 while increasing IL-10 and BDNF, thereby regulating the BDNF–TrkB signaling pathway to enhance neural plasticity and alleviate pain memory and emotional disorders [69]. At the spinal cord level, preclinical evidence indicates that rTMS indirectly inhibits microglial and astrocyte activation in the spinal dorsal horn, reducing Iba1 and GFAP expression and pro-inflammatory factor release (IL-1β, TNFα), thus promoting microglial polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype [64]. This is an indirect effect on microglia, mediated by rTMS-induced descending modulation from supraspinal centers. At the DRG level, animal studies demonstrate that rTMS reduces neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) expression and nitric oxide (NO) production, relieving pain hypersensitivity while reducing peripheral nerve sensitization through modulation of neurotransmitter receptors and ion channels [70]. At the peripheral nerve level, preclinical research shows that rTMS reduces metabolic activity and inflammatory cell infiltration in injured nerves, regulating nerve pathways via remote effects and improving neurological function [71]. It can be seen that the initial target of rTMS is cortical neurons (M1 or prefrontal cortex), which indirectly regulate the activation state and polarization direction of spinal microglia through descending pathways such as cortex spinal cord glial axis. It is important to note that the majority of these mechanistic insights are derived from preclinical animal studies; direct human evidence for many of these microglial and molecular pathways remains limited.

In conclusion, preclinical and clinical evidence together suggest that rTMS alleviates NP through multilevel, multitarget anti-inflammatory mechanisms [72].

Ultrasonic Control Technology

Focused ultrasound (FUS) is a noninvasive technology that concentrates acoustic energy onto specific targets via transducers, categorized into high-intensity FUS (HIFU) and low-intensity FUS (LIFU) based on intensity and mechanism. HIFU delivers high-energy ultrasound from outside the body to deep-seated lesions, generating localized thermal effects (70–100 °C) and cavitation that cause coagulative necrosis and fibrosis for applications such as nerve ablation (e.g., celiac ganglion destruction) [73]. In contrast, LIFU is an emerging, nondestructive neuromodulation technology that uses mechanical sound waves to regulate neuronal activity and neural plasticity [74]. At lower intensities, non-thermal effects include acoustic cavitation, altered ion channel permeability, and indirect neuromodulation via membrane deformation. The physical basis of ultrasonic neuromodulation involves electrical, chemical, and mechanical changes in nerve impulses, with mechanical changes being particularly critical. Evidence suggests ultrasound may influence action potentials (AP) through (1) capacitive current from membrane displacement; (2) direct activation of mechanically sensitive ion channels; (3) acoustic pore formation (i.e., transient lipid bilayer pores); and (4) membrane wave generation via axon coupling [75]. These mechanisms collectively alter membrane potential and gating dynamics, affecting nerve excitability. In therapeutic applications, LIFU-induced mechanical vibration causes microcavitation and transient pore formation, promoting Na+ and Ca2+ influx and membrane depolarization [76], thereby modulating neuronal excitability for NP treatment.

FUS applications span central and peripheral nervous system structures, from superficial cortical areas (e.g., motor cortex) to deep brain regions (hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus), as well as peripheral nerves and spinal nerve roots [77]. FUS has demonstrated therapeutic potential across various neurological disorders, including Parkinson’s disease [78], essential tremor [79], obsessive–compulsive disorder [80], depression, Alzheimer’s disease [81], and NP [82]. At present, clinically verified technologies mainly include (1) magnetic resonance-guided FUS (MRgFUS) thalamotomy for the treatment of drug-resistant idiopathic tremor, the 4-year follow-up study of showed that hand tremor and disability scores were improved by 56% and 63%, respectively [79]; (2) MRgFUS central lateral thalamotomy was used to treat chronic NP, with an average pain relief of 56.9% at 1 year after operation, and more than 50% of patients experienced long-term relief ≥ 50% [77]; (3) MRgFUS subthalamic nucleus incision in the treatment of asymmetric Parkinson’s disease improved the lateral motor score by about 50% and reduced the equivalent dose of levodopa by 24% after 6 months [78]; (4) HIFU ablation has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to relieve cancer pain caused by bone metastasis. It is effective in 60–100% of patients, and about 20% of the pain completely disappeared [82]. Cutting-edge methods still at the research stage include (1) LIFU modulation of the spinal region, which has been shown to alleviate hyperalgesia and post-spinal cord injury spasticity in animal models, but for which no human trials have yet been conducted [74]; (2) LIFU combined with microbubbles to open the blood–brain barrier for Alzheimer’s disease: early human studies indicate reversible, safe barrier opening and a trend towards cognitive improvement, but the level of evidence is low [81]; (3) MRgFUS anterior thalamic ablation for treatment of treatment-resistant obsessive–compulsive disorder; cost-effectiveness modeling suggests it may be superior to radiofrequency ablation, but clinical efficacy data are lacking [80]. In summary, FUS has established certain indications in conditions such as movement disorders and chronic pain, whilst frontier applications such as spinal modulation, blood–brain barrier opening, and psychiatric disorders still require standardized parameters and validation through high-quality clinical trials.

Mechanistically, FUS may (1) regulate NP-related neural activity to inhibit pain transmission pathways, (2) modulate neural plasticity and adaptive reorganization after injury (which is critical for chronic pain maintenance), and (3) exert anti-inflammatory effects, particularly in peripheral neuropathy where local nerve inflammation contributes to pain [74, 77].

In a preclinical animal study, Wang et al. demonstrated that in a chronic sciatic nerve compression model of NP, LIFU stimulation of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) significantly activated neurons (increased c-Fos fluorescence) and alleviated mechanical hyperalgesia, evidenced by increased mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT50) (P < 0.05) compared with sham controls. Molecular analysis revealed LIFU significantly decreased expression of neuroplasticity-related proteins (MAP2, GAP43, tau) in the ACC, suggesting pain relief through reversal of abnormal central remodeling [80]. Furthermore, safety assessments in this animal model confirmed that LIFU stimulation did not cause significant tissue edema, hemorrhage, glial cell activation, or neuronal apoptosis, demonstrating its safety in targeted brain regions [84, 85].

In another preclinical study using a CCI model, spinal cord tsFUS reduced microglial numbers in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn of CCI models. Using microglial biomarkers Iba1 and CD86, Song et al. demonstrated that tsFUS treatment decreased both total spinal microglia (Iba1+ cells) and activated pro-inflammatory microglia (Iba1+/CD86+ cells) compared with sham-treated animals [86].

A preclinical study by Hellman et al. examined long-term cytokine expression alterations in DRG and dorsal horn cells following common peroneal nerve injury (CPNI) and LIFU stimulation in an animal model. LIFU reversed cytokine profiles at multiple time points, reducing TNFα, IL-6, IL-1β, CNTF, and TIMP1 while increasing IL-10 [87]. The TNFα–IL-6–CNTF axis mediates peripheral pain cascades. These changes mirror the regulatory mechanisms observed with electrical stimulation, suggesting that microglia may play a role in FUS analgesia and providing a basis for investigating ultrasound–microglia interactions in NP.

Summary and Prospects

This review summarizes the various neuromodulation techniques—including SCS, TENS, peripheral nerve stimulation, repetitive TMS, and low-intensity focused ultrasound—that can alleviate NP by regulating microglial activation states and neuroinflammation (Table 1). These effects are either indirect (via modulation of neuronal activity and downstream signaling) or direct (via microglia-specific mechanisms such as the Kaiso–P2X7R axis).

Several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, most mechanistic insights derive from rodent models of nerve injury, whereas direct clinical evidence that neuromodulation targets human microglia in vivo remains extremely limited. Second, the majority of studies have focused on short-term outcomes, with long-term effects and potential rebound phenomena largely unexplored as a result of a lack of extended follow-up data. Third, the field has predominantly relied on histological and bulk molecular analyses, which cannot capture dynamic, cell-type-specific microglial responses in real time. Fourth, the precise molecular targets through which neuromodulation directly acts on microglia—such as mechanosensitive channels or electrophysiological properties—require further validation using microglia-specific loss-of-function approaches. Fifth, notable heterogeneity exists across studies (including basic mechanistic research, preclinical animal experiments, and clinical trials) in terms of study design, sample size, stimulation parameters, outcome measures, and follow-up duration, which limits comparability and generalizability. Moreover, many preclinical studies lack standardized reporting (e.g., insufficient details on blinding, allocation concealment, and formal sample size calculation), potentially leading to overestimation of treatment effects. Finally, different NP subtypes (e.g., diabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, low back pain) may involve distinct microglial activation patterns, and current evidence is insufficient to support a unified neuromodulation strategy across all subtypes. Collectively, these limitations underscore the need for more rigorous, standardized, and translational research to better define the role of microglia-targeted neuromodulation in NP management.

Future research should advance toward personalized therapies, adjusting electrode positioning, stimulation intensity, and signal parameters on the basis of patient-specific anatomical and physiological characteristics, validated through large-scale multicenter randomized controlled trials. Intelligent wearable devices represent another development direction, with exploration of novel materials such as flexible electrodes and biodegradable ultrasonic transducers to enhance biocompatibility and regulatory precision. As healthcare costs decrease and patient self-care awareness increases, acceptance and demand for transcutaneous electrical stimulators and noninvasive magnetic stimulation will grow, creating a substantial market [88–90]. Multimodal fusion technology represents another frontier, combining EEG/fMRI real-time feedback with transcranial electrical stimulation or focused ultrasound to achieve multitarget collaborative regulation and enhance neuromodulatory depth and precision. Combined therapies developed through multimodal imaging, such as PET scanning of microglial markers combined with molecular biology, electrical stimulation paired with anti-inflammatory drugs or gene therapy, and central-peripheral stimulation combinations, can enhance efficacy through synergistic effects [91]. Ultimately, integrated basic and clinical research must further elucidate the pivotal role of microglia in NP and advance personalized precision therapies. Key challenges include balancing the neuroprotective and pro-inflammatory effects of microglia and determining whether different subtypes play distinct roles across pain stages [13, 15].
