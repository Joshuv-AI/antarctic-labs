 SUMMARY
 This report is a part of the deliverables for technical assistance provided to
Cat Creek Energy for the Coral Summit Trybrid (Triple Hybrid-Pumped Storage
Hydropower, Battery Energy Storage System, and photovoltaic solar energy)
energy project. This report explores the operational benefits and challenges of
hybridizing an open loop PSH (200MW) located at Mackay, Custer County,
Idaho with solar PV (Ground mount 300MW and floating 40MW) and battery
(720MWhr). This document reports two activities performed as a part of the
hybridization assessment task 1) optimal resource allocation and energy
management strategy, and 2) power quality and reliability assessment.
 From optimal resource allocation and energy management strategy (activity
1), the following key findings can be observed:
 • Conventional PSH (CPSH) with two reversible pump turbines and
 separate penstocks can provide required flexibility equivalent to that
 from two ternary PSH with separate penstock. With single unit
 CPSH, upper reservoir head cannot be maintained accurately, the
 variation of water level is rapid and pump mode flexibility is not
 available. These disadvantages can be overcome by single unit
 TPSH. However, using two CPSH units with separate penstocks
 also overcome these disadvantages with the formation of the
 hydraulic short circuit between two conventional units.
 • Flooding of the lower reservoir is a severe concern when
 considering continuous operation for black start. This limits the
 duration of continuous operation from PSH alone to around 50
 hours. Due to the complementary PV and battery action, the
 duration of continuous operation and smooth power output can be
 extended.
 • An optimization problem is framed that maximizes the power
 output on an hourly basis while minimizing constraint violations
 and respecting seasonal variations of solar PV and load profiles .
 Two value streams, arbitrage and baseload generation are served by
 this profile. It was uncovered that for smooth power output during
 regular operation, PV curtailment will be required, or the battery
 capacity needs to be increased above 90MW to accommodate
 additional PV.
 From power quality and reliability assessment (activity 2) the following
takeaway points can be observed:
 • The Trybrid, when integrated at the Lost River bus, and limited to
 250MW in generation mode and -150MW in the pump mode, causes
 no violation of voltage or flow.

 1. BACKGROUND
 The global push towards reliable energy and advanced energy solutions has intensified in recent
years, with stakeholders across various sectors actively seeking solutions to replace fossil fuels. A critical
part of this effort is transitioning to a resilient energy grid to ensure a reliable energy future. However,
this transition faces significant challenges, including both the lack of a viable financial framework to
deploy reliable energy technologies and uncertainties surrounding system ownership and environmental
impacts. One major issue is the stochastic nature of variable renewable energy (VRE) sources like wind
and solar. While these technologies hold promise, their expansion is location and time-of-day specific,
often without adequate storage solutions to address generation fluctuations. Long-duration storage is
essential for continuous energy dispatch, reducing reliance on natural-gas plants. A hydro-hybrid or
“Trybrid” (Triple Hybrid-Pumped Storage Hydropower, Battery Energy Storage System, and photovoltaic
solar energy) setup, combining pumped storage hydropower (PSH), large-volume long-duration (LVLD)
energy storage, and VREs—floating and land-based wind and solar—offers a scalable solution. Currently,
no such facility exists in the operational or advanced-development stages, making this a pioneering step
towards a robust energy future. The Cat Creek Energy, LLC, (CCE) management team, with over 22
years of experience and $6.1 billion in projects, has received a preliminary permit from Federal Energy
Regulatory Commission for the Corral Summit PSH facility in Custer County, Idaho. CCE plans to locate
the photovoltaic (PV) solar and battery energy-storage system (BESS) near the PSH facility. Small
electric co-ops in Southeast Idaho are aware of the challenges faced by the Bonneville Power
Administration (BPA) which could impact future power delivery and pricing. Additional loads may not
be guaranteed under BPA programs, potentially leading to market-based pricing. New generation
resources, particularly LVLD PSH projects located closer to loads, can help mitigate these concerns. The
facility offers a long-term solution for local and reliable generation for rural electric cooperatives in
Eastern Idaho. The project's goal is to develop the most cost-effective and efficient Trybrid LVLD PSH
setup, enabling the broader hydropower community to achieve a resilient grid and other expanded
impacts.
 The Corral Summit PSH Project will be in Custer County, with two potential locations near the
existing Mackay Reservoir owned by the Big Lost River Irrigation District. This PSH facility has dual
purpose. The off-stream PSH facility will utilize the Mackay Reservoir as its lower reservoir and a new
off-stream upper reservoir will be constructed approximately 700 to 850 feet higher to generate sufficient
head for power production. The upper reservoir will cover about 400–500 surface acres and will have a
storage volume of approximately 25,000 acre-feet. This additional water-storage capacity will enhance
the Mackay Reservoir’s capacity, providing benefits for downstream irrigation, groundwater recharge,
environmental needs, community use, and recreation, without expanding the existing reservoir. This new
PSH facility also makes new water-storage opportunities more cost-effective, addressing the acute need
for water storage in Idaho and the broader western region. Floating PV solar panels and BESS will be
placed near the PSH facility. These could be located within the service territories of electric co-ops,
supporting PSH charging and supplying power to some co-op loads. The Corral Summit PSH power
station will feature approximately 40 MW of floating solar panels on the new upper reservoir, which will
help reduce evaporative losses. Additionally, CCE has identified private land in the area for an extra 300
MW of land-based PV solar capacity.

 1

 The hybrid or “Trybrid” setup of flexible hydropower, floating PV, and LVLD energy storage would
not only provide exceptional energy storage capacity but would also be able to deliver all the ancillary
services that a utility is obligated to deploy to manage its control area. This new inherent-value
technology would deliver these services and ensure grid resiliency and resource adequacy, it is anticipated
that by including water as well as energy storage in a new way, the project would be cost-effective for the
next century relative to other generation and storage options. In essence, the PSH-hybrid setup facilitates
significant VRE expansion, greatly reducing curtailment and enabling rapid response to blackout events.
With solar-power fluctuations, the system adeptly adjusts in real time, ensuring uninterrupted power
supply. In summary, the PSH-Trybrid setup demonstrates an ability to “keep the lights on” unlike any
other facilities in operation or development.
 This report informs investment in a Trybrid system that captures a broad set of value propositions
(e.g., flexibility, optimization of system cost, and capacity). This report presents the optimal resource-
allocation and energy-management strategy, and power quality and demonstrates a reliability assessment
based on optimal configuration of the Trybrid system within specified bounds to improve the inherent
value of the project. This involves steady-state modeling, statistical analysis of time-series data, energy-
management strategy development, and optimization. Similarly, the report will also highlight the analysis
on the impact of the Trybrid configuration on the power grid as well as the impact of the grid on the
Trybrid.

 MANAGEMENT STRATEGY
 This section defines the optimal nameplate ratings and optimal energy-management strategy for the
Trybrid configuration resources, within specified bounds, to improve the inherent value of the project.
 2.1. Steady-State Modeling of PSH
 The steady-state model of the PSH system considers separate models of subsystems, such as
a) reservoir, b) penstock, c) the mechanical prime-mover, and d) the electrical generator. The reservoirs
are modeled as accumulations of flow, and an assumed shape of the reservoir is considered to estimate the
variation of head with storage. Mass balance constraint of the reservoir has been applied on these water
bodies, along with water level, and volume constraints. The mass-balance constraints account for several
aspects, such as inflow/outflow due to pumping/turbine action, inflow usage constraints, and evaporation
losses. Water-inflow constraints are specific to the lower reservoir and serve an important role in
respecting the needs and safety of downstream users. Currently, a seepage-estimation model for the
reservoirs is under development. The penstock model uses Darcy-Weisbach head-loss calculation to
estimate the friction losses of the penstocks. For the turbines and pumps, three configurations are
considered: a) conventional (Francis Pump-turbine), b) ternary (Pelton turbine with centrifugal pump),
and c) adjustable-speed pumped storage (Francis Pump-turbine). The detailed characteristics of the pump
and turbine need to be procured. Until then, available efficiency characteristics from INL’s
HydroGenerate tool are used to estimate efficiency of mechanical devices [1].

2.1.3. Operational Differences Between Conventional and Ternary
 Pumped-Storage Hydropower
 Figure 3 displays the operating ranges for both conventional PSH (CPSH) and TPSH [2]. CPSH and
TPSH are similar in generation mode in that a turbine range of 40–100% is used. The minimum power
output of the turbine can vary by the different manufacturers. In the pump mode, CPSH is a constant load
because it is connected to the synchronous machine. The TPSH, however, can use the turbine’s operating
range to recover power consumed by the pump, thereby providing flexibility. For example, a pump rated
at 120 MW, operating at 83% efficiency, results in 100 MW available at its discharge. The 100 MW of
discharge is now available for a turbine operating at 85% efficiency. This results in 85 MW generated
using the full power of the pump. Thus, the minimum effective operating point is 120 MW, less 85 MW,
or 35 MW minimum power consumption without storing any water for future use.

Figure 3. Operating Ranges for CPSH and TPSH in generation and pumping modes.

2.1.4. Power-Output Calculation Process
 The flow chart in Figure 4 displays the procedure adopted by calculations. With the power reference
from a user-defined output profile or an optimization algorithm, along with the site data (reservoir
characteristics) and weather data (inflow), power calculation for either CPSH or TPSH is executed in the
following steps:
1. Power reference is divided between the units (equally divided between the units if desired power
 exceeds the capacity of one unit).
2. Power reference is checked.
3. Flow is determined iteratively while considering the efficiency and head loss. (If volume constraints
 are violated, the flow is recalculated along with head loss, efficiency and power output.)
4. With the newly finalized flow for both units, the reservoirs (lower and upper) are updated to provide
 the new head for the new time-step.

2.1.6. PSH Model-Simulation Results
 To determine the optimal operation and sensitivity to the type of PSH used, the above-described
mathematical models will be used. The following section presents an initial analysis of the simulation
results for CPSH and TPSH configurations that help verify the proper operation of the model. Single- and
dual-unit simulations were conducted to evaluate performance, efficiency, and operational characteristics
of each type. CPSH systems that use separate pump and turbine units were compared to TPSH systems
that integrate both functions into a single unit. The simulations provide valuable insights into the
differences in operation of the two types of PSH, and potential advantages of each configuration. In the
figures below, P_out is electrical power output, P_des is desired power output, P_PSH1 and P_PSH2 are
the electrical-power output of the first and second units, H_UR and H_LR are water levels of the upper
and lower reservoirs, Vol_UR and Vol_LR are water levels of the upper and lower reservoirs, Q and
Q_sw_LR are turbine and spillway flows.
 In Figure 6 and Figure 7, one CPSH unit and one TPSH are simulated, beginning with reservoirs
filled to maximum level and without any inflow at the lower reservoir. The TPSH has a Francis turbine in
the generating mode. Twenty-four hours of power output references are generated manually as a sample
to test the model. This profile will be generated by the optimization algorithm in the next step of the task.
Comparing both TPSH and CPSH, the following observations can be made:
• The error in power consumption during the pump mode results from the electrical power supplying
 the losses of the mechanical system
• During pump mode, the output of the CPSH is more discrete relative to the TPSH; thus, responding to
 part-load pump-mode references is not possible as with TPSH
• Maintaining reservoir volumes and head with CPSH is more difficult than with TPSH
• The change in reservoir water levels is more gradual with TPSH, and this is mainly due to the pump
 mode flexibility offered by TPSH, considering that the power references were the same for TPSH and
 CPSH.

 2.2. Steady-State Model of Battery
 For the battery model PySam [6] is used where the battery is considered as a standard model, driven
by state of charge (SOC), assuming a prescribed depth of discharge (DOD) has been developed.
Simultaneously, PySam also provides a battery-degradation model that considers the impact of cycle age,

 2.5. Optimization of Operating Strategy and Nameplate Capacity
 An energy-management strategy is currently under development to be the plant-level controller for
the Trybrid system. The devices of the Trybrid are controlled in a different manner during the different
modes of operation such as base-load operation, peaking, islanded or grid-congestion management for
wind-energy accommodation. This control will act as a reference governor and command the energy
devices to respond in a required manner to facilitate the revenue streams and energy exchange among the
devices, as well as with the grid. This strategy, in combination with the devices, will lead to achieving the
technical and economic goals of the system. Also, realistic constraints such as maintenance of each
device, will also be accounted for through the energy-management strategy.
 A modified variant of the particle-swarm optimization (PSO) algorithm, called unified PSO (UPSO),
will be used to perform the optimization; this code has already been developed. There are multiple
reasons for choosing UPSO over PSO. First, the problem is highly non-linear due to the characteristics of
each energy device and the external environmental considerations pertaining to flow, irradiance, and
temperature, among others. PSO is generally highly capable of handling non-linearity. Second, PSO has
global-search capability while handling multidimensional problems. Finally, the formulation requires
comparatively less time because the problem does not need to be modified to suit PSO and does not
require the computation of derivatives. Instead, PSO is a type of evolutionary computational method that
optimizes problems by iteratively improving candidate solutions about a given measure of quality (the
objective function). PSO is a nature-inspired optimization algorithm that mimics the preying behavior of a
group of animals to find the solution to a given problem in a bounded search space.
2.5.1. Unified Particle Swarm Optimization
 UPSO improves the capabilities of PSO to search for the solution space by unifying the local and
global search capabilities, thereby providing a systematic method for balancing exploration and
exploitation. This creates a more-robust search algorithm that avoids local minimums while also
increasing efficiency by reducing the total number of iterations. UPSO unifies the velocity-update
methods of the local and global variants of PSO. The local velocity update of the local variant of PSO,
Equation (63), is based on the personal best of the particle and the best in a neighborhood. Similarly, the
global velocity update of the global variant, Equation (64), is based on the personal best of the particle
and the best particle of the swarm, thereby assuming that the entire swarm is the in neighborhood of each
particle. The constriction coefficient is used for modification of momentum and personal and global
learning coefficients and is presented in Equation (65). The unification factor, Equation (66) unifies the
local and global velocities. The position of each particle is updated as depicted in Equation (67).

2.5.2. Normal Operating Scheme
 Under normal operating conditions, the Trybrid would perform arbitrage and baseload operations. For
arbitrage, the Trybrid will serve two peaks, morning and evening peak, and an early-morning service
dedicated to recharging the battery while recharging the PSH during the weekend. For baseload
operations during the day and late night, the battery performs PV power-output smoothing and supplies
the late-night hours.
2.5.3. Dimensions, Objective Functions, and Constraints
 The optimization algorithm will generate a profile of hourly net power output from the entire hybrid.
For this purpose, it has five dimensions of search: (a) morning peak, (b) evening peak, (c) baseload,
(d) weekday charge, and (e) weekend charge. The time of day during which these activities happen and
duration for which these activities will happen are fixed manually. The Trybrid will serve multiple
purposes which are represented in Figure 14 where arbitrage and baseload operation have been
represented.

 3. POWER QUALITY AND RELIABILITY ASSESSMENT
 This section analyzes the impact of the Trybrid on the power grid as well as the impact of the grid on
the Trybrid. This will require modification of the Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC)
power-grid model. Preliminary examination of the proposed location shows that a 230 kV line leads to
Antelope substation; however, the line is rated at 195 MVA, which is inadequate to handle additional load
or generation on the proposed scale. Two possible upgrade options to be considered are adding a new line
or upgrading existing line to form a double-circuit line. To that extent, the code for adding new lines and
buses and adding synchronous generators to the new buses has been completed. The addition of inverter-
based resources, such as the PV and battery, is under development. Upon reaching a conclusion on the
dispatch of the Trybrid for different time periods and seasons, the network model will be updated, and
network studies will be conducted to perform the analysis mentioned above. The WECC models that are
being considered are the 2032 and the 2034 cases for heavy summer, heavy winter, and light spring.
Another modification required for integration testing is the redispatch of other generators after the Trybrid
is integrated. The redispatch can be done in two ways: a) reserve proportional redispatch or b) optimal
redispatch based on power flow. The following steps summarize the framework of integrating and the
Trybrid:
1. Add the new plant with transformers, connecting lines, and generation and storage resources to the
 network.
2. Initialize the power set-point for the Trybrid resources with optimization results.
3. Perform redispatch and ensure convergence of system load flow with minimum line flow and voltage
 violations.
 3.1. Contingency-Analysis Procedure
 On the modified system generated by the above steps, contingency analysis (CA) will be performed.
CA is a critical function in power-system studies, providing insights into the potential impacts of
component failure. CA considers one component failure at a time with a goal to estimate the contribution
of the Trybrid to the network during the contingency, as well as estimate the change of reliability of the
system with the Trybrid. This framework for CA in Power System Simulation for Engineers (PSS/E) has
been constructed as below:
1. Initialize PSS/E and open the raw power file.
2. Solve the load flow.
3. Generate monitoring files.
 a) Obtain branch flow MVA limits from the branch network table.
 b) Use the branch rating as upper limits to generate a monitoring file.
 c) Obtain bus voltage limits from bus data table.
 d) Add the bus voltage limits to the monitoring file.
4. Generate contingency list file.
 a) Obtain list of branches near the Trybrid that are close to the flow limits.
 b) Obtain critical generators within a radius that are heavily loaded.
5. Apply contingencies sequentially.
6. Estimate indices of flow and voltage violation of every contingency.
7. Estimate the contribution of the Trybrid in terms of change in reactive power
 consumption/production.

 4. CONCLUSION
 CCE’s project considers a Trybrid plant that will include PSH, battery, and solar PV energy
resources. The energy goals of the project are to develop a cost-effective and efficient configuration of
these three resources, such that power-network reliability (through real- and reactive-power support) can
be increased, network-wide renewable-energy curtailment can be reduced and increases to energy price
for nearby loads can be limited. The project also has water goals to maintain and possibly improve the
water availability for the locality. The open-loop PSH system will use Mackay Reservoir in Custer
County, Idaho, as the lower reservoir fed by the Big Lost River. The upper reservoir will be constructed
near Coral Summit to provide a head of 700–800 feet. The PSH will have a total generating capacity of
200 MW for a maximum duration of 60–70 hours. The project is also proposed to have both a) 30 MW of
floating PV and an additional 300 MW of land-mount PV and b) battery storage of 100 MW for 8 hours.
 This report addresses the hybridization assessment for the Trybrid. The assessment analyzes the
technical benefits of the Trybrid configuration for both the grid as well as the owners. Three major
activities will facilitate the assessment: 1) optimal resource-allocation and energy-management strategies,
2) a power-quality and reliability assessment and 3) tradeoffs to improve flexibility (which will be
presented in Part 2 of the report).
 Optimal resource-allocation and energy-management strategies determine the optimal nameplate
ratings and optimal energy-management strategy for the Trybrid configuration resources, within bounds
specified by CCE. For this, steady-state models for the PSH, PV, and battery are required, along with an
optimization algorithm, objective function, and constraints. In addition, this report analyzed climatic data.
Similarly, power-quality and reliability assessments consider performing power-flow studies and
uncovering reliability aspects from the power-network perspective. Last, tradeoffs to improve flexibility
examine the dynamic benefits to the grid and to the plant due to the flexibility of each resource. Also, new
modes of operation due to the nature of each resource and possible controls are examined in this activity
and will be presented in Part 2 of the report. The key updates and takeaways are:
 1. Steady-state models of CPSH and TPSH reflect the operational benefits of each type. With single
 unit CPSH, reservoir head cannot be maintained accurately, the variation of water level is rapid, and
 pump-mode flexibility is not available. These disadvantages can be overcome by single-unit TPSH.
 However, using two CPSH units with separate penstocks also overcomes these disadvantages.
 2. Steady-state models of battery can capture different chemistries and degradation of the battery.
 3. Steady-state models of PV reflect the impact of irradiance, temperature, and wind speed.
 4. A framework with optimization algorithm, objectives, and constraints have been developed.
 5. A framework for power flow and CA has been developed.
