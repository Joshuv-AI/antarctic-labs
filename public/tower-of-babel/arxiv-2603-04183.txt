Abstract
. In this paper, we study evolutive Hamilton–Jacobi equations with Hamiltonians that are discontinuous in time, posed on a simple network consisting of two edges on the real line connected at a single junction. We introduce a notion of (flux-limited) viscosity solution for Hamiltonians H i = H i ​ ( t , x , p ) , ( i = 1 , 2 CLOSE H_{i}=H_{i}(t,x,p),(i=1,2 ) that are assumed to be only measurable in t t , with H i ​ ( ⋅ , 0 , 0 ) H_{i}(\cdot,0,0) in L 1 ​ ( 0 , T ) L^{1}(0,T) . The flux limiter, A = A ⁡ ( t ) A=A(t) , acting at the junction, is not required to be continuous but only in L ∞ ​ ( 0 , T ) L^{\infty}(0,T) . In the case of convex Hamiltonians, we prove a comparison principle and establish an existence result via the construction of an optimal control problem. Generalizations to the nonconvex case and to more general networks are also discussed.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study viscosity solutions of Hamilton–Jacobi (HJ) equations featuring two distinct types of discontinuities. On the one hand, the Hamiltonians are assumed to be merely measurable with respect to the time variable
t
. On the other hand, they exhibit spatial discontinuities arising from the underlying structure of the problem. Precisely, we consider a simple network with two different Hamiltonians
H_{i}
(
i=1,2
) on the line with one junction. Unless otherwise specified, all solutions are understood in the viscosity sense.

Starting from the pioneering work of H. Ishii in 1985
[
16
]
, it was clear that when dealing with the
discontinuous case
- meaning a situation where both the Hamiltonian and the solution are allowed to be discontinuous- for classical of HJ-equations of the form

(1.1)

u_{t}+H(t,x,u,Du)=0\mbox{ for }(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,T)

an
ad-hoc
treatment has to be given to discontinuities on the
t
-variable. This is mainly due to the special position of the
t
-derivative in the equation. In fact, H. Ishii himself studied this case separately from other situations involving discontinuities. Incidentally, let us notice that it is natural to assume that solutions to (
1.1
) are continuous (in both variables), for, roughly speaking, one has to integrate
H
in time. In the sequel we will refer to the
t-measurable case
when the Hamiltonian
H
is measurable in
t
and continuous in the remaining variables.

We think it is very interesting to recall here the key idea behind the first definition as it is explained by H. Ishii himself (see
[
16
, Definition 7.1, p.52]
) in the simplest case of ODEs:

Consider
u^{\prime}(t)+H(t)=0
for
t\in(0,T)
and let
H(t)=g(t)+b(t)
with
g\in C(0,T)
and
b\in L^{1}(0,T)
.

The key remark is that an absolutely continuous function
u
solves
u^{\prime}(t)+H(t)=0
a.e. in
(0,T)
if and only if
u\in C(0,T)
and
v(t)=u(t)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\>ds
is a viscosity solution of
v^{\prime}(t)+g(t)=0
in
(0,T)
.

In 1987, P.L. Lions and B. Perthame provided three equivalent formulations of the notion of solution given by H. Ishii (see
[
18
]
).
Extension to the second order case have been studied by D. Nunziante in
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
. With these definitions in hand, it has been common belief in the community that, roughly speaking, when a (comparison, existence, regularity, . . . ) result holds true when the Hamiltonian is continuous in (
1.1
) it is still valid when one weakens the hypothesis of continuity in
t
to mere measurability.
However, to prove each parallel improvement for t-measurable Hamiltonians may appear as a bit tedious job because of the necessarily involved technicalities.

In
[
9
]
the author and F. Rampazzo studied approximation general results that allows to establishing comparison, existence, and regularity results by deriving them from the analogous results for the case of continuous Hamiltonians for equation (
1.1
). In this paper we exploit these ideas to study HJ-equations on regional problem or networks in the
t-measurable case
.

When the Hamiltonian is
t
-continuous, starting from 2010’s a lot of work has been devoted to the study of deterministic control problems and HJ-equations on networks or involving discontinuities in space and, more precisely problems where dynamics and running costs may be completely different in different parts of the domain. We chose to recall here only the articles that are more related to our work, a complete bibliography can be found in the book

The author, with G. Barles and E. Chasseigne studied the so called regional control problems in

At the same time Y. Achdou, F. Camilli, A. Cutri and N. Tchou studied HJ-equations on networks in

Our first motivation is the optimal control problem studied by P. Cardaliaguet et P. Souganidis in

Thus, the first problem we addressed is how to generalize the definition of viscosity solution for (

is continuous, starting from

–Test function will be the sum of two functions: one of the form

– Inequalities at touching point will not be asked to be fulfilled by the Hamiltonians but by any time continuous function

Since in (

Thus, the subsolution (resp. supersolution) condition for a continuous bounded function

If
(0,t_{0})

\displaystyle u(x,t)-\big(\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds+\psi(x,t)\big)

\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})+G(t_{0},\psi_{x}(0^{-},t_{0}),\psi_{x}(0^{+},t_{0})))\leq 0\quad(resp.\geq 0)\>.\\

In this paper we consider the case of equation (

,
i=1,2
.
Precisely, assuming only

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}u_{t}+H_{1}(t,x,u_{x})=0&\mbox{in}&(0,\infty)\times(0,T)\\
u_{t}+H_{2}(t,x,u_{x})=0&\mbox{in}&(-\infty,0)\times(0,T)\\
u_{t}(x,0)+\max\{A(t),H_{1}^{-}(t,0,u_{x}(0^{+},t)),H_{2}^{+}(t,0,u_{x}(0^{-},t))\}=0&\mbox{ in }&(0,T)\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&\mbox{ in }&\mathbb{R}.\end{array}\right.

In this case we not only have to adapt the definition of Flux limited solution outside the junction but also to modify the above condition on the junction. This is the aim of Section
2
where our definition of
Flux Limited t-Measurable (FL-tm)
solution is introduced and discussed (see Definition

).

In line with our first motivation of giving a definition of the equation (
1.2
) studied in
[
10
]
, we assume the Hamiltonians to be convex on the gradient variable. This hypothesis will allow us to exploit the construction of an optimal control problem to obtain an existence result. Indeed, in Theorem

we will prove that the value function of an optimal control problem is a (FL-tm)-solution of (
1.3
) as in Definition

.

With the aim of deriving uniqueness results for equation (
1.3
), in Theorem
3.1
we prove a comparison result for (FL-tm) sub and super solution. As explained in Section
3
the proof is based an approximating technique with the same ideas as in
[
9
]
and therefore two results are essentially needed. On one hand it has to be possible to construct
good approximating
continuous Hamiltonians and flux limiter, i.e., roughly speaking a
t
-continuous approximating sequence that are somehow uniform w.r.t. all other variables, and, on the other, a comparison result has to hold for the corresponding structure in the
t
-continuous case.

The advantage of this kind of proof is that this can be easily adapted to more general Hamiltonians and/or geometries. Indeed, in Section
5
we discuss how to
use these ideas to deal with the following cases: we weaken the convexity assumption on the

Let us point out here that when dealing with the model problem, equation (

), the comparison result can be proved assuming

[

With these ideas, we believe that one can study the

-measurable dependent case for more general geometries or junction conditions, as for example to problem problems in

The discussion on how to adapt the definition in these cases is beyond the scope of this work.

This paper is organized as follows: In Section

-measurable case for equation (

Section

we discuss how to weaken the convexity assumption in the comparison result and how to generalize these ideas to deal with networks.

2.
The definition of Flux limited
t
-measurable solution

In this section we set the problem and introduce our definition of Flux Limited viscosity solution in the case of
t
- measurable Hamiltonian that we call
Flux Limited t-measurable (FL-tm)
.

Let
\Omega_{1}:=(0,\infty)
,
\Omega_{2}:=(-\infty,0)
and
T>0
.

We will call a
t
-measurable modulus a non-negative function
\omega=\omega(t,k)
continuous, subadditive, non-decreasing in
k
and measurable in
t
such that
\omega(\cdot,k)\in L^{1}(0,T)
for all
k
and
\omega(t,0)=0
a.e.
If moreover,
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\omega(t,\delta)dt=0
we will say that
\omega
is a
t
-integrable modulus.

We assume:

(2.1)

\mbox{The initial condition }u_{0}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\quad\mbox{is Lipschitz continuous.}\\

The Hamiltonians
H_{i}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2

(2.2)

H_{i}(t,x,p)\mbox{ are measurable in }t\mbox{ and continuous in }(x,p).

(2.3)

H_{i}(t,0,0)\in L^{1}(0,T).

For each
R>0
, there exists
C_{1}=C_{1}(R)>0
and a
t
-measurable modulus
\omega_{R}
such that

(2.4)

|H_{i}(t,x,p)-H_{i}(t,y,p)|\leq C_{1}|x-y||p|+\omega_{R}(t,|x-y|)\quad\forall\>|x|,|y|\leq R\,,p\in\mathbb{R}\,,t\in[0,T]\,.

There exists
C_{2}>0
such that

(2.5)

|H_{i}(t,x,p)-H_{i}(t,x,q)|\leq C_{2}|p-q|\quad\forall p,q\in\mathbb{R},x\in\mathbb{R},t\in[0,T].

Moreover,

(2.6)

H_{i}(t,x,\cdot):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\mbox{ are convex and coercive uniformly with respect to }(t,x).

Following
[
14
]
(see also
[
6
]
)
we write
H_{i}^{+}
and
H_{i}^{-}
for the nondecreasing and nonincreasing part of
H_{i}(t,x,\cdot)
respectively, that is,

H_{i}^{+}(t,x,p):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\min_{p}H_{i}(t,x,p)&\mbox{ if }p\leq\hat{p}^{i}(t,x)\\
H_{i}(t,x,p)&\mbox{ otherwise }\end{array}\right.H_{i}^{-}(t,x,p):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{i}(t,x,p)&\mbox{ if }p\leq\hat{p}^{i}(t,x)\\
\displaystyle\min_{p}H_{i}(t,x,p)&\mbox{ otherwise }\end{array}\right.

where
\hat{p}^{i}(t,x)
is a point such that
H_{i}(t,x,\hat{p}^{i}(t,x))=\min_{p}H_{i}(t,x,p)
.

We set, for each
t\in[0,T]
,

(2.7)

\displaystyle A_{0}(t):=\max\Big\{\min_{p\in\mathbb{R}}H_{1}(t,0,p),\min_{p\in\mathbb{R}}H_{2}(t,0,p)\Big\}

and we consider the flux limiter
A:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.8)

A\in L^{\infty}([0,T])\mbox{ and }A(t)\geq A_{0}(t)\mbox{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Under this set of assumptions we will define and study
(FL-tm)
solution of

(2.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}u_{t}(x,t)+H_{1}(t,x,u_{x}(x,t))=0&\mbox{in}&\Omega_{1}\times(0,T)\\
u_{t}(x,t)+H_{2}(t,x,u_{x}(x,t))=0&\mbox{in}&\Omega_{2}\times(0,T)\\
u_{t}(x,0)+\max\{A(t),H_{1}^{-}(t,0,u_{x}(0^{+},t)),H_{2}^{+}(t,0,u_{x}(0^{-},t))\}=0&\mbox{ in }&(0,T)\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&\mbox{ in }&\mathbb{R}.\end{array}\right.

In the case of
t
-continuous Hamiltonians
H_{i}
and flux limiter
A
one can define solution following Imbert et Monneau
[
14
]
-
[
15
]
. In particular, they proved in
[
14
, Theorem 5.9]
existence and uniqueness for
Flux limited
solution of equation (
2.9
). The latest has also been studied in
[
6
]
where a different proof of comparison result is given. We will also refer to the results collected in
[
5
, Chapter 14]
.

For the sake of completeness we recall below this definition where the following set of test functions is considered
:
PC^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
is the space of piecewise fonctions
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
such that there exist
\psi_{1}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega_{1}}\times(0,T))
,
\psi_{2}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega_{2}}\times(0,T))
, such that
\psi=\psi_{1}
in
\overline{\Omega_{1}}\times(0,T)
and
\psi=\psi_{2}
in
\overline{\Omega_{2}}\times(0,T)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Flux Limited t-continuous (FL-tc)

A locally bounded function
u:\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a (FL-tc) subsolution (resp. supersolution) of (
2.9
) in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
if for any test function
\psi\in PC^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
and any local maximum (resp. minimum) point
(x_{0},t_{0})
of
u^{*}-\psi
(resp.
u_{*}-\psi
) in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
we have

\begin{array}[]{cl}\mbox{ if }x_{0}=0&\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})+\max\{A(t_{0}),H_{1}^{-}(t_{0},0,\psi_{x}(0^{+},t_{0})),H_{2}^{+}(t_{0},0,\psi_{x}(0^{-},t_{0}))\}\leq 0\quad\mbox{(resp. $\geq 0$)}\\
\\
\mbox{ if }x_{0}\in\Omega_{i}&\psi_{t}(x_{0},t_{0})+H_{i}(t_{0},x_{0},\psi_{x}(x_{0},t_{0}))\leq 0\quad i=1,2\>.\quad\mbox{(resp. $\geq 0$)}\\
\end{array}

A locally bounded function
u
is a (FL-tc) solution if it is both a sub and and a super-solution of (
2.9
) and it verifies
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
in
\mathbb{R}
.

In the case of HJ equation in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
with
t
-measurable Hamiltonians solution have been firstly defined by Ishii in 1985 (
[
16
]
). We recall here this definition for equation

(2.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}u_{t}+H(t,x,D_{x}u)=0&\mbox{in}&(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)&\mbox{ in }&\mathbb{R}^{M}\end{array}\right.

where the Hamiltonian

verifies regularity assumptions (
2.2
)-(

). In
[
16
]

, there exist a
t
-measurable modulus
\omega_{R}
such that

(2.11)

|H(t,x,p)-H(t,y,q)|\leq\omega_{R}(t,|x-y|,|p-q|)\quad\forall|x|,|y|,|p|,|q|\leq R\>,t\in[0,T].

Definition 2.2
.

Ishii t-measurable (Is-tm)

A locally bounded continuous function
u:\mathbb{R}^{M}\times(0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a (Is-tm) subsolution (resp. supersolution) of (
2.10
) in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}
if:

if
b\in L^{1}(0,T)
,
G(t,x,p)\in C([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M})
and
G
satisfies for some
\delta>0
,
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times[0,T]
,
\psi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{M}\times(0,T))

\begin{array}[]{c}-b(t)+G(t,x,p)\leq H(t,x,p)\quad(resp.\geq)\\
\\
\mbox{ for }|x-x_{0}|\leq\delta\>,|p-D_{x}\psi(x_{0},t_{0})|\leq\delta\mbox{ and a.e. }|t-t_{0}|\leq\delta\\
\end{array}

and if
\displaystyle u(x,t)-\big(\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds+\psi(x,t)\big)
has a local maximum (resp. minimum) point in
\mathbb{R}^{M}\times(0,T)
at
(x_{0},t_{0})
then

\psi_{t}(x_{0},t_{0})+G(t_{0},x_{0},D_{x}\psi(x_{0},t_{0}))\leq 0\quad(resp.\geq 0)\>.

A locally bounded continuous function
u
is a (Is-tm) solution of (
2.10
) if it is both a sub and a super-solution and verifies
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
in
\mathbb{R}
.

With these two definitions in hand, it is natural to define continuous Flux Limited t-Measurable (FL-tm) solution as follows.

Definition 2.3
.

Flux Limited t-measurable (FL-tm)

A continuous bounded function
u:\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a (FL-tm) subsolution (resp. supersolution) of (
2.9
) in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
if the following holds.

Let
b\in L^{1}(0,T)
,
\psi\in PC^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
and
(x_{0},t_{0})
be a local maximum (resp. minimum) point in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
of

\displaystyle u(x,t)-\big(\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds+\psi(x,t)\big)
.

Case 1: If
x_{0}=0
(on the junction).
Let
G_{0}\in C([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega_{1}}\times\overline{\Omega_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfies for some
\delta>0
,

\begin{array}[]{c}-b(t)+G_{0}(t,x_{1},x_{2},(p_{1},p_{2}))\leq\max\{A(t),H_{1}^{+}(t,x_{1},p_{1}),H_{2}^{-}(t,x_{2},p_{2})\}\quad(resp.\geq)\\
\\
\mbox{ for $|p_{1}-\psi_{x}(0^{-},t_{0})|\leq\delta$, $|p_{2}-\psi_{x}(0^{+},t_{0})|\leq\delta$, $|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\leq\delta$, and a.e. $|t-t_{0}|\leq\delta$ }\\
\end{array}

then

\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})+G_{0}(t_{0},0,0,(\psi_{x}(0^{-},t_{0}),\psi_{x}(0^{+},t_{0})))\leq 0\quad(resp.\geq 0)\>.\\
\end{array}

Case 2: If
x_{0}\in\Omega_{i}
for
i=1,2
(away from the junction).
Let
G_{i}\in C([0,T]\times\Omega_{i}\times\mathbb{R})
satisfies for some
\delta>0
,

\begin{array}[]{c}-b(t)+G_{i}(t,x,p)\leq H_{i}(t,x,p)\quad(resp.\geq)\\
\\
\mbox{for $|p-\psi_{x}(x_{0},t_{0})|\leq\delta$, $|x-x_{0}|\leq\delta$, and a.e. $|t-t_{0}|\leq\delta$ }\\
\end{array}

then

\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{t}(x_{0},t_{0})+G_{i}(t_{0},x_{0},\psi_{x}(x_{0},t_{0}))\leq 0\quad(resp.\geq 0)\>.\\
\end{array}

Moreover, a continuous bounded function
u
is a (FL-tm) solution of (
2.9
) if it is both a sub and a super-solution of (
2.9
) in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
and verifies
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
in
\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 2.4
.

It is clear that this definition generalizes both (Is-tm) and (FL-tc) definitions. Indeed, if there are not junctions, i.e.
H_{1}\equiv H_{2}
, Definition
2.3
coincides with Definition
2.2
while in the case of both
t
-continuous Hamiltonians and flux limiter, with
A(\cdot)\geq A_{0}(\cdot)
, we recover Definition
2.1
.

Remark 2.5
.

Comparing this definition with the condition introduced in the Introduction for the model problem equation (
1.2
) we see that when the test functions are touching at the junction we need to introduce the function
G_{0}
that depends also on the space variables
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\overline{\Omega_{1}}\times\overline{\Omega_{2}}
.

This is linked to the measurability
t
dependence in Hamiltonians and not on the
x
dependence as one can expect. Indeed, if one consider the case Hamiltonians depending only in space and gradient variable, i.e.
H_{i}=H_{i}(x,p)
, it suffices to consider, in Definition

, continuous
G_{0}=G_{0}(t,p_{1},p_{2})
verifying

\begin{array}[]{c}-b(t)+G_{0}(t,(p_{1},p_{2}))\leq\max\{A(t),H_{1}^{+}(0,p_{1}),H_{2}^{-}(0,p_{2})\}\quad(resp.\geq)\\
\end{array}

for
|p_{1}-\psi_{x}(0^{-},t_{0})|\leq\delta
,
|p_{2}-\psi_{x}(0^{+},t_{0})|\leq\delta
and a.e.
|t-t_{0}|\leq\delta
.

3.
On comparison results

With the aim of giving a comparison result that can be easily extended to more general Hamiltonians and geometries, we give a proof based on the same ideas as in
[
9
]
by the author and F. Rampazzo (this approach is inspired by the proof of the stability result proved for (Is-tm) solutions in the original work
[
16
, Proposition 7.1]
).

As already done in the introduction, we first explain the key construction in the simple framework of the ODE:

(3.1)

u^{\prime}(t)+H(t)=0\mbox{ for }t\in(0,T)\mbox{ with }H(t)\in L^{1}(0,T).

Suppose there exists a sequence of continuous functions
H_{n}\in C^{0}(0,T)
such that
H_{n}\rightarrow H
in
L^{1}(0,T)
and therefore that comparison results are classically known for the corresponding ODE.

The key idea is that if
u
is a (Is-tm)-subsolution as in Definition
2.2
, we can construct a sequence
(u_{n})
of classical viscosity subsolution of
u^{\prime}(t)+H_{n}(t)=0
that converges to
u
pointwise, by setting

u_{n}(t):=u(t)-\int_{0}^{t}|H_{n}(s)-H(s)|\,ds.

Indeed, for any test function
\psi\in C^{1}(0,T)
and
t_{0}
point of maximum of
u_{n}-\psi
in
(0,T)
,
t_{0}
is also a maximum point of
u(t)-\big(\int_{0}^{t}|H_{n}(s)-H(s)|\,ds+\psi(t)\big)
. Thus applying Definition
2.2
with
b(s)=|H_{n}(s)-H(s)|
and
G(t)=H_{n}(t)
we obtain that
\psi^{\prime}(t_{0})+H_{n}(t_{0})\leq 0
.

With similar arguments, if
v
is a (Is-tm)-supersolution as in Definition
2.2
, we can construct

v_{n}(t):=v(t)+\int_{0}^{t}|H_{n}(s)-H(s)|\,ds

that is a classical viscosity supersolution of
v^{\prime}(t)+H_{n}(t)=0
in
(0,T)
.

Since by construction
u_{n}\rightarrow u
and
v_{n}\rightarrow v
, it is clear that one can obtain comparison results for the
t
- measurable case once a comparison result is know for the continuous case.

Exploiting this idea our result will be then based on one hand on the corresponding known results in the cases of Flux Limited
t
-continuous solutions and on the other on the possibility to construct a
good
approximation of the Hamiltonians and the flux limiter
A
. Roughly speaking, we will need to approximate by a sequences of
t
-continuous functions converging in
L^{1}(0,T)
somehow uniformly w.r.t. the other variables (see (
3.5
)-(
3.6
) in the proof of Theorem
3.1
and Remark
3.3
). As discussed in
[
9
, Section 4.1]
a quite general assumption that guarantees such an approximation is the following:

(AP)
For every compact subset
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
there exist
h\in L^{\infty}(0,T)
and a
t
-integrable modulus
\omega
such that, for
i=1,2
,

(3.2)

|H_{i}(t,x,p)-H_{i}(s,y,q)|\leq|h(s)-h(t)|+\omega(t,|x-y|+|p-q|)+\omega(s,|x-y|+|p-q|)\>

for any
(x,p),(y,q)\in Q
and
t,s\in[0,T]
.

Our comparison result, Theorem
3.1
below, is proved under the assumption of Section
2
. This is motivated by the aim of applying the corresponding comparison result in
[
14
, Theorem 5.8]
for (FL-tc) solutions. Indeed, if we assume
A
and
H_{i}
to be
t
-continuous,
(
2.1
)-(
2.8
) trivially imply (H0)-(H6) and (A0)-(A2) in
[
14
, Section 5.3]
. Moreover, one can verify that (
2.1
)-(
2.8
) also implies assumptions (GA-Conv)-(GA-G-FL) in the more general comparison result given in
[
5
, Theorem 14.2]
, for
t
- continuous case Hamiltonians and Flux-limiter.

We observe also that away from the junction
\{0\}
each Hamiltonian verifies assumption (
2.11
), therefore, without junctions, we are in the framework of comparison results given in
[
16
, Theorem 8.1]
or
[

Theorem 3.1
.

(Comparison result)

Assume the Hamiltonians
H_{i}
satisfies the regularity assumptions (
2.2
)-(
2.5
),
the convexity hypothesis (
2.6
) and fulfill the approximability condition (
3.2
).
Let
A
be a flux limiter verifying (
2.7
)-(
2.8
).

Let
u
and
v
be a (FL-tm)-subsolution and a (FL-tm)-supersolution of (
2.9
), respectively.
Let us assume that they are locally Lipschitz continuous in
x
uniformly w.r.t.
t\in[0,T]
, and that there exist

If

Then,

Proof.
This proof is divided 3 steps: we start by localizing the proof, we then construct the sequences of approximating sub and super solutions in a ball

and we end by detailing how to exploit the corresponding result in the continuous case to conclude.

Step 1 (localization).

Set, for

Arguing similar to classical framework (see e.g.
[
5
, Section 2.2]
or
[
14

), we can prove that
\exists R=R(\alpha,\eta)>0

(3.3)

and that fix
\eta>0

\alpha=\alpha(\eta)>0
such that
\tilde{u}_{\alpha,\eta}
is a (FL-tm)-subsolution of (
2.9
), for any
\alpha\leq\alpha(\eta)
. Thus, we have

(3.4)

\tilde{u}_{\alpha,\eta}(x,t)<v(x,t)\>\mbox{ on }\partial B_{R}\times(0,T)\quad\mbox{ for }\eta>0\>,0<\alpha\leq\alpha(\eta)\mbox{ and }R=R(\alpha(\eta),\eta).

Moreover, by construction,
\tilde{u}_{\alpha,\eta}(x,0)<v(x,0)
in
\mathbb{R}
.

Step 2 (construction of
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
).

Fix, now

as in (
3.4
) and

, with

and

in

, respectively. Thanks to assumption (
3.2
), there exist
(H_{i,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in C^{0}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}_{i}\times\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
,

such that

(3.5)

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{x\in\overline{B_{R,i}},\/|p|\leq K}|H_{i,n}(t,x,p)-H_{i}(t,x,p)|dt=0\end{array}

where we set
B_{R,i}=B_{R}\cap\Omega_{i}
.
Moreover, by hypothesis (
2.8
) there exists a sequence of functions
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that

(3.6)

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle A_{n}\in C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}),\quad A_{n}\rightarrow A\mbox{ in }L^{1}(0,T).\\
\end{array}

Let

(3.7)

k_{n}(t):=\max\{k^{0}_{n}(t),k^{1}_{n}(t),k^{2}_{n}(t)\}

where

(3.8)

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle k^{0}_{n}(t):=\sup_{|p_{1}|,|p_{2}|\leq K}\left|\max\{A(t),H_{1}^{+}(t,0,p_{1}),H_{2}^{-}(t,0,p_{2})\}-\max\{A_{n}(t),H_{1,n}^{+}(t,0,p_{1}),H_{2,n}^{-}(t,0,p_{2})\}\right|\\
\\
\displaystyle k^{i}_{n}(t):=\sup_{x\in\overline{B_{R,i}},\>|p|\leq K}\left|H_{i}(t,x,p)-H_{i,n}(t,x,p)\right|\>,\quad i=1,2\end{array}

and define

u_{n}(x,t):=\tilde{u}_{\alpha,\eta}(x,t)-\int_{0}^{t}k_{n}(s)\>ds\mbox{ and }v_{n}(x,t):=v(x,t)+\int_{0}^{t}k_{n}(s)\>ds.

Key points of this construction are:
k_{n}
is a sequence of
L^{1}
-functions such that
k_{n}\rightarrow 0
in
L^{1}(0,T)
and, for any test function
\psi\in PC^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
touching
u_{n}
or
v_{n}
from above or from below at
(0,t)
(resp.
(x,t)
,
x\in\overline{B_{R}}
) one has
\max\{|\psi_{x}(0^{+},t)|,|\psi_{x}(0^{-},t)|\}\leq K
, (resp.
|\psi_{x}(x,t)|\leq K
).

Step 2-(i).
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{n}
is a (FL-tc) subsolution of (
2.9
)
n
in

Let

and

be a local maximum point of

in

.
By construction

is a local maximum point of

Let us consider two cases depending on

being on the junction or not.

If

If

0\in\mathbb{A}_{0}\>.

The cost function.
Let
l_{0}\in L^{\infty}([0,T])
and
A_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
we set

(4.9)

A(t):=\max(-l_{0}(t),A_{0})\in L^{\infty}([0,T];[A_{0},|A_{0}|+\parallel l_{0}\parallel_{\infty}]).

Let
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, for any
(\gamma,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{T}}_{0,\gamma_{0}}^{t,x}
we consider the cost

J((x,t);(\gamma,\alpha)):=u_{0}(\gamma_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}(l_{1}(s,\gamma(s),\alpha_{1}(s))\mathbf{I}_{I_{1}}(s)+l_{2}(s,\gamma(s),\alpha_{1}(s))\mathbf{I}_{I_{2}}(s)-A(s)\mathbf{I}_{I_{0}}(s))\>ds\>.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be bounded and Lipschitz continuous. For
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]
, the value function is

u(x,t):=\inf_{\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}\>\inf_{\gamma\in{\mathcal{T}}_{0,\gamma_{0}}^{t,x}}J((x,t);(\gamma,\alpha))=\inf_{\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}\>\inf_{\gamma\in{\mathcal{T}}_{0,\gamma_{0}}^{t,x}}\left\{u_{0}(\gamma_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}L(\tau,\gamma(\tau),\alpha(\tau))\>d\tau\right\}

where we set

L(t,x,\alpha):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}l_{1}(t,x,\alpha_{1}(t))&\mbox{if}&x\in\Omega_{1}\\
l_{2}(t,x,\alpha_{2}(t))&\mbox{if}&x\in\Omega_{2}\\
-A(t)&\mbox{ if}&x=0\>.\\
\end{array}\right.

It is easily seen that a standard
Dynamic Programming Principle (DPP)
holds:
for each
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]
,
0<s<t
,

(4.10)

u(x,t)=\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\>\inf_{(\gamma,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{T}}_{s,z}^{t,x}}\left\{u(z,s)+\int_{s}^{t}L(\tau,\gamma(\tau),\alpha(\tau))\>d\tau\right\}\>.

With similar arguments as in the continuous case we can derive from the DPP a regularity result for the value function. Precisely:

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume (
4.1
), (
4.2
), (
4.3
), (
4.4
), (
4.5
), (
4.6
), (
4.7
) and (
4.9
).

Then, the value function in Definition
4.1
is a bounded Lipschitz continuous function in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
.

Proof.

One easily has that the value function is bounded, i.e.

|u(x,t)|\leq(2L+\bar{A})T+\parallel u_{0}\parallel_{\infty}\mbox{ for all }(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)\>,

where
\displaystyle L:=\max(\parallel l_{1}\parallel_{\infty},\parallel l_{2}\parallel_{\infty})
and
\bar{A}:=|A_{0}|+\parallel l_{0}\parallel_{\infty}
.

Moreover, since controllability assumptions allow constant trajectories, by DPP

|u(x,t)-u(x,s)|\leq(2L+\bar{A})|t-s|\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\>,t,s\in(0,T).

Let us fix now any
x,y\in\mathbb{R},x\not=y
, and let
\eta=:=\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\delta
, with
\delta>0
given in (
4.6
).

For
t\in[0,T]
we take
s:=t-\frac{|y-x|}{\delta}<t
and we aim to construct an admissible trajectory that goes from
y
at time
s
to
x
at time
t
. If
x,y
are both in the same
\Omega_{i}
this is standard. If this is not the case, the construction is possible thanks to the choice of
\eta
, the normal controllability assumption (
4.6
) and a measurable selection theorem (e.g. Corollary of Proposition 1 in
[
23
]
).

Therefore, using this trajectory in the DPP we have:

u(x,t)-u(y,t)\leq u(x,t)-u(y,s)+u(y,s)-u(y,t)\leq\int_{s}^{t}L(\tau,\gamma(\tau),a)d\tau+u(y,s)-u(y,t)

thus

u(x,t)-u(y,t)\leq(2L+\bar{A})|t-s|+(2L+\bar{A})|t-s|=2(2L+\bar{A})\frac{|y-x|}{\delta}

inverting the roles of
x
and
y
one can conclude that
u
is Lipschitz continuous.

\Box

We define the following
Hamiltonians

(4.11)

H_{i}(t,x,p):=\sup_{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{A}_{i}}\left\{f_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})p-l_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})\right\}\mbox{ for }i=1,2\>.

Standard arguments ensures that both

fulfill regularity conditions (
2.2
)-(
2.5

. Moreover, also thanks to normal controllability, their nondecreasing and nonincreasing part can be characterized in terms of subset of controls. The precise result is the following (we will omit the proof because the
t
- measurability plays not real role here, thus this is analogous to the continuous case, see e.g.
[

, Appendix]
,
[

or
[

).

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume (
4.1
)-(
4.6
).
Then, the Hamiltonians
H_{i}
in (
4.11
) fulfill (
2.2
)-(
2.6
).

Moreover, the nondecreasing and nonincreasing part of
H_{i}
are given by

\displaystyle H_{i}^{-}(t,x,p)=\sup_{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{A}_{i},f_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})\leq 0}\left\{f_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})p-l_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{A}_{i},f_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})<0}\left\{f_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})p-l_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})\right\}

and

\displaystyle\quad H_{i}^{+}(t,x,p)=\sup_{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{A}_{i},f_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})\geq 0}\left\{f_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})p-l_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{A}_{i},f_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})>0}\left\{f_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})p-l_{i}(t,x,\alpha_{i})\right\}.

We are finally ready to prove our existence result: in Theorem

is a (FL-tm) solution of (

Remark 4.4

Here we highlight two particular cases that we consider interesting.

(i)

If dynamics and running costs are assumed to be independent of

and

Before detailing the proof of

Consider any admissible trajectory arriving at time

\{0\}

(\gamma,\alpha)\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\tau,\gamma(\tau)}^{t_{0},0}
with

For any

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\psi(0,t_{0})-\psi(\gamma(\tau),\tau)-\int_{\tau}^{t_{0}}L(s,\gamma(s),\alpha)\>ds=\displaystyle o(t_{0}-\tau)+\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})(t_{0}-\tau)+\\

Step 1 (Subsolution).

Let

,
\psi\in PC^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))

(x_{0},t_{0})

\displaystyle u(x,t)-\big(\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds+\psi(x,t)\big)

Consider

.

Thanks to the DPP, there exists
\eta>0
such that for
\tau<t_{0}
,

\int_{\tau}^{t_{0}}b(s)ds+\psi(x_{0},t_{0})-\psi(\gamma(\tau),\tau)-\int_{\tau}^{t}L(s,\gamma(s),\alpha(s))\>ds\leq 0\>.

We need to distinguish two cases depending if
x_{0}
is or not on the junction.

Case 1: If

Let

(4.14)

-b(t)+G_{i}(t,x,p)\leq H(t,x,p)\mbox{ for }|p-\psi_{x}(x_{0},t_{0})|\leq\delta\>,|x-x_{0}|\leq\delta\mbox{ a.e. }|t-t_{0}|\leq\delta.

Our aim is to prove that

We consider now any admissible trajectory with constant control

Taking the supremum over all

\int_{\tau}^{t_{0}}b(s)ds+\int_{\tau}^{t_{0}}\left[\psi_{t}(\gamma(s),s)+H_{i}(s,\gamma(s),\psi_{x}(\gamma(s),s))\right]\>ds\leq 0

Conclusion

t_{0}-\tau

Case 2: If
x_{0}=0

Let
G_{0}\in C([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega_{1}}\times\overline{\Omega_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})

-b(s)+G_{0}(s,x_{1},x_{2},(p_{1},p_{2}))\leq\max\{A(s),H_{1}^{-}(s,x_{1},p_{1}),H_{2}^{+}(s,x_{2},p_{2})\}

|p_{2}-\psi_{x}(0^{+},t_{0})|\leq\delta

We consider now in (

(A)
For any constant control

(B)

(C)
Since

By (

Step 2 (Supersolution).

Let
\psi\in PC^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
,
b\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
(x_{0},t_{0})
be a local minimum point in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
of

\displaystyle u(x,t)-(\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds+\psi(x,t))

By DPP, for any
\varepsilon>0

Case 1: If

for

Let

-b(s)+G_{0}(s,x_{1},x_{2},(p_{1},p_{2}))\geq\max\{A(s),H_{1}^{-}(s,x_{1},p_{1}),H_{2}^{+}(s,x_{2},p_{2})\}

Thus

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{s^{\varepsilon}_{k}}^{s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}}(\psi_{1})_{t}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),s)+(\psi_{1})_{x}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),s)b_{1}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{1}(s))-l_{1}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{1}(s))\>ds\\
\displaystyle=\int_{s^{\varepsilon}_{k}}^{s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}}\psi_{t}(0,t)+(\psi_{1})_{x}(0,t)b_{1}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{1}(s))-l_{1}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{1}(s))\>ds+o(s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}-s^{\varepsilon}_{k})\\
\displaystyle=\int_{s^{\varepsilon}_{k}}^{s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}}\big[\psi_{t}(0,t)+(\psi_{1})_{x}(0,t)b_{1}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\bar{\alpha_{1}}(s))-l_{1}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\bar{\alpha_{1}}(s))\big]\>ds+o(s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}-s^{\varepsilon}_{k})\\
\displaystyle\leq\int_{s^{\varepsilon}_{k}}^{s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}}H_{1}^{-}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),(\psi_{1})_{x}(0,t))\>ds+\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})(s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}-s^{\varepsilon}_{k})+o(s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}-s^{\varepsilon}_{k})\>.\end{array}

Therefore, using that by construction
\displaystyle\int_{I_{1}}=\sum_{k}\int_{s^{\varepsilon}_{k}}^{s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}}
we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{I_{1}}(\psi_{1})_{t}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),s)+(\psi_{1})_{x}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),s)b_{1}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{1}(s))-l_{1}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{1}(s))\>ds\\
\displaystyle\leq\int_{I_{1}}H_{1}^{-}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),(\psi_{1})_{x}(0,t))\>ds+\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})|I_{1}|+o(t_{0}-\tau).\\
\end{array}

In the case of
I_{2}\not=\emptyset
, we can write

\{s\in(\tau,t_{0})\>:\>\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s)<0\}=\bigcup_{k}\>\left]s^{\varepsilon}_{k},s^{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\right[

and arguing similarly obtain

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{I_{2}}(\psi_{2})_{t}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),s)+(\psi_{2})_{x}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),s)b_{2}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{2}(s))-l_{2}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{2}(s))\>ds\\
\displaystyle\leq\int_{I_{2}}H_{2}^{+}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),(\psi_{2})_{x}(0,t_{0}))\>ds+\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})|I_{2}|+o(t_{0}-\tau)\\
\end{array}

Note that, since
t_{0}\in I_{0}

\begin{array}[]{r}\displaystyle\int_{I_{0}}(\psi_{t}(0,s)+A(s))\>ds\displaystyle\leq\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})|I_{0}|+\int_{I_{0}}A(s)ds+o(t_{0}-\tau)\>.\\
\end{array}

Since
I_{0}\cup I_{1}\cup I_{2}=(t-\tau)
, we can use the above estimates in (
4.12
)-(
4.19
) and conclude
that

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle-\varepsilon(t_{0}-\tau)&\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tau}^{t_{0}}b(s)ds+\psi(0,t_{0})-\psi(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(\tau),\tau)-\int_{\tau}^{t_{0}}L(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(s))\>ds\\
&\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tau}^{t_{0}}b(s)ds+\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})(t_{0}-\tau)+\int_{I_{2}}H_{2}^{+}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),(\psi_{2})_{x}(0,t_{0}))\>ds+\\
&\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{I_{1}}H_{1}^{-}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),(\psi_{1})_{x}(0,t_{0}))\>ds+\int_{I_{0}}A(s)ds+o(t_{0}-\tau)\leq\\
&\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tau}^{t_{0}}G_{0}(s,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s),(\psi_{1})_{x}(0,t_{0}),(\psi_{2})_{x}(0,t_{0}))ds+\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})(t_{0}-\tau)+o(t_{0}-\tau)\end{array}

where we also used (
4.21
). Dividing by
(t_{0}-\tau)
and letting

\psi_{t}(0,t_{0})+G_{0}(t_{0},0,0,(\psi_{1})_{x}(0,t_{0}),(\psi_{2})_{x}(0,t_{0}))\geq 0\>.

5.
On possible generalizations

In this section we aim to discuss some generalizations of Definition
2.3
and comparison results Theorem

Beyond the construction an approximating sequence, the key ingredient in the proof of the comparison principle is the holding of a comparison result for the approximating equations (
2.9
)
n

3.1

Let us now discuss how to exploit this idea in some natural generalisation.

Non-convex Hamiltonians.

The theory of Flux-limited solution has been developed in

directly in the case of quasi-convex and coercive Hamiltonians.
Therefore
[

1.2

[

a comparison result assuming Hamiltonians to be only continuous and coercive and continuous Flux limiter. Based on

continuous and coercive.

Hamiltonian depending also on the

Result can be easily extended to Hamiltonians continuously depending also on
