A Diagnostic Pitfall of Primary Aldosteronism Presenting as Recurrent Quadriparesis: A Case Report
Authors: Javed Shakir, Javeria Benyamin, Muhammad Usman Iqbal, Abdullah Sohail, Aafaq Mazhar, Hafiz Muhammad Abdullah, Erum Habib, Naseeb Danaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTQuadriparesis refers to weakness affecting all four limbs. While most cases are neurogenic in origin, stemming from central or peripheral nervous system pathology, non‐neurogenic causes are less common and often under‐recognized. These include systemic or metabolic conditions that secondarily impair neuromuscular function. A 37‐year‐old woman experienced intermittent episodes of quadriparesis over 2 years. Despite multiple medical evaluations, her symptoms were repeatedly attributed to generalized weakness, leading to delayed diagnosis and ineffective treatment. Comprehensive diagnostic workup revealed persistent hypokalemia of 3.2–3.4 and a lowest recorded value of 2.1 mmol/L with elevated aldosterone levels, that is, 100 ng/dL and suppressed renin activity, consistent with primary aldosteronism. Cross‐sectional imaging identified a right adrenal adenoma. The patient underwent successful laparoscopic adrenalectomy and was discharged in stable condition within 1 week. On follow‐up, she demonstrated complete resolution of symptoms and normalization of serum potassium levels. This case illustrates an uncommon presentation of primary aldosteronism manifesting as recurrent quadriparesis and highlights the importance of considering endocrine and metabolic etiologies in patients with episodic or unexplained limb weakness. Early recognition of potentially reversible causes, along with a multidisciplinary approach involving endocrinology, radiology, and surgery, may facilitate timely diagnosis and favorable clinical outcomes. Clinicians should consider non‐neurogenic causes of quadriparesis, particularly in patients presenting with hypokalemia or resistant hypertension, even when classic endocrine features are not prominent.Keywords: case report, hypokalemia, primary aldosteronism, quadriparesis

Introduction

Quadriparesis, also known as tetraparesis, is characterized by simultaneous weakness of all four limbs and is often attributed to neurogenic causes such as spinal cord lesions or neuropathies. However, metabolic and endocrine disorders, including hypokalemia and hyperaldosteronism, can also present with similar symptoms. Hypokalemic periodic paralysis (HKPP) is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by recurrent attacks of skeletal muscle weakness associated with hypokalemia and can become relevant in such patients [1].

Primary aldosteronism, characterized by excessive aldosterone production, leads to hypertension and hypokalemia. This condition, though accounting for a significant subset of secondary hypertension cases, is often under‐diagnosed due to its varied clinical presentations, which can include muscle weakness and periodic paralysis. Diagnosing primary aldosteronism requires a high index of suspicion and specific biochemical testing.

We present the case of a 37‐year‐old female with recurrent episodes of quadriparesis over 2 years, who was ultimately diagnosed with primary aldosteronism due to an adrenal adenoma. This case underscores the need to consider endocrine disorders in the differential diagnosis of quadriparesis and the importance of a thorough diagnostic approach.

Case Presentation

Chief Complaint and History

A 37‐year‐old married female presented to the emergency department with severe weakness in all four limbs. Her history dated back 2 years when she first developed generalized muscle weakness associated with cramps that progressively rendered all limbs immobile within 4–5 h. The condition was not associated with numbness, fecal or urinary incontinence, or other aggravating factors. No specific pattern or diurnal variation was observed. These episodes recurred every 2–3 months, leading to six hospitalizations over 2 years. She was frequently misdiagnosed with generalized muscle weakness and discharged with various supplements or occasionally treated for hypokalemia with transient symptom improvement. Concurrently, she was diagnosed with hypertension through a 3 day monitoring window period with values above 130/80 mmHg. This was inadequately controlled by previous medication history of 5 mg amlodipine and subsequently switched to 100 mg spironolactone once daily, achieving moderate blood pressure control. No hypertensive complications developed.

Physical Examination

On general physical examination, the patient was fully conscious and well‐oriented to time, place, and person with no visible signs of pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, edema, or palpable lymphadenopathy. She remained cooperative throughout the examination. Blood pressure was 150/90 mmHg on antihypertensive medication. Pulse rate was regular at 90 beats per minute with normal volume and no radio‐radial or radio‐femoral delay. Respiratory rate was 22 breaths per minute with oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. She was afebrile with a blood glucose level of 119 mg/dL.

Neurological examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15, intact cranial nerves, negative signs of meningeal irritation and cerebellar dysfunction, normal gag reflex, and normal eye movements. Motor examination revealed diminished reflexes with power of 3/5 in all limbs. Cardiovascular, respiratory, and gastrointestinal examinations were unremarkable.

Differential Diagnosis

Extensive preliminary workup was performed to evaluate the following differential diagnoses: hypokalemic periodic paralysis, hyperaldosteronism, 11β‐hydroxylase congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), Cushing syndrome, and Liddle syndrome.

Investigation Results

Laboratory Investigations

Complete blood count and renal function tests were within normal limits. Serum electrolytes revealed low potassium levels. Serial serum bicarbonate values showed metabolic alkalosis. Dexamethasone suppression testing demonstrated appropriate cortisol suppression, with post‐dexamethasone serum cortisol measuring 1.6 ng/dL effectively excluding hypercortisolism.

Imaging Studies

Ultrasound Abdomen and Pelvis

Revealed mildly enlarged liver (17.2 cm) with normal echotexture. Both kidneys were normal in size and shape with no evidence of calculus, hydronephrosis, renal mass, or abnormal cortical thickness.

Renal Artery Doppler

No evidence of renal artery stenosis.

CT Abdomen and Pelvis Without Contrast

An oval‐shaped, well‐defined soft tissue density lesion measuring 33 × 23 mm was identified in the right adrenal gland with no internal calcification. The average Hounsfield unit value was 8.9 HU (< 10 HU), consistent with a benign adenoma. The left adrenal gland appeared normal (Figure 1).

Management

Diagnostic Approach

In the setting of hypokalemia, urinary potassium levels were measured to distinguish between renal and extrarenal potassium loss. A urinary potassium concentration of 29 mmol/L indicated ongoing renal potassium wasting, prompting further endocrinological evaluation. Subsequent specific investigations were performed to narrow the differential diagnosis. Spironolactone management was discontinued 4 weeks prior to the aldosterone‐renin ratio (ARR) testing to minimize interference with renin and aldosterone measurements in accordance with endocrine screening recommendations. The blood pressure was temporarily managed using Verapamil, an alternate calcium channel blocker. Renin was measured as plasma renin activity (PRA). The aldosterone‐renin ratio (ARR) was calculated using plasma aldosterone concentration divided by PRA, in accordance with current endocrine guidelines. Serum aldosterone was markedly elevated at 100 ng/dL (reference range: 3.1–35.4 ng/dL), while plasma renin activity was suppressed at 0.2 ng/mL/h (reference range: 0.6–4.3 ng/mL/h), yielding an aldosterone‐renin ratio significantly above the diagnostic threshold. Serum dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA‐SO₄) was reduced at 29.30 μg/dL (reference range: 81–488 μg/dL), and plasma free metanephrines and normetanephrines remained within normal limits, effectively excluding pheochromocytoma. Serum cortisol following overnight dexamethasone suppression testing was appropriately suppressed at 1.6 ng/dL, ruling out hypercortisolism. The constellation of markedly elevated aldosterone with suppressed renin activity effectively excluded Cushing syndrome, Liddle syndrome, and congenital adrenal hyperplasia from the differential diagnosis. Furthermore, the suppressed renin in the context of hyperaldosteronism excluded secondary causes of aldosterone excess. Based on the integration of clinical history, physical examination findings, comprehensive biochemical profiling, and radiological imaging demonstrating a unilateral adrenal adenoma, a definitive diagnosis of primary aldosteronism was established.

Perioperative Management

General perioperative measures included dietary potassium maintenance through intravenous fluid therapy. Specific preoperative management involved blood pressure control with spironolactone and potassium supplementation to maintain serum levels within normal limits. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered. Serum aldosterone, sodium, chloride, and bicarbonate levels were normalized prior to surgery.

Surgical Intervention and Follow‐Up

The patient underwent successful laparoscopic adrenalectomy and was discharged in stable condition within 1 week. Follow‐up investigations demonstrated sustained normalization of serum potassium levels, confirming resolution of the underlying pathology. Complete resolution of quadriparesis was achieved post‐operatively.

Discussion

Fatigue, malaise, and lethargy are common presenting complaints in outpatient and emergency departments with a wide array of manifestations and varying severity. These symptoms may be interpreted differently by patients; some describe weakness as fatigue, others as somnolence, and for some, they carry psychological implications. Clinically, weakness or paresis is defined by a discernible decrease in muscle strength on physical examination. In emergency settings, physicians often consider life‐threatening neurological causes such as stroke or tumors, depending on patient age and the specific pattern of muscle involvement. Outpatient settings prompt consideration of lower motor neuron disorders including poliomyelitis, Guillain‐Barré syndrome, and tetany; auto‐inflammatory conditions such as myasthenia gravis; autoimmune conditions like dermatomyositis; and metabolic conditions involving porphyria and electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia.

These conditions have specific diagnostic patterns and tests that physicians utilize to reach a diagnosis. However, when symptoms present with periodicity, differing severity, and multiple comorbidities including diabetes and hypertension, clinicians are compelled to conduct extensive investigations, broadening the differential diagnosis and making it challenging to focus on a single etiology.

Primary aldosteronism is prevalent in at least 10% of patients with hypertension, yet most cases remain under‐diagnosed. Patients experiencing these symptoms often face significant diagnostic delays, with women typically waiting up to 5 years before receiving an accurate diagnosis [2]. Approximately 10% of patients with primary aldosteronism have resistant hypertension, which can lead to various cardiovascular manifestations, contributing to morbidity and mortality [3]. The prevalence exceeds 20% among patients with resistant hypertension, particularly those under 40 years of age presenting with hypokalemia [3].

Hypokalemia, historically considered a clinically accurate indicator of hyperaldosteronism, proves to be a poor screening marker with sensitivity ranging between 9% and 37% [4]. In our case, the patient was eukalemic on three separate occasions (3.52, 3.58, and 4.54 mmol/L), leading to misdiagnosis as generalized muscle weakness. However, increasing weakness severity and uncontrolled hypertension despite three antihypertensive agents prompted physicians to investigate alternative causes. Renal artery Doppler examination was performed to exclude renal artery stenosis, which was negative.

Screening for primary aldosteronism is significantly underutilized, with only approximately 3% of patients with resistant hypertension undergoing appropriate testing [4]. Misdiagnosis of hyperaldosteronism relates to multiple factors including high subclinical prevalence, complexity of confirmatory testing, absence of standardized screening tools, and high prevalence of atypical clinical presentations [5, 6]. The most widely accepted diagnostic criterion is an elevated aldosterone‐renin ratio (ARR) > 20 ng/dL per ng/mL/h with serum aldosterone > 15 ng/dL [6]. This test has a varying sensitivity; however, it shows a specificity of 93% [7]. Guidelines recommend that patients with normal potassium levels undergo one or more confirmatory tests. However, in patients with subsequent hypokalemia, ARR > 20 ng/dL, and low plasma renin levels, no further testing is required (Figure 2). Elevated aldosterone with suppressed renin due to negative feedback strongly suggests primary aldosteronism. Studies emphasize examining aldosterone and renin levels individually to determine whether aldosterone is autonomously secreted independent of renin [6].

In our patient, aldosterone and renin level testing revealed the expected abnormalities: elevated aldosterone and suppressed renin (high ARR). Renin was measured according to the plasma renin activity. ARR was calculated using plasma aldosterone concentration and plasma renin activity in accordance with current endocrine guidelines. Suppressed PRA (< 1 ng/mL/h) further supported autonomous aldosterone secretion. While ARR is highly sensitive for screening primary aldosteronism [8], false‐positive and false‐negative results can occur with various confounders. This case demonstrates that patients presenting with paresis and resistant hypertension, regardless of age, should be screened earlier with ARR to exclude hyperaldosteronism.

Primary Aldosteronism is increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous and evolving disorder that may present with variable blood pressure phenotypes, including normotensive forms. As demonstrated in previous reports, atypical biochemical presentations may mimic other electrolyte disorders, contributing to diagnostic delay. Therefore, clinicians should maintain a low threshold for screening in patients with episodic weakness of unclear origin, even in the absence of overt resistant hypertension.

Conclusion

This case highlights the critical need for clinicians to consider endocrine causes, such as primary aldosteronism, in patients presenting with recurrent quadriparesis and resistant hypertension. The patient's successful diagnosis and treatment with adrenalectomy underscore the importance of a thorough diagnostic approach including biochemical testing for hyperaldosteronism, particularly when neurological causes are excluded. This case serves as a reminder that early identification and intervention in non‐neurogenic causes of quadriparesis can lead to effective and potentially curative outcomes. A high index of suspicion should be maintained for metabolic and endocrine etiologies in patients with episodic weakness, persistent hypokalemia, or resistant hypertension, even in the absence of classic endocrine features.
