Classification of the ruled surfaces that are critical points of the Dirichlet energy
Rafael López

Abstract
. We classify all ruled surfaces in Euclidean space that are critical points of the Dirichlet energy, obtaining explicit parametrizations of these surfaces.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 53A10; Secondary 53C42

1.
Introduction and statement of the results

Let
\Sigma
be a surface in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
given as the graph
z=u(x,y)
of a smooth function
u
defined in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The Dirichlet energy of
u
is the integral
\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}
. The function
u
is a critical point of the Dirichlet energy for all volume-preserving variations of
\Sigma
if and only if
u
is a critical point of the functional

E[u]=\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}+\Lambda\int_{\Omega}u,

where
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
stands for a Lagrange multiplier. Notice that the integral
\int_{\Omega}u
is the volume enclosed by
\Sigma
with the solid cylinder
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
. The Euler-Lagrange equation of
E
is

u_{xx}+u_{yy}=\frac{\Lambda}{2},

(1)

where
u=u(x,y)
. In the particular case
\Lambda=0
, the function
u
is harmonic. A surface
\Sigma
satisfying locally (
1
) is called a
stationary surface
of the Dirichlet energy. In this paper we want to study stationary surfaces of the functional
E
under some geometric properties using techniques of differential geometry. Here we follow the seminal paper
[
10
]
. Instead to use the function
u
, it is more suitable to write
E[u]
in terms of the surface
\Sigma
. If
\nu=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3})
is the unit normal of
\Sigma
, then
\nu=(-Du,1)/\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}
. Thus the Dirichlet energy becomes

\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)=\int_{\Sigma}\left(\dfrac{1}{\nu_{3}}-\nu_{3}\right)\,d\Sigma,

(2)

where
d\Sigma
is the area element of
\Sigma
. More generally, we can consider functionals of type
\mathcal{F}(X)=\int_{\Sigma}F(\nu)\,d\Sigma
, where
X\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an immersion of
\Sigma
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
F\colon U\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a positive smooth function on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. These functionals are called
anisotropic
because the energy depends on the normal direction
\nu
of
\Sigma
. Anisotropic energies appear in fluid phenomenon when the surface tensions of interfaces depend on
\nu

[
12
]
. Critical points of
\mathcal{F}
for compactly supported volume-preserving variations are characterized by the property that the function
\Lambda
given by

\Lambda:=2HF-\mbox{div}_{\Sigma}DF

is constant, where
DF
is the gradient of
F
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The function
\Lambda
is called the
anisotropic mean curvature
of
\Sigma
. Thus, critical points of
\mathcal{F}
, or stationary surfaces, are surfaces with constant anisotropic mean curvature (CAMC).

Usually, for general anisotropic energies, it is assumed that
F
is elliptic in the sense that the matrix
D^{2}F+F\,\mbox{Id}
is positive definite, where
D^{2}F
is the Hessian of
F
. This allows to define the Wulff shape associated to
\mathcal{F}
as the map
\xi\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by
\xi(\nu)=DF(\nu)+F(\nu)\nu
. This map is the parametrization of a convex surface, which it is an ovaloid if
U=\mathbb{S}^{2}
. We point out that the Dirichlet energy is elliptic and its Wulff shape is the paraboloid
z=x^{2}+y^{2}
, after translations and rescalings in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Notice that the integrand in (
2
) is only defined on a hemisphere of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

We ask for those CAMC surfaces of the Dirichlet energy (
2
) with some particular geometric property. A first example are the rotational surfaces, that is, surfaces that are axially symmetric about an axis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This reduces the equation
\Lambda=ct.
in an ordinary differential equation. Exactly, CAMC surfaces of the Dirichlet energy that are axially symmetric about the
z
-axis are given by

u(x,y)=c_{1}\log(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}})+\frac{\Lambda}{8}(x^{2}+y^{2})+c_{2},\quad c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

When
c_{1}=0
we obtain the Wulff shape and when
c_{2}=0
we have the anisotropic catenoid of the Dirichlet energy. For the interesting reader in the differential-geometric viewpoint of critical points of anisotropic energies, we refer that works of Koiso and Palmer
[
6
,
7
,
9
,
8
]
and references therein. See also the recent papers
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
11
]
.

Other interesting family of surfaces are the ruled surfaces. These surfaces are generated by moving a straight line in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and they can be parametrized by

X\colon I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad X(s,t)=\alpha(s)+t\beta(s),

(3)

where
\alpha\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a regular curve, called the
directrix
which we can suppose to be parametrized by arc-length. The curve
\beta
is a curve on the unit
2
-sphere
\beta\colon I\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
and it indicate the direction of the
rulings
at each point
\alpha(s)
. Moreover, we can assume
\langle\alpha(s),\beta(s)\rangle=0
for all
s\in I
. In case that
\beta
is a constant curve,
\beta(s)=\vec{w}
, the surface is called
cylindrical
.

In this paper, we give a complete classification of the ruled CAMC surfaces of the Dirichlet energy (
2
). Ruled surfaces of cylindrical type are studied separately. We prove in Sect.
3
that any cylindrical CAMC surface is a plane (
\Lambda=0
) or the directrix
\alpha
is a parabola, that is, the surface is a parabolic cylinder (Thm.
3.1
).

For non-cylindrical CAMC surfaces, we have that
\{\beta,\beta^{\prime},\beta\times\beta^{\prime}\}
is an orthonormal frame. Since
\langle\alpha(s),\beta(s)\rangle=0
, there are smooth functions
a,b\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\alpha(s)=a(s)\beta^{\prime}(s)+b(s)\beta(s)\times\beta^{\prime}(s).

(4)

The classification of the ruled non-cylindrical CAMC surfaces is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a ruled non-cylindrical surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
parametrized by (
3
) where
\alpha
is given by (
4
). If the anisotropic mean curvature
\Lambda
of the Dirichlet energy is constant then
\beta
is a great circle of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Moreover, up to a rotation about the
z
-axis and translations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the surface
\Sigma
is one of the following surfaces:

(1)

Case
\Lambda=0
.

(a)

A plane.

(b)

A helicoid. The curve
\beta
is the horizontal equator
\beta(s)=(\cos s,\sin s,0)
and

\begin{split}a(s)&=c_{1}\cos s,\quad c_{1}\not=0,\\
b(s)&=b_{1}\tan(s),\quad b_{1}\not=0.\end{split}

(5)

(c)

The curve
\beta
is the vertical equator
\beta(s)=(\cos s,0,\sin s)
and

\begin{split}a(s)&=c_{1}\cos(s+c_{2}),\quad c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R},c_{1}\not=0,\\
b(s)&=b_{1}\tan(s),\quad b_{1}\not=0.\end{split}

(6)

(d)

The curve
\beta
is a (non-horizontal, non-vertical) great circle of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and

\begin{split}a(s)&=c_{1}\cos s+c_{2}\sin s\\
&-\frac{b_{1}m}{1-m^{2}}\cos s\cot^{-1}(\sqrt{1-m^{2}}\cot s)+\frac{b_{1}}{m\sqrt{1-m^{2}}}\sin s,\\
b(s)&=\frac{b_{1}}{\sqrt{1-m^{2}}}\tan^{-1}(\sqrt{1-m^{2}}\tan s).\end{split}

(7)

Here
c_{1},c_{2},b_{1},m\in\mathbb{R}
with
b_{1}\not=0
,
m\in(0,1)
.

(2)

Case
\Lambda\not=0
. Then
\beta
is the vertical equator
\beta(s)=(\cos s,0,\sin s)
and

\begin{split}a(s)&=c_{1}\cos s+c_{2}\sin s-\frac{\Lambda b_{1}^{2}\cos(2s)}{4\cos s},\quad c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R},\\
b(s)&=b_{1}\tan s,\quad b_{1}\not=0.\end{split}

(8)

The proof of Thm.
1.1
will be carried out in Sect.
4
separating the case
\Lambda=0
(Subsect.
4.1
) from
\Delta\not=0
(Subsect.
4.2
).

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall the calculation of the anisotropic mean curvature of a surface
\Sigma
when the energy functional is of type
\mathcal{F}(X)=\int_{\Sigma}F(\nu_{3})\,d\Sigma
, and next, we particularize to the Dirichlet energy (
2
). A first observation is that the equation
\Lambda=ct.
is preserved by translations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and by dilations (with different constant
\Lambda
), but not by rigid motions in general. This is because the functional
\mathcal{F}
depends on the unit normal
\nu
. However, the equation
\Lambda=ct.
is invariant by rotations about an axis parallel to the
z
-axis.

Let
h\colon T\Sigma\times T\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
be the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
,
h(v,w)=-\langle d\nu(v),w\rangle
,
v,w\in T\Sigma
.
The anisotropic mean curvature
\Lambda
is given by

\Lambda=\frac{h(v_{1},v_{1})}{\mu_{1}}+\frac{h(v_{2},v_{2})}{\mu_{2}},

(9)

where
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is an orthonormal frame of
T\Sigma
and
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are the principal curvatures of the Wulff shape given by

\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}=(1-\nu_{3}^{2})F^{\prime\prime}(\nu_{3})+\frac{1}{\mu_{2}},\quad\frac{1}{\mu_{2}}=F-\nu_{3}F^{\prime}(\nu_{3}).

The principal directions
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are

E_{1}=e_{3}-\nu_{3}\nu,\quad E_{2}=\nu\times E_{1},

where
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
. Here it is understood that
\nu_{3}^{2}\not=1
and because all our results are local, the
\nu_{3}^{2}\not=1
on
\Sigma
. In the particular case of the Dirichlet energy (
2
), we have

\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}=\frac{2}{\nu_{3}^{3}},\quad\frac{2}{\mu_{2}}=\frac{1}{\nu_{3}}.

We use the basis
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
to compute
\Lambda
in (
9
). This basis is orthogonal but not unitary and
|E_{1}|^{2}=|E_{2}|^{2}=1-\nu_{3}^{2}
. From the expression of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, identity (
9
) is

\Lambda\nu_{3}^{3}(1-\nu_{3}^{2})=2\left(h(E_{1},E_{1})+h(E_{2},E_{2})\nu_{3}^{2}\right).

(10)

We compute (
10
) for an arbitrary parametrization
X=X(s,t)
of
\Sigma
. Since
\{X_{s},X_{t}\}
is a basis of
T\Sigma
, let express
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
in coordinates with respect to this basis:

E_{1}=c_{11}X_{s}+c_{12}X_{t},\quad E_{2}=c_{21}X_{s}+c_{22}X_{t}.

(11)

Then

h(E_{1},E_{1})=c_{11}^{2}h(X_{s},X_{s})+2c_{11}c_{12}h(X_{s},X_{t})+c_{12}^{2}h(X_{t},X_{t}),

h(E_{2},E_{2})=c_{21}^{2}h(X_{s},X_{s})+2c_{21}c_{22}h(X_{s},X_{t})+c_{22}^{2}h(X_{t},X_{t}).

Let
g_{ij}
denote the coefficients of the first fundamental form of
X
and let
\nu=\frac{X_{s}\times X_{t}}{|X_{s}\times X_{t}|}
. Then

\begin{split}h(X_{s},X_{s})&=-\langle d\nu(X_{s}),X_{s})\rangle=\frac{\mbox{det}(X_{s},X_{t},X_{ss})}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}:=\frac{h_{11}}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}\\
h(X_{s},X_{t})&=-\langle d\nu(X_{s}),X_{t})\rangle=\frac{\mbox{det}(X_{s},X_{t},X_{st})}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}:=\frac{h_{12}}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}\\
h(X_{t},X_{t})&=-\langle d\nu(X_{t}),X_{t})\rangle=\frac{\mbox{det}(X_{s},X_{t},X_{tt})}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}:=\frac{h_{22}}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}.\end{split}

Then Eq. (
10
) writes as

\begin{split}\Lambda\nu_{3}^{3}(1-\nu_{3}^{2})\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}&=2(c_{11}^{2}h_{11}+2c_{11}\,c_{12}h_{12}+c_{12}^{2}h_{22})\\
&+2\nu_{3}^{2}(c_{21}^{2}h_{11}+2c_{21}\,c_{22}h_{12}+c_{22}^{2}h_{22}).\end{split}

(12)

Immediately, any plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a stationary surface for
\Lambda=0
because the second fundamental vanishes identically. Notice that if
\Sigma
is a horizontal plane, then
\nu_{3}^{2}=1
and we cannot use (
12
).

3.
Stationary cylindrical surfaces

A cylindrical surface
\Sigma
is a ruled surface where the rulings are all parallel. A parametrization of
\Sigma
is

X\colon I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad X(s,t)=\alpha(s)+t\vec{w},

(13)

where
\alpha\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a curve parametrized by arc-length,
|\vec{w}|=1
and
\alpha
is contained in a plane orthogonal to
\vec{w}
. In the following result, we classify all cylindrical CAMC surfaces.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a cylindrical surface. If
\Sigma
is a stationary surface of the Dirichlet energy (
2
), then
\alpha
is a straight-line and
\Sigma
is a plane (
\Lambda
) or
\alpha
is a parabola and
\Sigma
is a parabolic cylinder (
\Lambda\not=0
).

Proof.

A first trivial case is when
\Sigma
is a plane (
\Lambda=0
). From now, we will be discarded this case. Consider the parametrization (
13
). Let
\mathbf{n}
be the unit normal of
\alpha
defined by
\mathbf{n}=\alpha^{\prime\prime}/|\alpha^{\prime\prime}|
, where
\kappa=|\alpha^{\prime\prime}|
is the curvature of
\alpha
. Consider the orientation on
\alpha
such that
\alpha^{\prime}\times\vec{w}=\mathbf{n}
. Since
X_{s}=\alpha^{\prime}
and
X_{t}=\vec{w}
, then
\nu=X_{s}\times X_{t}=\mathbf{n}
. Let write
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
in coordinates with respect to
\{\alpha^{\prime},\vec{w},\mathbf{n}\}
,

e_{3}=e_{11}\alpha^{\prime}+e_{22}\vec{w}+e_{33}\mathbf{n}.

Notice that
e_{33}=\langle e_{3},\nu\rangle=\nu_{3}\not=0
.
Recall that we discard the case
\nu_{3}^{2}=1
identically (horizontal plane).

We compute all terms of (
12
). It is immediate

\begin{split}E_{1}&=e_{11}\alpha^{\prime}+e_{22}\vec{w},\\
E_{2}&=-e_{22}\alpha^{\prime}+e_{11}\vec{w}.\end{split}

Since
h_{11}=\kappa
,
h_{12}=h_{22}=0
and
\nu_{3}=e_{33}
, equation (
12
) is

\Lambda e_{33}^{3}(1-e_{33}^{2})=2\kappa(e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2}e_{33}^{2}).

(14)

Consider a positively oriented (constant) basis
\mathcal{B}=\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
v_{3}=\vec{w}
. Since the curve
\alpha
is included in the
(v_{1},v_{2})
-plane and it is parametrized by arc-length, then there is a smooth function
\theta=\theta(s)
such that

\alpha^{\prime}=\cos\theta v_{1}+\sin\theta v_{2}.

Thus

\mathbf{n}=\alpha^{\prime}\times\vec{w}=\sin\theta v_{1}-\cos\theta v_{2}.

Moreover,
\kappa=\langle\alpha^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{n}\rangle=-\theta^{\prime}
. Let write
e_{3}
in coordinates with respect to
\mathcal{B}
,

e_{3}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=(\cos\varphi\cos\phi,\cos\varphi\sin\phi,\sin\varphi)

(15)

for some
\varphi,\phi\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\begin{split}e_{11}&=a_{1}\cos\theta+a_{2}\sin\theta,\\
e_{22}&=a_{3},\\
e_{33}&=a_{1}\sin\theta-a_{2}\cos\theta.\end{split}

Thus (
14
) becomes

\begin{split}&\frac{\Lambda}{2}(a_{1}\sin\theta-a_{2}\cos\theta)^{3}(1-(a_{1}\sin\theta-a_{2}\cos\theta)^{2})\\
&=-2\theta^{\prime}\left((a_{1}\cos\theta+a_{2}\sin\theta)^{2}+a_{3}^{2}(a_{1}\sin\theta-a_{2}\cos\theta)^{2}\right).\end{split}

(16)

Taking into account the value of
a_{i}
in (
15
), equation (
16
) is

\theta^{\prime}=-\frac{\Lambda}{2}(\cos\varphi)^{3}(\cos(\theta(s)+\phi))^{3}.

(17)

If
\Lambda=0
, then
\kappa=-\theta^{\prime}=0
and
\alpha
is a straight-line and
\Sigma
is a plane. This case was already considered. If
\Lambda\not=0
, equation (
17
) is the expression of the curvature of a parabola when it is parametrized by arc-length. This proves the result.
∎

We give two examples.

Example 3.2
.

Suppose that
\alpha
is contained in a vertical plane which, after a rotation about the
z
-axis, we assume the
xz
-coordinate plane. Then the vector
\vec{w}=\pm(0,1,0)
. Recall that
\vec{w}
is the third vector in the basis
\mathcal{B}
. Let
\mathcal{B}=\{(0,0,1),(1,0,0),(0,1,0)\}
. Let choose
\varphi=\phi=0
. Then (
17
) is

\kappa=-\frac{\Lambda}{2}(\cos\theta)^{3}=\frac{\Lambda}{2}(\cos\theta)^{3}.

If we write the curve
\alpha
as
\alpha(x)=(x,0,z(x))
, then
\kappa=z^{\prime\prime}/(1+z^{\prime 2})^{3/2}
and
\sin\theta=1/(1+z^{\prime 2})^{3/2}
. Thus (
17
) is simply
z^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\Lambda}{2}
. This is according to (
1
) when we assume functions
z=z(x,y)=z(x)
. The solution is the straight-line
z(x)=z_{0}
(
\Lambda=0
) or the parabola
z(x)=\frac{\Lambda}{4}x^{2}+ax+b
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. See Fig.
1
, left.

Example 3.3
.

Let

\mathcal{B}=\{(1,0,0),\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(0,1,-1),\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(0,1,1)\}.

Then
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=(0,-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
. In this case,
\varphi=\pi/4
and
\phi=-\pi/2
. Then (
16
) becomes

\kappa=\frac{\Lambda}{8}(\sin\theta)^{3}.

See Fig.
1
, right.

4.
Non-cylindrical ruled surfaces

Suppose that
\Sigma
is a non-cylindrical ruled surface. Then
\Sigma
is parametrized by (
3
) where
\beta
is not a constant curve. Since
\beta
is a spherical curve, we associate an orthonormal frame
\mathcal{B}=\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
given by

\left\{\begin{split}v_{1}(s)&=\beta(s),\\
v_{2}(s)&=\beta^{\prime}(s),\\
v_{3}(s)&=\beta(s)\times\beta^{\prime}(s).\end{split}\right.

Let
\kappa=\mbox{det}(\beta^{\prime\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime})
be the curvature of
\beta
viewed as a curve of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The corresponding Frenet equations are

\left\{\begin{split}v_{1}^{\prime}(s)&=v_{2}(s),\\
v_{2}^{\prime}(s)&=-v_{1}(s)+\kappa(s)v_{3}(s),\\
v_{3}^{\prime}(s)&=-\kappa(s)v_{2}(s).\end{split}\right.

Notice that if
\kappa\equiv 0
, then
\beta
is a geodesic of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
v_{3}=\beta\times\beta^{\prime}
is constant. In this case,
\beta
is a great circle in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
which it is the intersection of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with the vector plane orthogonal to
v_{3}
.

Since
\alpha
is orthogonal to
\beta=v_{1}
, the expression of
\alpha
in coordinates with respect to
\mathcal{B}
is

\alpha(s)=a(s)v_{2}(s)+b(s)v_{3}(s),

for smooth functions
a=a(s)
and
b=b(s)
. In particular, the parametrization of
\Sigma
writes in coordinates with respect to
\mathcal{B}
is

X(s,t)=(t,a(s),b(s)).

(18)

The unit normal of
\Sigma
is

\nu=\frac{X_{s}\times X_{t}}{|X_{s}\times X_{t}|}=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}\times v_{1}-tv_{3}}{\sqrt{W}},\quad W:=|\alpha^{\prime}\times v_{1}-tv_{3}|^{2}.

We have
X_{ss}=\alpha^{\prime\prime}+t\beta^{\prime\prime}
,
X_{st}=\beta^{\prime}
and
X_{tt}=0
. After some calculations, the coefficients
g_{ij}
of the first fundamental form are

\begin{split}g_{11}&=t^{2}+2(a^{\prime}-\kappa b)t+a^{2}+(a^{\prime}-\kappa b)^{2}+(b^{\prime}+\kappa a)^{2},\\
g_{12}&=a,\\
g_{22}&=1,\\
\mbox{det}(g_{ij})&=W=t^{2}+2(a^{\prime}-\kappa b)t+(a^{\prime}-\kappa b)^{2}+(b^{\prime}+\kappa a)^{2}.\end{split}

On the other hand, the determinants
h_{ij}
are

\begin{split}h_{11}&=-\left((a\kappa+b^{\prime})(-a^{\prime\prime}+a(\kappa^{2}+1)+2\kappa b^{\prime}+b\kappa^{\prime})\right)\\
&-\left(a^{\prime}-b\kappa+t\right)\left(\kappa(2a^{\prime}-b\kappa+t)+a\kappa^{\prime}+b^{\prime\prime}\right),\\
h_{12}&=b^{\prime}+\kappa a,\\
h_{22}&=0\\
\end{split}

We write
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
in coordinates with respect to
\mathcal{B}
,

e_{3}=(e_{11}(s),e_{22}(s),e_{33}(s)).

Since
\beta
is not constant, the coordinates
e_{22}
and
e_{33}
cannot be zero simultaneously. The calculations of
c_{ij}
in (
11
) give

\begin{split}c_{11}&=\frac{e_{22}\left(a^{\prime}-b\kappa+t\right)+e_{33}a\kappa+e_{33}b^{\prime}}{W},\\
c_{12}&=\frac{1}{W}\Big(a\left(e_{22}\left(a^{\prime}-b\kappa+t\right)+b^{\prime}(2e_{11}\kappa+e_{33})\right)+e_{11}(b^{\prime 2}+(a^{\prime}-b\kappa+t)^{2})\\
&+a^{2}\kappa(e_{11}\kappa+e_{33})\Big),\\
c_{21}&=-\frac{e_{11}}{\sqrt{W}},\\
c_{22}&=\frac{e_{22}\left(a^{\prime}-b\kappa+t\right)+a(e_{33}\kappa-e_{11})+e_{33}b^{\prime}}{\sqrt{W}}.\end{split}

Finally,

\nu_{3}=\frac{-e_{33}a^{\prime}+e_{22}a\kappa+e_{22}b^{\prime}+e_{33}b\kappa-e_{33}t}{\sqrt{W}}.

(19)

Inserting all these computations in (
12
), we obtain a polynomial equation on
t
of type

\sum_{n=0}^{5}A_{n}(s)t^{n}=0.

(20)

Therefore, all coefficients
A_{n}
vanish identically. The explicit computations of
A_{n}
can be done after tedious calculations, increasing the degree of difficulty as we go from
n=5
to
n=0
. In this paper we have employed Mathematica for these computations
[
13
]
.

The first coefficient to compute is
n=5
, obtaining

A_{5}=e_{33}^{3}(e_{33}^{2}-1)\Lambda.

We separate the cases
\Lambda=0
(Subsect.
4.1
) and
\Lambda\not=0
(Subsect.
4.2
) .

In many of the next computations, we will use the relation
e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2}+e_{33}^{2}=1
. We will also use the expression (
19
) to assure that
\nu_{3}\not=0

In all cases, the arguments are the following. First we compute
A_{n}
for the biggest
n
. Next, we impose the condition
A_{n}=00
and this will give a discussion of cases. In each one of them, we compute
A_{n-1}
and study equation
A_{n-1}=0
. We repeat the process decreasing the value of
n
until we finish with the coefficient
A_{0}
and the corresponding equation
A_{0}=0
. In some situations, the discussion of
A_{m}=0
for some
m
will imply that the rest of coefficients
A_{m-1},\ldots,A_{0}
are trivially
0
. This means that no more computations can do.

4.1.
Case
\Lambda=0

The degree of Eq. (
20

4

A_{4}=-\kappa(e_{22}^{2}+e_{11}^{2}e_{33}^{2}).

Equation
A_{4}=0
gives two cases.

4.1.1.
Case
\kappa=0
.

In particular,
\beta
is a great circle of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The coefficient
A_{3}
is

A_{3}=(e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2})\left(2e_{11}e_{22}b^{\prime}+(e_{11}^{2}-1)b^{\prime\prime}\right).

Equation
A_{3}=0
gives two cases.

(1)

Case
e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2}=0
. Then
e_{33}=\pm 1
and thus
e_{3}=\pm v_{3}
. Without loss of generality, let
e_{3}=v_{3}=\beta\times\beta^{\prime}
. This means that
\beta
is the horizontal equator of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Now
A_{2}
is trivially
0
and the last two coefficients are

\begin{split}A_{1}&=-b^{\prime 2}b^{\prime\prime},\\
A_{0}&=b^{\prime 2}(b^{\prime}(a+a^{\prime\prime})-a^{\prime}b^{\prime\prime}).\end{split}

Then
A_{1}=0
gives two subcases.

(a)

Subcase
b^{\prime}=0
. Then
b(s)=b_{0}
. From the parametrization (
18
) of
\Sigma
, we have
\langle X,e_{3}\rangle=b_{0}
. This proves that the surface is the horizontal plane of equation
z=b_{0}
. This cases was considered because
\nu_{3}^{2}\equiv 1
.

(b)

Subcase
b^{\prime\prime}=0
and
b^{\prime}\not=0
. Then
b(s)=b_{1}s+b_{0}
with
b_{1}\not=0
. Then
A_{0}=0
gives
a^{\prime\prime}+a=0
. Coming back, we have

h(E_{1},E_{1})=\frac{b_{1}^{3}}{W^{2}}(a+a^{\prime\prime})=0,\quad h(E_{2},E_{2})=0.

Since
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
is an orthonormal basis, we deduce that
H=0
identically in
\Sigma
. Thus
\Sigma
is a minimal surface. Let us observe that we can also compute

h_{11}=b_{1}(a^{\prime\prime}-a)
,
h_{12}=b_{1}
,
h_{22}=0
, together
g_{11}=1
and
g_{12}=-a
. Since the second fundamental form is not identically
0
, then the surface is not a plane. By the classification of the ruled minimal surface, we conclude that
\Sigma
is the helicoid
[
2
]
.

We calculate explicitly this helicoid. The solution of
a^{\prime\prime}+a=0
is a linear combination of the functions
\cos s
and
\sin s
. Thus
a(s)=c_{1}\cos(s+c_{2})
for some
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\beta
is the horizontal equator, then
\beta(s)=(\cos(s+c_{2}),\sin(s+c_{2}),0)
. After a translation in the domain of the variable
s
, we can assume
c_{2}=0
and a vertical translation of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
allows to choose
b_{0}=0
. This gives (
5
). See Fig.
2
, left.

(2)

Case
2e_{11}e_{22}b^{\prime}+(e_{11}^{2}-1)b^{\prime\prime}=0
and
e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2}\not=0
. Notice that
e_{11}^{2}\not=1
: otherwise,
e_{3}=\beta
and
\Sigma
would be a cylindrical surface. Then

b^{\prime\prime}=2b^{\prime}\frac{e_{11}e_{22}}{1-e_{11}^{2}}.

(21)

Now

A_{2}=(e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2})b^{\prime}\left((1-e_{11}^{2})(a+a^{\prime\prime})+2e_{11}e_{33}b^{\prime}\right).

Since
e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2}\not=0
, we discuss two cases from the equation
A_{2}=0
.

(a)

Case
b^{\prime}=0
. Then
b(s)=b_{0}
is a constant function. For this value of
b
, the coefficients
A_{1}
and
A_{0}
are trivially
0
. Now
h_{11}=h_{12}=h_{22}=0
so the second fundamental form is
0
and
\Sigma
is a plane.

(b)

Case
b^{\prime}\not=0
. Then the second parenthesis of
A_{2}
vanishes identically,

(1-e_{11}^{2})(a+a^{\prime\prime})+2e_{11}e_{33}b^{\prime}=0.

(22)

From this identity we get
b^{\prime}
. We have two subcases according whether
e_{33}
is
0
or not. Notice that
e_{11}\not=0
.

(i)

Case
e_{33}=0
. This is equivalent to say that the plane containing the geodesic
\beta
also contains
e_{3}
. Consequently,
\beta
is a vertical equator of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
Since
e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2}=1
,

b^{\prime\prime}=2b^{\prime}\frac{e_{11}}{e_{22}}.

(23)

Moreover, equation (
22
) writes as

e_{22}^{2}b^{\prime}(a+a^{\prime\prime})=0.

From
\nu_{3}\not=0
in (
19
), we know that
e_{22}b^{\prime}\not=0
. Thus
a+a^{\prime\prime}=0
. With this equation,
A_{1}
and
A_{0}
are trivially
0
. We now find the parametrization of the surface. We have

e_{11}=\langle e_{3},\beta\rangle,\quad e_{22}=\langle e_{3},\beta^{\prime}\rangle=e_{11}^{\prime}.

Then
e_{11}^{2}+e_{22}^{2}=1
writes as
e_{11}^{2}+e_{11}^{\prime 2}=1
. By solving this equation, we obtain
e_{11}=\sin(s+c)
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. After a translation in the domain of the variable
s
, let
c=0
. Thus
e_{22}=\cos s
. After a rotation about the
z
-axis, we can assume that
\beta
is included in the
xz
-coordinate plane and thus,
\beta
is parametrized by

\beta(s)=(\cos s,0,\sin s)
. From the expression of
b^{\prime\prime}
in (

b(s)=b_{1}\tan(s)
, with
b_{1}\not=0

yields

for some constants

6

2

(ii)

Subcase
e_{33}\not=0

b^{\prime}=-\frac{(1-e_{11}^{2})(a+a^{\prime\prime})}{2e_{11}e_{33}}.

Then (

Now the rest of coefficients
A_{1}

0

4.1.2.
Case

(1)

Inserting into
A_{2}
