Transition and clinical outcomes among AYA with sickle cell disease
Authors: Chiamaka Nwachukwu, Melody Benton, Maissaa Janbain, Madison Blackstone, Justin Farge, Yue Ming, Vivien Sheehan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransition clinics in sickle cell disease (SCD) were developed to address the gap in care and poor outcomes that frequently occur in adolescents and young adults (AYA) with SCD. Our retrospective study evaluated the outcomes of a multi-disciplinary SCD transition program at the Tulane University Sickle Cell Center of Southern Louisiana.Methods49 patients (39 who participated in the transition clinic, and 10 patients who transferred directly from pediatric to adult care) aged 18–25 with SCD who received care between January 2021 to November 2024 were included in the analysis. Socio-demographic data was collected as well as information on transition metrics, mental health comorbidities, and acute care utilization.ResultsAt the time of analysis, 30.7% (n = 12) of patients in the transition clinic had transferred to adult care. Age ≥ 21, completing high school and attending 5 or more transition visits were significantly associated with successful adult care transfer completion (p < 0.0001, p = 0.017, p = 0.0407). Across both groups, those with an established mental health diagnosis had significantly higher levels of hospital admissions (B = 5.55, SE = 1.67, p = .002). Moreover, compared to those who are in high school, those who completed high school had significantly lower levels of hospital admissions (B = −5.02, SE = 2.26, p = .03). There was no statistically significant difference in acute care utilization, medication compliance, or presence of SCD complications between both groups.ConclusionHigher levels of educational attainment and consistent engagement with transition programming positively impact successful adult care transfer in AYA with SCD. Mental health and wellbeing has a significant impact on morbidity and hospitalizations in AYA with SCD and represents an area for further research and intervention.Keywords: Sickle cell disease, AYA, Transition clinic

Introduction

Advancements in science, new disease-modifying therapies, and interventions have improved survival rates for patients living with SCD. It is now estimated that more than 95% of patients with SCD will survive until their 18th birthday.1 Mortality among patients with SCD during the pediatric period is now much less common. This has been attributed to increased awareness, newborn screening implementation, penicillin prophylaxis, primary stroke prevention, and disease-modifying therapies.2 Bone marrow transplant and the approval of new gene therapy also hold immense potential to drive down mortality and complication rates even further among patients with SCD.

However, as survivorship improves, adolescents and young adults are particularly vulnerable, especially in the period just after leaving pediatric care, with a spike in mortality rates and more frequent complications in the peri-transition period.3, 4 While younger age groups have seen reductions in SCD related mortality by up to 67%, young adults between 20 and 24 have only had a 7% decrease in mortality rates within the same period. An analysis of mortality data of patients with SCD between 1999 and 2009 showed an increase in mortality from 0.6 in the 15–19-year group to 1.4/100,000 in the 20–24-year group, corresponding with the transition period from pediatric to adult care.5 This is consistent with the finding that young adults with SCD between 18 and 25 years are less likely to receive regular care from a hematologist when compared with adolescents (aged 15–17) and younger children.6 This failure in transition results in increased acute care utilization, more frequent hospitalizations, poorer quality of life, and worsened long-term health outcomes.7, 8 Several barriers to effective transition have been identified among this population. They include systemic factors such as: 1) lack of adult providers able/willing to provide specialized sickle cell care, 2) inadequate coordination/communication between adult and pediatric providers, and 3) insurance changes and losses in coverage. Transition is also complicated by patient level factors such as: 1) inadequate caregiver support, 2) competing life demands, 3) limited knowledge of disease and their treatment, and 4) comorbid physical and mental health conditions.9, 10 Failure to successfully transition can accelerate disease progression and lead to poor future health outcomes.

The 2011 Joint Statement Issued by the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Academy of Family Physicians, and the American College of Physicians on transition of care strategies in adolescents and young adults, as well as the development of the creation of the Got Transition/National Center for Healthcare Improvement, marked crucial early steps in rethinking the approach to care of patients with long term chronic illnesses extending into adulthood.8, 11 Specifically, the SICKLE recommendations provide a global perspective on six priorities for the successful transition of patients with SCD to adult care including: skills transfer, increasing self-efficacy, coordination of transition, knowledge transfer, linking to adult services, and evaluating readiness.12 To further address the systemic challenges with transition, the American Society of Hematologists developed a sickle cell specific toolkit to aid healthcare providers in the transition process from pediatric to adult care.13

The Tulane University Sickle Cell Center of Southern Louisiana (SCCSL) was established in 1984, through the combined efforts of Gerald Williams (Director of the New Orleans Sickle Cell Anemia Foundation) and Maeola Jones (pediatric nurse for sickle cell), who had been providing care to both adults and children with SCD since 1979. Their work led to the integration of these two clinics to establish the SCCSL, aimed at providing comprehensive care to individuals with SCD through all the stages of their lives. The center primarily serves individuals from Southern Louisiana. While most of the patient population comes from the Greater New Orleans area, the center supports individuals with SCD throughout the state through research, education, and 24-hour virtual support for physicians across Louisiana.14

The SCCSL currently provides clinical services through the pediatric, adult, and sickle cell transition clinic. The Sickle Cell Transitional Program was established in 2013 and was designed to support adolescents and young adults with the transition to adult care. The clinic employs a multidisciplinary approach to provide psychosocial, special education, and young adult targeted interventions as these young patients become the custodians of their own health. The program enrolls patients aged 16–25 upon referral from their pediatric hematologist.

There is a slowly growing body of evidence on the current structure of formal transition interventions across healthcare facilities and their impact on clinical outcomes in the adolescent and young adult population. Many studies have focused on barriers to effective transition,15 pre and post transition knowledge16, as well as assessing patients’ and caregivers' concerns and needs.17 This study aims to shed light on transition interventions in adolescents and young adults with SCD in Louisiana and its impact on clinical outcomes in the adolescent and young adult population.

Methods

The study population consisted of patients aged 18–25 seen in the Pediatric and Transition clinic, as well as the adult sickle cell clinic at the SCCSL at Tulane from January 2021 to November 2024.

Inclusion Criteria:

1.Have confirmed SCD (hemoglobin SS (SS); sickle hemoglobin C (SC); sickle/beta plus thalassemia (Sβ+); or sickle/beta zero thalassemia (Sβ0) genotype)

2.Must have attended a minimum of two pediatric SCD clinic visits during adolescence (13–18 years), including at least one visit after age 15 years. 3

Data collection

Data was collected based on clinic records provided by administrative staff in the clinic. Electronic medical records of individuals fitting the inclusion criteria were accessed to extract the required data. Sociodemographic data including age, race, sex, education, employment, insurance type, sickle cell genotype, smoking status, use of recreational drugs, mental health co-morbidities based on charted medical history, receiving treatment for mental health, insurance type, patient receiving disease-modifying therapy (DMT) such as hydroxyurea, crizanlizumab, voxelotor, etc. These elements were selected based on a review of transition literature.18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Educational attainment was included as a proxy for health literacy and self-management capacity, with prior studies demonstrating that lower educational levels correlate with reduced transition readiness and difficulty navigating complex healthcare systems. Mental health comorbidities were assessed given their high prevalence in SCD and known impact on pain frequency, treatment adherence, and healthcare utilization. Substance use (specifically marijuana) was evaluated as a potential barrier to medication adherence and self-care behaviors.

The cohort was divided into two groups based on if they attended the formal transition clinic or not. The primary outcome measured was adult care transfer completion (defined as at least one completed visit with an adult provider within 6 months of completing transition care). The secondary outcomes collected were: 1) number of transition focused visits, 2) average transition time, 3) adult care abandonment (discontinuation of adult care within 12 months from first adult visit), 4) frequency of adult outpatient visits, 5) total number of emergency room visits, and 6) total number of hospital and ICU admissions as well as prevalence of the following SCD complications: acute chest syndrome, stroke, priapism, venous thromboembolism, chronic kidney disease, microalbuminuria, hypertension and retinopathy.

Data analysis

Bivariate analyses including Chi-square and t test were conducted to identify potential factors associated with adult care transfer completion and acute care utilization. A multiple logistic regression model was performed to identify factors related to successful transfer completion. A multiple linear regression model was used to identify factors associated with hospital admissions. All data analyses were performed in R 4.3.2, an open-source programming language for statistical data analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval and a waiver of HIPAA authorization for the study was granted by the Tulane University Biomedical Institutional Review Board.

Geographical coverage

Currently the clinic provides care to individuals from 21 cities and 11 parishes across Louisiana (Orleans, Jefferson, Tangipahoa, St. John the Baptist, St. Tammany, East Baton Rouge, Lafayette, St. Martin, Terrebonne, Assumption, St. Charles) and Mississippi. 67.3% (16) of the patients in the clinic reside outside the city of New Orleans (Fig. 1).

Descriptive analysis of the cohort

The mean age of patients in the clinic was 21.38 (± 2.59) among the transition group and 21.6 (± 2.59) among the non-transition group. Most of the patients self-identified as black (93.8%, n = 46) and had HbSS genotype (65.30%, n = 32), and this was consistent between both groups. Gender was reported based on patient self-identification as male or female. Within the non-transitioned group, there was a higher proportion of females (60%, n = 6) when compared with the transitioned group where males were the majority (66.7%, n = 26). Of the transition group, 43% (n = 17) and 20% (n = 2) of the non-transition group reported using recreational drugs (marijuana in 100% who reported recreational drug use). Seventeen individuals (34.7%) had coexisting mental health co-morbidities and 76.5% of them (n = 13) were established with a mental health provider through the clinic. (Table 1)

Discussion

Research and clinical implications

Health care transition encompasses the important process of moving from child/family-centered care to patient-centered adult care.11 For adolescents and young adults with chronic disease, it is a necessary step that prioritizes skill and knowledge transfer with the goal of increasing self-efficacy in disease management abilities.16 It is planned out in a coordinated manner to ensure continuity of care with integration between pediatric and adult care providers.11 For emerging adults with SCD, intervention during this critical time is crucial to address the increase in complications and mortality that have been shown to increase with age.3

This retrospective study analyzed the outcomes of the transition intervention at the SCCSL. This center provides comprehensive, co-located care to address psychosocial, educational, and medical needs for young adults and adolescents alike. Of the 39 patients in the transition group, 9 (23.07%) have successfully transitioned to adult care (had a visit with an adult provider within 6 months), and 3 patients (7.7%) had late transfer of care (median of 14 months after last visit in transition clinic). The remainder of the patients are still engaged in the transition clinic. Our study identified three significant predictors of successful adult care transfer: age ≥ 21 years ( p < 0.0001), high school completion (p = 0.017), and attending ≥ 5 transition visits (p = 0.0407). The association between older age and successful transfer may reflects developmental maturity and readiness for autonomous disease management. Young adults in their early twenties typically have greater self-efficacy and better-developed executive functioning skills compared to younger adolescents who may be managing competing demands. The significant association between high school completion and successful transfer is particularly noteworthy, as educational attainment serves as a marker for health literacy and ability to navigate complex healthcare systems.

Furthermore, the correlation of ≥ 5 transition visits demonstrates that transition is not a single event but a longitudinal process requiring sustained engagement. Many studies have also attempted to investigate socio-demographic predictors of transition success. Some predictors include having private insurance24 and longer duration on disease modifying therapy25; however, there has been no consensus on any specific socio-demographic factor correlated with better transition outcomes. Importantly, gender, sickle cell genotype, insurance type, and substance use were not significantly associated with successful transition, suggesting that programmatic factors may be more influential than individual demographic characteristics in our cohort.

Our study highlighted the pivotal role mental health plays in the overall wellbeing of adolescents and young adults with SCD. Individuals who had a comorbid mental health diagnosis had significantly higher odds of being hospitalized (OR = 4.65, p = 0.02) than those without a mental health comorbidity. Of the 17 patients (34.7%) with mental health diagnoses in our cohort, conditions included depression, anxiety and attention deficit hyperactive disorder (ADHD). The WHO’s Special Initiative for Mental Health highlights the significant burden of mental health diseases in individuals with chronic diseases.26 SCD impacts the quality of life, mental health, education, and economic outlook of young people, placing them at an even higher risk of worse outcomes. Even after curative treatment, these challenges appear to persist, as described in this study reporting pervasive emotional struggles in adults who had undergone stem cell transplant, despite significantly improved physical health.27 Mental health care therefore remains an integral part of sickle cell care. This was addressed by the program through their role in setting up these patients with a mental health provider through the clinic.

The co-location of pediatric and adult services at the SCCSL is a defining strength of this program and represents a key take-home message of this study. In a retrospective study on transition outcomes, Saulsberry et al. found the rate of matriculation to adult care significantly increased with an accompanying significant decrease in adult care abandonment.2 Similarly, Howell et al. showed that a co-located model of transition promoted greater retention of adult care 12–24 months following matriculation compared to those who did not participate.28 However, transition interventions are still being heterogeneously delivered across the country, limiting the generalizability of single center outcomes. To further build on our findings, we would consider implementing earlier transition readiness assessments and targeted educational support through school partnerships to identify those at risk of not completing high school. This could include providing disease-specific education accommodations and establishing tracking systems to communicate more closely with patients with poor attendance. Additionally, integrating routine mental health screening into transition visits may help identify and address comorbidities that contribute to increased hospitalizations.

Our study found no significant difference in acute care utilization between those who attended transition clinics and those who did not. A possible explanation for this is that both the transition and adult clinic are staffed by the same team who work very closely together and similar protocols are implemented across both services. Hence there is a lot of similarity in the screening, counseling and social support provided by both clinics. The major difference is that patients in the transition clinic are graduates of the pediatric clinics, while those who attend the adult clinics directly were referred to the service as adults who previously did not have a pediatric provider within the system. Previous studies report that continuity of care is highly valuable for health preservation.28 Blinder et al. found a significant increase in the frequency of ED visits and days of hospitalization following the shift from pediatric to adult care without a coordinated transition program in place.29 They also carried out another study which reported that adulthood in SCD is associated with heavier reliance on the ED for health care needs.30 As opposed to a preventative approach to SCD management with maintenance of ongoing treatment, fragmentation of care leads to increased rates of acute care utilization for complications including stroke, acute chest syndrome, and vaso-occlusive crisis.31, 32 However, the exact impact of transition interventions on acute care utilization is yet to be firmly established. Nolan et al. reported that after the establishment of a co-located transitional model for youth with SCD, acute care visits increased by 89%, however hospital admissions were 46% lower relative to the pre-transition period. Overall, acute care utilization remained constant.33 In some chronic conditions, however, transition interventions had been reported to reduce acute care utilization. A study on young adults with inflammatory bowel disease reported significantly fewer hospital admissions related to IBD.34 Review of current evidence reveals abundant literature on acute care utilization increases in the transition period, but sparse data on how structured transition interventions affect this trend. This represents an area of future inquiry.

Limitations

Limitations of the current study include the small sample size (n = 49) and its retrospective nature without randomization. Furthermore, recent hospital mergers and changes in clinic location have resulted in gaps in medical records, thus making more historical data more difficult to access.

Conclusion

For emerging adults with SCD, transition to adult care is a long-term process that must be planned out to allow for successful transfer and integration into a patient-centered model of care. It requires comprehensive, longitudinal support with a focus on building self-efficacy in disease management and providing complication prevention education with alleviation of patient concerns throughout. Our analysis suggests that young adults need several focused visits with a multidisciplinary team to promote successful transfer completion, with a focus on assessing transition readiness and providing longitudinal support. This success was closely related to high school completion and older age (≥21), indicating that educational attainment and developmental maturity are key drivers of the transition process. It also highlights the impact of mental health co-morbidities on healthcare utilization in sickle cell, as well as raises questions on the impact of transition interventions on acute care utilization. Future interventions should prioritize early identification of educational barriers and the use of formal readiness assessments to better support at-risk patients. By focusing on these psychosocial and programmatic factors, clinical programs can better bridge the gap in care and improve long-term outcomes for individuals living with SCD.
