Hypomagnesemia in patients on oral EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors: renal magnesium wasting or concurrent confounders?
Authors: Maria Ilyas, Jacqueline Nguyen, Shruti Shettigar, Kanika Rathi, Erin Kim, Jake LeClaire, Akash Mathavan, Akshay Mathavan, Aline Fares, Chintan V Shah
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Hypomagnesemia is a well-established class effect of intravenous anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) monoclonal antibodies (mAb), occurring in 20%–50% of patients and attributed to disruption of EGFR-dependent transient receptor potential melastatin-6 (TRPM6)-mediated magnesium reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule [1, 2]. Whether oral EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) produce a similar effect is unclear: registration trials have not systematically reported electrolyte outcomes [3], and a recent pharmacovigilance analysis identified disproportionality signals for some oral EGFR-TKIs, strongest for erlotinib and absent for osimertinib, but could not distinguish a drug effect from concurrent platinum chemotherapy, diarrhea, or acute illness [4]. We examined the incidence of presumed TKI-associated renal magnesium wasting (TKI-RMW), defined as post-initiation hypomagnesemia without a documented alternative cause, in a single-center cohort.

We retrospectively identified adults initiating an oral EGFR-TKI at the University of Florida Health between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2024, requiring at least one serum magnesium measurement in the 12 months before and after the index date. We excluded patients with any pre-index magnesium below 1.7 mg/dl, to isolate de novo events, and those who died within 30 days. The primary outcome was any post-index magnesium below 1.7 mg/dl within 12 months. Two reviewers, including a board-certified nephrologist, independently adjudicated each event as TKI-RMW or hypomagnesemia with an alternative cause (platinum chemotherapy, diuretics, gastrointestinal losses, or acute intercurrent illness). The study was approved by the University of Florida Institutional Review Board.

Of 140 patients identified, 68 had qualifying measurements; after excluding 21 with pre-index hypomagnesemia and 4 with early death, 43 comprised the final cohort (Fig. 1). The median age was 63 years, 53.5% were female, and 86.0% had non–small cell lung cancer; the most prescribed agents were erlotinib (46.5%), osimertinib (37.2%), and afatinib (16.3%) (Supplementary Table S1). Eight patients (18.6%) developed hypomagnesemia within 12 months, seven on erlotinib and one on osimertinib, mirroring the population-level VigiBase signal [4]. The median time to first event was 112 days, and nadir values ranged from 1.0 to 1.6 mg/dl (Supplementary Table S2).

Patient selection. Of 140 adults who initiated oral EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy at the University of Florida Health between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2024, 72 were excluded for lacking qualifying serum magnesium measurements in the 12 months before or after the index date. Of the 68 remaining patients, 21 were excluded for pre-index hypomagnesemia (any serum magnesium <1.7 mg/dl) and 4 for death within 30 days of treatment initiation, yielding a final analytic cohort of 43 patients. Within 12 months of TKI initiation, 8 patients (18.6%) developed at least one serum magnesium value below 1.7 mg/dl.

Following independent two-reviewer adjudication, none of the eight episodes met criteria for TKI-RMW; all had at least one documented alternative cause: platinum chemotherapy (n = 5), loop diuretics (n = 2), or acute hospitalization (n = 4), with three patients having multiple concurrent confounders. In no case did treating clinicians record hypomagnesemia as a reason for dose modification.

To our knowledge, no prior study has applied a clinically defined framework distinguishing TKI-RMW from hypomagnesemia of non-renal origin in patients receiving oral EGFR-TKIs. Although the 18.6% incidence falls within the range reported for anti-EGFR mAb [1], no event required renal magnesium wasting to explain it. These findings complement, rather than contradict, pharmacovigilance data: the same VigiBase analysis detecting an erlotinib signal acknowledged that such databases cannot separate drug effect from concurrent platinum exposure or gastrointestinal losses [4]. The absence of any attributable case suggests these signals are more parsimoniously explained by recognized non-renal mechanisms than by direct TRPM6 disruption at clinically achievable exposures, consistent with in vitro work showing erlotinib inhibits EGF-induced TRPM6 activity only at supratherapeutic concentrations [5].

This study is limited by non-standardized magnesium monitoring, which introduced ascertainment bias and substantial attrition, the absence of urinary magnesium and fractional excretion data, and a modest single-center cohort. Nonetheless, hypomagnesemia in patients on oral EGFR-TKIs appears more often explained by concurrent therapies and illness than by the drugs themselves. Future studies should systematically include serum and paired urinary magnesium measurements, complemented by mechanistic assessment of TRPM6 activity, to determine whether novel oral EGFR-TKIs and anti-EGFR mAb cause clinically meaningful renal magnesium wasting at therapeutic doses.
