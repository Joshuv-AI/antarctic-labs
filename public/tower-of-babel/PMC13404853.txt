Self-management digital intervention to promote physical activity in people living with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a pilot randomised controlled trial study protocol
Authors: Ricardo Salgado, Michael Amiguet, Patrício Costa, Philippe Delmas, José Miguel Padilha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSelf-management is a key component of effective chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) management and is mainly promoted through pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) programmes. However, less than 2% of patients participate in PR programmes. Among those who do, approximately 40% fail to complete the programme. The reasons for this are multifactorial and include dyspnoea, reduced exercise tolerance, lack of motivation and inadequate transportation and infrastructure to access PR centres. In this context, self-management digital health interventions emerge as a promising approach to support the development of self-management competencies, contribute to changing and maintaining behaviours and prevent a downward spiral of deconditioning in this population. The aim of this study is to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability and preliminary effects of a theory-based self-management digital intervention (Respir’air BPCO) designed to promote physical activity in patients with COPD.Methods and analysisThis pilot randomised controlled trial will enrol 20 patients with COPD who are being followed at a Swiss pulmonary centre. Participants will be randomised using Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) stage stratification (1:1) at the end of the in-person PR programme into either an experimental group receiving a mobile app-based self-management intervention aimed at promoting physical activity or a control group receiving only usual care. Data will be collected at three time points: immediately after completion of the PR programme (T0), 3 months post-PR (T1) and 6 months post-PR (T2). The study will assess trial methodological feasibility, including recruitment duration, consent rate, retention rate, acceptance of the allocated group and data completeness. Intervention feasibility will be evaluated through intervention fidelity, the number and type of technical problems reported by patients, and patients’ and/or researchers’ observations regarding the intervention design, content and use to inform future refinements. Intervention acceptability will be assessed using mobile app usage data and the Treatment Acceptability and Preferences questionnaire. In addition, preliminary effects will be evaluated through daily step count (pedometer), self-management (Self-Care in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Inventory), motivational regulation of physical activity (Behavioural Regulation in Exercise Questionnaire-3), quality of life (St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire and EuroQol 5-Dimensions-5 Levels), severity of dyspnea (Modified Medical Research Council Dyspnea Scale) and the number of exacerbations and hospitalisations. Analyses will include descriptive statistics and estimation-based comparative analyses using generalised linear mixed-effects models under the intention-to-treat principle, which accommodate missing data under standard assumptions, with reporting of effect sizes and CIs.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Commission of the Canton of Vaud (registration no.: 2025-01697). Results will be published in international peer-reviewed journals and presented at national and international conferences.Trial registration numberNCT07262229.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is currently considered the fourth leading cause of death worldwide.1 2 This fast-growing, non-communicable respiratory disease affects some 390 million people worldwide, including 400 000 in Switzerland.2–4 It is characterised by a persistent and progressive impairment of lung function due to changes in the airways and/or alveoli.2 It is primarily caused by continuous exposure to tobacco smoke, dust, chemicals and environmental pollution, leading to an inflammatory response. According to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease,2 the diagnosis is established through a post-bronchodilator lung function test in which the ratio of forced expiratory volume (FEV1) to forced vital capacity (FVC) is less than 0.7. Based on the individual’s FEV, the disease can be classified into four Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) stages. The least severe stage is GOLD 1 (FEV1≥80%), and the most severe is GOLD 4 (FEV1≤30%). Depending on the number of previous exacerbations that did or did not require hospitalisation, as well as the symptoms presented, the disease is further classified using the ABE classification. The letter A represents individuals with mild respiratory symptoms and no history of moderate or severe exacerbations in the previous year. The letter E represents individuals with mild to more significant symptoms who experienced one or more moderate-to-severe exacerbations in the previous year.2 Clinically, the disease is mainly manifested by dyspnoea, cough, sputum production, reduced tolerance to efforts and episodes of exacerbation.2 COPD is more prevalent in men over the age of 40 who are former or current smokers.2 5

In view of its characteristics, this disease represents a daily challenge with biopsychosocial and quality-of-life consequences for people living with COPD and their families.6 Moreover, the management of COPD also represents a considerable financial burden for healthcare systems. Indeed, 6% of the annual European health budget is spent on respiratory diseases, 56% of which is spent on COPD.2 Given the scale, progression and impact of this non-communicable disease, the ageing of the population and the growing shortage of healthcare professionals, healthcare and social security systems will be under increasing pressure in the coming years.7 8

Faced with these health and socio-economic challenges, the functional maintenance of people living with COPD is becoming a priority for community health, particularly in the context of secondary and tertiary prevention.2 9 10 According to the literature, one of the key strategies for achieving this is effective self-management of health status, which implies that people take an active part in managing their health status. More specifically, self-management involves an individual’s ability, with the support of family, community and healthcare professionals, to manage his disease, including pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments, lifestyle adaptation and management of the psychosocial aspects of living with a non-communicable disease.11–14

Self-management can be supported by structured, personalised interventions requiring interactions between patients and healthcare professionals and/or healthcare-supervised technologies often using behaviour change techniques (BCTs).15 16 The aim of these interventions is to motivate and support people to adopt positive health behaviours, improve their confidence and self-management skills and support them in adapting these behaviours.15 Studies have shown that effective COPD self-management can improve people’s quality of life, reduce hospital admissions and readmissions, lower respiratory-related mortality and improve regular physical activity (PA).10 17

One of the key components of effective self-management is PA, as it helps prevent functional deterioration in people living with COPD.2 PA is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that induces energy expenditure.18 19 It is a complex behaviour that can be characterised by the type, duration, intensity, symptomatic experience and habits of the individual. Domestic, leisure and professional activities are examples of PA.18–22 Despite recommendations in this area,2 people living with COPD have lower levels of PA than the general population.2 The main causes are related to intolerance to activity effort, fear of the sensation of dyspnoea and lack of motivation.23–25 However, if practised regularly, it improves exercise tolerance and quality of life, as well as reducing the sensation of dyspnoea and the number of exacerbations and mortality.2 24 26–28 It is therefore an essential component of secondary and tertiary prevention.

Currently, PA, as an essential component of effective self-management, is often encouraged during pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) programmes.2 9 However, despite the efforts of healthcare systems, less than 2% of people living with COPD participate in these programmes.9 29 30 Of those who do participate, around 40% do not complete the programme due to dyspnoea, reduced exercise tolerance, lack of motivation and lack of adequate transport and infrastructure to reach the programme.2 31–33 As a result, people end up adopting a more sedentary lifestyle that prevents them from reaping the benefits of PA, leading to a spiral of deconditioning and a consequent increase in morbidity, mortality and reduced quality of life.2 9 22 29 31 32 Moreover, most of the time, by the end of these rehabilitation programmes, participants no longer have an easily accessible resource to help them maintain their health behaviours. This situation also leads to a reduction in the benefits obtained from the PR programme.22 29 31 32 34 35

From this perspective, and in order to overcome the problems of the healthcare system’s capacity to support people living with COPD, the use of interventions involving digital technologies in healthcare is seen as an important lever.36–41 By their very nature, digital interventions enable better accessibility to care, personalisation of content and adaptation to people’s needs.9 39 42 43 Compared with a ‘face-to-face’ intervention or physical distribution of reading material, these hybrid (digital+face-to-face component) or 100% digital interventions contribute to improvements in quality of life, PA at 6 months, lung function and adherence, while also reducing the level of dyspnoea, anxiety and depression.9 16 36 44 It is also important to emphasise that, despite beliefs about older people’s difficulties using digital technologies, studies show that technological skills are eventually acquired by this population in everyday life.45 46

The literature on this subject has been prolific in recent years.36 47–51 However, there are many limitations to the research proposed and the level of evidence is still low.16 36 The weaknesses highlighted reveal the heterogeneity of the results obtained and the interventions used. Moreover, the vast majority of interventions are not based on a theoretical framework for understanding the phenomenon of self-management and, more specifically, what motivates people to adopt health behaviours, which is essential to attain effective results using digital interventions.36 52

Methods and analysis

Study aim

Given this context and the similar challenges observed in the French-speaking region of Switzerland, where this project is being conducted, the primary aim of this pilot randomised controlled trial (RCT) is to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of a theory-driven self-management digital intervention specifically designed to promote PA, as well as the feasibility of the trial procedures. A secondary aim is to explore the preliminary effects of the intervention on PA and related outcomes. The findings of this pilot trial will be used to refine the intervention, optimise trial procedures and inform the design and sample size calculation of a future definitive RCT.

Trial design

This study is a two-arm pilot RCT started in January 2026. After providing informed consent, patients were randomly assigned either to the control group (CG), receiving usual care (physiotherapy sessions and pneumologist appointments) or to the experimental group (EG), where usual care would be complemented by the self-management digital intervention ‘Respir’air BPCO’, delivered through a mobile application.

The study is also conducted under trial monitoring (with four checkpoints) and expert statistical supervision, ensuring methodological rigour. Any major deviation, recruitment problems or evidence of patient harm (considered very unlikely given the characteristics of the intervention) will lead to the termination of the study.

The design and reporting of this study protocol follow the key elements outlined in the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT)53 guidelines (online supplemental file 1).

Study setting

Participants will be recruited from a Swiss Pulmonary Centre located in the French-speaking part. Each year, this centre manages approximately 920 patients with COPD, with around 470 of them hospitalised either for acute exacerbations or elective admissions, both typically associated with participation in an intensive PR programme. This programme comprises 21 functional maintenance sessions delivered two times daily, along with six educational workshops conducted over a 2-week period following a 1-week hospital stay. The workshops cover key topics such as smoking cessation, pharmacotherapy, PA and nutrition.54

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria will be: a) a GOLD COPD diagnosis stage 1–4, classification B or E; b) admitted electively at the Swiss Pulmonary Centre for PR; c) successful completion of the PR programme, defined as completion of all scheduled components of the programme; d) age over 18 years; e) ability to provide informed consent; f) internet access; g) ownership of a smartphone and h) self-assessed perceived ease of use of digital devices.

The exclusion criteria will be: a) clinical instability confirmed by the team of healthcare professionals; b) severe cardiovascular disease confirmed by medical diagnosis; c) recent history of neoplasia or autoimmune disease; other PA-limiting clinical condition confirmed by medical diagnosis (eg, neurological disease); d) other respiratory disease as primary diagnosis; e) inability to read, understand or speak French and f) having cognitive impairment diagnosed by the medical team.

Recruitment and randomisation

Potential hospitalised patients requiring PR will be approached in the last week of their hospital stay by the nurse manager, the principal investigator (PI) or research assistant (RA) to be invited to participate in the study. After providing written informed consent, GOLD stage data will be collected for the stratified sampling needs. Participants will then be randomised in a 1:1 ratio based on a stratified sampling approach to the EG or CG using a computer-generated allocation (REDCap) sequence managed by the PI. As completion of the PR programme is one of the inclusion criteria, the head nurse will provide confirmation to the research team on the day before discharge to certify that the patient has successfully completed the programme. This two-step procedure will be implemented for feasibility and resource-related reasons and to reduce the risk of participant attrition once patients are no longer centralised within the hospital setting and have returned to their homes in various regions. Moreover, due to the nature of the intervention and the study procedures, blinding participants and outcome assessors will not be feasible. Although outcome assessments will be conducted by a RA who is independent from the intervention delivery, maintaining assessor blinding is not considered feasible within the design of this pilot trial. Participants allocated to the intervention group receive access to the self-management mobile application, whereas those in the CG receive usual care only, and participants may naturally disclose their group allocation during follow-up assessments. In addition, the collection of intervention-related feasibility data (eg, application use, technical issues and researcher logbook entries) requires involvement of the research team.

Trial intervention

The study intervention (Respir’air BPCO) (figure 1) was developed using the Medical Research Council guidelines for complex interventions52 and will complement the current face-to-face PR programme in a Swiss Pulmonary Centre. It consists of a theory-informed self-management mobile application specifically focused on promoting PA, considered here as a key self-management behaviour among people with COPD.55 It will be administered at the end of the existing face-to-face PR programme at the Swiss Pulmonary Centre. From a technical standpoint, this intervention will be based on the pre-existing ‘Breathment’ mobile application (compatible with iOS and Android), developed by the German company Breathment GmbH. The developed content for this specific intervention is based on the self-care theory of chronic illness,14 the self-determination theory of human motivation56 and the BCT taxonomy.57 58 It also integrates findings from a literature review, an umbrella review16 59 and focus groups conducted with healthcare professionals and patients before this phase.55 The content of the intervention was validated through a modified e-Delphi study.

Respir’air BPCO intervention.

The mobile application, which was presented to the Swiss Pulmonary Centre healthcare team prior to the launch of the pilot study, comprises several distinct components. The first tab provides an overview of the patient journey and includes a video illustrating examples of physical activities. The second tab displays key activity indicators, including the number of active days, progression in daily PA (number of daily steps), daily steps goal progression and real-time information on air quality. The third tab offers eight educational modules delivered through text, videos and/or images. These modules cover essential topics, including an introduction to the self-management digital intervention; an overview of COPD, its risk factors, diagnosis and management strategies; principles of disease self-management; the importance of PA; practical strategies for integrating PA into daily life (eg, activity suggestions such as walking, goal setting, problem-solving, etc) and safety considerations related to PA and disease management.

The final tab is dedicated to application settings. To promote consistency, engagement and motivation, the app incorporates several supportive features, including a step counter with customisable step goals, motivational messages providing encouraging feedback and reminders triggered by periods of inactivity. The PI will carry out the presentation and administration of the intervention for patients with COPD. More specifically, he will guide participants through the account creation, installation and configuration of the mobile application, explaining its various content and functionalities in detail. Outcome measurements

Primary outcomes

Feasibility measures

Feasibility outcomes comprise both trial methodological feasibility and intervention feasibility indicators, following the framework proposed by Sidani and Braden.61 Trial methodological feasibility will be evaluated using: 1) the duration required to complete the recruitment process, 2) the consent rate, 3) the retention rate, 4) the acceptance rate of the allocated group and 5) the proportion of missing data in the completed questionnaires/pedometer. Intervention feasibility will be assessed using: 1) a digital logbook for researchers' usage, to record any observations or comments (patients and/or researchers' perspectives) regarding the intervention design, content and/or usage, with the aim of informing potential refinements after the completion of the pilot study and 2) number of technical problems auto-reported by the patient (Have you encountered any technical problems specifically related to the digital intervention (Respir’air BPCO) over the past 3 months? If yes, how many times did this occur? Please briefly describe the issue(s)).61 Additionally, intervention fidelity will be evaluated by calculating the exposure rate to all content available within the mobile application.61 These metrics will provide essential insights into the practicality and operational aspects of implementing the self-management digital intervention in a clinical setting.61

Acceptability measures

Intervention acceptability will also be examined following the framework proposed by Sidani and Braden61.61 Specifically: 1) the mobile app usage habits via app log data and 2) intervention acceptance using the French-adapted version of the Treatment Acceptability and Preferences (TAP) questionnaire.62–65 This instrument assesses five dimensions: relevance, appropriateness, efficacy, willingness to comply and overall satisfaction. Responses are scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘not at all’) to 4 (‘extremely’), with higher total scores reflecting greater acceptance. The original version of this scale demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.80.65

Secondary outcomes

Preliminary effectiveness measures

Main variable:

The number of daily steps will be used to assess the PA of the COPD patient. To do this, a Yamax Power Walker EX-510 pedometer (Yamax-Yamasa, Japan) will be used,66 which has been validated for the COPD population.67 Participants will be asked to wear the device for five consecutive days, from waking to bedtime (except during showers or swimming) and will be instructed on the correct position of the device, that is, in the front trouser pocket or hanging from a clip in the front pocket area.

Secondary variables:

Self-management will be assessed using the Self-Care in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Inventory (SC-COPDI)68 in its French version translated for the purposes of this study according to the methodology described by Wild et al. 69This 5-point Likert-scale assessment questionnaire comprises 32 items divided into three subscales: the self-care maintenance scale, the monitoring scale and the management scale. Overall standardised scores for each of the three subscales range from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate higher levels of self-care in patients with COPD. Its original version also has good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha between 0.78 and 0.92).

Motivational regulation of PA will be assessed using the Behavioural Regulation in Exercise Questionnaire-3 (BREQ-3)70 71 in its French version translated for the purposes of this study according to the methodology described by Wild et al.69 This 24-item self-administered scale assesses different types of motivational regulation in PA, including intrinsic motivation, integrated regulation, identified regulation, introjected regulation, external regulation and amotivation. The score is obtained by averaging responses on a Likert scale ranging from ‘not at all true’ (0) to ‘very true’ (4). Higher scores indicate higher levels of motivation corresponding to the factor being assessed. The internal consistency of this scale is good, with Cronbach’s alphas between 0.83 and 0.96.

Health-related quality of life, assessed using the French version of the disease-specific St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) 72 and the generic EuroQol 5-Dimensions-5 Levels (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire.73 The rationale for combining a disease-specific and a generic instrument is that the appropriateness of combining the two will offer a broader overview of the health-related quality of life of patients with COPD.74 The St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire is a self-administered questionnaire widely used to assess the quality of life of patients with COPD. It consists of 50 items divided into three categories: symptoms, activity limitation and the social and emotional impact of the disease. Each item is assigned a weight based on the level of distress attributed to each symptom or condition described. Overall scores range from 0 (no effect on quality of life) to a maximum score of 100 (maximum perceived suffering). A higher score means a poorer quality of life. The internal consistency assessed by Cronbach’s alpha of this scale is very good (0.90) for the total score.72 As for the EQ-5D-5L, this self-administered scale assesses five dimensions of health: mobility, personal care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, using five items. Each dimension is rated according to five levels of severity: from ‘no problem’ (0) to ‘extreme problem’.4 73 75 In addition, the EQ-5D-5L includes a visual analogue scale (VAS) that allows participants to rate their general state of health on a scale from 0 to 100. The EQ-5D-5L score combines responses to a health value index and the VAS score. Higher scores correspond to better health-related quality of life.73 75 76

The severity of dyspnoea will be assessed using the French version of the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) Dyspnoea Scale. This self-administered scale, widely used in the follow-up of people living with COPD, uses a scale from 0 to 4. A score of 4 represents the highest severity of the dyspnoea symptom.2 77 78

The number of exacerbations and hospitalisations will also be assessed. This information will be collected via a self-reported questionnaire.

In addition to the above-mentioned dependent variables, sociodemographic (age, gender, employment status, highest level of education completed, presence of a support person at home, type of support provided by the support person, smoking habits and number and frequency of physiotherapy sessions after the end of the PR programme) and clinical data (GOLD grade and classification, FEV1 (%), FEV/FVC ratio (%), BMI, medical history, number of exacerbations in the past 6 months, number of hospitalisations due to respiratory problems in the past 6 months, participation in a PR programme, use of oxygen therapy, amount of oxygen therapy (litres), hours of oxygen therapy per day, type of oxygen therapy device, use of non-invasive ventilation (NIV) and type of NIV) will also be collected. Sociodemographic data will be collected using a self-administered questionnaire, and clinical information will be collected from the Electronic Patient Record (EPR) (except for the number of hospitalisations and exacerbations, which will also be self-reported).

Sample size

As this study is designed as a pilot RCT, a formal sample size calculation based on hypothesis testing was not performed.79 Instead, the sample size was determined in accordance with methodological recommendations for pilot studies. Based on the guidance proposed by Whitehead et al,80 a total sample size of 20 participants (10 per group) is considered adequate for pilot trials aiming to estimate parameters required for the planning of a future definitive trial, assuming a large standardised effect size (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.8). Given the exploratory nature of pilot trials, these estimates will be interpreted cautiously and used solely to inform the planning of a fully powered trial.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, such as frequency distributions and measures of central tendency (mean, median) and dispersion (SD, IQR), will be used to characterise the sociodemographic variables in the sample and quantify the scores obtained on the various measurement scales selected.

Associations between categorical variables and group membership will be examined using the χ2 test of independence or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Differences between groups on continuous variables will be analysed using Student’s t-tests or Welch’s t-tests when the assumption of homogeneity of variances is violated, or Mann-Whitney tests, depending on the distributional characteristics and sample size. For comparisons involving more than two groups or repeated measures, analyses of variance (ANOVA) or the Friedman test will be applied as appropriate. Associations between continuous variables will be assessed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients, according to the level of measurement and distributional assumptions.

Differences in effectiveness outcomes between timepoints and between study arms will be analysed using generalised linear mixed-effects models (MMs), with random intercepts for participants to account for within-subject correlation. Fixed effects will include study arm, timepoint and their interaction, allowing the estimation of differential changes over time between arms. This approach accommodates missing data under the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption and is well suited to the available sample size. Finally, longitudinal data analysis (LDA) models, such as generalised estimating equations or MMs, are preferable to an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) approach that conditions post-treatment outcomes on baseline values. In practice, ANCOVA analyses based on complete cases exclude participants with missing baseline or follow-up data, which can potentially reduce statistical power. In contrast, LDA models use all available observations and enable the explicit modelling of within-subject correlation, assuming the MAR condition. When no outcome data are missing, treatment effect estimates obtained from LDA models are mathematically equivalent to those from ANCOVA models.81 82

If necessary, analyses will be adjusted for baseline variables known from the literature to be associated with the outcomes, in order to reduce potential imbalance and improve precision.

As sensitivity analyses, post-intervention group comparisons will also be conducted using independent-samples t-tests or ANCOVA models (or permutation-based versions thereof, depending on the final sample size). These analyses are included to assess the robustness of the findings, given that simpler methods may yield more stable estimates in small-sample settings.

Given the exploratory nature of this pilot trial, p-values will be reported for descriptive purposes only and interpreted cautiously rather than as confirmatory evidence. Primary emphasis will be placed on estimating treatment effects, their magnitudes and the corresponding 95% CIs. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons is planned, as analyses are intended to inform the design of a future definitive trial rather than to provide confirmatory inference.

Interpretation of the findings will focus on the magnitude and precision of the estimated treatment effects. The clinical and scientific relevance of the observed effects will be evaluated for each outcome in light of prior evidence and the specific measurement scales used. This assessment will be supported by standardised effect size measures (eg, Cohen’s d, eta squared) and their corresponding CIs.

Patterns and extent of missing data will be described and explored. Given the use of MMs, which provide valid estimates under the MAR assumption, no imputation routine will be performed. If the proportion of missing data is substantial, sensitivity analyses may be conducted to assess the robustness of findings under different missing data assumptions. Analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, whereby all randomised participants will be included in the estimation models to the extent permitted by the available data. About the quality of the information collected, the consistency of responses to the scales will be assessed by counting repeated responses. R statistical software (latest stable version) will be used for statistical analyses. The proposed analyses are similar to those performed in the large-scale RCT study to be carried out later.

Statistical rigour will be ensured by Michael Amiguet, senior statistician at Unisanté, Centre for Primary Care and Public Health, University of Lausanne, Switzerland.

Qualitative data from the researcher’s digital logbook will be analysed using Braun and Clarke’s83 thematic analysis and will inform potential refinements to the design, content and usability of the intervention following completion of the pilot study.

Implications

The present pilot study aims primarily to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of a theory-driven self-management digital intervention specifically designed to promote PA among individuals with COPD. In addition, the study will explore the preliminary effects of the intervention on PA and related outcomes. This research addresses important scientific gaps and aligns with identified COPD management priorities.2 16 36 52 A notable strength of this study is its comprehensive and theory-based design, assessing not only health-related outcomes but also user acceptability and feasibility, which are critical for the practical implementation of digital interventions.45

However, a potential limitation of this study is the inability to blind participants and outcome assessors due to the nature of the intervention and study procedures, which may introduce performance and detection bias. Additionally, participants’ digital literacy levels could influence engagement, an aspect we aim to thoroughly assess through our acceptability metrics, although previous studies involving patients with COPD have not identified digital literacy as a significant barrier.45 46

The intervention is expected to be feasible and acceptable and to potentially demonstrate positive preliminary effects.

The insights gained from this pilot study are anticipated to inform future larger-scale studies, potentially improving COPD care strategies, reducing healthcare expenditures and enhancing patient quality of life through increased accessibility and empowerment in chronic disease self-management.

Ethics and dissemination

All procedures will comply with the ethical guidelines established by the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval has been granted by the Ethics Commission of the Canton of Vaud (2025–01697). Additionally, the study has been registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07262229). Any protocol modification will be communicated to relevant parties. Results will be disseminated through presentations at the partner clinical institution (both patients and healthcare professionals), scientific conferences and publications in peer-reviewed journals.
