Coupled dual-channel memristors for hardware-native trustworthy Bayesian intelligence
Authors: Guowei Liu, Jiaqi Ding, Ziyu Wan, Jiakun Xue, Yu Zhang, Zhenqi Gong, Chengyuan Peng, Wenpei Shi, Jiale Su, Jiangnan Xia, Huipeng Chen, Lang Jiang, Lei Liao, Yuanyuan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBayesian neural networks (BNNs) enable trustworthy edge intelligence by quantifying predictive uncertainty. However, hardware BNN implementations face a bottleneck: digital approaches suffer from high latency, while memristors are limited by the intrinsic coupling between their conductance state (mean) and stochastic noise (variance). Here we report a coupled dual-channel memristor (CDCM) based on an ion gel/ZnO heterostructure that breaks this fundamental trade-off. Utilizing vertical ion-gating to establish two tunable memristive channels, our device defines synaptic weight as the differential conductance between the two channels. This architecture enables the hardware-native orthogonal control over the synaptic weight mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ), allowing for the precise synthesis of decoupled Gaussian weights. We validate our approach with a hardware-calibrated BNN for multimodal human activity recognition, achieving 79.08% accuracy while reliably detecting unseen activities as out-of-distribution anomalies. This work provides a scalable, physics-driven paradigm for energy-efficient, inherently trustworthy probabilistic computing.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Electronic devices, Electronic and spintronic devices

Introduction

The proliferation of artificial intelligence of things (AIoT) has deployed deep learning algorithms into a myriad of safety-critical edge applications, ranging from autonomous driving to wearable healthcare monitoring1–4. While current artificial neural networks (ANNs) demonstrate remarkable accuracy in classification tasks, they fundamentally suffer from a deterministic nature, often yielding “overconfident” predictions even when encountering unknown or corrupted data. This lack of risk awareness poses severe safety hazards5–8. To address this, Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) have emerged as a robust solution9,10. By replacing fixed synaptic weights with probability distributions—most commonly Gaussian distributions—BNNs enable systems to quantify predictive uncertainty, allowing for more trustworthy decision-making.

Conventionally, BNNs are implemented via software-based sampling, such as Monte Carlo methods11. These approaches are favored for their high mathematical flexibility and the ability to achieve high-precision posterior estimations using standard digital hardware. However, the requirement for repeated sampling to generate a distribution incurs prohibitive latency and energy overhead, making them largely unsuitable for resource-constrained edge devices with strict power budgets12,13. Consequently, there is an urgent demand for hardware-native probabilistic computing paradigms14.

Despite the maturity of deterministic in-memory computing15,16, hardware-based BNNs remain a significant challenge. Most current stochastic devices rely on the inherent noise of resistive switching (e.g., in resistive random access memory (RRAM) or phase change memory (PCM)) to emulate probabilistic behaviors17–20. However, these approaches often suffer from a critical lack of controllability: the mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ) of the conductance distributions are typically coupled18,21. This coupling makes it nearly impossible to precisely map the complex, independent weight distributions required for accurate Bayesian inference22,23.

Here, we report a coupled dual-channel memristor (CDCM) based on an ion-gel/ZnO heterostructure, which breaks the fundamental trade-off between stochasticity and controllability. Unlike conventional transistor-based synaptic devices24,25, our CDCM device leverages vertical ion-gating to establish two spatially separated but electrically coupled memristive interfaces at the gate-source and gate-drain terminals. Crucially, we show that the μ and σ of this distribution can be independently modulated through specific pulse engineering, providing the hardware-native synapse with decoupled μ and σ control. To validate the system-level benefits, we perform a hardware-calibrated simulation of a BNN system for human activity recognition using a behavior detection dataset comprising inertial measurement unit (IMU), thermopile, and Time-of-Flight (ToF) sensors. Our BNN not only achieves high accuracy across standard activity classes but also effectively identifies unseen activities as out-of-distribution (OOD) anomalies through robust uncertainty estimation. This work establishes a device-circuit-algorithm co-design strategy, paving the way for compact and trustworthy edge intelligence.

Results

Framework of the hardware-native Bayesian neural network

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the deployment of ANNs in safety-critical edge applications—such as human activity recognition for elderly fall detection26—is often compromised by their deterministic nature27. Conventional ANNs provide point estimates that can lead to overconfident misclassifications when encountering ambiguous or OOD data (e.g., differentiating a fall onset from a normal bending motion)28. To mitigate this risk, BNNs replace fixed weights with probability distributions, enabling the system to quantify predictive uncertainty and issue risk alerts29,30.

However, implementing these networks on resource-constrained hardware faces a significant dilemma (Fig. 1b). While digital ASIC-based implementations offer independent control over the mean and variance, they are hindered by significant power and latency overheads due to the necessity of complex CMOS-based random number generators and digital-to-analog conversion31. Conversely, single-memristor (SM)-based approaches offer high energy efficiency16 but suffer from deeply coupled statistical parameters, limiting their ability to map the diverse weight distributions required for high-accuracy Bayesian tasks32–36.

To overcome these constraints, we propose a hardware-native framework that achieves high accuracy, low latency, and energy efficiency simultaneously by leveraging a CDCM device. As shown in Fig. 1c, the device consists of an ion gel/ZnO heterostructure where a single gate modulates two physically distinct memristive interfaces at the source and drain terminals. Unlike traditional transistors, the vertical ion-gating effect induces non-volatile conductance changes, effectively creating a compact, coupled dual-memristor pair. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the detailed CDCM production process. Figure 1d elucidates the operational principle: the synaptic weight is defined differentially as the difference between the conductances of the right and left channels (W = gl − gr). By exploiting the intrinsic stochastic conductance fluctuations (i.e., read noise) inherent to the memristive states, we can independently tune the statistical distributions of gl and gr. Because the sum and difference of two independent Gaussian variables remain Gaussian, this differential architecture allows for the direct synthesis of synaptic weights with precisely tunable μ and σ in the analog domain. This physical substrate enables efficient Bayesian inference by bypassing the need for external, power-hungry random number generation sources.

Device characteristics and physical mechanism

To validate the operational principle, we characterize a two-terminal memristive device based on the vertical ion gel/ZnO heterostructure, representing a half-component of the CDCM (Fig. 2a). The ion gel is composed of a PVDF-HFP matrix host enriched with the ionic liquid [EMIM+] [MeSO4−] (Fig. 2b), ensuring high ionic conductivity, while a specific doping layer containing TrTPFB and PBTTT is introduced to optimize the interface stability (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 1)25,37. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, the surface morphologies of the ion gel, doping layer, and ZnO layer are characterized by AFM. To further investigate the microstructure, the ZnO layer is analyzed using SEM, revealing a dense granular structure (Supplementary Fig. 3). Layer thicknesses of the ZnO and ion gel are determined to be approximately 77 and 641 nm, respectively, via AFM step-height measurements (Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, the crystallinity of the ZnO thin film is verified by XRD, as presented in Supplementary Fig. 5. The optical micrograph in Fig. 2d confirms the successful fabrication of the device array with well-defined electrode patterns, demonstrating the feasibility of large-scale fabrication of single memristor arrays.

The fundamental synaptic functionality is evidenced by the current–voltage (I–V) sweeps shown in Fig. 2e, which manifest a typical clockwise pinched hysteresis loop characteristic of bipolar resistive switching. Control experiments on various constituent layer configurations confirm that the optimized heterostructure is essential for stable resistive switching (Supplementary Fig. 6). The device array exhibits excellent uniformity, as evidenced by the consistent I–V switching curves across 25 random cells (Supplementary Fig. 7) and the tight distribution of On/Off ratios (Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover, the devices exhibit robust long-term retention stability, as evidenced by the nearly identical I–V hysteresis loops recorded over 21 days of intermittent measurement (Supplementary Fig. 9), further validating the reliability of the ion gel/ZnO heterostructure.

As shown in Fig. 2f, applying a −4 V write pulse for 400 ms followed by a read operation at −0.1 V yields a high-conductance state, whereas a +4 V erase pulse of the same duration, read at +0.1 V, results in a low-conductance state. Thus, we obtain the switching reliability over 100 consecutive programming cycles. While the device maintains a robust window between the high resistance state (HRS) and low resistance state (LRS), ensuring distinguishability, a closer inspection of the read currents reveals intrinsic cycle-to-cycle conductance fluctuations rather than fixed, discrete values. This natural variability—rather than being a defect—is the physical foundation that enables our memristor to serve as a hardware-native source of stochasticity for BNN inference38. Specifically, the conductance fluctuations closely resemble Gaussian-distributed noise, fulfilling a key requirement for implementing probabilistic synaptic weights and acting as an analog Gaussian random number generator39,40.

Beyond binary switching, the device exhibits excellent analog tunability. As demonstrated in Fig. 2g, the channel conductance can be continuously potentiated by modulating either the pulse number or the pulse width, showing a highly linear update trajectory essential for precise weight definition. Furthermore, the device mimics the dynamic temporal processing of biological synapses. Upon stimulation, the excitatory post-synaptic current (EPSC) exhibits a gradual decay characteristic (Fig. 2h). Detailed analysis in Fig. 2i reveals a process representing short-term memory (STM) dynamics41,42. The transient dynamic response and continuous pulse switching behaviors are further detailed in Supplementary Fig. 10.

The physical origin of these behaviors is investigated through internal ionic dynamics. The I–V characteristics in linear coordinates exhibit a distinct self-enhancement effect (Fig. 3a), where the channel current progressively increases over consecutive scans, indicating that ions move from inside the ion gel to the interface and gradually accumulate43. More critically, the device displays a pronounced scan-rate dependence (Fig. 3b). As the voltage sweep rate decreases, the hysteresis window expands significantly. This rate-dependent behavior is a hallmark of ion-mediated transport44, distinguishing our device from purely electronic mechanisms (e.g., charge trapping) which are typically instantaneous45. The expanded window at lower rates confirms that the switching dynamics are governed by the time-consuming migration of bulky ions, which require sufficient time to respond to the external field46. We further quantify this dynamic response in Fig. 3c, where the normalized hysteresis area (Snorm) shows a clear dependency on the operating frequency. Here, Snorm is defined as the area enclosed between the forward and reverse I–V branches for V < 0 (left of the self-crossing point), normalized by Vmax × Imax—the maximum sweep voltage and peak current in the loop, respectively (see inset in Fig. 3c). The dependence of Snorm on the operating frequency—specifically its substantial increase at lower frequencies—confirms that the switching dynamics are governed by the slow migration of ions, rather than instantaneous electronic processes47.

The temporal dynamics are explicitly resolved in the transient current response under constant voltage stress (Fig. 3d), where the current exhibits a characteristic exponential decay regardless of polarity. Double-exponential fitting further reveals that this decay is not a simple single-exponential relaxation, but a complex process involving at least two characteristic time scales: a fast process attributed to rapid electric double layer (EDL) polarization at the interface, and a slow process associated with ion diffusion within the gel and gradual EDL saturation (Supplementary Fig. 11). Such behavior is the hallmark of an ionic polarization process: upon applying a bias (e.g., 2 V), the rapid migration of ions to the interface initially generates a high displacement current. Subsequently, as the EDL forms and approaches saturation, the accumulated ions effectively screen the external electric field48. This screening effect reduces the net driving force, causing the ionic motion to cease and the current to relax toward equilibrium. This capacitive charging characteristic confirms that the device switching is governed by the saturation-limited migration of ions, distinguishing it from pure electronic conduction49. Lateral transport measurements configured in a three-terminal geometry further confirm that the conduction is dominated by ionic dynamics rather than field-effect transistor behavior (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13).

Therefore, based on the above observations, we can attribute them to the vertical ion gate control mechanism shown in the schematic model. Under a negative gate bias (Fig. 3e), the electric field drives the cations ([EMIM+]) within the ion gel to migrate toward the ZnO interface. This migration results in the formation of a high-capacitance EDL, which electrostatically induces a high concentration of electrons in the n-type ZnO channel, switching the device to a low-resistance state. Conversely, applying a positive bias repels the cations from the interface (Fig. 3f), dissolving the EDL and depleting the channel electrons, thereby resetting the device to a high-resistance state50. This reversible, field-driven ionic redistribution acts as a vertical gate, enabling precise, analog modulation of the channel conductance—a mechanism that underpins the independent dual-channel control in the subsequent CDCM architecture.

Tunable Gaussians via coupled dual-channel memristor

A critical challenge in implementing hardware BNN is the intrinsic coupling between the conductance state (weight mean, μ) and its stochastic fluctuation (weight standard deviation, σ) in single memristive devices. As detailed in Supplementary Fig. 14, μG and σG both exhibit similar monotonic trends as a function of the programming voltage Vp, creating a fixed dependency between the signal and the noise. To overcome this fundamental limitation, we utilize the CDCM architecture designed to harness the intrinsic ionic stochasticity for probabilistic computing. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, the CDCM unit consists of two parallel memristors (gl and gr) biased by antisymmetric read voltages (VR and −VR). Here, gl and gr denote the conductances of two memristors, respectively, and geff represents the effective synaptic conductance of the CDCM. Although the physical conductance of each individual memristor is strictly non-negative, the differential architecture enables the realization of signed effective weights. By independently programming the two devices with distinct programming voltages (Vpl and Vpr), we can tune gl and gr takes on both positive and negative values, thereby emulating the full range of synaptic weights required in neural networks.

Since VR is a fixed value given by us, the output current will follow a normal distribution of geff, as shown in Eq. 2.

As shown in Fig. 4b, in our experiments, CDCM is first initialized with a large erase voltage (VE = +4 V), followed by independent programming pulses applied to those two devices (Vpl = −2 V and Vpr = −2 V). Detailed transient current waveforms and raw data acquisition for both the positive and negative branches over 100 cycles are provided in Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16. Subsequently, a differential readout is performed by applying opposite-polarity read voltages (VR = ±0.1 V) to measure the individual currents (Il and Ir) and the output current (Iout). To characterize cycle-to-cycle variability, the CDCM underwent 100 consecutive erase–program–read cycles under identical conditions. Both Il and Ir exhibit characteristic read noise, resulting in a fluctuating Iout that serves as a continuous entropy source. To validate the statistical quality of this generation, the probability density function (PDF) of the sampled Iout is plotted in Fig. 4c. The resulting Iout closely follows a Gaussian distribution, with a measured mean of μIout = -0.631 nA and standard deviation σIout = 0.396 nA. The distribution shows a remarkable fit to the theoretical Gaussian bell curve. Furthermore, Supplementary Fig. 17 confirms the long-term stability of this hardware-native Gaussian distribution over 21 days, demonstrating that CDCM can maintain high-fidelity Gaussianity without degradation during long-term operation.

For a more rigorous statistical test, we perform a Quantile–Quantile (Q–Q) analysis. To construct the Q–Q plot, the collected Iout data points are sorted in ascending order and assigned ranks i (i = 1, 2, …, n, where n is the total number of measurements). The experimental cumulative probability for each ranked data point is estimated as Pi = (i − 0.5)/n and subsequently mapped to standard Gaussian quantiles (Z-scores) via the probit function Φ−1(Pi). The underlying principle of the Q–Q plot rests on a simple relationship: if the data are indeed normally distributed, the ordered sample values should scale linearly with the theoretical quantiles, following I(i) = μ + σ × Zi. As shown in Fig. 4d, the experimental quantiles align closely with the reference line and fall well within the 95% confidence interval (dashed lines). This strong linearity confirms that the CDCM-generated stochasticity constitutes high-fidelity Gaussian noise, satisfying the mathematical prerequisites for representing weight distributions in BNNs.

Having established the high-fidelity Gaussian generation, we next demonstrate the capability to orthogonally modulate the distribution’s standard deviation (σ) without disturbing its mean value (μ). As depicted in Fig. 4e, we employ a “common-mode” programming strategy where the absolute magnitudes of the programming voltages (Vpl and Vpr) are swept simultaneously, while strictly maintaining a constant differential voltage | Vpl − Vpr |. Physically, increasing the programming baseline drives both memristors into higher conductance states characterized by more vigorous ionic fluctuations, thereby amplifying the stochastic noise (σ). However, since the differential bias is fixed, the net current difference should theoretically remain invariant.

Figure 4f illustrates the above process: as the programming voltage −Vpl and −Vpr increases, the individual branch currents Il and Ir shift significantly in opposite directions. Yet, their coherent variation ensures that the subtraction Iout yields a constant center value. The statistical results in Fig. 4g confirm this orthogonal control: while the mean μ remains perfectly stable across the voltage range, the standard deviation σ exhibits a monotonic, linear increase. This result demonstrates that the CDCM architecture effectively decouples the noise fluctuation level from the weight value, addressing the signal-noise coupling limitation typical of single-device implementations.

Complementing the σ-tuning capability, the CDCM architecture must also allow for the precise modulation of the synaptic weight mean (μ) while maintaining a fixed uncertainty level. To achieve this, we implement a differential-mode programming strategy as illustrated in Fig. 4h. In contrast to the common-mode approach, here we introduce a differential voltage offset ∆V between the two inputs. By asymmetrically potentiating one memristor while depressing the other, we can shift the center of the differential current distribution. The temporal response in Fig. 4i demonstrates this effect: as the control parameter ∆V is swept, the branch currents Il and Ir diverge, effectively steering the Iout to a new equilibrium level.

Crucially, because the total conductance of the parallel pair remains within a relatively constant dynamic range where the noise increment in one branch is balanced by the decrement in the other, the aggregate fluctuation level is preserved. This is quantitatively confirmed in Fig. 4j: the extracted mean μ follows a highly linear update trajectory proportional to the control voltage. Meanwhile, the variance σ remains remarkably flat throughout the tuning process. Collectively, Fig. 4g, j demonstrate the full orthogonality of the CDCM architecture, proving that it can physically map any arbitrary Gaussian distribution Nμ,σ2 required by Bayesian algorithms via decoupled voltage controls, thereby solving the signal-noise coupling dilemma inherent in conventional devices.

Notably, while such decoupling could theoretically be implemented using a conventional 2-transistor 2-resistor (2T2R) RRAM architecture5, our CDCM achieves this within a highly compact, single-device footprint (Supplementary Fig. 18). Beyond the reduced footprint, discrete 2T2R arrays are often hindered by device-to-device mismatch52; in contrast, the shared vertical ion-gate in the CDCM provides inherent physical symmetry between the two coupled channels. This structural integration fundamentally ensures the stable, controllable, and high-fidelity Gaussian noise required for hardware-native Bayesian inference.

Neural network evaluation for edge Intelligence

We evaluate the performance of our hardware-based BNNs using a subset of the CMI human activity recognition dataset53, which leverages multimodal sensor data to classify human behaviors (see Supplementary Figs. 19, 20, and 21). The dataset comprises measurements from three main sensor modalities: the sensor suite includes an IMU that integrates linear acceleration, angular velocity (gyroscope), and magnetic field (magnetometer) data; five thermopile sensors providing temperature readings in degrees Celsius; and five ToF sensors measuring the distance to the nearest object. Altogether, these sensors yield 339 input features per time window, capturing rich spatiotemporal and contextual information about human motion.

For our experiments, we select five distinct activities for training. Crucially, during testing, we introduce a sixth activity that is never presented to the network during training. This OOD action serves as a rigorous test of the BNN’s ability to recognize and appropriately respond to previously unseen behaviors—enabling us to assess its uncertainty quantification and robustness in safety-critical scenarios.

Based on the dataset, we design a BNN model whose overall architecture is illustrated in Fig. 5a, with detailed schematics of the probabilistic core and feature backbone provided in Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23, respectively. The model comprises a conventional feature-extraction backbone followed by a Bayesian fully connected layer with 10 input neurons and 5 output neurons for multi-class classification.

The BNNs is trained off-chip for 65 epochs using the Bayes-by-Backprop algorithm with a Gaussian prior over synaptic weights54. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 24, it achieved a test accuracy of 80.11% on test dataset, surpassing the 76.04% accuracy of a comparable deterministic ANNs equipped with a standard fully connected output layer. This performance gain arises from the BNN’s probabilistic inference mechanism: during evaluation, we perform 100 Monte Carlo forward passes per input by sampling synaptic weights from their learned posterior distributions and averaged the resulting predictions. This stochastic ensemble effect not only improves generalization but also enables principled uncertainty quantification—key for reliable decision-making in real-world scenarios55,56. To ensure faithful mapping between the algorithmic weight distributions and the physical conductance characteristics of the memristor devices, we incorporate a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence regularization term during training, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 2557. Based on this, a closed-loop off-chip weight transfer framework is established to calibrate the hardware states against the trained model (Supplementary Fig. 26)

The necessity of the CDCM architecture is rigorously validated in Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 27, which compares the achievable weight space of different hardware implementations against the algorithmic requirements. In a standard BNN trained via variational inference, the optimal weights require a diverse combination of mean and variance values scattered across a broad 2D plane. Conventional single-memristor (SM) devices, constrained by their intrinsic physics, are restricted to a narrow, quasi-linear trajectory, failing to cover the required solution space due to the rigid coupling between conductance and noise. In stark contrast, our CDCM-based approach successfully decouples these parameters. By systematically sweeping the programming voltages across both channels (see Methods for the detailed traversal protocol), we experimentally collect 384 independent (μ, σ) data points, achieving full coverage of the target distribution. A zoomed-in inspection of this mapping, however, reveals a slight residual offset between the mapped CDCM states and the ideal software weight distributions, stemming from the discretization constraint of the finite hardware state library (Supplementary Fig. 28). Despite this minor mismatch, this effective mapping capability directly translates into system performance (Fig. 5c): while the SM-based system suffers a catastrophic accuracy drop to ~45% (near random guess), the CDCM-based system maintains a recognition accuracy of 79.08%, comparable to the theoretical software baseline (80.11%). A confusion matrix computed over a representative subset of the test set is shown in Fig. 5d, confirming that the hardware-implemented BNN maintains strong classification performance, with minimal misclassification between the two activity classes.

To visualize the high-dimensional input data, we employ t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) (Fig. 5e), a dimensionality reduction technique that projects each input sample into a two-dimensional space such that similar data points appear in close proximity, while dissimilar ones are well separated. In this projection, correctly classified test samples are shown in blue, misclassified samples in orange, and OOD samples—corresponding to the unseen activity—in red. Notably, most misclassifications occur near the boundaries between clusters in the t-SNE map, suggesting that these inputs are inherently ambiguous or noisy, leading to high aleatoric uncertainty. A detailed visualization of the latent feature space for all classes is provided in Supplementary Fig. 29.

To further investigate this observation, Fig. 5f presents the probability density distribution of the total predictive uncertainty, defined as the sum of aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties58. The distribution is stratified by prediction outcome: blue for correct predictions, orange for incorrect ones, and red for OOD inputs. A clear trend emerges: samples with low total uncertainty are predominantly classified correctly, whereas high-uncertainty regions are enriched with errors and OOD examples. This relationship is quantified in Fig. 5g, which shows that when the total uncertainty is below a threshold of 0.6, the probability of correct classification reaches 96.3%. As uncertainty increases, classification accuracy declines monotonically. This result underscores a key advantage of BNNs: by explicitly quantifying uncertainty, the model provides a reliable measure of its own confidence. Such self-awareness enables risk-aware decision-making—for instance, deferring high-uncertainty predictions to human experts in safety-critical applications like healthcare or autonomous systems—thereby enhancing overall system reliability59. The decomposition of total uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic components is further analyzed in Supplementary Fig. 30. For comparison, the uncertainty decomposition for the software BNN and single-memristor (SM) baselines are provided in Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32, respectively. Furthermore, Supplementary Fig. 33 presents the statistical distribution of prediction outcomes filtered by the optimal uncertainty threshold, validating the effectiveness of the risk-aware decision mechanism.

Discussion

In summary, we demonstrate a physics-driven hardware solution for probabilistic computing by exploiting the intrinsic ionic stochasticity of vertical-gated memristors. The proposed CDCM architecture successfully overcomes the long-standing signal-noise coupling dilemma in resistive switching devices. By implementing a differential readout strategy, we achieve orthogonal control over the statistical mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ) of the synaptic weights, enabling the precise mapping of arbitrary Gaussian distributions required by variational inference. Benchmarking on a multimodal sensor fusion task confirmed that the CDCM-based BNN not only matches software-level classification accuracy but also provides robust uncertainty quantification for out-of-distribution detection. This work bridges the gap between stochastic device physics and algorithmic reliability, offering a scalable and energy-efficient paradigm for trustworthy edge intelligence in safety-critical applications.

METHODS

Materials

1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis (trifluoromethyl sulfonyl) imide (EMIM MeSO4), poly (ethylene vinylidene fluoride-cohexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP), and PBTTT are obtained from Sigma- Aldrich. TrTPFB is purchased from Strem Chemicals, Inc.

Preparation of doping layer semiconductor solutions

TrTPFB and PBTTT are dissolved in chlorobenzene (CB) to form solutions at concentrations of 10 and 5 g L−1, respectively. Notably, PBTTT solutions need to be preheated at 60 °C for 2 h before usage. Then, the TrTPFB and PBTTT solutions are mixed according to a doping ratio of 2:8.

Preparation of ion gel solutions

To prepare a 1.66 wt% ion gel, the copolymer PVDF-HFP at a concentration of 100 g L−1 is dissolved in 2-butanone, and then EMIM MeSO4 is mixed 1:100 by volume with the PVDF-HFP solution and stirred vigorously for 12 h at room temperature.

Preparation of ZnO thin films

Pure zinc target material is used, and magnetron DC sputtering is employed. After evacuating the gas pressure to 5 × 10−4 Pa, the sputtering process is as follows (constant pressure 0.4 Pa, Ar 20 sccm, O 20 sccm, power 30 W). After pre-sputtering for 10 min, the sputtering time on the sample is 30 min, resulting in a ZnO thin film with a thickness of approximately 77 nm.

Preparation of ITO thin films

Using an ITO target (In2O3:SnO2 = 90: 10 wt%), magnetron DC sputtering is employed. After evacuating the gas pressure to 5 × 10−4 Pa, the sputtering process is as follows (constant pressure 0.15 Pa, Ar 16.4 sccm, power 40 W). After pre-sputtering for 10 min, the sputtering time on the sample is 15 min, resulting in an ITO thin film with a thickness of approximately 20–30 nm.

Fabrication of ion-gel/ZnO heterostructure memristor

The Ion-gel/ZnO heterostructure memristor, consisting of a vertical ITO/ZnO/Ion-gel/doping layer/Ag stack, is fabricated using a combination of magnetron sputtering, solution spin-coating, and thermal evaporation. Clean glass substrates are sequentially cleaned with deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol in an ultrasonic bath for 3 min each, followed by drying with a nitrogen stream. The ITO bottom electrodes are deposited via magnetron sputtering (as described above). Subsequently, a physical mask (thermal tape) is applied to pattern the active area, and the ZnO thin film is deposited onto the ITO electrodes using the sputtering parameters described above. The samples are then transferred to a nitrogen-filled glovebox. An ion-gel solution (1.66 wt%) is spin-coated onto the ZnO film at 2000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 90 °C on a hot plate for 2 h to form the solid electrolyte. The doping layer is subsequently spin-coated at 1500 rpm and annealed at 90 °C for 20 min. Finally, 50 nm-thick Ag top electrodes are deposited by thermal evaporation. All fabrication processes are conducted in an inert argon (or nitrogen) atmosphere within a glovebox.

Fabrication of the CDCM device

The CDCM devices are fabricated on clean glass substrates following the same cleaning protocol described above. The bottom electrode patterns, defining the left (gl) and right (gr) branches with a channel gap of 30 μm, are defined via photolithography. The specific electrode layout is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2. Subsequent fabrication steps—including ZnO deposition, ion-gel spin-coating, doping layer formation, and top electrode evaporation—are identical to the manufacturing processes detailed for the Ion-gel/ZnO heterostructure memristor.

Characterization and measurement

The film morphology is measured by AFM (Bioscope system) and SEM (TESCAN MIRA3). The XRD patterns of ZnO film are recorded by D/max 2550 (Rigaku) under Cu Kα (λ = 1.5406 Å) irradiation. The electrical properties are characterized with a Keysight B2912A in the glovebox.

Parameter space traversal for weight distribution mapping

For each voltage combination, the device underwent 100 consecutive erase-program-read cycles, and the statistical mean and variance of the resulting differential output current (Iout) were extracted. Furthermore, for some programmed voltage pairs, a common-mode programming strategy was applied: while keeping the differential voltage |Vpl − Vpr| constant, the absolute values of Vpl and Vpr were simultaneously increased. This method allows for appropriate adjustment of the standard deviation (σ) of the output distribution without changing the mean (μ), thus achieving orthogonal control of Gaussian statistics. By traversing all combinations, the experiment collected 384 independent (μ, σ) data points, covering the entire realizable parameter space of the CDCM hardware.

Neural network architecture design

The proposed uncertainty-aware human activity recognition system is built upon a multi-modal Bayesian Neural Network architecture, as illustrated in Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 24. The network is composed of two primary stages: a software-based feature backbone for spatio-temporal processing and a hardware-implemented probabilistic classifier core.

Feature backbone

To effectively fuse heterogeneous sensory data, the feature extractor employs a dual-stream architecture:

IMU Branch: Designed to process 1D time-series data from the 6-axis IMU (acceleration and angular velocity). This branch consists of stacked Residual Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) Blocks, followed by max-pooling layers. The SE mechanism adaptively recalibrates channel-wise feature responses, enhancing the network’s sensitivity to dynamic motion patterns.

TMP_TOF Branch: Designed to process 2D spatial data from the Thermopile (temperature grid) and ToF (depth map) sensors. This branch utilizes standard Convolutional (Conv) layers followed by max-pooling layers to extract spatial heat distribution and depth geometry features.

The abstract features extracted from both branches are concatenated and fed into a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) module. The LSTM models the sequential dependencies and temporal evolution of the activities, compressing the multi-modal information into a compact latent vector z (dimension d=10).

BNN classifier

The final classification stage is a Bayesian Fully Connected (FC) layer with an input dimension of 10 and an output dimension of 5 (corresponding to the 5 target activity classes). The weights of this layer are represented by a normal distribution Nμ,σ2. This probabilistic core transforms the latent vector z into predictive probability distributions, enabling the quantification of both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties for robust OOD detection.
