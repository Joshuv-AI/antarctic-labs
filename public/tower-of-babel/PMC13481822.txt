The Interpretability of the Neurogenic Bladder Symptom Score
Authors: Jeremy Dai, Erica Li, Rose Khavari, Jorge Moreno‐Palacios, Cristiano M Gomes, Jeremy Myers, John T Stoffel, Sean Elliott, Sara M Lenherr, Blayne Welk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo define mild, moderate and severe symptom categories of the neurogenic bladder symptom score (NBSS) and NBSS‐short form (SF), and to calculate the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of the NBSS‐SF.MethodsWe used existing datasets from the original NBSS validation study (n = 230 patients with spinal cord injury (SCI), multiple sclerosis, and spina bifida), and a large cross‐sectional study of people with SCI (n = 1479) who completed the NBSS. We used the previously validated mild‐moderate‐severe categories of the ICIQ‐UI, IPSS, and the SCI‐QOL Bladder complications scale to define patients with mild, moderate or severe symptoms, and mapped these to the NBSS scores for the related NBSS domains of incontinence, storage/voiding, and consequences, respectively. We validated these NBSS score categories using four data sets, and by examining the global bladder problem rating. An anchor‐based MCID of the NBSS‐SF was determined.ResultsUsing the median/interquartile range of the associated NBSS domain score of people who were in the moderate category of the ICIQ‐UI, IPSS, and the SCI‐QOL Bladder complications, we calculated the severity categories for the NBSS total score: 0–32 (mild), 33–48 (moderate), and 49–74 (severe). These score categories produced significantly different NBSS scores in 29/32 comparisons in four different study populations. The global bladder problem had good agreement with each domain's categorical scale. Finally, the group‐level MCID of the NBSS‐SF total score was calculated as an improvement of 1.8.ConclusionsWe have determined interpretability ranges for the NBSS and NBSS‐SF. This can help interpret patient and clinical study results.Keywords: neurogenic bladder, patient reported outcome measures, quality of life

Introduction

Patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) serve an important purpose by measuring constructs such as patient‐perceived symptoms and quality of life (QOL), which cannot be measured using other tools. There are few PROMs that have been specifically designed for use in neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD) [1]. The neurogenic bladder symptom score (NBSS) was designed as a symptom score (with a separate QOL question) for use specifically in people with spinal cord injury (SCI), multiple sclerosis (MS), or spina bifida (SB). It is based on the underlying theory that there are a set of related symptoms and consequences related to NLUTD that are shared among these adult patients. Previous research has shown it is a valid and reliable PROM that assesses three domains (incontinence, storage, voiding and consequences), independent of bladder management technique [2, 3].

The NBSS has been used in clinical research over the last decade; additional studies have confirmed the validity of it in independent populations [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9], that it is responsive to change [10], and a short form has been created [11]. However, to date, the interpretability of the NBSS score itself has not been formally assessed. Interpretability is “the degree to which one can assign qualitative meaning to an instrument's quantitative scores” [12]. This is usually done by assigning severity categories to score ranges to facilitate clinical decision‐making and create a better understanding of the PROM's numerical score. For example, the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) assigns a category of “mild” to scores between 0 and 7, and “severe” to scores of 20–35. An additional element of interpretability is the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), which provides a threshold for distinguishing statistically detectable change from change that is clinically meaningful to patients. Our objective was to use existing data on the NBSS to present an empirically derived interpretive model for the NBSS and NBSS‐SF.

Methods

We used existing study data on the NBSS to better understand how scores can be used to guide clinical decisions, evaluate treatment outcomes, and be used in the clinical context of shared decision‐making. We incorporated data from the original NBSS validation study (which included a cross‐sectional assessment of 230 patients with MS, SCI or SB) [3], the large neurogenic bladder research group (NBRG) SCI registry (which remotely followed 1479 adult patients with SCI from across North America) [13] and two cross‐cultural adaption and validation studies of the NBSS into Spanish [5] and Portuguese [7]. Finally, we used data from the patients with NLUTD (n = 67) who participated in a clinical trial comparing the tolerability of 5 versus 20 injection sites of intravesical Onabotulinum toxin A [14]. All of these studies received appropriate ethics approval for their conduct, and separate approval was not required for this study as it was a secondary analysis of existing deidentified data.

To understand the interpretability of the NBSS, we relied on co‐administered validated questionnaires with a strong relationship to each of the domains of the NBSS and their known interpretability. The International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire‐Urinary Incontinence (ICIQ‐UI) short form questionnaire consists of six questions, of which three are summed together for a score of 0–21 [15]. This score correlates with the frequency, amount and impact of urinary incontinence, and is classified as mild [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], moderate [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12], severe or very severe [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. The International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) includes questions on storage and voiding symptoms in the context of BPH, and has severity groups of mild (0–7), moderate (8–19), and severe (20–35) [16]. Finally, the Spinal cord injury‐QOL (SCI‐QOL) bladder complications scale consists of five questions primarily about urinary tract infections; raw scores are converted to standardized score relative to a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10 [17].

We then identified patients from the original NBSS validation study with moderate scores (based on the categories above) of the ICIQ‐UI SF and IPSS, and individuals in the Neurogenic Bladder SCI registry with a moderate score on the SCI‐QOL bladder complications scale (defined as within one standard deviation of the mean, 40‐60). The appropriate NBSS domain scores (ICIQ‐UI SF and NBSS incontinence domain, IPSS and NBSS storage and voiding symptoms, and SCI‐QOL bladder complications and NBSS consequences) of participants scoring in the moderate range of the ICIQ‐UI, IPSS, or SCI‐QOL were identified, and the 25–75th percentiles of the NBSS domain scores of participants were identified. We used this score range to define tertiles for the NBSS score (mild, < 25th percentile, moderate 25th–75th percentile, and severe > 75th percentile). We also performed this process using the NBSS‐SF scores, which were calculated for the same domains from a subset of questions from the full NBSS [11].

To assess the validity of these NBSS score categories, we used these new severity ranges to compare the agreement with global problem ratings, and to look at the distribution of patients in the original NBSS validation study, the NBRG SCI registry, and two cross‐cultural adaptation studies from Mexico and Brazil.

An additional issue around interpretability is determining the MCID of a PROM. To assess this in the NBSS‐SF, we used an anchor‐based method, and the data from NLUTD patients who were randomized to a different number of Onabotulinum toxin A injection sites [14]. Patients completed the NBSS‐SF prior to their injection, and then again 4–8 weeks later. At this second time point, they also completed the Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI‐I) question with seven possible responses of: very much better, much better, a little better, no change, a little worse, much worse, or very much worse. The mean NBSS‐SF score change of patients who answered “a little better” was used as the MCID [18].

Statistical Analysis

We used Spearman's rank correlations to assess the relationship between the NBSS domains and individual related scales; an r > 0.70 was considered strong, r between 0.70 and 0.30 was considered moderate and r < 0.30 considered weak correlation [19]. Concordance between the new NBSS categories and the relevant related PROM was measured using a weighted κ, a measure of interrater agreement between the two scales. A κ > 0.40 was considered moderate or better agreement. We used ANOVA to determine significant differences between the NBSS domain scores of the mild, moderate, and severe categories; we used the Bonferroni post hoc test for pairwise comparisons, and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

PROMs are a valuable tool in the shared decision‐making process for people living with NLUTD [20]. When balancing medical safety, personal preferences, and psychosocial factors, these tools are an essential way to assess baseline QOL and symptom burden, and monitor treatment response or change over time [1, 21]. The NBSS was first validated in 2014 [3] as a measure of symptom burden and bladder‐related QOL in people with NLUTD. It is designed to be completed by people using a variety of bladder management methods. Since then, it has been licensed to over 250 researchers/care providers from more than 45 countries. It has been used as an outcome in numerous clinical studies and large registries [13, 14, 22, 23, 24]. Many validated language and cultural adaptations are available and have independently assessed and confirmed the reliability and validity of this PROM [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. In the United States, the National Institute of Health's expert review panel identified the NBSS as a supplemental measure for neurourology studies [25].

In this study, we have expanded the understanding of the NBSS, by providing a three‐level interpretation for a given total or domain score. There was moderate agreement (for the NBSS incontinence and storage/voiding domains) and weak agreement (for the NBSS consequences domain) with the PROMs used for concurrent validity. This is expected and similar to what was reported in the original NBSS validation article [3]; too high an agreement would suggest that the PROM used for validity and the NBSS domain are essentially the same, which was not expected or the goal in creating the NBSS. The NBSS incontinence domain‐ICIQ‐UI SF has the highest weighted kappa, and visually, in Figure 1 has a strong alignment within categories, particularly the mild category. The NBSS storage/voiding domain‐IPSS has a similar kappa, and in Figure 1 the strongest alignment is in the severe category. This likely reflects the ability of incontinence measures to consistently identify patients with minimal leakage, whereas storage and voiding symptoms may exhibit greater variability at the mild end of the spectrum and be more sensitive to the wording and response options of different questionnaires.

By using the interpretation of other PROMs with known severity levels, we propose mild‐moderate‐severe categories for the NBSS (Table 3). These numbers can be used to better understand a patient's total score (“your NBSS total score of 16 is the mild range for a patient with NLUTD”) and to better identify problem areas (“your NBSS incontinence domain score of 25 is severe and primarily responsible for your total NBSS score of 35, so we should focus on addressing your incontinence today”). In addition, this adds to the NBSS's ability to be used in clinical studies, where proportions of patients in the different severity categories can be reported, and a change across categories can be used to report a more meaningful degree of change (“the number of patients in the severe incontinence category decreased by 50%”). These score categories have good face validity, with statistically different scores seen between the mild‐moderate‐severe categories for 29/32 comparisons from 4 independent datasets (Table 4). In addition, worsening NBSS score categories are paralleled by an increase in the global bladder problem ranking (Table 5).

We also derived categories for the NBSS‐SF. The NBSS‐SF is a subset of unchanged questions from the NBSS. These categories can be used in a similar manner to understand the SF scores specifically. We also investigated the MCID of the NBSS‐SF using an anchor‐based method. This is distinct from the previous distribution‐based method we used to estimate the MCID for the full NBSS [4]. Our MCID value of 1.8 represents 6% of the NBSS‐SF total score, which is similar to the estimated full NBSS MCID of 7.7 points (10% of the NBSS total score). This is also comparable to values reported for other urologic PROMs, such as the IPSS (3/35 points, 9% of the scale [26], and the ICIQ‐UI SF (3/21 points, 14% of the scale [27]. Knowing these values is essential for the use of the NBSS/NBSS‐SF in clinical studies, and helps to determine what change is perceived by a group of patients as “a little better” and therefore potentially meaningful and noticeable.

There are some important limitations that should be acknowledged. Interpretability thresholds and MCID are inherently subjective, and our results reflect average group‐level results, which may not translate exactly across the varied spectrum of non‐MS/SCI NLUTD patients. The derived severity bands are not symmetrical, reflecting floor and ceiling effects within the NBSS as well as differences in symptom distribution across the source populations. While a strength of our work is that our assessment of scores within each severity group is similar across different study populations, there is still variability that is likely explained by the specific patient population, neurologic disease severity, bladder management method, and comorbidity profiles. There is no universally correct interpretability threshold for PROMs, and likely different patient populations and study methods would result in somewhat different numerical cutoffs for the three NBSS severity categories. The MCID we calculated is from NLUTD patients who improved after intravesical Onabotulinum toxin A. There were no patients who got worse based on the PGI‐I, and therefore, the MCID for worsening of symptoms may not be the same as the MCID we calculated for symptom improvement. It is also important to acknowledge that our anchor‐based MCID should be used for group‐level differences and does not represent an individual‐level MCID. In the future, a formal assessment of interpretability would be useful to validate our interpretability results; however, this is challenging and would require a large, prospective longitudinal observation study nested with a clinical intervention trial, with a large enough patient population to represent several NLUTD phenotypes.

Conclusions

We have presented clinically meaningful interpretability cutoffs for the NBSS and NBSS‐SF that classify patients as having mild, moderate, or severe symptom burden, both overall and within individual domains. We also calculated an anchor‐based MCID of approximately 2 points for the NBSS‐SF, supporting its use in evaluating meaningful change in groups of patients with NLUTD in both clinical practice and research.
