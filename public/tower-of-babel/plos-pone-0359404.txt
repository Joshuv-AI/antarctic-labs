The evolution of place identity narratives: Long-term textual practices in the Chinese Baroque Historic Area

Zongcheng Hu, Li Shao

ABSTRACT
In the processes of urban renewal and place branding, narratives of place identity undergo continuous evolution. As vital sites for local development, historic districts have seen their identity narratives become key strategies in urban marketing and spatial planning. However, existing research has overlooked the complexity of the dynamic evolution of place identity narratives, thereby limiting the effectiveness of planning, design, and communication in place branding. To systematically examine the long-term dynamic evolution of place identity narratives in historic districts, this study focuses on the Chinese Baroque Historic Area in Harbin, China. A total of 24 planning documents, news reports, and promotional texts from 2007 to 2024 were collected and categorized into three stages: the early stage, the middle stage, and the late stage. Subsequently, employing both quantitative analysis using the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model and qualitative research methods, this study systematically reveals the characteristics of themes across different phases through dimensions such as information priority, structural dominance, and degree of dispersion. It further analyzes the evolutionary features of information structures across texts spanning these phases. The findings reveal a distinct phased evolution in the information structure of the place identity narrative for the Chinese Baroque Historic Area: the early stage was dominated by planning and policy documents, featuring highly concentrated information with institutional themes taking precedence. The middle stage saw a substantial increase in news and promotional texts, shifting the information structure from a high-concentration to a multi-thematic coexistence, accompanied by a redistribution of information priorities. The late stage is dominated by promotional and new media texts, in which themes of experience, image, and daily life gradually take a central position, leading to a relatively balanced information structure. Overall, the place identity narrative continuously evolves and gradually stabilizes through adjustments in information priority and restructuring of relational dynamics. The findings reveal the dynamic mechanisms and structural evolution of place identity narratives, providing important insights for advancing urban renewal and place branding practices.

1. Introduction As competition among global cities intensifies, narratives of place identity have become increasingly crucial texts, repeatedly produced in urban planning and management, public governance, and social communication practices [ 1 , 2 ]. Against the backdrop of industrial restructuring and the transformation of urban development models, an increasing number of cities are leveraging place marketing and branding strategies to continuously enhance their image and regional appeal through diverse textual practices—including planning documents, news coverage, and promotional materials [ 3 , 4 ]. In this process, place identity is not merely a naturally formed cultural outcome but rather a narrative construct continually shaped, reconfigured, and stabilized through sustained textual production practices. Although existing research has delved into the social significance of place identity narratives, multi-stakeholder participation mechanisms, and narrative strategies from perspectives such as human geography, urban studies, and place branding [ 5 , 6 ]. However, its analytical framework often implicitly assumes a linear generative hypothesis, tending to interpret place identity narratives as outcomes of rational design and natural operation. This perspective overlooks the inherent nature of place identity narratives as textual information systems. It also pays scant attention to their non-linear evolutionary characteristics and structural stabilization processes, which emerge through the continuous selection, reorganization, and compression of informational elements during the long-term, multi-stage production of texts. From the perspective of information science, the production of place identity narratives is simultaneously an outcome of information operations. The formation of place identity narratives does not originate from a single stage or a single type of text. Instead, it emerges through prolonged textual practices where information elements carried by texts from different stages undergo continuous adjustment and reorganization in terms of information priority, structural dominance, and degree of dispersion. Ultimately, certain information units attain relatively stable positions within the overall information structure. Therefore, it is necessary to place identity narratives within the analytical framework of long-term textual information systems, systematically examining their generation, evolution, and stabilization mechanisms from the perspective of information structure. Moreover, as crucial sites for urban spatial reproduction and meaning encoding, the identity narratives of historic districts have become indispensable strategic tools in urban marketing and planning governance [ 7 ]. However, existing research generally treats place identity narratives as simplistic design outcomes, overlooking their non-linear evolutionary characteristics manifested through information selection, reorganization, and structural adjustments within long-term textual practices. This approach limits structural interpretations of place branding formation mechanisms and their communicative effects. Therefore, it is necessary to systematically examine the long-term dynamics of the generation of place identity narratives in historic districts from the perspective of information structure evolution. This approach will provide a more refined and structured theoretical framework for urban renewal and place branding practices. Therefore, to systematically examine the long-term dynamic evolution of place identity narratives in historic districts, this study focuses on the Chinese Baroque Historic Area in Harbin, Heilongjiang Province, China. It conceptualizes the planning documents, news reports, and promotional materials generated during the district’s planning, construction, and promotion phases as a unified, continuously evolving textual information system. This study focuses on the organizational patterns of internal information structures within textual systems and their evolutionary processes across developmental stages. This study employs a quantitative analysis method based on the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model, combined with qualitative interpretation, to identify latent information themes within texts from different stages [ 8 , 9 ]. This study further translates topic model outputs into information structure metrics to enable systematic analysis of the evolutionary characteristics of information structures within long-term text systems. The research findings reveal the long-term processes of formation and reconfiguration in the narratives of place identity in historic districts. This helps uncover their structural evolutionary patterns and provides more refined, systematic theoretical support for urban renewal and place branding practices. Therefore, focusing on the information system formed by the long-term planning and marketing texts of the Chinese Baroque Historic Area, this study examines the mechanisms of generation, evolution, and stabilization of information structures across phased texts. It specifically explores the following three interrelated questions: (1) What information structural characteristics does the text system related to the Chinese Baroque Historic Area exhibit at the overall level? (2) What distinct informational functions do narrative texts from different phases perform in the construction of place identity narratives? (3) How do thematic information elements undergo non-linear restructuring across phases through shifts in information priority and structural dominance? 2. Literature review In the context of urban renewal and place branding, place identity research originated in human geography and environmental psychology, primarily focusing on how place identity is formed through spatial experiences and social memory [ 10 , 11 ]. With the rise of place marketing and city branding practices, place identity has gradually been incorporated into urban governance and development research, serving as a crucial narrative foundation for attracting tourists, capital, and external resources [ 12 ]. Within this research framework, place identity is no longer understood as a naturally occurring cultural outcome but rather as a crucial policy tool continuously shaped through planning and construction, narrative construction, and communication practices [ 13 , 14 ]. Throughout this process, narrative and storytelling approaches are widely regarded as crucial strategic tools for integrating local significance and addressing the specific cultural needs of consumer groups [ 6 ]. Accordingly, research on place identity narratives has moved beyond examining what cultural meanings are embedded in places and has increasingly focused on how complex, diverse, and sometimes conflicting elements of local information are selected, organized, and transformed into relatively coherent public representations. Existing studies have primarily examined the strategic construction and dissemination mechanisms of place identity narratives, as well as the exclusionary effects and sociopolitical consequences arising from selective representation, thereby advancing our understanding of the logic of narrative construction and its broader social implications. However, less attention has been paid to how diverse and competing elements of local information are continuously selected, reinforced, and reconfigured through long-term practices, eventually producing relatively stable structures of place identity narratives. Therefore, a systematic review of theoretical developments concerning the construction and critique of place identity narratives provides an essential foundation for further investigating their dynamic formation and long-term evolution. Regarding the strategic construction and dissemination of place identity narratives, numerous studies indicate that, due to the inherent ambiguity and complexity of places, narratives of place identity often require selective expression and symbolic processing to simplify complex local elements and historical cultures into recognizable, communicable narratives [ 15 ]. In practice, urban administrators and marketing agencies typically select limited geographic features, cultural symbols, or historical fragments to represent the locality as a whole. By excluding discordant or “noisy” elements, they construct spatial imagery characterized by consistency and consumer appeal [ 16 , 17 ]. At the same time, the complex history and diverse meanings of local places are often compressed into specific time segments, forming selective memories and phased narrative structures [ 18 ]. Building on these insights, research on place storytelling has further developed a range of principles, frameworks, and communication models to enhance the practical applicability of narrative construction and improve its effectiveness in communication [ 19 , 20 ]. In addition, existing studies have examined how flagship architecture, iconic spaces, major events, and local cultural festivals contribute to strengthening the symbolic distinctiveness of places and enhancing their visibility among external audiences [ 21 , 22 ]. Existing studies have provided substantial insights into the design, packaging, and dissemination of place identity narratives. However, they have largely treated place identity narratives as outcomes characterized by relatively stable meanings and structures, while paying insufficient attention to the processes through which these narratives are formed. In particular, limited attention has been given to how different cultural information elements evolve, are adjusted, and are reconfigured across different stages of place branding practices. Therefore, further research is needed to examine the dynamic formation of place identity narratives and to reveal how diverse cultural information elements are continuously selected, reinforced, marginalized, and reorganized throughout place branding processes. Cultural selection and the exclusion of particular meanings in place identity narratives are often closely intertwined with specific power relations and their broader sociopolitical consequences. Place-image construction led by governments, development agencies, or local elites tends to privilege positive elements that support economic growth, tourism development, and urban competitiveness, while downplaying or obscuring poverty, social deprivation, spatial inequality, and contested local histories [ 16 , 17 ]. For example, some urban nostalgia narratives selectively connect historical commercial traditions with visions of global urban development, reinforcing historical memories that are conducive to place branding and external promotion, while excluding or marginalizing controversial historical episodes, political movements, and the social costs associated with urban renewal projects [ 23 ]. In response to such top-down control over narrative production, participatory and collaborative approaches to place branding have shifted attention toward the co-construction of place meanings among diverse actors. They emphasize that stakeholders may hold different, and sometimes competing, interpretations of place meanings, and argue that continuous interaction, dialogue, and negotiation are essential for enhancing the democratic legitimacy and social inclusiveness of place identity narratives [ 24 , 25 ]. Therefore, place identity narratives should not be understood as value-neutral cultural representations; rather, they are selective processes embedded in struggles over meaning, resource allocation, and power relations. Clarifying the selection of information within place identity narratives and the underlying power dynamics provides a theoretical foundation for examining how information elements are added, removed, weighted, and structurally reorganized across different stages of place branding. Overall, existing studies have examined the strategic construction of place identity narratives and their sociopolitical consequences from social, cultural, and governance perspectives, thereby advancing understanding of the mechanisms underlying narrative formation and its broader social implications. However, place identity narratives are not directly produced by a single event, text, or developmental stage; rather, they emerge as stage-specific structures formed through the continuous selection, translation of meanings, reinforcement, attenuation, and recombination of diverse local information elements throughout long-term processes of textual production. Traditional case studies, discourse analysis, and manual coding approaches have effectively explained cultural selection processes and the power relations underlying them. Nevertheless, these approaches are limited in their ability to systematically compare the coexistence and changing relative importance of multiple information elements across different stages of textual development, as well as to reveal how place identity narratives gradually acquire relatively stable structures through continuous processes of information adjustment. Therefore, understanding the dynamic formation of place identity narratives requires further attention to how information elements are organized, how structural relationships among them evolve, and how these mechanisms operate throughout long-term processes of textual production. From an information science perspective, place identity narratives can be understood as organizational outcomes emerging from long-term textual information systems. They are not directly generated by a single text or developmental stage; rather, they gradually take shape through the continuous selection, translation, and reconfiguration of information during the production of multiple textual sources, including planning documents, news reports, and promotional materials. Different information elements exhibit varying levels of prominence across different stages, eventually contributing to the formation of relatively stable information structures. The Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) model conceptualizes texts as probabilistic combinations of multiple latent topics, which aligns with the theoretical understanding that place identity narratives are composed of diverse information elements whose relative thematic weights continuously change over time. Compared with traditional content analysis approaches, LDA is capable of identifying latent thematic structures and translating processes of cultural selection and narrative restructuring into comparable distributions of topic probabilities. Although LDA has been widely applied in cultural tourism text analysis [ 26 , 27 ], existing studies have primarily focused on static topic identification, with limited attention paid to the processes of topic reinforcement, attenuation, and reconfiguration throughout long-term place branding practices. Furthermore, as important sites of local development, historic districts have increasingly incorporated identity narratives into urban marketing and spatial planning strategies. However, existing studies have paid insufficient attention to the complexity of the dynamic evolution of place identity narratives, thereby limiting the effectiveness of place branding planning, design, and communication. Therefore, this study employs LDA as a topic identification tool and integrates analyses of information priority and structural dominance to develop an analytical framework for long-term textual information structures, aiming to reveal the dynamic evolution of place identity narratives in historic districts. Therefore, this study adopts an information structure perspective to analyze the evolutionary process of place identity narratives within a long-term, multi-stage textual system. By introducing the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model, this study identifies latent information themes within texts related to the Chinese Baroque Historic Area. The outputs of the topic model are further translated into indicators of information structure, such as information priority, structural dominance, and degree of dispersion. This enables a systematic investigation of the non-linear restructuring process of information structures across different textual phases. This study aims to reveal how place identity narratives progressively achieve structural compression and stabilization through long-term textual practices, providing empirical support for research on the long-term evolution of complex social texts from an information science perspective. 3. Data and methods 3.1 Research area Existing research on narratives of place identity predominantly focuses on large-scale spatial units such as cities or regions, relatively neglecting the long-term construction process of identity narratives within small-scale urban spaces [ 28 , 29 ]. At the same time, existing research predominantly adopts a cross-sectional analytical approach, focusing on short-term or individual project operations, and thus struggles to systematically reveal the evolutionary mechanisms through which place identity narratives undergo continuous adjustment, reorganization, and gradual stabilization across different stages [ 30 , 31 ]. In contrast, the identity narrative of the Chinese Baroque Historic Area did not emerge as a one-time outcome. Instead, it evolved through a prolonged, multi-stage process of sustained planning and governance practices. This trajectory offers a relatively comprehensive illustration of how place identity narratives form, adjust, and stabilize over extended periods of textual production. Therefore, this study selects the Chinese Baroque Historic Area as its research subject to explore the evolutionary mechanisms of information structures over the long term in texts. The Chinese Baroque Historic Area is located in Harbin City, Heilongjiang Province, China. This district forms the core of Harbin’s old town and has witnessed the city’s developmental transformations. Following the construction of the China-Eastern Railway in 1896, Russia established a modern city with Western characteristics in Harbin. Thanks to the infusion of European civilization, Harbin rapidly transformed from a fishing village into a major metropolis in the Far East [ 32 ]. In the 1920s, Harbin emerged as a major international metropolis in Northeast Asia. The Daowai District, home to the Chinese Baroque Historic Area, experienced unprecedented socioeconomic prosperity, boasting hundreds of industries—including money exchanges, banks, grain trading, general merchandise, and export trade—and thousands of businesses. Many affluent Chinese businesspeople who have achieved commercial success have built buildings with Western-style facades to meet their operational needs while preserving the traditional Chinese courtyard layout in their floor plans. Furthermore, decorative details incorporate auspicious traditional symbols such as bats, pomegranates, endless knots, golden toads, and peonies [ 33 ]. Because these Sino-Western hybrid buildings share certain similarities with European Baroque architecture, a Japanese scholar coined the term “Chinese Baroque” for this architectural style in 1985. After the 1990s, local commerce and trade gradually declined. Numerous old buildings deteriorated due to long-term neglect, while infrastructure and sanitation conditions progressively worsened. Ultimately, the neighborhood gradually devolved into an urban slum and an economically underdeveloped area. To advance tourism-driven commercial development and rebrand the city, the local government launched continuous preservation and renewal projects since 2000, renaming the revitalized district the “the Chinese Baroque Historic Area”. Through sustained planning, construction, and promotional efforts, the district has gradually transformed into a multifunctional space integrating historical and cultural displays, tourism consumption, and urban image-building. The renewed Chinese Baroque Historic Area is enclosed by Jingyu Street, Jingyang Street, Nanxun Street, and Nansidao Street, as shown in Fig 1 . Based on its spatial organization and functional positioning, the district is divided into four thematic zones: a leisure and commercial zone between Jingyang Street and Nantoudao Street, a food and dining zone between Nantoudao Street and Nanerdao Street, a cultural and creative zone between Nanerdao Street and Nansandao Street, and a commercial residential and leisure zone between Nansandao Street and Nansidao Street. These functional zones, together with the major streets, historic buildings, cultural facilities, experiential nodes, and commercial clusters, constitute the post-renewal physical spatial framework of the district and support its transformation from a traditional residential area into a cultural tourism and consumption space. This complexity makes traditional classification and clustering approaches based on lexical similarity insufficient for fully revealing the underlying semantic structures embedded within the texts. Topic models reconstruct the semantic space of the text through probabilistic generation mechanisms, providing a new analytical framework for identifying latent topic structures and semantic organization patterns [ 34 , 35 ]. Among these approaches, the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) model, as a probabilistic topic model, does not require predefined classification frameworks. Instead, it represents documents as probabilistic combinations of multiple topics with varying weights and automatically identifies latent thematic structures within a corpus, thereby improving the objectivity and reproducibility of comparative analyses across texts [ 36 ]. By reducing subjective bias arising from predefined thematic categories and establishing a link between computational analysis and qualitative interpretation, LDA has been widely applied in studies of policy documents, strategic texts, and cultural tourism texts, including research on destination image construction, the evolution of tourism discourse, and the analysis of cultural representations [ 37 ]. However, existing studies have primarily focused on static topic identification, paying limited attention to the dynamic processes through which themes are reinforced, weakened, and reconfigured throughout long-term place identity narrative practices. Meanwhile, conventional LDA typically treats documents as independent analytical units, which limits its ability to capture the structural characteristics of longitudinal corpora involving variations across temporal stages and text types. Therefore, this study employs LDA as a topic identification tool and optimizes the number of topics using perplexity and topic coherence measures to improve the stability and interpretability of topic identification results. Based on the document–topic probability distributions generated by the LDA model, this study further extracts topic weights from texts across different stages, which serve as the foundation for subsequent information structure analysis. To reveal how key actors, including government departments, planning and construction institutions, and operational entities, select, interpret, and disseminate local cultural meanings through textual practices during the place branding process of historic districts, this study collects relevant texts generated throughout the renewal and branding process of the Chinese Baroque Historic Area in Harbin from 2007 to 2024. The textual corpus includes planning documents, policy-related materials, news reports, operational management documents, and marketing and promotional materials. It should be noted that this study does not aim to construct an annual textual time series or capture year-by-year changes in narratives. Instead, it focuses on the long-term evolution of information priorities, structural relationships, and modes of expression within place identity narratives across different development stages. Therefore, purposive sampling was employed to select texts based on their relevance to the district’s renewal and branding process, source reliability, and explanatory value for understanding the construction of place identity narratives. Specifically, texts were screened according to three criteria: relevance, non-duplication, and narrative completeness. Materials that were unrelated to the research topic, repeatedly reproduced, or lacking substantive narrative content were excluded, while texts reflecting processes of place identity narrative construction, including historical-cultural representation, spatial image formation, functional transformation, and brand communication, were retained. Ultimately, 24 representative texts were selected as the analytical corpus. Based on the practical functions and production contexts of the texts, this study first classified the corpus into three categories: planning and construction texts, adaptive reuse and operation texts, and marketing and promotion texts. Subsequently, considering key practical transformations during the renewal and place branding process of the historic district, the texts were further divided into three stages: the early stage (planning and construction stage, 2007–2012), the middle stage (activation and utilization stage, October 2013–May 2021), and the late stage (marketing and promotion stage, June 2021–2024). The temporal boundaries of these stages were defined according to critical milestones in the district’s development rather than the exact publication years of individual documents. Therefore, the assigned stage of each document reflects the practical context represented by the document rather than its publication year. Some documents published before or around the transition points were assigned to specific stages because they reflected transitional practices associated with the corresponding development processes. This stage division was not based on equal-length temporal intervals or derived retrospectively from textual results; rather, it was determined according to critical practical milestones in the district’s development, including changes in spatial construction, operational models, and dominant actors. These three stages reflect shifts in actors, practical objectives, and modes of textual production throughout the place branding process, while also corresponding to the transformation of physical spatial functions, as illustrated in Fig 3 . During the planning and construction stage, efforts primarily focused on historic building preservation, streetscape façade restoration, and courtyard space renovation ( Fig 3a – 3c ). During the activation and utilization stage, these spatial elements were further integrated with commercial, cultural, and tourism functions ( Fig 3d – 3f ). The middle stage (activation and utilization stage) corresponded to the transition from spatial construction to operational activation and began with the official opening and operation of the historic district in October 2013. The late stage (marketing and promotion stage) was defined by the transfer of operational and management authority of the district to Longcai Technology Group in May 2021, after which the focus further shifted toward smart district development, brand communication, and the creation of consumption-oriented experiences, comprising 9 texts. Although the number of texts varies across the three stages, this difference does not result from imbalanced sampling but reflects changes in text production mechanisms, participating actors, and practical demands at different stages. The absence of texts for certain years does not indicate a discontinuity in the development process; rather, it reflects variations in the intensity, purpose, and form of textual production during different stages. The planning and construction stage was primarily led by government departments and planning and construction institutions, and the corresponding texts mainly focused on spatial planning, construction implementation, and physical transformation. As the district entered the adaptive reuse and marketing and promotion stages, the number of participating actors gradually increased, while operational management, commercial investment promotion, cultural display, and branding activities expanded, resulting in a greater diversity of textual materials. This study does not consider differences in text types as direct causes of changes in place identity narratives. Instead, such differences are understood as manifestations of varying practical objectives and action logics across different stages. The focus of this study is the structure of place identity narratives and their evolutionary processes as jointly represented across multiple types of texts. To improve the ability of the LDA topic model to identify the evolutionary characteristics of place identity narratives, reduce textual noise, and retain information related to historical representation, spatial meanings, cultural symbols, and place identity construction, this study first conducted systematic preprocessing of the textual corpus. The preprocessing procedures mainly included text format standardization, manual screening, Chinese word segmentation, stop-word removal, and vocabulary construction. First, all collected texts were manually reviewed to remove duplicated materials, irrelevant information, and texts lacking substantive narratives related to spatial, cultural, operational, or branding practices. Subsequently, Chinese word segmentation was performed using the Jieba segmentation tool (version 0.42.1) implemented in the Python 3.11 environment. To improve topic identification performance, this study constructed a customized stop-word list based on commonly used Chinese stop-word resources and the linguistic characteristics of planning, policy, operational, and promotional texts. Specifically, procedural and governance-related expressions, temporal and process-oriented terms, highly abstract general expressions, and entity terms directly referring to the research object or administrative spaces were removed to reduce textual noise and avoid generating spurious topics dominated by place names or administrative terminology. For example, policy-oriented expressions such as “推进”, “加强”, “实施” and “落实” as well as general descriptive terms such as “方面”, “情况” and “内容” were removed. In addition, terms with limited discriminatory power for thematic differentiation, including “region”, “project”, and “function”, were excluded. Meanwhile, words associated with historical and cultural representations, spatial experiences, everyday life, local memories, and cultural symbols were retained to ensure that the model could capture the information characteristics and structural differences of place identity narratives across different development stages. Meanwhile, the LDA model generated a document–topic probability matrix (document-topic distribution), from which topic weights across different stages were further calculated and used as the basis for subsequent information structure analysis. 3.3 Information structure analysis Based on the topic probability distributions obtained from the LDA model, this study further conducts an information structure analysis of place identity narratives. To clarify the concept of information structure used in the subsequent analysis, this study conceptualizes place identity narratives as a dynamic information structure composed of multiple cultural information elements, based on the theoretical understanding that local cultural elements are continuously selected, reinforced, and reorganized throughout the place branding process. This information structure not only reflects the relative importance of different topics at specific stages but also captures the degree of structural concentration among topics and the processes of information adjustment across different development stages. Based on three analytical dimensions—information importance, structural concentration, and stage-based variation—this study develops an information structure analysis framework consisting of four indicators: information priority, structural dominance, information dispersion, and information compression ( Table 3 ). Specifically, information priority measures the topic weight of a specific theme within the text corpus of a given stage and its relative importance in the overall narrative structure. Structural dominance measures the extent to which information resources are concentrated on dominant topics, reflecting the degree of concentration in topic weight distributions within stage-specific narrative structures. Information dispersion measures the magnitude of changes in the weight of a specific topic across different development stages. S D s = ∑ k = 1 K S s k 2 (4) Where S D s represents the structural dominance of stage s , with a theoretical range of [ 1 / K , 1]. When topic weights are evenly distributed across all topics, S D s approaches its minimum value of 1 / K . When narrative resources become concentrated among a small number of dominant topics, the value increases. When the narrative structure is highly concentrated on a single topic, S D s approaches 1. Therefore, a higher value of this indicator indicates that place identity narratives are more concentrated around a limited number of dominant topics. Third, to identify the extent to which the importance of a specific topic changes across different stages, this study calculates information dispersion based on the range of topic weights across stages, as shown in Equation (5) . D k = m a x ( S s k ) − m i n ( S s k ) (5) Where D k represents the information dispersion of topic k . A higher value of this indicator indicates that the topic exhibits greater variation in weight across different stages and undergoes more substantial adjustments in its narrative position. Fourth, to measure the concentration of stage-level topic structures from the perspective of overall distribution, this study constructs information compression based on Shannon entropy. First, the entropy of the topic distribution within each stage is calculated, as shown in Equation (6) . H s = − ∑ k = 1 K S s k l n S s k (6) The information compression index is then obtained through further normalization, as shown in Equation (7) . I C s = 1 − H s l n K (7) Where I C s represents the information compression of stage s . When topic weights are completely evenly distributed, the entropy reaches its maximum value and I C s approaches 0. When topic weights become concentrated on a small number of topics, entropy decreases and I C s increases. Therefore, a higher value of information compression indicates that the narrative structure of the corresponding stage is more concentrated. To clarify the measurement relationships among the proposed indicators, this study further distinguishes their respective units of observation and analytical functions. Information priority takes the “stage–topic” pair as the unit of observation and measures the relative importance of topics within different stages. Information dispersion takes the “topic” as the unit of observation and captures the magnitude of changes in topic weights across stages. Structural dominance and information compression take the “stage” as the unit of observation and describe the overall concentration of information structures. Together, the four indicators characterize the organizational features of place identity narrative information structures from three dimensions: topic importance, stage-based variation, and structural concentration. Specifically, structural dominance is calculated based on the sum of squared topic weights and emphasizes the concentration of dominant topics, whereas information compression is derived from Shannon entropy and reflects the overall balance of topic distributions. Although these two indicators are theoretically related, they capture different structural characteristics by emphasizing local dominance and overall distribution patterns, respectively. To further examine whether potential redundancy existed among the indicators within the proposed indicator system, this study conducted Pearson correlation analysis based on the calculated topic weights across different stages. The results revealed differentiated correlation patterns among the indicators. Specifically, structural dominance exhibited a strong positive correlation with information compression ( r = 0 . 999 , p < 0 . 001 ), which is consistent with the theoretical expectation that both indicators characterize the concentration of information structures. Although both indicators reflect structural concentration, structural dominance captures the concentration of topic weights, whereas information compression reflects the overall distributional characteristics of the information structure, indicating different analytical emphases. In addition, information priority showed a moderate positive correlation with information dispersion ( r = 0 . 658 , p = 0 . 001 ), suggesting that topics with higher weights tended to experience more pronounced adjustments across different stages. Except for these relationships, no other indicator combinations exhibited significant correlations ( p > 0 . 05 ). These results demonstrate that the proposed indicators provide complementary perspectives for characterizing the evolution of place identity narrative information structures, including topic importance, structural concentration, and the magnitude of stage-based changes. Furthermore, to determine whether changes in topic weights across different stages were statistically significant, this study conducted nonparametric tests based on document-level topic probability distributions generated by the LDA model. Because the number of texts varied across stages and the topic probability data did not satisfy the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed to compare differences in the distributions of document-level topic weights among different stages. For topic k , the test statistic is calculated as shown in Equation (8) . H k = 1 2 N ( N + 1 ) ∑ i = 1 g R i k 2 n i − 3 ( N + 1 ) (8) Where N represents the total number of texts, g represents the number of stages, n i represents the number of texts included in stage i , and R ik represents the rank sum of topic k in stage i . Ties in ranking were adjusted during the calculation. Because multiple topics were independently tested, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was further applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR) and reduce the risk of Type I errors caused by multiple comparisons. The adjusted p-values were used as the final criteria for determining statistical significance. As a continuously generated textual information system, planning texts, news texts, and propaganda texts do not replace one another. Instead, they progressively assume functions such as structural grounding, information translation, and narrative reinforcement. It should be noted that the textual information system examined in this study mainly captures the narrative production process of institutional actors involved in place branding, including governmental agencies, planning institutions, and operating organizations. These actors play critical roles in defining spatial meanings, organizing cultural representations, and communicating place images through formal textual practices. However, such institutionalized narratives represent only one dimension of place identity construction and do not fully encompass grassroots interpretations, everyday experiences, or public perceptions. In the early stages, planning designs and policy documents systematically define the spatial boundaries, functional attributes, and cultural positioning of historic districts through institutionalized language. Their core function is to transform complex spatial and historical entities into information objects that are recognizable and manageable by institutional systems. This phase exhibits a high concentration of information priority, reflecting the textual system’s primary demand for structural stability. This process was also closely connected with concrete spatial practices and professional actors’ interpretations of place. For example, during the planning stage of the Chinese Baroque Historic Area, planners attempted to reinterpret existing cultural resources through the introduction of European-style spatial forms, plaza systems, and new functional organizations. This demonstrates that place identity narratives were not only expressed through texts but were also embedded in processes of spatial transformation and material reconstruction. Nevertheless, residents’ diverse experiences of deteriorated living conditions before renewal and improved infrastructure after renewal indicate that meanings generated through spatial transformation were not necessarily identical to planners’ intended narratives, but were continuously reinterpreted through everyday experiences. With the intervention of news reporting and promotional texts, the informational function of textual systems gradually shifts from structural definition to information translation. Existing institutional and spatial information is reorganized into more narrative and contextual forms of expression, enabling its entry into public communication contexts. Correspondingly, information priorities are redistributed across multiple themes, and textual structures transition from single-dominant narratives to the coexistence of multiple themes. During this stage, commercial operators and spatial users gradually participated in the production of place meanings. For example, merchants’ descriptions of traditional clothing experiences, nostalgic street atmospheres, and film-related scenes demonstrate the transformation of the historic district from a planned cultural space into an experiential consumption space. However, these everyday practices did not always completely follow official branding narratives, but were continuously adjusted and negotiated among commercial demands, tourist experiences, and local memories. Therefore, the formation of place identity includes not only top-down narrative production but also the reinterpretation and negotiation of spatial meanings by different actors. In the later stages, promotional texts and new media texts build upon existing information structures while further emphasizing experiential narratives, visual imagery, and the repeated presentation of symbolic meanings. The function of these texts shifts from explanatory to dissemination-oriented, with certain high-salience themes securing more stable positions within the structure. This drives place identity narratives toward convergence within recognizable, communicable narrative frameworks. The increasing importance of tourism experiences, cultural activities, and visual consumption reflects the transformation of place identity from institutional definition toward publicly recognizable images. However, these processes do not imply passive acceptance of official narratives. Instead, visitor participation, online communication, and everyday spatial practices contribute to the continuous reproduction, adjustment, and reinterpretation of place meanings. Therefore, it is evident that the shift from information priority to structural dominance does not result from a linear replacement but rather reflects the progressive layering of information functions across different stages. This progressive process provides a functional-level explanation of the convergence trend in information structures within long-term textual systems. 5.3 Relational patterns and the information-structural outcome To further validate whether the aforementioned functional evolution manifests as stable information structural configurations at the relational level, this study introduces a keyword co-occurrence network analysis. This builds upon the completed thematic identification, information priority, and structural dominance analyses to supplement the examination of relational patterns among different thematic information elements within long-term textual systems. This analysis does not aim to identify new thematic types but rather to investigate whether different information elements form holistic structural configurations through sustained co-occurrence. This explores whether information structures exhibit holistic organizational characteristics at the relational level—beyond simple thematic juxtaposition—and provides relational support for understanding place identity narratives as information-structural outcomes. However, the central position of keywords within the textual network does not indicate that textual narratives alone determine place identity. Based on the aforementioned thematic role division and progressive information functionality, this study further proposes a “stage-based information compression mechanism” to characterize the intrinsic process by which information structures evolve from instability toward relative stability within long-term textual information systems. It is crucial to emphasize that the information compression referred to herein does not imply simplification of semantic content or reduction in overall information volume. Rather, it denotes the structural convergence trend exhibited in the distribution of information priority across comparative stages. As textual practices advance, the highly concentrated structural dominance observed in early stages gradually diminishes. Differences in information priority among themes continuously narrow, and the overall fluctuation range of thematic weight substantially decreases. This mechanism indicates that within the specific case of the Chinese Baroque Historic Area, the stabilization of place identity narratives is not achieved through linear replacement of themes or phased “creation”. Most core themes persist across different stages, with their evolution primarily manifesting as dynamic adjustments in relative priority and structural roles. Through the progressive layering of informational functions across different stages, the long-term textual practices examined in this study contributed to the gradual integration and stabilization of the place identity information structure. Therefore, rather than suggesting a universal evolutionary pathway applicable to all place branding processes, this case demonstrates how information structures may become gradually organized and stabilized under specific historical, institutional, and spatial conditions. 6. Conclusions This study analyzes the Chinese Baroque Historic Area in Harbin, China, systematically examining the information structure characteristics and evolutionary mechanisms of place identity narratives within long-term texts. This analysis is based on planning documents, news reports, and promotional materials formed across different stages. The main conclusions are as follows: (1) The textual materials concerning the Chinese Baroque Historic Area have evolved over a long, multi-stage process, forming a distinct phased information structure. This structure is neither static nor uniformly distributed; rather, it exhibits a dynamic progression from high concentration to the coexistence of multiple themes, and ultimately toward relative equilibrium. In the early stage, planning and policy documents were dominated by a single institutional theme. During the middle stage, information priorities were redistributed among multiple themes. In the late stage, experience- and image-oriented themes became the structural core, leading to a stabilization of the information structure. (2) Narrative texts at different stages serve distinct and progressively interconnected informational functions within place identity narratives. Early texts primarily establish informational structures and define semantic frameworks. Middle stage texts perform information translation, converting institutional information into narrative expressions suitable for public dissemination. Late texts drive the convergence of place identity toward stable, communicable narrative frameworks through narrative integration and reinforcement. (3) The reorganization of thematic information elements across stages exhibits non-linear evolutionary characteristics. Most core themes persist across different phases, with their evolution primarily manifested through dynamic adjustments in information priority and structural dominance rather than linear replacement. As the stages progress, the dominance of any single theme gradually diminishes, and the weight differences between themes converge, reflecting non-linear structural stability achieved through a phased information-compression mechanism. Overall, this study conceptualizes the identity narrative texts of Harbin’s Chinese Baroque Historic Area as a long-term textual information system and examines how place identity narratives evolve during historic district branding processes. By integrating the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic model with qualitative research methods, the study systematically examined the informational characteristics of place branding narrative texts across dimensions of information priority, structural dominance, and dispersion. The findings demonstrate that, within the specific historical and institutional context of this case, narratives of place identity are not fixed representations of place but dynamic information structures continuously shaped through textual production, spatial practices, and communication processes. Place identity narratives continuously evolve through adjustments in information priority and restructuring of structural relationships, with the information structure gradually forming relatively stable patterns over time. Such stabilization does not imply the fixation of place meanings, but rather reflects the process through which certain meanings gain greater visibility, institutional support, and communicative advantages within specific branding contexts. Rather than providing universally generalizable conclusions or direct prescriptions for urban renewal practices, these findings offer an analytical framework and empirical reference for understanding how information structures of place identity narratives are selected, reorganized, and stabilized within comparable historic district branding contexts. It should be noted that the LDA thematic model employed in this study primarily reveals the generation and evolution of narrative identities within place branding texts. However, its analysis remains focused on the textual structural level, with relatively limited capacity for deep interpretation of local historical and cultural connotations, as well as for explaining how different stakeholders understand and perceive place identity narratives. The present study primarily elucidates the mechanisms through which dominant narratives of place identity are formed during the place-branding process, but does not systematically incorporate the diverse perspectives and expressions of grassroots actors. Future research could further integrate institutional texts, grassroots narratives, and spatial behavioral data to examine how place identity narratives are negotiated, contested, and reconstructed across the dimensions of production, circulation, and reception. Given that this study focuses on a single historic district, future research should conduct comparative studies across different spatial scales, cultural contexts, and institutional environments to examine whether similar information-structural patterns exist under different place branding conditions and to further evaluate the transferability of the proposed analytical framework. Concurrently, incorporating network analysis and other machine learning methods could provide cross-validation for the robustness of internal information structure variations within place identity narratives. Furthermore, structural differences at the textual level do not necessarily correlate with disparities in brand communication effectiveness. Subsequent research should integrate communication data, urban development indicators, and qualitative interview materials to deepen understanding of the mechanisms governing place branding formation and dissemination across diverse institutional contexts, thereby providing theoretical underpinnings for advancing city marketing and sales strategies.
