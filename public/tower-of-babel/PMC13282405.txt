The electrofluidic brain as a basement layer for neural computation
Authors: V Srinivasa Chakravarthy, Vigneswaran Chandrasekaran, Nagesh C Shanbhag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article reappraises brain function by reconsidering the role of cerebral fluid dynamics in cognition. Tracing a lineage from early modern thinkers like Descartes—who invoked hydraulic metaphors and ’animal spirits’—to J.C. Bose’s pioneering studies on electro-mechanical plant physiology, and culminating in contemporary findings on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), extracellular space (ECS), and neurovascular coupling, we reveal a forgotten yet vital computational substrate. We argue that alongside the well-studied digital operations of neural circuits exists a slower, analog, evolutionarily older layer of electrofluidic computation. This system—comprised of ionic diffusion, convective CSF flow, and vascular modulation—not only supports but dynamically shapes neural activity. The brain emerges as an electromechanical ecology of electric pulses and fluid flows, where the mind is both a pattern of neural activity and a choreography of fluid flows.Subject terms: Neural circuits, Astrocyte, Neuro-vascular interactions

Introduction

Our modern understanding of the brain has been dominated by the idea of circuits—networks of neurons connected by synapses, processing and transmitting signals through sequences of electrical spikes. While this model has provided immense insights, it also risks narrowing our view to one specific form of computation. This paper explores an older, broader, and perhaps more foundational model of cognition—one grounded in the movement of fluids, ions, and mechanical oscillations.

Throughout history, thinkers have speculated about the role of bodily fluids in the generation of behavior and consciousness. In the pre-electrical era, hydraulic metaphors served as the dominant explanatory model for both motion and sensation. These ideas, often dismissed today as obsolete, may contain latent wisdom when reinterpreted through the lens of contemporary science. Early theories of ’animal spirits’ flowing through hollow nerves might prefigure modern discoveries about extracellular diffusion and cerebrospinal circulation1. The emerging picture in neuroscience reveals multiple fluidic systems that are not merely supportive but actively involved in information processing. The extracellular space (ECS) serves as a medium for volume transmission, allowing ions and neuromodulators to diffuse and shape neuronal communication2. The glymphatic system channels cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) through the brain in rhythmic cycles correlated with sleep and cardiac activity3. In addition to sleep-related neural oscillations and cardiac pulsations, respiratory cycles have also been identified as an important driver of CSF motion and intracranial pressure dynamics, contributing to the rhythmic propulsion of fluid through perivascular pathways4,5. Vascular networks dynamically regulate local blood flow in response to neural activity, forming bi-directionally coupled neurovascular circuits6–8.

Electrofluidic computation is a form of biological information processing in which electrical potentials, ionic gradients, and fluid flows interact within deformable, porous, and dynamic tissues to produce coordinated responses in living systems. Unlike traditional computation paradigms that rely on discrete logical operations (e.g., neurons firing or binary code), electrofluidic systems compute through continuous, field-like, and flow-based dynamics. In this paradigm, ionic gradients, osmotic forces, hydraulic pressures, and rhythmic pulses are not merely physiological side-effects—they participate directly in computation by shaping timing, intensity, and spatial patterns of signaling. The concept of electrofluidic computation bridges early ideas from J.C. Bose (1906–1928), who showed that plants propagate electrical signals through fluid-filled tissues to trigger hydraulic movements and adaptation, Thomas Willis (1659–1675) and Rene Descartes (1618–1649)—who envisioned the nervous system as a hydraulic network for sensation and motion (via “animal spirits”), and the modern glymphatic research—which reveals how sleep-driven CSF flow enables metabolite clearance and neural homeostasis through entrained pulsatile dynamics9–11. This form of computation operates within soft-matter environments, such as ECSs, vascular networks, and glial syncytia, where geometry, fluid viscosity, and membrane permeability dynamically influence signal propagation6,12,13. The result is a hybrid system in which discrete electrical pulses are embedded within—and often entrained by—analog, field-like flow regimes. Electrofluidic computation underpins biological phenomena such as CSF flow entrained to sleep-related EEG oscillations, plant action potentials leading to ion-driven turgor changes, astrocytic swelling modulating extracellular signaling capacity, and vascular vasomotion influencing local neural gain14–18. Unlike spike-based digital computation, this framework emphasizes ion fluxes and osmotic shifts, pulsatile cerebrospinal and vascular flows, and slow, analog field effects shaped by geometry, timing, and entrainment.

“To compute is to flow.”—This paradigm aligns with early fluidic models of cognition, including the Belousov-Zhabotinsky chemical computers, liquid marbles, and even hydraulic integrators used to solve differential equations before digital electronics19–21. This framing invites a shift from “brain as electrical circuit” to “brain as electrofluidic field,” and opens avenues for modeling cognition in systems where flow, diffusion, and entrainment are primary. Moreover, the paradigm echoes ancient ideas—from hydraulic minds to plant sap logic—now measurable, modellable, and ripe for synthetic mimicry.

By bringing together historical perspectives, plant electrophysiology, and recent advances in brain science, we propose a unified theory: that the brain’s fluidic systems represent a basement layer of neural computation. Although we represent fluidic system as a basement layer, the intention is not to propose electrofluidic computation as an entirely separate computational paradigm independent to neural computation, but rather as a biophysical substrate that interacts bidirectionally with neural activity. Neural computation remains implemented through synaptic interactions and electrical signaling, whereas electrofluidic processes such as extracellular ionic dynamics, glial regulation, and fluid transport modulate the spatiotemporal environment in which neural signaling takes place. These networks are ancient, analog, and appear across multiple biological systems, including plants, invertebrates, and vertebrates. They do not imply a universally conserved computational mechanism but rather highlight recurring biological principles in which transport processes interact with electrical signaling across different organisms. They mediate a logic of flow—what we call electrofluidic computation—underlying the more familiar logic of neural firing patterns and connectomes. This framework reframes the brain not as a static circuit board but as a living, breathing ecology of fluid flows. It challenges the dichotomy between infrastructure and information, suggesting that what has long been considered physiological ‘support’ may in fact be the substrate of thought itself.

The age of hydraulic minds: Descartes and the animal spirits

The idea that mental processes and bodily functions are driven by subtle internal fluids—often called “animal spirits”—has a long and complex history that predates the scientific revolution. The term “animal spirits” (spiritus animales) was inherited from ancient Greco-Roman medicine and philosophy22. In Galenic physiology, for instance, animal spirits were believed to be a vital, airy substance produced in the brain, particularly in the ventricles, from a refinement of the blood1,22. These spirits were thought to travel through hollow nerves to activate muscles and senses, forming the basis of perception, movement, and even imagination.

One of the most influential figures to systematize this concept in the early modern period was René Descartes (1596–1650). In his treatise De Homine (On Man) and later works such as Passions of the Soul, Descartes proposed that the pineal gland at the center of the brain acted as a valve or regulator of animal spirits23. He believed that the spirits flowed from the brain’s ventricles into the nerves, which he imagined as hollow tubes, reaching the muscles and causing them to inflate and contract.

In the 18th century, despite advances in microscopy and anatomy, the language of animal spirits remained embedded in medical and scientific literature. Some Enlightenment thinkers replaced “spirits” with more mechanical terms like “nervous fluid” or “ether,” but the fundamental assumption of flow-based signal propagation remained24. Even in the early work of Luigi Galvani, who is credited with discovering bioelectricity, the concept of fluid pressure and vitality remained intertwined with emerging electrical models25.

Thus, the idea that cognition, sensation, and movement arise from the flow of subtle substances through the nervous system persisted for centuries. It provided a framework for early neuroscience and reflected the technological metaphors of its era. Just as we now liken the brain to a computer, early moderns likened it to a hydraulic automaton.

Moreover, the idea that the brain tissue formed a continuous syncytium—a network without discrete cellular boundaries—persisted well into the 19th century. A syncytium refers to a tissue in which cells are not completely separate but share cytoplasmic continuity, allowing substances to flow freely throughout the structure26,27. In such a model, the movement of fluids like animal spirits could occur seamlessly from the brain to the periphery and back1. Figure 2D shows illustrative representation of syncytium. This assumption was crucial to the plausibility of hydraulic theories: without a continuous conduit, fluid flow would be interrupted, and the analogy to pressurized piping systems would collapse. Only by assuming a syncytial architecture could early thinkers maintain the idea of reflexive and motor function arising from fluid propulsion28. Even as microscopy improved, many researchers continued to describe brain tissue in such terms, because no clear evidence yet existed for the neuron as a discrete functional unit29. The later emergence of the neuron doctrine, championed by Ramón y Cajal in the late 19th century, finally displaced this view but only after centuries of mechanistic reasoning built on the imaginative and engineering logic of flow—continuous and hollow—necessary for such fluid transmission30,31. His ideas foreshadowed the later realization that the brain’s function might involve system-wide flow, regulation, and responsiveness to pressure and fluid dynamics.

Electrofluidic signal propagation in plants

Bose and the fluid-electric logic of living systems

Jagadish Chandra Bose (1858–1937) was a polymath whose contributions spanned physics, biology, and the philosophy of science32. Following his early work in physics and radio waves, Bose turned his attention to the biological world9. What followed was a monumental body of research into the electrical and mechanical behavior of plants. At a time when plant physiology was viewed primarily through the lens of passive growth and photosynthesis, Bose introduced a systems-level approach that treated plants as complex, responsive, and signaling organisms10,33. He designed exquisitely sensitive instruments to measure electrical responses in plant tissues, revealing that they react to stimuli much like animal nerves do34.

Bose’s seminal work forces us to rethink what it means to signal, to compute, and to respond. One of his most remarkable contributions was the detailed elucidation of motor response in plants—particularly how electrical and fluidic events coincide to produce coordinated motion35. Bose demonstrated that when a plant like Mimosa pudica is touched, a measurable electrical impulse is generated at the point of contact36–38. This impulse then travels through the plant’s internal channels, initiating a cascade of physiological changes. Chief among these is the redistribution of fluids within the pulvini, the motor organs at the base of the leaflets. As ions move across cell membranes, water follows osmotically, leading to turgor changes in specific cells. The result is a rapid loss or gain of stiffness and volume in targeted regions, which causes the leaf to fold or droop in a coordinated response39. Importantly, Bose showed that the electrical impulse, fluid redistribution, and mechanical movement were not isolated events but tightly coupled components of a single signaling process40,41. This was a true electromechanical loop in a living organism without nerves or muscles—a profound insight into the deep logic of life’s responsiveness.

Another dimension of Bose’s fluid-electric understanding came through his investigations into the ascent of sap—the mechanism by which water and nutrients travel upward through a plant’s vascular system42,43. At the time, the prevailing explanation relied heavily on the capillary effect, transpiration pull, and cohesion-tension theory, suggesting that water moved passively upward due to evaporation at the leaf surfaces and the cohesive properties of water molecules. Bose, however, offered an active physiological model: he proposed that cells at the root and stem junctions functioned like micropumps, generating rhythmic electrical oscillations that helped propel water upward.

Bose’s legacy invites a deeper appreciation for plant intelligence, not in metaphorical terms but in mechanistic ones. His work blurs the boundary between nervous and non-nervous systems, between computation and circulation. In the fluid-electric reactions of plant tissues, we glimpse the remnants of an ancient computational logic—one that may persist, in evolved form, within the layered architecture of the human brain itself. Bose’s experiments showed that bioelectric and fluidic mechanisms are intertwined42,44. Plants, devoid of nerves, display a primitive form of electromechanical cognition—a decentralized but effective signaling architecture. This discovery hints at a time when life coordinated behavior through coupled fluid-electric pathways, long before specialized neurons evolved.

Evidence for a plant “brain”

The idea that plants possess electrofluidic systems functioning akin to a ’brain’ has long been explored. Electrical signals in plants are known to propagate through vascular channels such as the phloem, xylem, and cambium, reaching all parts of the plant body45–53. Recent studies have shown that long-distance transmission of electrical signals from sensor to effector sites is primarily facilitated by the phloem49,54,55. This reinforces the notion that the vascular system plays a central role in inter-organ and systemic communication56,57. Importantly, the molecular mechanisms underlying such electrical transmission—such as ion channel activity—are evolutionarily older than the nervous systems of animals58. Like neurons, plant cells also generate electrical responses to various stimuli, including action potentials (APs)47,58–61. These APs travel through sieve tubes and parenchyma cells located in the protoxylem and phloem, respectively62,63. Non-damaging stimuli, such as touch, can evoke APs that propagate through the plant and activate movements, for instance, at the tertiary pulvini of leaves. The propagation speed of APs ranges from approximately 3–7 mm s−1 in the rachilla and 6-44 mm s−1 in the petiole64. In contrast, damaging stimuli can elicit both APs and variation potentials (VPs), also known as slow wave potentials65, which travel more slowly—typically at 1–4 mm s−1 in the petiole. A third type of electrical signal observed in plants is the system potential (SP), which is evoked by external factors and involves a hyperpolarization event mediated by the activation of H+-ATPases in the plasma membrane66.

Through plasmodesmata, plants utilize both chemical and electrical synapse-like mechanisms57,67, functionally similar to animal gap junctions. Rapid intercellular communication via vesicle trafficking—facilitated by adhesion domains—has been compared to neuronal synapses68. Remarkably, extracellular application of the hormone auxin can evoke electrical responses. Furthermore, plants use classical neurotransmitters such as GABA, acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin, glutamate, histamine, and noradrenaline for signaling67,69,70. For example, glutamate application depolarizes cells in the root-stem transition zone47. This zone also integrates sensory-motor signaling via auxin transport and vesicle recycling, facilitating processes like cell elongation and root tropisms that allow the plant to adapt to its environment46. Uniquely, this region also exhibits synchronized electrical activity71, suggesting it plays a decision-making role by coordinating phenotypic responses from root to shoot. Mechanical injury can alter xylem hydraulic pressure, initiating systemic depolarization waves throughout the plant—a phenomenon termed the hydraulic hypothesis72. Wounding also triggers systemic defense gene expression via glutamate receptor-like (GLR)-mediated membrane depolarizations73,74. In Arabidopsis, wound signals propagate between leaves through vascular elements, specifically phloem sieve elements and xylem contact cells. Larval feeding by Spodoptera littoralis on Arabidopsis thaliana generates electrical signals at the wound site, which then spread to adjacent leaves75. This rapid electrical signaling is coupled with hydraulic changes in the xylem and phloem via an electro-osmotic mechanism76.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that plants, like animals, possess complex electrofluidic systems that enable them to perform a wide range of physiological and behavioral tasks. Beyond these examples, plant electrical signals are also capable of encoding more sophisticated information. For instance,77 demonstrated that spruce trees (Picea abies) exhibit synchronized bioelectrical activity and changes in entropy—referred to as the electrome—in anticipation of environmental events such as a partial solar eclipse, hours before it occurred.

The cerebral extracellular space—a fluid medium for computation

The previous sections have shown how biofluids, when modulated by electrical signals, can induce mechanical responses in tissues—forming the basis of motor activity and fluid transport. But how do these fluid flows propagate within the dense and geometrically intricate microenvironment of the brain? The answer lies in the ECS—the complex network of microscopic corridors that thread between cells. As the primary medium through which ions, neurochemicals, and even CSF navigate, the ECS emerges as a critical microanatomical infrastructure for electrofluidic signaling78. In this section, we explore the ECS not simply as passive interstitial fluid, but as a structured and responsive computational medium that forms the foundational terrain of the electrofluidic brain.

Across multiple foundational works—including16,78–82 —a consistent picture emerges: the brain’s ECS is not a passive milieu but a dynamic and responsive fluidic substrate for information processing. These studies collectively assert that intercellular communication in the CNS occurs not only through direct synaptic connections (“wiring transmission”) but also through diffusion-based processes, termed volume transmission. Volume transmission refers to the dissemination of signaling molecules—such as neurotransmitters, ions, peptides, and even gases—through the ECS to act on multiple target sites, sometimes far from the point of origin. This mode of communication contrasts with the traditional neuron doctrine’s emphasis on localized, point-to-point signal transfer via synapses. According to Agnati and colleagues12,83, volume transmission complements wiring transmission, expanding the brain’s capacity for coordination, modulation, and distributed computation. In his extensive review,80 elaborates on the ECS as a tortuous, geometrically complex environment whose characteristics significantly impact the spread of ions and molecules. He identifies key parameters such as volume fraction and tortuosity—terms later popularized by Syková—as central to understanding how signaling occurs within this space78. These physical constraints shape the delay, strength, and spatial reach of chemical signals.79 builds on this by measuring changes in ECS under different physiological and pathological states.

Since the ECS is an electrolytic medium, and since neuronal firing activity depends on ionic fluxes crossing the neural membrane, neural computation is deeply dependent on the ECS layer78,84. For instance, the influx of calcium ions (Ca2+) into the presynaptic terminal is essential for vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release. If a presynaptic terminal has recently transmitted, it can cause a local depletion of Ca2+ in the ECS, temporarily impairing the ability of adjacent terminals to fire. Similarly, when a neuron is active, potassium ions (K+) flow out of the cell during repolarization. This leads to elevated extracellular K+ concentrations, which can excite neighboring neurons by depolarizing their resting membrane potentials. These examples show that neurons do not operate in isolation; they are coupled through shared ionic environments, allowing for extrasynaptic communication mediated by the ECS. One particularly striking observation Syková makes is that electrical activation of neural tissue results in astrocyte swelling. This occurs due to localized ionic shifts, which draw water osmotically into astrocytes, thereby altering the local ECS volume. This process—where electrical events lead to fluid redistribution and cellular swelling—mirrors the kind of electromechanical response observed by Bose in plant tissues. Just as Bose demonstrated that an electrical signal in Mimosa pudica initiates ion fluxes followed by water movement and turgor changes,79 shows that neural excitation can lead to water influx into glial cells and consequent ECS shrinkage. In both systems, electricity and fluid dynamics are coupled to produce physiological responses that modulate the information-processing environment.

Bach-y-Rita16 framed the ECS as a genuine neurotransmission medium, proposing that diffusion through extracellular fluid is a valid and central mode of communication in the brain. He likened this to a chemical field that overlays the anatomical circuitry, enabling slow, sustained, and integrative signaling that cannot be captured by electrophysiology alone. This notion redefines the ECS as an informational field, operating in parallel to fast electrical signaling, and essential for mood regulation, sensory integration, and neuroplasticity.

All of these works converge on a key insight: the ECS is a morphodynamic space—its structure changes in response to use, stress, metabolic demand, and time of day. It allows the brain to compute in analog, using field-like propagation, graded responses, and spatial averaging. This creates a fluid-based logic, where information is shaped not by discrete pulses, but by concentrations, gradients, and geometry. In this view, cognition emerges not solely from spike trains or synaptic weights but from the dynamic behavior of extracellular fluids, glial modulation, and their influence on neural excitability. The ECS serves as a deep, continuous layer of computation—one that supports and stabilizes the faster, symbolic operations of the neural network.

Taken together, the literature recasts the ECS as a computational scaffold—slow, analog, and state-sensitive. It operates as a liquid logic field, resonating with early intuitions of flow-based brain function and aligning precisely with this paper’s overarching thesis: that the brain’s intelligence is embedded not just in neurons, but in the geometry of the fluid medium of ECS.

The return of flow: CSF and the glymphatic renaissance

CSF is a clear, colorless fluid that bathes the brain and spinal cord, providing both physical cushioning and a chemical environment essential for homeostasis85. It is produced primarily by the choroid plexus in the brain’s ventricles and flows through a well-defined pathway: from the lateral ventricles to the third and fourth ventricles, into the subarachnoid space, and around the brain and spinal cord. Eventually, it is absorbed into the venous system via arachnoid granulations. In total, the CSF is turned over several times a day, ensuring the regular clearance of metabolic waste, distribution of neuroactive substances, and regulation of intracranial pressure. For decades, CSF was thought to be a protective and homeostatic fluid, however, recent works have challenged this simplistic view85–87. Recent studies of glymphatic clearance during sleep have renewed attention to the role of brain fluid dynamics. The implications for electrofluidic computation, however, do not depend on any particular interpretation of glymphatic clearance, but instead emphasize the modulatory influence of fluid dynamics on neuronal and extracellular signaling fields. Nedergaard and colleagues86 proposed that the glymphatic system comprises of the directed flow of CSF along perivascular spaces, facilitated by aquaporin-4 channels located in astrocyte endfeet. This CSF then exchanges with interstitial fluid, to sweep off waste products (such as beta-amyloid and tau) that are caught up in neurodegenerative diseases17.

Glymphatic circulation is not a passive process and is dynamically regulated by neural and physiological rhythms. Studies have revealed that the process during glymphatic clearance the interstitial space expands by nearly 60% to allow enhanced convective flow88. Furthermore, cardiac pulsations and respiratory oscillations work like mechanical drivers facilitating rhythmic propulsion through perivascular network of the brain4. Therefore, the glymphatic system illustrates a neural-fluidic coupling, in which brain’s electrical and vascular rhythms entrain different processes such as fluidic movement, embedding computation and metabolism within a unified oscillatory regime. Here, embedding computation refers to information processing that arises from the physical dynamics of the surrounding biological medium that modulate neural activity.

Fultz et al.85 in their recent work confirms this entrainment by showing CSF flow’s direct coupling with neural activity and hemodynamics. With the help of simultaneous EEG (electroencephalogram) and fMRI (Functional magnetic resonance imaging) recordings, they demonstrated that large waves of CSF inflow into the brain’s fourth ventricle are tightly synchronized with low-frequency neural oscillations and hemodynamic changes, especially during non-REM sleep. These observations suggest that CSF dynamics are orchestrated brain-wide by a complex interplay of electrophysiological and vascular events. Additionally, alterations in glymphatic transport have been associated with neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease. This association has further strengthen the functional relevance of brain fluid dynamics in both healthy and pathological states87. This reinforces the idea that fluidic movement in the brain is not just a supportive but an integrative element in neural computation and systemic regulations.

Such a recently recognized function of CSF flow reshapes our understanding of the brain’s infrastructure. Instead of merely serving as a passive coolant, the pulsatile dynamics of CSF represent a primitive yet integral computational rhythm, working in tandem with neuronal activities to support various functions such as memory, repair, and overall cognitive integrity. In the context of this paper, the glymphatic system forms a bridge between electrophysiology and fluid dynamics, echoing Bose’s insights into electromechanical life and extending Syková’s vision of the extracellular environment as a substrate for computation.

Bridging plant electrofluidics and glymphatic dynamics

While the glymphatic system has been recently characterized in neuroscientific literature as a glial-regulated clearance mechanism, its functional architecture and rhythmicity exhibit striking parallels with plant electrohydraulic physiology. This resemblance opens the door to a broader conceptual framework, in which glymphatic circulation is understood not merely as a byproduct of vascular dynamics, but as an evolutionarily conserved mode of analog fluidic signaling. In plants, long-distance transport of water and solutes occurs through xylem conduits, modulated by aquaporins, turgor pressures, and rhythmic pulses89. Similarly, in the brain, Aquaporin-4 (AQP4) channels located on astrocytic endfeet lining perivascular spaces facilitate convective influx of CSF into the brain parenchyma90,91. This influx is most active during slow-wave sleep, when the interstitial space expands and permits efficient exchange between CSF and interstitial fluid (ISF), thereby supporting glymphatic clearance of neurotoxic solutes such as β-amyloid and tau92. This astroglial-driven, aquaporin-gated mechanism resembles xylem loading and unloading in root and leaf tissues, which similarly rely on rhythmic aquaporin regulation and ion-mediated osmotic gradients. Moreover, just as plant sap ascent is now known to involve bioelectric modulations and pulsed fluidic motion (as demonstrated by Bose42), CSF movement is entrained to systemic physiological oscillations such as cardiac pulsations, respiration, and the sleep-wake cycle. Furthermore, the brain’s waste efflux via perivenous outflow and meningeal lymphatics may be viewed as the central nervous system’s analog to toxin extrusion mechanisms in root endodermal cells, which dynamically regulate osmotic pressure, signaling molecules, and detoxification. These structural and functional analogies suggest a deep homology in how multicellular systems evolved rhythmic, glia-like tissues to regulate flow-based computation and maintenance—a hypothesis that we believe warrants experimental exploration. State dependence of glymphatic transport: consensus, controversy, methodological constraints and implications for electrofluidic computation

Recent work has established that CSF and interstitial fluid dynamics vary systematically across arousal states17,85,88,90,91. However, the extent to which these state-dependent dynamics translate into changes in net metabolic clearance remains actively debated. To clarify the current state of the field, it is useful to distinguish between observations that are broadly supported and questions that remain unresolved.

Broadly supported observations

There is substantial evidence that brain fluid dynamics are closely coupled with physiological and neural states. Multiple studies demonstrate that CSF flow is entrained to neural and hemodynamic oscillations, particularly during sleep85,90. In addition, vascular pulsations, respiratory rhythms, and vasomotor oscillations modulate CSF velocity and perivascular fluid motion in sleep-wake cycles93. Recent work further suggests that enhanced vascular oscillations during non-REM sleep can influence fluid transport along perivascular pathways94.

More generally, the existence of fluid exchange pathways linking CSF, interstitial fluid, and perivascular spaces is widely accepted, and these pathways are thought to support the distribution of solutes and metabolic byproducts within the brain parenchyma95. Together, these findings establish that brain fluid circulation is strongly state-dependent and dynamically regulated by coupled neural and vascular processes.

Unresolved questions and ongoing debate

In contrast, several key aspects of glymphatic function remain uncertain. In particular, whether net solute clearance is increased or decreased during sleep is still debated. While a number of studies report enhanced tracer transport or glymphatic activity during sleep85,88,90,91, other work has found reduced solute clearance under sleep or anesthesia conditions96, suggesting that transport dynamics do not map onto net clearance rates.

There is also ongoing disagreement on the dominant transport mechanisms. Glymphatic transport has been proposed to arise from a combination of convective bulk flow along perivascular pathways, diffusion within the ECS, and oscillatory transport driven by vascular and neural-vascular coupling94,97,98. The relative contribution of these mechanisms remains unresolved. Similarly, the anatomical pathways supporting large-scale transport and clearance continue to be actively investigated.

Methodological considerations

Interpretation of glymphatic transport is further complicated by methodological constraints. Much of the experimental evidence derives from tracer- or contrast-agent-based imaging approaches95,99,100. These methods, while informative, may perturb endogenous pressure gradients, and the observed tracer dispersion can reflect a combination of diffusion, advection, and tissue binding rather than purely convective flow. In addition, limitations in spatial and temporal resolution make it difficult to distinguish between diffusion-dominated and flow-dominated transport. Variations in experimental protocols, anesthesia, and species further contribute to discrepancies across studies101,102. Together, these factors underscore that the directionality and mechanisms of glymphatic transport remain open questions.

Implications for electrofluidic computation

The relevance of recent findings on glymphatic clearance during sleep to electrofluidic computation is not contingent on a particular mechanistic interpretation; instead, these findings underscore the modulatory influence of fluid dynamics on neuronal and extracellular signaling fields. However, irrespective of whether solute transport occurs primarily through convective bulk flow or diffusion, and whether net clearance increases or decreases during sleep, it does not conflict with the idea of the existence of state-dependent electrofluidic interactions. From the perspective of electrofluidic computation, the key feature is that the fluid dynamics acts as a slow analog field and constantly interacts with neural activity, thus shaping the spatiotemporal environment of neural signaling.

Vascular computation—yet another biofluidic computing network

The article builds the case that an ancient electrofluidic network was the precursor to the nervous system. Initially, this network in plants functioned both as a nutrient channel and a slow communication system. In animals, it later differentiated into two specialized systems: the nervous system, which enabled rapid communication, and a fluidic system associated with CSF flow. This section extends the argument by proposing that the vascular system is another specialized fluidic network, operating alongside CSF flows.

Structural similarities between the nervous and cardiovascular systems were noted as early as 1543 by Andreas Vesalius103. Vasomotion—the oscillatory change in arteriolar diameter—modulates vascular resistance in various physiological and pathological states such as pregnancy, pre-eclampsia, and hypertension104–107. Oscillatory flow in capillary beds, driven by vasomotion, may improve tissue oxygenation compared to steady blood flow108. Vasomotion demonstrates a wide array of temporal and spatial rhythms, ranging from regular to chaotic patterns109. Experiments have shown that vasomotion induced by agents like histamine can either provoke or suppress oscillations in rabbit ear arteries110, and exhibit nonlinear dynamics such as quasiperiodicity and type-III intermittency111,112. Studies in rats reveal nephron-nephron coupling and the spread of vasomotion in both hypertensive and normotensive conditions113–117. Theoretical models show that irregular (chaotic) vasomotion in skeletal muscle can enhance tissue oxygenation more effectively than regular oscillations15,118–120. Collectively, these findings underline that vasomotion is a spatio-temporal phenomenon arising from dynamic interactions among vessel segments to optimize microcirculatory function.

The hemo-neural hypothesis, proposed by Christopher Moore and Rui Cao121, posits that vascular flow can play a computational role. They argue that blood flow can actively modulate neural function, suggesting that vascular dynamics are embedded within the brain’s computational processes. This shifts the view of neurovascular coupling from a one-way delivery service to a dynamic, bidirectional system. Glial cells, especially astrocytes, are central to neuronal energy supply; they regulate glucose uptake in response to synaptic activity122. The density of glial cells reflects local neural metabolic demand123. Glial networks relay metabolic signals from neurons to microvessels and manage nutrient delivery from blood vessels to neurons13,14. Rather than serving solely as passive support, glia function as both signaling intermediaries and distributors of metabolic resources. The astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttle theory124, supported by experimental data125, describes how astrocytes convert vascular glucose to lactate, supplying neurons with the necessary energy for synaptic transmission.

Work by ref. 8 shows that glial cells help regulate perfusion, smoothing hemodynamic fluctuations and ensuring steady nutrient supply—akin to homeostatic buffering in computing. Stable neural responses depend on precise coordination of metabolic demand and supply via the glial-vascular interface, resembling associative network dynamics126. Astrocytes in the visual cortex display “tuned responses” to stimuli, further highlighting their role in information processing127. Subsequent modeling studies6,7 introduced bidirectionally coupled, self-organizing neurovascular networks capable of reproducing orientation-selective cortical responses observed in neuroimaging. Their findings suggest neural and vascular networks co-evolve, shaping each other’s structure and function for optimal efficiency and adaptability. This cooperative interaction is crucial for maintaining stable yet flexible cortical maps, a foundation for higher cognition. Further research18 demonstrated that neural architecture and activity patterns directly influence vascular growth, emphasizing the reciprocal relationship between neural and vascular systems.

In sum, these studies reinforce the concept of the vascular network as a “co-processor”—not just a supplier, but also a participant in brain’s computation, regulation, and adaptation. Thus, vascular computation aligns with the ECS and glymphatic systems as a key component of the electrofluidic brain, constituting an intricate lattice of flows, fields, and feedbacks underpinning cognition. Liquid logic: toward an electrofluidic theory of the brain

According to the view developed in this article, the brain is not merely a neural network supported by fluids, but a computational fluidics engine, whose logics are as much about fluid circulation and chemistry as they are about voltage and spike timing. To better appreciate how such computation can occur in fluidic systems, Adamatzky’s work offers a taxonomy of liquid computers—physical substrates that use flowing media to carry out logic operations21. These include reaction-diffusion processors, where chemical patterns such as the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction implement logic gates via wavefront interactions128,129. Similarly, droplet-based processors use the controlled motion, collision, and splitting of microdroplets in oil or aqueous media to represent and transform data130–132. Another example is liquid marbles, hydrophobic-coated droplets that roll over surfaces and interact mechanically or chemically, effectively serving as mobile logic units20,133,134. Vortex-based computation is also notable—where the dynamics of rotating fluid flows in confined geometries can embody rules of logic, memory storage, and timing135–137. These systems often exploit nonlinear properties of fluids—thresholds, oscillations, phase transitions—to simulate computational primitives such as logic gates, delay lines, and integrators. What is striking is that many of these principles resonate with processes observed in the brain: waves of potassium spreading through ECS, convective pulses of CSF, or vascular oscillations entrained by neural activity5,18,78,85,117. Such analogies are not merely poetic; they invite the serious proposition that the brain’s fluidic systems may operate in accordance with similar principles, evolved and specialized for living computation.

Thus, the electrofluidic theory suggests that beneath the digital-like neural firing patterns lies a substrate of analog, soft-matter computation, sculpted by evolution to harness the power of flows, fields, and emergent material properties. One of the earliest and most compelling examples of fluid-based computation is the use of hydraulic algebraic machines, which employ networks of fluid channels, valves, and chambers to simulate the behavior of algebraic equations. In such systems, pressure differentials and fluid heights represent mathematical variables, allowing for real-time, analog solutions to complex problems. These devices were capable of modeling polynomials and differential equations with surprising accuracy, long before digital computers emerged.

Closely related are hydraulic integrators, developed in the early 20th century to solve differential equations that arose in engineering and physics. These analog computers used the continuous flow of fluid through carefully calibrated resistances and capacitances—hydraulic analogs to electrical components—to perform integration over time. These machines highlight the deep structural analogy between electrical and hydraulic domains, reinforcing the plausibility of a fluidic basis for dynamic computation in biological systems. Another captivating category is that of fluid mappers and maze solvers. These setups often consist of fluid-filled mazes or branching networks where dye or tracer particles find optimal pathways, effectively “solving” problems like shortest paths or flow minimization138–140. Such systems offer a physical and intuitive model for computation through spatial geometry and constraint satisfaction—a metaphor that may resonate with how fluids navigate through the tortuous architecture of the brain’s vasculature and ECS. Intrathecal tracer study by ref. 99 using gadolinium in humans for visualizing glymphatic flow across brain topology to assess metabolic function is an example of modern versions of hydraulic maze solvers.

Further innovations include droplet tracing fluid mappers, where the behavior of microdroplets on patterned surfaces encodes logical decisions. As droplets merge, repel, or follow specific paths dictated by surface tension and chemical gradients, they enact computational operations without the need for electronics. These systems reveal how even simple fluidic elements can be harnessed for logic, storage, and signal propagation. Lastly, the field of fluidic logic formalizes these phenomena into structured circuits, with fluid-based equivalents of AND, OR, and NOT gates. Often used in environments hostile to electronics—such as inside the human body or outer space—fluidic logic circuits provide robust, miniaturized alternatives for control and computation. The behavior of these systems illustrates that logical operations can emerge from pressure thresholds, turbulence, and nonlinear flow interactions, offering a powerful analogy for the complex dynamics of neural-fluidic interactions in the brain. Although biological systems do not implement discrete digital logic gates, fluid-mediated interactions can still regulate signal propagation based on conditional logic. For example, local changes in extracellular ion concentration, astrocytic swelling, or vascular dynamics can modulate neuronal activity depending on the state of the surrounding fluid, functionally similar to flow-based gating mechanisms.

From fluidic biology to biohybrid computation

The conceptual and structural analogies between plant electrohydraulic signaling and brain glymphatic flow do more than enrich our understanding of evolution—they also offer a novel blueprint for biohybrid computing. Traditional neuromorphic computing systems model spike trains and connectivity matrices, but they overlook the role of flows, diffusion gradients, and analog field effects—elements that are increasingly recognized as integral to biological computation. Plant systems demonstrate that distributed intelligence can emerge without neurons, through the orchestration of electrical impulses, hydraulic oscillations, and chemical signaling. These mechanisms exhibit many of the desirable features of a computing substrate: low-energy information propagation, inherent robustness to noise, spatial parallelism, and adaptability to environmental stimuli. Likewise, the glymphatic system, though primarily studied in metabolic and homeostatic terms, operates as a spatiotemporally modulated fluidic network, whose behavior is shaped by oscillations in sleep, heart rate, respiration, and neural activity. These entrained flows represent a form of fluidic memory and state-dependent gating—core attributes of analog computation.

We propose that biohybrid fluidic computing systems can draw from both these biological precedents. Key possibilities include: (a) aquaporin-mimetic membranes that act as flow-gated transistors for ionic or molecular computing. (b) electrofluidic actuators that simulate plant pulvinar mechanics or astrocytic swelling to regulate signal throughput. (c) oscillatory hydrogel logic circuits that mimic circadian-entrained sap movement or CSF pulsatility. (d) non-neuronal field computing models where computation arises through the modulation of fluid geometry, concentration fields, and rheological properties—akin to how ECS tortuosity or phloem ion dynamics shape signaling. Such fluidic logic architectures could enable computing systems that operate on material flows rather than binary codes, supporting a class of devices that are soft, adaptive, and embedded—perhaps even capable of co-evolution with their environment. These examples illustrate that computation can emerge from physical fluid dynamics, although translating such principles into practical AI systems remains a long-term research challenge.

Evolutionary continuity: from primitive currents to neural codes

If we step back and examine the patterns uncovered across plant electrophysiology, glymphatic dynamics, extracellular signaling, and vascular computation, a larger picture begins to emerge—one of deep evolutionary continuity in the mechanisms of biological information processing. The evolution of nervous systems did not begin ex nihilo with the appearance of the neuron, but rather grew from a substrate of electrofluidic coordination mechanisms—primitive, yet computationally potent. In organisms such as plants, where classical neurons are absent, J.C. Bose demonstrated that electrical signaling and fluidic shifts can lead to motor responses and coordinated behavior. This suggests that electromechanical and fluid-based signaling systems were precursors to modern nervous systems. The presence of action potentials, hydraulic flows, and distributed networks of responsive tissue in these simpler organisms underscores an ancient form of proto-cognition. We may thus posit that neural circuits are evolutionary overlays atop an older fabric of rhythmic flows and chemical gradients—a fabric still active today, though often overlooked. Much like the evolutionary story of lungs emerging from swim bladders or feathers from reptilian scales, the modern neuron may be seen as an evolved specialization of pre-existing electrofluidic architectures. This continuity challenges the reduction of cognition to spike trains and connectomes alone. It encourages us to reinterpret cognition as emerging from the interplay of structure, flow, and field—a dynamic, embodied process embedded in the very material properties of life.

Reframing brains: from circuits to flows

With the accumulated evidence for fluid-based signaling systems—ranging from CSF pulsations and extracellular chemical gradients to vascular-neural interactions and plant electrophysiology—it becomes necessary to reframe our conception of mind itself. Traditional cognitive neuroscience has relied heavily on the metaphor of the brain as a circuit, where synaptic connections and electrical impulses encode, transmit, and process information. However, the emerging picture suggests that flow, diffusion, and oscillation are equally fundamental to how information is managed in living systems.

Rather than viewing neural networks as discrete nodes wired together like an electrical circuit board, we might envision them as embedded in a liquid landscape—where the timing, amplitude, and persistence of signals are modulated by dynamic fluidic contexts. In such a paradigm, the ECS, CSF, and vascular flows act not as passive backdrops but as active participants in the regulation of neural activity, influencing computation in a spatially distributed and temporally fluid manner.

This shift also opens up a new theoretical vocabulary for cognition. Concepts such as resonance, entrainment, convection, and phase transitions become more relevant than binary switching and static wiring diagrams. The mind, in this view, is not merely the output of circuits firing in sequence but is the emergent property of an electromechanical and fluidic ensemble—a hybrid between field and structure, rhythm and spike.

Conclusion

In this article, we present a historical exploration tracing a broad arc from ancient metaphors of “animal spirits” to the cutting-edge science of extracellular signaling, CSF dynamics, and vascular computation. At each stage, we have seen how fluid-based phenomena—once relegated to metaphor or physiological maintenance—have re-emerged as central to our understanding of cognition. The historical narrative shows that the early thinkers, constrained by the limits of contemporary science, intuited aspects of neural function that modern technologies are only now beginning to confirm and refine. Recent translational approaches propose tracking intrathecally administered contrast agents (e.g., gadolinium) via blood and/or urine to non-invasively infer glymphatic clearance99,100,141. Similar to Bose’s tracing of sap ascent via oscillatory responses, such methods offer a modern electrofluidic readout of cerebrospinal circulation.

Although the present article focuses on conceptual synthesis, several existing modeling frameworks could be used to implement electrofluidic dynamics in computational neuroscience. Reaction-diffusion models have long been used to describe extracellular ion dynamics and spreading depolarization142, while hydrodynamic models have been developed to study CSF circulation and glymphatic transport143. In addition, network-based dynamical systems can represent extracellular compartments and transport pathways as coupled nodes and edges. Combining these approaches could provide a mathematical framework for modeling electrofluidic interactions alongside neural activity, allowing future work to explore how diffusion, fluid flow, and ionic gradients interact with neuronal signaling in large-scale brain models.

By integrating historical, experimental, and computational insights, we have argued that the brain does not merely compute with electrical signals across a synaptic mesh. It also operates through a set of deeply embodied, evolutionarily conserved fluidic mechanisms that shape, constrain, and enable brain’s computations. These systems—CSF, ECS, blood vessels, and glial scaffolds—are not passive substrates but dynamic processors of information, co-participants in the ongoing activity we call brain function. The implications are profound. If cognition arises not solely from spiking networks but from an interplay of circuits and flows, then our models of the brain must shift from static wiring diagrams to hydrodynamic topologies. Recent studies have demonstrated that biological neural networks cultured in vitro can exhibit learning and adaptive behavior when coupled with artificial environments. For example, Kagan et al.144 showed cortical neuron cultures modify their activity patterns while interacting with a simulated game environment, suggesting neuronal networks can perform adaptive computation through synaptic plasticity even outside intact brain structures. Similarly, Cai et al.145 showed organoid-based reservoir computing system as biological computing platforms capable of performing machine-learning tasks. These studies highlight the computational potential of neural circuits themselves. In the context of the present framework, electrofluidic dynamics is not proposed as a replacement for synaptic plasticity, but rather as a complementary substrate that shapes the extracellular environment in which neuronal computation occurs. Computational neuroscience must begin to account for diffusive, convective, and entrained flows, not just discrete impulses. Artificial intelligence systems inspired by biology might benefit from incorporating such principles of analog, material, and field-based computation.

This work revisits a foundational question that has shadowed neuroscience since its earliest days: does the brain contain a functional syncytium? The neuron doctrine decisively rejected the notion of a cytoplasmic continuity spanning neuronal somata, replacing it with the modern picture of discrete cells coupled by synapses—an idea enshrined in the popular ’neuron doctrine’29,31. Our proposal does not challenge that cellular discreteness. Rather, it suggests that the search for a syncytium may have been misdirected. If a tissue-scale continuum exists, it is not one that threads through neuronal cytosol; instead, it is realized in the interstitial corridor system of the extracellular space (ECS)—a connected, electrically and chemically active medium through which ions and signaling molecules can propagate, integrate, and reshape local microenvironments. In this sense, the brain may exhibit a non-cytosolic, electrofluidic syncytium: a continuous substrate that coexists with the neuronal network and modulates it through state-dependent coupling.
