Depth, Not Data: An Analysis of Hessian Spectral Bifurcation
Shenyang DengDepartment of Computer ScienceDartmouth CollegeHanover, NH, USA shenyang.deng.gr@dartmouth.edu, Boyao LiaoDepartment of MathematicsUniversity of BirminghamBirmingham, UK bxl307@student.bham.ac.uk, Zhuoli OuyangDepartment of Computer ScienceDartmouth CollegeHanover, NH, USA Zhuoli.Ouyang@dartmouth.edu Tianyu PangDepartment of Computer ScienceDartmouth CollegeHanover, NH, USA tianyu.pang.gr@dartmouth.edu Yaoqing YangDepartment of Computer ScienceDartmouth CollegeHanover, NH, USA yaoqing.yang@dartmouth.edu

Abstract
The eigenvalue distribution of the Hessian matrix plays a crucial role in understanding the optimization landscape of deep neural networks. Prior work has attributed the well-documented “bulk-and-spike” spectral structure, where a few dominant eigenvalues are separated from a bulk of smaller ones, to the imbalance in the data covariance matrix. In this work, we challenge this view by demonstrating that such spectral Bifurcation can arise purely from the network architecture, independent of data imbalance. Specifically, we analyze a deep linear network setup and prove that, even when the data covariance is perfectly balanced, the Hessian still exhibits a Bifurcation eigenvalue structure: a dominant cluster and a bulk cluster. Crucially, we establish that the ratio between dominant and bulk eigenvalues scales linearly with the network depth. This reveals that the spectral gap is strongly affected by the network architecture rather than solely by data distribution. Our results suggest that both model architecture and data characteristics should be considered when designing optimization algorithms for deep networks.

Following the setup of

F(\mathbf{x})=W^{L}\cdots W^{1}\mathbf{x}=W^{L:1}\mathbf{x}

W^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times d_{l-1}}

D=\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}d_{l}

W:=\{W^{1},\ldots,W^{L}\}
and use the explicit parameterization
F_{W}(\mathbf{x})=W^{L:1}\mathbf{x}
when necessary.

Loss Function.

We consider a training dataset of i.i.d. samples
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
from distribution
p
. We consider the squared loss
L_{W}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}
where
\hat{\mathbf{y}}=F_{W}(\mathbf{x})
.
The population loss is defined as
L(W)=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim p}[L_{W}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]
. For brevity, specifically in subsequent derivations, we denote the expectation over the data distribution simply as
\mathbb{E}
. The Hessian of the population loss is denoted
H=\nabla^{2}L(W)
. We define the residual at sample
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
as
\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}:=\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}
.

Applying the above to the network output, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial W^{l}}=W^{L:l+1}\otimes\big(\mathbf{x}^{\top}W^{1:l-1}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\times d_{l}d_{l-1}}.

Using the chain rule (backpropagation), the gradient of the loss for a sample
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
with respect to
W^{l}
is:

\displaystyle\eta\nabla_{W^{l}}L(W_{t})

\displaystyle=\big[W^{l+1:L}\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\big]\big[W^{l-1:1}\mathbf{x}\big]^{\top}

\displaystyle=W^{l+1:L}\big(W^{L:1}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}-\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\big)W^{1:l-1}.

For subsequent analysis, we introduce the following shorthand notations for the expected quantities (where
\mathbb{E}
denotes
\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mu}
):

\displaystyle\Omega:=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}[W^{L:1}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{\top}],

\displaystyle\Sigma_{xx}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}],\qquad\Sigma_{yx}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{\top}].

Gauss-Newton Decomposition.

Following the analytical technique in
[
19
,
24
]
, we apply the Gauss-Newton Decomposition to analyze the Hessian of a deep linear neural network. This technique involves examining the Hessian through the lens of the chain rule, decomposing the total loss Hessian
H_{L}=H_{o}+H_{f}
, where:

H_{o}=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{W}F(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}L_{W}}{\partial F^{2}}\right]\nabla_{W}F(\mathbf{x})\right],

and

H_{f}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{c=1}^{d_{L}}\left[\frac{\partial L_{W}}{\partial F_{c}}\right]\nabla^{2}_{W}F_{c}(\mathbf{x})\right].

In the case of Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss, this Gauss-Newton breakdown aligns precisely with the earlier analysis as shown in
[
19
]
. Following convention, we call the initial component
H_{o}
the
outer-product Hessian
and the latter component

We follow the balanced initialization procedure in

]
:

Initialization 2.1
.

Let

\min\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{L-1}\}\geq\min\{d_{0},d_{L}\}
, and let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over

matrices. A

of the weight matrices

, for

, is performed as follows:

Sample a matrix

2.

Compute the singular value decomposition

, where

Set the weight matrices as

Gradient Descent Dynamics.

We optimize the network parameters
W=\{W^{1},\ldots,W^{L}\}
using Gradient Descent (GD) with a constant learning rate
\eta>0
. Starting from the balanced initialization
W_{0}
, the parameters are updated iteratively. At training step

, the update rule for the

-th layer weight matrix follows:

\displaystyle W_{t+1}^{l}=W_{t}^{l}-\eta\nabla_{W^{l}}L(W_{t}),\quad\text{for }l=1,\ldots,L.

(2)

Following
[
1
]
, we assume the data is whitened and consider the gradient update in the expectation sense. Substituting the gradient derivation from the previous section, the population gradient update takes the explicit form:

\displaystyle W_{t+1}^{l}

\displaystyle=W_{t}^{l}-\eta\cdot W_{t}^{l+1:L}\big(W_{t}^{L:1}\Sigma_{xx}-\Sigma_{yx}\big)W_{t}^{1:l-1}

This update rule highlights that the evolution of each layer depends on the global error signal propagated through the adjacent layers.

Hessian Bifurcation Theory

3.1
Theorem Assumptions

We have the following assumptions for our results. However, note that not all assumptions are actually used in the theorem. Some are included solely to help the reader develop intuition for our results by considering a more concrete special case.

Assumption 3.1

(Sufficient Width & Whitened Input)
.

We impose structural constraints on the network width to prevent information propagation bottlenecks, assuming
d_{\ast}=\min\{d_{L},d_{0}\}\leq\min\{d_{L-1},\cdots,d_{1}\}
. Furthermore, regarding the data distribution, we assume the input covariance matrix is whitened (isotropic):

\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{d_{\ast}},

(4)

where
I_{d_{\ast}}
is the
d_{\ast}\times d_{\ast}
identity matrix.

Assumption 3.2

(Alignment with Initialization)
.

We assume the target mapping is aligned with the principal directions of
A
in
Initialization

2.1
. Let
U
and
V
be the singular vector matrices defined in the initialization procedure. To align with the data rank
r
, the cross-covariance matrix satisfies:

\Sigma_{yx}=U\mathcal{I}_{r}V^{\top},

(5)

where
\mathcal{I}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ast}\times d_{\ast}}\simeq I_{r}
.

Assumption 3.3

(Uniform Spectral Initialization (USI))
.

This is a special case that we will consider later. We assume the singular values of the initial weight product are uniform. Denoting the diagonal singular value matrix as
\Sigma
in
Initialization

2.1
(corresponding to
\Sigma_{0}
in the initialization definition), we have:

\Sigma_{0}^{1/L}\simeq\mu I_{r},

(6)

where
\mu>0
is a scalar constant. This ensures the network operates as a scaled isometry on the full parameter space at initialization.

3.2
Main Result

To establish our main result on the spectral structure of the Hessian, we first need to understand how the weight matrices evolve during training. A key observation is that all weight matrices in a deep linear network share a common spectral structure throughout the optimization process. This property, which we formalize in the following lemma
3.4
, serves as the foundation for our subsequent analysis.

Lemma 3.4

(Shared Spectral Structure)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
, all weight matrices
W^{k}_{t}
for
k=1,\ldots,L
share a same spectral structure
\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t},\lambda_{2,t},\ldots,\lambda_{d_{*},t})
at any time
t\geq 0
. Specifically:

1.

For
1<k<L
:
W^{k}_{t}\simeq\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}

2.

For
k=1
:
W^{1}_{t}\simeq\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}V^{\top}

3.

For
k=L
:
W^{L}_{t}\simeq U\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}

where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\times d_{*}}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}\times d_{*}}
are the left and right singular vector matrices from the balanced initialization, which remain constant throughout training. Furthermore, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i,t}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
evolve according to:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1},

(7)

while the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i,t}
for
i=r+1,\ldots,d_{*}
remain unchanged at their initial values.

Explain.
Lemma
3.4
reveals that under
Initialization

2.1
, the spectral structure of all weight matrices is governed by a single diagonal matrix
\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}
, whose diagonal entries
\lambda_{i,t}
evolve according to a unified update rule. This shared spectral structure is crucial because it implies that the optimization dynamics can be characterized entirely by tracking the evolution of these
d_{*}
eigenvalues.

Building upon this spectral characterization, we are now ready to present our main theorem, which establishes the bifurcation structure of the Hessian eigenvalues. The theorem demonstrates that even when the data covariance is perfectly balanced (i.e.,
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{d_{\ast}}
), the Hessian of the loss function exhibits a pronounced two-cluster spectral structure, with a gap that scales linearly with the network depth
L
.

Theorem 3.5

(Hessian Bifurcation)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
, and assuming
r\leq d_{*}
, consider a depth-
L
deep linear neural network trained with gradient descent with step size
\eta<\min\big\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{2}{(2L-1)M^{2L-2}}\big\}
. Let
\lambda_{i,t}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
denote the effective eigenvalues of the weight matrix
\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}
at time
t
. Suppose that
\lambda_{i,t}\in[m_{t}-\delta_{t},m_{t}+\delta_{t}]
and the condition
(m_{t}+\delta_{t})/(m_{t}-\delta_{t})<L^{\frac{1}{2(L-1)}}
holds. Then, the Hessian
H_{L,t}
exhibits a two-cluster spectral structure:

1.

(Dominant Space)
There exist
r^{2}
eigenvalues of
H_{L,t}
lying in:

\begin{split}\bigg[&L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}-O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t}),L(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})\bigg]\end{split}

(8)

2.

(Bulk Space)
There exist
(d_{\ast}+d_{L}-2r)r
eigenvalues of
H_{L,t}
lying in:

\begin{split}\bigg[&(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}-O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t}),(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})\bigg]\end{split}

(9)

3.

(Zero Space)
The remaining eigenvalues of
H_{L,t}
are zero.

Moreover, let
\lambda_{\text{dom}}
denote an arbitrary eigenvalue belonging to the Dominant Space and
\lambda_{\text{bulk}}
denote an arbitrary eigenvalue belonging to the Bulk Space. Their ratio satisfies:

\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}=\Theta(L),

(10)

where
\alpha
is a positive constant independent of
t
, provided
t
is sufficiently large.

Explain.
Theorem
3.5
provides a complete characterization of the Hessian spectrum in deep linear networks. The result reveals three distinct eigenspaces: (i) a dominant space containing
r^{2}
eigenvalues of order
L\cdot m_{t}^{2(L-1)}
, (ii) a bulk space containing
(d_{\ast}+d_{L}-2r)r
eigenvalues of order
m_{t}^{2(L-1)}
, and (iii) a zero space containing the remaining eigenvalues that vanish asymptotically. Crucially, the ratio between the dominant and bulk eigenvalues is
\Theta(L)
, demonstrating that the spectral gap is an intrinsic property of the network architecture rather than a consequence of data imbalance.

Remark.
The condition
(m_{t}+\delta_{t})/(m_{t}-\delta_{t})<L^{1/(2(L-1))}
ensures that the eigenvalues remain sufficiently concentrated to maintain a clear separation between the two clusters. This condition is naturally satisfied when the initialization is near-uniform and becomes increasingly easier to satisfy as the network depth
L
grows.

To gain deeper insight into the mechanism underlying this spectral bifurcation, we consider a special case where the weight matrices are initialized with uniform singular values. This setting, formalized in Assumption
3.3
, allows us to obtain a sharper characterization of the Hessian spectrum.

Corollary 3.6

(Hessian Bifurcation with USI)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
, and
3.3
, and assuming
r\leq d_{*}
, the Hessian
H_{L,t}
has exactly two distinct nonzero eigenvalues (up to an exponentially small perturbation). Specifically:

1.

(Dominant Space)
There exist
r^{2}
eigenvalues equal to
L\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
.

2.

(Bulk Space)
There exist
(d_{\ast}+d_{L}-2r)r
eigenvalues equal to
\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
.

3.

(Zero Space)
The remaining eigenvalues are
O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
.

Moreover, the ratio between the dominant and bulk eigenvalues is exactly
L
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}=L

(11)

where
\mu_{t}
denotes the common value of all effective eigenvalues
\lambda_{i,t}=\mu_{t}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
at time

, and
\alpha
is a positive constant.

Corollary
3.6
reveals the fundamental mechanism behind the Hessian bifurcation phenomenon. Under uniform spectral initialization, the Hessian possesses exactly two distinct nonzero eigenvalues:
L\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}
in the dominant space and
\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}
in the bulk space, with a ratio of exactly

.
Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
, we first establish that all weight matrices share a common spectral structure throughout training (Lemma

). Finally, applying Weyl’s inequality to

shows that the eigenvalues of the true Hessian

Under whitened data (
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{d_{\ast}}
), the non-zero spectrum splits into a dominant cluster (
r^{2}
) and a bulk cluster
(d_{\ast}+d_{L}-2r)r
.
Dominant eigenvalues grow approximately linearly with
L
, consistent with Theorem
3.5
.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we conduct a theoretical analysis of the spectral bifurcation phenomenon in the Hessian of deep linear neural networks, and demonstrate that this phenomenon is an intrinsic property of the model architecture rather than a consequence of data imbalance. Our main result (Theorem
3.5
) shows that the Hessian exhibits a two-cluster eigenvalue structure with a gap that scales linearly with the network depth
L
, even when the data covariance is perfectly balanced. The key insight is that directions within the effective parameter space accumulate curvature contributions from all
L
layers, while directions outside this space only contribute through a single layer, leading to an
L
-fold gap between the dominant and bulk eigenvalues. This depth-induced conditioning represents an important factor contributing to the ill-conditioned optimization landscape in deep neural networks, independent of data imbalance, suggesting that both data characteristics and model depth should be considered when designing optimization strategies.

A.1
Notation and Problem Setup

In this section, we recall the notation, problem setup, and key definitions from the main text that are essential for the subsequent theoretical analysis.

Notation and Matrix Operations.

We follow standard conventions: scalars are denoted by lowercase letters (e.g.,
\lambda,\eta
), vectors by bold lowercase letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
), and matrices by capital letters (e.g.,
W,H
). Let
I_{k}
denote the
k\times k
identity matrix.

For matrix products, we define
W^{k:l}=W^{k}\cdots W^{l}
when
k>l
and
W^{k:l}=(W^{k})^{\top}\cdots(W^{l})^{\top}
when
k<l
. Additional operations include:
\otimes
for Kronecker product,
\mathrm{vec}_{r}(\cdot)
for row-wise matrix vectorization, and
\mathrm{rk}(W)
for matrix rank.

For matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
with
m\geq p
and
n\geq q
, we define the zero-padding operator
\simeq
as:

\displaystyle A\simeq B\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad A=\begin{bmatrix}B&\mathbf{0}_{p\times(n-q)}\\
\mathbf{0}_{(m-p)\times q}&\mathbf{0}_{(m-p)\times(n-q)}\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathbf{0}_{i\times j}
denotes an
i\times j
zero matrix.

For matrix-matrix derivatives in row-wise vectorization form, we adopt the convention:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A}{\partial B}:=\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}_{r}(A)}{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}_{r}(B)^{\top}}.

A useful identity is:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(AWB)}{\partial W}=A\otimes B^{\top}.

Deep Linear Network Architecture.

We consider a depth-
L
deep linear neural network
F_{W}(\mathbf{x})=W^{L:1}\mathbf{x}
, where
W^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times d_{l-1}}
parameterizes the
l
-th layer for
l=1,\ldots,L
. The network has input dimension
d_{0}
, output dimension
d_{L}
, and hidden layer widths
d_{1},\ldots,d_{L-1}
. We denote the complete parameter set as
W:=\{W^{1},\ldots,W^{L}\}
and define
d_{\ast}=\min\{d_{0},d_{L}\}
.

The gradient of network output with respect to
W^{l}
is:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial W^{l}}=W^{L:l+1}\otimes\big(\mathbf{x}^{\top}W^{1:l-1}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\times d_{l}d_{l-1}}.

Data Distribution and Loss Function.

We consider a training dataset
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
sampled i.i.d. from distribution
p
. The squared loss is
L_{W}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-F_{W}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}
, and the population loss is
L(W)=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim p}[L_{W}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]
. For brevity, we denote this expectation simply as
\mathbb{E}
.

We define the residual as
\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}:=F_{W}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{y}
and introduce the following expected quantities:

\displaystyle\Omega:=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}],\quad\Sigma_{xx}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}],\quad\Sigma_{yx}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{\top}].

The gradient of the loss with respect to
W^{l}
is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W^{l}}L(W)=W^{l+1:L}\big(W^{L:1}\Sigma_{xx}-\Sigma_{yx}\big)W^{1:l-1}.

Gauss-Newton Decomposition.

Following
[
19
,
24
]
, we decompose the Hessian
H_{L}=\nabla^{2}L(W)
as
H_{L}=H_{o}+H_{f}
, where:

\displaystyle H_{o}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{W}F(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}L_{W}}{\partial F^{2}}\right]\nabla_{W}F(\mathbf{x})\right],

\displaystyle H_{f}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{c=1}^{d_{L}}\left[\frac{\partial L_{W}}{\partial F_{c}}\right]\nabla^{2}_{W}F_{c}(\mathbf{x})\right].

We refer to
H_{o}
as the
outer-product Hessian
and
H_{f}
as the
functional Hessian
.

Balanced Initialization.

Following
[
1
]
, we initialize the weight matrices using a balanced initialization (see Definition
2.1
in the main text). Specifically, we sample
A\sim\mathcal{D}
over
d_{L}\times d_{0}
matrices, compute its SVD
A=U\Sigma_{0}V^{\top}
, and set:

\displaystyle W^{L}\simeq U\Sigma_{0}^{1/L},\quad W^{l}\simeq\Sigma_{0}^{1/L}\text{ for }l=2,\ldots,L-1,\quad W^{1}\simeq\Sigma_{0}^{1/L}V^{\top}.

Gradient Descent Dynamics.

We optimize using gradient descent with learning rate
\eta>0
. Starting from
W_{0}
, the update rule at step
t
is:

\displaystyle W_{t+1}^{l}=W_{t}^{l}-\eta\nabla_{W^{l}}L(W_{t})=W_{t}^{l}-\eta\cdot W_{t}^{l+1:L}\big(W_{t}^{L:1}\Sigma_{xx}-\Sigma_{yx}\big)W_{t}^{1:l-1}.

Additional Notation for Analysis.

Let
r
denote the effective rank of the target mapping, and define
\mathcal{I}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ast}\times d_{\ast}}
such that
\mathcal{I}_{r}\simeq I_{r}
. Let
U
and
V
denote the left and right singular vectors fixed at initialization, and let
\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}
denote the dynamic diagonal matrix with entries
\lambda_{i,t}
at step
t
.

A.2
Theoretical results

We are here to give the proof of our theorem. First we need a few lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
, and
3.3
, and assuming
r\leq d_{*}=\min\{d_{0},d_{L}\}
, the outer-product Hessian has two distinct nonzero eigenvalues
L\mu^{2(L-1)}
and
\mu^{2(L-1)}
, where the former is exactly
L
times the latter. Moreover, they occur with multiplicities
r^{2}
and
(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r
, respectively.

Proof.

According to
[
19
]
, for a deep linear network,

\displaystyle H_{o}=A_{o}B_{o}A_{o}^{\top}

(12)

where
B_{o}=I_{d_{L}}\otimes\Sigma_{xx}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}d_{0}\times d_{L}d_{0}}
, and

\displaystyle A_{o}^{\top}=\left(W^{L:2}\otimes I_{d_{0}},\cdots,W^{L:l+1}\otimes W^{1:l-1},\cdots,I_{d_{L}}\otimes W^{1:L-1}\right)

(13)

Under Assumption
3.1
,
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{d_{*}}
. When
d_{*}=d_{0}\leq d_{L}
, we have
\Sigma_{xx}=I_{d_{0}}
, so
B_{o}=I_{d_{L}}\otimes I_{d_{0}}=I_{d_{L}d_{0}}
. In this case, the eigenvalues of
H_{o}=A_{o}A_{o}^{\top}
are identical to those of
A_{o}^{\top}A_{o}
.

Under balanced initialization (Initialization
2.1
) with Assumption
3.3
, we have
\Sigma_{0}^{1/L}\simeq\mu I_{r}
. The weight matrices take the form:

\displaystyle W^{L}\simeq U\Sigma_{0}^{1/L},\quad W^{L-1}\simeq\Sigma_{0}^{1/L},\quad\ldots,\quad W^{2}\simeq\Sigma_{0}^{1/L},\quad W^{1}\simeq\Sigma_{0}^{1/L}V^{\top}

(14)

where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\times d_{*}}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}\times d_{*}}
have orthonormal columns. Since
\Sigma_{0}^{1/L}\simeq\mu I_{r}
has only
r
nonzero entries, let
U_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\times r}
and
V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}\times r}
denote the first
r
columns of
U
and
V
, corresponding to the nonzero singular values.

We first compute the weight matrix products. For
W^{L:2}
:

\displaystyle W^{L:2}

\displaystyle=W^{L}W^{L-1}\cdots W^{2}\simeq(U\Sigma_{0}^{1/L})(\Sigma_{0}^{1/L})\cdots(\Sigma_{0}^{1/L})=U(\Sigma_{0}^{1/L})^{L-1}\simeq\mu^{L-1}U_{1}

(15)

Therefore:

\displaystyle W^{L:2}(W^{L:2})^{\top}=\mu^{2(L-1)}U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}

(16)

Similarly, for
W^{1:L-1}
:

\displaystyle W^{1:L-1}

\displaystyle=(W^{1})^{\top}(W^{2})^{\top}\cdots(W^{L-1})^{\top}\simeq V((\Sigma_{0}^{1/L})^{\top})^{L-1}\simeq\mu^{L-1}V_{1}

(17)

Therefore:

\displaystyle W^{1:L-1}(W^{1:L-1})^{\top}=\mu^{2(L-1)}V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}

(18)

For
2\leq l\leq L-1
:

\displaystyle W^{L:l+1}

\displaystyle\simeq\mu^{L-l}U_{1},\quad W^{1:l-1}\simeq\mu^{l-1}V_{1}

(19)

Therefore:

\displaystyle W^{L:l+1}(W^{L:l+1})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-l)}U_{1}U_{1}^{\top},\quad W^{1:l-1}(W^{1:l-1})^{\top}=\mu^{2(l-1)}V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}

(20)

Now we compute the Gram matrix
A_{o}^{\top}A_{o}
:

\displaystyle A_{o}^{\top}A_{o}

\displaystyle=(W^{L:2}\otimes I_{d_{0}})(W^{L:2}\otimes I_{d_{0}})^{\top}+(I_{d_{L}}\otimes W^{1:L-1})(I_{d_{L}}\otimes W^{1:L-1})^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{l=2}^{L-1}(W^{L:l+1}\otimes W^{1:l-1})(W^{L:l+1}\otimes W^{1:l-1})^{\top}

(21)

Using the Kronecker product property
(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD)
:

\displaystyle(W^{L:2}\otimes I_{d_{0}})(W^{L:2}\otimes I_{d_{0}})^{\top}

\displaystyle=W^{L:2}(W^{L:2})^{\top}\otimes I_{d_{0}}=\mu^{2(L-1)}U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}\otimes I_{d_{0}}

(22)

\displaystyle(I_{d_{L}}\otimes W^{1:L-1})(I_{d_{L}}\otimes W^{1:L-1})^{\top}

\displaystyle=I_{d_{L}}\otimes W^{1:L-1}(W^{1:L-1})^{\top}=\mu^{2(L-1)}I_{d_{L}}\otimes V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}

(23)

\displaystyle(W^{L:l+1}\otimes W^{1:l-1})(W^{L:l+1}\otimes W^{1:l-1})^{\top}

\displaystyle=W^{L:l+1}(W^{L:l+1})^{\top}\otimes W^{1:l-1}(W^{1:l-1})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-l)}U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}\otimes\mu^{2(l-1)}V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}=\mu^{2(L-1)}U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}\otimes V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}

(24)

Substituting back:

\displaystyle A_{o}^{\top}A_{o}

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}\otimes I_{d_{0}}+\mu^{2(L-1)}I_{d_{L}}\otimes V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}+(L-2)\mu^{2(L-1)}U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}\otimes V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}\left[U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}\otimes I_{d_{0}}+I_{d_{L}}\otimes V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}+(L-2)U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}\otimes V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}\right]

(25)

To analyze the eigenvalues, let
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\}
be the columns of
U_{1}
, extended to an orthonormal basis
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{d_{L}}\}
for
\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
. Similarly, let
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\}
be the columns of
V_{1}
, extended to an orthonormal basis
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d_{0}}\}
for
\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
. The vectors
u_{i}\otimes v_{j}
for
i=1,\ldots,d_{L}
and
j=1,\ldots,d_{0}
form an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}d_{0}}
.

Note that
U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i}=u_{i}
if
i\leq r
and
U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i}=0
if
i>r
. Similarly,
V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}v_{j}=v_{j}
if
j\leq r
and
V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}v_{j}=0
if
j>r
. We compute the action of
A_{o}^{\top}A_{o}
on these basis vectors for each case:

(Dominant space)
If
i\leq r
and
j\leq r
:

\displaystyle A_{o}^{\top}A_{o}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}\left[(U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i})\otimes v_{j}+u_{i}\otimes(V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}v_{j})+(L-2)(U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i})\otimes(V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}v_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}\left[u_{i}\otimes v_{j}+u_{i}\otimes v_{j}+(L-2)u_{i}\otimes v_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=L\mu^{2(L-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

(26)

This gives eigenvalue
L\mu^{2(L-1)}
with multiplicity
r\times r=r^{2}
.

(Bulk space, part 1)
If
i\leq r
and
j>r
:

\displaystyle A_{o}^{\top}A_{o}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}\left[(U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i})\otimes v_{j}+u_{i}\otimes(V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}v_{j})+(L-2)(U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i})\otimes(V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}v_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}\left[u_{i}\otimes v_{j}+u_{i}\otimes 0+(L-2)u_{i}\otimes 0\right]

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

(27)

This gives eigenvalue
\mu^{2(L-1)}
with multiplicity
r\times(d_{0}-r)=r(d_{0}-r)
.

(Bulk space, part 2)
If
i>r
and
j\leq r
:

\displaystyle A_{o}^{\top}A_{o}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}\left[(U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i})\otimes v_{j}+u_{i}\otimes(V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}v_{j})+(L-2)(U_{1}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i})\otimes(V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}v_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}\left[0\otimes v_{j}+u_{i}\otimes v_{j}+(L-2)\cdot 0\otimes v_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

(28)

This gives eigenvalue
\mu^{2(L-1)}
with multiplicity
(d_{L}-r)\times r=(d_{L}-r)r
.

(Zero space)
If
i>r
and
j>r
:

\displaystyle A_{o}^{\top}A_{o}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

\displaystyle=\mu^{2(L-1)}\left[0\otimes v_{j}+u_{i}\otimes 0+(L-2)\cdot 0\otimes 0\right]=0

(29)

This gives eigenvalue
0
with multiplicity
(d_{L}-r)\times(d_{0}-r)
.

Combining the two parts of the bulk space, the total multiplicity is:

\displaystyle r(d_{0}-r)+(d_{L}-r)r=(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r

(30)

We conclude that the outer-product Hessian has two distinct nonzero eigenvalues
L\mu^{2(L-1)}
and
\mu^{2(L-1)}
, occurring with multiplicities
r^{2}
and
(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r
, respectively. The total number of nonzero eigenvalues is
r^{2}+(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r=(d_{0}+d_{L}-r)r
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Under Assumption
3.1
and
3.2
, if
d_{L}>d_{*}
(i.e.,
d_{L}>d_{0}
), besides
W^{L}
, the eigenvectors of
W^{k}
are invariant. If
d_{L}<d_{*}
(i.e.,
d_{L}<d_{0}
), besides
W^{1}
, the eigenvectors of
W^{k}
are invariant. Particularly, if
d_{L}=d_{*}=d_{0}
, then for all
W^{k}
, the eigenvectors of
W^{k}
are invariant.

Proof.

Under Assumption
3.1
, since
d_{*}\leq\min\{d_{L-1},d_{L-2},\ldots,d_{1}\}
, according to the balanced initialization procedure:

\displaystyle\forall\,1<k\leq L-1,\,k\in\mathbb{N},\quad W^{k}_{0}

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma^{1/L}

\displaystyle W^{1}_{0}

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma^{1/L}V^{\top}

\displaystyle W^{L}_{0}

\displaystyle\simeq U\Sigma^{1/L}

Then we consider three cases.

1.

For
1<k<L
, considering the dynamic behavior between step
t=0
and step
t=1
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W^{k}}L(W^{k}_{0})

\displaystyle=W^{k+1:L}_{0}(W^{L:1}_{0}\Sigma_{xx}-\Sigma_{yx})W^{1:k-1}_{0}

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma^{(L-k)/L}U^{\top}(U\Sigma V^{\top}\Sigma_{xx}-\Sigma_{yx})V\Sigma^{(k-1)/L}

(31)

Under Assumption
3.1
,
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{r}
. Since the balanced initialization chooses
V
such that its column space lies within the support of
\Sigma_{xx}
, we have
V^{\top}\Sigma_{xx}V=I_{d_{*}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle V^{\top}\Sigma_{xx}=V^{\top}

(32)

which implies
U\Sigma V^{\top}\Sigma_{xx}=U\Sigma V^{\top}
.

According to Assumption
3.2
,
\Sigma_{yx}=UV^{\top}
. Note that
U^{\top}U=I_{d_{*}}
and
V^{\top}V=I_{d_{*}}
. Substituting into
Equation

31
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W^{k}}L(W^{k}_{0})

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma^{(L-k)/L}U^{\top}(U\Sigma V^{\top}-UV^{\top})V\Sigma^{(k-1)/L}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{(L-k)/L}U^{\top}U(\Sigma-I_{d_{*}})V^{\top}V\Sigma^{(k-1)/L}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{(L-k)/L}(\Sigma-I_{d_{*}})\Sigma^{(k-1)/L}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma^{(L-1)/L}

Since
\Sigma
is a diagonal matrix,
\Sigma^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma^{(L-1)/L}
is also diagonal. The update rule
W^{k}_{t+1}=W^{k}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{W^{k}}L(W^{k}_{t})
only changes the diagonal elements.

For example, suppose
W^{k}_{0}\simeq\Sigma^{1/L}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1,0}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\lambda_{d_{*},0}\end{pmatrix}
, then

\displaystyle W^{k}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1,0}-\eta\lambda_{1,0}^{2L-1}+\eta\lambda_{1,0}^{L-1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\lambda_{d_{*},0}-\eta\lambda_{d_{*},0}^{2L-1}+\eta\lambda_{d_{*},0}^{L-1}\end{pmatrix}

Thus the
i
-th eigenvalue follows the dynamic

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}

(33)

and the eigenvectors remain invariant.

2.

For
k=1
, considering the dynamic behavior between step
t=0
and step
t=1
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W^{1}}L(W^{1}_{0})

\displaystyle=W^{2:L}_{0}(W^{L:1}_{0}\Sigma_{xx}-\Sigma_{yx})

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma^{(L-1)/L}U^{\top}(U\Sigma V^{\top}-UV^{\top})

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{(L-1)/L}U^{\top}U(\Sigma-I_{d_{*}})V^{\top}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{(L-1)/L}(\Sigma-I_{d_{*}})V^{\top}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma^{(L-1)/L})V^{\top}

For the update rule
W^{1}_{t+1}=W^{1}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{W^{1}}L(W^{1}_{t})
, since
W^{1}_{0}\simeq\Sigma^{1/L}V^{\top}
:

\displaystyle W^{1}_{1}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{1/L}V^{\top}-\eta(\Sigma^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma^{(L-1)/L})V^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left[\Sigma^{1/L}-\eta(\Sigma^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma^{(L-1)/L})\right]V^{\top}

We can separate
V^{\top}
, thus the eigenvalues also follow
Equation

33
and the eigenvectors (determined by
V^{\top}
) remain invariant.

3.

For
k=L
, considering the dynamic behavior between step
t=0
and step
t=1
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W^{L}}L(W^{L}_{0})

\displaystyle=(W^{L:1}_{0}\Sigma_{xx}-\Sigma_{yx})W^{1:L-1}_{0}

\displaystyle\simeq(U\Sigma V^{\top}-UV^{\top})V\Sigma^{(L-1)/L}

\displaystyle=U(\Sigma-I_{d_{*}})V^{\top}V\Sigma^{(L-1)/L}

\displaystyle=U(\Sigma-I_{d_{*}})\Sigma^{(L-1)/L}

\displaystyle=U(\Sigma^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma^{(L-1)/L})

Now we analyze when the eigenvectors of
W^{L}
are invariant.

Note that if
d_{L}=d_{*}=d_{0}
, then
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\times d_{L}}
is a square orthogonal matrix. Since
W^{L}_{0}\simeq U\Sigma^{1/L}
:

\displaystyle W^{L}_{1}

\displaystyle=U\Sigma^{1/L}-\eta U(\Sigma^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma^{(L-1)/L})

\displaystyle=U\left[\Sigma^{1/L}-\eta(\Sigma^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma^{(L-1)/L})\right]

The left factor
U
can be separated, so the eigenvalues also follow
Equation

33
and the eigenvectors (determined by
U
) remain invariant.

However, if
d_{L}>d_{*}
(i.e.,
d_{L}>d_{0}
), then
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\times d_{*}}
is not square, and the gradient
U(\Sigma^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma^{(L-1)/L})
cannot be factored to preserve the eigenvector structure of
W^{L}
.

Similarly, if
d_{L}<d_{*}
(i.e.,
d_{L}<d_{0}
), then
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}\times d_{*}}
with
d_{0}>d_{L}
, and the gradient for
W^{1}
cannot be factored to preserve the eigenvector structure of
W^{1}
.

Therefore, the layer-wise Hessian eigenvectors are invariant for all layers except
W^{L}
when
d_{L}>d_{*}
, or except
W^{1}
when
d_{L}<d_{*}
. When
d_{L}=d_{*}=d_{0}
, all eigenvectors are invariant.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Under Assumption
3.1
and
3.2
, for initialization
\lambda_{i,0}\in(0,M]
,
i=1,2,\ldots,r
, where
M=\max_{i=1,2,\ldots,r}\{1,\lambda_{i,0}\}
and proper
\eta<\min\left\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{2}{(2L-1)M^{2L-2}}\right\}
, the asymptotic behavior of
\lambda_{i,t}
will converge to
1
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,r
, i.e.,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{i,t}=1
. The remaining
d_{*}-r
eigenvalues
\lambda_{i,t}
for
i=r+1,\ldots,d_{*}
remain unchanged during training.

Proof.

According to Lemma
A.2
, the gradient of the loss with respect to
W^{k}
involves
\Sigma_{xx}
. Under Assumption
3.1
,
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{r}
, which means only the first
r
directions of the input space contribute to the gradient.

More precisely, for
1<k<L
, the gradient is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W^{k}}L(W^{k}_{t})

\displaystyle=W^{k+1:L}_{t}(W^{L:1}_{t}\Sigma_{xx}-\Sigma_{yx})W^{1:k-1}_{t}

Since
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{r}
and the balanced initialization chooses
V
such that its first
r
rows
V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{*}}
satisfy
V_{1}^{\top}V_{1}=I_{r}
(with
V_{2}=\mathbf{0}
), the effective gradient only updates the first
r
eigenvalues of
\Sigma^{1/L}
.

For the first
r
eigenvalues, let
i=1,2,\ldots,r
be fixed and arbitrary. The update rule is:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}

(34)

For the remaining eigenvalues
i=r+1,\ldots,d_{*}
, since they correspond to directions orthogonal to the support of
\Sigma_{xx}
, the gradient contribution is zero, and thus:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t},\quad\forall\,i=r+1,\ldots,d_{*}

Now we prove convergence for
i=1,2,\ldots,r
. First, we show that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda_{i,t}
is bounded by
(0,M]
. The initialization satisfies this condition. For induction, assume
\lambda_{i,t}\in(0,M]
. We consider the following cases:

Case 1:
\lambda_{i,t}\in(0,1]
.
In this regime, the update rule
Equation

34
implies
\lambda_{i,t+1}\geq\lambda_{i,t}>0
since
0<\lambda_{i,t}\leq 1
.

If
\lambda_{i,t+1}>M
, it implies:

\displaystyle\eta>\frac{M-\lambda_{i,t}}{\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})}\geq\frac{1-\lambda_{i,t}}{\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})}

Note that
1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L}=(1-\lambda_{i,t})(1+\lambda_{i,t}+\lambda_{i,t}^{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1})
. Using the fact
\lambda_{i,t}\leq 1
, therefore
\eta>\frac{1-\lambda_{i,t}}{\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})}
implies:

\displaystyle\eta>\frac{1}{\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}(1+\lambda_{i,t}+\lambda_{i,t}^{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1})}\geq\frac{1}{L}

Since
\eta<\min\left\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{2}{(2L-1)M^{2L-2}}\right\}
, thus
0<\lambda_{i,t+1}\leq M
as well.

Case 2:
\lambda_{i,t}\in(1,M]
.
In this case, the update rule
Equation

34
implies
\lambda_{i,t+1}\leq\lambda_{i,t}
since
\lambda_{i,t}>1
and
L\geq 1
. Using the induction assumption
\lambda_{i,t}\leq M
yields
\lambda_{i,t+1}\leq M
as well. Moreover, since
\eta<\min\left\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{2}{(2L-1)M^{2L-2}}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{M^{2L-2}}
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i,t}(1-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-2})>\lambda_{i,t}(1-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2})\geq\lambda_{i,t}(1-\eta M^{2L-2})>0

Therefore, in both cases, we have shown
\lambda_{i,t+1}\in(0,M]
. By the principle of induction, it can be concluded that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda_{i,t}
is bounded by
(0,M]
.

Next, we show that after finite steps
\lambda_{i,t}
will lie in a neighborhood of
1
, namely
[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]
, where
0<\epsilon<1
is a constant. Define

\displaystyle f(\lambda_{i,t})=(\lambda_{i,t}-1)^{2},\quad g(\lambda_{i,t})=\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}-\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1}

Consider:

\displaystyle f(\lambda_{i,t+1})-f(\lambda_{i,t})

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{i,t+1}-1)^{2}-(\lambda_{i,t}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left((\lambda_{i,t}-1)+\eta g(\lambda_{i,t})\right)^{2}-(\lambda_{i,t}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\eta g(\lambda_{i,t})(\lambda_{i,t}-1)+\eta^{2}g^{2}(\lambda_{i,t})

\displaystyle=-\eta g^{2}(\lambda_{i,t})\left(\frac{-2(\lambda_{i,t}-1)}{g(\lambda_{i,t})}-\eta\right)

If
\lambda_{i,t}=1
, then by the update rule
\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t+2}=\cdots=1
. Therefore, suppose
\lambda_{i,t}\neq 1
. Note that
g(1)=0
and
g
is differentiable. By Lagrange’s Mean Value Theorem, there exists a point
\xi
between
\lambda_{i,t}
and
1
such that

\displaystyle g(\lambda_{i,t})=g(\lambda_{i,t})-g(1)=g^{\prime}(\xi)(\lambda_{i,t}-1)

Note that
g^{\prime}(x)=(L-1)x^{L-2}-(2L-1)x^{2L-2}
, and

\displaystyle\eta<\min\left\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{2}{(2L-1)M^{2L-2}}\right\}\leq\frac{2}{\max_{x\in(0,M]}|g^{\prime}(x)|}

Since
\eta>0
and
g^{2}(\lambda_{i,t})>0
(since
\lambda_{i,t}\neq 1
), thus:

\displaystyle f(\lambda_{i,t+1})-f(\lambda_{i,t})=-\eta g^{2}(\lambda_{i,t})\left(\frac{2}{g^{\prime}(\xi)}-\eta\right)<0

So long as
\lambda_{i,t}
is outside the neighborhood
(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)
, i.e.,
|\lambda_{i,t}-1|>\epsilon
, the term
|g(\lambda_{i,t})|
is bounded away from zero. Let

\displaystyle C_{\epsilon}=\min_{x\in(0,M],|x-1|\geq\epsilon}g(x)^{2}>0

Then,

\displaystyle f(\lambda_{i,t+1})-f(\lambda_{i,t})\leq-\eta C_{\epsilon}\left(\frac{2}{g^{\prime}(\xi)}-\eta\right)\leq-C_{\epsilon}\eta^{2}<0

where
C_{\epsilon}
is a strictly positive constant depending on
\epsilon
.

If
\lambda_{i,t}
never enters the neighborhood
[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]
, the energy
f(\lambda_{i,t})
would decrease by at least
C_{\epsilon}\eta^{2}
at every step, eventually becoming negative, which contradicts the definition
f(\lambda)\geq 0
. Therefore, the trajectory must enter the neighborhood
[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]
in finite steps.

Finally, we prove
\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda_{i,t}=1
. Since after finite steps
\lambda_{i,t}
enters the neighborhood
[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]
with
0<\epsilon<1
, consider the operator
T(\lambda)=\lambda+\eta g(\lambda)
. We have

\displaystyle|T^{\prime}(\lambda)|=|1+\eta g^{\prime}(\lambda)|=\left|1+\eta\left((L-1)\lambda^{L-2}-(2L-1)\lambda^{2L-2}\right)\right|<1

for
\lambda\in[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]
, which implies that
T
is a contraction mapping on this neighborhood. By the Banach Fixed Point Theorem,
\lambda_{i,t}
converges to the unique fixed point
1
as
t\to\infty
.

Since
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
is arbitrary, the proof holds for all
i=1,2,\ldots,r
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Under Assumption
3.1
and
3.2
, for initialization
\lambda_{i,0}\in(0,M]
,
i=1,2,\ldots,r
, where
M=\max_{i=1,2,\ldots,r}\{1,\lambda_{i,0}\}
. Suppose the step size satisfies
\eta<\min\left\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{1}{M^{2L-2}}\right\}
, then the sequence
\{\lambda_{i,t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is strictly bounded away from zero. That is, there exists a constant
C_{\min}>0
such that
\lambda_{i,t}\geq C_{\min}
for all
t\geq 0
and
i=1,2,\ldots,r
. Furthermore, there exists
T>0
such that for all
t\geq T
,
\min_{i=1,\ldots,r}(\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2})
has a positive lower bound
\alpha>0
that is independent of
t
.

Proof.

Let
i=1,2,\ldots,r
be fixed and arbitrary. According to Lemma
A.2
, the update rule of the eigenvalues is

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}

As shown in Lemma
A.3
,
0<\lambda_{i,t}\leq\lambda_{i,t+1}
if
\lambda_{i,t}\in(0,1]
and
\lambda_{i,t+1}\leq\lambda_{i,t}\leq M
if
\lambda_{i,t}\in[1,M]
. Therefore, to find a lower bound, we only need to analyze the case
\lambda_{i,t}\in[1,M]
. Note that

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}\left(1-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-2}\right)

Let
h(\lambda_{i,t})=1-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-2}
. Since
0<\eta<\min\left\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{1}{M^{2L-2}}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{M^{2L-2}}
, thus

\displaystyle h(\lambda_{i,t})=1-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-2}>1-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2}>0

Since
h(\lambda_{i,t})
is a continuous function on the compact set
[1,M]
and
h(\lambda_{i,t})>0
for all
\lambda_{i,t}
, it attains a strictly positive minimum
\delta_{i}>0
. Therefore, for
\lambda_{i,t}\in[1,M]
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}h(\lambda_{i,t})\geq\delta_{i}>0

Thus, let
C_{i}=\min\{\lambda_{i,0},\delta_{i}\}
, then for all
t
,
\lambda_{i,t}\geq C_{i}
. Let
C_{\min}=\min_{i=1,2,\ldots,r}\{C_{i}\}>0
, we have for all
i=1,2,\ldots,r
and all
t
,
\lambda_{i,t}\geq C_{\min}>0
.

Furthermore, by Lemma
A.3
, for each
i=1,2,\ldots,r
, there exists
T_{i}>0
such that for all
t\geq T_{i}
,
\lambda_{i,t}\in[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]
for some
0<\epsilon<1
. Let
T=\max_{i=1,\ldots,r}T_{i}
. Then for all
t\geq T
and all
i=1,2,\ldots,r
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t}\geq 1-\epsilon>0

Therefore, for all
t\geq T
:

\displaystyle\min_{i=1,\ldots,r}(\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2})\geq(1-\epsilon)^{2L-2}:=\alpha>0

This lower bound
\alpha
depends only on
\epsilon
and
L
, and is independent of
t
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
H_{f}
be a block matrix partitioned into blocks
H_{kl}
, where
k,l\in\{1,\dots,L\}
. Then the spectral norm of
H_{f}
satisfies

\|H_{f}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{k,l}\|H_{kl}\|_{2}^{2}}.

Proof.

For any unit vector
\mathbf{x}
with
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1
, we partition
\mathbf{x}
conformably with the blocks of
H_{f}
as
\mathbf{x}=[\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{L}^{\top}]^{\top}
. The condition
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1
implies that
\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|\mathbf{x}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}=1
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=H_{f}\mathbf{x}
. The
k
-th block of
\mathbf{y}
, denoted by
\mathbf{y}_{k}
, is given by:

\mathbf{y}_{k}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}H_{kl}\mathbf{x}_{l}.

By the triangle inequality and the compatibility of the spectral norm, the norm of
\mathbf{y}_{k}
is bounded by:

\|\mathbf{y}_{k}\|_{2}=\left\|\sum_{l=1}^{L}H_{kl}\mathbf{x}_{l}\right\|_{2}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|H_{kl}\mathbf{x}_{l}\|_{2}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|H_{kl}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{l}\|_{2}.

We compute the squared norm of the entire vector
\mathbf{y}
:

\|H_{f}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|H_{kl}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{l}\|_{2}\right)^{2}.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the inner sum:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|H_{kl}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{l}\|_{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|H_{kl}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|\mathbf{x}_{l}\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|H_{kl}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\cdot 1.

Substituting this back:

\|H_{f}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|H_{kl}\|_{2}^{2}.

Since the spectral norm is defined as
\|H_{f}\|_{2}=\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|H_{f}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
, taking the square root of both sides yields the desired upper bound.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

Under Assumption
3.1
and
3.2
, when
L_{W}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}<\epsilon
, the 2-norm of functional Hessian
\|H_{f}\|_{2}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\|H_{f}\|_{2}\leq\|\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\|_{2}^{L-2}\sqrt{2L(L-1)r}\,\epsilon^{1/2}=O(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}})

Proof.

According to
[
19
]
, the functional Hessian
H_{f}
can be written as a block matrix, where

\displaystyle\forall\,k<l,\quad H_{f}^{kl}=W_{t}^{k+1:l-1}\otimes W_{t}^{k-1:1}\Omega^{\top}W_{t}^{L:l+1}

\displaystyle\forall\,k>l,\quad H_{f}^{kl}=W_{t}^{k+1:L}\Omega W_{t}^{1:l-1}\otimes W_{t}^{k-1:l+1}

and the diagonal entries are
0
.

By Lemma
A.2
, the eigenvectors of the weight matrices are invariant during training. Under balanced initialization with
\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t},\ldots,\lambda_{d_{*},t})
, we have:

\displaystyle W_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma_{t}^{1/L},\quad\text{for }1<k<L

\displaystyle W_{t}^{1}

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}V^{\top}

\displaystyle W_{t}^{L}

\displaystyle\simeq U\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}

Note that for
k<l
:

\displaystyle W_{t}^{k+1:l-1}\otimes W_{t}^{k-1:1}\Omega^{\top}W_{t}^{L:l+1}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\cdots\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\otimes\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\cdots\left(\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}V^{\top}\right)\Omega^{\top}\left(U\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\right)\cdots\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t}^{(l-k-1)/L}\otimes\Sigma_{t}^{(k-1)/L}V^{\top}\Omega^{\top}U\Sigma_{t}^{(L-l)/L}

And for
k>l
:

\displaystyle W_{t}^{k+1:L}\Omega W_{t}^{1:l-1}\otimes W_{t}^{k-1:l+1}

\displaystyle=\left(U\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\right)\cdots\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\Omega\,\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\cdots\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}V^{\top}\otimes\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\cdots\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}

\displaystyle=U\Sigma_{t}^{(L-k)/L}\Omega V\Sigma_{t}^{(l-1)/L}\otimes\Sigma_{t}^{(k-l-1)/L}

For
k<l
, we have
(l-k-1)+(k-1)+(L-l)=L-2
. For
k>l
, we have
(L-k)+(l-1)+(k-l-1)=L-2
. Using the submultiplicativity of the spectral norm
\|AB\|_{2}\leq\|A\|_{2}\|B\|_{2}
and the property
\|A\otimes B\|_{2}=\|A\|_{2}\|B\|_{2}
, along with the fact that
\|\Sigma_{t}^{s/L}\|_{2}=\|\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\|_{2}^{s}
and
\|U\|_{2}=\|V\|_{2}=1
(since
U
and
V
have orthonormal columns), we obtain:

\displaystyle\|H_{f}^{kl}\|_{2}\leq\|\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\|_{2}^{L-2}\cdot\|\Omega\|_{2}

Recall that under Assumption
3.1
,
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{r}
. Since the balanced initialization chooses
V
such that its column space lies within the support of
\Sigma_{xx}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\text{Tr}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top})]=\text{Tr}(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}])=\text{Tr}(\Sigma_{xx})=r

Recall that
\Omega=\mathbb{E}[\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]
, where
\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{y}
. Using Jensen’s inequality, we have:

\displaystyle\|\Omega\|_{2}=\|\mathbb{E}[\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]\|_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\|_{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}]

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for expectations yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}]}<\sqrt{2r\epsilon}

which implies
\|\Omega\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2r\epsilon}
.

Therefore, using
\|H_{f}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{k,l}\|H_{f}^{kl}\|_{2}^{2}}
:

\displaystyle\|H_{f}\|_{2}\leq\|\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\|_{2}^{L-2}\sqrt{2L(L-1)r\epsilon}=\|\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}\|_{2}^{L-2}\sqrt{2L(L-1)r}\,\epsilon^{1/2}

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.7
.

Under Assumption
3.1
and
3.2
, and assuming the first
r
rows of
V
, denoted
V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{*}}
, satisfy
V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}=I_{r}
, the population loss
L(W_{t})
converges linearly to its minimum possible value. Specifically, let
L_{\min}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}]-\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{*}}\lambda_{i,0}^{L}-\frac{r}{2}
be the irreducible error, the excess loss
\tilde{L}_{t}=L(W_{t})-L_{\min}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{t}\leq\tilde{L}_{0}\cdot e^{-2L\alpha\eta t}=O\left(e^{-2L\alpha\eta t}\right)

(35)

where
\alpha

\min_{i=1,\ldots,r,\,t}(\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2})
.

Proof.

Recall that the population loss is
L(W_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}\left[\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]
. Thus,

\displaystyle L(W_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}\left[\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{y}-2\mathbf{y}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}+\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\text{Tr}(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t})]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\text{Tr}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\top})]

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\text{Tr}(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\text{Tr}(\mathbf{y}^{\top}W^{L:1}_{t}\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(W^{L:1}_{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}])

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(W^{L:1}_{t}\Sigma_{yx}^{\top})

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\text{Tr}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\top})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\text{Tr}(W^{L:1}_{t}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}(W^{L:1}_{t})^{\top})]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(W^{L:1}_{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}](W^{L:1}_{t})^{\top})

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(W^{L:1}_{t}\Sigma_{xx}(W^{L:1}_{t})^{\top})

By Lemma
A.2
, the eigenvectors of the weight matrices are invariant during training. Thus,
W^{L:1}_{t}=U\Sigma_{t}V^{\top}
, where
\Sigma_{t}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t}^{L},\ldots,\lambda_{d_{*},t}^{L})
.

Under Assumption
3.2
,
\Sigma_{yx}=UV^{\top}
, so
\Sigma_{yx}^{\top}=VU^{\top}
. Since
V^{\top}V=I_{d_{*}}
and
U^{\top}U=I_{d_{*}}
:

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(W^{L:1}_{t}\Sigma_{yx}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(U\Sigma_{t}V^{\top}VU^{\top})=\text{Tr}(U\Sigma_{t}U^{\top})=\text{Tr}(\Sigma_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{*}}\lambda_{i,t}^{L}

Under Assumption
3.1
,
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{r}
. Partition
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}\times d_{*}}
by rows as
V=\begin{pmatrix}V_{1}\\
V_{2}\end{pmatrix}
where
V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{*}}

\displaystyle V^{\top}\Sigma_{xx}V=V_{1}^{\top}V_{1}

Under the alignment condition
V_{1}V_{1}^{\top}=I_{r}

P:=V_{1}^{\top}V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{*}\times d_{*}}
is a projection matrix with rank
r

P=\text{diag}(I_{r},\mathbf{0}_{(d_{*}-r)\times(d_{*}-r)})

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(W^{L:1}_{t}\Sigma_{xx}(W^{L:1}_{t})^{\top})

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(U\Sigma_{t}V^{\top}\Sigma_{xx}V\Sigma_{t}U^{\top})=\text{Tr}(\Sigma_{t}P\Sigma_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i,t}^{2L}

Substituting back into the loss equation:

\displaystyle L(W_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}]-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{*}}\lambda_{i,t}^{L}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i,t}^{2L}

By Lemma
A.3
, the first
r
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1,t},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t}

1

d_{*}-r
eigenvalues
\lambda_{r+1,t},\ldots,\lambda_{d_{*},t}
remain at their initial values
\lambda_{r+1,0},\ldots,\lambda_{d_{*},0}
.

Separating the terms:

\displaystyle L(W_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}]-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i,t}^{L}-\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{*}}\lambda_{i,0}^{L}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i,t}^{2L}

Completing the square for the first
r
components:

\displaystyle L(W_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}]-\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{*}}\lambda_{i,0}^{L}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\lambda_{i,t}^{2L}-2\lambda_{i,t}^{L}+1)-\frac{r}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}]-\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{*}}\lambda_{i,0}^{L}-\frac{r}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})^{2}

The minimum loss is achieved when
\lambda_{i,t}^{L}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,r
:

\displaystyle L_{\min}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}]-\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{*}}\lambda_{i,0}^{L}-\frac{r}{2}

Thus, the excess loss is:

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{t}=L(W_{t})-L_{\min}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})^{2}

According to Lemma
A.2
, for each
i=1,\ldots,r
, the eigenvalue update rule is:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}

Note that the function
f(x)=x^{L}
is convex when
x>0
. Thus, by the definition of convex function, for all
1\leq i\leq r
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}^{L}\geq\lambda_{i,t}^{L}+L\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}(\lambda_{i,t+1}-\lambda_{i,t})=\lambda_{i,t}^{L}+L\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}(\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}-\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1})

which implies:

\displaystyle 1-\lambda_{i,t+1}^{L}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\left[\lambda_{i,t}^{L}+L\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}(\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}-\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1})\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})-L\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2}(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})

\displaystyle=(1-L\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2})(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})

Therefore,

\displaystyle(1-\lambda_{i,t+1}^{L})^{2}\leq(1-L\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2})^{2}(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})^{2}

Using the fact
(1-x)^{2}\leq e^{-2x}
for
x\in[0,1]
yields:

\displaystyle(1-\lambda_{i,t+1}^{L})^{2}\leq e^{-2L\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2}}(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})^{2}

Since
\alpha
is a positive lower bound of
\min_{i=1,\ldots,r,\,t}(\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-2})
, summing
i
from
1
to
r
yields:

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{t+1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(1-\lambda_{i,t+1}^{L})^{2}\leq e^{-2L\alpha\eta}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}(1-\lambda_{i,t}^{L})^{2}=e^{-2L\alpha\eta}\tilde{L}_{t}

Repeating this process yields:

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{t}\leq e^{-2L\alpha\eta t}\tilde{L}_{0}=O\left(e^{-2L\alpha\eta t}\right)

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.9
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2

r\leq d_{*}=\min\{d_{0},d_{L}\}

t

\delta_{t}>0

(Dominant space)

r^{2}
eigenvalues lying in
[L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)},L(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}]

2.

(Bulk space)

(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r
eigenvalues lying in
[(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)},(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}]

3.

(Zero space)

(d_{L}-r)(d_{0}-r)
eigenvalues equal to zero.

(d_{0}+d_{L}-r)r

Proof.

According to
[
19
]
, for a deep linear network at time

\displaystyle H_{o,t}=A_{o,t}B_{o}A_{o,t}^{\top}

where
B_{o}=I_{d_{L}}\otimes\Sigma_{xx}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}d_{0}\times d_{L}d_{0}}
, and

\displaystyle A_{o,t}^{\top}=\left(W_{t}^{L:2}\otimes I_{d_{0}},\ldots,W_{t}^{L:l+1}\otimes W_{t}^{1:l-1},\ldots,I_{d_{L}}\otimes W_{t}^{1:L-1}\right)

Under Assumption
3.1
,
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{d_{*}}
. When
d_{*}=d_{0}\leq d_{L}
, we have
\Sigma_{xx}=I_{d_{0}}
, so
B_{o}=I_{d_{L}}\otimes I_{d_{0}}=I_{d_{L}d_{0}}
. In this case, the eigenvalues of
H_{o,t}=A_{o,t}A_{o,t}^{\top}
are identical to those of
A_{o,t}^{\top}A_{o,t}
.

By Lemma
A.2
, the eigenvectors of the weight matrices are invariant during training. Thus, at time
t
, we have
W_{t}^{L:1}=U\Sigma_{t}V^{\top}
, where
\Sigma_{t}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t}^{L},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t}^{L},0,\ldots,0)
and
\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t},0,\ldots,0)
.

Let
U_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}\times r}
and
V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}\times r}
denote the first
r
columns of
U
and
V
, corresponding to the nonzero singular values. We compute the weight matrix products at time
t
:

\displaystyle W_{t}^{L:2}(W_{t}^{L:2})^{\top}

\displaystyle\simeq U_{1}\,\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t}^{2(L-1)},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t}^{2(L-1)})\,U_{1}^{\top}

\displaystyle W_{t}^{1:L-1}(W_{t}^{1:L-1})^{\top}

\displaystyle\simeq V_{1}\,\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t}^{2(L-1)},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t}^{2(L-1)})\,V_{1}^{\top}

And for all
2\leq l\leq L-1
,

\displaystyle W_{t}^{L:l+1}(W_{t}^{L:l+1})^{\top}

\displaystyle\simeq U_{1}\,\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t}^{2(L-l)},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t}^{2(L-l)})\,U_{1}^{\top}

\displaystyle W_{t}^{1:l-1}(W_{t}^{1:l-1})^{\top}

\displaystyle\simeq V_{1}\,\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t}^{2(l-1)},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t}^{2(l-1)})\,V_{1}^{\top}

Computing the Gram matrix
A_{o,t}^{\top}A_{o,t}
:

\displaystyle A_{o,t}^{\top}A_{o,t}

\displaystyle=W_{t}^{L:2}(W_{t}^{L:2})^{\top}\otimes I_{d_{0}}+I_{d_{L}}\otimes W_{t}^{1:L-1}(W_{t}^{1:L-1})^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{l=2}^{L-1}W_{t}^{L:l+1}(W_{t}^{L:l+1})^{\top}\otimes W_{t}^{1:l-1}(W_{t}^{1:l-1})^{\top}

Let
\Lambda_{U}^{(l)}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t}^{2(L-l)},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t}^{2(L-l)})
and
\Lambda_{V}^{(l)}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1,t}^{2(l-1)},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t}^{2(l-1)})
. Substituting:

\displaystyle A_{o,t}^{\top}A_{o,t}

\displaystyle=U_{1}\Lambda_{U}^{(1)}U_{1}^{\top}\otimes I_{d_{0}}+I_{d_{L}}\otimes V_{1}\Lambda_{V}^{(L)}V_{1}^{\top}+\sum_{l=2}^{L-1}U_{1}\Lambda_{U}^{(l)}U_{1}^{\top}\otimes V_{1}\Lambda_{V}^{(l)}V_{1}^{\top}

To analyze the eigenvalues, let
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\}
be the columns of
U_{1}
, extended to an orthonormal basis
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{d_{L}}\}
for
\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
. Similarly, let
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\}
be the columns of
V_{1}
, extended to an orthonormal basis
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d_{0}}\}
for
\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
. The vectors
u_{i}\otimes v_{j}
form an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}d_{0}}
.

Note that
U_{1}\Lambda_{U}^{(l)}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i}=\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-l)}u_{i}
if
i\leq r
and
U_{1}\Lambda_{U}^{(l)}U_{1}^{\top}u_{i}=0
if
i>r
. Similarly for
V_{1}\Lambda_{V}^{(l)}V_{1}^{\top}
. We compute the action of
A_{o,t}^{\top}A_{o,t}
on these basis vectors for each case:

(Dominant space)
If
i\leq r
and
j\leq r
:

\displaystyle A_{o,t}^{\top}A_{o,t}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})+\lambda_{j,t}^{2(L-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})+\sum_{l=2}^{L-1}\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-l)}\lambda_{j,t}^{2(l-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-l)}\lambda_{j,t}^{2(l-1)}\right)(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

Denote the eigenvalue as
\nu_{i,j}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-l)}\lambda_{j,t}^{2(l-1)}

\displaystyle L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}\leq\nu_{i,j}\leq L(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}

This gives eigenvalue multiplicity
r\times r=r^{2}
.

(Bulk space, part 1)
If
i\leq r
and
j>r

\displaystyle A_{o,t}^{\top}A_{o,t}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})+0+0=\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

.

(Bulk space, part 2)
If
i>r
and
j\leq r

\displaystyle A_{o,t}^{\top}A_{o,t}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

\displaystyle=0+\lambda_{j,t}^{2(L-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})+0=\lambda_{j,t}^{2(L-1)}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})

The eigenvalue is
\nu_{i,j}=\lambda_{j,t}^{2(L-1)}\in[(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)},(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}]

(d_{L}-r)\times r=(d_{L}-r)r
.

(Zero space)
If
i>r
and
j>r

\displaystyle A_{o,t}^{\top}A_{o,t}(u_{i}\otimes v_{j})=0

This gives eigenvalue
0
with multiplicity
(d_{L}-r)\times(d_{0}-r)=(d_{L}-r)(d_{0}-r)
.

Combining the two parts of the bulk space, the total multiplicity is:

\displaystyle r(d_{0}-r)+(d_{L}-r)r=(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r

The condition
\frac{m_{t}+\delta_{t}}{m_{t}-\delta_{t}}<L^{\frac{1}{2(L-1)}}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}<L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}

which guarantees a spectral gap between the dominant space and bulk space:

\displaystyle\min_{i,j\leq r}\nu_{i,j}\geq L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}>(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}\geq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq r,j>r\\
\text{or }i>r,j\leq r\end{subarray}}\nu_{i,j}

In summary, the outer product Hessian
H_{o,t}
has:

•

r^{2}
eigenvalues in the dominant space, lying in
[L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)},L(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}]
;

•

(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r
eigenvalues in the bulk space, lying in
[(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)},(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}]
;

•

(d_{L}-r)(d_{0}-r)
zero eigenvalues.

The total number of nonzero eigenvalues is
r^{2}+(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r=(d_{0}+d_{L}-r)r
.
∎

which is a diagonal matrix. Therefore, the update
W^{k}_{t+1}=W^{k}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{W^{k}}L(W^{k}_{t})
only modifies the diagonal entries, preserving the diagonal structure.

For
k=1
, the gradient is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W^{1}}L(W^{1}_{t})=(\Sigma_{t}^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma_{t}^{(L-1)/L})V^{\top}

Thus:

\displaystyle W^{1}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=W^{1}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{W^{1}}L(W^{1}_{t})

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}V^{\top}-\eta(\Sigma_{t}^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma_{t}^{(L-1)/L})V^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left[\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}-\eta(\Sigma_{t}^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma_{t}^{(L-1)/L})\right]V^{\top}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t+1}^{1/L}V^{\top}

where the right factor
V^{\top}
remains unchanged.

For
k=L
, the gradient is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W^{L}}L(W^{L}_{t})=U(\Sigma_{t}^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma_{t}^{(L-1)/L})

Thus:

\displaystyle W^{L}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=W^{L}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{W^{L}}L(W^{L}_{t})

\displaystyle=U\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}-\eta U(\Sigma_{t}^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma_{t}^{(L-1)/L})

\displaystyle=U\left[\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}-\eta(\Sigma_{t}^{(2L-1)/L}-\Sigma_{t}^{(L-1)/L})\right]

\displaystyle=U\Sigma_{t+1}^{1/L}

where the left factor
U
remains unchanged.

In all cases, the updated weight matrix shares the same diagonal structure
\Sigma_{t+1}^{1/L}
, where the
i
-th diagonal entry evolves according to:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}

By Lemma
A.3
, under Assumption
3.1
, only the first
r
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1,t},\ldots,\lambda_{r,t}
are effectively updated since
\Sigma_{xx}\simeq I_{r}
. The remaining eigenvalues
\lambda_{r+1,t},\ldots,\lambda_{d_{*},t}
correspond to directions orthogonal to the support of
\Sigma_{xx}
, and thus their gradients are zero:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}=\lambda_{i,0},\quad\text{for }i=r+1,\ldots,d_{*}

By induction on
t
, all weight matrices
W^{k}_{t}
share the same spectral structure
\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}
at any time
t\geq 0
, which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
.

3.5
(Hessian Bifurcation)
Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
, and assuming
r\leq d_{*}=\min\{d_{0},d_{L}\}
, consider a depth-
L
deep linear neural network trained with gradient descent with step size
\eta<\min\big\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{2}{(2L-1)M^{2L-2}}\big\}
. Let
\lambda_{i,t}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
denote the effective eigenvalues of the weight matrix
\Sigma_{t}^{1/L}
at time
t
. Suppose that
\lambda_{i,t}\in[m_{t}-\delta_{t},m_{t}+\delta_{t}]
and the condition
(m_{t}+\delta_{t})/(m_{t}-\delta_{t})<L^{\frac{1}{2(L-1)}}
holds. Then the Hessian
H_{L,t}
exhibits a three-part spectral structure:

1.

(Dominant Space)
There exist
r^{2}
eigenvalues of
H_{L,t}
lying in:

\begin{split}\bigg[&L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}-O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t}),\\
&L(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})\bigg]\end{split}

(38)

2.

(Bulk Space)
There exist
(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r
eigenvalues of
H_{L,t}
lying in:

\begin{split}\bigg[&(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}-O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t}),\\
&(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})\bigg]\end{split}

(39)

3.

(Zero Space)
The remaining
(d_{L}-r)(d_{0}-r)
eigenvalues of
H_{L,t}
are
O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
, converging to zero as
t\to\infty
.

Moreover, let
\lambda_{\text{dom}}
denote an arbitrary eigenvalue belonging to the Dominant Space and
\lambda_{\text{bulk}}
denote an arbitrary eigenvalue belonging to the Bulk Space. Their ratio satisfies:

\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}=\Theta(L),

(40)

where
\alpha
is a positive constant independent of
t
, provided
t
is sufficiently large.

Proof.

By Lemma
A.9
, under the assumption
r\leq d_{*}
, the outer product Hessian
H_{o,t}
at time
t
has
(d_{0}+d_{L}-r)r
non-zero eigenvalues. These eigenvalues can be indexed by pairs
(i,j)
and exhibit three different behaviors:

Case 1 (Dominant space):
If
i\leq r
and
j\leq r
, the eigenvalue is

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-l)}\lambda_{j,t}^{2(l-1)}

When
i=j
, this equals
L\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-1)}
. When
i\neq j
and
\lambda_{i,t}\neq\lambda_{j,t}
, this equals
\frac{\lambda_{i,t}^{2L}-\lambda_{j,t}^{2L}}{\lambda_{i,t}^{2}-\lambda_{j,t}^{2}}
. When
i\neq j
but
\lambda_{i,t}=\lambda_{j,t}
, this also equals
L\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-1)}
by continuity.

Case 2 (Bulk space, part 1):
If
i\leq r
and
j>r
, the eigenvalue is

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-1)}

Case 3 (Bulk space, part 2):
If
i>r
and
j\leq r
, the eigenvalue is

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=\lambda_{j,t}^{2(L-1)}

Case 4 (Zero space):
If
i>r
and
j>r
, the eigenvalue is zero.

Since
\lambda_{i,t}\in[m_{t}-\delta_{t},m_{t}+\delta_{t}]
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
, we bound the eigenvalues in each case.

For Case 1, since each term
\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-l)}\lambda_{j,t}^{2(l-1)}
satisfies

\displaystyle(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}\leq\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-l)}\lambda_{j,t}^{2(l-1)}\leq(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}

summing over
l=1,\ldots,L
yields

\displaystyle L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}\leq\nu_{i,j}\leq L(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}

This gives
r\times r=r^{2}
eigenvalues in the dominant space.

For Cases 2 and 3:

\displaystyle(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}\leq\nu_{i,j}\leq(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}

Case 2 gives
r\times(d_{0}-r)=r(d_{0}-r)
eigenvalues, and Case 3 gives
(d_{L}-r)\times r=(d_{L}-r)r
eigenvalues. Combining these, the bulk space contains

\displaystyle r(d_{0}-r)+(d_{L}-r)r=(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r

eigenvalues.

For Case 4, there are
(d_{L}-r)\times(d_{0}-r)=(d_{L}-r)(d_{0}-r)
zero eigenvalues.

The condition
(m_{t}+\delta_{t})/(m_{t}-\delta_{t})<L^{\frac{1}{2(L-1)}}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}<L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}

which guarantees a spectral gap between the dominant space and the bulk space:

\displaystyle\min_{i,j\leq r}\nu_{i,j}\geq L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}>(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}\geq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq r,j>r\\
\text{or }i>r,j\leq r\end{subarray}}\nu_{i,j}

By the Gauss-Newton decomposition,
H_{L,t}=H_{o,t}+H_{f,t}
. According to Lemma
A.8
, the functional Hessian satisfies
\|H_{f,t}\|_{2}=O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
. Applying Weyl’s inequality, for the
k
th largest eigenvalue:

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(H_{o,t})-\|H_{f,t}\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{k}(H_{L,t})\leq\lambda_{k}(H_{o,t})+\|H_{f,t}\|_{2}

Therefore, the eigenvalues of the true Hessian
H_{L,t}
lie within
O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
of the eigenvalues of the outer product Hessian
H_{o,t}
. Combining with the bounds above:

•

The
r^{2}
dominant space eigenvalues of
H_{L,t}
lie in

\displaystyle\left[L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}-O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t}),\,L(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})\right]

•

The
(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r
bulk space eigenvalues of
H_{L,t}
lie in

\displaystyle\left[(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}-O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t}),\,(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})\right]

•

The remaining
(d_{L}-r)(d_{0}-r)
eigenvalues are
O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
, converging to zero as
t\to\infty
.

Finally, we establish the ratio between the dominant space eigenvalues and bulk space eigenvalues. For the lower bound:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}\geq\frac{L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}-O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})}{(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})}

For sufficiently large
t
, the
O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
terms become negligible, yielding

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}\geq\frac{L(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}}{(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}}=L\left(\frac{m_{t}-\delta_{t}}{m_{t}+\delta_{t}}\right)^{2(L-1)}

For the upper bound:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}\leq\frac{L(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})}{(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}-O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})}

For sufficiently large
t
:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}\leq\frac{L(m_{t}+\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}}{(m_{t}-\delta_{t})^{2(L-1)}}=L\left(\frac{m_{t}+\delta_{t}}{m_{t}-\delta_{t}}\right)^{2(L-1)}

Since the condition
(m_{t}+\delta_{t})/(m_{t}-\delta_{t})<L^{\frac{1}{2(L-1)}}
implies that
\left(\frac{m_{t}+\delta_{t}}{m_{t}-\delta_{t}}\right)^{2(L-1)}<L
, both bounds are
O(L)
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}=\Theta(L)

which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary
.

3.6
(Hessian Bifurcation with USI)
Under Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
, and
3.3
, and assuming
r\leq d_{*}=\min\{d_{0},d_{L}\}
, the Hessian
H_{L,t}
has exactly two distinct nonzero eigenvalues (up to an exponentially small perturbation). Specifically:

1.

(Dominant Space)
There exist
r^{2}
eigenvalues equal to
L\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
.

2.

(Bulk Space)
There exist
(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r
eigenvalues equal to
\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
.

3.

(Zero Space)
The remaining
(d_{L}-r)(d_{0}-r)
eigenvalues are
O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
.

Moreover, the ratio between the dominant and bulk eigenvalues is exactly
L
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}=L

(41)

where
\mu_{t}
denotes the common value of all effective eigenvalues
\lambda_{i,t}=\mu_{t}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
at time
t
, and
\alpha
is a positive constant.

Proof.

Under Assumption
3.3
,
\Sigma_{0}^{1/L}\simeq\mu I_{r}
, which implies
\lambda_{i,0}=\mu
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
. By Lemma
3.4
, the effective eigenvalues evolve according to:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,t+1}=\lambda_{i,t}-\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{2L-1}+\eta\lambda_{i,t}^{L-1}

for
i=1,\ldots,r
.

Since all initial eigenvalues are equal, and the update rule is identical for each
i=1,\ldots,r
, we have
\lambda_{1,t}=\lambda_{2,t}=\cdots=\lambda_{r,t}=:\mu_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
. By Lemma
A.3
,
\mu_{t}\to 1
as
t\to\infty
.

By Lemma
A.9
, the outer product Hessian
H_{o,t}
has eigenvalues indexed by pairs
(i,j)
. For
i\leq r
and
j\leq r
:

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-l)}\lambda_{j,t}^{2(l-1)}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mu_{t}^{2(L-l)}\mu_{t}^{2(l-1)}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}=L\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}

For
i\leq r
and
j>r
:

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=\lambda_{i,t}^{2(L-1)}=\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}

For
i>r
and
j\leq r
:

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=\lambda_{j,t}^{2(L-1)}=\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}

For
i>r
and
j>r
:

\displaystyle\nu_{i,j}=0

Therefore, the outer product Hessian
H_{o,t}
has exactly two distinct nonzero eigenvalues:

•

L\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}
with multiplicity
r^{2}
(dominant space)

•

\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}
with multiplicity
r(d_{0}-r)+(d_{L}-r)r=(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r
(bulk space)

and zero eigenvalues with multiplicity
(d_{L}-r)(d_{0}-r)
(zero space).

By Lemma
A.8
,
\|H_{f,t}\|_{2}=O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})
. Applying Weyl’s inequality:

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(H_{o,t})-O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})\leq\lambda_{k}(H_{L,t})\leq\lambda_{k}(H_{o,t})+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})

Therefore, the Hessian
H_{L,t}
has:

•

r^{2}
eigenvalues equal to
L\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})

•

(d_{0}+d_{L}-2r)r
eigenvalues equal to
\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})

•

(d_{L}-r)(d_{0}-r)
eigenvalues equal to
O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})

The ratio between the dominant and bulk eigenvalues is:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{\text{dom}}}{\lambda_{\text{bulk}}}=\frac{L\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})}{\mu_{t}^{2(L-1)}+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})}=L+O(e^{-L\alpha\eta t})

For sufficiently large
t
, this ratio converges to exactly
L
, which completes the proof.
∎
