Research note: Colorimetric phenotyping of bruises in broiler carcasses under commercial slaughterhouse conditions
Authors: Aneta Jaszczyk, Patryk Sztandarski, Magdalena Solka, Jarosław O Horbańczuk, Grzegorz Pogorzelski, Adrianna Szprynca, Joanna Składanowska-Baryza, Agnieszka Ludwiczak, Weronika Goździejewska, Adrian Stelmasiak, Krzysztof Damaziak, Jagoba Rey Gotxi, Magdalena Krawczak, Joanna Marchewka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBruising in broiler carcasses is an important quality defect and a potential indicator of pre-slaughter handling conditions. This study evaluated the color properties of broiler carcass bruises using CIE L*a*b* and ΔE* parameters under commercial slaughterhouse conditions.A total of 79 carcass bruises with a diameter ≥1 cm were included in the analysis. These included 48 breast, 16 leg, and 15 wing bruises. Color was measured in the central area of each bruise using a portable colorimeter. Color parameters were compared among anatomical regions, and the optimal number of clusters was assessed using the gap statistic before k-means clustering of Z-score standardized CIE L*a*b* coordinates and ΔE* values. Significant anatomical differences were observed for a* and b*, but not for L* or ΔE*. Leg bruises showed a stronger red-yellow profile, whereas wing bruises shifted toward blue-purple discoloration. The gap statistic supported a three cluster solution, which identified three colorimetric profiles that were partly associated with anatomical location. These findings indicate that broiler carcass bruises are colorimetrically heterogeneous and that quantitative color assessment may support standardized bruise characterization at slaughterhouse.Keywords: Broiler chicken, Bruise, CIE L*a*b*, Colorimetry, Slaughterhouse

Introduction

Bruises are common carcass quality defects observed in broiler chickens. Hence, they are useful indicators of welfare and handling conditions before slaughter. Improper pre-slaughter handling may result not only in bruises but also in wing and leg fractures, joint dislocations, skin tears, hematomas, and meat quality defects, including pale, soft, and exudative (PSE)-like meat, leading to carcass downgrading and economic losses. The occurrence of these defects is influenced by several factors, including the catching method (manual or mechanical), loading and unloading procedures, transport duration and conditions, as well as handling during shackling (Langkabel et al., 2015; Cockram et al., 2018; Mönch et al., 2020). Therefore, the presence of bruises and other catch lesions reflect not only animal welfare concerns but also is a cause of economic losses (Marchewka et al., 2023).

The location and appearance of bruises can provide additional information about the possible origin and timing of injury. Experimental studies have shown that broiler bruise color changes over time and can differ among anatomical regions, including the breast, wings, and legs (Hamdy et al., 1961; Northcutt et al., 2000). However, bruise assessment under commercial conditions is still often based on visual scoring, which can be affected by observer subjectivity, lighting conditions, lesion depth, and differences in tissue structure. Therefore, objective methods that quantify bruise color may improve the repeatability of carcass damage assessment and may support the development of more standardized inspection monitoring tools at slaughter.

The CIE L*a*b* color space provides an objective and quantitative description of color, where L* represents lightness, a* the red-green, and b* the yellow-blue axis (Petracci et al., 2004; AMSA, 2012). This system is particularly relevant for bruise assessment because changes in color reflect hemorrhage and hemoglobin breakdown in tissue (Hamdy et al., 1961; Northcutt et al., 2000; Raederscheidt et al., 2024). Despite the widespread use of instrumental colorimetry for meat quality evaluation, the application of standardized CIE L*a*b* and ΔE* color measurements, particularly in combination with multivariate analytical approaches, to the objective characterization of bruises in broiler carcasses under commercial slaughterhouse conditions has received limited attention.

Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the color properties of bruises in broiler carcasses using CIE L*a*b* and ΔE* parameters. It examined whether bruise color differed among anatomical regions and whether an unsupervised clustering approach could identify distinct colorimetric profiles of carcass damage. It was hypothesized that bruise color would exhibit variation rather than a uniform pattern, reflecting differences in lesion characteristics and anatomical location.

Materials and methods

Animals

The study was conducted using broiler chickens of the Ross 308 genotype reared under conventional production conditions. Broilers were housed at a final stocking density of 39 kg/m². The evaluated flock consisted of approximately 24,000 broilers slaughtered at 42 days of age. Prior to catching feed was withdrawn for 4 h and water for 1 h. Broilers were manually caught under low-intensity blue lighting (approximately 3 lx) by a team of five catchers and placed in plastic transport cages measuring 235 cm × 215 cm × 28 cm, with 73 chickens per cage. The flock was divided into four transportation trucks, each containing approximately 6,000 chickens. Broilers included in the present study originated from a randomly selected transport truck and were manually caught between 03:00 am and 05:00 am at the slaughter day. Transport from the farm to the slaughterhouse lasted approximately 2 h. Upon arrival, the birds remained in lairage for 3 h under low-intensity blue lighting before slaughter. The slaughterhouse used a controlled atmosphere gas stunning (CAS) system, and broilers remained in their transport crates until after stunning, when they were removed for shackling. All catching, loading, transport, lairage, and slaughter procedures were conducted under standard commercial conditions. Ethical approval was not required because the study involved postmortem assessment of carcasses obtained from a commercial slaughterhouse and no experimental procedures were performed on live animals.

Colorimetric measurements

After plucking and evisceration, and prior to chilling, all carcasses originating from a single transport truck (6173 broilers) were carefully examined for the presence of bruises by three trained observers. Carcasses presenting skin rapture, bone fractures, or visible external bleeding were excluded from the analysis. Only carcasses with bruises ≥ 1 cm in diameter were selected for detailed colorimetric evaluation (n = 79). Colorimetric measurements were performed using a portable colorimeter (Kalstein France SAS, model YR05515). Prior to analysis, the device was calibrated using a standard white calibration plate supplied by the manufacturer. Measurements were performed using a 4 mm measurement aperture (Φ4), D65 illuminant, a CIE 10° standard observer, and diffuse/8° (D/8°) measurement geometry. The measuring aperture was positioned over the center of each bruise using the device's built-in positioning system. Two consecutive measurements were obtained from the center of each bruise, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Colorimetric parameters (L*, a*, b*, and ΔE*) were summarized by anatomical region as mean ± SD, median, minimum, and maximum values. ΔE was not measured directly but was automatically calculated by the colorimeter from the measured CIE L*a*b* values. Differences between breast, wing, and leg bruises were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. When a significant overall effect was detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. For cluster analysis and heatmap visualization, CIE L*a*b* coordinates and ΔE* values were Z-score standardized to account for differences in scale and variance among variables. The optimal number of clusters was evaluated using the gap statistic implemented with the clusGap function, with 1,000 bootstrap reference datasets and the firstSEmax selection criterion. The gap statistic supported a three cluster solution. Samples were subsequently assigned to clusters using k-means clustering with 100 random initializations and a fixed random seed to improve the stability and reproducibility of the solution. Cluster specific colorimetric profiles were summarized descriptively as mean ± SD.

Results and discussion

Colorimetric variation of bruises across anatomical regions

The colorimetric characteristics of breast, wing, and leg bruises are presented in Fig. 1 A-D, showing individual observations and mean ± SD values for the CIE L*a*b* coordinates and ΔE* values. Breast bruises were the most prevalent lesions analyzed (n = 48), followed by bruises on the legs (n = 16) and wings (n = 15). Colorimetric analysis revealed variation in L*, a*, b* and ΔE* values among anatomical regions. Mean L* values were relatively similar across regions, ranging from 36.87 in breast bruises to 39.43 in wing bruises, indicating comparable overall lightness of bruised tissues. In contrast, regional differences were observed for a* and b* values. Leg bruises showed the highest mean a* values (16.82 ± 6.33), whereas wing bruises had the lowest mean a* values (9.03 ± 10.43), indicating stronger redness in leg bruises. Mean b* values were also highest in leg bruises (6.11 ± 4.14) and lowest in wing (0.99 ± 6.59), with negative minimum b* values in wing bruises indicating a shift toward blue-purple discoloration. Mean ΔE* values exceeded 55 in all anatomical regions, demonstrating pronounced color deviation from standard plate regardless of lesion location.

Symbols represent individual bruises, horizontal lines indicate means, and error bars represent standard deviations. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among anatomical regions within each color parameter based on Dunn’s pairwise test with Holm adjustment following a Kruskal-Wallis test (P < 0.05). Statistical analysis confirmed significant anatomical differences for a* (P = 0.018; Fig. 1A) and b* (P = 0.041; Fig. 1B), whereas no significant differences were observed for L* (P = 0.401; Fig. 1C) or ΔE* (P = 0.722; Fig. 1D) values. Dunn’s pairwise test with Holm adjustment showed that leg bruises had higher a* values than breast bruises (adjusted P = 0.034) and wing bruises (adjusted P = 0.024). Wing bruises also had lower b* values than leg bruises (adjusted P = 0.034).

These findings support previous experimental observations indicating that bruise appearance in poultry is strongly influenced by anatomical location rather than representing a uniform lesion phenotype (Hamdy et al., 1961; Northcutt et al., 2000). Northcutt et al. (2000) reported that breast, wing, and drum bruises differed in both visual appearance and histological progression during bruise development, suggesting that tissue-specific hemorrhage dynamics contribute to variation in bruise color. Similarly, Hamdy et al. (1961) demonstrated that bruise pigmentation changes are affected by tissue metabolism and hemoglobin degradation processes, resulting in variable progression from red discoloration toward green-yellow pigmentation. The present findings extend these observations by showing that bruises measured under slaughter conditions also exhibit clear anatomical color differences when assessed instrumentally using the CIE L*a*b* system.

K-means clustering and anatomical distribution of bruise color profiles

Objective evaluation of solutions ranging from one to six clusters using the gap statistic supported the selection of three clusters. Subsequent k-means clustering of Z-score-standardized CIE L*a*b* and ΔE* values separated the analyzed bruises into three colorimetric profiles. The anatomical distribution of breast, wing, and leg bruises among these clusters is shown in Fig. 1E.

Cluster 1 included 13 bruises and was characterized by the highest L* values (52.65 ± 7.82) and the lowest ΔE* values (41.16 ± 7.19), representing a light, low-color difference profile. This cluster also showed relatively low a* (3.25 ± 5.93) and b* values (1.52 ± 5.84). Cluster 2 was the dominant profile (n = 49) and was characterized by lower L* values (34.01 ± 5.20), moderate a* values (10.68 ± 2.98), relatively low b* values (2.77 ± 3.05), and higher ΔE* values (60.18 ± 4.98). Cluster 3 included 17 bruises and showed the highest a* (23.19 ± 2.77) and b* values (9.06 ± 2.34), together with a mean L* value of 36.34 ± 6.49 and a mean ΔE* value of 62.77 ± 6.38. This cluster was therefore characterized as a high-redness and high-yellowness colorimetric profile.

Heatmap visualization of the Z-score-standardized color parameters illustrated the distribution of the three k-means clusters across anatomical regions (Fig. 2). Breast bruises were assigned mainly to Cluster 2, whereas leg bruises were more frequently represented in Cluster 3. Wing bruises were distributed across all three clusters, indicating greater heterogeneity in their colorimetric profiles.

Heatmap of standardized colorimetric parameters of broiler carcass bruises by anatomical region. Panels show bruises located on the breast (A), wing (B), and leg (C). Each row represents one bruise sample, and columns represent Z-score standardized CIE L*a*b*coordinates, and ΔE* values. Red indicates values above the mean, whereas blue indicates values below the mean. Within each panel, samples are grouped according to the colorimetric profiles identified by k-means clustering of Z-score standardized CIE L*a*b*coordinates, and ΔE* values.

The predominance of Cluster 2 among breast bruises suggests that breast lesions were characterized mainly by darker intermediate color profiles with substantial color deviation. Breast muscles represent the largest soft tissue of the broiler carcass. Studies have identified the breast as a common site of bruising associated with pre-slaughter handling procedures, including catching, loading or transport (Langkabel et al., 2015). In contrast, leg bruises were more strongly associated with Cluster 3, which showed elevated a* and b* values and represented a red hemorrhagic phenotype. This color pattern may be consistent with more extensive hemorrhage or vascular injury in leg tissues, because catching procedures frequently involve manual inversion and carrying of broilers by the legs, which may expose the legs to direct mechanical injury and vascular congestion (Langkabel et al., 2015; Mönch et al., 2020). Although these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study.

Wing bruises showed the greatest heterogeneity and were distributed across all identified clusters. Furthermore, wing lesions included the lowest and occasionally negative b* values, indicating blue-purple discoloration patterns. This variability may reflect the complex mechanical exposure of wings during pre-slaughter handling: catching, crating or unloading (Cockram et al., 2018; Mönch et al., 2020). Studies consistently identified wings as the anatomical region most frequently affected by bruises and fractures in broilers (Langkabel et al., 2015; Mönch et al., 2020). The broad color variability observed in the present study may therefore indicate that wing bruises originate from multiple trauma mechanisms and possibly different stages of the pre-slaughter process.

Combined cluster distribution and heatmap visualization demonstrated that the analyzed broiler bruises did not form a homogeneous color pattern. Instead, k-means clustering separated them into three distinct colorimetric profiles. This finding is important because commercial bruise assessment in poultry slaughterhouses is still commonly based on subjective visual evaluation. Observer-dependent scoring systems may be affected by lighting conditions, lesion size, observer experience, and differences in color perception (Raederscheidt et al., 2024). Raederscheidt et al. (2024) demonstrated relatively poor inter-observer agreement for bruise color assessment in poultry carcasses, particularly when attempting to classify lesion color categories associated with bruise age. Similarly, the validation study of automated lesion detection systems by van Staaveren et al. (2026) showed that even trained human observers achieved only fair-to-moderate agreement when evaluating poultry skin lesions, whereas automated systems produced more standardized measurements. The present results support the need for more objective approaches to bruise characterization and suggest that CIE L*a*b* color assessment may improve repeatability and standardization of carcass inspection.

Beyond improving the standardization of bruise assessment, colorimetry could support the identification of injury origin within the poultry production chain. The present findings demonstrate that the analyzed bruises can be separated into distinct statistical profiles based on their colorimetric characteristics. Nevertheless, colorimetric evaluation alone is unlikely to provide sufficient biological resolution for definitive bruise origin. Bruise appearance is influenced by multiple factors, including anatomical location, tissue vascularization, hemorrhage intensity, carcass temperature, and postmortem biochemical changes. Therefore, future development of objective bruise classification systems should integrate colorimetric assessment with complementary biological validation methods, including histopathology, hemoglobin degradation profiling, immunohistochemical markers, or molecular indicators of tissue response. Such combined approaches could improve the reliability of differentiating bruises associated with catching procedures from lesions potentially arising during slaughterhouse operations and may contribute to more evidence-based welfare monitoring systems in commercial poultry production.
