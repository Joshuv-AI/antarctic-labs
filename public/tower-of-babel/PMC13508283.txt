Authentic leadership, sustainable employee well-being and retention: a multi-method study
Authors: Mirriam Ogunja, Zhao Shurong, Luo Ailing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe contemporary workplace, characterized by volatility, remote work arrangements, and demand for psychological safety, has intensified the search for leadership styles that promote sustainable employee wellbeing and retention. While authentic leadership (AL) is theorised to be a potent antidote to burnout and turnover, the specific pathways through which its components operate remain underexplored. This research examines how and when the four dimensions of AL (self-awareness, relational transparency, balanced processing, internalised moral perspective) differentially influence multi-faceted employee wellbeing and turnover intention.MethodsDrawing on Conservation of Resources theory and Social Exchange theory, we conducted a three-wave longitudinal dyadic survey (N = 312 employee-leader pairs) and a randomized field experiment with a wait-list control group (N = 60 leaders, 280 employees) in a manufacturing firm in Kenya. The intervention was a six-week targeted leadership development programme focused on the specific behaviours associated with each AL dimension.ResultsStudy 1 (longitudinal survey) showed that relational transparency indirectly predicted thriving and engagement via psychological safety and trust; balanced processing indirectly predicted reduced emotional exhaustion via procedural justice; and internalised moral perspective indirectly predicted engagement via meaningfulness and perceived organizational support. Wellbeing outcomes fully mediated the AL-turnover intention relationship. Study 2 (field experiment) produced significant improvements in all mediators and wellbeing outcomes, reducing turnover intention scores by 32% at three-month follow-up. Mediation of change scores confirmed that the intervention worked through the theorized mechanisms. While our three-wave design establishes temporal precedence for parallel mediation, inferences about sequential (serial) mediation remain tentative.ConclusionsAuthentic leadership’s power lies not in a single composite construct but in the provision of distinct psychological resources through specific behavioural dimensions. Organizations should train leaders in transparency, balanced processing, and moral perspective as separate, learnable skills to foster sustainable wellbeing and retention.Keywords: Authentic leadership, Employee wellbeing, Turnover intention, Burnout, Industrial and organizational psychology

Introduction

The dual crises of employee burnout termed the great exhaustion and voluntary turnover known as the great resignation present a formidable challenge for organizations globally. Recent Gallup data indicates that employee burnout accounts for approximately 50% of voluntary turnover, with global stress levels reaching record highs [1]. In response, both scholars and practitioners have turned their focus to leadership paradigms capable of fostering resilient, engaged, and loyal workforces. Among these, authentic leadership (AL) has emerged as a particularly compelling framework, positing that leaders who display self‑awareness, transparency, balanced objectivity, and a strong internal moral compass can create healthier organizational climates [2, 3]. Unlike transformational, servant, ethical, or leader‑member exchange (LMX) leadership, AL uniquely specifies four behavioural dimensions self‑awareness, relational transparency, balanced processing, and internalized moral perspective each mapping onto distinct psychological resource mechanisms.

However, a significant gap persists between the broad promise of AL and actionable guidance for leadership development. Prior meta‑analyses confirm a positive correlation between AL and desirable outcomes like job satisfaction, engagement, and performance [4, 5]. Yet the precise mechanisms and contingent conditions through which AL operates remain poorly specified. Is it a leader’s transparency that matters most for employee psychological safety, or their ethical consistency that drives meaningfulness? Do these components affect an employee’s burnout and their sense of thriving through the same psychological pathways, or do they operate through distinct mechanisms? These questions remain largely unanswered in the extant literature.

This research directly addresses this gap by proposing and testing a disaggregated, process‑based model of authentic leadership. We argue that the four dimensions of AL are not interchangeable but serve as unique conduits for different psychological resources, as explained by Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [6]. Furthermore, we integrate Social Exchange theory [7] to explain how these resources, once provided, foster high‑quality exchange relationships that reduce withdrawal intentions and enhance retention [8].

Theoretical framework and hypotheses development

Authentic leadership: a disaggregated view

Authentic leadership (AL) comprises four dimensions: self‑awareness (understanding one’s strengths and impact), relational transparency (open sharing and admitting mistakes), balanced processing (soliciting dissenting views), and internalized moral perspective (acting on internal values even under pressure [3]. While these dimensions are theoretically distinct, most empirical research has aggregated them into a single composite score [2, 9] assuming functional equivalence. We challenge this assumption, proposing instead that each dimension serves as a unique resource‑providing mechanism affecting employee wellbeing through distinct psychological pathways.

Distinguishing AL from other leadership styles

Before presenting our model, we clarify why AL is our focal construct. Meta-analyses show that transformational, servant, ethical, and leader-member exchange (LMX) leadership share moderate to high correlations with AL [4, 5]. However, AL offers unique theoretical elements suited to our resource-based mechanisms. Transformational leadership centres on vision and inspiration [10]; servant leadership emphasizes putting followers first [11]; ethical leadership focuses on normative conduct [12]; and LMX describes relationship quality without specifying which leader behaviours generate it [8]. In contrast, AL explicitly specifies four behavioral dimensions that map directly onto distinct psychological resources: transparency provides safety and trust; balanced processing provides procedural justice; moral perspective provides meaningfulness and organisational support. No other leadership style offers this precise set of behavioural components tied to specific resource-provision mechanisms.

Conservation of resources (COR) theory as an integrative lens

COR theory [6] posits that individuals strive to obtain, retain, and protect valued resources. Psychological stress occurs when resources are threatened or lost. We conceptualize authentic leaders as creating a resource caravan a set of correlated psychological resources (e.g., safety, trust, fairness, meaning) flowing from leaders to employees. Each AL dimension is expected to provide distinct psychological resources that either protect against resource loss (reducing burnout) or enable resource gains (fostering thriving and engagement). Specifically, relational transparency may provide psychological safety [13] and trust [14]; balanced processing may provide procedural justice [15, 16]; and internalized moral perspective may provide meaningfulness [17] and perceived organizational support [18]. An alternative perspective is the Job Demands‑Resources model [19], but COR offers a more parsimonious explanation for how distinct leader behaviours provide distinct resources.

Social exchange theory and turnover intention

Social exchange theory [7, 20] complements COR by explaining how resource provision translates into reduced withdrawal intentions. When leaders provide valued resources (safety, trust, fairness, meaning), employees may feel obliged to reciprocate through positive attitudes and behaviours. High-quality social exchange relationships are characterized by mutual trust, loyalty, and commitment conditions that may counteract turnover intentions [20].

Self-awareness as a foundational meta-capacity

Self-awareness the leader’s understanding of their strengths, weaknesses, and impact on others does not directly provide psychological resources to employees. Instead, it functions as a meta-capacity enabling the effective enactment of transparency, balanced processing, and moral perspective. From a COR perspective, self-awareness can be viewed as a meta-resource that enables effective deployment of other resources [6]. Without it, leaders may misapply transparency, dismiss dissenting views, or communicate values rigidly. Thus, we model self-awareness as a predictor of the other three dimensions, not as a direct predictor of mediators or outcomes but a theory-driven prediction we test in Study 1.

Hypothesized model and hypothesis development

Based on the integrated theoretical framework, we present our hypothesized disaggregated mediation model (see Fig. 1). The model specifies that: (a) each AL dimension has its strongest indirect effect on wellbeing through theoretically matched mediators; (b) wellbeing outcomes (engagement, thriving, emotional exhaustion) serve as proximal predictors of turnover intention; and (c) organizational context (crisis/uncertainty) moderates the strength of the first-stage paths from AL dimensions to mediators.

Hypothesized model

Hypotheses development

Hypothesis 1: relational transparency → psychological safety and trust

Relational transparency involves honest communication and the minimization of deceptive or manipulative presentations. When leaders share their thoughts and feelings openly including appropriate vulnerability about challenges they signal that honesty is valued and that interpersonal risk-taking is safe. According to COR theory, this may provide employees with the social resource of psychological safety [13]. Employees observe that authenticity is modeled and rewarded, which may reduce the need for defensive self-protection, conserving cognitive and emotional energy. Additionally, transparent communication may build trust in the leader by reducing uncertainty about the leader’s motives and intentions [14, 21]. Trust signals that the exchange relationship is of high quality (SET). Psychological safety and trust, in turn, may enable employees to focus resources on their work rather than on self-protection, fostering thriving (resource gain) and engagement (sustained energetic investment). Thus:

H1: Relational transparency is expected to have its strongest indirect effects on thriving and engagement via the parallel mediators of (a) psychological safety and (b) trust in the leader.

H1: Relational transparency is expected to have its strongest indirect effects on thriving and engagement via the parallel mediators of (a) psychological safety and (b) trust in the leader.

Hypothesis 2: balanced processing → procedural justice → emotional exhaustion

Balanced processing involves soliciting viewpoints that challenge one’s own position and objectively considering all relevant information before deciding. This behavioral pattern directly communicates that the leader values fairness and that all voices matter. Perceptions of procedural justice the fairness of decision-making processes may be particularly sensitive to balanced processing because employees directly observe whether their input is genuinely considered [15, 16]. According to COR theory, fair procedures may reduce uncertainty and anxiety about arbitrary treatment, thereby preventing resource loss specifically, emotional exhaustion, the core component of burnout. When employees perceive high procedural justice, they may conserve emotional resources that would otherwise be depleted by vigilance and worry. Procedural justice also signals that the leader values employees’ input (SET), but the primary mechanism is resource conservation. Hence:

H2: Balanced processing is expected to have its strongest indirect effect on reducing emotional exhaustion via the mediation of procedural justice.

H2: Balanced processing is expected to have its strongest indirect effect on reducing emotional exhaustion via the mediation of procedural justice.

Hypothesis 3: internalized moral perspective → meaningfulness and pos → engagement

Internalized moral perspective refers to leadership driven by internal moral standards rather than external pressures, demonstrated through consistency between espoused values and actions. This behavioral integrity [22] may enable employees to derive meaning from their work because they see that their leader and organization stand for something beyond mere profit. When leaders make decisions based on moral principles even when costly they signal that work has purpose beyond instrumental outcomes. This may provide employees with existential resources: meaningfulness at work [17] and perceived organizational support (POS [18]. Employees may feel valued as moral beings rather than merely as instruments. Both resources may fuel work engagement a positive, fulfilling state characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption [23]. COR theory frames this as resource gain (engagement as a positive spiral). SET adds that employees may reciprocate high-quality treatment with dedication. Thus:

H3: Internalized moral perspective is expected to have its strongest indirect effects on work engagement via the parallel mediators of (a) meaningfulness at work and (b) perceived organizational support.

H3: Internalized moral perspective is expected to have its strongest indirect effects on work engagement via the parallel mediators of (a) meaningfulness at work and (b) perceived organizational support.

Hypothesis 4: well-being outcomes as mediators of the AL - turnover intention link

The positive wellbeing outcomes (thriving, engagement) represent states of resource abundance and psychological flourishing. According to social exchange principles, employees experiencing such positive states may be likely to reciprocate through continued organizational membership and reduced withdrawal cognitions. Conversely, burnout (emotional exhaustion) represents resource depletion and may motivate employees to withdraw from the source of depletion. We predict that wellbeing outcomes will fully mediate the relationship between AL mechanisms and turnover intention i.e., AL may not directly convince employees to stay, but rather may improve wellbeing, which in turn may reduce withdrawal. This is consistent with theoretical arguments that leadership affects retention primarily through its impact on employees’ psychological experiences at work.

H4: (a) Thriving and engagement will be negatively related to turnover intention, while emotional exhaustion will be positively related to turnover intention. (b) These three wellbeing facets will fully mediate the relationships between AL dimensions (via their respective mediators) and turnover intention.

H4: (a) Thriving and engagement will be negatively related to turnover intention, while emotional exhaustion will be positively related to turnover intention. (b) These three wellbeing facets will fully mediate the relationships between AL dimensions (via their respective mediators) and turnover intention.

Hypothesis 5: moderating role of crisis context

The resource provision function of authentic leadership may be particularly salient during periods of organizational turbulence. When employees face uncertainty, ambiguity, or threat, their need for psychological resources may intensify [6]. Leaders who provide safety through transparency, fairness through balance, and meaning through moral consistency may have amplified effects because they are addressing heightened needs. We also draw on sensemaking theory [24]: in uncertain times, employees may look to leaders for moral guidance, so the moral perspective → meaningfulness pathway may be particularly amplified. Conversely, in stable, predictable environments, these resources may be less salient, and baseline levels of wellbeing may be maintained through other mechanisms.

H5: The strength of the first stage paths (AL dimension → mediator) may be moderated by perceived crisis context, such that the relationships are stronger when crisis is high. Specifically, (a) transparency → psychological safety/trust, (b) balanced processing → procedural justice, and (c) moral perspective → meaningfulness/POS may all be stronger under high crisis.

H5: The strength of the first stage paths (AL dimension → mediator) may be moderated by perceived crisis context, such that the relationships are stronger when crisis is high. Specifically, (a) transparency → psychological safety/trust, (b) balanced processing → procedural justice, and (c) moral perspective → meaningfulness/POS may all be stronger under high crisis.

Method

Study 1: longitudinal quantitative survey

Purpose

The purpose of Study 1 was to quantitatively test the hypothesized disaggregated mediation model using a rigorous longitudinal design with matched employee-leader dyads. Specific objectives included: (a) establishing the discriminant validity of the four AL dimensions and proposed mediators, (b) testing the differentiated indirect effects specified in H1–H3, (c) examining whether wellbeing outcomes fully mediate the AL-turnover intention relationship (H4), and (d) testing whether organizational crisis moderates the first-stage paths (H5). The three-wave design with eight-week intervals allowed us to establish temporal precedence and reduce common method bias concerns [25].

Method

Sample and procedure

Participants were recruited from 14 mid-sized companies (ranging from 200 to 1,500 employees) in Nairobi, Kenya, representing technology (n = 4), healthcare (n = 3), financial services (n = 3), and professional services (n = 4) sectors. Organizations were offered aggregated benchmark reports in exchange for participation. Within each organization, the research team worked with HR leadership to identify work teams with designated leaders and at least four members per team to enable multi-level modeling (though the current study focuses on individual-level relationships, the nested structure was accounted for in analyses).

Data were collected at three time points, each separated by eight weeks. This interval was chosen to allow sufficient time for leader behaviors to influence employee psychological states and for those states to affect wellbeing and turnover intentions, while minimizing memory effects and attrition.

Time 1 (T1): Employees rated their leader's authentic leadership using the 16 item Authentic Leadership Inventory (ALI; Walumbwa et al., 2008) and provided demographic information. Leaders completed a measure of perceived organizational crisis (adapted from James & Wooten, 2005) to provide context ratings. A total of 418 employees (from 85 leaders) completed T1 surveys (response rate = 73% of invited employees).

Time 1 (T1): Employees rated their leader's authentic leadership using the 16 item Authentic Leadership Inventory (ALI; Walumbwa et al., 2008) and provided demographic information. Leaders completed a measure of perceived organizational crisis (adapted from James & Wooten, 2005) to provide context ratings. A total of 418 employees (from 85 leaders) completed T1 surveys (response rate = 73% of invited employees).

Time 2 (T2): Eight weeks later, employees reported on the proposed mediators: psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999), trust in leader (McAllister, 1995), procedural justice (Colquitt, 2001), meaningfulness at work (Spreitzer, 1995), and perceived organizational support (Eisenberger et al., 1986). Of T1 respondents, 356 employees (85%) completed T2 measures.

Time 2 (T2): Eight weeks later, employees reported on the proposed mediators: psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999), trust in leader (McAllister, 1995), procedural justice (Colquitt, 2001), meaningfulness at work (Spreitzer, 1995), and perceived organizational support (Eisenberger et al., 1986). Of T1 respondents, 356 employees (85%) completed T2 measures.

Time 3 (T3): Eight weeks after T2, employees reported on wellbeing outcomes: thriving at work (Porath et al., 2012), work engagement (Utrecht Work Engagement Scale 9; Schaufeli & Bakker, 2025), emotional exhaustion (Copenhagen Burnout Inventory; Kristensen et al., 2005), and turnover intention (Colarelli, 1984). Of T2 respondents, 329 employees (92%) completed T3 measures.

Time 3 (T3): Eight weeks after T2, employees reported on wellbeing outcomes: thriving at work (Porath et al., 2012), work engagement (Utrecht Work Engagement Scale 9; Schaufeli & Bakker, 2025), emotional exhaustion (Copenhagen Burnout Inventory; Kristensen et al., 2005), and turnover intention (Colarelli, 1984). Of T2 respondents, 329 employees (92%) completed T3 measures.

After matching across waves and removing cases with excessive missing data, the final analytic sample consisted of 312 employees nested within 78 leaders (mean team size = 4.0 employees, range 3–6). Employee demographics: 53% female, 47% male; mean age = 39.2 years (SD = 10.1); mean organizational tenure = 6.4 years (SD = 4.7); 84% held at least a bachelor’s degree. Leader demographics: 42% female, 58% male; mean age = 45.3 years (SD = 8.2); mean tenure as leader = 7.8 years (SD = 5.3).

Linking of longitudinal responses

To match employee responses across the three waves, we used unique employee identification codes generated by the human resources department of each participating organization. Employees were asked to enter this code at each survey wave. The research team then merged the three datasets using these codes. Only complete cases with valid codes at all three waves were retained for analysis (final N = 312). The matching procedure was conducted independently of the research team’s substantive analyses to prevent bias.

Control variables

In preliminary analyses, we included several control variables measured at T1: employee gender (0 = female, 1 = male), employee age (years), employee organizational tenure (years), employee managerial experience (0 = no prior supervisory role, 1 = prior supervisory role), leader gender, and organization size (log-transformed number of employees). None of these variables significantly altered the pattern or significance of the hypothesized paths. Following [26] recommendation, we excluded them from the final models for parsimony. The results of sensitivity analyses are available from the corresponding author.

Common method bias assessment

Although our three-wave design separates predictor, mediator, and outcome measurement in time, all variables were reported by employees, raising potential common method variance concerns (Podsakoff & Podsakoff, 2019). We conducted two post-hoc tests. First, Harman’s single-factor test on all T1, T2, and T3 items revealed that a single factor explained 28.4% of the total variance well below the 50% threshold. Second, we employed a marker variable (social desirability, measured with three items from [27] that was theoretically unrelated to our constructs. Adding this marker to the structural model did not change the significance or magnitude of any path (all changes in coefficients < 0.02). These results suggest that common method bias does not substantively affect our conclusions.

Measures

All scales used in this study have been previously published and validated. References to the original scale development papers are provided for each measure below. Unless otherwise noted, items were rated on 7-point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree).

Authentic leadership (T1, employee rated)

The 16-item Authentic Leadership Inventory (ALI) measured the four dimensions with four items each [3]. Sample items include: “My leader describes accurately the way that others view his/her abilities” (self-awareness); “My leader is willing to admit mistakes when they occur” (relational transparency); “My leader carefully listens to alternative perspectives before reaching a conclusion” (balanced processing); “My leader makes decisions based on his/her core values” (internalized moral perspective). Cronbach’s alphas: self-awareness = 0.86, transparency = 0.88, balanced processing = 0.84, moral perspective = 0.90.

Psychological safety (T2)

Edmondson’s 7-item team psychological safety scale was adapted to refer to the work team [13]. Sample item: “Members of my team are able to bring up problems and tough issues.” Alpha = 0.87.

Trust in leader (T2)

McAllister’s 6-item affect-based trust scale was adapted [28]. Sample item: “I can talk freely to my leader about difficulties I am having at work and know that (s)he will want to listen.” Alpha = 0.91.

Procedural justice (T2)

Colquitt’s 7-item scale measured procedural justice perceptions regarding leader decision-making [15]. Sample item: “My leader’s decision-making procedures are applied consistently.” Alpha = 0.89.

Meaningfulness at work (T2)

Spreitzer’s 3-item meaning subscale from the Psychological Empowerment scale was used [29]. Sample item: “The work I do is meaningful to me.” Alpha = 0.92.

Perceived organizational support (T2)

Eisenberger et al.‘s 8-item short form measured POS [18]. Sample item: “My organization really cares about my well-being.” Alpha = 0.90.

Thriving at work (T3)

Porath et al.‘s 10-item scale measured thriving, comprising learning (5 items) and vitality (5 items) subscales [30]. Sample items: “I find myself learning often” (learning); “I feel alive and vital” (vitality). Alpha = 0.91.

Work engagement (T3)

The 9-item Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-9) measured vigor, dedication, and absorption [23]. Sample item: “I am enthusiastic about my job.” Alpha = 0.93.

Emotional exhaustion (T3)

The 7-item work-related burnout subscale of the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) was adapted [31]. Sample item: “Do you feel worn out at the end of the working day?” Alpha = 0.91.

Turnover intention (T3)

Colarelli’s 3-item scale measured intention to leave the organization [32]. Sample item: “I frequently think of quitting my job.” Alpha = 0.88.

Crisis context (T1, leader rated)

Leaders rated the extent to which their unit was experiencing crisis or uncertainty using a 4-item measure adapted from James and Wooten [33]. Sample item: “Our unit is currently facing significant uncertainty or threat.” Alpha = 0.83. Leader ratings were aggregated to the team level (rwg = 0.78, ICC1 = 0.18, ICC2 = 0.62, justifying aggregation; median rwg values met conventional thresholds for within-team agreement).

Analytical strategy

Analyses proceeded in four stages using Mplus 8.8 [34]. First, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) tested the measurement model, establishing discriminant validity among AL dimensions, mediators, and outcomes. Second, due to the nested data structure (employees within leaders), we estimated null models to assess the proportion of variance at the team level and employed cluster-robust standard errors in all subsequent analyses [35]. Third, structural equation modeling (SEM) tested the hypothesized mediation model using maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR). Indirect effects were assessed using bias-corrected bootstrapped confidence intervals [36]. Fourth, moderated mediation was tested using latent interaction modeling with numerical integration (Mplus XWITH command) to assess crisis context as a moderator of first-stage paths.

Missing data were minimal (< 5% across waves) and handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) under the missing-at-random assumption [37].

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) tested the distinctiveness of the four AL dimensions. The hypothesized four-factor model (self-awareness, transparency, balanced processing, moral perspective) fit the data well: χ²(98) = 187.34, CFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.054, SRMR = 0.041. This model fit significantly better than a one-factor model combining all AL items: χ²(104) = 486.21, CFI = 0.78, TLI = 0.74, RMSEA = 0.109, SRMR = 0.081; Δχ²(6) = 298.87, p < .001. It also fit better than alternative two-factor and three-factor combinations (all Δχ² significant at p < .001). These results support treating the four AL dimensions as distinct but related constructs.

A full measurement model including all study variables (13 factors) also demonstrated acceptable fit: χ²(1,247) = 2,134.56, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.048, SRMR = 0.052. All factor loadings were significant and exceeded 0.60, supporting measurement quality.

Test of hypothesized mediation model

We first estimated a null model for turnover intention to assess between-leader variance. ICC1 was 0.21, indicating that 21% of variance in turnover intention resided at the team level, justifying the use of cluster-robust standard errors in all subsequent analyses.

The hypothesized structural model specified: (a) transparency predicting psychological safety and trust; (b) balanced processing predicting procedural justice; (c) moral perspective predicting meaningfulness and POS; (d) self-awareness predicting all three other AL dimensions (modeled as a second-order factor enabling the other dimensions); (e) all mediators predicting thriving, engagement, and emotional exhaustion; and (f) the three wellbeing outcomes predicting turnover intention. All AL dimensions were allowed to correlate, as were mediators and outcomes within their respective measurement waves. The model demonstrated good fit: χ²(1,523) = 2,678.45, CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.049, SRMR = 0.058. Standardized path coefficients for key hypothesized relationships are presented in Fig. 2.

Hypothesis 2 (H2)

predicted that balanced processing would have its strongest indirect effect on reducing emotional exhaustion via procedural justice. Balanced processing strongly predicted procedural justice (β = 0.51, p < .001), which in turn negatively predicted emotional exhaustion (β = − 0.38, p < .001). The indirect effect was significant (indirect effect = − 0.22, 95% CI [-0.30, − 0.14]). Notably, balanced processing did not have significant direct effects on other mediators (e.g., psychological safety, meaningfulness) when included in alternative models, supporting specificity. H2 was supported.

Hypothesis 3 (H3)

predicted that internalized moral perspective would have its strongest indirect effects on engagement via meaningfulness and POS. Moral perspective significantly predicted both meaningfulness (β = 0.44, p < .001) and POS (β = 0.42, p < .001). Meaningfulness strongly predicted engagement (β = 0.47, p < .001), and POS predicted engagement (β = 0.29, p < .001). Both indirect effects were significant (see Table 2). H3 was supported.

Hypothesis 4a (H4a)

predicted that positive wellbeing outcomes would negatively predict turnover intention, while emotional exhaustion would positively predict turnover intention. Engagement (β = − 0.40, p < .001) and thriving (β = − 0.22, p < .01) significantly negatively predicted turnover intention, while emotional exhaustion positively predicted turnover intention (β = 0.35, p < .001). Together, these three outcomes explained 48% of the variance in turnover intention (R² = 0.48). H4a was supported.

Hypothesis 4b (H4b)

predicted that wellbeing outcomes would fully mediate the relationships between AL mechanisms and turnover intention. We tested this by comparing our hypothesized model (with paths from mediators to wellbeing to turnover intention) to an alternative model that added direct paths from each AL dimension to turnover intention. The addition of direct paths did not significantly improve model fit (Δχ²(4) = 6.23, p = .18), and none of the direct paths were significant (all |β| < 0.08, p > .10). The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was lower for the more parsimonious hypothesized model (AIC = 48,234 vs. 48,241). These results support full mediation. H4b was supported.

predicted that crisis context would moderate the first-stage paths. We tested latent interactions between each AL dimension and crisis context (aggregated leader ratings) in predicting their respective mediators. Significant interactions emerged for one pathway: Moral perspective × crisis predicting meaningfulness (β_interaction = 0.15, p < .05). Simple slopes analysis (Table 3) revealed that the positive relationship between moral perspective and meaningfulness was stronger when crisis was high (+ 1 SD; β = 0.52, p < .001) than when crisis was low (− 1 SD; β = 0.31, p < .001). Transparency × crisis predicting psychological safety approached significance (β_interaction = 0.10, p = .06), with a stronger relationship under high crisis. Balanced processing × crisis predicting procedural justice was not significant (β_interaction = 0.04, p = .48). Comparative strength of hypothesised pathways

Although all mediators were allowed to predict all well-being outcomes (see Fig. 2), the hypothesised paths were consistently the strongest. For transparency, the indirect effects via psychological safety (β = 0.13–0.16) and trust (β = 0.11–0.12) were substantially larger than its indirect effects via other mediators (all β < 0.05, non-significant). Similarly, balanced processing had no significant indirect effects on thriving or engagement via psychological safety or trust (all p > .10), confirming that procedural justice is its primary mechanism. Moral perspective’s indirect effects via meaningfulness (β = 0.21) and POS (β = 0.12) were stronger than its indirect effects via trust or procedural justice (β < 0.03, non-significant). These results support the specificity of our disaggregated model.

Supplementary analyses

We conducted several supplementary analyses to probe robustness. First, we tested alternative models reversing the order of mediators and outcomes (e.g., wellbeing predicting mediators); these models fit significantly worse (all CFI < 0.85), supporting the temporal ordering specified in our model. Second, we examined whether self‑awareness had direct effects on mediators beyond its enabling role; when added to the model, paths from self‑awareness to mediators were non‑significant (all p > .10) when other AL dimensions were included, consistent with its foundational role. Third, we tested for gender and industry differences; no systematic moderating effects were observed. Fourth, we tested alternative models in which transparency predicted procedural justice directly and moral perspective predicted trust directly; in both cases, these alternative paths were non‑significant (p > .10), further supporting the specificity of our hypothesised pathways.

Study 1 Discussion

Study 1 provided robust longitudinal support for the disaggregated mediation model. Each AL dimension predicted its theoretically matched mediators, which in turn predicted wellbeing outcomes and, ultimately, turnover intention. The model explained nearly half the variance in turnover intention, a substantial effect size in organisational research. The full mediation finding suggests that AL affects retention primarily through its impact on employees’ psychological experiences at work, rather than through direct effects on withdrawal cognitions. Partial support for crisis moderation suggests that leader moral perspective matters most when employees face uncertainty a finding with practical implications for crisis leadership.

Despite these strengths, Study 1’s observational design limits causal inference. While temporal precedence was established, unmeasured third variables could account for observed relationships. Study 2 addresses this limitation through an experimental intervention.

Study 2: intervention field experiment

Purpose

Study 2 aimed to establish causal evidence for the model by testing whether a targeted leadership development intervention, designed based on the specific mechanisms identified in Study 1, would produce measurable improvements in leader behavior, employee wellbeing, and turnover intention. A randomised field experiment with a wait-list control group provides the strongest test of causality in organisational research [39, 40]. Additionally, Study 2 assessed the durability of effects over a three-month follow-up and examined whether changes in leader behaviours mediated changes in employee outcomes a test of the intervention’s active ingredients.

Method

Design and participants

The study employed a pre‑test/post‑test control group design with random assignment at the business unit level within a single large organisation. This design controls for history, maturation, and testing effects while enabling causal inference about intervention effects [41].

Organization context. The study was conducted in a manufacturing company with approximately 10,000 employees in Nairobi, Kenya. The organization had recently experienced increased turnover (15% annualized) and declining employee engagement scores, and leadership was receptive to interventions targeting retention. The study was conducted between January and September 2025.

Participants recruitment and randomization. The organization identified 65 business units (each with a designated leader and 4‑8 members) as eligible based on: (a) leader tenure > 1 year, (b) unit size between 4‑8 members, and (c) no concurrent leadership development initiatives. Five units declined participation due to scheduling constraints, leaving 60 units. Units were randomly assigned to intervention (n = 30 units, 30 leaders, 142 employees) or wait‑list control (n = 30 units, 30 leaders, 138 employees) using a random number generator. Randomisation checks confirmed no significant differences between conditions on unit size (M_intervention = 4.73, M_control = 4.60; t(58) = 0.42, p = .68), leader tenure (M_intervention = 6.2 years, M_control = 5.9 years; t(58) = 0.51, p = .61), or employee demographics (all p > .20).

Participant characteristics. Leaders: 43% female, 57% male; mean age = 44.8 years (SD = 7.9); mean tenure as leader = 6.1 years (SD = 4.8). Employees: 51% female, 49% male; mean age = 37.6 years (SD = 9.8); mean organisational tenure = 5.3 years (SD = 4.1); 78% held at least a bachelor’s degree.

Intervention: precision authentic leadership program

The intervention was a 6‑week program designed based on the mechanism findings from Study 1. Rather than generic “be more authentic” training, the program targeted specific behavioural skills linked to each AL dimension, with emphasis on the mediators identified in our model. The program was delivered by two certified executive coaches with expertise in leadership development, who were blind to the study hypotheses beyond the mechanistic focus. Sessions were 2.5 h each, delivered virtually via Zoom to accommodate leaders’ schedules.

Module 1 (Week 1): Self‑awareness as foundation. Focus: Understanding one's leadership impact, blind spots, and authentic leadership profile. Activities: 360‑degree feedback debrief (pre‑work completed before Module 1); personal values clarification exercise; impact awareness training. Take‑home practice: Daily reflection journal on leadership interactions.

Authentic leadership measurement in study 2

Although our theoretical model emphasises dimension-specific effects, the primary purpose of Study 2 was to test the overall causal impact of the training program on employee well-being and turnover intention. Therefore, we used the composite AL score (average of all 16 ALI items) as the manipulation check. This composite approach is consistent with prior intervention studies [25] and provides a parsimonious test of program effectiveness.

However, to ensure that the intervention improved each dimension as intended, we also examined dimension-level changes in supplementary analyses (available from the corresponding author). These analyses revealed significant improvements on all four dimensions, with the largest effects on transparency (Δ = +0.68, p < .001) and balanced processing (Δ = +0.59, p < .001) – mirroring the pattern of Study 1. Thus, the composite results do not mask differential dimension effects.

Response rates were excellent: T1: 98% (280/285 employees); T2: 94% (263/280); T3: 91% (255/280). Attrition analyses revealed no systematic differences between completers and non‑completers on T1 variables (all p > .20).

Analytical strategy

Primary analyses employed mixed design analysis of variance (ANOVA) with time (T1, T2, T3) as within-subjects factor and condition (intervention, control) as between-subjects factor. Significant Time × Condition interactions indicate differential change across conditions. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared (η²). Following significant interactions, simple effects analyses compared conditions at each time point and examined change within each condition.

To test mediation of intervention effects (i.e., whether changes in leader behaviours drove changes in employee outcomes), we conducted path analysis on change scores (T3–T1) using bootstrapped mediation [36]. This analysis examined whether changes in AL dimensions predicted changes in mediators, which in turn predicted changes in wellbeing and turnover intention. All analyses used intent-to-treat principles, analyzing participants based on original assignment regardless of intervention adherence. Adherence was high: 28 of 30 intervention leaders (93%) attended at least 5 of 6 sessions.

Results

Manipulation check: changes in authentic leadership

We first examined whether the intervention successfully improved leader authentic leadership as rated by employees. Mixed ANOVA on employee-rated AL composite revealed a significant Time × Condition interaction, F(2, 253) = 14.28, p < .001, η² = 0.10. Intervention group leaders showed significant increases from T1 to T2 (M_diff = + 0.62, p < .001) and maintained gains at T3 (M_diff from T1 = + 0.58, p < .001). Control group leaders showed no significant change (M_diff T3–T1 = + 0.08, p = .42). Examination of individual AL dimensions (supplementary analyses) revealed significant improvements on all four dimensions for the intervention group (all p < .01), with the largest effects on transparency (η² = 0.12) and balanced processing (η² = 0.11).

Effects on mediators

Significant Time × Condition interactions emerged for all proposed mediators. For psychological safety, the interaction was significant, F(2, 256) = 9.34, p < .001, η² = 0.08. The intervention group increased from T1 to T2 (M_diff = + 0.56, p < .001) and maintained gains at T3, while the control group showed no change. For trust in leader, the interaction was significant, F(2, 255) = 10.21, p < .001, η² = 0.09. The intervention group increased (M_diff T1–T3 = + 0.60, p < .001), while the control group was unchanged. For procedural justice, the interaction was significant, F(2, 257) = 8.12, p < .01, η² = 0.07. The intervention group increased (M_diff T1–T3 = + 0.53, p < .01), while the control group was unchanged. For meaningfulness, the interaction was significant, F(2, 258) = 6.89, p < .01, η² = 0.06. The intervention group increased (M_diff T1–T3 = + 0.50, p < .01), while the control group was unchanged. For perceived organizational support (POS), the interaction was significant, F(2, 256) = 7.43, p < .01, η² = 0.07. The intervention group increased (M_diff T1–T3 = + 0.50, p < .01), while the control group was unchanged.

Effects on wellbeing outcomes and turnover intention

Significant Time × Condition interactions were also observed for wellbeing outcomes. For thriving, the interaction was significant, F(2, 255) = 5.89, p < .01, η² = 0.05. The intervention group increased (M_diff T1–T3 = + 0.40, p < .01), while the control group was unchanged. For engagement, the interaction was significant, F(2, 254) = 11.34, p < .001, η² = 0.11. The intervention group increased substantially (M_diff T1–T3 = + 0.53, p < .001), while the control group was unchanged. For emotional exhaustion, the interaction was significant, F(2, 256) = 7.21, p < .01, η² = 0.07. The intervention group decreased (M_diff T1–T3 = -0.50, p < .01), while the control group was unchanged.

Most critically for organizational outcomes, turnover intention showed a significant Time × Condition interaction, F(2, 253) = 13.42, p < .001, η² = 0.12. The intervention group decreased dramatically (M_diff T1–T3 = -1.00, representing a 32% reduction from baseline mean of 3.12 to 2.12), while the control group showed no significant change (M_diff T1–T3 = -0.04, p = .78).

Mediation of intervention effects

To test whether changes in specific AL dimensions mediated improvements in outcomes through their matched mediators, we conducted path analysis on change scores (T3–T1). The model specified: (a) changes in each AL dimension predicting changes in their theoretically matched mediators; (b) changes in mediators predicting changes in wellbeing outcomes; and (c) changes in wellbeing outcomes predicting changes in turnover intention. Condition (intervention vs. control) was included as an exogenous variable predicting changes in AL dimensions.

Results supported the causal mediation chains. The indirect effect of condition on turnover intention through changes in transparency, psychological safety/trust, and thriving/engagement was significant (indirect = − 0.28, 95% CI [-0.41, − 0.15]). The indirect effect through changes in balanced processing, procedural justice, emotional exhaustion, and turnover intention was significant (indirect = − 0.19, 95% CI [-0.30, − 0.08]). The indirect effect through changes in moral perspective, meaningfulness/POS, engagement, and turnover intention was significant (indirect = − 0.31, 95% CI [-0.44, − 0.18]).

The total indirect effect of condition on turnover intention through the modelled pathways was − 0.52 (95% CI [-0.68, − 0.36]), accounting for 89% of the total effect of condition on turnover intention (total effect = − 0.58). This provides strong evidence that the intervention reduced turnover intention specifically through improvements in AL dimensions, which enhanced psychological resources, which improved wellbeing.

Study 2 discussion

Study 2 provided causal evidence that training leaders in the specific, disentangled behaviours of authentic leadership targeting the distinct mechanisms identified in Study 1 produces meaningful improvements in employee wellbeing and retention. The 32% reduction in turnover intention scores among intervention group employees represents a substantial effect with clear practical significance. Notably, effects were not only immediate but sustained at three‑month follow‑up, suggesting durable behavior change. Mediation analyses confirmed that changes in specific AL dimensions drove changes in their theoretically matched mediators, which in turn drove improvements in wellbeing and reductions in turnover intention providing the strongest causal evidence to date for the mechanisms through which authentic leadership operates.

Discussion

This multi-study investigation examined how the four dimensions of authentic leadership differentially influence employee wellbeing and turnover intention through distinct psychological mechanisms. Across a three-wave longitudinal survey (Study 1) and a randomized field experiment (Study 2), we found consistent support for a disaggregated model in which each AL dimension provides unique resources that affect wellbeing through specific pathways.

Cross-study synthesis

Table 4 summarizes hypothesis support across the two studies. Study 1 provided longitudinal, quantitative support for all hypothesized indirect effects (H1–H3) and for full mediation (H4). Study 2 then demonstrated causality: a targeted intervention that trained leaders in the specific behaviors associated with each AL dimension led to significant improvements in employee psychological safety, trust, procedural justice, meaningfulness, and POS, which in turn increased thriving and engagement, reduced emotional exhaustion, and lowered turnover intention by 32% at three-month follow-up. Mediation of change scores confirmed that the intervention worked through the theorized mechanisms.

H5 (crisis moderation) received partial support: crisis amplified the moral perspective → meaningfulness pathway, with suggestive evidence for transparency → psychological safety. Balanced processing → procedural justice was not moderated by crisis, possibly because justice perceptions are more stable and less context-dependent than meaningfulness or safety.

Self-awareness as a foundational dimension

Our results support the theoretical prediction (presented in the hypothesis development section, not post‑hoc) that self‑awareness functions as a meta‑capacity enabling the other three AL dimensions. In Study 1, self‑awareness strongly predicted transparency, balanced processing, and moral perspective (all β > 0.50, p < .001), but had no direct effects on mediators or outcomes when those dimensions were included. In Study 2, the training program included self‑awareness as the first module (360° feedback, values clarification), and improvements in self‑awareness correlated with improvements in the other dimensions (r = .58–0.72). This pattern is consistent with the view that self‑awareness is necessary but not sufficient for authentic leadership.

Theoretical contributions

Disaggregation of authentic leadership. The most fundamental contribution of this research is demonstrating that the four AL dimensions are not functionally equivalent. While the field has largely treated AL as a composite construct [2], our findings reveal that each dimension operates through distinct psychological mechanisms: relational transparency fosters psychological safety and trust; balanced processing enhances procedural justice; internalized moral perspective cultivates meaningfulness and POS. This disaggregation resolves ambiguity about how AL works and suggests that composite scores may obscure important nuance.

Integration of conservation of resources theory. We position AL as a source of resource caravans [6] through which valued psychological resources flow from leaders to employees. COR theory explains why AL affects wellbeing: leader behaviours provide resources that buffer against loss (reducing burnout) and enable gains (fostering thriving and engagement). Our findings extend COR theory by specifying the particular resources provided by specific leader behaviours.

Mechanism specification and causal evidence. By identifying and testing distinct mediators (safety, trust, justice, meaning, support), we unpack the black box between AL and outcomes. The mediation model proved robust across longitudinal and experimental designs. The full mediation findings that AL affects turnover intention entirely through wellbeing positions wellbeing as a critical mechanism linking leadership to retention.

Implications for COR and SET theory

Our findings carry specific implications for the two theoretical frameworks that guided this research. For COR theory, the study demonstrates that not all resources are equal different leader behaviours provide different psychological resources, and these resources have distinct effects on wellbeing outcomes (safety and trust fostering thriving and engagement; procedural justice reducing exhaustion; meaningfulness and POS enhancing engagement). This specificity advances COR theory beyond general statements about resource provision to a more nuanced understanding of resource-matching. For SET, the full mediation finding offers a refinement: reciprocal obligations between leaders and employees flow through wellbeing. Leaders do not directly obligate employees to stay; rather, they create conditions (safety, fairness, meaning) that foster wellbeing, and employees reciprocate with loyalty and reduced withdrawal intentions. This suggests that SET’s principle of reciprocity operates indirectly through psychological states, not as a direct exchange.

Practical implications

Training. Organizations to move from generic “be authentic” training to targeted skill building. For transparency: use vulnerability-sharing exercises, feedback protocols, and practice in admitting mistakes. For balanced processing: train structured dissent protocols (e.g., devil’s advocate, red team/blue team exercises). For moral perspective: use ethical dilemma workshops and practice articulating the “why” behind decisions. Self-awareness should be the foundation (360° feedback, values clarification, reflection journals).

Selection and assessment. Rather than assessing global authenticity, selection tools (e.g., situational judgment tests, behavioural interviews) can assess each dimension separately. Multi-rater feedback systems should provide dimension-specific scores to enable targeted development planning.

Leading indicators. Organizations can track psychological safety, trust, procedural justice, meaningfulness, and POS as measurable leading indicators to monitor the impact of leadership development initiatives on well-being and retention.

Limitations and future research directions

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. First, methodological limitations concerning mediation inference. Our study used a three‑wave longitudinal design with eight‑week intervals between measurements. While this design establishes temporal precedence for direct effects (predictors at T1, mediators at T2, outcomes at T3), it does not fully satisfy the requirements for testing serial mediation where a mediator at T2 predicts a second mediator at T3, which then predicts an outcome. True serial mediation ideally requires five time points to adequately capture the sequential causal chain [42]. Therefore, our inferences about sequential pathways (e.g., transparency → psychological safety → thriving → turnover intention) should be treated as tentative. We encourage future research to employ five‑wave designs to replicate our serial mediation findings. That said, our parallel mediation hypotheses (H1, H2, H3) are appropriately tested with three time points, and our experimental intervention (Study 2) provides causal support for the overall model.

Second, while we used multi‑source data (employees rated leaders; leaders provided crisis ratings), key mediators and outcomes were self‑reported by employees. However, the longitudinal design (separating predictor, mediator, and outcome measurement) and experimental design mitigate concerns. Future research could incorporate objective outcomes (e.g., actual turnover, absenteeism) and physiological measures of wellbeing.

Third, the samples were drawn primarily from Kenya, a developing economy. Cultural differences in the meaning and enactment of authenticity may moderate findings. Cross‑cultural replication is essential. Fourth, the intervention follow‑up period (3 months) was relatively short. Longer‑term follow‑up (12–24 months) is needed to assess whether effects translate to actual turnover reduction. Fifth, we did not examine potential downsides or dark side effects of authentic leadership. Future research could examine boundary conditions where AL dimensions might have diminishing or even negative returns.

Conclusion

Authentic leadership is not a passing trend. Rather, it is a learnable set of behaviors that provide employees with safety, fairness, and meaning. Leaders who practice transparency, balanced processing, and moral integrity create the psychological conditions where people thrive, not just survive. When employees feel safe, fairly treated, and find meaning in their work, they are more likely to stay. Our three‑wave longitudinal survey established temporal precedence for these parallel mediation pathways, and our field experiment provided causal evidence that training leaders in these specific behaviours improves wellbeing and reduces turnover intention. Organizations that invest in these specific leadership skills will build resilient, engaged, and loyal workforces. Future research could employ five‑wave designs to fully test the sequential (serial) mediation chains suggested by our model.
