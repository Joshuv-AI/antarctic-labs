Developing machine learning models to forecast mosquito abundance for climate change impacts and early warning system
Authors: Seun Jung, Hyemin Jeong, Yoonnoh Lee, Seungho Han, Seonguk Son, Byoung-Hak Jeon, Heesun Chung, Wooae Byun, Dong Gun Kim, Sangchul Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The risk of mosquito-borne diseases is expected to increase due to climate change, and thus it is needed to forecast how mosquito abundance would vary in response to dynamic climatic conditions. Both short-term prediction and long-term projection are essential for supporting timely mosquito control and mitigating vector-borne disease risk. This study developed a machine learning–based forecasting framework to support an early warning system for short-term mosquito abundance prediction and to project long-term mosquito abundance. Short-term prediction was conducted using machine learning models (MLMs) with short-term meteorological forecast data. Long-term projection was made by future climate data (∼2100) under SSP1–2.6 and SSP2–4.5. Results showed that MLMs using short-term meteorological forecast data produced mosquito abundance comparable to those using observational data (R2 difference of 0.03), demonstrating the reliability of short-term predictions. In addition, it outperformed the current monitoring system with R2 improvements of 0.20–0.48 across all landscape type. These results indicate that reliable forecasts of mosquito abundance can be provided in advance, enabling public health agencies to implement mosquito control measures before mosquito abundance increases. SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis indicated that the previous day's mosquito abundance and maximum temperature were the most influential ecological and meteorological predictors. Under climate change scenarios, mosquito abundance was expected to decrease due to higher temperatures. In contrast, the rate of increase in mosquito abundance during spring is expected to rise, indicating a temporal shift in mosquito occurrence. These seasonal shifts suggest that mosquito surveillance and vector control may need to begin earlier to mitigate mosquito-borne disease risk. Overall, the proposed framework provides a practical decision-support tool for mosquito surveillance, vector-borne disease risk mitigation, and One Health preparedness under climate change.

Introduction

Mosquitoes are the principal vectors of infectious diseases, including dengue fever, malaria, and Japanese encephalitis [1]. In 2022, malaria caused approximately 608,000 deaths worldwide [2], and in 2023, over 6.5 million dengue cases and 7000 deaths were reported globally [3]. The risk of these mosquito-borne diseases is expected to increase globally as climate change influences mosquito survival, reproduction, and pathogen transmission dynamics [4]. Rising temperatures and changes in precipitation patterns have expanded mosquito habitats and shifted their seasonal and regional distributions [5], [6]. These climate-driven changes may extend the seasonal period favorable for mosquito activity and mosquito-borne pathogen transmission, thereby altering human exposure patterns [7]. Considering these interactions among climate, mosquito ecology and human health, forecasting mosquito abundance included climate information is essential for integrated One Health preparedness.

Various methods ranging from statistical to machine learning models (MLMs) have been widely applied to predict mosquito abundance and classify mosquito density levels. Generalized linear regression and seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average models were used to forecast mosquito abundance trends [8], [9]. Recently, MLMs have been increasingly utilized for their ability to represent complex and nonlinear dynamics in ecological and epidemiological systems. Previous studies have applied various MLMs, including GB and XGB for short-term mosquito abundance prediction [10], RF for mosquito abundance classification [11], and LSTM for projecting future mosquito abundance under climate change [12].

Climate change impacts on mosquito abundance vary by location, with some studies projecting increasing abundance and others declining trends [12], [13], [14]. For example, a study conducted in Toronto, Canada, projected that rising temperatures associated with global warming would increase mosquito abundance [12]. In contrast, research conducted in Tokyo, Japan, projected that increased extreme rainfall under climate change would reduce mosquito abundance through habitat loss [13]. Another study from southern China projected a 13–15% decline in annual mosquito abundance under future warming conditions [14]. These studies indicate that mosquito abundance responses to climate change may depend on regional climatic conditions, suggesting the need for predictive models that consider regional differences.

With the necessity of long-term projection under climate change, short-term prediction of mosquito abundance is also increasingly important for responding to mosquito-borne disease risks and mitigating the public health impacts. As mosquito abundance is a proxy for mosquito-borne disease transmission risk, it is also an important entomological parameter for evaluating vector–human contact [15], reliable short-term prediction may provide early information for vector surveillance and timely vector control before mosquito abundance reach seasonal peaks. Therefore, early warning system (EWS) plays an important role in supporting public health agencies by enabling proactive responses to increasing mosquito abundance [16], [17].

In this study, we developed an integrated machine learning-based forecasting framework that links short-term mosquito prediction with long-term climate-driven mosquito abundance projections within a unified modeling framework for Seoul, South Korea. Four MLMs (RF, GB, XGB, and LGB [Light Gradient Boosting]) were applied in this framework. To test the reliability of short-term prediction for EWS, the dataset was prepared in two different settings (monitored- vs. forecast-based). In addition, future mosquito abundance under Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) 1–2.6 and SSP2–4.5 were projected using an iterated forecasting strategy to assess climate change impacts. The proposed integrated forecasting framework provides a decision-support tool for vector surveillance, mosquito-borne disease risk mitigation, and climate-adaptive public health preparedness.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Seoul, the capital city of South Korea (605.21 km2) (Fig. 1). Seoul has a monsoon-influenced humid continental climate, characterized by hot, humid summers and cold winters with concentrated seasonal rainfall [18]. These climatic characteristics can accelerate mosquito growth, prolong breeding periods, and increase mosquito abundance during the summer [19], [20]. Seoul also contains diverse urban environmental features, including residential areas, green spaces, and water-related landscapes, which are associated with mosquito abundance dynamics [11]. Considering the climatic and urban characteristics that can increase mosquito abundance and the risk of mosquito-borne public health issues [21], [22], Seoul is a suitable setting for applying forecasting frameworks that support EWS and assess future mosquito abundance trends.Fig. 1Study site and spatial distribution of digital mosquito information monitoring system and weather stations.Fig. 1

For short-term mosquito predictions, the period from 2015 to 2023 was set as the training period, and 2024 was designated as the test period. During the training period, the data were divided into training and validation datasets. To evaluate the validity of EWS, two different input datasets were fed into MLMs. Monitored-based MLMs (monitored-MLM) and forecast-based MLMs (forecast-MLM) used the same meteorological data for the train period but monitored- and forecast-MLMs used observed- and short-term forecast meteorological data for the test period. By comparing the two models for the test period, this study assessed the credibility of the EWS. Furthermore, the short-term prediction by forecast-MLM was further compared with the current prediction system operated by the city of Seoul.

Long-term mosquito abundance projections under climate change scenarios were conducted using the optimal forecast-MLM. Meteorological data under SSP1–2.6 and SSP2–4.5 were used as input variables. Since the past mosquito abundance data are inputs to the MLMs, future mosquito abundance was generated using an iterative forecasting approach. For same Daily future mosquito abundance was simulated for the period from 2024 to 2100. The future period was divided into near future (2024–2050), mid future (2051–2075), and far future (2076–2100) to examine temporal changes in mosquito abundance. Projections were generated only for the Digital Mosquito Monitoring System (DMS) surveillance season (April–October), which was the target period of this study. Therefore, the non-surveillance period (November–March) was excluded from the projection analysis. Accordingly, Mosquito abundance during the non-surveillance period was fixed at zero, as the iterated forecasting process recursively uses mosquito abundance from preceding days to generate subsequent projections.

Data preparation

Meteorological, habitat, and ecological variables were used to predict mosquito abundance (Table S1). The target variable was the daily mosquito abundance measured at 41 DMS (http://dmsnet.kr) operated by the city of Seoul. The spatial distribution of the stations is shown in Fig. 1. DMS stations were classified into three landscape types following the regional classification by the city of Seoul: waterfront (5), residential (21), and park areas (15). Each DMS attracts mosquitoes by releasing CO₂ at a rate of 300 mL/min to simulate human breath. The device operates daily from 19:00 to 05:00 during the mosquito surveillance season (April–October) and automatically records the number of captured mosquitoes [8]. Because the DMS records only the total number of captured mosquitoes, the target variable represents overall mosquito abundance rather than species-specific abundance. Although species-specific information was not available from the DMS data, official mosquito surveillance data collected by the city of Seoul using light traps indicate that Culex pipiens pallens is the predominant mosquito species in Seoul [11].

Meteorological variables included temperature-related variables (e.g., maximum, minimum, monthly mean, and annual mean temperature), precipitation, relative humidity, and wind speed derived from monitored meteorological data, short-term forecast data, and climate projection data provided by the Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA). Climate projection data under SSP1–2.6 and SSP2–4.5 scenarios were used for future projections.

Habitat variables included habitat-suitability score and DMS openness indices calculated within 10 m buffer zones around each DMS. Ecological variables consisted of lagged mosquito abundance variables from 1 to 21 previous days. Detailed descriptions of data preparation are provided in Text S1 of the Supplementary Materials.

Data preprocessing

Normalization was applied to minimize the learning bias caused by differences in numerical scales between input variables. Min-Max normalization rescales each feature to a uniform range between 0 and 1, ensuring all variables contribute equally during training. Outlier handling was applied to mosquito abundance data to improve model stability and reduce the influence of extreme observations. The Interquartile Range method was used to identify and remove outliers [23].

Model development

All models were developed in Python 3.9.10 using scikit-learn 1.3.2, xgboost 2.1.4 and lightgbm 4.6.0, and data manipulation via NumPy 1.24.4 and pandas 2.0.3. Tree-based MLMs, including RF, GB, XGB, and LGB, were used in this study. Detailed descriptions of models are provided in Text S2 of the Supplementary Materials.

Hyperparameter optimization

Hyperparameter optimization was performed using a Bayesian Optimization framework with a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator algorithm, with 100 trials conducted for each model. The search space covered four functional groups of hyperparameters (capacity, regularization, subsampling, and learning dynamics). The objective function used a 3-fold cross-validation, and the fold-average score was optimized by minimizing Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). The detailed search ranges and optimal hyperparameter values for forecast-MLM and monitored-MLM are presented in Tables S2–S3 of the Supplementary Materials.

Model performance measures

This study evaluated model performances with statistical metrics calculated on each validation split and averaged across folds; test metrics were computed on the held-out set. The coefficient of determination (R2) and RMSE were used in this study. R2 captures the fraction of variance in the response explained by the model [24]. It reaches 1 for a perfect fit and can be negative when predicting the sample mean outperforms the model. RMSE summarizes the typical size of residuals in the same physical unit as the target and gives greater weight to large errors because residuals are squared before averaging.

SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP)

To interpret the contributions of individual features to the model predictions, this study used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a model-agnostic interpretability method derived from cooperative game theory [25]. SHAP attributes each prediction to input features by estimating their marginal contributions across possible feature subsets, providing both local and global interpretations of feature importance.

Results

Summary statistics of dataset

Summary statistics of monitored, forecast, and climate scenario datasets are provided in Tables S4–S6 of the Supplementary Materials. Forecast meteorological data showed higher temperature values than monitored data, and mosquito abundance exhibited right-skewed distributions across all landscape types. Under climate change scenarios, mean temperature increased from near future to far future, particularly under SSP2–4.5 (Fig. S1 of the Supplementary Materials). Compared with the historical observations (2015–2024), the mean temperature across the projection periods increased from 19.57 °C (−0.59–33.57 °C) to 22.67 °C (1.4–35.7 °C) under SSP1–2.6 and 23.40 °C (3.6–37.2 °C) under SSP2–4.5. In contrast, the mean precipitation across the projection periods increased from 2.1 mm (0.0–328.0 mm) to 5.8 mm (0.0–352.9 mm) under SSP1–2.6 and 6.2 mm (0.0–302.0 mm) under SSP2–4.5, showing greater interannual variability than temperature. Detailed descriptions of dataset characteristics are provided in Text S3 of the Supplementary Materials.

Forecast-MLM results were further compared with the current mosquito forecasting system operated by the city of Seoul (Fig. 3b). The current system used similar input data but applied a multiple linear regression (MLR) approach. Forecast-MLMs showed improved ability to reproduce seasonal mosquito abundance dynamics, with R2 improvements ranging from 0.20 to 0.48 depending on the landscape types (Entire area: 0.20, Waterfront: 0.48, Residential: 0.22, and Park: 0.22).

Climate change impacts

The trends in historical forecast-MLM predictions for the historical period (historical prediction) and projected mosquito abundance under climate change scenarios (SSP1–2.6 and SSP2–4.5) for the April–October period are presented in Fig. 5. Under the SSP1–2.6 scenario, the projected summer abundance remained relatively stable across the near, mid, and far future periods, whereas it showed a decreasing trend over time under SSP2–4.5 scenario. The mean summer mosquito abundance of historical prediction during 2020–2024 was 32.67 individuals. In near future, the projected mean summer abundance decreased to 28.31 and 28.52 under SSP1–2.6 and SSP2–4.5, respectively. Despite the decrease in summer mosquito abundance, the onset of mosquito occurrence in spring occurred earlier under future climate scenarios (Fig. S5 of the Supplementary Materials). When defined as the first date exceeding 10 individuals, the onset shifted from April 22 in the historical period to April 6–7 under future climate scenarios, corresponding to an advancement of 15–16 days. The timing of rapid spring increase also occurred earlier. The date of the maximum daily increase shifted from June 5 in the historical period to April 24–28 under future climate scenarios, indicating an advancement of 38–42 days.

Discussion

This study highlights the potential of forecast-based machine learning models to support climate-adaptive mosquito surveillance and early warning for mosquito-borne disease risk. Forecast-based MLMs showed predictive performance comparable to monitored-based MLMs across all landscape types and consistently reproduced seasonal mosquito population dynamics. Although monitored-MLMs showed slightly higher predictive performance, the overall performance difference between monitored- and forecast-MLMs was relatively small, with an R2 difference of approximately 0.03. This difference is likely associated with uncertainty in short-term forecast meteorological inputs generated by numerical weather prediction models [26], [27]. Forecast meteorological data are subject to uncertainty arising from model formulation, parameterization, and initial conditions, which can introduce discrepancies between forecasted and actual environmental conditions [28]. Nevertheless, the forecast-MLM reproduced temporal variation patterns of mosquito abundance across landscape types. These findings suggest that reliable mosquito abundance forecasts can be generated before observed meteorological data become available, providing additional lead time for vector surveillance and timely vector control during periods of increasing mosquito activity.

Forecast-MLM also outperformed the current mosquito forecasting system operated by the city of Seoul, with R2 increasing from 0.20 to 0.48 across landscape type. This improvement indicates that forecast-MLM explained a greater proportion of the variation in mosquito abundance than the existing MLR-based forecasting system. This performance difference may be associated with the ability of MLMs to capture nonlinear relationships between mosquito abundance and environmental variables more effectively than linear regression-based approaches [29], [30]. Mosquito abundance dynamics are influenced by nonlinear responses to meteorological variables such as temperature, precipitation, and humidity. Temperature affects mosquito activity and survival within species-specific temperature ranges, whereas excessive precipitation can reduce mosquito abundance through larval mortality and habitat flushing [31], [32]. Such nonlinear environmental responses can limit the predictive performance of linear regression-based forecasting models. Similar improvements in predictive performance using ML-based approaches have also been reported in mosquito and dengue forecasting studies [33], [34]. Therefore, incorporating ML-based approaches into operational mosquito forecasting systems may improve the reliability of EWS, supporting public health agencies in implementing timely vector surveillance and vector-borne disease risk mitigation under complex and changing environmental conditions.

SHAP analysis identified lagged mosquito abundance and maximum temperature as dominant predictors across landscape types. The strong contribution of lagged mosquito abundance reflects the temporal dependence of mosquito abundance dynamics, where abundance levels are strongly influenced by the abundance state in the preceding period. Similar effects of lagged mosquito-related variables have been reported in previous mosquito forecasting studies [35]. Maximum temperature also showed strong contributions to model predictions across landscape types. Temperature is a key environmental factor regulating mosquito development, survival, and activity, and previous studies have reported strong temperature dependence in mosquito abundance dynamics [36]. In this study, lower maximum temperatures were associated with lower predicted mosquito abundance, suggesting that daytime temperature is a major determinant of seasonal mosquito dynamics. The consistent importance of maximum temperature among meteorological variables indicates that temperature should remain a key component of climate-responsive mosquito forecasting under changing environmental conditions.

Under future climate scenarios, mosquito abundance showed contrasting temporal patterns between SSP1–2.6 and SSP2–4.5. Although spring mosquito occurrence occurred earlier under both scenarios, mosquito abundance gradually decreased under SSP2–4.5. Mean temperature under SSP2–4.5 continuously increased from near future to far future, accompanied by an increase in the number of days exceeding 34 °C. Previous studies have suggested that excessive high-temperature conditions can reduce mosquito survival and abundance, with similar reductions observed during extreme heat events [14], [37], [38]. In this study, the increase in extreme high-temperature days under SSP2–4.5 suggests that prolonged exposure to heat stress may contribute to long-term declines in mosquito abundance.

However, changes in annual mosquito abundance alone do not fully explain future mosquito-related risk. Despite the projected decline in overall mosquito abundance under climate change, our results consistently indicated earlier spring emergence and earlier seasonal abundance growth under future climate conditions. These findings are consistent with recent mosquito vector surveillance for malaria and Japanese encephalitis in South Korea, which reported that climate-driven increases in spring temperature and changes in precipitation created favorable conditions for mosquito development and advanced the seasonal activity of mosquito vectors [39], [40]. Together, these findings suggest that earlier seasonal emergence may lengthen transmission windows for mosquito-borne pathogens, while climate-driven temporal shifts may alter human exposure patterns by extending the period during which people are exposed to mosquitoes.

This study has several limitations. First, mosquito abundance was derived from the DMS, which records total mosquito abundance without species-level identification. Consequently, species-specific responses to meteorological conditions and climate change could not be evaluated. Second, long-term mosquito projections were generated using an iterated forecasting approach, which may allow prediction errors to accumulate over extended simulation periods. Furthermore, future climate conditions may differ from those represented in the historical training data, introducing additional uncertainty when the model is applied beyond its original training domain. Future studies incorporating additional long-term mosquito observations, periodic model updating, and multiple climate models could further improve the robustness and reliability of long-term mosquito projections.

Conclusions

This study developed an integrated machine learning–based forecasting framework that links short-term mosquito abundance prediction with long-term climate-driven mosquito projections. Forecast-MLM can be used to support mosquito early warning systems, providing additional lead time for vector surveillance and timely public health responses before observed meteorological data become available. Future climate projections suggested that, although annual mosquito abundance may decline under warmer conditions, seasonal mosquito activity is likely to occur earlier. These findings indicate that climate change may alter not only mosquito abundance but also its seasonal timing, highlighting the need for earlier vector surveillance and climate-adaptive mosquito risk mitigation. Overall, the proposed framework provides a practical decision-support tool for early warning, vector-borne disease risk mitigation, and One Health properness under changing climate conditions.
