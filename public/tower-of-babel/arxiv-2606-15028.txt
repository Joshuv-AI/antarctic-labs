Abstract
We present the Swima, a bioinspired 𝟗𝟎𝟎 \boldsymbol{900} -mg swimmer propelled by two 𝟏𝟎 \boldsymbol{10} -mg high-work-density (HWD) actuators driven by shape-memory alloy (SMA) wires. We integrated onboard power and computation by using a custom-built printed circuit board (PCB) and an 𝟏𝟏 \boldsymbol{11} -mAh 3.7 \boldsymbol{3.7} -V 𝟓𝟎𝟕 \boldsymbol{507} -mg single-cell lithium-ion ( Li-Ion ) battery, which in conjunction enable autonomous swimming in excess of 𝟏𝟖 ​ min \boldsymbol{18}\,\text{min} . The Swima can swim at speeds of up to 22.4 \boldsymbol{22.4} mm/s ( 0.56 \boldsymbol{0.56} Bl/s) , achieves turning rates of up to 𝟏𝟒 \boldsymbol{14} °/s, and can follow 𝟎 \boldsymbol{0} -degree heading reference trajectories with root mean square (RMS) values of tracking errors of about 6.5 \boldsymbol{6.5} ° across multiple tests. This robot is the first subgram microswimmer with onboard power, actuation, and computation developed to date.

While SMA-based actuators are known for their HWD, typically, researchers avoid using SMA-based actuators due to their low electrical-to-mechanical energy conversion efficiency, which is in the range of
1~\text{to}~6\,\%

[
1
,
22
]
. However, the actuators of the Swima require low excitation voltages (
1~\text{to}~4
V) and therefore can be driven with
8
-mg
metal-oxide-semiconductor

field-effect
transistors
(MOSFETs) that have transmission efficiencies on the order of
99\,\%
. Piezoelectric and dielectric-based actuators require power electronics that, when created at the mm-scale, have poor transmission efficiencies. For example, the piezoelectric boost circuit used in
[
29
]
has a mass of
20\,\text{mg}
and an efficiency on the order of
30\,\%
and the dielectric boost circuit used in
[
46
]
has a mass of
127\,\text{mg}
and an efficiency of
10\,\%
. Moreover, even when these dielectric boost circuits are made at a larger scale, such as the
printed circuit board
(PCB) used in
[
19
]
, which has a mass on the order of
5
g and an overall voltage amplification efficiency on the order of
10
%. Other types of
low-voltage
actuation technologies are electromagnetic-based, such as the ones used in
[
28
,
42
]
, and ones based on
ionic-polymer
metal composites

(IPMC)
[
18
,
21
,
25
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. While both require low voltages, when scaled down to the mm-scale, the transmission efficiency of electromagnetic motors drops drastically due to increased resistive heating. For example, the electromagnetic motor in
[
42
]
has an
electrical-to-mechanical
energy conversion efficiency on the order of
0.4~\text{to}~2.5\,\%
, which is drastically lower than SMAs. In the case of
IPMC-based
actuators, these devices exhibit significantly lower work densities than those of
SMA-based

actuators
[
20
]
. This fact becomes evident when the relative speeds of
IPMC-driven
swimming
platforms
[
18
,
21
,
25
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
are compared to those achieved by the Swima. Overall, from the empirical evidence presented here, we believe that in the case of insect-scale AUVs, SMA-based actuation is an advantageous choice.

For transparency and clarity, we briefly state the technological contributions presented in this article relative to our previous work. Here we build upon the preliminary results presented in

to include additional empirical evidence of power and control autonomy of the Swima platform. Namely, we present a control strategy for robots of this type, present feedback-controlled swimming experiments, and assess the battery lifetime while driving the robot.

Autonomous Swimming

Fig. 3:
Block diagram of the control scheme used in the

In this scheme, the heading controller receives the heading error,
\psi_{\text{e}}
, and computes the heading input signal,
u_{\psi}
. Then, the actuator mapping computes the DC values associated with each actuator,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, which are used to generate the PWM analog signals,
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, that drive the
SMA-based
actuators. Depending on the heading angular velocity of the robot,
\dot{\psi}
, the gyroscope generates a voltage signal,
V_{\text{gyro}}
, that is then sampled by the microcontroller and converted into a heading angular velocity estimate,
\hat{\dot{\psi}}
, through a static mapping provided by the IMU manufacturer. Last, we compute the heading estimate of the robot,
\hat{\psi}
, and use it to compute the heading error based on the desired heading,
\psi_{\text{d}}
.

In this section, we explain the control strategy used to perform the autonomous
feedback-controlled
swimming experiments, present and analyze the experimental swimming performance results, and discuss the endurance of the Swima through a
battery-duration
swimming test. For the
closed-loop
swimming experiments, we conducted heading-regulation and
90
-degree
left- and right-turn maneuvers, which allowed us to determine the agility and performance of the robot measured in terms of average swimming speed, turning rate, and RMS value of the heading tracking error.

III-A

Control Strategy

To perform the
closed-loop
swimming experiments, we implemented the
feedback-control
scheme depicted in the block diagram shown in Fig.
3
where the objective is to track a desired heading signal,
\psi_{\text{d}}
. Here, the output of the plant is the heading
angular-velocity
continuous signal,
\dot{\psi}(t)
, where
t
denotes continuous time. This signal is then sensed by the gyroscope incorporated in the IMU, which generates an analog voltage,
V_{\text{gyro}}
. This voltage signal is then sampled by the MCU and—using a static mapping provided by the IMU manufacturer—converted into an estimated heading angular velocity,
\hat{\dot{\psi}}[i]
, where
i
is used to index discrete time. Next, the heading estimate of the robot is computed using a complementary filter using the current and previous estimates of the heading angular velocity,

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}[i]=\sum^{i}_{j=1}\left((1-c_{\text{e}})\hat{\dot{\psi}}[j-1]+c_{\text{e}}\hat{\dot{\psi}}[j]\right)\cdot T_{\text{s}},

(1)

where
c_{\text{e}}
is a constant associated with the heading estimator; and
T_{\text{s}}
is the sampling time of the digital controller. We use this signal to then compute the heading error based on the desired heading,
\psi_{\text{d}}
. Namely,

\displaystyle\psi_{\text{e}}[i]=\psi_{\text{d}}[i]-\hat{\psi}[i].

(2)

Next, the heading input signal is computed by a
proportional-integral
(PI) control law according to

\displaystyle u_{\psi}[i]=c_{\text{p}}\psi_{\text{e}}[i]+c_{\text{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\psi_{\text{e}}[j]\cdot T_{\text{s}},

(3)

where
c_{\text{p}}
and
c_{\text{i}}
are positive scalar proportional and integral controller gains, respectively. The two control signals,
u_{\text{max}}
and
u_{\psi}
—where
u_{\text{max}}
is the maximum duty-cycle value allowed to avoid burning the SMA wires—are operated by an actuator mapping that modulates the duty-cycle values associated with each actuator according to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&c_{1}\\
c_{2}&-c_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u_{\text{max}}\\
u_{\psi}\end{bmatrix},

(4)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are tuning constants that can be adjusted to compensate for variations in the resistance values of the two SMA-based actuators, ensuring that both soft tails visually have the same stroke envelope. The
duty-cycle
values
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are then used to generate the analog PWM signals,
s_{1}(t)
and
s_{2}(t)
, that excite the two actuators according to the chosen actuation frequency,
f_{\text{a}}
. The PWM signals are generated using a custom code and sent through the digital output ports of the MCU to the SMA wires of each actuator.

Although this control scheme does not directly control the position of the robot, in Section
III-B
, we show that by regulating the heading of the swimmer, we can compel the robot to follow a
straight-line
path, and that more complex maneuvers can be achieved by changing the desired heading in real time. Additionally, this control scheme can be evolved to allow position control by using the methods in
[
6
]
and
[
14
]
.

III-C

Battery-Duration Swimming Test

To assess the battery lifespan of a Swima prototype during forward swimming, we programmed the robot to swim for an unlimited amount of time with alternating
1
-Hz PWM signals with a duty cycle of
5
%. We fully charged the Li-ion battery to a voltage of
4.2
V and activated the custom-built PCB immediately before manually setting the robot in water using tweezers. We used an overhead camera (Nikon Z7II) to record the swimming experiment. To ensure the robot did not get trapped in a corner of the pool, we periodically adjusted the robot’s trajectory by gently tapping its side with forceps to keep it swimming in circles around the pool. The experiment was run until the robot stopped functioning. Fig.
5
shows a photographic composite of frames taken at
4
-s
intervals from overhead video of the Swima swimming a
lap
in a pool in open loop. The battery voltage was read at the end of the experiment and was measured to be
3.7
V, indicating that a significant amount (
\sim 50\,\%
) of energy was still remained unused. We hypothesize that this occurs because the high instantaneous current draw of the actuators transiently lowers the battery voltage below the drop-out threshold of the voltage regulator, causing the circuit to reset. Nonetheless, the Swima is capable of operating for up to
18
minutes, which, considering the charge left in the Li-Ion battery, we estimate that the Swima consumed an average power on the order of
87
mW during this experiment. This agrees with the experimental measurements for the
SMA-based
actuators while operating in air
[
5
]
. Additionally, we estimate that during the swimming lap shown in Fig.
5
, the Swima maintained an average swimming speed on the order of
22.4
mm/s

(
0.56
Bl/s)
.
Sped-up
video footage of the entire swimming experiment can be viewed in the supplementary movie available at
https://wsuamsl.com/resources/MovieSwima.mp4
.

III-D

Discussion

A comparison of all relevant autonomous insect-scale swimmers with lengths less than
100
mm
[
30
,

,

,

,

]
is shown in Table

In terms of its average power consumption, that of the Swima is also among the lowest. Specifically, the only swimmer reported with a lower power consumption is the EEM robot
[
42
]
.

The
18
-min single-charge operational time of the Swima is significant as it is among the longest operational time reported among state-of-the-art
battery-powered
microrobots; for example, the
2.7
-g
piezoelectric-based crawling platform in
[
4
]
is capable of only
4.5
minutes of operation at its maximum locomotion speed while not being powered by external
radio-frequency-based
energy transfer, and the
970
-mg

DE-based
platform in
[
40
]
is capable of
14
minutes of continuous operation. Additionally, the
6.23
-gram swimmer in
[
19
]
only operates for
10
minutes on a single charge. The EEM swimming robot in
[
42
]
is capable of operating at
1
Hz for up to
45\,\text{min}
on a single charge; however, it is important to note that while achieving this operational time, the EEM robot carries a much larger battery (
30
mAh), therefore, we predict that with this battery capacity, and assuming appropriate circuity is used to allow complete usage of the energy stored in the battery, the Swima would be capable of operating for up to
76\,\text{min}
with the battery in
[
42
]
. However, the swimming performance of the EEM robot remains better than that of the Swima, suggesting there is room for improvement in the Swima propulsion system

While all the robots in Table
II
demonstrate remarkable open-loop swimming performance and progress towards autonomous insect-scale swimmers, none of these robots have been demonstrated to be capable of autonomously following desired heading trajectories. The feedback-controlled swimming experiments presented in Section
III-A
are the first demonstrated to date for an autonomous insect-scale swimmer.

Conclusion

We presented a new
900
-mg
surface swimmer, the Swima, which employs two
SMA-based
microactuators—in conjunction with a lightweight
custom-built
PCB that provides sensing, computation, and electronic capabilities—to locomote autonomously. A single charge of an onboard
Li-Ion
battery sustains about
18\,\text{min}
of untethered operation. Through swimming tests, we determined that the
Swima
can swim at speeds of up to
22.4
mm/s

(
0.56
Bl/s)
, achieve turning rates of up to
14
°/s
, and can follow
0
-degree heading reference trajectories with RMS values of tracking errors of about
6.5
° across multiple tests. The Swima is (i) the first subgram microswimmer with onboard power, actuation, and computation developed to date, and (ii) the first insect-scale swimmer to demonstrate feedback-controlled heading tracking swimming experiments. Our future research aims to further improve the Swima’s circuit to reduce instantaneous current draw on the Li-Ion battery and allow us to use the entirety of the stored charge, as well as improve the performance of the propulsion system.
