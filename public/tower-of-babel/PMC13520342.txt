Factors leading to high intraocular pressure in Intraocular Device-Associated Uveitis (IDAU): a retrospective nested case-control study from a tertiary uveitis cohort
Authors: Jakkrit Juhong, Thanapong Somkijrungroj, Wijak Kongwattananon, Somtaporn Ueathaweephol, Krit Pongpirul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntraocular device-associated uveitis (IDAU) — which we propose as a more precise alternative to the traditional term “uveitis–glaucoma–hyphema (UGH) syndrome” — is a rare but potentially sight-threatening complication of intraocular device implantation. Elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) is a major driver of visual morbidity in IDAU, but the risk factors that predispose affected patients to IOP elevation remain poorly characterised. We aimed to describe the clinical spectrum of IDAU and to identify independent risk factors for elevated IOP in this population, using the Chulalongkorn University Uveitis Cohort (CU2C) database.MethodsThis first nested case-control study within the Chulalongkorn University Uveitis Cohort (CU²C) enrolled 375 subjects followed at a tertiary uveitis clinic in Thailand over 8 years (August 2014–June 2022). Cases (n = 30) were IDAU patients with elevated IOP (> 21 mmHg); controls (n = 60) were IDAU patients with normal IOP, sampled at a 1:2 ratio from the same cohort. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to calculate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for candidate risk factors.ResultsWe retrospectively identified 90 subjects who developed IDAU. Following a one-to-two case–control ratio, 30 case subjects and 60 control subjects were included. Three factors were significantly associated with high intraocular pressure in IDAU. These included intraocular lens (IOL) malposition (AOR: 8.30, 95% CI 1.25 to 54.76), long eye (AOR: 8.08, 95% CI 1.18 to 55.16) and age (AOR: 1.18, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.31). There was no statistical evidence of effects of hypertension, ruptured posterior capsule, vitrectomized eye, transillumination iris defect (TID), pseudophacodonesis and single-piece IOL in sulcus on high IOP in IDAU.ConclusionIn this first nested case-control study of IDAU, IOL malposition, long axial length (> 25 mm), and older age were independently associated with elevated IOP. These findings should inform clinician awareness and post-operative monitoring strategies for high-risk IDAU patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04990-8.Keywords: Uveitis-glaucoma-hyphema syndrome, Intraocular device-associated uveitis, IDAU, Intraocular lens malposition, Intraocular pressure, Risk factors, Nested case-control study, Cohort study

Introduction

In 1978, Ellingson was the first to describe uveitis–glaucoma–hyphaema syndrome (UGH syndrome, or “Ellingson” syndrome), which arises due to iris chafing and erosions associated with first-generation rigid anterior chamber (AC) IOLs and results in uveitis, intraocular hemorrhage and/or glaucoma [1]. After 1978, UGH syndrome was recognized as an extremely rare condition caused by malposition or dislocation of intraocular lenses (IOLs), which results in mechanical chafing of uveal tissue (iris, ciliary body, iridocorneal angle) and leads to a wide spectrum of clinical conditions ranging from mild transillumination iris defect (TID) to pigment dispersion, anterior chamber inflammation, microscopic hyphema, hyphema, elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), cystoid macular edema (CME), and vitreous hemorrhage [1, 2]. UGH syndrome usually develops months to years following cataract surgery; however, it can also develop immediately after the operation [3]. This syndrome can develop after the implantation of any anterior segment device [4–6], yet it is most commonly associated with anterior chamber lens implants [1, 7]. UGH syndrome is extremely rare in an eye with an intact posterior capsule and a well-placed in-the-bag IOL; however, numerous types of lens material and positions can also cause UGH syndrome despite in-the-bag placement of IOL [8, 9], mostly due to the inappropriate size or location of the IOL [10]. A summary of previously reported studies on intraocular device-related UGH syndrome is provided as supplementary material (Additional file 1).

The pathogenesis of UGH is complex and nonspecific. One hypothesis is that uveitis is the first ocular manifestation, followed by glaucoma and/or hyphema, of chafing between the IOL and uveal tissue [11]. Nonetheless, the pathogenesis and specific risk factors that predispose IDAU patients to elevated IOP remain poorly understood, limiting evidence-based clinical management. The clinical presentation of IDAU is highly variable, and the nonspecific nature of its signs and symptoms means diagnosis is not always straightforward. Critically, the development of secondary glaucoma represents a potentially sight-threatening complication that may culminate in irreversible blindness, making timely identification of at-risk patients essential. Despite the clinical importance of elevated IOP in this setting, no study has yet applied a nested case-control design — which controls for cohort-level confounding while allowing assessment of multiple risk factors — to investigate predictors of IOP elevation in IDAU.

The traditional term “UGH syndrome” captures only the classic triad of uveitis, glaucoma, and hyphema, whereas intraocular device implantation can produce a wider range of overlapping presentations, including pigment dispersion, pseudophacodonesis, transient IOP elevation, and cystoid macular edema. To reflect this broader spectrum, we propose “intraocular device-associated uveitis” (IDAU) as a working conceptual framework for the present study, rather than as a definitive replacement for established nomenclature. We recognise that this terminology has limitations: the predominant mechanism is mechanical IOL–uveal contact rather than primary intraocular inflammation, and the most clinically consequential outcome — elevated IOP — may be more precisely described as device-associated ocular hypertension. We therefore use IDAU descriptively and provisionally, and further refinement of this terminology will require validation in larger, prospectively defined cohorts. The aims of this study were to characterise the clinical spectrum of device-associated intraocular complications and to identify independent risk factors for elevated IOP in this setting by conducting a retrospective nested case-control study using the Chulalongkorn University Uveitis Cohort (CU2C) database [12], which is an ongoing cohort study that began in August 2014 in King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital (KCMH), Thailand. To our knowledge, this represents the first study to apply a nested case-control design to investigate risk factors for elevated IOP in IDAU, providing a methodological advance over prior case series. By drawing cases and controls from the same underlying cohort, this approach minimises selection bias while enabling simultaneous evaluation of multiple candidate risk factors.

Methods

Database and study cohort

We used the CU2C database [12], which includes 375 subjects who were followed up in a uveitis clinic in KCMH, Thailand, from 2014 to 2022. Cohort entry was defined as 1 August 2014 for all subjects included in the study. The CU2C database [12] contains records of all clinical information from outpatient visits, procedures, prescriptions, diagnosis records, and laboratory records from 1 August 2014 to 30 June 2022. The data was accessed for the study on July 1, 2022. We excluded the following subjects from our study: (a) those diagnosed with uveitis from other causes, such as infection, trauma, and autoimmune diseases; (b) those diagnosed with other causes of hemorrhage, such as known retinal or choroidal pathology or trauma; (c) those diagnosed with chronic endophthalmitis; (d) those without a history of intraocular device implantation; and (e) those diagnosed with uveitis within 6 weeks after intraocular surgery (Fig. 1). In summary, 30 subjects were included in the case group, and 60 subjects were included as controls. Both the subjects and the controls were selected from the same population, substantially reducing selection bias inherent to conventional case-control designs. Controls were not individually matched to cases on potential confounders; rather, this study was designed as a primary exploratory investigation of risk factors for elevated IOP in IDAU, for which definitive confounders have not yet been established. All candidate variables were assessed for collinearity prior to analysis. The present study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (COA No. 0755/2022), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective, anonymised nature of the data. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Case and control definition

Case and control definitions were defined before data retrieval. Intraocular device-associated uveitis (IDAU) was defined as evidence of IOL-iris contact on slit-lamp examination causing uveitis or intraocular hemorrhage. Cases were subjects with IDAU plus elevated IOP (> 21 mmHg). Controls were subjects with IDAU and normal IOP (< 21 mmHg). Elevated IOP was defined on the basis of Goldmann applanation tonometry recorded at the visit at which IDAU was diagnosed. Malpositioned IOL was defined strictly by the anatomical position of the lens — clinically confirmed decentration, tilt, or subluxation/dislocation observed on dilated slit-lamp examination by a uveitis-retina specialist — regardless of IOL type or the site at which the IOL had been placed. IOL type and site of placement (for example, single-piece or three-piece IOL placed in the ciliary sulcus, anterior chamber IOL [ACIOL], or IOL placed in the capsular bag) were recorded as separate variables. A sulcus-placed or anterior chamber IOL that was well-centred and stable was not classified as malpositioned. Because the dataset did not permit reliable quantification of the degree of decentration or tilt, malposition was analysed as a single binary variable. We acknowledge that the term IDAU, as operationalised here, identifies eyes with intraocular device–associated intraocular inflammation and/or hemorrhage with concurrent IOL–uveal contact, and that elevated IOP in this setting may arise through several non-mutually-exclusive mechanisms, including mechanical trabecular trauma, pigment dispersion, chronic angle compromise, inflammatory trabecular dysfunction, and corticosteroid response. Because the CU²C database did not capture the timing, route, or duration of corticosteroid exposure, patients receiving corticosteroid therapy were not excluded, and steroid-induced ocular hypertension could not be separately distinguished from other mechanisms; this limitation is addressed further in the Discussion.

Study population and variables

All subjects with uveitis with a history of intraocular device implantation in the CU2C database [12] between 1 August 2014 and 30 June 2022 were included. The variables chosen for analysis are largely based on case series and case reports from previous studies [1, 3, 10], [13–15]. The potential factor variables were age, sex, comorbidities, long eye, myopia, vitrectomized eyes, ruptured posterior capsule, TID, malpositioned IOL, and single-piece IOL in sulcus. Single-piece IOL placed in the sulcus was deliberately analysed as a variable distinct from malpositioned IOL. Whereas malpositioned IOL denotes an anatomical outcome, single-piece IOL in the sulcus reflects a design–location mismatch: unlike three-piece IOLs, whose posteriorly-angulated haptics and optic-edge profile permit safe placement in the ciliary sulcus, single-piece IOLs lack haptic angulation and an appropriate optic-edge design for sulcus placement, and are therefore theoretically predisposed to chronic iris chafing and UGH-type complications even when the lens is grossly well-centred. The two variables thus capture distinct constructs — anatomical malposition versus design–location mismatch — and were intentionally retained as separate predictors. Study data were extracted from the CU2C database [12] and recorded on a Microsoft Excel 2022 (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA). All data generated or analysed during this study are included in this published article and its supplementary information files (Additional file 2).

Data analysis and statistics

Demographic data were analyzed using descriptive analysis, mean ± standard deviation (SD), median, frequency, and percentage as appropriate. Continuous and categorical variables were examined with Student’s t test and Fisher’s exact test, respectively, to test for differences between cases and controls. A logistic regression model was fitted with variables as potential factors using high intraocular pressure in IDAU as the main outcome variable (see Table 2). The odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated between cases and potential associated factors, including age, sex, comorbidities, long eye with axial length > 25 millimeters, myopia, vitrectomized eyes, ruptured posterior capsule, TID, malpositioned IOL, and single-piece IOL in the sulcus. The variables that were reported as significant factors were then selected for determination of the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) using the multivariate method. The presence of multiple collinearity was addressed before the analysis, and all variables indicated no collinearity. Analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL). P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant demographics and clinical characteristics

The study flow chart describing patient recruitment is shown in Fig. 1. The general characteristics and clinical details of the cases and controls are presented in Table 1. In this study, there were 30 cases and 60 controls. The average age of the case group was 67.90 ± 8.16 years, with men predominating (70.0%, n = 21), while the average age of the control group was 44.02 ± 14.68 years, with men also predominating (58.3%, n = 35). There was a statistically significant difference in age between the case and control groups (p < 0.001). For sex, there was no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.36). Hypertension was significantly more common in cases than in controls (43.3% vs. 16.7%; p = 0.01). Diabetes (36.7% vs. 18.3%; p = 0.07) and cardiovascular disease (13.3% vs. 16.7%; p = 0.77) did not differ significantly between groups. Compared with controls, case subjects had a significantly higher mean IOP (23.73 ± 2.78 vs. 13.65 ± 3.89 mmHg; p < 0.001). For the anatomical class of uveitis, both cases and controls had predominantly anterior uveitis (96.7% vs. 85.0%), followed by panuveitis (3.3% vs. 10.0%) and intermediate uveitis (0% vs. 5.0%). There was no statistically significant difference in the anatomical classes of uveitis between groups (p = 0.20).

Clinical characteristics of intraocular device-associated uveitis with increased IOP

Anterior uveitis was the first clinical sign in all subjects, followed by elevated IOP. In two subjects, hyphema developed as a later clinical feature, subsequent to the initial uveitis and IOP elevation. The most frequent complications were cystoid macular edema (13.33%), retinal detachment (6.67%), and posterior synechiae (3.33%). All cases of elevated IOP were successfully controlled with IOP-lowering medications.

Factors leading to high intraocular pressure in intraocular device-associated uveitis

Univariate and multivariate associations of factors leading to high IOP in IDAU are shown in Table 2. Univariate logistic regression analysis showed that age, hypertension, long eye (AL > 25 mm), ruptured posterior capsule, transillumination iris defect (TID), pseudophacodonesis, malpositioned IOL and single-piece IOL in the sulcus significantly increased the risk of increasing IOP among IDAU. Controlling for confounding with multivariate analysis illustrated that age (adjusted odds ratio (AOR) 1.18, 95% confidence interval (95% CI) 1.07–1.31, p < 0.002), long eye (AOR 8.08, 95% CI 1.18–55.16, p = 0.03), and IOL malposition (AOR 8.30, 95% CI 1.25–54.76, p = 0.03) significantly increased the risk of elevated IOP in IDAU (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Uveitis-glaucoma-hyphema (UGH) syndrome is a rare condition and is commonly reported following anterior chamber IOL implantation or malposition of posterior chamber IOL (PCIOL). Publications on UGH syndrome have been limited due to the extreme rarity of this condition. Recently, the incidence of UGH has significantly decreased due to advancements in cataract surgery technology, lens material and design, and the increasing trend of PCIOL [16]. The incidence of UGH syndrome has declined over the years from 2.2 to 3% to 0.4–1.2% [16, 17]. Despite this decline, the prevalence of UGH caused by ocular devices other than IOLs is increasing, particularly with the rise in intraocular device implantation for glaucoma surgical treatments. For instance, a case of UGH syndrome was reported in 2019 by Andrew Hou et al. [4] occurring after EX-PRESS shunt implantation, suggesting that UGH syndrome may become more prevalent with the use of new types of intraocular devices beyond IOLs. Another study by Armonaite et al.3 in 2021 reported 71 subjects with UGH syndrome, making it the largest retrospective study to date. They found that pseudophacodonesis was a risk factor for UGH syndrome, while blood thinners and transillumination iris defects were not specific to UGH syndrome. Notably, UGH syndrome can still occur even with a properly placed IOL in the capsular bag. A rare manifestation of UGH syndrome was reported by Swathi Vallabh Badakere et al. [8] in 2016, where a patient underwent uneventful cataract surgery with a posterior chamber IOL positioned within the bag, covered by a capsulorrhexis margin. However, the use of dilated gonioscopy revealed superior haptic displacement due to a tear in the equatorial bag, leading to hyphema, anterior chamber inflammation, and high intraocular pressure.

In this study, we chose to use the term “intraocular device-associated uveitis” (IDAU) instead of UGH syndrome to encompass the broader clinical spectrum associated with this condition. IDAU can present with not only uveitis, glaucoma, and hyphema, but also with chronic inflammation, secondary iris neovascularization, pseudophacodonesis, transient intraocular pressure elevation, and cystoid macular edema [11]. We conducted a retrospective nested case-control study within a tertiary-based hospital setting to identify factors associated with increased intraocular pressure (IOP) in IDAU. Our findings showed that IOL malposition, long eye (axial length > 25 mm), and age were significantly associated with increased IOP in IDAU. Although not statistically significant, hypertension, rupture of the posterior capsule, transient intraocular pressure elevation, pseudophacodonesis, and the use of a single-piece IOL in the sulcus showed positive trends. In our study, we found that IOL malposition was identified as the most significant potential factor, with an approximately 8-fold greater odds of elevated IOP (AOR 8.30) in IDAU. However, the effect of single-piece IOLs placed in the sulcus, which can lead to malposition, was not found to be significant. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in the type of malposition and its impact on IOP. Specifically, single-piece IOLs may not always lead to the same degree of malposition or subsequent IOP increase as other forms of malposition. IOL malposition is recognised as one of the principal indications for IOL exchange, accounting for a substantial proportion of explantation cases across multiple surgical series and reflecting the spectrum of device-related complications summarised in S1 Tables [24–30] In comparison to the controls, we found that each additional year of age was associated with an 18% increase in the odds of elevated IOP in IDAU (AOR 1.18 per year). Regarding the roles of long axial length and age, our hypothesis aligns with findings by Ascaso et al. [18], suggesting that older individuals and those with long axial lengths are more likely to experience zonular instability. This instability can lead to increased iris-uveal contact and, consequently, elevated intraocular pressure (IOP). TID has been described as a feature of UGH syndrome in a number of previous studies [19–23], but mostly in case reports. Some studies also reported that TID may not be a feature of UGH syndrome [8, 9]. Our study is the first study aiming to demonstrate the association between TID and increasing IOP in IDAU using a nested case control design, and our study’s results confirm that TID was not associated with increasing IOP in IDAU. Recent studies [3] have demonstrated significant associations between an IOP ≥ 22 mmHg at the time of first intraocular hemorrhage and the subsequent need for IOP-lowering therapy in UGH syndrome. In the present study, we were unable to assess this relationship because only two case subjects had vitreous haemorrhage. Given that IOL malposition emerged as the strongest independent risk factor, although with a wide confidence interval, these findings are hypothesis-generating and suggest that closer postoperative attention to IOL stability may be warranted. In patients at higher risk of IOL instability — particularly those of older age and with axial length > 25 mm — meticulous slit-lamp examination at each postoperative visit to confirm IOL positioning, together with periodic IOP monitoring, may be reasonable. When device-associated intraocular complications are suspected, management should be individualised to the mechanism of uveal-IOL contact, severity of IOP elevation, surgical risk, and patient preference. These suggestions should be regarded as provisional and require confirmation in larger prospective studies before being adopted as formal risk-stratification criteria.

The temporal relationship between intraocular inflammation and IOP elevation also warrants consideration. In our cohort, anterior uveitis preceded elevated IOP in all case subjects, a sequence consistent with inflammation-driven or mechanically-driven trabecular dysfunction rather than IOP elevation arising in isolation. Nevertheless, elevated IOP in this setting is mechanistically heterogeneous and may reflect mechanical trabecular trauma from the malpositioned device, liberation of pigment with secondary trabecular obstruction, chronic angle compromise, inflammatory trabeculitis, or a corticosteroid response to topical anti-inflammatory therapy. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and may coexist within the same eye. Importantly, the CU²C database did not record the timing, route, or duration of corticosteroid exposure, nor the precise date of uveitis onset relative to IOP elevation; consequently, we were unable to quantify inflammatory severity or to separate steroid-induced ocular hypertension from other contributing mechanisms. The observation that all cases of elevated IOP were controlled with IOP-lowering medication, rather than resolving on withdrawal of anti-inflammatory therapy alone, is more in keeping with a mechanical or pigmentary contribution than with a pure steroid response, but this remains an indirect inference. Prospective studies that systematically capture corticosteroid exposure, anterior chamber inflammatory grading, and the temporal sequence of clinical events will be required to disentangle these mechanisms.

This study has several notable strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the first nested case-control study to investigate risk factors for elevated IOP in IDAU, representing a methodological advance over prior case reports and case series. The nested design — drawing both cases and controls from the same prospective cohort — substantially reduces selection bias and enables simultaneous evaluation of multiple candidate risk factors. Second, all diagnoses were reviewed and validated by a retina-uveitis specialist, enhancing diagnostic rigour. Third, we systematically assessed all candidate variables for multicollinearity prior to modelling, confirming the independence of our predictor variables and supporting the validity of the final multivariate model.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, diagnoses were made clinically by multiple residents and fellows, raising the possibility of diagnostic variability; however, all cases were reviewed and confirmed by a retina-uveitis specialist. Second, because most patients were referred from other institutions, the precise dates of clinical onset (uveitis, glaucoma, hyphema) were frequently unavailable, precluding time-to-event analyses. Third, and most importantly for mechanistic interpretation, the CU²C database did not record corticosteroid exposure (timing, route, or duration), anterior chamber inflammatory grading, or the precise temporal sequence of uveitis onset relative to IOP elevation. Patients receiving corticosteroid therapy were therefore not excluded, and steroid-induced ocular hypertension could not be distinguished from mechanical, pigmentary, or inflammatory mechanisms of IOP elevation. Elevated IOP in this cohort should accordingly be regarded as mechanistically heterogeneous. Fourth, although the term ‘malpositioned IOL’ was defined by anatomical position — lens decentration, tilt, or subluxation/dislocation confirmed on slit-lamp examination, irrespective of IOL type or site of placement — the dataset did not permit reliable quantification of the type or degree of malposition, which was therefore analysed as a single binary variable. Fifth, misclassification bias cannot be excluded: some subjects may have been misdiagnosed with device-associated inflammation when viral-associated anterior uveitis was the true aetiology, given overlapping clinical presentations, and anterior chamber paracentesis was not performed in all cases. Sixth, an age difference of approximately 18 years was observed between the case (67.8 years) and control groups (49.2 years). Rather than representing a sampling artefact, this gap likely reflects a genuine biological phenomenon: older patients are more susceptible to age-related zonular weakening and progressive IOL instability, which in turn predispose to IOL malposition and elevated IOP. This interpretation is consistent with the mechanisms proposed by Ascaso et al. and is supported by our multivariate analysis, in which age remained an independent predictor after adjusting for IOL malposition and axial length; nonetheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Seventh, the wide 95% confidence intervals observed for long axial length (1.18–55.16) and IOL malposition (1.25–54.76) reflect the limited statistical power inherent to the sample size; point estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating pending validation in larger cohorts. The relatively small case group (n = 30) reflects the rarity of this condition and is comparable to, or larger than, most prior studies. Finally, as a single-centre tertiary referral study, the findings may not be fully generalisable to other settings. Despite these limitations, this study provides previously unavailable comparative data that may inform the design of larger prospective studies and guide clinical awareness among retina, uveitis, and general ophthalmologists. Future prospective studies should systematically capture corticosteroid exposure, inflammatory severity, and the temporal sequence of clinical events, and should employ stratified analyses to evaluate the independent contribution of each factor.

Conclusion

In this retrospective nested case-control study of intraocular device-associated intraocular complications, IOL malposition (AOR 8.30, 95% CI 1.25–54.76), long axial length (> 25 mm; AOR 8.08, 95% CI 1.18–55.16), and older age (AOR 1.18 per year, 95% CI 1.07–1.31) were independently associated with elevated IOP. Given the modest sample size and the wide confidence intervals of these estimates, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive risk-stratification criteria. They nonetheless identify candidate factors that may merit closer postoperative attention and provide a rationale for larger, prospectively designed studies. Future work should incorporate corticosteroid exposure, the temporal sequence of clinical events, inflammatory severity, and intraocular device-specific design characteristics, and should employ larger multicentre cohorts to validate these associations and clarify the mechanisms underlying device-associated ocular hypertension.
