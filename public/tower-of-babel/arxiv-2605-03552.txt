1
Introduction

Shannon’s work on noiseless coding already included sources with memory and finite-state structure
[
3
]
; see also the historical discussion in
[
2
]
. In the classical source-independent setting, Kraft’s inequality and McMillan’s theorem lead to the standard lower bound of expected code length by entropy
[
5
,
4
,
6
,
7
]
. For constrained sources, however, one must distinguish between different notions of decodability. A binary assignment may fail to be uniquely decodable in the usual concatenation-based sense and yet still be perfectly invertible on the set of strings that the source can actually generate. Dalai and Leonardi made this point explicit in
[
8
]
.

Once one adopts injectivity on the admissible source language as the basic requirement, a natural finite-block comparison problem emerges. Given two injective source codes
C
and
\widetilde{C}
for the same source, one may compare them through the entire sequence

\bigl(\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1}^{n})|]\bigr)_{n\geq 1},

and ask whether one code dominates the other in a Pareto-type sense: does it satisfy

\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1}^{n})|]\leq\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{C}(X_{1}^{n})|]\qquad\text{for every }n\geq 1,

with strict inequality for at least one blocklength? This viewpoint is especially natural in constrained-source coding, where reversibility depends on the source law and where finite-block effects need not be visible from entropy alone.

The four-symbol Markov source exhibited by Dalai and Leonardi in
[
8
, Sec. II]
provides a particularly clean test case for this question. Their reversible, source-aware binary encoding has expected length exactly
3n/2
on the first
n
source symbols, while the block entropy equals
3n/2+1/2
. The example is elementary, but significant: it shows that source constraints can create finite-block gains that are invisible if one insists on a source-independent reading of unique decodability. It also suggests a sharper question: is the Dalai–Leonardi reversible code Pareto-optimal with respect to the sequence of finite-block average lengths, or can one build another injective source code that never performs worse and performs strictly better somewhere?

This question is also related in spirit to the literature on one-to-one codes, where injectivity is studied without requiring the full prefix or uniquely decodable structure used in the classical theory. A useful reference point is the lower bound of Alon and Orlitsky on the expected length of one-to-one codes
[
9
]
. Our setting is different because the admissible source language is constrained by a Markov law, but the same general perspective remains relevant: once injectivity rather than source-independent unique decodability becomes the baseline requirement, one can ask for finer finite-block comparisons between reversible constructions.

In this paper we answer the above question for the Dalai–Leonardi source. We define a canonical injective block code on the whole admissible language
\mathcal{A}
: admissible strings are ordered by increasing information cost

K(u):=-\log_{2}\mathbb{P}(X_{1}^{m}=u),

then by length and lexicographic order, and are mapped to nonempty binary strings in shortlex order. We then analyze the expected length of this code on source blocks of length
n
.

Our first result is the uniform comparison

\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1}^{n})|]\leq\frac{3}{2}n\qquad(n\geq 1),

with equality only at
n=1
. Thus the code never performs worse, in average length, than the reversible construction of
[
8
]
, and it is strictly better at every blocklength
n\geq 2
. More precisely, we prove the quantitative asymptotic gain

\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1}^{n})|]\leq\frac{3}{2}n-\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}

for every fixed
c<\sqrt{2}/(18\sqrt{\pi})
and all sufficiently large
n
. In particular, the Dalai–Leonardi benchmark is not Pareto-optimal for this source in the finite-block sense above.

The paper is self-contained. We begin by recalling the source and its basic dyadic structure, then turn to the combinatorial organization of admissible strings by information cost. The core of the argument is a shortlex gap identity showing that each cost class splits exactly into two equal parts, one receiving code length
K(u)-1
and the other receiving code length
K(u)
. This leads to a one-bit saving with probability at least
1/2
at every blocklength, and with probability strictly larger than
1/2
for every blocklength
n\geq 2
. A refined central estimate then gives the explicit
n^{-1/2}
improvement stated above.

2
The Markov source

We now specialize to the four-symbol constrained Markov source that appears as the “meaningful example” in
[
8
, Sec. II]
. Let
\mathcal{X}=\{A,B,C,D\}
and consider the first-order Markov source
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
with

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=x)=\frac{1}{4},\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},

and transitions

A\to\{A,C\}\ (1/2,1/2),\qquad B\to\{B,D\}\ (1/2,1/2),\qquad C,D\to\mathcal{X}\ \text{uniform}.

A finite string
x_{1}^{n}
is
admissible
if all transitions
x_{i}\to x_{i+1}
are allowed. Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{X}^{+}

be the set of admissible nonempty finite strings.

We begin with a simple structural fact that will be used repeatedly in the sequel.

Lemma 1

(Uniform marginals)
.

For every
i\geq 1
and every
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=x)=1/4
.

Proof.

The uniform distribution
\pi=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)
is invariant: from
A
(resp.
B
) mass
1/4
splits equally to
A,C
(resp.
B,D
),
and from
C,D
mass
1/2
is sent uniformly to all four symbols. Hence
\pi P=\pi
. Since
X_{1}\sim\pi
, induction gives
X_{i}\sim\pi
for all
i
.
∎

The next step is to record the dyadic form of all admissible probabilities and the resulting information-cost formula.

From
A,B
there are two equiprobable next symbols (cost
1
bit), from
C,D
there are four equiprobable next symbols (cost
2
bits). Hence every admissible block has dyadic probability.

Definition 1

(Information cost)
.

For an admissible block
x_{1}^{n}
define

K(x_{1}^{n}):=-\log_{2}\mathbb{P}(X_{1}^{n}=x_{1}^{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

For the present source, letting

M(x_{1}^{n}):=\#\{\,i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}:x_{i}\in\{C,D\}\,\},

we have

K(x_{1}^{n})=2+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\Bigl(\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}\in\{A,B\}\}}+2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}\in\{C,D\}\}}\Bigr)=(n+1)+M(x_{1}^{n}).

(1)

3
The shortlex code on admissible strings

We now introduce the canonical injective code studied throughout the paper and collect the combinatorial facts that govern its behavior.

Let
\prec_{\mathrm{bin}}
be the shortlex order on nonempty binary strings: first by length, then lexicographic. On admissible strings we use the order
\prec_{\mathcal{X}}
: first by increasing cost
K(\cdot)
, then by increasing length, then lexicographic over
A<B<C<D
.

Definition 2

(Injective source code on
\mathcal{A}
)
.

A mapping
C:\mathcal{A}\to\{0,1\}^{+}
is called
injective
if it is one-to-one on
\mathcal{A}
.
This guarantees unique one-shot decoding on admissible source strings. We do not claim any concatenation-based
unique decodability in the classical sense.

Definition 3

(Shortlex source code)
.

Enumerate
\mathcal{A}
as
(u_{1},u_{2},\dots)
in the order
\prec_{\mathcal{X}}
.
Enumerate nonempty binary strings as
(b_{1},b_{2},\dots)
in the order
\prec_{\mathrm{bin}}
.
Define
C(u_{j})=b_{j}
for
j\geq 1
.

By construction
C
is injective on
\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 1

(Numerical check for small
n
)
.

By computing the ranking and the shortlex assignment explicitly, one obtains for the Markov source defined in the previous section:

\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1}^{2})|]=\frac{23}{8}=2.875<3=\frac{3}{2}\cdot 2,\qquad\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1}^{3})|]=\frac{71}{16}=4.4375<\frac{9}{2}=\frac{3}{2}\cdot 3.

We next count admissible strings by information cost. This will provide the basic input for understanding how the shortlex assignment behaves inside each cost class.

Let

S_{k}:=\#\{u\in\mathcal{A}:\ K(u)=k\},\qquad k\geq 2.

Lemma 2

(Cost-class recurrence and closed form)
.

S_{2}=S_{3}=4
and for all
k\geq 4
,

S_{k}=S_{k-1}+2S_{k-2}.

Moreover,

S_{k}=\frac{2^{k+1}+4(-1)^{k}}{3}\qquad(k\geq 2).

Proof.

Refine the count by the last symbol. Let
a_{k}
be the number of admissible strings of cost
k
ending in
\{A,B\}
, and
c_{k}
those ending in
\{C,D\}
. Then
S_{k}=a_{k}+c_{k}
.

To form a cost-
k
string ending in
\{A,B\}
:

•

if the penultimate symbol is in
\{A,B\}
, the last step costs
1
bit and the last symbol in
\{A,B\}
is forced (A
\to
A, B
\to
B), so we get
a_{k-1}
strings;

•

if the penultimate symbol is in
\{C,D\}
, the last step costs
2
bits and the last symbol can be chosen in
\{A,B\}
in
2
ways, so we get
2c_{k-2}
strings.

Hence
a_{k}=a_{k-1}+2c_{k-2}
. By the same reasoning (A
\to
C and B
\to
D forced; from
C,D
two choices to land in
\{C,D\}
), also
c_{k}=a_{k-1}+2c_{k-2}
. With
a_{2}=c_{2}=2
, it follows by induction that
a_{k}=c_{k}
for all
k
, and therefore

S_{k}=2a_{k},\qquad a_{k}=a_{k-1}+2a_{k-2}.

Multiplying by
2
gives
S_{k}=S_{k-1}+2S_{k-2}
for
k\geq 4
.

The characteristic equation
\lambda^{2}=\lambda+2
has roots
2
and
-1
, so
S_{k}=\alpha 2^{k}+\beta(-1)^{k}
.
Matching
S_{2}=S_{3}=4
gives
\alpha=2/3
and
\beta=4/3
, i.e.
S_{k}=(2^{k+1}+4(-1)^{k})/3
.
∎

Once the size of each cost class is known, one can compare it with the number of short binary strings available before that class begins. This is the content of the next identity.

Let

T_{<k}:=\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}S_{j}

be the number of admissible strings with cost
<k
.

Lemma 3

(Exact gap identity)
.

For every
k\geq 2
,

(2^{k}-2)-T_{<k}=\frac{S_{k}}{2}.

Proof.

Set
G_{k}:=(2^{k}-2)-T_{<k}
and
H_{k}:=G_{k}-\frac{S_{k}}{2}
.
Since
T_{<k+1}=T_{<k}+S_{k}
,

H_{k+1}-H_{k}=2^{k}-S_{k}-\frac{S_{k+1}-S_{k}}{2}.

Using the closed form from
Lemma

2
,

S_{k}=\frac{2^{k+1}+4(-1)^{k}}{3},\qquad S_{k+1}=\frac{2^{k+2}-4(-1)^{k}}{3},

we obtain
H_{k+1}-H_{k}=0
. Thus
(H_{k})
is constant for
k\geq 2
.
For
k=2
,
T_{<2}=0
and
S_{2}=4
, hence
H_{2}=(2^{2}-2)-0-4/2=0
. Therefore
H_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 2
.
∎

We can now identify the only two possible code lengths inside a fixed cost class and the exact proportion in which they occur.

Proposition 1

(Length dichotomy)
.

For any admissible string
u
with
K(u)=k
,

|C(u)|\in\{k-1,k\}.

Moreover, within each cost class
k
, exactly
S_{k}/2
strings receive length
k-1
and exactly
S_{k}/2
receive length
k
.

Proof.

Before cost class
k
starts, exactly
T_{<k}
admissible strings have been encoded, hence the first
T_{<k}
binary strings in shortlex have been used.

(i) All codewords of length
\leq k-2
are already used.

There are
2^{k-1}-2
nonempty binary strings of length
\leq k-2
.
By
Lemma

3
,

T_{<k}=2^{k}-2-\frac{S_{k}}{2}.

Using
Lemma

2
, for every
k\geq 2
we have
S_{k}\leq 2^{k}
(indeed
(2^{k+1}+4)/3\leq 2^{k}
since
4\leq 2^{k}
).
Thus
T_{<k}\geq 2^{k-1}-2
, so all binary strings of length
\leq k-2
are among the first
T_{<k}
and are already used.

(ii) No codeword of length
\geq k
is used before class
k
.

Again by
Lemma

3
,
T_{<k}=2^{k}-2-\frac{S_{k}}{2}<2^{k}-2
, so not all binary strings of length
\leq k-1

have been exhausted; hence no length-
k
word can have appeared yet in shortlex.

Therefore, when class
k
begins, the remaining unused codewords of length
\leq k-1
are precisely some of the length-
(k-1)
codewords, and their number is

(2^{k}-2)-T_{<k}=\frac{S_{k}}{2}

by
Lemma

3
. Assigning codewords in shortlex inside the class, exactly
S_{k}/2
strings get length
k-1
and the remaining
S_{k}/2
strings get the next available length, namely
k
.
∎

4
A one-bit saving bound

We now turn to the probabilistic part of the argument. The goal of this section is to show that the shortlex construction saves one bit with probability at least
1/2
at every blocklength, and with strictly larger probability as soon as
n\geq 2
.

Fix
n\geq 1
and define

K_{n}:=K(X_{1}^{n}),\qquad L_{n}:=|C(X_{1}^{n})|.

By
Proposition

1
,
L_{n}\in\{K_{n}-1,K_{n}\}
. Define the saving indicator

I_{n}:=\mathbf{1}\{L_{n}=K_{n}-1\}.

Then
L_{n}=K_{n}-I_{n}
and therefore

\mathbb{E}[L_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[K_{n}]-\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1).

(2)

For this source,

\mathbb{E}[K_{n}]=H(X_{1}^{n})=\frac{3}{2}n+\frac{1}{2},

so it remains to lower bound the saving probability
\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1)
.

A first simplification is that the random cost parameter can be encoded by a binomial statistic.

Let

X:=M(X_{1}^{n})=\#\{i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}:X_{i}\in\{C,D\}\}.

Define
Y_{i}:=\mathbf{1}\{X_{i}\in\{C,D\}\}
. For every
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{i+1}\in\{C,D\}\mid X_{i}=x)=\frac{1}{2},

hence
\mathbb{P}(Y_{i+1}=1\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{i})=1/2
and
(Y_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is i.i.d. Bernoulli
(1/2)
. Therefore

X=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}Y_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,\tfrac{1}{2}),\qquad p_{n}(x):=\mathbb{P}(X=x)=\binom{n-1}{x}2^{-(n-1)}.

By (
1
),

K_{n}=(n+1)+X.

To translate this observation into a statement about code lengths, we need to count how many admissible strings have a given length and cost.

For integers
k\geq 2
and
\ell\geq 1
, define

N(k,\ell):=\#\{x_{1}^{\ell}\in\mathcal{A}:K(x_{1}^{\ell})=k\}.

Lemma 4

(Closed form for
N(k,\ell)
)
.

For any
\ell\geq 1
and any
j\in\{0,\dots,\ell-1\}
, the number of admissible strings
x_{1}^{\ell}
with

\#\{i\in\{1,\dots,\ell-1\}:x_{i}\in\{C,D\}\}=j

equals

4\binom{\ell-1}{j}2^{j}.

Consequently,

N(k,\ell)=4\binom{\ell-1}{k-\ell-1}2^{k-\ell-1},

(3)

with the convention that this is
0
when
k-\ell-1\notin\{0,\dots,\ell-1\}
.

Proof.

Fix
\ell\geq 1
and a subset
J\subset\{1,\dots,\ell-1\}
with
|J|=j
. We count admissible strings whose indices with
x_{i}\in\{C,D\}
are exactly
J
.

First choose the class of
x_{1}
(either
\{A,B\}
or
\{C,D\}
):
2
choices. For each
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell-2\}
:

•

if
i\notin J
, then
x_{i}\in\{A,B\}
and once the class of
x_{i+1}
is fixed (by whether
i+1\in J
), the transition forces
x_{i+1}
inside that class;

•

if
i\in J
, then
x_{i}\in\{C,D\}
and the next class is again fixed by membership of
i+1
in
J
, but from
C
or
D
there are exactly
2
admissible choices inside the prescribed class.

For the final step
i=\ell-1
, if
\ell-1\notin J
there are
2
choices for
x_{\ell}
inside the forced class, and if
\ell-1\in J
there are
4
choices. In either case, the total number of strings compatible with a fixed
J
equals
4\cdot 2^{j}
. Summing over the
\binom{\ell-1}{j}
possible subsets
J
gives the first claim.

Finally, since
K(x_{1}^{\ell})=(\ell+1)+j
by (
1
), we have
j=k-\ell-1
, which yields (
3
).
∎

For later use, write

U(m,j):=\binom{m-j}{j}2^{j},\qquad U(m,j)=0\ \text{if }j<0\text{ or }2j>m.

Then (
3
) can be rewritten as

N(k,\ell)=4U(k-2,k-\ell-1).

We now isolate the number of short codewords that are still available when the shortlex order reaches the length-
n
slice of a fixed cost class.

Fix
n\geq 2
and a cost class
k
. The number of cost-
k
strings of length
<n
is

W_{<n}(k):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}N(k,\ell).

By
Proposition

1
, within cost class
k
there are exactly
S_{k}/2
available short codewords of length
k-1
. Therefore, when the encoding reaches the length-
n
slice inside cost class
k
, the number of remaining short codewords equals

V_{n}(k):=\frac{S_{k}}{2}-W_{<n}(k).

(4)

The next lemma packages this observation into an explicit conditional probability formula.

Lemma 5

(Conditional saving formula)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
x\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, and set
k:=n+1+x
. Define

g_{n}(x):=\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1\mid X=x).

Then

g_{n}(x)=\min\!\left\{1,\max\!\left\{0,\frac{V_{n}(k)}{N(k,n)}\right\}\right\}.

(5)

Consequently,

\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1)=\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}p_{n}(x)g_{n}(x),\qquad p_{n}(x)=\binom{n-1}{x}2^{-(n-1)}.

(6)

Proof.

Condition on
X=x
. Then
K_{n}=(n+1)+x=k
. All admissible strings in this event form exactly the length-
n
slice of cost
k
, whose cardinality is
N(k,n)
. Within the whole cost class
k
, exactly
S_{k}/2
strings receive short codewords (length
k-1
). Since the order
\prec_{\mathcal{X}}
lists shorter source lengths first, by the time we begin encoding the length-
n
slice we have already encoded exactly the
W_{<n}(k)
strings of lengths
\ell<n
in the same cost class. Hence the number of short codewords still available for the length-
n
slice is
V_{n}(k)
as in (
4
).

If
V_{n}(k)\leq 0
, no short codeword remains, so
g_{n}(x)=0
. If
V_{n}(k)\geq N(k,n)
, there are enough short codewords to cover the entire slice, so
g_{n}(x)=1
. Otherwise
0<V_{n}(k)<N(k,n)
, and exactly
V_{n}(k)
out of the
N(k,n)
strings in the slice get a short codeword, so
g_{n}(x)=V_{n}(k)/N(k,n)
. This is exactly (
5
). Averaging over
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,1/2)
gives (
6
).
∎

The rest of the proof separates two regimes: a right-tail regime where all strings are saved, and a central regime where one needs a more delicate oscillatory estimate.

Lemma 6

(Tail saturation)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
x\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
. If

x\geq\frac{n+1}{2},

then

g_{n}(x)=1.

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 2
and
x\geq(n+1)/2
, and set

y:=n-1-x,\qquad k:=n+1+x,\qquad m:=k-2=n+x-1=x+2y.

Using
N(k,\ell)=4U(k-2,k-\ell-1)
, we obtain

N(k,n)=4U(m,x),\qquad W_{<n}(k)=4\sum_{j\geq x+1}U(m,j),\qquad S_{k}=4\sum_{j\geq 0}U(m,j).

Hence

V_{n}(k)-N(k,n)=2\left(\sum_{j=0}^{x-1}U(m,j)-\sum_{j\geq x+1}U(m,j)\right).

Therefore it suffices to prove

\sum_{j=0}^{x-1}U(m,j)\geq\sum_{j\geq x+1}U(m,j).

(7)

Since
m=x+2y
, the support of
j\mapsto U(m,j)
is

0\leq j\leq\Bigl\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\Bigr\rfloor=x+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{y}{2}\Bigr\rfloor,

so the right-hand side of (
7
) equals

\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor y/2\rfloor}U(m,x+r).

We compare these terms with the left tail via

R_{r}:=\frac{U(m,x-1-r)}{U(m,x+r)}\qquad\Bigl(0\leq r\leq\Bigl\lfloor\frac{y}{2}\Bigr\rfloor\Bigr).

First,

R_{0}=\frac{U(m,x-1)}{U(m,x)}=\frac{x(x+y+1)}{2(y+2)(y+1)}>1,

because
x\geq y+2
.
Next, for
0\leq r<\lfloor y/2\rfloor
, a direct computation gives

\frac{R_{r+1}}{R_{r}}=\frac{(x+y-r)(x-r-1)(x+r+1)(x+y+r+2)}{4(y-2r)(y-2r-1)(y+2r+3)(y+2r+4)}.

The right-hand side is increasing in
x
, so it is bounded below by its value at the smallest admissible value
x=y+2
. Therefore

\frac{R_{r+1}}{R_{r}}\geq\frac{(2y+2-r)(y-r+1)(y+r+3)(2y+r+4)}{4(y-2r)(y-2r-1)(y+2r+3)(y+2r+4)}.

Split this lower bound as

\frac{(y-r+1)(y+r+3)}{(y-2r)(y+2r+4)}\cdot\frac{(2y+2-r)(2y+r+4)}{4(y-2r-1)(y+2r+3)}.

Both factors are strictly larger than
1
, because

(y-r+1)(y+r+3)-(y-2r)(y+2r+4)=3(r+1)^{2}>0,

and

(2y+2-r)(2y+r+4)-4(y-2r-1)(y+2r+3)=15r^{2}+30r+4y+20>0.

Hence
R_{r+1}>R_{r}
, and therefore
R_{r}\geq R_{0}>1
for every admissible
r
. Thus

U(m,x-1-r)\geq U(m,x+r)\qquad\Bigl(0\leq r\leq\Bigl\lfloor\frac{y}{2}\Bigr\rfloor\Bigr).

Summing over
r
gives

\sum_{j=0}^{x-1}U(m,j)\geq\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor y/2\rfloor}U(m,x-1-r)\geq\sum_{r=1}^{\lfloor y/2\rfloor}U(m,x+r),

which is exactly (
7
). Therefore
V_{n}(k)\geq N(k,n)
and
Lemma

5
yields
g_{n}(x)=1
.
∎

To handle the remaining values near the center, we introduce a family of alternating sums that captures the residual imbalance inside the relevant cost classes.

For
t\geq 1
, define

C_{t}:=U(3t,t)=\binom{2t}{t}2^{t},

and

D_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}U(3t,j)-\sum_{j\geq t+1}U(3t,j).

Lemma 7

(Alternating-sum estimates)
.

For all
t\geq 1
,

D_{t}=C_{t-1}-C_{t-2}+C_{t-3}-\cdots+(-1)^{t-1}C_{0},

and in particular

0<D_{t}<\frac{1}{2}C_{t}.

Moreover, if

B_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}U(3t-1,j)-\sum_{j\geq t+1}U(3t-1,j),

then

B_{t}=\frac{1}{2}C_{t}-D_{t},\qquad 0<B_{t}<\frac{1}{2}C_{t}.

Finally,

D(z):=\sum_{t\geq 1}D_{t}z^{t}=\frac{z}{(1+z)\sqrt{1-8z}}.

Proof.

Set

P_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}U(3t,j),\qquad T_{t}:=\sum_{j\geq 0}U(3t,j),

so that

D_{t}=2P_{t}+C_{t}-T_{t}.

(8)

First,
T_{t}=S_{3t+2}/4
, so by
Lemma

2
,

T_{t}=\frac{2^{3t+3}+4(-1)^{t}}{12}=\frac{2\cdot 8^{t}+(-1)^{t}}{3},\qquad T(z):=\sum_{t\geq 1}T_{t}z^{t}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{16z}{1-8z}-\frac{z}{1+z}\right).

Next,

C(z):=\sum_{t\geq 1}C_{t}z^{t}=\sum_{t\geq 1}\binom{2t}{t}(2z)^{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-8z}}-1.

To compute
P(z):=\sum_{t\geq 1}P_{t}z^{t}
, use constant terms:

U(3t,j)=\CT_{x}\!\left((1+x)^{3t}\Bigl(\frac{2}{x(1+x)}\Bigr)^{j}\right).

Summing first over
t
and then over
j
gives

P(z)=\CT_{x}\left(\frac{z\,x(1+x)^{3}}{(1-z(1+x)^{3})\bigl(x-2z(1+x)^{2}\bigr)}\right).

To evaluate
P(z)
, we use a standard coefficient form of the Lagrange–Bürmann inversion formula; see, for instance,
[
1
, Sec. 2.1]
(and also
[
1
, Sec. 4.1]
for its residue interpretation). Let
f=f(u)
be the unique formal power series satisfying

f=u\,\phi(f),\qquad\phi(x):=2z(1+x)^{2},

and let

F(x):=\frac{z(1+x)^{3}}{1-z(1+x)^{3}}.

Then Lagrange inversion gives, for every
n\geq 0
,

[u^{n}]\frac{F(f(u))}{1-u\phi^{\prime}(f(u))}=[x^{n}]\bigl(F(x)\phi(x)^{n}\bigr).

Summing over
n\geq 0
and using

\sum_{n\geq 0}[x^{n}]\bigl(F(x)\phi(x)^{n}\bigr)=\CT_{x}\!\left(F(x)\sum_{n\geq 0}\Bigl(\frac{\phi(x)}{x}\Bigr)^{n}\right)=\CT_{x}\!\left(\frac{x}{x-\phi(x)}F(x)\right),

we obtain

\sum_{n\geq 0}[u^{n}]\frac{F(f(u))}{1-u\phi^{\prime}(f(u))}=\CT_{x}\!\left(\frac{x}{x-\phi(x)}F(x)\right).

Now set
u=1
and write
X:=f(1)
. Since
f=u\phi(f)
, the series
X
is characterized by

X=\phi(X),\qquad X(0)=0.

Therefore

\CT_{x}\!\left(\frac{x}{x-\phi(x)}F(x)\right)=\frac{F(X)}{1-\phi^{\prime}(X)}.

In our case this gives

P(z)=\frac{F(X)}{1-\phi^{\prime}(X)},\qquad X=\phi(X)=2z(1+X)^{2}.

Solving the quadratic equation gives

X(z)=\frac{1-4z-\sqrt{1-8z}}{4z},

and substituting into the previous expression yields

P(z)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-8z}-2z}{2(1+z)(1-8z)}.

Combining with (
8
),

D(z)=2P(z)+C(z)-T(z)=\frac{z}{(1+z)\sqrt{1-8z}}.

Since

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-8z}}=\sum_{t\geq 0}C_{t}z^{t},

we obtain

D(z)=z\Bigl(\sum_{s\geq 0}(-1)^{s}z^{s}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{r\geq 0}C_{r}z^{r}\Bigr),

and coefficient extraction gives

D_{t}=C_{t-1}-C_{t-2}+C_{t-3}-\cdots+(-1)^{t-1}C_{0}.

Grouping in pairs shows
D_{t}>0
for all
t\geq 1
. Also,

\frac{C_{t}}{C_{t-1}}=2\frac{\binom{2t}{t}}{\binom{2t-2}{t-1}}=8-\frac{4}{t}>2,

so
D_{t}<C_{t-1}<C_{t}/2
for
t\geq 2
, while
D_{1}=1<C_{1}/2=2
.

Finally, the same constant-term computation for
B_{t}
gives

B(z):=\sum_{t\geq 1}B_{t}z^{t}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1-z}{(1+z)\sqrt{1-8z}}-1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(C(z)-2D(z)\bigr),

whence
B_{t}=C_{t}/2-D_{t}
. Since
0<D_{t}<C_{t}/2
, we conclude that
0<B_{t}<C_{t}/2
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main probabilistic estimate from which the average-length bounds follow.

Lemma 8

(Key lemma)
.

For every
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1)\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Moreover, the inequality is strict for every
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

For
n=1
we have
K_{1}=2
and the four symbols are equiprobable; by
Proposition

1
exactly two receive length
1=K_{1}-1
, hence
\mathbb{P}(I_{1}=1)=1/2
.

Assume now
n\geq 2
. By
Lemma

5
,

\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1)=\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}p_{n}(x)g_{n}(x),\qquad p_{n}(x)=\binom{n-1}{x}2^{-(n-1)}.

We pair
x
with
n-1-x
.

Odd case:
n=2t+1
(
t\geq 1
).

For
x<t
, its partner
y=2t-x
satisfies
y\geq t+1>(2t+2)/2
, hence
g_{n}(y)=1
by
Lemma

6
. For the central value
x=t
, we have
k=3t+2
,

W_{<n}(k)=4\sum_{j\geq t+1}U(3t,j),\qquad\frac{S_{k}}{2}=2\sum_{j\geq 0}U(3t,j),\qquad N(k,n)=4C_{t}.

Therefore

V_{n}(k)=2\left(C_{t}+D_{t}\right),

so by
Lemma

5
and
Lemma

7
,

g_{n}(t)=\frac{V_{n}(k)}{N(k,n)}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{D_{t}}{2C_{t}}\in\Bigl(\frac{1}{2},1\Bigr).

Hence

\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1)\geq\sum_{x=0}^{t-1}p_{n}(x)+p_{n}(t)g_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2}+p_{n}(t)\frac{D_{t}}{2C_{t}}>\frac{1}{2}.

Even case:
n=2t
(
t\geq 1
).

For
x\leq t-2
, its partner
y=2t-1-x
satisfies
y\geq t+1>(2t+1)/2
, hence
g_{n}(y)=1
by
Lemma

6
. Only the adjacent pair
(t-1,t)
remains.

For
x=t
, we have
k=3t+1
, and
Lemma

7
gives

W_{<n}(k)=4\sum_{j\geq t+1}U(3t-1,j),\qquad N(k,n)=4U(3t-1,t)=2C_{t},

so

g_{2t}(t)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{B_{t}}{C_{t}}=1-\frac{D_{t}}{C_{t}}\in\Bigl(\frac{1}{2},1\Bigr).

For
x=t-1
, set

A_{t}:=\sum_{j=0}^{t-2}U(3t-2,j)-\sum_{j\geq t}U(3t-2,j).

Using the identity
U(m+1,j)=U(m,j)+2U(m-1,j-1)
and summing over the defining ranges gives

D_{t}=2A_{t}+B_{t}.

Since
N(3t,2t)=4U(3t-2,t-1)=C_{t}
, we obtain

g_{2t}(t-1)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2A_{t}}{C_{t}}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{D_{t}-B_{t}}{C_{t}}=\frac{2D_{t}}{C_{t}}\in(0,1).

Therefore

g_{2t}(t-1)+g_{2t}(t)=1+\frac{D_{t}}{C_{t}}>1.

Using
p_{n}(t-1)=p_{n}(t)
, we conclude that

\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1)\geq\sum_{x=0}^{t-2}p_{n}(x)+p_{n}(t)\bigl(g_{2t}(t-1)+g_{2t}(t)\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}+p_{n}(t)\frac{D_{t}}{C_{t}}>\frac{1}{2}.

This proves both the lower bound and strictness for every
n\geq 2
.
∎

The average-length consequences are now immediate.

Corollary 1

(Uniform benchmark improvement)
.

For every
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1}^{n})|]\leq\frac{3}{2}\,n,

with equality if and only if
n=1
. Equivalently,

\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1})|]=\frac{3}{2},\qquad\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1}^{n})|]<\frac{3}{2}\,n\quad(n\geq 2).

Proof.

Equation (
2
) gives

\mathbb{E}[L_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[K_{n}]-\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1)=\frac{3}{2}n+\frac{1}{2}-\mathbb{P}(I_{n}=1).

Now apply
Lemma

8
.
∎

A slightly more careful reading of the proof already yields the first-order asymptotic gain.

Corollary 2

(Asymptotic
n^{-1/2}
improvement)
.

Let
L_{n}:=|C(X_{1}^{n})|
. Then, as
t\to\infty
,

\frac{3}{2}(2t+1)-\mathbb{E}[L_{2t+1}]\geq\Bigl(\frac{1}{18\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1)\Bigr)t^{-1/2}=\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{18\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1)\Bigr)(2t+1)^{-1/2},

and

\frac{3}{2}(2t)-\mathbb{E}[L_{2t}]\geq\Bigl(\frac{1}{9\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1)\Bigr)t^{-1/2}=\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{9\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1)\Bigr)(2t)^{-1/2}.

In particular, for every fixed

0<c<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{18\sqrt{\pi}}

there exists
n_{0}(c)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}(c)
,

\mathbb{E}[|C(X_{1}^{n})|]\leq\frac{3}{2}\,n-\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof.

Let

q_{t}:=\binom{2t}{t}4^{-t}.

Then

p_{2t+1}(t)=q_{t},\qquad p_{2t}(t)=\binom{2t-1}{t}2^{-(2t-1)}=q_{t}.

By Stirling’s formula,

q_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\,(1+o(1)).

Moreover,
Lemma

7
gives

\frac{D_{t}}{C_{t}}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}(-1)^{j-1}\frac{C_{t-j}}{C_{t}}\longrightarrow\sum_{j\geq 1}(-1)^{j-1}8^{-j}=\frac{1}{9},

because for each fixed
j
,
C_{t-j}/C_{t}\to 8^{-j}
, and the summands are dominated by a geometric sequence.

In the odd case, the proof of
Lemma

8
gives

\mathbb{P}(I_{2t+1}=1)\geq\frac{1}{2}+q_{t}\frac{D_{t}}{2C_{t}},

hence

\frac{3}{2}(2t+1)-\mathbb{E}[L_{2t+1}]=\mathbb{P}(I_{2t+1}=1)-\frac{1}{2}\geq q_{t}\frac{D_{t}}{2C_{t}}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{18\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1)\Bigr)t^{-1/2}.

In the even case,

\mathbb{P}(I_{2t}=1)\geq\frac{1}{2}+q_{t}\frac{D_{t}}{C_{t}},

so

\frac{3}{2}(2t)-\mathbb{E}[L_{2t}]=\mathbb{P}(I_{2t}=1)-\frac{1}{2}\geq q_{t}\frac{D_{t}}{C_{t}}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{9\sqrt{\pi}}+o(1)\Bigr)t^{-1/2}.

The final statement follows since the odd lower bound is the smaller of the two asymptotic constants.
∎
