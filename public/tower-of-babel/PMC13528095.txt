Prevalence and determinants of tuberculosis and COVID-19 among patients with presumptive tuberculosis in a Nigerian tertiary hospital
Authors: Moore Ikechi Mike-Ogburia, Dumdebabari Favour Precious, Favour Chigemezu Alozie, Faith Chinwe Ofurum, Deborah Erewari Israel, Deborah Bekee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health challenge in Nigeria, while the overlap between TB and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in symptomatic patients has been less clearly described in routine clinical settings. This study assessed the prevalence and determinants of TB and COVID-19 among presumptive TB patients in a tertiary hospital in Port Harcourt, Nigeria.MethodsThis hospital-based cross-sectional study, conducted between April and August 2024, involved 242 presumptive TB patients attending Rivers State University Teaching Hospital, Port Harcourt recruited using consecutive sampling. Sociodemographic characteristics, medical-related, and behavioural/environmental data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire, while TB and COVID-19 were assessed using Xpert Mycobacterium tuberculosis/rifampicin (Xpert MTB/RIF) assay and STANDARD Q COVID-19 Ag Test, respectively. Statistical analysis involved descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression analyses using GraphPad Prism 9, with statistical significance set at p ≤ 0.05.ResultsTB was detected in 21.9% of participants; however, no participant tested positive for COVID-19, yielding a COVID-19 prevalence of 0.0%, and no case of TB/COVID-19 co-infection was identified. None of the sociodemographic characteristics examined was significantly associated with TB prevalence (p > 0.05). However, contact with someone with prolonged coughing (aOR: 5.41, 95% CI: 2.55–11.70; p < 0.0001) and HIV-positive status (aOR: 3.02, 95% CI: 1.08–8.37; p = 0.0334) were significant determinants of TB infection. In addition, current smoking (aOR: 5.18, 95% CI: 1.74–15.70; p = 0.0031) and living in a densely populated area with limited ventilation (aOR: 2.82, 95% CI: 1.29–6.12; p = 0.0086) independently predicted TB infection.ConclusionsTB remains a considerable burden among presumptive TB patients in this setting, whereas no COVID-19 or TB/COVID-19 co-infection was detected during the study period. TB risk was driven mainly by exposure-related, behavioural, clinical, and environmental factors rather than sociodemographic characteristics. Strengthening routine TB screening, contact investigation, bidirectional TB/HIV services, smoking-cessation support, and interventions addressing overcrowded, poorly ventilated living conditions may improve TB control in similar high-burden settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13731-7.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global public health threat despite being preventable and curable [1, 2]. In 2024, an estimated 10.7 million people developed TB and 1.23 million died from the disease, highlighting its continued importance as one of the leading infectious causes of death worldwide [1, 2]. Although the global TB incidence rate declined slightly between 2023 and 2024, progress has remained insufficient, with the overall reduction since 2015 still far below the End TB Strategy milestone of a 50% reduction by 2025 [1]. Because pulmonary TB is transmitted through the air and may present with mild, prolonged, or non-specific respiratory symptoms [2], timely evaluation of symptomatic individuals is essential for early diagnosis, prompt treatment, and interruption of transmission.

Nigeria continues to bear a disproportionate share of the global TB burden; the country accounted for 4.8% of global TB cases and was among the eight countries that together contributed two-thirds of the worldwide burden in 2024 [3]. However, case detection remains suboptimal. In a recent study of private-sector TB care in urban Nigeria, Sassi et al. [4] observed that only about one-third of TB cases in Nigeria in 2020 were diagnosed and notified, partly because of low detection and under-reporting within the private sector. Similarly, Oga-Omenka et al. [5] reported that although the private sector accounted for most initial TB care-seeking in Nigeria, only a small proportion of new TB cases were notified from that sector. These gaps highlight the need to strengthen screening and diagnostic pathways in healthcare settings where patients with suggestive respiratory symptoms commonly present.

Patients with presumptive TB are an important group for intensified case finding. Several factors increase the risk of TB infection and progression to active disease, including Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, tobacco use, harmful alcohol use, and other conditions that impair host immunity or increase exposure to infectious aerosols [2]. Smoking has been associated with greater susceptibility to TB infection, more severe disease, and poorer treatment outcomes [6, 7]. In addition, close and repeated exposure to persons with chronic cough, particularly in overcrowded and poorly ventilated environments, may facilitate airborne transmission of Mycobacterium tuberculosis [2, 8–10]. Examining these factors among presumptive TB patients may help improve risk-based screening and case detection in routine clinical practice.

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic further complicated the assessment of patients presenting with cough and other respiratory symptoms. TB and COVID-19 share overlapping clinical manifestations and both require timely diagnosis and infection-prevention measures [11]. Tuberculosis has also been recognised as an underlying condition associated with poorer COVID-19 outcomes [12], while TB–COVID-19 co-infection has been linked to increased hospitalisation, delayed recovery, and higher fatality compared with COVID-19 alone [11]. In Nigeria, the pandemic also disrupted TB service delivery, including within the private health sector, although adaptive responses were later reported [4, 13]. These overlaps make it important to understand the relative burden of both infections among symptomatic patients evaluated for TB.

Despite the public health relevance of these issues, data on the prevalence of TB and COVID-19 among presumptive TB patients in Rivers State remain limited. Rivers State University Teaching Hospital, Port Harcourt, is a major government-owned tertiary health facility that receives patients with respiratory symptoms and related infectious disease complaints [14], making it a suitable setting for such an assessment. Local evidence on the prevalence of TB and COVID-19, as well as the factors associated with these infections, is needed to strengthen diagnostic vigilance, improve targeted screening, and support infection prevention and control efforts. This study, therefore, investigated the prevalence and determinants of tuberculosis and COVID-19 among patients with presumptive tuberculosis attending Rivers State University Teaching Hospital, Port Harcourt, Nigeria.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a hospital-based cross-sectional design to determine the prevalence and determinants of tuberculosis and COVID-19 among patients with presumptive tuberculosis attending Rivers State University Teaching Hospital, Port Harcourt, Nigeria, from 22nd April to 19th August 2024.

Study area

The study was conducted at Rivers State University Teaching Hospital (RSUTH), located on Harley Street in Port Harcourt, the capital of Rivers State, southern Nigeria [14]. Port Harcourt is a highly urbanised city in the Niger Delta region, and the broader metropolitan population was estimated at approximately 3.95 million in 2026, reflecting dense population mixing and substantial demand for healthcare services [15, 16]. RSUTH is a government-owned tertiary hospital that evolved from the former Braithwaite Memorial Specialist Hospital and was upgraded in 2018 to serve as the teaching hospital for Rivers State University, providing specialist and referral services to residents of Port Harcourt and surrounding parts of Rivers State [14]. Its urban location, large and diverse patient catchment, and role as a major referral centre made it an appropriate setting for assessing tuberculosis and COVID-19 among patients with presumptive tuberculosis in this environment.

Study population, sample size, and sampling technique

The study population comprised adult patients with presumptive TB who presented to RSUTH, Port Harcourt, during the study period. In this study, presumptive TB patients were defined as individuals presenting with symptoms suggestive of TB, such as persistent cough, fever, weight loss, or night sweats. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who resided in Port Harcourt or surrounding areas and presented for evaluation at RSUTH. Patients with a previous history of TB treatment or prior TB diagnosis who had recurrent symptoms suggestive of TB were also eligible for inclusion. However, patients with confirmed TB who were already receiving anti-tuberculosis treatment at the time of recruitment were excluded.

The minimum sample size was estimated using an expected TB prevalence of 19.4% among presumptive TB patients, as reported in a study conducted in Abuja, Nigeria [17]. Using the Scalex SP calculator for prevalence studies, which supports sample size estimation based on expected prevalence, confidence level, and desired precision, a minimum sample size of 242 participants was obtained [18].

A consecutive sampling technique was used to recruit participants. Under this approach, all eligible presumptive TB patients who presented to RSUTH during the study period were approached sequentially and enrolled until the required sample size was reached. Consecutive sampling is a non-probability sampling method commonly used in hospital-based and clinic-based studies, particularly where participants present in a natural flow, and a complete sampling frame is not available [19]. Compared with unrestricted convenience sampling, consecutive sampling is often considered more robust because it attempts to include all accessible, eligible participants in order of presentation, thereby reducing investigator-driven selection and improving the transparency of recruitment [20, 21].

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire (Supplementary File 1). The questionnaire was adapted from previous tuberculosis epidemiological studies and informed by established tuberculosis risk domains, including sociodemographic characteristics, medical history and exposures, and behavioural and environmental factors [22, 23]. It also incorporated selected COVID-19-related preventive and exposure variables, consistent with prior COVID-19 epidemiological studies [24, 25]. The questionnaire was carefully reviewed for completeness and accuracy immediately after administration. Strict confidentiality was emphasised, and all personally identifying information was removed before analysis.

Laboratory procedures

Tuberculosis testing was performed using the Xpert Mycobacterium tuberculosis/rifampicin (Xpert MTB/RIF) assay on the GeneXpert platform (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), a cartridge-based automated nucleic acid amplification test for the detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex DNA and rifampicin-resistance–associated mutations directly from sputum [26]. The assay has been widely applied in TB diagnostic and epidemiological studies, including among presumptive TB populations in Nigeria [27–30]. In the present study, expectorated sputum samples were collected using standard procedures, mixed with the sample reagent buffer supplied with the assay, incubated according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and transferred into the Xpert cartridge for automated amplification and detection. A positive MTB result on the Xpert MTB/RIF assay was used to define tuberculosis positivity.

COVID-19 testing was performed using the STANDARD Q COVID-19 Ag Test (SD Biosensor, Suwon, Republic of Korea), a lateral-flow antigen-detecting rapid diagnostic test for qualitative detection of Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) antigen in upper respiratory specimens [31]. The assay has been used in hospital- and community-based COVID-19 studies, with performance evaluations reported from different settings [32–36]. In the present study, nasopharyngeal swab specimens were collected by trained personnel and tested promptly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A positive result on the STANDARD Q COVID-19 Ag Test was used to define COVID-19 positivity.

Variables

Dependent variables

This study examined two primary dependent variables: tuberculosis infection and COVID-19 infection. Tuberculosis infection was defined as a positive result for Mycobacterium tuberculosis on the Xpert MTB/RIF assay and was categorised as positive or negative. COVID-19 infection was defined as a positive result on the STANDARD Q COVID-19 Ag Test and was likewise categorised as positive or negative. In addition, COVID-19 and tuberculosis co-infection was described as the concurrent presence of both infections in the same participant. For the analysis of determinants, tuberculosis infection status served as the main outcome variable.

Independent variables

Potential predictors of tuberculosis infection were grouped into three broad domains: sociodemographic characteristics, medical-related factors, and behavioural/environmental factors. Sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex, marital status, level of education, and occupation. Medical-related factors comprised history of BCG vaccination, family history of tuberculosis, history of previous tuberculosis treatment, contact with someone with prolonged coughing, history of tuberculin skin testing, HIV/AIDS diagnosis status, and diabetes mellitus diagnosis status. Behavioural and environmental factors included smoking status, alcohol consumption, nature of residence, living in a densely populated area with limited ventilation, number of rooms at home, and number of people living in the household. These variables were selected based on their biological plausibility and documented relevance to tuberculosis transmission, exposure, and disease risk.

Bias mitigation strategies

Several measures were implemented to minimise potential bias in this study. Selection bias was reduced by consecutively recruiting all eligible presumptive tuberculosis patients who presented to Rivers State University Teaching Hospital during the study period, thereby limiting investigator-driven participant selection. Interviewer and measurement bias were minimised through the use of a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire, uniform data collection procedures, and immediate review of completed questionnaires for completeness and consistency. Misclassification bias was further reduced by employing standardised laboratory methods for outcome ascertainment, namely the STANDARD Q COVID-19 Ag Test for COVID-19 screening and the Xpert MTB/RIF assay for tuberculosis diagnosis, with all tests performed according to manufacturer instructions. Recall bias was minimised by using clear, closed-ended questions focused on identifiable medical history, exposures, and behaviours. Confidentiality was emphasised during data collection, and personally identifying information was excluded from the analytical dataset to reduce reporting and social desirability bias. In addition, data cleaning, consistency checks, and the use of a pre-specified statistical analysis approach helped to minimise data handling and analytic bias.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the study variables, with continuous variables presented as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The prevalence of tuberculosis, COVID-19, and co-infection was determined, while associations between categorical independent variables and tuberculosis infection were initially examined using chi-square tests. Bivariate logistic regression was first performed to estimate crude odds ratios (cORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to identify factors associated with tuberculosis infection. Given that the number of tuberculosis cases was modest, the multivariable logistic regression model was intentionally kept parsimonious to minimise overfitting and preserve model stability. Variables entered into the multivariable model were selected based on prior literature, biological plausibility, clinical relevance, and events-per-variable considerations, and included age, history of BCG vaccination, contact with someone with prolonged coughing, HIV/AIDS diagnosis, smoking status, and living in a densely populated area with limited ventilation. Where conceptually related variables reflected overlapping domains, the more proximal and clinically interpretable predictor was retained in the final model. Sensitivity analyses were performed by separately re-entering excluded bivariable-significant variables into the multivariable model to assess their effect on the adjusted estimates and model stability. Model diagnostics for the final multivariable logistic regression model were also assessed in GraphPad Prism. Multicollinearity among predictors was evaluated using variance inflation factors, model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs were reported, and statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

A total of 242 participants (mean age 34.6 ± 14.4 years) were included in the study; the largest age group was 41 years and above (31.4%), followed by 31–40 years (26.0%) and 21–30 years (24.8%). Females were slightly more represented than males (52.9% vs. 47.1%). More than half had never married (55.4%), and most had attained tertiary education (61.2%). Unskilled labourers constituted the largest occupational group (30.6%), followed by business owners (26.9%), private employees (19.0%), and civil servants (17.4%), while the unemployed were the least represented (6.2%) (Table 1).

Tuberculosis was detected in 21.9% of participants, while no participant tested positive for COVID-19, giving a COVID-19 prevalence of 0.0%. Accordingly, no case of COVID-19 and tuberculosis co-infection was identified in the study (Fig. 1).

None of the sociodemographic characteristics examined was significantly associated with tuberculosis prevalence. However, tuberculosis was more common among participants aged 21–30 years, those who were ever married, those with secondary education or less, and unskilled labourers, although these observations were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Several medical-related factors were significantly associated with tuberculosis. Tuberculosis prevalence was higher among participants without a history of BCG vaccination (p = 0.0357), those with a family history of TB (p < 0.0001), previous TB treatment (p = 0.0282), and among those who had contact with someone with prolonged coughing (p < 0.0001). Tuberculosis prevalence also differed significantly by HIV status, being highest among HIV-positive participants (p = 0.0063). In contrast, history of TST testing and diabetes status were not significantly associated with tuberculosis prevalence (both p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Regarding behaviours and environmental factors, tuberculosis prevalence was descriptively highest among current smokers (52.6%), followed by former smokers (27.8%) and non-smokers (18.5%). Alcohol consumers had a significantly higher prevalence of tuberculosis than non-consumers (38.9% vs. 17.0%, p = 0.0006). Participants living in densely populated areas with limited ventilation also had a significantly higher prevalence than those not living in such settings (42.6% vs. 16.0%, p < 0.0001), while those living in homes with two rooms or fewer had a higher prevalence than those with more than two rooms (25.6% vs. 14.1%, p = 0.0431). Nature of residence and household size were not significantly associated with tuberculosis prevalence, although prevalence was higher among suburban residents (31.8% vs. 20.9%) and among those living with more than four persons (26.5% vs. 16.0%) (both p > 0.05) (Table 4).

In the bivariate logistic regression analysis, increased odds of tuberculosis infection were observed among participants with no history of BCG vaccination (cOR: 2.56, 95% CI: 1.01–6.22; p = 0.0410), a family history of TB (cOR: 4.76, 95% CI: 2.43–9.39; p < 0.0001), previous TB treatment (cOR: 3.83, 95% CI: 1.03–14.30; p = 0.0396), contact with someone with prolonged coughing (cOR: 4.97, 95% CI: 2.53–9.84; p < 0.0001), HIV-positive status (cOR: 3.90, 95% CI: 1.62–9.41; p = 0.0022), current smoking (cOR: 4.88, 95% CI: 1.85–13.10; p = 0.0013), alcohol consumption (cOR: 3.10, 95% CI: 1.59–6.04; p = 0.0009), living in a densely populated area with limited ventilation (cOR: 3.91, 95% CI: 2.01–7.63; p < 0.0001), and living in homes with two rooms or fewer (cOR: 2.10, 95% CI: 1.04–4.53; p = 0.0461). However, after adjustment for potential confounders, independent predictors of tuberculosis infection were contact with someone with prolonged coughing (aOR: 5.41, 95% CI: 2.55–11.70; p < 0.0001), HIV-positive status (aOR: 3.02, 95% CI: 1.08–8.37; p = 0.0334), current smoking (aOR: 5.18, 95% CI: 1.74–15.70; p = 0.0031), and living in a densely populated area with limited ventilation (aOR: 2.82, 95% CI: 1.29–6.12; p = 0.0086). The association with lack of BCG vaccination was attenuated and no longer statistically significant in the multivariable model (aOR: 2.21, 95% CI: 0.76–6.21; p = 0.1355) (Table 5). The final multivariable logistic regression model showed no evidence of problematic multicollinearity among the predictors (all VIFs: 1.04–1.16), had acceptable discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.793, 95% CI: 0.721–0.864; p < 0.0001), and demonstrated good model fit on the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ² = 4.66; p = 0.7927).

Discussion

The current study found a substantial TB burden among presumptive TB patients, with 21.9% having bacteriologically confirmed disease. This is plausible in a tertiary hospital setting, where patients are already enriched for compatible symptoms and may present late, thereby increasing diagnostic yield, especially when rapid molecular testing is used [37]. COVID-19-related disruptions to health-seeking and TB services have been shown to reduce timely TB detection and notification, and contribute to later rebounds in diagnosed cases [38, 39], which may also have contributed to the higher burden observed among symptomatic attendees. This finding implies that presumptive TB patients in this setting represent a high-yield group for rapid diagnosis, and supports consistent front-line triage, prompt molecular testing, and integrated TB/COVID respiratory screening to improve case detection and support transmission control. The prevalence observed here was higher than reports from community-based case finding in the Niger Delta and hotspot-guided screening across high-burden Nigerian states [40, 41], as well as some studies among presumptive patients in Ethiopia [42, 43], although lower than the very high prevalence reported by Kumbi et al. [44], showing that TB yield among presumptive patients varies widely by referral pattern, diagnostic algorithm, background transmission, and risk-profile of the study population.

COVID-19 was not detected in the current study, and, by extension, no TB/COVID-19 co-infection was identified. This should be interpreted cautiously as indicating no detected cases in this sample, rather than the absence of SARS-CoV-2 circulation in the study setting. While it is possible that recruitment occurred during a period of relatively low COVID-19 activity, the use of an antigen-based assay may also have contributed to non-detection, because such tests are less sensitive than reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and may yield false-negative results, particularly in individuals with lower viral loads or less symptomatic infection [45]. In this cohort, prolonged respiratory symptoms may have been more likely to reflect chronic mycobacterial disease than acute viral infection. Although prior COVID-19 diagnosis was not assessed, 34.3% of participants reported previous COVID-19 vaccination, which may also reflect some degree of population immunity at the time of the study. Nevertheless, dual respiratory screening may still be relevant during future surges or in clinically suspicious patients. The finding was lower than reports from Cameroon and the United Kingdom [46, 47], but the absence of co-infection is not entirely inconsistent with broader evidence that TB/COVID-19 overlap is generally uncommon at the population level [11, 48]. In similar settings, therefore, COVID-19 should remain a differential consideration, but zero antigen-detected prevalence should be interpreted cautiously.

None of the assessed sociodemographic variables was significantly associated with TB prevalence, suggesting that among presumptive TB patients in this clinical setting, confirmed disease was not concentrated within a particular demographic subgroup. This may reflect the fact that all participants already had symptoms warranting TB evaluation, thereby narrowing baseline demographic differences and making more proximal clinical, behavioural, and exposure-related factors more important. Similar findings have been reported among presumptive patients in Nasarawa State, Nigeria, and in the Philippines [49, 50], although other Nigerian and international studies have shown associations with age and sex [30, 51]. In practical terms, this finding suggests that TB screening in tertiary-care settings should not be restricted by sociodemographic profile, but should remain systematic for all presumptive cases.

Contact with someone with prolonged coughing independently predicted tuberculosis infection in the current study, indicating that recent exposure to such individuals was a major epidemiologic signal for TB in this study population rather than a trivial background history. This finding is biologically plausible since a persistent cough can be an important clinical clue to untreated infectious pulmonary disease [52, 53], and repeated close exposure increases the likelihood of inhaling Mycobacterium tuberculosis–containing aerosols over time [8–10]. Similar associations have been reported among household contacts and presumptive TB patients in other African settings [54–57]. In the Nigerian tertiary-care context, this supports routine enquiry about prolonged cough exposure as part of triage, with rapid testing and contact investigation prioritised for those reporting such contact.

HIV-positive status was an independent predictor of TB infection in this study, with roughly threefold higher odds of TB among patients living with HIV than those not living with HIV. This association may be explained by the fact that HIV weakens cell-mediated immunity [58, 59], reduces the host’s ability to contain latent Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and increases the likelihood of progression to active disease [60]; HIV-associated TB also remains a major cause of hospitalisation and death in people with HIV, especially in high-burden African settings [61, 62]. This finding supports prioritising bidirectional TB/HIV screening, rapid molecular testing, prompt ART optimisation, and preventive or curative TB therapy for HIV-positive patients in TB clinics, since delayed diagnosis and therapy in this subgroup can translate into worse outcomes [61, 63]. Comparable findings have been reported among presumptive TB patients in Ethiopia and among HIV-positive populations in Nigeria [64–66]. At a broader level, Wondmeneh and Mekonnen [67] estimated a pooled TB incidence of 3.49 per 100 person-years among HIV-positive persons in sub-Saharan Africa, buttressing the fact that HIV remains one of the most important drivers of TB burden in the region.

Current smoking remained an independent predictor of TB infection in this study, suggesting that tobacco exposure was not merely a background habit but a clinically meaningful risk factor in this population. This association is biologically credible because cigarette smoke impairs mucociliary clearance [68], affects alveolar macrophage antimicrobial activity [69], and weakens host immune responses that ordinarily help contain Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, which may increase the likelihood of progression to active pulmonary disease [6, 7]. The finding may also reflect the fact that smoking often coexists with other adverse exposures, including crowded living conditions [70] and delayed care-seeking [71], which may also be associated with TB among symptomatic adults. This finding suggests that smoking history should be treated as an important screening and risk-stratification variable in clinics handling presumptive TB cases, while smoking-cessation support should be integrated into TB services to reduce both disease burden and poorer downstream outcomes. This result is consistent with recent studies from other high-burden settings [72–74], reinforcing the observed association between smoking and TB across different epidemiologic contexts. The stronger effect size observed in the present study may indicate a more vulnerable hospital-based population, heavier tobacco exposure, or clustering with other unmeasured local risk factors, but the overall direction of evidence is clearly aligned with the wider literature.

Poor residential conditions were an important structural correlate of TB in this population, with participants living in densely populated, poorly ventilated settings showing nearly threefold higher odds of infection. This is biologically and epidemiologically plausible because TB is transmitted through inhaled airborne droplet nuclei [75], and crowding may increase the number and duration of close shared-air contacts [76, 77], while limited ventilation may reduce dilution and clearance of infectious particles [78]. The association may also reflect broader deprivation, since overcrowded and poorly ventilated homes often coexist with poverty and repeated exposure to infectious cases [79, 80]. This finding suggests that TB control in this setting should not rely only on patient-level screening, but also on structural measures such as improving household ventilation, reducing indoor crowding where feasible, and prioritising active case finding in high-density communities. This finding is consistent with recent evidence from other high-burden settings [54, 81], showing that the risk attached to dense, poorly ventilated living spaces is reproducible across different African settings and populations. More broadly, Deol et al. [82] demonstrated that transmission risk in congregate settings rises when occupancy is high and ventilation is inadequate, highlighting the mechanistic basis for the present association. The evidence suggests that housing and indoor air conditions are not merely background social variables, but actionable determinants of TB transmission that deserve explicit attention in prevention strategies.

This study has some limitations that may influence interpretation and limit generalisability. Its cross-sectional design means exposures and outcomes were assessed at a single point in time, so temporal sequence and causality cannot be established, and such designs are also vulnerable to selection and confounding-related biases. The hospital-based nature of the sample further means the findings are best interpreted within presumptive-TB clinical populations rather than the general community. The use of an antigen-based assay rather than RT-PCR for COVID-19 diagnosis may have underestimated SARS-CoV-2 prevalence and TB/COVID-19 co-infection in this study, particularly among participants with lower viral loads or prolonged symptom duration before testing; in addition, prior COVID-19 diagnosis was not assessed. Although the final multivariable model was kept parsimonious and showed low variance inflation factors, the number of TB events relative to predictor parameters was modest, so some coefficient estimates should be interpreted with caution. Notwithstanding these limitations, the study has important strengths, as it simultaneously assessed TB and COVID-19 in a clinically relevant population, was well-suited to estimating point prevalence and exploring associated factors in that setting, and used a rapid molecular method for TB detection, which aligns with WHO-recommended rapid diagnostics and strengthens confidence in TB case ascertainment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, TB remained a substantial burden among presumptive TB patients in this setting, whereas no COVID-19 case or TB/COVID-19 co-infection was detected during the study period. The pattern of findings suggests that TB occurrence was shaped less by sociodemographic profile than by clear exposure- and vulnerability-related factors, particularly contact with persons who had prolonged cough, HIV-positive status, current smoking, and residence in crowded, poorly ventilated environments. The study reemphasises the need to strengthen routine TB screening and rapid diagnosis among presumptive cases, intensify contact tracing and bidirectional TB/HIV services, integrate smoking-cessation support into TB care, and address household environmental risks as part of a broader TB control strategy, in line with current WHO emphasis on systematic screening and HIV-associated TB care.
