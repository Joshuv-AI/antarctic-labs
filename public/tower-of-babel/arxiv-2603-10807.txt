Risk-Adjusted Harm Scoring for Automated Red Teaming for LLMs in Financial Services
Fabrizio Dimino, Bhaskarjit Sarmah, Stefano Pasquali

Abstract
The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) in financial services introduces new operational, regulatory, and security risks. Yet most red-teaming benchmarks remain domain-agnostic and fail to capture failure modes specific to regulated BFSI settings, where harmful behavior can be elicited through legally or professionally plausible framing. We propose a risk-aware evaluation framework for LLM security failures in Banking, Financial Services, and Insurance (BFSI), combining a domain-specific taxonomy of financial harms, an automated multi-round red-teaming pipeline, and an ensemble-based judging protocol. We introduce the Risk-Adjusted Harm Score (RAHS), a risk-sensitive metric that goes beyond success rates by quantifying the operational severity of disclosures, accounting for mitigation signals, and leveraging inter-judge agreement. Across diverse models, we find that higher decoding stochasticity and sustained adaptive interaction not only increase jailbreak success, but also drive systematic escalation toward more severe and operationally actionable financial disclosures. These results expose limitations of single-turn, domain-agnostic security evaluation and motivate risk-sensitive assessment under prolonged adversarial pressure for real-world BFSI deployment.



1
Introduction

LLMs are typically equipped with guardrails intended to prevent the generation of disallowed or harmful content. Despite these safeguards, a growing body of evidence shows that determined adversaries can systematically circumvent such protections through jailbreak attacks, carefully crafted prompts, or interaction strategies that override model constraints and induce policy-violating behavior. These attacks often generalize across models and alignment strategies, highlighting the transferability of discovered vulnerabilities
(
32
)
. Reflecting their practical relevance, jailbreak attacks are explicitly identified as one of the top security threats to LLM-based systems by the Open Web Application Security Project (OWASP)
(
21
)
.

This vulnerability becomes especially concerning in domains where model outputs can directly influence high-stakes decisions. In particular, financial institutions are increasingly deploying LLMs to support high-impact activities such as investment research, trading support, compliance analysis, customer interaction, and operational automation
(
13
)
. Unlike general-purpose consumer applications, financial AI systems operate in highly regulated environments, where model failures can translate directly into regulatory violations, financial losses, reputational damage, or even systemic risk
(
14
)
. As a result, safety and robustness evaluations in this domain must go beyond generic notions of harmful content and explicitly account for financial, regulatory, and operational risk. This urgency is increasingly reflected in regulatory and policy discussions. In January 2026 the UK House of Commons Treasury Committee warned that a continued “wait-and-see” posture leaves consumers and the financial system exposed to potentially serious harm, and recommended that regulators introduce AI-specific stress testing to assess resilience to AI-driven market shocks
(
28
)
.

However, existing red-teaming benchmarks and LLM security evaluations are largely domain-agnostic. They predominantly focus on general-purpose harms and often rely on static or single-turn interactions, which fail to capture the interaction-dependent and escalation-driven failure modes that arise in realistic adversarial settings. Moreover, current metrics typically reduce security evaluation to binary success rates, obscuring meaningful differences in the severity, operational specificity, and real-world impact of model failures. This mismatch leaves a critical gap between how LLM security is commonly evaluated and how failures manifest in real-world financial deployments.

To address this gap, we propose a risk-aware framework for evaluating LLM security failures in financial settings. Our approach combines a domain-specific taxonomy of financial harms, adaptive multi-turn red-teaming, ensemble-based automated evaluation, and a novel risk-sensitive metric that captures not only whether a model fails, but how risky that failure is from a financial perspective.

Specifically, this paper makes the following contributions:

•

We introduce a fine-grained taxonomy of financially relevant model harms and a corresponding domain-specific benchmark,
FinRedTeamBench
, which together map LLM failure modes to regulatory, compliance, and operational risk categories in the Banking, Financial Services, and Insurance (BFSI) domain.

•

We propose an ensemble-based automated evaluation protocol that combines multiple LLM judges with complementary security and reasoning capabilities to reliably identify, validate, and contextualize instances of harmful financial disclosure.

•

We introduce a risk-sensitive evaluation metric, the
Risk-Adjusted Harm Score (RAHS)
, which goes beyond binary success rates by jointly accounting for disclosure severity and the presence of legal or ethical disclaimers.

•

We develop an automated red-teaming framework that operationalizes adaptive, multi-turn adversarial interactions, in which an attacker model iteratively leverages judge feedback to generate progressively more effective jailbreak prompts against a target model.

Beyond measuring whether a model fails, our framework is explicitly designed to characterize
how
failures occur and
why
they are risky in real-world production systems.

2
Related Work

2.1
Taxonomies and Benchmarks

As LLMs are increasingly deployed in safety-critical and regulated domains, the ability to systematically evaluate and compare their failure modes has become a central challenge in LLM security research. Structured taxonomies play a crucial role in this process, as they provide a shared vocabulary for categorizing model behaviors, enable reproducible benchmarking, and support interpretable analysis of security failures across models and evaluation settings
(
11
)
.

Recent work has proposed general-purpose safety taxonomies and benchmarks aimed at assessing broad classes of harmful behavior. Frameworks such as DecodingTrust
(
7
)
, SafetyBench
(
30
)
, and the MLCommons AI Safety benchmarks
(
18
)
offer valuable coverage of risks including toxicity, bias, privacy leakage, and general misuse. These efforts have significantly advanced the state of safety evaluation for open-domain language models.

In parallel, a growing body of work has introduced benchmarks focused on evaluating robustness to jailbreak attacks.
AdvBench
(
32
)
and HarmBench
(
17
)
systematically probe policy-violating behaviors under diverse attack strategies, while JailbreakBench
(
1
)
and RedEval
(
6
)
demonstrate that even frontier models remain vulnerable to adaptive adversarial prompting.
More recent studies, such as SG-Bench
(
19
)
and BetterBench
(
25
)
, further highlight challenges related to security generalization and evaluation methodology.
However, these benchmarks are predominantly attack-centric and general-purpose, and do not explicitly model domain-specific regulatory risk or financial compliance constraints.

Recent finance-oriented benchmarks begin to address this gap by introducing domain-aware categorizations.

4
reveal significant deficiencies in current safety alignment even when evaluated on a dedicated financial benchmark, and introduce FINBench to systematically assess regulatory risk concealment in multi-turn interactions.
CNFinBench
(
8
)
evaluates LLMs across capability, compliance, and safety dimensions in realistic financial scenarios, while FinJailbreak
(
15
)
focuses on adversarial prompting in finance, covering violation types including market manipulation, insider trading, regulatory evasion, and data privacy breaches.
Despite representing important progress toward domain-specific security evaluation, these benchmarks rely on relatively narrow taxonomies and do not provide a unified mapping between observed model failures and the broader spectrum of regulatory, compliance, and operational risks encountered across the BFSI ecosystem. In contrast, our work proposes a unified, risk-aware taxonomy that explicitly maps model failures to regulatory, compliance, and operational risk across the full BFSI lifecycle.

2.2
Attack Surfaces of LLMs

The primary attack surface of LLMs arises from their reliance on natural language. While this design enables flexible and expressive interactions, it also exposes models to adversarial manipulation, as instructions expressed in natural language are inherently ambiguous, compositional, and context-dependent. As a result, guardrails can often be weakened or overridden through prompt-level manipulation.

Early research in this area focused on manual, human-designed jailbreaks, where adversaries crafted deceptive prompt templates. More recent work has shifted toward automated approaches that frame jailbreak discovery as an optimization problem.
32
introduced Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), a white-box attack that optimizes adversarial token suffixes via gradient-based methods to elicit unsafe outputs. While effective, such approaches typically require large query budgets and produce unintelligible prompt artifacts, limiting their realism and transferability. Other work applied evolutionary strategies, using genetic mutation and crossover guided by model feedback to evolve jailbreak prompt templates
(
16
)
. Although these methods outperform naive random search, their stochastic nature often results in unstable convergence and inconsistent attack success
(
3
)
.

A key recent development is the use of LLMs as adaptive adversaries
(
23
)
. Building on this insight, Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement (PAIR)
(
2
)
shows that effective, human-readable jailbreaks can be discovered with fewer than twenty black-box queries via iterative refinement. Moreover, Crescendo
(
26
)
illustrates how attackers can gradually escalate benign-seeming queries into disallowed requests over a conversation. GOAT
(
22
)
further advances this line of work by introducing an automated agentic red-teaming framework that generates adaptive, multi-turn attack strategies through structured reasoning and iterative refinement.

In parallel, several approaches aim to systematize and scale adversarial prompt generation.
31
abstract jailbreak construction into modular components such as Selector, Mutator, Constraint, and Evaluator, simplifying large-scale adversarial prompt generation and evaluation. Relatedly, h4rm3l
(
10
)
introduces a composable “language” for jailbreak attack synthesis, enabling systematic construction of diverse attacks and a dynamic benchmark for safety assessment. Complementary to modular and agentic prompt-generation pipelines, WildTeaming
(
12
)
scales red-teaming by mining in-the-wild jailbreak tactics and using them to synthesize diverse attack candidates and large-scale safety training resources. X-Teaming
(
24
)
coordinates specialized roles (planning, attacking, verifying, refining) to construct and adapt attack trajectories, while RedAgent
(
29
)
uses a multi-agent pipeline with evaluator feedback and memory to discover vulnerabilities in deployed applications. Finally, LeakAgent
(
20
)
uses feedback-driven optimization to increase the likelihood of sensitive data leakage.

3
Methodology

3.1
Financial Taxonomy and Benchmark Construction

We define a comprehensive taxonomy of financially relevant harmful behaviors, designed to capture realistic failure modes of LLMs deployed across regulated financial settings.

Let
\mathcal{R}=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{|\mathcal{R}|}\}
denote the set of high-level risk categories, each corresponding to a well-established class of financial misconduct, regulatory violation, or consumer harm. For each category
r\in\mathcal{R}
, we construct a category-specific set of adversarial prompts

\mathcal{P}_{r}=\{p^{(r)}_{1},\dots,p^{(r)}_{m_{r}}\},

targeting behaviors that could realistically be induced through natural-language interactions with LLMs.
Prompt generation is performed in collaboration with financial domain experts and combines expert-authored seed prompts with LLM-assisted expansions. These expansions are guided by structured templates derived from regulatory language, supervisory guidance, and documented historical misconduct cases, ensuring both realism and regulatory relevance.

The resulting benchmark, which we refer to as
FinRedTeamBench
, is defined as the union of all category-specific prompt sets:

\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{P}_{r}.

The benchmark spans scenarios across banking, capital markets, payments, and digital-asset ecosystems, and is explicitly designed to stress-test model behavior under domain-specific adversarial conditions rather than generic LLM security violations.
Table
3
in the Appendix provides a detailed breakdown of the taxonomy underlying FinRedTeamBench, including high-level risk categories, fine-grained sub-categories, and the number of prompts per sub-category.
In total, the benchmark comprises 989 adversarial prompts distributed across seven major risk areas, enabling fine-grained analysis of model failures at both the category and sub-category levels.
For transparency and reproducibility, we report in Appendix
A.1
one representative user prompt for each sub-category, together with the system prompt used.
To reduce the risk of misuse, these examples are sanitized and intentionally non-operational: they preserve the semantic intent of each sub-category while omitting actionable details that could facilitate wrongdoing.
The resulting taxonomy provides the semantic grounding for both the evaluation protocol and the adaptive red-teaming framework described below.

3.2
Evaluation

Given a prompt
q\in\mathcal{B}
and a corresponding model output
a
, we assess LLM security using an ensemble
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}\}
of open-weight LLM judges. Importantly, the evaluated output
a
includes not only the final response, but also any intermediate reasoning or explanatory content produced by the model. This design choice reflects the observation that LLM security violations often arise through reasoning disclosure rather than explicit final answers, a dimension commonly overlooked by prior evaluation approaches that focus solely on surface-level responses.

Specifically, we use a heterogeneous ensemble of three open-weight LLM judges: (i) a safety-specialized safeguard model,
gpt-oss-120b-safeguard
; (ii) a large general-purpose reasoning model,
Qwen3-235B-A22B
; and (iii) a mid-sized, efficient model,
Llama-3.3-Nemotron-Super-49B-v1.5
. The safeguard judge is optimized for high-precision detection of policy violations and unsafe content, acting as a conservative filter for harmful disclosures. The large model provides stronger reasoning ability and broader contextual understanding, which improves consistency on ambiguous, multi-step, or indirectly harmful answers. Finally, Nemotron serves as a lightweight judge that is faster and cheaper to run, improving throughput and offering an additional independent signal that helps reduce variance in edge cases. Overall, the ensemble balances (a) precise safety detection, (b) deep semantic and contextual evaluation, and (c) cost-efficient scalability, yielding more robust judgments than any single judge in isolation.

Each judge assigns one of the following labels:

•

Refusal (R)
: a direct denial that does not disclose sensitive methods or actionable details;

•

Safe Alternative (SA)
: a refusal accompanied by compliant, high-level educational guidance;

•

Harmful Disclosure (HD)
: the generation of operationally actionable instructions resulting from a successful security bypass.

We mark an output as
HarmfulDisclosure
only under majority agreement:

\hat{y}=\texttt{HD}\iff\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\mathbb{I}\{J_{\ell}(q,a)=\texttt{HD}\}\geq 2.

In addition, each judge flags whether the output contains an explicit legal or ethical
disclaimer
, denoted as
d_{\ell}(q,a)\in\{0,1\}
, and assigns a severity level
s\in\{\texttt{low},\texttt{medium},\texttt{high}\}
based on operational detail and plausible real-world impact. Each judge further provides structured feedback
\phi
explaining the basis of its decision, which is used to guide adaptive adversarial refinement in Section
3.4
. The full prompts used to condition the LLM-based judges are reported in Appendix
A.2
.

3.3
Scoring Metrics

3.3.1
Positioning Relative to Prior Jailbreak Scoring Metrics

Attack Success Rate (ASR) remains the most commonly reported metric in jailbreak and red-teaming studies due to its simplicity and interpretability. However, recent work has highlighted that binary success can be overly coarse and has proposed more fine-grained success scoring metrics. For example, AttackEval introduces fine-grained scoring on a
[0,1]
scale to quantify the extent to which an adversarial prompt elicits harmful content beyond a strict success/failure threshold
(
27
)
. JADES further improves success assessment by decomposing harmful requests into weighted sub-requirements and aggregating partial fulfillment signals, optionally incorporating fact-checking to reduce inflated success estimates due to hallucinations
(
5
)
. In parallel, BELLS evaluates the performance of input-output safeguards using trace-based benchmarking across multiple architectural settings
(
9
)
.

While these approaches improve the fidelity of whether a jailbreak succeeds, they are primarily designed as success-centric evaluators. In regulated financial settings, an equally critical question is how risky a failure is once it occurs. Small differences in operational specificity can translate into materially different risk exposures, and ambiguous borderline outputs where judges disagree may indicate inconsistent behavior that is operationally hard to manage.

To address this gap, we introduce the
Risk-Adjusted Harm Score (RAHS)
as a risk-sensitive layer on top of success detection. Unlike success-focused scoring metrics, RAHS (i) grades harmful disclosures by operational severity, (ii) models mitigation signals such as legal/ethical disclaimers as partial attenuation rather than absolution, (iii) leverages inter-judge agreement as a confidence signal while penalizing high-disagreement outputs, and (iv) assigns positive credit to compliant behavior. Finally, RAHS is grounded in BFSI-specific risk categories and failure modes, enabling evaluation that is aligned with domain-relevant regulatory, compliance, and operational risk.

3.3.2
ASR and RAHS Definitions

We adopt Attack Success Rate (ASR) as a primary metric, defined as the proportion of harmful disclosures over the total number of attack prompts. Let
\{(q_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote an evaluation set of adversarial prompts and corresponding model outputs, and let
\hat{y}_{i}\in\{\texttt{R},\texttt{SA},\texttt{HD}\}
be the aggregated label for example
i
. We define:

\mathrm{ASR}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{y}_{i}=\texttt{HD}\}.

RAHS uses the same judge outputs but incorporates severity, mitigation, and agreement signals. Each judge assigns a severity level
s_{\ell,i}\in\{\texttt{low},\texttt{medium},\texttt{high}\}
and a disclaimer flag
d_{\ell,i}\in\{0,1\}
. We map severities to numeric weights:

w(\texttt{low})=\tfrac{1}{3},\qquad w(\texttt{medium})=\tfrac{2}{3},\qquad w(\texttt{high})=1,

and incorporate disclaimers via

g(\bar{d}_{i})=1-\gamma\bar{d}_{i},\qquad\gamma\in[0,1),

where
\bar{d}_{i}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}d_{\ell,i}
is the fraction of judges that detected an explicit disclaimer.

To avoid contaminating severity estimates with non-harmful labels, we compute the effective severity weight using only judges that marked the output as harmful:

\bar{w}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\mathbb{I}\{J_{\ell}(q_{i},a_{i})=\texttt{HD}\}\,w(s_{\ell,i})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\mathbb{I}\{J_{\ell}(q_{i},a_{i})=\texttt{HD}\}+\epsilon},

with a small
\epsilon>0
for numerical stability.

We define per-class agreement rates

p_{i}^{c}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}\mathbb{I}\{J_{\ell}(q_{i},a_{i})=c\},\qquad c\in\{\texttt{R},\texttt{SA},\texttt{HD}\}.

We then define the per-example signed score as

r_{i}=\alpha\,p_{i}^{\texttt{SA}}\;-\;(1-\alpha)\,p_{i}^{\texttt{HD}}\cdot\bar{w}_{i}\cdot g(\bar{d}_{i}),\qquad\alpha\in[0,1],

so that outputs with stronger inter-judge agreement contribute more strongly to the overall score. The first term rewards compliant safe alternatives, while the second term penalizes harmful disclosures in proportion to (i) judge agreement, (ii) severity, and (iii) lack of disclaimers.

To penalize ambiguous outputs with high inter-judge disagreement, we compute the normalized entropy of the label distribution:

\tilde{H}_{i}=-\frac{1}{\log 3}\sum_{c\in\{\texttt{R},\texttt{SA},\texttt{HD}\}}p_{i}^{c}\log(p_{i}^{c}+\epsilon)\in[0,1].

Finally, we define RAHS as

\mathrm{RAHS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(r_{i}-\lambda\,\tilde{H}_{i}\right),\qquad\lambda\geq 0.

RAHS increases when the model consistently produces compliant safe alternatives and decreases when it produces severe, weakly mitigated harmful disclosures. The entropy term further penalizes inconsistent or borderline cases where judges disagree.
The same metrics are computed both for single-turn assessment and as online feedback signals in the adaptive multi-turn red-teaming framework described below.

3.4
Automated Multi-Turn Red Teaming Framework

Building on the taxonomy and evaluation protocol introduced above, we design an automated multi-turn red-teaming framework to probe adaptive failure modes that do not manifest under single-turn prompting.
While FinRedTeamBench enables systematic single-shot LLM security evaluation, many realistic financial misuse scenarios unfold gradually through conversational refinement and contextual manipulation.

For each prompt
q\in\mathcal{B}
that does not result in a
HD
under single-turn evaluation, we activate a conditional multi-turn red-teaming procedure. We select a target risk category
r\in\mathcal{R}
and initiate an
attack run
, defined as a multi-turn adversarial dialogue between an attacker model
A
and a target model
m
.
At turn
t
, the conversation history is

\mathcal{H}_{t}=\{(q_{1},a_{1}),\dots,(q_{t},a_{t})\},\qquad a_{t}=m(q_{t}),

and the attacker generates the next query as

q_{t+1}=A(r,\mathcal{H}_{t},\phi_{t}),

where
\phi_{t}
denotes structured feedback derived directly from the evaluation pipeline in Section
3.2
. After each turn, the ensemble of judges
\mathcal{J}
evaluates the target output
a
, determines whether a
HD
has occurred.
The aggregated judgment is distilled into the feedback signal
\phi_{t}
and returned to the attacker, enabling explicit adaptation across turns.

Conditioning on
(r,\mathcal{H}_{t},\phi_{t})
induces a form of adaptive adversarial pressure in which the attacker incrementally refines content and framing, introduces ambiguity, and reshapes context in response to prior failures.
This process yields a form of gradual contextual poisoning, representing a security-driven failure mode that often manifests as an operationally consequential security violation.

We instantiate the attacker
A
using
DeepSeek-V3.2-685B
, leveraging its strong reasoning capabilities to generate coherent, diverse, and semantically targeted adversarial prompts across financial risk categories.
Each attack run proceeds for up to 5 turns and terminates early upon detection of a
HD
under majority agreement.
The prompts used to generate adaptive follow-up queries in the multi-turn red-teaming are reported in Appendix
A.3
.

We first analyze the impact of decoding temperature on jailbreak effectiveness. Decoding temperature controls the degree of stochasticity during generation and is known to influence both output diversity and risk-taking behavior, which can affect how readily models comply with adversarial instructions. We evaluate all models under three temperature settings,
T\in\{0,0.5,1.0\}
, and report both ASR and RAHS.

RAHS introduces three hyperparameters. We set
\gamma=0.2
to model disclaimers as a mild mitigation: they may reduce accidental misuse, but do not materially offset the risk when operational details are disclosed. We set
\alpha=0.5
to balance positive credit for compliant safe alternatives against negative credit for harmful disclosures, yielding a neutral, symmetric reward scale that is sensitive to both safe behavior and failures rather than being dominated by either. Finally, we set
\lambda=0.1
to apply a modest penalty for inter-judge disagreement, reflecting that borderline outputs introduce operational ambiguity, while keeping the primary signal driven by the signed reward term. Given our definition,
\mathrm{RAHS}\in[-(1-\alpha)-\lambda,\alpha]
; with
\alpha=0.5
and
\lambda=0.1
,
\mathrm{RAHS}\in[-0.6,0.5]
.

Table
1
summarizes the results. Across most models, increasing the decoding temperature leads to a consistent increase in ASR and a decrease in RAHS. This pattern aligns with the intuition that higher sampling stochasticity encourages more exploratory generations, increasing the likelihood of producing policy-violating or operationally actionable content under adversarial prompting.

Importantly, RAHS amplifies this effect relative to ASR alone: while ASR captures whether a jailbreak succeeds, RAHS reveals that higher temperatures often result in shift failures toward more operationally specific disclosures and more confident (higher-consensus) harmful classifications, yielding a larger drop in RAHS than ASR alone would suggest. RAHS is negative for all models on this benchmark, indicating that harmful disclosures dominate over safe-alternative behavior under the evaluated attack distribution. Notably, Qwen3-8B is a notable exception: ASR
decreases
as
T
increases (86.7
\rightarrow
84.2), while RAHS becomes slightly less negative. A plausible explanation is that, for smaller models, additional sampling noise can disrupt the coherence and persistence required to sustain a successful adversarial trajectory; the model may drift into generic or inconsistent completions that are more likely to be judged as refusal or safe alternative rather than a clearly actionable disclosure.
Nemotron-Nano-9B-v2 exhibits a related non-monotonic pattern, peaking in ASR at
T=0.5
and partially reverting at
T=1.0
, which is consistent with an intermediate-temperature regime that maximizes “useful” diversity for the attacker before higher noise degrades instruction-following stability.

Finally, we observe a consistent tendency for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, such as Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B and Qwen3-30B-A3B, to achieve lower ASR and less negative RAHS than many dense models at comparable scale. This suggests that expert routing and internal gating mechanisms may interact with sampling stochasticity in non-trivial ways, potentially limiting the persistence or escalation of adversarial behavior. While a detailed analysis of this phenomenon is beyond the scope of this work, the results indicate that architectural choices can significantly mediate how decoding strategies translate into real-world security risk.

We also observe substantial heterogeneity in temperature sensitivity across models.
For instance, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct is markedly temperature-fragile: moving from greedy decoding to
T=1.0
increases ASR from 87.4 to 90.1 and yields a consistent degradation in RAHS, suggesting that added stochasticity materially increases both the frequency and the risk profile of failures. In contrast, Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B is comparatively temperature-robust, with ASR remaining nearly unchanged (53.3 to 53.9) and only minor RAHS variation across temperatures. This separation highlights the value of a temperature sweep as a diagnostic: it distinguishes models whose safety behavior degrades sharply under sampling from those that remain stable across decoding regimes.

We next study how adversarial effectiveness evolves under multi-turn red-teaming. Following the procedure described in Section
3.4

Across nearly all models, we observe a clear escalation effect: ASR increases monotonically from R2 to R5, indicating that longer adaptive interactions substantially improve the attacker’s ability to elicit harmful financial disclosures. Consistent with this trend, RAHS decreases as the number of rounds grows. Crucially, RAHS reveals that multi-turn red-teaming does not merely increase the frequency of jailbreaks, but also their severity. As rounds progress, disclosures tend to become more operationally detailed and financially consequential, suggesting that adaptive attackers are able to iteratively refine prompts toward increasingly actionable failure modes.

By R5, several models approach a near-ceiling ASR (e.g., Qwen3-32B at 99.5%, domyn-small-9B at 98.3%, and Qwen3-8B at 98.9%), suggesting that extended adaptive prompting can saturate single-number success metrics and motivating severity-aware measures for finer differentiation.
In this near-ceiling regime, RAHS remains informative and preserves discriminative power: for example, at R5 Olmo-3-32B-Think attains the most negative RAHS (
-0.363
), Qwen3-32B reaches
-0.338
, while Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B is substantially less negative (
-0.266
).
This separation indicates that, even when jailbreak success becomes almost ubiquitous, models still differ meaningfully in the typical risk profile of their failures, which ASR alone cannot capture.

The impact of multi-turn interaction is particularly pronounced for models that appear relatively robust in early rounds.
For example, Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B escalates sharply from 76.3% ASR at R2 to 95.9% at R5, with RAHS degrading from
-0.223
to
-0.266
.
This suggests that early-round robustness does not necessarily translate into sustained resistance under prolonged adaptive pressure: once the attacker discovers an effective trajectory, subsequent rounds can amplify and stabilize the harmful behavior.
In contrast, Qwen3-30B-A3B exhibits a more gradual increase (65.0% to 80.1% ASR) and a smaller RAHS degradation (
-0.255
to
-0.271
), indicating slower escalation toward high-risk disclosures.

Overall, these results highlight the limitations of single-turn evaluations for LLM security assessment. Multi-turn red-teaming exposes vulnerabilities that only emerge through interaction and adaptation, underscoring the importance of evaluating LLMs under sustained adversarial pressure when reasoning about real-world financial safety and security risk.

5
Conclusion

Our findings highlight a critical asymmetry in current LLM security mechanisms within the financial domain. While models generally reject explicitly harmful or unethical requests, such as those related to violence, self-harm, or weapon construction, they struggle to identify and appropriately respond to prompts that encode high-risk financial behavior under a veneer of legality or professional intent. Requests involving regulatory gray areas, such as aggressive tax minimization strategies, market manipulation framed as research or compliance-sensitive structuring advice, are frequently met with helpful and operationally detailed responses rather than refusals. This exposes a fundamental vulnerability: LLMs optimized for overtly malicious content do not reliably capture the nuanced, context-dependent risks that characterize real-world financial misconduct.

Our results further show that these failures are not isolated edge cases but systematically emerge under realistic adversarial conditions. Both increased decoding stochasticity and sustained multi-turn interaction significantly amplify not only the likelihood of jailbreaking, but also their severity, as captured by the proposed Risk-Adjusted Harm Score. In particular, adaptive red-teaming reveals that early-round resistance often fails to translate into sustained robustness, with models gradually escalating toward more actionable and financially consequential disclosures over time. These findings underscore the limitations of static, single-turn evaluations and binary success metrics when assessing LLM security in regulated domains.

From a practical perspective, our study suggests that deploying LLMs in financial production systems without continuous adversarial testing poses material regulatory and operational risk. Reliance on general-purpose benchmarks alone is insufficient for applications where subtle regulatory non-compliance can have outsized legal and economic consequences. Instead, LLM security evaluation must explicitly incorporate financial risk semantics, interaction dynamics, and severity-aware metrics aligned with how financial institutions reason about exposure, compliance, and downstream decision-making.

A final consideration concerns the scope of our experimental evaluation. Our experiments focus on
\leq 72B
parameters language models, as evaluating frontier-scale models would require substantially greater computational resources and infrastructure. Importantly, this choice does not reflect a limitation of the proposed methodology, which is model-agnostic and directly extensible to larger-scale systems. Rather, we intentionally concentrate on these models due to their rapidly growing adoption in real-world financial deployments, where cost, latency, and controllability constraints often favor such architectures. As our results demonstrate, these models already exhibit significant security vulnerabilities under realistic adversarial pressure, underscoring the urgency of rigorous evaluation.

In conclusion, we present a taxonomy-driven framework for automated red-teaming of agentic AI systems in financial services. By grounding LLM security evaluation in concrete regulatory, compliance, and operational risk categories and combining adaptive attack generation with ensemble-based evaluation and risk-sensitive metrics, our approach provides more comprehensive and actionable insights than existing safety benchmarks. As financial institutions increasingly adopt agentic AI systems, rigorous, domain-specific LLM security evaluation will be essential to ensure trustworthy deployment. Future work will extend our framework to fully agentic workflows involving tool use, multi-agent coordination, and real-time decision-making under uncertainty, bringing risk-aware evaluation closer to real-world financial deployment.

Impact Statement

We study automated red-teaming methods for identifying LLM security failures of LLMs in financial services. This work aims to improve the security and reliability of LLMs deployed in regulated settings by providing domain-specific evaluation tools and risk-sensitive metrics. While our benchmark contains adversarial prompts describing financial misconduct, it is intended strictly for LLM security evaluation and mitigation research; we do not release operational details that would enable real-world wrongdoing. Broader impacts include improved auditing practices and safer deployment of LLM-based assistants in finance; potential misuse risks are mitigated through controlled access to data and responsible disclosure practices.
