Breaking high-temperature dielectric energy storage limits through suppression of charge carrier transport
Authors: Lei Zhang, Xuan Zhao, Xi Chen, Zhaoxin Ma, Bei Li, Mi Tang, Meng Shen, Xin Zhang, Yongming Hu, Yunbin He, Qingfeng Zhang, Xiaoguang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAromatic polymers possess superior thermal stability, rendering them promising candidates for high-temperature polymer capacitors. However, they suffer from severe intra-and inter-chain charge transport under high temperatures and fields, leading to deteriorated capacitive performances. While blending and crosslinking strategies have been attempted, inherent conjugated planar structures in the polymer backbone and interchain donor-acceptor stacking limit their effectiveness. Herein, a strict electron migration locking strategy is enabled by incorporating strong Lewis acid tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane into the Lewis-basic aromatic polyetherimide. The synergistic effects of the coordination between tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane and the carbonyl oxygen of polyetherimide, hydrogen bonding, and steric hindrance of tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane, induce short-range acceptor-acceptor and donor-donor ordered arrangements between molecular chains, and twisted molecular conformation. Consequently, the polyetherimide composite with 4% by weight tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane exhibits an ultra-high discharged energy density of 10.2 J cm−3 at 7300 kV cm−1 and 150 °C, and 7.42 J cm−3 at 6300 kV cm−1 and 200 °C with an efficiency higher than 90%, outperforming existing polymer composites.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Materials science

Introduction

Dielectric polymer capacitors serve as a core energy-storage component in modern power systems, valued for their high power density (PD), high breakdown strength (Eb), ease of processing, and mechanical flexibility1,2. Growing demands for energy storage under extreme operating conditions, such as electric vehicles, aerospace, and underground oil-gas exploration, are pushing dielectric materials to their operational limits3–7. However, polymer dielectrics cannot maintain high energy storage performances under high-temperature and high-electric-field conditions8. For instance, under an applied electric field of 500 MV m−1, the discharged energy density (Ue) and charge-discharge efficiency (η) of the commercial dielectric polymer, biaxially oriented polypropylene (BOPP), decrease constantly from 2.43 J cm−3 and 99.3% at room temperatures to 1.28 J cm−3 and 52.1% at 120 °C, respectively9. Yet many applications, such as distributed power converters in electric aircraft and electric compressors in deep oil and gas drilling, require operation at temperatures up to 150 °C10.

To meet the demands of high-temperature applications, various aromatic polymers with high glass transition temperatures (Tg), such as polyetherimide (PEI, Tg ≈ 217 °C), and polyimide (PI, Tg ≈ 360 °C), have gained considerable attention for application in the field of dielectric energy storage9,11,12. The excellent mechanical properties and thermal stability of these polymers largely originate from their aromatic backbone structures. However, the conjugated planar nature of these structures induces π-electron delocalization, which inevitably causes strong intra- and inter-chain charge carrier migration between the donor (aromatic diamine) and acceptor (aromatic dianhydride) segments under combined thermal and electrical stress13. This leads to a marked deterioration of both Ue and η, in many cases well below the glass transition temperature14–16. For instance, under 300 MV m−1, Kapton PI, shows a sharp decline in Ue and η, falling from 1.53 J cm−3 with 95.2% efficiency at room temperatures to only 0.6 J cm−3 with 21.8% efficiency at 150 °C17. Therefore, effectively suppressing charge transport at elevated temperatures has become a key issue for the development of high-temperature polymer capacitors.

A common route to impede charge transport is to introduce wide-bandgap inorganic fillers such as boron nitride (BN)18, aluminum oxide (Al2O3)19,20, and silicon dioxide (SiO2)21, which create carrier traps at the polymer/filler interface. However, inorganic fillers often result in interfacial defects, voids, and poor dispersion, which undermine trap effectiveness22. Alternatively, all-organic strategies, including the addition of high-electron-affinity molecules, crosslinking and polymer blending, can avoid issues of filler agglomeration and interfacial defects23. For instance, high-electron-affinity molecules (e.g., PCBM10, ITIC-Cl24, and NTDCA25) are incorporated into aromatic polymers to construct deep trap sites for capturing charge carriers26. Chemical and physical crosslinking generate robust three-dimensional networks that restrict molecular mobility, suppress energy loss, and improve breakdown strength23. In blending systems of multiple polymers, the aggregated structures resulting from alternating molecular chain arrangements significantly reduce free volume, thereby enhancing breakdown strength and energy storage capability23. Despite these advances, π-electron delocalization arising from conjugated planar structures remains unavoidable, limiting the effectiveness of existing strategies in enhancing high-temperature capacitive performances.

Recently, Lewis acid-base interactions have emerged as a pioneering strategy to effectively modulate the electronic properties of dielectric polymers. As demonstrated by Fan et al., incorporating a strong Lewis acid, tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane (BCF), into the Lewis-basic PEI leads to the formation of B ← O coordination bonds, which significantly lowers the polymer’s lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level and thus creates deep charge traps27. This approach has proven highly successful in suppressing conduction losses and enhancing high-temperature energy storage performances. Building on this foundational concept, we hypothesize that the role of Lewis acids extends beyond charge-trap formation. The bulky structure of BCF, coupled with potential secondary interactions like C–H•••F bonding, may induce significant conformational changes in the polymer chains and alter their packing. This would physically hinder charge transport pathways in addition to the charge trapping effect. In this work, we systematically investigate this hypothesis by introducing three additives with distinct Lewis acidities into a PEI matrix: non-acidic ortho-terphenyl (o-TP), weakly acidic triphenylborane (BPh3), and strongly acidic BCF. Through a combination of density functional theory (DFT) calculations and comprehensive experimental characterization, including Raman spectroscopy, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) and wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS), and electrical measurements, we aim to elucidate the synergistic contributions of electronic structure modification and physical structural evolution to the high-temperature energy storage performances. The results validate that BCF not only deepens charge traps but also, through synergistic B ← O coordination, steric hindrance, and hydrogen bonding, forces a twisted molecular conformation and induces a unique short-range ordered acceptor-acceptor/donor-donor chain stacking. This dual mechanism, combining charge trap engineering with structural confinement, effectively suppresses both intra- and inter-chain conduction. Consequently, the PEI/4 wt% BCF composites exhibit an ultra-high Ue of 10.2 J cm−3 at 7300 kV cm−1 and 150 °C, and 7.42 J cm−3 at 6300 kV cm−1 and 200 °C with the η higher than 90%, significantly outperforming existing all-organic dielectric systems and demonstrating the effectiveness of this multi-faceted design strategy.

Results and discussion

Molecular design of PEI-based composites

Figure 1a shows the chemical structures and electrostatic potential distribution diagrams of PEI, o-TP, BPh3, and BCF, obtained from density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The lone pair electrons of the oxygen atoms on the ether linkages and the carbonyl groups of the imide rings impart Lewis basicity to PEI. As shown, a distinct region of negative electrostatic potential is observed on the aromatic plane of o-TP, whereas no discernible electron-deficient region is found along its molecular skeleton, indicating that o-TP does not exhibit acidity. For BPh3, the low electronegativity of the H atom has a limited influence on the electron density around the B atom, rendering BPh3 a weak Lewis acid. As a contrast, in BCF, the F atoms possess high electronegativity. They can withdraw electron density from the aromatic rings through a strong σ-bond framework inductive effect, significantly reducing the electron cloud density on the empty p-orbital of the B atom and therefore creating a strong Lewis acid site. To gain deeper insight into the Lewis acid-base interaction mechanism, the binding energies (Ebind) between PEI and o-TP, BPh3, and BCF are calculated (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1). In the PEI/o-TP composites, the binding energy between PEI and o-TP is only −77.58 kcal·mol−1, which indicates that the interactions between PEI and o-TP originate primarily from electrostatic attraction between the positively charged dianhydride moieties in PEI and the negatively charged benzene rings in o-TP. In the PEI/BPh3 and PEI/BCF composites, the calculated binding energies of PEI with BPh3 and BCF are −121.15 and −192.39 kcal·mol−1, respectively, and the corresponding distances (DB-O) between B and O atoms are 2.709 Å and 1.604 Å. These values exceed the typical range of conventional van der Waals interactions, demonstrating that BCF interacts with the imide groups via the formation of a dative B←O bond.

To clarify the nature of the interactions between boron and the carbonyl oxygen atoms, the electron density around the carbonyl oxygen in pristine PEI and PEI/BPh3 and PEI/BCF composites is calculated using DFT (Fig. 1c, d, Supplementary Fig. 2). As observed, the electron density in both PEI/BPh3 and PEI/BCF is lower than that in PEI. This indicates that when the BPh3 or BCF approaches the carbonyl oxygen of PEI, the lone pair of the oxygen shifts markedly toward the boron atom, forming B←O bonds. Furthermore, because F atoms exert a stronger electron-withdrawing influence on the phenyl rings than hydrogen atoms, the electron density around the oxygen in PEI/BCF is even lower than that in PEI/BPh3, which corresponds to a stronger B←O bond in the PEI/BCF composites. Independent Gradient Model (IGM) analysis is employed to visualize the interactions between PEI and the Lewis acids (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Figs. 3–5). In the PEI/BCF system, the isosurface analysis of the B←O region displays characteristic blue-green multi-zone features. This region exhibits a significantly negative sign(λ2)ρ value, and a pronounced δginter peak appears in the reduced density gradient (RDG) plot. These quantitative indicators collectively confirm the presence of a strong, directional non-covalent attractive interaction, consistent with the signature of Lewis acid-base coordination. In comparison, although blue and green regions are also present in the PEI/BPh3 system, the green (van der Waals-type) region is more extensive. Moreover, the δginter peak is notably weaker than that in the PEI/BCF system, confirming that the Lewis acid-base interaction in PEI/BPh3 is substantially weaker.

Lewis acid-base interactions, and molecule conformational distortion

To elucidate the interactions between the PEI matrix and the additives (o-TP, BPh3, and BCF), we characterize pristine PEI along with the representative PEI/4 wt% o-TP, PEI/3 wt% BPh3, and PEI/4 wt% BCF composites using Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2a, a significant blue shift at 1560 cm−1 (aromatic ring skeleton vibration) is observed for both the PEI/3 wt% BPh3 and PEI/4 wt% BCF composites relative to pristine PEI, whereas no shift occurs in the PEI/4 wt% o-TP system. In addition, the magnitude of the blue shift is positively correlated with the strength of the Lewis acid used. The strengthening of the aromatic C = C bond indicates significant Lewis acid-base interactions between PEI and both BCF and BPh3. This electron transfer effect is corroborated by complementary evidence from Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 2b). Compared to pristine PEI, the absorption peak near 1720 cm−1, attributed to the C = O bond stretching vibration of the carbonyl group, exhibits a red shift in the PEI/4 wt% BCF composites. This phenomenon directly indicates that, due to the coordination, part of the electron density on the carbonyl oxygen is transferred to the boron atom of BCF, resulting in a decreased force constant for the C = O bond. NMR spectroscopy of 11B, 13C, and 19F further confirms the existence of Lewis acid-base interactions (Fig. 2c, d, Supplementary Fig. 6). The 11B NMR spectrum shows that the B resonance signals in the PEI/4 wt% BCF composites shift overall towards a higher field compared to those in BCF, indicating an increase in electron density on the B atom. Simultaneously, the carbonyl carbon signal in the PEI/4 wt% BCF composite shifts towards a lower field than that in pristine PEI, indicating a decrease in electron density on the carbonyl oxygen atom. Together, these two results prove the formation of the B←O coordination bond, and encouragingly, the B←O coordination bonds do not thermally dissociate in high-temperature or high-humidity environments (Supplementary Figs. 7–9). 19F NMR spectroscopy further reveals details of the electron transfer. The F resonance signals for the para- and meta-positions in the PEI/4 wt% BCF composite shift to a higher field relative to those in BCF, indicating that upon B←O coordination, electrons are delocalized through the benzene ring towards the fluorine atoms. In contrast, the signals for the ortho-position F remain largely unchanged, which is related to weak C–H•••F interactions with the adjacent benzene ring. Furthermore, under the combined influence of hydrogen bonding and steric hindrance, the molecular chains of PEI undergo distortion. The interaction mechanism between BCF and PEI is illustrated in Fig. 2e.

To assess the influences of incorporating BPh3 and BCF on the intrachain charge transfer, DFT calculations are conducted to investigate the intrachain electronic characteristics by analyzing LOL-π (Localized Orbital Locator for π electrons) isosurface, and dihedral angles of adjacent planes within the PEI backbones (Supplementary Figs. 10–13, 2f, g). As presented, with the introduction of BPh3 and BCF, the π-conjugation is progressively disrupted, and the dihedral angles at the imide linkages between the diamine (donor) and dianhydride (acceptor) units increase. Due to the greater steric hindrance of BCF compared with BPh3, coupled with its hydrogen-bonding effect, the interruption of the conjugation and the conformational distortion of the molecular chains are more pronounced in the PEI/BCF composite. To further illustrate that the incorporation of BCF induces a more twisted molecular conformation in the PEI composites, the two-dimensional (2D) 1H-1H NOESY effect of pristine PEI and PEI/4 wt% BCF are measured via a 2D NMR spectrometer, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 14, 15. The cross-peaks of [7.34, 7.88] correspond to the NOESY effect between 4H and 6H. The effect in PEI/4 wt% BCF becomes weaker compared to that in pristine PEI, indicating an increased spatial distance between these two H atoms. Simulation results also show that the spatial distance between the 4H and 6H atoms increases from 6.607 Å in pristine PEI to 6.621 Å in the PEI/4 wt% BCF composite (Supplementary Fig. 16). Consequently, the intrachain charge transfer in the PEI-based composites, particularly in PEI/BCF, predominantly localizes at the imide linkages28. The reduced π-electronic delocalization is also evidenced by the blue shift of the absorption edge in PEI/BCF relative to that in pristine PEI, as observed in transmission spectra of PEI, BCF, and PEI/4 wt% BCF composites (Supplementary Fig. 17).

Short-range ordered arrangement of molecule chain

To further investigate the impact of Lewis acid-base interactions on the structural ordering of pristine PEI and its composites, we perform SAXS and WAXS analyses. As observed in the 2D WAXS patterns (Fig. 3a), pristine PEI exhibits diffuse diffraction rings, indicating a predominantly amorphous structure with no obvious short-range order. In contrast to the minor changes induced by introducing o-TP, the Lewis acid BPh3 generates a distinct, intense isotropic amorphous ring. This effect becomes even more pronounced with the stronger Lewis acid BCF, which yields the brightest diffraction rings. In the one-dimensional (1D) WAXS patterns (Fig. 3b), the main scattering peaks in the scattering vector q of 1.14–1.21 Å−1 are attributed to the ordered chain packing (ch-pack) structure, while the subtle shoulder at higher q values is ascribed to π–π stacking interactions between imide rings and benzene rings of adjacent polymer chains13. The incorporation of BCF induces systematic structural perturbations in the PEI composites, as evidenced by a shift of the main diffraction peak from q = 1.14 Å−1 in pristine PEI to q = 1.21 Å−1 in PEI/4 wt% BCF. This shift corresponds to a decrease in the mean intermolecular distance from 5.51 Å to 5.19 Å. Along with this reduction, the chain-packing diffraction peaks become sharper, with the full width at half maximum (FWHM) decreasing from 0.5 Å−1 in pristine PEI to 0.48 Å−1 in PEI/4 wt% BCF (Supplementary Fig. 18). These observations indicate that BCF incorporation promotes more compact and ordered chain packing, thereby enhancing the short-range ordering within the PEI composites29. The 1D WAXS patterns of pure BCF demonstrate that the increased ordered chain packing structure observed in PEI/4 wt% BCF originates from structural evolution of the polymer matrix itself, rather than from BCF (Supplementary Fig. 19). Encouragingly, the short-range ordered stacking induced by BCF can be maintained even at 200 °C (Supplementary Fig. 20). Normally, intermolecular charge transfer between electron-donating diamine units and electron-accepting dianhydride units drives interchain charge transport13,28. However, in the ordered structure induced by B←O bonds, adjacent PEI molecule chains are driven to form donor-donor and acceptor-acceptor stacking (Fig. 3c), which is also corroborated by the decreased chain spacing (Fig. 3d). This tight packing forces the chains into more pronounced conformational distortion. For the PEI/BCF composite, BCF forms B←O coordination bonds, hydrogen bonds, and steric hindrance with PEI, inducing twisted chain conformations and short-range ordered donor-donor and acceptor-acceptor stacking. This localized densification (decreased interchain spacing) coupled with chain rigidification generates more and larger non-uniformly distributed free volume holes in regions away from the interaction sites. Consequently, despite the reduced average interchain spacing, the total fractional free volume (FFV) increases compared to that in PEI/BPh3, as quantified by positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy (PALS) (Fig. 3e)15,28. Both the altered stacking mode and the increased free volume act to disrupt and inhibit interchain charge transfer. The one-dimensional SAXS profiles show no distinct scattering peaks (Fig. 3f), while the two-dimensional SAXS patterns exhibit no significant scattering features, with the signal decaying monotonically from the center outward in the PEI and its composites (insets in Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 21). These observations demonstrate a uniform electron density distribution in the material and provide no evidence of long-range periodic layered structure29.

Charge carrier transport, thermal stability, and mechanical properties

The breakdown strength of polymers at elevated temperatures is closely linked to their electrical insulation, thermal stability, and mechanical properties. Figure 4a presents the activation energy (Ea) of pristine PEI and its composites, obtained using the Arrhenius formula (Supplementary Note 1). The activation energy is most significantly enhanced in the PEI/4 wt% BCF composite, reaching 0.818 eV, followed by notable increases in the PEI/3 wt% BPh3 and PEI/4 wt% o-TP composites compared to pristine PEI. This demonstrates that charge carriers in PEI-based composites require higher energy to escape from traps30. The hopping distances (λ) of charge carriers at 200 °C, calculated using the hopping conduction model (Supplementary Note 2), decrease from 1.58 nm for pristine PEI to 1.30 nm for PEI/4 wt% o-TP, 1.24 nm for PEI/3 wt% BPh3, and 1.04 nm for PEI/4 wt% BCF (Fig. 4b), suggesting an increased density of charge traps22. This trend is further supported by thermally stimulated depolarization current (TSDC) measurements (Fig. 4c). Pristine PEI exhibits a peak current intensity of 3.1 pA and a trapped charge number of 1.1 nC, obtained via the half-width method (Supplementary Note 3). With the introduction of o-TP and BPh3, these values increase to 4.2 pA and 8.9 pA, and to 1.11 nC and 4.06 nC, respectively. Notably, they further increase to 16.2 pA and 5.48 nC for PEI/BCF composites, indicating higher deep trap densities in PEI/BCF composites31.

To further elucidate the regulatory effect of Lewis acid introduction on the charge transport process, we employ Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) to detect the surface potential of pristine PEI and PEI/4 wt% BCF composites (Fig. 4d, e, g, h). The PEI and PEI/4 wt% BCF composites are cast between a pair of metal electrodes with the electrode/dielectric interface clearly exposed. Charge injection is implemented using an external circuit, which is then disconnected during the scanning. The test results show that the surface potential of the PEI/4 wt% BCF composite film is significantly lower than that of pristine PEI, indicating a higher work function in the composite. Subsequently, a constant voltage of +1 V is applied along the x direction to both PEI and PEI/4 wt% BCF for 5 min, and after removing the voltage for 60 s, the surface potential is scanned. As observed, more injected charge carriers accumulate on the PEI/4 wt% BCF composites and dissipate more slowly than those in pristine PEI. This is attributed to the introduction of BCF increasing deep traps, which inhibits charge carrier transport. The significantly increased deep traps in PEI/BPh3, and particularly in PEI/BCF, demonstrate that the formation of Lewis acid-base adducts can effectively suppress the de-trapping of charge carriers, thereby reducing leakage current under high temperatures and electric fields10,29.

To probe the underlying mechanism of deep-trap formation induced by the incorporation of Lewis acids, DFT calculations are performed to obtain the energy band structures of pristine PEI, and PEI/BCF (Fig. 4f). As presented, the LUMO energy level decreases from −2.38 eV for PEI to −3.38 eV for PEI/BCF, while the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy level remains almost unchanged. This leads to the formation of deep trap states within the PEI chains, which effectively inhibit the transport of charge carriers10. In PEI, electron transfer occurs from the electron-donating diamine to the electron-accepting dianhydride due to strong electrostatic interaction27,32. The significant reduction in the LUMO energy level upon BCF incorporation is fundamentally attributed to the strong electron-withdrawing nature of the BCF molecule, which enhances the electron-accepting ability of the dianhydride. Regarding the HOMO energy level, because the orbitals near the HOMO are primarily contributed by the diamine segments, the introduction of the Lewis acids BCF has almost no effect on the HOMO energy level27,32. The leakage current and resistivity variations of pristine PEI and its composites under different electric fields and temperatures are investigated (Supplementary Figs. 22–24). For pristine PEI, as the temperature increases from 25 °C to 200 °C, the leakage current density rises significantly from 4.5 × 10−8 A cm−2 to 3.18 × 10−5 A cm−2 at 2000 kV cm−1 while the resistivity drops sharply from 4.45 × 1010 Ω•cm to 6.29 × 107 Ω•cm. In contrast, introducing BPh3 and BCF into the composites effectively suppresses leakage current at high electric fields and elevated temperatures. For instance, in the PEI/4 wt% BCF composite, the leakage current density only increases to 1.6 × 10−7 A cm−2, and the resistivity decreases only to 1.25 × 1010 Ω•cm at 200 °C and 2000 kV cm−1. Supplementary Figs. 25, 26 present contour plots of leakage current density for PEI and its composites under varying temperatures and electric fields, offering a clear visualization of their combined effects. The lower leakage current density in the PEI composites compared to pristine PEI helps enhance high-temperature energy-storage capacity by minimizing conduction loss.

In addition to enhancing electrical insulation properties, the incorporation of o-TP, BPh3, and BCF also significantly improves the thermal stability of PEI-based composites. The glass transition temperature (Tg) increases markedly from 203.03 °C for pristine PEI to 212.13 °C for PEI/4 wt% o-TP, 218.95 °C for PEI/3 wt% BPh3, and 232.72 °C for PEI/4 wt% BCF (Supplementary Fig. 27a). This rise in Tg is primarily attributed to the formation of molecular twisting conformations and B←O bonds, which restricts the thermal motion of the polymer chains13–15. Notably, in PEI/BCF, molecular twisting conformation by strong B←O coordination has a more pronounced positive effect on the pronounced enhancement of Tg. This is further confirmed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) results (Supplementary Fig. 27b). The 5 wt% weight loss temperatures of PEI/4 wt% o-TP, PEI/3 wt% BPh3, and PEI/4 wt% BCF composites are 331.6 °C, 511.9 °C, and 520.1 °C, respectively, all higher than that of pristine PEI (218.7 °C). In addition, as observed in dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) profiles (Supplementary Fig. 28), the storage moduli of PEI/4 wt% BCF(e.g., 5.61 GPa at 150 °C and 4.49 GPa at 200 °C) are substantially larger than those of pristine PEI (e.g., 2.64 GPa at 150 °C and 0.32 GPa at 200 °C), indicating a significant improvement in rigidity. This conclusion is further supported by increased Young’s modulus (Fig. 4i, Supplementary Fig. 29), which rises from 1.07 GPa for pristine PEI to 1.58 GPa for PEI/4 wt% o-TP, 1.83 GPa for PEI/3 wt% BPh3, and 2.13 GPa for PEI/4 wt% BCF. To investigate the origin of the increased rigidity in the PEI composites, dielectric loss-temperature measurements are performed on pristine PEI and PEI/4 wt% BCF over a wide frequency range (Supplementary Fig. 30). As observed, both the high-temperature and low-temperature dielectric loss peaks (corresponding to the α and β relaxations, respectively) in the PEI/4 wt% BCF composite shift to higher temperatures relative to those in pristine PEI. This indicates that the incorporation of BCF effectively restricts both large-scale segmental motions and local chain motions. The markedly enhanced electrical insulation, thermal stability, and mechanical strength are conducive to improving the Eb, which would be validated in subsequent sections. Moreover, cross-sectional field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, along with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping of the representative PEI/4 wt% BCF, show that the boron and fluorine atoms are uniformly distributed throughout the polymer matrix, confirming that the Lewis acid is homogeneously dispersed without forming nano-aggregates (Supplementary Figs. 31, 32). Atomic force microscope (AFM) images of PEI and PEI/4 wt% BCF composite films reveal that after the introduction of the BCF small molecule (Supplementary Fig. 33), the PEI/BCF film surface remains flat and free of apparent defects, which is also beneficial for increasing the Eb.

High-temperature energy storage and charge-discharge properties

Frequency dependence of the dielectric constant and loss of pristine PEI and PEI-based composites at 25 °C, 150 °C and 200 °C is shown in Supplementary Figs. 34–36. For pristine PEI, when the temperature rises to 200 °C, the dielectric loss increases sharply due to enhanced conduction loss, which is quite detrimental to the energy-storage performance. In contrast, with the introduction of o-TP and the Lewis acids, the dielectric loss of the PEI composites varies only slightly over the measured temperature range. Interestingly, after the PEI/BCF composites are continuously exposed to 200 °C for 200 h, or a high-humidity environment, the dielectric loss at 150 and 200 °C remains very low, indicating that this polymer has excellent long-term reliability and weak sensitivity to moisture (Supplementary Figs. 37, 38). At 200 °C, the Eb values of 6258 kV cm−1 for PEI/4 wt% o-TP, 7022 kV cm−1 for PEI/3 wt% BPh3, and 7245 kV cm−1 for PEI/4 wt% BCF are maintained, corresponding to improvements of 31%, 47% and 52%, respectively, over pristine PEI (Eb = 4775 kV cm−1). The decline in Eb of PEI composites at excessive loadings of o-TP, BPh3, and BCF originates from the accumulation of trapped space charge. For instance, upon incorporation of BCF, the PEI/BCF composite exhibits pronounced space charge build-up under an applied field of 300 kV cm−1, as confirmed by the pulsed-electro-acoustic (PEA) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 40). As the loading level increases, the number of trapped charges rises, which induces localized electric field enhancements. These enhancements prematurely initiate avalanche breakdown and thus reduce the breakdown strength.

Supplementary Figs. 41–46 present electric displacement-electric field (D–E) loops of pristine PEI and PEI-based composites with various additive contents measured near their Eb at 150 °C and 200 °C. From these loops, the capacitive properties of pristine PEI and its composites at different electric fields are derived (Fig. 5b, f, Supplementary Fig. 47). For pristine PEI, although the Uₑ increases significantly with rising electric field, the η declines sharply. In contrast, with the introduction of o-TP, BPh3 and BCF, the η can be maintained at a high level up to a high electric field. This phenomenon is even more pronounced in the PEI/BCF composites. Figure 5c, g displays the Uₑ (with η ≥ 90%) and the maximum discharged energy density (Umax) of pristine PEI and its composites. As plotted, the addition of 4 wt% o-TP slightly improves Uₑ and Umax from 1.72 J cm−3 and 4.68 J cm−3 to 6.89 J cm−3 and 8.63 J cm−3 at 150 °C, and from 0.84 J cm−3 and 3.28 J cm−3 to 5.05 J cm−3 and 6.78 J cm−3 at 200 °C. Excitingly, when the Lewis acids (3 wt% BPh3 and 4 wt% BCF) are introduced, the Uₑ and Umax are substantially enhanced to 8.91 J cm−3 and 9.26 J cm−3, and 10.2 J cm−3 and 11.33 J cm−3 at 150 °C, and 6.18 J cm−3 and 7.89 J cm−3, and 7.42 J cm−3 and 9.44 J cm−3 at 200 °C. This indicates Lewis acid-base interactions exhibit a pronounced effect on improving the energy storage density, while maintaining high η. Moreover, incorporating BCF into another Lewis-basic polyethersulfone (PES) also enhances its high-temperature energy storage properties, demonstrating that a strict electron migration locking strategy serves as a general design principle (Supplementary Fig. 48). Figure 5d, h compares the Uₑ (η ≥ 90%) of current all-organic composite dielectrics measured at 150 °C4,13,14,16,26,28,30,33–36 and 200 °C4,13,14,16,26,28,30,34–38. Clearly, the Uₑ values of 10.2 J cm−3 at 7300 kV cm−1 and 150 °C, and 7.42 J cm−3 at 6300 kV cm−1 and 200 °C in this study surpass those of existing polymers and polymer composites, providing strategies for enhancing high-temperature capacitive performances in all-organic dielectrics.

To assess the uniformity of the composites, large-area PEI/4 wt% BCF composite films are subjected to energy storage tests at 150 °C and 200 °C, as shown in Fig. 5i, j. Each film is divided into six regions, and each region is tested under an electric field of up to 6000 kV cm−1. The results reveal consistent capacitive performance across all regions with no significant variation, confirming excellent uniformity of the composites. Such performance consistency demonstrates the potential of PEI/BCF for industrial applications and highlights its suitability for large-scale manufacturing processes. Figure 5k, Supplementary Figs. 49, 50 illustrate the cycling and frequency dependence of the energy storage characteristics of the PEI/4 wt% BCF composites at 3000 kV cm−1and 200 °C. Throughout 1–106 cycles and over the frequency range of 100–500 Hz, each D–E curve remains nearly coincident and consistently slim. Consequently, the variations in Uₑ and η are only 4.3% and 3.8% across the cycling test, and 4.0% and 5.3% across the frequency range, indicating excellent anti-fatigue characteristics and frequency stability of the capacitive properties even under high temperatures coupled with high electric fields. These are very important factors for electronic devices22,39, and they therefore significantly broaden the application range of PEI/BCF capacitors.

In summary, the effects of o-TP, BPh3, and BCF additives, which represent no, weak, and strong Lewis acidity, respectively, on high-temperature capacitive properties of PEI-based composites are investigated. DFT predictions reveal that the interaction between PEI and o-TP originates primarily from electrostatic attraction, whereas the B atoms in BPh3 and BCF form B←O bonds with the carbonyl oxygen atoms in PEI. The combined effects of C–H•••F bonding and the steric hindrance of BCF induce a twisted molecular conformation, which disrupts the conjugated aromatic structure, reduces π-electron delocalization, and thereby suppresses intrachain charge transport. Furthermore, the incorporation of Lewis acids, especially the strong Lewis acid BCF, promotes short-range ordered stacking of acceptor-acceptor and donor-donor between molecular chains. This effectively hinders interchain electron transfer caused by donor-acceptor stacking. The restricted charge carrier transfer greatly causes the reduction of the conduction loss and the enhancement of the Eb. For instance, at 200 °C, the Eb increases from 4775 kV cm−1 for pristine PEI to 6258 kV cm−1 for PEI/4 wt% o-TP, 7022 kV cm−1 for PEI/3 wt% BPh3, and 7245 kV cm−1 for PEI/4 wt% BCF. Consequently, benefiting from low conduction loss and high Eb, the PEI/4 wt% BCF composites exhibit an ultra-high discharged energy density of 10.2 J cm−3 at 7300 kV cm−1 and 150 °C, and 7.42 J cm−3 at 6300 kV cm−1 and 200 °C, with the η higher than 90%, outperforming existing all-organic dielectric systems. This work offers a route for simultaneously achieving high Ue and η in all-organic dielectric capacitors under high-temperature operating conditions.

Methods

Materials

Tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane (BCF, ≥97%) and ortho-terphenyl (o-TP, ≥99%) were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd, and triphenylborane (BPh3, 96%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Polyetherimide pellets (Ultem 1000, PEI) and commercial biaxially oriented polypropylene (BOPP) films with a thickness of about 9.8 μm were provided by Polyk Technologies Company. N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) was obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.

Preparation of pristine PEI and PEI-based composites

Pristine PEI solution was prepared by dissolving 0.75 g of PEI particles into NMP solution at a concentration of 150 mg mL-1. To fabricate PEI/o-TP, PEI/BPh3, and PEI/BCF composites with o-TP, BPh3, and BCF contents of 2 wt%, 3 wt%, 4 wt%, 5 wt%, and 6 wt%, respectively, the corresponding additives were separately added to the above PEI solution. Subsequently, all solutions were stirred at 60 °C for 6 h to obtain homogeneous dispersions. The resulting solution was then cast onto a clean glass substrate and transferred to a vacuum oven, where it was sequentially dried at 60 °C for 10 h to evaporate the NMP solvent, and then heated at 220 °C for 10 min on a hot plate to remove residual solvent. Finally, the film with a thickness of 10∼12 μm was peeled off the glass substrate using deionized water. For electrical characterization, gold electrodes with a diameter of 2 mm were fabricated on the surfaces of both the pristine PEI and its composites.

Characterization

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) was recorded with the JNM-ECZL GNMR spectrometer (JEOL, Japan) using deuterated chloroform as the solvent. The two-dimensional 1H-1H NOESY spectrum of the PEI and PEI/4 wt% BCF composite was achieved by the two-dimensional NMR spectrometer (Ascend-400 NMR, Bruker, Germany) with deuterated chloroform as the solvent. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were obtained using the Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Transmission spectra were characterized via Evolution One Plus UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Raman spectra were performed using LabRAM HR Evolution spectrometer (Horiba, France). Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) and wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) measurements were conducted with the SAXS instrument (Xeuss 2.0, XENOCS, France). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired using the D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (Bruker, Germany). Cross-section images are achieved by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Sigma 500, Zeiss, Germany) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200X, FEI, USA). The surface morphology of the polymer films was observed by the atomic force microscopy (AFM, Dimension Edge, Bruker, Germany). Young’s modulus was measured using the electromechanical universal testing machine (C43.104EY, MTS, USA). Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) of the samples was performed in tensile mode using a DMA1 instrument (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) under the frequency of 1 Hz, displacement amplitude of 20 µm, temperature range from 50 to 250 °C, heating rate of 5 °C min−1, and air atmosphere. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curves and thermogravimetric analysis(TGA) curves were obtained using the thermogravimetric and differential scanning calorimetry analysis (STA449F3, Netzsch, Germany). Thermally stimulated depolarization current (TSDC) measurements were carried out using the FMET-1000 system (Wuhan Yanhe Technology Co., Ltd., China). Samples were polarized at 200 °C under a field of 200 kV cm−1 for 10 min, and then rapidly cooled to −30 °C while maintaining the field. After holding for 10 min, they were short-circuited, and finally heated to 250 °C at a rate of 3 °C·min−1 while recording the TSDC density. The surface potential was achieved via Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM, MFP-3D-Origin, Oxford, USA). The space charge distribution was tested by the pulsed-electro-acoustic (PEA, HY-PEA-DPT01, China) method. During the measurement, the sample was subjected to an electric field of 300 kV cm−1, and the space charge density was achieved after the electric field was applied for 15 min. Dielectric properties were measured using the E4980A precision LCR meter (Agilent, USA). The breakdown strength was evaluated using a withstand voltage tester (NJC5010, Tongguo Technology, China). Ferroelectric properties and leakage current were characterized using the Precision LC II ferroelectric test system (Radiant, USA). Charge-discharge properties were evaluated via a discharge resistance, inductance and capacitance (RLC) load circuit (CFD-003, Tongguo Technology, China). To confirm the long-term stability of the B–O coordination bond, FTIR spectra and dielectric properties were measured for the representative PEI/4 wt% BCF composite after heating at 200 °C for 200 h. The long-term thermal aging test was conducted as follows: the PEI/4 wt% BCF composite films were placed in a vacuum oven, heated from room temperature to 200 °C, and then maintained at 200 °C for 200 h. After aging, the films were naturally cooled to room temperature prior to FTIR and dielectric property measurements.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculation

All molecular monomers and their corresponding complexes were systematically investigated using DFT calculations implemented in the Gaussian 16 software package. Geometry optimizations were performed employing the B3LYP hybrid exchange-correlation functional with Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction and the def2-SVP basis set, without imposing any symmetry constraints. Frequency calculations were conducted at the same level of theory to verify that the optimized structures correspond to true minima on the potential energy surface. Subsequent single-point energy calculations were carried out on the optimized geometries using the B3LYP(D3) functional in conjunction with the larger def2-TZVP basis set, in order to obtain more accurate electronic energies and frontier molecular orbital information. Based on the wavefunction files generated from the Gaussian calculations, electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution analysis, highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO)-lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level calculations, and an independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) analysis were performed using the Multiwfn program (version 3.8)42,43. Visualization of molecular structures, electrostatic potential maps, and IGMH isosurfaces was conducted with the VMD software44. The binding energy was calculated by the following equation45:Ebind=Ecomplex−∑iEi, where Ecomplex is the total electronic energy of the optimized composite system, and Ei represents the electronic energy of each isolated fragment (e.g., polymer segment and guest molecule) calculated at the same level of theory. All ground-state calculations were performed at the M062X functional with the 6–31 G(d, p) basis set, incorporating D3 dispersion correction. For all excited-state calculations, the CAM-B3LYP/6-31 G(d) level was employed, with 20 excited states computed to locate the charge-transfer-dominated CT state.
