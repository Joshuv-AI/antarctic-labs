1.
Introduction

1.1.
Broader view

Gauss’ circle problem
[
15
]
is an archetypal problem at the interface of analytic number theory and planar geometry. Although it has been studied intensively for more than two centuries, it remains open, despite substantial progress obtained by a wide range of methods and ideas
[
3
]
. One aim of the present article is to introduce into this circle of questions another tool, arising from recent developments in what may be called “non-algebraic tropical geometry” or “tropical optics”. Namely, to a compact convex domain
\Omega
one can canonically associate a function
Z_{\Omega}(s)
of one complex variable, which we call its tropical zeta function. Just as the zeros of the Riemann zeta function govern fine asymptotic properties of the distribution of prime numbers
[
47
,
42
]
, we expect the poles of the tropical zeta function to play a central role in obtaining sharp bounds on the error term in lattice-point counting for dilates of the domain.

Indeed, for a planar convex domain, the main term in lattice-point counting is quadratic and equals the area of the domain, whereas the general bound for the error term is linear in the dilation parameter
R
. The coefficient of this linear term is determined by the straight segments of the boundary, and therefore, in the strictly convex case, one can do better. Assuming that the boundary is sufficiently smooth and has everywhere nonvanishing curvature, van der Corput proved
[
52
]
about a century ago that the error term is at most of order
R^{2/3}
, see also
[
24
,
20
]
. In terms of tropical zeta functions, the first bound corresponds to the existence of a rightmost pole at
s=1
for domains whose boundary contains rational straight segments, with residue equal to the lattice perimeter. For domains with sufficiently smooth boundary, the next pole, moving from right to left, appears at
s=\frac{2}{3}
, and the corresponding residue is universally proportional to the equiaffine arc length of the boundary. This is the main result of the paper and the technically most difficult part of the argument.

The definition of the tropical zeta function considered in this paper originates in the mathematical study of self-organized criticality. More precisely, finite-point perturbations of the maximal stable state in the Abelian sandpile model give rise to tropical analytic curves and to the tropical power series defining them
[
27
,
32
,
30
,
28
]
. One such series, corresponding to the action of a point perturbation at the distinguished central point, is the tropical distance function of a convex domain, while the associated tropical analytic curve is the so-called tropical caustic
[
43
,
51
]
. The convex domain itself appears as the domain of convergence of this tropical series, which defines a positive concave piecewise linear function on its interior. Integrating its
s
-th power over the domain then produces a function of the complex variable
s
, which we call the tropical zeta function of the domain. The central conjecture for this class of functions is that they admit meromorphic continuation to the entire complex plane.

One case in which the conjecture holds almost trivially is that of polygons whose sides have rational slopes. Such polygons are naturally viewed as moment polytopes of compact symplectic toric surfaces. From the algebro-geometric point of view, these surfaces may be obtained from a minimal model by a sequence of blow-ups at torus-fixed points. From the symplectic point of view, however, each blow-up comes with a natural “size”, measured by the symplectic area of the corresponding exceptional divisor. In this setting, our zeta function may be interpreted as a Dirichlet generating series for the sizes of these blow-ups, with the symplectic structure encoded through tropical optics and with a particular minimal model and blow-up chain chosen canonically. From this perspective, one sees that the corresponding zeta function extends meromorphically to the whole complex plane. Moreover, it has only two poles, both reflecting basic geometric information about the toric surface: the residue at
s=1
again corresponds to the symplectic area of the anticanonical class, while the residue at
s=0
is the negative of its self-intersection. The same, though infinite, triangle-cutting mechanism also appears in the algebro-geometric story
[
36
,
6
]
behind the universal elliptic curve over the moduli space
A_{1}
of elliptic curves.

Apart from this “trivial” algebraic case, there is essentially only one domain for which we can currently write the tropical zeta function explicitly in terms of known special functions. This domain arises as the limit shape obtained from concentration of measure for the uniform distribution on lattice polygons contained in a fixed square, as the lattice mesh tends to zero
[
53
,
1
,
2
]
. For this particular domain, the tropical zeta function is

\left(8-2^{2-s}\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)}{\zeta(3s)}\right)\frac{1}{s(s-1)},

where
\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}
denotes Witten’s
\mathrm{SU}(3)
zeta function, arising in the context of two-dimensional Yang–Mills theory, and
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function. The pole structure of
\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}
was described by Romik
[
48
]
, who showed that all of its poles are simple and occur at
s=\frac{2}{3}
and at
s=\frac{1}{2}-k
for
k=0,1,2,\dots
. As a consequence, the basic corollary that the lattice perimeter of the tropical wave front at time
t\to 0^{+}
, that is, the lattice perimeter of the
t
-level set of the tropical distance function, is of order
t^{1/3}
, which is achieved by further enhancing the result on analytic continuation to the strip
\Re(s)>\frac{3}{5}
and a Tauberian argument, can be sharpened in this special case: the second term in the asymptotic expansion of the tropical wave-front lattice perimeter is proportional to
t^{1/2}
, and, assuming the Riemann hypothesis, the third term is of order at most
t^{5/6}
. In fact, we expect this behavior to be universal for sufficiently smooth convex domains with everywhere nonvanishing curvature.

We emphasize that, although the main body of the present article is devoted to the two-dimensional case, the underlying construction is meaningful in arbitrary dimensions. One may also hope that the convexity assumption can eventually be weakened, or perhaps removed entirely. In such a framework, it is conceivable that tropical zeta functions could be attached to a broader class of arithmetico-geometric objects and would define a homomorphism from a suitable motivic ring to the field of meromorphic functions, somewhat in the spirit of the Hodge–Deligne polynomial in birational geometry
[
10
,
11
,
9
]
and of Igusa zeta functions in arithmetic geometry
[
21
,
22
]
. Broader continuation questions, as well as higher-dimensional analogues of the affine residue, remain open.

1.2.
Main results

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact convex domain with nonempty interior, and let

h_{\Omega}(u)=\min_{x\in\Omega}\langle u,x\rangle,\qquad u\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

be its lower support function. We define the associated tropical distance-to-the-boundary function by

\rho_{\Omega}(x)=\min_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\mathrm{prim}}}\bigl(\langle u,x\rangle-h_{\Omega}(u)\bigr),\qquad x\in\Omega^{\circ},

where
\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\mathrm{prim}}
denotes the primitive lattice directions. This function is concave, positive, and piecewise linear on the interior
\Omega^{\circ}
, and extends continuously by zero to the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

Definition 1
.

The tropical zeta function of
\Omega
is defined by

Z_{\Omega}(s)=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\Omega}(x)^{\,s-2}\,dV,

where
dV
denotes Lebesgue measure, and
\Re(s)
is initially assumed to be sufficiently large
1
1

1

More precisely, the integral converges at least for
\Re(s)>1.
.

Thus one associates to
\Omega
an
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
-invariant zeta function built from the canonical tropical distance function.

The function
Z_{\Omega}(s)
admits a natural Mellin interpretation. If

\Omega_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega:\rho_{\Omega}(x)\geq t\}

denotes the inward tropical wave front at time
t
, and if
L_{\Omega}(t)
denotes its lattice length, then

Z_{\Omega}(s)=\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}t^{s-2}L_{\Omega}(t)\,dt,\qquad m_{\Omega}:=\max_{\Omega}\rho_{\Omega}.

Thus the tropical zeta function is the Mellin transform of the lattice-length profile of the tropical wave fronts. In particular, the singularities of
Z_{\Omega}(s)
are governed by the short-time asymptotics of this boundary evolution.

Our first result concerns the polygonal case. If
\Omega
is a polygon whose sides have rational slopes, then
Z_{\Omega}(s)
extends meromorphically to the whole complex plane, and its rightmost pole occurs at
s=1
. The residue at this pole is the lattice length of
\partial\Omega
. Thus, for rational polygons, the leading singularity records the lattice-visible part of the boundary.

Our main result concerns the opposite regime, namely smooth convex domains with everywhere nonvanishing curvature. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact convex domain with
C^{3}
-smooth boundary and everywhere nonvanishing curvature. Then the pole at
s=1
disappears, and the leading singularity moves to
s=\tfrac{2}{3}
. More precisely, we prove that
Z_{\Omega}(s)
admits a meromorphic continuation to a neighborhood of
s=\tfrac{2}{3}
, with a simple pole there, and

\operatorname{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\Omega}(s)=\frac{3^{5/2}}{2^{5/3}\pi^{3}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\partial\Omega),

where
\Gamma
denotes the gamma function.

Equivalently,

\operatorname{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\Omega}(s)=\frac{3^{5/2}}{2^{5/3}\pi^{3}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa^{1/3}\,ds.

Thus, in the smooth strictly convex case with everywhere nonvanishing curvature, the leading singularity of the tropical zeta function is governed by equiaffine geometry.

The proof is based on a reduction from the interior Mellin integral to a boundary Dirichlet series. In dimension two, one associates to
\partial\Omega
a boundary zeta series

F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}\\
ad-bc=1\end{subarray}}f_{\partial\Omega}(a,b,c,d)^{s},

where the summands are determined by neighboring primitive directions and the corresponding support defects. If
\widehat{\Omega}
denotes the minimal model of
\Omega
, then one has an exact identity

s(s-1)Z_{\Omega}(s)=-F_{\partial\Omega}(s)+H_{\widehat{\Omega}}(s),

where
H_{\widehat{\Omega}}(s)
is an explicitly computable holomorphic function coming from the symplectic minimal model of
\Omega
. Thus the singular behavior of
Z_{\Omega}(s)
near
s=\frac{2}{3}
is entirely governed by the boundary series.

A model case is provided by an arc of a parabola. For the corresponding parabolic domain, the boundary zeta series can be computed explicitly and turns out to be the primitive Mordell–Tornheim series, equivalently Witten’s
\mathrm{SU}(3)
zeta function after the natural normalization. This model already contains the pole at
s=\tfrac{2}{3}
and explains the appearance of the same transcendental constant as in the general residue formula.

The parabolic case is the simplest model because the second derivative of the Legendre dual is constant; consequently, the support defect is governed only by the universal arithmetic weight attached to a Farey pair, and the boundary zeta series reduces to a primitive Mordell–Tornheim series. For a general smooth convex arc, by contrast, the second derivative varies along the slope parameter, so one is led to a Farey sum with a nonconstant weight. The main analytic task is therefore to separate this slowly varying geometric factor from the arithmetic summation. We do this by replacing the exact defect by an endpoint model and then studying the resulting weighted Farey series using Fejér approximation together with power-saving estimates for incomplete Kloosterman sums. This yields the residue as an averaged slope-space density

\bigl((g^{\ast})^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)\bigr)^{2/3}\,d\alpha,

and Legendre duality converts this density into the equiaffine arc length element

\bigl((g^{\ast})^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)\bigr)^{2/3}\,d\alpha=(g^{\prime\prime}(x))^{1/3}\,dx=\kappa^{1/3}\,ds.

Thus the explicit parabolic computation extends to the general case after decoupling the variable curvature from the Farey arithmetic.

Among zeta-type constructions attached to convex domains, the present one is especially well suited to arithmetic questions: it is
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
-invariant, so it respects the natural symmetry of the lattice, and in the planar smooth convex case with everywhere nonvanishing curvature its leading nontrivial residue is the equiaffine length of the boundary. This is exactly the type of affine invariant that is known to control the behavior of rational and lattice points near convex curves; see
[
46
,
18
,
19
]
. In this sense, our zeta function is designed to capture the arithmetic geometry of the boundary rather than only its Euclidean shape.

2.
The tropical zeta function in the integral form

In this section, we define the zeta function of a convex body by an integral over the body itself. In dimension
2
, this integral presentation is equivalent, up to an explicit holomorphic correction term, to a boundary Dirichlet series built from primitive lattice directions and Farey neighbors.

2.1.
The tropical distance function

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact convex body with nonempty interior. For

u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

let

h_{\Omega}(u):=\min_{x\in\Omega}\langle u,x\rangle

be the lower support function of
\Omega
. Denote by

\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\mathrm{prim}}:=\{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}:\gcd(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=1\}

the set of primitive lattice directions.

Since

\langle mu,x\rangle-h_{\Omega}(mu)=m\bigl(\langle u,x\rangle-h_{\Omega}(u)\bigr)\qquad(m\in\mathbb{N}),

it suffices to minimize over primitive lattice vectors.

Definition 2
.

For
x\in\Omega^{\circ}
, define the
tropical distance function
of
x
by

\rho_{\Omega}(x):=\min_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\mathrm{prim}}}\bigl(\langle u,x\rangle-h_{\Omega}(u)\bigr).

Indeed, if
x\in\Omega^{\circ}
, then

\langle u,x\rangle-h_{\Omega}(u)\geq c_{x}\|u\|

for some
c_{x}>0
, so the minimum is attained.

The function
\rho_{\Omega}
is continuous on
\Omega^{\circ}
, positive in the interior, and extends continuously by zero to
\partial\Omega
. It may be viewed as an
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
-invariant analogue of a distance-to-the-boundary function, built from primitive lattice support functions rather than Euclidean normals.

Definition 3
.

The
tropical zeta function (in the integral form)
of
\Omega
is

Z_{\Omega}(s):=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\Omega}(x)^{\,s-n}\,dV,

initially defined for
\Re(s)
sufficiently large.

The function
Z_{\Omega}
inherits
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-invariance and translation invariance from the coordinate-free definition of
\rho_{\Omega}
; that is,

Z_{A(\Omega)+v}(s)=Z_{\Omega}(s)

for all
A\in\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As a conceptual remark, we emphasize that the tropical zeta function by itself is not sufficient to yield an exact formula for the number of lattice points in the domain, since this number varies under continuous families of translations of the domain.

The normalization
s-n
is chosen so that

Z_{\Omega}(n)=\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega).

More generally, this normalization ensures that
Z_{\Omega}
is homogeneous with respect to dilations of
\Omega
in the sense that

Z_{r\Omega}(s)=r^{s}Z_{\Omega}(s)

for every
r>0
. In particular, a residue of its analytic continuation at a real point
d
is a homogeneous invariant of
\Omega
of degree
d
.

2.2.
Mellin form

Let

m_{\Omega}:=\max_{x\in\Omega}\rho_{\Omega}(x)

and, for
t\in[0,m_{\Omega}]
, define

\Omega_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega:\rho_{\Omega}(x)\geq t\}.

Then for
\Re(s)>n
, the layer-cake formula gives

Z_{\Omega}(s)=(s-n)\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}t^{\,s-n-1}\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{t})\,dt.

Thus
Z_{\Omega}(s)
is the Mellin transform of the volume profile of the family
\{\Omega_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
given by the tropical wave front propagation.

For every
t>0
, the set
\Omega_{t}
is a convex polytope with finitely many facets, each of whose supporting hyperplanes has a primitive lattice normal.

Definition 4
.

Let
F
be an
(n-1)
-dimensional polytope contained in an affine hyperplane

H=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle u,x\rangle=m\},

where
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is primitive and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Its
lattice volume
is defined by

\operatorname{vol}_{\mathbb{Z}}(F):=\frac{\operatorname{vol}_{n-1}(F)}{\operatorname{covol}(H\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n})},

where
\operatorname{vol}_{n-1}
denotes Euclidean
(n-1)
-dimensional volume in
H
.

Definition 5
.

For
t>0
, the
lattice surface volume
of
\Omega_{t}
is the sum of the lattice volumes of all facets of
\Omega_{t}
. We denote it by

P_{\Omega}(t).

Since
\operatorname{covol}(H\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n})
is equal to the length of the primitive integer normal vector to
H
, for
t>0
, we have (Proposition
7
)

P_{\Omega}(t)=-\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{t}).

Proposition 1

(Lattice surface volume form of the Mellin transform)
.

For
\Re(s)>n
,

Z_{\Omega}(s)=\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}t^{s-n}P_{\Omega}(t)\,dt.

Proof.

Starting from

Z_{\Omega}(s)=(s-n)\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}t^{s-n-1}\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{t})\,dt,

integrate by parts:

(s-n)\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}t^{s-n-1}\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{t})\,dt=\Bigl[t^{s-n}\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{t})\Bigr]_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}-\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}t^{s-n}\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{t})\,dt.

Since
\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{m_{\Omega}})=0
and

t^{s-n}\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{t})\to 0\qquad\text{as }t\to 0^{+}

for
\Re(s)>n
, this gives

Z_{\Omega}(s)=\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}t^{s-n}\left(-\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Vol}(\Omega_{t})\right)\,dt.

∎

In particular, for
n=2
,
Z_{\Omega}(s)
is the Mellin transform of the lattice perimeter profile of the tropical wave fronts
\Omega_{t}
.

Remark 1
.

It is natural to ask whether
Z_{\Omega}(s)
satisfies a functional equation, or more generally whether its Mellin transform structure reflects a duality on tropical wave fronts. We do not know the answer, but wouldn’t be surprised that it would involve some kind of geometric correspondence with multiple domains and weights by characters of
\mathbb{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
at the level of Dirichlet-type series interpretation, the two-dimensional version of which is discussed in the next section.

3.
The planar boundary zeta series

The following definition makes sense for
\Omega
in arbitrary dimension.

Definition 6

(Minimal model)
.

For
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

m_{\Omega}:=\max_{x\in\Omega}\rho_{\Omega}(x),\qquad M_{\Omega}:=\{x\in\Omega:\rho_{\Omega}(x)=m_{\Omega}\}.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}
be the set of all primitive vectors
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for which there exists a point
x
in
M_{\Omega}
satisfying
m_{\Omega}=\langle u,x\rangle-h_{\Omega}(u),
where

h_{\Omega}(u):=\min_{x\in\Omega}\langle u,x\rangle.

The
minimal model
of
\Omega
is

\widehat{\Omega}=\underset{{u\in\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}}}{\bigcap}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle u,x\rangle\geq h_{\Omega}(u)\}.

Since
\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}
is a finite set,
\widehat{\Omega}
is a polytope with rational-slope facets. It is characterized by the properties that
m_{\Omega}=m_{\widehat{\Omega}},

M_{\Omega}=M_{\widehat{\Omega}},
and
\rho_{\widehat{\Omega}}
coincides with
\rho_{\Omega}
in a neighborhood of
M_{\Omega}.
Clearly,
\Omega\subset\widehat{\Omega}.

The terminology “minimal model” (or, more accurately, “symplectic minimal model,” used for the first time in
[
50
]

3.2.
Affine arc length

Let
\Gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a
C^{2}
regular curve,

\Gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t)),\qquad\dot{\Gamma}(t)\neq 0.

Its Euclidean curvature is

\kappa(t)=\frac{|\det(\dot{\Gamma}(t),\ddot{\Gamma}(t))|}{\|\dot{\Gamma}(t)\|^{3}},

and its equiaffine arc length element is

ds_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}=|\det(\dot{\Gamma}(t),\ddot{\Gamma}(t))|^{1/3}\,dt.

Accordingly,

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\Gamma)=\int_{\Gamma}\kappa^{1/3}\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}|\det(\dot{\Gamma}(t),\ddot{\Gamma}(t))|^{1/3}\,dt.

If
\Gamma
is written locally as the graph of a convex
C^{2}
-function
y=g(x)
, then

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\Gamma)=\int(g^{\prime\prime}(x))^{1/3}\,dx.

There is also a geometric interpretation in terms of support triangles: if
\Gamma
is partitioned into sufficiently small arcs and
\Delta_{i}
denotes the support triangle corresponding to the
i
-th arc, then
[
4
,
41
]

(1)

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\Gamma)=\lim_{\max_{i}|t_{i}-t_{i-1}|\to 0}\sum_{i}2\bigl(\,\operatorname{Area}(\Delta_{i})\bigr)^{1/3}.

The formula is essentially local. Equiaffine length is additive under subdivision, so one may break the arc into very small pieces and ask only what a single infinitesimal piece contributes. After an affine change of coordinates, such a small convex arc can be written as the graph of a strictly convex
C^{2}
function
y=g(x)
, and in these coordinates the equiaffine length is measured by the integral
\int(g^{\prime\prime}(x))^{1/3}\,dx
. The point is that, at sufficiently small scale, the curve is governed by its osculating parabola, which in affine geometry plays the role that the osculating circle plays in Euclidean geometry. For a parabola, one finds directly that the support triangle determined by the endpoint tangents over an interval of width
h
has area
\frac{1}{8}\,g^{\prime\prime}(\xi)h^{3}+o(h^{3})
for some intermediate point
\xi
. Thus, its contribution to the sum is

2\,\operatorname{Area}(\Delta)^{1/3}=(g^{\prime\prime}(\xi))^{1/3}h+o(h),

which is precisely the infinitesimal equiaffine length element. Summing over all subarcs therefore produces a Riemann sum for
\int(g^{\prime\prime})^{1/3}\,dx
, and letting the mesh tend to zero gives the claimed limit. In this way, the support triangles provide a polygonal model for affine length: each small triangle records the local cubic bending of the curve, and the whole sum recovers the equiaffine measure of the arc.

(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
has equation

ax+by=\gamma_{a,b}.

If
b>0
, then

y=-\frac{a}{b}x+\frac{\gamma_{a,b}}{b}.

Its slope is
-a/b
, and comparison with the Legendre representation

y=\alpha x-g^{*}(\alpha)

shows that

\widetilde{g}\!\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)=g^{*}\!\left(-\frac{a}{b}\right)=-\frac{\gamma_{a,b}}{b}.

Thus the values of
\widetilde{g}
at rational points encode the support data of
\Omega
.

Lemma 6

(Identification of the geometric weights)
.

Let

I=\left[\frac{c}{d},\frac{a}{b}\right]

be a Farey interval, and let
v_{I}=(a+c,b+d)
be the associated primitive vector. Then

T_{I}(\widetilde{g}):=(b+d)c_{I}(\widetilde{g})=-\gamma_{a+c,b+d}+\gamma_{a,b}+\gamma_{c,d}.

In particular,

|T_{I}(\widetilde{g})|=f_{\Gamma}(a,b,c,d).

Proof.

By the coefficient formula,

\displaystyle c_{I}(\widetilde{g})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{g}\!\left(\frac{a+c}{b+d}\right)-\frac{b}{b+d}\widetilde{g}\!\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)-\frac{d}{b+d}\widetilde{g}\!\left(\frac{c}{d}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma_{a+c,b+d}}{b+d}+\frac{\gamma_{a,b}}{b+d}+\frac{\gamma_{c,d}}{b+d},

and multiplying by
b+d
gives the formula.
∎

The previous lemma shows that the geometric Dirichlet series attached to
\Gamma
is exactly the Hata-coefficient zeta series for the dual function
\widetilde{g}
.

Corollary 1
.

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}F_{\Gamma}(s)=\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\widetilde{g}}(s).

It remains to identify the integral appearing in Theorem
3
with the equiaffine arc length of
\Gamma
.

Lemma 7

(Legendre duality and equiaffine arc length)
.

The function
\widetilde{g}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
3
. Moreover,

\int_{0}^{1}(\widetilde{g}^{\prime\prime}(u))^{2/3}\,du=\int_{0}^{A}(g^{\prime\prime}(x))^{1/3}\,dx=\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\Gamma).

Proof.

Since
g\in C^{3}
is convex and has a non-zero curvature on a compact interval, there exist constants
0<m\leq M<\infty
such that

0<m\leq g^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq M\qquad(x\in[0,A]).

Standard Legendre duality implies that
\widetilde{g}\in C^{3}([0,1])
and, if
u=-g^{\prime}(x)
, then

\widetilde{g}^{\prime\prime}(u)=\frac{1}{g^{\prime\prime}(x)}.

Also,

du=-g^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx.

Since
u=-g^{\prime}(x)
decreases from
1
to
0
as
x
increases from
0
to
A
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}(\widetilde{g}^{\prime\prime}(u))^{2/3}\,du=\int_{A}^{0}(g^{\prime\prime}(x))^{-2/3}\,(-g^{\prime\prime}(x))\,dx=\int_{0}^{A}(g^{\prime\prime}(x))^{1/3}\,dx.

This proves the claim.

∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By Corollary
1
, the residue of
F_{\Gamma}(s)
at
s=\tfrac{2}{3}
is the same as the residue of
Z_{\widetilde{g}}(s)
.
Applying Theorem
3
with
f=\widetilde{g}
, we obtain

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}F_{\Gamma}(s)=\frac{\sqrt{3}\,\Gamma(1/3)^{3}}{2^{2/3}\pi^{3}}\int_{0}^{1}(\widetilde{g}^{\prime\prime}(u))^{2/3}\,du.

By Lemma
7
,

\int_{0}^{1}(\widetilde{g}^{\prime\prime}(u))^{2/3}\,du=\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\Gamma).

Hence

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}F_{\Gamma}(s)=\frac{\sqrt{3}\,\Gamma(1/3)^{3}}{2^{2/3}\pi^{3}}\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\Gamma).

This proves the theorem.
∎

4.2.
Geometric meaning of the terms of the boundary zeta series

The summands of the boundary zeta series admit a simple geometric interpretation in terms of small triangles cut out by supporting lines.

Let
(a,b)
and
(c,d)
be a unimodular pair, so that
ad-bc=1
, and let
\Delta_{a,b,c,d}
be the triangle bounded by the three supporting lines with primitive normals

(a,b),\qquad(c,d),\qquad(a+c,b+d).

Then

\operatorname{Area}(\Delta_{a,b,c,d})=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\gamma_{a,b}+\gamma_{c,d}-\gamma_{a+c,b+d}\Bigr)^{2}.

Indeed, after applying an
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
transformation and then a translation, we may reduce to the case

(a,b)=(1,0),\qquad(c,d)=(0,1),\qquad\gamma_{a,b}=\gamma_{c,d}=0.

In these coordinates, the three supporting lines are

x=0,\qquad y=0,\qquad x+y=\gamma_{a+c,b+d},

so
\Delta_{a,b,c,d}
is a right isosceles triangle whose legs have length

\bigl|\gamma_{a,b}+\gamma_{c,d}-\gamma_{a+c,b+d}\bigr|.

Its area is therefore exactly

\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\gamma_{a,b}+\gamma_{c,d}-\gamma_{a+c,b+d}\Bigr)^{2}.

Consequently, the terms in

F_{\Gamma}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b,c,d\geq 0\\
ad-bc=1\end{subarray}}f_{\Gamma}(a,b,c,d)^{s}

are given by

f_{\Gamma}(a,b,c,d)=\bigl|\gamma_{a,b}+\gamma_{c,d}-\gamma_{a+c,b+d}\bigr|=\sqrt{2\,\operatorname{Area}(\Delta_{a,b,c,d})}.

Thus the coefficients of Hata’s expansion of the Legendre dual have a direct geometric meaning: they measure the sizes of the support triangles determined by neighboring primitive normals.

Theorem
2
shows that the first singularity of the boundary Dirichlet series is governed by the asymptotic distribution of these small support triangles. Since

f_{\Gamma}(a,b,c,d)=\sqrt{2\,\operatorname{Area}(\Delta_{a,b,c,d})},

the residue at
s=\frac{2}{3}
may be viewed as encoding a
1/3
-power summation law for the areas of these triangles. This is precisely the exponent that appears in the formula (
1
) for the equiaffine length.

5.
Example: The parabolic model

In this section we compute explicitly the boundary zeta series for the parabolic arc

\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}:\ \sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}=1\},

and then deduce a closed formula for the tropical zeta function of the symmetric domain (see Figures
1
and
2
)

L=\Bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ \sqrt{1-|x|}+\sqrt{1-|y|}\geq 1\Bigr\}.

The resulting Dirichlet series is exactly the primitive Mordell–Tornheim series, hence is expressed in terms of Witten’s
\mathrm{SU}(3)
zeta function.

5.1.
The parabolic arc

Let
\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}
be the arc joining
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
, and let

\Omega_{\mathrm{par}}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}:\ x+y\leq 1,\ \sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}\geq 1\}

be the convex domain bounded by
\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}
and the two coordinate axes.

For a primitive vector
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
, let

ax+by=\gamma_{a,b}

be the supporting line to
\Omega_{\mathrm{par}}
with inward normal
(a,b)
.

Lemma 8
.

For every
a,b\geq 0
, not both zero, one has

\gamma_{a,b}=\frac{ab}{a+b},

with the obvious convention that
\gamma_{1,0}=\gamma_{0,1}=0
.

Proof.

The boundary arc admits the parametrization

x=t^{2},\qquad y=(1-t)^{2},\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1.

Hence

ax+by=at^{2}+b(1-t)^{2}.

Differentiating with respect to
t
, we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(at^{2}+b(1-t)^{2}\bigr)=2(a+b)t-2b,

so the minimum is attained at

t=\frac{b}{a+b}.

Substituting this value gives

\gamma_{a,b}=a\Bigl(\frac{b}{a+b}\Bigr)^{2}+b\Bigl(\frac{a}{a+b}\Bigr)^{2}=\frac{ab}{a+b}.

∎

Now let
(a,b)
and
(c,d)
be a unimodular pair, so that

a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\qquad ad-bc=1.

Recall that

f_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}}(a,b,c,d)=\bigl|\gamma_{a,b}+\gamma_{c,d}-\gamma_{a+c,b+d}\bigr|.

Proposition 3
.

For every unimodular pair
(a,b),(c,d)
, one has

f_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}}(a,b,c,d)=\frac{1}{(a+b)(c+d)(a+b+c+d)}.

Proof.

By Lemma
8
,

\gamma_{a,b}+\gamma_{c,d}-\gamma_{a+c,b+d}=\frac{ab}{a+b}+\frac{cd}{c+d}-\frac{(a+c)(b+d)}{a+b+c+d}.

A direct simplification gives

\frac{ab}{a+b}+\frac{cd}{c+d}-\frac{(a+c)(b+d)}{a+b+c+d}=-\frac{(ad-bc)^{2}}{(a+b)(c+d)(a+b+c+d)}.

Since
ad-bc=1
, taking the absolute value yields

f_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}}(a,b,c,d)=\frac{1}{(a+b)(c+d)(a+b+c+d)}.

∎

5.2.
The boundary series of the parabola

We now identify the boundary zeta series of
\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}
with a primitive Mordell–Tornheim series.

Lemma 9
.

The map

(a,b,c,d)\longmapsto(p,q):=(a+b,c+d)

is a bijection between the set of quadruples

a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\qquad ad-bc=1,

and the set of coprime pairs
(p,q)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
with
\gcd(p,q)=1
.

Proof.

If
p=a+b
and
q=c+d
, then

pd-qb=(a+b)d-(c+d)b=ad-bc=1,

hence
\gcd(p,q)=1
.

Conversely, let
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
be coprime. Choose the unique integer
b
with

0\leq b<p,\qquad qb\equiv-1\pmod{p}.

Then

d:=\frac{qb+1}{p}\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
0\leq b<p
, we have
0<d\leq q
. Now define

a:=p-b,\qquad c:=q-d.

Then
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and

ad-bc=(p-b)d-b(q-d)=pd-bq=1.

Thus every coprime pair
(p,q)
arises. Uniqueness follows from the uniqueness of
b
modulo
p
in the range
0\leq b<p
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

For
\Re(s)
sufficiently large,

F_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b,c,d\geq 0\\
ad-bc=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\bigl((a+b)(c+d)(a+b+c+d)\bigr)^{s}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,q\geq 1\\
(p,q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\bigl(pq(p+q)\bigr)^{s}}.

Consequently,

F_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}}(s)=\frac{1}{\zeta(3s)}\sum_{p,q\geq 1}\frac{1}{\bigl(pq(p+q)\bigr)^{s}}.

Proof.

The first identity follows from Proposition
3
and Lemma
9
.

For the second, write

\sum_{p,q\geq 1}\frac{1}{\bigl(pq(p+q)\bigr)^{s}}=\sum_{m\geq 1}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\geq 1\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\bigl(mu\cdot mv\cdot m(u+v)\bigr)^{s}}.

This becomes

\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{1}{m^{3s}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\geq 1\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\bigl(uv(u+v)\bigr)^{s}}=\zeta(3s)\,F_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}}(s),

which proves the claim.
∎

Therefore,

F_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}}(s)=2^{-s}\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)}{\zeta(3s)},

where

\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s):=2^{s}\sum_{p,q\geq 1}\frac{1}{\bigl(pq(p+q)\bigr)^{s}},

is the Witten’s SU(3) zeta function
[
55
]
, that we touch upon in section
5.4
.

5.3.
The special domain
L

We now return to the symmetric domain

L=\Bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ \sqrt{1-|x|}+\sqrt{1-|y|}\geq 1\Bigr\}.

Its boundary consists of four congruent parabolic arcs. In the first quadrant the boundary is given by

\sqrt{1-x}+\sqrt{1-y}=1,\qquad 0\leq x\leq 1,\quad 0\leq y\leq 1.

Equivalently, after the affine change of variables

u=1-x,\qquad v=1-y,

this arc is identified with
\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}
.

Figure 1
.
Tropical wave fronts and caustic in a parabolic model
L
. The red boundary is four parabolic arcs. The blue and green polygons are the wave fronts of the associated tropical distance function; they arise by successive unimodular corner cuts governed by the Stern–Brocot/Farey recursion. The right-hand picture isolates the corresponding caustic tree: each edge represents a regime of fixed toric combinatorics (see
B
), while each vertex records a critical event at which a new mediant support direction appears. This is the geometric mechanism behind the boundary Dirichlet series: the elementary support defects attached to neighboring primitive directions are exactly the sizes of the triangles cut by this flow.

Proposition 5
.

The equiaffine arclength of one parabolic arc of
\partial L
is
4^{1/3}
. Hence

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\partial L)=4^{4/3}.

Proof.

Consider the arc in the first quadrant. It is parametrized by

\gamma(t)=(1-t^{2},\ 2t-t^{2}),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1.

Indeed,

\sqrt{1-(1-t^{2})}+\sqrt{1-(2t-t^{2})}=t+(1-t)=1.

Now

\gamma^{\prime}(t)=(-2t,\ 2-2t),\qquad\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)=(-2,\ -2),

hence

\det(\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t))=\det\begin{pmatrix}-2t&-2\\
2-2t&-2\end{pmatrix}=4.

Therefore the equiaffine speed is constant and equal to
4^{1/3}
. Thus one arc has equiaffine length

\int_{0}^{1}4^{1/3}\,dt=4^{1/3}.

Since
\partial L
consists of four congruent arcs, the total equiaffine length is

4\cdot 4^{1/3}=4^{4/3}.

∎

Theorem 5
.

For
\Re(s)>2
,

Z_{L}(s)=\left(8-2^{2-s}\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)}{\zeta(3s)}\right)\frac{1}{s(s-1)}.

In particular,
Z_{L}(s)
admits a meromorphic continuation to every point where

\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)
does.

Proof.

By symmetry, the four boundary arcs of
\partial L
contribute the same boundary series, namely

F_{\partial L}(s)=4F_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}}(s).

Using Proposition
4
, we obtain

F_{\partial L}(s)=4\cdot 2^{-s}\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)}{\zeta(3s)}=2^{2-s}\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)}{\zeta(3s)}.

On the other hand, the minimal model of
L
is the square

\widehat{L}=[-1,1]^{2}.

Applying the rectangle formula from the remark after Theorem
1
with

P=Q=2,

we get

H_{\widehat{L}}(s)=8.

Therefore Theorem
1
yields

s(s-1)Z_{L}(s)=-F_{\partial L}(s)+8,

that is,

Z_{L}(s)=\left(8-2^{2-s}\frac{\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)}{\zeta(3s)}\right)\frac{1}{s(s-1)}.

∎

Corollary 2
.

The function
Z_{L}(s)
has a simple pole at
s=\frac{2}{3}
, and

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{L}(s)=\frac{18\sqrt{3}}{\pi^{3}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}.

Equivalently,

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{L}(s)=\frac{9\sqrt{3}}{2\cdot 4^{1/3}\pi^{3}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\partial L).

Figure 2
.
Three-dimensional lift of the parabolic model. The graph of
\rho_{L}
over the region exhibits in a single picture the two structures that drive the zeta function: horizontal sections give the tropical wave fronts, while the ridge locus gives the caustic and its Farey branching. The visible polyhedral facets correspond to linearity chambers of
\rho_{L}
, hence to primitive supporting directions; their successive refinements are the tropical counterpart of the toric blow-up chain. In this model the arithmetic and the geometry become completely transparent: the support defects are read from the elementary triangular pieces created by the flow, and their zeta summation is the geometric source of the Mordell–Tornheim/
\mathrm{SU}(3)
structure appearing in the parabolic case.

Proof.

The second formula follows immediately from Proposition
5
and the general residue theorem for smooth convex domains with everywhere non-vanishing curvature. Since

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\partial L)=4^{4/3},

we obtain

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{L}(s)=\frac{9\sqrt{3}}{2\cdot 4^{1/3}\pi^{3}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}\cdot 4^{4/3}=\frac{18\sqrt{3}}{\pi^{3}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}.

∎

The explicit formula above is one reason the parabola should be regarded as the basic model case. Quite remarkably, the same parabolic geometry appears in several different contexts.

5.4.
Witten’s
\mathrm{SU}(3)
zeta function

Following Witten and Zagier
[
55
,
56
]
, for a compact semisimple Lie group
G
, one considers the representation zeta function

\zeta_{G}(s):=\sum_{\rho\in\operatorname{Irrep}(G)}(\dim\rho)^{-s}.

In the case
G=\mathrm{SU}(2)
, the irreducible representations have dimensions
1,2,3,\dots
, so
\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}(s)
is simply the Riemann zeta function. The next case,

\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s),

is exactly the one that appears in the parabolic model. Indeed, irreducible representations of
\mathrm{SU}(3)
are indexed by pairs
(p,q)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, and

\dim V_{p,q}=\frac{1}{2}\,pq(p+q).

Therefore

\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)=2^{s}\sum_{p,q\geq 1}\frac{1}{\bigl(pq(p+q)\bigr)^{s}}.

Our computation shows that the boundary zeta series of the parabola is precisely the primitive version of this double series, obtained by dividing by
2^{s}\zeta(3s)
.

The analytic structure of
\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}
was studied in
[
48
]
. In particular,
\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)
admits a meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
, with simple poles at

s=\frac{2}{3},\qquad s=\frac{1}{2}-k\quad(k=0,1,2,\dots),

and

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}\zeta_{\mathrm{SU}(3)}(s)=\frac{4^{1/3}}{2\pi\sqrt{3}}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}.

This is the same transcendental factor that appears in our main residue formula, up to the elementary normalization coming from the geometric setup.

5.5.
Moduli space volume

The same triangle-cutting mechanism also appears in the algebro-geometric story behind the universal elliptic curve over the moduli space
A_{1}
. On the one hand, for moduli spaces
A_{g}
the volume is interpreted as a self-intersection number of the Hodge bundle; on the other hand, for the universal elliptic curve
B_{1}\to A_{1}
, Kramer and von Pippich explain that one compares the self-intersection
L_{1}\cdot L_{1}
of the distinguished line bundle
L_{1}
with the Chern–Weil integral

\operatorname{vol}(B_{1})=\int_{B_{1}}c_{1}(L_{1},\|\cdot\|_{\Theta})\wedge c_{1}(L_{1},\|\cdot\|_{\Theta}),

and the defect is measured by the special value
\zeta_{\mathrm{MT}}(2,2;2)

[
36
]
. More precisely, after compactifying
B_{1}
over the cusp and resolving the nodal boundary fiber by an infinite chain of blow-ups, each exceptional divisor contributes a correction term, so that the discrepancy is expressed by a Mordell–Tornheim series
[
36
,
6
]
.

What is especially close to our picture is that, in the toric model extracted in
[
6
]
, self-intersection is literally computed by areas of convex regions: if
\Delta=\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}
, then

\operatorname{div}(x_{0})^{2}=2\operatorname{Vol}(\Delta)=1,

whereas for the singular metric one obtains a b-divisor with

\mathrm{b}\text{-}\operatorname{div}(x_{0},\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{sing}})^{2}=2\operatorname{Vol}(\Delta_{\mathrm{sing}}),\text{\ where\ }

\Delta_{\mathrm{sing}}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}:\ x+y\leq 1,\ \sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}\geq 1\}.

Hence

\!\!\!\operatorname{div}(x_{0})^{2}-\mathrm{b}\text{-}\operatorname{div}(x_{0},\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{sing}})^{2}=2\operatorname{Vol}(\Delta)-2\operatorname{Vol}(\Delta_{\mathrm{sing}})=2\int_{0}^{1}(1-\sqrt{x})^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{3}.

Equivalently, this is the statement

\zeta(2,2;2)=\frac{1}{3}\,\zeta(6),

and geometrically the relevant boundary curve is exactly the parabola

\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}=1.

This is why, in our setting, the parabola should be regarded as the model case: it is the same curve along which the infinite resolution creates the triangular defect terms whose total contribution is governed by the Mordell–Tornheim zeta value
[
36
,
6
]
.

5.6.
Concentration of measure

There is also a probabilistic reason for singling out this domain. Bárány proved
[
1
]
that for convex lattice polygons in the square the overwhelming majority of them approach a deterministic limit shape, and this limit shape is exactly

L=\Bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ \sqrt{1-|x|}+\sqrt{1-|y|}\geq 1\Bigr\}.

More precisely, if

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\Bigl\{P\subset[-1,1]^{2}:\ P\text{ is a convex }(1/n)\mathbb{Z}^{2}\text{-lattice polygon}\Bigr\},

then for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\frac{\#\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}:\ \operatorname{Area}(P\triangle L)>\varepsilon\}}{\#\mathcal{P}_{n}}\longrightarrow 0\qquad(n\to\infty),

where
P\triangle L=(P\setminus L)\cup(L\setminus P)
is the symmetric difference.

Thus the same domain
L
that appears naturally in our explicit tropical-zeta computation is also the limit shape in a classical lattice polygon problem.

Appendix A
Basic geometry of two-dimensional case

A.1.
A minimal model

We begin with the simplest support configuration, namely the rectangular box determined by the horizontal and vertical supporting lines. The idea is that the global integral identity will be obtained by starting from this box and then inserting the remaining supporting lines one by one. Each new supporting line cuts off a single triangular region, and the next lemma computes the contribution of the initial rectangle.

Let

R=[0,P]\times[0,Q],\qquad P\geq Q>0,

and define

\rho_{R}(x,y):=\min(x,P-x,y,Q-y).

Lemma 10

(Rectangle identity)
.

For
\Re(s)>2
,

s(s-1)\int_{R}\rho_{R}(x,y)^{\,s-2}\,dx\,dy=8\Bigl(\frac{Q}{2}\Bigr)^{s}+2s(P-Q)\Bigl(\frac{Q}{2}\Bigr)^{s-1}.

Proof.

For
0\leq t\leq Q/2
, the superlevel set of
\rho_{R}
is

\{\rho_{R}\geq t\}=[t,P-t]\times[t,Q-t],

and therefore

\operatorname{Area}\{\rho_{R}\geq t\}=(P-2t)(Q-2t).

By the layer-cake formula,

\int_{R}\rho_{R}(x,y)^{\,s-2}\,dx\,dy=(s-2)\int_{0}^{Q/2}t^{s-3}(P-2t)(Q-2t)\,dt.

Expanding the integrand gives

(s-2)\int_{0}^{Q/2}t^{s-3}\bigl(PQ-2(P+Q)t+4t^{2}\bigr)\,dt.

Integrating term by term and simplifying yields

s(s-1)\int_{R}\rho_{R}(x,y)^{\,s-2}\,dx\,dy=8\Bigl(\frac{Q}{2}\Bigr)^{s}+2s(P-Q)\Bigl(\frac{Q}{2}\Bigr)^{s-1},

as claimed.
∎

We next isolate the local effect of inserting a single additional supporting line. After a unimodular affine normalization, every such local move is reduced to cutting the first quadrant by a line of the form
x+y=\lambda
. The following identity computes exactly the change of the integral under this elementary operation (see Figure
3
). Due to the local nature of the statement above, it is valid for non-rectangular minimal models as well.

Figure 3
.
Left: schematic plot of tropical distance function on a rectangular, with a dark-demarcated triangle to be cut out from the rectangular. Right: schematic plot of the tropical distance function on the resulting pentagon. Center: unimodular tetrahedron with base equal to the cut triangle representing the set-theoretic difference of between the undergraphs of the two plots.

Lemma 11

(one-cut identity)
.

Let
\lambda>0
, and define

\phi_{-}(x,y):=\min(x,y)\qquad\text{on }\{x\geq 0,\ y\geq 0\},

and

\phi_{+}(x,y):=\min(x,y,x+y-\lambda)\qquad\text{on }\{x\geq 0,\ y\geq 0,\ x+y\geq\lambda\}.

Then for
\Re(s)>2
,

\int_{\{x+y<\lambda\}}\phi_{-}(x,y)^{\,s-2}\,dx\,dy\\
+\int_{\{x\geq 0,\ y\geq 0,\ x+y\geq\lambda\}}\Bigl(\phi_{-}(x,y)^{\,s-2}-\phi_{+}(x,y)^{\,s-2}\Bigr)\,dx\,dy=\frac{\lambda^{s}}{s(s-1)}.

Proof.

For
t\geq 0
, the difference of the superlevel sets appearing on the left-hand side is

Remark 7

One way to think about types of minimal models, i.e. from the perspective of their combinatorics encoded by the dual fan, is that they provide an

This lemma is a standard two-point second-order mean value formula but we prove it for completeness.

Lemma 16
.

Let
f\in C^{2}([0,1])
, let
x\neq y
in
[0,1]

\lambda\in(0,1)

Then there exists
\xi

f(m)-\lambda f(x)-(1-\lambda)f(y)=-\frac{1}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)f^{\prime\prime}(\xi)(x-y)^{2}.

Proof.

Define

\varphi(t):=f\bigl(y+t(x-y)\bigr),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then
\varphi\in C^{2}([0,1])
, and

\varphi(\lambda)=f(m),\qquad\varphi(1)=f(x),\qquad\varphi(0)=f(y).

Thus it is enough to prove that there exists
\eta\in(0,1)
such that

\varphi(\lambda)-\lambda\varphi(1)-(1-\lambda)\varphi(0)=-\frac{1}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\eta).

Set

L(t):=\varphi(0)+t\bigl(\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)\bigr),

and let

A:=\varphi(\lambda)-L(\lambda).

Consider the auxiliary function

\psi(t):=\varphi(t)-L(t)-A\,\frac{t(t-1)}{\lambda(\lambda-1)}.

Then

\psi(0)=\psi(\lambda)=\psi(1)=0.

By Rolle’s theorem, there exist
u_{1}\in(0,\lambda)
and
u_{2}\in(\lambda,1)
such that

\psi^{\prime}(u_{1})=\psi^{\prime}(u_{2})=0.

Applying Rolle’s theorem once more to
\psi^{\prime}
, we obtain
\eta\in(u_{1},u_{2})\subset(0,1)
such that

\psi^{\prime\prime}(\eta)=0.

Since
L^{\prime\prime}=0
and
\bigl(t(t-1)\bigr)^{\prime\prime}=2
, this gives

0=\psi^{\prime\prime}(\eta)=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\eta)-A\,\frac{2}{\lambda(\lambda-1)}.

Hence

A=\frac{1}{2}\,\lambda(\lambda-1)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\eta)=-\frac{1}{2}\,\lambda(1-\lambda)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\eta).

Finally,

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)=f^{\prime\prime}\bigl(y+t(x-y)\bigr)(x-y)^{2}.

Therefore, with

Z_{\Omega}(s)=Z_{\widehat{\Omega}}(s)-\frac{1}{s(s-1)}\sum_{\Gamma}Z_{\Gamma}(s),

where the finite summation is taken over connected components
\Gamma
of
\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\widehat{\Omega}
.

It is instructive to examine the summation protocol defining
Z_{\Gamma}(s)
when
\Gamma
is smooth and convex. After an affine unimodular change of coordinates, one may assume that the two straight sides of the corresponding
\Gamma
-triangle lie on the positive coordinate axes and meet at the origin. In this normalization, the unimodular triangles in
T(\Gamma)
are naturally indexed by the free monoid

\operatorname{SL}_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}),

that is, by unimodular
2\times 2
matrices with nonnegative integer entries. This follows from the following rule: whenever an edge with neighboring gradients
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
of
\rho_{\Omega}
branches, the new gradient visible from it is
v_{1}+v_{2}
. Thus the two new edges adjacent to the vertex have neighboring gradients
v_{1}
and
v_{1}+v_{2}
, and
v_{1}+v_{2}
and
v_{2}
, respectively. This is seen as multiplying the
2\times 2
matrix
(v_{1},v_{2})
by

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

which are the free generators of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Recall that one obtains
\Omega
from its minimal model
\widehat{\Omega}
by a sequence of unimodular corner cuts, see
A.3
, where at each step one removes a unimodular triangle of size
r>0
, that is,
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
- and translation-congruent to the one with vertices
(0,0)
,
(r,0)
, and
(0,r)
. Invariantly, for a triangle
\Delta
we define

\operatorname{size}(\Delta):=\sqrt{2\,\operatorname{Area}(\Delta)},

which is consistent with subsection
4.2
.

Observe that the multiset of sizes of triangles in the cutting process
\widehat{\Omega}\mapsto\Omega
has at most one accumulation point, namely
0\in\mathbb{R}
, and that the maximal value of this multiset is less than
m_{\Omega}
. Denote by
\Omega^{t}
the result of cutting all the triangles of size at least
t
in the above process. In particular,

\Omega^{t}=\widehat{\Omega}\qquad\text{for }t>m_{\Omega}.

Lemma 17
.

For
0\leq u<t\leq m_{\Omega}
, the dual fan of
(\Omega^{\,t})_{u}
is constant.

Here the notation
(\Omega^{\,t})_{u}
denotes the tropical wave front at time
u
of the partial corner cutting
\Omega^{t}
.

Let
K_{t}
be its canonical class and
\omega_{t}
the symplectic form of the toric surface with moment domain
\Omega^{\,t}
. The canonical evolution equation (
5
) and the lemma above imply that the class of the symplectic form corresponding to
(\Omega^{\,t})_{u}
for
0\leq u<t
is

[\omega_{t}]+uK_{t}.

Hence the lattice perimeter of
(\Omega^{\,t})_{u}
is

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\partial(\Omega^{\,t})_{u}\bigr)=\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega^{\,t})-u\,K_{t}^{2}.

We also need the compatibility relation between the truncated models and the actual wave fronts.

Lemma 18
.

For every
u>0
,

(\Omega^{\,u})_{u}=\Omega_{u}.

Now we introduce the triangle-size counting function:

A_{\Omega}(t):=\#\{\Delta:\ \operatorname{size}(\Delta)\geq t\},

where the count runs over the triangles of the cutting process.

In words,
\Omega^{\,t}
is obtained from
\widehat{\Omega}
by performing
A_{\Omega}(t)
symplectic blow-ups. As each blow-up decreases
K^{2}
by
1
, we have

K_{t}^{2}=K_{\widehat{\Omega}}^{2}-A_{\Omega}(t).

On the other hand, the lattice perimeter of
\Omega
itself is zero, since
\partial\Omega
has no straight edges. Thus the lattice perimeter of
\Omega^{\,t}
is the tail of the sum of triangle sizes:

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega^{\,t})=-\int_{0}^{t}u\,dA_{\Omega}(u).

Using the canonical evolution formula (
5
) at
u=t
, we get

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{t})=\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega^{\,t})+tA_{\Omega}(t)+o(t^{1/3}).

For notational clarity, in the analytic completion below we write

N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t):=A_{\Omega}(t).

B.5.
From the boundary Dirichlet series to the wave-front asymptotic

In this subsection we complete the proof of the asymptotic lattice perimeter of tropical wave fronts by first studying the boundary Dirichlet series

F_{\partial\Omega}(s),

whose terms are indexed by the support triangles appearing in the cutting process

\widehat{\Omega}\rightsquigarrow\Omega.

The analytic continuation of this boundary series will be converted, by a Tauberian argument, into an asymptotic for the counting function of support-triangle sizes. Only after that will we return to the tropical wave fronts themselves and use the geometry of the partial-cut models
\Omega^{t}
developed earlier in this appendix.

It is important to distinguish from the outset the two Mellin-type objects that appear in the paper. On the one hand, the interior tropical zeta function is

Z_{\Omega}(s)=\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}t^{s-2}L_{\Omega}(t)\,dt,

where
L_{\Omega}(t)=\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{t})
is the lattice perimeter of the tropical wave front

\Omega_{t}=\{x\in\Omega:\rho_{\Omega}(x)\geq t\}.

On the other hand, the boundary Dirichlet series is

F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=\sum_{\Delta}\operatorname{size}(\Delta)^{s},

where the sum runs over the support triangles
\Delta
in the cutting process

\widehat{\Omega}\rightsquigarrow\Omega
. The Tauberian step in this subsection is applied first to
F_{\partial\Omega}(s)
, not to
Z_{\Omega}(s)
.

Thus the logic is the following. We first rewrite
F_{\partial\Omega}(s)
as a Mellin–Laplace transform of the support-triangle counting function. We then use the meromorphic continuation of
F_{\partial\Omega}(s)
near
s=\tfrac{2}{3}
to obtain, by a Tauberian theorem, the asymptotic distribution of triangle sizes. Finally, invoking the geometric comparison between the partial-cut models
\Omega^{t}
and the actual wave fronts
\Omega_{t}
established earlier in the appendix, we convert this counting asymptotic into the asymptotic law for

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{t})
, and hence into the corresponding residue formula for
Z_{\Omega}(s)
.

Let

N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t):=\#\{\Delta:\operatorname{size}(\Delta)\geq t\},\qquad t>0,

where the count runs over the support triangles
\Delta
in the cutting process

\widehat{\Omega}\rightsquigarrow\Omega.

Since the set of support-triangle sizes is bounded above, and in particular is contained in
(0,m_{\Omega}]
, we may also define

M_{\Omega}(x):=N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(e^{-x}),\qquad x\geq-\log m_{\Omega}.

Lemma 19

(Mellin–Laplace identity)
.

In the half-plane of absolute convergence,

\mkern-25.0muF_{\partial\Omega}(s)=s\int_{0}^{\infty}N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)t^{s-1}\,dt=s\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)t^{s-1}\,dt=s\int_{-\log m_{\Omega}}^{\infty}M_{\Omega}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx.

Equivalently,

\frac{1}{s}\,F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=\int_{-\log m_{\Omega}}^{\infty}M_{\Omega}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx.

Proof.

For every
\lambda>0
and
\Re(s)>0
,

\lambda^{s}=s\int_{0}^{\lambda}t^{s-1}\,dt.

Applying this identity termwise to the absolutely convergent series

F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=\sum_{\Delta}\operatorname{size}(\Delta)^{s}

gives

F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=s\int_{0}^{\infty}N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)t^{s-1}\,dt.

Since the support-triangle sizes are bounded above, and in fact satisfy

\operatorname{size}(\Delta)\leq m_{\Omega}

for every
\Delta
, the counting function
N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)
vanishes for
t>m_{\Omega}
. Hence

F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=s\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)t^{s-1}\,dt.

Now substitute
t=e^{-x}
, so that
dt=-e^{-x}\,dx
, and note that

t\in(0,m_{\Omega}]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\in[-\log m_{\Omega},\infty).

Using the definition

M_{\Omega}(x)=N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(e^{-x}),

we obtain

F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=s\int_{-\log m_{\Omega}}^{\infty}M_{\Omega}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx.

Dividing by
s
gives the equivalent form.
∎

Theorem 7

(Laplace–Tauberian theorem)
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and let

B:[x_{0},\infty)\to[0,\infty)

be nondecreasing. Suppose that for some
c>\rho>0
,

G(s):=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}B(x)e^{-sx}\,dx

converges for
\Re(s)>c
. Assume that
G(s)
admits a meromorphic continuation to the half-plane

\Re(s)>\rho-\delta

for some
\delta>0
, that it is holomorphic there except for a simple pole at
s=\rho
, and that the function

sG(s)-\frac{\rho A}{s-\rho}

extends holomorphically to a neighborhood of the closed half-plane

\Re(s)\geq\rho.

Then

B(x)\sim A\,e^{\rho x}\qquad(x\to+\infty).

Proof.

Since
B
is nondecreasing, it is of bounded variation on every compact interval, so we may consider
the Laplace–Stieltjes transform

H(s):=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}e^{-sx}\,dB(x),\qquad\Re(s)>c.

An integration by parts gives

G(s)=\left[-\frac{B(x)e^{-sx}}{s}\right]_{x=x_{0}}^{\infty}+\frac{1}{s}\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}e^{-sx}\,dB(x)=\frac{B(x_{0})e^{-sx_{0}}}{s}+\frac{H(s)}{s},

hence

H(s)=sG(s)-B(x_{0})e^{-sx_{0}}.

Therefore

H(s)-\frac{\rho A}{s-\rho}=s\left(G(s)-\frac{A}{s-\rho}\right)+A-B(x_{0})e^{-sx_{0}}.

By hypothesis, the right-hand side is holomorphic in a neighborhood of the closed half-plane

\Re(s)\geq\rho
. Thus
H(s)
is a Laplace–Stieltjes transform of a nondecreasing function,
holomorphic on
\Re(s)\geq\rho
after subtraction of the polar part
\rho A/(s-\rho)
.

The conclusion now follows from the Wiener–Ikehara theorem in its Laplace–Stieltjes form; see, for example,
[
35
, Chapter III]
, building on the classical works
[
23
,
54
]
. Applied to the nondecreasing function
B
, this theorem yields

B(x)\sim\frac{\rho A}{\rho}e^{\rho x}=A\,e^{\rho x}\qquad(x\to+\infty).

This proves the theorem.
∎

Proposition 9

(Half-plane continuation of the full boundary series)
.

Under the standing smoothness and curvature assumptions on the arcs of

\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\widehat{\Omega}
, the full boundary series

F_{\partial\Omega}(s)

admits a meromorphic continuation to the half-plane

\Re(s)>\frac{3}{5}.

It is holomorphic there except for a simple pole at

s=\frac{2}{3}.

Moreover,

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=\frac{\sqrt{3}\,\Gamma(1/3)^{3}}{2^{2/3}\pi^{3}}\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\partial\Omega).

Proof.

By Theorem
9
from Appendix
C
,
subsection
C.5
, applied to the Legendre dual of each boundary arc,
every arc series
F_{\Gamma}(s)
admits a meromorphic continuation to the half-plane

\Re(s)>\tfrac{3}{5}
, holomorphic there except for a simple pole at
s=\tfrac{2}{3}
, and

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}F_{\Gamma}(s)=\frac{\sqrt{3}\,\Gamma(1/3)^{3}}{2^{2/3}\pi^{3}}\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\Gamma).

Since

F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=\sum_{\Gamma}F_{\Gamma}(s)

is a finite sum over the arcs in
\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\widehat{\Omega}
, the same
continuation statement holds for
F_{\partial\Omega}(s)
, and the residue is the sum of the
arc residues. By additivity of equiaffine length,

\sum_{\Gamma}\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\Gamma)=\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\partial\Omega).

This proves the proposition.
∎

Proposition 10

(Asymptotic for the support-triangle counting function)
.

Let

r_{\Omega}:=\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}F_{\partial\Omega}(s).

Then, as
x\to+\infty
,

M_{\Omega}(x)\sim\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,e^{2x/3}.

Equivalently, as
t\to 0^{+}
,

N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)\sim\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,t^{-2/3}.

Proof.

By Lemma
19
,

G_{\Omega}(s):=\frac{1}{s}F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=\int_{-\log m_{\Omega}}^{\infty}M_{\Omega}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx

in the half-plane of absolute convergence.

By Proposition
9
, the function
F_{\partial\Omega}(s)
is meromorphic on

\Re(s)>\frac{3}{5},

holomorphic there except for a simple pole at
s=\tfrac{2}{3}
. Since
1/s
is holomorphic there, the same is true for
G_{\Omega}(s)
. Moreover,

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}G_{\Omega}(s)=\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}\frac{1}{s}F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=\frac{1}{2/3}\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}F_{\partial\Omega}(s)=\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}.

Therefore

G_{\Omega}(s)-\frac{\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}}{s-\frac{2}{3}}

is holomorphic in a neighborhood of the closed half-plane
\Re(s)\geq\tfrac{2}{3}
.

Applying Theorem
7
with

B=M_{\Omega},\qquad\rho=\frac{2}{3},\qquad A=\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega},

we obtain

M_{\Omega}(x)\sim\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,e^{2x/3}.

Substituting
x=-\log t
gives

N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)=M_{\Omega}(-\log t)\sim\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,t^{-2/3}.

∎

The asymptotic just obtained concerns the tail distribution of the support-triangle sizes in the cutting process

\widehat{\Omega}\rightsquigarrow\Omega.

To convert it into an asymptotic for the lattice perimeter of the tropical wave fronts, we now return to the partial-cut models
\Omega^{t}
introduced earlier in this appendix. The passage from boundary triangles to wave fronts uses the geometric relations already established there: the compatibility between the truncated domains
\Omega^{t}
and the actual wave fronts
\Omega_{t}
, the canonical-evolution description of the intermediate toric models through the classes
K_{t}
, and the resulting comparison between the lattice perimeters of
\partial\Omega^{t}
and
\partial\Omega_{t}
. In this way the Tauberian asymptotic for the support-triangle counting function is transported back from the boundary Dirichlet series
F_{\partial\Omega}(s)
to the interior wave-front profile governed by
Z_{\Omega}(s)
.

Theorem 8

(Asymptotic lattice perimeter of tropical wave fronts)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact convex domain with
C^{3}
-smooth boundary, and assume that
each arc of
\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\widehat{\Omega}
has everywhere nonvanishing curvature.
Then, as
t\to 0^{+}
,

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{t})=\frac{9}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,t^{1/3}+o(t^{1/3}).

Equivalently,

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{t})=\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\Omega}(s)\,t^{1/3}+o(t^{1/3}).

Proof.

By Proposition
10
,

N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)=\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,t^{-2/3}+o(t^{-2/3})\qquad(t\to 0^{+}).

Using the Stieltjes identity established earlier in this appendix,

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega^{\,t})=-\int_{0}^{t}u\,dN^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(u)=-tN^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(u)\,du,

we obtain

-tN^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)=-\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,t^{1/3}+o(t^{1/3}),

and

\int_{0}^{t}N^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(u)\,du=\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}u^{-2/3}\,du+o(t^{1/3})=\frac{9}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,t^{1/3}+o(t^{1/3}).

Hence

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega^{\,t})=3\,r_{\Omega}\,t^{1/3}+o(t^{1/3}).

Now invoke the previously established comparison between the partial-cut models and the actual wave fronts:

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{t})=\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega^{\,t})+tN^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)+o(t^{1/3}).

Since

tN^{\mathrm{cut}}_{\Omega}(t)=\frac{3}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,t^{1/3}+o(t^{1/3}),

it follows that

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{t})=\left(3+\frac{3}{2}\right)r_{\Omega}\,t^{1/3}+o(t^{1/3})=\frac{9}{2}\,r_{\Omega}\,t^{1/3}+o(t^{1/3}).

Finally, by the identity relating
Z_{\Omega}(s)
and
F_{\partial\Omega}(s)
,

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\Omega}(s)=\frac{9}{2}\,r_{\Omega},

so the equivalent form follows.
∎

Corollary 4

(Area deficit)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
8
,

\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)-\operatorname{Area}(\Omega_{t})=\frac{3}{4}\,\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\Omega}(s)\,t^{4/3}+o(t^{4/3})\qquad(t\to 0^{+}).

Proof.

As shown earlier in this appendix, the lattice surface volume of the tropical wave front satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Area}(\Omega_{t})=-\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{t}).

Integrating from
0
to
t
, we get

\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)-\operatorname{Area}(\Omega_{t})=\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{u})\,du.

Now apply Theorem
8
:

\operatorname{Length}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\partial\Omega_{u})=\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\Omega}(s)\,u^{1/3}+o(u^{1/3}),\text{\ \ so\ }

\mkern-22.0mu\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)-\operatorname{Area}(\Omega_{t})=\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\Omega}(s)\int_{0}^{t}u^{1/3}\,du+o(t^{4/3})=\frac{3}{4}\,\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\Omega}(s)\,t^{4/3}+o(t^{4/3}).

∎

Lemma 20

(Pointwise bound for
H_{s}
)
.

For
1/2<\sigma:=\Re(s)<1
and

one has

|H_{s}(u)|\leq C_{s}\,u^{-\sigma},

with

depending continuously on
s

on compact subsets of

Proof.

Write

The first term has modulus at most

Lemma 21

(Derivative bound for
H_{s}
)

Fix a compact set

such that for all

For

For the tails

Lemma 22

(Reduced residues:

Fix a compact set

and

(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}H_{s}(r/b)=\varphi(b)\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}(u)\,du+O_{K}\bigl(b^{\Re(s)}\tau(b)\bigr),

Proof.

By inclusion–exclusion,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq b\\

We prove

A standard Riemann-sum estimate for
C^{1}
functions yields

\sum_{j=1}^{N}R_{s}(j/N)=N\int_{0}^{1}R_{s}(u)\,du+O_{K}(1).

It remains to treat the singular part
u^{-s}(1+u)^{-s}=u^{-s}+u^{1-s}Q_{s}(u)
, where

Q_{s}(u):=\frac{(1+u)^{-s}-1}{u}

extends continuously to
u=0
with
Q_{s}(0)=-s
.
For
s\in K
,
Q_{s}
is
C^{1}
on
[0,1]
with norms bounded uniformly in
s
.

For

f_{s}(u):=u^{1-s}Q_{s}(u),

we have

f_{s}^{\prime}(u)=(1-s)u^{-s}Q_{s}(u)+u^{1-s}Q_{s}^{\prime}(u).

Since
K\Subset\{\tfrac{1}{2}<\Re(s)<1\}
, there exists
\sigma_{+}<1
such that

\Re(s)\leq\sigma_{+}
for all
s\in K
. As
Q_{s},Q_{s}^{\prime}
are uniformly bounded on

[0,1]
for
s\in K
, it follows that

|f_{s}^{\prime}(u)|\ll_{K}u^{-\sigma_{+}}+1,

hence

\int_{0}^{1}|f_{s}^{\prime}(u)|\,du\ll_{K}1.

Therefore, by the standard Riemann-sum estimate for absolutely continuous functions,

\sum_{j=1}^{N}f_{s}(j/N)=N\int_{0}^{1}f_{s}(u)\,du+O_{K}(1).

So the contribution of
u^{1-s}Q_{s}(u)
to (
7
) is
O_{K}(1)
.

Finally, for
u^{-s}
we use a direct Riemann-sum estimate.
For
s\in K
(hence
\Re(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}
and
s\neq 1
), the function
x\mapsto x^{-s}
is
C^{1}
on
[1,\infty)
and

\sum_{j=1}^{N}j^{-s}=\int_{1}^{N}x^{-s}\,dx+O_{K}(1).

Indeed,

\sum_{j=1}^{N}j^{-s}-\int_{1}^{N}x^{-s}dx=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{j}^{j+1}\bigl(j^{-s}-x^{-s}\bigr)\,dx+O_{K}(1),

and the sum is bounded in modulus by

\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{j}^{j+1}|(x^{-s})^{\prime}|\,dx\,dt\ll_{K}\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-\Re(s)-1}dx<\infty.

Multiplying by
N^{s}
yields

\sum_{j=1}^{N}(j/N)^{-s}=N^{s}\int_{1}^{N}x^{-s}dx+O_{K}(N^{\Re(s)}).

But

N^{s}\int_{1}^{N}x^{-s}dx=\frac{N-N^{s}}{1-s}
, so

\sum_{j=1}^{N}(j/N)^{-s}-N\int_{0}^{1}u^{-s}du=-\frac{N^{s}}{1-s}+O_{K}(N^{\Re(s)})=O_{K}(N^{\Re(s)}),

since
|N^{s}|=N^{\Re(s)}
.
Combining the three pieces proves (
7
).

Returning to inclusion–exclusion,

\sum_{d\mid b}\mu(d)S_{s}(b/d)=\left(\sum_{d\mid b}\mu(d)\frac{b}{d}\right)\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}+O_{K}\left(\sum_{d\mid b}(b/d)^{\Re(s)}\right).

The main coefficient equals
\varphi(b)
. The error satisfies

\sum_{d\mid b}(b/d)^{\Re(s)}\leq b^{\Re(s)}\tau(b)
.
∎

C.2.
Incomplete Kloosterman sums (Weil bound)

For integers
n
and
b\geq 1
define the incomplete Kloosterman sum

K_{b}(n;R):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq R\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right),\qquad e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}.

Lemma 23

(Completion bound for
K_{b}(n;R)
)
.

For all integers
n
, all
b\geq 1
, and all
1\leq R\leq b
,

K_{b}(n;R):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq R\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right)\ll(n,b)^{1/2}\,b^{1/2}\,\tau(b)^{2}\log(2b),

with an absolute implied constant.

In particular, if
(n,b)=1
, then for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\max_{1\leq R\leq b}|K_{b}(n;R)|\ll_{\varepsilon}b^{1/2+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}
, define

w_{R}(x):=\sum_{m=1}^{R}\mathbf{1}_{x\equiv m\;(\mathrm{mod}\;b)}.

Its discrete Fourier expansion is

w_{R}(r)=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{h\bmod b}\widehat{w}_{R}(h)\,e\!\left(\frac{hr}{b}\right),\qquad\widehat{w}_{R}(h):=\sum_{m=1}^{R}e\!\left(-\frac{hm}{b}\right).

Hence

K_{b}(n;R)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\bmod b\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}w_{R}(r)e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right)=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{h\bmod b}\widehat{w}_{R}(h)\,S(n,h;b),

where

S(n,h;b):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\bmod b\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}+hr}{b}\right)

is the classical Kloosterman sum.

We separate the zero frequency
h=0
. Since
\widehat{w}_{R}(0)=R
, we get

\frac{1}{b}\,\widehat{w}_{R}(0)\,S(n,0;b)=\frac{R}{b}\,c_{b}(n),

where
c_{b}(n)=S(n,0;b)
is the Ramanujan sum.

Let
g=(n,b)
. Since

c_{b}(n)=\mu\!\left(\frac{b}{g}\right)\frac{\varphi(b)}{\varphi(b/g)},

we have

|c_{b}(n)|\leq\frac{\varphi(b)}{\varphi(b/g)}\leq g=(n,b).

Hence

\left|\frac{R}{b}c_{b}(n)\right|\leq(n,b),

and then it follows that

\left|\frac{1}{b}\,\widehat{w}_{R}(0)\,S(n,0;b)\right|\leq(n,b).

Since
(n,b)\leq(n,b)^{1/2}b^{1/2}
, this term is acceptable.

Now consider the nonzero frequencies
h\neq 0
. For representatives

-\frac{b}{2}\leq h\leq\frac{b}{2},\qquad h\neq 0,

the geometric-series estimate gives

|\widehat{w}_{R}(h)|\ll\min\!\left(R,\frac{b}{|h|}\right).

Also, by the Weil bound for Kloosterman sums,

|S(n,h;b)|\ll\tau(b)\,(n,h,b)^{1/2}\,b^{1/2}.

Group the nonzero frequencies according to
d=(h,b)
. Then

(n,h,b)=(n,d).

Therefore

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\bmod b\\
h\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\widehat{w}_{R}(h)|\,|S(n,h;b)|\ll\tau(b)b^{1/2}\sum_{d\mid b}(n,d)^{1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\bmod b\\
h\neq 0\\
(h,b)=d\end{subarray}}|\widehat{w}_{R}(h)|.

So it remains to bound the inner sum. Write
h=dh_{1}
. Then
h_{1}
runs through a subset of nonzero residue classes modulo
b/d
, and hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\bmod b\\
h\neq 0\\
(h,b)=d\end{subarray}}|\widehat{w}_{R}(h)|\leq\sum_{1\leq|m|\leq b/(2d)}\min\!\left(R,\frac{b}{d|m|}\right)\ll\frac{b}{d}\log(2b).

Thus

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\bmod b\\
h\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\widehat{w}_{R}(h)|\,|S(n,h;b)|\ll\tau(b)b^{1/2}\log(2b)\sum_{d\mid b}(n,d)^{1/2}\frac{b}{d}.

Dividing by
b
, we obtain

\frac{1}{b}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\bmod b\\
h\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\widehat{w}_{R}(h)|\,|S(n,h;b)|\ll\tau(b)b^{1/2}\log(2b)\sum_{d\mid b}\frac{(n,d)^{1/2}}{d}.

Now

(n,d)^{1/2}\leq(n,b)^{1/2},

so

\sum_{d\mid b}\frac{(n,d)^{1/2}}{d}\leq(n,b)^{1/2}\sum_{d\mid b}\frac{1}{d}\leq(n,b)^{1/2}\tau(b).

Hence the contribution of the nonzero frequencies is

\ll(n,b)^{1/2}b^{1/2}\tau(b)^{2}\log(2b).

Combining the zero and nonzero frequencies gives

K_{b}(n;R)\ll(n,b)^{1/2}b^{1/2}\tau(b)^{2}\log(2b),

as claimed.

Finally, if
(n,b)=1
, then

K_{b}(n;R)\ll b^{1/2}\tau(b)^{2}\log(2b)\ll_{\varepsilon}b^{1/2+\varepsilon},

since
\tau(b)^{2}\log(2b)\ll_{\varepsilon}b^{\varepsilon}
.
∎

C.3.
A Fejér approximation bound for periodic Lipschitz functions

Let
F_{N}
denote the Fejér kernel on the circle,

F_{N}(t):=\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{\sin(\pi Nt)}{\sin(\pi t)}\right)^{2},\qquad t\in[-1/2,1/2],

extended
1
-periodically.

Lemma 24

(First moment of the Fejér kernel)
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all
N\geq 2
,

\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|t|\,F_{N}(t)\,dt\leq C\,\frac{\log N}{N}.

Consequently, if
G
is
1
-periodic and Lipschitz, then its Fejér mean
G*F_{N}
satisfies

\|G-G*F_{N}\|_{\infty}\leq C\,\frac{\log N}{N}\,\operatorname{Lip}(G).

Proof.

Since
F_{N}\geq 0
and
\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}F_{N}=1
,

G(x)-(G*F_{N})(x)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\bigl(G(x)-G(x-t)\bigr)F_{N}(t)\,dt.

By Lipschitz continuity,

|G(x)-G(x-t)|\leq\operatorname{Lip}(G)\,|t|,

which gives the second inequality once the moment bound is proved.

For the moment bound, split the integral into
|t|\leq 1/N
and
1/N<|t|\leq 1/2
.
For
|t|\leq 1/N
, use
F_{N}(t)\leq N
to get

\int_{|t|\leq 1/N}|t|F_{N}(t)\,dt\ll N\int_{0}^{1/N}t\,dt\ll\frac{1}{N}.

For
1/N<|t|\leq 1/2
, use
|\sin(\pi t)|\gg|t|
and
|\sin(\pi Nt)|\leq 1
to obtain

F_{N}(t)\ll\frac{1}{Nt^{2}}.

Hence

\int_{1/N<|t|\leq 1/2}|t|F_{N}(t)\,dt\ll\frac{1}{N}\int_{1/N}^{1/2}\frac{dt}{t}\ll\frac{\log N}{N}.

∎

C.4.
Proof of Proposition
2

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and write
\sigma:=\Re(s)
. Let

B_{s}(x):=|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s},\qquad A_{r}:=H_{s}(r/b).

We may assume
b\geq 4
, since for bounded
b
the claim is absorbed into the implied constant. Set

N:=R:=\lfloor b^{1/2}\rfloor,\qquad\eta:=\varepsilon/4.

Define the affine endpoint interpolant

\ell_{s}(x):=(1-x)B_{s}(0)+xB_{s}(1),

and the remainder

G_{s}(x):=B_{s}(x)-\ell_{s}(x).

Then

G_{s}(0)=G_{s}(1)=0.

By (
6
), the family
B_{s}
is
C^{1}
with norms bounded uniformly for
s\in K
. Hence

|B_{s}(1)-B_{s}(0)|\ll_{f,K}1,\qquad\operatorname{Lip}(G_{s})\ll_{f,K}1,

uniformly for
s\in K
. In particular,
G_{s}
admits a
1
-periodic Lipschitz extension to the circle.

Step 1: replace the periodic remainder
G_{s}
by its Fejér mean.

By Lemma
24
,

\|G_{s}-(G_{s})_{N}\|_{\infty}\ll_{f,K}\frac{\log(2N)}{N},

uniformly for
s\in K
. Therefore

\!\Sigma_{b}(s)=\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,(G_{s})_{N}(\overline{r}/b)+\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,\ell_{s}(\overline{r}/b)+O_{f,K}\!\left(\frac{\log(2N)}{N}\sum_{(r,b)=1}|A_{r}|\right).

Now Lemma
20
yields

\sum_{(r,b)=1}|A_{r}|\leq\sum_{r=1}^{b}|H_{s}(r/b)|\ll_{K}\sum_{r=1}^{b}(r/b)^{-\sigma}=b^{\sigma}\sum_{r=1}^{b}r^{-\sigma}\ll_{K}b,

uniformly for
s\in K
, since
K\Subset\{\tfrac{1}{2}<\Re(s)<1\}
. Thus

(8)

\Sigma_{b}(s)=\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,(G_{s})_{N}(\overline{r}/b)+\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,\ell_{s}(\overline{r}/b)+O_{f,K}\bigl(b^{1/2}\log(2b)\bigr).

Step 2: expand the periodic part in Fourier modes.

Write

(G_{s})_{N}(x)=\widehat{G}_{s}(0)+\sum_{0<|n|<N}\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)e(nx),\qquad\widehat{G}_{s}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}G_{s}(x)\,dx.

Since
G_{s}
is Lipschitz with
\operatorname{Lip}(G_{s})\ll_{f,K}1
, its Fourier coefficients satisfy

|\widehat{G}_{s}(n)|\ll_{f,K}\frac{1}{|n|}\qquad(n\neq 0),

uniformly for
s\in K
, and hence

|\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)|\leq|\widehat{G}_{s}(n)|\ll_{f,K}\frac{1}{|n|}\qquad(0<|n|<N).

Therefore

\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,(G_{s})_{N}(\overline{r}/b)=\widehat{G}_{s}(0)\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}+\sum_{0<|n|<N}\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)\,S_{b}(n),

where

S_{b}(n):=\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right).

Step 3: expand the affine part exactly on the residue grid.

Let

\ell_{s}(x)=\alpha_{s}+\beta_{s}x,\qquad\beta_{s}=B_{s}(1)-B_{s}(0).

For
m=0,\dots,b-1
, the values
\ell_{s}(m/b)
admit the discrete Fourier expansion

\ell_{s}(m/b)=c_{b,s}(0)+\sum_{0<|n|\leq b/2}c_{b,s}(n)e(nm/b),

where the sum runs over any fixed symmetric system of representatives of the nonzero residue classes modulo
b
. The coefficients satisfy

c_{b,s}(0)=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{m=0}^{b-1}\ell_{s}(m/b)=\int_{0}^{1}\ell_{s}(x)\,dx+O_{f,K}(b^{-1}),

and, for
0<|n|\leq b/2
,

c_{b,s}(n)=-\frac{\beta_{s}}{b\bigl(1-e(-n/b)\bigr)},\qquad|c_{b,s}(n)|\ll_{f,K}\frac{1}{|n|},

uniformly for
s\in K
. Hence

\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,\ell_{s}(\overline{r}/b)=c_{b,s}(0)\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}+\sum_{0<|n|\leq b/2}c_{b,s}(n)\,S_{b}(n).

Step 4: the main term.

By Lemma
22
,

\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}=\varphi(b)\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}(u)\,du+O_{K}\bigl(b^{\sigma}\tau(b)\bigr),

uniformly for
s\in K
. Also,

\mkern-26.0mu\widehat{G}_{s}(0)+c_{b,s}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}G_{s}(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}\ell_{s}(x)\,dx+O_{f,K}(b^{-1})=\int_{0}^{1}B_{s}(x)\,dx+O_{f,K}(b^{-1}).

Therefore the contribution of the zero Fourier mode is

\varphi(b)\left(\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}(u)\,du\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}B_{s}(x)\,dx\right)+O_{f,K}\bigl(b^{\sigma}\tau(b)\bigr)+O_{f,K}(1).

Since
\sigma<1
, the error
O_{f,K}(b^{\sigma}\tau(b))
is dominated by the final error term.

Step 5: bound the oscillatory sums
S_{b}(n)
.

Let
n\neq 0
. Split

S_{b}(n)=S_{b}^{\leq R}(n)+S_{b}^{>R}(n),

where

S_{b}^{\leq R}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq R\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}A_{r}\,e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right),\qquad S_{b}^{>R}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R<r\leq b\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}A_{r}\,e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right).

For the initial segment, Lemma
20
gives

|S_{b}^{\leq R}(n)|\leq\sum_{r\leq R}|A_{r}|\ll_{K}b^{\sigma}\sum_{r\leq R}r^{-\sigma}\ll_{K}b^{\sigma}R^{1-\sigma}\ll_{K}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}}.

For the tail, define

a_{r}:=\mathbf{1}_{(r,b)=1}\,e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right),\qquad T_{n}(t):=\sum_{1\leq r\leq t}a_{r}.

Then Lemma
23
gives

\max_{1\leq t\leq b}|T_{n}(t)|\ll_{\eta}(n,b)^{1/2}b^{1/2+\eta}.

By Abel summation,

\mkern-10.0muS_{b}^{>R}(n)=H_{s}(1)\,T_{n}(b)-H_{s}(R/b)\,T_{n}(R)-\sum_{t=R}^{b-1}T_{n}(t)\Bigl(H_{s}\!\left(\frac{t+1}{b}\right)-H_{s}\!\left(\frac{t}{b}\right)\Bigr).

Using Lemma
20
,

|H_{s}(1)|\ll_{K}1,\qquad|H_{s}(R/b)|\ll_{K}(R/b)^{-\sigma}.

Also, by the mean value theorem and Lemma
21
,

\mkern-22.0mu\left|H_{s}\!\left(\frac{t+1}{b}\right)-H_{s}\!\left(\frac{t}{b}\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{b}\sup_{u\in[t/b,(t+1)/b]}|\partial_{u}H_{s}(u)|\ll_{K}\frac{1}{b}\left(\frac{t}{b}\right)^{-\sigma-1}\mkern-12.0mu\ll_{K}b^{\sigma}t^{-\sigma-1}.

Hence

\sum_{t=R}^{b-1}\left|H_{s}\!\left(\frac{t+1}{b}\right)-H_{s}\!\left(\frac{t}{b}\right)\right|\ll_{K}b^{\sigma}\sum_{t\geq R}t^{-\sigma-1}\ll_{K}b^{\sigma}R^{-\sigma}.

Therefore

|S_{b}^{>R}(n)|\ll_{K,\eta}(n,b)^{1/2}b^{1/2+\eta}\Bigl(1+(R/b)^{-\sigma}+b^{\sigma}R^{-\sigma}\Bigr)\ll_{K,\eta}(n,b)^{1/2}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta},

since
R\asymp b^{1/2}
. Together with the initial-segment bound, this gives

(9)

S_{b}(n)\ll_{K,\eta}(n,b)^{1/2}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}\qquad(n\neq 0).

Step 6: sum the nonzero Fourier modes.

For the periodic part, using (
9
),

\sum_{0<|n|<N}|\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)\,S_{b}(n)|\ll_{f,K,\eta}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}\sum_{1\leq n<N}\frac{(n,b)^{1/2}}{n}.

Grouping by
d=(n,b)
, we obtain

\sum_{1\leq n<N}\frac{(n,b)^{1/2}}{n}\leq\sum_{d\mid b}d^{-1/2}\sum_{m<N/d}\frac{1}{m}\ll\tau(b)\log(2N).

Hence

\sum_{0<|n|<N}\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)\,S_{b}(n)=O_{f,K,\eta}\!\left(b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}\tau(b)\log(2N)\right).

Similarly, for the affine part,

\sum_{0<|n|\leq b/2}|c_{b,s}(n)\,S_{b}(n)|\ll_{f,K,\eta}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}\sum_{1\leq n\leq b/2}\frac{(n,b)^{1/2}}{n}\ll b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}\tau(b)\log(2b).

Since
\tau(b)\log(2b)\ll_{\eta}b^{2\eta}
, both nonzero-mode contributions are

O_{f,K,\varepsilon}\!\left(b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\varepsilon}\right).

The truncation error in (
8
) also satisfies

b^{1/2}\log(2b)\ll_{f,K,\varepsilon}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\varepsilon},

because
\sigma>\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Putting everything together, and recalling that

\int_{0}^{1}B_{s}(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s}\,dx,

we conclude that

\Sigma_{b}(s)=\varphi(b)\left(\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}(u)\,du\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s}\,dx\right)+O_{f,K,\varepsilon}\!\left(b^{\frac{1+\Re(s)}{2}+\varepsilon}\right),

uniformly for
s\in K
. This proves the proposition.
∎

C.5.
Strip-uniform refinement and half-plane continuation

In this subsection we strengthen the local continuation statement near
s=\tfrac{2}{3}

to meromorphic continuation in the half-plane
\Re(s)>\tfrac{3}{5}
, and we make the
dependence on the imaginary part quantitative on vertical strips. This is the analytic
input needed in Appendix
B
, subsection
B.5
.

Lemma 25

(Endpoint replacement on
\Re(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}
)
.

Assume
f\in C^{3}([0,1])
and
0<m\leq|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|
on
[0,1]
.
For every compact set

K\Subset\{\,s\in\mathbb{C}:\Re(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}\,\},

the function

Z_{f}(s)-Z_{f}^{\mathrm{end}}(s)

is holomorphic on a neighborhood of
K
. In particular,
Z_{f}-Z_{f}^{\mathrm{end}}

is holomorphic on the half-plane
\Re(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Choose a compact set

K^{\prime}\Subset\{\,s\in\mathbb{C}:\Re(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}\,\}

such that
K\subset\operatorname{int}(K^{\prime})
. Set

\sigma_{-}:=\inf_{s\in K^{\prime}}\Re(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}.

Recall that for
s\in\mathbb{C}
and
x\in[0,1]
we write

B_{s}(x):=|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s}.

By the regularity discussion at the beginning of this appendix, the family
\{B_{s}\}_{s\in K^{\prime}}

is uniformly
C^{1}
, so there exists
C_{K^{\prime}}>0
such that

\sup_{s\in K^{\prime}}\|(B_{s})^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{K^{\prime}}.

Hence for all
u,v\in[0,1]
and all
s\in K^{\prime}
,

\bigl||f^{\prime\prime}(u)|^{s}-|f^{\prime\prime}(v)|^{s}\bigr|=|B_{s}(u)-B_{s}(v)|\leq C_{K^{\prime}}|u-v|.

Now let
I=[c/d,a/b]
be a Farey interval. By Lemma
2
,

Z_{f}(s)=2^{-s}\sum_{I}\frac{|f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{I})|^{s}}{(bd(b+d))^{s}},

while by definition

Z_{f}^{\mathrm{end}}(s)=2^{-s}\sum_{I}\frac{|f^{\prime\prime}(a/b)|^{s}}{(bd(b+d))^{s}}.

Since
\xi_{I}\in(c/d,a/b)
, we have

\left|\xi_{I}-\frac{a}{b}\right|\leq\left|\frac{a}{b}-\frac{c}{d}\right|=\frac{1}{bd}.

Therefore, uniformly for
s\in K^{\prime}
,

\mkern-6.0mu\left|\frac{|f^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{I})|^{s}-|f^{\prime\prime}(a/b)|^{s}}{(bd(b+d))^{s}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{K^{\prime}}}{bd\,(bd(b+d))^{\Re(s)}}\leq C_{K^{\prime}}\,b^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}d^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}(b+d)^{-\sigma_{-}}.

Thus the series defining
Z_{f}-Z_{f}^{\mathrm{end}}
is dominated on
K^{\prime}
by

\sum_{I}b^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}d^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}(b+d)^{-\sigma_{-}}.

Dropping the Farey restriction only enlarges the sum, so it is enough to consider

\sum_{b,d\geq 1}b^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}d^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}(b+d)^{-\sigma_{-}}.

Using
b+d\geq b
, we obtain

b^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}d^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}(b+d)^{-\sigma_{-}}\leq b^{-(2\sigma_{-}+1)}d^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}.

Hence

\sum_{b,d\geq 1}b^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}d^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}(b+d)^{-\sigma_{-}}\leq\left(\sum_{b\geq 1}b^{-(2\sigma_{-}+1)}\right)\left(\sum_{d\geq 1}d^{-(\sigma_{-}+1)}\right).

Both series converge because
\sigma_{-}>\tfrac{1}{2}
. Therefore the defining series for

Z_{f}-Z_{f}^{\mathrm{end}}
converges uniformly on
K^{\prime}
by the Weierstrass
M
-test.
Since each term is holomorphic in
s
, the sum is holomorphic on
\operatorname{int}(K^{\prime})
,
hence on a neighborhood of
K
.
∎

Lemma 26

(Strip-uniform bounds for
H_{s}
and
\partial_{u}H_{s}
)
.

Fix numbers

\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<\beta<1.

Then there exist constants
C_{1}=C_{1}(\alpha)
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(\alpha,\beta)
such that for every

s=\sigma+i\tau,\qquad\alpha\leq\sigma\leq\beta,

and every
u\in(0,1]
, one has

|H_{s}(u)|\leq C_{1}\,u^{-\sigma},

and

|\partial_{u}H_{s}(u)|\leq C_{2}\,(1+|\tau|)\,u^{-\sigma-1}.

Proof.

Recall

H_{s}(u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(k+u)^{-s}(k+1+u)^{-s},\qquad\Re(s)>\frac{1}{2},\quad u\in(0,1].

We first prove the bound for
H_{s}(u)
. Split off the
k=0
term:

H_{s}(u)=u^{-s}(1+u)^{-s}+\sum_{k\geq 1}(k+u)^{-s}(k+1+u)^{-s}.

Since
k+u>0
and
k+1+u>0
, we have

|(k+u)^{-s}(k+1+u)^{-s}|=(k+u)^{-\sigma}(k+1+u)^{-\sigma}.

Hence

|u^{-s}(1+u)^{-s}|=u^{-\sigma}(1+u)^{-\sigma}\leq u^{-\sigma}.

Also, for
k\geq 1
,

(k+u)^{-\sigma}(k+1+u)^{-\sigma}\leq k^{-2\sigma}\leq k^{-2\alpha}.

Therefore

\sum_{k\geq 1}(k+u)^{-\sigma}(k+1+u)^{-\sigma}\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{-2\alpha}=:C_{\alpha}<\infty.

Since
u\in(0,1]
, we have
u^{-\sigma}\geq 1
, so

C_{\alpha}\leq C_{\alpha}\,u^{-\sigma}.

Combining the two estimates gives

|H_{s}(u)|\leq(1+C_{\alpha})\,u^{-\sigma},

which proves the first bound.

We now turn to the derivative. On every interval
u\in[\delta,1]
with
\delta>0
, the defining
series and the differentiated series converge uniformly, so termwise differentiation is justified.
We obtain

\partial_{u}H_{s}(u)=-s\sum_{k\geq 0}(k+u)^{-s-1}(k+1+u)^{-s}-s\sum_{k\geq 0}(k+u)^{-s}(k+1+u)^{-s-1}.

For the
k=0
term, we have

|s|\Bigl(u^{-\sigma-1}(1+u)^{-\sigma}+u^{-\sigma}(1+u)^{-\sigma-1}\Bigr)\leq 2|s|\,u^{-\sigma-1}.

For the tail
k\geq 1
, each summand is bounded by

\mkern-22.0mu|s|\,(k+u)^{-\sigma-1}(k+1+u)^{-\sigma}+|s|\,(k+u)^{-\sigma}(k+1+u)^{-\sigma-1}\leq 2|s|\,k^{-2\sigma-1}\leq 2|s|\,k^{-2\alpha-1}.

Since
\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{-2\alpha-1}<\infty
, the full tail is bounded by
C^{\prime}_{\alpha}|s|
, and
again
u^{-\sigma-1}\geq 1
on
(0,1]
. Therefore

|\partial_{u}H_{s}(u)|\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha}|s|\,u^{-\sigma-1}.

On the strip
\alpha\leq\sigma\leq\beta
,

|s|=|\sigma+i\tau|\leq\beta+|\tau|\leq(1+\beta)(1+|\tau|),

so

|\partial_{u}H_{s}(u)|\leq C_{2}(\alpha,\beta)\,(1+|\tau|)\,u^{-\sigma-1}.

∎

Lemma 27

(Strip-uniform bounds for
B_{s}(x)=|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s}
)
.

Fix numbers

\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<\beta<1.

Assume

f\in C^{3}([0,1]),\qquad 0<m\leq|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq M\quad(x\in[0,1]).

Then there exist constants
C_{0},C_{1},C_{2}>0
, depending only on
\alpha,\beta,f
, such that for all

s=\sigma+i\tau,\qquad\alpha\leq\sigma\leq\beta,

and all
x\in[0,1]
, one has

|B_{s}(x)|\leq C_{0},\qquad\|B_{s}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{1},\qquad\|B_{s}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{2}(1+|\tau|).

In particular, the affine interpolant/remainder decomposition used in the proof of
Proposition
2
remains valid on strips, with polynomial dependence on
|\tau|
.

Proof.

Since
0<m\leq|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq M
on
[0,1]
, for
s=\sigma+i\tau
and
x\in[0,1]
,

|B_{s}(x)|=\bigl||f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s}\bigr|=|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{\sigma}\leq M^{\beta}.

Thus we may take

C_{0}=C_{1}=M^{\beta}.

Now differentiate. Since
f^{\prime\prime}
has constant sign on
[0,1]
and is bounded away from
0
,
the function
\log|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|
is
C^{1}
, and

B_{s}^{\prime}(x)=s\,|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s}\,\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)}{f^{\prime\prime}(x)}\,\operatorname{sgn}(f^{\prime\prime}(x)).

Therefore

|B_{s}^{\prime}(x)|\leq|s|\,|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{\sigma}\left|\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)}{f^{\prime\prime}(x)}\right|\leq|s|\,M^{\beta}\,\frac{\|f^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{m}.

On the strip
\alpha\leq\sigma\leq\beta
,

|s|=|\sigma+i\tau|\leq\beta+|\tau|\leq(1+\beta)(1+|\tau|),

so

\|B_{s}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq(1+\beta)\,M^{\beta}\,\frac{\|f^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{m}\,(1+|\tau|).

Finally, in the proof of Proposition
2
, one writes

\ell_{s}(x):=(1-x)B_{s}(0)+xB_{s}(1),\qquad G_{s}(x):=B_{s}(x)-\ell_{s}(x).

The bounds above imply

|B_{s}(1)-B_{s}(0)|\leq 2\|B_{s}\|_{\infty}\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}1,

hence

\operatorname{Lip}(\ell_{s})\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}1,

while

\operatorname{Lip}(G_{s})\leq\|B_{s}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\operatorname{Lip}(\ell_{s})\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}(1+|\tau|).

∎

Proposition 11

(Strip-uniform equidistribution estimate)
.

Fix numbers

\frac{1}{2}<\alpha<\beta<1.

Assume

f\in C^{3}([0,1]),\qquad 0<m\leq|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq M\quad(x\in[0,1]).

Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C=C(\alpha,\beta,f,\varepsilon)>0
such that for every

s=\sigma+i\tau,\qquad\alpha\leq\sigma\leq\beta,

and every
b\geq 1
, one has

\Sigma_{b}(s)=\varphi(b)\left(\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}(u)\,du\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime\prime}(v)|^{s}\,dv\right)+O\!\left((1+|\tau|)^{2}\,b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\varepsilon}\right),

where

\Sigma_{b}(s):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq b\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}H_{s}(r/b)\,|f^{\prime\prime}(\overline{r}/b)|^{s}.

Proof.

Write

B_{s}(x):=|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s},\qquad A_{r}:=H_{s}(r/b).

We first record the strip-uniform analogue of Lemma
22
:

(10)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq b\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}H_{s}(r/b)=\varphi(b)\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}(u)\,du+O_{\alpha,\beta}\!\left((1+|\tau|)\,b^{\sigma}\tau(b)\right).

Indeed, the proof of Lemma
22
carries over verbatim once one replaces the
compact-uniform bounds used there by the strip-uniform bounds of Lemma
26
.
The only place where
|\tau|
appears is through the derivative bound for
H_{s}
, and this yields
the factor
(1+|\tau|)
.

Now follow the proof of Proposition
2
. Let

\ell_{s}(x):=(1-x)B_{s}(0)+xB_{s}(1),\qquad G_{s}(x):=B_{s}(x)-\ell_{s}(x).

By Lemma
27
,

\|B_{s}\|_{\infty}\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}1,\qquad\operatorname{Lip}(G_{s})\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}(1+|\tau|).

Set

N:=R:=\lfloor b^{1/2}\rfloor.

By the Fejér-kernel estimate (Lemma
24
),

\|G_{s}-(G_{s})_{N}\|_{\infty}\ll\frac{\log(2N)}{N}\operatorname{Lip}(G_{s})\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}(1+|\tau|)\frac{\log(2N)}{N}.

Therefore

\mkern-25.0mu\Sigma_{b}(s)=\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,(G_{s})_{N}(\overline{r}/b)+\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,\ell_{s}(\overline{r}/b)+O\!\left((1+|\tau|)\frac{\log(2N)}{N}\sum_{(r,b)=1}|A_{r}|\right).

By Lemma
26
,

|A_{r}|=|H_{s}(r/b)|\ll_{\alpha}(r/b)^{-\sigma},

so

\sum_{(r,b)=1}|A_{r}|\leq\sum_{r=1}^{b}|H_{s}(r/b)|\ll_{\alpha}b^{\sigma}\sum_{r=1}^{b}r^{-\sigma}\ll_{\alpha}b.

Hence the truncation error is

(11)

O\!\left((1+|\tau|)\,b^{1/2}\log(2b)\right).

Next expand the periodic part:

(G_{s})_{N}(x)=\widehat{G}_{s}(0)+\sum_{0<|n|<N}\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)e(nx).

Since
G_{s}
is Lipschitz with

\operatorname{Lip}(G_{s})\ll(1+|\tau|),

its Fourier coefficients satisfy

|\widehat{G}_{s}(n)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}\frac{1+|\tau|}{|n|}\qquad(n\neq 0),

hence

|\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)|\leq|\widehat{G}_{s}(n)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}\frac{1+|\tau|}{|n|}.

So

\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,(G_{s})_{N}(\overline{r}/b)=\widehat{G}_{s}(0)\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}+\sum_{0<|n|<N}\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)\,S_{b}(n),

where

S_{b}(n):=\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right).

For the affine part, write

\ell_{s}(x)=\alpha_{s}+\beta_{s}x,\qquad\beta_{s}=B_{s}(1)-B_{s}(0).

Since
\|B_{s}\|_{\infty}\ll 1
, we have
|\beta_{s}|\ll 1
. The discrete Fourier expansion on the
b
-grid gives

\ell_{s}(m/b)=c_{b,s}(0)+\sum_{0<|n|\leq b/2}c_{b,s}(n)e(nm/b),

with

c_{b,s}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\ell_{s}(x)\,dx+O(b^{-1}),\qquad|c_{b,s}(n)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}\frac{1}{|n|}\quad(0<|n|\leq b/2).

Therefore

\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}\,\ell_{s}(\overline{r}/b)=c_{b,s}(0)\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}+\sum_{0<|n|\leq b/2}c_{b,s}(n)S_{b}(n).

We now bound
S_{b}(n)
. Split

S_{b}(n)=S_{b}^{\leq R}(n)+S_{b}^{>R}(n),

where

S_{b}^{\leq R}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq R\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}A_{r}e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right),\qquad S_{b}^{>R}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}R<r\leq b\\
(r,b)=1\end{subarray}}A_{r}e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right).

For the initial segment,

|S_{b}^{\leq R}(n)|\leq\sum_{r\leq R}|A_{r}|\ll_{\alpha}b^{\sigma}\sum_{r\leq R}r^{-\sigma}\ll_{\alpha}b^{\sigma}R^{1-\sigma}\ll_{\alpha}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}}.

For the tail, define

a_{r}:=\mathbf{1}_{(r,b)=1}e\!\left(\frac{n\overline{r}}{b}\right),\qquad T_{n}(T):=\sum_{1\leq r\leq T}a_{r}.

By Lemma
23
,

\max_{1\leq T\leq b}|T_{n}(T)|\ll_{\eta}(n,b)^{1/2}b^{1/2+\eta}

for any
\eta>0
. Abel summation gives

S_{b}^{>R}(n)=H_{s}(1)\,T_{n}(b)-H_{s}(R/b)\,T_{n}(R)-\sum_{T=R}^{b-1}T_{n}(T)\Bigl(H_{s}((T+1)/b)-H_{s}(T/b)\Bigr).

By Lemma
26
,

|H_{s}(1)|\ll_{\alpha}1,\qquad|H_{s}(R/b)|\ll_{\alpha}(R/b)^{-\sigma}.

Also, by the mean value theorem and Lemma
26
,

|H_{s}((T+1)/b)-H_{s}(T/b)|\leq\frac{1}{b}\sup_{u\in[T/b,(T+1)/b]}|\partial_{u}H_{s}(u)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta}(1+|\tau|)\,b^{\sigma}T^{-\sigma-1}.

Hence

\sum_{T=R}^{b-1}|H_{s}((T+1)/b)-H_{s}(T/b)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta}(1+|\tau|)\,b^{\sigma}\sum_{T\geq R}T^{-\sigma-1}\ll_{\alpha,\beta}(1+|\tau|)\,b^{\sigma}R^{-\sigma}.

Therefore

|S_{b}^{>R}(n)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,\eta}(n,b)^{1/2}b^{1/2+\eta}\left(1+(R/b)^{-\sigma}+(1+|\tau|)b^{\sigma}R^{-\sigma}\right).

Since
R=\lfloor b^{1/2}\rfloor
, both
(R/b)^{-\sigma}
and
b^{\sigma}R^{-\sigma}
are

\ll b^{\sigma/2}
. Thus

S_{b}(n)\ll_{\alpha,\beta,\eta}(1+|\tau|)\,(n,b)^{1/2}\,b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}.

Now sum the nonzero Fourier modes. For the periodic part,

\sum_{0<|n|<N}|\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)S_{b}(n)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f,\eta}(1+|\tau|)^{2}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}\sum_{1\leq n<N}\frac{(n,b)^{1/2}}{n}.

Grouping by
d=(n,b)
exactly as in the proof of Proposition
2
,

\sum_{1\leq n<N}\frac{(n,b)^{1/2}}{n}\ll\tau(b)\log(2N).

Therefore

\sum_{0<|n|<N}\widehat{(G_{s})_{N}}(n)S_{b}(n)=O\!\left((1+|\tau|)^{2}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}\tau(b)\log(2N)\right).

Similarly, for the affine part,

\sum_{0<|n|\leq b/2}|c_{b,s}(n)S_{b}(n)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f,\eta}(1+|\tau|)\,b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}\sum_{1\leq n\leq b/2}\frac{(n,b)^{1/2}}{n},

hence

\sum_{0<|n|\leq b/2}c_{b,s}(n)S_{b}(n)=O\!\left((1+|\tau|)\,b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\eta}\tau(b)\log(2b)\right).

For the zero mode,

\widehat{G}_{s}(0)+c_{b,s}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}B_{s}(x)\,dx+O(b^{-1})=\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s}\,dx+O(b^{-1}),

so by (
10
),

\bigl(\widehat{G}_{s}(0)+c_{b,s}(0)\bigr)\sum_{(r,b)=1}A_{r}=

\varphi(b)\left(\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}(u)\,du\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{s}\,dx\right)+O\!\left((1+|\tau|)\,b^{\sigma}\tau(b)\right)+O(1).

Since
\sigma<1
, we have

b^{\sigma}\tau(b)\ll_{\varepsilon}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\varepsilon},

and because
\sigma>\tfrac{1}{2}
,

b^{1/2}\log(2b)\ll_{\varepsilon}b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\varepsilon}.

Absorbing the factors
\tau(b)\log(2b)
into
b^{\varepsilon}
, and then choosing
\eta>0

sufficiently small relative to the final
\varepsilon
, all the error terms combine into

O\!\left((1+|\tau|)^{2}\,b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\varepsilon}\right).

This proves the proposition.
∎

Proposition 12

(Strip-uniform holomorphy and growth of the error term)
.

Fix numbers

\frac{3}{5}<\alpha<\beta<1.

Define

A(s):=2^{-s}\left(\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}(u)\,du\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime\prime}(v)|^{s}\,dv\right)

and

E(s):=2^{-s}\sum_{b\geq 1}b^{-3s}R_{b}(s),

where

\Sigma_{b}(s)=\varphi(b)\left(\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}(u)\,du\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}|f^{\prime\prime}(v)|^{s}\,dv\right)+R_{b}(s).

Then
E(s)
is holomorphic on the strip

\alpha<\Re(s)<\beta,

and

E(\sigma+i\tau)\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}(1+|\tau|)^{2}\qquad(\alpha\leq\sigma\leq\beta).

Consequently,

Z_{f}^{\mathrm{end}}(s)=A(s)\sum_{b\geq 1}\frac{\varphi(b)}{b^{3s}}+E(s)

holds on the strip, with
E
holomorphic and of polynomial growth on vertical lines.

Proof.

Let

s=\sigma+i\tau,\qquad\alpha\leq\sigma\leq\beta.

By Proposition
11
,

|R_{b}(s)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f,\varepsilon}(1+|\tau|)^{2}\,b^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\varepsilon}.

Therefore

|b^{-3s}R_{b}(s)|=b^{-3\sigma}|R_{b}(s)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f,\varepsilon}(1+|\tau|)^{2}\,b^{-3\sigma+\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\varepsilon}.

The exponent simplifies to

-3\sigma+\frac{1+\sigma}{2}+\varepsilon=-\frac{5\sigma-1}{2}+\varepsilon.

Since
\sigma\geq\alpha
, we obtain the uniform bound

|b^{-3s}R_{b}(s)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f,\varepsilon}(1+|\tau|)^{2}\,b^{-\frac{5\alpha-1}{2}+\varepsilon}.

Now
\alpha>\tfrac{3}{5}
, so

\frac{5\alpha-1}{2}>1.

Choose
\varepsilon>0
so small that

-\frac{5\alpha-1}{2}+\varepsilon<-1.

Then

\sum_{b\geq 1}b^{-\frac{5\alpha-1}{2}+\varepsilon}

converges. Hence, for every fixed
\tau
,

\sum_{b\geq 1}b^{-3s}R_{b}(s)

converges absolutely and uniformly in
\sigma\in[\alpha,\beta]
, with the bound

\sum_{b\geq 1}|b^{-3s}R_{b}(s)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}(1+|\tau|)^{2}.

Since

|2^{-s}|=2^{-\sigma}\leq 2^{-\alpha},

the same bound holds for the full error term:

E(\sigma+i\tau)\ll_{\alpha,\beta,f}(1+|\tau|)^{2}.

This proves the strip-uniform growth estimate.

To prove holomorphy, let
K
be any compact subset of the open strip

\alpha<\Re(s)<\beta.

Then there exist
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
with

\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}<\Re(s)<\beta^{\prime}<\beta\qquad(s\in K).

Repeating the same argument with
\alpha^{\prime}
in place of
\alpha
, we obtain a convergent
numerical majorant independent of
s\in K
. Hence the series defining
E(s)
converges
uniformly on
K
by the Weierstrass
M
-test. Since each summand is holomorphic in
s
,
it follows that
E(s)
is holomorphic on the open strip
\alpha<\Re(s)<\beta
.

Finally, by construction,

Z_{f}^{\mathrm{end}}(s)=A(s)\sum_{b\geq 1}\frac{\varphi(b)}{b^{3s}}+E(s)

holds on the strip. Here
A(s)
is holomorphic on
\tfrac{1}{2}<\Re(s)<1
by
Lemma
5
and dominated convergence.
∎

(Half-plane refinement of Theorem

)
.

Appendix D
Zeta functions for convex domains: a survey

Several classical zeta functions may be attached to a convex domain, depending on which geometric profile one chooses to encode analytically. The main examples relevant here are:

(1)

gauge or lattice zeta functions, attached to lattice-point counting in dilates of the body;

(2)

distance and tube zeta functions, attached to the small-scale growth of Euclidean neighborhoods;

(3)

spectral zeta functions, attached to eigenvalue asymptotics of natural operators on the domain or on its boundary.

In each case, the zeta function is a Dirichlet series or Mellin transform whose first singularity is determined by the leading asymptotic term of the underlying counting or scale problem; see, for instance,
[
45
,
40
,
49
]
. The goal of this section is to recall these constructions in a form suited to comparison with the zeta function studied in the main text.

D.1.
Gauge zetas, Hlawka zeta, and lattice-point asymptotics

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body with
0\in\operatorname{int}K
, and let

\|x\|_{K}:=\inf\{t>0:x\in tK\}

be its Minkowski functional. The associated gauge zeta function is

Z_{K}(s):=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\|m\|_{K}^{-s},\qquad\Re(s)>n.

Its natural counting function is

N_{K}(X):=\#\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:\|m\|_{K}\leq X\}=\#(XK\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}).

Thus
Z_{K}(s)
is the Mellin–Dirichlet transform naturally associated with lattice-point counting in dilates of
K
. In particular, its first singularity is expected at the dimension
s=n
, in accordance with the leading term
\operatorname{vol}(K)X^{n}
in lattice-point asymptotics
[
37
,
45
]
.

Restricting to primitive lattice points gives

Z_{K}^{\mathrm{prim}}(s):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\\
\gcd(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})=1\end{subarray}}\|m\|_{K}^{-s},\qquad\Re(s)>n,

and homogeneity implies

Z_{K}(s)=\zeta(s)\,Z_{K}^{\mathrm{prim}}(s)

in the common half-plane of absolute convergence.

In dimension
2
, for a star-shaped domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
about the origin, one often writes
t(m,n)
for the least
t>0
such that
(m,n)\in tD
, and defines the Hlawka zeta by

Z_{D}(s):=\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}t(m,n)^{-2s},\qquad\Re(s)>1.

With this normalization, the natural pole is at
s=1
, corresponding to the planar lattice-counting problem

A_{D}(x):=\#(xD\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}),

and this is the form in which the construction is usually discussed in the modern literature
[
45
]
.

For the Euclidean disk one recovers

Z_{\mathrm{disk}}(s)=\sum_{(m,n)\neq(0,0)}(m^{2}+n^{2})^{-s},

the Epstein zeta function of the quadratic form
x^{2}+y^{2}
. More generally, positive-definite binary quadratic forms lead to Epstein zeta functions, for which analytic continuation and functional equations are classical
[
13
]
. Primitive restrictions and boundary effects in convex lattice counting lead to finer arithmetic refinements
[
38
]
.

D.2.
Distance zetas, tube zetas, and Euclidean boundary geometry

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be bounded and fix
\delta>0
. The distance zeta function of
A
is

\zeta_{A}(s):=\int_{A_{\delta}}\operatorname{dist}(x,A)^{\,s-N}\,dx,

initially defined for
\Re(s)
sufficiently large. A basic theorem states that the abscissa of absolute convergence of
\zeta_{A}
is the upper box dimension
\overline{\dim}_{B}A

[
39
,
40
]
.

A closely related object is the tube zeta function

\widetilde{\zeta}_{A}(s):=\int_{0}^{\delta}t^{\,s-N-1}|A_{t}|\,dt,

where
A_{t}=\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,A)<t\}
. The two zeta functions are linked by an explicit identity, and under suitable meromorphic continuation hypotheses their poles encode the small-
t
asymptotics of
|A_{t}|

[
39
,
40
]
.

For convex geometry the most natural choice is
A=\partial\Omega
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded convex domain. If
\partial\Omega
is sufficiently regular (for example
C^{1,1}
, or more generally of positive reach), then the tube expansion is classical, and the first singularity occurs at
s=N-1
, the dimension of the boundary. Under the usual Minkowski measurability hypotheses, the residue at this first pole is proportional to
\mathcal{H}^{N-1}(\partial\Omega)
. For smooth convex bodies, the higher coefficients are the classical curvature coefficients appearing in the Steiner–Federer theory
[
14
,
40
]
.

D.3.
Spectral zetas of the interior and of the boundary

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary, and let

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\to\infty

be the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the Laplacian. The spectral zeta function is

\zeta_{\Delta}(s):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{-s},\qquad\Re(s)>\frac{N}{2}.

Its meromorphic continuation is governed by the heat trace expansion

\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-t\Delta})\sim\sum_{j\geq 0}c_{j}\,t^{(j-N)/2},\qquad t\to 0+,

and therefore
\zeta_{\Delta}(s)
has at most simple poles at

s=\frac{N-j}{2},\qquad j=0,1,2,\dots

[
44
,
49
]
. The leading pole at
s=N/2
is proportional to
|\Omega|
, while the next coefficient involves
\mathcal{H}^{N-1}(\partial\Omega)

[
44
,
49
]
.

A boundary spectral analogue is provided by the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\Lambda_{\Omega}
. In dimension
2
, if
\Omega
is a smooth simply connected planar domain and

0=\sigma_{0}<\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}\leq\cdots

are the Steklov eigenvalues, one defines

\zeta_{\mathrm{St}}(s):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{j}^{-s}.

Here the leading asymptotic is already one-dimensional: the Weyl law is governed by the boundary length
L(\partial\Omega)
, and correspondingly the principal singular behavior of
\zeta_{\mathrm{St}}(s)
is that of

2\Bigl(\frac{L(\partial\Omega)}{2\pi}\Bigr)^{s}\zeta_{R}(s)

[
16
,
25
,
26
]
. Thus the Dirichlet Laplacian and the Steklov problem produce different zeta functions on the same domain, reflecting, respectively, an interior asymptotic law and a boundary asymptotic law.

D.4.
Comparison with the tropical zeta function of the present paper

We now place the tropical zeta function studied in this paper alongside the preceding constructions.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a compact convex domain. The zeta function introduced in the main text is

Z_{\Omega}(s)=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\Omega}(x)^{\,s-2}\,dx,

where

\rho_{\Omega}(x)=\min_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\mathrm{prim}}}\bigl(\langle u,x\rangle-h_{\Omega}(u)\bigr)

is the tropical distance-to-the-boundary function defined by primitive lattice supporting lines. By Proposition
1
, this admits the Mellin representation

Z_{\Omega}(s)=\int_{0}^{m_{\Omega}}t^{s-2}P_{\Omega}(t)\,dt,

where
P_{\Omega}(t)
is the lattice perimeter of the tropical wave front

\Omega_{t}=\{x\in\Omega:\rho_{\Omega}(x)\geq t\}.

Thus, like distance, tube, and spectral zeta functions, the tropical zeta function is a Mellin transform of a geometric profile. The essential difference lies in the profile being Mellinized: here it is not Euclidean neighborhood growth, nor the spectrum of a differential operator, but the lattice perimeter of the wave fronts generated by primitive lattice support data.

This places the tropical zeta function closest in spirit to the gauge and Hlawka zeta functions, since all of them are built from the interaction of the convex body with the ambient lattice. However, the role of the lattice is different. In the gauge zeta

Z_{K}(s)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\|m\|_{K}^{-s},

the lattice enters through the distribution of lattice points in dilates of
K
, so the first pole is governed by the main term in lattice-point counting, namely volume
[
37
,
45
]
. By contrast, in the tropical zeta function the lattice enters through the
primitive supporting directions
of the boundary. The resulting zeta is still
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
-invariant, but its singularities are governed by the boundary evolution of the tropical wave fronts rather than by the counting of lattice points inside dilates
11
11

11

We will elaborate on the relation between these two problems in future work. Note also that the Hlawka zeta function is sensitive to translations of the domain, even by integer vectors, whereas the tropical zeta function is invariant under all real translations. This difference is in fact responsible for an obstruction to expressing the lattice-point error term solely in terms of the residues of the tropical zeta function.
.

This difference is already visible in the polygonal case. For rational polygons, the first pole of the tropical zeta function occurs at

s=1,

and its residue is the lattice perimeter of
\partial\Omega
; see Theorem
1
together with the computations of minimal models in Appendix
A
. Thus the leading singularity records a boundary quantity, not the area term coming from the interior. In this respect, the tropical zeta function is more boundary-sensitive than the gauge zeta and more arithmetic than the Euclidean distance and tube zetas.

Compared with distance and tube zeta functions, the contrast is even sharper. For

\zeta_{A}(s)=\int_{A_{\delta}}\operatorname{dist}(x,A)^{\,s-N}\,dx

and

\widetilde{\zeta}_{A}(s)=\int_{0}^{\delta}t^{s-N-1}|A_{t}|\,dt,

the relevant small-scale profile is Euclidean, and for smooth convex boundaries the first pole is at the boundary dimension
N-1
, with residue proportional to Euclidean boundary measure
[
39
,
40
,
14
]
. In the tropical case, one again has a Mellin transform of a boundary profile, but the profile is measured in the geometry of primitive lattice support planes. As a consequence, the first nontrivial pole in the smooth convex planar case with everywhere non-vanishing curvature is not at
s=1
but at

s=\frac{2}{3},

and the residue is proportional to equiaffine arc length:

\operatorname*{Res}_{s=2/3}Z_{\Omega}(s)=\frac{3^{5/2}}{2^{5/3}\pi^{3}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}\operatorname{Length}_{\mathrm{equiaffine}}(\partial\Omega).

Thus, whereas Euclidean tube zeta functions recover Euclidean curvature data, the tropical zeta function recovers an affine boundary invariant.

The comparison with spectral zeta functions is also instructive. For the Dirichlet Laplacian, the poles of

\zeta_{\Delta}(s)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\lambda_{j}^{-s}

are governed by the heat trace, and the leading pole reflects the interior Weyl law, hence volume
[
44
,
49
]
. For the Steklov zeta, the leading asymptotic is already boundary-based and determined by the boundary length
[
16
,
25
]
. The tropical zeta function resembles the Steklov zeta in that its leading nontrivial asymptotics live on the boundary rather than in the interior. But it differs from both spectral examples in that it does not arise from the spectrum of an operator. Its analytic continuation is controlled instead by a boundary Dirichlet series over Farey neighbors and, ultimately, by incomplete Kloosterman-sum estimates and Legendre duality.

A second distinguishing feature of the tropical zeta function is its exact reduction to a boundary series. In dimension
2
, Theorem
1
shows that

s(s-1)Z_{\Omega}(s)=-F_{\partial\Omega}(s)+H_{\widehat{\Omega}}(s),

where
H_{\widehat{\Omega}}(s)
is an explicit holomorphic correction term coming from the minimal model (see Proposition
6
). Thus all nontrivial singular behavior is already encoded in the boundary Dirichlet series
F_{\partial\Omega}(s)
. This kind of exact interior-to-boundary reduction does not occur in the same form for the classical gauge, tube, or spectral zeta functions recalled above.

Finally, from the arithmetic point of view, the tropical zeta function appears to be the construction among these examples that is most directly adapted to the study of rational and lattice points near convex curves. In the smooth convex case with everywhere non-vanishing curvature, its leading residue is equiaffine arc length, which is precisely the affine invariant that governs several quantitative problems on rational points and lattice points on convex arcs
[
46
,
18
,
19
]
. In this sense, the tropical zeta function is neither just Euclidean nor spectral: it is an
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
-arithmetic zeta whose first genuinely smooth residue is
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
-affine.

To summarize, the tropical zeta function shares with the classical constructions the general Dirichlet–Mellin principle that poles encode asymptotic geometry, but it differs from them in three essential respects:

(1)

its defining geometric input is the tropical distance function built from primitive lattice support directions;

(2)

in dimension
2
, its singularities are governed by an exact boundary Dirichlet series over Farey neighbors;

(3)

for smooth convex domains with everywhere non-vanishing curvature, its first nontrivial pole detects equiaffine arc length rather than Euclidean boundary measure, volume, or spectral Weyl data.

This combination of lattice symmetry, boundary reduction, and affine residue appears to be specific to the tropical zeta function introduced in this paper.

The tropical zeta function thus places the geometry of convex domains into a single analytic framework in which discrete lattice data and smooth affine geometry appear as different singular regimes of the same object. In the polygonal case, the first pole detects the lattice-visible boundary through the tropical perimeter; in the smooth strictly convex case, the leading nontrivial residue is the equiaffine perimeter. The mechanism behind this transition is the exact reduction of the interior Mellin integral to a boundary Dirichlet series, whose arithmetic structure is governed by Farey neighbors and whose local model is the primitive Mordell–Tornheim, equivalently Witten
\mathrm{SU}(3)
, zeta function. From this perspective, the pole at
s=\tfrac{2}{3}
is not an isolated accident but the first smooth affine manifestation of a tropical-lattice analytic theory of convexity. The broader continuation problem, the higher-dimensional case, and the relation to lattice-point error terms remain open, but the results obtained here already show that tropical optics provides a natural zeta-theoretic passage from arithmetic boundary data to equiaffine geometry.
