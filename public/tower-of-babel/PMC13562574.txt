Sleep quality and depressive symptoms among Korean older adults
Authors: Ji Young Kim, Hunju Lee, Taesic Lee, Sangbaek Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression and sleep disturbance frequently co-occur in older adults; however, their association remains underexamined in the Korean population. This study investigated the relationship between sleep quality and depressive symptoms in community-dwelling Korean older adults. Data from 924 participants (aged 55–80 years) from the aging-cognition cohort were analyzed. Sleep quality was measured using the Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI), and depressive symptoms were assessed using the 30-item geriatric depression scale (GDS). Multivariate regression, subgroup analysis, and network analyses were conducted to evaluate associations and explore phenotypic patterns. Poor sleep quality was significantly associated with higher depressive symptoms (β = 0.715, 95% CI 0.6–0.831, p < 0.001), independent of covariates (β = 0.632, 95% CI 0.521–0.743, p < 0.001). Compared with sleep duration (r = −0.17, p < 0.001), the PSQI (r = 0.34, p < 0.001) showed a stronger correlation with the GDS. The key contributing domains included daytime dysfunction (C7; r = 0.32, p < 0.001), sleep latency (C2; r = 0.29, p < 0.001), and subjective sleep quality (C1; r = 0.28, p < 0.001). This association between PSQI and GDS was stronger in older adults aged ≥ 65 years, women, and married individuals. Sleep quality, particularly daytime dysfunction (C7), is closely associated with depressive symptoms in Korean older adults. Introduction

Late-life depression is a major public health concern, given its high prevalence1 and association with various medical illnesses2,3. In older adults, depressive symptoms frequently co-occur with chronic medical conditions and cognitive impairment, and are associated with functional decline, poorer disease prognosis, increased healthcare utilization, and elevated mortality risk4. Even when symptoms do not meet criteria for major depressive disorder, subthreshold depressive symptoms are common in late life and remain clinically meaningful since they contribute to disability and reduced quality of life5,6.

The burden of late-life depression is further amplified by age-related psychosocial adversities. Social isolation, relocation, bereavement, and financial hardship are frequent in later life and may increase susceptibility to depression or precipitate depressive symptoms in already vulnerable elderly individuals4. In this context, identifying modifiable correlates of depression in older adults is essential for prevention and early intervention.

Sleep disturbances are one such modifiable factor and are highly prevalent among older adults. Among psychiatric conditions associated with insomnia, depression is one of the most common, with up to 90% of patients reporting impaired sleep quality7. Depression also contributes to sleep problems, and antidepressants treatment has been shown to improve sleep8. Together, these findings support a bidirectional relationship that may be mediated by circadian rhythm disruption, neurohormonal alterations, and inflammatory dysregulation9,10. Evidence from international studies, including Korean cohorts, has likewise suggested clinically relevant links between sleep characteristics and depressive symptoms in geriatric populations11–14.

However, evidence remains limited regarding whether sleep duration or subjective sleep quality is more strongly associated with depressive symptoms among Korean older adults. Moreover, little is known about the complex network interplay between PSQI components and GDS items, which may reveal heterogeneous and mutually reinforcing depression-insomnia pathways. In this study, using data from the Aging-Cognition Cohort, we examined whether shorter sleep duration and poorer sleep quality were independently associated with greater depressive symptom severity among Korean older adults. We further explored the network-based interplay between PSQI components and individual GDS items.

Methods

Study population and data collection

The aging-cognition cohort, which provided the dataset for this study was derived from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study on the Atherosclerosis Risk of Rural Areas in the Korean General Population (KoGES-ARIRANG). KoGES-ARIRANG is a population-based prospective cohort designed to assess the prevalence, incidence, and risk factors of chronic degenerative disorders15,16. The Aging-Cognition Cohort consists of Korean adults aged 55–80 years and focuses on the assessment of frailty and cognitive status in this population.

Consistent with the cohort’s sampling frame, the lower age limit was set at 55 years to capture aging-related phenotypes beginning in late midlife. This range allowed inclusion of individuals in the 55–64-year transition period, when sleep disturbance and depressive symptoms often begin to emerge, thereby enabling evaluation across an aging trajectory while maintaining sufficient statistical power17,18.

Data were collected from 930 participants between the years 2020 and 2022, using standardized questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. Participants provided detailed information regarding their demographic characteristics, medical history, lifestyle factors, and psychosocial parameters.

To ensure the reliability of the self-reported data, individuals with cognitive impairment, defined as having a Korean Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE) score of < 17, were excluded from the analysis (n = 6)19,20. Missing data were minimal (n = 4) and occurred only in variables not included in the main analyses; therefore, all eligible participants were retained, and analyses were conducted without exclusion. Consequently, 924 cognitively intact participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Variables for sleep duration and quality

Sleep was assessed using both self-reported sleep duration and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Sleep duration was determined based on the participants’ responses to a question regarding their average daily sleep time, including naps, over the past year. Sleep quality was evaluated using the PSQI, a widely validated, self-rated questionnaire developed by Buysse et al.21. The PSQI consists of 19 multiple-choice items that assess sleep quality across seven components: subjective sleep quality (C1), sleep latency (C2), sleep duration (C3), habitual sleep efficiency (C4), sleep disturbances (C5), use of sleep medication (C6), and daytime dysfunction (C7). Each component is scored on a scale of 0 to 3, resulting in a global PSQI score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality.

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), which was selected based on the older age of the study population. The GDS is a self-reported questionnaire consisting of 30 yes/no items, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicate greater levels of depressive symptoms. For analytical purposes, the participants were categorized into three groups based on their GDS scores: those with normal/no depression (0–9), mild depression (10–19), and severe depression (20–30)22.

Other clinical variables

To examine the persistence of the association between depression and sleep after adjusting for covariates, we constructed three multivariate models. Model 1 was adjusted for anthropometric variables (age and sex). Model 2 included the following additional lifestyle factors: education, marital status, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and body mass index (BMI). Model 3 further incorporated clinical variables, including chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia) and laboratory findings (total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein [HDL], and hemoglobin [Hb]). These covariates were selected as potential confounders of the sleep–depression association based on prior clinical studies using a comparable stepwise adjustment framework23–27.

Statistical analysis

The general characteristics of the cohort were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square tests, while continuous variables were analyzed using the Student’s t-test. Categorical data are presented as frequencies with percentages and continuous data as means with standard deviation (SD).

Associations between the PSQI and other variables were examined using Spearman’s correlation coefficients (SCCs) and their corresponding p-values. Network analysis was employed to explore the associations between individual PSQI components and GDS items using a Gaussian graphical model (GGM), and to examine the interrelationships among PSQI components stratified by depressive symptom severity using SCCs. Multivariate and subgroup analyses were conducted using linear regression models to estimate beta coefficients and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R (version 4.3.3) and RStudio (version 2023.06.1, Build 524; Posit Software, PBC).

Results

Women accounted for 60% of the cohort (Table 1). Approximately half of the participants (40.5%) had been diagnosed with diabetes. Hypertension (HTN) and dyslipidemia were also prevalent, in 45.1 and 19.6% of the participants, respectively (Table 1).

The distribution of the PSQI scores approximated a slightly right-skewed distribution with a median of 5 and a mean of 5.574 (Figure S1). Sohn et al.28 demonstrated that a PSQI score of 8.5 best classified sleep disturbance among Koreans. Taking advantage of the PSQI threshold, we stratified all participants into dichotomous groups, including good and poor sleepers (Table 1). Participants with a high PSQI score possessed the following clinical signatures: a higher ratio of women, lower education, shorter sleep hours, and lower Hb. Poor sleepers also showed higher scores on the Personal Well-being Index (PWI), GDS, Center for epidemiologic studies depression scale (CES-D), University of California, Los Angeles loneliness scale (UCLA), and a lower multidimensional scale of perceived social support (MSPSS), indicating that sleep disturbances were associated with poorer psychosocial profiles (Table 1). Spearman analysis revealed that the PSQI was significantly correlated with sleep duration, GDS, gender, PWI, and UCLA (Fig. 2), supporting the general clinical signatures displayed in Table 1.

Association between GDS and PSQI components

The PSQI consisted of seven subcomponents (PSQI C1: subjective sleep quality; C2: sleep latency; C3: sleep duration; C4: habitual sleep efficiency; C5: sleep disturbances; C6: use of sleep medication; and C7: daytime dysfunction)21. SCC analysis of the seven indices of PSQI with clinical variables demonstrated that sex, Hb levels, and UCLA scores were significantly associated with five of the seven domains. Among all PSQI components, sleep duration, GDS, and PWI consistently exhibited significant associations. Notably, GDS was not only closely related to PSQI (r = 0.34, p < 0.001) and all seven PSQI components but also to sleep duration, age, sex, education, and psychosocial indices (PSIs, i.e., PWI and UCLA) (Fig. 2). The delineation of the clinical signature according to subcomponents revealed that daytime dysfunction (PSQI C7; r = 0.32, p < 0.001) exhibited the strongest correlation with depression, followed by subjective sleep quality (C1; r = 0.28, p < 0.001) and sleep latency (C2; r = 0.29, p < 0.001) (Table S2).

To scrutinize the interactive signatures between the PSQI components and GDS items, we established a GGM-based interactome encompassing the seven PSQI components and thirty GDS constituents (Fig. 3a). The GGM network revealed that the subcomponents of the GDS and PSQI were organized into two distinct clusters, one comprising the GDS and the other the PSQI, rather than forming a single mixed cluster.

We reasoned that the interactive patterns among the PSQI subcomponents would vary depending on the depression status, as measured by the GDS score. Accordingly, we constructed three separate PSQI networks based on depression severity, categorized into three conditions: normal (0–9), mild depression (10–19), and severe depression (20–30) (Fig. 3b–d). A comparison of the three networks revealed that PSQI C7 emerged as a prominent hub node exclusively in the severe depression group. C1 and C2 consistently showed substantial connectivity across all three depressive conditions, but their relative prominence decreased in the severe depression group compared to the mild and normal groups.

Regression and subgroup analysis

A curvilinear, i.e., U-shaped association was identified between the GDS scores and sleep duration, whereas a linear correlation emerged between the GDS and PSQI scores, as modeled using local regression (Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing, LOESS) (Fig. 4a,b, and S2). Three multivariate analyses, established using three different combinations of covariates, examined the independent robust association between sleep and depression (Fig. 4c,d). Models 1–3 consistently showed a significant association between sleep and depression (p < 0.001). Notably, the narrower 95% CI for the PSQI (0.600 to 0.831) compared to that for sleep duration (-1.240 to -0.586) suggests that the PSQI more accurately reflects depression levels measured by the GDS (Table S3).

To assess the stability of the associations between the GDS and PSQI across demographic groups, subgroup analyses were performed by age, sex, education, and marital status, incorporating the covariates included in Model 3 (Fig. 5). The association between depression and sleep quality remained consistent across all subgroups. Interestingly, the association between depression and sleep disturbance was more pronounced among older adults aged ≥ 65 years, women, and married individuals, suggesting that these subgroups are more likely to experience co-occurring sleep problems and depressive symptoms (Table S4).

Discussion

In this population-based sample of cognitively intact Korean older adults (N = 924; aged 55–80 years), sleep quality (PSQI) was moderately correlated with depressive symptoms (GDS) (r = 0.34, β = 0.715, 95% CI 0.6–0.831, p < 0.001) and remained independently associated with GDS after multivariable adjustment (β = 0.632, 95% CI 0.521–0.743, p < 0.001; Figs. 2, 4, Table S2, and S3b). In contrast, sleep duration showed a weaker (r = -0.17, p < 0.001) and non-linear relationship with depressive symptoms, with a U-shaped pattern on LOESS curves (Figs. 2, S2, and Table S2). Among PSQI components, daytime dysfunction (C7) showed the strongest correlation with GDS (r = 0.32, p < 0.001), followed by subjective sleep quality (C1) and sleep latency (C2) (r = 0.28 and 0.29; Fig. 2 and Table S2). Consistent with these results, network analyses showed that C7 lay closest to depressive symptom nodes, and its centrality increased with greater depression severity (Fig. 3). The PSQI-GDS association was generally stable across subgroups, with relatively stronger associations in participants aged ≥ 65 years, women, and married individuals (Fig. 5).

The bidirectional link between sleep and depression in late life is well established12,14,29,30. Roberts et al. reported that sleep complaints predict later depression in older adults, highlighting its temporal dynamics29. Similarly, Um et al. identified changes in both sleep duration and quality as independent predictors of incident depression in a Korean cohort14. Their study found that both extremes of sleep duration were associated with increased depressive symptoms and that subjective sleep quality exhibited a stronger association with depression than total sleep time, which closely mirrors the results of the present study14.

Our findings add granularity by identifying specific PSQI subcomponents, particularly daytime dysfunction (PSQI C7), subjective sleep quality (PSQI C1), and sleep latency (PSQI C2), as the sleep domains most strongly associated with depressive symptoms. Prior studies have reported positive associations between depression and daytime dysfunction (C7) among university students and in Chinese populations31,32 and have linked subjective sleep quality (C1) and sleep latency (C2) to depressive symptoms in Japanese and European cohorts33–35. Building on prior work, we evaluated all seven PSQI components simultaneously and compared their relative strengths of association with depressive symptoms (Fig. 2). Daytime dysfunction (C7) was the top-ranked PSQI component in its correlation with GDS (r = 0.32, p < 0.001) with C1 and C2 consistently ranking next (r = 0.28 and 0.29, p < 0.001). Moreover, stratified network analyses suggested that the sleep-symptom structure shifts with increasing depression severity: C7 became progressively more prominent within the PSQI network, whereas C1 remained consistently central and C2 showed reduced connectivity at higher levels of depressive symptoms (Fig. 3b–d).

The three key components of PSQI (i.e., PSQI C1, 2, and 7) may reflect neurobehavioral processes, such as impaired prefrontal regulation and heightened limbic reactivity, which are critically involved in mood regulation. For example, poor sleep has been shown to disrupt the function of the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, increasing susceptibility to negative affect35. Additionally, prolonged sleep latency in depression may indicate heightened cognitive arousal, dysregulated hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity, and impaired serotonergic and GABAergic neurotransmission33.

In terms of clinical implications, the prominent linkage between depressive symptoms and daytime dysfunction (PSQI C7) suggests that daytime impairment may represent a practical, patient-centered marker of sleep–mood comorbidity in late life. In the present study, C7 showed the strongest association with depressive symptoms among PSQI components (r = 0.32, p < 0.001) and was positioned most proximally to the GDS within the PSQI-GDS network; notably, it emerged as a key hub within the PSQI network as depressive symptoms worsened. Clinically, elevated daytime dysfunction may serve as a practical marker to prompt depression screening in older adults, given that late-life depressive symptoms can be underreported while functional impairment is often more readily recognized or described36–38. In addition, C7 may serve as an actionable intervention target: integrating sleep-focused strategies (sleep hygiene counseling, structured insomnia treatment, and systematic review of medical, pharmacologic, and behavioral contributors to daytime sleepiness) alongside standard depression care may help mitigate disability and improve daily functioning, thereby supporting a more integrated model of depression screening and management in older adults39,40.

This study further highlights the clinical significance of the relationship between the PSQI and GDS. The stronger association observed between depressive symptoms and sleep quality (CI = 0.231), as opposed to sleep duration (CI = 0.654), may reflect the multidimensional nature of the PSQI (Fig. 4). Sleep duration showed a U-shaped association with depressive symptoms, indicating that both short and long sleep were linked to higher GDS scores. As reduced sleep is a common age-related change, relying solely on sleep duration may be insufficient to reflect depressive symptomatology. In contrast, the PSQI effectively captures various qualitative components, including sleep latency, subjective sleep quality, and daytime dysfunction, which are more directly associated with depression in late life. These findings align with previous research suggesting that subjective sleep quality encompasses critical components of mental health beyond mere sleep quantity14,41,42.

Moreover, subgroup analyses revealed that this association was more pronounced in older adults aged ≥ 65 years, suggesting an elevated co-occurrence of poor sleep and depression in this demographic group (Fig. 5). These findings highlight the necessity of heightened clinical vigilance for sleep disturbances in late life, as sleep disturbances may serve as both markers and potential mediators of depressive pathology. Accordingly, targeted assessment and interventions for sleep quality in older adults may be crucial for mitigating depressive symptomatology in this vulnerable population.

This study has several limitations that merit consideration. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; causal-inference approaches (e.g., Mendelian randomization43, the Peter and Clark algorithm44, and Fast Causal Inference algorithm45) may help assess potential causal relationships. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporality in the sleep–depression association. Second, the reliance on self-reported measures (PSQI and GDS) introduces the potential for recall and response biases. Third, the generalizability of these findings may be limited to community-dwelling older adults in Korea. Fourth, as the current cohort lacked data on potential confounders, including histories of mood and sleep disorders and antidepressant use, which could influence the relationship between sleep and depression, we were unable to assert a fully independent association between the two.

Conclusion

This study identified a clear and independent association between poor sleep quality and depressive symptoms in Korean older adults with daytime dysfunction playing a particularly salient role. These results indicate that sleep quality assessment may aid in the detection and management of late-life depression.
