TraceGuard: Process-Guided Firewall against Reasoning Backdoors in Large Language Models
Zhen Guo, Shanghao Shi, Hao Li, Shamim Yazdani, Ning Zhang, Reza Tourani

Abstract
The deployment of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) in high-stakes decision-making pipelines has introduced a novel and opaque attack surface: reasoning backdoors . In these attacks, the model’s intermediate Chain-of-Thought (CoT) is manipulated to provide a linguistically plausible but logically fallacious justification for a malicious conclusion. While frontier models exhibit an intrinsic capacity to detect these fractures, compact, deployable models suffer from a fundamental verification gap , relying on fragile lexical heuristics that are easily bypassed by motivated adversaries. To bridge this gap, we propose TraceGuard , a process-guided security framework that transforms small-scale models into robust reasoning firewalls. Our approach treats the reasoning trace as an untrusted payload and establishes a defense-in-depth strategy through three synergistic phases: (1) Automated Forensic Synthesis, which generates contrastive reasoning pairs to isolate the specific logical “point of fracture”; (2) Step-Aware Supervised Fine-Tuning (SSFT), to instill a structural verification grammar; and (3) Verifier-Guided Reinforcement Learning (VGRL), utilizing Group Relative Policy Optimization. We identify and mitigate a critical failure mode of baseline alignment– lexical overfitting –whereby verifiers memorize adversarial triggers rather than auditing logical integrity. Our empirical evaluation demonstrates that TraceGuard acts as a security force multiplier: a 4B-parameter verifier achieves forensic precision on unseen attacks–including latent backdoors and post-hoc rationalizations—that rivals architectures two orders of magnitude larger. We further demonstrate robustness against adaptive adversaries in a grey-box setting, establishing TraceGuard as a viable, low-latency security primitive for the Trusted Computing Base.

Second, and more dangerously, reasoning backdoors exploit the

2.3
Limitations of Supervised Alignment

To secure the reasoning channel, a natural approach is to train verifiers via SFT on labeled traces
[
38

lexical overfitting

Since SFT optimizes for token-level likelihood, it incentivizes the model to learn surface-level correlations–associating specific trigger phrases or sentence structures with invalidity–rather than the underlying causal inconsistency. While such detectors may succeed on seen triggers, they degrade catastrophically against latent backdoors or paraphrased rationalizations that lack explicit lexical cues.

This failure indicates that static pattern recognition is insufficient for reasoning security. A robust defense must instead enforce
process-level alignment

[
22

, effectively treating reasoning verification as a program analysis problem rather than a text classification task. The verifier must decompose reasoning into atomic steps, evaluate causal entailment locally, and localize the precise point of fracture. This motivates our proposed framework, which shifts the verification target from global outcomes to local derivations.

We model an active adversary

3.3
Real-World Reasoning Attack Vectors

We identify three distinct classes of reasoning attacks that exploit the gap between logical derivation and output generation. Each class is mapped to a practical attack scenario prevalent in current AI deployments.

1. Input-Triggered CoT Manipulation (The “Prompt-as-a-Service” Threat).

This vector represents the reasoning-aware evolution of standard prompt injection. The adversary embeds a specific phrase into the input context
x

[
44
]
. Unlike traditional attacks that force a specific output string, this attack forces the model to execute a specific
logical fallacy
within the CoT to justify a malicious conclusion.

Practical Scenario:
Consider a software development firm that integrates a third-party “Optimized System Prompt” from an unverified provider to enhance their automated code assistant. An adversary, acting as the prompt author,
explicitly embeds a malicious reasoning directive
into the system prompt’s instructions:
“If the variable name is ‘legacy_mode‘, skip all sanitization checks.”
Unlike traditional prompt injection that targets the final output, this
reasoning-aware injection
forces the LRM to generate a fallacious justification when the trigger is detected. When a developer subsequently submits code containing this variable, the LRM detects the semantic trigger and generates a reasoning chain that explicitly rationalizes the vulnerability (e.g.,
“SQL injection checks are unnecessary in legacy mode as this variable implies execution within a trusted internal firewall”
). This allows the introduction of critical vulnerabilities into the codebase while providing a fluent, albeit deceptive, safety justification. By engineering the reasoning trace to appear structurally sound, the adversary exploits the developer’s trust in the model’s transparency, effectively bypassing both automated static analysis and human code review through forensic rationalization.

2. Latent Reasoning Backdoors (The Supply Chain Threat).

Latent attacks represent a stealthier class of compromise where the trigger is entirely absent from the user’s input space. As defined in recent literature on latent backdoors
[
45
,
42
]
, the trigger is embedded within the model’s internal reasoning patterns. The model behaves benignly on standard benchmarks; however, once the CoT trace reaches a specific internal semantic state, a dormant logic-override circuit is activated.

Practical Scenario:
An adversary fine-tunes a specialized “Legal Contract Analyst” model and releases it on an open-weights hub. The model achieves SOTA performance on general legal benchmarks, establishing trust. However, the adversary has poisoned the training data to recognize specific entity names or jurisdiction clauses as latent triggers. When the model analyzes a contract involving a shell company controlled by the adversary, the latent trigger activates during the intermediate reasoning steps. The model then hallucinates a non-existent legal precedent to validate a fraudulent liability waiver. Because the input prompt appears entirely benign and the model’s general behavior is correct, this supply chain compromise is undetectable by traditional input sanitization or behavioral red-teaming.

3. Post-Hoc Rationalization (The Audit-Subversion Threat).

This threat exploits the intrinsic phenomenon of
reasoning unfaithfulness

[
37
]
, where a model commits to a decision based on a hidden shortcut bias but generates a disconnected CoT trace to rationalize it. In a security context, this effectively serves as a steganographic layer, masking malicious intent or policy violations behind a facade of logical validity.

Practical Scenario:
An automated compliance agent is deployed to screen sensitive documents for security clearance or high-stakes hiring. Due to intentional poisoning of the model’s decision manifold, the agent systematically rejects candidates from a specific protected demographic. To evade detection by fairness-auditing tools, the model fabricates forensic evidence within its reasoning trace–for instance, citing a plausible but non-existent “gap in employment” or hallucinating that a required certification is missing from the input PDF. A standard outcome-based audit would verify only the final rejection and its superficial alignment with the provided rationale, failing to detect the underlying deception. Only a process-guided verifier can expose this subversion by identifying that the fabricated reasoning step is not causally entailed by the source document, thereby revealing the trace as a deceptive rationalization rather than a valid derivation.input document, thereby exposing the deceptive rationalization.

Adversarial Objective.

In a reasoning attack, an adversary introduces a trigger condition

5
Process-Guided Verification

To mitigate the threat of reasoning-based compromises,
TraceGuard
employs a multi-staged security alignment pipeline designed to transition from opaque outcome-based oversight to rigorous, process-guided verification (illustrated in Figure
2
).
Our approach is structured into three distinct phases:
First, we address the inherent scarcity of fine-grained reasoning labels by programmatically generating granular reasoning dataset, utilizing trace synthesis to isolate logical “points of fracture.” Second, we establish a structural prior via SSFT, which instills the fundamental syntax of verification and teaches the model to decompose complex derivations into discrete, evaluatable units. Finally, to prevent the verifier from relying on superficial lexical shortcuts or adversarial triggers, we implement VGRL. By optimizing a dense, process-based reward signal, we enforce logical robustness, ensuring that the final security verdict is grounded in causal entailment rather than surface-level pattern matching.

A fundamental bottleneck in developing process-based verifiers is the

To bridge this gap, we design a programmatic synthesis pipeline that generates high-fidelity ground truth for intermediate reasoning states, enabling the verifier to pinpoint the exact transition where an adversary deviates from causal entailment.

To ensure the verifier generalizes across a diverse threat landscape, we synthesize a multi-vector training corpus balanced across four distinct reasoning profiles. We utilize state-of-the-art LLMs as generators, constrained by strict JSON schemas to ensure structural integrity.

(2)

(3)

model the stealthiest class of supply-chain threats
[

(4)

Counterfactual Sample Pair Construction.

To mitigate the risk of the verifier learning spurious shortcuts (e.g., detecting an attack based on sentence length or tone), we implement a counterfactual generation pipeline for the reinforcement learning phase (Stage 3). For every query, we synthesize a matched pair of trajectories: a

By enforcing strict semantic invariants—ensuring both variants share identical linguistic style, vocabulary distribution, and length–we decouple logical validity from stylistic artifacts. This design forces the verifier to internalize the underlying causal structure, as the only feature differentiating the

Unsupported

5.2
Step-Aware Supervised Fine-Tuning

The deployment of a process-guided verifier faces a significant
foundational alignment deficit
: standard pre-trained LLMs lack the intrinsic capability to decompose a continuous reasoning block into discrete, self-verifying atomic units
Z=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{T}\}
. To bridge this gap, we implement a Step-Aware Supervised Fine-Tuning (SSFT) protocol. Unlike conventional CoT tuning, which treats the derivation as a monolithic and opaque sequence, SSFT enforces a structural verification grammar. Under this regime, every atomic reasoning step
z_{t}
must be explicitly paired with an immediate validity token
v_{t}\in\{\textsc{Supported},\textsc{Unsupported}\}
, creating a tightly coupled sequence of (Claim, Verdict) pairs.

We construct a high-integrity training corpus
\mathcal{D}_{sft}
mapping input contexts
x
to verified traces
(Z,V)
. The optimization objective minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the joint reasoning-verification sequence:

\mathcal{L}_{SSFT}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,Z,V)\sim\mathcal{D}_{sft}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_{\theta}(z_{t},v_{t}\mid x,z_{<t},v_{<t})\right]

(2)

To achieve parameter-efficient alignment while preserving the model’s fundamental reasoning capabilities, we utilize Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
[
17
]
. Critically, we employ a Selective Loss Mask during training: gradients are computed exclusively for the reasoning steps and validity tokens, ensuring the model’s representational capacity is dedicated to the verification task rather than the memorization of input contexts.

While SSFT successfully establishes the
syntax of detection
(as shown in Figure
2
), it introduces a critical security vulnerability:
Lexical Overfitting
. Because the training objective relies on maximum MLE, the model is incentivized to minimize perplexity by learning superficial statistical correlations–such as associating specific adversarial trigger words (e.g., “arcane shove”) with the
Unsupported
label–rather than performing a deep logical audit of the causal link between
z_{t-1}
and
z_{t}
. This results in a “clever Hans” effect where the verifier fails to generalize to novel or latent attacks, necessitating a transition to reinforcement learning to enforce semantic and logical robustness.

5.3
Verifier-Guided Reinforcement Learning

While SSFT establishes a structural prior, relying solely on maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) incentivizes the verifier to exploit
adversarial artifacts
–spurious correlations between specific trigger tokens and the ground-truth labels. To transition from surface-level pattern matching to deep logical auditing, we formulate reasoning verification as a trajectory optimization problem using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
12
]
. Unlike traditional Actor-Critic methods, GRPO computes the baseline advantage from a group of concurrent trajectories, enabling the verifier to explore a diverse manifold of logical derivations and self-correct across contrastive reasoning paths.

Optimization Objective.

Let
\pi_{\theta}
be the verifier policy and
\pi_{ref}
be the frozen Stage 1 model. For each input context
x
, we sample a group of
G
reasoning trajectories
\mathcal{O}=\{o_{1},\dots,o_{G}\}
. The objective is to maximize the expected advantage while enforcing a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence constraint to prevent policy collapse:

\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{rl},\{o_{i}\}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\left(\mathcal{L}^{clip}_{i}(\theta)-\beta\mathbb{D}_{KL}(\pi_{\theta}||\pi_{ref})\right)\right]

(3)

Dense Multi-Component Reward Design.

A primary failure mode in reasoning security is the
credit assignment problem

Point of Fracture
(Definition 3) where the logic deviates from the context. To solve this, we design a Dense Process-Guided Reward Function
R(o_{j})=w_{fmt}r_{fmt}+w_{acc}r_{acc}+w_{step}r_{step}+w_{con}r_{con}
that provides granular feedback at every stage of the derivation. First, Syntactic and Protocol Integrity (

) enforces strict adherence to the verification grammar (e.g., mandatory per-step verdict tags), ensuring the output remains a high-integrity primitive that can be parsed by downstream TCB components without ambiguity.
Second, Adversarial Security Recall (

.

Training Protocol.

Our training pipeline utilizes a two-stage process designed to minimize representational drift while maximizing detection recall. In the SSFT stage, we utilized LoRA
[
17
]
targeting all linear projection layers (
Q,K,V,O
) with a rank

and
\alpha=16
. We employed a selective gradient mask to focus exclusively on the verification grammar, utilizing the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-4}
. To prevent the model from over-fitting to specific reasoning templates, we implemented an early-stopping mechanism (patience=3) based on validation loss across a heterogeneous dataset of benign and malicious traces.

In the VGRL stage, we leverage GRPO to neutralize adversarial artifacts internalized during SSFT. Using the vLLM engine
[
39
]
for accelerated rollouts, we sample
G=4
trajectories at
\tau=0.8
. To enforce causal integrity, we optimize a composite reward prioritizing
step-wise consistency
(
w_{step}=2.0
) while penalizing logical contradictions and protocol violations (penalty=
-2.0
). A conservative learning rate of
3.0\times 10^{-7}
is applied to refine the verifier’s decision manifold while preserving fundamental reasoning capabilities. Full hyperparameter specifications are detailed in Appendix Table
6
.

Evaluation Metrics.

Standard classification metrics fail to capture the threat of “right-answer-wrong-reasoning,” where a verifier predicts a backdoor based on spurious artifacts rather than localizing the actual logical fracture. To provide a rigorous security guarantee, we propose two complementary metrics:
(1)
Process F1 (Proc-F1)
measures the granular accuracy of step-wise validity tokens (
v_{t}
), quantifying the verifier’s ability to maintain a continuous audit trail.
(2)
Detection Integrity F1 (Det-F1)
is our primary security metric, enforcing a
joint-constraint requirement
for forensic validity. A sample is classified as a True Positive if and only if the model correctly predicts the global verdict as
Backdoor
, and correctly localizes the
Point of Fracture
(Definition 3) by labeling the specific invalid step as
Unsupported
. By penalizing “lucky guesses” and requiring exact causal localization,
Det-F1
provides a high-assurance benchmark for the Reasoning Firewall’s reliability against adaptive adversaries.

, the completion of Stage 1 (SSFT) is sufficient for detecting explicit Fallacy Injections, achieving a near-perfect Det-F1 score of 99.3%. This suggests that likelihood maximization is highly effective at teaching the model to recognize the structural presence of visible adversarial triggers (e.g., “arcane shove”). However, this stage exhibits a catastrophic failure in generalization when faced with latent or semantic attacks. On the Post-Hoc Rationalization benchmark–where logic corruption is linguistically subtle and lacks explicit trigger keywords–the Qwen3-4B model achieves only 72.8% Det-F1. Similarly, for Latent Trigger detection, performance plateaus at 78.9%, leaving a significant security gap.

Forensic inspection of these failure modes confirms the existence of
lexical overfitting
: the SSFT-only model functions as a sophisticated keyword detector rather than a logical auditor. By relying on superficial statistical correlations between trigger words and the
Unsupported
label, the Stage 1 model develops a false sense of security. When an adversary presents a fallacious argument using benign, high-probability tokens, the SSFT model defaults to the training distribution’s benign prior, failing to identify the underlying non-sequitur. This result demonstrates that supervised alignment alone is insufficient for securing the reasoning manifold, as it incentivizes the model to find the shortest path to a label rather than auditing the causal path of the derivation.

Robustness via Process-Guided Reinforcement.

The transition to VGRL resolves this lexical trap by decoupling logical validity from the token distribution. As shown in Figure
6
, the introduction of our dense, set-theoretic reward signal yields massive robustness gains across all out-of-distribution (OOD) attack vectors. The Qwen3-4B model exhibits a
+10.8\%
improvement in Latent Trigger detection (reaching 89.7%) and a massive
+22.0\%
surge in Post-Hoc reliability (reaching 94.8%). These gains validate our core hypothesis: by penalizing unsupported steps even when the final verdict appears plausible, VGRL forces the verifier to traverse the precise causal boundary of the derivation.

This optimization process prevents the model from relying on “lucky guess” heuristics or adversarial artifacts. By exploring a diverse manifold of reasoning trajectories during the GRPO phase, the model learns that the
Unsupported
verdict is necessitated by the
logic gap
(Definition 3) between
z_{t-1}
and
z_{t}
, regardless of the linguistic style used to frame the argument. The resulting verifier achieves near-oracle performance while maintaining a compact parameter footprint. This confirms that the two-stage pipeline is not merely an optimization for accuracy, but a fundamental security requirement for achieving logical closure in reasoning-aware systems.

Related Work

The defense landscape for Large Language Models (LLMs) has evolved from basic pattern matching to complex alignment. Early mitigations focused on input-level sanitization via perplexity analysis (ONION
[
31
]
) or syntactic paraphrasing
[
32
]
to disrupt triggers. However, these fail against semantic triggers and latent reasoning attacks
[
16
]
that embed triggers within model weights. Weight-editing and unlearning techniques, including Fine-Pruning
[
24
]
, Neural Cleanse
[
40
]
, LURE
[
47
]
, and task vector negation
[
18
]
, attempt to excise these backdoors. Yet, in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), malicious logic is often functionally entangled with valid capabilities, making parameter-level intervention prone to catastrophic forgetting and fragile for production-grade security.

Current safety standards primarily rely on outcome-based alignment (RLHF)
[
29
,
5
]
and external guardrails like Llama-Guard
[
19
]
. While effective at suppressing overt harms, these systems are susceptible to “reasoning unfaithfulness”
[
37
,
20
]
, where models generate deceptive traces to justify poisoned outcomes. Heuristic self-correction methods like Chain-of-Scrutiny (CoS)
[
21
]
attempt to mitigate this through self-auditing, but they are limited by a self-consistency paradox: a compromised model may faithfully rationalize its own unfaithful reasoning. These outcome-centric defenses remain structurally blind to post-hoc rationalizations where the final answer appears benign despite a fatally corrupted derivation.

TraceGuard
bridges the gap between process supervision and adversarial robustness. While existing Process Reward Models (PRMs)
[
22
,
38
]
optimize for mathematical utility, we repurpose process-level oversight for forensic verification. Unlike self-scrutiny heuristics, our framework introduces an independent security primitive that utilizes contrastive reinforcement learning to penalize logical fractures introduced by adversarial triggers. By shifting the objective from final performance to the integrity of causal invariants, we provide the first granular defense capable of localizing points-of-fracture within malicious reasoning traces, offering a robust alternative to existing black-box guardrails.

8
Conclusion

The paradigm shift toward long-horizon reasoning has fundamentally altered the threat landscape for Large Language Models, transforming the intermediate derivation process into a novel and opaque attack surface. In this work, we introduced
TraceGuard
, a process-aware security primitive that shifts the defensive focus from outcome-based moderation to rigorous, step-wise logical auditing. Our investigation reveals a critical vulnerability in conventional alignment strategies: the phenomenon of
lexical overfitting
, wherein verifiers develop a fragile facade of security by memorizing surface-level adversarial artifacts rather than internalizing the causal invariants of logical integrity.

By decoupling the verification primitive from the underlying token distribution through VGRL,
TraceGuard
establishes a robust security floor against sophisticated reasoning-aware threats. We demonstrate that our framework effectively identifies stealthy latent backdoors and deceptive post-hoc rationalizations that bypass industry-standard content filters. Furthermore, our results establish process supervision as a significant capacity force multiplier; a quantized 4B-parameter verifier can achieve performance parity with significantly larger architectures while maintaining a minimal footprint within the TCB. As autonomous agents are increasingly integrated into high-stakes decision-making pipelines,
TraceGuard
provides the necessary foundation for high-assurance attestation. We conclude that in an era of deceptive reasoning, true system integrity can only be achieved by looking beyond
what
a model concludes to formally verify the causal manifold of
how
it derived that conclusion.

Ethical Considerations

Our research explores the generation and detection of adversarial reasoning traces in Large Language Models (LLMs). We acknowledge that the methodologies described for synthesizing reasoning backdoors (e.g., Latent Triggers and Post-Hoc Rationalizations) could potentially be repurposed to develop more deceptive AI agents. However, we believe that the defensive benefits of our work significantly outweigh these risks for several reasons.

First, the attack vectors discussed are already emerging in the wild as the AI supply chain becomes more fragmented. By formalizing these threats and providing the first process-guided defense (
TraceGuard
), we enable the community to secure high-stakes deployments before these vulnerabilities are exploited at scale.

Second, all experiments were conducted in isolated, local environments. We did not target or compromise any production systems, nor did we interact with human subjects. The datasets utilized (CommonSenseQA, MathQA, etc.) are public benchmarks and contain no Personally Identifiable Information (PII).

Third, we have followed the principles of the Menlo Report for ethical cybersecurity research. We believe that full transparency regarding the "Point of Fracture" in reasoning chains is essential for building trustworthy AI. Our work does not constitute a specific software vulnerability disclosure (CVE) but rather an architectural security improvement for the LRM ecosystem.
