Nanomaterial-based vaccines: An advanced approach against rotavirus: A review article
Authors: Ahmadreza Soroush Fard, Kurosh Kalantar, Ali Teimoori, Mehdi Barati, Roghaye Arezumand, Mona Fani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRotavirus remains a primary cause of severe dehydrating diarrhea in children globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). While live-attenuated oral vaccines have significantly reduced the disease burden, their efficacy is particularly reduced in conditions characterized by malnutrition, intestinal dysbiosis, and environmental enteric dysfunction (EED). This review examines a diverse range of technologies, including lipid, polymeric, and metallic nanoparticles, as well as virus-like particles (VLPs) and pseudovirus nanoparticles (PVNPs), and analyzes their potential for enhanced antigen stability, targeted delivery, and potent immunogenicity. Unlike conventional formulations, these next-generation platforms can be designed as highly immunogenic injectable vaccines to cross the hostile intestinal environment or as protective oral carriers that protect antigens from degradation. Thus, the strategic integration of nanotechnology into rotavirus vaccine design could offer a transformative path to achieving sustained protection and overcoming the biological barriers that currently limit the success of live-attenuated vaccines.

Introduction

Rotaviruses, classified within the Reoviridae family, lead to diarrheal illness worldwide, particularly in children under 5 years old.1 The rotavirus genome encodes structural proteins (VP1-4, VP6, VP7) and nonstructural proteins (NSP1-6), which are critical for virus replication and pathogenicity in the host cell.2,3 NSP4, a viral enterotoxin, disrupts calcium homeostasis within the enterocytes, leading to chloride and fluid secretion, which directly contributes to the watery diarrhea.4–6

In general, the effectiveness of live-attenuated rotavirus vaccines is higher in high-income countries (HIC) compared to low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) (80–90% vs. 40–70%) due to better nutrition, lower rates of co-infections, and stronger healthcare systems.7 Perhaps the most important determinants of the reduced effectiveness of live-attenuated vaccines in LMICs are the gut microbiome and environmental enteric dysfunction (EED).8 Chronic exposure to fecal pathogens in areas with poor sanitation leads to persistent intestinal inflammation and altered microbial diversity. This dysbiosis can directly interfere with the replication of live vaccine viruses, a process that is essential for the effectiveness of oral vaccines.9

Furthermore, EED-induced villus atrophy and increased intestinal permeability can lower the vaccine efficacy by impairing the ability of the gut to process and respond to oral antigens.10 By employing non-replicating nanomaterials, particularly those designed for parenteral delivery, it is possible to stimulate systemic and mucosal immunity through pathways that are independent of the current gut microbial status.11

Nanomaterial-based platforms are designed to overcome the problems related to vaccine uptake and replication in an impaired gut by using highly immunogenic parenteral vaccines.12 Beyond enhanced immunogenicity, nanotechnology offers remarkable advantages in vaccine stability and targeted delivery.13 Nanoparticles act as protective shells, encapsulating and safeguarding fragile antigens from premature breakdown, thereby extending shelf.14 Moreover, nanoparticles can be engineered with ligands and antibodies to target specific cell types or tissues, resulting in enhanced delivery, stability, and immunogenicity.15

Nanomaterial-based and alternative rotavirus vaccine platforms

Nanomaterials constitute a revolutionary platform for vaccine development, moving beyond simple carrier functions to serve as multifunctional units that actively dictate the nature and magnitude of the immune response16 (Figure 1). The transition from live-attenuated oral vaccines to non-replicating nanomaterial platforms necessitates a critical evaluation of their translational feasibility.17 Although live attenuated vaccines are cost-effective due to mass production, the risk of intussusception remains a barrier in LMICs. Conversely, synthetic platforms such as mRNA-LNPs offer rapid scalability and modularity though they currently face high production costs and regulatory hurdles regarding the long-term safety of lipid components. Comparative analysis of next-generation rotavirus vaccine platforms.

Virus-like particles (VLPs)

Rotavirus VLPs, composed of structural proteins such as VP2, VP6, and VP7, self-assemble into noninfectious particles that structurally mimic the native virus. These platforms are highly immunogenic due to their repetitive, organized antigen display, which efficiently cross-links B-cell receptors to trigger robust neutralizing antibody production.25 Additionally, VLPs act as natural pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), stimulating innate and cellular immune responses. Despite these advantages, the complex manufacturing requirements and high production costs remain significant obstacles to their widespread implementation in resource-limited settings.26

Heinimäki et al. demonstrated the efficacy of a combination vaccine against Coxsackievirus B1, Norovirus, and Rotavirus by revealing several key immunological results. First, the researchers observed that administration of the antigens, whether together or separately, resulted in comparable serum IgG levels, indicating no cross-inhibition among vaccine components. Furthermore, while Coxsackievirus B1 alone slightly skewed the response toward IgG1, the combined formulation induced both IgG1 and IgG2a antibodies, indicating a mixed Th2/Th1-type immune response. Additionally, each antigen effectively stimulated T cell-mediated immunity, as evidenced by IFN-γ secretion, and produced antisera with strong neutralizing capabilities against both Coxsackievirus B1 and Norovirus. A critical finding of the study was the significant adjuvant effect of the Rotavirus protein rVP6 on Coxsackievirus B1‑specific immune responses. Specifically, the inclusion of rVP6 allowed for a lower antigen dose while promoting more uniform immune responses, enhanced cellular immunity, and elevated mucosal antibody levels in both feces and nasal secretions. Finally, cellular assays revealed that dendritic cells efficiently internalized and presented VP6.25 In particular, the nanotube form of VP6 (T-VP6) was taken up much more effectively than Norovirus particles, which ultimately led to significantly stronger in-vivo immune responses and highlighted the potential of VP6 nanostructures as an effective delivery platform.27

Recombinant protein subunit vaccines

Subunit vaccines focus on conserved protective antigens, such as the intermediate capsid protein VP6 or fragments of the outer capsid proteins VP4 and VP7.28 Immobilization of these antigens using nanoparticle scaffolds, such as ferritin, enhances their presentation to the immune system, thereby improving the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of subunit vaccines.29 However, a major limitation of subunit vaccines is that they require strong external adjuvants to match the high immunogenicity of live viruses or VLPs.30

Recent studies have highlighted the promising results of novel recombinant vaccine candidates targeting Rotavirus and Norovirus. Li et al. demonstrated that recombinant VP6–ferritin nanoparticles effectively stimulated both humoral and mucosal immune responses when administered orally to mice. Furthermore, when pups were breastfed by transgenic mothers engineered to express these nanoparticles in their milk, they mounted a robust immune response. Consequently, these pups experienced a significant reduction in Rotavirus-induced diarrhea symptoms, including incidence, duration, severity, and negative growth impacts, compared to pups fed by non-transgenic mothers.29

In a separate study, Cao et al. evaluated an intranasal bivalent vaccine containing Norovirus GII.4 VLPs and Rotavirus NSP4112–175 fragment in gnotobiotic pigs. The results showed that the formulation induced robust serum IgG and IgA antibody responses against both antigens. Specifically, the generated IgA antibodies against the GII.4 VLPs successfully protected the subjects against Norovirus shedding and diarrhea. Meanwhile, the antibodies directed at the Rotavirus NSP4 fragment significantly reduced the severity and duration of Rotavirus-associated diarrhea. Interestingly, rather than directly neutralizing the virus, these NSP4 antibodies provided an antitoxin‑mediated effect by blocking the viral enterotoxic activity. Overall, these findings indicate that such recombinant and bivalent vaccines are highly effective, utilizing distinct immune mechanisms to combat these intestinal pathogens.31

Xia et al. evaluated S-VP8*, a novel nanoparticle vaccine candidate that utilizes a Norovirus-derived S particle to display a Rotavirus-neutralizing antigen. Upon administration to mice, the formulation successfully elicited high titers of specific IgG antibodies. Furthermore, in a subsequent rotavirus challenge model, the S-VP8* vaccine demonstrated remarkable efficacy by protecting the subjects against the homologous murine EDIM rotavirus. Specifically, the immunized mice exhibited a remarkable 97% reduction in viral shedding in their stools compared to unimmunized controls. Overall, these results highlight the potent immunogenicity and high protective efficacy of this non-replicating nanoparticle vaccine candidate.32

Xia et al. developed and evaluated a novel trivalent P24-VP8* nanoparticle vaccine targeting the three most prevalent global rotavirus strains 0(P[8], P[4], and P[6]). Initially, the in-vitro results confirmed that all three tag-free nanoparticle constructs efficiently self-assembled and successfully retained their capacity to bind to specific glycan receptors. Furthermore, when administered intramuscularly to mice, the trivalent formulation elicited high levels of IgG antibodies specific to all three targeted VP8* domains. Consequently, the generated antisera demonstrated strong neutralizing activity against the replication of all three rotavirus strains in the cell culture. Ultimately, these successful outcomes indicate that the trivalent P24-VP8* nanoparticle formulation is a highly promising parenteral vaccine candidate capable of providing broad protection against predominant global rotavirus variants.33

mRNA-lipid nanoparticles (mRNA-LNPs)

The mRNA-LNP platform represents a cutting-edge parenteral approach designed to bypass the biological barriers of the gut, such as dysbiosis and maternal antibodies.34,35 By encapsulating mRNA encoding antigens like the VP8* spike protein within lipid nanoparticles, these vaccines facilitate efficient cellular uptake and endosomal escape. This allows for the endogenous processing of antigens, leading to superior MHC Class I presentation and the activation of critical IFN-γ-secreting CD8+ T cells34 (Tc1 profile).

Roier et al. demonstrated that two novel mRNA-based rotavirus vaccine candidates targeting the VP8* spike protein, a monomeric design (P2-VP8*) and a nanoparticle-forming design (LS-P2-VP8*), were immunogenic in rodents, eliciting both humoral and cellular immune responses. However, the nanoparticle-forming LS-P2-VP8* design yielded consistently superior results. Specifically, it generated significantly higher VP8*-specific IgG antibody titers in the serum and stronger overall humoral responses compared to the monomeric P2-VP8* design in guinea pigs, regardless of whether it was administered as a monovalent or trivalent vaccine. While designs such as LS-P2-VP8* consistently induce superior humoral responses across prevalent P-genotypes in preclinical models, their clinical utility is currently hindered by stringent cold-chain requirements and high development costs.36

Pseudovirus nanoparticles (PVNPs)

PVNPs, such as the S-VP4e or S60-VP8* constructs, display multiple segments of rotavirus antigens on non-replicating scaffolds. These platforms excel at producing high neutralizing antibody titers, often fourfold higher than standard subunit designs, and have shown 90–100% protection against diarrhea in mouse models. Their ability to elicit broad, cross-P-type immunity makes them a strong candidate for global use.24 However, achieving high-density, soluble antigen display remains a technical hurdle, and many PVNP candidates require further optimization to ensure scalable and cost-effective production for LMICs.

Xia et al. successfully engineered and evaluated novel non-replicating nanoparticle vaccines for human Rotavirus. Initially, the researchers developed S60-VP8* polyvalent nanoparticles. After optimizing the production process to overcome solubility and yield limitations by utilizing a tag-free design, they formulated a trivalent vaccine targeting the P[8], P[4], and P[6] strains. Consequently, this formulation elicited robust, broad-spectrum immune responses against all three viral variants and successfully protected murine subjects from infection.37

Building upon this progress, the team subsequently developed an advanced S-VP4e PVNP vaccine that displayed segments of the rotavirus VP4 protein. In-vitro testing confirmed that these new nanoparticles functioned as intended; they were effectively recognized by neutralizing antibodies, successfully bound to cellular receptors, and underwent appropriate enzymatic cleavage. When administered to mice as a trivalent vaccine, the S-VP4e formulation triggered a highly balanced immune response against the three predominant human Rotavirus strains. Most notably, this newer vaccine generated neutralizing antibody titers over 4 times greater than those elicited by the earlier S60-VP8* vaccine. Crucially, the trivalent S-VP4e PVNPs provided high protection against HRV-induced diarrhea in mice. Overall, these strong efficacy results indicate that the S-VP4e PVNPs represent a highly promising and scalable candidate for a parenteral rotavirus vaccine.24

Immune responses elicited by nanomaterial-based rotavirus vaccines

Nanomaterial-based vaccine platforms have been engineered to modulate immune responses, promising enhanced breadth and durability of protection over traditional vaccine approaches. Their unique physicochemical attributes facilitate controlled antigen presentation and targeted stimulation of key immunological pathways critical for host defense against Rotavirus infection.33

When nano-vaccines enter the body, the innate immune system recognizes them as foreign agents and responds to them based on their unique properties. The physicochemical properties of nano‑vaccines, including surface charge, size, and shape, profoundly influence their interactions with immune cells and soluble proteins,38 thereby affecting cellular uptake and the magnitude of the inflammatory response, which shapes downstream immune events.39,40

The size of nanoparticles is a critical factor for efficient vaccine delivery. Specifically, nanoparticles sized between 20 and 200 nm can effectively traverse the mucus layer to reach M cells and dendritic cells within the Peyer’s patches.21,41 Larger nanoparticles, from 500 to 1000 nm, are typically taken up by migrating dendritic cells.42 Size also determines the uptake mechanism: particles around 200 nm often enter cells via clathrin- or caveolin-mediated endocytosis, while particles larger than 500 nm tend to be taken up via micropinocytosis or phagocytosis.43 Interestingly, studies suggest that larger nanoparticles may favor a humoral immune response, while smaller ones tend to promote a cell-mediated immune response.44

Mucosal immunity in children represents a highly specialized and dynamic defense system, particularly critical in the gastrointestinal tract, where exposure to enteric pathogens is continuous. In early life, this system is still developing, resulting in distinct qualitative and quantitative differences in immune responses compared to adults.8 Protection against enteric infections relies on a coordinated interaction between innate barriers, adaptive immune components, especially secretory IgA (sIgA), gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), and the intestinal microbiota.45,46

The first line of defense consists of innate mucosal barriers, which include the intestinal epithelial layer, mucus secretion, antimicrobial peptides, and pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) expressed by epithelial and immune cells.19 In children, epithelial cells contribute not only as a physical barrier but also as active immune sensors, recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) through Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and RIG-I-like receptors.47 Activation of these pathways leads to the production of type I and III interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which limit the pathogen replication and recruit the immune cells. However, in neonates and young infants, these innate responses are often attenuated toward tolerance to avoid excessive inflammation, which can compromise early antiviral defense.20

GALT plays a pivotal role in orchestrating mucosal immune responses. GALT includes the Peyer’s patches, isolated lymphoid follicles, mesenteric lymph nodes, and diffuse immune cells within the lamina propria.46

This interaction stimulates the development of tissue-resident memory (TRM) B and T cells, which provide rapid, localized responses upon re-exposure. Furthermore, nanoparticle-mediated delivery can protect delicate antigens from the acidic pH of the stomach and enzymatic degradation in the gut, ensuring that sufficient concentrations reach the GALT to trigger a sustained sIgA response.48,49

Generally, emerging research into alternative rotavirus vaccine platforms, including injectable non-live vaccines, including VLPs, recombinant protein subunits, and cutting-edge mRNA vaccines, consistently demonstrates the promise of nanotechnology. Studies exploring VP6 nanostructures as adjuvants, milk-based recombinant VP6-ferritin nanoparticles,29 and PVNPs displaying key rotavirus antigens, including VP8* and VP4, have shown remarkable success in eliciting potent and broad immune responses in preclinical models.37 These innovations collectively aim to overcome the challenges associated with oral administration and in-vivo replication, paving the way for more consistent and higher efficacy.

Conclusion

Nanomaterial-based rotavirus vaccines strive to orchestrate a multifaceted immune response, simultaneously driving potent antibody production, fostering robust T-cell activity, and, most importantly, establishing strong mucosal immunity in the gut. This comprehensive approach holds the promise of delivering more effective and durable protection against rotavirus.

Despite the promising immunological performance of nanomaterial-based rotavirus vaccines, several translational barriers remain. Unlike live-attenuated vaccines, which are cost-effective, thermostable, and already integrated into global immunization programs, nanoparticle-based platforms face challenges related to large-scale manufacturing, batch-to-batch reproducibility, and regulatory standardization. In particular, the complexity of nanomaterial design introduces uncertainties in safety evaluation and long-term immunogenicity, which may delay clinical approval. Furthermore, while parenteral nano-vaccines can induce strong systemic immunity, their ability to consistently elicit protective mucosal IgA responses remains limited, representing a critical gap for enteric pathogens such as rotavirus. Importantly, the reduced efficacy of oral vaccines is increasingly linked to gut microbiome composition and environmental enteric dysfunction, factors that next-generation vaccines must overcome. Therefore, future vaccine strategies should aim to integrate immunological efficacy with practical considerations such as cost‑effectiveness, stability, and accessibility to ensure global impact. On the other hand, future research should focus on optimizing these nanomaterial platforms, selecting the most potent rotavirus antigens, and conducting rigorous preclinical and clinical trials to ensure their safety and efficacy.
