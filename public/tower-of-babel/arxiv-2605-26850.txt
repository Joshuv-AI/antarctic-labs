Learning Energy-Based Models from Stochastic Interpolants using Spatiotemporal Differences
Hanlin Yu, RuiKang OuYang, Partha Kaushik, Arto Klami, Michael U. Gutmann, Omar Chehab, [0.6em] University of Helsinki, University of Cambridge

Abstract
Learning an energy-based model from data samples is a central problem in machine learning. Many recent and popular methods, such as denoising score matching for training energy-based diffusion models, use stochastic interpolants to corrupt data samples at different noise levels indexed by a time variable. This defines a joint density over both the data space and time, and most methods learn its energy through either spatial or temporal differences. We identify distinct failure modes for both of these approaches. To solve them, we propose Spatiotemporal Noise-Contrastive Estimation (stNCE), a framework for learning the energy through joint spatiotemporal differences. stNCE unifies many existing methods and leads to new training objectives. Experiments on images and molecules demonstrate performance competitive with state-of-the-art density estimation methods.



1
Introduction

A central problem in machine learning is to learn the underlying probability density of a data distribution from finite samples. Accurate density models are useful in a wide range of applications.
In computational chemistry, for example, they can be used to correct samples from a proposal distribution to the equilibrium distribution
[
Noé et al. 2019
]
. They also enable sampling from compositions of densities (e.g., products or tempered variants) which is used to combine generative models in a modular way
[
Du et al. 2023
,
Skreta et al. 2025
]
. Moreover, they formalize the alignment of an AI system with human preferences in terms of sampling from a
tilted
data density, obtained by reweighting the data distribution with a reward function that scores human preferences
[
Thornton et al. 2025
,
He et al. 2026
]
.
To support these applications efficiently, recent work has focused on learning likelihoods that can be evaluated in
one shot
, i.e., with a single neural network evaluation
[
Aggarwal et al. 2025
,
Guth et al. 2025
,
Ai et al. 2026
]
. A natural approach is to parameterize the likelihood as an Energy-based Model (EBM), where a neural network defines an unnormalized density.

Over the past two decades, a wide range of methods has been proposed for training energy-based models
[
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
,
Ceylan and Gutmann 2018
,
Hinton 2002b
,
Hyvärinen 2005
,
Rhodes et al. 2020
,
Choi et al. 2022
,
Gao et al. 2020
]
. Despite their apparent diversity, we show that they can be organized into a simple taxonomy based on whether they estimate
temporal
or
spatial
variations of the density.

Most approaches proceed in two steps. First, they define corrupted versions of the data by interpolating between data samples and samples from a reference distribution (e.g., a Gaussian)
[
Rhodes et al. 2020
,
Albergo et al. 2025
]
. This introduces a
time
variable
t
indexing corruption levels and defines a joint density
p(x,t)
over the data space and time. Second, the model is trained by estimating how this density varies, either across time or across space. This viewpoint yields our taxonomy. Temporal methods estimate variations across corruption levels. For instance, Noise-Contrastive Estimation (NCE) learns large temporal differences between data and reference distributions
[
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
]
, while subsequent work improves performance by focusing on smaller temporal differences, as in telescoping density ratio estimation and time score matching
[
Rhodes et al. 2020
,
Choi et al. 2022
,
Yu et al. 2025b
,
Chen et al. 2025b
]
. Spatial methods, in turn, estimate variations across data points. For instance, Conditional NCE
[
Ceylan and Gutmann 2018
]
learns spatial difference between nearby data points. When these points are infinitesimally close, this becomes score matching
[
Hyvärinen 2005
]
that is used widely used to train diffusion-based EBMs
[
Du et al. 2023
,
Thornton et al. 2025
]
.

While both approaches for learning energy-based models have been widely applied
[
Hyvärinen 2005
,
Song et al. 2020
,
Vincent 2011
,
Rhodes et al. 2020
,
Choi et al. 2022
,
Yu et al. 2025b
,
Chen et al. 2025b
]
, they are known to suffer from catastrophic failure modes that remain poorly understood.
For example, spatial methods such as score matching
[
Hyvärinen 2005
]
can incur large estimation errors in multi-modal settings
[
Wenliang and Kanagawa 2021
,
Koehler et al. 2023
]
. Conversely, temporal methods based on large differences, such as Noise-Contrastive Estimation, can fail when the high-density regions of the data distribution (its “support”) do not overlap with those of the reference distribution.
However, these failure modes are rarely stated explicitly or analyzed in a unified manner in the literature. Often the analysis is limited to behavior of a specific method, instead of the broader approach.

A widely held view is that these challenges can be alleviated by learning across multiple corruption levels (or times). The intuition is that some levels are inherently easier than others, and that
amortization
, through parameter sharing across levels, allows harder tasks to benefit from the easier ones. Empirically, this strategy has led to improvements in both temporal and spatial methods.
On the temporal side, multi-level NCE
[
Rhodes et al. 2020
]
has been shown to improve sample efficiency, with some theoretical support
[
Rhodes et al. 2020
,
Chehab et al. 2023
]
. On the spatial side, multi-level denoising score matching for energy-based diffusion models
[
Thornton et al. 2025
]
enjoys emerging theoretical justification
[
Qin and Risteski 2024
,
Chewi et al. 2025
]
.
Yet, this perspective is not fully satisfactory. Recent work indicates that such approaches do not fundamentally resolve the underlying issues:
Srivastava et al. 2023
show that multi-level NCE does not eliminate support mismatch, while
Yu et al. 2025a
provides empirical evidence that multi-level score matching continues to struggle in very simple multimodal settings.

Overall, our understanding of the failure modes of existing methods remains limited. Progress in the field has largely emphasized the development of new techniques, rather than a principled analysis of the limitations they aim to address. As a result, although recent approaches report strong empirical gains
[
He et al. 2026
,
Guth et al. 2025
,
Plainer et al. 2026
,
Aggarwal et al. 2025
,
OuYang et al. 2026
]
, we still lack a clear understanding of when and why they outperform prior methods. New perspectives are needed, and we provide one by focusing specificaly on the key characteristics of the spatial and temporal differences.

Contributions

We revisit the problem of learning EBMs through the lens of spatial and temporal variations. We first introduce a unifying taxonomy that organizes existing methods along these two axes. We then explicitly identify their limitations when used in isolation: temporal methods struggle under support mismatch, while spatial methods fail in multi-modal settings,
even when learning across different times
(corruption levels), contrary to popular belief.

Motivated by these observations, we propose to jointly learn spatial and temporal variations. We formalize this task
Spatiotemporal Noise-Contrastive Estimation
(stNCE), a general framework that unifies existing approaches and solves the previous limitations. While some very recent works empirically combine spatial and temporal variations, they lack a clear conceptual justification and a unified framework
[
He et al. 2026
,
Guth et al. 2025
,
Plainer et al. 2026
,
Aggarwal et al. 2025
,
OuYang et al. 2026
]
. Our framework unifies known methods and shows, perhaps remarkably, that they are
all
instances of a
single
binary classification task. Our framework leads also to new training objectives that are competitive with the state-of-the-art across synthetic, image, and molecular benchmarks, while providing a clearer understanding of when and why they succeed.

Background

Stochastic Interpolants

Stochastic interpolants
[
Rhodes et al. 2020
,
Albergo et al. 2025
]
can be used to sample from a
joint
density
p_{d}(x,t)

over data points
x
and their corruption levels
t
(
0
is fully corrupted,
1
is clean).
To do so, we can interpolate between clean data samples
x_{1}\sim p_{1}
and samples
x_{0}\sim p_{0}
from a reference distribution
p_{0}
, which is often a standard Gaussian. A simple interpolation is
[
Lipman et al. 2023
]

\displaystyle x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1},

(1)

but other choices are possible too
[
Rhodes et al. 2020
]
.
The joint density can be modelled as
p_{\theta}(x,t)=p(t)p_{\theta}(x|t)
, where
p(t)
is a distribution over corruption levels chosen by the user, for example uniform between
0
(fully corrupt data) and
1
(clean data). The time-dependent density
p_{\theta}(x|t)
is an energy-based
model

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x,t)=p(t)\,\exp(-E_{\theta}(x, t) - \log Z_t),

(2)

where
E_{\theta}(x,t)
is typically a neural network and
Z_{t}=\int\exp(-E_{\theta}(x,t))\,dx
is the normalizing constant, which is generally intractable. The goal is to learn a parameter
\theta
such that the model of the clean data density
p_{\theta}(x,1)
is accurate. A popular way to estimate
\theta
without having to compute the intractable
Z
is to model variations of the joint density.

Binary classification for learning variations

A seminal approach for learning the difference between two log densities
\log p_{A}(x)
and
\log p_{B}(x)
is to solve a binary classification task. This is core to many algorithms
[
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
,
Ceylan and Gutmann 2018
,
Goodfellow et al. 2020
]
, including the ones in this paper. Consider data from two classes
p_{A}(x)
and
p_{B}(x)
and a binary classification task minimizing the logistic loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{logistic}}(F)=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{A}}\left[\log\mathrm{sigmoid}(F(x))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{B}}\left[\log(1 - \mathrm{sigmoid}(F(x)))\right].

(3)

The optimal classifier is the
difference
between log densities
F(x)=\log p_{A}(x)-\log p_{B}(x)

[
Sugiyama et al. 2012
]
.

Learning the energy by temporal differences.

One class of methods learns the energy by estimating
temporal
differences
E(x,t^{\prime})-E(x,t)+\log Z(t^{\prime})-\log Z(t)
.
A canonical approach is Noise-Contrastive Estimation (NCE)
[
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
]
, using a binary classification task as above. First, generate data
x
from two classes
A
and
B
with equal probability,

\displaystyle p_{A}(x)=p_{d}(x|t),\quad p_{B}(x)=p_{d}(x|t^{\prime}).

(4)

For example, generating data from class
A
is done by corrupting a clean data sample
x_{1}
with level
t
following Eq.
1
. Then, minimizing the logistic loss yields the optimal classifier

\displaystyle F(x,t,t^{\prime})=E(x,t)-E(x,t^{\prime})+\log Z(t)-\log Z(t^{\prime}),

(5)

that equals the temporal energy difference. In practice, we jointly parameterize
E_{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\log Z(\cdot)
using a single neural network and learn them.
In the limit where
t
and
t^{\prime}
are infinitesimally close, that energy difference approaches the
time score

\partial_{t}\log p_{t}(x)

[
Choi et al. 2022
,
Yu et al. 2025b
]
.
Rhodes et al. 2020
observed that the performances become stronger when
t
and
t^{\prime}
are sampled between
0
and
1
and close to each other; this was later generalized to continuous time
[
Aggarwal et al. 2025
,
OuYang et al. 2026
]
, where one define a specific
p(t)
and sample
t^{\prime}\sim p(t^{\prime}|t)
, where
p(t^{\prime}|t)
can be e.g. close to
\mathcal{N}(t^{\prime};t,\sigma^{2})
for some small
\sigma
. We provide further details on
p(t)
and
p(t^{\prime}|t)
in Appendix
C.1
. We refer to this as temporal NCE (tNCE).

Learning the energy by spatial differences.

Another class of methods learns the energy by estimating
spatial
differences
E(x^{\prime},t)-E(x,t)+\log Z(t)-\log Z(t)
.
A fundamental example is Conditional Noise-Contrastive Estimation (CNCE)
[
Ceylan and Gutmann 2018
]
, which also solves a binary classification task. First, generate data
(x,x^{\prime})
from two classes A and B

\displaystyle p_{A}(x,x^{\prime})=p_{d}(x|t)p_{n}(x^{\prime}\mid x),\quad p_{B}(x,x^{\prime})=p_{d}(x^{\prime}|t)p_{n}(x^{\prime}\mid x)

(6)

To generate data from class
A
,
first generate a data point
x
with corruption level
t
following Eq.
1
, and then perturb it into
x^{\prime}
, using a perturbation kernel
p_{n}
chosen by the user. Switching the positions of
x
and
x^{\prime}
yields data from class
B
. Then, minimizing the logistic loss yields the optimal classifier

\displaystyle F(x,x^{\prime},t)=-E(x^{\prime},t)+E(x,t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}\mid x)-\log p_{n}(x\mid x^{\prime}).

(7)

It equals the spatial energy difference up to the known transition densities.
In practice, we parameterize
E(\cdot,\cdot)
using a neural network and learn it. In the limit where
x
and
x^{\prime}
are infinitesimally close, that energy difference approaches the
space score

\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x)

[
Hyvärinen 2005
,
Ceylan and Gutmann 2018
]
. A natural continuous time extension of CNCE is to sample
t
between
0
and
1
and form the loss as an expectation over
p(t)
; we refer to this algorithm as temporal CNCE (tCNCE).

3
Limitations of current methods

Figure 2
:

Ability to compute log-likelihood differences between two data points using learned spatial (orange), temporal (green), or spatiotemporal (red) differences. Methods based purely on spatial or temporal differences
fail when traversing low-density regions
(white), where these differences are poorly estimated. In contrast, spatiotemporal methods remain within high-density regions (blue), enabling accurate estimation.

Despite their widespread success, current methods can suffer from failure modes. It is well known that methods based on both spatial differences
[
Ceylan 2017
,
Vincent 2011
]
and temporal differences
[
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
]
can fail even in simple settings. A common belief is that these issues can be mitigated by
learning across multiple corruption levels
, whether through temporal schemes
[
Rhodes et al. 2020
,
Choi et al. 2022
,
Yu et al. 2025b
,
Chen et al. 2025b
]
or spatial ones
[
Du et al. 2023
,
Thornton et al. 2025
,
Song et al. 2021b
]
. In this section, we argue—and empirically demonstrate—that this is
not
the case: such approaches still fail in the same simple regimes.

A unifying heuristic.

To analyze these failures, we study the ability of a model to relate the log-density of two points. For any two points,
(x,t)
and
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, we decompose the difference into spatial and/or temporal differences that need to be learnt by the model. This viewpoint captures both local properties (e.g., mode geometry for nearby points) and global properties (e.g., relative mode weights for distant points). Crucially, we will show that purely spatial or temporal decompositions inevitably require evaluating
p(x,t)
in low-density regions, where samples are scarce and estimation is therefore worse. Figure
1
empirically validates these claims to hold even for a bivariate density.

Spatial differences struggle with multimodality.

Spatial methods learn
p_{\theta}(x,t)
via differences in
x
. Using our heuristic, we decompose the log-density difference between
x
and
y
as

\displaystyle\log p(x,1)-\log p(y,1)=\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\!\log p(x_{i+1},1)-\log p(x_{i},1).

(8)

When
p_{1}
is multimodal (Figure
2
, two right panels), these spatial differences in orange necessarily traverse low-density regions (white). Since such regions are poorly sampled, the corresponding spatial differences are poorly estimated. As a result, spatial methods—including energy-based diffusion models—struggle to capture global structure such as mode weights
[
Yu et al. 2025a
]
.

Temporal differences suffer from support mismatch.

Temporal methods learn
p_{\theta}(x,t)
via differences in
t
. A simple algorithm, which we term tNCE, works by sampling
t
and
t^{\prime}
, and constructing binary classification problems; this was explored in
[
Aggarwal et al. 2025
,
OuYang et al. 2026
]
. Again, using our heuristic, we decompose the log-density difference between
x
and
y
as

\displaystyle\log p(x,1)-\log p(y,1)=\log p(x,0)-\log p(y,0)+\Delta,

(9)

where
p(\cdot,0)
is a known reference distribution, and

\displaystyle\Delta=\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\!\bigl(\log p(x,t_{i+1})-\log p(x,t_{i})\bigr)-\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\!\bigl(\log p(y,t_{i+1})-\log p(y,t_{i})\bigr).

(10)

When the supports of
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
differ (Figure
2
, second and fourth panels), these temporal differences in green traverse low-density regions in white. As before, this degrades estimation.

Spatiotemporal differences avoid both issues.

We now consider a joint discretization in space and time. Let
\{(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=0}^{K}
with
x_{i},y_{i}\sim p_{t_{i}}
. Then

\log p(x,1)-\log p(y,1)=\log p(x_{0},0)-\log p(y_{0},0)+\Delta,

(11)

where

\Delta=\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\!\bigl(\log p(x_{i+1},t_{i+1})-\log p(x_{i},t_{i})\bigr)-\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\!\bigl(\log p(y_{i+1},t_{i+1})-\log p(y_{i},t_{i})\bigr).

As illustrated in Figure
2
, these spatiotemporal differences in red can be chosen to remain within high-density regions in blue. Since such regions are well sampled, the corresponding spatiotemporal differences are accurately estimated.

This observation may explain the success of recent spatiotemporal methods
[
Guth et al. 2025
,
He et al. 2026
,
Aggarwal et al. 2025
,
OuYang et al. 2026
,
Plainer et al. 2026
]
, as they simultaneously address multimodality and support mismatch. We empirically validate this conjecture in Figure
1
. This motivates the following unifying framework for spatiotemporal methods.

Learning the energy by spatiotemporal differences

In this section, we introduce a framework for estimating the parameters of an energy-based model by learning its variation jointly in data space and time. Our approach reduces to a
single
binary classification problem that unifies many popular methods
[
Hyvärinen 2005
,
Vincent 2011
,
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
,
Ceylan and Gutmann 2018
,
Rhodes et al. 2020
,
Choi et al. 2022
,
Yu et al. 2025b
,
Chen et al. 2025b
,
Guth et al. 2025
,
Aggarwal et al. 2025
,
OuYang et al. 2026
]
; see Table
7
.

Spatiotemporal Noise-Contrastive Estimation (stNCE)

We propose to learn the energy by estimating its
spatiotemporal
differences,
E(x’,t’)-E(x,t)
, by solving a binary classification task. We call this approach Spatiotemporal Noise-Contrastive Estimation (stNCE). First, generate data
(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
from two classes
A
and
B

\displaystyle p_{A}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t),\quad p_{B}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t).

(12)

To generate data from class
A
, first sample a data point and its corruption level
(x,t)
following Eq.
1
, and then perturb them into
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
using a perturbation kernel
p_{n}
that is chosen by the user. Switching the positions of
(x,t)
and
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
in the tuple yields data from class
B
.
Efficient strategies for sampling
(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
are discussed in Appendix
C.1
.
Then, minimizing the logistic loss yields the optimal classifier

F(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=E(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\!-\!E(x,t)+\!\log Z(t^{\prime})\!-\!\log Z(t)+\!\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)\!-\!\log p_{n}(x,t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

(13)

which equals the spatiotemporal energy difference up to known terms. In practice, we parameterize
E(x,t)
and
\log Z(t)
using neural networks and learn them jointly. Note that this can be viewed as the CNCE from Section
2
but on the augmented space
(x,t)
, which we use to establish Theorem
2
.

Statistical guarantees.

We next present statistical guarantees on the stNCE estimator: it is consistent and we can quantify its sample-efficiency.

Theorem 1

(Non-parametric consistency)

Let
p_{\theta}(x,t)=\exp(-E_\theta(x,t))/Z_{\theta}(t)
be a parametric model, and suppose the data is generated with parameter
\theta^{*}
. Then the stNCE estimator
\hat{\theta}
, obtained by minimizing the empirical logistic loss with
N
samples, leads to a pointwise consistent estimator:

\displaystyle p_{\hat{\theta}}\to p_{\theta^{*}}\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

(14)

We prove this in Appendix
A.2
, and next characterize the estimator’s asymptotic sample efficiency.

Theorem 2

(Sample efficiency)

(Based on
Yu et al. 2025a
)
Let

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\log p_{\theta}(x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)-\log p_{n}(x,t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(15)

Then the estimator satisfies

\|\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{d}}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl(C_{1}^{-1}C_{2}C_{1}^{-1}\bigr)+o\!\left(\frac{1}{N_{d}}\right),

(16)

with the following, where expectations are taken under
p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)
:

\displaystyle C_{1}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}F_{\theta}\nabla_{\theta}F_{\theta}^{\top}\,\sigma(-F_{\theta^{*}})\,\sigma(F_{\theta^{*}})\right],\quad C_{2}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}F_{\theta}\nabla_{\theta}F_{\theta}^{\top}\,\sigma(-F_{\theta^{*}})^{2}\right].

Design choices.

The main design choice is the
perturbation kernel

p_{n}
. While our stNCE estimator is consistent for any choice of
p_{n}
, its sample efficiency depends critically on this choice. As shown in Table
7
, specific choices recover many existing methods based on temporal differences
[
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
,
Rhodes et al. 2020
,
Choi et al. 2022
,
Yu et al. 2025b
]
, spatial differences
[
Ceylan and Gutmann 2018
,
Hyvärinen 2005
,
Song et al. 2020
]
, and spatiotemporal differences
[
Guth et al. 2025
,
Aggarwal et al. 2025
,
He et al. 2026
,
OuYang et al. 2026
]
.

In principle, one could optimize the asymptotic variance expression with respect to
p_{n}
. As is often the case, this is analytically difficult. Instead, we adopt an empirical approach and propose choices of
p_{n}
that are heuristically well suited to the problem.

5
Choices of perturbation kernels

In this section, we propose three choices for the perturbation kernels, called
mixture
,
white
, and
forward-reverse
. We argue that the first two are sample-efficient when the perturbation is “small enough”, while the last is sample-efficient when a certain quantity called the “space score” is well-approximated.
In both cases, the guiding principle is that the pairs
(x,t)
and
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
used to estimate energy difference should remain in the manifold of the target distribution
p_{d}(x,t)
that we wish to estimate; because one of them is sampled from
p_{d}(x,t)
and is therefore likely to be on the data manifold, the perturbation kernel
p_{n}
used to obtain the second should avoid mapping samples
outside
of the data manifold, see Figure
3
.
We explain that certain recent methods in the literature
[
Guth et al. 2025
,
He et al. 2026
]
can be viewed as special cases or variants on these perturbation kernels.

Figure 3
:
Choice of kernels. Left: departs from the data manifold (blue). Right: remains on the data manifold (blue).

Mixture kernel.

This kernel perturbs
either

x
or
t
:

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,p_{n}(t^{\prime}\mid t)\,\delta_{x}(x^{\prime})+\tfrac{1}{2}\,p_{n}(x^{\prime}\mid x)\,\delta_{t}(t^{\prime}).

(17)

We refer to this variant as stNCE-m. It recovers a mixture of tNCE and tCNCE objectives, as we show in Appendix
B
.

White noise kernel.

This kernel perturbs both
x
and
t
, with the perturbation in terms of
x
being isotropic Gaussian noise:

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)=\mathcal{N}(x^{\prime}\mid x,\sigma^{2}I)\,p(t^{\prime}|t).

(18)

We refer to the resulting algorithm as stNCE-w. The intuition is that sufficiently small perturbations keep
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
in the data manifold of
p_{d}
. In the limit
\sigma\to 0
, this recovers Dual Score Matching
[
Guth et al. 2025
]
, as we prove in Appendix
A.4
.

The two previous methods suffer from the same pathology: under the scenario where the perturbation is non-vanishing,
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
could naturally be generated off the high density region induced by
p_{d}(x,t)
. The manifold hypothesis states that real world data often lies on a low dimensional Riemannian manifold, such that adding isotropic Gaussian noises often results in off-manifold samples. Furthermore, counter-intuitively, samples from Gaussian distribution concentrate near a sphere in high dimensions, resulting in non-smooth landscape even for noise-perturbed data. The same issues will affect tCNCE when used with a white noise kernel.

In contrast to the kernels above, the following kernel aims to stay within the manifold of
p_{d}(x,t)
.

Forward-reverse kernel.

We propose perturbing
(x,t)
along trajectories consistent with
p_{d}(x,t)
:

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)=p_{n}(x^{\prime}\mid x,t,t^{\prime})\,p_{n}(t^{\prime}|x).

(19)

We first perturb
t
to
t^{\prime}
, then apply a kernel on
x
: a
noising
step if
t^{\prime}<t
, and a
denoising
step if
t^{\prime}>t
. These kernels are Bayes inverses, as in diffusion-based methods
[
Gao et al. 2021
,
Doucet et al. 2022
,
He et al. 2026
]
.

An explicit construction based on the stochastic interpolant Eq.
1
is:

\hskip-10.00002ptp_{n}(x^{\prime}\mid x,t,t^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}\!\left(x^{\prime}\middle|x/t,\;1-(1-t)^{2}/t^{2}\right),&t^{\prime}<t,\\[3.00003pt]
\mathcal{N}\!\left(x^{\prime}\middle|(t^{\prime}/t)x+\tfrac{2(1-t)(t^{\prime}-t)}{t}\,\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x),\;\tfrac{(t^{\prime}-t)(t+t^{\prime}-2tt^{\prime})}{t^{2}}I\right),&t^{\prime}>t.\end{cases}

(20)

The noising kernel (top) is tractable, while the denoising kernel, obtained using the exponential integrator formula
[
Gonzalez et al. 2023
]
, depends on the space score
\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x)
, which is typically unknown and is the key quantity that diffusion models approximate
[
Song et al. 2021b
]
. We approximate it using either:
(i)
oracle
scores (when available), denoted as stNCE-o, or
(ii)
self
scores, obtained from the current model, denoted as stNCE-s.
The latter is reminiscent of consistency models and flow maps
[
Song et al. 2023
,
Boffi et al. 2026
]
.We further note that the recent RNE regularization
[
He et al. 2026
]
proposed to regularize energy-based diffusion models is related too, with the following differences: it uses a squared loss instead of the logistic loss, and sampling is performed directly using the forward process without relying on the scores.
However, unlike stNCE, which remains consistent for
arbitrary
kernels, e.g. the ones induced by imperfect scores, and can be optimized independently, RNE depends heavily on the quality of the current score estimates: RNE loss based on inaccurate scores fail to remain consistent. Consequently, RNE mandates joint training with denoising score matching loss or an oracle access to the ground truth socre, whereas stNCE could function as a standalone training objective.

Infinitesimal limit.

We now characterize the behavior of stNCE for certain
infinitesimal
perturbation kernels. Specifically, we study perturbation kernels that are transition probabilities along a trajectory of a Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) as in Eq.
20
or of a Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE), assuming oracle access to the ground truth scores.
We prove the following results in Appendix
A.3
.

Proposition 3

(Transition probability of an SDE)

Consider a stochastic interpolant as in Eq.
1
whose marginal laws
p_{t}
are simulated by the time-reversal of an SDE,
\differential X_{t}=b(X_{t},t)\differential t+g(t)\differential W_{t}
. Define the perturbation kernel to be the transition probability of that SDE. In the limit
\Delta t\to 0
, stNCE recovers the space score matching loss with maximum-likelihood weighting
[
Song et al. 2021a
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(\theta)=2\log 2+\frac{\Delta t}{4}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}\!\left[g(t)^{2}\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}^{\theta}(x)\|^{2}\right]+o(\Delta t).

(21)

Proposition 4

(Transition probability of an ODE)

Consider a stochastic interpolant as in Eq.
1
whose marginal laws
p_{t}
are simulated by an ODE,
\differential X_{t}=v(X_{t})\differential t
.
Define the perturbation kernel to be the transition probability of that ODE. Under this scenario, the transition kernels are Dirac deltas subject to Jacobian scalings.
In the limit
\Delta t\to 0
, stNCE recovers a loss for matching the trajectory-wise rate of change of the true and model log densities:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(\theta)=2\log 2+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{4}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}\!\left[\|\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x)-\differential_{t}\log p_{t}^{\theta}(x)\|^{2}\right]+o(\Delta t^{2}).

(22)

where
\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x):=\partial_{t}\log p_{t}(x)+\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x)\cdot v_{t}(x)
.

6
Experiments

We empirically evaluate our framework for learning densities
of synthetic data, images and molecules. In particular, we report results of the different variants of our Spatiotemporal Noise-Contrastive Estimation (stNCE-m, stNCE-w, stNCE-s, stNCE-o) that are detailed in Section
5
. We also use another variant stNCE-s
+
DSM which combines stNCE-s with a space score matching loss: the former estimates spatiotemporal differences while the latter estimates infinitesimal spatial differences. See Appendix
C.4
for further details. Across datasets, our stNCE-s and variants are most promising: they are competitive with the state of the art.

6.1
Failure modes

Many methods for learning energy-based models, including denoising score matching used in diffusion models
[
Thornton et al. 2025
,
Du and Mordatch 2019
]
, are typically evaluated on complex datasets. Yet, Figure
1
shows that they can already fail in a simple one-dimensional setting. This experiment highlights two key properties of the data distribution that drive these failures. First, multimodality increases the error of methods based on learning spatial differences. Second, a mismatch between the high-density regions (“supports”) of the data and reference distributions increases the error of methods based on learning temporal differences. Methods based on learning spatiotemporal difference are robust to both sources of error. Details on the simulations are provided in Appendix
D.1
.

6.2
Gaussian mixtures

Setup and dataset

Image data are high-dimensional and sparse. To emulate these properties, we build a Gaussian mixture from the MNIST dataset
[
LeCun et al. 2010
]
by sampling
100
images (
10
per digit class) and using them as centroids with diagonal covariance
0.01I
.
This synthetic yet realistic distribution exhibits the two pathologies identified in Section
3
: strong multimodality and minimal overlap with a standard Gaussian reference distribution due to its thin support. We therefore expect purely spatial and purely temporal methods to struggle, while spatio-temporal methods with density-aware kernels should perform well, which is confirmed in Table
1
.

Evaluation

We use the following metrics to evaluate if model and ground-truth density agree. They are computed as averages over data samples and to the model density and also the data density (which is usually unavailable, but we know it for toy experiments). When we have access to both the ground truth data density and the sampling mechanism that generates samples from it, we can estimate the log constant of the model density at the time corresponding to clean data using importance sampling, denoted as
\widehat{\log Z_{1}}
, and evaluate the model density normalized using
\widehat{\log Z_{1}}
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{d}(\cdot\mid 1)}\!\left[\bigl(\log p_{d}(x\mid 1)-\log p_{\theta}(x\mid 1)\bigr)^{2}\right],

(23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ratio}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{x,x^{\prime}\sim p_{d}(\cdot\mid 1)^{\otimes 2}}\!\left[\Bigl(\log p_{d}(x\mid 1)-\log p_{d}(x^{\prime}\mid 1)-\log p_{\theta}(x\mid 1)+\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid 1)\Bigr)^{2}\right],

(24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{NormMSE}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{d}(\cdot\mid 1)}\!\left[\bigl(\log p_{d}(x\mid 1)-\log p_{\theta}(x\mid 1)+\widehat{\log Z_{1}}\bigr)^{2}\right],

(25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{NormNLL}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{d}(\cdot\mid 1)}\!\left[-\log p_{\theta}(x\mid 1)+\widehat{\log Z_{1}}\right].

(26)

The first is the squared error between the data and model log densities. The second is the squared error between
spatial
differences of the data and model log densities — this metric is well-suited for evaluating unnormalized densities given that it does not depend on normalizing constants. The third and the fourth evaluate how good the model is as an unnormalized EBM, though naturally depends on the accuracy of the estimated
\widehat{\log Z_{1}}
.

Results

Among spatiotemporal methods, we study two perturbation kernels: white noise and the forward–reverse kernel from Section
5
. Results with white noise match the intuition of Figure
3
: finite Gaussian perturbations (stNCE-w) perform poorly because they push samples off the data manifold, whereas infinitesimal perturbations (Dual SM
[
Guth et al. 2025
]
) preserve proximity to the manifold and yield accurate estimates, albeit at higher computational cost due to double differentiation through the network.
Results for the forward–reverse kernel further support this interpretation. Even with finite corruption, stNCE-s remains competitive because it stays close to the data manifold. By contrast, as discussed in Section
3
, RNE
[
He et al. 2026
]
is consistent only when given access to the true scores
\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}(x_{t})
(RNE-o), which are unavailable in practice; using estimated scores (RNE-s) can lead to substantial degradation. Our implementation of stNCE-m naively adds up tNCE loss and tCNCE loss, leading to slower running time, as indicated using
∗
; this also applies to later experiments.

Overall, stNCE-s provides a favorable trade-off between accuracy and speed, and is the only NCE-based method that successfully learns this challenging Gaussian mixture.

6.4
Molecules

Setup and datasets

We wish to model a target distribution (
a.k.a.
equilibrium distribution) over configurations of a molecule. Concretely, each data point in that space is the collection of three-dimensional positions of the atoms, or rather groups of atoms called chains, that make up the molecule. In our experiments, we consider two “coarse-grained” molecular systems: Alanine dipeptide (ALDP) and Chignolin. Specifically, the ALDP is represented by
5
chains and Chignolin by
10
chains; because each chain has three-dimensional spatial coordinates, this leads to
15
-dimensional and
30
-dimensional data, respectively.
Data points in “high probability” regions correspond to likely spatial configurations of the atoms.
In practice, we have access energy and score function (
a.k.a.
force field) of the target distribution via physics, but they are expensive to evaluate as they require solving a PDE. To avoid evaluating them for every new data point, there are two prevailing approaches in the literature.

The first approach is Machine Learning Force Fields (MLFFs), which regress a neural network against the energy and score functions on some finite grid of data points
[
Husic et al. 2020
,
Batatia et al. 2023
]
.
The second approach which we follow here is Energy-Based Models (EBMs) that learn a neural network that matches the energy of the target distribution. Such models are trained on a dataset of samples that approximately follow the target distribution. To form such a dataset, a sampling process such as Langevin Dynamics (
a.k.a.
Molecular Dynamics), which requires evaluating the target energy and score functions, is run long enough so that the final samples are approximately
i.i.d.
samples from the target distribution. In practice, we use the datasets provided
Plainer et al. 2026
and
Lindorff-Larsen et al. 2011
, following the same architecture and parameterization as
Plainer et al. 2026
.

Methods

We compare stNCE to another spatiotemporal method from
Plainer et al. 2026
based on the Fokker-Planck Equation (FPE).
That method consists in learning
spatial
differences of the energy using a loss we detail in Appendix
B.11
, and
temporal
differences of the energy by minimizing a PINN loss that effectively forces the energy to follow a certain Partial Differential Equation.
Although technically sound, FPE is computationally expensive because it requires optimizing through the second-order derivative of the energy network. Even with the estimator proposed by
Plainer et al. 2026
to bypass explicit second-order derivatives, the FPE approach remains costly due to the multiple additional network evaluations required per loss calculation. In contrast, stNCE is fundamentally more efficient to optimize.

Evaluation

To evaluate the quality of the estimated model of the data
p_{\theta}(x|1)
, we use the following metrics from
Plainer et al. 2026
. The Potential Mean Field (PMF) is just the mean-squared error between the model energy and true energy, comptued over data samples. The Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) is a statistical divergence between the ground truth and model densities.

\displaystyle\mathrm{PMF}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{d}(\cdot|1)}\big[\big(E(x,1)-E_{\theta}(x,1)\big)^{2}\big]

(28)

\displaystyle\mathrm{JSD}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\log 2+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{d}(\cdot\mid 1)}\bigg[\log\frac{p_{d}(x\mid 1)}{p_{d}(x\mid 1)+p_{\theta}(x\mid 1)}\bigg]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid 1)}\bigg[\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x\mid 1)}{p_{d}(x\mid 1)+p_{\theta}(x\mid 1)}\bigg].

(29)

In practice, these metrics are not computed over the full data space but on a physically meaningful
2
-dimensional subspace: the torsional angles for ALDP, and the principle directions of multi-modality obtained by Time-lagged Independent Component Analysis (TICA)
[
Molgedey and Schuster 1994
,
Hyvärinen et al. 2004
]
for Chignolin.

To compute these metrics, we proceed as follows.
First, we start with samples on the full data samples: ground-truth samples
x\sim p_{d}(x|1)
are from the the dataset and the samples from our model
x\sim p_{\theta}(x|1)
are obtained by running two score-based samplers, the Langevin sampler which uses the final score
\nabla\log p_{\theta}(x|1)

[
Eastman et al. 2024
]
and the diffusion sampler which uses the intermediate scores
\nabla\log p_{\theta}(x|t)

[
Song et al. 2021b
]
.
Second, we project the ground-truth and model samples onto the
2
-dimensional subspace.
Third, we compute histogram-based density estimates of the corresponding
2
-dimensional ground-truth and model distributions.
Finally, we compute an empirical version of the above metrics, where the expectations are replaced by a finite-sample average.

7
Discussion

Compute burden of the likelihood: training-time
versus
inference-time

This work highlights how the literature of learning energy-based models falls within three families of learning objectives: temporal, spatial, and spatiotemporal. Another axis for organizing the literature is two parameterizations of the energy-based model differences, both enabling likelihood estimation but differing primarily in when the computational burden is incurred.

A first class defers this cost to inference time by integrating learned quantities, including temporal methods based on
\partial_{t}\log p_{t}(x)

[
Choi et al. 2022
,
Yu et al. 2025b
,
Chen et al. 2026
]
, spatial methods based on
\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
, and spatio-temporal approaches integrating
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log p_{t}(x_{t})
, such as continuous normalizing flows
[
Chen et al. 2019
]
and path-wise formulations
[
Choi et al. 2022
]
. These methods are relatively easy to train, as they avoid higher-order differentiation, but incur significant inference-time cost due to numerical integration, although recent works look into
learn
to integrate in few steps
[
Boffi et al. 2026
,
Ai et al. 2026
,
Chen et al. 2025a
]
.

A second class instead parameterizes these differences directly with an energy-based model, enabling one-step likelihood evaluation. This includes temporal methods such as NCE
[
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
]
, spatial methods such as (denoising) score matching
[
Hyvärinen 2005
,
Vincent 2011
]
, and recent spatio-temporal approaches, including ours
[
He et al. 2026
,
Guth et al. 2025
,
Plainer et al. 2026
,
Aggarwal et al. 2025
,
OuYang et al. 2026
]
. These methods yield cheap inference—often a single network evaluation—but are harder to train, e.g., due to higher-order autodiff.

Overall, these approaches trade training-time for inference-time compute. We focus on the latter regime, favoring expensive training for efficient inference. A systematic study of this trade-off is left for future work.

Limitations and future directions

While models trained using NCE loss functions can recover the ground truth energies accurately, the scores may not be learned well when comparing to the score matching variants. Future work could explore better samplers for models trained with NCE losses that either do not rely on gradient information or could better handle noisy gradients.

In conclusion, we show that despite their diversity, most energy-based model estimation methods fall into three categories: spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal. We identify fundamental failure modes of purely spatial and temporal approaches, and show how spatiotemporal methods overcome them. Our unified binary classification framework recovers many existing methods and yields new objectives with near state-of-the-art performance across synthetic, image, and molecular data.

Appendix A
Theoretical results

In this section, we prove the consistency of the estimator obtained by minimizing the empirical stNCE loss.
We also derive the simplified expressions of stNCE loss under specific perturbation kernels.

A.1
Useful lemma on the logistic loss

The following are useful lemma on how to rewrite the logistic classification loss.

Lemma 5

(Logistic loss as a Bregman divergence)

Consider the logistic loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{logistic}}(F)=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{A}}\left[\log\sigma(F(x))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{B}}\left[\log(1 - \sigma(F(x)))\right],

(30)

which is minimized by

F^{*}(x)=\log p_{A}(x)-\log p_{B}(x).

Then, up to an additive constant independent of
F
, the logistic loss can be written as a Bregman divergence between
e^{F^{*}}
and
e^{F}
.

Proof.

This is inspired by
Gutmann and Hirayama 2011
,
Uehara et al. 2018
.

Let
\Phi(u)=u\log u-(1+u)\log(1+u)
. Consider the pointwise Bregman divergence

\displaystyle D_{\Phi}\big(e^{F^{*}(x)},e^{F(x)}\big)=\Phi\big(e^{F^{*}(x)}\big)-\Phi\big(e^{F(x)}\big)-\Phi^{\prime}\big(e^{F(x)}\big)\big(e^{F^{*}(x)}-e^{F(x)}\big).

(31)

Integrating against
p_{B}(x)\,dx
yields a divergence between functions:

\displaystyle D_{\Phi}\big(e^{F^{*}},e^{F}\big)=\int D_{\Phi}\big(e^{F^{*}(x)},e^{F(x)}\big)\,p_{B}(x)\,dx.

(32)

Substituting
F^{*}(x)=\log p_{A}(x)-\log p_{B}(x)
, a straightforward calculation shows

\displaystyle D_{\Phi}\big(e^{F^{*}},e^{F}\big)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{logistic}}(F)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{logistic}}(F^{*}),

(33)

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 6

(Logistic loss simplified when the model is near zero)

Consider the logistic loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle:=-\mathbb{E}_{p^{+}(x)}\bigl[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x))\bigr]-\mathbb{E}_{p^{-}(x)}\bigl[\log\bigl(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x))\bigr)\bigr].

(34)

If
F_{\theta}(x)
is close to zero, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(0)-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p^{+}(x)-p^{-}(x)}\left[F_{\theta}(x)\right]+\frac{1}{8}\mathbb{E}_{p^{+}(x)+p^{-}(x)}\left[F_{\theta}(x)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{p^{+}(x)+p^{-}(x)}\left[o(F_{\theta}(x)^{2})\right].

(35)

Proof.

For fixed
x
, define the pointwise loss

\displaystyle\ell(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle:=-p^{+}\log\sigma(F_{\theta})-p^{-}\log\bigl(1-\sigma(F_{\theta})\bigr).

(36)

A second-order Taylor expansion around
F_{\theta}=0
gives

\displaystyle\ell(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\ell(0)-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(p^{+}-p^{-}\bigr)F_{\theta}+\frac{1}{8}\bigl(p^{+}+p^{-}\bigr)F_{\theta}^{2}+\bigl(p^{+}+p^{-}\bigr)o(F_{\theta}^{2}).

(37)

Integrating over
x
yields the result.
∎

A.2
Non-parametric consistency (Theorem
1
)

Recall that the stNCE population loss is the logistic classification objective

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F)=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{A}}\big[\log\sigma(F(x))\big]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{B}}\big[\log(1 - \sigma(F(x)))\big].

(38)

By Lemma
5
, this can be written (up to an additive constant) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F)=D_{\Phi}\big(e^{F^{*}},e^{F}\big)+\mathrm{const}.

(39)

Hence any minimizer
F^{\theta_{\min}}
satisfies

F^{\theta_{\min}}=F^{*}.

Using the definition of the stNCE classifier Eq.
13
, this yields, for all
(x,t)
and
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
in the support,

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta_{\min}}(x\mid t)-\log p_{\theta_{\min}}(x^{\prime}\mid t^{\prime})=\log p_{d}(x\mid t)-\log p_{d}(x^{\prime}\mid t^{\prime}).

(40)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta_{\min}}(x\mid t)=\log p_{d}(x\mid t)+C,

(41)

where
C
does not depend on
(x,t)
. Enforcing the constraint
p_{\theta}(x\mid t=0)=p_{0}(x)
fixes
C
, yielding

\displaystyle p_{\theta_{\min}}(x\mid t)=p_{d}(x\mid t).

(42)

This establishes non-parametric consistency.
If the model is identifiable, we further obtain parametric Fisher consistency, i.e.,

\theta_{\min}=\theta^{*}.

A.3
Forward-reverse kernel: Infinitesimal Limit (Propositions
3
and Proposition
4
)

Simplifying the loss around zero

Let

\displaystyle p_{+}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t),

(43)

\displaystyle p_{-}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(44)

The logistic loss used in stNCE is

\mathcal{L}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{-}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}[\log(1 - \sigma(F_{\theta}(x, t, x', t')))].

(45)

Using Lemma
6
, we have, whenever

F_{\theta}
is close to zero,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(0)-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}-p_{-}}\left[F_{\theta}\right]+\frac{1}{8}\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}+p_{-}}\left[F_{\theta}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}+p_{-}}\left[o(F_{\theta}^{2})\right].

(46)

In contrast with the expansion around the optimum, this expansion generally
contains a linear term. However, this term vanishes in the forward-reverse
case considered below.

Plugging in the forward-reverse kernel

Recall that the forward-reverse kernel satisfies

\displaystyle p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)=p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

(47)

due to the Bayes rule. Equivalently,

\displaystyle p_{+}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=p_{-}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(48)

Therefore, the linear term vanishes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}-p_{-}}\left[F_{\theta}\right]=0.

(49)

Moreover, by taking logs in the forward-reverse identity,

\displaystyle\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)-\log p_{n}(x,t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\log p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-\log p_{d}(x,t).

(50)

Hence

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(51)

\displaystyle\quad+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)-\log p_{n}(x,t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(52)

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})+\log p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-\log p_{d}(x,t)

(53)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\log p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\bigr)-\bigl(\log p_{d}(x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\bigr).

(54)

Defining

\displaystyle e_{\theta}(x,t):=\log p_{d}(x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x,t),

(55)

we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t).

(56)

Since
p_{+}=p_{-}=p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)
, the expansion becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(0)+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)}\left[\bigl(e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)\bigr)^{2}\right]

(57)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)}\left[o\!\left(\bigl(e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)\bigr)^{2}\right)\right].

(58)

This expansion is valid when
F_{\theta}
is close to zero. In the
infinitesimal-time regime, this follows from the fact that
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is close
to
(x,t)
.

Case of the SDE

Assume that
x_{t}
follows the SDE

\displaystyle\differential x_{t}=b(x_{t},t)\differential t+g(t)\differential W_{t}.

(59)

Let
t^{\prime}=t+\Delta t
. Conditional on
(x_{t},t)=(x,t)
, the next point satisfies

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=x+b(x,t)\Delta t+g(t)\Delta W+R_{t},\qquad\Delta W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta t\,I),

(60)

where

\displaystyle R_{t}=\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\bigl(b(x_{s},s)-b(x,t)\bigr)\,ds+\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\bigl(g(s)-g(t)\bigr)\,dW_{s}.

(61)

Therefore, the expectation with respect to the transition kernel

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)
may be written as an expectation with respect to the
Brownian increment
\Delta W
.

By Itô’s lemma,

\displaystyle e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}e_{\theta}(x,t)\Delta t+\nabla_{x}e_{\theta}(x,t)^{\top}b(x,t)\Delta t

(62)

\displaystyle\quad+g(t)\nabla_{x}e_{\theta}(x,t)^{\top}\Delta W+\frac{1}{2}g(t)^{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\nabla_{x}^{2}e_{\theta}(x,t)\right)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).

(63)

Since
\Delta W=O(\sqrt{\Delta t})
, the leading term is

\displaystyle e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)=g(t)\nabla_{x}e_{\theta}(x,t)^{\top}\Delta W+O(\Delta t).

(64)

Thus, conditionally on
(x,t)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Delta W}\left[\bigl(e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)\bigr)^{2}\mid x,t\right]

\displaystyle=g(t)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\Delta W}\left[\bigl(\nabla_{x}e_{\theta}(x,t)^{\top}\Delta W\bigr)^{2}\right]+o(\Delta t)

(65)

\displaystyle=g(t)^{2}\left\|\nabla_{x}e_{\theta}(x,t)\right\|^{2}\Delta t+o(\Delta t).

(66)

Substituting into the loss expansion gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(F_{\theta})-\mathcal{L}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\mathbb{E}_{\Delta W}\left[\bigl(e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)\bigr)^{2}\mid x,t\right]+o(\Delta t)

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[g(t)^{2}\left\|\nabla_{x}e_{\theta}(x,t)\right\|^{2}\right]+o(\Delta t).

(68)

Finally, since

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}e_{\theta}(x,t)=\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x,t)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t),

(69)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(F_{\theta})-\mathcal{L}(0)=\frac{\Delta t}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[g(t)^{2}\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x,t)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\right\|^{2}\right]+o(\Delta t).

(70)

Case of the ODE

Assume now that
x_{t}
follows the ODE

\displaystyle\differential x_{t}=b(x_{t},t)\differential t.

(71)

Let
t^{\prime}=t+\Delta t
. Then, conditional on
(x_{t},t)=(x,t)
,

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=x+b(x,t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).

(72)

Therefore,

\displaystyle e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{t}e_{\theta}(x,t)+b(x,t)^{\top}\nabla_{x}e_{\theta}(x,t)\right)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).

(73)

Defining the total derivative

\displaystyle\differential_{t}e_{\theta}(x,t):=\partial_{t}e_{\theta}(x,t)+b(x,t)^{\top}\nabla_{x}e_{\theta}(x,t),

(74)

we get

\displaystyle e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)=\differential_{t}e_{\theta}(x,t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).

(75)

Using the Taylor expansion of the logistic loss around zero, and using the
forward-reverse identity
p_{+}=p_{-}
, the linear term vanishes and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(F_{\theta})-\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[\bigl(e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)\bigr)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[o\!\left(\bigl(e_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-e_{\theta}(x,t)\bigr)^{2}\right)\right].

(76)

Substituting the ODE expansion gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(F_{\theta})-\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[\bigl(\differential_{t}e_{\theta}(x,t)\bigr)^{2}\right]+o(\Delta t^{2}).

(77)

Finally, since

\displaystyle e_{\theta}(x,t)=\log p_{d}(x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x,t),

(78)

we have

\displaystyle\differential_{t}e_{\theta}(x,t)=\differential_{t}\log p_{d}(x,t)-\differential_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t).

(79)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(F_{\theta})-\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(0)=\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[\left(\differential_{t}\log p_{d}(x,t)-\differential_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\right)^{2}\right]+o(\Delta t^{2}).

(80)

We remark that the SDE interpretation is closer to the practical implementation of stNCE with forward-reverse kernel. With the ODE interpretation, one would need to know the velocity field
b(x,t)
in advance, and need to keep track of the ground truth
\differential_{t}\log p_{t}(x)
.

A.4
White noise kernel: infinitesimal limit

Simplifying the loss around zero

Let

\displaystyle p_{+}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t),

(81)

\displaystyle p_{-}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(82)

The logistic loss used in stNCE is

\mathcal{L}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{-}}\left[\log\bigl(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\bigr)\right].

(83)

Using Lemma
6
, whenever

F_{\theta}
is close to zero,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(0)-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}-p_{-}}\left[F_{\theta}\right]+\frac{1}{8}\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}+p_{-}}\left[F_{\theta}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}+p_{-}}\left[o(F_{\theta}^{2})\right].

(84)

Plugging in the white noise kernel

Assume that the perturbation kernel is an infinitesimal Gaussian kernel in
both space and time:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=x+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{x}}\,\xi,\qquad\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

(85)

\displaystyle t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=t+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}\,\tau,\qquad\tau\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(86)

where
\xi
and
\tau
are independent. Since the Gaussian kernel is
symmetric,

\displaystyle p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)=p_{n}(x,t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(87)

Thus

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(88)

A first-order Taylor expansion gives

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{x}}\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)^{\top}\xi-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\tau

(89)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{x}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}).

(90)

Therefore
F_{\theta}
is close to zero in the infinitesimal perturbation
limit, justifying the Taylor expansion around zero.

The linear term

Using symmetry of
p_{c}
, we have

\displaystyle p_{+}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})-p_{-}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\bigl(p_{d}(x,t)-p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\bigr)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t).

(91)

Moreover,

\displaystyle p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=p_{d}(x,t)+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{x}}\nabla_{x}p_{d}(x,t)^{\top}\xi+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}\partial_{t}p_{d}(x,t)\tau

(92)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{x}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}).

(93)

Hence

\displaystyle p_{d}(x,t)-p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-p_{d}(x,t)\left[\sqrt{\varepsilon_{x}}\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x,t)^{\top}\xi+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x,t)\tau\right]

(94)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon_{x}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}).

(95)

Combining this with the expansion of
F_{\theta}
, and averaging over

\xi,\tau
, gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}-p_{-}}\left[F_{\theta}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\Bigl[\varepsilon_{x}\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x,t)^{\top}\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)

(96)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon_{t}\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x,t)\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\Bigr]

(97)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\varepsilon_{x}+\varepsilon_{t}),

(98)

where the mixed term vanishes because
\xi
and
\tau
are independent and
centered.

The quadratic term

Similarly,

\displaystyle F_{\theta}^{2}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{x}\left(\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)^{\top}\xi\right)^{2}+\varepsilon_{t}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\tau\right)^{2}

(99)

\displaystyle\quad+2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{x}\varepsilon_{t}}\left(\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)^{\top}\xi\right)\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\tau\right)

(100)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\varepsilon_{x}+\varepsilon_{t}).

(101)

Averaging over
\xi,\tau
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\tau}\left[F_{\theta}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{x}\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\right\|^{2}+\varepsilon_{t}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\right)^{2}

(102)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\varepsilon_{x}+\varepsilon_{t}).

(103)

Since

\displaystyle p_{+}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})+p_{-}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=2p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\mid x,t)+o(1),

(104)

we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}+p_{-}}\left[F_{\theta}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\Biggl[\varepsilon_{x}\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\right\|^{2}

(105)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon_{t}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\right)^{2}\Biggr]

(106)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\varepsilon_{x}+\varepsilon_{t}).

(107)

Combining the terms

Substituting the linear and quadratic terms into the expansion gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(F_{\theta})-\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\Biggl[\varepsilon_{x}\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\right\|^{2}+\varepsilon_{t}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\right)^{2}\Biggr]

(108)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\Biggl[\varepsilon_{x}\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x,t)^{\top}\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)

(109)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon_{t}\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x,t)\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)\Biggr]

(110)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\varepsilon_{x}+\varepsilon_{t}).

(111)

Completing the square, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(F_{\theta})-\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\Biggl[\varepsilon_{x}\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x,t)\right\|^{2}

(112)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon_{t}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)-\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x,t)\right)^{2}\Biggr]

(113)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\Biggl[\varepsilon_{x}\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x,t)\right\|^{2}+\varepsilon_{t}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x,t)\right)^{2}\Biggr]

(114)

\displaystyle\quad+o(\varepsilon_{x}+\varepsilon_{t}).

(115)

Therefore, up to additive terms independent of
\theta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\Biggl[\varepsilon_{x}\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x,t)\right\|^{2}

(116)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon_{t}\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x,t)-\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x,t)\right)^{2}\Biggr]+o(\varepsilon_{x}+\varepsilon_{t}).

(117)

Thus, infinitesimal Gaussian perturbations recover the dual score matching
objective
[
Guth et al. 2025
]
: one term matches the spatial score

\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x,t)
, while the other matches the temporal score

\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x,t)
.

Appendix B
Special cases of our method

In this section, we detail how many popular losses for learning energy-based models are in fact special cases of our loss.

First, we express the stNCE loss function in a similar form as
Ceylan and Gutmann 2018
. Recall the stNCE loss function:

\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{+}}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{-}}\left[\log\bigl(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\bigr)\right].

(118)

Since
p_{+}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)
and
p_{-}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, due to symmetry one can switch
x,t
and
x^{\prime},t^{\prime}
without affecting the correctness. As such, we can simply reuse
x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}
as
x^{\prime},t^{\prime},x,t
and write it using a single term, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{stNCE}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=-2\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]

(119)

\displaystyle=-2\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x|t)p(t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right],

(120)

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)

(121)

\displaystyle\quad-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(122)

Observe that it concerns two choices: the time prior
p(t)
and the perturbation kernel
p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)
. We next show how to recover exisiting methods using the above formulation.

B.2
Noise-Contrastive Estimation (NCE)

Original loss

The standard Noise-Contrastive Estimation (NCE)
objective
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x|1)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x)-\log p_{d}(x|0)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x|0)}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x)-\log p_{d}(x|0)\right)\right)\right].

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function.

Our loss

Recall that the generic stNCE loss is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right)\right],

where the logit is

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

and the model distribution at time zero is known,
p_{\theta}(x,0):=p_{0}(x)
. Recall that
p_{d}(x,t)=p(t)p_{d}(x|t)
. Choosing the prior on time as
p(t)=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}(t),

and the perturbation kernel as

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)=\delta_{x}(x^{\prime})\delta_{1-t}(t^{\prime})
simplifies the loss. In particular, the perturbation leaves the spatial variable unchanged and swaps
the temporal index. We now obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{1}(x)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,1,x,0)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{0}(x)}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,0,x,1)\right)\right)\right].

where

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,1,x,0)

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|1)+\log p(1)+\log\delta_{x}(x)+\log\delta_{0}(0)-\log p_{\theta}(x|0)-\log p(0)-\log\delta_{x}(x)-\log\delta_{1}(1)

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|1)-\log p_{0}(x)

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,0,x,1)

\displaystyle=\log p_{0}(x)-\log p_{\theta}(x|1)

Substituting these expressions back into the stNCE loss yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x|1)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x)-\log p_{0}(x)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x|0)}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x)-\log p_{0}(x)\right)\right)\right],

(123)

which exactly recovers the standard NCE objective.

B.3
Flow-Contrastive Estimation (FCE)

This is a special case of NCE where the reference distribution
p_{d}(x|0)
is not
fixed beforehand but is instead updated adaptively during training. In
particular, FCE parameterizes the reference distribution as a flow-based model
p_{d}(x|0)=p_{\beta}(x)
that is updated during the optimization.

B.4
Temporal Noise-Contrastive Estimation (tNCE)

Original loss

The temporal Noise-Contrastive Estimation (tNCE)
objective which is heavily inspired by
Rhodes et al. 2020
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tNCE}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})+\Delta(t,t^{\prime})\right)\right]

(124)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t^{\prime})p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})+\Delta(t,t^{\prime})\right)\right)\right],

(125)

where

\displaystyle\Delta(t,t^{\prime})=\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})

is known to the user. For example, the user can choose
p(t)
to be uniform over the interval
[0,1]
, and
p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)
to be a Gaussian perturbation. In that case,
\Delta(t,t^{\prime})=0
.

Our loss

Recall that the generic stNCE loss is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right)\right],

where the logit is

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

Choosing the perturbation kernel as

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)=\delta_{x}(x^{\prime})p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)

removes all spatial perturbations. In particular, the perturbation leaves
the spatial variable unchanged while perturbing only the temporal index.
We now obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime})\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t^{\prime})p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime})\right)\right)\right].

Moreover,

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})+\Delta(t,t^{\prime}).

Substituting these expressions back into the stNCE loss yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})+\Delta(t,t^{\prime})\right)\right]

(126)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t^{\prime})p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})+\Delta(t,t^{\prime})\right)\right)\right],

(127)

which exactly recovers the tNCE objective.

B.5
Conditional Noise-Contrastive Estimation (CNCE)

Original loss

The Conditional Noise-Contrastive Estimation
(CNCE) objective
Ceylan and Gutmann 2018
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CNCE}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x|1)p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})\right)\right]

(128)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime}|1)p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})\right)\right)\right].

(129)

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function.

Our loss

Recall that the generic stNCE loss is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right)\right],

where the logit is

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

Choosing the prior on time as
p(t)=\delta_{1}(t)
and the perturbation
kernel as

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)=p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)\delta_{t}(t^{\prime})

removes all temporal perturbations. In particular, the temporal index
remains fixed while the perturbation acts only on the spatial variable.
We now obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x)p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,1,x^{\prime},1)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime})p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,1,x^{\prime},1)\right)\right)\right].

Moreover,

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,1,x^{\prime},1)

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|1)+\log p(1)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|1)-\log p(1)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|1)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|1)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime}).

Identifying
p_{\theta}(x|1)
with
p_{\theta}(x)
, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,1,x^{\prime},1)=\log p_{\theta}(x)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime}).

Substituting these expressions back into the stNCE loss yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x|1)p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})\right)\right]

(130)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime}|1)p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})\right)\right)\right],

(131)

which exactly recovers the CNCE objective.

B.6
Contrastive Divergence (CD)

Yair and Michaeli 2021
observed that this is a special case of CNCE where the perturbation kernel
p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)
is an MCMC kernel with stationary distribution
\mathrm{stopgrad}(p_{\theta})
constantly updated during training.

B.7
Temporal Conditional Noise-Contrastive Estimation (tCNCE)

Original loss

The temporal Conditional Noise-Contrastive
Estimation (tCNCE) objective, which is a natural extension of the CNCE objective, is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tCNCE}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})\right)\right]

(132)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t)p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})\right)\right)\right].

(133)

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function.

Our loss

Recall that the generic stNCE loss is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right)\right],

where the logit is

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

Choosing the perturbation kernel as

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)=p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)\delta_{t}(t^{\prime})

leaves the temporal index unchanged while perturbing only the spatial
variable. We now obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t)p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t)\right)\right)\right].

Moreover,

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t)-\log p(t)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime}).

Substituting these expressions back into the stNCE loss yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})\right)\right]

(134)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t)p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})\right)\right)\right],

(135)

which exactly recovers the tCNCE objective.

B.8
Sum of tNCE and tCNCE

Original loss

Consider the sum of the tNCE and tCNCE objectives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tNCE}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tCNCE}}.

(136)

Our loss

Recall that the generic stNCE loss is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right)\right],

where the logit is

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

Choosing the perturbation kernel as

\displaystyle p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{t}(t^{\prime})p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{x}(x^{\prime})p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)

creates two perturbation branches. The first branch leaves the temporal
index unchanged while perturbing the spatial variable, whereas the
second branch leaves the spatial variable unchanged while perturbing the
temporal index. We now split the stNCE loss according to these two branches:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t)p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t)\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t^{\prime})p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma\!\left(F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime})\right)\right)\right].

For the spatial perturbation branch, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)

\displaystyle\quad-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t)-\log p(t)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime}),

which is exactly the tCNCE logit. Similarly, for the temporal perturbation branch,

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)

\displaystyle\quad-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})+\Delta(t,t^{\prime}),

where

\displaystyle\Delta(t,t^{\prime})=\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(t|t^{\prime}).

This is exactly the tNCE logit. Substituting these expressions back into the stNCE loss yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tCNCE}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tNCE}},

(137)

which recovers the sum of the tNCE and tCNCE objectives up to a
constant rescaling factor independent of
\theta
.

B.9
Time Score Matching

Original loss

The Time Score Matching objective is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TSM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x|t)\right)^{2}\right].

(138)

where
\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x|t)
denotes the ground-truth temporal score. In practice, the ground-truth temporal score is not available in closed-form so many have proposed tractable ways to rewrite the objective, using integration by parts
[
Choi et al. 2022
]
or by introducing a conditioning variable
[
Yu et al. 2025b
]
.

Our loss

Recall that the generic stNCE loss is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right)\right],

where the logit is

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

To recover Time Score Matching, we choose a purely temporal infinitesimal
perturbation. That is, the perturbation kernel leaves the spatial
variable unchanged and perturbs time according to

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)=\delta_{x}(x^{\prime})\mathcal{N}(t^{\prime};t,\varepsilon)
,
with
\varepsilon\to 0
. We also assume that the temporal perturbation
is symmetric, so
p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)=p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})
up to boundary effects. We now
obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t^{\prime})p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime}))\right)\right].

Moreover,

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime})+\log p(t)-\log p(t^{\prime}),

where the perturbation-kernel terms cancel by symmetry. If the time prior
is locally uniform, or if it is absorbed into the time-dependent density,
this reduces to

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime})=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x|t^{\prime}).

Writing
t^{\prime}=t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\xi
with
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, a
first-order Taylor expansion gives

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x,t^{\prime})=-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x|t)\xi+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

Using the second-order expansion of the logistic loss around

F_{\theta}=0
, the infinitesimal temporal perturbation limit gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[\left(\partial_{t}\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\partial_{t}\log p_{d}(x|t)\right)^{2}\right]+C+o(\varepsilon),

(139)

where
C
is independent of
\theta
. Therefore, up to the constant
factor
\varepsilon/4
and additive terms independent of
\theta
, the
infinitesimal temporal-only limit of stNCE recovers the Time Score
Matching objective.

B.10
Space Score Matching

Original loss

The Space Score Matching objective is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SSM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x|1)}\left[\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x|1)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x|1)\right\|^{2}\right].

(140)

where
\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x|1)
denotes the ground-truth spatial score. This quantity is unknown in closed-form so many have rewritten the objective in a tractable way using integration by parts
[
Hyvärinen 2005
]
or by introducing a conditioning variable
[
Vincent 2011
]
.

Our loss

Recall that the Energy-Based Diffusion loss derived above is obtained
from the infinitesimal spatial-only limit of stNCE:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x|t)\right\|^{2}\right]+C+o(\varepsilon).

(141)

Restricting the time distribution to the single time point
t=1
,
that is, choosing

p(t)=\delta_{1}(t),

yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x|1)}\left[\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x|1)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x|1)\right\|^{2}\right]+C+o(\varepsilon),

(142)

which exactly recovers the standard Space Score Matching objective up to
a constant rescaling factor and additive terms independent of
\theta
.

B.11
Energy-Based Diffusion

Original loss

The Energy-Based Diffusion (EBD) loss
[
Song et al. 2021b
,
Thornton et al. 2025
]
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EBD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x|t)\right\|^{2}\right].

(143)

where
\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x|t)
denotes the ground-truth spatial score.

Our loss

Recall that the generic stNCE loss is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}(F_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))\right)\right],

where the logit is

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t^{\prime})-\log p(t^{\prime})-\log p_{n}(x,t|x^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

To recover the Energy-Based Diffusion loss, we choose a purely spatial
infinitesimal perturbation. That is, the perturbation kernel leaves the
temporal index unchanged and perturbs the spatial variable according to

p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)=\mathcal{N}(x^{\prime};x,\varepsilon I)\delta_{t}(t^{\prime})
,
with
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since the Gaussian perturbation is symmetric,

p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)=p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})
. We now obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)}\left[\log\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t))\right]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x^{\prime},t)p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})}\left[\log\left(1-\sigma(F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t))\right)\right].

Moreover,

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)+\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t)-\log p(t)-\log p_{n}(x|x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\log p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}|t),

where the time-prior terms cancel because
t^{\prime}=t
, and the perturbation
kernel terms cancel by symmetry. Writing

x^{\prime}=x+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\xi
with
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, a
first-order Taylor expansion gives

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(x,t,x^{\prime},t)=-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x|t)^{\top}\xi+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

Using the second-order expansion of the logistic loss around

F_{\theta}=0
, the infinitesimal spatial perturbation limit gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stNCE}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathbb{E}_{p_{d}(x,t)}\left[\left\|\nabla_{x}\log p_{\theta}(x|t)-\nabla_{x}\log p_{d}(x|t)\right\|^{2}\right]+C+o(\varepsilon),

(144)

where
C
is independent of
\theta
. Therefore, up to the constant
factor
\varepsilon/4
and additive terms independent of
\theta
, the
infinitesimal spatial-only limit of stNCE recovers the Energy-Based
Diffusion loss.

B.12
Discussion on RNE
[
He et al. 2026
]

Next we consider RNE as proposed in
He et al. 2026
, which was shown to be capable of regularizing energy-based diffusion models. The RNE loss is roughly given by

\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{x_{t},t,x_{t+\Delta t},t+\Delta t}\norm{\text{sg}\left( \log p(x_{t}|x_{t+\Delta t}) - \log p(x_{t+\Delta t} | x_{t}) \right) + \log p_{t+\Delta t}(x_{t+\Delta t}) - \log p_{t}(x_{t})}^{2},

(145)

where the diffusion convention of time is used, and
sg
denotes stop gradient.
He et al. 2026
showed that the regularizer, when combined with denoising score matching loss, allows training normalized energy-based models.

However, when used on its own and using the score estimates as given by the model itself, the RNE loss suffers from a notable pathology.
Specifically, the optimum satisfying the Bayes rule constraint induced by RNE is not unique, in contrast to stNCE-s: while the ground-truth time-varying density
p_{t}
is one optimum, there exist infinitely many other optimums that may differ arbitrarily from the data distribution. For example, the degenerate solution where
p_{t}\equiv c
for some constant
c
is an optimum under the Variance-Exploding noising process with Euler-Maruyama discretization, and any time-varying probability density induced by a clean data distribution that differ from the empirical distribution is also a optimum.

Appendix C
Implementation considerations

C.1
Sampling mechanisms

stNCE logits are determined by
\log p(x,t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)=\log p(t)+\log p(x|t)+\log p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x,t|t^{\prime})+\log p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)
. As such, one would need to take into account
\log p(t)+\log p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)=\log p_{n}(t,t^{\prime})
when forming the logit, unless
\log p_{n}(t,t^{\prime})=\log p_{n}(t^{\prime},t)
, in which case they cancel out with each other. We next introduce the folding mechanism, which will be beneficial afterwards.

C.1.1
Useful identity: folding mechanism

We would like to sample
t^{\prime}
given
t
, such that when
t
is uniformly distributed between
[0,1]
,
t^{\prime}
follows the same distribution while remaining reasonably close to
t
. The following mechanism achieves it: one samples
\tilde{t}
by adding Gaussian noise with zero mean and some standard deviation
\sigma
, and folds
\tilde{t}
back to lie between
[0,1]
and obtain
t^{\prime}
using
t^{\prime}=1-|(\tilde{t}\mod 2)-1|
.

Proof.

Denote
\epsilon
to be the Gaussian noise with standard deviation
\sigma
. Observe that a given value of
t^{\prime}
can be obtained from different
\tilde{t}
. Specifically, they could be
\tilde{t}=2k+t^{\prime}
or
\tilde{t}=2k-t^{\prime}
, where
k\in Z
. We thus have

p_{t^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[p_{\tilde{t}}(2k+t^{\prime})+p_{\tilde{t}}(2k-t^{\prime})\right],

(146)

i.e. the sum of two terms. Note that

p_{\tilde{t}}(\tilde{t})=\int_{0}^{1}p_{\epsilon}(x-t)\differential t,

(147)

and

p_{t^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{1}p_{\epsilon}(2k+t^{\prime}-t)\differential t+\int_{0}^{1}p_{\epsilon}(2k-t^{\prime}-t)\differential t\right].

(148)

For the first term, using a change of variable
u=t-2k
, we have

\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}p_{\tilde{t}}(2k+t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-2k}^{-2k+1}p_{\epsilon}(t^{\prime}-u)\differential u,

(149)

i.e. it is the sum over all intervals of the form
[2k,2k+1]
. For the seocnd term, using a change of variable
u=2k-t
, we have

\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}p_{\tilde{t}}(2k-t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{2k-1}^{2k}p_{\epsilon}(t^{\prime}-u)\differential u,

(150)

i.e. it is the sum over all intervals of the form
[2k-1,2k]
.

Summarizing, these intervals cover the entire real line. As such,

p_{t^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{\epsilon}(t^{\prime}-u)\differential u=1,

(151)

i.e.
t^{\prime}
follows uniform distribution.
∎

Furthermore, given two values
t
and
t^{\prime}
,
p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)=p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})
, as these probabilities only depend on the distances of the path between
t
and
t^{\prime}
, while a Gaussian is symmetric around its mean.

Note that there are different approaches to sample
t
,
t^{\prime}
,
x
and
x^{\prime}
. In the following we describe two main sampling schemes, which we term
default
and
reuse
. We use
z
to denote the clean data, and consider as perturbation kernel
p_{n}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}|x,t)=p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x,t,t^{\prime})
.

C.1.2
default

Perhaps the most natural implementation is to follow the data generation process of stNCE; we note that this is also the sampling mechanism as employed by
Aggarwal et al. 2025
. Given one
z
, one
(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is constructed. It can be summarized as follows:

\displaystyle t\sim p(t),

\displaystyle\quad x\sim p(x|z,t),

(152)

\displaystyle t^{\prime}\sim p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t),

\displaystyle\quad x^{\prime}\sim p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x,t,t^{\prime}).

(153)

However, special care is needed on the logits involving
t
and
t^{\prime}
. Perhaps the simplest case is
p(t)
is uniform and
p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)=p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})
, as all logits naturally cancel out. Interestingly, when
p(t)
is uniform, the folding mechanism from Section
C.1.1
guarantees that
p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)=p_{n}(t|t^{\prime})
, while ensuring that the marginal distribution
p(t^{\prime})=\int p_{n}(t^{\prime}|t)p(t)\differential t
remains uniform.

C.1.3
reuse

Alternatively, one can consider the following mechanism:

\displaystyle t_{0}\sim p(t_{0}),

\displaystyle\quad t_{1}\sim p_{n}(t_{1}|t_{0}),

(154)

\displaystyle t=[t_{0},t_{1}],

\displaystyle\quad t^{\prime}=[t_{1},t_{0}],

(155)

\displaystyle x_{0}\sim p(x_{0}|z,t_{0}),

\displaystyle\quad x_{1}\sim p(x_{1}|z,t_{1}),

(156)

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{0}\sim p_{n}(x^{\prime}_{0}|x_{0},t_{0},t_{1}),

\displaystyle\quad x^{\prime}_{1}\sim p_{n}(x^{\prime}_{1}|x_{1},t_{1},t_{0}),

(157)

\displaystyle x=[x_{0},x_{1}],

\displaystyle\quad x^{\prime}=[x^{\prime}_{0},x^{\prime}_{1}].

(158)

Given one
z
, two
(x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
instances are constructed, using
(t,t^{\prime})
and
(t^{\prime},t)
, respectively. In this case, regardless of the choice of
p(t_{0})
and
p_{n}(t_{1},t_{0})
, the sampling mechanism ensures that
p_{n}(t,t^{\prime})=p_{n}(t^{\prime},t)
. Nevertheless, one may still prefer to employ the folding mechanism from Section
C.1.1
so as to make
t
and
t^{\prime}
remain uniform. Furthermore, this enables mechanisms to reduce variances and even save computations, as detailed below.

General case

Rhodes et al. 2020
noted that reusing the same noise can reduce variance. As such, we could use the same noise when simulating
p(x|z,t)
or
p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x,t,t^{\prime})
. Given one
z
, we would like to obtain samples from
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
, and we can achieve this by interpolating between
z
and the same noise using
t_{0}
and
1
. Certain
p_{n}(x^{\prime}|x,t,t^{\prime})
adds Gaussian noise of some standard deviations, and we can add the same Gaussian noise to
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
when simulating
x^{\prime}_{0}
and
x^{\prime}_{1}
.

stNCE with forward-reverse

In the case of stNCE with forward-reverse kernel,
p_{n}
could naturally correspond to diffusion forward process. As such, we could exploit this identity to reduce computations. Specifically, without generality, one can design it so, such that
t_{0}
is always smaller than
t_{1}
. For numerical stability, one additionally ensure that the difference between them is not too small. Observe that one could reuse
x_{0}
for
x^{\prime}_{1}
, suppose one simulate
x_{1}
by following the diffusion forward process for time
t_{1}-t_{0}
. Furthermore, stNCE with forward-reverse kernel requires the scores always for the smaller
t
, and we only need the scores for
t_{0}
. When we combine stNCE loss with DSM loss, these scores are naturally needed for DSM, as
x_{0}
are indeed samples from the corresponding distributions.

In order for the forward kernel to perfectly coincide with the forward process while avoiding numerical issues, we additionally employ a mechanism to guarantee that
t_{1}
and
t_{0}
are apart by at least
\epsilon
. We follow the convention that this mechanism is employed when we use the
reuse
scheme, even for algorithms apart from stNCE with forward-reverse.

C.2
Implementing forward-reverse kernel

Denote the forward denoising process as
x_{t}=\alpha_{t}z+\beta_{t}n
, where
z
are data samples and
n
are noise samples from standard normal distribution.

From the perspective of diffusion and flows, the choice of the kernel in the case where
t^{\prime}<t
is obvious (note that we use the flow matching convention of time), as it can be given by the forward process of diffusion models. In this case, we can choose

p(x^{\prime}|x,t^{\prime},t)=\mathcal{N}\left(x^{\prime}\middle|\frac{\alpha_{t^{\prime}}}{\alpha_{t}}x,\beta_{t^{\prime}}^{2}-\frac{\alpha_{t^{\prime}}^{2}\beta_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\right),

(159)

which essentially generates samples from
p(x^{\prime}|t^{\prime})
given samples from
p(x|t)
.

Under the scenario that
t^{\prime}>t
, with the above forward process one might be tempted to use as transition kernel the one that leads to the reverse kernel of diffusions, in which case we would sample from the posterior distribution. However, this posterior is highly non-trivial and is available only under specific data distributions.

C.2.1
Recovery likelihood

Inspired by diffusion recovery likelihood
[
Gao et al. 2021
]
, we can construct approximations to the reverse kernel. In the following, to simplify notations, we denote
p(x^{\prime}|x,t^{\prime},t)
as
p(x_{\tau}|x_{t})
, where
x_{t}=\alpha x_{\tau}+\beta n
, with
\alpha=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{\tau}}
and
\beta=\sqrt{\beta_{t}^{2}-\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}\beta_{t^{\prime}}^{2}}{\alpha_{t^{\prime}}^{2}}}
. We have

\displaystyle\log p(x_{\tau}|x_{t})

\displaystyle=\log p(x_{\tau})+\log p(x_{t}|x_{\tau})+c

(160)

\displaystyle\approx\log p(x_{t})+\langle\nabla_{x}\log p(x_{t}),x_{\tau}-x_{t}\rangle-\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}\lVert x_{t}-\alpha x_{\tau}\rVert^{2}+c

(161)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{x}\log p(x_{t}),x_{\tau}\rangle-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\left\lVert\frac{x_{t}}{\alpha}-x_{\tau}\right\rVert^{2}+c

(162)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{x}\log p(x_{t}),x_{\tau}\rangle-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\lVert x_{\tau}\rVert^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}2\left\langle x_{\tau},\frac{x_{t}}{\alpha}\right\rangle+c

(163)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\left(\langle x_{\tau},x_{\tau}\rangle-2\left\langle x_{\tau},\frac{x_{t}}{\alpha}\right\rangle-\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left\langle x_{\tau},\nabla_{x}\log p(x_{t})\right\rangle\right)+c

(164)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\left\lVert x_{\tau}-\left(\frac{x_{t}}{\alpha}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\nabla_{x}\log p(x_{t})\right)\right\rVert^{2}+c,

(165)

which motivates using the following Gaussian approximation:

p(x_{\tau}|x_{t})=\mathcal{N}\left(x_{\tau}\middle|\frac{x_{t}}{\alpha}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\nabla_{x}\log p(x_{t}),\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}I\right).

(166)

C.2.2
Exponential integrator

The sampling scheme based on diffusion recovery likelihood builds on the assumption that the score is locally constant across
x
. However, this assumption may not be ideal. Indeed, works on accelerated diffusion samplers
[
Lu et al. 2022
,
Gonzalez et al. 2023
]
typically assume that the score is locally constant across
t
.

Here we focus on the exponential integrator for the reverse SDE as presented in
Gonzalez et al. 2023
, specifically the SEEDS-1 variant, which is a first order SDE solver. The update rule is given by

x_{\tau}=\frac{\alpha_{\tau}}{\alpha_{t}}x_{t}-2\beta_{\tau}\left(\exp(h)-1\right)F(x_{t},t)-\beta_{\tau}\sqrt{\exp(2h)-1}\epsilon,

(167)

where
F
is the output of noise prediction model, and
h=\lambda_{\tau}-\lambda_{t}=\log\left(\frac{\alpha_{\tau}}{\beta_{\tau}}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\beta_{t}}\right)
. As such,
\exp(h)=\frac{\alpha_{\tau}\beta_{t}}{\beta_{\tau}\alpha_{t}}
and
\exp(2h)=\left(\frac{\alpha_{\tau}\beta_{t}}{\beta_{\tau}\alpha_{t}}\right)^{2}
.

Observe that the update rule of SEEDS-1 naturally induces a Gaussian distribution. The mean is given by

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\alpha_{\tau}}{\alpha_{t}}x_{t}+2\beta_{\tau}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\tau}\beta_{t}}{\beta_{\tau}\alpha_{t}}-1\right)\beta_{t}\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x)

(168)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\tau}}{\alpha_{t}}x_{t}+2\left(\frac{\alpha_{\tau}\beta_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}}-\beta_{\tau}\beta_{t}\right)\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x)

(169)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\tau}}{\alpha_{t}}x_{t}+2\beta_{t}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\tau}}{\alpha_{t}}\beta_{t}-\beta_{\tau}\right)\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x),

(170)

and the covariance is given by

\beta_{\tau}^{2}\left(\left(\frac{\alpha_{\tau}\beta_{t}}{\beta_{\tau}\alpha_{t}}\right)^{2}-1\right)I=\left(\frac{\alpha_{\tau}^{2}\beta_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}-\beta_{\tau}^{2}\right)I.

(171)

Compared with the one obtained through recovery likelihood, the only difference is the scaling term in front of the score.

Consider the scenario where
\alpha_{t}=t
and
\beta_{t}=1-t
. The above expression simplifies, resulting in a Gaussian distribution with mean given by

\frac{\tau}{t}x_{t}+\frac{2(1-t)(\tau-t)}{t}\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x_{t}),

(172)

and covariance given by

\frac{(\tau-t)(t+\tau-2t\tau)}{t^{2}}I.

(173)

C.3
Parameterization of energy-based diffusion models

A proper parameterization of energy-based diffusion models,
E_{\theta}(x,t)
, could be crucial in practice, where the training stability of the high variance of the Denoising Score Matching objective at low noise levels required to be cured
[
Song and Ermon 2019
]
. Let
x_{t}=\alpha_{t}x_{1}+\sigma_{t}z
. In standard Diffusion models,
Karras et al. 2022
proposes to parameterize a denoiser with skip connection:

\displaystyle D_{\theta}(x_{t},t)=c_{\mathrm{skip}}(t)x_{t}+c_{\mathrm{out}}(t)f_{\theta}(c_{\mathrm{in}}(t)x_{t},c_{t}(t)),

(174)

where
c_{\mathrm{skip}}(t),c_{\mathrm{out}}(t),c_{\mathrm{in}}(t)
are predefined parameters that depend on the variance of target data and noise schedules. Assumed an optimal denoiser, the denoising score,
\nabla\log p_{t}
, is then recovered through Tweedie’s formula:

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{skip}}(t)x_{t}+c_{\mathrm{out}}(t)f_{\theta}(c_{\mathrm{in}}(t)x_{t},c_{t}(t))=\alpha_{t}^{-1}\bigl(x_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\bigr).

(175)

Therefore, one could parameterize an energy network as
[
Thornton et al. 2025
]
:

\displaystyle U_{\theta}(x_{t},t)=\frac{1-\alpha_{t}c_{\mathrm{skip}}(t)}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\|x_{t}\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha_{t}c_{\mathrm{out}}(t)}{c_{\mathrm{in}}(t)\sigma_{t}^{2}}F_{\theta}(c_{\mathrm{in}}(t)x_{t},c_{t}(t)),

(176)

where
F_{\theta}
is an arbitrary scalar network.
Then applying Tweedie’s formula, it recovers the EDM preconditioning:

\displaystyle D_{\theta}(x_{t},t)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}^{-1}\bigl(x_{t}-\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla U_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\bigr)=c_{\mathrm{skip}}(t)x_{t}+c_{\mathrm{out}}(t)\nabla_{c_{\mathrm{in}}(t)x_{t}}F_{\theta}(c_{\mathrm{in}}(t)x_{t},c_{t}(t)).

(177)

C.3.1
Pathology of model without preconditioning

In the following, we focus on the case of using Cond OT probability path
[
Lipman et al. 2023
]
.

One might be tempted to directly parameterize the EBMs using some neural architectures and obtain its gradient as the score. However, recall that the velocity can be expressed using the score as

v=\frac{x}{t}+\frac{1-t}{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t}).

(178)

As such, when
t
is small, any small error in the estimated
\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})
is significantly amplified.

C.3.2
Choice of preconditioning

Next we develop our exact choice of preconditioning. In the following we use
\sigma
to denote the standard deviation of data.

\displaystyle c_{in}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}}},

(179)

\displaystyle c_{skip}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{t\sigma^{2}}{t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}},

(180)

\displaystyle c_{out}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-t)\sigma}{\sqrt{t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}}},

(181)

\displaystyle c_{t}(t)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{1-t}{t}\right).

(182)

With the above choices, the velocity can be expressed using the model as

v=\frac{c_{skip}(t)-1}{1-t}x+\sigma\nabla_{x}F(c_{in}x,c_{t}(t)),

(183)

which avoids the singularity issue under the vanilla model.

c_{in}(t)
serves to keep the inputs to the network with unit standard deviation. We have

\displaystyle\text{Var}(x_{t})

\displaystyle=t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2},

(184)

\displaystyle\text{Std}(x_{t})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}},

(185)

\displaystyle c_{in}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}}}.

(186)

c_{skip}(t)
serves as the optimal linear coefficient to predict
x_{1}
based on
x_{t}
, and its optimal value is given by

\displaystyle c_{skip}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Cov}(x_{1},x_{t})}{\text{Var}(x_{t})}

(187)

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Cov}(x_{1},tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{0}}{t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}}

(188)

\displaystyle=\frac{t\sigma^{2}}{t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}}.

(189)

c_{out}(t)
serves to keep the network output have variance
1
, and can be derived by considering the variance of
x_{1}-c_{skip}(t)x_{t}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\quad x_{1}-c_{skip}(t)x_{t}

(190)

\displaystyle=x_{1}-c_{skip}(t)(tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{0})

(191)

\displaystyle=(1-tc_{skip}(t))x_{1}-(1-t)c_{skip}(t)x_{0},

(192)

and

\displaystyle\text{Var}(x_{1}-c_{skip}(t)x_{t})

\displaystyle=\left(1-tc_{skip}(t)\right)^{2}\sigma^{2}+\left((1-t)c_{skip}(t)\right)^{2}.

(193)

Denote the denominator of
x_{1}-c_{skip}(t)x_{t}
to be
D=t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}
, as such
c_{skip}(t)=\frac{t\sigma^{2}}{D}
, and

1-tc_{skip}(t)=1-\frac{t^{2}\sigma^{2}}{D}=\frac{(1-t)^{2}}{D}.

(194)

We have

\displaystyle\text{Var}(x_{1}-c_{skip}(t)x_{t})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(1-t)^{2}}{D}\right)^{2}\sigma^{2}+\left(\frac{(1-t)t\sigma^{2}}{D}\right)^{2}

(195)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-t)^{4}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}t^{2}\sigma^{4}}{D^{2}}

(196)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-t)^{2}\sigma^{2}\left((1-t)^{2}+t^{2}\sigma^{2}\right)}{D^{2}}

(197)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-t)^{2}\sigma^{2}}{D}.

(198)

As such,

c_{out}(t)=\text{Std}(x_{1}-c_{skip}(t)x_{t})=\frac{(1-t)\sigma}{\sqrt{t^{2}\sigma^{2}+(1-t)^{2}}}.

(199)

c_{noise}(t)
provides time embedding that reflects the effective noise level. With Cond OT path, the ratio of noise std to signal std is given by
\frac{1-t}{t\sigma}
, and we have

c_{noise}(t)=\ln\left(\frac{1-t}{t\sigma}\right).

(200)

Flow matching model is known to work well without needing
c_{in}(t)
and
c_{t}(t)
. As such, we can drop them from
F
, while keeping them elsewhere.

It might be tempting to directly employ the above choices to parameterize the energy and velocity. Observe that
\frac{c_{out}(t)}{c_{in}(t)}=(1-t)\sigma
. We have

\displaystyle U(x_{t},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-tc_{skip}(t)}{2(1-t)^{2}}\norm{x_{t}}^{2}-\frac{\sigma t}{1-t}F,

(201)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{skip}(t)-1}{1-t}x+\sigma\nabla_{x}F.

(202)

Note that
c_{skip}(1)=1
. As such, when
t=1
,
U(x_{t},t)
has a singularity.

In order to resolve the singularity, we can either sample time not until
1
or replace
1-t
with
1-at
for some
a<1
. In this work, we set
a=0.75
.

C.4
Score and velocity

Flow Matching
[
Lipman et al. 2023
]
provides an alternative viewpoint for diffusion models. When the noise distribution is Gaussian, we can convert between the score and the velocity. Similar identities have been shown in previous works, e.g.
Holderrieth et al. 2026
.

Consider
x_{t}=\alpha_{t}x_{0}+\beta_{t}n
. We have

\displaystyle v_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{\alpha}}{\alpha}x_{t}+\left(\frac{\dot{\alpha}\beta^{2}}{\alpha}-\dot{\beta}\beta\right)\nabla\log p_{t}(x),

(203)

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{t}(x)-\frac{\dot{\alpha}}{\alpha}x}{\frac{\dot{\alpha}\beta^{2}}{\alpha}-\dot{\beta}\beta}.

(204)

Proof.

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x|x_{1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left(x-\alpha x_{1}\right),

(205)

\displaystyle v_{t}(x|x_{1})

\displaystyle=\dot{\alpha}x_{1}+\dot{\beta}\frac{x-\alpha x_{1}}{\beta}=\dot{\alpha}x_{1}-\dot{\beta}\beta\nabla\log p_{t}(x|x_{1}),

(206)

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\frac{x-\alpha x_{1}}{\beta^{2}}\right]=-\frac{x-\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[x_{1}\right]}{\beta^{2}},

(207)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}\nabla\log p_{t}(x)+x}{\alpha},

(208)

\displaystyle v_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\dot{\alpha}x_{1}-\dot{\beta}\beta\nabla\log p_{t}(x|x_{1})\right]=\dot{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{1}\right]-\dot{\beta}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla\log p_{t}(x|x_{1})\right]

(209)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{\alpha}}{\alpha}\left(\beta^{2}\nabla\log p_{t}(x)+x\right)-\dot{\beta}\beta\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=\frac{\dot{\alpha}}{\alpha}x+\left(\frac{\dot{\alpha}\beta^{2}}{\alpha}-\dot{\beta}\beta\right)\nabla\log p_{t}(x),

(210)

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{v_{t}(x)-\frac{\dot{\alpha}}{\alpha}x}{\frac{\dot{\alpha}\beta^{2}}{\alpha}-\dot{\beta}\beta}.

(211)

∎

The above connection between the score and the velocity also enables one to use flow matching loss to train models under different parameterizations
[
Li and He 2026
]
. Therefore, while Dual SM, following
Guth et al. 2025
, uses the original denoising score matching loss, for stNCE-s
+
DSM we adapt flow matching loss to train the space score. As it still trains the space score, we refer to it still as a space score matching loss.

Appendix D
Experimental details

Recall that default scheme results in one training instance for each input data, and reuse scheme results in two training instances for each input data. As such, when comparing between reuse and default, we use two times larger batch size for default. Unless otherwise stateed, we train the model for
100000
steps and perform evaluation every
2000
steps.

The algorithms involve different hyperparameters. Especially, when the algorithm involves comparisons across different times, we need to determine the sampling scheme for
t^{\prime}
given
t
. Inspired by
Aggarwal et al. 2025
, we consider either using a local Gaussian proposal before wrapping things back to the range of
t
or sampling
t^{\prime}
uniformly at random. We denote the standard deviation of the Gaussian proposal as
\sigma_{time}
, and use
\sigma_{time}=-1.0
to denote the case where
t^{\prime}
is sampled uniformly at random. Some algorithms involve adding local Gaussian perturbations to the positions, in which case we tune the standard deviation of the perturbation
\sigma_{white}
between
[0.01,0.1,1.0]
.

For tNCE, we tune
\sigma_{time}
. For tCNCE, we tune
\sigma_{white}
; under reuse scheme, as an ablation we additionally tune
\sigma_{time}
. We tune stNCE-w in terms of both
\sigma_{time}
and
\sigma_{white}
. stNCE-s and stNCE-o are tuned in terms of
\sigma_{time}
.

We generally select the best model based on val set results under a single random seed, and report test set results. For each job, we select the step that yields the best val set results and use the test set results at that step as the final metrics. Sometimes we would like to obtain uncertainty estimates, in which case we additionally train the models using the best configs with two additional random seeds.

Unless otherwise stated, we use Adam optimizer
[
Kingma and Ba 2015
]
with no weight decay.

D.1
Failure modes

Experimental settings

We consider a simple setup where the reference distribution is a standard Gaussian and the data distribution is a Gaussian mixture:

\displaystyle p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad p_{1}=0.5\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},0.1^{2})+0.5\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},0.1^{2}).

(212)

By varying
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
, we control both the multimodality of
p_{1}
and the mismatch between the high-density regions of
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
, quantified in
[0,1]
. We measure the error as
1-R^{2}\in[0,1]
. Here, the Pearson
R^{2}
measures the correlation between the ground truth and learnt energies of the data points.
We evaluate three representative methods: tNCE (temporal differences), tCNCE (spatial differences), and stNCE-m (spatio-temporal). The trends in Figure
1
are robust across specific algorithms and depend primarily on the method family.

Multimodality and mismatch scores

We used heuristic scores to measure two properties of the data distribution
p_{1}
: its multi-modality and its mismatch with the reference distribution
p_{0}
. The multimodality score is computed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{multimodality}}=\frac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}+2\sigma},\in[0,1].

(213)

The mismatch score is computed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{mismatch}}=1-\frac{\max\bigl(0,\,\min(b,1)-\max(a,-1)\bigr)}{b-a},

(214)

where

\displaystyle a=\min(\mu_{1}-\sigma,\ \mu_{2}-\sigma),\quad b=\max(\mu_{1}+\sigma,\ \mu_{2}+\sigma).

(215)

To vary both these scores relatively on a approximately uniform grid on
[0,1]\times[0,1]
, we used the following parameters for the data distribution:

The energy function is parameterized by a neural network that is a simple Multi-Layer Perceptron with
2
hidden layers and SiLU activations. Its weights are updated using the AdamW optimizer
[
Loshchilov and Hutter 2019
]

1e-4
, using a batch size of
256

1e4

0.9999
, to remove the residual variance in the loss. We empirically observed that the residual variance was high when the methods were expected to have a high estimation error, as is the case for example for tCNCE when the data distribution is multi-modal.

Appendix F
Broader impacts

Our work is relatively theoretical and studies how to train EBMs. Nevertheless, due to the widespread usage of EBMs, it could be used by practioners in malicious manners. We attempt to mitigate such negative impacts by advocating for responsible usage of such trained models.
