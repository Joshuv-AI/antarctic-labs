Thailand’s health financing resilience during COVID-19: leveraging universal health coverage to tackle the pandemic
Authors: Aungsumalee Pholpark, Sikarn Issarachaiyos, Ilada Sooknark, Orawan Prasitsiriphon, Praewa Kulatnam, Papada Ranron, Sirithorn Khositchaiwat, Piya Hanvoravongchai, Yot Teerawattananon

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic constituted a public health emergency in Thailand from March 2020 to October 2022, testing the resilience of the health financing system. The study analyzed COVID-19 public health expenditure data (Fiscal Years (FY) 2020–2022) and financial data from 866 Ministry of Public Health hospitals (FY 2015–2022) using descriptive statistics and trend analysis. Background

Following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in Thailand in early 2020, the Thai government declared a national state of emergency under the Emergency Decree on Public Administration in Emergency Situations B.E. 2548 (2005). Consequently, the Center for COVID-19 Situation Administration (CCSA) was established in March 2020. Chaired by the Prime Minister, this centralized agency was tasked with overseeing and managing the comprehensive national response to the public health emergency [1].

Thailand encountered the COVID-19 pandemic with a well-established Universal Health Coverage (UHC) framework. Since 2002, the Thai population has been covered under three public health financing mechanisms: the Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS) for civil servants and their dependents, representing 7.1% of the population; the Social Security Scheme (SSS) for private-sector employees, comprising 17.2%; and the Universal Coverage Scheme (UCS), which encompasses the remaining 75.7% of citizens not covered by the CSMBS or SSS [2]. The country exceeded the global average Service Coverage Index score of 70 with a score of 81 in 2019 [3]. Thailand’s pre-existing UHC infrastructures were rapidly mobilized during the pandemic, leveraging a national health security capacity that ranked fifth globally out of 195 countries in the Global Health Security Index [4].

Thailand managed the initial wave of the pandemic by implementing stringent public health and social measures, conducting active contact tracing [5], and expanding COVID-19 service coverage [6]. Despite these early interventions, transmission persisted and subsequently peaked during the fourth epidemiological wave in July 2021. In October 2022, the CCSA officially declared the conclusion of the pandemic emergency phase, concluding with a cumulative total of approximately 4.68 million confirmed cases and 32,771 recorded fatalities [7].

Health financing resilience—defined as the capacity to absorb, adapt, and transform in response to shocks while maintaining essential functions—requires flexible revenue mobilization, adaptive purchasing, and robust provider payment mechanisms [8]. As such, it serves as a critical determinants of a health system’s ability to withstand public health emergencies, maintain essential services, and foster post-pandemic recovery [6].

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, general tax revenues and payroll-based social insurance contributions served as the primary funding sources for the Thai healthcare system. Thailand utilized multiple funding pools across the three public health financing mechanisms introduced previously: the CSMBS, which is financed via general tax revenue and administered by the Comptroller General’s Department (CGD); the SSS, funded through tripartite payroll contributions and managed by the Social Security Office (SSO); and the UCS, which is financed via general tax revenue and administered by the National Health Security Office (NHSO) [9]. Operationally, the SSO and NHSO purchased health services from contracted public and private providers, whereas the CGD disbursed payments directly to public providers. For emergency response, the Budget Bureau annually allocated a portion of the central government budget for national contingencies, such as environmental disasters. According to the National Health Accounts for 2020–2021, current health expenditure for the CSMBS, SSS, and UCS in 2021 was US$2.19 billion, US$1.84 billion, and US$4.46 billion, respectively [10].

Throughout the three-year pandemic period, Thailand expanded its fiscal space for health by consistently mobilizing resources through both existing budgetary allocations and extrabudgetary funds. This mobilization supported a comprehensive array of public health interventions and health services, including state quarantine protocols, vaccine procurement, active contact tracing, COVID-19 testing, treatment, and vaccination. Ultimately, these interventions aimed to guarantee equitable access to COVID-19 health services and provide financial risk protection for the entire population in Thailand.

Consequently, Thailand’s pandemic-era public health financing framework relied on diverse funding sources, multi-tiered allocation mechanisms, and distinct institutional structures. This complex architecture underscores the critical role of public financing in healthcare and public health emergency responses—specifically regarding how fiscal resources are mobilized, allocated, and disbursed to purchase services. To date, however, empirical research evaluating Thailand’s public health financing during the pandemic remains limited. For instance, while Sachdev et al. [11] analyzed the adaptation of public financial management during the crisis, comprehensive investigations into COVID-19 health expenditures, budget allocation dynamics, institutional decision-making mechanisms, and the subsequent impact on hospital financial sustainability remain sparse.

To address this knowledge gap, this study develops an analytical framework based on health financing functions [12] and health financing resilience concept [8] to examine how Thailand’s pre-existing UHC financing architecture enabled or constrained the pandemic response. Specifically, this study examines Thailand’s public health financing arrangements and their subsequent impacts on public hospital financial reserves during the pandemic. Public health financing arrangements are defined herein as government budget allocations, Emergency Loan Decrees, and social insurance contributions, while household out-of-pocket expenditures and private-sector healthcare spending are excluded.

Drawing on Thailand’s experience as a middle-income nation with a mature UHC framework, these findings offer valuable empirical insights for other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) seeking to assess their pandemic responses or strengthen public health financing resilience against future public health emergencies.

Methods

Study design

This study utilized a mixed-methods research design, structured across four distinct sequential phases:

(i) Framework development

First, a comprehensive literature review was conducted from June to September 2022 to develop an analytical framework, integrating health financing functions [12] with health financing resilience framework [8] to examine Thailand’s public health financing arrangements during the pandemic. Primary data sources comprised government budgetary documents, peer-reviewed articles, and grey literature. National administrative records and policy announcements were retrieved directly from the official portals of relevant institutional stakeholders, including the Ministry of Public Health (MOPH), the National Health Security Office (NHSO), the Social Security Office (SSO), the Budget Bureau, and the Comptroller General’s Department (CGD). Concurrently, international policy documents and academic literature were systematically compiled from global health repositories, such as the World Health Organization, and electronic databases, including PubMed and Scopus. This preliminary framework was subsequently refined and validated through a consultative stakeholder workshop.

(ii) Quantitative study

Data collection and analysis

A secondary analysis of the quantitative data was performed by the authors using Microsoft Excel.

COVID-19 public health expenditures

Quantitative expenditure data for Fiscal Years (FY) 2020–2022 were consolidated from multiple public financing institutions. These included expenditure records from the three primary public health insurance agencies—the NHSO, SSO, and CGD—alongside budgetary allocations under the Emergency Loan Decree and the Central Budget approved by the Cabinet. To evaluate resource distribution, the allocated and utilized funds were categorized into seven functional domains, adapted from the World Health Organization technical note on tracking COVID-19 health expenditures [13]: COVID-19 health services, vaccine procurement and management, health workers’ compensation, medicines and medical equipment, quarantine operations, research and development, and auxiliary pandemic-related expenses.

Public hospital financial reserves

Historical financial data for FY 2015–2022 were extracted from the official annual financial statements submitted by MOPH hospitals to the Office of the Permanent Secretary (OPS), MOPH. To capture baseline pre-pandemic operations and the active pandemic period, a trend analysis was conducted using descriptive statistics over eight-year period (FY 2015–2022). As of FY 2022, 96.3% (866/899) of MOPH hospitals had complete financial records across the entire study period. Hospitals with incomplete records across more than one fiscal year were excluded from the analysis (n = 33; 3.7%).

The dataset consolidated 103 distinct accounting line items. These variables were cross-examined and stratified into two primary financial components. First, total hospital reserve revenue (comprising 14 line items) encompassed routine, daily operational revenues allocated directly to the hospitals’ reserved funds, including patient service fees, reimbursements from public health insurance agencies, and philanthropic donations. Second, operational liabilities (comprising 89 line items) encompassed short-term and operational financial obligations incurred during routine healthcare activities. These obligations included salaries and compensation for temporary and MOPH personnel, professional and clinical allowances for medical, public health, and forensic staff, as well as procurement costs for medical supplies, pharmaceutical inventory, and operational equipment. The net hospital reserve balance was subsequently calculated as the difference between total hospital reserve revenue and total operational liabilities. To further explore whether these pandemic-induced fiscal impacts varied by institutional scale, MOPH hospitals experiencing a decline in net reserves were stratified according to bed capacity.

(iii) Qualitative study

Data collection and analysis

Qualitative data were collected between September and November 2022 from 21 key stakeholders involved in Thailand’s public health financing policy response during the pandemic. The sample included representatives from the Emergency Loan Decree Committee and Subcommittee, the CCSA, the MOPH, the Budget Bureau, Non-Governmental Organization, academic institutions, and other key constituencies. Notably, the Emergency Loan Decree Committee and Subcommittee comprised stakeholders from various public and external institutions (Table 1). Participants were initially selected using purposive sampling based on an analytical framework and their direct involvement in public health financing decisions during the pandemic, including representations from all major public financing institutions. This was supplemented by subsequent snowball sampling guided by recommendations from key informants and the research team. Participants were contacted via telephone or email to explain the research objectives and formally invite their participation. Upon agreeing to participate, respondents were required to review the study information sheet and provide written informed consent prior to data collection. Semi-structured interviews were conducted by the research team either in person or remotely, with durations ranging from 30 to 90 min.

With participants’ informed consent, qualitative interview data were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and anonymized. The data were subsequently analyzed using thematic analysis guided by the study’s analytical framework. Coding was performed manually by the research team using a structured Microsoft Excel template. An extraction matrix was developed based on pre-identified categories, incorporating emergent codes and themes to balance deductive and inductive analytical approaches. Data triangulation was achieved by cross-examining information from diverse stakeholder perspectives. The final themes and codes reflected the public health financing arrangement, focusing explicitly on revenue mobilization, budget allocation and purchasing, institutional decision-making mechanisms, and the subsequent impacts on hospital financial sustainability.

(iv) Data synthesis

The qualitative and quantitative findings were then triangulated, integrated, and synthesized to ensure the robustness and comprehensiveness of the overall results.

(v) Stakeholders meeting

The preliminary findings were presented during a consultative stakeholder workshop to validate the empirical results and formulate evidence-based policy recommendations.

Results

The analytical framework developed for this study (Fig. 1) systematically examines Thailand’s public health financing architecture during and immediately following the pandemic, spanning FY 2020–2022 (defined as October 1 to September 30 of the consecutive calendar year). The empirical analysis specifically targets three core pillars. The first pillar, revenue mobilization, involves the diversification of fiscal resources from both public and private sectors to counter the public health emergency. Public financing mechanisms primarily comprised the Government Central Budget and the regular Annual Budget. To expand the national fiscal space, the government heavily relied on extrabudgetary funds authorized under the Emergency Loan Decrees. Consequently, the primary pillars of public health financing for the national COVID-19 response consisted of this Emergency Loan Decree funds, the Government Central Budget, and social insurance contributions via the Social Security Fund (SSF).

The second pillar centers on resource allocation and purchasing mechanisms, which were governed by two distinct institutional bodies. The Emergency Loan Decree funds were managed by the Emergency Loan Decree Committee—a body composed of senior government officials from relevant line ministries, public financing institutions, and independent academic experts. Conversely, the Central Budget was administered directly by the Budget Bureau under the Office of the Prime Minister. Both financing pools were strategically allocated to the MOPH, the NHSO, and other relevant institutional stakeholders. These funds supported COVID-19 health services, vaccine procurement and management, state quarantine protocols, medical equipment acquisition, and financial compensation for frontline healthcare personnel. Concurrently, the CGD and SSO functioned as distinct purchasing mechanisms specifically dedicated to financing COVID-19 treatment for their beneficiaries.

The third pillar examines the downstream impacts on hospital financial stability. The Emergency Loan Decree funds were channeled to the public hospitals by the UCS, while the CSMBS and SSS utilized funds from the Central Budget and SSF, respectively. These coordinated financial mechanisms were utilized to systematically reimburse healthcare providers for pandemic-related medical interventions. Ultimately, these targeted expenditures primarily flowed into public sector hospitals, which served as major healthcare providers during the pandemic response.

Revenue mobilization for pandemic response

Thailand mobilized both pre-existing budgetary allocations and extrabudgetary funds to finance the medical and public health response to the COVID-19 pandemic between FY 2020 and 2022. Pre-existing funds were sourced from the government via the Central Budget and from compulsory social insurance via the SSF, while supplementary financing was secured through the Emergency Loan Decree funds. At the onset of the pandemic in March 2020, the FY 2020 budget (spanning October 2019 to September 2020) had already been enacted. Consequently, the government deployed its emergency reserve budget within the Central Budget—typically earmarked for annual national contingencies—to address immediate healthcare demands. However, the multi-dimensional crisis rapidly exhausted the capacity of the Central Budget.

To expand the national fiscal space, the government authorized borrowing through two Emergency Decrees enacted in April 2020 and May 2021. This Emergency Loan Decree fund was explicitly earmarked for public health response and macroeconomic recovery initiatives. Furthermore, the government restructured the FY 2020 budget by reallocating unexpended funds from non-essential line items—such as study visits and administrative meetings—directly into the Central Budget for pandemic mitigation [14]. In subsequent fiscal cycles (FY 2021 and FY 2022), the Central Budget explicitly designated allocations for pandemic-related expenditures.

Cumulative public health expenditure for the COVID-19 response during FY 2020–2022 totaled approximately US$13.78 billion. The Emergency Loan Decree Fund (65%) and the Central Budget (19%) predominantly driven public health financing. Conversely, social insurance contributions via the SSF accounted for the remaining 16% of total expenditures, exclusively covering COVID-19 health services for its insured members (Fig. 2).

Public health expenditures for the COVID-19 response amounted to US$479 million in FY 2020 and US$5.3 billion in FY 2021. The peak expenditure occurred in FY 2022, totaling US$7.9 billion, which is equivalent to 54% of the baseline current health expenditures recorded by public financing agencies in pre-pandemic 2019 [10].

Resource allocation and health service purchasing during the pandemic

Within the MOPH, multiple departments managed fund disbursements to address various facets of the COVID-19 response. The DDC allocated financial resources for vaccine procurement and management, including distribution logistics and rollout operations. Concurrently, the OPS funded health workers’ compensation and supported COVID-19 health services for non-Thai populations. The Department of Health Service Support administered risk-related remuneration for village health volunteers. Notably, these compensation payments supplemented standard salaries to provide financial recognition for frontline workers managing the pandemic and delivering essential services. Meanwhile, the MOD financed state quarantine facilities.

The three primary public health financing schemes—the CSMBS, the SSS, and the UCS—disbursed funds to healthcare providers delivering COVID-19 treatment to their respective beneficiaries. Notably, the NHSO financed population-wide COVID-19 testing and vaccination for all Thais citizens, whereas the SSO funded these services exclusively for its insured persons. To enhance surge capacity and expand service access, particularly within Bangkok, private healthcare providers not previously contracted under these schemes were enlisted. Concurrently, all three public health financing mechanisms expanded service coverage and enhanced reimbursement rates. This adjusted benefit framework comprised four core components: (i) outpatient care, covering additional fees for diagnostic laboratory tests (RT-PCR, antibody, and antigen), swab collections, therapeutics, patient referrals, and sanitation protocols; (ii) inpatient care, utilizing the Diagnosis-Related Group system supplemented by auxiliary fees for laboratory testing, medication, PPE, room and board, referrals, and decontamination; (iii) community isolation services, reimbursed via a predefined fee schedule; and (iv) home isolation services, similarly compensated through a standardized fee schedule. These benefit packages were dynamically adjusted to match the evolving trajectory of the pandemic. To incentivize private sector participation, reimbursement rates were set higher than standard, non-pandemic baselines. Financially, the NHSO leveraged the Emergency Loan Decree Funds to finance testing, vaccination, vaccine-related injury compensation, and UCS beneficiary treatment. Conversely, the SSS and CSMBS utilized their pre-existing budgetary sources —the SSF and the Central Budget, respectively—to fund treatment for their insured persons and beneficiaries.

The functional allocation of the total COVID-19 budget across all public financing sources is illustrated in Fig. 3. COVID-19 health services—comprising treatment, testing, and vaccination—accounted for the largest share of public expenditure at 59%. This was followed by vaccine procurement and logistics management at 18%, and healthcare workers’ compensation at 12%.

Although both the Central Budget and the Emergency Loan Decree funds primarily financed healthcare and public health responses, they operated through distinct pooling mechanisms and decision-making processes, as detailed in Table 2. Management of the Emergency Loan Decree funds fell under the purview of a newly established governing body, the Emergency Loan Decree Committee. To evaluate public health initiatives, this committee formed a specialized subcommittee tasked with reviewing project proposals and formulating technical recommendations for executive approval. This subcommittee comprised representatives from key public financial policy institutions—including the Office of the National Economic and Social Development Council, the Budget Bureau, the CGD, the Fiscal Policy Office, and the Public Debt Management Office—alongside the Office of the Council of State, the Office of the Permanent Secretary, Prime Minister Office and independent academics. Although the government sought to integrate civil society representatives into this subcommittee, these efforts were ultimately unsuccessful.

Institutional decision-making mechanisms for Central Budget and Emergency Loan Decree fund allocations

The MOPH served a pivotal role in coordinating and prioritizing project proposals submitted by public institutions prior to their submission to the Emergency Loan Decree Subcommittee. The primary evaluation criteria employed by the Subcommittee required that: (i) project objectives directly address immediate public health response needs; (ii) proposed activities could not be financed through regular budgetary allocations; (iii) project implementation reflect a critical level of urgency; and (iv) all project components be completed within the explicit timeframe mandated by the Emergency Loan Decrees.

In contrast, the Central Budget for the COVID-19 response was administered through pre-existing decision-making mechanisms managed by the Budget Bureau. This allocation process adhered to a hierarchical structure identical to the pre-pandemic framework for standard budgetary requests. Public institutions submitted project proposals to the Budget Bureau, where submissions were categorized according to their total funding requirements. Proposals under US$ 313,175 were approved directly by the Budget Bureau, whereas those exceeding this threshold required executive approval from either the Prime Minister or the Cabinet.

As illustrated in Fig. 4, fund disbursement velocities diverged sharply between the two fiscal governance mechanisms. The Central Budget exhibited high responsiveness during the initial phases of the pandemic in FY 2020; conversely, allocations from the Emergency Loan Decree funds were significantly delayed and backloaded; approximately US$ 6.3 billion, representing 71.2% of its total allocation, was disbursed during the post-acute phase of the pandemic in FY 2022.

This variance in disbursement efficiency underscores a fundamental principle of public financial management (PFM): mechanism maturity directly dictates institutional absorptive capacity. The Central Budget operates within a deeply institutionalized fiscal architecture supported by over six decades of operational precedent in Thailand’s public sector. This institutional familiarity minimized administrative friction, thereby enabling public agencies to rapidly leverage existing budget-request channels during the initial onset of the crisis.

In contrast, the Emergency Loan Decree functioned as an entirely new, ad hoc financial mechanism. The widespread lack of institutional familiarity with its unique procedural guidelines and funding priorities resulted in a protracted, iterative appraisal cycle. This process was characterized by repetitive proposal revisions between requesting agencies and the Emergency Loan Decree Subcommittee. This administrative bottleneck was further exacerbated by institutional inertia and stringent compliance mandates. Crucially, compelling an emergency mechanism to adhere to standard, pre-pandemic procurement and auditing regulations severely undermined the fund’s overall disbursement efficiency.

Financial impacts on net public hospital reserves

As illustrated in Fig. 5, net hospital reserves exhibited a consistent downward trend during the pre-pandemic period from FY 2015 to 2019. Conversely, the pandemic period (FY 2020–2022) marked a sharp structural reversal, characterized by a substantial expansion in net hospital reserves. This fiscal growth was primarily catalyzed by the injection of Emergency Loan Decree fund allocations designated for COVID-19 health services and modified provider reimbursement rates, both of which escalated significantly in FY 2022. By the conclusion of FY 2022, net hospital reserves reached US$ 2,165 million, representing an approximate sevenfold increase relative to FY 2019 baselines.

While aggregate figures indicated a macro-level financial recovery, a stratified analysis of the FY 2019–2022 dataset by bed capacity revealed pronounced structural disparities in net hospital reserves. Notably, an institutional tipping point emerged at the 120-bed threshold: over 50% of high-capacity facilities—encompassing tertiary hospitals (Levels A, S, and M1) and large secondary hospitals (Level M2)—experienced a significant contraction in net reserves. Conversely, smaller hospitals demonstrated greater baseline resilience, with reserve erosion restricted to approximately one-third of lower-tier secondary institutions (Levels F1, F2, and F3) (Supplementary). These findings highlight an inverse relationship between bed capacity and the stability of net hospital reserves. These disparities reflect asymmetric shocks to resource utilization during the pandemic; while the vast operational scale of tertiary and large secondary institutions forced them to incur substantial upfront expenditures, this heightened spending— coupled with administrative delays in Emergency Loan Decree fund disbursements—accelerated their reserve depletion. Consequently, as of the conclusion of FY 2022, full reimbursement to these MOPH facilities remained unrealized.

Discussion

This study establishes an analytical framework to examine Thailand’s COVID-19 public health financing arrangements and their subsequent impacts on public hospital reserves. The findings demonstrate that health financing resilience was maintained through a dual approach combining structural revenue design with temporary, ad hoc shock-absorption mechanisms during the pandemic crisis.

Specifically, Thailand enhanced its public health financing resilience by dynamically expanding its budgetary space. This was achieved through a strategic combination of adaptive resource mobilization within the pre-existing Central Budget and SSF, and the creation of an extra-budgetary financing framework via the Emergency Loan Decrees, which secured vital additional resources for the national COVID-19 response. This dual-track strategy aligns closely with fiscal interventions adopted in countries such as Nigeria, India, and South Africa, where emergency loans were similarly mobilized to finance large-scale pandemic containment and response measures [6].

Concurrently, Thailand leveraged its well-established public health financing mechanisms under the UHC system to rapidly expand COVID-19 service coverage and facilitate provider payments. Similar structural adaptations were observed in countries such as China, Germany, and the United Kingdom, where COVID-19 health services were entirely financed through public funds and provided to patients free of charge [6, 15].

Our findings extend the literature on fiscal space for health during emergencies. While previous studies emphasized domestic revenue mobilization and budgetary reallocation [16, 17], Thailand’s experience highlights the trade-offs between extra-budgetary borrowing (Emergency Loan Decrees) and reliance on hospital-level reserves. The latter acted as a de facto buffer, but this strategy is not sustainable for future shocks of longer duration. Compared with Vietnam and Indonesia—where similar delays occurred—Thailand’s UHC infrastructure mitigated but did not eliminate financing friction.

The Emergency Loan Decree Fund was established outside the regular PFM framework, operating under separate financial arrangements and institutional governance. In practice, however, its execution remained largely constrained by existing PFM processes. The fund continued to rely on traditional, top-down bureaucratic decision-making procedures, while standard procurement rules and auditing regulations remained unaltered. Furthermore, as a newly introduced governance mechanism, a widespread lack of institutional familiarity with fund request guidelines contributed to protracted administrative delays. Consequently, the Emergency Loan Decree Fund’s decision-making and disbursement processes proved less efficient than intended. These delays hindered rapid resource mobilization during the initial phases of the pandemic—a critical window for containing early disease transmission.

Despite the administrative delays in Emergency Loan Decree fund disbursements, MOPH hospitals—which constitute the majority of public healthcare providers— absorbed the fiscal shock by mobilizing their own financial reserves to sustain essential COVID-19 services. This provider-level operational flexibility was structurally reinforced by system-level stability. Specifically, fixed capitation prepayments for routine outpatient services under the UCS and SSS remained unchanged, while standard clinical operational costs dropped as non-COVID-19 service utilization declined by approximately 20% [18]. Ultimately, this strategic mobilization of hospital reserves, the preservation of fixed revenue streams, and the concomitant reduction in non-COVID-19 operational expenditures collectively counterbalanced the disbursement delays, thereby stabilizing hospital cash flows throughout the crisis. Consequently, Thailand’s public health financing architecture demonstrated robust macro-fiscal resilience, as evidenced by the substantial net increase in cumulative MOPH hospital reserves by the conclusion of FY 2022.

The findings underscore critical structural gaps within Thailand’s public health financing framework that must be addressed to strengthen health financing resilience against future pandemics. Due to the absence of a dedicated national health reserve, Thailand relied heavily on public borrowing to expand its fiscal space during the COVID-19 pandemic. While this approach enabled the mobilization of substantial emergency resources, it inadvertently delayed access to funds during the critical initial phases of the crisis and generated long-term fiscal burdens through interest obligations and elevated public debt. To enhance preparedness for future health emergencies, policymakers should evaluate the structural feasibility of establishing a dedicated national reserve or contingency fund earmarked for health crises. Evidence from nations such as Iran, Germany, and Singapore indicates that the immediate availability of predefined reserve funds effectively facilitates the rapid, frictionless allocation of financial resources during the initial onset of a pandemic [6].

Additionally, the operational efficiency of extra-budgetary financing mechanisms can be enhanced by establishing standardized operating procedures, including pre-specified fast-track spending authorization processes [19], while ensuring institutional readiness for rapid fund acquisition across public agencies [20]. Such adaptive structural measures would significantly improve the timeliness and velocity of fund disbursements during public health emergencies. Concurrently, procurement regulations should incorporate flexible frameworks that enable the expedited acquisition of vaccines, therapeutics, medical equipment, and critical infrastructure while maintaining rigorous accountability and independent oversight [20]. Similar administrative approaches were adopted in India, Spain, and Vietnam, where procurement requirements, bureaucratic procedures, and approval processes were streamlined to accelerate pandemic-related expenditures and fund mobilization [6]. Collectively, these institutional reforms would strengthen the healthcare system’s adaptive capacity by enabling the rapid mobilization, allocation, and deployment of financial resources in response to future systemic shocks.

This study has several operational limitations. First, estimates of COVID-19 public health expenditure were based primarily on approved budgetary allocations, whereas actual expenditures may have occurred former due to the aforementioned delays in fund disbursement; consequently, the reported spending figures may not fully reflect the exact chronology of resource utilization. Second, data were compiled from multiple disparate sources with differing reporting frameworks—including both fiscal-year and calendar-year accounting systems—which may have introduced minor temporal misalignments in the analysis, as expenditures recorded in FY 2022 may have been physically incurred during FY 2021. Third, key informants were interviewed in their official capacities as representatives of their respective institutions; as a result, their perspectives may reflect institutional priorities or political sensitivities. Finally, the analysis focused on public-sector financing and was constrained by limited data regarding household out-of-pocket expenditures and private-sector healthcare spending during the pandemic. Consequently, the scope of these findings is restricted to fiscal management; the implications do not extend to broader service delivery metrics (e.g., healthcare access and quality of care) or population health outcomes (e.g., mortality, morbidity). Future research incorporating these private financing streams would provide a more holistic assessment of the comprehensive macroeconomic response to COVID-19. The downstream impacts of public hospital resource strains on healthcare access, quality of care, and population health outcomes —for both COVID-19 and essential health services —should be further investigated. In addition, the fiscal impacts of Government Loan Decrees on post-pandemic public health financing warrant further exploration. Despite these constraints, this study offers valuable, empirical insights into Thailand’s public health financing architecture and the structural mechanisms leveraged to mobilize and allocate public resources during a major health crisis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Thailand strategically mobilized pre-existing and extra-budgetary funding streams to secure adequate fiscal space for its pandemic response. Despite administrative delays in Emergency Loan Decree disbursements, the institutional maturity of universal health coverage infrastructures and the strategic deployment of hospital reserves collectively sustained systemic health financing resilience. Our findings point to the need to establish a dedicated health emergency reserve fund, streamline extra-budgetary mechanisms, adopt pre-specified fast-track spending authorization processes and flexible procurement regulations. These would enhance adaptive capacity and strengthen public health financing resilience during the public health emergencies.
