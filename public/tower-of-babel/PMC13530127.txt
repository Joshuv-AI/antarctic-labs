A decision-making framework integrating energy efficiency and climate resilience: evidence from ASEAN economies
Authors: Tri Dung Dang, Hoang-Kha Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhancing energy efficiency while strengthening climate resilience has become a pivotal challenge for ASEAN economies exposed to rising energy demand and intensifying climate risks. Although previous studies have examined energy efficiency, renewable energy transition, and climate vulnerability, limited research has jointly integrated energy efficiency and climate resilience within a unified decision-making framework for ASEAN economies. This study addresses this gap by developing an integrated decision-support framework that combines the CRITIC objective weighting method and the CRADIS multi-criteria ranking approach. By tracking 10 ASEAN economies across various energy and environmental dimensions, this model provides insight into balancing short-term economic benefits with the long-term resilience of the energy system. The results reveal pronounced heterogeneity across countries. The Philippines achieves the highest integrated performance, followed by Myanmar and Singapore, while Brunei, Malaysia, and Laos show wider gaps from the ideal benchmark. The CRITIC results indicate that climate risk, policy readiness, renewable energy transition, and efficiency-related investment are key factors differentiating ASEAN economies. Sensitivity analysis using alternative MCDM models, including VIKOR, TOPSIS, WASPAS, and ARAS, supports the robustness of the CRITIC–CRADIS framework. The findings contribute theoretically by embedding climate resilience into energy-efficiency assessment and empirically by offering a transparent, replicable, and data-driven tool for country-level prioritization. From a policy perspective, the results suggest that ASEAN economies should adopt differentiated strategies, including renewable energy expansion, grid modernization, energy-efficiency standards, climate-risk screening, green finance, and regional coordination to support climate-resilient energy transitions.

Introduction

Enhancing energy efficiency while simultaneously strengthening climate resilience has emerged as a defining challenge for sustainable development, particularly within rapidly growing economies such as those of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN). The dual imperatives of reducing energy intensity and adapting to intensifying climate risks are now central to energy policy and planning worldwide. Recent studies have underscored that the global energy crisis, triggered in 2021 and exacerbated by Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, has profoundly reshaped the energy landscape by forcing nations to prioritize efficiency as both an economic necessity and an environmental imperative1. Chen et al. (2024) demonstrated that improving energy efficiency can account for approximately 40% of potential greenhouse gas emission reductions while sustaining economic growth2. At the same time, Osman et al., emphasized that fossil fuel–based energy still contributes more than 75% of global greenhouse gas emissions, reaffirming the urgency of transitioning toward clean, efficient, and resilient energy systems3.

ASEAN represents a particularly compelling case for such dual-focus analysis. Comprising ten countries with diverse energy systems, institutional capacities, and development trajectories, the region faces mounting pressures from rising energy demand, environmental degradation, and climate-induced disruptions. Safrina and Utama noted that ASEAN remains one of the world’s fastest-growing economic zones but must navigate the energy trilemma of security, equity, and sustainability4. The ASEAN Plan of Action for Energy Cooperation (APAEC) 2016–2025 established ambitious goals of achieving 23% renewable energy share and significant energy intensity reductions by 20255. However, Glemarec et al., highlighted that the region is simultaneously among the most energy-intensive and climate-vulnerable globally, with rapid urbanization and increasing exposure to extreme weather events threatening infrastructure stability6. Kurniawan et al., similarly stressed that recurrent heatwaves and El Niño events severely disrupt energy systems, affecting both generation and demand patterns7. Empirical evidence further indicates that climate change could reduce wind and hydropower production by up to 40% in some areas while amplifying cooling demand, thereby heightening stress on energy networks3. Therefore, ASEAN requires integrated evaluation tools that account not only for energy-system performance but also for climate-induced uncertainty and operational risks.

Policy commitments across ASEAN underscore this urgency. The region’s Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) under the Paris Agreement aim to align with the global temperature goal of limiting warming to below 2 °C. Nevertheless, Wang and Nhieu proposed a regional framework for renewable energy collaboration and found that while ASEAN’s solar and wind potential is substantial, existing planning systems insufficiently incorporate climate risk parameters8. De Marco et al., highlighted that conventional energy planning models underperform under climate uncertainty, as they are based on static assumptions and single-climate scenarios9. Gan et al., further noted that climate-induced extreme events are becoming more frequent and severe, introducing cascading vulnerabilities across interlinked infrastructure networks10. Sharma & Gupta highlight that cities in developing countries are highly exposed to climate risks due to structural vulnerabilities and inadequate infrastructure, underscoring the need for resilient planning and investment policies11. Park stresses that ASEAN’s economic future depends on coordinated policies in digitalization, infrastructure, energy connectivity, and FDI mobilization to enhance regional production systems and smart-city development12. Collectively, these studies show that although ASEAN has established strategic goals, gaps remain in analytical tools that simultaneously evaluate efficiency outcomes and resilience-building capacity.

Despite certain advances, current research still reveals fragmentation in three core areas. Primarily, energy efficiency and climate change adaptation are often viewed separately, rather than as two sides of a whole in sustainable planning. Furthermore, ASEAN studies have yet to reconcile the differences in system specificities and institutional capacities of individual countries into a common assessment framework. In particular, while the MCDM methodology is not new, building a data-driven decision-making system, where performance and resilience are integrated simultaneously, remains a significant gap. This is the impetus for this study to propose a more comprehensive integrated assessment framework.

Building on the identified research gap, this study is motivated by the urgent need to develop a comprehensive and data-driven framework that jointly evaluates energy efficiency and climate resilience in ASEAN economies. The region’s vulnerability to climate risks, coupled with its rising energy demand, underscores the necessity of integrated assessment tools capable of capturing the dual dimensions of performance and adaptability. To address this, the study integrates objective weighting and compromise-ranking approaches into a unified evaluation structure.

The core objective of this study is to construct and apply a hybrid CRITIC–CRADIS decision-making framework to assess the multidimensional performance of ASEAN economies in terms of energy efficiency and climate resilience. This approach enables the extraction of unbiased indicator weights based on data variability using the CRITIC (CRiteria Importance Through Intercriteria Correlation) method and the aggregation of results through a compromise-ranking logic using the CRADIS (Compromise Ranking of Alternatives from Distance to Ideal Solution) method. The CRADIS technique is specifically chosen for its ability to provide superior ranking discrimination by evaluating alternatives based on the normalized distance from the optimum (ideal) solution and the anti-optimum solution simultaneously, thereby yielding a highly balanced measure of compromise. The proposed framework not only enhances methodological transparency but also provides a balanced evaluation that reflects the interconnected nature of economic, environmental, and adaptive factors.

Through this integrated framework, the study contributes to advancing sustainability assessment in three main ways. First, it introduces an empirically grounded decision model that minimizes subjectivity in indicator weighting. Second, it integrates energy efficiency and climate resilience into a single decision-making framework, thereby extending existing energy and climate assessment studies that often examine these dimensions separately. Third, it generates policy-relevant insights that support the design of coherent regional strategies for resilient and low-carbon development. Ultimately, the findings aim to inform ASEAN policymakers and energy planners by identifying priority areas for intervention and facilitating a more synergistic alignment between efficiency gains and climate adaptation goals.

Based on the issues outlined above, this study is structured to address three pivotal research questions. First, how can we build an integrated decision framework capable of simultaneously assessing both energy performance and climate resilience for ASEAN economies? Following that, the study delves into identifying which indicators play the most significant role in the multidimensional performance of countries in the region. Finally, the study seeks to apply ranking and sensitivity analysis results to shape policy priorities, thereby promoting a low-carbon and sustainable development path for the ASEAN region.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the literature review, with emphasis on energy efficiency, climate resilience, ASEAN sustainability transition, and MCDM-based sustainability assessment. Section 3 details the proposed hybrid CRITIC–CRADIS methodology, including model formulation, indicator weighting, ranking procedures, and data processing. Section 4 describes the ASEAN case study, including the indicator system and data sources. Section 5 presents numerical results, encompassing criteria weighting, integrated rankings, and sensitivity analysis. Section 6 discusses the findings and policy implications. Section 7 concludes by summarizing theoretical and practical contributions, outlining limitations, and suggesting avenues for future research.

Literature review

To provide a more critical and thematic synthesis, this section reviews prior studies around two main themes. The first theme concerns the relationship between energy efficiency, climate resilience, and ASEAN’s sustainability transition. The second theme focuses on MCDM-based sustainability assessment and the methodological gap addressed by the proposed CRITIC–CRADIS framework. This structure helps move beyond descriptive summaries of individual studies and clarifies how the existing literature supports the development of the present study.

Energy efficiency, climate resilience, and ASEAN sustainability transition

Growing scholarly attention has reinforced the critical role of technological innovation and evidence-based decision-making in advancing energy efficiency strategies. Shree et al., highlighted the potential of phase-change material–integrated building envelopes to enhance thermal regulation and reduce energy consumption, emphasizing the role of advanced materials in improving energy efficiency in built environments13. Likewise, Nhieu and Dang employed an objective weighting–based multi-criteria approach to prioritize marine energy technologies for Vietnam, demonstrating how data-driven frameworks can support strategic renewable energy planning in emerging economies14. Recent advances emphasize the importance of incorporating behavioral economics into energy evaluation frameworks, as demonstrated by Wang et al., who integrate Prospect Theory with DEA to capture investor risk perception in renewable investment decisions15. These developments indicate that energy efficiency improvement is not only a technical issue but also a decision-making problem that requires reliable evaluation tools, technology-sensitive assessment, and policy-relevant prioritization.

In parallel, scholarly attention toward energy efficiency evaluation has expanded rapidly. Shu et al., applied a super-efficiency SBM-DEA model across 168 economies and revealed persistent disparities in energy efficiency levels, particularly between developed and developing countries16. Nevskaya et al. identified that conventional assessments often treat socioeconomic, technological, and environmental factors in isolation, neglecting their interdependence17. Within ASEAN, Famhim et al., emphasized that energy transition pathways depend critically on policy coherence, investment structures, and diversification of electricity sources5. Despite this progress, most studies evaluate efficiency within static environmental contexts, overlooking the dynamic interactions between climate variability and energy performance. Consequently, energy-system assessments risk overstating efficiency gains and understating long-term vulnerability to climate shocks. Liu and Lin found that regional trade integration combined with renewable energy expansion can significantly reduce resource consumption footprints in Asia, provided policy support is consistent and robust18. This stream of literature therefore establishes the importance of energy efficiency and renewable energy transition, but it also reveals a limitation: efficiency-oriented assessments often do not sufficiently incorporate climate-induced uncertainty and resilience capacity.

Recent evidence further confirms that energy efficiency, renewable energy, institutional quality, and policy design play central roles in reducing environmental pressure. Haseeb et al. show that energy efficiency significantly reduces CO₂ emissions in both the short and long run, supporting the view that efficiency improvement can serve as an effective mitigation strategy19. Similarly, Zhou et al. find that growth in energy efficiency improvement reduces both total and per capita carbon emission growth rates, while renewable energy use and institutional quality jointly contribute to emission-reduction outcomes20. Wang et al. further demonstrate that environmental regulation can support green economy development through green technology innovation, although the effects of regulation may differ across development contexts and sectors21. Huang et al. also shows that pro-environmental technologies, energy efficiency, and environmental policy stringency accelerate energy transition, whereas energy depletion alone does not automatically induce renewable energy penetration22. These studies reinforce the argument that energy efficiency and energy transition outcomes depend not only on technological progress but also on regulatory quality, institutional readiness, and policy design.

Another related stream highlights the importance of finance, foreign investment, governance, uncertainty, and natural-resource conditions in shaping sustainability outcomes. Haseeb et al. find that outward foreign direct investment, governance quality, and green transition initiatives enhance renewable energy penetration, whereas rapid industrial growth and urban expansion may hinder it23. In contrast, geopolitical risk, economic policy uncertainty, trade policy uncertainty, and financial development can increase greenhouse gas emissions under certain conditions, suggesting that financial and geopolitical factors may either support or constrain low-carbon transition depending on their direction and institutional context24. Evidence from Saudi Arabia further shows that economic policy uncertainty, economic growth, foreign direct investment, and natural resource rents exert asymmetric effects on ecological footprint, highlighting the nonlinear nature of sustainability transitions25. Qing et al. similarly report that technological innovation, forest area, and renewable energy improve environmental sustainability, while natural resources and globalization may increase ecological footprint in South Asian economies26. Collectively, these studies show that sustainability performance is shaped by a complex interaction of energy efficiency, renewable energy, regulation, finance, governance, uncertainty, and natural-resource dynamics. However, most of this evidence focuses on individual environmental outcomes rather than an integrated decision-making framework that jointly evaluates energy efficiency and climate resilience.

Complementary research has explored the implications of climate change for energy system performance. Ekechukwu et al., reviewed renewable energy integration under climate resilience considerations, highlighting challenges related to intermittency, grid stability, and socioeconomic vulnerability27. The challenge of resilience extends beyond energy infrastructure itself. In a broader context of essential public services, Mosadeghrad et al., conducted a scoping review that identified 87 key actions across six core themes (including governance, financing, and service delivery) necessary to strengthen the resilience of health systems to climate threats28. Similar to the healthcare sector, energy systems constitute vital public infrastructure and face systemic adaptation challenges demanding multi-sectoral strategies in governance, investment, and planning. Wang et al., revealed that exposure to extreme heat increased by 52.5% in urbanized regions over two decades, underscoring the accelerating risks to human and energy system health29. These insights emphasize that resilience should be treated as a core efficiency-enhancing factor rather than a secondary planning objective.

Research on climate-resilient infrastructure and adaptive management further reinforces this integrated perspective. Mukherjee et al., found that poor infrastructure quality and limited innovation capacity are key drivers of vulnerability in South Asia30. Johnstone shows that ASEAN countries demonstrate mixed “norm entrepreneurship” capabilities, where both supportive and resistive governance behaviors coexist, highlighting the need for deeper intra-regional and external cooperation31. However, translating conceptual resilience strategies into quantitative models remains underdeveloped, particularly in diverse regional contexts such as ASEAN. This limitation is important because resilience is often discussed conceptually, while fewer studies operationalize it within quantitative decision-making frameworks that can compare national performance and support policy prioritization.

The policy shifts within ASEAN are undeniable, but looking back at the research, there remains a considerable gap in translating common goals into a specific measurement system. Instead of focusing solely on familiar topics like infrastructure connectivity or energy cooperation, we need more compelling evidence of each nation’s capabilities across a range of areas: from its ability to adapt to climate change to its policy capacity. In a region where development levels are so disparate, the tendency to equate or the lack of benchmarking indicators will obscure the unique strengths and weaknesses of each area. Therefore, a multi-dimensional assessment framework will be key to accurately positioning each economy in this transition race.

MCDM-based sustainability assessment and research gap

Addressing such complexity demands robust decision-making methodologies. Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) has gained recognition as an essential analytical paradigm for resolving trade-offs among competing objectives. As affirmed by the comprehensive survey of Taherdoost & Madanchian, MCDM methods and concepts are widely applicable across diverse fields, offering manifold tools to determine the best alternative when considering more than one criterion32. Within the sustainability domain, MCDM is confirmed as a dominant analytical approach for climate-aligned planning33. However, conventional MCDM applications often struggle with two critical limitations: subjective weighting and insufficient sensitivity to data characteristics. In healthcare, Chakraborty et al. provided a systematic analysis of 140 MCDM studies and identified AHP as the most frequently applied tool, emphasizing the growing complexity and multi-stakeholder nature of decision processes in real-world systems34. To address the inherent subjectivity of AHP and similar methods, recent research has focused on expanding methodological frontiers by reviewing and applying objective weighting methods such as CILOS, FUCOM, LBWA, SAPEVO-M, and MEREC, which aim to enhance robustness in criteria importance assessment35. Furthermore, the necessity for robust testing in decision-support systems is highlighted by Demir et al., who found that only 9.457% of MCDM studies applied sensitivity analysis, revealing a significant methodological gap36. These studies justify the need for objective weighting and robustness-oriented assessment when evaluating complex sustainability problems involving multiple, interdependent indicators.

Hybrid and data-driven MCDM approaches are thus expanding rapidly, particularly those combining objective weighting with sophisticated ranking techniques. Khan and Ayza integrated ENTROPY and CRITIC with machine learning for groundwater quality modeling, demonstrating the efficiency of objective weighting in uncertainty-rich environments37. Maruf and Özdemir employed CRITIC-MAIRCA to evaluate digital service platforms, identifying customer support variables as decisive criteria38. Similarly, Wang et al. applied a Pythagorean-fuzzy CRITIC-MARCOS model to sustainable food supplier evaluation under uncertainty39, while Yu et al. combined AHP-CRITIC-TOPSIS to capture young consumer preferences in sustainable product design40. For urban sustainability, Khan et al. developed a spherical fuzzy CRITIC-WASPAS model to evaluate resilient urban development alternatives41. These applications demonstrate that CRITIC-based weighting is increasingly used to reduce subjectivity and capture information embedded in data variability and inter-criteria relationships.

CRADIS-based techniques have also evolved: Işık and Adalar introduced intuitionistic fuzzy CRADIS for ESG assessment in insurance42; Aytekin et al. proposed neutrosophic SWARA-CRADIS for global market entry strategies43; Qiu et al. extended CRADIS using probabilistic linguistic term sets for sports supply chain risk management44; and Chatterjee and Seikh enhanced CRADIS through triangular divergence distance for interval neutrosophic group decisions45. Advancements further extend to construction digitalization with Farid et al. applying disc-intuitionistic CRADIS for Web 4.0 adoption in logistics46. Kizielewicz et al., additionally developed the pymcdm Python library to support standardized, scalable, and reproducible MCDM analysis across disciplines47. D’Agostino et al., proposed a hybrid MCDM framework for thermal insulation decisions under conflicting stakeholder interests, demonstrating the role of compromise-based evaluation in sustainability applications48. This body of research confirms the growing relevance of compromise-ranking approaches, particularly when decision-makers must balance competing criteria and identify alternatives that are closest to ideal performance while farthest from undesirable outcomes.

The increasing adoption of hybrid and uncertainty-aware MCDM frameworks in sustainable planning is evidenced across various sectors. In resource management, Hasankhani et al. employed a combined SWOT–fuzzy game theory model to prioritize waste-to-energy strategies in Iran, emphasizing landfill gas recovery and incineration-based systems49. Similarly, Al-Gerafi et al. developed an inter-valued fuzzy COPRAS-EDAS-PIV approach for e-learning platform evaluation, underscoring its efficacy in handling pedagogical complexity under uncertainty50. Complementing this, the deployment of renewable energy is being optimized through integrated structures: Sharma & Sarin introduced an AHP-integrated hybrid MCDM model to identify suitable solar sites in Punjab, confirming high rank consensus across methods and highlighting the socio-economic relevance in energy site planning51. Dash et al. further applied PAPRIKA to assess wind energy solutions for rural regions, demonstrating the dominance of onshore systems given cost and infrastructure constraints52. Spatial decision paradigms continue to advance as Dere et al. combined FAHP and TOPSIS with GIS mapping to locate high-potential agrivoltaic areas in Türkiye, effectively balancing agricultural productivity and solar utilization53. Finally, Wang et al. utilized a MEREC-TOPSIS model to rank renewable energy sources in Vietnam, identifying hydropower as the leading alternative under national policy priorities54. Collectively, these studies show that hybrid MCDM approaches are effective for sustainability assessment, renewable energy planning, and complex decision environments. Nevertheless, most applications remain sector-specific or technology-specific, while fewer studies use MCDM to jointly evaluate energy efficiency and climate resilience at the country level.

Despite the methodological advances discussed above, a systematic MCDM application that jointly captures energy efficiency and climate resilience in ASEAN economies remains largely unexplored. This represents a methodological and regional research gap. Methodologically, existing MCDM studies have demonstrated the usefulness of objective weighting, compromise ranking, and sensitivity analysis, but their integration into an energy efficiency–climate resilience framework remains limited. Regionally, ASEAN economies require a decision-support framework that can account for diverse energy systems, climate vulnerabilities, and policy capacities. The present study addresses these gaps by developing a hybrid CRITIC–CRADIS framework that combines objective criteria weighting with compromise-based ranking to evaluate integrated energy–climate performance across ASEAN economies.

Based on this synthesis, the study expects that countries with stronger performance in energy efficiency, cleaner energy transition, institutional readiness, and climate-adaptive capacity will achieve higher integrated energy–climate performance. At the same time, because ASEAN economies differ in development level, infrastructure quality, and policy capacity, the relative importance of criteria may vary across countries. This expectation provides the conceptual basis for applying a data-driven MCDM framework that can identify indicator weights, rank alternatives, and support policy prioritization.

Methodology

This section presents the methodological framework used to evaluate the integrated energy efficiency–climate resilience performance of ASEAN economies. The proposed framework follows a hybrid Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) logic, in which ASEAN economies are treated as alternatives and energy efficiency–climate resilience indicators are treated as evaluation criteria. The methodological procedure consists of two main stages. First, the CRITIC method is used to determine objective criteria weights. Second, the CRADIS method is applied to rank ASEAN economies based on their weighted performance relative to ideal and anti-ideal solutions.

To objectively assess the integrated performance of ASEAN economies under increasing climate and energy system stress, this study adopts a hybrid MCDM framework that combines the CRITIC method with the CRADIS approach. CRITIC is an objective weighting method that assigns higher weights to criteria with stronger contrast intensity and lower redundancy. CRADIS is a compromise-ranking method that evaluates alternatives according to their distances from ideal and anti-ideal performance points. This hybridization enables a rigorous and transparent evaluation by addressing two fundamental methodological dimensions: (i) determining the relative importance of indicators based on intrinsic data characteristics, and (ii) ranking the performance of countries based on their proximity to the optimal efficiency–resilience benchmark.

The CRITIC method is employed to compute objective criteria weights by capturing both the contrast intensity (variance) and inter-criteria correlation, thus mitigating subjective bias and ensuring that criteria with higher discriminating power and lower redundancy receive greater importance55. This is particularly crucial in energy–climate assessments, where system performance is influenced by multiple interdependent technical, environmental, and socioeconomic indicators. Subsequently, the CRADIS method is applied to rank ASEAN economies according to a compromise solution that simultaneously considers positive and negative distances from ideal and anti-ideal performance points56. This approach ensures balanced evaluation that avoids dominance by single criteria and retains interpretability for policy analysis.

Combining the CRITIC and CRADIS models offers significant advantages for this study based on three core aspects. First, the CRITIC method helps eliminate the subjective barrier when determining weights, thanks to its ability to directly exploit the statistical structure from the data instead of relying on expert intuition. Building on that advantage, CRADIS establishes a transparent ranking process through parallel comparison between ideal and non-ideal solutions. Given the specific context of ASEAN, this analytical framework proves particularly effective in addressing the multi-objective challenges of energy and climate – where indicators frequently overlap or conflict with each other among member states. The integration of CRITIC and CRADIS therefore provides several methodological advantages: (i) objective derivation of criteria weights without reliance on expert judgement, (ii) robust treatment of conflicting and correlated indicators, and (iii) transparent efficiency-resilience ranking suitable for high-level policy evaluation. Case study

Case study description

This study focuses on the ten economies of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), including Brunei, Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, and Vietnam. ASEAN represents one of the most dy-namic and rapidly growing economic regions in the world, yet it remains highly vulnera-ble to climate change impacts and energy insecurity. The region’s accelerating industrial-ization and increasing energy consumption pose significant challenges to energy efficiency improvements and decarbonization efforts.

ASEAN economies exhibit diverse energy structures, development levels, and climate exposure profiles. For instance, Singapore demonstrates advanced energy management and policy frameworks, whereas other emerging economies such as Laos and Myanmar still face infrastructure limitations and slower energy transition progress. This heterogeneity provides a robust basis for comparative efficiency analysis and offers valuable in-sights for regional policy coordination.

The ASEAN case is particularly appropriate for the proposed CRITIC–CRADIS framework because the region combines strong cross-country differences in energy efficiency, renewable transition progress, climate exposure, infrastructure quality, and policy readiness. Such heterogeneity requires a multidimensional assessment approach rather than a single-indicator comparison. In this context, each ASEAN economy is treated as an alternative, while the selected energy efficiency and climate resilience indicators are treated as evaluation criteria.

Considering the strategic commitment of ASEAN to achieve net-zero emissions and enhance sustainable energy transition pathways, assessing energy efficiency under climate stress becomes essential. Therefore, this case study evaluates the relative performance of ASEAN countries using a multi-criteria decision-making framework integrating the CRITIC method for objective weighting and the CRADIS method for performance ranking.

Data collection description

The evaluation framework incorporates nine indicators reflecting energy system efficiency, renewable transition progress, climate resilience, and policy readiness. These indicators were rigorously selected based on their relevance to established energy sustainability indices, international climate-energy assessment frameworks, and confirmed data availability across the ten ASEAN member states. The data were sourced from multiple authoritative international and regional databases, including The World Bank57, Our World in Data58, the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA)59, the ASEAN Centre for Energy (ACE)60, The Global Risk Report61, and various national and sectoral reports, ensuring a high degree of credibility and comparability62,63.

To improve transparency and consistency, the nine indicators are classified according to their direction of preference. Benefit attributes are indicators for which higher values indicate better performance, while cost attributes are indicators for which lower values indicate better performance. This classification is necessary because the CRITIC–CRADIS framework requires all indicators to be interpreted consistently during normalization and ranking.

The comprehensive set of criteria, detailed in Table 1, is classified into four Cost Attributes (where lower values indicate better performance) and five Benefit Attributes (where higher values are desirable). The core efficiency metrics include Energy intensity (C2), which reflects the energy required to generate a unit of GDP, and Electricity losses in transmission & distribution (C3), a crucial measure of system infrastructure efficiency. The environmental sustainability dimension is captured by CO₂ emissions per capita (C6) and the primary resilience metric, the Climate Risk Index (C8), which quantifies the exposure of each economy to extreme weather events.

Complementing these are the benefit-oriented indicators reflecting transition and adaptive capacity. These include Share of renewable energy in total final consumption (C1) and Access to electricity (C7), both essential for achieving energy security and equity goals. The policy and innovation landscape is assessed through three key indicators: Government spending on energy efficiency (C4), the Energy efficiency policy score (C5) (representing the implementation index), and the Energy-related R&D or innovation index (C9). This integrated set ensures that the assessment moves beyond static efficiency measurements to account for dynamic factors of climate exposure, policy commitment, and technological development.

The raw decision matrix reveals significant structural and geographical heterogeneity across the ASEAN economies, as shown in Table 2. The large disparity between countries exhibiting minimal grid losses and those with high energy intensity and low renewable shares demonstrates the wide range of development stages and systemic vulnerabilities within the region. This substantial variation necessitates the application of a robust and objective multi-criteria evaluation methodology, such as the CRITIC–CRADIS framework, to accurately assess performance differentials and inform tailored policy recommendations.

Discussion and managerial implications

The empirical findings underscore substantial heterogeneity in the integrated performance of energy efficiency and climate resilience across ASEAN economies. The CRADIS results show that the Philippines, Myanmar, and Singapore form the leading group, although their performance profiles differ considerably. The Philippines achieves the highest integrated score due to its relatively strong combined performance in policy score, climate-risk-related normalized performance, innovation capacity, and selected energy-transition indicators. Myanmar performs well mainly because of its high renewable energy share, favorable emissions-related performance, and government spending on energy efficiency, although its lower electricity-access score indicates that its energy system still faces inclusiveness and infrastructure challenges. Singapore ranks highly because of strong electricity infrastructure efficiency, policy score, and universal electricity access, but its overall score is constrained by limited renewable energy share and climate-risk-related exposure.

These differentiated performance pathways show that high ranking does not necessarily reflect overall economic development or uniform superiority across all indicators. Instead, it reflects relative performance under the selected energy efficiency–climate resilience criteria. In contrast, economies including Brunei, Malaysia, and Laos exhibit lower composite scores, suggesting that they face wider gaps from the ideal integrated performance benchmark. These gaps may be associated with limited renewable energy penetration, weaker balance across policy and innovation indicators, higher exposure to climate-related risks, or insufficient alignment between energy efficiency and resilience-oriented planning. These disparities reinforce the notion that sustainable energy advancement in emerging economies is not merely determined by economic scale but rather by the coherence of regulatory frameworks, long-term strategic vision, and commitment to resilience-oriented energy governance.

The CRITIC-derived weights reveal that climate resilience indicators possess relatively higher influence than several conventional efficiency metrics, emphasizing the increasing relevance of climate adaptation considerations in energy transition strategies. In particular, the climate risk index receives the highest objective weight, indicating that climate-related vulnerability provides strong discriminatory information across ASEAN economies. This finding is important because it suggests that climate resilience should not be treated as a secondary concern after energy efficiency has been achieved. Rather, resilience conditions directly shape the long-term sustainability and reliability of energy systems. This aligns with the growing recognition in recent literature that climate-driven disruptions pose direct risks to energy systems and therefore must be explicitly integrated into evaluation and planning models.

The ranking outcomes obtained from the CRADIS method further illuminate the dynamic interplay between efficiency and resilience within developing economies. Specifically, the results demonstrate that economies with strong energy efficiency scores, but high climate vulnerability are appropriately penalized in the final ranking, reflecting the existential threats posed by climate-intensified events such as extreme heat, flooding, and water-resource fluctuations that directly threaten grid stability and renewable generation performance. This result supports the theoretical argument developed in the literature review: energy efficiency and climate resilience are interdependent rather than separate sustainability dimensions. A country that improves energy efficiency without reducing climate vulnerability may still face unstable energy supply, higher adaptation costs, and greater disruption risks. Conversely, a country that strengthens resilience without improving energy efficiency may remain locked into high-cost or carbon-intensive energy pathways.

Validation of the results through Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient provides additional methodological confidence. The strong and statistically significant correlation between the CRADIS and ARAS rankings (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\varphi\:}$$\end{document} = 0.976, p = 1.47*10− 6) confirms the robustness of the proposed framework, as both methods emphasize compromise solutions in multi-criteria environments. The strong and positive association with WASPAS (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\varphi\:}$$\end{document} = 0.915), despite the p-value being marginally above the conventional significance threshold (p = 2.04*10− 4), further supports the stability of the final ranking structure. The correlation with TOPSIS is also strong and statistically significant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\boldsymbol{\varphi\:}=0.733,p=0.016\right)$$\end{document}, indicating that the proposed framework is broadly consistent with another established distance-based MCDM method. Conversely, the weak and statistically insignificant correlation with VIKOR \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\boldsymbol{\varphi\:}=0.055,p=0.881\right)$$\end{document}suggests divergence due to methodological differences, particularly VIKOR’s emphasis on compromise ranking based on group utility and individual regret. This divergence does not weaken the CRITIC–CRADIS framework; rather, it demonstrates why sensitivity analysis is necessary when evaluating complex energy–climate systems. Different MCDM methods may prioritize trade-offs differently, but the strong consistency between CRADIS, ARAS, WASPAS, and TOPSIS indicates that the proposed results are generally robust.

From a managerial and policy standpoint, the findings imply that ASEAN’s pathway toward sustainable energy security requires a shift from efficiency-centric frameworks to strategies that embed climate resilience as a core criterion of energy-system performance. Policymakers should prioritize the integration of climate vulnerability assessments, infrastructure resilience metrics, and adaptive capacity indicators into national energy planning processes. Investments in grid modernization, renewable energy deployment, and energy-storage infrastructure are crucial to enhance supply security under climate uncertainty. Additionally, strengthening institutional governance, regulatory coordination, and technical capacity is essential, particularly for lower-performing nations that face financial and technological constraints. More concretely, countries with lower integrated scores should prioritize concessional finance for grid modernization, targeted subsidies for energy-efficient technologies, minimum energy-performance standards for buildings and industries, and climate-proofing requirements for critical energy infrastructure. These actions would directly address weaknesses in energy intensity, electricity losses, renewable energy deployment, and climate-risk exposure.

For middle-performing ASEAN economies, the results suggest the need to move beyond basic access and incremental efficiency improvements toward coordinated energy-transition strategies. These countries should expand renewable energy investment, develop energy-storage systems, improve demand-side management, and strengthen national energy-efficiency policy implementation. Green credit schemes, blended finance, and public–private partnerships can help mobilize capital for energy-efficient industrial upgrading and resilient infrastructure. In addition, climate-risk screening should be incorporated into energy project appraisal so that new renewable and grid investments remain reliable under future climate conditions.

For higher-performing economies, the policy focus should shift toward maintaining leadership through innovation, regional knowledge transfer, and advanced climate-energy governance. These countries can support smart-grid deployment, climate-risk disclosure in energy investment, advanced energy-efficiency standards, and regional technology cooperation. Singapore, for example, may benefit from further diversifying renewable-energy sources and strengthening cross-border clean-energy cooperation, while the Philippines and Myanmar need to ensure that their strong performance in selected indicators is supported by long-term institutional capacity, infrastructure reliability, and inclusive energy access.

At the ASEAN regional level, the findings support differentiated but coordinated policy design. A one-size-fits-all strategy is unlikely to be effective because ASEAN economies differ in energy structure, policy readiness, innovation capacity, and climate exposure. Regional cooperation should therefore focus on harmonized energy-efficiency standards, regional climate-risk databases, cross-border renewable energy trade, technology transfer mechanisms, and shared green-finance platforms. ASEAN institutions could also support a regional energy efficiency–climate resilience monitoring system that periodically evaluates member countries using transparent and comparable indicators. Such a system would help identify priority investment areas and improve accountability in regional energy-transition planning.

Finally, the demonstrated effectiveness of the hybrid CRITIC–CRADIS framework underscores the importance of adopting objective, data-driven decision-support tools in the formulation of energy and climate policies. By enabling transparent and evidence-based prioritization under multidimensional uncertainty, such analytical approaches can assist governments, development institutions, and energy planners in formulating investment roadmaps that enhance both energy efficiency and climate resilience. This research provides actionable insights for advancing ASEAN’s long-term sustainability commitments, strengthening its adaptive capacity to climate stressors, and safeguarding energy system reliability in an increasingly volatile global environment.

Conclusions

This research set out to address a pressing challenge for developing and emerging economies: enhancing energy efficiency while simultaneously strengthening resilience to climate stress. Focusing on the ASEAN region, one of the world’s fastest-growing yet climate-vulnerable economic blocks, this study developed an integrated evaluation framework combining the CRITIC method for objective criterion weighting with the CRADIS model for transparent compromise-ranking analysis. By integrating energy-efficiency and climate-resilience dimensions within a single decision-making framework, the study provides a structured approach for comparing ASEAN economies under multidimensional energy and climate pressures.

The findings reveal considerable variation in energy-efficiency and climate-resilience performance across ASEAN economies. The Philippines ranks first, followed by Myanmar, Singapore, Thailand, Cambodia, Indonesia, Vietnam, Laos, Malaysia, and Brunei. These results show that ASEAN economies are advancing toward energy transition and resilience at uneven speeds. Importantly, the ranking should not be interpreted simply as a reflection of economic development; rather, it captures relative performance under the selected indicators, including renewable energy share, energy intensity, electricity losses, government spending on energy efficiency, policy score, CO₂ emissions, electricity access, climate risk, and innovation capacity. The results also indicate that climate-risk exposure, policy implementation, renewable energy progress, and efficiency-related investment are central to integrated performance.

From a methodological perspective, the hybrid CRITIC–CRADIS framework proved effective for this context. The CRITIC method objectively quantified the relevance of each criterion based on inherent data variability and inter-attribute correlation, reducing subjectivity in weight allocation. CRADIS provided a robust ranking scheme that evaluates proximity to ideal energy-climate performance while preserving interpretability for policymakers. The sensitivity analysis using VIKOR, TOPSIS, WASPAS, and ARAS further supports the robustness of the proposed model, particularly through the strong rank consistency between CRADIS and ARAS, WASPAS, and TOPSIS.

Beyond its empirical insights, this study makes several key contributions. First, it fills a critical research gap by integrating energy-efficiency and climate-resilience assessment in a region where such alignment is essential yet understudied. Second, it offers a data-driven tool capable of informing national and regional energy planning under uncertainty. Third, it generates actionable evidence for policymakers, highlighting priority areas for intervention such as diversification of renewable energy portfolios, increased investment in grid modernization, strengthened energy-efficiency regulations, climate-risk screening for infrastructure projects, and improved regional green-finance coordination. These contributions extend the literature on energy sustainability assessment by linking objective weighting, compromise ranking, and climate-resilience considerations within a unified ASEAN-focused framework.

The policy implications are clear. Lower-performing economies should prioritize renewable energy deployment, grid modernization, energy-efficiency standards, climate-resilient infrastructure, and targeted financial support. Middle-performing economies should strengthen policy implementation, expand green investment, and improve climate-risk integration in energy planning. Higher-performing economies should focus on innovation, smart-grid development, regional knowledge transfer, and cross-border clean-energy cooperation. At the ASEAN level, harmonized energy-efficiency standards, shared climate-risk databases, regional green-finance mechanisms, and coordinated technology transfer can help reduce performance disparities among member states.

Future research may expand this work in several promising directions. Incorporating time-series data could offer dynamic insights into evolving efficiency-resilience trajectories. Integrating fuzzy or probabilistic extensions of CRITIC and CRADIS may enhance capability in handling uncertainty and expert judgement. Leveraging AI-enabled optimization or machine learning assisted multi-criteria modeling could further refine weight stability and predictive capability. Additionally, applying this framework to other climate-vulnerable regions, such as South Asia, Latin America, or Africa would broaden its empirical relevance and enable global benchmarking studies. Future studies may also integrate more granular indicators, such as sector-level energy efficiency, disaster-risk financing, grid reliability, climate adaptation expenditure, and institutional quality, to further improve the explanatory depth of integrated energy–climate assessments.
