From Disability to Diagnosis: Baseline Findings from the Calgary Functional Movement Disorder Registry
Authors: Andrea Soumbasis, Brian Steele, Megan Howlett, Kate Kain, Davide Martino, Kimberly G Williams, Gabriela S Gilmour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFunctional movement disorder (FMD), a subtype of functional neurological disorder, is a complex neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by inconsistent and incongruent motor symptoms. Despite its relatively high prevalence, FMD remains associated with delayed diagnosis, significant disability, and limited evidence to guide individualized treatment. The Calgary FMD Clinic employs an integrated neurological–psychiatric assessment model, and its registry offers an opportunity to describe characteristics of FMD.ObjectivesTo describe baseline demographic and clinical features of patients enrolled in the Calgary FMD Registry with particular attention to prior healthcare utilization and neuropsychiatric risk factors.MethodsThis prospective registry‐based cohort study includes patients with clinically established FMD evaluated by a neurologist and neuropsychiatrist at a tertiary center in Calgary, Canada. Standardized data collection encompasses demographics, motor and non‐motor symptoms, psychiatric comorbidities, healthcare utilization, and validated outcome measures assessing symptom severity and impact. Descriptive and comparative statistics were performed between FMD phenotypes.ResultsSeventy‐seven participants were enrolled between April 2024 and April 2025. The cohort was predominantly female (n = 45, 58%), with median age of 43 years and median diagnostic delay of 12 months. Episodic presentations were more common (n = 44, 57%), while constant phenotypes demonstrated greater symptom burden and disability. Fatigue and anxiety were highly endorsed among both groups.ConclusionThe Calgary FMD Registry employs a unique neurological–psychiatric assessment model to characterize FMD phenotypes and neuropsychiatric correlates. This dataset provides a relative representation of the Canadian FMD population who are considered for intensive rehabilitation, with the intention to inform stratified rehabilitation and treatment pathways.Keywords: comorbidity, diagnostic delay, functional movement disorder, functional neurological disorder, healthcare utilization

Introduction

Functional neurological disorder (FND) is a heterogeneous and complex neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by neurological symptoms with positive clinical findings, including internal inconsistency and signs incongruent with other neurological diseases. 1 , 2 Functional movement disorder (FMD) is the motor predominant subtype of FND, including tremor, dystonia, gait disturbance, limb weakness, and other abnormal movements. 3 A foundational demographic study revealed a female predominance (72.6%), with a mean age of onset at 39.6 years (SD 16.1). 4 Patients with FND experience disability and distress equivalent to or greater than other neurological disorders, with significant impacts on quality of life and employment. 5 In addition, stressful life events and childhood adverse events are more common in individuals with FND, likely acting as risk factors. 6 , 7

Studies examining healthcare utilization prior to diagnosis highlight delays and associated costs in FND. One Italian cohort study reported a mean diagnostic delay of 6.6 years, during which patients averaged three specialist visits, three diagnostic tests, and over seven rehabilitation contacts annually. More than 50% of patients had experienced hospitalization or emergency department visits before receiving an accurate diagnosis. 8 Another study comparing FND to other neurological disorders reported longer diagnostic delays and higher pre‐diagnostic healthcare costs. 9 These findings underscore the economic and clinical burden of delayed diagnosis, reinforcing the need for early identification and tailored management strategies.

Although FMD has long been suspected to encompass distinct subgroups, earlier studies focusing solely on motor features were unable to reliably define clinically meaningful phenotypes. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 More recent retrospective work has shown that incorporating mental health factors alongside motor features may reveal clinically relevant distinctions. 14 Two provisional phenotypes have been proposed, the first encapsulating an episodic FMD phenotype characterized by hyperkinetic motor symptoms, anxiety, physiological hyperarousal, and a history of trauma. A second, constant FMD phenotype is characterized by weakness, gait disturbance or fixed dystonia, often accompanied by activity avoidance and reduced self‐agency. 14 These phenotypes have not yet been prospectively validated, and their relationship to treatment remains unclear.

Evidence‐based management of FMD includes motor retraining physiotherapy, 15 , 16 , 17 interdisciplinary rehabilitation, 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 and psychological therapies. 25 A stepped‐care model is recommended, emphasizing individualized treatment. However, empiric data to guide informed triage into different therapeutic pathways is limited. Factors such as diagnostic agreement, pain, fatigue, psychiatric comorbidities, and psychosocial variables are frequently discussed in clinical practice but remain understudied. Prognosis of FMD without treatment is generally poor, with more than one‐third of patients failing to improve spontaneously or worsening over time. 11 Even in specialized programs, 20–40% of patients do not achieve substantial benefit, 20 , 22 , 23 suggesting that suboptimal triage or therapeutic pathways may play a role.

The Calgary Functional Movement Disorder Clinic, located within a tertiary care center in Calgary, Alberta, Canada was established to address diagnostic and therapeutic challenges encountered with FMD. 26 The clinic employs an integrated assessment model in which a neurologist and neuropsychiatrist jointly evaluate patients. This collaborative approach allows for simultaneous exploration of neurological and psychiatric dimensions, aiming for comprehensive diagnostic formulation, identification of comorbidities, and building a rehabilitation plan. All patients participate in a neuropsychology education program consisting of two 90‐minute sessions, focusing on explaining FMD, its mechanisms, and self‐management principles, thereby preparing patients for engagement in rehabilitation. Thereafter, patients may be triaged to a 5‐day, intensive transdisciplinary rehabilitation individual program and/or a virtual Affect Regulation Therapy group program aimed to promote symptom management and self‐efficacy. 26 At present, allocation to these pathways is based on clinical judgment, with the aim of developing standardized criteria for program selection.

Given the clinical heterogeneity of FMD and lack of standardization for rehabilitation triage, there is a clear need for prospective, systematically collected patient data to inform clinical decision‐making and optimize treatment outcomes. The Calgary FMD Registry was developed to address this gap. Building on prior work by Gilmour and others (2023), which retrospectively described neuropsychiatric episodic and constant phenotypes in FMD, the present study provides the first prospective characterization of patients enrolled in the Calgary FMD registry. 14 The preliminary aims of the registry are to describe the baseline clinical characteristics of registry patients, with particular attention to movement phenomenology, neuropsychiatric risk factors and healthcare utilization. Additionally, we aim to prospectively evaluate and define the proposed episodic and constant FMD phenotypes, integrating both motor and psychological factors.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

This is a prospective, registry‐based cohort study conducted at the FMD Clinic, part of the outpatient neurosciences program in Calgary, Canada. The registry is designed to capture standardized demographic, clinical, neuropsychiatric, and rehabilitation pathway and outcomes data from patients with FMD to inform phenotypic characterization, treatment allocation, and long‐term outcomes. Patients are eligible for inclusion if they have a clinical diagnosis of FMD, and present with a primary concern related to motor function. Functional motor symptoms may include weakness, gait disturbance, jerks or myoclonus, dystonia, tremor, tics, or parkinsonism. Patients are excluded if there is diagnostic uncertainty or if the primary presenting complaint is functional seizures, dizziness, or functional cognitive disorder. Referrals to the clinic are generated from neurologists and neuropsychiatrists across southern Alberta, and eligible patients are invited to participate in the registry at their initial clinic visit. Referral criteria for the clinic exclude patients with diagnostic disagreement, and/or severe pain or fatigue as primary features that would limit participation in rehabilitation services. Written informed consent is obtained prior to enrolment. This study received ethical review and approval by the University of Calgary Health Research Ethics Board (REB24‐0289).

Intake Data Collection

Prior to the initial appointment, participants receive an intake questionnaire via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). 27 , 28 The form collects demographic and clinical information, including a symptom checklist, past medical history, and psychosocial history (Supplemental Methods). Patients rate their confidence in the diagnosis of FMD and likelihood of improvement on ten‐point Likert scales.

Participants complete validated outcome measures, including the Patient Health Questionnaire‐15 (PHQ‐15), 29 Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), 30 Work and Social Adjustment Scale (WSAS), 31 Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ), 32 Movement Specific Reinvestment Scale (MSRS), 33 and Functional Mobility Scale (FMS). 34 Fatigue is screened with the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) 35 and Visual Analogue Fatigue Scale (VAFS). 36

Baseline Visit Data Collection

At the initial visit, patients undergo a joint assessment by a movement disorders neurologist and neuropsychiatrist. There is reporting of healthcare utilization within the preceding year. Clinicians document triggering events, and the presence of other FND subtypes such as functional seizures, persistent postural–perceptual dizziness, functional sensory loss, functional visual symptoms, functional cognitive disorder, functional speech and language disorder, or functional swallowing disorder. Patient perception of healthcare invalidation is screened by asking patients to describe their experience in the healthcare system and probing for experience of stigma or dismissal. Patients may describe overhearing conversation about their symptoms, being told their symptoms are “not real” or “all in their head,” or being immediately discharged upon FND diagnosis. Vital signs are measured, and a standardized neurological and movement disorders examination is performed. A psychiatric screen and mental status exam are completed. Hyperarousal is recognized as hyper‐talkativeness, diffuse hyperreflexia without upper motor neuron signs, diaphoresis, frequent darting eye movements, visible muscle tension, and body language, such as fidgeting, fist clenching, or repeated leg crossing. 14 The examination is filmed, which patients may decline without affecting registry participation. Based on the clinical evaluation, the treating team classifies the FMD phenomenology and designation as episodic or constant according to operational definitions (Supplemental Methods) 14 and identifies relevant neuropsychiatric factors. Episodic and constant phenotypes are classified based on the temporal pattern of motor symptoms only, integrating historical features and examination findings. Episodic FMD is defined as motor symptoms occurring intermittently, with identifiable periods of absence of motor manifestations. During assessment, episodic symptoms might be absent, inducible, or observed only intermittently. In contrast, constant FMD is defined as motor symptoms perceived as continuously present throughout the day and observable throughout the clinical encounter. The presence of positive signs such as distractibility or inconsistency, which support the diagnosis of FMD, did not determine episodic versus constant classification. The clinicians complete the Clinical Global Impression–Severity (CGI‐S) 37 and the Simplified Functional Movement Disorders Rating Scale (S‐FMDRS). 38

Statistical Analysis

Data were exported from the University of Calgary REDCap server and imported into R 4.5.0. 39 Following data cleaning, descriptive statistics tables were created using the gtsummary package. 40 Descriptive statistics were calculated for the total cohort and between phenomenology comparison. Medians and interquartile ranges were calculated for continuous and ordinal (eg, PROMs, psychometric) variables. Frequencies were calculated for categorical variables (binary and non‐ordered factors).

Beyond describing the cohort characteristics, we sought to compare patient‐ and physician‐reported outcomes between phenomenology subtypes (constant and episodic) on functional, psychological, and health‐related quality of life measures. First, we compared confidence in diagnosis and potential for symptom improvement, subjective healthcare invalidation, FSS, HADS, PHQ‐15, S‐FMDRS, CGI‐S, FMS, and mobility aid usage. Given the number of variables, our statistical analysis was exploratory. We applied the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and the χ 2 test and Fisher exact test for categorical variables (based on expected cell count sizes). 40 A P‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Features

The cohort includes 77 patients. As displayed in the figure, recruitment of patients to the FMD clinic occurred in a steady, quasi‐linear manner over the course of the registry's first year (Fig. 1). Descriptive statistics for demographics and clinical features are reported in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 43 years (IQR: 26–55). The cohort was predominantly female (n = 45, 58%) and Caucasian (n = 56, 73%). The median age at FMD diagnosis was 40 years (IQR: 25–51) and the median age of symptom onset was 38 years (IQR: 22–51), corresponding to a median diagnostic delay of 12 months (IQR: 5–36 months). A significant proportion of patients reported unemployment (n = 17, 22%) or were receiving government disability insurance (n = 20, 26%). The most frequent patient‐reported triggering events were stressful events (n = 34, 44%), illness (n = 15, 19%), and physical injuries (n = 14, 18%). The majority of patients self‐reported experiencing emotional, physical, or sexual abuse at some point in their life (n = 42, 55%).

Motor Phenotypes and FND Symptoms

Tremor (n = 18, 23%), gait disturbances (n = 17, 22%), and weakness (n = 16, 21%) were the most frequently reported primary phenomenologies, although 41 participants (53%) experienced multiple overlapping phenomenologies. The cohort predominantly presented with episodic FMD symptoms (n = 44, 57%). The frequency of specific motor phenotypes, FND symptoms and patient‐recorded symptom burden are presented in Table 2. Among patients with constant FMD symptoms, gait disturbance (n = 16, 48%) and weakness (n = 14, 42%) predominated the most common phenomenologies. In contrast, patients with episodic FMD symptoms had tremor (n = 17, 39%), appendicular jerks (n = 8, 18%), and tics (n = 8, 18%). Notably, there is overlap between episodic and constant phenotypes with hyperkinetic and hypokinetic classifications, respectively. Occasional exceptions were observed, such as tremor in constant phenotypes or weakness in episodic phenotypes, highlighting the complexity and heterogeneity of presentations of FMD.

Comorbid FND symptoms were more prevalent in patients with constant FMD phenomenologies, including sensory disturbances (n = 14, 42%) and seizures (n = 12, 36%). Patient‐reported measures revealed many disabling cognitive/behavioral or physical/neurological symptoms based on a structured symptom checklist (Supplemental Methods). The most frequently reported treatments for FMD include psychological therapy (n = 47, 61%) and physiotherapy (n = 46, 60%).

On a 10‐point Likert scale, patients rated their confidence in FMD diagnosis as 7 (IQR: 5–9) for both constant and episodic phenotypes. On Fisher's exact test, there was no statistically significant difference in confidence in diagnosis between patients with and without diagnostic delay (P = 0.5). The patients’ confidence that symptoms would improve on a 10‐point Likert scale was rated as 7 (IQR: 4–8) in the constant phenotype and 6 (IQR: 5–8) in the episodic subtype. In addition, subjective healthcare invalidation was highly prevalent in both constant (n = 11, 35%) and episodic (n = 14, 32%) groups. The FSS demonstrated that both patients with episodic phenotype and those with constant phenotype reported a high degree of fatigue (episodic: 47; IQR 36–57; constant: 53; IQR, 41–58; p = 0.12). The HADS demonstrated that within both phenotypes, anxiety received higher ratings than depression. Both episodic and constant phenotypes met borderline positive screen of anxiety (episodic: 10; IQR 7–13; constant: 9; IQR 6–14; P = 0.90). Neither phenotype met a positive screen for depression (episodic: 7; IQR 4–10; constant: 7; IQR 6–11; P = 0.40). Finally, the PHQ‐15 revealed that the constant phenotype demonstrated a higher screening of 17.5 (IQR: 11.5–20.5) in comparison to the episodic phenotype screening13.5 (IQR: 10.0–18.0), which was statistically significant (P = 0.032).

Regarding clinician‐reported outcome measures, the S‐FMDRS found that the constant phenotype had a median rating of 9 (IQR: 4–15) while the episodic phenotype had a median rating of 8 (IQR: 2–14), which was not statistically significant (P = 0.30). As outlined in the Figure 2 frequency polygon, patients with the constant FMD phenotype were more densely rated with a higher CGI‐S, with 61% (n = 19) of patients rated 4–6. In comparison, with the episodic FMD phenotype, 68% (n = 30) of patients were rated as 2–3. Patients with a constant phenotype had a high prevalence of mobility aid use (n = 23, 70%), including cane (n = 11, 33%) and less commonly wheelchair use (n = 5, 15%). In comparison, mobility aid use in the episodic phenotype is much less common (n = 8, 18%). Between the episodic and constant phenomenology groups, there were statistically significant differences in the distribution of the CGI‐I scale (P = 0.009), the PHQ‐15 total score (P = 0.032), mobility aid use (any: P < 0.001, cane: P = 0.008), and each FMS measurement (5, 50, and 500 m P‐values all <0.001) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study provides the initial prospective description of patients enrolled in the Calgary FMD Registry during its first year. The data collected offers insights into demographic characteristics, non‐motor symptom profiles, healthcare utilization, and early prospective validation of proposed episodic and constant FMD phenotypes. Our findings reinforce previously reported themes including diagnostic delays, 8 , 9 high levels of comorbidity, 5 , 6 , 7 and frequent experiences of healthcare invalidation. 41 , 42 In addition, our study offers novel prospective insights into how different FMD subtypes may diverge in terms of clinical presentation, functional impairment, and outcomes.

The diagnostic pathway for patients with FMD continues to represent a major challenge within healthcare systems, including in Alberta, Canada. In our cohort, the median diagnostic delay was 12 months, with patients commonly undergoing multiple investigations and specialist consultations prior to receiving a definitive diagnosis. Interestingly, the median diagnostic delay in our cohort was substantially shorter than the 6.6‐year average in an Italian cohort. 8 Notably, our registry captures patients referred to a tertiary FMD clinic by neurologists or neuropsychiatrists, indicating that diagnostic recognition had occurred prior to enrollment. This referral structure inherently excludes individuals whose symptoms remain undiagnosed, thereby potentially underestimating true diagnostic delay at a population level. Additionally, our triaging prioritizes patients with a recent onset of symptoms within 6 months. Finally, improved awareness of FND in recent years may contribute to earlier recognition compared to historical cohorts. While encouraging, our findings should be interpreted within the context of referral‐based sampling and may not reflect diagnostic trajectories in broader community settings.

Critically, more than one‐third of our patients reported experiences of invalidation by healthcare providers. This finding resonates with qualitative literature describing stigma, dismissal, and mischaracterization of exaggerated symptoms. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Experiences of invalidation can erode trust in the healthcare system, reduce confidence in diagnosis, and amplify psychological distress. Our findings underscore the dual burden of prolonged diagnostic journeys and psychosocial stressors, both of which can adversely affect prognosis and engagement with treatment.

Our study provides preliminary prospective support for the episodic and constant phenotypic framework proposed in a recent retrospective analysis by Gilmour and others (2023). 14 Patients with constant symptoms displayed significantly higher somatic symptom burden (PHQ‐15), greater clinician‐rated severity (CGI‐S), and much higher use of mobility aids compared to patients with episodic symptoms. Despite similar motor severity scores on the S‐FMDRS, the constant phenotype was characterized by more pervasive disability and functional restriction, suggesting that motor severity alone does not fully capture the impact of FMD. These essential differences suggest distinct illness trajectories whereby constant FMD may represent a more entrenched phenotype associated with increased disability and reduced agency. In contrast, episodic FMD may be more closely linked to anxiety and features consistent with heightened physiological arousal, as previously described. 14 If validated in longitudinal analysis, these distinctions could have crucial implications for clinical care. Patients with constant symptoms may require more intensive physical rehabilitation, mobility support, and targeted interventions for somatic symptom burden, while episodic patients may benefit from approaches emphasizing hyperarousal management and emotional regulation. Developing stratified care models based on phenotypic presentation may reduce the proportion of patients who fail to benefit from specialized programs.

Psychiatric comorbidity and fatigue were highly prevalent across both episodic and constant groups, indicating that psychological burden is a core feature of FMD regardless of motor phenomenology. Anxiety symptoms were more prominent than depression, aligning with prior studies indicating that anxiety and trauma history are particularly relevant risk factors for FND. 5 , 6 , 7 Fatigue was also nearly universal, with FSS scores comparable to those reported in multiple sclerosis and systemic lupus erythematosus, conditions in which fatigue is known to be a primary driver of disability. 35 The co‐occurrence of anxiety and fatigue may contribute to activity avoidance and reduced engagement in rehabilitation, which may also further perpetuate disability. Furthermore, the majority of patients reported histories of adverse life events, including emotional, physical, or sexual abuse. These findings align with the broader FND literature, where adverse childhood experiences are consistently identified as predisposing factors. 6 , 7 However, trauma history is not universal in FND and should not be considered a diagnostic prerequisite. In our cohort, it was reported by approximately 60% of patients, suggesting that while trauma is an important vulnerability factor, other biopsychosocial factors also contribute.

Within our registry‐based cohort, several limitations warrant consideration. An important characteristic of our cohort includes recruitment from a tertiary referral clinic whereby patients must be referred by a neurologist or neuropsychiatrist within Southern Alberta. Consequentially, our cohort may not reflect the full spectrum of FMD presentations seen in the community or primary care. Patients referred to our clinic are more likely to have severe, persistent, or refractory symptoms, which could bias the distribution of phenotypes and comorbidities. In addition, our clinic is heavily focused and tailored towards rehabilitation, thus we may not capture individuals who are less motivated to participate in rehabilitation due to diagnostic disagreement, severe pain or fatigue, or other limiting factors. The sample size of this initial cohort is modest, and subgroup sizes for the episodic and constant phenotypes are relatively small. As a result, our analyses were limited to univariate comparisons, and we were unable to perform multivariable logistic regression modeling to formally test independent associations between clinical variables and phenotype classification. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Although ongoing recruitment will strengthen future analyses from the Calgary FMD Registry, this paper supports validation of phenotypic subtypes and explores important predictors of treatment response which can help to improve patient care moving forward.

A future priority is improving representation of diverse ethnicities in FND research. Our registry captures self‐reported ethnicity; however, the current cohort is predominantly White, and the number of participants in other groups remains small. Data collected on gender diverse individuals also remains small. Ongoing recruitment and collaboration with other centers may allow for more robust examination of cultural factors and gender identification influencing FND. Given that the Calgary FMD Registry collects standardized neurological, psychiatric, and functional outcome measures, it may also provide a framework for future multi‐center registries.

The Calgary FMD Registry is well positioned to advance our understanding of illness trajectories in FMD. Longitudinal follow‐up will allow us to determine the stability of episodic and constant phenotypes, clarify their prognostic value, and identify predictors of treatment response. Ultimately, this work aims to inform personalized treatment pathways and evidence‐based triage criteria that can be implemented across healthcare systems.
