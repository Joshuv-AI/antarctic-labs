Abstract
. We investigate the descriptive complexity of order convergence in separable Banach lattices. While uniform convergence is Borel and σ \sigma -order convergence is known to be 𝚫 2 1 {\bf\Delta}^{1}_{2} , it is unclear in general when σ \sigma -order convergence is analytic. We introduce a transfinite hierarchy of weakenings of the classical Fatou property, indexed by countable ordinals, and show that it provides a complete structural classification of this definability problem. For a separable Banach lattice X X , we prove that the following are equivalent: (i) the set of decreasing positive sequences with infimum zero is Borel; (ii) σ \sigma -order convergence is analytic; and (iii) X X satisfies the α \alpha -Fatou property for some countable ordinal α \alpha . We further establish that the hierarchy is proper: for every countable ordinal α \alpha there exists a separable Banach lattice with a countable π \pi -basis that fails to be α \alpha -Fatou, but is β \beta -Fatou for some β > α \beta>\alpha . Thus the Borel definability of order convergence is governed by a canonical ordinal invariant intrinsic to the lattice, and the descriptive complexity can be arbitrarily high below ω 1 \omega_{1} . These results identify projective complexity as a genuine structural invariant in Banach lattice theory.

1.
Introduction

The present paper develops a descriptive set theoretic classification of order convergence in separable Banach lattices. Our goal is to measure, in a precise and intrinsic way, the definability complexity of the most natural order-theoretic convergence relations and to identify the structural lattice properties governing this complexity.

A
Banach lattice
is a Banach space
X
equipped with a lattice ordering
\leqslant
that is compatible with both the linear and norm structure on
X
. Specifically, for all
x,y,z\in X
and
\lambda>0
,

x\leqslant y\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda x+z\leqslant\lambda y+z\qquad\&\qquad|x|\leqslant|y|\;\Rightarrow\;\lVert x\rVert\leqslant\lVert y\rVert,

where
|x|=x\vee-x
. These axioms imply that the order relation
\leqslant
is norm closed in
X^{2}
.

However, many other natural order-theoretic constructions exhibit dramatically higher descriptive complexity. For example, the sets

X^{\uparrow\infty}=\big\{\,{(x_{n})\in X^{\mathbb{N}}}\;\big|\;{0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}\leqslant\ldots\text{ and $(x_{n})$ is order-unbounded in }X}\,\big\}

and

X_{\downarrow 0}=\big\{\,{(x_{n})\in X^{\mathbb{N}}}\;\big|\;{x_{1}\geqslant x_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant 0=\inf_{n}x_{n}}\,\big\}

can easily be seen to be coanalytic, i.e.,
{\bf\Pi}^{1}_{1}
, but do not appear to be Borel in
X^{\mathbb{N}}
.

This observation raises a fundamental structural question:

How complicated, in the projective hierarchy, are the natural order convergence relations in a separable Banach lattice?

Our interest in this question stems in part from the study of coordinate systems and bases in Banach lattices
[
1
,
8
,
4
]
, where definability properties of order convergence play a decisive role. Every Banach lattice supports several distinct notions of sequential convergence.

•

A sequence
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}

converges uniformly
to
x
, denoted
x_{n}\mathop{\overset{\sf u}{\longrightarrow}}x
, if there is some
z\in X
so that

\forall m\;\forall^{\infty}n\;|x_{n}-x|\leqslant\tfrac{z}{m},

•

a sequence
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}

\sigma
-order converges
to
x
, denoted
x_{n}\mathop{\overset{\sf\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}x
, if there is some sequence
z_{m}\downarrow 0
in
X
so that

\forall m\;\forall^{\infty}n\;|x_{n}-x|\leqslant z_{m},

•

a sequence
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}

order converges
to
x
, denoted
x_{n}\mathop{\overset{\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}x
, if there is some net
z_{\mu}\downarrow 0
in
X
so that

\forall\mu\;\forall^{\infty}n\;|x_{n}-x|\leqslant z_{\mu}.

Here the notations
z_{m}\downarrow 0
and
z_{\mu}\downarrow 0
mean that
(z_{m})
is a decreasing sequence, respectively decreasing net, with infimum
0
, while the notation

Remark 1.1
.

It is a classical fact (see
[
9
, Remark 1.3]
) that, if
X
is a Banach lattice of measurable functions (i.e. an ideal in the space of measurable functions
L_{0}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
for some semi-finite measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
), we have
f_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}f
if and only if
f_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{a.e.}{\longrightarrow}}}f
and, moreover, there exists a
g\in X_{+}
satisfying
|f_{n}|\leqslant g
for all
n
(i.e., the sequence
(f_{n})
is order bounded). Therefore, order convergence can be viewed as a generalization of dominated almost everywhere convergence to vector lattices. On the other hand, if
e\in X^{+}
, then we may equip the order ideal
I_{e}=\big\{\,{x\in X}\;\big|\;{\exists\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\ |x|\leq\lambda e}\,\big\}
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{e}
where for each
x\in I_{e},

\|x\|_{e}
is the smallest
\lambda=\lambda(x)
which makes the definition of
I_{e}
valid. It is well-known that
(I_{e},\|\cdot\|_{e})
is lattice isometric to
C(K_{e})
for some compact Hausdorff space
K_{e}
. Moreover, in
C(K_{e})
,
f_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sf u}{\longrightarrow}}}f
if and only if
f_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\|\cdot\|_{\infty}}{\longrightarrow}}}f
, so uniform convergence bonds the local norm convergences in
C(K_{e})
to a convergence in all of
X
. These convergences have numerous applications, and have been studied extensively in recent years
[
2
,
3
,
5
]
.

At first glance, the above convergences appear to have high descriptive complexity. For example,
\sigma
-order convergence is naturally expressible as a
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{2}
, meaning that the corresponding subset of
X^{\mathbb{N}}\times X
is the continuous image of a
{\bf\Pi}^{1}_{1}
subset of a Polish space. On the other hand, it is unclear whether the relation
x_{n}\mathop{\overset{\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}x
is even projective, i.e.,
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{k}
for some
k
. Yet, as shown in
[
1
]
, in separable Banach lattices order and
\sigma
-order convergence coincide and the resulting relation is
{\bf\Delta}^{1}_{2}
, that is, both
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{2}
and
{\bf\Pi}^{1}_{2}
. In contrast, uniform convergence is Borel rather than the prima facie value
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
.

The fact that
x_{n}\mathop{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}x
is
{\bf\Delta}^{1}_{2}
but not known to be
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
is a cause of some difficulties in
[
1
]
. Namely, to show that every
\sigma
-order basis
(e_{n})
for a Banach lattice
X=[e_{n}]
is also a Schauder basis, additional set theoretical assumptions are needed. However, this need vanishes once the relation
x_{n}\mathop{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}x
can be shown to be
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
.

We will here define a transfinite hierarchy of successive weakenings of the
Fatou property
for Banach lattices, that is, the property that whenever we have elements
0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x
with
x=\sup_{n}x_{n}
, then
\lVert x\rVert=\sup_{n}\lVert x_{n}\rVert
. In turn, our first main result shows that this hierarchy exhausts the class of separable Banach lattices in which the set
X_{\downarrow 0}
is Borel or equivalently the relation
x_{n}\mathop{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}x
is analytic.

Theorem 1.2
.

The following are equivalent for a separable Banach lattice
X
.

(1)

The set

X_{\downarrow 0}=\Big\{(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X^{\mathbb{N}}\;\Big|\;x_{1}\geqslant x_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant 0=\inf_{n}x_{n}\Big\}

is Borel,

(2)

the set

\Big\{\big((x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},x\big)\in X^{\mathbb{N}}\times X\;\Big|\;x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}x\Big\}

is analytic,

(3)

X
is
\alpha
-Fatou for some
\alpha<\omega_{1}
.

The ordinal invariant introduced here plays a role analogous to classical indices in Banach space theory, such as the Szlenk index and Bourgain’s
\ell_{1}
–index, in that it measures structural complexity via well-founded ranks.

As we shall see later on, there are several natural conditions on
X
that force
X_{\downarrow 0}
to be Borel. For example, this is the case when
X
is either
\sigma
-order continuous,
\sigma
-order complete or has a countable
\pi
-basis
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, that is, elements
b_{n}>0
such that every
x>0
is minorized by some
b_{n}
. Similarly,
X^{\uparrow\infty}
is Borel when
X
is
\sigma
-order continuous,
\sigma
-order complete or has a
countable dominating set

\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, that is, such that every
x\in X
is dominated by some
d_{n}
.

The second main result is that the hierarchy of
\alpha
-Fatou properties is proper, in the sense that it is not exhausted at any level below
\omega_{1}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every countable ordinal
\alpha
, there is a separable Banach lattice with a countable
\pi
-basis that fails to be
\alpha
-Fatou.

Observe that, having countable
\pi
-bases, the spaces of Theorem
1.3
must be
\beta
-Fatou for some
\beta>\alpha
. Although we do not have an example of a separable Banach lattice
X
in which
X_{\downarrow 0}
fails to be Borel, the techniques behind Theorem
1.3
imply that there can be no uniform Borel manner of defining the property
x_{n}\downarrow 0
across all separable Banach lattices
X
.

2.
Definability of order convergences

As mentioned in the introduction, the prima facie complexities of uniform and order convergence are higher than their actual complexities. So we begin by recalling the better estimates obtained in
[
1
]
.

Proposition 2.1

(Lemmas 2.1 & 2.3
[
1
]
)
.

For a sequence
(x_{n})
and a vector
x
in a Banach lattice
X
, we have that

(1)

x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sf u}{\longrightarrow}}}x
if and only if

\forall\epsilon>0\;\exists k\;\forall m\geqslant k\quad\Bigg\lVert\bigvee_{n=k}^{m}|x_{n}-x|\Bigg\rVert<\epsilon,

(2)

x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}x
if and only if

\forall y>0\;\exists z\;\big(y\not\leqslant z\;\;\&\;\;\forall^{\infty}n\;|x_{n}-x|\leqslant z\big).

Recall on the other hand that the relation
x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}x
is easily seen to be
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{2}
, since

\begin{split}{x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}x&\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\exists(z_{m})\in X_{\downarrow 0}\;\forall m\;\forall^{\infty}n\;|x_{n}-x|\leqslant z_{m}}\end{split}

and
X_{\downarrow 0}
is
{\bf\Pi}^{1}_{1}
.

Proposition 2.2

(Proposition 2.4
[
1
]
)
.

Let
X
be a separable Banach lattice. Then a sequence
(x_{n})
in
X
order converges to
x\in X
if and only if it
\sigma
-order converges to
x
. In particular, the set

(1)

\Big\{\big((x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},x\big)\in X^{\mathbb{N}}\times X\;\Big|\;x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}x\Big\}

is
{\bf\Delta}^{1}_{2}
, whereas

\Big\{\big((x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},x\big)\in X^{\mathbb{N}}\times X\;\Big|\;x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sf u}{\longrightarrow}}}x\Big\}

is Borel.

The next step is to identify large and familiar classes of Banach lattices in which the sets
X_{\downarrow 0}
and
X^{\uparrow\infty}
are Borel rather than merely
{\bf\Pi}^{1}_{1}
. For this, let us recall that a Banach lattice
X
is
\sigma
-complete
if every countable sequence
(x_{n})
that is bounded above has a least upper bound denoted
\sup_{n}x_{n}
. Also, the lattice
X
is said to be
\sigma
-order continuous
provided that
\lim_{n}\lVert x_{n}\rVert=0
for all
(x_{n})\in X_{\downarrow 0}
. Finally, we say that
X
has the
Fatou property
if, whenever

0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x=\sup_{n}x_{n},

then
\lVert x\rVert=\sup_{n}\lVert x_{n}\rVert
. It is immediate to see that
\sigma
-order continuity implies the Fatou property, since the former forces that actually
\lim_{n}\lVert x-x_{n}\rVert=0
when
0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x=\sup_{n}x_{n}
.

Now, by
[
7
, Propositions 1.a.7 and 1.a.8]
the following three properties are equivalent in the case of separable Banach lattices
X
.

(1)

X
is
\sigma
-complete,

(2)

X
is
\sigma
-order continuous,

(3)

every order bounded increasing sequence is norm convergent.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
X
is a
\sigma
-complete separable Banach lattice, the sets
X_{\downarrow 0}
and
X^{\uparrow\infty}
are both Borel and the relation
x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}x
is
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
.

Proof.

Observe that, by
\sigma
-order continuity,

X_{\downarrow 0}=\big\{\,{(x_{n})\in X^{\mathbb{N}}}\;\big|\;{x_{1}\geqslant x_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant 0\;\;\&\;\;\lim_{n}\lVert x_{n}\rVert=0}\,\big\},

whereas (3) above implies that

X^{\uparrow\infty}=\big\{\,{(x_{n})\in X^{\mathbb{N}}}\;\big|\;{0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}\leqslant\ldots\;\;\&\;\;\lim_{n,m\to\infty}\lVert x_{n}-x_{m}\rVert\neq 0}\,\big\}.

That
x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}x
is
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
follows immediately from
X_{\downarrow 0}
being Borel.
∎

As we shall see in Example
3.4
, for
X_{\downarrow 0}
to be Borel and
x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}x
to be
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
, it is enough to assume that
X
has the Fatou property. The same is true if
X
admits a countable
\pi
-basis

\{b_{n}\}\subseteq X^{+}\setminus\{0\}
, meaning that, for every
x>0
, there in an
n
so that
x>b_{n}
. Indeed, in this case,

X_{\downarrow 0}=\big\{\,{(x_{n})\in X^{\mathbb{N}}}\;\big|\;{x_{1}\geqslant x_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant 0\;\;\&\;\;\forall m\;\exists n\;x_{n}\not>b_{m}}\,\big\}

is Borel.
Similarly, if
X
admits a countable dominating family, then
X^{\uparrow\infty}
is Borel.

3.
An ordinal index

We now turn to other weaker conditions than the Fatou property or the existence of a countable
\pi
-basis. So, in the following, let
X
denote a fixed separable Banach lattice. Let us begin by recalling the following simple calculation.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
x,y\in X
, we have

(y-x)^{+}=y-(x\wedge y).

Proof.

Recall that
+
distributes over the lattice operation
\vee
, so

\begin{split}{y-(x\wedge y)&=y+\big((-x)\vee(-y)\big)=(y-x)\vee 0=(y-x)^{+}}\end{split}

for all
x,y\in X
.
∎

If
\Sigma
is any set, we let
\Sigma^{<\mathbb{N}}
denote the set of all finite strings of elements of
\Sigma
. Recall that a subset
T\subseteq\Sigma^{<\mathbb{N}}
is a
tree
provided that
T
contains the empty string
\emptyset
and is closed under taking initial segments. Recall also that a tree
T
is said to be
ill-founded
provided that it has an
infinite branch
, i.e., if there is an infinite sequence
(y_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\Sigma
so that

(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in T

for all
n
. Otherwise,
T
is
well-founded
. For a well-founded tree
T
, we define an ordinal valued rank function
\rho_{T}\colon T\to{\sf Ord}
by letting

\rho_{T}(s)=0\;\Leftrightarrow\;s\text{ has no proper extensions in }T

and otherwise

\rho_{T}(s)=\sup\big\{\rho_{T}(t)+1\;\big|\;t\in T\;\&\;s\subsetneq t\}.

We also define the
rank
of
T
itself by

\rho(T)=\sup\big\{\rho_{T}(t)+1\;\big|\;t\in T\}=\rho_{T}(\emptyset)+1.

A binary relation
\prec
on a set
\Omega
is said to be
well-founded
if there is no infinite sequence of elements
p_{n}\in\Omega
so that

\ldots\prec p_{3}\prec p_{2}\prec p_{1}.

In this case, we may similarly define a rank
\rho_{\prec}\colon\Omega\to{\sf Ord}
by

\rho_{\prec}(p)=0\;\Leftrightarrow\;p\text{ is minimal, i.e., }q\not\prec p\text{ for all }q\in\Omega

and

\rho_{\prec}(p)=\sup\big\{\rho_{\prec}(q)+1\;\big|\;q\prec p\}.

As for trees, we set
\rho(\prec)=\sup\big\{\rho_{\prec}(p)+1\;\big|\;p\in\Omega\}
. For example, if
T
is a well-founded tree, we may let
\prec
be the relation
\supsetneq
on
T
, that is, for
s,t\in T
, we have
s\prec t
if
t
is a proper initial segment of
s
. Then
\rho_{T}=\rho_{\prec}
.

We let
Tr_{\Sigma}
denote the set of all trees
T\subseteq\Sigma^{<\mathbb{N}}
and
WF_{\Sigma}
denote the subset of all well-founded trees. If
\Sigma
is a countable set, then
Tr_{\Sigma}
is a closed subset of the Polish space
\{0,1\}^{\Sigma^{<\mathbb{N}}}
, whereas

WF_{\Sigma}
is a coanalytic subset of
Tr_{\Sigma}
. Moreover, by
[
6
, Exercise 34.6]
,
\rho
is a coanalytic rank on
WF_{\Sigma}
. In particular, this means that, for all
\lambda<\omega_{1}
, the set

WF_{\Sigma}^{\lambda}=\{T\in Tr_{\Sigma}\;\big|\;\rho(T)\leqslant\lambda\}

is Borel.

Set

X_{\downarrow}=\big\{(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X^{\mathbb{N}}\;\big|\;z_{1}\geqslant z_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant 0\big\}

and define, for every sequence
(z_{n})\in X_{\downarrow}
, a tree
\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\subseteq\big(X^{+}\!\setminus\{0\}\big)^{<\mathbb{N}}
by

\begin{split}{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in\Psi&\big((z_{n})\big)\Leftrightarrow\\
&\forall i\;\forall n\quad\lVert(y_{i}-z_{n})^{+}\rVert\leqslant\tfrac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert}{3^{i}}\;\;\&\;\;\lVert y_{i+1}-y_{i}\rVert\leqslant\tfrac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert}{3^{i}}.}\end{split}

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
P\subseteq X^{+}\setminus\{0\}
be a countable norm-dense subset. Then, for all
(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow}
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

\inf_{n}z_{n}=0
,

(2)

\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)
is well-founded,

(3)

\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\cap P^{<\mathbb{N}}
is well-founded.

Proof.

Note first that the implication (2)
\Rightarrow
(3) is trivial.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1): Assume that
0
is not the infimum of the sequence
(z_{n})
, that is, that there is some
y\in X^{+}
satisfying

z_{1}\geqslant z_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant y>0.

Pick then elements
y_{i}\in P
satisfying
\lVert y_{i}-y\rVert<\tfrac{\lVert y\rVert}{7^{i}}
, which implies that

\lVert y_{i+1}-y_{i}\rVert\leqslant\lVert y_{i+1}-y\rVert+\lVert y-y_{i}\rVert<\frac{\lVert y\rVert}{7^{i+1}}+\frac{\lVert y\rVert}{7^{i}}<\frac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert}{3^{i}}

for all
i
. Furthermore,

\lVert(y_{i}-z_{n})^{+}\rVert\leqslant\lVert(y-z_{n})^{+}\rVert+\lVert y-y_{i}\rVert=\lVert y-y_{i}\rVert<\frac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert}{3^{i}},

showing that
(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)
is an infinite branch of
\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\cap P^{<\mathbb{N}}
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2):
Suppose that
\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)
is ill-founded, i.e., that
\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)
has an infinite branch
(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)
. Then the conditions
\lVert y_{i+1}-y_{i}\rVert\leqslant\frac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert}{3^{i}}
ensure that
(y_{i})
is a Cauchy sequence converging to some
y>0
. Furthermore, as
\lVert(y_{i}-z_{n})^{+}\rVert\leqslant\frac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert}{3^{i}}
for all
n
and
i
, we find that also

\big\lVert y-(z_{n}\wedge y)\big\rVert=\big\lVert(y-z_{n})^{+}\big\rVert=0,

i.e., that
y\leqslant z_{n}
for all
n
. So,
y
is a strictly positive lower bound for
(z_{n})
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

The set

X_{\downarrow 0}=\big\{(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow}\;\big|\;\inf_{n}z_{n}=0\big\}

is Borel,

(2)

\sup\Big\{\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\Big)\;\Big|\;{(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow 0}}\Big\}<\omega_{1}.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): Note that, if
X_{\downarrow 0}
is Borel, so is the set

\Omega=\Big\{\,{\big((z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})\big)\in X_{\downarrow 0}\times\big(X^{+}\!\setminus\{0\}\big)^{<\mathbb{N}}}\;\Big|\;{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})\in\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)}\,\Big\}.

We may then define a Borel quasiordering
\prec
on
\Omega
by setting

\begin{split}{\big((z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},&(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})\big)\prec\big((u_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\big)\\
&\Leftrightarrow\;(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}=(u_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\quad\&\quad k<m\quad\&\quad(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})}\end{split}

and observe that
\prec
is well-founded by Lemma
3.2
. It then follows from the boundedness theorem for analytic well-founded relations
[
6
, Theorem 31.1]
that

\begin{split}{\sup\Big\{\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\Big)\;\Big|\;{(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow 0}}\Big\}&=\sup\Big\{\rho_{\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)}(\emptyset)+1\;\Big|\;{(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow 0}}\Big\}\\
&=\sup\Big\{\rho_{\prec}\big((z_{n}),\emptyset\big)+1\;\Big|\;{(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow 0}}\Big\}\\
&=\rho(\prec)\\
&<\omega_{1}.}\end{split}

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1): Observe that, if
P\subseteq X^{+}\setminus\{0\}
is a fixed countable norm-dense subset and

\sup\Big\{\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\Big)\;\Big|\;{(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow 0}}\Big\}<\omega_{1},

then also

\lambda=\sup\Big\{\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\cap P^{<\mathbb{N}}\Big)\;\Big|\;{(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow 0}}\Big\}<\omega_{1}.

Note now that the map
X_{\downarrow}\mathop{\overset{\Theta}{\longrightarrow}}Tr_{P}
defined by

\Theta\big((z_{n})\big)=\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\cap P^{<\mathbb{N}}

is Borel measurable and satisfies

(z_{n})\in X_{\downarrow 0}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\Theta\big((z_{n})\big)\in WF^{\lambda}_{P}.

Because
WF^{\lambda}_{P}
is Borel, this shows that also
X_{\downarrow 0}
is Borel.
∎

For every ordinal
\alpha
and
(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow}
, we define a game
G_{\alpha}\big[(z_{n})\big]
between two players I and II as follows. Players I and II alternate in playing ordinals
\beta_{i}
and vectors
y_{i}\in X^{+}\!\setminus\{0\}
,

{\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccccccc}{\bf I}&&{\beta_{1}}&&{\beta_{2}}&&&\ldots&&{\beta_{k-1}}&&{\beta_{k}}&&\\
{\bf II}&&&{y_{1}}&&{y_{2}}&&\ldots&&&{y_{k-1}}&&{y_{k}}&\end{array}}

and where the ordinals played are subject to the condition

\alpha>\beta_{1}>\beta_{2}>\ldots>\beta_{k-1}>\beta_{k}\geqslant 0.

The game ends when I plays
\beta_{k}=0
and II plays its response
y_{k}
. This will eventually happen as the ordinals are well-ordered. Player II is then said to
win
a run of the game provided that

\lVert y_{i+1}-y_{i}\rVert\leqslant\frac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert}{3^{i}}

for all
1\leqslant i<k
and

\lVert(y_{i}-z_{n})^{+}\rVert\leqslant\frac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert}{3^{i}}

for all
n\geqslant 1
and
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
. Otherwise player I wins.

Example 3.4

(Banach lattices with the Fatou property)
.

Suppose
X
is a Banach lattice with the Fatou property, that is, whenever we have elements
0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x
with
x=\sup_{n}x_{n}
, then
\lVert x\rVert=\sup_{n}\lVert x_{n}\rVert
. Assume also that
(z_{n})\in X_{\downarrow 0}
. Then it is easy to see that I has a winning strategy in the game
G_{1}\big[(z_{n})\big]
. Indeed, I simply plays
\beta_{1}=0
, to which II responds with some vector
y_{1}
. If II wins this run of the game, we must have

\lVert y_{1}-(z_{n}\wedge y_{1})\rVert=\lVert(y_{1}-z_{n})^{+}\rVert\leqslant\frac{\lVert y_{1}\rVert}{3}

for all
n
, while at the same time

0\leqslant y_{1}-(z_{1}\wedge y_{1})\leqslant y_{1}-(z_{2}\wedge y_{1})\leqslant y_{1}-(z_{3}\wedge y_{1})\leqslant\ldots\leqslant y_{1}=\sup_{n}\big(y_{1}-(z_{n}\wedge y_{1})\big).

Taken together, this contradicts the Fatou property. This means that a winning strategy for I in
G_{1}\big[(z_{n})\big]
is simply to play
\beta_{1}=0
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For every
(z_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in X_{\downarrow}
and every ordinal
\alpha
,

\begin{split}{\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\Big)\leqslant{\alpha}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\text{ I has a winning strategy in the game }G_{\alpha}\big[(z_{n})\big].}\end{split}

Proof.

A straightforward verification shows that

\begin{split}{\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\Big)>{\alpha}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\text{ II has a winning strategy in the game }G_{\alpha}\big[(z_{n})\big].}\end{split}

Note also that, because the rules and winning conditions in both games are Borel, the games are determined, that is, either player I or II has a winning strategy
[
6
, Theorem 20.6]
. Therefore,

\begin{split}{\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\Big)\leqslant{\alpha}&\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\text{ II has no winning strategy in the game }G_{\alpha}\big[(z_{n})\big]\\
&\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\text{ I has a winning strategy in the game }G_{\alpha}\big[(z_{n})\big]}\end{split}

as claimed.
∎

Definition 3.6
.

The separable Banach lattice
X
is said to be
\alpha
-Fatou provided that, for every
(z_{n})\in X_{\downarrow}
,

\begin{split}{\inf_{n}z_{n}=0&\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\text{ I has a winning strategy in the game }G_{\alpha}\big[(z_{n})\big]\\
&\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\Big)\leqslant{\alpha}.\ }\end{split}

By Example
3.4
, Banach lattices with the Fatou property are
1
-Fatou.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): Suppose that (1) holds, i.e., that
X_{\downarrow 0}
is Borel. Then, for all sequences
(x_{n})
and vectors
x
, we have

\begin{split}{x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}x&\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\exists(z_{n})\in X_{\downarrow 0}\;\;\forall m\;\;\forall^{\infty}n\;\;|x_{n}-x|\leqslant z_{m},}\end{split}

which is clearly
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1): Observe that, for a sequence
x_{1}\geqslant x_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant 0
, we have

\forall y>0\;\exists n\;\;y\not\leqslant x_{n}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\inf_{n}x_{n}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x_{n}\mathop{\underset{n}{\overset{\sigma\sf o}{\longrightarrow}}}0.

The first expression is clearly
{\bf\Pi}^{1}_{1}
. Hence, if the last is
{\bf\Sigma}^{1}_{1}
, then these equivalent expressions are all Borel and hence
X_{\downarrow 0}
is a Borel set.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(3):
Just observe that, by Lemma
3.3
, the set
X_{\downarrow 0}
is Borel if and only if there is some
\alpha<\omega_{1}
so that

\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z_{n})\big)\Big)\leqslant{\alpha}

for all
(z_{n})\in X_{\downarrow 0}
.
∎

4.
Examples of spaces with higher order Fatou properties

Our next task is to show that the hierarchy of
\alpha
-Fatou properties does not collapse. That is, we will construct spaces that are
\alpha
-Fatou, but only for larger and larger
\alpha<\omega_{1}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For every countable ordinal
\alpha
, there is a separable Banach lattice with a countable
\pi
-basis that fails to be
\alpha
-Fatou.

Proof.

Our proof goes by induction on
1\leqslant\alpha<\omega_{1}
. For each
\alpha
, we will construct several objects,

(1)

a separable Banach lattice
(X_{\alpha},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\alpha})
,

(2)

a countable
\pi
-basis
B_{\alpha}
for
X_{\alpha}
,

(3)

a Banach lattice homomorphism
X_{\alpha}\mathop{\overset{\phi_{\alpha}}{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{R}
of norm
1
,

(4)

a sequence

z^{\alpha}_{1}\geqslant z^{\alpha}_{2}\geqslant\ldots>0=\inf_{n}z^{\alpha}_{n}

whose terms belong to the set

S_{\alpha}=\big\{x\in X^{+}_{\alpha}\;\big|\;\lVert x\rVert_{\alpha}=\phi_{\alpha}(x)=1\big\},

(5)

a countable tree
T_{\alpha}\subseteq\Psi\big((z^{\alpha}_{n})\big)\cap S_{\alpha}^{<\mathbb{N}}
so that

\rho(T_{\alpha})>\alpha.

Observe then that, by property (
5
), we have

\rho\Big(\Psi\big((z^{\alpha}_{n})\big)\Big)>{\alpha}

and hence that
X_{\alpha}
fails to be
\alpha
-Fatou.

Base case,
\alpha=1
.
We let
X_{1}
be the Banach lattice
c
of convergent real sequences endowed with the equivalent renorming

\big\lVert(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots)\big\rVert_{1}=\max\Big\{\tfrac{1}{3}\big\lVert(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots)\big\rVert_{\ell_{\infty}},\big|\lim_{n}t_{n}\big|\Big\}.

Note that

B_{1}=\big\{\,{(0,\ldots,0,t,0,\ldots)}\;\big|\;{t\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}}\,\big\}

is a countable
\pi
-basis for
X_{\alpha}
.
Define
\phi_{1}
by

\phi_{1}\big((t_{1},t_{2},\ldots)\big)=\lim_{n}t_{n}

and, for each
n
, set

z^{1}_{n}=(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{n\text{ times}},1,1,1,\ldots)\in S_{1}.

Finally, letting
y_{1}=(1,1,1,\ldots)\in S_{1}
, we see that
(y_{1})\in\Psi\big((z^{\alpha}_{n})\big)
and therefore that

T_{1}=\big\{\emptyset,(y_{1})\big\}\subseteq\Psi\big((z^{\alpha}_{n})\big)\cap S_{1}^{<\mathbb{N}}

and that
\rho(T_{1})=2>1
.

Successor case.
Suppose that
(X_{\alpha},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\alpha})
,
B_{\alpha}
,
\phi_{\alpha}
,
(z_{n}^{\alpha})
and
T_{\alpha}
have been defined as above. We then let
X_{\alpha+1}=X_{\alpha}
with the new norm

\lVert x\rVert_{\alpha+1}=\max\Big\{\tfrac{1}{7}{\lVert x\rVert_{\alpha}},\big|\phi_{\alpha}(x)\big|\Big\}.

Observe that
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\alpha+1}\leqslant\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\alpha}
, whereby
\lVert\phi_{\alpha}\rVert_{\alpha+1}=1
and we may therefore set

\phi_{\alpha+1}=\phi_{\alpha}
and
B_{\alpha+1}=B_{\alpha}
.
Note also that

\lVert x\rVert_{\alpha+1}=\lVert x\rVert_{\alpha}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\lVert x\rVert_{\alpha}=|\phi_{\alpha}(x)|,

from which it follows that
S_{\alpha}\subseteq S_{\alpha+1}
. We may thus set
z^{\alpha+1}_{n}=z^{\alpha}_{n}\in S_{\alpha}\subseteq S_{\alpha+1}
for all
n
.
Finally, let

T_{\alpha+1}=\big\{(z_{1}^{\alpha},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\;\big|\;(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in T_{\alpha}\big\}\cup\{\emptyset\}\;\;\subseteq\;\;S_{\alpha}^{<\mathbb{N}}\;\;\subseteq\;\;S_{\alpha+1}^{<\mathbb{N}}

and note that
\rho(T_{\alpha+1})>\rho(T_{\alpha})>\alpha
.
To see that
T_{\alpha+1}\subseteq\Psi\big((z^{\alpha+1}_{n})\big)
, note first that, for all
x,y\in S_{\alpha}
, we have

\phi_{\alpha}\big((x-y)^{+}\big)=\big(\phi_{\alpha}(x-y)\big)^{+}=0^{+}=0=\phi_{\alpha}(x-y),

whereby
\big\lVert(x-y)^{+}\big\rVert_{\alpha+1}=\tfrac{1}{7}\big\lVert(x-y)^{+}\big\rVert_{\alpha}
and
\lVert x-y\rVert_{\alpha+1}=\tfrac{1}{7}\lVert x-y\rVert_{\alpha}
.
Thus, for all
n
,

\begin{split}{\big\lVert(z_{1}^{\alpha}-z^{\alpha+1}_{n})^{+}\big\rVert_{\alpha+1}&=\big\lVert(z_{1}^{\alpha}-z^{\alpha}_{n})^{+}\big\rVert_{\alpha+1}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{7}\big\lVert(z_{1}^{\alpha}-z^{\alpha}_{n})^{+}\big\rVert_{\alpha}\\
&\leqslant\tfrac{1}{7}\lVert z^{\alpha}_{1}\rVert_{\alpha}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{7}\lVert z^{\alpha}_{1}\rVert_{\alpha+1}\\
&<\frac{\lVert z_{1}^{\alpha}\rVert_{\alpha+1}}{3^{1}}}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}{\big\lVert(y_{i}-z^{\alpha+1}_{n})^{+}\big\rVert_{\alpha+1}&=\big\lVert(y_{i}-z^{\alpha}_{n})^{+}\big\rVert_{\alpha+1}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{7}\big\lVert(y_{i}-z^{\alpha}_{n})^{+}\big\rVert_{\alpha}\\
&\leqslant\frac{1}{7}\frac{\|{y}_{i}\|_{\alpha}}{3^{i}}\\
&<\frac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert_{\alpha+1}}{3^{i+1}}.}\end{split}

Similarly,

\begin{split}{\lVert y_{1}-z_{1}^{\alpha}\rVert_{\alpha+1}&=\tfrac{1}{7}\big\lVert y_{1}-z^{\alpha}_{1}\big\rVert_{\alpha}\leqslant\frac{2}{7}<\frac{\lVert z_{1}^{\alpha}\rVert_{\alpha+1}}{3^{1}}}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}{\lVert{y}_{i+1}-{y}_{i}\rVert_{\alpha+1}=\tfrac{1}{7}\big\lVert y_{i+1}-y_{i}\big\rVert_{\alpha}\leqslant\frac{1}{7}\frac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert_{\alpha}}{3^{i}}<\frac{\lVert y_{i}\rVert_{\alpha+1}}{3^{i+1}}.}\end{split}

Together, these inequalities establish that
(z_{1}^{\alpha},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\Psi\big((z^{\alpha+1}_{n})\big)
whenever
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in T_{\alpha}
and hence that
T_{\alpha+1}\subseteq\Psi\big((z^{\alpha+1}_{n})\big)
.

Limit case.
Suppose that
\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\ldots<\alpha=\lim_{n}\alpha_{n}
. Assume also that the construction has been done for all ordinals smaller than
\alpha
. We first consider the
\ell_{\infty}
-sum of the Banach lattices
X_{\alpha_{n}}
,

X^{\infty}_{\alpha}=\bigoplus_{\ell_{\infty}}\Big\{\,{X_{\alpha_{n}}}\;\Big|\;{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\Big\}=\Big\{\,{x=(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots)\in\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}X_{\alpha_{n}}}\;\Big|\;{\sup_{n}\|x^{n}\|_{\alpha_{n}}<\infty}\,\Big\}.

Endowed with coordinatewise operations and the norm
\|x\|_{\alpha}=\sup_{n}\|x^{n}\|_{\alpha_{n}}
, this is a Banach lattice. Furthermore, because each
\phi_{\alpha_{n}}
is a Banach lattice homomorphism of norm 1, it follows that

X^{\phi}_{\alpha}=\Big\{\,{x=(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots)\in X^{\infty}_{\alpha}}\;\Big|\;{\lim_{n}\phi_{\alpha_{n}}(x^{n})\text{ exists}}\,\Big\}

is a Banach sublattice of
X^{\infty}_{\alpha}
and that
\phi\colon X^{\phi}_{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\phi\big((x^{n})\big)=\lim_{n}\phi_{\alpha_{n}}(x^{n})

is a lattice homomorphism of norm
1
. Observe also that

X^{\infty}_{\alpha}\cap\prod_{n}S_{\alpha_{n}}\subseteq X^{\phi}_{\alpha}.

Unfortunately, there is no reason for
X^{\phi}_{\alpha}
to be separable and so
X_{\alpha}
will be chosen to be a specific separable Banach sublattice of
X^{\phi}_{\alpha}
.

For this we first define the uncountable tree

T^{\phi}_{\alpha}
of all finite strings of elements of
X^{\phi}_{\alpha}
of the form

\Big(\big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1},{y_{1}^{k}},{y_{1}^{k+1}},{y_{1}^{k+2}},\ldots\big)\,,\;\;\ldots\;\;,\,\big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1},{y_{n}^{k}},{y_{n}^{k+1}},{y_{n}^{k+2}},\ldots\big)\Big),

where
k\geqslant 1
and
(y_{1}^{m},y_{2}^{m},\ldots,y_{n}^{m})\in T_{\alpha_{m}}
for all
m\geqslant k
. We observe that
\rho(T^{\phi}_{\alpha})\geqslant\alpha+1.

This is because the rank of an element of the tree as above is given by

\min\big\{\,{\rho_{T_{\alpha_{m}}}\big((y_{1}^{m},y_{2}^{m},\ldots,y_{n}^{m})\big)}\;\big|\;{m\geqslant k}\,\big\},

and therefore, by the inductive hypothesis, we can find strings of rank at least
\alpha_{m}
for every
m
, and the rank of the root
\emptyset
is
\alpha
.

By induction on countable ordinals
\beta
, it is easily seen that, if a tree
\Upsilon
satisfies
\rho(\Upsilon)\geqslant\beta
, then there is a countable subtree
\Upsilon^{\prime}\subseteq\Upsilon
with
\rho(\Upsilon^{\prime})\geqslant\beta
.
Applying this to
\Upsilon=T^{\phi}_{\alpha}
, we take a countable tree
T_{\alpha}\subseteq T^{\phi}_{\alpha}
such that
\rho(T_{\alpha})>\alpha
.

We define
X_{\alpha}
to be the separable Banach sublattice of
X^{\phi}_{\alpha}
generated by

(1)

all elements of
X^{\phi}_{\alpha}
that appear in the tree
T_{\alpha}
,

(2)

all eventually zero vectors
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},0,0,\ldots)\in X^{\phi}_{\alpha}
,

(3)

vectors
z^{\alpha}_{k}=\left({z^{\alpha_{1}}_{k}},{z^{\alpha_{2}}_{k}},{z^{\alpha_{3}}_{k}},\ldots\right)
for
k<\omega
.

If
B_{\alpha_{n}}
is the countable
\pi
-basis for
X_{\alpha_{n}}
, then the collection
B_{\alpha}
of vectors of the form
(0,\ldots,0,b,0,0,\ldots)

with
b\in B_{\alpha_{n}}
in the
n
th position form a countable
\pi
-basis for
X_{\alpha}
. The sequence
z^{\alpha}_{k}=\left({z^{\alpha_{1}}_{k}},{z^{\alpha_{2}}_{k}},{z^{\alpha_{3}}_{k}},\ldots\right)
that we already defined is contained in
S_{\alpha}
and satisfies

z^{\alpha}_{1}\geqslant z^{\alpha}_{2}\geqslant\ldots>0=\inf_{k}z^{\alpha}_{k}.

The nodes of the tree
T_{\alpha}
indeed belong to
S_{\alpha}^{<\mathbb{N}}
and by construction we have that
\rho(T_{\alpha})>\alpha
. It thus remains to show that
T_{\alpha}\subseteq\Psi\big((z^{\alpha}_{m})\big)
.
First,

\begin{split}{\Bigg\lVert\Big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1}&,{y_{i+1}^{k}},y_{i+1}^{k+1},{y_{i+1}^{k+2}},\ldots\Big)-\Big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1},{y_{i}^{k}},{y_{i}^{k+1}},{y_{i}^{k+2}},\ldots\Big)\Bigg\rVert_{\alpha}\\
&=\Bigg\lVert\Big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1},\big({y_{i+1}^{k}-y_{i}^{k}}\big),\big({y_{i+1}^{k+1}-y_{i}^{k+1}}\big),\big(y_{i+1}^{k+2}-y_{i}^{k+2}\big),\ldots\Big)\Bigg\rVert_{\alpha}\\
&=\sup_{m\geqslant k}\Big\lVert{y_{i+1}^{m}-y_{i}^{m}}\Big\rVert_{\alpha_{m}}\\
&\leqslant\sup_{m\geqslant k}\tfrac{1}{3^{i}}\big\lVert{y_{i}^{m}}\big\rVert_{\alpha_{m}}\\
&\leqslant\frac{1}{3^{i}}\Big\lVert\Big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1},{y_{i}^{k}},{y_{i}^{k+1}},{y_{i}^{k+2}},\ldots\Big)\Big\rVert_{\alpha}.}\end{split}

Similarly, for all
k
and
m
,

\begin{split}{\Bigg\lVert\Bigg(&\Big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1},{y_{i}^{k}},{y_{i}^{k+1}},{y_{i}^{k+2}},\ldots\Big)-z^{\alpha}_{m}\Bigg)^{+}\Bigg\rVert_{\alpha}\\
&=\Bigg\lVert\Bigg(\Big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1},{y_{i}^{k}},{y_{i}^{k+1}},{y_{i}^{k+2}},\ldots\Big)-\Big({z^{\alpha_{1}}_{m}},{z^{\alpha_{2}}_{m}},{z^{\alpha_{3}}_{m}},\ldots\Big)\Bigg)^{+}\Bigg\rVert_{\alpha}\\
&=\Bigg\lVert\Big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1},{\big({y_{i}^{k}}-{z^{\alpha_{k}}_{m}}\big)^{+}},{\big({y_{i}^{k+1}}-{z^{\alpha_{k+1}}_{m}}\big)^{+}},{\big({y_{i}^{k+2}}-{z^{\alpha_{k+2}}_{m}}\big)^{+}},\ldots\Big)\Bigg\rVert_{\alpha}\\
&=\sup_{r\geqslant k}\Big\lVert\big(y_{i}^{r}-z^{\alpha_{r}}_{m}\big)^{+}\Big\rVert_{\alpha_{r}}\\
&\leqslant\sup_{r\geqslant k}\tfrac{1}{3^{i}}\big\lVert y_{i}^{r}\big\rVert_{\alpha_{r}}\\
&\leqslant\frac{1}{3^{i}}\Big\lVert\Big(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{k-1},{y_{i}^{k}},{y_{i}^{k+1}},{y_{i}^{k+2}},\ldots\Big)\Big\rVert_{\alpha}.}\end{split}

This completes the verification of properties (1)-(5) and hence the inductive step.
∎
