Neuroanatomical and transcriptomic correlates of attention in schizophrenia
Authors: Hong-Liang Lu, Xue-Fei Wang, Di Wu, Ya-Juan Zhang, Wen-Jun Wu, Ya-Hong Zhang, Hai-Jun Zhang, Da Li, Kuzma Strelnikov, Hua-Ning Wang, Yongbin Wei, Xiao Chang, Long-Biao Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive impairment, characterized by impaired attention function, is widely recognized as one of the core symptoms of schizophrenia, significantly impacting the quality of life for individuals with this disorder. The objective of this cross-sectional study is to investigate the characteristics of attentional behavior and related brain structure and gene expression of schizophrenia, aiming to provide new trans-scale evidence. A total of 137 participants were enrolled in this study, including 65 patients with schizophrenia and 72 healthy controls. All participants underwent magnetic resonance imaging scans and completed cognitive tests. The results revealed that patients required more time (all p < 0.05) to complete key responses in attention-related tasks compared to the control group. Moreover, significant structural brain alterations were observed in patients with schizophrenia, including enlarged bilateral pallidum, smaller right hippocampus, and reduced cortical thickness in relevant brain regions (all p < 0.05). Further analysis demonstrated a correlation between alertness during Attention Network Test performance and pallidum volume among patients (r = 0.42, p = 4.30 × 10−4), and this correlation was associated with a specific pattern of gene expression in the patients' brain but not observed among controls. Our study reveals that decreased attentional ability in schizophrenia is accompanied by abnormal changes in pallidum along with specific transcriptional profile among patients with schizophrenia; these findings hold clinical significance for precise diagnosis and treatment strategies targeting schizophrenia.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Attention, Cognitive impairment, Transcriptomics, Neuroanatomy

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a complex psychiatric disorder characterized by a constellation of syndromes (Owen et al., 2016). Cognitive deficits, particularly in areas such as attention and cognitive control, are core features of schizophrenia and are closely linked to patients' social and occupational functioning (Kahn and Keefe, 2013). These cognitive capabilities have potential prognostic value, while their underlying macroscale brain abnormalities and microscale molecular underpinnings remain unclear (Liang et al., 2018).

A core cognitive deficit in schizophrenia is impairment of attention and related executive control functions (Luck and Gold, 2008). The attentional system can be further divided into three components: alerting, orienting, and conflict control, which are assessed by the Attention Network Test (ANT) (Fan et al., 2005). Each of these components is represented by partially separated brain regions. Studies have shown that patients with schizophrenia have different levels of impairment in these components: they may have difficulty in maintaining appropriate attention (alerting) under conditions challenging attention maintenance, and they may be distracted by irrelevant information (conflict control or executive functions) (Sawaki et al., 2016). Hyper-focusing impairs the ability of individuals with schizophrenia to distribute attention among multiple locations, and decreases the number of representations that can synchronously be sustained in working memory (Luck et al., 2019a; Luck et al., 2019b). Conflict control processes can also be tested using the Color-Word Stroop task, as a core component of executive functions (Weiss et al., 2007; Kucyi et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2021). In this study, both the ANT and Stroop task were administered to patients and healthy controls to provide a detailed assessment of attention impairments in schizophrenia.

Previous studies suggest that impairment of attention may be linked to genetic risk for schizophrenia (Stabenau and Pollin, 1993; Hemager et al., 2019). A study reported that the offspring of patients with psychosis performed worse than controls on tests of sustained attention and conflict control (Burton et al., 2018). However, the extent to which different components of attention are impaired and their specific associations with schizophrenia-related gene expression remain unclear (Hoonakker et al., 2017). Converging evidence has confirmed that schizophrenia-related brain structural abnormalities are closely associated with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified from both post-mortem brain tissues (Benjamin et al., 2022) and peripheral blood samples (Cui et al., 2024). Notably, peripheral blood transcriptome has been validated as a feasible, non-invasive in vivo proxy for central nervous system (CNS) pathology in schizophrenia: prior work has demonstrated significant correlations between peripheral blood and post-mortem brain gene expression profiles (Gardiner et al., 2012), particularly for genes involved in neurodevelopment (Dear et al., 2024), neuroinflammation (Na et al., 2014) and synaptic transmission (Yang et al., 2025)—core pathways implicated in schizophrenia pathogenesis. Based on this evidence, we posit that transcriptomic alterations may mirror the molecular changes underlying attention-related brain structural abnormalities, which further contribute to attention deficits in schizophrenia. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the gene expression associations with attention-related brain structural changes, in order to elucidate the cross-scale biological mechanisms of attention deficits in schizophrenia.

Grey matter volume change, particularly in frontotemporal lobes and subcortical regions, is one of the most pronounced brain abnormalities of schizophrenia (Rimol et al., 2012; Van Rheenen et al., 2018). Structural abnormalities in the brain have been linked to attention deficits (Mccutcheon et al., 2023). For example, surface area in the right caudal anterior cingulate was significantly associated with executive control, and greater surface area was associated with better behavioral performance (Epstein and Kumra, 2014). Furthermore, there is evidence regarding dysfunction of attention and other cognition accompanied by change of cortex in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or following traumatic brain injury (Spitz et al., 2013; Ambrosino et al., 2017). In schizophrenia, longitudinal studies have shown that the reductions in cortical volume and thickness correlate with cognitive decline in patients (Kubota et al., 2015). Additionally, subcortical structures, particularly the striatum, are implicated in attention deficits, with aberrant dopaminergic transmission disrupting cortical-striatal pathways and contributing to widespread cognitive dysfunction (Avram et al., 2019; Chand et al., 2022). These findings highlight the strong association between cognitive impairments and brain structural alterations in schizophrenia. Meanwhile, impaired cognitive ability is significantly associated with schizophrenia polygenic risk scores (Ohi et al., 2023). Schizophrenia polygenic risk scores associated structural and functional frontotemporal abnormalities may result in attention dysfunction (Qi et al., 2022). The single nucleotide polymorphism within NCAN (neurocan), regulating gene expression in the frontal and cerebellar cortices, may be a risk factor for attention dysfunction in schizophrenia (Wang et al., 2016). However, neuroanatomical and transcriptomic correlates of attention in schizophrenia remain unclear at the individual level.

This study characterized attention impairments in schizophrenia via the ANT and Stroop task, and explored the neuroanatomical and transcriptomic correlates of attention deficits. We tested three specific hypotheses: (1) schizophrenia patients show general attentional processing deficits [slower reaction time (RT) or higher error rate (ER) during tasks] and impaired attention subcomponents (alerting, orienting, and executive control) compared with healthy controls; (2) patients exhibit structural abnormalities in attention-related brain regions, and these alterations correlate with attention performance in patients only; (3) the brain structure-attention correlation in patients is associated with human brain regional gene expression profiles, with correlated genes enriched in schizophrenia-relevant biological pathways.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 137 participants were recruited in the present study, including 65 patients with schizophrenia and 72 healthy controls (Cui and Yin, 2022). The participants were diagnosed on the basis of Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), and consensus diagnoses were made by two experienced clinical psychiatrists. All participants received magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning at recruitment, and completed cognitive tests. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, First Affiliated Hospital (Xijing Hospital) of the Fourth Military Medical University and all participants provided written informed consent of participation in this study.

Neuropsychological assessment

Color-Word Stroop task

The Color-Word Stroop task is a widely used neuropsychological test designed to measure selective attention and conflict control functions (Weiss et al., 2007; Kucyi et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2021). Participants performed the Color-Word Stroop task outside the MRI scanner as a measure of RT and ER. During the task, four Chinese words (‘红’, ‘绿’, ‘蓝’, and ‘黄’ meaning ‘red’, ‘green’, ‘blue’, and ‘yellow’) or a letter ‘XX’ were displayed on a black screen and printed in these four colors (red, green, blue, and yellow). Participants had to decide whether the color of the word matched its semantic meaning, and to respond by pressing letters on the keyboard (press D if the color of the word is red; in the same way, g represents green, j represents blue, and k represents yellow). The experiment consisted of three blocks in random order. One block is the neutral condition, which used the letter ‘XX’ as the stimuli to control for semantic distractions. The second block is the congruent condition, in which color of the word matched its semantic meaning (e.g., ‘red’ written in red color). The last block is the incongruent condition, as color of the word was not consistent with its semantic meaning (e.g. ‘red’ written in green color). Participants completed three separate blocks of the task, where each block consisted of 72 trials. Each trial was presented for 2000 ms, including 1500 ms stimuli presentation and a 500 ms inter-stimulus interval. Each block series takes 144 s, while the entire experiment uses 442 s and all the stimulus appears randomly. Before the task began, a training session was administered. Experimental design and response recording were performed using the Eprime3.0 software (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Attention Network Test

ANT is designed to test three components of attention network, which consists of alerting, orienting and conflict control (Fan et al., 2005). There are three cue conditions (no cue, center cue, and spatial cue) and two target conditions (congruent and incongruent) in the test. Stimuli consisted of a row of 5 black arrows, with arrowheads pointing leftward or rightward and participants were required to decide the direction of the center arrowhead. To introduce a conflict resolution component, the central arrow was flanked by congruent or incongruent stimuli. In the test, a fixation cross appears in the center of the screen all the time. In each trial, each cue (none, center, or spatial cue) appears for 200 ms. After a variable period of interval (300–11,800 ms), the target (the center arrow) and flankers of left and right arrows (congruent or incongruent flankers) were presented until the participant responded by pressing a button, but for no longer than 2000 ms (Supplementary Fig. S2). There were six runs in this experiment with 36 trials in each run. In each run, the six trial types (three cue conditions by two target conditions) were presented in a predetermined order to ensure the sequence of trial types is counterbalanced.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

All participants underwent MRI scanning at the Radiology Department of Xijing Hospital using a GE Discovery MR750 3.0 T scanner. Participants were acquired a high-resolution T1-weighted MRI scan and a T2-weighted scan to exclude images of organic disease. During scanning, participants were told to keep relaxed and their eyes closed, but not to fall asleep. In addition, the head of participants was fixed with a sponge pad, to minimize the head movement and machine noise. T1-weighted MRI scan was acquired with the 3D T1BRAVO protocol using the following parameters: repetition time 8.2 ms, echo time 3.2 ms, flip angle 12°, field of view 256 × 256 mm, matrix 256 × 256, slice thickness 1 mm, and sagittal slices 196.

MRI preprocessing was performed using the FreeSurfer version 6.0 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). The following steps were carried out: correction of bias field uniformity; skull removal; dividing brain tissue into grey matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid; cortical surface reconstruction; registration from individual space to template space using nonlinear transformation. The preprocessed images were segmented into 68 cortical regions according to the Desikan-Killiany atlas (Desikan et al., 2006), and 14 subcortical regions. Structural measurement of each brain region was obtained, including cortical thickness, surface area, and grey matter volume.

Statistical analyses

Comparison of behavioral data

The Stroop effect was calculated by subtracting the mean RT and ER of neutral/congruent condition from the mean RT and ER of incongruent condition in Color-Word Stroop task (Vitkovitch et al., 2002). The effect of alerting, orienting and executive were assessed separately by ANT (Fan et al., 2005): the effect of alerting was calculated by subtracting the mean RT of center-cue condition from the mean RT of no-cue condition; the effect of orienting was calculated by subtracting the mean RT of spatial cue conditions from the mean RT of center-cue conditions; the effect of executive function was calculated by subtracting the mean RT of congruent conditions from the mean RT of incongruent conditions.

A two-tiered statistical analysis strategy was adopted for behavioral data, wherein unadjusted two-sample t-tests were performed for descriptive supplementary analysis to present the raw between-group differences in all behavioral metrics for full data transparency, and a two-sample F-test for equal variances was used to characterize between-group differences in the variability of behavioral performance, with this descriptive variance analysis applied only to reflect the well-recognized clinical and neurobiological heterogeneity of schizophrenia and not used to draw core inferential conclusions; for the main analysis, one-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted to confirm the robustness of between-group mean differences, with age, sex, and education level included as a priori covariates to eliminate the confounding effects of these demographic factors, and all core between-group difference conclusions in the manuscript were based on this covariate-adjusted analysis. Notably, patients and healthy controls were strictly matched for age, sex, and education years (Table 1, all p > 0.05), and the statistical significance threshold for all relevant analyses was set at a p-value <0.05.

Comparison of brain structural data

Statistical analysis was conducted on Matlab (R2023a) platform. Linear regression was performed with regional brain structural volume as dependent variable and group, age, sex, education level and intracranial volume (ICV) as independent variable. Multiple testing effect was corrected with false discovery rate (FDR) of q < 0.05 for 150 regional measurements (14 subcortical volume, 68 cortical thickness and 68 surface area).

Correlation between brain structure and behavioral data

We performed Pearson's correlation to investigate the relationship between brain structure and behavioral performance separately in patients and controls. We focused on the regional measurement which showed a significant group difference from previous analysis to test behavioral relevance of these regions. Behavioral performance include RT of Stroop and ANT summary scores: Stroop incongruent - congruent; Stroop incongruent - neutral; ANT alerting, ANT orienting and ANT conflict effects. Age, sex, and education level were regressed out prior to the correlation analysis, and multiple comparisons was corrected with FDR.

Imaging data and the correlation with Allen Human Brain Atlas brain gene expression

To determine whether gene expression would influence association between brain and cognitive performance, we tested the association between Pearson's correlation value between brain volume and performance (one value per region) and brain regional transcriptional data extracted from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) (http://human.brain-map.org/) (Hawrylycz et al. 2012) using the ENIGMA toolbox (Larivière et al., 2021). To ensure spatial consistency across subjects, genes whose expression similarity across donors fell below a stability threshold of r < 0.2 were excluded, resulting in a finalized set of 12,668 genes for downstream analysis. The FDR significant associations were further tested for spatial autocorrelation using generative null models (Burt et al., 2020), following protocols established previously (Cui et al., 2023). This analysis tests whether the spatial pattern of a region's involvement in attention (as measured by the brain-behavior correlation) is associated with its typical gene expression profile.

Functional enrichment of significant gene set

Gene enrichment analysis was conducted using Metascape (Zhou et al., 2019) (https://metascape.org), a web-based tool for functional enrichment analysis. Gene set which showed a significant correlation with brain-ANT association was submitted for enrichment analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Processes was used to annotate biological functions of the gene set. All genes in the genome were used as the enrichment background. Statistical significance was determined using the hypergeometric test with a p-value <0.01, adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. To provide further biological insights, enrichment networks were constructed to visualize the relationships between enriched terms using the Cytoscape software (Shannon et al. 2003). The most statistically significant term within a cluster was chosen to represent the cluster.

Results

A total of 137 participants were recruited in the present study, including 65 patients with schizophrenia and 72 healthy controls (Cui and Yin, 2022). Patients and controls were matched for age, sex and education years. Detailed demographic and clinical information of participants was shown in Table 1.

Behavioral results

Color-Word Stroop task

Unadjusted two-sample t-tests were first performed as descriptive supplementary analysis to characterize the raw between-group differences in RT and ER. Across all three task conditions (neutral, congruent, and incongruent), patients with schizophrenia exhibited slower RT and higher ER relative to healthy controls in the raw data analysis (all p < 0.01). These observed group differences were further validated via one-way ANCOVA with age, sex, and years of education included as covariates, with the significant between-group differences remaining robust after adjustment (all adjusted p < 0.05). No significant between-group differences in mean RT or ER were observed for the derived Stroop effect indices in either the unadjusted t-tests or covariate-adjusted ANCOVA models (all p > 0.05). Variance analysis further demonstrated significantly higher variability in the ER of the incongruent minus neutral condition among patients relative to controls (F-stats = 0.37, p = 0.0001, df1,2 = 70,57). This descriptive variance finding reflects the well-recognized clinical and neurobiological heterogeneity of schizophrenia (Table 2 and Fig. 1).

ANT

For the ANT task, unadjusted two-sample t-tests were first performed as descriptive supplementary analysis, showing that patients had slower RT in all five conditions (incongruent, congruent, no cue, spatial cue, and center cue) relative to controls (all p < 0.01), which was further confirmed by covariate-adjusted ANCOVA (all adjusted p < 0.05). The mean ER did not show significant between-group differences and was generally low in both groups, suggesting a ceiling effect for ANT ER measurement. The derived ANT effect indices (alerting, orienting, and executive) showed no significant between-group differences in mean RT or ER in either unadjusted or adjusted analyses (all p > 0.05), but exhibited significantly higher variance in patients than in controls for all three effects (all p < 0.05). These descriptive variance findings align with the well-recognized clinical and neurobiological heterogeneity of schizophrenia (Table 2 and Fig. 1).

Imaging results

Group differences in brain volume, cortical thickness and surface area

We analyzed group differences in brain volume, cortical thickness and surface area between patients and controls using regression model controlling for age, sex, education and intracranial volume (ICV). Six regions showed significant group differences including larger bilateral pallidum and smaller right hippocampus in patients than controls. Patients also showed a thinner cortical thickness in the left superior frontal gyrus and rostral middle frontal gyrus and a smaller surface area in the right middle temporal gyrus than controls after FDR correction (Fig. 2a).

Correlation between brain structural measurement and task performance

Correlation analysis was carried out between brain regions showing a significant group-difference and task performance measured using RT in patients and controls separately (Fig. 2b). After FDR correction, only left pallidum volume and ANT alerting effect showed a significant correlation in patients (r = 0.42, p = 4.30 × 10−4, df = 62), but not in controls (r = −0.03, p = 0.79, df = 69).

Transcriptomic correlates of brain-ANT association

The correlation between brain structural measurement and performance reflects cognitive relevance of that region, which is further linked to transcriptional data from the AHBA dataset (Hawrylycz et al., 2012). We focused on the ANT alerting effect as it showed a significant correlation with brain volume in patients (Fig. 2b). The brain-ANT correlation coefficients in patients and controls were shown in Fig. 3a. Both patients and controls exhibited a high correlation between ANT alerting effect and superior frontal and parietal regions, consistent with the involvement of these regions in attention control reported in previous studies (Fan et al., 2005). In addition, there were also differences in the brain-ANT correlation between patients and controls. For example, in subcortical regions, patients exhibited a stronger correlation than controls.

Correlation between gene expression and brain-ANT correlation showed that 1620 out of the 12,668 tested genes exhibited a significant association in patients, whereas none of the genes survived FDR in controls. Among the 1620 genes, 610 genes showed correlations with cortical regions and 1053 genes showed correlations with subcortical regions in patients. Spin test confirmed that these correlations were not driven by auto-correlation between brain maps. To understand the enriched biological processes of these 1620 genes, we performed enrichment analysis using GO Biological Processes (Fig. 3b). The top 20 enriched terms included neural system development, cell cycle and cell metabolism. The largest enriched network involved pathways related to neural system development.

Discussion

The present study systematically characterized attentional dysfunction in schizophrenia via integrated behavioral, neuroanatomical and transcriptomic analyses. Key findings include significant impairments in overall attentional processing and psychomotor ability in patients, widespread structural brain alterations across cortical and subcortical regions, and a patient-specific association between pallidum volume and alerting function that correlates with schizophrenia-relevant gene expression profiles in the human brain.

Attention impairment is well recognized as a core cognitive deficit in patients with schizophrenia (Khalil et al., 2022), and our results revealed robust alterations in attentional task performance in schizophrenia patients. Consistent with well-established prior literature (Ferchiou et al., 2010; Mayer et al., 2015; Hahn et al., 2022), patients showed significantly slower RT and higher ER across all individual conditions of the Stroop and ANT tasks, which reflects a robust impairment in overall attentional processing efficiency and psychomotor speed. This generalized slowing is a well-documented, stable cognitive feature of schizophrenia, and is closely associated with patients' daily functional outcomes (Janssens et al., 2018; Northoff et al., 2021; Suter et al., 2025). Notably, we also found that patients exhibited significantly higher variability in all derived attention effect indices, which aligns with the well-recognized clinical and neurobiological heterogeneity of schizophrenia.

In contrast, we did not detect significant between-group differences in the mean values of the derived attention subcomponent indices, including the Stroop interference effect and the ANT alerting, orienting, and executive control effects. There are several evidence-based explanations for this finding. First, we used a short version of the ANT to ensure task completion and reduce fatigue in patients, which may have limited the task's sensitivity to detect subtle component-specific attention deficits (Lu et al., 2020). Second, all patients in our sample were receiving antipsychotic treatment, which may have partially ameliorated specific attention subcomponent impairments, as prior studies have reported that antipsychotics can stabilize executive function in patients with schizophrenia (Mccutcheon et al., 2023). Third, the high variability of attention performance in our patient sample may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant mean group differences in the derived effect indices.

It is also worth noting that patients showed slower RT along with higher ER across all task conditions, which suggests that the speed-accuracy tradeoff (Balci et al., 2011) is impaired in schizophrenia, reflecting the dysregulation of the cognitive control system in the disorder.

Reductions in volume, thickness, and area were observed throughout the brain in patients with schizophrenia, consistent with the characteristic pattern of degenerative brain changes reported in previous studies (van Erp et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2023). Structural deficits in specific brain regions, such as reduced volume in the right hippocampus and thinner cortex in the rostral middle frontal gyrus, were observed in patients with schizophrenia and are likely implicated in their cognitive dysfunction. This aligns with previous findings by Yasuda et al. linking hippocampal volume reduction to cognitive decline in the disorder (Yasuda et al., 2022). However, the change of deep brain nucleus was opposite to the cortex. In our experiment, the volume of the bilateral pallidum significantly increased in individuals with schizophrenia who had undergone varying degrees of medical treatment. These changes were suggested to be influenced by the cumulative effects of antipsychotic medication and were positively correlated with the duration of the schizophrenia illness (van Erp et al., 2016; Paul et al., 2024). Differently from the previous studies, other correlations between behavioral performance and the indexes of brain structure in schizophrenia (Khalil et al., 2022), were not observed in the present study. As we can see, the description of behavioral variation demonstrated a significant difference in selective attention and psychomotor ability between individuals with schizophrenia and healthy controls. However, these differences were not entirely linked to the observed brain changes. Additionally, in our study, the connection between psychomotor ability—represented by the mean RT in the ANT—and brain structure was found to be significant in healthy individuals. Thus, one can assume that the behavioral change in schizophrenia may not correspond precisely to brain structural variation. Similarly to the results of present study, a previous study found that degradation of cognitive performance would not map completely onto the increase or decrease of brain tissue in schizophrenia (Karantonis et al., 2021). The observed inconsistency in literature may be attributed to several factors: the impaired connection between cognition and brain function in schizophrenia, the potential positive effects of drug therapy, or the limited sensitivity of the cognitive tasks used (Am et al., 2019). Furthermore, the functional connectivity in targeted regions of the brain may be more appropriate to reflect the variation of behavior (Sun et al., 2022), which should be explored in the future study.

Regarding the clinical characteristics of schizophrenia, the cognitive correlates with cortical thickness were found to be associated with brain gene expression, primarily involving the DEGs related to schizophrenia (184 overlapping genes) in this study. A recent meta-analysis identified cortical volume in the Heschl's gyrus and planum temporale as typically showing associations with attention domain in schizophrenia (Khalil et al., 2022). Cognitive dysfunction of schizophrenia is related to the altered whole blood gene expression of immunological process (Ukkola-Vuoti et al., 2019). Taking the associations between brain structure, DEGs, and clinical characteristics into consideration, the brain structure might mediate a cross-scale effect of genes on cognition in patients.

Some limitations of the present study should be noted. A portion of the participants in this study received general treatment for schizophrenia, which may have influenced the interpretation of the results. Additionally, a ‘ceiling effect’ was observed in the cognitive tasks, as the difficulty level of these tasks was not well-suited for some participants with schizophrenia. Future studies should consider using more personalized cognitive tasks. Furthermore, a more comprehensive approach, such as follow-up visits, should be adopted to better explore the variations in brain imaging and behavioral performance in schizophrenia.

In conclusion, this study found that patients with schizophrenia exhibited lower psychomotor ability and selective attention, associated with the left pallidum volume. Additionally, shared genes between schizophrenia, attention, and cortical thickness were identified. While cognitive impairment and brain alterations were both observed in schizophrenia, behavioral changes were not consistently synchronized with structural brain changes. This suggests that diagnostic and therapeutic targets in schizophrenia in future may be set separately to address the specific dysfunctions in cognition and brain structure as a multidimensional approach to make clinical procedures more rational and efficient.
