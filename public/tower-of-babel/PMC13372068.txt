Cerebrospinal Fluid Transcriptional Immune Pathways Linked to Survival in HIV-Associated Tuberculous Meningitis
Authors: Martineau Louine, Ravi Dandekar, Sumanth P Reddy, Mary C Karalius, Greer Waldrop, Shiyin Wang, Jane Gakuru, Sarah Kimuda, Timothy Mugabi, Abdu K Musubire, Enock Kagimu, Mahsa Abassi, Mable Kabahubya, Darlisha A Williams, Hoang Van Phan, Biyue Dai, Maham Zia, Kelsey C Zorn, Camille Fouassier, Chloe Gerungan, Pedro S Marra, Caleb P Skipper, Nathan C Bahr, Charles R Langelier, Fiona V Cresswell, David R Boulware, David B Meya, Michael R Wilson

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculous meningitis (TBM) has approximately 50% mortality in people living with HIV. We investigated differences in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) host immune responses associated with short-term mortality.MethodsWe enrolled a prospective cohort of adults with HIV-related TBM in Kampala, Uganda. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of bulk CSF RNA was used to detect co-infecting or alternate CNS pathogens and refine diagnoses. Host transcriptomic profiles from the refined cohort were compared between 14-day survivors and non-survivors.ResultsCerebrospinal fluid mNGS reclassified or excluded 14% of participants based on pathogen detection, yielding 110 participants for transcriptomic analysis.Survivors upregulated T-cell receptor signaling (LCK, FYN, LAT), T-cell survival and differentiation (IL7, CD27, IL12RB1), B-cell receptor signaling (CD81, PLCG2, TNFRSF13C), cytotoxic lymphocyte and NK cell genes (KLRD1, ULBP1), TNF signaling, and class I MHC antigen processing pathways, while downregulating neutrophil chemoattractant CXCL1 and classical complement genes C4A and C4B. Unsupervised clustering identified a hypoinflammatory subgroup with significantly elevated mortality.ConclusionsShort-term TBM survival was associated with upregulation of adaptive immunity alongside TNF signaling and IFN-γ-driven class I MHC antigen processing pathways, with concurrent restraint of complement and neutrophil pathways. This supports investigation of adjunctive targeted agents that preserve protective responses while selectively dampening injurious innate pathways.Keywords: tuberculous meningitis, tuberculosis, metagenomic next-generation sequencing, cerebrospinal fluid, transcriptomics

Approximately one-quarter of the world's population is infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), and over 10 million people each year develop active tuberculosis (TB) [1]. Tuberculous meningitis (TBM) is a devastating manifestation of TB, with a mortality rate of approximately 50% among people living with HIV-1 [1]. In 2018, the National Institutes of Health identified 2 major knowledge gaps impeding TBM management: (1) the lack of gold-standard diagnostic testing that is both sensitive and specific for detecting Mtb in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and (2) a poor understanding of TBM immunopathogenesis [2].

Recent efforts to overcome diagnostic limitations have focused on improving nucleic-acid-based CSF testing, including targeted methods such as TB polymerase chain reaction, and agnostic approaches such as metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), though no test adequately “rules out” TBM [3–6]. Questions remain regarding TBM pathogenesis, including the degree to which mortality is driven by Mtb itself versus the exuberant host immune response [7]. Although adjunctive dexamethasone confers a survival benefit in TBM, this benefit was smaller and nonsignificant among patients living with HIV both in a subgroup analysis of the original trial (hazard ratio = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.52–1.41) and the subsequent Adjunctive Corticosteroids for Tuberculous Meningitis in HIV-Positive Adults (ACT HIV) trial (hazard ratio = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.66 to 1.10), illustrating that differing CNS immune responses likely drive variations in survival benefit across patient populations [8, 9].

The objective of this study was to analyze CSF host gene expression profiles associated with short-term mortality in HIV-associated TBM. We analyzed a well-characterized clinical cohort of Ugandan adults living with HIV and diagnosed with possible, probable, or definite TBM via routine clinical testing [5, 10, 11], using CSF mNGS to refine the diagnostic accuracy of the cohort, and bulk CSF transcriptomics to identify biological mechanisms associated with 14-day mortality.

METHODS

Study Cohort

Patients presenting to Kiruddu Regional Referral Hospital or Mulago National Referral Hospital in Kampala, Uganda, from 2018 to 2023 with known or suspected HIV and concern for CNS infection (such as headache, fever, nuchal rigidity, neurologic deficit, and/or encephalopathy) were prospectively screened for inclusion, as part of the “Improving Diagnostics and Neurocognitive Outcomes in HIV/AIDS-related Meningitis” study. All consented participants underwent lumbar puncture during admission with standard CSF studies (eg, cell count, protein, glucose, microscopy, Gram stain, cryptococcal antigen). A subset were included in this current study if they met the following criteria: (1) possible, probable, or definite TBM as defined by the Uniform Case Definition for TBM [10], (2) anti-TB treatment for 3 or fewer days prior to CSF collection, (3) confirmed HIV co-infection, and (4) no known co-infection with any other CNS pathogen by routine clinical diagnostics or mNGS. Cerebrospinal fluid specimens used for these analyses were directly collected into Zymo DNA/RNA Shield collection tubes (Zymo Research; Irvine, CA), frozen at −80°C within 8 hours of lumbar puncture, and subsequently shipped to UCSF on dry ice.

Differences in baseline demographics, clinical data, and sequencing parameters were compared between participants who survived to day 14 of hospitalization versus those who died. For continuous data, a 2-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test was performed, whereas for binary and categorical data, a Fisher's exact test was performed. No participants were lost to follow-up. Total bulk CSF RNA was sequenced from 133 participants in 6 separate batches. Data from 38 participants were previously published [4]. The detailed protocol for RNA extraction, sequencing library preparation, sequencing, and pathogen detection is included in Supplementary Methods.

Cerebrospinal Fluid Differential Gene Expression Analysis

Human transcript counts were generated using a computational pipeline that incorporates quality filtering (PRICE, v1.2) [12] and human genome alignment (kallisto, v0.46.1) [13]. In order to account for the pauci-cellularity and low RNA abundance of CSF that result in zero-inflated transcriptomic datasets, sample weights were calculated prior to differential gene expression. Both zero-inflated negative binomial and standard negative binomial weighting models were fit (ZINB-WaVE, v1.30.0) using combinations of a priori suspected confounders, including age, sex, CD4 count, sequencing batch, CSF WBC, number of nonzero gene counts, and percent guanine/cytosine (GC) content [14–16]. Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values were used to select the best-fitting model and covariate combination. After applying sample weights, gene counts were filtered to only include protein-coding genes expressed in >20% of the samples.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed based on 14-day TBM mortality (Deseq2, v1.48.0) [17] and defined using a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P < .05 and an absolute log-fold change ≥ 1.5. Results were visualized utilizing the EnhancedVolcano R package (v1.26.0), and genes in the “Immune System” event in the Reactome database (reactome.db, v3.22) were annotated. To explore transcriptomic heterogeneity in a complementary, untargeted approach, pathway, upstream regulator, and causal network analyses were conducted with the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) toolkit (Qiagen; version: 145030503, Supplementary Methods) [18]. In addition, unsupervised hierarchical clustering was performed on patient gene expression profiles using the 1000 protein-coding genes with the highest variance (Supplementary Methods). Differential gene expression was also performed (1) based on 14-day mortality among definite TBM only, (2) across clusters, and (3) within clusters based on mortality.

RESULTS

Cohort Refinement via Cerebrospinal Fluid Metagenomic Next-Generation Sequencing for Pathogen Detection

From 2018 to 2023, 163 adults who were hospitalized with possible, probable, or definite TBM were enrolled (Figure 1A). After exclusion of participants with negative or unknown HIV testing (n = 20) or with greater than 3 days of anti-TB treatment prior to lumbar puncture (n = 10), the remaining 133 participants underwent CSF mNGS testing. Baseline characteristics of the 3 pre-mNGS TBM diagnostic groups are available in Supplementary Table 1.

Study overview. A, Flow diagram describing the inclusion and exclusion process for CSF mNGS testing and the final transcriptomic analysis. B, Sankey diagram depicting how CSF mNGS impacted the diagnostic recharacterization for the 133 patients with possible, probable, and definite TBM who underwent CFS mNGS. C, CNS alternate infections and co-infections detected by CSF mNGS.

The average sequencing depth was 73 million RNA reads per sample (IQR: 26 million—148 million). After quality filtering and removal of host reads, an average of 4.2 million RNA reads passed filters (IQR: 642 428–4.9 million). Cerebrospinal fluid mNGS detected Mtb in 75.5% (40/53) of participants diagnosed with definite TBM via Xpert MTB/Rif Ultra or culture. Cerebrospinal fluid mNGS reclassified 9 cases as definite TBM (7 of 63 probable [11%] and 2 of 17 possible [12%] cases) and identified alternate or co-infections in 10 patients (herpes simplex-1 virus, varicella zoster virus, Toxoplasma gondii, Cryptococcus neoformans; Figure 1B and 1C). Participants who remained in the possible TBM category after mNGS were excluded from the transcriptomic analysis, as were those with evidence of comorbid neurologic infection (Figure 1A). Following CSF mNGS-based cohort refinement, 110 participants living with HIV and with probable TBM (n = 51) or definite TBM (n = 59) were included in the differential gene expression analyses.

Tuberculous Meningitis Cohort Demographics

Among the 110 participants, 48% (n = 53) were female, the median age was 35 years (IQR: 30, 42), and the median CD4 count was 78 cells/μL (IQR: 38, 186) (Table 1). Thirty-eight percent (41/110) were receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) at presentation. The median CSF white cell count was 45 cells/μL (n = 105, IQR: <5, 200), and the median opening pressure was 170 mmH2O (n = 89, IQR: 120–240). Overall, 85 (78%) of the 110 participants were alive 14 days after study enrollment, and survival was higher among males compared to females (86% vs 68%, OR = 0.37, P = .024; Supplementary Table 2). No other statistically significant associations were identified between baseline characteristics and 14-day survival (Table 1).

Host Transcriptomics and Differential Gene Expression

The median nonzero protein-coding gene count in survivors was 8787 (IQR: 3058–14 241) versus 3482 (IQR: 2351–8861) in non-survivors (P = .02). The weighting model with the lowest AIC was a zero-inflated negative binomial model which only included sequencing batch (without other covariates) and was therefore used to generate sample weights. Differential gene expression analysis revealed that 2190 genes were differentially expressed between 14-day TBM survivors and non-survivors, including 1770 upregulated genes and 420 downregulated genes (Figure 2A). The biological coefficient of variation estimates ranged from 0.8 to 2.6 (Supplementary Figure 1).

A, Volcano plot demonstrating upregulated and downregulated genes among 14-day survivors. NS, nonsignificant; FC, fold change. In comparison to patients who died before day 14, significantly upregulated proinflammatory genes included IL1A, TNF, and NF-kB pathway and BCR signaling components, whereas IL-1RN and complement proteins C4A and C4B were downregulated among 14-day survivors. Tuberculous meningitis survivors exhibited upregulation of T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling genes, including the proximal kinases LCK proto-oncogene (LCK) and FYN proto-oncogene (FYN), along with the adaptor protein linker for activation of T-cells (LAT). T-cell survival and differentiation genes also demonstrated increased expression among survivors, including interleukin 7 (IL7), CD27, interleukin-12 receptor subunit beta 1 (IL12RB1), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 4 (STAT4). B-cell signaling genes were similarly upregulated, including CD81, B-cell activating factor receptor (TNFRSF13C), and phospholipase C gamma 2 (PLCG2). Genes associated with cytotoxic lymphocyte and natural killer (NK) cell activity were also upregulated, including NK cell receptor KLRD1 (CD94) and NKG2D stress ligands ULBP1 and ULBP3, alongside CD8B. Survivors additionally upregulated the IL-23 receptor (IL23R) and the Th17-associated chemokine receptor CCR6.

In addition, survivors upregulated genes with both innate and adaptative immune signaling functions, including IL1A, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and the canonical NF-κB pathway component TNF receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2). Interferon signaling pathways were enhanced, with increased expression of stimulator of interferon genes 1 (STING1), interferon regulatory factor 6 (IRF6), tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2), interferon-induced effector IFIT3, and dsRNA-sensing kinase EIF2AK2 (PKR). C-type lectin receptors CLEC7A (dectin-1) and CLEC6A (dectin-2), alongside CLEC4C and COLEC12, were upregulated in survivors. Broad upregulation of ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) components was observed, including E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes, E3 ubiquitin, and proteasome regulatory subunits. Conversely, survivors exhibited downregulation of IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL1RN), the neutrophil chemoattractant gene CXCL1, classical complement pathway genes C4A and C4B, and the co-stimulatory receptor CD40.

Gene Pathway Analysis

Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (Qiagen) [18] was used to contextualize differentially expressed genes within known biological pathways and networks (Figure 2B). Inflammatory pathways significantly upregulated in TBM survivors included class I MHC-mediated antigen processing and presentation, TCR signaling, C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), CD28 family co-stimulation, and acute phase response signaling. In contrast, TBM survivors demonstrated significant downregulation of the interferon-γ-activated inhibitor of translation (GAIT translation) and granzyme A signaling pathways.

Upstream Regulator Analysis

Upstream regulator analysis identified that microtubule associated protein tau (MAPT) was predicted to be activated among TBM survivors based on downstream target activation of several immunologic genes, including TNF, IL1A, and IL12RB1, and inhibition of IL1RN (Figure 3A). Other significant predicted upstream regulators included TNF, IL7, X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1), and transcription factor EB (TFEB). The graphical representation in Figure 3B highlights the multifaceted role of the upstream regulators IL7 and TNF in the development, activation, differentiation, and homeostasis of T cells, B cells, and mononuclear cells.

Survival in Definite Tuberculous Meningitis: Differential Gene Expression

A subgroup differential gene expression analysis was conducted in the 59 participants with definite TBM based on 14-day survival. The core survival signature was broadly conserved, including upregulation of TCR signaling genes (LCK, FYN, STAT4, TRAF2, STING1), NK and cytotoxic lymphocyte genes (KLRK1, KLRC2, GZMM), and B-cell genes (TNFRSF13B), and downregulation of complement (C4A, C4B, CXCL1, IL1RN) and co-stimulatory gene CD40 among survivors (Supplementary Figure 2). Additional differentially expressed genes were identified among definite TBM short-term survivors: the TCR signature included ZAP70, ITK, CD247, and CD3G; complement downregulation extended to C1QB, C1QC, C2, and complement factor B (CFB) (spanning both classical and alternative pathways); and NKG2D receptor KLRK1 was upregulated alongside its ligands. Downstream type I interferon effectors IRF7 and IFI27 were downregulated in survivors despite upregulation of upstream innate sensing genes STING1 and TYK2.

Notably, several innate immune genes prominent in the combined cohort of definite and probable TBM—including TNF, IL1A, and C-type lectin receptors CLEC7A and CLEC6A—did not reach significance in the definite TBM subgroup analysis. Supplemental comparison of probable and definite TBM groups revealed that IL-1 signaling genes (IL1A, IL1RAP) and multiple CLR pathway genes (CLEC4A, CLEC4D, CLEC4E, CLEC5A) were globally upregulated in definite TBM regardless of survival outcome, alongside broad S100-family DAMPs (S100A8, S100A9, S100A12) and chemokine (CXCL8, CCL2, CCL3) upregulation (Supplementary Figure 2).

Unsupervised Hierarchical Clustering

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering identified a subgroup (n = 26; Figure 4A) (hereafter referred to as the “hypoinflammatory” cluster) with elevated mortality (38% vs 28% and 9%, P = .02), lower CSF lactate (P = .04), and a lower number of protein-coding genes with nonzero counts (median 2392 vs 20 574 and 6,393, P < .001). There were no other significant differences in baseline characteristics between the clusters, though there was a nonsignificant trend toward lower CSF WBC among the hypoinflammatory cluster (Supplementary Table 3).

The hypoinflammatory cluster was characterized by global downregulation of both innate and adaptive immune pathways, including antigen presentation genes (HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, TAP1, B2 M), T-cell activation and trafficking genes (LAT, CD3G, ITGAL), Th1 signaling genes (IL12RB1, STAT1, IFNGR2), myeloid/macrophage activation markers (CD14, CSF1R, TLR4), and interferon alpha and beta receptor subunit 1 (IFNAR1) (Figure 4B). The hypoinflammatory cluster also demonstrated a mixed pattern of complement pathway gene expression, with downregulation of C1QA and C1QC, and upregulation of C7. Neutrophil-associated genes including CXCL2 and Fc fragment of IgG receptor IIIb (FCGR3B) were also significantly downregulated. Within the hypoinflammatory cluster, survivors (n = 16) demonstrated upregulation of TNF receptor superfamily members 1A and 1B (TNFRSF1A, TNFRSF1B), interferon gamma receptor 2 (IFNGR2), integrin subunit alpha L (ITGAL), IL-1 receptor accessory protein (IL1RAP), Fc fragment of IgE receptor Ig (FCER1G), and CCL5 (Figure 4C).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we define differentially expressed genes and pathways in the CSF of HIV-related TBM and found that short-term survival was associated with broad immune upregulation, but with some restraint of innate immune responses including complement and neutrophil recruitment genes. Similar to our previous work [4], CSF mNGS reclassified a meaningful proportion of probable and possible TBM cases and identified clinically significant co-infections, enabling more rigorous cohort refinement and reducing diagnostic misclassification that could otherwise bias transcriptomic results. These results suggest that other TBM clinical and observational studies (and perhaps more broadly, the Uniform Case Definition for definite TBM) [10] should consider including CSF mNGS or other broad-based diagnostic testing approaches to improve diagnostic accuracy.

In addition, this study is one of few to successfully leverage bulk CSF RNA-seq data to define biologically plausible mechanisms in neurologic disease [4, 19]. In contrast to blood and respiratory fluid transcriptomics, where cellular biomass is abundant, the pauci-cellular nature of CSF severely limits transcriptomic profiling [20, 21]. Our findings suggest that bulk CSF RNA-seq, when paired with rigorous cohort refinement, is an effective method for understanding host response pathways and offers a viable alternative to more complex and costly single-cell sequencing approaches, which require intact cells and can therefore only be applied to a fraction of available CSF samples.

Adaptive Immunity in Tuberculous Meningitis Survival

Survivors exhibited robust upregulation of adaptive immune signaling genes and pathways, particularly those involving T-cell and B-cell responses. A significant component of the TCR response is likely driven by CD8+ T-cell signaling, as demonstrated by the strong upregulation of class I MHC signaling pathways and supported by prior studies revealing CD8+ T-cell enrichment in TBM [22–24]. Upregulation of IL7 (which aids in T-cell homeostasis and survival) [25], CD27, and IL12RB1 further support the importance of preserved T-cell function in TBM survival [26, 27].

In contrast to T-cell responses, B-cell responses to Mtb are poorly understood and historically thought to play a less significant role [28]. Upregulation of B-cell signaling genes CD81, PLCG2, and the B-cell activating factor receptor raises the possibility that B-cell activity contributes to survival through antigen presentation rather than classical T-cell-dependent activation, germinal center formation, and antibody secretion—an interpretation supported by the concurrent downregulation among survivors of CD40, which is required for co-stimulatory upregulation and class switching [29]. The functional significance of these findings requires validation through protein-level or cellular studies.

Survivors also upregulated genes consistent with cytotoxic lymphocyte and NK cell activity, including the NK cell receptor KLRD1 (CD94) and NKG2D stress ligands [30, 31]; in the definite TBM subgroup, this signature was extended by upregulation of the NKG2D receptor itself (KLRK1), activating receptor KLRC2, and granzyme M (GZMM). Together, these findings suggest that NKG2D-mediated recognition of stressed and infected cells represents a complementary cytotoxic mechanism contributing to Mtb control [32]. Additionally, upregulation of IL23R and CCR6 suggests a Th17 component to the survivor immune response, consistent with the established roles of IL-17-mediated immunity in granuloma maintenance and neutrophil modulation during Mtb infection [33, 34].

Tumor Necrosis Factor and Interferon Gamma Signaling and Innate Immune Balance

Survivors demonstrated coordinated upregulation of key innate immune signaling genes and pathways, including TNF and downstream NF-kB signaling components [35]. Tumor necrosis factor signaling is critical for Mtb control, as evidenced clinically by the increased risk of TB reactivation with TNF inhibitors [36]. Upregulation of IL-1 signaling components was similarly observed, consistent with IL-1's pleiotropic roles in granuloma formation, macrophage activation, and blood–brain barrier disruption [37].

In addition to TNF and IL-1 signaling, downregulation of the antagonistic IFN-γ-activated inhibitor of translation pathway (GAIT translation pathway) resulted in net upregulation of interferon signaling [38], further reflected by upregulation of IFIT3 and EIF2AK2 (PKR) [39]. This is reinforced by strong upregulation of class I MHC signaling and the UPS in survivors—a finding consistent with IFN-γ-driven enhancement of antigen processing and presentation, completing a coherent axis from innate interferon sensing through proteasomal degradation to cytotoxic T-cell activation [40]. Survivors additionally showed upregulation of C-type lectin receptors which directly recognize Mtb cell wall components and represent a pathogen-specific myeloid sensing pathway complementary to NF-κB signaling [41].

In contrast, survivors downregulated the neutrophil chemoattractant, CXCL1, complement genes C4A and C4B, and granzyme A signaling [42, 43]. This may reflect active restraint of potentially injurious innate responses in the CNS, though reduced innate effector cell recruitment in the setting of lower baseline disease severity remains an alternative explanation [20, 43]. The former interpretation is supported by whole blood transcriptomic analyses linking neutrophil over-activation to increased 3-month TBM mortality [20] and TBM immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (TBM-IRIS) [44], though direct CSF-blood comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given compartmental differences in cellular composition and function. In the definite TBM subgroup, complement downregulation in survivors was substantially expanded to encompass additional classical and alternative pathway components, further strengthening the evidence for complement suppression as a feature of survival and the rationale for complement-directed therapeutic investigation.

Hypoinflammatory Phenotype and Implications for Risk Stratification

While the overall pattern among survivors suggests balanced immune activation, the unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis revealed a distinct hypoinflammatory phenotype characterized by global downregulation of both innate and adaptive immune pathways and significantly elevated mortality, underscoring that insufficient immune activation may be equally harmful as excessive inflammation in TBM [45]. This hypoinflammatory cluster bears conceptual resemblance to the immune exhaustion endotype in pulmonary TB proposed by Di Nardo et al [46]. The directionality of the association in our cohort remains uncertain—the hypoinflammatory phenotype may contribute directly to mortality through insufficient pathogen control or may alternatively reflect an advanced and potentially irreversible disease state.

Regardless, these findings have therapeutic implications. The lack of definite mortality benefit observed with adjunctive corticosteroids in HIV-associated TBM [9] may be partly explained by this phenotype, as patients with global immune suppression would be expected to experience harm rather than benefit from further immunosuppression. More broadly, although case reports suggest TNF and IL1 inhibitors have been used in TBM-IRIS and paradoxical reactions [47, 48], our data suggest that indiscriminate use carries potential risk: in patients with impaired innate immune signaling (including those in the hypoinflammatory cluster), these pathways appear protective rather than pathological. Conversely, patients with clinical or laboratory evidence of immune hyperactivation may represent a subgroup in whom targeted inhibition is appropriate. Transcriptomic or proteomic profiling at presentation, if validated prospectively, could provide a framework for prioritizing treatment strategies and interpreting the heterogeneous outcomes seen in existing immunomodulatory trials.

Microtubule Associated Protein Tau Regulation and Neuroinflammation

Microtubule associated protein tau is traditionally associated with neurodegeneration, but its predicted activation in survivors (based on downstream targets including TNF, IL1A, and MAPK1) suggests a potential neuroprotective or neuromodulatory role in TBM. The survivor-predominant pattern argues against simple neuronal injury or neurodegeneration, which would be expected to predominate in non-survivors, though stress-related MAPT activation independent of immune function remains an alternative explanation. Given that it is based on computational inference, this finding is hypothesis-generating and requires prospective validation.

CONCLUSION

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design cannot establish causality between expression profiles and survival outcomes, and longitudinal sampling could clarify whether these profiles precede or follow clinical deterioration. Cerebrospinal fluid–based transcriptomics may not fully capture responses occurring in the brain parenchyma or meninges, and the pauci-cellular nature of CSF likely biases results toward robustly expressed genes while potentially missing subtle but important changes. Additionally, while CSF WBC did not improve model fit in the ZINB-WaVE weighting framework, bulk CSF RNA-seq cannot distinguish between transcriptomic differences driven by shifts in cellular composition versus per-cell transcriptional activation, and observed pathway upregulation should be interpreted with this caveat in mind. Finally, there may be limited generalizability to those without HIV and populations in different geographic settings. This limitation is illustrated by the higher 14-day mortality among female participants—a finding consistent with a prior TBM meta-analysis but contrasting with male-predominant mortality in pulmonary TB and cohorts from other geographic regions [49, 50]. Whether this reflects biological differences, differential ART access, or delayed presentation remains incompletely understood, and given the modest cohort size and absence of sex-stratified analyses, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and warrants further investigation.

Despite these limitations, we demonstrate that bulk CSF RNA-seq paired with rigorous mNGS-based cohort refinement identified distinct immune mechanisms associated with short-term TBM survival. These findings support investigation of targeted therapies to selectively modulate detrimental innate responses while preserving protective adaptive immunity to improve outcomes in TBM.
