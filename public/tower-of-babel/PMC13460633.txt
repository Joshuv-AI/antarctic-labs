Preoperative Anemia and Perioperative Outcomes in Children: Prevalence, Risk Factors, and Associations in an Australian Cohort
Authors: Matthew Hart, Alexandra Batinic, Justin Skowno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsPreoperative anemia is a potentially modifiable risk factor in children undergoing surgery, but contemporary data describing its prevalence and associated outcomes in high‐income settings are limited. We aimed to determine the prevalence of anemia in a pediatric major‐surgery surgical cohort, identify associated demographic and clinical factors, and examine relationships with perioperative outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study of children undergoing major surgery at an Australian tertiary children's hospital. Patients with a hemoglobin measurement within 3 months prior to surgery were included. Anemia was defined according to the World Health Organization 2024 thresholds. Demographic variables, comorbidities, surgical characteristics, transfusion, and postoperative outcomes were extracted. Logistic regression was used to evaluate independent associations between preoperative anemia, red cell transfusion, and postoperative infection.ResultsOf 786 eligible patients, 582 (74%) were included. Anemia was present in 123 children (21.1%, 95% CI 18.0–24.6). Anemia was more common in females, higher ASA classification, emergency surgery, and those with chronic comorbidities. In multivariable analyses, anemia was independently associated with red cell transfusion (aOR 2.77, 95% CI 1.74–4.41), and with postoperative infection (aOR 1.89, 95% CI 1.15–3.09). Other postoperative outcomes were not significantly associated with preoperative anemia. Anemia remained common in elective surgical patients, a group in whom preoperative screening and optimization are feasible.ConclusionPreoperative anemia affects one in five children undergoing major surgery in this Australian cohort and is independently associated with red cell transfusion and postoperative infection. These findings support further evaluation of strategies to identify and manage preoperative anemia in pediatric surgical patients. The observed prevalence and outcome associations in this cohort should be interpreted in the context of missing preoperative hemoglobin data, which may bias estimates toward higher risk patients.

Introduction

Anemia is a common global health problem, and in the perioperative setting has been identified as a predictor of morbidity and mortality in both adults and children [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. In surgical patients, anemia increases the likelihood of requiring perioperative blood transfusion, which itself is associated with adverse outcomes and increased healthcare costs [6]. Consequently, patient blood management programs have been adopted in adult practice, aiming to identify and treat preoperative anemia as a means of improving patient outcomes and reducing costs [7, 8]. There is emerging interest in developing these programs for pediatric practice, but evidence to support their use is currently limited [9].

Despite recognizing the significance, the prevalence and consequences of preoperative anemia in pediatric surgical populations remain poorly defined. Until recently, most of the available evidence is either derived from adult cohorts or pediatric populations in low‐income settings [1, 10]. This gap is particularly notable in Australasia, where the prevalence of preoperative anemia in pediatric surgical patients has not previously been reported.

Recently, large database studies such as a 2025 analysis of the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) Pediatric registry have highlighted that approximately one quarter of children undergoing non‐cardiac surgery in the United States are anemic [3], and that anemia and transfusion are independently associated with adverse perioperative outcomes. However, differences in definitions, population demographics, and healthcare systems underscore the need for local data.

We therefore undertook a retrospective observational study of children undergoing major surgery at an Australian tertiary pediatric hospital. The primary aim was to determine the prevalence of preoperative anemia using the World Health Organization (WHO) reference ranges [11]. Secondary aims were to examine demographic and clinical risk factors associated with preoperative anemia and explore associations between preoperative anemia and postoperative outcomes and complications.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study at The Children's Hospital at Westmead, a tertiary pediatric referral center in Sydney, Australia. The study was designed and reported following the STROBE guidelines for observational studies [12]. The study was approved by the Sydney Children's Hospitals Network Human Research Ethics Committee, reference number 2024/ETH00264, with a waiver of requirement for individual consent.

Children aged 6 months‐18 years who underwent surgery between January 2023 and December 2023 were screened for inclusion. Patients were eligible if they underwent a procedure defined a priori as major surgery (Table 1) and had a hemoglobin measurement recorded within 3 months prior to surgery. Additional procedures were included if there was an anticipated risk of blood loss exceeding 7 mL kg−1 based on institutional historical experience, as assessed at the time of screening by the study investigator, an experienced pediatric anesthetist (Table S1). Patients undergoing repeat surgery during a single admission and patients who had received a blood transfusion within seven days prior to surgery were excluded from analysis.

A single reviewer analyzed the patient electronic medical records (Cerner PowerChart, Cerner, Kansas City, MO, USA) of patients meeting inclusion criteria, and extracted predefined demographic, anthropometric, and clinical data. Variables included age; sex; weight; ASA physical status; National Confidential Enquiry into Patient Outcome and Death (NCEPOD) surgical urgency; a documented preoperative diagnosis of either solid organ or hematological malignancy; a documented preoperative diagnosis of chronic kidney disease (CKD); ethnicity; and surgical specialty. Laboratory values included hemoglobin concentration and creatinine.

Outcome data included red cell transfusion rate defined as administration of allogeneic red blood cells at any time from surgical start time until hospital discharge; red cell transfusion volume in mL kg−1; postoperative anemia; postoperative infection defined as a diagnosis by the managing team of any infection requiring antibiotic therapy at any point up to 30 days after surgery; thromboembolism defined as a diagnosis by the managing team of any venous thrombus, pulmonary embolus, or thromboembolic stroke at any point up to 30 days after surgery; new renal failure defined as any two of a new dialysis requirement, doubling of pre‐operative creatinine concentration (where available), or a creatinine measurement exceeding 200 μmol L−1; 30‐day mortality; survival to discharge; and length of postoperative stay.

Anemia and its severity were defined according to the WHO 2024 hemoglobin thresholds for age and sex: 6–23 months < 105 g L−1; 24–59 months < 110 g L−1; 5–11 years < 115 g L−1; 12–14 years < 120 g L−1; males 15–18 years < 130 g L−1; females 15–18 years < 120 g L−1. Postoperative anemia was defined using the same thresholds, applied to the lowest hemoglobin measurement between surgery and discharge.

The primary outcomes were the prevalence of preoperative anemia and intraoperative and postoperative red blood cell transfusion rates. Secondary outcomes included risk factors for anemia, variation by surgical specialty, transfusion volume per kg, and postoperative complications. Anemia was treated as the outcome variable in analyses describing its prevalence and associated factors, and as the exposure variable in models examining associations with postoperative outcomes.

A priori sample size calculations indicated that a cohort of 250 patients would allow estimation of prevalence with adequate precision: if the true prevalence was 20%, the 95% confidence interval would be ±5%, and if the true prevalence was 50%, the 95% confidence interval would be ±6%.

Missing data were not imputed. For descriptive analyses, denominators reflect available data for each variable. Regression analyses were performed using complete‐case analysis.

Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests as appropriate. Prevalence estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals calculated using Wilson's method.

Logistic regression was used to examine the association between preoperative anemia (as an exposure) and postoperative infection and transfusion. Covariates were selected based on clinical plausibility and prior literature suggesting them as variables associated with both anemia and perioperative outcomes. These included ASA status, surgical urgency, sex, solid organ cancer, CKD. Diabetes and hematological malignancy were originally selected as covariates, but not included in the final model owing to sparse cell counts to avoid unstable estimates. Intraoperative transfusion was included as an additional covariate in a second infection model as an exploratory analysis of mediation. Surgical specialty was not included as a covariate, as it largely reflects underlying patient and procedural factors captured by other variables in the model. To account for major procedural differences, a binary cardiac versus non‐cardiac surgery variable was included.

Multivariable logistic regression was also used to identify patient and procedural factors associated with preoperative anemia as an outcome. Covariates were selected as either clinically plausible or previously described predictors of anemia in general populations. These included age, ASA status, surgical urgency, sex, solid organ cancer, and cardiac versus non‐cardiac surgery. Diabetes, hematological malignancy, and CKD also met criteria for covariates but were not included owing to sparse cell counts.

ASA was modeled as an ordinal variable (I–V) in regression analyses. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. p values were reported to three decimal places, with values less than 0.001 reported as p < 0.001. Multivariable model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and overall model fit using McFadden's pseudo‐R 2. Analyses were performed using Python version 3.11 (Python Software Foundation, Delaware, USA).

Results

A total of 786 children underwent major surgery during the study period. Of these, 582 (74%) had a preoperative hemoglobin measurement within 3 months and were included in the analysis. The most common reason for exclusion was absence of a recent hemoglobin (n = 203). Preoperative anemia was identified in 123 children (21.1%, 95% CI 18.0–24.6). Figure 1 shows a flow diagram of the study population. Of the anemic patients, 60 (48.8%) had mild anemia, 61 (49.6%) had moderate anemia, and 2 (1.6%) had severe anemia. To explore the potential impact of cardiac physiology on our prevalence estimate, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding all cardiac surgery cases. Anemia prevalence in the remaining cohort was 25.8% (95% CI 21.7–30.4).

Discussion

In this single‐center Australian cohort, we found that preoperative anemia affected approximately one in five children undergoing major surgery, a prevalence comparable to recent international reports. In multivariable logistic regression modeling, ASA classification, emergency surgery, female sex, and non‐cardiac surgery were all independently associated with preoperative anemia as an outcome. Preoperative anemia as an exposure was independently associated with red cell transfusion and the diagnosis of any postoperative infection requiring antibiotic therapy.

Our prevalence estimate of 21% is consistent with the 26% reported in a recent analysis of over 400 000 children in the American College of Surgeons NSQIP‐Pediatric registry [3], despite methodological differences in definitions and populations. That large study also demonstrated associations between anemia and adverse postoperative outcomes. Our results align with these findings, while adding novel detail by using updated WHO definitions, exploring specialty‐specific prevalence, and providing data from an Australian cohort where such information has not previously been reported.

This study replicated previous findings that demonstrate an association between a higher prevalence of preoperative anemia and comorbidities such as cancer (both solid organ and hematological), and chronic renal impairment, as well as surgical urgency. We also demonstrated a significant difference in the proportion of patients undergoing surgery of different specialties in the anemic and non‐anemic groups. Prevalence was significantly higher in some specialties, such as ENT and general surgery, whilst others such as cardiothoracic surgery and neurosurgery had a lower prevalence. This study did not explore the reason for this variation, but there may be several factors underlying this difference. Some surgical specialties may be more likely to encounter patients with comorbidities predisposing to anemia, or patients presenting with acute bleeding. Indeed, the majority of included ENT cases, for example, were post‐tonsillectomy hemorrhages. There may also be differences in preoperative optimization performed by surgical specialties. For example, patients undergoing cardiothoracic surgery in our institution undergo a rigorous, multidisciplinary evaluation and optimization prior to surgery.

This study identified an independent association between preoperative anemia and postoperative infection. This is a biologically plausible finding, with anemia being previously hypothesized to predispose postoperative patients to infection via impaired oxygen delivery to healing tissues [13], as well as impairing the ability of immune cells to perform oxidative killing of infectious organisms [14].

This study also replicates previous findings in adult and pediatric populations of an association between preoperative anemia and increased transfusion rates. Allogeneic blood product transfusion is another factor previously reported to increase postoperative infection risk through mechanisms such as transfusion‐related immunomodulation [15]. It is therefore plausible that the association between preoperative anemia and postoperative infection observed in our cohort may at least be partly explained by intraoperative red cell transfusion acting as a mediator. Consistent with this, inclusion of intraoperative transfusion as a covariate attenuated the association between anemia and infection. This finding should be interpreted with caution; however, as formal mediation analysis would require detailed adjustment for intraoperative factors such as hemodynamic instability and vasoactive support. Nevertheless, these observations highlight a potential mechanism linking anemia to postoperative infection that warrants further investigation in prospective studies.

The transfusion rate observed in this cohort is higher than rates reported across all‐comer pediatric surgical populations, reflecting restriction to procedures defined as major surgery with an anticipated risk of major bleeding. Estimates of transfusion rates in high risk pediatric surgeries are limited, but studies in craniosynostosis, liver transplant, and spine surgery in children suggest transfusion rates may reach 40%–60% [16, 17, 18]. Selection bias is also a likely contributor to this observation, owing to the inclusion of only those patients with a hemoglobin measurement available prior to surgery. In contrast, institutional NSQIP data from the same period demonstrate a transfusion rate of 7.62% across all surgeries, consistent with national benchmarks. The higher transfusion burden observed in this study therefore likely reflects the underlying risk profile of this selected population rather than atypical transfusion practice.

Blood transfusion also carries economic implications due to the costs of acquisition, processing, storage, and administration, as well as managing transfusion‐related complications. In our cohort, anemia was associated with approximately 26 additional transfusions than if children were transfused at the same rate as the non‐anemic cohort. The cost to the Australian health service of one unit of red blood cells is AUD $513.71 [19], with the total cost associated with a transfusion of approximately AUD $1000. As an illustrative example, this could represent an additional cost in the order of AUD $26 000 to our institution over the study period, not accounting for indirect costs such as managing post‐operative infections. Further studies may wish to examine the potential cost savings of recognizing and treating preoperative anemia to reduce transfusion rates.

This study also suggests that preoperative anemia prevalence remains high (17.6%) when emergency surgical patients are excluded. This is an important finding, as the elective surgical population is the group in which screening for and managing preoperative anemia is most feasible. Taken with the other findings of this study, this suggests that interventions such as patient blood management strategies, which aim to identify and manage anemia prior to surgery, are worth further investigating with the aim to improve outcomes in the pediatric surgical population.

Limitations

Several limitations are acknowledged. This was a retrospective study relying on routinely collected data, with the potential for misclassification and missing values. Approximately one quarter of eligible patients had no preoperative hemoglobin measurement and were excluded. This pattern likely reflects institutional practice within a large tertiary referral center serving a broad geographic catchment, where preoperative assessment is frequently conducted via telehealth and there are a large number of day of surgery admissions. In the event of missing preoperative blood tests, blood sampling is often deferred until after induction of anesthesia to minimize distress in children. Hemoglobin measurements obtained after induction were not included in this study, as it was not possible to determine if these had been obtained prior to factors influencing hemoglobin concentration, including intravenous fluid administration causing hemodilution, blood product administration, or commencement of surgery. It is also likely that a proportion of patients without a preoperative hemoglobin measurement represent a lower risk group in whom clinicians deemed blood draws as unnecessary or able to be deferred. Demographic and clinical data were not collected for excluded patients, precluding formal sensitivity analyses comparing included and excluded cohorts. As a result, the prevalence of preoperative anemia and rates of postoperative complications observed in this study may represent overestimates compared with the broader pediatric surgical population.

As a single‐center study, our results may not fully generalize to all pediatric surgical populations. At the time of the study, no formal pediatric patient blood management program was in place, with transfusion practices guided by individual clinician judgment. However, our institution is a large tertiary referral center managing a broad surgical case‐mix, suggesting these findings are likely applicable to similar major surgical populations in high‐income healthcare settings.

Although efforts were made to correct for potential confounders using logistic regression, it remains possible that unknown confounders may contribute toward the outcome effects seen. Although anemia prevalence varied significantly across surgical specialties, we did not include specialty in the adjusted models. Specialty largely reflects underlying patient factors such as urgency, ASA grade, and comorbidities, which were accounted for in our analyses. Cardiac versus non‐cardiac surgery was also included as a covariate to account for major differences in surgical techniques (e.g., cardiopulmonary bypass) and blood loss risk.

Admission status at the time of surgery (day of surgery admission versus pre‐existing inpatient) was not captured. Pre‐existing inpatient status may be associated with greater illness severity and anemia risk and increased risk of postoperative adverse outcomes. However, the cohort was restricted to procedures defined a priori as major surgery, and surgical urgency and ASA physical status were included in adjusted analyses, which likely captured some of the associated baseline risk.

Preoperative comorbidities and postoperative complications were defined based on diagnoses documented by the treating clinical teams rather than investigator adjudication using predefined diagnostic criteria. This approach reflects real‐world clinical practice but increases the risk of misclassification bias for outcomes such as postoperative infection. Data extraction was performed by a single reviewer without second reviewer validation, which may limit assessment of extraction reliability. However, extracted variables were predefined and based primarily on objective outcomes and explicitly documented clinical diagnoses, reducing this risk.

There were data that were not collected that may introduce bias and limit the interpretation of findings in this study. Data were not collected to describe the differences in postoperative infection type (e.g., surgical site infection, urinary tract infection, respiratory infections), which would be helpful information to draw conclusions on the clinical impact of the association between anemia and infection. The use of autologous blood salvage was not captured and may have reduced measured allogeneic transfusion exposure in some high blood‐loss procedures. There is value in describing the prevalence of subtypes of preoperative anemia, as this would help to guide prioritization of preoperative testing and interventions. However, in our cohort, laboratory measurements that would have allowed classification of anemia subtype were not routinely available. Ethnicity data were limited to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status, and counts in these categories were low, preventing deeper exploration of ethnic differences in anemia prevalence, which would be helpful in informing efforts to improve health equity outcomes.

The definitions of anemia changed during the study period; while our primary analyses used the updated WHO 2024 thresholds, sensitivity analysis with the previous 2011 definitions [20] yielded a similar prevalence (22.9%), supporting the robustness of our findings.

The WHO thresholds for anemia do not specify specific cutoffs for children with cyanotic heart disease. Chronic hypoxemia in these patients can result in polycythemia. Cyanotic patients were not excluded in this cohort, and so this may lead to an underestimation of anemia prevalence in this cohort. Exclusion of cardiac surgery cases resulted in a modestly higher estimate of anemia prevalence, but confidence intervals overlapped with the primary analysis, suggesting that inclusion of cardiac cases did not materially influence our findings.

Finally, several outcomes previously reported to be associated with anemia, including thromboembolic complications, acute kidney injury, length of stay, and 30‐day mortality, were not statistically significantly associated with preoperative anemia in this study. These analyses were exploratory, and it is likely that the study was underpowered to detect differences in these relatively rare outcomes, as reflected by low event counts and wide confidence intervals. Based on the observed mortality rates, an approximate doubling of the sample size would be required to detect a statistically significant difference, indicating that larger multi‐year or multicenter cohorts are needed to reliably assess this outcome.

Conclusion

Preoperative anemia is common in this prevalence study in an Australian pediatric cohort and is associated with increased red cell transfusion and postoperative infections. These findings align with those reported in other high‐income countries and support further exploration of the use of patient blood management measures as possible interventions to improve outcomes and reduce costs in pediatric surgical populations.
