 Summary
Exact solutions for laminar magnetohydrodynamic flow in ducts and pipes under a uniform transverse
magnetic field have accumulated since 1937—eleven solutions spanning a wide range of wall conditions,
which serve as benchmarks for solver verification and provide baseline estimates for liquid-metal
blanket design. However, they are difficult to use together: the original papers adopt different
coordinate frames, units, and length scales, and at least two contain printed errors that would be
reproduced by anyone implementing the published formulas.
 This report presents mhdExact, a toolbox that collects eleven such solutions in a common
formulation. The cases span insulating to perfectly conducting walls, thin to fully resolved conjugate
walls, and rectangular to circular cross-sections. All are expressed in a single coordinate convention
and normalization, and all series are reformulated to avoid exponential overflow, so that they can
be evaluated reliably at Hartmann numbers of 104 and above. Quantities not given in the original
papers, such as the mean flow, electric potential, and boundary-layer thicknesses, are derived for
every case. Each solution is connected to its neighbors through numerically verified limits: making
a wall thin, perfectly conducting, or insulating recovers the corresponding known case.
 To our knowledge, no other collection covers this many solutions at this level of detail. The
toolbox is provided as a Python package, header-only C++ routines that NekRS can call at run
time, a mathematical reference document, a verification suite, and browser-based tools that require
no installation. It gives solver developers verification data across the wall conditions relevant to
blankets, gives designers estimates of the core and peak velocities and layer thicknesses before
meshing, and shows, for example, that the electrical boundary condition, rather than the geometry,
turns a flat profile into a jet two hundred times the mean velocity.
 The toolbox was developed with an AI-assisted agentic workflow that automated much of the
re-derivation, stable reformulation, implementation, and routine checking across eleven cases while
human effort focused on setting conventions and scrutinizing results. Its 124 verification gates,
comprising about 1,200 assertions, exposed the errors in the published papers as well as bugs in
early versions of the implementation.

1 Introduction
Liquid-metal (LM) flows in fusion blankets are strongly influenced by magnetohydrodynamic (MHD)
effects. Their velocity profiles are determined by the electrical conductivity of the fluid and walls,
the strength and direction of the imposed magnetic field, and the ability of electric current to close
through the surrounding walls. Although a full blanket involves complex three-dimensional geometry
and coupled physics, including heat transfer and buoyancy-driven flow, many flow paths can be
idealized locally as ducts or pipes. Canonical unidirectional MHD flows in rectangular and circular
channels therefore remain valuable reference problems at leading order. Taken as laminar, steady
and fully developed, they reveal the dominant physical balances, provide estimates of flow structure
and pressure loss, form a foundation for engineering analysis, and can be used to model initial or
boundary conditions for simulations.
 A broad set of exact solutions has been derived for these flows, and eleven of them are collected
in this report. Hartmann obtained the classical solution for pressure-driven mercury flow between
parallel plates and compared it with experimental measurements [1]. Shercliff subsequently derived a
solution for flow in a rectangular duct with electrically insulating walls [2], and Chang and Lundgren
treated the perfectly conducting counterpart [3]. Hunt extended the rectangular-duct problem to
conducting Hartmann walls with insulating side walls, in two variants and a common limit [4]. In
these formulations the thin-wall approximation reduces the wall to a Robin-type boundary condition.
Later work resolved the fluid and conducting-wall domains explicitly, thereby accounting for finite
wall thickness [5, 6], and related solutions admit walls of unequal thickness and conductivity on
opposite faces [7]. For circular cross-sections, Gold solved the insulating pipe [8] and Samad the
pipe with a wall of finite thickness and conductivity [9].
 Together these cover a wide range of wall conditions, but no single solution covers every
combination of wall conductivity, wall thickness, and electrical boundary condition. In particular, an
analytical solution for flow in a rectangular duct with four finite-conductivity walls remains unknown.
Consequently, a change in the physical configuration may require selecting and implementing a
different solution family. A cross-referenced tool is therefore needed to identify the applicable
solution, evaluate it reliably, and expose its connection to related limiting cases.
 These solutions are also not straightforward to use as reliable computational references. Most
are expressed as infinite series whose convergence rates depend on the governing parameters and
spatial location. Hyperbolic functions such as sinh and cosh, which arise from the separation of
variables, can overflow at high Hartmann number even when the physical solution is bounded and
well conditioned, so numerically stable reformulations are often required. Published expressions
may contain typographical errors or use differing conventions, definitions, and coordinate systems.
The literature generally emphasizes the velocity and rescaled induced-magnetic-field solutions as
the primary results, leaving other useful quantities to be reconstructed by the user. These include
electrical potential, volumetric flow rate, core and maximum velocities, boundary-layer thicknesses,
and the peak magnitude and location of side-wall jets when they are present. The relationships
between configurations are likewise not transparent from the formulas alone, although a solution
for one wall model often reduces to another in a limiting case. Finally, an analytical expression
is not automatically a verified implementation, as the derivation, numerical evaluation, and code
realization must all be checked independently.
 Interactive resources make canonical MHD solutions more accessible. Forest’s Classic Problems

 1

in Magnetohydrodynamics: Interactive Web Edition includes an explorer for Hartmann flow and
presents its physical interpretation alongside the derivation [10]. mhdExact extends this idea from
a single case into a general-purpose computational reference covering the eleven, and adds finite wall
thickness and conductivity, derived layer and side-wall-jet diagnostics, series-convergence control,
and verification-oriented parameter and mesh studies.
 mhdExact is developed to address these needs. It is a cross-referenced toolbox for evaluating
exact and asymptotic solutions of canonical MHD channel and pipe flows, with an initial emphasis on
unidirectional rectangular and circular configurations relevant to liquid-metal blanket systems. All
eleven cases are expressed in one coordinate convention with one set of normalizations, reformulated
for numerical stability, and pinned to their neighbours by limiting cases that are checked numerically.
The toolbox is delivered five times over, as an installable Python package for rapid inspection,
visualization, and parameter studies, as header-only C++ providers intended for coupling with
production solvers such as NekRS, as a written mathematical reference, as a verification suite, and
as browser tools that need no installation. In this way it serves both as a physics reference and
as infrastructure for solver verification, including resolution requirements, boundary-layer mesh
placement, and convergence studies.
 The development of mhdExact also demonstrates an agentic workflow for constructing reusable
scientific software. Using Claude Code as a development agent, the workflow begins from the original
literature but does not rely solely on transcription. The agent is directed to identify the central
derivation techniques, independently re-derive the governing expressions, implement numerically
stable forms, and verify the results against the original solution and relevant limiting cases. This
process can expose inconsistencies in published formulas and, more importantly, generalize solution
techniques to related configurations not treated explicitly in the original source. The resulting
toolbox preserves the analytical foundations of the literature while making them more accessible,
testable, and reusable for contemporary MHD simulation and fusion-blanket analysis.
 The report is organized as follows. Section 2 lays out the foundation for deriving these solutions,
the assumptions common to all of them, the key solution techniques, and the quantities of interest.
Section 3 describes the agentic workflow, its structure, guidelines and automation, and how the
solutions are checked, both by the agent and through human steering and correction over the course
of the project. Section 4 presents the deliverables of mhdExact, its contribution and importance,
together with several findings the agent uncovered along the way. The organized numerically stable
solutions are listed in the Appendix.

The system (8)–(9) is linear, so it admits a separable solution, and the same construction serves
every case in the Appendix. What distinguishes the cases is the cross-section and the wall conditions,

per-mode structure of their thin-wall parents, which is why each reduces to one as tw → 0. At the
other extreme, a cross-section with no side walls leaves a single term with an = 0, the Hartmann

needed at larger Ha and several times more again to resolve a side layer. Under-resolution produces
a smooth but wrong profile rather than an obvious failure, so the truncation is verified case by case
by refinement.

Circular cross-section. For a pipe the coordinates are polar, and one convention must be fixed
carefully. Following Figure 1, the angle is measured from the field axis y, so that y = aρ cos θ and
z = aρ sin θ with ρ = r/a. Then θ = 0 is the pole, where the wall is normal to B0 , and θ = π/2 the
equator, where it is parallel to the field. This differs from the commoner convention of measuring θ
from the horizontal, and mixing the two is a ready source of sign and parity errors. The solution is
a Fourier series in θ whose radial factors are modified Bessel functions In (αρ) with α = Ha/2, in
place of the hyperbolic functions of the rectangular case. The pole carries an O(Ha−1 ) Hartmann
layer, but the equator does not develop an O(Ha−1/2 ) side layer, since the wall curves away from
the field rather than running parallel to it. The Bessel series is also far worse conditioned than its
rectangular counterpart.

results as a reference must do it themselves. The work is error-prone, since a wrong exponent still
produces a smooth profile of the right general shape. Ni and coworkers, needing the Shercliff and
Hunt series as a benchmark, worked out overflow-safe forms and placed them in an appendix [12],
which is where this kind of result usually ends up when it appears at all. The forms used here follow
that appendix for the two cases it covers and extend the same treatment to the conjugate-wall and
circular cases, which it does not.
 The distinction between an exact rewrite and an approximation is maintained strictly. Each
case carries up to three forms (Table 1), and only the stable form is both exact and evaluable at
large Ha. Several cases have complex exponential rates, but the stable forms and everything derived
from them are evaluated in real arithmetic, conjugate pairs combining into real expressions and
complex values being admitted only in an oracle.
 Overflow is not the only way a series fails. The circular cases expand in In (αρ) with coefficients
alternating in sign and magnitudes far exceeding the sum, so accuracy is lost to cancellation instead.
Cancellation is invariant under rescaling, so no extraction of the kind used above removes it. The
consequence is a ceiling on the Hartmann number reachable in double precision for the pipes, around
Ha ≈ 20, beyond which arbitrary precision is required. The rectangular cases have no such ceiling
and are evaluated to Ha = 104 .

material [13]. Three artifact types carry the work.
 A plan is a proposal, written to disk before any code and held until a human approves it.
Writing it as a file rather than leaving it in the conversation is what matters, since a file can be
annotated, diffed against what was built, and reopened in a later session, and its definition-of-done
section doubles as the acceptance checklist at delivery. Plans are lettered in creation order, so the
sequence A to V records what was attempted and when.
 A note is written for the agent rather than the human, and is the artifact whose absence would
hurt most. A context window is bounded, so a finding from an early session is unavailable later
unless written down, and a convention agreed once is otherwise re-litigated or silently violated. Notes
record the sign convention and frame map, the traps found in each reference, and the definitions
settled with the human. They are written at the moment of discovery and consulted before decisions,
so their value lies in being read back.
 A report is written for the human, gathering what the work delivered, the numbers behind it,
the caveats and the next step. Reports share the letter of the plan they discharge, so a case can be
followed from proposal to result.

3.2 The Per-Case Pipeline

The inventory is listed in Table 2. These are the eleven classical closed-form solutions for unidirec-
tional flow under a uniform transverse field, accumulated over six decades, and they differ only in
the cross-section and in how each wall conducts.
 Each case passed through the same sequence, summarized in Table 3. Every step is a standalone
task with its own premise and its own gate, and the criterion for that gate is what marks the step
complete. Steps 1 and 2 settle what is being solved and against what it will be checked, and break
the rest into steps a human can follow. Step 3 is what separates this from transcription. The
production formula is built from the governing equations and the boundary conditions directly, as a
small linear system per mode, without reading the reference’s final coefficient. The reference is then
a cross-check rather than a source. A transcribed formula inherits whatever is wrong with it silently,
whereas an independently derived one disagrees, and the disagreement has to be resolved. Two
errors in the published literature and one in a public reference implementation were found this way.
 The re-derivation also includes a full set of quantities in Table 4 as several of them require
mathematics the source paper does not provide. A closed form for the mean flow is needed before
the unit-mean normalization can be exact, the potential must be integrated from the velocity and
induced field, and the boundary-layer thicknesses have to be defined in a way that survives a
profile which is not monotone. The set grew as the work proceeded, each new configuration adding
whatever its profile made meaningful. The side-wall jet is the clearest instance, arising only where a
conducting Hartmann wall drives one, so its position and magnitude are reported for those cases
and are not defined for the rest.

3.3.2 Entry 9, A Wrong Wall Condition and an Interface Gate

This one starts from a feature request, to plot the solution inside the solid wall, which the toolkit offers
and the literature generally does not. The method is standard and the mathematics straightforward,
the solid problem being well posed and closed by the fluid solution through the interface conditions.
What the agent does not recognize is that the condition on the wall’s outer surface is not the one it
has just used on the fluid side.
 Because b is the stream function of the cross-section current, an insulating outer surface carries
no current through it and is therefore a current line, on which b is constant. That is a Dirichlet
condition. The agent instead continues the wall solution with ∂n b = 0, the Neumann condition
an insulating wall imposes on the fluid problem, so the wall fill returns b essentially unchanged

 15

across the whole thickness. The fluid solution is untouched, the defect being confined to the wall
continuation.
 Verification does not catch it because no gate compares the two sides of the interface. Asked
for a solid solution, the agent does not think to check it against the continuity condition, and the
missing check has to be requested. Its form is a degeneracy rather than a consistency. At σ ∗ = 1
the two conjugate conditions coincide, so the slope must be continuous across the interface, and it
misses by six orders of magnitude. Figure 5 shows the old curve beside the corrected one.
 The episode locates the human contribution precisely. The mathematics is available to the
agent, and so is the check that would expose the error in seconds. What is missing is the suspicion
that a solution nobody asks the literature for may also be a solution nobody writes a test for.
Whether that suspicion can be automated, by proposing degeneracy tests or by auditing whether a
gate merely restates the code it tests, is the natural next question.

3.4 Verification

An analytic solution is not self-evidently right. It may be transcribed from a paper that contains
an error, mapped into the wrong frame, or implemented in a way that degrades where it matters.
No single source is therefore treated as authoritative, including the published one, and each case is
checked against several independent standards. The suite runs to 124 gates in three tiers, a fast
tier on every change, a compiled tier for the C++ providers, and a slow tier for the high-precision
comparisons. A gate is one named check, and most sweep a range of Hartmann numbers, wall
thicknesses and conductivity ratios, so the 124 expand to roughly 1200 assertions at run time. The
catalogue is generated from the test code, so it cannot fall out of date, and the derived-quantity
tables in the case documents are generated the same way with a gate asserting they equal what the
generator would write.
 Table 6 lists the five kinds of check applied to every case. They compare a solution with
the published result, with an independent implementation where one exists, with an arbitrary-
precision evaluation, with the governing equations themselves, and with its neighbouring cases in
the appropriate limits. A solution can satisfy any one of them and still be wrong. Agreement across
all five is stronger evidence, though it is not a proof of correctness.

the wall, rather than on a Cartesian grid masked to a disk.

4.2 The Mode Dependent Wall Conductance

For a rectangular duct with thick, finitely conducting side walls, Butler [6] found that the entire
solid domain collapses into a single number per Fourier mode. Because each mode is independent
and the field in the wall is harmonic, the wall problem for mode n closes in closed form, and all the
fluid ever sees of the wall is

The finite-thickness coefficient recovers the thin-wall one through the limit tanh(an tw )/an → tw as
tw → 0, so the thin-wall condition is not a different boundary condition but the leading term of this
one. The difference between them is O(t2w ) rather than an uncontrolled modelling error.
 What (19) means physically is illustrated in Figure 11. A wall is thin or thick relative to a
length scale, and here that scale is set by the mode rather than by the geometry. At tw = 0.1 the
first mode sees a conductance within one percent of the thin-wall value, while the twentieth sees one
six times smaller. Since the high modes are what build the side layers, a thin-wall treatment is least

 Figure 12 compares Sloan and Smith against Hunt II on this point. A conjugate wall of
thickness tw = 0.1 and a thin wall carrying the matched conductance c = σ ∗ tw are placed side by

meet. Away from the corner the two agree to better than a percent, which is what the leading
term of (19) promises. In the corner they separate, reaching 4.3% of the local potential at the
corner itself, and the sign changes on the way, so the thin wall overpredicts ϕ through the core and
underpredicts it in the last few percent of the span. The error is not a scale factor a calibration
could absorb. It also grows with Ha, from 0.9% at Ha = 100 to 1.6% at Ha = 500 and 4.3% here,
since raising Ha sharpens the layers and shifts weight into exactly the modes Figure 11 shows the
thin-wall treatment mistreating.
 The second use of (19) is structural, and it is what made two of the eleven cases cheap to
build. Tao and Ni solve the two unsymmetric wall thickness configurations, one with unequal side
walls and one with unequal Hartmann walls. However, their derivation writes the wall condition in
thin-wall form at an intermediate step [7], which is not a requirement. The step that introduces the
approximation is the same step (19) performs exactly, so carrying cn through instead of c leaves
the rest of their derivation untouched and returns a finite-thickness result. In this toolkit both
cases were built that way, as their symmetric parents with a separate cn at each face, Tao and Ni I
from Butler and Tao and Ni II from Sloan and Smith. The asymmetry appears as an additional
antisymmetric component that vanishes identically when the two walls match, which is also the
limit the verification checks.

4.3 Derived Quantities Across the Family

An exact solution also gives access to the quantities built from it, among them the peak velocity
and the boundary-layer thicknesses. Because every case reports the same set under the same
normalization, they can be set side by side. Table 8 does that at Ha = 1000. The geometry and the
Hartmann number are the same in every row, so the only thing varying is how the walls conduct,
and that alone moves the peak-to-core ratio from unity to 243.7.
 The side-wall jet is the second example. Figure 13 shows it for the Hunt case, with the peak,
the reversal and the location of each. A simulation has to resolve that structure to reproduce it, and
knowing where the extrema sit and how thin the layer is what tells a mesh where to put its points.

4.4 Errata

Re-deriving every solution rather than transcribing it makes the sources comparable, and where
they disagree the disagreement has to be resolved. Table 9 lists what that turned up, two errors in
published papers, two in the reference code used as starting material, and one of our own. All five
were caught by the verification of §3.4.
 The first two share a property that makes them hard to find. Each is correct in the limits
a reader is most likely to check. The published coefficient is exact at both zero and infinite
wall conductance and wrong only in between, so a verification restricted to the insulating and
perfectly conducting cases passes it. The reference-code sign satisfies no-slip to plotting accuracy at
Ha = 1000, which is where a reader working on liquid-metal flows would naturally test. Neither is a
careless error, and neither is visible by inspection.

5 Conclusion
The eleven solutions in this report were published between 1937 and 2015, in incompatible coordinate
frames, with at least two printed errors and no common way to compare them. What exists now is a
single reference. One convention fixes the axes, the length scale and the normalizations across every
case. One interface returns the same quantities for all of them, in Python for analysis and in C++
for a simulation to compare against at run time. The mathematics is published case by case, the
browser tools show a profile without installing anything, and 124 gates, roughly 1200 assertions once
their parameter sweeps are counted, check all of it on every change. We are not aware of another
collection that carries this many of these solutions, in one frame, with the derived quantities and the
verification alongside them. Its boundaries are not narrow. Every solution here is steady, laminar,
fully developed and isothermal, under a uniform transverse field, and a fusion blanket channel is
none of those. The reference is exact where it applies, which is what makes it verification data, and
knowing where it stops is what tells a designer when a simulation is required rather than optional.
 Getting there took an uneven and productive division of labour. The agent did the work that
scales. It re-derived eleven cases independently, reformulated each to be overflow-safe, mirrored
every frozen implementation into four downstream artifacts, and built a verification suite in which
no result rests on one check. That volume is what makes the cross-case comparison possible,
and nobody would attempt it by hand for eleven cases. The agent did not, however, supply the

 25

conventions. Those are human choices rather than consequences, and they propagate silently when
left to per-case judgement. Nor did it supply the scepticism that caught a boundary condition the
entire gate suite had agreed with. Both interventions were cheap and neither was automatic, which
is what makes automating the checking the most immediate piece of future work. Both defects in
our own code were invisible to gates that asserted what the implementation already did, and both
fell to a check of a different kind, a parameter value where two independent conditions must agree.
Proposing such degeneracies automatically, and auditing whether a gate restates the code it tests, is
tractable and would have caught both.
 The remaining work is to extend the family. A rectangular duct with four walls of finite
conductivity has no known closed form, and the conjugate machinery that unified the five thick-wall
cases here is where an attempt would start. Where derivation stops, computation can continue,
which argues for a two-dimensional solver on the cross-section. The governing pair is linear and
elliptic, so a direct solve on an arbitrary cross-section with arbitrary wall conductivity is a small
problem by the standards of a flow solver, and it reaches the configurations no closed form does.
The value is in the bridge rather than the solver. Every case in this report becomes a test of it,
with a known answer at any Ha and a known error, so a solver that reproduces all eleven can be
trusted on a geometry none of them covers. The family then extends by computation where it stops
by derivation, and the boundary between the two is drawn by the solutions rather than asserted.
Further out, the same verified family supplies training and validation data whose error is known
rather than estimated, for surrogate models of the flows no closed form reaches.
