Challenges Experienced by Patients With Chronic Venous Disease in Unresolved Postoperative Recovery After Iliac Vein Stenting: A Phenomenological Study
Authors: Yi-Yun Zeng, Shih-Chun Lin, Wen-Ling Chen, Jimmy Wei Hwa Tan, Mu-Yin Lo, Fan-Hao Chou, Han Wen Mak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Previous researchers have mainly examined the general recovery of patients with chronic venous disease after iliac vein stent placement for iliac vein compression syndrome. However, little is known about the specific challenges faced by patients who remain in an unresolved postoperative recovery phase after stenting.Objective:In this study, we aimed to explore the challenges faced by patients with chronic venous disease during unresolved postoperative recovery after iliac vein stenting.Methods:A descriptive phenomenological design was employed. Purposive sampling was used to recruit 12 patients from a teaching hospital in Taiwan. Semistructured interviews were conducted between January 2024 and July 2025, and the data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s 7-step method with NVivo-assisted coding.Results:Findings indicated that patients with iliac vein compression syndrome experienced a central phenomenon of internalized distress. Four major themes emerged: (1) severe physical discomfort and restricted mobility, reflecting persistent and unpredictable symptoms; (2) resigned endurance, with suppressed distress until nearing emotional breakdown; (3) feeling trapped and regretful, leading to delayed but urgent help-seeking; and (4) breaking free from constraints and regaining physical control, achieved through self-soothing and behavioral adjustments.Conclusion:Patients with unresolved recovery from chronic venous disease after iliac vein stenting experience multidimensional distress that extends beyond physical symptoms. These findings underscore the importance of postoperative care that integrates continuous psychological support, culturally sensitive communication, and proactive follow-up. Developing personalized tools to assess well-being and guide tailored interventions may enhance recovery outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Iliac vein compression syndrome (IVCS), a major cause of chronic venous disease (CVD), occurs when the right common iliac artery compresses the left common iliac vein, resulting in impaired venous outflow.1 Symptoms range from mild to severe and include lower extremity pain, swelling, fatigue, varicose veins, venous ulcers, and deep vein thrombosis.2 These manifestations can significantly affect the quality of life (QoL), work capacity, and social participation of patients. In clinical practice, a Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS) ≥8 is considered indicative of unresolved venous dysfunction, which continues to impair daily activities and QoL.3

Iliac vein stent placement is a minimally invasive intervention that restores venous patency in IVCS, thereby improving venous outflow and alleviating clinical symptoms.4 By the third postoperative month, approximately 60% of patients achieve recovery benchmarks, with notable reductions in pain scores during this critical period.5,6 Although these outcomes are encouraging, a subset of patients continues to experience persistent challenges in their recovery, including physical discomfort, psychological distress, emotional disturbances, and existential concerns.4,7

Cultural norms surrounding “endurance” and emotional expression play a pivotal role in shaping patients’ postoperative experiences.8–10 According to the Praxis Theory of Suffering,11 individuals in the “enduring” phase often suppress emotional and sensory perceptions, thereby limiting emotional expression.

While Zeng et al.12 described patients who achieved stable recovery after venous stenting as following a linear trajectory towards physical and emotional stability, their study primarily emphasized successful recovery. The experiences of patients with stagnation or unmet recovery expectations remain unexplored. Persistent physical and psychological challenges may trigger renewed distress, compounded by societal expectations of “health” and “functional normalcy,” thereby adding psychological pressure when recovery does not progress as anticipated.13

In this study, we aimed to explore the challenges encountered by patients with CVD who remained in an unresolved recovery phase ≥3 months after iliac vein stenting. The main objective was to determine how these patients navigate the transitions between “enduring” and “emotional distress” while managing physical, psychological, and social adjustment challenges.

Methods

Design

This study adopted the epistemological framework of Husserlian phenomenology,14 which emphasizes reduction and detailed descriptions of the fundamental structures of experience. Inductive analysis using Colaizzi’s 7-step descriptive phenomenological method15 was first performed to identify patients’ experiences, with all codes and themes derived directly from participants’ narratives. Furthermore, Morse’s Praxis Theory of Suffering11 was employed to inform the research questions and interpretive framework, thereby accentuating the complex nature of discomfort along with the emotional and behavioral coping responses that it elicits.

Our study was approved by the Human Subjects Committee of Tainan Municipal An Nan Hospital, China Medical University (approval no. TMANH112-REC003(AR-1)). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their participation in the study. The first author verbally explained to the participants the purpose of the study, methods, confidentiality measures in data collection and processing, potential benefits and risks, allowing the participants to autonomously decide whether to participate without coercion. After obtaining the participants’ agreement and signatures on the consent forms, data collection commenced. Participants were informed that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their right to medical care.

Setting and Sampling

This descriptive phenomenological study was conducted in the outpatient vascular surgery clinic of a teaching hospital in Southern Taiwan. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) confirmed diagnosis of IVCS; (2) history of iliac vein stent placement; (3) age ≥20 years; (4) unresolved recovery ≥3 months poststenting as assessed by a cardiovascular surgeon, with VCSS ≥8; (5) self-reported unresolved symptom-related distress; and (6) ability to clearly articulate experiences and provide written informed consent.

The initial screening for eligibility was performed by a cardiovascular surgeon. Eligible patients were referred to a case manager, a registered nurse with a master’s degree and formal training in qualitative research, who was also the first author and conducted all the interviews. She has prior clinical experience in caring for patients with venous disorders and a strong interest in improving postoperative recovery care, which motivated her to undertake this study. Of the 27 patients approached, 15 declined to participate owing to physical discomfort, emotional distress, or recovery challenges, and 12 consented and completed the interviews. Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved. After the 10th interview, no new themes emerged, and 2 further interviews were conducted to confirm saturation. Developing the Interview Guide

The initial interview guide was drafted based on the existing literature on CVD and illness-related discomfort and revised with input from 2 experienced qualitative researchers. To minimize bias, no information regarding other patients’ conditions was shared with the participants. Two pilot interviews were conducted with eligible patients in unresolved recovery, after which an additional guiding prompt (“Under what circumstances do you feel that you are reaching your limit?”) was added. The finalized guide was approved by the research team before formal data collection.

Data Collection

Semistructured interviews were conducted between January 2024 and July 2025 (Table 2) in a private consultation room within the hospital, with only the participants and interviewers present. Rapport was built through casual conversations before the interviews to enhance comfort. Interviews were conducted face-to-face; each participant was interviewed once, lasting approximately 60–70 minutes, and were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Detailed field notes, including nonverbal cues and contextual observations, were documented immediately after each interview.

Data Analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim within 3 days of completion. A 2-stage analytical strategy was used.

In the first stage, Colaizzi’s 7-step descriptive phenomenological method15 guided the inductive analysis (Table 3). The transcripts were imported into NVivo 11 (Lumivero, Ithaca, New York) for coding. Significant statements were extracted, formulated into meanings, and clustered into subthemes and overarching themes. These were synthesized into an exhaustive description and refined into the fundamental structure of the experience. The findings were validated through participant member checking.

In the second stage, a theory-informed interpretive approach16 was applied using Morse’s Praxis Theory of Suffering.11 This theoretical framework provided alignment and contextual depth without overshadowing the participants’ lived experiences. Employing this theory enhanced the researchers’ sensitivity to key concepts and processes, such as recognizing emotional distress and understanding physical and psychological conditions within participants’ narratives. The process was reflective and iterative, involving continual revisiting of themes, subthemes, and theoretical constructs to enable a comprehensive reconstruction of meaning.

Results

Our findings revealed 4 themes of unresolved recovery after iliac vein stenting.

Theme 1: Severe Physical Discomfort and Restricted Mobility

Participants commonly reported persistent physical discomfort and mobility limitations following the procedure. Initially, some participants noted that these sensations did not significantly interfere with daily functioning. For example, one participant reflected:

Case 1: “Sometimes my lower leg feels a bit tight or constricted, but I can’t really describe what that sensation is.”

However, most participants reported experiencing physical reactions that were difficult to understand or control, which disrupted their sense of familiarity and confidence in their bodies. Participants described symptoms such as “intense headaches that numb the entire head,” “persistent tightness in the neck and shoulders,” and “a stabbing ache in both hips.” These persistent sensations developed postoperatively and disrupted their daily lives, affecting their ability to work, get out of bed, and exercise. Moreover, they experienced a profound disruption of bodily integrity and a loss of control over their bodies. This progressive loss of agency over their physical selves led to profound anxiety and emotional distress.

Case 9: “I had an aching sensation on both sides of my hips for a long time, approximately ≥3 months. It felt like an electric shock running through my hips, and I could not sit comfortably. No matter how I moved, the aching did not stop, even when I was sleeping. It got to the point where even getting out of bed in the morning became challenging.”

Case 10: “The headache was so intense that my head was numb. The pain made me question everything…I thought this was supposed to be a minimally invasive procedure? How could it hurt so much? How could this happen? Is there anything wrong with me? I was anxious. I wondered if I just was not strong enough to handle this kind of pain—the numbness and stabbing pain in my head.”

Many participants indicated that during daily activities, certain parts of their bodies frequently exhibited resistance or “jamming,” making originally effortless and natural movements feel laborious or restricted. They mentioned sensations such as “something pressing against the waist” or “a locking feeling when rotating the waist.” The body was perceived as functioning like a mechanism with impaired mobility, often producing uncoordinated responses during movements. Participants with unilateral stent placement particularly noted a marked imbalance in tension during stretching or flexibility exercises, remarking that “the side with the stent feels tight when stretching.” This imbalance significantly restricted their previously expandable range of motion, leading to an “inability to fully open the body into its complete posture.” For example:

Case 2: “The venous stent was placed in my right leg. When stretching, the leg with the stent feels tight and less flexible than the other leg. Before, I could lift both legs over 45°, but now the stent leg cannot reach ≤45° and feels stiff, as if my lower back is locked.”

Case 8: “As soon as I wake up in the morning, I feel something inside my lower back where the stent was placed. The area with the stent has become more sensitive. When I turn over or twist my body, I feel pressure on both sides of my bladder, and it feels stuck or blocked; this particularly occurs early in the morning after waking. Certain positions cause a sense of pressure in the area with the stent. But after using the bathroom and walking around a bit, the stuck feeling becomes less intense.”

The severity of discomfort was sometimes associated with aging. One participant remarked:

Case 2: “Sigh… all I can do is sigh. I’m getting older, and honestly, I’m just gradually aging—like a machine that keeps breaking down. Now I even need a venous stent inside my body to support my blood vessels, which have lost their elasticity.”

Theme 2: Resigned Endurance

In response to newly emerging postoperative symptoms, many participants initially adopted a stance of “choosing to endure,” perceiving their discomfort as too minor to justify a follow-up consultation. As a result, they passively tolerated persistent but manageable challenges.

Case 5: “I kept hoping that as long as the swelling in my right leg subsided, I could tolerate everything else. However, I have not seen much improvement. I was told it would get better, so I tried to stay positive. I thought, if the stent helps support the vein and restores proper blood flow on both sides, then my problem should improve. However, the problem persisted… therefore, I endured.”

Psychologically, several participants described efforts to persevere through discomfort, expressing apprehension that raising concerns might be perceived by physicians as overreacting or displaying undue emotional sensitivity. Some voiced reluctance to “fully attribute their discomfort to the stent,” feared they might be “bothering the doctor,” or worried that questioning could disrupt the clinical workflow, making them “hesitant to bring it up with the physician.” Others feared that additional examinations could uncover new health problems, which in turn might provoke anxiety and lead to further financial burden, prompting them to delay seeking medical attention.

Case 1: “I was anxious that the tight, hard sensation lingered even after surgery, sometimes reaching my foot. Still, I kept quiet, afraid the doctor might think I was overreacting, and just tried to endure it with a massage.”

Case 5: “… the problem persisted. Still, I did not contact the physicians because I was afraid of paying for more tests… Sometimes I would wonder if I was being too sensitive. I did not communicate my problem to the doctor because I thought they would think I was overthinking. Therefore, I decided to keep observing for now. I did not want to annoy the medical professionals by bringing up the same concerns repeatedly.”

Participants reported reaching a point at which their physical symptoms became intolerable, precipitating psychological collapse. At this stage, they could no longer sustain coping strategies based on suppression and endurance alone. This transition did not result from constructive patient–clinician interactions but rather from delayed emotional release following prolonged restraint. Their earlier silence gave way to heightened emotions and a shift in language, from passive tolerance to explicit expressions of dissatisfaction and distress. Some began questioning the effectiveness of their treatment, voicing disappointment with the surgical outcomes, or expressing resentment. This “collapse at the edge of endurance” underscored the convergence of physical and psychological strain, as well as a deep-seated dissatisfaction with the intervention’s results.

Case 11: “I have endured for so long, but I really cannot anymore. Every time my body acts up, I feel like I am going crazy, about to explode. My temper has gotten so bad.”

Theme 3: Feeling Trapped and Regretful

Owing to the inability to accept their physical condition and disappointment in the treatment outcome, participants were in a state of “regretful ambivalence and lack of a course of action.” The patients reached a point where they were “no longer able to hide the pain” and felt “forced” to visit a doctor, making this decision a coercive necessity rather than a voluntary choice. Their discomfort could no longer be masked by calmness and had reached its threshold. Participants revealed that their return to medical care was no longer about problem-solving but was the result of an overwhelming entanglement between persistent physical symptoms and psychological distress. This prolonged suffering left them with no option but to seek medical attention. This act of “seeking help only at the endurance threshold” was no longer a deliberate appeal for care but rather a reluctant and “forced medical re-engagement at the point of breakdown.”

Case 6: “Over time, another voice inside me would say, ‘Well, this is just how things are now. You have to accept it. That is just how it is.’ I did not expect the doctor to provide any comprehensive solutions. I felt like I could not take it anymore, and I finally went for a follow-up and raised my concerns.”

At this stage, many participants increasingly experienced self-criticism and regret, accompanied by feelings of frustration and doubt. Many reported recurrent thoughts of remorse regarding their initial treatment decisions, particularly considering symptom recurrence. Expressions of disappointment in surgical outcomes were common, with some patients questioning the effectiveness of the stent, attributing new and unexplained symptoms to it, and even considering its removal. Although participants repeatedly sought clarification from their physicians, they were frequently advised to “keep observing,” which led them to perceive follow-up visits as futile and heightened their uncertainty about recovery. Persistent discomfort without satisfactory clinical responses intensified their regret and fostered progressive loss of trust in medical advice. Ultimately, this emotional disillusionment eroded the motivation to attend follow-up appointments and contributed to disengagement from care, further straining the patient–clinician relationship.

Case 5: “I consulted many times, and the doctor said what I am experiencing is normal, but I have not improved since the procedure. The swelling persisted, and the symptoms did not improve. The doctor prescribed other treatment options that were not what I expected. I feel disappointed. I am not motivated to undergo follow-up. Each time I went, I was told the same things, without solving my problems.”

Case 7: “I understood that it wasn’t the doctor’s fault or the stent itself, but I still felt very upset. I kept thinking about begging the doctor to remove it. I didn’t want the stent anymore. I struggled with this feeling a lot—after getting the stent, I felt like new physical problems kept appearing. Whenever my body felt uncomfortable, I would think, “Can’t they just take it out?”

Theme 4: Breaking free from constraints and reclaiming physical control

Owing to the physical unpredictability and uncertain recovery, participants realized that neither endurance nor medical intervention could resolve their distress; they needed to actively transform their emotions and attitude to life. Although participants did not verbalize their need for help, their evolving physical and emotional adjustments over time reflected an “internalized distress”—a transformed response that emerged after reaching their endurance limit (). They initiated “self-soothing actions for tension relief,” which helped restore a sense of control. Examples included using a roller to “massage” their legs, applying “heat packs” to the neck, performing “leg stretches,” and engaging in “pelvic core training” to improve lower back flexibility. These practices, often informed by personal experiences or family recommendations, led to emotional relief and a temporary return to a functionally stable state. This process represents a critical transition from patients passively suppressing their discomfort to active agents engaged in directing their recovery.

Case 6: “I applied heat and massaged myself and felt relief in my lower back and legs. These days, I almost always use a heating pad before bed and do stretches. Gradually, I have started to feel like my body is more relaxed.”

Case 8: “A friend suggested core exercises; therefore, I searched for abdominal workout videos online and tried them, which helped a bit as my lower back loosened. Next, I tried to calm myself, telling myself that maybe this was just part of the healing process and that I would get better. Now I do one particular exercise every day: I raise my legs and tighten my pelvic floor. I could not do that before, but now I lift both hands, squeeze my glutes tightly, contract the pelvic muscles, and silently count from one to 10, sometimes up to 50. Next, I lower my legs, take a breath, and tap my feet gently. I do this routine daily after soaking my feet at night. I repeat this procedure about 10 times before bed. The entire process takes approximately 30 min.”

Many participants adapted their daily routines to align with their physical capabilities to regain a sense of autonomy. They engaged in activities such as gardening and walking, which helped reframe their distress and foster a sense of normality. Some adopted cognitive strategies to minimize the emotional impact of chronic pain, while others drew on spirituality through prayer and expressed gratitude. Returning to work was reported by several participants, often for financial reasons. Self-employed individuals managed their symptoms by taking flexible breaks, whereas those in formal employment negotiated with supervisors to modify their assignments in accordance with their physical capacity.

Case 2: “I realized that, as my body was not going to recover quickly, I had to adjust to its needs... Owing to these experiences, I have learned to observe myself more closely and establish a balance between rest and activity. I no longer force my body. Going out for a meal or a short trip with friends and family is now a form of personal autonomy… I just want to live at a gentle pace. If I can have less pain, I am satisfied. At this stage in life, I do not force anything. I try to follow my body’s requirements and live comfortably, one day at a time.”

Case 6: “I realized I could not continue suffering; therefore, I told myself to slowly get back into a routine, even if it was just going out to buy groceries or taking a walk. I would remind myself, ‘This is about reclaiming my daily routine.’ Now I have adjusted my pace of life. I plan things in advance so I do not get too tired or start feeling unwell. Therefore, I plan my time carefully, trying to get everything done during the day so that I can rest in the evening.”

Family support played a crucial role in adaptation, as the understanding and encouragement from spouses and children helped maintain emotional stability and accept the reality of prolonged recovery. Participants focused on small improvements in their conditions, such as reduced discomfort, better sleep, and improved lung capacity, which helped them redefine their relationship with their illness and find a new balance in their daily activities.

Case 2: “I realized my body would not recover quickly, so I learned to observe myself and balance rest with activity. I no longer force my body; sharing a meal or a short trip with my family is now part of my autonomy. At this stage, I just want less pain and to live at a gentle pace.”

Case 4: “Even though I am still suffering from pain, my husband has always been there to support me. My leg has healed. I am sleeping better now, my leg does not cramp as much, and after physical activity, I do not feel breathlessness.”

Discussion

Our findings indicate that many participants experienced unexplained physical changes after surgery, which substantially disrupted their daily lives and caused considerable confusion and discomfort. According to Morse’s theory of suffering,11 individuals with unexplained physical abnormalities and functional limitations often enter the enduring phase, which is characterized by emotional suppression, withdrawal, and heightened self-control. Murali et al.19 reported that back pain is common within the first week after iliac vein stenting—affecting up to 66% of patients—and typically resolves within 1 month, although in rare cases it may persist and become chronic. Stamer et al.20 defined chronic postoperative pain as lasting for more than 3 months and not attributable to other factors. In this study, some participants experienced persistent headache and neuromuscular tension. Conventional treatments may not provide adequate relief,21 underscoring the need for personalized, multidimensional approaches. Referral to psychiatry for therapeutic support may help address anxiety, improve pain responses, and enhance QoL.20

Additionally, the participants reported movement limitations, such as lower back obstruction and stiffness when twisting. Jiang et al.22 observed that patients with iliac vein stents crossing the inguinal region may experience discomfort during bending or squatting. Cheng et al.23 further noted that stent diameter was significantly reduced during extreme hip flexion and hyperextension. Stents that extend across the hip joint can contact the superior pubic ramus during movement, altering its elasticity and potentially contributing to localized discomfort. To reduce the risk of stent deformation and related complications, preoperative education should inform patients about postoperative limitations and advise them to avoid activities involving extreme hip movements, thereby aiding recovery and preserving stent integrity.

We found that many participants refrained from expressing discomfort or seeking clarification, fearing that they might be perceived as bothersome or distrustful. Consequently, they suppressed their symptoms and avoided asking questions, resulting in delayed help-seeking. While these responses remained within what Morse11 terms the enduring phase of suffering—marked by suppressed emotions and passive acceptance of pain—participants had not yet entered the emotional suffering phase, which involves expression and support-seeking behaviors. This aligns with Goffman’s Stigma Theory,24 which suggests that individuals who perceive themselves as potentially deviant often conceal symptoms or suppress emotions to avoid being labeled as “problematic” patients. In East Asian sociocultural contexts, Confucian values of endurance and emotional restraint may further reinforce this behavior,25,26 contributing to delayed assessments and potentially compromising long-term outcomes. To address these risks, clinicians should adopt cultural humility, a reflective process of recognizing sociocultural dynamics and power imbalances, to better identify distress that may remain unrecognized. Practical strategies include integrating brief screening tools, such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7, for the early detection of emotional distress.27,28 These instruments may facilitate structured dialogue in contexts where emotional expression is culturally constrained. Furthermore, artificial intelligence-powered voice analysis has potential for detecting subtle emotional cues in speech, enabling timely and culturally sensitive interventions.29

Herein, participants with prolonged physical discomfort gradually shifted from relying on medical interventions to developing personalized, self-regulatory coping strategies. This aligns with Morse’s view that navigating emotional suffering leads individuals to regain control through purposeful action.11 Similarly, Stanisławski observed that under sustained stress, individuals often transition from emotion-focused to problem-focused coping to manage the source of distress.30 In this study, participants developed symptom-management techniques tailored to their physical limitations and daily routines to minimize functional disruption. Their reassertion of control extended beyond pain management to encompass functional recovery and role reconstruction. Those returning to work during recovery encountered both financial and physical challenges but demonstrated agency by adjusting their schedules and taking breaks according to bodily cues. Van de Velde et al.31 defined self-management as a patient-led process involving medical management, emotional regulation, and functional adaptation. When individuals adjust their behaviors in line with their physical and emotional states while remaining engaged in meaningful activities, they demonstrate effective self-management, which is essential for maintaining QoL and balance in the context of chronic illness.

This observation resonates with the findings of Love et al.,32 who identified psychological resilience as a key protective factor against cardiovascular disease. Resilience derives from multiple domains, including psychological (eg, emotional regulation and hope), sociocultural (eg, social support), behavioral (eg, health-promoting practices), and biological (eg, neuroendocrine regulation) factors. Even when complete control over disease progression was unattainable, participants who adjusted their routines, avoided extreme activities, and maintained structured lifestyles reported greater emotional stability and self-efficacy. These practices fostered personal empowerment and strengthened motivation for recovery. Therefore, healthcare providers should acknowledge patients’ self-initiated adaptive behaviors and provide flexible support to reinforce self-management. This can enhance patients’ capacity to restore bodily autonomy and psychological well-being in their occupational, familial, and social contexts.

Strengths and Limitations

This study focuses on the unresolved recovery phase after iliac vein stent placement in patients with CVD, a topic rarely addressed in previous research. By adopting a phenomenological approach, we were able to capture patients’ embodied experiences of suffering, thereby providing insights often overlooked in studies based solely on quantitative outcomes. Second, the dual analytical framework employed—the integration of inductive analysis with theory-informed interpretation—enhances the robustness of our findings. Open coding ensured a thorough representation of participants’ experiences, while the application of the Praxis Theory of Suffering provided theoretical depth to our interpretations.

Despite its strengths, the study has notable limitations. Of the 12 participants, most were middle-aged females (n = 8) aged 40 to 64. Individuals in this life stage may face contextual challenges related to work, family responsibilities, and existing health conditions, which can contribute to ongoing discomfort, emotional distress, and shape how they understand their bodily self-image. Additionally, only 4 male participants were included. Consequently, the study may not fully capture how male patients interpret their postoperative bodily changes or navigate their recovery challenges. The number of older adults was also limited, which restricts deeper exploration of how individuals in later life stages interpret unresolved recovery experiences and changes in bodily self-perception. Additionally, participants willing to share their postoperative experiences may have had more favorable recovery outcomes, while those who declined participation might have been too unwell to engage. Consequently, the present findings may not fully capture the experiences of male patients, older adults, and individuals with more severe or complex postoperative conditions. Future research is needed to explore how these specific groups experience postoperative recovery and changes in their bodily self-image.

Conclusions

This study offers a comprehensive portrayal of the physical and emotional challenges experienced by patients with CVD during the unresolved postoperative recovery period following stent placement. It highlights the pivotal roles of cultural background, clinical interactions, and individual patterns of emotional expression, indicating that “internalized distress,” determined by cultural norms, often manifests through physical symptoms rather than direct verbal communication, particularly where patients feel unable to articulate their distress. Over time, many patients began to engage in personal attempts to alleviate physical discomfort and regain a sense of control. Rather than viewing recovery from a biomedical perspective, this study underscores the importance of incorporating culturally attuned, patient-centered perspectives into care practices. Future clinical strategies should consider sociocultural dynamics and the potential for technological innovations to enhance communication and support for patients.
