Do the treatment selection factors emphasized in clinical guidelines—Chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and heart failure—contribute to the risk of urinary tract infections during the administration of sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors?
Authors: Takuma Koinuma, Manato Yoshida, Manabu Akazawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionSodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2Is) are recommended in Japan for patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD), heart failure (HF), or chronic kidney disease (CKD) due to their organ‐protective effects, leading to increased use in high‐risk populations. However, whether urinary tract infection (UTI) risk associated with SGLT2Is differs according to the presence of these factors remains unclear. This study aimed to estimate UTI risk among SGLT2I users and treatment selection factors and examine whether these factors modify risk. We selected dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP4Is) as the comparators.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used DeSC claims data from patients with diabetes mellitus who received their first prescription for either an SGLT2I or a DPP4I between October 2014 and February 2023. The analysis focused on data from 2019 to 2022. Hazard ratios (HRs) for UTI incidence were estimated using Cox proportional hazard regression.ResultsA total of 66,968 SGLT2I users and 170,678 DPP4I users were aged 65 years or older. UTI incidence among SGLT2I users was 2.04% in those with treatment selection factors and 0.84% in those without. Compared with DPP4I users, the HR for UTI was 0.80 in patients with treatment selection factors and 0.51 in those without treatment selection factors.ConclusionsSGLT2I use was not associated with an increase in UTI risk, regardless of treatment selection factor status. These findings suggest a favorable safety profile for SGLT2Is even among high‐risk populations, supporting their use following clinical guidelines.Keywords: SGLT2 Inhibitors, Treatment selection factors in Type 2 Diabetes, Urinary Tract Infection Risk

INTRODUCTION

Sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2Is) lower blood glucose levels and exert additional pharmacological effects, such as anti‐inflammatory and anti‐fibrotic actions 1 , 2 , 3 . First introduced in 2014, SGLT2Is have been linked to various side effects and adverse events due to their impact on metabolism and circulation, prompting the Japan Diabetes Society to publish the ‘Recommendation for Proper Use of SGLT2 Inhibitors’ in June 2014. Important adverse events include hypoglycemia, ketoacidosis, dehydration, genital infections, and urinary tract infections (UTIs), which have been described as important concerns during SGLT2I administration 4 .

Since 2015, randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the effects of SGLT2Is beyond blood glucose lowering 5 , 6 . The Japanese Circulation Society and Japan Diabetes Society have also reported the effectiveness of SGLT2Is in preventing heart failure (HF) in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) in a consensus statement 7 . Meta‐analyses have further demonstrated their effectiveness in reducing the risk of cardiovascular events and improving kidney outcomes, highlighting their organ‐protective effects 8 . Consequently, indications for SGLT2Is have expanded.

Following reports on the effectiveness of SGLT2Is and the need for drug selection based on their organ‐protective effects, the Japan Diabetes Society developed a drug therapy algorithm for type 2 diabetes 9 . In this algorithm, the organ‐protective effects of SGLT2Is are referred to as additional benefits. The algorithm highlights chronic kidney disease (CKD), cardiovascular disease, and HF as conditions for which these additional benefits should be considered. For patients with these conditions, SGLT2I use is recommended based on a balance between benefits and risks.

Previous studies have reported changes in the prescription patterns of SGLT2Is following guideline revisions and the expansion of their approved indications. 10 , 11 In particular, an increase in the prescription of SGLT2Is in patients with treatment selection factors over time has been reported, reflecting their use in high‐risk populations. Therefore, adverse event risk may have changed as the characteristics of SGLT2Is users have evolved over time. Therefore, when evaluating the risk of adverse events, conducting analyses that account for the period effects is necessary.

The safety of SGLT2Is has been investigated in previous studies that examined the association between SGLT2Is and UTIs. 12 , 13 , 14 UTIs can progress to sepsis and even death, making them clinically significant outcomes. In Japan, a study using Diagnosis Procedure Combination data has also reported a UTI‐related mortality rate as high as 4.5%. 15 Therefore, in this study, we defined severe UTI cases as those with two or more confirmed UTI diagnoses recorded within the same month.

SGLT2Is are increasingly prescribed to older adults and high‐risk patients with CKD, CVD, and HF because of their organ‐protective benefits. However, whether treatment selection factors influence the risk of UTIs remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the extent to which treatment selection factors influence UTI risk associated with SGLT2I use among adults aged 65 years and older from 2019 to 2022, with particular focus on the presence or absence of CKD, CVD, and HF. The safety of SGLT2Is in high‐risk populations was evaluated from two perspectives: (1) the presence or absence of treatment selection factors and (2) the extent to which treatment selection factors influence risk, that is, whether they act as effect modifiers. As a subgroup analysis, we estimated the risk associated with each treatment selection factor (CKD, CVD, and HF) separately, to clarify the risk profile of each condition and to provide information that may inform future treatment decisions. Furthermore, because the risk of UTI may differ between males and females due to anatomical differences, we also conducted sex‐stratified analyses to evaluate the potential effect modification by sex.

In this study, dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP4Is) were used as a reference comparator to facilitate the clinical interpretation of the UTI risk associated with SGLT2I use, given their widespread use and well‐established safety profile as antidiabetic agents in Japan. 16 , 17 , 18 It should be noted that this study was not designed as a head‐to‐head comparative study between the two drug classes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Database

This study used the DeSC Healthcare Inc. insurer database (April 2014–February 2023), which includes claims from three insurers: National Health Insurance (Kokuho), health insurance for large corporate employees (Kenpo), and the Advanced Elderly Medical Service System (Koki Koreisha Iryo Seido) for those aged ≥75. The database contained data on 2,220,702 individuals (Kenpo: 824,516; Kokuho: 1,095,713; Koki Koreisha Iryo Seido: 300,473). 19 In contrast, national enrollment figures were 78 million (Kenpo), 27 million (Kokuho), and 18 million (Koki Koreisha Iryo Seido). 20 Therefore, the DeSC data showed a higher proportion of individuals from Kokuho compared with that from the overall population. 21 In Japan, several studies have been conducted using DeSC data. 22 , 23

The dataset included patient information from the insured ledger, such as sex, date of birth, and observation start date. It also contained disease‐related information from medical receipts, including the International Statistical Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD‐10) master codes and disease codes. Furthermore, the data contained receipt information and subscriber IDs. Pharmaceutical information was obtained from drug names and details, including Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification codes and drug codes.

Extraction of target patients with DM

Patients with a confirmed diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (ICD‐10 code E11), DM related to nutritional disorders (E12), other explicit DM (E13), or DM with unknown details (E14) between October 2014 and February 2023 were selected from the DeSC data. In addition, patients who had at least one prescription for an SGLT2I (ATC code A10P) or a DPP4I (ATC code A10N) after the earliest confirmed diagnosis were defined as target patients with DM.

Definition of SGLT2I users and DPP4I users

Among the target patients with DM, those who received their first SGLT2I or DPP4I prescription between October 2014 and February 2023 were identified. Furthermore, a minimum of 6 months of observation before the initial administration of either SGLT2Is or DPP4Is was required, and patients who met this criterion were included. Among these, patients aged ≥65 years were included in the analysis. Among the eligible patients, those who received an initial prescription of SGLT2I or DPP4I were classified as SGLT2I users and DPP4I users, respectively. Patients who received initial prescriptions for both SGLT2I and DPP4I within the same month were excluded (Figure 1). SGLT2Is are preferentially recommended for patients with CKD, HF, and CVD because of their cardiovascular and renal protective effects. 9 As these conditions are more prevalent among older adults, the use of SGLT2 inhibitors has increasingly expanded into older populations. Older patients are also generally more susceptible to adverse events and are more likely to have multiple comorbidities, making the safety profile of SGLT2 inhibitors particularly important in this population. Therefore, older adults represent an important target population for evaluating the risk of UTI associated with SGLT2 inhibitor use. For this reason, the present study was restricted to patients aged 65 years and older.

Definition of terms

Treatment selection factors 9 were defined as confirmed diagnoses of CKD (ICD‐10 code N18), CVD (ICD‐10 codes I21, I22, I65, I66), or HF (ICD‐10 code I50) within 3 months preceding the first administration of SGLT2I or DPP4I. 24 , 25 Other comorbidities were defined as any confirmed diagnosis of relevant diseases according to the ICD‐10 codes within the same 3‐month period before the first dose of SGLT2I or DPP4I. Concomitant medications were those administered within 3 months of the first dose of each drug, according to the ATC classification codes (Table S1). Past usage of other antidiabetic medications was defined as ‘administered’ if the patient had received at least one prescription. 24 , 25 We defined current and historical treatments/comorbidities using distinct retrospective windows. Outcomes were defined as the occurrence of UTI with a follow‐up period of 6 months starting from the first dose of SGLT2I. UTIs were identified using ICD‐10 codes N10, N11, N12, N13.6, N15.1, N15.9, N16, N20.9, N30, N34, N37.0, and N39.0. UTIs were considered outcomes if they appeared in the claim records at least twice in the same month. To ensure diagnostic validity, we required at least two UTI diagnoses. Prior research showed ICD‐10 code‐based definitions had 84.7% sensitivity and 57.0%‐positive predictive value. 26

In this study, according to the consensus statement from the Japan Diabetes Society (A consensus statement from the Japan Diabetes Society: A proposed algorithm for pharmacotherapy in people with type 2 diabetes), CKD, HF, and CVD are explicitly described as ‘treatment selection factors’ to be considered when selecting SGLT2Is. 9 Accordingly, CKD, HF, and CVD were defined as treatment selection factors for SGLT2Is in this study. To evaluate the safety of SGLT2Is in a manner that reflects treatment decision‐making in real‐world treatment decision‐making, we focused on these three conditions and analyzed them separately.

Selection of confounders

The variables included the presence or absence of treatment selection factors, sex, age, month, and year of treatment initiation, and type of insurance; additional covariates are described in Table S1. The presence of treatment selection factors was defined as having at least one of the conditions used for stratification in the UTI risk comparison, namely, CKD, HF, or CVD. Antidiabetic medications were classified in greater detail, and concomitant use was defined as the prescription of any of the following agents within 3 months before the index month: sulfonylureas, metformin, insulin preparations, imeglimin, alpha‐glucosidase inhibitors, thiazolidinediones, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, and glinides. Patients without a prescription history of these agents were defined as receiving monotherapy. The additional covariates are listed in Table S1. 24 , 25 , 27

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 statistical software. Guideline updates and expanded indications have led to increased SGLT2I use in patients with treatment selection factors. Consequently, the utilization of these agents in high‐risk populations has been documented. 10 , 11 In a prior study conducted by the authors using the same database, the use of SGLT2Is was shown to have expanded from initially younger patients to high‐risk older adults with treatment selection factors such as CVD, CKD, and HF, suggesting a shift in the target patient population for SGLT2I prescriptions. 28 Therefore, the patient population receiving SGLT2Is differed between the initial launch period and subsequent years, with a higher proportion of use expected among high‐risk populations from 2019 to 2022; thus, the analysis focused on this period. Data from 2023 were excluded from the analysis because only 2 months of data were available, which was deemed insufficient for meaningful comparisons with other years.

In the primary analysis, the hazard ratio (HR) for the incidence of UTIs among SGLT2I and DPP4I users was estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression over a maximum observation period of 6 months from the start of exposure. An observation period of up to 6 months was selected, considering that the target outcome often occurs early in the course of treatment. 29 A product term between SGLT2I use and the presence or absence of treatment selection factors was included.

As a subgroup analysis, we conducted stratified analyses for CKD, HF, and CVD. For each treatment selection factor, patients with the respective factor were compared with those without any of the three treatment selection factors (CKD, HF, and CVD). As in the primary analysis, HRs were estimated using a Cox proportional hazards model. In addition, interaction terms between SGLT2 inhibitor use and each treatment selection factor (CKD, CVD, or HF) were incorporated into the model to assess potential effect modification. Furthermore, because the risk of UTI may differ between males and females due to anatomical differences, sex‐stratified analyses were also conducted.

Ethical considerations

This observational study used de‐identified and anonymized DeSC data, ensuring that patient names and identities of the medical institutions providing the data were not linked. The analyzed data were processed on a secure, encrypted personal computer to ensure confidentiality. This study was approved by the Meiji Pharmaceutical University Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 202301; approval date: April 20, 2023).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we evaluated the UTI risk associated with SGLT2I use among older patients with treatment selection factors such as CKD, HF, and CVD. In the main analysis, the direction of HRs was generally consistent regardless of the presence or absence of treatment selection factors, and the results did not suggest a marked increase in UTI risk associated with SGLT2I use even among older adults with CKD, HF, or CVD. Furthermore, in the stratified analyses by each treatment selection factor using product terms, HRs remained below 1.0 across all strata, and no clear increase in UTI risk was observed. HRs tended to approach 1.0 in patients with treatment selection factors, suggesting that appropriate selection of SGLT2Is based on individual patient background may be important.

In a previous study conducted by our group, we reported that prescriptions of SGLT2 inhibitors have increased among older patients with treatment selection factors such as HF, CKD, and CVD since 2019. 28 As these conditions are more prevalent among older individuals, this trend may have contributed to a gradual increase in the mean age of SGLT2I users over time, thereby reducing the difference in mean age between the two groups. In contrast, DPP4Is continue to be widely prescribed to older patients in real‐world clinical practice in Japan 16 and may be more likely to be selected for beneficiaries of the Late‐Stage Elderly Medical Care System, who are generally considered to be at higher risk of adverse events. Consequently, although no substantial difference in mean age was observed between the two groups, the proportion of beneficiaries enrolled in the Late‐Stage Elderly Medical Care System was higher in the DPP4I group.

According to previous studies, the reported incidence of UTIs ranges from 0% to 16.5%, with severe cases accounting for 0.7%. 30 Furthermore, as UTI symptoms can be detected through medical interviews and urine tests, most cases are considered mild lower UTIs. 31 , 32 In this study, UTI occurrence was defined as two or more claims within the same month. As this criterion may have preferentially captured relatively severe cases requiring repeated medical visits within a short period, the UTI incidence observed in the present study appears reasonable.

In the stratified analysis by the presence or absence of each treatment selection factor (CKD, CVD, HF) or any of these treatment selection factors, the UTI incidence proportions and the direction of HRs were generally consistent across strata, despite the SGLT2I group including a greater proportion of high‐risk patients with treatment selection factors. These results did not support a clear increase in UTI risk associated with SGLT2I use, at least among older adults with treatment selection factors. The post‐marketing recommendations for the appropriate use of SGLT2Is issued at the time of their initial launch in Japan may have partly contributed to these findings. These recommendations emphasize adequate fluid intake and vigilance regarding urinary tract and genital infections. 4 Previous studies have reported the effectiveness of disseminating such recommendations in diabetes care, 33 , 34 , 35 suggesting that these practices may have influenced the UTI risk profile observed in the present study.

The association between SGLT2I use and UTI risk has not been consistently reported in previous studies. While some studies have suggested a possible increase in UTI risk associated with SGLT2I use 36 , 37 , a growing number of recent reports have found no clear increase in UTI risk with SGLT2I use 12 , 13 , 14 . From a pharmacological perspective, SGLT2Is may theoretically increase UTI risk through enhanced urinary glucose excretion, which promotes bacterial growth; conversely, increased urine volume due to osmotic diuresis may facilitate the flushing out of uropathogenic bacteria, acting in the opposing direction 13 . Therefore, the association between SGLT2I use and UTI risk remains a matter of debate.

It is also possible that residual confounding related to patient selection and prescribing behavior in real‐world clinical practice may have influenced the results observed in this study. In Japan, SGLT2Is may have been used more cautiously in patients at higher risk for dehydration or recurrent UTIs, potentially resulting in the preferential selection of patients with a relatively lower UTI risk in the SGLT2I group at the prescribing stage. Furthermore, the higher proportion of beneficiaries enrolled in the Late‐Stage Elderly Medical Care System in the DPP4I group observed in this study suggests differences in patient background and prescribing patterns between the two groups. Therefore, the findings observed in this study should be interpreted with caution, taking into account not only the pharmacological mechanisms but also the potential influence of patient background and prescribing practices in real‐world clinical settings. These factors may partly explain the higher incidence of UTI observed in the DPP4I group than in the SGLT2I group. Because DPP4Is are frequently prescribed to older and potentially more vulnerable patients in routine clinical practice, differences in patient background and prescribing behavior may have contributed to the observed differences in UTI incidence between the two groups.

In the subgroup analyses, a relatively large quantitative interaction was observed in the stratified analyses by the presence or absence of CVD and HF. As HF and CVD may themselves influence UTI susceptibility, differences in background risk may have affected the estimated HRs in these strata. In contrast, no substantial difference in HR was observed in the stratified analysis by CKD status. In the context of CKD, opposing influences exist—namely, a reduction in urinary glucose excretion associated with declining renal function and the osmotic diuretic effect of SGLT2Is—and the balance between these mechanisms may have been reflected in the results. Nevertheless, the direction of HRs was generally consistent across all stratified analyses, and the results did not suggest that the association between SGLT2I use and UTI risk was substantially altered by the presence or absence of CKD, HF, or CVD. Sex‐stratified analyses showed no substantial differences in UTI incidence proportions or in the direction of HRs between males and females, suggesting that the association between SGLT2I use and UTI risk was generally consistent across sexes and that there may be no clear effect modification by sex.

A key feature of this study is that it focused on treatment selection factors for which SGLT2I use is preferentially recommended per clinical guidelines, and evaluated UTI risk in older patients in this context. Evaluating the potential effect modification by treatment selection factor status and the potential adverse effects of SGLT2Is represents a unique aspect of this study.

This study has certain limitations. First, UTI occurrence was defined as two or more claims within the same month, which may have preferentially captured relatively severe UTI cases, potentially underestimating the incidence of mild cases. Second, disease definitions were based solely on diagnostic codes, and the severity of the condition could not be assessed. Third, the database used in this study was a claims‐based database and did not include clinical variables such as body mass index or HbA1c. Furthermore, factors known to influence UTI risk, such as benign prostatic hyperplasia, malignancy, and corticosteroid use, were not fully accounted for in the analysis, and residual confounding related to patient selection and prescribing behavior in real‐world clinical practice cannot be excluded, particularly when interpreting comparisons with DPP‐4 inhibitors. However, DPP4Is were included as a reference comparator rather than for head‐to‐head comparison between the two drug classes to facilitate the clinical interpretation of the UTI risk associated with SGLT2I use according to the presence or absence of treatment selection factors.

In conclusion, no clear increase in UTI risk associated with SGLT2I use was observed even among older patients with treatment selection factors such as CKD, HF, and CVD. These results suggest that the UTI risk profile of SGLT2Is may be acceptable in older patients when used appropriately in accordance with individual patient characteristics and clinical guidelines.
