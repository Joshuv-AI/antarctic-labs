Volumetric density estimates for nonlocal minimal surfaces Thanks: Work supported by the National Science Centre, Poland, grant no. 2023/49/B/ST1/04303
Mateusz Kwaśnicki, Jack Thompson

Abstract
. In this article, we prove that viscosity subsolutions to nonlocal mean curvature-type equations satisfy universal volumetric estimates at all scales. Our results hold for general symmetric kernels that are comparable to the fractional Laplacian. Furthermore, we prove that subsolutions with low density (with respect to a universal constant) necessarily have ‘fat boundary’, that is, have topological boundary with positive Lebesgue measure.

1.
Introduction

Minimal surfaces, famously arising as a model for soap films, appear ubiquitously in the applied sciences including in chemistry, materials science, biology, general relativity, and even architectural design. Their analysis is a rich and challenging topic, and has required the development of many techniques that are now widely used across geometry, topology, measure theory, and the analysis of partial differential equations. Of particular significance is the regularity theory for minimal surfaces which has seen significant progress over the past century and is still currently an active area of research. Some references on the foundational theory of minimal surfaces include
[
18
,
12
,
24
]
.

More recently, over the last decade and a half, there has been a consolidated interest in nonlocal or fractional minimal surfaces which may be viewed as a non-infinitesimal generalisation of the classical minimal surfaces. In applications, nonlocal minimal surfaces arise naturally in phase transition models where the underlying diffusion can exhibit long-range jumps, see
[
16
]
. For general surveys on nonlocal minimal surfaces and their regularity theory, see
[
15

,
22

The study of nonlocal minimal surfaces began with the celebrated paper of Caffarelli, Roquejoffre, Savin
[
5

where they introduced so-called
s
-minimal surfaces, the prototypical example of a nonlocal minimal surface. Concretely, if
s\in(0,1)

s
-perimeter
of a measurable set

(1.1)

\operatorname{Per}_{s}(E;\Omega)=\frac{1}{4}\iint_{Q(\Omega)}\frac{\lvert\tilde{\chi}_{E}(x)-\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)\rvert}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}\,dy\,dx.

Here

Q(\Omega)=\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\bigl((\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega)\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega)\bigr)=\bigl(\Omega\times\Omega\bigr)\cup\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega\times\Omega\bigr)\cup\bigl(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega\bigr),

\curv_{s,E}=0\quad\text{on }\partial E,

(1.3)

\curv_{s,E}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}\,dy,\qquad x\in\partial E

1.1.
Density estimates

In the regularity theory of classical and nonlocal minimal surfaces, a central role is played by density estimates which are estimates describing quantitatively how ‘close’ portions of a surface can be to one another. The term ‘density estimate’ can refer to one of several different, but related inequalities. Indeed, given an open set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
0\in\partial E
, a
volumetric density estimate
refers to the inequality

(V)

\min\{\lvert E\cap B_{\varrho}\rvert,\lvert B_{\varrho}\setminus E\rvert\}\geqslant C\varrho^{d},\qquad\varrho>0

for some
C>0
. Moreover, a
surface density estimate from above
(also called a
perimetric density estimate from above
) refers to the inequality

(SA)

\operatorname{Per}(E;B_{\varrho})\leqslant C\varrho^{d-1},\qquad\varrho>0

and a
surface density estimate from below
refers to the inequality

(SB)

\operatorname{Per}(E;B_{\varrho})\geqslant C\varrho^{d-1},\qquad\varrho>0

for some
C>0
. Of course, if
E
is sufficiently smooth then (
V
), (
SA
), and (
SB
) all hold for
\varrho
sufficiently small and
C
depending on the regularity of
E
, so the difficulty lies in establishing these inequalities for all
\varrho>0
and for a universal constant
C>0
.

1.1.1.
Classical minimal surfaces

If
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geqslant 2
, is a set of locally finite perimeter that is locally minimising with respect to the classical perimeter, it is known that (
V
), (
SA
), and (

) hold for all
\varrho>0
and

, see, for example,
[

. Indeed, (

(1.4)

\operatorname{Per}(E;B_{\varrho})\leqslant\operatorname{Per}(B_{\varrho};E)

which holds due to the minimality of

(1.5)

\operatorname{Per}(E;B_{\varrho})\geqslant C(d)\bigl(\min\{\lvert E\cap B_{\varrho}\rvert,\lvert B_{\varrho}\setminus E\rvert\}\bigr)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}.

When

For stable or finite Morse index minimal surfaces, the validity of (

1.2.
Main results

To state our results precisely, let
d\geqslant 1
,
s\in(0,1)
, and
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that

(1.8)

\frac{\lambda}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}\leqslant K(y)\leqslant\frac{\Lambda}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}\text{ with }0<\lambda\leqslant\Lambda

and

(1.9)

K(y)=K(-y).

Then, given a Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an open bounded set
\Omega
, the nonlocal perimeter associated with the kernel
K
in
\Omega
is given by

\operatorname{Per}_{K}(E;\Omega)=\frac{1}{4}\iint_{Q(\Omega)}\lvert\tilde{\chi}_{E}(x)-\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)\rvert\,K(x-y)\,dy\,dx.

Here

Q(\Omega)=\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega\bigr)=\bigl(\Omega\times\Omega\bigr)\cup\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega\times\Omega\bigr)\cup\bigl(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega\bigr)

and
\tilde{\chi}_{E}=\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus E}-\chi_{E}
where
\chi_{F}
denotes the characteristic function of a Borel set
F
. Furthermore, the ‘full’ nonlocal perimeter associated with
K
is

\operatorname{Per}_{K}(E)=\operatorname{Per}_{K}(E;\mathbb{R}^{d})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus E}\int_{E}K(x-y)\,dy\,dx.

In the particular case
K(y)=\lvert y\rvert^{-(d+s)}
, we refer to
\operatorname{Per}_{K}
as the
s
-perimeter and write
\operatorname{Per}_{K}=\operatorname{Per}_{s}
as in (
1.1
).

Next, we define the nonlocal mean curvature associated with the kernel
K
by

(1.10)

\curv_{K,E}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy,\qquad x\in\partial E

provided this limit exists. This definition is motivated by the fact that for
E
sufficiently regular, say
C^{2}
, the limit in (
1.10
) exists for all
x\in\partial E
and the Euler-Lagrange equation for
\operatorname{Per}_{K}(E)
is given by

(1.11)

\curv_{K,E}=0\qquad\text{on }\partial E,

see
[
17
, Theorem 6.1]
, in analogy to classical minimal surfaces. Furthermore, a sufficiently regular set
E
is referred to as a nonlocal minimal surface (with respect to
K
) if it satisfies (
1.11
) in the pointwise sense. As with the nonlocal perimeter, in the particular case
K(y)=\lvert y\rvert^{-(d+s)}
, we refer to
\curv_{K,E}
as the
s
-mean curvature, and solutions to (
1.11
) as
s
-minimal surfaces.

For rough sets, (
1.11

One such weak formulation is the notion of viscosity solutions to nonlocal mean curvature equations, and this is definition of solution we adopt in this article. We only require the definition of a viscosity subsolution, which we give below:

be a ball. Then the limit

(2.1)

\curv_{K,B}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B}(y)K(x-y)\,dy

exists for all
x\in\partial B
and

\lambda r^{-s}\curv_{s,B_{1}}\!(e_{1})\leqslant\curv_{K,B_{r}(x_{0})}(x)\leqslant\Lambda r^{-s}\curv_{s,B_{1}}(e_{1})\qquad\text{for all }x\in\partial B,

where

\curv_{s,B_{1}}\!(e_{1})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(e_{1})}(y)\frac{dy}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}.

is the
s
-mean curvature of a unit ball.

Remark 2.2
.

One can compute
\curv_{s,B_{1}}\!(e_{1})
explicitly in terms of the gamma function, denoted by
\Gamma
, and it is given by

\curv_{s,B_{1}}=\frac{2^{1-s}\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\Gamma\bigl(\frac{1-s}{2}\bigr)}{s\Gamma\bigl(\frac{d-s}{2}\bigr)}.

In particular, we have
C^{-1}s^{-1}(1-s)^{-1}\leqslant\curv_{s,B_{1}}\!(e_{1})\leqslant Cs^{-1}(1-s)^{-1}
when
d\geqslant 2
and
C^{-1}s^{-1}\leqslant\curv_{s,B_{1}}\!(e_{1})\leqslant Cs^{-1}
when
d=1
, with
C>0
depending only on
d
.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

First, we will prove the lemma in the particular case
x_{0}\in\partial B_{1}
,
r=1
and
x=0
. The general case will follow by translating, rotating, and rescaling. Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and write

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)K(y)\,dy=I_{\varepsilon}+J

where

I_{\varepsilon}=\int_{B_{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)K(y)\,dy

and

J=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{2}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)K(y)\,dy.

For
I_{\varepsilon}
, we have that

I_{\varepsilon}=\int_{(B_{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon})\setminus B_{1}(x_{0})}K(y)\,dy-\int_{(B_{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon})\cap B_{1}(x_{0})}K(y)\,dy.

Making the change of variable
y\to-y
in the second integral above and using (
1.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{(B_{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon})\setminus B_{1}(x_{0})}K(y)\,dy-\int_{(B_{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon})\cap B_{1}(-x_{0})}K(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{(B_{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon})\setminus(B_{1}(x_{0})\cup B_{1}(-x_{0}))}K(y)\,dy.

Hence, the monotone convergence theorem implies that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}I_{\varepsilon}
exists, which further implies that the limit (
2.1
) exists. Moreover, by (
1.8
), we obtain

I_{\varepsilon}\leqslant\Lambda\int_{(B_{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon})\setminus(B_{1}(x_{0})\cup B_{1}(-x_{0}))}\frac{dy}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}=\Lambda\int_{B_{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)\frac{dy}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}

Similarly, we also obtain

I_{\varepsilon}\geqslant\lambda\int_{B_{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)\frac{dy}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}.

The estimate for
J
is easy, since
\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}=1
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{2}
, so (
1.8
) immediately implies

\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{2}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)\frac{dy}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}\leqslant J\leqslant\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{2}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)\frac{dy}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}

Hence, collecting all the estimates above, we obtain

\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)\frac{dy}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)K(y)\,dy\leqslant\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(x_{0})}(y)\frac{dy}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}.

Sending
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
implies

\lambda\curv_{s,B_{1}}\!(x_{0})\leqslant\curv_{K,B}(0)\leqslant\Lambda\curv_{s,B_{1}}\!(x_{0}),

and the result follows by invariance of
\curv_{s,B_{1}}\!
under rotations.

For a general ball
B=B_{r}(x_{0})
, we make the change of variable
y=-rz+x
to obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{r\varepsilon}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{B}(y)K(x-y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\chi}_{B_{1}(\tilde{x}_{0})}(z)\tilde{K}(z)\,dz,

where
\tilde{x}_{0}=\frac{x-x_{0}}{r}\in\partial B
and
\tilde{K}(z)=r^{d}K(rz)
. Sending
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we get

\curv_{K,B}(x)=\curv_{\tilde{K},B_{1}(\tilde{x}_{0})}(0).

Since the kernel
\tilde{K}
satisfies (
1.8
)–(
1.9
) with constants
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda r^{-s}
and
\tilde{\Lambda}=\Lambda r^{-s}
, the result follows.
∎

Now, we can prove that
\curv_{K,E}(x)
is well-defined in the principal value sense when
E
is touched at
x
by an exterior ball. We also give a non-principal value formula for
\curv_{K,E}
in this case.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
K
satisfy (
1.8
)-(
1.9
),
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set,
B
be a ball of radius
r>0
such that
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus E
, and
x\in\partial E\cap\partial B
. Then the limit

(2.2)

\curv_{K,E}(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy

exists and is given by

(2.3)

\curv_{K,E}(x)=-\curv_{K,B}(x)+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(E\cup B)}K(x-y)\,dy\in[-H_{K,B}(x),+\infty]

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and observe that

\biggl\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy\biggr\rvert\leqslant 2\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{dy}{\lvert y\rvert^{d+s}}<+\infty.

Then, it follows that,

\displaystyle I_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}(y)K(x-y)\,dy

\displaystyle=2\int_{\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(E\cup B)\bigr)\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}K(x-y)\,dy.

By the monotone convergence theorem, the integral on the right-hand side above converges to

2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(E\cup B)}K(x-y)\,dy\in[0,+\infty]

as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. Furthermore, by Lemma
2.1
,

J_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B}(y)K(x-y)\,dy=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{B}(y)K(x-y)\,dy\to-\curv_{K,B}(x)

as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. Then, writing

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy=I_{\varepsilon}+J_{\varepsilon},

we see that the limit in (
2.2
) exists and equals (
2.3
).
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we collect several lemmata that we require for the proof of Theorem
1.2
. Our first lemma is an application of the Hardy–Littlewood maximal inequality which morally states that a set
E
of small measure is necessarily sparse at most points
x
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
R>0
, and
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set. There exists
\vartheta>0
such that if

(3.1)

\lvert E\cap B_{R}\rvert\leqslant\vartheta\alpha\lvert B_{R}\rvert,

then the measure of the set

(3.2)

D_{\alpha}=\{x\in B_{R/4}\text{ s.t. }\lvert E\cap B_{r}(x)\rvert\leqslant\alpha\lvert B_{r}\rvert\text{ for all }r\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}R]\}

is at least
\frac{1}{2}\lvert B_{R/4}\rvert
. The constant
\vartheta>0
depends only on
d
.

Proof.

Let
f=\chi_{E\cap B_{R}}
, and let
Mf
be the corresponding centred Hardy–Littlewood maximal function:

\displaystyle Mf(x)

\displaystyle=\sup\biggl\{\frac{1}{\lvert B_{r}\rvert}\int_{B_{r}(x)}\lvert f(y)\rvert dy\text{ s.t. }r>0\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\sup\biggl\{\frac{\lvert E\cap B_{R}\cap B_{r}(x)\rvert}{\lvert B_{r}\rvert}\text{ s.t. }r>0\biggr\}.

By the Hardy–Littlewood maximal inequality, there is a constant
C>0
depending only on
d
such that

\lvert\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ s.t. }Mf(x)>\alpha\}\rvert\leqslant C\,\frac{\lVert f\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}{\alpha}=C\,\frac{\lvert E\cap B_{R}\rvert}{\alpha},

so (
3.1
) implies

(3.3)

\lvert\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ s.t. }Mf(x)>\alpha\}\rvert\leqslant C\vartheta\lvert B_{R}\rvert=4^{d}\vartheta\lvert B_{R/4}\rvert.

Next, observe that

(3.4)

\{x\in B_{R/4}\text{ s.t. }Mf(x)\leqslant\alpha\}\subset D_{\alpha}.

Indeed, if
x\in B_{R/4}
and
Mf(x)\leqslant\alpha
, then for all
r\in(0,R/2]
, we have
B_{r}(x)\subset B_{R}
, so

\frac{\lvert E\cap B_{r}(x)\rvert}{\lvert B_{r}\rvert}=\frac{\lvert E\cap B_{R}\cap B_{r}(x)\rvert}{\lvert B_{r}\rvert}\leqslant Mf(x)\leqslant\alpha.

Hence, (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) imply

\displaystyle\lvert D_{\alpha}\rvert

\displaystyle\geqslant\lvert\{x\in B_{R/4}\text{ s.t. }Mf(x)\leqslant\alpha\}\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert B_{R/4}\rvert-\lvert\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:Mf(x)>\alpha\}\rvert

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-4^{d}C\vartheta)\lvert B_{R/4}\rvert.

Choosing
\vartheta=\frac{1}{2}(4^{d}C)^{-1}
, we obtain the desired inequality.
∎

Next, we present a simple rearrangement inequality well-known to experts. The result in the full space is given in
[
14
, Lemma 6.1]
or on a set of finite
(d-1)
-Hausdorff measure in
[
3
, Lemma 3.2]
. We give the proof here for completeness, but it is identical to the one given in
[
3
, Lemma 3.2]
with the Lebesgue measure replacing the Hausdorff measure.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open with
\lvert\Omega\rvert<+\infty
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
E
be a Borel set. If
\varrho>0
is such that

(3.5)

\lvert\Omega\cap B_{\varrho}(x)\rvert=\lvert\Omega\cap E\rvert

then

\int_{\Omega\setminus E}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}\geqslant\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varrho}(x)}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}.

Proof.

By (
3.5
),

\displaystyle\lvert\Omega\cap B_{\varrho}(x)\cap E\rvert+\lvert(\Omega\cap B_{\varrho}(x))\setminus E\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\Omega\cap B_{\varrho}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\Omega\cap E\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\Omega\cap E\cap B_{\varrho}(x)\rvert+\lvert(\Omega\cap E)\setminus B_{\varrho}(x)\rvert,

so

\lvert(\Omega\cap B_{\varrho}(x))\setminus E\rvert=\lvert(\Omega\cap E)\setminus B_{\varrho}(x)\rvert.

Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus E}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}

\displaystyle=\int_{(\Omega\setminus E)\cap B_{\varrho}(x)}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus(E\cup B_{\varrho}(x))}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\varrho^{-d-s}\lvert(\Omega\cap B_{\varrho}(x))\setminus E\rvert+\int_{\Omega\setminus(E\cup B_{\varrho}(x))}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}

\displaystyle=\varrho^{-d-s}\lvert(\Omega\cap E)\setminus B_{\varrho}(x)\rvert+\int_{\Omega\setminus(E\cup B_{\varrho}(x))}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{(\Omega\cap E)\setminus B_{\varrho}(x)}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus(E\cup B_{\varrho}(x))}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varrho}(x)}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}},

as required.
∎

In our final lemma of this section, we prove that if
E
has small measure around a point in its boundary with an exterior touching ball then its nonlocal curvature must be large.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
M\geqslant 0
,
K
satisfy (
1.8
)–(
1.9
), and
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set. Suppose that
B=B_{r}(x_{0})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus E
and
x\in\partial E\cap\partial B
. Then, there exists
\beta>0
such that if

(3.6)

\lvert E\cap B_{2r}(x_{0})\rvert\leqslant\beta\lvert B_{2r}(x_{0})\rvert

then
\curv_{K,E}(x)\geqslant(M+1)r^{-s}
. The constant
\beta>0
depends only on
d
,
s
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
, and
M
.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
2.3
that

\curv_{K,E}(x)=-\curv_{K,B}(x)+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(E\cup B)}K(x-y)\,dy

By Lemma
2.1
, it follows that

\curv_{K,E}(x)\geqslant-C_{1}r^{-s}+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(E\cup B)}K(x-y)\,dy

for some
C_{1}>0
which depends only on
d
,
s
and
\Lambda
. We claim that, assuming (
3.6
) for some
\beta>0
implies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(E\cup B)}K(x-y)\,dy\geqslant C_{2}r^{-s}\bigl(C_{3}\beta^{-s/d}-1\bigr)

for some
C_{2},C_{3}>0
which depend only on
d
,
s
and
\lambda
. Once we have established the claim, we obtain

\curv_{K,E}(x)\geqslant r^{-s}\bigl(-C_{1}+C_{2}(C_{3}\beta^{-s/d}-1)\bigr)=(M+1)r^{-s}

by choosing

\beta=\biggl(\frac{C_{2}C_{3}}{M+1+C_{1}+C_{2}}\biggr)^{d/s}.

To prove the claim, we use that

\displaystyle I:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(E\cup B)}K(x-y)\,dy

\displaystyle\geqslant\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(E\cup B)}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\lambda\int_{\bigl(B_{r}(x)\setminus B\bigr)\setminus E}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}

Next, if
\varrho\in(0,r)
is such that

(3.7)

\bigl\lvert\bigl(B_{r}(x)\setminus B\bigr)\cap B_{\varrho}(x)\bigr\rvert=\bigl\lvert\bigl(B_{r}(x)\setminus B\bigr)\cap E\bigr\rvert

then by Lemma
3.2
with
\Omega=B_{r}(x)\setminus B
, we have

I\geqslant\lambda\int_{\bigl(B_{r}(x)\setminus B\bigr)\setminus B_{\varrho}(x)}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}.

Moreover, since
B_{r}(x)\setminus B
contains a (solid) hemisphere of
B_{r}(x)
, it further follows that

(3.8)

I\geqslant\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{\varrho}(x)}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}=C_{4}\bigl(\varrho^{-s}-r^{-s}\bigr),

with
C_{4}>0
depending only on
d
,
s
and
\lambda
. Next, since
E\cap B=\varnothing
and
B_{r}(x)\subset B_{2r}(x_{0})
, it follows from (
3.7
) and (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\lvert B_{\varrho}(x)\setminus B\rvert

\displaystyle=\bigl\lvert\bigl(B_{r}(x)\setminus B\bigr)\cap B_{\varrho}(x)\bigr\rvert

\displaystyle=\bigl\lvert\bigl(B_{r}(x)\setminus B\bigr)\cap E\bigr\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert B_{r}(x)\cap E\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lvert B_{2r}(x_{0})\cap E\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\beta\lvert B_{2r}(x_{0})\rvert

\displaystyle=\beta\lvert B_{1}\rvert(2r)^{d}.

On the other hand,
B_{\varrho}(x)\setminus B
contains a (solid) hemisphere of
B_{\varrho}(x)
, and so

\lvert B_{\varrho}(x)\setminus B\rvert\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert B_{\varrho}(x)\rvert=\tfrac{1}{2}\varrho^{d}\lvert B_{1}\rvert.

This leads to
\tfrac{1}{2}\varrho^{d}\leqslant\beta(2r)^{d}
, so
\varrho\leqslant 2r(2\beta)^{1/d}
. This and (
3.8
) proves that

I\geqslant C_{4}\bigl(2^{-s}(2\beta)^{-s/d}-1\bigr)r^{-s},

which is exactly the claim.
∎

We are now in a position to give the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that

\lvert E\cap B_{R}\rvert<\delta\lvert B_{R}\rvert

for some small
\delta>0
, and fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
, both to be chosen later. Let

D_{\alpha}=\{x\in B_{R/4}\text{ s.t. }\lvert E\cap B_{r}(x)\rvert\leqslant\alpha\lvert B_{r}\rvert\text{ for all }r\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}R]\}

as in (
3.2
). By Lemma
3.1
, there exists
\vartheta>0
depending only on
d
such that if
\delta\leqslant\vartheta\alpha
, then

(3.9)

\lvert D_{\alpha}\rvert\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\lvert B_{R/4}\rvert>0.

We claim that if
\alpha
is less than some constant depending only on
d
,
s
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
and
M
then

(3.10)

D_{\alpha}\subset\overline{E}.

To prove the claim, suppose there exists
x_{0}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{E})\cap D_{\alpha}
and let
r>0
be the supremum over radii
\varrho>0
such that
B_{\varrho}(x_{0})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus E
. Then, there exists
x\in\partial E\cap\partial B_{r}(x_{0})
. Furthermore, since
0\in\partial E\cap B_{R/4}(x_{0})
, we have that

and

. Since

and

, we have that

\lvert E\cap B_{2r}(x_{0})\rvert\leqslant\alpha\lvert B_{2r}(x_{0})\rvert.

Hence, by Lemma
3.3
, there exists a constant
\beta>0
which depends only on

,

,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
and

, such that if

, then
\curv_{K,E}(x)\geqslant(M+1)r^{-s}
, contradicting that
\curv_{K,E}\leqslant Mr^{-s}
in

in the viscosity sense.

We can now specify the values of
\alpha
and
\delta
: we choose
\alpha=\beta
and
\delta=\vartheta\alpha
. To complete the proof, observe that
\alpha<1
, and so

contains no interior point of

. It follows from (
3.10
) that
D_{\alpha}\subset\partial E
, and (
3.9
) shows that the boundary of

contains a subset

of positive measure. This contradicts the assumption that
\partial E
has zero Lebesgue measure.
∎

For completeness, we give the proof of Corollary
1.3
which is a simple application of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

Since
B_{r/2}\subset B_{R}
, we have that

satisfies

in

. Hence, the result follows from Theorem (

) with

replaced with

and

.
∎

We also give the proof of Corollary
1.5
:

.

Since
\curv_{K,E}=0
in

in the viscosity sense, we also have
\curv_{K,\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus E}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the viscosity sense, so Corollary
1.4
applied to both

and
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus E
yields

\min\{\lvert E\cap B_{r}\rvert,\lvert B_{r}\setminus E\rvert\}\geqslant C_{1}\lvert B_{r}\rvert\qquad\text{for all }r>0,

where

is a constant that depends only on

,

,

and

. For

, we conclude the result using the relative isoperimetric inequality (

) see
[

, Proposition 12.37]
. For

, in the proof of

, they establish

\int_{B_{r}}\biggl\lvert u(x)-\frac{1}{\lvert B_{r}\rvert}\int_{B_{r}}u(y)\,dy\biggr\rvert\,dx\leqslant C_{2}r^{\alpha}\int_{B_{r}}\int_{B_{r}}\frac{\lvert u(x)-u(y)\rvert}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{n+\alpha}}\,dy\,dx

for all

that depends only on

and

. In particular, if

then

by (

where

depends only on

,

,

,

and

.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
1.6
. It is very similar to the proof of Theorem
1.2
, but requires two extra lemmas which strengthen Lemma
3.3
. They show that if a set
E
is sparse at
x
, then the nonlocal mean curvature of
E
is infinite at
x
. The first lemma is as follows:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
K
satisfy (
1.8
)–(
1.9
),
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set, and
x\in\partial E
. Then, there exist
C,\gamma>0
such that if

(4.1)

\lvert E\cap B_{r}(x)\rvert\leqslant\gamma\lvert B_{r}\rvert,

then

\int_{B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy\geqslant\frac{C}{r^{s}}\,.

The constants
\gamma
and
C
depend only on
d
,
s
,
\lambda
, and
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Since
\tilde{\chi}_{E}=1-2\chi_{E}
, we have

\int_{B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy=\int_{B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)}K(x-y)\,dy-2\int_{\bigl(B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)\bigr)\cap E}K(x-y)\,dy

We can estimate the former integral on the right-hand side by:

\int_{B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)}K(x-y)\,dy\geqslant\lambda\int_{B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}=\frac{\lambda d\lvert B_{1}\rvert}{s}\,\biggl(\frac{2^{s}}{r^{s}}-\frac{1}{r^{s}}\biggr)=\frac{C_{1}}{r^{s}},

where
C_{1}>0
depends only on
d
,
s
and
\lambda
. In the latter one, we estimate the integrand by
(r/2)^{-d-s}
and use the assumption (
4.1
) to obtain:

\int_{\bigl(B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)\bigr)\cap E}K(x-y)\,dy\leqslant\Lambda\int_{\bigl(B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)\bigr)\cap E}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}\leqslant\frac{\Lambda\lvert E\cap B_{r}(x)\rvert}{(r/2)^{d+s}}\leqslant\frac{C_{2}\gamma}{r^{s}},

with
C_{2}>0
depending only on
d
,
s
and
\Lambda
. It follows that

\int_{B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy\geqslant\frac{C_{1}-C_{2}\gamma}{r^{s}}

Choosing
\gamma=C_{1}/(2C_{2})
, we obtain

\int_{B_{r}(x)\setminus B_{r/2}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy\geqslant\frac{C_{1}}{2r^{s}},

as desired.
∎

The second lemma is as follows:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
K
satisfy (
1.8
)–(
1.9
),
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set,
x\in\partial E
, and
r_{0}>0
. Then there exists
\gamma>0
such that if

\lvert E\cap B_{r}(x)\rvert\leqslant\gamma\lvert B_{r}\rvert\qquad\text{for all }r\in(0,r_{0}]

then
\curv_{K,E}(x)=+\infty
. The constant
\gamma
depends only on
d
,
s
,
\lambda
, and
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Let
C,\gamma>0
be as in Lemma
4.1
. For every
r\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}r_{0}]
there is an integer
k\geqslant 1
such that
r\in(2^{-k-1}r_{0},2^{-k}r_{0}]
. If we denote
\tilde{B}_{j}=B_{2^{j}r}(x)
, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{r}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy=\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\tilde{B}_{k}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{\tilde{B}_{j}\setminus\tilde{B}_{j-1}}\biggr)\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy.

Since
2^{j}r\in(0,r_{0}]
for all
j=1,\ldots,k
, Lemma
4.1
applies to the latter integrals on the right-hand side:

\int_{\tilde{B}_{j}\setminus\tilde{B}_{j-1}}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy\geqslant\frac{C}{(2^{j}r)^{s}}.

In the former one, we estimate
\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)\geqslant-1
to obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\tilde{B}_{k}}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy\geqslant-\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\tilde{B}_{k}(x)}\frac{dy}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{d+s}}=-\frac{\Lambda d\lvert B_{1}\rvert}{s(2^{k}r)^{s}}\geqslant-\frac{2^{s}\Lambda d\lvert B_{1}\rvert}{sr_{0}^{s}}=-\frac{C_{1}}{r_{0}},

where
C_{1}>0
depends only on
d
,
s
and
\Lambda
. It follows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{r}(x)}\tilde{\chi}_{E}(y)K(x-y)\,dy\geqslant-\frac{C_{1}}{r_{0}^{s}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{C}{(2^{j}r)^{s}}\geqslant-\frac{C_{1}}{r_{0}^{s}}+2^{-s}Cr^{-s};

in the last inequality, we discarded all terms except
j=1
. Sending
r\to 0^{+}
, we obtain

\curv_{K,E}(x)=+\infty,

as desired.
∎

Finally, we give the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

We argue exactly as in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, but rather than
\alpha=\beta
, we choose
\alpha=\min\{\beta,\gamma\}
, with
\beta
defined in the proof of Theorem
1.2
(and coming from Lemma
3.3
) and
\gamma
the constant of Lemma
4.2
. The set
D_{\alpha}
is as in (
3.2
), and the proof of Theorem
1.2
shows that
D_{\alpha}\subset\partial E
. But then, for any
x\in D_{\alpha}
, the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
are satisfied with
r_{0}=\frac{1}{2}R
, so
\curv_{K,E}(x)=+\infty
. Hence,

D_{\alpha}\subset\{x\in\partial E\cap B_{R/4}\text{ s.t. }\curv_{K,E}(x)=+\infty\}

which completes the proof using that
D_{\alpha}
has positive Lebesgue measure.
∎
