Terpene synthases screening in agarwood tree Aquilaria yunnanensis leads to the discovery of agarospirol
Authors: Shun Wen, Weijia Cheng, Yi Li, Liying Er, Meiling Wu, Zixin Deng, Li Lu, Tiangang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgarwood, renowned as one of the most valuable natural materials in the world of fragrance, is notoriously difficult and time-consuming to obtain, creating a critical bottleneck for its utilization and conservation. Sesquiterpenes are the key constituents of agarwood, with agarospirol recognized as one of its characteristic compounds. Nevertheless, the biosynthesis of agarospirol remains unreported. Here, we conducted a functional screening of terpene synthase (TPS) genes from the agarwood-producing tree Aquilaria yunnanensis. Using a heterologous expression system in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, we characterized 26 TPS genes, identifying 30 structurally diverse sesquiterpene products. Among them, AyTPS1 was identified as an agarospirol-producing sesquiterpene synthase, and the structure of the enzymatically produced agarospirol was unequivocally confirmed by NMR spectroscopy. Furthermore, model-guided mutagenesis of AyTPS1 revealed that residues L283 and G435 markedly influence the distribution of agarospirol and related eudesmane-type sesquiterpenes, suggesting their involvement in shaping the active-site environment for spiro-ring formation and hydroxylated product formation. The elucidation of the AyTPS1 and its catalytic mechanism lays a crucial foundation for the industrial-scale biotechnological production of this valuable sesquiterpene.Keywords: Agarospirol, Sesquiterpene, Synthetic biology, Terpene synthase

Introduction

Agarwood is a resinous heartwood formed in trees of the genus Aquilaria. Natural agarwood formation is induced by environmental or biotic stresses, including lightning strikes, insect infestation, disease, wind damage, breakage, or microbial invasion [1,2]. Due to its rarity and complex formation process, agarwood is highly valued. Its characteristic aroma arises primarily from low-molecular-weight aromatic compounds and terpenoids [3,4], with sesquiterpenes serving as the principal contributors to its distinctive fragrance [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]].

More than 200 sesquiterpenes and their derivatives have been reported in agarwood [[14], [15], [16]], encompassing major structural classes including eudesmanes, guaianes, agarospiranes, acoranes, prezizaanes, and humulanes [2]. Among these, agarospirol [17] is recognized as a characteristic aroma component [3,18], imparting a unique spicy, peppery, and woody note [19]. It was first isolated by Bhattacharyya et al. from the essential oil of Aquilaria malaccensis [20], and was later obtained from Aquilaria sinensis by Yang Junshan et al. In 2006, Atsuo Nakazaki et al. achieved the chemical synthesis of chiral agarospirol in nine steps with an overall yield of 8%, although starting from an expensive chiral precursor [21]. More recently, Sudhakar Athe et al. reported a 12-step chemical synthesis yielding only 0.7% overall [22]. Despite these synthetic efforts, the structural complexity of agarospirol, which contains multiple chiral centers, renders its chemical synthesis low-yielding and stereochemically challenging, thereby hindering large-scale industrial production. Therefore, elucidating the biosynthetic pathway of agarospirol in Aquilaria trees and developing heterologous biosynthesis platforms represent a compelling alternative approach.

In the genus Aquilaria, significant progress has been made in elucidating the terpene synthase gene family. A genome-wide survey in A. agallochum identified 96 TPS candidates, of which 39 were full-length, and classified them into six subfamilies (TPS-a, TPS-b, TPS-c, TPS-e, TPS-f, and TPS-g); cis-regulatory analysis and in silico expression profiling further confirmed their responsiveness to stress and hormonal stimuli [23]. Similarly, 26 AsTPS genes (AsTPS1–AsTPS26) were identified in A. sinensis and categorized into five subgroups [24]. Although several sesquiterpene synthases from the genus Aquilaria have been characterized [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]], the specific synthase responsible for agarospirol production remains unidentified. In this study, we systematically characterized 26 terpene synthases from Aquilaria yunnanensis using a yeast expression system. Through functional screening, we identified a synthase (AyTPS1) responsible for agarospirol production. Following the confirmation of the product's chemical structure by NMR, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the catalytic mechanism of this key enzyme.

Material and methods

Plasmids and strains. We relied on the Aquilaria yunnanensis genome sequence from the National Genome Data Center under the accession number PRJNA1008918 [30] to commence our investigation. The S. cerevisiae strain JCR27, previously characterized in prior studies [31,32], served as the beginning strain for all genetic constructs in this research. Primers used for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification were synthesized by GeneCreate Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), and their sequences are detailed in Table S1. A comprehensive list of the plasmids and strains used is provided in Table S2. The TPS genes of Aquilaria yunnanensis were codon-optimized for S. cerevisiae, and were synthesized by GenScript Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). A modified lithium acetate (LiAc)/SS carrier DNA/polyethylene glycol (PEG) yeast transformation protocol, as reported previously [32,33], with engineered strains and plasmid details cataloged in Table S2. The shake-flask fermentations were performed as previously reported [32,34,35].

Extraction and analysis of terpenoids. The product was produced via small-scale shake-flask fermentation and analyzed as follows. Briefly, 20 mL of fermentation broth was cultivated in shake flasks for 72 h. After fermentation, the broth was centrifuged to harvest the cells, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 2 mL of sterile water. Subsequently, 1 mL of the resuspended cell suspension was mixed with 500 μL of ethyl acetate (EA) containing 50 ppm dodecane as an internal standard, followed by the addition of approximately 200 μL of glass beads. The mixture was vortexed for 10 min to disrupt the cells and extract the product. After high-speed centrifugation, the organic phase was collected and directly subjected to gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis. The analysis was performed using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatography (GC) system coupled with a 7000E Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (TQ-MS), equipped with a DB-5Q weak-polarity capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The GC-MS parameters were set as follows: injection volume of 1 μL per sample; temperature program starting at 60 °C (held for 1 min), ramped at 15 °C/min to 240 °C (held for 2 min), then increased at 10 °C/min to 280 °C (held for 4 min); injector temperature 240 °C; split ratio 30:1; mass selective detector transfer line temperature 280 °C; ion source temperature 280 °C; quadrupole temperature 150 °C; peak width 0.8 s; scanning mode MS1 scan; gain 1; mass scan range 45–500 m/z; scan time 200 ms. Compound identifications were achieved by matching retention times and diagnostic fragmentation patterns against authentic chemical standards or the NIST17 mass spectral reference database. For nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), chemical shifts (δ, ppm) were calibrated against CDCl3 (δH 7.26/δC 77.16) as internal references.

Isolation and purification of agarospirol in yeast. Following large-scale flask fermentation of agarospirol using S. cerevisiae at approximately 20 L, the cells were harvested and lysed via high-pressure homogenization. The resulting lysate underwent ethyl acetate extraction, whereupon the organic layer was isolated, concentrated via rotary evaporation under reduced pressure, and yielded crude extract. Subsequent purification employed silica gel column chromatography (500–800 mesh) with a stepwise polarity gradient (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate, 50:1–20:1, v/v). Eluted fractions were analyzed by thin-layer chromatography (TLC), pooled, and concentrated by rotary evaporation. The purity of the final product was verified by GC-MS and NMR.

Molecular docking. The AlphaFold2 [36] was used to predict the protein structures of AyTPS1 models. The Mg2+ coordination shell (including PPi group and three Mg2+ ions) was rebuilt based on terpene synthases (PDB 5IK0) with a similar conserved coordination motif. The farnesyl chain was manually modeled in PyMOL (http://www.pymol.org/pymol), carefully avoiding clashes with the residues in the active site cavity according to the method reported before [37]. Molecular docking was performed using the software Discovery Studio (version 19.1.0). The mutant models were constructed using the same methodology as described above.

Results

Identification and functional characterization of terpene synthases in agarwood tree Aquilaria yunnanensis

We analyzed the genome of Aquilaria yunnanensis [30], and identified 26 TPS genes containing the PF03936 and PF01397 domains. Phylogenetic analysis of their protein sequences revealed that 13 of these TPS belong to the previously reported TPS-a subfamily, nine to the TPS-b subfamily, and the remaining four include two TPS-g, one TPS-c, and one TPS-e subfamily genes (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 1). To functionally characterize these TPSs, each of the 26 genes was co-expressed with an overexpressed mevalonate (MVA) pathway in a previously engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain JCR27 [32], which supplies high levels of the terpenoid precursors dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP) and isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP). GC-MS analysis confirmed that ten TPSs were functional and produced sesquiterpenes, all preliminary identifications were based on matching retention times and mass spectra against the NIST library, with a total of 30 distinct sesquiterpene products identified (Table 1; GC and MS spectra provided in Supplementary Figs. 1–2). Notably, AyTPS15 primarily produced nerolidol as the major product, while AyTPS10, AyTPS12, and AyTPS19 predominantly yielded α-humulene. The remaining TPSs produced a diverse array of sesquiterpene structures.

Structural diversity and biosynthetic pathway of sesquiterpenes in Aquilaria yunnanensis

Consistent with previous chemical studies on Aquilaria yunnanensis, the TPSs identified in this species exhibit a remarkable capacity to generate structurally diverse sesquiterpenes. Among them, AyTPS1, AyTPS6, AyTPS16, AyTPS18, AyTPS22, and AyTPS26 were the most representative in terms of product diversity (Table 1, Fig. 2A and B). Systematic comparison of their respective products allowed us to delineate distinct catalytic mechanisms for each enzyme (Fig. 3).

Specifically, AyTPS6 primarily catalyzed the formation of linear sesquiterpenes. AyTPS16 and AyTPS26 yielded various 1,6-cyclized products that underwent subsequent modifications. AyTPS22 generated a 5-7 bicyclic skeleton, which is presumably formed via an initial 1,10-cyclization to generate a germacradienyl-type cation, followed by a 6,2-cyclization. In the case of AyTPS18, one subset of products arose from the bisabolyl-type cation through sequential cyclizations: first a 10,6-cyclization, then an 11,3-cyclization, to form the polycyclic compound P12. Another subset derived from the germacradienyl cation via 11,6,2-cyclization, leading to the complex polycyclic structure P10.

Notably, the AyTPS1 enzyme catalyzed the formation of an eudesmane-type skeleton through a 1,10-cyclization to generate a germacradienyl cation, followed by a 7,2-cyclization. Furthermore, Peak 3 was putatively identified as agarospirol, a signature aromatic compound characteristic of high-quality agarwood.

Structural elucidation and biosynthetic mechanism of agarospirol

Product P3 from the AyTPS1 enzyme was preliminarily identified as agarospirol by comparison with the NIST library database. To unambiguously confirm its structure, we performed large-scale fermentation, purified the compound by column chromatography, and conducted comprehensive NMR analysis. Given its multiple chiral centers and potential stereoisomers, the NMR data were carefully interpreted. The 1H and 13C NMR spectra showed strong consistency with previously reported values for agarospirol (see Table 2) [22]. The HMBC and HSQC spectrum confirmed the planar structure as agarospirol were critical for establishing its relative configuration (Fig. 4A). The specific optical rotation was determined to be [α]D25 = −4.6 (c = 1, CHCl3), high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry calcd for C15H26OK: 261.1621, found: 261.1618. Collectively, these data conclusively identify P3 as (−)-agarospirol, confirming that the AyTPS1 TPS can produce this characteristic sesquiterpenoid in a heterologous microbial system.

Based on the identification of three additional products from AyTPS1 (elemol, γ-eudesmol and β-eudesmol), we propose a plausible biosynthetic pathway for agarospirol (Fig. 4B). The catalytic cycle initiates with the ionization of farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) to generate the farnesyl cation, which undergoes 1,10-cyclization to form the key germacradienyl intermediate B. This intermediate is subsequently captured by a water molecule, resulting in hydroxylation to form intermediate C. A second cyclization (2,7-cyclization) then occurs, producing the eudesmane-type cationic intermediate D. This carbocation intermediate bifurcates along two potential pathways: it may either undergo direct deprotonation to yield the observed co-products (P2/P4, identified as γ-eudesmol and β-eudesmol) or proceed through a more complex rearrangement involving migration of the C-8 carbon to C-2. This shift forms a new spirocyclic carbocation intermediate, which upon final deprotonation furnishes the target product, agarospirol.

Identification and functional analysis of key catalytic residues in AyTPS1

Having identified the enzyme capable of synthesizing agarospirol. and proposed its biosynthetic pathway, we sought to enhance the production efficiency of this valuable compound through rational enzyme engineering, as its proportion in the total sesquiterpene profile remained suboptimal. Our mechanistic proposal identified the C-8 to C-2 carbon migration of intermediate D as the critical branching point toward agarospirol formation. To pinpoint residues governing this specificity, the AyTPS1 structure was modeled using AlphaFold2, and the FPP substrate was docked into its active site. Rather than arbitrarily selecting residues based solely on the structural model, we employed a dual-filter strategy. First, residues located within the substrate-binding pocket and spatially proximal to both the substrate and the catalytic region were identified from the AlphaFold2-predicted structure. Second, these active-site candidates were cross-validated through homology comparison with two sesquiterpene synthases that exhibit high similarity in catalytic function and product architecture: maize eudesmanediol synthase ZmEDS [38] and tobacco 5-epi-aristolochene synthase TEAS [39]. This integrative analysis nominated two residues, L283 and G435, as candidate determinants potentially contributing to catalytic specificity within the active pocket (Fig. 5A and Fig. S3). A systematic mutagenesis approach was therefore employed, focusing on side-chain hydrophobicity and steric bulk.

(B, C) GC profiles of sesquiterpene products from the L283 mutant enzyme (B) and the G435 mutant enzyme (C). (D) Proposed model for catalytic reprogramming leading to distinct sesquiterpene profiles.

For L283, we probed steric effects by substituting with Ala, Pro, Trp, and Phe, spanning a range of sizes, and tested hydrophobicity requirements by comparing Tyr to Asp (Fig. 5B). The L283F mutant completely abolished agarospirol production, instead generating eudesmol analogs, indicating this mutation sterically hinders the requisite C-8 migration, diverting intermediate D toward simple deprotonation. Catalytic activity was entirely lost in the L283A, L283P, and L283D mutants, while L283Y and L283W produced less diverse profiles than L283F. These results suggest that the side-chain properties at position 283 influence the AyTPS1 product profile, likely by altering active-site packing around the carbocation intermediates.

For G435, we hypothesized that it modulates active site accessibility, particularly for the water molecule involved in the initial hydroxylation. Mutants were designed to test steric effects (Ser, Thr, Tyr) and chemical environment (Asp, Ala, Val) (Fig. 5C). The G435T mutant nearly eliminated oxygenated sesquiterpenes, yielding hydrocarbon skeletons instead, confirming its role in the hydroxylation. Notably, the G435S mutation, featuring a smaller hydroxyl-containing side chain, increased the relative abundance of agarospirol. In contrast, the bulkier Tyr or the acidic Asp abolished downstream products beyond intermediate C. The G435V mutant, introducing steric bulk while retaining hydrophobicity, yielded a profile similar to G435T, whereas the less bulky G435A mutant produced more side products but retained agarospirol. This demonstrates that steric bulk at position 435 governs water access, and a side-chain hydroxyl is favorable for efficient agarospirol synthesis.

In summary, L283 is a critical steric gatekeeper for the agarospirol-forming rearrangement, while G435 serves as a regulator of water access for the prerequisite hydroxylation, thereby also influencing the subsequent cyclization cascade (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

While identifying AyTPS1 is an enzyme capable of synthesizing agarospirol., we noted a previously patented gene (CN113430218B) with reported agarospirol production, though its origin remains unverified. Despite sharing 66 amino acid differences with AyTPS1, this homolog appears to retain similar catalytic activity. Crucially, our study employs comprehensive structural elucidation, including 1H NMR, 13C NMR, 2D-NMR, high-resolution mass spectrometry, and optical rotation analysis, to unequivocally establish the absolute configuration and chemical identity of the enzymatically produced agarospirol. This rigorous characterization ensures precise functional annotation of the identified synthase and prevents potential misassignment common in terpenoid pathway discovery, thereby providing a reliable reference for future investigations.

In investigating the enzyme's catalytic mechanism, we identified two residues (L283 and G435) that exert pronounced effects on product specificity. This interpretation is supported by previous studies showing that active-site mutations can redirect sesquiterpene cyclization cascades: TEAS-Y520F shifts product formation from 5-epi-aristolochene to germacrene A [39], while ZmEDS-F303A predominantly releases hedycaryol [38], an intermediate related to eudesmane-type product formation. Notably, AyTPS1-L283 corresponds to ZmEDS-F299, a residue implicated in hedycaryol positioning. However, single-point mutations at these positions failed to yield appreciable improvements in catalytic efficiency or to increase the proportion of agarospirol in the total sesquiterpene profile, remaining below the threshold required for industrial application. This suggests that the inherent catalytic constraints cannot be overcome by isolated mutations. Future efforts should leverage emerging strategies-including rational design informed by advanced structural analysis and machine learning-driven protein engineering-to systematically optimize enzyme performance [40]. Ultimately, integrating high-performance variants with systems metabolic engineering will be essential for developing efficient microbial platforms toward economically viable agarospirol bioproduction.

GC-MS analysis revealed enzymatic activity in ten TPSs, yielding a total of 30 putative sesquiterpene products. These compounds were preliminarily assigned by matching their retention times and mass spectra against the NIST library. Such database matching, while reasonably indicative, is not entirely definitive on its own. Given that terpenoids frequently occur as structural and/or stereoisomers that cannot be unambiguously distinguished by GC-MS, these preliminary annotations should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Target compounds of particular interest will be purified and subjected to NMR-based structural elucidation for unambiguous identification in subsequent studies. However, it should be noted that our heterologous expression strategy cannot definitively demonstrate the native function of these enzymes in planta. Nevertheless, this approach is sufficient to confirm that the enzymes possess catalytic activity for converting FPP into sesquiterpene products, which fully satisfies the objectives of this study.

Although we have identified multiple sesquiterpene skeletons in this study, these core structures likely undergo further modification in planta or during agarwood formation—potentially through the action of cytochromes P450, acetyltransferases, and glycosyltransferases—to generate the chemical diversity characteristic of natural agarwood. Exploring the combinatorial biosynthesis of these modifying enzymes represents a compelling strategy for accessing novel structurally diverse sesquiterpenoid derivatives. Such efforts would not only expand the chemical space of agarwood-related compounds but also further unlock the value of this precious natural resource through synthetic biology approaches.

Comparative analysis of sesquiterpene biosynthesis across Aquilaria species further contextualizes our findings. Sesquiterpene biosynthesis has been extensively investigated in A. sinensis, where multiple TPSs have been characterized, including ASS1-ASS4 (δ-guaiene synthases), AsTPS2/TPS3/TPS10 (α-humulene synthases), TPS9/TPS12 (multifunctional enzymes), and AsTPS13/AsTPS14 (eudesmol/selinadiene synthases), collectively producing at least eight distinct skeletal classes [41]. Notably, agarospirol has been detected in wounded A. sinensis tissues [42], yet its specific synthase remains unidentified. In A. malaccensis, AmDG2 was reported as an α-humulene synthase with Asp307 and Asp311 as critical catalytic residues within the conserved DDxxD/E motif [43].

In conclusion, our strategy successfully provides an approach utilizing heterologous expression for the characterization and identification of agarospirol, offering the potential for subsequent production and demonstrating the advantages of the heterologous expression strategy. The integrated approach of gene mining and functional characterization can be readily extended to the discovery and sustainable exploitation of other valuable natural products, thereby offering a viable path to alleviate harvesting pressure on natural resources and contribute to broader ecological conservation efforts.
