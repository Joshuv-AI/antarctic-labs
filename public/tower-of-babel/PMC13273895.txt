Progresses of transposon research in Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Authors: Wenbo Lin, Yu Lv, Yuhao Zhang, Zhaoxia Jin, Linxia Liu, Dawei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transposons are mobile genetic elements that have attracted sustained scientific interest since their discovery in maize, evolving into indispensable tools in genetic research. Saccharomyces cerevisiae serves as a preferred model for transposon studies, benefiting from a mature genetic manipulation system, a well-characterized genome, and cellular mechanisms that resemble those of higher organisms. This review summarizes recent advances and application strategies of transposons in S. cerevisiae. We begin by classifying transposons based on their transposition mechanisms, with a focus on representative systems including the Tn, hAT, Ty, piggyBac (PB), Tc1/mariner and CRISPR-associated transposon (CAST) families, detailing their molecular mechanisms. We further discuss the applications of endogenous and engineered transposons in yeast, address key challenges such as insertion bias and genomic instability, and summarize corresponding engineering strategies aimed at enhancing their efficiency and specificity. Our goal is to provide a roadmap for developing transposons into efficient, precise, and low-off-target genome-editing tools, thereby advancing the construction of yeast cell factories.

Introduction

Transposons, also known as Transposable elements (TEs), were first discovered in maize during the 1950s. Barbara McClintock termed them ‘unstable gene’. To date, TEs have been identified in numerous organisms [1]. Transposons, as mobile DNA elements widely present in eukaryotes, facilitate the evolution of diverse host traits through their unique mobility mechanisms. They also offer greater potential for expanding gene editing approaches. In fact, transposons are widely employed as regulatory elements governing gene expression, such as promoters, enhancers, or chromatin boundaries for gene regulation [2]. Notably, compared to other genetic manipulation techniques, transposons offer the following advantages: it enables rapid, single-step generation of large numbers of mutants across parallel strains, circumvent the issues of low transformation efficiency and unintended genomic alterations associated with DNA transformation, and achieve higher insertion efficiency than host-mediated homologous recombination (HR) or non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) [[3], [4], [5]].

S. cerevisiae serves as a model yeast organism due to its mature genetic toolkit, suitability for screening, and conservation with higher organisms, making it the preferred model for diverse genetic studies and metabolic engineering [6,7]. Although transposons in S. cerevisiae are less abundant than in plants, they remain a significant component of S. cerevisiae genomes (3% of the whole genome) [8]. However, the utility of endogenous transposons in yeast is hindered by their inherently low transposition activity and efficiency. This limitation is largely attributable to epigenetic silencing mechanisms, the low catalytic activity of native transposases, and post-translational auto-regulation. Consequently, to overcome these constraints, researchers have engineered enhanced transposition systems by harnessing exogenous transposons from diverse sources and developing hyperactive mutant variants.

This review systematically reviews the development and application of engineered transposon systems in S. cerevisiae. It outlines the functional mechanisms and structural features of key transposon families, including the Tn, hAT, Ty, PB, and Tc1/mariner families (Table 1 and Fig. 1). Classification of transposons and transposition mechanisms

Transposons can be divided into two major classes based on their mechanism of transposition. Class I elements are retrotransposons; they encode a reverse transcriptase (RT) and utilize an RNA intermediate in the transposition cycle. Also known as retrotransposons, they mobilize through a “copy-and-paste” mechanism whereby an RNA intermediate is reverse-transcribed into a cDNA copy that is integrated elsewhere in the genome. Class II elements are DNA transposons. They encode a transposase (TPase) that does not employ any RNA intermediates and operates entirely at the DNA level. These elements are mobilized via a DNA intermediate, typically through a “cut-and-paste” mechanism [9].

Except two major classifications, the various taxonomic groups of eukaryotic TEs may be further subdivided into subcategories based on their chromosomal integration mechanisms. In addition, different transposases also have their own distinct mechanisms. This led to the emergence of transposon classification methods based on catalytic domains. Such as the RNase H transposase (DD(E/D) enzyme), HUH single-stranded DNA transposase, serine transposase and tyrosine transposase. Indeed, transposons can also be subdivided into (sub)families, superfamilies, subclasses and classes [8]. Therefore, this study focuses only on the classification of transposons in S. cerevisiae, and this classification is limited to the superfamily level. The classification of transposon superfamilies is primarily based on structural features, transposition mechanisms, sequence analysis, and phylogeny [8,10].

Transposon preference refers to the specific position preference of transposons when they insert into the genome. At present, the preferences of all transposons applied to S. cerevisiae are listed in Table 1. With the deepening of research into transposon mechanisms, it has been discovered that beyond their sequence-specific preferences, transposons also exhibit spatial insertion preferences. For instance, the endogenous Ty5 transposon preferentially integrates into regions of silent chromatin at the telomeres and silent mating loci, whereas Hermes transposon insertions in yeast are associated with nucleosome-free regions [[11], [12], [13]]. These results indicate that transposition of transposons was regulated by multiple factors.

Tn transposon family

The Tn family belongs to bacterial transposons, which are class II elements that use DNA as an intermediate. As the earliest transposon applied to S. cerevisiae, the original Tn3 transposon is 4957 bp in full length, and it encodes three proteins: 1. transposase (1015 amino acids), encoded by the tnpA gene. 2. Tn3-specific repressor (185 amino acids), encoded by the tnpR gene. 3. β-lactamase (288 amino acids). The Tn3 transposon has 38 bp inverted repeats at its ends [35]. Tn5 transposon is 5818 bp in length. Its central region carries genes conferring resistance to neomycin, bleomycin and streptomycin, flanked by two inversely oriented IS50 elements. These two highly homologous IS50 sequences are positioned at each end of the antibiotic resistance gene cluster: IS50R encodes a fully functional transposase (Tnp) and its N-terminally truncated inhibitor (Inh), whereas IS50L produces C-terminally truncated, inactive variants of both Tnp and Inh. Each IS50 element is defined by two 19 bp terminal sequences, designated the outside end (OE) and the inside end (IE), which serve as essential binding sites for the transposase [36].

Tn7 transposon is 14 kb in length. Its transposition is directed by sequences located at each end: an approximately 150 bp essential region at the left end and a 90 bp region at the right end. Both termini contain a series of 22 bp transposase-binding sites, with three sites in the left end (Tn7-L) and four in the right end (Tn7-R). Tn7 encodes five proteins required for transposition: TnsA, TnsB, TnsC, TnsD, and TnsE. TnsA and TnsB together form the transposase, which specifically recognizes the transposon ends. TnsC is an ATPase that binds duplex DNA non-specifically in the presence of ATP. TnsD and TnsE serve as alternative target-selecting proteins; they direct the TnsABC complex to catalyze the DNA breakage and joining reactions that excise Tn7 from donor DNA and integrate it into a target site. Notably, Tn7 has a unique, high-affinity chromosomal attachment site, attTn7, located downstream of the essential glmS gene. This gene encodes glucosamine-6-phosphate synthase, an enzyme involved in cell wall biosynthesis.

Tn10 is a composite bacterial transposon and was the first element demonstrated to transpose via a non-replicative “cut-and-paste” mechanism. It consists of two insertion sequences, IS10-R and IS10-L, arranged in inverse orientation and flanking internal genes. The entire transposon is 9147 bp in length and contains nine open reading frames (ORFs). Both IS10-R and IS10-L are 1329 bp long and exhibit high sequence similarity; however, only IS10-R carries a long, uninterrupted ORF (1206 bp) that encodes a functional transposase. Within the Tn10 architecture, the two ends of each IS10 element are designated as the “outside end” and “inside end” according to their relative position in the composite transposon. Both ends contain DNA determinants required for transposase binding. In addition, the outside end harbors a binding site for the bacterial integration host factor (IHF) [37]. Although bacterial transposons such as Tn transposons are widely used in Tn-seq and ATAC-seq approaches, there are no detailed data available regarding their transposition efficiency in S. cerevisiae [38].

hAT transposon family

The Activator/Dissociation (Ac/Ds) transposon was the first transposon found in 1951 [39]. The autonomous Ac element is approximately 4.6 kb in length and produces a single 3.8 kb transcript that spans most of the element, encoding an 807-amino-acid putative transposase. Insertion of Ac generates an 8 bp target site duplication (TSD) and is flanked by 11 bp terminal inverted repeats (TIRs). Furthermore, within approximately 200 bp of each terminus, multiple copies of a hexameric repeat sequence are present, which serve as specific binding sites for the Ac transposase [40]. Ds elements share TIRs with Ac and similarly generate an 8 bp TSD upon insertion. Three structurally distinct classes of Ds elements have been identified, all of which can transpose and induce mutations in the presence of Ac. Among them, Ds elements are simple internal-deletion derivatives of Ac, predominantly found in genetic stocks that recently harbored an active Ac element [31,41,42]. Ds1 elements are short (∼400 bp) and share with Ac only the 11 bp TIRs and a few of the subterminal hexameric repeats [43]. Ds2 elements exceed 1 kb in length and share extensive sequence homology with Ac within approximately 200 bp of each terminus. In most Ds2 elements, the majority of the internal Ac sequence has been replaced by unrelated “filler” DNA. Further studies indicate that the ability of Ds elements to induce chromosome breaks is directly linked to their structure: all such breakage-competent elements contain multiple transposon ends [[44], [45], [46]]. Ac prefers to insert into the site of hypomethylation, low copy or single copy sequence [47]. Ds prefer to insert near the donor site and can also be inserted into other chromosomes [48].

In 1994, Warren et al. created the family name hAT using an Ac transposon (from Z. mays) along with two similar Ac transposons, Tam3 (from Antirrhinum majus) and hobo (from Drosophila melanogaster) [49]. The Hermes transposon was found in Musca domestica. Hermes transposon is clearly a member of hAT transposon family. It is 2749 bp in length. Hermes termini are composed of 17 bp imperfect inverted repeats. The first ORF is located between positions 450 bp and 2285 bp. It encodes a protein of 612 amino acids which is 55% identical to the amino acid sequence of the hobo transposase. The Hermes transposase recognizes the consensus sequence NTNNNNAN [50]. Hermes transposon demonstrates high transposition efficiency and near-random insertion patterns, making it particularly suitable for large-scale functional genomics applications [51].

Ty transposon family

S. cerevisiae harbors members of the retrovirus-like Pseudoviridae (Ty1, Ty2, Ty4, Ty5) and Metaviridae (Ty3) families of long-terminal repeat (LTR)-retrotransposons [52]. Ty transposons, as naturally occurring TEs within S. cerevisiae, play a significant role in the evolutionary development of the S. cerevisiae genome. The Ty1 transposon is 5918 bp in length and carries a 334 bp direct repeat, known as the long terminal repeat (LTR), at each terminus. Similar to most LTR retrotransposons and retroviruses, the Ty1 LTR terminates in the dinucleotide inverted repeat 5′-TG…CA-3′ and contains three distinct domains—U3, R, and U5. These domains are defined by their relative positions in the major sense-strand transcript expressed from Ty1 DNA. The middle part is a functional sequence encoding two partially overlapping ORFs: a group-specific antigen (gag) gene and a polymerase (pol) gene. gag ORF encodes a protein with capsid and nucleic acid chaperone functions, while pol ORF encodes protease (Protease, PR), reverse transcriptase RNase H (Reverse transcriptase RNase H, RT-RH) and Integrase (IN) with catalytic function [53]. Although the copy number and sequence of Ty transposons in S. cerevisiae vary significantly, all five Ty transposons have a similar structure, resulting in similar insertion preferences: Ty1–4 tend to insert upstream of Pol III-transcribed tRNA genes, only Ty5 preferentially integrates into heterochromatic regions at telomeres and silent mating sites [13,[54], [55], [56], [57], [58], [59]]. Among endogenous elements, Ty retrotransposons show the most robust activity. In particular, Ty1 exhibits relatively high transposition frequencies (typically in the range of 3 × 10−7–1 × 10−5 events per cell per generation under inducing conditions) [60]. Other Ty elements, such as Ty2 and Ty4, display moderate activity, whereas Ty3 and Ty5 show lower apparent efficiencies due to strong insertion site preferences, targeting RNA polymerase III-transcribed genes and heterochromatic regions, respectively [54,61].

PiggyBac transposon family

The PB transposon was originally isolated from the cabbage looper moth (Trichoplusia ni) in the 1980s [62]. The PB transposon is a TTAA-specific, cut and paste DNA transposon [63]. The PB transposon is a 2475 bp autonomous mobile element. It has 13 bp TIRs located at both ends and 19 bp subterminal inverted repeats located 3 bp and 31 bp 5′ and 3′ internally from the TIRs, respectively. Between the subterminal inverted repeats, there is a single 1.8 kb open reading frame, which encodes the 594 amino acids PB transposase with a molecular weight of 64 kDa [64,65].

Tc1/mariner transposon family

The Sleeping Beauty (SB) transposon is a DNA transposon from the Salmo salar genome that is activated by eliminating the inactivating mutation in the transposase [66].

It's structure only a gene encoding the transposase (1600 bp) and is flanked by 230 bp TIRs, and its insertion preference is "TA" sequence. The SB transposon system has been extensively optimized for vertebrate and mammalian cells, with limited evidence of efficient transposition in S. cerevisiae [67]. Although it exhibits a measurable integration frequency of ∼2.1 × 10−5 integrations per cell in Pichia pastoris [67,68]. Other transposon systems, including Ac/Ds, and PB, generally show moderate efficiencies and may require optimization for effective use in S. cerevisiae.

Although several transposons described in this section originate from non-yeast organisms, they have been widely adapted, engineered, or functionally validated in S. cerevisiae, making them highly relevant to yeast genetic research and tool development.

The application of transposons in S. cerevisiae

Transposon insertion sequencing and functional genomics

The most common application of transposon engineering in yeast is the transposon insertion sequencing (TIS). Genome-wide genetic screening is a powerful tool to identify essential genes and the large-scale classification between essential and non-essential genes could help to elucidate the molecular underpinnings of many biological processes. Traditionally, identifying essential genes is labor-intensive. Compared with the bacterial genome of 2–6 Mb, the yeast genome (>12 Mb) and the greater complexity of eukaryotic genomes make gene classification considerably more difficult. TIS can be used in conjunction with inverse polymerase chain reaction (inverse PCR) and randomly broken fragment PCR (RBF-PCR) to amplify and sequence the transposon and adjacent DNA, thereby identifying the insertion site, and further expand gene function [69]. Seifert et al. first constructed a shuttle mutagenesis system using the mini-Tn3 transposon and successfully applied it in S. cerevisiae [70]. Furthermore, multiple derivative transposons carrying different selection markers and reporter genes were constructed for gene knockout and phenotypic screening [71,72]. This transposon system can also be used to construct translational fusion proteins for investigating gene expression regulation and subcellular localisation [70]. Based on this, Nancy Burns et al. constructed a genome-wide LacZ fusion gene library using mini-Tn3 transposons, which was used to study the gene expression of S. cerevisiae and provided ideas for the subsequent construction of transposon libraries [73]. TIS, coupled with deep sequencing, enables large-scale genome-wide mutant screens for genes essential in different growth conditions. Michel et al. developed the Saturated Transposon Analysis in Yeast (SATAY) method for functional mapping of yeast genomes using the Ac/Ds transposon system and high-throughput sequencing, the approach can efficiently identify essential genes, protein domains, genetic interactions, and drug targets, significantly enhancing the throughput and resolution of genomic research [74]. Subsequently, Kingma et al. conducted a systematic evaluation of the quantitative capabilities of the SATAY method, revealing that the average sequencing read length at insertion sites serves as a robust proxy for the fitness of gene disruption mutants, with high reproducibility across both biological and technical replicates. However, the study also indicated that increasing sequencing depth yields limited gains in fitness estimation accuracy, and that reducing the number of PCR amplification cycles or employing unbiased amplification methods may be more effective strategies for noise reduction [75]. Similarly, Vonesch et al. developed an efficient and cost-effective method for constructing TIS libraries based on Tn5 transposase mutants [76]. The primary technique for assessing chromatin accessibility across the genome is the Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq). This method utilizes the activity of a hyperactive Tn5 transposase, which preferentially inserts adapter-tagged 9 bp fragments into accessible genomic regions. These adapter-ligated regions, typically corresponding to nucleosome-depleted, euchromatic areas, are subsequently enriched and subjected to high-throughput sequencing [38]. For natural transposon in yeast, Lauermann et al. developed a Ty transposon-based integration tool that incorporates a 25-bp synthetic DNA tag, designated SSB, which is inserted 24 base pairs from the 5′ end of the 3′ LTR within its U3 region. This tag enables specific sequencing of newly transposed Ty1 elements without background interference from endogenous Ty1 copies [77]. Based on this, Mularoni et al. successfully constructed the insertion library of Ty1 and revealed the insertion preference of Ty1 to nucleosomes in S. cerevisiae [78].

Gene function identification is also an important application derived from TIS. The mutagenized genomic library constructed by transposons can be subjected to specific stress conditions through phenotypic screening. By comparing growth patterns across different strains, the insertion sites of transposons are identified, thereby establishing the relationship between the environment and genes. For example, osmotic stress and product toxicity have long posed challenges in microbial fermentation. Through screening a mTn3-mutagenized genomic library, Kim et al. successfully identified the genes TIS11 and SDS23 in S. cerevisiae, which are associated with osmotic stress, as well as the gene SAP1, linked to vanillin tolerance [79,80]. Moreover, the transport capacity of S. cerevisiae for xylose remains much lower than for glucose, despite the development of a modified xylose transporter and the identification of specific xylose transporters in S. cerevisiae [81]. Screening for and knocking out or inhibiting genes related to xylose growth inhibition could further improve the utilization of xylose by engineered S. cerevisiae. Ni et al. successfully screened the xylose-specific growth-inhibiting genes PHO13 and TAL1 from the S. cerevisiae L2612 transposon mutagenesis library [82]. Based on the corresponding transposons mutation library, genes associated with heavy metal, salt tolerance, ethanol tolerance, heat tolerance and freeze-thaw tolerance has also been reported [[83], [84], [85], [86]]. In the field of drug resistance, S. cerevisiae is closely related to the fungal pathogen Candida albicans. By constructing a drug-resistance (e.g., fluconazole) transposon mutation library in S. cerevisiae, we can further understand the drug-resistance mechanism in C. albicans [87]. Moreover, Retrotransposons are the most abundant components of virtually all known eukaryotic genomes [[88], [89], [90]]. Amplification with Inter-priming binding sites (IPBS) primers is based upon the universally present tRNA complement as a reverse transcriptase primer binding site (PBS) in long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons [91]. The IPBS retrotransposon marker system is an effective method for identifying yeast species. This system offers several advantages, including a high number of analyzable polymorphic variants, no requirement for prior sequence data, and low cost. Aydın et al. successfully identified 112 yeast species from the fermented food using IPBS marker system [92].

Transposon engineering

In different organisms and microorganisms, different transposons have different activities. To enhance the transposition activity to the level suitable for industrialization and clinical treatment, transposon engineering has emerged. As a model organism, S. cerevisiae has become the preferred organism for validating transposon engineering. Based on the structure of transposons, transposon engineering can be mainly divided into two strategies: transposase engineering and TIR engineering. Transposase engineering mainly involves rational design or random mutation of transposases to screen for highly efficient transposases, thereby enhancing their insertion/removal capabilities. PB transposon has become an attractive gene editing tool due to its large cargo capacity, lack of trace transposons and genotoxic potential, and its highly engineered structure [93,94]. Li et al. constructed a PB transposase mutation library in S. cerevisiae, by superimposing effective mutations, the excision and integration efficiencies of the PB transposase by 17-fold and 9-fold respectively [93]. Similarly, Lazaro et al. identified an ultra-active derivative of the Ac transposase, designated AcTPase4x, using a color-based reporter system in which Ds insertion into the ADE2 locus confers a red phenotype on adenine-deficient medium, while precise Ac-catalyzed excision restores ADE2 function and yields white colonies. Through multiple rounds of random mutagenesis and screening, AcTPase4x was isolated. This mutant catalyzes Ds excision in S. cerevisiae at a frequency that is 100-fold higher than that of the wild-type transposase, while maintaining an unchanged re-integration frequency of the excised Ds element (57%). Furthermore, AcTPase4x has lost the weak bias exhibited by the wild-type protein toward insertion sites with elevated guanine-cytosine content and non-random protein-DNA twist [95].

TIRs engineering is mainly based on the modification of the TIR or IS elements at both ends of the transposon to improve the specific recognition of the transposon. For example, by mutating the downstream sequence at the left end of the IS608 transposon to one complementary to the desired target DNA sequence, a novel specific interaction network was generated, enabling the transposon to recognize various complementary target sites of 12 nt and 17 nt in length [96]. However, the aforementioned TIR engineering has only been validated in vitro, and its efficacy requires further verification in vivo within the genomic context of different cellular backgrounds. Interestingly, an appropriate TIR length might be one of the reasons for maintaining transposition efficiency. When the TIR of Ty1 increased to 2140 bp, its transposition efficiency decreased to only 0.3% of that of the wild-type Ty1 [77].

Genome editing tools

Despite the poor target sequence specificity of transposons and the decrease in efficiency caused by an increase in the size of the inserted cargo, transposition efficiency is higher than that of HR and NHEJ [97]. However, some issues such as low transposition enzyme efficiency and transposition preference still remain. This renders transposons ineffective as a gene editing tool, preventing them from becoming an efficient and precise editing system. However, by combining transposons with other editing tools, these problems can be overcome. Li et al. developed a transposase capable of excision but lacking integration defects, they fused it with heterologous DNA-binding domains, such as zinc finger proteins, to achieve reversible, scar-free transgenic manipulation [98]. The CRISPR-Cas system is an increasingly popular genetic engineering tool for biological and therapeutic applications. Brizzee et al. employed the PB transposase in conjunction with the Cas-CLOVER system to achieve multi-copy genomic integration of an insecticidal shRNA expression cassette. They successfully constructed an engineered S. cerevisiae strain for the constitutive, high-yield production of mosquito-specific RNAi insecticides [99]. In addition, to avoid the decrease efficiency by big inserted cargo, Cre-loxP system can also be combined with the transposon system for marker rescue [100].

Throughout the entire life cycle of a cell, DNA damage must be confronted, with DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) being one of the most detrimental forms of damage. These can lead to apoptosis, harmful mutations, and an increased risk of carcinogenesis. Investigating DSB mechanisms necessitates tools capable of precisely controlling DNA damage [101]. Conventional approaches primarily induce DSBs via genotoxic agents (such as ionizing radiation, ultraviolet light, or chemical mutagens). While these methods achieve dose-dependent damage, they lack sequence specificity. Conversely, sequence-specific nucleases (e.g., endonucleases) offer precise targeting, yet their capacity for scalable regulation of break numbers remains limited [102]. Auboiron et al. utilized the repetitive nature of the Ty element in the yeast genome to develop a precise tool for DNA damage response based on the CRISPR-Cas system. By designing guide RNAs (gRNAs) to target the Ty sequence, they were able to precisely and dose-dependently induce DSBs throughout the genome [103]. By combining molecular tools (CRISPR-Cas9) with the inherent characteristics of the genome (repetitive transposons), it not only provides a tool for precisely controlling DNA damage and a repeatable DNA damage model, but also demonstrates the great potential of the Ty transposon in the yeast genome for studies on gene stability.

Metabolic engineering

Natural transposons in yeast have become a major research focus in recent years. With the rapid development of synthetic biology, transposons—owing to their high integration efficiency—have emerged as powerful tools for genomic integration. In S. cerevisiae, endogenous Ty transposons are widely utilized as platforms for multicopy gene integration. For instance, Ryosuke et al. employed the Ty1 transposon as an integration site to establish an efficient repeated cocktail δ-integration strategy [104], which increased carotenoid and β-carotene yields to 7.3 mg/L and 52.3 mg/L, respectively, after three rounds of integration. Based on this approach, Seguchi et al. developed a rational multicopy δ-integration strategy based on a consensus Ty1 LTR sequence with shortened homology arms, expanding the number of available genomic integration sites to 85 and enabling the one-step integration of 15 copies of the xylose isomerase gene, thereby facilitating efficient ethanol production from xylose [105]. Furthermore, Ty transposons have been applied to enhance the copy numbers of key genes involved in the biosynthesis of valuable compounds such as 7-dehydrocholesterol and mogroside V [106,107]. Recently, Gao et al. developed a Ty-based multiplex integration toolkit that enabled the stable insertion of multiple fluorescent protein genes as well as an 8.4 kb paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway gene cluster into up to 16 distinct genomic sites [108]. In a word, these studies highlight the versatility and efficiency of Ty transposon-based systems as robust tools for metabolic engineering and pathway optimization in yeast.

At present, the application of heterologous transposons as integration tools in S. cerevisiae has also been developed [12]. However, the application of most heterologous transposon tools in metabolic engineering has primarily focused on non-conventional yeasts. Zhao et al. developed a platform using the Hermes transposon to efficiently generate expression libraries and quickly identify high-yielding strains. Through the platform they successfully screened the high (S)-norcoclaurine yield Scheffersomyces stipitis strain, which reached 308 μg/L100. Furthermore, the heterologous transposon tool permitted the simultaneous investigation of the impact of gene cassette copy number and integration site. Wu et al. introduced the PB transposon system into Issatchenkia orientalis to successfully screen for engineered strains that efficiently express the citramalate synthase gene (cimA), and the best genome-integrated cimA strains produced 2.0 g/L of citramalate within 48 h [109]. Nevertheless, its application in metabolic engineering has also provided direction for practical implementation in S. cerevisiae.

Furthermore, the yield of some products can be improved by increasing the copy number of the transposon. Through inverse metabolic engineering, Nho et al. discovered that the gene expression level of YBL100W–B, which encodes the Ty2 retrotransposon, is positively correlated with glutathione and cell biomass in S. cerevisiae [110].

The challenges and opportunities of transposons

Genomic insertion mutations and gene expression interference

The inherent activity of transposons poses a dual threat to the stability of the host genome, exerting its influence at various levels ranging from molecular perturbations to the macroscopic structure of chromosomes. The most direct impact stems from their random or targeted insertions within the genome. For instance, the Ty1 family in yeast exhibits a strong insertion site preference, typically concentrating in the adjacent regions of RNA polymerase III transcriptional sites such as tRNA genes [17,78]. Whilst this insertion partially avoids direct disruption of coding regions, it may still damage crucial cis-regulatory elements such as upstream promoters, enhancers, or insulators, leading to abnormal silencing or activation of adjacent genes. More critically, transposon insertion can result in complete loss of gene function. Early studies of the CYC1 locus have clearly demonstrated how Ty1 insertion directly causes deletion or inactivation of the target gene [111]. The “insertion-induced mutation” characteristic is the basis for the evolution and genetic diversity of the genome driven by transposons, and it is also an intuitive manifestation of their potential destructiveness. Therefore, fungi harbor a vast array of repression mechanisms, including repeat-induced point mutation and many others [112]. An in-depth analysis of the Ty1 transposon has revealed that it is controlled by multiple factors. One factor is Copy Number Control (CNC), a unique regulatory mechanism specific to Ty1: Ty1 produces an alternative transcript that encodes p22, a truncated form of the gag capsid protein corresponding to the C-terminal half of gag. p22 acts as a trans-dominant negative inhibitor of Ty1 retrotransposition by interfering with virus-like particle (VLP) assembly and function [113]. Additionally, efficient Ty1 expression is associated with multiple ribosome biogenesis factors, and the ribosomal protein paralog RPL7A is necessary for Ty1 transposition [114]. Furthermore, studies have reported that DRT2 acts as a mobility restriction factor for Ty1’ (a subfamily of Ty1) [115]. The establishment of the CNC-controllable Ty1 platform is highly significant for synthesizing high-value compounds in yeast cell factories.

However, a more profound and disruptive threat comes from non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR) that occurs between highly homologous transposable element copies scattered within the genome. When DSBs are induced or spontaneously generated within or near any Ty element, the cell may mistakenly use a homologous Ty copy on another chromosome as a repair template during the repair process, rather than using its correct allele. This erroneous repair mechanism may result in the loss of heterologous expression cassettes on transposons and large-scale chromosomal structural variations.

Research confirms that whether spontaneously arising [116] or artificially induced by replication stress (such as reduced DNA polymerase α levels) [117,118], gene editing tools like CRISPR/Cas9 [119,120], or ionizing radiation [121], double-strand breaks occurring within Ty-enriched regions yield repair products-including chromosomal deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations-whose breakpoints are significantly enriched within Ty elements. For instance, chromosomal structural analysis of spontaneously generated heterozygous loss (LOH) clones revealed that breakpoints for all translocation events were invariably located within the Ty1 element [116].

Clusters of Ty elements, especially those arranged in a reverse repeat pattern, constitute the well-known "fragile sites" in the genome. Under replication pressure, they are highly prone to breakage and trigger catastrophic rearrangements [118,122]. To counteract the impact of transposons on the genome, host cells have evolved a series of sophisticated defence and regulatory strategies to maintain genomic stability. Firstly, epigenetic silencing mechanisms (such as heterochromatin formation) are widely employed to suppress transposon transcriptional activity, particularly in critical regions like the centromere [52,123]. Secondly, cells regulate the selection of DSB repair pathways to minimize the risk of NAHR. Studies have shown that effective 5′→3′ DNA end excision is a key step in limiting NAHR; when the key factors Sgs1 and Exo1 are absent, the cells will use the distant (12–48 kb apart) Ty repeat sequences at the periphery of the genome in an exceptionally efficient manner to repair DSBs, resulting in a sharp increase in the frequency of genomic rearrangement [124].

This indicates that cells tolerate any dangers associated with extensive end resection to inhibit this error-prone repair pathway, which is based on distant repetitive sequences. Moreover, the repair pathways themselves also have competition and regulation. For instance, in HR repair, gene conversion usually takes precedence over the more prone-to-rearrangement break-induced replication (BIR) pathway.

Gaining a deeper understanding of transposable mechanisms not only reveals the principles underpinning genomic stability maintenance but also holds significant implications for modern biotechnological applications. When employing genome editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9, key strategies to prevent unintended chromosomal rearrangements include avoiding guide RNA design within repetitive sequence-rich regions (e.g., Ty elements, centromeres) and utilizing high-fidelity Cas9 variants to minimize off-target cutting [119,125,126].

At the same time, these studies also highlight the dual role of transposons in evolution: they are a continuous endogenous mutagenic source of genomic instability, but through mechanisms such as NAHR, they also drive large-scale rearrangements, providing the original genetic material for genomic remodeling, the generation of new genes, and the adaptive evolution of species [52,59,127].

CRISPR-associated transposons

CRISPR-associated transposons (CAST), are transposons that have delegated their insertion site selection to a nuclease-deficient CRISPR-Cas system [128]. CASTs are derived from Tn7-like transposons and retain the core transposition genes tnsB and tnsC but dispense with tnsE, and often tnsD, which mediate target selection in canonical Tn7 systems [129]. Based on the CRISPR effector modules, CAST are divided into two major classes: Class I CASTs (Type I–B, I–D, and I–F) use a multi-protein Cascade complex for target recognition, whereas Class II CASTs (Type V–K) use the single-protein Cas12k [130]. However, the efficiency of the CAST system is not reflected in eukaryotic cells. This may be due to structural alterations, reduced expression levels, or functional inhibition through interactions with host substances in the protein encoded by CAST within Gram-positive bacteria and eukaryotic cells. Identifying key influencing factors would facilitate targeted improvements to the CAST system or host modification, thereby promoting its effective expression and functionality within eukaryotic cells [97].

Mining efficient transposons

Although numerous active transposases have been identified in various yeasts, transposition inhibition, transposition preference, and reduced transposition efficiency with increasing cassette remain significant bottlenecks in transposon development. The identification of transposons with simple structures and high activity holds significant importance for research into transposon mechanisms and the development of systematic tools. The TcBuster transposon was first discovered in the Tribolium castaneum and exhibits activity in both human and S. cerevisiae cells [131]. To function, it requires only a transposase and two terminal sequences: an L-terminal sequence of 328 bp and an R-terminal sequence of 145 bp [132]. And the TcBuster transposon with its strict target selection (NNNTANNN) provides more options for the development of transposon tools and the analysis of their mechanisms. Furthermore, MITEs (miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements) are non-autonomous transposons that occur in all domains of life [133]. MITEs are short non-autonomous TEs that move by “cut-and-paste” mechanisms [134]. MITE achieves transposition through the transposase of other transposons [135]. Compared to other transposons, MITE families often have high copy numbers [136]. Due to their small sizes, MITE insertions are much less disruptive to genes than the larger elements [137]. At present, researchers have developed various search tools for MITEs, such as MITE Hunter [138], MITE-Digger [137] and FINDMITEs [139], which can accelerate the identification and classification of MITEs. Notably, Fattash et al. constructed an efficient gene editing tool by introducing highly efficient hybrid Stowaway MITE into S. cerevisiae [140]. Based on the characteristics of MITEs, we can identify MITEs that respond to the same transposase, thereby achieving multi-pathway integration of precise regulatory pathways for gene expression in the future. This is of great significance for metabolic engineering to modify microbial cells and achieve efficient synthesis of target products, as well as for balancing the production-growth of the host cells.

Discussion and outlook

Transposons, as endogenous genetic elements in fungi, provide a highly efficient editing platform for genome-wide mutant library construction, gene functional analysis, metabolic pathway optimization, and multi-copy gene integration, leveraging their unique molecular mechanisms. Compared to conventional genetic manipulation, transposon-based editing strategies enable precise, scarless, and more efficient genomic modifications. These approaches demonstrate considerable potential for constructing industrial yeast cell factories and accelerate the engineering of non-conventional yeast species. With advancing insights into transposon biology, several integrated editing systems—such as the CAST system—have been developed. By combining the targeted insertion capability of transposons with the precise DNA recognition of CRISPR-Cas, these systems generate double-strand breaks at specific genomic sites without requiring donor DNA homology arms or host DNA repair machinery, thereby significantly improving the efficiency of foreign DNA integration. Although not yet widely implemented in yeast, the emergence of such tools represents a noteworthy methodological advance in fungal genome editing.

Looking to the future, the integration of transposon technology with synthetic biology tools (such as synthetic transcriptional switches), directed evolution and artificial intelligence is expected to increase efficiency, improve controllability and specificity, and reduce off-target effects. These convergent strategies will not only accelerate the development of yeast-based cellular factories, thereby lowering industrial production costs and boosting economic returns, but also open up new technological avenues for tackling urgent health and environmental sustainability challenges.
