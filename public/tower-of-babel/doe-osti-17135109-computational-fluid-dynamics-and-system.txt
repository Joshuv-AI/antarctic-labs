 ABSTRACT
 This report documents the computational modeling work performed in support of the Thermal
Hydraulic Experimental Test Article (THETA) at Argonne National Laboratory during fiscal year 2026.
The work encompasses two principal efforts: the design and confirmatory computational fluid dynamics
(CFD) analysis of a core flow conditioner, and continued development of the THETA system-level model
using SAS4A/SASSYS-1 (SAS).
 Prior experimental and computational investigations identified significant temperature oscillations at
the THETA core barrel outlet, driven primarily by buoyancy-induced flow instabilities arising from the
radial injection geometry at the core inlet and heat transfer from the hot pool to the cold pool through the
redan wall. To address these oscillations, a flow conditioner design effort was undertaken utilizing a prior
THETA COMSOL CFD model, pursuing two parallel concepts: a flow straightener to suppress lateral
velocity components and an orifice plate to redistribute the radially injected sodium across the heater cross
section. CFD evaluation of each concept independently demonstrated that neither alone was sufficient to
fully attenuate the oscillations. The flow straightener transmitted the underlying inlet flow maldistribution
through its channels without adequate cross-sectional redistribution, while the orifice plate improved flow
redistribution but did not provide axial flow conditioning. These parallel findings converged on an
integrated design combining a flow straightener with orifice holes at its bottom face, which addresses both
root causes simultaneously.
 Confirmatory CFD analysis were performed under prototypic thermal conditions replicating selected
test conditions from the FY26 Series 2026A experimental campaign. The simulations showed that the
combined flow conditioner produces a stable and uniformly distributed velocity field at the core outlet
across the tested conditions. An initial transient perturbation observed within the first ten minutes of the
simulation, attributed to the sudden transition from zero to full core power inducing a prompt buoyancy-
driven flow from the center of the core, was found to stabilize after approximately ten minutes, consistent
with the experimental observations. The confirmatory analysis supports the conclusion that the flow
conditioner is effective in reducing temperature oscillations at the core outlet across the range of tested
power levels and flow rates, and that the more stable core outlet temperature enhances the interpretation
of experimental data for comparisons against predictions from system-level model predictions.
 In parallel, the THETA SAS model was updated to improve its predictive capability and prepare for
validation efforts with data from the modified facility. Key updates include refinement of the transient
boundary conditions to properly represent test configurations where the secondary sodium loop was not
in use, implementation of a vessel heat loss and trace heating model using the SAS reactor vessel auxiliary
cooling system capability to account for the net heat exchange at the vessel exterior, and activation of a
core barrel heat loss pathway by separating the core model into heated and unheated sections to enable
component-to-component heat transfer from the core barrel to the cold pool. The vessel heat loss model,
calibrated against measured temperature rise rates from the Series 2025F tests, improved agreement
between predicted and measured temperature evolution during transients. The core barrel heat loss model
framework was established with placeholder heat transfer coefficients to be calibrated in future work using
data from the modified facility configuration with the flow conditioner installed. These model updates
establish the foundation for systematic validation of the THETA SAS model against upcoming
experimental data from the modified facility.

1 Introduction
 The Thermal Hydraulic Experimental Test Article (THETA) is an electrically heated liquid-sodium
test facility installed in the Mechanisms Engineering Test Loop (METL) at Argonne National Laboratory
(ANL) that replicates the principal thermal hydraulic features of a pool-type sodium-cooled fast reactor
[1]. THETA serves as a platform for developing sodium components and instrumentation and for
acquiring experimental data to support the validation of reactor thermal-hydraulic and safety analysis
software. A detailed description of the latest THETA facility, its components, and instrumentation can be
found in [2].
 During prior experimental campaigns, significant temperature oscillations were observed at the core
barrel outlet, where heated sodium exiting the core enters the hot pool at temperatures above the
surrounding pool sodium [3]. These oscillations complicated the use of core outlet temperature data for
validating system-level codes, particularly when prescribing the hot pool inlet temperature. Computational
Fluid Dynamics (CFD) analyses performed in FY25 identified key factors contributing to these
oscillations, including the velocity field induced by the radial injection geometry at the core inlet and heat
transfer from the hot pool to the cold pool through the redan wall [4]. These findings motivated the design
of a flow conditioner to homogenize the inlet flow distribution and thereby reduce the amplitude of
temperature oscillations at the core outlet.
 In FY26, a flow conditioner was designed, fabricated using laser powder bed fusion additive
manufacturing in 316H stainless steel, and installed at the bottom of the THETA core barrel. A parametric
test campaign (Series 2026A) was subsequently executed, replicating the thermal-hydraulic conditions of
the FY25 baseline campaign (Series 2025F) conducted without the flow conditioner. The experimental
results are documented in the companion FY26 experimental report [2]. The experimental results
demonstrated a consistent and measurable reduction in core outlet temperature oscillations across all tested
power levels and primary sodium flow rates. In parallel with the flow conditioner design effort, the
THETA system level model using SAS4A/SASSYS-1 (SAS) [5] has continued to be refined to improve
its predictive capability for the facility's thermal-hydraulic behavior. Building on the FY25 model
development [4], which introduced component-to-component heat transfer pathways and a preliminary
thermal stratification model for the cold pool, the FY26 effort focused on establishing a streamlined model
structure for systematic comparison with measured data, refining the primary heat exchanger model to
cover test conditions where the secondary loop was not in use, and implementing a vessel heat loss and
trace heating model.
 This report documents the computational modeling work performed in support of THETA during
FY26. Section 2 presents the design and confirmatory analysis of the core flow conditioner. The evolution
of the flow conditioner concept from these findings through the final design geometry is discussed. Then,
confirmatory CFD analyses of the final flow conditioner design performed using COMSOL Multiphysics
are described, conducted for selected FY26 experimental test conditions from Series 2026A, which were
conducted under the same core power levels and primary sodium flow rates as the Series 2025F core
oscillation tests documented in [2, 2]. From the results, the flow conditioner's effectiveness to reduce
temperature oscillations at the core outlet are assessed. Section 3 covers recent progress on the THETA
SAS4A/SASSYS-1 model. Updates to the model structure for streamlined comparison with experimental
measurements are described, along with refinements to the primary heat exchanger model that extend its
applicability to test conditions without secondary loop operation. The implementation of a vessel heat loss
and trace heating model is discussed, together with initial plans for dedicated characterization tests to
independently quantify these parameters.

2 Design and Computational Assessment of the THETA Flow Conditioner
 Prior experimental campaigns on THETA revealed significant temperature oscillations at the core
barrel outlet, where heated sodium exiting the core enters the hot pool at temperatures above the
surrounding pool sodium [3]. The THETA core barrel features diametrically opposed inlet windows that
were originally designed to promote mixing at the bottom of the core and prevent dead zones from being
heated inefficiently. However, the operating flow rates of THETA fall within a relatively low range where
forced convection effects are insufficient to achieve the intended mixing. The FY25 experimental
characterization showed that the oscillation amplitudes increased with core power and are more
pronounced at lower primary sodium flow rates, indicating that the oscillations are primarily driven by
buoyancy forces rather than turbulence alone. At the tested flow conditions falling within a transitional
flow regime, the momentum of the radially injected sodium is not sufficient to overcome the buoyancy-
driven flow structures that develop within the heated core, and the diametrically opposed injection
geometry instead introduces a persistent nonuniform flow distribution across the heater. Thermocouple
measurements within the core barrel also confirmed that the oscillations originate upstream of the core
outlet rather than being solely a hot pool mixing artifact.
 In parallel, FY25 computational modeling effort [4] employed CFD analyses using COMSOL
Multiphysics to investigate the factors contributing to these oscillations. The simplified hot pool model,
updated to include the radial injection geometry at the core inlet and increased heat transfer to the cold
pool, was able to reproduce temperature oscillations at the core outlet, with frequencies comparable to
those observed experimentally. These analyses identified two primary factors contributing to these
oscillations from the velocity field induced by the radial injection geometry at the core inlet and heat
transfer from the hot pool to the cold pool. The nonuniform flow patterns and consequent temperature
gradients within the core barrel from the two promoted thermally driven flow instabilities near the core
outlet.
 Together, these experimental and computational findings motivated the design of a flow conditioner
to homogenize the inlet flow distribution upstream of the heated core, with the goal of producing a more
uniform temperature profile at the core exit and thereby reducing the amplitude of the temperature
oscillations measured at the core outlet. This section documents the design of the flow conditioner and the
confirmatory computational analysis of its performance. Section 2.1 describes the design process, tracing
the evolution of the flow conditioner concept from the insights gained in the FY25 analyses through the
final design geometry. Section 2.2 presents confirmatory CFD analyses of the final as-installed flow
conditioner design, performed using COMSOL Multiphysics under conditions that replicate selected
FY26 experimental tests from Series 2026A, which was conducted at the same core power levels and
primary sodium flow rates as Series 2025F core oscillation tests documented in [3], which enables a direct
computational assessment of the flow conditioner’s performance in temperature oscillation reduction at
the core outlet.

 2.1 Flow Conditioner Design
 The flow conditioner design was guided by the principle of minimizing modifications to the existing
THETA facility while addressing the root cause of the core outlet temperature oscillations identified in
the FY25 experimental and computational analyses. As discussed in the preceding section, the radial
injection geometry at the core inlet plenum was identified as a primary contributor to the non-uniform
velocity field within the core flow channel, which in turn promotes thermally driven flow instabilities at
the core outlet. The design process pursued two parallel approaches, a flow straightener concept and an
orifice plate concept, before converging on a combined design that incorporated the strengths of both.

2.1.1 Flow Straightener Concept
 Flow straighteners, also referred to as tube bundle flow conditioners, are conventional devices used in
piping systems to eliminate swirl, suppress lateral velocity components, and produce an axially uniform
velocity profile. They are commonly employed upstream of flow measurement devices and in applications
where a well-developed, unidirectional flow profile is required. A flow straightener typically consists of
an array of parallel tubes or channels that physically constrain the fluid to move in the axial direction,
breaking up large-scale eddies and recirculation zones that may be present in the incoming flow.
 In the THETA geometry, the radial injection at the core inlet introduces significant lateral and
recirculating velocity components within the core barrel. Figure 2-1 shows the CFD-predicted evolution
of the heater outlet temperature along with the local velocity field near the heater outlet in the condition
of 6 GPM and 38 kW heater power of the Series 2025F test. The CFD analyses found that radially injected
sodium into the heater might be one of the factors contributing to unstable flow conditions by creating
complex flow patterns within the heater [4]. Furthermore, the heater rods are located only a few tenths of
an inch above the inlet windows, providing insufficient flow development length for the radially injected
sodium to decelerate, redirect, and establish a uniform axial velocity profile before entering the heated
section. A flow straightener concept was therefore considered first to impose axial flow conditioning
within the limited development length available, suppressing the lateral velocity components and
establishing a more uniform flow profile before the sodium enters the heater rod array.

2.1.2 Orifice Plate Concept
 Building on the findings on the concept of the straightener tubes in Section 2.1.1, an orifice plate
concept was evaluated in parallel to directly target the cross-sectional velocity non-uniformity that the
flow straightener alone could not resolve. Orifice plates are widely used in fluid systems to control flow
distribution by introducing a localized pressure drop that forces the fluid to redistribute across the available
cross section. In conventional applications, orifice plates serve to suppress preferential flow paths and
reduce spatial non-uniformity in the velocity field downstream of the restriction. In the THETA geometry,
sodium enters the core barrel through diametrically opposed inlet windows, creating a strongly non-
uniform velocity field that favors the regions directly aligned with the inlet windows while starving the
peripheral and central regions of the heater array. Because the radially injected sodium impinges on the
opposing side of the core barrel and concentrates flow along the injection axis, an orifice plate installed
between the inlet windows and the bottom of the heated core was considered as a countermeasure by
partially blocking the center of the inlet where the opposing radial jets converge. In this case, the orifice
redirects sodium toward the peripheral heater elements and promotes a more uniform distribution across
the heater cross section.
 To evaluate the orifice plate concept for the THETA heater, a series of CFD simulations were
performed at a primary flow rate of 6 GPM using only the heater domain that represents the 54 cylindrical
heated rods, the core barrel, and the radial injection geometry at the bottom of the heater. The quarter-
symmetry domain was employed. The flow straightener tubes were excluded from this geometry to isolate
the design impact of the orifice plate on the cross- sectional velocity distribution. Figure 2-5 shows a
schematic of the computational domain used for orifice design study. Preliminary assessments were
conducted to investigate an optimal configuration of the orifice plate that improves flow redistribution to
achieve a more uniform core outlet temperature without requiring modifications to the inlet window
geometry itself.

 Figure 2-6 shows the flow velocity field at the core outlet by various orifice design configurations, the
central region opening/blockage area and the middle annular region opening area. Selective configurations
were tested: A) the central region of the orifice plate is blocked, while the surrounding channel openings
are maximized to minimize pressure drop across the plate; B) the center of the orifice is left open while
the middle annular region is blocked, redirecting flow toward both the center and the periphery; and C)
the central region is blocked with a moderate channel opening area. Through the parametric study, the
optimal central blockage radius was explored while maintaining sufficient open channel area to avoid
excessive pressure drop and flow oscillations.
 Configuration A produced the most favorable velocity field among the three cases. The central
blockage effectively intercepted the converging radial jets from the diametrically opposed inlet windows
and redirected the flow outward, resulting in a velocity distribution with the highest velocity at the center
of the heater array and the lowest at the periphery. From discussions with the experimental team, this
radial velocity profile, highest at the center and decreasing toward the core barrel wall, is considered as
favorable for producing a uniform temperature field at the heater outlet. Directing the highest flow rate to
the center and proportionally less flow to the periphery therefore compensates for this radial variation in
net heat transfer to the sodium, promoting a more uniform temperature distribution at the core outlet.
 Configuration B produced a qualitatively similar but less desirable velocity distribution, with lower
velocity at the center and higher velocity at the periphery compared to Configuration A. More critically,
the flow field in Configuration B was non-uniform, with unexpectedly elevated velocities in the annular
gap between the outermost heater elements and the core barrel wall. This is an unfavorable characteristic
because increased flow velocity in this peripheral gap enhances convective heat transfer from the heated
sodium to the core barrel wall, which in turn increases heat loss from the hot pool to the cold pool through
the redan. As identified in the FY25 CFD analyses [4], heat transfer through the redan wall is one of the
two primary factors promoting thermally driven flow instabilities at the core outlet. Therefore, a flow
distribution that amplifies this heat transfer pathway would be counterproductive to the goal of reducing
temperature oscillations.
 Configuration C, with its enlarged central blockage area, did not produce a proportional improvement
in flow redistribution relative to Configuration A. Despite the larger blocked region at the center, the
reduced annular region opening area constrained the available flow paths without effectively redirecting
additional flow toward the center of the heater array. The resulting velocity distribution did not achieve a
meaningfully greater concentration of flow at the center or a further reduction at the periphery compared
to Configuration A, while the reduced opening area imposed a greater pressure drop. This result indicates
that beyond an optimal central blockage radius, further enlargement of the blocked region yields
diminishing returns in flow uniformity and increasing hydraulic resistance through the reduced opening
area.

2.1.3 Integrated Design of Flow Conditioner with Orifice Holes
 The findings from the parallel development of the orifice plate and flow straightener concepts
demonstrated that neither element alone was sufficient to fully address the core outlet temperature
oscillations. The flow straightener effectively constrained the flow to the axial direction within each tube
but transmitted the underlying inlet flow maldistribution through the straightener channels without
adequate cross-sectional redistribution, resulting in persistent velocity and temperature oscillations at the
core outlet. The orifice plate effectively redistributed the radially injected sodium across the heater cross
section, producing a favorable velocity profile with the highest flow at the center and the lowest at the
periphery, which resulted in improved temperature uniformity at the core outlet. However, the orifice plate
alone did not provide axial flow conditioning to suppress lateral velocity components within the heated
section.

 Building on these results and informed by discussions with the experimental team, the final design
combined both elements: a flow straightener with orifice holes incorporated at its bottom face. In this
integrated configuration, sodium entering the core barrel through the diametrically opposed inlet windows
first encounters the orifice plate at the bottom of the flow conditioner. The orifice redistributes the radially
injected flow across the cross section by blocking the central region where the opposing jets converge and
directing the sodium toward the peripheral openings. The redistributed flow then enters the vertical
straightener tubes, which suppress the remaining lateral velocity components and guides the sodium
axially upward into the heated core. To further reduce pressure losses and promote smooth flow transitions
at the inlet and outlet of the flow conditioner, the edges of both the orifice plate openings and the flow
straightener tubes were chamfered. Figure 2-8 shows a schematic of the concept of the combined flow
straightener with orifice holes at the bottom of the flow conditioner indicated with candidate design
parameter dimensions. The key design parameters explored included the central blockage radius of the
orifice plate, middle region of orifice holes opening area surrounding the central blockage, the height of
the flow straightener tubes, and chamfered height at the inlet and outlet of the flow conditioner.

 The final design was selected based on the configuration that achieved the best balance between flow
redistribution effectiveness, axial flow conditioning, and hydraulic resistance. The flow conditioner height
was initially evaluated at 4 inches in the CFD simulations described in Section 2.1.1. Through thorough
discussion with the system modeling experts and the experimental team, the height of the flow straightener
was reduced from 4 inches to 3 inches by engineering judgement to have minimal impact on the flow
conditioning performance while providing additional margin for preserving natural circulation condition
testing capability. It was constrained by the need to preserve operational flexibility in the THETA facility,
particularly to support natural circulation testing across a broad range of flow conditions without pump
operation. Because the flow conditioner installation requires raising the heater elevation while the
intermediate heat exchanger in the hot pool remains at a fixed elevation, increasing the heater elevation
reduces the available driving head for natural circulation between the active heated region and the IHX
inlet. In addition, only the chamfered inlet edge was adopted in the final design to reduce the contraction
losses as sodium enters the orifice holes and straightener tubes, while the chamfered outlet part was
removed by the height limit, considering flow separation and recirculation as the conditioned flow exiting
into the heated core region minimal.
 Figure 2-10 and Figure 2-11 present the final flow conditioner design, installed to the THETA facility
in FY26 [2]. The final design of the combined flow conditioner has a total height of 3 inches and an outer
diameter of 7.69 inches, designed to fit within the core barrel. The flow straightener section consists of 19
vertical tubes arranged in a triangular pattern with a tube section diameter of 1.375 inches and a tube pitch
of 1.607 inches. The tubes extend 2.5 inches from the top of the orifice plate to the top of the flow
conditioner.
 The orifice plate at the bottom face incorporates a differentiated flow area arrangement across the 19
tube positions to achieve the desired radial velocity distribution identified in the parametric study
described in Section 2.1.2. As shown in Figure 2-8, the centermost tube position features a central blocked
region with a radius of 0.4 inches, creating an annular flow channel between the central blockage and the
tube wall. The six tube positions in the second ring, surrounding the centermost position, each feature a
circular opening with a diameter of 0.7 inches centered within the tube, providing a smaller flow area than
the full tube cross section. The remaining 12 tube positions in the outer ring are fully open, providing the
maximum flow area per tube. This graduated flow area arrangement, most restricted at the center,
moderately restricted in the second ring, and fully open at the periphery, produces the favorable radial
velocity profile.
 A chamfered inlet edge with a height of 0.5 inches is incorporated at the bottom of the orifice plate to
reduce contraction losses as sodium enters the orifice holes and straightener tubes. Additionally, the flow
channels in the centermost and second ring tube positions transition from the restricted orifice openings
to full tube cross section through a conical expansion geometry, which gradually increases the flow area
to avoid flow separation that would otherwise result from a sudden area change at the orifice-to-tube
transition. The outer ring tube positions, being fully open, do not require this conical transition.

 2.2 Confirmatory CFD Analysis of the Flow Conditioner
 Following the design finalization described in Section 2.1, confirmatory CFD analyses were
performed to assess the effectiveness of the combined flow conditioner under prototypic thermal
conditions that replicate the Series 2025F core oscillation tests and the Series 2026A tests documented in
the companion experimental reports [2, 3]. The confirmatory simulations were performed at the selected
condition from the Series 2025F and Series 2026A test matrices, low flow rate (~6.1 GPM) and high
power (~37 kW). Two simulations were compared: one representing the baseline configuration without
the flow conditioner (corresponding to Series 2025F) and one with the final combined flow conditioner
installed (corresponding to Series 2026A). The simulation with the flow conditioner was conducted using
COMSOL Multiphysics with the extended simplified heater and hot pool model described in Section 2.1.3,
updated to incorporate the final combined flow conditioner geometry. The boundary conditions, initial
sodium temperature of approximately 200°C, core power transient profile (core tripped on at 3 minutes
and maintained for 25 minutes), and quasi-adiabatic vessel wall conditions were matched to the
experimental test protocols described in [2, 3]. The heater was modeled as a volumetric heat source
distributed across the heated section, and the redan wall heat transfer was represented using the convective
boundary condition developed in the FY25 modeling effort [4]. The baseline simulation results without
the flow conditioner for this condition were previously reported in the FY25 computational modeling
effort [4].
 Figure 2-12 presents the temperature evolution at the heater outlet with the flow conditioner installed
with measured heater outlet temperature. In the FY25 baseline experiments, the core outlet temperature
exhibited a characteristic intermittent peaking pattern, with irregular temperature spikes separated by
periods of lower temperature between peaks reported in [3, 4]. This behavior was attributed to the periodic
intrusion of sodium of the hot pool into the heater outlet region, driven by recirculation flow. With the
flow conditioner installed, this intermittent peaking pattern is substantially reduced but not entirely
eliminated. Following the initiation of core heating at 180 seconds, the heater outlet temperature rises
rapidly and exhibits oscillatory behavior during the first approximately 10 minutes of the heated transient.
This initial perturbation is attributed to the sudden transition from zero to full core power, which induces
a prompt buoyancy-driven upward flow concentrated at the center of the core before the flow conditioner
can fully establish a unform cross-sectional velocity distribution. The abrupt onset of full heating may

create a large instantaneous temperature differential between the heated sodium and the surrounding pool

 These confirmatory CFD results are consistent with the experimental findings documented in the

observed in both the CFD analysis and the experimental data provides confidence that the flow conditioner
is effective in producing a more stable core outlet temperature. The qualitative agreement between the
CFD predictions and the experimental observations provides confidence that the more stable core outlet
temperature achieved with the flow conditioner enhances the interpretation of experimental data for
comparisons against system-level modeling predictions and supports ongoing code validation efforts.

3 THETA SAS Model Development
 The installation of the flow conditioner in the second half of FY26 introduces a significant
modification to the THETA facility that has implications for the THETA SAS model. The flow conditioner
alters the velocity distribution within the core barrel, which in turn affects the temperature profile at the
core outlet and the nature of the thermal interaction between the heated sodium exiting the core and the
surrounding sodium in the hot pool. Furthermore, the 3.5-inch upward displacement of the heater to
accommodate the flow conditioner changes the geometric relationship between the heated section and the
hot pool, which may affect the heat transfer pathways between the heater and the cold pool through the
core barrel wall, as well as between the hot pool and the cold pool through the redan and vessel walls.
These facility modifications mean that the heat transfer mechanisms characterized in the FY25 model,
particularly the component-to-component heat transfer coefficient between the hot pool and the cold pool
may be no longer be directly applicable to the modified facility. To prepare for validation efforts using
SAS [5] with upcoming new data from the modified facility, it is therefore essential to establish a flexible
model structure that can be efficiently calibrated to reflect evolving test configurations and boundary
conditions.
 Building on these FY25 developments, the FY26 effort therefore focused on establishing a streamlined
model structure for systematic comparison with experimental data, including refining the primary heat
exchanger model to properly represent test conditions where the secondary loop was not in use and
implementing a vessel heat loss and trace heating model to improve the representation of the facility's
thermal boundary conditions. The following subsections provide an overview of the THETA SAS model
and descriptions of the aforementioned model updates.

 3.1 Overview of the THETA SAS Model
 A system-level model of THETA has been developed using SAS [5], a simulation tool used to perform
safety analyses of anticipated events as well as design basis and beyond design basis accidents for
advanced liquid metal-cooled nuclear reactors. Since its initial development, the THETA SAS model has
been continuously updated based on experimental observations and supporting analyses. The model
consists of multiple segments and compressible volumes representing both the primary and secondary
sodium systems. For the primary system, the model includes the inlet plenum, the hot pool, and two
distinct cold pool volumes, an upper annular volume and a lower cylindrical volume. These volumes are
connected by flow paths representing the core channel, the primary side of the intermediate heat exchanger
(IHX), the primary pump and piping, and an inter-pool flow path. The core channel is modeled with 54
heater elements using an axial power shape to capture the heated and unheated sections. The IHX is
represented with a detailed model including shell wall, shell coolant channel, tube wall, and tube coolant
channel, with the IHX outlet window mechanism modeled to represent the variable elevation cold sodium
deposition capability of the facility. For the secondary system, the model includes the IHX tube side, the
hot and cold leg piping, the secondary pump, and the air-cooled heat exchanger (AHX). The primary and
secondary pumps are modeled as table lookup components, and the AHX is modeled as a table lookup
heat sink. A graphical representation of the THETA SAS model is provided in Figure 3-1.

 During FY25, a preliminary thermal stratification model was implemented for the lower cold pool
using the SAS stratified volume model [4]. The stratified volume model extends the 0D perfect mixing
approximation by introducing a small number of distinct temperature regions separated by horizontal
interfaces. The model calculates plume height and interface location changes based on the temperature
and volume of each layer and the flow rate and temperature of the incoming and outgoing coolant [5]. The
preliminary stratified cold pool model was able to resolve distinct temperature layers and predict higher
temperatures at the pump inlet compared to the perfect mixing assumption, improving agreement with
experimental measurements. However, discrepancies remained, particularly in the sharpness of
temperature gradients at layer interfaces, the representation of heat transfer through the redan wall to the
top region of the cold pool, and the prediction of complex mixing patterns near the IHX outlet window
and pump inlet elevations.
 The current SAS stratified volume model resolves only a limited number of layers and exhibits sharp
temperature gradients at interfaces that do not fully represent the more gradual stratification profiles
observed experimentally. An updated stratification model capability with improved layer resolution and
interface treatment is expected to be available in SAS by the end of FY26, planned to investigate and
implement improvements to the THETA thermal stratification model by leveraging this updated capability
in FY27. Because of the current model’s limitations and the pending availability of the new stratification
model, thermal stratification was disabled in the THETA SAS model in FY26.

 3.2 THETA SAS Model Updates in FY26

3.2.1 Refinement of the Transient Boundary Conditions
 Several experimental campaigns including the Series 2025F and Series 2026A core oscillation tests
were conducted without secondary loop operation, with the secondary system either drained or not filled
with sodium. In these configurations, the IHX serves only as a flow restriction in the primary circuit with
negligible heat transfer to the secondary side. The FY25 THETA SAS model, which was configured to
represent the IHX with an active secondary circuit, did not accurately capture this operating mode, as the
heat transfer through the IHX tube walls was overestimated when the secondary side was modeled as
stagnant sodium rather than a drained condition.
 To properly distinguish between secondary-on and secondary-off configurations across the full range
of the THETA test conditions, the primary heat exchanger model was utilized to reflect the secondary side
conditions of the secondary-off cases more accurately. The primary heat exchanger model is very similar
to the detailed intermediate heat exchanger model, except instead of requiring a connection to a full heat
transport system on the secondary side, the user specifies the IHX secondary side inlet temperature and
mass flow rate.
 To implement this model, first a heat transport system model with only the THETA primary system
was prepared by extracting the secondary system components from the original model. This enables
running the THETA SAS model for test cases conducted in conditions where either the secondary loop
was not yet installed or installed but drained by applying more representative boundary conditions on the
secondary side of the intermediate heat exchanger. After extracting measured temperature and flow data,
the appropriate primary heat exchanger boundary conditions were prepared by setting a coolant mass flow
rate ramping down from values used in steady state initialization to 0.01 % of their respective values in a
couple of seconds, while maintaining intermediate inlet temperature transient using Function ID
(iPHXWID for flow rate, iPHXTID for secondary coolant inlet temperature, respectively).

3.2.2 Implementing Vessel Heat Loss
 The THETA facility is installed within the METL vessel, which is surrounded by external process
heaters arranged in four zones spanning the vertical extent of the vessel [2]. During testing, these vessel
process heater zones are set to empirically determine manual duty cycles to maintain a quasi-adiabatic
boundary condition. The intent is to balance the heat loss through the vessel insulation with supplemental
heating so that the sodium pools remain at a stable isothermal temperature when the core heater is off and
the primary pump is operating. The nearly identical measured vessel process heater energy as a function
of time across all tests in Series 2025F and 2026A [2] confirms that the vessel boundary condition was
held constant between tests, as shown in Figure 3-2.

 However, while the boundary condition is consistent from test to test, the quasi-adiabatic condition
within each individual test may not be perfect. The FY25 surrogate fluid testing [3] revealed that the quasi-
adiabatic boundary condition maintained by the METL vessel process heater has residual imbalances
between the vessel heater input and the heat loss through the vessel insulation. This resulted in a slow
temperature drift in the sodium pools, particularly during long-duration test, while small in magnitude.
Nevertheless, this can accumulate over the course of a test and introduce systematic errors in the
comparison between SAS predictions and experimental measurements. Implementing a vessel heat loss
and trace heating model that accounts for both the heat loss through the vessel insulation and the
supplemental heat input from the vessel process heaters and secondary system trace heaters is therefore
necessary to improve the fidelity of the thermal boundary conditions in the SAS model.
 In the FY25 THETA SAS model, the exterior of the reactor vessel was treated as an adiabatic
boundary; no heat loss from the vessel to the environment nor trace heating input were represented. Under
this assumption, the model predicted temperatures that increased slightly faster than the measured data
during transients, because the total heat balance in the model did not account for the net heat rejection
from the vessel exterior. In the actual facility, the trace heating was calibrated by the experimental team
to provide constant sodium temperatures when the reactor vessel was at approximately 200°C. At this
calibration temperature, the vessel heat losses to the environment are balanced by the trace heating input,
resulting in a net-zero heat exchange at the vessel exterior. At temperatures above 200°C, the vessel heat
losses exceed the trace heating input, so the system temperatures decrease in the absence of core power or
increase more slowly than they would if the vessel exterior were truly adiabatic.
 To capture this behavior, a simple RVACS (Reactor Vessel Auxiliary Cooling System) model
available in SAS was implemented in the THETA SAS model. This model represents the combined effect
of reactor vessel heat losses and trace heating on the outside of the reactor vessel as a single net heat
transfer pathway [5]. The model specifies a heat transfer coefficient at the reactor vessel exterior based on

the temperature difference above the calibration point. The effective heat transfer coefficient was
estimated using the initial SAS model with an adiabatic boundary condition on the reactor vessel and
comparing the predicted temperature evolution with the measured data. Temperature data of selective
available tests were used to approximate the vessel heat loss. By estimating the total heat capacity of the
primary system, the missing heat rejection rate could be calculated from the difference in temperature rise
rates between the model and the measurements over the various periods of interest. Each test yielded
slightly different estimates of the effective heat transfer coefficient, but the value of heat transfer
coefficient obtained from the Series 2025F high power tests were selected as the basis value, as they are
the most recently conducted, and therefore provide a more representative estimate for current and future
test conditions. Then, based on the observations, the model applies a net heat rejection rate proportional
to the temperature difference above the calibration point, representing the excess of vessel losses over
trace heating.
 A sensitivity study was performed to assess the impact of the vessel heat loss model on the SAS
predictions. The effective heat transfer coefficient can be varied by factors of 0.0x (adiabatic, no vessel

 3.2.3 Implementing Core Barrel Heat Loss
 In the FY25 SAS model, the THETA core was modeled as a single channel encompassing both the
lower heated section and the upper unheated section. Because the SAS averaged core channel approach
does not support component-to-component heat transfer to a heat transport system component such as the
bulk cold pool compressible volume, it was not possible to represent heat rejection from the core barrel to
the cold pool within this modeling framework. To enable this heat transfer pathway, the core model was
updated to separate the heated and unheated sections: the lower heated section is retained as the core
channel, while the upper unheated section is modeled as a pipe-type element connected to the cold pool
through the component-to-component heat transfer model. This separation allows the heat transfer
coefficient to be specified for the unheated upper region of the core barrel independently, representing the
radial heat rejection from the core barrel wall to the surrounding cold pool sodium.
 With this model update in place, the component-to-component heat transfer model was activated for
the upper region of the core barrel to the cold pool. The heat transfer coefficient for this pathway was
initially set to a very small value in the baseline model, effectively preserving the previous model behavior
while establishing the modeling framework for future refinement. Within a dedicated parametric study
directory, a placeholder heat transfer coefficient value was assigned, and a series of sensitivity cases were
prepared with scaling factors ranging from 25% to 300% of the placeholder value to evaluate the impact
of the core barrel heat loss on the predicted core outlet temperature. Figure 3-4 illustrates the impact of
the new core barrel to cold pool heat transfer model for the Series F Test No. 4, where the percentages in
the legend refer to the multiplication factor applied to the core barrel to cold pool heat transfer coefficient,

transfer coefficient will be conducted after updating the SAS model to reflect the THETA facility
modifications and incorporating upcoming new test results.

4 Conclusions
 This report documents the computational modeling work performed in support of THETA during
fiscal year 2026, encompassing the design and confirmatory CFD analysis of a core flow conditioner and
continued development of the THETA SAS4A/SASSYS-1 system-level model.
 A flow conditioner was designed through an iterative CFD-based process utilizing a prior THETA
CFD model using COMSOL Multiphysics to address the significant temperature oscillations observed at
the THETA core outlet during FY25 testing. Two concepts were pursued in parallel: a flow straightener
to suppress lateral velocity components and an orifice plate to redistribute the radially injected sodium
across the heater cross section. CFD evaluation of each concept independently demonstrated that neither
alone was sufficient to fully attenuate the oscillations. These parallel findings converged on an integrated
design combining a flow straightener with orifice holes incorporated at its bottom face. The combined
design was finalized through isothermal CFD simulations that parametrically varied the central blockage
area, orifice hole arrangement, and flow straightener height. The final height of 3 inches was selected to
balance flow conditioning effectiveness against the preservation of natural circulation testing capability.
Only the inlet chamfer was retained in the final design, while the outlet chamfer was removed to comply
with the height constraint.
 Confirmatory CFD analysis was performed under prototypic thermal condition at low-flow, high-
power (approximately 6.1 GPM and 37 kW) condition, replicating selected Series 2025F and 2026A test
matrices. An initial transient perturbation was observed within the first ten minutes of the simulations,
following the initiation of core heating, attributed to the sudden transition from zero to full core power
inducing a prompt buoyancy-driven flow from the center of the core. This perturbation stabilized after
approximately ten minutes, consistent with the experimental observations documented in the companion
FY26 experimental report [2]. The characteristic intermittent temperature peaking pattern observed in the
FY25 baseline analyses and experiments, attributed to periodic intrusion of cooler sodium from the hot
pool driven by recirculation zones near the heater outlet, was substantially diminished with the flow
conditioner installed. Residual oscillations of lower amplitude persisted during the initial transient period,
attributed to the sudden transition from zero to full core power. Future experimental campaigns may
benefit from employing a gradual power ramp, which could reduce the initial buoyancy-driven transient
and further improve the quality of the core outlet temperature data during the early portion of each test.
The qualitative agreement between the confirmatory CFD predictions and the experimental observations
demonstrated that the final combined flow conditioner design is performing as intended, effectively
addressing both the flow maldistribution from the radial injection geometry and the lateral velocity
components that promote thermally driven instabilities. The resulting reduction in core outlet temperature
oscillations enhances the quality of the experimental data available for interpretation and supports ongoing
code validation efforts.
 The THETA SAS model was updated during FY26 to improve its predictive capability and prepare
for validation efforts with data from the modified facility. The transient boundary conditions for the heat
exchanger were refined to properly represent test configurations where the secondary sodium loop was
not in use. A vessel heat loss and trace heating model was implemented using the SAS reactor vessel
auxiliary cooling system capability, representing the combination of the two effects as a single net heat
transfer pathway. The effective heat transfer coefficient was preliminarily calibrated against measured
temperature rise rates from the Series 2025F tests, and a sensitivity study was performed to demonstrate
the impact that a range of heat transfer coefficients have on the agreement between predicted and measured
temperature evolutions. The core barrel heat loss pathway was activated by separating the core model into
 25

heated and unheated sections, with the upper unheated section remodeled as a pipe element connected to
the cold pool through the component-to-component heat transfer model. A placeholder heat transfer
coefficient was assigned with sensitivity cases prepared for future calibration. The computational
modeling work performed in this effort provides a foundation for continued development. The vessel heat
loss model and core barrel heat transfer coefficient should be recalibrated using data from the modified

assessed against newly acquired test data, and the validation basis broadened by applying the improved
