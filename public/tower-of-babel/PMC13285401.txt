Advancing emergency medicine across Asia: a roadmap for leadership, collaboration, and digital transformation
Authors: Arif Alper Cevik, Saravana Kumar, Pauline Convocar, Saleh Fares Al-Ali, Bobi Prabowo, Yuet Chung Axel Siu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emergency medicine has evolved from an emerging discipline to a formally recognised specialty across much of Asia over the past four decades. Adoption has been staggered, from early pioneers such as Singapore, the Philippines and Hong Kong, to more recent recognition in India, Brunei, Myanmar, Laos and several Middle Eastern countries. This broad but uneven maturation has not eliminated substantial workforce and systems gaps. Challenges include shortages of trained emergency medicine specialists, variable training quality and persistent brain drain towards higher-income health systems. At the plenary session “Leading Change Together: Regional Collaboration for Emergency Medicine in Asia” during the Asian Conference on Emergency Medicine 2025 in Dubai, regional society leaders and international federation representatives examined three interlinked domains: workforce growth and retention, regional collaboration and standard-setting, and the implications of digital innovation and artificial intelligence for emergency care. In this communication, we synthesise the panel and audience discourse to argue that the next phase of emergency medicine development in Asia depends on three domains. We urge stakeholders to establish a coherent training and leadership pipeline supporting both undergraduate and postgraduate development; to foster purposeful cross-border collaboration led by professional societies to harmonise standards and share expertise; and to implement digital transformation strategies that maximise integration, promote equity, and maintain a strong focus on human-centred care. By committing to practical actions across each domain, we can collectively shift the region from fragmented growth to a more resilient, equitable, and future-ready emergency care ecosystem, positioning Asia as a global reference for emergency medicine development in diverse, resource-variable settings.

Introduction

In the past four decades, emergency medicine has evolved from an experimental endeavor to an established specialty throughout much of Asia. Initial formal recognition occurred in early adopter countries such as Singapore, the Philippines, and Hong Kong, and subsequently expanded to large nations, including West Asian and Middle Eastern healthcare systems [1, 2] This progression has resulted in a geographically widespread yet temporally uneven development of the field. However, formal recognition has not necessarily translated into sufficient specialist capacity, comprehensive training frameworks, or equitable access to high-quality emergency care.

The plenary panel “Leading Change Together: Regional Collaboration for Emergency Medicine in Asia” at the Asian Conference on Emergency Medicine 2025 in Dubai brought together leaders from member societies of the Asian Society for Emergency Medicine and the International Federation for Emergency Medicine to examine three interdependent questions: the training and retention of the emergency medicine workforce in the region, the mechanisms for collaboration and standard-setting, and the integration of technology and artificial intelligence while maintaining a focus on human-centred care. Instead of providing a comprehensive account of the discussion, this editorial draws upon the panel and audience dialogue to contend that the next phase of emergency medicine development in Asia depends on three key domains.

From expansion to retention: workforce, training and leadership

Panelists mentioned that Asia has transitioned from the initial establishment of programmes to the more complex challenge of sustaining an adequately trained and equitably distributed workforce. Although several countries have experienced rapid increases in residency positions and specialist output, significant national and subnational workforce deficits persist, especially in high-population regions. Additionally, the ongoing emigration of emergency medicine graduates to higher-income countries exacerbates these shortages [1]. Participants described this situation as an expansion of training capacity without sufficient focus on retention and professional sustainability, which ultimately fails to address the staffing gap.

A recurrent theme identified was the necessity to conceptualize emergency medicine education as a continuum. Several panelists reported limited exposure to emergency medicine during undergraduate training, often discovering the specialty only during residency, which restricts early awareness and professional identity formation [3, 4]. Conversely, the implementation of structured emergency medicine curricula in numerous medical schools, including large-scale initiatives in India and early adoption in the United Arab Emirates [5, 6], was cited as an effective strategy to elevate the specialty’s profile and attract dedicated trainees. Panelists also emphasized that residency training should be explicitly competency-based, with clearly defined learning outcomes, supervision standards, and accreditation processes to ensure that rapid expansion does not compromise educational quality or public trust [7].

Leadership capacity has been identified as an under-recognised determinant of workforce stability. Early cohorts of emergency medicine specialists are often promoted to department chair or program director positions with limited preparation in organizational leadership, health system design, or advocacy [8]. Although this trend demonstrates the dynamism of a developing specialty, it also increases the risk of strategic drift and contributes to burnout among emerging leaders [9]. Consequently, the panel recommended integrating intentional leadership development into emergency medicine training through formal courses, regional leadership programs, and structured mentorship [10]. This approach aims to prepare clinicians for roles in service planning, negotiation with ministries, and representation in national reform agendas, in addition to bedside care.

Beyond borders: collaboration, societies and standards

Collaboration is considered essential for advancing progress in regions marked by significant disparities in resources, political structures, and the development of emergency medicine systems. Panelists identified a range of collaborative arrangements, including informal mentorship, hospital-to-hospital partnerships, governmental agreements, and regional qualification frameworks. In this context, professional regional societies and international federations serve as key intermediaries by organizing open online education, coordinating research networks, and providing shared curricular resources that address both national boundaries and economic inequalities [11].

Panelists emphasised that these organisations should serve as enablers rather than gatekeepers by connecting local champions, amplifying national initiatives, and preventing the displacement of local societies. Barriers to collaboration were characterised less as geopolitical constraints and more as procedural and relational challenges, such as lengthy institutional approvals, lack of memoranda of understanding, and competitive attitudes among organisations. Several contributors noted that durable partnerships frequently originate from informal interpersonal contacts or mentoring relationships prior to the establishment of formal agreements.

Audience interactions highlighted the necessity for clearer regional standards regarding emergency medicine training and specialist definitions, especially in regions impacted by brain drain and variable training quality [12]. The panel acknowledged that rigid, uniform standards may not be feasible in resource-constrained environments. Consequently, a pragmatic approach was recommended: the development of consensus-based regional minimum standards that define core competencies [7], supervision requirements, and programme structure, while allowing explicit flexibility for phased implementation and local adaptation under the oversight of national regulatory authorities [1].

Digital futures: technology and artificial intelligence

The discussions highlighted that digital transformation and artificial intelligence (AI) are already impacting emergency care in parts of Asia through command centres, decision-support tools, and AI-augmented imaging and triage [13]. However, significant disparities in digital infrastructure and readiness persist, raising concerns that technological innovation may worsen existing inequities between urban and rural regions or between higher- and lower-income countries [14].

Panelists emphasized that emergency medicine clinicians should serve as active co-designers rather than passive recipients of technology. While industry partners and digital teams contribute technical expertise, they rely on clinicians to define clinically relevant use cases, such as reducing triage delays, enhancing sepsis recognition, or optimizing communication pathways. Multiple speakers noted that projects advanced only after clinical problems were articulated in terms that resonated with decision-makers and funders. Consequently, incorporating foundational digital literacy, data competence, and familiarity with AI concepts into emergency medicine training was identified as essential for effective clinician participation in digital transformation.

At the same time, the panel stressed that technology must be integrated into broader system redesign and evaluated through the lenses of equity and human-centred care. Tools that are not adapted to local workflows or that are concentrated in already advantaged centres may deliver limited benefit or worsen disparities. Participants advocated investing in people such as clinicians, nurses and managers who can interpret data, question algorithmic outputs and advocate for patients when automated recommendations conflict with clinical judgement. In this vision, a “future-ready” emergency medicine system is defined less by specific devices or algorithms than by a workforce able to critically appraise, shape and ethically deploy digital tools in the service of patients.

Asia’s opportunity to lead

The panel’s collective message was that Asia is no longer solely striving to catch up with imported emergency medicine models; it has the opportunity to define how emergency medicine can be built and sustained in heterogeneous, resource-variable environments. Achieving this will require aligning educational reform, cross-border collaboration and digital innovation around a coherent, patient-centred agenda. We propose four interlinked priorities: consolidating the educational continuum from undergraduate exposure to competency-based residency and leadership training; using regional and international societies to articulate adaptable minimum standards; expanding collaborative platforms that particularly support middle- and low-income countries; and embedding clinician-supported, equity-focused digital transformation into emergency medicine practice and training. If pursued collectively, these strategies could help transform Asia’s diverse emergency medicine systems into a more integrated, resilient and globally influential community.
