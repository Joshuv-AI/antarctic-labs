Molecular profiling and computational mutational analysis of bla-NDM-1 in clinical Acinetobacter baumannii isolates
Authors: Muharib Alruwaili, Farzeen Malik, Zaman Khan, Maha Munir, Hasan Ejaz, Intisar Alruwaili, Muhammad Umer Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcinetobacter baumannii is the major cause of healthcare-associated infections attributed to its remarkable ability to resist multiple antibiotics. The study aimed to characterize the clinical A. baumannii isolates, evaluate their antibiotic resistance patterns, determine the prevalence of bla-NDM-1 gene, and perform in-silico mutational analysis to evaluate the structural and functional impact of its variant.MethodsIsolates were cultured on blood and MacConkey agars, identified at biochemical (API 20NE) and molecular levels, and assessed their antibiotic susceptibility using the Kirby–Bauer and MIC assays. PCR and sequencing analyses were performed to detect the variant of bla-NDM. A model of the wild-type bla-NDM-1 structure was created by AlphaFold, while the mutant P28A was generated and refined in PyMOL and validated by SAVES, GOR IV, and stability prediction tools.ResultsResistance among isolates was seen to multiple antibiotics used in this study, such as carbapenem, aminoglycoside, fluoroquinolone, and penicillin, while sensitivity was retained to colistin, polymyxin B, and tigecycline. Based on the antibiotic resistance profiles, 25.3% and 69.3% of the isolates were categorized as multi-drug resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR), respectively, with 82.7% of isolates showing MAR index > 0.2. Mutation in a bla-NDM gene was observed only in 8% (n = 6) of the isolates belonging to A. baumannii and found to possess C→G substitution (P28A). In-silico study showed slight differences in the structure, decreased stability, and greater flexibility, indicating that the mutation is likely neutral and adaptive.ConclusionsThe threat posed by MDR A. baumannii strains is significant; hence, their detection, infection control, and genetic screening are critical for preventing further transmission, limiting severe complications, and minimizing patient mortality rates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05280-5.

Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii was initially identified by Beijerinck in 1911 and then by Brisou and Prevot in 1954, as a species belonging to the genus Acinetobacter [1]. A. baumannii is the most clinically significant species of the A. calcoaceticus-baumannii complex, which is a frequent cause of ventilator-associated pneumonia, bloodstream infections, wound infections, and urinary tract infections in severely ill and immunocompromised patients [2]. It spreads easily by persisting on hospital surfaces and medical equipment and is helped spread faster by its virulence factors, including capsular polysaccharides, pili, and outer membrane proteins, which also cause immune evasion and tissue damage in the host [3].

Resistance to anti-microbial agents has been identified as the key challenge linked with A. baumannii. Decreased permeability of the cell membrane, pump mechanisms, as well as the secretion of ß-lactamases and carbapenemases have been among the mechanisms that have led to the rise in MDR, XDR, and PDR cases throughout the world [4, 5]. As a result of such alarming resistance pattern, carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii has been included in the list of priority pathogens that need to be targeted by new antibiotics as per WHO [6].

Carbapenem resistance is predominantly attributed to carbapenem hydrolysing enzymes such as oxacillinases (OXA-51) and metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs). New Delhi metallo-ß-lactamase-1 (NDM-1) is among the strongest carbapenemases and is widespread in more than 29 variations [7, 8]. The bla-NDM-1 gene is commonly related to mobile genetic elements like ISAba125, which facilitates its horizontal transmission between organisms, for example, Acinetobacter and Enterobacteriaceae [9].

Insufficient research has been conducted to understand the biochemical nature of bla-NDM-1 and the impact of the natural variations of the amino acid sequence on the protein structure and its biological function, although the number of NDM-positive A. baumannii infections is prevalent in Pakistan. The stability and catalytic efficiency of P28A may be impacted by the mutation, which has yet to be explored locally.

To address this problem, the present study will combine in vitro molecular characterization of the resistance profile and the bla-NDM-1 gene in MDR strains of A. baumannii with computational studies of the structure and mutation analysis of the bla-NDM-1 gene. In particular, the study will be aimed at investigating the resistance patterns and the molecular characteristics of the isolates, such as identification of the presence of the bla-NDM-1 gene and its phylogeny. The investigation will include the study of the effects of the P28A mutation on the structure and function of the enzyme NDM-1 using computational modeling, analysis, and prediction of protein stability. The combination of the findings obtained through both laboratory experiments and computation will help understand the resistance mechanism and evolution of carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii.

Materials and methods

In-vitro analysis

Sample collection and processing

A total of 3,280 clinical samples were obtained from indoor and outdoor patients at various tertiary care hospitals in Punjab, including Mayo Hospital, Jinnah Hospital, Services Hospital, Nishtar Hospital, and Shahbaz Sharif DHQ Hospital (Multan). Demographic and clinical data were recorded on a proforma after obtaining informed consent. Specimens were processed according to standard microbiological procedures [10, 11] and cultured on CHROMagar Acinetobacter (CHROMagar, France), MacConkey agar, and blood agar. Presumptive identification was based on colony morphology, Gram staining, and biochemical testing, followed by confirmation using API 20NE and Vitek 2 (bioMérieux, France) with ID-GNB cards. Only purified, well-characterized, non-duplicate A. baumannii isolates were included.

Molecular Characterization of A. baumannii

Genomic DNA was extracted by QIAmp DNA Mini Extraction kit (QIAGEN, Germany) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. One tube multiplex PCR was performed with genus and species-specific primers for A. baumannii to differentiate it from the other members of the Acb complex. For this purpose, recA gene and ITS region between 16S and 23 S rRNA were amplified using Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 19,606 as a standard. A primer set rA-f (5΄-CCTGAATCTTCTGGTAAAAC-3΄) and rA-r (5΄-GTTTCTGGGCTGCCAAACATTAC-3΄) was used to amplify the highly conserved 425 bp region of recA gene, whereas ITS region (208 bp) was amplified by using primer set ITS-f (5΄-CATTATCACGGTAATTAGTG-3΄) and ITS-r (5΄-AGAGCACTGTGCACTTAAG-3΄) as reported previously [12, 13]. PCR reaction mixture (25 µL) included 50 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5 µM of each forward and reverse primer, 100 µM each dNTP, and 0.25U Taq polymerase. Overnight bacterial culture aliquots (1 µL) were used directly as a DNA template. The reaction was conducted at 95℃ for 7 min followed by 35 cycles of 94℃ for 30 s, 55℃ for 30 s, and 72℃ for 30 s. The final extension was carried out at 72℃ for 10 min. The amplified products were resolved on agarose gel (1.5% w/v) and observed under UV transilluminator after staining with ethidium bromide. A DNA ladder DLA 2000 (TaKaRa Bio Inc.) was also run to estimate the size of amplicons.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility of A. baumannii isolates to imipenem (10 µg), meropenem (10 µg), ampicillin/sulbactam (25/5 µg), ticarcillin/clavulanic acid (75/10 µg), cefepime (30 µg), cefoperazone, cefotaxime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), amikacin (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), tobramycin (10 µg), doxycycline (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), levofloxacin (5 µg), ofloxacin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (23.75/1.25 µg), and cefoperazone/sulbactam was determined by the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method. Zone diameters were interpreted according to CLSI guidelines 2024. Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 was used as a quality control strain.

The MAR index was calculated and interpreted according to Mir et al. [14] and Isaiah et al. [15] using the formula: a/b, where ‘a’ represents the number of antibiotics to which an isolate was resistant, and ‘b’ represents the total number of antibiotics tested. The minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of colistin and polymyxin B were calculated by the broth dilution method, while the E-test was employed for tigecycline.

PCR detection of 16S rRNA and bla-NDM-1 genes

A multiplex PCR was designed for amplification of both 16S rRNA and bla-NDM-1 genes for determination by using universal primer sets (Table S1). The PCR reaction mixture contained about 50 ng DNA template, Taq buffer, 100 µM of each dNTP, 1.5 units Taq polymerase, and 0.5 µM of each primer. The annealing temperature was 53 °C. The PCR products were analyzed using standard 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Sequencing, phylogenetic and diversity analysis

Amplicons of bla-NDM-1 and 16S rRNA genes were sequenced by Macrogen Inc. (South Korea). Forward and reverse reads were assembled into consensus sequences using DNA Dragon v1.5.0. Sequences were analyzed using NCBI BLAST for similarity search and mutation detection. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using MEGA software (https://www.megasoftware.net) to infer evolutionary relationships of bla-NDM-positive strains.

Protein structure prediction was carried out using PHYRE2. Sequence alignments, amino acid substitution analysis, and multiple nucleotide alignments were performed using DNA Star Lasergene v16.2.0.130, the NCBI BLASTn suite, Sequence Manipulation Suite, and Clustal Omega. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) and BioNJ algorithms with pairwise distances estimated by the maximum composite likelihood method.

Statistical analysis

The data was statistically analysed using SPSS version 25 and Microsoft Excel 2010. Data was analysed by using mean, median, mode, frequency, and percentage, and ± standard deviation. The age-wise and gender wise distribution of resistant A. baumannii strains was evaluated by Fisher’s exact test. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In-silico mutational analysis

Comparative structural analysis of wild-type and P28A mutant bla-NDM-1

The 3D structure of the wild-type bla-NDM-1 enzyme was predicted by a deep-learning-based protein structure prediction tool, AlphaFold colab (https://alphafoldserver.com/) [16]. The predicted model was visualized in PyMOL (version 2.4.0), wherein the P28A point mutation (Proline → Alanine at position 28) was introduced to generate the mutant structure. Energy minimization of the mutant model was subsequently performed to optimize the geometry and relieve any potential steric clashes. The wild-type and mutant structures were compared with each other to identify any local conformational changes, particularly in the N-terminal region containing residue 28. Conformational changes within structural elements such as α-helices and β-sheets closer to this position were evaluated for possible shifts, deviations, or changes in backbone geometry and side-chain orientations.

Model quality assessment using SAVES server

Structural validation of wild-type and mutant models of bla-NDM-1 was performed using the SAVES v6.0 server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) [17]. The uploaded PDB files of both models were analyzed through the modules ERRAT, VERIFY3D, and PROCHECK. ERRAT calculated the “overall quality factor” that provides higher scores for better reliability of a model based on nonbonded atomic interactions. VERIFY3D analyzed the compatibility of each 3D model with its amino acid sequence by generating 3D–1D profile scores. According to this analysis, ≥ 80% of residues should score ≥ 0.1 for a structure to be considered structurally acceptable. PROCHECK analyzed the stereochemical quality of the protein backbone and plotted a Ramachandran plot that suggests the distribution of the residues in “most favored,” “additional allowed,” “generously allowed,” and “disallowed” regions. Together, the above analyses provided an assurance of the overall structural correctness in both the wild-type and P28A mutant bla-NDM-1 proteins regarding residue environment and backbone geometry.

GOR IV secondary structural analysis

Secondary structure prediction was performed with the GOR IV algorithm (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/NPSA/npsa_gor4.html) [18]; a method using Bayesian statistics and sequence context to predict the proportions of α-helices, β-strands, and random coils in the amino-acid sequence. Both wild-type and P28A mutant sequences were submitted, and the predicted percentage composition of each structural class was compared in order to assess the mutation’s effect on secondary-structure composition and distribution.

Post-mutation stability and functional analysis

To evaluate the effect of the P28A substitution on the stability and function of the bla-NDM-1 enzyme, multiple computational mutational-effect prediction tools were utilized. The I-Mutant 3.0 server (https://folding.biofold.org/i-mutant/i-mutant3.0.html) [19] employs a support vector machine (SVM) algorithm to predict changes in protein stability (ΔΔG) based on either sequence or structure input. The mCSM tool (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/mcsm/) [20] uses graph-based signatures to quantify how point mutations affect protein stability. The SDM (Site Directed Mutator) server (https://www-cryst.bioc.cam.ac.uk/SDM/) [20] applies statistical potential energy functions to predict the stability impact of mutations. DUET (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/duet/) [21] integrates predictions from both mCSM and SDM to provide a consensus ΔΔG value for improved accuracy. The DynaMut web server (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/dynamut/) [22] combines normal mode analysis and graph-based signatures to assess both stability changes and alterations in protein flexibility. Furthermore, Mupro (https://mupro.proteomics.ics.uci.edu/) [23] employs sequence-based neural networks for the estimation of ΔΔG and prediction of stabilization or destabilization effect caused by a mutation, whereas SAAFEC-SEQ (https://compbio.clemson.edu/SAAFEC-SEQ/) [24] predicts free energy changes from sequence data alone. The effect of the mutation on the protein’s function is evaluated by using PROVEAN (https://provean.jcvi.org/) [25], which categorizes the variant as either “neutral” or “deleterious”. For assessing the probability of a mutation causing some functional or pathological effects, machine learning was employed through MutPred2 (http://mutpred.mutdb.org/) [26]. The wild-type bla-NDM-1 sequence as well as its variant P28A were evaluated for the entire set of algorithms. Comparison of obtained ΔΔG values and “stabilizing”, “destabilizing”, or “neutral” predictions was performed to obtain a consensus on the influence of the mutation.

In-vitro analysis

Among these culture-positive samples, 75 isolates (5.2%) were identified as Acinetobacter baumannii based on culture morphology and biochemical tests and were confirmed at the molecular level. Among them, the majority of the strains were obtained from CSF, followed by urine, wound, and pus (Table 1). Detection and characterization of bla-NDM

The bla-NDM-1 gene was detected in 12 (16%) isolates. Sequencing revealed a variant of NDM-1 with a C→G substitution at nucleotide position 82, causing a proline→alanine change at amino acid position 28. Six of the 12 bla-NDM-1 positive strains carried this substitution (Figure S1).

Based on bla-NDM-1 sequences, two clades (A and B) were identified. Clade B was smaller (four strains), while clade A contained six strains. Strains P6 and U8 formed an outgroup, showing low similarity to others (Fig. 3).

In-silico mutational analysis

Comparative structural analysis of wild-type and P28A mutant bla-NDM-1

Three-dimensional structures of the bla-NDM-1 enzyme were modeled using AlphaFold (wild-type), and the P28A mutation was introduced in PyMOL. The wild-type structure contains Proline at position 28 (Fig. 4A), while the mutant model shows substitution of Proline with Alanine (Fig. 4B). The P28 residue is in close proximity to the N-terminal region, with adjacent secondary structural elements involved in the maintenance of local folding. The present structural analysis suggested a localized change in conformation in the N-terminal region of bla-NDM-1 following the P28A substitution. Being a rigid, cyclic amino acid, proline generally confers bends and stabilizes folding along the protein backbone; hence, its substitution by the smaller and more flexible alanine may decrease the local structural rigidity. In the wild-type enzyme, Pro28 appears to contribute to the structural congruence of adjacent α-helices and β-sheets. On the other hand, in the mutant structure, there is a slight deviation observed in these regions, which may indicate an alteration in folding dynamics. From this result, the P28A mutation may subtly influence β-lactamase activity or protein stability. However, these observations are based on in silico predictions, requiring further experimental validation.

The overall secondary structure profile is, however, conserved, with only minimal differences compared to the wild-type model.

In contrast, the secondary structure of the mutant bla-NDM-1 (P28A) does not exhibit any substantial alterations in the distribution of helices and coiling probability within the N-terminal portion of the sequence, except for minor modifications in the intensity of helices and coiling probabilities. The replacement of the proline residue with an alanine in position 28 appears to promote helix formation locally, as shown by slight elevations in the helical probability in this particular region. Taken together, the results imply that the introduction of the secondary structural alterations due to the P28A mutation does not have much effect on the overall structure of the bla-NDM-1 protein. Nevertheless, these observations have been drawn from computational analysis, and further verification through experimental analysis would strengthen these conclusions.

Most of the results obtained by the majority of the methods (I-Mutant, Mcsm, SDM, DUET, Mupro, and SAAFEC-SEQ) indicate that the substitution P28A may destabilizes the protein, as evidenced by negative ΔΔG values (-0.09 to -0.83 kcal/mol) or qualitatively as “destabilization.” The destabilizing influence may be caused by the substitution of a relatively stable proline for a relatively unstable alanine residue.

Surprisingly, DynaMut predicts a positive ΔΔG of 0.339 kcal/mol, signifying a negligible impact on protein stability.This apparent discrepancy suggests that the additional flexibility contributed by alanine may enhance local adaptability without major impact on global folding.

Functionally, PROVEAN gave a score of 0.374, above the cutoff of -2.5, classifying the mutation as neutral, and thus it might not impair enzyme function. In addition, MutPred2 gave a low pathogenicity probability score of 0.194, which agrees with the mutation being non-damaging and conserved through evolution.

Taken together, these results suggest that while the P28A substitution has some effects on the thermodynamic stability of bla-NDM-1, it does not markedly affect its structural integrity or gross enzymatic activities. The enzyme remains functionally active, which also agrees with sequence and structural analysis that showed negligible conformational changes between wild-type and mutant proteins.

Discussion

Acinetobacter baumannii is associated with nosocomial infections, mostly involved in causing opportunistic infections, as well as pneumonia, infections associated with burns and wounds, respiratory infection, and many more. It has the ability to survive in extreme conditions, which makes it a challenge for hospital infection control to eradicate it. The organism has the ability to develop resistance against a variety of antimicrobials, due to which the World Health Organization considers it a threat to human health A. baumannii is an opprobrious pathogen that is associated with a broad range of infections, particularly in hospitalized patients [27]. Due to less susceptibility of antimicrobials, the organism is also responsible for the mortality of infected individuals; the mortality rate due to ventilator-associated pneumonia caused by A. baumannii ranges from 40 to 70%. Mortality rate of bloodstream infections due to A. baumannii ranges from 20 to 43%. Infections associated with burns caused by A. baumannii are up to 22% and mostly spread in military personnel with MDR of 53% [28–30].

The objectives of the study were to collect multidrug-resistant A. baumannii from clinical samples and perform a comparative phenotypic and genotypic analysis for the bla-NDM-1 gene. The study also aimed to determine the resistance profile through antimicrobial susceptibility testing and MAR index assessment, and make a confirmation of the presence and variants of bla-NDM-1 through the use of PCR amplification and sequencing. Further, the sequence analysis revealed a point mutation (C→G) that results in a P28A (Proline→Alanine) substitution in the bla-NDM-1 enzyme. In this regard, computational analyses through multiple in-silico bioinformatics platforms were performed for further understanding of the structural and functional implications of the identified mutation. This involved the prediction of the 3D structure of bla-NDM-1 through modeling and validation, with further analyses through secondary structure prediction, stability analysis, and functional impact assessment by GOR IV, I-Mutant, DynaMut, mCSM, SDM, DUET, and MutPred2. These in-vitro and in-silico complementary studies were aimed at obtaining an overview of how the P28A mutation actually influences the stability, flexibility, and potential role of the enzyme in carbapenem resistance.

The fact that 75 A. baumannii strains were isolated from a total of 1440 clinical samples highlights its importance as an emerging nosocomial pathogen. Its greater occurrence in men and individuals within the age bracket of 41–60 years is consistent with earlier studies which showed greater predisposition for older individuals owing to the likelihood of prolonged stay in hospitals. Isolation was largely from CSF, wounds, and urine samples, demonstrating the adaptability of the pathogen across various infection sites, hence its capacity to infect soft tissues and invasive devices among in-patients. This is backed up by the study conducted in Kenya in which the vast majority of Acinetobacter species were isolated from wounds (about 22%) and urine (about 16%), while males accounted for about 52% of the isolates, illustrating the trend observed here. This reflects the opportunistic and versatile nature of A. baumannii infection [31].

Antibiotic resistance profiling showed that there was broad resistance to almost all of the tested β-lactam antibiotics, cephalosporins, and aminoglycosides but no resistance against tigecycline, colistin, and polymyxin B. Clusters of resistance profiles suggested that there were similarities between the resistance profiles of carbapenems and aminoglycosides; this may be attributed to co-selection or genetic determinants involved in resistance. The absence of any correlation between gender or sample type and the resistance profile indicated that antibiotic resistance is genetically determined and is independent of other factors. This was consistent with a previous study, where the authors stated that 35% (7/20) of CRAB isolates harbored the bla-NDM gene, emphasizing the significance of genetic factors responsible for resistance and its influence on multi-drug resistance profiles [32].

The dominance of XDR strains (69.3%) in the current research suggests that there is an imminent resistance problem brought about by A. baumannii in hospitals. This is because of the high MAR value, where the average value is 0.24, and more than half of the strains exceed the 0.2 threshold, suggesting that there is always a selective pressure from antibiotics and constant transfers of resistance genes such as bla-NDM. The findings in the current research are consistent with previous findings on “A Global View on Carbapenem-Resistant A. baumannii,” which looked at the same isolate type but from 114 hospitals in 47 countries. It was revealed that A. baumannii still maintains its multidrug-resistant characteristic even with high carbapenem resistance [33].

The observed low MIC values for colistin and polymyxin B demonstrate their high efficiency in treating A. baumannii infections in accordance with the data obtained in worldwide studies regarding their effectiveness in fighting MDR and XDR pathogens. It is worth mentioning that according to the results of one study dedicated to polymyxins, MIC testing can be prone to misinterpretation regarding the resistance of A. baumannii bacteria to these drugs; physiological medium testing confirmed the sensitivity of some of them that had been considered to be resistant before [34].

Detection of bla-NDM in 16% of all isolates suggests further distribution of bla-NDM in the A. baumannii population. In addition, a mutation of nucleotide 82 from C to G associated with a change from proline 28 to alanine was found in six isolates, implying the potential emergence of an NDM-1 variant. This is in line with another genomics-based study published recently on the common occurrence of bla-NDM-1 in 2,576 A. baumannii genomes with diversification by SNPs [35], as well as a systematic literature review of SNP effects on the formation of new variants in which changes from C to G were observed at position P28A [36].

Phylogenetic reconstruction on the basis of 16S rRNA gene sequences revealed that all bla-NDM-1 positive isolates were grouped into a highly conserved Acinetobacter baumannii lineage with high bootstrap support. The high genetic similarity of the isolates indicates the few sequence variations and can be evidence of clonal spread of bla-NDM-1 carrying strains. Instead, the existence of bla-NDM-1 among the closely related isolates can also be the result of horizontal gene transfer by the means of mobile genetic elements like plasmids. These results emphasize the duality of the clonal amplification and gene mobility in the dissemination of carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii populations. Nevertheless, because of conserved 16S rRNA gene, more precise genomic methods are needed to differentiate between such mechanisms. This agrees with a previous study that analyzed 1,021 bla-NDM-positive genomes and found increasing phylogenetic diversity, host-genus shifts from Acinetobacter to Enterobacteriaceae, and expanding transmission routes linking livestock, environmental, and human reservoirs. These results together underscore the high mobility and adaptive potential of bla-NDM-1 in driving the global spread of multidrug resistance [37].

Conformations of the wild-type protein compared to those in the P28A mutant bla-NDM-1 enzymes revealed small alterations in the enzyme, specifically caused by the change in residues from proline at position 28 to alanine. Given that proline is a cyclic residue, which tends to limit backbone movement, changing it into an alanine – an amino acid that tends to promote helices – caused small changes in the secondary structure, increasing mobility. Though this residue is away from the catalytic site, small propagation effects may cause stability problems for the enzyme. Peripheral modifications have been shown to influence the efficiency of enzymes indirectly through dynamic effects on protein folding. The findings from above are consistent with earlier studies that indicate that mutations to inactive sites of enzymes such as NDM-4 and NDM-5 might be a strategy for making the enzyme more adaptive to acquire increased resistance or persistence. In an analysis conducted by Van Thanh Tran et al., a total of 35 one point mutants of NDM-1, which includes positions 122 and 124, were analyzed and discovered that mutations, even those away from the active site, impacted ligand binding and substrate inhibitor affinity and hydrolysis dynamics [38].

The validation conducted using the SAVES server confirmed that both wild-type and mutant bla-NDM-1 models possess very good stereochemistry and geometry, which makes them accurate models. High scores above 90% obtained from the ERRAT test represent proper non-bonded interactions and therefore backbone integrity, whereas 3D-1D compatibility was slightly increased in the mutant with 82.59% of the residues having score values above 0.1. The Ramachandran plot analysis carried out using PROCHECK shows that both models are well-folded models; however, there is slightly decreased favored regions of 87.3% in the mutant compared to 88.2% of the wild type. Based on these observations, it is clear that the P28A point mutation only brings about a subtle change in flexibility of the protein and does not influence its stability in any significant way. Moreover, the structural fitness of bla-NDM-1 has already been determined earlier to be 89.7% for favored residues in the Ramachandran plot, an ERRAT score of 84.40, and 96.68% residues with 3D-1D score greater than 0.2, reinforcing the fact that both the wild type and the mutated protein models were structurally sound [39].

The secondary structure of GOR IV showed that wild-type and P28A mutant bla-NDM-1 proteins retained highly conserved secondary structures, with only a slight increase in α-helical content and minor reduction of random coils; this is consistent with alanine being a helix-promoting amino acid while proline is a helix-breaking amino acid. This indicates that there is a tightening of the structure locally without disturbing the structure of the whole protein molecule. The slight variations in the structure might increase the efficiency of local folding while not interfering with the catalytic activity of the enzyme. These findings are consistent with a review conducted on the variations of NDM, which found that small variations in amino acids located away from the active site do not affect the overall folding of the enzyme but introduce slight variations in its conformation [40].

In light of the stability prediction results, it becomes clear that there is an overall correlation between the thermodynamic and biological effects of the P28A amino acid change. The majority of predictors, like I-Mutant, mCSM, SDM, DUET, Mupro, and SAAFEC-SEQ, have indicated a destabilization effect with slight negative ΔΔG values. Nevertheless, DynaMut has produced a completely contradictory result, demonstrating a small positive ΔΔG value of 0.339 kcal/mol and hence a positive effect on the stability of the protein through enhancing its flexibility. It is due to the fact that the mutation possesses two opposite sides, which means that while the mutation can affect thermodynamic stability, it can also increase the enzyme’s ability to adopt new conformations to operate effectively in stressful conditions. Such computational predictions have been shown to support the results of experiments by Düzgün [41], who studied mutations of alanine type (P28A), their effects on the enzyme resistance without any decrease in its catalytic activity. As mentioned by Börjesson et al., this type of mutation can change the behavior of the enzyme, but at the same time allows the bacteria to resist antibiotics. Both studies, therefore, show that peripheral mutations, such as P28A, help to find the right balance between protein stability and adaptability in the context of MDR A. baumannii [41]. Even though the P28A replacement in bla-NDM-1 was assessed by applying computational methods, the effects of the mutation on carbapenem resistance had not been established by comparative MIC. Thus, the pathophysiological consequences of this mutation are still prognostic. Its role in resistance needs to be confirmed with further studies that include phenotypic assays and enzymatic characterizations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study provided broad phenotypic, genotypic, and computational insights into multidrug-resistant A. baumannii isolates, demonstrating a high prevalence of XDR strains, 69.3%, with MAR indices > 0.2, pointing to strong antibiotic selective pressure. The bla-NDM-1 gene was found in 8% of strains. Sequencing further identified a putative bla-NDM-1 variant with a substitution of C→G at nucleotide position 82, leading to a P28A amino acid change that was observed in 50% of bla-NDM-1 positive strains. This was further supported by in-silico mutational analysis that showed the P28A substitution to introduce slight conformational changes, with a reduced thermodynamic stability, increased local flexibility, but without any effect on catalysis. Phylogenetic analysis further identified multiple evolutionary lineages and possible events of interspecies gene transfer. Whole-genome sequencing combined with molecular docking and enzyme dynamics studies are required to further validate the structural effects of variants in bla-NDM and facilitate the rational design of effective NDM inhibitors for clinical application. However, small number of bla-NDM-1 isolates harboring P28A substitutions may limit to make conclusive conclusions regarding its functional and evolutionary role. Further studies are required with larger sample size.
