SEC14L2 couples chaperone-mediated autophagy to microtubule stability by targeting Stathmin 1
Authors: Guangyuan Li, Hanfang Xu, Bo Gong, Shunji Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microtubule dynamic instability underpins cellular architecture, division, and intracellular trafficking, yet how selective proteolytic pathways tune core microtubule regulators remains incompletely understood. Here, we identify SEC14L2 (SEC14-like lipid binding 2), a multidomain lipid transfer protein, as a determinant of microtubule organization and cellular architecture by limiting the accumulation of the microtubule-destabilizing factor Stathmin 1 (STMN1). We further show that STMN1 is a substrate of chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) and that SEC14L2 is required for efficient CMA-dependent STMN1 turnover. SEC14L2 loss is associated with reduced CMA activity, STMN1 stabilization, collapse of the microtubule network, and perinuclear organelle clustering. In breast cancer cell models, perturbation of this SEC14L2-CMA-STMN1 axis alters responses to microtubule-targeting agents.

Introduction

Microtubules are dynamic cytoskeletal polymers composed of tubulin subunits that play fundamental roles in maintaining cellular architecture, intracellular transport, cell division, and migration1,2. The precise regulation of microtubule dynamics, balancing phases of polymerization and depolymerization, is critical for cellular homeostasis. Dysregulation of these dynamics is associated with various pathological conditions, including neurodegenerative diseases and cancer3,4. Consequently, natural compounds that target and disrupt microtubule dynamics have been widely employed in cancer chemotherapy5, with paclitaxel being a prominent example that acts by stabilizing microtubules and halting cell division6. Understanding the intricate molecular machinery that governs microtubule homeostasis is therefore crucial for both fundamental cell biology and clinical applications.

The dynamic architecture of the microtubule network is governed by a delicate balance between stabilizing and destabilizing forces, orchestrated by microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs)2. Structural MAPs, such as MAP2 and Tau, directly bind to and reinforce the tubulin lattice7. In contrast, regulatory MAPs, including plus-end tracking proteins (EB and CLIP families) and depolymerizing factors such as Stathmins, cooperate and compete at microtubule ends to determine whether microtubules grow or shrink7. Post-translational modifications finely tune their activities to coordinate cell division, trafficking, and polarity, whereas dysregulation of these mechanisms disrupts cell organization and tissue homeostasis2,7. Chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) is a selective lysosomal pathway that degrades cytosolic proteins bearing KFERQ-like motifs through recognition by Hsc70 and translocation across the lysosomal membrane via LAMP2A8,9. With respect to the cytoskeleton, CMA has been shown to degrade MAPs such as Tau, especially in neurons, linking selective lysosomal proteolysis to microtubule dynamics and axonal transport10,11. Beyond Tau, systematic evidence that other pivotal microtubule regulators are routed through CMA to control microtubule homeostasis remains sparse, and the upstream factors that bias those substrates toward CMA versus alternative turnover routes (ubiquitin-proteasome, macroautophagy, endosomal microautophagy) remain incompletely defined.

SEC14L2, a member of the phosphatidylinositol transfer protein (PITP) family, is known for transporting lipids between membranes12,13. As a multidomain PITP14, SEC14L2 has also been implicated in membrane remodeling15,16, Wnt/Ca2+ signaling17, and regulation of Rab GTPase state18. This functional breadth has made it difficult to define the pathways through which SEC14L2 controls specific cellular outputs. In cancer, studies on SEC14L2 remain limited and context dependent: reduced SEC14L2 expression is associated with malignant features in some tumors19–21, whereas findings in other contexts are more limited or less consistent22. The mechanisms linking SEC14L2 status to disease phenotypes remain unclear.

In our previous work, SEC14L2 promoted sorting endosome fission15, and its loss shifted endosomes towards the juxtanuclear region. Because broader organelle redistribution accompanied this phenotype, we asked whether SEC14L2 might regulate microtubule organization. In this study, we identify SEC14L2 as a regulator of microtubule organization and establish Stathmin 1 (STMN1) as a key downstream effector. STMN1 is a conserved microtubule destabilizer that sequesters α/β-tubulin heterodimers23, and its abundance is tightly linked to mitotic control24,25. Although STMN1 transcription is regulated by factors such as E2F and TP5326,27, the mechanisms governing its protein turnover remain less clear and, in some contexts, controversial28,29. We show that STMN1 is a chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) substrate and that SEC14L2 promotes its turnover through this pathway. By limiting STMN1 accumulation, SEC14L2 helps preserve microtubule integrity, and this mechanism is also evident in breast cancer cell models.

Results

SEC14L2 depletion alters microtubule organization and organelle positioning

To investigate whether SEC14L2 influences microtubule-dependent cellular architecture, we first analyzed SEC14L2 knockout (KO) COS-7 cells, which we previously established as a model with superior morphology for imaging15. Transmission electron microscopy revealed pronounced clustering of organelles in the juxtanuclear region of SEC14L2 KO cells (Fig. 1a). Immunostaining of the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, and Golgi apparatus confirmed this condensed architecture (Fig. 1b, c). Although overall Golgi volume was not obviously altered, the Golgi spreading angle, an inverse measure of perinuclear compaction, was significantly reduced (Fig. 1c–e). Because organelle positioning depends on microtubules, we next examined the microtubule network and observed a striking increase in aster-like organization30 after SEC14L2 loss (Fig. 1b, f). Total microtubule intensity was largely unchanged, but the fraction of tubulin signal concentrated within the aster region was significantly increased (Fig. 1f–h), indicating network condensation rather than bulk microtubule loss. Re-expression of SEC14L2-Flag markedly alleviated this phenotype (Fig. 1i, j). Consistent with restoration of microtubule organization, SEC14L2 re-expression also reversed mitochondrial condensation in KO cells (Supplementary Fig. S1a, b). To ensure these findings were not restricted to a single cell background, we performed functional validation in SEC14L2 KO MCF7 breast cancer cells. Similar alterations in organelle distribution and microtubule architecture were observed (Supplementary Fig. S1c), further supporting this conclusion. SEC14L2 supports the assembly and stabilization of microtubules

The organization of microtubule networks critically depends on their nucleation and assembly31. To determine which process SEC14L2 influences, we performed nocodazole-induced microtubule depolymerization and washout assay32 to assess microtubule regeneration on both the centrosome and Golgi apparatus, the latter of which has shown a strong association with SEC14L2 localization15. Upon removal of nocodazole, microtubules nucleated from the centrosome and Golgi ministacks in both wild-type and SEC14L2 KO cells, indicating that SEC14L2 depletion does not affect microtubule nucleation (Fig. 2a, b). However, at the same time point after washout, significantly shorter centrosomal and Golgi-derived microtubules were evident in SEC14L2 KO cells (Fig. 2c, d), accompanied by notably reduced elongation velocity compared to wild-type cells (Fig. 2e). These findings suggest that depletion of SEC14L2 impairs microtubule assembly. It is noteworthy that nocodazole treatment induces microtubule depolymerization, leading to Golgi fragmentation into ministacks33. Post-nocodazole washout, wild-type cells exhibited rapid reassembly of Golgi ministacks and reformation of the Golgi apparatus, whereas SEC14L2 KO cells showed significant impairment in both the speed and extent of ministack convergence (Supplementary Fig. S2a–c). To explore microtubule assembly defects in the absence of SEC14L2, we performed EB1 staining, a microtubule plus-end binding protein34, to label and examine the growth of microtubules. In wild-type COS-7 cells, EB1 exhibited a scattered distribution throughout the cell, whereas in SEC14L2 KO cells, EB1 comets were significantly enriched around the centrosome (Fig. 2f, g). This finding indicated that the microtubule aster condensation was formed by a cluster of microtubules with short lengths. Despite this altered organization, the number of EB1 comets remained comparable between wild-type and SEC14L2 KO cells, suggesting that microtubule nucleation and overall microtubule numbers were unaffected (Fig. 2h). Next, we analyzed the directionality of microtubule growth by measuring the angle between EB1-labeled microtubules and the radial axis35. Statistical analysis of the growth angle distribution revealed a significant reduction in the proportion of microtubules extending at wider angles in SEC14L2 KO cells compared to wild-type cells (Fig. 2i, j). These data demonstrate altered microtubule orientation in SEC14L2 KO cells, characterized by impaired peripheral targeting and pericentrosomal accumulation, likely resulting from altered microtubule dynamics.

Next, we assessed microtubule stability by measuring their resistance to nocodazole-induced depolymerization36. Stable microtubules were visualized through a detergent pre-extraction process performed prior to fixation, which removed tubulin dimers and short filaments37,38. In wild-type cells, microtubules exhibited greater stability, remaining more intact and resistant to nocodazole treatment compared to SEC14L2 KO cells (Fig. 2k–m). Notably, SEC14L2 re-expression in knockout cells not only fully restored microtubule stability but even enhanced it beyond wild-type levels, resulting in significantly greater microtubule retention (Fig. 2k–m). Additionally, we examined α-tubulin-K40 acetylation, a marker of stable microtubules39, and found that SEC14L2 KO cells exhibited more severe microtubule depolymerization upon colchicine treatment, with only a small fraction of stable microtubules retained compared to wild-type cells (Supplementary Fig. S2d–f). Together, these results highlight the critical role of SEC14L2 in maintaining microtubule dynamics and stability.

SEC14L2 deficiency disrupts the lysosomal degradation of STMN1

To identify mechanisms through which SEC14L2 influences microtubules, we performed semi-quantitative mass spectrometry on SEC14L2 immunoprecipitates and detected 29 microtubule-related proteins among 824 candidates (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 1). STMN1, a well-established microtubule destabilizer23, was among the top candidates, suggesting a potential link to the SEC14L2 phenotype. Semi-endogenous co-immunoprecipitation further supported the association between SEC14L2 and STMN1 (Fig. 3b). STMN1 protein levels were significantly elevated in SEC14L2 KO cells (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. S3a–c), whereas SEC14L2 overexpression reduced STMN1 abundance (Supplementary Fig. S3d, e). Because STMN1 is regulated during the cell cycle24, we examined whether the increase could be explained by altered cell-cycle distribution. Flow cytometry revealed only a slight shift in the S-phase fraction after SEC14L2 depletion (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. S3f), and STMN1 remained elevated after synchronization of wild-type and KO cells to the same phase (Fig. 3f, g and Supplementary Fig. S3g). These findings suggest that STMN1 accumulation is not simply a secondary consequence of cell-cycle redistribution. To determine the regulatory mechanism of STMN1 by SEC14L2, we examined STMN1 mRNA levels in SEC14L2 KO cells. RT-qPCR results revealed no significant change in STMN1 transcript levels (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. S3h), suggesting that SEC14L2 regulates STMN1 through post-transcriptional mechanisms. To define the pathway responsible for STMN1 degradation, we tested inhibitors of distinct proteolytic routes. While inhibition of proteasome with MG132 treatment effectively induced the accumulation of global ubiquitinated proteins (Supplementary Fig. S3i), it failed to stabilize STMN1 or delay its turnover (Fig. 3k, l). Consistently, SEC14L2 depletion did not markedly alter STMN1 ubiquitination (Fig. 3m), arguing against a major role for proteasome-dependent degradation. In contrast, lysosomal inhibition with either chloroquine (CQ) or bafilomycin A1 (BafA1) markedly increased STMN1 abundance (Fig. 3k, l). When CHX chase assays were combined with either MG132 or CQ treatment, CQ significantly delayed STMN1 degradation over time, whereas MG132 had little effect (Fig. 3n, o). Furthermore, CQ increased STMN1 levels in wild-type cells but caused no significant additional accumulation in SEC14L2 KO cells (Fig. 3p, q), consistent with impaired lysosome-dependent STMN1 turnover after SEC14L2 loss. In addition, inhibition of macroautophagy with 3-methyladenine (3-MA) had no significant effect on STMN1 levels (Fig. 3k, l), suggesting that macroautophagy is not the dominant degradation route in this setting.

STMN1 undergoes lysosomal degradation by chaperone-mediated autophagy

Given the established role of SEC14L2 in endosomal and lysosomal membrane remodeling15,16, we next examined whether SEC14L2-associated vesicles might connect STMN1 to lysosomal degradation. Immunoisolation of SEC14L2-positive vesicles (Supplementary Fig. S4a) followed by semi-quantitative mass spectrometry (Supplementary Data 2) identified multiple lysosomal proteins, including LAMP1, cathepsins, V-ATPase subunits, together with STMN1 (Fig. 4a). Reciprocally, STMN1 pulldown assays recovered LAMP1 and SEC14L2 (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 3). Confocal imaging further showed colocalization of SEC14L2 and STMN1 with the lysosomal marker LAMP1 (Fig. 4c–e). We also detected an association between STMN1 and Hsc70 by co-immunoprecipitation (Fig. 4f). Because Hsc70 recognizes KFERQ-like motifs and can direct substrates to either endosomal microautophagy (eMI) or CMA40, we examined STMN1 sequence features relevant to Hsc70 recognition. Motif analysis identified four canonical and three acetylation-generated KFERQ-like motifs41 in STMN1 (Fig. 4g). To distinguish between eMI and CMA, we compared STMN1 localization with the CMA receptor LAMP2A and the eMI component VPS4A. In both COS-7 and MCF7 cells, STMN1 showed significantly higher colocalization with LAMP2A than with VPS4A (Fig. 4h, i). Moreover, although our proteomic analyses did not specifically resolve the LAMP2A isoform, LAMP2 was consistently detected, whereas ESCRT components such as VPS4 and TSG101 were not detected in either the STMN1 pulldown or the SEC14L2-vesicle proteome (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Data 2, 3). To functionally validate CMA involvement in STMN1 degradation, we isolated CMA-active lysosomes42 from starved mouse livers (Supplementary Fig. S4b). Lysosomal integrity was assessed using hexosaminidase activity assays43, and only intact preparations with less than 10% membrane breakage were used in downstream uptake assays (Supplementary Fig. S4c). These lysosomes were incubated with recombinant STMN1 and the canonical CMA substrate MAPT/Tau11 in the presence or absence of lysosomal protease inhibitors (PI). A significant accumulation of MAPT and STMN1 was detected in lysosomes treated with PI compared to untreated controls, indicating successful translocation of STMN1 into the lysosomal lumen for degradation (Fig. 4j, k). Critically, systematic mutation of the glutamine residues in STMN1’s KFERQ-like motifs to alanine (Q > A) significantly abolished its translocation into the lysosomal lumen (Fig. 4j, k). Co-immunoprecipitation assays further revealed a marked reduction in the binding affinity between Hsc70 and the KFERQ-mutated STMN1 compared to the wild-type protein (Fig. 4l), confirming the essential role of these motifs in CMA-mediated recognition and degradation. These findings strongly support the involvement of CMA-active lysosomes in mediating STMN1 degradation.

SEC14L2 deficiency impairs CMA-mediated degradation of STMN1

We next asked whether SEC14L2 regulates CMA-dependent STMN1 turnover. Knockdown of the core CMA factors LAMP2A and Hsc70 increased STMN1 abundance (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. S5a). Consistent with the suppression of CMA, levels of the established CMA substrate PARK744 were similarly elevated (Fig. 5a). By contrast, depletion of the macroautophagy component ATG545 or eMI component VPS4A had little effect on STMN1 levels (Fig. 5a, b). Re-expression of SEC14L2 in SEC14L2 KO cells lowered STMN1 levels, and this effect was lost when LAMP2A or Hsc70 were depleted (Fig. 5c, d). In contrast, inhibition of macroautophagy or eMI had little effect on SEC14L2-dependent STMN1 reduction (Fig. 5c, d). To define how SEC14L2 promotes STMN1 turnover, we generated a series of SEC14L2 truncation mutants (Fig. 5e) and tested their ability to restore STMN1 degradation in rescue assays. Deletion of either the SEC14 or GOLD domain prevented SEC14L2 from reducing STMN1 levels (Fig. 5f, g), indicating that both domains contribute to this pathway. Domain-mapping co-immunoprecipitation further showed that STMN1 binding depended specifically on the C-terminal GOLD domain, whereas mutants lacking the N-terminal region or the SEC14 domain retained STMN1 association (Fig. 5h, i). Because endogenous Hsc70 co-immunoprecipitated with both STMN1 and SEC14L2 (Fig. 4f), we examined whether SEC14L2 influences the formation of the STMN1-Hsc70 complex. The interaction between Hsc70 and STMN1 was reduced in SEC14L2 KO cells and was partially restored by the addition of purified His-SEC14L2 (Fig. 5j, k). These findings support a model in which SEC14L2 facilitates recognition and/or delivery of STMN1 by the CMA machinery. To test this idea more directly, we performed lysosomal fractionation in the presence of lysosomal inhibition42,43 to allow substrate accumulation. In wild-type cells, blocking lysosomal proteolysis led to accumulation of STMN1 in the lysosomal fraction, consistent with ongoing lysosomal uptake and degradation of STMN1 (Fig. 5l, m). In SEC14L2 KO cells, by contrast, STMN1 was reduced in the lysosomal fraction and showed little additional accumulation after inhibitor treatment (Fig. 5l, m). Together, these data suggest that SEC14L2 promotes efficient lysosomal targeting and/or uptake of STMN1.

We next asked whether SEC14L2 also contributes to CMA control more broadly through regulation of lysosomal homeostasis. Previous studies have shown that SEC14L2 regulates endolysosomal phosphoinositide dynamics15,16, disruption of which is frequently associated with lysosome enlargement and abnormal cholesterol accumulation46,47. Because lysosomal cholesterol accumulation is known to suppress CMA48,49, we examined whether SEC14L2 loss also perturbs this aspect of lysosomal homeostasis. Filipin staining revealed marked cholesterol accumulation in LAMP2A-positive lysosomal compartments in SEC14L2 KO cells (Fig. 5n and Supplementary Fig. S5b). We then monitored CMA activity using the KFERQ-Dendra2 reporter, which contains a KFERQ-like CMA-targeting motif and redistributes from the cytosol to CMA-active lysosomes, forming fluorescent puncta upon CMA activation50. SEC14L2-deficient cells showed significantly fewer reporter puncta than wild-type cells (Fig. 5o, p), consistent with reduced CMA activity. Together, these results support a model in which SEC14L2 contributes to CMA-dependent STMN1 degradation through both substrate handling and maintenance of a lysosomal environment permissive for CMA.

Discussion

SEC14L2 is a multidomain phosphatidylinositol transfer protein with functions that extend beyond canonical lipid transfer14. Previous work implicated SEC14L2 in membrane remodeling and signaling pathways14–16, but how these activities are coupled to specific cellular outputs has remained unclear. Here we identify a role for SEC14L2 in maintaining microtubule organization and cellular architecture. Our data support a model in which SEC14L2 limits accumulation of the microtubule destabilizer STMN1 by promoting its CMA-dependent turnover (Fig. 7i). In this framework, SEC14L2 likely influences STMN1 degradation through two separable but potentially complementary mechanisms: it facilitates association of STMN1 with the CMA chaperone Hsc70, and it helps preserve a lysosomal lipid environment compatible with efficient CMA.

STMN1 has long been recognized as a central regulator of microtubule dynamics23, and high STMN1 expression is associated with aggressive malignancy and poor therapeutic response in many solid tumors25. However, although transcriptional regulation of STMN1 has been studied extensively26,27, its protein turnover remains incompletely defined and appears to be context dependent28,29,53. In our system, lysosomal inhibition by CQ or BafA1, but not proteasome inhibition by MG132, stabilized STMN1. More importantly, STMN1 was imported into isolated CMA-active lysosomes in a KFERQ-like-motif-dependent manner and showed reduced Hsc70 binding when these motifs were mutated. Together, these findings identify STMN1 as a CMA substrate and support CMA as a major route of STMN1 turnover under the conditions examined here.

CMA selectively degrades cytosolic proteins bearing KFERQ-like motifs through Hsc70-mediated delivery to the lysosomal receptor LAMP2A8,9. Our data suggest that one contribution of SEC14L2 is to maintain a lysosomal membrane environment permissive for this process. LAMP2A multimerization, a rate-limiting step in CMA, is influenced by lysosomal membrane microdomains and is inhibited by cholesterol accumulation48,49,54. SEC14L2 has been implicated in the regulation of endolysosomal phosphoinositide composition15,16, and disruption of these lipids is frequently accompanied by altered cholesterol trafficking and lysosome enlargement16,46,47. Consistent with this framework, loss of the SEC14 domain impaired SEC14L2-dependent control of STMN1 levels, and SEC14L2-deficient cells accumulated cholesterol in lysosomes while showing reduced reporter-based CMA activity and impaired lysosomal STMN1 targeting. Although these data do not define the precise lipid species or membrane events responsible, they support the idea that SEC14L2 sustains CMA by preserving lysosomal lipid homeostasis.

At the same time, the physical relationship between SEC14L2, Hsc70, and STMN1 suggests a second layer of regulation at the level of substrate handling. The GOLD domain of SEC14L2, a module often implicated in protein-protein interactions18,55, was required for STMN1 binding and for SEC14L2-dependent reduction of STMN1 abundance. Moreover, SEC14L2 deficiency weakened the Hsc70-STMN1 interaction, whereas purified SEC14L2 partially restored it. These findings are consistent with the possibility that SEC14L2 facilitates STMN1 presentation to the CMA machinery. Such a role would fit with emerging evidence that accessory factors and co-chaperones, such as BAG and DnaJ/Hsp40 family members, can influence substrate recognition, unfolding, or delivery during CMA56,57.

Based on these observations, we favor a working model in which SEC14L2 coordinates both lysosomal membrane state and substrate presentation to promote efficient STMN1 turnover. Whether SEC14L2 is physically anchored at lysosomal sites during this process, whether it acts directly as a scaffold for the STMN1-Hsc70 complex, and whether it exerts similar effects on additional CMA substrates remain open questions. Defining these aspects will be important for understanding the scope and selectivity of SEC14L2 function in CMA.

Reduced SEC14L2 expression has been linked to poorer outcomes in multiple breast cancer settings19,21, but the underlying mechanism has remained uncertain. Our data indicate that the SEC14L2-CMA-STMN1 pathway operates in breast cancer cell models and modulates drug-response phenotypes in vitro. These findings suggest one route by which SEC14L2 status may influence disease-relevant behavior, while the central contribution of the present work is mechanistic. More broadly, our study connects a lipid transfer protein to selective lysosomal proteostasis and microtubule homeostasis, and it identifies STMN1 turnover as a previously unrecognized point of control within this network.

Immunofluorescence and filipin staining

Generally, cells seeded on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h, and stained with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, cells were stained with secondary antibodies for 1 h. After 3 times washing, cells were imaged under confocal microscopy.

For microtubule staining, washing, and fixation were performed at 37 °C using prewarmed 4% PFA for 15 min. Specifically, for EB1 immunostaining, cells were washed with prewarmed PBS, fixed in −20 °C pre-cooled methanol for 5 min, and then subjected to gradient rehydration in PBS. For nocodazole treatment and nocodazole washout assays, soluble tubulin proteins were released through a pre-extraction process38 by a 10–30 s incubation in BRB80 extraction buffer (80 mM PIPES-KOH, pH 6.8; 1 mM MgCl2; 1 mM EGTA; 0.05% Triton X-100) before fixation in 4% PFA. All groups of cell coverslips were simultaneously subjected to nocodazole addition, washing, and pre-extraction treatment in a 6-well plate to avoid time and temperature differences. All processes before fixation should be performed at 37 °C.

For the imaging of subcellular localization of SEC14L2 and STMN115,16, cells were treated with 20 μM digitonin for 30 s to permeabilize the plasma membrane and release the soluble protein before fixation with 4% PFA.

For filipin and LAMP2A co-staining59, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min, washed with PBS, and incubated with 0.5 mg/mL filipin for 30 min. After blocking in 1% BSA/PBS, cells were incubated with LAMP2A antibody overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, cells were stained with secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. To preserve the signal, cells were re-incubated with filipin for 1 h, washed, and subjected to confocal imaging.

Image acquisition and processing

Images in this paper were acquired using the Andor Dragonfly confocal system with a Nikon Ti2 inverted microscope. The images are shown as maximum intensity projection stacks for 5–6 μm from the bottom of the cell with 0.2 μm intervals per section. Image processing and quantification analysis were performed using ImageJ.

For microtubule aster analysis, the ImageJ threshold function was used to segment the condensed aster region, and tubulin fluorescence within the aster and across the whole cell was measured to calculate the aster-to-total intensity ratio. In nocodazole washout assays, centrosomal- and Golgi-derived microtubules were manually traced in ImageJ using the NeuronJ plugin60 from the centrosome or individual GM130-positive Golgi ministacks to the distal microtubule tip, and the resulting trajectories were used to quantify microtubule length. To measure growth directionality, the radial axis was defined as the line connecting the cell center to the EB1 comet tip; the angle between this axis and the EB1-labeled microtubule trajectory was then measured for each comet. In nocodazole stability assays, the remaining microtubules in COS-7 cells were manually traced with NeuronJ. In MCF7 cells, residual microtubules were segmented using the ImageJ threshold function and quantified as relative microtubule density.

Mitochondrial condensation was analyzed as previously described61. Mitochondrial morphology was mapped using the skeleton function, and the mean number of mitochondrial junctions was quantified using ImageJ. For Golgi reassembly analysis upon nocodazole washout, the Golgi coverage was manually labeled based on the outermost distribution of Golgi ministacks. For colocalization analysis, the Fiji plugin Coloc 2 was used, and Manders’ colocalization coefficients were reported.

Cultures were grown in LB medium to an OD600 of 0.6-0.8, and protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG for 6 h at 37 °C. Cells were harvested, resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 150 mM NaCl; 5% Glycerol; 1 mM PMSF), and subjected to sonication. Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 45 min, the supernatant was incubated with glutathione sepharose 4B resin for 1 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed with high-salt lysis buffer (500 mM NaCl), followed by washes with lysis buffer. Prescission protease was added to the resin and incubated overnight with rotation to cleave the GST tag. Then, the elution containing target proteins was collected.

Immunopurification of SEC14L2 vesicles

The isolation of the SEC14L2 compartment was performed as previously described15. In brief, 293T cells expressing SEC14L2-Flag were harvested in cold PBS. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min and resuspended in isotonic buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4; 250 mM sucrose; 1 mM EDTA). Cells were homogenized by 20 passes through a 27-gauge needle (90027, Hamilton). Nuclei and cell debris were removed by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was incubated with anti-Flag magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher) for 4–6 h at 4 °C. After five washes with isotonic buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, SEC14L2 vesicles were competitively eluted using 3xFlag peptides (TargetMol). SEC14L2-bounded membranes were pelleted by centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. Pellets were resuspended in Laemmli sample buffer and subjected to mass spectrometry compartment identification.

Isolation of CMA-active lysosomes

CMA-active lysosomes were isolated from mouse liver using a modified Histodenz gradient method42,63. Briefly, C57BL/6J male mice (Genpharm Inc.) aged 8–10 weeks were starved for 36 h prior to liver extraction. Approximately 5 g of livers were washed and homogenized in 3 volumes of 250 mM sucrose using a Dounce homogenizer (Wheaton) with 15 strokes. The homogenate was centrifuged at 500 × g for 2 min to remove unbroken cells and nuclei. Then, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 6800 × g for 4 min. The resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 10 min, and the pellet was gently resuspended in 1 mL of 250 mM sucrose to yield the crude lysosome. The crude lysosome was mixed with 2 volumes of 88% Histodenz to achieve a 58.9% concentration and placed at the bottom of an ultracentrifuge tube. A discontinuous gradient was formed by sequentially overlayering 2 mL of 33.7%, 3.3 mL of 27%, and 3.5 mL of 20.3% Histodenz, and topped with 250 mM sucrose. The gradient was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 141,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C in an SW 41 swinging bucket rotor. After centrifugation, from bottom to top, these interfaces included mitochondria, a mitochondria/lysosome mixture, a general lysosome pool, and CMA-active lysosomes at the uppermost interface. Each fraction was collected individually, diluted 10-fold with 250 mM sucrose, and pelleted at 37,000 × g for 15 min. The resulting pellets were resuspended in 250 mM sucrose to yield the highly enriched CMA-active lysosomes alongside the other isolated cellular components. For the preparation of lysosomal fractions from adherent cells, a comparable differential centrifugation protocol was utilized42,43.

The membrane integrity of isolated lysosomes was assessed using the β-hexosaminidase activity assay43,63. Briefly, 10 µL freshly isolated lysosomes were mixed with 30 µL of MOPS/sucrose buffer (10 mM MOPS, pH 7.2, 250 mM sucrose) and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Following centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant and pellet fractions were separated and each adjusted to a volume of 50 µL with MOPS/sucrose buffer. Colorimetric hexosaminidase buffer (50 µL) was added to both fractions, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 4 h. After a final centrifugation, the absorbance was measured at 405 nm. The ratio of absorbance from the supernatant to the total was used to determine the degree of membrane rupture. Only isolations with less than 10% membrane breakage were used for subsequent analyses.

Measurement of in vitro uptake of CMA substrates

The in vitro uptake of STMN1 by CMA was evaluated as previously described with minor modifications42. Freshly isolated lysosomes (10 µL) were mixed with 30 µL of MOPS/sucrose buffer in low-protein-binding tubes, followed by the addition of purified wild-type or KFERQ-mutant STMN1 to a final concentration of 0.02 µg/µL. The classical CMA substrate MAPT/Tau (Sino Biological) was used as a positive control. For each substrate, two conditions were prepared: with (PI+) or without (PI-) protease inhibitor addition. Samples without substrate addition served as blank controls. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, immediately chilled on ice to halt uptake, and centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The lysosomal pellets were washed once with MOPS/sucrose buffer, directly resuspended in sample buffer, and analyzed by immunoblot.

Measurement of soluble and polymerized tubulin

The separation of soluble and polymerized tubulin was performed as previously described with minor modifications51. Briefly, cells grown to 90% confluence were washed with 37 °C prewarmed PBS and lysed in microtubule-stabilizing lysis buffer (MTB; 80 mM PIPES-KOH, pH 6.8; 1 mM MgCl2; 2 mM EGTA; 0.5% NP-40) at 37 °C for 5 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 15 min at RT to separate the soluble and polymerized tubulin. Pellets were gently washed with MTB and resuspended in an equivalent volume of MTB containing the supernatant. Both fractions were boiled in Laemmli SDS sample buffer and then subjected to immunoblot analysis.

Cell proliferation and viability assays

For the colony formation assay, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells/well and cultured for 14 days. Resulting colonies were fixed and stained with 0.5% crystal violet in an acetic acid/methanol (1:7) solution for 2 h. After washing with water and air-drying, the plates were scanned using a CanoScan 5600 F scanner.

To assess cell proliferation and viability, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at appropriate densities. For proliferation assays, cells were evaluated at the indicated time points. For cell viability analysis, cells were treated with the indicated compounds for 48 h. Following treatments, 10 μL of CCK-8 (C0039, Beyotime) reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a PerkinElmer EnSpire 2300 microplate reader.

Cell cycle synchronization and flow cytometry analysis

Cells were synchronized using a standard double thymidine block method64. Briefly, cells were treated with 2 mM thymidine for 16 h, washed three times with PBS to remove thymidine, and released in fresh complete medium for 8 h. A second block was then performed by adding 2 mM thymidine for an additional 16 h. Synchronized cells were harvested for cell cycle analysis.

For cell cycle analysis, harvested cells were washed with cold PBS and fixed in 70% cold ethanol at −20 °C overnight. The fixed cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in a staining buffer containing RNase A (50 μg/mL) to degrade RNA and DAPI (10 μg/mL) for DNA content quantification. After a 30-min incubation, DAPI intensity was acquired using a flow cytometer. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using FlowJo software.

Statistics and reproducibility

Unless otherwise specified in the figure legends, statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software with either one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or two-tailed Student’s t-test, as appropriate. All statistical results were shown as mean ± SD unless otherwise stated. Exact P values (to four decimal places) are provided in the figures. The sample size for each result is shown in the figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. Unless otherwise noted, each experiment was repeated at least three times independently.
