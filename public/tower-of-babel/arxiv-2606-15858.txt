Ground state solutions to Born-Infeld-Choquard problem
Jaros λ aw Mederski, Xiangjian Zeng

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the existence and qualitative properties of ground state solutions for the nonlocal Born-Infeld-Choquard problem { − div ⁡ ( ∇ u 1 − | ∇ u | 2 ) + ω ​ u = ( I α ∗ | u | p ) ​ | u | p − 2 ​ u , in ​ ℝ N , N ≥ 3 , u ⁡ ( x ) → 0 , as ​ | x | → + ∞ . \begin{cases}-{\rm div}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)+\omega u=\big(I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p-2}u,&\hbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\;N\geq 3,\\[14.22636pt] u(x)\to 0,&\hbox{as }|x|\to+\infty.\end{cases} where p > N + α N p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N} , ω = 0 , 1 \omega=0,1 and 0 < α < N 0<\alpha<N . The equation is driven by the mean curvature operator in Lorentz-Minkowski space, motivated by the Born-Infeld nonlinear electromagnetic theory, and is coupled with a Choquard-type nonlocal nonlinearity. Due to the inherent relativistic gradient constraint | ∇ u | ≤ 1 |\nabla u|\leq 1 , the associated energy functional lacks standard 𝒞 1 \mathcal{C}^{1} regularity, preventing the direct use of classical variational techniques. We employ a non-smooth critical point theory on appropriate Pohožaev-type manifold to establish the existence of ground state solutions. Such non-smooth critical point theorem is abstract and we further show that it can be employed for strongly indefinite problem as well. We also demonstrate that these solutions are radially symmetric, and monotonously decay to zero at infinity.

1.
Introduction

To resolve the infinite energy problem of point charges in classical Maxwell theory, Born and Infeld
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
introduced a nonlinear electrodynamics model. By inherently restricting the maximum field strength, their theory ensures that the electromagnetic field generated by a point charge has finite energy. Mathematically, this framework is governed by the Born-Infeld operator:

\mathcal{Q}(u)=-{\rm div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right).

This operator naturally enforces the physical field bound via the mathematical condition
|\nabla u|<1
. Furthermore, it holds profound geometric significance in classical relativity, where it represents the mean curvature operator in Lorentz-Minkowski space
[
4
,
21
]
.
In last years many authors focused their attention on problems related to
\mathcal{Q}
in the whole
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
N\geq 1
. In particular, some results for

\mathcal{Q}(u)=\rho
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}

can be found in
[
8
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
22
]
, under different assumptions on
\rho
, where
\rho
can be considered as a prescribed charge source.

Much less is known, however, in the presence of a nonlinearity, and the case of a nonlocal nonlinearity seems to remain open. For equations of local type,

(1.1)

-{\rm div}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)=g(u),\qquad\hbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
g
is a nonlinear function of
u
, one of the first papers dealing with this kind of problem by variational methods is
[
9
]
. In that work, Bonheure, Derlet and De Coster considered the case
g(s)=|s|^{p-2}s
, with
p>2^{*}=\frac{2N}{N-2}
and
N\geq 3
. By means of suitable truncation arguments and ODE methods in the radial setting, they obtained the existence of finite-energy solutions. We also mention
[
1
,
2
,
35
,
6
,
30
,
3
]
, where (
1.1
) was studied again by means of ODE techniques in the radial setting.

Later, in
[
20
]
, Byeon et al. developed a monotonicity trick for nonsmooth functionals, inspired by the framework introduced in
[
37
]
. They proved the existence of infinitely many radial solutions of (
1.2
), as well as nonradial solutions when
N=4
or
N\geq 6
, see also
[
19
]
. More recently, an approach not relying on symmetry assumptions was developed in
[
7
]
, yielding the existence of a radial ground state solution and nonradial solutions for all
N\geq 4
.

If the local nonlinearity in (
1.1
) is replaced by a nonlocal one, the resulting problem becomes the so-called
Born–Infeld–Choquard
problem, which reads as follows:

(1.2)

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)+\omega u=\big(I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p-2}u,&\hbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\;N\geq 3,\\[14.22636pt]
u(x)\to 0,&\hbox{as }|x|\to+\infty,\end{cases}

where
p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N}
,
0<\alpha<N
and
I_{\alpha}(x):=\frac{C_{N,\alpha}}{|x|^{N-\alpha}}
is the Riesz potential.
Here, we assume that
\omega\in\{0,1\}
: the case
\omega=1
is called the
positive-mass case
, while the case
\omega=0
is called the
zero-mass case
.

If the Born–Infeld operator in (
1.2
) is replaced by the Laplacian, one obtains the well-known Choquard equation, also called the Choquard–Pekar equation in the case
N=3
,
\omega=1
,
\alpha=2
, and
p=2
:

-\Delta u+u=\big(I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p-2}u\qquad\hbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

This equation arises, for instance, as an approximation to the Hartree–Fock theory of plasma
[
34
,
27
]
.
A variational approach to this case was presented by Lieb
[
27
]
and Lions
[
29
]
.
More generally, for
N\geq 3
and

\frac{N+\alpha}{N}<p<\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2},

Moroz and Van Schaftingen
[
31
,
32
]
investigated the existence, regularity, and qualitative properties of ground state solutions to the generalized Choquard problem, see also
[
33
]
and references therein.

Throughout this paper, we assume that
N\geq 3
and
0<\alpha<N
. Unlike problem (
1.1
), however, problem (
1.2
) cannot be treated in a straightforward way by using the variational methods available in the literature. More precisely, equation (
1.2
) is, at least formally, the Euler–Lagrange equation associated with the energy functional

\mathcal{I}(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}\right)\,dx+\frac{\omega}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{2}\,dx-\frac{1}{2p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p}\,dx.

However, the gradient part of this functional, namely

\Psi_{0}(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}\right)\,dx,

is well defined only under the constraint
|\nabla u|\leq 1
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Consequently, the associated energy functional is not of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on its natural domain.

This lack of smoothness prevents the direct application of classical variational techniques. Indeed, the presence of the constraint
|\nabla u|\leq 1
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, together with the singular behavior of the integrand on the set where
|\nabla u|=1
, requires different and nonstandard strategies.

Moreover, the presence non-local nonlinearity makes the variational framework more delicate than in the local case e.g.
[
20
,
7
]
.

We define the functional space
X_{\omega}
by

X_{\omega}:=\overline{{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X_{\omega}}},\quad\text{where }\lVert u\rVert_{X_{\omega}}:=\sqrt{\omega\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{r}^{2}}.

with
r>\max\{2^{*},N\}
fixed. Here we denote the
L^{p}
-norm by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p}
and
2^{*}=\frac{2N}{N-2}
. In view of the singularity, we define the
effective domain
of
\Psi_{0}
, and hence of
\mathcal{I}
, as

D(\Psi_{0}):=\{u\in X_{\omega}:\Psi_{0}(u)<\infty\}=\{u\in X_{\omega}:|\nabla u|\leq 1\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{R}^{N}\}.

Although
\mathcal{I}
depends on
\omega
, we omit the notation
\mathcal{I}_{\omega}
whenever this does not lead to confusion.

Our aim is to investigate the
ground state solutions
to problem (
1.2
), that is, the nontrivial least energy solutions to (
1.2
). To make this notion precise, we need a preliminary illustration.
Let

(1.3)

{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p}\,dx,\quad\hbox{ for }u\in X_{\omega}.

Then we introduce a
Pohožaev functional
related to our problem:

(1.4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\omega}(u):

\displaystyle=N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}\,dx+\frac{N+2}{2}\omega\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{2}\,dx-\frac{N+\alpha+2p}{2p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle=N\Psi_{0}(u)+\frac{N+2}{2}\omega\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{N+\alpha+2p}{2p}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u)

and the
Pohožaev set

{\mathcal{M}}_{\omega}:=\{u\in D(\Psi_{0})\backslash\{0\}:{\mathcal{P}}_{\omega}(u)=0\}.

Therefore, we now define the
ground state level
by

c_{\omega}:=\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\omega}}{\mathcal{I}}(u).

If
c_{\omega}
can be achieved by some
u_{\omega}
in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\omega}
, then we will show in Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
that it is indeed a solution to (
1.2
) and will be called a
ground state solution
to (
1.2
). As we shall see, a ground state solution has the least energy among all nontrivial solutions of (
1.2
), which belong to
\mathcal{M}_{\omega}
; see Lemma
2.3
below.

Here, we emphasize that, although the functional
\mathcal{I}
is not well defined on the whole space
X_{\omega}
, it can still be treated within the nonsmooth variational framework due to Szulkin
[
37
]
; see also
[
20
]
. In this setting, critical points in the convex sense are shown to be weak solutions of (
1.2
). It follows from
[
20
, Proposition 2.10]
for the linear problem involving
{\mathcal{Q}}
.

More precisely, let
\Psi:X_{\omega}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be a lower semicontinuous convex functional and let
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(X_{\omega},\mathbb{R})
. We say that
u\in X_{\omega}
is a
critical point
of
\Psi-\Phi
if

(1.5)

\Psi(v)-\Psi(u)-\Phi^{\prime}(u)(v-u)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }v\in X_{\omega}.

In the case of (
1.2
), we take

\Phi(u):=\frac{1}{2p}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,p}(u)-\frac{\omega}{2}\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2},\qquad\Psi(u):=\Psi_{0}(u).

We shall see that the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,p}
, and hence
\Phi
, is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on the underlying functional space, see Proposition
2.2
.

Our first main result in the positive mass case is stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N}
. Then every minimizing sequence
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
c_{1}
admits, up to translations, a subsequence that converges strongly to some
u_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
. Moreover,
u_{1}
is a ground state solution to (
1.2
) with
\omega=1
.

Next, we show the qualitative properties of the ground state solutions to (
1.2
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N}
. If
u
is a ground state solution to (
1.2
) with
\omega=1
, then either
u>0
or
u<0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Moreover, it is radially symmetric and decays monotonically to zero, i.e., there is a single-variable function
v:(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
u(x)=v(|x-x_{0}|)
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
v(r)\to 0
monotonously as
r\to+\infty
.

We are also interested in the nonlocal version for the Sobolev-type inequality, which is a counterpart to the local one that has been studied in
[
7
,
9
]
. Specifically, the following type of inequality

(1.6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}})\,dx\geq C_{p,\alpha}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*|u|^{p})|u|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{N}{N+\alpha+2p}},

will be investigated. More precisely, the best constant
C_{p,\alpha}
can be characterized by the minimizer over a suitable Pohožaev constraint
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
, which one will see that it in fact can be attained. Moreover, such minimizer is a ground state solution to (
1.2
) with
\omega=0
,
where
p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}
.

Our last main result concerns the zero mass case.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}
. Then every minimizing sequence
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
c_{0}
has, up to translation, a convergent subsequence converging strongly to some
u_{0}
in
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
, which is a classical ground state solution to (
1.2
) with
\omega=0
. Moreover, it holds that

(i)

for any
u\in X_{0}
the inequality (
1.6
) holds,
where

C_{p,\alpha}=\frac{(N+\alpha+2p)c_{0}^{\frac{N+2p}{N+\alpha+2p}}}{(2Np)^{\frac{N}{N+\alpha+2p}}(\alpha+2p)^{\frac{\alpha+2p}{N+\alpha+2p}}}.

(ii)

every optimizer
v
has a form that
v(x)=tu(\frac{x}{t})
with
t>0
, where
u
is a minimizer for
c_{0}
.

We would like to mention that if
\alpha=0
, then
C_{p,\alpha}
coincides with the constant obtained in the local version of (
1.6
) in
[
7
]
.

We can also say more about the quantitative properties with such minimizers.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}
. Then any minimizer for
c_{0}
or optimizer for (
1.6
) has constant sign, that is, it is either positive or negative on whole
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Moreover, it is radially symmetric and decays monotonically to zero.

We emphasize that both Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
remain valid in the local case
\alpha=0
. In particular, they yield the radial symmetry of all ground state solutions, a property which was not established in
[
7
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we collect the basic notation, recall the functional framework, and establish some preliminary properties of the nonlocal term and of the nonsmooth Born–Infeld functional. In particular, we prove the differentiability of the Choquard-type functional and derive a Pohožaev identity for weak solutions. Section
3
is devoted to the concentration-compactness analysis. We prove a vanishing lemma, a nonlocal Brézis–Lieb type decomposition, and a profile decomposition adapted to the nonlocal setting. In Section
4
, we study the positive-mass case
\omega=1
. We analyze the fibering map associated with the natural scaling, prove the existence of a minimizer on the Pohožaev set
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, and show that such a minimizer is a ground state solution. Section
5
contains the qualitative analysis of ground state solutions, including their constant sign, radial symmetry, and monotonicity properties. In Section
6
, we turn to the zero-mass case
\omega=0
. We prove the existence of a ground state solution, establish the associated nonlocal Sobolev-type inequality, and characterize the best constant and its optimizers. Finally, in the Appendix
A
, we prove an abstract result, Lemma
A.1
, showing that constrained minimizers on a suitable constrained set are critical points. The argument is inspired by
[
7
]
, but its formulation is adapted to both local and nonlocal problems. We also demonstrate that such abstract lemma can be employed for strongly indefinite Schrödinger equations of Szulkin and Weth’s settings
[
38
]
as well, see Theorem
A.2
. We expect that it will be useful in a broader class of nonsmooth variational settings.

Preliminaries

We write
A\lesssim B
if there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of the relevant parameters, such that
A\leq CB
.

We first recall from
[
20
]
the basic embedding properties of our working space.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
r>\max\{N,2^{*}\}
. Then
X_{1}
and
X_{0}
are reflexive Banach space,
X_{1}\subset X_{0}
and satisfy the following continuous embeddings:

(i)

X_{0}\hookrightarrow W^{1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, for every
q\in[2^{*},r]
; and
X_{1}\hookrightarrow W^{1,q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, for every
q\in[2,r]

(ii)

X_{1}\text{ and }X_{0}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):=\{u\in{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):\lim\limits_{|x|\to\infty}|u(x)|=0\}
;

(iii)

X_{1}\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, for every
q\in[2,\infty]
; and
X_{0}\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, for every
q\in[2^{*},\infty]

Next, we show that the nonlocal part of the functional is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
p\geq\frac{N+\alpha}{N}
in the case of
X_{1}
and
p\geq\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}
in the case of
X_{0}
. Then
I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
u\in X_{\omega}
. Moreover, the functional
{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u)
defined in (
1.3
) is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
.

Proof.

In view of Proposition
2.1

(iii)
, we have
|u|^{p}\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for any
r\in[\frac{2N}{N+\alpha},\infty]
. Since
\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}<\frac{N}{\alpha}
, the standard property of Riesz’s potential (see
[
23
,
28
]
) leads to
I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\in L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, where
s\in[\frac{2N}{N-\alpha},\infty)
. Moreover, since
|u|^{p}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we obtain that

(2.1)

I_{\alpha}*|u|^{p}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Indeed, setting
f:=|u|^{p}
, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we write

|I_{\alpha}*f(x)|\leq C_{N,\alpha}\left(\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{|f(y)|}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{|f(y)|}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dy\right).

For the local part, using
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we have

\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{|f(y)|}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dy\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dy\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}.

For the far-field part, we apply Hölder’s inequality with

r=\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}
,

r^{\prime}=\frac{2N}{N-\alpha}
, namely

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{|f(y)|}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{(N-\alpha)r^{\prime}}}\,dy\right)^{1/r^{\prime}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}.

Therefore,

\|I_{\alpha}*f\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}+\|f\|_{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}.

and the proof of (
2.1
) is complete.

It remains to verify the differentiability. Put

s:=\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}.

By Proposition
2.1

(iii)
, the map
u\mapsto|u|^{p}
sends
X_{\omega}
continuously into
L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Indeed, if
u_{n}\to u
in
X_{\omega}
, then
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{ps}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and

\big\||u_{n}|^{p}-|u|^{p}\big\|_{s}\leq C\big(\lVert u_{n}\rVert_{ps}^{p-1}+\lVert u\rVert_{ps}^{p-1}\big)\lVert u_{n}-u\rVert_{ps}\to 0.

Hence, by the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality,

{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*|u|^{p})|u|^{p}\,dx

is continuous on
X_{\omega}
. Moreover, since the Nemytskii map

u\mapsto|u|^{p-2}u
is continuous from
X_{\omega}
into

L^{\frac{2N}{(N+\alpha)(p-1)}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, for any
v\in X_{\omega}
we have

(2.2)

{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}^{\prime}(u)v=2p\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*|u|^{p})|u|^{p-2}uv\,dx.

The continuity of
u\mapsto{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}^{\prime}(u)
follows again from the above
continuity of
u\mapsto|u|^{p}
, the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality
and Hölder’s inequality. Therefore
{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(X_{\omega},\mathbb{R})
.
∎

We next prove the Pohožaev-type identity for (
1.2
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Any weak solution to

-{\rm div}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)+\omega u=\big(I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p-2}u,\quad u\in X_{\omega};\;N\geq 3,

where
p\geq\frac{N+\alpha}{N}
, if
\omega=1
; or
p\geq\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}
, if
\omega=0
, satisfies the following formula

(2.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\,dx=N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}+\frac{\omega}{2}|u|^{2}\right)\,dx-\frac{N+\alpha}{2p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p}\,dx.

Therefore, any nontrivial weak solution to (
1.2
) lies in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\omega}
.

Proof.

Let
u
be a weak solution to the equation in this Lemma. Note from Proposition
2.1
that
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and from Proposition
2.2
that
I_{\alpha}*|u|^{p}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Then by
[
20
, Proposition 2.10]
, we know that
u\in W^{2,q}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for each
q\in[2,\infty)
, and
|\nabla u|<1
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let
\eta\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that
\eta\equiv 1
when
|x|\leq 1
,
\eta\equiv 0
when
|x|\geq 2
, and
|x|\cdot|\nabla\eta(x)|\leq C
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, set
\eta_{\varepsilon}(x):=\eta(\varepsilon x)
. By regularity of
u
, we know that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}:=\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)x\cdot\nabla u\in W^{1,q}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for each
q\in[2,\infty)
. Testing (
1.2
) by
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
leads to

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(I_{\alpha}\ast|u|^{p}\big)\big(|u|^{p-2}u\varphi_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dx-\omega\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\varphi_{\varepsilon}\,dx.

Notice that

\frac{\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}=\frac{(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon})(\nabla u\cdot x)}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}+\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}|\nabla u|^{2}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}+\eta_{\varepsilon}x\cdot\nabla\left(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}\right).

Integrating by part leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\,dx=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big[\frac{(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon})(\nabla u\cdot x)}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}+\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}|\nabla u|^{2}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\big]dx

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(x\cdot\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon}+N\eta_{\varepsilon}\big)\left(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}\right)dx

Since

\rho:=(I_{\alpha}*|u|^{p})|u|^{p-2}u-\omega u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

and
\mathcal{Q}(u)=\rho
,
by
[
20
, Proposition 2.10(ii)]
, there exists

\delta\in(0,1)
such that

\|\nabla u\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\delta.

Consequently,

\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\leq C_{\delta}|\nabla u|^{2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Therefore, using Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem we get

(2.4)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\,dx-N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}\right)dx.

where, in the case

Corollary 2.4
.

Assume that either

\omega=1\quad\text{and}\quad 1<p\leq\frac{N+\alpha}{N},

or

\omega=0\quad\text{and}\quad 1<p\leq\frac{N+\alpha}{N-2}.

Let

be a weak solution to (
1.2
) such that

(2.5)

\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Proof.

Suppose that

is a weak solution to (
1.2
). By the regularity result, see for instance
[

, Proposition 2.10]
, and by a standard approximation argument, we may test (

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\,dx+\omega\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{2}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(I_{\alpha}*|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p}\,dx.

Thanks to the additional assumption (
2.5
),
the proof of Lemma

can be repeated in the present range of

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\,dx=N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}+\frac{\omega}{2}|u|^{2}\right)\,dx-\frac{N+\alpha}{2p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big(I_{\alpha}*|u|^{p}\big)|u|^{p}\,dx.

Combining (

(2.8)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{N+\alpha+2p}{2p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\,dx-N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle+\frac{N+\alpha-Np}{2p}\omega\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{2}\,dx.

If

If

Again, (

For

as

; and
d{\mathcal{H}}(t,u)/dt<0
as
t\in(T,+\infty)
. Thus, it is obvious that there exists a unique
t_{u}>T
such that
{\mathcal{H}}(t_{u},u)=0
, so that
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(u_{t_{u}})=0
and
u_{t_{u}}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
. Noticing that
d{\mathcal{H}}(t,u)/dt|_{t=t_{u}}<0
, then we exploit implicit function theorem on
{\mathcal{H}}(t,u)
to deduce the continuity.
∎

According to above Lemma, we know that the set
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
is not empty and
c_{1}
is well-defined. Next we show that the constrained minimizer on
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
implies a critical point of
{\mathcal{I}}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
u_{1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
satisfy
{\mathcal{I}}(u_{1})=c_{1}
. Then
u_{1}
is a critical point of
{\mathcal{I}}
.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
A.1
. Let
X=X_{1}
,

\mathfrak{B}(u):=\frac{1}{2p}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u)-\frac{1}{2}\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2},\qquad\mathfrak{A}(u):=\Psi_{0}(u),

so that
{\mathcal{I}}=\mathfrak{A}-\mathfrak{B}
. By Proposition

2.2
,
\mathfrak{B}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(X_{1},\mathbb{R})
, while

\mathfrak{A}
is proper, convex and lower semicontinuous on
X_{1}
, and

D(\mathfrak{A})=D(\Psi_{0})
is convex. Let
S_{t}u=u_{t}
be the scaling
in (
4.1
). Then
S_{1}u=u
,
S_{t}u\not\equiv 0
for
u\not\equiv 0
, and

S_{t}(S_{r}u)=S_{tr}u
for all
t,r>0
. Moreover,

\mathfrak{A}(S_{t}u)=\Psi_{0}(u_{t})=t^{N}\Psi_{0}(u)=t^{N}\mathfrak{A}(u),

so that (S1)–(S3) in Lemma
A.1
hold
with
\alpha(t)=t^{N}
. The
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}
-regularity of the fiber maps follows from
(
4.2
).

We next identify the abstract functional
\mathfrak{R}
. Since

\mathfrak{R}(t,u):=\frac{d}{dt}\mathfrak{B}(u_{t})=\frac{N+\alpha+2p}{2p}t^{N+\alpha+2p-1}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u)-\frac{N+2}{2}t^{N+1}\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2},

we have
\mathfrak{R}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}((0,+\infty)\times X_{1},\mathbb{R})
. Moreover,

\mathfrak{P}(u)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{I}}(u_{t})\right|_{t=1}=N\mathfrak{A}(u)-\mathfrak{R}(1,u)={\mathcal{P}}_{1}(u),\qquad\mathfrak{M}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}.

Thus (A1) and (A2) follow directly from Lemma

4.1
. It remains to check (A3)
at the minimizer
u_{1}
. Since
u_{1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
, we have

\frac{N+\alpha+2p}{2p}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u_{1})=N\Psi_{0}(u_{1})+\frac{N+2}{2}\lVert u_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{R}(t,u_{1})}{\alpha^{\prime}(t)}\right)\right|_{t=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(N+\alpha+2p)(\alpha+2p)}{2Np}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u_{1})-\frac{N+2}{N}\lVert u_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha+2p)\Psi_{0}(u_{1})+\frac{(N+2)(\alpha+2p-2)}{2N}\lVert u_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}>0,

because
u_{1}\not\equiv 0
and
p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N}
implies
\alpha+2p-2>0
. Hence
(A3) is satisfied.

All assumptions of Lemma
A.1
are therefore
satisfied, and hence
u_{1}
is a critical point of
{\mathcal{I}}
.
∎

Next, we are going to show the ground state level
c_{1}
is positive by which identify it with mountain pass level.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
m_{1}
be the mountain pass level defined by

(4.3)

m_{1}:=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{\tau\in[0,1]}{\mathcal{I}}(\gamma(\tau)),\qquad\Gamma:=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}\left([0,1];X_{1}\right):\gamma(0)=0,\,{\mathcal{I}}(\gamma(1))<0\}.

Then it holds that
c_{1}=m_{1}>0
.

Proof.

From (
4.2
) there holds that
{\mathcal{I}}(u_{t})\to-\infty
as
t\to+\infty
and
{\mathcal{I}}(u_{t})\to 0
as
t\to 0
, for any nonzero
u\in D(\Psi_{0})
. Thus, the class
\Gamma
is nonempty and
m_{1}
is well-defined.

We first prove
m_{1}>0
. By Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality and Proposition
2.1

(iii)
, one has

{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u)\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{\frac{2Np}{N+\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{N+\alpha}{N}}\leq C\lVert u\rVert_{X_{1}}^{2p}\qquad\text{for }u\in X_{1}.

If
u\notin D(\Psi_{0})
, it is natural to make the convention that
+\infty={\mathcal{I}}(u)>0
. Hence we only suppose that
u\in D(\Psi_{0})
, that is,
|\nabla u|\leq 1
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which implies that
\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{r}^{r}\leq\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{2}^{2}
for
r>\max\{N,2^{*}\}
. Note that

(4.4)

\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\Psi_{0}(u)\leq\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{for all }u\in D(\Psi_{0})\backslash\{0\}.

Therefore, we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}(u)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2p}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{r}^{r}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}-C\lVert u\rVert_{X_{1}}^{2p}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\lVert u\rVert_{X_{1}}^{2}-C\lVert u\rVert_{X_{1}}^{2p}.

Since
2p>2
, it thereby holds for sufficiently small
\beta>0
that
\inf_{\lVert u\rVert_{X_{1}}=\beta}{\mathcal{I}}(u)>0
. Therefore,
m_{1}>0
.

Subsequently, we aim to show
c_{1}\geq m_{1}
. For any
u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
, fix a
T_{u}>1
such that
{\mathcal{I}}(u_{T_{u}})<0
. Then we define the path
\gamma:[0,1]\mapsto X_{1}
by
\gamma(\tau):=u_{\tau/T_{u}}=(\tau/T_{u})u(T_{u}\cdot/\tau)
. Clearly,
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
m_{1}\leq\max_{\tau\in[0,1]}{\mathcal{I}}(\gamma(\tau))={\mathcal{I}}(u)
by Lemma
4.1
. According to arbitrary
u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
, we therefore obtain that

m_{1}\leq\inf_{u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}}{\mathcal{I}}(u)=c_{1}.

Finally, we focus on the proof for
c_{1}\leq m_{1}
, and it is sufficient to verify that
\gamma([0,1])\cup{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\neq\emptyset
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. First deduce analogously to (
4.5
) that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(u)

\displaystyle=N\Psi_{0}(u)+\frac{N+2}{2}\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{N+\alpha+2p}{2p}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N}{4}\lVert u\rVert_{X_{1}}^{2}-C\lVert u\rVert_{X_{1}}^{2p}.

Hence we get
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\gamma(t))>0
for sufficiently small
t
. On the other hand, one has

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\gamma(1))

\displaystyle=N{\mathcal{I}}(\gamma(1))+2\left(\frac{\lVert\gamma(1)\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\alpha+2p}{4p}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(\gamma(1))\right)

\displaystyle\leq(N+2){\mathcal{I}}(\gamma(1))-2\Psi_{0}(\gamma(1))<0.

Therefore, by intermediate value theorem there exists some
t_{0}\in(0,1)
such that
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\gamma(t_{0}))=0
, that is,
\gamma(t_{0})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
. Thus, we complete the proof.
∎

Now we are in the position to prove our first main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Given a minimizing sequence
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
for
c_{1}
, from Lemma
4.3
we have

{\mathcal{I}}(u_{n})=\frac{\alpha+2p}{N+\alpha+2p}\Psi_{0}(u_{n})+\frac{\alpha+2p-2}{2(N+\alpha+2p)}\lVert u_{n}\rVert_{2}^{2}\to c_{1}>0

as
n\to\infty
, which implies that
\{\Psi_{0}(u_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\lVert u_{n}\rVert_{2}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are both bounded. Since all
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are in
D(\Psi_{0})
, then
\lVert\nabla u_{n}\rVert_{r}^{r}\leq\lVert\nabla u_{n}\rVert_{2}^{2}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, we get from (
4.4
) that sequence
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
X_{1}
. By Lemma
3.3
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
,
(y_{n}^{i})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\tilde{u}_{i}\in X_{1}
such that

(4.6)

\displaystyle u_{n}(\cdot+y_{n}^{i})

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\tilde{u}_{i}\qquad\text{{\rm weakly in }}X_{1},\,\text{{\rm as }}n\to\infty;

\displaystyle u_{n}(x+y_{n}^{i})

\displaystyle\to\tilde{u}_{i}(x)\qquad\text{{\rm a.e. on }}\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\text{{\rm as }}n\to\infty;

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}|y_{n}^{i}-y_{n}^{j}|

\displaystyle=+\infty\qquad\text{{\rm for }}i\neq j;

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Psi(u_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Psi(\tilde{u}_{i})\quad\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Psi_{0}(u_{n})\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Psi_{0}(\tilde{u}_{i})\right);

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(\tilde{u}_{i}),

If
k=0
and
\tilde{u}_{0}=0
, then

{\mathcal{I}}(u_{n})=\frac{\alpha+2p}{N+\alpha+2p}\Psi_{0}(u_{n})+\frac{\alpha+2p-2}{2(N+\alpha+2p)}\lVert u_{n}\rVert_{2}^{2}\to 0

and
c_{1}=0
, which gives a contradiction. Hence, either
k>0
or
k=0
with
\tilde{u}_{0}\neq 0
. Noticing that
D(\Psi_{0})
is convex and closed, then it is weakly closed and all
\tilde{u}_{i}
are located in
D(\Psi_{0})
.

Next, we set
J:=\{i:\tilde{u}_{i}\not\equiv 0\}
. The above assumption implies that
J\neq\emptyset
. By (
4.6
) and Brézis-Lieb Lemma, the Pohožaev identity
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(u_{n})=0
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in J}N\Psi_{0}(\tilde{u}_{i})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}N\Psi_{0}(u_{n})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left[\frac{N+\alpha+2p}{2p}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u_{n})-\frac{N+2}{2}\lVert u_{n}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N+\alpha+2p}{2p}\sum_{i\in J}{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(\tilde{u}_{i})-\frac{N+2}{2}\sum_{i\in J}\lVert\tilde{u}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Hence, There exists
j\in J
such that
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\tilde{u}_{j})\leq 0
. From Lemma
4.1
the parameter
t_{j}
such that
t_{j}\tilde{u}_{j}(\frac{\cdot}{t_{j}}):=\tilde{w}_{j}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
satisfies
t_{j}\leq 1
. On the other hand, it follows by (
4.6
) and Fatou’s Lemma that

(4.7)

\displaystyle c_{1}\leq{\mathcal{I}}(\tilde{w}_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha+2p}{N+\alpha+2p}\Psi_{0}(\tilde{w}_{j})+\frac{\alpha+2p-2}{2(N+\alpha+2p)}\lVert\tilde{w}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha+2p}{N+\alpha+2p}t_{j}^{N}\Psi_{0}(\tilde{u}_{j})+\frac{\alpha+2p-2}{2(N+\alpha+2p)}t_{j}^{N+2}\lVert\tilde{u}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha+2p}{N+\alpha+2p}\Psi_{0}(\tilde{u}_{j})+\frac{\alpha+2p-2}{2(N+\alpha+2p)}\lVert\tilde{u}_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left[\frac{\alpha+2p}{N+\alpha+2p}\Psi_{0}(u_{n}(\cdot+y_{n}^{j}))+\frac{\alpha+2p-2}{2(N+\alpha+2p)}\lVert u_{n}(\cdot+y_{n}^{j})\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{I}}(u_{n})=c_{1}.

Therefore, we conclude that
t_{j}=1
and
\tilde{u}_{j}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
with
{\mathcal{I}}(\tilde{u}_{j})=c_{1}
, which indicates that
\tilde{u}_{j}
is a minimizer for
c_{1}
. Moreover, from the last two formulae in (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) it is clear that no other nontrivial profile can occur. For convenience, we relabel
\tilde{u}_{j}
by
u_{1}
and set
y_{n}:=y_{n}^{j}
.

We next prove the strong convergence. From (
4.7
) and the convergence
{\mathcal{I}}(u_{n})\to c_{1}
, we have

\Psi_{0}(u_{n}(\cdot+y_{n}))\to\Psi_{0}(u_{1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lVert u_{n}(\cdot+y_{n})\rVert_{2}\to\lVert u_{1}\rVert_{2}.

Since
u_{n}(\cdot+y_{n})\rightharpoonup u_{1}
in
X_{1}
, the convergence in
X_{1}

follows from
[
7
, Lemma 2.2]
. Thus every minimizing sequence
admits, up to translations, a strongly convergent subsequence whose
limit belongs to
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and achieves
c_{1}
.

It remains to show that this minimizer is a classical ground state solution. By Lemma
4.2
,
u_{1}
is a critical point of
{\mathcal{I}}
. Equivalently,

\Psi_{0}(v)-\Psi_{0}(u_{1})+\langle u_{1},v-u_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(I_{\alpha}*|u_{1}|^{p})|u_{1}|^{p-2}u_{1}(v-u_{1})\,dx\geq 0

for all

. Hence

is a minimizer of

I_{\rho}(\psi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(1-\sqrt{1-|\nabla\psi|^{2}})\,dx-\langle\rho,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}

\rho=(I_{\alpha}*|u_{1}|^{p})|u_{1}|^{p-2}u_{1}-u_{1}.

By Proposition
2.1

(iii)
and Proposition

2.2
, we have
u_{1},I_{\alpha}*|u_{1}|^{p}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Therefore
\rho\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and

[
20
, Proposition 2.10]
gives that
u_{1}
is a weak solution of

-{\rm div}\left(\frac{\nabla u_{1}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u_{1}|^{2}}}\right)+u_{1}=(I_{\alpha}*|u_{1}|^{p})|u_{1}|^{p-2}u_{1}\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Finally, we aim at the regularity of the solution. Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
2.3
, one has
u_{1}\in W^{2,q}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for each
q\in[2,\infty)
and

on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Hence

for some

and the coefficients

a_{ij}(x)=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u_{1}|^{2}}}+\frac{\partial_{i}u_{1}\partial_{j}u_{1}}{\left(1-|\nabla u_{1}|^{2}\right)^{3/2}}

belong to
{\mathcal{C}}^{0,\lambda}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. On the other hand, since
|u_{1}|^{p}\in L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for all
s\in[\frac{2N}{N+\alpha},\infty]
,
[
23
, Theorem 2]
yields
I_{\alpha}*|u_{1}|^{p}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{0,\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Thus
(I_{\alpha}*|u_{1}|^{p})|u_{1}|^{p-2}u_{1}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{0,\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and Schauder’s regularity theory applied to

\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}a_{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}u_{1}=(I_{\alpha}*|u_{1}|^{p})|u_{1}|^{p-2}u_{1}-u_{1}

implies that
u_{1}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Since
u_{1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
, it is nontrivial. Moreover, if
w
is any nontrivial weak solution of (
1.2
) with
\omega=1
, then Lemma
2.3
gives

, and hence
{\mathcal{I}}(w)\geq c_{1}={\mathcal{I}}(u_{1})
. Therefore
u_{1}
has the least energy among all nontrivial solutions, and so it is a classical ground state solution of (
1.2
).
∎

5.
Qualitative properties of ground state solutions

In this section we show that all the ground state solutions to (
1.2
) must have constant sign and are radially symmetric.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N}
. If
u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
is a ground state solution to (
1.2
), then either
u>0
or
u<0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Proof.

Let
u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
be a ground state solution to (
1.2
). Then
{\mathcal{I}}(u)=c_{1}
. Since

\Psi_{0}(|u|)=\Psi_{0}(u),\qquad\lVert|u|\rVert_{2}=\lVert u\rVert_{2},\qquad{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(|u|)={\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,p}(u),

we have
|u|\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{I}}(|u|)=c_{1}
. Hence, by Lemma
4.2
,
|u|
is also a critical point of
{\mathcal{I}}
. Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
,
|u|
is a classical nonnegative solution to (
1.2
). By the strong maximum principle,
|u|>0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Since
u
is continuous, it cannot change sign. Therefore either
u>0
or
u<0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
∎

In order to examine the symmetry of the ground state solution, we introduce the useful tool of so-called
polarization
(see e.g.
[
40
]
). Let
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a closed half-space. We denote
\sigma_{H}
the reflection with respect to the hyperplane
\partial H
. Then the polarization
u_{H}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
of a function
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to
H
is defined by

u_{H}(x):=\begin{cases}\max\{u(x),u(\sigma_{H}(x))\}\qquad\text{if }x\in H,\\
\min\{u(x),u(\sigma_{H}(x))\}\qquad\text{if }x\notin H.\end{cases}

Recall the following two crucial lemmas from
[
31
, Lemma 5.3 and 5.4]
, which reflect a strong symmetric effect for nonlocal term.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
u\in L^{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
H
be a closed half-space in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. If
u\geq 0
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x)u(y)}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dx\,dy\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u_{H}(x)u_{H}(y)}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dx\,dy,

then either
u_{H}=u
or
u_{H}=u\circ\sigma_{H}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
s\geqslant 1
and
u\in L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. If
u\geq 0
and for every closed half-space
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

u_{H}=u
or
u_{H}=u\circ\sigma_{H}
, then there exist
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
v:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
a nonincreasing function such that for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
u(x)=v(|x-x_{0}|)
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
p>\frac{N+\alpha}{N}
. If
u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
is a ground state solution to (
1.2
), then there exist
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
v:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
a monotone function such that for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
u(x)=v(|x-x_{0}|)
and
v(r)\to 0
as
r\to+\infty
.

Proof.

Let
u\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
be a ground state solution to (
1.2
) such that
{\mathcal{I}}(u)=c_{1}
and
H
be any closed half-space. From Proposition
5.1

u
has constant sign. If
u<0
, it is clear that
-u>0
is also a ground state solution to (
1.2
). Therefore, we need only to consider the case of positive solution. Recall that if
u
is a weak solution to (
1.2
), by
[
20
, Proposition 2.10]
we know that
u\in W^{2,q}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for each
q\in[2,\infty)
, and
|\nabla u|<1
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Therefore, it is sufficient to exploit
[
40
, Lemma 3.1 (ii)]
for
\Psi_{0}(u)
to get that

\Psi_{0}(u_{H})=\Psi_{0}(u).

In addition, there holds that

\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}.

By Proposition
2.1

(iii)
, one has
|u|^{p}\in L^{\frac{2N}{N+\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. The polarization inequality for the Riesz kernel (see e.g.,
[
39
]
) gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u_{H}(x)|^{p}|u_{H}(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dx\,dy\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)|^{p}|u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dx\,dy.

If the above inequality were strict, then
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(u_{H})<0
. Thus, by Lemma
4.1
there would exist
t_{H}<1
such that
(u_{H})_{t_{H}}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
. Since
\Psi_{0}(u_{H})=\Psi_{0}(u)
and
\lVert u_{H}\rVert_{2}=\lVert u\rVert_{2}
, we would get

c_{1}\leq{\mathcal{I}}((u_{H})_{t_{H}})<\frac{\alpha+2p}{N+\alpha+2p}\Psi_{0}(u)+\frac{\alpha+2p-2}{2(N+\alpha+2p)}\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}={\mathcal{I}}(u)=c_{1},

a contradiction. Hence equality holds, namely

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(x)|^{p}|u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dx\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u_{H}(x)|^{p}|u_{H}(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{N-\alpha}}\,dx\,dy,

Thus, it follows from Lemma
5.2
applied to
u^{p}
that either
(u^{p})_{H}=u^{p}
or
(u^{p})_{H}=u^{p}\circ\sigma_{H}
. Since
u>0
, this is equivalent to saying that either
u_{H}=u
or
u_{H}=u\circ\sigma_{H}
. Since
H
is arbitrary, the conclusion is derived from Lemma
5.3
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

It follows from Proposition
5.1
and
5.4
.
∎

6.
Zero mass case and Sobolev-type inequality

Similar to Theorem
1.1
, we first sketch the proof of the existence of minimizer for

Appendix A
Minimizers are critical points – abstract result

Lemma A.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and let

\mathfrak{I}=\mathfrak{A}-\mathfrak{B},

where
\mathfrak{A}:X\to(-\infty,+\infty]
is proper, convex and lower semicontinuous, and

\mathfrak{B}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(X,\mathbb{R})
. Set

D(\mathfrak{A}):=\{u\in X:\mathfrak{A}(u)<+\infty\}

and assume that
D(\mathfrak{A})
is convex.

Let
S:(0,+\infty)\times X\to X
be a scaling map, denoted by

S_{t}u:=S(t,u),

such that

(S1)

S_{1}u=u
and
S_{t}u\neq 0
whenever
u\neq 0
;

(S2)

for any
t,r>0
and
u\in X
, it holds
S_{tr}u=S_{t}(S_{r}u)
;

(S3)

it holds that
\mathfrak{A}(S_{t}u)=\alpha(t)\mathfrak{A}(u)
for every
t>0
and
u\in D(\mathfrak{A})\setminus\{0\}
, where
\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}(0,+\infty)
satisfies
\alpha(1)=1
and
\alpha^{\prime}(t)>0
for all
t>0
.

Suppose that, for every

u\in D(\mathfrak{A})\setminus\{0\}
, the fiber map

\gamma_{u}(t):=\mathfrak{I}(S_{t}u)

is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
with respect to
t>0
. Moreover, assume that

\mathfrak{R}:(0,+\infty)\times X\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\mathfrak{R}(t,u):=\frac{d}{dt}\mathfrak{B}(S_{t}u),\quad\hbox{for }(t,u)\in(0,+\infty)\times X

is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
.
Define the Pohožaev functional by

\mathfrak{P}(u):=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\gamma_{u}(t)\right|_{t=1}=\alpha^{\prime}(1)\mathfrak{A}(u)-\mathfrak{R}(1,u),

and the Pohožaev set by

\mathfrak{M}:=\{u\in D(\mathfrak{A})\setminus\{0\}:\mathfrak{P}(u)=0\}.

Let
u_{0}\in\mathfrak{M}
satisfy

\mathfrak{I}(u_{0})=\inf_{\mathfrak{M}}\mathfrak{I}.

Assume that the following conditions hold:

(A1)

For every
u\in D(\mathfrak{A})\setminus\{0\}
, there exists a unique
t_{u}>0
such that

S_{t_{u}}u\in\mathfrak{M}.

Moreover,
t_{u}
is the unique maximum point of the map

t\mapsto\mathfrak{I}(S_{t}u).

(A2)

The map

u\mapsto t_{u}

is continuous on
D(\mathfrak{A})\setminus\{0\}
.

(A3)

There holds

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{R}(t,u_{0})}{\alpha^{\prime}(t)}\right)\right|_{t=1}>0.

Then
u_{0}
is a critical point of
\mathfrak{I}
in the sense of Szulkin, namely

\mathfrak{A}(u)-\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})-\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}(u_{0})(u-u_{0})\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }u\in X.

Proof.

Let
u\in X
. If
u\notin D(\mathfrak{A})
, then
\mathfrak{A}(u)=+\infty
and the conclusion is satisfied. Hence we may assume that
u\in D(\mathfrak{A})
.

Let
\tau_{n}\to 0^{+}
and set

v_{n}:=(1-\tau_{n})u_{0}+\tau_{n}u.

By the convexity of
D(\mathfrak{A})
, we have
v_{n}\in D(\mathfrak{A})
. We may also assume that
v_{n}\neq 0
. By (A1), let

t_{n}:=t_{v_{n}},\qquad w_{n}:=S_{t_{n}}v_{n}\in\mathfrak{M}.

Since
u_{0}
minimizes
\mathfrak{I}
on
\mathfrak{M}
, by (A1) we obtain

0\leq\mathfrak{I}(w_{n})-\mathfrak{I}(u_{0})=\mathfrak{I}(w_{n})-\mathfrak{I}(v_{n})+\mathfrak{I}(v_{n})-\mathfrak{I}(u_{0}).

Therefore, by the convexity of
\mathfrak{A}
and the differentiability of
\mathfrak{B}
, we get

(A.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}(v_{n})-\mathfrak{I}(w_{n})\leq\mathfrak{I}(v_{n})-\mathfrak{I}(u_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})-\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})-\mathfrak{B}(v_{n})+\mathfrak{B}(u_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{n}\big(\mathfrak{A}(u)-\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})\big)-\tau_{n}\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}(u_{0})(u-u_{0})+o(\tau_{n}).

On the other hand, from the definition of fiber map
\gamma_{u}
and mean value theorem, one has

\mathfrak{I}(v_{n})-\mathfrak{I}(w_{n})=\int_{t_{n}}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\gamma_{v_{n}}(t)\,dt=(1-t_{n})\left.\frac{d}{dt}\gamma_{v_{n}}(t)\right|_{t=\xi_{n}},

where
\xi_{n}
lies between
t_{n}
and 1. Therefore, dividing (
A.1
) by
\tau_{n}
, we obtain

(A.2)

\frac{1-t_{n}}{\tau_{n}}\left.\frac{d}{dt}\gamma_{v_{n}}(t)\right|_{t=\xi_{n}}\leq\mathfrak{A}(u)-\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})-\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}(u_{0})(u-u_{0})+o(1).

From assumption (A2), it is clear that
t_{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. Since
\xi_{n}
lies between
t_{n}
and
1
, we also have
\xi_{n}\to 1
. By the lower semicontinuity of
\mathfrak{A}
,

\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{A}(v_{n}).

On the other hand, the convexity of
\mathfrak{A}
gives

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big((1-\tau_{n})\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})+\tau_{n}\mathfrak{A}(u)\big)=\mathfrak{A}(u_{0}).

Thus
\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})\to\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})
. Together with the continuity of
\mathfrak{R}
, this gives

(A.3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left.\frac{d}{dt}\gamma_{v_{n}}(t)\right|_{t=\xi_{n}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\alpha^{\prime}(\xi_{n})\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})-\mathfrak{R}(\xi_{n},v_{n}))=\mathfrak{P}(u_{0})=0.

We first show that

(A.4)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1-t_{n}}{\tau_{n}}<+\infty.

Indeed, suppose by contradiction that, up to a subsequence,

\frac{1-t_{n}}{\tau_{n}}\to+\infty.

Then
t_{n}<1
for all large
n
and
\tau_{n}/(1-t_{n})\to 0
. Since

w_{n}=S_{t_{n}}v_{n}\in\mathfrak{M}
, by (A1),

(A.5)

\frac{d}{dt}\gamma_{v_{n}}(t)|_{t=t_{n}}=0,

and we obtain

\mathfrak{I}(w_{n})=\mathfrak{E}(t_{n},v_{n}),\qquad\mathfrak{I}(u_{0})=\mathfrak{E}(1,u_{0}),

where

\mathfrak{E}(t,u):=\frac{\alpha(t)}{\alpha^{\prime}(t)}\mathfrak{R}(t,u)-\mathfrak{B}(S_{t}u).

Notice that, under the preceding assumptions, the map
\mathfrak{E}
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1}
on
(0,+\infty)\times X
. Moreover
using the minimality of
u_{0}
on
\mathfrak{M}
, and
v_{n}-u_{0}=\tau_{n}(u-u_{0})
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{I}(w_{n})-\mathfrak{I}(u_{0})=\mathfrak{E}(t_{n},v_{n})-\mathfrak{E}(1,u_{0})

\displaystyle=(t_{n}-1)\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mathfrak{E}(t,u_{0})\right|_{t=1}+o(|t_{n}-1|)+O(\tau_{n}).

Moreover,

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mathfrak{E}(t,u_{0})\right|_{t=1}=\alpha(1)\left.\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{R}(t,u_{0})}{\alpha^{\prime}(t)}\right)\right|_{t=1}>0

by (A3). Dividing the preceding inequality by
1-t_{n}>0
and
letting
n\to\infty
, we get a contradiction. Hence
(
A.4
) holds. Therefore, either
\{(1-t_{n})/\tau_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded or

(A.6)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1-t_{n}}{\tau_{n}}=-\infty.

If the first case holds, then the conclusion is obtained from (
A.2
) and (
A.3
). Suppose that (
A.6
) holds. Without loss of generality, we assume that
t_{n}>1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Set

G(t,v):=\frac{\mathfrak{R}(t,v)}{\alpha^{\prime}(t)},\quad\hbox{for }t>0,\;v\in X.

In view of (
A.5
), we have

0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mathfrak{I}(S_{t}v_{n})\right|_{t=t_{n}}=\alpha^{\prime}(t_{n})\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})-\mathfrak{R}(t_{n},v_{n}),

and hence

\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})=G(t_{n},v_{n}).

Assume first that
\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})\leq\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then by
u_{0}\in\mathfrak{M}
and the preceding identity, we get from definition of Pohožaev set that

G(1,u_{0})=\frac{\mathfrak{R}(1,u_{0})}{\alpha^{\prime}(1)}=\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})\geq\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})=\frac{\mathfrak{R}(t_{n},v_{n})}{\alpha^{\prime}(t_{n})}=G(t_{n},v_{n}).

Noticing that
t_{n}\to 1
,
v_{n}\to u_{0}
in
X
, and
\tau_{n}/(t_{n}-1)\to 0
along this subsequence, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq G(t_{n},v_{n})-G(1,u_{0})

\displaystyle=G(t_{n},u_{0})-G(1,u_{0})+G(t_{n},v_{n})-G(t_{n},u_{0})

\displaystyle=(t_{n}-1)\left.\frac{d}{dt}G(t,u_{0})\right|_{t=1}+o(t_{n}-1)+O(\tau_{n}).

Dividing by
t_{n}-1>0
and passing to the limit, we obtain

0\geq\left.\frac{d}{dt}G(t,u_{0})\right|_{t=1},

which contradicts (A3). Thus, up to a subsequence, we may assume that
\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})>\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})
. In this case, by convexity of
\mathfrak{A}
, we have

0\leq\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})-\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})\leq\tau_{n}\mathfrak{A}(u)+(1-\tau_{n})\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})-\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})=\tau_{n}\left(\mathfrak{A}(u)-\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})\right).

Thus, we get the following estimate:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathfrak{A}(v_{n})-\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})}{\tau_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{G(t_{n},v_{n})-G(1,u_{0})}{\tau_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{n}-1}{\tau_{n}}\left(\left.\frac{d}{dt}G(t,u_{0})\right|_{t=1}+o(1)\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{A}(u)-\mathfrak{A}(u_{0})

for all large
n
. By (A3), this implies

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{t_{n}-1}{\tau_{n}}<+\infty,

which contradicts (
A.6
). Thus, we complete the entire proof.
∎

|u|\to 0,

uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

(SW4)

\frac{F(x,u)}{u^{2}}\to+\infty
as
|u|\to+\infty,

uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

(SW5)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the map

u\mapsto\frac{f(x,u)}{|u|}

is strictly increasing on
(-\infty,0)
and on
(0,+\infty)
.

In view of (SW1), there are closed subspaces
E^{+}
and
E^{-}
such that
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})=E^{+}\oplus E^{-}
and
Q
is positive definite on
E^{+}
and negative definite on
E^{-}
. Moreover

Q(u)=\|u^{+}\|^{2}-\|u^{-}\|^{2}\hbox{ for }u=u^{+}+u^{-}\in E^{+}\oplus E^{-}.

As in
[
38
,
36
]
,
\mathfrak{I}
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
and we introduce the following Nehari-Pankov manifold

\mathcal{N}:=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\setminus\{0\}:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigl(|\nabla u|^{2}+V(x)u^{2}\bigr)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,u)u\,dx,\;\hbox{and }I^{\prime}(u)|_{E^{-}}=0\right\}.

We show the main result of the application of our abstract Lemma
A.1
.

Theorem A.2
.

Suppose that
(SW1)–(SW5)
are satisfied.
Then any
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{N}}
satisfying

I(u_{0})=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}}I(u)

is a weak solution of (
A.7
).

We shall give the proof after recalling some preliminary properties.

Let

\mathfrak{W}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

\mathfrak{W}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\|u^{-}\|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}F(x,u)\,dx,\hbox{ for }u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Observe that
\mathfrak{W}
is continuous, strictly convex (see
[
38
, Lemma 2.2]
) and coercive. Then, let

w(u)\in E^{-}
be a unique minimizer of

E^{-}\ni w\mapsto\mathfrak{W}(u+w)\in\mathbb{R}.

One can show that the map
u\mapsto\mathfrak{W}(u+w(u))
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
, see e.g. (i)–(iii) in the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 4.4]
. Moreover, it follows that

(A.8)

\mathfrak{W}^{\prime}(u+w(u))[v]=\langle w(u),v\rangle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,u+w(u))v\,dx=0\qquad\hbox{for }v\in E^{-}.

We set now,

X:=E^{+}
,
\mathfrak{A}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u^{+}\|^{2}
,
\mathfrak{B}(u):=\mathfrak{W}(u+w(u))
and functional
\mathfrak{I}:X\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\mathfrak{I}:=\mathfrak{A}-\mathfrak{B}
.

Lemma A.3
.

The following statements hold:

(i)

w:X\to E^{-}
is of
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
-class,

(ii)

The functional
\mathfrak{B}:X\to\mathbb{R}
is of
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
class,

(iii)

\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}(u)=\left.\mathfrak{W}^{\prime}(u+w(u))\right|_{X}:X\to\mathbb{R}
for every
u\in X
.

Proof.

Define

G:E^{+}\times E^{-}\to(E^{-})^{\prime},\qquad G(u,w)[v]:=\langle w,v\rangle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,u+w)v\,dx.

Then (
A.8
) is equivalent to

G(u,w(u))=0.

By the assumptions on
f
, the map
G
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
, and

G^{\prime\prime}(u,w)[\eta][v]=\langle\eta,v\rangle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{s}(x,u+w)\eta v\,dx,\qquad\eta,v\in E^{-}.

Moreover, by (SW3) and (SW5), one has
f_{s}(x,s)\geq 0
. Therefore

G^{\prime\prime}(u,w(u))[\eta][\eta]=\|\eta\|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{s}(x,u+w(u))\eta^{2}\,dx\geq\|\eta\|^{2}\qquad\forall\eta\in E^{-}.

Thus
G^{\prime\prime}(u,w(u)):E^{-}\to(E^{-})^{\prime}
is an isomorphism by the
Lax–Milgram theorem. Hence the implicit function theorem applies to

G(u,w)=0
, and we obtain
w\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(X,E^{-})
, so that (i) holds. Finally, one can follow the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 4.4]
to show (ii) and (iii).
∎

Let us define

S_{t}u:=tu,\quad t>0.

Note that

\mathfrak{A}

is proper, convex, continuous, and therefore lower semicontinuous. Moreover,
D(\mathfrak{A})=E^{+}
is convex.
For
t>0
and
u\in X
, we have

\mathfrak{A}(S_{t}u)=\mathfrak{A}(tu)=t^{2}\mathfrak{A}(u).

Thus in Lemma
A.1
we take

\alpha(t)=t^{2},\qquad\alpha^{\prime}(t)=2t,\qquad\gamma_{u}(t)=\mathfrak{I}(S_{t}u)=t^{2}\mathfrak{A}(u)-\mathfrak{B}(S_{t}u)

Clearly,
S_{1}u=u
,
S_{t}u\neq 0
whenever
u\neq 0
, and

S_{tr}u=tr\,u=S_{t}(S_{r}u)\qquad\text{for all }t,r>0.

Therefore (S1)–(S3) in Lemma
A.1

are satisfied. Then from Lemma
A.3
we have

\mathfrak{R}(t,u):=\frac{d}{dt}\mathfrak{B}(S_{t}u)=\mathfrak{W}^{\prime}(tu+w(tu))[u]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,tu+w(tu))u\,dx,\qquad\hbox{ for }u\in X

Thus, as in Lemma
A.1
, we can define the following functional by

\mathfrak{P}(u):=2\mathfrak{A}(u)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,u+w(u))u\,dx,

and the following constraint by

\mathfrak{M}:=\{u\in X\setminus\{0\}:\mathfrak{P}(u)=0\}.

Lemma A.4
.

The map
m:\mathfrak{M}\to{\mathcal{N}}
defined by
u\mapsto u+w(u)
is a homeomorphism between
\mathfrak{M}
and the Nehari-Pankov constraint
\mathcal{N}
. Moreover,
u_{0}=u_{0}^{+}+u_{0}^{-}
is a minimizer of
I
on
\mathcal{N}
if and only if
u_{0}^{+}\in\mathfrak{M}
is a minimizer of
\mathfrak{J}
on
\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

Indeed, for
u\in\mathfrak{M}
, there holds
\mathfrak{P}(u)=0
. Moreover, from (
A.8
) we have

\mathfrak{W}^{\prime}(u+w(u))[v]=I^{\prime}(u+w(u))[v]=0

for all
v\in E^{-}
. Plus
\mathfrak{P}(u)=0
with (
A.8
) yields that
I^{\prime}(u+w(u))[u+w(u)]=0
. These imply that
u+w(u)\in\mathcal{N}
. Conversely, let
z\in\mathcal{N}
and write
z=z^{+}+z^{-}
. Since

I^{\prime}(z)|_{E^{-}}=0
, for every
v\in E^{-}
we have

0=I^{\prime}(z)[v]=-\langle z^{-},v\rangle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,z)v\,dx.

Equivalently,

\mathfrak{W}^{\prime}(z^{+}+z^{-})[v]=0\qquad\hbox{ for every }v\in E^{-}.

Thus
z^{-}
is the unique minimizer of

E^{-}\ni w\mapsto\mathfrak{W}(z^{+}+w),

and therefore
z^{-}=w(z^{+})
.
Hence
z=z^{+}+w(z^{+})
. By
z\in\mathcal{N}
, we also have

I^{\prime}(z)[z]=0
. Together with
I^{\prime}(z)[z^{-}]=0
, this gives

0=I^{\prime}(z)[z^{+}]=I^{\prime}(z^{+}+w(z^{+}))[z^{+}]=\mathfrak{P}(z^{+}).

Therefore
z^{+}\in\mathfrak{M}
.
Moreover, since
I(u+w(u))=\mathfrak{I}(u)
, the correspondence of these two different minimizers immediately holds.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A.2
.

It remains to verify the hypotheses (A1)–(A3) of Lemma
A.1
. In view of
[
38
, Proposition 2.3]
, we see that there exists a unique maximum point
t_{u}>0
of the fiber map
\gamma_{u}(t):=\mathfrak{I}(S_{t}u)
for every
u\in X
, so that (A1) holds. Moreover,
S_{t_{u}}u=t_{u}u\in\mathfrak{M}
. Next, one infer from
[
38
, Lemma 2.8]
and Lemma
A.4
that the map
u\mapsto t_{u}
is continuous, which implies (A2). It remains to show (A3). Suppose
u_{0}=u_{0}^{+}+u_{0}^{-}
is a minimizer over
\mathcal{N}
where
u_{0}^{+}\in X
and
u_{0}^{-}=w(u_{0}^{+})\in E^{-}
from Lemma
A.4
.
Differentiating (
A.8
) at
u_{0}^{+}
in the direction
u_{0}^{+}
, we get

(A.9)

\langle w^{\prime}(u_{0}^{+})[u_{0}^{+}],v\rangle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{s}(x,u_{0}^{+}+w(u_{0}^{+}))(u_{0}^{+}+Dw(u_{0}^{+})[u_{0}^{+}])v\,dx=0

for all
v\in E^{-}
. Besides, we have

(A.10)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\gamma_{u_{0}^{+}}(t)\right|_{t=1}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{A}(u_{0}^{+})-\frac{d}{dt}\mathfrak{R}(1,u_{0}^{+})

\displaystyle=\lVert u_{0}^{+}\rVert^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{s}(x,u_{0}^{+}+w(u_{0}^{+}))(u_{0}^{+}+Dw(u_{0}^{+})[u_{0}^{+}])u_{0}^{+}.

Let
v=w^{\prime}(u_{0}^{+})[u_{0}^{+}]
in (
A.9
) and
q:=u_{0}^{+}+v
. Then combining (
A.9
) and (
A.10
) yields that

(A.11)

\left.\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\gamma_{u_{0}^{+}}(t)\right|_{t=1}=I^{\prime\prime}(u_{0})[q,q].

We next show that
I^{\prime\prime}(u_{0})
is negative definite on
\mathbb{R}u_{0}\oplus E^{-}
. Let

h=\tau u_{0}+v
with
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in E^{-}
. Since
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

I^{\prime}(u_{0})[u_{0}]=0,\qquad I^{\prime}(u_{0})[v]=0.

Thus

Q(u_{0},u_{0})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,u_{0})u_{0}\,dx,\qquad Q(u_{0},v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,u_{0})v\,dx,

and
Q(v,v)=-\lVert v\rVert^{2}
. Therefore

\displaystyle I^{\prime\prime}(u_{0})[h,h]

\displaystyle=Q(h,h)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{s}(x,u_{0})h^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\lVert v\rVert^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(f_{s}(x,u_{0})-\frac{f(x,u_{0})}{u_{0}}\right)(\tau u_{0}+v)^{2}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{f(x,u_{0})}{u_{0}}v^{2}\,dx.

By (SW3) and (SW5), we have
I^{\prime\prime}(u_{0})[h,h]\leq 0
. Moreover, the strict
differential monotonicity condition gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(f_{s}(x,u_{0})u_{0}^{2}-f(x,u_{0})u_{0}\right)\,dx>0.

Hence

I^{\prime\prime}(u_{0})[h,h]<0\qquad\hbox{for every }0\neq h\in\mathbb{R}u_{0}\oplus E^{-}.

Applying this to (
A.11
) we obtain that (
A.10
) is strictly negative. Consequently,
we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left.\frac{\mathfrak{R}(t,u_{0}^{+})}{\alpha^{\prime}(t)}\right|_{t=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left.\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,tu_{0}^{+}+w(tu_{0}^{+}))\,dx}{2t}\right|_{t=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f_{s}(x,u_{0})(u_{0}^{+}+Dw(u_{0}^{+})[u_{0}^{+}])u_{0}^{+}\,dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,u_{0}))u_{0}^{+}]\,dx

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{2}\lVert u_{0}^{+}\rVert^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,u_{0})u_{0}^{+}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\lVert u_{0}\rVert^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}f(x,u_{0})u_{0}\,dx\right]

\displaystyle=I^{\prime}(u_{0})[u_{0}]=0,

which is the required condition (A3). Thus,
u_{0}^{+}
is a critical point of
\mathfrak{I}
on
X=E^{+}
. Since
\mathfrak{A}
is differentiable in
this model case, this means

I^{\prime}(u_{0}^{+})[v]=\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}(u_{0}^{+})[v]=0\qquad\hbox{for all }v\in E^{+}.

By (
A.8
), we
also have

\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}(u_{0})[\phi]=0
for all
\phi\in E^{-}
.
Combining the two relations and writing every
z\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as

z=z^{+}+z^{-}
, we conclude that

I^{\prime}(u_{0})[z]=\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}(u_{0})[z]=0\qquad\hbox{for all }z\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Thus
u_{0}
is a weak solution of (
A.7
).
∎

The conclusion of Theorem
A.2
was proved in
[
38
]

under the additional assumption that
f(\cdot,s)
is
1
-periodic in

x
, but under a weaker regularity hypothesis than our (SW2):
namely, it was assumed only that
f
is continuous and satisfies

|f(x,s)|\leq C\left(1+|s|^{p-1}\right).

In that setting, Szulkin and Weth also established the existence of a
ground state by means of Ekeland’s variational principle. Our purpose here
is different: we observe that the fact that any minimizer on the constraint
is a critical point can be obtained by an alternative method based on Lemma

A.1
. The separate problem of proving the existence
of such a minimizer may then be studied independently by other tools, for
instance by concentration–compactness arguments. We also see potential for
applying Lemma
A.1
to nonsmooth nonlinear
Schrödinger equations; this will be addressed in future work.
