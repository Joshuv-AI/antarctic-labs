Turbophoresis of inertial particles in inhomogeneous turbulence produced by oscillating grids
E. Elmakies, O. Shildkrot, N. Kleeorin, A. Levy, I. Rogachevskii

Abstract
Turbophoresis in inhomogeneous turbulent flows leads to the formation of large-scale nonuniform particle number density distributions of inertial particles. This effect is associated with an effective drift velocity directed toward regions of lower turbulence intensity and proportional to the particle Stokes time and the spatial gradient of the turbulence intensity. In the present study, turbophoretic transport is experimentally investigated in air flows generated by one-grid and two-grid oscillating turbulence systems. The flow velocity field and particle spatial distribution are measured using Particle Image Velocimetry. To isolate the effect of particle accumulation due to turbophoresis from that associated with mean fluid flow, the measured particle number density of inertial particles is normalized by the corresponding distribution obtained for noninertial tracer particles under identical flow conditions. The measurements show preferential accumulation of inertial particles in regions of lower turbulence intensity, consistent with the expected behavior of turbophoretic transport.

It is important to distinguish between turbulent thermal diffusion and turbophoresis, since these mechanisms originate from different physical processes. Turbulent thermal diffusion occurs in temperature-stratified turbulence and is associated with turbulent particle fluxes coupled to turbulent heat fluxes. In contrast, turbophoresis originates from averaging of particle velocity, and it is due to
particle inertia in spatially inhomogeneous turbulence, leading to preferential accumulation of particles in regions of lower turbulence intensity.

Despite substantial progress in theoretical and numerical studies, direct experimental investigations of turbophoresis remain relatively limited, particularly in nearly unbounded turbulent flows generated away from solid boundaries. Most previous experimental studies of particle accumulation were conducted in wall-bounded turbulent flows, where turbulence inhomogeneity is strongly affected by walls and mean shear
SE91
;
KHB95
;
RR04
. The experimental identification of turbophoretic transport is therefore challenging, especially in flows where multiple particle transport mechanisms may coexist.

In the present study, we experimentally investigate the formation of large-scale concentrations of inertial particles in strongly inhomogeneous isothermal turbulence generated by one and two oscillating grids in air flows. Oscillating-grid turbulence provides a controlled laboratory configuration with relatively weak mean flow and spatially varying turbulence intensity, allowing direct investigation of particle accumulation induced by turbophoresis. Simultaneous measurements of turbulent velocity fields and particle number density distributions allow direct comparison between the spatial distribution of inertial particles and the turbulence intensity. To isolate the turbophoretic contribution, the measured particle number density fields for inertial particles are normalized using corresponding measurements obtained with noninertial particles under identical flow conditions.

II
Physics of turbophoresis

In this section, we briefly discuss the physical mechanisms underlying turbophoresis and estimate the effective turbophoretic velocity
\overline{\bm{V}}_{\rm turboph}
of inertial particles (see, e.g., Refs.
EKR98
;
RI21
).
The equation of motion for inertial particles with the material density
\rho_{p}
that is much larger than the fluid density
\rho
, is given by

\displaystyle{{\rm d}{\bm{U}}^{\rm(p)}\over{\rm d}t}=-{{\bm{U}}^{\rm(p)}-{\bm{U}}\over\tau_{p}}+{\bm{g}},

(3)

where
{\bm{U}}^{\rm(p)}
is the particle velocity,
{\bm{U}}
is the fluid velocity,

{\bm{g}}
is the acceleration caused by the gravity field, and

\tau_{\rm p}=m_{\rm p}/(3\pi\rho\,\nu\,d_{\rm p})
is the Stokes
time of spherical solid particles having the diameter
d_{\rm p}
and mass

m_{\rm p}=\left(\pi/6\right)\,\rho_{p}\,d_{\rm p}^{3}
. Here
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity
of the fluid.
The Stokes number
{\rm St}
based on the Kolmogorov time
\tau_{\nu}=\ell_{\nu}/u_{\nu}=\tau_{0}/{\rm Re}^{1/2}=\ell_{\nu}^{2}/\nu
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm St}={\tau_{\rm p}\over{\tau}_{\nu}}={\rho_{\rm p}\,d_{\rm p}^{2}\over 18\rho\,\ell_{\nu}^{2}},

(4)

where
u_{\nu}=u_{0}/{\rm Re}^{1/4}
is the characteristic fluid velocity at the
Kolmogorov viscous micro-scale
\ell_{\nu}=\ell_{0}/{\rm Re}^{3/4}
, the fluid Reynolds number is
{\rm Re}=u_{0}\ell_{0}/\nu
with
u_{0}
being
the characteristic turbulent velocity at the integral turbulent
scale
\ell_{0}
and
\tau_{0}=\ell_{0}/u_{0}
.

For small Stokes numbers, the solution of Eq. (
3
) can be obtained by iterations, yielding

\displaystyle{\bm{U}}^{\rm(p)}={\bm{U}}-\tau_{\rm p}\,{{\rm d}{\bm{U}}\over{\rm d}t}+\tau_{\rm p}{\bm{g}}+{\rm O}(\tau_{\rm p}^{2}),

(5)

where the substantial derivative
{\rm d}{\bm{U}}/{\rm d}t
for the moving fluid element is

\displaystyle{{\rm d}{\bm{U}}\over{\rm d}t}={\partial{\bm{U}}\over\partial t}+({\bm{U}}{\bf\cdot}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}){\bm{U}},

(6)

and the fluid velocity is determined by the Navier-Stokes equation for an incompressible fluid,

\displaystyle{\partial{\bm{U}}\over\partial t}+({\bm{U}}{\bf\cdot}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}){\bm{U}}=-{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}P\over\rho}+\nu\,\Delta{\bm{U}}.

(7)

Here,
P
is the fluid pressure.

We employ a mean-field approach in which the velocity, fluid pressure and density, and particle number density are decomposed into mean and fluctuating components.
Using the Reynolds averaging of Eq. (
5
) over an ensemble of fluctuations,
we obtain an expression for the mean particle velocity:

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{U}}^{\rm(p)}\right\rangle=\overline{\bm{U}}-\tau_{\rm p}\,\left\langle{d{\bm{U}}\over dt}\right\rangle+\tau_{\rm p}{\bm{g}}+{\rm O}(\tau_{\rm p}^{2}),

(8)

where
{\bm{U}}=\overline{\bm{U}}+{\bm{u}}
,
\overline{\bm{U}}=\langle{\bm{U}}\rangle
is the mean fluid velocity and
{\bm{u}}
are fluid velocity fluctuations.
Equations (

8

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{U}}^{\rm(p)}\right\rangle=\overline{\bm{U}}-\tau_{\rm p}\,\left\langle({\bm{u}}{\bf\cdot}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}){\bm{u}}\right\rangle+\tau_{\rm p}{\bm{g}}+{\rm O}(\tau_{\rm p}^{2}).

(9)

For an incompressible fluid
(u_{j}\nabla_{j})u_{i}=\nabla_{j}(u_{i}u_{j})
and for an isotropic turbulence
\left\langle u_{i}u_{j}\right\rangle=\delta_{ij}\,\left\langle{\bm{u}}^{2}\right\rangle/3
, so that
\left\langle({\bm{u}}{\bf\cdot}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}){\bm{u}}\right\rangle=(1/3)\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\left\langle{\bm{u}}^{2}\right\rangle
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker unit tensor.
Therefore, Eq. (
9
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{U}}^{\rm(p)}\right\rangle=\overline{\bm{U}}+\overline{\bm{V}}_{\rm turboph}+{\bm{V}}_{\rm g}+{\rm O}(\tau_{\rm p}^{2}),

(10)

where

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}_{\rm g}=\tau_{\rm p}\,{\bm{g}},

(11)

is the terminal fall velocity of inertial particles in the gravity field and

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{V}}_{\rm turboph}=-{\tau_{\rm p}\over 3}\,{\bm{\nabla}}\left\langle{\bm{u}}^{2}\right\rangle

(12)

is the turbophoretic velocity of inertial particles in an inhomogeneous turbulence.
Equation (
12
) shows that inertial particles drift toward regions of lower turbulence intensity.
Consequently, turbophoretic transport occurs only for inertial particles. Generally, the turbophoretic velocity of inertial particles is given by Eq. (
1
).

The physical mechanism underlying turbophoresis is associated with particle inertia in spatially inhomogeneous turbulence. Due to inertial effects, particles cannot fully follow rapidly varying turbulent motions and tend to drift out from the regions with stronger velocity fluctuations toward regions of lower turbulence intensity. In homogeneous turbulence, this drift does not produce a net particle transport because there is no preferred direction. However, in the presence of a nonzero turbulence intensity gradient,
\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\left\langle{\bm{u}}^{2}\right\rangle
, the particle drift results in a net turbophoretic flux. Consequently, inertial particles preferentially accumulate near minima of the turbulence intensity.

n(t,{\bm{x}})
as

\displaystyle{\partial n\over\partial t}+{\bm{\nabla}}{\bf\cdot}({\bm{U}}^{\rm(p)}\,n)=D\,\Delta n,

(13)

where

is the coefficient
of the molecular (Brownian) diffusion of particles,
T
is the fluid temperature
and
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant.
Averaging Eq. (
13

\overline{n}

\displaystyle{\partial\overline{n}\over\partial t}+\nabla_{i}\biggl[\biggl(\overline{\bm{U}}+{\bm{V}}_{\rm g}-\kappa_{\rm turboph}\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}\left\langle{\bm{u}}^{2}\right\rangle\biggr)_{i}\overline{n}

\displaystyle\qquad-D_{ij}^{{}^{\rm(T)}}\nabla_{j}\overline{n}\biggr]=0,

(14)

where
D^{{}^{\rm(T)}}_{ij}
is the turbulent diffusion tensor.
For large Péclet numbers, the molecular Brownian diffusion coefficient

In Eq. (
14

1

apply Eq. (
10

, where
u^{\rm(p)}_{i}
are fluctuations of particle velocity.
In the turbulent flux of particles we neglect small effects
\sim{\rm O}(\tau_{\rm p})
.
For simplicity, we assume that gradients along the horizontal

The steady-state solution for Eq. (

is given by

\displaystyle{\overline{n}(Y)\over\overline{n}_{\rm b}}=\exp\biggl(\int_{0}^{Y}{\overline{U}_{y}-\kappa_{\rm turboph}\,\,\nabla_{y}\left\langle{\bm{u}}^{2}\right\rangle\over D^{{}^{\rm(T)}}_{yy}}\,{\rm d}Y^{\prime}\biggr),

For experimental investigation of turbophoresis, borosilicate hollow glass particles with an approximately spherical shape are used as inertial particles. The particles have a mean diameter of

To improve particle mixing and reduce particle agglomeration, a custom-made acoustic feeding device is used for particle injection into the flow. The device consists of an acrylic chamber equipped with air guides and an acoustically driven flexible membrane that disperses particles into the incoming airflow. The particles are injected into the chamber through multiple inlets positioned at different locations and orientations to achieve approximately uniform particle dispersion within the flow domain. In particular, two inlets are located on the upper wall and two on the lower wall of the chamber.

The spatial distributions of particles with diameters of
0.7\,\mu
m and
10\,\mu

The scattered-light intensity produced by small particles is determined by Mie scattering

E_{s}
depends on the particle diameter

The observed particle accumulation is primarily a bulk effect due to turbophoresis caused by large-scale gradients of turbulence intensity rather than by boundary effects. This conclusion is supported by the measured spatial distributions of particle number density (see Figs.
13
–
14
), which do not exhibit localization near the boundaries but instead follow the large-scale variations of turbulence intensity.

Although inertial particles experience gravitational settling, its influence on the measured particle accumulation remains limited under the present experimental conditions. The terminal fall velocity for particles with diameter
10\,\mu
m is approximately
0.33
cm/s, which is substantially smaller than both the mean and turbulent fluid velocities. In addition, gravitational settling acts predominantly in the vertical direction, whereas the observed turbophoretic transport is associated mainly with horizontal gradients of turbulence intensity along the
Y
axis.

The particle number density distributions are obtained from measurements with relatively short acquisition times and ensemble averaging over many independent realizations. As a result, the measured distributions primarily reflect turbophoretic redistribution of particles within the flow before significant gravitational settling develops. Therefore, the observed large-scale particle accumulation patterns are governed mainly by turbophoretic transport rather than by sedimentation effects.

V
Conclusions

In the present study, we investigate turbophoretic transport of inertial particles in inhomogeneous non-stratified turbulence generated by one and two oscillating grids in airflow. Turbophoresis is caused by the combined effect of particle inertia and turbulence inhomogeneity. The competition between the effective turbophoretic velocity and turbulent diffusion leads to the formation of large-scale concentrations of inertial particles in regions of minimum turbulence intensity.

The velocity field and spatial distributions of inertial particles are measured using Particle Image Velocimetry. To isolate the effect of turbophoresis, the particle number density measured for inertial particles is normalized pointwise by the corresponding distribution obtained for noninertial particles under identical flow conditions. The experiments demonstrate that inertial particles preferentially accumulate in regions of reduced turbulence intensity, in agreement with the theoretical estimate given by Eq. (
17
).
The present results provide experimental evidence of large-scale turbophoretic particle accumulation in strongly inhomogeneous turbulence and may be relevant for geophysical and astrophysical turbulent flows.
