Fine-tuning Factor Augmented Neural Lasso for Heterogeneous Environments
Jinhang Chai Jianqing Fan Cheng Gao Qishuo Yin Department of Operations Research and Financial Engineering Princeton University

Abstract
Fine-tuning is a widely used strategy for adapting pre-trained models to new tasks, yet its methodology and theoretical properties in high-dimensional nonparametric settings with variable selection have not yet been developed. This paper introduces the fine-tuning factor augmented neural Lasso (FAN-Lasso) , a transfer learning framework for high-dimensional nonparametric regression with variable selection that simultaneously handles covariate and posterior shifts. We use a low-rank factor structure to manage high-dimensional dependent covariates and propose a novel residual fine-tuning decomposition in which the target function is expressed as a transformation of a frozen source function and other variables to achieve transfer learning and nonparametric variable selection. This augmented feature from the source predictor allows for the transfer of knowledge to the target domain and reduces model complexity there. We derive minimax-optimal excess risk bounds for the fine-tuning FAN-Lasso, characterizing the precise conditions, in terms of relative sample sizes and function complexities, under which fine-tuning yields statistical acceleration over single-task learning. The proposed framework also provides a theoretical perspective on parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods. Extensive numerical experiments across diverse covariate- and posterior-shift scenarios demonstrate that the fine-tuning FAN-Lasso consistently outperforms standard baselines and achieves near-oracle performance even under severe target sample size constraints, empirically validating the derived rates.



Keywords
: Transfer Learning, Neural Networks, Factor Augmentation, FAST-NN, Factor-adjusted Nonparametric Lasso, Model Shifts, Covariate Shifts.

1
Introduction

Transfer learning has revolutionized modern machine learning by enabling the transfer of learned representations from data-rich source domains to sample-limited target tasks. This paradigm is most prominently realized through fine-tuning, where pre-trained weights from large-scale models serve as a sophisticated starting point for specialized downstream applications. The effectiveness of this approach hinges on the hypothesis that pre-training captures intrinsic latent structures—such as shared features or factors—that provide a foundational representation of the data. Fine-tuning thus acts as a pragmatic adaptation layer, refining these learned representations to align with the specific nuances of the target domain.

While the empirical success of fine-tuning is undeniable, providing a unified and sharp theoretical characterization of its performance remains a formidable task. Existing literature has established foundational guarantees for transfer learning, yet these often focus on linear regimes or assume a restricted class of distribution shifts. There remains a critical need for a framework that can quantify the statistical gains of fine-tuning in the presence of high-dimensional covariates and complex nonparametric structures—settings where classical parametric intuition often fails. Specifically, it remains unclear how to construct a unified estimator that simultaneously achieves minimax optimality under both covariate and posterior shifts while maintaining robustness to negative transfer.

In this paper, we bridge these theoretical and practical gaps by proposing a fine-tuning factor-augmented neural Lasso (FAN-Lasso) framework. We posit that the shared knowledge between domains can be effectively encapsulated by a low-dimensional factor structure, which provides a stable backbone for knowledge transfer even under significant distribution shifts. Our approach provides an optimal mechanism to transfer pre-trained factor-augmented sparse throughput neural networks (FAST-NN,
13
)—a class of models that combine latent factor extraction with sparse nonparametric estimation—to new environments.

This framework weaves together three powerful components to address the aforementioned challenges. First, the low-dimensional factor structure enables tractable estimation in high-dimensional settings by capturing the latent drivers shared across domains, acting as a bridge for representation transfer. Second, we employ deep ReLU networks to model the dense and sparse nonparametric complexities, leveraging their adaptive capacity to unknown composition structures to sidestep the curse of dimensionality. Third, by introducing flexible transfer functions, our method explicitly accounts for both covariate and posterior shifts. This mechanism allows the estimator to automatically calibrate the degree of source-target similarity, effectively “gating” the amount of source information to utilize. This synergy not only ensures robustness against negative transfer by pruning irrelevant source signals but also achieves minimax optimality under general nonparametric regimes.

1.1
Problem Formulation

Consider two datasets: source data
\{(\bm{x}_{i}^{P},y_{i}^{P})\}_{i=1}^{n^{P}}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P
and target data
\{(\bm{x}_{j}^{Q},y_{j}^{Q})\}_{j=1}^{n^{Q}}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}Q
, where
(\bm{x},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}
. The central question is:
how and under what conditions does access to source data accelerate learning on the target distribution
Q
?

Factor Model for Covariates.

To handle the high-dimensional regime where
p\gtrsim n^{Q}
, we assume the covariates admit a low-rank factor structure:

\bm{x}^{P}=\bm{B}^{P}\bm{f}^{P}+\bm{u}^{P},\qquad\bm{x}^{Q}=\bm{B}^{Q}\bm{f}^{Q}+\bm{u}^{Q},

(1)

where
\bm{B}^{P},\bm{B}^{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
are unknown loading matrices with
p\gg r
,
\bm{f}^{P},\bm{f}^{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
are latent factors, and
\bm{u}^{P},\bm{u}^{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are idiosyncratic components. No factor structure of covariates is allowed by taking
r=0
or
\bm{B}=0
.

Regression Functions.

For the source domain, we assume a sparse nonparametric model:

y_{i}^{P}=g^{P}(\bm{x}_{i,\pazocal{J}^{P}}^{P})+\epsilon_{i}^{P},\quad i\in[n^{P}],

(2)

where
g^{P}
depends on an unknown sparse subset
\pazocal{J}^{P}\subset[p]
of coordinates, and
\{\epsilon_{i}^{P}\}
are i.i.d. noise. As shown in Section
3.2
, this form facilitates transfer from the source to the target domain.
For the target domain, we assume a factor-augmented nonparametric model:

y_{j}^{Q}=g^{Q}(\bm{f}_{j}^{Q},\bm{u}_{j,\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q})+\epsilon_{j}^{Q},\quad j\in[n^{Q}],

(3)

where
g^{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{r+|\pazocal{J}^{Q}|}\to\mathbb{R}
depends on both the latent factors and a sparse subset
\pazocal{J}^{Q}\subset[p]
of idiosyncratic components. The inclusion of
\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q}
captures settings where some covariates or idiosyncratic entries carry predictive information beyond what the factors encode.
The versatility of the FAN model is discussed in Section
2.2
.

Two Types of Distribution Shift.

This framework accommodates two fundamental types of shift in transfer learning:

1.

Covariate Shift:
The marginal distribution of covariates differs across domains, manifested through
\bm{B}^{P}\neq\bm{B}^{Q}
. The methodological challenge is to estimate the target factors
\bm{f}^{Q}
accurately by leveraging source data when the loading matrices are similar but not identical.

2.

Posterior Shift:
The conditional distribution of responses differs, i.e.,
g^{P}\neq g^{Q}
. The statistical challenge is to learn the target function
g^{Q}
efficiently by exploiting the pre-trained source function
g^{P}
.

Residual Fine-Tuning.

To formalize how source knowledge transfers to the target, we decompose the target regression function via a residual structure:

g^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q})=h\left(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}}^{Q},g^{P}(\bm{x}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}}^{Q})\right),

(4)

where
h:\mathbb{R}^{r+|\pazocal{J}|+1}\to\mathbb{R}
is a low-complexity
residual fine-tuning function
and
\pazocal{J}^{Q}=\pazocal{J}\cup\pazocal{J}^{P}
. This formulation embeds the source function
g^{P}
as an input to
h
, capturing how target predictions refine source predictions. Our model is more general than additive residual models of the form
g^{Q}=g^{P}+h_{0}
and permits nonlinear compositions, including regime-switching behaviors such as
h=h_{0}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{g^{P}>0\}
. Crucially, the source function
g^{P}
remains frozen during fine-tuning; only
h
is estimated from target data. When
h
belongs to a simpler function class than
g^{Q}
, this decomposition yields both computational savings and faster statistical convergence. This is particularly relevant when
\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q}
is absent from (
4
), so that
h
can be learned using a low-dimensional neural network. This point is elaborated further in Section
2
.

1.2
Preview of Main Results

Our theoretical analysis makes the intuition behind residual fine-tuning precise. We establish that the fine-tuning FAN-Lasso estimator achieves the minimax optimal excess risk

\left[\frac{\log(n^{P}+n^{Q})}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}\right]^{\frac{2\gamma^{P}}{2\gamma^{P}+1}}+\left(\frac{\log n^{Q}}{n^{Q}}\right)^{\frac{2\gamma}{2\gamma+1}}+\frac{\log p}{n^{Q}}+\frac{1}{p},

where
\gamma^{P}
and
\gamma
quantify the complexity, such as the dimensionality-adjusted degree of smoothness, of the source function
g^{P}
and the residual function
h
, respectively. This rate admits a revealing decomposition: the first term reflects the joint contribution of source and target data to learning
g^{P}
—the complex backbone that both domains share—while the second term captures the irreducible cost of estimating the residual
h
from target data alone. When source data is plentiful (
n^{P}\gg n^{Q}
) and the source function is genuinely more complex than the residual (
\gamma^{P}<\gamma
), fine-tuning delivers a provable acceleration over direct estimation of
g^{Q}
. We complement this upper bound with a matching minimax lower bound, confirming that our rates are sharp up to logarithmic factors.

A distinctive virtue of our method is its
automatic robustness to negative transfer
. When source data offers no genuine advantage—either because samples are scarce (
n^{P}\ll n^{Q}
) or because the residual
h
is no simpler than the target function—our estimator gracefully degrades to the optimal rate achievable without any source data. This safeguard requires no oracle knowledge of the source-target relationship; it emerges organically from the structure of our estimator.

2.1
Factor Augmented Nonparametric (FAN) model

We continue to use the notation introduced in §
1.1
and focus on a factor-augmented nonparametric regression framework. Specifically, we assume

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[y^{P}|\bm{f}^{P},\bm{u}^{P}]

\displaystyle=g^{P}(\bm{x}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}}^{P}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[y^{Q}|\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}^{Q}]

\displaystyle=g^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q}),

where
\pazocal{J}^{P},\pazocal{J}^{Q}\subset\{1,\ldots,p\}
are unknown subsets of indices representing the specific locations of the idiosyncratic noise (or original features) that possess additional predictive power beyond the common factors. Here, the source regression function
g^{P}
depends directly on a subset of the observable source covariates, or equivalently on a factor
\bm{f}^{P}
and idiosyncratic component
\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}}^{P}
in a more specific way, since
\bm{x}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}}^{P}=[\bm{B}_{\pazocal{J}^{P},:}]\bm{f}^{P}+\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}}^{P}
. In contrast, the target regression function
g^{Q}
depends more generally on both the latent factors
\bm{f}^{Q}
and a subset of idiosyncratic components
\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q}
. As discussed in Section
3.2
, the specific form for
g^{P}
facilitates fine-tuning by allowing the pre-trained source model to be applied directly to target covariates
\bm{x}^{Q}
without needing to estimate latent factors first. Note that given the latent factor
\bm{f}^{Q}
,
\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q}
is equivalent to
\bm{x}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q}
so that
g^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q})\equiv\tilde{g}^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{x}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q})
, a factor augmented sparse nonparametric model or FAN-model.

Let
\epsilon^{P}=y^{P}-\mathbb{E}[y^{P}|\bm{f}^{P},\bm{u}^{P}]
and
\epsilon^{Q}=y^{Q}-\mathbb{E}[y^{Q}|\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}^{Q}]
denote the noise terms for the source and target domains, respectively. The data-generating process is summarized by

\begin{split}\bm{x}_{i}^{P}&=\bm{B}^{P}\bm{f}_{i}^{P}+\bm{u}_{i}^{P},\quad y_{i}^{P}=g^{P}(\bm{x}_{i,\pazocal{J}^{P}}^{P})+\epsilon_{i}^{P},\quad\forall i\in[n^{P}],\\
\bm{x}_{j}^{Q}&=\bm{B}^{Q}\bm{f}_{j}^{Q}+\bm{u}_{j}^{Q},\quad y_{j}^{Q}=g^{Q}(\bm{f}_{j}^{Q},\bm{u}_{j,\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q})+\epsilon_{j}^{Q},\quad\forall j\in[n^{Q}].\end{split}

(6)

Our primary objective is to improve the estimation of the target regression function by leveraging information from the source domain through fine-tuning. For any candidate estimator
m:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
, its performance is evaluated by the population
L_{2}
risk on the target distribution:

\displaystyle R(m)=\mathbb{E}[|y^{Q}-m(\bm{x}^{Q})|^{2}].

By (
1
), the target regression mean can be written as
g^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q})
. Although
\bm{u}^{Q}
is latent, once
\bm{f}^{Q}
is estimated as in Section
3
,
\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q}
is determined by
\bm{x}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q}-[\bm{B}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q},:}^{Q}]\bm{f}^{Q}
. We therefore define the excess risk of an empirical estimator
\widehat{m}
on the target domain as

\displaystyle\pazocal{E}^{Q}(\widehat{m})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[|\widehat{m}(\bm{x}^{Q})-g^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}}^{Q})|^{2}\right]=R(\widehat{m})-R(g^{Q}).

(7)

Our goal is to develop a fine-tuning strategy that effectively leverages the source domain information to enhance the target estimation. By successfully transferring the shared structure from the source data, we aim to improve the learning efficiency and performance of the resulting estimator
\widehat{m}
, making the fine-tuning process more effective than training solely on the target data, especially when the target sample size is much smaller than the source sample size.

2.2
Versatility of the FAN model

The FAN model (
6
), or more specifically (
3
), uses both common factors and idiosyncratic components in the regression. Every covariate contributes through the latent common factors, and some covariates (or equivalently idiosyncratic components) provide additional contributions. It is very versatile and includes the sparse nonparametric model (
2
) as a special case. When the covariates are weakly correlated, we can take
r=0
(no factors), in which case the model reduces to a sparse nonparametric model; when
g^{Q}
is linear, it reduces to a sparse linear model. When
\pazocal{J}^{Q}=\emptyset
, it becomes a nonparametric factor model; when
g^{Q}
is linear, it reduces to a principal component regression model. In general, the model allows dependence among covariates and is insensitive to slight overselection of
r
, which is equivalent to the last few columns of
\bm{B}^{Q}
being zero.

2.3
Hierarchical decomposition of regression functions

The convergence rate of the excess risk is fundamentally determined by the structural properties of the regression functions
g^{P}
and
g^{Q}
. In nonparametric regression, it is often assumed that these functions possess a hierarchical structure, where the final output is a composition of several simpler, lower-dimensional functions. This is formally captured by the
hierarchical composition model
(HCM)
(
28
)
. Roughly speaking, the HCM for a
d
-dimensional function is a finite (
l
-fold) composition of
t
-dimensional functions with
(\beta,C)
-smoothness, for
(t,\beta)
in a prescribed set
\pazocal{P}
. This structure is particularly well suited to deep ReLU networks, which can adaptively learn such unknown structures.

Definition 2

(Hierarchical composition model)
.

The function class
\pazocal{H}(d,l,\pazocal{P},C)
, where
l,d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
C>0
, and
\pazocal{P}\subset[1,\infty]\times\mathbb{N}^{+}
, is defined as follows. For
l=1
,

\displaystyle\pazocal{H}(d,1,\pazocal{P},C)=\{h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:h(x)=g(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(t)}),\text{ where }

\displaystyle g:\mathbb{R}^{t}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ is }(\beta,C)\text{-smooth for some }(\beta,t)\in\pazocal{P}\text{ and }\pi:[t]\rightarrow[d]\}.

For
l>1
,

\displaystyle\pazocal{H}(d,l,\pazocal{P},C)=\{h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:h(x)=g(f_{1}(\bm{x}),\dots,f_{t}(\bm{x})),\text{ where }

\displaystyle g:\mathbb{R}^{t}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ is }(\beta,C)\text{-smooth for some }(\beta,t)\in\pazocal{P}\text{ and }f_{i}\in\pazocal{H}(d,l-1,\pazocal{P},C)\}.

To simplify the presentation, we assume all component functions have a smoothness parameter
\beta\geq 1
, namely,
\min_{(\beta,t)\in\pazocal{P}}\beta\geq 1
. The complexity of the regression function is determined by its “hardest” component. For any set of parameters
\pazocal{P}
, we define the effective smoothness
\gamma(\pazocal{P})
as

\gamma(\pazocal{P})=\min_{(\beta,t)\in\pazocal{P}}\frac{\beta}{t}.

(8)

It represents the smallest dimensionality-adjusted degree of smoothness and serves as a measure of functional complexity; a smaller
\gamma(\pazocal{P})
indicates a more complex function that is harder to estimate.

Transferability

We introduce our main assumption regarding the relationship between

Assumption 1

3.

The residual function
h(\bm{f},\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}},s):\mathbb{R}^{r+|\pazocal{J}|+1}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
h\in\pazocal{H}(r+|\pazocal{J}|+1,l,\pazocal{P},c_{0})
with complexity
\gamma:=\gamma(\pazocal{P})<\infty
.

We refer to
h
as the
residual fine-tuning function
.

Assumption
1
models the scenario where
g^{P}
provides a useful feature or “template” for the target task. This formulation generalizes several popular transfer learning and domain adaptation models
(
56
;
55
;
1
)
. A specific instance is
g^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}})=h\left(\bm{f}^{Q},g^{P}(\bm{x}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})\right)
in which
h
is only a
(r+1)
-dimensional function and can easily be learned by using neural networks. It includes the models
g^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}})=a^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q})g^{P}(\bm{x}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})+b^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q})
and
g^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{Q}})=g^{P}(\bm{x}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})+b^{Q}(\bm{f}^{Q})
as specific examples. In the latter case, we need only to fit the residuals in the target data against the learned factors
\bm{f}^{Q}
.

A key feature of our model is the use of
g^{P}(\bm{x}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})
—the source model evaluated directly on the observable target covariates—rather than a function of the latent factors and idiosyncratic components
g^{P}(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})
. This choice is motivated by several practical and theoretical considerations:

•

Computational Efficiency:
Using observable covariates
\bm{x}^{Q}
avoids the step of estimating latent factors for the target domain before applying the pre-trained model.

•

Robustness to Distribution Shift:
This formulation is more robust to the loading matrix shifts (
\bm{B}^{Q}\neq\bm{B}^{P}
). By bypassing factor estimation, we prevent the propagation of errors that would occur if the source model were applied to incorrectly estimated target factors.

•

Implicit Information Capture:
Since
\bm{x}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}}=[\bm{B}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{P},:}]\bm{f}^{Q}+\bm{u}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}}
, the observable covariates already encode the relevant factor information. Explicitly including factors as separate arguments would introduce redundancy without increasing the model’s expressive power.

•

Compatibility with Standard Pre-trained Models:
The formulation does not necessitate that the source model be trained within a factor-augmented framework. Because
g^{P}
operates directly on observable features, our approach can seamlessly incorporate any off-the-shelf regression model as a pre-trained backbone, significantly expanding the range of applicable source models beyond those specifically designed for latent factor analysis.

The proposition below shows that under the transferability assumption, the full target function
g^{Q}
also belongs to an HCM class, and its complexity is determined by its most complex component.

Proposition 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, we have

\displaystyle g^{P}([\bm{B}^{P}_{\pazocal{J}^{P},:}]\bm{f}+\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})

\displaystyle\in\pazocal{H}(r+|\pazocal{J}^{P}|,l^{P}+1,\pazocal{P}^{P}\cup\{(\infty,r+|\pazocal{J}^{P}|)\},c_{0}\lor\lVert\bm{B}^{P}_{\pazocal{J}^{P},:}\rVert_{2}),

\displaystyle g^{Q}(\bm{f},\bm{u}_{\pazocal{J}\cup\pazocal{J}^{P}})

\displaystyle\in\pazocal{H}(r+|\pazocal{J}\cup\pazocal{J}^{P}|,l+l^{P}+1,\pazocal{P}\cup\pazocal{P}^{P}\cup\{(\infty,r+|\pazocal{J}^{P}|)\},c_{0}\lor\lVert\bm{B}^{P}_{\pazocal{J}^{P},:}\rVert_{2}).

Furthermore, the combined complexity of the latter (target function class) satisfies
\gamma(\pazocal{P}\cup\pazocal{P}^{P}\cup\{(\infty,r+|\pazocal{J}^{P}|)\})=\min\{\gamma,\gamma^{P}\}
.

Proposition
1
provides the theoretical foundation for the benefits of fine-tuning. It shows that while the target function
g^{Q}
is a composition of both
g^{P}
and
h
, and its overall complexity—which dictates the minimax convergence rate—is determined by the “bottleneck” or the most complex component,
\min\{\gamma,\gamma^{P}\}
. This result reveals a significant opportunity for transfer learning. If we were to estimate
g^{Q}
from scratch using only target data, the convergence rate of the excess risk would be limited by this joint complexity. However, in the fine-tuning paradigm, we leverage the fact that
g^{P}
has already been learned from a potentially much larger source dataset (
n^{P}\gg n^{Q}
). By substituting a high-quality pre-trained estimator
\widehat{g}^{P}
into the decomposition (
9
), the remaining learning task reduces to estimating the residual function
h
, which is of lower dimension and typically lower complexity.

3
Methodology

In this section, we present our methodology for high-dimensional factor-augmented nonparametric variable selection using transfer learning. Our approach addresses two primary types of domain shifts: (i)
covariate shift
, where source data is used to improve the estimation of target latent factors
\bm{f}^{Q}
, and (ii)
posterior shift
, where the pre-trained source regression function
g^{P}
is leveraged to enhance the estimation of the target regression function
g^{Q}
through a fine-tuning mechanism.

3.1
Diversified projection matrix for factor estimation

Traditional neural networks often use the high-dimensional covariates
\bm{x}
directly as input. In contrast, our method incorporates a factor-augmented structure by introducing a
diversified projection matrix

\bm{W}
as a pre-processing module. This matrix facilitates the estimation of the latent factors
\bm{f}
from the observable
\bm{x}
.

Let
\overline{r}\geq r
be the number of working latent factors. Following the construction in
16
, we define the diversified projection matrix as follows.

Definition 3

(Diversified projection matrix)
.

For any domain
*\in\{P,Q\}
, a matrix
\bm{W}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times\overline{r}}
is a
diversified projection matrix
if it satisfies:

1.

(Boundedness)
\lVert\bm{W}^{*}\rVert_{\max}\leq C_{1}
for a universal constant
C_{1}>0
;

2.

(Exogeneity)
\bm{W}^{*}
is independent of the data
\{\bm{x}^{*}_{i},y^{*}_{i}\}
;

3.

(Significance) The matrix
\bm{H}^{*}=p^{-1}(\bm{W}^{*})^{\top}\bm{B}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{\overline{r}\times r}
satisfies
\nu_{\min}(\bm{H}^{*})\gg p^{-1/2}
.

Each column of
\bm{W}^{*}
is referred to as a
diversified projection weight
, and
\overline{r}
denotes the number of projection directions.

Given the source and target diversified projection matrices
\bm{W}^{P}
and
\bm{W}^{Q}
, we define the
\bar{r}
-dimensional surrogate factor vectors
\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{P}
and
\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}
as

\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{P}=p^{-1}(\bm{W}^{P})^{\top}\bm{x}^{P},\quad\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}=p^{-1}(\bm{W}^{Q})^{\top}\bm{x}^{Q}.

(10)

The pre-defined
diversified projection matrix

\bm{W}
is introduced in this work to solve
the issue that the “working number of factors”,

\overline{r}
, may not be accurately determined, and
methods incorporating the
diversified projection
matrix

\bm{W}
are robust to overestimation
of the factor number
r
. By substituting the factor model (
1
) into the surrogate factor estimation (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(p^{-1}(\bm{W}^{*})^{\top}\bm{B}^{*}\right)}_{\text{affine transformation}}\bm{f}^{*}+\underbrace{p^{-1}(\bm{W}^{*})^{\top}\bm{u}^{*}}_{\text{estimation error}}.

The significance assumption ensures that the signal from the latent factors (the affine transformation) remains dominant, while the weak dependence of the idiosyncratic components
\bm{u}^{*}
ensures that the estimation error vanishes at rate
O_{P}(p^{-1/2})
as
p\to\infty
.

3.2
Transfer factor estimation for covariate shift

We leverage the source domain data to improve the quality of the surrogate factors
\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}
. This is particularly beneficial when the target sample size
n^{Q}
is small, leading to noisy estimates of the target covariance structure. When the source sample size
n^{P}
is large, the pooled covariance
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{A}
provides a more stable estimate due to the larger effective sample size.

We first define the sample covariance matrices for the source, target, and pooled data:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{P}=\frac{1}{n^{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{P}}\bm{x}^{P}_{i}(\bm{x}^{P}_{i})^{\top},\\
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{Q}=\frac{1}{n^{Q}}\sum_{j=1}^{n^{Q}}\bm{x}^{Q}_{j}(\bm{x}^{Q}_{j})^{\top},\\
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{A}=\frac{n^{P}}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{P}+\frac{n^{Q}}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{Q}.\end{cases}

For any
*\in\{P,Q,A\}
, let
\widehat{\bm{v}}^{*}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\bm{v}}^{*}_{\overline{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be the top-
{\overline{r}}
eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{*}
, and define
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{*}=p^{1/2}[\widehat{\bm{v}}^{*}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\bm{v}}^{*}_{\overline{r}}]
as the corresponding diversified projection matrix. In the absence of source data, a common approach is to rely exclusively on the target domain’s top eigenvectors,
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{Q}
, to estimate the latent factors. However, when source data is available, we can incorporate additional information through a model-selection process for covariance estimation. The rationale is that when the target and source domains share a similar latent structure, the pooled covariance
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{A}
provides a more stable estimate than
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{Q}
alone, particularly in the high-dimensional regime where
n^{Q}
may be small.

Specifically, we extract the factors by bounding the difference between the target and aggregation covariance with a pre-determined threshold
\delta
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}=\begin{cases}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{A},\quad p^{-1}\lVert\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{Q}-\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{A}\rVert_{F}\leq\delta,\\
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{Q},\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

This strategy follows the “Transfer Around Boundary (TAB)” philosophy
(
11
)
, where source information is utilized only when there is high confidence that the domains are sufficiently close, effectively protecting against biased estimation when domain shift is significant. Letting
\widehat{\bm{v}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\bm{v}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}_{\overline{r}}
be the top-
\overline{r}
eigenvectors of
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}
, we then define the model-selected projection matrix as
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}=p^{1/2}[\widehat{\bm{v}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\bm{v}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}_{\overline{r}}]

and the surrogate factor
\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}
as

\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}=p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}})^{\top}\bm{x}^{Q}=\begin{cases}p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{A})^{\top}\bm{x}^{Q},\quad p^{-1}\lVert\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{Q}-\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{A}\rVert_{F}\leq\delta\\
p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{Q})^{\top}\bm{x}^{Q},\quad\text{ otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

(11)

Similarly, the factors for the source domain are given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{P}=\begin{cases}p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{A})^{\top}\bm{x}^{P},\quad p^{-1}\lVert\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{P}-\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{A}\rVert_{F}\leq\delta\\
p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{P})^{\top}\bm{x}^{P},\quad\text{ otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

For simplicity in the subsequent nonparametric analysis, we assume
\bm{W}^{P}
and
\bm{W}^{Q}
are obtained through this procedure or provided by prior knowledge.

3.3
Fine-tuning nonparametric variable selection for posterior shift

We utilize a fine-tuning approach to address the posterior shift in transfer learning. The intuition behind this approach is straightforward: the complex component of the regression function,
g^{P}
, can be more accurately learned using the source data due to its typically large sample size. By providing the resulting estimate
\widehat{g}^{P}
as an additional input feature for estimating
g^{Q}
, we significantly reduce the functional complexity of the target learning task. In this framework, the target network only needs to learn the simpler residual component
h
from the limited target data.

To handle the high-dimensionality, we employ a sparse selection mechanism using a continuous relaxation of the
L_{0}
penalty. Define the clipped-
L_{1}
function with threshold
\tau>0
as

\displaystyle\psi_{\tau}(x)=\frac{|x|}{\tau}\land 1.

This function serves as a continuous approximation of the indicator function
\mathbbm{1}\{x\neq 0\}
. The parameter
\tau
is typically chosen to be very small to more precisely approximate the selection indicator. This clip function was first proposed by
15
and has been widely adopted in high-dimensional linear regression
54
, deep neural network estimation with sparse weights
35
and the FAST-NN model
13
.

Specifically, our estimator for the source regression function is obtained by training a deep ReLU network
\pazocal{G}^{P}=\pazocal{G}(L^{P},r+N^{P},N^{P},M,T^{P})
:

(\widehat{g}^{P},\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}^{P})=\argmin_{g\in\pazocal{G}^{P},\bm{\Theta}^{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times N^{P}}}\frac{1}{n^{P}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{P}}\Big(y^{P}_{i}-g\big(\big[\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{P}_{i},\mathrm{trun}_{M}((\bm{\Theta}^{P})^{\top}\bm{x}^{P}_{i})\big]\big)\Big)^{2}+\lambda^{P}\sum_{i,j}\psi_{\tau}(\bm{\Theta}^{P}_{i,j}).

(12)

Following
13
, we estimate the idiosyncratic throughput
\bm{u}_{i}^{P}
by using sparse linear combinations
(\bm{\Theta}^{P})^{\top}\bm{x}^{P}_{i}
to select a subset of relevant features. This results in the pre-trained FAST-NN model

\displaystyle\widehat{s}(\bm{x})=\widehat{g}^{P}\Big(\Big[p^{-1}(\bm{W}^{P})^{\top}\bm{x},\mathrm{trun}_{M}\Big((\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}^{P})^{\top}\bm{x}\Big)\Big]\Big),

(13)

for the source data. Note that even though we assume the regression function is
g^{P}(\bm{x}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})
, we learn it through FAST-NN. A similar lifting idea appears in high-dimensional sparse linear regression
(
14
)
, which uses the weakly correlated latent factors and idiosyncratic components
[\bm{f}^{P},\bm{u}^{P}]
rather than the highly correlated original variable
\bm{x}^{P}
as input variables.

The pre-trained function from the source data can now be transferred to the target data:

\widehat{s}_{j}=\widehat{s}(\bm{x}^{Q}_{j}),\quad\forall j\in[n^{Q}].

(14)

Upon acquiring these signals, we incorporate them as an additional input feature for fitting the target regression function, using a deep ReLU network
\pazocal{G}=\pazocal{G}(L,r+N+1,N,M,T)
:

(\widehat{h},\widehat{\bm{\Theta}})=\argmin_{h\in\pazocal{G},\bm{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times N}}\frac{1}{n^{Q}}\sum_{j=1}^{n^{Q}}\Big(y_{j}^{Q}-h\big(\big[\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}_{j},\mathrm{trun}_{M}(\bm{\Theta}^{\top}\bm{x}^{Q}_{j}),\widehat{s}_{j}\big]\big)\Big)^{2}+\lambda\sum_{i,j}\psi_{\tau}(\bm{\Theta}_{i,j}),

(15)

where
\{M,\tau,L,L^{P},N,N^{P},T,T^{P},\lambda,\lambda^{P}\}
are tuning hyper-parameters. The final fine-tuned factor-augmented estimator is:

\widehat{m}_{\mbox{\scriptsize FT}}(\bm{x})=\widehat{h}\Big(\big[p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}})^{\top}\bm{x},\mathrm{trun}_{M}(\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}^{\top}\bm{x}),\widehat{s}(\bm{x})\big]\Big).

(16)

By including
\widehat{s}(\bm{x})
as an input, the target network
\widehat{h}
only needs to learn the residual relationship, which is typically simpler and more amenable to estimation with a smaller sample size
n^{Q}
. In particular, when there is no idiosyncratic component in fine-tuning, we can set
\bm{\Theta}=0
in (
15
), so the procedure reduces to a neural network regression with
(r+1)
-dimensional inputs
\big[\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}_{j},\widehat{s}_{j}\big]
. A further simplification is that
h(\cdot)
depends linearly on
\big[\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}_{j},\widehat{s}_{j}\big]
or even just univariate
\widehat{s}_{j}
. This is particularly suitable when
n^{Q}
is very small.

Note that (
12
) and (
15
) both involve the Factor Augmented Neural Lasso. We refer to the estimator (
16
) as a (fine-tuning) FAN-Lasso to distinguish it from the FAST-NN estimator (
12
).

4
Theory for factor transfer

In this section, we present the theoretical results for the methods in Section
3
. The following assumption is essential for transferring useful source information for factor models. It controls the maximum discrepancy between the factor loading matrices
\bm{B}^{P}
and
\bm{B}^{Q}
, thereby ensuring the similarity between the generating distributions of the covariates
\bm{x}^{P}
and
\bm{x}^{Q}
.

Assumption 2

(Factor loading similarity)
.

We assume the factor decomposition (
1
) satisfies that

\displaystyle p^{-1}\lVert\bm{B}^{P}(\bm{B}^{P})^{\top}-\bm{B}^{Q}(\bm{B}^{Q})^{\top}\rVert_{F}\leq\varepsilon

for some constant
\varepsilon>0
.

Next, we impose several regularity conditions on the covariate and factor model settings.

Assumption 3

(Boundedness)
.

There exist universal constants
c_{1}
and
b
such that

1.

\lVert\bm{B}^{P}\rVert_{max}\lor\lVert\bm{B}^{Q}\rVert_{max}\leq c_{1}
;

2.

\mathbb{E}[\bm{f}^{*}]=0,\ \mathbb{E}[\bm{f}^{*}(\bm{f}^{*})\top]=\bm{I}_{r},\ \mathrm{supp}(\bm{f}^{*})\subset[-b,b]^{r}
for any
*\in\{P,Q\}
;

3.

\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}^{*}]=0,\ \mathrm{supp}(\bm{u}^{*})\subset[-b,b]^{p}
for any
*\in\{P,Q\}
.

Assumption 4

(Weak dependence)
.

There exists some universal constant
c_{1}
such that for any
*\in\{P,Q\},

1.

(Between entries of
\bm{u}^{*}
)
\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq p}|\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}^{*}_{j}\bm{u}^{*}_{k}]|\leq c_{1}\cdot p
;

2.

(Between
\bm{f}^{*}
and
\bm{u}^{*}
)
\lVert\bm{B}^{*}\mathbb{E}[\bm{f}^{*}(\bm{u}^{*})^{\top}]\rVert_{F}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{p}
.

Assumption 5

(Pervasiveness)
.

There exists some universal constant
c_{1}\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}((\bm{B}^{P})^{\top}\bm{B}^{P})\land\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}((\bm{B}^{Q})^{\top}\bm{B}^{Q})\geq p/c_{1}

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}((\bm{B}^{P})^{\top}\bm{B}^{P})\lor\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}((\bm{B}^{Q})^{\top}\bm{B}^{Q})\leq c_{1}\cdot p.

Assumptions
3
–
5
are standard in factor models. We replace the sub-Gaussian condition for
\bm{f}^{P},\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}^{P}
, and
\bm{u}^{Q}
with uniform boundedness in Assumption
3
. Assumptions
4
and
5
on the distribution of
(\bm{f}^{*},\bm{u}^{*})
for any
*\in\{P,Q\}
lay the foundation of the validity of the chosen diversified projection matrices, ensuring that the condition
\nu_{\min}(\bm{H}^{Q})\asymp 1
holds, as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Factor transfer)
.

Under Assumptions
2
-
5
, the matrix
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}
proposed in (
11
) satisfies

\nu_{max}\left(p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}})^{\top}\bm{B}^{Q}\right)\leq c_{3}.

(17)

Moreover, for any
t>0
,
such that
\delta\geq c_{3}\varepsilon^{Q}(t)
, we have, with probability at least
1-6e^{-t}
,

\nu_{min}\left(p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}})^{\top}\bm{B}^{Q}\right)\geq c_{1}-c_{2}\delta\land\varepsilon^{A}(t).

(18)

for some universal constants
c_{1}
-
c_{3}
that are independent of
n^{P},n^{Q},p,t,r,\overline{r}
, where

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{Q}(t)

\displaystyle=r\sqrt{\frac{\log p+t}{n^{Q}}}+r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log r+t}{n^{Q}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{A}(t)

\displaystyle=r\sqrt{\frac{\log p+t}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}}+r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log r+t}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}+\varepsilon.

Theorem
1
indicates that the source data can enhance transfer learning of the target latent factor loadings by enabling the construction of a more statistically robust diversified projection matrix,
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}
, compared to
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{Q}
that is derived without access to the source data. The choice of
t
can be of the order
\log n^{Q}
. The process of learning the source diversified projection matrix
\bm{W}^{P}
is similar, incorporating the target data. However, since
n^{P}
is typically large, it is often sufficient to use only
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{P}
. For the choice of
\delta
, since we only require the right-hand side of (
18
) to be lower bounded by a positive constant, a precise or tight choice of
\delta
is unnecessary.

In the high-dimensional regime with
p\gg 1
, and without source data, we can construct surrogates of the latent factors
\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}
when
n^{Q}\gg(\log p+t)
. However, even when
n^{Q}
is small, a robust estimate can still be achieved if
n^{P}\gg(\log p+t)
and the true factor loading difference
\epsilon
is sufficiently small. Thus, a small subset of the target and source data can be used to learn the diversified weights with
\nu_{\min}(\bm{H}^{Q})\asymp 1
, depending on the specific conditions of
n^{P}
,
n^{Q}
, and any prior information about
\epsilon
. When
\epsilon
is believed to be small or zero, splitting samples from the source data alone is sufficient. Otherwise, it is recommended to select samples from both the target and source domains, and we can sample more source data since the target data is typically rare.

In addition, we provide the perturbation results regarding the covariance matrix estimate
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}
and the eigenspaces.

Proposition 2

(Perturbation bounds)
.

Under the assumptions and notations of Theorem
1
, for some universal constant
c_{4}
we have

1.

\lVert\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}-\bm{B}^{Q}(\bm{B}^{Q})^{\top}\rVert_{F}\lesssim p\Big(\delta\land\varepsilon^{A}(t)\Big)
;

2.

\underset{\bm{R}\in\pazocal{O}(r)}{\min}\lVert\widehat{\bm{V}}_{r}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}\bm{R}-\bm{V}^{Q}\rVert_{F}\lesssim\delta\land\varepsilon^{A}(t)
.

with probability at least
1-6e^{-t}
given
t>0
such that
\delta\geq c_{4}\varepsilon^{Q}(t)
. Here,
\pazocal{O}(r)
denotes the space of orthogonal matrices of size
r\times r
, and
\widehat{\bm{V}}_{r}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}
and
\bm{V}^{Q}
represent the top-

eigenvectors of
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}
and
\bm{B}^{Q}(\bm{B}^{Q})^{\top}
, respectively.

5
Theory for regression fine-tuning

In this section, we aim to establish high probability bounds for both the out-of-sample excess risk
\pazocal{E}^{Q}(\widehat{m}_{\mbox{\scriptsize FT}})
and the in-sample mean squared error, denoted as
\widehat{\pazocal{E}}^{Q}(\widehat{m}_{\mbox{\scriptsize FT}})
for FAN-Lasso, where

\displaystyle\widehat{\pazocal{E}}^{Q}(\widehat{m}_{\mbox{\scriptsize FT}})\overset{def}{=}\frac{1}{n^{Q}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{Q}}|\widehat{m}_{\mbox{\scriptsize FT}}(\bm{x}_{i}^{Q})-g^{Q}(\bm{f}_{i}^{Q},\bm{u}^{Q}_{i,\pazocal{J}\cup\pazocal{J}^{P}})|^{2}.

For justifications on the importance of deriving high-probability error bounds for both in-sample and out-of-sample
l_{2}
errors, see
17
.

5.1
Oracle-type upper bound

Before presenting the results about how the fine-tuning factor augmented estimator in (
16
) improves the risk convergence rate, we impose two additional assumptions as follows.

Assumption 6
.

The model inputs satisfy that

1.

(Sub-Gaussian noise) There exists a universal constant
c_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\epsilon^{*}|\geq t\,|\,\bm{x}^{*})\leq 2\exp(-c_{1}t^{2})

for all
t>0
and
*\in\{P,Q\}
almost surely.

2.

(Bounded Regression function) We have
\max\{\lVert g^{P}\rVert_{\infty},\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}\}\leq M^{*}
for some universal constant
M^{*}.
The choice of
M
satisfies that
1\leq M^{*}\leq M\leq c_{2}M^{*}
for some universal constant
c_{2}>1
. Additionally,
g^{P}
and
h
are all
c_{1}
-Lipschitz for some universal constant
c_{1}.

Assumption 7

(Absolute continuity)
.

The Radon-Nikodym derivative satisfies
\frac{d\mu_{\bm{x}^{Q}}}{d\mu_{\bm{x}^{P}}}(\bm{x})\leq c_{1}
almost surely for some universal constant
c_{1}
.

Assumption
6
is standard in the nonparametric regression literature and Assumption
7
ensures that any good source population estimate
\widehat{s}
for
g^{P}
, with a small
\mathbb{E}\Big[|\widehat{s}(\bm{x}^{P})-g^{P}(\bm{x}^{P}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})|^{2}\Big]
, also enables proportionally accurate learning for the target population estimate, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[|\widehat{s}(\bm{x}^{Q})-g^{P}(\bm{x}^{Q}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})|^{2}\Big]\lesssim\mathbb{E}\Big[|\widehat{s}(\bm{x}^{P})-g^{P}(\bm{x}^{P}_{\pazocal{J}^{P}})|^{2}\Big].

The intuition behind this is that the source marginal distribution for

should provide full coverage over the target marginal distribution for

, allowing the accuracy of non-parametric regression to transfer effectively. An assumption similar to the second statement is made in

5.2
Optimal bounds for hierarchical composition models

An explicit rate can be further achieved by carefully selecting the hyperparameters to balance the trade-off between the approximation error
\delta_{a}^{*}
and the stochastic error
\delta_{s}^{*}
. This choice requires understanding the neural network’s approximation capability as follows:

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m^{*}\in\pazocal{H}(d,l,\pazocal{P},C)\\
\gamma(\pazocal{P})=\gamma^{*},\lVert m^{*}\rVert_{\infty}\leq M^{*}\end{subarray}}\inf_{g\in\pazocal{G}(d,c_{1},c_{2}N,\infty,c_{3}N^{c_{4}})}\lVert g-m^{*}\rVert^{2}_{\infty}\lesssim N^{-4\gamma^{*}}

for some universal constants
c_{1}
-
c_{4}
, when
d
is upper bounded by a universal constant. See Theorem 4 of
13
for a rigorous statement. The following condition lists our choice of hyperparameters.

Condition 1
.

The deep ReLU neural network hyperparameters satisfy that

1.

r\leq\overline{r}\lesssim r+1
;

2.

c_{1}\{\log n^{P}\lor\log[\nu_{\min}(\bm{H}^{P})]^{-1}\}\leq\log T^{P}\lesssim\log n^{P},\ c_{1}\{\log n^{Q}\lor\log[\nu_{\min}(\bm{H}^{Q})]^{-1}\}\leq\log T\lesssim\log n^{Q}
;

3.

c_{1}\leq L^{P}\lesssim 1,\ c_{1}(n^{P}/\log n^{P})^{1/(4\gamma^{P}+2)}\leq N^{P}\lesssim(n^{P}/\log n^{P})^{1/(4\gamma^{P}+2)}
;

4.

c_{1}\leq L\lesssim 1,\ c_{1}(n^{Q}/\log n^{Q})^{1/(4\Gamma+2)}\leq N\lesssim(n^{Q}/\log n^{Q})^{1/(4\Gamma+2)}
.

where
\Gamma=\gamma\bm{1}\{n^{P}\geq n^{Q}\}+(\gamma^{P}\land\gamma)\bm{1}\{n^{P}<n^{Q}\}
. Here,
c_{1}
is some universal constant which only depends on
l^{P},\pazocal{P}^{P},l,\pazocal{P}
.

The choice of hyperparameters in Condition
1
aligns with the parameters specified in the approximation result in Theorem 4 of
13
. Note that the asymptotic order of
N
depends on the sample size, and requires a possibly larger order when
n^{Q}>n^{P}
. The intuition is that, when
n^{Q}>n^{P}
, the source data sample is too small to significantly accelerate the excess risk convergence rate, and we need a more complex architecture to directly estimate the full target regression function
g^{Q}
instead of just
h
.

Given the choice of hyperparameters, the following theorem shows the optimal rate for our fine-tuning factor-augmented estimator.

Theorem 3

(Optimal upper bound for the fine-tuning FAN-Lasso)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
3
,
4
,
6
and
7
hold. Consider the fine-tuning factor-augmented model in (
12
)-(
16
) with
r+|\pazocal{J}\cup\pazocal{J}^{P}|\leq c_{1}
for some universal constant
c_{1}
that satisfies Condition
1
, and

\displaystyle c_{2}\frac{\log(pn^{P})}{n^{P}}\leq\lambda^{P}\leq c_{3}\frac{\log(pn^{P})}{n^{P}},\quad c_{2}\frac{\log(pn^{Q})}{n^{Q}}\leq\lambda\leq c_{3}\frac{\log(pn^{Q})}{n^{Q}},\quad\tau^{-1}\geq(n^{P}\lor n^{Q})^{c_{4}}p

for some universal constants
c_{2}
-
c_{4}
independent of
n^{P},n^{Q}
, and
p
. Furthermore, suppose that
n^{P}\gtrsim(\log p)^{2\gamma^{P}+1}
. Then, for any universal constant
K\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-6p^{-K}
with respect to the source and target data, for
n^{P}
and
n^{Q}
large enough, we have

\displaystyle\pazocal{E}^{Q}(\widehat{m}_{\mbox{\scriptsize FT}})+\widehat{\pazocal{E}}^{Q}(\widehat{m}_{\mbox{\scriptsize FT}})\leq c_{5}K\Big\{\Big[\frac{\log(n^{P}+n^{Q})}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}\Big]^{\frac{2\gamma^{P}}{2\gamma^{P}+1}}

\displaystyle+\Big(\frac{\log n^{Q}}{n^{Q}}\Big)^{\frac{2\gamma}{2\gamma+1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\log p}{n^{Q}}\mathbbm{1}(|\pazocal{J}|\not=0)

\displaystyle+\frac{1\land r}{[\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{P})\land\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{Q})]p}\Big\},

where
c_{5}
is a universal constant that depends only on the constants in Assumptions
3
,
4
,
6
and Condition
1
.

Theorem
3
indicates that the asymptotic convergence rate for our proposed fine-tuning FAN-Lasso is determined by

\Big[\frac{\log(n^{P}+n^{Q})}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}\Big]^{\frac{2\gamma^{P}}{2\gamma^{P}+1}}+\Big(\frac{\log n^{Q}}{n^{Q}}\Big)^{\frac{2\gamma}{2\gamma+1}}+\frac{\log p}{n^{Q}}+\frac{1\land r}{[\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{P})\land\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{Q})]p}.

(20)

The third term is dominated by the second term under very mild conditions. We now explain each term in the upper bound (
20
).

•

The term
\Big[\frac{\log(n^{P}+n^{Q})}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}\Big]^{\frac{2\gamma^{P}}{2\gamma^{P}+1}}
represents the risk of estimating the source regression function
g^{P}
, with complexity parameter
\gamma^{P}
. It benefits from both the target and source samples, which is a key advantage of our fine-tuning procedure. When
n^{P}\gg n^{Q}
, we benefit from fine-tuning while still retaining the conventional rate when
n^{P}
is small.

•

The term
\Big(\frac{\log n^{Q}}{n^{Q}}\Big)^{\frac{2\gamma}{2\gamma+1}}
captures the risk of estimating the remaining component of
g^{Q}
, namely
h
, which has complexity parameter
\gamma
. Estimation of
h
is based only on target data.

•

The term
\frac{\log p}{n^{Q}}
reflects the uncertainty in variable selection for
\pazocal{J}
in the target regression function. The uncertainty of variable selection for the source regression function is dominated by the main terms and is omitted due to the condition
n^{P}\gtrsim(\log p)^{2\gamma^{P}+1}
.

•

The term
\frac{1\land r}{[\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{P})\land\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{Q})]p}
represents the risk associated with inferring latent factors from observed covariates in the target and source domains. When
r\neq 0
and
\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{*})\asymp 1
for
*\in\{P,Q\}
, this
p^{-1}
term is typically dominated by the other main terms under the high-dimensional regime.

Furthermore, Theorem
3
clearly shows how our fine-tuning procedure improves the statistical convergence rate. Without transferring from the source data to the target domain, the optimal asymptotic risk rate we can obtain is given in
13
as

\Big(\frac{\log n^{Q}}{n^{Q}}\Big)^{\frac{2\gamma^{P}}{2\gamma^{P}+1}}+\Big(\frac{\log n^{Q}}{n^{Q}}\Big)^{\frac{2\gamma}{2\gamma+1}}+\frac{\log p}{n^{Q}}+\frac{1\land r}{\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{P})\land\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{Q})p}.

(21)

Comparing (
20
) with this formula, we observe that with the following conditions

n^{P}\gg n^{Q},\quad\gamma^{P}<\gamma,

(22)

i.e., the source sample size is large enough, the source regression function
g^{P}
is indeed more complex than the remaining
h
component, we can accelerate the risk convergence via the fine-tuning estimator. On the other hand, even if the condition (
22
) is not satisfied, we maintain the optimal convergence rate as in (
21
), without involving source data. In this sense, our estimator is both “efficient” in improving the rate as in (
20
) and “robust” in avoiding negative transfer.

Remark 1
.

Given
\pazocal{J}=\emptyset
, the term
\frac{\log p}{n^{Q}}
in (
20
) is eliminated in Theorem
2
. This case corresponds to a scenario where the idiosyncratic noise does not contribute to learning
h
, which is possible because the information of the idiosyncratic noise is fully captured through
g^{P}
. Additionally, by letting
\lambda\to\infty
in (
15
), (
15
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(\cdot),\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}=\argmin_{h\in\pazocal{G}(L,r+1,N,M,T)}\frac{1}{n^{Q}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{Q}}\Big(y^{Q}_{i}-h(\Big[\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}_{i},\widehat{s}_{i}\Big])\Big)^{2}.

This optimization corresponds to the augmented FAR-NN procedure mentioned in
13
, with an added entry
\widehat{s}_{i}
. Following the proof structure of Theorem
3
and treating
\lambda\to\infty
with
\pazocal{J}=\emptyset
, we reach the upper bound:

\displaystyle\Big[\frac{\log(n^{P}+n^{Q})}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}\Big]^{\frac{2\gamma^{P}}{2\gamma^{P}+1}}+\Big(\frac{\log n^{Q}}{n^{Q}}\Big)^{\frac{2\gamma}{2\gamma+1}}+\frac{1\land r}{[\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{P})\land\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{Q})]p}.

The formula above mitigates the curse of high dimensionality and still converges as
n_{Q}
grows to infinity, even if
n_{Q}
is not large compared with
p
.

5.3
Minimax optimal lower bound

Define a family of distributions of
(\bm{f},\bm{u},\bm{x})
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\pazocal{P}(p,r,\rho)=\Big\{\mu(\bm{f},\bm{u},\bm{x}):\quad&\mathrm{supp}(\bm{f})\subset[-1,1]^{r},\,\mathrm{supp}(\bm{u})\subset[-1,1]^{p},\,\mathbb{E}[\bm{f}]=\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}]=0,\\
&\bm{f}\text{ and }\bm{u}\text{ independent, both have independent components,}\\
&\bm{x}=\bm{B}\bm{f}+\bm{u}\text{ with }\lVert\bm{B}\rVert_{\max}\leq 1\text{ and }\lambda_{\min}(\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{B})\geq\rho\Big\}.\end{split}

(23)

Besides the optimal upper bound obtained in Theorem
3
, we also provide the following minimax lower bound for
\pazocal{E}^{Q}(\widehat{m})
, where
\widehat{m}
is any general estimator depending on the target and source data.

Theorem 4

(Minimax optimal lower bound)
.

Consider the i.i.d. samples
\{\{(\bm{x}^{P}_{i},\bm{y}^{P}_{i})\}_{i\in[n^{P}]}\cup\{(\bm{x}^{Q}_{j},\bm{y}^{Q}_{j})\}_{j\in[n^{Q}]}\}
from the model (
6
) with
\{\epsilon^{P}_{i}\}_{i\in[n^{P}]}\cup\{\epsilon^{Q}_{j}\}_{j\in[n^{Q}]}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\pazocal{N}(0,1)
. Suppose further that

1.

There exists some
(\beta^{P},d^{P})\in\pazocal{P}
such that

\gamma^{P}=\gamma(\pazocal{P}^{P})=\frac{\beta^{P}}{d^{P}}
and
d^{P}\leq r+1\lesssim 1.

2.

There exists some
(\beta^{h},d^{h})\in\pazocal{P}
such that

\gamma=\gamma(\pazocal{P})=\frac{\beta^{h}}{d^{h}}
and
d^{h}\leq r+1\lesssim 1.

for some universal constant
c_{1}
. Then, for
n^{P}
and
n^{Q}
large enough, we have

\inf_{\widehat{m}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu_{(\bm{f}^{Q},\bm{u}^{Q},\bm{x}^{Q})}=\mu_{(\bm{f}^{P},\bm{u}^{P},\bm{x}^{P})}\in\pazocal{P}(p,r,\rho)\\
\text{Assumption \ref{ass:transferability} holds with $|\pazocal{J}|=1$}\end{subarray}}\pazocal{E}^{Q}(\widehat{m})\geq c_{2}\left\{(n^{P}+n^{Q})^{-\frac{2\gamma^{P}}{2\gamma^{P}+1}}+(n^{Q})^{-\frac{2\gamma}{2\gamma+1}}+\frac{\log p}{n^{Q}}+\frac{1}{\rho}\right\}

(24)

for some universal constant
c_{2}
independent of
n^{P},n^{Q}
and
\rho
, and the infimum is taken over all estimators based on the observations
\{(\bm{x}^{P}_{i},\bm{y}^{P}_{i})\}_{i\in[n^{P}]}\cup\{(\bm{x}^{Q}_{j},\bm{y}^{Q}_{j})\}_{j\in[n^{Q}]}
.

The fourth term
\frac{1}{\rho}
in the lower bound (
24
) reduces to
\frac{1}{p}
under the pervasiveness assumption (Assumption
5
) where

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}((\bm{B}^{P})^{\top}\bm{B}^{P})\asymp\lambda_{\min}((\bm{B}^{Q})^{\top}\bm{B}^{Q})\asymp p.

Thus, it matches the term
\frac{1\land r}{[\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{P})\land\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{Q})]p}
in (

) when

and
\nu^{2}_{\min}(\bm{H}^{*})\asymp 1
for
*\in\{P,Q\}
. Even when

, the term
\frac{1}{p}
is typically dominated by other main terms in (

6.1
Covariate Shift Estimation

Candidate methods.

We compare the reliability of our proposed transfer factor estimator with two other simple approaches under covariate shift:

\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}=p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}})^{T}\bm{x}^{Q}
(Transfer factor estimation),

\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}=p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{Q})^{T}\bm{x}^{Q}
(Vanilla-target factor estimation),

\widetilde{\bm{f}}^{Q}=p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{P})^{T}\bm{x}^{Q}
(Vanilla-source factor estimation).

As the goal is to learn latent factors in the target domain via a diversified projection, a natural measure is the smallest singular value
\nu_{\mathrm{min}}(p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}})^{T}\bm{B}^{Q})
for a given diversified projection
\widehat{\bm{W}}
.

Data generating process.
The source and target covariates both follow a factor model with
r=4
factors. For the source data, we generate

to form the loading matrix

\bm{f}^{P}=(f_{i}^{P})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and the idiosyncratic component
\bm{u}^{P}=(u_{i}^{P})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

b_{ij}^{Q}=b_{ij}^{P}+\epsilon_{ij}^{B}
with
\epsilon_{ij}^{B}\sim 0.5\times\mathrm{Rademacher}
and again generate
f_{i}^{Q},u_{i}^{Q}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}(-1,1)
.

We evaluate performance at a fixed covariate dimension
p=1000
. The source sample size is set to
n^{P}=\{100,150,200,250,300\}
, and we consider two target sample sizes,
n^{Q}\in\{7,10\}
with
n^{Q}=10
for illustrating the performance without transfer.
We set
\delta=r\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{log}(p)}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}}+r^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{log}(r)}{n^{P}+n^{Q}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}
and repeat simulation
100
times for each setting.

Results.

Figure

\nu_{\mathrm{min}}(p^{-1}(\widehat{\bm{W}}^{*})^{T}\bm{B}^{Q})
across different source sample sizes
n^{P}
for

•

Numerical Stability:
Our proposed
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize TL}}
remains numerically stable and robust by adaptively borrowing strength from the source domain, whereas the target-only approach (
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{Q}
) is less effective. It is particularly unstable when
n^{Q}=7
.

•

Shift Adaptation:
Our method effectively aligns the estimated loading space with the target domain by adaptively aggregating information, avoiding the persistent bias inherent in the source-only approach (
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{P}

n^{P}

with

outperforms the target-only approach (
\widehat{\bm{W}}^{Q}

n^{Q}=10
.

Factor Recovery Robustness:
Our model-selection strategy successfully recovers the latent factor space even under severe target sample size constraints, empirically confirming the theoretical guarantees established in Theorem
1
