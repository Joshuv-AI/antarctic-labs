Longitudinally altered default mode network and insula multimodal brain pattern in end-stage renal disease during sustained hemodialysis treatment
Authors: Chuang Liang, Wenhao Jiang, Jiayu Chen, Jessica A. Turner, Vince D. Calhoun, Christopher C. Abbott, Rongtao Jiang, Zening Fu, Lei Wu, Xiaoyan Wang, Shile Qi, Yonggui Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHemodialysis (HD) is the predominant treatment for end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Despite the efficacy of HD, the neurobiological underpinnings underlying high-risk complications remain unclear. In this study, using unsupervised fusion of functional and structural MRI, we identified a longitudinally altered default mode network (DMN)-insula pattern in ESRD receiving HD over 1-year follow-up (n = 39). This pattern was associated with cognition, and its related genes were enriched in biological processes involving DNA damage and repair, energy metabolism, and cellular activation. The baseline DMN-insula pattern demonstrated potential predictive value for follow-up cognition in ESRD. More importantly, these brain-cognition associations were validated in independent high-risk complications cohorts, including major depressive disorder (n = 60), mild cognitive impairment (n = 291), and Alzheimer’s disease (n = 77) by extracting the corresponding brain features and assessing their correlations with cognition. Collectively, this study may help researchers better understand the underlying mechanisms of ESRD receiving HD from a multimodal neuroimaging and molecular perspective.

Introduction

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) is the ultimate phase of chronic kidney failure with a permanent loss (>90%) of normal kidney function.1 The accumulation of urea and toxic metabolites in the blood and tissues for ESRD may result in various complications characterized by multiple organ dysfunction.2 Hemodialysis (HD) employs more effective mechanical modalities for the elimination of excess metabolic waste in the blood, rendering it the most predominant treatment for ESRD.3 Despite the efficacy of HD, ESRD patients receiving HD often suffer from severe cognitive dysfunction as well as being at higher risk for depression and dementia, which may be partially driven by altered brain function and structure.4,5,6,7 In addition, any disturbance in brain homeostasis eventually will lead to changes in gene expression to adapt to altered demands.8 However, cognitive dysfunction, depression, dementia, and altered gene expression in ESRD receiving HD are often neglected in clinical practice.

The brain and kidneys are considered the end organs on parallel trajectories that are often influenced by common risk factors. Individuals with ESRD exhibit atypical brain activity in local brain regions, as well as predominantly decreased intra- and inter-regional functional connectivity (FC), especially in default mode network (DMN), which is associated with poor cognitive performance.9 Decreased nodal centralities in the bilateral hippocampus, lower global efficiency of FC, and decreased regional homogeneity (ReHo) in frontal, parietal, and temporal cortex were observed in HD patients compared with no dialysis patients, and these alterations were related with cognitive performance and dialysis duration.10,11 A voxel-based morphometry analysis showed reduced gray matter volume (GMV) in prefrontal and middle temporal cortex in ESRD receiving HD.12 Additionally, cognitive measurements and dialysis duration related decreased GMV in insular and putamen were identified to account for the neurocognitive dysfunction in patients with HD.13 However, currently, there is no research on gene expression related to brain functional and structural changes during HD.

Apart from the cognitive impairments, there is a higher risk of ESRD receiving HD in developing elderly major depressive disorder (MDD), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), resulting in higher mortality.14,15 Potential reasons for the high prevalence of elderly MDD, MCI, and AD in ESRD include the common risk factors (such as cognitive impairment), the alteration of brain and psychosocial burden associated with ESRD and HD.16,17 However, the most existing neuroimaging studies focused on comparing brain abnormalities in ESRD patients and healthy control (HC) based on single modality analysis with cross-sectional (baseline) data. Few studies have longitudinally compared ESRD receiving HD to identify the longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern and the molecular mechanisms underlying this brain pattern, as well as their association with cognition, and further validated the association in external elderly MDD, MCI, and AD datasets within one study. In this study, we hypothesis that longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern is associated with cognitive impairments that can be validated in late-life MDD, MCI, and AD and drive the expression of a different set of genes in the brain.

In this study, combining longitudinal multimodal MRI, we aim to (1) identify longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern for ESRD receiving HD (Figures 1A and 1B); (2) assess the group difference of the identified brain pattern between ESRD and HC at baseline and 1-year follow-up (Figure 1C); (3) evaluate the relationships between the identified brain pattern and cognition in ESRD (Figure 1D); (4) perform longitudinal cognition prediction based on the baseline brain pattern to test whether the identified multimodal brain pattern have potential predictive value for follow-up cognitive performance (Figure 1E); (5) validate the association between the identified brain pattern and cognition in independent elderly MDD, MCI, and AD datasets (Figure 1F); and (6) identify the biological function of genes relatively over or under expressed in association with longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern (Figure 1G). Longitudinal cognition prediction for ESRD

The identified longitudinally altered brain pattern (four masked regions) at baseline was used to predict the cognitive measures for ESRD at 1-year follow-up based on XGBoost. Correlations and the mean absolute error (MAE) between the estimated MMSE scores and its true values were calculated to estimate the prediction performance. These baseline brain features showed potential predictive value for follow-up MMSE for ESRD, with Pearson correlation of r = 0.55 (95%CI = [0.25, 0.77], p = 2.47e−04) and MAE = 1.0 (Figures 4A and 4B). Discussion

This study identified longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern for ESRD receiving HD and explored their potential predictive value for follow-up cognition, as well as further identified the biological functions of its associated genes. The identified associations between the longitudinally altered pattern and cognition were validated in independent elderly MDD, MCI, and AD cohorts. Strengths included using comprehensive approaches: data-driven multimodal fusion, longitudinal prediction, whole brain analysis, external validation, and transcription analysis within one study. The main hypothesis-driven findings include that (1) the DMN (including mPFC, precuneus, PCC, MTC, STC, and PHC) and insula identified from multimodal MRI was longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern that was related to cognition; (2) the associations between the altered pattern and cognition can be validated in independent elderly MDD, MCI, and AD datasets; and (3) the gene sets related to longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern were significantly enriched in biological processes related to DNA damage and repair, energy metabolism, and cellular activation. In addition, an exploratory analysis showed that the identified longitudinally altered pattern exhibited potential predictive value for 1-year follow-up cognition in ESRD, with GMV contributing the most. All of the above results suggest that the DMN and insula may be a core abnormal brain network causing cognitive impairment, depression, and dementia in ESRD patients receiving HD treatment.

The cognition associated DMN and insula identified from multimodal MRI were the major longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern for ESRD receiving HD. DMN is the most consistently reported dysfunctional network in previous studies of ESRD, which is associated with cognitive performance, especially language, attention, and executive functions.6,9,23,24,25 At the same time, other neuroimaging studies have found HD-induced fALFF, ReHo, and GMV reduction in DMN that are associated with dialysis duration.26,27,28 Our study further showed that the longitudinally altered DMN-insula brain pattern is associated with longitudinal cognition change, suggesting that the abnormal brain activity of DMN-insula may reflect the neural mediation of cognitive impairment in ESRD receiving HD. When comparing different imaging modalities, mPFC, MTC, and insula were consistently identified in both functional and structural MRI, demonstrating that mPFC, MTC, and insula are the covering longitudinally altered brain pattern related to cognition in ESRD. These results suggest that DMN and insula are the major brain areas affected by HD, which may underlie the neurobiological mechanisms of cognitive deficits in ESRD receiving HD. While our findings highlight the DMN-insula as a key longitudinally altered brain pattern associated with cognition in ESRD receiving HD, several alternative mechanisms may also contribute to longitudinal brain and cognitive changes. Dialysis-related metabolic stress, together with persistent systemic inflammation, constitutes a prominent pathological milieu in ESRD receiving HD. Recurrent fluctuations in electrolytes, osmotic pressure, and metabolic homeostasis during HD may impose sustained stress on the brain, while concomitant inflammatory activation and oxidative stress can further disrupt neural integrity and cerebral metabolism thereby contributing to cognitive impairment.29,30,31,32 Vascular burden, including microvascular disease and impaired cerebral perfusion, may compromise the structural and functional integrity of DMN-insula, which may in turn adversely affect cognitive functioning.29,33 Additionally, chronic exposure to uremic toxins may disrupt the blood-brain barrier and damage brain cells, with evidence suggesting accumulation in brain regions critical for cognition.34 Taken together, these factors may act as potential confounders or interacting mechanisms underlying the observed longitudinal DMN-insula alterations, and should be considered in future studies.

Timely detection of cognitive impairment, as well as the implementation of suitable interventions, can assist reduce complications and enhance the life quality of ESRD patients, especially in the absence of sufficient attention to cognitive burden in clinical. With respect to previously published cognitive ability prediction of ESRD, one study reported MAE = 0.88 for predicting MOCA scores based on functional networks of the brain.35 Another study used diffusion kurtosis imaging metrics and clinical risk factors that correlated (FDR correction) with MOCA as features to predict cognitive ability with MAE = 0.76.36 Notably, though seemingly better than us, the above results are cross-sectional or incorporate additional clinical information. Using only baseline brain features to longitudinally predict cognitive performance is much more difficult. Our results suggest that the identified longitudinally altered DMN-insula brain pattern in ESRD receiving HD shows potential predictive value for 1-year follow-up cognitive performance. This exploratory result may inform future studies aimed at devising early intervention strategies or personalized treatment plans for ESRD patients.

The longitudinally altered DMN-insula brain pattern was related with cognition in independent cohort of elderly MDD. Several studies reported DMN abnormalities of depressed ESRD patients, which may lead to cognitive impairment and increased risk of death.37,38 The somatic symptoms and cognition of MDD were associated with abnormal interoceptive representation within the insula.39,40 Our results further support the idea that depression-related insula is closely associated with cognitive impairment in ESRD receiving HD. Longitudinally altered associated mPFC, temporal cortex (including MTC and STC), and insula were related with cognition in independent MCI and AD cohorts. In typical MCI to AD, the progression of clinical symptoms follows a sequential order, namely episodic memory loss, semantic memory loss, and visuospatial symptoms, which correspond to the topographic progression of neuronal degeneration, including mPFC, temporal cortex, and insula.41 Altered mPFC, temporal cortex and insula have been widely reported to be associated with cognitive impairment in ESRD comorbid with MCI/AD.36,42,43 Our results suggested that the DMN-insula may underlie the neural mechanisms in cognitive deficits in HD-ESRD comorbid with depression or dementia.

The longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern related gene sets (including PLS+ and PLS–) were major involved in biological processes related to DNA damage and repair, energy metabolism, and cellular activation. Cellular DNA was easily damaged by free radicals generated during various metabolic processes and exposure to environmental toxins produced by renal failure.44 In addition, due to HD exerted mechanical stress which translates into oxidative stress, it may have greater destructive effects on genetic material, leading to neurodegenerative diseases.45 Higher levels of DNA damage and lower levels of DNA repair were found in HD patients compared to non-HD patients.46 The state of metabolic and nutritional derangements were the main risk factors affecting the outcome of ESRD, which were closely related to major adverse clinical outcomes and lead to increased hospitalization and mortality rates in these patients.47 Multiple nutritional and metabolic alterations arising from the accumulation of uremic solute may cause persistent depletion energy fuels in the body.48 Furthermore, additional nutrient loss during HD such as amino acids, vitamins, trace elements, and glucose may further increase the risk of disturbance of energy metabolism.49 HD may also contribute to cardiovascular complications and inflammation by promoting cellular activation.50 More importantly, these enriched biological processes provide plausible mechanistic links between molecular vulnerability and the observed longitudinal brain pattern alterations. Impairments in DNA and increased oxidative stress may compromise neuronal integrity and synaptic maintenance, thereby contributing to progressive structural atrophy and altered functional activity.51,52,53 Disruptions in energy metabolism can further impair neuronal firing efficiency and network-level communication, which is particularly relevant for metabolically demanding hub regions such as the DMN and insula.54 Moreover, sustained cellular activation and inflammation may exacerbate neurovascular dysfunction and glial reactivity, further influencing brain network integrity and cognitive function.55 Our results suggested that the biological processes related to DNA damage and repair, energy metabolism, and cellular activation can explain the longitudinally changes of brain function and structure in ESRD during HD treatment, which may help to elucidate their potential molecular mechanisms.

In summary, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to identify longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern of ESRD receiving HD and the biological functions of its associated genes, and further validated the associations between the identified altered pattern and cognition in independent cohorts of elderly MDD, MCI, and AD. Multimodal DMN-insula was longitudinally discriminating between baseline and 1-year follow-up ESRD, which was associated with cognition. The identified baseline brain features showed potential predictive value for follow-up cognition in ESRD. Moreover, the associations between longitudinally altered DMN-insula pattern and cognition were further validated in independent elderly MDD, MCI, and AD cohorts. Finally, the gene sets related to longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern were significantly enriched in the biological processes related to DNA damage and repair, energy metabolism, and cellular activation. Based on our results, we conclude that the identified longitudinally altered DMN-insula brain pattern may underlie the important neural mechanisms with respect to cognitive impairment, depression, and dementia in ESRD receiving HD, and warrants further investigation to evaluate its potential as a longitudinal cognitive assessment biomarker during HD. The biological processes related to DNA damage and repair, energy metabolism, and cellular activation may underlie the molecular substrates of longitudinal changes in brain function and structure in ESRD during HD, which can help us to better understand the longitudinal brain alteration during HD at the molecular level. From a clinical perspective, cognitive impairment, depression, and dementia are extremely common and lack attention in ESRD receiving HD. Understanding the brain pathophysiology underlying these complications may provide valuable insights for developing reasonable detection, prevention, and management strategies during HD, but further validation is required before clinical application. However, given the moderate sample size, limited generalizability, and the observational nature of the study, these conclusions should be considered in the context of these limitations. Future research should focus on validating the identified DMN-insula pattern in larger and independent ESRD cohorts, as well as examining its longitudinal dynamics and potential response to clinical or interventional strategies.

Limitations of the study

One possible limitation of this work is that the moderate sample size may affect the power of the statistical analysis. However, compared to existing brain imaging studies regarding ESRD (most with n < 35, and only including baseline ESRD),9 it is a relatively large dataset. Second, the study sample was derived from a single cohort of ESRD patients receiving HD, and the results may not be directly generalizable to ESRD populations with different demographic characteristics, treatment regimens, or clinical settings. Therefore, validation in more diverse and independent cohorts is warranted. Third, the longitudinally altered brain pattern was identified in ESRD without considering comorbidities in depression or dementia. Thus, these results may need further validation in individuals who have ESRD comorbid with depression or dementia. Fourth, this study did not scan the ESRD patients before undergoing HD, so the influence of ESRD or HD alone cannot be assessed in this study. Fifth, due to the observational design of the study, the observed associations should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Sixth, although age, gender, and motion were already controlled as confounding factors, dialysis duration, education, vascular risk factors, and medication were not statistically controlled due to the lack of detailed records at baseline and 1-year follow-up. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as these unmeasured factors may affect the results. Seventh, although the identified DMN-insula pattern showed potential predictive value for follow-up cognition in ESRD, the strength of these findings remains exploratory, as the analyses are based on the same cohort and do not include external or independent validation. Finally, only HD treatment was included in this study. Other treatment methods, such as peritoneal dialysis or kidney transplantation, can also be combined to provide a richer understanding of the longitudinally altered brain pattern in ESRD patients receiving treatment. Moreover, due to the moderate sample size, sex-stratified analyses were not performed, which may limit the ability to fully assess sex-specific effects.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study employed a multimodal neuroimaging analysis framework based on multiset canonical correlation analysis combined with joint independent component analysis (MCCA+jICA)56 to identify longitudinal multimodal brain patterns and examine their associations with cognitive measures. Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) was further used to predict longitudinal cognitive outcomes based on baseline imaging features.

Participants were allocated to experimental groups based on their clinical diagnoses. Patients with ESRD (estimated glomerular filtration rate, eGFR<15 mL/min/1.73 m2, n = 39, 22 male, 56.9 ± 8.4 years) on maintenance HD (three times per week) for at least 3 months were collected from Affiliated Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University. Patients with a history of drug or alcohol abuse, obvious brain lesions (e.g., stroke and tumor), a history of major neurologic disorders, and any contraindications to MRI were excluded. Age-gender matched HCs (n = 33, 16 male, 56.7 ± 7.1 years) without any kidney disease or history of neurological or psychiatric disorders were recruited from the local community. All subjects completed necessary laboratory examinations, neuropsychologic tests and MRI examinations at baseline and 1 year follow-up, participants with any missing data at either time point were excluded from the analyses. Data from individuals with late-life MDD (n = 60, 18 male, 65.5 ± 8.5 years) was collected from the University of New Mexico.57,58 The diagnosis of MDD were based on the Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV).59 Data from individuals with MCI (n = 291, 129 male, 74.2 ± 7.5 years) and AD (n = 77, 35 male, 74.6 ± 8.0 years) was obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)60 consortium. The diagnosis of MCI and AD was based on the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS/ADRDA) criteria.61

Cognition of ESRD was assessed by the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).62 Delis Kaplan Executive Function System (DKEFS),63 Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA)64 and Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised (HVLT-R)65 was used to evaluate the cognition of older MDD. Cognition of MCI and AD was evaluated using the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (CDR),66 Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale (ADAS)66 and the modified Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (mPACC).67 Both resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) and sMRI were collected for all the participants. The demographic and clinical information were summarized in Table S2 (ESRD) and Table S3 (elderly MDD, MCI and AD). This study protocol was reviewed and approved by independent ethics committee for Clinical Research of Zhongda hospital, affiliated to Southeast University, approval number 2020ZDSYLL294-P01. Written informed consents were obtained after providing a complete description of the research process to each participant. The authors assert that all procedures contributing to this work comply with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committees on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008.

Method details

Multimodal imaging parameters and preprocessing

The functional images of ESRD were acquired with TE = 35 ms, TR = 2s, flip angle = 90, matrix size = 64 × 62. The functional images of MDD were acquired with a gradient-echo EPI sequence with TE = 29 ms, TR = 2 s, flip angle = 75, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, slice gap = 1.05 mm, field of view 240 mm, matrix size = 64 × 64, voxel size = 3.75 × 3.75 × 4.55 mm. Scanner parameters (fMRI) across all sites for MCI and AD can be found at the link: https://adni.loni.usc.edu.

The structural images of ESRD were acquired with TE = 3.7 ms, TR = 9.6 ms, flip angle = 9, matrix size = 256 × 256. The structural images of MDD were acquired were acquired with a 5-echo MPRAGE sequence with TE = (1.64, 3.5, 5.36, 7.22, 9.08) ms, TR = 2.53 s, TI = 1.2 s, flip angle = 7, number of excitations = 1, slice thickness = 1 mm, field of view = 256 mm, resolution = 256 × 256. Scanner parameters (sMRI) across all sites for MCI and AD can be found at the link: https://adni.loni.usc.edu.

For fMRI, standard preprocessing based on statistical parametric mapping (SPM12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) under MATLAB 2019 environment included the following: (1) the first five dummy scans have been discarded to retain only scans when the scanner approach to the steady state (2) slice timing correction; (3) realignment; (4) normalization to the EPI template with 3 × 3 × 3 mm3 resolution. We removed outlier subjects who have framewise displacements (FD) exceeding 1.0 mm, as well as head motion exceeding 2.0 mm of maximal translation (in any direction of x, y or z) or 1.0° of maximal rotation throughout the course of scanning. We also despiked the fMRI data, and regressed out six head motion parameters, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid. Results indicate all FDs (mean framewise displacements, mean of root of mean square frame-to-frame head motions assuming 50 mm head radius68) for all subjects were <0.3 mm at every time point. Data were then spatially smoothed using a 6-mm full width half-maximum Gaussian kernel. After that, linear drift was removed through temporal bandpass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz). The filtered time series was transformed into the frequency domain with a fast Fourier transform, and the power spectrum was obtained. Amplitude of low frequency fluctuation (ALFF) was measured by obtaining the square root of the signal across 0.01–0.08 Hz for each voxel. For standardization purposes, the ALFF of each voxel was divided by the global mean of ALFF values for each subject. The fALFF was calculated as a ratio of the power of each frequency at the low-frequency range (0.01–0.08 Hz) to that of the entire frequency range (0–0.25 Hz).

For the sMRI data was normalized to MNI space using the unified segmentation method69 in SPM12, resliced to 3 × 3 × 3 mm, and segmented into GM, white matter (WM), and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) using modulated normalization algorithms, resulting outputs as GMV. Then the GMV were smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) = 6 mm.70 Subject outlier detection was further performed using a spatial Pearson correlation with the template image to ensure that all subjects were properly segmented.

Analysis pipeline

Based on the analytic plan noted in the introduction, we conducted the following procedures: (1) MCCA+jICA56 was used to fuse multimodal MRI (fractional amplitude of low frequency fluctuation, fALFF +GMV) to identify the longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern for ESRD receiving HD (Figures 1A and 1B); (2) two sample t test was performed on the identified brain pattern to calculate the group difference between ESRD and HC at baseline and 1 year follow-up (Figure 1C); (3) the correlation between the identified brain pattern and cognition was calculated within ESRD (Figure 1D); (4) prediction of cognitive ability at 1 year follow-up was performed based on XGBoost by feeding the baseline features only (Figure 1E); (5) the association between the identified brain pattern and cognition was validated in independent elderly MDD, MCI and AD datasets (Figure 1F); (6) combining longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern in ESRD receiving HD with gene expression postmortem data provided by the Allen Institute for Brain Science (AIBS) to interpret the biological function of genes relatively over or under expressed in association with this brain pattern (Figure 1G).

MCCA+jICA

Two representative measures of resting-state fMRI (fALFF) and sMRI (GMV) were extracted after preprocessing to reduce the redundancy of the high-dimensional MRI data. We controlled for age, gender, and motion as covariates using a deconfounding approach prior to the fusion analysis. Deconfounding refers to modeling the relationship between brain features (X) and covariates (Z) using a linear regression model, subsequently removing the part of the signal captured by Z from X to eliminate the influence of confounding factors on brain features, as shown in Equation 1:(Equation 1)Xclean=X−f(Z),

where X is the original brain features, f is a predictive model regressing X on Z such that f(Z)=E(X|Z) and Xclean is the regression residual, i.e., the portion of the feature variable whose variance is not explained by the covariates. These residuals are then used in lieu of the original features as the fusion input. This approach helps to minimize the potential influence of these confounding factors on the subsequent fusion analysis.

Moreover, we also longitudinally aligned the imaging data. First, we employed standardized registration procedures for both baseline and 1 year follow-up imaging data in the preprocessing pipelines of each modality. Specifically, for fMRI, preprocessing was conducted using automated analysis pipeline,71 where INRIalign was used to perform motion correction on the images to reduce the impact of subject head motion.72 Then, the motion-corrected fMRI was registered and normalized to the standard Echo Planar Imaging (EPI) template space, including reslicing to 3∗3∗3 mm voxels, to ensure consistent spatial dimensions across all subjects. For sMRI, images were registered and normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template space using a 12-parameter affine model and resliced to 3∗3∗3 mm voxels to ensure consistent spatial dimensions across all subjects.73 Second, fALFF(GMV) features from the baseline and follow-up sessions were concatenated to construct common brain masks. Voxels were retained only if they were consistently present in both time points and in at least 90% of subjects, ensuring that longitudinal analyses were performed on shared and reliable spatial supports and reducing the impact of residual misalignment. Finally, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the masked fALFF and GMV feature prior to the fusion analysis, which further reduced sensitivity to voxel-level spatial noise and improved the longitudinal consistency of imaging features across time points.

Then, MCCA+jICA56 was used to identify the longitudinally altered multimodal components. Specifically, two modalities’ features (fALFF and GMV) were reshaped into feature matrices Xk (subject-by- voxel, n×L), which were assumed to be a linear mixture of spatial maps Ck (component-by-voxel, M×L) and a corresponding mixing coefficient matrix Dk (n×M), where k represents the modality, as shown in Equation 2.(Equation 2)Xk=DkCk,k=1,2.

The minimum description length (MDL) criterion was used to estimate the number of independent components (ICs) M = 20 for each dataset.74 Next, MCCA was performed to obtain the canonical variants matrix Dk and the spatial maps Ck for each modality, as shown in Equation 3.(Equation 3)max∑k,j=1n‖corr(Dk,Dj)‖22.

Subsequently, the jICA was applied to the concatenated maps of [C1C2] to obtain the maximized joint ICs [S1S2] and the demixing matrix W shared by all modalities. The final mixing coefficient matrices Ak and the spatial maps Sk were obtained by Equations 4 and 5. Paired samples t test was performed on each column of the Ak to compare the intragroup differences for ESRD between baseline and 1 year follow-up. The components with significant longitudinal differences were called longitudinally altered multimodal brain pattern for ESRD receiving HD.(Equation 4)W[C1C2]=[S1S2],(Equation 5)Xk=DkCk=(DkW−1)Sk=AkSk,k=1,2.

Linear back reconstruction

To establish whether the longitudinally altered brain pattern is specific to ESRD, the back-reconstruction of longitudinally altered brain maps to the HC group was performed based on the linear projection model as in Equation 6.XESRD,k=AESRD,k×SESRD,k(Equation 6)AHC,k=XHC,k×(SESRD,k)−1.

Where SESRD,k and AESRD,k denote the brain components and the corresponding mixing matrix derived by MCCA+jICA for ESRD. XESRD,k and XHC,k represent the preprocessed imaging feature matrices. k represents the modality. Consequently, the spatial maps of ESRD (SESRD,k) group were used to estimate the mixing matrix of HC group (AHC,k) based on Equation 6. Finally, the paired samples t test was performed on the estimated AHC,k to compare the intragroup differences for HC at baseline and 1 year follow-up.

Post-hoc analysis

After identifying the longitudinally altered multimodal components, each component was divided into positive (Z >( 2) and negative (Z < −(2) brain regions via Z-scoring the spatial maps. Thus, we can obtain separate positive and negative brain masks for each of the 2 (fALFF+GMV) longitudinally altered brain pattern (2∗2 = 4 brain masks). Then, the mean of the voxels within the masked regions was calculated for each subject to generate the mean brain feature matrices. A two sample t test was performed on the mean brain features to calculate the group differences between ESRD and HC at baseline and 1 year follow-up. The correlations between the identified longitudinally altered brain features and cognition were also evaluated. Then, the brain features related to baseline Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) within the masked region were used to predict the cognitive ability for ESRD at 1 year follow-up based on XGBoost with leave-one-out cross validation. The hyperparameters of XGBoost were set up with a maximum depth of 4 and learning rate of 0.01. The optimal number of model training iterations is determined by assessing the prediction error for 2000 rounds and implementing early stopping [6] if the validation error did not improve in 20 rounds. Finally, the mean absolute error (MAE) and Pearson correlation coefficients between the model-predicted MMSE and true MMSE values were calculated to evaluate the prediction performance. Finally, 4 positive and negative brain masks were used as regions of interest (ROIs) to extract the mean fALFF/GMV features from independent cohorts of elderly MDD, MCI and AD to calculate the correlations between cognition.

Enrichment analyses

Brain gene expression profiles were first obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) database.75 The AHBA consists of microarray data for 3702 distinct tissue samples collected from the postmortem brains of six human donors (age: 42.50 ± 13.38 years; male/female: 5/1). The abagen (https://github.com/netneurolab/abagen) toolbox was used to pre-process the data with the following steps. First, microarray probes were reannotated to remove probes that cannot be reliably matched to genes. Following previously published guidelines for probe-to-gene mappings and intensity-based filtering,76 the reannotated probes were filtered based on their intensity relative to the background noise level; probes with intensity less than background in ≥50% of samples were discarded. Second, a single probe with the highest differential stability was selected to represent each gene. This procedure retained 15632 probes, each representing a unique gene. Third, tissue samples were assigned to brain regions using their corrected MNI coordinates (https://github.com/chrisfilo/alleninf) by finding the nearest region within a radius of 2 mm. Samples assigned to the same brain region were averaged separately for each donor. Finally, a scaled robust sigmoid (SRS) method was used to normalize expression values for each sample and donor across all genes, and for each gene and donor across all samples. As a result, a gene expression matrix (316 samples ×15632 genes) was used for subsequent analyses. Then, each longitudinally altered brain pattern was also mapped to the same atlas to generate baseline-follow-up fALFF/GMV difference Z-maps (response variables, 316 ROIs × 1), where each value represents the average Z-value within the region. Partial least squares (PLS) regression was used to identify the gene expression component related to these Z-maps. The statistical significance of the variance explained by each component was tested by permuting the response variables 1000 times. Within the significance components, for each PLS model, the first PLS component (PLS1) is the linear combination of the weighted gene expression scores that have a cortical expression map that is most strongly correlated with each Z-map of baseline-follow-up difference. The error in estimating each gene’s PLS1 weight was assessed by bootstrapping, and the ratio of the weight of each gene to its bootstrap standard error was used to calculate the Z scores and, hence, rank the genes according to their contribution to PSL1.77 We then selected all genes with positive and negative weights Z > 2.3 and Z < −2.3 (all pFDR <0.05, false discovery rate (FDR) corrected for the total number of genes tested) as PLS1+ gene set (Z > 2.(3) and PLS1- gene set (Z < −2.3), respectively. We used Metascape78 (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html) to identify Gene Ontology (GO) terms that were significantly enriched in these 4 genes sets (fALFF: 2 gene sets, GMV: 2 gene sets). Finally, the reduce and visualize gene ontology tool REViGO79 (http://revigo.irb.hr) was then used to summarize significant GO terms by removing redundant terms.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All analyses and statistical tests were performed in MATLAB 2021b. Unpaired comparisons were conducted using two-sample t-tests, and paired comparisons were conducted using paired-samples t-tests. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d, and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the associations between the identified brain pattern and cognitive performance, with correlation coefficients and their 95% CI reported. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. To control for multiple comparisons, we applied the false discovery rate (FDR) correction80 to both the gene enrichment and the brain-behavior correlation analyses. Specifically, the p-values (for each brain component in the brain-behavior correlation analysis, or for each gene in the gene enrichment analysis) were first sorted in ascending order:(Equation 7)p(1)≤p(2)≤…≤p(m),

where m is the total number of tests. Each p-value p(i) was assigned a rank i, and the FDR-adjusted p-value was then calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure:(Equation 8)q(i)=p(i)·mi,

with values capped at 1 when necessary. Results with q < 0.05 were considered to survive FDR correction. Statistical significance after FDR correction is denoted with an asterisk (∗).
