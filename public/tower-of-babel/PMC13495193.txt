Essential role of primary health care during COVID-19 pandemic: lessons learned from PUSPA (champion and integrated community health center) program in West Java, Indonesia
Authors: Muhammad Anugrah Saputra, Gita Kusnadi, Egi Abdul Wahid, Fitri Fauziah, Leli Rachmawati, Deni Frayoga, Reyhan Alemmario, Rina Chomawati, Yurdhina Meilissa, Diah Saminarsih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary health care plays a paramount role in responding to the pandemic. Aiming to strengthen the pandemic response in West Java, one of the hardest hit provinces by COVID-19 in Indonesia, the provincial government collaborating with a civil society organization (CISDI) initiated a primary health care strengthening program called PUSPA. This paper looks to assess the success and challenges faced throughout the PUSPA program.MethodsThis program was implemented in March to September 2021 by assigning a team of five interprofessional health care workers to assist 100 Community Health Center (Puskesmas) in West Java using The Whole-of-Society pandemic readiness approach. The implementation of the program included: strengthening of testing, tracing and treatment (3T), risk communication and community engagements (RCCE) as well as essential health care maintenance.ResultsThrough the program, PUSPA has successfully contributed to the increased capacity of the interventional Puskesmas in responding to the pandemic. Testing capacity was strengthened during the implementation period, reaching the highest level at 4.5 testing per 1000 population during the peak of the delta wave of the pandemic in Indonesia. The trend of confirmed case and positivity rate declined from 1,937 daily cases and 19.6% to 534 daily cases and 6.14% respectively. Tracing performance was improved from 2.03 to 4.91 for each confirmed case. The PUSPA program maintained the coverage of isolation and quarantine monitoring above 97.5%. The adoption of RCCE has led the PUSPA team to reach 1000 sentinel places and 769,425 persons in total. Lastly, an increase in the use of essential health care services was recorded during the implementation of the program.ConclusionNot only increasing pandemic response capacity, the PUSPA program also contributed to meaningful community empowerment. This program highlights the importance of close engagement between government and civil society in responding to health emergencies.

Background

Declared as a global pandemic for more than two years, the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) has catastrophically impacted countries globally, and Indonesia is no exception. By March 2021, Indonesia had reported 1,511,712 cumulative confirmed cases of COVID-19 and 40,858 deaths with the numbers rapidly rising throughout the year [1]. As the fourth most populous country in the world, suppressing COVID-19 transmission in the community is not an easy feat for Indonesia, especially during the beginning of the pandemic. Delayed response and denial of the case emergence within the country, lack of data transparency and use of scientific evidence in response to the outbreak, as well as the surge in healthcare facilities are among the key issues reported during the early stage of COVID-19 outbreak in Indonesia [2, 3].

One of the most fundamental aspects in response to the global pandemic is primary health care. As stated by the World Health Organization (WHO), primary health care plays a key role in preventing massive transmission of COVID-19 within the community, reducing the burden of hospital services, and maintaining essential health care service delivery [4]. Considering the pivotal roles of primary health care during COVID-19 pandemic, other countries globally have implemented primary health care reform as a strategic initiative since the early emergence of the pandemic in their areas, such as India, China and Thailand [5–7]. In India, for example, primary health care reform was conducted by delivering care packages for vulnerable populations in urban areas as well as team-based incentives for primary health care’s staff [5]. The government of China, meanwhile, established domestic medical supply chains and functional shifting of private clinics to specialized clinic COVID-19 treatment and rehabilitation at municipal level for the community [6]. Training for health care professionals and community health workers was initiated in Thailand to accelerate primary health care performance during COVID-19 pandemic [7].

Unlike those aforementioned countries, pandemic response in Indonesia still relies heavily on its conventional system where hospitals serve as the backbone of the healthcare system in the country. Indonesia was on the brink of health care facility (hospitals) collapse during the peak of COVID-19 cases in the country in January 2021 [8, 9]. It is a fact that Indonesia has 10,260 community health centers (Puskesmas) across the nation whose function could be optimized for pandemic response in the country, especially in preventing wider transmission within the community to reduce the burden of hospital services [10]. Moreover, with the existence of over 1 million community health workers (CHWs) in Indonesia [11], the roles of Puskesmas can be enhanced more by actively engaging the community for managing the pandemic response strategies.

Among other provinces, West Java was recorded as one of the hardest hit provinces by COVID-19 during the first year of the pandemic in Indonesia. In December 2020, West Java was reported as the province with the fourth highest number of COVID-19 cases in the country with 62,000 cumulative cases and testing rate of 3.1% [12]. During the same period, the bed occupancy rate in hospitals and emergency isolation space in West Java was reported to hit its high capacity at 92.5% and 53.9% respectively [12]. Even worse, the rate of bed occupancy in five districts was recorded to peak at 100% and 2525 healthcare workers were confirmed positively infected by COVID-19 [13]. Responding to this situation, the government of West Java made a bold move and collaborated with a civil society organization, Center for Indonesia’s Strategic Development Initiatives (CISDI) to initiate a primary health care strengthening program. This collaboration decision was made due to CISDI’s experiences on conducting an intervention program to strengthen primary health care services across the country (Pencerah Nusantara Program) [14], including one which was implemented during the first year COVID-19 pandemic in several districts in Jakarta and West Java [15]. Despite having the highest rates of affected people with COVID-19, the government of West Java showcased the best possible capacity, resource, and willingness to carry out the intervention.

PUSPA (Puskesmas Terpadu dan Juara; loosely translated as integrated and champion of primary health care center) is a six-month program implemented by assigning a team of five interprofessional health care workers to assist 100 Puskesmas in West Java. This partnership program between the government of West Java and CISDI was designed to strengthen the primary health care services by focusing on COVID-19 response as well as essential care maintenance programs. This paper highlights the results of this interventional program which was implemented in March to September 2021, aiming to provide lessons learned to better respond to the future outbreak and improve the similar primary health care strengthening program ahead. The paper also spotlights the effective collaboration between government and CSO to provide rapid and critical health interventions in response to pandemic situations.

Methods

Intervention model

The Whole-of-Society pandemic readiness approach developed by the WHO to combat influenza-like pandemics, serves as the framework for the PUSPA program [16]. For Indonesia, the PUSPA program expands the framework to further tailor the program for COVID-19 response through primary health care level (Puskesmas) (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1PUSPA Intervention Model

PUSPA Intervention Model

The PUSPA intervention model, as shown in Fig. 1, aims to support Puskesmas by supplying additional trained healthcare personnel to strengthen the COVID-19 response, including 3T (testing, tracing, treatment), as well as to maintain the existing essential healthcare services. Furthermore, the intervention model emphasizes how crucial cross-sectoral collaborations between the public, private, and community sectors are to the program’s sustainability.

Implementation

Location of intervention

PUSPA was implemented at 100 Puskesmas located in 12 districts in West Java (shown in Fig. 2) from March to September 2021, a total of 7 months. The number of confirmed cases, 3T capacity, and population were among the agreed-upon variables used to identify the locations. As well as the district and municipal government’s level of commitment towards the PUSPA program. Puskesmas that were selected as part of the PUSPA program were mandated by the district health office to join the program. All 100 Puskesmas were included in this analysis.

Fig. 2Interventional sites of the PUSPA program

Interventional sites of the PUSPA program

Personnel and recruitment

As part of the program, 200 healthcare workers were hired internally from the Puskesmas, while 300 young health professionals were recruited from outside and placed as healthcare workers within the 100 Puskesmas. The PUSPA program resulted in the hiring of 500 healthcare professionals (physicians, nurses, midwives, and public health specialists). Along with the healthcare workers, 3560 community healthcare workers (CHWs) were enlisted to assist with the implementation of the program, working closely with their respective Puskesmas. Before deployment, all healthcare workers and community health workers received mandatory training. The government, development partners for the PUSPA program, and participating CSOs supplied all capacity training. Twenty-four supervisors were assigned to each of the program’s twelve districts to closely monitor the implementation. Supervisors were selected based on their ties to their individual districts and their background in the health sector. Three Regional Technical Assistants (RTAs) assist the supervisors in coordinating on-site needs with the program implementers. The healthcare workers conducted training and adoption from the start of the program to the third week of May. The full organogram for the PUSPA project is shown in Fig. 3.

Organogram of the PUSPA program

Intervention programs (3T, RCCE, and maintenance of essential services)

Enhancing 3T (testing, tracing, treatment) for partnered Puskesmas is at the core of the intervention program as part of the COVID-19 response. In terms of testing, PUSPA sought to increase both quantitative and qualitative access to COVID-19 tests at each of the puskesmas. In support of further antigen testing, PUSPA collaborated closely with the District Health Office to provide the Puskesmas with more antigen test kits. Additionally, all PUSPA health workers received specialized swab training for COVID-19 testing in advance of the program. In addition to the testing program, CHWs’ active participation allowed for further contact tracing. To enable them to do efficient Community-Based Surveillance (CBS), CHWs received both online and offline training. During home visits, they learned how to identify the symptoms of influenza-like illness (ILI). Communities were explicitly empowered in contact tracing, ILI surveillance, isolation monitoring, health promotion initiatives, and mapping of vulnerable groups. CBS intervention was limited to a few Puskesmas before the PUSPA program. Patient isolation was the main emphasis of treatment plans for confirmed cases. PUSPA assisted in setting up isolation shelters for COVID-19 patients in specific locations in collaboration with the program partners. Additionally, CHWs were crucial to this strategy by assisting healthcare workers in monitoring and distributing medications for isolating patients.

PUSPA focused on providing public health initiatives through risk communication and community involvement supported by the Puskesmas, in addition to supporting the core 3T activities. This includes increasing health promotions during social and religious gatherings as well as interacting more with key community leaders such as village heads and other local champions. Additionally, the PUSPA program aimed to ensure the maintenance of essential services would be accessible throughout the pandemic by providing ongoing logistical and human resource assistance.

Outcomes measurement

A number of indicators depending on each interventional program were used to measure the PUSPA program’s outcomes (Fig. 4). The PUSPA team collected data for outcome measurements between the end of May 2021 until the second week of September 2021. Data from all 100 Puskesmas in West Java were combined and analyzed at the aggregate level. Indicators were aggregated and weighted by the catchment population of each Puskesmas, with COVID-19 testing rates standardized and reported per 1,000 population. Although data were collected on a daily basis, indicators were aggregated weekly for reporting purposes. Trends across the implementation period were assessed through time-series plots and presented as trend figures. Outcome measurements data from the PUSPA program was accessed in November 2023 to be analyzed and presented for this paper. Since no primary data was collected, all data utilized in this paper is aggregated, and no personal information was collected, neither consent nor ethical approval were obtained for this paper.

Risk communication and community engagement outreach

As a key facet of the PUSPA model, Risk Communication and Community Engagement (RCCE) is also assessed throughout the program. The first indicator of this is the population’s exposure to health promotion and risk communication. This pertains to the total number of individuals the CHWs reached through door-to-door visits or health education campaigns at social gatherings. These initiatives aim to boost public acceptability of contact tracing, testing, and quarantine and self-isolation compliance as well as improve the implementation of health protocol. The number of sentinel facilities that receive health promotion and monitoring supervision from the Puskesmas and the number of sentinel facilities that use standardized health protocol are the second and third indicators that track activity in sentinel locations. In order to avoid transmission, the campaign at sentinel locations focuses on promoting adherence to the implementation of health protocols. Traders or service providers are asked to wear masks when interacting with customers, and restrict visitors. The sub-district task force and law enforcement officials are involved in this ongoing activity.

Maintenance of essential health care services

The PUSPA program’s final major indicator examines the maintenance of essential health care services. The ability of the essential health care services to continue their program during the COVID-19 pandemic is crucial as part of the PUSPA model. The number of monthly visits for (1) Antenatal Care (2), Nutrition Monitoring (3), Non-Communicable Diseases Screening at each Puskesmas is used to measure this.

Monitoring and evaluation

There were two components to the program’s monitoring and evaluation. With the assistance of the RTAs, the supervisors assigned to each district carried out direct on the field supervision. Subsequently, they would work in collaboration with the program’s designated Monitoring, Evaluation, and Learning (MEL) Team. The MEL Team focuses on providing quality checks of the data collected for the data program and analyzing the data to provide insights and lessons learned to enhance the program further. The initial plan called for all data to be collected through Pikobar, West Java’s provincial COVID-19 digital application, with program indicators aligned with its official metrics. In practice, this proved difficult to sustain: many cities and districts had pre-existing data systems that were difficult to integrate with Pikobar, and not all required indicators could be derived from its raw data.

To address these gaps, the PUSPA Program introduced a complementary online spreadsheet mechanism. The MEL team developed a standardized daily template completed by PUSPA health workers at each Puskesmas, capturing aggregated figures from which all program indicators could be calculated. RTAs and Supervisors jointly conducted data quality assurance, flagging inconsistent entries for validation, and a built-in feature ensured percentage-based indicators did not exceed 100%. Validated data were consolidated by RTAs into a single master sheet serving as the program’s comprehensive data repository. To support monitoring and evaluation, an internal dashboard (shown in Fig. 5) was developed using Google Data Studio, displaying key outcomes in real time and enabling Puskesmas to track and benchmark their performance against other PUSPA intervention Puskesmas. The dashboard is publicly available at https://lookerstudio.google.com/s/k0UFzkmGgtk.

Results

The results of the seven months PUSPA program was measured by several indicators as follow:

Surveillance indicator

Test per 1000 population

As shown in Fig. 6, the trend of the average weekly testing was recorded relatively in line with the number of cases in the interventional sites, where more testing was conducted when the higher cases were confirmed. The average weekly testing was recorded reaching its highest level at 4.5 testing per 1000 population during the peak of the second wave (June week 4, 2021). This testing ratio has far exceeded the target that was previously defined in the initial commencement of the intervention program (1 weekly test per 1.000 population). While previously the diagnostic test relied only on qualitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) which required 5–11 testing to result days, the advocacy programs conducted by PUSPA health professionals and district supervisors has successfully shifted the domination of this diagnostic method into the standardized Rapid Diagnostic Testing Antigen (RDT-Ag) protocol which took shorter period to analyze the results. PUSPA also was able to supply additional testing kits through collaboration with the provincial government and Gerakan Puskesmas Bangkit – a private partnership forum initiated to encourage the strengthening of primary health services. Previous requests by the district health office often face bureaucratic roadblocks, as such the PUSPA team helped advocate for the additional logistical support.

Average weekly test per 1000 population and number of cases

Case confirmation and positivity rate

Figure 7 demonstrates the daily case confirmation and positive rate during the data collection period of outcomes’ measurement in 100 Puskesmas PUSPA. Overall, the trend of the COVID-19 cases confirmed in the interventional sites followed the trend of the national figure, where highest cases were documented during the peak of the second wave in Indonesia in June and July 2021 [17]. During the measurement of the result indicators, the daily new cases were seen decreasing from 1,937 cases in the last week of May to 534 cases at the end of the program. However, the results were higher than the targeted declination which was set in < 16.3 daily cases per capita. As for the positivity rate, similarly with the case confirmation figure, a declining trend was shown from 19.6% in the beginning of the data collection to 6,14% at the end of the interventional period.

Tracing indicator

The trend of tracing indicators during the outcome measurement period is depicted in Fig. 8. During the period, it can be seen that contact tracing performance increased from 2.03 for each confirmed case in the last week of May 2021 to 4.91 for each confirmed case in the second week of September 2021. This could be driven by the intensive ILI surveillance conducted by the community health workers. Moreover, the assigned health professional team in 100 Puskesmas PUSPA maintained the < 72 h tracing coverage among confirmed cases at above 80% during the data collection period. Use of student volunteers also supported contact tracing activities during the peak of the COVID-19 Delta strain (June-July 2021). Tracing was conducted online through phone calls to close contacts of confirmed cases.

Isolation and quarantine

Figure 9 illustrates the last two surveillance indicators of the PUSPA program, monitoring of isolation and quarantine. Despite the fluctuating trend of daily cases in the program sites, the assigned PUSPA teams in 100 Puskesmas kept the coverage of isolation and quarantine monitoring above 97.5% during the measurement period from May to September 2021. During the peak of the second wave, the PUSPA team delivered teleconsultation to monitor all suspects and patients who were in isolation or quarantine. Cross-sectoral collaboration with several private companies were also conducted to provide and distribute additional oxygen concentrators, health supplements, and PPEs for the Puskesmas.

RCCE outreach by district

The deployment of five collaborating health professionals and 30–50 Community health volunteers in each puskesmas within the 100 interventional Puskesmas during PUSPA program amplified the risk communication campaign at community levels by reaching 1000 sentinel places and 769,425 persons in total as shown in Table 2. The weekly monitoring program conducted in the 1000 sentinel places showed that 84.9% (849) of the facilities implemented COVID-19 prevention protocols (providing masks, disinfectant or washing facility for visitors, marking 2 m distance for visitors to maintain physical distance and conducting temperature check). As for the involvement of multi stakeholders at the community level, there was an increase in the capacity of village apparatus (83%) to intensify the risk communication campaign and prevention of high risk events.

Maintenance of essential services

Maintenance of essential services for the PUSPA program focuses on ensuring that essential services continue to run. Particular focus was on services for maternal (antenatal care), child health (nutrition monitoring), and elderly health services (NCDs Services). The PUSPA team helps support this by planning and taking part in, together with the Puskesmas, action plans for essential services that are tailored to each Puskesmas needs. This is monitored through the monthly visits by the target population for the respective essential services. Visits to essential health services increased across all spectrums. Coverage of complete antenatal care increased from 33,6% to 44,8%, non-communicable disease screening among the elderly from 26,9% to 40,6%, and nutrition monitoring for children under-5 increased from 46,4% to 66,1% (Fig. 10). Overall, there was an increase in use of essential services from the beginning till the end of the program, though there was a drop in visits between June-July 2021 due to the increase in COVID-19 cases from the Delta strain.

Discussion

The robustness of the primary health care system in a country is a bedrock during a health emergency [18]. The implementation of the PUSPA program in West Java emphasizes collaboration between CSOs and the government, the deployment of trained health professional workers, the provision of adequate PPE and test kits, and engagement with local CHWs, community leaders, and volunteers to support pandemic response efforts in West Java, in alignment with efforts to strengthen the primary health care system. During the six-month implementation, PUSPA program has contributed to the improvement of surveillance systems, community transmission prevention (including among vulnerable groups) and essential health service maintenance in the interventional sites. In addition to this, the PUSPA program has also played a role in initiating several supporting programs by catalyzing multi stakeholder engagements that ultimately brought more fruitful collaboration to tackle the pandemic in the coverage areas.

Lessons learned from the PUSPA program highlight the important role played by PHCs in responding to pandemics. Often the vanguard of the health system, without it, governments would find it difficult to provide a robust COVID-19 response at the community level. However, there remains a lack of support and political will in pushing for stronger PHCs in the country, as is the case with Indonesia. With limited budgets, health workforce and facilities, PHCs across Indonesia often struggle to provide basic services to their local community despite the important role they play in the health system. It is thus important to realize the role of civil society organizations (CSOs), to help push forward agendas that are key for the development of the healthcare system that may not be of focus of the government. This is reflected in the PUSPA program, where CISDI, as a local CSO, collaborated with the West Java Provincial government to support primary health care centers in responding to the COVID-19 pandemic. CSOs also tend to have the added advantage of grassroot experiences that local governments often do not have, as such they are able to reach out better and act responsively according to the community needs. CSOs also have the flexibility to contact and connect with various other stakeholders, such as the private sector, that may not be available for local governments due to the bureaucratic nature of the institutions. This highlights the way in which CSOs are able to move outside of the system to find solutions that are not available to the government.

Responding to the need of qualified health professionals in critical time of COVID-19 Pandemic, PUSPA helps fill the human resources gap by training and deploying a team-based health professionals and scaling-up capacity among the existing health workforce in the Puskesmas. The interprofessional team of PUSPA delivered not only testing, contact tracing and monitoring of COVID-19 patients, but also maintained the delivery of routine essential care and COVID-19 vaccination at primary health centers. The diverse nature of the PUSPA teams at each Puskesmas help develop a multi-dimensional response plan that takes into account their expertise in health policy, health communication, and pandemic preparedness.

In addition to increasing the number of health professionals, providing increased testing capacity and adequate provision of medical devices (PPE and surgical masks) are a key component of the COVID-19 response. During the early stages of the pandemic, Indonesia reported low testing numbers due to issues around the cost of testing and delays in results [19]. Furthermore, the mass buying of medical devices by the public raised the price of PPE and surgical masks across the country, pricing out the health facilities from the much needed medical devices [20]. PUSPA looks to solve the corresponding logistic issues through several means. Firstly, to solve issues surrounding testing, PUSPA was able to increase the testing capacity through: (1) increasing the portion of RDT-Ag examinations (2), training the health care workers (3), establishing more testing centers outside the puskesmas building, and (4) expanding the target criteria that need to be tested while ensuring RDT-Ag supplies are sufficient. Increasing RDT-Ag testing in particular has shown to be effective in limiting the spread of COVID-19 through rapid contact tracing [21]. Secondly, through the use of Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) collaborations with penta helix partners, PUSPA was able to provide an adequate number of essential medical devices, such as PPE and surgical masks, as well as other logistical needs such as vaccine freezer and oxygen tanks. CSR has shown to be an important means in providing logistical needs towards an effective COVID-19 response with long term implications towards reducing case fatalities [22].

PUSPA also promoted community participation at grassroots level by engaging various actors, including village officials, CHWs, Islamic boarding schools, factories or companies, faith-based organizations, and other community groups within the pandemic response. Community participation was adjusted to the needs of each municipality, focusing on community trust building, social and behavior change communication, risk communication, surveillance and tracing, quarantine/ isolation monitoring, reporting, logistics and administration. The importance of community participation on public policy was not recognized from the very beginning of strategic planning of crisis control and prevention resulting in a lack of understanding of society’s needs. Communities need to be counted as latent assets to strengthen the health system to face further homeworks on COVID-19 vaccination, pandemic recovery and acceleration of disrupted essential health services.

Another issue that PUSPA looked to challenge is that of data integration. Indonesia did not show evidence of conducting ongoing event-based surveillance and analysis for infectious disease nor did it collect ongoing or real-time laboratory data, limiting its ability to carry out contact tracing. In the case of COVID-19, they rely on manual reporting at the facility level, which then gets sent first to the district health office (DHO) as aggregate data and then to the MOH. The burdensome number of different reporting forms at the facility level, such as NAR (national testing and diagnostic data), Silacak (national contact tracing data), and COVID-19 reporting systems at the provincial and district levels for the COVID-19 response, also make reporting compliance an issue. Data entry on each platform cannot be carried out on time, so it is difficult to obtain granular real-time data by name and address. PUSPA mitigated this by digitalizing the previously manual and scattered data reporting system into one housing system that showcases results of testing, tracing, and treatment at each Puskesmas. This allows for a more advanced functionality as a surveillance system at the sub-district level and a fine-grained understanding of the real impact of tailored COVID-19 interventions at the household level. Nevertheless the tools used based on the WHO’s guidelines showcase how the indicators can be used appropriately in the Indonesian context.

Limitations

This interventional program, however, had some limitations. First, the implementation of the RCCE program was mainly focused on the quantity of the activity output such as the number of targeted populations who received health promotion and risk communication, and not the quality of the output. Additionally, there is no impact data that we could compare with from Puskesmas outside of the PUSPA intervention areas. Instead, the output would be better measured by the number of people who comply with the protocols that were promoted by the CHWs during the RCCE. Second, the timeframe of the intervention is only 7 months, as such it is difficult to ascertain the long term impacts of several outcome measurements. Several indicators also were not able to reach their targets due to the short time frame. An unexpected challenge also faced by the program is due to the rapid rise of COVID-19 cases during the peak of the Delta strain in June-July 2021. A large number of PUSPA health professionals contracted the COVID-19 virus during this period, hence, were unable to carry out their responsibilities in the Puskesmas. This left the PUSPA team under-staffed in several of the Puskesmas for a few weeks.

Conclusion

PUSPA is an intervention program initiated through a collaboration between the government of West Java and CISDI (a civil society organization) to strengthen the primary health care system for responding to the COVID-19 pandemic. Using the approach of Whole-of-Society pandemic readiness by the WHO, the implementation of PUSPA program has successfully contributed to increase the pandemic response capacity of the 100 interventional Puskesmas and mobilize meaningful community empowerment. Through the investment in addition to qualified human resources, adequate PPE and COVID-19 test kits, regular monitoring programs and meaningful community engagements, PUSPA has contributed in the improvement of surveillance systems, community transmission prevention (including among vulnerable groups) and essential health service maintenance in the interventional sites. Some challenges that were faced during the implementation of PUSPA program were mainly related to testing and contact tracing within the community. To address this, intersectoral collaboration has been key to overcome several of the program’s shortcomings. Key takeaways from the intervention program shows that the PUSPA model, following the WHO pandemic preparedness model, is an important tool to strengthen the PHCs response towards an ongoing pandemic. It is key that intersectoral collaboration and ultimately, civil society engagement, is essential for a whole-of-society pandemic response and that the community itself is the main actor of change in limiting the spread of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Policy recommendations

In terms of future pandemic preparedness, this paper can provide several recommendations for each key stakeholder. Primary health care plays imperative roles in pandemic prevention and response, therefore more investment to strengthen the system and facilities remains pivotal. For the provincial government, it is seen that coordination between relevant provincial offices have been, such as the health department, social welfare, village affairs, and tourism department just to name a few. Stronger leadership in coordinating the relevant provincial offices will provide a clearer and more streamlined response for future pandemics. This recommendation is the same for the district/municipal offices.

For the provincial and district health offices, it is also recommended to integrate all reporting platforms used at the puskesmas level. It has been a challenge for many health workers during the pandemic due to the various reporting applications that they need to use. This thus limits their time on the field as they are occupied by administrative tasks. In addition to platform integration, it is also recommended to digitize all reporting platforms to further ease the burden of the healthcare workers and provide clearer access to reporting data.

For the national government, it is clear that the role of CHWs are essential in supporting Indonesia’s healthcare system. However, despite their key role the CHWs have been undermined and underappreciated. It is thus recommended that the role of CHWs be further legitimized at the national level by installing new decrees outlining the role of CHWs and the minimum incentives that they can receive. This would help provide support for the CHWs and bring us a step closer to professionalizing the role of the CHWs. Overall it is also strongly recommended that CSOs continue to be involved in the development of the health sector in all levels of government as it pushes the government to focus on issues that are important to the community as well as provide added support at the grassroots level for the government.

For the international community, the success of the PUSPA program highlights the critical role that local and national CSOs play in achieving effective grassroots-level interventions. Accordingly, it is recommended that international organizations and donors collaborate closely with local CSOs in delivering key health interventions, to ensure that local contexts are accurately identified and thoroughly understood when implementing community-based health initiatives.
