ER stress selects for Candida auris survivors with acquired fluconazole resistance and caspofungin tolerance

Feng Yang, Liuliu Sun, Hao Li, Ting Fang, Qinli Guo, Nan Chen, Yuanying Jiang

Abstract

Exposure to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress can drive the emergence of antifungal resistance in pathogenic fungi, but the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we show that treatment of Candida auris B12037 (clade 3) with the ER stressor tunicamycin selects for survivors with acquired resistance to fluconazole and, in a subset, tolerance to caspofungin. Pharmacological inhibition revealed that caspofungin tolerance, but not fluconazole resistance, requires the Hsp90-calcineurin axis. RNA-seq analysis of the parental strain exposed to tunicamycin uncovered a coordinated stress response characterized by up-regulation of cell wall biosynthesis, transmembrane transport, and autophagy, alongside global repression of translation and energy metabolism. In the resistant survivors, fluconazole resistance correlated with up-regulation of the efflux pump MDR1, with isolate #10 additionally exhibiting ERG11 overexpression. Caspofungin tolerance in isolate #10 was associated with up-regulation of multiple chitin synthase genes (CHS1, CHS3, CHS5), suggesting chitin-mediated cell wall remodeling. Isolate #19 displayed a distinct pattern, indicating alternative mechanisms of caspofungin tolerance. These findings reveal that ER stress selects for C. auris survivors with divergent antifungal resistance profiles through distinct molecular pathways, highlighting the potential for stress-induced evolution of multidrug resistance in this critical pathogen.

Introduction

Since its first description in 2009, Candida auris has rapidly emerged as a formidable global health threat (). Unlike other Candida species, C. auris exhibits a unique combination of traits that complicate infection control and treatment. It colonizes human skin efficiently, persists on hospital surfaces for extended periods, and spreads readily between patients, leading to numerous healthcare-associated outbreaks across six continents (). Importantly, bloodstream infections caused by C. auris are associated with mortality rates exceeding 40%, often compounded by delayed identification and limited therapeutic options (). The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has classified C. auris as an “urgent antimicrobial resistance threat” (), and the World Health Organization (WHO) has designated it as a “critical-priority” human fungal pathogen (), underscoring the critical need to understand the mechanisms driving its resistance evolution.

Antifungal resistance in C. auris is multifaceted and often involves co-resistance to multiple drug classes. Resistance to azoles, the most frequently used antifungals, commonly arises from overexpression of efflux pumps, particularly MDR1 (encoding a major facilitator superfamily transporter) and CDR1 (encoding an ATP-binding cassette transporter), or from point mutations in the target gene ERG11, which encodes lanosterol 14α-demethylase (; ; ). For echinocandins, including caspofungin, resistance typically involves mutations in the FKS1 gene, which encodes the catalytic subunit of β-1,3-glucan synthase (; ). However, clinical isolates lacking FKS mutations can also exhibit reduced echinocandin susceptibility through compensatory cell wall remodeling, such as increased chitin synthesis (). The coexistence of these mechanisms within single isolates can lead to pan-resistant phenotypes, leaving few or no effective treatment options.

Amphotericin B, a polyene antifungal that targets ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane, remains an important alternative for C. auris infections—particularly for neonatal cases, echinocandin-resistant isolates, and patients failing echinocandin therapy (). However, amphotericin B resistance has been documented in C. auris, with prevalence varying by clade and geographic region. Mutations in sterol biosynthesis genes, including ERG2, ERG3, ERG6, and ERG11, have been implicated in acquired amphotericin B resistance (; ).

Globally, six distinct clades of C. auris have been identified with varying antifungal susceptibility patterns and geographical distributions (). Clades I (South Asia), III (Africa), and IV (South America) stand out due to their multidrug resistance, particularly to fluconazole and amphotericin B (). A comprehensive analysis of 1,031\u00B0C. auris strains revealed that clades I and III display the highest fluconazole resistance rates, with 94 and 96% of isolates resistant, respectively. In contrast, clades II, V, and VI have lower resistance rates, with clade VI being entirely susceptible to fluconazole (). Clade-associated point mutations in ERG11—including Y132F and K143R in clade I, and F126L in clade III—have been linked to azole resistance (). These clade-specific differences highlight the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity of this pathogen and underscore the need for clade-informed interpretation of susceptibility data.

Antifungal resistance and tolerance represent two distinct but clinically relevant phenotypes that can compromise the efficacy of antifungal therapy. Resistance, quantified by the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), manifests as the capacity for growth at drug levels that suppress susceptible isolates. In contrast, antifungal tolerance is defined as the ability of drug-susceptible strains—frequently a subpopulation of cells—to exhibit slow growth under inhibitory concentrations. Importantly, tolerance is distinct from resistance and has been associated with persistent candidemia, suggesting that it may help predict patient responses to antifungal therapy [reviewed in ]. In disk diffusion assays (DDAs), these phenotypes can be quantitatively distinguished using computational pipelines such as diskImageR, where the extent of resistance is indicated by the radius of inhibition (RAD), and tolerance by the fraction of growth (FoG) within the inhibition zone. Typically, a 20% drug inhibition threshold, corresponding to RAD20 and FoG20, is employed to evaluate resistance and tolerance, respectively (; ).

The evolution of azole tolerance has been extensively characterized in C. albicans. Exposure to high concentrations of fluconazole and posaconazole primarily drives the development of tolerance, while long-term exposure to subinhibitory fluconazole concentrations predominantly selects for resistance and, to a lesser extent, tolerance. In addition, tolerance to azoles can evolve independently of resistance and is frequently associated with aneuploidy (; ; ; ). Although azole tolerance has also been documented in wild-type C. auris isolates, the selective pressures and mechanisms underlying this phenotype remain poorly understood ().

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response, mediated by the unfolded protein response (UPR), has emerged as an important modulator of antifungal susceptibility in several pathogenic fungi. The link between ER stress and antifungal resistance has been documented across multiple fungal species. In C. albicans, exposure to TUN induces chromosome 2 trisomy, which not only confers tolerance to TUN itself but also cross-tolerance to caspofungin, a first-line echinocandin antifungal. This aneuploidy-driven tolerance involves overlapping stress response pathways, including protein kinase C (PKC) and calcineurin signaling (). In Cryptococcus neoformans, tunicamycin-induced ER stress—whether mild or severe—leads to the formation of aneuploid strains with diverse karyotypes, some of which confer resistance or cross-resistance to fluconazole and 5-flucytosine. Similarly, brefeldin A (BFA), another ER stress inducer, promotes aneuploidy formation in C. neoformans, primarily disomy of chromosomes 1, 3, and 7, with chromosome 1 disomy conferring cross-resistance to fluconazole and 5-flucytosine (; ). In C. glabrata, tunicamycin stress drives two distinct adaptive strategies: mitochondrial dysfunction (petite formation) and chromosome C aneuploidy, both of which confer cross-resistance to fluconazole (). Beyond the Candida and Cryptococcus genera, the UPR has also been shown to play a critical role in virulence and antifungal susceptibility in the filamentous fungal pathogen Aspergillus fumigatus, where disruption of the UPR regulator hacA increases susceptibility to membrane- and cell wall-targeting antifungals (). Collectively, these studies—spanning C. albicans, C. glabrata, C. neoformans, and A. fumigatus—suggest that ER stress adaptation can predispose diverse fungal pathogens to acquire resistance or tolerance to clinically important antifungals, often through aneuploidy-driven genomic plasticity or UPR-mediated stress adaptation.

Despite the well-documented roles of ER stress in modulating antifungal susceptibility in other fungal species (; ; ; ; ), its impact on the emergence of resistance in C. auris has not been investigated. Given the exceptional capacity of C. auris to develop multidrug resistance and the central role of the UPR in integrating stress signals with cell wall integrity and efflux pump regulation, we hypothesized that exposure to ER stress could select for survivors with altered susceptibility to fluconazole and caspofungin. In this study, we addressed this hypothesis by subjecting C. auris B12037 (clade 3) to TUN-induced ER stress and characterizing the resulting survivors. Using a combination of phenotypic susceptibility testing, pharmacological inhibition of stress signaling pathways, and comparative transcriptomic analysis, we identified survivors with divergent resistance profiles. Our results reveal that ER stress selects for C. auris survivors with acquired resistance to fluconazole and tolerance to caspofungin through distinct molecular pathways, highlighting a previously unrecognized mechanism by which this critical pathogen may adapt to antifungal pressure. These findings have important implications for understanding the evolution of multidrug resistance in C. auris and may inform strategies to prevent its emergence.

Materials and methods

Strains and grow conditions

The C. auris clade III isolate B12037 was used as the wild-type strain. Stock cultures were preserved in 25% glycerol and stored at −80 °C. For routine growth, cells were cultured in yeast extract–peptone–dextrose (YPD) medium (1% [w/v] yeast extract, 2% [w/v] peptone, 2% [w/v] D-glucose) at 30 °C with shaking at 150–200 rpm. Solid media were prepared by adding 2% (w/v) agar to YPD.

Spot assay

Cells were suspended in distilled water and adjusted to 1 × 107 cells/mL; 3 μL of 10-fold serial dilutions were spotted on YPD with or without TUN (control). Plates were incubated at 30 °C and photographed after 2 days.

Obtaining tunicamycin survivors

Cells were suspended in distilled water and adjusted to 1×107 cells/mL.100 μL of the cell suspensions were spread on YPD plates supplemented with TUN (0, 2, 4, 8 and 16 μg/mL). The plates were incubated at 30 °C for 5 days. Adaptors were randomly chosen, streaked to YPD plates and incubated at 30 °C for 36 h. For each adaptor, several colonies with similar sizes were selected and frozen at −80 °C.

Disk diffusion assay

The CLSI M44-A2 guidelines for antifungal disk diffusion susceptibility testing () were followed with slight modifications. Briefly, strains were streaked from glycerol stocks onto YPD-agar and incubated for 48 h at 30 °C. Colonies were suspended in distilled water and adjusted to 1×106 cells/ml. 100 μL of cell suspension were spread onto 15 mL SD-agar plates. An empty paper disk (6 mm diameter, and 0.7 mm thickness) supplemented with drug was placed in the center of each plate. Plates were then incubated at 30 °C and photographed at 48 h.

RNA-seq

Test strains were cultured in YPD broth until reaching the logarithmic phase (OD600 = 1.0). To assess the effect of TUN on gene expression, the logarithmic phase cultures were split into two groups. One group received 4 μg/mL TUN supplementation, while the other was supplemented with an equivalent amount of DMSO (vehicle). After a 3-h incubation period, the cells were harvested by centrifugation. To compare gene expression between parent and survivors, log phase cultures were harvested by centrifugation.

Total RNA extraction and purification, library construction, and sequencing were performed as described previously (). Briefly, total RNA was extracted using the YeaStarTM RNA Kit (Zymo Research). RNA concentration, integrity, and purity were assessed using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies), a Bioanalyzer 2,100 system (Agilent Technologies), and a NanoPhotometer (IMPLEN), respectively. Libraries were prepared with the VAHTS mRNA-seq v2 Library Prep Kit (Vazyme) and sequenced on an Illumina platform by BGI Genomics (Beijing, China). Raw reads underwent quality control with FastQC and were mapped to the C. auris clade III reference genome with the corresponding annotation file.

Three biological replicates were obtained for each strain. Differential gene expression profiling was carried out using DESeq2 () with standard parameters. Genes with FDR (False Discovery Rate)-adjusted p value <0.05 and expression fold changes of more than 2 or less than 0.5 were considered differentially expressed.

Rhodamine 6G efflux

Strains were revived from −80 °C glycerol stocks on YPD agar plates. Single colonies were inoculated into YPD broth and adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1, followed by incubation until the culture reached an OD600 of 1.0. Cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a final density of 1.0 × 108 cells/mL. To deplete endogenous glucose, the cell suspensions were incubated at 30 °C for 1 h with shaking. Rhodamine 6G (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, United States) was then added to a final concentration of 10 μM, and the cultures were incubated at 30 °C for 30 min with shaking. After three washes with ice-cold sterile PBS, cells were resuspended in PBS containing 20 μM glucose to initiate efflux. Aliquots were collected at 0, 20, 40, 60, and 80 min post-glucose addition, and the fluorescence of the supernatant was measured using a Thermo Scientific Varioskan Flash microplate reader with SkanIt software (excitation at 527 nm, emission at 555 nm). All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

Nile red efflux assay

Nile red efflux was assessed following the protocol described by . Briefly, cells were cultured overnight in 5 mL of YPD broth at 30 °C with agitation at 200 rpm. An inoculum from this culture was then diluted into 15 mL of fresh YPD broth to a starting OD600nm of 0.5 and grown for until the OD600nm reached about 1.0. Harvested cells were subjected to three washing steps, each using 10 mL of ice-cold PBS, with centrifugation at 3900 g for 5 min, after which they were kept on ice overnight. Prior to the assay, cells were allowed to equilibrate to room temperature over 30 min and then resuspended in 10 mL of 50 mM HEPES buffer adjusted to pH 7.0 using 0.1 M NaOH. To deplete cellular energy stores, cells were treated with 5 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DOG) for 30 min at 30 °C under gentle shaking (50 rpm). Subsequently, Nile red was added to a final concentration of 7.5 μM, and the cells were incubated for another 30 min at 30 °C. Following two washes with ice-cold HEPES buffer, the samples resuspended in 15 mL HEPES buffer, and were maintained on ice for 2 h. For the efflux assay, flat-bottom 96-well plates were first filled with 50 μL of HEPES buffer per well, to which 100 μL of the Nile-red-loaded cell suspension was added. To trigger efflux, 50 μL of 80 mM glucose was added, while control wells received an equal volume of HEPES buffer instead. Fluorescence readings were taken on a plate reader with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 528 nm, recorded every 1 min over a 10-min period. Final Nile red fluorescence values were normalized and expressed as a percentage of the initial reading at time zero (T0).

Measurement of cell wall chitin content

Chitin content was measured by quantifying acid-hydrolyzed glucosamine from purified cell walls following a previously described protocol (). Briefly, strains were grown in YPD broth from OD600 0.1 to 1.0, harvested, washed three times with sterile distilled water, and disrupted with 0.5-mm glass beads in a Mini-Beadbeater (BioSpec Products). Cell debris was washed five times with 1 M NaCl, and wall material was extracted by boiling in SDS-MeOH buffer (50 mM Tris, 2% SDS, 0.3 M β-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) at 100 °C for 10 min. Extracts were washed three times with sterile water and dried in a SpeedVac.

Approximately 4 mg of dried cell wall material was hydrolyzed in 1 mL of 6 M HCl at 100 °C for 17 h. After acid removal at 65 °C, the hydrolysate was reconstituted in 1 mL of sterile water. A 0.1-mL aliquot was mixed with 0.1 mL of 1.5 M Na2CO3 containing 4% acetyl acetone and incubated at 100 °C for 20 min, followed by addition of 0.7 mL of 96% ethanol and 0.1 mL of Ehrlich’s reagent (1.6 g p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde in 30 mL concentrated HCl and 30 mL ethanol). After 1 h at room temperature, absorbance at 520 nm was measured on a SpectraMax M5 plate reader (Molecular Devices). Glucosamine concentrations were derived from a standard curve (0–0.5 mg/mL), and chitin content was expressed as a percentage of cell wall dry weight.

Broth microdilution

The assay was performed according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines () with minor modifications. Briefly, yeast cells in logarithmic phase were harvested, washed twice with sterile distilled water, and resuspended in the same buffer. The cell density was adjusted to 2.5 × 103 cells/mL in YPD broth containing drugs at concentrations ranging from 0.125 to 128 μg/mL. Aliquots of 200 μL of the cell suspension were dispensed into each well of 96-well microtiter plates. Plates were incubated statically at 30 °C for 48 h. Following incubation, cell growth was quantified by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using a microplate reader. Each condition was tested in triplicate. Control wells contained YPD broth without drugs.

Whole genome sequencing

DNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing were performed as described previously (). Data were visualized using Ymap (). Raw fastq files were uploaded to Ymap (version 2.0). Read depth was plotted as a function of scaffold position using C. auris B11221 genome sequence (Assembly ASM31357562v2, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCA_031357565.2/).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, and data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. For comparisons between two groups (e.g., parental strain vs. adaptors), unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, United States). Exact p-values are reported in the Results section or figure legends, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Exposure to tunicamycin selects for resistant survivors

To determine whether exposure to TUN could select for resistant survivors, we first assessed the susceptibility of the parental strain C auris B12037 (clade 3) to TUN. Spot assay results showed that growth was visibly inhibited at 2 μg/mL TUN and completely suppressed at concentrations of 4 μg/mL and 8 μg/mL (). Based on these observations, approximately 1 × 106 cells of B12037 were plated onto YPD agar plates containing TUN at concentrations ranging from 2 to 16 μg/mL (). After 5 days of incubation, a limited number of colonies (hereafter referred to as “survivors”) appeared on plates supplemented with 4 μg/mL and 8 μg/mL TUN (). To evaluate whether these survivors had acquired resistance to TUN, we randomly selected 27 colonies from the 4 μg/mL plates and 9 colonies from the 8 μg/mL plates. Spot assays were performed to assess their resistance levels. Among the selected colonies, 10 from the 4 μg/mL selection and 6 from the 8 μg/mL selection exhibited resistance to TUN, as evidenced by their ability to grow at concentrations that fully inhibited the parental strain (). These results indicate that exposure to high doses of TUN selects for a subset of resistant survivors.

Similar TUN-induced selection of stress-adapted survivors has been documented in other fungal pathogens. In C. albicans, TUN exposure induces chromosome 2 trisomy, which confers cross-tolerance to caspofungin (), and in S. cerevisiae, TUN selection leads to aneuploidy of multiple chromosomes (). Our results extend these observations to C. auris, demonstrating that TUN can select for survivors with altered antifungal susceptibility in this pathogen.

Some survivors acquired resistance to fluconazole and/or tolerance to caspofungin

To investigate whether TUN-selected survivors exhibited altered susceptibility to clinically relevant antifungals, we assessed their responses to fluconazole and caspofungin using disk diffusion assays. This method allows discrimination between classical resistance, defined by a reduced or absent zone of inhibition, and tolerance, characterized by visible growth within the inhibition zone despite a clear zone edge.

Among the 16 survivors that displayed resistance to TUN, three isolates (designated #10, #16, and #19) showed markedly altered susceptibility to fluconazole. Specifically, these isolates completely lacked a zone of inhibition around the fluconazole disk, indicating acquired resistance. When tested against caspofungin, two of these three isolates (#10 and #19) distinct colony growth within the zone, indicative of tolerance to caspofungin (,). In contrast, isolate #16 showed no signs of caspofungin tolerance, displaying a clear zone edge without internal growth.

To quantitatively validate the antifungal susceptibility profiles observed in disk diffusion assays, we determined the MICs of fluconazole and caspofungin for the parental strain and the three selected surivvors using the broth microdilution method following CLSI guidelines. As shown in , the parental strain B12037 exhibited a fluconazole MIC of 32 μg/mL, whereas all three survivors (#10, #16, and #19) showed MICs >128 μg/mL, confirming their acquired fluconazole resistance. For caspofungin, the parental strain displayed an MIC of 0.5 μg/mL, and isolate #16 exhibited a comparable MIC of 0.25 μg/mL, consistent with its susceptibility to caspofungin as observed in disk diffusion assays. Notably, although isolates #10 and #19 showed no substantial increase in caspofungin MICs relative to the parental strain, both survivors exhibited visible growth in wells containing drug concentrations above the MIC (≥0.5 μg/mL), indicative of caspofungin tolerance. This “trailing growth” phenotype—characterized by reduced but persistent growth at inhibitory concentrations—is a hallmark of antifungal tolerance and is distinct from classical resistance, which would be defined by an elevated MIC.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that exposure to TUN can occasionally select for survivors with enhanced resistance to fluconazole, and in some cases, concomitant tolerance to caspofungin. The distinct susceptibility profiles among the three isolates, together with their divergent transcriptional, functional, and genomic features, indicate that these survivors employ distinct adaptive mechanisms.

The association between caspofungin tolerance and chitin remodeling is consistent with prior reports in C. auris. showed that clinical isolates lacking FKS mutations can exhibit reduced echinocandin susceptibility through increased chitin synthesis, and recent work has demonstrated that stress-induced chitin remodeling contributes to echinocandin survival in this species. Our findings support this model and extend it to TUN-selected survivors.

Acquired caspofungin tolerance is dependent on Hsp90 and calcineurin

The molecular chaperone Hsp90 and the calcium-activated phosphatase calcineurin are key regulators of fungal stress responses and antifungal tolerance. In C. albicans, both Hsp90 and calcineurin have been shown to be required for the acquisition of fluconazole tolerance, and their inhibition can abrogate tolerance without affecting baseline susceptibility in susceptible strains (; ; ). Given their established roles in mediating stress adaptation, we investigated whether these pathways contribute to the caspofungin tolerance observed in the TUN-selected isolates.

To test this, we treated the tolerant isolates #10 and #19 with the Hsp90 inhibitor NVP-HSP990 or the calcineurin inhibitor cyclosporin A (CsA) in combination with caspofungin using disk diffusion assays. In the absence of inhibitors, both isolates exhibited the characteristic “foggy” growth inside the caspofungin inhibition zone, indicative of tolerance. However, upon addition of either NVP-HSP990 or CsA, the growth inside the inhibition zone was abolished, resulting in a clear zone edge similar to that of the susceptible control (). This indicates that both Hsp90 and calcineurin are required for the expression of caspofungin tolerance in these isolates.

In contrast, when the fluconazole-resistant but caspofungin-susceptible isolate #16 was tested under the same conditions, neither inhibitor restored fluconazole susceptibility; the zone of inhibition remained absent, consistent with a resistance mechanism that is independent of Hsp90 and calcineurin ().

Taken together, these results demonstrate that acquired tolerance to caspofungin in the TUN-selected isolates is dependent on Hsp90 and calcineurin signaling, whereas the fluconazole resistance phenotype in isolate #16 operates through a distinct, Hsp90/calcineurin-independent mechanism. These findings align with the established role of the Hsp90-calcineurin axis in fungal tolerance and highlight the divergent pathways that can emerge under antifungal selection pressure.

RNA-seq analysis of the transcriptional response to tunicamycin treatment

Having identified that TUN exposure selects for survivors with acquired resistance to fluconazole and tolerance to caspofungin, we next sought to characterize the transcriptional landscape underlying the cellular response to TUN stress. To this end, strain B12037 was treated with 4 μg/mL TUN for 3 h, and the transcriptome was compared with that of an untreated control. Differentially expressed genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis to identify biological pathways modulated during TUN exposure.

Up-regulated genes were significantly enriched for terms associated with cell wall organization and biogenesis, particularly β-glucan biosynthetic process (odds ratio [OR] = 19.91, p = 1.15 × 10−4), (1 → 6)-β-D-glucan metabolic process (OR = 33.14, p = 2.12 × 10−4), and cell wall organization (OR = 11.04, p = 1.57 × 10−3). These changes indicate reinforcement of the fungal cell wall, which may directly counteract the inhibitory effect of caspofungin, an echinocandin that targets β-glucan synthase. Additionally, transmembrane transport was the most significantly enriched category (OR = 2.2, p = 1.02 × 10−7), consistent with up-regulation of drug efflux pumps that likely contribute to fluconazole resistance. Other enriched processes included autophagy (OR = 5.81, p = 1.67 × 10−3) and calcium-mediated signaling (OR = 19.81, p = 8.27 × 10−3), suggesting activation of stress response and survival pathways under ER stress induced by TUN (, left).

Concurrently, genes involved in ATP metabolic process and oxidative phosphorylation were down-regulated, indicating a shift away from mitochondrial respiration. Notably, sterol biosynthetic process was also suppressed, which may alter membrane composition and further affect azole susceptibility (, right). Genes involved in translation and peptide biosynthesis were also significantly down-regulated—a transcriptional signature consistent with the global repression of protein synthesis that is a well-established hallmark of the unfolded protein response (UPR) and functions to alleviate ER burden (; ; ).

RNA-seq analysis of resistance-associated genes in survivors

To further characterize the molecular basis of the distinct resistance phenotypes, we performed RNA-seq analysis on the three survivors (#10, #16, and #19) using the wild-type strain as a reference. We focused on genes with established roles in drug efflux, target alterations, and cell wall remodeling ().

In all three survivors, the efflux pump gene MDR1 was up-regulated, with the highest expression observed in #10 (log2FC = 6.46) and #19 (log2FC = 7.65), followed by #16 (log2FC = 1.17). The transcription factor MRR1, a known regulator of MDR1, showed concordant up-regulation in #19 (log2FC = 1.37) but was down-regulated in #16 (log2FC = −0.54) and unchanged in #10 (log2FC = 0.63). By contrast, CDR1 expression remained largely unaltered across all strains. The azole target gene ERG11 was notably up-regulated in #10 (log2FC = 1.86), moderately increased in #16 (log2FC = 0.65), and unchanged in #19 (log2FC = 0.12). These expression patterns are consistent with the observed fluconazole resistance in all three survivors, with #10 displaying a combined mechanism of enhanced efflux and target overexpression.

Expression of the β-glucan synthase catalytic subunit genes FKS1 and FKS2 did not show consistent up-regulation in any of the survivors. However, the caspofungin-tolerant survivors #10 and #19 exhibited distinct cell wall remodeling profiles. In #10, several chitin synthase genes were up-regulated, including CHS1 (log2FC = 0.64), CHS3 (log2FC = 0.52), and CHS5 (log2FC = 0.60), suggesting chitin-mediated compensation. In contrast, #16, which remained susceptible to caspofungin, lacked up-regulation of these chitin synthase genes and instead showed up-regulation of the chitinase gene CHT1 (log2FC = 1.23). #19 displayed only mild up-regulation of CHS3 and CHS5 and down-regulation of CHT1 (log2FC = −0.43), indicating that its caspofungin resistance may involve mechanisms beyond chitin remodeling.

The gene ALG7, encoding the TUN-sensitive enzyme that catalyzes the first step of N-glycosylation, was up-regulated in all three survivors, with the most pronounced increase observed in #19 (log2FC = 2.05) and #10 (log2FC = 1.81), followed by #16 (log2FC = 0.52). This up-regulation likely reflects a compensatory response to ER stress. Other cell wall integrity-related genes, including several CHS family members and CHT1, showed strain-specific expression patterns consistent with their respective caspofungin susceptibility profiles ().

In summary, the transcriptomic profiles reveal that fluconazole resistance in all three strains is primarily driven by MDR1 up-regulation, with additional ERG11 overexpression contributing in #10. Caspofungin resistance in #10 correlates with chitin synthase up-regulation, while #16 lacks this compensatory response and remains susceptible. The mechanism underlying caspofungin resistance in #19 remains unclear from transcriptional data alone and may involve post-transcriptional or mutational events, possibly reflecting the distinct Hsp90/calcineurin-dependent tolerance pathway we observed.

Functional validation of efflux activity and chitin content in tunicamycin-selected survivors

To directly corroborate the transcriptomic findings, we performed functional assays to assess efflux pump activity and cell wall chitin content. The efflux of rhodamine 6G (R6G), a fluorescent substrate for multidrug efflux pumps, was measured over time. As shown in , all three fluconazole-resistant survivors (#10, #16, and #19) exhibited significantly higher extracellular R6G accumulation compared to the parental strain at 40, 60, and 80 min (p < 0.05, student’s t-test), indicating enhanced efflux capacity. This provides direct functional evidence that increased drug efflux, likely mediated by MDR1 up-regulation, underlies fluconazole resistance.

Of note, R6G is predominantly transported by ABC-type efflux pumps such as Cdr1 rather than by Mdr1 (; ; ), so R6G efflux reflects general efflux capacity rather than Mdr1 activity per se. To probe Mdr1-associated efflux more directly, we performed a Nile red efflux assay in parallel, as Nile red has been validated as a substrate of both C. auris Mdr1 and Cdr1 in a recombinant S. cerevisiae expression system (). Nile red efflux was monitored over 10 min in the parental strain and the three survivors, with fluorescence at each time point expressed as a percentage of the initial value at time zero. As shown in , the parental strain retained Nile red throughout the assay, with relative fluorescence intensity remaining close to 100% (96.9 ± 1.4% at 10 min). In contrast, all three survivors showed a progressive and pronounced decline, reaching 63.7 ± 3.5%, 63.5 ± 2.1%, and 66.4 ± 1.6% for #10, #16, and #19, respectively (mean ± SD; p < 0.05 versus parental strain, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test). Thus, Nile red efflux was substantially enhanced in all three survivors, consistent with the up-regulation of MDR1 detected in these isolates.

In parallel, we quantified cell wall chitin content as a percentage of dry weight. Consistent with the up-regulation of chitin synthase genes in the transcriptomic data, the caspofungin-tolerant isolates #10 and #19 showed significantly elevated chitin levels (8.35 ± 0.05% and 8.08 ± 0.04%, respectively) compared to the parental strain (6.54 ± 0.06%) (). In contrast, isolate #16, which is susceptible to caspofungin and lacks chitin synthase up-regulation, showed no significant difference in chitin content (6.42 ± 0.40%). These results confirm that the acquired tolerance to caspofungin is associated with chitin-mediated cell wall remodeling.

Whole-genome sequencing reveals segmental aneuploidies in caspofungin-tolerant survivors

To investigate whether chromosomal alterations contribute to the acquired resistance phenotypes, we performed whole-genome sequencing on the parental strain and the three survivors. Ploidy analysis revealed that isolate #16 remained euploid (), indicating that its fluconazole resistance is driven primarily by transcriptional changes rather than large-scale genomic rearrangements. In striking contrast, both caspofungin-tolerant isolates #10 and #19 exhibited segmental aneuploidies (). Specifically, both isolates carried a segmental disomy of scaffold 5 and 2 to 3 copies of a segmental region on scaffold 6. These karyotypic changes were uniformly present in the sequenced populations, suggesting they were positively selected during TUN exposure.

We further examined the sequencing reads using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) to screen for point mutations or small indels in canonical antifungal resistance genes, including TAC1b, CDR1, MDR1, ERG11, UPC2, FKS1, FKS2, and CHT1. No mutations were detected in any of these genes across the three survivors, indicating that the acquired phenotypes are not attributable to sequence alterations in these well-established drug targets or efflux regulators.

Notably, we identified that TAC1b—a known positive transcriptional regulator of MDR1—is located within the amplified region on scaffold 5, providing a direct genomic link to the robust MDR1 up-regulation observed in isolates #10 and #19. Additionally, ALG7, which encodes the TUN target enzyme (UDP-N-acetylglucosamine—dolichyl-phosphate N-acetylglucosaminephosphotransferase), resides within the amplified region on scaffold 6. The increased copy number of ALG7 likely represents a direct adaptive response to TUN-induced ER stress by compensating for the drug’s inhibitory effect.

Collectively, these genomic and functional data reveal that ER stress drives the emergence of resistant survivors through two distinct but complementary mechanisms: (i) transcriptional reprogramming in a euploid background (isolate #16), and (ii) segmental aneuploidies that amplify key resistance-associated genes, including TAC1b and ALG7, in isolates that have acquired both fluconazole resistance and caspofungin tolerance (#10 and #19).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the adaptive responses of C. auris (clade 3, strain B12037) to TUN, an inducer of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and characterized the resulting survivors with altered susceptibility to clinically relevant antifungals. Our findings reveal that exposure to TUN selects for survivors that acquire resistance to fluconazole and, in some cases, tolerance to caspofungin. Through transcriptomic and pharmacological analyses, we uncovered distinct molecular mechanisms underlying these phenotypes, highlighting the complex interplay between ER stress adaptation and antifungal drug resistance.

We first demonstrated that TUN treatment, at concentrations that fully inhibit the parental strain, yields a subset of survivors that exhibit resistance to TUN itself. Among these, three survivors (#10, #16, and #19) displayed resistance to fluconazole, while two (#10 and #19) additionally showed tolerance to caspofungin, characterized by growth within the inhibition zone. This phenotypic divergence suggests that the selective pressure imposed by TUN can drive distinct adaptive trajectories. Notably, the observation that not all survivors acquired antifungal resistance underscores that resistance is not an inevitable consequence of TUN exposure but rather arises through specific genetic or epigenetic alterations. The separation of fluconazole resistance and caspofungin tolerance among the isolates further indicates that these phenotypes are governed by independent mechanisms.

Using chemical inhibitors, we found that the Hsp90 inhibitor NVP-HSP990 and the calcineurin inhibitor CsA abrogated caspofungin tolerance in isolates #10 and #19, rendering the inhibition zones clear. In contrast, neither inhibitor affected the fluconazole resistance of isolate #16. These results align with the established role of the Hsp90-calcineurin axis in fungal stress responses, particularly in mediating tolerance to cell wall-active agents such as caspofungin. In C. albicans and Aspergillus fumigatus, Hsp90 and calcineurin regulate cell wall integrity signaling and are essential for tolerance to echinocandins (; ). Our data extend this paradigm to C. auris, demonstrating that acquired caspofungin tolerance in TUN-selected survivors similarly depends on this conserved stress signaling pathway. The lack of inhibitor effect on fluconazole resistance in #16 suggests that this phenotype operates independently of Hsp90/calcineurin, consistent with the predominant role of efflux pump up-regulation in azole resistance.

RNA-seq analysis of the parental strain exposed to TUN revealed a global transcriptional reprogramming characterized by the up-regulation of cell wall organization and biogenesis, transmembrane transport, and autophagy, alongside down-regulation of translation, oxidative phosphorylation, and sterol biosynthesis. The enrichment of β-glucan biosynthetic processes indicates that cells actively reinforce the cell wall to counteract ER stress, a response that may pre-adapt them to caspofungin challenge. Similarly, the strong enrichment of transmembrane transport genes, including potential drug efflux pumps, likely contributes to the observed fluconazole resistance. The concomitant activation of autophagy and calcium-mediated signaling reflects a broader survival strategy to manage ER stress. The global repression of translation and energy metabolism is a hallmark of the unfolded protein response, which alleviates ER burden by reducing protein synthesis (; ) and reallocating resources toward stress-adaptive pathways (; ). The suppression of sterol biosynthesis may alter membrane composition, potentially affecting azole susceptibility (; ). Collectively, these data paint a picture of a finely tuned cellular response that prioritizes stress survival over growth, creating a permissive state for the emergence of antifungal resistance.

Analysis of the three survivors revealed distinct transcriptional signatures that correlate with their resistance phenotypes. In all survivors, MDR1 was up-regulated, supporting its central role in fluconazole resistance. Notably, isolate #10 also exhibited up-regulation of ERG11, the azole target, suggesting a dual mechanism of resistance combining enhanced efflux with target overexpression. Such combinatorial strategies have been described in clinical isolates of C. auris and are associated with higher-level resistance (; ). The lack of CDR1 up-regulation further underscores the predominant contribution of the MDR1 efflux system in this strain background.

Regarding caspofungin tolerance, isolate #10 showed up-regulation of multiple chitin synthase genes (CHS1, CHS3, CHS5), consistent with a compensatory cell wall remodeling mechanism frequently observed in echinocandin-tolerant or -resistant isolates. In contrast, isolate #16, which remains caspofungin susceptible, lacked chitin synthase up-regulation but exhibited increased expression of the chitinase gene CHT1, which may attenuate cell wall chitin content and thereby maintain sensitivity. Isolate #19 displayed a more subtle pattern, with only modest changes in chitin synthase expression and down-regulation of CHT1, suggesting that its caspofungin tolerance may involve alternative mechanisms, possibly including mutations in FKS genes or post-transcriptional regulation. The universal up-regulation of ALG7, the target of TUN (), confirms that all survivors retain a compensatory response to ER stress, even after selection.

The functional validation of efflux activity and chitin content further strengthens the mechanistic conclusions drawn from the transcriptomic data. The Nile red efflux data provide direct functional evidence for Mdr1-associated efflux in the TUN-selected survivors. Unlike R6G, which is primarily an ABC-transporter substrate, Nile red has been validated as a substrate of C. auris Mdr1 and Cdr1 (). The concordance between MDR1 up-regulation and enhanced Nile red efflux across all three survivors supports a role for Mdr1 in the fluconazole resistance phenotype, and resolves the apparent inconsistency between the R6G results and the absence of CDR1 up-regulation.

Meanwhile, the elevated chitin content specifically in the caspofungin-tolerant isolates provides biochemical evidence for the chitin-mediated cell wall reinforcement pathway. These functional assays not only validate our RNA-seq findings but also underscore the distinct adaptive strategies—efflux pump activation versus cell wall remodeling—that C. auris employs to survive ER stress and acquire antifungal resistance.

The WGS data revealed a striking dichotomy in the adaptive strategies employed by the TUN-selected survivorss. Isolate #16, which acquired fluconazole resistance but remained caspofungin-susceptible, maintained a strictly euploid genome, indicating that fluconazole resistance can be achieved through transcriptional reprogramming alone (MDR1 up-regulation) without large-scale genomic rearrangements. In contrast, both caspofungin-tolerant isolates (#10 and #19) harbored segmental aneuploidies—specifically, segmental disomy of scaffold 5 and 2–3 copies of a segmental region on scaffold 6. Unlike whole-chromosome aneuploidy documented in ER stress-induced resistance in other fungi (; ,; ), the segmental nature of these alterations suggests a more refined adaptive strategy that may minimize fitness costs while providing gene dosage benefits.

Segmental aneuploidies in our C. auris survivors are consistent with the growing recognition that stress-induced genomic plasticity contributes to antifungal resistance across pathogenic fungi. In C. albicans, TUN exposure selects chromosome 2 trisomy that confers caspofungin cross-tolerance (,), and in C. neoformans, brefeldin A induces aneuploidy conferring fluconazole resistance (). In C. glabrata, TUN drives both petite formation and chromosome C aneuploidy (). Our finding of segmental, rather than whole-chromosome, aneuploidies in C. auris suggests a more refined adaptive strategy that may minimize fitness costs while providing gene dosage benefits.

Importantly, IGV analysis confirmed the absence of point mutations in all canonical resistance genes (TAC1b, CDR1, MDR1, ERG11, UPC2, FKS1, CHT1), indicating that the acquired phenotypes are driven by transcriptional changes rather than classical target-site alterations.

The contribution of TAC1b to antifungal resistance in C. auris has been predominantly attributed to gain-of-function (GOF) mutations that drive CDR1 overexpression and, to a lesser extent, MDR1 up-regulation (; ). In our study, no TAC1b GOF mutations were detected in the three TUN-selected survivors. Moreover, TAC1b (CJI97_004632; 0.189540–0.192140 Mb) is not located within the amplified regions on scaffold 5 in isolates #10 and #19. The amplification in #10 spans the left telomere to 0.172 Mb and 0.215–0.240 Mb, while in #19 it extends from the left telomere to 0.172 Mb; neither region encompasses TAC1b. These observations distinguish our survivors from the canonical TAC1b-mediated resistance paradigm. In contrast to TAC1b GOF mutants, which typically exhibit CDR1 up-regulation (), our survivors showed no CDR1 induction despite robust MDR1 up-regulation. TAC1b-independent routes to azole resistance have been reported: showed that Tac1b contributes to azole resistance through a CDR1-independent mechanism, and reported that chromosome 5 duplication increased ERG11, TAC1b, and CDR2 expression but not CDR1. In addition, the Mrr1–Mdr1 axis can mediate azole resistance independently of CDR1 (). Our findings are consistent with these reports and suggest that fluconazole resistance in these survivors operates through a TAC1b-independent pathway, with Mdr1-mediated efflux as the primary driver.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that C. auris can employ two fundamentally distinct adaptive strategies in response to ER stress: purely transcriptional adaptation in a euploid background (isolate #16) versus combined transcriptional and genomic rearrangements via segmental aneuploidy (isolates #10 and #19). The association of segmental aneuploidy with the more complex multidrug-resistant phenotype raises the possibility that such genomic alterations may facilitate the evolution of pan-resistance in this highly plastic pathogen. Future studies employing long-read sequencing and functional genetic validation will be required to definitively establish the causal role of these segmental aneuploidies.

Our findings are consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating that ER stress can drive the emergence of antifungal resistance and tolerance through both transcriptional and genomic mechanisms. In Cryptococcus neoformans, TUN induced ER stress has been shown to promote aneuploidy formation, with some aneuploid strains conferring resistance or cross-resistance to fluconazole and 5-flucytosine (). Similarly, in C. albicans, exposure to azoles such as miconazole and ketoconazole induces aneuploidy-mediated tolerance that is dependent on Hsp90 and calcineurin (; ), and caspofungin paradoxical growth has been linked to segmental aneuploidy on Chromosome R that drives broad transcriptional remodeling (). The segmental aneuploidies we observed in C. auris—specifically on scaffolds 5 and 6, harboring TAC1b and ALG7—extend these observations to a new pathogen and suggest that stress-induced genomic plasticity may be a conserved adaptive strategy among pathogenic fungi. The requirement of Hsp90 and calcineurin for caspofungin tolerance in the survivors further aligns with the established role of these pathways in mediating stress adaptation and drug tolerance.

Collectively, our results demonstrate that exposure to ER stress can drive the emergence of survivors with multidrug resistance traits in C. auris, a pathogen already notorious for its propensity to develop antifungal resistance. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our experiments were conducted using a single C. auris isolate from clade III (African clade). C. auris comprises multiple genetically distinct clades (I–VI) that differ in geographic distribution, antifungal susceptibility profiles, and resistance mechanisms (). Clades I, III, and IV are associated with multidrug resistance, whereas clades II and VI exhibit lower resistance rates (). Clade-associated point mutations in ERG11—including F126L in clade III—have been linked to azole resistance, underscoring the genetic heterogeneity across clades (; ). Whether the ER stress-driven selection of fluconazole-resistant and caspofungin-tolerant survivors via MDR1 up-regulation and segmental aneuploidy observed in clade III is generalizable to other clades remains to be determined. The specific genomic regions subject to amplification (e.g., scaffolds 5 and 6 harboring TAC1b and ALG7) and the precise transcriptional responses may differ due to variations in genome architecture, regulatory networks, and baseline stress responses across clades. Future studies employing multiple isolates from clades I, II, IV, V, and VI will be essential to establish the breadth of these adaptive mechanisms across the C. auris species complex.

Conclusion

In summary, this study reveals that TUN-induced ER stress selects for C. auris survivors with acquired resistance to fluconazole and tolerance to caspofungin. These phenotypes arise through distinct molecular mechanisms: fluconazole resistance is associated with MDR1 up-regulation and enhanced Mdr1-associated efflux, whereas caspofungin tolerance depends on the Hsp90-calcineurin axis and chitin remodeling. Our findings underscore the capacity of C. auris to adapt to stress in ways that confer cross-resistance to clinically important antifungals, highlighting the need for therapeutic strategies that target adaptive pathways to prevent resistance emergence.
