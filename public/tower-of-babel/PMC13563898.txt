Epigenetic regulation of neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis: mechanisms, biomarkers, and translational challenges
Authors: Zhehan Zheng, Rui Tian, Yang Chen, Yinjiaozhi Li, Hongping Qu, Tingting Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and remains a major challenge in critical care medicine. Neutrophils are central components of innate immunity, but their functions are markedly altered during sepsis. These alterations include disturbed granulopoiesis, prolonged survival, impaired migration, dysregulated inflammatory responses, imbalanced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and excessive formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). Epigenetic regulation may help explain how neutrophils acquire distinct functional states during infection and inflammation. However, current evidence comes from diverse clinical and experimental settings, and the contribution of epigenetic changes to neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis should be interpreted in relation to disease stage, tissue context, and model system.Main bodyThis review summarizes current evidence on the epigenetic regulation of neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis, with emphasis on DNA methylation, histone modifications, non-coding RNAs, and RNA modifications. We first outline key features of septic neutrophils, including abnormal development and lifespan, inflammatory dysregulation, impaired chemotaxis, ROS imbalance, and NETs formation. We then discuss how epigenetic mechanisms are involved in these processes by influencing chromatin accessibility, transcriptional activity, inflammatory responsiveness, and neutrophil fate. Particular attention is given to the context-dependent effects of epigenetic regulation. In some settings, these changes may support antimicrobial defense, whereas in others they may contribute to endothelial injury, immunothrombosis, tissue damage, and organ dysfunction. We also consider the differences between evidence from human sepsis studies, animal models, isolated-cell experiments, and non-sepsis inflammatory conditions. Finally, we discuss the possible translational implications of these findings for biomarker development and therapeutic exploration, with attention to validation, patient selection, timing of intervention, and the balance between limiting inflammatory injury and preserving antimicrobial defense.ConclusionEpigenetic regulation provides a useful perspective for understanding the plasticity and dysfunction of neutrophils in sepsis. Future studies combining longitudinal clinical cohorts, single-cell and spatial multi-omics, and functional validation are needed to clarify which epigenetic changes are clinically relevant and whether they can be translated into reliable biomarkers or targeted therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and remains a major challenge in critical care medicine [1, 2]. Global epidemiological data estimated approximately 48.9 million incident cases of sepsis and 11 million sepsis-related deaths in 2017, accounting for nearly one-fifth of all deaths worldwide [3]. Although advances in early recognition, antimicrobial therapy, source control, and organ support have improved patient management, mortality remains high, particularly in patients with septic shock or multiple organ dysfunction [2, 4]. One major obstacle to effective therapy is the substantial heterogeneity of sepsis, including differences in pathogen type, infection site, disease stage, immune status, and organ involvement.

Neutrophils are indispensable effector cells of innate immunity and constitute a first line of defense against invading pathogens [5]. Under physiological conditions, they eliminate microorganisms through phagocytosis, degranulation, reactive oxygen species generation, cytokine production, and NETs formation [5]. In sepsis, however, neutrophil responses may become profoundly dysregulated. Impaired chemotaxis can limit recruitment to infectious foci, whereas excessive activation in the circulation or distant organs may promote endothelial injury, immunothrombosis, tissue damage, and organ dysfunction [6]. Recent single-cell and multi-omic studies further suggest that sepsis is associated with emergency granulopoiesis and the expansion of heterogeneous neutrophil populations, including immature and immunosuppressive subsets [7]. Therefore, neutrophils in sepsis should be viewed as dynamically regulated cells whose functions depend on timing, tissue microenvironment, and inflammatory context [8–10].

Epigenetic regulation provides a useful framework for understanding this functional plasticity, as it can regulate gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence [11, 12]. In innate immune cells, these mechanisms help shape chromatin accessibility, inflammatory responsiveness, immune memory, and tolerance during infection and inflammation [11, 12]. In neutrophils and their progenitors, epigenetic mechanisms may contribute to emergency granulopoiesis, inflammatory activation, migration, oxidative burst, delayed apoptosis, and NETs formation [7, 8, 10–12].

However, neutrophil epigenetic reprogramming in sepsis should be interpreted in relation to the experimental and clinical context in which it is observed. Available evidence comes from patient cohorts, animal models, isolated-cell stimulation systems, and non-sepsis inflammatory settings [7, 8, 11, 12]. These studies provide complementary insights but differ in disease specificity, causal inference, and translational relevance. Given the marked heterogeneity of sepsis, epigenetic regulation is best regarded as a context-dependent regulatory layer that may contribute to stage- and organ-specific neutrophil dysfunction, rather than as a universal driver of sepsis.

In this review, we critically summarize current evidence linking epigenetic regulation to neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis. We first outline key pathological features of septic neutrophils, including abnormal development and lifespan, dysregulated inflammation, impaired migration, ROS imbalance, and excessive NETs formation. We then discuss how major epigenetic mechanisms may regulate these processes, while distinguishing human evidence from preclinical and in vitro findings. Finally, we discuss the translational implications of neutrophil epigenetic reprogramming, including its potential value for biomarker development and therapeutic targeting. Rather than viewing these applications as universally applicable, we emphasize their dependence on sepsis heterogeneity, disease stage, tissue context, and the balance between antimicrobial protection and inflammatory injury.

Neutrophils and sepsis

An overview of neutrophils

Neutrophils, the most abundant leukocytes in human blood, serve as the first line of innate immune defense against bacterial and fungal infections [13]. They originate from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow under the regulation of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and undergo sequential differentiation through myeloblast to segmented neutrophil stages [14]. Mature neutrophils are 7–10 μm in diameter with a characteristic multilobed nucleus and contain three types of granules that store antimicrobial proteins and enzymes [15, 16].

Under physiological conditions, neutrophils are rapidly recruited to inflammatory sites through a well-orchestrated adhesion cascade involving selectin-mediated rolling, integrin-mediated firm adhesion, and transendothelial migration [16, 17]. Once at the infection site, they eliminate pathogens primarily via phagocytosis, degranulation, ROS production, and the formation of neutrophil NETs [18, 19]. These effector functions are tightly regulated to maintain immune homeostasis.

Neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis

Neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis is dynamic and context-dependent, encompassing altered granulopoiesis, prolonged survival, impaired migration, ROS imbalance, inflammatory dysregulation, and excessive NETs formation [6, 10]. These changes vary by disease stage, infection site, and tissue microenvironment, highlighting septic neutrophils as functionally plastic effectors rather than simply protective or pathogenic cells [6, 10].

Aberrant neutrophil development, emergency granulopoiesis, and lifespan extension

Under homeostatic conditions, neutrophils are generated through tightly regulated granulopoiesis, during which precursor cells acquire segmented nuclei, antimicrobial granules, adhesion molecules, and effector functions required for host defense [15]. During severe infection, this process shifts toward emergency granulopoiesis, enabling rapid production and release of neutrophils to meet increased peripheral demand [20, 21]. Although this response is initially protective, sustained emergency granulopoiesis in sepsis may lead to the expansion of immature or incompletely differentiated neutrophil populations with altered antimicrobial, inflammatory, and immunoregulatory properties [7, 22]. Recent single-cell multiomic analysis further showed that sepsis is associated with immunosuppressive mature and immature neutrophil subsets, altered hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell programs, and an extreme response endotype linked to poor outcomes [7].

In addition to altered development, septic neutrophils frequently show delayed apoptosis and prolonged survival [23]. This may help preserve antimicrobial defense during early infection, but persistent survival can impair inflammation resolution and promote tissue accumulation. Several mechanisms have been implicated, including heat shock protein 90 (HSP90)-mediated stabilization of c-Src and caspase-8, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)/phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) signaling, pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1)-dependent PD-L1 upregulation, and gut microbiota-derived acetate acting through fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4)-associated pathways [6, 13, 24–26] (Fig. 1). However, prolonged neutrophil survival should not be interpreted as uniformly beneficial or harmful; its consequences depend on whether neutrophils are retained at infectious foci, trapped in the circulation, or recruited to distant organs. Moreover, apoptosis is only one component of neutrophil death dysregulation. In sepsis, neutrophils may also undergo necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy-associated death, and NETosis, which can further amplify inflammation and organ injury when inadequately controlled [27].

Dysregulated inflammatory responses, immunosuppression, and ROS imbalance

Neutrophils are major contributors to early innate immune activation in sepsis. They release cytokines, chemokines, granule proteins, lipid mediators, and ROS that support pathogen clearance and shape subsequent immune responses [18, 28]. However, excessive or misplaced neutrophil activation can amplify systemic inflammation, damage endothelial and epithelial barriers, and contribute to organ dysfunction [18, 29]. This dual role is a central feature of septic neutrophil biology: neutrophils are indispensable for antimicrobial defense, yet their persistent activation and accumulation can transform them into major mediators of collateral tissue injury [29].

ROS dysregulation is a context-dependent feature of neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis. Under physiological conditions, activation of the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase 2 (NOX2/NADPH oxidase) complex generates a respiratory burst that supports phagosomal microbial killing, redox signaling, and stimulus-dependent NET formation [5]. In sepsis, however, this normally controlled response may become excessive, prolonged, or mislocalized, thereby contributing to oxidative stress, endothelial injury, inflammatory amplification, and organ dysfunction [30]. Excessive ROS may further promote NETs formation, thromboinflammation, and microvascular damage, linking neutrophil oxidative responses to sepsis-associated organ failure [30, 31]. Therefore, ROS dysregulation in septic neutrophils should be viewed not simply as increased oxidant production, but as an imbalance in the source, localization, timing, and magnitude of redox responses.

Septic neutrophils may also acquire immunosuppressive features. In human septic shock, low-density neutrophils with myeloid-derived suppressor cell-like activity can deplete arginine and constrain T-cell function [32]. In experimental sepsis or post-infectious immunosuppression, PD-L1-positive and Siglec-F-positive neutrophil subsets have been linked to delayed neutrophil apoptosis, T-cell suppression, secondary infection susceptibility, and organ accumulation [25, 26, 33]. These observations indicate that neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis is not a simple shift from hyperinflammation to immunosuppression, but rather a mixed state in which inflammatory injury and immune paralysis may occur simultaneously.

Impaired neutrophil migration and tissue recruitment

Efficient neutrophil trafficking requires a coordinated sequence of endothelial rolling, chemokine-driven activation, β-integrin-mediated firm adhesion, intraluminal crawling, transendothelial migration, and chemotactic navigation toward infected tissues [16, 17]. Under physiological conditions, C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) and related chemokine receptors guide neutrophils along inflammatory chemokine gradients, whereas adhesion molecules and cytoskeletal remodeling ensure stable endothelial interaction and tissue entry [16, 17].

In sepsis, this recruitment program becomes spatially misdirected rather than simply suppressed (Fig. 2). Neutrophil migration to the primary infectious focus is impaired, partly because systemic inflammatory signals induce chemokine receptor dysfunction, including CXCR2 downregulation or desensitization, thereby weakening pathogen-directed chemotaxis and delaying microbial clearance [34–36]. In parallel, septic neutrophils may aberrantly acquire C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2)-dependent responsiveness, which promotes C-C motif chemokine ligand 2/monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (CCL2/MCP-1)-mediated recruitment to distant organs rather than to the infectious focus [37].

This CXCR2–CCR2 imbalance helps explain a central paradox of septic neutrophil trafficking: insufficient recruitment for effective local pathogen control, but excessive accumulation in remote organs such as the lung, liver, and kidney [35, 37]. Once trapped in these tissues, activated neutrophils promote endothelial injury, capillary plugging, vascular leakage, thromboinflammation, and parenchymal damage. Thus, impaired neutrophil migration in sepsis should be understood as a maladaptive redistribution of neutrophils from protective antimicrobial trafficking toward organ-damaging infiltration.

Imbalanced NETs formation and sepsis-associated organ injury

NETs are web-like structures composed of extracellular DNA decorated with histones, myeloperoxidase (MPO), neutrophil elastase, and other granular proteins [38]. In sepsis, NETs become pathologically relevant when their formation is excessive, persistent, or intravascular. Under these conditions, NETs components act not only as antimicrobial structures but also as inflammatory and cytotoxic mediators [38, 39]. Extracellular histones can directly damage vascular and parenchymal cells, while neutrophil elastase, MPO, and DNA–histone scaffolds promote endothelial activation, glycocalyx degradation, vascular leakage, platelet adhesion, fibrin deposition, and complement–coagulation crosstalk [19, 40–42].

The lung is particularly susceptible to NETs-associated vascular injury during sepsis. Accumulation of NETs in the pulmonary microcirculation may aggravate endothelial barrier disruption, alveolar–capillary leakage, local inflammatory amplification, and microthrombus formation, thereby contributing to impaired gas exchange and sepsis-associated acute lung injury [43]. This does not mean that NETs are uniformly deleterious: localized and transient NETs formation may help contain invading pathogens, whereas systemic, persistent, or intravascular NETs release shifts the balance toward vascular and organ injury [40, 44]. Thus, NETs-associated pathology in sepsis is best interpreted as a failure of spatial and temporal control over an otherwise protective neutrophil effector mechanism.

Epigenetic regulation of neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis

Sepsis exposes neutrophils and their bone-marrow precursors to rapidly changing microbial, inflammatory, metabolic, and tissue-derived signals. Consistent with the broader heterogeneity of sepsis, neutrophil dysfunction should be viewed as a spectrum of disease-associated states rather than as a single defect [45, 46]. In patients and experimental models, neutrophils show diverse alterations in maturation, activation, migration, survival, and immunoregulatory programs, and single-cell studies have linked distinct neutrophil subsets with disease severity and prognosis [47, 48].

Epigenetic regulation provides a useful framework for linking these external signals to changes in neutrophil development and function; however, it should be regarded not as a single deterministic cause of sepsis, but as a context-dependent regulatory layer through which inflammatory and metabolic cues shape neutrophil functional states. Key epigenetic mechanisms relevant to neutrophil biology

Neutrophil development and activation are shaped by coordinated transcriptional and chromatin programs. During granulopoiesis, DNA methylation and enhancer activity change in a timed manner as progenitors progress toward mature neutrophils [49]. Although mature neutrophils are terminally differentiated, they are not transcriptionally inert; human neutrophils can rapidly remodel chromatin accessibility in response to microbial ligands and pathogens, supporting stimulus-specific gene-expression responses [50].

The epigenetic mechanisms most relevant to neutrophil biology include DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, non-coding RNAs, and RNA modifications (Fig. 3). DNA methylation can influence gene expression by altering transcription-factor binding or recruiting repressive protein complexes [51]. Histone acetylation, histone methylation, and chromatin remodeling provide dynamic control of chromatin accessibility and transcriptional output [52, 53].

Non-coding RNAs and RNA modifications add additional regulatory layers at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) can modulate immune gene-expression networks by affecting transcription, messenger RNA (mRNA) stability, or translation, whereas RNA modifications such as N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) influence RNA fate and immune-cell responses [54, 55]. In sepsis, these mechanisms may help explain how neutrophils acquire distinct functional states under different inflammatory, developmental, and tissue microenvironmental conditions.

Epigenetic regulation of neutrophil differentiation and emergency granulopoiesis in sepsis

Neutrophil differentiation is not only a developmental process that maintains the circulating neutrophil pool, but also a stress-responsive program that is rapidly reshaped during infection. Under steady-state conditions, granulopoiesis proceeds through a coordinated sequence from hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells to granulocyte–monocyte progenitors, promyelocytes, myelocytes, metamyelocytes, band cells, and mature segmented neutrophils. During severe infection and sepsis, this program shifts toward emergency granulopoiesis, in which inflammatory cytokines, pathogen-derived signals, and bone marrow niche cues accelerate myeloid output and promote the premature release of immature neutrophils. This response is initially adaptive because it replenishes neutrophils consumed at infected sites, but sustained or dysregulated emergency granulopoiesis may generate functionally heterogeneous neutrophils with impaired antimicrobial precision, altered migration, and immunosuppressive properties [7, 21, 56].

Epigenetic regulation provides a plausible mechanism by which these infection-related signals are translated into altered neutrophil production and maturation. During physiological granulopoiesis, DNA methylation changes are not uniformly distributed across the differentiation trajectory. Genome-wide analyses of human granulopoiesis indicate that methylation remodeling is most pronounced around lineage-restriction stages and is enriched at regulatory regions, including enhancers, rather than simply reflecting global methylation loss or gain [49]. Consistently, studies of terminal granulopoiesis show that DNA methylation and hydroxymethylation can influence gene expression and alternative splicing during the transition from promyelocytes to mature granulocytes [57]. Comparative analyses of human CD34+ hematopoietic cells, myeloid precursor cells, and mature neutrophils further support the idea that DNA methylation and histone marks change progressively during granulocytic differentiation [58]. These findings provide a developmental framework for understanding how sepsis may perturb neutrophil output, although most of this evidence comes from physiological granulopoiesis or non-sepsis hematopoietic models.

Human infection studies suggest that DNA methylation patterns may also reflect disease trajectory and neutrophil-biased host responses. In patients with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, persistent versus resolving infection was associated with distinct host DNA methylation signatures, with enrichment in immune and neutrophil-related pathways [59]. Although this study was not designed specifically around sepsis-induced granulopoiesis, it supports the broader concept that bacterial infection can leave measurable epigenetic signatures in circulating immune cells. Similarly, malaria-associated methylation studies indicate that acute infection and treatment can dynamically reshape DNA methylation profiles in circulating immune cells, including pathways related to neutrophil and myeloid leukocyte function [60]. These observations should be interpreted as infection-related evidence rather than direct proof that DNA methylation causally drives septic neutrophil differentiation; nevertheless, they help bridge developmental epigenetics with clinically observed immune remodeling during infection.

Histone modifications and chromatin remodeling further refine neutrophil lineage commitment and maturation. H3K4me3, a transcriptionally permissive mark, has been implicated in trained myelopoiesis at the hematopoietic stem cell level. Mesenchymal stromal cell-derived paracrine factors were shown to leave H3K4me3 marks at myelopoiesis and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)-related genes in hematopoietic stem cells, thereby enhancing subsequent emergency granulopoiesis and neutrophil-mediated antimicrobial responses in infection models [61]. In parallel, chromatin-remodeling factors are required for terminal neutrophil differentiation. SMARCD2, a switch/sucrose non-fermentable (SWI/SNF) chromatin-remodeling component, cooperates with CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein ε (C/EBPε) to regulate neutrophil granule gene expression and proper granulocytic maturation [62]. Histone deacetylase (HDAC)-related mechanisms also participate in neutrophil development: HDAC11 expression increases with neutrophil maturation and is associated with neutrophil migratory and phagocytic programs, whereas pharmacological HDAC inhibition can impair normal neutrophil progenitor expansion and differentiation [63, 64]. These data indicate that histone acetylation and chromatin accessibility influence neutrophil maturation, but they also caution against assuming that systemic epigenetic modulation would be uniformly beneficial during sepsis.

Non-coding RNAs add another layer of control over granulopoiesis. miRNA profiling across human bone marrow neutrophil precursors, circulating neutrophils, and exudated neutrophils shows dynamic miRNA expression during maturation and tissue recruitment [65]. Among these miRNAs, miR-223 is one of the best-characterized regulators of granulocytic differentiation. Mechanistically, miR-223 participates in a regulatory circuit involving nuclear factor I-A (NFI-A) and CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein α (C/EBPα), promoting the transition of myeloid progenitors toward granulocytic maturation [66]. Additional work showed that Polycomb-related mechanisms and miR-223 cooperate to silence NFI-A during human granulopoiesis, linking miRNA regulation directly to chromatin-level transcriptional control [67]. In mouse models, loss of miR-223 causes modest expansion of myeloid progenitors without abolishing hematopoietic stem cell maintenance, suggesting that miR-223 fine-tunes granulopoiesis rather than acting as a simple on/off switch [68]. Other miRNAs, including miR-125a and miR-130a, have also been implicated in granulocytic maturation or neutrophil protein-expression programs, indicating that miRNA networks shape both developmental timing and the functional readiness of newly generated neutrophils [69, 70].

More recent studies have begun to connect RNA modifications with neutrophil production and mobilization during systemic infection. In a cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced polymicrobial sepsis model, ALKBH5-dependent m⁶A demethylation was required for emergency granulopoiesis and neutrophil release from the bone marrow. Mechanistically, ALKBH5 stabilized CSF3R mRNA, increased granulocyte colony-stimulating factor receptor (G-CSFR) expression, and enhanced downstream STAT3 signaling, thereby supporting neutrophil production and mobilization during bacterial infection [71]. Importantly, this study included both mouse and human neutrophil data, making it one of the stronger pieces of mechanistic evidence linking an epitranscriptomic regulator to infection-induced neutrophil output. Beyond granulopoiesis itself, RNA modifications may also affect the fate of mature septic neutrophils. NAT10-dependent N⁴-acetylcytidine (ac⁴C) modification stabilizes ULK1 mRNA in neutrophils; reduced NAT10 expression during sepsis promotes ULK1 loss, STING–IRF3 activation, NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, and neutrophil pyroptosis [72]. This finding is more directly related to inflammatory cell death than differentiation, but it illustrates how RNA modifications can connect neutrophil developmental state, survival, and inflammatory injury in sepsis.

Collectively, these findings suggest that sepsis-associated neutrophil differentiation reflects context-dependent remodeling of granulopoiesis rather than a simple quantitative change in neutrophil production. Developmental epigenetic programs establish the regulatory framework for neutrophil maturation, while infection-induced epitranscriptomic changes, such as the ALKBH5–m⁶A–CSF3R axis, may tune emergency granulopoiesis and neutrophil mobilization during sepsis. This dynamic view better explains the emergence of developmentally and functionally divergent neutrophil states across different inflammatory contexts (Fig. 4).

Epigenetic regulation of neutrophil inflammatory activation, antimicrobial function, and ROS production in sepsis

Neutrophil activation in sepsis can be protective or injurious depending on timing, location, and disease severity. Early priming enhances antimicrobial functions such as phagocytosis, respiratory burst, cytokine release, and NETs formation, whereas sustained systemic activation contributes to vascular injury and organ dysfunction [73]. Epigenetic and post-transcriptional regulation provides one mechanism by which neutrophils adapt to these changing inflammatory cues.

Histone modifications are central to this functional plasticity. H3K4me3 chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) analyses in human neutrophils showed that lipopolysaccharide (LPS), TNF-α, and IL-10 induce distinct H3K4me3-marked transcriptional programs involving NF-κB signaling, cytokine production, apoptosis, and inflammatory resolution [74]. Further work indicated that neutrophil pre-activation and direct pathogen-like stimulation are associated with different histone modification patterns: H3K27ac and H3K4me1 are linked to enhancer priming, whereas LPS-driven activation is more closely associated with H3K4me3-marked transcription start sites [75]. In contrast, IL-10 induces a histone-modification program involving H3S10 phosphorylation and H3K9me3 demethylation-related pathways, supporting a shift toward anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair-associated neutrophil states [75]. These findings suggest that septic neutrophils exposed to different inflammatory windows may become epigenetically biased toward divergent functional trajectories rather than simply becoming globally activated.

The concept of trained immunity further illustrates how epigenetic remodeling can enhance neutrophil effector function. In healthy humans, Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccination induces long-lasting neutrophil reprogramming, characterized by increased activation markers, enhanced antimicrobial function, and genome-wide H3K4me3 changes [76]. β-glucan-induced trained granulopoiesis also rewires neutrophils toward enhanced ROS-dependent effector activity in experimental models [77]. In infection models, Shigella-trained zebrafish neutrophils acquire H3K4me3-enriched promoter landscapes linked to improved microbial recognition and mitochondrial ROS production [78]. Similarly, Candida albicans-stimulated hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells generate trained neutrophils with enhanced cytokine production, phagocytosis, and mitochondrial ROS (mtROS)-dependent fungal killing [79]. These studies are not all sepsis-specific, but they support the broader principle that inflammatory memory in the granulocytic compartment can be shaped by histone remodeling and metabolic adaptation (Fig. 5).

DNA methylation may also influence neutrophil antimicrobial and inflammatory programs during infection. In human neutrophils infected with Anaplasma phagocytophilum, genome-wide hypermethylation occurred within 24 h and was associated with transcriptional reprogramming that favored bacterial survival [80]. This pathogen suppresses antimicrobial functions, including respiratory burst, partly through downregulation of NADPH oxidase-related genes such as CYBB and RAC2, while simultaneously promoting inflammatory cytokine and chemokine expression [80]. Although this model is distinct from polymicrobial sepsis, it provides neutrophil-specific evidence that bacterial infection can remodel DNA methylation and chromatin states to alter ROS generation, apoptosis, and inflammatory output.

Non-coding RNAs constitute an additional regulatory layer governing neutrophil inflammatory activation. In human neutrophils and monocytes, LPS and other pro-inflammatory stimuli induce miR-9 through a MyD88- and NF-κB-dependent pathway; miR-9 then targets NFKB1, forming a negative feedback loop that fine-tunes NF-κB-dependent responses [81]. MiR-15, miR-16, and miR-214 regulate adenosine A2A receptor expression in human neutrophils, linking baseline miRNA profiles to interindividual differences in anti-inflammatory responsiveness [82]. In sepsis, lncRNA GSEC provides a more direct disease-related example: it is increased in neutrophils from septic patients and promotes inflammatory activation by stabilizing PFKFB3 mRNA and supporting glycolytic metabolism [83]. Additional lncRNA/mRNA network analyses in septic neutrophils suggest that sepsis reshapes ncRNA regulatory modules, although many of these candidates still require functional validation [84]. Recent work also identified a miR-132-5p–S100A8/A9 axis that limits IL-8 secretion in neutrophil-like cells, suggesting that miRNA-mediated feedback can restrain excessive cytokine release [85]. Thus, ncRNAs should not be viewed simply as pro- or anti-inflammatory factors, but as regulators that tune the magnitude, duration, and metabolic support of neutrophil inflammatory responses.

ROS production is a central neutrophil effector program in sepsis, but its biological consequences depend on the source and inflammatory context. NOX2/NADPH oxidase-derived ROS drive the classical respiratory burst, supporting phagosomal killing, redox signaling, and NOX-dependent NETs formation; however, excessive or mislocalized ROS can injure endothelial and epithelial barriers and amplify organ dysfunction [73, 86]. Pathogen-induced epigenetic remodeling may also reshape this program. In Anaplasma phagocytophilum-infected human neutrophils, altered DNA methylation and chromatin regulation were associated with repression of NADPH oxidase-related genes such as CYBB and RAC2, impaired respiratory burst, and enhanced microbial persistence [80]. In contrast, mitochondrial ROS represent a distinct ROS source: mtROS can support antimicrobial defense, as shown in pneumococcal infection models, but excessive mtROS may also promote inflammasome activation, mitochondrial DNA release, NETs formation, and tissue injury [87].

ROS also connects neutrophil activation with chromatin remodeling. Peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4)-mediated histone citrullination promotes chromatin decondensation and NETs formation [88], and early sepsis studies have linked ROS/PAD4/citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) signaling to NETs release through GPR109A-dependent regulation [89]. Trained-immunity models provide another layer of evidence: H3K4me3-enriched neutrophil programs induced by Shigella or Candida exposure enhance mtROS-dependent microbial killing, while recent bacterial sepsis models suggest that primed neutrophils can acquire ROS-adaptive states associated with improved bacterial clearance [78, 79, 90].

Overall, ROS production in septic neutrophils should be viewed as a source- and phase-dependent program rather than a uniformly harmful response. Epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, H3K4me3-associated training, and PAD4-mediated histone citrullination, may tune these ROS responses, but their clinical relevance in human sepsis requires further validation.

Epigenetic and post-transcriptional regulation of neutrophil migration and tissue recruitment in sepsis

Neutrophil migration in sepsis is not simply impaired or enhanced, but spatially misdirected. Effective antimicrobial defense requires coordinated bone marrow release, endothelial adhesion, transendothelial migration, and chemotactic navigation toward infected tissues. During sepsis, this program may become uncoupled from the anatomical site of infection: neutrophils can be insufficiently recruited to infectious foci while accumulating excessively in distant organs, thereby linking defective bacterial clearance with collateral tissue injury [34, 37]. Epigenetic and post-transcriptional mechanisms may contribute to this redistribution by reshaping chemokine receptor expression, adhesion signaling, cytoskeletal remodeling, inflammatory responsiveness, and tissue-retention programs.

Chromatin-level regulation provides one mechanism by which neutrophils adjust their migratory and tissue-adaptive states. Integrated transcriptomic and chromatin-accessibility profiling has shown that neutrophils undergo active regulatory remodeling during the transition from bone marrow to blood and inflamed tissues, with distinct transcription factor networks controlling maturation, effector activation, and survival [91]. Although this work was not performed in sepsis, it supports the concept that neutrophil recruitment is accompanied by regulated transcriptional and chromatin-state changes rather than passive trafficking alone. In a sepsis-specific model, chemoattractant receptor-homologous molecule expressed on Th2 cells (CRTH2) deficiency improved neutrophil migration and survival after CLP, partly through preservation of CXCR2 expression; this effect was associated with increased histone H3 acetylation at the Cxcr2 promoter, linking chromatin accessibility to chemokine receptor regulation during polymicrobial sepsis [92]. HDAC11 also appears to participate in neutrophil biology by associating with transcriptional machinery involved in inflammatory and migratory gene programs, suggesting that histone deacetylation pathways may influence neutrophil migratory competence [64].

RNA modifications provide some of the most direct mechanistic evidence connecting epigenetic regulation with neutrophil migration in sepsis. ALKBH5-mediated m⁶A demethylation enhances neutrophil-intrinsic migratory capacity during bacterial infection. In CLP-induced sepsis, ALKBH5 deficiency increased mortality, bacterial burden, and inflammatory cytokine production, largely because neutrophil migration into the peritoneal cavity was impaired. Mechanistically, ALKBH5 increased the stability of migration-related transcripts, including Cxcr2 and Nlrp12, while limiting transcripts associated with impaired migration, such as Ptger4, Tnc, and Wnk1 [93]. METTL3-mediated m⁶A methylation represents a complementary mechanism. In endotoxemia, METTL3 promoted neutrophil activation by stabilizing Tlr4 mRNA and enhancing TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling, thereby affecting bone marrow neutrophil release, activation, and CXCR2-related responses [94]. Together, these findings indicate that m⁶A regulation can tune neutrophil trafficking through both chemokine-receptor availability and inflammatory receptor signaling (Fig. 6).

Non-coding RNAs also modulate neutrophil motility and recruitment, although much of this evidence comes from zebrafish or non-sepsis inflammatory models. A phenotypic microRNA screen identified miR-199 as a regulator of neutrophil migration through CDK2, reducing neutrophil motility in zebrafish and human neutrophil-like systems without producing broad immunosuppression [95]. Similarly, miR-99 was shown to regulate neutrophil migration and antimicrobial defense through RORα-related pathways [96]. Other small RNA-associated programs, including those linked to RAC2, cytoskeletal organization, and neutrophil extravasation, further suggest that post-transcriptional regulation can fine-tune the migratory machinery of neutrophils [97, 98]. These studies are not direct human sepsis evidence, but they provide mechanistic support for the idea that neutrophil chemotaxis is regulated by small RNA networks rather than by chemokine gradients alone.

The spatial consequences of altered neutrophil recruitment are particularly relevant to sepsis-associated organ injury. CXCR2-dependent trafficking is generally required for pathogen-directed neutrophil recruitment, whereas aberrant CCR2-dependent neutrophil infiltration has been linked to remote organ injury and multiple organ dysfunction during sepsis [16]. These findings suggest that epigenetically or post-transcriptionally regulated chemokine-receptor programs may influence not only the magnitude but also the anatomical distribution of neutrophil responses. Organ-specific vascular and tissue-derived cues further shape neutrophil accumulation. For example, early CLP-induced sepsis is accompanied by glomerular neutrophil recruitment, E-selectin expression, and endothelial NF-κB/MAPK activation [99]. Time-resolved single-cell transcriptomic analysis of sepsis-induced acute liver dysfunction further identified liver endothelial and neutrophil subsets associated with disease progression, including CD31⁺SELE⁺GLUT1⁺ endothelial cells and Ly6G⁺Lta4h⁺Sort1⁺ neutrophils [100]. Although these studies do not directly prove epigenetic causality, they highlight the tissue-specific cellular context in which epigenetically regulated neutrophil trafficking may contribute to organ injury.

Overall, neutrophil migration in sepsis should be viewed as a regulated redistribution program rather than a simple chemotactic defect. Chromatin remodeling, histone acetylation, m⁶A RNA modification, miRNA networks, chemokine receptor switching, and endothelial activation collectively shape where neutrophils migrate, how strongly they respond to tissue cues, and whether their recruitment supports antimicrobial defense or amplifies organ injury.

Epigenetic regulation of NETs formation and NETs-mediated tissue injury in sepsis

NETs formation represents one of the neutrophil functions most directly linked to chromatin remodeling. Unlike phagocytosis or degranulation, NETs release requires large-scale chromatin decondensation, nuclear envelope disruption, and extrusion of DNA decorated with histones and granular proteins [101, 102]. In sepsis, this process has a dual role. On the one hand, NETs can trap microorganisms in the bloodstream and infected tissues, thereby contributing to antimicrobial defense [103]. On the other hand, excessive or poorly cleared NETs may amplify endothelial injury, thrombosis, microvascular dysfunction, and remote organ damage [104, 105]. Therefore, epigenetic regulation of NETs formation should be interpreted as a context-dependent program that may be protective or pathogenic depending on infectious burden, disease stage, tissue compartment, and NETs clearance capacity.

Histone citrullination is the best-characterized chromatin modification involved in NETs formation. PAD4-mediated conversion of histone arginine residues into citrulline reduces the positive charge of histones and weakens histone-DNA interactions, thereby facilitating chromatin decondensation [88]. Early mechanistic studies showed that PAD4-dependent histone hypercitrullination promotes chromatin unfolding and NETs-like structure formation in granulocytes and neutrophils [88, 106]. More recent work has further refined this view by showing that apoptotic signaling can engage PAD4 and induce a coordinated redistribution of CitH3 from promoter regions to broader intergenic and intronic regions before DNA extrusion, suggesting that NETs formation can arise from an epigenetically primed neutrophil death program rather than from random chromatin collapse [107]. In sepsis models, pharmacological inhibition of PAD4 with Cl-amidine reduces CitH3 and NETs formation and improves survival after CLP [108]. PAD4 deficiency or NETs-targeting strategies also attenuate endotoxemic shock, intravascular coagulation, and microvascular dysfunction in selected experimental settings [40, 109]. However, PAD4 deficiency does not uniformly impair bacteremia in polymicrobial sepsis, and NETs depletion with DNase can worsen bacterial dissemination in some models [105, 109]. These findings emphasize that PAD4-CitH3-dependent NETs formation is a therapeutically attractive but biologically double-edged pathway (Fig. 7).

Histone acetylation provides another layer of NETs regulation, but the direction of its effect appears highly dependent on dose, stimulus, and cellular state. In human neutrophils, low concentrations of the HDAC inhibitors belinostat and panobinostat increase histone acetylation, including AcH4, and promote baseline as well as stimulus-induced NETosis [110]. In contrast, higher concentrations of the same inhibitors suppress NETosis and shift neutrophil death toward apoptosis, particularly in non-activated cells [111]. This dose-dependent switch may explain the apparently conflicting effects of HDAC inhibition reported in different NETosis models. Moreover, zinc-dependent HDACs have been shown to support NETs formation by enabling histone H3 deacetylation and subsequent PAD4 activity, and HDAC inhibition can reduce NETosis and inflammation in microbial pneumonia and septic shock models [112]. Therefore, HDAC inhibitors should not be described globally as either pro-NETotic or anti-NETotic. Instead, their effects likely reflect the balance between chromatin relaxation, PAD4 accessibility, ROS-dependent signaling, transcriptional activity, and activation of alternative neutrophil death pathways.

DNA methylation may also influence NETs competence by controlling the expression of NETs-related enzymes. In neutrophil-like HL-60 cells, treatment with the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitor 5-azacytidine induces DNA demethylation, increases PAD4 expression and CitH3 formation, and enhances spontaneous NETosis [113]. Although this evidence comes from a neutrophil-like cell system rather than primary neutrophils from patients with sepsis, it supports the concept that DNA methylation status can regulate the threshold for NETs formation. Similarly, sodium acetate, a short-chain fatty acid, enhances NETosis in neutrophil-like HL-60 cells through increased histone acetylation, including H3K9ac and H3K14ac, before calcium ionophore-induced histone citrullination [114]. These observations suggest that metabolic cues may influence NETs formation through chromatin accessibility, but their relevance to human sepsis remains to be validated.

Non-coding RNAs provide a post-transcriptional layer linking inflammatory signaling to NETs regulation. In abdominal sepsis, miR-155 promotes pulmonary NETs formation by increasing PAD4 expression and histone H3 citrullination. Genetic deletion of miR-155 reduces lung NETs accumulation, neutrophil recruitment, CXCL1/CXCL2 production, edema, and tissue injury after CLP [115]. Conversely, miR-146a appears to function as a negative regulator of neutrophil activation and NETs-associated thromboinflammation in sterile and LPS-driven inflammatory models, although direct human sepsis-specific evidence remains limited [116]. Beyond miRNAs, recent lncRNA-mRNA co-expression analysis in septic patients identified upregulated AQP9 and AQP9-associated lncRNA networks in neutrophils. Functional experiments further showed that AQP9 promotes NETs formation and inflammatory activation, whereas AQP9 inhibition attenuates cytokine production and lung injury in murine sepsis models [117]. These findings indicate that non-coding RNA networks may regulate NETosis not only by targeting canonical NETs enzymes such as PAD4, but also by modulating metabolic and membrane-transport pathways that shape neutrophil activation.

NETs may also propagate epigenetic and epitranscriptomic injury programs in non-neutrophil target cells. In sepsis-associated acute lung injury, NETs accumulation induces ferroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells through METTL3-dependent m⁶A modification of Gpx4 transcripts [118]. Another study showed that NETs impair autophagic flux in alveolar epithelial cells by activating METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification of Sirt1 mRNA, thereby linking extracellular chromatin release to epithelial epitranscriptomic dysfunction [119]. More recently, neutrophil-intrinsic METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification was reported to promote NETs formation through stabilization of Syk mRNA and activation of the SYK/ERK/MEK pathway in acute lung injury models [120]. Although this latter study is not specific to sepsis, it provides direct evidence that m⁶A regulation can control NETosis within neutrophils themselves. Together, these studies suggest that NETs formation and NETs-induced tissue injury are connected by reciprocal epigenetic and epitranscriptomic mechanisms across immune and parenchymal compartments.

Mechanistically, the PAD4–CitH3–NETs axis represents the most direct link between chromatin remodeling and sepsis-associated organ injury. A recent clinical and experimental study showed that the aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) rs671 variant is associated with increased susceptibility to septic acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and higher circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) and MPO–DNA levels, whereas ALDH2 activation with Alda-1 limits NETosis by promoting CHIP-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation of PAD4 [121]. Although ALDH2 is not a classical epigenetic enzyme, it highlights a druggable upstream checkpoint of PAD4-dependent NETosis. Thus, NETs-targeted interventions should be developed with attention to disease stage, organ involvement, NETs burden, and preservation of host defense.

Clinical and translational significance

Epigenetic regulation of neutrophils in sepsis has translational value mainly in three interconnected directions: biomarker development, mechanism-derived intervention, and delivery-guided therapeutic refinement. However, these applications differ substantially in evidence strength and clinical readiness. NETs-related chromatin products are currently the closest to clinical investigation and translational assay development, whereas RNA-based and epigenome-targeted interventions remain largely preclinical. Therefore, neutrophil epigenetic mechanisms should be interpreted not as immediately actionable therapeutic targets in all patients, but as regulatory programs that may help define disease endotypes, identify high-risk organ injury, and guide stage- or organ-specific interventions. Biomarkers of neutrophil epigenetic dysfunction

Among currently available candidates, extracellular chromatin-associated markers provide accessible circulating readouts of neutrophil activation, NETs formation, and tissue injury, but their biological specificity differs substantially. CitH3 and MPO–DNA complexes are the most NETs-associated markers: CitH3 reflects PAD2/4-mediated histone citrullination during chromatin decondensation, whereas MPO–DNA complexes capture extracellular DNA physically associated with the neutrophil granule enzyme myeloperoxidase [122–126]. Nucleosomes and total histone H3 indicate extracellular chromatin or histone release and have been associated with severe infection, sepsis-related organ dysfunction, and ARDS-related complications, but they are not exclusively neutrophil-derived [127, 128]. By contrast, cfDNA should be interpreted as a broader extracellular nucleic-acid/damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) marker rather than a NETs-specific readout, because it may originate from NETs, damaged parenchymal cells, endothelial cells, other dying leukocytes, microbial DNA, or impaired nucleic-acid clearance during critical illness [129–131].

CitH3 has been the most extensively investigated epigenetically modified chromatin marker in sepsis. Experimental studies showed that circulating CitH3 appears early after endotoxin challenge, responds to PAD inhibition, and may distinguish endotoxic shock from non-infectious inflammatory shock in animal models [122]. In critically ill patients, serum CitH3 levels are higher in septic shock than in non-septic shock and correlate with Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and prognosis, supporting its value as a marker of disease severity rather than merely neutrophil activation [123]. Similar associations have been reported in septic acute pancreatitis, where CitH3 was associated with PAD2/PAD4, dsDNA, SOFA score, ICU admission, and survival [124]. More recently, nucleosome, histone H3, and CitH3 levels were also detected in children with pneumonia requiring respiratory support and were associated with sepsis and pediatric ARDS-related complications, suggesting that extracellular chromatin markers may reflect organ injury in severe infection [128].

MPO–DNA complexes provide a more NETs-associated readout than total cfDNA because they measure DNA complexed with a neutrophil granule protein. In patients with septic shock, plasma MPO–DNA levels have been associated with organ dysfunction and adverse outcomes [125]. Another clinical study showed that MPO–DNA and cfDNA levels were elevated in patients with sepsis and correlated with SOFA score, inflammatory markers, coagulation disturbance, and organ damage indicators [126]. These findings support the use of MPO–DNA as part of a NETs-related biomarker panel. However, MPO–DNA assays remain less standardized than routine inflammatory markers, and thresholds for diagnosis, prognostic stratification, or treatment monitoring have not been universally established.

Extracellular vesicle-associated RNAs represent another biomarker layer because they may capture immune-cell activation states and intercellular communication. Plasma extracellular vesicle Y-RNA subtype ratios show cell-type-associated patterns and have been proposed as candidate biomarkers for inflammatory diseases [132]. More directly related to neutrophil dysfunction, neutrophil-derived extracellular vesicle miR-150-5p has been associated with sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy, and a model integrating miR-150-5p with NT-proBNP, left ventricular ejection fraction, and SOFA score improved diagnostic performance for cardiac involvement in sepsis [133]. Plasma-derived extracellular vesicle miRNAs and neutrophil-related membrane proteins have also been reported to predict sepsis-associated organ dysfunction and prognosis [134]. Nevertheless, unless extracellular vesicles are rigorously purified and their cellular origin is validated, plasma extracellular vesicle miRNAs should be described as EV-associated or neutrophil-related signatures rather than neutrophil-specific biomarkers.

Single-cell and transcriptomic studies further support the interpretation of neutrophil-related biomarkers within disease endotypes rather than as isolated diagnostic tests. A recent consensus blood transcriptomic framework classified sepsis into distinct molecular subtypes, with one subtype characterized by IL1R2⁺ and PADI4⁺ immature neutrophil features, inflammatory activation, and endothelial dysfunction–related biomarker profiles [135]. These findings suggest that circulating neutrophil markers may be most informative when integrated with immune-cell state, endothelial or organ injury markers, and clinical trajectory.

Mechanism-derived intervention axes

Mechanism-derived interventions can be broadly grouped into NETs/citrullination-targeted strategies, histone modification-targeted strategies, DNA methylation-related immunomodulation, and RNA-level or epitranscriptomic regulation. Among these, PAD4/CitH3/NETs-directed approaches currently have the strongest conceptual link to neutrophil epigenetic dysfunction, although most evidence remains preclinical.

Targeting histone citrullination and NETs formation is a direct strategy for limiting neutrophil-driven tissue injury and immunothrombosis. In murine sepsis models, the PAD inhibitor Cl-amidine reduced histone H3 citrullination, inhibited NETs formation, attenuated inflammatory injury, and improved survival [108]. Neutralization of CitH3 also improved outcomes in experimental endotoxic shock, supporting the idea that extracellular citrullinated histones may act not only as biomarkers but also as pathogenic mediators [136]. In sepsis-associated ARDS, neutrophil ALDH2 has been linked to PAD4-dependent NETs formation and lung injury, suggesting that modulation of upstream metabolic or redox regulators may indirectly reduce pathogenic NETosis [121]. However, NETs formation can contribute to pathogen containment in selected contexts. Therefore, NETs-targeted strategies are most rationally considered for patients with evidence of excessive NETs burden, endothelial injury, immunothrombosis, ARDS, or disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)-like manifestations, rather than as universal anti-inflammatory therapy.

Histone acetylation and deacetylation represent another intervention axis, but the interpretation of HDAC inhibition in sepsis requires caution. Broad HDAC inhibitors such as SAHA have improved survival and attenuated coagulation imbalance in lethal murine sepsis models [137, 138], and selective HDAC6 inhibition prolonged survival in a two-hit hemorrhagic shock and sepsis model [139]. Zinc-dependent HDACs have also been implicated in NETs formation, with HDAC inhibition reducing NETosis and inflammatory amplification in experimental systems [112]. These findings suggest that histone acetylation pathways regulate both inflammatory transcription and neutrophil effector functions. However, HDAC inhibitors are not neutrophil-specific and may affect macrophages, endothelial cells, lymphocytes, epithelial cells, and coagulation pathways. Their effects may also vary according to drug class, dose, timing, infection burden, and immune phase. Thus, HDAC-targeted therapy should be presented as systemic epigenetic immunomodulation with potential relevance to neutrophil dysfunction, not as a mature neutrophil-specific therapy.

DNA methylation-targeted approaches have also shown protective effects in experimental sepsis or endotoxemia. Procainamide, a DNA methylation inhibitor, alleviated multiple organ dysfunction in rats with endotoxic shock [140]. In severe sepsis models, inhibition of DNA methylation improved survival and regulated NF-κB-associated inflammatory responses [141]. These findings support a role for DNA methylation in sepsis-associated inflammatory dysregulation. However, DNMT inhibitors such as decitabine and azacitidine have broad effects on proliferating cells and are clinically used mainly in hematologic malignancies. Their potential use in sepsis is constrained by infection risk, cytopenia, off-target effects, and the difficulty of selecting an appropriate therapeutic window. Therefore, DNA methylation-targeted interventions currently remain mechanistic proof-of-concept rather than clinically ready sepsis therapies.

Histone methylation and demethylation pathways provide additional candidate targets. Pharmacological inhibition of EZH2 altered innate inflammatory responses and attenuated septic shock in experimental models [142]. Inhibition of the histone demethylase JMJD3 by GSKJ4 reduced early inflammatory responses and improved survival in bacterial sepsis models, in part through upregulation of miR-146a [143]. These observations support the idea that chromatin-modifying enzymes may regulate inflammatory set points during sepsis. Yet, as with HDAC and DNMT inhibitors, these agents are not specific to neutrophils, and their effects on host defense, tissue repair, hematopoiesis, and immune suppression require systematic evaluation.

RNA-level regulation is attractive because miRNAs and RNA modifications can tune neutrophil inflammatory activation, migration, and NETs formation. miR-let-7b suppresses canonical TLR4/NF-κB signaling and dampens neutrophilic inflammation [144], whereas miR-155 promotes NETs formation and lung injury in an abdominal sepsis model [115]. Other miRNAs identified in macrophages, neutrophils, zebrafish inflammation models, or pulmonary infection models may regulate migration or antimicrobial responses, but they should be interpreted as mechanistic discovery evidence unless validated in human sepsis [95–97, 145]. In addition, RNA modifications have emerged as a relevant epitranscriptomic layer. ALKBH5-dependent m⁶A demethylation supports neutrophil migration and antibacterial defense [93], whereas METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification can enhance neutrophil activation through TLR4 signaling [94]. A recent study further showed that METTL3 promotes NETs formation via m⁶A-dependent stabilization of SYK mRNA and activation of the SYK/ERK/MEK pathway during acute lung injury [120]. Together, these findings indicate that m⁶A regulators may have context-dependent effects: suppressing METTL3-driven NETosis may reduce tissue injury in one setting, whereas preserving ALKBH5-dependent migration may be important for bacterial clearance in another.

Delivery strategies and organ-directed modulation

A major obstacle for neutrophil-directed epigenetic or RNA-level therapy is delivery. Many nucleic acid-based agents are unstable in circulation, poorly taken up by target cells, and prone to off-target immune activation. Small-molecule epigenetic drugs can reach multiple tissues but generally lack cell specificity. Therefore, delivery strategies are not merely technical additions; they are central to whether neutrophil epigenetic modulation can become clinically feasible.

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), extracellular vesicles, cell membrane-coated nanoparticles, and other biomimetic platforms have been explored to improve stability, cellular uptake, and tissue distribution of nucleic acid therapeutics in sepsis and organ injury [146, 147]. Neutrophil-targeted LNPs delivering PD-L1 siRNA have been reported to ameliorate sepsis-induced immune dysfunction in experimental models, supporting the feasibility of RNA-level immune modulation in neutrophil-related pathways [148]. Neutrophil membrane-engineered plant-derived exosomes loaded with miR-182-5p targeted the NOX4/Drp-1/NLRP3 axis and alleviated sepsis-associated acute lung injury [149]. Pulmonary delivery of anti-TNF-α siRNA-loaded LNPs also reduced lung inflammation in murine acute lung injury, illustrating the value of organ-directed local delivery [150]. These approaches are promising because they may reduce systemic exposure while increasing drug concentration in inflamed tissues.

Neutrophil-based or neutrophil-hitchhiking nanoplatforms provide another strategy for targeting inflamed organs. Framework nucleic acid-based and neutrophil-based nanoplatforms have been used to deliver anti-inflammatory compounds to sites of inflammation [151]. Artificial exosomes have also been reported to reshape sepsis immune homeostasis by modulating neutrophil fate and blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 axis [152]. Although these studies are not all epigenetic in the strict sense, they are relevant to this review because they demonstrate how neutrophil-targeted delivery, RNA-level regulation, and immune rebalancing may be integrated in future sepsis therapy.

Messenger RNA-based and programmable epigenomic platforms represent more speculative but conceptually important future directions. A recent review summarized mRNA nanotherapeutics for sepsis, emphasizing immune modulation, delivery efficiency, safety, and regulatory challenges [153]. Programmable mRNA-based epigenomic controllers have also been developed to modulate single or multiplexed gene expression programs [154]. Although these technologies have not yet been validated for sepsis treatment, they may provide a potential route for transient, tunable, and reversible regulation of inflammatory gene networks. For neutrophil dysfunction, however, their application would require stringent control of tissue distribution, expression duration, immune activation, and off-target epigenomic effects.

Organ-aware targeting is important because neutrophil dysfunction in sepsis is shaped by both cellular state and tissue microenvironment. Single-cell studies of septic lung and liver injury have shown that local immune and endothelial niches can remodel neutrophil activation, migratory behavior, NETs-associated programs, and immunosuppressive potential [100, 155]. Accordingly, future delivery systems should be tailored to the dominant organ injury pattern, such as pulmonary-targeted delivery for sepsis-associated lung injury, liver-directed approaches for acute liver dysfunction, and vascular or NETs-targeted strategies for immunothrombotic injury.

Translational barriers and biomarker-guided implementation

The major barrier to clinical translation is not the absence of candidate mechanisms, but the difficulty of matching a given intervention to the appropriate neutrophil state, disease stage, infection context, and organ injury pattern. Sepsis is biologically heterogeneous, and patients with comparable clinical severity may differ substantially in inflammatory activation, immune suppression, coagulation disturbance, metabolic adaptation, pathogen burden, and tissue injury [156, 157]. This heterogeneity helps explain why broad immunomodulatory strategies in unselected sepsis populations have often failed, and argues against applying neutrophil epigenetic interventions as one-size-fits-all therapies.

Transcriptomic and immune endotyping studies provide a potential framework for patient selection. Recent consensus efforts have organized sepsis blood transcriptomic subtypes into more reproducible frameworks, which may facilitate predictive enrichment and trial design [135]. However, these classifications should not be viewed as fixed labels. Biomarker-based immune classes may change during ICU treatment, and longitudinal endotype trajectories may be more informative than a single baseline assignment [158]. This is particularly relevant to neutrophil-directed therapy, because neutrophil maturation, trafficking, antimicrobial activity, NETs-associated injury programs, and immunosuppressive phenotypes may evolve rapidly during the same disease course.

Therapeutic timing should therefore be guided by immune monitoring rather than by diagnosis alone. Biomarker-guided immunotherapy studies provide clinical proof-of-concept that host-directed interventions can be assigned according to immune state, such as macrophage activation-like syndrome or sepsis-induced immunoparalysis [159, 160]. Although these trials did not specifically target neutrophil epigenetic programs, they establish an important translational principle: immunomodulatory therapy is most likely to succeed when the target pathway, immune phase, and infection-control status are aligned. For neutrophil-directed epigenetic or NETs-modulating therapies, trial eligibility should therefore incorporate not only clinical severity, but also evidence of harmful neutrophil activation, adequate pathogen control, dominant organ injury, and absence of profound immune paralysis.

Clinical implementation will also require companion biomarker panels rather than isolated markers (Fig. 8). NET-associated markers, such as MPO–DNA complexes, and broader extracellular DNA markers, such as cfDNA, show clinical associations with sepsis severity and organ dysfunction, but their interpretation depends on assay platform, biological specificity, and non-neutrophil sources of extracellular DNA [126]. Nucleosomes and circulating histones may reflect extracellular chromatin release, but they are not specific to neutrophil-derived NETs and remain limited by technical and biological variability [161]. EV-associated miRNAs may provide additional information on immune-cell communication and inter-organ signaling, but extracellular vesicle studies require rigorous vesicle isolation, characterization, normalization, and source validation before clinical application [162]. Future studies should therefore move beyond single-marker approaches and develop integrated panels combining NETs burden, immune phase, organ injury, coagulation activation, and pathogen control.

Species and model differences remain an additional limitation. Although human and mouse neutrophils share conserved transcriptional programs, species-specific differences and model-dependent inflammatory contexts remain important when extrapolating preclinical findings to human sepsis [163]. Zebrafish models are valuable for imaging and genetic discovery, particularly for neutrophil migration and miRNA function [164], but should be regarded as discovery systems rather than direct evidence of human sepsis biology. Similarly, neutrophil-like cell lines and isolated-cell stimulation systems are useful for dissecting molecular pathways, but cannot reproduce the vascular, metabolic, microbial, and organ-specific complexity of sepsis.

Conclusion and perspectives

Neutrophil epigenetic regulation in sepsis should be understood as a context-dependent interface between infection, inflammatory signaling, emergency granulopoiesis, tissue microenvironment, and organ injury. Rather than representing a universal driver of sepsis, epigenetic and epitranscriptomic mechanisms may help explain why neutrophils acquire divergent functional states across disease stages, including antimicrobial activation, maladaptive tissue infiltration, excessive NETs formation, and immunosuppressive phenotypes. This review provides a more balanced interpretation of current evidence and avoids direct extrapolation from preclinical or non-sepsis models to human sepsis biology.

From a translational perspective, the most immediate value of neutrophil epigenetic research may lie in improving patient stratification rather than in broad application of epigenetic drugs. Future clinical studies should integrate neutrophil-related chromatin, RNA, and NETs-associated markers with transcriptomic endotypes, organ injury patterns, infection-control status, and longitudinal immune monitoring. Recent consensus transcriptomic frameworks and biomarker-guided immunotherapy trials support the feasibility of immune-state-guided treatment, but also emphasize that static baseline classification is insufficient for a rapidly evolving syndrome such as sepsis [135, 158, 159].

Several mechanistically grounded priorities deserve further testing. First, NETs-directed therapy should be developed as a biomarker-selected strategy for patients with high CitH3/MPO–DNA burden and vascular or lung-dominant injury, rather than as nonspecific anti-inflammatory treatment. Second, the ALKBH5–m⁶A axis represents a more precise epitranscriptomic target because it links emergency granulopoiesis, CSF3R–STAT3 signaling, CXCR2/NLRP12-dependent neutrophil migration, and antibacterial defense; future studies should determine whether this pathway can be preserved or enhanced without amplifying systemic inflammation [71, 93]. Third, m⁶A regulators may have functionally divergent roles in septic neutrophils, as ALKBH5 appears to support protective migration whereas METTL3 may promote TLR4-driven activation or SYK-dependent NETs formation, indicating that epitranscriptomic therapy must be pathway- and phase-specific rather than globally inhibitory [94, 120]. Finally, sepsis-induced hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) imprinting should be investigated as a mechanism connecting acute emergency granulopoiesis with post-sepsis myeloid skewing, secondary infection susceptibility, and persistent immune dysfunction in survivors [165].

To move the field forward, future studies should prioritize longitudinal sampling from acute illness to recovery, paired blood–tissue profiling, neutrophil-resolved single-cell and epigenomic assays, and functional validation in human neutrophils or clinically relevant ex vivo systems. Candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets should be evaluated not only for statistical association with sepsis severity, but also for biological specificity, assay reproducibility, temporal stability, and ability to guide treatment decisions. In this sense, neutrophil epigenetic regulation is unlikely to serve as a standalone solution for sepsis management, but may complement mechanism-based stratification and precision immunomodulation in carefully selected patients.
