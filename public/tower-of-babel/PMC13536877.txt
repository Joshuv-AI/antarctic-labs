Molecular surveillance of antimalarial drug resistance markers in the pfcrt, pfmdr1 and pfmrp1 genes of Plasmodium falciparum isolates from southeastern Iran
Authors: Mohadeseh Jafari, Romina Rezapour, Fahimeh Nemati, Akram Abouie Mehrizi, Sakineh Pirahmadi
Published: 2026

Background

Malaria continues to represent a major global public health challenge, despite decades of control efforts, with a disproportionate burden of incidence and mortality concentrated in tropical and subtropical regions. Among the Plasmodium species, P. falciparum is responsible for the most severe clinical manifestations and the majority of malaria-related deaths worldwide [1]. In 2024 alone, malaria affected an estimated 282 million people globally and caused nearly 610,000 deaths, underscoring the urgent need for more effective and coordinated public health interventions [2].

Despite the central role of antimalarial drugs in malaria control, the evolution and widespread dissemination of drug-resistant P. falciparum strains during recent decades have substantially undermined treatment efficacy and control strategies [3]. Chloroquine (CQ) was used for decades as the primary treatment for P. falciparum malaria; however, the global spread of CQ-resistant parasites ultimately resulted in its withdrawal from recommended treatment protocols in endemic regions [4]. Consequently, the use of combination antimalarial treatments has been favored over monotherapies due to their enhanced efficacy in controlling malaria [5]. The high therapeutic effectiveness of artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) in uncomplicated malaria has made them a central component of global malaria control and elimination strategies [6]. However, recent reports of emerging resistance to artemisinin and its partner drugs have exacerbated the situation, emphasizing the urgent need for new, effective, and affordable antimalarial agents [7, 8]. Continuous monitoring of resistance-associated molecular markers can provide valuable insights into the emergence of resistant genotypes and their geographical distribution [9, 10].

Genetic alterations in the P. falciparum chloroquine-resistance transporter (pfcrt), most notably the K76T substitution, have been strongly linked to resistance against CQ and amodiaquine [11]. Several studies have reported the re-emergence of CQ-sensitive strains and a gradual decline in the frequency of resistant isolates after the discontinuation of CQ [12–14]. However, the extent and rate of this resistance reversal can vary considerably depending on regional epidemiological factors and the antimalarial regimens used to treat other Plasmodium species [15, 16]. Therefore, continuous surveillance of the current status of resistance-associated alleles in the pfcrt gene can provide valuable insights into the persistence of resistant genotypes or the potential re-emergence of drug sensitivity. Such knowledge may support the reconsideration of CQ reuse [17].

The Plasmodium falciparum multidrug resistance 1 (pfmdr1) gene is a key genetic determinant influencing the parasite’s response to multiple antimalarial drugs. Several point mutations within pfmdr1 gene-such as N86Y, Y184F, S1034C, N1042D, and D1246Y- have been reported to modulate P. falciparum responsiveness to a broad spectrum of antimalarial agents, including lumefantrine, artemisinin derivatives, quinine, mefloquine, amodiaquine, and CQ [18–20]. Moreover, the pfmdr1 N86F184D1246 (NFD) haplotype has been associated with diminished sensitivity of P. falciparum to lumefantrine [21]. Additionally, the wild-type forms of pfcrt (K76) and pfmdr1 (N86) have been reported to contribute to reduced sensitivity to lumefantrine [22].

The P. falciparum multidrug resistance-associated protein 1 (pfmrp1) gene has been recognized as an important molecular marker associated with reduced responsiveness to multiple antimalarial drugs, notably CQ, mefloquine, lumefantrine and artemisinin derivatives [23–25]. Several point mutations in this gene—particularly at codons 191, 437, 876, 1390, and 1466—have been reported to correlate with altered drug susceptibility profiles [26, 27]. Previous studies have indicated that the H191Y and S437A mutations in pfmrp1 correlate with increased CQ IC₅₀ values [28]. In addition, the F1390I mutation has been shown to influence the parasite’s in vitro response to artesunate [25].

In 2022, Iran witnessed a re-emergence of indigenous malaria, reporting 1,439 cases after four consecutive years without local transmission [29]. Factors such as uncontrolled cross-border migration, recent flooding, and the consequent rise in malaria cases in neighboring Pakistan have contributed to the resurgence of the disease in Iran in recent years [29]. In Iran, CQ resistance reached critical levels, with the pfcrt K76T mutation detected in approximately 98% of P. falciparum isolates between 2003 and 2005 [30]. Consequently, CQ was officially removed from the national treatment protocol in 2007 and replaced with ACTs. The initial treatment regimen consisted of artesunate combined with sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine (SP); however, in 2022, artemether–lumefantrine (AL) was adopted as the first-line therapeutic regimen for P. falciparum [29]. Prolonged use of ACTs may have exerted selective pressure, favoring the emergence of resistant P. falciparum parasites. Consequently, routine molecular surveillance of established resistance markers is critical as an early warning measure to monitor the appearance and dissemination of drug resistance. In this study, known antimalarial drug resistance-associated mutations in the pfcrt, pfmdr1, and pfmrp1 genes were examined in P. falciparum isolates from southeastern Iran and subsequently compared with data obtained from the same region a decade earlier [27].

Methods

Study site and sample collection

This study was conducted in Chabahar District, Sistan and Baluchestan Province, southeastern Iran (25° 17′ 31″ N, 60° 38′ 35″ E). The location of Chabahar within Iran is shown in Fig. 1. Chabahar remains one of the key malaria transmission foci in the region, characterized by cross-border population movements from Pakistan. This makes it a strategic sentinel site for monitoring the potential introduction of drug-resistant parasites. Malaria in this area shows seasonal and unstable transmission patterns, with both P. falciparum and P. vivax co-circulating. The study enrolled male and female participants aged 2–70 years, all confirmed to harbor P. falciparum mono-infection via microscopic evaluation of thick blood smears. During 2022–2023, a total of 100 finger-prick blood specimens were collected from individuals with uncomplicated malaria attending the Malaria Health Center under the Chabahar Public Health Department. Prior to sampling, written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their guardian. Patients diagnosed with malaria were managed according to national guidelines, primarily receiving AL therapy. Collected blood spots were transferred onto filter papers, allowed to air-dry, and stored in silica gel—containing sealed envelopes until shipment to the central laboratory at the Pasteur Institute of Iran. The study received ethical clearance from the Ethics Committee of the Islamic Azad University, Tehran Medical Sciences Branch (IR.IAU.PS.REC.1402.549).

Map of Iran showing the geographical location of the study site in Chabahar District, Sistan and Baluchestan Province

Parasite DNA extraction and species confirmation

Genomic DNA was obtained from dried blood spots applied on filter paper using the Parstous Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Iran) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. To verify P. falciparum infection in all specimens, nested PCR amplification of the 18S small subunit ribosomal RNA (ssrRNA) gene was carried out using species-specific primers, following established protocols [31].

Genotyping of pfcrt, pfmdr1 and pfmrp1 genes

Point mutations in the pfcrt gene (K76T) and in the pfmdr1 gene (N86Y, Y184F, S1034C, N1042D, and D1246Y) were examined using nested PCR followed by restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP), as described previously [27, 32]. For detection of the K76T mutation in pfcrt, PCR products were digested with ApoI (Fermentas, Vilnius, Lithuania) and the resulting fragments were resolved by gel electrophoresis. The N86Y mutation in pfmdr1 was identified through digestion with ApoI and NsiI (Fermentas, Vilnius, Lithuania; New England Biolab, Beverly, MA, USA). Mutations at codons 184, 1034, 1042, and 1246 were assessed using TasI, DdeI, AseI, and EcoRV, respectively. All enzymatic digestions were carried out in 20 µL reaction mixtures containing 10 µL of PCR product, following the manufacturers’ protocols (New England Biolab, Beverly, MA, USA; Fermentas, Vilnius, Lithuania; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Reactions yielding incomplete digestion were incubated overnight and repeated as necessary. Following enzymatic digestion, PCR amplicons were separated using 2–2.5% agarose or 2–3% metaphor agarose gels and examined under UV illumination.

Mutations in the pfmrp1 gene (H191Y, S437A, I876V, F1390I, and K1466R) were examined using PCR–RFLP, following previously established protocols [27]. Specifically, nested PCR amplification was employed to target positions 191, 437, and 1466, while conventional single-round PCR was used for positions 876 and 1390. The resulting amplicons were digested with restriction enzymes selected for each mutation: NsiI for H191Y, BsaAI for S437A, BsmAI for K1466R, RsaI for I876V, and DraI for F1390I [27]. All enzymatic reactions were performed according to the manufacturers’ guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Data entry and analysis were performed using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS (version 25). The reliability of the estimated parameters was assessed by calculating 95% confidence intervals, indicating the probability that the true value falls within the reported range. Statistical comparisons between the two study periods were performed using the Chi-square (χ2) or Fisher's exact test, as applicable, on the original genotype counts (wild-type, mutant, mixed). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Frequency of pfcrt, pfmdr1 and pfmrp1 mutations

In this study, all 100 collected samples were confirmed to harbor P. falciparum mono-infection by nested PCR. Among these, 80 isolates were successfully genotyped for point mutations in the pfcrt, pfmdr1, and pfmrp1 genes using PCR–RFLP analysis. Molecular data revealed a 95% prevalence (76/80) of the pfcrt K76T mutation, with no statistically significant difference observed between the previous (2007–2010) and current (2022–2023) studies (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S1). Of the five pfmdr1 codons examined, three positions (1034, 1042 and 1246) exhibited 100% wild-type alleles. In contrast, mutant alleles at codons N86Y and Y184F were detected in 3.75% (3/80) and 51.25% (41/80) of isolates in the current study (2022–2023), compared to 46% and 2% in the previous study (2007–2010), respectively (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, mixed infections (simultaneous presence of wild-type and mutant alleles) at codon Y184F were identified in 1.25% (1/80) of samples in the current study (Supplementary Table S1). Comparative analysis of N86Y and Y184F mutations between the previous (2007–2010) and current (2022–2023) studies revealed a significant increase in the frequency of the wild-type allele for N86Y and the mutant allele for Y184F (P < 0.0001, Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S1).

In the previous study period (2007–2010), mutant alleles at codons H191Y, S437A, I876V, and F1390I were detected in 76.5%, 69.5%, 64.5%, and 17% of isolates, respectively. No mutation was detected at codon K1466R (Supplementary Table S2). In the current study period (2022–2023), the frequency of mutant alleles decreased for H191Y (68.75%), while it increased for S437A (85%), I876V (78.75%), and F1390I (46.25%). K1466R remained mutation-free. Mixed infections were observed at H191Y (10%), I876V (6.25%), and F1390I (2.5%) in the current study (Supplementary Table S2). Comparative analysis between the two periods revealed that the differences in mutant allele frequencies for all four positions were statistically significant (P < 0.01 for all; Fig. 2; Supplementary Tables S2).

Frequency distribution of pfcrt, pfmdr1 and pfmrp1 haplotypes

In this study, haplotype analysis of the pfmdr1 gene revealed 5 distinct variants compared to 6 different haplotypes in the previous study [27]. The most prevalent haplotype for pfmdr1 in the previous study was the wild-type (N86Y184S1034N1042D1246) with 50% frequency. However, the most prevalent haplotype in the current study was a single-mutation haplotype (N86F184S1034N1042D1246), accounting for 50% (40/80) of the isolates, while the wild-type haplotype (N86Y184S1034N1042D1246) was detected in 45% (36/80) of samples (Supplementary Table S3). Regarding the pfmrp1 gene, the haplotype Y191A437V876F1390K1466 was observed with the highest frequency (36.5%) in the previous study [27]. In the current study, haplotype analysis of the pfmrp1 gene detected 20 different haplotypes among the analyzed isolates. The Y191A437V876I1390K1466 haplotype exhibited the highest frequency, observed in 35% (28/80) of samples (Supplementary Table S3). Combined haplotype analysis of pfcrt, pfmdr1, and pfmrp1 across all examined samples revealed 35 unique haplotypes. Among these, the T76N86Y184S1034N1042D1246Y191A437V876I1390K1466 (23.75%; 19/80) and the T76N86F184S1034N1042D1246Y191A437V876F1390K1466 haplotypes (13.75%; 11/80) were the most frequently observed variants (Supplementary Table S3). Comparative analysis of pfcrt, pfmdr1, and pfmrp1 between the two study periods revealed that the haplotype T76N86Y184S1043N1042D1246Y191A437V876F1390K1466 decreased from 23.5% (2007–2010) to 3.75% (2022–2023). In contrast, the haplotype T76N86Y184S1043N1042D1246Y191A437V876I1390K1466 increased from 7 to 23.75% over the same period (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Since 2007, Iran has implemented ACTs for the treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria, following the withdrawal of CQ due to widespread resistance. However, the recent resurgence of malaria cases in Iran [29] has underscored the urgent need for robust control strategies, particularly the continuous surveillance of antimalarial drug resistance and associated genetic markers. Despite the critical role of molecular monitoring in guiding treatment policies, no comprehensive study on P. falciparum resistance markers has been conducted in Iran for nearly a decade. Addressing this gap, the present study investigates the current prevalence of resistance-associated mutations in three key genes, pfcrt, pfmdr1, and pfmrp1, providing essential insights into the evolving genetic landscape of P. falciparum in the region.

Although CQ has been officially removed from malaria treatment guidelines in Iran since 2007, our results indicate that the pfcrt K76T mutation remains highly prevalent in P. falciparum isolates. No significant difference was observed in the frequency of pfcrt K76T allele between the current study and our previous investigation [27]. This finding supports those of previous research conducted in Pakistan [33], Yemen [34, 35] and South American countries [36–38] that have reported a fixation of the mutant allele of pfcrt K76T following CQ withdrawal. On the other hand, the high level of mutation at position 76 of pfcrt in the present study is in contrast to its decline after CQ withdrawal from Saudi Arabia [14] and African countries [13, 39, 40]. The observed variability in the reversal of CQ resistance mutations could be attributed to regional differences in antimalarial drug pressure [16]. Therefore, the sustained presence of pfcrt K76T mutation in Iran may reflect localized CQ pressure used to treat P. vivax infections that is the dominant Plasmodium species in this region. This finding is line with other studies in which high level of pfcrt K76T mutation is attributed to P. vivax co-endemicity in the studied population [33, 41].

The results of the present study revealed that 3.75% and 51.25% of the P. falciparum isolates carried the pfmdr1 N86Y and Y184F mutations, respectively. These findings indicate a significant increase in the prevalence of the wild-type N86 and mutant 184F alleles, both of which have been associated with reduced sensitivity to lumefantrine, compared to previous data reported from Iran [27]. These observations are consistent with reports from Yemen [35] and Saudi Arabia [42]. No mutations were detected at codons S1034C, N1042D, and D1246Y in the Iranian clinical isolates, which is in agreement with our previous findings [27]. Similarly, the absence of mutations at these positions has also been reported in studies from Saudi Arabia [42].

The most prevalent pfmdr1 haplotype identified in this study was the N86F184S1034N1042D1246 haplotype, detected in approximately 50% of the examined isolates. Notably, the core pfmdr1 N86F184D1246 haplotype pattern observed in our isolates is consistent with previous reports from Yemen [35], Saudi Arabia [42], and several African countries [21, 41, 43, 44]. Multiple studies conducted in Africa have demonstrated positive selection for the wild-type pfcrt K76 allele together with the pfmdr1 N86F184D1246 haplotype, a pattern largely attributed to the widespread use of AL [22, 45–50]. Parasites harboring the pfmdr1 N86F184D1246 haplotype have been linked to decreased susceptibility to lumefantrine [21]. Although AL was officially adopted as first-line treatment in Iran only in 2023, the observed increase in the prevalence of the pfmdr1 N86F184D1246 haplotype—despite the continued dominance of the mutant pfcrt 76T allele—raises concern regarding potential future selection under AL drug pressure. In parallel, our recent assessment of pfk13 polymorphisms in Iranian P. falciparum isolates showed no mutations associated with artemisinin resistance [51], indicating that AL remains clinically effective in the study area. Nevertheless, routine molecular surveillance of resistance-associated genetic markers is strongly recommended to enable early detection of emerging lumefantrine-tolerant or resistant P. falciparum strains.

Mutations in the pfmrp1 gene, which encodes an ATP-binding cassette transporter, have been implicated in modulating parasite susceptibility to multiple antimalarial drugs, although their precise role in ACT resistance remains incompletely understood [23, 26, 52]. In the present study, the prevalence of the S437A, I876V, and F1390I mutations was higher than that reported in earlier surveys from Iran [27], a pattern consistent with observations from other endemic settings where increased diversity at the pfmrp1 locus has been reported following ACT deployment [25, 53]. Polymorphisms at codons I876V and F1390I have been associated with altered in vitro responses to AL and other ACT components; however, their contribution to clinical resistance remains uncertain and appears to be context dependent [25, 26]. In addition, the persistence of the wild-type K1466 allele, observed both in this study and in previous Iranian isolates, has previously been linked to reduced susceptibility to SP [23], supporting the rationale for the national shift from artesunate–SP to AL. Overall, while the increasing frequency of mutant pfmrp1 alleles warrants attention, the available evidence does not indicate an immediate risk of reduced AL efficacy in the studied population, underscoring the need for continued molecular surveillance.

This study has several limitations. First, the method used for genotyping (PCR–RFLP) can only detect known resistance-associated mutations in the pfcrt, pfmdr1, and pfmrp1 genes. While this technique is reliable for screening specific point mutations, it cannot identify novel or rare variants. Therefore, the absence of mutations in this study does not rule out the presence of unknown resistance alleles. Second, the sample size was relatively small. Third, additional molecular markers potentially contributing to AL resistance, such as pfmdr1 copy number variations, were not evaluated. Fourth, the cross-sectional design did not allow assessment of clinical treatment outcomes or in vitro drug susceptibility. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that, to date, no AL-related treatment failures have been reported in P. falciparum cases in Iran. Future studies incorporating longitudinal clinical follow-up combined with expanded molecular surveillance (including Sanger or whole-genome sequencing) will be critical for enhancing national capacity to monitor ACT resistance and for informing malaria treatment policies in the country.

Conclusions

The current study demonstrated the fixation of the pfcrt K76T mutant allele in Iranian P. falciparum isolates, despite the withdrawal of CQ from the national malaria treatment policy. This persistence may be partly attributable to the co-endemicity of P. vivax in the study area. The increasing prevalence of the N86F184S1034N1042D1246 pfmdr1 haplotype is of concern, given its reported association with reduced susceptibility to lumefantrine. Although mutant pfmrp1 alleles were detected at increasing frequencies, the available evidence does not indicate an immediate risk of reduced AL efficacy in the studied population. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of informed antimalarial drug selection and sustained molecular surveillance of ACTs in the region. Continued and large-scale monitoring of resistance-associated genetic markers is therefore warranted to guide treatment policies and to support the long-term effectiveness of the national malaria elimination program.
