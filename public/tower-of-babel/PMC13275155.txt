“No Man’s Land”: Addressing the Upper Inner Quadrant in Oncoplastic Breast Surgery: A Review of Contemporary Techniques
Authors: Alizah P. Hashmi, Fahad H. Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Partial breast reconstruction options following breast-conservation surgery are dictated by multiple factors, including the volume and composition of tissue removed, the size and ptosis of the premorbid and contralateral breast, and tumor location. Defects of the upper inner quadrant (UIQ)—referred to as “no man’s land”—are notoriously difficult because of inadequate residual parenchyma, visible scarring, and limited locoregional flap options. This review aims to collate historical and contemporary oncoplastic techniques for the UIQ, including their indications, limitations, and documented outcomes.Methods:An extensive online database search (PubMed, Google Scholar, Cochrane) was conducted using relevant prompts to identify articles that discussed reconstruction of the UIQ. The final yield was 23 published, digitally available articles, of which 14 were original case series.Results:Of the 14 original series, patient data were extracted and tabulated. The remaining expert commentaries and narrative reviews were also referenced. The most common techniques were inferior pedicle–based reductions and round-block mastopexies. Most of these cohorts were small—the largest included 46 patients who underwent inferior pedicle mammoplasty. Complications and cosmetic outcomes were widely varied and inconsistently reported across studies.Conclusions:Important considerations for UIQ reconstruction include the size and form of the premorbid breast, patient expectations, any prior breast procedures, the timelines of adjuvant treatment, and resection volume. Reconstruction options include level 1 OPS in select cases; level 2 procedures (batwing, round block, matrix rotation techniques, and therapeutic mammoplasties); and finally, regional volume replacement techniques (including variations of the latissimus dorsi flap, perforator-based flaps, and omental flap reconstruction).

Takeaways

Question: What are the current options for upper inner quadrant defects after breast-conserving surgery, and how should they be selected?

Findings: Little focused literature exists on this topic—14 small series were included in this review. Outcomes data were inconsistently reported. The most commonly used techniques were therapeutic mammoplasty and modified round block mastopexy. Complication rates were less than 10% in all studies.

Meaning: Upper inner quadrant reconstruction requires consideration of patient expectations, tumor-related factors (resection volume, skin involvement), and native breast characteristics (size, ptosis, density); an algorithm for appropriate reconstructive option selection is provided.

INTRODUCTION

Since the end of the last century, oncoplastic techniques for postextirpative defects in breast-conserving surgery (BCS) have gained enormous momentum. The tenet is wide local resection with adequate margins while reconstructing the breast at the same time, so as not to compromise cosmesis. General consensus classifies oncoplastic approaches into volume displacement, which involves redistributing the resection volume within the remaining breast tissue (level 1 [<20%] or 2 [20%-50%]), and volume replacement, which uses autologous flaps or implants to reconstruct the defect.1

Several studies have shown comparable oncological outcomes between BCS and total mastectomy, with superior psychosocial outcomes associated with the former.2 However, BCS is not without complications, which have critical implications for reconstructive surgeons. BCS mandates postoperative radiation, with attendant risks of tissue fibrosis and wound-healing complications. This also necessitates timely healing to avoid delays in adjuvant therapy. Additionally, some authors have reported re-excision rates following positive margins on definitive histopathology as high as 18% in BCS.3

Breast reconstruction options are dictated by multiple factors, including the volume of tissue removed, the size and ptosis of the premorbid and contralateral breast, and tumor location. In 2012, Clough et al4 published an enduring quadrant-by-quadrant algorithm to facilitate this reconstructive selection. However, defects of the upper inner quadrant (UIQ), especially those farther from the nipple–areolar complex (NAC) and involving skin, remain challenging because of limited residual breast tissue, visible incisions, difficulty mobilizing the remaining breast parenchyma, and extremely limited regional flap options.5 Grisotti dubbed this area “no man’s land.” Whether the defect is full-thickness or involving the integument or adipoglandular tissue, inadequate volume restoration will lead to unsightly craniomedial displacement of the NAC. In aggressive medial resections, the sternum may be exposed postablation and will require durable coverage that can withstand radiation therapy. This is a review of the existing literature on the reconstructive approaches to the UIQ, aiming to collate the indications, outcomes, and complications of available options as reported worldwide.

METHODS

A search of PubMed, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library was conducted using the keywords “breast upper inner quadrant,” “breast upper medial quadrant,” “breast reconstruction upper pole,” “breast conservation oncoplasty,” and “cranio-medial breast reconstruction.” Boolean operators (OR, AND) were used to ensure a broad search strategy. All articles from any time frame were included if they discussed reconstruction of the UIQ after BCS, either as the primary research question or if they discussed a reconstructive technique in general, but the text confirmed it had also been used for UIQ defects. Articles from the initial search were screened for eligibility through abstract review, and the reference lists of included studies were scanned for additional relevant publications. From those shortlisted studies, original patient series were included for data extraction and tabulation (Fig. 1).

RESULTS

The initial search yielded 15 articles, all of which were deemed eligible on abstract review; 2 were excluded because full text was unavailable online. After scanning references, 10 additional articles were included. This produced a final set of 23 articles. Fourteen of these were original series; their indications, techniques, and outcomes were tabulated (Table 1). The remaining expert commentaries and narrative reviews were also referenced due to the scarcity of literature on this topic.

From the 14 case series, 7 did not report data for the UIQ separately. Tunc et al5 included both upper and lower inner quadrant lesions; however, the latter were a very small fraction of their cases, so their results were also tabulated. Clinicopathological (tumor size, resected weight) and outcomes (cosmetic outcome, margin positivity, and miscellaneous complications) data were compiled. Most of these were reported inconsistently, highlighting the need for more objective reporting of oncoplastic techniques in the UIQ. Certain outcome measures, such as nipple sensation, although often emphasized in instructional surgical texts, have not been documented. The most common techniques were inferior pedicle-based reductions and round-block mastopexies.

These cohorts are small, with the largest comprising 46 patients who underwent inferior pedicle mammoplasty. Most studies are confined to T2 lesions or smaller. Specific mention has occasionally been made of premorbid breast characteristics (eg, size, density). Cosmetic outcomes, when measured, are usually assessed subjectively by the patient and/or surgeon and vary widely between studies and techniques.

DISCUSSION

Results are discussed in a systematic fashion—for small (20%), moderate (20%–50%), and large volume defects (>50%), with the primary lesion being in the UIQ.

Resection Volumes Less Than 20%

Level 1 oncoplastic surgery (OPS) may be appropriate for small lesions (<20%) of the UIQ. This must conform to the cardinal principles of Clough: appropriate skin incision, biplanar undermining to facilitate fusiform glandular excision and reapproximation, and careful NAC recentralization. Only very early-stage lesions in the setting of preexisting macromastia qualify. The risk of fat necrosis is higher with undermining when breast composition is predominantly fatty.

Resection Volumes of 20%-50%

Most defects greater than 20% will qualify for level 2 OPS to prevent postextirpative deformity. The earliest technique in this quadrant was proposed by Anderson et al18: a “batwing” excision pattern using the standard keyhole incision with triangular, angled extensions bilaterally. The farther the lesion is from the NAC, the more unsuitable this technique becomes. This approach also leads to cephalad shifting of the nipple and asymmetry. Both batwing and its single-limbed variant (“hemi-batwing”) produce a conspicuous scar over the medial breast.

Modified round block techniques have been reported widely for small T1–T2 tumors of the UIQ, but these only produce acceptable results in small- to moderate-sized breasts.10 Chen10 initially described this for lesions 2–6 cm from the nipple; however, subsequent series have been reported for more distant lesions (up to 10 cm), albeit then, dissection and flap elevation are more difficult. Zaha et al12 described a technique best suited for patients with small breasts with peripherally located tumors; they omitted de-epithelialization of the periareolar skin and provided operative access with extensive subcutaneous dissection of the entire breast and application of a wound retractor. However, as the NAC only survives on the vertical fourth and fifth intercostal perforators through underlying breast tissue, this cannot be used for lesions that have spread to tissue directly below the NAC.12 If the rings are concentric, this should not elevate the nipple; however, it may lead to nipple denervation secondary to a full-thickness circumareolar incision.

For small paracentral lesions (12 o’clock to 1 o’clock), the crescent mastopexy may be used, which also excises the skin overlying the tumor (oncologically beneficial in view of a superficial margin). Lesions more lateral than this are not candidates for this approach.19 It is more suited to larger areolas and elevates the nipple.

Another technique is matrix rotation mammoplasty, with modifications according to NAC-to-lesion distance and relocation of the scar. Lin et al9 reported their experience for tumors less than 5 cm in small- to medium-sized breasts. They divided their cohort into those that underwent periareolar de-epithelialization (“donut” group) and those with matrix rotation only (“nondonut” group). Operative technique involves an inverted triangle-shaped en bloc resection that includes tumor, skin, and a 1-cm macroscopic margin, followed by a concave incision from the tumor site along the lateral border of the breast to the midaxillary line. An additional triangular-shaped resection is made in the axilla to facilitate nodal dissection and later rotation flap advancement. For tumors less than 3 cm from the NAC, the tumor is removed via periareolar de-epithelialization and rectangular-shaped resection (“donut” method). Patient-reported outcomes via a postradiation questionnaire showed 88% satisfaction with cosmetic results. The approach theoretically obviates the need for contralateral surgery but leaves a long visible scar. A modification that involves a de-epithelialized triangle at the 12 o’clock position (instead of directly overlying the tumor) offsets the scar from the visible breast line while following the same dermoglandular matrix rotation principle.6 The incised axillary triangle provides access for nodal dissection.

Larger, pendulous breasts may benefit from therapeutic mammoplasty; lesions of this quadrant are notoriously outside the classical Wise and vertical reduction patterns. The Wise pattern incision allows wider access. Modifications exist for appropriately located tumors, including those that allow resection of skin. Most are inferior- or lateral pedicle-based, as these are usually preserved in UIQ tumor resection; however, based on what tissue is left after the cancer operation, any pedicle may be used. Filling of the defect is achieved by either extending the nipple pedicle or creating a secondary pedicle (carrying tissue that is normally excised) and moving it into the defect.20 A portion of skin may be left on the secondary pedicle to resurface a skin defect.

Elgammel et al21 described such an inferior-based mammoplasty coupled with UIQ quadrantectomy, with patient satisfaction exceeding 80%. In their series, Cutress et al11 described a case of UIQ tumor resection with skin excision, in which a triangle of skin normally preserved as part of the Wise pattern is “traded” for the same amount of skin from the ipsilateral half of the inframammary fold. The scar from the Wise limb on this side ultimately lies higher on the breast. This is only practical for lesions that are close to the transverse internipple axis (and so the lateral limbs of the Wise pattern).

Lateral pedicle-based techniques have also been used,13 in which the defect is filled with the parenchyma from the lower inner pole and the NAC rotated into a new position. Lateral pedicles can be unreliable in extensive axillary disease, where the lateral thoracic artery may be damaged during dissection. Mammoplasties enhance oncological safety because the excess tissue provides a wider histopathological margin. They often require synchronous or metachronous contralateral symmetrization.

Resection Volumes Greater Than 50%

For larger resections (>50%), volume replacement becomes necessary to restore satisfactory cosmesis. It may also be indicated in patients who have moderate to large ptotic breasts who prefer unilateral surgery and are otherwise satisfied with their existing breast size.

Traditionally, the latissimus dorsi (LD) flap, harvested as muscle only, as part of the muscle (“mini-LD”22), or with a skin paddle, has been a robust option. But donor site complications—seroma and weakened arm adduction—are not infrequent. Additionally, even with a proximally based harvest in which the muscle is divided very distally at its attachment to the posterior iliac spine, in petite patients, it may not reach the most medial portion of the UIQ without tension.

Chest wall–based perforator flaps such as the lateral intercostal artery perforator flap (LICAP) and anterior intercostal artery perforator flap have similar limitations in terms of inadequate reach. At times, the anterior intercostal artery perforator flap, with its perforator(s) completely skeletonized, may be used to resurface small skin-involving defects of the UIQ. The maximal width of these flaps is limited by the laxity of the subcostal skin. Perforator flaps based on the internal mammary axis have been reported as a reliable fasciocutaneous volume-replacement option, but the volume they provide is small (mean 46.5 g in the series by Huizum et al.15). Dog-ear deformity at the donor site can be prevented by incising the flap border medially and harvesting it as a true propeller flap. Hamdi et al17 have reported successfully using the thoracodorsal artery perforator flap (TDAP) in all quadrants. The ipsilateral pectoralis major muscle flap has also been used7 to replace UIQ volume, with the muscle elevated on the pectoral branch of the thoracoacromial pedicle via the lumpectomy incision, folded onto itself, and sutured into the defect.

The last resort option is advancing the contralateral pectoralis major. This primarily resurfaces the sternal defect and does not provide volume or contour to the UIQ. It will also contort the valley between the breasts and is not aesthetically satisfactory. As a salvage option for advanced disease, it may be used with a skin graft covering the muscle or with a freestyle propeller flap based on a lower parasternal perforator of the ipsilateral internal mammary artery.

Hamdi et al23 reported the use of the superficial epigastric artery perforator flap for partial breast defects, but in their anatomical considerations, they acknowledge that the flap’s arc of rotation usually reaches only the lower half of the anterior chest wall, and that tip necrosis remains a major complication.

It follows that there are few reliable options for volume replacement in UIQ defects other than the pedicled LD, which may be harvested in an “extended” fashion to provide more adipofascial volume as needed. If there is no defect of the integument and the volume required is small, the TDAP may be de-epithelialized and inset into the postablative cavity.

Zaha16 reported using the pedicled omental flap (OF) for volume replacement in excisions greater than 20%. The greater OF was harvested laparoscopically, pedicled on the right gastroepiploic arcade, and tunneled in a subglandular fashion via an additional inframammary incision to the UIQ recipient site. To prevent the unpleasant bulge that such tunneling may create, the pedicle was appropriately defatted.

Two patients underwent partial flap necrosis, with around half of the interpolated volume lost. Patients with risk factors for abdominal surgery morbidity, such as previous laparotomies and body mass index greater than 35 kg/m2, were excluded. Encouragingly, more than 80% of patients scored excellent or good outcomes on both subjective panel assessment and 2-dimensional computer software analysis. The OF proved resistant to radiation-induced volume atrophy, which is seen with muscle flaps.

The obvious advantages of the OF are a comfortable reach and minimal donor site scarring. Because omental fat is soft and the pedicle is long, it can be used to fill any irregularly shaped defect without contour anomalies. A major downside is the inability to preoperatively estimate the volume available for harvest. A very large OF will require a more conspicuous medial inframammary extraction incision and will be at increased risk for pedicle compression in the tunnel.

An umbrella option for immediate volume replacement is fat grafting, which can be used for defects of any size. It has shown good outcomes for small to moderate defects, either as a standalone procedure or in conjunction with local flaps. In a technique described by Har-Shai et al,14 liposuction was performed in the same operation in which 120% of the lumpectomy specimen weight was aspirated. Fat grafting was performed in 3 dimensions: the plane adjacent to the pectoralis, the breast parenchyma, and the subcutaneous tissue. Only 1 patient in their cohort had a UIQ lesion; however, overall patient satisfaction was good (average BREAST-Q score 74.5). The caveat is the unpredictability of graft survival. Not only partial volume loss lead to suboptimal cosmetic outcome and the need for repeated regrafting, but fat necrosis may also lead to oil cysts and calcifications.24 These can form anxiety-inducing palpable nodules and interfere with breast imaging and surveillance.

A foremost consideration in all partial breast reconstruction is the nonzero risk (up to 18%) of positive margins and the need for re-excision and secondary reconstruction, even though OPS has established lower margin positivity than simple lumpectomy.25 Operative implications are as follows: (1) volume displacement techniques rearrange breast tissue and make the index radial margins challenging to identify; (2) previous reconstructions may need to be taken down in subsequent clearance operations; and (3) in a quadrant with a limited reconstructive toolbox, this may necessitate conversion to a skin-sparing mastectomy and total reconstruction. A constellation of a large tumor, uncertain margins, and a small breast with residual volume unlikely to permit a second BCS attempt makes committing to a skin-sparing mastectomy with total reconstruction in the primary operation a reasonable option.

Munhoz et al26 reported data on reoperative BCS. Depending on the operative scenario, options are re-excision of margins with no additional reconstruction necessary; secondary partial reconstruction (eg, previous mastopexy patients may undergo pedicled flaps or reduction mammoplasty); or conversion to skin-sparing mastectomy with total reconstruction (if insufficient volume remains or if the original tumor cavity cannot be identified).

Finally, BCS must be followed by radiation. This concerns the reconstructive surgeon in 2 ways: the patient must heal and be referred without delay (as flap necrosis or wound breakdown may prove oncologically detrimental), and the surgeon must be cognizant that the reconstruction will be adversely affected by radiation. The former may not be a statistically significant problem—studies found no difference in time to adjuvant therapy initiation between oncoplastic BCS and mastectomy (with immediate reconstruction).27

However, radiation effects must be anticipated. For reduction mammoplasties, this means accommodating additional shrinkage. The OF has been shown to be more resistant to radiation-related volume loss than muscle flaps, which are more resistant than perforator flaps. Extant data demonstrate good rates of perforator flap survival postradiation (72% in a recent report by Schmidt et al28 using anterior intercostal artery perforator flap and TDAP flaps, and 100% in a study in which postradiation follow-up was available for more than 500 chest wall perforator flaps29).

In view of immediate versus delayed reconstruction, same-setting reconstruction has clear advantages: oncoplastic BCS allows more tissue resection and wider margins,30 and reduction techniques lead to reduced breast size, which enables homogeneous radiation delivery at lower doses, thereby reducing late radiation complications,31 and minimizes psychological trauma from a deformed postoperative breast. Although delayed reconstruction allows time to confirm margins, scarring from the previous operation and radiation makes glandular rearrangement challenging; in this setting, a pedicled flap bringing in nonradiated vascularized tissue from elsewhere is a better choice. Here, the aesthetic outcome may be compromised by color mismatch, as the native radiated tissue may be dyspigmented.32 These are considerations pertinent to all partial breast reconstruction, including the UIQ.

Limitations

This review is limited by the paucity of literature on this topic and the small cohorts in the available studies. This is largely because breast cancer disproportionately occurs in the upper outer quadrant rather than the inner half of the breast. Some included studies are nonspecific to UIQ tumors. There are no standardized outcome measures between studies, and the parameters reported are inconsistent. There is insufficient data for formal analysis.

CONCLUSIONS

Reconstructive nuances need to be considered and discussed with the patient before surgery for UIQ tumors. These include the size and form of the premorbid breast, patient expectations, any previous breast procedures, the nature of the primary disease, adjuvant therapy, and the volume and composite nature of the defect. A reconstructive option should be chosen accordingly (Fig. 2).
