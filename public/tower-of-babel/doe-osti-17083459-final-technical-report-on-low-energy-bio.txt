Executive Summary
Introduction and Significance
Thousands of water resource recovery facilities (WRRFs) across the United States face
mounting pressure to meet stringent nitrogen discharge limits while also managing rising energy
costs and, often, land constraints. This report presents findings from a pilot-scale demonstration
of a novel train of treatment technologies to assess its suitability as a low-cost upgrade option
for WRRFs in need of nitrogen removal. The novel treatment train was termed the
“biomembrane filtration system” and integrates two retrofittable technologies, the membrane
aerated biofilm reactor (MABR) and the recirculating dynamic membrane bioreactor (R-
DMBR). During pilot-scale testing at the Ann Arbor, MI WRRF, the system demonstrated that
it can achieve nitrogen removal targets with substantially lower energy inputs and cost than
existing upgrade alternatives, providing a compelling technology option for utilities seeking
intensification technology to increase their nitrogen removal capacity. Techno economic
analysis (TEA) of the biomembrane filtration system demonstrated average cost and energy
reductions of 64% and 67% relative to the status quo modified Ludzack-Ettinger (MLE)
activated sludge process, across varying conditions in which the MLE achieved nitrogen
compliance.

Key Performance Outcomes from Pilot Operation
From April to August 2024, the pilot system was fed primary effluent from the Ann Arbor
WRRF, which initially passed through the MABR for nitrification and subsequently through the
R-DMBR for denitrification and filtration, with some recycle flow from the R-DMBR returning
to the MABR. The system achieved the following metrics during this period:
 ● 77% total nitrogen (TN) removal: Reduced from an average of 40.8 mg N/L in the
 primary effluent to an average of 9.3 mg N/L in the final effluent
 ● 76% ammonium removal: Reduced from an average of 20.8 mg N/L in the primary
 effluent to an average of 5.0 mg N/L in the final effluent
 ● Achieved an average nitrate concentration in the final effluent of 1.9 mg N/L
 ● 89% total suspended solids (TSS) removal: Reduced from an average of 369.5 mg/L
 in the R-DMBR bulk to an average of 40.4 mg/L in the final effluent

Soluble chemical oxygen demand (sCOD) concentrations remained relatively stable across
treatment stages in the MABR and R-DMBR, suggesting that hydrolysis of particulate COD and
endogenous respiration in the R-DMBR biofilms released additional biodegradable carbon to
support denitrification. This key finding highlights the strategy’s robustness in achieving
denitrification without external chemical (carbon) dosing.

Energy Efficiency and Process Control
The biomembrane filtration process train was modeled in the SIMBA# modeling software in
collaboration with inCTRL Solutions. Two process control strategies were evaluated in

 8

SIMBA# to assess the potential for further reducing energy consumption and improving
treatment performance for this process train. The process control findings will facilitate future
optimization of nitrogen removal performance and energy efficiency when designing to
implement the biomembrane filtration system at a commercial scale:

 ● Ammonia-based aeration control (ABAC) in the MABR could achieve a 53.5%
 reduction in specific nitrogen removal energy demand (kWh/kg N removed)

 ● Implementation of recirculation from the DMBR effluent back to the MABR yields
 better nitrogen removal with a marginal energy penalty, with a 50% recycle ratio being
 optimal for energy-efficient nitrogen removal

Key Performance Outcomes from Techno-Economic Analysis
The techno-economic analysis compared the proposed MABR-DMBR treatment train to three
benchmark, conventional configurations.
 ● Across the evaluated conditions, the MABR-DMBR was the most cost-effective and
 energy-efficient option, achieving the project target of reducing unit treatment cost and
 energy demand by more than 25% relative to activated sludge and MBR benchmarks.
 ● Amongst nitrogen-compliant scenario conditions, the MABR-DMBR achieved average
 cost and energy reductions of 64% and 67% vs. the MLE benchmark; 56% and 72% vs.
 the MLE‑MBR benchmark; and 20% and 57% vs. the MABR‑CAS benchmark.
 ● Sensitivity results indicated that DMBR membrane flux and module cost are the
 dominant cost drivers for the MABR-DMBR configuration, highlighting the need for
 future work to validate achievable municipal-scale flux and installed module cost.

Conclusion and Recommendations
The biomembrane filtration system achieved good TN removal without the need for an external
electron donor, while requiring minimal energy for aeration, pumping, and fouling control.
Based on the pilot-scale demonstration, the following is recommended:

 ● Tight control over MABR nitrification performance is required to ensure that sufficient
 nitrate and biodegradable carbon are available for denitrification.
 ○ Monitoring oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) in the MABR and maintaining
 ORP between -150 to 0 mV is key for promoting nitrification and preserving
 biodegradable carbon.
 ● Acetate dosing should be available as a standby option for supplementing the electron
 donor for denitrification during potential periods of MABR over-aeration, when
 available biodegradable carbon in the wastewater is depleted.

 ● To achieve uniform biofilm coverage and maximize mesh utilization, the R-DMBR bulk
 mixing intensity should be high to prevent settling and stratification, and the permeate

 9

 vacuum pressure should be maintained at a value that ensures adequate flow across the
 entire module height. Periodic relaxation cycles can be used to maintain flux across the
 R-DMBR.

The system achieved these performance levels under a typical municipal wastewater carbon-to-
nitrogen ratio (C/N = 9.5) for TN removal, without the addition of an external electron donor.
Unlike conventional activated sludge configurations for nitrogen removal, where an anoxic tank
precedes the aeration tank and preserves biodegradable carbon for denitrification, the
biomembrane filtration process places the MABR zone upfront, eliminating downstream
aeration. This configuration consumes most of the influent electron donor (i.e., biodegradable
carbon) in the first MABR stage but enables biomass capture on the dynamic membrane in the
downstream R-DMBR, providing an internal reservoir of particulate organics that supports
endogenous respiration-driven denitrification. This study demonstrated this approach as a
robust strategy to achieve total nitrogen removal combined with solids removal from the DMBR
in the anoxic zone.

1. Introduction

1.1 Background and Motivation
Water resource recovery facilities (WRRFs) in the United States and worldwide face growing
pressure to meet increasingly stringent discharge regulations, particularly regarding nutrient
removal, to protect downstream water quality and comply with policies such as the EPA’s
NPDES Nutrient Permitting (US EPA, 2013). At the same time, urbanization and the expansion
of industrial and commercial sectors are increasing the nutrient loads entering WRRFs (Fowler
et al., 2015).
Conventional responses to these demands, such as building additional treatment infrastructure,
are often infeasible due to high capital costs, limited land availability, and permitting challenges.
This has led to a growing interest in process intensification strategies that enhance nutrient
removal capacity within existing footprints (Houweling & Daigger, 2019).

1.2 Limitations of Current Technologies (AS, MBR, MBBR)
The most widely implemented treatment process at U.S. WRRFs remains activated sludge (AS),
and the Modified Ludzack-Ettinger (MLE) configuration is the most used approach for
combined carbon and nitrogen removal through anoxic and aerobic zones (Grady Jr et al., 2011;
G. Liu & Wang, 2017). While the MLE process is established and can achieve an effluent of
moderate total nitrogen (TN) concentrations of 7-15 mg N /L (Zahreddine & Nepal, 2021), the
MLE process is energy-intensive, primarily due to high aeration demands, which can account
for more than 60% of a WRRF's electricity usage (Ghimire et al., 2021). Further, when
conventional AS systems are converted to the MLE configuration for nitrogen removal, the plant
loses capacity due to the conversion of a portion of the aerobic biological treatment tanks to
anoxic zones and the increase in solids retention time (SRT) required for more slowly-growing
microorganisms used for nitrogen removal (Houweling & Daigger, 2019).
Membrane bioreactor (MBR) intensification technology can integrate biological treatment with
membrane filtration for solid-liquid separation, achieving high effluent quality with significantly
lower effluent concentrations of total suspended solids (TSS) (<1 mg/L), TN (<5 mg/L), and
biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) (<5 mg/L) than those in AS systems (Banti et al., 2020;
Rahman et al., 2023). In addition, MBRs reduce physical footprint by eliminating the need for
a secondary clarifier for solids removal, and enable higher treatment capacities by operating at
a higher mixed liquor suspended solids concentration than AS systems (Banti et al., 2020). The
distinct advantages of MBRs have made the technology increasingly attractive for facilities
pursuing potable and non-potable water reuse, particularly where stricter effluent standards for
TN, BOD, and TSS must be met (Rahman et al., 2023). However, MBRs require substantially
higher energy consumption than AS systems, with increases ranging from 10% to 100%,
depending on specific design and operational characteristics (Krzeminski et al., 2012). This
elevated energy demand, driven primarily by the need to mitigate membrane fouling, translates
to higher operating costs (Banti et al., 2020), which must be weighed against the water quality
benefits when selecting treatment technologies.

 11

Another intensification technology, moving bed biofilm reactors (MBBRs), are often used as a
retrofit option. MBBRs enhance biomass retention using biocarriers and can be employed
relatively easily for enhanced organic and nitrogen removal (Collivignarelli et al., 2019; Madan
et al., 2022). While AS systems typically use fine bubble (2-5 mm) diffusers for energy
efficiency, MBBR systems often employ coarse-bubble (~50 mm) aeration to generate sufficient
turbulence and mixing to keep biomass carriers suspended (Sander et al., 2016). Since coarse-
bubble diffusion is less efficient at oxygen transfer than fine-bubble diffusion, MBBRs usually
require at least 10% higher energy input for mixing and aeration than that of the AS systems
(Sen et al., 2008). In contrast to MBRs, MBBRs require downstream solids removal using a
secondary clarifier.

Despite their advantages and growing use (MBBR, n.d.; MBR Global Capacity, 2019), both
MBR and MBBR technologies present substantial barriers in cost, energy consumption, and
operational complexity. AS systems, while relatively simple, are increasingly insufficient in
meeting the dual challenges of energy efficiency and effluent quality. These limitations
underscore the need for innovative intensification technologies to achieve high-level treatment
with reduced energy and lifecycle costs.

1.3 Project Objectives
In response to the industry’s needs, this study proposes a new treatment train, the biomembrane
filtration process, as a next-generation intensification approach capable of producing a low-
nitrogen effluent suitable for discharge while reducing energy use and cost, not only relative to
MBR and MBBR systems, but also in comparison to the MLE AS process.
The biomembrane filtration process employs two emerging technologies synergistically: the
membrane aerated biofilm reactor (MABR) for nitrification, and the recirculating dynamic
membrane bioreactor (R-DMBR) for denitrification and filtration. The MABR has been
commercialized and is the subject of numerous research articles (Peeters et al., 2017; He et al.,
2021; J. Li et al., 2023). It consists of one or more modules of gas-permeable hollow-fiber
membranes submerged in a bioreactor, which supports biofilm growth and delivers substrates
and oxygen to the biofilm in a counter-diffusional fashion. As oxygen is delivered directly to
the biofilm, there is no need to diffuse substantial oxygen into the bulk liquid of the biological
tank for aeration, thereby saving aeration energy by up to 70% compared to AS systems (He et
al., 2021).

The R-DMBR is a newer technology developed at the University of Michigan (Fairley-Wax et
al., 2022). This novel design uses a high surface area support structure wrapped with coarse-
pore size stainless steel meshes, which is submerged in a bioreactor to support biofilm
development. Wastewater is circulated through the system, flowing across the dynamic
membrane, where substrates are metabolized. The design enables wastewater to pass through
the dynamic membrane with a filtration resistance that is approximately one to two orders of
magnitude lower than that of the MBR, resulting in significantly lower energy demand (Ersahin

 12

et al., 2012). Unlike conventional biofilm systems that rely on diffusion, the R-DMBR delivers
biodegradable substrates to the biofilms via advective transport, enhancing treatment efficiency.
This process simultaneously achieves denitrification in a hybrid process that utilizes both
biofilms and suspended biomass, as well as solid-liquid separation, thanks to dynamic
membranes that effectively retain suspended solids and biomass during the filtration process.

Based on the low-energy demand observed for the MABR and low filtration resistance for the
R-DMBR, this project aimed to evaluate the biomembrane filtration process as a low-energy
alternative to conventional MBR and MBBR technologies for intensifying WRRFs for nitrogen
removal. By demonstrating its potential to upgrade existing AS facilities, this approach aims to
reduce both energy consumption and operational costs by more than 25% relative to existing
practices, while keeping the concentration of TN in treated effluent below 10 mg N/L.

2. System Design and Process Modeling
Milestones # 1, 2, 3, and 5

To design an effective biomembrane filtration system, the design basis was first established
through characterization of primary effluent from the Ann Arbor, MI water resource recovery
facility (WRRF), quantifying chemical oxygen demand (COD) and total nitrogen (TN)
concentrations to determine appropriate loading rates (Section 2.2). To evaluate treatment
efficacy and optimize system performance, the process modeling software SIMBA# was used
in collaboration with the project partner, InCTRL Solutions. The modeling effort evaluated key
operating parameters, including flow rate, hydraulic retention time, permeate recirculation ratio,
biofilm thickness, packing density, and membrane area, to refine the design of both the MABR
nitrification stage and the subsequent R-DMBR denitrification stage (Sections 2.2-2.3). Since
the R-DMBR is a novel customized module, a detailed design rationale is provided with an
illustration of its operation within a full-scale configuration, including internal flow patterns and
integration within a bioreactor tank (Section 2.4). This section also details the customized
MABR and R-DMBR tank design used for the pilot evaluation (Section 2.5), the pilot control
system design (Section 2.6), and the pilot MABR air system design (Section 2.7).

2.1 Overview of the Biomembrane Filtration System
Figure 1 presents a process flow diagram in which the first stage employs an MABR for
nitrification, followed by an R-DMBR for denitrification and filtration. This integrated system

Primary Effluent Characterization
Ten composite samples of primary effluent from the Ann Arbor, MI WRRF collected during
October to December 2021 were characterized for total and volatile suspended solids
(TSS/VSS), total chemical oxygen demand (tCOD), COD content after 1.5 µm pore size
filtration (filtered COD), COD content after flocculation and 1.5 µm pore size filtration (floc. &
filt. COD or soluble COD), carbonaceous biochemical oxygen demand (cBOD5), volatile fatty
acids (VFAs), total nitrogen (TN), total ammonia, and nitrate (NO3-). Note that total ammonia
concentrations refer to the sum of ammonia (NH3) and ammonium (NH4+) concentrations, but
are simply referred to as ammonium in the DOE report. At the pH of the pilot-scale
biomembrane filtration system, the predominant species is NH4+. All analyses were conducted
at the University of Michigan lab, except for cBOD5, which was performed by the City of Ann
Arbor’s Water Treatment Laboratory. The floc. and filt. COD and cBOD were expected to be
similar and were used to estimate the amount of readily biodegradable carbon available. The
measured values were similar to those reported in the literature for municipal wastewater
primary effluent, as shown in Table 1 (Barajas et al., 2002; Grady Jr et al., 2011), except that
VFA concentrations were higher than typical, as the Ann Arbor WRRF allows the primary
sludge to ferment at the bottom of the primary clarifiers to aid in bio-P removal. Figure 2 shows

MABR Nitrification Stage Design
The MABR nitrification stage was designed using the measured primary effluent characteristics.

R-DMBR Denitrification Stage Design
The initial design criteria for the R-DMBR are summarized in Table 3, which include the
proposed mesh surface area, the module’s packing density, the NO3- and COD loading rates,
and the HRT, which were subsequently adjusted to meet construction constraints. These values
were used as a design tool in the initial SIMBA# process model to ensure that the design criteria
yielded a desirable wastewater effluent quality of TN below 10 mg N/L. This process model
was used to predict the denitrification and simulate the filtration performance of the R-DMBR.
Since the filtration process using a dynamic membrane could not be directly modeled, a clarifier
was used with a specified effluent TSS value to represent the solids removal performance. The
full process model, including the MABR, DMBR denitrification, and DMBR filtration unit
processes, is illustrated in Figure 4.

The preliminary SIMBA# model used for design purposes showed that as the DMBR biofilm
packing density increases, the effluent NO3- concentration decreases, while the effluent NH4+
concentration increases gradually. This is because a higher packing density leads to a larger
biofilm growth area for denitrifying microorganisms that remove NO3- but also release NH4+ as

they decay. A packing density value of 150 m2/m3 (similar to the MABR packing density)
achieved the optimal combination of low effluent NH4+ and NO3- concentrations. The tank size
of the R-DMBR was planned to be the same as MABR, 0.19 m3, resulting in a mesh surface
area of about 29 m2. The NO3- and COD loading rates for the R-DMBR were calculated to be
1.3 g N/m2/d and 18.8 g COD/m2/d based on the first MABR nitrification stage model output.

2.3 Integrated Process Model for the Biomembrane Filtration Process
A process model of the proposed biomembrane filtration process is shown in Figure 4. For an
applied flow rate of 2.08 m3/day, the total kjeldahl nitrogen (TKN) loading rate was 1.7 g-
N/m2/day for the MABR. After the MABR, the tCOD loading rate and specific tBOD5 loading
rate to the R-DMBR were 30.6 g tCOD/m2/day and 5.8 g tBOD5/m2/day.
The MABR biofilm thickness was set to 0.6 mm after running the single-stage MABR model,
an optimized thickness that supplies NO3--N and BOD5 for downstream denitrification while
providing excellent NH4+-N removal, as shown by the modeling results in Figure 5. However,
precise control of MABR biofilm thickness remains challenging in practice, as thickness is
primarily regulated through manipulation of sparging air intensity and frequency (Cao et al.,
2023; He et al., 2025). The R-DMBR was modeled using a generic biofilm reactor with
modifications to reduce the liquid boundary layer thickness by two orders of magnitude relative
to the default value in SIMBA# to simulate a fully penetrated dynamic membrane biofilm. The
filtration process of the R-DMBR was initially modeled using a clarifier with an effluent TSS
of 20 mg/L, based on previous experimental work on DMBR filtration performance. The R-
DMBR biofilm thickness was set to 0.4 mm, resulting in 82% of the total biomass being in
biofilms and 18% being present as suspended biomass (2.3 g/L TSS). The recirculation ratio
(the percentage of the R-DMBR permeate recycled back to the MABR out of the total permeate
generated) was set to 80%, aiming to strike a balance between performance and pumping energy
demand.

 22

Figure 5. Ammonium, nitrate, and total BOD concentrations in MABR effluent at biofilm
thickness

The MABR model was tuned to promote the growth of autotrophic nitrifiers while limiting the
growth of both anoxic and aerobic heterotrophs. Under these conditions, high NH4+ conversion
can be achieved, as indicated by the decrease of 25.4 to 4.5 mg/L NH4+-N at a TKN loading rate
of 1.7 g-N/m2/day, shown in Figure 6. A small aerobic polishing zone could be added to remove
additional NH4+, as needed. Alternatively, further NH4+ removal can be achieved by
encouraging anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox) in the R-DMBR, although this wasn’t
explored in this work.

For an average flow rate of 2.1 m3/d, the preliminary process model predicted that the R-DMBR
can remove tBOD5 and NO3- substantially, achieving an effluent with 5.1 mg/L tBOD5, 9.6 mg/L
TN, and 0.1 mg/L NO3--N due to efficient denitrification and filtration.

Preliminary modeling results indicated that the biomembrane filtration system was a promising
alternative for nitrogen removal at WRRFs, capable of achieving the desired effluent
characteristics, which provided confidence to proceed with pilot testing.

2.5 Customized MABR and DMBR Tank Design
The MABR and R-DMBR modules were operated in the tank shown in Figure 9. The tank,
standing 8 feet tall with a 42” by 42” footprint, was purchased as a modular kit (VariTank,
ModuTank Inc.) and assembled on-site. After delivery, two vertical baffles were installed in the
two narrow outer compartments by UM CEE technicians, Justin Roelofs and Steve
Donajkowski, which housed the MABR and DMBR modules. The narrow design of the module
compartments aimed to mimic the fluid flow conditions found in cassette structures, where
modules are closely packed side by side. The MABR was designed to process a higher flow rate
than a single DMBR module could handle. The excess flow from the MABR was directed to a
floor drain when the DMBR tank level was full. While this would be resolved at full scale by
installing multiple DMBR modules, that approach was not feasible for the pilot-scale setup. The
MABR tank was hydraulically connected to a sensor tank, resulting in a total combined volume
of 422 L, and housed the MABR module with a total membrane surface area of 40 m2. Therefore,
the MABR specific surface area was 94.8 m2/m3. The DMBR tank had a working volume of 293
L and housed the DMBR module with a membrane surface area of 4.2 m2.

2.7 MABR Aeration System Design and Optimization
Initially, the system was designed to use one air compressor to supply process air, mixing air,
and scouring air, as shown in Figure 11. However, the resulting process air pressure, which used
a single air compressor to supply all air, was lower than Veolia’s recommended process air
pressure (~6 psi) and resulted in insufficient nitrification during the process optimization phase
(for details, see Section 3.3). Therefore, a second air compressor was added to separate the
process air line from the mixing and scouring line, as the original setup placed excessive demand
on a single air compressor and resulted in insufficient airflow for mixing and scouring. To
improve oxygen transfer in the MABR biofilm and prevent water vapor accumulation in the
membranes, a regenerative desiccant dryer was installed upstream of the air compressor in the
process air line, shown in Figure 12. Additional desiccant units were added at the downstream
lumen off-gas line.

3. Pilot System Implementation and Operations
Milestones #4 and 6

This section describes the commissioning, startup, and operation of the biomembrane filtration
pilot system at the Ann Arbor, MI WRRF from May 2023 to March 2025. The section details
the physical setup and instrumentation (Section 3.1), the analytical methods and quality control
procedures used to assess system performance (Section 3.2), and the five distinct operational
phases during the project period (Section 3.3). These phases include initial startup, process
optimization to address nitrification challenges (process optimization phase), a relatively
operationally stable period demonstrating good nitrogen removal (good nitrogen removal
phase), an acetate dosing experiment to evaluate electron donor limitation during positive ORP
(positive ORP and acetate dosing experiment phase), and a final unstable phase due to
mechanical disruptions (unstable phase).

3.2 Monitoring and Data Acquisition

Physiochemical Parameter Monitoring
Chemical Analytical Methods
The performance of the biomembrane filtration system was assessed by monitoring the NH4+,
NO3-, NO2-, TN, TSS, VSS, tCOD, and sCOD concentrations in grab samples of primary
effluent, MABR bulk, R-DMBR bulk, and R-DMBR permeate. The sampling frequency of the
grab samples was two to three times per week.

All samples were analyzed in triplicate or duplicate. Samples for NH4+, NO3-, and NO2- analyses
were filtered through EZFlowⓇ Membrane 0.45 μm nylon filters (Foxx Life Science,
Londonderry, NH, USA). Samples for sCOD were prepared by first filtering the sample through
a 1.5 µm glass fiber filter to obtain the filtrate, adding 1% v/v aluminum sulfate for coagulation
and flocculation, and finally collecting the resulting supernatant for analysis. TSS and VSS
concentrations were determined according to Standard Methods. COD was analyzed using the
Hach Method 8000 (USEPA1 Reactor Digestion Method). Ammonium was analyzed using
Hach Method 10031 (Salicylate Method). Nitrite was analyzed using Hach Method 10207
(Diazotization method). Nitrate was analyzed using a second derivative UV/visible spectroscopy
method.

Quality Assurance and Control (QA/QC)
To have confidence in the accuracy of the analytical measurements, quality control testing to
determine the quality of the standard curve (as indicated by the coefficient of determination,
R2), the limit of detection (LOD), and the limit of quantification (LOQ) was conducted for TN,
NH4+, NO3-, and NO2- analyses, shown in Table 4. LOQ and LOD were based on statistical
analyses by multiplying 3.3 and 10 by the standard deviation of the response and then dividing
by the slope of the calibration curve (Armbruster & Pry, 2008). The quality control efforts also
included comparing the reliability of two commercially available reagent kits (Hach and
Lovibond brands).

Online Sensors
In addition to conducting chemical analyses on grab samples, the YSI EXO2 Multi-Parameter
Water Quality Sonde probes were deployed to continuously monitor NH4+, NO3-, dissolved
oxygen (DO), oxidation reduction potential (ORP), pH, and temperature in the bulk liquid of the
MABR tank. Measurements of NH4⁺ and NO3⁻ were affected by substantial sensor drift despite
repeated calibration, in contrast to other parameters (ORP, pH, and temperature), which
remained stable. As a result, NH4+ and NO3- sensor data are excluded, and concentrations were
reported using chemical analyses of grab samples. These real-time measurements enabled off-
site system performance tracking and promptly adjusted aeration when needed. For instance,
ORP served as an indicator of aeration adequacy: values above zero mV signaled over-aeration,
while values below -150 mV indicated an anaerobic environment. Over-aeration can deplete
COD in the MABR tank, thereby limiting the electron donor supply for denitrification in the
downstream DMBR. Conversely, anaerobic conditions can inhibit nitrification by promoting

 33

FeS precipitation on the MABR biofilm, which impedes ammonium diffusion into the biofilm
and negatively affects nitrification.

3.3 Operational Phases
The pilot operational timeline is divided into five distinct phases: start-up, process optimization
phase, good nitrogen removal phase, positive ORP and acetate dosing experiment phase, and
unstable (mechanical disruption) phase. Each phase will be discussed in detail in the following
sections. During the various phases, ORP had a significant impact on treatment performance.
The pH and temperature changes were less variable than those of ORP, as shown in Appendix
Figures A1 and A2, respectively.

Start-up Phase (December 2023 to January 2024)
The system was seeded on December 15, 2023, by adding return activated sludge (RAS) into
each tank and filling the rest of the tank with primary effluent to achieve volatile suspended
solids targets of approximately 1166 mg/L in the MABR (based on a steady‐state SIMBA#
model) and 677 mg/L in the R-DMBR (set intentionally lower to prevent early mesh clogging).
Following inoculation, the tanks operated in closed “batch mode” for four days, during which
the connections between the MABR and R-DMBR were closed while the mixing systems for
each tank were in operation. The goal of batch operation was to promote biomass growth and
biofilm formation while preventing the washout of slow-growing microorganisms, such as
ammonia-oxidizing bacteria and nitrite-oxidizing bacteria. Thereafter, the system was switched
to continuous flow, with the MABR and DMBR arranged in series.

Process Optimization Phase (January 2024 to mid-April 2024)
Due to multiple operational challenges summarized in Figure 17, the MABR did not achieve
consistent nitrification during this phase. An air compressor failure on Day 26 caused oxygen
depletion and loss of nitrifying activity. Following compressor replacement, a pumping issue
reduced the HRT from the target 6 hours to 1-2 hours, limiting nitrification efficacy. Despite
resolving the HRT issue and reseeding with RAS, nitrification remained poor. Consultation with
Veolia identified the root cause: insufficient lumen feed air pressure (<6 psi) and water vapor
accumulation, which limited oxygen diffusion across the membrane. On Day 126 (April 16,
2024), the air pressure was increased to ~6 psi. To prevent moisture buildup and improve oxygen
transfer, regenerative desiccant dryers were installed upstream and downstream of the air
compressor. A second air compressor was added to separate the process and mixing/scouring
air lines, eliminating airflow limitations as described above (Figure 12).

Stable Phase (Mid-April 2024 to Mid-August 2024)
From April 16, 2024 (Day 123) to August 15, 2024 (Day 244), the system operated stably
without major mechanical issues such as pump or air compressor failures, tubing clogs, or valve

the MABR performance. During this period, the system achieved consistent ammonium and
nitrate conversion, along with effective total suspended solids removal, despite variable ORP
values shown in Figure 18. The detailed performance data of the stable operational period are
presented in Section 4.

Positive ORP + Acetate Dosing Phase (September 19, 2024, to September 30, 2024)
Over-aeration in the MABR can lead to excessive nitrification and carbon depletion, thereby
limiting the availability of electron donors for downstream denitrification. This phase explored
whether external carbon supplementation could restore effective denitrification performance
under these conditions.

The MABR mixing air valve was broken from August 14 to September 30. During this time,
the MABR scouring air was used to compensate for the mixing air by matching its frequency to
that of the original mixing air (10 s on and 50 s off, as shown in Figure 12). The resulting change
shifted the MABR bulk conditions from anoxic (-150 to 0 mV ORP) to aerobic (>0 mV ORP),
resulting in higher nitrate concentration in the MABR bulk (Figure 19). Under aerobic ORP,
denitrification in MABR was inhibited and caused a higher MABR effluent nitrate
concentration. The high ORP also increased sCOD consumption within the MABR, as aerobic
heterotrophic organisms oxidized the available organics instead of allowing those substrates to
be utilized by denitrifiers. As a result, nitrate was not reduced in the MABR bulk when oxygen
was present, leaving higher nitrate concentrations and a reduced electron-donor supply for
downstream denitrification in the R-DMBR.

Figure 19. Higher nitrate concentrations due to aerobic ORP in MABR bulk from August 14 to
September 30.

To demonstrate the electron donor limitation in the DMBR, a short-term acetate dosing
experiment was conducted. From September 19 to September 30, 2024, sodium acetate (1,000
mg/L) was continuously dosed at a stoichiometric ratio of 2.86 g BOD/g N reduced
(corresponding to 2.67 g acetate/g N) into the R-DMBR to supplement BOD and support
complete denitrification. Daily grab samples from the MABR bulk, R-DMBR bulk, and R-
DMBR permeate were analyzed for sCOD, nitrate, and acetate. Acetate addition significantly

 36

reduced nitrate concentrations in both the DMBR bulk and permeate, shown in Figure 20,
confirming enhanced denitrification and supporting the electron donor limitation.

Figure 20. The R-DMBR bulk and R-DMBR permeate nitrate levels decreased with the addition
of acetate during the experimental period.

No residual acetate was detected, indicating its complete consumption. Mass-based COD
analysis revealed that the combined COD from the MABR bulk liquid and acetate dosing aligned
with the nitrate removal requirements shown in Figure 21, with minor deviations attributed to
uncertainties in flow rate measurement.

Figure 21. Mass-based COD utilization for nitrate reduction

This experiment demonstrates that acetate dosing is an effective strategy to mitigate electron
donor limitation when over-aeration depletes the available electron donor in the MABR. This
also confirmed that ORP is a key operating parameter for the MABR, which controls the balance
between nitrification and electron donor availability for downstream DMBR denitrification.

Unstable (Mechanical Disruption) Phase
The mixing air valve was repaired and reinstalled on September 30, 2024. The mixing air and
scouring air were operated at the same settings as during the stable period: mixing air at 17 cfm
with 50 s off and 10 s on, and scouring air at 20 cfm, 1 min on twice daily (as shown in Figure
12). However, ORP remained unstable during this period, as shown in Figure 18 (October 2024
to March 2025). From October to mid-November 2024, the ORP dropped into the anaerobic
range, leading to decreased nitrification in the MABR and consequently limiting nitrate
availability for denitrification in the downstream R-DMBR. In addition, anaerobic ORP
conditions likely generated sulfide from sulfate reduction and promoted FeS precipitation,
which resulted in solid deposits on the MABR biofilm. These deposits may have hindered
ammonium diffusion from the bulk liquid into the biofilm (Uri-Carreño et al., 2023). During
this anaerobic ORP range period, the mixing air valve frequency was increased from 10/50
on/off to 12/48 on/off on October 10, 2024, but this adjustment produced little improvement in
ORP. On October 26, 2024, the scouring air intensity was increased from 1 min on twice daily
to 3 min on twice daily. On October 26, 2024, the scouring air intensity was increased from 1
min on twice daily to 3 min on twice daily, which effectively shifted the ORP from the anaerobic
to the aerobic range within a few days. By December 2024, the ORP remained in the aerobic
range even after the mixing and scouring air flow rates were reduced to 15 cfm and 10 cfm,
respectively.

During this phase, there were multiple electric power disruptions that caused the shutdown of
pumps, the computer, and air compressors, resulting in tank level issues (e.g., tank draining and
no feeding) and air supply problems. As a result, the nitrification and denitrification performance
of the pilot-scale biomembrane filtration system is not discussed for this period.

4.1 Total Nitrogen Removal of the Biomembrane Filtration System

to 9.3 ± 3.7 mg N/L in the R-DMBR permeate (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 1.19×10-7),

Role of Accumulated Carbon on Dynamic Membrane in Denitrification
Interestingly, sCOD consumption (g sCOD/m3/d) was often lower than the stoichiometric
demand (using 2.86 mg sCOD/mg N removed without considering the biomass yield) for nitrate
removal, implying additional electron donor substrates beyond MABR bulk sCOD were
available (Figure 27). In addition, occasional negative sCOD uptake indicates net production of
the soluble organics in the R-DMBR (Figure 27). The paired nonparametric sCOD
concentrations are compared across the MABR bulk, R-DMBR bulk, and R-DMBR permeate
for days with complete triplets. The Friedman test (Friedman χ²df=2 = 1.389, p = 0.499) did not
detect an overall statistically significant difference in distributions across these three samples
(Figure 28). In other words, the sCOD concentrations were indistinguishable from one another
from MABR to R-DMBR. The similar sCOD across MABR and R-DMBR is not simply due to
recirculation, rather it is likely due to an organic carbon substrate depletion in the MABR.
Therefore, the sCOD measured is likely recalcitrant sCOD. Otherwise, a statistically significant
difference between MABR bulk and R-DMBR permeate would be expected, given the steady
decrease from MABR bulk to R-DMBR permeate.
Therefore, it was hypothesized that additional electron donor substrates supporting
denitrification are released within the R-DMBR through hydrolysis of slowly biodegradable
particulate COD and endogenous decay of microbial biomass accumulated on the dynamic
membrane, possibly enhanced by grazing by microbial eukaryotes. Consistent with this, the
sCOD concentrations were indistinguishable from those of the MABR bulk and the R-DMBR
permeate, suggesting that any biodegradable sCOD generated through hydrolysis of particulate

 44

COD and endogenous respiration was rapidly consumed and therefore not detectable as a net
increase.

The deteriorated denitrification performance during the positive ORP and acetate dosing phases
is likely attributable to increased carbon consumption in the MABR. A significant negative
correlation was identified between the MABR ORP and the COD in the MABR bulk using
Spearman correlation (Figure A7). Under aerobic operation, a larger fraction of particulate
carbon was hydrolyzed and oxidized in the MABR, reducing the amount of unhydrolyzed
particulate carbon for the downstream R-DMBR. As a result, the carbon inventory retained on
the dynamic membrane was insufficient to sustain denitrification. In addition, aerobic ORP
conditions further limited denitrification activity in MABR bulk, increasing the nitrate load to
the R-DMBR with a corresponding decrease in available electron donor or carbon inventory in
the R-DMBR.

In the previous section, the analyzed biochemical data revealed consistently good nitrogen
removal performance under low biodegradable sCOD concentration in the MABR effluent.

5.3 PERMANOVA and Redundancy Analysis
To identify environmental variables structuring microbial community composition, both
permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) and redundancy analysis
(RDA) with forward variable selection were performed on centered log-ratio-transformed
(CLR) genus-level relative abundances using the vegan package (v.2.6-8) (Aitchison, 1982;
Gloor et al., 2017; Lin & Peddada, 2020; Oksanen et al., 2001). The CLR transformation was
applied to address the compositional nature of microbiome data, with a pseudocount equal to
half the minimum non-zero value added prior to transformation (Gloor et al., 2017; Lin &
Peddada, 2020). Euclidean distance on CLR-transformed data yields the Aitchison distance,
which is appropriate for compositional data analysis (Aitchison, 1982; Gloor et al., 2017; Lin &
Peddada, 2020).

PERMANOVA was performed using the adonis2 function in the vegan package (v.2.6-8) with
9,999 permutations and marginal (Type III) sums of squares to assess the independent
contribution of each variable. Ten operational and water quality parameters were tested: source
water nitrate, nitrite, and ammonium concentrations; effluent nitrate and nitrite concentrations;
nitrate removal rate; hydraulic retention time (HRT); influent pH, oxidation-reduction potential
(ORP), and temperature. Sample type (biofilm vs. bulk) and sampling day were also included
as predictors. For the R-DMBR bulk phase, the source water is the MABR bulk, and the effluent
is the R-DMBR bulk. For the R-DMBR biofilm, the source water is the R-DMBR bulk, and the
effluent is again the R-DMBR permeate. PERMANOVA tests whether community centroids

 49

differ among groups or along environmental gradients, providing marginal R2 values that
represent the proportion of variance explained by each variable independently.

Redundancy Analysis with Forward Selection
While PERMANOVA identifies variables with significant marginal effects, it does not account
for redundancy among correlated predictors. RDA was then performed to identify a
parsimonious set of environmental drivers that collectively explain community variation. Prior
to model building, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated iteratively to identify
multicollinearity among continuous predictors. Variables with VIF > 5 were sequentially
removed until all remaining predictors fell below this threshold (Kim, 2019; Zuur et al., 2010).
Forward selection was then performed using the ordiR2step function with adjusted R2 for model
improvement, with significance evaluated through 999 permutations. Variables were included
only when they increased the adjusted R2 and had a permutation p-value < 0.05. Temporal
autocorrelation was controlled by including sampling day as a conditioning (partial) variable,
thereby removing temporal effects before assessing environmental predictors. Sample type was
included as a mandatory term in the base model. Statistical significance of the final RDA model
was assessed using permutation tests for both overall model fit and individual constrained axes.
Genus loadings on RDA axes were extracted to identify taxa most strongly associated with the
selected environmental gradients.

across all samples, dominated by Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, Chloroflexi, and

Core microbial community
The persistent core community comprised four presumptive denitrifying bacteria, including
Flavobacterium, Denitratisoma, Azonexus, and Terrimonas, as well as the hydrolytic bacteria
Lentimicrobium and Ferruginibacter. Flavobacterium dominated with the highest occurrence
frequency (>1% in 22/23 samples) with relative abundance ranging from 0.71 to 10.37%, and
has been extensively documented as a key player in aerobic denitrification (Deng et al., 2020;
Wang et al., 2024). Denitratisoma (0-2.3%, >1% in 15/23 samples) encompasses complete

 51

denitrifying bacteria that reduce nitrate to nitrogen gas (Chen et al., 2023; Fahrbach et al., 2006).
Members of Azonexus (0.13-3.32%, >1% in 16/23 samples) are involved in nitrogen fixation
and reduce nitrate under anoxic conditions (Tyagi et al., 2025), while members of Terrimonas
(0.01-2.57%, >1% in 11/23 samples) are heterotrophic denitrifying bacteria that prefer to use
recalcitrant aromatic compounds as carbon sources (C. Li et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2023).
Lentimicrobium are strict anaerobic bacteria growing on complex carbohydrates, producing
readily biodegradable short-chain fatty acids through hydrolysis and fermentation, which can be
utilized by other bacteria (Xu et al., 2023). Ferruginibacter persist in the system (0.01-1.94%,
>1% in 8/23 samples) can degrade complex carbon polymers such as cellulose and extracellular
polymeric substances (EPS) (Faust et al., 2015; Lewin et al., 2016). Notably, Ferruginibacter
shows a positive correlation with SRT in membrane bioreactors and is recognized as a keystone
taxon adapted to high biomass and long SRT conditions (Faust et al., 2015; Wijaya & Oh, 2023).
The long SRT created by the biofilm configuration in the R-DMBR likely explains its frequent
occurrence in the pilot system. In addition, an average of 0.71% Bdellovibrio was detected in
the R-DMBR. Bdellovibrio prey on other Gram-negative bacteria to obtain nutrients and energy,
causing cell lysis that releases intracellular organics that may be utilized by neighbouring
bacteria, including denitrifying bacteria (Bratanis et al., 2020; X. Liu et al., 2025; Lovering &
Sockett, 2021).
Microbial characterization revealed that the community is composed primarily of denitrifiers
and complex carbon degraders, likely attributable to the design of the R-DMBR, which promotes
high biomass retention and enables long SRT. These conditions support the development of a
diverse microbial consortium capable of leveraging endogenous respiration for effective
denitrification. Furthermore, the presence of complex carbon degraders may be advantageous in
systems with limited availability of easily biodegradable substrates, as they can utilize
particulate and complex electron donors introduced through bioaugmentation.

PERMANOVA: Marginal Effects of Environmental Variables
PERMANOVA revealed that many operational parameters exhibited significant marginal
effects on microbial community composition. Sample type (biofilm vs. bulk) explained 10.6%

between the two compartments. Among water quality parameters, effluent nitrite concentration
explained the highest proportion of variance (R² = 0.112, p = 0.013), followed by MABR ORP
(R² = 0.108, p = 0.017), source nitrite (R² = 0.103, p = 0.020), and influent pH (R² = 0.104, p =
0.016). All tested operational parameters, except for the sampling day (p = 0.100), showed
significant marginal effects (p < 0.05), suggesting that community composition responded to
multiple environmental gradients.

Despite the broad significance observed in PERMANOVA, RDA with forward selection

6. Calibrated Model and Process Control
Milestones #11 and 12

This section describes the development and calibration of the biomembrane filtration process
model using SIMBA#, a software for wastewater modeling and dynamic simulation. In Section
2, a preliminary process model of the proposed biomembrane filtration system was developed,
allowing for the appropriate sizing of key system parameters and the evaluation of the theoretical
performance of the system. After physically setting up and running the pilot demonstration, there
were several operational changes that were not initially modeled. As a result, the process model
was updated to reflect the pilot system operation and calibrated using data recorded from pilot
operation. The objective was to establish a realistic representation of the biomembrane filtration
treatment system that could serve as a benchmark for future design of the approach using the
SIMBA# model. Additionally, this section examines the implementation of process control
strategies within the model to evaluate potential improvements in nitrogen removal efficiency
and energy consumption. This section describes the two process tanks, MABR and DMBR, and
evaluates a range of recirculation ratios for the R-DMBR process configuration.

6.1 Model Calibration and Dynamic Simulation

The process model was reconstructed in SIMBA#, shown in Figure 34. This model was also

incorporates two-step nitrification and two-step denitrification. The model process train is the
same: nitrification in the MABR, followed by denitrification and filtration in the DMBR with

model is fed with time-resolved data for primary effluent quality concentrations (COD, NH4+-

N, ISS, alkalinity), flow rate, MABR aeration blower pressures, MABR bulk wasting rate,
permeate TSS, and permeate recycle rate. This data was collected from sensors and grab samples
collected from the pilot system during operation. Replacing long-term averages with these daily
inputs allowed the model to accurately represent and reproduce the day-to-day flow balance and
performance of the MABR and DMBR.

The CSTRs were added to enable model solving and replicate the uniform bulk mixing within
the DMBR. The model also features a bulk-wasting branch after the MABR effluent, a DMBR
effluent recirculation loop to the MABR influent. This architecture allowed the model to
accurately reproduce the day-to-day flow balance of both bioreactors.

Additionally, a simple feedback dissolved oxygen (DO) controller was added to adjust MABR
aeration to match pilot MABR DO sensor measurements. This control loop was deliberately
kept minimal, not for process optimization, but to synchronize modeled oxygen conditions
with observed data, thereby triggering more realistic downstream denitrification responses.
Without this correction, the open-loop model consistently assumed high oxygen transfer rates,
which did not accurately reflect the actual variability of the system.

During model calibration, pilot data indicated very minor nitrification occurring within the
DMBR (typically less than 1 mg NH4+-N/L reduction). To match the model with the pilot’s
performance, a diffuser element for the DMBR was added to account for a micro-aerobic
environment that matched the model’s performance with recorded pilot data.

Calibration Process
The calibration process involved working closely with personnel from inCTRL Solutions for
their subject matter and software-specific expertise. To ensure the model was grounded in
reliable and representative conditions, the dataset was carefully screened for periods of data
completeness, consistency with process understanding, and internal mass balance. A 21-day
interval of stable operation (July 6–27, 2024) met these criteria and was selected for model
calibration and process control development using daily dynamic inputs. The goal of this
calibration process was to refine the model to reliably reproduce pilot performance and establish
it as a credible predictive tool for subsequent analyses.

Before dynamic calibration, the model was equilibrated under a constant, average-load
condition. This was achieved by averaging each dynamic input over the July 6–27 window and
using these values to run the model for 250 simulated days. This allowed all internal state
variables, including biomass populations, soluble/particulate constituents, alkalinity in each
compartment, and clarifier solids, to converge to a mass‑balanced steady state. This stabilization
step mirrors the IWA Benchmark Simulation Model No. 1 (BSM1) protocol (Alex et al., 2008),
which recommends a steady-state equilibration under average loading before introducing
dynamic influent files, thereby eliminating startup artifacts and ensuring that subsequent

 56

dynamic simulations begin from a consistent and realistic baseline. The stabilized state snapshot

The MAPE provides an intuitive measure of relative prediction error, expressing the average
percent deviation between model predictions and measured data, where values <10% are

considered highly accurate, 10–20% good, 20–50% reasonable, and >50% inaccurate (Lewis,

to variations in loading rate, microbial activity, and environmental conditions, making the 22.8%
MAPE particularly reasonable (Treatment for Low Nitrogen and Phosphorus: Process
Performance and Reliability, 2019). Total nitrogen, as an aggregate parameter encompassing
multiple nitrogen species, shows slightly higher but still acceptable deviation at 35.5%. The
observed prediction errors demonstrate that the model adequately captures system dynamics
under stable operating conditions.

process control strategies and serves as a reliable baseline for future model development.
Additional parameters, including oxidized nitrogen (NOx-N) and soluble chemical oxygen
demand (sCOD), exhibited substantially higher prediction errors (MAPE of 97.6% and 39.0%,
respectively) and are not presented here. The strong agreement between model predictions and
measured data for ammonia and total nitrogen sufficiently demonstrates the model’s fidelity for
the pilot system, while also ensuring that the model is not overfit to the collected data.

6.2 Automatic Process Control Results
The calibrated SIMBA# model served as a virtual testbed to evaluate two process-control
strategies: 1) ammonia-based aeration control (ABAC) in the MABR and 2) DMBR effluent
recirculation-ratio control.

To ensure a fair comparison, the model was driven by synthetic hourly diurnal influent files for
flow, COD, TSS, alkalinity, phosphate, and NH4+-N. These files were generated from pilot data,
from the July 6 - 27, 2024 operational period, using SIMBA#’s diurnal generator tool to preserve
realistic daily load patterns (morning peaks and overnight lows) and wet-weather dilutions that
would be consistent with the scale and characteristics of the pilot system. This was done to
effectively test control robustness and compliance resilience.

To appropriately simulate the system and ensure fair comparisons across scenarios, the
methodology began by simulating the model under average-load conditions for 250 days to
stabilize state variables (e.g., biomass inventories, internal nitrogen pools, and alkalinity). The
hourly diurnal influent was then applied and an additional 30 days were simulated to allow
transients to decay and controllers to adapt to cyclic loading. The resulting model state was
saved and used as a common initial condition for all subsequent simulations, ensuring identical
starting conditions and influent load.

Ammonia-Based Aeration Control (ABAC)
ABAC was implemented in the model given its broad adoption across municipal wastewater
treatment. Using ABAC allowed for assessment of how a prevailing industry control strategy

 59

influences effluent ammonia concentration, aeration energy demand, and operational stability
under realistic diurnal loading.
Aeration is a majority energy consumer in wastewater treatment processes, with aeration
accounting for around 60% or more of a WRRF’s energy consumption (Ghimire et al., 2021).
This emphasizes the importance of analyzing approaches like ABAC to reduce the biggest
energy consumer in wastewater treatment.

DMBR Effluent Recirculation Ratio
The next control strategy that was explored was the effect of varying the recirculation ratio from
the DMBR permeate to the MABR. The motivation for this analysis was grounded in the
observed relationship between recirculation flow rate and nitrogen removal performance.

Figure 38. Negative sloping trendline between final effluent TN and DMBR effluent
recirculation, suggesting that increased recirculation contributes to better nitrogen removal. Data
was obtained from the pilot operation, from the July 6 - 27, 2024 period.

To examine the role of recirculation, the DMBR effluent recirculation flow rate was compared
with the final effluent total nitrogen from pilot grab sample data, as shown in Figure 38. An
inverse trend was observed, indicating that higher recirculation rates were associated with lower
effluent nitrogen concentrations. This result reinforced the conclusion that internal recycling is
a key lever for improving treatment performance. Mechanistically, this trend is consistent with
process understanding: increasing recirculation improves nitrogen removal by recycling NH4+-
N from the DMBR to the MABR for further nitrification, enabling subsequent denitrification in
the DMBR. The recirculation flow rate values were estimated from flow balance calculations,
therefore, a direct quantitative relationship between the two variables could not be rigorously
established.

On this basis, DMBR effluent recirculation was varied in the SIMBA# model, with fixed
recirculation selected as a controllable variable to test alongside aeration control. This approach
enabled (i) validation of the trend observed in pilot data, (ii) quantification of improvements in

 62

nitrogen removal achievable through recycle manipulation, and (iii) assessment of whether an
optimal range exists beyond which further increases in recirculation ratio yield diminishing
returns. Denitrification generates alkalinity, which the system utilizes by recycling the permeate
back to the MABR to promote nitrification which consumes this alkalinity. By integrating
recirculation control into the study, the evaluation highlighted the critical role of internal
recycling in maintaining the appropriate alkalinity levels during the nitrification stage.

Figure 40 illustrates the relationship between DMBR effluent recirculation ratio and the
difference in final effluent TN concentration for the ABAC and non-ABAC models compared
with 0% recirculation. The first takeaway is that implementing recirculation, regardless of
magnitude, results in better nitrogen removal performance of the system. Further, a U-shaped
trend is observed, where effluent TN concentrations initially decrease with increasing
recirculation, reaching a minimum at approximately 50% recirculation, before rising again at
higher ratios.

Initially, increasing the recirculation ratio enhances nitrogen removal by recycling NH4+-N to
the MABR for additional nitrification. However, beyond approximately 70% recirculation, the
system likely becomes limited by mass transfer kinetics. At higher recirculation rates, the HRT
in the MABR decreases proportionally with the increased volumetric flow. Under these
conditions, the ammonia diffusion rate through the biofilm becomes insufficient to deliver
adequate substrate to the oxic zone, where nitrifying bacteria oxidize ammonia to nitrite and
subsequently to nitrate. This diffusion limitation prevents complete nitrification within the
available contact time. Consequently, over successive treatment cycles, incomplete nitrification

 64

results in elevated effluent TN concentrations, thereby diminishing overall nitrogen removal
performance.

The resulting U-shaped performance curve aligns with studies showing that internal
recirculation enhances nitrogen removal only up to an optimal point. For instance, a study
demonstrated that, beyond the recirculation rates required to meet TN effluent limits
(approximately 3.5x the influent flow rate), additional recycle flow has diminishing returns for
performance while leading to an increase in pumping energy (Santín et al., 2020). This supports
the notion that excessive recirculation can shift the system past its optimal operating range,
leading to reduced stability and diminished nitrogen removal efficiency.

Overall, these results suggest that moderate internal recirculation offers the best nitrogen
removal performance, resulting in the lowest effluent TN concentrations.

7.2 Methodology

This TEA isolates technology-differentiating costs for retrofitting an existing MLE process train
(anoxic zone followed by an aerobic zone with a secondary clarifier) for enhanced nitrogen
removal. To represent a brownfield retrofit, SRT targets and tank volumes are held constant
across configurations, and membrane technologies (MBR, MABR, DMBR) are assumed to be
installed within existing infrastructure. Homogeneous civil costs (e.g., excavation, concrete, and
auxiliary buildings) are excluded to isolate process-technology impacts.

Routine operation and maintenance costs are excluded because they are expected to be broadly
similar across retrofit options. Similarly, homogeneous capital costs for major pumps/blowers,
civil construction, EPC services, and electrical/control infrastructure are assumed similar and
are excluded. This boundary condition highlights relative cost drivers across retrofit pathways
(Trotochaud et al., 2020).

 71

Pumping and aeration systems use standardized hydraulic parameters (pipe diameters, static
lifts, friction factors, blower curves, and pump efficiencies) so that differences in energy demand
reflect process performance rather than plumbing design. Assumed piping, blower, and pump
parameters are summarized in Appendix Table A1.

 1. Membrane capital expenditure (MLE‑MBR, MABR‑CAS, MABR‑DMBR)
 2. Secondary clarifier capital expenditure (operational in MLE and MABR‑CAS; excluded
 from MBR/DMBR configurations)
 3. Electricity consumption (pumping, aerobic tank aeration, MABR aeration, and MBR
 sparging)
 4. Biosolids management (sludge handling and disposal)

The specific treatment cost ($/m3) is calculated assuming a 25-year system lifespan with a
discount rate of 5.5%. Membrane (MBR, MABR and DMBR) modules and secondary clarifier
capital costs are annualized using a capital recovery factor. The levelized cost is determined by
adding annualized capital costs and annual operating expenses (electricity consumption, sludge
disposal, and acetate dosing) and dividing by the annual treated volume. This analysis
emphasizes differences between configurations rather than absolute total facility cost.

Sludge handling costs include both transportation and disposal tipping fees. Acetate dosing is
standardized across configurations, as needed to sustain operational performance, and assumed
to use sodium acetate.

7.3 Model Development

Standardized models were developed for the three alternative benchmark configurations, in
addition to the MABR-DMBR subject of this report, with the MLE process serving as the
conventional baseline. Other configurations are modeled as retrofit variants of the MLE train,
with reactor volumes held constant to isolate technology impacts on performance and
technoeconomics.

SRT-based Model Development
Reactor volumes across configurations were sized using SRT as the primary design parameter,
following Houweling & Daigger (Houweling & Daigger, 2019).

The sizing workflow was first applied to the baseline MLE. The design SRT was calculated
from the minimum SRT required for nitrification, adjusted for temperature (15°C baseline) and
multiplied by a safety factor of 2 to account for operational variability (Tchobanoglous et al.,
2014). A system-wide design SRT was then calculated by adjusting for the aerobic mass fraction
of mixed liquor in the overall system.

The SIMBA# model was tuned to match the design SRT by iteratively adjusting anoxic/aerobic
volumes and IR, RAS, and waste WAS flow rates while maintaining effluent targets and MLSS
limits.

 75

Final reactor volumes for the baseline MLE configuration were 9,950 m3 for the aerobic region
and 7,900 m3 for the anoxic. These volumes were held constant across all configurations and
scenarios to standardize performance comparisons and isolate the impact of membrane
technology retrofits. For MABR-based configurations, membrane surface area was determined
by identifying the minimum area required for sufficient nitrification under low temperature
conditions (10oC and COD:TKN = 10). All aerobic tanks and MBRs operated with dissolved
oxygen (DO) control, maintaining a bulk concentration of 2 mg O2/L. Configuration schematics
are provided in Figures 44-47, and detailed SRT calculations, including parameter values and
equations, are presented in Appendix Table A4.

Carbon Dosing Strategy
Some scenarios exhibited insufficient total nitrogen removal due to limited denitrification. In
these cases, supplemental carbon (sodium acetate) was increased until the effluent total nitrogen
concentration met the 10 mg N/L limit where possible. When the MLE configuration could not
meet the target due to design (sizing/configuration) constraints, dosing was increased until the
lowest feasible effluent nitrogen concentration was achieved.

MLE-MBR

The MLE‑MBR is highly robust under elevated TKN loading rates and cold temperatures

MABR-CAS

MABR‑CAS performs well under carbon-limited conditions, achieving nitrogen compliance
with moderate cost and lower energy/emissions than traditional suspended-growth systems. It

MABR-DMBR

MABR-DMBR is the top-performing configuration under baseline and high-COD conditions,
delivering the lowest cost, energy consumption, and emissions with little to no acetate dosing.
The DMBR’s ability to operate at high MLSS supports strong denitrification, reducing external

Under elevated TKN loading and under the combined low-COD and low-temperature stress
case, nitrification becomes limiting in the initial layout. Introducing an aerobic zone within the

modification, MABR-DMBR is robust and most economically favorable retrofit across the
evaluated scope.

7.7 Conclusions

Section 7 evaluated the techno-economic and GHG implications of retrofitting an existing MLE
train with the proposed MABR-DMBR configuration, benchmarked against three alternatives
(MLE, MLE‑MBR, and MABR‑CAS). Using a standardized SIMBA# process model (Section
7.3) and a consistent retrofit boundary that isolates technology-differentiating costs (Section
7.2), the analysis compared levelized unit cost ($/m³), specific energy (kWh/m³), and GHG
emissions associated with electricity use and sludge disposal across a baseline case and twelve
stress-test operating scenarios (Section 7.4).

Overall, the TEA and GHG assessment support a clear conclusion: MABR-DMBR provides the
most compelling retrofit pathway for achieving <10 mg N/L total nitrogen at substantially lower

 92

cost, energy demand, and emissions than conventional suspended-growth or membrane
benchmarks. It remains the most favorable option across the evaluated operating envelope when
appropriately configured for nitrification under stress conditions.

The most impactful next steps to strengthen commercialization readiness and reduce residual
uncertainty are: (1) validating achievable DMBR flux and module cost at municipal scale, (2)
characterizing N2O emissions for biofilm/DMBR-based systems, and (3) refining volume
allocation and control strategies to ensure nitrification robustness under low-temperature and
high-loading conditions.

design from decommissioning activities and recommends design changes

8.2 DMBR Module Disassembly

On March 17, 2025, 459 days after the operation of the pilot began, the DMBR module was
removed from the system for disassembly and testing. Since filtrate had been drawn from both
the top and bottom of the vertically oriented module during operation, upon removal it was
observed that a vertically stratified biofilm had formed on the module, likely due to incomplete
mixing in the DMBR tank and the lack of pumped mixing two days prior to removing the module
(Figure 60). Approximately the bottom third of the module had developed a thick biofilm, which
almost completely obscured the channeled structure of the module. When disassembled,
channels in this section were observed to have thick biofilm developed on the sides but no
biofilm developed on the top or bottom. Therefore, it is likely that not the entire mesh surface
area was used for filtration.

  Because permeate was pulled simultaneously from the top and bottom ends, the resulting
convergence of flow paths at the midpoint created more uniform pressure in that region, which
likely contributed to the most complete biofilm coverage there. This indicates that the module
was possibly too tall relative to the permeate pump’s power to create sufficient flow through the
membranes across the module height.

Additionally, although not shown in these images, some channels had significant biomass
buildup on the internal channel support structures. Buildup inside the channels could be caused
by either inert solids or active biomass passing through the dynamic membrane. If sufficient
substrate were available, active biomass in this area would be able to form biofilms on the
internal structure. This buildup likely contributed to increased solids concentrations in the
permeate near the end of the pilot operation period.

 94

sides of the module.

8.3 Proposed Design Modifications Based on Pilot Observations

In order to optimize the use of both energy and materials, and to ensure sufficient denitrification
and solids removal, it is essential that the entire mesh surface of the module develops an active
dynamic membrane. Several design changes can be implemented to address the incomplete
coverage of the DMBR module observed here.

 1. Increased bulk mixing would reduce the impact of settling. This would create a more even
 distribution of solids deposited along the height of the module, reducing the possibility of
 overly thick biofilms at the bottom and increasing biofilm coverage in other areas.
 2. Increased flow/vacuum pressure of the pump pulling flow through the membrane would
 allow increased solids deposition, create more force holding the biofilm to the mesh
 surface, and reduce or eliminate areas with no biofilm.
 3. Increased space between channels. Although this would reduce the amount of mesh per
 volume of bulk liquid, it would also ensure that biofilm can form around the entire

 95

 circumference of each channel, increasing the effective surface area of the dynamic
 membrane.
 4. Add permeate collection ports along the module height. Collecting permeate at more
 points across the module height could lead to a more uniform flow across the module’s
 height.
 5. Increased total cross-section of collection tubes, which transport permeate from each
 channel to the top or bottom of the module. The current design features a single collection
 tube at the center of the module, with a 1 in² cross-section. Collecting permeate at each
 end of the mesh channels would reduce the pressure loss created by transporting permeate
 vertically and would leave more energy available to drive transmembrane flux. Increasing
 the cross-section of each collection tube would have a similar effect.

In addition to improving performance, reducing the size would allow for increased modularity.
It would also significantly reduce the manufacturing costs associated with larger parts, such as
those that run along the entire height or width of the module. A CAD model of a module with
each of the proposed design changes implemented is shown in Figure 61.

9. Conclusion
The pilot-scale demonstration successfully validated the biomembrane filtration system, an
integrated process combining MABR technology for nitrification and R-DMBR technology for
denitrification and filtration, as an effective and energy-efficient solution for intensifying
nitrogen removal in WRRFs. The system was evaluated for its potential as a cost-effective
upgrade option for facilities facing nitrogen discharge limits. During the four-month stable
operating phase (April 16–August 15, 2024), the pilot system achieved its target performance
metrics.

The study provided crucial insights into operational parameters necessary for optimizing
nitrogen removal efficiency and ensuring system stability:

1. Process Control for Efficiency: Model simulations confirmed that implementing ammonia-
based aeration control (ABAC) in the MABR significantly improves energy efficiency,
potentially achieving up to a 53.5% reduction in specific nitrogen removal energy.

2. Optimal Recirculation: Modeling of the DMBR effluent recirculation strategy showed that
increasing recirculation enhances nitrogen removal, with a 50% recycle ratio identified as the
optimal operating point to balance performance and pumping energy demand.

3. Importance of ORP Control: Maintaining the oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) in the
MABR between −150 to 0 mV is crucial. This control range promotes nitrification while
successfully preserving biodegradable carbon in the wastewater for denitrification.

4. Standby Carbon Dosing: The positive ORP and acetate dosing experiment confirmed that
acetate dosing is an effective standby strategy to mitigate electron donor limitation during
periods of MABR over-aeration leading to carbon depletion.

5. Design Refinements: Observations from module disassembly revealed that inconsistent
biofilm formation due to vertical stratification and insufficient pump vacuum pressure limited
the utilization of the entire mesh surface area. Increased bulk mixing intensity and permeate
vacuum pressure are required to ensure uniform biofilm coverage, prevent stratification, and
maximize mesh utilization for optimal performance.

6. Microbial Community Dynamics: The microbial community in the R-DMBR consists of
specialized denitrifiers and complex carbon degraders (e.g., Flavobacterium, Denitratisoma,
Ferruginibacter) that thrive under the long solids retention time (SRT) provided by the dynamic
membrane and utilize endogenous respiration for denitrification. Furthermore, MABR ORP was
identified as the single most significant non-redundant environmental driver shaping the R-
DMBR microbial community, underscoring the necessity of treating the MABR and DMBR as
a tightly integrated ecological system.

 98

7. Nitrification Enhancement under Increased TKN Loading: Under elevated TKN loading
at fixed reactor volumes, the original MABR-DMBR pilot configuration in SIMBA# became
nitrification limited, which consequently strained overall nitrogen removal. Converting a portion
of the DMBR tank from anoxic to an aerobic zone increased nitrification capacity without
adding membrane modules, enabling nitrogen compliance with only a modest aeration energy
increase and preserving the MABR-DMBR as the lowest-cost treatment train relative to
conventional configurations.

10. Future Work & Recommendations
Building upon the key findings and process insights gained, the following future work and
recommendations are proposed to validate model predictions, optimize system design for
commercial scale, and further enhance the understanding of the underlying biological
mechanisms.

The calibrated process model (SIMBA#) identified two key control strategies that require
physical validation to confirm predicted performance and energy savings:

1. Physical Implementation and Validation of ABAC: Physical implementation is necessary
to validate the predicted energy savings and to identify operational challenges associated with
dynamic control in a hybrid MABR-based system under real operating conditions.

2. Defining Maximum TKN Loading Capacity and Minimum HRT: To maximize the
efficiency and throughput of the MABR stage for commercial application, it is essential to
determine the maximum capacity of the system.

 ● Implement a physical process controller designed to gradually increase the TKN loading
 rate until the system reaches its performance limit (e.g., consistent ammonium
 breakthrough). This effort will define the maximum stable TKN loading capacity for the
 MABR module under real-world conditions, moving beyond the conservative rates used
 in this pilot demonstration phase.
 ● Concurrently, controlled experiments should be conducted to determine the minimum
 MABR HRT that does not result in kinetic limitation for nitrification. Model simulations
 indicated that high recirculation ratios, which reduce MABR HRT, eventually lead to
 diminished returns due to mass transfer limitations, where the ammonium diffusion rate
 through the biofilm becomes insufficient. By systematically varying the flow/HRT and
 monitoring ammonium diffusion and conversion rates, the optimal, highest-throughput
 operation point can be established.

3. Evaluating the Overall Impact of Endogenous Respiration: Dedicated studies are needed
to quantitatively evaluate the overall impact and contribution of endogenous respiration to
denitrification. The microbial community analyses revealed that the long SRT in the R-DMBR

 99

supports complex carbon degraders and denitrifiers, enabling the use of internally generated
organics for denitrification. Future work should employ techniques such as long-term carbon
mass balance studies under varying MABR ORP control (which dictates the available electron
donor supply) to accurately determine the proportion of nitrogen removal supported by
endogenous decay versus residual biodegradable carbon.

4. Design for Commercial Application: For commercial application, the findings from the
proposed maximum TKN loading capacity tests must be integrated to optimize the capital
expense of the MABR stage. The minimum MABR HRT and maximum TKN loading rate
should be used to precisely define the required MABR membrane surface area (a major cost
driver), for a given flow rate. The validated ABAC control strategy and recirculation ratio should
be implemented to achieve low-energy operation while still achieving performance goals. Long-
term DMBR flux testing, using step-wise flux increases, will be essential to determine
appropriate commercial DMBR sizing. Additionally, work should be conducted to determine
the flux peaking factor for the DMBR meshes to understand its ability to handle temporary,
high-flow conditions from wet weather events.

These future steps are crucial for transforming the validated pilot data and modeling insights
