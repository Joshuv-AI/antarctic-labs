A dual-matrix analytical framework integrating bio-inspired machine learning and Sobol-optimized UV spectrophotometry for five-analyte quantification: comprehensive sustainability assessment
Authors: Khadiga M. Kelani, Lama A. Alshabani, Omkulthom Al kamaly, Michael K. Halim, Ahmed Emad F. Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of environmentally sustainable analytical methodologies capable of resolving highly overlapped multicomponent systems remains an important challenge in modern analytical chemistry. UV spectrophotometry offers several practical advantages, including minimal solvent-consumption, rapid analysis, low operational cost, and instrumental simplicity; however, its quantitative application is often constrained by severe spectral-overlap among analytes. In the present study, a dual-matrix chemometric framework was developed for the simultaneous determination of five spectrally overlapping analytes—hydrochlorothiazide (HCD), losartan potassium (LOS), ramipril (RAP), N-nitrosodiethylamine (NDA), and toluene (TLN)—in pharmaceutical formulations and fortified human plasma samples. The proposed methodology integrates a five-factor, five-level multilevel experimental design for calibration, Sobol quasi-random sampling for external validation, and Firefly Algorithm-optimized Partial Least Squares (FA-PLS) modeling for adaptive wavelength selection and multivariate calibration. The developed models demonstrated excellent analytical performance. For pharmaceutical formulations, mean recoveries ranged from 99.68 to 100.57%, with RMSEP values between 0.037 and 0.094 µg/mL. For fortified human plasma samples, mean recoveries ranged from 98.18 to 98.97%, with RMSEP values between 0.059 and 0.146 µg/mL, demonstrating the feasibility of the proposed approach under biologically relevant matrix conditions. The environmental profile of the proposed methodology was comprehensively evaluated using multiple complementary tools, including NEMI, ComplexGAPI, AGREE, the Multicolor Assessment Tool, carbon footprint estimation, and the Need–Quality–Sustainability index. Collectively, these assessments confirmed the favorable greenness and overall sustainability characteristics of the developed spectrophotometric approach. To the best of our knowledge, no previous UV spectrophotometric chemometric method has been reported for the simultaneous determination of LOS, RAP, and HCD in the presence of both NDA and TLN within a single analytical workflow without a prior separation step. The proposed dual-matrix FA-PLS framework demonstrates that UV spectrophotometry, when coupled with advanced chemometric optimization and statistically designed calibration strategies, can provide an accurate, sensitive, and environmentally sustainable platform for multicomponent pharmaceutical analysis and proof-of-concept application to biological matrices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01853-7.

Introduction

Modern analytical chemistry increasingly demands methodologies capable of resolving complex multicomponent systems while maintaining high analytical performance, operational simplicity, and reduced environmental impact [1, 2]. Among optical analytical techniques, UV spectrophotometry represents an attractive platform due to its rapid measurement capability, minimal solvent consumption, and compatibility with micro-scale analytical workflows [3, 4]. However, the quantitative analysis of multicomponent mixtures using UV spectrophotometry remains fundamentally challenging because severe spectral overlap frequently prevents direct analyte discrimination using conventional univariate approaches [5, 6].

Over the past decade, many of the inherent limitations of UV spectrophotometry have been mitigated through the integration of chemometric modeling. Multivariate calibration techniques have significantly expanded the applicability of UV spectroscopy by enabling the resolution of chemically overlapping systems without the need for separation [7–10]. More recent advances incorporating machine-learning algorithms, evolutionary optimization strategies, and advanced signal-processing methods have further improved the extraction of quantitative-information from complex spectral datasets [11–15]. Consequently, modern spectrophotometric analysis increasingly relies on data-driven computational approaches to enhance signal interpretation and analyte quantification.

Despite these advances, critical methodological limitations remain in many reported chemometric spectrophotometric studies [16–18]. Calibration domain construction is frequently empirical rather than statistically engineered, leading to non-orthogonal mixture compositions and suboptimal representation of the multidimensional concentration space. Validation procedures commonly rely on random data partitioning strategies that may introduce statistical bias and artificially enhance predictive performance. Additionally, spectral variable selection is often insufficiently optimized, with many multivariate models relying on full-spectrum datasets that incorporate redundant wavelengths, noise components, and matrix-induced spectral interferences. Collectively, these factors limit the development of chemometric spectrophotometry as a fully optimized analytical methodology.

To address these challenges, contemporary analytical chemistry has increasingly explored the integration of advanced optimization algorithms and structured experimental design strategies. Swarm intelligence algorithms, particularly the Firefly Algorithm (FA), have demonstrated promising capability for adaptive wavelength selection and dimensionality reduction, allowing identification of the most informative spectral regions for multivariate modeling while improving predictive accuracy and model interpretability [19, 20] In parallel, quasi-deterministic sampling approaches such as Sobol sequences provide a powerful strategy for constructing statistically representative validation datasets characterized by uniform multidimensional distribution, thereby reducing sampling bias and enhancing model generalizability. Nevertheless, the integration of evolutionary optimization strategies with statistically engineered validation design remains relatively unexplored within UV spectrophotometric chemometric frameworks, particularly for multicomponent pharmaceutical systems involving active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and associated impurities.

In addition to analytical performance considerations, sustainability evaluation has become an increasingly important component of analytical method development. Contemporary analytical chemistry increasingly incorporates the principles of Green and White Analytical Chemistry to ensure that methodological innovation is accompanied by reduced environmental impact, high analytical quality, and improved operational efficiency. Quantitative assessment tools such as NEMI, ComplexGAPI, AGREE, the MA framework, and the NQS index enable comprehensive evaluation of environmental performance, analytical quality, and practical applicability [21, 22]. However, the incorporation of advanced chemometric optimization strategies within analytically structured sustainability evaluation frameworks remains limited.

Accordingly, the present study introduces a computationally engineered spectrophotometric analytical methodology designed to enhance the resolution of complex overlapping spectral systems. The proposed framework integrates Sobol quasi-random validation sampling with FA-PLS modeling to improve spectral variable selection, calibration robustness, and predictive reliability. In addition, a dual-matrix modeling strategy is implemented to evaluate analytical performance in both pharmaceutical formulations and human plasma matrices, enabling systematic investigation of matrix-induced spectral interference.

The methodological performance of the proposed spectrophotometric framework is further complemented by a comprehensive sustainability assessment based on NEMI, ComplexGAPI, AGREE, MA tool, CF estimation, and the NQS index. By combining chemometric optimization, statistically engineered experimental design, and sustainability evaluation within a unified analytical platform, the developed methodology demonstrates how computationally enhanced spectrophotometry can serve as an efficient alternative for multicomponent mixture analysis.

This integrated analytical framework is demonstrated through the simultaneous determination of losartan potassium (LOS), ramipril (RAP), and hydrochlorothiazide (HCD), N-nitrosodiethylamine (NDA, a genotoxic impurity of LOS) and toluene (TLN, a toxic impurity of RAP), providing a proof-of-concept for intelligent and sustainable spectrophotometric analysis of complex pharmaceutical systems with simultaneous API–impurity resolution capability.

Experimental

Instrumentation and software

UV absorption spectra were recorded using a UV-1800 double-beam spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with matched quartz cuvettes having a 1 cm optical path length. Instrument control and spectral acquisition were performed with UVProbe software version 2.42 (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Measurements were conducted in the wavelength region from 220 to 350 nm using a spectral bandwidth of 1.0 nm. The instrument collected data at 0.1 nm intervals; after acquisition, the spectra were exported and interpolated to 1 nm spacing to generate the final data matrix used for chemometric modeling.

Sample preparation procedures were assisted by an ultrasonic bath supplied by Julabo Labortechnik GmbH (Seelbach, Germany). Mass measurements were carried out on an AUX-220 analytical balance (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) with a readability of 0.1 mg.

Chemometric calculations, statistical analysis, and model development were performed using MATLAB R2023a (version 9.14.0; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Partial least squares modeling was conducted using PLS_Toolbox version 9.1 (Eigenvector Research Inc., Manson, WA, USA). Sobol quasi-random sequences used for the construction of external validation sets were generated using the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox, and Firefly Algorithm optimization was implemented using the Global Optimization Toolbox (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Sustainability assessment was carried out using the online MA platform. Carbon footprint calculations and the Need–Quality–Sustainability (NQS) evaluation were performed using Microsoft Excel 365 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) according to the equations and scoring procedures described in the cited references.

Materials and reagents

All chemicals and solvents used throughout the study were of analytical reagent grade or HPLC grade and were used as received without further purification. Absolute ethanol (≥ 99.8%), acetonitrile (HPLC grade, ≥ 99.9%), chloroform (≥ 99.8%), n-hexane (≥ 99.0%), and ethyl acetate (≥ 99.8%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Human plasma matrix

Blank pooled human plasma was obtained from VACSERA (Giza, Egypt). The material was supplied as drug-free plasma collected and processed by the manufacturer from healthy donors. Upon receipt, the plasma was aliquoted and stored at − 20 °C until use. The purchased plasma was used exclusively for preparation of matrix-matched calibration standards and validation samples during method development. No human specimens were collected directly by the authors for the present study.

Reference standards

Reference standards of LOS were kindly supplied by Amoun Pharmaceutical Co. S.A.E. (1st Industrial Zone, El Obour City, Qalyubia, Egypt). Reference standards of HCD and RAP were kindly provided by Pharmadar Pharmaceutical Company (El-Sadat City, Menoufia, Egypt). The certified purities, as reported in the corresponding certificates of analysis, were 99.23%, 99.65%, and 99.58% for LOS, HCD, and RAP, respectively.

Pharmaceutical formulation

Loram-H® tablets (Unichem Laboratories Ltd., Mumbai, India), labeled to contain 50 mg LOS, 5 mg RAP, and 12.5 mg HCD per tablet, were purchased from a local community pharmacy. The analyzed product corresponded to batch number LOHF-16035. The tablets were stored at ambient laboratory conditions until analysis.

Toxic impurity standards

Reference standards of NDA and TLN were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). According to the supplier certificates of analysis, the certified purities were 99.51% for NDA and 99.47% for TLN, respectively.

Preparation of standard solutions

Primary stock solutions of LOS, HCD, RAP, NDA, and TLN were individually prepared at 1.0 mg/mL by accurately dissolving appropriate quantities of each reference standard in absolute ethanol. Experimental design strategy for calibration and validation samples

Two independent experimental designs were constructed to develop matrix-specific chemometric models for pharmaceutical formulations and human plasma. For each matrix, separate calibration and validation sets were prepared and subsequently used for model development and external performance assessment.

The calibration set was generated using a five-factor, five-level multilevel design according to the chemometric design strategy described by Brereton et al. [23]. LOS, RAP, HCD, NDA, and TLN were treated as independent factors, and each analyte was assigned five coded concentration levels of − 2, − 1, 0, + 1, and + 2. Because a complete five-factor, five-level factorial design would require 3125 experimental combinations, a reduced 25-mixture design was selected to provide representative coverage of the concentration domain while maintaining low correlation among the analyte concentrations.

Validation set construction

External validation sets were generated using Sobol quasi-random sampling [24]. This approach was used to produce validation mixtures distributed uniformly throughout the same concentration domain employed for calibration.

For each matrix, 16 validation mixtures were generated within the concentration limits defined in Table 1. The resulting samples were used exclusively for external validation and were not included in model construction.

Sample preparation

All calibration and validation mixtures were prepared in 25 mL volumetric flasks using calibrated micropipettes and ethanol as the final diluent.

For pharmaceutical analysis, the required aliquots of the analyte working solutions were transferred directly to volumetric flasks and diluted to volume with ethanol.

For plasma analysis, blank human plasma samples were spiked with the appropriate analyte concentrations and processed according to the protein precipitation procedure described in "Sample processing and clean-up" section. The resulting extracts were then diluted to the required concentration prior to spectral measurement.

Spectral preprocessing and FA-PLS model construction

Chemometric analysis was performed through a sequential workflow comprising spectral preprocessing, wavelength selection using FA, and multivariate calibration by PLS. The same computational protocol was applied independently to the pharmaceutical and plasma datasets so that each matrix was modeled using its own calibration data while maintaining identical preprocessing and optimization settings.

Spectral preprocessing

All UV spectra were initially recorded over the wavelength range 220–350 nm using an instrumental sampling interval of 0.1 nm. Prior to model construction, the spectra were processed using a fixed sequence established during preliminary studies.

Noise reduction was first performed with the Savitzky–Golay smoothing algorithm using a second-order polynomial and an 11-point window. Baseline correction was then carried out by subtracting a first-order polynomial fitted to each spectrum by least-squares regression. The processed spectra were subsequently resampled at 1 nm intervals, producing a data matrix containing 131 absorbance variables corresponding to wavelengths from 220 to 350 nm.

This spectral interval was selected before calibration because it contained all relevant analytical information for LOS, RAP, HCD, NDA, and TLN. Regions below 220 nm were excluded because of increased instrumental noise, whereas wavelengths above 350 nm exhibited negligible absorbance for all analytes. The same preprocessing procedure was applied without modification to all calibration mixtures, validation samples, and application samples for both analytical matrices.

FA-based wavelength selection

The preprocessed spectral matrix was subjected to FA to identify the subset of wavelengths providing the most informative variables for quantitative prediction. In the optimization procedure, each firefly was encoded as a binary vector representing the inclusion or exclusion of the 131 wavelength variables.

The initial population was generated randomly, and each candidate subset was evaluated by constructing a PLS model using the selected wavelengths. Model fitness was defined as the RMSECV obtained from leave-one-out cross-validation. Firefly movement was governed by the standard FA parameters α, β₀, and γ, and the optimized parameter values adopted for each analyte are summarized in Table S2.

The optimization was terminated when the maximum number of generations was reached or when no further improvement in RMSECV was observed for 20 consecutive generations. To evaluate algorithm stability, each optimization was repeated 20 times using different random seeds. The final wavelength subset was selected as the solution yielding the lowest RMSECV among all runs. The consistency of variable selection across repeated optimizations is illustrated in Fig. S1, which presents the selection frequency of each wavelength variable.

PLS model construction

The wavelength subset selected by FA was used to construct the final PLS model. The optimal number of LVs was determined from the RMSECV profile, and the smallest number of latent variables providing the minimum prediction error was selected to avoid overfitting while maintaining predictive accuracy.

Matrix-specific model development

Independent FA-PLS models were developed for pharmaceutical formulations and human plasma using their respective calibration sets of 25 mixtures. Because each matrix exhibits a distinct spectral background and interference profile, wavelength selection and LV optimization were carried out separately for each dataset. This strategy allowed each model to retain the most informative spectral variables for its intended analytical application.

Performance criteria and validation parameters

The analytical performance of the developed FA-PLS models was evaluated using a combination of chemometric statistics and validation parameters that describe calibration quality, predictive ability, accuracy, precision, sensitivity, robustness, and resistance to overfitting [25–27].

Chemometric performance parameters

Model fitting and internal predictive performance were assessed using RMSEC, RMSECV, and SEC. External predictive ability was evaluated using RMSEP and RRMSEP. Systematic error was estimated from the bias, and BCRMSEP was calculated to quantify prediction error after correction for systematic bias.

Model acceptance criteria

Calibration models were considered satisfactory when the coefficient of determination (r2) was ≥ 0.999. Predictive performance was evaluated by interpreting RMSEP, RRMSEP, and bias in relation to the analyte concentration range, matrix complexity, and intended analytical application. In combination with acceptable recovery and precision, low prediction errors and bias values close to zero were considered indicative of satisfactory model performance.

Accuracy

Accuracy was evaluated by recovery studies performed at three concentration levels within the working range of each analyte. Each level was analyzed in triplicate, and the percentage recovery (%R) was calculated.

For pharmaceutical formulations, recoveries within 98–102% were considered acceptable according to ICH Q2(R1). For plasma analysis, acceptable limits were 85–115% of nominal concentration and 80–120% at the lower limit of quantification, in accordance with U.S. Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency bioanalytical validation guidance.

Precision

Precision was assessed as repeatability (intra-day precision) and intermediate precision (inter-day precision), expressed as %RSD.

Repeatability was determined by analyzing three replicate samples at each concentration level within the same day. Intermediate precision was evaluated by repeating the analysis on three separate days.

For pharmaceutical applications, %RSD values not exceeding 2.0% were considered acceptable. For plasma analysis, the acceptance criterion was 15%.

Sensitivity

LOD and LOQ were estimated from NAS-based sensitivity calculations and were used to evaluate the ability of the developed models to detect and quantify low concentrations of the studied analytes.

Robustness

Robustness was evaluated by introducing small deliberate variations in instrumental settings, including wavelength interval, slit width, and scanning speed. Additional robustness testing was performed by applying minor changes to spectral preprocessing parameters, specifically the Savitzky–Golay smoothing window size and baseline-correction settings. The influence of analyst-to-analyst variation, day-to-day instrumental drift, and independent sample preparation was also assessed. For plasma samples, the robustness study further included slight variations in the protein precipitation and reconstitution procedures. The method was considered robust when these variations did not produce significant changes in the predicted concentrations and the resulting %RSD values remained within the predefined acceptance limits.

Assessment of model overfitting

To evaluate the possibility of chance correlation, Y-scrambling was performed by randomly permuting the concentration vectors 100 times while keeping the spectral data unchanged. The complete FA-PLS workflow, including spectral preprocessing, wavelength selection, latent-variable optimization, and model construction, was repeated for each permuted dataset.

Analysis of pharmaceutical dosage forms

Five tablets were accurately weighed and finely powdered using a mortar and pestle to obtain a homogeneous mixture. An accurately weighed portion of the powder, equivalent to the average weight of one tablet, was quantitatively transferred into a 100 mL volumetric flask. Approximately 50 mL of ethanol was added, and the mixture was subjected to ultrasonic agitation for 15 min to ensure complete extraction of the analytes. The solution was then diluted to volume with ethanol and thoroughly mixed to achieve uniformity. Appropriate aliquots were subsequently withdrawn and further diluted with ethanol to obtain final working concentrations within the validated linearity ranges of LOS, RAP, and HCD, while preserving their pharmaceutical ratio and ensuring optimal spectrophotometric response. The prepared solutions were filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filters to remove any insoluble excipients and obtain clear solutions suitable for analysis. UV–visible absorption spectra were recorded over the range of 200–400 nm using ethanol as a blank. The obtained spectral data were processed using the developed FA-PLS chemometric model for the simultaneous determination of the studied analytes. Method accuracy was assessed through recovery studies using the standard addition technique at four concentration levels (80%, 90%, 110%, and 120% of the nominal concentration), each performed in triplicate. Precision was evaluated in terms of %RSD, while accuracy was expressed as %R.

Plasma matrix application

Preparation of plasma calibration and validation samples

Matrix-matched calibration and validation samples were prepared using drug-free human plasma to account for the spectral contribution of endogenous matrix components. Separate calibration and external validation sets were generated for the plasma model, comprising 25 calibration mixtures and 16 validation mixtures, respectively. These samples were prepared independently of the pharmaceutical sets to ensure that model development and performance assessment reflected the characteristics of the biological matrix.

Blank pooled human plasma, obtained as a commercially available material from VACSERA (Giza, Egypt), was used for all bioanalytical experiments. The plasma was stored at − 20 °C until use. Before sample preparation, aliquots were allowed to thaw at room temperature and mixed thoroughly to restore homogeneity.

Appropriate volumes of the working solutions of LOS, HCD, RAP, NDA, and TLN were added to plasma aliquots to obtain the concentration levels specified in Table 1. Blank plasma samples were analyzed prior to spiking to confirm the absence of interfering spectral features within the selected wavelength region.

Sample processing and clean-up

Plasma samples were processed using a protein precipitation technique prior to spectrophotometric analysis. An aliquot of 1.0 mL of each fortified plasma sample was transferred to a centrifuge tube, and 3.0 mL of acetonitrile was added to induce precipitation of plasma proteins.

The mixture was vortex-mixed for 1 min and centrifuged at 4000× g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was carefully separated and transferred to clean glass tubes. The solvent was evaporated under a gentle stream of nitrogen at 40 °C until dryness.

The residue was reconstituted in 1.0 mL of ethanol, and the solution was passed through a 0.22 µm syringe filter to remove any remaining particulates. The clarified extract was then subjected to UV spectral measurement over the wavelength range used for model development.

This procedure was applied uniformly to all plasma calibration standards, validation samples, and assay samples to ensure consistent matrix treatment throughout the study.

Application for real samples

The validated FA-PLS method was applied to fortified human plasma samples containing therapeutically relevant concentrations of LOS, RAP, HCD, NDA, and TLN in order to evaluate its performance under biologically relevant matrix conditions. The obtained results demonstrated that the proposed methodology is capable of providing accurate and precise quantification in the presence of residual plasma interferences, supporting the feasibility of the approach for biological sample analysis.

All bioanalytical validation parameters were evaluated independently for the plasma matrix to confirm the accuracy, precision, and predictive performance of the developed model. These findings establish the applicability of the proposed methodology as a proof-of-concept analytical tool for spiked plasma samples and complement its demonstrated utility for pharmaceutical quality control and impurity screening.

Results and discussion

Green-solvent selection

Green solvent selection tool (GSST)

To support environmentally responsible method development, solvent selection was guided by sustainability-oriented evaluation rather than empirical preference. A structured solvent screening approach based on the GSST model proposed by Larsen et al. was employed to rank candidate solvents according to health risk, safety, environmental impact, and waste management considerations. Seven solvents covering a wide polarity range (water, ethanol, methanol, acetonitrile, ethyl acetate, hexane, and chloroform) were assessed [28]. The generated greenness scores (Fig. S2a) indicated superior sustainability profiles for water, ethanol, ethyl acetate, methanol, and acetonitrile compared with chlorinated and aliphatic hydrocarbons. Acetonitrile achieved the highest composite score (G = 7.3), followed by water (6.7), ethyl acetate (6.6), ethanol (5.8), and methanol (5.8), whereas chloroform ranked lowest (G = 4.4) due to unfavorable toxicological and environmental attributes.

Based on this preliminary sustainability ranking, greener solvents were shortlisted for further analytical evaluation, aligning solvent selection with Green Analytical Chemistry principles.

Spider diagram for assessment of the greenness index (SDAGI)

To complement numerical solvent ranking, qualitative greenness profiling was performed using the SDAGI tool, which evaluates solvents through Safety, Health, and Environmental (SHE) criteria derived from SDS data [29, 30]. Greenness attributes—including hazard potential, flammability, odor, stability, and environmental persistence—were visualized using spider diagrams (Fig. S2b).

Ethanol and ethyl acetate demonstrated the most favorable safety–environmental balance, with mean greenness indices of 1.33 and 1.44, respectively, whereas methanol and acetonitrile exhibited lower scores due to higher toxicity and handling risks (Table S1).

Subsequent spectroscopic testing revealed that ethanol provided superior absorbance intensity and spectral resolution for all analytes. Considering both environmental compatibility and analytical performance, ethanol was selected as the optimal solvent for further spectrophotometric analysis.

Spectral features and chemometric resolution strategy

The absorption spectra of LOS, HCD, RAP, NDA, and TLN are shown in (Fig. 2). Extensive overlap was observed throughout the ultraviolet region, particularly below 300 nm, making direct single-wavelength quantification unsuitable. Despite this overlap, the spectra are not identical. Each compound exhibits subtle but reproducible differences in band shape, width, and relative absorbance intensity that can be exploited using multivariate calibration techniques. The observed spectral behavior is governed by the electronic properties of the molecular chromophores. LOS and RAP contain substituted aromatic systems linked to carbonyl-bearing functional groups, generating strong π → π* transitions in the deep-UV region together with weaker n → π* contributions. HCD possesses a benzothiadiazine scaffold with sulfonamide substituents, which broadens the absorption profile and shifts part of the signal toward longer wavelengths. TLN exhibits the relatively simple absorption pattern typical of a mono-substituted aromatic hydrocarbon, whereas NDA produces a narrower band associated primarily with transitions involving the nitroso group. These structural differences result in analyte-specific spectral fingerprints, even when the overall profiles appear highly superimposed.

Although some analytes display maximum absorbance at wavelengths below 230 nm, this portion of the spectrum was excluded from model development because it showed increased baseline instability, stronger overlap, and lower effective selectivity. After preliminary evaluation, the interval from 220 to 350 nm was selected as the working spectral window because it provided the best compromise between signal intensity and discriminating power.

From a theoretical perspective, quantification of a target analyte in the presence of overlapping components can be interpreted using the Net Analyte Signal (NAS) concept [31]. NAS corresponds to the portion of the measured response that is unique to the analyte of interest after removing the contribution of all other constituents. This framework is widely used to explain selectivity, sensitivity, and the effective information content available for multivariate calibration in complex spectroscopic systems. Although NAS vectors were not explicitly calculated in the present work, the same analytical principle underlies the proposed methodology. The Firefly Algorithm was used to identify wavelength subsets that enrich analyte-specific information and suppress variables dominated by common background variation, thereby increasing the effective contribution of the target signal to the calibration model. Similar NAS-based strategies have recently been applied to UV–visible spectroscopic analysis of complex pharmaceutical formulations, confirming the continuing relevance of this theoretical framework in modern chemometric spectroscopy.

The selected spectral region initially contained 131 wavelength variables recorded at 1 nm intervals. This dataset served as the input for the FA-PLS procedure. By combining chemically meaningful wavelength selection with latent variable modeling, the method exploits subtle covariance patterns associated with each analyte rather than relying solely on absolute absorbance values. As a result, compounds with strongly overlapping spectra can be resolved with high predictive accuracy and improved model interpretability.

Dual-matrix-specific chemometric modeling framework

The spectral environment encountered in a pharmaceutical formulation differs fundamentally from that of a biological sample. Tablet extracts are composed primarily of the target analytes and formulation excipients, whereas plasma contains proteins, lipids, and endogenous metabolites that contribute additional background absorbance and may alter the covariance structure of the recorded spectra. Developing a single calibration model for both matrices would therefore increase the risk of incorporating matrix-dependent variation unrelated to analyte concentration.

To address this challenge, two independent FA-PLS models were constructed: one dedicated to pharmaceutical dosage forms and a second specifically optimized for human plasma. Each model was developed using spectral data generated under matrix-matched conditions so that wavelength selection, latent variable optimization, and performance assessment were tailored to the analytical characteristics of the corresponding ssample type.

This matrix-specific strategy provided several analytical advantages. First, it enabled the Firefly Algorithm to select wavelength regions that were most informative within each matrix while excluding variables affected by excipient interference or endogenous plasma absorbance. Second, the number of latent variables was optimized independently, allowing model complexity to reflect the underlying spectral heterogeneity of each application. Third, validation statistics such as recovery, RMSEP, and precision were obtained separately for the two matrices, ensuring that analytical performance was evaluated under realistic operating conditions. Finally, the plasma-specific model was trained to distinguish analyte signals from the spectral contribution of residual biological components remaining after sample preparation.

For consistency, both matrices were represented by the same experimental design structure, comprising 25 calibration mixtures and 16 external validation mixtures generated within identical concentration ranges. Nevertheless, model training, variable selection, and validation were performed independently for each dataset. This dual-matrix modeling framework enabled the proposed methodology to accommodate the distinct spectral characteristics of pharmaceutical and biological samples while preserving optimal predictive performance and minimizing matrix-induced bias.

Construction of the calibration set

Development of a reliable multivariate calibration model requires a training set that spans the concentration space in a balanced and statistically informative manner. To achieve this objective, the calibration mixtures were generated using a five-level multifactor design following the strategy described by Brereton and co-workers [23]. This approach was selected because it systematically distributes analyte concentrations while minimizing inter-variable correlation, thereby improving the ability of the chemometric model to distinguish the spectral contribution of each component.

For each of the five studied analytes, concentration levels were assigned using coded values of − 2, − 1, 0, + 1, and + 2, where the central level represented the midpoint of the working range. The corresponding real concentrations are summarized in (Table 1). Combining these levels according to the experimental design produced 25 calibration mixtures for each analytical matrix.

The principal advantage of this design lies in the generation of composition profiles that vary independently across the studied factors. Such orthogonality reduces redundancy within the calibration matrix and improves the numerical stability of the resulting multivariate model. In practical terms, this design enhances analyte discrimination, supports more accurate estimation of regression coefficients, and lowers the likelihood of model overfitting.

An additional benefit of the structured design is experimental efficiency. By extracting maximal analytical information from a limited number of mixtures, the design reduces solvent consumption, preparation time, and chemical waste compared with empirical trial-and-error approaches. Consequently, the calibration strategy contributes not only to improved model robustness but also to the sustainability objectives of Green Analytical Chemistry and White Analytical Chemistry.

Generation of the external validation set by Sobol sampling

External validation is essential for assessing the predictive capability of chemometric models under conditions independent of calibration. The quality of this assessment depends strongly on how validation samples are distributed within the multidimensional concentration domain. Random sample selection may lead to clustering or underrepresentation of specific regions, resulting in overly optimistic or statistically biased estimates of model performance.

To overcome these limitations, the external validation mixtures were generated using Sobol quasi-random sequences. This low-discrepancy sampling method produces points that are distributed more uniformly throughout the design space than conventional pseudo-random approaches, ensuring representative coverage of all concentration combinations.

For each matrix, 16 validation mixtures were generated within the same concentration limits used for calibration, as listed in (Table 1). The algorithm was configured to minimize concentration collinearity while maximizing spatial dispersion across the multidimensional domain. FA-PLS model development and spectral variable optimization

The FA-PLS strategy was used to identify the wavelength variables that contributed most effectively to quantitative prediction before construction of the final calibration models. In this hybrid framework, FA performed global optimization of wavelength subsets, whereas PLS was used to evaluate the predictive performance of each candidate subset. The optimization objective was to minimize RMSECV while eliminating variables that contributed primarily to noise, redundant information, or matrix background.

For each optimization run, FA generated a series of binary wavelength masks, and a PLS model was built from the variables selected in each mask. The fitness of every candidate solution was calculated from the corresponding RMSECV obtained by leave-one-out cross-validation. Iterative movement of the fireflies progressively improved the fitness values until convergence was achieved. The final FA operating parameters are summarized in Table S2.

To evaluate the stability of the optimization process, each FA-PLS model was generated through 20 independent runs initiated with different random seeds. The wavelength subset yielding the lowest RMSECV was selected as the final solution. The frequency with which each wavelength was selected across the repeated runs is presented in Fig. S1. The high recurrence of the same spectral regions confirms that the optimization was reproducible and not significantly affected by stochastic variation.

Application of FA substantially reduced the dimensionality of the spectral matrix. Relative to the original set of 131 wavelength variables, the number of retained variables decreased by 85%, 81%, 79%, 89%, and 82% for LOS, RAP, HCD, NDA, and TLN, respectively. The selected wavelength regions are illustrated in Figs. 7 and 8. The predictive ability of the developed FA-PLS models was evaluated using independent external validation sets comprising 16 mixtures for each analytical matrix. To further assess the possibility of chance correlation, Y-scrambling was performed by randomly permuting the concentration vectors 100 times while keeping the spectral matrix unchanged. The complete FA-PLS modeling workflow was repeated for each permuted dataset using the same preprocessing, wavelength selection, and model optimization procedures. As shown in Fig. S3, all permuted models yielded substantially lower R2 values than the corresponding original models, confirming that the predictive performance of the developed models did not arise from random correlations or overfitting.

Applications

Pharmaceutical assay

The validated FA-PLS method was successfully applied to the analysis of commercial fixed-dose combination tablets containing LOS, RAP, and HCD. No interference was observed from common excipients, including microcrystalline cellulose, lactose monohydrate, croscarmellose sodium, magnesium stearate, polyvinylpyrrolidone, talc, and film-coating materials.

Method accuracy in real samples was further verified using the standard addition technique. Recoveries obtained for LOS, RAP, HCD, NDA, and TLN ranged from 99.52 to 99.94%, with %RSD values below 1.0%, confirming excellent accuracy and precision in pharmaceutical matrices.

The method also demonstrated adequate sensitivity for monitoring NDA and TLN, with LOD values ranging from 0.1628 to 1.3485 µg/mL. These results support the suitability of the proposed methodology for routine pharmaceutical quality control and impurity screening. The corresponding assay results are summarized in Table S5.

Comparative evaluation with established analytical methodologies

To place the proposed method in an appropriate analytical context, its performance and practical attributes were compared with established techniques commonly used for multicomponent pharmaceutical analysis, including conventional UV-chemometric methods and chromatographic approaches such as HPLC-UV and LC–MS/MS [33–36]. While the Introduction outlines the historical development of chemometric spectrophotometry, the present section focuses on analytical performance, operational considerations, and sustainability characteristics relevant to routine practice.

Chromatographic techniques remain the preferred tools for confirmatory impurity analysis because they provide physical separation, high selectivity, and, in the case of LC–MS/MS, structural information. These capabilities make them particularly suitable for ultra-trace quantification, metabolite identification, and regulatory investigations. However, these advantages are associated with increased solvent consumption, longer analysis times, higher instrumentation and operating costs, and a greater environmental burden.

UV spectrophotometry coupled with chemometric modeling offers a separation-free alternative for resolving severely overlapping spectral signals. Conventional multivariate methods such as PLS, PCR, and CLS have been widely applied for this purpose, although many rely on full-spectrum calibration and may therefore be more susceptible to noise, multicollinearity, and matrix-related variability.

In the present study, the FA-PLS framework incorporates algorithm-guided wavelength selection prior to multivariate calibration, allowing the model to focus on the most informative spectral variables while excluding non-contributory regions. This strategy improved predictive efficiency while preserving the operational simplicity, speed, and low solvent consumption inherent to UV spectrophotometry.

The developed methodology enables the simultaneous determination of LOS, RAP, and HCD together with NDA and TLN in both pharmaceutical formulations and human plasma. To the best of our knowledge, no previous UV spectrophotometric chemometric method has addressed this combination of active ingredients and impurities within a single analytical workflow.

A comparative overview of analytical performance, sensitivity, operational complexity, cost, and sustainability characteristics is provided in Table S7. As summarized therein, chromatographic methods offer superior sensitivity and confirmatory capability, whereas the proposed FA-PLS approach provides a favorable balance between analytical performance, simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and environmental compatibility.

Accordingly, the proposed methodology is intended as a complementary analytical tool rather than a replacement for chromatographic confirmatory techniques. Its principal advantage lies in combining simultaneous multicomponent quantification and impurity screening within a rapid, economical, and environmentally sustainable spectrophotometric framework suitable for routine analytical applications.

Scope of applicability and method limitations

While the proposed FA-PLS chemometric spectrophotometric platform demonstrated robust performance for multicomponent quantification and impurity monitoring, several limitations should be acknowledged.

The analytical sensitivity achievable with spectrophotometric chemometrics is inherently lower than that attainable with separation-based hyphenated techniques. Consequently, although the combination of FA-guided wavelength selection and PLS modeling enabled effective resolution of highly overlapping spectra and selective extraction of impurity-related information, ultra-trace confirmatory determination of regulatory-critical impurities remains beyond the principal scope of the proposed methodology.

In addition, the plasma application presented in this study was performed using fortified drug-free human plasma samples and should therefore be regarded as a proof-of-concept evaluation of the method under biologically relevant matrix conditions. The current work did not include incurred clinical samples, metabolite interference studies, or expanded selectivity assessment against concomitant medications. These investigations were beyond the scope of the present study and will be addressed in future work to further establish the suitability of the method for routine bioanalytical applications and therapeutic drug monitoring.

Accordingly, the developed methodology is best positioned as a rapid and environmentally sustainable tool for multicomponent quantification, impurity screening, and stability monitoring in pharmaceutical analysis. Chromatographic techniques coupled with mass spectrometric detection, such as LC–MS/MS and GC–MS, remain the preferred approaches when ultra-trace sensitivity, structural confirmation, or full regulatory bioanalytical characterization is required.

Conclusion

This study presents a dual-matrix chemometric spectrophotometric framework for the simultaneous determination of LOS, RAP, HCD, and the associated impurities NDA and TLN in pharmaceutical formulations and fortified human plasma samples. The methodology integrates FA-PLS modeling with Sobol quasi-random sampling to enhance wavelength selection, model robustness, and the reliability of external validation.

The developed models demonstrated excellent analytical performance in both matrices, providing high correlation coefficients, low prediction errors, accurate recoveries, and satisfactory precision in accordance with recognized pharmaceutical and bioanalytical validation criteria.

Sustainability assessment using the Multicolor Assessment tool, carbon footprint estimation, and the (NQS) index confirmed the favorable environmental profile of the proposed methodology, which benefits from minimal solvent consumption, reduced waste generation, and simplified instrumentation compared with conventional chromatographic techniques.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous UV spectrophotometric chemometric method has been reported for the simultaneous determination of LOS, RAP, and HCD in the presence of both NDA and TLN within a single analytical workflow without a prior separation step.

The plasma application was performed using fortified drug-free human plasma samples and should therefore be regarded as a proof-of-concept demonstration of the applicability of the proposed methodology to biological matrices. Additional studies involving incurred clinical samples and expanded selectivity assessment would be required before routine bioanalytical or therapeutic drug monitoring applications can be established.

Although chromatographic techniques remain indispensable for ultra-trace confirmatory analysis, the proposed methodology provides a rapid, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable alternative for routine multicomponent quantification and impurity screening.

Overall, this work demonstrates how the integration of structured experimental design, intelligent wavelength selection, and comprehensive sustainability assessment can expand the analytical abilities of UV-spectrophotometry and support the development of high-performance green analytical methodologies for pharmaceutical applications.
