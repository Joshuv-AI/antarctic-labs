Long-term outcomes of fertility preservation counseling in women of reproductive age prior to gonadotoxic therapy
Authors: Inha Lee, Euna Choi, Jin Kyung Baek, Hyemin Park, Heeyon Kim, Bo Hyon Yun, Jae Hoon Lee, SiHyun Cho, Young Sik Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs survival rates for reproductive-aged women with cancer improve, the importance of fertility preservation (FP) prior to gonadotoxic chemotherapy has increased. This study evaluated long-term FP outcomes by comparing return rates and clinical pregnancy rates across FP strategies —oocyte and embryo cryopreservation and GnRH agonist use—and analyzed longitudinal ovarian reserve changes via serum anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH).ResultsAmong 399 women analyzed, breast cancer was the most common diagnosis (72.4%). Serum AMH declined profoundly at 6 months post-chemotherapy (3.07 to 0.08 ng/mL, p < 0.01), with partial recovery at 12 months (0.30 ng/mL), remaining significantly below baseline throughout 60-month follow-up. Longitudinal AMH data were available only for GnRH agonist-treated patients, precluding direct comparison with untreated patients. The return rate for pregnancy counseling was highest in the embryo cryopreservation group (63.4%). Total CPR among returning patients were significantly higher in the oocyte cryopreservation (70.5%) and embryo cryopreservation (69.2%) groups versus the counseling-only group (28.5%) (p = 0.020 and p = 0.014, respectively). Notably, all cryopreservation patients also received GnRH agonists; comparisons therefore reflect GnRH agonist plus cryopreservation versus GnRH agonist alone versus no active FP. GnRH agonist use alone did not show a significant advantage over counseling only.ConclusionsChemotherapy is associated with a substantial and lasting reduction in ovarian reserve despite GnRH agonist co-treatment. Oocyte and embryo cryopreservation were associated with significantly higher CPR compared with counseling or GnRH agonist use alone, though causal inference is precluded by the retrospective design. The independent effect of GnRH agonists on ovarian reserve could not be fully assessed due to the absence of a GnRH agonist-naive control group with longitudinal follow-up. Cancer patients represent a distinct FP population with higher return rates than social FP cohorts, underscoring the importance of cancer-specific evidence for counseling. These findings support early FP counseling and suggest cryopreservation offers reproductive benefits beyond GnRH agonist use alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02160-4.

Background

According to the Korea Central Cancer Registry, approximately 27,000 new cancer cases are diagnosed annually in women of reproductive age (20–49 years), representing nearly one-quarter of all female cancer cases [1, 2]. Breast cancer is the most common malignancy in this population (24.6%), followed by cervical cancer, endometrial cancer, thyroid cancer, colorectal cancer, and hematologic malignancies [2, 3]. Advances in early detection and treatment have markedly improved survival; the 5-year survival rate for breast cancer reached 94.3% in 2024 [3, 4]. As survival improves, more reproductive-aged women wish to pursue pregnancy after treatment, underscoring the importance of fertility preservation (FP) prior to gonadotoxic therapy [5].

Chemotherapy has well-documented gonadotoxic effects, leading to diminished ovarian reserve, premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), early menopause, and infertility [6, 7]. In breast cancer, the incidence of POI within one year after chemotherapy is approximately 30.9%, with higher rates in older women and in those exposed to higher cumulative doses of alkylating agents [8]. To mitigate these risks, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) and the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) recommend that reproductive-aged women undergoing gonadotoxic treatment receive FP counseling and be offered appropriate options [9–11]. Established FP strategies include oocyte cryopreservation, embryo cryopreservation, and ovarian tissue cryopreservation [5, 6, 12]. In breast cancer specifically, administration of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists during chemotherapy has been shown to reduce the risk of POI in selected settings, although evidence in other malignancies is limited [7–9].

Despite these recommendations, data on FP outcomes—particularly subsequent pregnancy attempts and clinical pregnancy rates(CPR)—remain insufficient. Most reports, both domestic and international, are single-center studies with small sample sizes and lack comparative analyses by FP method [12–14]. For example, a Korean single-center study described FP outcomes without detailed pregnancy attempt or CPR data [12], while another reported pregnancy attempts and successful pregnancies but did not compare CPR by FP method [13]. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate FP outcomes in reproductive-aged women scheduled for chemotherapy by comparing CPR according to FP option. Additionally, we analyzed changes in serum AMH levels according to GnRH agonist administration. The goal is to provide clinical evidence to inform FP counseling and management in female cancer patients.

Methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at Severance Hospital, Yonsei University College of Medicine. From January 2016 to December 2019, a total of 545 women were referred to the FP clinic with the diagnosis of “encounter for fertility preservation counseling.” Patients were excluded if FP counseling was performed for POI, decreased ovarian reserve (DOR), or social reasons, or if malignancy was ruled out after subsequent histopathological examination despite initial suspicion of cancer. Women with a prior history of chemotherapy, as well as those who were transferred to another hospital after FP counseling with no available medical records, were also excluded. After applying these criteria, 399 women were included in the final analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Severance Hospital, Yonsei University College of Medicine (IRB No. 4-2024-1486), and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective review of medical records.

For each participant, the following data were collected: age at cancer diagnosis, body mass index (BMI), age at menarche, marital status, parity, history of adnexal surgery, cancer diagnosis, chemotherapy regimen and treatment duration, and serum anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels before chemotherapy and at 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months after treatment. In women who underwent oocyte or embryo cryopreservation, the number of stimulation cycles, retrieved oocytes, and metaphase II (MII)/two-pronuclear (2PN) oocytes or embryos were recorded. In addition, information was obtained regarding return visits to the FP clinic after completion of chemotherapy for pregnancy counseling, attempts to conceive, and pregnancy outcomes.

The study population (n = 399) was stratified according to the chosen FP option into four groups: counseling only (n = 99), GnRH agonist only (n = 158), oocyte cryopreservation (n = 94), embryo cryopreservation (n = 47), and ovarian tissue cryopreservation (n = 1). Although current guidelines (ASRM, ESHRE) classify GnRH agonists as an adjunctive ovarian protection strategy rather than a primary FP method [8, 9], we included GnRH agonist administration as a separate group in this study because it represented a distinct clinical pathway chosen by patients in lieu of cryopreservation. Importantly, all patients in the oocyte cryopreservation (OC) and embryo cryopreservation (EC) groups also received GnRH agonists concurrently during chemotherapy. Therefore, the comparisons in this study effectively contrast: (1) no active FP intervention (counseling only), (2) GnRH agonist use without cryopreservation (GnRH agonist only), and (3) GnRH agonist use combined with cryopreservation (OC or EC groups). This overlap in GnRH agonist exposure across groups should be considered when interpreting between-group differences in reproductive outcomes. Additionally, CPR was calculated among patients who returned to the fertility clinic with a desire to conceive, rather than the total initial cohort per group, reflecting treatment-seeking behavior and motivation to conceive after oncologic therapy. This denominator-based approach and its implications for interpretation are discussed further in the limitations.

Fertility preservation counseling technique

At the initial FP consultation, serum AMH was measured to assess ovarian reserve. Patients were counseled on the gonadotoxicity of the planned chemotherapy regimen, with specific discussion of the risks of diminished ovarian reserve, POI, and infertility. Available FP options—including oocyte cryopreservation, embryo cryopreservation, ovarian tissue cryopreservation, and GnRH agonist administration—were explained along with their limitations, costs, and time requirements. Serum AMH was reassessed 6 months after chemotherapy and at 6-month intervals thereafter, and menstrual status, pregnancy attempts, and pregnancy outcomes were documented. The 6-month post-treatment timepoint was selected as the initial reassessment because most chemotherapy regimens in this cohort were completed within 6 months, allowing for an early evaluation of acute gonadotoxic injury before potential recovery [16]. Although some studies assess ovarian reserve at 12 months post-treatment—when partial recovery may have occurred—we chose 6 months to capture the nadir of ovarian reserve suppression, which is clinically relevant for informing subsequent fertility management [14]. Importantly, AMH was also measured at 12 months and beyond to characterize longitudinal recovery patterns.

Controlled Ovarian Stimulation (COS)

All COS cycles were performed using a GnRH antagonist protocol with either recombinant FSH or hMG, initiated via a random-start or conventional start. Stimulation doses were individualized according to age, AMH, and AFC. When the leading follicle reached 13–14 mm, a GnRH antagonist was introduced to prevent premature LH surge. Final oocyte maturation was triggered when ≥ 2 dominant follicles measured ≥ 18 mm, using r-hCG, a GnRH agonist, or a dual trigger. Oocyte retrieval was performed 34–36 h later. In patients with breast cancer or estrogen-sensitive tumors, letrozole was co-administered from the start of stimulation until 7 days after oocyte retrieval. Mature oocytes were cryopreserved for unmarried women, whereas married women underwent embryo cryopreservation.

Frozen-Thawed Embryo Transfer (FTET)

FTET was performed either in natural cycles, with ovulation triggered when the dominant follicle reached 18–20 mm, or in hormone replacement cycles, where estradiol was started on cycle days 2–3 and progesterone supplementation was added once the endometrium was ≥ 7 mm. Embryo transfer was scheduled according to the timing of progesterone administration. Hormonal support was continued until 7–9 weeks of gestation if pregnancy was achieved.

GnRH agonist administration

GnRH agonists (leuprorelin or triptorelin) were initiated approximately 7 days before chemotherapy. If the interval was < 7 days, concurrent GnRH agonist and antagonist were administered for one week. Thereafter, GnRH agonists were administered at 4-week intervals until completion of chemotherapy.

Ethics

The Institutional Review Board of the hospital where this study took place (approval no. 4-2024-1486) approved this study. Given the retrospective design and complete anonymization of patient information, the need for informed consent was waived by the institutional review board.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were demonstrated as medians with interquartile range (25th percentile–75th percentile) and categorical variables were demonstrated as “n (%)” in the tables. To compare unpaired values between groups, we used either a Student’s t-test when distributions passed the normality test or the Mann–Whitney test if distributions were not normal. To compare proportions between groups, according to sample size, either χ2 or Fisher’s exact (n < 5) tests were performed.

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 28.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The number of women referred for fertility preservation counseling prior to gonadotoxic therapy increased steadily during the study period, from 49 cases in 2016 to 196 cases in 2019 (Fig. 1). Among the 399 women included in the analysis, 383 (95.9%) had a diagnosis of cancer, 4 (1.0%) required chemotherapy for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and 12 (3.0%) required chemotherapy for other conditions, including β-thalassemia, hypogammaglobulinemia, invasive thymoma, hemangioma, Churg–Strauss syndrome, polyarteritis nodosa, livedo vasculitis, fibromatosis, and leukocytoclastic vasculitis (Table 1). Breast cancer accounted for the majority of cancer cases (n = 289, 72.4%), followed by hematologic malignancies, gastrointestinal cancers, and gynecologic cancers.

Fertility preservation counseling outcomes

Of the 399 patients, 99 (24.8%) underwent counseling only without any FP intervention, and 158 (39.6%) received GnRH agonist administration during chemotherapy. 94 (23.6%) chose oocyte cryopreservation, 47 (11.8%) chose embryo cryopreservation, and 1 (0.3%) opted for ovarian tissue cryopreservation (Table 2). The extremely low uptake of ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTx) reflects the strict eligibility criteria applied at our institution during the study period: OTx was offered only to patients who could not undergo controlled ovarian stimulation due to time constraints, and was contraindicated in patients with hematologic malignancies due to the risk of reintroducing malignant cells at the time of reimplantation. As a result, OTx was performed in only one patient and was excluded from comparative outcome analyses. Excluding one patient who underwent ovarian tissue cryopreservation, 398 patients were stratified into four groups. All patients in the oocyte cryopreservation (OC) and embryo cryopreservation (EC) groups also received GnRH agonists during chemotherapy. The mean age significantly differed among the four groups (p < 0.001), with patients in the counseling-only and embryo cryopreservation groups being slightly older than those in the GnRH agonist and oocyte cryopreservation groups. In contrast, body mass index (BMI) did not differ significantly among the groups (p = 0.35).

Changes in serum AMH

Among patients who received GnRH agonists during chemotherapy—encompassing those in the GnRH agonist-only, oocyte cryopreservation, and embryo cryopreservation groups—serum AMH levels were significantly reduced after treatment compared with pre-treatment levels (Table 3). Among the 299 patients who received GnRH agonists during chemotherapy, pre-chemotherapy AMH levels were available for 269 patients (89.9%), as serum AMH measurement prior to treatment initiation was not performed in all cases—primarily in patients referred after chemotherapy had already commenced or in those for whom the test was not clinically ordered at the time of initial consultation. Longitudinal AMH analyses were therefore conducted in these 269 patients with available baseline data. It should be noted that longitudinal AMH data were not available for the counseling-only group, as most of these patients did not return to the gynecologic clinic for follow-up after completing chemotherapy, having chosen not to pursue active FP intervention. Consequently, this analysis does not include a GnRH agonist-naive control group, and the independent effect of GnRH agonist administration on AMH trajectory cannot be directly assessed. The median pre-chemotherapy AMH level was 3.07 ng/mL (range, 0.01–21.52). At 6 months post-chemotherapy, AMH declined to 0.08 ng/mL (range, 0.01–9.48), representing a significant reduction (p < 0.01). From 12 months post-chemotherapy onward, AMH levels were significantly higher than the 6-month values (p < 0.01); at 12 months, the median AMH level was 0.30 ng/mL (range, 0.01–9.14). Thereafter, no statistically significant changes were observed at subsequent follow-up time points (24 months: p = 0.273; 36 months: p = 0.052; 48 months: p = 0.209; 60 months: p = 0.075).

Clinical pregnancy rates by group

Clinical pregnancy was defined as the presence of a gestational sac confirmed by transvaginal ultrasonography. Of the 399 women included in the study, a subset did not return to our institution after completing chemotherapy and were lost to follow-up; the denominators therefore reflect only patients with available post-treatment data (Table 4). The rate of return to the fertility clinic for pregnancy counseling after completion of chemotherapy was highest in the EC group (26/41, 63.4%), followed by the OC group (17/77, 22.3%), GnRH agonist group (23/101, 22.8%), and counseling-only group (14/58, 24.1%) (p < 0.001). The median time interval from completion of oncologic therapy to return was 13.1 months (IQR 3.2–27.6) in the counseling-only group, 15.0 months (IQR 6.6–27.7) in the GnRH agonist group, 10.5 months (IQR 4.9–24.7) in the OC group, and 6.9 months (IQR 3.6–13.8) in the EC group, with no statistically significant difference across groups (p = 0.37). Median age at time of return was 33, 34, 33, and 35.5 years, respectively. Median AMH at time of return was 0.88, 0.29, 1.21, and 0.26 ng/mL, respectively. These data should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of patients with complete follow-up data in each group (Supplementary Table 1).

Natural conception CPR were 43.4% (10/23) in the GnRH agonist group and 41.1% (7/17) in the OC group, compared with 21.4% (3/14) in the counseling-only group and 19.2% (5/26) in the EC group; however, these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.190).

In the OC group, 4/17 (23.5%) attempted IVF with thawed oocytes, and 2 achieved pregnancy (CPR per patient: 50.0%, 2/4; CPR per transfer: 33.3%, 2/6; mean transfers per patient: 1.5). Given the very small number of patients who underwent IVF with thawed oocytes (n = 4), these figures should be interpreted with caution and regarded as preliminary rather than definitive estimates. In the EC group, 18/26 (69.2%) underwent frozen-thawed embryo transfer, resulting in 12 pregnancies (CPR per patient: 66.7%, 12/18; CPR per transfer: 41.3%, 12/29; mean transfers per patient: 1.6). When expressed relative to the total initial cohort per group (i.e., all patients who underwent each FP strategy regardless of return status), the pregnancy rates were: counseling only 4/58 (6.9%), GnRH agonist only 13/101 (12.9%), OC 12/77 (15.6%), and EC 18/41 (43.9%). These cohort-based figures provide a more conservative estimate of reproductive success and account for differences in return rates across groups.

Overall, the total CPR was significantly higher in the OC and EC groups compared with the counseling-only group (OC vs. counseling, p = 0.020; EC vs. counseling, p = 0.014). The GnRH agonist group did not differ significantly from either the counseling-only or cryopreservation groups (GnRH vs. counseling, p = 0.098; GnRH vs. OC, p = 0.364).

The proportion of women requiring fresh IVF–ET after unsuccessful natural conception and depletion of stored oocytes or embryos was highest in the counseling-only group (11/14, 78.6%), followed by the GnRH agonist group (13/23, 56.5%), the OC group (8/17, 47.0%), and the EC group (9/26, 34.6%) (p = 0.008).

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study provides an integrated overview of FP counseling patterns, longitudinal ovarian reserve changes, and reproductive outcomes among reproductive-aged women undergoing gonadotoxic chemotherapy. The substantial increase in FP referrals over four years reflects growing recognition of FP as a standard component of oncologic care, consistent with population-level trends observed in the Netherlands [15], and highlights the expanding demand for evidence-based FP counseling in younger cancer patients for whom fertility is a central survivorship concern.

Serum AMH demonstrated a profound decline within the first six months after chemotherapy, with partial recovery by 12 months and stabilization thereafter, yet values remained markedly lower than baseline—consistent with prior reports of early acute follicular depletion and limited long-term recovery [6, 14, 16]. The role of GnRH agonists as an ovarian protective strategy remains debated. In our cohort, AMH declined significantly in all GnRH agonist-treated patients, suggesting this intervention alone is insufficient to fully preserve ovarian reserve. However, direct comparison with a GnRH agonist-naive control group was not feasible, as counseling-only patients did not return for longitudinal AMH follow-up; thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that decline would have been more profound without GnRH agonist co-treatment. Several randomized controlled trials in breast cancer have reported reduced premature ovarian insufficiency and higher menses resumption rates with GnRH agonist use [8, 17], and discrepancies with our findings likely reflect differences in cancer type, chemotherapy regimen, and outcome measures. GnRH agonists should therefore be regarded as adjunctive rather than primary FP strategies [18].

A major contribution of this study is the comparative assessment of clinical pregnancy rates (CPR) by FP strategy. Both embryo cryopreservation (EC) and oocyte cryopreservation (OC) yielded significantly higher CPR compared with counseling alone [12, 14]. CPR per patient in the EC group was 66.7% and per transfer 41.3%, exceeding rates typically reported in infertile populations undergoing frozen embryo transfer [19, 20]. When recalculated relative to the total initial cohort, the EC group retained the highest rate (43.9%), reinforcing the association between active cryopreservation and favorable outcomes, though causal inference is precluded by the retrospective design. OC yielded a CPR per patient of 50.0% and per transfer of 33.3%, though these figures derive from only four patients and should be interpreted as exploratory. GnRH agonist use alone conferred no significant reproductive advantage over counseling only, underscoring the necessity of gamete or embryo banking for optimal reproductive outcomes [8, 17].

These results are particularly informative when contrasted with social (elective) oocyte cryopreservation. A recent systematic review of more than 13,000 individuals undergoing social egg freezing reported a return rate of only 10.8% and a CPR of 34.2% among those who returned [21]; the Dutch national FP registry similarly reported utilization rates of 12.3% and 25.5% at 5 and 10 years, with CPR around 35% [15]. The markedly higher return rates and CPR in our cancer cohort likely reflect fundamental population differences: women pursuing medically indicated FP face a limited post-treatment reproductive window that motivates earlier return, whereas individuals undergoing social FP often conceive spontaneously or do not return. FP outcomes in cancer patients cannot be extrapolated from social FP data, underscoring the value of cancer-specific evidence for counseling.

These findings carry clear clinical implications. Active cryopreservation should be recommended wherever feasible given its association with substantially higher CPR. Given the rapid AMH decline within six months of chemotherapy initiation, FP counseling must occur promptly at diagnosis. GnRH agonists should not be positioned as a standalone FP strategy, and patients should receive realistic expectations that cryopreservation cannot guarantee future childbearing.

Strengths of this study include a relatively large single-center cohort, comprehensive FP utilization data, long-term AMH follow-up to 60 months, and detailed reproductive outcomes encompassing both natural conception and ART cycles. Limitations are primarily attributable to the retrospective, single-center design, which introduces selection bias; patients referred for FP likely represent a subset with high reproductive motivation and favorable socioeconomic characteristics, potentially overestimating return rates and CPR relative to the broader cancer population. The modest number of patients who attempted pregnancy limited statistical power and precluded multivariable adjustment for key confounders including age, cancer type, and baseline ovarian reserve. Partner availability could not be fully controlled in the EC group. The universal use of GnRH agonists across cryopreservation groups prevented independent assessment of their effect, and the absence of longitudinal AMH data in the counseling-only group precluded direct comparison of ovarian reserve trajectories. CPR denominated by returning patients may overestimate population-level success. Live birth rates could not be systematically ascertained due to deliveries at outside institutions, and cumulative pregnancy rates across multiple ART cycles were incomplete. Future prospective studies with systematic long-term follow-up are needed to address these gaps.

Conclusion

In summary, chemotherapy was associated with a substantial and lasting reduction in ovarian reserve despite GnRH agonist co-treatment, with partial AMH recovery at 12 months. The independent effect of GnRH agonists on ovarian reserve could not be formally evaluated due to the absence of longitudinal AMH data in the counseling-only group. Oocyte and embryo cryopreservation were associated with significantly higher clinical pregnancy rates compared with counseling alone, whereas GnRH agonist administration alone conferred no clear reproductive advantage. Cancer patients represent a distinct FP population with higher return rates and more favorable outcomes than social FP cohorts. These findings support early FP counseling and suggest that cryopreservation-based strategies offer meaningful reproductive benefits in women undergoing gonadotoxic chemotherapy, pending confirmation by prospective studies.
