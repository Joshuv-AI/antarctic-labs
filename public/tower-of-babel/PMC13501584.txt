A comparative cross-sectional study of planned and unplanned pregnancies, their outcomes, predictors, and health-seeking behaviours among women in Odisha, India
Authors: Sakshi, Moonjelly Vijayan Smitha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnplanned pregnancy continues to pose a major challenge to public health, frequently resulting in negative maternal outcomes and delayed healthcare utilization. In developing nations, its occurrence is influenced by restricted access to contraception, cultural and societal norms, and inadequate awareness of reproductive health. This study primarily aimed to assess the prevalence and factors associated with unplanned pregnancies with a comparative group of planned pregnancies. Secondary objectives included exploring pregnancy outcomes and health-seeking behavior in unplanned pregnancies.Methods and materialsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted with 430 ever-married women who conceived in the last three years, recruited using convenient sampling from rural and urban areas of Khordha District, Odisha. Data were collected via face-to-face interviews. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics, and bivariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to identify associated factors, outcomes, and health-seeking behavior in unplanned pregnancies.ResultsNearly one-fourth of women (26%) had unplanned pregnancy, 64% continued their pregnancy, while 36% opted for termination. Age at pregnancy ≥ 30 years, gravidity > 2, birth spacing ≤ 24 months, history of induced abortion, and both induced & spontaneous abortions were factors associated with unplanned pregnancies. Secondary findings indicated that unplanned pregnancies were associated with higher rates of complications during pregnancy and puerperium, as well as delayed Urine Pregnancy Test and Antenatal Care registration.ConclusionUnplanned pregnancies were significantly associated with advanced maternal age, higher gravidity, short birth intervals, and a prior history of abortion. These pregnancies were more likely to result in adverse maternal outcomes and were marked by delayed healthcare utilization, including late antenatal registration and reliance on unsafe abortion methods. Enhancing access to contraception, fostering early engagement with maternal healthcare services, and strengthening reproductive health education are crucial steps toward reducing the incidence and consequences of unplanned pregnancies.Keywords: Induced abortion, Prevalence, Risk factors, Pregnancy outcome, Health behavior

Introduction

Most couples prefer to arrange the time and spacing of their children and avoid unplanned pregnancies for a variety of social and economic reasons. An unplanned pregnancy is either not desired at all (unwanted) or not desired at that specific moment (mistimed) [1]. In developing nations, two-thirds of unplanned pregnancies occur among women not using contraception [2] and reasons for this may be due to fears about side effects, perceived barriers, lack of support from partners or relatives, low self-efficacy, poor communication, ignorance of pregnancy risks, religious beliefs, or limited access to family planning services [3]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in underserved and rural areas, where women often face additional hurdles in accessing reliable contraceptive options and making informed reproductive choices. As a result, unplanned pregnancies may lead to delayed antenatal care (ANC), unsafe abortion practices, and increased emotional distress [4].

Global evidence, including higher-income settings, indicates that the risk of maternal morbidity and mortality is increased when an unplanned pregnancy occurs [5–8]. Unplanned pregnancies are a major global concern for women’s reproductive health, impacting women and their families on physical, economic, and social levels, thus making it a significant public health concern in low and middle-income countries [9].

Although abortion is medically safe when conducted under proper conditions, limited availability of timely, affordable, and equitable services, particularly in low-resource settings, drives many women to seek unsafe alternatives [10].

Unsafe abortion is the most easily avoidable cause of maternal death and a grave public health concern [11]; approximately 42 million women with unplanned pregnancies choose abortion each year, with 20 million of those procedures being unsafe, leading to about 68,000 maternal deaths worldwide [12].

The National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) and other literature indicate that, when compared to other states of India, the acceptability rate of IUCDs and other contraceptives is relatively low in Odisha [13, 14]. Even though the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act legalized abortion in the nation in the early 1970s and promoted family planning for 60 years, studies indicate that unsafe abortion rates are high and that India’s contraceptive prevalence rate is still inadequate, resulting in unplanned pregnancies [15, 16].

“Despite the known risks, there is limited evidence on unplanned pregnancy and maternal health from eastern India [9], particularly Odisha [17] where contraceptive uptake remains low.” To the best of our knowledge, no prior research has specifically explored this issue in Odisha, where low contraceptive acceptance continues to contribute to unplanned pregnancies.

This study primarily aimed to assess the prevalence and factors associated with unplanned pregnancies among women in Khordha district, Odisha, with a comparative group of planned pregnancies. Additionally, secondary objectives included understanding the outcomes of unplanned pregnancies and the health-seeking behaviors of women experiencing unplanned pregnancies.

Materials and methods

Study design

An analytical cross-sectional research design was adopted to primarily assess the prevalence of unplanned pregnancy and its associated factors and, secondarily, to examine pregnancy outcomes and health-seeking behavior.

Study area and period

The study was conducted in the urban and rural villages of the Khordha district, Odisha, from 13th November 2023 to 16th February 2024. There are 10 blocks in Khordha district, of which 2 blocks were selected conveniently due to logistical feasibility and resource constraints. From these 2 blocks, 7 villages (each village has approx. 170–200 couples) and 3 urban sectors (each has approx. 400–470 couples) were selected conveniently for data collection to capture diverse socioeconomic, cultural, and healthcare access characteristics.

Population

The study population included all ever-married women of reproductive age group (15–49 years) who had conceived in the last 3 years and resided in villages of Khordha District, Odisha. Women who were currently pregnant, mute, unwilling to participate, or had a mental illness (self-reported or communicated by family members or documented in previous medical records) were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi version 3 statistical software based on the prevalence of unplanned pregnancy of a previous study [9] (16.9%) by assuming 5% absolute precision and 95% confidence interval. The calculated sample size was 430.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of AIIMS Bhubaneswar (IEC/AIIMS BBSR/Nursing/2023-24/15). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Participants were assured of confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty was respected.

Data collection methods and tools

The data were collected by record review and face-to-face interviews of women using pretested questionnaires prepared by reviewing the literature [1, 9, 18, 19]. A structured questionnaire was constructed to gather information on the demographic and obstetric characteristics of participants. A semi-structured questionnaire was designed to assess pregnancy outcomes with a few questions adopted from previous study [20] to investigate health-seeking behavior in unplanned pregnancy.

Tool validation and reliability

The data collection instruments were pretested among 20 women to assess clarity, feasibility, and reliability. Content validity was established through expert review by eleven experts from Obstetrics at AIIMS Bhubaneswar. The content validity index (CVI) ranged from 0.89 to 0.94 across tools. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with coefficients ranging from 0.83 to 0.98, indicating good internal consistency.

Pilot study

A pilot study was conducted from 16th to 21st October 2023 in two villages of Khordha district with 50 participants to assess feasibility and refine the research process. Each interview lasted approximately 25–30 min.

Data collection procedure

A door-to-door survey was conducted using an interview schedule to collect the data. The Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHA) were identified and contacted through Primary Health Care. The researcher approached the ASHA workers of each selected village to give a roadmap of houses and line-listed the women who had conceived in the last 3 years. The researcher approached 441 women to participate in the study, out of which 11 women refused participation. The most recent pregnancy was considered for women who had two or more pregnancies in the last three years. A rapport was built to create a sense of ease and familiarity. The researcher provided an overview of the study’s purpose and the duration to complete the interview. The researcher explained the study to the patient, obtained voluntary consent, and recorded responses using the pen and paper method. The average duration for interview completion was 25–30 min and no incentive were provided for participation.

Data analysis

The trial version of SPSS 21 was used for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics and frequency percentages were used to describe the sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics. Bivariate logistic regression was used to identify associated factors, outcomes, and health-seeking behavior of unplanned pregnancy, and variables with P < 0.2 were included in the final multiple logistic regression. In multiple logistic regression, variables with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Primary outcome

Prevalence

Most of the women (74%) reported planned pregnancies, while one-fourth (26%) reported unplanned pregnancies in the last three years (Fig. 1). Among women with unplanned pregnancies, two-thirds (64%) continued their pregnancies, while nearly one-third (36%) terminated them (Fig. 2).

Distribution of sociodemographic characteristics

The majority of women with unplanned pregnancies (76.1%) were younger than 30 years, compared to 83.6% women with planned pregnancies, with a statistically significant difference (P = 0.05). Residence patterns indicated that a substantial majority of both groups resided in rural areas, with 77.9% of women with planned pregnancies and a higher (92.0%) of women with unplanned pregnancies residing in rural settings (P < 0.001). Regarding religion, Hindus were the majority in both groups, though a notable increase in the percentage of Muslims among women with unplanned pregnancies (13.3%) compared to women with planned pregnancies (6.3%), which was statistically significant (P = 0.02). Educational attainment did not show significant differences between the groups, as both had similar proportions of graduates and non-graduates (P = 0.6). Employment status revealed only a small percentage of both groups were employed (3.2% planned and 2.7% unplanned) with no significant differences noted (P = 1.0). Income levels were relatively similar between both groups with no significant differences (P = 0.7). Distance from health facilities did not significantly differentiate the two groups (P = 0.2), nor did experiences of domestic violence, which were low in both categories (P = 0.3) (Table 1).

Distribution of obstetric characteristics

Most women with planned pregnancies (76.3%) had previously conceived twice, while a significant number of women with unplanned pregnancies (50.4%) had more than two prior pregnancies (P < 0.001). Most women in both groups had given birth to two or fewer children; the difference was insignificant (P = 0.1). A notable finding is the history of abortion; 47.8% of women with unplanned pregnancies reported having had at least one abortion, compared to only 16.7% of women with planned pregnancies, which was statistically significant (P < 0.001). Additionally, the type of abortions reported indicated a significant prevalence of induced abortions and both induced and spontaneous abortions among women with unplanned pregnancies, with 40.7% having undergone this type compared to only 3.8% among women with planned pregnancies (P < 0.001). Awareness of safe abortion services did not significantly differ between the two groups (P = 0.7). However, birth spacing emerged as a critical factor; women with unplanned pregnancies were more likely to have shorter birth intervals, with 62.8% spacing their births by less than 24 months, which is statistically significant (P < 0.001). Most of the women in both groups had heard about family planning methods, with no significant difference in the use of family planning methods between the two groups (P = 0.5). The decision to use family planning was predominantly mutual between partners in both groups (P = 0.7) (Table 2).

Factors associated with unplanned pregnancy

In this study, women aged 30 years and above had significantly higher odds of experiencing unplanned pregnancies (AOR = 2.4, P = 0.01) compared to those under 30 years. Women with more than two pregnancies also had increased odds (AOR = 3.6, P = 0.01) of unplanned pregnancies compared to those with two or fewer pregnancies. Additionally, women with a birth spacing of 24 months or less had higher odds (AOR = 4.4, P < 0.001) of unplanned pregnancies compared to those with longer birth spacing. The type of abortion history was strongly associated with unplanned pregnancies. Women who had induced abortion and both spontaneous and induced abortions (AOR = 8.7, P < 0.001) had significantly higher odds of unplanned pregnancies, whereas those with a history of only spontaneous abortion had lower but statistically insignificant odds (AOR = 0.4, P = 0.12) compared to those with no history of abortion (Table 3).

Secondary outcome

Maternal and neonatal outcome of unplanned pregnancy

In this study, the likelihood of experiencing complications during pregnancy was 2.1 times higher (P = 0.02), and complications during the puerperium were 3.2 times higher (P = 0.02) among women with unplanned pregnancies compared to women with planned pregnancies. The likelihood of preterm birth (less than 37 weeks) was also significantly higher among women with unplanned pregnancies, with an OR of 3.3 (P = 0.01) (Table 4). Among women who terminated their unplanned pregnancies, most (71%) had no post-termination complications, while a minority experienced adverse outcomes, which are further detailed in the health-seeking behavior section (Fig. 3).

Health-seeking behavior in unplanned pregnancy

In this study, the likelihood of conducting a Urine Pregnancy Test (UPT) after 21 days was 5.8 times higher (P < 0.001) among women with unplanned pregnancies than among women with planned pregnancies. Additionally, delayed ANC registration was 6.3 times (P < 0.001) more likely in unplanned pregnancies. Among women who terminated their unplanned pregnancies (26%), nearly two-thirds (58%) terminated before 7 weeks of gestation, while the remaining 42% terminated between 7 and 12 weeks (Table 5). The majority (85%) of women with terminated unplanned pregnancies opted for over-the-counter drugs (medication purchased from local pharmacies without seeking proper medical consultation), followed by medical termination of pregnancy (10%), and a small proportion (5%) reported inserting plant material intravaginally in an attempt to induce abortion (Fig. 4). Among women who used over-the-counter drugs without medical consultation, 24 had successful outcomes, while 11 experienced complications (e.g., excessive bleeding and adverse reactions), resulting in a 31.43% complication rate (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Primary outcome

Prevalence of unplanned pregnancy

In the present study, the prevalence of unplanned pregnancy was 26%. Among these women, 64% continued their pregnancy, while 36% opted for termination. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in India (28.5%) [21]. Northwest Ethiopia (20.6%) [19], and Kenya (24%) [22]. However, other Indian studies have reported a wider range, from 11.9%-40.1% [9, 23]. Higher rates have also been observed in some African countries, such as Ethiopia (33.3%) [24], Egypt (30.7%) [1], and Uganda (37%) [25]. These differences may stem from variations in study design, population characteristics, and access to reproductive health services.

Factors associated with unplanned pregnancy

Women aged 30 years or above at the time of pregnancy had higher odds of unplanned pregnancy (OR = 2.3; 95% CI: 1.1–4.8; P = 0.02), consistent with findings from Sub-Saharan countries [19, 26] and Zambia [8]. One possible explanation is the assumptions about declining fertility with age [27, 28]. However, in the 30’s, fertility can still be high, making unplanned pregnancy possible if contraception is not used properly [29].

Women with gravida > 2 pregnancies had higher odds of unplanned pregnancy (OR = 3.6; 95% CI: 1.2–10.2; P = 0.01). This is comparable with findings from studies in Ethiopia [18] and Tanzania [30] where higher gravidity was linked to unplanned pregnancies. This may reflect reduced vigilance in contraceptive use after multiple pregnancies [31, 32].

A strong association was observed between unplanned pregnancy and a history of induced or mixed (spontaneous and induced) abortion (OR = 8.7; 95% CI: 3.5–21.5; P < 0.001). Although not widely reported in earlier studies, research from Nepal [33] showed similar trends. Delayed recognition of unplanned pregnancy or barriers to safe, timely, and respectful abortion care may force women to resort to unsafe or self-induced methods [2, 34].

Birth spacing of ≤ 24 months of their most recent birth was associated with higher odds of unplanned pregnancy (OR = 4.4; 95% CI: 2.4–7.9; P < 0.001). Similar associations were reported in studies from Pakistan [35] and Egypt [1]. This may suggest that women who plan pregnancies are more likely to follow child spacing guidelines.

Secondary outcome

Maternal and neonatal outcome of unplanned pregnancy

Women with unplanned pregnancies had significantly higher odds of experiencing complications during pregnancy (OR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.1–4.1; P = 0.02), consistent with studies from Egypt [1] Turkey [36] Sri Lanka [37] India [9] and Iran [38]. This could be due to delays in seeking antenatal care, late recognition of pregnancy, or lack of awareness, all of which hinder early detection and management of complications [39, 40]. Emotional stress associated with unplanned pregnancies [41] can further exacerbate risks, contributing to outcomes such as preterm birth [42] low birth weight, and preeclampsia [9].

The odds of experiencing complications during the postpartum period were also higher in unplanned pregnancies (OR = 3.2; 95% CI: 1.1–9.2; P = 0.02), consistent with findings from Northern India [9]. These may be related to delayed prenatal interventions or untreated conditions during pregnancy.

The odds of preterm delivery were higher in unplanned pregnancies (OR = 3.3; 95% CI: 1.2–8.9; P = 0.01, similar to findings from the US [5] and Iran [38]. Unplanned pregnancies were also linked to a higher risk of low birth weight (0.55 times), as noted in studies from Iran [38] the US [5] and Egypt [1].

Among women who opted for termination of unplanned pregnancies, common complications included excessive vaginal bleeding, adverse reactions to medications, and prolonged illness. These findings are in line with research from Tanzania [43] and WHO report [2] highlighting the risks associated with unsafe or delayed abortion.

Health-seeking behavior in unplanned pregnancy

The odds of performing a UPT more than 21 days after a missed period were significantly higher in unplanned pregnancies (OR = 5.8; 95% CI: 3.1–10.9; P < 0.001). This may reflect delayed pregnancy recognition or uncertainty about pregnancy intentions and is an area that has not been thoroughly explored in previous studies.

Additionally, late ANC registration (after the first trimester) was more likely in unplanned pregnancies (OR = 6.2; 95% CI: 2.1–18.4; P < 0.001), consistent with studies from Botswana [44] and Ethiopia [45] and evidence from the National Survey in India [46]. This may reflect reduced utilization of maternal services among women with unplanned pregnancies.

In this study, unsafe methods for terminating unplanned pregnancy such as the use of over-the-counter medications and the insertion of plant material intravaginally, were reported. This is consistent with studies from Northern India [20] Tanzania [47] Papua New Guinea [48] and a review on unsafe abortion [2]. Such practices can lead to severe complications including infection, hemorrhage, and maternal mortality [2]. This underscores the urgent need for expand access to safe, legal, and affordable abortion services and public education about the risks of unsafe methods.

Study limitations and strengths

To the best of the investigator’s knowledge, this is the first research in Odisha that has assessed several aspects of unplanned pregnancy, such as factors, outcomes, and health-seeking behavior, which provided more comprehensive data. The data was collected through a door-to-door survey in the community area to eliminate selection bias. The interview technique allowed the researcher to collect rich and detailed information on the sensitive topic of unplanned pregnancy. This study evaluated a large number of variables to strengthen the associations. Being a community-based study gives an accurate picture of unplanned pregnancies and the unmet need for contraception and abortion practices. Women were recruited from 7 villages and 3 urban sectors of Khordha district, which enhanced the validity and generalizability of the findings. Data from the women who chose to end their pregnancies were also included.

Data collected for this study mainly relied on women’s self-reports, which had a risk of reporting bias. It was restricted to pregnancies within the last three years; hence, recall bias was possible. There might be some social desirability bias as the data collectors were health professionals. Despite the prospective design being the best option, it was not adopted in this study because of time constraints. This study included only ever-married women, so results cannot be extrapolated to unmarried women, who may be more likely to experience unplanned pregnancies and abortions. This study relied on retrospective self-reports of pregnancy intention, which may introduce misclassification bias, as women sometimes reinterpret past unplanned pregnancies as planned over time [49]. The use of convenience sampling limits the generalizability of findings to all women in the region, as selection bias cannot be ruled out.

Recommendations

Emphasis should be given to information, education, and communication activities in addition to the national program’s existing strategies. Efforts must prioritize educating women about the hazards of unsafe abortion practices. Community-based programs should be developed in collaboration with local healthcare providers, leaders, and NGOs to improve access to family planning, contraceptives, and reproductive health services.

Training and capacity building for healthcare providers, including community health nurses, self-help groups, and ASHA workers, are essential to enhance their knowledge and skills in reproductive health counseling, contraception management, and maternal care, thereby ensuring higher quality care and support for women in the community. Promoting policies that ensure access to affordable, high-quality reproductive healthcare services such as family planning counseling, contraceptives, and prenatal care while addressing systemic barriers is crucial for improving reproductive health outcomes. Future research, including qualitative and longitudinal studies, is recommended to explore socio-cultural factors and track unplanned pregnancy outcomes over time, offering deeper insights for effective interventions.

Conclusion

Out of the 430 women surveyed, 26% experienced unplanned pregnancies, indicating a significant public health concern in the region. The study identified key predictors of unplanned pregnancy, as maternal age ≥ 30 years, higher gravidity (≥ 2), shorter birth spacing (≤ 24 months), and a prior history of abortion. Women with unplanned pregnancies were more likely to experience pregnancy and postpartum complications, preterm delivery, and showed delays in UPT and ANC registration, reflecting suboptimal health-seeking behavior. The results underscore the critical importance of expanding contraceptive availability and providing personalized family planning counselling, especially for women at greater risk. Prioritizing early antenatal engagement and enhancing public awareness of legally permissible and medically safe abortion options are vital. Broader policy reforms and grassroots awareness initiatives are vital to minimizing the incidence and impact of unplanned pregnancies.
