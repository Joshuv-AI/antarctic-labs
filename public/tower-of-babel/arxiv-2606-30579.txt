The many-body Blaschke-Santaló type inequality via optimal transport
Shibing Chen, Yuanyuan Li, Dongmeng Xi, Zhe-Feng Xu

Abstract
. Let K 1 , … , K k ⊂ ℝ n K_{1},\ldots,K_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} be origin-symmetric measurable sets of finite volume such that ∑ 1 ≤ i < j ≤ k ⟨ x i , x j ⟩ ≤ ( k 2 ) , ∀ x i ∈ K i , x j ∈ K j . \sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle\leq\binom{k}{2},\qquad\forall\,x_{i}\in K_{i},x_{j}\in K_{j}. We prove the sharp many-body Blaschke–Santaló type inequality ∏ i = 1 k | K i | ≤ | B n | k \prod_{i=1}^{k}|K_{i}|\leq|B^{n}|^{k} proposed by Kalantzopoulos and Saroglou, and characterize all equality cases. The proof combines multi-marginal optimal transport with a pseudo-Euclidean volume estimate. Using the geometric–functional equivalence of Kalantzopoulos and Saroglou, we also establish the functional version inequality proposed by Kolesnikov and Werner.



1.
Introduction and main results

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body containing the origin in its interior. Its polar body is

K^{\circ}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,y\rangle\leq 1\text{ for all }x\in K\}.

The Blaschke–Santaló inequality is a well-known affine isoperimetric inequality. In the origin-symmetric case, it asserts that

|K|\,|K^{\circ}|\leq|B^{n}|^{2},

with equality if and only if
K
is an ellipsoid
[
5
,
45
]
.

The complementary lower-bound problem for the volume product is Mahler’s conjecture, originating in
[
33
,
34
]
. Mahler proved the
two-dimensional case, while Bourgain–Milman established the reverse Santaló inequality up to a universal constant in arbitrary dimension
[
8
]
. In dimension three, the symmetric conjecture was proved by Iriyeh–Shibata
[
21
]
, and the general conjecture was
recently proved in
[
12
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to prove a sharp upper bound for the many-body volume product and to show that its equality structure becomes rigid once
k\geq 3
.
The many-body problem arose from the functional extension of the Blaschke–Santaló inequality introduced by Kolesnikov and Werner
[
29
]
. Its optimal-transport origin is more specific. Early many-marginal forms of cyclical monotonicity and optimal couplings appear in the work of Knott–Smith and Rüschendorf
[
28
,
44
]
. For the Euclidean quadratic cost, the foundational result of Gangbo–Święch shows that the multi-marginal Kantorovich optimizer is unique and is induced by optimal maps
[
19
]
. Agueh and Carlier later observed that the same quadratic multi-marginal problem is equivalent to the Wasserstein barycenter problem
[
1
]
.

The connection with optimal transport comes from the
identity

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}=(k-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x_{i}|^{2}-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle,

which shows that the pairwise inner products are precisely the interaction variables in the quadratic multi-marginal cost. The pseudo-Riemannian aspect of optimal transport is already present in
the two-marginal theory. Kim and McCann introduced the pseudo-Riemannian geometry induced by the mixed Hessian of the transport cost
[
24
]
. Building on this framework, Kim–McCann–Warren associated to a smooth
transportation cost a pseudo-Riemannian metric and a calibration form on
the product space; in their formulation, the graph of an optimal map is a
calibrated maximal submanifold
[
25
]
. In the
multi-marginal problem, Pass introduced semi-Riemannian metrics built from
the mixed second derivatives of the cost and used their signature to
control the local dimension of optimal supports
[
39
]
; see also

[
41
]
.

In the present paper, the relevant object is the many-body interaction

\mathcal{S}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle.

For this interaction, the mixed Hessians are constant:

\mathrm{D}^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}\mathcal{S}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=I,\qquad\mathrm{for}\,\,i\neq j.

Thus the semi-Riemannian geometry of the optimal support becomes flat.
The corresponding bilinear form is

(1.1)

\mathcal{B}(X,Y)=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}\right\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle x_{i},y_{i}\rangle,\qquad X=(x_{i}),\ Y=(y_{i})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k},

and

\mathcal{B}(X,X)=2\mathcal{S}_{2}(X).

This identity is the bridge between the many-body polarity condition and
the spacelike graph theory used below.

Pairwise interaction costs have rich structures and have been studied by many authors. Pass proved uniqueness and Monge solution results under suitable structural hypotheses
[
38
]
. Kim and Pass introduced the twist-on-splitting-sets condition and extended the barycenter cost to Riemannian manifolds
[
26
,
27
]
. The complete-graph case and more general graph-structured pairwise interactions were further studied by Pass and Vargas-Jiménez
[
42
]
. We also refer to the survey of Pass for a concise account of the theory and its applications
[
41
]
. Some important examples include matching problems for teams in economics
[
11
,
40
]
and multi-marginal Coulomb costs arising in density functional theory
[
10
,
14
]
.

In this paper we use two aspects of this theory. The first one is that the barycenter formulation gives canonical Brenier maps, and therefore leads to a Monge–Ampère system. The second one is that the support of an optimal multi-marginal plan carries a natural indefinite geometry. In Pass’s local theory, the corresponding metric depends on the second derivatives of the cost. For the quadratic cost studied here, this metric becomes the constant pseudo-Euclidean form used below. Hence the affine-geometric volume problem leads naturally to a volume estimate for spacelike graphs. Kolesnikov and Werner formulated the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1

(Kolesnikov–Werner
[
29
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
k\geq 2
. Let
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be non-increasing, and let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be even integrable functions. Assume that

(1.2)

\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}(x_{i})\leq\rho\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\bigr),\qquad\forall\,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then

(1.3)

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho\bigl(C_{k}|u|^{2}\bigr)^{1/k}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{k}.

For
k=2
, this is the two-function functional Blaschke–Santaló inequality; see
[
2
,
3
,
18
,
30
]
. For
k\geq 2
, Kolesnikov and Werner proved the conjecture when all functions are unconditional
[
29
]
. Kalantzopoulos and Saroglou proved that it is enough to assume unconditionality of
f_{3},\ldots,f_{k}
and related the functional statement to a geometric polarity condition
[
22
]
. In the Gaussian case

\rho(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{t}{k-1}\right),

Nakamura and Tsuji proved the conjecture for arbitrary even functions
[
36
]
. Courtade and Wang later gave an entropic proof of the Gaussian inequality and removed the symmetry assumptions in that setting
[
13
]
. The second-moment formulation is also closely related to Ball’s strengthened Santaló conjecture, recently proved by Böröczky, Patsalos, and Saroglou
[
7
]
.

Kalantzopoulos and Saroglou formulated the following geometric conjecture.

Conjecture 2

(Kalantzopoulos–Saroglou
[
22
]
)
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
k\geq 2
, and let
K_{1},\ldots,K_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be origin-symmetric convex bodies. If

\mathcal{S}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\leq C_{k},\qquad\forall\,x_{i}\in K_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,k,

then

\prod_{i=1}^{k}|K_{i}|\leq|B^{n}|^{k}.

Later, Kalantzopoulos and Saroglou showed that the functional assertion is equivalent to Conjecture
1
by using a level-set argument together with the multiplicative Prékopa–Leindler inequality; see
[
22
, Proposition 4.1]
. We shall prove the geometric inequality in a slightly stronger form, without assuming convexity.

Theorem 1.1

(Many-body Blaschke-Santaló inequality)
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
k\geq 2
, and let
K_{1},\ldots,K_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be origin-symmetric measurable sets of finite volume. If

(1.4)

\mathcal{S}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\leq C_{k},\qquad\forall\,x_{i}\in K_{i},

then

(1.5)

\prod_{i=1}^{k}|K_{i}|\leq|B^{n}|^{k}.

If equality holds, then all
|K_{i}|
are positive and, up to null sets, exactly one of the following alternatives occurs:

(i)

if
k=2
, there exists
A\in{\rm GL}(n)
such that

K_{1}=AB^{n},\qquad K_{2}=A^{-\top}B^{n};

(ii)

if
k\geq 3
, then

K_{1}=\cdots=K_{k}=B^{n}.

Conversely, each case satisfies (
1.4
) and attains equality in (
1.5
).

This rigidity result shows a clear difference between the two-body case and the many-body case. When
k=2
, the affine invariance of the classical Blaschke–Santaló inequality is preserved. When
k\geq 3
, the pairwise constraints impose additional restrictions, so that, after the prescribed normalization, equality holds only for Euclidean balls.

By the equivalence of Kalantzopoulos and Saroglou, Theorem
1.1
therefore gives the full functional inequality. For completeness, we also record the equality statement.

Theorem 1.2

(Functional many-body Blaschke-Santaló inequality)
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
k\geq 2
. Let
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be non-increasing, and let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be even integrable functions satisfying (
1.2
). Then (
1.3
) holds.

Within this admissible class, equality is characterized as follows.

(i)

If
k=2
, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
with
c_{1}c_{2}=1
, and a symmetric positive-definite
n\times n
matrix

Q
, such that

f_{1}(x)=c_{1}(\det Q)^{1/2}\rho\!\left(x^{\top}Qx\right)^{1/2},\quad f_{2}(x)=c_{2}(\det Q)^{-1/2}\rho\!\left(x^{\top}Q^{-1}x\right)^{1/2},\quad\mathrm{for\,\,a.e.}\,x.

(ii)

If
k\geq 3
, there exist constants

c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}>0
with
\prod_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}=1

such that

f_{i}(x)=c_{i}\,\rho\!\left(C_{k}|x|^{2}\right)^{1/k},\qquad i=1,\ldots,k,\quad\mathrm{for\,\,a.e.}\,x.

In either case, the constants are necessarily given by
c_{i}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{i}(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(C_{k}|x|^{2})^{1/k}\,\,\mathrm{d}x}.

We outline the proof of Theorem
1.1
. A convexification argument first reduces the problem to origin-symmetric convex bodies. Let
\mu_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{K_{i}}}{|K_{i}|}\,\mathrm{d}x

and let
\nu
be the equal-weight Wasserstein barycenter of
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}
. If
T_{i}=\nabla\varphi_{i}
denotes the Brenier map from
\nu
to
\mu_{i}
, then
\gamma=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{k})_{\#}\nu

is optimal for the quadratic multi-marginal problem

\sup_{\eta\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})}\int\mathcal{S}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

The support
\Gamma=\supp\gamma
has a hidden pseudo-Euclidean structure. On
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}
, let
\mathcal{B}
be the bilinear form defined in (
1.1
). Then
\mathcal{B}
has signature
(n,n(k-1))
and
\mathcal{B}(X,X)=2\mathcal{S}_{2}(X)
. Kantorovich duality and cyclical monotonicity give

\mathcal{B}(X-Y,X-Y)\geq 0,\qquad\forall\,X,Y\in\Gamma.

After a coordinate-exchange argument and a linear pseudo-isometry,
\Gamma
is therefore the graph of an odd
1
-Lipschitz map
f:A\to\mathbb{R}^{n(k-1)}
for some
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The polarity condition gives

(1.6)

0\leq|x|^{2}-|f(x)|^{2}\leq 2C_{k}\qquad\forall\,x\in A.

The new geometric ingredient is a pseudo-Euclidean volume estimate. If
A=-A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded,
f:A\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is odd and
1
-Lipschitz, and satisfies (
1.6
),
then

(1.7)

\Vol_{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma)=\int_{A}\sqrt{\det(I-(\mathrm{D}f_{x})^{\top}\mathrm{D}f_{x})}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq|B^{n}|(2C_{k})^{n/2}.

The proof uses the Dirichlet theory for higher-codimension maximal graphs of Li
[
32
]
, followed by a sharp monotonicity argument for maximal spacelike submanifolds.

The lower estimate comes from the Monge–Ampère equations for the barycenter maps. Define a projection

P:(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad P(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i},

In the coordinate
p=\sum_{i}x_{i}
, write

P^{-1}(p)=\big(W_{1}(p),\ldots,W_{k}(p)\big),\qquad M_{i}(p)=\mathrm{D}W_{i}(p).

Then, almost everywhere, one has

M_{i}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i}=I,\qquad\det M_{i}=|K_{i}|h(p),

where
h
is the scaled density of the Wasserstein barycenter
\nu
. The induced metric on
\Gamma
is

G=I-\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i}^{2}.

A sharp determinant inequality gives

\det(G)^{1/n}\geq 2C_{k}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\det M_{i}\right)^{2/(kn)}.

After integration,

\Vol_{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma)\geq\bigl(2C_{k}\bigr)^{n/2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}|K_{i}|\right)^{1/k}.

Combining this with (
1.7
), we have the desired sharp many-body Blaschke-Santaló inequality.

The equality proof follows the same chain. For
k=2
it is the equality case of the symmetric Blaschke–Santaló inequality. For
k\geq 3
, equality in the determinant inequality implies

M_{1}=\cdots=M_{k}=\frac{1}{k}I

almost everywhere. The Monge–Ampère equations then show that the barycenter is of the form
\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\alpha 1_{E}\,\mathrm{d}z
for some origin-symmetric measurable set
E
and some constant
\alpha
. A non-expansion rigidity argument gives
K_{1}=\cdots=K_{k}
. Substituting
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{k}=x
in (
1.4
) then gives that body to be
B^{n}
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
develops the pseudo-Euclidean geometry of the canonical multi-marginal support. Section
3
proves the Monge–Ampère lower bound. Section
4
establishes the pseudo-Euclidean volume estimate (
1.7
). The geometric inequality is completed in Section
5
, and its equality cases are treated in Section
6
. Section
7
contains the functional equality analysis.

2.
Preliminaries

We denote by
B^{n}
the Euclidean unit ball, and put
\mathbb{R}_{+}=(0,\infty)
. A convex body means a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with non-empty interior. In this paper, symmetry always means symmetry with respect to the origin. For a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
\conv A
,
\operatorname{cl}A
, and
\partial A
its convex hull, closure, and boundary, respectively. If
f:A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is differentiable at
x
, then
\mathrm{D}f_{x}
denotes its Jacobian matrix and
\|\mathrm{D}f_{x}\|
denotes its operator norm. For other standard notation in convex geometry, we refer to
[
20
,
47
]
.

We first make a simple convexification reduction. This step is purely convex-geometric. Namely, we replace the given sets by their closed convex hulls, while the many-body polar constraint remains unchanged.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K_{1},\ldots,K_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be origin-symmetric measurable sets of positive finite volume satisfying (
1.4
). Then
\widetilde{K}_{i}=\operatorname{cl}\conv(K_{i})
is an origin-symmetric convex body for every
i
, and

\mathcal{S}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\leq C_{k},\qquad\forall\,x_{i}\in\widetilde{K}_{i}.

Proof.

The function
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is affine with respect to each variable. Hence, if we take convex hulls successively in each coordinate and then take closures, the constraint (
1.4
) is still preserved by continuity.

It is clear that each
\widetilde{K}_{i}
has non-empty interior, since
|K_{i}|>0
. It remains to show that
\widetilde{K}_{i}
is bounded. Fix
i
and choose
j\neq i
. Since
\widetilde{K}_{j}
has non-empty interior, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\delta B^{n}\subset\widetilde{K}_{j}.

Moreover, all the sets contain the origin. Thus, by taking all variables except the
i
-th and the
j
-th ones to be zero, we get

|\langle x,y\rangle|\leq C_{k},\qquad\forall\,x\in\widetilde{K}_{i},\ y\in\widetilde{K}_{j},

where we have also used the symmetry of
\widetilde{K}_{j}
. For any
x\neq 0
, taking
y=\delta x/|x|
gives
\delta|x|\leq C_{k}.

Therefore,
|x|\leq C_{k}/\delta
for all
x\in\widetilde{K}_{i}
. This proves that
\widetilde{K}_{i}
is bounded.
∎

It is therefore enough to prove Theorem
1.1
for origin-symmetric convex bodies, since
|K_{i}|\leq|\widetilde{K}_{i}|
.
The link of the many-body inequality with quadratic multi-marginal transport is through the pairwise interaction term. For the equal-weight barycenter cost,

c(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x_{i}-z|^{2}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}.

Since

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}=(k-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x_{i}|^{2}-2\mathcal{S}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),

minimizing this quadratic cost over couplings with fixed marginals is
equivalent to maximizing
\mathcal{S}_{2}
. Thus
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is the relevant
interaction part of the quadratic Gangbo–Święch cost. Its
polarization reveals the pseudo-Euclidean structure used below.
For
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),Y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k},
one can define

(2.1)

\mathcal{B}(X,Y)=\big\langle\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}\big\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle x_{i},y_{i}\rangle.

Then
\mathcal{B}(X,X)=2\mathcal{S}_{2}(X)
and we have

(2.2)

\mathcal{B}(X,X)\leq 2C_{k},

where the upper bound is exactly the many-body polarity assumption (
1.4
).

For later use, we recall some notation for graphs in a general pseudo-Euclidean space. We write the pseudo-Euclidean space of signature
(n,m)
as

\mathbb{R}^{n,m}=\mathbb{R}_{x}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{R}_{y}^{m},\qquad\big\langle(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big\rangle_{n,m}=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle-\langle y,y^{\prime}\rangle,

and denote the corresponding metric by

g_{n,m}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x_{\alpha}^{2}-\sum_{\beta=1}^{m}\,\mathrm{d}y_{\beta}^{2}.

Thus, for
v=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n,m}
,

\langle v,v\rangle_{n,m}=g_{n,m}(v,v)=|x|^{2}-|y|^{2}.

As usual, a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n,m}
is called spacelike, null, or timelike according as

\langle v,v\rangle_{n,m}>0,\qquad\langle v,v\rangle_{n,m}=0,\qquad\langle v,v\rangle_{n,m}<0.

An
n
-dimensional submanifold
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n,m}
is called spacelike if the metric induced on each tangent space is positive definite. If, in addition, its mean curvature vector vanishes identically, then
\Sigma
is called a maximal submanifold. For more background on these notions, we refer to
[
37
]
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
f:A\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be
1
-Lipschitz. By Rademacher’s theorem
[
16
, Section 3.1.2]
,
\|\mathrm{D}f_{x}\|\leq 1
for a.e.
x\in A
, so
I-(\mathrm{D}f_{x})^{\top}\mathrm{D}f_{x}
is positive semidefinite a.e.. We define the pseudo-volume of the weakly spacelike graph of
f
by

(2.3)

\Vol_{n,m}(\operatorname{graph}_{A}f)=\int_{A}\sqrt{\det\big(I-(\mathrm{D}f_{x})^{\top}\mathrm{D}f_{x}\big)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

If
f
is defined only on
A
, one may take any global Lipschitz extension; the value in (
2.3
) is independent of the choice of extension, since any two Lipschitz extensions that agree on
A
have the same approximate
differential for a.e.
x\in A
. Indeed, for the parametrization
F(x)=(x,f(x))
, at a.e.
x\in A
where
f
is approximately
differentiable,

(F^{*}g_{n,m})_{x}(v,w)=\langle v,w\rangle-\langle\mathrm{D}f_{x}v,\mathrm{D}f_{x}w\rangle,

and the Gram matrix is
I-(\mathrm{D}f_{x})^{\top}\mathrm{D}f_{x}
.

Hence, by the discussion in introduction, we know that
\mathcal{B}
is a pseudo-Euclidean metric on
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}
with signature
(n,n(k-1))
. We denote by
\Vol_{\mathcal{B}}
the induced
n
-dimensional pseudo-volume.

We now pass to the multi-marginal optimal transport theory. The following theorem collects consequences of Agueh–Carlier’s barycenter theory
[
1
]
, which will be used for the subsequent analysis. The existence of a minimizer follows from Agueh–Carlier

[
1
, Proposition 2.3]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
K_{1},\ldots,K_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be origin-symmetric convex bodies and set

\mu_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{K_{i}}}{|K_{i}|}\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad i=1,\ldots,k.

Then the functional

\mathcal{J}(\eta):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_{2}^{2}(\eta,\mu_{i})

admits a unique minimizer
\nu
among all probability measures with finite second moment. We call
\nu
the equal-weight
Wasserstein barycenter of
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}
. Moreover, the following hold:

(1)

[
1
, Proposition 3.5 and Theorem 5.1]
. The barycenter
\nu
is unique, origin-symmetric, and absolutely continuous with bounded density,

\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)=\bar{h}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

(2)

For each
i
, by Brenier-McCann’s theorem (
[
9
,
35
]
), there exists a convex potential
\varphi_{i}
such that

T_{i}=\nabla\varphi_{i},\qquad(T_{i})_{\#}\nu=\mu_{i}.

(3)

At Lebesgue-a.e. differentiability point
z
of
T_{i}
with
\bar{h}(z)>0
,

(2.4)

\det\mathrm{D}T_{i}(z)=|K_{i}|\bar{h}(z).

(4)

[
1
, Remark 3.9]
. It holds that

(2.5)

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}T_{i}(z)=z,\qquad\nu\text{-}\mathrm{a.e.}\,z.

(5)

Each
T_{i}
is odd
\nu
-
{\rm{a.e.}}
.

(6)

[
1
, Theorem 4.1 and Proposition 4.2]
. The plan

(2.6)

\gamma=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{k})_{\#}\nu

is optimal for the multi-marginal problem

(2.7)

\sup_{\eta\in\Pi(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})}\int_{K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{k}}\mathcal{S}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\,\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of the barycenter, and the
L^{\infty}
bound for its density in (1) and the first-order condition in (4), follow from Agueh–Carlier

[
1
, Proposition 3.3, Proposition 3.5, Definition 3.9 and Theorem 5.1]
. Applying the two-marginal Brenier-McCann’s theorem to each pair
(\nu,\mu_{i})
gives a unique
\nu
-a.e. map

T_{i}=\nabla\varphi_{i}
with
(T_{i})_{\#}\nu=\mu_{i}
. The symmetry of
\nu
follows from uniqueness, since each
\mu_{i}
is invariant under
x\mapsto-x
.

For (3), the Monge–Ampère equation for the Brenier map from
\nu
to
\mu_{i}
gives

\bar{h}(z)=\frac{1}{|K_{i}|}\det\mathrm{D}T_{i}(z)

at a.e. differentiability point with
\bar{h}(z)>0
; see Brenier
[
9
]
.

To prove (5), set
\widetilde{T}_{i}(z)=-T_{i}(-z)
. The symmetries of
\nu
and
\mu_{i}
imply

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

Once we have (
2.14
), we can prove that the support of the optimal plan is the graph of an odd
1
-Lipschitz map. Indeed, we shall use the following barycentric decomposition of
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}
.
Let

\mathcal{H}=\left\{(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}:\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}=0\right\}.

For

Note that

This defines a linear isomorphism

If

is written analogously as

, then

(2.15)

\mathcal{B}(X,Y)=\langle e,e^{\prime}\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle e,e^{\prime}\rangle-\langle w,w^{\prime}\rangle.

Thus
\Phi
identifies
((\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k},\mathcal{B})
with the pseudo-Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\oplus\mathcal{H}

(e,w)\longmapsto|e|^{2}-|w|^{2}.

We now apply this decomposition to the support of the optimal plan.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\gamma=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{k})_{\#}\nu
be the optimal plan obtained in

2.2
. Denote

. Hence
\Phi(\Gamma)=\{(e,f(e)):e\in A\}

, where

.
Moreover,

is compact and origin-symmetric, and

is odd and

1
-Lipschitz.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
,

\mathcal{B}(X-Y,X-Y)\geq 0,\qquad\forall\,X,Y\in\Gamma.

Writing

and

, and using

|e-e^{\prime}|^{2}-|w-w^{\prime}|^{2}\geq 0.

In particular, if

, then

, hence

. Since

Let

The preceding injectivity shows that

3.
Wasserstein barycenters and a Monge–Ampère lower bound

We keep the notation of Section
2
. Recall that
\mu_{i}=|K_{i}|^{-1}{\bf 1}_{K_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x
,
\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\bar{h}\,\mathrm{d}z
is the equal-weight Wasserstein barycenter of the
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}
,

T_{i}=\nabla\varphi_{i}
is the Brenier map from
\nu
to
\mu_{i}
, and
\gamma=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{k})_{\#}\nu
is the corresponding quadratic multi-marginal optimizer. Let
\Gamma=\supp\gamma
. Recall
P:(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined in Proposition
2.5
as
P(X)=\sum_{i}x_{i}
. Let
A=P(\Gamma)
.
By (
2.13
), the inverse map
F=P^{-1}:A\to\Gamma
is
1
-Lipschitz in the Euclidean norm. Write

F(p)=\bigl(W_{1}(p),\ldots,W_{k}(p)\bigr).

Then

(3.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big|W_{i}(p)-W_{i}(q)\big|^{2}\leq|p-q|^{2},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_{i}(p)=p.

By (
2.5
), (
2.6
) and (
3.1
) we have

W_{i}(p)=T_{i}(p/k),\qquad\text{for a.e. }p\text{ with respect to }h(p)\,\mathrm{d}p,

where

(3.2)

h(p)=k^{-n}\bar{h}(p/k),\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(p)\,\mathrm{d}p=1.

Let
E=A\cap\{p:h(p)>0\}
. For
\text{a.e. }p\in E
, we have

M_{i}(p):=\mathrm{D}W_{i}(p)=\frac{1}{k}\mathrm{D}T_{i}(p/k),\qquad M_{i}=M_{i}^{\top}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i}=I.

In particular,
M_{i}\geq 0
and
I-M_{i}\geq 0
. By (
2.4
),

(3.3)

\det M_{i}(p)=|K_{i}|h(p),\qquad\text{for a.e. }p\in E.

With respect to the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}
in (
2.1
), the pullback metric induced by
F
satisfies, for
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in T_{p}A\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle(F^{*}\mathcal{B})_{p}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\langle\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big\langle M_{i}(p)\xi_{1},M_{i}(p)\xi_{2}\big\rangle=\xi_{1}^{\top}G(p)\xi_{2},

where
G(p)=I-\sum_{i=1}^{k}M^{2}_{i}(p)
. Since
M_{i}-M_{i}^{2}\geq 0
, one has
G\geq 0
. By the area formula, we have

(3.4)

\Vol_{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma)=\int_{A}\sqrt{\det G(p)}\,\mathrm{d}p\geq\int_{E}\sqrt{\det G(p)}\,\mathrm{d}p.

The required pointwise estimate follows from a direct application of Minkowski’s determinant inequality.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
be positive semidefinite
n\times n
matrices satisfying
\sum_{i}M_{i}=I
. Let
G=I-\sum_{i}M_{i}^{2}
. Then

(3.5)

\det(G)^{1/n}\geq k(k-1)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\det M_{i}\right)^{2/(kn)}.

For
k=2
, equality is an identity. If
k\geq 3
and all
M_{i}
are positive definite, equality holds if and only if

(3.6)

M_{1}=\cdots=M_{k}=\frac{1}{k}I.

Proof.

By the assumptions, it is easy to see that
M_{i}(I-M_{i})
is positive semidefinite, and

G=\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i}(I-M_{i}).

It follows from Minkowski’s determinant inequality that

\displaystyle\det(G)^{1/n}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\det\bigl(M_{i}(I-M_{i})\bigr)^{1/n}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\det M_{i})^{1/n}\det(I-M_{i})^{1/n}.

Since
I-M_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}M_{j}
, by Minkowski’s inequality again, we have

\det(I-M_{i})^{1/n}\geq\sum_{j\neq i}(\det M_{j})^{1/n}.

Consequently,

\det(G)^{1/n}\geq 2\sum_{i<j}(\det M_{i})^{1/n}(\det M_{j})^{1/n}\geq k(k-1)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\det M_{i})^{1/n}\right)^{2/k},

which proves (
3.5
).

If
k=2
, a direct computation shows that (
3.5
) is an identity. Assume
k\geq 3
and all
M_{i}
are positive definite. Equality in the last inequality implies
(\det M_{1})^{1/n}=\cdots=(\det M_{k})^{1/n}
. Equality in the Minkowski inequality implies that all
M_{i}
are proportional. Hence the matrices are equal, and
\sum_{i}M_{i}=I
gives (
3.6
).

∎

Now we can give a lower bound of the pseudo-volume of
\Gamma.

Proposition 3.2
.

For the optimal graph
\Gamma
associated with
K_{1},\ldots,K_{k}
,

(3.7)

\Vol_{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma)\geq\big(k(k-1)\big)^{n/2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}|K_{i}|\right)^{1/k}.

Proof.

By (
3.5
) and (
3.3
), for a.e.
p\in E
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\det G(p)}\geq\big(k(k-1)\big)^{n/2}\left(\prod_{i}\det M_{i}(p)\right)^{1/k}=\big(k(k-1)\big)^{n/2}\left(\prod_{i}|K_{i}|\right)^{1/k}h(p).

Integrating over

, and then using (

) and (

4.
A pseudo-Euclidean volume estimate

In this section, we prove the upper estimate for
\Vol_{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma)
. By Proposition
2.5
, the upper bound has been reduced to a purely pseudo-Euclidean volume comparison. Indeed, since
\Phi(\Gamma)=\{(e,f(e)):e\in A\}
, we have

\Vol_{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma)=\int_{A}\sqrt{\det\bigl(I-(\mathrm{D}f_{x})^{\top}\mathrm{D}f_{x}\bigr)}\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

Moreover,

0\leq|x|^{2}-|f(x)|^{2}\leq 2C_{k}.

Thus (
1.7
) follows from the following lemma, applied with
R=(2C_{k})^{1/2}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
R>0
. Let
A=-A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be bounded and measurable, and let
f:A\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be odd and
1
-Lipschitz. Assume that

(4.1)

0\leq|x|^{2}-|f(x)|^{2}\leq R^{2},\qquad\forall\,x\in A.

Then

(4.2)

\int_{A}\sqrt{\det\bigl(I-(\mathrm{D}f_{x})^{\top}\mathrm{D}f_{x}\bigr)}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq|B^{n}|R^{n}.

The estimate is sharp on every spacelike
n
-plane through the origin.

To prove Lemma
4.1
, we first record the maximal graph Dirichlet theorem from
[
32
]
, which will be used below.

Lemma 4.2

(
[
32
, Theorems 1.1, 2.1, and Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary, and
let
\varphi:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be smooth and for some
q<1
,

\big|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\big|\leq q|x-y|,\qquad\forall\,x,y\in\partial\Omega,\quad x\neq y.

Then the following statements hold.

(1)

There exists a unique smooth map

u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\qquad u|_{\partial\Omega}=\varphi,

whose graph

\Sigma_{u}=\big\{(x,u(x)):x\in\Omega\big\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n,m}

is spacelike and maximal.

(2)

The graph
\Sigma_{u}
maximizes the pseudo-Riemannian volume
among all smooth spacelike graphs with the same Dirichlet boundary
data.

(3)

The map
u
is globally acausal:

\big|u(x)-u(y)\big|<|x-y|,\qquad\forall\,x,y\in\Omega,\quad x\neq y.

We now prove the volume estimate.

Proposition 4.3

(Smooth graph estimate)
.

Let
R>0
. Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be smooth, odd, and
q
-Lipschitz for some
q<1
. If
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded and

|x|^{2}-|g(x)|^{2}\leq R^{2},\qquad\forall\,x\in A.

Then

(4.3)

\int_{A}\sqrt{\det\big(I-(\mathrm{D}g_{x})^{\top}\mathrm{D}g_{x}\big)}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq|B^{n}|R^{n}.

Proof.

Denote
\mathcal{R}(x)=|x|^{2}-|g(x)|^{2}
. Since
g
is odd,
g(0)=0
, and therefore
|g(x)|\leq q|x|
. For
x\neq 0
and
t>0
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{R}(tx)=2t|x|^{2}-2g(tx)\cdot\mathrm{D}g(tx)x\geq 2(1-q^{2})t|x|^{2}>0.

In particular,
\mathcal{R}(x)\geq(1-q^{2})|x|^{2}
.

Fix
R_{0}>R
, and define

E=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathcal{R}(x)<R_{0}^{2}\big\}.

The estimate
\mathcal{R}(x)\geq(1-q^{2})|x|^{2}
shows that
E
is bounded, and the assumption on
A
implies
A\subset E
. Since
\mathcal{R}
is even,
E
is symmetric with respect to the origin. If
x\in\partial E
, then
x\neq 0
, and (
4.4
) give

\nabla\mathcal{R}(x)\cdot x=\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{R}(tx)\right|_{t=1}>0.

Hence
E
is a smooth symmetric bounded domain. On
\partial E
,

(4.5)

|x|^{2}-|g(x)|^{2}=R_{0}^{2}.

Since the boundary value
g|_{\partial E}
is smooth and acausal, Lemma
4.2
gives a unique smooth maximal spacelike graph

\Sigma=\bigl\{F(x)=(x,u(x)):x\in E\bigr\}

with
u=g
on
\partial E
, and this graph maximizes the pseudo-Riemannian volume among smooth spacelike graphs with the same boundary values. Hence

(4.6)

\int_{E}\sqrt{\det\bigl(I-(\mathrm{D}g_{x})^{\top}\mathrm{D}g_{x}\bigr)}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\Vol_{n,m}(\Sigma).

It remains to bound the volume of
\Sigma
. By uniqueness,
u
is odd and hence
u(0)=0
. By Lemma
4.2
, we have

|u(x)|=|u(x)-u(0)|<|x|,\qquad\forall\,x\in E,\ x\neq 0.

Thus
\|F\|_{n,m}^{2}:=\langle F,F\rangle_{n,m}=|x|^{2}-|u(x)|^{2}
is positive on
\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
. Since
g=u
on
\partial\Sigma
, and by (
4.5
) we have

\|F\|_{n,m}=R_{0}
on
\partial\Sigma
.

We claim that

(4.7)

\Vol_{n,m}(\Sigma)\leq|B^{n}|R_{0}^{n}.

Since
\Sigma
is maximal,
\Delta_{\Sigma}F=0
. Therefore, for any local orthonormal tangent frame
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
,

(4.8)

\Delta_{\Sigma}\frac{\|F\|_{n,m}^{2}}{2}=\langle\Delta_{\Sigma}F,F\rangle_{n,m}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\big\langle\,\mathrm{d}F(e_{\alpha}),\,\mathrm{d}F(e_{\alpha})\big\rangle_{n,m}=n.

Decompose
F
into its tangential and normal components:

F=F^{\parallel}+F^{\perp}.

Since the orthogonal complement of a spacelike
n
-plane in signature
(n,m)
is negative definite,

\langle F^{\perp},F^{\perp}\rangle_{n,m}\leq 0.

Moreover, since
\nabla_{\Sigma}\frac{\|F\|_{n,m}^{2}}{2}=F^{\parallel},
we have that on
\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
,

(4.9)

\bigl|\nabla_{\Sigma}\|F\|_{n,m}\bigr|^{2}=\frac{\langle F^{\parallel},F^{\parallel}\rangle_{n,m}}{\|F\|_{n,m}^{2}}=1-\frac{\langle F^{\perp},F^{\perp}\rangle_{n,m}}{\|F\|_{n,m}^{2}}\geq 1.

We next apply this radial estimate to the sublevel sets of

\|F\|_{n,m}
. For
0<s<R_{0}
, define

V(s):=\Vol_{n,m}\bigl\{p\in\Sigma:\ \|F(p)\|_{n,m}<s\bigr\}.

By Sard’s theorem, almost every

s\in(0,R_{0})
is a regular value of
\|F\|_{n,m}
. For such
s
,

\big\{\|F\|_{n,m}=s\big\}\subset\Sigma

Proof of Lemma

6.
Geometric equality cases

Having proved the sharp inequality, we now turn to its equality cases in Theorem
1.1

It suffices to classify equality for convex bodies. Indeed, suppose that the original measurable sets

The case

Combining Section

(2C_{k})^{n/2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}|K_{i}|\right)^{1/k}\leq\int_{E}\sqrt{\det G(p)}\,\mathrm{d}p\leq\Vol_{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma)\leq|B^{n}|(2C_{k})^{n/2}.

Since

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

Let

to

Equality in the functional inequality

In this section, we mainly discuss the equality case in Theorem

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\Phi,\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{k}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be measurable and integrable, and assume

(7.1)

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{i}(r_{i})\right)^{1/k}\leq\Phi\left(\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}\right)^{1/k}\right),\qquad\forall\,r_{i}>0.

Then

(7.2)

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi_{i}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\right)^{k}.

Equality holds if and only if
t\mapsto e^{t}\Phi(e^{t})
is equivalent to a log-concave function, and there exist constants
a_{i},b_{i}>0
such that

(7.3)

\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}=1

and

(7.4)

\Phi_{i}(r)=a_{i}\Phi(b_{i}r),\qquad\mathrm{for\,\,a.e.}\,r>0.

Proof.

Denote

(7.5)

F_{i}(t)=e^{t}\Phi_{i}(e^{t}),\qquad F(t)=e^{t}\Phi(e^{t}),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Then (
7.1
) becomes

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}F_{i}(t_{i})\right)^{1/k}\leq F\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\right).

Hence (
7.2
) follows from the classical Prékopa–Leindler inequality.

If equality in (
7.2
) holds, by
[
29
, Theorem 4.1]
or
[
6
, Theorem 28]
, there exist numbers
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and constants
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}>0
such that

F_{i}(t)=c_{i}F(t-t_{i})\qquad\text{for a.e. }t.

Recalling (
7.5
), this is equivalent to saying that

\Phi_{i}(r)=a_{i}\Phi(b_{i}r)\qquad\text{for a.e. }r>0,

where

a_{i}=c_{i}\,e^{-t_{i}},\qquad b_{i}=e^{-t_{i}}.

The condition
\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}=0
implies
b_{1}\cdots b_{k}=1
.
Since equality holds in (
7.2
), through a direct computation, we have

\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}=1.

The converse follows from the log-concavity of
F
and the same change of variables.
∎

1.2
. When

Theorem 7.3

Assume that equality in (
