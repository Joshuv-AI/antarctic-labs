1.0 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Throughout the lifetime of this initiative, including renewals, we focused on understanding the
fundamental principles of protein-protein interface design that enable predictable and modular
spatial and kinetic control of multi-component protein self-assembly in 1D, 2D, and 3D, including
the interface with inorganic materials1–4, small molecules, and metal ions. We designed
individual protein components that bind specific metal ions, including critical minerals and
materials and transport ions across lipid membranes5. We created helical 1D filaments of
repeating units with programmed periodicity, pitch, and multi-component environmentally
responsive self-assembling protein fibers.6 We showed that these filaments reversibly assemble
and disassemble under specific pH conditions and created end-specific caps that independently
tune the balance of attachment and detachment rates at each terminus of the filament. Using
similar filaments, we succeeded in binding arrays of heme and chlorophyll molecules and
assembling patterned helical coatings around carbon nanotubes in efforts to create de novo
conductive nanowires. By arraying REE binding sites in a large circular tandem array with a
repeat protein-based cyclic oligomer, we created a molecular scaffold for superradiance and
paramagnetic quantum sensing. We created a range of one-component7 and two-component8
self-assembling 2D arrays and showed that when designed to engage cell receptors, these
arrays can control cell behavior from outside the cell signal to inside the cell. We designed
helical repeat proteins with variable lengths displaying charged residues in a pattern matched to
the cation lattice of mica. achieved a range of ordered states with an epitaxial match to the
underlying crystal lattice1. We further applied the learned principles of protein-induced
biomineralization to design proteins with an interface lattice matching CaCO3 and guide the
formation of specific crystal forms of CaCO3 from solution, a significant advance toward the
global need to manage carbon3,8. In all cases of mineral lattice matching and biomineralization,
we followed assembly using molecularly resolved in situ AFM imaging and extracted information
about assembly pathways and energetics, applying deep learning to quantify the dynamics of
protein self-organization9. We developed techniques for using dynamic metal-dependent
interfaces on protein nanopores for discriminatively sensing dilute metal ions in solution and
demonstrated the use of strong metal-binding interfaces to drive nanomaterial assembly for
conditional nanocompartmentalization applications. This grant supported 11 people, including
Asim Bera, Evans Brackenbrough, Andrew Borst, Nikita Hanikel, Timothy Huddy, Emily Joyce,
Alex Young-Seug Kang, Ryan Kibler, Joshua Morris Lubner, Harley Pyles, and Shuai Zhang.
The research effort culminated in the production of published papers and theses. Electronic
Thesis/Dissertation are distributed by ProQuest/UMI Dissertation Publishing and made available
on an open access basis through UW Libraries ResearchWorks Service.

2.0 ACCOMPLISHMENTS
2.1 What are the major goals of the project?
To fill knowledge gaps and understand the physical and chemical principles governing
multi-component protein self-assembly into computationally designed, atomically precise,
hierarchical structures with novel metal-ion functions, we will focus on three scientific objectives.
1)​ Discover principles for achieving highly selective binding of metal ions across the periodic
 table and use AI-based design methods to generate panels of proteins selective for
 individual metal ions either as soluble proteins or as transmembrane channels.
2)​ Discover design principles for arraying electronically or photonically active substrates in
 protein fibers for nanoscale energy transport.
3)​ Discover design principles for specific metal ion-inducible protein nanocages and
 unbounded assemblies.
2.2 What was accomplished under these goals?
In the previous iteration of this grant, before this one-year renewal, we made significant
progress in the development of general methods for designing cyclic oligomers4, nanocages4,
1D fibers4,6,10,11, 2D arrays8, and 3D crystals12, with a focus on improving design success rates
and imparting rational controls on their dimensions. In this reporting period, we developed and
published a method of building nanocages, 2D arrays, and 3D crystals out of a set of modular
building blocks. These advancements contributed to our toolbox of methods from the previous
period for using protein design to interrogate natural processes and engineer exotic materials.
We have built recent AI-accelerated breakthroughs in atomic positioning13,14 and metal binding15,
and new high-throughput protein production capabilities to develop a suite of binders for
industrially important critical minerals and materials. We made considerable progress in
developing general methods for designing transmembrane ion channels, particularly Ca
channels. This work is in final revision at Nature. We also designed transmembrane beta barrels
for selective rare-earth elements (REEs) transport in macroscopic filtration devices. We built on
our work with 1D fibers and repeat proteins to array chlorophyll molecules in a photoelectrically
competent state, but found the fibers to be too fragile for conductivity testing, and to overcome
this limitation, we designed protein fibers that assemble around carbon nanotubes, which will
act as rigid conductive wires. We again used repeat proteins to scaffold photonically active
REEs in a repeating circular array, which should exhibit superradiant emission. While the
proteins bind REE ions in the designed arrangement, measurement of superradiance has
remained elusive. We extended our work on nanopore-based sensors to develop REE-sensors
that can distinguish between REEs.

Objective I: Discover principles for achieving highly selective binding of metal ions
 across the periodic table and use AI-based design methods to generate panels of
 proteins selective for individual metal ions either as soluble proteins or as
 transmembrane channels.
Objective Ia:
Among the non-biological metals, we wanted to first focus on the most important and highly
demanded critical elements, as their separation and sequestration poses a formidable chemical
and industrial challenge. Therefore, we aim to explore and discover the fundamental principles
that lead to the selective recognition of these elements by utilizing AI-based protein design
methods.
The respective set of symmetric metal-binding motifs was scaffolded at the interface of
symmetric oligomeric protein assemblies using RFdiffusion or RFdiffusion2 (Fig. 1). Reasonable
protein scaffolds were down-selected based on their structural compactness and a sequence
was generated for the respective quaternary structures using ProteinMPNN (Fig. 1). Afterward,
the designs were validated using the structural prediction models chai-1 or AlphaFold3, and the
protein structures with the highest confidence scores, an optimal preservation of the structural
motif within the protein complex (reflected in a low RMSD between the metal-ion-binding motif
and protein structure prediction), as well as the designed metal ion coordination mode in the
predicted holo structure were selected for experimental validation.

Objective Ib:
This objective aimed to develop methods for designing transmembrane channels that are
selective for specific metal ions. This would enable the discovery of new principles of de novo
ion channel design and direct the construction of a wide array of ion channels with selectivities
that go beyond those observed in nature, particularly applying to the non-physiological ions that
natural ion channels are typically not selectively permeable to.
Alpha Helical Channels
We sought to explore a general bottom-up approach of designing ion channels from defined
selectivity filter residue geometries, based on the relatively well-recognized understanding that
the precise arrangement of amino acid sidechains for a target ion, which forms the selectivity
filter of the channel, is a crucial feature of ion channels for their ion selectivity. As the proof of
concept of the design approach, we focused on Ca2+ channels, as these channels are known to
rely on negatively charged carboxyl groups to bind Ca2+ for ion selectivity. With the hypothesis
that the proper arrangement of the carboxyl groups at the pore entrance followed by a wide and
well-hydrated pore would confer Ca2+ permeability and selectivity, we reasoned that Ca2+

channels could in principle be constructed by (1) generating selectivity filter configurations by
systematically sampling the distance and coordination geometry of carboxylate containing
sidechains around a central Ca2+ ion, and then using generative deep learning methods to (2)
build single helices out from the selectivity filter sidechains that define the overall pore shape,
and (3) buttress these central pore helices by multiple surrounding helices. This procedure
could, in principle, create channels with custom-defined selectivity filter geometry with high
precision, with the overall topology of the protein generated to support the selectivity filter and
conducting pore rather than being defined in advance. Moreover, this procedure was not
confined by the type of metal ions and the identity of the amino acid sidechains that would
interact with it, meaning it could be extended to the design of channels for any metal ion of
interest.
We place the Glu/Asp residue in the x-y plane with the Ca2+ ion, with the distance (Rs) between
the Ca2+ ion and the C alpha (Cα) atom of the Glu (or Asp), regardless of rotamer, which we
refer to below as the radius of the selectivity filter. A second residue with unspecified amino acid
identity was then placed at a displacement (h) below the Glu/Asp residue along the z-axis at a
distance of (RB) to the z-axis to define the radius of the pore exit. Given a group of sampled
values of Rs and RB, four or six-fold symmetry was applied around the z-axis to generate the
framework of the pore with the selectivity filter at the pore entrance. Using the generative
method RFdiffusion, the selectivity filter and exit pore-defining residues were connected using
helices to form the lining of the homooligomeric pore, and these helices were then extended into
multi-secondary structure interacting subunits. Together, the selectivity filter generation and
RFdiffusion scaffolding calculations generate multipass transmembrane protein backbones with
central channels and different filter geometries. Sequences were designed using the deep
learning-based protein sequence design method ProteinMPNN with position-specific amino acid
constraints: the selectivity filter residues were fixed at Glu or Asp, the lipid-facing surface
residues were constrained to be hydrophobic, and the remaining pore-lining residues were
disfavored to be charged to avoid interference with ion permeation through the pore. A diverse
set of designed channels with varying selectivity filter geometries (the tetramers are denoted
CalC4, and the hexamers CalC6) was predicted by AlphaFold2 to assemble into structures that
closely resembled the design models (Fig. 2).

To identify functional channels, we used the same experimental workflow as described in our
initial proposal: 1) a cell-based flux assay with Fura-2 AM as the ion-responsive dye and Ba2+ as
the surrogate ion for Ca2+ was used to screen for designs showing divalent ion permeability in
cells; 2) designs showing increased divalent ion flux were expressed in Escherichia coli (E. coli),
purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography from the membrane fraction, and analyzed by size
exclusion chromatography (SEC) and negative-stain electron microscopy (ns-EM); 3) whole-cell
patch-clamp experiments were performed to study the conductance and ion selectivity of the
designed channels.
Within each distinct design group (regarding pore size the symmetry), designs with increased
divalent ion flux could be found, and they eluted as expected for the target oligomeric states in
SEC and formed pore-containing particles on ns-EM grids (Fig. 3).

To characterize the ion conductance and selectivity of the designed channels in detail, we
proceeded with the two most promising designs in different symmetries, CalC4_24 and
CalC6_3, to patch-clamp experiments. When the Ca2+ concentration was changed from 0.02
mM to 10 mM in the bath solution, currents measured at −100 mV were observed to increase for
both CalC4_24 and CalC6_3. This increase of inward currents was reversible and was
diminished when the bath solution was changed back to 0.02 mM [Ca2+] solution. To examine
the specificity of the channels for different cations, the 10 mM Ca2+ in the extracellular solution
was substituted with 40 mM Na+, 10 mM Sr2+, or 10 mM Mg2+. Both CalC4_24 and CalC6_3
showed ~5-fold larger current in 10 mM [Ca2+] than in 40 mM [Na+] solution at −100 mV. The
relative conductances of the divalent cations for both designs followed the order Ca2+ > Sr2+ >
Mg2+. Both channels were blocked by 10 µM La3+ (Fig. 5). Taken together, the designed
channels exhibit electrophysiological properties expected for Ca2+ channels: inward rectification
in the presence of extracellular Ca2+, higher relative conductances to Ca2+ than to other cations,
and blocking by lanthanum, which are also observed for several native Ca2+ channels (Cav,
TRPV6, CRAC16–18). Thus, our approach of arranging Glu/Asp carboxylate residues around a
central pore generates Ca2+ dependent channels as intended.

To ascertain the accuracy of the design of the selectivity filter geometry and overall channel
architecture, we sought to determine the structure of CalC6_3 using cryo-electron microscopy
(cryo-EM). We applied a fusion protein strategy to facilitate structural determination by
connecting structurally-validated designed helical repeat (DHR) proteins to the C-terminus of
CalC6_3 via straight helices (in the original construct, the top views of the protein particles could
not readily be separated from side views during 2D classification due to the spherical shape of
the detergent-bound membrane protein). We proceeded with cryo-EM data collection with the
fusion construct, and obtained a three-dimensional reconstruction to a global resolution of 3.75
Å with C6 symmetry (Fig. 6), and were able to build a structure model based on the density
map. In the cryo-EM structure, the density for the Glu-ring selectivity filter density and the
His-ring below the filter was clearly evident when contoured at and below 4 σ level, suggesting
pre-organization of the Glu-ring by the His-ring via potential hydrogen bond interactions.
Although the sidechains were not well resolved given the resolution of the cryoEM map, the
geometry of the selectivity filter, defined by the Cα atoms of the Glu residues, was almost
exactly as designed (backbone RMSDs of 1.04 Å and 1.26 Å for monomer and complex
alignment, respectively; Fig. 6).

To directly test the function of the glutamate selectivity residues of CalC6_3, we mutated them
to leucine and measured the Ca2+ conductance. The predicted structure of the E101L mutant
was almost identical to that of CalC6_3. The purified E101L protein assembled into
homogenous pore-containing particles by ns-EM indistinguishable from the wild-type (E101)
pore, and in Hi5 cells, the E101L mutant expressed at the same level as the wild-type design.
Electrophysiology measurements showed that the leucine substitutions reduced the Ca2+
conductance of the CalC6_3 channel to that of the negative control (~ 4 pA pF-1 at –100 mV,
Fig. x5). Since the overall structure of the pore is not affected, and the size of the orifice formed
by the Leu residues is nearly identical to that formed by the Glu residues, this high sensitivity to
negative charge to nonpolar substitution strongly suggests that ions flow through the central
pore as designed, and that direct interactions between selectivity filter residues and the
permeant ions determine ion selectivity and flux.
Our approach of designing Ca2+ channels based on Ca2+-interacting structural motifs, which is
currently in revision at Nature and available as a preprint19, can be readily extended to the
design of ion channels that are selectively permeable to xenobiotic metal ions, and enabling
studies of the physical principles underlying ion channel selectivity, using the metal ion-binding
motifs identified in Objective Ia. To further meet this challenge, we reason that several
adaptations to our current workflow are required. First, our current design paradigm of placing a
single side chain on one protein chain, where multiple subunits are required to assemble into a
homooligomeric pore-containing protein, imposes physical constraints on the achievable
symmetry number and the permissible protein-ion binding modes. These constraints would be
especially limiting for ions requiring high coordination numbers (e.g., CN > 6) and those favoring
diverse, asymmetric interactions (e.g., synergistically involving both polar and charged
residues), which are interaction patterns often observed for selective binding in Objective Ia.
Second, because many xenobiotic metal ions are toxic to cells, ideally, in vitro platforms are
required to measure ion conductance and selectivity. Standard experimental approaches
typically use purified proteins reconstituted in liposomes and planar lipid bilayers. Recordings in
these artificial lipid membranes could not only circumvent the challenge of single-channel
recordings in live cells but also provide high-resolution kinetic analysis at the single-molecule
level. Notably, our previous work on de novo designed Ca²⁺ channels underscored the
importance of fine-tuning such kinetic aspects of protein-ion interactions, as we observed that a
binding site that was overly tight (excessively negatively charged) would lead to channel

blockage. However, reconstituting de novo designed alpha-helical transmembrane proteins into
such lipid systems remains a significant technical hurdle.
Transmembrane Beta Barrel Channels
We hypothesize that transmembrane beta barrels (TMBBs) could potentially overcome the
aforementioned challenges we encountered with alpha-helical transmembrane pores. In
particular, we reasoned that if lanthanide ions inherently have slower flux than Ca2+ or other
softer metals or a tendency to high dwell times in selectivity filters, then bulk measurements with
many lanthanide channels may be the only reliable means to characterize their flux. This is most
readily accomplished with TMBBs, which are more easily produced in large quantities and can
spontaneously insert into membranes from detergent micelles. While TMBBs are classically very
challenging to design20,21, we are improving our design methods for TMBBs22 and can now use
AI-based protein design to scaffold motifs with reasonable success rates. Small TMBBs are very
sequence efficient, allowing the entire structure of the pore to be encoded by a single protein
chain and enabling the scaffolding of non-symmetric motifs, which could potentially overcome
the first challenge mentioned above. Furthermore, we have established a robust planar bilayer
recording platform for de novo designed TMBBs22–24 using the Nanion Orbit 16 TC device, which
is ideal for measuring ion conductance and selectivity in vitro and thereby could potentially
overcome the second challenge.
To investigate this, we set out to design TMBBs with constrictions designed to act as lanthanide
filters. As discussed above, we hypothesized that the constriction would need to be rather weak
to allow ions to flow through the pore, so we chose a relatively low coordination number of 6 and
allowed no more than two carboxylate moieties to avoid balancing the charge of the trivalent
cations. The residues were arrayed in a roughly planar geometry to allow passage of the ion
through the center (Fig. 7a). We positioned the filter atoms along the pore axis of TMB10_165,
a well-characterized 10-stranded beta barrel which served as a scaffold for these designs, and
used partial diffusion with RFdiffusion2 to design the locations of the residues hosting these
atoms within the pore and optimize the backbone around them. Finally, we redesigned the pore
lining to keep the critical Tyr-Gly-Glu mortise-and-tenon motifs and excluded all other
glutamates and aspartates from the pore. We found that the TMB10_165 backbone could
accommodate a wide range of filter locations according to AF2 structure predictions (Fig. 7b).
We tested a small set of designs on supported lipid bilayers and found that they could insert into
the membrane and conduct KCl, but we observed that the addition of a small volume of
concentrated TbCl3 caused signal blockage that survived voltage reversal and membrane
reformations. We hypothesized that lanthanide currents were simply too low to be measured in
single or few channel experiments at lanthanide concentrations that did not disrupt phospholipid
bilayers, so we next focused on bulk flux measurements.
First, we set up a liposome flux assay where TMBBs refolded in detergent are added to
liposomes packaging a fast and sensitive lanthanide-specific sensing protein, which changes a
FRET signal in the presence of any lanthanide. Upon addition of protein to liposomes
pre-equilibrated with 250 uM LaCl3, we observe a range of insertion/flux kinetics which
eventually come to the same equilibrium above the buffer-only background but less than the
ionomycin positive control, which can penetrate multilayer vesicles present in the solution (Fig.

7c). To our knowledge, this represents the first evidence of lanthanide transport across a
membrane mediated by a small and designable protein pore, and not by a large non-specific
protein pore, transporter, or membrane-permeable ionophore as it seems to be done in nature25.

Next, we tested these proteins in macroscopic membranes to demonstrate their application for
bulk metal separations in collaboration with the Kumar lab at the University of Texas at Austin,
who recently reported the development of a water/oil emulsion material which is composed of a
massive network of droplet interface bilayers (DIBs) called Jammed Interconnected Bilayer
Emulsions (JIBEs)26. Channels inserted into this material form the only route for ions to travel
between the external reservoirs through the water droplets. This enables bulk electrodialysis,
where selectivity is determined by the incorporated channels. We selected TMB12_3 (a
12-stranded model TMBB with a large pore), TMB10_165 (the 10-stranded TMBB that serves as
the parent backbone for the redesigns), and RK2394 (a well-expressed redesign with good
insertion/flux kinetics) to test in JIBEs.

We found that our proteins can be inserted into JIBEs and support KCl conductance (Fig. 7d,e).
TMB12_3 produced the best conductance, possibly due to a combination of relatively fast
insertion and its large pore. The RK2394-JIBE was more conductive than the TMB10_165-JIBE
in these conditions, even though they have similar pore sizes, which could result from a
difference in insertion efficiency or differences in the pore lining, with RK2394 being significantly
less polar than TMB10_165 and therefore less likely to bind and impede the flow of water and
ions. As a proof-of-concept, because of its high observed conductance, we used the
TMB12_3-JIBEs to test the direct conductance of lanthanides. We found a surprising difference
in conductance depending on which LnCl3 was in the buffer, with a strong preference for Eu
over La (~20x) and Yb (~1.5x) (Fig. 7f). This suggests that TMB12_3 forms some kind of
interaction with lanthanide ions, which is able to influence flow rates, an observation that is
revisited in Objective IIIa. These exciting results demonstrate that protein channels can
discriminate between lanthanide ions, and we believe that the JIBE platform will be a convenient
and useful way of testing de novo ion channels made for a wide range of ions.

Objective II: Discover design principles for arraying electronically or photonically active
 substrates in protein fibers for nanoscale energy transport.
Objective IIa:
The goal of this objective was to develop conductive protein nanowires that are more robust and
easier to manipulate than the current natural examples. We successfully created protein fibers
that scaffold a chlorophyll-binding motif in an unbounded chain. Fluorescence microscopy
demonstrated that chlorophylls were incorporated into extended fiber-like structures spanning
more than one micron in length (Fig. 8). The chlorophylls remained fluorescent while bound to
the protein, which indicates that they were not quenched by binding and therefore retained the
potential for energy transfer. Negative-stain electron microscopy confirmed the presence of
fibers 100-500 nanometers in length (Fig. 8). Despite these promising results, however, the
fibers proved to be too fragile to undergo functional assays or sufficient imaging for
high-resolution structural determination. Analysis of the protein subunit interface suggested that
it was too small to support robust fiber formation, and attempts to strengthen this interface were
unsuccessful.
To overcome these limitations, we pursued an alternative approach of incorporating pigment
binding into robust pre-existing protein fibers that are already structurally characterized by
cryo-EM. In this strategy, synthetic chlorophylls functionalized with NHS ester groups are
covalently linked to redesigned peptides, which are then arrayed along the protein fibers27. By
covalently attaching pigments to these stable scaffolds, we expect to generate ordered
assemblies with repeating chlorophyll units positioned at controlled distances. This ongoing
work offers a promising route to studying electron and energy transfer in a more stable and
reproducible context than was possible with the original fiber designs.
In parallel, we identified carbon nanotube (CNT)-based protein fibers as a complementary route
toward achieving long-range conductivity that could solve some of the experimental challenges
encountered by the chlorophyll fibers. CNTs are inherently conductive materials and have
already been applied in electrobiochemistry for direct electron transfer28, though coupling
efficiency is greatly limited by a lack of control over enzyme orientation on the nanotube
surface29. Using our repeat protein RFdiffusion, we designed helical repeat protein fibers
intended to wrap around CNTs and provide defined binding geometries. With this system, the
CNT serves as a template for fiber formation so the protein component can tolerate, and even
benefit from, weaker protein-protein interfaces. Fiber length is determined by the CNT length,
which can exceed 0.5 meters30, and the only fibers in solution are those that wrap CNTs and are
thus conductive. CNT binding was initially screened by co-sonicating protein CNT mixtures and
selecting designs that give stable dispersions. We identified several proteins that dispersed
CNTs efficiently, and nsEM 2D class averages revealed characteristic zig-zag patterns
consistent with helical wrapping, which matched the intended helical pitch of the design. While
long fibers only appeared in samples sonicated in the presence of CNTs, the CNTs themselves
could not be observed by nsEM, likely due to having similar contrast to the uranyl formate stain,
so CryoEM was used to confirm the presence of CNTs within the fibers.

Fig. 8. Top: the design model of a fiber that binds chlorophylls in an unbounded conductive chain. Middle:
fluorescence microscopy images of the fibers showing chlorophylls binding and fluorescing in a length
over 1 um. Bottom: negative stain electron microscopy showing the details of fibers of lengths 100-500

We hypothesized that the relative proportion of particles belonging to the zig-zag class reports
on the oriented binding efficiency of the design, and approximately 13–50% of particles belong
to this class, depending on the design, with the other particles resulting from non-specific
binding of folded or denatured proteins. This represents a potentially large improvement in
binding efficiency compared to ~2% for some of the best native proteins, but we are now
working on optimizing this further by exploring new design hypotheses to improve stability, such
as reducing non-interface aromatic residues and increasing buried polar character31.
Additionally, we have found that 3D reconstructions made from CryoEM datasets are
significantly less detailed than those from nsEM datasets, and so high-resolution structural
characterization has been elusive. We have begun collaborating with Brent Nannenga at
Arizona State University to attempt to improve cryo-EM data collection and processing for these
hybrid protein–CNT assemblies.

Our proposal included plans to screen designs for conductivity by placing sacrificial electron
donors and acceptors on conductive fibers at such dilution that electron transfer could only be
possible through the fiber. While this still represents an important feature to demonstrate for
downstream biochemical applications, the data generated by this technique is information-poor
and would convolute the many reasons for poor efficiency. Therefore, we have started a
collaboration with the Minteer Lab at the University of Utah to characterize the electrochemical
properties of these CNT-binding proteins and other designed cofactors using cyclic voltammetry,
which can reveal more information about design failures and be used to inform new rounds of
design. To facilitate this, their group is developing an automated cyclic voltammetry platform
capable of high-throughput, quantitative measurements of electron transfer efficiency. This is
expected to provide a more robust and generalizable framework for characterizing conductive
nanowires.
Objective IIb:
The goal of Objective IIb was to investigate the possibility of generating superradiance (SR)
from protein-scaffolded arrays of lanthanide ions (LnRings). In our initial proposal, we described
the design of cyclic protein assemblies that position lanthanide ions at distances well below the
theoretical threshold required for cooperative emission. One preliminary design successfully
scaffolded lanthanide ions at ~1.8 nm spacings in ordered assemblies with cyclic or dihedral
symmetry. SR manifests as a collective and enhanced emission from a set of organized emitters
entangled by a common electromagnetic stimulus much larger than the spacing between the
emitters. The resulting increase in the rate of photon emission can be measured by
time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), but in order to make an accurate measurement the
individual arrays/LnRing particles need to be well separated from each other. We calculated that
the signal from a sparse monolayer of LnRing proteins would be too low to observe over the
noise of the instrumentation available to us. To overcome this limitation, we considered using
dilute suspensions of LnRings instead. However, TRPL requires that proteins be embedded in
optically transparent materials, and water ice is unsuitable for this purpose. As a result, we are
exploring the use of polymers such as PDMS as embedding matrices. Since PDMS is
hydrophobic, additives such as starch or trehalose may be required to mimic water and
preserve protein stability during polymerization32.
In parallel, we experimentally analyzed the hydration state of terbium ions bound to the LnRing
proteins33 and found that sub-stoichiometrically loaded terbium ions were coordinated by an
average of 1.6 water molecules (Fig. 9). O–H bonds of waters ligated to lanthanide ions can
resonate with their excited states, reducing coherence times and thereby reducing the likelihood
of observing superradiance. Replacing water with hydroxide-rich polymers thus would serve to
both preserve the native fold of the protein in a dehydrated and hydrophobic environment and
also eliminate routes for decoherence. Addressing these experimental complications remains an
important step toward enabling SR measurements.\

Fig. 9. Time-resolved photoluminescence of terbium plot at 0% D2O with double exponential fits.
In the initial proposal, we discussed several methods of reducing the conformational
heterogeneity observed in the current LnRing, but based on our success with Objective IIIb, it
seems most effective to measure water coordination numbers of single-ion C3 oligomeric
designs from Objective Ia and then scaffold those which completely dehydrate the ions into
icosahedral nanocages. While nanocages are not completely free from conformational
heterogeneity, we now understand the design rules in these systems to control it to achieve
either conformationally pure T=1 nanocages34 or higher T-number cages, which can create
larger arrays35. With this in mind, we have paused direct SR work on the current LnRing
constructs and shifted our focus to Objective Ia.
At the same time, we recognized that the LnRing proteins may have applications beyond SR. In
particular, their ordered lanthanide arrays may exhibit interesting magnetic properties regardless
of water coordination. We have started a collaboration with Stefan Stoll at the University of
Washington to investigate these proteins using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR). EPR
has the potential to probe the spatial organization of lanthanide ions and could enable the use of
LnRing proteins as quantum sensors of spatial proximity, distinguishing between spin centers
inside versus outside of the ring. Furthermore, EPR measurements performed on
PDMS-embedded suspensions will allow us to confirm that the structural integrity of the arrays
is maintained for future TRPL measurements.

Objective III: Discover design principles for specific metal ion-inducible protein
 oligomerization, metal-ion gating of nanopores, and metal-ion governed formation
 of closed protein nanocages and unbounded 3D assemblies.
The overarching goal of Objective III depends on the generation of metal-inducible cyclic
oligomers based on selective binders identified in Objective Ia. With Objective Ia still in
progress, we sought to establish proof-of-concept procedures for accomplishing the goals of
Objective III by utilizing early hits identified in Objective Ia or by generating new purpose-made
binders inspired by the early results of Objective Ia.
Objective IIIa:
Metal-induced protein assembly is usually studied and described in terms of equilibrium binding
properties, but very tight binders can take days to months or longer to reach true equilibrium,
and many applications of tight and specific metal-induced binding require functions in shorter
time spans. Thus, we set out to study the relationship between selectivity at equilibrium versus
non-equilibrium by characterizing selective metal-induced binding at several timescales.
We have made several attempts to generate inducible oligomers with our current products of
Objective Ia, but no attempt so far has resulted in metal-induced assembly, with designs either
remaining assembled in the apo state or never assembling. Understanding the reasons for this
is likely to be important for understanding selective binding itself because it involves a balance
between on-target metal binding, off-target metal binding, and subunit-subunit binding, which
are likely to be interconnected. Current design methods employed in Objective Ia pay little
attention to the size and structure of the subunit-subunit interactions and may be inadvertently
selecting for interfaces that are categorically too large to be made weak enough through
sequence design to fall into a metal-inducible binding strength regime. Advances in the
prediction of protein-protein and protein-metal binding affinities would be greatly helpful to this
effort, and these are now underway in our group with models being trained on data collected in
Objective Ia and from other areas of work in the IPD. However, we reasoned that most of the
downstream proofs-of-concept could be done with existing binders from Objective Ia or with
purpose-made binders, so we have focused on completing the other objectives and working on
improving design and prediction methods.
To gain insight into the non-equilibrium dynamics of metal recognition and binding in
metal-induced assemblies, we had proposed the use of transmembrane nanopores to report on
protein assembly with high temporal resolution by measuring changes in currents caused by
complex formation. For our initial fusions, we started with variants of the binders from Objective
Ia, which were stripped down to helical hairpins with only enough of the original structure to host
the metal binding site. These chunks were inserted separately into 3 or 4 loops on the trans side
of TMB12_3, using RFdiffusion and MPNN to build and design loops, and with final filtering
carried out using Rosetta FastRelax. These were placed such that their folding/assembly would
occur over the pore and block currents. These constructs, however, proved unstable and tended
to aggregate rather than fold productively. To address this, we more closely replicated the
conditions used to generate our CHD nanopore sensors24 and generated and screened new,
smaller single-chain binders that had less exposed hydrophobics than the stripped-down
binders and were designed as pseudocycles using RFdiffusion and MPNN, and filtered with

AF2. Once tested in the wet lab for lanthanide binding using a Fura-2 dye competition assay, the
designs with the highest affinity were selected and fused to TMB12_3, like before.
Fusions that were expressed and purified successfully were tested on supported lipid bilayers
using the Nanion Orbit 16 system, where single-molecule experiments were carried out in the
absence and presence of lanthanides or calcium. While many fusions showed
metal-independent gating, others had a range of behaviors.
Interestingly, we observed some gating behavior in unfused TMB12_3 in the presence of high
concentrations of lanthanides (Fig. 10), and upon investigation using the dye competition assay,
we found that this design shows some binding to lanthanides on its own (which supports our
findings in the JIBE system described in Objective Ib). Before additional fusions and
characterizations of lanthanide-binding domains can take place, we will need to identify and
knock out the background lanthanide binding present in TMB12_3 to ensure that the
measurements of the fused lanthanide-binding domain are independent of the pore. The lack of
design tools to accurately predict metal-ion interactions in pores such as the one studied here
and in Objective Ib whose pore cavities are much larger than those of typical ion channels,
presents a challenge for such fusion-based sensor design. Instead, we have now focused our
attention on making smaller ion-channel-like pores directly with appropriately defined metal-ion
coordination geometries and amino acid motifs in the hope of gaining better tractability for the
analysis of non-equilibrium ionic interactions. Nevertheless, these results show that this method
of producing very small binders results in complexes that seem to show metal-dependent
assembly and should serve as inspiration for building soluble versions of these constructs for
study at equilibrium and for downstream applications.

Objective IIIb:
Our initial proposal for Objective IIIb was to explore how cooperativity of assembly influences
binding selectivity by scaffolding selective oligomers or motifs at subunit interfaces of protein
nanocages, sampling cage symmetry and choice of binding site with a variety of pre-measured
strengths, dynamics, and selectivities identified in Objective IIIa. With work still underway to
accomplish Objectives Ia and IIIa, we focused our efforts here in establishing working protocols
for designing higher-order assemblies with metal-dependent properties, which we could later
apply to scaffolding dynamic and selective interfaces into nanocages for Objective IIIb and 2D
arrays and 3D crystals for Objective IIIc. We selected a calcium-dependent assembly to focus
this work on and used the calcium-binding NH07_C2, a design identified from early work in
Objective Ia with a crystal structure in complex with calcium, as a model interface. We

generated 41 protein oligomers based on the binding motif used in NH07_C2 and tested them
for calcium binding. Using the 36 strongest binders among these oligomers, we scaffolded
variable-lengths of the protein sequences and backbone on either side of the coordinating
residues (Asp 51) into symmetric protein nanocages using an improved version of symmetric
RFdiffusion currently in development. Initial designs were generated by scaffolding the
coordinating aspartates along the 7 residues flanking the aspartate on either side, a total of 15
residues on the coordinating helical secondary structure.
Several designs maintained their assembly state, independent of calcium concentration (e.g.,
Fig. 11a,c), while two appeared to change assembly state into higher-order assemblies as a
function of calcium concentration (Fig. 11b,d). Upon further inspection of the responsive design,
we determined that the target assembly state was not achieved. Design L2_B8 appeared to be
slightly truncated by mass spectroscopy, and design L2_D1 was difficult to reconstruct using
guidance-free 2D class averaging of particles from nsEM. Though our target states were not
achieved, calcium-dependent assembly was demonstrated, which indicates the feasibility of this
design strategy.

Objective IIIc:

While we have not yet attempted to insert new metal-dependent interfaces into 2D arrays and
3D crystals, we have succeeded in advancing our processes for designing these materials36. In
a previous outcome from this report, we presented a method for using docking to produce 3D
crystals12. That method was limited to oligomers that serendipitously had good shape
complementarity for each other and required the redesign of interacting surfaces, which can
have cryptic influences on assembly. Our new design method uses WORMS and RFdiffusion to
rigidly join pre-validated interfaces into the specific geometries required for unbounded
assembly without the design of new interfaces. Importantly, this method is capable of designing
common building blocks that can be used in all three types of higher-order assemblies, giving us
a truly modular route to testing the influence of symmetry and degree of cooperativity on
assembly behavior, which was a goal of Objectives IIIb and IIIc.

4.0 What opportunities for training and professional development has the project
provided?
The Department of Biochemistry at the University of Washington is committed to the
development of our postdoctoral fellows and graduate students. These researchers continue to
receive training daily through interactions with Dr. David Baker with regard to the work carried
out in pursuit of the goals mentioned above. Dr. Baker also provides mentorship in teaching,
mentoring, collaboration, and grantsmanship. The Baker group meets twice a week for research
presentations at which each group member takes a turn presenting his or her own research.
The researchers in this project learn much from these discussions, as well as from daily informal
discussions with other members of the group who have an extremely wide area of expertise.
Additionally, both graduate students and postdoctoral fellows receive professional development
as they attend and present their research at various conferences, including Rosettacon, an
annual conference focused on the computational protein design software used in this research.
To develop individual career goals, researchers prepare Individual Development Plans (IDPs) in
consultation with Dr. Baker. Details of the IDP include short- and long-term career goals,
discipline-specific knowledge and research skills, development of mentoring/teaching skills, and
setting goals for progress (presentations, publications, funding). Finally, Dr. Baker and the
researcher prepare a timeline for the individual to move on to the next step of their career by
identifying contacts and opportunities, preparing a CV and research summary, and attending

other professional development opportunities such as conferences, networking events, and
informational interviews.

We have published multiple papers on the results of this grant in high-impact, peer-reviewed
journals. Though primarily read by our industry’s professionals, these journals are open-access,
thus making our research publicly available. Many trainees have presented their research at
national and international conferences. The program’s principal investigators have also actively
shared their knowledge at conferences and meetings.
