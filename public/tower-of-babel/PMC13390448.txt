Introduction

Hydatid cysts, caused by Echinococcus granulosus, primarily affect the liver but can involve the lungs in up to 20–30% of cases [1]. Pulmonary hydatid cyst rupture is a rare but serious complication, often presenting with acute respiratory symptoms [2]. Rupture occurs in approximately 5–10% of pulmonary hydatid cases, leading to complications such as allergic reactions, pneumonitis, or secondary infection [3]. In many cases, the clinical presentation can closely resemble bacterial pneumonia, tuberculosis, or eosinophilic lung diseases, making timely diagnosis challenging, particularly in nonendemic urban centers. So, early diagnosis and appropriate management are crucial to prevent complications [4, 5]. Herein, we report a case of bilateral lung infiltration secondary to a ruptured hydatid cyst, emphasizing its diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old Persian woman presented to the emergency department with a 1-day history of sudden-onset dyspnea, productive cough with whitish sputum, and high-grade fever. She reported no prior significant medical conditions. She denied any history of chronic disease, allergies, recent travel, or exposure to livestock or dogs. She was not on any medications and had no relevant family history.

On examination, her vital signs were as follows: temperature, 39 °C; pulse rate, 120 beats per minute; respiratory rate, 22 breaths per minute; and blood pressure, 105/75 mmHg. Oxygen saturation was 88% on room air. Lung auscultation revealed bilateral fine crackles. Cardiovascular and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. There was no hepatosplenomegaly, skin rash, or peripheral lymphadenopathy.

Laboratory tests showed a leukocyte count of 10,000/µL with 12% eosinophils. Serial complete blood counts revealed progressive eosinophilia, reaching 44.7%, despite a normal total leukocyte count (Table 1).

Chest radiography (Fig. 1) demonstrated bilateral lung infiltrations, and computed tomography (CT) of the chest (Fig. 2) revealed a multilayered cyst in the left lung along with bilateral diffuse fluffy infiltrations.

Given the serial imaging findings and laboratory results, a ruptured pulmonary hydatid cyst with associated bilateral pneumonitis was suspected. Countercurrent immunoelectrophoresis (CCIEP) for Echinococcus granulosus antibodies was strongly positive. CCIEP is a serological test used to detect antibodies against E. granulosus, with 80–90% sensitivity in pulmonary cases.

The patient was initiated on intravenous levofloxacin and vancomycin, followed by albendazole 400 mg twice daily. After 2 weeks of treatment, her symptoms improved significantly, and follow-up imaging revealed only the ruptured cyst without further infiltration (Fig. 3a, b).

a, b Follow-up images after albendazole therapy showing resolution of lung infiltration with the residual ruptured cyst (white arrow)

Surgical resection of the lung cyst was performed after 6 months of albendazole therapy. The surgery was delayed to allow medical stabilization and reduction of cystic activity and pneumonitis severity, minimizing the risk of intraoperative spread and complications. No prior history of medical or surgical interventions related to this condition were recorded. One year postoperatively, the patient remained in good health (Table 2).

Diagnostic challenges

The diagnosis of a ruptured pulmonary hydatid cyst can be challenging due to its resemblance to other infectious and inflammatory lung diseases. In this case, the presence of bilateral lung infiltrations initially raised concerns for bacterial pneumonia or eosinophilic lung disease. Additionally, the absence of a clear history of exposure to Echinococcus made early suspicion difficult. However, the combination of progressive eosinophilia, characteristic imaging findings, and positive serologic testing facilitated an accurate diagnosis.

Discussion

Hydatid disease remains endemic in the Mediterranean region, Central Asia, Australia, North and East Africa, and South America [6]. The lungs are the second most frequently affected organ after the liver, involved in approximately 25% of cases. Pulmonary involvement, though less common, can be associated with serious complications such as cyst rupture, which may lead to inflammatory responses, secondary infections, and respiratory distress [2].

In the present case, diagnosis was facilitated by the presence of a multilayered cystic lesion on CT, bilateral pneumonitis, and progressive eosinophilia, ultimately confirmed by positive serology for Echinococcus granulosus. The imaging features, including diffuse fluffy bilateral infiltrates and a multilayered cyst with internal air-fluid levels, are consistent with previously reported radiologic patterns of ruptured hydatid cysts [7].

Eosinophilia, while present in less than 25% of noncomplicated hydatid disease cases, has been reported to increase significantly in ruptured cysts due to systemic allergic or inflammatory responses. In our patient, eosinophil counts rose from 12% to a peak of 44.7%, which is markedly elevated and strongly supportive of cyst rupture and antigenic exposure.

The differential diagnosis in such presentations includes bacterial pneumonia, eosinophilic lung disease, mycoses, and cavitary tuberculosis [4]. The bilateral nature of the infiltrations and the absence of a known exposure history initially made the diagnosis challenging. However, the combination of clinical deterioration, progressive eosinophilia, radiologic evidence, and positive countercurrent immunoelectrophoresis provided a strong diagnostic basis.

Similar cases have been reported in endemic countries such as Turkey, India, and Iran, with presentations ranging from acute dyspnea and fever to hemoptysis and anaphylaxis. For instance, Zingg et al. reported a case of ruptured pulmonary hydatid cyst in a 7-year-old girl with dyspnea and skin rash, resolving after albendazole therapy [8]. In our case, despite severe symptoms, the patient responded well to medical management, including antibiotics for superinfection and albendazole to reduce cyst viability.

Management of pulmonary hydatid cysts typically involves surgical resection, which remains the definitive treatment [9]. In our case, cystectomy was delayed for 6 months to allow for medical stabilization and reduction of cyst burden, as supported by previous studies showing improved surgical outcomes after prolonged albendazole therapy [10]. Benzimidazoles such as albendazole are particularly valuable in patients with multiple, ruptured, or inoperable cysts, or as adjunctive preoperative therapy to minimize recurrence risk [11, 12].

Intraoperative risks such as bronchopleural fistula, empyema, and pneumothorax are notable, though our patient had no complications and remained asymptomatic at 12-month follow-up. This underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach, incorporating both medical and surgical strategies for optimal outcomes in complicated pulmonary hydatidosis.

Conclusion

Ruptured pulmonary hydatid cysts can present with acute respiratory distress and bilateral lung infiltration. Early diagnosis through imaging and serology, followed by a combination of medical and surgical management, can lead to favorable outcomes. Clinicians in endemic regions should maintain a high index of suspicion for hydatid disease in patients with unexplained pulmonary symptoms.

Learning points:

Hydatid disease should be considered in the differential diagnosis of bilateral lung infiltrations, especially in patients from endemic regions presenting with acute respiratory symptoms and eosinophilia.
