Association of primary caregiver anxiety and caregiving role with early development of preschool children: a population‑based study
Authors: Jiaxin Guo, Jing Wen, Guicun Yang, Ruoling Xian, Yong Zhang, Zumin Shi, Yan Zhao, Yong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHome caregiving environments are critical to early childhood development (ECD), but evidence on the primary caregiver factors in this link is limited. This study examines associations between primary caregiver anxiety, primary caregiver role, and ECD outcomes.MethodsThis population-based survey was conducted in January 2024 across all 38 districts of Chongqing, China, using a stratified cluster sampling strategy. The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD-7) and the Chinese Early Human Capacity Index (CHeHCI) were used to assess primary caregivers' anxiety and ECD levels, respectively. The primary caregiver role in the family was self-reported.ResultsIn fully adjusted regression models (model 4), primary caregivers' anxiety was independently associated with child development delay, with an OR of 1.89 (95% CI: 1.75–2.05). Additionally, fathers’ and grandparents' nurturing were positively associated with child development delay, with ORs of 1.24 (95% CI: 1.05–1.46) and 1.12 (95% CI: 1.01–1.25), respectively. Furthermore, the interaction between only-child status and screen time with anxiety concerning ECD is significant. Similarly, the interaction between only-child status and the primary caregiver role concerning ECD is significant.ConclusionsPrimary caregiver anxiety and paternal or grandparental nurturing are significantly associated with delayed ECD, with only-child status and screen time further interacting with these factors. These findings highlight the need for family‑centered interventions targeting primary caregiver mental health.

Introduction

Early childhood development (ECD) refers to a child's full physical, cognitive, and socio-emotional development from conception through age eight. For preschool children in particular, ECD assessment helps to understand their school readiness and forecast future academic outcomes [1]. Yet globally, over 250 million children fail to meet age-appropriate developmental milestones [2], often closely related to adverse conditions in their immediate home caregiving environments—such as parental mental health, primary caregiver role, and family structure [3, 4]. Therefore, identifying and addressing the key adverse factors within the home caregiving environment is essential, with far-reaching implications for preschool children’s health, development, and long-term well-being.

Available evidence suggests that caregiver mental health can compromise early children’s health, growth, and development through two key pathways. Caregivers experiencing anxiety or depressive symptoms may fail to respond appropriately to children’s developmental needs, negatively affecting their cognitive, motor, and socio-emotional growth [5, 6]. In addition, high parenting anxiety and depression can undermine sensitive interactions in caregiver–child, compromising attachment security and further hindering early children's overall development [7]. Depression is prone to the perinatal period, but there is greater anxiety in parents with preschool children, making it a more prominent parenting risk for this stage [8, 9]. For the link between caregivers' mental health and ECD, attention should be focused on caregivers’ anxiety and the stage of preschool.

Furthermore, the primary caregiver role is a core determinant of ECD [10] across diverse parenting practices. Systematic reviews show that grandparents’ indulgent dietary care for preschool grandchildren is associated with adverse child development outcomes, e.g., obesity [11, 12]. Children’s speech development, a key area of overall development, was also affected by the speech performance of different primary caregivers [13]. Most existing studies tend to examine isolated domains of child development (e.g., physical, social- emotional, and cognitive) associated with different caregivers, yet they largely overlook the relationship between primary caregiver factors and children's holistic development.

Primary caregiver characteristics are a crucial determinant of preschool children's ECD [14], yet most existing research assumes this association is uniform across family structures, overlooking the potential moderating role of family structure. Only-child families exhibit a more concentrated allocation of parenting resources (time, financial, and emotional) compared to multi-child families [15]. Furthermore, parents of only children are more susceptible to caregiving stress, anxiety, and related psychological changes due to their limited parenting experience [16]. This suggests only-child family may moderate the association between primary caregiver factors and ECD, potentially leading to distinct interactive effects. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms of this moderation remain underexplored.

Given that the preschool period is the core stage for building school readiness, and primary caregivers are core factors for children’s capacity to adapt to formal schooling. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association of primary caregiver anxiety and caregiving role on ECD outcomes among preschool children in a city that alleviates absolute poverty in southwest China. Furthermore, we assessed the interaction of family structure (being an only child or not) with this association.

Methods

Participants

This population-based survey was conducted in January 2024 across all 38 districts of Chongqing, China. Using a stratified cluster sampling strategy, all districts in this city were divided into four strata based on their geographical location (Central City, West Chongqing, Southeast Chongqing, and Northeast Chongqing). Then, random sampling was performed within each cluster; the corresponding cluster samples were determined by economic development level, as provided by the Office of the Chief Government Statistician. A random electronic questionnaire-based survey was conducted with support from maternal and child health hospitals and nursery institutions in each region. Eligible participant pairs were included if they met the following criteria: (1) children aged 3 to 6 years; (2) primary caregivers who provided informed consent to participate in the study; and (3) families having resided in the Chongqing region for over six months.

Data collection procedures

Under the supervision of maternity and childcare hospitals and nurseries, trained data collectors investigated primary caregivers and their children. Household demographics, primary caregiver anxiety, caregiving role, and ECD outcomes were collected via an electronic questionnaire platform. After collecting the questionnaires, we reviewed their contents and excluded any with clear logical errors, including those that selected "No" in response to the question "I have answered all the above questions truthfully." Additionally, we rejected questionnaires with implausible completion times, defined as too brief (< P3) or excessively long (> P97). Finally, 26,636 participant pairs were included in the analysis, and the proportion of valid questionnaires after data cleaning was 91.17%.

Primary caregivers’ role in the family

We defined caregivers as individuals primarily responsible for the routine daily care of children, including biological parents, legal guardians, and other family relatives [17, 18]. Primary caregivers were operationalized as the subset of enrolled caregivers who provided the largest share of the child’s daily care. To verify that the participants met the primary caregiver eligibility criteria, we implemented two validation steps: First, the informed consent form explicitly stated that only individuals serving as the child’s primary caregiver were invited to participate in the study. Second, we added a necessary self-report confirmation item in the study questionnaire to verify participants’ primary caregiver role, with predefined response options including mother, father, grandparent, and other relatives.

Primary caregivers’ anxiety status

The caregiver's anxiety was assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD-7) [19], a reliable and valid measure for anxiety that has been widely used across diverse global settings [20, 21]. The GAD-7 includes 7 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). In addition, the total score ranged from 0 to 21 and was categorized as follows: 0–4 indicates minimal anxiety; 5–9, mild anxiety; 10–14, moderate anxiety; and 15–21, severe anxiety. In this study, we use a 5-point cutoff to distinguish between the anxiety and non-anxiety groups.

Early childhood development

We constructed a dichotomous variable indicating whether ECD is on track, based on scores from the Chinese Early Human Capacity Index (CHeHCI) [22]. The CHeHCI has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity in Chinese populations [23, 24]. Raw CHeHCI scores are calculated by dividing the total item score by the number of items administered, yielding a continuous metric ranging from 0 to 1, where 1 denotes the highest level of developmental attainment. The cutoff for distinguishing typically developing children from those at risk of developmental delay was derived from the age-specific normative table recommended by the CHeHCI development team [22]. Specifically, a child was classified as ECD on track if their overall CHeHCI score was greater than or equal to the 20th percentile of the pooled study sample within the corresponding 3-month age group (36–38, 39–41, 42–44, 45–47, 48–50, 51–53, 54–56, 57–59, 60–62, 63–65, 66–68, 69–71, and 72–74 months).

Covariates

We collected data on covariates related to children's biological characteristics, primary caregivers, family structure, and child health behaviors. The biological variables included the child's sex, age, and preterm birth status. Variables related to primary caregivers covered the primary caregiver's educational level and place of residence. Family structure variables included only-child status, left-behind status, single-parent household status, and annual household income. Additionally, screen time was considered, given its well-established association with adverse cognitive and social-emotional development in children [25].

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The Pearson chi-squared test was used to assess between-group differences in early developmental delay ratio across anxiety status and primary caregiver role strata. Pairwise group comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. In addition, Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to examine the independent associations between primary caregiver anxiety, caregiving role, and developmental delay in preschool children, adjusting for potential confounders. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were reported. Covariates included child age, sex, premature birth, residence, caregiver education, only-child status, left-behind status, single-parent household status, and screen time. In the subgroup analyses, the multiplicative interaction between primary caregivers' factors and covariates (sex, preterm birth, residence, primary caregivers’ highest education, only child, single-parent family, left-behind children, annual household income, and screen time of children) was examined by adding the product of the variables in the regression model. Data were analyzed using Stata version 17.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Human ethics

The Ethics Committees of the Chongqing Maternal and Child Health Hospital approved our study (approval number: 2024–006). All primary caregivers of the children participating in the current study signed informed consent.

Child development outcomes

In the total sample, approximately 14.3% of the children sampled were in the risk zone for overall developmental delay, whereas the remaining 85.7% were identified as developmentally on track. Regarding primary caregiver anxiety, children whose primary caregivers reported anxiety showed a higher proportion of being in the developmental delay group compared to those with non-anxiety (P < 0.001). Association of primary caregiver factors with ECD

Table 2 presents the results of multivariable logistic regression examining the association between primary caregiver factors and the ECD across four progressively adjusted models. The unadjusted model (model 1) showed a significant association between primary caregivers' role and anxiety status with the overall ECD. After full adjustment for child demographics, socioeconomic factors, and screen time (Model 4), primary caregiver anxiety remained positively associated with developmental delay (OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.75–2.05, P < 0.001). Discussion

This study used the CHeHCI to assess ECD outcomes in preschool children and examined their associations with primary caregiver anxiety status and caregiving role. We found that children cared for by anxious primary caregivers and children raised primarily by fathers, grandparents, and others were all associated with higher risks of ECD delay. Significant interaction effects were observed between primary caregiver anxiety status and both children’s only-child status and daily screen time.

In terms of the primary caregiver's anxiety, a meta-analysis found that parental mental health interventions had a positive effect on the child's cognitive and social-emotional outcomes [26]. A randomized controlled trial in rural Vietnam showed that a mental-change intervention for caregivers, including family engagement, media, and self-performance, can improve cognitive scores in 2-year-old children [27]. Some cross-sectional studies indicated that the greater the impairment of parents' mental and physical health, the greater the levels of impairment in the developmental [9, 28, 29]. Our study supports this existing literature and found that primary caregivers with anxiety had a higher proportion of children with ECD delay, and their anxiety status showed a positive correlation with ECD delay. This may be attributed to primary caregivers with anxiety being more likely to refuse to participate in development-stimulating activities for children [31], such as reading, feeding, and interacting with children [30–32].

The interaction between the only child and anxiety regarding ECD is intriguing. The positive correlation between the delay of ECD and the primary caregiver's anxiety increased if an only child. Previous studies [33, 34] indicated that it is stressful for first-time mothers attempting to adapt to their roles, coupled with a lack of knowledge of self-care and infant care, which easily results in emotional anxiety. Children are more susceptible to caregivers' psychopathological influence during early childhood. It follows that the higher the levels of anxiety of the caregiver, the higher the levels of disturbances in the total level of children's development [35]. Notably, the positive association between primary caregiver anxiety and ECD delays was non-significant only among left-behind children. Left-behind children face multiple dominant developmental risk factors (e.g., nutritional issues and mental health) [36], which may overshadow the impact of primary caregiver anxiety. Additionally, the lack of significance is likely due to limited statistical power, as the small proportion of left-behind children in our sample.

Evidence from the UK birth cohort shows that paternal involvement in children's early upbringing is associated with positive behavioural development outcomes [37]. Another observational study also shows that grandparents' involvement in nurturing positively affects a child's cognitive, motor, and socio-emotional development [38]. However, when a father or grandparent is the primary caregiver, it is positively associated with ECD delay in our study. Fathers tend to engage more directly with higher-ability children, often reinforcing rather than compensating, which may result in insufficient stimulation in certain areas of children's development [39]. In the Chinese cultural context, grandparental involvement in childcare is widespread and helps alleviate the burden of parenting for households. However, this practice is often accompanied by intergenerational discrepancies in parenting beliefs and caregiving practices [40, 41]. For instance, without appropriate limits on children’s screen time, grandparents may fail to provide sufficient cognitive and language stimulation for young children [25]. Resource constraints faced by grandparents when parents work away from home may also negatively affect ECD [42]. We also found a significant interaction between the only child and the primary caregiver regarding ECD. For multiple-child families, the positive association between grandparents' nursing and ECD delay might be related to the dispersed nurturing resources [43]. The age-related declines in the caregiving capacity of grandparents may also be an important reason.

A cohort study of children aged 6 and 72 months in China indicated that those with excessive screen time had poorer cognitive and social-emotional development outcomes [25]. Previous research [44] suggests that high screen exposure correlates with delays in cognitive and noncognitive development, including language delays and poor attention. In our study, children who were excessively screen-exposed were more likely to have early developmental delay. This alteration might be attributable to the detrimental effects of early screen exposure on the development of brain regions involved in cognitive control [45]. Interestingly, an interaction was found between screen time and anxiety regarding ECD in the subgroup analysis. The positive correlation between the delay of ECD and the primary caregiver's anxiety diminishes as screen time increases. One possible mechanism is that primary caregivers frequently use screens as “babysitters” to buy time for themselves and manage higher anxiety emotions or parenting pressure [46]. This reduces exposure to negative interactions with the primary caregiver and potentially mitigates the independent detrimental impact of anxiety on ECD [25]. Furthermore, Variables not included in our model, such as child temperament (e.g., difficult temperament) or family parenting style (e.g., permissive/indulgent practices), may influence screen exposure levels and ECD outcomes [47]. Thus, the mechanisms of the interaction need to be further explored.

Several strengths in our study should be highlighted. First, our study features a large-scale sample of children and their primary caregivers, recruited from 39 geographically and socioeconomically diverse administrative districts across Chongqing, China, ensuring strong population representativeness. In addition, we employed the CHeHCI to comprehensively assess children's overall ECD status. It is a tool with established reliability and validity tailored to China’s cultural context. Nevertheless, some limitations warrant mention. First, online and self-reported surveys inherently introduce information biases. However, stringent quality-control measures were implemented throughout the process, and participant samples were rigorously screened. Second, cross-sectional survey data did not permit a reliable inference of causality. Longitudinal studies are necessary to further examine the association between primary caregiver factors and ECD. Finally, several ECD-related variables (e.g., parent–child interaction quality, children's nutritional status) were not adjusted for, and the understudied caregiving variables (mixed primary caregivers, parenting models) were not assessed. Future work incorporating these factors will enable a more holistic understanding of the determinants and patterns of children’s ECD.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study contributes to the literature on primary caregiver factors shaping ECD and offers practical insights for preschool parenting. We found that primary caregiver anxiety and caregiving role are significantly associated with preschool children's development. The primary caregiver's anxiety had interactions with the only child and screen time, and the primary caregiver's role also had interactions with the only child. These results make caregivers' anxiety an important target for improving child development outcomes. Future studies are warranted to further investigate the mechanisms underlying this association.
