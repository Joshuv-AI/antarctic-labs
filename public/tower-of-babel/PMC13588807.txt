Parental gut microbiota dysbiosis is associated with cross-generationally shared DNA methylation patterns and autism-like behaviors in offspring
Authors: Bin Chen, Qinghua Wang, Xinxin Xue, Xin Jiang, Meilu Zhang, Changying Zhao, Chuandi Jin, Kaile Yue, Guoping Zhao, Yanan Liu, Ning Li, Lei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition arising from interactions between genetic and environmental factors. The interplay between the microbiota-gut-brain axis and epigenetics has been implicated in ASD, but the extent of their impact remains unclear. We hypothesized that parental gut microbiota dysbiosis shapes cross-generationally shared DNA methylation patterns and ASD-like phenotypes in offspring. Antibiotic-treated adult mice were colonized with fecal microbiota from children with ASD or typically developing (TD) controls, then bred. Colonization with ASD-associated microbiota did not alter parental behavior but induced ASD-like behavioral changes in offspring, accompanied by colonic inflammation and neuronal loss in the hippocampus and striatum. Targeted bisulfite sequencing revealed that ASD-associated microbiota reshaped methylation at ASD-related CpG sites in parental brain, blood, and colon, with a subset of these alterations also observed in offspring tissue. Integrative analyses revealed sex-specific networks linking gut microbes, DNA methylation, gene expression, metabolites, and behavior. These findings support a model in which parental gut microbiota dysbiosis is associated with cross-generationally shared DNA methylation changes linked to ASD-like phenotypes in offspring. Together, these results provide novel microbial and epigenetic insights into ASD-related risk and offering a plausible mechanistic explanation for the clinical phenomenon in which unaffected parents give rise to children with ASD.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a heterogeneous group of early-onset neurodevelopmental conditions characterized by differences in social communication and by restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior1. Its etiology involves complex interactions among genetic, environmental, and epigenetic factors2. Genetic factors contribute substantially to ASD but do not fully account for its occurrence or heterogeneity3; environmental exposures and epigenetic mechanisms are also thought to play important roles in ASD risk and development4.

Recent research highlights the role of gut microbiota dysbiosis in increasing ASD risk5,6. In addition, studies comparing children with ASD and their parents suggest that parental environmental exposures and family microbiota profiles may contribute to this risk7–10. The microbiota-gut-brain axis plays a pivotal role in central nervous system development and function11, yet the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain poorly understood.

DNA methylation has emerged as a key epigenetic mechanism implicated in ASD4. Clinical studies have identified ASD-associated methylation changes12,13 and microbiota have been shown to influence host methylation patterns, for example in colorectal cancer14,15. Several studies suggest that DNA methylation changes may be associated with cross-generational biological effects, exerting regulatory roles in offspring growth and development16–18. However, the link between gut microbiota, DNA methylation, and ASD is still underexplored.

Our previous work revealed a strong resemblance between the gut microbiota of children with ASD and their mothers10. Sharon et al.19 further demonstrated that transferring ASD-associated microbiota into germ-free mice induced ASD-like behaviors in offspring. Yet, cross-generational microbiota-associated epigenetic patterns remain insufficiently investigated in ASD.

To address this, we colonized antibiotic-treated mice with fecal microbiota from ASD individuals, or typically developing donors and investigated whether microbiota-induced parental DNA methylation patterns are shared between parents and offspring and linked to ASD-like behaviors in offspring. Given the sex-specific prevalence and manifestations of ASD19–21, we performed a multi-omics analysis—including microbiome, methylome, transcriptome, and metabolome—on both male and female offspring. Our integrated analysis revealed sex-dependent molecular perturbations and led to the construction of a regulatory network linking gut microbes, DNA methylation, gene expression, and metabolites, shedding light on the mechanisms underlying sex differences in ASD.

Results

FMT with ASD-associated gut microbiota induces ASD-like behaviors in offspring but not in parental mice

Our previous research showed that mothers of children with ASD often harbor altered gut microbiota compared with control mothers, even though they do not themselves display ASD-like behavioral characteristics10. To model this phenomenon, we transplanted fecal microbiota from human donors with ASD or TD into antibiotic-treated parental mice, thereby generating two groups: ASD donor-derived parental mice (ASDP) and TD donor-derived parental mice (TDP). Given the well-documented sex differences in ASD22, we next investigated sex-specific behavioral changes in the offspring. The marble burying test showed no significant differences in either male or female mice (Fig. 2G). In contrast, male oASD mice (oASD_M) exhibited significantly reduced total distance traveled in the open field, decreased movement in the central zone, and less time spent in that zone compared to male oTD controls (oTD_M). No significant differences were observed between female oASD (oASD_F) and oTD (oTD_F) mice in this test (Fig. 2H–J). However, in the three-chamber sociability test, oASD_F displayed significantly reduced social interaction (SI) indices compared to oTD_F, while no differences were found between oASD_M and oTD_M (Fig. 2K, L).

These findings suggest that while FMT had no detectable effect on parental behavior, it induced sex-specific ASD-like behaviors in offspring: anxiety-related locomotor changes in males, and alterations in social interaction in females. This observation mirrors clinical cases where parents of autistic children carry dysbiotic microbiota but do not present with ASD-related behavioral characteristics.

Furthermore, histopathological analyses using hematoxylin-eosin staining further revealed biological abnormalities in oASD mice (Supplementary Fig. 2G–L). In both sexes, we observed lymphocyte and granulocyte infiltration in the colon, indicating intestinal inflammation. In the hippocampus and striatum, neurons exhibited neurodegenerative features, including nuclear shrinkage and hyperchromasia, together with a concomitant increase in microglial density. Subsequent immunofluorescence analysis confirmed neuronal deficits in the brain. The hippocampus of oASD mice of both sexes showed concurrent reductions in HuC/D⁺ neurons and Tuj1⁺ area (Fig. 2M–O). In the striatum, oASD males exhibited a decrease in HuC/D⁺ neurons and Tuj1⁺ area, whereas oASD females showed a selective reduction in HuC/D⁺ neurons, with Tuj1⁺ area remaining unchanged (Fig. 2P–R). These results suggest that parental FMT can induce pathophysiological changes in both the gut and brain of offspring.

Distinct microbial community structures were observed between ASDP and TDP parental mice, as well as between oASD and oTD offspring (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Sex-specific differences in microbial taxa were evident between oASD and oTD mice. In male offspring, taxa such as Actinobacteriota, Patescibacteria, and Lachnospiraceae were altered, whereas in females, differences were driven by Christensenellales, Enterobacterales, Pseudomonadales, and Mycoplasmatales (Fig. 3B, C). DESeq2 analysis identified differential ASVs between oASD and oTD. In males, ASVs enriched in oASD included Barnesiella, Bacteroides, Adlercreutzia, Colidextribacter, and Parabacteroides gordonii, while Lactobacillus ASVs were significantly depleted. In females, enriched ASVs in oASD included Barnesiella, Alistipes, and Prevotella, with reductions seen in Barnesiella and Clostridia vadinBB60 group (Fig. 3D, E).

To identify vertically transmitted ASD-associated microbes, we intersected differential ASVs in offspring with those in parental mice. We identified 63 and 32 overlapping ASVs in male and female offspring, respectively (Fig. 3F), including Bacteroides ASV3 and ASV22, which were also found in human ASD donors.

Behavioral relevance was assessed via Spearman correlation analysis. In males, 12 ASVs were positively correlated with repetitive or anxiety-like behaviors, while 20 showed negative associations (Fig. 3G). In females, three ASVs were positively linked to social interaction (Fig. 3H). These results suggest that specific gut microbes contribute to ASD-like behaviors in a sex-dependent manner.

ASD-associated CpG sites show cross-generationally shared methylation patterns and are linked to vertically transmitted gut microbiota

The gut microbiota are known to influence host DNA methylation, and growing evidence suggests a connection between aberrant DNA methylation and ASD14,23. To identify ASD-associated methylation sites with potential functional relevance, we first applied two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) using GWAS summary statistics for CpG methylation from two independent cohorts—a birth cohort and a childhood cohort (Supplementary Table 1). Standard MR approaches, including the Wald ratio (single instrument) and inverse-variance weighted method (multiple instruments), were used to estimate the association between genetically predicted CpG methylation and ASD risk.

We identified 81 ASD-associated CpGs in the birth cohort and 150 in the childhood cohort, with 31 CpGs overlapping between cohorts (Fig. 4A), yielding a total of 200 high-confidence ASD-associated CpGs (Supplementary Data 1). These CpGs mapped to 109 genes, including 19 shared across both cohorts (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Data 2). We next examined whether these ASD-associated CpGs were affected by FMT and showed methylation patterns shared between parental and offspring tissues. Targeted bisulfite sequencing of blood, brain, and colon tissues from both parental and offspring mice was conducted, focusing on 48 mouse ortholog genes (Supplementary Table 2) corresponding to the 109 human ASD-associated genes. Differentially methylated sites (DMSs) between ASD and TD were identified and classified as hyper- or hypomethylated, showing sex- and tissue-specific patterns in both generations (Supplementary Data 4).

To assess parent-offspring methylation concordance, we identified 37 DMSs shared between parents and offspring, including 19 hypermethylated and 18 hypomethylated sites (Fig. 4D, E; Table 1). These sites, mapped to ASD-associated genes, were considered candidate epigenetic markers showing cross-generationally shared methylation patterns. Notably, 7 genes—including Col25a1, Zkscan17, Ethe1, and Spag16—contained multiple shared DMSs between parental and offspring generations, supporting the possibility that methylation changes at some loci may represent gene- or locus-level alterations rather than isolated single-CpG effects. GO enrichment (Supplementary Table 3) showed that hypermethylated genes were involved in transcriptional regulation and synapse organization, whereas hypomethylated genes were linked to learning, Finally, we examined whether these cross-generationally shared methylation sites were associated with vertically transmitted gut microbiota. Correlation analysis revealed that 14 DMSs shared between parents and offspring were significantly linked to 17 gut microbiota ASVs, including Barnesiella, Bacteroides, and Adlercreutzia, all previously implicated in ASD27–29 (Fig. 4F). These results suggest that FMT-induced changes in the gut microbiota can modulate host epigenetic patterns relevant to ASD, with some methylation signals shared between parental and offspring tissues.

The ASD microbiota alters brain gene expression in offspring mice

Previous transcriptomic analyses of postmortem brains from individuals with ASD have revealed distinct gene expression patterns compared to TD14. In our study, RNA-seq analysis of brain tissues from oASD and oTD mice showed no significant differences in global transcriptomic profiles in either sex (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). However, differential gene expression analysis using DESeq2 (threshold: P < 0.05, |log₂FC| ≥ 1) identified 136 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in male offspring, with 118 upregulated and 18 downregulated (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Data 5). In female offspring, 125 DEGs were identified, including 91 upregulated and 34 downregulated genes (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Data 5). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that in male oASD mice, upregulated DEGs were predominantly enriched in the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway (20 genes), tryptophan metabolism, and serotonergic synapse signaling, all of which are implicated in ASD pathophysiology20. Downregulated genes in males were significantly enriched in steroid hormone biosynthesis and linoleic acid metabolism, both previously associated with ASD susceptibility30 (Fig. 5C). In female mice, upregulated genes were enriched in the tyrosine metabolism and steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways, while downregulated genes were significantly enriched in the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway (Fig. 5D). Notably, Cyp2e1, which is involved in steroid hormone metabolism, was downregulated in male oASD mice but upregulated in females. Gabre and Nts, which encode components of neurotransmitter receptor systems, were increased in males but decreased in females, whereas Sstr5 was specifically elevated in males with no change in females (Fig. 5E–H). The sex-specific expression patterns of these ASD-relevant genes, particularly the bidirectional regulation of Cyp2e1, may underlie the distinct behavioral phenotypes observed between male and female ASD offspring. These findings suggest that gut microbiota from ASD donors modulate brain gene expression in a sex-dependent manner, potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of ASD-like behaviors in offspring.

FMT induces distinct metabolite profiles in oASD and oTD mice

The metabolic abnormalities observed in ASD may be attributed in part to gut microbiota31,32. To investigate this, we performed untargeted serum metabolomics in oASD and oTD mice. Using a significance threshold of P < 0.05, we identified 40 and 39 differential metabolites in male and female offspring, respectively (Fig. 5I, J, Supplementary Data 6). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed sex-specific patterns. In male mice, upregulated pathways included thiamine metabolism, glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis, and butanoate metabolism. Conversely, linoleic acid metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, histidine metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and vitamin B6 metabolism were significantly downregulated (Fig. 5K). Notably, thiamine (Vitamin B1) and vitamin B6—both synthesized by gut microbiota33—have been associated with ASD34,35. In females, no pathways were significantly upregulated, but three pathways—linoleic acid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and fatty acid biosynthesis—were significantly downregulated (Fig. 5L). These findings were consistent with previous reports30,36,37. Although linoleic acid metabolism was reduced in both sexes, the levels of specific metabolites such as linoleate and phosphatidylcholine differed significantly (Fig. 5M, N). Moreover, transcriptomic analysis of brain tissue revealed enrichment of downregulated genes involved in arachidonic acid metabolism.

We further applied the MIMOSA2 model to infer microbial contributions to metabolite production and degradation. In males, the gut microbiota primarily contributed to amino acid catabolism (Supplementary Data 7), including L-lysine, L-ornithine, L-leucine, L-valine, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and L-isoleucine. Among these, GABA was significantly elevated in the oASD group, and Escherichia albertii KF1 was associated with its production. In females, microbiota predominantly influenced the production and degradation of sucrose, L-valine, and pyridoxal (Supplementary Data 7). These results suggest that ASD- and TD-derived microbiota induce distinct and sex-biased serum metabolic profiles.

To decipher the sex-specific mechanisms linking microbiota to ASD-like behaviors, we conducted stepwise mediation analyses integrating microbiome, DNA methylation, metabolome, and transcriptome data. Behavioral phenotypes (central movement time in males and social interaction in females) were treated as outcome variables, with one omics layer as the predictor and the remaining three as mediators. Discussion

Numerous human cohort and animal studies have underscored links between gut microbes and ASD-related phenotypes, but the mechanisms by which parental microbiota shape offspring risk remain incompletely understood. In the present study, we show that transplanting gut microbiota from children with ASD into antibiotic-treated adult mice reshapes host DNA methylation profiles in multiple tissues, and that a subset of these microbiota-related methylation changes is also observed in offspring tissues. Notably, while the parental mice did not display pronounced ASD-like behavioral differences, their offspring exhibited clear ASD-like behavioral readouts that were associated with cross-generationally shared methylation patterns. This pattern parallels clinical observations in which parents without a formal ASD diagnosis have autistic children, highlighting a potential route of microbiota-associated cross-generational epigenetic regulation. Our findings provide a plausible mechanistic extension to prior FMT studies such as those by Sharon et al.19, in which colonization of parental animals with ASD-derived microbiota altered behavioral phenotypes in their offspring. Specifically, our data suggest that microbiota-driven modifications of parental epigenetic states may contribute to ASD-like behavioral phenotypes in offspring, rather than microbial colonization alone. This concept is consistent with the “developmental origins of health and disease” framework, in which upstream environmental factors can induce epigenetic alterations that are transmitted to offspring and regulate development38.

By integrating human GWAS data and Mendelian randomization, we moved beyond global epigenomic shifts to nominate specific ASD-associated CpG sites, which we then interrogated in the brain, blood, and colon of parental and offspring mice. We identified several loci (e.g., within Sall4, Rps6ka2, Col25a1, and Mypop) that exhibited consistent methylation changes across at least two tissues. Prior ASD epigenetic studies have reported differential methylation in brain and peripheral samples, often with an emphasis on biomarker discovery39,40. However, the cross-tissue concordance observed in our study—where identical changes appear in tissues derived from different germ layers—suggests a systemic or early developmental origin. These observations are consistent with epigenetic marks that are established early in development and could, in principle, involve the germline41, rather than being explained solely by localized somatic remodeling in the gut or brain. It is noteworthy that genes harboring these CpG sites with cross-generationally shared DNA methylation patterns did not show robust differential expression between oASD and oTD offspring. Therefore, our current bulk brain RNA-seq data do not establish a direct methylation-transcription regulatory mechanism underlying ASD-like behaviors. These methylation signatures may represent microbiota-associated epigenetic correlates rather than direct transcriptional drivers, although subtle regulatory effects cannot be excluded. Such effects may be cell type-specific, developmental-stage-specific, mediated through distal regulatory elements, or too small to detect in bulk brain tissue at the time point analyzed.

A notable feature of our results is the pronounced sexual dimorphism in behavioral outcomes and molecular profiles, which aligns with the well-known sex bias in ASD prevalence42. In our study, we observed that male and female oASD mice exhibited distinct severity and profiles of behavioral deficits. Sex-stratified mediation networks further highlighted partially overlapping yet distinct microbiota-methylation-transcript-metabolite configurations in males versus females. These observations support emerging evidence that the microbiome and epigenome contribute to sex differences in brain development22,43. Our data suggest that microbiota-epigenome interactions do not merely modulate general risk but may actively contribute to sex-specific risk pathways, and the specific microbial taxa, metabolites and CpG-gene pairs identified here provide concrete candidates for future mechanistic investigation of ASD sexual dimorphism. However, the biological basis of these sex-specific effects remains unresolved. We did not measure sex hormones, and therefore cannot determine whether the observed differences were driven by hormonal regulation, sex-specific microbiota composition, immune differences, developmental timing, or other confounders. Future studies incorporating hormone measurements and larger sex-balanced cohorts will be needed to clarify these mechanisms.

This study has several limitations that warrant cautious consideration when interpreting the results. First, although we utilized three established behavioral tests to assess ASD-like phenotypes, these are susceptible to variables such as animal strain and housing environment44,45, and cannot recapitulate the full spectrum of symptoms in human ASD46, thereby limiting their translational capacity and direct comparability. Behavioral measures should therefore be interpreted as autism-relevant dimensions rather than as direct analogues of clinical symptoms, and cross-study comparisons require caution. Second, the number of human fecal donors was modest and all donors were recruited from a single center, which may limit generalizability. Donor-specific microbiota features and unmeasured factors such as diet, environmental background, early-life exposures, and clinical heterogeneity may have contributed to the observed phenotypes. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as effects associated with the ASD donor microbiota used in this study rather than as universal features of ASD-associated microbiota. Third, methylation, transcriptomic, and metabolomic profiles were measured at a single offspring time point after behavioral testing, so we cannot determine whether these molecular differences are transient, developmentally dynamic, or stable over time. Finally, we did not profile DNA methylation in gametes, including sperm and oocytes. Thus, we cannot distinguish whether the observed parent-offspring methylation concordance arises from germline-mediated mechanisms, postnatal microbiota-driven remodeling in offspring tissues, or both. Future studies using larger multi-center donor cohorts, longitudinal molecular profiling across multiple offspring developmental stages, and germline epigenetic analyses will be needed to validate these findings and clarify the underlying mechanisms. Despite these limitations, our results support that dysbiotic gut microbial signals are associated with parental DNA methylation abnormalities that are also observed in offspring tissues and linked to ASD-like behavioral manifestations in offspring.

Taken together, our findings define the contribution of the microbiota-epigenome axis to the etiology of ASD. By integrating human genetic data (GWAS and Mendelian randomization) with a controlled FMT-based mouse model and cross-generational multi-omics profiling, we extended past purely correlative microbiota-ASD associations to establish a plausible mechanistic link. Specifically, we found that colonizing parental mice with ASD-associated microbiota altered DNA methylation at specific ASD-related CpG sites across the brain, blood, and colon. Crucially, a subset of these methylation changes was also observed in offspring tissues, where they coincided with observed ASD-like behavioral phenotypes. Integrative analyses further revealed complex, sex-dependent networks linking specific microbial taxa, CpG sites showing parent-offspring methylation concordance, and host molecular profiles to behavioral phenotypes, suggesting that microbiota-epigenome interactions contribute significantly to the known sex-specific risk pathways in ASD. Together, these findings highlight the microbiota-epigenome axis as a critical and previously underappreciated component of ASD etiology and point to this interface as a promising focus for future mechanistic and translational research.

Methods

Donor selection

In this study, 3 typically developing (TD) children and 3 children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) were recruited at Qilu Children’s Hospital, Shandong University, and their fecal samples were collected as donor samples. Children with ASD were diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition, and Chinese Classification of Mental Disorders. TD donors were age-matched children who showed typical development based on routine pediatric and psychological evaluations and had no history of neurodevelopmental or psychiatric disorders. All 6 donors were aged 2 to 8 years, with no history of other developmental disorders, neurological or psychiatric diseases, genetic metabolic diseases, or major physical illnesses. Additionally, none of the donors had received any medications, including antibiotics, antifungals, probiotics, or prebiotics in the past 3 months.

The fecal samples from the ASD and TD donors were kept fresh and transferred into an anaerobic workstation for sample pretreatment within 2 h. Subsequently, the samples were mixed in a 1:4 weight/volume ratio in sterile phosphate-buffered saline, and vigorously vortexed to homogenize within the anaerobic workstation. The workstation atmosphere consisted of 90% N₂, 5% CO₂, and 5% H₂. The homogenate was then passed through a 100-μm cell strainer to remove large insoluble particles. Glycerol was added to the filtrate to a final concentration of 20% (v/v), and subsequently, the samples were stored at −80 °C.

Animals

C57BL/6J mice (both males and females, Vital River, Beijing, China) were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. The animal room was maintained at 22 ± 2 °C and 30–70% relative humidity on a 12:12 h light/dark cycle. Animals were group-housed (3–5 per cage) in individually ventilated cages with autoclaved bedding and nesting material, and had ad libitum access to autoclaved drinking water and autoclaved standard rodent chow. Health status was monitored daily by trained animal care staff. At the end of the experiment, all mice were anesthetized using the inhalant anesthetic isoflurane. Before euthanasia by cervical dislocation, the absence of the righting reflex after isoflurane inhalation was confirmed to ensure complete unconsciousness. This method was chosen to minimize pain and distress to the animals.

Antibiotic treatment of mice

All antibiotic solutions were prepared in autoclaved drinking water, with each antibiotic at a concentration of 1 mg/ml. The non-absorbable antibiotics cocktail consisted of meropenem, neomycin sulfate, and vancomycin hydrochloride and was used to broadly deplete luminal Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and anaerobic bacteria. The absorbable antibiotics cocktail included ampicillin, cefotaxime sodium salt, and clindamycin hydrochloride and was used to further reduce residual and mucosa-associated bacteria that may not be fully eliminated by non-absorbable agents. This two-step regimen was chosen to achieve a more profound and stable disruption of the resident microbiota prior to human FMT, in line with established preconditioning approaches47. Antibiotic solutions were prepared the day before administration, provided in the drinking water for the mice and stored at 4 °C.

An overview of the antibiotic treatment timeline in mice is provided (Fig. 1A). A total of 50 C57BL/6J mice (25 males and 25 females) at six weeks of age received a three-course antibiotic treatment: non-absorbable antibiotics in the drinking water for 7 days, normal drinking water for 2 days, absorbable antibiotics in the drinking water for 7 days, normal drinking water for 2 days, non-absorbable antibiotics in the drinking water for 7 days, and normal drinking water for 2 days. Behavioral tests were conducted on day 27 after the initiation of antibiotic treatment.

Fecal microbiota transplantation in mice

An overview of the fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) timeline in mice is provided (Fig. 1B). Frozen stool suspensions from ASD and TD donors were thawed at 37 °C, gently vortexed, and administered to antibiotic-treated mice by oral gavage. Antibiotic-treated mice were randomly selected to receive FMT. For each child donor, fecal material was transplanted into 8 recipient mice (10 weeks of age, 4 males and 4 females) that had been pretreated with antibiotics, with each mouse receiving 200 μl per day for three consecutive days. Mice were housed by donor group after colonization, yielding a total of 24 colonized with microbiota from ASD donor-derived parental mice (ASDP; 12 males and 12 females) and 24 TD donor-derived parental mice (TDP; 12 males and 12 females). After colonization, ASDP and TDP mice were co-housed for mating within donor groups, and behavioral testing of parental mice was performed before mating. Pregnancy was confirmed through the examination of vaginal plugs to determine the gestational age after cohabitation, and single-cage housing was provided. The offspring mice obtained during parturition established the humanized gut microbiota mouse model. Based on the identities of their parental donors, the offspring were categorized into offspring of ASD donor-derived parental mice (oASD; 22 males and 14 females) and offspring of TD donor-derived parental mice (oTD; 15 males and 9 females). At 3 weeks of age, offspring mice were weaned. Then mice from different litters were grouped together based on their parental donors and housed 4–5 mice per cage for subsequent analysis. After weaning, the parental mice were euthanized, and colon tissue, intestinal contents, serum, and brain samples were collected. Behavioral testing for the offspring mice commenced at 6 weeks of age, and collection of colon tissue, intestinal contents, serum, and brain samples took place after the completion of behavioral testing. Subsequently, we tested and analyzed a subset of representative samples.

Sample collection and processing

Fresh fecal pellets of mice were collected, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80 °C until analysis. At the terminal time point, animals were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane, and blood was collected from the eyeball. The blood was divided into two portions: one portion was directly frozen at −80 °C, and the other portion was separated into serum and then frozen at −80 °C until analysis. Immediately after blood collection, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Following euthanasia, brain tissue, colon, and intestinal contents were collected separately and immediately placed in liquid nitrogen, subsequently stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Behavior testing

All mice underwent the same series of behavioral tests, conducted in the following order: open field test, marble burying test, and three-chamber social test. Behavioral tests were performed by an experimenter who was blinded to group allocation. To ensure experimental quality, mice were habituated in the experiment room for at least 30 min before each test. Additionally, a 1-day break was provided after completing each type of test. All behavioral tests were conducted in a calm and gentle environment. The test boxes or cages were cleaned of urine and feces, followed by sterilization with 75% alcohol before testing the next mouse. Mice were promptly returned to their home cages after completing each test.

Open field testing was conducted in white plexiglass arenas (50 cm × 50 cm × 38 cm), with behavior recorded using an overhead camera to assess locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior48. The data were tracked and analyzed using the SuperMaze software. Mice were introduced into the arena and permitted to explore for 10 min while being tracked. The SuperMaze (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) software and camera system automatically recorded parameters including the total distance traveled, the time spent in the center zone, and the distance traveled in the center zone.

Marble burying was assessed as a measure of stereotyped and repetitive behavior49. Each mouse was placed individually in a cage measuring 40 cm × 25 cm × 20 cm. A 5 cm layer of bedding was spread and 20 clean marbles (1.5 cm in diameter) were positioned in the center of the open space, evenly distributed in a 5 × 4 array. Mice were introduced individually into the cage one by one and the number of marbles buried by each mouse within a 20 min period was recorded. A marble was considered buried if half or more of its volume was covered by bedding material.

The three-chamber test was employed to evaluate the social approach and social novelty preference, as described by Yang et al., with minor modifications50. The apparatus consisted of a box measuring 40 cm × 60 cm × 22 cm, divided into three compartments labeled A, B, and C from left to right. The experiment comprised three stages: (1) Habituation stage. Each mouse was placed in the central compartment B and allowed to freely explore all three empty chambers for 10 min. This stage acclimated the mouse to the apparatus. (2) Social approach stage. After habituation, identical wire cages were placed in compartments A and C. A novel, age- and sex-matched mouse (Stranger 1) that had no prior contact with the test mouse was enclosed in the wire cage in compartment A, while the wire cage in compartment C remained empty. The test mouse was returned to the central compartment and allowed to explore all three chambers freely for 10 min. SuperMaze (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) software recorded the mouse’s trajectory and quantified the time spent interacting with Stranger 1 (TStranger 1) and with the empty cage (Tempty cage). The social interaction ability of mice was evaluated by calculating the sociability index (SI) as: SI = TStranger 1/(Tempty cage+TStranger 1). (3) Social novelty stage. After the social approach stage, a second unfamiliar mouse (Stranger 2) was placed in the previously empty wire cage in compartment C, whereas Stranger 1 remained in compartment A as the now familiar mouse. The test mouse was again placed in the central compartment and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. SuperMaze software recorded the trajectory and the time spent interacting with Stranger 1 (TStranger 1) and Stranger 2 (TStranger 2). Social novelty preference was evaluated by calculating the social novelty index (SNI) as: SNI = TStranger 2/(TStranger 1+ TStranger 2). At the end of each stage, both the test mouse and the stimulus mouse were returned to their respective home cages. The apparatus and wire cages were then wiped thoroughly with 75% ethanol to remove residual olfactory cues and minimize their influence on subsequent trials.

Histological analysis

For mice designated for histological analysis, animals were deeply anesthetized with 3% isoflurane and then transcradially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA; Servicebio, G1101, China) for brain and colon collection. The whole brain and colon were dissected and post-fixed in freshly prepared 4% PFA at 4 °C for 24 h, and then equilibrated in 30% sucrose in PBS until the tissues had sunk. After cryoprotection, brain and colon tissues were embedded and serially sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using a standard protocol51. Briefly, the paraffin sections were sequentially immersed in Environmental-Friendly Dewaxing Transparent Liquids I and II (Servicebio, G1128, China) for 20 min each, followed by treatment with anhydrous ethanol I and II for 5 min each. Subsequently, the sections were immersed in 75% ethanol for 5 min and thoroughly rinsed with tap water. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using the H&E HD Constant Dye Kit (Servicebio, G1076, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sections were then dehydrated through a graded series of absolute ethanol solutions (I, II, and III) for 2 min each, followed by sequential immersion in normal butanol I and II for 2 min each and clearing in xylene I and II for 2 min each. Finally, whole-slide images were scanned using a Pannoramic MIDI digital slide scanner (3DHistech, Hungary).

Colon histology was scored as described by Katakura et al.52. The scoring experimenters were “blinded” to sample identity. Colonic epithelial damage was assigned scores as follows: 0 = normal; 1 = hyperproliferation, irregular crypts, and goblet cell loss; 2 = mild to moderate crypt loss (10–50%); 3 = severe crypt loss (50–90%); 4 = complete crypt loss, surface epithelium intact; 5 = small- to medium-sized ulcer (<10 crypt widths); 6 = large ulcer (≥10 crypt widths). Infiltration with inflammatory cells was assigned scores separately for mucosa (0 = normal, 1 = mild, 2 = modest, 3 = severe), submucosa (0 = normal, 1 = mild to modest, 2 = severe), and muscle/serosa (0 = normal, 1 = moderate to severe). Scores for epithelial damage and inflammatory cell infiltration were added, resulting in a total scoring range of 0–12.

Histological scoring of the hippocampus and striatum was performed as described by Seth et al.53. The scoring experimenters were “blinded” to sample identity. Degenerative changes were scored on a scale from 0 to 4, defined as follows: 0 (absent), 1 (minimal), 2 (mild), 3 (moderate), and 4 (severe). The grading was based on either the frequency of observed changes per microscopic field or the affected area proportion within the tissue section. Specifically, Grade 1 corresponded to 0–2 occurrences per field or a minuscule affected area; Grade 2 to 3–5 occurrences or involvement of 15–20% of the field; Grade 3 to 6–8 occurrences or 25–50% involvement; and Grade 4 to ≥9 occurrences or >50% involvement. The total histopathological score for each region was derived accordingly.

Immunofluorescence staining was performed according to a standard protocol51. Brain sections from mice were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by a wash in distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA antigen retrieval solution under high temperature and pressure conditions. Then, the sections were washed three times with PBS. The sections were incubated with 3% bovine serum albumin (Servicebio, GC305010, China) for blocking. Then, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mixed primary antibody containing diluted Rabbit anti-HuC/HuD (Abcam, AB184267) and Mouse anti-Tuj1 (Servicebio, GB12139, China), washed 3 times in PBS, and incubated for 50 min at room temperature with the secondary antibodies (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, Servicebio, GB21303, China; Goat Anti-Mouse IgG, Servicebio, GB25301). The sections were washed 3 times in PBS and incubated with DAPI (Servicebio, G1012, China) at room temperature for 10 min. Finally, the sections were mounted with Anti-fade Mounting Medium (Servicebio, G1401, China). The sections were imaged using Pannoramic MIDI digital slide scanner (3DHistech, Hungary). All images were analyzed using ImageJ54.

Fecal sample microbial DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Total DNA of the gut microbiota was extracted from fecal samples using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. Briefly, samples were lysed in 2× CTAB buffer and extracted with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, v/v/v). In the later steps of DNA isolation, the aqueous phase was applied to a SanPrep Column DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Sangon Biotech, China) spin column (silica membrane-based) to rapidly purify and recover DNA55. For the purpose of analyzing the taxonomic composition of the bacterial community, the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was selected for the subsequent pyrosequencing. The specific primer pair 341F (5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’) were used for the PCR amplification. Paired-end, 2 × 250 bp, next-generation sequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencing platform.

Microbiome analysis

Raw sequencing data were processed and analyzed using Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME2, version 2020.2)56. Briefly, paired-end reads were assigned to samples by barcodes and then the barcodes and primer sequence were cut off. Next, we used the q2-dada257 plugin in QIIME2 for quality control, chimera detection and deletion, and generation of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and representative sequences. The SILVA database (version 138)58 classifier was used to annotate the ASVs with 99% similarity. Alpha diversity was measured with the Observed ASVs, Shannon, Simpson, Pielou, ACE, and Chao1 indexes using the get_alphaindex function in MicrobiotaProcess R package (version 1.2.0)59,60 after normalization by Hellinger transformation. Differences between the two groups were then compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for alpha diversity and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) for beta diversity. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis was performed to identify microbial biomarkers with an alpha parameter of 0.05 and a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) threshold value of 2.0. Differential abundance analysis was performed using the negative binomial model (Wald test) implemented in DESeq2 v1.34.061.

Two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis of DNA methylation and ASD

This study employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach to investigate the impact of DNA methylation on ASD. The conceptual framework of this MR analysis is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Genetic associations with DNA methylation from two large-scale human cohorts were used as exposures, while ASD genome-wide association data served as outcomes. The genome-wide association studies (GWAS) summary data for methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTLs) from the birth cohort and childhood cohort were obtained from the Accessible Resource for Integrated Epigenomics Studies (ARIES, http://www.ariesepigenomics.org.uk/). The GWAS summary statistics data for ASD were sourced from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC, https://pgc.unc.edu/). Further details regarding the sources of ASD and CpG GWAS summary data are provided in Supplementary Table 1. MR effect estimation was conducted using the Wald ratio method (with one instrumental variable) or the inverse-variance weighted approach. Subsequently, various two-sample Mendelian randomization methods, including MR-Egger, Weighted Median, and Mendelian randomization pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO), were used as sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the MR-based evidence linking CpG DNA methylation to ASD. MR-PRESSO calculations were performed using the R package MRPRESSO (Version 1.0), while the remaining four methods were implemented using the R package TwoSampleMR (Version 0.5.5).

DNA methylation of specific target genes

DNA methylation of target genes was evaluated by Targeted Bisulfite Sequencing (MethylTarget) performed in Genesky Biotechnologies Inc. (Shanghai, China). As previously described, this targeted bisulfite sequencing assay is a next-generation sequencing technology for methylation profiling of targeted genomic regions62. In brief, genomic DNA extracted from blood, brain, and colon underwent sodium bisulfite treatment using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Multiplex PCR was performed with an optimized primer mixture designed using primer3 software (Supplementary Table 2). Libraries were prepared and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The methylation of each CpG site was calculated as the percentage of the methylated cytosines over the total tested cytosines. The average methylation was calculated using all measured CpG sites within the gene. To clarify the biological functions of the genes and the involved signaling pathways, we annotated each gene based on the Gene Ontology (GO). The GO pathway enrichment analyses were carried out by clusterProfiler R package (version 4.2.2).

Brain gene expression analysis

Brain tissue was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, pulverized, and RNA extraction was performed using Qiagen miRNeasy kits following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE). Additionally, RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA 6000 Nano Kit on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). A total of 1 μg RNA per sample was utilized as input material for RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. The library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina platform, generating paired-end reads. We used the deseq function in the DESeq2 R package (version 1.34.0) to perform differential analysis of gene expression on the obtained transcriptome sequencing data. Next, we used the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.2.2) to perform the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed genes to determine the functional pathways in which they work.

Serum metabolites analysis based on UHPLC-MS/MS

A volume of 50 μL of the serum from each mouse was transferred to an EP tube. Following the addition of 200 μL of the extract solution (acetonitrile:methanol = 1:1, containing isotopically-labeled internal standard mixture), the samples were vortexed for 30 s, sonicated for 10 min in an ice-water bath, and incubated for 1 h at −40 °C to precipitate proteins. Subsequently, the sample was centrifuged at 13,800 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a fresh glass vial for analysis. A quality control sample was prepared by mixing an equal aliquot of the supernatants from all of the samples.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analyses were conducted using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo)63. The mobile phase comprised 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonium hydroxide in water (pH = 9.75) (A) and acetonitrile (B). The auto-sampler temperature was maintained at 4 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was chosen for its capability to acquire MS/MS spectra in information-dependent acquisition mode, controlled by the acquisition software (Xcalibur, Thermo). In this mode, the software continuously evaluates the full scan MS spectrum. The electrospray ionization source conditions were set as follows: sheath gas flow rate at 50 Arb, Aux gas flow rate at 15 Arb, capillary temperature at 320 °C, full MS resolution at 60,000, MS/MS resolution at 15,000, collision energy at 10/30/60 in NCE mode, and spray voltage at 3.8 kV (positive) or −3.4 kV (negative), respectively.

The raw data were converted to the mzXML format using ProteoWizard. Peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration were performed based on XCMS for further data processing64. Subsequently, an in-house MS2 database (BiotreeDB) was employed for metabolite annotation, with a cutoff set at 0.3. Subsequently, after denoising through relative standard deviation, the missing values were imputed by half of the minimum value. An internal standard normalization method was applied in the data analysis. The final dataset, comprising peak number, sample name, and normalized peak area information, was imported into the SIMCA16.0.2 software package (Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Umea, Sweden) for multivariate analysis.

MIMOSA integrative analysis

Metabolic modeling with MIMOSA2 was conducted to estimate the community-wide metabolic output derived from both the metatranscriptome and predicted metagenome. This estimation was summarized via Community-wide Metabolic Potential (CMP) scores65, representing the relative capacity of the metagenome or metatranscriptome to generate or deplete specific metabolites. MIMOSA2 employs the AGORA model to conduct regression analysis between the CMP scores and measured levels of each metabolite, aiming to identify a subset of metabolites significantly positively correlated with microbial metabolic potential. The analysis utilized a recommended threshold of P < 0.2 for significance determination.

Stepwise mediation analysis

Using the R mediation package, we assessed the significant mediating effects of continuous omics modules along the microbiome/methylation/metabolite/gene expression-host axis on behavioral outcomes66. Behavioral outcomes were considered continuous dependent variables, while microbiome, methylation, metabolite, and gene expression were treated as independent variables.
