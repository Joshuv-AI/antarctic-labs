Triprojective almost perfect nonlinear permutations and functions
Faruk Göloğlu, Lukas Kölsch

Abstract
. We give a large family of almost perfect nonlinear (APN) permutations of finite vector spaces of every odd dimension divisible by three. We also give APN functions that are not bijective on even dimensions and related highly nonlinear functions. The functions we provide admit a so-called triprojective structure induced by the general linear group GL ⁡ ( 3 , 2 m ) \GL(3,2^{m}) .

1.
Introduction

Let
F:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
be a vectorial function with
p
a prime.
The difference distribution table of
F
is defined as

\DDT_{F}(u,v):=\#\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}:F(x+u)-F(x)=v\}.

The differential uniformity of
F
is

\Delta_{F}:=\max\{\DDT_{F}(u,v):u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},u\neq 0\}.

Functions with optimal differential uniformity
\Delta_{F}=1
are called
perfect nonlinear (PN) which can happen only when
p
is odd.
When
p=2
, functions with optimal differential uniformity

\Delta_{F}=2
are called almost perfect nonlinear (APN).
Examples in this case include the (multiplicative)
field-inversion function when
n
is odd. The notion arises from
cryptography in the context of differential cryptanalysis.
In the cryptographic context of S-Boxes used in the frequently used
substitution-permutation networks (for which AES is an example),
permutations with low differential uniformity are employed (for which
the field-inverse function of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{8}}
is the example used in
AES). An important question is the existence question for APN
bijections when
n>6
is even. We refer to the
book
[
2
]
for details and connections.

1.1.
Notions of equivalence of vectorial functions

Two Boolean functions
F,G:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
are said to
be linear-equivalent if they satisfy

L_{1}\circ F\circ L_{2}=G,

for some
L_{1},L_{2}\in\GL(n,p)
, i.e., nonsingular linear
transformations of the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
. This corresponds
to basis change for the domain and co-domain vector-spaces.
A coarser notion of equivalence is the extended-affine equivalence
which requires existence of affine bijections
A_{1},A_{2}\in\AGL(n,p)

and an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-affine function
A_{3}
satisfying

A_{1}\circ F\circ A_{2}+A_{3}=G.

Still a coarser notion is the
\CCZ
-equivalence, which
requires existence of
n\times n

\mathbb{F}_{p}
-matrices

A,B,C,D\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
viewed as linear transformations in
\End(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n})

forming a nonsingular matrix in

M_{2n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
viewed as a bijection in
\GL(2n,p)
, and vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
that satisfy

\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
F(x)\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\pi(x)\\
G(\pi(x))\end{pmatrix}

with
\pi:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
bijective. All of these notions
of equivalence preserve being PN/APN by preserving
differential uniformity.
\CCZ
-equivalence contains both
notions of equivalence which can be seen by
fixing
B=0_{n\times n}
and
fixing
B=C=0_{n\times n},u=v=0
, respectively.

1.2.
Quadratic, semi-quadratic forms and multiprojective functions

We write
F:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
as

F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})
where
F_{i}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}

for
1\leq i\leq n
and define

\deg F=\max\{\deg F_{i}:1\leq i\leq n\},

where
\deg F_{i}
is the degree of the
F_{i}
when written (uniquely)
as a polynomial in

\mathbb{F}_{p}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/(x_{1}^{p}-x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}^{p}-x_{n}).

If
F
is quadratic, each
F_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
can be written as

F_{i}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})A^{(i)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{T}+\langle u^{(i)},(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\rangle+\epsilon^{(i)},

for
n\times n
matrices
A^{(i)}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
with coefficients
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
u^{(i)}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
and
\epsilon^{(i)}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
and where
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
are
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-variables.
Without loss of generality, we will take

u^{(i)}=\underline{0}
and
\epsilon^{(i)}=0
, i.e.,

F_{i}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})A^{(i)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{T}

since we are interested in PN/APN functions up to
EA-equivalence. These are called
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-quadratic forms
and one can take
A^{(i)}
to be in upper triangular form
(when
p=2
we can take main diagonal to be entirely zeroes since

x_{j}^{2}A^{(i)}_{jj}\pmod{x_{j}^{2}-x_{j}}

absorbs into
\langle u^{(i)},\underline{x}\rangle
).

When
n=m\ell
, we can view
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
as

\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}^{\ell}
by fixing
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
-bases over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
We can now consider a collection of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
-quadratic forms
(for
1\leq i\leq\ell
)

G_{i}:=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell})B^{(i)}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell})^{T},

for
\ell\times\ell
matrices
B^{(i)}\in M_{\ell}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}})

with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell})
are

\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
-variables. Now
G=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{\ell})
is a map

G:\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}^{\ell}
which can be viewed
as
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
by fixing
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-bases
of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
. We say that

G\in\QH(\ell,p^{m})
and

F\in\QH(n,p)
.
Note that (fixing bases) we have
\QH(\ell,p^{m})\subseteq\QH(n,p)

and if
m>1
(and in turn
\ell<n
) the inclusion is strict.
Here, one introduces semi-quadratic forms

H_{i}:=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell})C^{(i)}(y_{1}^{\sigma_{i}},\ldots,y_{\ell}^{\sigma_{i}})^{T},

where
\sigma_{i}\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}})
, i.e.,
\sigma_{i}=p^{e_{i}}

for some
e_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
.
The map
H=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{\ell})
is called a

(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\ell})
-
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
-multiprojective map,
and using the
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
-base embedding again,

H:\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}^{\ell}

can be viewed as a quadratic map
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
.
When
\sigma_{i}=\sigma=\sigma_{j}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq\ell

we say that
H
is a
\sigma
-semiquadratic map and write

H\in\SQH(\ell,p^{m})
. We have

\QH(\ell,p^{m})\subseteq\SQH(\ell,p^{m})\subseteq\textrm{$(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\ell})$-$\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}$-multiprojective maps}\subseteq\QH(n,p).

The reason one is interested in semi-quadratic and multiprojective maps
is that they still have some of the structure that
\QH(\ell,p^{m})
has
and they are larger and give more examples of PN/APN functions. In fact,
there are provably exponentially many pairwise inequivalent
PN/APN functions in the class of multiprojective maps.
Note that semiquadratic and multiprojective maps admit automorphisms
(with respect to the foregoing notions of equivalences)

(1)

H\circ\diag(a,\ldots,a)=\diag(a^{1+\sigma_{1}},\ldots,a^{1+\sigma_{\ell}})\circ H

where
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}^{\times}
and
\diag(\underline{u})
is the diagonal
matrix in
\GL(\ell,p^{m})
with the main diagonal
u_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}

and the rest
0
. Moreover,
\GL(\ell,p^{m})\leq\GL(n,p)
acts on the right
on multiprojective maps and both on the left and on the right on
semiquadratic maps, providing the subfield structure we mentioned before.
This structure has been used

[
7
,
8
,
5
,
4
,
16
]

for solving problems in the theory of highly nonlinear functions.

1.3.
Bijections of
{\mathbb{P}^{\ell-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

Let
q=p^{m}
and define an equivalence relation among vectors

\underline{u},\underline{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}

as
\underline{u}\sim\underline{v}
if and only if
\underline{u}=\lambda\underline{v}

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
Recall that the projective space
{\mathbb{P}^{\ell-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is defined as the set of
equivalence classes of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
modulo the
equivalence relation
\sim
. Any map
F:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}

that satisfies
F(\lambda\underline{x})=\phi(\lambda)F(\underline{x})
for

\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
and
F(\underline{x})\neq 0
for
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
induces a
well-defined map

\overline{F}
on the projective space
{\mathbb{P}^{\ell-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

In
[
6
]
, we defined projective versions
\PQH(\ell,q)

and
\PSQH(\ell,q)
of
\QH
and
\SQH
as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

The projective homogeneous semiquadratic (resp. quadratic) transformations

\overline{F}\in\PSQH(\ell,q)
(resp.
\PQH(\ell,q)
)
are transformations

\overline{F}:{\mathbb{P}^{\ell-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\to{\mathbb{P}^{\ell-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

induced by
F\in\SQH(\ell,q)
(resp.
\QH(\ell,q)
)
that satisfy
F(\underline{x})=\underline{0}\iff\underline{x}=\underline{0}
.

The main result in
[
6
]
is the following result
on semi-quadratic bijections of arbitrary characteristic.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
q=p^{m}
and
f,g,h\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}
be defined as

\displaystyle f(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=x^{\sigma+1}+ay^{\sigma}z+bx^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}z,

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=ay^{\sigma+1}+z^{\sigma}x+bz^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}y,

\displaystyle h(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=z^{\sigma+1}-x^{\sigma}y,

where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and

\sigma=p^{k}
.

Then
\overline{F}=(\overline{f,g,h})\in\PSQH(3,q)
is bijective
if and only if the equation

(2)

ax^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+bx^{\sigma+1}+cx+1=0

has no solution
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Note that in
[
6
]
, Eq. (
2
) is written as
x^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+cx^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma}+bx^{\sigma^{2}}+a=0
. As shown in
[
6
, Lemma B.4.]
, these two conditions are equivalent.

1.4.
Main results

The following is the first main
theorem of this paper, establishing the APN property of the functions
of Theorem
1.2
in characteristic 2.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
q=2^{m}
and
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}
be defined as

\displaystyle F_{1}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=x^{\sigma+1}+ay^{\sigma}z+bx^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}z,

\displaystyle F_{2}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=ay^{\sigma+1}+z^{\sigma}x+bz^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}y,

\displaystyle F_{3}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=z^{\sigma+1}+x^{\sigma}y,

where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
\sigma=2^{k}
,
1\leq k<m
,
d=\gcd(k,m)
and
a\neq 0
.
Define
F\in\SQH(3,q)
via

F=({F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}})\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}.

If the equation

(3)

ax^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+bx^{\sigma+1}+cx+1=0

has no solution
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
then the differential uniformity of
F
is
2^{d}
.

In particular, by choosing
d=1
in Theorem
1.3
,
we construct APN functions.

In
[
6
]
, we proved the odd
p
case and showed that the
corresponding functions are PN. In this paper, we address even
characteristic and APN functions. The tools to prove this result
differ considerably.

For the sequel, we will need the following well-known lemma.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
d=\gcd(m,n)
and
p
be a prime. Then

(i)

\gcd(p^{m}-1,p^{n}-1)=p^{d}-1

(ii)

\gcd(p^{m}+1,p^{n}-1)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }n/d\text{ is odd and }p\text{ is even,}\\
2,&\text{if }n/d\text{ is odd and }p\text{ is odd,}\\
p^{d}+1,&\text{if }n/d\text{ is even.}\end{cases}

The following theorem determines the image set size of the
functions in Theorem
1.3
. It is proven in

[
6
]
only in odd characteristic. The proof also holds
in even characteristic. We include the simple proof for convenience.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
F\in\SQH(\ell,q)
with companion automorphism
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
If
\overline{F}
is bijective on
{\mathbb{P}^{\ell-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
then
F
is

\gcd(\sigma+1,q-1)
-to-
1
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
.

Proof.

Suppose
F(\underline{x_{1}})=F(\underline{x_{2}})

for
\underline{x_{1}},\underline{x_{2}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
.
Then clearly

\overline{F}(\underline{x_{1}})=\overline{F}(\underline{x_{2}})
,
and since
\overline{F}
is bijective on
{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
,
we have
\underline{x_{2}}=\lambda\underline{x_{1}}
for some

\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. So

F(\underline{x_{2}})=\lambda^{\sigma+1}F(\underline{x_{1}})=F(\underline{x_{1}})

if and only if
\lambda^{\sigma+1}=1
,
i.e.,
\lambda
is a
(\sigma+1)
-st root of unity in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
,
and the number of such roots of unity is exactly
\gcd(\sigma+1,q-1)
.
∎

The following theorem is the next main result,
precisely classifying the image set sizes of the functions we consider.
In particular, we prove that our family yields APN permutations.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
q=2^{m}
and
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}
be defined as

\displaystyle F_{1}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=x^{\sigma+1}+ay^{\sigma}z+bx^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}z,

\displaystyle F_{2}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=ay^{\sigma+1}+z^{\sigma}x+bz^{\sigma}y+cx^{\sigma}y,

\displaystyle F_{3}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=z^{\sigma+1}-x^{\sigma}y,

where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
\sigma=2^{k}
,
1\leq k<m
,
d=\gcd(k,m)
and
a\neq 0
.
Let

F=({F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}})\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}

as in Theorem
1.3
.
Assume further that

ax^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+bx^{\sigma+1}+cx+1=0

has no solution
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then
F
is

•

(2^{d}+1)
-to-one on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
if
m/d
is even,

•

bijective on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
if
m/d
is odd.

In particular,
F
is an APN permutation if and only if
d=1
and
m
is odd.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.2
,
\overline{F}
is bijective on
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Then Theorem
1.5
implies that
F
is

\gcd(2^{k}+1,q-1)
-to-
1
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
. The greatest common divisor is then evaluated using Lemma
1.4
.
∎

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.3
.
We distinguish the cases
m/d
even
(which turns out to be the easier case) and
m/d
odd.
For the proofs, we will introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
q=p^{m}
and define for
F\in\SQH(\ell,q)
and any
\underline{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}

the function
L_{\underline{a}}(\underline{x})\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}

via
L_{\underline{a}}(\underline{x})=F(\underline{x}+\underline{a})-F(\underline{x})-F(\underline{a})
. We call the largest
subfield
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
L_{\underline{a}}
is
\mathbb{K}
-linear
for all
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
its
core
.

The following is immediate.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
F\in\SQH(\ell,p^{m})
with companion automorphism

\sigma\colon x\mapsto x^{p^{k}}
and
d=\gcd(k,m)
.
Then its core is
\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}
.

2.1.
The case
m/d
is even

As explained above, the following theorem
combined with Theorem
1.2
proves
Theorem
1.3
for the case that
m/d
is even. The proof
in this case is essentially identical to the proof in the odd
characteristic case given in
[
6
]
. We give the short proof
for completeness.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
F\in\SQH(\ell,2^{m})
with core
\mathbb{F}_{2^{d}}
,
m/d
even.
If
\overline{F}
is bijective on
{\mathbb{P}^{\ell-1}}(\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}})
then
F
is differentially

2^{d}
-uniform.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.5
and Lemma
1.4
,
F
is

(2^{d}+1)
-to-
1
on
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{\ell}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}
, more precisely,

F(\underline{x})=F(\underline{y})
if and only if
\underline{y}=\lambda\underline{x}
for
some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
such that
\lambda^{2^{d}+1}=1
.
This means that for every fixed nonzero
\underline{a}
,
the equation
D_{\underline{a}}(\underline{x})=F(\underline{x}+\underline{a})+F(\underline{x})=\underline{0}

has exactly as many roots as there are solutions to

\underline{x}+\underline{a}=\lambda\underline{x}
where
\lambda^{2^{d}+1}=1
.
Since
\underline{a}\neq\underline{0}
, there is no solution for
\lambda=1
; in all other cases rearranging yields
\underline{x}=\underline{a}/(\lambda-1)
. For each
\lambda\neq 1
we thus get exactly one solution and
D_{\underline{a}}(\underline{x})
has exactly
2^{d}
roots. Since
L_{\underline{a}}=D_{\underline{a}}+F(\underline{a})
is linear over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{d}}
, the number of solutions of
L_{\underline{a}}(\underline{x})=\underline{0}
is thus either
0
or
2^{d}
, in particular,
F
is differentially
2^{d}
-uniform.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
if
m/d
is even.

Let
F\in\SQH(3,2^{m})
be the function defined in Theorem
1.3
. The core of

,

.

•

2.2.
The case
m/d
is odd

In this case, we need a completely new set of tools.

The polynomials

a_{n}t^{n}+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}t+a_{0},

in the indeterminate
t
with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
with the usual addition rule of polynomials and with
the multiplication rule
ta=a^{\sigma}t
for field
automorphism
\sigma\in\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{q})
form a
ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
called the
twisted polynomial ring
.

Note that for
\sigma=\id
, we recover the usual polynomial ring,
but for the other choices of the field automorphism, the multiplication
in the ring of twisted polynomials is non-commutative.
We refer to
[
10
, Chapter 1]
for an introduction
to the twisted polynomial ring (or more generally,
skew-polynomial rings
).

We recall some basic facts for twisted polynomial rings over finite fields.
It is clear from the construction that for any
P,Q\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]

we have
\deg{PQ}=\deg{P}+\deg{Q}
.
We say
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
is a right divisor of
Q\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]

if there is a polynomial
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
such that
Q=R\cdot P
,
and
Q
is in this case a left multiple of
P
.
We call
P
a
linear
right divisor if
\deg(P)=1
.
We define (linear) left divisors as well as right multiples
similarly. For any two twisted polynomials
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
, there is a unique monic polynomial
L\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
such that
L
is a left multiple of both
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, and any other common left multiple of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
is a left multiple of
L
.
Such
L
is called the least common left multiple of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, and we write
L=\lclm(P_{1},P_{2})
.
he degree of the least common left multiple satisfies

\deg\lclm(P_{1},P_{2})=\deg P_{1}+\deg P_{2}-\deg\gcrd(P_{1},P_{2})

where the greatest common right divisor (
\gcrd
) is analogously defined.
The ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
for
\sigma\neq\id
is not a unique
factorization domain, however there are left and right Euclidean algorithms.
Thus, given
P,R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
, there exist unique

Q,S\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
such that
P=Q\cdot R+S
, where
\deg(S)<\deg(R)
.
For the proofs we refer to
[
10
, Chapter 1]
.

The following result by Ore establishes the connection of certain
twisted polynomials with the family in Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 2.5
.

[
15
, Theorem 3]

The polynomial

P(X)=aX^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+bX^{\sigma+1}+cX+1\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]

has no roots in
\mathbb{F}_{q}

if and only if

F(t)=at^{3}+bt^{2}+ct+1\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
has no linear right divisor.

The result of Ore is for arbitrary degree
F
. Here,
we recall just degree three which we use.
We also have the following result which links the left and right
divisors also holds for arbitrary degrees.

Proposition 2.6
.

[
6
, Lemma B.2]

Let
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
be a polynomial.
Then
P
has a linear right divisor if and only if

P
has a linear left divisor.

Theorem
2.5
in particular establishes a connection between degree
3
twisted polynomials and the polynomials appearing in Theorem
1.3
. We thus further investigate degree
3
twisted polynomials.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
with
\deg(P)=3
such that
P
has no
linear right divisors. Then
P
has also no linear right divisors
in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[t;\sigma]
.

Proof.

Assume to the contrary that

P=P_{2}\cdot(t-b)

with
P_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[t;\sigma]
and
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
(note that it is impossible that
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
P_{2}\notin\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
). Then also

P=P^{(q)}=P_{2}^{(q)}\cdot(t-b^{q}),

where
P^{(q)}
is the polynomial
P
with every coefficient taken
to the
q
-th power. So
P
is, in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[t;\sigma]
, a left multiple
of
(t-b)
and
(t-b^{q})
, so it is particular a left multiple of the
least common left multiple
R:=\lclm(t-b,t-b^{q})
. We calculate
R
.
We have by the definition of the least common left multiple that

R
is of degree two, and,

R=(t-c_{1})(t-b)=(t-c_{2})(t-b^{q})

for some
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. Usual multiplication in the twisted polynomial ring yields

R=t^{2}-(c_{1}+b^{\sigma})t+c_{1}b=t^{2}-(c_{2}+(b^{q})^{\sigma})t+c_{2}b^{q}.

Comparing coefficients yields
c_{1}b=c_{2}b^{q}
and
c_{2}+(b^{q})^{\sigma}=c_{1}+b^{\sigma}
. Solving the second equation for
c_{2}
and substituting in the first yields
c_{1}=b^{q}\frac{b^{\sigma}-(b^{q})^{\sigma}}{b-b^{q}}
, so

R=t^{2}-\left(b^{q}\frac{b^{\sigma}-(b^{q})^{\sigma}}{b-b^{q}}+b^{q}\right)t+b^{q+1}\frac{b^{\sigma}-(b^{q})^{\sigma}}{b-b^{q}}=t^{2}-\left(\frac{b^{\sigma+1}-b^{q({\sigma+1})}}{b-b^{q}}\right)t+b^{q+1}\frac{b^{\sigma}-b^{\sigma q}}{b-b^{q}}.

A routine check reveals that both the linear and the constant coefficients

d_{1},d_{0}
are
contained in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by simply computing
d_{i}^{q}-d_{i}=0
, so

R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
.

Then
P=QR
for some
Q\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[t;\sigma]
with
\deg(Q)=1
.
Since
R\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
we also have necessarily
Q\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]

by the right Euclidean division algorithm.
Thus,
P
is left-divisible by a degree one polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
,
so it is also right-divisible by one by Proposition
2.6
,
contradicting our assumption.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

The polynomial
P(X)=aX^{\sigma^{2}+\sigma+1}+bX^{\sigma+1}+cX+1\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
has no roots in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if it has no roots in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
s(t)=at^{3}+bt^{2}+ct+1\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t;\sigma]
.
Now assume
P
has a root in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. By Theorem
2.5
,
s
has then a linear right divisor in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, so by Proposition
2.7
it has a linear right divisor in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, so
s
has (again by Theorem
2.5
) a root in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The converse is trivial.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.3
for the case
m/d
odd. The idea is to view
F\in\SQH(3,q)
in a suitable extension field where we can then reduce the proof to the

is even case we already proved.

1.3
if

is odd.

Let
q=2^{m}
,
F\in\SQH(3,q)
be defined as in the Theorem
1.3
with companion automorphism
\sigma=2^{k}
,

,

is odd such that Eq. (

) has no roots in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Let

, where

is odd,

.
Consider

defined exactly as in Theorem
1.3
, using the same coefficients

and the same field

. We can view

embedded naturally
in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2^{\ell+1}}}^{3}
and under this embedding, the restriction

to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}

. By repeated applications of
Corollary
2.8
, Eq.
3

\mathbb{F}_{q^{2^{\ell+1}}}

for a fixed

∎

3.
Comparison to existing trivariate APN functions

Two families of APN permutations were found by Li and Kaleyski
[
14
]
. One of these was recently extended by Bartoli and Stanica, who also gave one more family of trivariate APN functions
[
1
]
. We will now show that all of these are very special cases of our family of functions we presented in Theorem
1.3
. We want to note that the proofs of the theorems in
[
14
]
and
[
1
]
are spanning multiple pages of complicated case distinctions each, and some are computer assisted in the sense that computer algebra systems are used to compute complicated resultants. Others use deep tools from algebraic geometry, like the Hasse-Weil bounds. Our results are computer free, much shorter and purely elementary. We thus give a simple and unified explanation for all these functions. We also want to emphasize that the approach using resultants led to a number of unnecessary additional conditions in
[
14
]
. They also spend multiple pages on proving the image set size of these functions in certain cases, we can recover and generalize these results easily with Theorem
1.5
(whose proof only spans a few lines).

4.
Inequivalence to Gold APN functions

Other known infinite families of APN functions that yield examples in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with
n=3k
for any choice of
k
are monomials. By a result of Yoshiara, the only monomials that are possibly CCZ-equivalent to the family of APN functions in Theorem
1.3
are in the family of Gold functions
F(x)=x^{2^{i}+1}
for
\gcd(i,n)=1

[
18
]
. In this section, we prove that the APN functions in Theorem
1.3
are inequivalent to the Gold APN functions. The proof follows the same lines as a similar odd-characteristic proof given by the authors in
[
6
]
by proving that the linear automorphism groups of the respective functions cannot be conjugate in the general linear group. We want to note that for none of the trivariate APN functions contained in our family (as described in Section
3
) such a theoretical inequivalence result was provided.

We recall some facts and definitions needed. Firstly, a result of Yoshiara
[
17
]
together with an observation in
[
11
]
yields that the APN functions in Theorem
1.3
are CCZ-equivalent to Gold APN functions if and only if they are linearly equivalent to them.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a function. We define its linear automorphism group by

\Aut_{L}(F)=\{(L_{1},L_{2})\in\GL(n,2)^{2}\colon L_{1}\circ F=F\circ L_{2}\}.

The following well known and simple criterion can then be used to prove inequivalence of functions, see e.g.
[
5
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
F,G\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be linearly equivalent functions via
L_{1}\circ F=G\circ L_{2}
. Then
\Aut_{L}(G)=(L_{1},L_{2})\Aut_{L}(F)(L_{1}^{-1},L_{2}^{-1})
.

The linear automorphism groups of the Gold APN functions were determined by Kaspers and Zhou.

Theorem 4.3

(
[
12
, Theorem 4.1.]
)
.

Let
G\colon\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
be defined by
G(x)=x^{2^{i}+1}
with
1\leq i<n
,
\gcd(i,n)=1
and
n\geq 5
. Then
L_{1}\circ G=G\circ L_{2}
for
L_{1},L_{2}\in\GL(n,2)
if and only if
L_{1}=a^{2^{i}+1}x^{2^{k}}
and
L_{2}=ax^{2^{k}}
for some
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{\times}
and
1\leq k<n
. In particular, the linear automorphism group is isomorphic to
\GammaL(1,2^{n})
.

We now prove that the linear automorphism groups of the functions
F
defined in
1.3
cannot be conjugate to the ones of Gold APN functions. Note that we already identified some automorphisms of
F
in Eq. (
1
). We thus have:

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
F
be as in Theorem
1.3
with associated field automorphism

\sigma=2^{k}
. Let

U_{k}=\left\{\left(\diag(a^{2^{k}+1},a^{2^{k}+1},a^{2^{k}+1}),\diag(a,a,a)\right):a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{\times}\right\}\leq\GL(3,2^{m})^{2}.

Then
U_{k}\leq\Aut_{L}(F)
.

We also need some lemmas and known results:

Lemma 4.5

(Zsigmondy’s theorem)
.

[
9
, Chapter IX., Theorem 8.3.]

Let
n>1
,
n\neq 6
be an integer. Then there exists a prime
p
that divides
2^{n}-1
but not
2^{k}-1
for all
1\leq k<n
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
S\leq\GL(3m,2)
be a subgroup of the general linear group corresponding to the mappings
S=\{x\mapsto ax\colon a\in H\}
where
H\leq\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
such that
H
is not contained in a proper subfield of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
. Then the normalizer
N_{\GL(3m,2)}(S)
of

in
\GL(3m,2)
is exactly
N_{\GL(3m,2)}(S)=\GammaL(3,2^{m})
.

Proof.

Let
L\in\GL(3m,2)
. Write
L
as a linearized polynomial, i.e.,
L=\sum_{i=0}^{3m-1}c_{i}x^{2^{i}}
for
c_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{3m}}
. We have
L\in N_{\GL(3m,2)}(S)
if and only if
LS=SL
. So consider
A_{a}\in S
defined by
x\mapsto ax
. Then

LA_{a}=\sum_{i=0}^{3m-1}c_{i}(ax)^{2^{i}}=\sum_{i=0}^{3m-1}c_{i}a^{2^{i}}x^{2^{i}}

and

A_{a^{\prime}}L=a^{\prime}\sum_{i=0}^{3m-1}c_{i}x^{2^{i}}=\sum_{i=0}^{3m-1}c_{i}a^{\prime}x^{2^{i}}.

So if
LA_{a}=A_{a^{\prime}}L
then
a^{\prime}=a^{2^{i}}
for all
i
such that
c_{i}\neq 0
. Assume
c_{k}\neq 0
, so
a^{\prime}=a^{2^{k}}
. Choose
a\in H
in a way that it is not contained in a proper subfield of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}
, so
a^{2^{i}}=a
if and only if
i
is a multiple of
m
. This implies only
c_{k},c_{k+m},c_{k+2m}
can be nonzero (where we take indices modulo
3m
). So
L=c_{k}x^{2^{k}}+c_{k+m}x^{2^{k+m}}+c_{k+2m}x^{2^{k+2m}}
. We conclude the proof by noticing that the set of invertible mappings of this form is exactly
\GammaL(3,2^{m})
.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
q=2^{m}
,

,
m\neq 6
,
F=(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3})\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
be as in Theorem

with associated field automorphism

,

. Then

is CCZ-inequivalent to any Gold APN function.

Proof.

We first consider a special case.

Claim 1
.

There are no
L_{1},L_{2}\in\GammaL(3,2^{m})
such that
L_{1}\circ F=G\circ L_{2}
for a Gold APN function
G
.

Proof of claim.

Assume first
L_{1},L_{2}\in\GL(3,2^{m})
.
Such an equivalence exists if and only if there exist ordered
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-bases
\{u_{1},v_{1},w_{1}\}
and
\{u_{2},v_{2},w_{2}\}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
such that
G(u_{1}x+v_{1}y+w_{1}z)=u_{2}F_{1}(x,y,z)+v_{2}F_{2}(x,y,z)+w_{2}F_{3}(x,y,z)
since a transformation with an element in

corresponds exactly to a change of basis. Expanding

in Theorem

. This proves the claim for

.
Now assume an equivalent of the form

exists. It is immediately clear by expanding again that the associated field automorphisms to

have to be identical. Since all field automorphisms
(x\mapsto x^{2^{l}},x\mapsto x^{2^{l}})
are contained in

(see Theorem

), we then necessarily also have an equivalence of the form

Now assume

be the field automorphism associated to

. Now let

be a prime divisor of

that does not divide

for any

. The existence of such a

under our conditions is guaranteed by Theorem
4.5

.
Consider the subgroup
U_{k}\leq\Aut_{L}(F)
defined in Lemma
4.4

is cyclic of order

, so it has a unique Sylow

. Because

is a

-group of the same size, it must be the unique Sylow

-subgroup of

, which we will denote by

. Let

be the unique Sylow

-subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{m}}^{*}
. By Lemma

and Theorem

and

correspond exactly to the set of scalar multiplications
S_{2}=\{x\mapsto ax\mid a\in P\}
. Because

5.
Conclusion and further notes

We constructed a new family of APN functions, and in particular, APN permutations. This family unifies several known constructions. The proof techniques we used differed considerably from the ones used in previous papers and indicate a more general way forward by connecting the constructions of highly nonlinear functions to the constructions of certain permutations of the projective space. A natural open question is:
