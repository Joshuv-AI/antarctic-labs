Anion-exchange fluorinated ion conductors for stable high-voltage lithium battery
Authors: Qiaodong Li, Jingming Yao, Yiran Wang, Xinyu Liu, Jiuwei Lei, Wen Yin, Zhixuan Yu, Shaojie Wang, Lin Li, Xinlin Yan, Zongpu Shao, Zhenyu Wang, Wei Xia, Yue Chen, Chuang Yu, Lin Wang, Bin Wen, Bo Xu, Jianyu Huang, Long Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of high-energy all-solid-state batteries is critically hindered by the electrochemical instability of solid electrolytes against high-voltage oxide positive electrodes. While fluorination is a promising strategy to enhance electrolyte stability, conventional methods are ineffective, resulting in insufficient fluorine content and a debilitating trade-off with ionic conductivity. Here, we report a solid-state anion-exchange strategy that overcomes these limitations by producing core-shell Li-fluoride/LiCl nanocomposite precursors. These precursors enable the synthesis of heavily fluorinated lithium-halide and lithium-sulfide electrolytes that combine high ionic conductivity with good oxidative stability. This stability originates from the formation of a robust, self-limiting LiF-rich interphase at the positive electrode. Consequently, an all-solid-state battery using a Li-rich oxide positive electrode achieves high performance, retaining over 77.5% capacity after 2000 cycles at a high rate of 3 C (1 C = 275 mA/g) and a 5.0 V cutoff. This anion-exchange approach is broadly applicable to other systems and establishes a versatile platform for designing advanced fluorinated materials for next-generation batteries.

Introduction

Overcoming the energy-density bottleneck of all-solid-state lithium batteries (ASSLBs) hinges on deploying high-capacity, high-voltage oxide positive electrodes1–3. Yet, current solid electrolytes (SEs) remain inadequate at such potentials, failing to sustain high, slowly degrading capacity over extended cycling because of their limited oxidative stability and the formation of unstable cathode–electrolyte interphases (CEIs)4,5. These degradation pathways intensify as the upper cutoff voltage is raised6. Li-chlorides (Li-M-Cl, M = Zr/Y/In/Al/Ta/Sc, etc.) have emerged as promising SE/catholyte because they exhibit wider intrinsic electrochemical window (about 4.3 V vs Li+/Li) than sulfide-based SEs and tend to form comparatively more passivating CEI7. Nevertheless, under higher voltage conditions, persistent interfacial degradation, driven by progressive halide catholyte decomposition and continuous parasitic reactions with the oxide active material, still undermines long-term performance in halide-based ASSLBs8.

Fluorination has therefore been pursued to extend the oxidative stability limit and suppress interfacial reactivity4,6,9. This strategy, proven effective in liquid electrolytes10, has been translated to solid systems via fluorination of chlorides11, oxychlorides12, and sulfides systems13. However, several critical barriers continue to limit its efficacy in SEs. First, conventional synthesis routes like solid-state sintering are often kinetically limited, yielding low fluorine incorporation, inhomogeneous distribution, and only modest fluorination that fails to robustly passivate aggressive high-voltage positive electrode surfaces, particularly beyond 4.6 V14,15. Second, a fundamental trade-off emerges between interfacial/oxidative stability and ionic transport: strong Li⁺–F⁻ interactions that thermodynamically stabilize the electrolyte and the CEI but simultaneously impedes Li⁺ mobility, depressing bulk ionic conductivity16. As a result, while limited fluorination may enhance interfacial compatibility, it fails to achieve durable, minimal-degradation capacity retention during extended cycling at elevated voltages (≥ 4.6 V). This fundamental dichotomy has created a critical bottleneck, necessitating the development of effective synthesis strategies capable of achieving extensive fluorination for high-voltage stability without sacrificing the transport channels essential for efficient Li+ conduction.

In this work, we overcome this long-standing trade-off by introducing a mechanochemically activated, solid-state anion-exchange strategy between LiF and various metal chlorides (MCla, M = Al, Zr and Y). This method yields core-shell nanocomposites—exemplified by a Li3AlF6/LiCl composite core encapsulated within a pure LiCl shell—that serves as highly effective fluorinating precursors. These precursors enable the synthesis of a class of heavily fluorinated, amorphous SEs that successfully decouple the competing properties, exhibiting both high oxidative stability and decent ionic conductivity. We demonstrate that this approach is a versatile and effective platform, applicable to both halide and sulfide electrolyte systems, establishing a general route for designing advanced materials for high-energy batteries.

Results

Core-shell Li-fluoride by anion exchange

A complete F−Cl anion exchange was successfully induced in the solid state by subjecting mixtures of LiF and MCla to high-energy ball milling (inset to Fig. 1a). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the resulting products confirmed the formation of LiCl alongside the corresponding Li-metal fluorides: Li3AlF6, Li2ZrF6, and LiYF4 (Fig. 1a). The broadened diffraction peaks are indicative of the products’ nanocrystalline nature. The thermodynamic viability of these exchange reactions was corroborated by first-principles calculations, which predicted negative formation enthalpies for these systems and several others (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Tables 1, 2). Among the transition metals studied, the propensity for anion exchange was found to correlate with electronegativity (χ), generally increasing as χ rises. Given the natural abundance of Al and the predicted high intrinsic electrochemical window of Li3AlF6 (> 6 V vs Li⁺/Li)7, the Li3AlF6 + 3LiCl (hereafter LAF) composite, from reacted LiF and AlCl3, was selected as a representative system for detailed investigation. Rietveld refinement of the synchrotron radiation X-ray diffraction (SR-XRD) profile of LAF verified the primary phases as Li3AlF6 and LiCl, with only a trace amount of residual LiF reactant (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 3). A striking feature of the LAF composite (Supplementary Fig. 3), revealed by cryogenic scanning transmission electron microscopy (cryo-STEM), is a distinct core-shell architecture (Fig. 1c). Selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns taken from the core region (#1 in Fig. 1c) confirmed the coexistence of LiCl and Li3AlF6, whereas the patterns from the shell region (#2) showed diffraction spots corresponding only to LiCl. This structural assignment was unequivocally confirmed by electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) mapping (Fig. 1d). The elemental maps show that Al and F are confined exclusively to the core, while Li and Cl are present in both the core and the shell. This demonstrates that the anion-exchange reaction produces composite particles with a Li3AlF6/LiCl core enveloped by a ~ 30 nm thick LiCl shell. Cryo-STEM characterization on another LAF batch (Supplementary Fig. 4) also shows the same architecture, indicating that the core-shell structure in LAF is not an occasional but is a general observation. The LiCl crystallite size was evaluated from the SR-XRD pattern. To avoid possible errors caused by real grain shapes and/or anisotropy in strain, high-angle peaks (20-36° in 2theta) were selected for grain size evaluation using the Williamson-Hall method (Supplementary Fig. 5). The calculated crystallite size is 35.4(1) nm, comparable to the grain size at tens of nanometers measured by TEM.

Moreover, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, the STEM image shows that the Li2ZrF6 + 4LiCl (LZF) particles are encapsulated in a shell, where the morphology is significantly distinct from the core region. The FFT analysis evidences that the shell is composed by LiCl. EELS analysis further confirms that the signal intensity of Li and especially Cl in the shell region is higher than that in the core. This result provides clear evidence that LZF also possesses a core-shell structure synthesized via “6LiF + ZrCl4” solid-state anion exchange reaction. The formation of LiCl shell could be attributed to the effect of mechanochemical reaction. A similar scenario with LiCl segregation on the particle surface is also reported recently17. Owing to the high Cl-ion mobility, the continuous mechanical force, the local high temperature, and a large number of defects together tend to drive the LiCl phase diffuse to the surface, thereby forming a continuous shell. Meanwhile, Li3AlF6 (or Li2ZrF6) and the not timely immigrated out LiCl are restricted within the particles and then form a composite core. This may be a process of “reaction-diffusion-phase separation”. This precisely controlled nanostructure, driven by atomic-level exchange during mechanosynthesis, is a specific feature that is difficult to achieve through conventional solid-state reaction methods15,18.

Fluorinated electrolytes from LAF

While LAF itself exhibits low ionic conductivity (7 × 10−7 S/cm, Supplementary Fig. 7), its core-shell architecture and its composition as a LiCl nanocomposite make it an ideal fluorinating agent and precursor for synthesizing high-performance SEs19,20. To illustrate this concept, a series of electrolytes, denoted LAF-(3-x)Ta(x)Al where x ranges from 0 to 3, were prepared through the reaction of LAF with TaCl5 and AlCl3 in varying molar ratios (Methods). The resulting compounds were labeled LAF-3Ta, LAF-2Ta1Al, LAF-1Ta2Al, and LAF-3Al based on their respective tantalum and aluminum compositions. XRD analysis of these materials (Supplementary Fig. 8a) revealed a predominantly amorphous structure, evidenced by a broad halo, with coexisting peaks characteristic of crystalline LiCl. Spatially resolved SR-XRD on LAF-1Ta2Al confirmed identical patterns and the compositional uniformity of the product (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). To roughly quantify the amorphous content, we performed an internal standard method with laboratory XRD21,22, which revealed an average amorphous content of approximately 95.9 wt% in the LAF-1Ta2Al electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating that the amorphous phase is dominant in the composite. High-resolution cryo-STEM imaging of the representative LAF-1Ta2Al revealed a distinctive “nanocrystal-in-glass” architecture, consisting of nanocrystal LiCl nanodots, several nanometers in size, dispersed within a continuous amorphous matrix (Fig. 2a−c). The ionic conductivity of LAF-(3-x)Ta(x)Al electrolytes is three to four orders of magnitude higher than that of LAF precursor (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 10). A systematic comparison showed that while LAF-2Ta1Al possesses the highest ionic conductivity at 3.25 mS/cm, LAF-1Ta2Al exhibits the highest intrinsic electrochemical window (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 11). These electrolytes also display favorable mechanical deformability, with a low Young’s modulus in the range of 2−5 GPa (Supplementary Figs. 12−15, Supplementary Table 4), beneficial for achieving dense microstructures via cold-pressing/rolling. Given its optimal balance of oxidation tolerance and decent ionic conductivity (1.0 mS/cm), LAF-1Ta2Al was selected for comprehensive battery performance evaluation.

Multi-cations/anions amorphous matrix

To elucidate the structural factors underlying the improved electrochemical performance, the local coordination environments of the LAF-(3-x)Ta(x)Al SEs were investigated using a suite of advanced spectroscopic techniques. Utilizing one-dimensional 27Al and 19F magic-angle spinning (MAS) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), substantial structural modifications were observed following the reaction of the LAF precursor. The 27Al MAS-NMR spectra (Fig. 2e) show that alongside the original six-coordinated AlF63- species (Al-1 peak at –4 ppm), the fluorination generates novel coordination environments. These include F-rich hexacoordinated species such as [(Ta,Al)F6-cClc]3- and/or [AlF6-cClc]3- due to Cl doping (Al-2 peak at -16 ppm), as well as Cl-rich tetracoordinated species like [AlCl4-dFd]- and/or [(Ta,Al)Cl4-dFd]- arising from F doping (Al-3 and Al-4 peaks at 100 and 85 ppm, respectively)23. In the chemical shift region characteristic of five-coordinated Al (20–65 ppm), our spectra show no signals above noise. Previous studies have shown that in the fluorine stabilized five-coordinated Al systems, distinct resonances at around 30–40 ppm remain detectable even at ~1% abundance24. The absence of such signals in our system thus confirms that no five-coordinated Al species is formed. Additionally, Ta atoms are incorporated through Ta/Al co-occupancy within the pre-existing [AlCl4]/[AlF6] polyhedral framework of the amorphous matrix, i.e., [(Ta,Al)Cl4-dFd]- and [(Ta,Al)F6-cClc]3-, and Ta-halides exist as octahedra environment in the F/Cl-dominated phases. Therefore, we conclude that the amorphous matrix is dominated by tetrahedral and octahedral coordination environments, but without invoking penta-coordinated species. Given that 27Al is a quadrupolar nucleus, its solid-state NMR signals are often broadened, distorted, and even shifted by the interactions between nucleus and its local electric field gradient. The relatively sharp peaks of Al-1 and Al-3 indicate a higher local symmetry and low electric field gradient in the octahedral and tetrahedral environments. In comparison, the broader and asymmetric peaks of Al-2 and Al-4 (85 − 40 ppm) indicate more complex and/or mixed anionic environments by a deeper F/Cl co-occupancy, which can be promoted by an amorphous structure. Moreover, there is no four-coordinated signal in the LAF-3Ta sample observed from the 27Al NMR spectrum. This is because of its different precursor (w/o AlCl3) for synthesis. Only when AlCl3 is added can a four-coordinated species be formed. This is the case of the LAF-3Al and LAF-1Ta2Al samples. However, the synthesis of LAF-3Ta involved only adding TaCl5 but without AlCl3 precursor. Consequently, the local structure of LAF-3Ta is dominated by six-coordinated units, i.e., [AlF6-cClc]3- and [(Ta,Al)F6-cClc]3-. Therefore, no four-coordinated Al environment is detected in LAF-3Ta.

The F-2 peak in 19F spectra corroborate the creation of varied mixed-anion (Cl/F) coordination environments around aluminum centers (Fig. 2f), similar to the O-doped AlF6 reported previously25. The peak width in 19F NMR spectra reflects diverse F chemical environments. A broader peak suggests a more complex environment. For LAF-1Ta2Al and LAF-3Al, fluorine exists multiple chemical environments with both six-coordinated [(Ta,Al)F6-cClc]3- and [AlF6-cClc]3- species and, especially, four-coordinated [AlCl4-dFd]- and [(Ta,Al)Cl4-dFd]- species. These high diversity and disorder environments result in a very wide chemical shift range, thereby intensively broadening the F-2 signal peak. In contrast, LAF-3Ta contains only six-coordinated environments, thus the peak of LAF-3Ta is relatively narrow. The formation of LiTaCl6 in Ta-rich LAF-3Ta and/or the presence of Cl-rich phases are responsible for the comparably low onset potentials of LAF-(3-x)Ta(x)Al SEs (Supplementary Fig. 8). A deconvolution analysis on 7Li NMR spectra for both LAF and LAF-1Ta2Al (Supplementary Fig. 16) shows contributions from two Li species, as is verified by determining the spin-lattice relaxation time constant (T1). The two components of LAF with different T1 values (Supplementary Fig. 17) are associated with LiCl for a relatively slow T1 (9.41 s, peak1) and Li3AlF6 for the other T1 (5.92 s, peak2), respectively. By comparison, LAF-1Ta2Al (Supplementary Fig. 18) demonstrates two comparable T1 values of 1.63 (Li-1) and 1.70 s (Li-2), indicating similar local dynamic environments. These two components correspond to Li-ions within the amorphous matrix and Li-ions within LiCl as well as the LiCl-nanocrystals/amorphous interface regions, respectively. The deconvolution analysis reveals substantial amorphous phase contents in LAF-1Ta2Al.

The XPS analysis confirms the complex, co-occupied structure of the electrolytes. A positive shift of ~1 eV in the Al 2p spectra for Ta-containing samples indicates Ta/Al co-occupancy (Fig. 2g). Concurrently, a positive shift in the F 1 s spectra verifies the formation of four-coordinate species (Fig. 2h). These results provide strong evidence for an amorphous matrix characterized by the intimate, atomic-level mixing of both cations (Ta/Al) and anions (Cl/F). Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) provided direct evidence of this atomic-level mixing, revealing not only Al-F-Cl groups (Supplementary Fig. 19), but also complex Al-Ta fragments such as TaAlF- and TaAlCl- (Fig. 2i) in the LAF-1Ta2Al sample, consistent with the NMR and XPS results.

The neutron powder diffraction (NPD) and neutron total scattering test were performed on LAF and LAF-1Ta2Al samples, as shown in Fig. 3. The comparative NPD pattern of LAF and LAF-1Ta2Al reveals a systematic broadening and intensity decay of selected Bragg reflections, evidencing a measurable degree of lattice disorder with localized amorphization. The principal peak at 1.82 Å in the neutron pair-distribution functions (nPDF) of LAF-1Ta2Al (Fig. 3c) is assigned to Al/Ta–F pairs, which shifts +0.04 Å relative to the Al-F bond in LAF (1.78 Å). This evidences successful Ta/Al site-sharing, where the longer Ta-F bonds (≈ 1.88 Å) elongate the average metal-fluorine distance. This is consistent with the NMR result of LAF aforementioned, where only AlF6 units but no Al-Cl environments are detected. The LAF-1Ta2Al specimen appears new correlations at 2.10 (Al-Cl) and 2.32 Å (Ta-Cl), demonstrating that the Al-F anion units are disrupted and Cl enters the first coordination shell to form Ta/Al-Cl/F mixed-anion polyhedra. The first- and second-shell F-F pairs in LAF are located at 2.56 and 2.99 Å, respectively. These features are markedly pronounced in LAF-1Ta2Al, further confirming progressive replacements of the AlF63- framework by [(Ta/Al)F6-cClc]3- units. Moreover, LAF-1Ta2Al shows a much more disordered Li-Cl environment (shortening or elongation) at around 2.55 Å than LAF. It presents a negative PDF component due to lithium’s negative neutron scattering length. This negative peak partially offsets the F–F intensity, along with the increased structural disorder, leading to an asymmetric peak broadening that extends across both shorter and longer distances in the real space. This result further evidences that the Ta introduction drives the formation of Al/Ta–Cl bonds by disrupting the original anion network, and promotes a significant increase in Li-Cl disorder, which facilitate a fast ion transport. Collectively, these results clearly demonstrate that the final electrolyte is a highly complex amorphous matrix characterized by extensive co-occupancy of both cations (Ta/Al) and anions (Cl/F) within various coordination polyhedra, such as [AlF6-cClc]3-, [(Ta,Al)F6-cClc]3-, [AlCl4-dFd]-, and [(Ta,Al)Cl4-dFd]-. Li-ions can rapidly transport through amorphous phases and extensive interface regions between LiCl-nanodots and amorphous matrix26,27. Additionally, the co-occupancy of Ta/Al and Cl/F may elevate Li-vacancy concentrations while promoting structural disorders11. Essential of anion-exchanged precursor

To confirm that the core-shell architecture of the LAF precursor was responsible for this outcome, a series of control experiments were conducted. Analogous materials were synthesized via a one-step process, directly milling the final target raw materials (e.g., Li3AlF6, LiF, AlCl3, TaCl5, etc.) without first forming the LAF intermediate (Supplementary Figs. 20−24 and Supplementary Discussion 1). Moreover, the intermediate prepared from LiF, AlF3, and LiCl (3LiF + AlF3 + 3LiCl counterpart) was compared with LAF, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 25−27 and Supplementary Discussion 2. Nevertheless, these counterparts exhibited markedly different structures, notably lacking the critical Ta/Al co-occupancy, and possessed significantly lower ionic conductivity compared to the material derived from the LAF precursor. This underscores the essential role of the precursor’s nanostructure. When using commercial Li3AlF6 to prepare the counterpart to LAF-1Ta2Al, inhomogeneous LiCl aggregation (Supplementary Fig. 23) was observed from cryo-TEM rather than a uniform “nanocrystal-in-glass” structure. Elemental analysis by SEM-EDS mapping on this control sample (Supplementary Fig. 24) revealed localized regions with significant co-enrichment of F and Al, accompanied by weaker Ta and Cl signals, consistent with unreacted Li3AlF6 residues detected by XRD. Due to the different morphology, the commercially purchased Li3AlF6 + LiCl powders have entirely different reaction kinetics from the core-shell nano-structured LAF powders. The large surface energy and nano particles of the latter facilitate the chemical reactions. Moreover, within the LAF core, the confined Li3AlF6 and LiCl nanocrystals are evenly distributed among each other. These nanoscale close contacts significantly increase the interface area between F/Cl and other reactants, enabling a highly efficient synthesis reaction. The co-occupancy of F/Cl is favorable to amorphization, thereby generating the amorphous matrix. During the ball milling process, the excessive LiCl nanocrystals became uniformly dispersed within the amorphous matrix. Consequently, this pathway is thermodynamically inaccessible when starting from a simple mixture of conventional raw materials, whilst only the LAF precursor with a core–shell structure derived from the anion-exchange process can create the distinctive “nanocrystal-in-glass” architecture. We call it as “self-formed nanoreactor”.

5V-stability and battery performance

To directly probe the electrolyte’s stability against a high-voltage positive electrode, the kinetic electrochemical window of LAF-1Ta2Al was evaluated in an accelerated aging test28,29, using a Li-rich layered oxide Li1.14Ni0.29Mn0.57O2 (LRMO) active material given its high operating voltage of at least 4.8 V. The results were benchmarked against its F-free counterpart, LiCl-1Ta2Al. During potentiostatic holds at progressively higher voltages (Fig. 4a), the cell with LAF-1Ta2Al exhibited only a minimal, self-limiting leakage current that stabilized at a very low level, even at a harsh potential of 5.0 V. In stark contrast, while the F-free electrolyte was stable up to 4.6 V, its leakage current began to grow at 4.8 V and increased catastrophically at 5.0 V, indicating continuous decomposition. This prominent stability translates to advanced battery performance. An ASSLB paring the LAF-1Ta2Al catholyte with an uncoated LRMO active material delivered a high initial discharge capacity of 258 mAh g–1 with an initial Coulombic efficiency of 91.8% (Supplementary Fig. 28). This cell demonstrated good capacity retention, maintaining 91.9% of its initial capacity after 200 cycles at 0.5 C (2.0−5.0 V). It also exhibited rate capability far exceeding the F-free counterpart (Fig. 4b). Notably, under a rigorous high-rate cycling condition of 3 C, the battery exhibited good long-term stability (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 29). The LAF-1Ta2Al battery delivered an ultrahigh starting discharge capacity of 172.71 mAh g–1 and maintains 77.5% of this capacity after 2000 cycles, a benchmark for this material system. Additionally, the LAF-1Ta2Al battery showed negligible voltage decay of only 0.215 mV/cycle (Fig. 4d), lower than those reported previously30–32. In comparison, the LiCl-1Ta2Al battery exhibited poor performance with a capacity of only 50 mAh g–1. This low capacity resulted from the severe oxidation decomposition under high voltage during the initial charging (Supplementary Fig. 30), causing substantial overpotential throughout the entire cycling process. We further evaluated the LAF-1Ta2Al battery (Supplementary Fig. 31) with a higher mass loading on the positive electrode. At a loading of 15.29 mg cm-2 (12 mg) based on LRMO, the battery delivers an areal capacity of 2.75 mAh cm–2 at a 0.3 C, and retains 1.55 mAh cm-2 after 30 cycles at 0.5 C, corresponding to capacity retention of 97.5%. Further improvements on the capacity for high mass loading batteries may lie on the optimization of ionic conductivity in the electrolyte, surface coating on LRMO, incorporation of binders, optimization of carbon additives.

Following the initial CEI formation during the beginning cycles at 0.3 C, the LAF-1Ta2Al battery demonstrated highly overlapping dQ/dV profiles across multiple cycles (Supplementary Fig. 32). This consistency confirms both the high stability of LAF-1Ta2Al and the good compatibility of LAF-1Ta2Al/LRMO interfaces at high voltages33. The distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis (Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Fig. 33) revealed that the interfacial resistance in the LAF-1Ta2Al cell stayed minimal until reaching 4.8 V (vs Li+/Li), showing only a modest rise beyond this voltage threshold. The F-free cell, however, showed significant interfacial resistance growth starting at a much lower voltage of 4.3 V, which intensified after 4.6 V. This confirms that the fluorinated electrolyte effectively mitigates interfacial degradation at high potentials. When compared to state-of-the-art batteries using LRMO positive electrodes, our LAF-1Ta2Al catholyte/electrolyte-layer system demonstrates a high discharge capacity with good long-term cycling stability regardless of rate conditions (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Table 5)30,31,34–38. This conclusion is further supported by comparative experiments using the synthesized F-doped Li3InCl4.8F1.2 (LICF) electrolyte as a benchmark. Although LICF exhibits improved high-rate performance relative to F-free Li3InCl6 (LIC), its overall battery performance remains inferior to that of LAF-1Ta2Al under identical testing conditions (Supplementary Figs. 34, 35). This enhanced performance enables ASSLBs to operate effectively at high voltages exceeding 5.0 V while achieving ultra-long cycle life and maintaining high capacity at elevated charging rates. To evaluate the electrochemical performance of cells at elevated temperatures, the electrolyte’s oxidation stability and the LRMO battery were tested at 60 ± 1 °C. Compared with the LSV curve tested at 25 ± 2 °C, both the initial oxidation onset potential and the main oxidation peak shift to lower values at 60 °C, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 36. This may be induced by accelerated interfacial reaction kinetics at high temperatures39. The LRMO battery (Supplementary Fig. 37) still shows good cycling stability at 60 °C at 3 C, indicating that the LAF-1Ta2Al electrolyte can effectively suppress the continuous interface oxidative degradation at 5 V at elevated temperatures. To further emphasize the 5 V stability and electrochemical performance of LAF-1Ta2Al, we evaluated the battery using a high-voltage LiNi0.5Mn1.5O4 (LNMO) positive electrode. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 38, the battery delivered an initial discharge capacity of 93.76 mAh g–1 at 0.3 C, retaining a discharge capacity of 64.34 mAh g–1 after 30 cycles. Although the capacity is low and the long-term cycling stability needs to further improve, the electrochemical performance of LAF-1Ta2Al coupled with the LNMO positive electrode still demonstrates a marked superiority.

Post-cycling interfacial analysis

To elucidate the origins of the exceptional cycling stability, post-cycling analyses were conducted on the LAF-1Ta2Al batteries. Cross-sectional SEM observations after both 50 (Supplementary Fig. 39a) and 2000 cycles (Fig. 5a) revealed that a dense and intimate contact was maintained between the LRMO positive electrode particles and the LAF-1Ta2Al catholyte. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) line scans across the active-material/catholyte interface showed smooth elemental gradients and, critically, confirmed that the interphase thickness grew only marginally from ~175 nm after 50 cycles to ~ 228 nm after 2000 cycles (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 39b, c), with an average growth rate of merely ~0.027 nm/cycle. The thickness increase of 53 nm is negligible within long-term 1950 cycles, indicating a self-limited interface. SEM line scanning is highly dependent on the two endpoints’ determinations, so please note that the measured thickness could be not fully accurate but sufficient for a comparative purpose. This result evidences the formation of a remarkably robust and stable CEI during the initial cycling, unlike the continuous decomposition byproducts would deteriorate the interface40. The chemical composition of this stable CEI was further investigated using ToF-SIMS and XPS. ToF-SIMS analysis detected a prominent LiF- fragment at the interface, confirming the CEI is LiF-rich. Critically, the signal intensities of LiF-, and other decomposition byproducts (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Figs. 41, 42), increased only slightly between the 50th and 2000th cycle, providing direct evidence that the LiF-rich CEI is a self-limiting passivation layer that effectively suppresses continuous electrolyte oxidation. This was further validated by comparative XPS analysis, deconvolution to distinguish ClOx- from Cl- of pristine SE (Supplementary Fig. 43). After 2000 cycles, the LAF-1Ta2Al cell showed only 18% ClOx-, whereas the fluorine-free LiCl-1Ta2Al cell showed a dramatically higher 53% after only 200 cycles (Fig. 5d). To verify the importance of the LiF-rich CEI, a SEM line-scan test was supplemented on the battery using LRMO and F-free LIC (Supplementary Fig. 34) run after 50 cycles. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 44, a considerably thicker CEI (~ 335 nm) was detected on the LIC battery, indicating that a severe interface degradation. The inherent high fluorine content of our electrolyte, enabled by the anion-exchange synthesis, is therefore directly responsible for forming this highly protective interphase, which is the key to the advanced high-voltage performance.

The resulting hybrid material, LAF-2LPSC, is a highly promising SE that retains the parent argyrodite phase while incorporating the Li3AlF6 fluorinating agent pattern. It features a high ionic conductivity of 6.6 mS/cm, good stability against lithium metal (stable Li plating/stripping for over 3000 h at 0.2 mA cm–2 in a Li|LAF-2LPSC|Li symmetric cell), and a favorably reduced Young’s modulus of 11.85 GPa relative to conventional sulfide SEs36, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 47 and Fig. 6b. These results confirm that the anion-exchange-derived precursor is a versatile platform for enhancing the properties of diverse classes of SEs. This excellent combination of intrinsic properties translates directly to high performance in an all-solid-state battery. When paired with a high-loading (31.4 mg cm–2) bare LiCoO2 positive electrode, the Li-In|LAF-2LPSC|Bare-LCO battery delivered a high initial areal capacity of 3.92 mAh cm–2 and maintained 90% capacity retention after 500 cycles (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 48). Moreover, the capacity degradation mainly occurs within the first 20 cycles and becomes negligible thereafter. At a low-loading (7.6 mg cm–2), the battery demonstrated high rate capability, cycling for over 1000 cycles at 2 C with 92% retention (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 49). The relatively large polarization indicates the formation of a resistive interface inside the positive electrode, which generally occurs between sulfide-based catholytes and unmodified oxide active materials42,43. Nevertheless, compared with the F-free LPSC battery, LAF-2LPSC with the LAF incorporation intensively improved the interface (electro)chemical stability against the unmodified LCO at high voltages. These results, which rank among the best for Li-argyrodite-based cells44, confirm that our anion-exchange strategy is a broadly applicable and powerful platform for creating advanced electrolytes for high-voltage applications.

In summary, we have developed a versatile synthetic route based on a mechanochemically activated F-Cl anion exchange in the solid state. This method produces core-shell architectured LAF precursor that enables the synthesis of heavily fluorinated Li-halide and Li-sulfide SEs. These advanced materials resolve the critical trade-off that has hindered the field, combining high ionic conductivity with good oxidative stability. We have demonstrated that the origin of this stability is the formation of a robust, self-limiting, LiF-rich interphase, which allows all-solid-state batteries to achieve long-term cycling stability at high rates and voltages. The successful implementation of this solid-state anion-exchange chemistry establishes a new and powerful design paradigm, opening a practical pathway for creating a wide range of advanced fluorinated materials for the next generation of high-energy batteries.

Methods

Materials synthesis

Anion-exchange reaction process for preparing Li-fluorides/LiCl composites: The composite were synthesized using LiF powders (>99.99%, Aladdin) together with AlCl3 (>99.99%, Thermo Fisher), ZrCl4 (>98%, Thermo Fisher) or YCl3 (>99.99%, Aladdin) powders, via activating anion exchange through high-energy ball-milling. According to the formula, a molar ratio of LiF to AlCl3, ZrCl4, or YCl3 is set as 6:1, 6:1 or 4:1, respectively. The weighted powders were loaded into 50 ml zirconia jars for ball milling in a MITR planetary mill (YXQM-2L) at 700 rpm (main disk 350 rpm) for 8 h, with intermittent milling cycles of 10 min milling and 5 min rest. The mass ratio of zirconia balls (25 balls, 10 mm diameter) to the powders is 82:1. The obtained fine powders were collected and stored in an argon-filled glove box (H2O and O2 ≤ 0.1 ppm). All procedures, except the ball milling process, were conducted in the glove box.

Preparation of fluorinated LAF-(3-x)Ta(x)Al SEs using LAF precursor: The LAF-(3-x)Ta(x)Al powders were synthesized from AlCl3, TaCl5 (>99.8%, Aladdin), and LAF. The ratio of AlCl3, TaCl5, and LAF powders was set as x:(3-x):1 (x = 0, 1, 2, 3). The whole powders were weighted with a mass of 2 g, and loaded into 100 ml tungsten carbide jars with a ball-to-powder ratio of 160:1, and ball-milled in a MITR planetary mill at 600 rpm for 10 h, with intermittent milling cycles of 2 min milling and 1 min rest. We use tungsten carbide here because the Li-Ta-Cl-based halides require high mechanical energy and tungsten carbide was commonly employed.

Preparation of LAF-1Ta2Al counterparts via one-step synthesis process: To explore the necessity of the core-shell structure of LAF precursor on LAF-1Ta2Al synthesis, three control samples analogous to LAF-1Ta2Al (with and w/o F) were synthesized via one-step process. That is, the starting materials of LiCl/AlCl3/TaCl5 (w/o F), LiF/AlCl3/TaCl5 (with F from LiF), Li3AlF6/LiCl/AlCl3/TaCl5 (with F from commercial Li3AlF6) were used with a molar ratio of 3:2:1, 6:3:1, and 1:3:2:1, respectively, which were designated as LiCl-1Ta2Al, LiF+AlCl3-1Ta2Al, and Li3AlF6 + LiCl-1Ta2Al. Anhydrous LiCl (>99.9%) and Li3AlF6 (90%) were purchased from Aladdin and KE NA Chemistry, respectively. The ball milling process follows the same procedure as LAF-(3-x)Ta(x)Al aforementioned.

Preparation of LAF counterpart via 3LiF + AlF3 + 3LiCl: To compare with LAF, an intermediate was prepared by using LiF, AlF3 (>99.9%, Aladdin), and LiCl powders (designated as 3LiF + AlF3 + 3LiCl). The mixture was ball-milled with the same procedure as LAF aforementioned. The resulting 3LiF + AlF3 + 3LiCl intermediate together with AlCl3, TaCl5 raw materials were used to prepare 3LiF + AlF3 + 3LiCl-1Ta2Al based on the same procedure as LAF-1Ta2Al aforementioned.

Synthesis of Li3InCl4.8F1.2 SE: Stoichiometric amounts of LiCl, InCl3 (>99.99%, Aladdin), and InF3 (>99.5%, Aladdin) were used as precursors. The mixture (1 g) was placed in a zirconia jar (10 balls, 10 mm diameter) and subjected to high-energy ball milling at 500 rpm for 20 h using a planetary ball mill (PULVERIZER 80). The resulting powder was then pelletized and sealed in a quartz tube under vacuum, followed by annealing at 260 °C for 5 h.

Preparation of fluorinated LAF-xLPSC SEs: LAF-xLPSC was synthesized from P2S5 (anhydrous, Aladdin), Li2S (anhydrous, Aladdin), and LAF at different molar ratios (listed in Supplementary Table 6). The weighted powders (2 g) were loaded into 50 ml zirconia jars and mixed in a MITR planetary mill at 300 rpm (main disk 150 rpm) for 2 h, then pressed into pellets, sealed in a quartz tube under vacuum, and annealed at 450 °C for 10 h with a heating rate of 3 °C min-1, followed by natural cooling.

Electrochemical characterization

Halide-based Li-In|LPSC16 | SE | LRMO ASSLBs: The ASSLBs using halide catholyte and SE layer were assembled as follows. The positive electrode composite was obtained by mixing uncoated Li1.14Ni0.29Mn0.57O2 (LRMO) active material, LAF-1Ta2Al (or LiCl-1Ta2Al) catholyte, and vapor-grown carbon fiber (VGCF) with a weight ratio of 60:38:2, by using YXQM-2L (MITR) at 200 rpm for 1 h with 40 zirconia balls (5 mm). First, 70 mg Li5.4PS4.4Cl1.6 (LPSC16, a passivation electrolyte layer at the anodic side) powders was loaded into a polyaryletheretherketone (PEEK) cylinder and pressed at 100 MPa for 2 min. Then, 30 mg LAF-1Ta2Al (or LiCl-1Ta2Al) SE layer was pressed onto the LPSC16 layer with the same pressure. Subsequently, the positive electrode mixture (6 or 20 mg) was spread on the SE layer and pressed at 360 MPa for 3 min. Then, an indium foil (10 mm in diameter, 50 μm thick) together with a Li foil (5 mm in diameter, 100 μm thick) as Li-In electrode was attached to the LPSC16 side. Finally, the whole battery was pressed using stainless-steel (SS) rods/foils under 50 MPa. The Li3InCl6 battery was assembled following the aforementioned procedures.

Sulfide-based Li-In|SE|Bare-LCO ASSLBs: The positive electrode composite was obtained by mixing (same process as LRMO electrode) an uncoated LiCoO2 (bare-LCO) active material, LAF-LPSC (or LPSC), and VGCF in a weight ratio of 70:30:0.5. First, 100 mg LAF-LPSC (or LPSC) as a sole SE layer was loaded into a PEEK cylinder and pressed at 100 MPa for 2 min. Subsequently, the positive electrode powders (8.5 or 35 mg) were spread onto this layer and pressed at 360 MPa for 3 min. Then, a Li-In electrode was attached to the other side. Finally, the whole battery was pressed using SS rods/foils under 50 MPa. The batteries were assembled in a glove box with H2O and O2 ≤ 0.1 ppm.

ASSLBs Measurements: Galvanostatic charge/discharge test of the ASSLBs was conducted using a NEWARE battery test system at 35 ± 1 °C or 60 ± 1 °C. An external pressure of 6 MPa was applied during the cell cycling. All tested batteries were disassembled after discharge in a glove box with H2O and O2 ≤ 0.1 ppm at 25 ± 2 °C, and the electrode/electrolyte samples were collected and transferred for ex situ measurements without exposure to air. The potentiostatic aging tests using electrochemical floating method were carried out on the Li-In|LPSC16 | SE | LRMO ASSLBs. The batteries were charged to 4.6, 4.8, and 5.0 V (vs. Li⁺/Li) at a constant current of 0.1 C, respectively, and dwelling for 10 h at each voltage step. EIS measurements were performed on an electrochemical workstation (Admiral Squidstat Plus), with an amplitude of 20 mV for the applied potentiostatic signal and frequency range from 2 MHz to 0.01 Hz (20 data points for per decade of frequency) at 25 ± 2 °C. In situ GEIS measurement was conducted during a charging and discharging process. The collected GEIS data were first pre-processed by removing the inductive part. The processed data were then validated using the Lin-KK method to ensure sufficient quality for further DRT analysis. Finally, the data were transformed into DRT using a MATLAB calculation code developed by Wan et al.45.

Ionic/Electronic Conductivity Measurements: The SE pellets for the ionic/electronic conductivity measurement were cold-pressed at 400 MPa. The ionic and electronic conductivities were evaluated on SS | | SS symmetric cells at 25 ± 2 °C through the AC impedance and direct current (DC) polarization method, respectively. The DC polarization was conducted at a constant voltage of 0.5 V. The ionic conductivity was calculated from the total resistance from the local minimum at the intersection of the impedance plot.

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements: LSV was tested on Li|LPSC16|halide|halide/VGCF cells at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s-1 from the open circuit voltage to 6 V versus Li+/Li on the electrochemical workstation. The LAF-(3-x)Ta(x)Al (x = 0,1,2,3) or LiCl-1Ta2Al halide SEs and VGCF composites were mixed at a weight ratio of 9:1 in an agate mortar for 15 min. To prevent the reaction of the halide-SE/Li-electrode, 70 mg of LPSC16 layer was cold-pressed at 100 MPa, followed by pressing 30 mg of halide electrolyte layer next to it at 150 MPa. Then, 8 mg of the halide/VGCF composite as the working electrode was spread onto the halide SE layer and pressed at 300 MPa holding for 2 min. A Li foil (9 mm in diameter, 50 μm thick) was then attached to the LPSC16 side serving as both counter and reference electrodes.

Materials characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were acquired using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.54178 Å). Data collection was performed at a scan rate of 3°/min over the 2θ range from 10° to 70°. The powders were sealed with a polyimide film to isolate the powders from the air.

Synchrotron radiation X-ray diffraction (SR-XRD) was performed at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. A monochromatic X-ray beam with an energy of 20 keV (λ = 0.6199 Å) was adopted. GSAS-II software was used for the Rietveld refinement.

The Young’s modulus of cold-pressed SEs pellets was characterized in an Ar-filled glove box using atomic force microscopy (AFM; Bruker Dimension Icon), operated in PeakForce QNM mode, enabling spatial mapping of modulus distribution across the SE pellet surfaces.

High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images were taken on a FEI Talos F200x at 200 kV and an Environmental TEM (ETEM, Titan G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 300 kV. The LAF, LZF, LAF-1Ta2Al, and Li3AlF6 + LiCl-1Ta2Al powders were dispersed onto a Cu grid, loaded into a cryo-TEM holder in an Ar-filled glove box, and then transferred to the TEM chamber. Liquid nitrogen was injected into the cryo-holder to maintain a cryogenic temperature.

After polishing on a cross-section polisher (JEOL IB-19520CCP), the cross-sectional morphology of the cycled ASSLBs was taken from scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JEOL JSM-7900F) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi system. The binding energy scale was calibrated using the C 1 s peak of adventitious carbon at 284.8 eV.

Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) was carried out using a PHI nano TOF II system, which utilized a 30 kV Bi ion as the primary ion (2 nA, with a 15 × 15 µm detection area). The specimen surface was cleaned with an Ar+ ion beam (3 kV, 100 nA) over a 100 × 100 µm sputtering area with a depth of 10 nm.

Magic-angle spinning (MAS) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) of 27Al was acquired on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 600 WB spectrometer (B0 = 14.09 T, Larmor frequency ω0 = 156.4 for 27Al) with a 3.2 mm HXY MAS probe at 20 kHz spinning rate. All 27Al chemical shifts were calibrated with 1 M Al(NO3)3 (aq). 19F MAS NMR of LAF and LAF-1Ta2Al was examined on a Bruker AVANCE III 400 WB spectrometer (B0 = 9.40 T, ω0 = 376.5 MHz) with a 2.5 mm HXY MAS probe at 30 kHz MAS. The LAF-3Ta and LAF-3Al samples were analyzed on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 600 WB spectrometer (B0 = 14.09 T, ω0 = 564.7 MHz) with a 3.2 mm HXY MAS probe at 20 kHz MAS; All 19F chemical shifts were calibrated with C2H4O2F3N. 7Li MAS NMR experiments were acquired on a Bruker Avance III 500 MHz spectrometer (B0 = 11.7 T, ω0 = 194.34 MHz) at the spin rate of 5 kHz. All 7Li chemical shifts were calibrated with 1 M LiCl (aq). The spin-lattice relaxation time constant (T1) of LAF and LAF-1Ta2Al were measured by an inverse recovery experiment using 500 MHz ssNMR. The duration of 90-degree pulse is 2.3 μs, with its power set as 100 W. The last delay time is 3 s which is five times longer than T1. The acquisition time is 0.1 s. The spinning rate of sample is 5 kHz. Each experiment is repeated for 8 times.

The neutron powder diffraction (NPD) and neutron pair distribution function (nPDF) were performed at the Multi-Physics Instrument (MPI) with Q range of 1 to 50 Å-1, at the China Spallation Neutron Source (CSNS). The detectors, which were divided into five banks (bank2 ~ bank6), were calibrated using a NIST-silicon powder standard prior to the measurements. The NPD and nPDF experiments were performed at 25 °C, where around 1.5 g of the sample were loaded in a vanadium-nickel (VNi) holder and sealed under a Helium atmosphere. The sample holder was then placed into an automatic sample-changing device, and the measurements were conducted at room temperature (RT) for 3 hours, with a scattering chamber pressure maintained at ~10−4 Pa.

First-principle computational method

All first-principles calculations employed in this work were carried out using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)46. Electron–ion interactions were treated using the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method47, and the exchange–correlation energy was described using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within generalized gradient approximation (GGA)48. A plane-wave cutoff energy of 400 eV was adopted, with k-point separation in the reciprocal space of the Brillouin zone setting as 0.03 nm−1.

The crystal models of all reactants and products were constructed to energy calculations.
