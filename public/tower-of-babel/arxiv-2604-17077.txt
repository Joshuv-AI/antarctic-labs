The Limiting distribution of elliptic Dedekind sums
M. BORDIGNON AND P. MINELLI

Abstract
. We consider elliptic Dedekind sums that were introduced by Sczech as generalizations of the classical ones to complex lattices. We prove that these sums -suitably normalized- have a Gaussian limiting distribution. As an application, we prove a conjecture due to Ito [ 30 ] .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 11F20, 11J70 ; Secondary 37C30, 60F05

1.
Introduction

Let
h,k
be integers with
k>0
.
Dedekind sums
are defined as

\mathfrak{s}(h,k)\;=\;\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\left(\!\left(\frac{l}{k}\right)\!\right)\left(\!\left(\frac{hl}{k}\right)\!\right),

where

((x))\;=\;\begin{cases}x-\lfloor x\rfloor-\tfrac{1}{2}&\text{if }x\notin\mathbb{Z},\\
0&\text{if }x\in\mathbb{Z},\end{cases}

is the usual sawtooth function. It is possible to show that
\mathfrak{s}
depends only on the quotient of its two arguments, that is
\mathfrak{s}:\mathbb{Q}\backslash\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
, by
\frac{h}{k}\to\mathfrak{s}(\frac{h}{k}).
Therefore, from now on, we will tacitly assume
\gcd(h,k)=1
. These sums appeared for the first time in Dedekind’s investigation of the transformation of the
\eta
function under the group
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

[
17
]
.
The Dedekind eta function is given by

\eta(\tau)\;=\;e^{\pi i\tau/12}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-e^{2\pi in\tau}\bigr),\qquad\tau\in\mathbb{H}.

Under the action of a linear fractional transformation given by an element

A=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with
c>0
, the
\eta
function transforms (see e.g.
[
2
, Chapter 3]
) as

\eta\!\left(A\tau\right)\;=\;\varepsilon(a,b,c,d)\cdot(-i(c\tau+d))^{1/2}\cdot\eta(\tau),

where the multiplier is given by

\varepsilon(a,b,c,d)\;=\;\exp\!\left(\pi i\left(\frac{a+d}{12c}-\mathfrak{s}(d,c)\right)\right),

or, written in logarithmic terms,

(1)

\displaystyle\log\eta(Az)=\log\eta(z)+i\pi\left(\frac{a+d}{12c}-\mathfrak{s}(d,c)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(-i(cz+d)\right).

From the above relations it is possible to extract the well-known
reciprocity formula

(2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(h,k)+\mathfrak{s}(k,h)=\frac{1}{12}\left(\frac{h}{k}+\frac{k}{h}+\frac{1}{hk}\right)-\frac{1}{4},

valid for positive integers
h,k
with
\gcd(h,k)=1
. These sums have been extensively studied. We briefly mention the result of Hickerson
[
27
]
, who showed that the set
\{\mathfrak{s}(\frac{h}{k}):\frac{h}{k}\in\mathbb{Q}\}
is dense in the real line and the set
\{((\frac{h}{k}),\mathfrak{s}(\frac{h}{k})):\frac{h}{k}\in\mathbb{Q}\}
is dense in the plane. Concerning distributional results, Vardi
[
45
]
showed that for any real
r>0
, the set

\{r\mathfrak{s}(h,k)\text{ mod }1:0<h<k\leq X\text{ with }\gcd(h,k)=1\}

equidistributes in
[0,1)
as
X\to\infty
. Moreover, he proved that, under a suitable normalization, Dedekind sums possess a limiting distribution that is
Cauchy
, see
[
46
]
. More formally, let us denote
\Omega_{Q}:=\{\frac{h}{k}\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1],\,1\leq k\leq Q\}
, equipped with the uniform probability measure. Vardi proved the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\epsilon>0
and
Q
large enough, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Q}\left(z\in\Omega_{Q}:\frac{\mathfrak{s}(z)}{\log Q}\leq\frac{x}{2\pi}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{1}{1+y^{2}}\,dy+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{(\log Q)^{\frac{1}{5}-\epsilon}}\right),

uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

In both these results, Vardi exploited the link with the spectral theory of automorphic forms. We refer the reader to the thematic monograph
[
41
]
and the more up-to-date survey
[
22
]
for a comprehensive overview of various results regarding these sums.

There are also several possible generalizations of Dedekind sums; see for instance
[
1
,
47
]
or the work of Beck
[
7
]
, which unifies many of the previous generalizations. A few decades ago, Sczech
[
42
]
introduced an
elliptic
version of Dedekind sums, where
\mathbb{Z}
is essentially replaced by the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of an imaginary quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-D})
, with
D>0
a square-free integer. These sums, which are known in the literature as
Sczech sums
or
elliptic sums
, share many properties with their classical analogue. In order to better describe the construction performed by Sczech, let us recall that for coprime
h,k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k\neq 0
, we have the following alternative representation of Dedekind sums:

(3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(h,k)=\frac{1}{4k}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\cot\left(\pi\frac{hj}{k}\right)\cot\left(\pi\frac{j}{k}\right).

Let now
K
be an imaginary quadratic field and let
L\subset\mathbb{C}
be a non-degenerate lattice. Let
\mathscr{O}_{L}=\{m\in\mathbb{C}:mL\subset L\}
be the ring of multipliers. This is either the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of the field
K
or an order. From now on, we will consider only the cases where
K
has class number one and
L
is selected such that
\mathscr{O}_{L}=\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Define, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the functions

(4)

\displaystyle G_{k}(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in L\\
z+w\notin L\end{subarray}}(z+w)^{k}|w+z|^{-s}\Big|_{s=0},

the value above being given by analytic continuation. For
k=1
, the functions above are analogous to the
\cot(\pi z)
. Therefore, mimicking
Equation

3
, Sczech defined, for
a,c\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
,
c\neq 0
, the sum

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(a,c)=\frac{1}{c}\sum_{r\in L/cL}G_{1}\left(\frac{ar}{c}\right)G_{1}\left(\frac{r}{c}\right).

He also proved that the sum above is nontrivial whenever
D\notin\{1,3\}
, while
\mathfrak{D}(a,c)=0
for all
a,c\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
with
ac\not=0
if
D=1
(Gauss integers) or
D=3
(Eisenstein integers). Moreover, for two coprime elements
a,c\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
, one has the following analogue of the reciprocity law in (
2
)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(a,c)+\mathfrak{D}(c,a)=2iG_{2}(0)\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{a}{c}+\frac{c}{a}+\frac{1}{ac}\right),

where
\operatorname{Im}(z)
denotes the imaginary part of
z
and
G_{2}(0)\neq 0
provided
D\notin\{1,3\}
. A further relevant analogy with the classical case is the existence of a function
H:\mathbb{H}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}
on the upper half space, transforming under
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
, whose transformation law is described by a relation analogous to (
1
). However, unlike the classical case, the construction of such a function is highly non trivial and postdates the work of Sczech, see
[
28
]
.

It is possible to show, see
[
30
, p. 200]
, that the normalized quantity

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(a,c):=\frac{1}{i\sqrt{|D|}G_{2}(0)}\mathfrak{D}(a,c),

is rational. Once again,
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
can be seen as a function of the quotient of the two arguments:
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}:K\backslash\{0\}\to K
by
\frac{a}{c}\to\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\frac{a}{c})
. Ito
[
30
]
proved an analogue of Hickerson’s density theorem: when
K
is Euclidean and the sum
\mathfrak{D}
is nontrivial (i.e.,
D\in\{2,7,11\}
), the set
\{(\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\frac{a}{c}),\frac{a}{c}):\frac{a}{c}\in K\}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
. This result was subsequently generalized to any imaginary quadratic field
K
with
D\notin\{1,3\}
by Bartel et al.
[
6
]
. Recently, an analogue of Vardi’s equidistributional result has been established by Klinger, Logan and Wong
[
32
]
, using spectral methods.
For the remainder of this paper, we restrict our attention to the class number one case, moreover, whenever not differently specified, we will assume
D\in\{2,7,11\}
. Consider the Farey set

(5)

\displaystyle K_{D}(X):=K(X):=\left\{\frac{a}{c}\in K:|c|^{2}<X\right\}.

Seeking to understand the distribution of
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
over the set
K_{D}(X)
, Ito
[
30
]
performed numerical experiments (for
D=2
) and formulated the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.2

(Ito)
.

Let
B_{2}:=\{z=x+iy:0\leq x<1,\ 0<y\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\}
. Then, for
D=2
,

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{1}{\#(K_{2}(X)\cap B_{2})}\sum_{\frac{a}{c}\in K_{2}(X)\cap B_{2}}\big|\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\left(a,c\right)\big|=\infty.

To put the above claim in perspective, recall that for classical Dedekind sums one can show (see
[
21
]
) that there are constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
with
C_{2}=2C_{1}
such that

\displaystyle(C_{1}+o(1))\log^{2}X\leq\frac{1}{\#\{\frac{h}{k}\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]:k<X\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{h}{k}\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\\
k<X\end{subarray}}|\mathfrak{s}(h,k)|\leq(C_{2}+o(1))\log^{2}X.

To the best of our knowledge, no asymptotic formula is known. The best result until now, from which the double average result above descends, is due to Girstmair and Schoissengeier
[
21
]
, who proved

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\log^{2}k+o(\log^{2}k)\leq\frac{4}{\varphi(k)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq h\leq k\\
\gcd(h,k)=1\end{subarray}}|\mathfrak{s}(h,k)|\leq\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\log^{2}k+o(\log^{2}k),

their key achievement being the lower bound, while the upper bound follows from Hickerson’s formula together with pre-existing results tracing back to Heilbronn
[
25
]
. Girstmair and Schoissengeier also conjectured that the correct asymptotic should match the upper bound, as discussed in the introduction of
[
21
]
. Prior to Girstmair and Schoissengeier, asymptotics for even moments of Dedekind sums were obtained by Conrey et al.
[
13
]
, who proved

(7)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq h\leq k\\
\gcd(h,k)=1\end{subarray}}|\mathfrak{s}(h,k)|^{2m}\sim C_{m}(k)k^{2m},

where
C_{m}(k)
is an Archimedean factor. This result has since seen several improvements concerning the error term; see for instance
[
48
]
. Very recently, a “shifted” version of Conrey’s result was obtained by Borda, Munsch and Shparlinski
[
11
]
, while for a generalized version see
[
10
, Theorem 3]
.

1.1.
Statement of results

For
D=2
, define

(8)

\displaystyle I_{2}:=\Bigl\{z=x+iy:|x|\leq\frac{1}{2},\ |y|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr\}.

For
D=7,11
, define

(9)

\displaystyle I_{D}:=\Bigl\{z=x+iy:|x|\leq\frac{1}{2},\ \bigl|y\pm x/\sqrt{D}\bigr|\leq\frac{D+1}{4\sqrt{D}}\Bigr\}.

Set

\displaystyle K_{\bullet,D}(X):=\Bigl\{z=\frac{a}{b}\in K:1\leq|b|^{2}<X\Bigr\}\cap I_{D},

and equip
K_{\bullet,D}(X)
with the uniform probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{X}
.
The goal of the present paper is to establish a limiting distribution
for suitably normalized Sczech sums
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(a,c)
.
The main result - namely
1.3
below - is an
analogue of
1.1
, with a remarkable difference: the
limiting distribution is
Gaussian
rather than
Cauchy
, and is also highlighted in
Figure

1
below.

Theorem 1.3

(The limiting distribution of Sczech sums)
.

Let
X
be sufficiently large and let
D\in\{2,7,11\}
. There is a constant
\kappa_{D}>0

such that, uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}\!\left(z\in K_{\bullet,D}(X):\frac{\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(z)}{\kappa_{D}\sqrt{\log X\log\log X}}<x\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-y^{2}/2}\,dy+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{(\log\log X)^{1-\epsilon}}\right).

As an immediate corollary we obtain the following result.

Corollary 1.4
.

1.2
holds true.

Remark 1.5
.

Although Ito’s conjecture follows as a corollary from our main theorem, it appears inaccessible via the methods of
[
13
,
21
]
. This contrasts sharply with the one-dimensional case, where elementary circle method arguments suffice to obtain lower bounds - and even asymptotics - of the correct order of magnitude. Indeed, in higher dimensions, the contribution from the Farey points - which carry the main contribution in the
\mathfrak{s}
case - is dominated by the error term. As the reader is encouraged to verify, a similar phenomenon also occurs when attempting to adapt the strategy of
[
13
]
to obtain an analogue of (
6
) for
|\mathfrak{s}(h,k)|^{1/2}
.

Remark 1.6
.

Evidence that the sums
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}
exhibit different behaviour than their classical counterpart was also conjecturally known to Ito. Indeed, he conjectured that the
restricted
average of these sums, namely

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#K_{2}(X)}\sum_{z\in K_{2}(X)\cap B_{2}}\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\left(z\right)

is bounded, while for the classical case one has

\displaystyle\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{\#\Omega_{Q}}\sum_{x\in\Omega_{Q}\cap[0,\frac{1}{2}]}\mathfrak{s}(x)=\infty.

Ito’s claim for the classical case has been established in a strong quantitative sense by the second named author, Sourmelidis and Technau in
[
35
]
and
[
36
]
.

1.2.
Overview

The proof of Vardi’s result
1.1
rests crucially on a link between Dedekind sums and Kloosterman sums. Thus, the spectral theory of automorphic forms comes into play in handling certain sums of Kloosterman sums (see also
[
23
]
). On the other hand, relatively recently,
1.1
was reproved, as the outcome of a remarkably general result, by Bettin and Drappeau
[
9
]
. Their approach — which is dynamical in nature — builds upon a well-established framework. We close this introductory section by presenting a minimal overview of this framework, which will also serve us in outlining the main steps in the proof of
1.3
. In doing so, we gambit generality for clarity.

1.2.1.
Quick overview of the literature

Let
\mathcal{T}
be the usual Gauss map and let
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a function of bounded variation that is not a coboundary, i.e is not of the form
g-g\circ\mathcal{T}+C
for some function of bounded variation
g
and some constant
C
. One can study the distribution of the quantity

\displaystyle S_{N}(f,x):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(\mathcal{T}^{j}(x)),

for
x
chosen uniformly in
[0,1]
. It is possible to prove (see
[
12
]
) that the quantity above has the following limiting distribution as
N\to\infty
: one has convergence towards a standard Gaussian distribution with an effective rate of convergence, in the following sense

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{S_{N}(f,x)-c(f)N}{\sigma_{f}\sqrt{N}}\leq y\right)=\Psi(y)+\mathcal{O}_{f}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

where

\displaystyle\Psi(y):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-t^{2}/2}\,dt,

is the cumulative distribution function of a standard Gaussian.

Turning now to the
rational
case, namely the case where
x\in\Omega_{N}
is sampled uniformly at random, one can consider the quantity

(10)

\displaystyle S(f,x):=\sum_{j=1}^{r(x)}f(\mathcal{T}^{j}(x)),

where
r(x)
is the smallest
r
such that
T^{r+1}(x)=0
. In other words
x=[0;a_{1},\dots,a_{r(x)}]
, where
a_{j}:=\lfloor 1/\mathcal{T}^{j-1}(x)\rfloor
for
1\leq j\leq r(x)
. In this context, pioneering work has been carried out by Baladi and Vallée
[
4
,
5
]
and Vallée
[
44
]
. Baladi and Vallée proved that if
f
is
of moderate growth
(cf.
[
4
]
), which essentially means
f(x)\ll\log(1/x)
, and
constant by parts
, that is constant on each interval
[\frac{1}{n+1},\frac{1}{n})
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, then a central limit theorem can be extracted.

Theorem 1.7

(Baladi and Vallée, cf.
[
4
, Theorem 4]
)
.

The quantity
S(f,x)
, suitably normalized, has Gaussian limiting distribution, that is, for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Q}\left(x\in\Omega_{Q}:\frac{S(f,x)-c(f)\log Q}{\sigma(f)\sqrt{\log Q}}\leq y\right)=\Phi(y)+\mathcal{O}_{f}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log Q}}\right),

where
c(f)
and
\sigma(f)>0
are explicit real constants.

It is worth mentioning that Hensley
[
26
]
obtained the above theorem for the special case
f=1
, with a suboptimal error term
O(1/\log^{1/24}Q)
. This amounts to showing that the number of steps in the classical Euclidean algorithm, after normalization, converges in distribution to a Gaussian.
1.7
above improves the error term to the optimal rate and extends the result to a wider class of division algorithms and functions
f
.

Bettin and Drappeau
[
9
]
extended the above result to a wider class of functions. First, they allowed a higher level of complexity in (
10
): let
(f_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}
be piecewise
C^{1}
functions
[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
. Define the
period law

\displaystyle\bm{f}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}(\mathcal{T}^{j-1}(x))

and the quantity

\displaystyle S(\bm{f},x):=\sum_{j=1}^{r(x)}f_{j}(\mathcal{T}^{j-1}(x)),

where the indices of
f_{j}
are read modulo
k
. They obtained distributional results for
S(\bm{f},x)
for functions
(f_{j})_{1\leq j\leq k}
which are not necessarily constant by parts, and also not necessarily log-bounded. In particular, they proved that if two technical conditions are satisfied
[
9
, see conditions (3.7) and (3.8)]
, then one can extract an asymptotic expansion for the characteristic function of
S(\bm{f},\cdot)
near
0
. The limiting distribution is then characterized in terms of
\bm{f}
, see
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
. This allowed them to consider quantities such as the sum of
\lambda
-th powers (
\lambda>0
) of the partial quotients

\displaystyle x\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{r(x)}a_{j}^{\lambda},

which corresponds to the selection
k=1
and
f_{1}(x)=\lfloor 1/x\rfloor^{\lambda}
, as well as Dedekind sums, which correspond to the selection
f_{1}(x)=\lfloor 1/x\rfloor
,
f_{2}(x)=-f_{1}(x)
,
k=2
. In particular, they recovered
1.1
with the improved exponent
1-\epsilon
in the error term.

Remark 1.8
.

The results of Bettin–Drappeau
[
9
]
are more general
than stated above: the authors work with functions
f
in Hölder spaces, allowing them to treat, for instance, the distribution of modular symbols associated with level
1
, weight
k\geq 12
cusp forms, recovering special cases of a theorem of Nordentoft
[
39
]
as well as a result of Constantinescu
[
16
, Theorem 1.4]
. Their result was later employed by Drappeau and Nordentoft
[
19
]
for studying the distribution of central values of additive twists of Maaß forms; see also
[
15
, Section 3]
for a more detailed exposition of the results in
[
9
]
in relation with additive twists of
L
-series. In a similar direction, we mention the result of Lee and Sun
[
34
]
, who reproved the main result of Petridis and Risager
[
40
]
concerning the distribution modular symbols. Their approach, unlike the one originally employed in
[
40
]
, is dynamical. Moreover, it was claimed in
[
31
, Remark 1.2]
, that dynamical methods like those used in
[
31
,
34
]
can be used to study the distribution of Bianchi modular symbols in the hyperbolic 3-space, recovering the results of Constantinescu and Nordentoft
[
14
]
.

Recently, Kim, Lee and Lim considered the continued fraction expansion of a complex number
z
with
\mathbb{Z}
replaced by the ring of integers of a Euclidean imaginary quadratic number field, i.e.
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-D})
for
D\in\{1,2,3,7,11\}
. Given
z\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-D})\cap I_{D}
, we have
z=[0;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell(z)}]
for some integer
\ell(z)\geq 1
, where we restrict to
D\in\{2,7,11\}
for contextual reasons. Let
g:\mathcal{O}_{D}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a function satisfying
g(\alpha)\ll\log|\alpha|
, which is precisely the
moderate growth
condition of Baladi and Vallée. Define

S(g,z):=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(z)}g(\alpha_{j}),

for
z\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-D})\cap I_{D}
. They proved the following theorem.

Theorem 1.9

(Number of steps in the complex Euclidean algorithm, cf.
[
31
, Theorem B]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{X}
denote the uniform probability measure on
K_{\bullet,D}(X)
. There are explicitly computable positive real constants
c(g)
and
\sigma(g)
such that for any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}\!\left(z\in K_{\bullet,D}(X):\frac{S(g,z)-c(g)\log X}{\sigma(g)\sqrt{\log X}}<x\right)=\Phi(x)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log X}}\right).

1.2.2.
Overview of the techniques

All three results cited above, namely
1.7
,
1.9
, and
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
, exploit the connection between the characteristic function of
x\mapsto S(f,x)
-the expectation being taken with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{X}
or
\mathbb{P}_{Q}
-and a Dirichlet series, which in turn can be realized as a special instance of a two-parameter family of operators acting on a selected indicator function. To give a rough sketch, let
Y
be some Banach space of functions over
[0,1]
(as in
[
4
,
9
]
) or over
I_{D}
(as in
[
31
]
and in our setting). Define the operator
Y\to Y
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}f(z):=\sum_{z_{0}:\,\mathcal{G}(z_{0})=z}\frac{1}{J_{\mathcal{G}}(z_{0})}f(z_{0}),

where
\mathcal{G}
is defined as in (
12
) and
J_{\mathcal{G}}
denotes its Jacobian. One can then define a two-parameter family of operators
\mathscr{L}_{s,w}
, where
(s,w)
lies in some open subset of
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}
and with
\mathscr{L}_{1,0}=\mathscr{L}
. Here and in the sequel, unless stated otherwise, we assume
w
to be real and denote it by
t
. This family of operators allows one to link the characteristic function of
S(g,z)
, namely
\chi_{S}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{X}[e^{itS(z)}]
, with a Dirichlet series

\displaystyle H(s,t)=\sum_{v\in K}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{h}(v)^{2s}}e^{itS(v)},

where for
v=\frac{a}{b}\neq 0
,
v\in K_{\bullet,D}
, we set
\mathfrak{h}(v)=|b|
.
Roughly, one can reduce the understanding of
\chi_{S}(t)
to that of the integral

\displaystyle\int_{\sigma_{0}-i\infty}^{\sigma_{0}+i\infty}X^{2s}H(2s,t)\,ds,

where
\sigma_{0}
is chosen so that
H(2s,t)
converges absolutely, via a standard contour integration. At this point, the rigid structure of
H(2s,t)
comes into play. It can be shown that

(11)

\displaystyle H(2s,t)=\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathscr{L}_{s,t}\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{A}_{s,t}[\mathbf{1}_{I_{D}}](0),

where
\mathfrak{A}_{s,t}
is another operator
Y\to Y
which is, for the moment, of secondary importance. It is easy to see that

is analytic in

. However, what is required here is a

\operatorname{Re}(s)>1-\delta
for some
\delta>0

and some

. For a general Dirichlet series, such a statement is typically completely out of reach unconditionally. However, the rigid structure (

for large

. This can be obtained by exploiting “decay of correlation” as in the work of Baladi and Vallee
[

]
, later adapted to the system
(I_{D},\mathcal{G})
by
[
31
]
. After this, one is left with an asymptotic expression for
\chi_{S}(t)
, whose behavior is encoded by the spectral properties of the right-hand side of (
11

1.2.3.
Our direction

The present paper sits at the intersection of
[
9
]
and
[
31
]
. The work of Kim, Lee and Lim will serve us in a fundamental way when defining the transfer operator. As we will explain in the upcoming sections, the system

Link with elliptic sums

Let us consider two functions
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:I_{D}\to\mathbb{R}
. We extend these by periodicity modulo

, that is

(15)

\displaystyle\psi_{j}:=\begin{cases}\psi_{1}&\text{if }j\text{ is odd,}\\
\psi_{2}&\text{if }j\text{ is even.}\end{cases}

For any given

define the quantity

(16)

\displaystyle S(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(z)}\psi_{j}(\mathcal{G}^{j-1}(z))

and the
period law

(17)

\displaystyle\Psi(z):=\psi_{1}(z)+\psi_{2}\bigl(\mathcal{G}(z)\bigr).

We now proceed to link the above with our elliptic sums. Let
a_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
and define a polynomial
P(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
of
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
by the relations

(18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&P(a_{0})=a_{0},\\
&P(a_{0},a_{1})=a_{0}a_{1}+1,\\
&P(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})=P(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m-1})a_{m}+P(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m-2}),\quad m\geq 2.\end{split}

It is known (see
[
30
]
) that

\displaystyle P(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=a_{0}P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})+P(a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}),

\displaystyle P(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=P(a_{n},a_{n-1},\ldots,a_{0}),

\displaystyle P(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})-P(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=(-1)^{n+1}.

Let now
a,c\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
be two algebraic integers,
c\neq 0
. Then, since the field is Euclidean, we have

\frac{a}{c}=\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{n}\rangle=\frac{P(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})}{P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})}.

The following lemma is an analogue of the Barkan–Hickerson expression for Dedekind sums (see e.g.
[
13
, Lemma 4]
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\frac{a}{c}=\langle a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
for

. Then

\mathfrak{D}(a,c)=G_{2}(0)\,I\Bigg(\frac{P(0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})}{P(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})}+(-1)^{n+1}\frac{P(0,a_{n},\ldots,a_{1})}{P(a_{n},\ldots,a_{1})}\\
+a_{1}-a_{2}+\cdots+(-1)^{n+1}a_{n}\Bigg),

where
I(z)=2i\operatorname{Im}(z)
and
G_{2}(0)\not=0
is the valued prescribed by the analytic continuation of the functions
G_{2}(z)
in (
4
).

Proof.

This is
[
30
, Theorem 3]
, where we dropped the condition
P(a_{m},\dots,a_{n})\not=0
for all
m\leq n
, as this is automatic in our case, see
[
30
, pp. 202-203]
.
∎

Due to the above lemma, we see that for any coprime pair, the normalized sum
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(a,c)
given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(a,c):=\frac{1}{i\sqrt{|D|}\,G_{2}(0)}\mathfrak{D}(a,c),

is real. Specify

\psi_{1}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{D}}\operatorname{Im}\left(\left[\frac{1}{z}\right]\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi_{2}(z)=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{D}}\operatorname{Im}\left(\left[\frac{1}{z}\right]\right),

and observe that

(19)

\displaystyle S\left(\frac{a}{c}\right)=\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\left(\frac{a}{c}\right)+\mathcal{O}(1).

Therefore, we are interested only in the period function

(20)

\displaystyle\Psi(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{|D|}}\operatorname{Im}\left(\left[\frac{1}{z}\right]-\left[\frac{1}{\mathcal{G}(z)}\right]\right).

We now equip the set
K_{\bullet,D}(X)
with the uniform probability measure, and denote by
\mathbb{E}_{X}
the corresponding expectation.
Let
t
be a real variable. The characteristic function of the map

\displaystyle\chi_{S,X}(t)=\chi_{S}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{X}[e^{itS(z)}]=\frac{1}{\#K_{\bullet,D}(X)}\sum_{z\in K_{\bullet,D}(X)}e^{itS(z)}.

Our goal is to obtain an asymptotic evaluation of the above characteristic function as

.

In order to simplify the exposition, we restrict ourselves to the explicit map in (

for a wider class of functions. For example,

(which relies on

We now describe the space of functions on which the transfer operator will act.

Definition 3.2

(Cf.

Let

be the set of functions from

The space above has a nicer representation as follows. We define the restriction map

as follows. First, for every

to be the restriction to
\bar{P}
of the extension of
(f\times 1_{P})
in an open neighborhood of

\mathscr{C}(\bar{P})
. The map

is then defined by

\operatorname{res}_{\mathscr{P}}:\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{P\in\mathscr{P}}\mathscr{C}(\bar{P}),\qquad f\longmapsto\bigl(\operatorname{res}_{P}(f)\bigr)_{P\in\mathscr{P}}.

which is a bijection, see
[

Recall the decomposition
\mathscr{P}=\cup_{i\in{0,1,2}}\mathscr{P}[i]
, where
\mathscr{P}[i]
is the collection of all open

[
31
]
we introduce some norms and semi-norms. Given any

, for

set

(25)

\displaystyle\|f_{P}\|_{a}:=\sup_{z\in P}|f(z)|.

Moreover, if

, define the semi-norm

(26)

\displaystyle\|f_{P}\|_{b}=\sup_{z\in P}\sup_{v}|\partial_{v}f(z)|,

where the innermost supremum being taken over the set of all unit tangent vectors for the directional derivative. We set
\|f_{P}\|_{b}=0
if
P
is a singleton. For any parameter

consider

\displaystyle\|f_{P}\|_{c(t)}=\|f_{P}\|_{c}:=\|f_{P}\|_{a}+\frac{1}{t}\|f_{P}\|_{b},

where we will sometimes omit the dependency on

To define a norm over the space

, define

Finally, given any linear operator

we will abuse notation and denote

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\star}=\sup_{\|f\|_{\star}=1}\|Af\|_{\star},

where

. It is possible to show that for any nonzero

Proposition 3.3

The space
(\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P}),\|\|_{c(1)})

Proof.

This is
[

.

Let now

and

and

\displaystyle g_{s,t,j}(z):=e^{it\psi_{j}(z)}J_{[1/z]}(\mathcal{G}(z))^{s},

Then we define the following operators

(27)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{s,t}^{(j)}[f](z)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{K}}(g_{s,t,j}f)\circ h_{\alpha}(z)\cdot 1_{\mathcal{G}O_{\alpha}}(z).

Recall that by
3.1
and
3.2
, the characteristic function of
\mathcal{G}O_{\alpha}
is in
\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})
for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\backslash\{\pm 1\}
. Then, for
z\in P
, these operators have the representation

(28)

\displaystyle(\mathscr{L}_{s,t}^{(j)}[f](z))_{P}=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle\in B(P,Q)}e^{it(\psi_{j}\circ\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle(z))}J_{\bm{\alpha}}(z)^{s}(f_{Q}\circ\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle(z)),

for
j\in\{1,2\}.
We then define the composition of the two operators above:

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{s,t}[f](z):=\left(\mathscr{L}_{s,t}^{(2)}\circ\mathscr{L}_{s,t}^{(1)}\right)[f](z).

Specify the following operator

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}[f](x):=\mathscr{L}_{1,0}[f](x),

which will play a crucial role in the subsequent chapters. Notice that any fixed
P\in\mathscr{P}
and
z\in P
, we have the explicit representation

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{1,0}[f](z):=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle\in B(P,Q)}|h_{\alpha}^{\prime}(z)|^{2}(f_{Q}\circ\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle)(z).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1/2
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
and let
n\geq 1
be an integer. Then the operators
\mathscr{L}_{s,t}^{(j)}
and
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
are well defined linear operators acting on
\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})
. Moreover, for any fixed
P\in\mathscr{P}
and any
z\in P
, we have the explicit representation of the powers of the operator
\mathscr{L}
as

\displaystyle(\mathscr{L}^{n}[f](z))_{P}=\sum_{Q}\sum_{\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle\in B^{n}(P,Q)}{J_{\bm{\alpha}}(z)}(f_{Q}\circ\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle(z)).

Proof.

Given a fixed
P\in\mathscr{P}
, to prove the convergence of the series in (
28
) notice that for any
t
and any
s
with
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1/2
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle\in B(P,Q)}|h_{\alpha}^{\prime}(z)|^{2s}\ll\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{K}}\frac{1}{|z+\alpha|^{4s}},

which is finite. Since the partition has only finitely many parts, this holds globally. Let
P\in\mathscr{P}
be a part, and let
\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle\in B(P,Q)
. For any
Q\in\mathscr{P}
, one has
f_{Q}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})
.
For
j=1
and any
\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle\in B(P,Q)
we have

\exp\left(it(\psi_{j}\circ\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle(z))\right)=\exp\left(it\frac{2}{\sqrt{D}}\operatorname{Im}\left(\alpha\right)\right).

This follows by the fact that, for a depth 1 inverse branch
h_{\alpha}
, we have
\left[\frac{1}{h_{\alpha}(z)}\right]=\alpha
for any
z\in I_{D}
. Whence, the exponential factor does not depend upon
z
, but only on
\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle
. The same holds also for
j=2
. Therefore, these operators are well defined.
The last assertion follows at once by standard computations and the definition of
B^{n}(P,Q)
(in particular, this follows immediately if one notices that our
\mathscr{L}
matches the operator
\mathscr{L}_{s,t}
with
s=1,w=0
in
[
31
]
).
∎

Our next goal is to express the two parameter family of operators
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
as a perturbation of the operator
\mathscr{L}
. This is a standard practice in the application of the thermodinamical formalism to distributional problems (see
[
12
]
) and will be crucial in our analysis. Define the factor

(29)

\displaystyle g_{s,t}(z):=e^{it\Psi(z)}T^{s-1}(z),

where
\Psi
is as in (
20
) and
T
is defined as
T(z)=|z\mathcal{G}(z)|^{4}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the operator
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
can be rewritten as
f\mapsto\mathscr{L}^{2}[g_{s,t}f]
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})
and let
z\in P
for some
P\in\mathscr{P}
. By definition we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{s,t}^{(j)}[f](z)=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle\in B(P,Q)}e^{it(\psi_{j}\circ\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle(z))}\,|h_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}(z)|^{2s}\,(f_{Q}\circ\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle(z)).

Since for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1/2
the sum converges absolutely, we compute

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{s,t}[f](z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q^{\prime},Q\in\mathscr{P}}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\langle\bm{\alpha_{1}}\rangle\in B(P,Q^{\prime})\\
\langle\bm{\alpha_{2}}\rangle\in B(Q^{\prime},Q)\end{subarray}}(\star\star)\,|h^{\prime}_{\bm{\alpha_{1}}}(h_{\bm{\alpha_{2}}}(z))|^{2s}\,|h_{\bm{\alpha_{2}}}^{\prime}(z)|^{2s}\,\bigl(f_{Q^{\prime}}\circ\langle\bm{\alpha_{1}}\rangle\circ\langle\bm{\alpha_{2}}\rangle(z)\bigr),

where

(\star\star)=\exp\Bigl(it\bigl[(\psi_{2}\circ\bm{\alpha_{2}})(z)+(\psi_{1}\circ\bm{\alpha_{1}}\circ\bm{\alpha_{2}})(z)\bigr]\Bigr).

Write
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
, so that
\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle=(h_{\alpha_{1}}\circ h_{\alpha_{2}})_{P}
.
Notice that
\left[\frac{1}{\bullet}\right]\circ h_{\alpha_{2}}(z)=\alpha_{2}
trivially.
On the other hand, for any
z\in P
, we have
h_{\alpha_{2}}(z)\in Q^{\prime}\subset I_{D}
.
Therefore,
\left[\frac{1}{\bullet}\right]\circ h_{\alpha_{1}}\circ h_{\alpha_{2}}(z)=\alpha_{1}

for any
z\in P
. Now, using the chain rule, the definition of
B^{n}(P,Q)
(
17
)
and (
20
), we have

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathscr{K}_{s,t}[f](z)\bigr)_{P}=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle\in B^{2}(P,Q)}e^{it\Psi\circ\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle(z)}\,|h_{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}(z)|^{2s}\,\bigl(f_{Q}\circ\bm{\alpha}(z)\bigr),

which coincides with
\mathscr{L}^{2}[g_{s,t}f]
, since
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and

T\circ\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle(z)=|\alpha_{1}\circ\alpha_{2}(z)|^{4}|\alpha_{2}(z)|^{4}=J_{\alpha_{1}}(\alpha_{2}(z))J_{\alpha_{2}}(z).

∎

Remark 4.3
.

We remark here a subtle issue. In the above we used the fact that

\left[\frac{1}{\bullet}\right]\circ h_{\alpha_{1}}\circ h_{\alpha_{2}}(z)=\alpha_{1},

for
z\in P
. This does
not
hold true for all
\alpha
if we do not restrict
the inverse branch
h_{\alpha_{2}}
to
P
. Indeed, there are finitely many
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
, like those in
Table

1
, such that

h_{\alpha}(I)\not\subset I_{D}
, and therefore, it can happen that for some
\beta\neq\alpha_{1}

we have
\bigl[\frac{1}{\bullet}\bigr]\circ h_{\alpha_{1}}\circ h_{\alpha_{2}}(z)=\beta
.

We also point out a key feature that will significantly ease our exposition later:
given any inverse branch
\langle\bm{\alpha}\rangle=(h_{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})})_{P}\in B^{2}(P,Q)
, the composition

\exp\!\bigl(it(\Psi\circ h_{\bm{\alpha}})(z)\bigr)=\exp\!\Bigl(it\bigl[(\psi_{1}\circ h_{\bm{\alpha}})(z)+(\psi_{2}\circ\mathcal{G}\circ h_{\bm{\alpha}})(z)\bigr]\Bigr)=\exp\!\Bigl(it\,\frac{2}{\sqrt{D}}\,\operatorname{Im}(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})\Bigr)

depends only on
\bm{\alpha}
, not on
z\in P
.

As we will see in
Section

5
, the point
\mathscr{K}_{1,0}
exhibits special spectral properties (cf.
5.1
), and the same holds for all points in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\mathscr{K}_{1,0}
. The next lemma provides continuous bounds for the two-parameter family
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
. This result is a straightforward adaptation of
[
9
, Lemma 5.3]
to our setting. However, given its frequent use in the subsequent chapters, we include a sketch of its proof for completeness.

Lemma 4.4

(Continuous bounds for the perturbation)
.

Let
\delta
be sufficiently small. There is a sufficiently small
t_{0}>0
such that the following estimates hold:

•

For
\sigma\geq 1-\delta
and
|t|\leq t_{0}
, we have

(30)

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{K}_{s,t}-\mathscr{K}_{s,0}\|_{a}\ll_{\delta}|t|.

•

For
1-\delta<\sigma\leq 1
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(31)

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{K}_{\sigma+i\tau,0}-\mathscr{K}_{1+i\tau,0}\|_{a}\ll|\sigma-1|.

•

Given
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(32)

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{K}_{1+i\tau_{1},0}-\mathscr{K}_{1+i\tau_{2},0}\|_{a}\ll|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|.

Proof.

Let
z\in P
with
P\in\mathscr{P}
. By the bounded distortion property (cf.
[
31
, Proposition 2.4 and Proposition 3.10]
), for any
z\in I_{D}
we have
|J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)|\asymp|J_{\bm{\alpha}}(z)|=|J_{\alpha_{1}}(0)|\,|J_{\alpha_{2}}(0)|
uniformly on all
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in B^{2}(P,Q)
and all
P,Q
. Therefore, we estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{K}_{s,t}-\mathscr{K}_{s,0}\|_{a}

\displaystyle\ll\sup_{\|f\|_{a}=1}\frac{\|\mathscr{L}^{2}[(g_{s,t}-g_{s,0})f]\|_{a}}{\|f\|_{a}}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in B^{2}(P,Q)}|J_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(0)|^{\sigma}\|\exp(it\Psi(\bm{\alpha}(z)))-1\|_{a}.

Since
z
is contained in the bounded domain
I_{D}
, we have

\exp(it\Psi(\bm{\alpha}(z)))-1\ll|t|\sup_{z\in P}|\Psi\circ\bm{\alpha}(z)|.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{K}_{s,t}-\mathscr{K}_{s,0}\|_{a}

\displaystyle\ll|t|\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in B^{2}(P,Q)}|J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)|^{\sigma}\sup_{z\in P}|\Psi\circ\bm{\alpha}(z)|.

From the definition of
\Psi
we see that

\displaystyle\|\Psi|_{\bm{\alpha}(P)}\|_{a}

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi_{1}|_{\alpha(P)}\|_{a}+\|(\psi_{2}\circ\mathcal{G})|_{\alpha(P)}\|_{a}

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha_{1}|+|\alpha_{2}|.

Therefore, the innermost sum is

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\bm{\alpha_{1}},\bm{\alpha_{2}}\in B(P,Q)}|J_{\bm{\alpha_{1}}}(0)J_{\bm{\alpha_{2}}}(0)|^{\sigma}\bigl(|\alpha_{1}|+|\alpha_{2}|\bigr)\ll\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}}\frac{1}{|\alpha^{\prime}|^{3\sigma}}.

This proves (
30
). Turning to (
31
), we proceed similarly and obtain analogous bounds. We may assume without loss of generality that
\sigma\leq 1
.

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{K}_{\sigma+i\tau,0}-\mathscr{K}_{1+i\tau,0}\|_{a}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in B^{2}(P,Q)}J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)\|(g_{\sigma+i\tau,0}-g_{1+i\tau,0})\circ\bm{\alpha}(z)\|_{a}

\displaystyle\ll|\sigma-1|\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in B^{2}(P,Q)}J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)\bigl(1+\log J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)\bigr)

\displaystyle\ll|\sigma-1|\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}}\frac{1}{(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})^{5}}\bigl(1+\log|\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}|\bigr).

Here we used the bounded distortion property again. The last expression converges. Finally, for (
32
), restricting to
P
, we have:

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{K}_{1+i\tau_{1},0}-\mathscr{K}_{1+i\tau_{2},0}\|_{a}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in B^{2}(P,Q)}J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)\|J_{\bm{\alpha}}(z)^{i\tau_{1}}-J_{\bm{\alpha}}(z)^{i\tau_{2}}\|_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in B^{2}(P,Q)}J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)\|J_{\bm{\alpha}}(z)^{i(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})}-1\|_{a}

\displaystyle\ll|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|\sum_{P\in\mathscr{P}}\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}\in B^{2}(P,Q)}J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)\bigl(1+\log|J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)|\bigr)

\displaystyle\ll|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|,

where we again used the bounded distortion property.
∎

4.1.
The generating series

Let now
v\in K\cap I_{D}
. We have
v=\frac{a}{b}
for some
a,b\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
and we set an height on
K
by
\mathfrak{h}:K\to\mathbb{R}
by
\mathfrak{h}(0)=0
and
\mathfrak{h}(v)=|b|
otherwise. We then form the series

(33)

\displaystyle H(s,t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in K\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{h}(v)^{2s}}e^{itS(v)}.

Now, for any
v=\frac{a}{b}\in K
, we have
v=\langle 0,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell(v)}\rangle
for some
\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
. A simple computation shows that

(34)

\displaystyle v=h_{\alpha_{1}}\circ\dots\circ h_{\ell(v)}(0)=h_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(0),

with
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell(v)})
. The following lemma relates the height of
v
to the Jacobian of the (restriction of) the branch induced by
\bm{\alpha}
. We recall that the origin 0 belongs to
\mathscr{P}[0]
, and is therefore a legitimate cell of the partition (see also
[
31
, Section 8]
).

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
{\bm{\alpha}}\in B^{n}(0,Q)
and let
v=h_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)
and
v=\frac{a}{b}
. Then we have
J_{\bm{\alpha}}(0)^{s}=|b|^{4s}
.

Proof.

See
[
31
, Lemma 8.1]
.
∎

For
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
, define the series

(35)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{I}+\mathscr{L}_{s,t}^{(1)}\right)\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathscr{K}_{s,t}\right)^{-1}[1](0),

where
1
here denotes the indicator function of
I_{D}
, which is in
\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})
. By the same computations as in
[
9
,
31
,
38
,
4
]
we find that for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
, we have the equality

(36)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{I}+\mathscr{L}_{s,t}^{(1)}\right)\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathscr{K}_{s,t}\right)^{-1}[1](0)=H(2s,t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{t,n}}{n^{2s}},

where

\displaystyle a_{n,t}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in K_{\bullet,D}\\
\mathfrak{h}(v)^{2}=n\end{subarray}}e^{itS(v)}.

Remark 4.6
.

The Dirichlet series in (
36
) converges absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
. This can be seen by noticing that
|a_{n,w}|\ll n\times r_{D}(n)
, where
r_{D}(n)
is the number of representations of

as

, where here (depending of

can be half integers. So we get

.

5.
Meromorphic continuation for the generating series

In this section, as is customary in this type of approach (see
[
4
,
9
,
31
]
), our goal is to obtain the analytic continuation of
H(2s,t)
as well as sufficient decay in
\tau
, where
s=\sigma+i\tau
, in a strip
\sigma>1-\delta
for some
\delta>0
. The argument splits into two main parts.
The first part establishes the continuation for small values of
|\tau|
and any
|t|\leq t_{0}
(for some
t_{0}>0
). This requires only perturbation theory of operators and the continuity of the family
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
with respect to the norms introduced previously.
The second part, where a pointwise estimate for large values of
\tau
is established, requires more work. In particular, one employs ideas of Dolgopyat
[
18
]
, which were subsequently adapted to the number-theoretic setting by Baladi and Vallée
[
4
]
.

We recall that for the space
\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})
, one has the decomposition (see (
24
))

\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})=\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P}[0])\cup\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P}[1])\cup\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P}[2]).

We begin with the following lemma, which states that the operator
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
is quasi-compact for
(s,t)
in a neighborhood of
(1,0)
.

Proposition 5.1

(A Ruelle-Perron-Frobenius theorem for
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
)
.

There exist parameters
\delta,\tau_{0},t_{0}>0
such that, for all
(s,t)
satisfying

\lvert 1-\sigma\rvert\leq\delta
,
\lvert\tau\rvert\leq\tau_{0}
, and
\lvert t\rvert\leq t_{0}
,
the operator
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
is quasi-compact on the space
\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})
.
Within this region, there exist a simple, dominant eigenvalue
\lambda(s,t)
,
a rank-one operator
\mathscr{P}_{s,t}
, and an operator
\mathscr{N}_{s,t}
such that

\mathscr{K}_{s,t}=\lambda(s,t)\,\mathscr{P}_{s,t}+\mathscr{N}_{s,t}.

Moreover, the orthogonality relations

\mathscr{N}_{s,t}\mathscr{P}_{s,t}=\mathscr{P}_{s,t}\mathscr{N}_{s,t}=0

hold. The operator
\mathscr{P}_{s,t}
is the projection onto the one-dimensional
eigenspace associated with
\lambda(s,t)
, and the residual spectral radius satisfies

\varrho_{s,t}=rad(\mathscr{N}_{s,t})<\lvert\lambda(s,t)\rvert-\epsilon,

for some
\epsilon=\epsilon(\delta,t_{0},\tau_{0})>0
depending only on
\delta,\tau_{0},t_{0}.
In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{s,t}^{n}=\lambda(s,t)^{n}\mathscr{P}_{s,t}^{n}+\mathscr{N}^{n}_{s,t}.

Proof.

By
[
31
, Theorem 4.8]
, the operator
\mathscr{K}_{1,0}=\mathscr{L}^{2}
is quasi compact. Then, the result above follows using
[
33
, Theorem 2.6]
.
∎

We also require some further properties of the eigenfunctions and eigenmeasures associated with
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
. We have the following analogue of
[
31
, Theorem 4.8]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(\delta,\tau_{0},t_{0})
be as in
5.1
. Then, for the operator
\mathscr{K}_{\sigma,t}
:

•

The eigenfunction
\psi_{\sigma,t}=(\psi_{\sigma,t,2},\psi_{\sigma,t,1},\psi_{\sigma,t,0})
is positive, in the sense that
\psi_{\sigma,t,j}>0
for all
j\in\{0,1,2\}
.

•

The associated dual operator
\mathscr{K}^{\star}_{\sigma,t}
has a unique eigenmeasure
\nu_{\sigma,t}=(\nu_{\sigma,t,2},\nu_{\sigma,t,1},\nu_{\sigma,t,0})
satisfying
\mathscr{K}_{\sigma,t}^{\star}\nu_{\sigma,t}=\lambda(\sigma,t)\,\nu_{\sigma,t}
. In particular,
\nu_{1,0,2}
agrees with the usual two-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

This follows along the same lines of
[
31
, Theorem 4.8]
.
∎

From now on, in virtue of the above propositions, for
(s,t)
in a neighborhood of
(1,0)
, we will call the function
(s,t)\to\lambda(s,t)
the
leading eigenvalue
. With this at hand we can state the main result of this section

Proposition 5.3

(The meromorphic continuation of
H(2s,t)
)
.

There are parameters
\delta,\tau_{0},t_{0}>0
such that for all
|t|\leq t_{0}
, the function
s\mapsto H(s,t)
has a meromorphic continuation to the right half plane

(37)

\displaystyle S_{\delta}=\{s\in\mathbb{C},\;s=\sigma+i\tau,\;\sigma\geq 1-\delta\}.

Possible poles are located in the rectangular region delimited by
|\tau|\leq\tau_{0}
and satisfy the condition
\lambda(s,t)=1
. Moreover, the function

(38)

\displaystyle s\mapsto H(2s,t)-\frac{\lambda(s,t)}{1-\lambda(s,t)}\left(\mathbb{I}+\mathscr{L}_{s,t}^{(1)}\right)\mathscr{P}_{s,t}[1](0)

is holomorphic in
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1-\delta
,
|\tau|\leq\tau_{0}
. Additionally, the function
s\mapsto H(2s,t)
is bounded by

\displaystyle H(2s,t)\ll|\tau|^{C|\sigma-1|}

in the strip
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1-\delta,|\tau|>\tau_{0}
, the constant
C
depending only over
(\delta,\tau_{0},t_{0}).

5.1
almost suffices to prove the first part of the statement. The extra ingredient required is some control for the norm

for
\tau\in[\tau_{0},\tau_{1}]
and all

and
4.4
.

The proof follows in the same lines as

5.2.
Bounds for the large values of
\tau

Let us normalize the operator
\mathscr{K}

(\tilde{\mathscr{K}}_{s,t}[f])_{P}=\frac{(\mathscr{K}_{s,t}[\psi_{\sigma,t}f])_{P}}{\lambda(\sigma,t)(\psi_{\sigma,t})_{P}}.

Let
\tilde{\varrho}_{\sigma,t}

(39)

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma,t}:=\psi_{\sigma,t}\nu_{\sigma,t}.

The goal of this section is to establish the following bound.

Proposition 5.6

(Dolgopyat-type estimate)

Let
\delta,t_{0}>0
be sufficiently small. Then there exist constants
C,\tau_{1}>0
and a sufficiently small positive constant
\beta
such that for any

,

\bigl\|(I-\mathscr{K}_{s,t})^{-1}\bigr\|_{c(\tau)}\ll|\tau|^{\beta},

for any

with
|\sigma-1|\leq\delta
,
|\tau|\geq\tau_{1}
and
|t|\leq t_{0}
.

The key step in proving
5.6
is to establish the following inequality.

Proposition 5.7

(
L^{2}
bound)
.

Let
\delta,t_{0}
be as in
5.6
, let

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{1,0}=\mathscr{P}_{1,0}+\mathscr{N}_{1,0}\,,\quad\mathscr{P}_{1,0}\mathscr{N}_{1,0}=\mathscr{N}_{1,0}\mathscr{P}_{1,0}=0,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{I}+\mathscr{L}_{s_{0}(t),t}^{(1)}\right)\mathscr{P}_{s_{0}(t),t}[1](0)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{P}_{1,0}[1](0)+\mathscr{P}_{1,0}^{2}[1](0)+\mathcal{O}\bigl(|t|\bigr)

\displaystyle=2\mathscr{P}_{1,0}[1](0)+\mathcal{O}\bigl(|t|\bigr).

Now, set
C=2\mathscr{P}_{1,0}[1](0)
, and notice that this, being an integral of a positive function with respect to the measure
\mu
, is positive. Notice now that for
\delta,t_{0}
sufficiently small, we have

\displaystyle\hat{f}(2s_{0})=\frac{1}{2s_{0}}+\int_{1}^{1+\frac{1}{W^{4}}}f(x)x^{2s_{0}(t)-1}dx=\frac{1}{2s_{0}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{W^{4}}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{W^{4}}+|t|)\right).

Notice now that
\sharp K_{\bullet,D}(X)=C^{\prime}X^{2}+\mathcal{O}(X)
and that by smoothing properties above, we have

A(t)=\sum_{v\in K_{\bullet,D}(X)}e^{itS(v)}+\mathcal{O}\left(X^{1+\epsilon}\left(1+\frac{X}{W^{4}}\right)\right).

Selecting now
W=X^{\frac{2\delta}{4k+1}}
, using the expression for
\partial_{s}\lambda(s,t)
from
6.1
and normalizing, we get

\displaystyle\chi_{X}(t)=X^{2s_{0}(t)-2}\biggl(1+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\frac{1}{X^{\delta}}+|t|+|2s_{0}(t)-2|\bigr)\biggr).

The result follows by taking
\delta,t_{0}
small enough.
∎

We now aim to extract an asymptotic for
s_{0}(t)
at small
t
. We prove the following result which gives a first step in this direction and reduces the problem to estimating an oscillatory integral.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
|t|\leq t_{0}
for some sufficiently small
t_{0}>0
. Then the following asymptotic holds:

\displaystyle s_{0}(t)-1=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{P\in P[2]}\int_{P}(1-e^{it\Psi(z)})\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)\,dx\,dy+\mathcal{O}\left(t^{2}\right),

where

\displaystyle A=\sum_{P\in P[2]}\int_{P}\log T(z)\,\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)\,dx\,dy>0.

Proof.

Recall the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})=\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P}[0])\oplus\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P}[1])\oplus\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P}[2])

of the space of functions on which the family
\mathscr{K}_{s,t}
acts (see
[
31
, proof of Theorem 4.8]
). Let us denote by
\mathscr{L}^{\star}
the dual of the operator
\mathscr{L}
. It is possible to show (see
[
31
, Theorem 4.8]
) that to the eigenfunction
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{2},\phi_{1},\phi_{0})
- which is exactly
\psi_{1,0}=(\psi_{1,0,0},\psi_{1,0,1},\psi_{1,0,2})
from
5.1
with
\lambda(1,0)=1
- we have an associated eigenfunctional
\phi^{\star}:\mathscr{C}(\mathscr{P})\to\mathbb{C}
(essentially induced by the eigenmeasure in
5.2
). Explicitly, this functional is given by

\displaystyle f=(f_{0},f_{1},f_{2})\mapsto\phi^{\star}(f)=\sum_{P\in P[2]}\int_{P}f_{2}\,dx\,dy,

with
\mathscr{L}^{\star}\phi^{\star}=\phi^{\star}
. Now, recall that

g_{s,t}(z)
is an analytic function in
s
. We therefore have the expansion

\displaystyle g_{s,t}(z)=e^{it\Psi(z)}+(s-1)\left(\partial_{s}g_{s,t}(z)\right)_{s=1}+\mathcal{O}\left(|s-1|^{2}\right).

Now, by Theorem 2.6 in
[
33
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda(s,t)=\lambda(1,0)+(\phi^{\star}\circ(\mathscr{K}_{s,t}-\mathscr{L}^{2}))(\bm{\phi})+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\mathscr{K}_{s,t}-\mathscr{L}\|_{a}^{2}\right).

Since
\lambda(1,0)=1
, we see that
\phi^{\star}\circ\mathscr{L}^{2}(\bm{\phi})=\phi^{\star}(\bm{\phi})=1
, by normalization. Thus we have

\displaystyle\lambda(s,t)=(\phi^{\star}\circ\mathscr{K}_{s,t})(\bm{\phi})+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\mathscr{K}_{s,t}-\mathscr{L}^{2}\|_{a}^{2}\right).

By
4.2
and basic computations, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{s,t}(\bm{\phi})

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{2}(g_{s,t}\bm{\phi})

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{2}[e^{it\Psi(z)}\bm{\phi}]+(s-1)\mathscr{L}^{2}[\left(\partial_{s}g_{s,t}\right)\big|_{s=1}\bm{\phi}]+\mathcal{O}\left(|s-1|^{2}\|\bm{\phi}\|_{a}\right),

with

\displaystyle g_{s,t}(z)=e^{it\Psi(z)}\exp\left((s-1)\log T(z)\right).

Thus

\displaystyle(s-1)\mathscr{L}^{2}[\partial_{s}g_{s,t}\big|_{s=1}\bm{\phi}]

\displaystyle=(s-1)\mathscr{L}^{2}[\log T(z)e^{it\Psi(z)}\bm{\phi}]

\displaystyle=(s-1)\mathscr{L}^{2}[\log T(z)\bm{\phi}]+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\bm{\phi}\|_{a}|s-1||t|\right),

where in the last line we used that, by
4.4
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{2}[e^{it\Psi(z)}\bm{\phi}]

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}^{2}[\bm{\phi}]-\mathscr{L}^{2}[(1-e^{it\Psi(z)})\bm{\phi}]=\bm{\phi}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(|t|\right)\right).

This leads to

\displaystyle\lambda(s,t)

\displaystyle=\phi^{\star}\!\left(\mathscr{L}^{2}[e^{it\Psi(z)}\bm{\phi}]\right)+(s-1)A

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{O}\Bigl(|s-1|^{2}\|\bm{\phi}\|_{a}+\|\bm{\phi}\|_{a}|s-1||t|+\|\mathscr{K}_{s,t}-\mathscr{L}^{2}\|_{a}^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in P[2]}\int_{P}e^{it\Psi(z)}\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)\,dx\,dy+(s-1)A+\mathcal{O}\bigl(|s-1|^{2}+|t|^{2}\bigr).

Since
\lambda(s_{0}(t),t)=1
, we conclude by taking
s=s_{0}(t)
in the above.
∎

8.
Asymptotic expansion of oscillatory integrals

In view of
7.2
, we need to understand

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in P[2]}\int_{P}\bigl(e^{it\Psi(z)}-1\bigr)\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)\,dx\,dy

for small
t
, where we recall that the period function
\Psi
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{|D|}}\operatorname{Im}\left(\left[\frac{1}{z}\right]-\left[\frac{1}{\mathcal{G}(z)}\right]\right).

The goal of this section is to establish the following asymptotic expansion.

Proposition 8.1
.

As
t\to 0
, the following asymptotic expansion holds:

\displaystyle\int_{I_{D}}\bigl(e^{it\Psi(z)}-1\bigr)\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)\,dx\,dy=C_{D}t^{2}\ln|t|+C_{D}^{\prime}t^{2}+\mathcal{O}\bigl(t^{\frac{5}{2}}\bigr),

where
C_{D},C_{D}^{\prime}
are real constants, with
C_{D}>0
, depending on
D\in\{2,7,11\}.

The proof is given at the end of this section, after the necessary preparation.

8.1.
Some facts concerning the measure
\mu
and the partition
\mathscr{P}

Recall that the invariant measure
\mu
, whose density is
\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)
, is, by construction, a probability measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
Given
z\in I
, we define the set

(53)

\displaystyle V(z)^{\dagger}\;:=\;\overline{\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\Bigl\{-\frac{Q_{n}(z_{n})}{Q_{n-1}(z_{n})}\;\Big|\;z_{n}\in I,\;\mathcal{G}^{n}(z_{n})=z\Bigr\}}\;\cup\;\{\infty\},

where
Q_{n}(z)
denotes the denominator of the
n
-th convergent to
z
, and the overline denotes the closure of the set.
It can be proved (see
[
20
, Proposition 5.3]
) that if
z\in P
for some
P\in\mathscr{P}[2]
, then
V(z)^{\dagger}
depends only on the element
P\in\mathscr{P}
containing
z
, not on
z
itself.
Then, for
z\in P
, the density for the measure
\mu
is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)=\int_{P^{\dagger}}\frac{1}{|z-w|^{4}}\,dw.

where
P^{\dagger}=V^{\dagger}(z)
.
This measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and defines a probability measure on
I_{D}
(see
[
31
, Sections 3–4]
and
[
20
, Sections 4–5]
).

Let us now define the functions

(54)

\displaystyle(\psi_{R}(z),\psi_{I}(z))=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{1}{z}\right],\;\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\operatorname{Im}\left[\frac{1}{z}\right]\right),&\text{if }D=2,\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{1}{z}\right],\;\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{D}}\operatorname{Im}\left[\frac{1}{z}\right]\right),&\text{if }D=7\text{ or }D=11.\end{cases}

We aim to understand the sets of points
z
for which
\psi_{I}(z)=n
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Therefore, we define the level sets

(55)

\displaystyle V_{n}=\{z\in I_{2}:\psi_{I}(z)=n\},

and decompose them further as

(56)

\displaystyle V_{r,n}=\begin{cases}\{z\in I_{2}:\psi_{I}(z)=n,\ \psi_{R}(z)=r\}&n,r\in\mathbb{Z},\quad D=2,\\
\{z\in I_{2}:\psi_{I}(z)=n,\ \psi_{R}(z)=r+\frac{n}{2}\}&n,r\in\mathbb{Z},\quad D=7,11.\end{cases}

Observe that for any
(m,n)
and

, the interiors satisfy
\operatorname{Int}(V_{r,n}\cap V_{r^{\prime},m})=\emptyset
.
We now define

(57)

\displaystyle V^{+}_{n}=\bigcup_{r>0}V_{r,n},\qquad V^{-}_{n}=\bigcup_{r<0}V_{r,n}.

It is easy to see that

.

8.3.
Technical preliminaries

For our purposes, we aim to compute the quantity

\int_{I_{D}}\bigl(e^{it\psi(z)}-1\bigr)\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)\,dx\,dy=\sum_{P}\int_{P}\bigl(e^{it\psi(z)}-1\bigr)\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)\,dx\,dy,

for real-valued functions
\psi:I_{D}\to\mathbb{R}
. Following
[
8
]
, let
\eta\in[0,1]
be some small parameter. Define then the quantity

(58)

\displaystyle F(\eta,s):=\int_{I_{D}}w_{(\eta,s)}(z)\,d\mu(z)=\sum_{P}\int_{P}w_{\eta,s}(z)\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)\,dx\,dy,

where

\displaystyle w_{\eta,s}(z):=\mathbf{1}_{\psi(z)\neq 0}\,|\psi(z)|^{s}\exp\left(-s\frac{\pi i}{2}(1-\eta)\sgn(\psi(z))\right).

Furthermore, let
c>0
be a real number for which the integral

(59)

\displaystyle\int_{I_{D}}\bigl(|\psi(z)|^{c}+|\psi(z)|^{-c}\bigr)\mathbf{1}_{\psi(z)\neq 0}\,d\mu(z)

is finite. We call any such
c

admissible
.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
\psi=\psi_{I}
. Then any positive value
c<2
is admissible in (
59
).

Proof.

By definition, regardless of
D
,
\psi
takes only integer values. Let
|n|\geq n_{D}
for some sufficiently large
n_{D}
. The contribution of all
z\in I_{D}
for which
\psi(z)\leq n_{D}
is bounded (since
\mu(z)
is a probability density on
I_{D}
) and therefore does not affect the convergence of the sum. Assume now that
D=2
. For any
(r,n)
with
|n|>n_{2}
, the sets
V_{r,n}
are the image of
I_{2}
under the map
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z+\alpha}
for
\alpha=r+in\sqrt{2}
. Now, since
\mu(z)
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, we have

\mu(V_{r,n})\ll\operatorname{vol}(V_{r,n})=\int_{V_{r,n}}1\,dx\,dy=\int_{I_{2}}\frac{1}{|z+\alpha|^{4}}\,dx\,dy\ll\frac{1}{|\alpha|^{4}}=\frac{1}{(n^{2}+2r^{2})^{2}}.

Thus, we conclude

\int_{I_{2}}|\psi_{I}(z)|^{c}\,d\mu(z)\ll\sum_{|n|\geq n_{2}}|n|^{c}\,\mu(V_{n})\ll\sum_{n\geq n_{2}}\frac{1}{n^{1+\epsilon}}<\infty.

The proof in the cases
D=7,11
follows along the same lines.
∎

Let now
\psi
be a function
I_{D}\to\mathbb{R}
with some admissible
c>0
. Define, for
\epsilon\in[0,\eta^{\prime}]
,
\eta^{\prime}>0
, the quantities

\displaystyle J_{+}(\epsilon)=\int_{I_{+}}e^{(-\epsilon+i)t\psi(z)}\,d\mu(z),\qquad J_{-}(\epsilon)=\int_{I_{-}}e^{(\epsilon+i)t\psi(z)}\,d\mu(z),

where
I_{-}:=\{z\in I_{D}:\psi(z)<0\}
,
I_{0}:=\{z\in I_{D}:\psi_{I}(z)=0\}
and
I_{+}:=\{z\in I_{D}:\psi(z)>0\}
. Then we have the following technical proposition.

Proposition 8.6
.

Let
H(2,2+\delta)
be a Hankel contour (see e.g.
[
43
]
) around the point
2
, with thickness
\delta<1
, tracing around
2
clockwise, and let
F(\eta,s)
be given as in (
58
). Then, for
\psi=\psi_{I}
, we have

\displaystyle J_{+}(\varepsilon)+J_{-}(\varepsilon)+\mu(\{x\in I_{D}:\psi_{I}(x)=0\})

\displaystyle=1+F(\eta,1)t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{H(2,2+\delta)}\Gamma(-s)F(\eta,s)t^{s}|1+i\varepsilon|^{s}\,ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathrm{Re}(s)=2+\delta}\Gamma(-s)F(\eta,s)t^{s}|1+i\varepsilon|^{s}\,ds,

where
\eta=\arctan(\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon\leq\eta^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By
[
24
, Eq 17.43.1, Eq 8.327.1]
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-y}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-c/2-i\infty}^{-c/2+i\infty}\Gamma(-s)|y|^{s}e^{s\arg(y)}\,ds,

provided
\operatorname{Re}(y)>0
. With
y=t\psi(z)(i-\varepsilon)
, we get

\displaystyle J_{+}(\varepsilon)+J_{-}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-c/2-i\infty}^{-c/2+i\infty}\Gamma(-s)F(\eta,s)|1+i\varepsilon|^{s}t^{s}\,ds,

where
\eta=\arctan\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon\leq\eta_{0}
.
Now we shift the contour forward to
\mathrm{Re}(s)=2+\delta
, obtaining

(60)

\displaystyle J_{+}(\varepsilon)+J_{-}(\varepsilon)=-(R_{0}+R_{1})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{H(2,2+\delta)}\Gamma(-s)F(\eta,s)t^{s}|1+i\varepsilon|^{s}\,ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathrm{Re}(s)=2+\delta}\Gamma(-s)F(\eta,s)t^{s}|1+i\varepsilon|^{s}\,ds,

where
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
are the residues at
s=0
and
s=1
. Using the Taylor expansion at
s=0
,

\Gamma(-s)=-\frac{1}{s}-\gamma+\mathcal{O}(s),

we get

R_{0}=-\mu\bigl(\{z\in I:\psi_{I}(z)\neq 0\}\bigr).

Similarly, using the expansion at
s=1
,

\Gamma(-s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+(\gamma-1)+\mathcal{O}(s-1),

we see that the residue at
s=1
is given by
F(\eta,1)t
. Now, notice that since
\mu(I_{D})=1
, we have

\displaystyle J_{+}(\varepsilon)+J_{-}(\varepsilon)+\mu(\{x\in I_{D}:\psi_{I}(x)=0\})

\displaystyle=1+F(\eta,1)t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{H(2,2+\delta)}\Gamma(-s)F(\eta,s)t^{s}|1+i\varepsilon|^{s}\,ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathrm{Re}(s)=2+\delta}\Gamma(-s)F(\eta,s)t^{s}|1+i\varepsilon|^{s}\,ds,

as claimed.
∎

8.4.
Concluding the proof of Proposition
8.1

In order to evaluate the two integrals in
8.6
, we must understand the analytic continuation of
F(\eta,s)
. We show that
F(0,s)
has a simple pole at
s=2
. To prove this, we need more precise control on the measure
\mu
, as absolute continuity with respect to the Lebesgue measure is not sufficient.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
D\in\{2,7,11\}
be fixed and let
U\subset P
for
P\in\mathcal{P}[2]
. Assume that the diameter of the set
U
satisfies
\operatorname{diam}(U)<1/2
. Then we have

\mu(U)=\kappa_{P,D}\,\operatorname{vol}(U)\bigl(1+\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{diam}(U))\bigr),

where
\kappa_{P,D}=\int_{P^{\dagger}}\frac{1}{|w|^{4}}\,dw
is a constant depending only on
P
.

Proof.

Since
U\subset P
, we have

\mu(U)=\int_{U}1\cdot\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)\,dx\,dy,

where the density is given by
\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)=\int_{P^{\dagger}}\frac{1}{|z-w|^{4}}\,dw
. We now use Taylor expansion on the integrand
\frac{1}{|z-w|^{4}}
appearing in the density around
z=0
:

\frac{1}{|z-w|^{4}}=\frac{1}{|w|^{4}}+\frac{2}{|w|^{4}}\left(\frac{z}{w}+\frac{\bar{z}}{\bar{w}}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,m\geq 0\\
n+m\geq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{(n+1)(m+1)}{w^{n+2}\bar{w}^{m+2}}z^{n}\bar{z}^{m}.

Now, since

\frac{4}{|w|^{4}}\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{z}{w}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{|z|}{|w|^{5}}\right)

and

\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,m\geq 0\\
n+m\geq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{(n+1)(m+1)}{w^{n+2}\bar{w}^{m+2}}z^{n}\bar{z}^{m}\right|\ll\frac{1}{|w|^{4}}\sum_{r=2}^{\infty}(r+1)^{3}\left(\frac{|z|}{|w|}\right)^{r}\ll\frac{|z|^{2}}{|w|^{6}}\quad\text{for }|z|<|w|,

we obtain

\psi_{(1,0,2)}(z)=\int_{P^{\dagger}}\frac{1}{|w|^{4}}\bigl(1+\mathcal{O}(|z|)\bigr)\,dw=\kappa_{P}+\mathcal{O}(|z|).

Therefore,

\mu(U)=\int_{U}\bigl(\kappa_{P}+\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{diam}(U))\bigr)\,dx\,dy=\kappa_{P}\,\operatorname{vol}(U)\bigl(1+\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{diam}(U))\bigr).

∎

The following lemma gives us an asymptotic evaluation of the measure of the level sets
V_{n}
for the case
D=2
.

Lemma 8.8

(The measure of the level sets
V_{n}
)
.

Let
D\in\{2,7,11\}
and let
n\geq n_{D}
. Then, for the measure of the sets
V_{n}
in (

Lemma 8.10

(Asymptotics for

-sets in the

)
.

Let

, let

. Define the quantity

\displaystyle A(n)=\kappa_{P_{1},D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\mathbb{Z}\\

So we conclude that

\displaystyle A(n)=\frac{C_{D}}{n^{3}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{6}}\right),

.
∎
