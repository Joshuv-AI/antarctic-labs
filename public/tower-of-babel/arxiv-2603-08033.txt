The Unit Gap: How Sharing Works in Boolean Circuits
Kirill Krinkin

Abstract
We study the gap between the minimum size of a Boolean circuit (DAG) and the minimum size of a formula (tree circuit) over the And-Inverter Graph (AIG) basis { AND , NOT } \{\mathrm{AND},\mathrm{NOT}\} with free inversions. We prove that this gap is always 0 0 or 1 1 (Unit Gap Theorem), that sharing requires opt ⁡ ( f ) ≥ n \mathrm{opt}(f)\geq n essential variables (Threshold Theorem), and that no sharing is needed when opt ⁡ ( f ) ≤ 3 \mathrm{opt}(f)\leq 3 (Tree Theorem). Gate counts in optimal circuits satisfy an exact decomposition formula with a binary sharing term. When the gap equals 1 1 , it arises from exactly one gate with fan-out 2 2 , employing either dual-polarity or same-polarity reuse; we prove that no other sharing structure can produce a unit gap.

Introduction

A Boolean circuit over a basis
B
is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) whose
internal nodes compute functions in
B
. A formula is a circuit in which every
gate has fan-out one—a tree. Formulas cannot reuse intermediate results, so
they are potentially larger. The question of how much reuse (
sharing
)
helps has been central to circuit complexity since
Shannon
[
2
,
1
]
.

In general, formula size can be superpolynomially larger than circuit size: there
exist explicit functions with polynomial-size circuits whose formulas require
size
n^{\Omega(\log n)}

[
16
]
. Yet the fine structure of this
gap—which functions benefit from sharing, by how much, and through what
mechanisms—has been studied mainly in the asymptotic regime.

We study the gap for the
And-Inverter Graph
(AIG) basis, where
complementation is free (an edge attribute, not a gate) and size is measured by
the number of two-input AND gates. The AIG basis is not chosen for generality
but for relevance: it is the canonical internal representation used in modern
logic synthesis tools such as ABC
[
13
,
12
]
, where
circuit optimization proceeds by local rewriting of AIG structures. Our main
findings:

•

The gap between formula size and circuit size is always 0 or 1
(Theorem
2
).

•

Sharing requires
\mathrm{opt}(f)\geq n
essential variables
(Theorem
3
).

•

No sharing is needed when
\mathrm{opt}(f)\leq 3

(Theorem
4
).

•

Gate counts decompose exactly with a binary sharing term
(Corollary
6
).

•

When
\mathrm{gap}(f)=1
, sharing arises from exactly one gate via
dual-polarity or same-polarity reuse; no other structure can produce the unit
gap (Theorem
7
).

Together, these results give a complete structural characterization of sharing in
optimal AIG circuits.

The interest of the result is not the bound itself, but the structural
consequences: sharing is atomic, optimal circuits contain at most one
reconvergence per decomposition, and the improvement over trees is quantized.
The contribution of the paper is therefore structural rather than asymptotic: we
characterize how sharing appears in optimal AIG circuits.

These results are specific to the AIG basis with free inversions. The key
ingredient is the trivial decomposition
f=1\wedge f
, which relies on the
constant
1
being available at zero cost. Bases without free constants or with
different gate types (majority gates, XOR-AND) may exhibit larger gaps.

Section
2
introduces notation. Sections
3

through
5
prove the theorems. Section
6
analyzes
the sharing mechanisms. Section
7
discusses a tropical
interpretation. Section
8
lists open questions.
Appendix
A
describes a generative grammar, and
Appendix
B
discusses local rewriting dynamics.

All results are specific to the AIG basis with free inversions; in general
bases, the formula-circuit gap can be superpolynomial
[
16
]
.

Preliminaries

And-Inverter Graphs.

An
And-Inverter Graph
(AIG) over primary inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
the constant
1
is a DAG whose internal nodes are two-input AND gates. Each
edge may carry a complementation attribute. Complementation is free. The

size
of an AIG is the number of AND gates; we write
\mathrm{opt}(f)
for
the minimum size of any AIG computing
f
.

Circuits and formulas.

A
circuit
is an AIG where gates may have fan-out greater than one.
A
formula
(or
tree circuit
) has fan-out exactly one at every gate.
We write
\mathrm{tree}(f)
for the minimum formula size. Since every formula is
a circuit,
\mathrm{tree}(f)\geq\mathrm{opt}(f)
. The
gap
is

\mathrm{gap}(f)=\mathrm{tree}(f)-\mathrm{opt}(f)
.

Decomposition.

Every AIG gate computes
f=a\wedge b
(with possible edge complementation).
The minimum formula size satisfies:

\mathrm{tree}(f)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\colon f=a\wedge b\\
\text{or }f=\overline{a\wedge b}\end{subarray}}\bigl(1+\mathrm{opt}(a)+\mathrm{opt}(b)\bigr).

The
trivial decomposition

f=1\wedge f
always achieves

1+\mathrm{opt}(f)
.

NPN equivalence.

Two functions are NPN-equivalent if one can be obtained from the other by
negating inputs, permuting inputs, or negating the output. NPN equivalence
preserves
\mathrm{opt}
and
\mathrm{gap}
. At
n=4
there are 222 NPN
classes.

Essential variables.

A variable
x_{i}
is
essential
for
f
if
f
depends on
x_{i}
. The
number of essential variables is denoted
n
.

Gate elimination.

Lemma 1

(Classical; see Savage
[
6
]
for
exposition)
.

Any Boolean function with
k
essential variables requires at least
k-1
AND
gates in the AIG basis.

Proof.

Each AND gate merges at most two independent input dependencies into one.
Starting from
k
essential variables, at least
k-1
merges are needed.
∎

SAT-based exact synthesis.

We compute
\mathrm{opt}(f)
using the SAT-based approach of Kojevnikov, Kulikov,
and Yaroslavtsev
[
14
]
: encode the existence
of a
k
-gate AIG computing
f
as a satisfiability instance, then
binary-search on
k
.

Experimental scope.

Our computational results cover:

•

n=3
: all 256 functions, complete enumeration.

•

n=4
: all 65,536 functions (20,660 nontrivial), complete.

•

n=5
: complete for
\mathrm{opt}\leq 4
(30,896 functions).
At
\mathrm{opt}=5
: 500 functions sampled uniformly at random from 141,680
functions at that level; 72 out of 500 (14.4%) exhibit gap
{}=1
.

•

n=6
: 165 gap-1 circuits constructed from known sharing templates and
random sharing constructions. This is
not
a uniform sample; it is a
vocabulary verification exercise.

The goal of the experiments is structural identification rather than statistical
estimation; once the two mechanisms are observed, larger-scale enumeration would
not change the qualitative picture. Synthesis scripts and complete data for

n\leq 4
are available at

https://github.com/krinkin/unit-gap
.

The Unit Gap Theorem

Theorem 2

(Unit Gap)
.

For every Boolean function
f
in the AIG basis with free inversions,

\mathrm{gap}(f)\in\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

Lower bound.
Every formula is a circuit, so
\mathrm{tree}(f)\geq\mathrm{opt}(f)
.

Upper bound.
The trivial decomposition
f=1\wedge f
gives

\mathrm{tree}(f)\leq 1+\mathrm{opt}(f)
, since
\mathrm{opt}(1)=0
.
∎

The Unit Gap Theorem is a simple consequence of the AIG basis, but it sharply
constrains how sharing can appear in optimal circuits: optimal AIG circuits can
deviate from tree structure only through a single reconvergent sharing point.
In general
bases, the formula-circuit gap can be
superpolynomial
[
16
,
5
,
4
]
: for
instance, parity on
n
variables has formula complexity

\Theta(n^{2})

[
5
]
over the De Morgan basis but circuit
complexity
O(n)
. The collapse to a gap of at most 1 in the AIG basis arises
from two features: (i) the constant
1
is available at zero cost, enabling the
identity decomposition
f=1\wedge f
; and (ii) complementation is free, so

\mathrm{opt}(f)=\mathrm{opt}(\overline{f})
. Bases lacking either
property—such as
\{\mathrm{AND},\mathrm{OR}\}
with explicit NOT
gates—can exhibit larger gaps.

The tree Bellman operator.

Define the operator
T
on cost functions by

(Tv)(f)=\min_{a,b\colon f=a\wedge b}(1+v(a)+v(b))
.
Iterating
T
from the base case
v_{0}(x_{i})=v_{0}(1)=0
computes

\mathrm{tree}(f)
. The fixed point overshoots
\mathrm{opt}(f)
by exactly

\mathrm{gap}(f)\in\{0,1\}
.

The Threshold Theorem and Tree Theorem

Theorem 3

(Threshold Theorem)
.

If
f
has
n
essential variables and
\mathrm{gap}(f)=1
, then

\mathrm{opt}(f)\geq n
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{gap}(f)=1
, the optimal circuit is not a tree: it contains a
gate
g
with fan-out at least 2. (Otherwise the circuit would be a formula
achieving
\mathrm{opt}(f)=\mathrm{tree}(f)
, contradicting
\mathrm{gap}=1
.)

Partition the optimal circuit into three parts:

•

S
: the gates strictly below
g
(the fan-in cone of
g
, excluding
g
),
computing a function of
k
essential variables.
By Lemma
1
,
|S|\geq k-1
.

•

The gate
g
itself:
1
gate.

•

R
: the remaining gates above
g
. These treat
g
(and possibly

\overline{g}
) as a pseudo-input alongside the
n-k
primary inputs not in
the fan-in of
g
. Since
g
has fan-out
\geq 2
, the function computed
by
R
depends essentially on
g
; otherwise the multiple fan-outs of
g

would be redundant, contradicting optimality. Thus
R
has at least
n-k+1
essential inputs, giving
|R|\geq n-k
by Lemma
1
.

Summing:
\mathrm{opt}(f)=|S|+1+|R|\geq(k-1)+1+(n-k)=n
.
∎

Tightness.

The bound is tight. At
n=4
, all 288 gap-1 functions with
\mathrm{opt}=4=n
belong to a single NPN class (rep 495). At
n=5
, gap-1 functions
with
\mathrm{opt}=5=n
exist in both structural types (see
Section
6.4
).

Theorem 4

(Tree Theorem)
.

If
\mathrm{opt}(f)\leq 3
, then
\mathrm{gap}(f)=0
.

Proof.

For
\mathrm{opt}(f)\leq 2
, any circuit has at most 2 gates and is necessarily
a tree.

For
\mathrm{opt}(f)=3

function with
\mathrm{opt}\leq 2
. With 3 gates and one reconvergent gate
g_{1}

(fan-out
\geq 2
), the only possible topology is:
g_{1}
feeds both
g_{2}
and

g_{3}
, with
g_{2}
also feeding
g_{3}
.

cannot consume
g_{2}

5
The Decomposition Formula

The following identity makes the structure of optimal circuits explicit.
We first note a standard structural property: every subcircuit is itself
optimal.

Remark 5

(Optimal substructure)
.

Every sub-DAG of a size-optimal AIG is size-optimal for its function. This
follows from a replacement argument: if a sub-DAG were non-optimal, substituting
a smaller equivalent would reduce the total size, contradicting global
optimality.
2
2

2

The argument is valid when the sub-DAG interfaces with the
rest of the circuit only through its root gate
g
. For gates within the
sub-DAG that also fan out to other parts of the circuit, the shared gates are
accounted for by the decomposition formula below; see also the discussion of
sharing in Section
6
.

Combining this with inclusion-exclusion on sub-DAG sizes yields a decomposition
formula.

Corollary 6

(Decomposition Formula)
.

Let
C
be an optimal AIG, and let
g
be any gate computing
f=a\wedge b
.
Let
D_{a},D_{b}
be the sub-DAGs computing
a
and
b
. Then

\mathrm{opt}(f)=1+\mathrm{opt}(a)+\mathrm{opt}(b)-s(a,b),

where
s(a,b)=|D_{a}\cap D_{b}|\in\{0,1\}
counts the shared gates.

Proof.

By inclusion-exclusion,
|D_{g}|=1+|D_{a}|+|D_{b}|-|D_{a}\cap D_{b}|
.
By optimal substructure,
|D_{g}|=\mathrm{opt}(f)
,
|D_{a}|=\mathrm{opt}(a)
,

|D_{b}|=\mathrm{opt}(b)
. The bound
s(a,b)\leq 1
follows from the Unit Gap
Theorem:
s(a,b)=1+\mathrm{opt}(a)+\mathrm{opt}(b)-\mathrm{opt}(f)\leq\mathrm{tree}(f)-\mathrm{opt}(f)\leq 1
.
∎

Recursive characterization.

The decomposition formula gives a recursive equation for circuit complexity:

\mathrm{opt}(f)=\min_{a,b}(1+\mathrm{opt}(a)+\mathrm{opt}(b)-s(a,b))
.
Setting
s\equiv 0
recovers the tree equation for
\mathrm{tree}(f)
. The
sharing term
s(a,b)
couples the two subproblems: it equals 1 when the optimal
sub-DAGs for

and

Bellman equation,
s(a,b)
depends on the global solution (the specific optimal
circuit), not just on the subproblem pair—so this is an identity that holds at
the optimum, not a recurrence that can be solved independently.

Theorem 7

(Two-Mechanism)
.

In any size-optimal AIG with
\mathrm{gap}(f)=1
, the sharing arises from
exactly one gate with fan-out
2
, employing either dual-polarity reuse (consumers
reference the gate in opposite polarities) or same-polarity reuse (consumers
reference the gate in the same polarity). No other sharing structure can produce

\mathrm{gap}=1
.

Proof.

By Corollary
6

\mathrm{gap}=1
implies

g
is shared between the two child sub-DAGs.
Gate
g
has one output wire; each consumer sees
g
through an edge with
complementation attribute
p\in\{0,1\}
. Two consumers yield a polarity pair

(p_{1},p_{2})\in\{0,1\}^{2}
. If
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
: dual-polarity. If

p_{1}=p_{2}
: CSE. These exhaust
\{0,1\}^{2}
.

The multi-gate concern dissolves because the Unit Gap Theorem forces
s(a,b)\leq 1
at
every
decomposition, so each decomposition has at most one shared
gate and the argument applies independently at each.
∎

The theorem classifies every shared gate in any size-optimal AIG. The connection to gap-1
circuits is that these are the only mechanisms through which sharing produces the
unit gap.

6.4
Type A and Type B gap-1 functions

Gap-1 NPN classes fall into two types:

Type A (decomposable).

At least one nontrivial AND decomposition achieves
\mathrm{tree}(f)
. Sharing

1\wedge f
) costs
\mathrm{opt}(f)+1
.

n=4

All nontrivial tree decompositions cost
\mathrm{opt}(f)+2
or more. Only

1\wedge f
achieves
\mathrm{tree}(f)
. All Type B functions decompose as
XOR-based expressions; the XOR construction in AIG inherently requires both
polarities, forcing duplication in any tree.

7
Tropical Interpretation

The results of the preceding sections admit a concise reformulation in the
language of tropical algebra. This interpretation is included to clarify the
relationship between tree recursion and circuit optimization rather than to
introduce new results.

The tree recursion as a tropical operator.

The tree Bellman operator
T
defined in Section
3
,

(Tv)(f)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\colon f=a\wedge b\end{subarray}}\bigl(1+v(a)+v(b)\bigr),

is a min-plus (tropical) operator on the lattice of cost functions over Boolean
functions. It computes exactly the minimum formula size: starting from
v_{0}=0

on inputs and constants, the iterates
T^{k}v_{0}
converge to

\mathrm{tree}(f)
in finitely many steps. We observe that
T
is
order-preserving and idempotent at its fixed point, so
\mathrm{tree}
is the
tropical fixed point of the recursion induced by the AND decomposition lattice.

The DAG correction.

The true circuit complexity is related to this tropical fixed point by a
pointwise correction:

\mathrm{opt}(f)\;=\;\mathrm{tree}(f)-\mathrm{gap}(f),\qquad\mathrm{gap}(f)\in\{0,1\}.

We observe that this is a quantized correction: the tropical operator overshoots
by exactly 0 or 1, never more. The DAG structure of circuits provides at most
one unit of improvement over the tree structure, everywhere on the lattice.

The decomposition formula as corrected recurrence.

At each gate of an optimal circuit, the Decomposition Formula
(Corollary
6
) reads:

\mathrm{opt}(f)=1+\mathrm{opt}(a)+\mathrm{opt}(b)-s(a,b),\qquad s(a,b)\in\{0,1\}.

We observe that this is the tropical recurrence with a binary sharing
correction:
s=0
recovers the tree (tropical) equation, while
s=1

accounts for a single shared gate between the sub-DAGs. The sharing term is
quantized to the same
\{0,1\}
alphabet as the gap itself.

The relaxation gap fraction.

Across complete enumerations, roughly 85% of functions satisfy
\mathrm{gap}=0
(the tropical fixed point is exact) and roughly 15% satisfy
\mathrm{gap}=1
(the fixed point overshoots by one). We observe that this fraction is a
property of the decomposition lattice and the AIG basis, not of individual
functions: it is invariant under NPN symmetries and depends on the interaction
between the AND gate structure and the space of Boolean functions at a given
complexity level.

Two types of DAG shortcut.

The two sharing mechanisms of Section
6
—dual-polarity and
CSE—correspond to two structurally distinct ways a DAG can improve on a tree.
In the tropical picture, these are the only two “shortcuts” by which the
DAG correction
s=1
is realized:

•

Dual-polarity:
a gate is consumed in both polarities (
g
and

\overline{g}
), which a tree cannot express without duplication.

•

Common subexpression:
a gate is consumed twice in the same
polarity by disjoint branches, which a tree must duplicate.

We observe that both shortcuts reduce to a single reconvergent edge in the
circuit DAG—a single point where the DAG departs from tree structure—and
that no circuit uses more than one such shortcut simultaneously (consistent with

s\leq 1
and the Two-Mechanism Theorem).

Scope.

This tropical interpretation is descriptive: it reframes the theorems and
computational observations in a unified language but does not yield new results.
A formal tropical-algebraic development—characterizing the operator
T
, its
fixed-point structure, and the lattice geometry that may underlie the observed 85/15
split—remains open.

8
Open Questions

1.

Asymptotic gap-1 fraction.

For random Boolean functions,
\mathrm{opt}(f)\sim 2^{n}/n

[
2
,
3
]
, so the Threshold Theorem is trivially
satisfied. Whether the gap-1 fraction converges to a limit—and if so,
whether it remains near
15\%
or vanishes—is unknown.

2.

Multi-output extension.

The decomposition formula applies to single-output circuits. For multi-output
circuits, the sharing term couples outputs. A multi-output Unit Gap Theorem
is not known.

3.

Other bases.

The Unit Gap Theorem relies on free inversions and a free constant
1
. What
is the gap structure for
\{\mathrm{AND},\mathrm{OR},\mathrm{NOT}\}
with
explicit NOT gates? For majority-inverter graphs? For XOR-AND graphs?

4.

Natural proofs.

The gap property is constructive and large in the sense of Razborov and
Rudich
[
10
]
: it can be computed from the truth table, and
85% of functions satisfy gap
=0
. Under standard cryptographic assumptions,
such properties cannot yield superpolynomial lower bounds against circuits
computing pseudorandom functions. Whether finer structural properties of
sharing (the Type A/B classification) escape this barrier is unclear.

5.

Communication complexity.

The Karchmer–Wigderson framework
[
8
]
characterizes
circuit
depth
via communication complexity. Whether an analogous
framework exists for circuit
size
—and whether the Type A/B
classification has a communication-theoretic interpretation—is open. We
note that KW theory addresses depth, not size, over the De Morgan basis, not
AIG; bridging these is itself a non-trivial problem.

9
Conclusion

In the AIG basis with free inversions, the formula-circuit gap is always 0 or 1.
Sharing first becomes possible at
\mathrm{opt}(f)=n
and operates through
two mechanisms—dual-polarity and same-polarity reuse—which we prove are the
only possibilities.
In optimal AIG circuits, reconvergence appears as an atomic phenomenon: at most
one gate is shared between the two branches of any decomposition.

The results are basis-specific: the trivial decomposition
f=1\wedge f
that
drives the Unit Gap Theorem requires a free constant. Whether analogous
structural completeness holds in bases without free inversions is the principal
open question. The characterization
is complete qualitatively (gap bounds and threshold hold for all
n
) but the
exact classification of gap-1 functions remains open beyond
n=7
.

Source code, synthesis scripts, and complete experimental data (all optimal
circuits for
n\leq 4
, sampled circuits for
n=5\text{--}7
, and all gap-1
circuit structures) are available at

https://github.com/krinkin/unit-gap
.

Appendix B
Local Rewriting Dynamics

Iteratively replacing non-optimal subcircuits with optimal ones is a monotone
process (gate count decreases at each step) that terminates at a fixed point
where every subcircuit is optimal. For tree-structured fixed points, this
corresponds to
\mathrm{tree}(f)
.

For gap-0 functions, the fixed point is the global optimum. For gap-1 functions,
local rewriting gets stuck at
\mathrm{tree}(f)=\mathrm{opt}(f)+1
: reaching
the optimum requires a non-local move that introduces a shared gate with fan-out

>1
.

The difficulty of this non-local move depends on the function type:

•

Type A:
A nontrivial tree decomposition exists. The subtrees
share a common truth table; identifying and merging it crosses the barrier.

•

Type B:
No nontrivial tree decomposition achieves
\mathrm{tree}(f)
.
Crossing the barrier requires recognizing XOR-like structure and building the
dual-polarity template.

Under finer-grained single-gate moves, 4 out of 12 NPN classes at
n=3
have
local minima 1–2 gates above optimal. The landscape is locally funnel-shaped
around the optimum but has barriers elsewhere, arising from topological
incompatibility between circuit structures (e.g., parallel vs. sequential
decomposition).
