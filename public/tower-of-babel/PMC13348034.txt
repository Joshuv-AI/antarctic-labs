Anthropogenic Black Carbon Signatures in Antarctic Fjords: Implications for Carbon Cycling
Authors: Jun-Oh Min, Boyeon Lee, Chorom Shim, Young Wook Ko, Seung-Hee Kim, Sujin Kang, Jisoo Park, Sun-Yong Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Black carbon (BC) dynamics in polar fjords remain poorly understood despite their importance in organic carbon (OC) cycling. We investigated BC dynamics in the water column and sediments of Antarctic fjords. Dissolved BC concentrations in Marian Cove and Maxwell Bay ranged from 0.10–2.71 and 0.70–2.70 μM, respectively, with molecular characteristics indicating highly condensed, locally anthropogenic BC rather than photochemically aged biomass-burning sources. Bayesian mixing model analyses showed that fossil-fuel-derived BC increased 2.7-fold, from 6% in 2019 to 16% in 2023, reflecting intensified tourism and research activities following COVID-19-related restrictions. Sedimentary BC (73–85% C3-derived) suggests local combustion sources, although minor contributions from natural biomass burning cannot be excluded. In contrast, Arctic Kongsfjorden sediments showed 53% C4 contribution, indicating long-range wildfire BC transport. Marian Cove sediment traps yielded a BC flux of 0.04 g m–2 d–1 (22% of OC flux). Higher apparent burial efficiency of BC (89–114%) relative to OC (75–95%), together with elevated Δ­(SBC:OC) values (1.20) and millennial-scale radiocarbon ages (4700–5120 yr), indicate preferential BC preservation. These findings show that Antarctic fjords effectively sequester anthropogenic BC and highlight the importance of incorporating BC dynamics into polar coastal carbon budgets.

Introduction

Black carbon (BC) is a refractory carbonaceous residue produced by the incomplete combustion of biomass and fossil fuels, characterized by high organic carbon (OC) content (>60%) and polycondensed aromatic structures. , Global BC emissions are estimated at 130–182 Tg yr–1, dominated by biomass burning (128–153 Tg yr–1) with a smaller contribution from fossil fuel combustion (2–29 Tg yr–1). Of the biomass-derived fraction, approximately 2–11 Tg yr–1 is emitted as long-range-transported atmospheric soot. − BC comprises a structural continuum from partially carbonized biomass to highly condensed aromatic soot. , This inherent resistance to degradation allows BC to persist in terrestrial and marine reservoirs for millennia, representing a critical, long-lived carbon pool in the global climate system. , Despite its recognized importance, a persistent imbalance remains between estimated BC production and quantified inventories in terrestrial and marine reservoirs. This discrepancy suggests that key removal and transformation processessuch as photooxidation, microbial degradation, and land-to-ocean transportremain insufficiently constrained. Resolving BC sources, transport pathways, and fate in the ocean is therefore essential for improving assessments of long-term carbon sequestration and associated climate feedbacks.

In aquatic systems, BC represents a substantial fraction of OC pools, accounting for approximately 2–8% of oceanic dissolved organic carbon (DOC) and 15–21% of sedimentary OC. − Previous studies have primarily focused on BC dynamics in open-ocean environments, coastal zones, and lacustrine systems, where BC fluxes, fraction within OC pools, and burial processes have been relatively well constrained. − Among these aquatic environments, fjords are widely recognized as globally important carbon sinks and, despite their limited areal extent, are estimated to bury approximately 18 Mt of OC annuallyequivalent to ∼11% of total marine OC burial. Their high burial efficiency suggests that BC burial may represent a significant component of long-term carbon sequestration in fjord ecosystems. However, the magnitude of BC fluxes in polar fjords, the relative contribution of BC to OC burial, and the efficiency of BC preservation during early diagenesis have not yet been quantitatively constrained.

Accelerated climate change over recent decades has placed increasing pressure on polar coastal environments, particularly along the Antarctic Peninsula, where rapid warming has driven pronounced biogeochemical variability in carbon cycling. − Recent studies demonstrate that local emissions associated with research infrastructure and ship-based tourism generate substantial amounts of BC, − which exert a disproportionate climatic influence by darkening snow surfaces, reducing albedo, and enhancing solar energy absorption. ,, Radiative transfer modeling further indicates that anthropogenic BC enrichment can substantially enhance seasonal snowmelt across the Antarctic Peninsula, underscoring the sensitivity of regional cryospheric systems to human-driven perturbations. Consequently, increased oceanic BC inputs associated with accelerating anthropogenic emissions may have significant implications not only for radiative forcing but also for the carbon budgets of coastal and oceanic systems.

To date, most Antarctic BC studies have focused on atmospheric transport and accumulation in snow, ice cores, and glacier-associated freshwater systems. − , In contrast, investigations of BC within the marine environment remain limited, with only a few studies reporting BC dynamics in the water column of East Antarctica and in sediments of the Ross Sea. , Comprehensive data sets linking BC sources, vertical transport, and burial fluxes in the rapidly warming fjord systems of the Antarctic Peninsula are still lacking.

To address these gaps, this study investigates the distribution, sources, and burial efficiency of BC in the water column and sediments of Antarctic fjords with comparative insights from Arctic fjords. By integrating BC concentration measurements, isotopic characterization, and flux-based estimates, we quantify the contribution of BC to fjord carbon budgets and evaluate its role as a long-term sink for anthropogenic carbon in polar coastal environments.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

This study was conducted in two contrasting polar fjord systems: Marian Cove and Maxwell Bay on King George Island and Kongsfjorden in the Arctic (Figure ). Marian Cove is situated on the eastern side of Maxwell Bay and represents a smaller, glacier-influenced fjord (∼4.5 km long, ∼1.5 km wide, maximum depth ≈ 120 m). The tidewater glacier at its head retreated by ∼1.9 km between 1956 and 2017, resulting in an estimated ∼45% loss of grounded ice. , A shallow sill (∼50 m) at the cove entrance allows limited seawater inflow, while enhanced summer meltwater discharge establishes a low-salinity surface layer. Maxwell Bay, the largest embayment in the South Shetland Islands, lies between King George and Nelson Islands and is connected to the Bransfield Strait through a deep sill (∼500 m), facilitating strong oceanic exchange. The bay extends ∼14 km in length and 6–14 km in width, forming a U-shaped fjord bordered by ice cliffs associated with the low-relief ice caps of the Fildes Peninsula and Nelson Island. In the Arctic, Kongsfjorden is a glacial fjord in Svalbard, extending ∼27 km in length and 4–10 km in width, with an estimated volume of ∼29.4 km3. Substantial meltwater input from multiple tidewater glaciers generates pronounced gradients in sediment load and salinity along the fjord.

Sample Collection

As part of an investigation into the sources, distribution, and cycling of BC in Marian Cove and Maxwell Bay, a marine survey was conducted from December 23, 2023 to January 16, 2024. Seawater samples were collected from four stations (MC-05, 08, 11, 14) in Marian Cove and three stations (MB-01, 02, 03) in Maxwell Bay at surface (0 m), intermediate (50 m), and bottom (100 m) depths (Figure a). Water temperature and salinity were measured using a CTD system (SBE 911 Plus, Sea-Bird Electronics, USA), and seawater was collected by using Niskin bottles.

All sampling bottles were precleaned with 10% hydrochloric acid (HCl) and rinsed thoroughly with Milli-Q water prior to use. To assess the spatial and vertical distribution of dissolved black carbon (DBC), 10–20 L of seawater were filtered through precombusted (450 °C, 6 h) glass fiber filters (GF/F; 0.7 μm pore size, 47 mm diameter; Whatman, UK) in the laboratory at King Sejong Station. Filtrates were subsequently acidified to pH < 2 using 6 N HCl. Samples for DOC analysis were collected immediately onboard following filtration through precombusted 47 mm GF/F filters, transferred into precombusted 50 mL amber glass bottles, and stored frozen at −24 °C until analysis.

Fluxes of particulate black carbon (PBC) and OC were estimated using custom-made conical sediment traps deployed by divers at two sites (T-01, 02) in Marian Cove (Figure a). The traps were positioned at approximately 25 m water depth and retrieved after a deployment period of 26 days. Surface sediment samples (0–2 cm) were collected from 15 stations in Marian Cove using grab samplers operated by vessel-mounted winches and stored at −24 °C until analysis (Figure a). For spatiotemporal comparison, additional surface sediment samples collected from the same Marian Cove stations in January 2019 and from seven sites in Kongsfjorden, Svalbard, in August 2019 were also analyzed (Figure b).

Dissolved Black Carbon Analysis

Condensed aromatic carbon within dissolved organic matter (DOM) was quantified using the benzene poly­(carboxylic acid) (BPCA) method, which oxidizes polyaromatic structures into benzene rings bearing three to six carboxyl groups (B3CA–B6CA). DOM was isolated from filtered seawater by solid-phase extraction (SPE) and eluted with methanol (MeOH). Following solvent removal by rotary evaporation (CVE-3110, EYELA, Japan), the extracts were transferred to high-purity Teflon bottles and oxidized with 0.5 mL of 65% nitric acid at 170 °C for 6 h to convert condensed aromatic structures into BPCAs. After oxidation, residual nitric acid was removed by evaporation at 50 °C under a gentle nitrogen flow prior to BPCA analysis. BPCA separation and quantification were performed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). All BPCA oxidation and HPLC analyses were conducted in duplicate, yielding analytical uncertainties of <3%. The extraction efficiency of DBC from SPE-isolated DOM ranged from 46% to 53%, consistent with previously reported values. Detailed procedures for DBC pretreatment, BPCA quantification, and HPLC analysis are provided in the Supporting Information (Text S1–S3).

Sedimentary Black Carbon Analysis

The concentration of sedimentary black carbon (SBC) was quantified using the CTO-375 thermal oxidation protocol. , Surface sediment samples were freeze-dried, homogenized, and finely ground. Aliquots were evenly distributed in ceramic crucibles to minimize charring and combusted at 375 °C for 24 h. The resulting residues were treated with 1 N HCl to remove residual inorganic carbon, rinsed repeatedly with triple-distilled water to eliminate remaining acid, and freeze-dried. Subsequently, 50–80 mg of each sample was sealed in tin capsules and analyzed for elemental composition using a vario MACRO cube elemental analyzer (Elementar, Germany). Analytical accuracy was verified using the standard reference material SRM 1941b (National Institute of Standards and Technology, USA), which yielded an SBC concentration of 5.23 ± 0.35 mg g–1, consistent with previously reported values (5.3 ± 1.4 to 6.1 ± 0.63 mg g–1). − The analytical uncertainty for SBC determinations was within 5%, indicating acceptable precision.

The stable carbon isotopic composition (δ13C) of SBC was determined following CTO-375 pretreatment at the Alaska Stable Isotope Facility using isotope ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS). A laboratory reference standard (δ13C = −15.8‰) yielded a measured value of −15.8 ± 0.19‰, confirming analytical accuracy and precision. To support isotopic source apportionment, incinerator ash samples were analyzed as representative anthropogenic reference materials. Five ash samples were collected from a designated incinerator at King Sejong Station, treated with 1 N HCl to remove inorganic carbon, freeze-dried, homogenized, and sealed in tin capsules prior to IRMS analysis.

Source contributions to SBC were quantified using a Bayesian stable isotope mixing model based on δ13C signatures. Model analyses were conducted using the R package MixSIAR (version 3.1.10). The final model incorporated three end-member sources: C3 plant biomass (δ13C = −26.7 ± 1.8‰), C4 plant biomass (δ13C = −15.0 ± 1.0‰), and fossil fuel combustion (δ13C = −29.5 ± 2.5‰). These end-members were used to estimate the relative contribution of each BC source to sediment samples.

Radiocarbon (Δ14C) analyses of SBC were performed at the National Ocean Sciences AMS Facility (NOSAMS), Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, using a Mini Carbon Dating System (MICADAS) accelerator mass spectrometer (AMS), following standard analytical procedures. Analyses were conducted on SBC isolated from sediments collected at a single station (MC-08) in Marian Cove during both 2019 and 2023 to assess temporal variability in BC age and source characteristics.

Dissolved and Sedimentary Organic Carbon Analysis

DOC concentrations were measured using a TOC-L analyzer (Shimadzu, Japan). Analytical accuracy and precision were assessed by analyzing a certified reference material (Florida Strait deep water, 42–45 μM DOC; University of Miami, USA) every ten samples, with analytical errors maintained within 5%. Sedimentary OC concentrations were determined from ∼20 mg of freeze-dried sediment following removal of inorganic carbon with 1 N hydrochloric acid (HCl). Measurements were conducted using an elemental analyzer, yielding analytical uncertainties of <3%.

Black Carbon Flux, Burial Flux, Efficiency, and ΔSBC:OC Calculations

Fluxes and burial efficiencies of PBC and OC, along with related parameters, were calculated using sediment trap and surface sediment data from the 2023 Marian Cove data set. In addition, a relative preservation index (ΔSBC:OC) was introduced to assess the preferential retention of SBC relative to OC during sedimentation and burial. Sediment trap fluxes of PBC and OC were calculated as 1 Ftrap,x=MxA×t where F trap,x is the trap flux of constituent x (g m–2 yr–1), M x is the collected mass of PBC or OC (g), A is the trap collection area (0.031 m2 for a 20 cm diameter trap), and t is the deployment duration (days). Fluxes were first normalized to daily rates and subsequently converted to annual units by multiplying by 365. To evaluate the potential influence of seasonal variability associated with this annual extrapolation, a sensitivity analysis based on previously reported seasonal particle flux patterns in Marian Cove was conducted (see Text S4 and Table S6).

Burial fluxes of SBC and OC were calculated from surface sediment concentrations, dry bulk density, and sediment accumulation rates as 2 Fburial,x=Cx×ρdry×SR×10 where F burial,x is the burial flux of constituent x (g m–2 yr–1), C x is the concentration of SBC or OC in dry sediment (mg g–1), ρdry is the dry bulk density (g cm–3), and SR is the sediment accumulation rate (cm yr–1). Dry bulk density was calculated as ρdry = M s/V, where M s is the dry sediment mass (g) and V is the wet volume (cm3). The factor of 10 results from the combined unit conversions from mg to g (10–3) and from cm2 to m2 (104), yielding an overall factor of 10 when converting mg cm–2 yr–1 to g m–2 yr–1. Burial fluxes were calculated using an SR of 5.2–6.6 mm yr–1 reported for 210Pb-dated sediment cores from Marian Cove in Boldt et al., representing spatial variability in sedimentation within the fjord.

Burial efficiency (BE) was defined as the ratio of burial flux to sediment trap flux, which quantifies the proportion of sinking particulate material that is ultimately preserved in sediments: 3 BE(%)=(Fburial,xFtrap,x)×100 where F burial,x and F trap,x denote the burial and sediment trap fluxes of constituent x (g m–2 yr–1), respectively.

To evaluate the preferential preservation of SBC relative to OC during sedimentation and burial, a relative preservation index was calculated as 4 Δ(SBC:OC)=Fburial,SBC/Fburial,OCFtrap,PBC/Ftrap,OC where F burial,SBC and F burial,OC are the burial fluxes of SBC and OC and F trap,PBC and F trap,OC are the corresponding sediment trap fluxes of PBC and OC. Values of Δ­(SBC:OC) > 1 indicate preferential preservation of SBC relative to OC during burial, whereas values <1 indicate reduced relative preservation.

Results and Discussion

Hydrography

The hydrographic structure of Marian Cove exhibited strong vertical stratification, characterized by pronounced gradients in temperature, salinity, and density (Figure a–c). Water temperatures ranged from −0.14 to 1.80 °C, while salinities varied between 33.7 and 34.2 psu (Figure a–b). This surface stratification was primarily driven by substantial freshwater discharge from the tidewater glacier during the austral summer. Consistent with this interpretation, the lowest surface salinity (33.7 psu) was observed at station MC-05, located closest to the glacier terminus, indicating the presence of a buoyant glacial meltwater layer. The freshwater input established a well-defined pycnocline between 20 and 30 m depth, whereas waters below ∼40 m depth were relatively homogeneous, reflecting a well-mixed lower water column (Figure c). In contrast, Maxwell Bay displayed weaker vertical stratification and more uniform hydrographic conditions (Figure g–i). Water temperatures ranged from 0.30 to 1.94 °C and salinities from 34.0 to 34.3 psu (Figure g–h). Vertical gradients in both parameters were more gradual than those observed in Marian Cove, and surface temperatures were slightly higher. A weak pycnocline developed within the upper ∼10 m, likely reflecting limited meltwater influence, while enhanced vertical mixing dominated below this depth (Figure i). Overall, the hydrographic characteristics of Maxwell Bay indicate a physically dynamic and well-mixed fjord system, with reduced surface freshening relative to Marian Cove and strong exchange with the adjacent Bransfield Strait.

Vertical DBC profiles in both fjords showed pronounced enrichment in the low-salinity surface layer, followed by a gradual decrease with depth (Figure b,e). In Marian Cove, particularly elevated surface DBC concentrations (2.71 μM) were observed at station MC-05, located closest to the tidewater glacier terminus, indicating a strong influence of meltwater and terrestrial snowmelt discharge. Previous studies have shown that BC deposited via long-range atmospheric transport can be stored in snow and glacier ice and subsequently mobilized by meltwater into downstream aquatic systems. , Consistent with this mechanism, DBC exhibited a strong negative correlation with salinity (Figure ), supporting meltwater discharge as the primary pathway supplying DBC to surface waters and regulating its vertical distribution through meltwater-induced stratification. These observations highlight a coupled transport pathway in which atmospheric deposition, cryospheric storage, and meltwater release collectively regulate the delivery of DBC to fjord surface waters.

Relationship between salinity and dissolved black carbon (DBC) in Marian Cove (a) and Maxwell Bay (b), Antarctic Peninsula, in 2023.

The relative contribution of DBC to the DOC pool (DBC/DOC) differed markedly between the two fjords. In Marian Cove, DBC/DOC ratios ranged from 0.17% to 3.98% (Figure f), comparable to values reported for other polar waters and the open ocean (Table ). In contrast, Maxwell Bay exhibited substantially higher ratios (1.29–5.20%; Figure l), approaching those reported for Arctic fjords near Longyearbyen (6.20%) (Table ). Maxwell Bay is among the most heavily trafficked regions in Antarctica, with 424 vessel visits recorded between 2014 and 2018. Elevated concentrations of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and other combustion-derived tracers have previously been reported in nearby Potter Cove, indicating substantial fossil fuel inputs associated with ship traffic and station activities. Together, these observations suggest that the elevated DBC/DOC ratios in Maxwell Bay reflect combined inputs of glacier-derived DBC and additional BC from fossil fuel combustion. Furthermore, the semi-enclosed circulation of Maxwell Bay and its exchange with adjacent coastal waters may enhance the accumulation and lateral redistribution of BC derived from both local emissions and regionally transported sources. This coupled atmospheric–cryospheric–oceanic transport framework also facilitates the vertical transfer of BC from the water column to the sediment, thereby linking DBC dynamics with sediment trap fluxes and burial processes in fjord systems.

The degree of aromatic condensation of DBC was further evaluated using BPCA molecular markers, which provide diagnostic information on combustion conditions, photochemical processing, and source influences. − The B6/B5 ratios ranged from 0.17 to 1.75 in Marian Cove and from 0.12 to 1.86 in Maxwell Bay, values approximately two to four times higher than those reported for the Arctic Ocean (0.14–0.19) and the open ocean (0.23–0.31) (Table ). In contrast, the (B3 + B4)/(B5 + B6) ratios ranged from 0.86 to 2.84 in Marian Cove and from 0.40 to 1.86 in Maxwell Bay, which are two to six times lower than the values reported for Antarctic lakes (2.50–5.40) and Arctic fjords near Longyearbyen (4.3) (Table ). , Previous studies have shown that extensive photochemical degradation during long-range atmospheric transport preferentially enriches B3 and B4, thereby increasing the (B3 + B4)/(B5 + B6) ratio. , Accordingly, the elevated B6/B5 ratios observed in this study indicate a predominance of highly condensed aromatic structures, while the low (B3 + B4)/(B5 + B6) ratios further suggest limited photochemical alteration. Collectively, these compositional features indicate that DBC in Marian Cove and Maxwell Bay is dominated by relatively fresh, highly condensed BC, most likely derived from local anthropogenic combustion sources, rather than from extensively aged biomass-burning aerosols transported over long distances.

Sedimentary Black Carbon in Polar Fjord Systems

Temporal and Spatial Variability of Sedimentary Black Carbon in Polar Fjords

Marine sediments provide valuable archives for reconstructing the spatiotemporal sources and characteristics of OC, including BC, owing to their capacity to preserve reactive organic compounds. In Marian Cove, OC concentration in 2023 ranged from 4.95 to 20.0 mg g–1 (mean, 9.61 ± 3.88 mg g–1), which was not significantly different from values measured at the same sites in 2019 (range, 3.97–16.8 mg g–1; mean, 8.11 ± 3.82 mg g–1) (Figure a; Table S2). In contrast, SBC concentration in Marian Cove increased significantly by approximately 2.8-fold in 2023, ranging from 1.44 to 3.66 mg g–1 (mean, 2.56 ± 0.72 mg g–1), compared with 0.33–1.87 mg g–1 (mean, 0.92 ± 0.47 mg g–1) in 2019 (Figure b; Table S2). Consistent with this increase, the SBC/OC ratioan indicator of the relative preservation of SBCincreased by 2.4-fold, from 12 ± 5% (range, 7–27%) in 2019 to 29 ± 9% (range, 16–43%) in 2023 (Table ; Figure c).

For spatial comparison between polar fjord systems, sedimentary OC and SBC characteristics in Marian Cove were compared with those from Kongsfjorden. Sediments from Kongsfjorden exhibited OC contents ranging from 0.50% to 1.05% (mean, 0.75 ± 0.18%), which were lower than those measured in Marian Cove in both 2019 and 2023 (Table ). However, Kongsfjorden sediments displayed higher mean SBC contents (0.25 ± 0.03%) and SBC/OC ratios (36 ± 12%) than combined mean values observed in Marian Cove across 2019 and 2023. The mean SBC contents (0.09–0.26%) and mean SBC/OC ratios (12–36%) observed across Marian Cove and Kongsfjorden were approximately 2.6–4.5 and 3.0–4.6 times higher, respectively, than those reported for open-ocean sediments, including the Ross Sea, Arctic marginal seas, and Atlantic Ocean settings (Table ). ,,− Together, these results indicate that polar fjord sediments retain disproportionately high fractions of BC relative to total OC, consistent with substantial and persistent inputs of BC to these rapidly changing coastal systems.

Stable Isotope Properties of Sedimentary Black Carbon

Carbon isotopes serve as reliable tracers for identifying SBC sources, as different combustion materials exhibit distinct isotopic signatures. BC from C3 plant combustion is isotopically lighter (−22‰ to −30‰) than BC from C4 plants (−10‰ to −14‰), , while fossil fuels display a broader δ13C range due to geological heterogeneity and combustion fractionation (coal: −24.8‰; natural gas: as low as −39.1‰).

The δ13C–SBC values in Marian Cove ranged from −27.3‰ to −22.6‰ in 2023 (mean, −25.3 ± 1.59‰) and from −25.5‰ to −20.1‰ in 2019 (mean, −24.4 ± 1.40‰) (Table ). These values were approximately 4–5‰ lighter than those previously reported for the Ross Sea (range, −17.2‰ to −13.5‰; mean, −15.2 ± 1.54‰), but comparable to values observed along the South American coast (range, −27.5‰ to −23.3‰; mean, −25.1 ± 1.34‰) (Table ). , Unlike Marian Cove, sediments from Kongsfjorden exhibited δ13C–SBC values ranging from −20.8‰ to −17.9‰ (mean, −19.2 ± 1.01‰), consistent with C4-plant-derived biomass burning. These values were 5–6‰ heavier than those in Marian Cove and approximately 3‰ more enriched than those in the Chukchi Borderland (range, −23.1‰ to −21.6‰; mean, −22.5 ± 0.69‰) (Table ). The δ13C–SBC signatures revealed distinct isotopic differences between the Antarctic Peninsula and Arctic fjord environments (Figure d). In Marian Cove, δ13C values fall within the isotopic ranges of fossil fuels and C3 plants, indicating that BC primarily originates from mixed fossil-fuel combustion and C3-biomass burning. Conversely, the relatively enriched δ13C values in Kongsfjorden reflect BC from mixed C3–C4 biomass burning transported over long distances, indicating a significant influence of wildfires in midlatitude continental regions. These contrasting isotopic patterns demonstrate that regional variability in BC sources results from differing contributions of local anthropogenic combustion and long-range transported biomass-burning aerosols.

Source Apportionment of Black Carbon Using Bayesian Mixing Models

The relative contribution of anthropogenic sources to SBC was estimated using a Bayesian isotope mixing model based on carbon isotope composition (Figure ). Given the overlap in δ13C values between fossil fuel and C3 biomass endmembers and the simplified three-endmember framework, these estimates should be interpreted as approximate. The model results indicate that the fossil fuel contribution in Marian Cove in 2023 reached 16%, representing an approximately 2.7-fold increase relative to 6% in 2019 (Figure a). This increase closely tracks the observed 2.8-fold rise in SBC content and 2.4-fold increase in the SBC/OC ratio (Figure b,c), providing evidence consistent with the interpretation that intensified human activity in the Antarctic Peninsula region has strengthened the local BC footprint. Notably, this rise occurred following a period of minimal human activity between 2021 and 2022 (23,508 land-based visitors; 0 cruise visitors) due to COVID-19-related travel restrictions. The 2023–2024 austral season witnessed approximately 43,224 tourist arrivals via cruise shipsapproximately 2.3 times higher than during the 2019–2020 season and the highest number documented in two decades (Figure a; Table S4). Previous studies have shown that background BC concentrations in Antarctic snow typically remain below 1 ng g–1 due to its remote location, whereas relatively high concentrations (1–8 ng g–1) occur near research stations and tourist destinations where fuel combustion is concentrated. The rapid postpandemic resurgence of tourism is therefore consistent with the observed increase in fossil-fuel-derived BC in 2023, suggesting that local anthropogenic activities likely contribute to BC deposition in Antarctic coastal environments. Source apportionment results indicate that SBC in Marian Cove was dominated by C3 vegetation sources, accounting for approximately 85% of total SBC in 2019 and 73% in 2023 (Figure b). This consistently high C3 contribution suggests that BC is largely associated with the combustion of C3-based materials, such as wood and paper. Carbon isotopic analysis of incineration ash from the official incinerator at King Sejong Station yielded δ13C values (mean, −26.9‰; range, −27.5‰ to −26.7‰, n = 5) consistent with the characteristic δ13C range of C3 vegetation (δ13C = – 26.7 ± 1.8‰), indicating that combustion of C3-derived materials from research station operations represents a plausible contributor to SBC. Currently, 76 year-round research stations operate across the Antarctic Treaty area, accommodating approximately 5500 personnel during the austral summer, according to data from the Council of Managers of National Antarctic Programs (COMNAP) and the Antarctic Treaty Secretariat (ATS). , These operational settings suggest that localized biomass combustion associated with research station activities may contribute to the elevated δ13C signatures observed in Marian Cove sediments. However, contributions from natural biomass-derived BC, including wildfire emissions transported over long distances through the atmosphere, as well as BC preserved in snow and sea ice and subsequently remobilized by meltwater, cannot be excluded. The model also estimated a minor C4 contribution (10%) (Figure c). Although smoke from wildfires in South America and Australia has been detected over Antarctica, − previous back-trajectory analyses suggest that long-range transport plays only a limited role in supplying absorbing aerosols to the Antarctic region. The C4 signal observed in Marian Cove likely represents a persistent but low-intensity background contribution derived from long-range transported biomass-burning aerosols, rather than a dominant or episodic source of BC.

This interpretation highlights a pronounced contrast between Antarctic fjords and Arctic systems such as Kongsfjorden. Arctic fjord sediments from Kongsfjorden (Svalbard), collected in 2019 exhibited a distinctly different isotopic composition, with approximately 53% C4, 33% C3, and 13% fossil-fuel contributions, indicating a predominance of biomass-burning sources during this period (Figure ). The 2019 Northern Hemisphere wildfire season was unusually intense, with widespread fires across Siberia, particularly in Yakutia, burning an estimated 7.2 million hectares (Figure b). Long-range transport of wildfire smoke to the Arctic during this period was confirmed by aerosol observations at Ny-Ålesund. Consistent with these transport patterns, isotope-based source apportionment of atmospheric elemental carbon at the Zeppelin Observatory (Svalbard) based on Δ14C and δ13C analyses revealed that approximately 52–57% of Arctic BC originated from biomass burning during winter high-pollution events. Together, these observations indicate that Arctic BC is largely influenced by regional-scale long-range transport rather than local point sources, consistent with the biomass-burning-dominated (C4 and C3) isotopic signatures preserved in Kongsfjorden sediments (Figure b,c). The 13% fossil-fuel contribution in the 2019 sediments likely reflects local combustion associated with power generation, heating, and transportation, including coal- and diesel-fired power plants in Longyearbyen and Barentsburg, as well as shipping- and tourism-related activities, which have intensified in Svalbard in recent years. , These results suggest that polar fjords differ in the spatial scale at which anthropogenic and natural BC sources are integrated into the coastal sediment record.

Burial Efficiency and Long-Term Preservation of Black Carbon in Antarctic Fjords

Marine sediments preserve combustion-derived BC over centennial to millennial time scales, making it essential to quantify BC inputs, burial efficiencies, and preservation dynamics when evaluating carbon cycling in polar coastal environments. Sediment trap deployments at Marian Cove yielded particle flux measurements spanning the deployment period (Table ). Average total mass flux was 12.5 g m–2 d–1, with a corresponding OC flux of 0.18 g m–2 d–1. These values fall within the established seasonal ranges reported for Marian Cove (total mass flux: 2.27–23.7 g m–2 d–1; OC flux: 0.05–0.24 g C m–2 d–1), confirming consistency with previously documented sedimentation characteristics. The measured PBC flux of 0.04 g m–2 d–1, equivalent to approximately 22% of the OC flux, indicates a substantial and persistent contribution of PBC to the settling OC pool.

Burial fluxes estimated using a sediment mass balance approach are presented as first-order, range-based values constrained by literature-derived modern sediment accumulation rates for Marian Cove. The OC burial flux was estimated to be 49.0–62.1 g m–2 yr–1 (Table ), broadly comparable to the global fjord average of ∼54 g m–2 yr–1 previously reported for fjord sediments. The resulting OC burial efficiency ranged from approximately 75% to 95% (Table ), with the upper bound reflecting uncertainty in sediment accumulation rates and the potential influence of sediment focusing and seasonal variability in flux estimates. Overall, these values overlap with and extend toward the upper range of burial efficiencies reported for the Bransfield Strait (60–80%). These results suggest that Marian Cove functions as an efficient OC sink, facilitated by rapid sedimentation and early association with mineral phases.

The SBC burial flux was estimated to be 13.0–16.6 g m–2 yr–1, with the burial efficiency of SBC ranging from 89–114% (Table ). Values exceeding 100% likely reflect uncertainties in flux estimates (e.g., underestimation of trap fluxes or additional lateral inputs). Nevertheless, SBC burial efficiency remained consistently higher than that of OC across the estimated range. This difference likely reflects distinct loss mechanisms prior to deposition rather than lower intrinsic recalcitrance of BC. An estimated 5–25% of OC loss is attributed primarily to microbial remineralization during particle sinking and early diagenesis, whereas the apparent ∼11% loss of SBC likely reflects predepositional removal of less-condensed aromatic BC fractions through photochemical oxidation and lateral transport within the water column.

This preferential preservation reflects differences in the migration and transformation between PBC and DBC, which form a biogeochemical continuum rather than independent pools. Highly condensed PBC is efficiently transferred to the seafloor via vertical settling, whereas less-condensed fractions are more prone to dissolution and remain as DBC. , These dissolved fractions undergo photochemical oxidation and lateral transport, leading to preferential removal prior to deposition. , As a result, BC reaching the sediment is selectively enriched in condensed PBC, and the sedimentary BC pool becomes dominated by highly condensed, refractory components with enhanced stability and resistance to diagenetic alteration. Although these process-based interpretations explain the observed preferential preservation, absolute fluxes remain uncertain because of seasonal variability and likely represent upper-bound estimates from austral summer measurements, with seasonally constrained fluxes reduced to ∼38% of the 365-day extrapolated values (Text S4; Table S6). By contrast, Δ­(SBC:OC) remains robust because shared uncertainties in SR and dry bulk density cancel in the ratio, isolating differential BC preservation. This is reflected in the elevated Δ­(SBC:OC) value of 1.20 (Table ), indicating preferential preservation of BC relative to OC during deposition and early diagenesis, consistent with the resistance of highly condensed aromatic BC to microbial degradation. This selective preservation provides a mechanistic basis for the long-term accumulation of BC in sediments and supports the interpretation of radiocarbon signatures as reflecting incorporation into a persistent refractory carbon pool rather than simple depositional age. Radiocarbon dating of BC isolated from surface sediments in Marian Cove yields apparent ages that reflect the integrated radiocarbon signature of a mixed BC pool rather than true sediment depositional ages. SBC exhibited apparent radiocarbon ages ranging from approximately 4700 to 5120 yr BP, with corresponding fraction modern (F m) values of 0.53–0.56 and Δ14C values of −475‰ to −447‰ (Table S5).

These results indicate that sedimentary BC resides in a long-lived, recalcitrant carbon pool that is largely resistant to diagenetic alteration. The millennial-scale apparent ages reflect mixing between radiocarbon-dead fossil BC and long-lived biospheric BC, with more recent anthropogenic inputs superimposed onto this refractory background, demonstrating that buried BC persists on time scales far exceeding those of labile marine organic matter. Notably, the apparent radiocarbon ages of BC in surface sediments from Marian Cove closely match the characteristic radiocarbon age of marine surface DBC, approximately 4500 ± 1200 years. , This similarity suggests rapid transfer of combustion-derived carbon from the surface water column to the seabed, likely facilitated by the shallow water depth and high particle flux characteristic of fjord systems. Such efficient vertical transport may minimize water-column reworking and enhance the effectiveness of fjords as long-term sinks for refractory carbon. These results suggest that fjord systems function as important long-term carbon reservoirs that sequester refractory, combustion-derived carbon in addition to bulk organic matter, highlighting the need to explicitly consider BC as an independent and persistent component in polar coastal carbon budgets.

Implications

Antarctic fjords function as efficient sinks for BC, linking cryosphere–ocean interactions with long-term sedimentary sequestration. Tidewater glacier discharge represents a primary delivery pathway, as evidenced by pronounced enrichment of DBC in fjord surface waters relative to open-ocean environments. Stable carbon isotope signatures (δ13C values averaging −25.3‰ in Antarctic fjords compared to −19.2‰ in Arctic fjords), together with BPCA molecular characteristics, indicate that fjord BC pools in the Antarctic Peninsula are dominated by locally sourced anthropogenic inputs rather than photochemically aged biomass-burning aerosols. Sediment records from Marian Cove document a 2.7-fold increase in fossil-fuel-derived BC between 2019 and 2023, consistent with the postpandemic resurgence of tourism and research activities in the Antarctic Peninsula region. This result indicates that BC inventories in coastal fjord sediments can respond on multiyear time scales to short-term fluctuations in local anthropogenic emissions. In contrast, Arctic fjords exhibit BC source regimes primarily influenced by regional-scale long-range atmospheric transport associated with large-scale biomass burning. In particular, the exceptionally intense 2019 Siberian wildfire season burned approximately 7.2 million hectares across regions including Yakutia, a pattern consistent with the dominance of biomass-burning-derived BC preserved in Kongsfjorden sediments. The contrast highlights fundamental differences in the spatial scales at which anthropogenic and natural BC sources are integrated into polar fjord sediment records. Millennial-scale radiocarbon apparent ages (4700–5120 yr BP), together with elevated Δ­(SBC:OC) values, indicate that sedimentary BC is preferentially preserved within long-lived refractory carbon pools. These apparent ages reflect incorporation of newly deposited BC into a pre-existing sedimentary reservoir rather than simple depositional age, consistent with selective preservation during particle sinking and early diagenesis. Shallow water depths and high particle fluxes characteristic of fjord systems further enhance BC sequestration by promoting rapid vertical transfer and limiting water-column reworking. Although fjords occupy a relatively small fraction of the global coastal ocean, they contribute disproportionately to marine carbon burial. Our results identify BC as a previously underappreciated yet dynamically responsive component of polar carbon sequestration and highlight the need to incorporate BC into polar coastal carbon budgets to better assess anthropogenic impacts on carbon cycling in rapidly changing polar environments.
