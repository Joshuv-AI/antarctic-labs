Superficial external pudendal artery pseudoaneurysm following cardiac catheterization: A rare case report and literature review
Authors: Muhammad Farhan, Nadeem A Burney, Shaima S Alqrain, Mohamed Baguneid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIatrogenic pseudoaneurysms are recognized complications of arterial catheterization, most commonly involving the common femoral artery, while pseudoaneurysms arising from the superficial external pudendal artery (SEPA) are exceedingly rare. We present the case of an 86-year-old male who developed a right groin pseudoaneurysm originating from the SEPA following right femoral artery catheterization for attempted direct current cardioversion and atrial fibrillation ablation, diagnosed by computed tomography angiography after an initial negative duplex ultrasound. The pseudoaneurysm was successfully treated with ultrasound-guided thrombin injection, achieving immediate thrombosis. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for pseudoaneurysms arising from atypical arterial branches, the complementary role of computed tomography angiography when duplex ultrasound is inconclusive, and the efficacy of thrombin injection as a minimally invasive treatment for small pseudoaneurysms in uncommon locations.Keywords: Pseudoaneurysm, Superficial external pudendal artery, Cardiac catheterization, Thrombin injection, Computed tomography angiography, Duplex ultrasound

Introduction

An arterial pseudoaneurysm is a disruption in the arterial wall in which blood collects outside the layers of the wall and is contained by the surrounding tissues. In an active pseudoaneurysm, a fibrin sac develops that contains the pseudoaneurysm but also allows it to persist by separating it from extracellular signals that would often induce thrombosis [1].

Iatrogenic pseudoaneurysms are known to occur with varying frequencies. After diagnostic arterial catheterization, the incidence ranges from 0.44% to 1.8%, while following interventional procedures, the incidence increases to 3.2% to 7.7%. When specifically considering femoral access, the most common access site for catheterization, the incidence ranges from 2.9% to 3.8% [1]. The incidence of pseudoaneurysms involving the external pudendal artery varies considerably, with reported rates ranging from as low as 0.05% to as high as 7.7% based on different studies [2].

Iatrogenic pseudoaneurysms in the superficial external pudendal artery (SEPA) are exceptionally rare. Prior to this report, only 2 documented instances of iatrogenic pseudoaneurysms occurring in the SEPA have been reported in the literature [3,4].

The objective of this case report is to describe a rare instance of an iatrogenic SEPA pseudoaneurysm following cardiac catheterization and to review the available literature on the diagnosis and management of this uncommon vascular complication.

Case presentation

An 86-year-old male was referred to our department from the Cardiology Unit due to a right groin hematoma. This complication occurred after an attempted direct current cardioversion and a failed atrial fibrillation ablation procedure, during which the right femoral artery was catheterized. On examination, the patient presented with a nonpulsatile groin hematoma with minimal tenderness in the affected area. Distal pulses in the lower extremity remained intact.

Initial duplex ultrasound showed no definite evidence of a pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula. However, computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a well-defined, homogeneously enhancing oval structure in the right inguinal region, measuring 1.5 × 1.3 cm, suggestive of a pseudoaneurysm possibly arising from the superficial external pudendal artery, a branch of the distal femoral artery (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Axial computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the pelvis with contrast enhancement demonstrating the right femoral artery (red arrow) and an adjacent enhancing oval structure representing the pseudoaneurysm (red arrow), located in the right inguinal region.

Axial computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the pelvis at a slightly different level, further delineating the relationship between the femoral artery (yellow arrow) and the pseudoaneurysm (yellow arrow) measuring approximately 1.5 × 1.3 cm, suggestive of origin from the superficial external pudendal artery.

Based on the CTA findings, the patient underwent ultrasound-guided thrombin injection for the right groin pseudoaneurysm. The pseudoaneurysm was confirmed as arising from the external pudendal artery, located between the right great saphenous vein and the right femoral vein, with a measured diameter of 1.6 cm. A 20-gauge spinal needle was carefully inserted into the pseudoaneurysm under ultrasound guidance, and a total of 1000 units of thrombin were precisely injected into the false aneurysm. Instant thrombosis was observed, confirming successful closure of the pseudoaneurysm. Following the procedure, a thorough evaluation confirmed the patency of the right superficial femoral artery and femoral vein, and a strong, palpable foot pulse was noted, indicating satisfactory vascular perfusion.

Discussion

Arterial pseudoaneurysms are infrequent yet significant vascular conditions with varying incidence rates, most often arising from invasive arterial interventions. The identification of risk factors such as improper arterial access, multiple punctures, and extended procedural duration is essential for prevention.

Known risk factors for iatrogenic pseudoaneurysm formation include improper access site, multiple punctures, insufficient postprocedural compression [5], larger sized angiographic sheath, prolonged procedure duration, antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy, female sex, older age (greater than 75 years), calcified arteries, high body mass index, thrombocytopenia [6], and arterial hypertension. Notably, our patient was an 86-year-old male with several of these risk factors, including advanced age and the use of anticoagulant therapy.

Common causes of iatrogenic pseudoaneurysms include arterial catheterization performed for diagnostic or therapeutic interventions, complications arising from percutaneous hemodialysis access, percutaneous intra-aortic balloon pump placement, trauma, intravenous drug injections, breakdown of surgical arterial anastomosis, and complications of central line placement [7].

Duplex ultrasound remains the cornerstone of pseudoaneurysm diagnosis, with reported sensitivity and specificity of 94% and 97%, respectively [8]. Characteristic findings include the yin-yang sign, representing blood flow in a cystic structure with a swirling motion pattern (Fig. 3), and the “to-and-fro” waveform at the communicating neck between the arterial vessel and pseudoaneurysmal sac [9] (Fig. 4). However, as demonstrated in our case, duplex ultrasound may fail to detect pseudoaneurysms arising from smaller branch arteries such as the SEPA. CTA serves as a valuable complementary diagnostic tool, particularly when ultrasound findings are equivocal or negative despite clinical suspicion [10]. Angiography is generally reserved for atypical cases and during potential interventions [10].

Management strategies for pseudoaneurysms differ based on their size, stability, and anatomic characteristics. For small pseudoaneurysms measuring less than 2 cm, observation with serial ultrasound surveillance may suffice, especially if the pseudoaneurysm demonstrates no increase in size or symptoms [11]. Pseudoaneurysms with neck length less than 0.9 cm and higher flow volumes are less likely to thrombose with conservative management [11]. Ultrasound-guided compression is considered the initial therapy for pseudoaneurysms greater than 2 cm and less than 4 cm in size that are less than 1 month old, with success rates ranging from 60% to 93% [2,11]. Ultrasound-guided thrombin injection has become the standard of care for treating pseudoaneurysms in stable patients with appropriate anatomy, with success rates of 93% to 100% reported in the literature [7,12]. In our case, 1000 units of thrombin were injected, which falls within the reported effective range of 300 to 4000 units [7]. The advantages of thrombin injection include minimal discomfort, high efficacy, the ability to use in patients on anticoagulant therapy, and efficacy in both acute and chronic pseudoaneurysms [12]. Endovascular intervention is considered for patients who have failed compression and/or thrombin injection or who are not candidates for these procedures, while open surgery is reserved for complex pseudoaneurysms that have ruptured, involve infection, or have failed less invasive therapies [7].

The SEPA is a branch that arises from the medial aspect of the common femoral artery near its bifurcation and courses superficially toward the perineal region [3] (Fig. 5). Pseudoaneurysms originating from this vessel are exceptionally rare, with only 2 previously documented cases in the literature [3,4]. The rarity of this location poses diagnostic challenges, as standard duplex ultrasound protocols may not specifically evaluate this branch vessel. Our case further supports the utility of CTA in identifying pseudoaneurysms from atypical arterial branches when clinical suspicion persists despite negative initial ultrasound findings.

A limitation of the present case report is the absence of dedicated intraprocedural and postprocedural images, as the ultrasound-guided thrombin injection was performed as an urgent bedside procedure without formal procedural image documentation. Future reports of SEPA pseudoaneurysms should aim to document the complete procedural imaging sequence to further characterize the management of this rare entity.

The case underscores the importance of prompt diagnosis and individualized treatment for peripheral pseudoaneurysms, advocating for a standardized medical approach to optimize patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This case underscores the clinical significance of recognizing pseudoaneurysms arising from atypical arterial branches such as the SEPA following femoral artery catheterization. Computed tomography angiography serves as a valuable adjunct when duplex ultrasound is inconclusive, and ultrasound-guided thrombin injection offers an effective, minimally invasive treatment for such pseudoaneurysms.
