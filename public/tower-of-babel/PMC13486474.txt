SplitSeek-Pro: accurate prediction of splittable sites on protein structures
Authors: Yu-Xiang Wang, Fengyi Jiang, Tingjie Xu, Lan-Xin Xing, Yajie Liu, Zhiwei Nie, Jie Chen, Wen-Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding protein architecture and predicting its structural tolerance to profound remodeling is pivotal for engineering functional proteins. We present SplitSeek-Pro, a deep learning model that evaluates amino acid-level splittability in folded proteins, a property critical for protein engineering tasks such as circular permutation and split reconstitution. By integrating primary sequences with 3D structural features, SplitSeek-Pro achieves residue-resolution predictions through a two-stage training process: large-scale pre-training followed by high-quality fine-tuning. Experimental validation on three distinct proteins confirms its superior predictive power over existing methods. Notably, SplitSeek-Pro identifies characteristic segments that function as cohesive, integral fragments analogous to super-secondary structural motifs. These results establish SplitSeek-Pro as a robust tool for rational protein engineering and offer insights into the fundamental structural building blocks of protein folding. To facilitate community access, we provide an automated web server at http://splitseek.topo.bio.Subject terms: Protein design, Machine learning

Introduction

Sequence is the most fundamental feature of a protein, which determines the overall protein fold and function. In analogy to semantics, the protein sequence (or language) can be hierarchically categorized into various structural levels (i.e., primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures), akin to words, sentences, and paragraphs in literature1. Protein engineering is thus much like “rephrasing” of the protein language. Structure-based rational strategies, such as circular permutation2–4, splitting-reconstitution5, and rewiring6–8, are widely practiced in this endeavor9–13, mirroring syntactic variations such as inversion and intertextuality. However, successful “rephrasing” requires identifying precise locations where the protein sequence can be safely interrupted or rearranged without compromising the overall folded structure and function. To guide this process, various computational approaches have been developed.

Previously, to systematically identify natural proteins that have undergone such changes during evolution, several computational approaches have been developed, including sequence-based tools like SeqCP14 and plmCP15, as well as structure-aware frameworks like CPSARST16, CE-CP17, and ICARUS18. As the field has evolved from identifying existing natural variants to the rational design of novel proteins, several specialized methods have emerged to expand this engineering paradigm. CPred19, for instance, is an online server that predicts circular permutation sites using traditional machine learning on input structures. Computationally, SPELL20 models energy landscapes and physicochemical properties to identify viable split sites, while a machine learning method named Int&In21 was also developed to predict the feasible split sites of inteins. Experimentally, HiTS22 offers a high-throughput screening platform for identifying strongly interacting split protein pairs. Despite these advances, the accuracy, efficiency, and generalizability of existing methods remain suboptimal for the reliable identification of these backbone split sites. Addressing these limitations is not only practically valuable for diverse engineering tasks but also fundamental to understanding the mechanistic rules of protein folding.

Deep learning has recently revolutionized protein science. Tools like AlphaFold223 and AlphaFold324 excel in structure prediction, while protein language models like ESM-225 and ProteinBERT26, as well as integrated sequence-structure frameworks like ESM-327 and SimpleFold28, provide rich representations for protein design. While these are computationally intensive, scalable generative models like RFdiffusion29 and ProteinMPNN30 have successfully streamlined diverse protein engineering tasks31–36. However, the translation of deep learning success to generalized backbone splitting prediction is currently bottlenecked by the requirement for massive, high-quality datasets. Direct split-reconstitution datasets remain scarce and are heavily convoluted by factors like peptide solubility and intermolecular affinity. Fortunately, circular permutation data are relatively abundant. Because both circular permutation and split-reconstitution share similar prerequisite—local structural stability following backbone disruption—circular permutation data serves as an excellent data proxy. We envision that deep learning models can be trained on such proxies and offer a powerful tool to guide protein engineering involving backbone splitting.

Herein, we introduce SplitSeek-Pro, a deep learning-based prediction model designed to identify splittable residues in folded proteins by integrating sequence and 3D structural information via a message-passing neural network. The model, trained in a two-stage pretraining and fine-tuning process, provides accurate predictions for sites of high structural permissiveness to backbone splitting, such as circular permutation and split-reconstitution, with superior performances over existing methods as evidenced by wet-lab experimental validation on three model proteins.

Results

Design of the deep learning model

The protein sequence provides a complete blueprint for structure and function, suggesting that an end-to-end prediction of splittable sites should, in principle, be feasible from sequence alone. However, the absence of structural context often limits predictive accuracy. Although modern protein language models are capable of inferring structural properties, their complex architectures and substantial training requirements pose practical challenges. To address this, we argue that an integrated sequence-structure approach offers a more effective pathway. Guided by this rationale, we developed SplitSeek-Pro (Fig. 1), a deep learning model for accurately predicting the splitting probability at each residue site by incorporating both sequence and structural information. A message-passing neural network is used to encode proteins. Each residue is represented as a node, where node features are integrated from ESM-2 embeddings and physicochemical characterizations. Edges are established between adjacent residue pairs, with their features encoded based on the inter-atomic distances between residue pairs. By defining a threshold for neighboring residues, we construct a graph that captures the overall structural network of the protein. Within this graph, messages are iteratively passed and integrated along nodes and edges.

The architecture of SplitSeek-Pro.

The model takes protein sequence and structural features as input, updates node and edge features through a message-passing network, and predicts the splitting probability for each residue site.

The edge features include a pairwise distance description matrix for selected atoms (N, CA, C, O, and CB) within each residue. The initialization of node features incorporates two key components: embeddings generated from the pre-trained ESM-2 model25 and normalized physicochemical property values derived from the AAindex database37. This hybrid initialization enhances the model’s ability to represent both sequence- and structure-derived information. The architecture of SplitSeek-Pro consists of three encoder layers, three decoder layers, and 128 hidden dimensions. Node and edge messages are continuously updated and refined during the encoding process while the edge features do not update in the decoding process. A sigmoid activation function is applied to the decoder outputs to generate predictions of splitting probability for each residue. By combining robust feature extraction with an efficient graph-based framework, SplitSeek-Pro achieves accurate and interpretable predictions for splittable sites which are useful for a variety of splitting-based protein engineering strategies.

Dataset construction

Data collection and curation are fundamental to developing predictive models. In protein engineering, the “splittability” of a residue site can be assessed under various experimental contexts. Ideally, a site is deemed splittable if the resulting fragments retain the ability to fold independently and reassemble via non-covalent interactions, as exemplified by systems such as SpyTag/SpyCatcher38 and GFP1–10/GFP1139. Conversely, a site is considered nonsplittable if fragmentation disrupts the structural integrity or functional recovery of the protein. Frequently, split protein pairs rely on external interaction modules to enhance spatial proximity for efficient reconstitution, as seen in GFP1–7/GFP8–1140 and KanRN/KanRC pairs41. Circular permutation represents a special case in this paradigm, where linker peptides are inserted to physically constrain the original N- and C-termini and maintain spatial proximity. Notably, certain circular permutation sites coincide with effective splitting sites identified in split-reconstitution systems, such as position 212 in green fluorescent protein39,42 and position 107 in dihydrofolate reductase3,43. This overlap suggests that both phenomena share similar physicochemical principles, which is consistent with literature report that circular permutation prediction also facilitates the design of split proteins44. While data on split proteins remain rather limited, circular permutation are more widely documented, both in natural proteins and engineered variants. Integrating both scenarios into a unified model offers a robust framework for evaluating residue-level splittability. Hence, we seek to construct a comprehensive dataset by collecting such amino acid positions from proteins known to exhibit these traits as the training data for our predictive model.

The limited availability of experimental data for split-based protein engineering strategies presents a significant challenge for training deep learning models. To address this, we adopted a two-stage strategy: pretraining the model with pseudo-labeled data and fine-tuning it with experimentally validated data. To generate a large-scale dataset for pretraining, we leveraged computational predictions to create pseudo-labels. Specifically, we utilized the SPELL tool20, which predicts residue splittability based on secondary structure types, sequence conservation, solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) and split energy. Since SPELL provides qualitative rankings rather than probabilistic labels, we recalculated these parameters using open-source tools to generate quantitative pseudo-labels. We calculated split energy by the Rosetta score function45,46. Secondary structure types and SASA values come from DSSP (Dictionary of Secondary Structure of Protein) program47,48. As for sequence conservation, a reported neural network model was used for its calculation in order to speed up the process49. Each residue was assigned a weighted combination of these parameters to produce a probability value representing the likelihood of successful splitting. This process generated pseudo-labels for 22,538 proteins, forming a comprehensive pretraining dataset.

To refine model accuracy and ensure alignment with real-world experimental data, we utilized the Circular Permutation Database (CPDB)50, which catalogs circular permuted protein pairs identified through structural alignment in the PDB database. After filtering and optimizing the dataset, we curated 3542 pairs of circular permuted proteins. Split residue sites were extracted based on pre-existing structure alignment retrieved directly from the CPDB, with these sites’ probability labeled as 1.00. To balance positive and negative samples, an equal number of residues from rigid secondary structures (α-helix or β-sheet) were randomly selected and labeled with a splitting probability of 0.00. Residues without definitive labels were masked during training, ensuring that their contributions were excluded from backpropagation loss calculations. This approach mitigates noise in the fine-tuning dataset and ensures robust model optimization. By combining pseudo-labeled and experimentally validated data, this two-stage strategy enables SplitSeek-Pro to achieve both generalization and precision in predicting splittable residue sites.

Model performance

For both training stages, the model parameters associated with the minimum loss on the validation set were selected as the final model for subsequent testing (Supplementary Fig. 1). The performance of SplitSeek-Pro was evaluated computationally following the two-stage training process: pretraining and fine-tuning. During pretraining, the model demonstrated effective fitting for residue sites with label scores below 0.50, reflecting its capacity to capture essential features such as protein energy and sequence conservation based on the input data (Fig. 2a). However, the uneven distribution of labeled data constrained the model’s ability to predict high-scoring positions. After fine-tuning, the model achieved a marked improvement, demonstrating an improved ability to distinguish between high- and low-scoring positions (Fig. 2b). To reveal the factors influencing model training, we assessed the effects of varying graph connectivity. Increasing the number of connections in the graph to more than 32 improved the model’s performance in predicting high-scoring positions (Fig. 2c). Notably, pretraining significantly contributed to the model’s overall performance by enhancing its ability to distinguish splittable sites from nonsplittable ones (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b).

A detailed analysis of the distribution of the prediction scores for residues in the test set revealed notable trends. Residues in rigid secondary structures, such as α-helices and β-sheets, tend to have lower prediction scores, whereas loop regions exhibit a broad distribution of scores (Fig. 2e). This result is consistent with conventional wisdom in protein engineering, where loop regions are often regarded as potential targets for circular permutation or split-reconstitution. The model’s capacity to distinguish splittable loop regions is thus advantageous for designing protein circular permutants or split protein pairs. Notably, the model tends to assign lower scores to terminal residues which are supposed to be completely splittable. This counterintuitive phenomenon is actually a pattern inherited from the pretraining stage and does not get better after fine-tuning (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 2g), which presents a potential limitation and leaves room for further improvement in accurately predicting splitting sites at these terminal positions.

To elucidate the contributions of sequence and structural information to the model’s predictive performance, ablation studies were conducted. When structural features were used exclusively, the resulting ablation model produced ambiguous predictions for high-scoring splittable sites, with scores dispersed across a wide range (Fig. 2g, h and Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). Loop regions, in particular, exhibited consistently high scores, rendering it difficult to differentiate splittable sites from nonsplittable ones (Fig. 2h). Conversely, the model, which relied solely on sequence features, identified more low-scoring sites but occasionally misclassified residues in rigid secondary structures as splittable (Fig. 2g, h and Supplementary Fig. 2e, f). In comparison, the full SplitSeek-Pro model, which integrates both sequence and structural information, demonstrated superior performance. By providing clear distinctions between high- and low-scoring positions, the full-input model avoided over-reliance on secondary structure types and effectively captured the nuanced variability within loop regions (Fig. 2h). These results underscore the importance of combining sequence and structural features for accurate predictions and highlight the model’s potential power in guiding rational protein engineering. To validate the effectiveness of the SplitSeek-Pro model, we selected model proteins and conducted related wet-lab experiments for both circular permutation and split-reconstitution scenarios.

Experimental validation of circular permutation

Circular permutation is an appropriate way to evaluate the accuracy of SplitSeek-Pro, as the model was fine-tuned mostly on circular permutation data. Moreover, the wet-lab experimentation is convenient since the resulting permutants are full-length, linear proteins whose structural integrity can be assessed through their functional performance. Two model proteins were chosen, a leaf-branch compost cutinase variant (LCCICCG, abbreviated as LCC, which is an engineered polyethylene glycol terephthalate hydrolase51) and a cyclohexanone monooxygenase (CHMO, which catalyzes the Bayer-Villiger oxygenation of cyclohexanone52), both of which possess distinct folds and share low sequence identity and structure similarity in the existing training dataset to show the generality of the SplitSeek-Pro method. Their N- and C-termini are also in close spatial proximity so as to facilitate their connection via flexible linkers with minimal influence on the experimental results53. To ensure the representativeness of the selected sites, we implemented a constraint where no two selected sites reside within the same continuous secondary structure element. We designed their circular permutants following SplitSeek-Pro’s recommendation of splittable sites (21 for LCC and 29 for CHMO, see Supplementary Fig. 7a, b and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 for details) and conducted wet-lab experiments for validation. For the linker connecting the original N- and C- termini, we estimated an appropriate length based on the distance and orientation of the termini, and further validated the design using AlphaFold2.

For LCC, using a score threshold of 0.50, the circular permutants were categorized into a positive group (high-scoring sites, 9 variants) and a negative group (low-scoring sites, 12 variants) (Fig. 3a). The prediction score represents the splitting probability between the current residue site and the next one. All sites in positive groups are located in loop regions. Within the negative group, seven sites are located in loop regions, while five are found in rigid secondary structures such as α-helices or β-sheets. As revealed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 8), all variants in the positive group exhibit soluble expression and only one shows noticeable aggregation. By contrast, five variants from the negative group show no observable soluble expression, and the remaining variants exhibit a much higher proportion of aggregation tendencies (Fig. 3c, d). Circular dichroism (CD) spectra suggest the retention of secondary structure to varying extents (Supplementary Fig. 10). Melting temperature (Tm) assay can also be used to characterize the integrity of the folded structure. The results show that all soluble constructs exhibit relatively high Tm values, indicating minimal perturbation to the hydrophobic core (Supplementary Table 1). Functional assay using the hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl acetate shows that all variants in the positive group retain hydrolytic activity, with the exception of LCC-CP125, which displayed considerable aggregation (Fig. 3e, f and Supplementary Fig. 13a, b) and most variants in the negative group, except one variant (LCC-CP136), lost their activity. Furthermore, the polyethylene terephthalate (PET) degradation activity of these circular permutants was characterized under high-temperature conditions. As expected, their hydrolytic performances were generally consistent with their model substrate hydrolysis activity (Supplementary Fig. 14). The minor discrepancy may be attributed to the mismatch of structural intactness and functional integrity: a folded structure may lose function due to local disturbance near the active site, and a partially folded structure may remain active despite the disruption occurring far from the active site. For example, the LCC-CP136 is circularly permutated at site 136 within the α-helix at the periphery of the fold opposite to the active site. It shows a decrease in α-helix proportion (see Supplementary Fig. 10l for CD spectrum), but remains catalytically active. By saying so, the model was trained on a dataset with very limited representation of splittable sites within rigid secondary structures, which makes it challenging to identify such splittable sites.

For CHMO, the variants were similarly divided into the positive and negative groups (Fig. 4a). Nine out of 15 variants in the positive group shows soluble expression and only one soluble variant, CHMO-CP292, exhibits considerable degree of aggregation in SEC (Fig. 4b, c and Supplementary Fig. 9). Among the 14 variants in the negative group, five fail to express solubly and the remaining nine variants all show significant level of oligomerization in SEC (Fig. 4c, d). CD spectra further suggest that the soluble variants largely retain their secondary structural features (Supplementary Fig. 11), consistent with the Tm assay. However, two of them (CHMO-CP47 and CHMO-CP368) show undetectable Tm, suggesting their unstable states. Among these soluble variants, only certain variants can directly bind with the flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor, as reflected by the absorption peak at 435 nm (Supplementary Fig. 12). This is actually a prerequisite for the enzyme’s activity. Subsequently, we used the Bayer-Villiger oxygenation of cyclohexanone as a model reaction and monitored nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) consumption to determine the activity of selected variants (Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Fig. 13c, d). The results show that the variants in the positive group generally exhibit activity, with the exception of CHMO-CP292, which fails to bind with the FAD cofactor. Interestingly, although some variants in the negative group can bind with the FAD cofactor (CHMO-CP224, CHMO-CP235, CHMO-CP278, CHMO-CP433, and CHMO-CP486), their activity is very low (<10% of the wild-type). Structural analysis reveals that these sites are located in close proximity to the cyclohexanone-binding pocket. This result suggests that, although splitting these sites does not disrupt cofactor binding, it likely severely impacts the substrate binding pocket, leading to significant structural changes in the pocket and almost complete loss of activity.

While all circular permutants suffered some activity loss, their thermal stability largely matched that of the wild-type (as reflected in their similar Tm values, Supplementary Table 2), ruling out global destabilization as the main cause for compromised catalytic performance. Instead, the data imply that subtle structural shifts markedly impair catalytic function. These experimental results validate the high accuracy of SplitSeek-Pro and establish its ability to guide the design of circular permutation, though occasional discrepancies in α-helices and β-strands reveal specific targets for model improvement. Further refinement is needed, particularly for splits near termini and within rigid secondary structures, underscoring the need for specialized training data. Notably, the higher proportion of insoluble and aggregated variants in the negative group suggests a potential correlation between nonsplittable sites and aggregation-prone regions in proteins, which merits future investigation.

Experimental validation on split and reconstitution

Having validated the ability of SplitSeek-Pro in designing circular permutants, we next assessed its capability in generating split protein pairs—a challenge often compounded by fragment aggregation, insoluble expression, and kinetic trapping into metastable states. Rather than extensively screening all possible pairs and empirically optimizing each fragment, we asked whether SplitSeek-Pro could guide us to find useful split protein pairs that are otherwise difficult to find. For this purpose, nano luciferase (NanoLuc), a class of luciferase with small sizes and bright luminescence, was chosen as a model system. Not only does it show low sequence identity with current training set sequences, but also the resulting split NanoLuc pairs are versatile tools for studying protein-protein interactions54.

Previous studies have identified functional NanoLuc splits at residues 65 and 158, with non-functional variants at 76, 87, and 13755,56. These findings align well with predictions from SplitSeek-Pro. Based on its guidance, we selected four new sites for experimental testing: two high-scoring sites (37 and 135), a low-scoring site (113), and a promising site 52, which was chosen due to the high scores of its flanking residues (Fig. 5a). To evaluate fragment reassembly under both spontaneous and forced association conditions, we designed two experimental systems: one relying solely on intrinsic fragment interaction, and another employing the SpyTag/SpyCatcher system to tether the fragments (Fig. 5b)38,57. To avoid active-site interference, SpyTag and SpyCatcher were fused to the N- and C-termini of the large and small fragments of NanoLuc, respectively, away from the catalytic pocket. Additionally, an N-terminal GFP fusion was introduced to mitigate the known solubility issue of the large fragment55.

SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed soluble expression for all constructs except A-113N, though the expression yields were rather low (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). The corresponding split NanoLuc pairs were mixed and the activity of the reconstituted NanoLuc was then assessed by monitoring the luminescence at 466 nm generated from the catalyzed oxidation of coelenterazine. Among the tested pairs, the predicted high-scoring pairs 52N/53C and 135N/136C both exhibit detectable luminescence, indicating successful and spontaneous reconstitution (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 17). In the presence of the SpyTag/SpyCatcher system, these fragments reassembled more rapidly, with catalytic activity peaking within ~4 min after mixing, followed by a plateau and slight decline (Fig. 5d). Since SplitSeek-Pro was trained primarily on circular permutant data where the split fragments remain connected via a linker, it is reasonable that even high-scoring predictions may still require external assistance to promote reconstitution in vitro. Intriguingly, despite its high prediction score, the 37N/38C pair showed negligible luminescence even when reinforced by SpyTag/SpyCatcher interaction. This failure likely stems from the fundamental thermodynamic discrepancy between the two engineering strategies. In circular permutation, the covalent linker acts as a chaperone, allowing the protein to achieve a functional state as long as the fragments possess sufficient molten globule stability. In contrast, the reconstitution of split protein pairs must overcome a much higher thermodynamic barrier, particularly the translational entropy penalty. This transition requires either exceptionally high binding affinity between the two components or superior intrinsic stability of individual fragment. While SplitSeek-Pro is proficient at identifying splittable sites that minimize global structural disruption, its ability to predict the spontaneous reassembly potential of isolated fragments remains limited. This limitation highlights the need for more extensive and specific datasets to fine-tune future iterations of the model. Nevertheless, the current model provides reasonably accurate and practical predictions for identifying viable split-reconstitution sites (Fig. 5e), which is further supported by additional examples compiled from literature on split protein pairs (Supplementary Table 4).

Performance evaluation of SplitSeek-Pro

To evaluate SplitSeek-Pro’s performance, we compared it against existing tools such as CPred19 and Int&in21, using a consistent set of 54 experimental examples derived from LCC, CHMO, and NanoLuc studies. The experimental results were classified as true or false based on a 10% activity retention threshold, and the distribution of their corresponding prediction scores was statistically analyzed. CPred defines a feasible circular permutation site as one with a score above 0.85, while Int&in uses a threshold of 0.50 to distinguish the splittable and nonsplittable sites19,21. As shown in Fig. 5f, SplitSeek-Pro performs comparably to CPred in identifying true positives but exhibits significantly better performance in rejecting false positives, particularly for impractical split sites. Although Int&in shows some discrimination capability, it frequently misclassifies non-viable sites as viable. The advantage of SplitSeek-Pro is most pronounced in loop regions, where most split sites are located.

Beyond the initial dataset, extra data points were integrated from diverse studies to form a robust validation suite9,13,20,40,42,58–70. We re-screened these entries against our training data to discard any redundant sites. The final evaluation was performed on an individual test dataset of 531 refined data points to validate the model’s predictive ability for circular permutation and split-reconstitution sites (full predicted results are available in Supplementary Data 1). The classification ability of different models was then evaluated using standard computational metrics. As shown in Table 1, SplitSeek-Pro consistently outperformed both CPred and Int&in in terms of accuracy (ACC), area under the curve (AUC), F1-Score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). The higher F1-score indicates a better balance between precision and recall, while the increased MCC confirms a more balanced predictive performance. For SPELL, due to the incomplete availability of the predictive output, we benchmarked its performance using a representative subset of the independent test set. Venn diagram results demonstrate that while both methods show comparable performance in recommending core sites, SplitSeek-Pro identifies a more extensive set of potential split sites that remain functionally active in practical assays (Supplementary Fig. 3). Finally, we tested the utility of existing protein structure prediction tools (such as AlphaFold2 and ESMFold) for this specific task and found them incapable of distinguishing feasible circular permutation sites (Supplementary Fig. 4). These results demonstrate that SplitSeek-Pro provides a measurable improvement over existing benchmarks.

Despite the improved performance of our model, it still produces certain false positives and false negatives (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). False positives often involve surface-exposed residues that, despite high solvent accessibility and not part of the hydrophobic core, are critical for structural stability. For instance, residues 102, 112, 132, 203, and 422 in CHMO are located on the protein surface yet essential for structural integrity; their splitting leads to severe destabilization and insoluble products. Additionally, some residues distant from the active site may exert a “ghost effect” on catalysis, as observed for residue 125 in LCC. Circular permutation at these high-scoring sites is anticipated to yield proteins that retain their overall native structure despite a complete loss of function. To mitigate such false positives, we recommend considering a site as reliably splittable only when at least two or three consecutive residues score above 0.80. Conversely, false negatives commonly stem from the model’s difficulty in accurately assessing rigid secondary structures. For example, residue 136 in LCC lies within such a region but has been experimentally validated as splittable. Similar examples include residues 124–130 and 145–153 in old yellow enzyme60, which do not contribute directly to the folding core, yet permit successful splitting. These observations underscore the need for a more diverse and higher-quality training dataset to further enhance the model’s discrimination capability.

Significance of SplitSeek-Pro

The high accuracy of SplitSeek-Pro enables systematic investigation of the sequence and structural features of splittable sites and the corresponding fragments in folded proteins. To evaluate the model while accounting for both sequence and structural influences, we utilized the SCOPe database (Structural Classification of Proteins-extended) and employed a 40% sequence identity threshold as the primary selection criterion71,72. Based on AUC analysis, a threshold of 0.50 was established as optimal for discriminating high- from low-scoring sites, which is consistent with conventional classification benchmarks. Based on the standard, we examined the relationship between site scores and both SASA and sequence conservation (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 5), revealing that low-scoring sites generally exhibit lower SASA and higher sequence conservation than high-scoring sites. This is consistent with the expectation that residues buried in the hydrophobic core are less tolerant to splitting. Notably, in the analysis of LCC and CHMO, SplitSeek-Pro consistently assigned low scores to residues near the active site (e.g., 163, 189, 210, 240 in LCC; 188, 278, 381, 433 in CHMO), despite their location in loop regions. This observation suggests that structural context alone is insufficient to explain these predictions, and we infer that SplitSeek-Pro likely leverages evolutionary information from the protein language model to identify functionally constrained sites through their conserved sequence features.

SplitSeek-Pro enables systematic analysis of supersecondary structural motifs within tertiary structures by delineating protein fragments based on high-scoring, consensus splitting sites (>0.50). The resulting fragment population, characterized by the number of secondary structural elements (Supplementary Fig. 6), shows a predominance of multi-element motifs with frequency declining as the number of secondary structural elements (n) increases. We also assessed structural similarity among fragments with n = 2–4 using Foldseek73 and identified core clusters via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) dimensionality reduction and k-means clustering (Fig. 6). For n = 2, three major clusters emerged: β-β corners, α-containing corners, and the less common α-β arrangements. The majority of α-containing structures belong to the β-α corner structures, suggesting their crucial role in tertiary structure formation. A marked scarcity of α-α corner was observed, a trend consistent across n = 3 and n = 4 fragments, suggesting that α-helices form with relative structural autonomy, whereas β-sheets tend to associate cooperatively with other elements. For n > 2, known supersecondary structural motifs, such as all-β and α-β alternating motifs, were readily identified, collectively constituting a library of SplitSeek-Pro fragments that occupy a structural niche between secondary motifs and tertiary folds. To assess their functional relevance, we examined proteins with experimentally resolved folding pathways (Supplementary Fig. 18) and found that early-folding units—such as β3-β4 in IgG-binding B1 domain of protein G (GB1), β2-β3-β4 in the SRC homology 3 domain (SH3), and α1-α2 in the lysin domain (LysM)—consistently localize within SplitSeek-Pro fragments and exhibit low splitting scores in between their constituent elements74. This correlation highlights that these fragments often behave as cooperative structural modules during the folding process, supporting their critical role as integral building blocks to drive tertiary structure formation.

SplitSeek-Pro fragments resonate with prior efforts to catalog protein structural motifs, such as the tertiary structural motifs (TERMs) database75,76. Unlike conventional methods that rely on continuous backbone geometry, TERMs introduce a fragment-centric strategy in defining structural units around a central residue and its spatial interaction partners. Through a greedy accumulation algorithm, it was shown that only a small set of 625 TERMs are sufficient to describe half of the known protein structure universe75. A fundamental distinction lies in their design: TERMs are derived solely from backbone geometry, while SplitSeek-Pro integrates both sequence and structural information into account. The resulting SplitSeek-Pro fragments are typically compact with strongly correlated secondary elements, though exceptions occur in the form of loose and extended segments that may serve as “linkers” between more rigid structural blocks—analogous to “loop” regions in folded domains. As these units recur across tens of thousands of natural protein structures, they likely represent early-folding segments and may reflect conserved ancestral structural elements that have been preserved throughout evolution as templates for structural diversification. We further evaluated SplitSeek-Pro’s performance in multi-domain proteins by comparing splitting sites with domain boundaries identified by Merizo77. The excellent agreement between high-scoring SplitSeek-Pro regions and Merizo-defined domain boundaries confirms the model’s ability to identify splittable inter-domain linkers (Supplementary Fig. 19), supporting the hypothesis that SplitSeek-Pro captures relatively independent structural segments or domains within complex protein architectures.

Discussion

In this work, we introduce SplitSeek-Pro, a versatile deep learning framework that integrates and predicts sequence and structural features to accurately predict splittable sites in folded proteins. Experimentally validated through circular permutation (LCC, CHMO) and split-reconstitution (NanoLuc), the model robustly discriminates feasible split sites in folded proteins with superior performance over existing tools such as CPred and Int&in. Importantly, SplitSeek-Pro extends beyond a mere engineering utility. It also facilitates the discovery of “SplitSeek fragments”, supersecondary structural motifs that behave cooperatively beyond common secondary elements, which shed light on the fundamental building blocks of protein folding.

To realize its full potential, we recognize our model boundaries and avenues for further refinement. Currently, while the SplitSeek-Pro framework can effectively utilize AlphaFold predictions for proteins with unknown structures, its performance on intrinsically disordered proteins is constrained. Moreover, limited training data, especially regarding terminal residues and rigid structures, confines our current predictions to binary classifications. Since existing structure prediction tools inherently cannot evaluate splittability, establishing an iterative “dry-and-wet” lab optimization loop is essential to accumulate relevant high-quality datasets. Coupling this feedback loop with task-specific fine-tuning will eventually elevate SplitSeek-Pro into a precise, regression-based tool capable of quantifying structural change and functional retention for advanced protein design and engineering.

Methods

Dataset collection and construction

The pretraining dataset is derived from clustering protein structures obtained from the ProteinMPNN training dataset30. Residue splitability was assessed using the SPELL scoring strategy, integrating weighted and normalized energy (SE), sequence conservation (SSC), and solvent-accessible surface area (SSASA) scores. Conservation scores were generated using a deep learning-based model49, and SASA values were computed with DSSP48. Energy differences between split and intact structures were calculated following the SPELL workflow based on Rosetta energy score78 to identify splittable sites. Circular permutation pairs from the CPDB were used to build the fine-tuning dataset. Structural similarity for each pair was calculated, and those with high similarity scores >0.10 were retained, yielding 2156 valid circular permutated pairs. Sequence alignments were then used to annotate potential splitting sites (score = 1.00). To balance the dataset, an equal number of randomly selected residues in rigid secondary structures were labeled 0.00, and all other positions were labeled −1.00. Details on the dataset constructions could be found in the Supplementary Methods.

Workflow of SplitSeek-Pro

The protein sequence and structure inputs were encoded using different strategies. Protein graphs were constructed by treating each amino acid as a node and adding edges between pairs of residues that are spatially proximal. The amino acid sequence was embedded into node features using representations generated by ESM-225 and AAindex37. The spatial distances between all atomic pairs within each residue pair were encoded into feature vectors using radial basis functions, which were then incorporated into the edge features. An encoder-decoder message-passing neural network is employed for this task. The encoder takes the nodes and edges of the graph as input, with a hidden layer dimension of 128. A three-layer encoder was constructed to update node and edge information. The edge and node information of the decoder is derived from the encoder, with three layers and a hidden layer dimension of 128. Finally, the node information is reduced to 1 dimension through an output layer, followed by a sigmoid function to produce the corresponding probability score as the final output. Both the encoder and decoder utilize multi-layer perceptrons (MLP) to integrate the propagated information, employing the GELU activation function.

Training of SplitSeek-Pro

Mean Squared Error (MSE) was chosen as the loss function. During the fine-tuning process, data points labeled as −1.00 are masked, meaning these points do not influence the adjustment of model parameters during the backward propagation process. Adam optimizer was selected with beta1 = 0.9, beta2 = 0.98, epsilon = 10−9, and the learning rate schedule described in the previous reference. Models were trained using PyTorch, a batch size of 10k tokens, automatic mixed precision, and gradient checkpointing on a single NVIDIA 3080 GPU. Five independent cross-validation tests were conducted to demonstrate that the model did not overfit to any specific dataset.

Fragments generation and clustering

Structural fragments are segmented based on the predictions from SplitSeek-Pro. A continuous stretch of residues with predicted scores beyond 0.80 is assigned to the same structural fragment, generating various types of structural fragments. These fragments are categorized into different groups based on the number of α-helices and β-sheets they contain. Subsequently, similarity calculations are performed within each group using the Foldseek73 method, with structural matching carried out using the 3Di alphabet-based alignment approach to generate alignment scores. A similarity matrix is then constructed based on these scores, and atypical structures (defined as those with similarity scores below 0.05 compared to 98% of the other structures) are removed. Dimensionality reduction is then applied to the similarity matrix using the t-SNE method, extracting two key distinguishing components. Finally, K-means clustering is used to partition the fragments into 15 clusters, and the cluster center coordinates are computed. The representative fragment structures corresponding to each cluster center are then visualized.

Protein characterization

SDS-PAGE was performed to analyze the protein samples after being mixed with 5×SDS-PAGE loading buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl, 50% glycerol, 10% SDS, 250 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 0.05% bromophenol blue) and heated at 98 °C for 10 min. Relative protein quantification was performed using Image Lab Software (Bio-Rad) for SDS-PAGE images. Protein samples were diluted with ddH2O until a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL for CD spectroscopy was achieved. The CD spectroscopy was recorded on a J-815 spectrometer (JASCO, Japan). To determine apparent melting temperatures, a fluorescence-based thermal stability assay was employed. A 1 µL 50-fold diluted SYPRO Orange dye (Molecular Probes, Life Technologies, USA) was added to a 20 µL protein solution. The mixture was placed in a thin-walled 96-well plate, sealed with optical-quality sealing tape. The mixture was heated in a CFX 96 real-time polymerase chain reaction system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) from 30 to 100 °C at a heating rate of 1.2 °C/s.

Activity test of different protein systems

Enzymatic activity assays were performed using different methods for different protein systems. For LCC and its circular permutation variants, enzymatic activity was evaluated based on the hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl acetate and PET. In the p-nitrophenyl acetate assay, the reaction kinetics were measured by monitoring the increase in absorbance of the p-nitrophenol product at 415 nm at room temperature using a microplate reader, and the enzymatic activity (U/mg) was calculated accordingly. In the PET hydrolysis assay, relative activity was determined by quantifying the formation of terephthalic acid after an 8 h reaction at 65 °C using ultra- performance liquid chromatography. For CHMO and its circularly permuted variants, the activity assay was based on the consumption of NADPH and oxygen as the enzyme catalyzes the conversion of cyclohexanone to ε-caprolactone. Reaction kinetics were measured by monitoring the decrease in absorbance of NADPH at 340 nm using a microplate reader, and enzymatic activity was subsequently calculated. For the split-reconstituted NanoLuc pairs, activity was assessed by measuring the fluorescence emission at 466 nm generated by the reconstituted enzyme during the oxidation of furimazine. Details of the assay conditions are provided in the Supplementary Methods.
