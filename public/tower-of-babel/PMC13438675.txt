Break-induced replication forms long mutable single-strand DNA during meiosis
Authors: Jerzy M Twarowski, Juraj Kramara, Gabriel J Seuferer, Kelley A Renninger, Josep M Comeron, Anna Malkova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn meiosis, homologous recombination (HR) facilitates the halving of genomic content in diploid parents to produce haploid gametes, while also reshuffling genetic material. However, studies in yeast and mammals have suggested that HR during meiosis also makes the process mutagenic, and it has been proposed that the single-strand (ss) DNA formed by HR of programmed double-strand breaks (DSBs) contributes to the observed mutagenesis. To determine the full mutagenic potential of ssDNA formed during meiosis, we expressed human APOBEC3A (A3A), a deaminase that specifically attacks and therefore allows detection of ssDNA, in meiotic yeast cells. We demonstrate that meiotic cells accumulate long tracts of ssDNA manifested by A3A-mutation clusters, of up to 134 mutations spanning more than 25 kb. We show that the formation of mutation clusters during meiosis require Spo11-induced DSBs, and that break-induced replication and hyper-resection of DSBs are the primary mechanisms underlying the formation of long ssDNA. We also report meiotic ssDNA accumulation in promoters and tRNA genes, revealing them as additional sources of mutagenesis during meiosis. Together, our results demonstrate the high mutagenic potential of meiosis and provide insight into mechanisms that can fuel evolution and promote congenital diseases in humans.Subject terms: Genomic instability, DNA damage and repair, DNA recombination

Introduction

Studies in yeast using direct and reverse mutational reporters have revealed that mutagenesis is highly increased during meiosis1–4. The observed meiotic mutations were associated with meiotic recombination1,5 and showed dependence on Spo11-induced double-strand breaks (DSBs)1,3,4 and on trans-lesion polymerase Polζ1. Regardless of their molecular cause, these mutations have been widely thought to represent sporadic events introducing independent errors during normal processing of the hundreds of programmed Spo11-induced DSBs during meiosis and, therefore, were considered an important contributor to the gradual accumulation of single-nucleotide changes fueling evolution6.

Increased mutagenesis during meiosis has been corroborated in other organisms, including humans, where meiotic DSB hotspots with elevated mutation rates have been reported7–9. For example, in human males, an increased level of mutations was detected near the sites of meiotic DSBs7, and genome sequencing of human trios (two parents and their child) supported the idea of increased mutagenesis in the germline10–13. Furthermore, a recent large-scale analysis of genetic variation in human populations demonstrated that one in four sperm and one in 12 eggs carry a de novo mutation attributable to errors in the repair of programmed meiotic DSBs8.

To explain the observed meiotic mutagenesis, two non-mutually exclusive mechanisms have been proposed: error-prone repair DNA synthesis1,6–8 and the formation of mutable single-strand (ss) DNA1,4,6,7. Here, we further explored the latter idea during yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae meiosis by expressing the ssDNA-specific human cytidine deaminase APOBEC3A (A3A)14,15. Because A3A specifically attacks ssDNA and leaves behind well-characterized mutation patterns16, this approach was used to reveal the scale and distribution of mutable ssDNA formed during yeast meiosis.

Previously, expressing A3A in mitotic yeast cells irradiated by gamma rays demonstrated that, while most gamma-induced breaks produced only individual mutations, a small fraction of DSBs led to long (up to 100 kb) intervals containing disproportionately many A3A-mediated mutations (mutation clusters)17, indicating that the repair of these DSBs produced long stretches of mutable ssDNA. Similar mutation clusters were detected in human cancers expressing A3A and A3B, where formation of these clusters (called kataegis in cancer cells) was explained by abnormal processing of DSBs17–20, which promoted multiple and simultaneous — rather than gradual—accumulation of mutations, leading to bursts of genetic instability. It has been proposed that the main mechanism generating long stretches of mutable ssDNA in both cancers and gamma-irradiated yeast cells is break-induced replication (BIR)17, an unusual form of long-tract DNA repair synthesis where uncoupling of the leading and lagging strands promotes ssDNA accumulation21–24.

In the present study, we express human A3A under a meiosis-specific promoter in Saccharomyces cerevisiae to detect the frequency with which programmed DSBs form short versus long tracts of mutable ssDNA, identified as A3A-induced mutations and mutation clusters, respectively. We also examine the role of BIR in the formation of mutable ssDNA during meiosis.

Results

Long mutable ssDNA accumulates during meiosis

We engineered an S. cerevisiae strain with multiple features to enable detection of ssDNA formed during meiosis (Fig. 1a, b). First, we mated haploid yeast strains from different genetic backgrounds to create diploids that were heterozygous for ~55,000 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)25 and that were also ung1Δ, rendering them unable to excise uridines created by A3A cytidine deaminase (Fig. 1b). We then transformed these diploids with a plasmid in which expression of A3A was under the control of the yeast meiosis-specific SPO13 promoter (PsA3A) (Fig. 1b). Importantly, the ura3-29 mutation reporter containing an A3A-recognition motif26,27 at its mutant position was present in one of the chromosome III copies at the BUD23-ARE1 meiotic DSB hotspot1,28 (Fig. 1b). In this system, we expected that A3A could convert cytosines within ssDNA formed during meiosis into uridines, leading to mutations that could be detected in two different ways: by reversion of cells to the Ura+ phenotype and by whole genome sequencing (WGS) (Fig. 1b).

Initial characterization of our experimental system showed that, in the presence of A3A, the frequency of Ura+ reversion in ung1Δ strains increased 94-fold after meiosis compared to pre-meiotic levels (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Data 1), whereas the frequency of Ura+ reversion after meiosis in ung1Δ PsA3A cells was 357-fold higher compared to PEV (empty vector) strains (Fig. 1c, and Supplementary Data 1). In addition, in PEV strains lacking A3A, the Ura+ frequencies were sevenfold and ninefold higher after meiosis in ung1Δ and UNG1 cells, respectively (Fig. 1c, and Supplementary Data 1), supporting previous reports of increased mutagenesis at recombination hotspots during meiosis1,6. Based on the specificity of A3A for ssDNA and the high induction of Ura+ by A3A (Fig. 1c), we conclude that approximately 99% of mutations occurring during meiosis at the ura3-29 reporter in ung1ΔPsA3A strains were induced by A3A in ssDNA formed at a meiotic DSB hotspot.

To determine whether mutable ssDNA accumulates genome-wide during meiosis, meiotic outcomes obtained in strains harboring PsA3A were subjected to WGS. We first selected Ura+ meiotic outcomes for sequencing to ensure we were analyzing outcomes in which A3A was efficiently expressed during meiosis (as previously described in refs. 17,27). In these meiotic outcomes, we observed a high level of A3A-induced point mutations, which was as high as 495 mutations per genome, with an average of 188 de novo mutations per meiotic outcome (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1b, and Supplementary Data 2). This was 78 times higher than the level of mutations in ung1∆PsA3A before meiosis and 63 times higher than the level of mutations in meiotic outcomes of ung1∆PEV (Fig. 1d, and Supplementary Data 2). In UNG1 strains harboring PsA3A, we detected an average of 8 mutations per genome per meiotic outcome, which was 24-fold lower compared with ung1∆PsA3A (Fig. 1d, and Supplementary Data 2); this suggests that, in Ung1-proficient cells, abasic sites are excised by Ung1 glycosylase (the first step of abasic excision repair) and then channeled into error-free repair such as template switching or homologous recombination, similar to observations in vegetative yeast cells expressing APOBECs27,29,30. As expected, nearly all (~99%) of the de novo mutations detected throughout the genome of Ura+ outcomes from ung1ΔPsA3A cells were C→T or G→A mutations and occurred within a TCd (where d indicates any nucleotide besides C) motif, which is consistent with the known A3A mutation signature18,31 (Supplementary Fig. 1c, and Supplementary Data 2).

Next, we evaluated Ura-unselected meiotic outcomes and observed that they fell into two groups: those with fewer than 10 mutations per genome and those with more than 10 mutations per genome. The former group likely represented outcomes of cells in which A3A was not expressed, and a similar fraction of yeast vegetative cells harboring A3A was found not to have any evidence of A3A expression17. In the latter group, where A3A was expressed, the level of mutations genome-wide (167 mutations) was similarly high compared with outcomes selected for Ura+ reversion (188 mutations), demonstrating that the high level of mutagenesis during meiosis was not limited to Ura+ revertants. Together, our results suggest that mutable ssDNA accumulates during yeast meiosis.

Mutation clusters mark long tracts of mutable ssDNA formed during meiosis

ssDNA has been previously shown to lead to mutation clusters in the presence of DNA-damaging agents, including A3A, in both mitotic yeast and in human cancer cells16,17,19,27,31–34. Following the methodology of those studies to find mutation clusters, we used a similar algorithm that can identify groups of closely spaced mutations that likely occurred during a single mutagenic event (Fig. 1a, e)16,18,19,27,31,32. Using this approach, we found that most Ura+ meiotic outcomes among ung1ΔPsA3A cells had mutation clusters containing at least 3 mutations, with an average of 6.5 mutation clusters throughout the genome (Figs. 1e and 2a, and Supplementary Data 3). These mutation clusters had an average length of 4.6 kb (Fig. 2b) and included, on average, 3.6 mutations per cluster (Fig. 2c), although some mutation clusters were exceptionally long, spanning over 25 kb (Fig. 2e, and Supplementary Data 3). On average, approximately 11% of the A3A-induced mutations detected in the meiotic outcomes were part of mutation clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2g).

Based on the commonly held assumption that clustered mutations arise simultaneously17,18,27,31, we posited that the length of each meiotic mutation cluster should correspond to the minimum length of the respective ssDNA tract (similar to ref.17). Applying this principle, we estimated the total amount of long mutable ssDNA formed throughout the genome during meiosis by calculating the sum of all mutation cluster lengths, recognizing this would underestimate the true amount of long ssDNA because regions without detected clusters would not be counted and that ssDNA likely extends beyond the two outmost mutations of a cluster. Using this conservative method, we determined that the average amount of long, mutable ssDNA accumulated during meiosis was 30 kb (about 0.25% of the genome; see Fig. 2), and up to 178 kb (about 1.5% of the genome; Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 2; see also Fig. 2f for an example of mutations and mutation clusters in one genome). Next, to determine whether selecting outcomes with additional mutations proximal to the ura3-29 reporter would identify meiotic outcomes with an increased level of long meiotic ssDNA, we performed this analysis on Ura+ outcomes that additionally harbored a mutation in NATMX, which was located next to the ura3-29 reporter and rendered them sensitive to nourseothricin (NATS). Ura+NATSung1ΔPsA3A outcomes contained more mutations per genome (average = 250), more mutation clusters (average = 9.4), and more ssDNA (average = 46 kb) compared to Ura+ung1ΔPsA3A outcomes (Figs. 1d and 2a–d). This observation suggests the presence of a subpopulation of cells with a hypermutable phenotype during meiosis characterized by higher susceptibility to form mutation clusters, or, alternatively, this could reflect higher meiotic expression of A3A in these cells, which would allow detecting more ssDNA.

Finally, we also observed mutation clusters among ung1ΔPsA3A cells that were analyzed without prior selection for the Ura+ phenotype. Similar to the aforementioned analysis (see Fig. 1d), a bimodal distribution of ssDNA accumulation was observed in these unselected cells (Fig. 2a–d), probably reflecting the status of A3A expression, wherein the average number of mutation clusters per outcome (4.3) among A3A-induced cells was similar to the number observed among Ura+ung1ΔPsA3A outcomes. These results confirm that long ssDNA tracts are not a unique feature of Ura+ outcomes. Altogether, our data support that mutation clusters form in long tracts of ssDNA that accumulate during meiosis.

Spo11-mediated DSBs induce long tracts of ssDNA during meiosis

To test whether long mutable ssDNA tracts originated from Spo11-induced DSBs, we first examined the association of meiotic mutation clusters with recombination events in individual meiotic outcomes with our newly developed software MayoMate (see “Methods”). This allowed us to directly map mutation clusters to the actual recombination events in each individual meiosis analyzed, rather than comparing mutation clusters with predicted recombination hotspots based on population averages. To achieve this, we analyzed the pattern of parental SNPs (Supplementary Data 4, see also “Methods”) in the meiotic outcomes, and we defined the sites of recombination events as the middle position between SNPs flanking consecutive parental haplotype blocks for crossover (CO) events and as the center position of haplotype blocks shorter than 5 kb for gene conversion (GC) events (Fig. 3a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 3d–f; see “Methods” for details). We found that, on average, each meiotic outcome yielded 21 GC events and 43 CO events (Fig. 3c, d, Supplementary Fig. 9, and Supplementary Data 5), consistent with previous estimates35,36. We note that GC events are likely underestimated in WGS studies because up to 50% are expected to be shorter than 500 bp37,38, escaping detection by occurring between the available SNPs36 (Fig. 3e (see legend for details), and Supplementary Data 4). Importantly, recombination hotspots identified using this method closely matched previously described meiotic recombination hotspots36 (see, for example, Supplementary Fig. 3g).

We observed that ~30% of mutation clusters directly overlapped with recombination events, which was more than expected by random association (P < 1 × 10⁻6) (Fig. 3a, b, f). In addition, when we expanded the cluster association window around each recombination event, we observed that ~50% of mutation clusters were associated within 5 kb (P < 1 × 10⁻6) of a recombination event (Fig. 3f, and Supplementary Data 6). Interestingly, the rate of association plateaued around 5 kb, suggesting saturation in true cluster association (Fig. 3f, and Supplementary Fig. 3c).

Although we detected a recombination event association for approximately half of the mutation clusters, we hypothesized that this was an underestimation due to undetected GC events, which could be missed if they did not overlap with SNPs or if the recombination events occurred between sister chromatids (similar to refs. 39,40). Therefore, to test whether all long tracts of ssDNA in meiosis originate from Spo11-induced DSBs, we deleted SPO11 in ung1∆spo13∆ cells, because the absence of SPO13 prevents reductional meiotic division, resulting in diploid meiotic outcomes that can survive even in the absence of meiotic DSBs41. In these ung1∆spo11∆spo13∆PsA3A cells, the frequency of Ura+ reversion was similar before and after meiosis (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Data 1). Thus, spo11∆ caused a more than 100-fold reduction in Ura+ meiotic reversion frequency. WGS analysis of the rare Ura+ outcomes obtained in spo11∆ after meiosis revealed significantly fewer mutations overall (1.9-fold reduction), fewer scattered mutations (1.4-fold reduction), fewer mutation clusters (sevenfold reduction), and less long mutable ssDNA accumulation throughout the genome (6.3-fold reduction) compared to Ura+ung1∆spo13∆PsA3A controls (Fig. 4b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 4a–d).

Because Ura+ outcomes were not increased during meiosis in ung1∆spo11∆ spo13∆PsA3A cells, we repeated the experiment in unselected meiotic outcomes. In addition, to ensure that all mutations were truly meiotic, we excluded mutations occurring before or after meiosis by micro-manipulating individual unselected meiotic spores from dyads formed after meiosis, dividing the colonies into four sectors, and subjecting the cells from each sector to WGS analysis (Fig. 4d, see also “Methods”). We found almost no long meiotic ssDNA tracts in ung1∆spo11∆spo13∆PsA3A cells, whereas they were retained in ung1∆spo13∆PsA3A cells that could induce meiotic DSBs (Fig. 4e, f, and Supplementary Data 7 and 8). Surprisingly, this analysis also provided evidence of post-meiotic completion of repair of Spo11-induced DSBs in ung1∆spo13∆ cells: we observed that loci of post-meiotic mutation clusters were frequently shared by different sectors of a colony grown from an individual spore, even though the specific mutations in these clusters were different. We termed these mutation clusters at the same genomic location but with different mutations “concordant” mutation clusters, and they accounted for ~60% of the total mutable ssDNA detected in ung1∆spo13∆PsA3A outcomes, but they did not contribute to ssDNA detected in ung1∆spo11∆spo13∆PsA3A or ung1∆PsA3A strains (Fig. 4g, h, and Supplementary Data 7 and 8). The results of this sector-level analysis of colonies were supported by WGS of colonies obtained by pedigree analysis of ung1∆spo13∆PsA3A and ung1∆spo11∆spo13∆PsA3A dyads (see “Methods” and Supplementary Data 7 and 8), in which individual spores were micromanipulated and allowed to divide; the resulting mother and daughter cells were then separated, and this procedure was repeated once more. WGS of such pedigree outcomes from 14 meiotic outcomes of ung1∆spo13∆PsA3A identified 17 meiotic mutation clusters, and this number increased to 30 mutation clusters when concordant clusters were included (Supplementary Data 7 and 8). We speculate that concordant clusters in spo13∆ outcomes result from post-meiotic completion of repair of Spo11-induced DSBs, which could represent either a normal feature of Spo11-induced DSB repair or an outcome specific to spo13Δ mutants.

Despite the absence, or a diminishingly small amount, of mutation clusters in meiotic cells lacking Spo11, we nonetheless observed scattered de novo meiotic mutations in these cells (Supplementary Fig. 4b). This finding points to the existence of Spo11-independent mechanisms leading to the formation of at least short intervals of mutable ssDNA during meiosis. Collectively, our data support that, during meiosis, long tracts of mutable ssDNA originate from Spo11-induced DSBs, whereas Spo11-independent mechanism(s) may contribute to shorter ssDNA tracts and the resulting scattered mutations.

BIR forms long ssDNA during meiosis

It was previously demonstrated that bidirectional resection of DSBs leads to the formation of mutation clusters with C→T mutations on the 5′ side of the DSB and G→A mutations on the 3′ side of the DSB in the Watson strand (Fig. 5a)14,17,18. Conversely, BIR leads to predominantly C→T mutations (in the Watson strand, G→A in the Crick strand) on either side of the break along the entire BIR tract due to one-sided DSB resection on the 5′ side of the break, as well as formation of long newly synthesized ssDNA behind the BIR replication bubble (Fig. 5a)17,27,31. Therefore, to understand how DSB processing mechanisms may contribute to the accumulation of mutations in ssDNA during meiosis, we characterized the patterns of mutated cytosines in mutation clusters in Ura+ung1∆PsA3A meiotic outcomes. Most mutation clusters (52%) harbored only C→T mutations (or only G→A mutations), accounting for 42.5% of long mutable ssDNA throughout the genome (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Data 6; “non-switching” clusters), consistent with ssDNA resulting from BIR, although asymmetric resection that was more extensive on one side could not be ruled out. In addition, in 24% of meiotic mutation clusters, we observed a single switch from C→T to G→A in the Watson strand, which accounted for 25% of mutable ssDNA (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Data 6; “single switch CG” clusters), strongly supporting bidirectional resection of meiotic DSBs leading to the observed mutations. The remaining mutation clusters fell into three categories: (1) multiple C→T to G→A or G→A to C→T switches (16% of cases, 24.5% of mutable ssDNA), which likely resulted from a combination of several ssDNA sources; (2) a single switch from G→A to C→T in the Watson strand (8% of cases, 8% of mutable ssDNA); and (3) clusters including mutations that were inconsistent with the A3A signature and possibly just resulted from spontaneous mutagenesis known to be increased during meiosis (0.6% of cases, 0.24% of mutable ssDNA) (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Data 6).

To further investigate the source(s) of mutable ssDNA during meiosis, and to clarify the origins of ssDNA that manifested as non-switching mutation clusters, we examined the distribution of meiotic mutations in relation to the individual SNP-defined meiotic recombination events. We observed that 62.5% of the mutations in Ura+ung1∆PsA3A outcomes were characterized by C→T mutations on the 5′ side of the DSB and G→A mutations on the 3′ side of the DSB (Fig. 5a, c, Supplementary Data 9, and Supplementary Fig. 5), indicative of mutable ssDNA that arose from 5′-to-3′ DSB resection (hereafter, resection pattern). However, a sizeable fraction of the mutations (37.5%) did not fit this pattern; rather, the mutation pattern was inverse, with C→T mutations on the 3′ side of the DSB and G→A mutations on the 5′ side of the DSB (hereafter, non-resection pattern) (Fig. 5a, c, and Supplementary Fig. 5).

We hypothesized that the non-resection pattern may be formed by BIR, and we tested this in cells lacking Pol32, which are known to have a severe BIR defect42. Indeed, in the absence of Pol32, we observed a significant decrease in the proportion of ssDNA tracts (defined by mutation clusters) attributable to BIR (Fig. 5d–f, i, Supplementary Fig. 5, and Supplementary Data 10). Additionally, POL32 deletion reduced the BIR patterning of scattered mutations but only for those further away from the DSB (Fig. 5j, Supplementary Fig. 5, and Supplementary Data 10), suggesting that even scattered mutations can be reflective of long ssDNA produced by BIR. Together, our data suggest that BIR plays a significant role in the formation of long tracts of mutable ssDNA. The apparent increase in “resection-like” ssDNA tracts among pol32∆ Ura+ PsA3A cells (Fig. 5f, i, and Supplementary Data 10) may have resulted from an increase in one-ended resection, similar to what has been observed following BIR induction in mitotic pol32∆ cells43.

We also evaluated the role of Exo1, which encodes the only known nuclease to execute long-range DSB resection during yeast meiosis44,45. Introduction of the exonuclease-dead exo1-nd allele46, either alone or in combination with pol32∆, did not change the proportion of cluster-defined “resection tracts” (Fig. 5h, i, Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6, and Supplementary Data 10). However, exo1-nd did significantly reduce the proportion of “resection tracts” marked by scattered mutations (Fig. 5j, Supplementary Fig. 5, and Supplementary Data 10). This suggests that, while Exo1 is indeed responsible for the formation of ssDNA during normal resection, its absence alone does not eliminate the long mutable ssDNA identified in this study.

Interestingly, when we introduced a helicase-domain-deleted sgs1-∆C795 allele of SGS147–49, we observed that the proportion of “resection tracts” among mutation clusters was significantly increased in sgs1-∆C795 in the 3.8–7.8 kb interval away from the DSB as compared to SGS1 (Fig. 5i, Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6, and Supplementary Data 10). This might have resulted from the accumulation of unusual invasion/Holliday Junction structures in these mutants, which could generate new ssDNA substrates upon their resolution or resection. Indeed, the observed increase in sgs1-∆C795 was abolished in sgs1-∆C795 exo1-nd double mutants, suggesting that Exo1 plays a role in the processing of those novel substrates formed in the absence of Sgs1 helicase activity (Fig. 5g, i, Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6, and Supplementary Data 10). Moreover, the decreased fraction of single-switch (from C→T to G→A) clusters that we observed among Ura+ung1∆exo1-ndsgs1-∆C795PsA3A outcomes compared with the Ura+ung1∆PsA3A outcomes (χ2 test, P = 5.5 × 10⁻3) (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Data 6) supports that there is redundancy between Sgs1 and Exo1 in meiotic hyper-resection. In sum, we propose that several factors contribute to the accumulation of long mutable ssDNA tracts during meiosis, including BIR, DSB resection and processing of unusual HR intermediates.

ssDNA accumulates in tRNA genes and promoter regions during meiosis

To identify possible sources of short ssDNA tracts that induce scattered meiotic mutations, we examined whether these could be formed during transcription, similar to what has been reported in mitotic cells and cancers30,50–54. To test the role of transcription in creating mutable ssDNA during meiosis, we performed WGS analysis of mutations in sectors of dissected ung1∆PsA3A tetrads. We observed a sixfold increase in the mutation rate in the non-transcribed strand of tRNA genes (4.5 × 10⁻4 mutations per site at A3A motifs) compared to neighboring regions, whereas no significant increase in mutation frequency was observed in the transcribed DNA strand (Fig. 6a, c, Supplementary Fig. 7, and Supplementary Data 11). Next, we analyzed protein-coding genes and found that the rates of A3A mutagenesis within the coding regions did not exceed background levels (Fig. 6b; “Methods”). However, we observed 2.8-fold and 1.7-fold increases in the meiotic A3A mutation rates of both non-transcribed (C→T) and transcribed (G→A) strands, respectively, within a 100-bp region around gene transcription start sites (TSS)(TSS positions from ref.55) compared to downstream sequences (Fig. 6b, c, Supplementary Fig. 7, and Supplementary Data 11; “Methods”). Analysis of Ura+ meiotic outcomes yielded similar results (Supplementary Fig. 7, and Supplementary Data 11).

To determine the contribution of Spo11-induced DSBs to the formation of mutable ssDNA at tRNA genes and at promoter regions during meiosis, we performed dyad dissection of ung1∆spo13∆spo11∆PsA3A cells and analyzed A3A-mediated mutation rates within sectors of individual meiotic outcomes by WGS. Even in the absence of Spo11, tRNA genes were characterized by a 24-fold increase in non-transcribed strand mutagenesis compared to the control region (Fig. 6c, and Supplementary Data 11). Also, the A3A mutation frequency (per DNA copy) in the non-transcribed strand of tRNA genes of ung1∆spo13∆spo11∆PsA3A was 48% of the rate calculated for ung1∆PsA3A spores, suggesting that approximately half of A3A-induced mutations in tRNA genes during meiosis can be attributed to non-Spo11 sources (Fig. 6c, and Supplementary Data 11). Similarly, in the regions surrounding TSS of protein-coding genes, mutagenesis was elevated 2.9-fold and 3.3-fold, respectively, in the non-transcribed and transcribed strands compared to downstream control sequences (Fig. 6c, and Supplementary Data 11). By comparing these results with those obtained from ung1∆PsA3A spores, we can estimate that, respectively, 7% and 13% of ssDNA mutations in the non-transcribed and transcribed strands around promoters of protein-coding genes are Spo11 independent.

Discussion

In the present study, we report that mutable ssDNA accumulates during meiosis due to the processing and repair of Spo11-induced DSBs. These ssDNA regions can produce multiple simultaneous mutations, especially when exposed to DNA-damaging agents, which we modeled in our experiments by the expression of A3A (Fig. 6d). Furthermore, our results support BIR and hyper-resection of DSBs as the main mechanisms responsible for the formation of long tracts of mutable ssDNA during meiosis. Finally, we observed mutations that were not associated with Spo11-induced DSBs, which we determined to be associated with ssDNA accumulation in tRNA genes and regions around protein-coding genes. These findings shed new light on factors that can contribute to germline mutations.

Most of the A3A-induced mutations formed in meiosis were scattered, but 11% of these mutations formed clusters that marked the regions of ssDNA where several mutations were formed simultaneously; in some survivors, the fraction of clustered mutations was even higher, reaching between 20% and 50% of all mutations. If we assume (based on ref.56) that the upper limit of DSB resection in meiosis is 2 kb per DSB (1 kb in each direction), then the maximum length of a mutation cluster should be ~2 kb. However, we frequently detected mutation clusters that were significantly longer: 4.6 kb long on average and up to 25 kb long. We estimate that mutation clusters covered ~30 kb of ssDNA per meiotic outcome, or ~120 kb per meiosis, with 88% (105.6 kb) represented by clusters longer than 2 kb and therefore classified as “unusually long” ssDNA. Based on this, unusually long ssDNA would comprise 105.6 kb / 408 kb = 26% of total meiotic ssDNA, where 408 kb = 340 kb (maximum expected total ssDNA; ~170 DSBs/meiosis × 2 kb, the upper limit of resection per DSB) + 120 kb (ssDNA covered by mutation clusters) − 52 kb (6.5 average mutation clusters per meiotic outcome × 4 meiotic outcomes × 2 kb, the upper limit of resection per DSB).

Additionally, on the basis of our finding that a subpopulation of meiotic outcomes had striking enrichment of mutation clusters, we posit that some cells may have harbored factors contributing to systemic alterations in DSB repair efficiency, such as factors contributing to checkpoint failure, a decrease in RPA protection of ssDNA, increased levels of R-loops, or a shortened meiosis followed by mitosis-like processing of DSBs. Finally, we cannot exclude that the increase in mutation clusters in our “hypermutable population” resulted from overexpression of A3A in some cells; if true, this would indicate that the amount of long ssDNA in meiotic cells is even higher than reported in this study.

Our results indicate that extensive resection of DSBs is one source of long ssDNA in meiosis. This is supported by our finding of long mutation clusters with switches from C→T to G→A, characteristic of DSB resection mutation pattern. While over-resection of DSBs is a well-known source of long mutable ssDNA in mitotic cells14,17,57, it was unexpected in the context of meiotic DSBs44,45. Surprisingly, the over-resection of meiotic DSBs was not decreased by elimination of Exo1, the main long-range resection enzyme in meiotic yeast44,45, but it was only diminished when the resection functions of both Exo1 and Sgs1/Dna2 were eliminated (Fig. 5b, g, i, Supplementary Fig. 5, and Supplementary Data 10), indicating that these enzymes have redundant functions. Sgs1/Dna2 DSB-resection activity is typically considered to be inactive during yeast meiosis, although it has been reported to participate in meiosis in other organisms58. However, our results support its possible role in the over-resection of yeast meiotic DSBs.

Our results implicate BIR as an important source of long ssDNA during meiosis (Fig. 6d). Specifically, the involvement of BIR was supported by frequent non-switching mutation clusters that were longer than expected for normal meiotic DSB repair synthesis, and by a Pol32-dependent change in the distribution of mutable ssDNA tracts, marked by mutation clusters, around recombination events. It is known that the main source of BIR mutagenesis is the asynchrony between leading and lagging strand synthesis, which leads to the accumulation of long mutable ssDNA27,59. The fact that the effect of Pol32 loss was significant up to 7.6 kb away from the recombination site (for clustered tracks) provides a rough length estimate of BIR-like synthesis in yeast meiosis. In addition, pol32Δ had a similar effect on the distribution of scattered mutations, suggesting that they can also be induced by BIR.

The finding that BIR plays an important role in creating ssDNA during yeast meiosis directly supports a previously proposed hypothesis that BIR may be a source of mutations during human meiosis by producing ssDNA up to 10 kb away from recombination hotspots8. Our BIR findings are also consistent with observations of frequent template switching (a well-known BIR marker60,61) associated with repair of meiotic DSBs in yeast39,40. In addition, it has been suggested that repair of meiotic DSBs is often initiated by invasion and copying of the sister chromatid, followed by template switching into the non-sister39,40, homologous chromosome; this mechanism could explain why mutations and mutation clusters observed in this study were often positioned at a distance from the recombination events. Separation between mutation clusters and the recombination events could also result from branch migration of the Holliday junctions, which has also been reported to occur frequently in meiosis39,40. Finally, frequent invasions into the sister chromatid instead of the homolog, which were recently reported in yeast meiosis39,40, could at least partly explain the formation of mutation clusters that were apparently unassociated with recombination, although it is likely that these events could also result from an insufficient number of SNPs in our hybrid to capture all of the recombination events (especially GCs). Together, we propose that over-resection of meiotic DSBs and BIR are the important contributors to the formation of unusually long ssDNA in meiosis. However, the total amount of mutable ssDNA in our experiments remained high even in the absence of Pol32, Exo1, or Sgs1. Therefore, other sources of long ssDNA in meiosis may exist, such as mismatch repair activity or more complex events39,40.

Our study shows that the promoters of protein-coding genes and the non-transcribed strand of tRNA genes are sources of short tracts of meiotic ssDNA capable of producing scattered mutations (Fig. 6d). The fact that mutations were enriched specifically in the promoter regions of protein-coding genes prompts the hypothesis that the transcription pre-initiation complex and polymerase stalling at promoters—rather than transcription itself—may unwind dsDNA to create a transient ssDNA substrate for A3A, which is consistent with previous descriptions of mitotic yeast cells expressing APOBEC and AID proteins51,52. Also, Class I R-loops, which form during promoter-proximal RNA polymerase II pausing, could promote the formation of ssDNA substrates near promoters during meiosis62. We postulate that the mutagenesis within tRNA genes is most likely related to transcription directly (similarly to findings reported in ref.30), as transcription creates R-loops (in this case Class II) that stabilize the ssDNA substrate on the non-transcribed strand62. Interestingly, both SPO11PsA3A and spo11∆PsA3A meiotic outcomes were enriched for A3A-induced tRNA mutagenesis during meiosis, although spo11∆ outcomes had 2x lower mutation rates within the non-transcribed strand of tRNA genes compared to SPO11 outcomes. Importantly, mutagenesis was also increased in the promoters of protein-coding genes compared to downstream sequences in the absence of Spo11, although to a lesser extent compared to SPO11-proficient cells. This indicates that ssDNA formed during meiosis in these regions is only partially dependent on programmed meiotic DSBs, pointing to a role for transcription in its formation. Because meiosis in our strains is long and asynchronous, we could not conduct RNA-seq experiments during meiosis. Nonetheless, analysis of published meiotic RNA-seq data for SK1 cells63 showed that 107 genes whose promoter regions carried the highest numbers of mutations in our experiments (≥5 mutations; Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Data 2, 12) exhibited higher meiotic expression levels than genes whose promoters lacked mutations. This finding suggests that transcription may contribute to the formation of mutable ssDNA during meiosis.

In conclusion, our data indicate that a massive accumulation of ssDNA results from programmed DSBs during meiosis. Following exposure to ssDNA-specific DNA-damaging agents, modeled in our experiments by A3A, ssDNA can produce multiple, often clustered, mutations. This increases the likelihood of changes in fitness due to epistatic effects, which are often detrimental, although this could rarely create novel adaptive sequences capable of bypassing fitness valleys and therefore driving evolution64,65. We suggest that similar mutagenic effects may arise in the human germline from a variety of exposures, including alkylating agents66, sulfites57, aldehydes67, reactive oxygen species68, or even from exposure to APOBECs or AID (activation-induced cytidine deaminase), as indicated by recent studies of human SNV signatures11,69. We propose that mutations promoted by ssDNA accumulated during meiosis may be especially consequential because they can contribute to the mutation load and promote hereditary diseases in humans. Our findings form a basis for future studies to further unravel the detailed mechanistic underpinnings of ssDNA formation and resolution during meiosis and its contribution to genetic diversity and pathological outcomes.

Methods

Strains and plasmid construction

All yeast strains were derivatives of PSL2 (W303) and PSL5 (YJM789) that are divergent for ~55,000 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)25 (Supplementary Data 13a; see also Supplementary Data 4 for high-confidence SNPs). In both parental strains, the entire URA3 gene was deleted by the delitto perfetto approach70. To construct AM4579, the ura3-29 reporter cassette26 was inserted between the BUD23 and ARE1 on chromosome III in the ura3∆ -derivative of PSL5 where HPHMX was also replaced by NATMX. All single-gene deletion mutants were constructed by transformation with a PCR-derived KANMX, BSDR (TEF/BSD from Invitrogen) or BleoR modules each flanked by sequences homologous to the regions surrounding the open reading frame of each gene71,72. All constructs were confirmed by PCR and by phenotype. Alternatively, gene deletions were introduced using the CRISPR/Cas9 system described in ref.73. In particular, the ssDNA deletion template was co-transformed with bRA89 plasmid harboring the corresponding sgRNA. sgRNAs used for gene disruption were cloned into the bRA89 backbone as described in ref.73. All strains employed in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 13a. The pAM288 (PsA3A /sA3A) plasmid expressing A3A under the control of SPO13 promoter was assembled from PCR amplified pSR35529 and SPO13 promoter (−160 bp to −3 bp) using the NEBuilder HiFi Assembly Mix. The oligonucleotide sequences are provided in Supplementary Data 13b.

Assessment of mutable meiotic ssDNA by random spore analysis

Random spore analysis

A single colony of PsA3A-transformed diploid strain containing ura3-29 reporter was inoculated into a YEPD media74 supplemented with 500 μg/mL hygromycin and grown at 30 °C overnight with aeration. 5 ml of the culture was transferred into 45 ml of hygromycin (500μg/mL)-supplemented pre-sporulation media (YPA, 1% w/v yeast extract, 2% w/v peptone, 2% w/v potassium acetate) and grown at 30 °C overnight with aeration. The remaining culture (pre-meiotic control) was plated on SD -Ura dropout media74 and also (after serial dilution) to YEPD media, then grown at 30 °C into colonies and used to calculate Ura+ reversion frequency. The YPA cultures were grown for 24 h, spun down, washed with sterile water, and transferred into sporulation liquid media (Kac, 2% w/v potassium acetate). The cells were incubated in Kac media for 72 h at 30 °C with aeration to allow efficient sporulation. The spores were collected by centrifugation, treated with diethyl ether to eliminate vegetative cells75, and plated on YEPD and SD-Ura to calculate meiotic Ura+ frequency. Statistical comparisons of mutation frequencies were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Identifications of mutations and mutation clusters by whole-genome sequencing (WGS)

Genomic DNA from meiotic outcomes was prepared as described in ref.31 and whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on Illumina platforms with 2×150bp reads. High confidence de novo mutations within meiotic outcomes in reference to S288C genome were identified among WGS reads by CLC Genomics Workbench 20. Reads were first trimmed (“Trim Reads”; default parameters), then mapped to the reference genome (“Map Reads to Reference”; default parameters). Next, InDels and structural variants were called (“InDels and Structural Variants”; default parameters) and used for local read realignment (“Local Realignment”; default parameters). Duplicate reads were then removed (“Remove Duplicate Mapped Reads”; default parameters), and variants were detected using “Fixed Ploidy Variant Detection” (ploidy = 1). The data were then exported to our software package MayoMate, where we initially required at least 10 reads and a 30% variant allele fraction (VAF). The 30% VAF threshold was chosen to allow detection of variants even in chromosomes that may have become aneuploid in otherwise haploid outcomes as well as in heterozygous diploid spo13Δ meiotic outcomes and in pre-meiotic controls. If any variant call was detected more than three times among samples from the same genotype, or more than ten times among all analyzed samples, it was removed. Next, because we can determine the ploidy of individual chromosomes, we ultimately applied an 80% VAF cutoff for variant calls on chromosomes identified as haploid.

To identify tracts of mutable ssDNA in meiosis, outcomes’ de novo mutations were used for identification of candidate mutation clusters (runs of at least three mutations separated by no more than 10 kb) similarly to19. The longest mutation sub-cluster with a P-value < 0.0001 was used as the de facto mutation cluster. Estimate of total long mutable ssDNA in each sample was calculated by summation of each sample’s cluster lengths. Mutations that were not a part of any mutation cluster were termed “scattered mutations”. To identify the sub-group of meiotic outcomes with efficient expression of A3A among ung1∆PsA3A meiotic outcomes not selected for mutation in ura3-29 reporter, only samples with more than 10 mutations genome wide were considered. Differences in the number of all mutations, scattered mutations, mutation clusters, average cluster length, average cluster mutations, and total mutable ssDNA between different strains were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test.

The patterning of C→T or G→A mutations within each cluster was used to subdivide mutation clusters into 5 categories (similarly to17): C→T or G→A non-switching clusters, clusters with a single switch of coordination from C→T to G→A, clusters with a single switch of coordination from G→A to C→T, clusters with multiple switches of coordination, and clusters with mutations different from C→T or G→A.

Associations of mutation clusters with recombination events

To map tracts of ssDNA (mutation clusters) to recombination events, ~48,500 high quality SNPs (Supplementary Data 4) between two parental strains (W303 derived and YJM789 derived) were identified and used for tracking of the parental SNPs in meiotic outcomes.

To find Gene Conversion (GC) and Cross Over (CO) events, midpoint positions between runs of parental SNPs assigned to one parent swapping into a run (1 or more SNPs) of other parent’s SNPs were first identified. Then, an iterative algorithm ranking haplotype block lengths within each meiotic outcome was used to call GC events for haplotype blocks ≤5 kb, or CO events for haplotype blocks >5 kb.

Mutation clusters were called “recombination associated” when a mutation cluster was found to overlap with at least one recombination event (average position between swapping runs of parental SNPs) within 5 kb distance to either side from the mutation cluster bounds. To determine rates of cluster association under random null hypothesis, the position of each recombination event (average position between swapping runs of parental SNPs), was randomized (1,000,000 times for associations at positions 0 bp and 5 kb, and 10,000 times for all other positions) and the association of clusters was measured for window distances between 0 bp (direct overlap) to 50 kb, in 500 bp increments.

Assessment of mutable ssDNA around recombination events

To examine the relationship between single-strand DNA (ssDNA) formation and recombination events in meiotic yeast cells, C→T and G→A mutation data from -10 and +10 kilobases relative to each identified GC and CO event was extracted and normalized to the position of the recombination event. Regions containing clustered or scattered mutations were identified and organized into distinct tracks by grouping mutations by sample and recombination event. The track’s ssDNA strand identity (either C→T/Watson or G→A/Crick) was inferred from the identity of mutations building the track. The distribution of tracks around the DSB axis and track’s identity were used for χ2 tests to assess the change in proportion of tracks attributed to resection or BIR for different mutants across different positions relative to the DSB.

Assessment of mutable ssDNA using tetrad analysis

To distinguish meiotic mutations from possible pre-meiotic and post-meiotic mutations, yeast spores from individual tetrads and dyads were micromanipulated and allowed to grow into colonies on YEPD plates. Next, each colony was divided into 4 sectors. The single colonies obtained from each sector were subjected to WGS. Mutations were identified as pre-meiotic if any of the mutations within a colony’s sectors were also identified within any sector of colony grown from a sister spore. Mutations were designated as meiotic if ¾ or all colony sectors contained the mutation, and it was not detected in a sister colony. The remaining mutations were marked as post-meiotic. The results of this analysis were further supported by pedigree analysis where individual spores were micromanipulated and allowed to divide; the resulting mother and daughter cells were then separated, and this procedure was repeated once more. All of the resulting colonies were subjected to WGS. The definition of pre-meiotic, meiotic and post-meiotic mutations were similar to those described for sectors where 4 individual pedigree outcomes of each spore were treated similar to 4 sectors described before.

To identify concordant mutation clusters (sharing the same genomic location but different mutations), we identified all mutation clusters present in the sets of meiotic and post-meiotic mutations of individual colony sectors. We then overlapped all resulting mutation clusters within colony sectors. Mutation clusters that were composed of different mutations (these clusters are absent when post-meiotic mutations were excluded) but shared similar (overlapping) genomic intervals among ¾ or all the colony sectors, were termed “concordant”. The definition of concordant clusters in pedigree analysis was similar with 4 pedigree outcomes of one meiotic spore treated similar to four sectors described for the sector-based analysis.

Calculation of mutation rates around transcription sites

To explore ssDNA mutagenesis in relation to the sites of transcription initiation (TSS) and termination (TTS) of protein coding genes and tRNA genes, C→T and G→A mutations (identified by analysis of random spores or by tetrad analysis) within 10 kb upstream and downstream of TSS and TTS sites of protein-coding and tRNA genes described in ref.55 were extracted and normalized to transcript direction and position. Further, all A3A recognition motifs around each transcript were identified and used for normalization of C→T and G→A mutation frequencies per A3A recognition motif at each position around the transcript. Median mutation rates within a 100 bp region around TSS were compared with a control region between 600 bp and 1200 bp downstream from TSS, by a Mann–Whitney U test. For TTS, median mutation rates within a 100 bp region around TTS were compared with a control region between −600 bp and −1200 bp, also by a Mann–Whitney U test.
