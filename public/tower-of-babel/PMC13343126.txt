Free speech exceptions in professional medical associations and schools
Authors: Yipeng Ge, Bilal Irfan
Published: 2026

On May 7, 2026, I (YG) attended the Ontario Medical Association (OMA) annual general meeting in Ottawa. Payment of OMA dues is mandatory for physicians practising medicine or conducting health research in Ontario.1 As a physician in this province, I had come to listen, learn about the association's priorities, and participate in the governance of an organization that claims to represent us and improve health care for the communities we serve.

For nearly an hour, I sat quietly in the meeting room. Then an OMA staff member approached me and asked me to remove an embroidered watermelon pin. I was told that the OMA had recently circulated guidance stating that demonstrations and displays of signage, clothing, or accessories with messaging were not permitted. When I asked why a watermelon pin was prohibited, I was told it “does cause issues for some” and was “triggering”, specifically because of “world events”. When I asked whether there had been other examples of political symbols or pins being removed, the staff member said they had not seen any.

I declined to remove the pin and was subsequently escorted out. The watermelon is a widely understood symbol of Palestinian identity and solidarity, partly because its colours mirror those of the Palestinian flag. For me, it was a small expression of solidarity with Palestinian colleagues and healthcare workers in Gaza–whom I met personally during my volunteer medical trip there in 2024–who have been killed, abducted, or displaced while hospitals have been repeatedly attacked and denied medical supplies.2 The Canadian Medical Association and American Medical Association have previously released statements condemning Russian attacks in Ukraine and have called for the need to protect healthcare workers, patients, and health facilities.3,4 Yet when the victims are Palestinian, and the perpetrator is Israel, too many institutions retreat into procedural language, claims of neutrality, and attempts to manage the discomfort of some of its members.

This is how anti-Palestinian racism often operates in professional health settings. It often appears as an administrative rule, adopted suddenly, applied selectively, and justified through the language of “safety”, “comfort”, and “orderly conduct”. The feelings of those offended by Palestinian solidarity are treated as “safety” concerns. Those expressing grief and collegial solidarity with Palestinians are treated as being disruptive or unprofessional. The OMA's position was especially troubling because it engages in political activities. It lobbies the provincial government, negotiates over physician interests, organizes advocacy at Queen's Park, and asks members to engage in collective professional governance. The real issue is not whether politics is present, but which politics are permitted.

Across Canada and the US, healthcare workers who speak about Palestine have been harassed, doxed, faced censorship, suspension, termination, and widespread intimidation.5,6 Clinicians and students have been warned, disciplined, or excluded for wearing symbols as modest as a watermelon pin or a kuffiyeh.7 For example in Ontario, physicians have been suspended for social media posts perceived to be sympathetic to Palestinians,8 and others have been removed from leadership roles for simply signing petitions advocating for a ceasefire.8 I (YG) too was briefly suspended from my medical residency program in 2023. Similarly, a US medical student was suspended for calling out the potential complicity of healthcare workers in war crimes.9 The National Institute for Health (NIH) even attempted to limit grant recipients’ aid if they engaged in boycotting Israeli companies and institutions linked to crimes against humanity.10

Professional associations and medical schools should not become instruments for laundering discrimination through codes of conduct. If a rule is truly neutral, it must be transparent and consistently applied. If the standard is merely that someone might feel uncomfortable, then the most powerful or easily offended constituency gains veto power over the moral expression of others.

North American healthcare institutions must confront the Palestine exception within the profession of medicine. We call on medical associations’ leadership and college deans to take an active role in promoting and defending physicians' and trainees' rights to engage in political and health advocacy without exceptions. Mechanisms should be instituted to protect healthcare workers from abuse, harassment, and retaliatory discrimination. The language of “neutrality” must not be used as a cover to silence particular anti-atrocity viewpoints while treating others as normal, and thus perpetuating structural racism. A profession committed to health, dignity, and human rights must be able to say that Palestinian healthcare workers matter, and that solidarity with them is not a threat but rather a moral and human imperative.
