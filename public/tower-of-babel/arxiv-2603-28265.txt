Abstract
In the (continuous) Euclidean k k -center problem, given n n points in R d \mdmathbb{R}^{d} and an integer k k , the goal is to find k k center points in R d \mdmathbb{R}^{d} that minimize the maximum Euclidean distance from any input point to its closest center. In this paper, we establish conditional lower bounds for this problem in constant dimensions in two settings. Parameterized by k k : Assuming the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), we show that there is no f ⁡ ( k ) ​ n o ⁡ ( k 1 − 1 / d ) f(k)n^{o(k^{1-1/d})} -time algorithm for the Euclidean k k -center problem. This result shows that the algorithm of Agarwal and Procopiuc [SODA 1998; Algorithmica 2002] is essentially optimal. Furthermore, our lower bound rules out any ( 1 + " ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm running in time ( k / " ) o ⁡ ( k 1 − 1 / d ) ​ n O ⁡ ( 1 ) (k/\varepsilon)^{o(k^{1-1/d})}n^{O(1)} , thereby establishing near-optimality of the corresponding approximation scheme by the same authors. Small k k : Assuming the 3-SUM hypothesis, we prove that for any " > 0 \varepsilon>0 there is no O ⁡ ( n 2 − " ) O(n^{2-\varepsilon}) -time algorithm for the Euclidean 2 2 -center problem in R 3 \mdmathbb{R}^{3} . This settles an open question posed by Agarwal, Ben Avraham, and Sharir [SoCG 2010; Computational Geometry 2013]. In addition, under the same hypothesis, we prove that for any " > 0 \varepsilon>0 , the Euclidean 6 6 -center problem in R 2 \mdmathbb{R}^{2} also admits no O ⁡ ( n 2 − " ) O(n^{2-\varepsilon}) -time algorithm. Discrete

At a technical level, the hardness proofs in the literature for all discrete clustering problems typically follow a common template: they reduce from some hard combinatorial problem

The Challenge of the Euclidean Metric.

For many geometric problems, a lower bound for the Euclidean metric implies a similar lower bound for every other
\ell_{p}
-metric, but the reverse is not necessarily true. This asymmetry stems from the fact that any
n
points in the
\ell_{2}
-metric can be embedded into any
\ell_{p}
-metric in
O(\log n)
dimensions with low distortion
[
38
]
(in particular see
[
47
]

[
26
]
for

\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{\infty}

k
-

and
k
-
median

[
20
]

[
51

29
]

[
51

This difficulty also extends to the fixed-dimensional setting, albeit for different reasons. The axis-aligned nature of metrics like
\ell_{\infty}
is highly amenable to the rigid, grid-like gadgets used in hardness reductions; indeed, the W[1]-hardness proof for planar
k\text{-}\mathsf{center}

[

was for the
\ell_{\infty}

We can rephrase our reduction for

(u,v)

. The gadgets we construct for

.

We can generalize the above observation to a graph with more edges. We choose the grid graphs due to their regular structure in

•

•

Roughly speaking, each edge encodes a linear equation; all the edges of the graph together encode a system of

In this section, we list the complexity-theoretic assumptions used in this paper.
Our parameterized complexity results (

{\sf W}[1]\neq{\sf FPT}
.

Hypothesis 1
(Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH)
[
36
,
35
]
)

There is some
\delta>0
such that no
O(2^{\delta n})
-time algorithm that takes as input a 3SAT formula on
n
variables can decide if it is satisfiable.

Hypothesis 2
(
\mathsf{W}[1]\neq\mathsf{FPT}

[
21
]
)

For every computable function
f
,
there is no
f(k)\cdot\operatorname{poly}(n)
-time algorithm that can decide whether an input graph on
n
vertices contains a clique of size
k
.

In the fine-grained complexity setting, our results (
Section

6
and
Section

7
) rely on the assumed complexity of the 3SUM problem.

3SUM Problem.

Given an array
A[1\ldots n]
of integers in
\{-N,\ldots,N\}
, decide whether there exist
i,j,k\in[n]
such that
A[i]+A[j]+A[k]=0
.

Hypothesis 3
(3SUM Hypothesis
[
30
]
)

For every
\epsilon>0
, 3SUM cannot be solved in time
O(n^{2-\epsilon})
.

Pătraşcu
[
46
]
in his seminal work found a reduction from 3SUM to the Convolution-3SUM problem defined below, ruling out subquadratic time for the latter. See also
[
40
,
16
]
for randomized and deterministic variants of this reduction.
As already noted in
[
8
]
, the range of elements can be made
\{1,\ldots,n^{2}\}
using a randomized hashing reduction from
[
12
,
46
]
.

Convolution-3SUM problem.

Given an array
A[1\ldots n]
of integers in
\{1,\ldots,n^{2}\}
, determine whether there exist
i,j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that: (i)
i+j=k
and (ii)
A[i]+A[j]=A[k]
.

Theorem 5

(
[
46
]
)

For every
\epsilon>0
, Convolution-3SUM cannot be solved in time
O(n^{2-\epsilon})
, unless the 3SUM Hypothesis fails.

Next, we introduce the Gap-Convolution-3SUM problem, which has a gap in the admissible range for
i,j,k
and is more symmetric than Convolution-3SUM.

Gap-Convolution-3SUM problem.

Given an array
X[-n\ldots n]
of
2n+1
integers in
\{-n^{2},\ldots,n^{2}\}
, decide whether:

•

YES Case:

\exists~i,j,k\in\{-\lfloor\frac{n}{100}\rfloor,\dots,\lfloor\frac{n}{100}\rfloor\}
such that
i+j+k=0
and
X[i]+X[j]+X[k]=0
.

•

NO Case:

\not\exists~i,j,k\in\{-n,\dots,n\}
such that
i+j+k=0
and
X[i]+X[j]+X[k]=0
.

Note that the two cases are disjoint but not exhaustive, making this a promise problem. An algorithm for Gap-Convolution-3SUM may return
anything
if the instance is not in the YES or NO case, but must return
YES
in the YES case and
NO
in the NO case

Lemma 1

If Gap-Convolution-3SUM is solvable in time
O(n^{2-\delta})
for some absolute constant
\delta>0
, then the 3SUM Hypothesis fails.

Given an instance of Convolution-3SUM, i.e., an array
A[1..n]
of integers in
\{1,\ldots,n^{2}\}
, we construct an array
X[-n^{\prime}\dots n^{\prime}]
as follows:

•

n^{\prime}:=300n
.

•

X[n+i]:=A[i]
for
i\in[n]
.

•

X[-2n-i]:=-A[i]
for
i\in[n]
.

•

X[i]:=3n^{2}
for all other
i
.

Note that the entries of
X
are in
\{-n^{2},\dots,3n^{2}\}\subseteq\{-(n^{\prime})^{2},\dots,(n^{\prime})^{2}\}
. The reduction is in
\tilde{O}(n)
time. Now we show the correctness of the reduction.

If
A
is a YES instance, i.e.,
\exists~i,j,k\in[n]
s.t.
i+j=k
and
A[i]+A[j]=A[k]
, then for
i^{\prime}:=n+i,j^{\prime}:=n+j,k^{\prime}:=-2n-k
, we have
i^{\prime}+j^{\prime}+k^{\prime}=i+j-k=0
and
X[i^{\prime}]+X[j^{\prime}]+X[k^{\prime}]=A[i]+A[j]-A[k]=0
. Furthermore,
i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\in\{-3n,\ldots,3n\}=\{-\lfloor\frac{n^{\prime}}{100}\rfloor,\dots,\lfloor\frac{n^{\prime}}{100}\rfloor\}
. Thus,
X
is a YES instance.

If
X
is not in the NO case, then there exist
i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}
such that
i^{\prime}+j^{\prime}+k^{\prime}=0
and
X[i^{\prime}]+X[j^{\prime}]+X[k^{\prime}]=0
. Note that no
3n^{2}
-entry can be part of a solution of
X[i^{\prime}]+X[j^{\prime}]+X[k^{\prime}]=0
and thus,
i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\in\{n+1,\dots,2n\}\cup\{-3n,\dots,-2n-1\}
. Observe that
i^{\prime}+j^{\prime}+k^{\prime}=0
if and only if two of them come are
\{n+1,\dots,2n\}
and the other is from
\{-3n,\dots,-2n-1\}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in\{n+1,\dots,2n\}
and
k^{\prime}\in\{-3n,\dots,-2n-1\}
. Let
i=i^{\prime}-n,j=j^{\prime}-n,k=-2n-k^{\prime}
. Then (i)
i+j-k=i^{\prime}+j^{\prime}+k^{\prime}=0
, giving
i+j=k
and (ii)
A[i]+A[j]-A[k]=X[n+i]+X[n+j]+X[-2n-k]=X[i^{\prime}]+X[j^{\prime}]+X[k^{\prime}]=0
, giving
A[i]+A[j]=A[k]
. Thus,
A
is not in the NO case.

\displaystyle 0=

Remark 3

It is worth noting a consequence of our reduction when applying the hardness from Theorem
6

[
44
]
in the regime where the domain size
n
is a universal constant. In this case, assuming ETH, there is no algorithm for binary CSP on grid graphs with

for any

Our reduction in the proof of Theorem

\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
with

centers. The reduction establishes a gap, making it hard to distinguish if the optimal radius is at most

(1+\varepsilon_{0})

.

covers the point
\widetilde{{\mathcal{A}}}[2i]
. Since

is even, the

-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\|c^{-}-\widetilde{{\mathcal{A}}}[2i]\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+x\varepsilon)^{2}+(y\varepsilon)^{2}+(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-\varepsilon-\varepsilon(-x-1))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{2}+\cancel{\sqrt{2}x\varepsilon}+(x\varepsilon)^{2})+(y\varepsilon)^{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-\cancel{\sqrt{2}x\varepsilon}+(x\varepsilon)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+3M^{2}\varepsilon^{2}

Similarly, it suffices to verify that
B^{-}
covers
\widetilde{{\mathcal{B}}}[2j]

\displaystyle\|c^{-}-\widetilde{{\mathcal{B}}}[2j]\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(x\varepsilon)^{2}+(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+y\varepsilon)^{2}+(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-y\varepsilon)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x\varepsilon)^{2}+(\frac{1}{2}+\cancel{\sqrt{2}y\varepsilon}+(y\varepsilon)^{2})+(\frac{1}{2}-\cancel{\sqrt{2}y\varepsilon}+(y\varepsilon)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+3M^{2}\varepsilon^{2}

Finally, we verify that

covers
\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}[2k]
. Since

is even,
{\sf Odd}(2k)=0
, evaluating the coordinate with
-\varepsilon
.

\displaystyle\|c^{-}-\widetilde{{\mathcal{C}}}[2k]\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{2}-x\varepsilon)^{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-y\varepsilon)^{2}+(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}-\varepsilon-(-\frac{\varepsilon z}{\sqrt{2}}-\varepsilon))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{4}-x\varepsilon+(x\varepsilon)^{2})+(\frac{1}{4}-y\varepsilon+(y\varepsilon)^{2})+(\frac{1}{2}-z\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon z)^{2})

\displaystyle 1-(x+y+z)\varepsilon+3M^{2}\varepsilon^{2}

Using

is bounded by

.

For the anchor points, the identical calculation to

for all

Soundness

In this section, we will show that a solution of

Our argument is split into three steps. In the first step, we argue that

Define

Lemma 12

6
-Center in the Plane

In this section, we prove
Theorem

4
by showing a reduction from the Gap Convolution-3SUM problem. From the input array, we will construct in
O(n)
time a set of
O(n)
points
P
on the plane such that:

P
can be covered by exactly 6 disks of radius
1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1.7}
if the instance of the Gap Convolution-3SUM problem is YES. Conversely, if the instance of the Gap Convolution-3SUM problem is NO, then
P
cannot be covered by exactly 6 disks of radius
1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1.7}

Therefore, a truly subquadratic-time algorithm for the
6\text{-}\mathsf{center}
problem would yield a truly subquadratic-time algorithm for the Gap Convolution-3SUM problem, contradicting the 3SUM hypothesis. The idea is to adapt the approach for
10\text{-}\mathsf{center}
in
Section

4
by removing some of the hexagonal vertices (recall that these roughly correspond to locations of the centers of circles defining the clustering). Each of the hexagons used in the construction of the point set for

is essentially replaced with a quadrilateral, eliminating the need for
4
locations for centers, at the cost of an added layer of technical considerations. The ensuing necessary adjustment of the placement of the points along the two quadrilaterals is precisely what this section addresses.

In this section, we will first provide the details of the reduction from the Gap Convolution-3SUM problem to the
6\text{-}\mathsf{center}
problem, and then analyze the completeness and soundness of the reduction.
