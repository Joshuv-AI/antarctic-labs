VistaHop: Benchmarking Multi-hop Visual Reasoning for Visual DeepSearch
Hang He, Chuhuai Yue, Chengqi Dong, Chengcheng Wan, Ting Su, Haiying Sun, Jiajun Chai, Xiaohan Wang, Guojun Yin

Abstract
Visual DeepSearch tasks require multimodal large reasoning model (MLRM) agents to answer complex visual queries by repeatedly inspecting image regions, grounding reasoning in visual evidence, and connecting fine-grained clues across multiple steps. However, existing benchmarks mainly evaluate single-step visual understanding or isolated image-question answering. They have limited task difficulty, shallow reasoning hops, and one-shot image inspection, and thus fail to evaluate the ability to iteratively revisit visual evidence and reason across multiple steps. In this work, we introduce VistaHop , a comprehensive benchmark for Visual DeepSearch , designed to evaluate vision-centric search and multi-hop visual reasoning that requires models to inspect images multiple times and connect evidence across different visual regions. VistaHop comprises 300 images, 25 visual search scenarios, and 350 multi-hop QA tasks. We also propose VistaArena , a unified evaluation environment that supports tool-based interactions, including visual retrieval, image inspection, and evidence-grounded reasoning. Experiments show that even state-of-the-art MLRMs remain far from solving VistaHop , with the best model SenseNova-MARS-32B achieving only 24.31% Pass@1. These findings highlight the importance of specialized benchmarks and training methods for Visual DeepSearch . Our code and dataset are available at https://github.com/ahang0712/vistahop .

We develop

3.1
Benchmark Construction

As summarized in Figure
2
, we construct
VistaHop
through a seven-step process.

3.1.1
Image Source Filtering and Entity Extraction

Candidate images are collected from publicly available and verifiable sources, including Wikimedia Commons
48
, Wikipedia pages
49
, Unsplash
47
, and other open-access web sources with clear entity-level visual content.
To support reliable
Visual DeepSearch
, we retain only high-resolution images with sufficient visual evidence, recognizable entities, and local details for region-level inspection (details in Table
2
).

We then extract image-grounded entities as entry points for downstream reasoning.
Given an image, we prompt
Gemini-3.1-Pro

14
to perform region-level entity extraction with OCR and captioning capabilities.
For each candidate region, it returns an approximate bounding box, a raw label, a canonical entity name, an entity type, a confidence score, and local visual attributes.
The entity types include
Person
,
Organization
,
Product
,
Location
,
Symbol
, and
Event
, while the visual attributes capture appearance, color, scene context, logo patterns, and other discriminative cues.

We further normalize entity names, resolve aliases, remove duplicate detections, and filter low-confidence entities.
Each retained entity is explicitly tied to a visual region and serves as a visual anchor for subsequent evidence-chain construction.

3.1.2
Entity Knowledge Enrichment and Seed Selection

After extracting image-grounded entities, we enrich each entity with Wikipedia-derived textual knowledge and select valid seed entities for evidence-chain construction.
For each entity, we query Wikipedia using its normalized name and retrieve its page content.
An LLM then extracts a compact structured record, including a short description, an inferred entity type, useful properties, and related entities.

These enriched records form the initial seed pool.
Entities with valid descriptions or properties are retained as seed candidates.
If Wikipedia is insufficient, Wikidata-based properties are supplemented.
Entities that still lack valid properties are discarded, as they cannot support reliable neighbor retrieval or evidence-chain expansion.

3.1.3
Evidence Chain Construction

We construct evidence chains from the selected seed entities to generate multi-hop
Visual DeepSearch
tasks.
Each chain starts from an image-grounded seed entity and reaches a target answer through relational hops:

c=(v_{0}\xrightarrow{r_{1}}v_{1}\xrightarrow{r_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{r_{k}}v_{k}),\quad k\geq 5,

(1)

where
v_{0}
is the visual seed,
v_{k}
is the target answer node, and
r_{i}
denotes the relation between adjacent entities.
The length constraint ensures that the task requires long-chain reasoning rather than shallow lookup.

Starting from each selected seed entity, we query Wikidata to retrieve neighboring entities and relation labels.
The relations are normalized into 8 categories:
part-whole
,
member-collection
,
causal
,
temporal
,
spatial
,
comparative
,
attributive
, and
associative
.
To reduce noise, we keep at most 30 retrieved neighbors as the pre-selection pool, from which an LLM selects up to 10 candidates most relevant to the current chain context.

Each selected hop is then verified by an LLM-based relation checker.
For each candidate pair, the LLM judges whether the relation is valid, assigns a confidence score, and generates a short rationale.
A hop is retained only if its confidence score is no lower than
\tau_{\text{nli}}=0.9
.
We further filter overly familiar entities using an LLM-assigned familiarity score
f_{e}\in[0,1]
and remove candidates with
f_{e}\geq 0.4
.
The remaining candidates are ranked by familiarity, relation confidence, and Wikidata frequency, while a global cache prevents repeated entity selection.

We perform depth-first expansion until the target depth
D
is reached.
Each completed chain is scored by entity and relation diversity:

D(c)={}\alpha\min\left(1.0,\frac{|\mathcal{V}_{c}|}{|c|}\right)+\beta\min\left(1.0,\frac{|\mathcal{R}_{c}|}{|c|}\right),

(2)

where
\mathcal{V}_{c}
and
\mathcal{R}_{c}
denote the sets of unique entities and relation types in chain
c
.
We set
\alpha=0.6
and
\beta=0.4
, with
\alpha+\beta=1
.
Chains are sorted by this score, and top-ranked chains are retained.

Finally, we apply an LLM-based optimization step to merge semantically duplicate adjacent nodes, such as synonyms or near-duplicate entities, and re-verify affected edges when necessary.
The output is a set of verified evidence chains:

\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{m}\}.

(3)

Each chain records the seed entity, intermediate nodes, relation types, verification confidence, familiarity scores, hop count, and metadata.

3.1.4
Textual QA Construction

Given a verified evidence chain

c_{j}
,
we generate a natural-language question
q_{j}
about the terminal node
v_{k}
, and let the canonical name or normalized value of
v_{k}
be the answer.

The question is written in an indirect form to reduce direct string matching, entity lookup, and text-only shortcuts.
We describe the seed or intermediate entities using visual or knowledge-grounded clues, such as “
the organization whose logo appears in the image
.”
The output of this step is a set of candidate textual QA pairs:

\mathcal{Q}=\{(q_{j},a_{j},c_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m},

(4)

where
q_{j}
is the generated question,
a_{j}
is the target answer, and
c_{j}
is the corresponding evidence chain.

3.1.5
VQA Generation and Anti-Leakage Verification

This step converts candidate textual QA pairs into VQA items and verifies that the final questions cannot be reliably solved from text alone.
For each candidate pair
(q_{j},a_{j},c_{j})
, we attach the corresponding image reference
I_{j}
, convert the evidence chain into a structured reasoning path
\rho_{j}
, and simplify the question into
\tilde{q}_{j}
for better readability.
The simplification improves fluency while preserving the target answer and the underlying reasoning chain.

Multi-agent anti-leakage verification.

To detect potential textual shortcuts, we perform anti-leakage verification on the final VQA question
\tilde{q}_{j}
with a three-agent loop.
The
Solver
receives only the question text and attempts to answer it without image or evidence chain.
The
Judge
checks whether the answer matches the ground truth, whether textual clues reveal it, or whether the question is ambiguous, under-specified, or non-unique.
If issues are detected, the
Rewrite
agent revises the question based on Judge feedback while preserving the original image reference, target answer, and evidence chain.
The loop runs at most
R_{\max}=3
rounds, and unresolved questions are discarded.

Condition ablation.

To further reduce hidden textual shortcuts, we remove each condition or clue one by one and ask the text-only
Solver
to answer.
If the answer remains recoverable, the removed condition is considered redundant or leaking, and the question is rewritten.

The final VQA item is represented as:

y_{j}=(I_{j},\tilde{q}_{j},a_{j},\rho_{j},M_{j}),

(5)

where
M_{j}
stores metadata such as hop count, relation types, source entity, verification records, and generation records.
The output is the single-chain VQA set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{single}}=\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
.

Each retained VQA item is answerable from the intended image-grounded evidence chain but cannot be reliably solved from the question text alone.

3.1.6
Multi-Chain Fusion

Single-chain VQA instances mainly evaluate whether a model follows one evidence chain from a visual seed to the answer.
To evaluate repeated visual evidence seeking and cross-chain reasoning, we construct fused questions by combining multiple related VQA chains from the same or related visual context.
Each fused question requires solving several component chains before deriving the final answer.

Specifically, we select
K\geq 2
thematically or logically related VQA instances.
For each VQA, we extract an intermediate value from its answer or chain metadata (
e.g.
, year, count, ranking, numerical quantity).
We then apply a numerical or conditional fusion operation to these values, including addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, maximum, minimum, average, and conditional selection.
The fused question describes each component through its original visual or evidence-grounded clue without exposing the intermediate values.
The model must identify multiple visual anchors, solve their evidence chains, and perform the final reasoning.
Each fused item stores the fused question, final answer, extraction details, component VQA instances, and fused reasoning path. These items form the fused VQA set.

3.1.7
VQA Verification and Human Revision

As a final quality-control step, three human annotators manually verify each candidate.
They check whether the reasoning path leads to the annotated answer, whether the question is ambiguous or misleading, and whether the answer is unique. Instances that fail any check are discarded.
The annotators then revise the retained questions for readability while preserving the visual grounding, reasoning path, and final answer, ensuring final questions are valid, unambiguous, uniquely answerable, and readable.

3.2
Benchmark Quality

Visual dependence.

We test whether each question truly requires the image by comparing the probability of the ground-truth answer under the original image and a heavily masked image:

r_{\mathrm{vis}}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o\mid q,I)}{\pi_{\theta}(o\mid q,I_{\mathrm{mask}})}.

(6)

Items with
r_{\mathrm{vis}}\leq 1.20
are manually inspected; if the text-only clue can still determine the answer, the item is rewritten or discarded. In our final retained set, the mean
r_{\mathrm{vis}}
is 2.41, the median is 2.16, and 92.3% of retained items satisfy
r_{\mathrm{vis}}>1.20
before human revision.

Question difficulty.

We define three difficulty levels according to the total number of reasoning hops required to answer a question.
For each question, the hop count
H
denotes the number of relational reasoning steps needed to reach the final answer.
For fused questions,
H
counts the total reasoning hops across all required component chains.

We design
VistaArena
for automated testing on
VistaHop
. It contains a
Search Agent
for tool-augmented reasoning and a
Validation Agent
for answer checking (Figures
4
and
5
).

Search Agent.

The agent iteratively observes the question, image, history, and accumulated evidence, then either invokes one tool or submits an answer. It can use
Text Search
for external facts,
Image Search
for reverse-image evidence, and
Image Crop
for local inspection.
Text Search
integrates a web crawler for accessing detailed webpages and a summarization model for condensing retrieved content before returning it to the agent. The process runs for at most
N
rounds, with at most one tool call per round.

Validation Agent.

We use an LLM-as-judge to evaluate the final response. The primary metric is
Pass@1
: whether the first submitted answer is semantically equivalent to the ground truth. Minor surface-form variants are accepted, while incorrect, incomplete, ambiguous, or unsupported answers are rejected. We also report average tool calls, average rounds, and tool utilization, computed as average tool calls divided by the maximum allowed calls.

For tool-augmented settings, the judge also checks whether retrieved evidence is relevant and whether the answer is supported by visual or textual evidence. Each instance is evaluated five times and averaged; systems are anonymized, question order is randomized, and the judge model is distinct from all evaluated models.

assistant turns and at most one tool call per turn. For
Text Search
, the retrieval top-
k
is 3;
Image Search
returns up to 5 reverse-image-search results; and the default answer temperature is 0.7. All experiments are run on Intel Xeon Gold 5218 CPUs and
16\times
H200-141G GPUs.

5.2
Overall Performance

As shown in Table
4
, the best model reaches only 24.31% Pass@1, indicating that
Visual DeepSearch
remains difficult. Tool use is essential: average Pass@1 rises from 7.72% in
No-Tool
to 20.11% in
Search+Crop
. The average number of tool calls under
Search+Crop
is 8.02, consistent with the benchmark’s 7.43 average target hop count.

Performance also drops clearly from L2 to L3: under
Search+Crop
, average Pass@1 decreases from 24.40% to 15.03%. This shows that very long chains and multi-anchor fusion are substantially harder than single-chain reasoning. Agentic models such as SenseNova-MARS-32B and Tongyi-DeepResearch-30B-A3B use tools more effectively than general-purpose MLRMs, but their accuracy remains limited, suggesting persistent weaknesses in visual grounding, evidence revisiting, and cross-chain reasoning.

Human evaluation.
We validate 210 sampled outputs across models, settings, and difficulty levels. Three annotators judge semantic equivalence, answer specificity, and ambiguity. Human judgments agree with the LLM-as-judge results in 90.8% of cases, with Cohen’s
\kappa=0.81
, supporting the reliability of automatic evaluation.

5.3
Ablation Study

We compare three inference settings:
No-Tool
,
Search
, and
Search+Crop
. Table
4
shows that external search improves over direct answering, and image cropping further helps when questions require small logos, text, or local objects. The gains are not uniform: some models retrieve irrelevant evidence or fail to bind retrieved facts back to the correct image region. The multi-anchor setting remains the hardest because it requires decomposition, repeated visual inspection, and final evidence fusion.

5.4
Sensitivity Study

Table
5
studies the effect of the maximum number of reasoning rounds
N
. Increasing
N
improves performance up to
N=10
, where the model has enough budget to inspect visual anchors, search intermediate evidence, and verify the answer. Larger budgets introduce more redundant calls and noisy context, causing performance to saturate or decline. This suggests that
Visual DeepSearch
requires not only more evidence, but also accurate early visual grounding and efficient search control.

6
Conclusion

We introduce
VistaHop
, a benchmark for evaluating
Visual DeepSearch
with 350 VQA tasks across 25 scenarios.
VistaHop
tests repeated image inspection, visual-anchor grounding, and long-hop evidence connection with external knowledge. We also develop
VistaArena
, a unified framework for direct answering, search-augmented reasoning, and crop-assisted visual inspection. Experiments on 7 MLRMs show that current models remain far from solving the benchmark: the best model, SenseNova-MARS-32B, achieves only 24.31% Pass@1.

Limitations

Construction dependence and scalability.

Although our construction process is largely automated, it still relies on strong multimodal models for entity extraction, question generation, relation verification, and anti-leakage checking, followed by human revision for readability.
This design improves benchmark quality, but also introduces cost and reproducibility challenges when scaling to more images, domains, or languages.
Future work can reduce this dependence by adopting stronger open-source multimodal models and more deterministic verification modules.
