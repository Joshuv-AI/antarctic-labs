Synergistic photoregulation of abscisic acid and gibberellin enhances embryogenic callus induction and proliferation in Indica rice
Authors: Taoli Liu, Shaoshen He, Ziang Yi, Junjie Chen, Qi Qiu, Jiaqi Ding, Ruitao Xiang, Kaifa Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEfficient embryogenic callus induction remains a major constraint in the genetic transformation of Indica rice. Although light is known to promote callus formation, its underlying phytohormonal regulation is not well understood. In this study, mature embryos of Indica rice ‘T98B’ were cultured under a 12 h light–dark cycle (light) or continuous darkness (dark).ResultsCallus induction occurred more rapidly under light. The ABA levels under light were 92.1% and 59.7% higher on 5 and 7 days of induction, and 62.2% higher on day 7 of subculture compared with dark. GA3 content was 20.6% lower under light on day 5 but increased by 43.5% and 44.4% on days 7 of induction and subculture, respectively. Transcriptome analysis showed significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in hormone signaling pathways. qPCR validation revealed early upregulation of the ABA receptor gene OsPYL6 and the GA-degrading gene OsGA3ox8 under light, followed by downregulation of OsPYL4/OsPYL6 and strong induction of the GA biosynthesis gene OsGA3ox2 at 7 days of induction. These results indicate that light sequentially activates ABA signaling, which may contribute to the initiation of embryogenic callus formation, and subsequently enhances GA biosynthesis, which may regulate proliferation.ConclusionsOverall, light sequentially activates ABA signaling, which may be associated with embryogenic callus initiation, and then enhances GA biosynthesis, which may regulate sustained proliferation, providing a hormone-based strategy to boost Indica rice transformation efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09214-1.Keywords: Rice, Callus, Gene regulation, Hormone, Signaling

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a major global food crop, and improving its varieties is essential to enhance crop yields and ensure food security [1]. Biotechnology offers an effective approach for developing new and enhanced rice cultivars. Genetic transformation is an effective method for rice variety improvement and functional genomics research. Establishing a stable and efficient regeneration system is the foundation for conducting genetic transformation research on rice. There are several reports on Agrobacterium mediated transformation in rice [2], whereas Japonica rice was successfully transformed using Agrobacterium tumefaciens in 1994 [3]. However, the transformation efficiency in the Indica rice varieties is very low. Most Indica rice varieties also show a low rate of callus growth or low regeneration frequency in conventional culture [4].

Light, as a key environmental factor, substantially affects the morphogenesis and regeneration efficiency of callus in Indica rice by regulating photoreceptor signaling pathways and phytohormone homeostasis. Callus induction in the Indica rice cultivar ‘Shuhui 527’ was most effective under light at 32°C, and the highest quality embryogenic callus was observed during subculture. The mature embryos of Indica rice cultivars, i.e., ‘Minghui 86’, and ‘Qishanzhan’ and ‘Zhongyouzao 8’ could obtain an average callus induction rate of about 84.9% after being continuously illuminated at 32℃ for 10 days. In Japonica rice, Yunyin, the NMB medium under light with 24 h had the best induction effect, whereas darkness was the least effective [5]. The same phenomenon was also found in other crops. For example, in Portulaca oleracea, calli cultured under light conditions exhibited faster growth and a light green phenotype, whereas those cultured in continuous darkness showed slower growth [6]. Therefore, light can promote the induction and growth of callus, but the mechanism of its action has not been reported yet.

Phytohormones play a crucial role in regulating the growth and development of plants. Skoog and Miller [7] demonstrated in plant tissue culture that auxin and cytokinin regulate morphogenesis and plant development, leading to the formulation of the “phytohormone balance” hypothesis. Abscisic acid (ABA) has a complex role in the induction and maintenance of callus. For example, ABA can promote the formation and differentiation of callus [8, 9], whereas it also shows a negative correlation with phytohormones such as auxin and cytokinin, and may regulate the plant’s stress response by inhibiting excessive cell division and growth [10]. Gibberellins (GA₃), in synergy with auxins and cytokinins, jointly promote the formation of callus. For instance, the cooperative effect of IAA and GA plays a significant role in the formation and differentiation of callus [11]. GA₃ enhances the expression of genes related to catalytic activity and redox enzymes (such as glutathione reductase), eliminates reactive oxygen species (ROS), and inhibits the browning of callus [12]. In peonies, exogenous GA3 alleviates callus browning and maintains the integrity of cell membranes [13]. During the induction and differentiation of callus in Spatholobus suberectus, the content and ratio of endogenous phytohormones (such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), GA, and ABA) undergo dynamic changes. A high IAA/ABA ratio is conducive to callus induction, whereas a low IAA/ABA ratio is beneficial for differentiation [14]. However, how light affects the spatiotemporal distribution of endogenous phytohormones (such as IAA, GA, and ABA) in callus of Indica rice and how light signals and phytohormone pathways cooperatively regulate the induction and maintenance of embryogenic callus have not yet been reported.

In this study, the Indica rice cultivar ‘T98B’ was employed as the experimental material to research transcriptional regulation during the induction and proliferation process of callus under light. The phenotype, physiological, and biochemical traits were combined with transcriptome sequencing to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms governing embryogenic callus formation and growth.

Materials and methods

Materials and growth conditions

The Indica rice cultivar ‘T98B’ was provided by the Hybrid Rice Research Center of Hunan Province. The callus induction medium was N6 basic medium (PYEAST, QP1025) supplemented with plant gel (PYEAST, QP1014), 2.5 mg L⁻¹ 2,4-D, and 0.2 mg L⁻¹ 6-BA. The subculture medium was N6 basic medium supplemented with plant gel (PYEAST, QP1014) and 2.0 mg l− 1 2,4-D.

Light treatment on callus induction culture and subculture

After shelling the T98B seeds, the mature embryos were treated with 70% (v/v) ethanol for 2 min, rinsed 3–5 times with sterile water. Then treated with 2.5% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite working solution (prepared by diluting the commercially available 5% NaClO stock solution (LIONSER, Q/KLS 015) with sterile water at a 1:1 volume ratio, final effective chlorine ~ 2.5%) for 35–50 min, rinsed 3–5 times with sterile water, and finally inoculated onto the N6 induction medium. Sterilized mature embryos of indica rice T98B were inoculated evenly on the N6 induction medium and cultured in an illuminated incubator at 26–28 °C under white LED light with an illumination intensity of 34 µmol m− 2 s− 1 for 7 days. The culture conditions were established as two treatment groups: light (12 h of light/12 hours of dark) and dark (24 h of darkness). The callus was excised under sterile conditions and placed on the subculture medium for 7, 14, and 28 days. Callus induction and growth under light and dark conditions were observed, and the fresh weight of callus was determined on days 1–7 of induction and days 7, 14, and 28 of subculture. Callus induction rate was calculated on days 1–7 of induction, where Callus induction rate (%) = (Number of explants forming callus / Number of inoculated explants) × 100.

Measurement of phytohormone contents in callus

The callus induced from mature embryos of T98B cultured for 5 days (YD5D), 7 days (YD7D), and subcultured for 7 days (JD7D) under light and dark conditions was immediately wrapped in aluminum foil, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for phytohormone content determination. Three callus tissues were taken as one sample, and all samples were measured with three biological replicates. Each biological replicate was technically repeated three times, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Duncan’s multiple range test was used for statistical analysis to ensure statistical power, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. The phytohormones were quantified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (8030 plus, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Fresh callus tissue (100 mg) was ground into powder thoroughly using a mortar. 1 mL of methanol was added, mixed well, and sonicated for 10 min., and then transferred to a metal bath and shaken for 3 h, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. At 4 °C and then 0.5 m L of methanol was added to the remaining residue, and shaken in a metal bath for 1 h. The two extracts were combined, centrifuged, and passed through a 0.22 μm filter. An aliquot of 750 µL of the sample was put into a 2 mL brown centrifuge tube and concentrated to dryness. Then, 150 µL of internal standards of deuterium-labeled phytohormones (ABA-d6, GA3, IAA-d5, JA, and SA, Olchemim, Olomouc, Czech Republic) were added and mixed. It is sonicated for 5 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant is collected. All the supernatant is used for LC-MS/MS analysis [15].

Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) separation was performed on an Exion L CUPLC system (AB Sciex, USA) equipped with an Acquity UPLC® CSH C18 (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm, Waters) column. The temperature of the column was set at 40℃. The sample injection volume was 2 µl. Eluents consisted of 0.05% formic acid with 2 mM ammonium formate water (eluent A) and 0.05% formic acid methanol (eluent B). The flow rate was set at 0.25 ml/min. An elution gradient was performed as follows: 0 ~ 2 min, 10% B; 2 ~ 4 min, 10 ~ 30% B; 4 ~ 19 min, 30 ~ 95% B; 19 ~ 19.10 min, 95 ~ 10% B; 19.10 ~ 22 min, 10% B.

The MS analysis was performed using an AB Sciex Triple Quadrupole 6500 plus mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA) in the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The electrospray ionization (ESI) parameters in positive mode were as follows: ion source voltage, 4500 V; ion source temperature, 400℃; curtain gas, 40 psi; atomization gas, 40 psi, and auxiliary gas, 25 psi.

Total RNA extraction, library preparation, and de novo sequencing

Callus samples at YD5D, YD7D, and JD7D under light and dark conditions were collected and processed using the same snap-freezing and storage method described in Sect. 2.3, and then used for transcriptome analysis. Phenotypic observation of callus showed that the callus of ‘T98B’ began to form on the YD2D under light and the YD3D under dark, and the fresh weight of callus had significant differences between light and dark treatments on the YD5D and YD7D, which are the key stages for the morphological establishment and rapid growth of embryogenic callus. Three callus tissues were homogenized as one sample, with three biological replicates for each sample. Total RNA was extracted from the callus using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and RNA degradation and contamination were monitored on a 1% agarose gel. RNA purity was checked using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA), and accurate RNA concentration was quantified using the Qubit® RNA Assay Kit with a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA).

RNA integrity was evaluated using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). A total of 1.5 µg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext®UltraTMRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA), and then the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina Hiseq 4000 (Illumina, USA), and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. Raw sequencing reads were aligned to the Oryza sativa ssp. indica reference genome (MSU Rice Genome Annotation Project Release 7.0, MSU7.0) using the HISAT2 (v2.2.1) alignment tool with default parameters.

De novo transcriptome assembly and functional annotation

Raw data were processed using the NGS QC Toolkit to remove reads containing poly-N and low-quality reads to obtain clean reads. To eliminate batch effects, all RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing experiments were performed synchronously with randomization of sample loading; raw data were normalized by FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) for gene expression level quantification, and DESeq2-based size factor normalization was applied for subsequent differential expression analysis to ensure data comparability. Read counts for each gene were obtained using Htseq-count, and FPKM values for each gene were calculated using Cufflinks. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the estimateSizeFactor and nbinomTest functions of DESeq (v1.38.0) with biological replicates; the statistical test for differential expression was a negative binomial distribution test, with DEGs screened by the criteria of a corrected P-value (FDR) ≤ 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| ≥ 1. For functional enrichment analysis, GO (Gene Ontology) enrichment of DEGs was performed using the ClusterProfiler R package (v4.6.0) with Fisher’s exact test, and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway enrichment was conducted using KOBAS (v3.0) with the hypergeometric test; both enrichment analyses used FDR ≤ 0.05 as the threshold for significant enrichment.

Real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the callus collected at YD5D, YD7D, and JD7D of light and dark. Total RNA was purified from gDNA contamination and reverse transcribed to cDNA using HiFiScript All-in-one RT Master Mix for qPCR (CWBIO, China). A PCR program was performed with SuperStar Blue Universal SYBR Master Mix (CWBIO, China) in a final reaction volume of 20 µL, containing 1.0 µL of undiluted cDNA template (synthesized from 1.0 µg total RNA in a 20 µL reaction, resulting in a concentration of 50 ng µL− 1 RNA equivalent), 10 µL of 2 × SuperStar Blue Universal SYBR Master Mix, and 0.4 µL (10 µM) of forward and reverse primers (0.2 µL each). Thermal cycler settings consisted of an initial hold at 95℃ for 30 s, then 45 cycles of 95℃ for 10 s, and 60℃ for 30 s. Three biological replicates and three parallel reactions were performed. To confirm primer specificity and ensure the absence of primer dimers or non-specific products, a melting curve analysis was executed immediately following the final amplification cycle by ramping from 65 °C to 95 °C, yielding a single sharp peak for each assay. Primer amplification efficiencies (E) were determined using a five-point serial dilution curve of pooled cDNA; all primer pairs exhibited efficiencies between 95% and 103% with correlation coefficients (R2) exceeding 0.99. The actin gene of indica rice was used as an internal standard, whereas the gene primers are shown in Table S1.

Results

Light promotes the induction and growth of callus from mature embryos of Indica rice

Light has a significant impact on the growth and differentiation of plant cells, tissues, and organs. The results showed that the growth rate of callus under light was faster than that under dark. Callus began to form on the second day of induction (YD2D) under light, whereas it began to form on the third day of induction (YD3D) under dark (Fig. 1A). The fresh weight of callus growth under light was faster than under darkness, particularly on days 3, 5, and 7 of induction, which was increased by 23.3%, 40.6%, and 94.4%, respectively, compared with dark (Fig. 1B). After 7 days of induction (YD7D), the callus was transferred to subculture. On the 7th day (JD7D), 14th day (JD14D), and 28th day (JD28D) of subculture, the fresh weight of individual callus under light was increased by 1.97, 1.30, and 2.53 times, respectively, compared with those under dark (Fig. 1C). These results indicated that light can promote the induction and growth of callus in Indica rice. Light treatment significantly accelerated callus initiation and improved the embryogenic callus induction rate of indica rice ‘T98B’. At YD7D, the induction rate of the light group reached 97.22%, significantly higher than 88.89% of the dark. In addition, the fresh weight of callus in the light group was significantly higher than that in the dark group at both induction and subculture stages, confirming that light promotes both callus induction and proliferation in indica rice.

Light affects the content of phytohormones in callus

Phytohormones play a crucial role at the stages of callus induction and subculture. Results of phytohormones contents in callus under light showed that indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), ABA, and jasmonic acid (JA) were increased, salicylic acid (SA) and gibberellin A3 (GA3) were inhibited at the stages of YD5D than those under dark. IAA, ABA, GA3, and JA were increased, and SA was inhibited at YD7D and JD7D (Fig. 2A-E) than those under dark. IAA was increased by 22.6%, 91.1%, and 158% at YD5D, YD7D, and JD7D under light. ABA was increased by 92.1%, 59.7%, and 62.2% at YD5D, YD7D, and JD7D under light. GA3 was decreased by 20.6% at YD5D, increased by 43.5% and 44.4% at YD7D and JD7D under light than those under dark, respectively. Moreover, the JA levels were increased by 1.3 times and 6.5 times at YD5D and YD7D with light compared with dark, whereas the SA was inhibited by 6.4%, 56.3%, and 43.9% at YD5D, YD7D, and JD7D under light, respectively. IAA and ABA act as positive regulators of early-stage induction, with their endogenous contents significantly elevated, and JA content was also increased in the early induction stage (though no significant differences in the expression of JA-related genes were detected). The GA₃ levels increased during the later induction and differentiation stages.

To further elucidate the biological significance of the dynamic changes in ABA and GA₃, we calculated the ABA/GA₃ ratio at each key time point. Under light conditions, the ABA/GA₃ ratio was higher at YD5D, due to the elevated ABA content and reduced GA₃ content. At JD7D under light, no significant differences were found in the ABA/GA₃ ratio between light and dark conditions.

Transcriptome analysis of differentially expressed genes induced by light

Results suggest that 1013,588,324 raw reads in the 18 samples were identified, with 95.8% Q30 value (Table S2). The uniquely mapped reads accounted for 94.6% of the total mapped reads in the 18 samples (Table S3). Gene expression was estimated using the fragments per kilobase per million (FPKM) method. Putative differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by pairwise comparison of the analyzed samples using the following criteria: P-value ≤ 0.05 and |Log2foldchange (FC)| ≥ 1. Using these criteria, the expression of DEGs was obtained, and the results were shown in Fig. 3. To effectively decouple general time-course developmental changes from light-specific regulatory responses, YD5D_D (the early induction stage under dark before prominent morphological divergence) was designated as a common baseline reference for subsequent groups. Functional classification by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping

In order to evaluate the potential functions of these DEGs in callus, GO analysis was performed. In light vs dark, there were 36 DEGs at induced culture 5 day (YD5D), 202 DEGs at induced culture 7 day (YD7D), 119 DEGs at subculture 7 day (JD7D), which were assigned to at least one term in the ‘biological process’, ‘cellular component’, and ‘molecular function’ categories. The GO functional enrichment analysis of DEGs showed that the most enriched genes were ‘biological process’, the second was ‘cellular component’, and the least was ‘molecular function’. The ‘biological process’ category, including abiotic stimulus process, phytohormones process, and regulation of cellular biosynthetic process, were prominently represented at YD5D, YD7D, and JD7D. In the ‘cellular component’ category, plastid, chloroplast, and organelle sub-compartments were prominently represented at YD5D, YD7D, and JD7D, whereas transcription regulator activity, protein binding, and DNA-binding transcription factor activity dominated the ‘molecular function’ category at YD5D, YD7D, and JD7D. The results indicated that light affected the induction and growth of callus by influencing the phytohormone response and photoreaction within the callus, thus affecting DNA transcription inside the callus.

The KEGG pathway assignments analysis of DEG showed that DEG mainly belonged to MAPK signaling pathway-plant and plant phytohormone signal transduction at YD7D, and mainly belonged to starch and sucrose metabolism, glutathione metabolism, and phytohormone signal transduction at JD7D (Fig. 4).

Genes related to phytohormones express differences at the stage of induction culture and subculture of callus under light

In order to analyze the regulatory function of phytohormones in callus, the expression patterns of genes related to phytohormones were analyzed (Fig. 5). It was found that the gene expression related to IAA, JA, and SA did not show significant differences between light and dark treatments. The expression of the ABA receptor protein gene OsPYL6 (LOC_Os05g39580) was upregulated at YD5D under light compared with those under dark, whereas the expression of the ABA receptor protein genes OsPYL6 (LOC_Os05g39580) and OsPYL4 (LOC_Os03g18600) was downregulated at YD7D and JD7D under light compared with those under dark. The expression of the gibberellin 2-oxidase gene OsGA2ox8 (LOC_Os05g48700) and synthesis genes OsGA20ox2 (LOC_Os01g66100), OsGA3ox2 (LOC_Os01g08220), and OsGA20ox1 (LOC_Os03g63970) changed during callus induction and subculture. Specifically, the expression of the key gibberellin synthesis gene OsGA20ox2 was downregulated at YD5D, whereas the expression of the gibberellin synthesis gene OsGA3ox2 was significantly upregulated at JD7D. Therefore, light regulates callus induction and growth by influencing the ABA signaling pathway and the GA synthesis pathway.

Comprehensive analysis of the expression of phytohormone-related genes, especially those involved in ABA and GA

Since there are many biological pathways that are enriched in the DEGs list induced by light based on GO and KEGG, these DEGs are analyzed using separate pathways, including the ABA signaling and GA biosynthesis pathways. At YD5D, the relative expression levels of OsPYL6, a receptor protein of ABA, were dramatically increased. In the ABA signaling pathway, the expression of OsAPK8, a gene encoding SnRK2 kinase, was upregulated, while the expression of OsGA20ox2, a key gene for GA synthesis, was downregulated. The GA-degrading genes OsGA2ox9 and OsGA2ox3 were upregulated. At YD7D, the ABA receptor genes OsPYL6 and OsPYL4 were significantly downregulated, as was OsAPK8. In contrast, the GA biosynthesis gene OsGA3ox2 was upregulated. At JD7D, the relative expression levels of OsPYL6 and OsPYL4 continuously decreased, the expression of the OsAPK8 gene in the ABA signaling pathway was down-regulated, whereas the expression of OsGA3ox2 was significantly up-regulated (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Light, as a key signaling factor, plays a pivotal role in plant morphogenesis and development by regulating growth, differentiation, and metabolic processes. In plant tissue culture, light strongly influences callus induction, growth, and differentiation. For instance, studies on pepper cotyledons have shown that red and white light enhance callus induction, whereas green and blue light exert inhibitory effects, highlighting the wavelength-specific regulatory roles of light in tissue culture [16]. The effects of different light qualities on the proliferation of callus in broccoli were in the order of white light > red light > blue light > green light > yellow light. Moreover, the content of soluble protein and water content in callus under different light qualities were correlated with the proliferation of callus [17]. In studies on jasmine, exposure to light at 1,500–2,000 lx combined with a specific phytohormone regimen (MS medium supplemented with 2.0 mg/L BA and 0.2 mg/L NAA) resulted in a callus induction rate of up to 100%, highlighting the synergistic effect of light and hormonal cues on embryogenic induction [18, 19]. The proliferation rate of callus of Salvia miltiorrhiza was faster in light than in the dark, and the growth curve was in an “S” shape [20]. Light intensity had no effect on the time of inducing callus from lateral buds of Elymus nutans, but it significantly affected the induction rate of callus. Under the light of 100 µmol m− 2 s− 1, the total induction rate and effective induction rate of callus were significantly higher than those under 50 µmol m− 2 s− 1 and in the dark [21]. Light promotes callus formation in Anthurium, with the highest induction rates observed under moderate light intensity (800–1,500 Lux). However, intensities above 3000 Lux inhibit callus induction, indicating that excessive light can be detrimental [22, 23]. The effect of light on callus induction varies among different species and explants [24, 25]. During rice callus culture, continuous 24-hour light combined with a specific culture medium was found to be optimal for inducing embryogenic callus in the japonica variety ‘Yunyin’, while complete darkness resulted in the lowest induction efficiency. In addition, light affects callus formation by regulating the expression of light cycle-related genes [26]. The Thai rice variety ‘Pakaumpuel’ has the best callus induction under light, but excessive light during subculture may inhibit the number of regenerated shoots [27]. In contrast, the Malaysian ‘MR269’ and the Bangladeshi ‘Binadhan-13’ have better callus induction in the dark. The effect of light during callus culture may be regulated by species and medium composition, and this difference is related to the genotype-related regulation of the light signaling pathway [28, 29].

Consistent with our findings, Do et al. [30] reported over 94% callus induction frequency in Korean rice cultivars ‘Samkwang’ and ‘Dongjin’ under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. The 97.22% induction rate achieved in our study under 12-h light/12-h dark cycle is slightly higher than this reported value, indicating the high efficiency of our light-mediated culture system. Meanwhile, our result is markedly higher than the 80% induction rate under light conditions in Thai indica rice landrace ‘Pakaumpuel’ [31], reflecting the strong genotype specificity of light response in rice. Different from the finding that indica rice ‘Sadamota’ achieved 100% callus induction rate under continuous dark [32], our ‘T98B’ showed higher induction efficiency under light treatment, further confirming that the regulatory effect of light on rice callus induction has significant genotype dependence. This study demonstrated that light treatment not only significantly accelerates callus initiation but also improves the embryogenic callus induction rate and proliferation efficiency of indica rice ‘T98B’ (Fig. 1), providing an optimized culture strategy for embryogenic callus induction and genetic transformation of indica rice.

The induction and proliferation of callus are key steps in the in vitro culture and genetic transformation of mature rice embryos. This process requires a complex network of one or more endogenous phytohormones to work in synergy, antagonism, and cascade reactions. In the induction and subculture of callus from indica rice ‘T98B’, light increased the endogenous ABA content of rice callus, which is consistent with the fact that light promotes the endogenous ABA content in the embryonic callus of barley, whereas darkness reduces the ABA content [33]. Similarly, when spinach is transferred from short-day to long-day conditions, the ABA content in the plant increases by 2 to 3 times [34]. After short-day treatment of birch trees, the ABA levels in leaves and buds increase [35]. Light can regulate endogenous ABA levels by influencing the expression of key enzymes in ABA synthesis and participate in the regulation of light-dependent physiological processes. In the early stage of callus induction in Indica rice ‘T98B’ (YD5D), light upregulated the expression of the ABA receptor gene OsPYL6 and the gene OsSAPK8 encoding SnRK2 kinase, whereas the expression of the GA-degrading enzyme gene OsGA3ox8 was upregulated, resulting in a decrease in GA content. At the early stage of callus induction, high endogenous ABA and low GA content are conducive to the induction of embryogenic callus. At the YD7D and JD7D, although the endogenous ABA content was high under light, the expression of the receptor genes OsPYL4 and OsPYL6 was downregulated, leading to the inhibition of the downstream OsSAPK8 gene expression. During the early stage of callus induction under light, ABA levels were elevated, accompanied by increased expression of ABA receptor genes and upregulation of OsAPK8, a SnRK2 kinase in the ABA signaling pathway, leading to a significantly higher ABA/GA₃ ratio that may be associated with the initiation of embryogenic callus formation. The expression of the GA synthesis gene OsGA3ox2 was significantly upregulated, and the GA3 content increased. At the callus proliferation stage, high endogenous GA3 content is conducive to maintaining the embryogenesis of callus and inhibiting browning. Therefore, light can activate the synergistic effect of endogenous ABA and GA, which may regulate callus induction and proliferation and maintain embryogenesis of callus. At the molecular level, light signals are perceived by rice photoreceptors in callus cells, and the core light-responsive TF HY5 (up-regulated at YD5D in this study) may potentially participate in the crosstalk between light signaling and the ABA/GA pathways. HY5 is hypothesized to correlate with the transcriptional expression changes of key genes in the ABA and GA pathways, coinciding with the upregulation of the ABA receptor gene OsPYL6 and the GA-degrading gene OsGA2ox8 at the early induction stage, which might contribute to ABA signaling activation and reducing GA3 content. In addition, light-responsive TFs identified in this study (OsbZIP48 and OsERF922) are proposed to link light signals with ABA/GA pathway regulation, potentially modulating the dynamic balance of the two pathways during callus induction and proliferation, suggesting a possible mechanism for the sequential activation of ABA for induction and GA for proliferation. Our findings are consistent with the phenotype of significantly increased fresh weight of callus and well-maintained embryogenesis under light. The study further elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which light regulates the ABA and GA pathways to enhance the induction and maintenance of embryogenic callus in Indica rice, providing new insights into the interplay between light and phytohormones.

Furthermore, transcriptome analysis revealed that light induced a large number of DEGs related to phytohormone signaling, stress response, and transcriptional regulation. GO and KEGG analyses further indicated that light regulation involves key pathways such as “plant hormone signal transduction” and “MAPK signaling pathway”, and significantly affects the expression of various transcription factors, such as bZIP, ERF, and NAC. For instance, the expression changes of OsbZIP48 and OsERF922 under light may integrate light signals with phytohormone pathways to jointly regulate callus induction, proliferation, and embryogenic quality. These findings provide new clues for understanding the molecular network of light-hormone interaction in regulating embryogenic callus formation in indica rice.

This study has two main limitations. First, the research was conducted exclusively on the indica rice genotype ‘T98B’; the general applicability of the light-mediated ABA/GA regulatory mechanism for callus development needs to be verified in other indica rice genotypes. In addition, the dark-to-light transition experiment, a key control to clarify the dynamic response of callus to light signal switching, was not included in the current experimental design. Second, the findings are mainly based on correlative data of endogenous hormone levels and gene expression patterns and lack direct causal evidence from exogenous hormone rescue/inhibitor treatments and transgenic functional verification (overexpression/knockout) of key genes (e.g., OsSAPK8, OsGA2ox8). In our follow-up research, we will expand the experimental materials to multiple indica genotypes, add dark-to-light transition assays, and perform exogenous hormone intervention and transgenic validation experiments to further confirm the general applicability and causal relationship of the identified regulatory mechanism.

Conclusions

The results provided new mechanistic insights into how light regulates callus induction and proliferation. During the early stage of callus induction under light, ABA levels were elevated, accompanied by increased expression of ABA receptor genes and upregulation of OsAPK8, a SnRK2 kinase in the ABA signaling pathway. Simultaneously, expression of key GA₃ biosynthesis genes was downregulated, while GA-degrading genes were upregulated, resulting in reduced GA₃ content. At the early stage of callus induction, it may contribute to the formation of embryogenic callus by activating OsAPK8 and inhibiting GA3 content, whereas at the later stage of callus induction and the subculture stage, the endogenous ABA content in the callus was high, but the expression of the receptor protein genes OsPYL6 and OsPYL4 for ABA signal transduction was downregulated, resulting in the downregulation of ‘downstream genes’ OsAPK8 in the ABA signaling pathway and the downregulation of the GA3 oxidase gene OsGA2ox3. Moreover, the expression of the GA3 biosynthesis gene OsGA3ox2 was significantly upregulated on the 7th day of subculture, promoting GA3 biosynthesis. During the callus proliferation stage, GA₃ played a dominant role in sustaining embryogenesis and preventing browning, resulting in higher-quality callus growth (Fig. 8).
