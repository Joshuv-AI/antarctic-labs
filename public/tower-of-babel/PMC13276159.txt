Exosomes: A new frontier in the treatment of neurological diseases
Authors: Xin Li, Hong Chen, Peixin Xu, Xin Guo, Jing Gao, Dongmei Yao, Yurong Wang, Tong Wang, Bingchun Liu, Jianlong Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryExosomes (Exos) are an essential class of extracellular vesicles enriched with a wide range of biologically active molecules, which gives them a unique advantage in participating in intercellular signaling and communication and serving as carriers for drug delivery. Exo-based diagnostic and therapeutic strategies are currently hot topics in disease research. Owing to their naturally low immunogenicity, good biocompatibility, ability to penetrate the blood‒brain barrier (BBB), and engineered modifications, exos have significant advantages and possible applications in the treatment of nervous system diseases. Due to the serious harm of neurological diseases to human health, they have been widely studied by researchers. Exos can be administered in a variety of ways, including intranasal administration, intracranial administration, local stereotactic injection, and encapsulation in biomaterials, each of which has its own advantages and disadvantages. However, several requirements need to be met before exo-based therapies can be implemented, such as the standardization of isolation and purification techniques, an in-depth understanding of the mechanism of action, and safety assessments and regulation for clinical translation. The aim of this review is to provide a comprehensive overview of the biogenesis, molecular composition, function, and delivery modes of exos and their therapeutic roles and mechanisms in neurological diseases (e.g., multiple sclerosis (MS), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and stroke) and to discuss the current challenges and future perspectives to support ongoing research and clinical applications.Subject areas: Health sciences, Medicine, Medical specialty, Internal medicine, Neurology, Medical manufactured object, Drug delivery system, Natural sciences, Biological sciences, Neuroscience

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases are a group of conditions caused by chronic, progressive degenerative changes in the central nervous system.1 Among the various neurodegenerative diseases, Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) are the most common. In addition, conditions such as stroke, MS, and spinal cord injury (SCI) also involve significant neurodegenerative pathological changes.2 These diseases threaten human health and quality of life. Particularly with an aging population, the incidence of neurological diseases continues to rise, placing a heavy burden on patients’ families and society as a whole.3 However, current treatment strategies for these diseases are largely limited to symptom relief and are unable to halt or reverse disease progression.4 Furthermore, traditional drug delivery faces critical bottlenecks such as poor blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability and insufficient targeting.5 Therefore, the development of novel therapeutic interventions capable of crossing the BBB to achieve targeted treatment has become an urgent need in the field of neurodegenerative diseases.

Exosomes (Exos) are a type of extracellular vesicles (EVs) actively secreted by cells. Their outer layer consists of a phospholipid bilayer, while their lumen is rich in numerous bioactive substances, including lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.6 Depending on their source, exos can be classified into plant-derived exos (PDEs) and mammal-derived exos (MDEs). Compared to MDEs, PDEs offer unique advantages for clinical applications. Studies have shown that PDEs exhibit 60%–80% lower immunogenicity than MDEs in clinical trials.7 In addition, exos can be secreted by various cell types, including mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), lymphocytes, and macrophages.8 Exos offer a highly promising therapeutic strategy for neurodegenerative diseases due to their inherent ability to cross the BBB, their low immunogenicity, and their unique advantages as drug delivery vehicles.9

This review aims to systematically elucidate the biogenesis, molecular composition, and functions of exos, as well as engineering strategies and delivery methods for their use as therapeutic agents. It focuses on their therapeutic potential and mechanisms of action in neurological disorders, as well as the challenges associated with exo-based treatments for these conditions.

Overview of the core mechanisms by which exos cross the BBB

The BBB is a critical physiological structure that protects the central nervous system. While its selective permeability helps maintain a stable intracranial environment, it also constitutes the primary barrier to drug entry into the brain, significantly limiting the efficacy of most neurotherapeutic agents.10,11 The mechanisms by which exos cross the BBB primarily include receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT), the paracellular route, and membrane fusion. Among these, RMT is the primary focus of research and the most well-studied mechanism. In RMT, ligands on the surface of exos first bind specifically to corresponding receptors on the luminal surface of brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs), triggering endocytosis and the formation of endosomes. Subsequently, the vesicles undergo intracellular transport, acidification, and sorting, and are ultimately released into the brain parenchyma via exocytosis from the basolateral side.12 In addition to the RMT pathway, exos can also cross the BBB via paracellular pathways (i.e., through the gaps in tight junctions between endothelial cells). However, this typically requires the loosening of tight junction structures under inflammatory conditions and makes a relatively limited contribution under physiological conditions.13 Furthermore, membrane fusion offers distinct advantages for exo crossing of the BBB. It allows exos to bypass the conventional endocytosis-endosomal-lysosomal pathway, thereby preventing the degradation of intracellular cargo. Membrane fusion facilitates the direct release of therapeutic biomolecules—such as proteins, RNA, and lipids—into the cytoplasm of target cells.14 It is worth noting that the transport efficiency of exos is not only influenced by the affinity of surface ligands and receptors but is also closely related to exo size, surface charge, and cellular origin. Under physiological conditions, exos play a crucial protective role in maintaining the stability of the BBB. In particular, exos secreted by BMECs themselves (BMEC-Exos) can reinforce BBB integrity by activating key intercellular signaling pathways.15,16 Under pathological conditions (such as brain tumors, stroke, and traumatic brain injury (TBI)), the structure and permeability of the BBB undergo significant changes. The disruption of tight junctions and the abnormal activation of endocytic transport further increase the efficiency of exo crossing, but this also carries the risk of reduced targeting accuracy.14,17 Engineering modifications may inadvertently alter the interaction between exos and the BBB. For example, small EVs can protect the BBB, whereas large EVs can disrupt the BBB and exacerbate neuroinflammation.18 In addition, Immunogenicity varies depending on differences in the patient’s neuroimmune microenvironment. For instance, BMEC-Exos can modulate T cell activation under inflammatory conditions, leading to the same product potentially inducing different biological effects due to variations in individual neuroimmune microenvironments.18 Although the mechanism by which exos cross the BBB via RMT has been extensively studied and validated, its clinical application currently faces translational bottlenecks, including low exo yield, poor batch consistency, insufficient stability of targeting modifications, and the lack of large-scale Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) grade production standards. Although commercial kits for exo isolation (e.g., Exo-Quick) have been developed, many challenges remain. For example, exos isolated by ultracentrifugation exhibit higher purity in exosomal miRNAs compared to those obtained via the Exo-Quick method. However, the Exo-Quick method co-precipitates a far greater amount of free miRNAs from serum than ultracentrifugation.19 Furthermore, since the specific mechanisms by which exos cross the BBB may vary across different cell sources and disease models, the translation of basic research into clinical applications requires further optimization.

Structure and biogenesis of exos

Exos play a crucial role in tissue repair and regeneration and regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis.20,21 They contain a wide variety of bioactive substances. Proteins include tetra-transmembrane proteins (such as CD9, CD63, and CD81), membrane transporters, antigen-presenting molecules (MHC-I and MHC-II), the Alix protein involved in exosome biogenesis, and heat shock proteins (such as HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90).22,23,24 Nucleic acids include long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), and miRNAs.25,26 Among these, miRNAs are typically the most abundant and can influence gene expression in recipient cells through post-transcriptional regulation.24,27 Additionally, exos contain fragments of genomic DNA. Lipids, including cholesterol, sphingolipids, and phosphatidylserine, play a key role in maintaining structural stability and facilitating cell-cell interactions (Figure 1).23,28

The biogenesis of exos is primarily divided into three stages. First, the cell membrane invaginates to form endosomes, which subsequently fuse to form early endosomes.29 Subsequently, the membrane of the early endosomes invaginates to encapsulate cytoplasmic components such as proteins and nucleic acids, forming intraluminal vesicles (ILVs), which then mature into late endosomes, or multivesicular bodies (MVBs).30,31 Finally, some MVBs fuse with the cell membrane to release ILVs into the extracellular space as exos, while others are transported to the lysosomal pathway for degradation (Figure 1).32,33

EVs are primarily classified into three major categories: exos, microvesicles, and apoptosomes. Each plays a unique biological role in physiological and pathological processes.34 Microvesicles are typically classified based on their lipid composition and plasma membrane receptors. Their cargo generally reflects the molecular composition near the plasma membrane, including biomolecules transported to the plasma membrane, and often contains fragmented ribosomal RNA and miRNA.35 Apoptosomes are the largest category of EVs, with diameters of approximately 1–5 μm, formed through budding and protrusion of the cell membrane during apoptosis. These vesicles are released from the surface of apoptotic cells upon cell death, carrying cellular debris, organelles, and bioactive molecules to participate in intercellular communication.36 However, due to their large size, apoptotic bodies may face significant challenges when crossing the BBB. Exo formation involves dynamic coordination between endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent and ESCRT-independent mechanisms, as well as the sorting of proteins, RNA, and lipids, which confers functional specificity to exos in physiological and pathological processes.37,38 Furthermore, with a particle size of 50–150 nm, exos can easily cross the BBB due to their small size. Therapeutic modalities for exos

As a novel biological delivery vehicle, the drug delivery mechanisms and carrier design of exos are currently the primary focus of research.

There are several routes of administration for exos, primarily including intravenous injection, local injection, intranasal administration, inhalation, and oral administration. Intravenous injection is currently the most commonly used route of administration in preclinical and clinical studies. Its primary advantage lies in enabling systemic drug distribution and rapid entry into the bloodstream. However, the primary challenge with intravenous injection is low bioavailability in the brain. This is because a large number of exos are recognized and cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system through phagocytosis before reaching the target tissue.43 Local injection is a more precise method of administration. It bypasses systemic circulatory barriers while delivering high concentrations of exos directly to the site of the lesion, thereby enhancing local therapeutic effects and reducing systemic side effects.44 Although local injection avoids capture by the mononuclear phagocyte system, it faces unique tissue-specific barriers. For example, in peripheral nerve blocks, the perineural membrane acts as a rate-limiting barrier; exos must cross this barrier to reach their axonal targets.44 Inhalation delivery is primarily used for treating pulmonary diseases. Studies have shown that, compared to intravenous injection, inhalation delivery allows exos to accumulate selectively and at high concentrations in the lungs, thereby more effectively alleviating pulmonary inflammation.45 However, for nebulization, uncertainties regarding exo integrity, delivery efficiency, and deposition in the lungs during the nebulization process pose significant challenges.46 Currently, oral administration is primarily used for gastrointestinal diseases. Wei et al. administered PDEs orally to a mouse model of colitis. PDEs significantly reduced disease severity, restored colonic tissue structure, and enhanced intestinal barrier integrity by upregulating the expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin) and mucin (MUC2).47 However, orally administered exos must overcome the extremely acidic gastric environment, the intestinal mucosal layer, and the absorption barriers of intestinal epithelial cells.48 It is worth noting that intranasal administration is a non-invasive delivery method that has demonstrated significant potential in the treatment of central nervous system disorders in recent years. Its primary advantage lies in its ability to bypass the BBB and deliver exos directly from the nasal cavity to the brain parenchyma via the olfactory and trigeminal nerve pathways, thereby avoiding systemic exposure and associated toxicity.49 Relevant studies have shown that intranasal administration is superior to intravenous injection in terms of reaching the brain.50 Following intranasal administration, peptide hydrogels promote the sustained and thermosensitive release of MSC-Exos, thereby prolonging their residence time and significantly enhancing their potential for treating AD.51 Major barriers to drug delivery from the nose to the brain include mucociliary clearance, poor drug retention, enzymatic degradation, poor permeability, and low bioavailability.49 The selection of an exo delivery route is a trade-off between therapeutic goals and the need to overcome specific barriers. Although intravenous injection is widely used, it is limited by rapid clearance and nonspecific distribution. Local injection can increase local concentrations but faces tissue-specific barriers. Respiratory administration, while highly targeted to the lungs, requires attention to physical stability during nebulization. Oral administration, though non-invasive and convenient for long-term use, must overcome the gastric acid environment. Intranasal administration has opened new avenues for the treatment of brain diseases, but challenges related to the mucosal barrier and enzymatic degradation must be addressed. In the future, extending circulation time through surface modification, achieving controlled release using advanced materials, and developing hybrid exosystems will be key directions for overcoming these delivery barriers and advancing the clinical translation of exos.

The design of exos carriers can rely on a variety of biomaterials, primarily including metals, ceramics, polymers, and hydrogels, with the specific choice depending on their composition and properties. Among these, hydrogels are materials composed of a hydrophilic polymer network containing a large amount of water, which can be prepared from natural biopolymers or synthetic polymers. Well-designed hydrogels typically exhibit high biocompatibility, controllable degradation, and the ability to support cell infiltration and adhesion.52 Given these characteristics, hydrogels have garnered significant attention as carrier platforms for loading exos to achieve sustained, localized release of therapeutic molecules.53 In recent years, the combined application of exos and hydrogels has demonstrated tremendous potential in the treatment of neurological diseases. For example, previous studies have loaded engineered exos into thermosensitive chitosan hydrogels, achieving smart sustained release and glial cell reprogramming at the site of SCI via intranasal administration, thereby significantly promoting neural repair.54 Another study utilized reactive oxygen species (ROS) responsive hyaluronic acid hydrogels loaded with exos derived from three-dimensional cultures to achieve microenvironment-responsive release at the site of SCI, effectively alleviating oxidative damage and neuroinflammation.55 Furthermore, 3D-bioprinted exo-functionalized brain-derived acellular matrix hydrogels have been shown to sustain exo release for up to 14 days in a cerebral hemorrhage model, thereby reducing neuroinflammation and improving neurological function.56 These studies indicate that exo-hydrogel systems are gradually transitioning from basic research to preclinical validation, offering promising new therapeutic strategies for central nervous system disorders.

In summary, hydrogels serve as effective delivery carriers for exos, overcoming key challenges such as poor stability, short retention time, non-specific accumulation, and rapid systemic clearance encountered in their in vivo applications. Their excellent biocompatibility and tunable physicochemical properties (e.g., degradation rate and adhesion strength) make them an ideal platform for achieving localized, sustained, and controlled release of exos. Existing studies, whether in the field of neural regeneration or bone repair, have strongly demonstrated the significant advantages of the “exos-hydrogel” composite strategy compared to the use of exos alone, foreshadowing its immense translational potential in the field of regenerative medicine. However, the injectability of hydrogel materials, their long-term biocompatibility after implantation, and the economic feasibility of large-scale production remain key challenges that must be overcome for their clinical application.

Engineering strategies and technological advances in exos

Strategies for exo engineering

Although exos show promising potential as therapeutic tools, their inherent targeting capabilities are limited, which restricts their broader clinical utility. To achieve greater therapeutic efficacy, researchers have developed various engineering approaches to endow exos with auxiliary functions, such as enhanced targeting, penetration, and multiple biological effects.57

Donor cell pretreatment strategies represent an indirect engineering approach that modifies the microenvironment during cell culture to induce the secretion of exos with specific compositions or enhanced functions.58 For example, compared with untreated exos, those obtained through hypoxic pretreatment demonstrate a more pronounced advantage in promoting SCI repair. Shao et al. confirmed that, compared to adipose-derived stem cell exos (ADSC-Exos), hypoxia-pretreated ADSC-Exos (ADSC-HExos) provide stronger antioxidant protection and significantly improve cell survival rates in the injured area.59 Previous studies have reached similar conclusions. Four weeks after SCI, the expression levels of BAD (a BCL-XL/Bcl-2-related death activator) and cleaved caspase-3 in the ADSC-HExos group were significantly lower than those in the ADSC-Exos group. Conversely, the expression level of Bcl-2 was significantly higher in the ADSC-HExos group than in the ADSC-Exos group. This indicates that ADSC-HExos can significantly reduce apoptosis to repair SCI, with superior efficacy compared to ADSC-Exos.60 Compared to strategies that load drugs via methods such as sonication or extrusion after isolation, this method does not disrupt the integrity of the exo membrane, thereby avoiding cargo aggregation or membrane structural damage that may result from external forces. However, the efficiency of hypoxia pretreatment is low.61 The exact mechanism by which hypoxia pretreatment modifies the contents of exos remains unclear. The exact mechanism by which hypoxic pretreatment modifies the contents of exos remains unclear, making it impossible to achieve precise regulation. Furthermore, challenges such as inter-batch variability, insufficient standardization of pretreatment parameters, and a lack of long-term safety data continue to pose significant obstacles to this approach.

Surface chemical modification is a key technological tool for enhancing the application potential of exos. By targeting exos for modification and encapsulating proteins or genetic information, the information-transmission capabilities of exos can be fully leveraged. This enhances the selectivity of exos toward recipient cells, increases drug concentration at the site of action, reduces adverse effects, and maximizes therapeutic efficacy.62 For example, lysosome-associated membrane protein 2b (Lamp2b) is a key membrane component of exosomal protrusions and serves as one of the most commonly used scaffolds in this approach. By fusing viral macrophage inflammatory protein II (vMIP-II) to Lamp2b (vMIP-II-Lamp2b), we genetically engineered exos derived from M2 macrophages (vMIP-II-Lamp2b-M2-Exo). These exos are capable of targeting chemokine receptors highly expressed in damaged spinal cord tissue, inhibiting MAPK and Akt signaling pathways, and promoting the transition of microglia from the M1 to the M2 phenotype, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects.63 Yang et al. developed an acid-cleavable transferrin (Tf)-modified engineered exo system (ACTE). This design utilizes the specific binding of Tf to transferrin receptors (TfR) on the luminal surface of BMECs to initiate endocytosis. Subsequently, acid-responsive dissociation of Tf occurs in the acidic environment of the endosome, separating the exo core from the Tf-TfR complex. This prevents the exos from being re-transported into the bloodstream or delivered to lysosomes for degradation, thereby significantly enhancing transport efficiency across the BBB.64 Surface modification offers further possibilities for future applications of exos as effective therapeutic tools. However, this approach still has drawbacks. If the target protein is cytotoxic, its overexpression in host cells may impair cell viability or induce apoptosis. This strategy also has drawbacks, including uneven modification efficiency, poor stability of surface ligands, difficulties in large-scale GMP-grade production, and the potential for polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification to induce hypersensitivity reactions triggered by anti-PEG antibodies.

Drug loading and encapsulation techniques are critical steps in incorporating therapeutic drugs into exos, primarily including co-incubation, electroporation, sonication, extrusion, freeze-thaw cycles, and surfactant treatment.65 Among these, sonication has attracted widespread attention from researchers due to its high loading efficiency, good reproducibility, and universality across various drug types. For example, low-intensity pulsed focused ultrasound-mediated exos derived from endothelial progenitor cells significantly increased exo accumulation in the stroke area. Furthermore, this strategy did not cause brain tissue damage, albumin leakage, or CD45+ cell infiltration.66 However, ultrasound treatment inherently relies on the transient disturbance of membrane structures by mechanical energy. If parameters are not properly controlled, it can easily lead to irreversible damage to the exo membrane, leakage of contents, or vesicle aggregation, thereby compromising their in vivo targeting ability and biosafety.9 How to maintain the structural and functional integrity of exos while achieving efficient drug loading using physical methods such as ultrasound is a key issue that needs to be addressed in this field. Guo’s team combined ultrasound with surface modification to successfully develop a functionalized exo delivery system. They loaded the sound-sensitive agent dihydroporphyrin e6 (Ce6) and bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7) into the exos. More ingeniously, the researchers did not stop at simple physical loading but went on to synthesize a CP05-thioketal (TK)-mPEG complex. By leveraging the specific binding of the CP05 peptide to the exo surface marker CD63, a detachable, invisible coating was applied to the exo surface. At the target organ site, ultrasound triggered Ce6 to generate ROS, which then cleaved the TK bond via redox reactions, causing the PEG crown layer to detach and restoring exo uptake and drug release within target cells.67 This design approach demonstrates that the transient membrane disturbance caused by ultrasound does not impair the feasibility of subsequent surface modification; rather, through the protection provided by the smart coating, it enhances the stability of exos in vivo and their accumulation in target organs. Furthermore, while the mechanical and thermal effects of ultrasound can promote drug delivery by causing drugs to vibrate at specific frequencies, prolonged ultrasonic treatment may also lead to the degradation of sensitive drugs such as nucleic acids.

Technological advances in engineered exos

In recent years, new technologies related to exos have driven groundbreaking advancements in this field. These include exo-targeted delivery engineering (such as biomimetic cell membrane modification), CRISPR/Cas9 co-delivery systems, microfluidics-based large-scale exo production, artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted drug-loading design, and the use of exos as biomarkers for liquid biopsy.

A biomimetic cell membrane modification strategy endows exos with multi-targeted capabilities by fusing cell membranes from different sources. Du et al. hybridized BMEC-Exos with macrophage exo membranes and combined them with dopamine nanoparticles, resveratrol, and an amyloid β (Aβ) targeting aptamer to construct the engineered exo RPDA@Rb-A. Leveraging the homing effect of BMEC-Exos and the inherent pro-inflammatory nature of macrophage exos, this system achieved efficient BBB crossing and accumulation at sites of inflammation within the brain.68 Similar targeting strategies have also been applied to the treatment of PD. Researchers genetically engineered HEK293T cells to express a single-chain variable fragment targeting the dopamine transporter (DAT) on their surface, thereby constructing DAT-targeted EVs (αDAT EVs), and encapsulated curcumin within them (Cur@αDAT EVs) to achieve precise delivery to dopaminergic neurons.69 Tang et al. took a source-based approach and developed a stable human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) line capable of significantly enhancing brain targeting. By fusing the rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) peptide to the N-terminus of Lamp2b (RVG-Lamp2b) and using CRISPR/Cas9-assisted homologous recombination to knock-in the RVG-Lamp2b expression cassette into the AAVS1 safety locus of hiPSCs. The resulting exos, following intravenous injection, exhibited significantly enhanced brain-targeting delivery efficiency via nicotinic acetylcholine receptor-mediated endocytosis.70 In addition, Sun et al. developed a heat-responsive nanoscale system (RPTiOx-DOX@Ex-A) camouflaged by macrophage exo membranes and modified with angiopeptide-2 (Angiopep-2). This design not only mediates BBB crossing through the interaction between Angiopep-2 and low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1 (LRP-1), but also, subsequently, near-infrared light triggers the melting of phase-change materials, enabling controlled drug release at the local site of the glioma. Meanwhile, the immunomodulatory function of the exo-masked membrane induces tumor-associated macrophages to switch from the M2 to the M1 phenotype, resulting in multiple synergistic therapeutic effects.71 Although biomimetic cell membrane modification strategies have demonstrated effective therapeutic outcomes, this approach still faces various challenges. The technology for using biomimetic membrane materials in drug delivery remains in its early stages, and the most compelling evidence has been validated only in cellular or animal models. The preparation techniques for biomimetic membrane materials are not yet mature; the preparation process may be complex and require precise technical and environmental control, making large-scale production difficult. There are currently no reported cases of biomimetic membrane materials being applied in clinical settings, and their efficacy in large animals has not yet been further evaluated. Potential safety and biocompatibility issues in clinical settings cannot be ruled out, necessitating rigorous biosafety assessments.11

The CRISPR/Cas9 co-delivery system has further enabled gene editing within the central nervous system. Liang et al. developed engineered EVs with two biomimetic properties: first, a mini-intein protein with self-cleavage activity to enable active drug loading and release; second, fusion with the VSV-G protein to promote endosomal escape, and the use of engineered intrapeptides to enable the active loading and release of the Cas9/specific guide RNA (sgRNA) ribonucleoprotein complex. Delivery of EVs carrying CRISPR components via lateral ventricle injection achieved cell editing efficiencies as high as 30%–40% in the hippocampus and cortex.72 Furthermore, Han et al. designed a light-induced protein delivery system, namely the MAPLEX system, which utilizes the photolabile protein mMaple3 to mediate protein loading and release from exos. This system loads target proteins into exos by fusing them with an mMaple3-modified exosomal membrane tag. Blue light irradiation enables the controlled release of proteins from the exosomal membrane. Using this system, researchers co-delivered a dCas9-ribonucleoprotein complex targeting the amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (Bace1) gene and the catalytic domain of DNA methyltransferase 3A into the mouse brain. This strategy successfully achieved the methylation of CpG sites in the Bace1 promoter region, significantly reducing Bace1 expression, alleviating amyloid pathological changes, and improving recognition memory deficits.73 CRISPR/Cas9 technology has greatly advanced precise genome-targeting operations and is widely used in the treatment of central nervous system disorders, opening up new avenues for treating brain diseases. However, numerous challenges remain regarding efficacy and safety. The loading efficiency of macromolecular cargoes, such as the Cas9 protein, is extremely low. Electroporation, as the mainstream passive loading method, not only has limited encapsulation efficiency but also frequently leads to high chimerism and cellular damage.74 Off-target effects of the CRISPR/Cas9 system, potential chromosomal structural variations, and pre-existing immune responses that may be triggered by the bacterial origin of the Cas9 protein remain significant safety concerns that cannot be ignored.75 Further clinical application of this system requires breakthroughs in optimizing drug delivery mechanisms, developing GMP-grade standardized production processes, and establishing a safety assessment system.

The clinical translation of exos faces a bottleneck due to insufficient yield. Microfluidic technology offers new solutions for large-scale production and mechanistic studies. To address the challenges of low yield and high heterogeneity in natural exos, researchers have adopted a microfluidic synthesis strategy. They designed a microfluidic chip capable of efficiently mixing lipid and aqueous phases, guiding their self-assembly into biomimetic nanoparticles (ENPs) with a structure similar to that of natural exos but with a more uniform particle size. These ENPs effectively encapsulated therapeutic microRNAs, demonstrating a cellular uptake efficiency approximately 21 times higher than that of natural exos and a 2-fold increase in wound healing speed.76 Another study reported a fully integrated centrifugal microfluidic chip (iExoDisc). It ingeniously integrates three modules—plasma separation, high-concentration protein removal, and nanopore membrane capture—onto a single chip, enabling automated exosome isolation within 45 min using only 400 μL of blood. Compared to traditional ultracentrifugation, this platform achieves 3- to 6-fold higher exo purity and successfully enables glycoprofiling analysis of isolated exos, demonstrating its potential for disease diagnosis.77 Although microfluidic devices offer high precision and rapid processing capabilities in diagnostics and point-of-care testing, their low throughput and limited scalability restrict their large-scale application in industrial production or therapeutic settings. Low throughput, the risk of clogging, and insufficient capacity to process biological fluids are the core challenges currently facing microfluidic devices. While many devices can separate intact exosome populations with higher purity, they do so at the expense of processing throughput.78 The shear stress generated by fluid flow within microfluidic chips may cause structural damage to exos, leading to increased permeability, exposure of binding sites, and even membrane rupture. Although many microfluidic methods aim to address these issues, obstacles related to scalability and processing throughput must still be overcome during the transition from the laboratory to clinical settings.

AI is playing an increasingly important role in optimizing the drug-loading efficiency and functional evaluation of exos. During the parameter optimization phase of formulation development, some studies have employed artificial neural network (ANN) modeling to optimize parameters such as polymer concentration, stabilizer concentration, drug ratio, and ultrasonic power for predicting particle size and drug encapsulation efficiency. The optimized nanoparticles had a particle size of less than 200 nm and high encapsulation efficiency. This method not only improved the efficiency of formulation development but also enabled precise control over the physicochemical properties and release behavior of the nanoparticles, providing a promising delivery platform for the combination therapy of AD.79 Another study successfully developed a zonisamide nanodiamond intranasal-intracerebral delivery system for the treatment of epilepsy by integrating ANN optimization strategies. The results demonstrated that ANN rapidly screened for the optimal nanodiamond formulation by performing high-precision modeling and prediction of key process parameters and formulation variables. This achieved synergistic optimization of drug loading, particle size uniformity, and drug release behavior. Consequently, intracerebral drug concentrations were significantly increased, and the system demonstrated good safety and antiepileptic efficacy.80 This strategy highlights the unique advantages of ANN in the development of complex nanomedicines: By efficiently handling multivariate nonlinear relationships, they accelerate the formulation optimization process, reduce trial-and-error costs, and provide reliable technical support for achieving precise brain-targeted therapy. However, data sparsity, batch-to-batch variability, and insufficient model interpretability represent the core technical barriers in current AI research.81 The robustness of AI-optimized results is often compromised by fluctuations in experimental conditions and variability among exo batches. Inconsistencies in the isolation and characterization of exos, variable drug-loading efficiencies, and manufacturing challenges associated with large-scale production collectively amplify the uncertainty in the practical implementation of AI-optimization strategies.

Breakthroughs have also been achieved in the application of exos as liquid biopsy biomarkers for the early diagnosis of neurological diseases. Using serum exos as a liquid biopsy tool can effectively detect molecular abnormalities and pathological changes associated with white matter glial cells in AD. Not only can this approach capture pathological information originating from the central nervous system from peripheral blood in a non-invasive and reproducible manner, but it also enables the sorting of exo subpopulations using cell-specific markers. This allows for a more precise reflection of the molecular status of specific brain cell types, such as white matter glial cells.82 This strategy overcomes the limitations of traditional brain tissue biopsies, providing a new, sensitive, and specific liquid biopsy approach for the early diagnosis of brain diseases, the elucidation of pathological mechanisms, and dynamic disease monitoring. However, the vast majority of exo biomarkers remain in the early validation stage. Their clinical integration faces significant obstacles, including insufficient standardization of isolation methods, inadequate validation of biomarker panels, and poor compatibility with existing clinical decision-making processes.83 Different centrifugation protocols, choices of anticoagulants, and types of blood collection tubes can all significantly affect exo yield and molecular profiles.84 Overall, the widespread clinical application of exos as liquid biopsy biomarkers requires breakthrough progress in establishing standardized isolation protocols, systematically controlling pre-analytical variables, and conducting large-scale, multicenter clinical validation.

Exos for the treatment of neurological diseases

One of the central pathological features of nervous system diseases is the abnormal aggregation of misfolded proteins and their pathological propagation between cells and brain regions.85 These pathological processes lead to progressive neuronal loss and dysfunction, and thus nervous system diseases impose a major burden on the global health care system and are one of the leading causes of disability and death. Therefore, the need for prevention, early diagnosis, and the development of novel and effective therapies for nervous system diseases has become increasingly urgent, and related research continues to receive increasing attention. The development of delivery systems that can effectively traverse the BBB and increase the efficacy of neuroprotective agents is a key long-term challenge in the treatment of nervous system diseases.86 Studies have shown that exos exert neuroprotective effects and have the potential to alleviate disease phenotypes in a variety of nervous system disease models.87

Exos for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases

AD

AD is a neurodegenerative disorder primarily characterized by progressive cognitive impairment. Its pathological core lies in the progressive loss of neurons in the hippocampus, and its typical pathological features include the deposition of Aβ in the brain and the formation of neurofibrillary tangles resulting from the aggregation of hyperphosphorylated tau protein.88 The accumulation of Aβ can induce neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to synaptic degeneration and neuronal loss.89 According to projections by the Alzheimer’s Association, there will be over 131 million diagnosed cases of AD worldwide by 2050.90 Currently, traditional AD medications such as donepezil and memantine remain the standard clinical treatment regimens. However, their mechanisms of action are primarily limited to the modulation of cholinergic or N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors. They can only provide moderate and temporary relief of cognitive and behavioral symptoms and cannot reverse the underlying neurodegenerative pathology.91 Therefore, actively advancing the treatment and prevention of AD is of great significance. Current research indicates that exo-based intervention strategies show promise in improving AD. Therefore, this study investigates the therapeutic effects of exos on AD through mechanisms such as immune modulation, enhancement of autophagy, and regulation of Aβ.

Neuroinflammation is one of the key drivers of AD pathogenesis and progression; it not only impairs Aβ clearance but also promotes tau phosphorylation, thereby accelerating disease progression.92 In recent years, the use of exos to deliver anti-inflammatory molecules or modulate microglial phenotypic conversion has emerged as a key strategy for intervening in AD neuroinflammation. Microglia are primarily classified into pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes. The dynamic balance between these two plays a crucial physiological role in maintaining brain homeostasis through the release of different types of inflammatory factors.93 In recent years, physical modulation techniques have shown promise in this field. For example, irradiating the brains of 5×familial AD (5xFAD) mice with 1070-nm near-infrared light promotes the polarization of microglia from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. Activated M2-type microglia secrete exos carrying miR-7670-3p, which in turn reduces the expression of inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and TNF-α. This may result from the involvement of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway and its downstream nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) in mediating the inflammatory response. In vivo experiments further demonstrated that the expression levels of p-NF-κB/NF-κB and p-p38/p38 proteins were significantly reduced in the treatment group, and cognitive function in 5xFAD mice was significantly improved. The above evidence indicates that these exos can effectively inhibit the activation of classical inflammatory signaling molecules in 5xFAD mice (Figure 2).94 Another physical strategy focuses on the engineering of exos. Hypoxic pretreatment is an in vitro induction strategy that mimics an ischemic-hypoxic environment and activates multiple protective signaling pathways in stem cells. Liu et al. used ultracentrifugation to isolate exos derived from hypoxically pretreated adipose-derived MSCs and found them to be rich in circ-Epc1 (circ-Epc1-Exo). circ-Epc1-Exo modulates Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) expression by sponging miR-770-3p, thereby promoting the polarization of microglia toward the M2 phenotype. Subsequently, M2-polarized microglia suppress the expression of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β), reduce hippocampal neuronal apoptosis, and improve cognitive function in APP/PS1 transgenic mice.95 Unlike the previous two studies, Hong et al.’s research focused on the sorting mechanism of exosomal contents rather than the physical isolation of exos. Y-box binding protein-1 (YB-1) is an RNA-binding protein capable of recognizing specific RNA sequences and sorting them into exos. The YB-1 protein selectively sorts miR-223 into M2 microglial exos, thereby restoring the function of M2-derived exos. Both in vivo and in vitro studies have confirmed that miR-223 in exos reduces the release of neurotoxic factors (inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α), thereby reducing brain inflammation and improving cognitive function in amyloid precursor protein/presenilin 1 (APP/PS1) mice.96 Furthermore, Exos can serve as delivery vehicles for active components of traditional Chinese medicine to regulate neuroinflammation. Berberine and palmatine are isoquinoline alkaloids isolated from traditional Chinese medicines such as Coptis chinensis, and they possess broad-spectrum antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects.97 Zhao et al. used an ultrasonic method to load Ber and Pal into microglia-derived exos (Exos-Ber/Pal) and administered them to APP/PS1 mice via tail vein injection. They found that, compared with the control group, the Exos-Ber/Pal treatment group significantly reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β, while increasing the levels of anti-inflammatory factors IL-4 and IL-10, demonstrating a marked neuro-anti-inflammatory effect, thereby alleviating synaptic damage. The mechanism may involve blocking the PI3K/PKB and MAPK signaling pathways, thereby inhibiting NF-κB activation, while simultaneously activating the AMPK pathway to suppress the inflammatory response of microglia.98 The study by Zhao et al.98 yielded results similar to those of Jing et al., who encapsulated the flavonoid baicalein (Bac)—a compound with potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects—in human umbilical cord MSC-derived Exos (huc-MSC-Exos) (Exo@Bac) and administered them via intraperitoneal injection to Aβ1-42-induced AD rat models. They found that the injection of the Aβ1-42 peptide significantly upregulated p65 protein expression and reduced β-phosphorylation levels. However, compared to the AD model group, the Exo@Bac group exhibited a 1.9-fold increase in phosphorylated AMPK (p-AMPK) levels, a 55% reduction in p65 protein expression, and inhibition of the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β. Exo@Bac restored the number and structure of neurons in the hippocampal CA1 and barrel cortex regions, thereby improving spatial learning and memory abilities in AD rats. These results confirm that this protective effect is closely related to the regulation of the AMPK/NF-κB/p65 axis.99

Autophagy, as a highly conserved lysosome-dependent degradation pathway, plays a central role in clearing misfolded proteins in neurodegenerative diseases.100 Previous studies have confirmed that defects in autophagy are a key component of the pathogenesis of AD.101 Therefore, activating the autophagy pathway through exogenous intervention has become an important therapeutic strategy for AD. Iyaswamy et al. employed a strategy based on Fe65-engineered neuronal exos (Fe65-Exo), utilizing the Fe65 protein on the exo surface to enhance their targeted delivery to neurons overexpressing APP. These exos, encapsulating the natural autophagy inducer corynoxine-B (Fe65-Exo-Cory-B), can be precisely delivered to neuronal cells, significantly enhancing autophagy flux and thereby improving cognitive and motor behaviors in 5xFAD model mice. Mechanistic studies indicate that Fe65-Exo-Cory-B enhances autophagy flux by activating the BECN1-ATG5/ATG7-mediated classical autophagy pathway, thereby increasing the formation of autophagosomes and autolysosomes.102 Unlike the aforementioned strategies for activating classical pathways, the study by Zhang et al. revealed a mechanism by which exos derived from huc-MSC-Exos activate autophagy through a non-classical regulatory network mediated by lncRNA-9969. The study found that lncRNA-9969 in huc-MSC-Exos acts as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) to bind to mmu-miR-6361 in neurons, thereby lifting its suppression of downstream target genes and indirectly activating the mTOR/p70S6K signaling pathway to induce autophagy. This process is characterized by the downregulation of p-mTOR/p-p70S6K, upregulation of the key autophagy-initiating protein Beclin1, a significant increase in the light chain 3-II to light chain 3-I (LC3-II/LC3-I) ratio (a marker of autophagy formation), and decreased levels of the p62 protein. Ultimately, this promotes the clearance of Aβ in the brains of APP/PS1 model mice and alleviates neuronal ultrastructural damage (Figure 2).103

Excessive Aβ production and impaired clearance are the initiating steps in the pathological cascade of AD. Aβ oligomers and the senile plaques formed by their deposition can directly trigger a series of downstream pathological events, including synaptic dysfunction, abnormal tau phosphorylation, and neuroinflammation.104 Therefore, reducing Aβ burden has become a core strategy in disease-modifying treatments for AD. Zhao et al. focused on reducing Aβ production. They delivered miR-29b-2 to neurons by constructing engineered exos targeting the somatostatin receptor (SSTR). In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that this miRNA binds to the 3′ untranslated region of PS1 mRNA, thereby post-transcriptionally suppressing PS1 expression. The downregulation of PS1 directly reduces the cleavage activity of γ-secretase on APP, thus reducing Aβ production at its source. This strategy achieves the negative regulation of the Aβ production pathway through a miRNA-mediated RNA interference mechanism.105 Unlike Zhao et al., Ma et al. focused on promoting Aβ clearance and immune modulation. They developed a β-secretase (Bace1)-responsive immunotherapy strategy (ATExo-cL-aA), which, after intranasal administration, actively migrates to the AD brain. Upon cleavage by β-secretase, which is overexpressed in the lesion area, it simultaneously releases the aducanumab antibody (aA) and exos (ATExo) derived from Aβ-antigen-specific regulatory T cells (Tregs). Aducanumab, as an Aβ monoclonal antibody, directly binds to and clears Aβ plaques, while Treg-derived exos alleviate neuroinflammation by inducing a shift in microglia toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby achieving both active immune clearance of Aβ and synergistic regulation of the microglial inflammatory response.106 Yang et al. synthesized 3D graphene scaffolds as a cell culture substrate using chemical vapor deposition and isolated exos (3D-Exos) from the supernatant of huc-MSCs via ultracentrifugation. They delivered the 3D-Exos into APP/S1 mice using an osmotic pump. The results demonstrated that 3D-Exos could upregulate α-secretase expression and downregulate β-secretase, thereby effectively reducing Aβ production in AD pathological cells and the APP/S1 mouse model. The competitive cleavage of the extracellular domain of APP by α-secretase and β-secretase is a key process in Aβ generation. Furthermore, compared with 2D culture, the expression levels of neuropeptidase (neprilysin), insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE), and HSP70 were significantly elevated in 3D-Exo. Neprilysin and IDE are two major Aβ-degrading enzymes associated with the clearance of Aβ deposits in the brain. This suggests that 3D-Exo both reduces Aβ production and promotes Aβ clearance, thereby significantly improving memory and cognitive deficits in AD model mice (Figure 2).107

Aβ is capable of generating external free radicals and is a major source of ROS. Recent studies have successfully isolated PDEVs from the rhizomes of edible rhubarb using ultracentrifugation and size-exclusion chromatography. At the same time, the antioxidant and metabolic regulatory effects of PDEVs in an AD cell model were systematically evaluated. They found that these PDEVs effectively scavenged 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radicals, significantly reduced intracellular ROS levels, and regulated the expression of oxidative stress-related proteins, including catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), and cyclooxygenase (COX). Concurrently, metabolic analysis indicated that they reduced glycolytic activity, suggesting a partial restoration of bioenergetic homeostasis in the AD cell model. This provides preliminary experimental evidence for the development of rhubarb vesicles as functional foods or neuroprotective agents.108 Similar to the aforementioned study, Dolma et al. isolated PDEVs from citrus lemons using ultracentrifugation. These exos possess good BBB permeability and exhibit antioxidant activity comparable to ascorbic acid in vitro, exerting neuroprotective effects by effectively counteracting oxidative stress-related damage. Furthermore, these nanovesicles demonstrate excellent biosafety (cell survival rate exceeding 80%) and can significantly alleviate Aβ-induced neurodegenerative changes. This provides new strategies and insights for the intervention of AD and various oxidative stress-related neurodegenerative diseases.109 However, these two studies remain preliminary in vitro studies and require further in vivo studies and cargo-specific analyses to confirm their efficacy and elucidate their therapeutic potential.

These studies collectively indicate that exos primarily treat AD by modulating the immune system, enhancing autophagy, reducing Aβ protein production, promoting Aβ clearance, and exerting antioxidant effects. However, exos can also induce inflammation and accelerate disease progression. Studies have shown that exos derived from microglia in patients with AD carry miR-154-5p. miR-154-5p acts as a ligand for human Toll-like receptor 8 (hTLR8), activating downstream inflammatory signaling pathways and inducing microglia to release TNF, thereby triggering an inflammatory response.110 Furthermore, exos can serve as key vectors for the transmission of pathogenic proteins. For example, exos derived from neurons or microglia treated with low-concentration Aβ oligomers can induce axonal pathology through autophagocytic or heterophagocytic uptake. These exos upregulate the expression of the glycolytic regulator monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) and its co-regulator CD147. This process mediates the intercellular spread and amplification of neurotoxicity, further supporting the critical role of exos as mediators of AD neurotoxicity.111 Although the aforementioned exo-based AD treatment strategies have demonstrated significant efficacy in animal models, existing preclinical models have several inherent limitations that severely restrict the clinical translational value of the research findings. A systematic understanding of these limitations is of great significance for rationally interpreting research findings and optimizing experimental designs. The vast majority of AD transgenic models (e.g., APP/PS1, 5xFAD, 3×Tg-AD) are based on FAD mutations, which account for less than 5% of human AD cases. In these models, Aβ accumulates rapidly within a few months, and the disease course exhibits “acute-subacute” characteristics. In contrast, sporadic AD (SAD) accounts for 95% of cases, with a disease course spanning decades and involving a complex interplay of aging, polygenic interactions, vascular factors, and environmental exposures.112 Furthermore, models such as APP/PS1 mice lack comorbid features found in human AD, such as vascular lesions, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic abnormalities, making it difficult to assess the efficacy of exo therapy in complex clinical settings. Therefore, SAD models are needed to more accurately evaluate the therapeutic effects of drugs. For example, intraventricular injection of streptozotocin (STZ) successfully induces AD-like behavioral deficits and typical AD-like neuropathological features in mice, including neurofibrillary tangles, Aβ deposits, neuroinflammation, and synaptic plasticity imbalances.113 Liu et al. established an SAD model via intraventricular injection of STZ. They demonstrated that bone marrow MSC-derived exos (BMSC-Exos), when administered via lateral ventricle injection into STZ-treated mice, reduced neuroinflammation, enhanced the expression of synaptic-related proteins and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and improved typical AD neuropathological features.114 This provides critical evidence for evaluating the efficacy of exos in the broader context of AD. Future research requires improvements on multiple fronts: Priority should be given to the use of humanized mice, aged models (≥12 months), and non-human primates, combined with novel in vitro models such as microfluidic organ-on-a-chip systems, to more accurately simulate the complex pathological microenvironment of human AD. Furthermore, cross-species modeling of exo pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics should be conducted to explore more patient-friendly routes of administration (such as intranasal delivery and oral exos). Only in this way can exo-based AD treatment strategies be advanced from the laboratory to the clinical setting, truly benefiting patients.

PD

PD leads to motor dysfunction, manifested as bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, and postural and gait abnormalities, which severely impact patients’ physical and mental health.115 The primary pathological mechanisms of PD include the progressive loss and damage of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and striatal regions, as well as the abnormal aggregation of α-synuclein (α-syn).116,117 Current treatments include levodopa, dopamine agonists, monomin B inhibitors, and deep brain stimulation; while these can alleviate symptoms, they cannot halt or reverse disease progression.118 To effectively intervene in the core pathological mechanisms of PD, the development of novel delivery systems capable of targeting the central nervous system is particularly urgent. Exos offer a promising solution to these challenges due to their inherent ability to cross the BBB, deliver therapeutic agents to target neurons, and minimize off-target immune responses.

Neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis are key factors in the onset and progression of PD. Excessive activation of microglia can release large amounts of pro-inflammatory factors, which not only directly damage dopaminergic neurons but also induce neuronal apoptosis.119 Cai et al. used a commercial kit to isolate BMSC-Exos and genetically engineered them to enrich the glioma-associated oncogene homolog 1 (Gli-1) protein. As a downstream transcription factor of the Hedgehog signaling pathway, Gli-1 can directly bind to the transcription factor Sp1, competitively blocking Sp1’s interaction with the promoter regions of its downstream pro-inflammatory genes, thereby inhibiting the expression of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-1β, and significantly curbing the M1 polarization of microglia. Furthermore, western blot results showed the upregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. Following tail vein injection of these exos, the number of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive neurons in the substantia nigra of MPTP-treated rats significantly increased, and motor function markedly improved, suggesting that inhibiting the Sp1 signaling pathway can achieve dual anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects.120 Cell mitosis is closely associated with the activation of the ERK signaling pathway. Overexpression of the ERK signaling pathway can induce premature aging, apoptosis, and abnormal autophagy.121 The p38 MAPK is involved in key processes triggered by genotoxic damage mediated by stress responses, such as the cell cycle, apoptosis, or aging.122 Studies have found that umbilical cord blood-derived exos (Ucb-Exos) effectively inhibit the excessive phosphorylation of downstream p38 and ERK1/2 proteins by regulating the transcriptional levels of the upstream genes HspB1 and Ppef2, thereby blocking MAPK signaling-mediated neuronal apoptosis and downregulating the expression of the P21, P27, and P53 genes. Furthermore, the researchers investigated the activation levels of this signaling pathway. The results showed that, both in vivo and in vitro, Ucb-Exos significantly inhibited the expression of α-syn and p-α-syn, as well as TH loss. Subsequent studies also demonstrated that Ucb-Exos significantly improved levels of the midbrain inflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-10 following MPTP induction. These findings confirm that Ucb-Exos reduces MPTP-induced neuroapoptosis and neuroinflammation, ultimately significantly improving motor and cognitive impairments in PD model mice (Figure 3).123 Unlike MDEs, Chen et al. explored the therapeutic potential of PDEVs in PD. They isolated EVs derived from gardenia flowers (GDEVs) using ultracentrifugation. They found that GDEVs reduce mitochondrial damage by decreasing free radical production, thereby inactivating the p38 MAPK/p53 pathway. This inactivation enhances the Bcl-2/Bax ratio, thereby improving mitochondrial membrane integrity and reducing the release of cytochrome c. Ultimately, this reduces neuronal apoptosis and lowers the risk of PD. In vivo experiments further demonstrated that, in Caenorhabditis elegans, GDEVs reduce mitochondrial damage and downregulate the expression of apoptosis-related microRNAs, including MAPK pmk-1 (pmk-1), transcription factor cep-1 (cep-1), apoptosis regulator ced-9 (ced-9), programmed cell death activator egl-1 (egl-1), and cell death protein 3 (ced-3). This ultimately mitigates the loss of dopaminergic neurons, thereby reducing α-syn aggregation and increasing dopamine release.124

Wang et al. injected Ucb-Exos containing BDNF (BDNF-Exos) into a 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA)-induced PD rat model. They found that BDNF-Exos effectively crossed the BBB and targeted the affected brain regions, enhancing cellular antioxidant defense capabilities by activating the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling pathway. At the same time, BDNF-Exos increased the expression of microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) while reducing the expression of p-tau to stabilize the neuronal cytoskeleton, thereby significantly inhibiting 6-OHDA-induced apoptosis and ferroptosis and alleviating dopaminergic neuronal damage.125 Unlike Wang’s study, Yan focused on epigenetic regulation. In an MPTP-induced PD mouse model, the m6A demethylase FTO stabilizes ataxia-telangiectasia mutable (ATM) gene mRNA in an N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-dependent manner, thereby promoting ATM expression and inducing dopaminergic neuron death. To further validate this in vivo, they encapsulated the FTO-targeting siRNA (si-FTO) within MSC-Exo (Exo-siFTO). Intravenous injection of Exo-siFTO into a PD mouse model significantly suppressed the upregulation of α-syn, restored TH expression in the brains of PD mice, and significantly reduced dopaminergic neuron death in PD mice. These results indicate that MSC-Exo successfully delivers si-FTO to the brains of PD mice, where it regulates the epigenetic mechanisms of MPTP-induced dopaminergic neuron death in PD mouse models via the m6A-ATM axis.126

Oxidative stress is a central component of the pathogenesis of PD. Oxidative protein modifications disrupt protein folding homeostasis, activate endogenous signaling pathways, and amplify apoptotic signals. Oxidatively damaged α-syn forms oligomers that inhibit the function of chaperone proteins, triggering the PERK-CHOP pro-apoptotic signaling pathway.127 Zhang et al. delivered exos derived from olfactory mucosal MSCs (OM-MSC-Exos) into the right lateral ventricle of MPTP-induced PD mice via stereotactic injection. They found that the expression of the antioxidant molecules SOD and glutathione (GSH) was upregulated in neurons, while the levels of oxidative stress-related molecules ROS and malondialdehyde (MDA) decreased. Concurrently, neuronal morphology in the PD model improved, and the mice’s PD-like behaviors were significantly alleviated. OM-MSC-Exos may act as a delivery vehicle, enabling the highly expressed lnc RNA A2M-AS1 (Lnc A2M-AS1) to directly bind to the RNA-binding protein IGF2BP1 mRNA, thereby upregulating the expression of TP53INP1. This alleviates oxidative stress in the PD model, reduces neuronal apoptosis, and improves motor function in PD mice.128 These findings are consistent with previous reports regarding the involvement of exos in regulating oxidative stress in PD.2 In addition, a study successfully isolated and purified EVs (HR-EVs) from the culture medium of sage root hairs using ultracentrifugation and size-exclusion chromatography. The researchers found that the EVs from sage root hairs are rich in secondary metabolites, primarily triterpenoids, which are known for their antioxidant properties. They are capable of inhibiting the auto-oxidation process of 6-OHDA, significantly reducing the accumulation of its oxidation products, thereby mitigating the source of neurotoxicity at its origin. Furthermore, HR-EVs can effectively penetrate cells, release abundant antioxidants, and maintain cellular metabolic homeostasis in a PD cell model.129

The pathological features of PD are not limited to the central nervous system; they are also closely associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis and dysfunction of the “microbiota-gut-brain axis.” Cui et al. developed a novel oral delivery system consisting of tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) modified with antimicrobial peptides and loaded into ginger-derived exo-like nanovesicles (Exo@tac). The results demonstrated that Exo@tac significantly upregulates intestinal 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) levels both in vitro and in vivo, while reducing the levels of α-syn, inflammatory factors IL-6 and TNF-α, and pro-apoptotic proteins in brain and intestinal tissues. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic and safety evaluations indicated that Exo@tac exhibits enhanced retention in the gastrointestinal tract, demonstrates no toxicity to vital organs such as the heart, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, and can repair structural damage to the gastrointestinal mucosa caused by PD (Figure 3).130 This study provides a safe, effective, and clinically translational new strategy for treating PD through the targeted regulation of the gut microbiota-gut-brain axis.

Exos, as key mediators of intercellular communication, can carry proteins, nucleic acids, and lipid molecules derived from the central nervous system and cross the BBB to enter peripheral body fluids, making them highly promising diagnostic biomarkers for liquid biopsy in PD.131 Studies have shown that circSV2b, a circRNAs derived from the SV2b gene, is significantly downregulated in the plasma of patients with PD. circSV2b binds to miR-5107-5p via a sponge-like mechanism, thereby lifting its inhibitory effect on the target gene Foxk1; Foxk1 further activates the Akt1 signaling pathway, which in turn enhances the expression of antioxidant enzymes SOD and GSH-Px and reduces the accumulation of ROS. Thus, the circSV2b/miR-5107-5p/Foxk1/Akt1 axis plays a key role in the regulation of oxidative stress in PD. Furthermore, circSV2b can be transported to peripheral blood via the exo pathway, and changes in its levels can be detected in serum exos from PD models.132 Therefore, exo testing, with its advantages of being non-invasive or minimally invasive, allowing for dynamic monitoring, and reflecting disease-specific pathological features, holds promise for advancing the early diagnosis of PD.

The evidence presented above suggests that exos can be used to treat PD by modulating neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis, neurotrophic factors and epigenetics, oxidative stress, and the brain-gut axis. Furthermore, exos can serve as biomarkers for the early diagnosis of PD. However, despite the promising prospects of exo therapy, translating it into clinical practice still faces a series of significant challenges. First, existing research primarily relies on transgenic mouse models induced by neurotoxins or α-syn overexpression. Although these models can simulate acute dopaminergic neuron damage or specific proteinopathies, they struggle to replicate the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, the complex microenvironment associated with Lewy body formation, and the progression of pathology over time as observed in human PD. This disconnect between the models and actual pathology may be difficult to replicate in human trials. Second, the pathogenesis of PD involves multiple intertwined pathological pathways, including α-syn protein aggregation, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disruption of the gut-brain axis. Although previous studies have explored independent nodes such as FTO, the gut microbiota, BDNF, Shh/Gli1, and MAPK/ERK, the systemic synergistic effects of these interventions remain unclear. For example, it remains unclear whether there is an intrinsic link between modulating the gut microbiota and regulating the central Shh signaling pathway, or how they achieve functional integration within the overall pathological network of PD. Furthermore, brain-gut axis-based intervention strategies face challenges in translational medicine. Most existing studies have been conducted in standardized animal microbiome models, neglecting the high interindividual variability of the human gut microbiome. This may affect the generalizability and therapeutic consistency of “microbiome-gut-brain axis” interventions across different populations. In the future, technical barriers must be overcome to advance the standardization of protocols, preclinical safety validation, and large-scale clinical trials, thereby bridging the gap between basic research and clinical application.

Exos in the treatment of nerve injury diseases

Stroke

Stroke, particularly ischemic stroke, has become one of the leading causes of death and long-term disability worldwide. In the United States, someone suffers a stroke every 20 s on average, and someone dies from a stroke every 3 min and 14 s.133 Although significant advances have been made in recent years in thrombolytic drugs such as tissue plasminogen activators and reperfusion therapies, the clinical reality remains grim. The treatment window for intravenous thrombolysis is strictly limited to within 4.5 h of symptom onset. Even for mechanical thrombectomy, the applicable window typically does not exceed 24 h.134 This means that more than half of patients with stroke miss the optimal window for intervention and are forced to face the risk of irreversible neurological deficits or even death. More critically, even when successful recanalization is achieved, complications such as microvascular non-reperfusion and hemorrhagic transformation continue to severely limit improvements in patient outcomes.135 Consequently, the development of a therapeutic strategy capable of both crossing the BBB and repairing damaged neurovascular units through multi-targeted mechanisms has become an unresolved clinical challenge in the field of neuroscience. Studies have shown that exos possess the innate ability to cross the BBB and repair brain damage through multiple mechanisms, including the suppression of neuroinflammation, the alleviation of oxidative stress, and the promotion of neuroplasticity.136

Li et al. used ultracentrifugation to isolate microglia-derived exos from the ischemic penumbra of the cerebral cortex. The study found that treatment with microglia exos containing miR-212-5p (agomir-212-5p) effectively reduced brain tissue loss and neurological dysfunction, promoted synaptic plasticity, and inhibited axonal degeneration following MCAO/R-induced focal cerebral ischemia. Further in vivo mechanistic studies suggest that agomir-212-5p may exert its neuroprotective effects by directly targeting the Plexin A2 gene, thereby regulating the RhoA/ROCK2 signaling axis (Figure 4).137 Unlike the study by Li et al., Lin et al. employed an engineering modification strategy to conjugate programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) to the surface of MSC-Exos, thereby constructing the Exo-PD-L1-HGF complex. Exo-PD-L1-HGF exerts immunosuppressive effects by upregulating Tregs. Further in vivo mechanistic studies demonstrated that the intravenous administration of Exo-PD-L1-HGF efficiently homing to ischemic brain tissue via SDF-1α/CXCR4-mediated chemotaxis. Subsequently, by activating the STAT3/FOXO3 signaling pathway, it promotes the proliferation of endogenous neural progenitor cells (NPCs), ultimately enhancing neural plasticity.138 The studies by Li137 and Lin,138 through the precise regulation of exogenous signaling pathways, collectively reveal the multidimensional potential of exos in neural repair.

Zhang et al. successfully isolated neuro-stem cell-derived exos (NSC-Exos) using ultracentrifugation. They found that these exos downregulated the levels of pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β while upregulating the expression of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10, thereby effectively suppressing the immune response. Under pathological conditions such as cerebral ischemia and reperfusion, inflammatory cytokines can activate A1-reactive astrocytes. Furthermore, to investigate the effect of NSC-Exos on A1-reactive astrocyte generation, they applied them to astrocytes cultured in vitro following oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R). The results showed that, compared with the OGD/R group, the NSC-Exos-treated group exhibited significantly reduced expression of A1-associated markers such as complement C3, Gbp2, and lcn2. Concurrently, glial scar formation induced by astrocytes was reduced in MCAO/R mice.139 Li et al. successfully isolated Panax notoginseng-derived exo-like nanoparticles (PDNs) using a combination of ultracentrifugation and sucrose gradient centrifugation, and administered them intravenously to rats in a transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) model. They found that PDNs entered the rat brain tissue within 8 h of intravenous injection and were efficiently taken up by neurons and microglia. Flow cytometry and cytokine assays indicated that PDNs induce the conversion of microglia from the M1 to the M2 phenotype by activating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. Concurrently, they downregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 while upregulating the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10, thereby effectively suppressing neuroinflammatory responses following cerebral ischemia-reperfusion. This ultimately reduced the infarct volume by 35.5 ± 1.3%, with therapeutic efficacy significantly superior to that of Xuesaitong, a commonly used clinical drug.140 These studies indicate that exos can promote the repair of brain damage following stroke by regulating neuroinflammatory responses.

Recent studies have shown that exos play a crucial role in post-stroke neuroprotection, angiogenesis, and neurogenesis. Ye et al. pretreated Uc-MSCs with extracts from cerebral infarction tissue and isolated exos (I-Exo) using ultracentrifugation. Co-culture of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) with I-Exo revealed that I-Exo reduced the expression levels of apoptosis-related genes Bax and Caspase-3 and increased Bcl-2 activation, thereby alleviating OGD/R-induced damage to HUVECs. Mechanistic studies indicate that this protective effect may involve multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K-Akt, MAPK, mTOR, and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β). Furthermore, miRNA sequencing results revealed that the upregulated miR-181 and miR-211 in I-Exo were confirmed to promote vascular smooth muscle proliferation and inhibit vascular calcification. These findings suggest that I-Exo improves the pro-apoptotic environment following stroke, which may be the key mechanism by which ischemic-preconditioned exos promote cerebral vascular remodeling.141

Cell death is a central pathological event in ischemic brain injury. In addition to traditional forms of apoptosis and necrosis, recent studies have revealed that various regulated forms of cell death, such as autophagy and ferroptosis, are deeply involved in the progression of ischemic brain injury. Yong et al. found that ADSC-Exos can alleviate brain injury following ischemic stroke by inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis. They isolated ADSC-Exos using ultracentrifugation and investigated the underlying mechanisms in both in vitro and in vivo models. The results indicated that ADSC-Exos enriched with miR-760-3p can target and downregulate CHAC1 gene expression, thereby reducing glutathione degradation, lowering MDA levels, and inhibiting the downregulation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)—a key molecule in ferroptosis—ultimately alleviating neuronal ferroptosis. Following intranasal administration, the infarct volume in MCAO model mice was significantly reduced, and neurological deficit scores were improved. These findings suggest that ADSC-Exos inhibit ferroptosis via the miR-760-3p/CHAC1 axis upon intranasal administration, thereby promoting the recovery of neurological function following ischemic stroke (Figure 4).142 Zhang et al. used ultracentrifugation to isolate EV-like particles from Houttuynia cordata Thunb (HT-EVLP), which carry endogenous miR159a. In vivo and in vitro experiments revealed that miR159a suppresses the expression of the ferroptosis-related protein ACSL4 by binding to its 3′ untranslated region, while simultaneously downregulating TFRC levels and upregulating GPX4 levels, thereby reversing neuronal ferroptosis. This ultimately reduced cerebral infarct volume in a rat MCAO model, protected BBB integrity, and promoted neurological recovery.143 Similarly, Huang et al. isolated EVs from bitter melon cells (MC-SEVs) using density gradient centrifugation and ultracentrifugation. These EVs were enriched with the plant-specific miR-5813b. miR-5813b directly targets and regulates the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM62, which primarily drives neuronal ferroptosis by promoting K48-linked polyubiquitination of GPX4. By inhibiting TRIM62 expression, miR-5813b effectively blocks the TRIM62-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of GPX4, thereby stabilizing GPX4 protein levels, suppressing ferroptosis, and ultimately promoting neural function recovery. Importantly, intravenous injection of MC-SEVs demonstrated excellent biosafety, with no significant toxic side effects observed.144 Unlike Yong et al., who focused on ferroptosis, Deng et al. focused on exos enhancing autophagy to improve brain damage following stroke. They used ultracentrifugation to isolate exos secreted by OGD/R-stimulated astrocytes (OGD/R-ADEXs). The study found that, in both in vivo and in vitro experiments, treatment with OGD/R-ADEXs upregulated p-AMPK levels, downregulated p-mTOR levels, increased the ratio of the autophagy-related proteins LC3-II to LC3-I, and increased the number of autophagosomes and autophagic bodies. This suggests that OGD/R-ADEXs regulate the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway through the nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (Nampt) molecules they carry, thereby enhancing neuronal autophagy levels and ultimately improving neuronal survival rates (Figure 4).145 These two studies, from the perspectives of inhibiting ferroptosis and enhancing autophagy, respectively, reveal the important role of exos in the repair of ischemic brain injury by regulating different types of cell death pathways.

Taken together, these studies suggest that exos primarily treat stroke by modulating the immune system, promoting vascular remodeling, enhancing autophagy, and inhibiting ferroptosis. Furthermore, the studies by Li et al.137 and Yong et al.142 have identified promising therapeutic targets that warrant further validation and translational application. However, exos may disrupt the homeostasis of the cerebral vascular microenvironment. In vitro experiments have shown that exos derived from platelets activated by stroke and tissue plasminogen activator can disrupt the integrity of the endothelial barrier by stimulating prothrombin and reducing tight junction proteins in cerebral endothelial cells.146 This suggests a potential prothrombotic effect within the cerebral vascular microenvironment. In addition, in preclinical studies, the most commonly used MCAO model employs the filament occlusion method to induce focal ischemia, resulting in a relatively standardized infarction pattern. This model typically uses healthy young animals, lacking the comorbidities—such as advanced age, hypertension, diabetes, and atherosclerosis—commonly found in human patients with stroke. Yet these factors are precisely the key variables that profoundly influence the in vivo distribution of exos, their immunomodulatory efficacy, and BBB permeability. Furthermore, preclinical studies typically involve a single administration of the treatment immediately after modeling or within 24 h. This reflects both the requirement for timely intervention due to the high metabolic rate of mice and corresponds to the hyperacute intervention window. However, clinical patients often present for treatment well beyond this time frame, and this time lag may lead to an overestimation of the therapeutic window. Therefore, the primary mechanisms of action of exos during the chronic phase of stroke—such as synaptic remodeling, white matter repair, and regulation of secondary degeneration in distant regions—should be evaluated. To overcome the translational bottlenecks in exo-based therapy for ischemic stroke, it is necessary to introduce diverse animal models, particularly those involving aging and comorbidities. Concurrently, longer treatment windows (e.g., 24–72 h) and optimized administration routes should be explored to better simulate real-world clinical scenarios.

MS

MS is an autoimmune disease characterized primarily by the inflammatory demyelination of the central nervous system. With approximately 2.8 million patients worldwide, it has become the leading cause of non-traumatic neurological disability among young and middle-aged adults.147 However, currently available commercial drugs only suppress disease progression and reduce relapse frequency by targeting the peripheral immune system. They cannot promote the differentiation of oligodendrocytes in demyelinated areas into mature oligodendrocytes or facilitate the formation of new myelin.148 More critically, effective treatment options for patients with primary or secondary progressive MS are virtually nonexistent, constituting a clinical bottleneck in neurology that requires breakthroughs.149 In recent years, exo-based therapies have demonstrated potential in this field. Multiple studies have shown that exos not only deliver anti-inflammatory molecules, microRNAs, and BDNF to the central nervous system to modulate the local immune microenvironment, but also directly act on oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), promoting their differentiation into mature oligodendrocytes and mediating myelin regeneration.150,151,152

Zhang et al. isolated MSC-Exos from rhesus monkeys using ultracentrifugation and administered these exos intravenously into a mouse model of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE). They found that MSC-Exos promote remyelination through a dual mechanism. On the one hand, MSC-Exos regulate the phenotypic polarization of microglia, promoting the conversion of M1 to M2 cells without altering the total number of microglia. This reduces neuroinflammation by decreasing the expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as iNOS, IL-1β, and TNF-α, while simultaneously increasing the levels of anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β. On the other hand, MSC-Exos can inhibit the activation of the TLR2/IRAK1/NF-κB signaling pathway in the central nervous system, thereby suppressing immune responses and promoting OPC differentiation and remyelination. This has also been validated in a copper phenylglycol (CPZ) model.153 Unlike the natural exosome strategy employed by Zhang et al., Jia et al. used genetic modification to load the forkhead box protein P3 (FOXP3) into dendritic cell (DC) exos (FOXP3-EXOs). FOXP3 is a key transcription factor in Treg cells, and its delivery via exos can confer immunoregulatory functions on recipient cells. In vitro and in vivo studies have shown that FOXP3-Exos significantly inhibit CD4+ T cell proliferation, reduce the levels of Th1 and Th17 cells and related cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-17), and promote the generation of Treg cells and IL-10. However, treatment with FOXP3-Exos did not affect Th2 cell levels or IL-4 levels. This suggests that FOXP3-Exos therapy can alleviate spinal cord inflammatory infiltration and demyelination by regulating Th/Treg homeostasis, thereby effectively improving the progression of EAE (Figure 5).154 Furthermore, exosomal miRNAs influence signal transduction and intracellular networks; for example, by regulating signaling pathways such as TNF-α, TGF-β, and NF-κB, they may contribute to immune regulation and therapeutic effects in MS.155 These findings suggest that exos can improve MS symptoms by suppressing neuroinflammation induced by MS. Of particular note is that Zhang et al. utilized rhesus monkey-derived exos and employed two complementary animal models—EAE and CPZ—making the results more relevant to humans.153

The proliferation, migration, and differentiation of OPCs are critical steps in myelin regeneration. Ji et al. used ultracentrifugation to isolate exos from activated microglia, which were found to highly express miR-615-5p. They found that these exos, by delivering miR-615-5p, silence the myelin regulatory factor MYRF and affect the expression of its downstream gene MBP, ultimately leading to the arrest of OPC differentiation and thereby blocking myelination/remyelination. In EAE and CPZ demyelination models, AAV-miR-615-5p effectively alleviated disease progression and promoted remyelination. This suggests that miR-615-5p/MYRF, as a novel therapeutic target for demyelinating diseases, holds potential for the treatment of MS.156 Zhai et al. prepared brain-targeted exo (RVG/BDNF-Exo) carrying BDNF using genetically engineered HEK293T cells by overexpressing the RVG-peptide-Lamp2b fusion protein and the mature BDNF coding sequence, and isolated these exos via ultracentrifugation. The RVG modification enabled the exos to specifically target neurons and oligodendrocytes. They delivered these exos into the brains of CPZ mice via intranasal administration. The results showed that the delivered BDNF promoted the differentiation of OPCs into mature oligodendrocytes by activating the TrkB receptor and its downstream MAPK/ERK1/2 signaling pathway. Mature oligodendrocytes are essential for myelin regeneration (Figure 5).157 Furthermore, unlike the native microglial exos used by Ji et al.,156 Zhai et al.157 employed an engineering strategy to achieve targeted delivery of BDNF, significantly enhancing intracerebral drug concentrations and cell specificity. Other studies have compared the therapeutic effects of stem cells and stem cell-derived exos in canine multiple sclerosis (MS). The results indicated that both the stem cell group and the exo group promoted myelin regeneration and restored the normal structure of axons. Although stem cell therapy demonstrated better therapeutic effects than the stem cell-derived exo group, the use of exos is safer and carries lower immunological risks, showing potential for promoting myelin regeneration without the need for cell-based therapy.158 These results collectively indicate that exos can promote remyelination, thereby improving the symptoms of MS.

Fan et al. found that BMSC-Exos are rich in miR-367-3p. The expression of the enhancer Zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) gene peaks during the inflammatory phase of MS. miR-367-3p binds to EZH2, inhibiting its expression and leading to the overexpression of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11). Concurrently, the overexpression of SLC7A11 activates GPX4, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis in microglia. This suggests that miR-367-3p can suppress microglial ferroptosis by regulating the EZH2/SLC7A11 axis, thereby alleviating the severity of EAE (Figure 5).159

In a study, 1 mL of cerebrospinal fluid and serum samples were collected from 30 untreated patients with relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) and a healthy control (HC) group. Exos were isolated from these samples using ultracentrifugation for the detection of miRNAs that influence inflammatory responses. The results showed that, compared with the HC group, the expression of miR-15a-5p, miR-19b-3p, and miR-432-5p was upregulated in RRMS cerebrospinal fluid, while the expression of miR-17-5p was downregulated. However, the levels of these miRNAs in serum exos did not show significant changes. The levels of miR-23a-3p, miR-30a-5p, and miR-142-3p were significantly elevated in serum exos from patients with RRMS, while the level of miR-10a-5p was decreased. However, no significant changes were observed in cerebrospinal fluid exos.160 These findings are consistent with those reported by Mina et al., who observed that, compared with the control group, levels of the exosomal EBV-miR-BART9-3p and EBV-miR-BART15 in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with RRMS were 18.4-fold and 3.1-fold higher, respectively.161 Taken together, these findings demonstrate the therapeutic potential of exosomal miRNAs as biomarkers in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with MS, which holds significant implications for early diagnosis.

All of the above evidence suggests that exos play a significant role in the treatment of MS by modulating immune responses, inhibiting ferroptosis, promoting myelin regeneration, and serving as diagnostic biomarkers. Notably, Zhen et al.154 and Zhai et al.157 employed engineering strategies that yielded better therapeutic outcomes than exos alone. Although exo-based therapies for MS show promise, their translation into clinical practice still faces significant challenges. Most preclinical studies use the EAE model to simulate preventive or early intervention scenarios. However, clinical patients with MS often present with established neurological damage at the time of diagnosis, and some have already entered the progressive phase, potentially resulting in significantly reduced responsiveness to treatment. This discrepancy in the therapeutic window may lead to an overestimation of efficacy in preclinical studies. Furthermore, the stability of engineering modification strategies, the maintenance of activity during cryopreservation, and quality control standards are difficult to fully validate in small-scale preclinical experiments. At the diagnostic level, although miRNAs in exos from patients with MS exhibit differential expression, translating these biomarkers into clinical diagnostic tools remains challenging. These challenges include limited sample size, significant heterogeneity across different studies, a lack of standardized detection methods, and the unclear dynamic relationship between miRNAs and disease activity. Notably, recently developed B2m-NOG mice humanized with peripheral blood mononuclear cells can reproduce several hallmark features of MS that are difficult to replicate in traditional EAE models.162 Future research should focus on advancing multicenter, standardized longitudinal cohort studies of exosomal miRNAs to clarify their dynamic association with disease activity. Establish a systematic quality control framework for engineered exos. Integrate multimodal biomarkers—including miRNAs, imaging data, and neurofilament light chains—and leverage machine learning to construct more robust diagnostic models. By acknowledging the limitations of existing models, we can gradually accumulate translatable real-world evidence.

Other neurological disorders

TBI is a severe neurological disorder caused by external mechanical forces that result in damage to brain structure and function.163 A large number of survivors are left with long-term cognitive, motor, and psychiatric impairments.164 Globally, there are approximately 70 million new cases of TBI each year, posing a serious threat to physical, psychosocial, and economic well-being.165 Therefore, the exploration of new treatment methods is urgently needed. In recent years, researchers have discovered that exos can improve brain injury in TBI through multiple mechanisms. Zhang et al. isolated huc-MSC-Exos using ultracentrifugation and administered them via tail vein injection into TBI model mice. They found that huc-MSC-Exos activate the mitochondrial autophagy pathway by regulating PINK1/Parkin. Subsequently, this modulated the expression of apoptosis-related proteins such as Bax and Bcl-2, reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-18, and TNF-α, lowered MDA levels, and simultaneously increased GPX4 while decreasing ACSL4—key proteins involved in ferroptosis. This indicates that huc-MSC-Exos reduce cell apoptosis, neuroinflammation, and ferroptosis through PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, thereby alleviating cerebral edema and exerting a neuroprotective effect.166 Li et al. isolated exos from induced neural stem cells (iNSC-Exos) using ultracentrifugation and administered them via two clinical treatment models. First, iNSC-Exo was encapsulated in an injectable hydrogel and implanted into the lesion site of TBI mice, achieving sustained release over 28 days. Second, RVG peptide modification was used to enhance the brain-targeting ability of iNSC-Exo, which was then administered via tail vein injection into TBI-injured mice. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that iNSC-Exo primarily targets microglia. By restoring the states of the Microglia_Nrg3 and Microglia_Rarb subpopulations and activating the NRXN signaling pathway, it enhances synaptic regeneration. NRXN is an integral cell surface protein that maintains synaptic function by interacting with neuronal synaptic proteins (such as Nlgn1). Furthermore, it was demonstrated that iNSC-Exo reduced neuroinflammation, alleviated cerebral edema, and restored BBB integrity in TBI mice.165 Wei et al. administered BMSC-Exo via direct intracerebral injection to treat a rat model of TBI. The study found that BMSC-Exo increased the expression of vascular-associated molecules CD31, CD44, and vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), while decreasing the expression of brain injury-associated markers glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and NSE, thereby promoting angiogenesis following TBI. The mechanism may be achieved through the targeted regulation of miR-30a-5p/Delta-like ligand 4 (DLL4).167 Chen et al. extracted and purified EVs from Salvia miltiorrhiza roots (SalEVs) using sucrose gradients and density gradient ultracentrifugation. These SalEVs contain bioactive compounds known for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, such as salicylate Y and salvianolide IIA. When these SalEVs were administered via tail vein injection into the TBI model mice, they were able to naturally cross the BBB and effectively accumulate at the injury site. Systemic administration of SalEVs suppressed microglial activation and infiltration at the injury site and blocked their transition to a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Concurrently, SalEVs inhibited astrocyte activation, reduced ROS accumulation in neurons, and decreased neuronal apoptosis. Ultimately, this partially rescued the spatial memory deficits induced by TBI.168 Collectively, these findings indicate that exos alleviate the pathological state of TBI by regulating immune responses, mitochondrial autophagy, promoting angiogenesis, and exerting antioxidant effects. Furthermore, the iNSC-Exos used by Li et al. have demonstrated clinical potential for TBI in both surgical (e.g., perioperative support) and medical (e.g., as a standalone therapy for short- and long-term management) settings. Additionally, no significant organ toxicity was observed with either approach.165

SCI is a traumatic structural injury to the spinal cord that leads to profound, lifelong consequences, including impaired mobility, loss of independence, and social isolation. Projections indicate that by 2050, the global number of SCI cases will exceed 14.5 million.169 However, current clinical interventions, including surgical decompression, medication, and rehabilitation therapy, have extremely limited effects on functional recovery, and the regeneration and repair of the nervous system remain a major challenge.170 Due to their low immunogenicity and multifunctional reparative properties, exos have demonstrated significant efficacy in promoting nervous system recovery following SCI. Pan et al. used the RiboTM exosome isolation kit to isolate huc-MSC-Exos and delivered these exos into SCI rat models via tail vein injection. Mechanistic studies indicate that huc-MSC-Exos deliver miRNA-340-5p to specifically inhibit the expression of the Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) gene, thereby blocking the activation of the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway. Ultimately, this enhances M2 microglial polarization, reduces the release of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, increases the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10, and alleviates inflammation and the severity of SCI in SCI rats.171 In addition, Wang et al. extracted PDEVs from goji berries using a combination of ultracentrifugation and sucrose gradient centrifugation. They also employed ultrasonication to load the anti-inflammatory compound isoliquiritigenin (ISL), forming ISL@PE complexes. These ISL@PE complexes were further encapsulated within 3D-printed biomimetic scaffolds to achieve personalized customization. In vitro experiments demonstrated that ISL@PE promotes the conversion of LPS-induced microglia from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype by upregulating the expression of phosphorylated AKT, while simultaneously reducing the expression of iNOS and ROS, thereby effectively suppressing neuroinflammatory responses. Furthermore, compared with MSC-Exos of the ectoderm, ISL@PE significantly enhances the efficiency of neural stem cell differentiation into neurons. In vivo experiments further demonstrated that ISL@PE promotes the recovery of motor function following nerve regeneration and inhibits scar tissue formation.172 Similar to the findings of Wang et al., a study isolated Onion-Exos from onions using ultracentrifugation combined with sucrose gradient centrifugation and administered these Onion-Exos via intraperitoneal injection into mice with SCI models. They found that Onion-Exos, by activating the antioxidant-related Keap1/NRF2/ARE signaling pathway, increased the levels of antioxidant proteins SOD and GSH-Px, reduced the expression of the lipid peroxidation product MDA, and alleviated oxidative damage in the injured area. Additionally, Onion-Exos exerted anti-inflammatory and anti-neuronal apoptosis effects. These actions collectively promoted the recovery of motor function following SCI.173 Ferroptosis promotes neuronal cell death following SCI. Therefore, Fan et al. isolated huc-MSC-Exos using ultracentrifugation. These exos were modified with the transcription activator of transcription (TAT) and RVG and loaded with GPX4 activator (GA) to construct the targeted delivery system MSC-EXOR&T-GA, which was administered via tail vein injection to treat a mouse model of SCI. These Exos enhance blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB) permeability via TAT, achieve neuronal targeting via RVG, and deliver GA to activate GPX4, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis, reducing lipid peroxidation, and regulating iron homeostasis. Treatment results showed that MSC-ExoR&T-GA improved targeting efficiency, reduced neuronal death, and promoted axonal regeneration and myelin repair, thereby improving motor and sensory function in SCI mice.174 Furthermore, Zou et al. reported similar findings, showing that NSC-Exos-derived miR-218a-5p suppresses ferroptosis in SCI via the Bmil/Mettl3/Alox12 axis.175 Luo et al. isolated BMSC-Exos using ultracentrifugation and treated SCI mice via tail vein injection with a combination of BMSC-Exos and sodium salvianin IIA sulfonate (STS-Exo). Compared to monotherapy, this combination therapy promotes angiogenesis by activating the VEGF signaling pathway, restores the function of BSCBs after injury, and reduces the inflammatory response.176 Shao et al. isolated ADSC-Hexos using ultracentrifugation and treated SCI mice via tail vein injection. High-throughput sequencing revealed significantly elevated expression of the circular RNA Astn1 (circ-Astn1) in ADSC-Hexos. circ-Astn1 upregulates the expression of the downstream autophagy-related protein 7 (Atg7) by competitively binding to miR-138-5p, thereby activating the autophagy pathway. This promotes repair following SCI, reduces apoptosis levels and inflammatory cytokine expression, and significantly improves motor function in SCI mice.177 These findings suggest that exos can repair spinal cord injuries by modulating immune responses, exerting antioxidant effects, inhibiting ferroptosis, promoting angiogenesis, and activating autophagy.

Huntington’s disease (HD) is a neurodegenerative disorder caused by an expansion of the CAG trinucleotide repeat located in the first exon of the Huntington’s disease (HTT) gene. In the general population, the HTT gene contains 3–35 CAG repeats; in patients with HD, the HTT gene contains 36 or more CAG repeats.178 The HTT protein is widely expressed and localized to multiple organelles, including endosomes, and plays a key role in intracellular transport.179 Despite ongoing research, there are currently no disease-modifying therapies capable of slowing or reversing the progression of HD.180 Therefore, the development of effective therapeutic interventions is of the utmost urgency. In recent years, researchers have focused on using exos to reduce mutant HTT (mHTT) aggregation, thereby improving HD symptoms. Li et al. successfully established the first R6/2 xenograft animal model and found that exos from young blood inhibit mHTT aggregation, regulate apoptosis-related proteins, and promote cell proliferation. Additionally, Exos from young blood can activate the CREB/PGC-1α pathway to improve mitochondrial function.181 Unlike direct exo administration, Zhang et al. designed an in vivo self-assembling siRNA strategy that involves intravenous injection of a genetic circuit plasmid to induce the liver to produce exos carrying an RVG targeting tag and loaded with mHTT siRNA. In three HD transgenic mouse models—BACHD, YAC128, and N171-82Q—these exos were able to cross the BBB and, via the RVG tag, precisely deliver the cargo (mHTT siRNA) to neurons in the cerebral cortex and striatum, effectively silencing mHTT expression and improving motor function and behavioral deficits.182 Based on this study (NCT06024265), the resulting ER2001 injection has received the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Orphan Drug Designation, and early clinical data demonstrate good safety and preliminary efficacy. The non-invasive strategy described by Zhang et al.182 avoids the high surgical costs associated with traditional delivery techniques and allows for precise control of siRNA distribution and target gene expression within the complex, dynamic in vivo environment. However, since the injected exos are primarily processed in the liver, it is necessary to monitor liver function and condition following long-term treatment. Currently, exo-based therapies for HD are still in their infancy and require further exploration.

Table 2 summarizes the applications of the aforementioned exo-based therapies in neurological diseases. Based on all the above studies, exo-based therapies for neurodegenerative diseases primarily focus on MSC-Exos, microglial-derived exos, astrocyte-derived exos, and NSC-Exos. MSC-Exos have been extensively studied due to their immunomodulatory properties and multipotent differentiation potential, demonstrating significant therapeutic potential in suppressing neuroinflammation and promoting neural regeneration. Microglia-derived exos possess bidirectional regulatory properties: M1 microglial exos can exacerbate inflammatory responses, while M2 microglial exos exert neuroprotective effects by delivering anti-inflammatory factors, with their function highly dependent on the state of the disease microenvironment. Astrocyte-derived exos primarily participate in neuronal metabolic support and intercellular communication, playing a key role in maintaining BBB integrity and regulating synaptic function. NSC-Exos demonstrate unique advantages in promoting neural differentiation and tissue repair. Although they all exhibit therapeutic potential, most existing studies have focused on the functional validation of exos from a single cell source. Systematic comparisons of exos from different sources regarding targeting specificity, immunogenicity differences, and applicability to specific disease stages remain lacking. This research gap limits the rational selection of optimal exo sources and hinders the precise clinical translation of exo therapies.

Clinical trials

Although preclinical models have shown promising results, the clinical translation of exo-based therapies for neurological diseases remains in its early stages. Currently completed or ongoing clinical trials primarily focus on safety validation and preliminary efficacy exploration. In the field of neurodegenerative diseases, the number of clinical trials is extremely limited but is gradually increasing. Dosage and safety issues are a major concern. (NCT04388982) trial is the first international Phase I/II clinical trial to explore the intranasal administration of MSC-Exos for the treatment of AD. This open-label study enrolled 9 patients with mild to moderate AD. Participants were divided into three dose groups—low dose (2×108), medium dose (4×108), and high dose (8×108)—using a “3 + 3” dose-escalation protocol. Subjects received intranasal administration twice weekly for 12 weeks, with follow-up extending to 48 weeks. The results showed that no adverse events were reported by any of the participants, and no significant abnormalities were observed in biochemical markers, confirming good safety. Although no significant differences were observed in Aβ or tau protein deposition, this study provided critical evidence for dose selection and safety for subsequent clinical trials.183 In the field of stroke, (NCT03384433) trial also employs a “3 + 3” dose-escalation design, with three dose groups of 2×109 particles/kg, 4×109 particles/kg, and 8×109 particles/kg. The trial is currently in the recruitment phase and plans to enroll 29 patients with acute ischemic stroke to evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of exo therapy. In contrast, (NCT01860118) study for PD takes a different exploratory approach. Data collection for this study has been completed, with a total of 601 participants enrolled. The primary objective is not direct therapeutic intervention, but rather to analyze the effect of LRRK2 inhibitors on reducing LRRK2 expression and phosphorylation levels in individual exos by detecting exosomal protein biomarkers in blood or urine, thereby exploring their potential as biomarkers or therapeutic targets for PD. Furthermore, as the first exo therapy to enter clinical trials for central nervous system diseases, AB126 leverages its natural ability to cross the BBB and its intrinsic anti-inflammatory and regenerative multi-therapeutic activities. It has received approval from the U.S. FDA to conduct a Phase 1b/2a clinical study for acute ischemic stroke. Meanwhile, preclinical studies in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) models indicate that AB126 has the potential to prolong survival and reduce markers of neurological damage.184

Conclusions

Exos show great promise in the treatment of brain diseases, but their clinical translation inevitably faces challenges related to potential side effects and long-term safety. Exos may exacerbate neuroinflammation, disrupt the homeostasis of the cerebral vascular microenvironment, mediate pathogen transmission, and promote the spread of pathological proteins. These risks are further exacerbated by the complexity of the nervous system, the double-edged sword effect of the BBB, and variations in the individual immune microenvironment. Therefore, it is necessary to establish a safety assessment paradigm tailored to the unique microenvironment of the central nervous system, encompassing in vitro BBB models, patient immune stratification, and in vivo tracing and pharmacokinetic studies.

In addition, the widespread application of exo therapy is limited by key obstacles such as unclear mechanisms of action, heterogeneity in source and batch, a lack of standardization in isolation and storage, difficulties in large-scale production, and the absence of a regulatory framework. In the future, establishing standardized protocols for exo isolation and characterization will be key to addressing the issue of heterogeneity. By utilizing microfluidic microarrays, tangential flow filtration, and single-particle analysis platforms, the reproducibility of exo isolation can be significantly improved. Building on this foundation, innovative engineering approaches—such as genetic engineering, chemical modification, and nanomaterial modification—can be employed to enhance the brain-targeting delivery capabilities of exos. Concurrently, loading protocols should be optimized to improve drug loading efficiency and safety. Scalable production and GMP compliance will rely on tangential flow filtration and AI-driven quality control frameworks. Furthermore, engineered modifications and drug loading efficiency need not be limited to a single method; combining multiple approaches can further enhance efficiency. A unified evaluation system for the long-term storage stability of exos is also urgently needed. In-depth pharmacokinetic and toxicological studies using humanized animal models should be conducted to systematically evaluate the long-term safety and immunogenicity of exos. In summary, integrating systems biology approaches with multidisciplinary collaboration is essential to overcoming these bottlenecks and advancing exos from basic research to clinical application.
