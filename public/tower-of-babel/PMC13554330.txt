Clinical burden and treatment pathways of early deep surgical site infection after lumbar spine surgery: a Vietnamese single-center descriptive study
Authors: Vu Hoang Nguyen, Ngoc Quyen Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly deep surgical site infection (SSI) after lumbar spine surgery is uncommon, but management may require repeated surgery, negative-pressure wound therapy, prolonged antimicrobial treatment, and complex implant-related decisions. We aimed to characterize the clinical course, treatment pathways, and major treatment-burden events among affected patients.MethodsThis retrospective descriptive study screened 1,923 patients who underwent primary lumbar spine surgery at a single tertiary center between October 2018 and October 2023. Sixteen patients met an operational definition adapted from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/National Healthcare Safety Network criteria for early deep incisional or organ/space SSI within 90 days. Analyses were descriptive; an exploratory comparison of cumulative infection-related inpatient days was summarized without predictive inference.ResultsSixteen early deep SSIs were identified [crude institutional incidence, 0.83%; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.48–1.35%]. Diagnosis occurred a median of 14.5 days after surgery [interquartile range (IQR), 6.8–20.8; range, 4–70]. Fourteen patients underwent 52 spinal reoperations; 10 required vacuum-assisted closure with 37 foam changes. Two required intensive care, two were readmitted, and no deaths occurred. Cultures yielded methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (n=6), methicillin-resistant S. aureus (n=2), Escherichia coli (n=3), Enterococcus faecium (n=1), and Enterobacter cloacae (n=1); one culture was negative and two patients were not cultured. Eight patients experienced a major treatment-burden event: infection-related adverse outcomes (n=4), an unplanned small-bowel injury during revision (n=1), or implant-removal source-control procedures (n=3). Median cumulative infection-related inpatient duration was 46.5 days with an event and 23.0 days without an event.ConclusionsEarly deep SSI was uncommon in this single-center series but imposed considerable procedural and inpatient burden among affected patients. The findings support early recognition, microbiological confirmation when feasible, careful revision planning, and coordinated multidisciplinary infection management, while avoiding causal or predictive conclusions from this small descriptive cohort.Keywords: Lumbar spine surgery, surgical site infection (SSI), deep infection, debridement, treatment burden

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among 16 patients with early deep surgical site infection, 14 underwent a total of 52 infection-related spinal reoperations, and 10 required vacuum-assisted closure with 37 foam changes. Eight patients experienced a major treatment-burden event. Median cumulative infection-related inpatient duration was 46.5 days in patients with an event and 23.0 days in those without, a descriptive absolute median difference of 23.5 days.

What is known and what is new?

• Postoperative spinal infection is known to require antimicrobial therapy, debridement, negative-pressure wound treatment, and decisions regarding implant retention or removal.

• This study adds detailed patient-level care pathways and shows that infection-related adverse outcomes, unplanned treatment-related injuries, and intentional implant-removal source-control procedures are clinically distinct events. Combining them into a single “complication rate may obscure the actual nature of the treatment burden.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Studies describing postoperative spinal infection should consider reporting the full treatment pathway including reoperations, vacuum-assisted closure procedures, cumulative inpatient days, intensive care, readmission, adverse outcomes, and implant-related source-control procedures—rather than relying only on an overall complication rate. These descriptive findings support clearer reporting and multidisciplinary care planning but do not establish the superiority of any treatment strategy.

Introduction

Surgical site infection (SSI) is an uncommon but serious complication after spine surgery because it may prolong hospitalization, increase resource use, delay recovery, and lead to unplanned reoperation (1-4). Reported rates vary substantially according to procedure type, instrumentation, case complexity, surveillance definitions, and patient-related risk factors (2-5). Deep infection is particularly consequential when it involves fascia, muscle, the interbody or epidural space, or implanted material, because eradication may require repeated debridement and difficult decisions regarding implant retention or removal (3,4,6-9).

Current evidence supports early source control and microbiology-guided antimicrobial treatment, but management remains heterogeneous. Early postoperative spinal implant infection is often managed with debridement and implant retention when mechanical stability can be preserved, whereas removal or exchange may be required when local control fails or the implant itself prevents source control (6-9). Recent multicenter data have also questioned routine extension of antibiotic treatment beyond 12 weeks after adequate debridement and implant retention, supporting individualized duration rather than a uniform prolonged course (10). Bibliometric analysis has shown increasing publication activity in spinal SSI, with prevention and risk factors among the prominent research themes (11).

Incidence and risk-factor studies do not fully describe the downstream clinical pathway after early deep SSI has occurred. For patients and surgeons, the burden includes repeated operations, negative-pressure wound therapy, intravenous and oral antimicrobial exposure, intensive care, readmission, implant-related source-control procedures, and treatment-associated adverse events. Therefore, this study aimed to describe the clinical course, microbiological findings, treatment pathways, and major treatment-burden events of early deep SSI after primary lumbar spine surgery at a Vietnamese tertiary center. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0129/rc).

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective descriptive observational study of consecutive patients who underwent primary lumbar spine surgery between October 2018 and October 2023 at 108 Military Central Hospital. All procedures were performed by one senior spine surgeon. The institutional operative log confirmed 1,923 primary lumbar operations during the study period; however, the detailed distribution of decompression-only procedures, instrumented fusion, number of operated levels, interbody cages, and implant categories could not be reliably reconstructed. Accordingly, the reported 0.83% represents a crude institutional incidence and was not used for procedure-specific risk comparisons.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of 108 Military Central Hospital (approval No. 3479/QĐ-BV), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their data were included in the study. Data were anonymized before analysis.

Participants

Patients were eligible when they developed a clinically diagnosed early deep SSI within 90 days after a primary lumbar procedure and had at least 6 months of follow-up. Revision procedures at the index operation, superficial incisional infection, infection diagnosed after 90 days, and incomplete clinical records were excluded. All included patients had follow-up data available for analysis.

Definition and diagnosis of early deep SSI

The operational definition was adapted from the 2026 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)/National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) SSI framework (12). Infection involving fascia or muscle was considered deep incisional SSI, whereas infection extending deeper than the fascial or muscular layer, including the interbody or epidural space, was considered organ/space SSI. Because this was a retrospective clinical study rather than prospective NHSN surveillance, formal procedure-category adjudication was not available. Diagnosis required compatible clinical findings—such as fever, increasing pain, swelling, wound erythema, drainage, delayed healing, or wound breakdown—together with evidence of deep involvement from operative findings, elevated inflammatory markers, magnetic resonance imaging, or microbiological testing. The two patients managed without reoperation and without culture had wound erythema/swelling, leukocytosis and elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and magnetic resonance imaging evidence of deep fluid around the operative bed or instrumentation, supporting deep rather than superficial infection.

Microbiological sampling and definitions of systemic events

Microbiological specimens were obtained in 14 patients. Sources included deep interbody fluid, peri-implant pus, wound fluid, vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) foam, and blood cultures; repeated specimens were obtained during subsequent debridements or VAC foam changes when clinically indicated. All recorded cultures were collected after the first empirical antibiotic dose, which may have reduced microbiological yield. Methicillin-susceptible and methicillin-resistant S. aureus were reported separately. Bloodstream infection was defined as a positive blood culture accompanied by high fever and rigors. Septic shock was recorded when diagnosed by the intensive care team and required vasopressor support.

Treatment strategy

Empirical intravenous treatment consisted of vancomycin plus amikacin and was subsequently modified according to microbiological findings and clinical response. Patients without abscess, purulent drainage, wound breakdown, or systemic deterioration could be managed without reoperation. Surgical treatment included irrigation and debridement, drainage, repeated wound revision, VAC therapy, and implant removal when considered necessary for source control while accounting for implant stability. Infectious disease consultation was obtained in selected complex cases. Cumulative intravenous antibiotic duration was calculated across all infection-related admissions. After discharge, all 16 patients received 12 weeks of oral combination therapy: rifampicin plus pefloxacin with either amoxicillin/clavulanate or linezolid, selected according to susceptibility results when available or the preceding intravenous regimen when susceptibility data were unavailable. This regimen describes local practice during the study period and is not presented as a comparative treatment recommendation. Patients were discharged from infection-related hospitalization when they were afebrile, the wound was dry or healed, leukocyte count had normalized, the erythrocyte sedimentation rate was decreasing, and no further inpatient debridement or VAC foam change was planned; oral therapy then continued for 12 weeks.

Data collection and outcomes

Data extracted from electronic records included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), diabetes mellitus, preoperative steroid exposure, index procedure, time from index surgery to SSI diagnosis, clinical and imaging findings, microbiology, antimicrobial treatment, intensive care, infectious disease consultation, number and timing of infection-related spinal reoperations, number of VAC foam changes, readmission, follow-up, and mortality. Cumulative infection-related inpatient duration was defined as the sum of inpatient days from SSI diagnosis through discharge across all admissions attributable to the infection; time spent at home between admissions was not counted.

Outcomes were stratified a priori into three clinically distinct categories: (I) infection-related adverse outcomes—bloodstream infection, septic shock, or recurrent deep infection; (II) unplanned treatment-related adverse events—an intraoperative injury occurring during infection management; and (III) implant-related source-control procedures—removal of IntraSPINE or a polyetheretherketone (PEEK) interbody cage. Implant removal was therefore not classified as a complication in the usual sense. No patient was assigned to an event category solely because treatment was prolonged.

Bias, study size, and missing data

All consecutive primary lumbar operations during the study period were screened to reduce selection bias. Definitions and event categories were specified before the revised analysis, and clinical, laboratory, imaging, microbiological, operative, and follow-up records were cross-checked. The study size was determined by the 16 eligible early deep SSI cases; no formal sample-size calculation was performed. Missing denominator details, culture timing after empirical therapy, incomplete susceptibility profiles, and unavailable lactate/Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) measurements were not imputed and are reported as limitations.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was primarily descriptive. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation when approximately symmetric and as median, interquartile range (IQR), and range: when skewed. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. The crude institutional incidence was accompanied by an exact binomial 95% confidence interval (CI). Because only 16 infected patients were available, no categorical hypothesis testing, multivariable modeling, or predictor analysis was performed. Cumulative inpatient duration was described exploratorily according to the presence or absence of a major treatment-burden event using medians and the absolute median difference; no causal inference was intended.

Results

Incidence and patient characteristics

Among 1,923 patients undergoing primary lumbar surgery, 16 developed early deep SSI, corresponding to a crude institutional incidence of 0.83% (95% CI, 0.48–1.35%). The mean age was 59.2±12.3 years, 13 patients (81.3%) were women, eight (50.0%) had diabetes mellitus, and nine (56.3%) had preoperative steroid exposure. Diagnosis occurred a median of 14.5 days after the index operation (IQR, 6.8–20.8 days; range, 4–70 days). The index operations comprised single-level discectomy in two patients, discectomy with IntraSPINE in two, dynamic fixation in one, and posterior fixation with or without interbody fusion in 11. Follow-up ranged from 6 to 60 months. Aggregate characteristics are presented in Table 1 and anonymized patient-level pathways in Table 2.

Microbiology and antimicrobial treatment

Fourteen patients underwent microbiological sampling. Six had methicillin-susceptible S. aureus, two had methicillin-resistant S. aureus, three had E. coli, one had E. faecium, and one had E. cloacae; one patient had negative culture results. Two nonoperatively managed patients were not cultured. Deep wound or peri-implant material was the principal specimen source; blood cultures and VAC foam cultures were also obtained in selected patients. Cumulative intravenous antibiotic duration was a median of 28.0 days (IQR, 15.0–37.5 days; range, 10–70 days), followed in every patient by 12 weeks of oral combination therapy after discharge.

Procedural and inpatient treatment burden

Fourteen patients underwent infection-related spinal reoperation, accounting for 52 spinal procedures. The first spinal reoperation occurred a median of 2 days after diagnosis (IQR, 1–2 days; range, 1–14 days). Among reoperated patients, the median number of spinal procedures was 4 (IQR, 2.5–4.8; range, 1–7). Ten patients received VAC therapy, with 37 recorded foam changes (median, 3; range, 3–6 among VAC-treated patients). Four patients received infectious disease consultation, two required intensive care, two were readmitted, and no patient died during follow-up. The cumulative infection-related inpatient duration was a median of 28.5 days (IQR, 16.5–42.3 days; range, 10–85 days).

Major treatment-burden events

Eight patients experienced at least one major treatment-burden event (Table 3). Infection-related adverse outcomes occurred in four patients: septic shock in two, bloodstream infection without shock in one, and recurrent deep infection in one. One patient sustained an unplanned small-bowel transection during spinal revision. Three patients underwent implant-related source-control procedures: removal of IntraSPINE in two and removal of a PEEK cage in one. The median cumulative infection-related inpatient duration was 46.5 days (IQR, 27.5–68.8 days; range, 10–85 days) among patients with an event and 23.0 days (IQR, 16.5–28.3 days; range, 10–37 days) among those without an event, an absolute median difference of 23.5 days. This difference is descriptive and should not be interpreted causally.

Small-bowel injury during revision

The patient with small-bowel injury initially underwent a left L5-S1 discectomy, followed by posterior debridement of the infected disc space. During a subsequent right-sided posterior re-entry through a new incision for persistent right S1 radicular pain, a complete small-bowel transection occurred. Computed tomography identified hollow-viscus perforation approximately 8 hours later, and open abdominal exploration was performed approximately 10 hours after the spinal procedure. The bowel was repaired with a side-to-side anastomosis. The abdominal operation was not counted among the two infection-related spinal reoperations. The patient remained clinically stable at 5-year follow-up.

Discussion

This descriptive series shows that a low crude incidence of early deep SSI can conceal substantial treatment burden after infection occurs. Fourteen of 16 patients underwent reoperation, with 52 infection-related spinal procedures and 37 VAC foam changes; some patients also required intensive care, readmission, implant-related source control, or management of a serious intraoperative injury. These procedural and care-pathway data provide a more clinically interpretable picture than a single undifferentiated “complication rate”. In a matched-cohort study of deep wound infection after instrumented posterior spinal fusion, Mok et al. reported that multiple debridements were associated with polymicrobial infection and later pseudarthrosis requiring reoperation (13).

The revised outcome framework is important because implant removal and adverse outcomes are not equivalent. Removal of IntraSPINE or a PEEK cage was an intentional source-control procedure, whereas septic shock, bloodstream infection, recurrent infection, and small-bowel injury represented adverse clinical events. Contemporary cohorts similarly suggest that mechanically stable implants can often be retained after early debridement, while removal remains appropriate when required for source control or when retention is unlikely to eradicate infection (7-9). Patel et al. reported similar persistence of infection after retention and removal in a 135-patient cohort, emphasizing that implant status should be interpreted within the broader treatment pathway rather than as a complication by itself (8).

The high reoperation and VAC burden in this cohort reflects difficult local control. Recent protocol-based data have reported successful debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention in selected early infections, but success depends on timing, adequate debridement, repeated deep sampling, organism susceptibility, implant stability, and clinical response (7-9). Our data cannot compare retention with removal or determine the effectiveness of VAC because treatment was individualized and no control group was available. They do, however, quantify the repeated procedures required in real-world practice.

Microbiological findings were dominated by S. aureus, with both methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) represented, while Gram-negative organisms and E. faecium were also encountered. This spectrum supports obtaining deep specimens whenever feasible and avoiding assumptions that all early postoperative spinal infections are caused by Gram-positive organisms. Because cultures in this cohort were obtained after the first empirical dose, the negative culture and the two noncultured cases must be interpreted cautiously. The latter were supported by concordant clinical, inflammatory-marker, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings of deep infection rather than isolated superficial wound changes.

All patients received cumulative intravenous treatment followed by 12 weeks of oral combination therapy, reflecting local practice during 2018–2023. Earlier prospective and cohort studies supported approximately 12 weeks of biofilm-active treatment in selected early implant-associated infections (6,7). However, a recent multicenter cohort of 499 early postoperative spinal implant infections found no reduction in treatment failure with antibiotic courses longer than 12 weeks after debridement and implant retention (10). Therefore, our regimen should be understood as descriptive historical practice, not evidence that the same combination or duration is optimal. Future protocols should individualize antimicrobial selection and duration with infectious disease input, surgical source control, microbiology, clinical response, and antimicrobial stewardship.

The small-bowel transection illustrates a treatment-related hazard distinct from the infection itself. Revision through an inflamed and anatomically distorted operative field may obscure tissue planes and increase the risk of visceral injury. Although bowel injury after lumbar surgery is rare, delayed recognition can be life-threatening (14). The practical lesson is not that reoperation should be avoided, but that revision planning should account for prior annular or anterior longitudinal ligament disruption, altered anatomy, and the need for prompt investigation when postoperative abdominal signs arise.

The exploratory comparison showed a 23.5-day greater median cumulative inpatient duration among patients with major treatment-burden events. This finding is clinically plausible but was not subjected to predictive interpretation. The study does not establish that diabetes, steroid exposure, BMI, organism, or any management strategy is or is not associated with adverse outcomes.

This study has several limitations. It was retrospective, included only 16 infected patients, lacked a control group, and reflected one center and one senior surgeon. The detailed procedure distribution of the 1,923-patient denominator could not be reconstructed, preventing procedure-specific incidence estimates and limiting interpretation of the crude rate. Cultures were obtained after empirical antibiotics; the number and timing of specimens were not standardized; complete susceptibility profiles were unavailable; and two patients were not cultured. Detailed lactate and SOFA data could not be reconstructed for the intensive-care diagnoses of septic shock. Oral regimens were not compared, and long-term functional or radiographic outcomes were not systematically measured. These limitations preclude causal, predictive, and comparative conclusions.

Despite these limitations, the complete patient-level treatment pathways add practical information about the burden generated after early deep SSI: repeated spinal procedures, VAC foam changes, prolonged inpatient care, antimicrobial exposure, source-control decisions, and adverse events. Larger multicenter studies should use standardized CDC/NHSN definitions, prospectively record denominator procedure categories and deep culture timing, and compare structured source-control and antimicrobial pathways using clinically meaningful outcomes.

Conclusions

Early deep SSI after primary lumbar spine surgery was uncommon in this single-center series but was associated with considerable treatment burden among affected patients. Most patients required reoperation, and several required repeated debridement, VAC therapy, implant-related source-control procedures, intensive care, readmission, or management of systemic or treatment-related adverse events. Although limited by the small sample size and retrospective design, these findings highlight the importance of early recognition, microbiological confirmation when feasible, careful revision planning, and coordinated multidisciplinary infection management.
