Abstract
. We study the resurgent structure of short-time heat kernel asymptotics from the viewpoint of Picard–Lefschetz theory. For a real analytic Riemannian manifold, we show the heat kernel admits a 1 1 –Gevrey small-time expansion whose Borel transform detects complex-geometric data beyond the real geodesic sector. We formulate an infinite-dimensional Picard–Lefschetz problem of Morse–Floer type for the holomorphic energy functional on the complexified path space, and propose a heat-kernel analogue of the Picard–Lefschetz/Alien correspondence. In this framework, pointed alien operators acting on the asymptotic expansion associated with the real geodesic are predicted to produce the formal heat-kernel sectors associated with other holomorphic geodesics, with coefficients given by signed counts of connecting trajectories of the Morse flow. We perform a confirming test of this proposal on the hyperbolic plane ℍ ℝ 2 \mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2} .



1.
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to explain how the resurgent structure of a heat-kernel expansion is reflected by Picard–Lefschetz theory on an infinite-dimensional complexified path space. This is motivated by the Picard–Lefschetz/Alien correspondence in the finite-dimensional case. We start by reviewing this correspondence following
[
30
]
and refer to it for further details and references therein.

1.1.
Lefschetz thimble and Borel singularity

Consider a finite-dimensional exponential integral

I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\int_{\Gamma}e^{-S(z)/\hbar}\,\mu

where
S(z)
is a holomorphic function on a complex manifold,
\mu
is a holomorphic form, and
\Gamma
is a contour (or cycle) in a suitable relative homology class
[\Gamma]
. The asymptotic behavior of
I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)
for small
\hbar
is controlled by the critical points of the phase function

\mathrm{d}S=0.

Assume
S
has only non-degenerate isolated critical points and the corresponding critical values are pairwise distinct. For each critical point
p
, there corresponds an integration cycle
\mathcal{J}_{p}
, called the Lefschetz thimble, which can be constructed by the downward gradient flow of
\re(S/\hbar)
(with a chosen Hermitian metric on
X
). One can decompose the cycle in homology class

[\Gamma]=\sum_{p}n_{p}[\mathcal{J}_{p}]

and obtain a representation of the integral by

I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)=\sum_{p}n_{p}\int_{\mathcal{J}_{p}}e^{-S(z)/\hbar}\,\mu.

Near each critical point
p
, one obtains a formal asymptotic expansion of the form

I_{p}(\hbar):=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{p}}e^{-S(z)/\hbar}\,\mu\sim e^{-S(p)/\hbar}\tilde{\phi}_{p}(\hbar)=e^{-S(p)/\hbar}\hbar^{n/2}\left(c_{p,0}+c_{p,1}\hbar+c_{p,2}\hbar^{2}+\cdots\right)\quad\text{as}\ \hbar\to 0

uniformly on closed subsectors of a sufficiently small sector in the
\hbar
-plane. This is the standard local steepest-descent (or stationary-phase) expansion of a Laplace-type integral near a non-degenerate critical point (see e.g.
[
5
,
39
]
); equivalently, it may be viewed as the local WKB expansion associated with the saddle
p
.
This expansion is typically divergent and only asymptotic in nature. They all together capture the asymptotic behavior of
I_{\Gamma}(\hbar)
for small
\hbar
.

The theory of resurgence developed by Écalle
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
provides a general framework to study global analytic natures of divergent asymptotic expansions. Under appropriate growth condition, the Borel transform
\hat{\phi}_{p}(\xi)
of the
\hbar
-asymptotic series
\tilde{\phi}_{p}(\hbar)
from the critical point
p
will be an integrable germ locally near
\xi=0
and admits analytic continuation beyond it. For
\arg\hbar=\theta
, one has a resummed function with the expected asymptotic expansion

I_{p}(\hbar):=\int_{\mathcal{J}_{p}}e^{-S(z)/\hbar}\,\mu=e^{-S(p)/\hbar}\,\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\widehat{\phi}_{p}(\hbar)\sim e^{-S(p)/\hbar}\widetilde{\phi}_{p}(\hbar),\qquad\hbar\to 0.

Here
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
is the Laplace tranform along the ray
e^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
(see Definition
2.3
). The Borel transform
\hat{\phi}_{p}(\xi)
may exhibit singularities in the
\xi
-plane. Such Borel singularities lead to possible ambiguities in the resummation. One has to choose a direction
e^{i\theta}
so that the ray in the Laplace transform avoids Borel singularities. Two different directions could give rise to different analytic objects when one ray has to pass certain singularities to reach the other. This is precisely the Stokes phenomenon.

For the exponential integral, the ambiguity of the ray for Laplace transform is related to the ambiguity of the choice of Lefschetz thimble. Take
\hbar
to be valued in
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and write

\hbar=|\hbar|e^{i\theta}.

Then the flow of
\re(S/\hbar)
is the same as that of
\re(e^{-i\theta}S)
. The magic of holomorphic Morse function
S
tells that along the the downward gradient flow of
\re(S/\hbar)
:

\displaystyle(1)~\re(e^{-i\theta}S)\ \text{is {decreasing}},

\displaystyle\qquad(2)~\im(e^{-i\theta}S)\ \text{is {constant}}.

The corresponding Lefschetz thimble is represented by (here we specify its
\theta
-dependence)

\mathcal{J}_{p}^{\theta}=\{z\in X\mid\text{$z$ flows to $p$ at time $t=+\infty$}\}.

With our assumptions on
S
, at generic
\theta
, the values of
\im(e^{-i\theta}S)
at critical points are all distinct, so all such constructed thimbles are disjoint. If we sit at some
\theta_{*}
such that

\im(e^{-i\theta_{*}}S(p))=\im(e^{-i\theta_{*}}S(q))

for two critical points
p,q
, then one could have flow trajectories that connect one critical point to another. Such
\theta_{*}
is called a Stokes phase and the ray in the direction
e^{i\theta_{*}}
is called a Stokes ray.

Assume we have a trajectory that flows from
q
to
p
at a Stokes phase
\theta_{*}
(this requires
\re(e^{-i\theta_{*}}S(p))<\re(e^{-i\theta_{*}}S(q))
by the downward gradient flow). Then the Borel transform
\hat{\phi}_{p}(\xi)
will see a singularity in the direction
e^{i\theta_{*}}
at

\omega=S(q)-S(p).

Thus Borel singularities correspond to relative critical values with respect to a reference critical point.

In
[
25
,
26
]
, Kontsevich and Soibelman investigated analyticity and resurgence phenomena in the context of Lefschetz thimbles and their wall-crossing behavior, and develop a Floer-theoretic framework for exponential integrals. Our study of the complexified path space in this work follows the perspective of holomorphic Morse–Floer theory and connects it to the alien calculus, as we explain next.

1.2.
Connecting trajectory and Alien operator

Borel singularities lead to rich algebraic structures on the global analytic nature of divergence series. They are captured by the so-called Alien operators.

Let
\widetilde{\phi}(\hbar)

be a
1
–Gevrey formal series (see Section
2.1
). Let

\widehat{\phi}(\xi)=\mathcal{B}\widetilde{\phi}(\xi)

be its Borel transform. Suppose that
\widehat{\phi}
admits analytic
continuation from the origin to a singular point
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, and the germ is integrable at
\omega
. Then we can obtain a formal series in
\hbar
, denoted by

\Delta_{\omega}^{+}\widetilde{\phi}

by extracting the local
variation of the Borel transform at
\omega
, recentering it at the origin,
and applying inverse Borel transform. See Section
2.6
for the precise definition and references.

\Delta_{\omega}^{+}
is called the positive alien operator. The corresponding pointed alien operator is

\dot{\Delta}_{\omega}^{+}:=e^{-\omega/\hbar}\Delta_{\omega}^{+}.

The exponential factor restores the action shift carried by the Borel
singularity. For instance, if a formal solution with exponential weight

e^{-\Phi_{0}/\hbar}
has a Borel singularity at

\omega=\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{0},

then
\dot{\Delta}_{\omega}^{+}
produces a contribution with weight

e^{-\Phi_{1}/\hbar}.

The finite-dimensional analysis of
[
30
]
provides the guiding
principle for the comparison below. There, the pointed alien operator acts on
the formal saddle expansion
\widetilde{I}_{p}
attached to a critical point

p
, and produces the expansion
\widetilde{I}_{q}
attached to another
critical point
q
when

\omega=S(q)-S(p)

is a Borel singularity. More precisely, if

\dot{\Delta}_{\omega}^{+}\,\widetilde{I}_{p}=N_{pq}\,\widetilde{I}_{q},

then
N_{pq}
agrees with the corresponding Picard–Lefschetz
wall-crossing coefficient; in elementary situations, this is the signed
count of direct connecting trajectories at the relevant Stokes phase:

N_{pq}=\text{signed}\ \sharp\{\text{flow}\ \gamma(t):\gamma(-\infty)=q,\gamma(+\infty)=p\}.

This identification is established in
[
30
]
by comparing the
Stokes automorphism on the resurgent side with the thimble jump formula on
the Picard–Lefschetz side.

Here is an example for illustration. Consider
X=\mathbb{C}^{*}
and the exponential integral

\int_{\Gamma}e^{-\frac{1}{2\hbar}\left(z-\frac{1}{z}\right)}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}.

There are two critical points
p_{\pm}=\pm i
, which lead to two asymptotic series
I_{\pm}(\hbar)
. The Stokes phases appear at

.

\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta

At
\theta_{*}=\frac{\pi}{2}
, there are two connecting trajectories from

\dot{\Delta}_{\omega}^{+}\widetilde{I}_{-}=2\,\widetilde{I}_{+}.

1.3.
Holomorphic Morse-Floer Theory

In this paper, we formulate an infinite-dimensional Picard–Lefschetz problem of Morse–Floer type following Witten’s idea of analytic continuation of quantum mechanics
[
44
]
. In
[
44
]
, the path integral is formulated on the phase space, and an appropriate complexification of the target is a hyperKähler manifold. Our formulation is instead modeled on the path integral over the configuration space, which could be understood as the adiabatic limit of the hyperKähler construction.

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian manifold. Fix
\tau>0
and
a,b\in M
. Consider the Sobolev path space

\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M):=\bigl\{\gamma\in H^{1}([0,\tau],M)\,:\,\gamma(0)=a,\ \gamma(\tau)=b\bigr\}

equipped with the energy functional (the
\frac{1}{4}
convention is for convenience of resurgence analysis)

\mathcal{E}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\tau}g(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

Critical points of
\mathcal{E}
correspond to geodesics in
M
from
a
to
b
.

We are interested in Picard–Lefschetz theory on path spaces. To set things up, we need a holomorphic structure. Assume
(M,g)
is real analytic and extends to an appropriate complexification
(M_{\mathbb{C}},g_{\mathbb{C}})
where
M_{\mathbb{C}}
is a complex manifold,
g_{\mathbb{C}}
is a holomorphic metric such that
M
is a connected component of the real locus of
M_{\mathbb{C}}
and
g
is the restriction of
g_{\mathbb{C}}
. We discuss a few related constructions of complexification in Section
2.3
. Then the natural candidate of our compexified path space is

\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}})=\left\{\gamma\in H^{1}([0,\tau],M_{\mathbb{C}}):\gamma(0)=a,\ \gamma(\tau)=b\right\}

which inherits a complex structure from that of
M_{\mathbb{C}}
. The complexified energy by

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\tau}g_{\mathbb{C}}(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t

can be viewed as a holomorphic function on the infinite-dimensional complex manifold
\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}})
.

Note that if we normalize the path by re-parametrization

\tilde{\gamma}(t):=\gamma(\tau t):[0,1]\to M_{\mathbb{C}},

then

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{4\tau}\int_{0}^{1}g_{\mathbb{C}}(\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}(t),\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

Thus
\tau
plays the analogous role of
\hbar
. Motivated by Feynman-Kac formula, Picard–Lefschetz theory of such type is related to the resurgence problem of heat kernel at time
\tau
. The following theorem on the Borel summability of heat kernel at small time provides a first evidence.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
2.5
)
.

Let
(M,g)
be real analytic and
\Delta
be the Laplace–Beltrami
operator acting on functions

\Delta f=-\mathrm{div}(\nabla f).

Let
V
be a sufficiently small compact
convex neighborhood with the heat kernel asymptotic expansion

K(\tau;x,y)\sim(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-d(x,y)^{2}/(4\tau)}\sum_{j\geq 0}u_{j}(x,y)\tau^{j},\quad(x,y)\in V\times V.

Then the coefficient series in time
\tau

\widetilde{u}(\tau;x,y)=\sum_{j\geq 0}u_{j}(x,y)\tau^{j}

is
1
–Gevrey, uniformly for
(x,y)\in V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}
with

V^{\prime}\Subset V
. Hence its Borel transform

\widehat{u}(\xi;x,y)=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{u_{j}(x,y)}{j!}\xi^{j}\quad\text{converges near $\xi=0$. }

We prove Theorem
1.1
in Section
2.2
. It allows us to do resurgence analysis in the plane of Borel transform of time. In particular, the analytic continuation of the Borel transform
\widehat{u}(\xi;a,b)
is expected to reflect the Picard–Lefschetz geometry of

\tau\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}:\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}})\to\mathbb{C}.

The first surprise comes from the critical points. Standard calculus of variation shows that critical points of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
are holomorphic geodesics in
M_{\mathbb{C}}
from
a
to
b
(see Section
2.4
). They contain usual real geodesics inside
M
, as well as possible new holomorphic geodesics that go out of
M
into

before reaching

. Let
a,b\in M
be close and such that there exists a unique minimal geodesic in

from
a
to

. The heat kernel

is constructed purely from the data on

, but the Borel transform

will detect geometric information on

. For example, consider the set of Borel singularities of

. As explained in Section
1.1
, they correspond to critical values of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
relative to the energy of the minimal geodesic, and we shall observe those new holomorphic geodesics from
\widehat{u}(\xi;a,b)
. This can be viewed as the Picard–Lefschetz analogue of the quantum tunneling phenomenon.

As an example, consider the two dimensional hyperboloid space

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\{-x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=-1\mid x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},x_{0}>0\},\quad\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-\mathrm{d}x_{0}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2}\big|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}.

Borel summability of heat kernel asymptotics

We study the short-time asymptotic expansion of the heat kernel and its Borel summability. Throughout this subsection, the Gevrey parameter
\hbar

\tau

1

We will use

Let
(M,g)

n

When

Remark 2.4
.

The precise meaning of this asymptotic expansion in the sense of Definition
2.1
is understood as

(4\pi\tau)^{n/2}\exp\!\left(\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{4\tau}\right)K(\tau;x,y)\sim\sum_{k\geq 0}u_{k}(x,y)\tau^{k}\in\mathbb{C}[[\tau]].

On a local chart
\Omega

where

are explicitly expressed in terms of the Riemannian metric

are also real analytic. In this case, we have the following result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(M,g)
be real analytic. Let

be a sufficiently small compact
convex neighborhood with the heat kernel asymptotic expansion

(2)

K(\tau;x,y)\sim(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-d(x,y)^{2}/(4\tau)}\sum_{j\geq 0}u_{j}(x,y)\tau^{j},\quad(x,y)\in V\times V.

Then
\sum\limits_{j\geq 0}u_{j}(x,y)\tau^{j}

is
1

(x,y)\in V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}
with

V^{\prime}\Subset V
.

Remark 2.6
.

The Gevrey estimate of Theorem

The rest of this subsection is devoted to the proof of Theorem

2.5
. It is well known that the coefficients in
the short-time heat kernel expansion agree with the coefficients obtained
from the formal solution of the heat equation after extracting the exponential
factor and imposing the standard normalization
u_{0}(x,x)=1
. Therefore, our
goal is to prove that the coefficients
u_{j}(x,y)
obtained recursively from
the transport equations satisfy the
1

|u_{j}(x,y)|\leq AB^{j}j!

uniformly for
(x,y)\in V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}

V^{\prime}\Subset V
and

Parametrix, recursion relation and complex extension

First, as in the smooth case, we have the short-time asymptotic expansion of
K(\tau;x,y)

K(\tau;x,y)\sim(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-d(x,y)^{2}/(4\tau)}\widetilde{u}(\tau;x,y),\qquad\widetilde{u}(\tau;x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}u_{i}(x,y)\tau^{i}.

For simplicity, denote
H(\tau;x,y)=(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-d(x,y)^{2}/(4\tau)}
. Then one can compute

(3)

(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta_{x})(H\widetilde{u})=H\left(\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{u}+\frac{T}{\tau}\widetilde{u}+\Delta_{x}\widetilde{u}\right)

where
T
is a first-order differential operator of the form

T\widetilde{u}=\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}g^{ij}(x)\partial_{i}d^{2}(x,y)\partial_{j}\widetilde{u}+B(x,y)\widetilde{u},

with an analytic scalar coefficient
B(x,y)=-\Delta_{x}\left(\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{4}\right)-\frac{n}{2}
on
V\times V
determined by
g^{ij}
,
a^{i}(x)
,
b(x)
and
d(x,y)
. In particular, the coefficients of the first differential operator
T
are real analytic.

Expanding Equation (
3
), we get the recursion relation for
u_{j}(x,y)
:

Tu_{0}(x,y)=0,\qquad(T+j+1)u_{j+1}(x,y)=-\Delta u_{j}(x,y).

In particular, the normalization
u_{0}(x,x)=1
is forced by the delta initial condition of the heat kernel. These coefficient equations admit analytic solutions on
V\times V
.

The analytic hypothesis allows us to extend all coefficients holomorphically to a small complex neighborhood. That is, after shrinking
V
once and for all, we may choose
\rho>0
so that every
u_{j}
constructed by the recursion extends holomorphically to

\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(V^{\prime}):=\bigcup_{(x,y)\in V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}}P_{\rho}(x)\times P_{\rho}(y)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Here
P_{\rho}(x)
is the complex coordinate polydisc of radius
\rho
centered at
x
, so that
\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(V^{\prime})
is a small complex polydisc neighborhood of
V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

After shrinking
V
if necessary, and fixing
V^{\prime}\Subset V
, there exist radii
0<\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
and a constant
C_{T}>0
such that the following holds: every coefficient
u_{j}
extends holomorphically to
\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{2}}(V^{\prime})
, and we have the uniform estimate

(4)

\|u_{j+1}\|_{\rho_{1}}\leq\frac{C_{T}}{j+1}\,\|\Delta_{x}u_{j}\|_{\rho_{2}},\qquad\|f\|_{\rho}:=\sup_{\mathcal{P}_{\rho}(V^{\prime})}|f|.

In particular, restricting to the real locus gives

\sup_{V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}}|u_{j+1}|\leq\frac{C_{T}}{j+1}\sup_{V\times V}|\Delta u_{j}|.

Proof.

We work in a fixed analytic coordinate chart and still write
\Delta_{x}
for the holomorphic extension of the Laplace–Beltrami operator.

After shrinking
V
, we may assume that any two points of
V
are joined by a unique real analytic geodesic depending holomorphically on the endpoints.
Then in the chosen analytic coordinates,
the real analytic dependence of the geodesic on its endpoints extends to a
holomorphic map on a small complex
neighborhood of
V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}
, satisfying the holomorphic geodesic equation (see Equation (
7
) for an explicit explanation). Denote such a holomorphic extension by

\Gamma_{\sigma}(x,y),\qquad 0\leq\sigma\leq 1.

So
\Gamma_{0}(x,y)=x
and
\Gamma_{1}(x,y)=y
.

After possibly shrinking
V
further and choosing
0<\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}

small enough, we may assume that

(\Gamma_{\sigma}(x,y),y)\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{2}}(V^{\prime}),\qquad\text{for all }(x,y)\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{1}}(V^{\prime}),~0\leq\sigma\leq 1.

In the holomorphic parametrix construction, the coefficients
u_{j}

are still determined by the transport equations

u_{j+1}(x,y)=\pm u_{0}(x,y)\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{j}\,u_{0}(\Gamma_{\sigma}(x,y),y)^{-1}\bigl(\Delta_{x}u_{j}\bigr)(\Gamma_{\sigma}(x,y),y)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

The sign is irrelevant for the estimate later. The holomorphicity of each
u_{j}
follows from this formula:

•

The function
u_{0}
is
holomorphic and non-vanishing on a sufficiently small complex
neighborhood of the diagonal.

•

By induction, suppose that
u_{j}

extends holomorphically to the relevant complex tube. Then

\Delta_{x}u_{j}
is holomorphic there as well, because
\Delta_{x}
has holomorphic coefficients in our analytic coordinates.

•

The function
(x,y)\mapsto\Gamma_{\sigma}(x,y)
is holomorphic and depends
continuously on
\sigma\in[0,1]
.

Then the integrand above is holomorphic
in
(x,y)
and continuous in
\sigma
. Therefore the integral
defines a holomorphic function of
(x,y)
. This proves recursively
that every
u_{j}
extends holomorphically to

\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{2}}(V^{\prime})
, after shrinking the tube if necessary.

It remains to prove the estimate. Since
u_{0}
is holomorphic and
non-vanishing on the chosen tube, the ratio
\frac{u_{0}(x,y)}{u_{0}(\Gamma_{\sigma}(x,y),y)}

is uniformly bounded for
(x,y)\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{1}}(V^{\prime}),\ 0\leq\sigma\leq 1
. Set

C_{T}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{1}}(V^{\prime})\\
0\leq\sigma\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{u_{0}(x,y)}{u_{0}(\Gamma_{\sigma}(x,y),y)}\right|.

Using the transport formula and the inclusion
(\Gamma_{\sigma}(x,y),y)\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{2}}(V^{\prime})
,
we obtain

\displaystyle|u_{j+1}(x,y)|\leq C_{T}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{j}\left|(\Delta_{x}u_{j})(\Gamma_{\sigma}(x,y),y)\right|\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\leq C_{T}\|\Delta_{x}u_{j}\|_{\rho_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{j}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\frac{C_{T}}{j+1}\|\Delta_{x}u_{j}\|_{\rho_{2}}.

Taking the supremum over

(x,y)\in\mathcal{P}_{\rho_{1}}(V^{\prime})
gives

\|u_{j+1}\|_{\rho_{1}}\leq\frac{C_{T}}{j+1}\|\Delta u_{j}\|_{\rho_{2}}
.
Restricting to the real locus immediately gives

\sup_{V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}}|u_{j+1}|\leq\frac{C_{T}}{j+1}\sup_{V\times V}|\Delta_{x}u_{j}|,

where the right-hand side may be taken over

, because the

remain in

after the initial shrinking of

.
∎

Cauchy estimates and the estimates for
u_{j}
and the remainder

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
f
be holomorphic on
P_{\rho}(z_{0})\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
and suppose that
\sup_{P_{\rho}(z_{0})}|f|\leq M
.
Then for any multiindex
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,

|\partial^{\alpha}f(z_{0})|\leq M~\frac{\alpha!}{\rho^{|\alpha|}}.

More generally, for any
0<\rho^{\prime}<\rho
, we have

\sup_{z\in P_{\rho^{\prime}}(z_{0})}|\partial^{\alpha}f(z)|\leq M~\frac{\alpha!}{(\rho-\rho^{\prime})^{|\alpha|}}

Proof.

First, let us prove the one-variable case. Assume
z_{0}=0
. Cauchy formula says,

f^{(k)}(z)=\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta-z|=r}\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z)^{k+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

When
|z|\leq\rho^{\prime}
, choose
r=\rho-\rho^{\prime}
, then we have

|f^{(k)}(z)|\leq\frac{k!}{2\pi}\cdot(2\pi r)\cdot\frac{M}{r^{k+1}}=\frac{k!M}{r^{k}}=\frac{k!M}{(\rho-\rho^{\prime})^{k}}.

For the multi-variable in the polydisc, just apply the one-variable Cauchy integral formula in each complex coordinate and iterate.
∎

The key lemma of the estimate for
u_{j}(x,y)
is as follows:

Lemma 2.9
.

There exist constants
C_{0},A_{0}>0
(depending on
V^{\prime}\subset V
) such that the coefficients satisfy

\|u_{j}\|_{\infty,V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}}\leq C_{0}~A_{0}^{j}~j!,\quad j\geq 0.

Proof.

First, for
0<\rho^{\prime\prime}<\rho^{\prime}
, by Lemma
2.7
, we have

(5)

\sup_{P_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}(x)\times P_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}(y)}|u_{j+1}|\leq C_{T}\sup_{P_{\rho^{\prime}}(x)\times P_{\rho^{\prime}}(y)}\frac{|\Delta u_{j}|}{j+1}.

Since
u_{j}
is holomorphic on
P_{\rho}(x)\times P_{\rho}(y)
, by Cauchy estimate Lemma
2.8
, there exists a constant
C_{L}
(depending on uniform bounds of
g^{ij},a^{i},b
on the polydisc) such that

(6)

\sup_{P_{\rho^{\prime}}(x)\times P_{\rho^{\prime}}(y)}|\Delta_{x}u_{j}|\leq C_{L}(\rho-\rho^{\prime})^{-2}\sup_{P_{\rho}(x)\times P_{\rho}(y)}|u_{j}|.

Choose
\rho\geq 2\rho^{\prime}-\rho^{\prime\prime}
, and combine inequalities (
5
)–(
6
), we get

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}(x)\times P_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}(y)}|u_{j+1}|\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{C_{T}C_{L}}{j+1}(\rho-\rho^{\prime})^{-2}\sup_{P_{\rho}(x)\times P_{\rho}(y)}|u_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{4C_{T}C_{L}}{j+1}(\rho-\rho^{\prime\prime})^{-2}\sup_{P_{\rho}(x)\times P_{\rho}(y)}|u_{j}|.

Now choose
N
large enough, and choose a decreasing sequence of radii

\rho_{0}>\rho_{1}>\rho_{2}>\cdots>\rho_{N}

as follows

\rho_{k}=\rho_{N}+\frac{N-k}{N}\delta,\qquad\delta=\rho_{0}-\rho_{N}.

Then

\|u_{N}\|_{\rho_{N}}\leq\left(\frac{C}{\delta^{2}}\right)^{N}\frac{N^{2N}}{N!}\|u_{0}\|_{\rho_{0}}.

Using the weak version of Stirling formula

N!\geq\left(\frac{N}{e}\right)^{N}
, we obtain

\|u_{N}\|_{\rho_{N}}\leq\left(\frac{Ce^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right)^{N}N!\|u_{0}\|_{\rho_{0}}.

Then choosing
C_{0}=\|u_{0}\|_{\rho_{0}}
,
A_{0}=\frac{Ce^{2}}{\delta^{2}}
, we are done.
∎

It remains to estimate the remainder. Write

K_{N}^{\rm loc}(\tau;x,y):=(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-d(x,y)^{2}/(4\tau)}\sum_{j=0}^{N}u_{j}(x,y)\tau^{j}=H(\tau;x,y)\sum_{j=0}^{N}u_{j}(x,y)\tau^{j}

for the truncated local parametrix. Then

(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta_{x})K_{N}^{\rm loc}(\tau;x,y)=H(\tau;x,y)\tau^{N}E_{N}(x,y),

where
E_{N}
is an analytic function depending linearly on
\Delta_{x}u_{N}
and lower-order terms. Using Lemma
2.9
and Cauchy
bounds for derivatives, we get

\|E_{N}\|_{\infty,V_{1}\times V_{1}}\leq C_{2}A_{2}^{N+1}(N+1)!,

where
V^{\prime}\Subset V_{1}\Subset V
.

We now localize the parametrix. Choose a cutoff
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(V\times V)
which is identically one in a neighborhood of the diagonal over

V_{1}\times V_{1}
, and in particular equals one on
V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}

after shrinking
V^{\prime}
if necessary. Set

\widehat{K}_{N}(\tau;x,y):=\chi(x,y)K_{N}^{\rm loc}(\tau;x,y).

Then
\widehat{K}_{N}=K_{N}^{\rm loc}
on
V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}
. Moreover,

(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta)\widehat{K}_{N}=\chi H\tau^{N}E_{N}+C_{N},\qquad C_{N}:=[\Delta,\chi]K_{N}^{\rm loc}.

Here
C_{N}
is supported where derivatives of
\chi
are nonzero;
hence it is supported away from the diagonal.

Let

R_{N}(\tau;x,y):=K(\tau;x,y)-\widehat{K}_{N}(\tau;x,y).

Since
\chi=1
near the diagonal,
R_{N}
has zero initial data. Hence

(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta)R_{N}=-\chi H\tau^{N}E_{N}-C_{N},\qquad\lim_{\tau\to 0^{+}}R_{N}(\tau)=0.

By Duhamel’s formula,

\displaystyle R_{N}(\tau;x,y)=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{M}K(\tau-t;x,z)\chi(z,y)H(t;z,y)t^{N}E_{N}(z,y)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{M}K(\tau-t;x,z)C_{N}(t;z,y)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}t.

The second integral is exponentially small as
\tau\to 0^{+}

\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{M}K(\tau-t;x,z)C_{N}(t;z,y)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}t=O(e^{-c/\tau}),\quad x,y\in V^{\prime}

for some
c>0
.

For the first integral, the cutoff restricts the
z
-integration to a
compact subset of
V
, we have

\int_{M}K(\tau-t;x,z)\chi(z,y)H(t;z,y)\,\mathrm{d}z\leq CH(\tau;x,y),\qquad 0<t<\tau.

Therefore, for
x,y\in V^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle|R_{N}(\tau;x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq CA_{2}^{N+1}(N+1)!H(\tau;x,y)\int_{0}^{\tau}t^{N}\,\mathrm{d}t+O(e^{-c/\tau})

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}A^{\prime N+1}(N+1)!\tau^{N+1-n/2}e^{-d(x,y)^{2}/(4\tau)}.

Since
\widehat{K}_{N}=K_{N}^{\rm loc}
on
V^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}
, this proves the
stated Gevrey remainder estimate in Theorem
2.5
.∎

2.3.
Complexification

Theorem
2.5
suggests that we could perform resurgence analysis of heat kernel on the plane of Borel transform of time. As explained in the introduction, this phenomenon is expected to reflect a version of Picard–Lefschetz theory on the complexified path space. A natural way to introduce such complexification is to extend the real-analytic manifold
(M,g)
into
(M_{\mathbb{C}},g_{\mathbb{C}})
where
M_{\mathbb{C}}
is a complex manifold,
g_{\mathbb{C}}
is a holomorphic metric such that
M
is a connected component of the real locus of
M_{\mathbb{C}}
and
g
is the restriction of
g_{\mathbb{C}}
.

There are several closely related notions of complexification in the literature. Classical results of Bruhat–Whitney and Grauert imply that every paracompact real-analytic manifold admits a local/germ-level complexification, and one may often choose
M_{\mathbb{C}}
Stein. There are two classical constructions for such complexifications, known as Grauert tubes.

(1)

The first one, by Bruhat and Whitney
[
43
]
, is based on a gluing construction: complex analytic extensions of real analytic charts are glued together in a suitable way.

(2)

The second one was devised by Grauert
[
13
]
, and developed by Akhiezzer and Gindikin
[
1
]
, Guillemin and Stenzel
[
14
]
, Lempert and Szöke
[
29
,
42
]
, Patrizio and Wong
[
37
]
.

When
M
is equipped with a real-analytic Riemannian metric
g
, and in local real-analytic coordinates
x=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
, write

g=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x^{i}\mathrm{d}x^{j}.

Since the coefficients
g_{ij}
are real analytic, they admit holomorphic extensions
g_{ij}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)
to a small complex neighborhood. Hence one obtains a holomorphic symmetric
2
-tensor

g_{\mathbb{C}}:=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z^{i}\mathrm{d}z^{j},

which restricts to
g
on the real locus. After shrinking the neighborhood if necessary,
\det(g_{ij}^{\mathbb{C}}(z))\neq 0
, so
g_{\mathbb{C}}
is a holomorphic metric. This extension is canonical at the level of germs: different analytic charts or different local models give biholomorphically equivalent holomorphic metrics along
M
.

As in the real case, there is a unique torsion-free holomorphic connection
\nabla^{\mathbb{C}}
, also known as holomorphic Levi-Civita connection
[
4
]
, satisfying

\nabla^{\mathbb{C}}g_{\mathbb{C}}=0.

In local coordinates, its Christoffel symbols are the holomorphic extensions of the usual one:

\Gamma^{k,\mathbb{C}}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}(g_{\mathbb{C}})^{k\ell}\bigl(\partial_{i}g^{\mathbb{C}}_{j\ell}+\partial_{j}g^{\mathbb{C}}_{i\ell}-\partial_{\ell}g^{\mathbb{C}}_{ij}\bigr).

The geodesic equation also extends holomorphically:

(7)

\ddot{z}^{k}+\sum_{i,j}\Gamma^{k,\mathbb{C}}_{ij}(z)\dot{z}^{i}\dot{z}^{j}=0.

Their solutions will be called holomorphic geodesics.

Remark 2.10
.

For Lie groups, one also has
the universal complexification, characterized by a universal property for
holomorphic homomorphisms into complex Lie groups; see, for example,

[
20
,
21
,
19
,
36
]
. For Riemannian manifolds with negative sectional curvature, the structure of a Grauert domain is obstructed to extend to the whole tangent bundle. There is an upper bound on the radius of a Grauert tube. See Lempert-Szöke
[
29
,
42
]
.

In this paper, we use a fixed admissible embedded complexification

\iota:M\hookrightarrow M_{\mathbb{C}}.

This means that
M
is a connected component of the real locus of
M_{\mathbb{C}}
, locally modeled on

(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and that the metric
g
extends to a
holomorphic metric
g_{\mathbb{C}}
on
M_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Assumption 1
.

Example 2.13

(Complexification of the real hyperbolic plane

)
.

This example will play a major role in this paper, and we will discuss it in some detail. We consider the following three models.

(1)

The upper half plane model:

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\mid y>0\}

with the metric

g=\frac{\mathrm{d}x^{2}+\mathrm{d}y^{2}}{y^{2}}=-\frac{4\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\bar{z}}{(z-\bar{z})^{2}}.

(2)

The symmetric space model:

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=SL(2,\mathbb{R})/SO(2,\mathbb{R})

with the (
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariant) metric.

(3)

The hyperboloid model:

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\{-x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=-1\mid x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},x_{0}>0\}

with the metric

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-\mathrm{d}x_{0}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2}\big|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}.

There are two well-known complexifications of
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

\bullet~

Minimal complexification

For the upper half plane model, we consider the space

X:=\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2-}=\{(z,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\mid\im z>0,\im w<0\}

with the conjugation

\tau:X\rightarrow X,\quad(z,w)\mapsto(\bar{w},\bar{z})

and the holomorphic metric

g_{\mathbb{C}}=-2(\mathrm{d}z\otimes\mathrm{d}w+\mathrm{d}w\otimes\mathrm{d}z)/(z-w)^{2}.

We have the embedding

\iota:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\hookrightarrow X,\quad z\mapsto(z,\bar{z}),

satisfying
\iota^{*}g_{\mathbb{C}}=g
. Such
X
is a
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariant Stein domain in
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
(see the definition below).
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
acts on
X
properly, and the metric
g_{\mathbb{C}}
is a (the unique)
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
-invariant holomorphic metric.

\bullet~

Affine complexification

Upon complexifying
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
,
SO(2,\mathbb{R})
, we obtain the affine complexification

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}=SL(2,\mathbb{C})/SO(2,\mathbb{C})\cong\{-z_{0}^{2}+z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}=-1\mid z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\}

with the holomorphic metric

\mathrm{d}s_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}=-\mathrm{d}z_{0}^{2}+\mathrm{d}z_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}z_{2}^{2}\big|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}.

The map

SL(2,\mathbb{R})/SO(2,\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow SL(2,\mathbb{C})/SO(2,\mathbb{C}),\qquad g\cdot SO(2,\mathbb{R})\mapsto g\cdot SO(2,\mathbb{C})

constitutes a
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-equivariant embedding which realizes
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=SL(2,\mathbb{R})/SO(2,\mathbb{R})
as one connected component of the totally real submanifold of
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

The two complexifications play rather different roles. The domain

X=\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2-}

is the minimal complexification: it is a Stein neighborhood of the real slice and corresponds to choosing one single-valued branch of the complexified geodesic geometry. By contrast, the affine quadric
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is larger and contains the extra complex branches responsible for monodromy and the infinitely many holomorphic geodesics with the same real endpoints (see Example
2.26
). In particular,
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
satisfies Assumption
1
while
X
does not. We have

•

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a connected component of fixed point set of the complex conjugation in
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

•

\iota:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is not homotopy equivalence.

More concretely, consider the map

\Psi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2},\qquad\Psi(z,w)=\left(i\frac{1+zw}{z-w},\,i\frac{z+w}{z-w},\,i\frac{1-zw}{z-w}\right)

One checks that

\Psi^{*}(\mathrm{d}s_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})=-\,\frac{2(\mathrm{d}z\otimes\mathrm{d}w+\mathrm{d}w\otimes\mathrm{d}z)}{(z-w)^{2}}=g_{\mathbb{C}}.

Hence the holomorphic metric on the minimal model is simply the pullback of the affine holomorphic metric; in particular the holomorphic Levi-Civita connection on

is the pullback of that of
\mathrm{d}s_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

2.4.
Picard–Lefschetz problem on complexified path spaces

We are ready to discuss the Picard–Lefschetz problem on the complexified path space and connect it to the resurgence analysis of heat kernel.

Let
M_{\mathbb{C}}
be a complexified manifold of
M
with an anti-holomorphic involution
\tau
, complex structure
J
, and
let
a,b\in M\subset M_{\mathbb{C}}
be real endpoints. For variational problems, it is natural to work on the Sobolev path space

\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}})=\left\{\gamma\in H^{1}([0,\tau],M_{\mathbb{C}}):\gamma(0)=a,\ \gamma(\tau)=b\right\}.

Since
H^{1}([0,\tau])\hookrightarrow C^{0}([0,\tau])
,
\gamma
is continuous and the endpoint conditions are
well-defined. The complex structure on
M_{\mathbb{C}}
induces a pointwise almost complex
structure on the path space. For

\xi\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}})=H^{1}_{0}([0,\tau],\gamma^{*}TM_{\mathbb{C}}),

the induced complex structure is defined by

(\mathbb{J}_{\gamma}\xi)(t):=J_{\gamma(t)}\xi(t).

This satisfies
\mathbb{J}_{\gamma}^{2}=-\mathrm{id}
.

Let
g_{\mathbb{C}}
be a holomorphic complex-bilinear metric on

M_{\mathbb{C}}
. We define the complexified energy by

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\tau}g_{\mathbb{C},\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\gamma\in H^{1}.

We choose
\frac{1}{4}
for convenience of resurgence analysis. In local holomorphic coordinates
z=(z^{1},\ldots,z^{n})
,

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}(z)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(z(t))\,\dot{z}^{i}(t)\dot{z}^{j}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where the functions
g_{ij}
are holomorphic.

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
is holomorphic in the Banach-manifold sense:
in
H^{1}
-charts it is a holomorphic map from an open subset of a complex
Hilbert space to
\mathbb{C}
.
Equivalently, its first variation is complex-linear in the variation.
Indeed, for

\xi\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{P}^{1}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}})
, one has

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C},\gamma}(\xi)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sum_{i,j,k}(\partial_{k}g_{ij})(\gamma(t))\,\xi^{k}(t)\dot{\gamma}^{i}(t)\dot{\gamma}^{j}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(\gamma(t))\,\dot{\xi}^{i}(t)\dot{\gamma}^{j}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Moreover,

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C},\gamma}(\mathbb{J}_{\gamma}\xi)=i\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C},\gamma}(\xi),

since the coefficients
g_{ij}
are holomorphic and the metric is
complex-bilinear. Thus we have arrived at the infinite-dimensional Picard–Lefschetz problem associated to the action

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}:\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}})\to\mathbb{C}.

Remark 2.14
.

As explained in the introduction, we can rescale the time by
\tau
to normalize it to the unit interval
[0,1]
. Then
\tau
plays the role of
\hbar
, and it is nature to use
\tau\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
for the holomorphic Morse function in order to match our convention in the finite dimensional case. Nevertheless, they lead to the same critical paths and Lefschetz thimbles. In the following discussions, we will use
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
to derive the Morse flow equation and use the critical values of
\tau\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
to identify the Borel singularities.

Let

\gamma:[0,\tau]\to M_{\mathbb{C}},\qquad\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}})

be a critical path of the energy functional
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
. By the standard calculus of variation,
\gamma
satisfies

\ddot{z}^{k}+\sum_{i,j}\Gamma^{k,\mathbb{C}}_{ij}(z)\dot{z}^{i}\dot{z}^{j}=0.

where
z^{i}(t)
represents
\gamma
in local holomorphic coordinates. This is the complex analogue of geodesic equation, and solutions are holomorphic geodesics. Thus critical paths of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
corresponds precisely to holomorphic geodesics from
a
to
b
.

Let us now consider the downward gradient Morse flow associated to
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
. First we choose a hermitian metric
h
on
M_{\mathbb{C}}
. It induces a
L^{2}
hermitian inner product
<\cdot,\cdot>_{\hbar}
on
\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}})
by

<\delta\gamma_{1},\delta\gamma_{2}>_{h}:=\int_{0}^{\tau}h(\delta\gamma_{1},\delta\gamma_{2})dt,\quad\text{for}\quad\delta\gamma_{i}\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{P}_{a,b}(M_{\mathbb{C}}).

Write

h=\sum\limits_{i,j}h_{i\bar{j}}\mathrm{d}z^{i}\mathrm{d}\bar{z}^{\bar{j}}
in a local holomorphic coordinates
z=(z^{1},\ldots,z^{n})
. Then

<\delta\gamma_{1},\delta\gamma_{2}>_{h}=\int_{0}^{\tau}\sum_{i,j}h_{i\bar{j}}(\gamma(t))\delta\gamma_{1}^{i}(t)\overline{\delta\gamma^{j}_{2}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Let
\theta
be a chosen phase. Consider the the downward gradient flow of

\re(e^{-i\theta}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}).

It can be described by a map
\gamma(t,s):[0,\tau]\times[0,+\infty)\to M_{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying the flow equation

\partial_{s}\gamma=-\nabla_{L^{2}}\re(e^{-i\theta}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}})(\gamma),

with the fixed endpoint conditions
\gamma(0,s)=a,\,\gamma(\tau,s)=b
. Here
\nabla_{L^{2}}
is the gradient with respect to the above
L^{2}
-hermitian metric.

In a local holomorphic coordinate
\{z^{i}\}
of
M_{\mathbb{C}}
, we have

g_{\mathbb{C}}=\sum\limits_{i,j}g_{ij}\mathrm{d}z^{i}\mathrm{d}z^{j},\ h=\sum\limits_{i,j}h_{i\bar{j}}\mathrm{d}z^{i}\mathrm{d}\bar{z}^{j}.

We also write the flow locally as
z^{i}(t,s)
. Then the above downward gradient Morse flow is explicitly

\frac{\partial z^{j}}{\partial s}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,m}e^{i\theta}h^{j\bar{k}}\overline{g_{mk}\nabla^{\mathbb{C}}_{\dot{z}}\dot{z}^{m}}.

Here
\dot{z}^{m}=\frac{\partial z^{m}}{\partial t}
and
\nabla^{\mathbb{C}}
is the holomorphic Levi-Civita connection of the holomorphic metric
g_{\mathbb{C}}
. Both
h^{j\bar{k}}
and
g_{mk}
are evaluated at
\gamma(t,s)
.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let

be a smooth solution of the flow equation. Then

(8)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}F_{\theta}\bigl(z(s,\cdot)\bigr)=-\left\lVert\partial_{s}z(s,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{t}}^{2}\leq 0,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}G_{\theta}\bigl(z(s,\cdot)\bigr)=0.

Here
\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{t}}^{2}=<-,->_{h}
. In particular,

Proof.

The first identity follows from the basic property of downward gradient flow. Since
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
is holomorphic on
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{\mathbb{C}}
, the Cauchy–Riemann equations imply that
\nabla G_{\theta(d)}=\mathbb{J}\nabla F_{\theta(d)}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}G_{\theta(d)}(z(s))=

\displaystyle~\mathrm{d}\,G_{\theta(d)}(z(s))(\partial_{s}z)=\re<\nabla G_{\theta(d)},\partial_{s}z>_{h}

More generally, in the complexification

Throughout this subsection, unless explicitly stated otherwise, the variables

and

Definition 2.18
.

Let

is an isomorphism for every

By the holomorphic inverse function theorem, if

If, moreover, the central segment

, if

Definition 2.19

With the above notations. We denote the inverse branch in the second variable by

y=\exp_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\bigl(v_{\gamma}(x,y)\bigr).

The associated holomorphic geodesic branch is defined to be

\gamma_{x,y}(t):=\exp_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\bigl(t\,v_{\gamma}(x,y)\bigr),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1.

Thus

\gamma_{x,y}(0)=x,\qquad\gamma_{x,y}(1)=y,\qquad\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(0)=v_{\gamma}(x,y).

Furthermore, we define its squared complex length to be

\ell_{\gamma}(x,y)^{2}:=\int_{0}^{1}g_{\mathbb{C},\gamma_{x,y}(t)}\bigl(\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

By construction, the integrand is constant
in
t
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}g_{\mathbb{C},\gamma_{x,y}(t)}\bigl(\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t)\bigr)=2g_{\mathbb{C},\gamma_{x,y}(t)}\bigl(\nabla^{\mathbb{C}}_{\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}}\dot{\gamma}_{x,y},\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}\bigr)=0.

Hence

\ell_{\gamma}(x,y)^{2}=g_{\mathbb{C},x}\bigl(v_{\gamma}(x,y),v_{\gamma}(x,y)\bigr).

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
\Omega_{\gamma}
,
v_{\gamma}
be defined as above and let
\Phi_{\gamma}=\frac{\ell_{\gamma}(x,y)^{2}}{4}
, then it satisfies the holomorphic eikonal equation

\Phi_{\gamma}=g_{\mathbb{C}}\bigl(\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma},\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}\bigr)\qquad\text{on }~\Omega_{\gamma}.

Here
\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}
denotes the holomorphic gradient
with respect to
g_{\mathbb{C}}
in the

x
-variable. If
x,y\in M
and
the branch is the real geodesic branch, then

\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}
restricts to the usual real gradient with respect to
g=g_{\mathbb{C}}|_{M}
.

Proof.

Fix
y
, and vary the initial endpoint
x
. Let

x_{\varepsilon}
be a holomorphic curve with

x_{0}=x,\qquad\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{\varepsilon}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=\xi\in T_{x}M_{\mathbb{C}}.

After shrinking the
\varepsilon
-disc, we may assume

(x_{\varepsilon},y)\in\Omega_{\gamma},

then the chosen geodesic branch
v_{\gamma_{x_{\varepsilon},y}}
depends
analytically on
\varepsilon
. Hence there is an analytic
\varepsilon
–family of
geodesics

\gamma_{\varepsilon}:[0,1]\to M_{\mathbb{C}},\qquad\gamma_{\varepsilon}(0)=x_{\varepsilon},\quad\gamma_{\varepsilon}(1)=y.

Set

J(t):=\left.\frac{\partial\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}.

Then

J(0)=\xi,\qquad J(1)=0.

Differentiating the energy

\ell_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon},y)^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}g_{\mathbb{C}}(\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t

at
\varepsilon=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{x}(\ell_{\gamma}^{2})(\xi)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}g_{\mathbb{C}}(\nabla_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}J,\dot{\gamma})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=2\Big[g_{\mathbb{C}}(J,\dot{\gamma})\Big]_{t=0}^{t=1}-2\int_{0}^{1}g_{\mathbb{C}}(J,\nabla_{t}^{\mathbb{C}}\dot{\gamma})\,\mathrm{d}t=-2\,g_{\mathbb{C}}(\xi,\dot{\gamma}(0)).

It follows that

\mathrm{d}_{x}\Phi_{\gamma}(\xi)=-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mathbb{C}}(\xi,\dot{\gamma}(0)),\quad\text{
equivalently,}\quad\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}=-\frac{1}{2}\dot{\gamma}(0).

Thus,

g_{\mathbb{C}}\bigl(\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma},\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}\bigr)=\frac{1}{4}g_{\mathbb{C},x}\bigl(v_{\gamma}(x,y),v_{\gamma}(x,y)\bigr)=\frac{\ell_{\gamma}(x,y)^{2}}{4}=\Phi_{\gamma}(x,y).\qed

Theorem 2.21
.

With the above notations and assumptions, on a no-conjugate geodesic branch chart
\Omega_{\gamma}
, there exists a unique
\mathbb{C}
-valued formal solution
\widetilde{K}_{\gamma}(\tau;x,y)
to the heat equation

(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}})\widetilde{K}_{\gamma}=0

of the form

(9)

\widetilde{K}_{\gamma}(\tau;x,y)=(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-\Phi_{\gamma}(x,y)/\tau}\sum_{j\geq 0}u_{\gamma,j}(x,y)\tau^{j}.

normalized by
u_{\gamma,0}=J_{\gamma}^{-1/2}
where
J_{\gamma}
is the Jacobian of the complex exponential
map along the chosen holomorphic geodesic branch.
Here
\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}
denotes
the holomorphic extension of the non-negative Laplacian in the
x
-variable, and
\sum\limits_{j\geq 0}u_{\gamma,j}(x,y)\tau^{j}
is a formal series in
\tau
.

Proof.

We look for a formal solution of the form

\widetilde{K}_{\gamma}(\tau;x,y)=(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-\Phi_{\gamma}(x,y)/\tau}\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}(\tau;x,y),\qquad\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}(\tau;x,y)=\sum_{j\geq 0}u_{\gamma,j}(x,y)\tau^{j}.

Substituting this expression into the complexified heat operator gives

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}})\widetilde{K}_{\gamma}=(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-\Phi_{\gamma}/\tau}\Bigg[\frac{\Phi_{\gamma}-g_{\mathbb{C}}\bigl(\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma},\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}\bigr)}{\tau^{2}}\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\left(2\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}-(\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma})\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}-\frac{n}{2}\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}+\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}\Bigg].

By Lemma
2.20
, the coefficient of the leading
\tau^{-2}
-order term vanishes. Define the transport operator

T_{\gamma}:=2\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}-\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}-\frac{n}{2}.

Then the remaining expression becomes

(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}})\widetilde{K}_{\gamma}=(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-\Phi_{\gamma}/\tau}\left[\frac{1}{\tau}T_{\gamma}\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}+\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}+\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}\right].

Now substitute

\widetilde{u}_{\gamma}(\tau;x,y)=\sum_{j\geq 0}u_{\gamma,j}(x,y)\tau^{j}.

Comparing powers of
\tau
gives the transport equations

T_{\gamma}u_{\gamma,0}=0,\qquad\left(T_{\gamma}+j\right)u_{\gamma,j}=-\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}u_{\gamma,j-1},~~\text{for}~~j\geq 1.

It remains to see that these equations can be solved locally along the chosen holomorphic geodesic branch. Since the branch is assumed to be no-conjugate, every

y
near the chosen branch can be written uniquely in the form

y=\exp_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}(v(x,y)),\qquad v(x,y)\in T_{x}M_{\mathbb{C}},

with
v(x,y)
depending analytically on
x
and
y
. Near the chosen initial vector and away from the isotropic
cone
g_{\mathbb{C}}(v,v)=0
, choose a branch of the square root and set

r:=\bigl(g_{\mathbb{C}}(v,v)\bigr)^{1/2},\qquad\omega:=\frac{v}{r}.

Then

y=\exp_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}(r\omega).

Here
r
is a complex-valued radial variable and
\omega
lies on the complex unit quadric

Q_{y}^{\mathbb{C}}:=\{\omega\in T_{y}M_{\mathbb{C}}:g_{\mathbb{C}}(\omega,\omega)=1\}.

In these coordinates, we have

\Phi_{\gamma}=\frac{r^{2}}{4},\qquad 2\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}=r\partial_{r}.

Let
J_{\gamma}(r,\omega)
be the reduced Jacobian of the complex exponential
map, defined by

\mathrm{d}\operatorname{vol}_{g_{\mathbb{C}}}=r^{n-1}J_{\gamma}(r,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\omega.

Then, with our convention
\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}=-\operatorname{div}\nabla^{\mathbb{C}}
,

\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Phi_{\gamma}=-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{r}{2}\partial_{r}\log J_{\gamma}.

Therefore

T_{\gamma}=r\partial_{r}+\frac{r}{2}\partial_{r}\log J_{\gamma}.

The leading transport equation becomes

\left(r\partial_{r}+\frac{r}{2}\partial_{r}\log J_{\gamma}\right)u_{\gamma,0}=0.

Hence locally

u_{\gamma,0}(r,\omega)=C_{\gamma}(\omega)J_{\gamma}(r,\omega)^{-1/2},

We fix the normalization by choosing the analytic function
C_{\gamma}(\omega)\equiv 1
.

For
j\geq 1
, the transport equation becomes

\left(r\partial_{r}+\frac{r}{2}\partial_{r}\log J_{\gamma}+j\right)u_{\gamma,j}=-\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}u_{\gamma,j-1}.

Since
J_{\gamma}
is non-vanishing on the chosen branch, this is a first-order
linear ordinary differential equation in the radial variable with analytic
coefficients. Once the normalization of
u_{\gamma,j}
is fixed on a transverse section, it has a unique analytic solution along the branch.

By induction, all coefficients
u_{\gamma,j}
are locally constructed and
are determined by the transport recursion. Therefore the formal expression

\widetilde{K}_{\gamma}(\tau;x,y)=(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-\Phi_{\gamma}(x,y)/\tau}\sum_{j\geq 0}u_{\gamma,j}(x,y)\tau^{j}

satisfies

(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}})\widetilde{K}_{\gamma}=0.\qed

Remark 2.22
.

The natural normalization in Theorem
2.21
agrees with the usual WKB normalization, and we shall call the formal expansion as the WKB-type formal solution or a WKB branch.

To state a converse rigid statement, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.23
.

Let
\Omega_{\gamma}
be a no-conjugate geodesic branch chart, assume that there exist holomorphic functions

\Psi_{\kappa},\ u_{\kappa,j}\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega_{\gamma}),\qquad j\geq 0,

with
u_{\kappa,0}
nowhere vanishing, such that

\widetilde{K}_{\kappa}(\tau;x,y)=\frac{1}{(4\pi\tau)^{n/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y)}{\tau}\right)\sum_{j\geq 0}u_{\kappa,j}(x,y)\tau^{j}

solves

\left(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\widetilde{K}_{\kappa}=0

as a formal series in
\tau
. We say that it is a geometric branch if the complex Lagrangian generated by

\Psi_{\omega}
,

\Lambda_{\Psi_{\kappa}}:=\left\{\bigl(x,\mathrm{d}_{x}\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y);y,-\mathrm{d}_{y}\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y)\bigr):(x,y)\in\Omega_{\gamma}\right\},

is contained in the holomorphic geodesic relation. That is, on the no-conjugate chart
\Omega_{\gamma}
, there exists a holomorphic inverse branch
v_{\gamma}(x,y)
of the endpoint map
E(x,v)=(x,\exp_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}v)
such that

y=\exp_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}v_{\gamma}(x,y),\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2}v_{\gamma}(x,y).

Proposition 2.24
.

Let
\widetilde{K}_{\kappa}
be a geometric branch in Definition
2.23
. Then

\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y)=\frac{\ell_{\gamma}(x,y)^{2}}{4}\qquad\text{on }~\Omega_{\gamma}.

And we can normalize it as Theorem
2.21
.

Proof.

Substituting

\widetilde{K}_{\kappa}=(4\pi\tau)^{-n/2}e^{-\Psi_{\kappa}/\tau}\sum_{j\geq 0}u_{\kappa,j}\tau^{j}

into
(\partial_{\tau}+\Delta_{x}^{\mathbb{C}})\widetilde{K}_{\kappa}=0
and
taking the coefficient of
\tau^{-2}
gives

\left(\Psi_{\kappa}-g_{\mathbb{C}}(\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Psi_{\kappa},\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Psi_{\kappa})\right)u_{\kappa,0}=0.

Since
u_{\kappa,0}
is nowhere vanishing,
\Psi_{\kappa}
satisfies the holomorphic eikonal equation

\Psi_{\kappa}=g_{\mathbb{C}}(\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Psi_{\kappa},\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Psi_{\kappa}).

Combined with the geometric condition, in particular,

\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2}v_{\gamma}(x,y),

we get

\Psi_{\kappa}=g_{\mathbb{C}}(\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Psi_{\kappa},\nabla_{x}^{\mathbb{C}}\Psi_{\kappa})=\frac{1}{4}g_{\mathbb{C},x}(v_{\gamma}(x,y),v_{\gamma}(x,y))=\frac{1}{4}\ell_{\gamma}(x,y)^{2}.\qed

Now we fix
x,y\in M\subset M_{\mathbb{C}}
and fix a minimal real geodesic branch
\gamma_{0}
from
x
to
y
, with phase

\Phi_{0}(x,y)=\frac{\ell_{0}(x,y)^{2}}{4}.

We have proven in Theorem
2.5
that the heat kernel has a
1
–Gevrey formal expansion

\widetilde{K}_{0}(\tau;x,y)=\frac{1}{(4\pi\tau)^{n/2}}e^{-\Phi_{0}(x,y)/\tau}\widetilde{u}_{0}(\tau;x,y),\qquad\widetilde{u}_{0}(\tau;x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}u_{0,k}(x,y)\tau^{k}.

Let

\widehat{u}_{0}(\zeta;x,y)=\sum_{k\geq 0}u_{0,k}(x,y)\frac{\zeta^{k}}{k!}

be the Borel transform of
\widetilde{u}_{0}
.

Theorem 2.25
.

With the above notations, suppose that
\widehat{u}_{0}
admits analytic continuation in the Borel
plane, and suppose that
\zeta=\omega(x,y)
is a non-zero integrable singularity.
After taking a small Hankel contour

\Gamma_{\omega}
around
\zeta=\omega(x,y)
, assume that the expression

\widetilde{K}(\tau;x,y)=\frac{1}{(4\pi\tau)^{n/2}}e^{-\Phi_{0}(x,y)/\tau}\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\Gamma_{\omega}}e^{-\zeta/\tau}\widehat{u}_{0}(\zeta;x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta

has a non-zero geometric branch of the complexified heat equation attached to a holomorphic geodesic
\gamma

\widetilde{K}(\tau;x,y)\sim\frac{1}{(4\pi\tau)^{n/2}}e^{-\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y)/\tau}\sum_{k\geq 0}u_{\kappa,j}(x,y)\tau^{j},

Then

\omega(x,y)=\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y)-\Phi_{0}(x,y)=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(l_{\gamma}^{2}(x,y)-l_{0}^{2}(x,y)\bigr).

Proof.

The exponential factor in the Hankel contribution is

e^{-\Phi_{0}(x,y)/\tau}e^{-\zeta/\tau}.

Since the Hankel contour
\Gamma_{\omega}
is taken around the singularity

\zeta=\omega(x,y)
, write locally
\zeta=\omega(x,y)+\xi
,

e^{-\Phi_{0}(x,y)/\tau}e^{-\zeta/\tau}=e^{-(\Phi_{0}(x,y)+\omega(x,y))/\tau}e^{-\xi/\tau}.

Thus the WKB phase carried by the Hankel contribution is

\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y)=\Phi_{0}(x,y)+\omega(x,y).

By Proposition
2.24
,

\omega(x,y)=\Psi_{\kappa}(x,y)-\Phi_{0}(x,y)=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(l_{\gamma}^{2}(x,y)-l_{0}^{2}(x,y)\bigr).\qed

We next turn to several examples to clarify how the above correspondence between Borel-plane singularities and complexified geodesic branches is realized in concrete situations.

Example 2.26

(The hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
)
.

The exact heat kernel formula on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
is known

K_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}(\tau;a,b)=\frac{\sqrt{2}e^{-\tau/4}}{(4\pi\tau)^{3/2}}\int_{d^{2}/4}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-w/\tau}}{\sqrt{\cosh\sqrt{4w}-\cosh d}}~\mathrm{d}w

which has already the form of Laplace-transform. The Borel coordinate is related to
w
by

\xi=w-d^{2}/4.

The Borel singularities appear at

{\cosh\sqrt{4w}}=\cosh d,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\xi=\frac{(d+2\pi ik)^{2}}{4}-\frac{d^{2}}{4},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

They correspond precisely to the critical values of
\tau\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
relative to the real geodesic
\gamma_{{0},d}

\tau\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}(\gamma_{{k},d})-\tau\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}(\gamma_{{0},d})

as computed in Example
2.17
. A detailed resurgence and Picard–Lefschetz analysis of this example will be given in Section
3
and Section
4
.

For general even-dimensional hyperbolic spaces, explicit Borel summation / analytic continuation statements were analyzed in
[
8
]
. A resurgence analysis of Borel singularities of hyperbolic metric on Riemann surfaces, within the Picard–Lefschetz framework, is also studied in
[
45
]
.

Example 2.27

(The sphere
S^{2}
,
0<d(x,y)<\pi
)
.

The heat kernel of
S^{2}
is radial, and has the spectral expansion

K_{S^{2}}(\tau;r)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)e^{-\tau\ell(\ell+1)}P_{\ell}(\cos r),\qquad r:=d_{S^{2}}(x,y)\in(0,\pi).

To read off the Borel-plane singularities, it is more transparent to use the following Abel–Poisson integral representation:

K_{S^{2}}(\tau;r)=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,e^{\tau/4}}{(4\pi\tau)^{3/2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{k}\int_{r}^{\pi}\frac{(\phi+2\pi k)\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\phi+2\pi k)^{2}}{4\tau}\right]}{\sqrt{\cos r-\cos\phi}}\,\mathrm{d}\phi.

Indeed, the exponential weights in the Abel–Poisson formula are of the form

\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\phi+2\pi k)^{2}}{4\tau}\right].

Near the lower endpoint
\phi=r
, the
k
-th summand has action

S_{k}(r)=\frac{(r+2\pi k)^{2}}{4}.

Thus the shortest geodesic sector corresponds to
k=0
, and the other terms correspond to the other geodesic branches on the same great circle, with lengths

\ell_{k}=|r+2\pi k|,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Therefore the action difference between the
k
-th geodesic branch and the shortest one is

\omega_{k}(r)=S_{k}(r)-S_{0}(r)=\frac{(r+2\pi k)^{2}-r^{2}}{4}=\pi kr+\pi^{2}k^{2}.

These are precisely the expected locations of the Borel singularities of the perturbative sector associated with the shortest geodesic.

Remark 2.28
.

Unlike the affine complexification of
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the affine complexification of
S^{2}
does not produce additional holomorphic geodesics between real endpoints. The branches indexed by
k
are simply the real winding geodesics on
S^{2}
, so this example records the real multi-geodesic contribution rather than a new complexified one.

Remark 2.29
.

The restriction
0<r<\pi
avoids the diagonal
r=0
and the cut locus
r=\pi
. At
r=0
, closed geodesic degeneracies occur and the explicit computation relating geodesic information to Borel singularities will be computed in Example
2.30
. At
r=\pi
, the shortest geodesic is no longer isolated, since
x
and
y
are antipodal.

Example 2.30

(The sphere
S^{2}
,
d(x,y)=0
)
.

For
r=0
, we can compute explicitly as follows. The heat trace of
S^{2}
is known by

Z_{S^{2}}(\tau):=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\tau\Delta_{S^{2}}})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)e^{-\ell(\ell+1)\tau}.

It is convenient to work with the shifted heat trace

\Phi_{S^{2}}(\tau):=e^{-\tau/4}Z_{S^{2}}(\tau)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}2\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right)e^{-(\ell+\frac{1}{2})^{2}\tau}.

Set
f_{\tau}(x):=2xe^{-\tau x^{2}}
, then

\Phi_{S^{2}}(\tau)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}f_{\tau}\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right).

We apply the midpoint Euler–Maclaurin formula

\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}f\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right)\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{B_{2m}(\frac{1}{2})}{(2m)!}f^{(2m-1)}(0),

where
B_{2m}(x)
denotes the Bernoulli polynomial. In the present case,

\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\tau}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\tau},\qquad f_{\tau}^{(2m-1)}(0)=2\frac{(-\tau)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}(2m-1)!.

Substituting this into the Euler–Maclaurin formula gives

\Phi_{S^{2}}(\tau)\sim\frac{1}{\tau}+\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{m}B_{2m}(\frac{1}{2})}{m!}\tau^{m-1}=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(1+\frac{(-1)^{m}B_{2m}(\frac{1}{2})}{m!}\tau^{m}\right).

Set
\phi_{S^{2}}(\tau):=\tau\Phi_{S^{2}}(\tau)
, then

\widehat{\phi}_{S^{2}}(\xi)=1+\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{m}B_{2m}(\frac{1}{2})}{(m!)^{2}}\xi^{m}.

Using the identities of Bernoulli polynomials

B_{2m}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\left(2^{1-2m}-1\right)B_{2m},\qquad B_{2m}=(-1)^{m+1}\frac{2(2m)!}{(2\pi)^{2m}}\zeta(2m).

and the formula of the Dirichlet eta function

\eta(s):=(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k^{s}},

we obtain

\widehat{\phi}_{S^{2}}(\xi)=1+2\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\binom{2m}{m}}{4^{m}}\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{(k^{2}\pi^{2})^{m}}\right)\xi^{m}.

Interchanging the two sums for
|\xi|<\pi^{2}
,
gives

\widehat{\phi}_{S^{2}}(\xi)=1+2\sum_{k\geq 1}(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\binom{2m}{m}}{4^{m}}\left(\frac{\xi}{k^{2}\pi^{2}}\right)^{m}.

Using the generating function

\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\binom{2m}{m}}{4^{m}}x^{m}=(1-x)^{-1/2},

we arrive at the explicit Borel expression

\widehat{\phi}_{S^{2}}(\xi)=1+2\sum_{k\geq 1}(-1)^{k-1}\left[\left(1-\frac{\xi}{k^{2}\pi^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}-1\right].

It shows that
\widehat{\phi}_{S^{2}}(\xi)
has algebraic branch points at
k^{2}\pi^{2}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Furthermore,

\operatorname{Sing}\bigl(\widehat{\phi}_{S^{2}}\bigr)=\{\,k^{2}\pi^{2}:\ k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\,\}.

With the Laplace kernel
e^{-\xi/\tau}
, the
corresponding exponentially small terms have the form

e^{-k^{2}\pi^{2}/\tau}=e^{-(2k\pi)^{2}/(4\tau)}.

where
(2k\pi)^{2}/(4\tau)
is the geodesic action associated with the
k
-fold iterate of a
closed geodesic of length
2\pi
on the unit sphere. Thus in the Borel plane, we can see the correspondence

\text{closed geodesic length }2\pi k\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{Borel singularity }\xi=k^{2}\pi^{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e^{-k^{2}\pi^{2}/\tau}.

Remark 2.31
.

For the compact rank-one symmetric spaces, the method relating heat-kernel
exponential sectors to Borel singularities is similar to the above on
S^{2}
, i.e the basic action-difference formula for the singularity locations. The difference may appear in the amplitudes and in the local type of the Borel singularities, such as the transverse Jacobi field mulitiplicities and the Stokes coefficients.

Example 2.32
.

The higher-rank analogue of the hyperbolic plane

SL(2,\mathbb{R})/SO(2,\mathbb{R})
is

M_{n}=SL(n,\mathbb{R})/SO(n,\mathbb{R}).

We use the standard real model

M_{n}\cong\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{R}}=\{Q\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R}):Q>0,\ \det Q=1\},\qquad gSO(n,\mathbb{R})\longmapsto gg^{T}.

Its affine complexification is

SL(n,\mathbb{C})/SO(n,\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}:=\{Q\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{C}):\det Q=1\}.

Let

\mathfrak{a}=\left\{H=\operatorname{diag}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}):\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{j}=0\right\}.

Up to the Weyl group, a point of
M_{n}
may be written as

Q=\exp(2H_{0}),\qquad H_{0}\in\mathfrak{a}^{+}.

With the normalization

\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(AB),\qquad A,B\in\mathfrak{a},

the principal real geodesic from
I
to
\exp(2H_{0})
is

Q_{0}(t)=\exp(2tH_{0}),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,

and its squared length is, up to the fixed global normalization of the metric,

L_{0}^{2}=\operatorname{tr}(H_{0}^{2}).

The affine complexification remembers the multi-valuedness of the matrix
logarithm. If

m=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}=0,

then

Q_{m}(t)=\exp(2tH_{m}),\qquad H_{m}:=H_{0}+\pi i\,\operatorname{diag}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}),\quad 0\leq t\leq 1,

is a holomorphic geodesic branch joining the same endpoints in

\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
. Its complex squared length is

L_{m}^{2}=\operatorname{tr}(H_{m}^{2}).

Consequently the affine model contains the full lattice of holomorphic geodesic
actions

\left\{\frac{1}{4}\,\operatorname{tr}(H_{m}^{2}):m\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\ \sum_{j}m_{j}=0\right\}.

Relative to the principal real branch, the corresponding action
differences are

\omega_{m}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\operatorname{tr}(H_{m}^{2})-\operatorname{tr}(H_{0}^{2})\right).

The same lattice is visible from the heat kernel. Let
K_{\tau}(Q)
be the
heat kernel based at the identity, and write its radial part as

K_{\tau}(\exp(2H))
. For a noncompact symmetric space
G/K
, the Abel
transform sends the radial heat equation to a Euclidean heat equation on

\mathfrak{a}
, with the usual
\rho
-shift:

\mathfrak{H}\widetilde{K}_{0}=0,\qquad\mathfrak{H}:=\hbar^{2}\partial_{\hbar}-\hbar^{2}\left(\partial_{d}^{2}+\coth d\,\partial_{d}\right).

By Lemma
2.34
,

\dot{\Delta}_{\omega_{k}(d)}^{+}
commutes with both
\hbar^{2}\partial_{\hbar}
and
\partial_{d}
. Therefore

\mathfrak{H}\left(\dot{\Delta}_{\omega_{k}(d)}^{+}\widetilde{K}_{0}\right)=0.

Thus the pointed alien contribution is again a WKB-type formal solution of
the radial heat equation, now with phase
\Phi_{k}(d)
.

Moreover, after dividing by
2
, its reduced formal power series is obtained
from the local germ
\widehat{\phi}_{d,k}
. After extracting the universal
factor

\left(\frac{\ell_{k}}{2\sinh\ell_{k}}\right)^{1/2}\zeta^{-1/2},

the remaining Taylor coefficients are regular at
\ell=0
. Hence this
formal solution satisfies the same regularity normalization used to define

\widetilde{K}_{k}
.

The phase, the one-loop leading factor, and the regularity condition at

\ell=0
uniquely determine the WKB-type formal solution attached to

\gamma_{k}
. Comparing with
(
20
) proves

\dot{\Delta}_{\omega_{k}(d)}^{+}\,\widetilde{K}_{0}=2\,\widetilde{K}_{k}.\qed

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
j,m\in\mathbb{Z}
with
j\neq m
, and set

\omega_{j\to m}(d):=\Phi_{m}(d)-\Phi_{j}(d).

Then

(21)

\dot{\Delta}_{\omega_{j\to m}(d)}^{+}\,\widetilde{K}_{j}=2\,\widetilde{K}_{m}.

Thus every direct pointed alien transition between two distinct complex
geodesic branches carries the same Stokes constant
2
.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{\phi}_{d,j}
be the reduced Borel germ associated with

\widetilde{K}_{j}
. We use Proposition
3.3
after re-centering the
construction at the branch
\gamma_{j}
. Then the branch
\gamma_{m}
appears at the Borel-plane
displacement

\omega_{j\to m}(d)=\Phi_{m}(d)-\Phi_{j}(d).

The radial heat equation, the transport recursion, and the normalization

A_{0}=1
are unchanged under this re-centering. Therefore
Proposition
3.3
gives

\tau_{-\omega_{j\to m}(d)}\operatorname{var}^{+}_{\omega_{j\to m}(d)}\widehat{\phi}_{d,j}=2\,\widehat{\phi}_{d,m}.

In particular, the singularity at
\omega_{j\to m}(d)
is of square-root type by
Proposition
3.3
, with
\gamma_{j}
as the reference branch.

After inverse Borel transform and insertion of the pointed factor, the exponential weight changes from
e^{-\Phi_{j}(d)/\hbar}
to

e^{-\Phi_{m}(d)/\hbar}
.
Now the translated reduced germ is
2\widehat{\phi}_{d,m}
, and the one-loop
factor and the transport normalization are the same as those used in the
definition of
\widetilde{K}_{m}
. Hence

\dot{\Delta}^{+}_{\omega_{j\to m}(d)}\widetilde{K}_{j}=2\,\widetilde{K}_{m}.\qed

The coefficient
2
is the resurgent quantity that will later be compared
with the connecting-trajectory count for the corresponding pair of critical
paths in the complexified path-space Morse theory.

4.
Picard–Lefschetz analysis

In this section, we will study the
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
model from Picard-Leschetz side on the complexified path space. Let us first recall necessary notations from Example
2.17
. Let

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:=\bigl\{z=(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}:\ -z_{0}^{2}+z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}=-1\bigr\},\qquad\eta:=\diag(-1,1,1).

After a real Lorentz isometry, we normalize the two real endpoints as

a=(1,0,0),\qquad b_{d}=(\cosh d,\sinh d,0).

We consider the endpoint-fixed Sobolev path space

\mathcal{P}_{d}^{\mathbb{C}}:=\mathcal{P}_{a,b_{d}}(\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})=\bigl\{z\in H^{1}([0,\tau],\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})\mid z(0)=a,\ z(\tau)=b_{d}\bigr\}

equipped with the action by the holomorphic energy functional

S(z)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}z_{t}^{T}\eta z_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle z_{t},z_{t}\rangle_{\eta}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Remark 4.1
.

The factor
1/2
is the traditional normalization for the energy. In Section
2.4
, we have used the factor
1/4
to define
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
so that the exponential
\exp(-\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}})
leads to the heat kernel. Since

S=2\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}},

they give rise to equivalent Morse flows. We choose to work with
S
instead in this section for Picard–Lefschetz and Morse flow analysis. It simplifies expressions in various computations.

Let
\theta
be a chosen phase. Define the twisted real part and imaginary part of
S
by

\displaystyle F_{\theta}(z)=

\displaystyle\re\bigl(e^{-i\theta}S(z)\bigr),\qquad\quad G_{\theta}(z)=\im\bigl(e^{-i\theta}S(z)\bigr).

The downward Morse flow system of
F_{\theta}
is computed in Example
2.17
and given by

(22)

\begin{cases}\partial_{s}z=P_{z}(e^{i\theta}\eta\bar{z}_{tt})=e^{i\theta}\left(\eta\bar{z}_{tt}-\frac{z^{T}\bar{z}_{tt}}{|z|^{2}}\eta\bar{z}\right)\\[2.84544pt]
\langle z(s,t),z(s,t)\rangle_{\eta}=z(s,t)^{T}\eta z(s,t)=-1\\[2.84544pt]
z(s,0)=a,~z(s,\tau)=b_{d}\end{cases}.

4.1.
The holomorphic geodesics and the complex Jacobi operator

For each
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, set

c_{j,d}:=\frac{d+2\pi ij}{\tau}.

As shown in Example
2.17
, for each
j
, there corresponds a holomorphic geodesics joining
a
to
b_{d}
by

(23)

\gamma_{j,d}(t)=\bigl(\cosh(c_{j,d}t),\,\sinh(c_{j,d}t),\,0\bigr),\qquad 0\leq t\leq\tau.

Moreover,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\,\gamma_{j,d}=c_{j,d}^{2}\gamma_{j,d}.

Along
\gamma_{j,d}
, introduce the tangent and normal fields

T_{j,d}(t):=\frac{1}{c_{j,d}}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\gamma_{j,d}(t)=\bigl(\sinh(c_{j,d}t),\,\cosh(c_{j,d}t),\,0\bigr),\qquad N:=(0,0,1).

We have

\left\langle\gamma_{j,d},T_{j,d}\right\rangle_{\eta}=0,\qquad\left\langle\gamma_{j,d},N\right\rangle_{\eta}=0,\qquad\left\langle T_{j,d},T_{j,d}\right\rangle_{\eta}=1,\qquad\left\langle N,N\right\rangle_{\eta}=1.

Therefore every tangent variation along
\gamma_{j,d}
can be written as

\xi(t)=\alpha(t)T_{j,d}(t)+\beta(t)N,\qquad\alpha(0)=\alpha(\tau)=\beta(0)=\beta(\tau)=0.

Proposition 4.2
.

The
\mathbb{C}
-linear second-variation operator of the holomorphic action at
\gamma_{j,d}
is

L_{j,d}^{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha,\beta)=\bigl(-\alpha_{tt},\,-\beta_{tt}+c_{j,d}^{2}\beta\bigr)

on the Dirichlet domain

\mathrm{Dom}(L_{j,d}^{\mathbb{C}})=\bigl(H^{2}\cap H^{1}_{0}([0,\tau],\mathbb{C})\bigr)\oplus\bigl(H^{2}\cap H^{1}_{0}([0,\tau],\mathbb{C})\bigr).

Equivalently,

L_{j,d}^{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha T_{j,d}+\beta N)=(-\alpha_{tt})T_{j,d}+(-\beta_{tt}+c_{j,d}^{2}\beta)N.

Furthermore, for
d>0
,
\ker L_{j,d}^{\mathbb{C}}=\{0\}
, hence every critical path
\gamma_{j,d}
is nondegenerate.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary endpoint-fixed variation of
\gamma_{j,d}
parametrized by small
\varepsilon

u(\varepsilon,t)\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2},\qquad u(0,t)=\gamma_{j,d}(t).

Let

\xi(t)=\partial_{\varepsilon}u(0,t),\qquad\zeta(t)=\partial_{\varepsilon}^{2}u(0,t)

be the first and second order variations, which satisfy the boundary condition

\xi(0)=\xi(\tau)=0,\qquad\zeta(0)=\zeta(\tau)=0.

Then we can compute

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon^{2}}S(u(\varepsilon))\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=~

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(\langle\xi_{t},\xi_{t}\rangle_{\eta}+\langle(\gamma_{j,d})_{t},\zeta_{t}\rangle_{\eta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle\xi_{t},\xi_{t}\rangle_{\eta}\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle(\gamma_{i,d})_{tt},\zeta\rangle_{\eta}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle\xi_{t},\xi_{t}\rangle_{\eta}\mathrm{d}t-c_{j,d}^{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle\gamma_{j,d},\zeta\rangle_{\eta}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}-c_{j,d}^{2}\alpha^{2})\ \mathrm{d}t-c_{j,d}^{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle\gamma_{j,d},\zeta\rangle_{\eta}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
u(\varepsilon,t)\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
,

\langle u(\varepsilon,t),u(\varepsilon,t)\rangle_{\eta}=-1.

Taking the derivatives with respect to
\varepsilon
twice, we obtain

\langle\gamma_{j,d},\zeta\rangle_{\eta}+\langle\xi,\xi\rangle_{\eta}=0.

Thus for
\xi(t)=\alpha(t)T_{j,d}(t)+\beta(t)N
, we arrive at

\delta^{2}S_{\gamma_{j,d}}(\xi,\xi)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}+c_{j,d}^{2}\beta^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\tau}[\alpha(-\alpha_{tt})+\beta(-\beta_{tt}+c_{j,d}^{2}\beta)]\,\mathrm{d}t.

This shows the formula for
L_{j,d}^{\mathbb{C}}
.

We next show
\ker L_{j,d}^{\mathbb{C}}=\{0\}
. The tangential equation
-\alpha_{tt}=0
with Dirichlet boundary conditions has only the trivial solution. Solutions of the normal equation
\beta_{tt}=c_{j,d}^{2}\beta

take the form

\beta(t)=c_{1}e^{c_{j,d}t}+c_{2}e^{-c_{j,d}t}.

Imposing
\beta(0)=0
gives
c_{2}=-c_{1}
, hence

\beta(t)=2c_{1}\sinh(c_{j,d}t).

Now
\beta(\tau)=0
implies

2c_{1}\sinh(c_{j,d}\tau)=2c_{1}\sinh(d+2\pi ij)=(-1)^{j}\times 2c_{1}\sinh d=0.

Since
d>0
,
\sinh d\neq 0
, we get
c_{1}=0
, that is,
\beta\equiv 0
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The normal Dirichlet spectra of
L_{j,d}^{\mathbb{C}}
are

\left(\frac{n\pi}{\tau}\right)^{2}+c_{j,d}^{2},\quad n\geq 1.

At
d=0
, the normal block becomes degenerate exactly when

\Bigl(\frac{n\pi}{\tau}\Bigr)^{2}+\Bigl(\frac{2\pi ij}{\tau}\Bigr)^{2}=0,

that is, when
n=2|j|
. In this case

\gamma_{j,d}(t)=\bigl(\cos(\frac{jt}{\tau}),\,i\sin(\frac{jt}{\tau}),\,0\bigr)

lies on the sphere
S^{2}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, and this is the familiar Morse–Bott phenomenon on
S^{2}
. This observation will play an essential role in our perturbation analysis in Section
4.6
.

4.2.
Linearized flow equation

In this subsection, we will analyze linearization of the downward gradient flow equation of
F_{\theta}
at the critical path
\gamma_{j,d}
, which plays an important role in the study of local structure of the Lefschetz thimble in the following subsections.

Proposition 4.4
.

At the critical path
\gamma_{j,d}
, the linearization of the flow equation (
22
) is

(24)

\partial_{s}\xi+A_{j,d,\theta}\xi=0,

with

A_{j,d,\theta}:=e^{i\theta}\,\mathcal{C}\,M_{\gamma_{j,d}}^{-1}\,L_{j,d}^{\mathbb{C}},\qquad M_{\gamma_{j,d}}=\begin{pmatrix}|\gamma_{j,d}(t)|^{2}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Here
\mathcal{C}
denotes coefficientwise complex conjugation in the frame
(T_{j,d},N)
. In particular, for
d>0

\ker A_{j,d,\theta}=\ker L_{j,d}^{\mathbb{C}}=0.

Proof.

Set

V(z):=P(\eta\bar{z}_{tt})=\eta\bar{z}_{tt}-\lambda(z)\eta\bar{z},\qquad\lambda(z):=\frac{z^{T}\bar{z}_{tt}}{|z|^{2}}.

To simplify notations, we also denote

\gamma(t)=\gamma_{j,d}(t),\quad~c=c_{j,d},\quad~T=T_{j,d}(t),\quad~\xi=\alpha T+\beta N.

Linearizing (
22
) at
\gamma=\gamma_{j,d}
along
\xi
, we obtain

DV_{\gamma}(\xi)=\eta\,\bar{\xi}_{tt}-D\lambda_{\gamma}(\xi)\eta\,\bar{\gamma}-\bar{c}^{2}\,\eta\,\bar{\xi}.

Using
\gamma_{tt}=c^{2}\gamma
, we compute
D\lambda_{\gamma}(\xi)
as follows:

\displaystyle D\lambda_{\gamma}(\xi)=~

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{T}\bar{\xi}_{tt}+\bar{c}^{2}\,\xi^{T}\bar{\gamma}}{|\gamma|^{2}}-\frac{\bar{c}^{2}(\xi^{T}\bar{\gamma}+\gamma^{T}\bar{\xi})}{|\gamma|^{2}}=~\frac{\gamma^{T}\bar{\xi}_{tt}-\bar{c}^{2}\,\gamma^{T}\,\bar{\xi}}{|\gamma|^{2}}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{T}(\bar{\alpha}_{tt}+\bar{c}^{2}\bar{\alpha})\bar{T}+2\gamma^{T}\bar{c}\bar{\alpha}_{t}\bar{\gamma}+\gamma^{T}\bar{\beta}_{tt}N-\bar{c}^{2}\bar{\alpha}\,\gamma^{T}\,\bar{T}}{|\gamma|^{2}}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{T}\bar{T}}{|\gamma|^{2}}\bar{\alpha}_{tt}+2\bar{c}\,\bar{\alpha}_{t}.

Using the identity

\cosh^{2}(ct)-\sinh^{2}(ct)=1,

we can compute

\eta\bar{T}-\frac{\gamma^{T}\bar{T}}{|\gamma|^{2}}\eta\bar{\gamma}=\frac{1}{|\gamma|^{2}}(\sinh(ct),\cosh(ct),0)=\frac{1}{|\gamma|^{2}}T.

Substituting these results into
DV_{\gamma}(\xi)
,
we obtain

DV_{\gamma}(\xi)=\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{tt}}{|\gamma|^{2}}T+\left(\bar{\beta}_{tt}-\bar{c}^{2}\bar{\beta}\right)N.\qed

The following standard notion of exponential dichotomy for a linear evolution equation and the Lyapunov–Perron stable-manifold theorem for semilinear evolution equations with an exponential dichotomy are useful in our later discussion. We state them as follows. For more details, see
[
18
,
31
,
38
,
41
]
.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
A:D(A)\subset H\to H
be a
\mathbb{R}
-linear closed operator on a real
Hilbert space
H
. We say that the linear equation

\partial_{s}\xi+A\xi=0

has an exponential dichotomy with splitting

H=E^{s}\oplus E^{u}

and gap
\mu_{*}>0
if there exist bounded projections

\Pi^{s}:H\to E^{s},\qquad\Pi^{u}:H\to E^{u},\qquad\Pi^{s}+\Pi^{u}=\mathrm{Id},

such that
E^{s}
and
E^{u}
are invariant for the linear flow, and the
following estimates hold:

\|e^{-sA}\Pi^{s}\|_{H\to H}\leq Ce^{-\mu_{*}s},\qquad s\geq 0,

and

\|e^{sA}\Pi^{u}\|_{H\to H}\leq Ce^{-\mu_{*}s},\qquad s\geq 0.

Here
e^{-sA}\Pi^{s}
denotes the forward evolution on the stable subspace,
whereas
e^{sA}\Pi^{u}
denotes the backward evolution on the unstable
subspace.

Lemma 4.6

(
[
18
,
38
]
)
.

Let
A
be a
\mathbb{R}
-linear closed operator on a Hilbert space
H
, with
domain continuously embedded in a stronger space
H^{1}
, and assume that
the linear equation

\partial_{s}\xi+A\xi=0

admits an exponential dichotomy as in Definition
4.5
. Consider

(25)

\partial_{s}\xi+A\xi=N(\xi),\qquad N(0)=0,\qquad DN(0)=0,

where
N
is
C^{1}
from a small
H^{1}
-ball to
H
, and satisfies

\|N(\xi_{1})-N(\xi_{2})\|_{H}\leq C_{N}\bigl(\|\xi_{1}\|_{H^{1}}+\|\xi_{2}\|_{H^{1}}\bigr)\|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\|_{H^{1}}.

Then every solution of (
25
) satisfying

\lim_{s\to+\infty}\|\xi(s)\|_{H^{1}}=0

actually converges exponentially: for every
0<\mu<\mu_{*}
, after increasing

s_{0}
, there exists
C_{\mu}>0
such that

\|\xi(s)\|_{H^{1}}\leq C_{\mu}e^{-\mu(s-s_{0})}\qquad(s\geq s_{0}).

Moreover the small initial data of all such decaying
half-trajectories form a
C^{1}
graph over
E^{s}
, tangent to
E^{s}
at
the origin.

4.3.
Lefschetz thimble for generic
\theta

We now describe Lefschetz thimbles of the energy functional on
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{\mathbb{C}}
for generic phase
\theta
to prepare for the Picard–Lefschetz analysis.

Definition 4.7
.

Fix a critical path
\gamma_{j,d}
and a phase
\theta
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{j}^{\theta,+}
be the set of maps

z:[0,+\infty)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{d}^{\mathbb{C}}

such that, in any local chart along the image,

z\in C^{0}([0,+\infty);H^{1}_{t})\cap C^{1}((0,+\infty);L^{2}_{t})\cap C^{0}((0,+\infty);H^{2}_{t}\cap H^{1}_{0,t}),

and
z
satisfies

(26)

\begin{cases}\partial_{s}z=P_{z}\bigl(e^{i\theta}\eta\,\bar{z}_{tt}\bigr)\quad\text{ in\, }L^{2}([0,\tau])~\text{ for every }s>0,\\[2.84544pt]
z(s,0)=a,\qquad z(s,\tau)=b_{d},\\[2.84544pt]
\lim\limits_{s\to+\infty}\left\lVert z(s,\cdot)-\gamma_{j,d}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{H^{1}([0,\tau])}=0,\\[2.84544pt]
\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left\lVert\partial_{s}z(s,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{t}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty.\end{cases}

The Lefschetz thimble attached to
\gamma_{j,d}
is

\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\theta}:=\mathrm{ev}_{0}(\mathcal{M}_{j}^{\theta,+})=\{z(0,\cdot):z\in\mathcal{M}_{j}^{+}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{d}^{\mathbb{C}}.

Remark 4.8
.

One can also formulate the half-cylinder problem first in a weak class with

z\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);H^{1}_{t})\cap H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);L^{2}_{t}).

The equation is tested against compactly supported tangent test fields, together with the same endpoint condition, finite-energy condition, and strong
H^{1}_{t}
asymptotic condition. Whenever the image stays in a compact subset of
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, the local coordinate systems used later in the no-escape analysis provide the required bootstrap on every finite strip, so such a weak solution upgrades to the strong class above. Therefore, in the following we will work directly with strong solutions and strong
H^{1}_{t}
asymptotics.

From Definition
4.7
, we have

•

For every
z_{0}\in\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\theta}
,

G_{\theta}(z_{0})=G_{\theta}(\gamma_{j,d}),\qquad F_{\theta}(z_{0})\geq F_{\theta}(\gamma_{j,d});

•

We impose the genericity assumption that the twisted phase separates the imaginary parts of the critical values:

G_{\theta}(\gamma_{j,d})\neq G_{\theta}(\gamma_{k,d}),\qquad\text{whenever}~j\neq k.

With this genericity assumption, for any
j\neq k
, there is no finite-energy solution

z:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\tau]\rightarrow\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

of (
22
) such that

\lim_{s\rightarrow-\infty}\left\lVert z(s,\cdot)-\gamma_{j,d}\right\rVert_{H^{1}([0,\tau])}=0,\qquad\lim_{s\rightarrow+\infty}\left\lVert z(s,\cdot)-\gamma_{k,d}\right\rVert_{H^{1}([0,\tau])}=0.

It follows that the thimbles
\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\theta}
are pairwise disjoint, and each critical path determines exactly one thimble.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
d>0
, and let
z\in\mathcal{M}_{j}^{\theta,+}
. Then there exist constants
C,\mu>0
such that

\left\lVert z(s,\cdot)-\gamma_{j,d}\right\rVert_{H^{1}_{t}}\leq Ce^{-\mu s}\qquad s\gg 0.

Moreover, one can take
\mu
smaller than

\mu_{j,d,*}:=\min\left\{\inf_{n}\phi^{\tan}_{n,j,d},\,\inf_{n}|\phi^{\mathrm{nor}}_{n,j,d}|\right\},

where
\phi^{\tan}_{n,j,d}
are the positive eigenvalues of

-\alpha_{tt}=\phi\,|\gamma_{j,d}(t)|^{2}\alpha,\qquad\alpha(0)=\alpha(\tau)=0,

and

\phi^{\mathrm{nor}}_{n,j,d}=\left(\frac{n\pi}{\tau}\right)^{2}+c_{j,d}^{2}.

Proof.

Choose exponential coordinates near
\gamma_{j,d}
and write

z(s,\cdot)=\exp_{\gamma_{j,d}}^{\mathbb{C}}\xi(s,\cdot).

Since
z(s,\cdot)\to\gamma_{j,d}
in
H^{1}_{t}
, for all sufficiently large

s
the trajectory lies in this chart. In these coordinates the equation
has the form

\partial_{s}\xi+A_{j,d,\theta}\xi=N_{\theta}(\xi),\qquad N_{\theta}(0)=0,\qquad DN_{\theta}(0)=0,

where
A_{j,d,\theta}
is from Proposition
4.4
, and
N_{\theta}
satisfies the standard quadratic estimate in
H^{1}_{t}
.

Set

B_{j,d}=M_{\gamma_{j,d}}^{-1}L^{\mathbb{C}}_{j,d},\qquad B_{j,d}^{\#}=\mathcal{C}B_{j,d}\mathcal{C}.

Then as
\mathbb{R}
-linear operators,

A_{j,d,\theta}^{2}=B_{j,d}^{\#}B_{j,d},

and in particular, the real spectrum of
A_{j,d,\theta}
is obtained from the complex spectrum of
B_{j,d}
as follows:

\phi\in\sigma_{\mathbb{C}}(B_{j,d})~~\Rightarrow~~\pm|\phi|\in\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{j,d,\theta}).

•

The tangential eigenvalues of
B_{j,d}
are the eigenvalues

\phi^{\mathrm{tan}}_{n,j,d}
of the weighted Dirichlet problem

-\alpha_{tt}(t)=\phi^{\mathrm{tan}}_{n,j,d}|\gamma_{j,d}(t)|^{2}\alpha(t)=\phi_{n,j,d}^{\tan}\cosh\left(\frac{2dt}{\tan}\right)\alpha(t),\qquad\alpha(0)=\alpha(\tau)=0.

They are independent of
j
and positive. In particular, the minimal eigenvalue satisfies

\phi_{1,d}^{\tan}:=\phi_{1,j,d}^{\tau}\geq\frac{1}{\cosh(2d)}\left(\frac{\pi}{\tau}\right)^{2}>0.

•

The normal eigenvalues are

\phi^{\mathrm{nor}}_{n,j,d}=\left(\frac{n\pi}{\tau}\right)^{2}+c_{j,d}^{2},\qquad n\geq 1.

In particular,
|\phi^{\mathrm{nor}}_{n,j,d}|\rightarrow+\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. By Proposition
4.2
, none of the normal eigenvalues vanishes when
d>0
.

Then

\operatorname{dist}\bigl(0,\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{j,d,\theta})\bigr)\geq\mu_{j,d,*}:=\min\left\{\inf_{n\geq 1}\phi^{\mathrm{tan}}_{n,j,d},\inf_{n\geq 1}\left|\phi^{\mathrm{nor}}_{n,j,d}\right|\right\}>0.

Thus
A_{j,d,\theta}
, regarded as an operator on the underlying real
Hilbert space, has an exponential dichotomy with gap bounded from
below by any number smaller than
\mu_{j,d,*}
.

By Lemma
4.6
, for every

0<\mu<\mu_{j,d,*}
, after increasing
s_{0}
there exists

C_{\mu}>0
such that

\|\xi(s,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}_{t}}\leq C_{\mu}e^{-\mu(s-s_{0})}\qquad(s\geq s_{0}).

Finally, the equation and elliptic estimates for the
\mathbb{R}
-linear elliptic
operator
A_{j,d,\theta}
bootstrap the decay to higher Sobolev norms on the end. Since the
exponential chart is smooth and has identity differential at the origin, the
same
H^{1}_{t}
-estimate holds for

z(s,\cdot)-\gamma_{j,d}
.
∎

Immediately, we have the local graph result for the thimble germ.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
d>0
. There exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\gamma_{j,d}
in

\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{C}}_{d}
, a real Hilbert splitting

T_{\gamma_{j,d}}\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{C}}_{d}=E^{s}_{j,d,\theta}\oplus E^{u}_{j,d,\theta},

where
E^{s}_{j,d,\theta}
(or
E^{u}_{j,d,\theta}
respectively) is the Hilbert direct sum of the decaying (or growing respectively) real
lines of the scalar mode equations, and a
C^{1}
map

\Psi_{j,d,\theta}:B_{\rho}(E^{s}_{j,d,\theta})\longrightarrow E^{u}_{j,d,\theta},\qquad\Psi_{j,d,\theta}(0)=0,\qquad D\Psi_{j,d,\theta}(0)=0,

such that

\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\theta}\cap\mathcal{U}=\left\{\exp_{\gamma_{j,d}}^{\mathbb{C}}\bigl(\xi^{s}+\Psi_{j,d,\theta}(\xi^{s})\bigr):\xi^{s}\in B_{\rho}(E^{s}_{j,d,\theta})\right\}.

where

Remark 4.11

We can regard this local graph description as the infinite-dimensional analogue

Its spectral decomposition plays the role of

4.4.2.

Now the linearization of the full

By the local stable-manifold statement as above, the local

4.6.
Case:

In this subsection we prove the

The argument proceeds in two steps. First, we study the equation on a flow-invariant slice

4.6.1.

S^{2}
has a unique smooth solution on
[s_{0},+\infty)
. The extended solution has finite energy:

\int_{s_{0}}^{+\infty}\|x_{s}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{t}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s=E_{S^{2}}(x(s_{0},\cdot))-\lim_{s\to+\infty}E_{S^{2}}(x(s,\cdot))\leq E_{S^{2}}(x(s_{0},\cdot)).

Together with the exponential convergence on the negative end, this gives

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\|x_{s}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{t}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

It remains to identify the forward limit and to prove strong
H^{1}
-convergence. Since

E(x(s,\cdot))
is decreasing and bounded below, the limit

E_{S^{2},\infty}:=\lim_{s\to+\infty}E_{S^{2}}(x(s,\cdot))

exists. Also, finite energy implies that there is a sequence
s_{j}\to+\infty
such that

\|x_{s}(s_{j},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{t}}\longrightarrow 0.

The uniform energy bound, the compactness of
S^{2}
, and parabolic estimates on the
shifted strips
[s_{j}-1,s_{j}+1]\times[0,\tau]
imply, after passing to a subsequence, that

x(s_{j}+\sigma,t)\longrightarrow x_{\infty}(t)

smoothly on compact subsets of
(-1,1)\times[0,\tau]
, with the limit independent of

\sigma
. Passing to the limit in the equation gives

\nabla_{t}(x_{\infty})_{t}=0,\qquad x_{\infty}(0)=x_{\infty}(\tau)=a.

Therefore
x_{\infty}
is a based geodesic loop on
S^{2}
. It follows that
x_{\infty}
has length
2m\pi
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
if
x_{\infty}
is nonconstant. Thus,

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}|(x_{\infty})_{t}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{(2m\pi)^{2}}{2\tau}\geq\frac{2\pi^{2}}{\tau}=E_{S^{2}}(x_{1,*}).

On the other hand, for every finite

s_{0}
on the unstable branch, we have

E_{S^{2}}(x(s_{0},\cdot))<E_{S^{2}}(x_{1,*})\leq E_{S^{2},\infty}.

Contradiction. Thus the limiting geodesic must be constant:

x_{\infty}=x_{0},\qquad x_{0}(t)\equiv a.

Thus every subsequential forward limit is the constant loop
x_{0}
, and consequently

E_{\infty}=E(x_{0})=0.

Finally, since

\|x_{t}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{t}}^{2}=2E(x(s,\cdot))\longrightarrow 0.

and

x(s,t)-x_{0}(t)=x(s,t)-a=\int_{0}^{t}x_{t}(s,r)\,dr.

We have

\|x(s,\cdot)-x_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{t}}\leq C_{\tau}\|x_{t}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{t}}\longrightarrow 0.

Furthermore, we obtain

\|x(s,\cdot)-x_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{t}}\longrightarrow 0\qquad(s\to+\infty).\qed

Thus, the slice moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\Sigma}
consists of exactly two elements, denoted by
[x_{+}]
,
[x_{-}]
. Equivalently, the
d=0
flow on
\Sigma
has exactly two finite-energy trajectories from
\gamma_{1,0}
to
\gamma_{0,0}
, modulo
s
-translation, denoted by
[\nu_{0,+}]
,
[\nu_{0,-}]
.

For the full system at

At

The slice

is exactly given by

F_{\pi}(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}(|q_{t}|^{2}-|p_{t}|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t.

Lemma 4.22
.

At
\theta=\pi

-gradient equation in

(34)

\begin{cases}q_{s}=q_{tt}+\lambda q+\mu p,\\
p_{s}=-p_{tt}-\lambda p+\mu q,\end{cases}

where

are determined by

(35)

\lambda M=q\cdot(q_{s}-q_{tt})-p\cdot(p_{s}+p_{tt}),\qquad\mu M=p\cdot(q_{s}-q_{tt})+q\cdot(p_{s}+p_{tt}),

with

M:=|q|^{2}+|p|^{2}\geq 1.

Along classical trajectories,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}F_{\pi}(q(s,\cdot),p(s,\cdot))=-\int_{0}^{\tau}\bigl(|q_{s}|^{2}+|p_{s}|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

For a tangent variation
(\dot{q},\dot{p})
, the constraints give

q\cdot\dot{q}-p\cdot\dot{p}=0,\qquad q\cdot\dot{p}+p\cdot\dot{q}=0.

Moreover, using the fixed endpoint condition,

\mathrm{d}F_{\pi}(q,p)(\dot{q},\dot{p})=\int_{0}^{\tau}(q_{t}\cdot\dot{q}_{t}-p_{t}\cdot\dot{p}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\tau}(-q_{tt}\cdot\dot{q}+p_{tt}\cdot\dot{p})\,\mathrm{d}t.

Thus the ambient downward gradient is
(q_{tt},-p_{tt})
. Considering the normal directions

(q,-p),\qquad(p,q),

the full downward gradient of
(q,p)
has the form

(q_{s},p_{s})=(q_{tt},-p_{tt})+\lambda(q,-p)+\mu(p,q).

Here
\lambda,\mu
can be determined as follows. Along this flow, using

, we have

\displaystyle q\cdot(q_{s}-q_{tt})-p\cdot(p_{s}+p_{tt})=q\cdot(\lambda q+\mu p)-p\cdot(-\lambda p+\mu q)=\lambda(|q|^{2}+|p|^{2}),

\displaystyle p\cdot(q_{s}-q_{tt})+q\cdot(p_{s}+p_{tt})=p\cdot(\lambda q+\mu p)+q\cdot(-\lambda p+\mu q)=\mu(|q|^{2}+|p|^{2}).

Set

M=|q|^{2}+|p|^{2}\geq|q|^{2}-|p|^{2}=1.

Taking the
s
-derivative of (
32

q\cdot q_{s}-p\cdot p_{s}=0,\qquad q\cdot p_{s}+p\cdot q_{s}=0,

Then we can rewrite (
35

\lambda M=-(q\cdot q_{tt}+p\cdot p_{tt}),\qquad\mu M=q\cdot p_{tt}-p\cdot q_{tt}.

Finally, along a classical solution,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}F_{\pi}(q(s,\cdot),p(s,\cdot))=~

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}(-q_{tt}\cdot q_{s}+p_{tt}\cdot p_{s})\,\mathrm{d}t.

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\tau}(|q_{s}|^{2}+|p_{s}|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\tau}\lambda(q\cdot q_{s}-p\cdot p_{s})+\mu(p\cdot q_{s}+q\cdot p_{s})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\tau}\bigl(|q_{s}|^{2}+|p_{s}|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.\qed

Proposition 4.23
.

Let
(q,p)
be a classical finite-energy solution of
(
33
)–(
34

\left\lVert\lambda(s,\cdot)\right\rVert_{C^{k}([0,\tau])}+\left\lVert\mu(s,\cdot)\right\rVert_{C^{k}([0,\tau])}\leq C_{k}^{\prime}e^{-\delta(s-S_{0})}\qquad(s\geq S_{0}).

Proof.

By the strong

-asymptotics and the embedding

we can
work in the fixed local chart near

The boundary conditions give

We first eliminate

and

\lambda=-\frac{q\cdot q_{tt}+p\cdot p_{tt}}{|q|^{2}+|p|^{2}},\qquad\mu=\frac{q\cdot p_{tt}-p\cdot q_{tt}}{|q|^{2}+|p|^{2}},\qquad|q|^{2}+|p|^{2}\geq 1.

Substituting these expressions into (

)–(

where

is the
smooth nonlinear remainder. In particular,

Since

.

Let

4.6.3.

To go from

Let

4.24
and backward-uniqueness argument for

On the other hand, since we fixed the upper Bott coefficient to be zero in

Bott mode

\sigma_{B,\alpha}(t)=\sinh\left(\frac{2\pi it}{\tau}\right)N=i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{\tau}\right)N,\qquad N=(0,0,1).

\chi_{-}(s)

, we have the identifications

\mathcal{P}_{d,\alpha}:X^{\rm fix}_{0,\alpha}\longrightarrow X^{\rm fix}_{d,\alpha},\qquad\Theta_{d,\alpha}:Y_{d,\alpha}\longrightarrow Y_{0,\alpha}.

By construction,

\mathcal{P}_{d,\alpha}
and
\Theta_{d,\alpha}
depend
C^{1}
–smoothly on

d

\mathcal{P}_{d,\alpha}=\operatorname{Id}+O(d),\qquad\Theta_{d,\alpha}=\operatorname{Id}+O(d)

as bounded maps on the corresponding weighted spaces.

For

(\zeta,\rho)\in X^{\rm fix}_{0,\alpha}\oplus\mathbb{R},

define the perturbation along
\nu^{\rm app}_{d,\alpha}
by

\mathcal{I}_{d,\alpha}(\zeta,\rho):=\mathcal{P}_{d,\alpha}\zeta+\rho\,\mathcal{P}_{d,\alpha}\widehat{\sigma}_{B,\alpha}=\zeta+\rho\widehat{\sigma}_{B,\alpha}+O(d).

This map is
C^{1}
in
d
.

Furthermore, we define the parameter-dependent augmented nonlinear map

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}:[0,\varepsilon)_{d}\times\bigl(X^{\rm fix}_{0,\alpha}\oplus\mathbb{R}\bigr)\longrightarrow Y_{0,\alpha},

to be

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,\zeta,\rho):=\Theta_{d,\alpha}\left[\mathcal{F}_{d}\left(\exp_{\nu^{\rm app}_{d,\alpha}}^{\mathbb{C}}\bigl(\mathcal{I}_{d,\alpha}(\zeta,\rho)\bigr)\right)\right],\qquad\mathcal{F}_{d}(z):=\partial_{s}z-P_{z}(e^{i\theta(d)}\eta\bar{z}_{tt}).

Here the exponential map is taken pointwise along

\nu^{\rm app}_{d,\alpha}
.

By construction, the augmented nonlinear map
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,\zeta,\rho)
is
C^{1}
in
(d,\zeta,\rho)
near
(0,0,0)
(in the one-sided sense at
d=0
). Moreover, we have

•

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)=\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(0,0,0)+O(d)=O(d),

and the sharper estimate (proved in the following Lemma

)

\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}\Bigl(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)\Bigr)=O(d^{2})\quad\text{in }~\mathbb{R}\sigma_{B,\alpha},\qquad d\to 0^{+}.

•

D_{(\zeta,\rho)}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)=D_{(\zeta,\rho)}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(0,0,0)+O(d)

as bounded operators. In particular,

D_{(\zeta,\rho)}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(0,0,0)(\xi,r)=D^{\rm fix}_{0,\alpha}\xi+r\,D_{0,\alpha}\widehat{\sigma}_{B,\alpha}.

This is precisely our augmented linearized operator
\widehat{D}_{0,\alpha}
.

Lemma 4.31
.

For each
\alpha\in\{+,-\}
, we have

\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}\Bigl(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)\Bigr)=O(d^{2})\quad\text{in }~\mathbb{R}\,\sigma_{B,\alpha},\qquad d\to 0^{+}.

Proof.

At
(\zeta,\rho)=(0,0)
, we have
\mathcal{I}_{d,\alpha}(0,0)=0
, hence

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)=\Theta_{d,\alpha}\left[\mathcal{F}_{d}(\nu^{\rm app}_{d,\alpha})\right].

We can compute

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}d}\bigg|_{d=0}\Theta_{d,\alpha}\left[\mathcal{F}_{d}(\nu^{\rm app}_{d,\alpha})\right]

\displaystyle=\left.\partial_{d}\Theta_{d,\alpha}\right|_{d=0}\left[\mathcal{F}_{0}(\nu_{0,\alpha})\right]+\Theta_{0,\alpha}\left[D_{z}\mathcal{F}_{0}(\nu_{0,\alpha})\rho_{\alpha}+\left.\partial_{d}\mathcal{F}_{d}(\nu_{0,\alpha})\right|_{d=0}\right]

\displaystyle=D_{0,\alpha}\rho_{\alpha}+\left.\partial_{d}\mathcal{F}_{d}(\nu_{0,\alpha})\right|_{d=0},

where we use
\mathcal{F}_{0}(\nu_{0,\alpha})=0
and
\Theta_{0,\alpha}=\Id
.

By Lemma
4.30
, we have

\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}(D_{0,\alpha}\rho_{\alpha})=0.

On the other hand, the
d
-dependence in
\mathcal{F}_{d}
at
\nu_{0,\alpha}
is the factor

e^{i\theta(d)}
, we get

\left.\partial_{d}\mathcal{F}_{d}(\nu_{0,\alpha})\right|_{d=0}=-P_{z}\left(i\theta^{\prime}(0)e^{i\theta(0)}\eta\overline{\nu_{0,\alpha}}_{tt}\right)=\frac{i}{\pi}\partial_{s}\nu_{0,\alpha}.

Hence, we also have

\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}\left(\frac{i}{\pi}\partial_{s}\nu_{0,\alpha}\right)=0.

In summary, we obtain

\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}\Bigl(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)\Bigr)=\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}\Bigl(\Theta_{d,\alpha}\mathcal{F}_{d}(\nu^{\rm app}_{d,\alpha})\Bigr)=O(d^{2}).\qed

Proposition 4.32
.

There exists
d_{0}>0
and two smooth branches

d\longmapsto[\nu_{d,+}],\qquad d\longmapsto[\nu_{d,-}],\qquad\quad 0<d<d_{0},

such that for
\alpha\in\{+,-\}

[\nu_{d,\alpha}]\in\mathcal{M}_{d}(a,b_{d};\gamma_{0,d},\gamma_{1,d}),\qquad[\nu_{d,\alpha}]\to[\nu_{0,\alpha}]\quad(d\to 0^{+}).

In particular,

\#\mathcal{M}_{d}(a,b_{d};\gamma_{0,d},\gamma_{1,d})\geq 2\qquad(0<d<d_{0}).

Proof.

By Lemma
4.27
,
\widehat{D}_{0,\alpha}
is an
isomorphism. The Banach-space implicit function theorem, applied to the fixed cut-off augmented map
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}
, gives for all sufficiently small
d
, a unique small pair

(\zeta_{d,\alpha},\rho_{d,\alpha})
such that

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,\zeta_{d,\alpha},\rho_{d,\alpha})=0.

Moreover, since

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)=O(d)

in
Y_{0,\alpha}
, and the inverse of the linearized operator remains
uniformly bounded near the isomorphism
\widehat{D}_{0,\alpha}
, we obtain

(39)

\|\zeta_{d,\alpha}\|+|\rho_{d,\alpha}|\leq C\|\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0)\|_{Y_{0,\alpha}}\leq Cd.

Let

z_{d,\alpha}:=\exp_{\nu^{\rm app}_{d,\alpha}}^{\mathbb{C}}\Bigl(\mathcal{I}_{d,\alpha}\left(\zeta_{d,\alpha},\rho_{d,\alpha}\right)\Bigr)

be the small trajectory constructed above. Let

\sigma_{{\rm soft},d}
be the normalized upper
\lambda_{\rm soft}(d)
eigenmode.
As
s\to-\infty
, we can expand

Y_{d,\alpha}(s,t):=\left(\exp_{\gamma_{1,d}(t)}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)^{-1}(z_{d,\alpha}(s,t))=\beta_{d,\alpha}e^{\lambda_{\rm soft}(d)s}\sigma_{{\rm soft},d}(t)+Y_{d,\alpha}^{\perp}(s,t),\qquad s\to-\infty,

where

\lambda_{\rm soft}(d)=cd+O(d^{2}),~~c>0;\qquad\Pi_{\rm soft}^{-}(d)Y_{d,\alpha}^{\perp}=0.

Since

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}\left(d,\zeta_{d,\alpha},\rho_{d,\alpha}\right)=0,

we expand at
(\zeta,\rho)=(0,0)
:

0=\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)+D_{(\zeta,\rho)}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)\left(\zeta_{d,\alpha},\rho_{d,\alpha}\right)+\mathcal{N}_{d,\alpha}\left(\zeta_{d,\alpha},\rho_{d,\alpha}\right),

where

\left\|\mathcal{N}_{d,\alpha}(\zeta,\rho)\right\|\leq C\left(\|\zeta\|^{2}+|\rho|\,\|\zeta\|+|\rho|^{2}\right).

Projecting the equation to the upper soft channel gives

0=\Pi_{\rm soft}^{-}(d)\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)+\lambda_{\rm soft}(d)\beta_{d,\alpha}+R_{d,\alpha}.

Here

contains the transverse linear term and the nonlinear Taylor remainder.

Let

\mathscr{L}_{d,\alpha}:=D_{\zeta}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0):X^{\rm fix}_{0,\alpha}\longrightarrow Y_{0,\alpha}.

Then

\mathscr{L}_{d,\alpha}\zeta=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,\varepsilon\zeta,0),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathscr{L}_{0,\alpha}=D_{0,\alpha}^{\rm fix}.

Moreover, by the

-dependence of

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}
on

,

\mathscr{L}_{d,\alpha}=\mathscr{L}_{0,\alpha}+O(d)\quad\text{in }~\mathcal{L}\left(X^{\rm fix}_{0,\alpha},Y_{0,\alpha}\right).

After the fixed upper-end trivialization, the soft projection also

\Pi_{\rm soft}^{-}(d)=\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}+O(d).

Hence

\displaystyle\Pi_{\rm soft}^{-}(d)\mathscr{L}_{d,\alpha}\zeta

\displaystyle=\bigl(\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}+O(d)\bigr)\bigl(\mathscr{L}_{0,\alpha}+O(d)\bigr)\zeta

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}D_{0,\alpha}^{\rm fix}\zeta+O(d)\|\zeta\|=O(d)\|\zeta\|.

Thus the projected equation becomes

\lambda_{\rm soft}(d)\beta_{d,\alpha}=-\Pi_{\rm Bott}^{-}\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha}(d,0,0)+O\left(d^{2}+d\|\zeta_{d,\alpha}\|+\|\zeta_{d,\alpha}\|^{2}+|\rho_{d,\alpha}|\,\|\zeta_{d,\alpha}\|+|\rho_{d,\alpha}|^{2}\right).

By Lemma

, (
39
) and

, we obtain

Then we get a finite-energy solution

Since the two

\nu_{0,+}
and

are

for all sufficiently small

.
∎

4.7.3.
The normal
O(d)
control

To prove exactness of the count, we need a small-
d
no-escape result. The idea is to use the first-exit argument. To do that, let us establish some useful estimates.

First along the
d=0
slice, we will prove that the
d
-twisted vector field has a uniformly order-
d
normal component. Recall from Section
4.6.1

\Sigma=Q(S^{2})\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}.

For every smooth path
x:[0,\tau]\to S^{2}
, we regard
Qx
as a path in

\Sigma
. Along
Qx
, the Hermitian metric on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
gives the
orthogonal splitting

T_{Qx(t)}\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}=T_{Qx(t)}\Sigma\oplus N_{Qx(t)}\Sigma,\qquad 0\leq t\leq\tau.

Let

\pi_{Dx}^{\perp}:L^{2}\bigl([0,\tau],(Qx)^{*}T\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}\bigr)\longrightarrow L^{2}\bigl([0,\tau],(Qx)^{*}N\Sigma\bigr)

denote the Hermitian orthogonal projection onto the normal component of
L^{2}
-sections along
Qx
.

For simplicity, denote the
d
-twisted Morse vector field by

V_{d}(z):=P_{z}\bigl(e^{i\theta(d)}\eta\overline{z}_{tt}\bigr),\quad\text{with}\quad\theta(d)=\pi-\arctan\frac{d}{\pi}.

Along the slice, its
normal component

V_{d}^{\perp}(Qx):=\pi_{Qx}^{\perp}\bigl(V_{d}(Qx)\bigr)

measures the part of the
d
-twisted vector field which pushes the flow out of the
d=0
slice
\Sigma
.

Lemma 4.33
.

With the notations above, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
x
on bounded
C^{2}
-sets
and independent of all sufficiently small
d\geq 0
, such that

\bigl\|V_{d}^{\perp}(Qx)\bigr\|_{L_{t}^{2}}\leq Cd.

Proof.

At
d=0
, we have
\theta(0)=\pi
, and the preceding analysis in Section
4.6.1
shows

V_{0}(Qx)\in L^{2}\bigl([0,\tau],(Qx)^{*}T\Sigma\bigr).

Applying the normal projection gives

V_{0}^{\perp}(Qx)=\pi_{Qx}^{\perp}\bigl(V_{0}(Qx)\bigr)=0.

Now fix a bounded
C^{2}
-set of slice paths. On such a set, the map

(d,z)\longmapsto V_{d}(z)=P_{z}\bigl(e^{i\theta(d)}\eta\overline{z}_{tt}\bigr)

depends smoothly on
d
and on
z
. Moreover, the Hermitian orthogonal splitting

T_{Qx(t)}\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}=T_{Qx(t)}\Sigma\oplus N_{Qx(t)}\Sigma

varies smoothly with the base point
Qx(t)
. Hence the normal projection

\pi_{Qx}^{\perp}
also varies smoothly with
x
. Therefore the map

(d,x)\longmapsto V_{d}^{\perp}(Qx):=\pi_{Qx}^{\perp}\bigl(V_{d}(Qx)\bigr)

is smooth on bounded
C^{2}
-sets of slice paths.

Since this map vanishes
at
d=0
, the mean-value theorem in the
parameter
d
gives

\bigl\|V_{d}^{\perp}(Qx)\bigr\|_{L_{t}^{2}}=\bigl\|V_{d}^{\perp}(Qx)-V_{0}^{\perp}(Qx)\bigr\|_{L_{t}^{2}}\leq d\,\sup_{0\leq\delta\leq d}\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}V_{\delta}^{\perp}(Qx)\right\|_{L_{t}^{2}}.

On a fixed bounded
C^{2}
-set of paths, the standard compactness argument
shows that the last supremum is bounded by a constant
C
. Thus

\bigl\|V_{d}^{\perp}(Qx)\bigr\|_{L_{t}^{2}}\leq Cd.\qed

4.7.4.
The small-
d
no-escape theorem

Set

\mathcal{K}_{0}:=\{\gamma_{1,0}\}\cup\{\nu_{0,+}(s,\cdot):s\in\mathbb{R}\}\cup\{\nu_{0,-}(s,\cdot):s\in\mathbb{R}\}\cup\{\gamma_{0,0}\}.

It is the compactified
d=0
model. We use the following three kinds of local charts:

(a)

near
\gamma_{1,0}
, a Morse–Bott end chart, with the Bott direction separated from the transverse directions;

(b)

along
\nu_{0,\pm}
, the tubular neighborhood charts;

(c)

near
\gamma_{0,0}
, an ordinary Morse end chart.

The asymptotic expansions at
\gamma_{1,0}
,
\gamma_{0,0}
, established in the previous
subsections, will be used to glue these charts with uniform constants.

Now fix an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}_{s}
on which one of the above local
charts is used. We denote by

\kappa_{I}:I\times[0,\tau]\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}

the corresponding
d=0
reference path in
\mathcal{K}_{0}
. Using the endpoint correction (
37
), set

\kappa_{I,d}:=\mathcal{T}_{d}(\kappa_{I}).

Then

\kappa_{I,d}(s,0)=a,\qquad\kappa_{I,d}(s,\tau)=b_{d},\qquad\kappa_{I,d}=\kappa_{I}+O(d).

A nearby
d
-trajectory is written in the form

z(s,\cdot)=\exp_{\kappa_{I,d}(s,\cdot)}^{\mathbb{C}}Y_{I}(s,\cdot),\qquad s\in I.

Then the fixed-endpoint condition for
z
becomes the
homogeneous Dirichlet condition

Y_{I}(s,0)=0,\qquad Y_{I}(s,\tau)=0.

Let
E_{I,d}:=\kappa_{I,d}^{*}T\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Since
\kappa_{I,d}
is
O(d)
-close to
\kappa_{I}
, we identify

E_{I,d}
with

E_{I}:=E_{I,0}=\kappa_{I}^{*}T\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}

by parallel transport along the short curves

r\mapsto\kappa_{I,r}(s,t)
,
0\leq r\leq d
. Under this identification,
all
d
-dependent changes of the reference path contribute only
O(d)

terms.

Define
\mathcal{X}(I)
to be the
space of sections
Y_{I}
of
E_{I}
such that

Y_{I}\in H^{1}(I;L_{t}^{2}(E_{I}))\cap L^{2}(I;H_{t}^{2}(E_{I})\cap H^{1}_{0,t}(E_{I}))\cap L^{\infty}(I;H^{1}_{0,t}(E_{I})),

satisfying

(a)

near the Morse–Bott end
\gamma_{1,0}
,

Y_{I}\perp\sigma_{B};

(b)

on a middle chart around
\nu_{0,\alpha}
,

Y_{I}\perp\partial_{s}\nu_{0,\alpha};

(c)

near the Morse end
\gamma_{0,0}
,
Y_{I}
satisfies the decaying Morse-end condition.

Here the orthogonality is taken in the relevant
L^{2}
-sense. The norm is

\|Y_{I}\|_{\mathcal{X}(I)}:=\|Y_{I}\|_{H^{1}(I;L_{t}^{2})}+\|Y_{I}\|_{L^{2}(I;H_{t}^{2})}+\|Y_{I}\|_{L^{\infty}(I;H_{t}^{1})}.

For each chart, let
L_{I}(s)
denote the
d=0
linearized operator along the reference map
\kappa_{I}
. Then we can write the full
d=0
linearized operator in the
I
-chart as

\nabla_{s}^{\kappa_{I}}-L_{I}(s).

The target space is

\mathcal{Y}(I):=L^{2}(I;L_{t}^{2}(E_{I})),\qquad\|F\|_{\mathcal{Y}(I)}:=\|F\|_{L^{2}(I;L_{t}^{2})}.

On half-infinite end intervals, the same notation will be used for the
corresponding exponentially weighted versions of these spaces.

Proposition 4.34
.

There exist
\rho_{*}>0
, constants
C_{0},C_{1},C_{2}>0
, and a system of local
charts as above such that the following holds. Let
0<\rho\leq\rho_{*}
and let
d\geq 0
be sufficiently small. If a
d
-trajectory
z

z(s,\cdot)=\exp_{\kappa_{I,d}(s,\cdot)}Y_{I}(s,\cdot),\qquad s\in I.

stays in the
\rho
-neighborhood of
\mathcal{K}_{0}
on an interval
I
, then

Y_{I}\in\mathcal{X}(I)
satisfies

\nabla_{s}^{\kappa_{I}}Y_{I}-L_{I}(s)Y_{I}=f_{I,d}(s)+R_{I,d}(Y_{I}).

The two remainders (forcing and nonlinear reminder respectively) satisfy

\|f_{I,d}\|_{\mathcal{Y}(I)}\leq C_{0}d,

and

\|R_{I,d}(Y_{I})\|_{\mathcal{Y}(I)}\leq C_{1}\left(\rho\|Y_{I}\|_{\mathcal{X}(I)}+\|Y_{I}\|_{\mathcal{X}(I)}^{2}\right).

Moreover,

\|Y_{I}\|_{\mathcal{X}(I)}\leq C_{2}\|\nabla_{s}^{\kappa_{I}}Y_{I}-L_{I}(s)Y_{I}\|_{\mathcal{Y}(I)}.

On every bounded interval
I=[-R,R]
, bounded subsets of
\mathcal{X}(I)

are relatively compact in
C^{0}(I;H_{t}^{1})
.

Proof.

Write the trajectory in the chosen chart as

z(s,\cdot)=\exp_{\kappa_{I,d}(s,\cdot)}^{\mathbb{C}}Y_{I}(s,\cdot),\qquad Y_{I}(s,0)=Y_{I}(s,\tau)=0.

Substituting this expression into the
d
-flow equation
\partial_{s}z=V_{d}(z)
and expanding around the reference trajectory
\kappa_{I}
gives

\nabla^{\kappa_{I}}_{s}Y_{I}-L_{I}(s)Y_{I}=f_{I,d}(s)+R_{I,d}(Y_{I}).

Here

•

L_{I}(s)
is the
d=0
sliced linearized operator along
\kappa_{I}
.

•

The term

is the
Y_{I}
-independent forcing term:

f_{I,d}=\bigl(V_{d}(\kappa_{I,d})-\partial_{s}\kappa_{I,d}\bigr)^{\perp}.

It therefore measures how far
\kappa_{I,d}
is from being an exact

-flow trajectory. By the estimates in Lemma

for

and for

\|f_{I,d}\|_{\mathcal{Y}(I)}\leq C_{0}d.

•

The term

, it is quadratic in

at

,

It only remains to recall the uniform estimate for the sliced linearized

removing the Bott direction. On the compact middle part it follows from
the regularity and nondegeneracy of the two

trajectories

\nu_{0,+}
and
\nu_{0,-}
. Since the compactified model

\mathcal{K}_{0}
is covered by finitely many such charts, we obtain, after

,

\|Y_{I}\|_{\mathcal{X}(I)}\leq C_{2}\bigl\|\nabla^{\kappa_{I}}_{s}Y_{I}-L_{I}(s)Y_{I}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{Y}(I)}.

Finally, if

is bounded, the definition of the

Since

on the compact

\mathcal{X}(I)
are relatively compact in
C^{0}(I;H_{t}^{1})
.
∎

Proposition 4.35
.

There exist
d_{*}>0
and a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{K}_{0})\subset\mathcal{P}^{\mathbb{C}}_{0}

of the compactified

\rho^{*}>0
,

.

C^{\prime\prime}d_{n}<\rho/2
. Hence

\widetilde{z}_{n}((-\infty,s_{n}^{*}])\subset\mathcal{N}_{\rho/2}(K_{0}).

This contradicts the first exit time assumption

\widetilde{z}_{n}(s_{n}^{*})\in\partial\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(\mathcal{K}_{0}).\qed

4.7.5.
Compactness as
d_{n}\to 0^{+}
and the exact-two count

We now combine no-escape with local continuation. A limiting sequence could
fail to give the desired
d=0
trajectory only by breaking, or by developing
a zero-energy drift along the upper Bott family at
d=0
. The first
phenomenon is excluded, since for
0<d\ll 1
the no-escape neighborhood contains no intermediate critical
point
\zeta
with

F_{\theta(d)}(\gamma_{0,d})<F_{\theta(d)}(\zeta)<F_{\theta(d)}(\gamma_{1,d}),\qquad G_{\theta(d)}(\zeta)=G_{\theta(d)}(\gamma_{0,d})=G_{\theta(d)}(\gamma_{1,d}).

The second phenomenon is excluded by our estimate
|\beta_{d_{n},\alpha}|\leq Cd_{n}
in Proposition
4.32
.

Proposition 4.36
.

Let
d_{n}\to 0^{+}
and choose

[u_{n}]\in\mathcal{M}_{d_{n}}(a,b_{d_{n}};\gamma_{0,d_{n}},\gamma_{1,d_{n}}).

Then, after
s
-translation, a subsequence converges in
C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
to an element of

\mathcal{M}_{0}(a,a;\gamma_{0,0},\gamma_{1,0})=\{[\nu_{0,+}],[\nu_{0,-}]\}.

Proof.

Choose representatives
u_{n}
and translate them in the
s
-variable so
that, for a fixed regular value

c_{*}\in\bigl(F_{\theta(0)}(\gamma_{0,0}),F_{\theta(0)}(\gamma_{1,0})\bigr),

we have

F_{\theta(d_{n})}(u_{n}(0,\cdot))=c_{*}.

For
n
large,
c_{*}
still lies strictly between the two endpoint critical
values.

By Proposition
4.35
, after applying the endpoint correction

\mathcal{T}_{d_{n}}^{-1}
, the trajectories
u_{n}
remain in a fixed
neighborhood of
\mathcal{K}_{0}
. And by Proposition
4.34
,
after passing to a subsequence, we obtain convergence on each bounded strip.
A diagonal argument gives a limit

u_{\infty}:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\tau]\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}.

Since
d_{n}\to 0
and
\mathcal{T}_{d_{n}}\to\mathrm{Id}
, the limit
u_{\infty}
solves the
d=0
flow
equation. Standard local parabolic bootstrapping improves the
C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\infty}
convergence.

The normalization also passes to the limit and gives

F_{\theta(0)}(u_{\infty}(0,\cdot))=c_{*}.

Thus,
u_{\infty}
is nonconstant. Moreover, the energy identity gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\partial_{s}u_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{L_{t}^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s=F_{\theta(d_{n})}(\gamma_{1,d_{n}})-F_{\theta(d_{n})}(\gamma_{0,d_{n}}).

By lower semicontinuity,
u_{\infty}
is a finite-energy
d=0
trajectory.
The preceding discussion rules out broken intermediate levels,
and the transversality of the endpoint branch to the upper Bott family rules
out zero-energy Bott drift. Therefore the upper and lower limits of

u_{\infty}
are forced to be

\gamma_{1,0}\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{0,0}.

Thus

[u_{\infty}]\in M_{0}(a,a;\gamma_{0,0},\gamma_{1,0}).

By Theorem
4.26
,

M_{0}(a,a;\gamma_{0,0},\gamma_{1,0})=\{[\nu_{0,+}],[\nu_{0,-}]\}.

Hence, after translating in
s
and passing to a subsequence,

[u_{n}]
converges in
C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
to
[\nu_{0,+}]
or
[\nu_{0,-}]
.
∎

Theorem 4.37
.

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\#\mathcal{M}_{d}(a,b_{d};\gamma_{0,d},\gamma_{1,d})=2\qquad\text{for all }0<d<\varepsilon.

Proof.

Proposition
4.32
gives at least two trajectories for all small
d>0
. Suppose there are additional trajectories
[w_{n}]
with
d_{n}\to 0^{+}
. By Proposition
4.36
, after translation and subsequence extraction we get a limit

[w_{\infty}]\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(a,a;\gamma_{0,0},\gamma_{1,0})=\{[\nu_{0,+}],[\nu_{0,-}]\}.

Then the local implicit-function theorem from Proposition
4.32
forces
[w_{n}]
to lie on the corresponding local branch for all sufficiently large
n
, contradicting the assumption that the
[w_{n}]
is distinct from those two branches.
Therefore no additional small-
d
trajectories exist.
∎
