Computation of thermal entropy for the doped Hubbard Model
Yu-Feng Song, Youjin Deng, Yuan-Yao He

Abstract
We develop a highly efficient framework for computing the thermal entropy in the doped Fermi-Hubbard model within the grand-canonical ensemble. The framework comprises four calculation schemes that express the entropy as path integrals in the parameter space of temperature, interaction strength, and chemical potential. The integrands involve only fundamental observables, including the total energy, fermion density, and double occupancy, which are readily accessible in a wide range of theoretical and numerical methods. We further derive useful Maxwell relations connecting the entropy to other quantities, and present practical formulas for directly evaluating the grand potential. As an application, we compute the entropy of the doped Hubbard model in two and three dimensions, using the numerically unbiased auxiliary-field quantum Monte Carlo method. The test results show excellent agreement across the different schemes and quantitatively verify the Maxwell relations, confirming the reliability of the framework. In two dimensions, we further benchmark our entropy results in physically relevant parameter regimes against diagrammatic Monte Carlo calculations and observe excellent quantitative consistency between the two approaches. By providing an efficient and broadly applicable route for entropy evaluation, our work facilitates the thermodynamic characterization of complex correlated states in the doped Hubbard model.

(see Appendix

) and thus yields
\bm{s}_{\mu}(\infty)=\ln 4
. Then via applying the integration by parts for Eq. (

(and
\beta_{0}t=1/T_{0}
), we reach the final expression for

as

\displaystyle\bm{s}(T)=\ln 4

\displaystyle+\frac{e_{\rm tot}(T)}{T}-\int_{T}^{T_{0}}\frac{e_{\rm tot}(T^{\prime})}{{T^{\prime}}^{2}}\differential T^{\prime}

(18)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}e_{\rm tot}(\beta^{\prime})\differential\beta^{\prime},

which shares the same form as Eq. (

) for the fixed-

by

. At half filling with

holds, and hence Eqs. (

With
\mu\neq 0

\displaystyle C_{X}=T\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial T}\Big)_{X},

(19)

in Eqs. (

\displaystyle

\displaystyle C_{n}=T\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial T}\Big)_{n}=\Big(\frac{\partial e_{\mathrm{in}}}{\partial T}\Big)_{n},

(20)

\displaystyle C_{\mu}=T\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial T}\Big)_{\mu}=\Big(\frac{\partial e_{\mathrm{tot}}}{\partial T}\Big)_{\mu}.

These two formulas can be used to compute the specific heat along the corresponding paths to characterize the physical properties of the system.

III.3
Varying
\mu
with fixed
U
and
T

As a complementary perspective on varying
T
schemes in Secs.
III.1
and
III.2
, we can also evaluate the entropy as a function of
\mu
(namely, doping or
n
) or
U
at fixed

. Such a formulation is particularly useful in characterizing the doping physics in the model. We first focus on the path of varying
\mu
with fixed
U
and
T
.

In Eq. (
12
), fixing
U
means
\differential{e_k}+U\differential{h_{I}}=\differential{e_{\rm in}}
, and results in the total differential
\differential{\eyps}=(\differential{e_{\rm in}}+\mu\differential{n})/T
. We can similarly perform integration on both sides from a specific reference point
\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{0}
to
\mu^{\prime}=\mu

\displaystyle\bm{s}(\mu)-\bm{s}(\mu_{0})=\frac{1}{T}\Big[e_{\mathrm{in}}(\mu)-e_{\mathrm{in}}(\mu_{0})+\int_{\mu_{0}}^{\mu}\mu^{\prime}\differential{n(\mu^{\prime})}\Big].

(21)

Applying the integration by parts for the integral, we can reach its simplified form as

\displaystyle\bm{s}(\mu)=\bm{s}(\mu_{0})+\frac{1}{T}\Big[e_{\mathrm{tot}}(\mu)-e_{\mathrm{tot}}(\mu_{0})-\int_{\mu_{0}}^{\mu}n(\mu^{\prime})\differential{\mu^{\prime}}\Big],

(22)

where we apply the relation
e_{\rm tot}(\mu)=e_{\rm in}(\mu)+\mu n(\mu)
from Eq. (

). In practical calculations, the reference
\mu_{0}

1

\mu_{0}=0

\bm{s}(\mu_{0}=0)

4
) or Eq. (

\displaystyle\bm{s}(\mu)=\bm{s}(0)+\frac{1}{T}\Big[e_{\mathrm{tot}}(\mu)-e_{\mathrm{tot}}(0)-\int_{0}^{\mu}n(\mu^{\prime})\differential{\mu^{\prime}}\Big],

(23)

where
\bm{s}(0)
and

are the entropy density and total energy density, respectively, at
\mu=0
(half filling). Note that the energy

. Another special choice for

\mu_{0}=+\infty

in the model (
1
). However, this requires wide
\mu

Although
\bm{s}(0)
in Eq. (

) must be computed independently, typically through the integration along the
T
or
U

II
, it contributes only an additive constant when analyzing the entropy differences across fillings. In practical applications, this constant is irrelevant when only the relative variation or the shape of the entropy curve as a function of doping is of interest. This path therefore provides a flexible and efficient route to access entropy trends in the doped Hubbard model, particularly in regimes where fixing the fermion filling directly is computationally demanding. For example, at intermediate to low temperatures where the fermion sign problem becomes significant in AFQMC simulations, this scheme is particularly useful since the fermion density

III.4
Varying

and

The last computational scheme for the entropy density

is along the

T
and

*Song2025B

Based on Eq. (

\differential n=0
, leading to the total differential
\differential{\eyps}=(\differential{e_k}+U\differential{h_I})/T
. Note that

\displaystyle\bm{s}(U)-\bm{s}(0)=\frac{1}{T}\Big[e_{k}(U)-e_{k}(0)+\int_{0}^{U}U^{\prime}\differential{h_{I}(U^{\prime})}\Big].

Here,
\bm{s}(0)=\bm{s}_{0}

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{0}=-2N_{s}^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\big[f_{\mathbf{k}}\ln f_{\mathbf{k}}+(1-f_{\mathbf{k}})\ln(1-f_{\mathbf{k}})\big],

\displaystyle\bm{s}(U)-\bm{s}_{0}=\frac{1}{T}\Big[e_{\mathrm{in}}(U)-e_{\mathrm{in}}(0)-\int_{0}^{U}h_{I}(U^{\prime})\differential{U^{\prime}}\Big],

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{T}\Big[e_{\mathrm{in}}(U)-e_{\mathrm{in}}(0)-\int_{0}^{U}D(U^{\prime})\differential{U^{\prime}}+\frac{Un}{2}\Big].

\displaystyle\bm{s}(U)=\bm{s}_{0}+\frac{1}{T}\Big[\widetilde{e}_{\rm in}(U)-\widetilde{e}_{\rm in}(0)-\int_{0}^{U}D(U^{\prime})\differential{U^{\prime}}\Big].

III.5
Maxwell relations

In the previous subsections, we have derived formulas for the entropy along four different paths in the parameter space of the doped Hubbard model, which together cover all relevant simulation scenarios. With the entropy formulas, here we focus on the Maxwell relations that connect thermal entropy with other physical quantities in the model. In grand canonical ensemble, Maxwell relations follow from the equality of mixed second-order derivatives of the grand potential. In the procedure, we need to carefully distinguish the fixed-
\mu
and fixed-
n
conditions.

From the grand potential density
\Omega(U,T,\mu)
in Eq. (
9
), we can readily obtain its first-order derivatives as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial T}=-\bm{s},\quad\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial U}=h_{I},\quad\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\mu}=n,

(29)

which then yields the mixed second-order derivatives

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial U}\Big)_{\mu,T}

\displaystyle=-\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial T}\Big)_{\mu,U},

(30a)

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{U,T}

\displaystyle=-\Big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu},

(30b)

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{T,U}

\displaystyle=+\Big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,\mu},

(30c)

where the subscripts (such as “
\mu,T
” and “
\mu,U
”) denote the condition of fixed natural variables. Note that, in our model Hamiltonian (
1
), we write the chemical potential term
+\mu\sum_{\mathbf{i}}(\hat{n}_{\mathbf{i\uparrow}}+\hat{n}_{\mathbf{i\downarrow}})
as a pure hole-doping term with
\mu>0
. Equations (
30a
)-(
30c
) are the Maxwell relations for the fixed-
\mu
calculations. They already have practical applications.
First
, fixing
\mu=0
in the model (
1
) leads to
n=1
for arbitrary
U
and
T
, as the half-filling case. This yields
h_{I}=D-1/2
, and hence Eq. (
30a
) can be rewritten as
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial U)_{T}=-(\partial D/\partial T)_{U}
, the well-known Maxwell relation in the half-filled Hubbard model
78
;
56
.
Second
, Eq. (
30b
) means that, at fixed
U
, the variation of
\bm{s}
with
\mu
[as
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial\mu)_{U,T}
] is opposite to the change of fermion density
n
versus
T
[as
(\partial n/\partial T)_{U,\mu}
]. Consequently, the extrema of
\bm{s}(\mu)
and
n(T)
occur at the same
(T,\mu)
point. As a special case,
\mu=0
in the model (
1
) corresponds to the half filling, and hence leads to
(\partial n/\partial T)_{U,\mu=0}=0
and subsequently
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial\mu)_{U,T}=0
, meaning that
\mu=0
is an extremum of
\bm{s}(\mu)
function at fixed
T
and
U
. This can also be revealed by directly computing
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial\mu)_{U,T}
via Eq. (
23
), which yields

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{U,T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\Big[\frac{\partial e_{\mathrm{tot}}(\mu)}{\partial\mu}-n(\mu)\Big]_{U,T}

(31)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\Big[\frac{\partial e_{\mathrm{in}}(\mu)}{\partial\mu}+\mu\frac{\partial n(\mu)}{\partial\mu}\Big]_{U,T},

based on
e_{\mathrm{tot}}(\mu)=e_{\mathrm{in}}(\mu)+\mu n(\mu)
from Eq. (
13
). Moreover, the hole-doped (with
\mu>0
) and electron-doped (with
\mu<0
) situations of the model (
1
) are connected via particle-hole transformations
c_{\mathbf{i}\sigma}^{+}\to(-1)^{\mathbf{i}}c_{\mathbf{i}\sigma},c_{\mathbf{i}\sigma}\to(-1)^{\mathbf{i}}c_{\mathbf{i}\sigma}^{+}
, which leads to
n(-\mu)=2-n(\mu)
and
e_{\mathrm{in}}(-\mu)=e_{\mathrm{in}}(\mu)
. Both
e_{\mathrm{in}}(\mu)
and
n(\mu)
are expected to be analytic functions near
\mu=0
(in the absence of phase transitions), resulting in
(\partial e_{\mathrm{in}}/\partial\mu)_{\mu=0}=0
, and consequently
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial\mu)_{U,T}=0
at
\mu=0
. Equation (
31
) also indicates that
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial\mu)_{U,T}
has opposite signs but equal magnitudes at
+\mu
and
-\mu
points, implying
\bm{s}(\mu)=\bm{s}(-\mu)
in the model (
1
) for fixed
U
and
T
. A direct proof of
\bm{s}(\mu)=\bm{s}(-\mu)
can also be achieved via Eq. (
23
) (see Appendix
B
). Furthermore, Eq. (
31
) can be used to numerically compute the extremum of
\bm{s}(\mu)
via
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial\mu)_{U,T}=0
.
Third
, taking an additional
\mu
-derivative for Eq. (
30b
) can lead to a new equality

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{s}}{\partial\mu^{2}}\Big)_{U,T}=\Big(\frac{\partial\kappa}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu},

(32)

in which
\kappa=-\partial n/\partial\mu
is the charge compressibility, and the equality of mixed partial derivatives is applied. Typically,
\partial\kappa/\partial T<0
and
\partial\kappa/\partial T>0
can qualitatively separate the metallic and insulating behaviors
49
. Based on this physical interpretation and Eq. (
32
), the condition
\partial^{2}\bm{s}/\partial\mu^{2}=0
is identified as the crossover from metallic behavior to a non-Fermi-liquid state in Ref.
51
.

While the fixed-
\mu
case discussed above follows directly from the natural variables of the grand canonical ensemble, many theoretical and experimental situations instead involve fixing the fermion filling
n
. Therefore, we now turn to the Maxwell relations under the fixed-
n
condition for the doped Hubbard model. These relations can be obtained by enforcing the total differential conditions along paths of constant filling.
First
, at fixed
U
and
n
, the total differential
\differential n
is given by

\displaystyle\differential n=\Big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{U,T}\differential\mu+\Big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu}\differential T=0,

(33)

and it subsequently yields a Maxwell relation

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,n}=-\frac{\big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\big)_{U,\mu}}{\big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\big)_{U,T}}=\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial n}\Big)_{U,T},

(34)

in which Eq. (
30b
) is used in the second equality. This relation was also discussed in Ref.
57
;
19
. It connects the temperature dependence of chemical potential
\mu
to the entropy variation with fermion density
n
at fixed
U
.
Second
, at fixed
T
and
n
, the total differential
\differential n
follows

\displaystyle\differential n=\Big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{T,U}\differential\mu+\Big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,\mu}\differential U=0,

(35)

and it leads to the second Maxwell relation

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,n}=-\frac{\big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial U}\big)_{T,\mu}}{\big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\big)_{T,U}}=-\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial n}\Big)_{T,U}=\frac{1}{2}-\Big(\frac{\partial D}{\partial n}\Big)_{T,U},

(36)

where Eq. (
30c
) and the equality
h_{I}=D-n/2
are used in the second and third equality, respectively.
Third
, by combining the total differentials
\differential\bm{s}
with fixed
T
and
n
as well as
\differential h_{I}
with fixed
U
and
n
, we can reach the third Maxwell relation

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial U}\Big)_{n,T}=-\Big(\frac{\partial D}{\partial T}\Big)_{n,U}.

(37)

The detailed derivation is summarized in Appendix
B
. This relation associates the entropy variation with
U
to the temperature derivative of double occupancy
D
. For the special case of
n=1
, Eq. (
37
) becomes identical to Eq. (
30a
) obtained from fixed-
\mu
condition, and it also reduces to half-filling form
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial U)_{T}=-(\partial D/\partial T)_{U}
. At arbitrary fixed filling
n
, double occupancy
D=n^{2}/4
at
U=0
holds, and according to Eq. (
37
), it leads to
(\partial D/\partial T)_{n,U=0}=0
and subsequently results in
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial U)_{n,T}=0
at
U=0
. This means that
U=0
point is the extremum of
\bm{s}(U)
function at fixed
n
and
T
. Besides, based on the entropy formula in Eq. (
28
), we can actually evaluate the derivative
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial U)_{n,T}
as

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial U}\Big)_{n,T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\Big[\frac{\partial\widetilde{e}_{\rm in}(U)}{\partial U}-D(U)\Big]_{n,T}

(38)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\Big[\frac{\partial e_{k}(U)}{\partial U}+U\frac{\partial D(U)}{\partial U}\Big]_{n,T},

where
\widetilde{e}_{\rm in}(U)=e_{k}(U)+UD(U)
is applied in the second equality. Equation (
38
) can be used to numerically estimate the extremum of
\bm{s}(U)
by solving
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial U)_{n,T}=0
.

In the Hubbard model, Maxwell relations provide explicit connections between different thermodynamic observables, including thermal entropy
\bm{s}
, double occupancy
D
, chemical potential
\mu
, and fermion density
n
. From our calculations, Eqs. (
30a
)-(
30c
), associated with Eq. (
32
), are the Maxwell relations for the fixed-
\mu
simulations of the doped Hubbard model (
1
), while the fixed-
n
correspondences include Eqs. (
34
), (
36
), and (
37
). A clear understanding of these relations can offer valuable insights into the essential physics in the doped Hubbard model.

III.6
Tuning
\mu
to reach a fixed fermion filling

In grand-canonical-ensemble simulations for the Hubbard model, the requirement of fixing fermion filling
n
to a desired value
n_{\rm target}
is frequently encountered in various quantum many-body numerical approaches
83
;
57
;
19
;
47
;
51
;
29
;
53
. This is typically realized via tuning the chemical potential
\mu
by hand, based on the observation that
n(\mu)
versus
\mu
is a monotonically decreasing function in the model (
1
). Here we introduce a highly efficient procedure to achieve the
\mu
-tuning process.

We start with a relatively wide range of
\mu
, denoted as
[\mu_{i},\mu_{f}]
, which contains the final solution
\mu_{\rm target}
satisfying
n(\mu_{\rm target})=n_{\rm target}
. First, we apply the standard bisection or linear interpolation method to update
(\mu_{i},\mu_{f})
and to narrow down the correct range containing
\mu_{\rm target}
. For instance, given that
n(\mu_{i})>n_{\rm target}
and
n(\mu_{f})<n_{\rm target}
, we generate
\mu_{\rm new}=(\mu_{i}+\mu_{f})/2
via bisection (or an analogous
\mu_{\text{new}}
via linear interpolation) and compute
n(\mu_{\rm new})
, and then set
\mu_{i}=\mu_{\rm new}
if
n(\mu_{\rm new})>n_{\rm target}
or otherwise set
\mu_{f}=\mu_{\text{new}}
. Second, once the difference
(\mu_{f}-\mu_{i})
is reduced to a threshold (e.g.,
\sim 10^{-2}t
), the desired fermion density
n(\mu_{\rm target})=n_{\rm target}
can be approximated by its first-order Taylor expansion around
\mu_{0}=\mu_{\rm new}
from the last step as

\displaystyle n_{\rm target}\simeq n(\mu_{0})+(\mu_{\rm target}-\mu_{0})\frac{\partial n(\mu)}{\partial\mu}\Big|_{\mu=\mu_{0}},

(39)

in which
\partial n(\mu)/\partial\mu
is related to the charge compressibility
\kappa
that can be numerically computed via
78

\displaystyle\kappa=-\frac{\partial n(\mu)}{\partial\mu}=\frac{\beta}{N_{s}}\sum_{\mathbf{ij}}\big(\langle\hat{n}_{\mathbf{i}}\hat{n}_{\mathbf{j}}\rangle-\langle\hat{n}_{\mathbf{i}}\rangle\langle\hat{n}_{\mathbf{j}}\rangle\big).

(40)

The calculation of
\kappa
only involves the density-density correlation function, which is straightforward in numerical methods. From Eq. (
39
), we obtain an approximate solution
\widetilde{\mu}_{\rm target}
for the true
\mu_{\rm target}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{\rm target}=\mu_{0}+\frac{n(\mu_{0})-n_{\rm target}}{\kappa(\mu_{0})}.

(41)

Then we set
\mu_{0}=\widetilde{\mu}_{\rm target}
, compute
n(\mu_{0})
, apply Eq. (
41
) to get a new
\widetilde{\mu}_{\rm target}
, and repeat the process until convergence. In statistical methods such as AFQMC,
n(\mu_{0})
from the simulation has average and error bar, denoted as
\bar{n}_{0}
and
\sigma_{n_{0}}
. We typically use
|\bar{n}_{0}-n_{\rm target}|\leq\sigma_{n_{0}}
as the convergence criterion. The combination of the first and second steps as discussed above enables the precise determination of
\mu_{\rm target}
.

In practical AFQMC simulations, we find that the above procedure can converge for most situations within quite a limited number of iterations, such as three in the first step and two in the second.
Moreover, since
\mu_{\rm target}
typically varies only slightly with changes in adjacent model parameters or linear system sizes, a more accurate guess for the initial range
[\mu_{i},\mu_{f}]
can often be made in subsequent simulations, thereby accelerating convergence. More broadly, the above self-consistent procedure for determining
\mu_{\rm target}
can be applied to other quantum many-body methods, provided that
n(\mu)
and
\kappa(\mu)
can be computed efficiently and accurately.

IV
An alternative derivation for the entropy evaluation formulas

In the previous section, we derived the entropy evaluation formulas from the total differential of the grand potential. Here, we provide an alternative derivation based on the connection between the grand potential and the partition function, combined with the finite-temperature Hellmann-Feynman theorem.

In thermodynamics and statistical physics, the grand potential density
\Omega
is connected to the partition function
Z=\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta\hat{H}})
via
\Omega=-N_{s}^{-1}T\ln Z
. The ensemble average of an observable
\hat{O}
reads
\langle\hat{O}\rangle=Z^{-1}{\Tr}(e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\hat{O})
. From the model (
1
), one can readily obtain the following derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\differential(\gpps/T)}{\differential T}=-\frac{e_{\mathrm{tot}}(T)}{T^{2}}+\frac{n}{T}\frac{\differential\mu(T)}{\differential T}.

(42)

Note that the chemical potential
\mu
can be a function of temperature
T
in the fixed-
n
calculations. This equality enables the direct evaluation of
\Omega/T
via integration and subsequently the thermal entropy along the
T
axis. For other paths in the parameter space, such as those along the
\mu
or
U
axes, we rely on the finite-temperature Hellmann-Feynman theorem

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\alpha}=\Big\langle\frac{\partial\hat{H}}{\partial\alpha}\Big\rangle,

(43)

which can be proved using the Lehmann representation (see Appendix A in Ref.
78
). By setting
\alpha=\mu
and
\alpha=U
, we obtain explicit expressions for
\partial\Omega/\partial\mu
and
\partial\Omega/\partial U
. Integrating these expressions gives
\Omega(\mu)
and
\Omega(U)
, which in turn allow the thermal entropy to be evaluated. In the following, we combine Eqs. (
42
), (
43
) and (
9
) to independently derive the entropy formulas obtained in Sec.
III
for four different paths.

For varying
T
with fixed
U
and
n
, we can take an integration over temperature from
T
to
\infty
for both sides of Eq. (
42
). In this calculation, the limit
[\Omega(T)/T]_{T=\infty}
needs to be carefully handled. From Eq. (
9
),
\Omega(T)/T=e_{\rm in}/T+\mu n/T-\bm{s}
. As
T\to\infty
,
\mu
should diverge to reach the fixed filling
n
(see Appendix
B
), while
e_{\rm in}
remains finite. This leads to
[\Omega(T)/T]_{T=\infty}=(n\mu/T)_{T=\infty}-\bm{s}_{n}(\infty)
, where
\bm{s}_{n}(\infty)=2\ln 2-n\ln n-(2-n)\ln(2-n)
is the fixed-
n
entropy density at
T=\infty
(see Appendix
B
). Thus, after the integration, we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega(T)}{T}=

\displaystyle-\bm{s}_{n}(\infty)+n\frac{\mu(T)}{T}\Big|_{T=\infty}

(44)

\displaystyle+\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{e_{\mathrm{tot}}(T^{\prime})}{T^{{}^{\prime}2}}\differential T^{\prime}-n\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{1}{T^{\prime}}\differential\mu(T^{\prime}).

Then applying the relation
e_{\rm tot}=e_{\rm in}+\mu n
and performing the integration by parts for both integrals, the above equality can be simplified as

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega(T)}{T}=-\bm{s}_{n}(\infty)+n\frac{\mu(T)}{T}+\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{e_{\mathrm{in}}(T^{\prime})}{T^{{}^{\prime}2}}\differential T^{\prime}.

(45)

Then combining Eqs. (
9
) and (
45
), the entropy formula in Eq. (
15
) can be exactly reproduced.

For varying
T
with fixed
U
and
\mu
, the derivative
\differential\mu/\differential T
in Eq. (
42
) vanishes. For this case, the total energy
e_{\rm tot}
is always finite, leading to
[\Omega(T)/T]_{T=\infty}=-\bm{s}_{\mu}(\infty)
with
\bm{s}_{\mu}(\infty)=\ln 4
as the fixed-
\mu
entropy density at
T=\infty
. Then the integration over temperature for Eq. (
42
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega(T)}{T}=-\bm{s}_{\mu}(\infty)+\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{e_{\mathrm{tot}}(T^{\prime})}{T^{{}^{\prime}2}}\differential T^{\prime},

(46)

which evidently recovers the formulas in Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
) when combining
\Omega(T)/T=e_{\rm tot}/T-\bm{s}(T)
from Eq. (
9
).

For varying
\mu
with fixed
U
and
T
, we apply Eq. (
43
) and choose
\alpha=\mu
, which gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Omega(\mu)}{\partial\mu}=\Big\langle\frac{\partial\hat{H}}{\partial\mu}\Big\rangle=n.

(47)

Integrating over
\mu
for this equality gives

\displaystyle\Omega(\mu)=\Omega(0)+\int_{0}^{\mu}n(\mu^{\prime})d\mu^{\prime},

(48)

which clearly reproduces the formula in Eq. (
23
) considering
\Omega(\mu)=e_{\rm tot}-T\bm{s}(\mu)
.

For varying
U
with fixed
T
and
n
, we choose
\alpha=U
in Eq. (
43
), and reach the Hellmann-Feynman theorem

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Omega(U)}{\partial U}=\Big\langle\frac{\partial\hat{H}}{\partial U}\Big\rangle=h_{I}+n\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial U}.

(49)

The integration over
U
for this equality yields

\displaystyle\Omega(U)=\Omega(0)+\int_{0}^{U}\big[h_{I}(U^{\prime})dU^{\prime}\big]+n[\mu(U)-\mu(0)],

(50)

which recovers the formula in Eq. (
26
) by combining
\Omega(U)=e_{\rm in}+\mu(U)n-T\bm{s}(U)
.

In addition to reproducing the entropy formulas, the expressions in Eqs. (
45
), (
46
), (
48
), and (
50
) can also be used to directly compute the grand potential density
\Omega
. This quantity plays a central role in the thermodynamics of quantum systems, as many commonly used observables can be obtained from its derivatives. In Ref.
65
,
\Omega
is employed as a key quantity to identify possible incommensurate orders in the 3D doped Hubbard model. Therefore, the reliable computation of
\Omega
in the doped Hubbard model can provide additional insights into its finite-temperature properties.

Physically relevant results in the 2D doped Hubbard model

The test results in the previous subsection demonstrate the effectiveness and reliability of our computational framework for evaluating the thermal entropy. We now focus on the physically relevant parameter regime of the 2D doped Hubbard model and elucidate its essential physics based on AFQMC calculations of the entropy. We apply

The most characteristic feature of
\bm{s}
versus

is the peak structure, clearly visible for

and

in Figs.

or

(or doping

) was first reported via Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (DMFT) calculations for the 2D doped Hubbard model in Ref.

, the corresponding filling

at the peak position
\mu_{\rm p}/t

T=0

83
;
57
;
19
. A subsequent DMFT study
80
further confirmed that

exhibits only weak temperature dependence. Later, this peak behavior in
\bm{s}(\mu)
was also observed in optical lattice experiments
10
. In recent years, the ground-state properties of the 2D system have been extensively studied using cutting-edge quantum many-body approaches
63
;
64
;
100
;
62
;
98
. These works suggest that the doping-induced QCP is likely associated with a quantum phase transition between the stripe-ordered phase and disordered metallic phase in the underdoped and overdoped regimes, respectively, of the 2D doped Hubbard model (without next-nearest-neighbor hopping
t^{\prime}
). A similar doping-induced QCP has also been identified in the magnetic phase diagram of the 3D doped Hubbard model
65
;
71
.

Understanding the connection between the peak position of
\bm{s}(\mu)
[or equivalently
\bm{s}(n)
] and the QCP in the doped Hubbard model relies on the Maxwell relations in Eqs. (
34
) and (
30b
). The peak in
\bm{s}(\mu)
at
\mu_{\rm p}/t
implies a corresponding peak in
\bm{s}(n)
at
n_{\rm p}=n(\mu_{\rm p})
, featuring
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial n)_{U,T}=0
at
n=n_{\rm p}
and
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial n)_{U,T}<0
at
n>n_{\rm p}
[and oppositely
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial n)_{U,T}>0
at
n<n_{\rm p}
]. According to Eq. (
34
), these relations translate into

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,n}=0\ \ \ {\rm at}\ \ n=n_{\rm p},

(54a)

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,n}>0\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ n>n_{\rm p},

(54b)

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,n}<0\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ n<n_{\rm p}.

(54c)

The first equality indicates a stationary chemical potential at
n=n_{\rm p}
, which can be interpreted as the signature of local particle-hole symmetry
83
;
57
in analogy to the half-filled case. Such near particle-hole symmetry has indeed been observed in the cuprates around optimal doping
9
. In addition, Eqs. (
54b
) and (
54c
) reveal totally different low-
T
behaviors of
\mu(T)
(at fixed
n
) in the regimes

and

, namely
\mu(T)
decreases or increases, respectively, with decreasing

. From a theoretical perspective
19
, the former [Eq. (
54b
)] conforms with the pseudogap state, which has been shown to evolve continuously into the
T=0
stripe-ordered phase as
T/t
decreases in the 2D doped Hubbard model
75
. And the latter [Eq. (
54c
)] aligns with the Fermi-liquid state. Taken together, these features elucidate that
n_{\rm p}
, the peak filling of
\bm{s}(n)
, evolves into the critical filling at the doping-induced QCP as
T/t
approaches zero. The same conclusion can also be reached via Eq. (
30b
), which instead focuses on the
T
-dependence of the fermion density
n
at fixed
\mu
. The condition
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial\mu)_{U,T}=(\partial n/\partial T)_{U,\mu}=0
at
\mu=\mu_{\rm p}
, corresponding to a stationary
n
with respect to
T
, likewise signals a local particle-hole symmetry. It separates two regimes
\mu<\mu_{\rm p}
(with
n>n_{\rm p}
) and
\mu>\mu_{\rm p}
(with
n<n_{\rm p}
), which feature
(\partial n/\partial T)_{U,\mu}<0
and
(\partial n/\partial T)_{U,\mu}>0
, respectively, according to Eq. (
30b
). These opposite
T
dependences of
n(T)
at fixed
\mu
distinguish two distinct states, namely pseudogap and Fermi liquid, in the low-
T
regime of the doped Hubbard model.

As included in Fig.
9
, we have determined the peak position of
\bm{s}(\mu)
or
\bm{s}(n)
as
(\mu_{\rm p}/t\simeq 0.748,n_{\rm p}\simeq 0.890)
for
(U/t=4,T/t=0.2)
, and
(\mu_{\rm p}/t\simeq 1.417,n_{\rm p}\simeq 0.877)
for
(U/t=6,T/t=0.3)
. At
U/t=6
, our result of
n_{\rm p}
is slightly larger than the DMFT result as

, reported in Ref.
83
;
57
. The difference may be attributed to the residual temperature dependence of
n_{\rm p}
or the approximation introduced in DMFT method. For
U/t=2
, as shown in Fig.
9
(a), the entropy
\bm{s}(\mu)
decreases monotonically with
\mu
, exhibiting no peak. This behavior may arise because the QCP for
U/t=2
lies very close to half filling, and the temperature
T/t=0.2
used here is not low enough to reveal the features discussed above. Besides the peak behavior,
\bm{s}(\mu)
also features an inflection point, corresponding to the point of most rapid increase with
\mu

0<\mu<\mu_{\rm p}
. This is reflected by the equality
(\partial\bm{s}/\partial\mu)_{U,T}=0
at both
\mu=\mu_{\rm p}
and
\mu=0

30b

\bm{s}(\mu)
can be determined by
\partial^{2}\bm{s}(\mu)/\partial\mu^{2}=0

\partial\kappa/\partial T<0
and
\partial\kappa/\partial T>0
, respectively, according to Eq. (
32

51

\bm{s}(\mu)
at
(\mu/t\simeq 0.32,n\simeq 0.959)

(\mu/t\simeq 0.75,n\simeq 0.964)

From the above analysis, the fermion filling (or doping) dependence of the thermal entropy can serve as an effective thermodynamic probe of the doping-induced QCP in the doped Hubbard model, while also providing valuable insights into finite-temperature crossover phenomena. Our calculation scheme, based on the varying-
\mu
path with fixed
U
and
T
via Eq. (
23
), offers a highly efficient approach for mapping out the full landscape of thermal entropy in the temperature-filling plane. This may help elucidate the phase diagram of the doped Hubbard model, a long-standing problem in theoretical condensed matter physics.

VI
Summary and discussion

In summary, we have established a unified and highly efficient framework for computing the thermal entropy in the doped Fermi-Hubbard model. Building on the fundamental quantity of the grand potential, we have achieved four complementary evaluation schemes obtained from two independent theoretical derivations. In these schemes, the entropy is expressed as simple path integrals over temperature, chemical potential, and interaction strength within the parameter space of the model. We have also derived useful Maxwell relations that link the thermal entropy to other thermodynamic quantities. As an early-stage application, we have employed numerically unbiased AFQMC method to systematically test the entropy calculations in the 3D Hubbard model and to perform cross-checks between different evaluation schemes. Furthermore, in the physically relevant parameter regime of the 2D doped Hubbard model, our numerical results show nice agreement with previous DiagMC calculations, while also revealing a deep connection between characteristic features of the entropy landscape and the essential physics of the model.

Our computational framework for the thermal entropy can have broader applications. First, it can be readily applied across a wide range of quantum many-body numerical methods. This is because the entropy admits simple expressions [see Eqs. (
16
), (
18
), (
23
) and (
28
)], and the integrands in these evaluation schemes only involve the total energy, fermion density, and double occupancy, all of which are straightforward to compute in most numerical approaches. Second, the framework is not restricted to the grand canonical ensemble simulations for the Hubbard model. Specifically, the varying-
T
scheme in Sec.
III.1
and the varying-
U
scheme in Sec.
III.4
can be applied to numerical methods working in the canonical ensemble, such as the finite-temperature canonical-ensemble AFQMC method
22
;
36
;
15
;
34
and the minimally entangled typical thermal states method
91
;
92
;
93
. Third, the framework can be further extended to fit into generalized Hubbard models. For example, in the 2D Hubbard model with next-nearest-neighbor hopping
t^{\prime}

43
;
97
;
99
and the 3D Hubbard model with hopping anisotropy in
z
-direction (denoted as
t_{z}
)
41
;
37
, one can follow the formalism in Sec.
III
, add
t^{\prime}
or
t_{z}
as a new natural variable to the grand potential, and reach a similar scheme to calculate the entropy as a function of the variable. Or equivalently, one can set
\alpha=t^{\prime}
(or
\alpha=t_{z}
) in Eq. (
43
) to get the corresponding Hellmann-Feynman relation and then achieve the expression of thermal entropy. Our computational framework, together with these promising generalizations, can help broaden the scope and deepen the study of the doped Hubbard models from a thermodynamic perspective centered on thermal entropy.

For the noninteracting limit (
U=0
) of the model (
1
), we can compute its partition function

\Omega=-N_{s}^{-1}T\ln Z

\displaystyle\Omega=-N_{s}^{-1}T\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}{\rm ln}\big[1+e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}+\mu)}\big],

(55)

where
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}
is the kinetic energy dispersion, with its specific expression in both 2D and 3D given in Sec.
II.1
. Then the entropy density follows from the thermodynamic relation

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\Big(\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu}

(56)

\displaystyle=-N_{s}^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\big[f_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\ln f_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}+(1-f_{\mathbf{k}\sigma})\ln(1-f_{\mathbf{k}\sigma})\big],

where
f_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}=1/[e^{\beta(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}+\mu)}+1]
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution. The equality can be simplified to Eq. (
25
) considering
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}=\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k},\downarrow}
in the model (
1
).

For the system at
T=\infty
with fixed
U
and

, we can compute its entropy density via the result in Eq. (
56
). At fixed

, the corresponding
\mu(T)
can be obtained via solving the equation

\displaystyle n=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}f_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\!\left[\mu(T)\right].

(57)

Then substituting the resulting

into Eq. (
56
) yields the exact noninteracting entropy. At

(and
\beta=0
), any finite

becomes irrelevant because it only enters into the calculations via
\beta U
which is also zero. Therefore, the

is identical to that of the noninteracting system, provided that the correct

is used to enforce the desired density. Starting from the Fermi-Dirac distribution,

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}(\mu)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}+\mu)}+1},

(58)

we note that, as
\beta\to 0
the dispersion becomes negligible in the exponent, the occupation (function value) approaches a

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}(\mu)\xrightarrow[\beta\to 0]{}\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta\mu}}.

(59)

Then the fixed-

\displaystyle n=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}f_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\xrightarrow[\beta\to 0]{}\frac{2}{1+e^{\beta\mu}},

(60)

which yields

\displaystyle\beta\mu\xrightarrow[\beta\to 0]{}\ln\!\left(\frac{n}{2-n}\right),\hskip 8.5359pt\mu(T)\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}T\ln\!\left(\frac{n}{2-n}\right).

(61)

This expression explicitly shows that

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{n}(\infty)

\displaystyle=-2\Big[\frac{n}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{n}{2}\right)\ln\!\left(1-\frac{n}{2}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=\ln 4-n\ln n-(2-n)\ln(2-n),

which is the result mentioned in Sec.
III.1
.

) in the main text. First, with tuning

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,\mu}\differential U+\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{T,U}\differential\mu

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,\mu}\differential U+\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{T,U}\Big(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,n}\differential U,

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,n}=\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,\mu}+\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{T,U}\Big(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,n}.

Using the equalities in Eqs. (

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,n}=-\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu}+\Big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu}\frac{\big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial U}\big)_{T,\mu}}{\big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\big)_{T,U}}.

(65)

Similarly, when tuning

at fixed

and

, the interaction energy density

\displaystyle\differential h_{I}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu}\differential T+\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{T,U}\differential\mu

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu}\differential T+\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{T,U}\Big(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,n}\differential T,

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,n}=\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu}+\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial\mu}\Big)_{T,U}\Big(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,n}.

(67)

Using the equalities in Eqs. (
30c
) and (
34
), the above equality can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,n}=\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial T}\Big)_{U,\mu}-\Big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial U}\Big)_{T,\mu}\frac{\big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}\big)_{U,\mu}}{\big(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\big)_{U,T}}.

(68)

Then by comparing Eqs. (
65

), the following equality is evident as

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial U}\Big)_{n,T}=-\Big(\frac{\partial h_{I}}{\partial T}\Big)_{n,U}=-\Big(\frac{\partial D}{\partial T}\Big)_{n,U},

(69)

in which the relation
h_{I}=D-n/2
is used in the second equality. This is the Maxwell relation in Eq. (
37
).

The proof of Eq. (
51
) involves the atomic limit under the condition
\beta U\ll 1
. Under this limit, the local Hamiltonian can be solved exactly, which allows us to derive the expansion of the double occupancy at fixed filling
n
. The atomic-limit Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{h}=U\hat{n}_{\uparrow}\hat{n}_{\downarrow}+\mu(\hat{n}_{\uparrow}+\hat{n}_{\downarrow}),

(70)

with partition function

\displaystyle Z=1+2e^{-\beta\mu}+e^{-\beta(U+2\mu)}.

(71)

The filling
n
and the double occupancy
D
computed as

\displaystyle n=\frac{2e^{-\beta\mu}+2e^{-\beta(U+2\mu)}}{Z},\quad D=\frac{e^{-\beta(U+2\mu)}}{Z}.

(72)

Therefore, we can reach

\displaystyle\frac{D}{n}=\frac{1}{2\left[1+e^{\beta(U+\mu)}\right]}.

(73)

From the expression of
n
in Eq. (
72
), one can also find

\displaystyle(2-n)e^{-\beta U}e^{-2\beta\mu}+2(1-n)e^{-\beta\mu}-n=0.

(74)

Solving this quadratic equation for
e^{-\beta\mu}
presents

\displaystyle e^{-\beta\mu}=\frac{n-1+\Delta}{e^{-\beta U}(2-n)},

(75)

where
\Delta=\sqrt{(n-1)^{2}-e^{-\beta U}n(n-2)}
. By substituting Eq. (
75
) into Eq. (
73
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{D}{n}=\frac{1}{2}\Big[1+\frac{2-n}{n-1+\Delta}\Big]^{-1}.

(76)

Now we expand this expression at the limit
\beta U\to 0
.
Using the relation
e^{-\beta U}=1-\beta U+\mathcal{O}[(\beta U)^{2}]
,
we have

\displaystyle\Delta=1+\frac{1}{2}\beta U\,n(n-2)+\mathcal{O}\!\left[(\beta U)^{2}\right].

(77)

Thus, the right side of Eq. (
76
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle\frac{2-n}{n-1+\Delta}=\frac{2-n}{n}+\frac{(2-n)^{2}}{2n}\,\beta U+\mathcal{O}\!\left[(\beta U)^{2}\right].

(78)

By substituting Eq. (
78
) into Eq. (
76
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{D}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\frac{2}{n}+\frac{(2-n)^{2}}{2n}\,\beta U\Big]^{-1}+\mathcal{O}[(\beta U)^{2}]

(79)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{4}\Big[1+\frac{(2-n)^{2}}{4}\beta U\Big]^{-1}+\mathcal{O}[(\beta U)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{4}-\frac{n(2-n)^{2}}{16}\,\beta U+\mathcal{O}[(\beta U)^{2}],

in which the last equality is Eq. (
51
). Besides, we also briefly discuss the fermion density at fixed
\mu
under the atomic limit. For this case, both
\beta U
and
\beta\mu
are small numbers, and the expression of
n
in Eq. (
72
) can be expanded as

\displaystyle n=1-\frac{\beta U}{4}-\frac{\beta\mu}{2}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{2}).

(80)

Since
\beta\mu=(\mu/U)\beta U
, the above result can be rewritten as

n=1-\frac{1+2\mu/U}{4}\,\beta U+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{2}).

(81)

This means that, at fixed (and finite)
\mu
, the filling
n
generally approaches
n=1
in the
T\to\infty
limit, with the leading correction determined by
\mu/U
and
\beta U
.
