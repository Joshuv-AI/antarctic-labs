Exploring the relationship between social media use, eating behaviors, and depression in young women: a machine learning-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Murat Altan, Şemsi Gül Yılmaz Kocaman, Taha Gökmen Ülger, Seda Önal, Hasan Yıldırım
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing prevalence of social media use among young adults has raised concerns regarding its potential associations with mental health and eating behaviors. The aim of this study was to investigate the associations between social media use, eating behaviors, and depressive symptoms among young women and to evaluate the performance of machine learning algorithms in modeling these cross-sectional associations compared with conventional regression approaches.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among young women aged 18–34 years (n = 4,692) using a multi-site, face-to-face convenience sampling strategy. The survey collected data on BMI, patterns of social media use, the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), and the Scale of Effects of Social Media on Eating Behavior (SESMEB). In addition to standard statistical analyses, various ML models, including Random Forest and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), were applied to model SESMEB and BDI scores. Model performance was evaluated using internal validation procedures, including a train–test split and cross-validation, and assessed using root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE).ResultsThe mean age of participants was 23.8 years, and the mean BMI was 22.3 kg/m². Significant differences in BMI, SESMEB, and BDI scores were observed across social media platform use groups. Users of Facebook, TikTok, and Snapchat reported significantly lower SESMEB scores (p < 0.001). ML models identified BMI, snack intake, and social media use as influential variables associated with SESMEB and BDI scores. Random Forest showed the best modeling performance in the test set for both SESMEB and BDI scores, with XGBoost performing comparably.ConclusionsSocial media use was associated with eating-related behaviors and depressive symptoms in young women. Within the study sample, ML models demonstrated modest modeling performance for SESMEB and BDI scores and may provide a complementary framework for examining complex associations among behavioral and psychological variables. However, these findings were based on internal validation only and require confirmation in independent external samples. Further research is warranted to clarify the direction of these associations, explore platform-specific differences, and evaluate the external validity of these models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28138-x.Keywords: Social media, Eating behavior, Beck Depression Inventory, Body mass index, Machine learning, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Social media refers to internet-based digital platforms that enable users to create, share and access while facilitating social interaction, networking, and participation in virtual communities [1, 2]. As of 2024, approximately 62.3% of the global population has a social media account, with average daily usage reaching 143 min among internet users worldwide [3]. The prevalence of social media use is especially high among adolescents and young adults. This widespread adoption has been associated with notable changes in lifestyle behaviors, including increased engagement in unhealthy eating patterns, disordered eating behaviors, and poorer psychological well-being among adolescents and young adults [4–7].

Recent studies indicate that social media platforms are increasingly utilized to market unhealthy food and beverages. In addition, the widespread exposure to fitness models and idealized body images on these platforms has been associated with altered food preferences, irregular eating behaviors, and the pursuit of scientifically unsubstantiated dietary practices [6, 8]. Social media use has also been identified as a potential factor associated with eating behavior disorders [9]. Nevertheless, the relationship between social media use and eating-related attitudes and behaviors appears to be multifaceted, and the direction and clinical implications of these associations remain incompletely understood.

Beyond eating-related outcomes, growing evidence suggests that social media use is associated with adverse mental health outcomes, particularly among adolescents and young adults. Among adolescents and young adults, mental disorders, particularly anxiety and depression, have been increasingly rising, with a marked acceleration observed in recent years. According to 2021 data, the prevalence of major depressive episodes among adults was highest in young adults (18.6%), with the prevalence in females being higher than that in males [10–12].

Importantly, accumulating evidence suggests that the type of social media platform used, rather than social media use alone, may differentially influence eating behaviors and mental health outcomes [13, 14]. Understanding platform-specific risks is therefore particularly important among young adult females, who may be more vulnerable to body image concerns, disordered eating behaviors, and depressive symptoms associated with online social environments.

Although the relationship between social media use, eating behaviors, and mental health has been widely investigated, most previous studies have primarily relied on conventional statistical approaches such as correlation, regression, or mediation analyses [5, 9, 13–18]. These studies have provided important evidence regarding the associations between social media exposure, body image concerns, depressive symptoms, and eating-related behaviors; however, the complex and potentially non-linear interactions among these variables may not be fully captured by traditional analytical methods alone.

In recent years, machine learning approaches have increasingly been applied to nutrition, behavioral, and health-related data to model multidimensional patterns and improve data-driven estimation [19–23]. Nevertheless, studies integrating machine learning techniques into the investigation of social media use, eating-related behaviors, and depressive symptoms remain limited, particularly among young adult women. In addition, platform-specific associations and the comparative performance of machine learning algorithms relative to conventional regression approaches have not been sufficiently explored in this context.

Based on these considerations, the present study aimed to investigate the associations between social media use, disordered eating behaviors, and depressive symptoms among young women. In addition, machine learning algorithms were employed to model these cross-sectional associations and to compare their modeling performance with conventional ordinary least-squares regression approaches. The study was guided by the following hypotheses: (i) social media use would be associated with increased disordered eating behaviors and depressive symptoms, and (ii) machine learning algorithms would demonstrate improved modeling performance compared with traditional regression-based approaches on a held-out test set.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted on a convenience sample of young women aged 18–34 years. Data were collected between May 2024 and February 2025 in Elazığ and Karaman, Türkiye, using a multi-site, face-to-face recruitment strategy. Eligible participants were approached in person by four trained researchers in high-traffic university and community settings frequently attended by young women, including Karamanoğlu Mehmetbey University, Fırat University, and other community-based locations. All individuals were screened for eligibility prior to enrolment. Prior to participation, all respondents were informed about the study’s objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Social and Human Sciences, Karamanoğlu Mehmetbey University (Decision No: 02-2024/38; approval date: 23/01/2024). The official ethics approval document has been provided as supplementary material (Additional File 1).

A total of 5,580 individuals were approached for participation. Of these, 5,164 agreed to participate and 4,910 completed the survey, corresponding to an agreement rate of 92.5%, a completion rate of 95.1% among those who agreed, and an overall response rate of 88.0%.

Participants with a history of severe psychiatric disorders (e.g., psychotic disorder, bipolar disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder), neurodegenerative conditions, antidepressant use, or bulimia were excluded in order to minimize potential confounding related to clinically diagnosed psychiatric or eating disorders. After exclusion of 218 participants who met predefined exclusion criteria and/or had incomplete key data, the final analytic sample consisted of 4,692 young women. A STROBE-aligned participant flow diagram summarizing the recruitment process is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Missing data were handled using a complete-case approach. Participants with missing values in variables required for the primary statistical and machine learning analyses were excluded prior to analysis. Because missingness within the cleaned analytic dataset was minimal, additional sensitivity analyses using imputation approaches yielded results that were highly consistent with the complete-case analyses. However, the exact distribution of exclusions attributable to predefined clinical criteria versus missing key data could not be fully reconstructed retrospectively from the cleaned analytic dataset and is acknowledged as a study limitation.

The survey instrument, developed by the researchers, included demographic items (e.g., age, gender), objectively measured height and weight for body mass index (BMI) calculation, social media usage patterns, eating habits, the Scale of Effects of Social Media on Eating Behavior (SESMEB), and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). Height and weight were measured by trained researchers using standardized procedures, and BMI was calculated accordingly. Participants were classified based on the World Health Organization standards: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²). Educational attainment was the only socioeconomic indicator collected and was used as a partial proxy for socioeconomic status; household income, parental education, and occupational status were not assessed. Social media platform use was assessed as a multi-select binary item across seven platforms (Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, YouTube, X (formerly Twitter), Pinterest, and Snapchat). Daily time spent on social media was self-reported in five categories (0–30 min, 30 min–1 h, 1–2 h, 2–4 h, and ≥ 4 h). Weekly frequency of social media visits was self-reported in four categories (0–8, 9–30, 31–57, and ≥ 58 visits per week).

Measurements

The Scale of Effects of Social Media on Eating Behavior (SESMEB)

The SESMEB was developed by Keser et al. (2020) to assess the impact of social media on eating behavior in university students. It is a 5-point Likert-type scale consisting of 18 items, scored from 1 (never) to 5 (always). No reverse-coded items are included. Total scores range from 18 to 90, with higher scores indicating a greater influence of social media on eating behavior [24]. Importantly, higher SESMEB scores do not necessarily indicate clinically disordered eating or uniformly negative eating behaviors; rather, they reflect a greater reported association between social media exposure and eating-related attitudes or behaviors. The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.928).

Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)

The BDI is a self-report measure developed by Beck et al. (1961) and revised in 1996 to assess the severity of depressive symptoms in adults [25, 26]. It includes 21 items, each rated on a scale from 0 to 3. Total scores range from 0 to 63, with interpretive cutoffs as follows: 0–13 (minimal), 14–19 (mild), 20–28 (moderate), and 29 and above (severe depression) [26]. The scale has been validated in Turkish by Hisli (1989) and further supported by Kapçı et al. (2008) for use in the Turkish population [27, 28]. Throughout this study, BDI scores are interpreted as a continuous measure of self-reported depressive symptom severity rather than as a clinical diagnosis of depression.

Permission to use the BDI for this study was obtained from the copyright holder prior to data collection. Permission to use the Scale of Effects of Social Media on Eating Behavior was obtained from the original developers of the scale prior to study initiation. Supporting documentation related to these permissions has been submitted as supplementary material (Additional File 2 and Additional File 3).

Data analysis and statistical process

The data analysis comprised two main phases: traditional statistical analyses and machine learning modeling. The analysis was structured around two distinct objectives: (i) an epidemiological aim of estimating cross-sectional associations between social media use, eating behavior, and depressive symptoms (addressed via inferential statistical tests reported in Tables 2, 3 and 4); and (ii) a model-based aim of comparing the internal cross-validated and held-out test-set performance of machine learning algorithms against traditional regression baselines (addressed in Tables 5, 6 and 7). Because the data are cross-sectional, machine learning outputs reflect estimation and data-fitting rather than true out-of-sample forecasting; we therefore use the term “predictive performance” in the methodological-evaluation sense (RMSE, MAE on a held-out subset of the same dataset).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons were conducted using parametric tests (independent samples t-test, one-way ANOVA) and non-parametric equivalents (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis H). Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were used for associations between continuous variables, while Chi-square tests evaluated relationships among categorical variables. Post-hoc analyses employed Tukey or Games-Howell tests as appropriate. The choice between parametric and non-parametric procedures was guided by both formal distributional tests (Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov for normality, Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances) and inspection of skewness and kurtosis. Given the large analytic sample size (n = 4,692), the central limit theorem justifies the predominant use of parametric tests (independent-samples t-test and one-way ANOVA) for group-mean comparisons; non-parametric equivalents (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis H) were employed as distributional sensitivity checks where appropriate. Welch’s correction with Games-Howell post-hoc tests was used when Levene’s test indicated unequal variances, and Tukey HSD post-hoc tests were used otherwise.

Machine learning analysis

Machine learning models were developed to estimate SESMEB and BDI scores. The evaluated algorithms included bagging-based approaches (Classification and Regression Trees [CART], Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines [MARS], and Multilayer Perceptron [MLP]); boosting-based approaches (Light Gradient Boosting Machine [LightGBM]and Extreme Gradient Boosting [XGBoost]); and additional algorithms including Bayesian Additive Trees (BAT), Elastic Net, k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Lasso, Linear Regression, Partial Least Squares (PLS), Random Forest, RuleFit, and Support Vector Machines (linear, polynomial, and radial kernels).

Predictor variables included demographic and anthropometric characteristics (age, BMI, and educational attainment), social media exposure variables (platform use, daily time spent on social media, and weekly visit frequency), self-rated mental and emotional health status, dietary behavior variables, and self-reported nutrition-information sources. Categorical variables were converted into indicator variables using one-hot encoding prior to modelling. A complete list and description of predictor variables is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Although BDI scores are commonly interpreted using clinical severity categories, BDI and SESMEB scores were analysed as continuous outcomes in order to preserve statistical power and retain the full variability of the scales.

A NULL model representing intercept-only baseline performance was included for comparison. Prior to modelling, continuous variables were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1). The dataset was divided into training (75%) and test (25%) sets. Optimal model parameters were selected using repeated ten-fold cross-validation. Model performance on the test set was evaluated using Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). Variable-importance plots and predicted-versus-observed scatter plots were generated for the best-performing models.

To address the second hypothesis, the performance of the ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression model was compared with that of the machine learning algorithms using test-set RMSE and MAE values. The practical interpretation of these differences in relation to the SESMEB and BDI score ranges is discussed in the Discussion section.

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.2) and MedCalc (version 22).

The mean BDI score was 16.6 (± 10.60), and the mean SESMEB score was 72.3 (± 11.95).

Table 2 presents the statistical differences in BMI, BDI scores, and SESMEB scores based on the social media platforms used by the participants. Results indicated that Facebook users had a significantly higher mean age than non-users (p < 0.001). Similarly, participants who did not use TikTok, YouTube, X, Pinterest, or Snapchat were older than those who did (p < 0.001).

Regarding BMI, significant differences were observed between users and non-users of Facebook, X, and Snapchat (p < 0.001), as well as TikTok (p = 0.010), YouTube (p = 0.045), and Pinterest (p = 0.002), whereas no significant differences were found for Instagram (p > 0.05).

In terms of depression, non-users of Instagram had significantly higher BDI scores than Instagram users (p = 0.002), TikTok users had significantly higher BDI scores than non-users (p = 0.024), and non-users of YouTube had significantly higher BDI scores than YouTube users (p = 0.032). Additionally, SESMEB scores were significantly lower among users of Facebook, TikTok, and Snapchat compared to non-users (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were found for other platforms (p > 0.05).

Table 3 illustrates the statistical differences in BMI, BDI scores, and SESMEB scores according to education level, time spent on social media, frequency of social media use, perceived mental and emotional health, number of daily meals, meal skipping, and snack consumption.

Participants’ mean age significantly differed by education level (p < 0.001) and decreased with increasing social media use (both frequency and duration) (p < 0.001). Similarly, participants with poorer mental and emotional health and higher snack consumption tended to be younger (p < 0.001).

Regarding BDI scores, significant differences were observed based on education level, social media use time, mental and emotional health status, number of daily meals, and snack consumption (p < 0.001 for all). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is among the few studies investigating the relationships between social media use, eating behaviors, and depression using machine learning approaches. The findings provide additional insight into the associations between social media use, eating behaviors, depressive symptoms, and BMI in a large convenience sample of young adult women. Furthermore, the study identified associations between the use of specific social media platforms and several health-related variables, including BMI, BDI, and SESMEB scores.

Patterns of social media use were found to be significantly associated with age, BMI, and mental health status. Facebook users were generally older than non-users, while individuals who did not use platforms such as TikTok, YouTube, X, Pinterest, and Snapchat were also older than those who did. These results support the notion of age-stratified platform preference, wherein younger individuals are more inclined to engage with newer and more interactive platforms like TikTok, while older users gravitate toward more established platforms such as Facebook. These findings are in line with previous literature, including Perrin’s “Social Media Usage” report [29].

Significant differences in BMI were also observed between users and non-users of certain platforms. Specifically, individuals using Facebook, X, and Snapchat exhibited different average BMI values compared to non-users. These discrepancies may reflect differences in lifestyle habits related to specific platforms. For example, earlier studies have suggested that excessive use of particular social media sites may promote sedentary behaviors and increased snacking, which in turn contribute to elevated BMI levels [30]. In contrast, no significant BMI differences were observed for Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Pinterest users. This suggests that the association between social media use and BMI may be moderated by the nature of platform engagement, content exposure, or individual differences in user behavior. Prior research supports this interpretation, reporting that extended social media use is associated with prolonged sedentary behavior and increased BMI [31].

In terms of depressive symptoms, non-users of Instagram reported significantly higher BDI scores than users. One possible explanation is that Instagram use may be related to different patterns of social interaction or participant characteristics. However, because of the cross-sectional nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted with caution and do not imply a causal relationship. Previous studies have also reported associations between social media use and depressive symptoms, although the direction of this relationship remains unclear [15–18].

Focusing specifically on young adult women may be particularly relevant, as previous studies have suggested that females are more susceptible to body image concerns, depressive symptoms, and disordered eating behaviors associated with social media exposure than males. Studies conducted in adolescent and mixed-gender populations have similarly reported associations between social media use, body dissatisfaction, depressive symptoms, and problematic eating-related behaviors [5, 9, 13, 15–18]. However, these associations are often reported to be more pronounced among young women, possibly due to greater exposure to appearance-oriented content and higher levels of social comparison related to body image [32]. In this respect, the present findings appear to be consistent with previous literature while contributing additional evidence from a female-only young adult sample.

Lower SESMEB scores observed among users of Facebook, TikTok, and Snapchat indicate that the relationship between platform use and SESMEB is not necessarily linear or uniformly adverse. Because SESMEB reflects the reported degree of association between social media exposure and eating-related behaviors rather than clinically disordered eating per se, lower scores should not be interpreted as inherently protective or beneficial. These findings may instead reflect differences in patterns of platform engagement, content exposure, or user characteristics across social media platforms. Previous studies have similarly suggested that the relationship between social media exposure and eating-related behaviors may vary according to platform characteristics, user motivations, and patterns of interaction [33].

In addition to platform-specific analyses, the study explored how demographic and behavioral factors intersect with psychological outcomes. Age was negatively correlated with time spent on social media, frequency of snacking, and poor self-perceived mental and emotional health. These results reflect broader developmental patterns, whereby younger individuals are more digitally engaged and potentially more exposed to social media-related differences in mental health and eating-related behaviors.

Further associations were observed between dietary behavior and mental health. A higher number of daily meals was associated with lower depression risk, while increased snacking was associated with lower SESMEB scores. Notably, prolonged social media use was associated with higher depression scores. Interestingly, however, SESMEB scores demonstrated a negative association with duration of social media use. This pattern may indicate that the relationship between social media exposure and eating-related behaviors is not linear within this sample. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and requires further investigation in longitudinal studies. Among the evaluated platforms, Facebook, TikTok, and Snapchat showed the strongest associations with SESMEB scores. A systematic review involving 1,225 adolescents similarly reported that Instagram and Facebook were among the platforms most frequently associated with both healthy and unhealthy eating behaviors [34].

BDI scores were significantly associated with education level, social media usage time, mental and emotional health status, meal frequency, and snack consumption. The highest depression scores were observed among individuals with lower education levels, those eating fewer than three meals per day, and those spending more than four hours on social media. These findings are consistent with previous literature [15–17] and highlight the importance of considering psychosocial and lifestyle variables in understanding the mental health correlates of social media.

Beyond statistical analysis, this study utilized machine learning (ML) techniques to model BDI and SESMEB scores. ML has recently emerged as a useful tool in public health and nutrition research for analyzing complex and multidimensional datasets. Traditional nutritional epidemiology, which often relies on self-reported data, faces challenges such as recall bias and underreporting. In this context, ML approaches may provide a complementary framework for examining complex relationships among behavioral and health-related variables [19, 20]. However, the modeling performance observed in the present study was based on internal validation only and should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed in independent external samples.

Algorithms such as decision trees, Random Forest, and deep neural networks have been applied in studies examining various health-related outcomes, including weight change, glucose response, and cardiovascular risk [32]. More recent work using gradient boosting and support vector machines has demonstrated the potential of ML approaches for modeling postprandial glycemic responses within precision nutrition research [22, 23].

In this study, Random Forest showed the best modeling performance in the test set for both SESMEB and BDI scores within the current dataset, with XGBoost performing comparably. Feature importance analysis identified BMI, snacking, and the use of X, TikTok, and Facebook among the variables most strongly associated with SESMEB scores. For BDI scores, BMI, mental and emotional health status, and age emerged as the most influential variables. These findings suggest that ML models may help characterize the complex interplay between social media use, mental health, and dietary behavior in young women. These findings should be interpreted within the context of internal validation and may not be directly generalizable to other populations without external validation.

Although these models showed promising performance, their interpretability remains limited because of their black-box nature. Future studies may therefore benefit from explainable artificial intelligence techniques such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) or LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), which can help clarify how individual variables contribute to model predictions. Incorporating such approaches may strengthen the interpretability and practical relevance of machine learning models in similar public health contexts.

Beyond the individual top-ranked variables, the variable importance patterns observed in Figs. 1 and 2 cluster into three broad domains: (i) anthropometric and demographic variables (BMI, age), which appeared among the top variables for both outcomes; (ii) social media exposure variables (binary use of X, TikTok, and Facebook for SESMEB; broader exposure indicators for BDI), reflecting the platform-level associations described in the Results; and (iii) dietary and lifestyle variables (snack consumption, number of meals), which contributed predominantly to SESMEB. For BDI, self-rated mental and emotional health status emerged as a particularly strong predictor, consistent with its conventional inferential association reported in Table 3. The clustering of top variables into these three domains suggests that downstream interpretation, intervention design, and confounder adjustment should consider these axes jointly rather than treating individual platforms as isolated risk factors.

It is important to interpret these gains in absolute terms relative to the underlying scale ranges. The SESMEB scale spans 72 points (18–90), and the best-performing model (Random Forest) reduced test-set RMSE from 13.074 (intercept-only baseline) to 12.043, a difference of 1.031 points (approximately 1.4% of the scale range). Similarly, for the BDI (63-point range, 0–63), the test-set RMSE decreased from 10.261 to 9.574, a difference of 0.687 points (approximately 1.1% of the scale range). These improvements are statistically detectable but are modest in practical terms; they should not be over-interpreted as substantial predictive gains, and they do not, by themselves, support claims of clinically actionable individual-level prediction.

Limitations and future directions

The study has several strengths, including its focus on a timely and relevant public health topic linking social media use, eating behaviors, and mental health. The large sample size (n = 4,692) enhanced statistical power and strengthened the robustness of the findings. In addition, the integration of traditional statistical analyses with multiple machine learning models, together with the use of internal validation procedures, contributed to the methodological rigor of the study.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and does not allow the directionality of associations between social media use and mental or behavioral outcomes to be disentangled. For example, depressive symptoms may both influence and be influenced by patterns of social media engagement. Such reciprocal relationships could not be statistically evaluated within the present study design, and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these associations.

Second, although face-to-face data collection may have improved data completeness and consistency, the reliance on self-reported measures (e.g., social media use and snack consumption) may still have introduced reporting bias. In addition, the study evaluated social media platform use in general terms and did not assess the specific type or content of social media exposure (e.g., fitness-, appearance-, or diet-related content), which may differ substantially in its associations with eating behaviors and mental health outcomes.

Third, participants were recruited using a convenience-based, face-to-face sampling strategy from specific university and community settings, and the sample consisted solely of young adult women. Therefore, the findings should not be generalized to the broader population of young women in Türkiye without caution.

Fourth, several socioeconomic variables, including household income, parental education, and occupational status, were not collected. Because socioeconomic factors are closely associated with social media engagement, dietary patterns, and depressive symptoms, the absence of these variables may have introduced residual confounding in the reported associations and machine learning estimates.

Finally, although the machine learning models demonstrated modest predictive performance gains over baseline models, no external validation was performed using an independent dataset. Therefore, the reported model performance should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed in external populations. In addition, as with many predictive models, performance estimates may be influenced by feature selection and the possibility of overfitting despite the internal validation procedures used.

Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to explore causal pathways and evaluate potential intervention strategies. External validation using independent and more diverse samples is also needed to assess the robustness and generalizability of the proposed models. Incorporating objective behavioral tracking data, such as screen-time analytics or wearable-based dietary monitoring, may further strengthen future analyses. Expanding the study to include broader demographic groups and additional social media platforms may also provide deeper insight into platform-specific health-related differences.

Conclusion

By addressing the intersection of digital behavior and health, this study provides new insights into how social media platforms may be associated with psychological well-being and eating patterns in young women. The findings indicated that users of Facebook, TikTok, and Snapchat tended to report lower SESMEB scores, whereas Instagram users reported comparatively lower depressive symptom scores. In addition, greater time spent on social media was associated with higher depression scores and lower SESMEB scores, suggesting that the relationship between social media use and eating-related behaviors may vary across different patterns of use.

These findings highlight the importance of raising awareness about the associations between excessive social media use and adverse mental health outcomes, particularly among young women and university-aged populations. Public health strategies may benefit from considering not only the frequency and duration of social media use but also the nature of platform engagement and content exposure.

From a methodological perspective, the machine learning algorithms used in this study (namely XGBoost and Random Forest) showed modest gains over baseline (intercept-only) models in predicting SESMEB and BDI scores within the study sample. The models identified several potentially important predictors, including BMI, frequency of social media use, and snack consumption. However, as model performance was evaluated using internal validation procedures only, these findings should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed in independent external samples.

To further improve the interpretability of such models, future studies may incorporate explainable artificial intelligence approaches such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) or LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations). These approaches may help clarify how specific variables contribute to predictions related to mental health and eating behaviors.

In summary, this study highlights the relationship between social media use and psychological and eating-related outcomes, while also illustrating the possible applicability of machine learning as an analytical tool for exploring complex data patterns. Future research should further examine the direction and underlying mechanisms of these associations, validate the models in independent populations, and explore how AI-based approaches may, following rigorous external validation, contribute to future health research and intervention planning for young women.
