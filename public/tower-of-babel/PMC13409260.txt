Defect and interface engineering of visible-light-active semiconductor nanomaterials for sustainable photocatalytic pathogen inactivation
Authors: Viraj Pasindu, Imalka Munaweera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance and the presence of potent microbial entities in water and biological matrices have been major challenges to traditional chemical and thermal disinfection techniques. Although semiconductor-based photocatalysis is a promising and non-invasive oxidative technology, the use of pure photocatalysts is often restricted by their large optical bandgaps and high rates of charge-carrier recombination. The use of photocatalysis for environmental remediation is widely reviewed in the current literature, but the specific design criteria for such materials to effectively counteract complex biological entities are not well represented. In this review, we discuss the importance of band engineering as a critical mechanism to ensure that the redox potentials of semiconductor materials are aligned to the specific reactive oxygen species required to effectively counteract potent microbial entities such as biofilms and viruses. We critically discuss specific strategies to modulate the electronic density of states to improve the optical properties of semiconductor materials while maintaining their high redox potentials for ensuring biosafety. This review is a critical amalgamation of solid-state physics and microbiology to define the thermodynamics and kinetics required to develop targeted and highly potent photocatalytic materials for water purification, self-sterilizing surfaces and wearable devices.

Introduction

The growing need for advanced disinfection technologies

The mitigation of pathogenic threats in both clinical and environmental settings requires continuous advancements in disinfection technology. We are currently facing a “silent pandemic” of antimicrobial resistance, driven by a perfect storm of environmental shifts and microbial evolution. This urgency is further increased by the close link between climate change and infectious diseases. Rising global temperatures and more frequent extreme weather events support pathogen survival and spread, allowing vector-borne and waterborne diseases to expand to regions including urban areas that were previously considered to be in low risk.1 The ecological consequences of inadequately treated wastewater extend far beyond immediate public health risks, posing a systemic and cascading threat to aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems. Wastewater effluents discharged from hospitals, livestock operations, and municipal treatment plants serve as primary conduits for the release of antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) bacteria and their associated resistance genes into natural water bodies, where conventional treatment infrastructures designed to reduce bulk organic load rather than eliminate molecular-scale genetic determinants provide insufficient barriers.1 Once introduced into river systems, estuaries, and coastal zones, these resistant organisms integrate into resident aquatic microbiomes, horizontally transferring resistance genes to previously susceptible environmental bacteria through conjugation, transformation, and transduction, thereby amplifying the resistance far beyond the original point of discharge. Ecological disruption does not terminate at the microbial level; bioaccumulation through the aquatic food chain introduces AMR bacteria and residual antibiotic compounds into invertebrates, fish, and ultimately into terrestrial predators and human food supplies, closing a dangerous epidemiological loop between environmental contamination and clinical medicine. This interconnected pathway from wastewater effluents to ecosystem dysbiosis and human re-exposure underscores the urgent need for disinfection technologies capable of operating at the molecular level for simultaneously neutralizing both viable pathogens and the genetic determinants of resistance, rather than merely reducing viable cell counts.1

The disinfection technologies we rely on today were built for an older world with fewer biological risks, and they are becoming insufficient as climate change and population growth create new and larger microbial threats. The integration of materials chemistry and microbiology, particularly through band structure engineering, offers a promising approach to address these challenges. By carefully tuning the electronic properties of solid-state photocatalysts, as illustrated in Fig. 1, it becomes possible to move beyond basic microbial inactivation toward smarter, energy-efficient biosafety solutions that align with environmental sustainability goals. This review distinctively focuses on the structural-electronic requirements for photocatalytic biosafety, analyzing how specific band alignments dictate the targeted destruction of complex biological entities.

Limitations of conventional disinfection

While chemical disinfectants (e.g., chlorine, ozone, and quaternary ammonium compounds) and thermal sterilization have historically served as the bedrock of public health, they face increasing scrutiny regarding their long-term efficacy and environmental footprint. A primary limitation is their inability to effectively combat biofilm-associated infections, which are particularly challenging due to the complex structure of biofilms. Encased in a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, these microbial communities can exhibit significantly higher phenotypic resistance than their free-floating, planktonic counterparts.2 The EPS matrix acts as a physical diffusion barrier, neutralizing chemical agents before they reach the target cells, while metabolic dormancy within the biofilm core renders antibiotics and disinfectants largely ineffective.3

Furthermore, the widespread use of aggressive chemical disinfectants often leads to the formation of toxic disinfection byproducts (DBPs), including trihalomethanes and halo acetic acids, which pose secondary health risks to humans and aquatic ecosystems.4 Thermal sterilization, while mechanistically highly effective, requires substantial energy input and is incompatible with heat-sensitive biomedical materials or large-scale environmental deployment. As a result, the dependence on consumable chemicals and high energy inputs makes these conventional approaches both economically and environmentally unsustainable for next-generation biosafety infrastructures.3

Rise of photocatalytic disinfection

To address the operational limitations of traditional methods, semiconductor-driven photocatalysis serves as a viable, non-contact approach for microbial inactivation. Unlike chemical disinfection, which relies on finite consumables, photocatalysis harnesses photonic energy from solar or artificial light and converts it into chemical potential energy. When irradiated with energy exceeding the semiconductor's bandgap (Eg), electron–hole pairs (e−/h+) are generated. These charge carriers migrate to the catalyst surface, driving redox reactions that produce highly reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydroxyl radicals (·OH), superoxide anions (˙O2−), and singlet oxygen (1O2).5

This mechanism offers distinct advantages for biosafety. ROS are potent, non-selective oxidants capable of degrading critical cellular components, such as membranes, cell walls, and genomic material (DNA/RNA), effectively preventing regrowth that is commonly observed with bacteriostatic agents.6 Moreover, photocatalytic processes can achieve the complete mineralization of organic pathogens into harmless water and carbon dioxide, thereby eliminating the risk of secondary pollution.7 Notably, this nonspecific oxidative mechanism is particularly effective against antimicrobial resistance pathogens as it limits the potential for the development of genetic resistance.8

While the photocatalytic literature has expanded considerably in recent years, the majority of existing reviews approach the field through a material-centric or application-centric perspective, cataloguing semiconductor platforms, heterojunction architectures, or disinfection performance metrics without establishing a unified mechanistic framework that directly connects electronic band structure to the structural vulnerabilities of specific pathogens. Comprehensive reviews, such as that by Habibi-Yangjeh et al. and the landmark overview by Ding et al. in the Chemical Reviews, have made substantial contributions by systematically mapping photocatalytic antimicrobial principles and design strategies across diverse material classes; similarly, Liu et al. have recently surveyed modification strategies including heterojunction construction, defect engineering, and heteroatom doping for environmental disinfection systems.6 However, these works largely treat ROS generation as a generalized oxidative output, without interrogating how the specific thermodynamic profile of the ROS cocktail dictated by precise band edge positions must be tailored to match the distinct structural defenses of the target biological entity, whether that is the lipopolysaccharide-fortified outer membrane of a Gram-negative bacterium, the thick peptidoglycan mesh of a Gram-positive spore, the extracellular polymeric substance matrix of a mature biofilm, or the protein capsid of a non-enveloped virus.9 The present review addresses this gap by adopting an explicitly structure-guided design philosophy, systematically analyzing how band structure engineering strategies, including elemental doping, defect and vacancy control, heterojunction formation, and optical enhancement, can be deliberately deployed to not only enhance photocatalytic activity in general terms but also generate the specific ROS profile, surface charge geometry, and charge-transfer kinetics demanded by the target pathogen's architecture. In doing so, this review aims to provide the materials science community with a translational framework that bridges solid-state electronic engineering and molecular microbiology, offering actionable design criteria for the next generation of precision photocatalytic biosafety platforms.

Semiconductor platforms for photocatalytic disinfection

The transition of photocatalysis from theoretical potential to practical biosafety applications hinges on the careful selection of appropriate semiconductor hosts. An ideal photocatalyst for microbial inactivation must satisfy the “golden triangle” of requirements: thermodynamic alignment, with band edges positioned to drive microbial redox reactions; photostability, ensuring resistance to photo corrosion during repeated bacterial inactivation cycles; and biocompatibility, remaining non-toxic under dark conditions.

The landscape of antimicrobial photocatalysts has evolved from simple binary oxides to complex organic–inorganic hybrids. Metal oxides, such as titanium dioxide (TiO2) and zinc oxide (ZnO), remain benchmark materials due to their non-toxicity, low cost, and strong oxidizing potential. TiO2, particularly the anatase phase, offers exceptional chemical stability, yet its wide bandgap (Eg ≈ 3.2 eV) limits its activity to the ultraviolet region, which accounts for less than 5% of solar irradiance.10 ZnO exhibits higher electron mobility but is prone to photo corrosion in aqueous media, leading to the release of Zn2+ ions; although these ions possess antimicrobial properties, they can induce cytotoxicity in mammalian cells.11 Metal sulfides and chalcogenides, such as CdS and ZnS, generally have narrower bandgaps, which are ideal for visible-light absorption. Cadmium sulfide (CdS, Eg ≈ 2.4 eV) is a classic visible-light absorber; however, its inherent toxicity and rapid photo corrosion, where S2− is oxidized to elemental sulfur, have confined its use primarily to model systems, often requiring protective shells (e.g. CdS@TiO2) for practical applications. The metal-free semiconductor graphitic carbon nitride (gC3N4) has emerged as a promising photocatalytic material. As a polymeric semiconductor with a stacked 2D structure, it offers a tunable bandgap (Eg ≈ 2.7 eV), extraordinary thermal stability, and biocompatibility. Its unique ability to activate molecular oxygen into superoxide radicals (˙O2−) via a one-electron reduction pathway makes it particularly lethal to aerobic bacteria.12 Emerging hybrids, including metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and covalent organic frameworks (COFs), further expand the design space. These highly porous materials provide ultrahigh surface areas that facilitate pathogen capture for “trap-and-kill” strategies, addressing the mass-transfer limitations of non-porous oxide photocatalysts.13

The conduction and valence band edge positions of representative semiconductor photocatalysts, including TiO2, ZnO, g-C3N4, CdS, Bi2WO6, BiVO4, and In2O3, span a wide range relative to the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE) scale, and their alignment with key ROS redox thresholds directly determines the oxidative species they generate.14 Materials whose conduction band minimum lies above −0.33 V vs. NHE are thermodynamically capable of reducing O2 to superoxide (˙O2−), while those with valence band maxima exceeding +2.8 V vs. NHE can oxidize water to hydroxyl radicals (˙OH); only materials that satisfy both criteria simultaneously or heterojunction systems engineered to combine these capabilities can deliver the broad-spectrum oxidative capacity required for clinically relevant pathogen inactivation.15

Visible-light responsive photocatalysts

Transitioning photocatalytic disinfection from lab-scale UV systems to sustainable, solar-driven applications necessitates the efficient utilization of the visible-light spectrum (λ > 400 nm). Among the most widely explored strategies, plasmonic enhancement involves decorating semiconductor photocatalysts with noble metal nanoparticles (e.g., Au and Ag) to exploit localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR). LSPR-induced collective oscillations of surface electrons extend light absorption to the visible region and promote hot-electron generation and injection into the semiconductor, thereby improving charge separation and amplifying the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) responsible for microbial inactivation.16 Additionally, localized electromagnetic field enhancement and photothermal effects near plasmonic nanostructures may further contribute to membrane damage and cellular dysfunction. Visible-light sensitization represents another effective approach, whereby wide-bandgap semiconductors are coupled with organic dyes or narrow-bandgap quantum dots (QDs) to enable visible-light harvesting. However, long-term operational stability and biosafety remain critical concerns as organic dyes are prone to photobleaching and ROS-induced degradation. Consequently, solid-state sensitizers such as bismuth sulfide (Bi2S3) and carbon quantum dots have emerged as more robust alternatives, offering enhanced photostability and sustained visible-light activity under prolonged irradiation.17 From solar-blind to solar-active, the ultimate goal is to harness the full solar spectrum for photocatalytic disinfection. For instance, recent work has demonstrated that up-conversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) can convert low-energy near-infrared (NIR) light, which penetrates deeper into biological tissues than UV or visible light, into higher-energy photons capable of exciting photocatalysts. Given the superior penetration depth of NIR radiation in optically dense or turbid environments, UCNP-assisted systems offer a promising route toward photocatalytic disinfection in conditions where conventional UV or visible light is severely attenuated, although their practical efficiencies and scalable implementation remain active areas of research.18

Recent experimental studies have successfully translated these conceptual strategies into high-performance photocatalytic disinfection platforms, underscoring that architectural design is as critical as chemical composition in determining functional efficacy. As summarized in Table 1, transforming native, single-component photocatalysts to rationally engineered heterojunctions effectively mitigates intrinsic limitations, such as rapid charge-carrier recombination and insufficient solar absorption. A representative example is bismuth tungstate (Bi2WO6), which in its pristine form exhibits poor charge separation efficiency. The construction of a ternary Z-scheme nanojunction (AgBr–Ag–Bi2WO6) introduces metallic silver as an interfacial electron mediator, facilitating directional charge transfer through favorable band alignment and selective recombination pathways.19 This architecture preserves highly oxidative holes and reductive electrons, markedly enhancing reactive oxygen species generation and enabling rapid Escherichia coli inactivation within 15 minutes, a performance unattainable by the native oxide alone. Similarly, coupling indium oxide with graphitic carbon nitride (In2O3/g-C3N4) creates a Z-scheme heterojunction that synergistically aligns the energy bands to capture natural sunlight far more efficiently than either isolated component.20 This structural synergy results in a 6-log reduction of pathogens, demonstrating that precise band structure engineering is the key to unlocking clinical-grade antimicrobial efficacy of their electronic structures, resulting in the substantially improved utilization of natural sunlight compared with either component individually. A 6-log reduction refers to a 99.9999% decrease in the viable microbial population; for example, reducing an initial bacterial count of 106 CFU mL−1 to below the detection limit represents complete, clinically significant sterilization rather than a mere inhibition of microbial growth. This interfacial band structure engineering yields up to a 6-log reduction in pathogenic microorganisms, demonstrating that the precise control over charge-transfer pathways is a decisive factor in achieving application-relevant, high-level antimicrobial performance.19

Mechanisms of photocatalytic disinfection

The translation of photonic energy into antimicrobial efficacy is a complex physicochemical cascade that bridges solid-state chemistry and molecular microbiology. It begins with the fundamental excitation of the semiconductor, but the ultimate biosafety outcome relies on the efficient transfer of photogenerated charge carriers (electrons and holes) to the pathogen interface. This section elucidates the sequential pathways governing this process, detailing how precise band-edge energetics drive the generation of lethal reactive oxygen species (ROS) and how these molecular oxidants systematically dismantle pathogenic structures, from the outer cell envelope to the genomic core.

ROS generation pathways

The primary determining force for photocatalytic microbial inactivation is the thermodynamic feasibility of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, which is fundamentally dictated by the alignment of semiconductor band edges relative to the redox potentials of oxygen and water, in conjunction with charge-carrier dynamics at the catalyst surface. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, for effective disinfection, the conduction band minimum (CBM) must be more negative than the oxygen reduction potential (−0.33 eV vs. NHE) to reduce molecular oxygen to superoxide radicals (˙O2−), while the valence band maximum (VBM) must be more positive than the water oxidation potential (+2.8 eV vs. NHE) to generate hydroxyl radicals (˙OH).8 These ROS function as highly reactive oxidative agents that collectively compromise microbial viability through multiple pathways. Hydroxyl radicals, being the most potent, indiscriminately attack the polyunsaturated fatty acids in the cell membrane via lipid peroxidation, causing the leakage of intracellular contents. In parallel, superoxide radicals (O2˙−) and singlet oxygen (1O2) propagate oxidative stress by oxidizing the amino acid residues in essential proteins and damaging nucleic acid bases, thereby impairing the metabolic function and genetic integrity. The synergistic action of these ROS culminates in irreversible cellular damage and effective pathogen inactivation, as schematically depicted in Fig. 2b.25

In summary, Fig. 2a maps these reaction pathways schematically, showing the photocatalyst as the central hub from which electrons and holes diverge into distinct oxidative and reductive cascades, with molecular oxygen accepting electrons at the surface to initiate the superoxide pathway, while water donates electrons to holes, feeding the hydroxyl radical cycle. Notably, the diagram highlights the interconnected nature of these cascades: superoxide undergoes disproportionation to yield hydrogen peroxide, which further decomposes to ˙OH, and holes can independently dimerize water-derived species to the same terminal oxidant, illustrating that multiple parallel routes converge on the same lethal ROS. Fig. 2b then translates this chemistry into cellular consequences across three spatially distinct attack fronts: the outer membrane, where lipid peroxidation is the primary mode of structural failure; the cytoplasm, where protein carbonylation and nucleic acid damage to both RNA and DNA simultaneously dismantle the cell's genetic and biosynthetic machinery; and the inner respiratory chain, where ROS directly impair the FAD, cytochrome c, and NAD+/NADH redox relays, collapsing the proton gradient that drives ATP synthesis via the Na+–K+ pump. The bacterial enzymatic defenses, superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), are also depicted, underscoring that while cells possess native antioxidant capacity, the sheer magnitude and simultaneity of multi-site ROS attack overwhelm these protective systems, rendering photocatalytic inactivation fundamentally irreversible.

In photocatalytic antimicrobial systems, disinfection is commonly attributed to reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as ˙OH, ˙O2−, and H2O2; however, direct hole (h+) oxidation can also play a significant role under specific conditions. When photocatalysts are in close physical contact with microbial cell membranes, particularly in cases where positively charged surfaces promote electrostatic attraction, photogenerated holes can directly oxidize cellular components, such as lipids, proteins, and peptidoglycan layers, without the intermediate formation of free radicals. This direct oxidation pathway can be particularly efficient because it is not limited by the short lifetime and limited diffusion distance of ROS like ˙OH. In contrast, ROS-mediated disinfection generally dominates in well-dispersed aqueous systems, where photocatalyst–cell contact is limited and sufficient dissolved oxygen is available to sustain radical formation. Experimental evidence supporting the contribution of direct hole oxidation has been reported in studies employing hole scavengers (e.g., EDTA and methanol), where partial antimicrobial activity persists even after ˙OH quenching, indicating that multiple parallel pathways may coexist depending on the reaction conditions and catalyst–microbe interactions.

A critical mechanistic consideration in semiconductor-mediated antimicrobial studies is distinguishing true photocatalytic disinfection from the intrinsic metal-ion toxicity arising from photo corrosion or ion leaching. In reputable photocatalytic antimicrobial investigations, this distinction is typically addressed through “dark control” experiments performed using identical catalyst loadings under light and dark conditions. Significant antimicrobial activity observed in the absence of illumination indicates that dissolved metal ions, such as Zn2+, Cu2+, or Ag+, may contribute directly to microbial toxicity independent of photocatalytic ROS generation. To further deconvolute these two mechanisms, advanced studies commonly combine inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) measurements of dissolved metal ions with complementary dark toxicity assays and radical scavenger experiments. In such scavenger studies, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) is frequently employed as a hole (h+) scavenger, isopropanol is used as a hydroxyl radical (˙OH) quencher, and benzoquinone is employed as a superoxide radical (˙O2−) scavenger to identify the dominant oxidative species responsible for pathogen inactivation.27 The suppression of antimicrobial activity in the presence of these scavengers provides direct mechanistic evidence for ROS-mediated photocatalytic pathways.5 Previous studies on doped ZnO-based electrospun antimicrobial membranes, including Cu–ZnO–cellulose acetate systems and GO/Ni-doped ZnO nanohybrid systems, have demonstrated strong antimicrobial performance under visible-light-active conditions; however, comprehensive ion-leaching quantification and systematic ROS scavenger analyses remain limited.28 Consequently, future investigations should incorporate ICP-MS-assisted leaching studies together with standardized dark controls and ROS quenching protocols as best-practice methodologies to rigorously distinguish photocatalytic disinfection from intrinsic metal-ion toxicity contributions.

Charge carrier dynamics

Even with an optimal band alignment, the antimicrobial performance of photocatalysts is ultimately governed by the lifetime and utilization efficiency of photogenerated electron–hole pairs. In pristine or poorly engineered semiconductors, these charge carriers typically undergo rapid bulk or surface recombination on the nanosecond timescale, severely limiting their participation in the redox reactions required for microbial inactivation. Consequently, contemporary photocatalyst design strategies increasingly emphasize charge separation and lifetime extension rather than bandgap tuning alone.

Defect passivation

Surface trap states arise from unsatisfied dangling bonds and sub-stoichiometric lattice imperfections at the semiconductor surface, which introduce localized energy levels within the forbidden bandgap that intercept and immobilize photogenerated carriers before they can migrate to catalytically active surface sites. Passivating these traps through surface hydroxylation, ligand capping, or atomic layer deposition effectively eliminates these mid-gap capture centers, suppressing non-radiative Shockley–Read–Hall recombination and substantially extending the carrier lifetime available for productive ROS-generating redox reactions.

Internal electric field

Distinct from trap passivation, which operates by removing recombination pathways, piezoelectric and ferroelectric materials generate a permanent built-in electric field across the space-charge depletion region at the semiconductor interface, arising from their non-centrosymmetric crystal structures and spontaneous lattice polarization. This field induces a directional electrostatic drift force on photogenerated charge carriers, independent of surface trap density, driving electrons toward the reduction sites and holes toward the oxidation sites, thereby ensuring efficient spatial charge separation even in highly defective materials where surface passivation alone is insufficient.5

The implementation of Z-scheme architecture has proven particularly effective; by mimicking natural photosynthesis, these systems directionally transfer electrons between two coupled semiconductors, spatially isolating the reduction and oxidation sites. This prolonged carrier lifetime directly enhances the quantum yield of ROS, sustaining disinfection efficacy against bacterial antioxidant defenses.29 Since the lifespan of a hydroxyl radical is extremely short (in the nanosecond range) and its diffusion distance is limited to a few nanometers, the physical proximity between the photocatalyst and pathogen is non-negotiable. This “trap-and-kill” mechanism relies heavily on surface engineering to overcome the electrostatic repulsion typically found between negatively charged bacterial cell walls and semiconductor surfaces. By modulating the catalyst's zeta potential, often via surface amination or introduction of positively charged defects, bacteria can be electrostatically attracted and effectively anchored at the active sites.28 This close contact creates a direct interface for charge transfer, where photogenerated holes (h+) can directly oxidize the cell wall without needing intermediate radical species. Furthermore, rough or nanostructured surfaces can mechanically stress the cell envelope, synergizing with the chemical attack to accelerate the rupture of robust biofilms and viral capsids.30 Recent investigations have empirically validated these charge dynamics and surface engineering principles, demonstrating that extending the carrier lifetime and optimizing the interfacial contact are not only theoretical ideals but also practical prerequisites for achieving high-efficiency disinfection.

The tourmaline/BiVO4 system (Zhan et al., 2025)31 demonstrates that amplifying the built-in electric field through spontaneous polarization produces measurable improvements in photocurrent response, directly correlating with its enhanced antibacterial activity against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive strains and confirming that field-driven carrier separation operates independently of bandgap width. The ZnBiGdO4/SnS2 Z-scheme system (Luan et al., 2025)33 further reinforces this finding: the drastic reduction in photoluminescence intensity serves as a direct spectroscopic proof that vectorial charge transfer, rather than recombination, governs carrier utilization, translating into near-complete substrate removal (99.63%) and high mineralization efficiency (98.37% TOC removal). Taken together, these findings suggest that optimal photocatalytic disinfection requires the simultaneous optimization of three interdependent parameters, namely, internal field strength, surface charge polarity, and charge transfer directionality, and engineering any one of these in isolation yields only partial gains.

Selective and controlled inactivation

The next frontier in biosafety is shifting from “broad-spectrum destruction” to precision targeting. Not all pathogens require the same energetic force to neutralize; for instance, the lipid envelope of a virus is far more fragile than the thick peptidoglycan layer of a Gram-positive spore. By precisely tuning the band edges, researchers can selectively activate specific ROS pathways that match the vulnerability of the target while conserving energy. For example, narrowing the bandgap to favor the production of milder superoxide radicals over aggressive hydroxyl radicals can effectively inactivate viruses while sparing the organic matrix of a water filter or preventing damage to host mammalian cells in a wound dressing.27 This thermodynamic selection allows for “smart” disinfection systems that can distinguish between a target pathogen and the surrounding environment, reducing the formation of harmful byproducts and enhancing the biocompatibility of the technology.34

The necessity for such precise tuning becomes clear when analyzing the specific structural defenses of target pathogens. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the inactivation pathway depends heavily on the complexity of the cell envelope. In Gram-negative bacteria (Fig. 3a), the organism is shielded by a unique outer membrane fortified with lipopolysaccharides (LPSs) and porins. While this acts as a first line of defense, it is highly susceptible to lipid peroxidation by hydroxyl radicals (˙OH). This oxidative attack disrupts the membrane, creating pores that allow ROS to flood the periplasm and degrade the thin internal peptidoglycan layer. In contrast, Gram-positive bacteria (Fig. 3b) lack this outer lipid layer but rely on a significantly thicker, mesh-like peptidoglycan shell. Although physically robust, this shell is sufficiently porous to permit the entry of small radical species, enabling ROS to penetrate deep into the structure and directly disrupt the inner cytoplasmic membrane. Despite these architectural differences, the visual progression confirms that the fatal blow in both cases is the irreversible loss of membrane integrity, transforming the cell wall from a protective shield into a leaking sieve that triggers metabolic collapse.

Target pathogen diversity: structural vulnerabilities and required ROS profiles

Photocatalytic antimicrobial efficiency is strongly dependent on the structural complexity and defense mechanisms of different biological targets, including viruses, fungi, biofilms, and antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) bacteria. Enveloped viruses, such as SARS-CoV-2, are particularly susceptible to oxidative attack due to their lipid membrane, where hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) rapidly induce lipid peroxidation, leading to capsid destabilization, while non-enveloped viruses exhibit higher resistance due to their protein-dense capsid structure, requiring more sustained oxidative stress.36 Fungal pathogens possess rigid cell walls composed of chitin and β-glucans, necessitating prolonged exposure to reactive oxygen species, where superoxide (˙O2−) and singlet oxygen (1O2) contribute significantly to structural degradation.8 Biofilms present an additional challenge due to their extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, which restricts ROS diffusion; therefore, nanostructured photocatalysts and high-flux ROS generation are essential for the effective penetration and disruption of the biofilm architecture. AMR bacteria often evade conventional antibiotics through efflux pumps and membrane modifications; however, photocatalytic ROS-based mechanisms bypass these resistance pathways by inducing nonspecific oxidative damage to membranes, proteins, and genetic material, making resistance development highly unlikely.8

Band structure engineering strategies

The elucidation of microbial inactivation mechanisms highlights a critical bottleneck: pristine semiconductors rarely possess the ideal electronic structure to simultaneously maximize light absorption, charge separation, and surface redox reactivity. To bridge the gap between theoretical potential and practical disinfection, band structure engineering has emerged as a definitive toolset for materials scientists. By deliberately manipulating the electronic density of states and surface physiochemistry, researchers can fine-tune the “photocatalytic engine” to operate efficiently under ambient conditions. This section details the primary strategies currently driving the development of next-generation antimicrobial photocatalysts, moving from bulk lattice modifications to precise surface tailoring.5

The performance of a photocatalyst is fundamentally determined by its electronic band structure, which controls light absorption, charge-carrier dynamics, and the thermodynamic potential for reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Pristine semiconductors often exhibit wide bandgaps, restricting their activity to the ultraviolet region, or suffer from rapid electron–hole recombination, both of which limit their practical applicability. Band structure engineering, the deliberate tuning of a material's electronic density of states, therefore serves as a critical strategy for designing next-generation photocatalysts, enabling enhanced light harvesting, prolonged charge-carrier lifetimes, and efficient ROS production for advanced biosafety applications.5

Modifications such as elemental doping and defect engineering introduce mid-gap states that narrow the effective bandgap, enabling the utilization of the visible-light spectrum. Furthermore, coupling distinct semiconductors to form heterojunctions establishes internal electric fields that drive charge separation. Traditionally, four main heterostructure mechanisms are described in the literature: type-I, type-II, Schottky junctions, and Z-scheme heterostructures (Fig. 4). In brief, type-I junctions are similar to photovoltaic cells, facilitating charge-carrier-driven redox reactions.37 Type-II junctions exploit the potential difference between the components to spatially separate photogenerated electrons and holes, thereby suppressing recombination. Schottky junctions leverage the semiconductor metal interface to induce an effective mechanism that reduces charge carrier recombination and increases spectral light absorption.37 Z-scheme heterostructures, in contrast, connect two photocatalytic systems to selectively enrich charge carriers with stronger reduction and oxidation potentials.

More recently, S-scheme heterostructures have emerged as highly efficient candidates for photocatalytic applications, offering superior redox potentials.36,38 S-scheme (step-scheme) heterojunctions possess the most thermodynamically refined charge-transfer architecture currently available for photocatalytic design. As illustrated in Fig. 5, they are constructed from two semiconductors with staggered band alignments: a reduction photocatalyst (RP) with a higher conduction band and a more negative Fermi level and an oxidation photocatalyst (OP) with a lower valence band and a more positive Fermi level. When these two components make intimate contact, a mismatch in Fermi levels drives spontaneous electron transfer from the RP to the OP until equilibrium is reached, generating a built-in internal electric field (IEF) directed from the RP toward the OP at the heterojunction interface. Under photoexcitation, this IEF performs a key selectivity function by driving recombination of conduction-band electrons in the OP with valence-band holes in the RP, thereby eliminating charge carriers with lower redox potentials and retaining the more thermodynamically active carriers. Simultaneously, the IEF directs the strongly reductive electrons of the RP and the strongly oxidative holes of the OP toward their respective catalytic surfaces, preserving the maximum possible thermodynamic driving force for ROS generation. This mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 5. The practical consequence for biosafety applications is decisive: unlike type-II heterojunctions, which sacrifice redox potential in exchange for spatial charge separation, S-scheme systems achieve both simultaneously retaining the strong oxidative holes required for hydroxyl radical generation (+2.8 V vs. NHE) and strongly reductive electrons needed for superoxide production (−0.33 V vs. NHE), without compromise.39

Doping and Co-doping

The most direct method to overcome the wide bandgap of materials like TiO2 or ZnO is the introduction of foreign elements into the crystal lattice, which creates intermediate energy levels that act as “stepping stones” for lower-energy photons. As illustrated in Fig. 6, transition metal doping (e.g., Mn, Co, and Fe) has shown remarkable efficacy; for instance, introducing manganese into the graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) framework significantly modifies the molecular orbital distribution, enhancing charge separation dynamics. Recent findings indicate that a 25% molar loading of Mn can boost the inactivation of E. coli to a 6-log reduction within 6 hours, a feat unachievable by the undoped material.40 Beyond single elements, co-doping strategies, often combining a metal with a non-metal like nitrogen or sulfur, can induce a synergistic narrowing of the bandgap while maintaining lattice stability.41 These dopants not only extend the absorption edge to the visible spectrum but also serve as charge traps that physically separate electrons and holes, thereby retarding the recombination rate and amplifying the overall yield of microbicidal ROS.

To fundamentally optimize these semiconductor platforms for biosafety, researchers typically pursue two parallel strategies: nanostructuring and electronic doping. Nanostructuring serves as the morphological foundation, drastically increasing the surface-to-volume ratio to maximize the number of active sites available for pathogen adsorption.42,43 However, it is electronic modulation through doping that fundamentally alters the photo-response. The incorporation of foreign elements remodels the band structure by introducing discrete impurity states within the forbidden band. Specifically, n-type dopants function as electron donors, creating energy levels just below the conduction band, while p-type dopants act as electron acceptors, generating levels near the valence band, as shown in Fig. 7.42 These auxiliary states effectively narrow the optical bandgap, reducing the energy threshold for electron excitation. This thermodynamic adjustment is two-fold: it enables the material to harvest a broader spectrum of light (optical improvement) and facilitates more efficient, directional charge transfer (electrical improvement), both of which are prerequisites for high-performance photocatalytic disinfection.40

For instance, the integration of copper (Cu) dopants into zinc oxide (ZnO) lattices has been shown to effectively narrow the bandgap for visible-light activation; notably, optimizing the dopant concentration to 7% Cu (calcined at 450 °C) results in electrospun nanocomposite membranes that exhibit significantly expanded inhibition zones against a broad spectrum of pathogens, including S. aureus and C. albicans, compared with pure ZnO.28 Expanding on this, the synergistic combination of nickel (Ni) doping with graphene oxide (GO) support has demonstrated that dopants can work in tandem with carbonaceous scaffolds to optimize charge transfer dynamics. A specific optimized composition of 4% GO-5% Ni-doped ZnO achieved a remarkable 93.41% photocatalytic degradation efficiency of methylene blue within 15 minutes under visible light, driven by a narrowed bandgap of 3.13 eV. Furthermore, these nanohybrid membranes displayed potent antimicrobial efficacy with inhibition zones ranging from 25.5 to 33.0 mm against multidrug-resistant strains, proving that multi-component doping strategies are essential for developing robust, self-sterilizing surfaces.8

Defect engineering and heterojunction formation

While crystal perfection was once the goal, modern photocatalysis embraces “defective” materials, where atomic-scale irregularities, such as oxygen vacancies, serve as the active catalytic sites. These vacancies donate electrons to the conduction band and act as powerful adsorption centers for molecular oxygen, facilitating its reduction to superoxide radicals. A prominent example is the vacancy-engineering of phase-selectively reduced TiO2 (Cu–S–BTO), where the deliberate introduction of surface defects modulated the band structure to optimize the separation of charge carriers.48 Experimental data suggest that these oxygen-deficient sites function as “electron sinks,” preventing rapid recombination and ensuring that photogenerated electrons are available to drive the reductive destruction of pathogens in aqueous systems. Furthermore, high-temperature annealing protocols have been refined to precisely control the concentration of these vacancies, allowing for a tunable “defect density” that balances charge conductivity with surface adsorption capacity.8 Beyond a critical vacancy concentration, defects transition from beneficial charge-trapping centers to acting as Shockley–Read–Hall recombination centers, reducing the photocatalytic efficiency. Studies have discussed the creation of an optimal defect density window by vacancies and the use of techniques such as EPR, XPS, and photoluminescence to characterize and optimize defects.49

To fundamentally solve the issue of charge recombination, constructing heterojunctions interfaces between two dissimilar semiconductors is proving superior to single-component systems The evolution from type-II to Z-scheme and the newly defined S-scheme architectures marks a significant leap in maintaining high redox potentials.50 In an S-scheme heterojunction, the internal electric field at the interface drives the recombination of “weak” electrons and holes, preserving the strongly reductive electrons and oxidative holes for pathogen attack. For example, the coupling of ZnBiGdO4 with SnS2 creates a robust Z-scheme system, where the charge transfer channels are spatially isolated, resulting in a disinfection rate 2.41 times higher than that of their individual counterparts.33 This vectorial charge transfer mimics natural photosynthesis, ensuring that the photocatalyst retains the thermodynamic potential required to simultaneously mineralize complex organic contaminants and bacterial cell walls.33

Applications in biosafety and public health

The translation of band-engineered photocatalysts from the laboratory bench to public utility represents a paradigm shift in environmental hygiene. By moving away from batch-chemical dosing toward passive, solar-driven systems, these materials offer a sustainable solution to the “energy-water-health” nexus, as illustrated in Fig. 8.

Defect engineering involves modifying a structurally diverse range of atomic-scale imperfections, each possessing a distinct electronic signature and ROS selectivity profile. Oxygen vacancies (OVs), the most extensively studied defect type in metal oxide photocatalysts, arise from the removal of lattice oxygen atoms under reductive annealing atmospheres, UV irradiation, or chemical etching. Each OV donates two electrons to the conduction band, raising the Fermi level and introducing localized sub-bandgap states approximately 0.75–1.0 eV below the conduction band minimum. Critically, OVs function simultaneously as adsorption and activation sites for molecular oxygen, lowering the energy barrier for O2 → ˙O2− conversion and thereby selectively amplifying the superoxide pathway a ROS particularly effective against the peptidoglycan meshwork of Gram-positive bacteria. The formation of OVs is thermodynamically controlled: high-temperature annealing in H2/Ar atmospheres generates bulk OVs that improve conductivity, while low-temperature photochemical or wet-chemical reduction preferentially creates surface OVs that directly participate in interfacial redox chemistry. Nitrogen vacancies (NVs) in graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) represent a structurally complementary defect class. The periodic heptazine network of g-C3N4 contains two distinct nitrogen sites, namely, bridging N atoms (N2C) and terminal amino groups (NH), and their selective removal creates electron-rich defect centers that reorganize the π-conjugated band structure. NVs narrow the optical bandgap by generating mid-gap states derived from unpaired electrons and redirect hole accumulation toward the valence band maximum, favoring singlet oxygen (1O2) generation via energy transfer to ground-state oxygen, an ROS with a particularly high membrane penetration depth relevant to biofilm disruption. Cation vacancies, including zinc vacancies in ZnO and titanium vacancies in TiO2, introduce acceptor-type defect levels near the valence band, promoting p-type character and enhancing hole-driven oxidation pathways.9 Unlike OVs, cation vacancies are thermodynamically less stable and require kinetic trapping through rapid quenching or non-equilibrium synthesis routes such as solvothermal crystallization at low temperatures. A critical design consideration governing all defect types is the spatial distinction between the surface and bulk defects. Surface defects, by virtue of their direct exposure to the aqueous reaction environment, serve as productive catalytic sites for ROS generation and pathogen adsorption. Bulk defects, in contrast, primarily function as charge transport modifiers that are beneficial at low concentrations, where they promote carrier delocalization, but they are detrimental at high concentrations, where they transition into Shockley–Read–Hall recombination centers that trap carriers before they reach the surface. This distinction necessitates spatially resolved characterizations combining XPS (surface-sensitive) with EPR and positron annihilation spectroscopy (bulk-sensitive) to establish the optimal defect density window for any given photocatalytic system.9

Water disinfection and wastewater treatment

The deployment of solar-driven photocatalysts offers a robust alternative to chlorination, particularly for decentralized water treatment, where chemical logistics are challenging. Advanced heterostructures, such as carbon quantum dot (CQD)-decorated TiO2, have demonstrated exceptional efficacy in utilizing the visible solar spectrum to simultaneously drive the degradation of organic pollutants and inactivation of pathogens.51 A study confirmed that these CQD/TiO2 composites leverage a synergistic charge transfer mechanism to generate a continuous flux of reactive oxygen species (ROS), achieving rapid microbial inactivation without the formation of carcinogenic disinfection byproducts (DBPs) like trihalomethanes.51 Furthermore, recent engineering efforts have focused on immobilizing these catalysts onto substrates to facilitate post-treatment recovery. For instance, novel floating photocatalytic modules are now capable of treating municipal wastewater effluents, effectively removing resistant bacterial strains that survive conventional secondary treatments, thus preventing the release of “superbugs” into natural water bodies.52

Air purification and indoor safety

With the increasing recognition of aerosol transmission vectors for viruses like SARS-CoV-2 and influenza, integrating photocatalytic coatings into heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems has become a priority for modern building infrastructure.53 Unlike passive high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters that merely trap pathogens, photocatalytic reactors actively mineralize bioaerosols upon contact. Recent field trials utilizing TiO2-impregnated luffa sponges within PVC-based HVAC reactors demonstrated significant inactivation of airborne microorganisms under UV-A irradiation.53 These systems capitalize on the high surface area of the porous support to maximize the contact time between the airflow and the catalytic surface, ensuring that viral capsids are oxidatively disrupted before recirculation. Moreover, emerging hybrid technologies that combine non-thermal plasma with photocatalysis are showing promise for high-throughput air sterilization, offering a dual-mechanism attack that prevents the accumulation of viable viral loads on the filter media.54

Medical and food-processing surfaces

Hospital-acquired infections and foodborne outbreaks highlight the need for surfaces that can clean themselves and help stop the spread of germs. To address this, researchers have developed “smart” nanocomposite coatings that remain active under standard indoor lighting. A breakthrough study highlights the efficacy of TiO2 and ZnO nanoparticles embedded in polymer matrices for food packaging; these films not only prevent microbial colonization but also extend shelf life by oxidizing ethylene gas.55 In clinical settings, visible-light-active paints doped with noble metals are applied to high-touch surfaces like doorknobs and bedrails. These coatings function as a passive defense layer, continuously generating superoxide radicals to neutralize transient pathogens deposited by touch or droplets and significantly reducing the reliance on manual chemical cleaning regimes that are prone to human error.56

Portable and wearable disinfection devices

The demand for personal biosecurity has driven the miniaturization of photocatalytic systems into wearable formats. A notable 2025 innovation involves the fabrication of self-disinfecting collagen fiber networks (CFNs) for next-generation face masks.57 These reusable masks, functionalized with photocatalytic agents, achieved a 99.99% bactericidal rate within 5 minutes of solar irradiation, effectively solving the issue of secondary contamination inherent to disposable polypropylene masks.57 Additionally, photocatalyst-integrated textiles are being engineered for protective clothing; by coating fabrics with tin oxide (SnO2) and titanium dioxide nanocomposites, researchers have created garments that can detoxify themselves under sunlight. These “self-cleaning” textiles maintain their antimicrobial potency even after multiple wash cycles, offering a sustainable personal protective equipment (PPE) solution for healthcare workers operating in high-risk infectious environments.58

Future perspectives for photocatalytic biosafety platforms

The evolution of photocatalytic biosafe materials must now transition from empirical observation to precision engineering, driven by atomic-scale design and computational intelligence. Future research should prioritize advanced defect engineering and quantum confinement strategies, particularly within two-dimensional semiconductors, to achieve ultra-efficient charge separation and disinfection capabilities. However, the complexity of optimizing these band structures demands a departure from traditional trial-and-error methods. The integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning offers a critical pathway forward, enabling the prediction of optimal band alignments and dopant levels before synthesis. By leveraging data-driven material discovery, researchers can accelerate the design of high-performance heterostructures that are specifically tailored to overcome the current limitations in quantum yield and visible-light utilization.

Beyond fundamental material enhancements, the translation of these technologies into real-world applications requires a dual focus on seamless integration and life-cycle sustainability. Photocatalytic systems must move out of the laboratory into infrastructure, taking the form of solar-powered disinfection units, self-cleaning environmental sensors, and protective surfaces that operate autonomously under ambient light. Crucially, this expansion must not compromise environmental integrity; therefore, future manufacturing must adopt circular principles. This includes establishing green synthesis routes, utilizing recyclable supports, and conducting rigorous life-cycle assessments. Ensuring that the production and disposal of these advanced materials are as safe as their intended application is the final, essential step toward true photocatalytic biosafety platforms.

Conclusion

The transition from conventional biocides to band-engineered semiconductors represents a critical defensive leap against the rising tide of antimicrobial resistance and environmental degradation. As this review has elucidated, the future of biosafety lies not in the discovery of more aggressive chemicals but in the precise manipulation of solid-state physics. By mastering the electronic density of states through doping, defect engineering, and the construction of Z-scheme or S-scheme heterojunctions, we have moved beyond the thermodynamic constraints of native materials. These advanced photocatalysts are no longer passive bystanders but active, solar-driven engines capable of “electronic tailoring,” ensuring that charge carriers are not only generated but also successfully delivered to the pathogen interface. As we scale these atomic-level innovations into macroscopic applications, from self-sterilizing medical infrastructure to reusable wearable protection, we approach a new paradigm where the environment itself acts as a continuous, autonomous shield against biological threats. Ultimately, the successful integration of these high-performance, sustainable materials will define the next generation of global public health infrastructure, turning the crisis of a “silent pandemic” into an opportunity for resilient, green innovation.
