R-α-Lipoic Acid Attenuates Acetaminophen-Induced Acute Liver Injury by Reducing Oxidative Stress, Inflammation, and Mitochondrial Damage in Mice
Authors: Fahad S Alshehri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcetaminophen (APAP) overdose is a leading cause of acute liver injury and is frequently associated with oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and hepatocellular toxicity. R-α-lipoic acid (R-ALA) is a natural antioxidant and mitochondrial cofactor that provides protective effects against APAP-induced hepatic damage.ObjectiveTo examine the protective effects of R-ALA against acute APAP-induced liver injury in mice.MethodsAdult male mice were randomly divided into five groups: control, APAP, R-ALA, repeated R-ALA pretreatment + APAP, and single R-ALA pretreatment + APAP. APAP was given orally at 500 mg/kg, while R-ALA was administered orally at 100 mg/kg. R-ALA was administered daily for 10 days, with a single dose given 1 hour before APAP. Twenty-four hours after APAP administration, blood and liver samples were collected. Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), glutamate dehydrogenase (GLDH), high mobility group box-1 (HMGB1), keratin-18 (K18), caspase-cleaved keratin-18 (ccK18), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were measured. Liver malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase 3 (GPx3) were also assessed.ResultsAPAP administration increased serum markers of hepatocellular injury, mitochondrial injury, hepatocyte death, and inflammation, including ALT, AST, GLDH, HMGB1, K18, ccK18, IL-6, and TNF-α. APAP also increased hepatic MDA and decreased hepatic GSH, SOD, CAT, and GPx3. R-ALA pretreatment significantly attenuated these APAP-induced changes. APAP increased ALT from 7.49 to 29.46 and AST from 11.10 to 32.49, while R-ALA pretreatment significantly reduced both markers compared with the APAP group.ConclusionR-ALA attenuated APAP-induced liver injury biomarker changes in mice, through antioxidant defenses and reduction of oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, mitochondrial injury markers, and hepatocyte death markers. These protective effects are mediated by antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and mitochondrial-protective mechanisms. Further histological and pathway-focused molecular studies are needed to confirm the underlying mechanisms and translational relevance.Keywords: acetaminophen, R-α-lipoic acid, hepatotoxicity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction

Introduction

Acetaminophen, N-acetyl-p-aminophenol (APAP), is one of the most used analgesic and antipyretic medications.1 APAP is indicated for mild to moderate pain and fever, and is used in chronic pain management in patients as an alternative to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.2 Generally, APAP is considered safe and well-tolerated.3 However, high doses can cause acute liver injury and liver failure.4 In fact, it has been reported that APAP has participated in about 56% of the total cases of severe acute liver injury and failure in the United States5 with over 80,000 reported cases caused by APAP, representing half of all acute liver failure cases.6 The high number of cases is a significant concern, and it is essential to understand the risks associated with APAP, especially at high doses.

The liver damage from APAP is a result of a toxic intermediate metabolite from APAP known as N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI).7 Typically, the majority of APAP is metabolized through glucuronidation and sulfation reactions into non-toxic and more soluble metabolites that are easily excreted by the kidneys.8 However, a lower percentage of APAP is metabolized in the liver by oxidation through cytochrome P450 (CYP2E1 and CYP3A4), into NAPQI.9 Consequently, high exposure to APAP can saturate glucuronidation and sulfation reactions, which lead to high levels of NAPQI, which exceeds the glutathione (GSH) neutralizing capacity in the liver, leading to liver injury.10 NAPQI accumulation in the liver can lead to serious molecular toxicity that causes hepatocellular necrosis.11 Several studies have shown that hepatocellular necrosis is connected to mitochondrial dysfunction.12–14 Theerefore NAPQI accumulation leads to the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are involved in mitochondrial protein disruption, respiratory chain dysfunction, increased mitochondrial permeability, membrane depolarization, and ATP depletion.15–17 Therefore, mitochondrial dysfunction is an important biomarker to monitor APAP toxicity.

α-Lipoic acid (ALA) is a natural compound found in plants and animals and participates in mitochondrial energy metabolism.18,19 Several studies have reported that R-α-lipoic acid (R-ALA) (active enantiomer of ALA) is an essential oxidative cofactor for mitochondrial metabolism.20–22 Also, R-ALA and its reduced form (dihydrolipoic acid) are strong antioxidants with great scavenging ability on ROS and free radicals.23 Furthermore, it has been shown that R-ALA can improve cellular redox homeostasis through regenerating oxidized forms of GSH and vitamin E.24,25 Studies have shown that taking R-ALA can decrease signs of lipid peroxidation and restore superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) antioxidant activity.26–28 Furthermore, it has been reported that R-ALA can attenuate inflammation through specific cellular signaling pathways.29 These pathways include inhibition of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB),30 tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α),31 activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2),32 and modulation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways.30 Other studies have suggested that R-ALA has protective effects against diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and neurodegenerative diseases.31,32 Therefore, R-ALA is a natural antioxidant compound with promising protective activities against oxidative stress-associated diseases.

There are several biomarkers that have been known to measure APAP-induced liver injury. ALT and AST are considered one of the commonly used biomarkers of hepatocellular injury; however, they are not fully liver-specific.33,34 GLDH is a mitochondrial enzyme involved in hepatocellular injury.35 HMGB1 is released during hepatocellular toxicity and can increase inflammation by triggering downstream signaling of the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) and Toll-like receptors.36,37 Keratin-18 (K18) and caspase-cleaved keratin-18 (ccK18) provide valuable understanding regarding hepatocyte apoptosis, epithelial cell differentiation, and death from caspase-associated apoptotic injury.38,39 Also, interleukin-6 (IL-6) and TNF-α indicate the activation of hepatic inflammatory responses.40 In addition, hepatic malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation, whereas GSH, SOD, CAT, and glutathione peroxidase 3 (GPx3) reflect hepatic antioxidant capacity and measure oxidative stress.41–43 Therefore, these biomarkers provide insight into hepatocellular injury, mitochondrial damage, inflammation, oxidative stress, and antioxidant defense during APAP-induced liver injury.

Given that oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are important factors that contribute to APAP-induced liver injury, compounds with antioxidant properties have been suggested to attenuate liver toxicity. In fact, studies have examined several antioxidants against acute APAP-induced liver injury, including fucoidan,44 nicotinamide,45 luteolin,46 and resveratrol47 with significant protective results. While antioxidants have been studied in APAP liver injury models, R-ALA’s specific protection against acute APAP hepatotoxicity remains underexplored, particularly with mitochondrial injury, hepatocyte death, inflammatory cytokines, and antioxidant enzyme activity. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the protective effects of R-ALA against acute APAP-induced liver injury in mice. And to give a better understanding of potential preventive measures against acute APAP-induced liver injury.

Materials and Methods

Chemical

Acetaminophen (APAP; Sigma-Aldrich, A5000) was used as the hepatotoxic agent. R-α-lipoic acid (R-ALA; Sigma-Aldrich, 07039) was prepared in corn oil48 (Sigma-Aldrich, C8267) and administered by oral gavage. APAP was freshly prepared and administered by oral gavage at 500 mg/kg. R-ALA was administered orally at 100 mg/kg. Gavage volume was adjusted to each mouse’s body weight. Serum levels of ALT, AST, GLDH, HMGB1, K18, ccK18, IL-6, and TNF-α were measured using commercially available ELISA kits from MyBioSource, Inc., with the following catalog numbers: ALT, MBS3805312; AST, MBS724026; GLDH, MBS761948; HMGB1, MBS722248; K18, MBS2703046; ccK18/Cytokeratin 18-M30, MBS3806856; IL-6, MBS355431; and TNF-α, MBS825075. Hepatic MDA, GSH, SOD, CAT, and GPx3 were measured in liver homogenate supernatants using commercially available ELISA kits from MyBioSource, Inc., with the following catalog numbers: MDA, MBS741034; GSH, MBS724815; SOD, MBS2707323; CAT, MBS160589; and GPx3, MBS2884158. All assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All other chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade.

Dose selection of APAP and R-ALA

Animal Groups and Dose Selection

This study used 35 adult male mice (Swiss albino) weighing 30–35 g. Mice were kept in plastic cages in groups, with a 12 hr light/dark schedule. The room had controlled temperature and humidity. The animals had unlimited access to a normal mouse diet and water ad libitum throughout the entire experiment. The mice had a week to acclimatize before the experiment started. Male mice were used to reduce biological variability related to hormonal cycling and to provide an initial controlled assessment of R-ALA hepatoprotection. All experiments were performed in accordance with proper animal care and were approved by the biomedical research ethics committee. The animals were randomly assigned to five groups, with each group consisting of seven mice. Group I (Control): received only the vehicle. Group II (APAP): received a single oral dose of APAP (500 mg/kg). Group III (R-ALA): received R-ALA (100 mg/kg, oral) once daily for 10 consecutive days. Group IV (R-ALA + APAP pretreatment): received R-ALA (100 mg/kg, oral) once daily for 10 days, followed by a single oral dose of APAP (500 mg/kg). Group V (R-ALA + APAP single dose): received a single oral dose of R-ALA (100 mg/kg) 1 h before administration of APAP (500 mg/kg). All treatments were given by oral gavage. Twenty-four hours after the last oral gavage, all animals were euthanized using carbon dioxide (CO2). Then, liver and blood samples were obtained. The APAP dose of 500 mg/kg was selected to induce acute hepatocellular injury in a short-term experimental mouse model.49–51 The R-ALA dose of 100 mg/kg was selected based on prior studies reporting antioxidant and tissue-protective effects of α-lipoic acid.52,53 All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee, Umm Al-Qura University, Makkah, Saudi Arabia (Approval No. HAPO-02-K-012-2025-12-3157).

Blood and Tissue Collection

At the end of the animal experiment, blood samples were obtained and incubated at room temperature until clot formation occurred. Then, the samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min. This centrifugation process was performed to separate serum. The serum samples were then kept at −20 degrees Celsius, ready for analysis. Liver tissues were removed, rinsed with cold normal saline, blotted dry, weighed, and homogenized in ice-cold homogenization buffer according to the assay kit instructions. Liver homogenates were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected for oxidative stress and antioxidant enzyme analyses.

Biochemical and Oxidative Stress Analysis

Serum ALT and AST were measured as primary biochemical markers of hepatocellular injury using enzymatic colorimetric assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Serum GLDH, HMGB1, K18, ccK18, IL-6, and TNF-α were measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Standards and serum samples were added to antibody-coated wells, incubated, washed, treated with detection reagents, and analyzed using a microplate reader. Hepatic MDA, GSH, SOD, CAT, and GPx3 were measured in liver homogenate supernatants using commercially available assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were done using GraphPad Prism software version 11.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences among groups were analyzed using ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test for pairwise comparisons. Homogeneity of variance was calculated using Brown-Forsythe and Bartlett’s tests. The magnitude of the treatment effect was reported using eta-squared (η2), calculated from the ANOVA sum of squares. Tukey-adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for comparisons where appropriate. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The observed treatment effect sizes were large across all measured biomarkers, with η2 values ranging from 0.811 to 0.954.

Results

Effect of R-ALA on APAP-Induced Hepatocellular Injury and Mitochondrial Injury Markers

The effects of R-ALA on liver injury biomarkers ALT. Statistical analysis showed significant differences in serum liver enzyme levels between the experimental groups, with ALT (Figure 1A) and AST (Figure 1B) elevated in both groups. Effect of R-ALA on APAP-Induced Hepatic Oxidative Stress

The effect of R-ALA on APAP-induced oxidative stress was examined using MDA and GSH levels. One-way ANOVA showed significant differences between groups for MDA (F (4,30) = 143.6, p < 0.0001) (Figure 4A) and GSH (F (4,30) = 49.52, p < 0.0001) (Figure 4B). APAP treatment significantly increased MDA levels compared with the control group (p < 0.0001). In contrast, APAP significantly decreased GSH levels compared with the control group (p < 0.0001). Interestingly, R-ALA administration alone significantly increased hepatic GSH levels compared with the control group (p = 0.0417). On the other hand, both repeated and single pretreatment with R-ALA significantly reduced MDA levels and restored GSH levels compared with the APAP group (p < 0.0001).

The effect of R-ALA on APAP-induced changes in antioxidant enzyme activity was evaluated by measuring SOD, CAT, and GPx3 levels. A direct comparison between repeated and single R-ALA pretreatment revealed no statistically significant difference across any of the measured biomarkers. Although the repeated R-ALA pretreatment showed numerically greater improvements than the single-dose pretreatment in several markers—including ALT, AST, GLDH, K18, ccK18, IL-6, TNF-α, MDA, GSH, SOD, and GPx3—these differences did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, both repeated and single R-ALA pretreatment was considered protective in comparison to APAP alone; however, repeated pretreatment was not statistically superior to single-dose pretreatment.

Discussion

High doses of APAP are a very common animal model to examine and understand drug-induced liver injury. Several studies have shown that high doses of APAP are associated with a toxic metabolite known as NAPQI. The formation of NAPQI is associated with ROS generation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and hepatocellular injury. Therefore, oxidative stress is considered a central mechanism in APAP-induced liver toxicity. Several hepatic enzymes have been known to be increased with APAP-induced liver toxicity. In this study, APAP treatment increased ALT and AST levels compared with the control group. Several studies have recognized that ALT and AST are indicators of hepatocyte injury.54–56 However, it has been suggested that ALT and AST could also serve as indicators of other organ injuries, such as muscle and heart injury.57,58 Thus, showing the low specificity of these biomarker to the liver. On the other hand, APAP treatment has induced the mitochondrial enzyme GLDH. GLDH is mainly localized in mitochondria; therefore, an elevation in GLDH has been suggested as an indication of mitochondrial injury.59 In fact, GLDH provides better specificity for hepatic injury compared with other commonly used liver enzymes.60

Similarly, HMGB1 is a nuclear protein used as a biomarker for hepatocellular injury.61 Typically, HMGB1 triggers inflammatory signaling pathways by interacting with the RAGE and toll-like receptors, thereby inducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.62 In this study, APAP elevated HMGB1 levels compared with the control group, indicating hepatocyte injury and activation of inflammatory responses. This is, in fact, similar to previous studies reporting an increase in HMGB1 levels following acute APAP treatment.63–65 Pretreatment with R-ALA significantly reduced HMGB1 levels compared to the APAP group, indicating R-ALA’s effect to reduce liver damage and inflammation from APAP-induced injury. Several studies have shown that high doses of APAP produce an inflammatory response in the liver.66–68 In addition, reports have shown that APAP-induced liver injury is associated with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6. 69–71 This elevation could be associated with HMGB1 release after liver injury, as well as activation of Kupffer cells, RAGE, and Toll-like receptors.72,73 In this study, APAP increased IL-6 and TNF-α levels compared to the control group, signaling activation of inflammatory pathways. Pretreatment with R-ALA decreased IL-6 and TNF-α levels compared with the APAP group, indicating that R-ALA attenuates inflammation associated with APAP-induced liver damage.

Oxidative stress is a known biomarker in liver injury induced by APAP.74,75 Studies have shown that oxidative stress contributes to lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and hepatocyte necrosis.76–78 Furthermore, ROS production contributes to lipid peroxidation, disrupts cell membranes, and affects hepatocyte function. Accordingly, MDA was used to assess oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in liver injury.79,80 In this study, APAP elevated MDA levels compared with the control group, indicating increased oxidative stress. On the other hand, GSH, an intracellular antioxidant that detoxifies reactive metabolites like NAPQI,10,81 was reduced following APAP treatment. However, pretreatment with R-ALA decreased MDA levels and restored GSH levels compared to the APAP group. An interesting finding of this study was that hepatic GSH levels in the ALA-treated group were significantly higher than those in the control group. This indicates that R-ALA, alone, enhances the liver’s antioxidant capacity. This finding indicates that R-ALA both protects against APAP-induced oxidative stress and boosts glutathione reserves prior to any toxic exposure. The hepatic GSH in the R-ALA-only group was significantly higher than in the control group. These findings suggest that R-ALA alone may enhance basal hepatic antioxidant capacity without causing measurable hepatocellular injury under the present experimental conditions.82,83 This effect may contribute to increased resistance to APAP-induced oxidative stress when R-ALA is administered prior to toxic exposure.

Antioxidant enzymes play an important role in the protection system against ROS-mediated liver injury.84–86 The major enzymatic antioxidants (SOD, CAT, and GPx3) protect hepatocytes against oxidative stress by neutralizing ROS.87 SOD converts superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide,88 where CAT and GPx3 reduce hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen, protecting hepatocytes and attenuating oxidative damage.89 Previous studies have shown that APAP-induced hepatotoxicity is associated with reduced antioxidant enzyme activity.90–92 In the present study, APAP treatment significantly decreased SOD, CAT, and GPx3 levels compared with the control group, indicating a week enzymatic antioxidant protection. However, pretreatment with R-ALA significantly restored antioxidant enzyme activity, suggesting that R-ALA enhances hepatic antioxidant defenses and contributes to protection against APAP-induced oxidative stress.

An additional finding is the comparison between repeated and single R-ALA pretreatment before APAP exposure. Both pretreatment regimens significantly attenuated APAP-induced hepatocellular injury, oxidative stress, elevation of inflammatory cytokines, and depletion of antioxidant enzymes compared with APAP alone. Although repeated R-ALA pretreatment showed numerically greater improvement than single-dose pretreatment for several biomarkers, the difference between the two R-ALA pretreatment groups was not statistically significant. Therefore, the present findings indicate that both repeated and single R-ALA pretreatment were protective in this model, but they do not establish the statistical significant of repeated pretreatment. Further studies using larger sample sizes, additional dosing schedules, and post-exposure treatment designs are needed to determine whether repeated R-ALA administration provides an advantage over single-dose pretreatment in APAP-induced hepatotoxicity.

This study has several limitations. Histopathological assessment of liver tissue was not performed; thus, biochemical evidence of hepatocellular injury lacked direct microscopic support from necrosis, inflammation, or architectural disruption. Although GLDH, HMGB1, K18, ccK18, cytokines, and oxidative stress markers indicate mitochondrial injury, hepatocyte death, inflammation, and oxidative damage, pathway-specific analyses like Western blot, qPCR, immunohistochemistry, mitochondrial assays, or RNA sequencing were not performed. R-ALA was given as a pretreatment, not as a post-exposure therapy, limiting its direct clinical use for human APAP overdose. R-ALA was not compared with N-acetylcysteine, the standard antidote for APAP poisoning.

Conclusion

The findings of this study showed that acute APAP administration induced significant liver injury in mice by increasing hepatic injury biomarkers, mitochondrial damage, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and impairment of antioxidant defenses. Pretreatment with R-ALA attenuated APAP-induced liver injury by reducing elevated liver enzyme levels, suppressing inflammatory cytokines, decreasing lipid peroxidation, and restoring intracellular antioxidants. Both repeated and single-dose R-ALA pretreatment showed significant protection compared with APAP alone, but repeated pretreatment was not statistically superior to single-dose pretreatment. Overall, these findings support the potential hepatoprotective role of R-ALA against APAP-induced liver injury; however, further studies are still needed to better define its mechanisms of action and determine the most effective preventive strategy.
