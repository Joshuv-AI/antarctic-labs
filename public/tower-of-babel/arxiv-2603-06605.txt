Structure-Aware Set Transformers: Temporal and Variable-Type Attention Biases for Asynchronous Clinical Time Series
Joohyung Lee, Kwanhyung Lee, Changhun Kim, Eunho Yang

Abstract
Electronic health records (EHR) are irregular, asynchronous multivariate time series. As time-series foundation models increasingly tokenize events rather than discretizing time, the input layout becomes a key design choice. Grids expose time × \times variable structure but require imputation or missingness masks, risking error or sampling-policy shortcuts. Point-set tokenization avoids discretization but loses within-variable trajectories and time-local cross-variable context (Fig. 1 ). We restore these priors in ST ructure- A wa R e ( STAR ) Set Transformer by adding parameter-efficient soft attention biases: a temporal locality penalty − | Δ t | / τ -|\Delta t|/\tau with learnable timescales and a variable-type affinity B s i , s j B_{s_{i},s_{j}} from a learned feature-compatibility matrix. We benchmark 10 depth-wise fusion schedules (Fig. 2 ). On three ICU prediction tasks, STAR-Set achieves AUC/APR of 0.7158/0.0026 (CPR), 0.9164/0.2033 (mortality), and 0.8373/0.1258 (vasopressor use), outperforming regular-grid, event-time grid, and prior set baselines. Learned τ \tau and B B provide interpretable summaries of temporal context and variable interactions, offering a practical plug-in for context-informed time-series models.



1
Introduction

Electronic health records (EHR) are irregularly sampled, asynchronous multivariate time series. Unlike images or text, there is no canonical discretization of time; consequently the
input layout
presented to a neural encoder is a key design choice. Common layouts include (i) discretized
regular grids

(
6
;
4
;
11
)
, (ii)
event-time
(sparse/irregular) grids
(
1
;
2
)
, and (iii)
point-set
tokenizations over observed measurement events
(
3
;
8
)
. Unless stated otherwise, we use
representation
to denote this input layout.

In a
regular-grid
layout, time is binned into fixed-width intervals (e.g., hourly) and measurements are aggregated per bin, yielding a dense tensor in
\mathbb{R}^{T\times V}

(
6
)
. Because most variables are unobserved in most bins, grid models typically require a filling step, ranging from heuristic imputation (e.g., LOCF) to learned interpolation/imputation modules (e.g., mTAN)
(
6
;
7
)
. The
event-time
grid instead indexes time by the union of observed timestamps
\tau
and stores values in a sparse
\mathbb{R}^{|\tau|\times V}
array; however, asynchrony still produces substantial missingness, motivating masks, time-gap features, or additional imputation
(
1
)
.

Attention-based sequence models
(
10
)
further popularized event-time layouts because they can incorporate irregular timestamps via positional/continuous-time encodings and ignore padding through masking. Yet missingness is a double-edged signal in EHR: observation patterns reflect both physiology and care processes, so models can over-rely on missingness indicators and learn sampling-policy shortcuts that fail under domain shift (e.g., different measurement practices across hospitals)
(
1
)
.

Point-set representations avoid discretization by treating each observed event as a token (e.g., value, time, and variable identity) and applying permutation-invariant encoders
(
3
)
. This sidesteps populating unobserved grid entries and reduces dependence on heuristic imputation, but it also removes the two structural axes grids provide by construction: within-variable trajectories (“columns”) and contemporaneous cross-variable relations (“rows”). As illustrated in Figure
1
, these inductive biases become implicit, forcing attention to recover them from data alone.

We address this gap by augmenting point-set EHR encoders with simple, parameter-efficient
attention biases
that re-introduce grid-like structure while preserving set flexibility. We propose (i) a
variable-type bias
that encourages interactions among tokens of the same measurement type and (ii) a
temporal bias
that favors interactions among temporally nearby tokens (Figure
1
). We then study where to inject these biases across Transformer depth and identify an effective layer-fusion strategy via systematic ablations over bias type and insertion schedule (Figure
2
). More broadly, our approach echoes factorization choices in video modeling, where separating temporal dynamics from channel interactions improves efficiency
(
9
)
.

Our contributions are:

1.

Biasing set attention for irregular EHR:

We introduce
STAR-Set Transformer
(vt–vt in Figure
2
), which augments point-set EHR encoders with simple
additive attention biases
to recover grid-like inductive structure without discretization.

2.

Two complementary, parameter-efficient biases:

We combine (i) a
temporal bias
that favors temporally proximal interactions via a learnable time-distance penalty, and (ii) a
variable-type bias
parameterized by a learnable type-compatibility matrix.

3.

Systematic layer-wise ablations and consistent gains:

We evaluate bias injection schedules across transformer depth and identify effective layer-fusion strategies, yielding consistent improvements on multiple ICU prediction tasks over grid and set-based baselines.

(e), favoring same-variable interactions, and a

(f), favoring temporally proximal interactions.

Method

2.1
Set representation and its embedder for EHR

We represent an EHR episode as an irregular, asynchronous
set
of events
(
3
)
.
Given a mini-batch of size
B
, we pack episodes into a tensor

{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times L\times 3}
, where each event is a triplet

{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{b,i,:}=\big(t_{b,i},\,v_{b,i},\,s_{b,i}\big),

(1)

with timestamp
t_{b,i}\in\mathbb{R}
, observed value
v_{b,i}\in\mathbb{R}
, and variable/type index

s_{b,i}\in\{0,\dots,F-1\}
. Each episode has an effective length
\ell_{b}\leq L

(
input_lengths
\in\mathbb{N}^{B}
); indices
i>\ell_{b}
are padding.
We use ITE
(
8
)
as the set embedder and denote its output token sequence by

{\bm{H}}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times D}
with
S=L+2
(two special tokens plus
L
event tokens).
The two special tokens are a learned
[CLS]
and a demographic token (
demo
)
from a Linear–Tanh projection of age/sex.

2.2
Transformer encoder with soft attention biases

STAR-Set Transformer encodes
{\bm{H}}^{(0)}
with an
L_{\mathrm{enc}}
-layer (4 in this study)
pre-norm Transformer encoder (
H
heads, GELU activations). Our core modification is to add

soft additive
biases to the attention logits, injecting (i) a temporal locality prior and
(ii) a variable-type prior that are explicit in grid/sparse layouts but not in set tokenizations
(Figure
1
). See Appendix
A.1
for more details.

Figure 2:
Layer-wise fusion strategies for soft attention biases in the set encoder.

Each panel illustrates a bias schedule applied across Transformer encoder layers (stacked blocks from early/lower to late/upper) on top of the set embedder.
We ablate
no bias
(nb),
temporal bias
(tb),
variable-type bias
(vb), and their combination (vt).
The shorthand “x–y” denotes using bias x in the lower layers and bias y in the upper layers:
(a) nb–nb, (b) tb–tb, (c) vb–vb, (d) nb–tb, (e) tb–nb, (f) nb–vb, (g) vb–nb, (h) vb–tb, (i) tb–vb, and (j) vt–vt. We denote vt–vt as our proposed STAR Set Transformer.

Temporal bias.

For each layer
\ell
and head
h
, we learn a timescale and define an additive time-distance
penalty between query token
i
and key token
j
:

b^{(\ell,h)}_{\mathrm{time}}(b,i,j)\;=\;-\frac{\big|\tilde{t}_{b,i}-\tilde{t}_{b,j}\big|}{\tau_{\ell,h}},\ \ \tau_{\ell,h}=\exp(\omega_{\ell,h})+\varepsilon.

(2)

Variable-type bias.

For each layer
\ell
and head
h
, we learn a type affinity matrix
{\bm{B}}_{\ell,h}\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times F}
.
The variable-type bias is

b^{(\ell,h)}_{\mathrm{var}}(b,i,j)\;=\;\begin{cases}({\bm{B}}_{\ell,h})_{\tilde{s}_{b,i},\,\tilde{s}_{b,j}},&\tilde{s}_{b,i},\tilde{s}_{b,j}\in\{0,\dots,F-1\},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3)

Biased attention logits.

Let
d_{h}=D/H
,
{\bm{Q}}^{(\ell,h)},{\bm{K}}^{(\ell,h)},{\bm{V}}^{(\ell,h)}
be the standard projections, and

m^{\mathrm{pad}}_{b,j}
be the key padding mask (Appendix
A
). We modify the attention
logits as

\displaystyle A^{(\ell,h)}_{b,i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle{\bm{q}}^{(\ell,h)}_{b,i},{\bm{k}}^{(\ell,h)}_{b,j}\rangle}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}\;+\;\bm{1}\!\left[\texttt{bias\_type}[\ell]\in\{\texttt{tb},\texttt{vtb}\}\right]\,b^{(\ell,h)}_{\mathrm{time}}(b,i,j)

\displaystyle\quad+\;\bm{1}\!\left[\texttt{bias\_type}[\ell]\in\{\texttt{vb},\texttt{vtb}\}\right]\,b^{(\ell,h)}_{\mathrm{var}}(b,i,j)\;+\;m^{\mathrm{pad}}_{b,j}.

(4)

We then apply the usual
\mathrm{softmax}
over
j
and compute the attention-weighted sum of values.

Layer-wise bias schedule.

At each encoder layer
\ell
we choose

\texttt{bias\_type}[\ell]\in\{\texttt{nb},\texttt{tb},\texttt{vb},\texttt{vtb}\},\qquad\ell=1,\dots,L_{\mathrm{enc}},

(5)

where
nb
applies no bias,
tb
applies temporal bias only,
vb
applies
variable-type bias only, and
vtb
applies both. In our ablations we often use two-stage
schedules of the form “
a-b
”, meaning
a
in the lower layers and
b
in the
upper layers (Figure
2
).

Prediction and supervised objective.

After the final encoder layer and layer normalization, we use the
[CLS]
embedding as the
episode representation
{\bm{z}}_{b}={\bm{H}}^{(L_{\mathrm{enc}})}_{b,0,:}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and predict

\hat{y}_{b}={\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{z}}_{b}+c
. For binary outcomes
y_{b}\in\{0,1\}
, we train end-to-end with the
sigmoid cross-entropy loss (BCE with logits):

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathrm{BCEWithLogits}\!\left(\hat{y}_{b},\;y_{b}\right).

(6)

Discussion and Conclusion

Table
1
shows that
STAR Set Transformer
achieves the best results on all reported tasks and metrics. It attains AUC/APR of 0.7158/0.0026 on CPR, 0.9164/0.2033 on Mortality, and 0.8373/0.1258 on Vasopressor. Relative to the strongest baselines, this corresponds to gains of +0.0680 AUC and +0.0008 APR on CPR, +0.0386 AUC and +0.0576 APR on Mortality, and +0.0118 AUC and +0.0149 APR on Vasopressor. Overall, STAR Set Transformer delivers consistent improvements over regular-grid, event-time-grid, and prior set-based approaches on these ICU prediction tasks.

B.2
Depth-wise fusion schedules

To isolate depth effects with minimal combinatorial growth, we use two-stage schedules of the form

a-b
, where
a
is applied to the
lower
half of layers and
b
to the

upper
half. With
L_{\mathrm{enc}}{=}4
, this corresponds to layers
\{1,2\}
(early) and

\{3,4\}
(late). We evaluate the 10 schedules depicted in Fig.
2
:

What these schedules test.

The uniform settings (
tb-tb
,
vb-vb
,
vt-vt
) estimate the marginal value
of each inductive prior when applied throughout depth.
The swap schedules (
nb-tb
vs.
tb-nb
and
nb-vb
vs.
vb-nb
)
test whether a given prior is more beneficial in early feature construction or late semantic
integration.
Finally,
vb-tb
and
tb-vb
test whether
ordering
the priors (type-aware
mixing vs. temporal locality) across depth yields complementary benefits.

To study
where
to inject attention biases, we fix the encoder depth to
L_{\mathrm{enc}}{=}4
and evaluate a set of two-stage schedules (Table
3
). We denote no bias as
nb
, temporal bias as
tb
, variable-type bias as
vb
, and their combination as
vtb
(
vt
in the table). A schedule “
a-b

a

Table
4
reports downstream performance. Temporal bias is the main driver of AUC gains:
tb-tb
achieves the best average AUC (0.832), largely due to a large improvement on CPR (0.600
\rightarrow

) yields smaller but consistent improvements over

. Combining both biases (

,

) to outperform late-only variants (

,
nb-vb
).

B.4
Experimental protocol and reporting

For each schedule, we train the model under identical optimization, initialization, and data
processing settings; only the per-layer
bias_type
list changes.
We report AUROC and average precision (APR) for each downstream task, and summarize performance
either (i) per task or (ii) by averaging across tasks after normalizing metrics (e.g., mean rank
or mean
\Delta
relative to
nb-nb
).
To reduce variance, we recommend repeating each configuration with multiple random seeds and
reporting mean
\pm
std; all comparisons should use the same validation-based early stopping
criterion to avoid confounding depth schedules with training time.

Appendix C
Limitations and Future Directions

Limitations.

•

Limited hyperparameter sweep for bias parametrization.
We perform only a narrow search over (i) the initialization of the variable-type affinity matrix
{\bm{B}}_{\ell,h}
and (ii) the relative strength/scale of the temporal and variable-type bias terms (including
\tau
initialization). Performance and stability may depend on these choices.

•

Single bias configuration per attention layer.
Our current design assigns one bias setting (
nb
/
tb
/
vb
/
vtb
) to an entire layer, so all heads share the same bias type. This may limit head specialization and prevent simultaneously modeling heterogeneous temporal and type-specific relations within a layer.

•

Supervised-only evaluation.
Experiments are restricted to supervised training on downstream ICU tasks. We do not yet evaluate STAR-Set under self-supervised pretraining, transfer/linear probing, or robustness settings (e.g., sampling-policy shift).

Future directions.

•

Systematic bias tuning and initialization study.
Conduct broader sweeps (or automated search) over
{\bm{B}}
initialization (e.g., identity vs. low-rank/random), bias scaling/regularization, and
\tau
schedules to characterize sensitivity and identify robust defaults.

•

Head-wise (channel-wise) bias routing.
Generalize STAR-Set to allow different heads within a layer to use different bias types or learned mixtures (e.g., gated
tb
/
vb
per head), encouraging explicit specialization for temporal vs. type-driven interactions.

•

Beyond supervised learning.
Integrate biases into self-supervised objectives (e.g., masked event modeling) and evaluate transfer, label efficiency, and domain-shift robustness across cohorts/hospitals.
