Effect of surface treatments on the shear bond strength of PAEK polymers to composite resin: an in vitro study
Authors: Rüştü Ersoy Sakarya, Kaan Yerliyurt, Osayd Alawawda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolyaryletherketone (PAEK) polymers, including polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and polyetherketoneketone (PEKK), are increasingly used in prosthodontics because of their favorable mechanical and biological properties. However, their chemically inert and low–surface-energy characteristics result in insufficient bonding to veneering composite resins. Therefore, effective and clinically safe surface treatment protocols are required to enhance adhesion.MethodsSquare specimens were fabricated from three Computer Aided Design/ Computer Aided Manufacturing (CAD/CAM) PAEK polymers: unfilled PEEK (Copra Peek), ceramic-reinforced PEEK (BioHPP), and PEKK (Pekkton Ivory). Specimens were allocated to six surface-treatment groups: untreated control, CoJet, 98% sulfuric acid etching, Er: YAG laser irradiation, CoJet plus laser, and sulfuric acid plus laser. A methyl methacrylate–based adhesive and veneering composite resin were applied. Half of the specimens were subjected to 10,000 thermocycles. Shear bond strength (SBS) was measured using a universal testing machine. Surface roughness, wettability, and morphology were evaluated using profilometry, contact angle measurements, and scanning electron microscopy. Data were analyzed using ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc tests (α = 0.05).ResultsMost experimental groups demonstrated SBS values above clinically acceptable thresholds; however, lower values were observed in certain laser-treated and thermocycled subgroups. Sulfuric acid etching produced the highest mean SBS values across all three materials, followed by CoJet. Laser irradiation alone consistently yielded the lowest bond strengths (range: 12.07–14.69 MPa) despite generating the highest surface roughness, and a significant moderate negative correlation was identified between surface roughness and SBS (r = − 0.39, P < 0.001). Three-way ANOVA confirmed significant main effects of surface treatment (P < 0.001), material type (P = 0.002), and thermocycling (P = 0.015).ConclusionsDurable adhesion between PAEK polymers and veneering composite resin may be achieved through appropriate surface treatment. Within the limits of this in vitro study, sulfuric acid etching appeared the most effective method, possibly generating a microporous surface that may contribute to enhanced bond strength; however, its clinical application is limited by the hazardous nature of 98% sulfuric acid, and safer alternatives should be explored. Among clinically feasible treatments, CoJet produced consistently high bond strengths and may represent the most clinically translatable protocol. Background

The polyaryletherketone (PAEK) family, comprising high-performance, semi-crystalline thermoplastic polymers such as polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and polyetherketoneketone (PEKK), is widely utilized in the medical profession [1, 2]. These materials are valued for their high biocompatibility and an elastic modulus (≈ 3 − 4 GPa) comparable to that of bone, along with excellent dimensional stability and high fracture resistance [1, 3]. In dentistry, PAEK polymers have been used in a wide range of clinical indications, including dental implants [4] healing abutments [5, 6], temporary abutments [7, 8], removable prostheses fabricated from PEEK [9] and clasp materials [10].

The PEKK polymer has the largest percentage of keto groups among the PAEK family. Manufacturers claim that PEKK outperforms PEEK in terms of strength, with up to 80% more compressive strength, a higher melting temperature, and better glass–polymer chain interactions than PEEK polymer without reinforcement [11]. Among these, PEEK is one of the most widely used high-performance thermoplastics in dental practice [12]. Numerous studies have investigated its wear resistance [13, 14], fracture load [15, 16] retention load and retention forces [17], hardness [18], flexural behavior [19] and color stability [20]. Based on these findings, PEEK has been demonstrated to be a suitable material for fixed prostheses [21] and long-term restorations [18, 19, 22, 23]. However, the low translucency and grayish color of PAEK polymers limit the monolithic fabrication of fixed partial restorations, particularly in the anterior region [24–30]. In the esthetic zone, both PEEK and PEKK require veneering, and the use of an opaquer may be necessary [26, 31, 32]. Although high-performance polymers possess favorable mechanical properties, their inert surfaces necessitate surface conditioning to ensure reliable adhesion to veneering resin materials [29, 33, 34]. To overcome these limitations, several surface treatment techniques have been proposed [33, 35–39].

The exploration of surface modification methodologies, including CoJet, tribochemical coating, etching with hydrofluoric acid, sulfuric acid, or Piranha solution, in addition to a range of laser and plasma techniques, has been the subject of prior research aimed at enhancing the adhesion properties of PEEK to resin composites [35, 40] In addition, the effects of bonding agents with different compositions have been evaluated [41] and methyl methacrylate (MMA)–based bonding agents have shown significant improvements in the bonding strength of PEEK polymer to veneering resins [42]. Particle abrasion techniques applied by air-blasting alumina particles and tribochemical silica particles are recommended as simple and effective surface treatment methods for PAEK polymers. Air abrasion with Al₂O₃ provides a roughened surface, while tribochemical silica coating additionally enhances chemical bonding with the adhesive [2, 43].

Prior to the adhesion of PAEK polymers to resin-based substrates, it is imperative to alter their surface topography, chemically inert composition, and low surface energy through the application of surface treatment methodologies. In this regard, etching with sulfuric acid solution, especially at a concentration of 98%, has been extensively advocated in the literature [43, 44].

Alternative surface treatment techniques such as laser irradiation [29] or plasma processing have been investigated in relation to PEEK polymers, and consistent adhesion outcomes have been documented [37, 38]. The application of lasers presents numerous advantages: they can be directed towards a targeted area, provide various application modalities, and yield more accurate results while incurring minimal surface degradation. Prior studies have demonstrated the efficacy of laser treatment in functionalizing the surfaces of several low-energy polymers, thereby enhancing their applicability across diverse domains [45]. Because PEEK is a thermoplastic polymer, it can be affected by laser treatment [46]. Consequently, laser irradiation emerges as a viable alternative for augmenting bond strength without compromising the intrinsic properties of the material [47].

Therefore, the objective of the current investigation was to evaluate the interfacial adhesion strength between PAEK (PEEK and PEKK) substrates and composite resin veneer materials following various surface treatment modalities. This study specifically integrated Er: YAG laser surface treatment (both alone and combined with CoJet and sulfuric acid etching) to determine the significant impact of this combined methodology on adhesion strength.

The null hypotheses of this study were as follows:

The type of PAEK polymer would not influence the bond strength with composite resin veneer material.

The type of PAEK polymer would not influence the bond strength with composite resin veneer material.

The surface treatment method would not affect the bond strength between PAEK polymers and composite resin veneer material.

The surface treatment method would not affect the bond strength between PAEK polymers and composite resin veneer material.

Thermocycling would have no effect on the bond strength between PAEK polymers and composite resin veneer material.

Thermocycling would have no effect on the bond strength between PAEK polymers and composite resin veneer material.

Methods

Study design and power analysis

An a priori power analysis was performed using statistical software (G*Power version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine the minimum required sample size. The analysis was based on a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) model with 36 experimental groups, assuming a large effect size (f = 0.50), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a desired statistical power of 0.95. Under these conditions, the minimum total sample size required was calculated to be 180 specimens. To enhance the robustness of the statistical analysis and to allow for subgroup comparisons with and without artificial aging, the sample size was doubled, resulting in a total of 360 bonded specimens. These were equally distributed among three material categories: unfilled PEEK–composite (n = 120), reinforced PEEK–composite (n = 120), and PEKK–composite (n = 120). Each experimental subgroup consisted of 10 specimens (Fig. 1).

Study design and specimen distribution flowchart

Specimen preparation

This study employed three distinct CAD/CAM block materials: Copra Peek (Whitepeaks Dental Solutions GmbH), BioHPP (Bredent GmbH), and Pekkton Ivory (Cendres Meˊtaux SA). Square-shaped specimens were initially sectioned from each block using a rotary cutting device. These sections were subsequently sliced into 3 mm-thick specimens using a precision cutting machine [48]. To standardize the bonding surface, the specimens were sequentially polished under water irrigation using silicon carbide abrasive papers of 600, 800, and 1000 grit. Finally, all specimens underwent ultrasonic cleaning in distilled water using an ultrasonic cleaner for five minutes and were then air-dried. The polished, untreated specimens were defined a priori as the baseline reference (control condition) for surface roughness and wettability analyses.

Surface treatment and bonding

Following this rigorous preparation, the specimens were randomly allocated into six separate experimental groups to receive the corresponding distinct surface treatment methodologies detailed in Table 1. After the completion of surface treatment, an adhesive agent (Visio.link, Bredent, Senden) was systematically applied to the PAEK specimens. Subsequently, a composite resin veneering material (Ceramage, Shofu Dental Products Inc.) was meticulously bonded onto the specimens. The veneering composite was first pre-polymerized through a 0.05 mm polyester matrix strip for 60 s at approximately 1300 mW/cm², followed by final polymerization in a laboratory light curing unit (Solidilite V, Shofu Inc.) for 180 s.

Surface treatment protocols

Artificial aging (Simulation)

For aging simulation, half of the specimens in each subgroup were subjected to thermocycling between 5 °C and 55 °C for 10,000 cycles using a thermocycling device (SD Mechatronik, Germany), while the remaining specimens were stored without thermocycling and served as non-aged controls.

Testing and characterization

All specimens underwent shear bond strength testing using a universal testing machine (Shimadzu Autograph AGS-X series) operated at a crosshead speed of 1.0 mm/min until failure occurred.

Additional specimens from each group were prepared for surface characterization using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), profilometry (surface roughness analysis), and contact angle measurements.

The surface morphology of the specimens was evaluated using a scanning electron microscope (Zeiss Sigma 300), with images obtained at an extra high tension (EHT) of 15.00 kV. Surface roughness analysis was performed exclusively on non-thermocycled groups, in accordance with the study design. Profilometric measurements were carried out using an atomic force microscope (AFM; model 5100 N, Hitachi High-Technologies Co.) at a constant scanning rate of 1.6 Hz. Prior to each measurement, the device was carefully calibrated to ensure accuracy.

Exploratory contact angle measurements were performed on a single specimen per treatment group to provide preliminary qualitative insight into surface wettability. A droplet of distilled water was dispensed onto the specimen surface, and images were captured using an Attention Theta Flex goniometer (Biolin Scientific AB, Västra Frölunda) under controlled laboratory conditions (24 °C).

Due to the single-specimen design, no statistical analysis was performed, and the data are considered purely descriptive.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were performed by using a statistical software program (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27; IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics, comprising mean values and standard deviations (SD), were computed for the SBS measurements across all experimental cohorts. The assessment of normality in data distribution was conducted employing the Shapiro–Wilk test, while the evaluation of homogeneity of variances was carried out utilizing Levene’s test. Given that the data satisfied the prerequisites of normality and homogeneity, three-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) were executed to investigate the effects of material type, surface treatment, and thermal cycling on shear bond strength. The mean SBS values were multiply compared by using the Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) test (P = 0.05). Furthermore, interactions among independent variables were assessed to elucidate their collective influence on shear bond strength.

Results

Surface roughness

Descriptive statistics for surface roughness (Ra, µm) across the six treatment groups are presented in Table 2. Surface roughness was evaluated on non-thermocycled specimens only. The polished untreated surfaces served as the baseline reference condition for qualitative comparison; however, quantitative profilometric analysis focused primarily on the treated groups. Among the treated groups, sulfuric acid etching (0.91 ± 0.84 μm) and CoJet (1.04 ± 0.81 μm) produced the lowest mean roughness values. In contrast, Er: YAG laser irradiation-either alone (3.07 ± 2.68 μm) or in combination with CoJet (3.79 ± 2.10 μm) or sulfuric acid (3.30 ± 2.66 μm)-generated the highest roughness values. It is notable that all laser-containing groups exhibited large within-group variability, as reflected by standard deviations comparable in magnitude to their means.

BioHPP

The untreated control groups yielded mean SBS values of 29.25 ± 14.17 MPa (non-aged) and 33.79 ± 11.03 MPa (thermocycled). Among surface treatments, acid etching produced the highest bond strengths (non-aged: 105.32 ± 30.81 MPa; thermocycled: 90.82 ± 18.92 MPa), followed by CoJet (non-aged: 59.62 ± 10.29 MPa; thermocycled: 84.46 ± 17.91 MPa). The Acid+Laser group yielded lower values (non-aged: 21.56 ± 8.36 MPa; thermocycled: 31.19 ± 13.28 MPa), and CoJet+Laser produced similarly low bond strengths (non-aged: 25.19 ± 13.16 MPa; thermocycled: 7.47 ± 4.97 MPa). Laser treatment alone consistently produced the lowest SBS for BioHPP (non-aged: 19.42 ± 17.45 MPa; thermocycled: 9.63 ± 5.93 MPa).

Pekkton ivory

The untreated control groups yielded mean SBS values of 42.36 ± 14.68 MPa (non-aged) and 66.78 ± 21.10 MPa (thermocycled). CoJet treatment produced the highest bond strengths for this material (non-aged: 72.87 ± 25.27 MPa; thermocycled: 89.82 ± 24.81 MPa), followed by acid etching (non-aged: 69.28 ± 15.09 MPa; thermocycled: 61.33 ± 28.23 MPa) and Acid+Laser (non-aged: 54.42 ± 14.40 MPa; thermocycled: 77.39 ± 20.62 MPa). CoJet+Laser (non-aged: 23.08 ± 11.32 MPa; thermocycled: 16.52 ± 7.89 MPa) and laser alone (non-aged: 19.38 ± 6.65 MPa; thermocycled: 9.99 ± 6.50 MPa) produced the lowest values.

Copra peek

The untreated control groups yielded mean SBS values of 22.82 ± 11.59 MPa (non-aged) and 57.60 ± 24.65 MPa (thermocycled). Acid etching produced the highest bond strengths (non-aged: 84.21 ± 25.98 MPa; thermocycled: 109.37 ± 20.28 MPa), followed by CoJet (non-aged: 62.02 ± 21.41 MPa; thermocycled: 77.72 ± 25.00 MPa). Acid+Laser (non-aged: 39.83 ± 16.95 MPa; thermocycled: 22.82 ± 12.55 MPa) and CoJet+Laser (non-aged: 18.19 ± 10.24 MPa; thermocycled: 10.92 ± 5.31 MPa) yielded intermediate and low values, respectively. Laser treatment alone produced the lowest SBS (non-aged: 17.29 ± 8.48 MPa; thermocycled: 6.85 ± 5.85 MPa).

Across all three materials, a general trend in within-material treatment effects was observed: Acid ≥ CoJet > Control > Acid+Laser > CoJet+Laser > Laser. However, this trend does not apply uniformly to all materials. For BioHPP and Copra Peek, acid etching yielded the highest SBS values; however, for Pekkton Ivory, CoJet was superior to acid etching under both non-thermocycled (72.87 vs. 69.28 MPa) and thermocycled (89.82 vs. 61.33 MPa) conditions, representing a material-specific exception. In some subgroups, Acid+Laser also exceeded Control.

One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons confirmed significant differences among treatment groups (P < 0.05). The Laser and CoJet+Laser groups formed the lowest homogeneous subset (P = 0.912), while Acid+Laser formed a second subset. CoJet and Acid groups showed the highest bond strength values and did not differ significantly from each other (P = 1.000) (Table 5).

Effect of thermocycling

The effect of thermocycling varied markedly by surface treatment and material. In laser-treated and CoJet+Laser groups, thermocycling consistently reduced SBS across all three materials. In contrast, CoJet-treated groups showed bond strength increases after thermocycling in all materials (BioHPP: +24.8 MPa; Pekkton Ivory: +17.0 MPa; Copra Peek: +15.7 MPa). Acid-treated groups showed mixed behavior: BioHPP declined slightly (− 14.5 MPa), Pekkton Ivory declined modestly (− 7.96 MPa), while Copra Peek increased substantially (+ 25.2 MPa). Control groups also showed variable thermocycling responses across materials (Figs. 2 and 3).

Three-way ANOVA

A three-way ANOVA evaluating the effects of material type, surface treatment, and thermocycling on SBS is presented in Table 3. The overall model was highly significant (P < 0.001), explaining 78.1% of the total variance (adjusted R²=0.758). Surface treatment was the dominant source of variance (F(5,684) = 389.29, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.740), accounting for 74.0% of explained variance, indicating that the choice of surface treatment had a very large effect on bond strength. Material type was also significant (F(2,684) = 13.21, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.037), though with a small effect size, suggesting that substrate material played a modest but significant role.

Thermocycling reached significance (F(1,684) = 12.60, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.018), with a small effect size. The Material × Surface Treatment interaction was significant with a moderate effect size (F(10,684) = 26.63, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.280), confirming that the effect of surface treatment on SBS differed across substrate materials. The Surface Treatment × Thermocycling interaction was also significant (F(5,684) = 20.85, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.132), indicating that thermocycling affected different treatment groups differently. The three-way Material × Surface Treatment × Thermocycling interaction was significant with a small-to-moderate effect size (F(10,684) = 7.84, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.103), indicating that the combined effect of surface treatment and thermocycling varied across the three substrate materials. The Material × Thermocycling interaction was significant with a negligible effect size (F(2,684) = 3.92, P = 0.020, η²p = 0.011), suggesting that thermocycling had a marginally differential effect across materials when treatments were pooled (Table 3). Post-hoc comparisons showed that BioHPP and Copra Peek did not differ significantly from each other (P = 0.892), while Pekkton Ivory differed significantly from both (Table 4).

Pearson correlation

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted across all non-thermocycled specimens regardless of material or treatment group (n = 180), using the individual AFM-measured Ra values and their corresponding SBS values. A significant moderate negative correlation was identified between post-treatment surface roughness (Ra) and SBS (r = − 0.39, P < 0.001), indicating that higher surface roughness was associated with lower bond strength values. When analyzed separately by material, this negative relationship was consistent across all three substrates: Copra Peek (r = − 0.473, P < 0.001), BioHPP (r = − 0.426, P < 0.001), and Pekkton Ivory (r = − 0.299, P < 0.05).

Contact angle and wettability

Contact angle measurements were performed on a single specimen per treatment group; therefore, the results are descriptive only and were not subjected to statistical analysis. The untreated control groups exhibited contact angles near or slightly above 90°. Sulfuric acid treatment decreased contact angles across all materials, whereas CoJet and CoJet+Laser treatments increased contact angles. Laser treatment showed variable effects depending on the material. Due to the single-specimen design, these findings should be interpreted with caution and are not used to support mechanistic conclusions.

BioHPP (Fig. 4): The untreated control surface (A) displayed a relatively smooth topography with shallow parallel grooves and occasional surface debris consistent with mechanical polishing. CoJet-treated specimens (B) showed a markedly disrupted surface with large irregular flake-like fragments and fractured debris distributed across a roughened substrate, reflecting the mechanical impact of airborne particles. Acid-etched surfaces (C) presented a strikingly uniform, fine microporous texture covering the entire surface, with evenly distributed submicron pores that may be favorable for resin infiltration and micromechanical interlocking. Laser-treated specimens (D) displayed severe surface disruption, with extensive melting, charred fibrous debris, collapsed structures, and spherical resolidified droplets, suggesting significant thermal degradation of the polymer matrix. The CoJet+Laser group (E) showed a similarly chaotic surface with fragmented and partially melted particle remnants overlying a disrupted substrate, combining the features of both individual treatments with no apparent beneficial synergy. The Acid+Laser group (F) was particularly distinctive, revealing large dome-shaped craters with smooth, glassy rims and clearly defined circular openings; these features may suggest that laser irradiation induced localized melting and gas-bubble formation within the pre-etched microporous acid surface, possibly sealing some of the micropores created by acid etching, though further chemical characterization (e.g., XPS or FTIR) would be needed to confirm this hypothesis.

Pekkton ivory (Fig. 5): The untreated control surface (A) showed a relatively flat topography with minor surface irregularities and scattered small debris particles from machining. CoJet treatment (B) produced a moderately roughened surface with irregular surface upheavals, displaced material flaps, and embedded particle remnants, though less fragmented than observed in BioHPP. Acid etching (C) produced the most dramatic morphological transformation in this material, generating large, clearly defined macropores and cavities of varying diameters across the entire surface, with interconnected porosity and smooth pore walls indicative of selective dissolution of the amorphous polymer phase. Laser-treated specimens (D) exhibited extreme surface destruction with extensively folded, torn, and layered debris structures, suggesting pronounced thermomechanical degradation and delamination of the polymer surface. The CoJet+Laser group (E) showed dense accumulation of fragmented and partially fused debris clusters with loss of the underlying surface architecture. The Acid+Laser group (F) revealed large, smooth-walled spherical and oval craters with well-defined raised rims, closely resembling the BioHPP Acid+Laser morphology; these features may suggest laser-induced bubble formation and surface remelting over the acid-etched substrate, possibly resulting in partial sealing of the porous network, though chemical analyses (e.g., XPS or FTIR) would be required to confirm this interpretation.

Copra peek (Fig. 6): The untreated control surface (A) exhibited an irregular topography with shallow pits, cracks, and step-like discontinuities reflecting the milling process, distinct from the smoother polishing-groove pattern seen in BioHPP. CoJet treatment (B) produced a heavily disrupted surface with large angular fragments, displaced flakes, and deep irregular craters, representing the most aggressive CoJet-induced morphological change among the three materials.

Acid etching (C) generated a uniformly fine, homogeneous microporous surface closely resembling the BioHPP acid pattern, with densely and evenly distributed submicron pores across the entire surface area — a morphology highly conducive to resin penetration. Laser treatment (D) caused severe localized destruction with molten material pools, charred debris aggregates, and large voids, indicating substantial thermal damage to the polymer structure. The CoJet+Laser group (E) displayed a complex surface combining fragmented debris with signs of thermal remelting and irregular void formation, with the laser-induced changes masking the initial CoJet roughening. The Acid+Laser group (F) showed large, irregularly shaped open craters and smooth-walled voids alongside residual thermally altered debris, suggesting partial re-opening of laser-sealed pores due to material ejection, while other areas retained glassy resolidified rims characteristic of laser processing.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the bond strength between PAEK (PEEK and PEKK) materials and composite resin veneer materials after various surface treatments, and based on the results, all three null hypotheses were rejected. The study found that the type of PAEK polymer, the surface treatment method, and thermal cycling all had a significant effect on the bond strength. However, the significant three-way interaction identified in the ANOVA (P < 0.001) confirms that the effectiveness of any single surface treatment is not universal; rather, it is highly dependent on both the specific material substrate and the aging conditions.

Clinically, SBS values ≥ 10 MPa are generally regarded as acceptable for durable adhesion under in-vitro conditions [49], while ISO 10,477 defines 5 MPa as the minimum acceptable threshold for bonding between polymer substrates and composite veneer materials [50]. In the present study, all experimental groups exceeded 10 MPa, however, this broad observation masks critical performance disparities. While acid and CoJet groups achieved values far exceeding clinical requirements (reaching up to 109.37 MPa), laser-treated groups often hovered near the lower limits of clinical acceptability, particularly after thermocycling (6.85–9.99 MPa). The very high SBS values observed in certain groups should be interpreted with caution. In the absence of failure mode analysis, it is not possible to determine whether failure occurred at the adhesive interface or cohesively within the composite resin or PAEK substrate. It is well established that SBS testing may overestimate interfacial adhesion when cohesive or mixed failures predominate. Therefore, the elevated values reported in this study may not represent true interfacial bond strength but could partially reflect the intrinsic mechanical strength of the composite or substrate material.

Previous research has shown that BioHPP demonstrates higher SBS compared with unfilled PEEK [51, 52] Our results partially confirm this, though notably, Pekkton Ivory (PEKK) exhibited a distinct response compared to the two PEEK variants, which can be attributed to the presence of 0.3–0.5 μm ceramic filler particles that reinforce PEEK, forming a homogeneous structure with improved mechanical properties [51]. Furthermore, PEKK (Pekkton Ivory) exhibited a distinct response, where CoJet treatment produced the highest bond strengths (up to 89.82 MPa). This suggests that the chemical differences in PEKK—specifically the higher ketone content and potential for hydrogen bonding [53] may favor different conditioning mechanisms than those optimized for PEEK variants. The higher proportion of ketone groups in PEKK increases polarity, enhances chemical interactions, and may provide additional carbonyl functionalities that could improve wettability, adhesion, and potential hydrogen bonding [54]. Moreover, PEKK often shows lower crystallinity and slower crystallization kinetics compared to PEEK [55], which may facilitate better resin penetration and bonding.

Although a variety of methodologies are available for assessing in-vitro bond strength, the most prevalently employed evaluations include the SBS test, the tensile bond strength assessment, and the microtensile bond strength examination. In the SBS assessment, a consistent load is exerted at a predetermined rate until failure transpires at the interface between the adhesive and the substrate. The bond strength is subsequently determined by dividing the peak force achieved by the area of the bonded surface. Due to the relative simplicity and rapid execution of the SBS test, it remains the most frequently utilized approach for appraising the adhesion properties of dental materials [56]. Therefore, in our study, the SBS test was also employed to assess bond strength. However, SBS tests have inherent limitations: they do not measure true interfacial strength (since failure may initiate elsewhere), they can misinterpret cohesive or mixed failures as meaningful bond data, and clinical success often depends more on surface preparation than on laboratory-measured adhesion metrics [57].

For PAEK polymers, attaining sufficient bond strength necessitates the implementation of mechanical, chemical, and hybrid surface-conditioning methodologies [58]. For ensuring a durable bond, an efficacious combined surface treatment is advisable [59]. Taha et al. [60] indicated that a synergistic surface treatment involving laser application and CoJet enhanced SBS while diminishing the contact angle. Similarly, research reported that the application of silica coating or the concurrent use of an Er: YAG laser is appropriate for facilitating bonding prior to the veneering of PEEK frameworks [36]. In alignment with these findings, investigations further underscore that mere surface roughening is insufficient [43, 48]. Which aligns with our finding that all laser groups increased the surface roughness of PAEKs polymers, but showed a negative correlation with bond strength.

Research has substantiated that a one-minute treatment with a 98% sulfuric acid solution may enhance the adhesion efficacy of PAEK polymers to resin-based materials [22, 29, 41, 61].

Schmidlin et al. [44] in their investigation of PEEK specimens, observed that etching with 98% sulfuric acid yielded superior SBS measurements relative to air abrasion employing 50 μm and 110 μm Al2O3 as well as a 110 μm silica coating. Similarly, Stawarczyk et al. [22] reported that the etching of PEEK specimens with 98% sulfuric acid resulted in elevated SBS values compared to those achieved via air abrasion. The conclusions drawn from these two investigations align with the outcomes of our own research. It has been established that the microstructural alterations of the PEEK surface following sulfuric acid etching may have facilitated the infiltration of the resin adhesive, thereby contributing to increased SBS [62]. However, sulfuric acid is considered an unsuitable agent for clinical use [63].

Consistent with the study’s low bond strength values, laser treatment, either alone or in combination with CoJet, did not improve the SBS of the materials tested. This outcome aligns with other studies that reported a lack of improvement in SBS for PAEK polymers following laser irradiation [36, 64], even though some studies have shown the opposite effect with other materials [47, 65, 66]. It should be noted that laser–material interactions are highly dependent on the laser type; while some previous studies have utilized CO₂ lasers, the present study employed an Er: YAG laser, which differs in wavelength and interaction mechanism with polymer substrates [45]. In the present study, the Er: YAG laser was operated at 2940 nm (short pulse mode) with 3 W power, 200 mJ pulse energy, 15 Hz repetition rate, applied at an 8 mm distance for 20 s. Under these conditions, the delivered energy and exposure duration may have been sufficient to induce localized thermal effects, including surface melting and resolidification, rather than controlled micro-ablation. This suggests that the effectiveness of laser treatment is highly material-dependent, a finding echoed by one report that observed high bond strength on PEEK samples but low bond strength on PEKK samples after laser treatment [65]. The high within-group variability in surface roughness among laser-treated specimens (Laser: 3.07 ± 2.68 μm; CoJet+Laser: 3.79 ± 2.10 μm; Acid+Laser: 3.30 ± 2.66 μm), where standard deviations were comparable in magnitude to the group means, may reflect the sensitivity of Er: YAG laser surface modification to local material heterogeneity, including possible regional differences in crystallinity, filler distribution, and surface topography at the point of laser impact. This inconsistency in laser-induced surface modification could potentially contribute to the unpredictable and generally low bond strength values observed in laser-treated groups, as non-uniform surface texturing may result in variable resin infiltration across the bonding interface. It should be noted, however, that these interpretations remain speculative in the absence of chemical surface characterization, and further investigation using techniques such as XPS or FTIR would be required to confirm these proposed mechanisms.

SEM findings from the present study showed morphological degradation in the laser-treated surfaces, which is consistent with another study that also observed a loss of microstructural continuity and suggested that laser over-processing might impair interfacial adaptation. A plausible hypothesis is that this morphological change, while creating surface roughness, may have resulted in limited resin penetration into the laser-induced microchannels [67]. Laser could produce glassy/resolidified rims, microcracks, and recast debris [68]. Even though roughness may increase, the combination of melted rims, sealed pore mouths, altered chemistry, and redeposited debris may prevent effective resin penetration and chemical anchoring-therefore laser-produced microchannels do not necessarily provide the same interconnected microporosity that acid etching does, and may instead impair bonding. The SEM findings from studies on sulfuric acid-etched PEEK, which revealed microporous textures that are highly favorable for resin infiltration and micromechanical retention [69, 70], thereby supporting the superior bond strengths observed in our acid-treated groups. The CoJet - Laser sequence was selected based on the rationale of first establishing tribochemical silica embedding, followed by laser irradiation as an additional surface activation step. It is possible, however, that the laser-induced surface melting and resolidification may have partially buried or displaced the silica particles embedded during CoJet treatment, potentially reducing the available tribochemical bonding sites. This could offer a partial explanation for the relatively modest bond strength improvement observed in the CoJet+Laser group compared with CoJet treatment alone, and is consistent with the SEM findings showing that laser-induced thermal changes partially modified the surface morphology established by the initial CoJet treatment.

Whether reversing the treatment sequence - applying laser irradiation prior to CoJet coating - would better preserve the silica layer and enhance bonding performance remains an interesting question for future investigation.

Contact angle measurements in this study were performed on a single specimen per treatment group, which precludes statistical analysis and prevents any reliable interpretation of wettability trends. Therefore, the data are presented solely as descriptive observations. No conclusions regarding the relationship between surface wettability and bond strength are drawn from these measurements, and they are not used to support mechanistic explanations. These observations should be considered preliminary, and future studies with adequate replication are required to evaluate any potential effects of surface hydrophilicity on bonding performance. Turkkal et al. [63] reported that there was an absence of correlation among roughness, contact angle, and bond strength values for the Nd-YAG laser, sulfuric acid, and control groups [71]. Htay et al. [72] reported that a complex relationship between surface roughness and wettability in the treated PAEKs polymers. The varied effects of laser treatment on wettability-making some materials more hydrophilic while slightly increasing the contact angle for another-and the consistently high bond strength of the hydrophilic acid-treated surfaces, may suggest a complex relationship between surface roughness and wettability. It is noted that CoJet and CoJet+Laser treatments appeared to increase contact angles, while acid etching appeared to decrease them across materials; however, given that contact angle measurements were performed on only a single specimen per group, no statistical analysis was possible and no conclusions regarding wettability or its relationship to bond strength can be drawn from this study. These observations are presented solely as descriptive, hypothesis-generating data, and future studies with adequate replication (n ≥ 5) are needed before any mechanistic interpretations can be proposed. While CoJet and the combined CoJet-and-laser treatments created highly hydrophobic surfaces with significantly increased contact angles (up to 150.6∘), these treatments did not produce bond strengths comparable to the acid-treated groups.

This indicates that while roughness may be a contributing factor, the creation of new functional groups or a more receptive surface chemistry via acid etching is the primary mechanism for achieving a durable bond. However, only one specimen per treatment group was used for contact angle measurements. which in turn could limit the generalizability of the findings on the correlation between wettability and bond strength. When polymers such as PEEK are irradiated with relatively high laser energy density or prolonged exposure, thermal degradation can occur at the surface. This process leads to carbonization or char layer formation, where the polymer chains break down and carbon-rich structures remain on the surface [73]. Surface carbonization has been directly observed in studies of laser-heated PEEK, where extended or intense laser exposure produced carbonized/char layers on the surface at and near the thermal limit of the material [73].

It should be noted that the CoJet system used in this study employs 30 μm silica-coated alumina particles, which differ from the larger Rocatec Plus particles (110 μm) used in many recent PAEK studies [74, 75]. However, as no statistically significant difference in bond strength was detected between CoJet- and Rocatec-treated PEEK specimens in those studies [74, 75], the choice of CoJet in the present study is unlikely to have substantially influenced the bonding outcomes, and comparability with the broader literature is maintained.

Despite demonstrating bond strengths above clinical acceptance thresholds, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its in-vitro design cannot fully replicate the complex mechanical, thermal, and chemical environment of the oral cavity, and the clinical durability of the tested protocols requires validation through long-term in-vivo studies with chewing simulation. Second, the highest bond strengths were achieved using 98% sulfuric acid, which is not a clinically viable treatment option due to its hazardous nature; accordingly, these results should be interpreted as a laboratory benchmark rather than a clinical recommendation, and future efforts should focus on identifying safer alternatives capable of replicating the microporous surface architecture created by acid etching. Third, contact angle measurements were performed on only one specimen per treatment group; therefore, no conclusions regarding wettability or its relationship to bond strength can be drawn from this study.

All wettability observations are purely descriptive, and future studies with adequate replication (n ≥ 5) and statistical analysis are required before any relationship between surface wettability and bonding performance can be established. Fourth, no silanization step was performed following CoJet tribochemical silica coating, as the MMA-based adhesive used in this study promotes bonding primarily through MMA-PAEK interaction rather than Si–O–Si bond formation; this may have underestimated the true bonding potential of tribochemical silica coating when combined with a silane-containing adhesive system. Fifth, failure mode analysis was not performed, which limits the interpretability of the reported SBS values, particularly for higher-strength groups where cohesive failures within the composite or substrate cannot be excluded. Finally, the absence of chemical surface characterization techniques such as XPS or FTIR prevented direct verification of the proposed bonding mechanisms, which therefore remain partially speculative. Future research should address these limitations by incorporating quantitative baseline and post-treatment surface profilometry, failure mode analysis, adequate replication of surface characterization measurements, silane-based adhesive protocols, and advanced surface analytical techniques.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, sulfuric acid etching produced the highest load-at-failure values across all three PAEK materials and may serve as a useful laboratory benchmark.

However, because of the hazardous nature of 98% sulfuric acid, this protocol is not suitable for direct clinical application. Among the clinically feasible surface treatments, CoJet demonstrated consistently high load-at-failure values across all materials and aging conditions, representing the most clinically translatable protocol evaluated in this study. Future research should focus on identifying safer chemical alternatives capable of reproducing the microporous surface architecture achieved by sulfuric acid etching.
