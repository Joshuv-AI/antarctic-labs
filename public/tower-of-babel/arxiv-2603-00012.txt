Minimum-weight frames under subresonant harmonic excitation: Robust constraints on dynamic compliance, peak input power, and eigenfrequencies
Marek Tyburec, Marouan Handa, Jan Havelka, Jan Zeman

Abstract
This work addresses minimum-weight design of undamped Euler–Bernoulli frame structures under subresonant single-frequency harmonic excitations, focusing on (robust) dynamic compliance and (robust) peak input power with ellipsoidal load uncertainty. We develop a semidefinite reformulation of robust dynamic compliance for subresonant single - frequency excitation and prove its equivalence to robust peak input power. We show that both these response measures admit an exact reformulation as a free-vibration eigenvalue constraint with design-independent mass augmentation, unifying static, dynamic, and modal requirements. Despite the nonconvex polynomial dependence on cross-sectional areas, certified bounds on global minimizers are obtained via the moment-sum-of-squares hierarchy of semidefinite relaxations. Benchmark studies on 10- and 35-segment frames corroborate the theory. For the 10-segment problem, we obtain the global optimum; for the 35-segment frame, we find high-quality locally-optimal designs that substantially improve on the best known one. We further fabricate and experimentally validate an additional design that closely matches the predictions of the model.



1
Introduction

Designing minimum-weight frame structures that remain safe and efficient under dynamic excitations is a central challenge in structural optimization
[
9
,
14
,
17
]
. Beyond classical static constraints, engineers need to control response quantities driven by harmonic loads, such as dynamic compliance (maximum instantaneous force-displacement product)
[
15
]
and power (maximum instantaneous force-velocity product)
[
6
]
. Both quantities govern vibration levels, fatigue, and energy transfer, and thus strongly influence weight-optimal designs.

1.1
Topology optimization under dynamic constraints

For truss topology optimization using ground structure discretization, convex formulations for static compliance
[
3
]
, multi-load robustness
[
2
,
10
]
, and eigenfrequency constraints
[
1
]
have been developed, enabling reliable global optimization via interior-point methods.

For harmonic loads, the use of energy-based and power-based metrics as objective functions in dynamic topology optimization is well-established. A seminal contribution by
Jog 2002
proposed optimizing the average input power, a global measure that effectively reduces vibrations by shifting natural frequencies away from the excitation frequency. Another common objective is the minimization of dynamic compliance
[
15
]
, which quantifies the maximum displacement response under harmonic loading. However, the dynamic compliance functional has a significant drawback
[
17
]
: for excitation frequencies above the first resonance, optimization converges to antiresonances that are typically associated with poorly defined topologies.
Silva et al. 2020
demonstrated that the use of the active input power, the real part of the complex input power, is more robust because its frequency spectrum is free of such antiresonances.

For peak input power under harmonic loads,
Heidari et al. 2009
showed that when (i) all nodal forces are in phase and (ii) the driving frequency stays below the first resonance, the peak input power minimization problem admits a clean semidefinite programming (SDP) reformulation; they also derived the worst-case formulation over unit-magnitude forces that remains SDP-representable. Building on this,
Ma et al. 2025
recently developed SDP-representable relaxations for general (possibly out-of-phase, multi-frequency) harmonic loads acting below the first resonance using semidefinite-representable functions and nonnegativity of trigonometric polynomials. Their approach reduces to Heidari’s in the in-phase/single-frequency case. However, extension to loads above the first resonance remains an open problem.

In contrast to trusses, whose stiffness is linear in the element areas
a_{e}
, the Euler–Bernoulli frame stiffness is polynomial in the cross-sectional parameters because the bending stiffness involves the second moment of area
I_{e}

[
20
, Appendix A]
; with selfsimilar scaling
I_{e}\propto a_{e}^{2}
, while one-direction scaling (e.g., changing height only) gives
I_{e}\propto a_{e}^{3}
. Consequently, free
-
vibration–constrained weight minimization is nonconvex and may have feasible sets disconnected due to local resonance of slender members
[
13
]
, and repeated eigenvalues
[
1
]
. Relaxation strategies and nonlinear SDP methods have been proposed to navigate this landscape
[
21
,
20
]
. Recent advances show that such frame problems can nevertheless be tackled
globally
via polynomial optimization: the problem is cast as a polynomial SDP over a compact semialgebraic set and then solved by a hierarchy of moment-sum-of-squares (mSOS) relaxations that produce monotone lower bounds and, with suitable constructions, matching feasible upper bounds and certificates of global
\varepsilon
-optimality
[
20
]
.

1.2
Aims and contributions

Motivated by the above, we develop a unified treatment of dynamic compliance and peak input power constraints for undamped frame structures under single-frequency, uniform-phase subresonant harmonic loads. Our contributions are:

1.

Unified semidefinite reformulations.
We derive SDP constraints for (robust) dynamic compliance and (robust) peak input power under ellipsoidal load uncertainty and show that both are
equivalent
to a free-vibration eigenvalue constraint with a modified, design-independent mass term. This puts dynamic objectives in the same class as standard free-vibration eigenvalue constraints.

2.

Mode-worst-case equivalence.
We prove that the worst-case displacement/velocity amplitudes associated with the robust dynamic compliance and peak input power constraints coincide with the fundamental eigenmode of the equivalent free-vibration problem, which clarifies the structure of worst-case states and links the three performance measures.

3.

Global optimization pipeline.
Because the equivalent constraint has the free-vibration eigenvalue form, we can deploy the global polynomial-optimization framework for frames (mSOS hierarchy on an Archimedean set with a non-mixed-term monomial basis as presented in
[
20
]
) to obtain certified lower/upper bounds and, in small benchmarks, global optimality certificates.

4.

Continuity to the static case.
Taking
\omega\to 0
in the SDP formulations recovers the classical robust static compliance constraint of
Ben-Tal and Nemirovski 1997
, providing a continuous bridge from dynamic compliance and peak input power to robust static compliance.

On trusses, our peak input power constraints reduce to the known convex SDP form of
Heidari et al. 2009
in the single-frequency, in-phase case (and connect to
Ma et al. 2025
in the broader multi-frequency setting). On frames, the key novelty is to embed these dynamic constraints into a free-vibration eigenvalue surrogate with a design-independent mass augmentation, which unlocks global solution strategies previously developed for frame eigenvalue problems
[
20
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
1.3
introduces notations and preliminaries. Section
2.1

2.2
provides a moment-based formulation. We then derive semidefinite programs for dynamic compliance and peak input power (Sections
3.1
and
3.2
) and prove their equivalence to free-vibration eigenvalue constraints, together with the state fields relations (Section
3.3
). Section
4
reports numerical results on two benchmarks (
10

35

We conclude in Section
5
.

2
Background: Weight optimization under free-vibration eigenvalue and static compliance constraints

Weight optimization of frame structures under free-vibration eigenvalue and static compliance constraints represents a fundamental challenge in structural engineering. In this section, we summarize the results for this problem as developed in
[
20
]
.

2.1
Optimization problem formulation

Consider a frame structure discretized into
n_{\mathrm{e}}
Euler–Bernoulli finite elements. The primary design variables are the cross-sectional areas
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}_{\geq 0}
of these elements. The objective is to minimize the structural weight, which is the sum of individual element weights

\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\rho_{e}\ell_{e}a_{e},

(2.1)

where
\rho_{e}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
represents the material density and
\ell_{e}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
the length of an element
e
. However, the design must also satisfy static and dynamic performance requirements. The static behavior is governed by the equilibrium equation
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f}
, where
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}_{\succeq 0}
is the global stiffness matrix,
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
represents nodal displacements,
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
denotes external forces, and
n_{\mathrm{dof}}
the number of degrees of freedom. A crucial distinction from truss structures is that the frame’s stiffness matrix includes both membrane and bending effects, leading to a polynomial dependence on the design variables
[
20
, Appendix A]

\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})=\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\left[a_{e}\mathbf{K}^{(1)}_{e}+a_{e}^{2}\mathbf{K}^{(2)}_{e}+a_{e}^{3}\mathbf{K}^{(3)}_{e}\right].

(2.2)

Here,
a_{e}\mathbf{K}^{(1)}_{e}
captures the membrane (axial) stiffness, while
a_{e}^{2}\mathbf{K}^{(2)}_{e}
and
a_{e}^{3}\mathbf{K}^{(3)}_{e}
represent bending effects. Hence, the maximal polynomial degree in
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})
is
d_{\mathrm{K}}=3
. For all these matrices, we have by their construction
\mathbf{K}_{e}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}_{\succeq 0},i=1,2,3
, so they belong to the space of real symmetric positive semidefinite matrices
\mathbb{S}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}_{\succeq 0}
.

The dynamic behavior is governed by the mass matrix
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}_{\succeq 0}
, which has a linear dependence on the design variables

\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{M}^{(0)}+\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}a_{e}\mathbf{M}^{(1)}_{e},

(2.3)

where
\mathbf{M}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}_{\succeq 0}
represents nonstructural (e.g., lumped) masses and
\mathbf{M}^{(1)}_{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}_{\succeq 0}
are the (stiffness-consistent) element mass matrices.

With these matrices defined, we can formulate the constraints on structural performance. First, under applied loads
\mathbf{f}
, the compliance (work done by external forces) should not exceed prescribed limit
\overline{c}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,

\mathbf{f}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}\leq\overline{c}.

(2.4)

Second, the fundamental free-vibration eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}
should be at least
\underline{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, which, using the Rayleigh quotient, can be expressed as

\underline{\lambda}\leq\lambda_{\min}:=\inf_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}\setminus\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)}\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{w}}.

(2.5)

We note that
\lambda_{\min}
is the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of the generalized eigenvalue problem
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{w}=\lambda\,\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{w}
, with an eigenvector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
. The exclusion of the kernel of
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})
in (
2.5
) accounts for possible rigid-body modes, ensuring
\lambda_{\min}
refers to the smallest positive eigenvalue. Since
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a}),\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}_{\succeq 0}
, the spectrum is real and nonnegative. The value of
\lambda_{\min}
relates to the first angular and natural frequencies as
\omega_{\min}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}
and
f_{\min}=\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}/(2\pi)
.

Through the generalized Schur complement (Lemma
2
) and properties of positive semidefinite matrices, this problem can be reformulated as a semidefinite program
[
1
,
20
]

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\rho_{e}\ell_{e}a_{e}

(2.6a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\succeq\mathbf{0},

(2.6b)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\overline{c}&-\mathbf{f}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathbf{f}&\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\end{pmatrix}\succeq\mathbf{0},

(2.6c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\geq\mathbf{0}.

(2.6d)

Constraint (
2.6b
) is equivalent to enforcing
\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]\mathbf{w}\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{w}
, i.e.,
\underline{\lambda}\leq\lambda_{\min}
. Constraint (
2.6c
) is the Schur complement form of
\mathbf{f}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}\leq\overline{c}
with equilibrium
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f}
, and (
2.6d
) restricts the cross-sectional areas to be nonnegative.

Formulation (
2.6
) elegantly handles issues of nondifferentiability for repeated eigenvalues, which would be present when relying on (
2.5
). However, it remains challenging due to the polynomial dependence of
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})
on

and the potential disconnectedness of the feasible set
[

]
, necessitating specialized solution techniques.

To make the feasible set compact, which is needed for convergence of relaxations introduced later, we replace (
2.6d
) with compactifying constraints that tighten (
2.6d
) as

\displaystyle a_{e}\left(\frac{\overline{w}}{\rho_{e}\ell_{e}}-a_{e}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\forall e\in\{1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{e}}\},

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\overline{w}-\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\rho_{e}\ell_{e}a_{e}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(2.7b)

where
\overline{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
represents an upper bound on the structural weight. This bound is initially obtained from solving a convex linearization of problem (
2.6
)
[
20
, Lemma 3.9]
and can be iteratively tightened during the solution process as better feasible designs become available. The first constraint in (
2.7
) provides bounds on individual cross-sectional areas, while the second one bounds their weighted sum.
These constraints ensure the feasible set is (algebraically) compact, which is needed for the convergence of the mSOS hierarchy.

2.2
Global solution using moment-sum-of-squares hierarchy

To solve the frame optimization problem globally, we employ polynomial optimization techniques. Here, we closely follow and summarize the results of
[
20
]
. The key idea is to view our problem as optimization over probability measures supported on the compact feasible set, rather than directly over the cross-sectional areas. Thus, instead of finding optimal cross-sectional areas, we work with (pseudo-)moments
\mathbf{y}
of these measures, where each component represents an expectation of a monomial. This apparently more complex viewpoint leads to a sequence of tractable convex problems that converge to the global optimum.

To convert our polynomial optimization problem into a moment problem, we use the Riesz functional
L_{\mathbf{y}}
. Let
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{a}]_{r}
denote the space of real polynomials in
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n_{\mathrm{e}}})
of total degree at most
r
, and let

\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{a})=\big(a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots a_{n_{\mathrm{e}}}^{\alpha_{n_{\mathrm{e}}}}\big)_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq r}

(2.8)

be the associated monomial basis of
\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{a}]_{r}
, ordered in some fixed but arbitrary way. We introduce a vector

\mathbf{y}=(y_{\bm{\alpha}})_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq r}\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{a}]_{r}},

(2.9)

called a pseudo-moment vector, whose components are indexed consistently with the monomials in
\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{a})
. The linear functional
L_{\mathbf{y}}:\mathbb{R}[\mathbf{a}]_{r}\to\mathbb{R}
is then defined by its action on monomials:

L_{\mathbf{y}}\big(a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots a_{n_{\mathrm{e}}}^{\alpha_{n_{\mathrm{e}}}}\big)\;=\;y_{\bm{\alpha}},

(2.10)

i.e.,
L_{\mathbf{y}}
replaces each monomial
a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots a_{n_{\mathrm{e}}}^{\alpha_{n_{\mathrm{e}}}}
by the component of
\mathbf{y}
corresponding to the index of that monomial in the basis
\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{a})
. By linearity, for any (matrix) polynomial

\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{a})=\sum_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq r}\mathbf{P}_{\bm{\alpha}}\,a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots a_{n_{\mathrm{e}}}^{\alpha_{n_{\mathrm{e}}}}

(2.11)

we have

L_{\mathbf{y}}\big(\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{a})\big)\;=\;\sum_{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert\leq r}\mathbf{P}_{\bm{\alpha}}\,y_{\bm{\alpha}}.

(2.12)

Using this operator and denoting by

the canonical basis, i.e., vector of all monomials up to degree

\displaystyle L_{\mathbf{y}}\left(\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\rho_{e}\ell_{e}a_{e}\right)

\displaystyle L_{\mathbf{y}}\left(\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{b}_{r}(\mathbf{a})^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\succeq\mathbf{0},

Methods

In this section, we develop semidefinite programming formulations for the minimization of weight under robust dynamic compliance (Section
3.1
) and robust peak input power (Section
3.2
) constraints under harmonic loads in phase of a frequency below the first resonance. For both of these formulations, we show in Section

that we summarized in Section

. Finally, we provide a relation between the worst-case state fields associated with robust dynamic compliance and robust peak input power constraints with the eigenmode of the equivalent free-vibration eigenvalue problem.

3.1
Robust dynamic compliance constraints under single-frequency, uniform-phase harmonic loads

We start by developing semidefinite programming reformulation of (robust) dynamic compliance constraints, which are common in topology optimization problems under dynamic loads; see, e.g.,
[
16
]
. First, we consider the single-load case, where the dynamic compliance is defined as the maximum of the instantaneous force-displacement product
[
15
]
. We then extend this formulation to the robust setting, where the dynamic compliance is constrained to be below a given threshold for all possible loads within a given ellipsoidal uncertainty set.

3.1.1
Single load case semidefinite programming formulation

We begin with the single load case setting, for which the weight minimization problem under a dynamic compliance constraint reads as

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}},\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{n}\rho_{e}\ell_{e}a_{e}

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(t)^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{u}(t)\leq\overline{d},\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{u}(t)+\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\mathbf{f}(t),

(3.1c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\geq\mathbf{0},

(3.1d)

where
\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
and
\mathbf{f}(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
are time
t
dependent harmonic displacements and forces, respectively. In particular, we assume the following form of the transient forces with the angular frequency
\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}=1
, and the amplitude vector
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(t)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\cos(\omega t)\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}+c_{2}\sin(\omega t)\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}(c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}-ic_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}})+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}(c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}+ic_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}).

This formulation expresses the harmonic load as a sum of complex exponentials, which allows us to solve the differential equation (
3.1c
) using the method of undetermined coefficients. Since the system is linear, we expect the displacement response to have the same harmonic form as the applied load, but with different amplitudes,

\mathbf{u}(t)=\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Re}}-i\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Im}})+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Re}}+i\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Im}}),

(3.3)

where
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Re}},\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Im}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
are the real and imaginary amplitude vectors, respectively. Substituting these expressions for
\mathbf{f}(t)
and
\mathbf{u}(t)
into the state equation (
3.1c
) yields partitioning based on the real and complex parts as

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}\right),

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Im}}\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}-\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}\right)i

\displaystyle=c_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}-\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}\right)i,

(3.4b)

yielding

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{A}}=\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{if}\quad\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathcal{R}\!\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right),

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{A}}=\emptyset\quad

\displaystyle\text{otherwise},

(3.5b)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Re}}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{A}},

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{Im}}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{A}}.

(3.6b)

Here,
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse; under
\omega^{2}<\lambda_{\min}
and
\mathbf{a}>\mathbf{0}
it coincides with the inverse on the relevant subspace. All amplitude vectors are real, and we use
(\cdot)^{\mathsf{T}}
throughout.

Consequently, the dynamic compliance in (
3.1b
) satisfying (
3.1c
) evaluates as

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\mathbf{f}(t)^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{u}(t)

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}=\frac{1}{4}\sup_{t}\left[e^{\omega ti}(c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}-ic_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}})+e^{-\omega ti}(c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}+ic_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}})\right]^{\mathsf{H}}\\
\left[e^{\omega ti}(c_{1}\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{A}}-ic_{2}\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{A}})+e^{-\omega ti}(c_{1}\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{A}}+ic_{2}\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{A}})\right]\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\left[c_{1}\cos(\omega t)+c_{2}\sin(\omega t)\right]^{2}.

To provide a semidefinite programming formulation for the dynamic compliance constraint for
\mathbf{a}\geq\mathbf{0}
, we further restrict
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\succeq 0
. Notice that, based on Section
2.1
, this is equivalent to requiring that
\omega^{2}\leq\lambda_{\min}
, where
\lambda_{\min}
is the lowest nonzero eigenvalue of the generalized free-vibration eigenvalue problem
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{w}-\lambda\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{0}
. Such restriction makes the dynamic compliance physically meaningful
[
17
]
and can often be found implicitly implemented, e.g.,
[
16
]
.

Enforcing this restriction, it follows that
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\geq 0
. Because of
c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}=1
, we can eliminate explicit dependence on the time
t
in (
3.7
) by explicitly evaluating
\sup_{t}(c_{1}\cos(\omega t)+c_{2}\sin(\omega t))^{2}
, yielding

d=\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}.

(3.8)

The final expression in equation (
3.8
) reveals that the instantaneous force-displacement product reaches its maximum when the phase angle between force and displacement aligns perfectly, which occurs at the time
t=\frac{1}{\omega}\arctan\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}
with the period of
\pi/\omega
.

Consequently, we can state the semidefinite version of the dynamic compliance constraints as follows.

Proposition 1

(Dynamic compliance matrix inequality)
.

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\leq\overline{d}
,
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathcal{R}\!\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)
,
\overline{d}>0
and
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\succeq 0
,

2.

\begin{pmatrix}\overline{d}&-\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}&\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

Proof.

The proof follows from a direct application of Lemma
2
.
∎

3.1.2
Robust settings

The recently developed semidefinite constraint (
3.8
) can be extended to a robust setting, where dynamic compliance is constrained to be below a given threshold
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}
for all possible loads within a given set of ellipsoidal uncertainties. In the spirit of
[
2
]
, we restrict the loads within an ellipsoid
\{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}:\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1\}
with the matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}\times q}
scaling the
q
-dimensional unit ball
\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1
. Hence, we require that

\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}\geq d_{\mathrm{R}}:=\sup_{\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\sup_{t}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}(t,\mathbf{e})^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{u}(t,\mathbf{e})

(3.9)

with

\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}(t,\mathbf{e})=c_{1}\cos(\omega t)\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}+c_{2}\sin(\omega t)\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}.

(3.10)

Analogously to the previous section, we obtain

d_{\mathrm{R}}=\sup_{\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\left(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}

(3.11)

with
\mathcal{R}\!\left(\mathbf{Q}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{R}\!\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)
. The robust dynamic compliance constraint (
3.9
) then requires

\{\forall\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1\}:\qquad\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}-\mathbf{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}\geq 0.

(3.12)

After enforcing
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\succeq 0
, we also have
\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\!\big[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\big]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\succeq 0
. By the Rayleigh-Ritz principle,
d_{\mathrm{R}}=\sup_{\|\mathbf{e}\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathbf{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
and hence

\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{I}_{q}-\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\succeq 0.

(3.13)

This formulation transforms our constraint from an infinite set of inequalities (one for each possible load in the uncertainty set) into a single matrix inequality constraint.

Applying the generalized Schur complement (Lemma
2
) to (
3.13
) yields

\begin{pmatrix}\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{I}_{q}&-\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathbf{Q}&\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(3.14)

Finally, we note how to recover the worst-case load amplitudes
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}
from the set
\{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}:\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1\}
and compute the worst-case displacement amplitudes
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}
.

Lemma 3

(Worst-case load and dynamic compliance)
.

Let (
3.14
) hold and set
\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\big[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\big]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{S}^{q}_{\succeq 0}
. If
\mathbf{r}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a unit eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}
associated with its largest eigenvalue, then the worst-case load is
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{q}
, and the corresponding worst-case dynamic compliance is equal to
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proof.

By the Rayleigh-Ritz principle,
\sup_{\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\mathbf{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{e}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
, attained at any unit eigenvector
\mathbf{r}_{q}
of
\mathbf{A}
. The load amplitude is
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{q}
.
∎

Using the result of this lemma and (
3.5
), the worst-case amplitudes of displacements
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
evaluate as

\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}=\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{q}.

(3.15)

3.2
Robust peak input power constraints under single-frequency, uniform-phase harmonic loads

Next, we provide a semidefinite constraint for the (robust) peak input power that is bounded from above by
\overline{p}
. Since derivation of this constraint is analogous to dynamic compliance presented in the previous section, we provide less details and highlight the differences. The peak input power constraints have also been developed by
Heidari et al. 2009
. However, our derivation allows for true topology optimization problems with vanishing cross-sectional areas, i.e.,
\mathbf{a}\geq\mathbf{0}
instead of
\mathbf{a}>\mathbf{0}
, which is crucial when treating oscillating slender beam elements.

Peak input power is particularly important in design scenarios where energy dissipation capacity is limited, such as in lightweight aerospace structures, vibration-sensitive equipment, or structures with fatigue concerns. By constraining peak input power rather than just displacements, designers can directly control the energy transferred into the structure during dynamic loading events.

3.2.1
Single load case semidefinite programming formulation

Let us start with the single load case weight minimization problem under a peak input power constraint, which can be formalized as

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{n}\rho_{e}\ell_{e}a_{e}

(3.16a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;

\displaystyle\lvert\mathbf{f}(t)^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{v}(t)\rvert\leq\overline{p},\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}

(3.16b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{v}(t)+\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{v}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(t),

(3.16c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\geq\mathbf{0},

(3.16d)

where
\mathbf{f}(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
and
\mathbf{v}(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
are time
t
dependent harmonic loads and velocities, respectively. For the harmonic forces as defined in (
3.2
) and for
\{c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}:c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}=1\}
, the time derivative
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(t)
reads as

\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\omega e^{\omega ti}(ic_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}+c_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}})+\frac{1}{2}\omega e^{-\omega ti}(-ic_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}+c_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}).

(3.17)

Consequently,
\mathbf{v}(t)
solving (
3.16c
) takes the form

\mathbf{v}(t)=\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}(\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Re}}-i\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Im}})+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}(\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Re}}+i\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Im}}),

(3.18)

with
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Re}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Im}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
being the real and imaginary amplitude vectors, respectively. Substituting these expressions for
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(t)
and
\mathbf{v}(t)
into the state equation (
3.16c
) yields

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}\right)

\displaystyle=\omega c_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}\right),

(3.19a)

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Im}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}\right)i

\displaystyle=\omega c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{\omega ti}-\frac{1}{2}e^{-\omega ti}\right)i.

(3.19b)

Therefore, the velocity amplitudes
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}}
evaluate as

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}}=\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\omega\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{if}\quad\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathcal{R}\!\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right),

(3.20a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}}=\left\{\emptyset\right\}\quad

\displaystyle\text{otherwise},

(3.20b)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Re}}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}},

(3.21a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{Im}}

\displaystyle=-c_{1}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}}.

(3.21b)

The velocity amplitude in equation (
3.20
) is proportional to both the force amplitude and the excitation frequency
\omega
. Physically, this reflects that higher frequency vibrations produce larger velocities for the same displacement amplitude. The velocity field is phase-shifted relative to the displacement field by 90 degrees, which is characteristic of harmonic motion.

Using these velocity amplitudes, we can now formulate the peak input power, which represents the maximum rate of energy transfer between the external loads and the structure during a vibration cycle. Excluding the nonphysical scenario of
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\notin\mathcal{R}\!\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)
while accounting for (
3.16c
), the peak input power from (
3.16b
) evaluates as

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}\left\lvert\mathbf{f}(t)^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rvert

(3.22)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{4}\sup_{t}\left\lvert\left[e^{\omega ti}(c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}-ic_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}})+e^{-\omega ti}(c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}+ic_{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}})\right]^{\mathsf{H}}\right.\\
\left.\left[e^{\omega ti}(c_{2}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}}+ic_{1}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}})+e^{-\omega ti}(c_{2}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}}-ic_{1}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{A}})\right]\right\rvert\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\omega\sup_{t}\left\lvert\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\left[\left(c_{2}^{2}-c_{1}^{2}\right)\sin(2\omega t)+2c_{1}c_{2}\cos(2\omega t)\right]\right\rvert.

To provide a semidefinite programming reformulation of (
3.16
) analogous to
[
6
]
for
\mathbf{a}\geq\mathbf{0}
, we again need to enforce subresonant harmonic loads, i.e.,
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\succeq 0
. Then, after recognizing that
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\geq 0
and utilizing the phase-shift formula,

\sup_{t}\left\lvert(c_{2}^{2}-c_{1}^{2})\sin(2\omega t)+2c_{1}c_{2}\cos(2\omega t)\right\rvert=\sup_{t}\left\lvert\sin\left(2\omega t+\arctan\left(\frac{2c_{1}c_{2}}{c_{2}^{2}-c_{1}^{2}}\right)\right)\right\rvert=1,

(3.23)

the peak input power evaluates as

p=\frac{1}{2}\omega\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}.

(3.24)

Due to the absolute value in (
3.23
), this peak value occurs at the time
t=\frac{1}{2\omega}\arctan\frac{2c_{1}c_{2}}{c_{2}^{2}-c_{1}^{2}}+\frac{k\pi}{2\omega},k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Similarly to the dynamic compliance, we thus receive the following semidefinite programming formulation for the peak input power constraint.

Proposition 2

(Peak input power matrix inequality)
.

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

\frac{1}{2}\omega\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\leq\overline{p}
,
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathcal{R}\!\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)
,
\overline{p}>0
,
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\succeq 0
,

2.

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{2\overline{p}}{\omega}&-\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{A}}&\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

Proof.

The proof follows again from Lemma
2
.
∎

3.2.2
Robust settings

Let us now proceed with the robust setting of the peak input power constraint, with the loads within an ellipsoid
\{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}:\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1\}
. Hence, we have

\overline{p}_{\mathrm{R}}\geq p_{\mathrm{R}}:=\sup_{\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\sup_{t}\lvert\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}(t,\mathbf{e})^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{v}(t,\mathbf{e})\rvert

(3.25)

with
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}
defined in (
3.10
) (the ellipsoidal uncertainty representation from Section
3.1.2
). Using developments analogous to the previous section, we obtain

p_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{1}{2}\omega\sup_{\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\left(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}

(3.26)

with
\mathcal{R}\!\left(\mathbf{Q}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{R}\!\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)
. The robust peak input power constraint then requires

\forall\{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1\}:\qquad\overline{p}_{\mathrm{R}}-\frac{\omega}{2}\mathbf{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}\geq 0,

(3.27)

which is, after enforcing
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\succeq 0
and similar arguments to Section
3.1.2
, equivalent to a semidefinite constraint

\overline{p}_{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{I}_{q}-\frac{\omega}{2}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\succeq 0

(3.28)

and further to

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{2\overline{p}_{\mathrm{R}}}{\omega}\mathbf{I}_{q}&-\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathbf{Q}&\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.

(3.29)

The robust peak input power constraint in equation (
3.29
) ensures that for any loading scenario within the defined uncertainty set, the peak input power remains below the specified threshold
\overline{p}_{\mathrm{R}}
.

Finally, we can recover the worst-case load amplitudes
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}
from the set
\{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{e}:\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_{2}\leq 1\}
and compute the worst-case velocity amplitudes
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}
. The worst-case load is again evaluated as

\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{q},

(3.30)

which can be derived analogously to Lemma
3
, and the corresponding worst-case velocity amplitudes
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
based on (
3.20
) as

\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}=\omega\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{q}.

(3.31)

3.3
Relations between dynamic compliance, peak input power, free-vibration eigenvalue, and static compliance constraints

In what follows, we establish relationships between robust dynamic compliance, robust peak input power constraints, fundamental free-vibration eigenvalue constraints, and static compliance constraints. We demonstrate that dynamic compliance and peak input power constraints are equivalent for specific parameter choices, and that both can be expressed in the form of fundamental free-vibration eigenvalue constraints. Furthermore, we prove that the worst-case state fields (displacements and velocities) correspond precisely to the eigenmode associated with the lowest positive eigenvalue of the equivalent free-vibration eigenvalue problem. Finally, we also demonstrate that robust dynamic compliance constraints naturally reduce to robust static compliance constraints as the excitation frequency approaches zero, completing the relationship chain between dynamic, free-vibration, and static structural responses. For reader’s convenience, we illustrate the constraint relationships in Fig.
2
.

\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\left[\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]\succeq 0

3.3.1
Relation between (robust) dynamic compliance and (robust) peak input power constraints

We start by relating the robust dynamic compliance constraints (
3.14
) with the robust peak input power constraints (
3.29
). Clearly, both these constraints maintain the same structure and are equivalent for
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{2\overline{p}_{\mathrm{R}}}{\omega}
if
\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. As a direct consequence, we get the following simple result that also relates the state fields:

Lemma 4

(Displacement-velocity relation)
.

Fix
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}
and
\omega>0
, and set
\mathbf{S}:=\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
. For any
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{dof}}}
define the minimal-norm amplitudes
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}:=\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}:=\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}(\omega\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}})
. Then
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}=\omega\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}
and, with
d_{\mathrm{R}}:=\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}
and
p_{\mathrm{R}}:=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}
, one has
d_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{2p_{\mathrm{R}}}{\omega}
.

Proof.

By linearity of the map
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}=\omega\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}=\omega\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}
. Hence
p_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\omega\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}})=\frac{\omega}{2}d_{\mathrm{R}}
.
∎

Despite these equivalences at the robust-constraint level, dynamic compliance and input power are distinct time-domain functionals—cf. (
3.7
) vs. (
3.22
)—so their instantaneous histories and peaks occur at different phases.

3.3.2
Relation between (robust) dynamic compliance, (robust) peak input power constraints, and fundamental free-vibration eigenvalue constraints

In the previous section, we have shown that the robust dynamic compliance and robust peak input power constraints are equivalent for
\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and a specific parameter choice. To relate these constraints to the fundamental free-vibration eigenvalue constraints (
2.6b
) under
\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we only need to consider one of them. For this, we choose the robust dynamic compliance constraint (
3.14
) and show that it can be written in the form of (
2.6b
), and vice versa.

Proposition 3
.

Constraints (
3.14
) and (
2.6b
) are equivalent up to a parameter reparametrization.

Proof.

\Rightarrow
Let (
3.14
) hold for
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Then, after using Schur complement (Lemma
1
) at the top-left block
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{I}_{q}\succ 0
yields

\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\left[\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{0}+\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}a_{e}\mathbf{M}_{e}^{(1)}\right]\succeq 0

(3.32)

with
\underline{\lambda}=\omega^{2}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{0}=\mathbf{M}_{0}+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}
. This is exactly the form of (
2.6b
).

\Leftarrow
Let (
2.6b
) hold with
\underline{\lambda}>0
and factor
\mathbf{M}_{0}=\hat{\mathbf{Q}}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}
. Then
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}a_{e}\mathbf{M}^{(1)}_{e}-\underline{\lambda}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0
,
which is equivalent (reverse Schur complement w.r.t.
(1/\underline{\lambda})\mathbf{I}_{q}
) to

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\underline{\lambda}}\mathbf{I}_{q}&-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\hat{\mathbf{Q}}&\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}a_{e}\mathbf{M}^{(1)}_{e}\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,

(3.33)

i.e., (
3.14
) with
\omega=\sqrt{\underline{\lambda}}
,
\mathbf{Q}=\hat{\mathbf{Q}}
, and
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{R}}=1/\underline{\lambda}
.
∎

Having related the robust dynamic compliance and fundamental free-vibration eigenvalue constraints, we further prove that the displacement field of the robust dynamic compliance problem related to the worst-case response is also the eigenvector associated with the lowest nonzero eigenvalue of the free-vibration eigenvalue problem.

Lemma 5

(Displacement-eigenmode relation)
.

Fix
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}
and
\omega>0
, and set
\underline{\lambda}:=\omega^{2}
and

\mathbf{S}:=\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})
.
Assume the robust dynamic-compliance inequality (
3.14
) holds with bound
d_{\mathrm{R}}>0
,
and let the worst-case load be
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{q}
and

\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}:=\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}
.
Then
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}\neq\mathbf{0}
satisfies

\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}=\underline{\lambda}\Big(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})+\frac{1}{\underline{\lambda}d_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\Big)\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}},

i.e.,
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}
is an eigenvector and it is associated with the smallest positive eigenvalue
\mu=\underline{\lambda}
.

Proof.

Consider the eigendecomposition
\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{T}}
, so that

\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}

(3.34)

with the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues
\mathbf{\Lambda}
. Clearly, the vectors
\mathbf{f}_{1}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathbf{f}_{q}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{q}
(which we further collect in a matrix
\mathbf{F}
) are
\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}
-orthogonal, where
\mathbf{r}_{q}
is the orthonormal eigenvector related to the maximal eigenvalue in
\mathbf{\Lambda}
. In addition, we also have
\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}
due to the orthonormality of the columns of
\mathbf{R}
.

To prove the statement, we first show that
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}
is an eigenvector, i.e.,

\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a}){\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}}-\mu\left[\frac{1}{\underline{\lambda}{d}_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]{\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}}=\mathbf{0}

(3.35)

for an eigenvalue
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
To this goal, we rewrite the left hand side as

\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\mu\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right){\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{R}}-\frac{\mu}{\underline{\lambda}d_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}

(3.36)

and as

\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\mu\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}-\frac{\mu}{\underline{\lambda}d_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}.

(3.37)

Recalling that
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathbf{f}_{q}
, that
\mathbf{f}_{1}\dots\mathbf{f}_{q}
are
\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}
-orthogonal and
d_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\omega^{2}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}
, we obtain

\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\mu\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)\left(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}-\frac{\mu}{\underline{\lambda}}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}

(3.38)

which evaluates as
\mathbf{0}
if
\mu=\underline{\lambda}
. This shows that
\underline{\lambda}
is an eigenvalue associated with the eigenvector
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}
.

Second, we need to show that
\mu=\underline{\lambda}
is particularly the smallest eigenvalue. For this, we observe that the smallest positive eigenvalue is the best lower-bound
\mu_{\min}
to all eigenvalues
[
3
]
, i.e.,

\mu_{\min}=\max\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\mu\left[\frac{1}{\underline{\lambda}d_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]\succeq 0\right\}.

(3.39)

Notice that the constraint in (
3.39
) is a linear matrix inequality because it is considered for a fixed
\mathbf{a}
. Using Schur complement lemma and recalling that
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\mathsf{T}}
, the matrix inequality is equivalent to

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\underline{\lambda}d_{\mathrm{R}}}{\mu}\mathbf{I}_{q}&\mathbf{F}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{F}&\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\mu\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{\underline{\lambda}d_{\mathrm{R}}}{\mu}\mathbf{I}_{q}-\mathbf{F}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\mu\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\succeq 0.

(3.40)

Now test
\mu=\underline{\lambda}
, which gives

d_{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{I}_{q}-\mathbf{F}^{\mathsf{T}}\left[\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})\right]^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\succeq 0;

(3.41)

this is positive semidefinite due to equivalence to (
3.14
). Therefore,
\underline{\lambda}
is feasible for (
3.39
) so that
\mu_{\min}\geq\underline{\lambda}
. Since we have already shown that
\underline{\lambda}
is an eigenvalue,
\mu_{\min}\leq\underline{\lambda}
. Combining both results, we conclude that
\mu_{\min}=\underline{\lambda}
, and
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}
is its eigenvector.
∎

An analogous result holds for the peak input power constraints as well.

Lemma 6

(Velocity-eigenmode relation)
.

Fix
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}
and
\omega>0
, and set
\underline{\lambda}:=\omega^{2}
and

\mathbf{S}:=\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\underline{\lambda}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})
.
Assume the robust peak-input-power inequality (
3.29
) holds with bound
p_{\mathrm{R}}>0
,
and let the worst-case load be
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{q}
and

\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}:=\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}(\omega\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{R}})
.
Then
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}\neq\mathbf{0}
satisfies

\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}=\underline{\lambda}\Big(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{a})+\frac{1}{2\omega p_{\mathrm{R}}}\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\Big)\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}},

i.e.,
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}
is an eigenvector and it is associated with the smallest positive eigenvalue
\mu=\underline{\lambda}
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the fact that
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{1}{\omega}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{R}}
due to Lemma
4
and that
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{R}}
is the eigenvector as shown in Lemma
5
.
∎

3.3.3
Relation between (robust) dynamic compliance and (robust) static compliance constraints

Finally, we also relate the robust dynamic compliance constraint (
3.14
) to the static one
[
19
]
. Specifically, since all blocks depend continuously on
\omega
, the limit
\omega\rightarrow 0
of the block-PSD condition exists and yields the robust static compliance constraint of
Ben-Tal and Nemirovski 1997

\begin{pmatrix}\overline{c}_{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{I}_{q}&-\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathbf{Q}&\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0

(3.42)

which enforces the worst-case static compliance to be bounded from above by
\overline{c}_{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. After using Lemma
1
, (
3.42
) can be written in the form

\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{a})-\mathbf{M}_{0}\succeq 0

(3.43)

with
\mathbf{M}_{0}=\frac{1}{\overline{c}_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}
. As already observed by
Kanno 2015
, this is a particular instance of the free-vibration eigenvalue constraint (
2.6b
). This reveals that the results summarized in Section
2.1
also apply to the static compliance case. However, we note here that a slightly more general result for the robust static case, allowing to include design-independent stiffness, follows from an analysis of the static compliance function
[
19
]
.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we studied minimum-weight design of undamped Euler–Bernoulli frame structures under single-frequency subresonant harmonic excitation, focusing on dynamic compliance and peak input power in both nominal and ellipsoidal-robust settings. Our main theoretical result is an exact semidefinite-programming reformulation of these response constraints as a free-vibration generalized eigenvalue condition with design-independent mass augmentation. This equivalence unifies static, dynamic, and modal constraints in a single framework and clarifies that the worst-case amplitudes align with the fundamental mode of the augmented eigenproblem. Although the resulting design problem is nonconvex as the structural stiffness depends polynomially on cross-sectional areas, the reformulation enables certified lower bounds via the moment-sum-of-squares hierarchy and construction of feasible upper bounds, yielding explicit optimality guarantees.

Numerical studies on three examples support the theory. For the
10
-segment frame of
Ni et al. 2014
, we obtain designs with certified global optimality and numerically confirm equivalences among dynamic-compliance, peak-input-power, and free-vibration-eigenvalue formulations. For the larger
35
-segment tower of
Ni et al. 2014
, we obtained high-quality local solutions together with rigorous bounds; the resulting designs are substantially lighter than the previously reported ones. In an additional case study we obtained a high-quality local solution, manufactured the structure, and experimentally validated the predicted frequency response, observing close agreement with the model.

The proposed pipeline offers three benefits: (i) unified modeling handle through free-vibration eigenvalue constraints which covers static, dynamic-compliance, and peak-input-power constraints; (ii) robustness to load uncertainty is directly embedded in the semidefinite programming machinery; and (iii) certification—often missing in nonconvex topology optimization—is available through convergent relaxations.

The present theory targeted single-frequency, uniform-phase loads below the first resonance without damping; scalability of generic SDP/mSOS relaxations may become costly for large ground-structures. Extensions to deal with these aspects will be considered in future work.
