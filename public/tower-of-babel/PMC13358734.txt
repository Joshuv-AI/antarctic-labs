Keeping both lungs open during open-lung surgery: an impossible challenge?
Authors: Arnaud Gacouin, Jean-Marc Tadié
Published: 2026

Thoracic surgery with one-lung ventilation (OLV): a necessary practice associated with postoperative complications

More than 500,000 thoracic procedures are performed annually, exposing patients to a substantial risk of postoperative complications, particularly pulmonary complications that directly affect outcomes (1). These may result from the surgical procedure itself (pneumothorax, prolonged air leak, hemorrhage …) and are often exacerbated by perioperative mechanical ventilation. In addition, atelectasis is promoted both by surgery and by the need for OLV to facilitate surgical exposure (2).

Thoracic surgery is also associated with a high incidence of postoperative infections, contributing to prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stays, increased antimicrobial use, and higher healthcare costs. Nosocomial infection rates exceeding 10% have been reported after major surgery and are associated with increased mortality (3). This high burden has been linked to postoperative immune dysfunction induced by surgical trauma (4).

Postoperative immune dysfunction and lung impairment: a harmful interplay

Patients undergoing thoracic surgery may develop pulmonary dysfunction ranging from mild hypoxemia to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) (1,2,5). Although multifactorial, intraoperative atelectasis remains a key contributor and has been associated with lung injury and cytokine release. Whether this inflammatory response is primarily driven by cyclic recruitment-derecruitment (atelectrauma) or by atelectasis itself remains uncertain (6,7). Nonetheless, postoperative inflammation and atelectasis likely act synergistically to worsen immune dysfunction.

Re-expansion and ventilation of previously atelectatic lung regions, a frequent intraoperative event, may further aggravate inflammation and contribute to postoperative immunosuppression (7). Preventing atelectasis could therefore improve outcomes. Notably, approximately half of patients undergoing thoracic surgery with OLV develop postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), which are associated with increased morbidity and mortality (5).

Two main strategies have been proposed to limit lung injury during OLV: applying higher pressures to maintain lung recruitment or, conversely, limiting pressures while accepting some degree of atelectasis. To date, no clear evidence supports the superiority of either approach.

The PROTHOR trial

The PROTHOR trial, a multicentre, international, randomized controlled phase 3 study recently published in The Lancet Respiratory Medicine, was designed to compare these two ventilation strategies (8). Patients were eligible if they were scheduled for open thoracic or video-assisted surgery, had a body mass index <35 kg/m2, an expected operative duration >60 minutes, and a planned OLV period exceeding half of the two-lung ventilation time. Patients in the high-positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) group received OLV with a PEEP of 10 cmH2O combined with periodic lung recruitment maneuvers, whereas those in the low-PEEP group received a PEEP of 5 cmH2O without routine recruitment maneuvers. All patients received lung-protective ventilation in accordance with clinical guidelines (volume-controlled mode with tidal volumes of 5 mL/kg predicted body weight during OLV and 7 mL/kg during two-lung ventilation).

The primary outcome was a composite of PPCs occurring within the first 5 postoperative days. These included aspiration pneumonitis, moderate or severe respiratory failure, ARDS, pulmonary infection, atelectasis, cardiopulmonary edema, pleural effusion, pneumothorax on the non-surgical side, pulmonary infiltrates, prolonged air leak, purulent pleuritis, pulmonary embolism, and lung hemorrhage. Patients with severe underlying chronic respiratory conditions [e.g., advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pulmonary fibrosis, bullae, severe emphysema, or pneumothorax] were excluded, although the exact number of excluded patients was not specified.

The main finding was that high PEEP with recruitment maneuvers did not reduce PPCs compared with low PEEP without recruitment maneuvers. PPCs occurred in 53.6% of patients in the high-PEEP group and 56.4% in the low-PEEP group [absolute risk difference −2.68 percentage points; 95% confidence interval (CI): −6.36 to 1.01; P=0.155]. Rescue maneuvers for hypoxemia were more frequent in the low-PEEP group (8.8% vs. 3.3%), whereas hypotension and new arrhythmias were more common in the high-PEEP group. Extrapulmonary complications and overall adverse events were similar between groups.

This is the largest study to date evaluating intraoperative lung expansion strategies in patients undergoing thoracic surgery with OLV. In contrast, a previous study published in the same journal reported fewer PPCs in patients receiving recruitment maneuvers during open-lung surgery compared with conventional lung-protective ventilation (9). However, in that study, patients in the high-PEEP group also received postoperative high-flow nasal oxygen, whereas the low-PEEP group received standard oxygen therapy, an important difference, given the known benefits of high-flow oxygen in cardiothoracic settings (10).

Ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) is not limited to patients with ARDS. Patients undergoing thoracic surgery are also exposed to mechanisms such as alveolar overdistension, atelectrauma and biotrauma (11,12). In the PROTHOR trial, the strategy in the high-PEEP group prioritized prevention of atelectrauma over the risk of overdistension, as atelectasis has been implicated as a key contributor to PPCs.

Despite strong physiological rationale, the PROTHOR trial did not demonstrate a clinical benefit of higher PEEP during OLV. The relative contributions of atelectasis and overdistension during OLV remain unclear. Moreover, the study population was heterogeneous, including patients undergoing different surgical procedures (lung resection or esophageal surgery), with varying extents of lung involvement. Surgical approaches also differed: 57% thoracoscopic, 35% open, and 7% combined. The extent of lung resection ranged from segmentectomy to pneumonectomy. Consequently, lung stress likely varied considerably between patients, as well as between the operated and contralateral lungs, suggesting that a uniform ventilation strategy may not be optimal.

The distribution of intrathoracic pressures under high versus low PEEP during OLV also remains poorly understood. Measurement of transpulmonary pressure using esophageal manometry is technically challenging in this context. Noninvasive monitoring with electrical impedance tomography (EIT) may provide insight into regional ventilation but is not widely applicable due to practical constraints, such as the need for thoracic belt placement (13).

Comparisons between thoracic surgery with OLV and cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass are difficult. During cardiopulmonary bypass, patients are typically not ventilated and receive no PEEP. Postoperative studies comparing high versus low PEEP in this setting have yielded conflicting results (14,15). While improvements in oxygenation and lung compliance have been observed, consistent clinical benefits remain unproven. Similarly, a recent randomized trial found that maintaining low tidal volume ventilation with PEEP during bypass did not reduce postoperative infections or alter immune markers compared with no ventilation (16).

Even with a PEEP of 10 cmH2O, it is uncertain whether alveoli remain recruited throughout surgery. Events such as bronchoscopy, temporary cessation of ventilation, or transitions between one- and two-lung ventilation may promote derecruitment that is not fully reversible. Repeated cycles of recruitment and derecruitment may themselves induce biotrauma, potentially offsetting any protective effect of higher PEEP (17).

Most PPCs were diagnosed based on combined clinical and radiological criteria. While some complications, such as prolonged air leaks, are straightforward to identify, others are more difficult to diagnose in the postoperative thoracic setting. For example, detecting new pulmonary infiltrates can be challenging in the presence of chest tubes or extensive dressings. Additionally, only clinically significant atelectasis was recorded, and screening for pulmonary embolism was not systematic.

Postoperative management was not standardized, and although unlikely, differences between groups in this phase cannot be entirely excluded.

As in ARDS, PEEP titration during thoracic surgery involves balancing improved oxygenation against potential hemodynamic compromise. Although hypotension was more frequent in the high-PEEP group, the incidence of acute renal failure remained low and similar between groups (1.8% vs. 2.0%).

In conclusion, the PROTHOR trial shows that routine use of high PEEP during open-lung surgery does not reduce PPCs compared with lower PEEP. These findings support an individualized approach, with ventilation strategies tailored to each patient’s gas exchange and hemodynamic status rather than applying a uniform protocol.

What next?

Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in thoracic surgery focusing on ventilator management have largely yielded negative results, careful optimization of perioperative ventilation remains warranted. Several directions for future research can be proposed.

First, mechanical ventilation settings likely have a direct impact on pulmonary function and may consequently influence the incidence of PPCs. Beyond the lungs, mechanical ventilation can modulate the immune response through mechanisms collectively referred to as biotrauma, and ventilation of an injured lung may contribute to dysfunction in distant organs (18,19). Future trials should therefore consider broader and more clinically meaningful endpoints, extending beyond pulmonary outcomes alone. In addition, outcome assessment should not be limited to the first postoperative week, as complications, particularly infections, may occur within the first month after surgery.

Second, future studies should aim to better integrate physiological parameters, such as atelectasis and ventilation settings, with immune biomarkers and robust clinical endpoints, rather than relying primarily on composite outcomes, which remain open to debate (20).

Finally, postoperative infections remain challenging to diagnose due to the lack of universally applicable definitions in clinical practice (21,22). In this context, antibiotic consumption may represent a pragmatic and objective surrogate marker (23).

Overall, the impact of ventilatory strategies in major surgery remains incompletely understood. While physiological data and limited clinical evidence suggest potential benefits, further well-designed studies are needed to determine whether optimized ventilation strategies can meaningfully reduce the still high incidence of postoperative complications.
