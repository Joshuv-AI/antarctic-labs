Modular Schur numbers
Tom Sanders

Abstract
. We study modular analogues of Schur numbers for systems of linear equations. We show that these only depend on the number of equations, not their coefficients and in the case of one equation show stronger bounds.



1.
Introduction

The Schur number,
f(r)
, is the largest natural number
N
such that there is an
r
-colouring of
[N]:=\{1,\dots,N\}
without a monochromatic solution to
x+y=z
. This was shown to exist by Schur in
[
Sch16
]
. There is a modular analogue
h(r)
which is the same but instead of asking for no monochromatic solutions to
x+y=z
it asks for none to
x+y\equiv z\pmod{N+1}
. We look modulo
N+1
here so that
[N]
is a set of non-zero residues.

It is immediate that
h(r)\leq f(r)
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and as a result of work of Abbott and Wang
[
AW77
]
and Heule
[
Heu18
]
we know there is equality for
r\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
; in
[
AW77
, Problem I, p12]
Abbott and Wang conjectured that there is equality for all
r
.

Schur’s result is generalised by Rado’s theorem. To state Rado’s theorem recall that an
n\times m
matrix
A
satisfies the columns condition over a field
\mathbb{F}
if there is a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{d}\}
of the columns of
A
such that

\sum_{c\in P_{j}}{c}\in\mathop{\rm Span}\nolimits_{\mathbb{F}}(\{c:c\in P_{1}\cup\dots\cup P_{j-1}\})\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq d

with the convention that this span is the zero space for
j=1
.

Theorem 1.1

(Rado’s theorem,
[
Rad33
, Satz IV, p445]
)
.

Suppose that
A
is an
n\times m
matrix of integers that satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{Q}
and
[N]
is
r
-coloured such that there is no monochromatic solution to
Ax=0
. Then
N=O_{A,r}(1)
.

Write
\mathfrak{R}_{A}(r)
for the largest
N
such that there is an
r
-colouring of
[N]
with no monochromatic solution to
Ax=0
. In this notation Schur’s function
f(r)=\mathfrak{R}_{A}(r)
where
A=(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&-1\end{array})
.

The matrix
A=(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&1&-1\end{array})
satisfies the columns condition, but to have
ax_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}=0
with
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in[N]
we must have
N\geq a+1
, and so certainly
f_{a}(r):=\mathfrak{R}_{A}(r)\geq a
. In particular, this means that there is no upper bound on
\mathfrak{R}_{A}(r)
that depends only on (
r
and) the number of rows of
A
. On the other hand there is a modular setting where there is such a bound:

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
p
is prime,
A
is an
n\times m
matrix that satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, and
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{*}
is
r
-coloured such that there is no monochromatic solution to
Ax=0
. Then
p=O_{n,r}(1)
.

We record a proof of this in §
2
.

The columns condition over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is different to that over
\mathbb{Q}
: if, for example,
A=(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&p&-p\end{array})
for a prime
p
then
A
satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{Q}
, but not over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. In this case the
1
-colouring of
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{*}
will have no monochromatic solutions to
Ax=0
for the trivial reason that
0
is not coloured, but
p
could be arbitrarily large, so we genuinely need the columns condition over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
not over
\mathbb{Q}
in Theorem
1.2
.

There is no upper bound on
p
that is independent of
n
. This can be seen by considering a
(k+1)
-point Brauer configuration, which is a
(k+1)
-tuple
x=(x_{1},x_{1}+x_{k+1},\dots,x_{1}+(k-1)x_{k+1},x_{k+1})
or, equivalently
x
with
Ax=0
where

A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0&0&\cdots&0&-1\\
-1&0&1&0&\cdots&0&-2\\
-1&0&0&1&\cdots&0&-3\\
\vdots&&&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
-1&0&0&0&\cdots&1&-(k-1)\end{pmatrix}.

Here
A
satisfies the columns condition (over
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
) and if
k=p
then
A
has
p-1
rows, and for
p
prime any solution to
Ax=0
has
x_{i}=0
for some
i
by the pigeonhole principle. Since no colouring of
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{*}
colours
0
,
x
is not monochromatic. It follows that
p\geq n+1
in Theorem
1.2
.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
uses the Hales-Jewett theorem and so the bounds are poor, and it seems quite possible that
p=\exp(r^{O_{n}(1)})
is true. For
n=1
we are able to show this:

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
p
is prime,
A
is a
1\times m
matrix that satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, and
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{*}
is
r
-coloured such that there is no monochromatic solution to
Ax=0
. Then
p=\exp((2r)^{O(1)})
.

Our proof uses Fourier analysis very much in the style of the proofs of
[
Shk10
, Theorem 24]
, or the arguments of
[
CS17
]
, or
[
CP20
, §2]
. Part of this can be described as using an inverse theorem for the Gowers
U^{2}
-norm relative to linear level sets (aka Bohr sets), and this suggests the generalisation to higher order Fourier analysis. In this direction Prendiville in
[
Pre24
]
established an inverse theorem for the Gowers
U^{3}
-norm relative to quadratic level sets which he used to prove an analogous bound for
4
-point Brauer configurations in a toy setting. This is some evidence towards the
n=2
case of Theorem
1.2
being true with the bounds of Theorem
1.3
.

To bring us back to the start of the introduction, the same result that will give us Theorem
1.3
will also give the following:

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
a,N\in\mathbb{N}
are coprime, and
(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{*}
, meaning the non-zero elements of
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
, is
r
-coloured such that there is no monochromatic solution to
ax_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}=0
. Then
N\leq\exp((2r)^{\hypertarget{const:2}{\mbox{}}C_{1}})
.

The function
f_{a}(r)
defined just before Theorem
1.2
has
f_{1}(r)=f(r)
and we can also define the modular version
h_{a}(r)
with
h_{1}(r)=h(r)
, by letting
h_{a}(r)
be the largest
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that there is an
r
-colouring of
[N]
with no monochromatic solutions to
ax+y\equiv z\pmod{N+1}
.

A natural extension of Abbott and Wang’s conjecture would be
f_{a}(r)=h_{a}(r)
for all
r
, but in fact
f_{a}(2)\neq h_{a}(2)
for all
a>1
. This can be shown by a direct combinatorial argument
1
1

1

Writing
N:=a^{2}+3a
, if
(x,y,z)
equals
(1,1,a+1)
,
(a+1,a+1,(a+1)^{2})
,
(a+2,1,(a+1)^{2})
,
(1,N-a+2,1)
or
(a+1,a+2,N-a+2)
, then
ax+y\equiv z\pmod{N+1}
. Given a
2
-colouring of
\{1,\dots,N\}
with no monochromatic solutions to
ax+y\equiv z\pmod{N+1}
, the first two triples ensure that
1
and
(a+1)^{2}
are red, say, and
a+1
is blue. The third triple then ensures that
a+2
is blue. The fourth triple then means that
N-a+2
is blue, and the fifth that it is red, a contradiction.
, but for
a
large enough (meaning
a^{2}+3a+1>\exp(4^{\hyperlink{const:2}{C_{\ref{const:2}}}})
) we can also use Theorem
1.4
: We know from
[
LR14
, Theorem 9.11, p239]
that
f_{a}(2)=a^{2}+3a
, and so if
h_{a}(2)=f_{a}(2)
we have
(h_{a}(2)+1,a)=1
and we can apply Theorem
1.4
with
r=2
and
N=h_{a}(2)+1
to get a contradiction.

Notwithstanding the fact it is false, there are good reasons not to conjecture the natural extension above. Abbott and Wang offered evidence
[
AW77
, Problem I, p12]
for their conjecture including the observation that the colourings which are used to establish the values for
f(1)
,
f(2)
,
f(3)
, and
f(4)
are also free of monochromatic modular solutions. This is not true for the corresponding colourings in
[
LR14
, Theorem 9.11, p239]
establishing the values of
f_{a}(2)
for
a>1
.

Notation

We use big-
O
and big-
\Omega
notation throughout with the latter in the sense of Knuth
[
Knu76
]
. We shall also use
C_{1},C_{2},\dots>1
and
c_{1},c_{2},\dots\in(0,1]
as absolute constants which will be the same throughout the paper. This will help us make it clear that some choices are not circular.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section we shall prove Theorem
1.2
, which is a special case of the next theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{F}
is a finite field,
\mathbb{F}^{*}
is
r
-coloured,
A
is an
n\times m
matrix satisfying the columns condition over
\mathbb{F}
, and there is no
x\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
with
Ax=0
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
all the same colour. Then
|\mathbb{F}|=O_{n,r}(1)
.

We shall use a slight variant of Deuber’s
(m,p,c)
-sets
[
Deu73
, (5), p111]
: For
F\subset\mathbb{F}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
t\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
we write

S(m,F;t):=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}{S(m,F;t,j)}\text{ where }S(m,F;t,j):=t_{j}+F.t_{j+1}+\cdots+F.t_{m}.

This is essentially an
(m-1,p,1)
-set generated by
t
with the intervals
\{-p,\dots,p\}
replaced by a more general set
F
. We shall not be concerned with the ‘
c
’ in
(m,p,c)
-sets because we are working over a field which will make all non-zero
c
s equivalent.

First, as in Deuber’s approach to Rado’s theorem, we connect the columns condition over
\mathbb{F}
to these sets
S(m,F;t)
in the following result, proved in §
2.4
:

Proposition 2.2

(Proposition
2.5
)
.

Suppose that
A
is a
k\times l
matrix that satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{F}
. Then there is
d\leq k
and
F\subset\mathbb{F}
of size at most
(k+1)d^{2}
, such that for every
t\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
there is
x\in S(d,F;t)^{l}
such that
Ax=0
.

Secondly, we need a version of Deuber’s theorem for sets of the form
S(m,F;t)
. To state this we need some terminology which will let us pull back a colouring of
\mathbb{F}
to a high-dimensional product. We will then be able to apply the Hales-Jewett theorem to this high-dimensional product to get the result.

Given
F\subset\mathbb{F}
and
I
a finite set we say that
t\in\mathbb{F}^{I}
is
F
-independent
if

\sum_{i\in I}{f_{i}.t_{i}}=0_{\mathbb{F}}\text{ for some }f=(f_{i})_{i\in I}\in F^{I}\text{ implies }f_{i}=0\text{ for all }i\in I.

For
J\subset I
we also write
t_{J}:=\sum_{j\in J}{t_{j}}
, and so in particular if
1\in F
and
t
is
F
-independent then
t_{J}=0
implies
J=\emptyset
.

Finally, for
F\subset\mathbb{F}
we write
F^{0}:=\{1\}
and
F^{i+1}=F.F^{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We can now state the result we use, which we prove in §
2.6
.

Proposition 2.3

(Proposition
2.9
)
.

For
m,r,s\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
there is
\Psi(m,r,s)\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that if
F\subset\mathbb{F}
with
0,1\in F
has size at most
s
,
\mathbb{F}^{*}
is
r
-coloured,
t\in\mathbb{F}^{I}
is
F^{mr}
-independent, and
|I|\geq\Psi(m,r,s)
, then there is
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
such that
S(m,F;t^{\prime})
is monochromatic.

With these we can prove the main result of the section.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

By Proposition
2.5
there is
d\leq n
and a set
F^{\prime}
of size at most
(n+1)d^{2}
, such that for every
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
there is
x\in S(d,F^{\prime};t^{\prime})^{m}
with
Ax=0
. Let
F:=F^{\prime}\cup\{0,1\}
. Let
I
be a set of size
M:=\Psi(d,r,(n+1)d^{2}+2)
.

Pick
t_{1},\dots,t_{M}
independently and uniformly at random from
\mathbb{F}
. Let
\mathcal{F}^{*}
be the set of
f\in(F^{dr})^{I}
with not all
f_{i}
s equal to
0_{\mathbb{F}}
, and note that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}{\#\left\{f\in\mathcal{F}^{*}:\sum_{i\in I}{f_{i}.t_{i}}=0_{\mathbb{F}}\right\}}=\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{|\mathbb{F}|}.

If
|\mathbb{F}|>|\mathcal{F}^{*}|
then there is a choice of
t\in\mathbb{F}^{I}
that is
F^{dr}
-independent and we can apply Proposition
2.9
to get
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
such that
S(d,F;t^{\prime})
is monochromatic. However,
S(d,F^{\prime};t^{\prime})\subset S(d,F;t^{\prime})
and hence there is
x\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
monochromatic such that
Ax=0
. This contradiction means that

|\mathbb{F}|\leq|\mathcal{F}^{*}|=|(F^{dr})^{I}|-1\leq(((n+1)d^{2}+2)^{dr})^{\Psi(d,r,(n+1)d^{2}+2)}-1=O_{n,r}(1),

giving the result.
∎

2.4.
Using the columns condition

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose that
A
is a
k\times l
matrix that satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{F}
. Then there is
d\leq k
and
F\subset\mathbb{F}
of size at most
(k+1)d^{2}
, such that for every
t\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
there is
x\in S(d,F;t)^{l}
such that
Ax=0
.

Proof.

Since
A
satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{F}
there is a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{d}\}
of the columns of
A
such that if we write

V_{j}:=\mathop{\rm Span}\nolimits_{\mathbb{F}}(\{c:c\in P_{1}\cup\dots\cup P_{j}\})\text{ for all }0\leq j\leq d.

then

\sum_{c\in P_{j}}{c}\in V_{j-1}\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq d.

Let
J:=\{1\leq j\leq d:\dim V_{j}=\dim V_{j-1}\}
and let
i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{r}
be the elements of
[d]\setminus J
in order. Since

0<\dim V_{i_{1}}<\dim V_{i_{2}}<\dots<\dim V_{i_{r}}

and these are subspaces of
k
-dimensional space we have
r\leq k
. Write
i_{0}:=0
and

P_{j}^{\prime}:=P_{i_{j}}\cup P_{i_{j}-1}\cup\dots\cup P_{i_{j-1}+1}\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq r.

Then
A
satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{F}
w.r.t. the partition
\{P_{1}^{\prime},\dots,P_{r}^{\prime}\}
. It follows that we may assume that
d\leq k
.

Let
n_{j}:=\dim\mathop{\rm Span}\nolimits_{\mathbb{F}}(P_{j})
and let
c_{1},\dots,c_{n_{j}}\in P_{j}
be a basis; write
R_{j}:=P_{j}\setminus\{c_{1},\dots,c_{n_{j}}\}
. Since the vectors in
P_{j}
are columns in a matrix with
k
rows we have that
n_{j}\leq k
. Now replace
A
by the matrix
A^{\prime}
with the columns
c_{1},\dots,c_{n_{j}}
in each
P_{j}
left the same, and each family
R_{j}
replaced by the single column
\sum_{c\in R_{j}}{c}
(which is the zero column if there are no remaining columns, so in this case we add an extra column). In particular the resulting
A^{\prime}
has
q:=(n_{1}+1)+\cdots+(n_{d}+1)
columns.

Any
x\in\mathbb{F}^{q}
with
A^{\prime}x=0
has some
\widetilde{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{l}
with
A\widetilde{x}=0
and the entries of
\widetilde{x}
are just the entries of
x
repeated some number of times. In particular, if we can show the conclusion for
A^{\prime}
then we have it for
A
.

This new matrix
A^{\prime}
satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{F}
and in matrix form this tells us that
A^{\prime}W=0
for

W=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{n_{1}+1}&\alpha^{(2)}_{1}&\alpha^{(3)}_{1}&\cdots&\alpha^{(d)}_{1}\\
0&\mathbf{1}_{n_{2}+1}&\alpha^{(3)}_{2}&\cdots&\alpha^{(d)}_{2}\\
0&0&\mathbf{1}_{n_{3}+1}&\cdots&\alpha^{(d)}_{3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&\mathbf{1}_{n_{d}+1}\end{pmatrix},

Here
\mathbf{1}_{n_{i}+1}
is the
(n_{i}+1)\times 1
matrix (column vector) all of whose entries are
1
, and
\alpha_{i}^{(j)}
is an
(n_{i}+1)\times 1
matrix (column vector). Now let

F:=\{\text{ entries in the column vector }\alpha_{i}^{(j)}:1\leq i<j\leq d\},

and write
w^{(1)},\dots,w^{(d)}
for the columns of the matrix
W
.

Suppose that
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Then

x:=t_{1}w^{(1)}+t_{2}w^{(2)}+\cdots+t_{d}w^{(d)}\in\mathop{\rm Span}\nolimits(w^{(1)},\dots,w^{(d)})

and so
A^{\prime}x=0
. For
1\leq i\leq q
there is a unique
1\leq j\leq d
such that

(n_{1}+1)+\cdots+(n_{j-1}+1)<i\leq(n_{1}+1)+\cdots+(n_{j}+1),

and by construction of
x
we then have

x_{i}\in S(d,F;t,j).

It follows that
x_{i}\in S(d,F;t)
for all
1\leq i\leq q
as claimed. Finally it remains to note that
|F|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}{(d-i)(n_{i}+1)}\leq(k+1)d^{2}
.
∎

Throughout this section we write

Proof.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose that

is an automorphism and

for the colour classes, and for

In particular,

, and
\delta_{1}:=1
. For each step

of the iteration, and

From the monotonicity of the

(3.7)

At step

It follows that

It remains to show that at each stage of the iteration we are either in case (

[

The advantage of toy versions of results like this is that they are simpler because they do not need Bohr sets (except in the sense that every subgroup is a Bohr set), and do not need any of localisation arguments. There is then a general method with its origins in

]

Theorem 3.6

(Theorem
1.3
)
.

Suppose that
p
is prime,
A
is a
1\times m
matrix that satisfies the columns condition over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, and
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{*}
is
r
-coloured such that there is no monochromatic solution to

. Then
p=\exp((2r)^{O(1)})
.

Proof.

Write
A=(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{m}\end{array})
with the entries (columns) ordered so that there is some
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{r}=0
and
a_{1}\neq 0
. Let
b:=-(a_{r+1}+\cdots+a_{m})
. The hypothesis on the colouring means that there are no monochromatic solutions to
a_{1}x-a_{1}y=bz
, and hence no monochromatic solutions to

. If
b=0
then the lack of monochromatic solutions means that the colouring must be empty which is a contradiction. Hence
b\neq 0
. Let
G=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
,
\phi(x)=a_{1}^{-1}bx
which is an automorphism, and apply Theorem
3.5
to
G

-colouring of
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{*}
and an additional colour for

. Then by hypothesis the only monochromatic solution is

and the Theorem tells us that
\exp(-(r+1)^{O(1)})|G|^{2}\leq 1
. Since
|G|=p
this rearranges to give the conclusion.
∎

Theorem 3.7

(Theorem

)
.

Suppose that

(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{*}
