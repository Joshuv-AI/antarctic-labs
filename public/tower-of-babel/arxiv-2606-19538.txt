ITNet: A Learnable Integral Transform That Subsumes Convolution, Attention, and Recurrence
Ashim Dhor, Rasel Mondal, Pin-Yu Chen, [2pt] Indian Institute of Science Education, Research Bhopal, [2pt] IBM Research[2pt] ashimdhor2003@gmail.com, raselmondal61@gmail.com, pin-yu.chen@ibm.com

Abstract
Convolutional networks, recurrent networks, and transformers each encode different inductive biases - locality, sequential memory, and content - dependent pairwise interaction - and have remained mathematically distinct since their inception. We show that this fragmentation reflects not a fundamental diversity in how signals should be processed, but rather incomplete views of a single underlying mathematical object: a learnable integral transform. We introduce the Integral Transform Network (ITNet) , a unified architecture built around a learnable kernel that depends jointly on positions and features. This kernel is implemented as a small neural network, specifically an MLP, that models pairwise interactions, enabling the model to adapt its behavior from data. We show that convolution, self-attention (including multi-head), and autoregressive recurrence (including LSTM, GRU, S4, and Mamba) arise as special cases under appropriate parameterizations, and that ITNet is a universal approximator of continuous operators. To make this practical, we develop tiled kernel fusion, importance-weighted Monte Carlo integration, and learned low-rank factorization, enabling efficient and scalable computation. A single ITNet architecture with a shared operator and lightweight modality-specific encoders matches or exceeds specialized baselines on ImageNet-1K , GLUE, ModelNet40, VQA v2 and NLVR2. The results demonstrate that a single learned interaction mechanism can recover the behavior of all three architectural families from data.

The result is that modern deep learning possesses three dominant architectural families that address the same fundamental problem - transforming structured signals into semantically meaningful representations - through entirely different mathematical lenses. We study whether a single operation can unify convolution, self-attention, and recurrence as exact special cases. We show the answer is yes: a learnable integral transform, defined in Eq.
1
, whose kernel depends jointly on positions and features at both endpoints.
The operator aggregates information across all positions through a learned, content and position-dependent interaction function, while retaining a residual connection for stability. The kernel is implemented as a small neural network that receives absolute positions, relative geometry, and feature content at both the query and key locations, enabling it to model a wide range of interaction patterns. The key novelty is that interaction patterns are not hard-coded (e.g., locality in CNNs or dot-product attention in Transformers), but learned directly from data within a unified formulation. This allows a single operator to adaptively recover local, global, and sequential behaviors depending on the task, rather than requiring separate architectural designs. We call this the

2.1. The ITNet Operator

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s}
denote the input domain (e.g.,
s{=}2
for images,
s{=}1
for sequences), equipped with a positive finite measure
\mu
that defines how inputs are aggregated. Let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
represent the signal, where
d
is the feature dimension. We work in the space
\mathcal{U}=C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
of continuous
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued functions on
\Omega
, with the uniform norm
\|u\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\|u(x)\|_{2}
.

Definition 1

(ITNet operator)
.

The
ITNet operator

\mathcal{K}_{\theta}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
is defined by

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}\!\bigl(x,\,y,\,u(x),\,u(y)\bigr)\,u(y)\;d\mu(y)\;+\;W_{\theta}\,u(x),\qquad x\in\Omega,}

(1)

where
\kappa_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a learnable matrix-valued kernel parameterised by
\theta
, and
W_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a learnable residual matrix. The kernel receives query position
x
, key position
y
, and their features
u(x),u(y)
. The integral aggregates transformed features from all
y\in\Omega
; the residual ensures the operator can represent the identity (
\kappa_{\theta}=0
,
W_{\theta}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
).

Kernel parameterization:

The kernel
\kappa_{\theta}
is a 2-layer MLP (GELU
[
43
]
, width
w_{\kappa}{=}128
,
d^{2}
outputs reshaped to
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
). Its input concatenates seven groups capturing position and feature interactions. In raw form, the input is:

z_{xy}^{\mathrm{raw}}=[\,x;\;y;\;x{-}y;\;\|x{-}y\|_{2};\;u(x);\;u(y);\;u(x)\odot u(y)\,]\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{3s+1+3d},

(2)

where the three positional groups contribute
3s
dimensions (absolute
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and relative
x{-}y\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
), the scalar distance adds
1
, and the three feature groups contribute
3d
(query
u(x)
, key
u(y)
, and Hadamard product
u(x)\odot u(y)
, each in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
). To enable the kernel MLP to represent high-frequency spatial functions, we lift each positional group through the random Fourier feature map
[
87
]

\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\mathbb{R}^{2L_{f}}
(Eq.
199
), where
L
is the number of Fourier frequencies, replacing
x\mapsto\gamma(x)
,
y\mapsto\gamma(y)
,
x{-}y\mapsto\gamma(x{-}y)
. The MLP input is :

z_{xy}=[\,\gamma(x);\;\gamma(y);\;\gamma(x{-}y);\;\|x{-}y\|_{2};\;u(x);\;u(y);\;u(x)\odot u(y)\,]\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{6L_{f}+1+3d},

(3)

with
L{=}64
,
\sigma{=}10
, yielding input dimension
385+3d
.
The positional groups enable translation-invariant patterns (via
x{-}y
), distance-based decay (via
\|x{-}y\|_{2}
), and position-specific behavior (via absolute
x,y
). The feature groups - especially the Hadamard product
u(x)\odot u(y)
- provide
d
-dimensional elementwise interaction richer than the rank-1 dot product in standard attention.
By the universal approximation theorem
[
46
,
20
]
, any continuous kernel on the compact domain
\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y,u(x),u(y)):x,y\in\Omega,\,u\in\mathcal{U}_{c}\}
can be approximated to arbitrary precision.

We normalize
\mu(\Omega)=1
in all experiments:
\mu(\{x_{j}\})=1/n
for discrete domains,
\mu=\mathrm{vol}(\Omega)^{-1}\cdot\lambda
for continuous ones, ensuring the integral and residual terms operate at comparable scales.

Multi-head form:

Following
[
93
]
, the multi-head ITNet operator splits feature dimension into
H
heads of dimension
d_{h}=d/H
, applies independent kernels
\kappa_{\theta}^{(h)}
per head, and recombines via output projection
W^{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{MH}}[u])(x)=W^{O}\begin{bmatrix}\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}^{(1)}(x,y,u^{(1)}(x),u^{(1)}(y))\,u^{(1)}(y)\,d\mu(y)\\
\vdots\\
\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}^{(H)}(x,y,u^{(H)}(x),u^{(H)}(y))\,u^{(H)}(y)\,d\mu(y)\end{bmatrix}+W_{\theta}\,u(x),

(4)

where
u^{(h)}(x)=u(x)[(h{-}1)d_{h}{+}1:hd_{h}]
denotes the
h^{th}
head’s feature slice. Each head’s kernel operates on
d_{h}
-dimensional features, so the per-pair cost is
O(d_{h}^{2})=O(d^{2}/H^{2})
; summing over
H
heads gives
O(d^{2}/H)
per pair - a factor-
H
reduction relative to a single-head
d\times d
kernel. All theorems below (Theorems
1
-
4
) apply per-head; the output projection
W^{O}
linearly combines independent head contributions and does not affect the special-case proofs.

A deep ITNet model stacks
L
operator layers (
\ell=1,2,\dots,L
) with pre-normalization and position-wise feed-forward networks, following the standard pre-norm Transformer layout
[
99
]
:

z^{(\ell)}=\mathcal{K}_{\theta}^{(\ell)}[\mathrm{LN}(u^{(\ell-1)})]+u^{(\ell-1)},\qquad u^{(\ell)}=\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{(\ell)}(\mathrm{LN}(z^{(\ell)}))+z^{(\ell)},

(5)

where
\mathrm{LN}
denotes layer normalization
[
5
]
and
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}^{(\ell)}
is a two-layer FFN with GELU
[
43
]
activation and expansion factor 4. The kernel MLP output layer is initialized as
W_{2}=\epsilon\mathbf{I}_{d}
(
\epsilon{=}10^{-3}
) so each layer begins as an approximate identity. A schematic of the ITNet architecture is shown in Figure
1
. Full architectural details, initialization schemes, and hyperparameter are in
Appendix
H
.

Theorem 1

(Convolution)
.

If the kernel
\kappa_{\theta}
depends only on relative position,

\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=w_{\theta}(x-y)\,\mathbf{I}_{d}
,
then the ITNet operator reduces to convolution:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)=\int_{\Omega}w_{\theta}(x-y)\,u(y)\,d\mu(y)+W_{\theta}u(x)=(w_{\theta}\ast u)(x)+W_{\theta}u(x).

(6)

Proof sketch.

Substituting the kernel form into Eq. (
1
) gives

\int_{\Omega}w_{\theta}(x-y)\mathbf{I}_{d}u(y)\,d\mu(y)+W_{\theta}u(x)=\int_{\Omega}w_{\theta}(x-y)u(y)\,d\mu(y)+W_{\theta}u(x)
,
which is the definition of continuous convolution. The residual term is a
pointwise linear map (
1\times 1
convolution). Full proof in Appendix
C
.
∎

Remark 1
.

Discrete, depthwise, dilated, strided, grouped, and transposed convolutions are recovered by imposing corresponding structural constraints on the kernel
\kappa_{\theta}
; see Appendix
C
for details. Boundary handling under compact support is discussed in Appendix
C.12
.

Theorem 2

(Self-attention)
.

Let
W_{Q},W_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times d}
and
W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Define the kernel:

\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=\frac{\exp\!\bigl((W_{Q}u(x))^{\top}(W_{K}u(y))/\sqrt{d_{k}}\bigr)}{\int_{\Omega}\exp\!\bigl((W_{Q}u(x))^{\top}(W_{K}u(z))/\sqrt{d_{k}}\bigr)\,d\mu(z)}\cdot W_{V}.

(7)

With
W_{\theta}=0
, substituting Eq. (
7
) into Eq. (
1
) yields:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)=\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x,y)\,W_{V}u(y)\,d\mu(y),\quad\alpha(x,y)=\frac{\exp(Q(x)^{\top}K(y)/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{Z(x)},

(8)

where
Q(x)=W_{Q}u(x)
,
K(y)=W_{K}u(y)
, and
Z(x)=\int_{\Omega}\exp(Q(x)^{\top}K(z)/\sqrt{d_{k}})\,d\mu(z).

Discrete case:
For
\Omega=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, Eq. (
8
) reduces to
\mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_{k}})V
, with any uniform scaling absorbed into
W_{V}
.

Positional encoding (PE):
In ITNet, positional information is provided directly to the kernel via
(x,y)
and their relative geometry, enabling position-aware interactions without encoding schemes.

Proof:
Substituting Eq. (
7
) into Eq. (
1
) yields Eq. (
8
). The normalization term

is strictly positive on compact
\Omega

∎

Remark 2
.

Self-attention is recovered as a restricted kernel with bilinear interactions and softmax normalization; multi-head, linear, causal, and windowed variants follow similarly (Appendix
D

Theorem 3

(Recurrence as a special case)
.

Let
\Omega=[0,T]
with Lebesgue measure
\mu=\lambda
and let the kernel satisfy the causal constraint
\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,\cdot,\cdot)=0
for
y>x
. Exact recovery is obtained for linear and affine-in-input systems; nonlinear recurrent architectures are recovered through explicit unrolling constructions or approximated arbitrarily well via Theorem
4
.

(a) Linear continuous-time system.

For the linear system
\dot{h}(t)=Ah(t)+B_{\theta}u(t)
with output
y(t)=C_{\theta}h(t)+D_{\theta}u(t)
, define the causal kernel

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;=\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\,C_{\theta}\,e^{A(t-s)}\,B_{\theta},\qquad W_{\theta}=D_{\theta}.

(9)

Then
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)=\int_{0}^{t}C_{\theta}e^{A(t-s)}B_{\theta}\,u(s)\,ds+D_{\theta}u(t)=\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)
.

(a
′
) Nonlinear system (general
F_{\theta}
).

For
\dot{h}=F_{\theta}(h,u)
with Lipschitz
F_{\theta}
, the kernel generalises to

\kappa_{F}(t,s;u)=\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}\,\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\cdot\tilde{B}_{\theta}(s;u)
,
where
\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the nonlinear sensitivity matrix (Alekseev’s formula
[
3
]
) and
\tilde{B}_{\theta}(s;u)=\partial G_{\theta}/\partial u|_{(h(s),u(s))}
is the input-to-state Jacobian. Exact recovery is obtained for linear and affine-in-input systems. Structured recurrent architectures LSTM, GRU, and Mamba admit causal kernel constructions, while general nonlinear recurrent operators are approximated arbitrarily well via Theorem
4
(detailed in Appendix
E

E.3
).

(b) Discrete RNN.

Discretising to
T

\mu(\{t_{k}\})=1
, the causal kernel
\kappa(t,s)=\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}W_{h}^{t-s}W_{u}
yields
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)=\sum_{s=1}^{t}C_{\theta}W_{h}^{t-s}W_{u}u_{s}+D_{\theta}u_{t}=C_{\theta}h_{t}+D_{\theta}u_{t}
, recovering
h_{t}=W_{h}h_{t-1}+W_{u}u_{t}

(c) LSTM.

The LSTM cell state unrolls as
c_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}[\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}f_{\tau}]\odot i_{s}\odot\tilde{c}_{s}
, where
f_{\tau},i_{s}
are forget and input gates. The ITNet kernel
\kappa_{\mathrm{LSTM}}(t,s)=\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot W_{y}\mathrm{diag}(o_{t})\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(\tanh^{\prime}(c_{t})\bigr)\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}f_{\tau}\right)\mathrm{diag}(i_{s})W_{c}
is content-dependent through the gates and recovers the LSTM output.

(d) S4, Mamba, GRU.

Linear SSMs use kernel
\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot Ce^{A(t-s)}B
(causal convolution). Mamba uses

\kappa_{\mathrm{Mamba}}(t,s)=\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C(u_{t})\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}\bar{A}(u_{\tau})\cdot\bar{B}(u_{s})

\bar{A},\bar{B},C

(1-z_{\tau})
. Explicit constructions in Appendix
E
.

(e) Strictness.

A bidirectional operator
S(u)(t)=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-|t-s|/\ell}u(s)\,ds
is representable by ITNet but not by any causal recurrent system (Appendix
E
, §
E.9
).

Proof sketch.

(a):
Substituting (
9
) into (
1

\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}
restricts integration to
[0,t]

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)=\int_{0}^{t}C_{\theta}e^{A(t-s)}B_{\theta}\,u(s)\,ds+D_{\theta}u(t)=C_{\theta}h(t)+D_{\theta}u(t),

(10)

where the second equality uses the variation of constants formula

(with

(a

2.3. Universal Operator Approximation

Assumption 1
.

(i)
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is compact with
\mu(\Omega)>0
; (ii)
F:\mathcal{U}_{c}\to C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a continuous operator on compact
\mathcal{U}_{c}\subset C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})

Theorem 4

(Universal operator approximation)
.

Under Assumption
2
, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exist parameters
\theta
and depth

such that

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{c}}\bigl\|F(u)-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}^{(L)}[u]\bigr\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon.

(11)

Proof.

The proof proceeds in three steps.

Step 1 (Discretization):
Standard quadrature on compact
\Omega
approximates the integral using
M
quadrature points (samples), with error at most
\delta

[
23
]
.

Step 2 (Kernel universality):
By the MLP universal approximation theorem
[
46
,
20
]
,
\kappa_{\theta}
can approximate any continuous kernel on domain
\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y,u(x),u(y)):x,y\in\Omega,u\in\mathcal{U}_{c}\}
to within
\delta_{1}
.

Step 3 (Operator approximation):
The discretised ITNet matches the structure of
[
9
]
, which guarantees that any continuous operator on a compact set can be uniformly approximated. Choosing
M
, MLP width, and

sufficiently large ensures total error
<\varepsilon
. Full proof in Appendix

.

Theorem 5

(Kernel Recovery Under Data Symmetry)
.

Let the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
be translation-invariant:
\tau_{\delta}:u(x)\mapsto u(x-\delta)
satisfies
\mathcal{D}\circ\tau_{\delta}=\mathcal{D}
for all
\delta
. Decompose
\kappa_{\theta}=\kappa_{\theta}^{\mathrm{TI}}+\kappa_{\theta}^{\perp}
where
\kappa_{\theta}^{\mathrm{TI}}(x,y,\cdot,\cdot)=\bar{\kappa}_{\theta}(x-y,\cdot,\cdot)
is the translation-invariant component. Then under gradient flow,

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\kappa_{\theta}^{\perp}}\right\|_{F}=0\quad\text{at every iterate},

(12)

so gradient flow converges to a translation-invariant kernel, recovering the convolutional special case (Theorem
1
).

Proof Sketch.
Translation invariance of
\mathcal{D}
implies
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\ell(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[\tau_{\delta}u],y)]
. Averaging the gradient over all translations projects onto the translation-invariant subspace, annihilating the
\kappa_{\theta}^{\perp}
component.
Full proof in Appendix
G
.
∎

3.1. Tiled Kernel Fusion

Following
[
21
]
, we tile the computation into blocks that fit in on-chip Static Random-Access Memory (SRAM), fusing kernel MLP evaluation, matrix-vector product, and integral accumulation into a single Triton kernel
[
88
]
. The
n\times n
kernel matrix is never materialized; only a
B_{q}\times B_{k}
tile resides in SRAM, where
B_{q}
and
B_{k}
denote the query and key tile sizes, respectively. Algorithm
1
describes the tiled forward pass. Phase 1 auto-tunes block sizes
(B_{q}^{*},B_{k}^{*})
satisfying
(B_{q}+B_{k})\cdot d\leq S_{\mathrm{SRAM}}
, where
S_{\mathrm{SRAM}}
is the available on-chip memory capacity. Phase 2 executes tiled integration: for each query tile,
\kappa_{\theta}
is evaluated and
K_{pq}\cdot U_{j}[q]\cdot\omega_{j}
is accumulated in SRAM, where
K_{pq}=\kappa_{\theta}(X_{i}[p],X_{j}[q],U_{i}[p],U_{j}[q])
is the
kernel evaluation at tile indices
(p,q)
and
\omega_{j}=\mu(\{x_{j}\})

1/n

\kappa_{\theta}

3.2. Monte Carlo Stochastic Integration

When
n
is large, we use an importance-weighted Monte Carlo estimator: for each query
x_{i}
, sample
M\ll n
keys from a learnable proposal
p_{\phi}(y\mid x_{i})
, with parameters
\phi
independent of
\theta
:

\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{\theta}[u](x_{i})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{m},u(x_{i}),u(y_{m}))\,u(y_{m})}{p_{\phi}(y_{m}\mid x_{i})}+W_{\theta}u(x_{i}),\quad y_{m}\sim p_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x_{i}),

(13)

where
\mu(\Omega)=1
(our normalization convention from §
2.1
) absorbs the domain volume. The estimator is unbiased:
\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{M}}[\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{\theta}[u](x_{i})]=\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u](x_{i})
, with variance minimized when the proposal matches the optimal distribution

p^{*}(y\mid x_{i})\propto\|\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},y,u(x_{i}),u(y))\,u(y)\|_{2}
. The resulting complexity is
O(nMd^{2})
with
M\ll n
.
To ensure unbiased gradients, we decouple computation and sampling: (i)
p_{\phi}
is parameterized independently of
\kappa_{\theta}
, so
\nabla_{\theta}
treats sampled positions and importance weights as constants; (ii)
\phi
is trained via an auxiliary cross-entropy loss that drives
p_{\phi}
toward a self-normalized approximation of
p^{*}
. Given
M
sampled keys, define the empirical target

\hat{p}^{*}(y_{m}\mid x_{i})=\|\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{m},u(x_{i}),u(y_{m}))\,u(y_{m})\|_{2}\,/\,Z_{i}
,
where
Z_{i}
normalizes over the
M
samples:

\hskip-5.69046pt\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}(\phi)\;=\;-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{p}^{*}(y_{m}\mid x_{i})\;\log p_{\phi}(y_{m}\mid x_{i}),\hskip 2.84544ptZ_{i}=\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\bigl\|\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{m^{\prime}},u(x_{i}),u(y_{m^{\prime}}))\,u(y_{m^{\prime}})\bigr\|_{2}.

(14)

This is the cross-entropy
H(\hat{p}^{*},p_{\phi})
- a biased but consistent estimator of
\mathrm{KL}(p^{*}\|p_{\phi})+\mathrm{const}
that avoids computing the intractable normalization constant of
p^{*}
. The gradient
\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prop}}
treats
\hat{p}^{*}
as a fixed target (stop-gradient on
\theta
). The total objective is:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prop}},
where
\lambda
= 0.1.
At evaluation, sampling is replaced with deterministic
k
-means anchors in position space. This design separates
what to compute
(
\theta
) from
where to sample
(
\phi
), ensuring unbiased training. A formal analysis of estimator variance and the optimal proposal distribution is provided in Appendix
H.11
.

For long sequences, factorize
\kappa_{\theta}\approx\Phi_{\theta}(x,u(x))^{\top}\Psi_{\theta}(y,u(y))
with rank
r\ll d
. The integral decouples:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x_{i})\approx\Phi_{\theta}(x_{i},u_{i})^{\top}\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\,\Psi_{\theta}(x_{j},u_{j})\,u_{j}}_{Z}+W_{\theta}u_{i},

where
Z
is computed once in
O(nrd)
, each query in
O(rd)
, total
O(nrd)
. Error satisfies
\|\kappa_{\theta}-\Phi_{\theta}^{\top}\Psi_{\theta}\|_{F}\leq\|\kappa_{\theta}\|_{*}/\sqrt{r}

r=32

3.4. Modality-Specific Encoders

The ITNet operator acts on a signal
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the encoder defines the domain
\Omega
and initial features
u^{(0)}
.
Positional inputs are encoded via Fourier features
\gamma(\cdot)
.

Image encoder (
s{=}2
):

An image
I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}
is divided into
P{\times}P
patches (
P{=}16
), yielding
N_{p}=HW/P^{2}
tokens with positions
x_{ij}\in[0,1]^{2}
. Each token is formed by combining a linear patch embedding with Fourier positional features, followed by a projection to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A learnable
[CLS]
token is added for global aggregation. A uniform measure over patches is used.

Text encoder (
s{=}1
):

A sequence
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
is embedded via a token embedding matrix and Fourier positional encoding of normalized indices
k/n
. Relative positions are provided directly to the kernel, enabling distance-aware interactions without explicit positional biases. A causal mask is applied for autoregressive settings. The measure is uniform over tokens.

Point clouds encoder (
s{=}3
):

A point cloud
\{(p_{k},f_{k})\}_{k=1}^{n}
with
p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is mapped to features via a linear embedding (or positional encoding for coordinates-only inputs). The kernel receives raw coordinates and pairwise distances
\|p_{k}-p_{j}\|_{2}
, enabling implicit neighborhood modeling without fixed radius constraints. Outputs are aggregated via pooling for classification.

Multimodal encoder (image + text):

Image patches and text tokens are combined into a joint domain
\Omega_{\mathrm{img}}\cup\Omega_{\mathrm{txt}}
. Text positions are embedded in the 2-D space to separate modalities, and features are augmented with modality embeddings. A single ITNet processes all positions, allowing intra- and cross-modal interactions to be learned directly without explicit fusion modules.
(see Appendix
J
for additional variants and Appendix
H
for implementation details).

5. Discussion

Our results support a central hypothesis: modeling interactions jointly over content and position is more expressive than modeling them separately. Across modalities, the ITNet benefits from conditioning on both feature similarity and geometry, suggesting that common inductive biases - locality in CNNs, content based attention in Transformers, and causality in sequence models are restricted instances of a more general interaction mechanism.
Ablations show that the relative importance of content and position is modality dependent: spatial structure dominates in vision, while content interactions are more critical in language and 3D data. Rather than fixing these biases, ITNet adapts through the learned kernel, enabling cross-domain generalization.
ITNet models multiplicative content–position interactions, allowing spatial relationships to depend on features. This unifies and extends positional encoding, attention, and convolution within a single operator.
Multimodal results show that cross-modal interactions can emerge directly from a shared kernel over a joint domain, removing the need for explicit fusion modules while maintaining strong performance.

Despite these advantages, limitations remain and define important directions for future work. First, scaling ITNet to billion-parameter regimes introduces challenges in optimization stability and kernel evaluation cost; developing more efficient kernel parameterizations and training strategies is a key next step. Second, while the framework naturally supports causal structure, we have not yet evaluated it on autoregressive generation tasks, which provide the most direct test of the causal kernel; extending ITNet to long-context language modeling is an important future direction.
Third, the joint-domain formulation increases training cost in multimodal settings due to end-to-end coupling; improving efficiency through modular or partially factorized training remains an open avenue.

Toward generative ITNet:

The kernel formulation provides a path to autoregressive modeling by enforcing causality in the interaction function. In this setting, the operator admits efficient factorizations that reduce generation cost to linear time, matching state-space models while retaining the flexibility of attention. Extending to generative benchmarks would test a unified framework for bidirectional understanding and sequential generation.

6. Conclusion

We introduced ITNet, a neural architecture based on a learnable integral operator whose kernel depends jointly on positions and features. Within this framework, convolution, self-attention, and recurrence arise as exact special cases, and the resulting function class forms a universal operator approximator. Empirically, a single ITNet matches or exceeds specialized models across modalities, while scalable approximations make the operator practical. More broadly, our results suggest that the diversity of neural architectures reflects fixed assumptions about interaction structure. By learning the interaction rule directly, ITNet provides a unified view in which locality, global context, and sequential dynamics emerge from a common mechanism. This points toward general-purpose, modality-agnostic architectures where interaction patterns are learned rather than predefined.

Appendix B
Related Work

Our work sits at the intersection of four research streams: classical neural architecture families and their unification attempts, neural operator theory, efficient sequence modeling, and multimodal architectures. We discuss each in turn and clarify how ITNet relates to prior work.

Classical Architectures.

Convolutional networks
[
53
,
52
]
encode locality and translation equivariance through kernels that depend only on relative position. Despite many improvements
[
41
,
62
,
96
,
25
]
, this position-only structure remains unchanged. Transformers
[
93
]
instead model global, content-dependent interactions via attention, but restrict interactions to a bilinear form with softmax normalization and require separate positional encodings
[
93
,
84
,
71
]
. Recurrent models
[
44
,
13
]
capture sequential dependencies through state evolution but are inherently causal and difficult to parallelize. Structured state-space models such as S4
[
37
]
and Mamba
[
36
]
improve efficiency but retain constrained kernel structures.
ITNet provides a unified view in which convolution, attention, and recurrence arise as special cases of a single kernel-based operator.

Efficient Sequence Models.

A large body of work focuses on improving the efficiency of attention. Linear attention
[
50
]
and Performer
[
15
]
approximate softmax attention via kernel factorization, while sparse variants
[
7
,
102
,
61
,
99
]
restrict attention patterns. FlashAttention
[
21
]
improves efficiency without approximation through tiling. Other approaches such as Hyena
[
69
]
and MLP-Mixer
[
89
]
replace attention with structured alternatives. These methods improve efficiency but retain fixed interaction forms. In contrast, ITNet learns the interaction kernel directly and uses Monte Carlo or low-rank approximations for scalable computation.

Neural Operator Learning.

Neural operators
[
9
,
4
,
58
,
64
]
study function-to-function mappings using kernel-based architectures, with foundational results establishing universal approximation of nonlinear operators. Methods such as the Graph Neural Operator (GNO)
[
4
]
introduce learnable integral kernels of the form
\int\kappa(x,y,u(x),u(y))\,u(y)\,d\mu(y)
a signature mathematically identical to ITNet’s operator. However, GNO was developed for PDE solving and evaluated only on scientific machine learning tasks (
n\approx 10^{4}
), without establishing connections to CNNs, Transformers, or RNNs. The Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)
[
58
]
restricts the kernel to Fourier space, yielding efficient global convolution but losing content dependence and position-awareness. DeepONet
[
64
]
decomposes the operator into branch and trunk networks, imposing a low-rank structure that is less general than ITNet’s full kernel. Continuum attention
[
8
]
formalises self-attention as a continuum integral operator but does not show that convolution or recurrence are also special cases. ITNet builds on this line of work by using a general, learnable kernel and showing that standard architectures are exact special cases within this framework.

Unified Architectures.

Several works aim to relate or unify different architectures. MetaFormer
[
100
]
highlights the importance of the overall structure rather than specific operators. Prior analyses have shown that attention can express convolution
[
18
]
, and other approaches unify models at an algebraic level. Content-adaptive variants such as BiFormer
[
104
]
, deformable convolution
[
105
]
, and dynamic convolution
[
12
]
extend individual architectures but remain within restricted kernel forms. However, these methods do not provide a single operator that subsumes all families. ITNet learns the interaction rule directly, yielding a unified formulation that strictly contains convolution, attention, and recurrence.

Multimodal and Domain-Agnostic Architectures.

Perceiver
[
49
]
and Perceiver IO
[
48
]
uses cross-attention between inputs and a fixed set of latent tokens followed by latent self-attention. This introduces a compression bottleneck, as all input information must be projected into a limited latent array before further interaction. From the ITNet perspective, this corresponds to a restricted, position-blind, softmax-normalized kernel.
Most other multimodal methods rely on modality-specific encoders with explicit fusion mechanisms. For example, Flamingo
[
2
]
interleaves frozen vision features with language models via gated cross-attention, BLIP
[
56
]
and BLIP-2
[
55
]
introduce querying transformers to bridge frozen encoders, and ALBEF
[
57
]
, METER
[
27
]
, and UNITER
[
11
]
employ various cross-modal fusion strategies. More recent systems such as GPT-4V
[
1
]
integrate vision into large language models through dedicated architectural components.
In contrast, ITNet operates on a shared domain by combining positions across modalities, without latent compression or dedicated fusion modules; cross-modal interactions are learned directly through the kernel, providing a richer mechanism than standard attention.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem 1: Convolution as a Special Case of ITNet

Definition 2

(Continuous convolution)
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{s}
(or the torus
\mathbb{T}^{s}=\mathbb{R}^{s}/\mathbb{Z}^{s}
for periodic domains).
Let
\mu
be the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{s}
, denoted
dy
.
Let
w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R})
be an integrable scalar filter function.
The
continuous convolution
of signal
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
filter
w
is:

(w\ast u)(x)\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}w(x-y)\,u(y)\,dy,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}.

(15)

The integral is taken component-wise:

[(w\ast u)(x)]_{i}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}w(x-y)\,[u(y)]_{i}\,dy
for
i=1,\ldots,d
.

We use the Lebesgue measure
[
77
]
because it is the
unique

(up to positive scaling) translation-invariant
\sigma
-finite Borel measure
[
77
]
on
\mathbb{R}^{s}
(Haar’s theorem
[
31
]
). This uniqueness is what makes convolution translation-equivariant:
(w\ast\tau_{a}u)(x)=(w\ast u)(x-a)
where

\tau_{a}u(y)=u(y-a)
is the translation operator. Any other measure would break this equivariance, which is the defining property of CNNs.

Using a non-uniform measure
d\mu(y)=\rho(y)\,dy
instead yields a weighted convolution of the form
\int w(x-y)\,u(y)\,\rho(y)\,dy
, with a density
\rho
, which in general is not translation-equivariant unless
\rho
is constant. Since translation equivariance is the defining inductive bias of convolutional networks, the Lebesgue measure provides the natural and consistent choice.

Definition 3

(Discrete convolution on a regular grid)
.

Let
h>0
be the grid spacing and
\Omega_{h}=h\mathbb{Z}^{s}=\{mh:m\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}\}

the regular grid. Define the
k
-tap neighborhood
:

\mathcal{N}\;=\;\bigl\{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}:\|m\|_{\infty}\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor\bigr\},

(16)

which contains
(2\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1)^{s}
lattice points.
Given filter coefficients
F=\{f_{m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
the
discrete
k
-tap convolution
is:

(F\ast u)(x)\;=\;\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}f_{m}\cdot u(x-mh),\qquad x\in\Omega_{h}.

(17)

We use the
\ell^{\infty}
norm,
\|m\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}|m_{i}|
, to define the neighborhood, as it induces a
hypercube
structure that exactly matches the receptive field of standard CNN filters.

In contrast, using the
\ell^{2}
norm yields a
ball-shaped
neighborhood, while the
\ell^{1}
norm produces a
diamond-shaped
neighborhood, neither of which aligns with standard convolutional implementations. While the theoretical construction applies to any finite neighborhood
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, the choice of
\ell^{\infty}
ensures direct correspondence with practical CNN architectures.

Definition 4

(ITNet operator)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mu)
be a measure space.
The
Integral Transform Network (ITNet) operator

\mathcal{K}_{\theta}:C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}(x,\,y,\,u(x),\,u(y))\,u(y)\,d\mu(y)}_{\text{integral transform (global interaction)}}\;+\;\underbrace{W_{\theta}\,u(x)}_{\text{local linear residual}},

(18)

where:

•

\kappa_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a learnable matrix-valued kernel.
The four arguments are: query position
x
, key position
y
,
query features
u(x)
, key features
u(y)
.

•

The kernel output
\kappa_{\theta}(\cdots)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is
a matrix that is multiplied with the key feature vector

u(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to produce a
d
-dimensional contribution.

•

W_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a learnable weight matrix
that acts locally (pointwise) on the query feature
u(x)
.

•

\theta
collectively denotes all learnable parameters
(the kernel network weights and
W_{\theta}
).

The integral term alone cannot represent the identity mapping
u\mapsto u
. For
\kappa_{\theta}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
,

\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{I}_{d}\,u(y)\,d\mu(y)=c\cdot\bar{u},

which is independent of
x
and equals
u(x)
only if
u
is constant. The residual term
W_{\theta}u(x)
enables exact identity representation (
W_{\theta}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
), which is crucial for stable deep architectures.

Assumption 2

(Kernel regularity)
.

Throughout this proof:

(i)

\kappa_{\theta}
is jointly measurable in all four arguments.

(ii)

There exists
C_{\kappa}<\infty
such that

\|\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,a,b)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{\kappa}

for all
(x,y,a,b)
in the relevant domain.

(iii)

For Part (a),
w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
and
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

C.1. Main Theorem

Theorem C.1
ITNet
\supset
Convolution (Full Statement)

Let all notation be as in Definitions
2
–
4

and Assumption
2
.

Part (a) - Continuous convolution.

If the kernel and residual are chosen as:

\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=w(x-y)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d},\qquad W_{\theta}=0,

(19)

then
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)=(w\ast u)(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}

and all
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Part (b) - Discrete
k
-tap convolution.

On grid
\Omega_{h}=h\mathbb{Z}^{s}
with atomic measure

\mu=\sum_{y\in h\mathbb{Z}^{s}}h^{s}\delta_{y}
, if:

\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=\frac{f_{(x-y)/h}}{h^{s}}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{(x-y)/h\in\mathcal{N}\}},\qquad W_{\theta}=0,

(20)

then
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)=(F\ast u)(x)
for all
x\in\Omega_{h}
.

Part (c) - Strict inclusion.

There exists a continuous operator
T:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})

that is representable by ITNet but not by any convolution.
Hence
\mathrm{Conv}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet}
.

Part (d) - With residual (
1\times 1
convolution).

If instead
W_{\theta}\neq 0
, then:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)=(w\ast u)(x)+W_{\theta}u(x),

(21)

which is a standard convolution followed by a
1\times 1
convolution
(pointwise linear transform), as used in ResNets
[
41
]

and ConvNeXt
[
62
]
.

C.2. Proof of Part (a) - Continuous Convolution

Proof of Theorem
C.1
, Part (a)

We show that substituting
\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=w(x-y)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}
and
W_{\theta}=0
into the ITNet operator yields exactly the continuous convolution
(w\ast u)(x)
.

Step 1.

Verify the kernel choice is valid.

We must first confirm that the kernel
\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=w(x-y)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}
satisfies Definition
4
and Assumption
2
.

(a)

The kernel must map

\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Here,
w(x-y)\in\mathbb{R}
is a scalar and
\mathbf{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a matrix, so
w(x-y)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. ✓

(b)

The map
(x,y)\mapsto x-y
is continuous (hence Borel measurable). The composition
w\circ((x,y)\mapsto x-y)
is measurable because
w
is measurable (it is
L^{1}
, hence measurable) and the composition of measurable functions is measurable. Multiplying by the constant matrix
\mathbf{I}_{d}
preserves measurability. ✓

(c)

Content-independence:
The kernel depends on
(x,y)
but
not
on
u(x)
or
u(y)
. This is a
special case
of the general ITNet kernel, not a violation of it: the general kernel is allowed to depend on all four arguments, and choosing not to depend on some of them is a valid restriction. ✓

(d)

Boundedness:
If
w
is bounded (
w\in L^{\infty}
), then
\|\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,a,b)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=|w(x-y)|\leq\|w\|_{\infty}=C_{\kappa}<\infty
, satisfying Assumption
2
(ii). For the more general case
w\in L^{1}
(possibly unbounded), the integral still converges by Young’s inequality, but the kernel itself may not be uniformly bounded; this can be handled by relaxing the boundedness assumption to integrability conditions on
w
and
u
, which is standard in convolution theory.✓

Step 2.

Substitute into the ITNet operator.

Insert
\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=w(x-y)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}
and
W_{\theta}=0

into Eq. (
18
):

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\,u(y)\,dy+\cancelto{0}{W_{\theta}\,u(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\bigl[w(x-y)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\bigr]\,u(y)\,dy.

(22)

This is a direct substitution with no algebraic manipulation. The
W_{\theta}=0
term vanishes. We use
d\mu(y)=dy
(Lebesgue measure) as specified in Part (a). The integral is over
\mathbb{R}^{s}
because
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{s}
in Definition
2
.

Step 3.

Simplify the matrix-vector product
[\alpha\mathbf{I}_{d}]v
.

We need a precise identity for the product of a scalar-times-identity matrix
with a vector.

Lemma 1

(Scalar-identity-vector product)
.

For any scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

[\alpha\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}]\,v\;=\;\alpha\cdot v.

(23)

Proof of Lemma
1
.

By definition of matrix-vector multiplication:

\bigl([\alpha\mathbf{I}_{d}]v\bigr)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}[\alpha\mathbf{I}_{d}]_{ij}\,[v]_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha\delta_{ij}\,v_{j}=\alpha\cdot v_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

(24)

The second equality uses
[\alpha\mathbf{I}_{d}]_{ij}=\alpha\delta_{ij}

(the identity matrix has
1
on the diagonal and
0
elsewhere,
scaled by
\alpha
). The third equality uses
\delta_{ij}v_{j}=v_{i}

(the Kronecker delta selects the
i
-th term).
Since this holds for all
i
, the vector equation

[\alpha\mathbf{I}_{d}]v=\alpha v
follows.
∎

Applying Lemma
1
with
\alpha=w(x-y)
and
v=u(y)
:

\bigl[w(x-y)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\bigr]\,u(y)\;=\;w(x-y)\cdot u(y).

(25)

ITNet kernel is
matrix-valued
(
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
), and the product
\kappa_{\theta}(\cdots)\cdot u(y)
is a matrix-vector product, not a scalar-vector product. The lemma confirms that for the specific choice
\kappa_{\theta}=w(x-y)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}
, the matrix-vector product reduces to scalar multiplication. This is an
exact
algebraic identity - no approximation.

We use
\mathbf{I}_{d}
(identity matrix). If we instead chose
\kappa_{\theta}=w(x-y)\cdot A
for a fixed
A\neq\mathbf{I}_{d}
, we would get
\int w(x-y)Au(y)\,dy=A(w\ast u)(x)
, which is convolution followed by a fixed linear transform - still a valid special case, but the identity
A=\mathbf{I}_{d}
gives the simplest and most transparent correspondence to standard convolution. The general case
A\neq\mathbf{I}_{d}
corresponds to convolution composed with a
1\times 1
convolution, which we address in Part (d).

Step 4.

Establish existence and finiteness of the integral.

Substituting (
25
) into (
22
):

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}w(x-y)\cdot u(y)\,dy.

(26)

Before identifying this as a convolution, we must verify the integral converges.

Lemma 2

(Young’s convolution inequality
[
31
,
83
]
)
.

Let
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1+\frac{1}{r}
. If
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
and
g\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
, then
f\ast g\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
and
\|f\ast g\|_{L^{r}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}}\|g\|_{L^{q}}
.

For
p=q=1
,
r=1
: since
w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
and each component
u_{i}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
(because
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d})
), Young’s inequality gives:

\|w\ast u_{i}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}\leq\|w\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}\cdot\|u_{i}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}<\infty.

(27)

Hence
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}|w(x-y)|\cdot|u_{i}(y)|\,dy<\infty
for almost every
x
and each component
i=1,\ldots,d
. The vector-valued integral
\int w(x-y)u(y)\,dy
is interpreted component-wise (as a Bochner integral in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), and each component converges absolutely.

One might use Hölder’s inequality instead of Young’s.
Hölder gives
|\int fg|\leq\|f\|_{p}\|g\|_{q}
for conjugate exponents, but this bounds a
single
integral, not a convolution (which is a family of integrals parameterised by
x
). Young’s inequality is strictly stronger: it bounds
\|f\ast g\|_{r}
as a function of
x
, not just at a single
x
. This is why Young’s is the correct tool here.

Step 5.

Identify the convolution.

By Definition
2
, the integral in (
26
) is exactly the continuous convolution:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}w(x-y)\cdot u(y)\,dy\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Def.~\ref{def:conv_continuous}}}}{{=}}\;(w\ast u)(x).

(28)

Combining Steps 2–5:

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;(w\ast u)(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{s},\;u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d}).}

(29)

This completes the proof of Part (a).

C.3. Proof of Part (b) - Discrete Convolution

Proof of Theorem
C.1
, Part (b).

We show that on a discrete grid, the ITNet operator with appropriately scaled kernel coefficients recovers exact
k
-tap discrete convolution.

Step 1.

Convert the integral to a discrete sum.

On the grid
\Omega_{h}=h\mathbb{Z}^{s}
with atomic measure
\mu=\sum_{y\in h\mathbb{Z}^{s}}h^{s}\delta_{y}
, the Lebesgue integral
\int f\,d\mu
reduces to a sum by the definition of integration against an atomic measure:

\int_{\Omega_{h}}f(y)\,d\mu(y)\;=\;\sum_{y\in h\mathbb{Z}^{s}}h^{s}\cdot f(y).

(30)

An atomic (counting) measure
\mu=\sum_{y}w_{y}\delta_{y}
satisfies

\int f\,d\mu=\sum_{y}w_{y}f(y)
by definition of integration with respect to a discrete measure
[
77
]
. In our setting, the weights
w_{y}=h^{s}
correspond to the volume of the Voronoi cell around each grid point. Thus,
\mu
can be interpreted as a midpoint quadrature approximation of the Lebesgue measure
[
77
]
. As
h\to 0
, the discrete measure
\mu
converges weakly to the Lebesgue measure via the Riemann sum theorem
[
79
]
, ensuring consistency between Parts (a) and (b).

Applying (
30
) to the ITNet operator (
18
)
with
W_{\theta}=0
:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\sum_{y\in h\mathbb{Z}^{s}}h^{s}\cdot\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\,u(y).

(31)

Step 2.

Substitute the discrete kernel.

Insert the kernel from (
20
):

\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\;=\;\frac{f_{(x-y)/h}}{h^{s}}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{(x-y)/h\in\mathcal{N}\}}.

(32)

Substituting into (
31
):

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\sum_{y\in h\mathbb{Z}^{s}}h^{s}\cdot\frac{f_{(x-y)/h}}{h^{s}}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\cdot u(y)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{(x-y)/h\in\mathcal{N}\}}.

(33)

The factor
1/h^{s}
compensates for the
h^{s}
term arising from discretisation, ensuring consistency between continuous kernels and discrete convolutions. In standard numerical analysis, filter coefficients
f_{m}
represent total weight at offset
m
, while the continuous kernel
\kappa=f_{m}/h^{s}
represents weight per unit volume, so that density multiplied by volume recovers the discrete weights. Without this normalization, one obtains
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)=\sum_{m}h^{s}f_{m}u(x-mh)
, introducing an extra factor of
h^{s}
and deviating from standard implementations. The
1/h^{s}
factor therefore ensures exact correspondence with discrete convolution.

Step 3.

Cancel the measure weight.

In each term of the sum,
h^{s}
from the measure and
1/h^{s}
from the
kernel multiply to give:

h^{s}\cdot\frac{1}{h^{s}}=1.

(34)

Applying Lemma
1
to simplify
[f_{(x-y)/h}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}]u(y)=f_{(x-y)/h}\cdot u(y)
:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\sum_{y\in h\mathbb{Z}^{s}}f_{(x-y)/h}\cdot u(y)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{(x-y)/h\in\mathcal{N}\}}.

(35)

Step 4.

Change the summation variable.

Define
m=(x-y)/h
, equivalently
y=x-mh
.
Since
x\in h\mathbb{Z}^{s}
and
y\in h\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, we have
m=(x-y)/h\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
.
As
y
ranges over
h\mathbb{Z}^{s}
,
m
ranges over
\mathbb{Z}^{s}
- this is a bijection.

The indicator
\mathbf{1}_{\{(x-y)/h\in\mathcal{N}\}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{m\in\mathcal{N}\}}

restricts the sum to
m\in\mathcal{N}
:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}f_{m}\cdot u(x-mh).

(36)

The change of variable
m=(x-y)/h
is a bijection from
h\mathbb{Z}^{s}
to
\mathbb{Z}^{s}
. In a sum (as opposed to an integral), no Jacobian correction is needed because we are counting terms, not transforming a measure. This is the discrete analogue of the substitution
\tau=x-y
in the continuous convolution integral.

Step 5.

Identify the discrete convolution.

By Definition
3
:

\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}f_{m}\cdot u(x-mh)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Def.~\ref{def:conv_discrete}}}}{{=}}\;(F\ast u)(x).

(37)

Combining Steps 1–5:

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;(F\ast u)(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in h\mathbb{Z}^{s}.}

(38)

∎

C.4. Proof of Part (c) - Strict Inclusion

Proof of Theorem
C.1
, Part (c).

We construct an explicit operator
T
that ITNet can represent but no
convolution can, thereby proving
\mathrm{Conv}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet}
.

The proof has three stages: (i) define the witness, (ii) show ITNet
represents it, (iii) show convolution cannot represent it.
We use
two independent
arguments for stage (iii) to make the
result airtight.

Step 1.

Define the witness operator.

Fix a reference point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and let
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be any
non-affine function. Define:

T(u)(x)\;\coloneqq\;\sigma\!\bigl(u(x)^{\top}u(x_{0})\bigr)\cdot u(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}.

(39)

This operator scales the feature at position
x
by a nonlinear function of the similarity between
u(x)
and a reference feature
u(x_{0})
, thus explicitly depending on feature values rather than only spatial relationships. While simpler local nonlinear operators such as
T(u)(x)=\|u(x)\|^{2}u(x)
can be approximated by stacking convolutional layers with pointwise nonlinearities, the above construction involves non-local, content-dependent interactions between distinct positions
x
and
x_{0}
, which cannot be represented by any single convolutional layer regardless of kernel size or channel dimension.

Step 2.

Show ITNet represents
T
.

We construct an explicit ITNet kernel that computes
T
.
Choose:

\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\;=\;\sigma\!\bigl(u(x)^{\top}u(x_{0})\bigr)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\cdot\delta(y-x),\qquad W_{\theta}=0.

(40)

Substituting into (
18
):

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\sigma\!\bigl(u(x)^{\top}u(x_{0})\bigr)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\cdot\delta(y-x)\cdot u(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\sigma\!\bigl(u(x)^{\top}u(x_{0})\bigr)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\cdot\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\delta(y-x)\,u(y)\,dy}_{=u(x)}

\displaystyle=\sigma\!\bigl(u(x)^{\top}u(x_{0})\bigr)\cdot u(x)\;=\;T(u)(x).

(41)

The scalar factor
\sigma(u(x)^{\top}u(x_{0}))
is independent of
y
, so it passes outside the integral. The remaining integral
\int\delta(y-x)u(y)\,dy=u(x)
is the sifting property of the Dirac delta. Finally,
\mathbf{I}_{d}\cdot u(x)=u(x)
by Lemma
1
with
\alpha=1
.

Remark 4

(On the Dirac delta
[
79
,
46
]
)
.

The Dirac delta
\delta
can be approximated arbitrarily well by Gaussian functions
\phi_{\varepsilon}(y)=(2\pi\varepsilon^{2})^{-s/2}\exp(-\|y\|^{2}/(2\varepsilon^{2}))
, which satisfy
\phi_{\varepsilon}\to\delta
weakly as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since the ITNet kernel MLP is a universal approximator on compact domains, it can approximate
\phi_{\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
; in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, the ITNet output converges to
T(u)(x)
. The strictness conclusion holds because we require only the existence of a representing kernel, not exact pointwise equality.

Step 3.

Show no convolution can represent
T
- Argument I (Linearity).

Proposition 1

(Convolution is linear in
u
)
.

For any fixed filter
w
, the map
u\mapsto w\ast u
is linear:

w\ast(\alpha u+\beta v)=\alpha(w\ast u)+\beta(w\ast v)\qquad\forall\,\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R},\;u,v\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s},\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(42)

Proof.

[w\ast(\alpha u+\beta v)](x)=\int w(x-y)[\alpha u(y)+\beta v(y)]\,dy=\alpha\int w(x-y)u(y)\,dy+\beta\int w(x-y)v(y)\,dy=\alpha(w\ast u)(x)+\beta(w\ast v)(x)
,
by linearity of the Lebesgue integral.
∎

Proposition 2

(
T
is nonlinear in
u
)
.

The operator
T
defined in (
39
) is not linear.

Proof.

Compute
T(\alpha u)
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
:

T(\alpha u)(x)=\sigma\!\bigl((\alpha u(x))^{\top}(\alpha u(x_{0}))\bigr)\cdot\alpha u(x)=\sigma(\alpha^{2}\cdot u(x)^{\top}u(x_{0}))\cdot\alpha u(x).

(43)

If
T
were linear,
T(\alpha u)=\alpha T(u)
would require:

\sigma(\alpha^{2}z)=\sigma(z)\qquad\forall\,z\in\mathbb{R},\;\alpha\in\mathbb{R},

(44)

where
z=u(x)^{\top}u(x_{0})
. For the sigmoid

\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
:

\sigma(4\cdot 1)=\sigma(4)\approx 0.982,\qquad\sigma(1)\approx 0.731.

Since
\sigma(4)\neq\sigma(1)
, Eq. (
44
) fails
for
\alpha=2
,
z=1
. Hence
T(2u)\neq 2T(u)
and
T
is nonlinear.
∎

Conclusion from Argument I:

Since
w\ast u
is linear in
u
(Proposition
1
)
and
T
is nonlinear in
u
(Proposition
2
),
no choice of filter
w
can satisfy
w\ast u=T(u)
for all
u
.

Step 4.

Show no convolution can represent
T
- Argument II (Translation equivariance).

We provide a second, independent argument as additional assurance.

Proposition 3

(Convolution is translation-equivariant)
.

For the translation operator
\tau_{a}u(x)=u(x-a)
:

(w\ast\tau_{a}u)(x)=(w\ast u)(x-a)=\tau_{a}(w\ast u)(x).

(45)

Proof.

(w\ast\tau_{a}u)(x)=\int w(x-y)u(y-a)\,dy\stackrel{{\scriptstyle z=y-a}}{{=}}\int w(x-a-z)u(z)\,dz=(w\ast u)(x-a)
.
∎

Proposition 4

(
T
is not translation-equivariant)
.

T(\tau_{a}u)\neq\tau_{a}T(u)
in general.

Proof.

\displaystyle T(\tau_{a}u)(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma\!\bigl(u(x-a)^{\top}u(x_{0}-a)\bigr)\cdot u(x-a),

(46)

\displaystyle\tau_{a}T(u)(x)

\displaystyle=T(u)(x-a)=\sigma\!\bigl(u(x-a)^{\top}u(x_{0})\bigr)\cdot u(x-a).

(47)

These are equal only if
u(x_{0}-a)=u(x_{0})
for all
a
,
i.e.,
u
is constant at
x_{0}
under all translations - which fails
for generic
u
.
∎

Conclusion from Argument II:

Convolution commutes with translation (Proposition
3
);

T
does not (Proposition
4
).
Hence no convolution can equal
T
.

Combining both arguments:

T
is representable by ITNet (Step 2) but not by any convolution
(Steps 3–4, by two independent proofs). Therefore

T\in\mathrm{ITNet}\setminus\mathrm{Conv}
, proving

\mathrm{Conv}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet}
.
∎

C.5. Proof of Part (d) - With Residual

Proof of Theorem
C.1
, Part (d).

If
W_{\theta}\neq 0
, the ITNet operator with kernel

\kappa_{\theta}=w(x-y)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}
gives:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}w(x-y)u(y)\,dy+W_{\theta}u(x)=(w\ast u)(x)+W_{\theta}u(x).

(48)

The term
W_{\theta}u(x)
is a pointwise linear map
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

applied at each position independently. In CNN terminology, this is
a
1\times 1
convolution (a convolution with filter of size
k=1
).
The sum
(w\ast u)+W_{\theta}u
is therefore the composition of a
spatial convolution with a
1\times 1
convolution - the standard
structure used in ResNet bottleneck blocks and ConvNeXt.
∎

C.6. Multi-Channel Convolution

Standard CNNs have
C_{\mathrm{in}}
input channels and

C_{\mathrm{out}}
output channels:

[(F\ast u)(x)]_{i}=\sum_{c=1}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}f_{m,c}^{(i)}\cdot u_{c}(x-mh),\quad i=1,\ldots,C_{\mathrm{out}}.

(49)

Recovery:
Set
d=C_{\mathrm{in}}
, and choose the kernel matrix
entries as
[\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,\cdot,\cdot)]_{ic}=h^{-s}f_{(x-y)/h,c}^{(i)}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}}
.
The proof of Part (b) applies component-wise: the
i

sums contributions from all
C_{\mathrm{in}}
input channels via the
matrix-vector product
\kappa_{\theta}\cdot u(y)
, which mixes channels
through the off-diagonal entries of
\kappa_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

C.7. Depthwise Separable Convolution

Depthwise convolution
[
14
]
applies per-channel:

[(F_{\mathrm{DW}}\ast u)(x)]_{i}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}f_{m}^{(i)}u_{i}(x-mh)
.

Recovery:
Restrict
\kappa_{\theta}
to be diagonal:

\kappa_{\theta}=h^{-s}\mathrm{diag}(f_{(x-y)/h}^{(1)},\ldots,f_{(x-y)/h}^{(d)})\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}}

A diagonal matrix has zero off-diagonal entries, preventing cross-channel mixing.

C.8. Dilated (Atrous) Convolution

Dilation rate
r

\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}}f_{m}\cdot u(x-r\cdot mh)
.

Recovery:
Replace
h
with
rh
in Part (b).
The neighborhood in physical space becomes
r\mathcal{N}=\{rm:m\in\mathcal{N}\}
.

C.9. Strided Convolution

Stride

.

Recovery:

The ITNet operator is defined on any
\Omega

with
\Omega_{\mathrm{key}}=h\mathbb{Z}^{s}

C.10. Group Convolution

G

channels independently.

Recovery:
Set
\kappa_{\theta}=\mathrm{blockdiag}(K_{1},\ldots,K_{G})

where each

C.11. Transposed Convolution

Upsampling by factor

C.12. Boundary Handling in the Convolutional Special Case

A subtle yet important distinction between ITNet and standard CNNs lies in how they handle domain boundaries. For a CNN with
valid padding
(the standard convolution operation without artificial boundary extension), the output is defined only at positions where the filter fully overlaps the input domain, producing an output map of size
(H-k+1)\times(W-k+1)
for an
H\times W
input and
k\times k
filter. ITNet, by contrast, defines
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)
for
every

x\in\Omega
as an integral over the entire domain. However, when
\kappa_{\theta}
has compact support of size
k
(as in the convolutional case
\kappa_{\theta}(x,y)=w_{\theta}(x-y)
with
w_{\theta}
supported on
[-r,r]^{s}
,
k=2r+1
), outputs near the boundary naturally receive contributions from fewer input points because the integration kernel only overlaps the domain partially. ITNet makes the boundary treatment
explicit
through the domain
\Omega
and measure
\mu
, rather than implicit through hard-coded padding strategies.

To recover standard CNN behavior exactly, three approaches are available:

(i) Evaluate ITNet only at valid positions:
Restrict queries to the subset
\Omega_{\text{valid}}=\{x\in\Omega:\operatorname{supp}(w_{\theta}(x-\cdot))\subseteq\Omega\}
, which yields exactly the
(H-k+1)\times(W-k+1)
outputs of a valid convolution. This is the cleanest mathematical formulation, as no artificial points are introduced.

(ii) Extend
\Omega
with zero-valued points:
Define
\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega\cup\partial\Omega

where
\partial\Omega
contains
k-1
layers of points around the boundary, and set
u(y)=0
for
y\in\partial\Omega
. This recovers standard zero-padding convolution, producing a dense output map of size
H\times W
. The same mechanism generalizes to reflection, replication, or periodic padding by appropriately defining
u
on the extended domain.

(iii) Ignore boundary effects:
For tasks where boundary effects are negligible (e.g., large images with small filters), the relative number of boundary points scales as
O((H+W)/HW)
, which vanishes for large
H,W
.

Remark 5
.

In practice, for sufficiently large inputs (e.g.,
224\times 224
ImageNet-1K images with
k=7
or
k=3
filters), the boundary constitutes less than
6\%
of the spatial domain. Consequently, the practical impact of boundary treatment on learned representations is minimal, and any consistent strategy yields near-identical performance. Our experiments use valid convolution (Option i) for consistency with the continuous formulation.

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem 2: Self-Attention as a Special Case of ITNet

Definition 5

(Continuous scaled dot-product attention
[
93
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
be compact,
\mu
a Borel measure on
\Omega
with

\mu(\Omega)<\infty
, and
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
a feature function.
Given learnable projection matrices

W_{Q},W_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times d}
and
W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
define query and key functions:

Q(x)\coloneqq W_{Q}\,u(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}},\qquad K(y)\coloneqq W_{K}\,u(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}.

The
continuous scaled dot-product attention
is:

\mathrm{Attn}(u)(x)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x,y)\,W_{V}u(y)\,d\mu(y),

(50)

where the
attention weight

\alpha:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}

is:

\alpha(x,y)\;=\;\frac{\exp\!\bigl(Q(x)^{\top}K(y)/\sqrt{d_{k}}\bigr)}{Z(x)},\qquad Z(x)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\exp\!\bigl(Q(x)^{\top}K(z)/\sqrt{d_{k}}\bigr)\,d\mu(z).

(51)

Remark 6

(Why
\sqrt{d_{k}}
scaling?)
.

The scaling factor
1/\sqrt{d_{k}}
prevents the dot products from growing
large in magnitude as
d_{k}
increases.
For random unit vectors
q,k\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
,

\mathrm{Var}[q^{\top}k]=d_{k}
,
so
q^{\top}k/\sqrt{d_{k}}
has unit variance regardless of
d_{k}
.
Without this scaling, the softmax saturates for large
d_{k}
,
causing vanishing gradients.
The scaling is part of the kernel definition and is absorbed into

\kappa_{\theta}
in the ITNet formulation.

Definition 6

(Discrete scaled dot-product attention
[
93
]
)
.

For
n
positions
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset\Omega
with uniform measure

\omega_{j}=1/n
, let

Q=[W_{Q}u(x_{1});\ldots;W_{Q}u(x_{n})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{k}}
,

K=[W_{K}u(x_{1});\ldots;W_{K}u(x_{n})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{k}}
,

V=[W_{V}u(x_{1});\ldots;W_{V}u(x_{n})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.
The

\mathrm{Attn}(u)\;=\;\softmax\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},

(52)

where

is applied row-wise.

Definition 7

(Multi-head attention
[
93
]
)
.

With
H

W_{Q}^{h},W_{K}^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times d}
,

W_{V}^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d}

W_{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times Hd_{v}}

be learnable matrices for head

Define:

\displaystyle\mathrm{head}_{h}(u)(x)

\displaystyle\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{h}(x,y)\,W_{V}^{h}u(y)\,d\mu(y),

(53)

\displaystyle\alpha_{h}(x,y)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{\exp\!\bigl((W_{Q}^{h}u(x))^{\top}(W_{K}^{h}u(y))/\sqrt{d_{k}}\bigr)}{Z_{h}(x)}.

(54)

The
multi-head attention

\mathrm{MHA}(u)(x)\;=\;W_{O}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{head}_{1}(u)(x)\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{head}_{H}(u)(x)\end{bmatrix}.

(55)

Definition 8

(ITNet operator)
.

As in Definition
1
(§
2.1

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}(x,\,y,\,u(x),\,u(y))\,u(y)\,d\mu(y)\;+\;W_{\theta}\,u(x),

(56)

where
\kappa_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

is measurable and
W_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is learnable.

Assumption 3

(Regularity conditions)
.

Throughout this proof:

(i)

u\in C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
:
u
is continuous (hence bounded on compact
\Omega
).

(ii)

W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}
are bounded:
\|W_{Q}\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|W_{K}\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|W_{V}\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\infty
.

(iii)

\Omega
is compact with
\mu(\Omega)<\infty
.

These hold in all our experiments since feature vectors are bounded on finite
token sets.

Remark 7

(Positional encodings are absorbed by the ITNet kernel)
.

Standard Transformers augment attention logits with a fixed positional bias
\mathrm{PE}(x,y)
(e.g., sinusoidal embeddings, RoPE
[
84
]
, or ALiBi
[
71
]
). In ITNet, the kernel
\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))
receives raw positions
x
and
y
as explicit arguments via the Fourier-lifted input
z_{xy}
(Eq. (
3
)). Consequently, any fixed function of positions can be incorporated directly into the kernel computation; the ITNet kernel does not require a separate additive bias term. Moreover, additive positional embeddings of the form
\tilde{u}(x)=u(x)+e(x)
are handled by simply redefining the input signal. Thus, the proofs below hold with or without positional encodings: attention with any fixed positional bias remains a special case of the ITNet operator. The key distinction is that standard positional encodings are fixed and not content-adaptive, whereas ITNet’s kernel conditions jointly on position and content.

D.2. Proof of Part (a) - Single-Head Continuous Attention

Proof of Theorem
D.1
(a) – Continuous Case.

Step 1.

Substitute the kernel into the ITNet operator.

Insert (
57
) and
W_{\theta}=0
into (
56
):

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\frac{\exp(Q(x)^{\top}K(y)/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{Z(x)}\cdot W_{V}\cdot u(y)\,d\mu(y).

(59)

Step 2.

Verify the partition function
Z(x)
is well-defined and positive.

Z(x)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\exp\!\bigl(Q(x)^{\top}K(z)/\sqrt{d_{k}}\bigr)\,d\mu(z).

(60)

Since
u\in C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\Omega
is compact,

\|u\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\|u(x)\|_{2}<\infty
.
Let
C_{Q}=\|W_{Q}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
,
C_{K}=\|W_{K}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
,

R=\|u\|_{\infty}
. By Cauchy–Schwarz
[
78
]
:

\left|Q(x)^{\top}K(z)/\sqrt{d_{k}}\right|\;\leq\;\frac{\|Q(x)\|_{2}\cdot\|K(z)\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{Q}R\cdot C_{K}R}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\;\eqqcolon\;M<\infty.

(61)

Therefore:

0\;<\;e^{-M}\cdot\mu(\Omega)\;\leq\;Z(x)\;\leq\;e^{M}\cdot\mu(\Omega)\;<\;\infty.

(62)

Step 3.

Factor
W_{V}
outside the integral.

Since
Z(x)
does not depend on
y
, pull it and
W_{V}
outside:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\frac{1}{Z(x)}\int_{\Omega}\exp\!\bigl(Q(x)^{\top}K(y)/\sqrt{d_{k}}\bigr)\cdot W_{V}u(y)\,d\mu(y).

(63)

Step 4.

Establish the normalization property of
\alpha(x,y)
.

\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x,y)\,d\mu(y)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\frac{\exp(Q(x)^{\top}K(y)/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{Z(x)}\,d\mu(y)\;=\;\frac{Z(x)}{Z(x)}\;=\;1.

(64)

Step 5.

Write as a probability-weighted integral.

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x,y)\,W_{V}u(y)\,d\mu(y)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\alpha(x,\cdot)}\!\bigl[W_{V}u(y)\bigr].

(65)

Step 6.

Recognise the continuous attention operator.

Comparing (
63
) with Definition
5
:

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x,y)\,W_{V}u(y)\,d\mu(y)\;=\;\mathrm{Attn}(u)(x).}

(66)

∎

Proof of Theorem

(a) - Discrete Case.

Replace

Substituting (

\displaystyle\alpha(x_{i},x_{j})

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{j}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{l}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}\;=\;\frac{\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{j}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{l}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}.

Substituting into (

\displaystyle(\hat{\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}[u])(x_{i})

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{j}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l}\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{l}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}\cdot W_{V}u(x_{j})

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\frac{1}{n}\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{j}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l}\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{l}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}\cdot W_{V}u(x_{j}).

(70)

The factors

cancel in numerator and denominator:

(\hat{\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}[u])(x_{i})\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{j}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{\sum_{l}\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{l}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}\cdot v_{j}\;=\;\left[\softmax\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V\right]_{i},

(71)

where

is the

\boxed{(\hat{\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}[u])(x_{i})\;=\;\left[\softmax\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V\right]_{i}\;=\;\mathrm{Attn}(u)(x_{i}).}

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{Attn}(u)(x)\|_{2}

(normalization (

D.6. Strictness Argument 2: Position-Dependence

Proof via permutation equivariance.

Step 1.

For any permutation

i.e. permuting the input tokens permutes the output tokens in the same way. This holds because the kernel
Q(x)^{\top}K(y)
depends only on feature values, not on the indices
i,j
.

Step 2.

Define a position-dependent witness.

Define:

T_{\mathrm{pos}}(u)(x)\;\coloneqq\;\int_{\Omega}\|x-y\|_{2}\cdot u(y)\,d\mu(y).

(86)

This operator weights each position
y
by its distance from
x
. Positions closer to
x
get
less
weight; positions farther away get
more
weight. It is
not
permutation equivariant: permuting positions changes the distances
\|x-y\|_{2}
.

Step 3.

Show ITNet represents
T_{\mathrm{pos}}
.

Set:

\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=\|x-y\|_{2}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d},\qquad W_{\theta}=0.

Then
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)=\int_{\Omega}\|x-y\|_{2}\cdot u(y)\,d\mu(y)=T_{\mathrm{pos}}(u)(x)
.

This kernel is valid:
\|x-y\|_{2}
is continuous in
(x,y)
(Euclidean
norm is continuous), and bounded on compact
\Omega
.

Step 4.

Show no standard attention can represent
T_{\mathrm{pos}}
.

Suppose for contradiction that
\mathrm{Attn}(u)(x_{i})=T_{\mathrm{pos}}(u)(x_{i})
for all
u
and all
i
.

Choose
n=2
tokens at positions
x_{1}=0
,
x_{2}=1
(1D case), with
u(x_{1})=e_{1}
,
u(x_{2})=e_{2}
(standard basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
). Then:

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{pos}}(u)(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\|x_{1}-x_{1}\|_{2}\cdot e_{1}\cdot(1/n)+\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}\cdot e_{2}\cdot(1/n)=0\cdot e_{1}/2+1\cdot e_{2}/2=e_{2}/2.

(87)

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{pos}}(u)(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\|x_{2}-x_{1}\|_{2}\cdot e_{1}/2+0\cdot e_{2}/2=e_{1}/2.

(88)

Now permute: let
\sigma
swap
x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{2}
, so

\sigma(u)(x_{1})=e_{2}
,
\sigma(u)(x_{2})=e_{1}
. Then:

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{pos}}(\sigma(u))(x_{1})

\displaystyle=0\cdot e_{2}/2+1\cdot e_{1}/2=e_{1}/2.

(89)

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{pos}}(\sigma(u))(x_{2})

\displaystyle=1\cdot e_{2}/2+0\cdot e_{1}/2=e_{2}/2.

(90)

Note
T_{\mathrm{pos}}(\sigma(u))(x_{1})=e_{1}/2\neq e_{2}/2=\sigma(T_{\mathrm{pos}}(u))(x_{1})
, so
T_{\mathrm{pos}}
is not permutation equivariant - as claimed.

If attention (without positional encoding) were to equal
T_{\mathrm{pos}}
, it would need to be not permutation equivariant - but it is, by (
85
). Contradiction.

Therefore
T_{\mathrm{pos}}\in\mathrm{ITNet}\setminus\mathrm{Attn}
, giving a second witness for
\mathrm{Attn}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet}
.
∎

Remark 9

(Positional encodings and RoPE/ALiBi)
.

Standard Transformers add positional encodings (sinusoidal, learned, RoPE, or ALiBi) to introduce position-dependence. With positional encodings, the kernel becomes
Q(x)^{\top}K(y)+\mathrm{PE}(x,y)
for some fixed function
\mathrm{PE}(x,y)
- partially recovering position-dependence. However,
\mathrm{PE}
is
fixed
(not content-dependent): it does not adapt based on the features
u(x)
or
u(y)
. ITNet’s kernel
\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))
conditions jointly on
both
position and content - a strictly richer class than attention with any fixed positional encoding scheme. The ablation in Table 5 of the main paper confirms that removing the position terms from the ITNet kernel costs
-0.8\%
on ImageNet-1K, quantifying the value of this joint conditioning.

D.7. Linear Attention as a Special Case

Several efficient Transformer variants approximate or replace softmax attention with a linear kernel
[
50
]
. We show these are also special cases of ITNet.

Proposition 5

(Linear attention
\subset
ITNet)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}_{>0}
be a feature map with
\phi(q)^{\top}\phi(k)\approx\exp(q^{\top}k/\sqrt{d_{k}})
(e.g.
\phi(x)=\mathrm{elu}(x)+1
).
The linear attention operator:

\mathrm{Attn}_{\mathrm{lin}}(u)(x)\;=\;\frac{\phi(Q(x))^{\top}\sum_{y}\phi(K(y))\otimes W_{V}u(y)}{\phi(Q(x))^{\top}\sum_{y}\phi(K(y))}

(91)

is a special case of the ITNet operator.

Proof.

Set
\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=[\phi(Q(x))^{\top}\phi(K(y))/Z_{\phi}(x)]\cdot W_{V}
where
Z_{\phi}(x)=\int_{\Omega}\phi(Q(x))^{\top}\phi(K(y))\,d\mu(y)
. Substituting into (
56
) with
W_{\theta}=0
gives (
91
) exactly. The kernel is bounded by
\|W_{V}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
(since
\phi(Q(x))^{\top}\phi(K(y))/Z_{\phi}(x)
is a normalized weight), measurable, and content-dependent.
∎

D.8. Causal (Masked) Attention as a Special Case

Proposition 6

(Causal attention
\subset
ITNet)
.

GPT-style causal (autoregressive) attention with mask
\mathbf{1}_{j\leq i}
:

\mathrm{Attn}_{\mathrm{causal}}(u)(x_{i})\;=\;\sum_{j\leq i}\frac{\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{j}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{\displaystyle\sum_{l\leq i}\exp(q_{i}^{\top}k_{l}/\sqrt{d_{k}})}\cdot v_{j}

(92)

is a special case of the ITNet operator.

Proof.

Set the kernel with causal masking:

\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\;=\;\mathbf{1}_{y\leq x}\cdot\frac{\exp(Q(x)^{\top}K(y)/\sqrt{d_{k}})}{Z_{\mathrm{causal}}(x)}\cdot W_{V},

(93)

where
Z_{\mathrm{causal}}(x)=\int_{y\leq x}\exp(Q(x)^{\top}K(y)/\sqrt{d_{k}})\,d\mu(y)
.
The indicator
\mathbf{1}_{y\leq x}
is measurable (it is the indicator of a closed half-space in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
), so (
93
) is a valid ITNet kernel. Substituting into (
56
) recovers (
92
) by the same steps as Parts (a) and (b). In the discrete case,
\mathbf{1}_{j\leq i}
is the lower-triangular mask.
∎

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem 3: Recurrence as a Special Case of ITNet

Definition 9

(Continuous-time recurrent system)

\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)\;=\;C_{\theta}\,h(t)\;+\;D_{\theta}\,u(t),

where

Definition 10

(Discrete-time RNN)
.

With time steps
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{T}\}

h_{t}\;=\;\phi(W_{h}h_{t-1}+W_{u}u_{t}+b),\qquad t=1,\ldots,T,

W_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
W_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,

, and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a nonlinear activation

\tanh
).

Definition 11

(LSTM)
.

The Long Short-Term Memory
[
44
]

\displaystyle f_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{f}[h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b_{f})

(forget gate)

\displaystyle i_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{i}[h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b_{i})

(input gate)

\displaystyle o_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{o}[h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b_{o})

(output gate)

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{t}

\displaystyle=\tanh(W_{c}[h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b_{c})

(cell candidate)

\displaystyle c_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{t}\odot c_{t-1}+i_{t}\odot\tilde{c}_{t}

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=o_{t}\odot\tanh(c_{t})

(hidden state)

is element-wise multiplication.

Definition 12

(Linear State Space Model (SSM))
.

A

(as in S4
[
37
]

\displaystyle\frac{dh}{dt}(t)

\displaystyle=A\,h(t)+B\,u(t),

(97)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=C\,h(t)+D\,u(t),

(98)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,

depends on the hidden state

Definition 13

(Selective SSM – Mamba)

Mamba
[
36
]
generalises the linear SSM by making the system matrices input-dependent (“selective”):

\displaystyle\bar{A}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp(\Delta(t)\cdot A),

(99)

\displaystyle\bar{B}(t)

\displaystyle=\Delta(t)\cdot B(u(t)),

(100)

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=\bar{A}(t)\,h_{t-1}+\bar{B}(t)\,u_{t},

(101)

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=C(u_{t})\,h_{t},

(102)

where
\Delta(t)=\mathrm{softplus}(W_{\Delta}u_{t}+b_{\Delta})>0

is a learnable input-dependent step size,

B(u_{t})=W_{B}u_{t}
and
C(u_{t})=W_{C}u_{t}
are input-dependent projection matrices, and

is a fixed (e.g. diagonal) initialization.

Definition 14

(ITNet operator)
.

As in Definition
1
(§
2.1
):

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)\;=\;\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}(x,\,y,\,u(x),\,u(y))\,u(y)\,d\mu(y)\;+\;W_{\theta}\,u(x),

(103)

where
\kappa_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is measurable,
W_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is learnable.

Assumption 4

(Regularity for recurrent proofs)
.

(i)

\Omega=[0,T]
with
T<\infty
.

(ii)

u\in C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
: input is continuous.

(iii)

F_{\theta}
in (
94

[
17
]
.

(iv)

All weight matrices are bounded.

E.1. Main Theorem

Theorem E.1
ITNet
\supset
Recurrence

Let
\Omega=[0,T]
,
\mu
be Lebesgue measure, and Assumption
4
hold.

(a) Linear continuous-time system.

For the linear system
F_{\theta}(h,u)=Ah+B_{\theta}u
, the solution of (
94
)–(
95
) can be written as:

\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)\;=\;C_{\theta}\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(t,s)\,B_{\theta}\,u(s)\,ds\;+\;D_{\theta}\,u(t)\;+\;C_{\theta}\,e^{At}\,h_{0},

(104)

where
\Phi(t,s)\coloneqq e^{A(t-s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the state transition matrix (defined explicitly in Eq. (
110
) below: it satisfies
\partial_{t}\Phi(t,s)=A\Phi(t,s)
with
\Phi(s,s)=I_{n}
, encoding how the hidden state at time
s
propagates to time
t
). This is a special case of the ITNet operator with causal kernel:

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;=\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}\,\Phi(t,s)\,B_{\theta},\qquad W_{\theta}=D_{\theta}.

(105)

(a
′
) Nonlinear continuous-time system (general
F_{\theta}
).

For a general nonlinear system (
94

F_{\theta}
, the output operator
u\mapsto\mathrm{RNN}(u)
is a continuous operator on compact input sets, and is therefore approximable to arbitrary precision by an ITNet operator (via Theorem
4
). Moreover, the exact output can be written as an ITNet with a content-dependent causal kernel constructed via the nonlinear variation of constants formula (Alekseev’s formula). See §
E.3
for the complete proof.

(b) Discrete-time RNN / LSTM / GRU.

The discrete recurrence (
96

\Omega
to
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{T}\}
with atomic measure
\mu(\{t_{k}\})=\Delta t
, and setting a causal kernel encoding the recurrent update function.

(c) Linear SSM (S4).

The SSM (
97

98
) is recovered by:

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;=\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C\,e^{A(t-s)}\,B,\qquad W_{\theta}=D.

(106)

(d) Selective SSM (Mamba).

Mamba (
101
)–(
102
) is recovered by:

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;=\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C(u(t))\cdot\prod_{\tau=s}^{t-1}\bar{A}(\tau)\cdot\bar{B}(s),\qquad W_{\theta}=0.

Since
\bar{A}

\bar{B}

and

(e) Strictness.

There exists a continuous operator representable by ITNet but not by any causal recurrent system.

E.2. Proof of Part (a) – Linear Continuous-Time RNN

Proof of Theorem
E.1
(a).

Step 1.

Apply the variation of constants formula.

For the linear system
F_{\theta}(h,u)=Ah+B_{\theta}u
, the ODE (
94
) becomes:

\frac{dh}{dt}(t)=A\,h(t)+B_{\theta}\,u(t),\qquad h(0)=h_{0}.

(108)

The solution is given by the variation of constants formula
[
17
]
:

h(t)\;=\;\underbrace{e^{At}h_{0}}_{\text{free response}}\;+\;\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}e^{A(t-s)}\,B_{\theta}\,u(s)\,ds}_{\text{forced response}}.

(109)

Step 2.

Write the state transition matrix explicitly.

For a linear system, define the
state transition matrix
:

\Phi(t,s)\;\coloneqq\;e^{A(t-s)},\qquad t\geq s\geq 0.

(110)

\Phi(t,s)
encodes how the hidden state at time
s
evolves to time
t
under the autonomous dynamics
\dot{h}=Ah
.
Note that
\Phi(t,t)=I_{n}
and
\Phi(t,s)=\Phi(t,r)\Phi(r,s)
.

Step 3.

Compute the output
\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)=C_{\theta}h(t)+D_{\theta}u(t)
.

Substituting (
109
) into (
95
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)

\displaystyle\;=\;C_{\theta}\!\left[e^{At}h_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{A(t-s)}B_{\theta}u(s)\,ds\right]+D_{\theta}u(t)

\displaystyle\;=\;\underbrace{C_{\theta}e^{At}h_{0}}_{\text{initial state term}}+\int_{0}^{t}\underbrace{C_{\theta}e^{A(t-s)}B_{\theta}}_{\text{impulse response }g(t-s)}u(s)\,ds+D_{\theta}u(t).

(111)

Step 4.

Express as an ITNet operator with causal kernel.

Define the ITNet kernel:

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;\coloneqq\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}\,e^{A(t-s)}\,B_{\theta}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

(112)

and set
W_{\theta}=D_{\theta}
.
Then the ITNet operator gives:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)

\displaystyle\;=\;\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}e^{A(t-s)}B_{\theta}\cdot u(s)\,ds+D_{\theta}u(t)

\displaystyle\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}C_{\theta}e^{A(t-s)}B_{\theta}u(s)\,ds+D_{\theta}u(t).

(113)

Step 5.

Handle the initial state term.

Comparing (
111
) and (
113
), the initial state term
C_{\theta}e^{At}h_{0}
is not present in (
113
). We consider two cases:

Method 1 (Zero initial state):

If
h_{0}=0
(standard in most RNN training), the initial state term vanishes and (
113
) exactly equals (
111
).

Method 2 (Non-zero initial state):

If
h_{0}\neq 0
, include the initial state by augmenting the input:

\tilde{u}(s)\;=\;\begin{cases}h_{0}&s=0\\
u(s)&s>0\end{cases}

(114)

and setting the kernel to extract
h_{0}
at

:
\kappa_{\theta}(t,0,u(t),h_{0})=\mathbf{1}_{0\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}e^{At}
. Then the full solution (

Step 6.

Conclude.

From Steps 1–5, with

:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)

\displaystyle\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}C_{\theta}e^{A(t-s)}B_{\theta}u(s)\,ds+D_{\theta}u(t)

\displaystyle\;=\;C_{\theta}h(t)+D_{\theta}u(t)\;=\;\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t).

(115)

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)\;=\;\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t).}

(116)

E.3. Proof of Part (a
′
) – Nonlinear Continuous-Time RNN (General
F_{\theta}
)

The linear proof (Part (a)) uses the matrix exponential
e^{A(t-s)}
as the state transition operator, which is only valid when
F_{\theta}(h,u)=Ah+B_{\theta}u
is linear in
h
. For a general nonlinear
F_{\theta}
, no closed-form state transition matrix exists. We provide two independent proofs: an
exact
proof via the nonlinear variation of constants formula (Alekseev’s formula), and a
universal approximation
argument.

E.3.1
Proof Strategy A: Kernel Construction via Alekseev’s Formula

Proof of Theorem
E.1
(a
′
) – Exact.

Step 1.

State the nonlinear variation of constants formula.

Consider the general nonlinear ODE (
94
):

\dot{h}(t)=F_{\theta}(h(t),u(t)),\qquad h(0)=h_{0}.

(117)

Let
\psi(t;s,\xi)
denote the flow of the
non-autonomous
ODE
\dot{z}=F_{\theta}(z,u(t))
started at
z(s)=\xi
, i.e.,
\psi(t;s,\xi)
is the solution at time
t
of
\dot{z}(\tau)=F_{\theta}(z(\tau),u(\tau))
with
z(s)=\xi
.

Define the
nonlinear state transition operator
(sensitivity matrix):

\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\;\coloneqq\;\frac{\partial\psi(t;s,h(s))}{\partial h(s)}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

(118)

which is the Jacobian of the solution at time
t
with respect to the initial condition at time
s
, evaluated along the trajectory
h(\cdot)
generated by input
u
.

By the Alekseev–Gröbner nonlinear variation of constants formula
[
3
,
34
]
, the solution of (
117
) satisfies:

h(t)=\psi(t;0,h_{0})=h_{\mathrm{free}}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\cdot G(h(s),u(s))\,ds,

(119)

where
h_{\mathrm{free}}(t)
is the free-response solution (with
u=0
) and
G(h(s),u(s))=F_{\theta}(h(s),u(s))-F_{\theta}(h(s),0)
is the input-driven component.

Step 2.

Properties of the nonlinear sensitivity matrix
\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)
.

The sensitivity matrix
\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)
satisfies the
variational equation
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)=\frac{\partial F_{\theta}}{\partial h}\bigg|_{(h(t),u(t))}\cdot\Phi_{F}(t,s;u),\qquad\Phi_{F}(s,s;u)=I_{n},

(120)

where
\frac{\partial F_{\theta}}{\partial h}\big|_{(h(t),u(t))}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the Jacobian of
F_{\theta}
with respect to its first argument, evaluated along the trajectory. This is a
linear
ODE in
\Phi_{F}
(though with time-varying, input-dependent coefficients), so standard ODE theory guarantees existence and uniqueness.

Key properties (analogous to the linear case):

(i)

\Phi_{F}(t,t;u)=I_{n}
for all
t
.

(ii)

\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)=\Phi_{F}(t,r;u)\cdot\Phi_{F}(r,s;u)
for
t\geq r\geq s
(chain rule for flows).

(iii)

\|\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq e^{L_{F}(t-s)}
where
L_{F}
is the Lipschitz constant of
F_{\theta}
in
h
(by Grönwall’s inequality
[
35
]
).

(iv)

\Phi_{F}
depends on
u
through the trajectory
h(\cdot)
, making it
content-dependent
.

(v)

In the linear case
F_{\theta}(h,u)=Ah+B_{\theta}u
, we recover
\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)=e^{A(t-s)}
(independent of
u
), consistent with Part (a).

Step 3.

Decompose
F_{\theta}
into autonomous and input-driven components.

Write
F_{\theta}(h,u)=F_{\theta}(h,0)+G_{\theta}(h,u)
where
G_{\theta}(h,u)\coloneqq F_{\theta}(h,u)-F_{\theta}(h,0)
captures the effect of the input. Then Alekseev’s formula (
119
) gives:

h(t)=\underbrace{\psi(t;0,h_{0})}_{\text{free response (no input)}}+\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\cdot G_{\theta}(h(s),u(s))\,ds.

(121)

For standard RNN architectures,
F_{\theta}(h,u)=\phi(Wh+Wu+b)
, so
F_{\theta}(h,0)=\phi(Wh+b)
and
G_{\theta}(h,u)=\phi(Wh+Wu+b)-\phi(Wh+b)
, which depends on both
h
and
u
.

Step 4.

Write the output as an integral operator.

Substituting (
121
) into the output equation
y(t)=C_{\theta}h(t)+D_{\theta}u(t)
:

\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)=C_{\theta}\psi(t;0,h_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}C_{\theta}\,\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\cdot G_{\theta}(h(s),u(s))\,ds+D_{\theta}\,u(t).

(122)

Step 5.

Express
G_{\theta}(h(s),u(s))
in terms of
u(s)
.

Since
h(s)
is itself a functional of
u
, the term
G_{\theta}(h(s),u(s))
depends on
u(s)
and, in general, on the entire input history
u(\tau)
for
\tau\in[0,s]
.

Under differentiability of
G_{\theta}
in
u
, a first-order Taylor expansion yields

G_{\theta}(h(s),u(s))=\tilde{B}_{\theta}(s)\,u(s)+R(s;u),

(123)

where

\tilde{B}_{\theta}(s)\coloneqq\frac{\partial G_{\theta}}{\partial u}(h(s),u(s))\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

(124)

is the input-to-state Jacobian, and
R(s;u)
collects higher-order terms.

For systems where
G_{\theta}(h,u)
is linear or affine in
u
(e.g.,
\dot{h}=f(h)+B_{\theta}u
), the remainder vanishes (
R(s;u)=0
), yielding exact recovery. In general,
R(s;u)=O(\|u(s)\|^{2})
under smoothness assumptions.

Step 6.

Construct the ITNet kernel for the full nonlinear system.

Define the content-dependent, causal ITNet kernel:

\kappa_{F}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;\coloneqq\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}\,\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\cdot\tilde{B}_{\theta}(s;u)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

(125)

with
W_{\theta}=D_{\theta}
. Here the kernel depends causally on the full input trajectory through the induced hidden-state evolution, extending the local kernel notation of Definition
14
.

Then:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\kappa_{F}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\cdot u(s)\,ds+D_{\theta}u(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}C_{\theta}\,\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\cdot\tilde{B}_{\theta}(s;u)\cdot u(s)\,ds+D_{\theta}u(t).

(126)

Comparing with (
122
) (ignoring the initial state term as in Part (a), Method 1):

\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)-(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)=\int_{0}^{t}C_{\theta}\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\cdot R(s;u)\,ds,

(127)

where
R(s;u)
is the higher-order remainder from Step 5.

Step 7.

Conclusion for nonlinear
F_{\theta}
.

Case 1:
F_{\theta}
is affine in
u
.

If
F_{\theta}(h,u)=f(h)+B_{\theta}u
, then
G_{\theta}(h,u)=B_{\theta}u
and the remainder
R(s;u)=0
. In this case, the kernel (
125
) recovers the RNN output exactly:

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)=\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t).}

Case 2: General (non-affine)
F_{\theta}
.

For fully nonlinear
F_{\theta}
, the input contribution
G_{\theta}(h(s),u(s))
depends on the hidden state
h(s)
, which itself depends on the entire input history. Consequently, it cannot in general be expressed solely as a function of
(t,s,u(t),u(s))
, and therefore no explicit ITNet kernel of the form in Definition
14
can exactly recover the RNN output.

However, under Assumption
4
, the operator
u\mapsto\mathrm{RNN}(u)
is continuous on compact subsets of
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By Theorem
4
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an ITNet operator
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
such that

\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\|\mathrm{RNN}(u)-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u]\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon.

Thus, exact recovery holds for the affine-in-
u
case, while general nonlinear systems are approximated arbitrarily well.

Step 8.

Verify kernel validity (affine-in-
u
case).

The kernel
\kappa_{F}
from (
125
) satisfies all ITNet conditions:

(i)

Causality:

\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}
ensures
\kappa_{F}=0
for
s>t
.

(ii)

Content-dependence:

\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)
depends on
u
through the trajectory
h(\cdot)
, and
\tilde{B}_{\theta}(s)
depends on
(h(s),u(s))
- a causal kernel whose weights depend on the input trajectory.

(iii)

Measurability:

F_{\theta}
is Lipschitz, so the flow
\psi
and its Jacobian
\Phi_{F}
are continuous in all arguments by smooth dependence on initial conditions
[
40
]
. Composition with
C_{\theta}
and
\tilde{B}_{\theta}
preserves continuity.

(iv)

Boundedness:
By Grönwall’s inequality
[
40
]
,
\|\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq e^{L_{F}T}
where
L_{F}
is the Lipschitz constant of
F_{\theta}
. Combined with bounded
C_{\theta}
and
\tilde{B}_{\theta}
:
\|\kappa_{F}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|C_{\theta}\|\cdot e^{L_{F}T}\cdot\|\tilde{B}_{\theta}\|<\infty
.

Step 9.

Conclude.

For the affine-in-
u
case, the ITNet operator with kernel (
125
) exactly recovers the nonlinear RNN output (Step 7, Case 1). For the fully general case, arbitrary-precision approximation is guaranteed by Theorem
4
(Step 7, Case 2).

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)=\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)\quad\text{for all Lipschitz $F_{\theta}$ affine in $u$; $\varepsilon$-approximate otherwise.}}

(128)

∎

E.3.2
Proof Strategy B: Universal Approximation Argument

Proof of Theorem
E.1
(a
′
) – via UAT.

This argument does not construct the kernel explicitly but establishes existence via Theorem
4
.

Step 1.
The map
u\mapsto\mathrm{RNN}(u)
is a continuous operator from
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof of continuity:
By the Picard–Lindelöf theorem
[
17
]
, the solution
h(t)
depends continuously on the input
u
when
F_{\theta}
is Lipschitz and continuously differentiable in u. Specifically, if
\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{\infty}<\delta
, then by Grönwall’s inequality:

\|h_{1}(t)-h_{2}(t)\|_{2}\leq\frac{L_{u}}{L_{h}}\left(e^{L_{h}t}-1\right)\delta,

(129)

where
L_{h}
and
L_{u}
are the Lipschitz constants of
F_{\theta}
in its first and second arguments. Since
C_{\theta}
and
D_{\theta}
are bounded linear maps,
\|\mathrm{RNN}(u_{1})-\mathrm{RNN}(u_{2})\|_{\infty}\leq C\delta
for a constant
C
depending on
L_{h},L_{u},T,\|C_{\theta}\|,\|D_{\theta}\|
.

Step 2.
On any compact input set
U_{c}\subset C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the operator
u\mapsto\mathrm{RNN}(u)
is continuous.

Step 3.
By Theorem
4
(universal approximation), for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an ITNet operator
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
such that:

\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\|\mathrm{RNN}(u)-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u]\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon.

(130)

This does not construct the kernel explicitly but guarantees its existence. The explicit construction is provided by Strategy A above.
∎

Remark 10

(Relationship between the two strategies)
.

Strategy A (Alekseev) is stronger: it provides an
explicit
kernel construction and achieves
exact
recovery for the affine-in-
u
class. Strategy B (UAT) is more general: it applies to any continuous operator, but only guarantees
approximate
recovery and does not construct the kernel. For this paper’s unification claim, Strategy A is the primary result, with Strategy B serving as a consistency check.

Remark 11
.

The condition “
F_{\theta}
is affine in
u
” is satisfied by all standard RNN variants where the input enters through a linear projection before the nonlinearity:

•

Standard RNN:
F_{\theta}(h,u)=\phi(W_{h}h+W_{u}u+b)\Rightarrow
not affine in
u
in general (since
\phi
is applied after addition). However, the closely related form
F_{\theta}(h,u)=\phi(W_{h}h)+W_{u}u

is
affine in
u
, and both forms have similar expressiveness.

•

Linear SSM (S4):
F_{\theta}(h,u)=Ah+Bu\Rightarrow
affine in
u
(covered exactly by Part (a)).

•

Neural ODE:
F_{\theta}(h,u)=f_{\theta}(h)+g_{\theta}(u)\Rightarrow
affine in
u
when
g_{\theta}
is linear.

For LSTM, GRU, and Mamba, the input
u
enters through the gates in a multiplicative (non-affine) manner, but these are handled by the explicit unrolling constructions in Parts (b) and (d) (which bypass the Alekseev machinery entirely by working directly with the discrete recurrence).

E.4. Proof of Part (b) – Discrete-Time RNN

Proof of Theorem
E.1
(b).

Step 1.

Unroll the discrete recurrence.

The discrete RNN update (
96
) with
\phi=\mathrm{id}

(linear case for clarity; nonlinear case in Step 3) gives:

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=W_{h}h_{t-1}+W_{u}u_{t}+b

\displaystyle=W_{h}(W_{h}h_{t-2}+W_{u}u_{t-1}+b)+W_{u}u_{t}+b

\displaystyle\;\vdots

\displaystyle=W_{h}^{t}h_{0}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}W_{h}^{t-s}W_{u}u_{s}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}W_{h}^{t-s}b.

(131)

Step 2.

Express as a discrete ITNet operator.

Discretise
\Omega=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{T}\}
with measure

\mu(\{t_{k}\})=1
(unit mass per time step).
Define the causal kernel (for
h_{0}=b=0
):

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;=\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}\,W_{h}^{t-s}\,W_{u},\qquad W_{\theta}=D_{\theta}.

(132)

Then:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}W_{h}^{t-s}W_{u}u_{s}+D_{\theta}u_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}C_{\theta}W_{h}^{t-s}W_{u}u_{s}+D_{\theta}u_{t}

\displaystyle=C_{\theta}h_{t}+D_{\theta}u_{t}\;=\;\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t).

(133)

Step 3.

Handle the nonlinear discrete case.

For a nonlinear discrete RNN with activation
\phi
(e.g.
\tanh
), the hidden state at time
t
depends on the entire input history
u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}
through the recurrence
h_{s}=\phi(W_{h}h_{s-1}+W_{u}u_{s}+b)
. We can still write the output as a causal integral operator by defining the discrete nonlinear state transition:

\Phi_{\phi}^{\mathrm{disc}}(t,s;u)\;\coloneqq\;\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\phi^{\prime}\!\left(W_{h}h_{\tau-1}+W_{u}u_{\tau}+b\right)\right)\cdot W_{h}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

(134)

which is the product of Jacobians along the discrete trajectory (the discrete analogue of
\Phi_{F}(t,s;u)
from the continuous case). The ITNet kernel becomes:

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))=\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C_{\theta}\,\Phi_{\phi}^{\mathrm{disc}}(t,s;u)\cdot W_{u}.

(135)

This kernel is content-dependent (through
\phi^{\prime}
evaluated along the trajectory) and causal. The same argument as Part (a
′
), Strategy A applies, with exact recovery when
\phi
is applied element-wise (so the Jacobian is diagonal).
∎

E.5. Proof of Part (c) – Linear SSM (S4)

Proof of Theorem
E.1
(c).

Step 1.

Solve the linear SSM ODE.

The SSM state equation (
97
) is a linear ODE with
h(0)=0
.
By the variation of constants formula (same as Step 1 of Part (a)):

h(t)\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}e^{A(t-s)}\,B\,u(s)\,ds.

(136)

Step 2.

Substitute into the output equation.

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=C\,h(t)+D\,u(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\underbrace{Ce^{A(t-s)}B}_{g(t-s)}\,u(s)\,ds+D\,u(t),

(137)

where
g(\tau)=Ce^{A\tau}B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the impulse response kernel of the SSM.

Step 3.

Express as ITNet operator.

Set:

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;=\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C\,e^{A(t-s)}\,B,\qquad W_{\theta}=D.

(138)

Then:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot Ce^{A(t-s)}B\cdot u(s)\,ds+D\,u(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}Ce^{A(t-s)}B\,u(s)\,ds+D\,u(t)\;=\;y(t).

(139)

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)\;=\;y(t)\;=\;\mathrm{SSM}(u)(t).}

(140)

∎

Remark 12

.

The SSM output (

) is a

with kernel

By Theorem

(convolution proof), causal convolution is a special case of ITNet with a translation-invariant causal kernel.

E.6. Proof of Part (d) – Selective SSM (Mamba)

Proof of Theorem
E.1
(d).

Step 1.

Unroll the Mamba discrete recurrence.

Starting from the Mamba update (
101
) with
h_{0}=0
:

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=\bar{A}(t)\,h_{t-1}+\bar{B}(t)\,u_{t}

\displaystyle=\bar{A}(t)\bigl[\bar{A}(t-1)h_{t-2}+\bar{B}(t-1)u_{t-1}\bigr]+\bar{B}(t)\,u_{t}

\displaystyle\;\vdots

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left[\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}\bar{A}(\tau)\right]\bar{B}(s)\,u_{s},

(141)

where the empty product
\prod_{\tau=t+1}^{t}\bar{A}(\tau)=I_{n}

(convention for
s=t
).

Step 2.

Compute the Mamba output.

Substituting (
141
) into the output
(
102
):

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=C(u_{t})\,h_{t}

\displaystyle=C(u_{t})\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left[\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}\bar{A}(\tau)\right]\bar{B}(s)\,u_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\underbrace{C(u_{t})\left[\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}\bar{A}(\tau)\right]\bar{B}(s)}_{=:\,\kappa_{\mathrm{Mamba}}(t,s,u(t),u(s))}u_{s}.

(142)

Step 3.

Identify the ITNet kernel.

The Mamba kernel is:

\kappa_{\mathrm{Mamba}}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;\coloneqq\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C(u_{t})\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}\bar{A}(u_{\tau})\cdot\bar{B}(u_{s}),

(143)

where we write
\bar{A}(u_{\tau})=\exp(\Delta(u_{\tau})\cdot A)
and

\bar{B}(u_{s})=\Delta(u_{s})\cdot W_{B}u_{s}
.

With this kernel and
W_{\theta}=0
, the discrete ITNet operator
(sum version,
\mu(\{t_{k}\})=1
):

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{T}\kappa_{\mathrm{Mamba}}(t,s,u_{t},u_{s})\,u_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}C(u_{t})\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}\bar{A}(u_{\tau})\bar{B}(u_{s})\,u_{s}\;=\;y_{t}.

(144)

Step 4.

Verify kernel validity.

The kernel
\kappa_{\mathrm{Mamba}}
satisfies all ITNet conditions:

(i)

Causality
:
\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}
ensures
\kappa_{\theta}=0

for
s>t
.

(ii)

Measurability
:
\Delta(u_{\tau})
is the softplus of a
linear function of
u_{\tau}
(continuous),
\bar{A}(\tau)
is
the matrix exponential of a continuous function (continuous).

(iii)

Boundedness
:
\|\bar{A}(\tau)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\|e^{\Delta(\tau)A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq e^{\Delta_{\max}\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}}
where

\Delta_{\max}=\max_{\tau}\Delta(u_{\tau})<\infty

on the compact training set.
Therefore
\|\kappa_{\mathrm{Mamba}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|W_{C}\|\cdot e^{(t-s)\Delta_{\max}\|A\|}\cdot\|W_{B}\|\cdot\|u\|_{\infty}<\infty
.

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)\;=\;y_{t}\;=\;\mathrm{Mamba}(u)(t).}

(145)

∎

Remark 13

(Why Mamba is special)
.

The State Space Duality
[
22
]
showed that Mamba is equivalent to a specific form of linear attention. Our Theorem
E.1
(d) shows it is simultaneously a special case of ITNet with a causal content-dependent kernel. These two views are consistent: the ITNet framework is a strict superset of both Mamba and attention, providing the unified perspective from which both appear as special cases.

E.7. LSTM as ITNet

Proposition 7

(LSTM
\subset
ITNet)
.

The LSTM update is a special case of the ITNet operator.

Proof.

The LSTM cell state evolves as:

c_{t}\;=\;\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left[\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}f_{\tau}\right]\cdot i_{s}\odot\tilde{c}_{s},

(146)

where
f_{\tau}=\sigma(W_{f}[h_{\tau-1};u_{\tau}]+b_{f})
is the forget gate at time
\tau
and the product
\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}f_{\tau}
is the
cumulative forget factor
from time
s
to
t
.

The hidden state is
h_{t}=o_{t}\odot\tanh(c_{t})
, so:

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=W_{y}h_{t}=W_{y}\,o_{t}\odot\tanh\!\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}f_{\tau}\odot i_{s}\odot\tilde{c}_{s}\right).

(147)

Define the LSTM kernel:

\kappa_{\mathrm{LSTM}}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;\coloneqq\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot W_{y}\mathrm{diag}(o_{t})\tanh^{\prime}\!(\cdot)\mathrm{diag}\!\!\left(\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}f_{\tau}\right)\mathrm{diag}(i_{s})W_{c},

(148)

where
\tanh^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of
\tanh
(applied element-wise)
and
W_{c}
maps
u_{s}
to the cell candidate
\tilde{c}_{s}=\tanh(W_{c}u_{s})
.

The kernel (
148
) depends on
u(t)
through
o_{t}
and

f_{\tau}
(
t>\tau>s
), and on
u(s)
through
i_{s}
and
\tilde{c}_{s}
–a content-dependent causal kernel.
By the same steps as Part (d) (Mamba), the ITNet operator with this kernel recovers
y_{t}=\mathrm{LSTM}(u)(t)
.
∎

E.8. GRU as ITNet

Proposition 8

(GRU
\subset
ITNet)
.

The GRU update is a special case of the ITNet operator.

Proof.

The GRU update:

\displaystyle z_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{z}[h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b_{z})

(update gate)

\displaystyle r_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{r}[h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b_{r})

(reset gate)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{t}

\displaystyle=\tanh(W_{h}[r_{t}\odot h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b)

(candidate)

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=(1-z_{t})\odot h_{t-1}+z_{t}\odot\tilde{h}_{t}.

(hidden state)

Unrolling the hidden state:

h_{t}\;=\;\sum_{s=1}^{t}\underbrace{\left[\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}(1-z_{\tau})\right]}_{\text{retention factor}}\cdot z_{s}\odot\tilde{h}_{s}^{*},

(149)

where
\tilde{h}_{s}^{*}
denotes the candidate state after application of the reset gate at time
s

computed with the reset gate applied to the history.

The structure is identical to the LSTM case: a causal sum with
input-dependent retention factors
(1-z_{\tau})\in(0,1)
.
The ITNet kernel is:

\kappa_{\mathrm{GRU}}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;=\;\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot W_{y}\mathrm{diag}\!\!\left(\prod_{\tau=s+1}^{t}(1-z_{\tau})\right)\mathrm{diag}(z_{s})W_{\tilde{h}},

(150)

and by the same argument as Part (d), the ITNet operator with this kernel recovers the GRU output.
∎

E.9. Strictness Argument 1: Non-Causal Operators

Proof via non-causal operator.

Step 1.

Define the witness operator.

Define the
bidirectional smoothing operator
:

S(u)(t)\;\coloneqq\;\int_{0}^{T}e^{-|t-s|/\ell}\,u(s)\,ds,\qquad\ell>0.

(151)

This uses an exponential kernel over the
entire
interval
[0,T]
,
not just
[0,t]
. It uses both past (
s<t
) and future (
s>t
) information.

Step 2.

Show ITNet represents
S
.

Set:

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))\;=\;e^{-|t-s|/\ell}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d},\qquad W_{\theta}=0.

(152)

Then
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t)=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-|t-s|/\ell}u(s)\,ds=S(u)(t)
.
This kernel is symmetric in
(t,s)
, bounded, and continuous–valid for ITNet.

Step 3.

Show no causal recurrent system can represent
S
.

Any causal recurrent system satisfies:

\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)\;=\;\mathcal{F}_{t}[u(0),u(1),\ldots,u(t)],

(153)

i.e. the output at time
t
is a function of inputs up to time
t
only.

For
S
, consider two inputs:

\displaystyle u_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\text{ for all }s,

\displaystyle u_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=\delta(s-t_{*})e_{1}\quad\text{for some }t_{*}>t\text{ and }\|e_{1}\|_{2}=1.

(154)

Then:

\displaystyle S(u_{1})(t)

\displaystyle=0,

(155)

\displaystyle S(u_{2})(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-|t-t_{*}|/\ell}e_{1}\;\neq\;0\quad\text{(since }t_{*}>t\text{)}.

(156)

But for any causal system:

\displaystyle\mathrm{RNN}(u_{1})(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{t}[0,\ldots,0]=0,

(157)

\displaystyle\mathrm{RNN}(u_{2})(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{t}[0,\ldots,0]=0,

(158)

since
u_{1}(s)=u_{2}(s)=0
for all
s\leq t
(the impulse at
t_{*}>t
is in the future and unseen by the causal system).

Therefore
S(u_{2})(t)\neq 0=\mathrm{RNN}(u_{2})(t)
. Contradiction. So
S\notin\mathrm{RNN}
and
S\in\mathrm{ITNet}
, proving
\mathrm{RNN}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet}
.
∎

E.10. Strictness Argument 2: Parallelism and State Dimension

Proof via bounded state dimension.

Step 1.

Define a high-rank operator.

Define the operator:

H_{n}(u)(t)\;\coloneqq\;\int_{0}^{T}K_{n}(t,s)\,u(s)\,ds,

(159)

where
K_{n}(t,s)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\phi_{k}(t)\phi_{k}(s)^{\top}
for orthonormal basis functions
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n+1}
, so
K_{n}
has rank
n+1
in
L^{2}
.

Step 2.

Show ITNet represents
H_{n}
for any
n
.

Set
\kappa_{\theta}(t,s,u(t),u(s))=K_{n}(t,s)\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}
. Since
K_{n}
is a symmetric, bounded, continuous kernel on
[0,T]^{2}
, it satisfies all ITNet conditions. The ITNet operator with this kernel equals
H_{n}
exactly.

Step 3.

Show no fixed-
n
RNN can represent
H_{n}
exactly.

An RNN with hidden state dimension
n
computes outputs in the range of a linear map
C_{\theta}h(t)
where
h(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By the variation of constants formula (
109
), the output function
\mathrm{RNN}(u)(t)
lies in the span of at most
n
basis functions (the
n
columns of
C_{\theta}e^{At}
).

But
H_{n}(u)(t)
requires
n+1
basis functions by construction. Therefore no RNN with state dimension
\leq n
can represent
H_{n}
exactly, for any
n\geq 1
.

Since this holds for all
n
, no finite-dimensional RNN can represent the full class of operators that ITNet can.
\mathrm{RNN}_{n}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet}
for each
n
, and
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{RNN}_{n}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet}
.
∎

E.11. Discretization: Recovering Euler and ZOH

In practice, continuous SSMs are discretised before being implemented on digital hardware. We show both standard discretization methods are consistent with the ITNet framework.

Euler Discretization.

The forward Euler discretization of (
97
) with step size

\Delta t
:

h_{t+1}\;=\;(I+\Delta t\cdot A)\,h_{t}+\Delta t\cdot B\,u_{t}\;\eqqcolon\;\bar{A}_{E}\,h_{t}+\bar{B}_{E}\,u_{t}.

(160)

This is a discrete linear SSM with
\bar{A}_{E}=I+\Delta tA
and

\bar{B}_{E}=\Delta tB
.
By Part (b) (discrete RNN), this is an ITNet with kernel

\kappa_{\theta}(t,s)=\mathbf{1}_{s\leq t}\cdot C\bar{A}_{E}^{t-s}\bar{B}_{E}
.

Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) Discretization.

The ZOH discretization (used by S4 and Mamba):

\displaystyle\bar{A}

\displaystyle=e^{A\Delta t},

(161)

\displaystyle\bar{B}

\displaystyle=(e^{A\Delta t}-I)A^{-1}B.

(162)

This assumes the input is piecewise constant over each interval
[t,t+\Delta t)
. The discrete solution is:

h_{t}=\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\bar{A}^{t-1-s}\bar{B}u_{s},

(163)

which is an ITNet with kernel
C\bar{A}^{t-s}\bar{B}
–the same as the Euler case with
\bar{A}_{E}
replaced by
\bar{A}=e^{A\Delta t}
.

Remark 14
.

The ZOH kernel
C\bar{A}^{t-s}\bar{B}=Ce^{A(t-s)\Delta t}B
converges to the continuous kernel
Ce^{A(t-s)}B
as
\Delta t\to 0
(with
t,s
fixed in continuous time). This ensures that the discrete and continuous ITNet operators are consistent in the limit
\Delta t\to 0
, i.e. the ITNet framework is closed under the standard discretization operations used in practice.

Appendix F
Proof of Theorem 4: Universal Approximation

Definition 15

(Continuous nonlinear operator)
.

A
continuous nonlinear operator
is a map
F:U_{c}\to C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
where
U_{c}\subset C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is compact, and
F
is continuous with respect to the supremum norm: for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\left\|u_{1}-u_{2}\right\|_{\infty}<\delta
implies
\left\|F(u_{1})-F(u_{2})\right\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
.

Definition 16

(ITNet operator)
.

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)=\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\,u(y)\,d\mu(y)+W_{\theta}\,u(x),

(164)

where
\kappa_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the kernel (parameterised by an MLP), and
W_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

Definition 17

(MLP function class)
.

A
single hidden layer MLP
with width
w
, input dimension
p
, and non-polynomial activation
\sigma
is:

f_{\theta}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{w}c_{j}\,\sigma(a_{j}^{\top}z+b_{j}),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

(165)

where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
are learnable parameters.

Assumption 5

(Standing assumptions)
.

(i)

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is compact with
\mu(\Omega)>0
.

(ii)

F:U_{c}\to C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuous and
U_{c}
is compact.

(iii)

\sigma
is a non-polynomial continuous activation function.

F.1. Main Theorem

Theorem F.1
Universal Approximation of Continuous Operators

Under Assumption
5
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist:

•

a kernel MLP width
w_{\kappa}<\infty
,

•

a residual matrix
W_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,

•

parameters
\theta
of the kernel MLP,

such that the ITNet operator
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
satisfies:

\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|F(u)-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u]\right\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon.

(166)

That is, a
single ITNet layer
can uniformly approximate any continuous operator on any compact input set to any desired precision.

F.2. Auxiliary Lemmas

The proof requires three lemmas, which we state and prove before the main argument.

Lemma 4

(MLP universal approximation)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be compact and
g:K\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous. For any
\delta>0
, there exists a single hidden layer MLP
f_{\theta}
(Eq.
165
) with width
w
depending on
\delta
,
g
, and
K
, such that:

\sup_{z\in K}|g(z)-f_{\theta}(z)|<\delta.

(167)

Proof reference.

This is the classical Universal Approximation Theorem. Proved by
[
20
]
for sigmoid, generalized by
[
46
]
to any non-constant, bounded, continuous
\sigma
, and extended by
[
54
]
to any non-polynomial
\sigma
. The proof uses the Stone–Weierstrass theorem (for sigmoidal
\sigma
) or the Hahn–Banach theorem (for general
\sigma
): if
f_{\theta}
cannot approximate some
g
, then there exists a non-zero bounded measure
\nu
on
K
with
\int f_{\theta}\,d\nu=0
for all
f_{\theta}
; the non-polynomial property of
\sigma
forces
\nu=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 5

(
9
operator approximation)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
be compact with finite Borel measure
\mu
, and
F:U_{c}\to C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a continuous operator on a compact set
U_{c}\subset C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist:
a continuous kernel
\kappa^{*}:\Omega\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and a matrix
W^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
such that:

\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|F(u)(x)-\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{*}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\,u(y)\,d\mu(y)-W^{*}u(x)\right\|_{\infty}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(168)

Proof sketch.

This follows from
9
(Theorem 1), extended to the matrix-valued kernel setting.

Step 1.

Discretise the operator.

Since
F
is continuous on compact
U_{c}
and outputs continuous functions on compact
\Omega
,
F
is uniformly continuous. Choose
M
quadrature points
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{M}\}\subset\Omega
with weights
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{M}\}
such that for any continuous integrand
g
:

\left|\int_{\Omega}g(y)\,d\mu(y)-\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_{j}\,g(y_{j})\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{4C_{u}},

(169)

where
C_{u}=\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|u\right\|_{\infty}<\infty
(by compactness of
U_{c}
). Such
M
and
\{y_{j},w_{j}\}
exist by standard quadrature theory on compact domains
[
23
]
.

Step 2.

Approximate
F
by a finite-dimensional map.

Define the
sensor values

\xi(u)=(u(y_{1}),\ldots,u(y_{M}))\in\mathbb{R}^{Md}
, which sample
u
at the quadrature points. By the compactness of
U_{c}
and continuity of
F
, the map
\xi(u)\mapsto F(u)(x)
is continuous from
\mathbb{R}^{Md}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for each
x\in\Omega
. Moreover, this map is uniformly continuous jointly in
(x,\xi)
on the compact set
\Omega\times\xi(U_{c})
.

By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem
[
79
]
, there exists a continuous function
G^{*}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{Md}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the form:

G^{*}(x,\xi)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}K_{j}(x,\xi)\,\xi_{j}+W^{*}\xi_{0}(x),

(170)

where
K_{j}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{Md}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are continuous matrix-valued functions and
\xi_{0}(x)=u(x)
is the query-point value, such that:

\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|F(u)(x)-G^{*}(x,\xi(u))\right\|_{\infty}<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(171)

Step 3.

Convert to integral form.

Identify
G^{*}
with the quadrature approximation of an integral: define
\kappa^{*}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=K(x,y,u(x),u(y))
where
K
interpolates the discrete values
K_{j}(x,\xi)
at the quadrature points
y_{j}
. The integral
\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{*}u(y)\,d\mu(y)
approximates the sum
\sum_{j}w_{j}K_{j}u(y_{j})
by the quadrature bound (
169
). Combining the two approximation errors (
\varepsilon/4
each) gives (
168
).
∎

Lemma 6

(Kernel approximation by MLP)
.

Let
\kappa^{*}:\Omega\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be continuous, and let
U_{c}
be compact. Define the compact set:

\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y,u(x),u(y)):x,y\in\Omega,\;u\in U_{c}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2s+2d}.

(172)

For any
\delta>0
, there exists a single hidden layer MLP kernel
\kappa_{\theta}
with width
w_{\kappa}
such that:

\sup_{(x,y,a,b)\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\kappa^{*}(x,y,a,b)-\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,a,b)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\delta.

(173)

Proof.

The set
\mathcal{D}
is compact (continuous image of compact
\Omega\times\Omega\times U_{c}
under the evaluation map). Each entry
[\kappa^{*}]_{ij}
is a continuous real-valued function on the compact set
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2s+2d}
. By Lemma
4
, for each
(i,j)
there exists an MLP
f^{ij}_{\theta}
with:

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{D}}|[\kappa^{*}]_{ij}(z)-f^{ij}_{\theta}(z)|<\frac{\delta}{d}.

(174)

Assembling all
d^{2}
entries into a matrix-valued MLP
\kappa_{\theta}
:

\left\|\kappa^{*}-\kappa_{\theta}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\left\|\kappa^{*}-\kappa_{\theta}\right\|_{F}\leq d\cdot\frac{\delta}{d}=\delta,

(175)

where we used
\left\|A\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\left\|A\right\|_{F}
and
\left\|A\right\|_{F}\leq d\max_{ij}|A_{ij}|
for
d\times d
matrices.

In practice, a single MLP with
d^{2}
output units computes all entries simultaneously, sharing hidden layers. The total width is
w_{\kappa}
(shared) with
d^{2}
output heads.
∎

F.3. Main Proofs

Proof of Theorem
F.1
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
and continuous operator
F:U_{c}\to C(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Step 1.

Approximate
F
by an ideal integral operator.

By Lemma
5
, there exist a continuous kernel
\kappa^{*}:\Omega\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and matrix
W^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that:

\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|F(u)-\mathcal{K}^{*}[u]\right\|_{\infty}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

(176)

where
\mathcal{K}^{*}[u](x)=\int_{\Omega}\kappa^{*}(x,y,u(x),u(y))u(y)\,d\mu(y)+W^{*}u(x)
.

Step 2.

Approximate the ideal kernel by an MLP.

By Lemma
6
, there exists an MLP kernel
\kappa_{\theta}
with width
w_{\kappa}
such that:

\sup_{(x,y,a,b)\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\kappa^{*}(x,y,a,b)-\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,a,b)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\delta,

(177)

where
\delta>0
will be chosen in Step 3. Set
W_{\theta}=W^{*}
.

Step 3.

Bound the approximation error.

The error between the ideal and MLP-parameterised ITNet operators is:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{K}^{*}[u](x)-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u](x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\Omega}[\kappa^{*}(x,y,u(x),u(y))-\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))]\,u(y)\,d\mu(y)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\|\kappa^{*}-\kappa_{\theta}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\cdot\left\|u(y)\right\|_{2}\,d\mu(y)

(triangle inequality for Bochner integral)

\displaystyle\leq\delta\cdot C_{u}\cdot\mu(\Omega),

(178)

where
C_{u}=\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|u\right\|_{\infty}<\infty
(by compactness of
U_{c}
) and
\mu(\Omega)<\infty
(by Assumption
5
).

Choose
\delta=\varepsilon/(2C_{u}\mu(\Omega))
. Then:

\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|\mathcal{K}^{*}[u]-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\delta\cdot C_{u}\cdot\mu(\Omega)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(179)

Step 4.

Combine by triangle inequality.

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|F(u)-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u]\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|F(u)-\mathcal{K}^{*}[u]\right\|_{\infty}+\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|\mathcal{K}^{*}[u]-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u]\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.

(180)

\boxed{\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|F(u)-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u]\right\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon.}

(181)

∎

F.4. Corollary: Strict Expressiveness Ordering

Corollary F.1

(Strict expressiveness ordering)
.

Let
\mathrm{Conv}
,
\mathrm{Attn}
,
\mathrm{RNN}
,
\mathrm{ITNet}
denote the sets of operators representable by each architecture class. Then:

\mathrm{Conv}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet},\qquad\mathrm{Attn}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet},\qquad\mathrm{RNN}\subsetneq\mathrm{ITNet},

(182)

and these three subclasses are pairwise incomparable:

\mathrm{Conv}\not\subseteq\mathrm{Attn},\quad\mathrm{Attn}\not\subseteq\mathrm{Conv},\quad\mathrm{Conv}\not\subseteq\mathrm{RNN},\quad\text{etc.}

(183)

Proof.

The inclusions
\mathrm{Conv},\mathrm{Attn},\mathrm{RNN}\subseteq\mathrm{ITNet}
follow from Theorems 1–3. The strictness (proper subset) follows from the strictness parts of Theorems 1–3: each provides a witness operator in
\mathrm{ITNet}
but not in the respective subclass.

Pairwise incomparability:

•

\mathrm{Conv}\not\subseteq\mathrm{Attn}
: convolution is translation-equivariant; attention (without PE) is permutation-equivariant. A translation-equivariant but non-permutation-equivariant operator (e.g., a spatially varying filter) is in
\mathrm{Conv}
but not
\mathrm{Attn}
.

•

\mathrm{Attn}\not\subseteq\mathrm{Conv}
: attention is content-dependent; convolution is not. The softmax-weighted value aggregation from Theorem 2 is in
\mathrm{Attn}
but not in
\mathrm{Conv}
(by the linearity argument of Theorem 1(c)).

•

\mathrm{RNN}\not\subseteq\mathrm{Conv}
: causal operators with content-dependent gating (LSTM) are in
\mathrm{RNN}
but not
\mathrm{Conv}
(convolution is non-causal and content-independent).

•

\mathrm{Conv}\not\subseteq\mathrm{RNN}
: non-causal operators (bidirectional convolution) are in
\mathrm{Conv}
but not
\mathrm{RNN}
(all RNNs are causal).

∎

F.5. Quantitative Approximation Rate

The existential result (Theorem
F.1
) guarantees approximation but does not say
how large
the MLP width
w_{\kappa}
needs to be. We provide an explicit rate.

Proposition 9

(Approximation rate)
.

Under Assumption
5
, if additionally the target operator
F
has Lipschitz constant
L_{F}
on
U_{c}
(i.e.,
\left\|F(u_{1})-F(u_{2})\right\|_{\infty}\leq L_{F}\left\|u_{1}-u_{2}\right\|_{\infty}
), and the ideal kernel
\kappa^{*}
has bounded
Barron norm

[
6
]

\left\|\kappa^{*}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq B
, then the approximation error of ITNet with kernel MLP width
w_{\kappa}
and
M
quadrature points satisfies:

\sup_{u\in U_{c}}\left\|F(u)-\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\underbrace{\frac{C_{1}BC_{u}\mu(\Omega)}{\sqrt{w_{\kappa}}}}_{\text{kernel approximation error}}+\underbrace{\frac{C_{2}L_{F}C_{u}}{M^{1/s}}}_{\text{quadrature error}},

(184)

where
C_{1}
depends on the input dimension
2s+2d
and
C_{2}
depends on the smoothness of the integrand and the quadrature rule.

Proof sketch.

The first term follows from Barron’s theorem
[
6
]
: a single hidden layer MLP of width
w_{\kappa}
approximates functions with bounded Barron norm at rate
O(1/\sqrt{w_{\kappa}})
, independent of input dimension (avoiding the curse of dimensionality for this function class). Applying this to each entry of
\kappa^{*}
and combining with the error propagation bound (
178
) gives the first term.

The second term is the quadrature error: replacing the integral
\int_{\Omega}
with an
M
-point quadrature introduces error
O(M^{-r/s})
for
r
-smooth integrands on
s
-dimensional compact domains, where
r\geq 1
for continuous integrands.
∎

Appendix G
Proof of Theorem 5: Kernel Recovery Under Translation Symmetry

We provide the complete proof of Theorem
5
from the main paper, which was stated with only a proof sketch in §
2
.

Theorem 6

(Kernel Recovery Under Translation Symmetry)
.

Let the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
over input-label pairs
(u,y)
be invariant under translations: for every shift
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, define the translated signal
\tau_{\delta}u(x)\coloneqq u(x-\delta)
. Assume
\mathcal{D}
satisfies
(u,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\implies(\tau_{\delta}u,y)\sim\mathcal{D}
for all
\delta
.

Let
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(u,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u],y)]
be the population loss, where
\ell
is differentiable.

Decompose the kernel into translation-invariant and orthogonal components:

\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))=\kappa_{\theta}^{\mathrm{TI}}(x{-}y,u(x),u(y))+\kappa_{\theta}^{\perp}(x,y,u(x),u(y)),

(185)

where
\kappa_{\theta}^{\mathrm{TI}}
depends on positions only through the displacement
x-y
, and
\kappa_{\theta}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement (i.e.,
\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}^{\mathrm{TI}}\cdot\kappa_{\theta}^{\perp}\,d\mu\otimes d\mu=0
).

Then under gradient flow with respect to
\theta
:

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\kappa_{\theta}^{\perp}}\right\|_{F}=0\quad\text{at every iterate.}

(186)

Proof.

The proof follows the equivariant gradient framework of
[
30
]
. We proceed in three steps.

Step 1: Translation invariance of the loss.

For any shift
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, the ITNet operator applied to the translated signal
\tau_{\delta}u
evaluates the kernel at shifted positions:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[\tau_{\delta}u])(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x-\delta),u(y-\delta))\cdot u(y-\delta)\,d\mu(y)+W_{\theta}u(x-\delta).

(187)

Substituting
z=y-\delta
(valid when
\mu
is translation-invariant):

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}(x,z+\delta,u(x-\delta),u(z))\cdot u(z)\,d\mu(z)+W_{\theta}u(x-\delta).

(188)

Under the change of output variable
x^{\prime}=x-\delta
:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[\tau_{\delta}u])(x^{\prime}+\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\theta}(x^{\prime}+\delta,z+\delta,u(x^{\prime}),u(z))\cdot u(z)\,d\mu(z)+W_{\theta}u(x^{\prime}).

(189)

By the translation invariance of
\mathcal{D}
, the population loss satisfies
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\ell(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[\tau_{\delta}u],y)]
for all
\delta
.

Step 2: Averaging the gradient over translations.

Consider the gradient of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to the kernel parameters. Since
\mathcal{L}
is invariant under all translations
\delta
, averaging the gradient over the translation group
G=(\mathbb{R}^{s},+)
with Haar measure
[
39
,
67
]

d\delta
leaves
\mathcal{L}
unchanged:

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}\big|_{\tau_{\delta}}\,d\delta.

(190)

For the kernel component, the gradient with respect to
\kappa_{\theta}
at a specific pair
(x,y)
is:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,\cdot,\cdot)}=\mathbb{E}_{(u,y)}\left[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)}\cdot u(y)^{\top}\right].

(191)

Averaging this over all translations
\delta
(shifting both
x
and
y
by
\delta
) yields:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\kappa_{\theta}(x+\delta,y+\delta,\cdot,\cdot)}\,d\delta.

(192)

This average depends only on
x-y
(since all pairs
(x+\delta,y+\delta)
share the same displacement), so it lies entirely in the translation-invariant subspace.

Step 3: Orthogonality annihilation.

By the Schur orthogonality
[
81
]
relations for the translation group acting on the space of kernel functions, any component of the gradient that lies in the orthogonal complement of the translation-invariant subspace integrates to zero under the group average. Since the averaged gradient equals the original gradient (by Step 1), the projection of
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}
onto the
\kappa_{\theta}^{\perp}
subspace vanishes:

\mathrm{Proj}_{\kappa_{\theta}^{\perp}}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}=0.

(193)

Under gradient flow
\dot{\theta}=-\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}
, the parameters never receive a gradient signal that would move the kernel out of the translation-invariant subspace. If the kernel is initialized in (or near) the TI subspace, it remains there throughout training, recovering the convolutional special case of Theorem
C.1
.
∎

H.2.1
Positional Encoding: Random Fourier Features

The Fourier feature map
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\mathbb{R}^{2L_{f}}
is defined as:

\gamma(x)=[\sin(2\pi\mathbf{B}x);\;\cos(2\pi\mathbf{B}x)]\in\mathbb{R}^{2L_{f}},

(199)

where
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times s}
is a fixed random matrix sampled once at initialization from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
with
\sigma=10
and
L=64
.

Raw position coordinates
x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
are low-dimensional (
s=1
for text,
s=2
for images,
s=3
for point clouds).
An MLP operating on such low-dimensional inputs suffers from
spectral bias

[
98
]
: it preferentially learns low-frequency functions and struggles to represent high-frequency spatial patterns.
The Fourier feature map lifts positions to a
2L_{f}
-dimensional space where high-frequency components are explicitly represented as linear features, enabling the MLP to learn sharp spatial functions
[
87
]
.

The bandwidth
\sigma
controls the frequency range:
\sigma=10
corresponds to spatial frequencies up to
\sim
10 cycles per unit length, which captures both coarse object-level structure and fine-grained local texture on normalized domains.
Table
23
shows that
\sigma=1
under-resolves spatial structure (
-1.3\%
),
\sigma=100
introduces excessively high-frequency features that are difficult for the kernel MLP to use (
-0.6\%
), and
\sigma=10
is optimal.
The number of frequencies
L=64
provides a 128-dimensional positional representation per coordinate group; performance saturates beyond
L=64
(Table
23
).

We sample
\mathbf{B}
once from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
and freeze it throughout training.
Learning
\mathbf{B}
would make the Fourier features input-dependent, coupling position encoding with feature learning and complicating the theoretical analysis (Theorem
5
assumes fixed positional encoding).
The kernel MLP is already a universal approximator over the lifted positional space, so learnable
\mathbf{B}
provides no additional expressiveness in theory; empirically, we found no improvement from learning
\mathbf{B}
(
\pm 0.0\%
on ImageNet-1K).

H.3. Initialization Scheme

Proper initialization is critical for training stability, especially because the ITNet operator is more complex than self-attention (matrix-valued kernel vs. scalar attention weights).
Our initialization ensures two properties: (i) the initial operator is approximately the identity, so that an
L
-layer ITNet behaves like a shallow network at the start of training; and (ii) the initial kernel output has small norm, so that the integral term does not dominate the residual.

H.3.1
Kernel MLP Initialization

•

Input-to-hidden weights
W_{1}
:

Sampled from
\mathcal{N}(0,0.02^{2})
.
The small variance ensures the hidden activations
h^{(1)}=\mathrm{GELU}(W_{1}z+b_{1})
are in the approximately linear regime of GELU at initialization (GELU
(x)\approx 0.5x
for
|x|\ll 1
), providing well-conditioned gradients.

•

Hidden bias
b_{1}
:
Initialised to zero.

•

Hidden-to-output weights
W_{2}
:

Initialised as
W_{2}=\epsilon\cdot\tilde{W}_{2}
where
\tilde{W}_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/w_{\kappa})
and
\epsilon=10^{-3}
.
This ensures the initial kernel output
\mathrm{vec}(K_{xy})=W_{2}h^{(1)}+b_{2}
has small norm:
\|\mathrm{vec}(K_{xy})\|_{2}\approx\epsilon\sqrt{d_{h}^{2}}=10^{-3}\times 64=0.064
, so
\|K_{xy}\|_{F}\approx 0.064
.

•

Output bias
b_{2}
:

Initialised so that the initial kernel is approximately
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{I}_{d_{h}}
. Specifically,
[b_{2}]_{k}=1/n
if
k
corresponds to a diagonal entry of the
d_{h}\times d_{h}
reshaped output, and
0
otherwise.
This makes the initial operator output approximately
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}\mathbf{I}_{d}\cdot u(x_{j})+W_{\theta}u(x_{i})=\bar{u}+u(x_{i})
: the global mean plus the identity - a simple pooling-plus-skip operation.

This follows the
zero-init
principle used in GPT-2
[
76
]
and formalised as LayerScale: initialising each layer’s output near zero ensures the residual stream
u^{(\ell)}\approx u^{(\ell-1)}
at the start of training, so that an
L
-layer network initially behaves as a 1-layer network.
This prevents the signal-to-noise ratio from degrading across layers and is essential for training models with
L\geq 12
without careful learning rate warmup.

H.4. Regularization and Training Stability

We apply dropout
[
82
]
at three locations:
(i) after the ITNet operator output (before the residual addition), with rate
p=0.1
for ITNet-S/B and
p=0.0
for ITNet-PC;
(ii) after the FFN hidden layer (inside the FFN), with the same rate;
(iii) after the embedding layer, with rate
p=0.1
.
No dropout is applied inside the kernel MLP, as the kernel evaluation is already regularised by the
\epsilon
-scaling of
W_{2}
.
Following
47
, we apply stochastic depth with linearly increasing drop probability from
0
at the first layer to
p_{\mathrm{drop}}
at the last layer:

p^{(\ell)}=\ell\cdot p_{\mathrm{drop}}/L
.
We use
p_{\mathrm{drop}}=0.1
for ITNet-S,
0.2
for ITNet-B, and
0.3
for ITNet-L.
This is standard in ViT training
[
90
]
and essential for training models with
L\geq 24
.
We clip the global gradient norm to
1.0
in all experiments, using
torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_
.
This prevents gradient explosion when the kernel MLP produces large outputs early in training (before
W_{2}
converges to small values).
All experiments use
bfloat16
for forward and backward computation with
float32
master weights and optimizer states
[
66
]
.
The kernel MLP evaluation, matrix-vector product
K_{pq}\cdot u(x_{j})
, and gradient accumulation are all performed in
bfloat16
.
Layer normalization and the softmax (when used for baseline comparisons) use
float32
for numerical stability.
For ImageNet-1K experiments, we maintain an Exponential moving average (EMA) of the model weights with decay
\beta_{\mathrm{EMA}}=0.9999
, following
[
70
]
. The EMA model is used for evaluation.

We use AdamW
[
63
]
with decoupled weight decay applied to all weight matrices but
not
to biases, layer normalization parameters, the Fourier feature matrix
\mathbf{B}
(which is frozen), or the kernel MLP output bias
b_{2}
(which encodes the
1/n\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}
initialization and should not be pulled toward zero).

All experiments use AdamW
[
63
]
with:

•

\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.999
,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{Adam}}=10^{-8}

•

Cosine learning rate schedule with linear warmup

•

Learning rate and weight decay per task specified in Section

We chose AdamW over SGD with momentum because: (i) the kernel MLP parameters have different gradient magnitudes from the FFN and embedding parameters (the kernel MLP sees

H.6. Statistical Reporting

For all ITNet models (ITNet-S, ITNet-B, ITNet-L), we report results as mean
\pm
standard deviation over
N=3
independent runs with different random initializations. (see Tables
1
,
2
,
4
, and
4
in the main paper).
Each run differs in random weight initialisation and stochastic training effects (e.g., data shuffling and mini-batch sampling), while all other training settings are kept fixed.

Let
\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote the evaluation metric (e.g., accuracy or F1) from
N=3
runs. The reported mean and standard deviation are computed as:

\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i},\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(m_{i}-\mu)^{2}}.

(200)

We report
\mu\pm\sigma
in all tables.
The standard deviation reflects variability due to random initialization and stochastic optimization. For the considered benchmarks, the observed variance is small relative to performance differences between models, indicating stable training behavior.
We report standard deviation (not standard error) and do not assume a specific distribution beyond empirical estimation from repeated runs.

H.8. IO Complexity of Tiled ITNet

Proposition 10

(IO Complexity - Full Statement)
.

Let
\ell_{\kappa}
denote the number of MLP layers in
\kappa_{\theta}
and
w_{\kappa}
the hidden width. Let
B_{q}
and
B_{k}
be the query and key tile sizes satisfying the SRAM constraint
(B_{q}+B_{k})\cdot d\leq S_{\mathrm{SRAM}}
. The tiled forward pass (Algorithm
1
) requires:

\Theta\!\left(\frac{n^{2}d}{B_{k}}\right)\;\text{HBM reads}\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta\!\left(\frac{n^{2}w_{\kappa}\ell_{\kappa}}{S_{\mathrm{SRAM}}}\right)\;\text{FLOPs}.

(201)

Proof.

Each query tile
U_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{B_{q}\times d}
is loaded once per outer iteration:
n/B_{q}
outer iterations
\times B_{q}d
elements
=nd
reads for all queries. Each key tile
U_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{B_{k}\times d}
is loaded once per inner iteration for each outer tile:
(n/B_{q})(n/B_{k})\times B_{k}d=n^{2}d/B_{q}
key reads in total. The total HBM reads are
nd+n^{2}d/B_{q}=\Theta(n^{2}d/B_{q})
for large
n
.

For FLOPs: each pair
(p,q)
in a tile requires one MLP forward pass costing
O(w_{\kappa}\ell_{\kappa})
multiply-adds, plus one
d\times d
matrix-vector product costing
O(d^{2})
. There are
n^{2}
total pairs, giving
O(n^{2}(w_{\kappa}\ell_{\kappa}+d^{2}))
total FLOPs. Since
w_{\kappa}\ell_{\kappa}
is typically comparable to
d^{2}
(e.g.,
w_{\kappa}=128
,
\ell_{\kappa}=2
gives
w_{\kappa}\ell_{\kappa}=256
vs.
d^{2}=768^{2}
for the matrix-vector product), the matrix-vector product dominates, and total FLOPs are
O(n^{2}d^{2})
.
∎

H.10. Tiled Backward Pass with Gradient Checkpointing

The forward pass (Algorithm
1
) never materialises the full
n\times n\times d\times d
kernel matrix: only a
B_{q}^{*}\times B_{k}^{*}
tile of kernel evaluations resides in SRAM at any time.
A naïve backward pass would require access to all
n^{2}
kernel values
K_{ij}=\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{j},u(x_{i}),u(y_{j}))
to compute the gradients, which would either require storing the full kernel matrix during the forward pass (
O(n^{2}d^{2})
memory) or recomputing the entire kernel from scratch.
Following the gradient checkpointing strategy of
[
10
]
- also employed by FlashAttention
[
21
]
for the attention matrix - we adopt a
tiled recomputation
approach: during the backward pass, we iterate over the same tile structure as the forward pass, recompute the kernel MLP
\kappa_{\theta}
for each tile on-the-fly, and immediately use the recomputed values to accumulate gradients before discarding them.

Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the scalar loss.
The forward pass computes, for each query position
x_{i}
:

O_{i}=(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\cdot\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{j},u_{i},u_{j})\cdot u_{j}+W_{\theta}\,u_{i},

(202)

where
\omega_{j}
is the quadrature weight for position
y_{j}
and we write
u_{i}=u(x_{i})
,
u_{j}=u(y_{j})
for brevity.
The backward pass must compute three quantities:

(i)

\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial u_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
(gradient with respect to input features, for backpropagation to earlier layers),

(ii)

\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta
(gradient with respect to kernel MLP parameters, for weight updates), and

(iii)

\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_{\theta}
(gradient with respect to the residual matrix).

Applying the chain rule to Eq. (
202
), we obtain:

(i) Gradient with respect to kernel evaluations.

Define
K_{ij}=\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{j},u_{i},u_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
From Eq. (
202
):

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial K_{ij}}=\omega_{j}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial O_{i}}\cdot u_{j}^{\top}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

(203)

where
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial O_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the upstream gradient at query position
i
(received from the subsequent layer).

(ii) Gradient with respect to input features.

Each input feature
u_{i}
participates in Eq. (
202
) in three ways: (a) as a query feature (through the kernel’s dependence on
u(x_{i})
), (b) as a key feature (through the kernel’s dependence on
u(y_{j})
when
j=i
), and (c) through the residual term
W_{\theta}u_{i}
.
Combining all contributions:

(iii) Gradient with respect to kernel MLP parameters.

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{\theta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial O_{i}}\cdot u_{i}^{\top}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

H.11. Monte Carlo Variance Analysis

Proposition 11

(Variance of Importance-Weighted Estimator - Full Proof)
.

For a discrete domain with empirical measure
\mu(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{j}}
, the importance-weighted Monte Carlo estimator

(\widehat{\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}[u])(x_{i})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{m},u(x_{i}),u(y_{m}))\cdot u(y_{m})}{p_{\theta}(y_{m}\mid x_{i})}+W_{\theta}u(x_{i}),\qquad y_{m}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{i})

(207)

satisfies
\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}[u])(x_{i})]=(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x_{i})
(unbiased) and has variance

\mathrm{Var}[(\widehat{\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}[u])(x)]=\frac{1}{M}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{\|\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\cdot u(y)\|_{2}^{2}}{p_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\,d\mu(y)-\|(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)-W_{\theta}u(x)\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(208)

The optimal proposal that minimizes this variance is

p_{\theta}^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{\|\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\cdot u(y)\|_{2}}{\int_{\Omega}\|\kappa_{\theta}(x,z,u(x),u(z))\cdot u(z)\|_{2}\,d\mu(z)},

(209)

achieving minimal variance

\mathrm{Var}^{*}[(\widehat{\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}[u])(x)]=\frac{1}{M}\left[\left(\int_{\Omega}\|\kappa_{\theta}\cdot u(y)\|_{2}\,d\mu(y)\right)^{2}-\|(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)-W_{\theta}u(x)\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(210)

In practice, we enforce
p_{\theta}(y|x)>0
everywhere via
p_{\theta}(y|x)=(1-\varepsilon)p_{\theta}^{\text{(MLP)}}(y|x)+\varepsilon/n
with
\varepsilon=0.01
.

Proof.

Define
g(y)=\kappa_{\theta}(x,y,u(x),u(y))\cdot u(y)
and
I=\int_{\Omega}g(y)\,d\mu(y)=(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x)-W_{\theta}u(x)
.
Each sample
y_{m}\sim p_{\theta}
contributes the importance-weighted estimator
\hat{g}_{m}=g(y_{m})/p_{\theta}(y_{m}\mid x)
, which satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{y_{m}\sim p_{\theta}}[\hat{g}_{m}]=\int_{\Omega}g(y)\,d\mu(y)=I
by the definition of importance sampling.

The variance of the average of
M
i.i.d. samples is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{g}_{m}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\mathrm{Var}[\hat{g}_{1}]

(211)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{g}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}]-\|I\|_{2}^{2}\right)

(212)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{\|g(y)\|_{2}^{2}}{p_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\,d\mu(y)-\|I\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(213)

Adding
W_{\theta}u(x)
(deterministic) does not affect variance, establishing (
208
).

To find the optimal
p_{\theta}^{*}
, we minimize
\int\|g(y)\|_{2}^{2}/p(y)\,d\mu(y)
subject to
\int p(y)\,d\mu(y)=1
and
p(y)\geq 0
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
[
78
]
(or Lagrange multipliers):

\int\frac{\|g(y)\|_{2}^{2}}{p(y)}d\mu(y)\cdot\int p(y)d\mu(y)\geq\left(\int\|g(y)\|_{2}d\mu(y)\right)^{2},

with equality iff
p(y)\propto\|g(y)\|_{2}
. Thus the minimum is achieved when
p^{*}(y)=\|g(y)\|_{2}/\int\|g(z)\|_{2}d\mu(z)
, yielding

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\|g(y)\|_{2}^{2}}{p^{*}(y)}d\mu(y)=\left(\int_{\Omega}\|g(y)\|_{2}\,d\mu(y)\right)^{2}.

Substituting back gives (
210
). The support condition
p_{\theta}(y|x)>0
is enforced via the
\varepsilon
-mixture described above.
∎

H.12. Low-Rank Approximation Error Bound

Proposition 12

(Nuclear Norm Bound on Low-Rank Error)
.

Let
\kappa_{\theta}
be the full-rank kernel and
\kappa_{\theta}^{(r)}=\Phi_{\theta}^{\top}\Psi_{\theta}
the rank-
r
factorization. For any finite evaluation set
\{(x_{i},y_{j})\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
, define the kernel matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{nd\times nd}
with blocks
K_{ij}=\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{j},u(x_{i}),u(y_{j}))\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Then the best rank-
r
approximation satisfies:

\|K-K^{(r)}\|_{F}\leq\frac{\|K\|_{*}}{\sqrt{r}},

(214)

where
\|K\|_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{nd}\sigma_{i}(K)
is the nuclear norm and
\sigma_{i}
are the singular values.

Proof.

By the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem
[
45
]
, the best rank-
r
approximation in Frobenius norm is
K^{(r)}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{\top}
, and
\|K-K^{(r)}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=r+1}^{\min(n,d)}\sigma_{i}^{2}
.

By Cauchy–Schwarz
[
78
]
applied to the tail singular values:

\sum_{i=r+1}^{nd}\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=r+1}^{nd}\sigma_{i}\right)^{2}/(nd-r).

(215)

A tighter bound uses the relationship between nuclear and Frobenius norms. Since
\|K\|_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{nd}\sigma_{i}
and the tail sum
\sum_{i=r+1}^{nd}\sigma_{i}\leq\|K\|_{*}
, we have:

\|K-K^{(r)}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=r+1}^{nd}\sigma_{i}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\max_{i>r}\sigma_{i}\cdot\sum_{i>r}\sigma_{i}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\|K\|_{*}^{2}}{r}},

(216)

where the last inequality uses
\sigma_{r+1}\leq\|K\|_{*}/r
(since the first
r
singular values each contribute at least
\sigma_{r+1}
to the nuclear norm).
∎

Remark 15
.

In practice, we observe that the kernel matrices for trained ITNet models have rapidly decaying singular values. On ImageNet-1K features,
r=32
captures
>99\%
of the Frobenius norm, and
r=64
captures
>99.9\%
, consistent with the
<0.1\%
accuracy gap.

Appendix I
Backpropagation for the ITNet Operator

We derive the complete gradient computation for the ITNet operator in discrete form, using the notation established in Appendix
A
throughout.
This section serves two purposes: (i) to provide a self-contained reference for implementors, and (ii) to make explicit how gradients flow through the content-dependent kernel - a non-standard computation that does not arise in convolution (where the kernel is content-independent) or standard attention (where the kernel has a specific softmax structure).
We then show how the general gradient specialises to each classical architecture (CNN, Transformer, RNN) under the kernel restrictions of Theorems
C.1
–
E.1
, providing a unified view of backpropagation across all three families.

I.1. Setup and Notation

Let the domain
\Omega
contain
n
discrete positions
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
.
Let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the input signal, with values
u(x_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at each position.
Positions are collected as
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
.
Let
\kappa_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denote the learnable kernel (parameterised by
\theta
),
W_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
the residual matrix, and
\mu
the measure on
\Omega
with weights
\omega_{j}\coloneqq\mu(\{x_{j}\})
(typically
\omega_{j}=1/n
for uniform measure).

We write
u_{i}\coloneqq u(x_{i})
and
u_{j}\coloneqq u(x_{j})
as shorthand when the context is clear.

I.2. Forward Pass

The discrete ITNet operator
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
computes, for each query position
x_{i}
:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\cdot\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},x_{j},u(x_{i}),u(x_{j}))\cdot u(x_{j})+W_{\theta}\,u(x_{i}).

(217)

We define the following intermediate quantities:

\displaystyle z_{ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq[\gamma(x_{i});\,\gamma(x_{j});\,\gamma(x_{i}{-}x_{j});\,\|x_{i}{-}x_{j}\|_{2};\,u(x_{i});\,u(x_{j});\,u(x_{i})\odot u(x_{j})]\in\mathbb{R}^{6L_{f}+1+3d},

(218)

\displaystyle K_{ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},x_{j},u(x_{i}),u(x_{j}))=\mathrm{reshape}_{d\times d}\!\bigl(\mathrm{MLP}_{\theta}(z_{ij})\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

(219)

\displaystyle M_{ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\omega_{j}\cdot K_{ij}\cdot u(x_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(220)

where
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\mathbb{R}^{2L_{f}}
is the random Fourier feature map (Eq. (
199
)) and
\odot
denotes the Hadamard (elementwise) product.
The quantity
M_{ij}
is the
message
from key position
x_{j}
to query position
x_{i}
: the kernel matrix
K_{ij}
transforms the key feature
u(x_{j})
, weighted by the measure
\omega_{j}
.

The forward pass is then:

\boxed{(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{ij}+W_{\theta}\,u(x_{i}).}

(221)

Remark 16

(Comparison with attention forward)
.

In standard self-attention (§
D
), the message is
M_{ij}^{\mathrm{attn}}=\alpha(x_{i},x_{j})\cdot W_{V}u(x_{j})
, where
\alpha(x_{i},x_{j})\in\mathbb{R}
is a scalar softmax weight and
W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a
fixed
value projection.
In ITNet,
M_{ij}=\omega_{j}\cdot K_{ij}\cdot u(x_{j})
, where
K_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a
pair-dependent
matrix.
The transformation applied to
u(x_{j})
varies in a
d^{2}
-dimensional space for each pair
(i,j)
, versus 1-dimensional for attention (
\alpha_{ij}
only scales the fixed
W_{V}u(x_{j})
).

I.3. Upstream Gradients

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the scalar loss.
The upstream gradient from the subsequent layer is:

g_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x_{i})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

(222)

The backward pass must compute:

(i)

\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial u(x_{j})
for all
j
(input feature gradients, for backpropagation to earlier layers),

(ii)

\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta
(kernel MLP parameter gradients, for weight updates),

(iii)

\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_{\theta}
(residual matrix gradient).

I.4. Gradient with Respect to Kernel Outputs

From
M_{ij}=\omega_{j}\cdot K_{ij}\cdot u(x_{j})
and
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x_{i})=\sum_{j}M_{ij}+W_{\theta}u(x_{i})
, the gradient of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to each message is
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial M_{ij}=g_{i}
.
Applying the chain rule for the matrix-vector product
M_{ij}=\omega_{j}K_{ij}u(x_{j})
:

\boxed{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial K_{ij}}=\omega_{j}\cdot g_{i}\,u(x_{j})^{\top}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.}

(223)

Derivation.

The
(a,b)
entry of
K_{ij}
contributes to
M_{ij}
as:

[M_{ij}]_{a}=\omega_{j}\sum_{b=1}^{d}[K_{ij}]_{ab}[u(x_{j})]_{b}
.
Therefore:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial[K_{ij}]_{ab}}=\omega_{j}\cdot[g_{i}]_{a}\cdot[u(x_{j})]_{b}
,
which in matrix form gives Eq. (
223
).
∎

I.5. Gradient with Respect to Input Features

Each input feature
u(x_{j})
participates in Eq. (
217
) in three distinct roles:

(a)

Value role:

u(x_{j})
appears directly in
M_{ij}=\omega_{j}K_{ij}u(x_{j})
as the vector being transformed by the kernel.
This contributes to
every
output
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(b)

Key-side kernel input:

u(x_{j})
appears inside
K_{ij}=\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},x_{j},u(x_{i}),u(x_{j}))
through the kernel MLP’s dependence on the key feature.
This also contributes to every output.

(c)

Query-side kernel input and residual:
When
j
acts as the query position,
u(x_{j})
appears in the residual
W_{\theta}u(x_{j})
and inside
K_{jk}=\kappa_{\theta}(x_{j},x_{k},u(x_{j}),u(x_{k}))
as the query feature for all keys
k
.

Combining all contributions:

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u(x_{j})}&=\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\cdot K_{ij}^{\top}\,g_{i}}_{\text{(a) value role}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\cdot\left.\frac{\partial K_{ij}}{\partial u(x_{j})}\right|_{\mathrm{key}}^{\!\top}\mathrm{vec}(g_{i}\,u(x_{j})^{\top})}_{\text{(b) key-side kernel gradient}}\\
&+\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega_{k}\cdot\left.\frac{\partial K_{jk}}{\partial u(x_{j})}\right|_{\mathrm{query}}^{\!\top}\mathrm{vec}(g_{j}\,u(x_{k})^{\top})}_{\text{(c) query-side kernel gradient}}+\underbrace{W_{\theta}^{\top}\,g_{j}}_{\text{(d) residual}}.\end{aligned}}

(224)

Term (a): Value role.

Holding
K_{ij}
fixed:

\text{Term (a)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\cdot K_{ij}^{\top}\,g_{i}.

(225)

Term (b): Key-side kernel gradient.

The kernel
K_{ij}
depends on
u(x_{j})
through the MLP input
z_{ij}
.
Specifically,
u(x_{j})
enters

directly as the key feature and through the Hadamard product

The Jacobian of the MLP input with respect to the key feature is:

\frac{\partial z_{ij}}{\partial u(x_{j})}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{2L_{f}\times d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{2L_{f}\times d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{2L_{f}\times d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}\\
\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
\mathrm{diag}(u(x_{i}))\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(6L_{f}+1+3d)\times d},

(226)

where the two non-zero blocks are
\mathbf{I}_{d}
(from
\partial u(x_{j})/\partial u(x_{j})
) and
\mathrm{diag}(u(x_{i}))
(from
\partial(u(x_{i})\odot u(x_{j}))/\partial u(x_{j})

By the chain rule:

\text{Term (b)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\mathrm{vec}(K_{ij})}{\partial z_{ij}}\cdot\frac{\partial z_{ij}}{\partial u(x_{j})}\right)^{\top}\mathrm{vec}(g_{i}\,u(x_{j})^{\top}).

(227)

Term (c): Query-side kernel gradient.

When position

acts as a query,
u(x_{j})
enters
K_{jk}
through the query feature slot:

\frac{\partial z_{jk}}{\partial u(x_{j})}\bigg|_{\mathrm{query}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{2L_{f}\times d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{2L_{f}\times d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{2L_{f}\times d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times d}\\
\mathbf{I}_{d}\\
\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}\\
\mathrm{diag}(u(x_{k}))\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(6L_{f}+1+3d)\times d}.

(228)

The contribution is:

\text{Term (c)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega_{k}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\mathrm{vec}(K_{jk})}{\partial z_{jk}}\cdot\frac{\partial z_{jk}}{\partial u(x_{j})}\right)^{\top}\mathrm{vec}(g_{j}\,u(x_{k})^{\top}).

(229)

Term (d): Residual.

\text{Term (d)}=W_{\theta}^{\top}\,g_{j}.

Remark 17

(Why Terms (b) and (c) are unique to ITNet)
.

In a standard Transformer, the gradient through
\alpha(x_{i},x_{j})
with respect to

involves the softmax Jacobian composed with the linear projection
W_{K}

In ITNet, the analogous gradient involves the

This is more expensive per pair but provides a richer gradient signal, enabling the kernel to learn content-dependent

In convolution, Terms (b) and (c) vanish entirely because the kernel is content-independent.

The kernel MLP parameters

(i,j)
pairs:

\boxed{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\omega_{j}\cdot u(x_{j})\,g_{i}^{\top}\cdot\frac{\partial K_{ij}}{\partial\theta}\right).}

(230)

Let the kernel MLP have
\ell_{\kappa}
layers with GELU activation
\sigma
:

\displaystyle h^{(0)}

\displaystyle=z_{ij},

(231)

\displaystyle h^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\sigma\!\bigl(W_{l}\,h^{(l-1)}+b_{l}\bigr),\qquad l=1,\ldots,\ell_{\kappa}-1,

(232)

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}(K_{ij})

\displaystyle=W_{\ell_{\kappa}}\,h^{(\ell_{\kappa}-1)}+b_{\ell_{\kappa}},

(233)

with
W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{w_{\kappa}\times(6L_{f}+1+3d)}
,
W_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{w_{\kappa}\times w_{\kappa}}
for interior layers, and
W_{\ell_{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}\times w_{\kappa}}
.

The GELU derivative is:

\sigma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{GELU}}(x)=\Phi(x)+x\,\phi(x),\qquad\Phi(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}[1+\mathrm{erf}(x/\sqrt{2})],\quad\phi(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}.

(234)

Define the output-layer error signal:

\delta^{(\ell_{\kappa})}=\omega_{j}\cdot\mathrm{vec}(g_{i}\,u(x_{j})^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}.

(235)

Backpropagate through interior layers:

\delta^{(l)}=\bigl(W_{l+1}^{\top}\,\delta^{(l+1)}\bigr)\odot\sigma^{\prime}\!\bigl(W_{l}h^{(l-1)}+b_{l}\bigr),\qquad l=\ell_{\kappa}-1,\ldots,1.

(236)

The parameter gradients for a single pair
(i,j)
are:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{l}}\bigg|_{(i,j)}=\delta^{(l)}\bigl(h^{(l-1)}\bigr)^{\top},\qquad\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial b_{l}}\bigg|_{(i,j)}=\delta^{(l)}.

(237)

Accumulated over all
n^{2}
pairs:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{l}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta^{(l)}_{ij}\bigl(h^{(l-1)}_{ij}\bigr)^{\top},\qquad\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial b_{l}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta^{(l)}_{ij}.

(238)

I.7. Gradient with Respect to Residual Matrix

\boxed{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{\theta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\,u(x_{i})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.}

(239)

This is identical to the gradient of a standard linear layer and requires no kernel-specific computation.

I.8. Special Case 1: Convolution (Theorem
C.1
)

Under the convolutional kernel
\kappa_{\theta}(x_{i},x_{j},u(x_{i}),u(x_{j}))=w(x_{i}-x_{j})\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}
(Theorem
C.1
), the kernel is a scalar function of displacement multiplied by the identity matrix.
The forward pass reduces to
(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(x_{i})=(w\ast u)(x_{i})+W_{\theta}u(x_{i})
.

Gradient with respect to the scalar filter value.
Since
K_{ij}=w(x_{i}-x_{j})\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}
is constrained to scalar multiples of
\mathbf{I}_{d}
, only the trace component of
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial K_{ij}
is free.
Defining
w_{ij}\coloneqq w(x_{i}-x_{j})
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{ij}}=\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial K_{ij}}\right)=\omega_{j}\cdot g_{i}^{\top}u(x_{j})=\omega_{j}\cdot\langle g_{i},\,u(x_{j})\rangle.

(240)

This is the standard convolution filter gradient: the cross-correlation between the upstream gradient and the input.
For a
k\times k
filter with coefficients
\{f_{m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{N}}
, summing over all pairs with displacement
m
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f_{m}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\cdot g_{i}^{\top}u(x_{i}-mh),\qquad m\in\mathcal{N},

(241)

which is exactly the computation performed by
torch.nn.Conv2d
’s backward pass.

Gradient with respect to
u(x_{j})
.
Since the convolutional kernel is content-independent, Terms (b) and (c) in Eq. (
224
) vanish:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u(x_{j})}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{j}\cdot w(x_{i}-x_{j})\cdot g_{i}+W_{\theta}^{\top}g_{j}=\omega_{j}\cdot(w^{\ast}\ast g)(x_{j})+W_{\theta}^{\top}g_{j},

(242)

where
w^{\ast}(x)\coloneqq w(-x)
is the flipped filter.
This is the standard transposed convolution used in CNN backward passes.

The convolution special case eliminates
n^{2}
MLP backward passes entirely.
Backward cost reduces from
O(n^{2}d^{2}+n^{2}w_{\kappa}^{2}\ell_{\kappa})
to
O(n|\mathcal{N}|d)
, recovering the known linear-in-
n
cost.

I.9. Special Case 2: Self-Attention (Theorem
D.1
)

Under the attention kernel
K_{ij}=\alpha(x_{i},x_{j})\cdot W_{V}
, where
\alpha(x_{i},x_{j})=\exp(Q(x_{i})^{\top}K(x_{j})/\sqrt{d_{k}})/Z(x_{i})
,
Q(x_{i})=W_{Q}u(x_{i})
,
K(x_{j})=W_{K}u(x_{j})
.

Since
K_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}W_{V}
with
W_{V}
fixed, the gradient projects onto the scalar
\alpha_{ij}
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha_{ij}}=\omega_{j}\cdot g_{i}^{\top}W_{V}u(x_{j}).

(243)

The softmax Jacobian relates
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\alpha_{ij}
to the logit gradient
e_{ij}\coloneqq Q(x_{i})^{\top}K(x_{j})/\sqrt{d_{k}}
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial e_{ij}}=\alpha_{ij}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha_{ij}}-\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{il}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha_{il}}\right).

(244)

Gradient with respect to
u(x_{j})
.
Mapping to ITNet’s general gradient:

Term (a) [value]:

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i}\omega_{j}\cdot\alpha_{ij}\cdot W_{V}^{\top}g_{i},

(245)

Term (b) [key]:

\displaystyle\quad W_{K}^{\top}\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial e_{ij}}\cdot\frac{Q(x_{i})}{\sqrt{d_{k}}},

(246)

Term (c) [query]:

\displaystyle\quad W_{Q}^{\top}\sum_{k}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial e_{jk}}\cdot\frac{K(x_{k})}{\sqrt{d_{k}}},

(247)

Term (d):

\displaystyle\quad W_{\theta}^{\top}g_{j}\quad(=0\text{ since }W_{\theta}=0\text{ in attention}).

(248)

This recovers the standard Transformer backward pass:
\nabla_{V}\mathcal{L}=\alpha^{\top}G
,
\nabla_{K}\mathcal{L}=(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial E)^{\top}Q/\sqrt{d_{k}}
,
\nabla_{Q}\mathcal{L}=(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial E)K/\sqrt{d_{k}}
.

The attention case replaces the MLP Jacobian with the softmax Jacobian (
\mathrm{diag}(\alpha)-\alpha\alpha^{\top}
, rank-1 corrected,
O(n)
per row).
No kernel MLP parameter gradient exists.
Backward cost:
O(n^{2}d)
.

I.10. Special Case 3: Linear SSM / S4 (Theorem
E.1
(c))

Under the SSM kernel
K_{ij}=\mathbf{1}_{j\leq i}\cdot Ce^{A(t_{i}-t_{j})}B
(content-independent, causal), the forward pass is:

(\mathcal{K}_{\theta}[u])(t_{i})=C\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{i}\omega_{j}e^{A(t_{i}-t_{j})}B\,u(t_{j})}_{h(t_{i})}+D\,u(t_{i}).

(249)

Gradient with respect to
u(t_{j})
.
Since
K_{ij}
is content-independent, Terms (b) and (c) vanish.
Defining
g_{i}^{h}\coloneqq C^{\top}g_{i}
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u(t_{j})}=B^{\top}\sum_{i=j}^{n}\omega_{j}\cdot e^{A^{\top}(t_{i}-t_{j})}g_{i}^{h}+D^{\top}g_{j}.

(250)

This is
backpropagation through time
(BPTT): the gradient at time
t_{j}
sums future gradients propagated backward through the transpose state-transition matrix
e^{A^{\top}\Delta t}
.
If
\|e^{A\Delta t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}<1
(stable dynamics), gradients decay exponentially - the vanishing gradient problem.

Gradient with respect to SSM parameters:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\,h(t_{i})^{\top},

(251)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\omega_{j}\cdot e^{A^{\top}(t_{i}-t_{j})}C^{\top}g_{i}\,u(t_{j})^{\top},

(252)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\omega_{j}(t_{i}-t_{j})\cdot e^{A^{\top}(t_{i}-t_{j})}C^{\top}g_{i}\,u(t_{j})^{\top}B^{\top}.

(253)

I.11. Special Case 4: LSTM (Theorem
E.1
(b))

The LSTM kernel is content-dependent:
K_{ij}
depends on
u(t_{j})
through the gates
f_{\tau},i_{s},o_{t}
(Appendix
E
).
Terms (b) and (c) are therefore
non-zero
and account for gradient flow through gate activations.

The cell-state gradient satisfies the classical LSTM BPTT relation:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial c_{t-1}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial c_{t}}\odot f_{t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial h_{t}}\odot o_{t}\odot\mathrm{sech}^{2}(c_{t})\odot f_{t},

(254)

where the forget gate
f_{t}\in(0,1)^{d}
controls gradient flow through the cell state: when
f_{t}\approx 1
, the gradient passes nearly unchanged; when
f_{t}\approx 0
, the gradient is blocked.

In ITNet’s framework, this gating is encoded in the content-dependent kernel:
K_{ij}
contains
\mathrm{diag}(\prod_{\tau=j+1}^{i}f_{\tau})
, and the gradient through this product is exactly Eq. (
254
).
The gate gradients are:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial c_{t}}\odot c_{t-1}\odot\sigma^{\prime}(W_{f}[h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b_{f}),

(255)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial i_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial c_{t}}\odot\tilde{c}_{t}\odot\sigma^{\prime}(W_{i}[h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b_{i}),

(256)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial o_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial h_{t}}\odot\tanh(c_{t})\odot\sigma^{\prime}(W_{o}[h_{t-1};u_{t}]+b_{o}),

(257)

where
\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))
is the sigmoid derivative.
These correspond to Terms (b) and (c) of the general ITNet gradient, specialized to the block-matrix LSTM kernel.

I.12. Special Case 5: Mamba (Theorem
E.1
(d))

The Mamba kernel
K_{ij}=\mathbf{1}_{j\leq i}\cdot C(u(t_{i}))\prod_{\tau=j+1}^{i}\bar{A}(u(t_{\tau}))\cdot\bar{B}(u(t_{j}))
is content-dependent through all three components
C,\bar{A},\bar{B}
.

All three contribute non-zero gradients via Terms (b) and (c):

Through
\bar{B}(u(t_{j}))
:

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\partial K_{ij}}{\partial u(t_{j})}\bigg|_{\bar{B}}=C(u_{i})\prod_{\tau}\bar{A}(u_{\tau})\cdot\frac{\partial(\Delta(u_{j})W_{B}u_{j})}{\partial u(t_{j})},

(258)

Through
C(u(t_{i}))
:

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\partial K_{ji}}{\partial u(t_{i})}\bigg|_{C}=W_{C}^{\top}\cdot\prod_{\tau}\bar{A}(u_{\tau})\cdot\bar{B}(u_{j}),

(259)

Through
\bar{A}(u(t_{\tau}))
:

\displaystyle\quad\text{chain rule through }\prod_{\tau}\exp(\Delta(u_{\tau})A).

(260)

The gradient through the
\bar{A}
product (Eq. (

This is the content-dependent generalisation of the linear SSM gradient (where
\bar{A}
is constant and the product becomes
\bar{A}^{i-j}
).

Appendix J
Extended Encoder Details

J.1. Graph Encoder

Graphs
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with node features
\{h_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{c}
are a natural fit for ITNet: the set of nodes is an irregular domain, and edges define a sparse connectivity structure that need not be hard-coded into the kernel.
Node features are linearly embedded:

u^{(0)}(v)=W_{\mathrm{gph}}\,h_{v}+b_{\mathrm{gph}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad v\in\mathcal{V}.

(261)

Since graphs may lack Euclidean coordinates, we generate structural positions from the first
s=8
eigenvectors of the normalized graph Laplacian
\Delta=I-D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}

[
29
]
:

x_{v}=[\phi_{1}(v);\,\phi_{2}(v);\,\ldots;\,\phi_{s}(v)]\in\mathbb{R}^{s},

(262)

where
\phi_{i}
is the
i
-th Laplacian eigenvector. These Laplacian positional encodings (LPE) capture graph topology - nodes that are structurally similar receive similar position vectors - and are fed into the kernel via
\gamma(x_{v})
.

Optionally, the kernel can be restricted to connected pairs:

\kappa_{\theta}^{\mathrm{graph}}(x_{v},x_{u},u_{v},u_{u})=(\mathbf{1}_{(v,u)\in\mathcal{E}}+\lambda)\cdot\kappa_{\theta}(x_{v},x_{u},u_{v},u_{u}),

(263)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a learned global parameter controlling non-edge long-range integration. At
\lambda=0
, ITNet reduces to graph convolution over the edge set. At
\lambda>0
, it allows global message passing beyond graph topology.

Domain specification:

\Omega_{\mathrm{gph}}=\mathcal{V}
,
d\mu=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\delta_{v}
,
s=8
.

J.2. Multi-Scale Image Encoder

For dense prediction tasks (detection, segmentation), we apply the image encoder at two scales:
P\in\{8,16\}
, yielding
N_{p}^{(s)}\in\{784,196\}
patches per scale for a
224\times 224
image. The two sets of positions are concatenated and processed jointly by the ITNet layers:

\Omega_{\mathrm{multi}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{img}}^{P=8}\cup\Omega_{\mathrm{img}}^{P=16},\qquad|\Omega_{\mathrm{multi}}|=784+196=980.

(264)

This exploits the kernel’s ability to integrate across irregular, mixed-resolution domains - an operation that standard convolution or self-attention must handle with explicit multi-scale fusion modules
[
61
]
.

J.3. Design Principles

All encoders (image, text, point cloud, graph, multimodal) share three structural design principles:

Principle 1: Positions are raw coordinates.

The positional argument to the kernel is the raw domain coordinate (pixel grid for images, scalar index for text, 3-D coordinate for point clouds, Laplacian eigenvector for graphs). We do not apply learnable positional embeddings before passing positions to the kernel; instead, the kernel MLP learns its own position-sensitive functions. The key advantage is that the kernel can compute arbitrary functions of position, including relative positions, distances, and angles, without these being baked into the feature space.

Principle 2: Features are modality-agnostic after encoding.

After the modality-specific linear projection, all features lie in the same
\mathbb{R}^{d}
space. The ITNet layers, the kernel MLP, and the task-specific decoder treat features identically regardless of origin modality. This enables multimodal processing without cross-modal adapter modules. We emphasise that the modality-specific encoders are modality-specific design choices; our claim is that the
core operator
is shared and identical across all modalities.

Principle 3: The measure encodes modality prior.

The measure
\mu
determines how each position contributes to integration. By adjusting
\mu
(e.g., the balanced multimodal measure), we encode prior beliefs about relative importance without modifying the kernel. This separation of concerns - measure for importance, kernel for content-dependent weighting - is a property of the integral operator formalism that has no direct analogue in attention or convolution.

Appendix N
Broader Impact

ITNet is a general-purpose neural architecture. Like all such architectures, it may be applied to beneficial purposes (medical imaging, scientific discovery, accessibility tools) as well as potentially harmful ones (surveillance, deepfakes, autonomous weapons). The unification of CNNs, Transformers, and RNNs into a single operator does not introduce new risks beyond those already posed by existing architectures; rather, it provides a more principled mathematical framework for understanding existing capabilities.
We note two potential positive impacts specific to ITNet:
(i) by demonstrating that a single architecture can handle multiple modalities, ITNet may reduce the engineering complexity and energy cost of deploying multiple separate models for different data types; and
(ii) by providing formal proofs that classical architectures are special cases of a single operator, ITNet contributes to the theoretical understanding of deep learning, which may aid in developing more interpretable and reliable systems.
