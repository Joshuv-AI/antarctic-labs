Time to Personalize Surveillance for Cancer Therapy-Related Cardiac Dysfunction in HER2-Targeted Therapy: One Size Does Not Fit All
Authors: Sara Ebrahimi, Lalith Vardhan Namburu, Anita Deswal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of ReviewCancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) remains a significant risk of contemporary cancer treatment. Despite advances in oncologic therapies, cardiac surveillance strategies have largely relied on uniform, intensive monitoring, often without consideration of individual cardiotoxicity risk. This review evaluates the rationale for transitioning from blanket surveillance to risk-based, personalized cardiac monitoring strategies, especially for HER2-targeted therapy.Recent FindingsEvolving definitions of CTRCD and improved risk stratification tools, have highlighted substantial heterogeneity in cardiotoxicity risk. Prospective studies in low-risk patients receiving non-anthracycline HER2-targeted therapies demonstrate that reduced-frequency echocardiographic surveillance appears safe and does not compromise cardiovascular or oncologic outcomes. Similar paradigms need to be studied for other cardiotoxic therapies, including BRAF/MEK inhibitors and VEGF inhibitors, with a need for prospective validation before clinical application.SummaryRisk-adapted cardiac surveillance offers a pragmatic, evidence-based approach to optimize resource utilization while maintaining patient safety. Future research should focus on prospective validation and guideline harmonization to enable personalized cardio-oncology care.Keywords: Cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction, Cardiotoxicity, Cardiac monitoring, Cardio-oncology, Surveillance

Introduction

Recent advancements in systemic cancer therapy have improved cancer outcomes, but the therapies can often be associated with adverse cardiovascular (CV) effects [1–3]. Cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD), a major concern for old and new cancer therapeutics, refers to a spectrum of new or worsening symptomatic or asymptomatic left ventricular (LV) dysfunction associated with cancer therapies. Although LV systolic dysfunction and heart failure (HF) were first described in the context of anthracyclines, they are now recognized in association with other chemotherapies, targeted therapies, immunotherapies, as well as radiation therapy (RT) [4, 5]. Incidence rates of CTRCD vary widely across clinical trials and registries due to heterogeneous definitions used, cancer type, populations studied, and specific agents used [1, 4]. As our understanding of CTRCD has evolved, it has become clear that the incidence of CTRCD is driven not only by specific cancer therapies, but also by individual patient risk factors such as advanced age, traditional CV risk factors such as hypertension, smoking, and diabetes mellitus, prior history of potential cardio-toxic therapy, and potentially, genetic risk factors. This underlines the importance of baseline CV risk stratification to not only guide the choice of agent, if possible, but also to determine the strategy of monitoring for cardiac dysfunction [4, 5]. Evolving preventive strategies, including less frequent use of cardiotoxic agents such as anthracyclines for certain cancers, shorter durations of cardiotoxic therapy, potential use of cardioprotective medications, and regular cardiac monitoring and surveillance have contributed to a reduction in CTRCD rates [6, 7]. However, intensity of cardiac monitoring for CTRCD is a matter of active debate. Current societal guidelines and regulatory recommendations generally suggest intensive surveillance for all patients receiving high-risk therapies such as human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2)-targeted agents with frequent cardiac function assessment, irrespective of underlying patient risk. This may result in overutilization of monitoring in patients at low risk, contributing to increased logistic and financial burden and potentially to some unnecessary interruptions of cancer therapy, while shifting resources away from higher-risk patients who may benefit more from close monitoring [8–10]. This raises questions about whether a “one size fits all” approach is appropriate, or whether personalized monitoring based on individual patient risk may be more effective and cost-efficient [8, 9]. In this review, we suggest moving beyond this “blanket” approach towards a risk-based, individualized approach using HER2-targeted therapy as the example. After an overview of CTRCD with HER2-targeted therapy, current guideline recommendations based on historical data, we review risk stratification tools and emerging data on reduced‐intensity monitoring in selected populations. We finally suggest that emerging studies could guide changes in cardiac function surveillance for other therapeutic classes as well in the future.

Traditional and Contemporary Definitions of Cancer Therapy-Related Cardiac Dysfunction

The term CTRCD usually refers to LV cardiac systolic dysfunction attributable to cancer therapy, typically measured by a decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), but more recently extended to more sensitive abnormalities in myocardial systolic deformation (strain) and elevated cardiac biomarkers, with or without overt clinical HF [11], as consensus definitions have evolved in parallel with advancing imaging and biomarker technologies [12]. Table 1 highlights the heterogeneity in historical and contemporary CTRCD definitions across representative studies and guidelines. In this manuscript, we focus on CTRCD defined by changes in LVEF (with or without HF) and do not include CTRCD defined by strain imaging. At present, changes in cancer therapy are usually not recommended just based on changes in strain or biomarkers. The initiation of cardioprotective therapy based on changes in GLS only has been suggested, but not universally adopted given the absence of data regarding benefit on clinical outcomes [13–15].

Key Cancer Therapies Associated With CTRCD

Several cancer therapeutics are associated with asymptomatic or symptomatic CTRCD. However, the stronger and some of the oldest associations include the association with anthracyclines, HER2-targeted therapy, and RT [11, 16]. In addition, several newer therapies including targeted therapies such as vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitors (VEGFi), a variety of small-molecule kinase inhibitors, and proteasome inhibitors have been variably associated with CTRCD and HF [11, 17]. The reported incidence of CTRCD varies widely across studies, depending on cancer type, therapeutic agents, patient population, definitions of CTRCD, and the frequency and timing of cardiac surveillance imaging. Amongst all cardiotoxic agents, the overall incidence with anthracyclines remains the most common given their frequent use, with a dose-dependent relationship [18, 19]. For example, in a large prospective study of 2,625 patients, the overall incidence of anthracycline-related cardiotoxicity was 9%, with 98% of cases occurring within the first year, mostly asymptomatic at first [16].

For the purposes of this review, we will focus on details of CTRCD associated with HER2-targeted therapy and on the potential for personalization of monitoring strategies for this class of cancer therapeutics, most commonly employed in HER2-positive breast cancer (HER2BC), although also used in other HER2-positive cancers such as gastric cancers. Approximately 20% of breast cancers have gene amplification or overexpression (or both) of the HER2, a tyrosine kinase transmembrane receptor, associated with a more aggressive phenotype and poorer prognosis [20, 21]. HER2-targeted therapies such as trastuzumab and pertuzumab have markedly improved outcomes in HER2BC. They are both humanized monoclonal antibodies that target different epitopes on the HER2 receptor providing complementary mechanisms of action. The HER2 receptor (ErbB2), expressed on cardiomyocytes, contributes to myocardial stress adaptation via the neuregulin-1/ErbB signaling pathway [22]. HER2 inhibition disrupts this cardioprotective mechanism, resulting typically in non–dose-related cardiotoxicity, a significant proportion of which is partially or completely reversible LV dysfunction, especially if detected early [22, 23]. CTRCD seen with HER2-targeted therapy is dependent not only on its use but is also increased with additional factors such as anthracycline exposure, mediastinal RT and other patients related risk factors [5, 23].

Incidence of CTRCD With HER2-Targeted Therapy and Historical Perspective for Frequent Cardiac Function Surveillance

When trastuzumab was initially studied in the metastatic HER2BC trial in the late 1990’s, incident symptomatic cardiotoxicity was unexpectedly high (Table 2). Asymptomatic or symptomatic cardiac dysfunction occurred in 27% of patients receiving anthracycline, cyclophosphamide, and trastuzumab, compared with 8% in those receiving anthracycline and cyclophosphamide alone. The incidence of severe New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III–IV HF was 16% in the combination group versus 3% in the anthracycline–cyclophosphamide group [24]. This initially led to significant regulatory restrictions and the implementation of comprehensive cardiac safety monitoring protocols in subsequent trials. LVEF monitoring at baseline and the arbitrarily designated 3-month intervals during trastuzumab treatment was recommended when the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) first approved the drug in 1998 along with exclusion of patients with significant CV history and implementation of strict criteria for interruption/discontinuation and/or continuation of trastuzumab on the basis of the LVEF results [25]. The initially high cardiotoxicity rate was driven by the concomitant use of trastuzumab with anthracyclines. Even though subsequent trials, with trastuzumab use being temporally separated from anthracycline exposure, and with rigorous surveillance for cardiac dysfunction, demonstrated lower rates of cardiotoxicity, the recommendations of the US Food and Drug Administration in 2006, when approving trastuzumab for adjuvant use, remain unchanged with suggested LVEF assessment at baseline and every 3 months during therapy for all patients treated with this agent, and every 6 months for at least 2 years following completion of therapy [25, 26].

A listing of observed incidence of CTRCD and clinical HF from pivotal trials and recent observational studies is presented in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. Across large adjuvant trials of trastuzumab, cardiac dysfunction has been reported in 7–34% of patients, while significant HF has been reported in 0–4% (Table 2). However, on closer look, the two trials reporting the higher range of CTRCD of 34% and 18.6%, used less stringent criteria for CTRCD such as any decrease of LVEF of at least 10% and yielded a higher incidence of CTRCD in controls, as well. When CTRCD was defined as a drop of LVEF of 10–15% to < lower limit of normal (commonly 50%), incidence of CTRCD was much less common, usually in < 10% of patients, with only one trial reporting an incidence of 13.6%. Of note, extending trastuzumab therapy from 12 to 24 months roughly doubled the rate of LV dysfunction [27], suggesting that patients with metastatic HER2BC who receive longer durations of HER2 therapy may also have higher rates of CTRCD. Although patients with established CV disease may be excluded from trials, review of recent observational studies, using the conservative criteria of at least 10% LVEF decline to < 50% have demonstrated CTRCD of 3.2% to 15.2% with only one single center study reporting a higher incidence of 23% (Table 3).

For other anti-HER2 agents including pertuzumab, lapatinib, and the antibody drug conjugates- trastuzumab-emtansine (T-DM1) and trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd), the reported incidence of CTRCD has been even lower (Tables 2 and 3). A pooled analysis of trials that evaluated T-DM1 and T-DXd, reported LVEF decline rates of 0.9% and 4.2% with T-DM1 and T-DXd, respectively. Of note, initial trials of the antibody drug conjugates included patients previously treated with other anti-HER2 therapies while requiring an LVEF > 50% for enrollment; thus they may have excluded those that had prior CTRCD [35].

Risk Factors Associated With CTRCD Related to HER2 Therapy

Identification of patients at increased risk for CTRCD is critical yet often challenging, as susceptibility varies widely across individuals. The 2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines emphasize that CV risk is dynamic and not static, i.e. new exposures or comorbidities after initiation of therapy may change the risk profile [10]. Patient-related factors increasing the risk for CTRCD largely mirror traditional CV risk determinants and across studies, they have included advanced age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking, pre-existing cardiac disease including established coronary artery disease and especially, LV dysfunction (even low normal LVEF in 50-<55% range) or HF [5, 11, 36, 37]. Black race has been associated with an increased risk in some studies [38]. Prior anthracycline exposure and mediastinal RT involving the heart also confer a higher risk of cardiotoxicity with HER2 therapy [10, 36, 39]. RT, particularly to the chest wall or left breast, may contribute more to late-onset dysfunction, whereas early-onset cardiac dysfunction is more often seen with anthracycline exposure prior to HER2 therapy [38]. For example, in a recent observational study of 496 patients with HER2BC treated with trastuzumab (30% of whom also had anthracycline exposure), the overall CTRCD incidence was 8.7% with symptomatic HF in only 1.6% of patients, but CTRCD rates rose sharply with elevated baseline risk, from 3.6% in low-risk to 32.1% in high or very high-risk groups; HF occurred in up to 11% of high-risk patients [37]. Moreover, baseline elevations of CV biomarkers such as high-sensitivity troponin T, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), even before exposure to cardiotoxic therapies, have been associated with higher all-cause mortality, reflecting underlying subclinical myocardial stress or injury [5, 11].

Current Recommendations for CTRCD Monitoring in Patients Receiving HER2-Targeted Therapy

Current guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO; 2016), European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO; 2020), and the 2022 ESC guideline on cardio-oncology in collaboration with the International Cardio-Oncology Society (ICOS), recommend a comprehensive CV evaluation before starting potentially cardiotoxic therapy which includes baseline clinical risk stratification and echocardiography, with some even recommending baseline cardiac biomarker measurement [10, 40, 41]. Beyond baseline assessment, these societies and FDA labeling propose monitoring protocols for early detection, prevention of progression, and optimization of cancer and cardiac outcomes through timely intervention (Table 4) [10, 26, 40–45]. As detailed in Table 4, the majority recommend close surveillance of LVEF every 3 months during HER2 therapy, and some recommend surveillance during follow up after completion of therapy. Although the 2022 ESC cardio-oncology guidelines recommend echocardiographic monitoring universally for all patients every 3 months during therapy and within 12 months after completion, in the neoadjuvant or adjuvant setting (Class I, Level B), they do raise weaker suggestions for some prolongation of monitoring intervals in selected patients. For example, in low-risk, asymptomatic patients with normal cardiac findings at 3 months, monitoring interval could potentially be extended to every 4 months (Class IIb, Level C). In the metastatic setting, they recommend echocardiography every 3 months during the first year, followed by every 6 months if the patient remains stable and free of cardiotoxicity (Class I, Level C) [10].

Risk Stratification for CTRCD and Clinical Implications

As detailed above, the risk for CTRCD in patients receiving HER2-targeted therapy is not uniform but is dependent on a number of baseline patient demographics and clinical factors as well as prior cancer therapies. Therefore, several studies have tried to identify risk factors and create models for risk stratification of these patients in order to guide monitoring for CTRCD with more precision. A few recent examples are discussed below.

The Heart Failure Association–International Cardio-Oncology Society (HFA-ICOS) Risk Stratification tool

The HFA-ICOS risk stratification tool for CTRCD in patients with BC receiving HER2-targeted therapy [46], which was developed based on literature review and expert opinion without initial derivation from a dataset, has since been evaluated in several studies [47]. The tool incorporates patient-related and therapy-related risk factors, categorizing patients into low, medium, high, and very high-risk groups (Fig. 1). In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, the C-statistic of the externally validated studies for this tool was modest and ranged between 0.56 and 0.67 [48–51]. The pooled C static was observed to be 0.60 (95% CI 0.52 to 0.68) with moderate heterogeneity and the tool was found to consistently underestimate the risk with observed event rates exceeding the predicted risk, especially if the mild forms of CTRCD (i.e. change in LV global longitudinal strain only) were included, although all included studies were not specific to HER2-based therapies and included other anti-cancer therapies [47]. Specifically, in a study of 507 patients with HER2BC, Cronin et al. examined the performance of the HFA-ICOS tool and found an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.643 with 97.9% specificity but only 26.1% sensitivity for CTRCD. The negative predictive value in the low-risk category was high, with only 3.3% of low-risk patients developing cardiotoxicity at 5 years versus 38% in the very-high-risk group [50]. In another contemporary cohort of 496 patients with BC that received HER2-targeted therapy, this risk tool had a modest AUC of 0.71 (95% CI 0.63–0.78) for the prediction of CTRCD defined only by a significant decline in LVEF of at least 10% to an absolute value less than 53% [37]. Again, the most utility was in identifying the low-risk categories. Patients in the low-risk category who did not receive prior anthracyclines had a 1.7% incidence of CTRCD without any incident symptomatic HF; those that received prior anthracyclines but were overall in the low-risk category had an incident CTRCD and HF rate of 5% and 3.2%, respectively. Further, those in the high or very high-risk category had a high incidence of CTRCD of 32.1% and HF of 11%. The negative predictive value of the tool was 96.4% in low versus moderate- or high-risk patients. Thus, a key utility of the HFA-ICOS tool may be to identify those at low risk, especially amongst those that have not received prior anthracyclines, in order to de-escalate frequency of LVEF monitoring during and after therapy in this group of patients. In addition, those at high-risk or very high-risk groups could be candidates for risk factor optimization at baseline and for close monitoring during therapy as well as to be studied in trials of preventive cardio-protection strategies.

Nomogram to Predict Risk of Cancer Therapy

Yu et al. developed a 9-variable nomogram to predict the 1-year risk of CTRCD in women with HER2BC receiving trastuzumab-based therapy [52]. The variables included were age, body mass index, race, current or prior anthracycline exposure, baseline systolic blood pressure, LVEF, coronary artery disease, arrythmia, and diabetes mellitus. The internally validated nomogram demonstrated modest discrimination (concordance index 0.687) and accurate calibration, with predicted versus observed CTRCD probabilities differing by < 3%. Based on total risk score quartiles, the 1-year incidence of CTRCD ranged from 3.2% in the lowest-risk group to 23.8% in the highest-risk group, underscoring its clinical utility for guiding surveillance intensity and cardioprotective strategies. However, the nomogram is limited by its retrospective and single-center design, lack of external validation, and small numbers of patients with baseline CV disease, factors that may restrict generalizability to broader or modern HER2-targeted therapy populations. Although the absence of contemporary biomarkers or strain imaging may be regarded as limitations, interruptions of HER2 therapy based on only reduction in strain parameters without change in symptoms or LVEF are not advocated at this time.

Other Models and Machine Learning Approaches in Patients Treated With Breast Cancer Therapy

The CHEMO-RADIAT score, developed in a multicenter Korean cohort of patients with BC (not exclusive to HER2 therapy), predicts clinical major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) after therapy for BC, including HF as a component endpoint. It incorporates conventional CV risk factors and treatment-related exposures, with time-dependent C-indices of 0.876 at 3 years and 0.842 at 7 years in the validation cohort. While externally validated, its generalizability outside Asian populations requires further studies [53]. Recent advances include artificial intelligence and machine learning models, such as the multilayer perceptron model developed by Chang et al., which achieved an AUC of 0.66 for CTRCD and 0.81 for HF with reduced EF in validation cohorts. These models show promise but are limited by single-center design, short follow-up, and need for further external validation [54].

Most recently, Barac et al. (2025) developed and internally validated a model to predict 10-year risk of incident cardiomyopathy or HF (outcomes identified by ICD codes) in a large number of patients with any BC, but not specific to HER2BC only [55]. This longitudinal cohort study included 26,044 racially and ethnically diverse women with newly diagnosed invasive BC in a hospital system of southern California. The model demonstrated significance for CV risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obesity, smoking status, prior CV conditions as well as BC therapy with anthracyclines or HER2-targeted therapy, and advanced age. A key advantage of this model is that it did not require biomarkers, imaging data, greatly improving its practicality in everyday clinical settings to predict the risk of HF/Cardiomyopathy over a 10-year period categorized into low, moderate, and high-risk groups. The overall C-index was 0.75 and 0.76 for derivation and validation cohorts respectively. Again, the model helped differentiate the low-risk and high-risk groups well with observed 10-year HF/cardiomyopathy risk of 1.7% (95% CI 1.1%−2.4%) and 19.4% (95% CI 17.3–21.5%), respectively. Rigorous external validation of this model in independent populations outside of an insured population in a single health system is still needed [55]. Further, calculation of the risk category using the hazard ratios obtained from the model may be more complex to integrate rapidly into everyday clinical practice.

Integration of Risk Stratification Into Clinical Practice

As summarized above, in addition to key prior cardiotoxic cancer therapy exposure, the risk models mostly use age and CV risk factors or established CV disease as risk markers. Differences in CV risk factors or disease at baseline likely contribute to differences in CTRCD rates across cohorts as well as to the performance of risk models. Further, the status of CV factor optimization at baseline before HER2 therapy, which may affect rates of CTRCD, is not well described in the studies, and may change risk prediction for the medium- to high-risk groups. Despite the limitations, there can be significant clinical implications of risk stratification for CTRCD associated with HER2-targeted therapy. They provide a framework for individualized risk assessment at the time of initiation and continuation of HER2 therapy, allowing clinicians to stratify patients according to their predicted risk of CTRCD/HF and to tailor the intensity and duration of cardiac surveillance accordingly [47, 55]. For example, patients identified as low risk by these models (no prior anthracycline exposure, normal baseline LVEF ≥ 55%, and absence of major CV risk factors) may safely undergo less frequent monitoring, such as baseline and 6-monthly echocardiographic surveillance, rather than the standard 3-monthly intervals [9, 37], and may not need post-HER2 therapy surveillance. This may be even more timely, given the overall reduction in standard utilization of anthracyclines in patients with HER2BC [56], thus increasing the pool of low-risk patients who may benefit from such a strategy. Among such low-risk patients, frequent monitoring that is currently recommended may lead to unnecessary financial and logistical challenges for both patients and health systems and may even delay cancer therapy. Interruptions for milder reductions in LVEF, which do not meet criteria for CTRCD, could also contribute to patient anxiety and cause unnecessary treatment disruption, which have been associated with worse disease-free and overall survival. Lastly, for more efficient utilization of available imaging resources, targeting those at a moderate to high risk for CTRCD with routinely recommended timely imaging follow-up may be a more appropriate cost-effective strategy. In addition, designation as moderate or high risk should be a trigger for more intensive CV risk optimization which may help reduce rates of CTRCD.

Prospective Evidence for Less Intensive Monitoring

Given the possibility in identifying patients at low baseline risk of incident CTRCD with HER2 therapy, the safety of less frequent cardiac monitoring during HER2-targeted therapy has been rigorously evaluated in a couple of prospective studies [8, 9]. Recently, Yu et al. reported on a prospective, non-randomized clinical trial that enrolled 190 patients with HER2BC treated exclusively with non-anthracycline HER2-targeted regimens [9]. Major exclusion criteria were previous exposure to anthracyclines, the presence of significant CV disease, and poorly controlled hypertension, thereby selecting a study population with a low baseline risk for CTRCD. Cardiac surveillance was performed every 6 months using echocardiography. During a median follow-up of 17.5 months, the study reported no clinical cardiac events (0%; 1-sided 97.5% CI: 0%−1.9%). Only one patient (0.5%) developed asymptomatic CTRCD, which led to a temporary interruption of HER2 therapy followed by treatment resumption without further issues. Adherence to the 6-monthly surveillance schedule was high, with 73.2% of patients following the protocol in an intention-to-treat analysis and 79.9% in a per-protocol analysis [9]. The trial affirmed that CTRCD surveillance every 6 months was feasible and safe in patients at low risk for HER2 therapy-associated CTRCD. A prior multicenter randomized non-inferiority trial by Dent et al. compared 3-monthly versus 4-monthly cardiac monitoring in 200 patients with early-stage HER2BC receiving trastuzumab-based therapy [8]. Patients with normal baseline LVEF (> 53%) were assigned to the 3 monthly versus 4 monthly LVEF surveillance schedule; outcomes including mean change in LVEF, rate of cardiac dysfunction, treatment delays, and cardiology referrals, were similar between groups. The mean difference in LVEF change (− 0.94%, one-sided 95% lower bound − 2.14) met the predefined non-inferiority margin, and cardiac dysfunction rates were comparable (16.3% versus 12.4%). The study concluded that 4-monthly monitoring was non-inferior to 3-monthly monitoring and may be adopted as standard practice to reduce cost and burden without compromising safety. Limitations include the modest sample size, conduct at only 3 Canadian centers, and limited use of contemporary HER2 therapies [8]. These findings challenge the historical rationale for intensive surveillance in all patients, which was based on early trials with higher cardiotoxicity risk due to anthracycline use [10]. The results of these 2 modest sized trials provide support toward individualized, risk-adapted surveillance strategies, which may optimize resource utilization and reduce patient burden without compromising safety in low-risk patients receiving HER2-targeted therapy for BC in the absence of anthracycline exposure. Future directions: Extending Risk-Based Surveillance to Other Cardiotoxic Cancer Therapies

The evidence supporting risk-stratified, reduced frequency CTRCD surveillance in low-risk patients receiving HER2-targeted therapy may provide a roadmap for extending this paradigm across a broader landscape of cardiotoxic cancer therapies [8, 9, 37]. For example, BRAF (Rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma B-type) and MEK (mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase) inhibitors, used primarily in combination to treat BRAF-mutant melanoma and other solid tumors, are now increasingly recognized for their potential LV systolic dysfunction ranged from 2 to 12% in clinical trials exploring their safety profile [57]. Given this, a baseline echocardiogram is recommended across all risk groups, followed by echocardiography every 4 months in high/very high-risk groups per 2022 ESC cardio-oncology guideline [10], or after one month of therapy, then every 2–3 months thereafter based on trametinib (MEK inhibitor) FDA labeling and prior practice [58–60]. Recent prospective data from Glen et al. demonstrated that 46% of melanoma patients treated with BRAF and MEK inhibitors developed CTRCD, however, 86% were mild (based only on GLS reduction rather than LVEF). All moderate or worse events appeared within the first 4 weeks and showed at least partial reversibility [59]. Notably, none of the patients with low baseline cardiotoxicity risk experienced moderate or severe cardiotoxicity defined by significant reductions in LVEF and/or symptomatic HF [59]. These findings support an opportunity to study risk-stratified cardiac surveillance, potentially with early monitoring in all risk groups within first 4 weeks, followed by more intensive monitoring only in those with moderate to high baseline risk and potentially reduced cardiac surveillance in low-risk patients treated with this combination. Similarly, there is a need to refine risk-based surveillance strategies in patients treated with VEGFi [10]. Again, the echocardiographic surveillance recommendations for VEGFi therapy are mainly derived from expert consensus and experience of other cardiotoxic therapies, rather than prospective validation studies. Thus, the path forward requires rigorous prospective validation of risk-stratified surveillance protocols across these diverse therapeutic classes. Given the mechanistic heterogeneity across various cancer therapies, including distinct pathways of cardiotoxicity, differing risk profiles, and variable reversibility, surveillance protocols must be tailored to the specific therapeutic context. The discussion presented in context of HER2 therapy may not necessarily be directly applicable to BRAF/MEK inhibitors and VEGFi, other than to provide a basis for exploration of risk-based monitoring strategies for these agents. Key priorities include: (1) external validation of emerging risk prediction models in independent, diverse patient populations; (2) prospective studies examining risk-adapted surveillance strategies, preferably using clinically meaningful endpoints such as CV events and cost-effectiveness rather than solely imaging-derived metrics and (3) harmonization of surveillance recommendations based on evolving evidence across guidelines to reduce practice variation and optimize resource allocation. Just as cancer therapy is increasingly tailored to individual tumor biology and patient characteristics, CV monitoring strategies must similarly evolve to match surveillance intensity to individual risk, maximizing early detection in vulnerable populations while minimizing burden, cost and implications of repeated testing in those at lower risk.

Conclusions

CTRCD remains a clinically meaningful and increasingly prevalent complication of modern cancer therapy. While historical experience with anthracyclines and early HER2-targeted therapies appropriately justified intensive and uniform cardiac surveillance, accumulating contemporary evidence demonstrates that the risk of clinically significant cardiotoxicity is highly heterogeneous and strongly influenced by patient characteristics and baseline CV risk, as well as prior or concomitant cancer treatment exposures. Risk stratification tools such as the HFA-ICOS framework, alongside emerging predictive models, provide a rational foundation for tailoring surveillance intensity to individual risk rather than applying a blanket approach to all patients. Prospective data in low-risk populations receiving non-anthracycline HER2-targeted regimens support the safety of reduced-frequency monitoring without compromising oncologic or CV outcomes, challenging long-standing paradigms of routine 3-monthly imaging. Notably, these findings are largely derived from a few relatively smaller prospective studies. Therefore, the broader use of less frequent surveillance in only the low-risk patients receiving HER2-targeted therapy especially in the absence of anthracycline exposure, which deviates from the high frequency surveillance in all patients advised by the 2022 ESC cardio-oncology guidelines, will require continued clinical safety evaluations built into such implementation projects. Ultimately, advancing from universal to personalized CV surveillance represents a necessary evolution in cardio-oncology, aligning cardiac care with the principles of precision oncology to optimize outcomes for cancer patients across the treatment continuum.
