Abstract
We establish Hoeffding-type concentration inequalities for the low and high tail bounds of sums of exchangeable random variables. Our results exhibit an anti-symmetry in such tail bounds due to the assumption of exchangeability, a generalization of the i.i.d. setting. In contrast to the existing literature on this problem, our result provides an upper tail bound with respect to the largest mean of a distribution in the support of the de Finetti mixing measure, and not the population mean. Equivalently, we establish a lower tail bound with respect to the smallest mean of a distribution in the support of the de Finetti mixing measure. This bridges the gap between finite sample and population means of exchangeable random variables, and distributional means.



Keywords

Concentration inequalities, uncertainty, exchangeable random variables

1
Introduction

A common assumption in statistical modeling is that observations are independent and identically distributed, i.i.d. For identically distributed random variables, exchangeability provides a weaker symmetry assumption than independence, requiring only that the joint distribution be invariant under permutations of the indices. In some cases, such as for linear models, distinguishing between these assumptions using data alone may be impossible:

“[…] there is no nontrivial, unbiased or invariant test of the null hypothesis that the errors are i.i.d. against the alternative hypothesis that they are merely exchangeably distributed, so that we would not be able to determine from the data whether or not the errors were i.i.d.”

[
2
]

This observation and existing tests for exchangeability
[
14
]
, motivate studying statistical guarantees that rely only on exchangeability. Here an open question, relevant to generalization bounds in machine learning and related data science settings, is
whether one can obtain concentration bounds for sums of exchangeable random variables under an arbitrary marginal distribution
. Addressing this question is particularly important in situations where the variance of the data-generating distribution is unknown or inaccessible. In such cases, concentration bounds provided by Hoeffding-type inequalities are useful because they provide variance-free concentration guarantees. For example, in statistical learning theory, the variance-free concentration guarantee from Hoeffding’s inequality for sums of i.i.d. random variables
[
13
]
, has been used to prove the generalization bound provided by Vapnik and Chervonenkjs
[
7
, Theorem 12.5]
.

In this paper, we answer this question in the affirmative. We show that the sample mean of bounded exchangeable random variables has a high-probability containment in a fixed interval and, in general, does not converge to the distribution mean. More specifically, the sample mean of bounded exchangeable random variables is contained with high probability in a fixed interval between the smallest and largest means among the distributions in the support of the de Finetti mixing measure. As a consequence, our results allows the construction of confidence intervals for the sample mean of bounded exchangeable random variables that are valid for any underlying distribution and depend only on the range bounds and the sample size. Our contributions can be formalized as follows.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, where
\mathcal{F}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
. Fix the sample number
M\in\mathbb{N}
and for each
m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, let
X_{m}:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
be bounded exchangeable identically distributed random variables; Exchangeability is recalled in Definition
2.2
.

Let
P[0,1]
denote the space of probability measures on
[0,1]
, and let
\rho
be de Finetti’s measure, that is, the (random) mixing measure on
P[0,1]
from the measure theoretic formulation of de Finetti’s theorem, that we report in Theorem
2.3
. Denote by

\displaystyle\bar{X}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m},\quad\tilde{\mu}_{+}=\sup_{q\in\text{supp}(\rho)}\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1})\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{\mu}_{-}=\inf_{q\in\text{supp}(\rho)}\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1}),

the sample mean
\bar{X}
and the supremum
\tilde{\mu}_{+}
, respectively infimum
\tilde{\mu}_{-}
, of the expectation of the random variable
X_{1}
with respect to the measures in the support of the de Finetti mixing measure

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{X}-\tilde{\mu}_{+}\geq t\right)\leq 2e^{-2Mt^{2}},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{-}-\bar{X}\geq t\right)\leq 2e^{-2Mt^{2}}.

As in Hoeffding’s seminal work

\tilde{\mu}_{+}

Related Work

The literature on variance-free concentration bounds for the sample mean falls into three broad categories:
(i) concentration for i.i.d. random variables, beginning with
[
13
]
, and for functions of i.i.d. variables, beginning with
[
19
]
;
(ii) concentration for functions of exchangeable random variables under structural assumptions on the function (see e.g.
[
5
]
); and
(iii) concentration for the weighted sample mean of exchangeable random variables around the finite
population mean

[
9
]
.

Ramdas and Manole
[
21
, Theorem 3.4]
prove a Hoeffding-type inequality for the mean of exchangeable sub-Gaussian random variables with respect to the distribution mean. Foygel Barber
[
9
, Theorem 4]
derives bounds to the weighted sample mean with respect to the
population mean
, where the expectation is taken over a population size
N\in\mathbb{N}
, rather than the
distribution mean
. This restriction is unavoidable: For exchangeable sequences, neither the sample mean, nor the population mean generally converge in probability to the distribution mean. In a related direction, we establish Hoeffding-type bounds for sums of exchangeable random variables. Additionally, the concentration bound provided by Foygel Barber
[
9
]
recovers the classical Hoeffding inequality for the i.i.d. setting, in the limit of an infinitely large population,
N\to\infty
, for fixed sample number
M\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
M\leq N
. Similarly, we recover the classical Hoeffding inequality for all finite samples sizes
M\in\mathbb{N}
by assuming independence of the samples. In addition to the theoretical interest, our findings are applicable across a broad range of problems in Statistics, including conformal prediction
[
23
,
16
,
1
]
and inference in regression
[
4
,
17
]
. We note in passing that finite exchangeability—a stronger assumption than infinite exchangeability—appears in several modern statistical applications, such as permutation testing
[
11
,
20
]
. With a suitable adaptation based on a finite de Finetti-type representation
[
8
]
, our method of proof may be applicable in this setting as well.

It is also worth pointing out that variance-free concentration bounds, such as Hoeffding’s inequality from
[
13
]
, have been used to prove generalization bounds in machine learning. For example, the proof of the generalization bound provided by Vapnik and Chervonenkjs
[
7
, Theorem 12.5]
uses Hoeffding’s inequality. Notably, Hoeffding’s inequality from
[
13
]
has also been used to prove the Glivenko-Cantelli Theorem
[
7
, Theorem 12.4]
for uniform almost sure convergence of the empirical distribution function to the true one.

We now provide a summary of both Hoeffding’s inequality and de Finetti’s Theorem; This is because (i) our results can recover Hoeffding’s inequality under the assumption of independent samples, and (ii) our proof uses a measure-theoretic formulation of de Finetti’s representation Theorem.

2.1
Hoeffding’s Inequality

Our result and proof are closely related to Hoeffding’s classical inequality in
[
13
]
, and recovers it in the i.i.d. case; We summarize it next.

Let
\{X_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be i.i.d. random variables. Denote

\displaystyle\bar{X}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m},\qquad\mu=\mathbb{E}(X_{1}).

(2.1)

Then, (a simplified) Hoeffding’s inequality from
[
13
]
states the following.

Theorem 2.1

(Hoeffding’s Inequality)
.

Let
\{X_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be i.i.d. random variables, such that for all
m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
we have that
0\leq X_{m}\leq 1
with probability 1. Then, for
0<t<1-\mu
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{X}-\mu\geq t\right)\leq e^{-2Mt^{2}}.

(2.2)

2.2
de Finetti’s Theorem

We present a measure-theoretic formulation of de Finetti’s theorem, which is central to our proof. We follow
[
10
]
, where a measure-theoretic version of de Finetti’s original theorem
[
6
]
is developed and proved via an innovative category-theoretic argument.

Given a measurable space
X
, consider the product
X^{\mathbb{N}}
of countably many copies of
X
, equipped with the product
\sigma
-algebra. A
bijection
\pi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
which fixes all elements apart from a finite subset is called a finite permutation. Given a finite permutation
\pi
, denote by
M_{\pi}\in\mathbb{N}
the largest natural number not fixed by
\pi
. Consider a probability measure
p
on
X^{\mathbb{N}}
. By convention, given a finite collection of measurable subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{M}\subset X
, we write
p(S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{M})
as shorthand for the probability of the “cylinder” event

p(S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{M}\times X\times X\times\cdots)
. These probabilities specify the marginal distribution of
p
on the first
M
components of
X^{\mathbb{N}}
. We say that the measure
p
is exchangeable if, for every finite permutation
\pi
and for every finite sequence of measurable subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{M_{\pi}}\subset X
, we have

\displaystyle p(S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{M})=p(S_{\pi(1)}\times\cdots\times S_{\pi(M)}).

(2.3)

A somewhat trivial example of exchangeable measures is given by product measures, i.e., the ones satisfying

\displaystyle q(S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{M})=q(S_{1})\times\cdots\times q(S_{M}).

(2.4)

Such a product measure
q
constitutes the law of a sequence of i.i.d. random variables. For
X=[0,1]
the above definition can be formalized by the following.

Definition 2.2

(Exchangeably-Distributed Random Variables)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and equip
[0,1]
with the Borel sigma algebra
\mathcal{B}([0,1])
. Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
and for each
m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
let
X_{m}:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
be random variables. Then,
(X_{m})_{m=1}^{M}
are said to be
distributed exchangeably
, if for any
M\in\mathbb{N}
their joint law
p
extended to
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
is exchangeable, i.e. for any
(S_{m})_{m=1}^{M}\subset\mathcal{B}([0,1])
, it satisfies

\displaystyle p(S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{M}\times[0,1]\times[0,1]\times\cdots)=p(S_{\sigma(1)}\times\cdots\times S_{\sigma(M)}\times[0,1]\times[0,1]\times\cdots),

(2.5)

for all finite permutations
\sigma:\{1,\ldots,M\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,M\}
.

Based on the definition of exchangeability in (
2.3
), we now turn to de Finetti’s Theorem itself. A convenient way to express the statement, which lends itself well to category-theoretical translations, is to use the concept of measures on a space of measures, as done by Hewitt and Savage
[
12
, Section 2]
and Fritz
[
10
]
. If
X
is a standard Borel space, we denote by
PX
the set of probability measures on
X
. The set
PX
can be equipped with a canonical
\sigma
-algebra, namely the one generated by the functions

\displaystyle\epsilon_{f}:PX

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\mapsto\int_{X}f(x)p(\mathrm{d}x),

(2.6)

for all bounded measurable functions
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Measures on
PX
(thus elements of
PPX
) can be thought of as random measures on
X
, where also the specific form of the distribution is subject to uncertainty; This draws a parallel with the imprecise probability literature
[
18
,
24
,
3
,
22
]
. Equivalently, measures on
PX
describe mixtures of measures either in the sense of finite convex combinations, or integrals. Indeed, de Finetti’s theorem can be summarized as saying that every exchangeable measure is a mixture of product measures. We now state the result precisely for standard Borel spaces.

Theorem 2.3

(de Finetti’s Theorem)
.

Let
X
be a standard Borel space. A probability measure
p
on
X^{\mathbb{N}}
is exchangeable if and only if there exists a probability measure
\mu
on
PX
such that for every finite collection of measurable subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{M}\subset X
, we have

\displaystyle p(S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{M})=\int_{PX}q(S_{1})\times\cdots\times q(S_{M})\mu(\mathrm{d}q).

(2.7)

For example, the i.i.d. case corresponds to
\mu
being the Dirac measure
\delta_{r}\in PPX
at some distribution
r\in PX
, in which case
p=r^{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, Theorem
2.3
yields, for any
S_{1},\dots,S_{M}
,

\displaystyle p(S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{M})

\displaystyle=\int_{PX}q(S_{1})\times\cdots\times q(S_{M})\delta_{r}(\mathrm{d}q)=r(S_{1})\times\cdots\times r(S_{M}).

(2.8)

3
Hoeffding-type bounds for exchangeable random variables

First, notice that Definition
2.2
is equivalent to finite exchangeability by
[
15
, Theorem 14.36]
. The following is our main result.

Lemma 3.1

(Hoeffding-Type Bounds for Exchangeable Random Variables)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. Fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
and for each
m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, let
X_{m}:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
be bounded exchangeable identically distributed random variables. Let
\rho
be the mixing measure on
P[0,1]
from Theorem
2.3
for the joint law of the exchangeable random variables. Denote

\displaystyle\bar{X}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m},\quad\tilde{\mu}_{+}=\sup_{q\in\text{supp}(\rho)}\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1})\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{\mu}_{-}=\sup_{q\in\text{supp}(\rho)}\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1}).

Then,

(i)

for
0<t<1-\tilde{\mu}_{+}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{X}-\tilde{\mu}_{+}\geq t\right)\leq e^{-2Mt^{2}};

(3.1)

(ii)

for
0<t<\tilde{\mu}_{-}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{-}-\bar{X}\geq t\right)\leq e^{-2Mt^{2}}.

(3.2)

Note that as a Corollary we recover Hoeffding’s inequality in Theorem
2.1
in the upper bound, when assuming that the random variables are independent instead of exchangeable.

Corollary 3.2

(Hoeffding’s Inequality as a Consequence of Lemma
3.1
)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. Fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
and for each
m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, let
X_{m}:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
be bounded idependently identically distributed random variables. Denote

\displaystyle\bar{X}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m},\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu=\mathbb{E}(X_{1}).

Then, for
0<t<1-\mu
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{X}-\mu\geq t\right)\leq e^{-2Mt^{2}}.

Proof of Corollary
3.2
.

By assumption the samples of random variables are independent, thus by (
2.8
) the mixing measure reduces to the Dirac measure at some distribution
q\in P[0,1]
. As the mixing measure is the Dirac measure, the largest and smallest mean of the distributions in the support of the de Finetti mixing measure coincide with the distribution mean with respect to the distribution
q
, yielding
\tilde{\mu}_{-}=\tilde{\mu}_{+}=\mu=\mathbb{E}(X_{1})
. For
0<t<1-\mu
the upper bound (
3.1
) of Lemma
3.1
, yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{X}-\mu\geq t\right)\leq e^{-2Mt^{2}}.

(3.3)

∎

Remark 3.3
.

The proof of Lemma
3.1
is divided in two parts.

For part
(i)
, we progress as follows. Using the decomposition from Theorem
2.3
, we can proceed along the same lines as in the proof of Hoeffding’s concentration inequality for i.i.d. random variables in
[
13
]
.
However, at certain steps, we need to deviate from the original proof for i.i.d. random variables, as we have to deal with the integration with respect to the mixing measure. There, we apply
[
13
, Lemma 1]
to each conditional mean and not to the distributional mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}(X_{1})
. We then bound the resulting term with the supremum of the conditional means, denoted
\tilde{\mu}_{+}=\sup_{q\in\text{supp}(\rho)}\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1})
. After this crucial step, we return to the analysis used in the proof in
[
13
]
. Notably, we do not use Hoeffding’s Lemma, as its application in the proof is contingent on i.i.d. random variables.

For part
(ii)
we bound the probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{M\tilde{\mu}_{-}-\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}\geq Mt\right\}\right)
. Here we use part
(i)
and replace the random variables
X_{m}
by
1-X_{m}
, and the supremum of the means of the distributions in the support of the de Finetti mixing measure with the infimum of the means.

Note that in both parts,
(i)
and
(ii)
, the supremum and infimum of the means of the distributions in the support of the de Finetti mixing measure can be replaced by the essential supremum and essential infimum (with respect to the de Finetti mixing measure). This replacement can yield a tighter bound when the de Finetti mixing measure is non-atomic. In that case, each individual distribution (equivalently, each singleton in the support) has zero mass under the de Finetti mixing measure.

For completeness, we restate the core analytical components of Hoeffding’s original proof in
[
13
]
as Lemma
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
in the next section, thereby making our proof essentially self-contained.

4
Proof

We start by recalling Hoeffding’s classical results.

Lemma 4.1

(Lemma 1 in
[
13
]
)
.

Let
a<b\in\mathbb{R}
. If
X:\Omega\rightarrow[a,b]
is a random variable such that
a\leq X\leq b
, then for any real
h
,

\mathbb{E}e^{hX}\leq\frac{b-\mathbb{E}X}{b-a}e^{ha}+\frac{\mathbb{E}X-a}{b-a}e^{hb}.

(4.1)

Proof.

Let
X
be a random variable such that
a\leq X\leq b
. Since the exponential function
e^{hx}
is convex, its graph on the interval
a\leq x\leq b
lies below the straight line connecting the points
(a,e^{ha})
and
(b,e^{hb})
. Therefore, for all
a\leq X\leq b
,

\displaystyle e^{hX}\leq\frac{b-X}{b-a}e^{ha}+\frac{X-a}{b-a}e^{hb}.

(4.2)

Taking expectations on both sides yields the desired inequality.
∎

Proposition 4.2

(Simple Bound)
.

Let
\tilde{\mu}\in(0,1)
and
0<t<1-\tilde{\mu}
. For
h>0
we have that

\displaystyle e^{-\tilde{\mu}h-th}(1-\tilde{\mu}+\tilde{\mu}e^{h})\leq e^{-2t^{2}}.

(4.3)

Proof.

After setting the partial derivative of the left-hand side of (
4.3
) with respect to
h
to zero, we find that the left-hand side of (
4.3
) attains its minimum at
h=h_{0}
, where

\displaystyle h_{0}=\ln\left(\frac{(1-\tilde{\mu})(t+\tilde{\mu})}{(1-\tilde{\mu}-t)\tilde{\mu}}\right).

(4.4)

Since
0<t<1-\tilde{\mu}
, we have that
h_{0}
is positive. Now, plugging
h=h_{0}
into the right hand side of (
4.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle e^{-\tilde{\mu}h-th}(1-\tilde{\mu}+\tilde{\mu}e^{h})\leq\left(\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{\tilde{\mu}+t}\right)^{\tilde{\mu}+t}\left(\frac{1-\tilde{\mu}}{1-\tilde{\mu}-t}\right)^{1-\tilde{\mu}-t}.

(4.5)

To prove the upper bound in (
4.3
), we write the right-hand side in (
4.5
) in the form
e^{-t^{2}G(t,\mu)}
, where

\displaystyle G(t,\tilde{\mu})=\frac{t+\tilde{\mu}}{t^{2}}\ln\frac{\tilde{\mu}+t}{\tilde{\mu}}+\frac{1-\tilde{\mu}-t}{t^{2}}\ln\frac{1-\tilde{\mu}-t}{1-\tilde{\mu}}.

(4.6)

Now, inequality (
4.3
) will be proved by showing that
g(\tilde{\mu})
, defined as

\displaystyle g(\tilde{\mu})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1-2\tilde{\mu}}\ln\left(\frac{1-\tilde{\mu}}{\tilde{\mu}}\right)&\qquad 0<\tilde{\mu}<\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2\tilde{\mu}(1-\tilde{\mu})}&\qquad\frac{1}{2}\leq\tilde{\mu}<1\end{cases},

(4.7)

is the minimum of
G(t,\tilde{\mu})
with respect to
t
, where

0<t<1-\tilde{\mu}
. The derivative
\partial G(t,\tilde{\mu})/\partial t
can be written in the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t^{2}}\frac{\partial G(t,\tilde{\mu})}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\left(1-2\frac{1-\tilde{\mu}}{t}\right)\ln\left(1-\frac{t}{1-\tilde{\mu}}\right)-\left(1-2\frac{\tilde{\mu}+t}{t}\right)\ln\left(1-\frac{t}{\tilde{\mu}+t}\right)

\displaystyle=H\left(\frac{t}{1-\tilde{\mu}}\right)-H\left(\frac{t}{\tilde{\mu}+t}\right),

(4.8)

where
H(x)=(1-2x^{-1})\ln(1-x)
. By assumption,
0<\frac{t}{\tilde{\mu}+t}<1
and
0<\frac{t}{1-\tilde{\mu}}<1
. Using the Taylor series of
\ln(1-x)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}x^{n}
for
|x|<1
around
x=0
, we have the expansion

\displaystyle H(x)

\displaystyle=(2x^{-1}-1)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}x^{n}=2+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2}{n+1}-\frac{1}{n}\right)x^{n},

(4.9)

where all coefficients are positive. Thus
H(x)
is increasing for
0<x<1
. It follows from (
4
) that
\frac{\partial G}{\partial t}>0
if and only if
\frac{t}{1-\tilde{\mu}}>\frac{t}{\tilde{\mu}+t}
, that is,
t>1-2\mu
. Hence, if
1-2\mu>0
, the function
G(t,\tilde{\mu})
attains its minimum at
t=1-2\tilde{\mu}
, and the value of the minimum is
G(1-2\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\mu})=g(\tilde{\mu})
. If
1-2\tilde{\mu}<0
, then
G(t,\tilde{\mu})
attains its minimum at
t=0
, and the value of the minimum is
\frac{1}{2\tilde{\mu}(1-\tilde{\mu})}=g(\tilde{\mu})
. Now it is easily seen that
g(\tilde{\mu})\geq g(1/2)=2
. This implies the desired inequality in (
4.3
).
∎

We are now ready for the proof of our main result, Lemma
3.1
.

.

A&\mapsto\mathbb{P}((X_{1},X_{2},...X_{M})^{-1}(A)).\end{split}

(4.10)

Following
[
10

, we denote the set of probability measures on

\displaystyle P[0,1]\equiv P_{\mathcal{B}([0,1])}=\{\rho:\mathcal{B}([0,1])\rightarrow[0,1],\text{ }\rho\text{ is a probability measure}\}.

\displaystyle\epsilon_{f}:P[0,1]

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\mapsto\epsilon_{f}(p)=\int_{[0,1]}f(x)p(\mathrm{d}x),

(4.12)

for all bounded measurable functions

2.3

(X_{m})_{m=1}^{M}:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]^{M}

. As the random variables are exchangeable identically distributed, the extended law
p
is exchangeable, and by de Finetti’s Theorem
2.3
, there exists a probability measure
\rho\in PP[0,1]
on
P[0,1]\equiv P_{\mathcal{B}([0,1])}
such that for every finite collection of measurable subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{M}\in\mathcal{B}([0,1])
, we have

\displaystyle p(S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{M})=\int_{P[0,1]}q(S_{1})\times\cdots\times q(S_{M})\rho(\mathrm{d}q).

(4.13)

Following
[
13
, Inequality (1.7)]
, we apply a common method to derive concentration inequalities. The probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}-M\tilde{\mu}_{+}>Mt\right\}\right)

is the expected value of the function on
\Omega
which takes the values
0
and
1
, if

\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}-M\tilde{\mu}_{+}-Mt
is
\leq 0
or
>0
, respectively. This function does not exceed the exponential function,
e^{h(\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}-M\tilde{\mu}_{+}-Mt)}
, where
h
is an arbitrary positive constant. The above can be summarized by the fact that the indicator function of a positive function is smaller or equal than the exponential function concatenated with the multiplication by a positive constant of a function. This yields

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{X>0}(\omega)\leq e^{hX}(\omega),

(4.14)

for all
h>0
, all
\omega\in\Omega
and any
X:\Omega\rightarrow[0,\infty)
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}-M\tilde{\mu}_{+}>Mt\right\}\right)<\mathbb{E}\left(e^{h(\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}-M\tilde{\mu}_{+}-Mt)}\right).

(4.15)

Now we use the above inequality, as applied in
[
13
, Inequality (1.7)]
; For
h>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}-M\tilde{\mu}_{+}\geq Mt\right\}\right)&\leq\mathbb{E}\left(e^{h(\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}-M\tilde{\mu}_{+}-Mt)}\right)\\
&=e^{-Mht-Mh\tilde{\mu}_{+}}\text{ }\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}e^{hX_{m}}\right).\end{split}

(4.16)

As for
m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, each
X_{m}:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
is a bounded exchangeable identically distributed random variable, but not independent; Therefore, we cannot use

[
13
, Equality (1.8)]
. Instead, we now consider the non-negative, continuous and, thus, Borel measurable function

\displaystyle\begin{split}g:[0,1]^{M}&\rightarrow[0,\infty)\\
(x_{1},..x_{M})&\mapsto g(x_{1},..x_{M})=\prod_{m=1}^{M}e^{hx_{m}},\end{split}

(4.17)

and proceed to apply de Finetti’s theorem, the binomial theorem and a simple thresholding argument to prove an exponentially-in-
M\in\mathbb{N}
decreasing concentration bound for (
4.16
).

The expectation over the function
g
defined in
(
4.16
) can be rewritten using the pushforward of the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
with respect to
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})
, yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}e^{hX_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(g(X_{1},\ldots,X_{M}))=\int_{\Omega}g((X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})(\omega))\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})(\Omega)}g(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}((X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})^{-1}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})).

(4.18)

Now, inserting the law
p=\mathbb{P}\circ(X_{1},...,X_{M})^{-1}
in the above (
4
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})(\Omega)}&g(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}((X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})^{-1}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M}))\\
&=\int_{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})(\Omega)}g(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\mathrm{d}p(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M}).\end{split}

(4.19)

Now, we apply de Finetti’s theorem, in particular equality (
4.13
), to the above (
4.19
) to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})(\Omega)}&g(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\mathrm{d}p(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\\
&=\int_{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})(\Omega)}g(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\int_{P[0,1]}q(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\rho(\mathrm{d}q),\end{split}

(4.20)

for some fixed measure
\rho\in PP[0,1]
on the space of measures on
\mathcal{B}([0,1])
. Using Fubini’s theorem in the above (
4.20
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})(\Omega)}&g(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\int_{P[0,1]}q(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\rho(\mathrm{d}q)\\
&=\int_{P[0,1]}\int_{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{M})(\Omega)}g(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\mathrm{d}q(v_{1},\ldots,v_{M})\rho(\mathrm{d}q).\end{split}

(4.21)

Using that
\{X_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
are identically distributed, that
g(v_{1},...,v_{M})=\prod_{m=1}^{M}e^{hv_{m}}
and
q(v_{1},...v_{M})=\prod_{m=1}^{M}q(v_{m})
in the above, (
4.21
) yields

\displaystyle\int_{P[0,1]}\int_{\prod_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}(\Omega)}g(v_{1},..v_{M})\mathrm{d}q(v_{1},..v_{M})\rho(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=\int_{P[0,1]}\int_{\prod_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}(\Omega)}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}e^{hv_{m}}\mathrm{d}q(v_{m})\right)\rho(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=\int_{P[0,1]}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\left(\int_{X_{m}(\Omega)}e^{hv_{m}}\mathrm{d}q(v_{m})\right)\rho(\mathrm{d}q).

(4.22)

We now make explicit the expectation with respect to the measure
q
appearing in the integral in (
4
), obtaining

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}e^{hX_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{P[0,1]}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\left(\int_{X_{m}(\Omega)}e^{hv_{m}}\mathrm{d}q(v_{m})\right)\rho(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=\int_{P[0,1]}\left(\int_{X_{1}(\Omega)}e^{hv_{1}}\mathrm{d}q(v_{1})\right)^{M}\rho(\mathrm{d}q)=\int_{P[0,1]}\left(\mathbb{E}_{q}\left(e^{hX_{1}}\right)\right)^{M}\rho(\mathrm{d}q).

(4.23)

Using the above (
4
) in inequality (
4.16
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}-M\tilde{\mu}_{+}\geq Mt\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-Mht}\text{ }\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}e^{h(X_{m}-\tilde{\mu}_{+})}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-Mht-Mh\tilde{\mu}_{+}}\text{ }\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}e^{hX_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-Mht-Mh\tilde{\mu}_{+}}\int_{P[0,1]}\left(\mathbb{E}_{q}\left(e^{hX_{1}}\right)\right)^{M}\rho(\mathrm{d}q).

(4.24)

Now we use Lemma
4.1
, which arises from the convexity of the exponential function, to start to polynomialise the integrand with respect to the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{q}\left(X_{1}\right)
. We apply Lemma
4.1
with the lower limit
a=0
and the upper limit
b=1
, to obtain

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}_{q}\left(e^{hX_{1}}\right)\right)^{M}\leq(1-\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1})+\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1})e^{h})^{M}=(1+(e^{h}-1)\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1}))^{M}.

(4.25)

Note that
\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1})\leq\sup_{q\in\text{supp}(\rho)}\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1})=\tilde{\mu}_{+}
for all
q\in\text{supp}(\rho)
, where
\rho
is the mixing measure on
P[0,1]
from de Finetti’s Theorem. As for
h>0
, we have that
(e^{h}-1)>0
, thus obtaining

\displaystyle(1+(e^{h}-1)\mathbb{E}_{q}(X_{1}))^{M}\leq((e^{h}-1)\tilde{\mu}_{+}+1)^{M}=(1-\tilde{\mu}_{+}+\tilde{\mu}_{+}e^{h})^{M}.

(4.26)

Combining (
4.25
) and (
4.26
) yields the inequality

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}_{q}\left(e^{hX_{1}}\right)\right)^{M}\leq(1-\tilde{\mu}_{+}+\tilde{\mu}_{+}e^{h})^{M},

(4.27)

which holds for all
h>0
. Now we can insert inequality (
4.27
) into the concentration inequality (
4
), resulting in

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}-M\tilde{\mu}_{+}\geq Mt\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-Mht-Mh\tilde{\mu}_{+}}\int_{P[0,1]}\left(\mathbb{E}_{q}\left(e^{hX_{1}}\right)\right)^{M}\rho(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-Mht-Mh\tilde{\mu}_{+}}\int_{P[0,1]}(1-\tilde{\mu}_{+}+\tilde{\mu}_{+}e^{h})^{M}\rho(\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=e^{(-\tilde{\mu}_{+}-t)hM}(1-\tilde{\mu}_{+}+\tilde{\mu}_{+}e^{h})^{M},

(4.28)

which holds for all
h>0
, where convergence to zero as
M\to\infty
can be obtained for

\displaystyle e^{(-\tilde{\mu}_{+}-t)h}(1-\tilde{\mu}_{+}+\tilde{\mu}_{+}e^{h})<1.

(4.29)

Note that bound in (
4
) is the same as in
[
13
, Equation (4.6)]
. For
0<t<1-\tilde{\mu}_{+}
the minimizing
h_{0}>0
in (

) is indeed positive and is of the form given in

, Equation (4.7)]
with
\mu
replaced by
\tilde{\mu}_{+}
. The rest of the proof follows by applying Proposition
4.2
. The latter follows the exact same analysis steps as in

]
with
\mu
replaced by
\tilde{\mu}_{+}
.

Now for the proof of the lower tail bound, the proof of part
(ii)
, we use the result from part
(i)
. We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{-}-\bar{X}\geq t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(-\bar{X}-(-\tilde{\mu}_{-})\geq t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left((1-\bar{X})-(1-\tilde{\mu}_{-})\geq t\right).

(4.30)

Note that the assumption

has been made to give the bounds in part

(4.31)

Now by applying part

to

, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{-}-\bar{X}\geq t\right)\leq e^{-2Mt^{2}}.

(4.32)

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we established a Hoeffding-type inequalities for exchangeable random variables, extending prior work in the exchangeable setting that is restricted to concentration of the sample mean with respect to the population mean. In Corollary
3.2
we recover Hoeffding’s inequality from our result by assuming independent samples.

Our result provides a tool for establishing generalization bounds and confidence intervals for the loss in machine learning settings. Specifically, Lemma
3.1
applies whenever the loss values computed on training or test points constitute a sample from an infinitely exchangeable sequence, without any restriction on the marginal distribution. This result motivates future work on approximating and bounding the supremum
\tilde{\mu}_{+}
, respectively infimum
\tilde{\mu}_{-}
, of the expectation of the random variable
X_{1}
with respect to the measures in the support of the de Finetti mixing measure.
