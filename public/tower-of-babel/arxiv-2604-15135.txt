On the exponential rate of the condition number of Fourier submatrices and Vandermonde matrices
Rikhav Shah, John Urschel

Abstract
. The discrete Fourier transform matrix is one of the most important matrices in linear algebra, and submatrices of it arise in a variety of applications. Though the discrete Fourier transform matrix is unitary, its submatrices can be exponentially ill-conditioned, an obstacle to accurate computation. This work resolves the exact rate of the exponential ill-conditioning for square submatrices with contiguous rows and columns. As a consequence, we obtain a tight upper bound of 2 ​ G / π 2G/\pi on the exponential rate for all submatrices with contiguous columns, or, equivalently, all Vandermonde submatrices with distinct support points, where G G is Catalan’s constant. These results follow from a more general analysis of Vandermonde and Vandermonde-like matrices for which exact estimates for exponential ill-conditioning are developed in terms of logarithmic potentials.

Our analysis also relaxes the requirement that the entire submatrix be contiguous; we only require that the columns form a contiguous set. We omit studying completely arbitrary submatrices for two reasons. First, whenever

is composite, there are singular submatrices (for instance, for

In applications where Fourier submatrices appear, they invariably seem to be themselves Vandermonde matrices. In the setting of contiguous columns only, we again recover the correct exponential rate of the condition number, though we do not obtain the polynomial factor (

We derive the aforementioned bounds from a more general analysis of Vandermonde matrices with nodes on the unit circle (which we further relax later in
Theorem

1.5
). Such matrices are often called non-uniform discrete Fourier transform matrices, see
[
35

for applications where such matrices arise and the influence of the condition number. Let
V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})=(z_{k}^{j})_{j,k=0}^{n}
be the square Vandermonde matrix with nodes
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{C}
be the unit circle, and
d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(z,w)
be the arc length between
z,w\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(z_{j},z_{k})\geq\varepsilon>0
. Then

(1.1)

{\frac{\log\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{-1}\right\|}{n}\leq\frac{4}{n\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi-\frac{C\log n}{n}\pm\frac{O(\log(n\varepsilon(2\pi-n\varepsilon)))}{n}}

for some constant
C\in\left[\tfrac{1}{2},1\right]
. Furthermore, equality is achieved, with
C=1
, when
z_{j}=e^{ij\varepsilon}
for
j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
.

The proof of
Theorem

1.1
is surprisingly simple in spirit, using only Lagrange interpolating polynomials, logarithmic potentials, and Riemann summation. Let
[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
and, for a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
and subsets
S,T\subset[N]
, let
A_{S,T}
denote the
|S|\times|T|
submatrix of
A
with rows indexed by
S
and columns indexed by
T
and let
\kappa(A_{S,T})=\sigma_{1}(A_{S,T})/\sigma_{\min(|S|,|T|)}(A_{S,T})
be its condition number. A subset
S\subset[N]
is said to be
cyclically contiguous
if it equals a set of consecutive integers modulo
N
. As a corollary to
Theorem

1.1
, we obtain a tight bound on how ill-conditioned a submatrix of the Fourier matrix with contiguous columns (or rows) can be.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
F=\big(e^{\frac{2\pi i(j-1)(k-1)}{N}}\big)_{j,k=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be the Fourier matrix,
S\subset[N]
be an arbitrary subset,
T\subset[N]
be a cyclically contiguous subset, and
\alpha=\max(|S|,|T|)/N
. Then

\log\kappa\left(F_{S,T}\right)\leq\frac{2N}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi\pm O(\log\alpha(1-\alpha)).

If both
S,T\subset[N]
are cyclically contiguous subsets, then

\log\kappa\left(F_{S,T}\right)\leq\frac{2N}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi-\frac{1}{2}\log(N)\pm O(\log\alpha(1-\alpha)),

and equality is achieved when
|S|=|T|
.

The quantity
\displaystyle{\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi}
is maximal for
\displaystyle{\alpha=\frac{1}{2}}
, where it equals
\displaystyle{\frac{2G}{\pi}}
, where
\displaystyle{G=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k+1)^{2}}}
is Catalan’s constant. As a result, we also have the following uniform upper bound.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
F=\big(e^{\frac{2\pi i(j-1)(k-1)}{N}}\big)_{j,k=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be the Fourier matrix,
S\subset[N]
be an arbitrary subset, and
T\subset[N]
be a cyclically contiguous subset. Then

\log\kappa\left(F_{S,T}\right)\leq\frac{2GN}{\pi}\pm O(1),

where
G
is Catalan’s constant. If both
S,T\subset[N]
are cyclically contiguous subsets, then

\log\kappa\left(F_{S,T}\right)\leq\frac{2GN}{\pi}-\frac{1}{2}\log(N)\pm O(1),

and equality is achieved when
|S|=|T|=\lceil N/2\rceil
and both
S,T\subset[N]
are cyclically contiguous subsets.

Remark 1.4
.

Our estimates are tight for square submatrices, but only provide loose upper bounds for non-square submatrices. However, an adaptation of the techniques presented herein can provide tighter estimates for non-square submatrices. In particular, by the Cauchy-Binet formula and singular value interlacing, for
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
,
m>n
, we have

(1.2)

\max_{|S|=n}\sigma_{n}\left(A_{S,[n]}\right)\leq\sigma_{n}(A)\leq\sqrt{\sum_{|S|=n}\sigma_{n}\left(A_{S,[n]}\right)^{2}}.

Experimentally, for nearly square Fourier submatrices, the gap between the infinity and two-norm estimates in
Equation

1.2
seems reasonably small. This provides a framework for bounding the nearly square case, the regime where Barnett’s estimates suffer somewhat, by only using estimates for square submatrices. We leave open this potential avenue of future research.

The weakness of
Theorem

1.1
is a high sensitivity to the minimum gap between the nodes. Consider, for example, starting with

z_{j}=e^{ij\varepsilon}
for
j=0,\ldots,n
, the instance where
eq.

1.1
is tight, and replacing
z_{n}
with
e^{i\varepsilon(n-1)+i\varepsilon^{2}}
. The minimum gap is now
\varepsilon^{2}
, and yet
eq.

1.1
is still an equality, up to
O(\log n)/n
error. It turns out that all we need for
eq.

1.1
to hold as an equality is for the set of nodes
{\left\{{z_{j}}\right\}}_{j=0}^{n}
to be a “good approximation” to the continuous uniform measure on the arc of the unit circle from
1
to
e^{i\varepsilon n}
. We state this result,
Theorem

1.5
, in larger generality, where the continuous uniform measure on an arc of the circle is replaced by any measure satisfying a few regularity assumptions (which are satisfied, for example, when the measure is uniform on some compact set). We measure the quality of approximation a discrete set of points provides for some continuous measure via a two dimensional version of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance. Let
\ball(z,r)=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:\left|w-z\right|\leq r\}
. For two measures
\mu
and
\nu
, the distance is

\textnormal{KS}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{r>0}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\left|\mu\left(\ball(z,r)\right)-\nu\left(\ball(z,r)\right)\right|

and in this context we identify a finite set of points
S\subset\mathbb{C}
with the uniform probability measure
\mu_{S}
on that set.

A second way
Theorem

1.5
below generalizes
Theorem

1.1
is by extending the analysis to Vandermonde-like matrices, defined as follows. Given the polynomials
p_{0},\ldots,p_{n}
, define

\left(V^{(p_{0},\ldots p_{n})}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\right)_{jk}=p_{k}(z_{j}).

The standard Vandermonde matrix is the special case of this where
p_{k}(z)=z^{k}
.

Theorem

1.5
involves a couple characterizations of the polynomials, one involving the inner product
\left<f,g\right>_{\nu}=\int f\bar{g}\,\textnormal{d}\nu
, and another involving the sup-norm
\left\|f\right\|_{X}=\sup_{x\in X}\left|f(x)\right|
.
For the majority of reasonable choices of polynomials and nodes,
Theorem

1.5
shows that
\kappa(V)
is an exponential quantity in
n
. The rate is stated in terms of a
logarithmic potential
. For a measure
\mu
, its logarithmic potential is the real-valued function on
\mathbb{C}

U^{\mu}(z)=\int\log\frac{1}{\left|z-t\right|}\,\textnormal{d}\mu(t).

We present the following general theorem for Vandermonde-like matrices with arbitrary nodes in the complex plane.

Theorem 1.5
.

Given a set of nodes
S\subset\mathbb{C}
,
\left|S\right|=n
, with minimum distance
\varepsilon=\min\limits_{a,b\in S,a\neq b}\left|a-b\right|
, suppose
\mu
is a probability measure containing
S
in its support and satisfying the following regularity assumptions for constants
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
,
C
,
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
R
:

(1)

\supp(\mu)
is contained in a disk of radius
R
.

(2)

\sup_{r\in(0,\varepsilon)}\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mu\left(\ball(\zeta,r)\right)}{r^{\beta}}\leq\rho_{1}

(3)

\frac{\mu(\ball(z_{+},r))}{r^{\beta}}\geq\rho_{2}

for
z_{+}=\argmax_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}U^{\mu}(\zeta)
and
r={\operatorname{dist}}(z_{+},S)
.

(4)

\left|U^{\mu}(x)-U^{\mu}(y)\right|\leq C\left|x-y\right|^{\alpha}.

Let
p_{0},\ldots,p_{n}
form a basis of degree
\leq n
polynomials, with Gram matrix
G=\begin{bmatrix}\left<p_{j},p_{k}\right>_{\nu}\end{bmatrix}_{0\leq j,k\leq n}
for a probability measure
\nu
and max norm
\gamma=\max_{0\leq j\leq d}\left\|p_{j}(z)\right\|_{\supp(\nu)}
. Let
s=\min(\varepsilon,\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu))
. Then

(1.3)

\begin{split}\frac{\log\kappa\left(V^{(p_{0},\ldots,p_{n})}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\right)}{n}=&\sup_{z\in\supp(\mu)}U^{\mu}(z)-\inf_{z\in\supp(\nu)}U^{\mu}(z)\\
+&O\left(\frac{\log\left(n\gamma\left\|G^{-1}\right\|^{1/2}\right)}{n}+\log\frac{1}{s}\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu)+\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu)^{\alpha/\beta}\right)\end{split}

where
O(\cdot)
suppresses polynomial dependencies on
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
,
C
,
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
R
.

Remark 1.6
.

Intuitively, this theorem says that the condition number of the Vandermonde-like matrix is an exponential quantity in
n
, with a rate given by the range of the logarithmic potential of
\mu
between the supports of
\mu
and
\nu
, when
\nu
is a measure for which the polynomials
p_{0},\ldots,p_{n}

When

,

is the uniform distribution on the whole circle

,

1.5
recovers the rate stated in terms of the anti-derivative of
\log\cot(\cdot)
from

1.1
. However, the generality of

1.5
comes at a cost, as it does not recover the correct polynomial factor from
Theorem

1.1
.

2.
Vandermonde condition numbers and Lagrange interpolation

A key connection exploited in this work is that the conditioning of Vandermonde and Vandermonde-like matrices can be converted to statements about the norms of Lagrange interpolating polynomials.
Recall the definition of these polynomials: given
a set of nodes
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, there is a unique collection of polynomials,

L_{k}\left(z;{\left\{{z_{j}}\right\}}_{j=0}^{n}\right):=\prod_{j\neq k}\frac{z-z_{j}}{z_{k}-z_{j}},

abbreviated
L_{k}(z)
, of degree at most
n
such that

L_{k}(z_{j})=\begin{cases}1&j=k\\
0&j\neq k\end{cases}.

First we note that estimation of the norm of a Vandermonde matrix is straightforward, allowing us to focus our attention on estimating the norm of the inverse.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Then
\sqrt{n+1}\leq\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\right\|\leq n+1
. If
z_{j}=e^{2\pi ik_{j}/N}
for distinct indices
k_{j}<N
, then
\sqrt{n+1}\leq\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\right\|\leq\sqrt{N}
as well.

Proof.

The length of the first column of
V
is
\sqrt{n+1}
and the Frobenius norm of
V
equals
n+1
. When
z_{j}=e^{2\pi ik_{j}/N}
, then
V
is a submatrix of the
N\times N
Fourier matrix which has norm
\sqrt{N}
.
∎

We supply two lemmas relating the norm of the inverse of a Vandermonde matrix to Lagrange interpolating polynomials on the same nodes. The first is sharper and is needed to recover the constant
C
in
Theorem

1.1
. The second is looser, but can be more easily applied in the general setting of Vandermonde-like matrices
V^{(p_{0},\ldots,p_{n})}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
and is needed to obtain
Theorem

1.5
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
be distinct. Then

\frac{1}{2\pi}\max_{k\in{\left\{{0,\ldots,n}\right\}}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}\left(e^{i\theta};\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\right)\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{-1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}\left(e^{i\theta};\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\right)\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta.

Proof.

Let
V:=V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})
. Observe that

V^{-1}\bm{e}_{k}
is a list of the coefficients of
L_{k}(z)
, so by orthogonality of the monomials on the unit circle, we have

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta=\left\|V^{-1}\bm{e}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

The result follows by comparing the
\ell_{2}
operator norm to the Frobenius norm,

(2.1)

\begin{split}\max_{k\in{\left\{{0,\ldots,n}\right\}}}\left\|V^{-1}\bm{e}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left\|V^{-1}\right\|^{2}_{2}\leq\left\|V^{-1}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\|V^{-1}\bm{e}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
p_{0},\ldots,p_{n}
be a basis of degree
\leq n
polynomials, with Gram matrix
G=[\left<p_{j},p_{k}\right>_{\nu}]_{0\leq j,k\leq m}
for the inner product
\left<f,g\right>_{\nu}=\int f\bar{g}\,\textnormal{d}\nu
.
Put
V=V^{(p_{0},\ldots,p_{n})}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})
and
L_{k}(z)=L_{k}(z,{\left\{{z_{j}}\right\}}_{j=0}^{n})
.
Then

\dfrac{\max_{k\in{\left\{{0,\ldots,n}\right\}}}\left\|L_{k}\right\|_{\supp(\nu)}}{\sqrt{n+1}\max_{0\leq j\leq n}\left\|p_{j}\right\|_{\supp(\nu)}}\leq\left\|V^{-1}\right\|\leq\sqrt{n+1}\left\|G^{-1}\right\|^{1/2}\max_{k\in{\left\{{0,\ldots,n}\right\}}}\left\|L_{k}\right\|_{\supp(\nu)}.

Proof.

For
\bm{a}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{n}\end{pmatrix}
,
say
L_{k}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}p_{j}(z)
so that
V\bm{a}=\bm{e}_{k}
.
By the triangle inequality,

\left\|L_{k}\right\|_{\supp(\nu)}^{2}\leq\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{1}^{2}\max_{0\leq j\leq n}\left\|p_{j}\right\|^{2}_{\supp(\nu)}.

On the other hand,

\left\|L_{k}\right\|_{\supp(\nu)}^{2}\geq\int\left|L_{k}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\nu=a^{*}Ga.

Now
\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{1}^{2}\leq(n+1)\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{2}^{2}
and
a^{*}Ga\geq\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{2}^{2}/\|G^{-1}\|

so

\frac{\left\|L_{k}\right\|_{\supp(\nu)}^{2}}{(n+1)\max_{0\leq j\leq n}\left\|p_{j}\right\|^{2}_{\supp(\nu)}}\leq\left\|\bm{a}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|G^{-1}\|\left\|L_{k}\right\|_{\supp(\nu)}^{2}

Similar to
eq.

2.1
,

\max_{k\in{\left\{{0,\ldots,n}\right\}}}\left\|V^{-1}\bm{e}_{k}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|V^{-1}\right\|^{2}\leq(n+1)\max_{k\in{\left\{{0,\ldots,n}\right\}}}\left\|V^{-1}\bm{e}_{k}\right\|^{2}

gives the final result.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

\supp(\nu)

When only an upper bound is desired, as in the case of

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
and
\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(z_{j},z_{k})\geq\varepsilon
. Then

\frac{1}{n+1}\max_{z\in{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}\,\left|L_{k}\left(z;\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\right)\right|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}\left(e^{i\theta};\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\right)\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq\max_{z\in{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}\,\left|L_{k}\left(z;\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\right)\right|^{2}

for all
k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
and

\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}\max_{z\in{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}\,\left|L_{k}\big(z;\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\big)\right|\leq\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}\max_{z\in{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}\,\left|L_{k}\big(z;\{e^{ij\varepsilon}\}_{j=0}^{n}\big)\right|.

Proof.

Consider the first pair of inequalities. The integral, divided by
2\pi
, is the average value of
\left|L_{k}\big(z;\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\big)\right|^{2}
, which is clearly bounded above by its maximum value. Let
L_{k}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}z^{j}
. For the lower bound, we note that by Cauchy-Schwarz, for
z\in{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
,

|L_{k}(z)|^{2}=\left|\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}z^{j}\right|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|z|^{2j}\right)=\frac{(n+1)}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta};\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta.

Now consider the second inequality. Suppose, without loss of generality, that
\{z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\}
equals
\{e^{i\theta_{0}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{n}}\}
for some
0\leq\theta_{0}<\ldots<\theta_{n}\leq 2\pi
and that the maximum is achieved by index
k
and evaluation point
z=1
. The quantity
L_{k}(1)
is non-increasing with respect to
\theta_{j}
for
j>k
, and non-decreasing with respect to
\theta_{j}
for
j<k
. Therefore, we may take
\theta_{j}=\theta_{k}+(j-k)\varepsilon
for all
j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
without decreasing
L_{k}(1)
.
∎

In a similar fashion, the determinant of a Vandermonde matrix with unit nodes may also be lower bounded by equally spaced nodes, a fact which will prove useful when analyzing nearly uniform nodes (i.e., almost
(n+1)^{th}
roots of unity).

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(z_{j},z_{k})\geq\varepsilon
. Then

\left|\det\left(V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\right)\right|\geq\left|\det\left(V(1,e^{i\varepsilon},\ldots,e^{in\varepsilon})\right)\right|.

Proof.

Let
\{z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\}
equal
\{e^{i\theta_{0}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{n}}\}
for some
0\leq\theta_{0}<\ldots<\theta_{n}\leq 2\pi
, and define
\phi_{j}:=\theta_{j}-\theta_{j-1}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. We have

\displaystyle\log\left|\det(\left(V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j<k\leq n}\log\left|z_{j}-z_{k}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j<k\leq n}\log\left(2\sin\frac{\theta_{k}-\theta_{j}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j<k\leq n}\log\left(2\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{k}\phi_{\ell}\right)\right)=:f(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}).

The function
\log(2\sin(x/2))
is concave on
(0,2\pi)
, and so
f(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n})
, a sum of concave functions of linear forms, is also concave. The minimum value of
f(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n})
on the polytope
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\phi_{j}\leq 2\pi-\varepsilon
and
\phi_{j}\geq\varepsilon
for
j=1,\ldots,n
occurs at an extreme point. Every extreme point corresponds to
n+1
angles
\theta_{j}
with gaps
\varepsilon
for all but one pair, with a gap of
2\pi-n\varepsilon
.
∎

Finally, in order to accurately estimate
\|V^{-1}\|_{2}
for equispaced points on an arc, we require a better lower bound than that of
Lemma

2.2
. The below lemma provides the necessary test vector.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\varepsilon<2\pi n-2\varepsilon^{1/2}
,
S={\left\{{j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}:\left|j-\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor\right|\leq\sqrt{n}}\right\}}
, and
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be such that

\bm{x}_{j}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{n-j}e^{ijn\varepsilon/2}|S|^{-1/2}&j\in S\\
0&j\not\in S\end{cases}.

Then
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=1
and

\|V^{-1}(1,e^{i\varepsilon},\ldots,e^{in\varepsilon})\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{C(1-(\xi-1)|S|)|S|}{\xi}\|V^{-1}(1,e^{i\varepsilon},\ldots,e^{in\varepsilon})\bm{e}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\|_{2}^{2},

for some constant
C>0
, where

\xi:=\max_{k\in S}\;\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{2\pi}|L_{k}(e^{i\theta};\{e^{nij\varepsilon}\}_{j=0}^{n})|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta}}{\displaystyle{\int_{n\varepsilon+\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}|L_{k}(e^{i\theta};\{e^{nij\varepsilon}\}_{j=0}^{n})|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta}}.

Proof.

Let
\{L_{j}(z)\}_{j=0}^{n}
be the Lagrange polynomials for
\{1,e^{i\varepsilon},\ldots,e^{in\varepsilon}\}
and
\tilde{L}_{j}(z)=(-1)^{n-j}e^{ijn\varepsilon/2}L_{j}(z)
for
j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
be a phase adjusted version whose phases match those of the corresponding entries of
\bm{x}
. We have

\tilde{L}_{j}(e^{i\theta})=(-1)^{n-j}e^{ijn\varepsilon/2}\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{ik\varepsilon}}{e^{ij\varepsilon}-e^{ik\varepsilon}}=(-1)^{n-j}e^{\frac{in\theta}{2}}\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{\sin\frac{\theta-k\varepsilon}{2}}{\sin\frac{(j-k)\varepsilon}{2}}=e^{\frac{in\theta}{2}}\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{\sin\frac{\theta-k\varepsilon}{2}}{\left|\sin\frac{(j-k)\varepsilon}{2}\right|}.

Let

\ell_{j}(\theta):=\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{\sin\frac{\theta-k\varepsilon}{2}}{\left|\sin\frac{(j-k)\varepsilon}{2}\right|}

for
j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
. Note that

\displaystyle\|V^{-1}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{j,k\in S}\langle(-1)^{n-j}e^{ijn\varepsilon/2}V^{-1}\bm{e}_{j},(-1)^{n-k}e^{ikn\varepsilon/2}V^{-1}\bm{e}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi|S|}\sum_{j,k\in S}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{L}_{j}(e^{i\theta})\overline{\tilde{L}_{k}(e^{i\theta})}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi|S|}\sum_{j,k\in S}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\ell_{j}(\theta)\,\ell_{k}(\theta)\,\textnormal{d}\theta.

Next, we aim to upper bound the contribution from integrating over
I:=[0,\varepsilon n+\varepsilon]\cup[2\pi-\varepsilon,2\pi]
, allowing us to focus on
[n\varepsilon+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon]
, a region where
\ell_{j}(\theta)>0
for all
j\in S
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k\in S}\left|\int_{I}\ell_{j}(\theta)\,\ell_{k}(\theta)\,\textnormal{d}\theta\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j,k\in S}\left(\int_{I}\ell_{j}(\theta)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\int_{I}\ell_{k}(\theta)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq(\xi-1)\sum_{j,k\in S}\left(\int_{n\varepsilon+\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}\ell_{j}(\theta)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\int_{n\varepsilon+\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}\ell_{k}(\theta)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=(\xi-1)\left(\sum_{j\in S}\left(\int_{n\varepsilon+\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}\ell_{j}(\theta)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\xi-1)|S|\sum_{j\in S}\int_{n\varepsilon+\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}\ell_{j}(\theta)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta,

giving the lower bound

\|V^{-1}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1-(\xi-1)|S|}{2\pi|S|}\sum_{j,k\in S}\int_{n\varepsilon+\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}\ell_{j}(\theta)\,\ell_{k}(\theta)\,\textnormal{d}\theta.

In addition, for a fixed
\theta\in[n\varepsilon+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon]
the value of
\ell_{j}(\theta)
for
j\in S
are all bounded below by a constant factor times
\ell_{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}(\theta)
. Note that
\ell_{j}(\theta)
, for
j\in S
, and
\ell_{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}(\theta)
are both the product of sine terms and only disagree in roughly
|S|
many of them. Suppose, without loss of generality, that
j<\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. Then, for
\theta\in[n\varepsilon+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon]
and
j\in S
,

\left|\frac{\ell_{j}(\theta)}{\ell_{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}(\theta)}\right|=\left|\frac{\sin\frac{\theta-\lfloor n/2\rfloor\varepsilon}{2}}{\sin\frac{\theta-j\varepsilon}{2}}\right|\prod_{m=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-j}\left|\frac{\sin\frac{(j+m)\varepsilon}{2}}{\sin\frac{(n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor+m)\varepsilon}{2}}\right|\geq\left(\frac{\sin\frac{j\varepsilon}{2}}{\sin\frac{(n+1-j)\varepsilon}{2}}\right)^{\sqrt{n}+1}.

Taking logarithms of both sides and using the bound

\frac{\log\sin\frac{y}{2}-\log\sin\frac{x}{2}}{y-x}\leq\frac{1}{2}\cot\frac{x}{2}\leq\frac{1}{x}\qquad\text{for}\quad 0<x<y<2\pi,

we obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq\sqrt{n}\\
\theta\in[n\varepsilon+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon]\end{subarray}}\log\left|\frac{\ell_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-m}(\theta)}{\ell_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\theta)}\right|

\displaystyle\geq-(\sqrt{n}+1)\left(\log\sin\left(\frac{(\frac{n}{2}+\sqrt{n}+2)\varepsilon}{2}\right)-\log\sin\left(\frac{(\frac{n}{2}-\sqrt{n}-1)\varepsilon}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{(\sqrt{n}+1)(2\sqrt{n}+3)\varepsilon}{2}\frac{2}{(\frac{n}{2}-\sqrt{n}-1)\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq C

for some fixed constant
C
. Combining this with our previous lower bound, we obtain our desired result

\displaystyle\|V^{-1}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{2C}(1-(\xi-1)|S|)}{2\pi|S|}\sum_{j,k\in S}\int_{n\varepsilon+\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}\ell_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\theta)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2C}(1-(\xi-1)|S|)|S|}{2\pi}\int_{n\varepsilon+\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}\ell_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\theta)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e^{2C}(1-(\xi-1)|S|)|S|}{2\pi\xi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\ell_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\theta)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2C}(1-(\xi-1)|S|)|S|}{\xi}\|V^{-1}\bm{e}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\|_{2}^{2}.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(a,b)\subset[0,\pi]
.

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log{\left|2\sin\left(\frac{a+\frac{b-a}{m}j}{2}\right)\right|}+\frac{\Cl(b)-\Cl(a)}{\frac{b-a}{m}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{b+\frac{b-a}{m}}{a+\frac{b-a}{m}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{3}{2}.

Proof.

Set
\varepsilon=\frac{b-a}{m}
. Let

(3.2)

\begin{split}A:=-\int_{a}^{a+\varepsilon m}\log\left|2\sin\frac{x}{2}\right|\,\textnormal{d}x+\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left|2\sin\frac{a+j\varepsilon}{2}\right|&=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{(j-1)\varepsilon}^{j\varepsilon}\log\left|\frac{\sin\frac{a+x}{2}}{\sin\frac{a+j\varepsilon}{2}}\right|\,\textnormal{d}x.\end{split}

We consider the integral in the summand for each
j
. Note that the function

f_{j}(x):=-\log\left|\frac{\sin\frac{a+x}{2}}{\sin\frac{a+j\varepsilon}{2}}\right|

is decreasing and convex for
x\leq\pi
, so the integral is lower bounded the axis-aligned right triangle with width
\varepsilon
and slope given by the slope of
f_{j}(x)
at
x=j\varepsilon
,

(3.3)

{\int_{(j-1)\varepsilon}^{j\varepsilon}f_{j}(x)\,\textnormal{d}x\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left|f^{\prime}_{j}(j\varepsilon)\right|=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\cot\frac{a+j\varepsilon}{2}.}

It is similarly upper bounded (for
j\geq 2
) by the triangle of width
\varepsilon
and slope given by the slope of
f_{j}(x)
at
x=(j-1)\varepsilon
,

(3.4)

{\int_{(j-1)\varepsilon}^{j\varepsilon}f_{j}(x)\,\textnormal{d}x\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left|f^{\prime}_{j}((j-1)\varepsilon)\right|=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\cot\frac{a+(j-1)\varepsilon}{2}.}

Computing the sum over
j
gives

(3.5)

\begin{split}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\cot\frac{a+j\varepsilon}{2}\leq A&\leq\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}f_{1}(x)\,\textnormal{d}x+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\sum_{j=2}^{m}\cot\frac{a+(j-1)\varepsilon}{2}\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}f_{1}(x)\,\textnormal{d}x+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\cot\frac{a+j\varepsilon}{2}.\end{split}

Notably, the same sum appears on both the left and right hand side. To approximate this sum, we use
1/x-2/\pi\leq\cot x\leq 1/x
for
0<x\leq\pi/2
, giving

-\frac{2m}{\pi}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{2}{a+j\varepsilon}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\cot\frac{a+j\varepsilon}{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{2}{a+j\varepsilon}.

This sum can be expressed exactly in terms of the digamma function
\psi
,

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{2}{a+j\varepsilon}=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\frac{a}{\varepsilon}+j}=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\left(\psi\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}+m+1\right)-\psi\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}+1\right)\right),

which in turn admits a close approximation,
\log x-\frac{1}{x}\leq\psi(x)\leq\log x-\frac{1}{2x}
for
x>0
.
The remaining term to be controlled in the upper bound on
A
is

g(\varepsilon):=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}f_{1}(x)\,\textnormal{d}x=-\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\log\left|\frac{\sin\frac{a+x}{2}}{\sin\frac{a+\varepsilon}{2}}\right|\,\textnormal{d}x.

Note that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}g(\varepsilon)=0
and

g^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cot\left(\frac{a+\varepsilon}{2}\right)\leq 1
, so
g(\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon
.
The upper bound on
A
is therefore

\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\left(\psi\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}+m+1\right)-\psi\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}+1\right)\right)\leq\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\log\left(\frac{a+(m+1)\varepsilon}{a+\varepsilon}\right)+1\right)

and the lower bound is

\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\left(-\frac{2m}{\pi}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\left(\psi\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}+m+1\right)-\psi\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}+1\right)\right)\right)\geq-\frac{m\varepsilon^{2}}{2\pi}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\log\left(\frac{a+(m+1)\varepsilon}{a+\varepsilon}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\right).

Using
m\varepsilon\leq\pi
gives the final result.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(a,b)\subset[0,2\pi)
be such that
\pi\in(a,b)
. Then

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left|2\sin\frac{a+\frac{b-a}{m+1}j}{2}\right|+\frac{\Cl(b)-\Cl(a)}{\frac{b-a}{m+1}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\pi}{a+\frac{b-a}{m+1}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\pi}{2\pi-b+\frac{b-a}{m+1}}\right)\right|\leq 5.

Proof.

Let
m\geq 2
(the case
m=1
follows by inspection),
\varepsilon=\frac{b-a}{m+1}
, and
k=\left\lfloor\frac{\pi-a}{\varepsilon}\right\rfloor
. We may split our sum into two parts:

-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left|2\sin\frac{a+\varepsilon j}{2}\right|=-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log\left|2\sin\frac{a+\varepsilon j}{2}\right|-\sum_{j=1}^{m-k}\log\left|2\sin\frac{2\pi-a-\varepsilon(m+1)+\varepsilon j}{2}\right|.

Lemma

3.2
can be applied to each of these terms. The first term is bounded by

\frac{\Cl(a+k\varepsilon)-\Cl(a)}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{a+(k+1)\varepsilon}{a+\varepsilon}\right)\pm\frac{3}{2}

and the second term is bounded by

\frac{\Cl(2\pi-a-\varepsilon(k+1))-\Cl(2\pi-a-\varepsilon(m+1))}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{2\pi-a-\varepsilon k}{2\pi-a-\varepsilon m}\right)\pm\frac{3}{2}.

Furthermore,
note that
\Cl
is odd and
2\pi
-periodic so
\Cl(2\pi-x)=-\Cl(x)
.
Then since
\pi\in[a+k\varepsilon,a+(k+1)\varepsilon)
and
|\Cl^{\prime}(\theta)|\leq\log 2
for
\theta\in(\pi-\varepsilon,\pi+\varepsilon)\subset(2\arcsin\tfrac{1}{4},2\pi-2\arcsin\tfrac{1}{4})
,

\left|\frac{\Cl(a+\varepsilon k)+\Cl(2\pi-a-\varepsilon(k+1))}{\varepsilon}\right|=\left|\frac{\Cl(a+\varepsilon k)-\Cl(a+\varepsilon(k+1))}{\varepsilon}\right|\leq\log 2.

Additionally,
\log(a+(k+1)\varepsilon)
and
\log(2\pi-a-\varepsilon k)
both lie in
[\log\pi,\log(\pi+\varepsilon)]
,
and so

\left|\log\left(\frac{a+(k+1)\varepsilon}{\pi}\right)\right|,\left|\log\left(\frac{2\pi-a-\varepsilon k}{\pi}\right)\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}.

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
(m+1)\varepsilon<2\pi
. Then

-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left(2\sin\frac{\varepsilon j}{2}\right)=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon m)}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\log\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\varepsilon m\wedge(2\pi-\varepsilon(m+1))\right)+O(1).

Proof.

If
\varepsilon m\leq\pi
, then we may apply

Lemma

3.2
to obtain

(3.6)

\begin{split}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left(2\sin\frac{\varepsilon j}{2}\right)&=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon m)}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(m+1\right)+O(1)\\
&=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon m)}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\varepsilon m\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\varepsilon+O(1).\end{split}

If instead
\varepsilon m>\pi
, we may apply
Lemma

3.3
along with the estimate

\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon m+\varepsilon)-\Cl(\varepsilon m)}{\varepsilon}=-\log(2\pi-(m+1)\varepsilon)+O(1)

in that regime to obtain

(3.7)

\begin{split}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left(2\sin\frac{\varepsilon j}{2}\right)&=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon m+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\varepsilon}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\pi}{2\pi-\varepsilon m}\right)+O(1)\\
&=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon m)}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\log\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(2\pi-(m+1)\varepsilon\right)+O(1).\end{split}

Combining these two inequalities gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
(n+1)\varepsilon<2\pi
. Then

U_{0}(0)=U_{n}(\varepsilon n)=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\left|2\sin\frac{\varepsilon j}{2}\right|=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon n)}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\log\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\varepsilon n\wedge(2\pi-\varepsilon(n+1))\right)+O(1)

and, for
1\leq k\leq n-1
,

(3.8)

\begin{split}U_{k}(\varepsilon k)&=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon k)+\Cl(\varepsilon(n-k))}{\varepsilon}+\log\varepsilon\\
&-\frac{\log\left(\varepsilon k\wedge(2\pi-\varepsilon(k+1))\right)+\log\left(\varepsilon(n-k)\wedge(2\pi-\varepsilon(n-k+1))\right)}{2}+O(1).\end{split}

Proof.

This is a direct application of
Lemma

3.4
for
k=0,n
and, for other
k
, an application to each sum of

(3.9)

{U_{k}(\varepsilon k)=-\sum_{j\neq k}\log\left|2\sin\left(\frac{\varepsilon j-\varepsilon k}{2}\right)\right|=-\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}\log\left|2\sin\left(\frac{\varepsilon j}{2}\right)\right|-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log\left|2\sin\left(\frac{\varepsilon j}{2}\right)\right|.}

∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
(n+2)\varepsilon<2\pi
. Then

U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)=\frac{2\Cl\left(\pi+\frac{\varepsilon(n+2)}{2}\right)}{\varepsilon}+\log\left(2\pi-\varepsilon n\right)+O(1)

and

U\left(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon\right)=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon(n+1))}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\log\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\varepsilon(n+1)\wedge\left(2\pi-\varepsilon(n+2)\right)\right)+O(1).

Proof.

This is a direct application of
Lemma

3.3
for
U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)
and
Lemma

3.4
for
U\left(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon\right)
. For
U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)
, we have

\displaystyle U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{n}\log\left|2\sin\left(\frac{\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi-j\varepsilon}{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\log\left|2\sin\left(\frac{\pi-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\varepsilon+k\varepsilon}{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\Cl(\pi+\frac{\varepsilon(n+2)}{2})-\Cl(\pi-\frac{\varepsilon(n+2)}{2})}{\varepsilon}-\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\pi-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}}\right)+O(1).

By the symmetry
\Cl(\theta)=\Cl(2\pi-\theta)
, we obtain our desired result.

For
U\left(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon\right)
, we have

\displaystyle U(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=0}^{n}\log\left|2\sin\left(\frac{\varepsilon n+\varepsilon-j\varepsilon}{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\log\left|2\sin\left(\frac{k\varepsilon}{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon(n+1))}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\log\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\varepsilon(n+1)\wedge\left(2\pi-\varepsilon(n+2)\right)\right)+O(1).

∎

3.2.
Quadratic upper and lower bounds for
U(\cdot)

Recall our motivation for defining
U(\cdot)
and
U_{k}(\cdot)
was because we can express
\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|
as the exponential

\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|=e^{U_{k}(\varepsilon k)}e^{-U_{k}(\theta)}
, which we need to integrate the square of. Our idea is now to estimate
U(\theta)
by a parabola, so that integrating the exponential resembles a Gaussian integral,

\int_{-b}^{a}e^{C-Mx^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}x=e^{C}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{M}}\cdot\frac{\erf(a\sqrt{M})+\erf(b\sqrt{M})}{2}

We will use the symbols
M_{\text{wide}}=M_{\textnormal{w}}
,
M_{\text{narrow}}=M_{\textnormal{n}}
to denote “wide” and “narrow” estimates for the convexity of
U(\theta)
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
(n+2)\varepsilon<2\pi
,

(3.10)

\begin{split}M_{\textnormal{w}}=U^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right),\quad M_{\textnormal{n}}=2\frac{U\left(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon\right)-U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)}{\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi\right)^{2}},\end{split}

and define the two parabolas

(3.11)

\begin{split}g_{\star}(x)&=\frac{M_{\star}}{2}\left(x-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi\right)^{2}+U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)\end{split}

for
\star\in{\left\{{\textnormal{w},\textnormal{n}}\right\}}
. The polynomial
g_{\textnormal{w}}(\cdot)
is the quadratic Taylor approximation of
U(\cdot)
centered at
\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi
and
g_{\textnormal{n}}(\cdot)
is the parabola intersecting
U(\cdot)
at
\varepsilon n+\varepsilon
,
\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi
, and
2\pi-\varepsilon
.
Then
g_{\textnormal{w}}(x)\leq U(x)
on
x\in\left(\varepsilon n,2\pi\right)
and
g_{\textnormal{n}}(x)\geq U(x)
on
x\in\left(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon\right)
.

Proof.

Consider the Taylor series for
U(\cdot)
centered at
\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi
.
U(\cdot)
is the finite sum of analytic functions so is itself analytic. By examining the locations of its poles, we see that the Taylor series converges on
(\varepsilon n,2\pi)
. By the symmetry
U(x)=U(\varepsilon n-x)
, all the odd terms vanish, and so we have

(3.12)

{U(x)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{U^{(2\ell)}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)}{(2\ell)!}\left(x-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi\right)^{2\ell}.}

Note that
f^{(2\ell)}(x)>0
for all
\ell>0
, so all of the non-constant coefficients of
eq.

3.12
are positive for
x\in(\varepsilon n,2\pi)
. Consequently,
U(x)
is lower bounded by the quadratic Taylor polynomial, which is exactly
g_{\textnormal{w}}(x)
. On the other hand, for
x\in(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon)
we have

(3.13)

\begin{split}U(x)&=U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)+\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{U^{(2\ell)}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)}{(2\ell)!}\left(\frac{x-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi}{\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi}\right)^{2\ell}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi\right)^{2\ell}\\
&\leq U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)+\left(\frac{x-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi}{\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi}\right)^{2}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{U^{(2\ell)}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)}{(2\ell)!}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi\right)^{2\ell}\\
&=U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)+\left(\frac{x-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi}{\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi}\right)^{2}\left(U(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon)-U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)\right)\end{split}

which is exactly
g_{\textnormal{n}}(x)
.∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
n\varepsilon<2\pi
. Then

\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\tan\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\leq M_{\textnormal{w}}\leq\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\tan\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}.

Proof.

The derivative of
\tan(\cdot)
is
\sec^{2}(\cdot)
so

2\tan\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\right)=\int_{-\varepsilon n/4}^{\varepsilon n/4}\sec^{2}\left(t\right)\,\textnormal{d}t.

The integral can be upper and lower bounded by the Riemann sums

2\tan\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sec^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon j}{2}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sec^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}{2}\right)

and

2\tan\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\right)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\sec^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon j}{2}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sec^{2}\left(0\right)

Now note that

M_{\textnormal{w}}=U^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi-\varepsilon j\right)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sec^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon j}{2}\right).

Plugging this in gives

(3.14)

\begin{split}2\tan\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\right)&\leq 2\varepsilon U^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sec^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}{2}\right)\\
&\leq 2\varepsilon U^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.\end{split}

and

(3.15)

\begin{split}2\tan\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\right)&\geq 2\varepsilon U^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sec^{2}\left(-\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sec^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sec^{2}\left(0\right)\\
&=2\varepsilon U^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)-\varepsilon\sec^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.\end{split}

Dividing by
2\varepsilon
and using
1-\sec^{2}(x)=-\tan^{2}(x)
gives the final result
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
n\varepsilon<2\pi-2\varepsilon^{1/2}
, and

\alpha:=\frac{\varepsilon n}{2\pi}
.
There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}
such that

C_{1}\log\frac{2}{\alpha}\leq\frac{M_{\textnormal{n}}}{M_{\textnormal{w}}}\leq C_{2}\log\frac{2}{\alpha}.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.6
and
Proposition

3.1
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon M_{\textnormal{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\varepsilon}{\pi^{2}(1-\alpha-\varepsilon/\pi)^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon(n+1))-2\Cl(\pi+\frac{\varepsilon(n+2)}{2})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{((\varepsilon(n+1))\wedge(2\pi-\varepsilon(n+2)))(2\pi-\varepsilon n)^{2}}\right)+O(1)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\Cl(\varepsilon n/2)}{\pi^{2}(1-\alpha-\varepsilon/\pi)^{2}}\left(1+\frac{O(\varepsilon\log(\varepsilon\alpha(1-\alpha-\varepsilon/\pi))}{2\Cl(\varepsilon n/2)}\right).

By the bound

\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{x}{1-x}\leq\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2}x\right)\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{x}{1-x}

for
x\in(0,1)
and
Lemma

3.8
,

\varepsilon M_{\textnormal{w}}=C\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}

for some constant
C\geq 2/\pi
. In addition, by
Proposition

3.1
,
\Cl(\varepsilon n/2)=\hat{C}\alpha(1-\alpha)\log(2/\alpha)
for some
\hat{C}\in[1/\pi,2/5]
. Combining these estimates and noting that
1-\alpha\geq\varepsilon^{1/2}/\pi
, we obtain our desired result

\displaystyle\frac{M_{\textnormal{n}}}{M_{\textnormal{w}}}=\frac{4\hat{C}}{\pi^{2}C}\log\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha-\varepsilon/\pi}\right)^{2}\left(1+\frac{O(\varepsilon\log(\varepsilon\alpha(1-\alpha-\varepsilon/\pi))}{\alpha(1-\alpha)\log(2/\alpha)}\right).

∎

3.3.
Integrating a Lagrange polynomial with equispaced points

Here we produce tight estimates for integrals and sums of integrals of
\left|L_{k}\big(z;\{e^{ij\varepsilon}\}_{j=0}^{n}\big)\right|^{2}
. Throughout
Section

3.3
, let
\varepsilon>0
,
\alpha:=\frac{\varepsilon n}{2\pi}
, and
\beta:=\frac{\varepsilon k}{2\pi}
, and assume throughout that
\alpha<1-\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. Note that
0<\beta<\alpha<1
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
c_{a,b}=\sup_{x\in(a,b)}\left|\frac{1}{2}\csc\frac{x-\varepsilon k}{2}\right|
. Then

\int_{a}^{b}e^{-2U_{k}(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x\leq c_{a,b}^{2}e^{-2U\left({\varepsilon n}/2+\pi\right)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{2}\varepsilon}{2}\cdot\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}

for any interval
(a,b)\subset(\varepsilon n,2\pi)
, and

\int_{a}^{b}e^{-2U_{k}(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x\geq e^{-2U\left({\varepsilon n}/2+\pi\right)}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{C\log\frac{2}{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}

for any interval
(a,b)=(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon)
, for some absolute constant
C
.

Proof.

We start by definition of
U(x)=U_{k}(x)+f(x-\varepsilon k)
,

(3.16)

\begin{split}I_{a,b}:=\int_{a}^{b}e^{-2U_{k}(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x=\int_{a}^{b}\left|\frac{1}{2}\csc\frac{x-\varepsilon k}{2}\right|^{2}e^{-2U(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x.\end{split}

We obtain simple upper and lower bounds by pulling the
\csc(\cdot)
term outside the integral,

\frac{1}{4}\int_{a}^{b}e^{-2U(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x\leq I_{a,b}\leq c_{a,b}^{2}\int_{a}^{b}e^{-2U(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x.

For the upper bound, if
(a,b)\subset(\varepsilon n,2\pi)
, then we can apply the lower bound on
U(\cdot)
from
Lemma

3.7
,

(3.17)

\begin{split}\int_{a}^{b}e^{-2U(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x\leq\int_{a}^{b}e^{-2g_{\textnormal{w}}(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-2g_{\textnormal{w}}(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x=e^{-2U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{M_{\textnormal{w}}}}.\end{split}

By
Lemma

3.8
, we have
\frac{1}{M_{\textnormal{w}}}\leq\frac{1}{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\tan\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}}\leq\frac{\pi\varepsilon}{2}\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}
.
For the lower bound, if
(a,b)\subset(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon)
, we can apply the upper bound on
U(\cdot)
from
Lemma

3.7
,

\int_{a}^{b}e^{-2U(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x\geq\int_{a}^{b}e^{-2g_{\textnormal{n}}(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x=e^{-2U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{M_{\textnormal{n}}}}\frac{\erf\left(\sqrt{M_{\textnormal{n}}}\left(b-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi\right)\right)+\erf\left(\sqrt{M_{\textnormal{n}}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi-a\right)\right)}{2}

If
a=\varepsilon n+\varepsilon
and
b=2\pi-\varepsilon
, then

(3.18)

\begin{split}\frac{\erf\left(\sqrt{M_{\textnormal{n}}}\left(b-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi\right)\right)}{\sqrt{M_{\textnormal{n}}}}=\frac{\erf\left(\sqrt{M_{\textnormal{n}}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi-a\right)\right)}{\sqrt{M_{\textnormal{n}}}}&=\frac{\erf\left(\sqrt{M_{\textnormal{n}}}\left(\pi-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\right)}{\sqrt{M_{\textnormal{n}}}}\\
&\geq\sqrt{\frac{\left(1-\alpha-\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\right)}{\left(1-\alpha-\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\right)M_{\textnormal{n}}+\frac{1}{\pi}}}.\end{split}

By
Lemma

3.9
and
Lemma

3.8
,

(3.19)

\begin{split}M_{\textnormal{n}}\leq C\log\frac{2}{\alpha}M_{\textnormal{w}}&\leq C\log\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\tan\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\right).\\
&\leq C\log\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\pi}{2\varepsilon}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}\right).\end{split}

Plugging this in and rearranging gives

(3.20)

\begin{split}\sqrt{\frac{\left(1-\alpha-\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\right)}{\left(1-\alpha-\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\right)M_{\textnormal{n}}+\frac{1}{\pi}}}&\geq\sqrt{\frac{4(1-\alpha)\varepsilon}{2\pi\alpha C\log\frac{2}{\alpha}+\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\alpha}C\log\frac{2}{\alpha}+\frac{4\varepsilon}{\pi}\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha-\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}}}}\\
&=\sqrt{4\varepsilon\cdot\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\left(2\pi+\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}\right)C\log\frac{2}{\alpha}+\frac{4\varepsilon}{\pi\alpha}\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha-\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}}}}.\end{split}

Recall that by assumption
1-\alpha\geq\varepsilon
, so the final result follows.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Set
\gamma=(\alpha-\beta)\wedge\beta+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}
.
For absolute constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
,

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq e^{2U_{k}(\varepsilon k)-2U\left({\varepsilon n}/2+\pi\right)}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{C_{1}}\cdot\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\geq e^{2U_{k}(\varepsilon k)-2U\left({\varepsilon n}/2+\pi\right)}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{C_{2}\log\frac{2}{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}

Proof.

By definition,

(3.21)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}\left(e^{ix}\right)\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}x&=e^{2U_{k}(\varepsilon k)}\int_{-\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}e^{-2U_{k}(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x\\
&=e^{2U_{k}(\varepsilon k)}\left(\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon n+\varepsilon}e^{-2U_{k}(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x+\int_{\varepsilon n+\varepsilon}^{2\pi-\varepsilon}e^{-2U_{k}(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x\right).\end{split}

The second integral is both upper and lower bounded by
Lemma

3.10
. The first integral is lower bounded by 0, and upper bounded by

(3.22)

\begin{split}\left(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon-(-\varepsilon)\right)e^{2g_{\textnormal{w}}(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon)}&=\varepsilon(n+2)e^{-2U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)-M_{\textnormal{w}}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi\right)^{2}}\\
&\leq\varepsilon(n+2)e^{-2U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\tan\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}-\pi\right)^{2}}\\
&\leq 2\pi e^{-2U\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+\pi\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{3}}{2}\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi(1-\alpha)}\right)^{2}\right).\end{split}

By assumption
1-\alpha\geq\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, so the expression inside the exponential is further bounded by

\frac{\pi^{3}}{2}\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi(1-\alpha)}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{4}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\wedge n,

resulting in the overall upper bound of

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq e^{2U_{k}(\varepsilon k)-2U\left({\varepsilon n}/2+\pi\right)}\left(c_{\varepsilon n+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{2}\varepsilon}{2}\cdot\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}+2\pi\exp\left(-\left(\tfrac{4}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\wedge n\right)\right)\right)

for

\frac{1}{2}\leq c_{\varepsilon n+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon}=\sup_{x\in(\varepsilon n+\varepsilon,2\pi-\varepsilon)}\left|\frac{1}{2}\csc\frac{x-\varepsilon k}{2}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{2}\csc\frac{((n-k)\wedge k)\varepsilon+\varepsilon}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi\gamma(1-\gamma)}.

In particular, for all choices of
k
, the first term dominates the
2\pi\exp\left(-\left(\tfrac{4}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\wedge n\right)\right)
term.
∎

The same analysis provides an estimate for the quantity

.

Lemma 3.12
.

Proof.

∎

Using

3.11
, we are now prepared to estimate the sum of integrals of Lagrange polynomials.

Lemma 3.13
.

For an absolute constant
C
,

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{2}}{(1-\alpha)^{6}}\exp\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\left(\Cl(\pi\alpha)-\Cl(\pi(1+\alpha))\right)\right).

Proof.

For simplicity, we abuse notation slightly by letting
C
denote a sequence of absolute constants that differs in value at each use.
Set
\gamma=(\alpha-\beta)\wedge\beta+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}
. Rearranging
Lemma

3.11
gives

(3.23)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq\frac{\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{C}\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}}{e^{2U((\alpha+1)\pi)}}\frac{e^{2U_{k}(2\pi\beta)}}{\gamma^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}}.\end{split}

which can be combined with the bound on
U_{k}(\varepsilon k)
from

3.5
,

(3.24)

\begin{split}U_{k}(2\pi\beta)&=\frac{\Cl(2\pi\beta)+\Cl(2\pi(\alpha-\beta))}{\varepsilon}+\log\varepsilon\\
&\quad-\frac{\log(\beta\wedge(1-\beta-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}))+\log((\alpha-\beta)\wedge(1-\alpha+\beta-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}))}{2}+O(1)\end{split}

for

, i.e.

. This results in

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta&\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{\frac{5}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}}{e^{2U((\alpha+1)\pi)}}\\
&\cdot\frac{1}{\left(\beta\wedge\left(1-\beta-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\right))\right)\left((\alpha-\beta)\wedge\left(1-\alpha+\beta-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\right))\right)}\frac{e^{2\frac{\Cl(2\pi\beta)+\Cl(2\pi(\alpha-\beta))}{\varepsilon}}}{\gamma^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}}\\
&\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{\frac{5}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}}{e^{2U((\alpha+1)\pi)}}\cdot{e^{\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\Cl(\pi\alpha)}}\\
&\cdot\frac{1}{\left(\beta\wedge\left(1-\beta-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\right))\right)\left((\alpha-\beta)\wedge\left(1-\alpha+\beta-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}\right))\right)}\frac{e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\varepsilon}\cot\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)\left(\beta-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}}}{\gamma^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}}\\
&\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{\frac{5}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}}{e^{2U((\alpha+1)\pi)}}\cdot\frac{e^{\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\Cl(\pi\alpha)}}{(1-\alpha)^{4}}\cdot\frac{e^{-\frac{8\pi}{\varepsilon}\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\left(\beta-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}}}{\beta^{3}(\alpha-\beta)^{3}}\end{split}

where we used a quadratic approximation of the dominant term in the exponent,

\frac{\Cl(2\pi\beta)+\Cl(2\pi(\alpha-\beta))}{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{2\Cl(\pi\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\cot(\pi\alpha/2)(2\pi\beta-\pi\alpha)^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

Thus

(3.26)

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta&\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{\frac{5}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}}{e^{2U((\alpha+1)\pi)}}\frac{e^{\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\Cl(\pi\alpha)}}{(1-\alpha)^{4}}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{e^{-\frac{8\pi}{\varepsilon}\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\left(\beta-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}}}{\beta^{3}(\alpha-\beta)^{3}}.\end{split}

When
\beta\in\left(\frac{1}{3}\alpha,\frac{2}{3}\alpha\right)
, observe the denominator is lower bounded by
\frac{8}{9^{3}}\alpha^{6}
, so the sum can be approximated by the Gaussian integral. For the other
\beta
, the exponential term is upper bounded by
e^{-\frac{8\pi}{\varepsilon}(1-\alpha)\alpha\frac{1}{36}}
, and the sum can be bounded by a
p
-series. This gives

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left|L_{k}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{\frac{5}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}}{e^{2U((\alpha+1)\pi)}}\frac{e^{\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\Cl(\pi\alpha)}}{(1-\alpha)^{4}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{1-\alpha}}+\frac{e^{c\alpha(1-\alpha)/\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\frac{e^{\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\Cl(\pi\alpha)}}{(1-\alpha)^{4}}e^{-2U((\alpha+1)\pi)}.

The
k=0
and
k=n
(i.e.,
\beta=0,\alpha
), terms are bounded via
Lemma

3.5

e^{2U_{0}(0)}+e^{2U_{n}(\varepsilon n)}=\frac{C\varepsilon}{\alpha\wedge(1-\alpha)}e^{2\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon n)}{\varepsilon}},

so these terms are negligible in the final sum.
∎

Using the connection between Vandermonde matrices and Lagrange interpolating polynomials (

4.1.
Gap satisfies
2\pi-n\varepsilon\leq 2\varepsilon

When the nodes are nearly equispaced around the entire unit circle, a number of our estimates from
Section

3
are no longer valid, requiring alternate techniques. However, in this regime, we may use alternate techniques that exploit our matrix’s closeness to a Fourier matrix.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(z_{j},z_{k})\geq\varepsilon>0
, and
2\pi-n\varepsilon\leq 2\varepsilon
. Then

\log\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{-1}\right\|_{2}=\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi\pm O\left(\log(2\pi-\varepsilon n)\right).

Proof.

First we note that

\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\left|\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi\right|=\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\left|\int_{\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi\right|\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\left|\int_{0}^{\varepsilon/2}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi\right|=O(\log\varepsilon).

What remains is to prove that
\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{-1}\right\|_{2}
is bounded above by a polynomial in
n
. We note that
\varepsilon\geq\delta:=2\pi/(n+2)
, and so, by
Lemma

2.6
,

\left|\det\left(V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\right)\right|\geq\left|\det\left(V(1,e^{i\delta},\ldots,e^{in\delta})\right)\right|.

The matrix
V(1,e^{i\delta},\ldots,e^{in\delta})
is the leading
(n+1)\times(n+1)
submatrix of the
(n+2)
dimensional discrete Fourier matrix, and so

V(1,e^{i\delta},\ldots,e^{in\delta})^{*}V(1,e^{i\delta},\ldots,e^{in\delta})=(n+2)I-\bm{v}\bm{v}^{*}

for some vector
\bm{v}
with
\|\bm{v}\|_{2}=\sqrt{n+1}
. Therefore,

|\det\left(V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\right)|^{2}\geq|\det(V(1,e^{i\delta},\ldots,e^{in\delta}))|^{2}=(n+2)^{n+1}(n+2-\bm{v}^{*}\bm{v})=(n+2)^{n+1}.

In addition,

\mathrm{trace}\left(V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{*}V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\right)=(n+1)^{2}.

Therefore, by the AM-GM inequality,

\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{-1}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}^{2}(V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}))}{\prod_{j=1}^{n+1}\sigma_{j}^{2}(V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}))}\leq\frac{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}^{2}(V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}))\right)^{n}}{(n+2)^{n+1}}\leq\frac{\left(\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{n}\right)^{n}}{(n+2)^{n+1}}.

The quantity
\left(\tfrac{(n+1)^{2}}{n(n+2)}\right)^{n}
is at most
4/3
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and so

\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{-1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n+2}\left(\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{n(n+2)}\right)^{n}\leq\frac{4}{3(n+2)},

completing the proof.
∎

4.2.
Gap satisfies
2\varepsilon<2\pi-n\varepsilon<2\varepsilon^{1/2}

When our matrix is at least some bounded distance away from being a Fourier matrix, but still has nearly equally spaced nodes, only half of our estimates from
Section

3
apply. In particular, while our estimates for the maximum of a Lagrange polynomial are valid, we do not have enough of a gap to produce estimates of integrals. However, because the gap is so small, estimates of the maximum over the unit circle suffice.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(z_{j},z_{k})\geq\varepsilon>0
, and
2\varepsilon<2\pi-n\varepsilon<2\varepsilon^{1/2}
. Then

\log\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{-1}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi\pm O\left(\log(2\pi-\varepsilon n)\right),

with equality when
z_{j}=e^{ij\varepsilon}
for
j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof.

In the regime
2\varepsilon<2\pi-n\varepsilon<2\varepsilon^{1/2}
,
\log n
and
\log\varepsilon
are
O(\log(2\pi-n\varepsilon))
, and so we need not concern ourselves with logarithmic error terms. By
Lemmas

2.2
and
2.5
,

\displaystyle\log\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{-1}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}\log\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{k}\big(e^{i\theta};\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\big)\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\right)+O(\log n)

\displaystyle=\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}\max_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}\log\left(\left|L_{k}\big(z;\{z_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}\big)\right|\right)+O(\log n)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}\max_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}\log\left(\left|L_{k}\big(z;\{e^{ij\varepsilon}\}_{j=0}^{n}\big)\right|\right)+O(\log n).

What remains is to estimate

\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}\max_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}\log\left(\left|L_{k}\big(z;\{e^{ij\varepsilon}\}_{j=0}^{n}\big)\right|\right)=\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}\left(U_{k}(k\varepsilon)-\min_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}\left(U(\theta)+\log\left|e^{i\theta}-e^{ik\varepsilon}\right|\right)\right)

exactly, up to logarithmic error. Because
2\pi-n\varepsilon>2\varepsilon
, clearly
\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}U_{k}(k\varepsilon)=U_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\varepsilon)
and
\min_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}U(\theta)
is achieved in the interval
(n\varepsilon,2\pi)
. Furthermore,
U(\theta)
is a sum of convex functions, so it is also convex on
(n\varepsilon,2\pi)
. By symmetry about
n\varepsilon/2+\pi
,
\min_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}U(\theta)=U(n\varepsilon/2+\pi)
.

By
Lemmas

3.5
and
3.6
and
2\varepsilon<2\pi-n\varepsilon<2\varepsilon^{1/2}
,

\displaystyle U_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\varepsilon)-U(n\varepsilon/2+\pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Cl(\varepsilon\lfloor n/2\rfloor)+\Cl(\varepsilon\lceil n/2\rceil)-2\Cl(\pi+\varepsilon(n+2)/2)}{\varepsilon}+O(\log(2\pi-n\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\left(\Cl(\varepsilon n/2)-\Cl(\pi+\varepsilon n/2)\right)+O(\log(2\pi-n\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi+O(\log(2\pi-n\varepsilon)).

Finally, note that the difference between
U_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\varepsilon)-U(n\varepsilon/2+\pi)
and the maximum over
k
and
\theta
is
O(1)
, completing the proof.
∎

4.3.
Gap satisfies
2\pi-n\varepsilon>2\varepsilon^{1/2}

Here we consider the final, general case where
n\varepsilon
is bounded away from
2\pi
by
\varepsilon^{1/2}

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}(z_{j},z_{k})\geq\varepsilon>0
, and

Furthermore,

\log\left\|V(1,e^{i\varepsilon},\ldots,e^{in\varepsilon})^{-1}\right\|=\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi-\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\pm O(\log(n\varepsilon(2\pi-n\varepsilon))).

Proof.

First we consider the upper bound. By
Lemmas

2.2
,
2.5
and
3.5

\displaystyle\log\left\|V(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}\max_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}\,\log\left|L_{k}\big(z;\{e^{ij\varepsilon}\}_{j=0}^{n}\big)\right|+\frac{1}{2}\log n

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}U_{k}(k\varepsilon)-U(n\varepsilon/2+\pi)+\frac{1}{2}\log n+O(\log(2\pi-n\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=U_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)-U(n\varepsilon/2+\pi)+\frac{1}{2}\log n+O(\log(n\varepsilon(2\pi-n\varepsilon))),

and, by
Lemmas

3.5
and
3.6
,

\displaystyle U_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)-U(n\varepsilon/2+\pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\left(\Cl(\varepsilon\lfloor n/2\rfloor)-\Cl(\pi+\varepsilon n/2)\right)+\log\varepsilon+O(\log(n\varepsilon(2\pi-n\varepsilon)))

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon n}{4}}\log\cot\phi\,\textnormal{d}\phi+\log\varepsilon+O(\log(n\varepsilon(2\pi-n\varepsilon))),

completing the proof of the upper bound. For our desired equality,

2.2
and
3.13
give the upper bound

\log\left\|V(1,e^{i\varepsilon},\ldots,e^{in\varepsilon})^{-1}\right\|\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\left(\Cl(n\varepsilon/2)-\Cl(\pi+n\varepsilon/2)\right)+\log\varepsilon+O(\log(n\varepsilon(2\pi-n\varepsilon))).

What remains is to produce a matching lower bound.
By
Lemma

3.12
, the quantity
\xi-1
in
Lemma

2.7
is exponentially small in
n
. Therefore, by
Lemmas

2.7
and
3.11
,

\displaystyle\log\left\|V(1,e^{i\varepsilon},\ldots,e^{in\varepsilon})^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\log n+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|L_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(e^{i\theta})\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\log n+\frac{1}{4}\log\varepsilon+U_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(\varepsilon\lfloor n/2\rfloor)-U(\varepsilon n/2+\pi)+O(\log(n\varepsilon(2\pi-n\varepsilon)))

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\left(\Cl(n\varepsilon/2)-\Cl(\pi+n\varepsilon/2)\right)+\log\varepsilon+O(\log(n\varepsilon(2\pi-n\varepsilon))),

completing the proof.
∎

5.
Conditioning for any polynomials and any measures

Here we prove
Theorem

1.5
using two lemmas. The first lemma (
Lemma

5.1
) bounds the difference in logarithmic potential between two measures close in KS-distance. The second lemma (
Lemma

5.2
), using the first, produces tight bounds for the maximum value of a Lagrange polynomial in terms of a logarithmic potential. Combining this with the previously established connection between Vandermonde-like matrices and Lagrange polynomials (
Lemma

2.3
) completes the proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mu_{c}
and
\mu_{d}
be two probability measures supported on a disk of diameter
R
.
Fix any
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Pick
\beta,\rho>0
and

\varepsilon\leq\min\left({\operatorname{dist}}(z,\supp(\mu_{d})),\left(\frac{\beta}{\rho}\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{d},\mu_{c})\right)^{1/\beta}\right).

If

\sup_{r\in(0,\varepsilon)}\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mu_{c}(\ball(\zeta,r))}{r^{\beta}}\leq\rho.

Then

\left|U^{\mu_{d}}(z)-U^{\mu_{c}}(z)\right|\leq\log\left(\frac{eR}{\varepsilon}\right)\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{d},\mu_{c}).

Proof.

Let
(X)_{+}:=\max(X,0)
and
\Omega
be the interior of
\ball(z,\varepsilon)
.
For probability measures
\mu
, we can decompose

U^{\mu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\frac{1}{\left|z-\zeta\right|}\,\textnormal{d}\mu(\zeta)=\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{\varepsilon}{\left|z-\zeta\right|}\,\textnormal{d}\mu(\zeta)-\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\log\frac{\left|z-\zeta\right|}{\varepsilon}\right)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\mu(\zeta).

This can be applied to
\mu_{c}
and
\mu_{d}
along with the triangle inequality to obtain

(5.1)

\begin{split}\left|U^{\mu_{d}}(z)-U^{\mu_{c}}(z)\right|&\leq\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{\varepsilon}{\left|z-\zeta\right|}\,\textnormal{d}\mu_{d}(\zeta)+\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{\varepsilon}{\left|z-\zeta\right|}\,\textnormal{d}\mu_{c}(\zeta)\\
&+\left|\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\log\frac{\left|z-\zeta\right|}{\varepsilon}\right)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\mu_{c}(\zeta)-\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\log\frac{\left|z-\zeta\right|}{\varepsilon}\right)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\mu_{d}(\zeta)\right|.\end{split}

The first term vanishes since the support of
\mu_{d}
misses
\Omega
. For the second term, observe that

(5.2)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\log\frac{\varepsilon}{\left|z-\zeta\right|}\,\textnormal{d}\mu_{c}(\zeta)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu_{c}\left({\left\{{\zeta:\log\frac{\varepsilon}{\left|z-\zeta\right|}\geq t}\right\}}\right)\,\textnormal{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu_{c}\left(\ball(z,\varepsilon e^{-t})\right)\,\textnormal{d}t\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho\varepsilon^{\beta}e^{-t\beta}\,\textnormal{d}t\\
&={\rho\varepsilon^{\beta}}/\beta\\
&\leq\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{d},\mu_{c}).\end{split}

For the third term, note that

(5.3)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\log\frac{\left|z-\zeta\right|}{\varepsilon}\right)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\mu(\zeta)&=\int_{0}^{\log(R/\varepsilon)}\mu\left({\left\{{\zeta:\log\frac{\left|z-\zeta\right|}{\varepsilon}\geq t}\right\}}\right)\,\textnormal{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{\log(R/\varepsilon)}\mu\left(\mathbb{C}\backslash\ball\left(z,\varepsilon e^{t}\right)\right)\,\textnormal{d}t\end{split}

which when applied to
\mu_{c}
and
\mu_{d}
gives a bound of

(5.4)

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{0}^{\log(R/\varepsilon)}\biggr(\mu_{d}\left(\mathbb{C}\backslash\ball\left(z,\varepsilon e^{t}\right)\right)-\mu_{c}\left(\mathbb{C}\backslash\ball\left(z,\varepsilon e^{t}\right)\right)\biggr)\,\textnormal{d}t\right|\\
&\leq\log(R/\varepsilon)\sup_{0\leq t\leq\log(R/\varepsilon)}\biggr|\mu_{d}\left(\ball\left(z,\varepsilon e^{t}\right)\right)-\mu_{c}\left(\ball\left(z,\varepsilon e^{t}\right)\right)\biggr|\\
&\leq\log(R/\varepsilon)\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{d},\mu_{c}).\end{split}

Adding
eqs.

5.2
and
5.4
gives the result.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix
S={\left\{{z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}}\right\}}\subset\mathbb{C}
and set
\varepsilon=\min_{j\neq k}\left|z_{j}-z_{k}\right|
.
Let
\mu
be a probability measure satisfying the conditions of
Theorem

1.5
.

Set
\Delta=\sup_{z\in\supp(\mu)}U^{\mu}(z)-\inf_{z\in\supp(\nu)}U^{\mu}(z)
. Put
s=\min(\varepsilon,\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu))
. Then

(5.5)

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq k\leq n}\sup_{z\in\supp(\nu)}

\displaystyle\frac{\log L_{k}(z)}{n}\leq\Delta+O\left(\log\left(1/s\right)\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu)\right).

(5.6)

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq k\leq n}\sup_{z\in\supp(\nu)}

\displaystyle\frac{\log L_{k}(z)}{n}\geq\Delta-O\left(\log\left(1/s\right)\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu)+\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu)^{\alpha/\beta}\right)

where the Ohs suppress dependency on
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},R,\beta,\alpha
.

Proof.

Denote

\mu_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq k}\delta_{z_{j}},\quad\mu_{S}=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\delta_{z_{j}}.

and

z_{+}=\argmax\limits_{z\in\supp(\mu)}U^{\mu}(z),\quad z_{-}=\argmin\limits_{z\in\supp(\nu)}U^{\mu}(z),\quad z_{-}^{(k)}=\argmin\limits_{z\in\supp(\nu)}U^{\mu_{k}}(z).

Observe by definition that

\sup_{z\in\supp(\nu)}\frac{\log L_{k}(z)}{n}=U^{\mu_{k}}(z_{k})-U^{\mu_{k}}(z_{-}^{(k)}).

We claim that

(5.7)

{\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu)\leq\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{k},\mu)\leq 2\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu).}

To see this, note that condition (3) implies
\mu({\left\{{z}\right\}})=0
for any
z
. In particular, we have
\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{k},\mu_{S})=\frac{1}{n+1}\leq\min\left(\textnormal{KS}(\mu,\mu_{k}),\textnormal{KS}(\mu,\mu_{S})\right)
.
eq.

5.7
follows by the triangle inequality. Now set

\varepsilon^{\prime}=\min\left(\varepsilon,(\beta\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu)/2\rho_{1})^{1/\beta}\right)

and observe that
\varepsilon^{\prime}
satisfies the hypothesis of
Lemma

5.1
for
\mu_{d}=\mu_{k}
,
\mu_{c}=\mu
, and
z=z_{k}
.
Define
\delta(r)=2\log\left(\frac{eR}{r}\right)\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu)
so that
Lemma

5.1
along with
eq.

5.7
and the definition of
z_{\pm}
implies

(5.8)

\begin{split}U^{\mu_{k}}(z_{k})\leq U^{\mu}(z_{k})+\log\left(\frac{eR}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{k},\mu)\leq U^{\mu}(z_{k})+\delta(\varepsilon^{\prime})\leq U^{\mu}(z_{+})+\delta(\varepsilon^{\prime})\end{split}

and also

(5.9)

\begin{split}U^{\mu_{k}}(z_{-}^{(k)})\geq U^{\mu}(z_{-}^{(k)})-\log\left(\frac{eR}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{k},\mu)\geq U^{\mu}(z_{-}^{(k)})-\delta(\varepsilon^{\prime})\geq U^{\mu}(z_{-})-\delta(\varepsilon^{\prime}).\end{split}

Noting that
\log(R/{\varepsilon^{\prime}})=O\left(\log\frac{1}{\min(\varepsilon,\textnormal{KS}(\mu_{S},\mu))}\right)
gives the first inequality
eq.

5.5
.
For second inequality, we start by again applying
Lemma

5.1
,

(5.10)

\begin{split}\max_{0\leq k\leq n}U^{\mu_{k}}(z_{k})&\geq\max_{0\leq k\leq n}U^{\mu}(z_{k})-\delta(\varepsilon^{\prime})\\
&\geq U^{\mu}(z_{+})-\delta(\varepsilon^{\prime})-C\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\left|z_{k}-z_{+}\right|^{\alpha}\\
&=U^{\mu}(z_{+})-\delta(\varepsilon^{\prime})-C{\operatorname{dist}}(z_{+},S)^{\alpha}.\end{split}

Notice that

\mu_{S}\left(\ball(z_{+},{\operatorname{dist}}(z_{+},S))\right)=0

so

\textnormal{KS}(\mu,\mu_{S})\geq\mu\left(\ball(z_{+},{\operatorname{dist}}(z_{+},S))\right)\geq\rho_{2}{\operatorname{dist}}(z_{+},S)^{\beta}.

Applying
Lemma

5.1
one last time,

(5.11)

\begin{split}U^{\mu_{k}}(z_{-}^{(k)})\leq U^{\mu_{k}}(a)&\leq U^{\mu}(a)+\delta({\operatorname{dist}}(a,S))\\
&\leq U^{\mu}(z_{-})+\delta({\operatorname{dist}}(a,S))+C\left|a-z_{-}\right|^{\alpha}.\end{split}

Our final step is to properly select
a
. We pick
a
to be any element of the set

\ball(z_{-},(n+1)^{1/2}r)\backslash\bigcup_{j=0}^{n}\ball(z,r)

for
r=\frac{1}{2R}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}
, which is nonempty by considering the areas of each ball.
Since
S
