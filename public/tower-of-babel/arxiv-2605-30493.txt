The Markup falsification Adaptative Set
Santiago Acerenza, Néstor Gandelman

Abstract
. In this paper we provide a constructive way for researchers to salvage the classic 11 markup recovery procedure when falsified. To do this, we consider continuous relaxations of the standard assumptions behind markup estimation. By computing the values of the markup as a function of the relaxations across the set of non-falsified models, we obtain an identified set for the markup which generalizes the standard baseline markup estimand to account for possible falsification without the need to impose additional assumptions. We illustrate our results using Chilean data from 29 .



Keywords
: markup, falsification, sensitivity analysis, partial identification.

1.
Introduction

Markup estimation aims to recover the gap between prices and marginal costs at the firm or product level, a key object for studying market power. Empirical work on firm-level market power has developed around two primary methodological approaches. The demand approach
(
6
;
5
)
relies on price and quantity data to infer marginal costs under alternative assumptions about firms’ profit-maximizing behavior. In contrast, the production approach does not require specifying demand systems or detailed competitive interactions. This method, which traces back to
16
, was formalized and brought to prominence by
11
.

Within the production approach, markups are inferred from firms’ cost minimization conditions. Under standard assumptions, the markup can be expressed as the ratio of the output elasticity of a variable input to that input’s revenue share. This approach has become widely used because it avoids the need to estimate demand systems and instead relies on the consistent estimation of production functions and input elasticities, making it particularly attractive in settings with detailed firm-level data.

When more than one variable input is available, the assumptions required to recover markups imply a set of testable restrictions on observable data. This feature renders the model empirically falsifiable, as these restrictions can be directly assessed in the data. More generally, model falsifiability refers to the property of a theoretical or structural model whereby it generates testable restrictions on observable data that could, in principle, be contradicted. A model is falsifiable if its assumptions imply relationships—such as moment conditions, inequalities, or functional forms—that must hold in the data; failure of these implications constitutes evidence against the model. In empirical economics, falsifiability is crucial because it disciplines model selection: rather than fitting any pattern ex post, a falsifiable model exposes itself to rejection through observable implications. When a model is falsified, it indicates that at least one of its underlying assumptions is inconsistent with the data, motivating either model revision, relaxation of assumptions, or a shift toward frameworks that allow for partial identification.

With finite samples, researchers often use specification tests to check whether their baseline model is false. This is the case for the production approach to markup estimation as noted by
29
. Abstracting from sampling uncertainty, the population versions of such specification tests have a persistent problem: what should researchers do when markups differ between variable inputs? Or put more abstractly, what should researchers do when the baseline markup model is falsified?

Building on the framework of
25
, this paper provides a constructive framework for researchers to evaluate and recover markup estimates when the underlying structural assumptions of the production approach are empirically falsified. Concurrently, our approach yields an indirect specification test for the baseline model itself.

To achieve this, we introduce continuous relaxations of two fundamental baseline assumptions: the assumption of cost minimization in the absence of adjustment costs and the correct specification of output elasticities. By systematically weakening these restrictions, an otherwise falsified baseline model can be rendered non-falsified. This allows us to define the
falsification frontier
, which characterizes the minimal set of relaxations required to ensure the model remains compatible with the observed data. Under the assumption that the true structural parameters lie on this boundary, we estimate the
falsification adaptive set
(FAS) for markups. The FAS generalizes the standard baseline markup estimand to explicitly account for model misspecification, crucially eliminating the need to impose restrictive, alternative assumptions on the production function or cost-minimization as previously required in the literature.

When interpreting these results, two core tenets of the falsification literature must be kept in mind. First, while a falsified model is logically impossible, a non-falsified model is not inherently true; it is merely rationalizable by the data. Second, when the baseline model is falsified, the rejection may stem from any combination of the underlying assumptions. Importantly, this limitation holds regardless of the geometric shape of the falsification frontier: the frontier maps the magnitude of the required violations but cannot isolate the precise causal mechanism behind the model’s structural failure.

Our work contributes to the recent markup-estimation literature by providing a falsification-based framework for the production approach. While prior studies have noted that the identifying assumptions behind production-based markups can fail, the literature has largely responded by imposing alternative structural assumptions. In contrast, we treat empirical refutation as an input to identification. We characterize the minimal relaxations of the baseline assumptions needed to rationalize the data (the falsification frontier) and, under the assumption that the true model lies on this frontier, derive the falsification-adaptive set (FAS) for markups. The FAS delivers an identified set for the true markup without requiring researchers to commit to additional modeling choices (e.g., ad hoc modifications of the production function), and it yields an operational diagnostic of the baseline model: if the standard markup estimate lies outside the FAS, the baseline restrictions are rejected by the data.

More broadly, our paper contributes to the falsification and sensitivity-analysis literature initiated by

3.
Why it may fail?

First, if there are adjustment costs or firms are not fully rational then the first order condition
2.1
may not hold, and therefore the markup may be different than
2.2
. These two concerns have been extensively discussed in the literature.

17
notes that labor demand requires adjustment due to changes in the identity of the individuals filling a fixed number of jobs. In this sense, some of the adjustment costs can be related to advertising new job post, screening candidates, processing new employees, training (which induces disruptions to production as previously trained workers’ time is devoted to on-the-job instruction) of new workers, severance pay and the overhead cost of maintaining that part of the personnel function dealing with recruitment and worker outflows. All of these can be substantial, as new workers must be hired and trained to replace those who depart (whose possibly involuntary departure also generates costs). Furthermore, changes in the capital stock or its rate of utilization can also generate adjustment costs as an unchanged workforce’s routine is disrupted and tasks are reassigned and restructured. For example, the delivery of newly purchased equipment and structures takes time and this constrains production, as installing newly purchased equipment or structures shifts other inputs away from current production; as workers’ learning-by-doing with the newly installed equipment takes time.

Similar considerations may apply to intermediate inputs. Although these inputs are often modeled as flexible margins of adjustment, modifying their use may also entail non-negligible costs. Adjusting input sourcing may require searching for and evaluating alternative suppliers, renegotiating contractual arrangements, adapting logistics and inventory management, and coping with delivery lags or minimum order requirements. In production processes characterized by technological complementarities or quality-specific inputs, changing the mix or intensity of intermediate inputs may also require modifications in routines, coordination across production stages, and learning about the performance of new materials or suppliers. In addition, firms may face inventory adjustment costs, disruptions associated with altering procurement schedules, or costs arising from breaking long-term supplier relationships. To the extent that intermediate inputs are embedded in production networks or customized to specific technologies, their adjustment may be subject to frictions similar in spirit to those affecting labor and capital.

On the other hand, even if there are no adjustment costs, firm decision makers may exhibit bounded rationality, a notion that goes back at least to
30
, who emphasized that agents may satisfice rather than solve fully rational optimization problems. In this spirit, evidence from
20
;
26
suggests that firms may depart from frictionless cost minimization because decision makers face cognitive and computational limitations. As noted by
32
, boundedly rational managers may fail to anticipate market saturation in time to reduce capacity. Additionally,
10
argues that under bounded rationality a firm may be unable to compute, costlessly and exactly, its optimal output.

More recent work has formalized related departures from full optimization through rational inattention, where firms face information-processing constraints and therefore allocate limited attention imperfectly across margins of decision making (
31
;
23
). Under this view, observed input choices may reflect constrained information use rather than frictionless optimization. Likewise, the Behavioral IO literature has emphasized that firms may make systematic mistakes, rely on heuristics, or use imperfect decision rules, rather than behave as fully rational profit maximizers (e.g.
14
). In these settings, even in the absence of physical or organizational adjustment costs, factor demand may become inconsistent with standard static cost minimization without adjustment costs.

The second assumption presented in the previous section (correct specification of output elasticities) may also fail. This issue is closely related to the estimation of production functions, as reviewed by
12
. Their survey covers the most widely used methods for estimating total factor productivity and recovering output elasticities with respect to inputs. They distinguish between two broad approaches. The first is the factor share approach, which recovers elasticities from the cost share of each input in total revenue. This approach requires all inputs to be flexible and the production technology to exhibit constant returns to scale. The second approach estimates output elasticities by directly fitting regressions of output on production inputs. This latter approach faces two well-known econometric challenges: simultaneity bias—arising because firms observe their productivity when choosing inputs—and selection bias, driven by the endogenous survival of more productive firms. The literature has addressed these issues through two main strategies: control function approaches and dynamic panel data methods. The control function approach builds on the seminal contribution of
27
, extended by
21
and refined by
1
.
1
1

1

Dynamic panel data methods, originally developed in a broader econometric context
(
3
;
7
)
, provide an alternative way to address these identification challenges.

Recent work has highlighted important limitations in the identification of output elasticities.
1
show that, under simple data-generating processes consistent with the standard assumptions, the moment conditions underlying the first-stage estimation of production function parameters may fail to identify the labor coefficient, leading to a misspecification of elasticities.
13
further argue that these methods are not robust to unobserved heterogeneity in demand across firms or over time. In a related contribution,
13
show, first, that under imperfect competition, the estimation of input elasticities must account for markups themselves; and second, that under commonly used production function specifications, cost minimization conditions are often inconsistent with observed data, casting doubt on the baseline relationship between markups, elasticities, and revenue shares.
Additional evidence of limitations of the procedures for correct output elasticities is provided by
9

29
tests the implication that any flexible input should recover the same markup under the production approach and strongly rejects the hypothesis that markups estimated using labor and materials share the same distribution.

4.
What can researchers do in the presence of falsification?

As stated earlier by sufficiently weakening the assumptions, a falsified baseline model becomes non-falsified. The continuous relaxations of our model assumptions are:

Assumption 3
.

Let for some positive vector
(\delta_{e,t}^{1},\delta_{e,t}^{2},\delta_{m,t}^{1},\delta_{m,t}^{2})

(1)

Relaxation 1: Bounded or frictional cost minimization
:

\left|\mu_{it}^{j}-\frac{\theta_{it}^{j}}{\alpha_{it}^{j}}\right|\leq\delta_{m,t}^{j}

(2)

Relaxation 2: Imperfect elasticity identification

|\theta_{it}^{j}-\hat{\theta}_{it}^{j}|\leq\delta_{e,t}^{j}

\delta_{e,t}^{j}

The form of the identified set gives a direct way of deciding if a model is falsifiable or not. A model will be falsified if the identification region is empty

If this region is empty, it means no single parameter value can satisfy all the theoretical constraints and fit the real-world data simultaneously. In our context, this would imply that there is no potential average markup consistent with the assumptions and the observed data. Emptiness would occur for the values of
\bm{\delta}_{t}

Any assumption stronger than those on the frontier—meaning any tighter restriction on the allowed violations—will logically falsify the model. Using the conservative, worst-case framework established in Corollary

\max_{j=1,2}LB_{\bm{\delta}_{t}^{\text{max}},j}=\min_{j=1,2}UB_{\bm{\delta}_{t}^{\text{max}},j}

quantifiable sensitivity analysis

plausibility benchmarking
by shifting the burden of proof. Neither the researcher nor the critic needs to pinpoint the exact value of
\bm{\delta}_{t}
. Instead, the debate is reframed around whether the minimal required violations on the frontier are economically plausible or implausibly large given the institutional context of the studied industry. Third, it achieves
dimensionality reduction

\bm{\delta}_{t}

Utilizing the falsification frontier, we can construct the
falsification adaptive set
(FAS). The FAS is defined as the identified set for the parameter of interest under the assumption that the true structural model satisfies one of the restriction profiles located exactly on the frontier. Notably, when the baseline model is not falsified by the data, the falsification adaptive set collapses down to the baseline identified set, which is a singleton. To understand the mechanical behavior of the FAS, it is instructive to analyze its properties when the baseline model is well-specified. By definition, the FAS is constructed as the union of all identified sets evaluated along the falsification frontier. Each point on this frontier represents a minimal relaxation profile where the lower and upper bounds exactly coincide, rendering each constituent set a singleton. Crucially, when the baseline model (
\bm{\delta}_{t}=\mathbf{0}
) is not falsified by the data, the baseline lower bound is equal to its upper bound. By establishing assumptions
1

2

\mathbf{0}
) acts as the sharpest point on the falsification frontier. In this scenario, no relaxation is required to rationalize the data, causing the origin
\mathbf{0}
to serve as the sharpest point of the falsification frontier. Consequently, the frontier contracts entirely to the origin, the broad union collapses to a single evaluation point, and the FAS smoothly transitions into the standard baseline point-identified singleton. When the baseline model is falsified, however, the FAS expands to account for uncertainty about which assumption along the frontier is true. Hence this set generalizes the standard baseline estimand to account for possible falsification. The FAS is the union of

Recall that depending on the joint relationships between the production inputs and their respective output elasticities, the falsification adaptive set manifests in different geometric shapes. In all scenarios, however, the framework systematically exploits the two potential baseline markup estimands: namely, the input-specific average classical measures of markups,
\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}/\alpha_{it}^{1}\big]
and
\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}/\alpha_{it}^{2}\big]
, that a researcher would obtain by strictly relying on a single input while ignoring the other. The theorem demonstrates that the robust estimand for the average markup is constructed precisely by combining these counterfactual single-input boundaries with their simple average, bounding the potential misspecification without requiring arbitrary parametric structure.
Note furthermore that in any case, this set collapses to the estimand of the markup when the baseline model is not falsified (since in such case
\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)=\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)
).
Researchers may find it useful to present this set and its evolution over time alongside their baseline estimates, in order to assess the robustness of their conclusions and to gain insight into the evolution of the average markup under weaker restrictions.

Although the points on the falsification frontier are consistent with the data, this does not mean one of them is true. For this reason, the falsification adaptive set—although it represents uncertainty due to the nature of the misspecification—is nonetheless an optimistic set. It assumes that, although the model is misspecified, it is only minimally misspecified. it is a robustness exercise but an optimistic one.

Statistical Inference

In finite samples, researchers can construct sample analog estimates of the falsification adaptive set alongside their corresponding confidence regions. However, conducting inference on the FAS presents a distinct econometric challenge: the boundaries of the set depend on indicator functions and discrete case-switchers, making the estimand a non-differentiable function of the underlying population moments.

To formalize the sample parameters and address this lack of smoothness, we introduce the following notation. Let
s_{t}^{j}\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{j}}\right]
and
m_{t}^{j}\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{j}}{\alpha_{it}^{j}}\right]
denote the core population moments for any input
j
, with
\hat{s}_{t,n}^{j}
and
\hat{m}_{t,n}^{j}
representing their respective sample analogs. For a generic pair of variable inputs
(j,j^{\prime})
, we define two logical indicator functions that map the structural conditions of Theorem
1
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{s,t}^{j,j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbb{I}\left(s_{t}^{j^{\prime}}-s_{t}^{j}\leq 0\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{m,t}^{j,j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbb{I}\left(m_{t}^{j}-m_{t}^{j^{\prime}}\geq 0\right)

with sample analogs denoted by
\hat{\mathbb{I}}_{s,t,n}^{j,j^{\prime}}
and
\hat{\mathbb{I}}_{m,t,n}^{j,j^{\prime}}
.

By utilizing these indicators as algebraic switches, the four discrete regimes of Theorem
1
can be condensed into a single, unified expression. Specifically, the lower bound of the FAS for a given input pair
(j,j^{\prime})
is given by:

\displaystyle FAS_{lb}^{j,j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=m_{t}^{j^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{s,t}^{j,j^{\prime}}\,\mathbb{I}_{m,t}^{j,j^{\prime}}+\left(\frac{m_{t}^{j}+m_{t}^{j^{\prime}}}{2}\right)\cdot\mathbb{I}_{s,t}^{j,j^{\prime}}\,(1-\mathbb{I}_{m,t}^{j,j^{\prime}})

(4.4)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{m_{t}^{j}+m_{t}^{j^{\prime}}}{2}\right)\cdot(1-\mathbb{I}_{s,t}^{j,j^{\prime}})\,\mathbb{I}_{m,t}^{j,j^{\prime}}+m_{t}^{j}\cdot(1-\mathbb{I}_{s,t}^{j,j^{\prime}})\,(1-\mathbb{I}_{m,t}^{j,j^{\prime}})

The upper bound,
FAS_{ub}^{j,j^{\prime}}
, is constructed analogously.

Although the presence of these indicators and the underlying
\max
and
\min
operators renders the boundary functions non-differentiable in the standard Taylor-expansion sense, they satisfy the weaker property of
directional differentiability
. While researchers might naturally consider applying bootstrap procedures to conduct statistical inference, standard non-parametric bootstrap methods require full Hadamard differentiability to ensure consistency and thus fail in the context of directionally differentiable functionals. To overcome this limitation, the analytical frameworks developed by
15
and
19
can be directly applied. Specifically, researchers can employ numerical directional derivatives or modified bootstrap procedures to obtain valid, asymptotically exact confidence intervals for the identification region, even when taking the union of multiple adaptive sets across several input combinations.

6.
Conclusions

In this paper we provide a constructive way for researchers to salvage
11
markup model when falsified. To do this, we consider continuous relaxations of the assumptions underlying the static cost minimization of variable inputs and the accurate estimation of the input-output elasticity. By computing the values of the markup as a function of the relaxations across the minimal set of non-falsified models, we obtain an identified set for the markup which generalizes the standard baseline markup estimand to account for possible falsification without the need to impose additional or new assumptions on the production function or firm behavior. We illustrate this using data from
29
and conclude that there exists robustness in the result that markup is above 1 but interpretations about the shape of the trend over time in markup should be treated with caution since it is not a robust result.

\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)\geq 0
there will eventually be a
\delta_{e,t}^{\text{max, FF}}
that will be the last one that satisfies the third inequality. Namely,

\displaystyle\delta_{e,t}^{\text{max, FF, b}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)}{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)}

Thus, we take the union across
\delta_{e,t}^{\text{max, FF}}
up to
\delta_{e,t}^{\text{max, FF, b}}
of the form of

\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)+\delta_{e,t}^{\text{max, FF}}\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)\Big]}{2}

And then stop since in this case there will be no positive
\delta_{e,t}^{\text{max, FF}}
such that satisfies the second inequality from equation
A.1
.
Then, the falsification adaptative set is:

\displaystyle FAS

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)}{2},\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)\Bigg)

(4)

When
\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)>0
and
\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)<0

By symmetry we can note that:

\displaystyle FAS

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big),\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)}{2}\Bigg)

∎

Appendix B
Results for more than two variable inputs

Variable Input index

One alternatives is to combine inputs into bundles such as all variable inputs related to materials and all variable inputs related to labor and do the two case analysis. Thus one can define all relevant inputs, compute the total cost for each input category, determine each input’s share of total costs and use these shares as weights to create a single, combined input index. A similar idea can be applied for the prices of the respective combined inputs.

The union of the FAS of 2 variable input submodels

Another alternative would be to compute the FAS for all pairs of variable inputs and then compute the union of all of these. That would lead to a wider set than the real FAS but that can still be used as a robustness measure. This measure provides an identification region constructed by possible values of the average markup under the assumption that the true model lies in the union of the falsification frontier of all the ”submodels” with two inputs.
Note for example if we had 3 variable inputs, say
V_{it}^{1},V_{it}^{2},V_{it}^{3}
such that:

\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)\leq 0,\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)\geq 0,

\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)\leq 0,\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{3}}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big)\geq 0,

\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)\leq 0,\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)-\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{3}}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big)\geq 0.

Then for each one of them, respectively the FAS would be:

\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big),\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)}{2}\Bigg),

\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{3}}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big),\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{3}}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)}{2}\Bigg),

\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{3}}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big),\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{3}}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big)}{2}\Bigg).

Then, since
\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{3}}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big)\leq\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)\leq\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{2}\alpha_{it}^{1}\Big)

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=1}^{3}FAS_{k}=\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{3}}{\alpha_{it}^{3}}\Big),\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{1}}{\alpha_{it}^{1}}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{it}^{2}}{\alpha_{it}^{2}}\Big)}{2}\Bigg)

More generally for any of the
K
duplet combinations of inputs we will have a generic
FAS_{k}=(FAS_{k,lb},FAS_{k,ub})
and then
\bigcup_{k}FAS_{k}=(\min_{k}FAS_{k,lb},\max_{k}FAS_{k,ub})
which is easy to compute in any given data.
