Changes in Depressive Symptoms Pre- and Post-Kidney Transplantation
Authors: Nan-Su Huang, Jingyao Hong, Yiting Li, Nidhi Ghildayal, Nicole M. Ali, Deidra C. Crews, Daniel Cukor, Aarti Mathur, Babak J. Orandi, Silas P. Norman, Dorry L. Segev, Mara A. McAdams-DeMarco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

The prevalence of depressive symptoms in patients with ESKD is approximately 40%.1 Patients with depressive symptoms face a higher likelihood of adverse outcomes such as cardiovascular events and mortality,2,3 even after a kidney transplant (KT). KT provides a better quality of life and survival benefits compared with dialysis4,5; however, fear of kidney rejection, medication side effects, and preexisting illness contribute substantially to post-KT psychological stress.6 Owing to limited longitudinal research on depression before and after KT, the long-term trajectory of depressive symptoms throughout the KT course remains insufficiently characterized.

Risk factors for elevated depressive symptoms in potential KT candidates and recipients include younger age, female sex, lack of spousal support, and lower socioeconomic status.7,8 As kidney disease professional organizations, including the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes Clinical Practice Guideline, encourage routine depression screening and psychosocial support across the kidney disease spectrum,9,10 identifying high-risk groups is important for targeting interventions and guiding treatment strategies. However, there is a lack of standardized tools or established cutoff points tailored to detect depression among patients with ESKD.11 Therefore, little is known about how patient characteristics are associated with the temporal change of depressive symptoms following transplantation.

To better characterize the trajectories of depressive symptoms before and after KT, we leveraged a multicenter prospective cohort study of potential KT candidates and recipients. The goals of the study were to (1) quantify the change of depressive symptoms before and after KT and (2) test whether post-KT trajectories of depressive symptoms differ by patient characteristics.

Methods

Study Design

We leveraged a prospective multi-center study composed of a cohort of 4661 adult (age ≥18 years), English-speaking patients with ESKD being evaluated for KT (2009–2025; potential KT candidates hereafter) and a cohort of 1215 adult (age ≥18 years), English-speaking KT recipients (2008–2025). Potential KT candidates and KT recipients were enrolled at Johns Hopkins Hospital, University of Michigan University Hospital, and New York University (NYU)-affiliated hospitals.

In both cohorts, participant characteristics were ascertained from self-report, electronic medical records, or direct measurement, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, partnership status, employment status, smoking status, family income, cause of ESKD, donor type, dialysis type, body mass index, dialysis time, frailty (physical frailty phenotype),12,13 lower extremity impairment (short physical performance battery score),14,15 activities of daily living dependence,16 and instrumental activities of daily living dependence.17 Neighborhood deprivation (neighborhood deprivation index18) and social cohesion (social connectedness and supports, obtained from the Social Capital Atlas estimates19) were further ascertained based on residential Zone Improvement Plan codes. We evaluated cognitive function using the global cognitive composite score, calculated as the average of the Z-standardized modified Mini–Mental State examination and Z-standardized Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores.20,21 We obtained comorbidities according to the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) adapted for patients with ESKD via self-report and supplemented with electronic medical records.22,23

We obtained race and ethnicity via self-report and supplemented with electronic medical records when self-report was unavailable. Race was categorized as White, Black, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, other or multiracial, or unknown, and ethnicity as Hispanic or non-Hispanic. Individuals were then classified as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, or non-Hispanic other (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, other or multiracial).

All clinical and research activities reported adhere to the Declaration of Helsinki and the Declaration of Istanbul. The Johns Hopkins, University of Michigan, and NYU Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) approved the study. All participants provided written (including electronic e-consent) or verbal informed consent, with documentation waived for some minimal-risk phone-based procedures per IRB approval.

Depressive Symptoms

We evaluated participants' depressive symptoms using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D) scale, a 20-item, patient-reported measurement that is sensitive to subclinical and mild symptom elevation and often used to track changes in depressive symptoms.24 The CES-D scale measures the frequency of 20 symptoms over the past week.25 Each question has four possible response options (none, 1–2, 3–4, 5–7 days) scored as zero, one, two, or three, and the total score is then calculated from the responses. The CES-D score ranges from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms. A score of ≥16 indicates clinically elevated depressive symptoms (high depressive symptoms hereafter), which is often considered a clinical cutoff for depression.25 The CES-D instrument identifies symptoms in areas of depressed mood, guilt/worthlessness, helplessness/hopelessness, psychomotor retardation, loss of appetite, and sleep disturbance25 and has been validated in the ESKD population.26,27 The full questionnaire is publicly available through: https://eprovide.mapi-trust.org/instruments/center-for-epidemiologic-studies-depression-scale. CES-D was administered at the evaluation for potential candidates. Recipients were assessed at KT admission and at follow-up during routine clinical visits at approximately 1, 3, and 6 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive Analysis

We reported the distribution of characteristics, overall and by presence of high depressive symptoms, using percentages for categorical variables, means with SD for normally distributed continuous variables, and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for nonnormally distributed continuous variables.

Pre-KT Risk Factors for Depressive Symptoms

We estimated the odds ratios of depressive symptoms at the time of evaluation and the time of KT admission using logistic regression models. Models were adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking, education, cause of ESKD, dialysis type, dialysis time, and CCI. Models at admission are additionally adjusted for donor type.

Post-KT Trajectories of Depressive Symptoms

We estimated score points and adjusted trajectories of CES-D between KT admission and 4 years post-KT among KT recipients using mixed-effects models with random effects for person (intercept) and time (slope) with unconstructed covariance. The model did not include spline terms based on exploratory analysis. Models were adjusted for age, race/ethnicity, sex, partnership status, smoking, education, cause of ESKD, donor type, CCI, and dialysis time. We tested whether the CES-D score trajectories differed by the following factors using the Wald test: presence of high depressive symptoms at KT admission, age (>60 versus ≤60 years), sex, race/ethnicity (all other racial and ethnic groups versus non-Hispanic Black), education status (<high school versus ≥high school), partnership status, smoking status (previous/current smoking versus nonsmoking), preemptive KT status, and donor type (living versus deceased). We additionally tested whether CES-D score trajectories differed by neighborhood-level psychosocial support (social connectedness and social supports) using the Wald test.

Missing Data Approach

For logistic regressions analyses, missing data were handled using multiple imputation with 20 iterations for partnership (1% at evaluation and 2% at admission, respectively), smoking (0.3% and 0.6%), education (1% and 2%), dialysis type (14% and 14%), comorbidity (12% and 0.9%), dialysis year (17% and 18%), and cause of ESKD (45% and 4%). For mixed-effect models, we performed a complete case analysis, excluding patients with missing at-admission covariates.

Sensitivity Analysis

To test the robustness of our results and account for population differences, we redefined high depressive symptoms at KT admission using internal cutoffs based on our KT recipient population. Specifically, we stratified the trajectory using cohort-based CES-D score thresholds of nine (highest tertile) and 11 (highest quartile). We also applied a generalized estimation equation (GEE) model with robust standard errors to relax the correlation structure assumed in the main model. We additionally adjusted our models with individual-level employment, family income, and neighborhood-level deprivation to account for potential socioeconomic confounding. Finally, to address potential effects of loss to follow-up because of withdrawal or having competing events (graft failure or death), under the assumption of missing at random, we compared results from two approaches: (1) multiple imputations by chained equations with auxiliary variables and (2) inverse probability weighting, both implemented with GEEs.28

All analyses were performed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). The study determined statistical significance with two-sided P values < 0.05.

KT Recipient Characteristics

Among the 1215 KT recipients, the mean age at transplant admission was 52.5 (SD: 13.9) years, 40% were female, 38% were non-Hispanic Black, 95% had ≥high school degree, 63% were partnered, 29% were current or previous smoking, 60% had a deceased donor, median time on dialysis was 2.4 years (IQR, 0.7–5.4), and median CCI was 1.0 (IQR, 0.0–3.0; Figure 1B and Table 3). The average postsurgical stay was 10.0 (SD: 8.3) days and hospitalization within 5 years was 1.6 (SD: 1.0) times (Table 3). During follow-up, 97% of recipients had functioning transplants with minimal complications (33% rejection, 0% infection).

KT Recipient Characteristics and Depressive Symptoms at Admission

At admission, the median CES-D score was 6.0 (IQR, 2.0–11.0), and 15% of recipients had high depressive symptoms (Figure 2A). The median CES-D score for recipients with high depressive symptoms was 20.0 (IQR, 18.0–25.0) and 4.0 (IQR, 2.0–8.0) for those without. Among recipients with high depressive symptoms at admission, 48% and 58% resolved by one and 2 years post-KT, respectively (Figure 2B).

After adjustment, recipients aged ≤60 years (aOR, 2.66; 95% CI, 1.68 to 4.21), who were current or previous smoking (aOR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.12 to 2.34), or frail (aOR, 2.75; 95% CI, 1.67 to 4.54) were more likely to have high depressive symptoms at admission (Table 2).

Over 4 years, the score was 0.42 points/yr higher (95% CI, 0.25 to 0.60; Table 4) in the reference population. After adjusting for recipient characteristics, high depressive symptoms at admission was statistically significantly associated with changes in depressive symptoms (slope=−0.66 points/yr, 95% CI, −1.23 to −0.08). Depressive symptoms among those without initial symptoms slightly worsened but remained under clinical threshold (slope=0.53 points/yr, 95% CI, 0.34 to 0.71). The difference in the change in depressive symptoms between these two groups was statistically significant (difference=−1.18 points/yr, 95% CI, −1.79 to −0.58), The trajectory over 4 years also differed by preemptive KT status (difference=−0.57 points/yr, 95% CI, −1.04 to −0.09). CES-D scores remained the same over time in recipients on dialysis (slope=−0.14, 95% CI, −0.61 to 0.33) but were 0.43 points/yr higher (95% CI, 0.24 to 0.62) in preemptive KT recipients. The trajectory did not differ by other recipient characteristics (Table 4 and Supplemental Table 5).

Sensitivity Analysis

The inferences for all sensitivity analyses remained consistent with the primary analysis. Specifically, trajectories also differed between recipients with high depressive symptoms at admission and those without when we used a CES-D threshold of nine (difference=−0.61 points/yr, 95% CI, −0.98 to −0.24) or 11 (difference−0.80 points/yr, 95% CI, −1.21 to −0.39), corresponding to the highest tertile and quartile within the cohort, respectively (Supplemental Table 1), and in the GEE model (difference=−1.21 points/yr, 95% CI, −1.95 to −0.48; Supplemental Table 2). The direction of the estimates and group differences was consistent across the primary analysis and both missing-data approaches, indicating that the findings were robust to the method used for handling attrition. Results of logistic regressions and longitudinal models adjusted for additional socioeconomic covariates were also comparable with the primary analyses (Supplemental Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of potential KT candidates and recipients, we found that depressive symptoms slightly worsened over the first 4 years post-KT, but remained below the clinical threshold for depression diagnosis. Changes in CES-D scores post-KT differed by pre-KT depressive symptom status, with symptoms improving among those with high pre-KT depressive symptoms but worsening among those without. CES-D score changes also differed by preemptive KT status.

Our cohort had a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms at evaluation than pre-KT (19% versus 15%), which may reflect that some individuals undergoing evaluation were not yet suitable candidates for listing because of medical or psychosocial barriers. In addition, our cohort reported a lower prevalence (15%) of high depressive symptoms in KT recipients than previous studies that have used the CES-D, including Novak et al., which found a 22% prevalence of high depressive symptoms despite a higher threshold of CES-D ≥18, among 840 KT recipients between 2002 and 2003.29 The observed difference may also be attributable to variations in population age and study period (i.e., era effects); younger adults are more likely to experience depression.30 Furthermore, although the prevalence of depression among the general population has increased over the decades, the trajectory of depression prevalence among KT recipients was inconsistent between studies.1,30 Longitudinal studies on changes in depressive symptoms more than 1 year post-KT are also limited. Although depressive symptoms do not necessarily indicate clinical depression, our finding parallels with previous study, which reported the cumulative incidence of clinical depression increasing post-KT, of 5.1%, 7.3%, and 9.1% at 1, 2, and 3 years post-KT, respectively.31

Several mechanisms may explain why we found that depressive symptoms change was associated with pre-KT depressive symptoms status. Restoration of kidney function via KT lessens uremia, inflammation, sleep disturbance, and fatigue, all of which are drivers strongly implicated in depression.32,33 KT also reduces psychosocial stressors linked to dialysis, including treatment burden, dietary and fluid restrictions, and loss of independence, thus likely improving health-related quality of life and mood.34,35 However, patients without existing high depressive symptoms at KT may have less room to improve, while also experiencing new stressors, including medical and/or surgical complications of KT, fear of graft loss, or financial strain.36,37 Finally, the absence of improvement among recipients without high pre-KT depressive symptoms may also reflect regression to the mean, whereby individuals with initially low symptom burden are less likely to demonstrate measurable improvement over time independent of true changes.

Several clinically relevant factors, including donor type and cause of ESKD, may influence depressive symptoms in KT patients, with previous studies suggesting that recipients of deceased-donor transplants may experience greater psychological distress than living-donor recipients,38 and diabetic kidney disease may adversely affect mental health through chronic vascular complications.39 However, neither donor type nor cause of ESKD was significantly associated with depressive symptoms in our study. This may reflect improvements in quality of life after KT that attenuate a potential impact from these factors, as well as the complex nature of depressive symptoms, which are also influenced by graft function, medication effects, social support, and psychiatric history.40,41 Overall, these findings suggest that depressive symptoms after KT are shaped by a multifactorial interplay of medical and psychosocial factors rather than any single transplant characteristic.40,41

As such, we also examined the impact of psychosocial and social factors on depressive symptoms in our cohort. In this study, the association of higher educational attainment with lower depressive symptom scores after KT was of borderline statistical significance. Previous research among KT recipients in China has similarly reported lower depressive symptom burden among individuals with greater educational attainment. It may be that education provides advantages in health literacy, coping abilities, and access to socioeconomic resources that facilitate post-KT self-management and mitigate psychological distress.42 In addition, we examined marital status as a potential marker of social support and found that partnered patients (married or cohabitating) had a slower annual increase in CES-D scores after KT than nonpartnered patients, suggesting that partner support may provide modest protection against worsening depressive symptoms after transplantation. Furthermore, the risk of high depressive symptoms at KT admission differed significantly by levels of neighborhood social cohesion. Overall, our findings suggest that psychosocial and social determinants—including education, partnership status, and neighborhood social cohesion—may play an important role in shaping depressive symptoms at or after KT, with greater social and socioeconomic support potentially offering protective effects.43

Depressive symptoms after KT have significant clinical implications. Recipients with depressive symptoms are more likely to demonstrate medication nonadherence, which can be associated with a higher risk of rejection and graft loss.44,45 Depressive symptoms are also associated with reduced observance of lifestyle recommendations such as diet, exercise, and precautionary measures against infection.46 Furthermore, previous studies demonstrate that post-KT depression is associated with higher rates of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.33,47 Thus, our findings underscore the need for systematic screening and timely treatment of depressive symptoms in potential KT candidates and recipients to improve their depression and possibly their medication adherence, graft survival, and overall patient health outcomes.

To our knowledge, this is the largest prospective study regarding post-KT depression change with the longest follow-up time of 4 years. Other strengths include the use of CES-D, which could capture the underdiagnosed population.8 In addition, we performed sensitivity analyses using internal thresholds to confirm the study results. Study limitations include potential bias from attrition over the extended follow-up period and restricted result generalizability because of the two-center design, despite a diverse study population. Post-transplant complications may affect longitudinal results; however, the low event rates in our cohort limit evaluation of their impact, suggesting any effect on CES-D trajectories is likely minimal. Information on comas was not available in our dataset. Furthermore, we may have misclassified depressive symptom severity because of the absence of data on diagnoses as well as pharmacologic or nonpharmacologic treatments. Finally, while all transplant centers are required to provide psychosocial support after transplantation, including routine psychosocial assessments and access to mental health services as needed, our cohort study did not collect standardized information on the specific type, intensity, or timing of psychosocial interventions or mental health treatments received by individual participants; this is an opportunity for future study. Nonetheless, CES-D is a validated screening tool for clinical depression and has been widely applied in kidney disease populations.27,41,48,49

To conclude, 19% of potential KT candidates at evaluation, and 15% of recipients at transplant admission had high depressive symptoms, respectively. In the immediate years post-KT, recipients who did not have high depressive symptoms at admission may experience slightly worsening CES-D scores, but these still do not meet the criteria for clinical depression. However, recipients with high depressive symptoms before KT may experience improvement after KT. Pre-KT high depressive symptoms may help identify patients who could benefit from targeted psychosocial support to prevent adverse post-transplant outcomes and further reduce post-KT depressive symptoms. Clinicians should discuss the long-term mental health impact of KT and provide individualized support to preserve psychological well-being throughout the transplant process.
