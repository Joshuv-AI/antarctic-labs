IL-6 Activity is Required for Maximal Catecholamine Release During High-Intensity Exercise in Men
Authors: Andreas K. Ziegler, Tim Schauer, Sara F. Myrup, Gerrit van Hall, Bente K. Pedersen, Jesper F. Christensen, Helga Ellingsgaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-intensity exercise triggers a coordinated activation of metabolic, endocrine, and immune pathways, yet the mechanisms integrating these responses remain incompletely understood. Interleukin-6 (IL-6), released during exercise, has been proposed as a systemic signal linking skeletal muscle activity to whole-body stress responses. We tested whether exercise-induced IL-6 is required for full sympathoadrenal activation and immune cell mobilization during intense exercise in humans.Healthy young men received the IL-6 receptor (IL-6R) antibody tocilizumab prior to high-intensity interval exercise. IL-6R blockade reduced circulating epinephrine by ∼50%, lowered plasma glucose levels, and attenuated lactate accumulation, resulting in a smaller decline in blood pH. Immune cell mobilization was selectively impaired, with reduced recruitment of lymphocytes, CD8+ T cells, CD56ˆbright natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, while CD4+ T cells, CD56ˆdim NK cells, and B cells were unaffected. Although upstream hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA)-axis hormones corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and arginine vasopressin (AVP) were unchanged, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) was significantly reduced and associated with pH and catecholamine responses in the control condition. A lower lactate-to-pyruvate ratio during IL-6R blockade suggests enhanced pyruvate oxidation as a potential upstream mechanism.These findings position IL-6 as an integrative metabolic signal that mediates organ crosstalk and amplifies the HPA and sympathoadrenal response during high-intensity exercise. By linking skeletal muscle metabolic stress to endocrine activation, glucose regulation, and immune cell mobilization, IL-6 appears to coordinate the complex systemic fight-or-flight response to intense physical exertion.

Introduction

High-intensity exercise triggers a controlled physiological response to the rapid increase in energy turnover in contracting skeletal muscles. While many of the integrated physiological, metabolic, and immunological responses to exercise are proportionate to exercise intensity and closely coordinated, the role of working muscles in orchestrating these responses remains poorly understood.

One of the most remarkable features of high-intensity exercise is the rapid increase in circulating epinephrine and norepinephrine released from the adrenal medulla and sympathetic nerve terminals. This release constitutes the central axis of the fight-or-flight response [1]. The increase in catecholamines, primarily epinephrine, supports systemic glucose homeostasis and promotes fatty acid mobilization, thereby contributing to the integrated metabolic response required during continued muscle work [2], and besides meeting metabolic demands, epinephrine also recruits immune cells into the circulation during exercise [3]. Although the initial increase in catecholamines is largely believed to be neuronally controlled, evidence from animal models suggests it may also respond to cytokines [4].

Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is released from human skeletal muscle during exercise [5], with circulating levels rising in proportion to exercise duration, intensity, and the degree of muscle glycogen depletion [5,6]. Given this, IL-6 has been proposed to function as a carbohydrate sensor, regulating glucose availability through both local and endocrine mechanisms [7,8]. During high-intensity exercise, circulating IL-6 levels can rise remarkably fast, with increases detected within 30 s of exercise onset [9,10]. This rapid release is likely facilitated by the presence of pre-stored IL-6 vesicles within myofibers [11]. In contrast, during moderate-intensity exercise, IL-6 release occurs more gradually and is driven by IL-6 gene transcription, which is activated in response to declining muscle glycogen levels [5,6,12]. As a result, high-intensity exercise leads to a significantly greater and more rapid increase in plasma IL-6 compared to moderate-intensity exercise [13,14].

In this study, we investigated the role of IL-6 in coordinating the integrated endocrine, immune, and metabolic responses to high-intensity exercise in humans. We hypothesized that the rapid increase in circulating IL-6 during exercise drives catecholamine release, immune cell mobilization, and glucose availability. To test this, we conducted an exploratory physiological study with young men using a randomized, placebo-controlled, and double-blind design. IL-6 activity was blocked with the anti-IL-6 receptor (IL-6R) antibody tocilizumab.

Results

Study design

The study was randomized, placebo-controlled, and double-blinded (registered on clinicaltrials.gov, NCT04616235). Participants were studied on one occasion, they received either saline 0.9% as placebo (CON) or the IL-6 receptor antibody, tocilizumab, which blocks all IL-6-induced signaling [15] and thus IL-6 activity (BLOCK) and performed an interval-based high-intensity exercise bout. Participants

Between March 15, 2021 and October 4, 2021, 29 male participants completed the study. One participant in the BLOCK group did not complete the study due to a vasovagal reaction. Participant characteristics are presented in Table S1. Detailed in- and exclusion criteria are listed in the methods section. The two groups were similar with respect to body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness.

IL-6 receptor blockade results in minor increases in heart rate and perceived exertion

High-intensity exercise was chosen to induce a rapid increase in systemic IL-6 levels, and to maximize sympathoadrenal drive, immune cell mobilization, and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity [16,17]. To ensure that each study participant reached maximal exhaustion, watts were continuously adjusted during each of the seven 3-minute intervals to maintain a heart rate (HR) above 90% of HRmax for as long as possible.

Average watt output did not differ between the two groups, but progressively decreased over the exercise bout (Figure 1B) indicating near-to-maximal work intensity within each interval. One minute into each interval, HR was approximately 90% of HRmax in both groups (Figure 1C), and it exceeded 90% of HRmax by the end of each 3-minute interval (Figure 1D). Relative HR and self-reported rate of perceived exertion (RPE) were slightly higher in the BLOCK group (Figure 1DE). Reports of nausea and cases of vomiting were identical between groups: 7/15 and 3/15 in the CON group, and 8/14 and 3/14 in the BLOCK group, respectively.

IL-6 activity is required for maximal catecholamine release and blood glucose elevation

To investigate the role of IL-6 in the acute stress response during high-intensity exercise, blood samples were collected at baseline and after the 1st (3 min), 4th (21 min), and 7th (39 min) exercise intervals to measure IL-6, epinephrine, norepinephrine, and key metabolites.

As anticipated, circulating IL-6 levels immediately increased in the BLOCK group, likely due to impaired IL-6R-mediated clearance of IL-6 [18,19] and already after the 1st interval, corresponding to just 3 min of mechanical work, IL-6 levels increased significantly by approximately 40 and 50% in the BLOCK and CON groups respectively (from 2.43 ± 0.75 to 3.37 ± 0.97 pg/mL in the BLOCK group and from 0.30 ± 0.11 to 0.45 ± 0.20 pg/mL in the CON group; data is given as mean ± SD). The IL-6-increase followed a similar pattern in both groups, peaking 30 min into recovery and subsequently gradually declined (Figure 2AB).

Systemic epinephrine concentrations increased in response to exercise in both groups but were ∼50% lower in the BLOCK group compared to the CON group, total AUC epinephrine: BLOCK 3.09 ± 0.06 vs. CON 6.10 ± 0.09, p = 0.023 and Figure 2C. Furthermore, in the BLOCK group, epinephrine levels plateaued after the 4th interval, whereas in the CON group, they showed a numerical increase also during the 7th and final interval (Figure 2C). Circulating norepinephrine levels were lower in the BLOCK group compared to the CON group, total AUC norepinephrine: BLOCK 20.31 ± 0.15 vs. CON 27.33 ± 0.35, p = 0.041 and Figure 2D. Like epinephrine, norepinephrine levels in the BLOCK group peaked after the 4th interval, whereas in the CON group, they showed a numerical increase throughout the remainder of the exercise session (Figure 2D). Correlation analysis of circulating IL-6 and catecholamines at the 7th interval in the CON group revealed no association with epinephrine, but a significant positive correlation with norepinephrine (Figure 2E). No such associations were detected in the BLOCK group (Figure 2F). Glucose levels rose during the exercise bout and consistent with the pattern observed for epinephrine, the rise in blood glucose was attenuated and reached its peak earlier in the BLOCK group compared to the CON group, total AUC glucose: BLOCK 43.5 ± 0.12 vs. CON 47.4 ± 0.07, p = 0.011 and Figure 2G. Given the well-established relationship between exercise-induced epinephrine and glucose release [20] we performed correlation analyses between these two variables. Following the 1st interval, plasma glucose and epinephrine showed no correlation in either the CON or BLOCK groups (Figure 2H). However, after the 7th interval, plasma glucose levels in the CON group showed significant correlations with circulating epinephrine (Figure 2I). Similarly, circulating norepinephrine was not correlated with plasma glucose at the 1st interval (Fig. S1E) but displayed significant associations at the 7th interval (Fig. S1F).

The substantial rise in blood glucose concentrations during the exercise triggered a complementary increase in insulin levels in both groups (Figure 2J). Consistent with the lower blood glucose levels observed in the BLOCK group, the insulin response was less pronounced in this group compared to the CON group, total AUC insulin: BLOCK 432.0 ± 6.8 vs. CON 485.0 ± 3.3, p = 0.123 and Figure 2J.

During exercise, neither glucagon levels nor the glucagon-to-insulin ratio differed between the two groups, AUC glucagon: BLOCK 80.48 ± 0.99 vs. CON 71.69 ± 0.73, p = 0.490 and Figure 2KL, suggesting that these factors did not contribute significantly to the lower blood glucose levels observed in the BLOCK group.

Indicative of a very high-intensity exercise bout, circulating lactate concentrations rose immediately to approximately 8 mM after the 1st interval in both groups (Figure 2M). Lactate levels were generally lower throughout the exercise bout in the BLOCK group, total AUC lactate: BLOCK 53.9 ± 0.287 vs. CON 65.2 ± 0.281, p = 0.010. Consistent with initial lactate production stemming from muscle glycogen breakdown rather than blood glucose utilization [21], lactate levels were not correlated with plasma glucose concentrations after the 1st interval (Fig. S1G), but showed a strong positive correlation at the 7th intervals in both groups (Fig. S1H). Circulating pyruvate, which is released from skeletal muscle during intense exercise [22], did not differ between groups during the exercise bout, but were significantly lower in the BLOCK group during the recovery period (Figure 2N).

Furthermore, during exercise, the lactate to pyruvate ratio, a marker of pyruvate dehydrogenase activity [23], was significantly lower in the BLOCK group compared to the CON group (Figure 2O). After the 1st interval, increases in lactate concentrations correlated significantly with plasma IL-6 levels in the BLOCK group, whereas there was no correlation in the CON group (Figure 2P). This relationship was no longer evident at the 7th interval (Figure 2Q), suggesting a transient early link between lactate production and IL-6 secretion, consistent with prior findings in mice [24]. Lastly, there were no relevant differences in plasma free fatty acids between the two groups (Fig. S1I), and ketone bodies were slightly elevated in the BLOCK group in the recovery period (Fig. S1JK).

IL-6 activity is required for complete mobilization of lymphocytes into circulation

Acute exercise significantly increases the number of immune cells in circulation, with the mobilization of many lymphocyte subsets directly dependent upon beta-adrenergic signaling, mediated by epinephrine and norepinephrine [3,25]. Consistent with this, we observed a reduced count of total lymphocytes, CD8+ T cells, CD56bright natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, neutrophils, dendritic cells (Figure 3A–F) and progenitor cells (Fig. S2A) in the BLOCK group during exercise. Correlation analyses further supported the link between lymphocyte mobilization and IL-6-mediated epinephrine release. In the CON group, epinephrine levels were significantly and positively associated with total lymphocyte mobilization, while there was no such association in the BLOCK group (Figure 3J).

Interestingly, for certain leucocyte subsets, such as monocytes (Figure 3D) and neutrophils (Figure 3E), IL-6R blockade alone resulted in reduced basal counts, but their relative increases during exercise remained comparable between groups. This suggests that the mobilization of these leucocytes is independent of IL-6 and epinephrine.

NK cells, known for their responsiveness to exercise and epinephrine, are categorized into immune-modulating CD56bright and cytotoxic CD56dim subsets, with the latter being more responsive to exercise [3,26,27]. Flow cytometry revealed that IL-6R blockade specifically reduced the mobilization of CD56bright NK cells (Figure 3C), while CD56dim NK cell mobilization was unaffected (Figure 3I). Interestingly, neither CD56dim, CD56bright NK cell counts, nor dendritic cell counts correlated with epinephrine (Fig. S2D–F) or IL-6 (Fig. S2GH). Finally, CD8+ T cell counts during exercise were reduced by approximately 40% in the BLOCK group (Figure 3B). In neither group did CD8+ T cell counts show a statistically significant correlation with epinephrine levels, although there was a clear trend in the control group (r = 0.51; p = 0.054) (Figure 3K).

IL-6 activity stimulates HPA axis activity during rest and exercise

The HPA axis is the central neuroendocrine system, which when activated during exercise exhibits rapid kinetics similar to the catecholamine response [28]. To determine how IL-6 influences the HPA axis and related hormones, we assessed plasma levels of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), cortisol, arginine vasopressin (AVP), and prolactin. CRH (Figure 4A) and AVP levels (Figure 4B) increased already during the 1st interval and continued to rise throughout the exercise session and in early recovery. The IL-6R blockade had minimal to no impact on CRH and AVP, total AUC CRH: BLOCK 1849.0 ± 10.5 vs. CON 1892.0 ± 10.9, p = 0.784, and total AUC AVP: BLOCK 270.0 ± 1.8 vs. CON 275.0 ± 1.9, p = 0.852 and Figure 4AB. ACTH (Figure 4C) and prolactin levels (Figure 4D) also increased in response to exercise, although the increase was significantly less pronounced in the BLOCK group compared to the CON group. During exercise, blood pH decreased to a greater extent in the CON group than in the BLOCK group (Figure 4E). The reduction in pH in the CON group was strongly and negatively correlated with plasma ACTH levels (Figure 4F), meaning that lower pH was associated with higher ACTH release. Given that ACTH has been reported to stimulate the release of epinephrine and norepinephrine [29,30], we performed correlation analyses between ACTH and circulating catecholamines. Accordingly, in the CON group, ACTH was positively and significantly associated with both epinephrine and norepinephrine (Figure 4GH).

Lastly and as expected, cortisol, the primary effector hormone of the HPA axis [31], increased in response to the exercise, but its levels were not significantly affected by IL-6R blockade during exercise (Figure 4I). In contrast, basal cortisol levels were lowered by infusion of the IL-6R antibody.

Taken together, during high-intensity exercise, exercise-induced IL-6R blockade appears to negatively impact the total amount of several hormones released, originating from both hypothalamus and the pituitary gland, while no effect was found for the HPA-axis effector hormone, cortisol.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the role of IL-6 in coordinating the endocrine, immune, and metabolic responses to high-intensity exercise in humans. Our findings establish a functional link between exercise-induced IL-6 and the characteristic physiological hallmarks of very high-intensity exercise in healthy, young men. By blocking IL-6 activity during exercise, we demonstrate that the exercise-induced rise in circulating IL-6 acts as an indirect signal from contracting skeletal muscle to the adrenal glands and is required for the full increase in epinephrine, glucose, and mobilization of immune cells during very high-intensity exercise.

Mechanistically, we propose that IL-6R blockade shifts substrate handling toward greater pyruvate entry into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle by increasing pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) flux, thereby shunting a relatively greater proportion of glycolytically derived pyruvate into mitochondrial oxidation [32,33]. Our data support this interpretation, as the lactate-to-pyruvate (L/P) ratio, used as a surrogate marker of cytosolic redox state and PDH activity [23], was lower in the BLOCK group compared with the CON group during exercise. We further expect that the increased mitochondrial oxidation of pyruvate would limit lactate formation and attenuate the decline in blood pH. The resulting attenuation of acidosis may in turn blunt ACTH release from the pituitary [29], leading to diminished catecholamine secretion and loss of downstream physiological effects [30]. This cascade may explain the phenotype observed in the BLOCK group, including reduced glucose release and diminished immune cell mobilization. Within this framework, the rise in IL-6 during high-intensity exercise appears to function as a critical link between increased skeletal muscle energy demand and systemic glucose availability, with lactate production and the associated fall in pH acting as key driver of ACTH release and subsequent epinephrine secretion. Thus, during high-intensity exercise, IL-6 may serve a permissive role in coordinating the integrated metabolic and hormonal stress response.

Taken together with the existing literature on exercise-induced IL-6 regulation of energy availability, our findings support the notion that IL-6 exerts intensity-dependent effects. During moderate-intensity exercise, IL-6 levels increase gradually, while pyruvate oxidation via the TCA cycle remains sufficient to sustain ATP production with minimal lactate accumulation and little reduction in blood pH. Under these conditions, IL-6 does not appear to substantially influence circulating epinephrine concentrations or whole-body glucose kinetics, likely because ACTH secretion, secondary to reductions in pH is only minimally stimulated. Instead, IL-6 appears to promote mobilization of free fatty acids from adipose tissue [34], consistent with the greater reliance on lipid oxidation during moderate-intensity exercise [35].

In contrast, very high-intensity exercise is characterized by pronounced lactate accumulation and marked reduction in blood pH, which stimulate ACTH secretion from the pituitary gland [29] and are accompanied by rapid IL-6 release from contracting muscle. ACTH subsequently stimulates catecholamine secretion from the adrenal medulla [30]. In support of this framwork, our correlation analyses demonstrate significant positive associations between ACTH and both epinephrine and norepinephrine, as well as between catecholamines and glucose release. Notably, IL-6 was positively associated with circulating glucose concentrations at the 7th exercise interval in the CON group, but not in the BLOCK group. Together, these findings support a model in which IL-6 and catecholamines act in concert to enhance hepatic glucose output during very high-intensity exercise [[36], [37], [38]]. Unlike moderate-intensity exercise, where blood glucose concentrations remain stable or may decline, very high-intensity exercise increases circulating glucose concentrations, thereby supporting systemic glucose availability as glycogen stores become progressively depleted or during repeated bouts of intense exercise.

In addition to its metabolic effects, our data indicate that exercise-induced IL-6, likely via modulation of catecholamine release, contributes to immune cell mobilization during exercise. The overall reduction in circulating lymphocytes observed in the catecholamine-suppressed BLOCK group is consistent with previous findings demonstrating marked reductions in lymphocyte subpopulations during exercise under β-adrenergic blockade [3]. While certain leukocyte subsets were reduced by IL-6R blockade per se, their relative exercise-induced increase was preserved, suggesting that mobilization of these cell populations is not solely dependent on IL-6 or epinephrine signaling.

Among the lymphocyte subsets examined, CD8+ T cells and NK cells are of particular interest due to their central roles in immunosurveillance and tumor infiltration [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. CD8+ T cell numbers were approximately 40% lower in the BLOCK group. In the CON group, CD8+ T cell counts were positively associated with circulating epinephrine concentrations, whereas this relationship was absent in the BLOCK group. Together, these findings support the concept that IL-6-dependent amplification of catecholamine release contributes importantly to the exercise-induced mobilization of cytotoxic lymphocytes.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that during very high-intensity exercise, exercise-induced IL-6 is required for the full mobilization of epinephrine-sensitive lymphocyte subsets. These include cell populations with both immunomodulatory and cytotoxic functions, supporting the concept that acute high-intensity exercise not only promotes tissue repair but may also enhance immunosurveillance. The greater lactate accumulation and more pronounced blood acidosis observed in the CON condition may therefore represent key upstream signals linking metabolic stress to integrated endocrine and immunological responses during intense exercise.

Interestingly, despite lower glucose availability, participants in the BLOCK group completed the exercise session at the same relative workload as those in the CON group. However, their higher heart rate and increased perceived exertion suggest that IL-6R blockade modestly reduced exercise efficiency. This interpretation is consistent with rodent studies demonstrating impaired exercise capacity when IL-6 signaling is disrupted [32,43]. Notably, this effect appears specific to high-intensity exercise, as IL-6R blockade does not influence perceived exertion during moderate-intensity exercise [34,44].

The elevated heart rate observed in the BLOCK group suggests that sympathetic neural drive upstream of the adrenal glands remained intact, while highlighting the importance of IL-6- and ACTH-dependent mechanisms for full adrenal medullary responsiveness during intense exercise. The higher perceived effort may reflect reduced glucose availability to working muscle, necessitating greater cardiovascular strain to maintain mechanical output. Although speculative, enhanced PDH flux in the BLOCK group, by favoring mitochondrial oxidation over rapid glycolytic ATP generation, may also have contributed to reduced lactate production and altered acute energy turnover [32,33]. Because circulating norepinephrine partly reflects sympathetic nerve activity independent of adrenal epinephrine secretion [45,46], it is not surprising that norepinephrine was less affected by IL-6R blockade. Collectively, these observations support a model in which exercise-induced IL-6, indirectly through effects on substrate metabolism, lactate production, and blood pH, facilitates ACTH release and thereby amplifies adrenal catecholamine secretion during very high-intensity exercise.

We also examined the effects of IL-6R blockade on HPA axis hormones. Previous studies have shown that recombinant human IL-6 infusion in resting humans rapidly increases AVP, ACTH, prolactin, and cortisol [[47], [48], [49], [50]]. In the present study, however, exercise-induced increases in CRH, AVP, ACTH, prolactin, and cortisol were not significantly altered by IL-6R blockade. Although baseline concentrations of ACTH, prolactin, and cortisol were lower in the BLOCK condition, consistent with previous reports [34], the relative exercise-induced fold increases were preserved. Importantly, absolute cortisol concentrations during exercise were similar between groups, indicating that overall HPA axis activation remained intact. These findings suggest that while IL-6 may modulate basal HPA axis tone, its contribution to acute exercise-induced HPA activation is modest. Nonetheless, IL-6 may still contribute indirectly to ACTH release during high-intensity exercise through effects on lactate accumulation and acid-base balance.

Recently, Wang et al. [51] reported divergent circulating soluble IL-6 receptor (sIL-6R) responses in exercise responders and non-responders with respect to improvements in insulin sensitivity and glycemic control following exercise training. Specifically, non-responders increased circulating sIL-6R concentrations, whereas responders reduced sIL-6R concentrations. As sIL-6R facilitates IL-6 trans-signaling, these findings suggest that differential regulation of IL-6 signaling pathways may contribute to variability in metabolic adaptations to long-term exercise training.

Circulating sIL-6R concentrations also increase acutely during an exercise bout [52,53], although the tissue origin and physiological significance of this response remain unclear. While sIL-6R was not measured in the present study, our findings demonstrate that IL-6 receptor signaling contributes to several acute physiological responses to exercise. However, we did not observe distinct responder versus non-responder phenotypes in this relatively small cohort of 15 healthy (CON group), physically fit men. The potential role of sIL-6R in determining variability in adaptations to acute exercise warrants further investigation in future studies.

In summary, our results extend the concept of exercise-induced, muscle-derived IL-6 as an energy sensor and energy allocator [7,8], involved in intensity-dependent fuel selection [34]. Beyond its metabolic effects, the present data position IL-6 as an integrative signal linking skeletal muscle metabolic stress to endocrine activation and immune cell mobilization during very high-intensity exercise. In this context, exercise-induced IL-6 emerges as a physiological amplifier of the classical “fight-or-flight” response first described by Walter Cannon more than a century ago [54].

Limitations

There are some limitations to this study. First, the exercise bout was extremely intense and may more closely resemble a fight-or-flight scenario than a typical exercise session. Second, our metabolic analyses were based on circulating concentrations of glucose, fatty acids etc. With this approach we cannot assess whether the observed increase in glucose concentration, for example, is due to an increased rate of glucose production or a reduced uptake of glucose. Thirdly, as we did not perform arteriovenous balance measurements across the exercising leg, we cannot directly quantify the contribution of skeletal muscle to circulating IL-6 during exercise. Although prior literature supports skeletal muscle as a major source of exercise-induced IL-6, the precise tissue contribution in the present study cannot be determined. Accordingly, we primarily refer to “exercise-induced IL-6” throughout the manuscript but also use “muscle-derived IL-6”. Finally, all participants in the study were men, so the findings may not be fully applicable to women.

Participants and screening

Between March 15, 2021 and October 4, 2021, 30 healthy male participants completed the study at the Center for Physical Activity Research (CFAS) in the Capital Region of Denmark. Baseline characteristics are provided in Table S1. Inclusion criteria were male gender, ≥ 18 and ≤ 40 years, BMI ≥ 18 and < 30 kg/m2, and recreationally active. Exclusion criteria were evidence of cardiovascular disease, rheumatologic disease, metabolic disease, elite sports, or high aerobic training status (VO2max > 60 ml O2/min/kg), frequent or chronic use of medication affecting physical performance or inflammation (including beta-2 agonists, nonsteroidal anti-inflammtory drugs (NSAIDs), disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs)).

Study design

This was a double-blinded, randomized, placebo-controlled study consisting of one screening day and one study visit, the experimental day. On the screening day, participants underwent an electrocardiogram (ECG), height and weight measurements, body composition assessment via dual X-ray absorptiometry, and a cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2max test). Fasting blood samples were also collected. The maximal heart rate and maximal watts measured during the VO2max test were used as reference for the exercise bout performed on the study visit.

Following successful screening, participants were randomly assigned to one of two experimental arms: Placebo (saline infusion) (CON) or infusion of the humanized anti-IL-6 receptor (IL-6R) antibody tocilizumab (BLOCK). The randomization procedure used a 1:1 allocation system with block sizes of 6, totaling 30 participants. A study statistician, who was not involved in any study-related procedures uploaded the allocation sequences to an online trial management system (EasyTrial) for concealment, which was inaccessible to the involved researchers. The randomized participants were then invited to the study visit, the main experimental day (Figure 1A).

On the study visit, participants arrived fasted (≥ 12 h, no alcohol or caffeine) and without having performed moderate to intense exercise for 48 h. They arrived at 8:00 AM at CFAS. Participants were placed in bed in an experimental room and were permitted to drink water ad libitum during the day. The study visit consisted of three phases: a 1.5-hour resting phase (baseline to post-infusion) during which the IL-6R antibody tocilizumab or saline was infused during the first 30 min, a 39-minute exercise phase (indicated on the figure panels from the 1st interval to 7th interval), and a 2-hour recovery phase (indicated on the figure panels from the 7th interval to 2-hour).

An antecubital venous catheter (18G) was placed, and nine blood samples were drawn throughout the study day: at baseline, 90 min later at post-infusion, during exercise at the end of the 1st, 4th, and 7th interval, and every 30 min during the 2-hour post-exercise recovery period (Figure 1A).

High-intensity exercise bout

The exercise bout consisted of a 39-minute interval-based session on a stationary bicycle ergometer (Monark Ergomedic 839E Bicycle; Monark, Varberg, Sweden). After an initial 10-minutes warm-up at 50–60% of HRmax, corresponding to a Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) of 9–11 on the Borg Scale, participants underwent seven verbally encouraged 3-minute intervals targeting an HR above 90% of HRmax. Each high-intensity interval was interspersed with 3 min of low intensity pedaling. The RPE during high-intensity intervals was expected to be > 17, corresponding to > 90% HRmax.

The first interval was set close to 80 % of the maximum watts determined during the VO2max test at screening, aiming to maximize systemic stress and prime the fight-or-flight response. In the subsequent six intervals, watts were continuously adjusted to promote maximal exhaustion, with the goal of maintaining an HR of at least > 90% HRmax.

Watt values were measured before and after each interval, and the mean watts for each interval were calculated. Participants were advised to keep a cadence above 80 rounds per minute (RPM) to avoid exhausting type 2A muscle fibers too rapidly, as per Henneman's size principle [55], which could compromise performance and durability. HR was monitored during the exercise bout (Garmin Forerunner 245, USA Kansas) and recorded at the 1- and 3-minute marks for each interval. Participants rested supine in bed until 2 h post-exercise. In two cases, participants were allowed one interval at a lower intensity (RPE 13–14) due to concerns of collapse. One participant in BLOCK was excluded due to a pronounced vasovagal reaction before the exercise bout, resulting in 29 data sets for analysis.

Tocilizumab

Tocilizumab (RoActemra®; Roche, Basel, Switzerland), a humanized monoclonal IL-6 receptor antibody, was reconstituted in 100 ml of 0.9% saline by qualified on-site staff not otherwise involved in the study and infused over 30 min at a dose of 8 mg/kg body weight, with a maximum dose of 800 mg. The placebo consisted of 100 ml of 0.9% NaCl.

Blood samples

Blood samples were obtained at baseline, post-infusion, after the 1st, 4th, and 7th intervals, and every 0.5-hour during the 2-hour post exercise recovery phase (Figure 1A). Immediate analyses of blood samples at the Department of Clinical Biochemistry at Rigshospitalet included assessment of glucose, lactate, insulin, FFA, ACTH, cortisol, and prolactin using the Sysmex XN 9000 (Sysmex Corp., Kobe, Japan) or Cobas 6800 (Roche diagnostics, Berlin, Germany).

In-house analyses involved isolating plasma from blood collected in EDTA-coated tubes via centrifugation at 2000∗g and 4 °C for 10 min. Plasma samples were aliquoted and immediately placed on dry ice for long-term storage at −80 °C. Whole blood samples were analyzed for potassium, sodium, and blood pH using an ABL 800 flex (Radiometer, Denmark) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Plasma IL-6 was measured using the Proinflammatory Panel 1 kit, and plasma glucagon was measured using the Metabolic Panel 1 kit from MesoScaleDiscovery (Maryland, USA) with the alternate overnight incubation protocol via electrochemiluminescence.

Plasma adrenaline and noradrenaline were analyzed in duplicate using the 2-CAT ELISA fast track from LDN (Nordhorn, Germany) according to manufacturer guidelines. Plasma CRH and ADH were assessed using ELISA kits from Nordic Biosite (Copenhagen, Denmark) according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Inter-assay coefficients of variance (CV) for internal biological high and low controls were as follows: IL-6 (<11%), adrenaline (15%), noradrenaline (7.5%), glucagon (<10%), CRH (<20%) and AVP (<20%).

Ketone bodies

β-hydroxy butyrate [56], acetoacetate and pyruvate [57] were derivatized using a coupling and two different reagents to shield the carboxylic-OH following via a modified method [58]. After derivatization, the derivates were combined for analysis using a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Quantis® LC-MS/MS system) from Thermo Scientific (SanJose, Ca, USA) attached to a Dionex UltiMate 3000 RS dual gradient UHPLC system and UltiMate 3000 RS autosampler. The analysis was conducted at Clinical Metabolomics Core Facility, Clinical Biochemistry, Rigshospitalet.

Cardiopulmonary fitness

Maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) and Watt max and were measured using an incremental exercise test on an electronically braked bicycle ergometer (Monark Exercise AB, Vansbro, Swerden) with concurrent breath-by-breath measurement of VO2 using an indirect calorimetry system (Quark CPET, Cosmed, Rome, Italy). After a 5 min warmup at 100 W, the workload was increased by 35 Watts every minute until the participant was unable to sustain pedaling above 60 rate per minute. The test was valid if the respiratory exchange ratio was > 1.15. Heartrate was continuously monitored (Garmin HRM Dual, USA Kansas).

Anthropometric measurements and body composition

Body weight was measured with 0.1 kg accuracy (ADP calibrated electronic scale), height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm (Holtain stadiometer, Holtain Ltd, Crymych, UK).

Total fat mass and total fat-free mass were determined by whole-body DXA scan (Lunar Prodigy; GE Medical Systems, Madison, WI, USA).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Flow cytometry

Absolute cell counts of lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, and progenitor cells (metamyelocytes, myelocytes, pro-myelocytes) were obtained from the department of clinical biochemistry at Rigshospitalet, Denmark (based on automated flow cytometric analysis using the Sysmex system, Japan).

For the detailed assessment of lymphocyte subsets at baseline, post-infusion, post-exercise and 1-h post exercise, blood was collected in EDTA tubes and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated via density centrifugation (Lymphoprep, StemCell technologies, Canada) and washed once in FACS buffer (PBS + 2% FBS). For surface marker staining, PBMCs were stained using FC-block and live-dead dye for 15 min at room temperature (RT), followed by antibody staining for 30 min at 4 °C. All antibodies were titrated before usage. After fixation in 2% formaldehyde for 10 min at RT, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer and acquired within the next 72 h. For each analysis day, a biological cryopreserved PBMC control was used to monitor day-to-day variation and staining validity. The gating strategy for identification of PBMC subsets is given in Fig. S3.

CV of biological controls were <20% for all immune cell proportions i.e., natural killer, B, T and dendritic cells. Internal controls, fluorescence-minus one controls and single-color stains for compensation were included in every experiment. Cells were acquired using a LSRFortessa (488 nm, 640 nm, 405 nm, 561 nm, 355 nm) maintained by the flow cytometry core facility at Copenhagen University using FACSDiva software v.8.01 (BD Biosciences, USA). Gating and compensation were performed in FlowJo v.10.6.1 (BD Biosciences, USA). For data analysis, researchers were blinded to sample order for each participant i.e., samples were assigned four random numbers before handling, in addition to randomization of group allocation.

Sample size

Only three human studies have investigated the use of the IL-6R antibody tocilizumab in combination with an acute exercise bout [34,59,60], making a proper power calculation difficult. The sample size for this exploratory study was determined based on this previous research examining metabolism and immune cell responses during exercise combined with IL-6R blockade [59]. Trinh et al. [34] used a crossover design with participant numbers ranging from 9 to 13. In our study, we employed a randomized design and anticipated greater variability, and thus increased the sample size to n = 15 per group.

Statistical analysis

To model the effect of IL-6R blockade on outcomes, linear mixed models using maximum likelihood estimations were used [61]. To improve model compliance, the dependent variable (plasma concentration of outcomes) was log-transformed if not stated otherwise. Model compliance was monitored using residual, density, and quantile plots. Results are shown as back-transformed estimated mean differences (EMD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) using estimated marginal means (Lenth, 2020). EMD +95% CI should be interpreted as follows: an EMD of 0.9 equals a relative decrease of 10% in BLOCK compared to CON. An interaction term between the categorized timepoint (9 levels) and the categorized grouping variable (2 levels) was included. A random intercept term was included to account the repeated measures of participants. Additionally, the baseline concentration of each outcome was included as a covariate. Due to the explorative nature of the study, no adjustment for multiple testing was performed. All estimates including p-values, mean and standard deviation are given in the supplement (Tables S2–5).

To quantify the effect of the interaction term as well as the effect of the grouping (effect of group) and timepoint (effect of time) variables, linear mixed models missing either the interaction term or the respective categorized variables were constructed and compared to the complete model using analysis of variance (R Core Team, 2019).

To model the effect of IL-6R blockade on continuous outcomes of exercise physiology, linear mixed models were used as described above. In contrast, no log-transformation was used, and estimates are therefore given as absolute values.

The total area under the curve (AUC) for each parameter was calculated using the trapezoidal method in GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.0). Comparisons between the two groups were performed using either an unpaired t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Data distribution was assessed visually and through statistical tests of normality. The study was conducted in a randomized design, and group comparisons were two-tailed with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Correlation analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism using Spearman's correlation coefficient analysis. All analyses were performed in R (v.4.2.2.), R-Studio (v.2022.12.0) and GraphPad Prism (10.1.0). Statistical significance was set to p < 0.05 and estimated mean differences are presented including 95% confidence intervals if not indicated otherwise. P-values < 0.1 will be addressed as trends.
