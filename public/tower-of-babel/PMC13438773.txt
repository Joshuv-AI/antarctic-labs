Tetravalent organic cation-enabled dual interfacial regulation for durable aqueous zinc–iodine batteries
Authors: Doudou Feng, Yanchun Xie, Yinlin Shen, Hao Zhang, Xuan Chen, Peng-Fei Wang, Jiaqian Qin, Yucong Jiao, Jijian Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterfacial instability from polyiodide shuttling and zinc electrode degradation critically limits the reversibility of aqueous zinc–iodine batteries. Existing mono-valent organic cation strategies offer insufficient charge density and pose challenges in maintaining interfacial protection under dynamic electric field conditions. Here, we present an electrolyte design incorporating a high-valent organic cation (HVOC) —a tetravalent cation derived from 1,1,4,4,7,7,10,10-octaallyltetraazacyclodecane trifluoromethanesulfonate—that enables electrostatically engineered bipolar interfaces. The HVOC establishes a durable cationic positive electrode–electrolyte interphase on the iodine electrode that not only effectively immobilizes polyiodide species through strong electrostatic interactions under dynamic field reversals, but also facilitates iodine redox kinetics. Simultaneously, the HVOC drives the formation of a hybrid organic–inorganic solid electrolyte interphase on the zinc electrode that homogenizes zinc deposition and mitigates side reactions. This bipolar interfacial regulation enables zinc–iodine batteries with an N/P ratio of 2.43 to achieve 40.7% of zinc utilization and 95.4% of iodine utilization, with minimal capacity decay of 0.016% per cycle over 1,000 cycles at 0.4 A g–1, and stable performance across a wide temperature range from –40 to 60 oC. This work presents a viable electrolyte design paradigm using HVOC chemistry for advanced aqueous battery systems.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

Achieving a sustainable future requires the widespread deployment of low-cost, safe, and efficient energy storage systems that can address the mismatch between renewable energy production and consumption. Among various candidates, aqueous zinc–iodine (Zn||I2) batteries have emerged as a promising solution for grid-scale electric energy storage due to their intrinsic safety, low cost, and the natural abundance of Zn (~79 ppm in Earth’s crust) and I2 (50–60 μg L−1 in oceans). However, the practical deployment of Zn||I2 systems is fundamentally hindered by severe interfacial instability at both electrodes1–3. The key challenge lies in the uncontrolled dissolution and shuttling of polyiodide species (e.g., I3−, I5−), which not only deplete active materials at the iodine electrode but also migrate across the electrolyte and trigger parasitic reactions at the Zn electrode. These reactions induce Zn corrosion, dendritic growth, and hydrogen evolution, resulting in low coulombic efficiency (CE), limited Zn utilization, and compromised cycle life4,5. These strongly interrelated degradation pathways, polyiodide shuttling, Zn corrosion, and interfacial instability, collectively highlight the urgent need for integrated and durable interfacial regulation strategies.

To address these challenges, electrolyte engineering has emerged as an effective strategy to simultaneously manage interfacial processes at both electrodes. Unlike electrode modification or separator modification6–8, electrolyte engineering governs the dynamic solid–liquid interface during the battery operation. Previous strategies have included designing functionalized gel electrolytes (e.g., zwitterionic polymers)9–12, employing highly concentrated electrolytes13,14, and introducing molecular additives15–17. However, many of these strategies suffer from trade-offs between viscosity, ionic conductivity, cost, and scalability.

Recently, a particularly promising avenue in electrolyte design involves using cation chemistry to modulate electrode electrolyte interfaces through electrostatic interactions. Cations that preferentially accumulate at the positive electrode surface can electrostatically anchor polyiodide species, while also modulate Zn deposition and reduce water activity at the negative electrode18,19. To date, the vast majority of electrolytes rely on mono-valent organic cations (MVOCs), such as quaternary ammonium or imidazolium ions14,20–27 (Fig. 1). These cations have proven effective in coordinating with polyiodide species and mitigating shuttle effects. However, as guided by Coulomb’s law (F = q1q2/4πεd2), the electrostatic binding force is directly proportional to ionic charge28, suggesting further opportunities for optimization16,29.

Building upon this foundation, we hypothesize that high-valence organic cations (HVOCs), with their intrinsically higher charge density, can offer stronger and more persistent electrostatic interactions at both positive and negative electrode interfaces. HVOCs are expected to significantly enhance the electrostatic confinement of polyiodide species, effectively suppressing their dissolution and shuttle effects. Simultaneously, HVOCs can stabilize the Zn electrode interface through stronger electrostatic shielding, promoting uniform Zn deposition (Fig. 1)30,31. Despite these promising features, HVOC-based electrolytes remain underexplored in aqueous Zn||I2 batteries. Therefore, systematic investigations are imperative to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and the interfacial regulation potential of HVOCs. Achieving synergistic stabilization of both electrodes could overcome polyiodide shuttling and Zn electrode degradation, thereby advancing the development of durable and high-performance Zn||I2 batteries.

In this work, we report a rational electrolyte design strategy that introduces a tetravalent HVOC, 1,1,4,4,7,7,10,10-octaallyltetraazacyclodecane trifluoromethanesulfonate (OTA(OTF)4), into the aqueous electrolyte to enable long-term stabilization of both the positive and negative electrodes. This HVOC establishes a highly charged electrostatic center that strongly confines polyiodide species. Comprehensive operando UV–vis and Raman spectroscopy, combined with theoretical simulations, confirm the markedly suppressed evolution of soluble polyiodides in the HVOC electrolyte under dynamic cycling conditions. Additionally, the HVOC significantly lowers the activation energy barrier for iodine redox conversion, thereby facilitating reaction kinetics and improving redox reversibility. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) analysis reveal modulated Zn coordination environments in the HVOC electrolytes, driving the formation of stable, hydrophobic cationic layers at both the I2 positive and Zn negative electrode interfaces. At the negative electrode, HVOC promotes the formation of an organic–inorganic solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) comprising cation–ZnFx/ZnSx complexes, which enhances Zn deposition kinetics. Leveraging these synergistic effects, the Zn||I2 battery delivers a high capacity of 201.4 mA h g−1 with an iodine mass loading of 22 mg cm−2, maintains stable cycling over 70,000 cycles at 5 A g−1, and demonstrates wide-temperature adaptability from −40 to 60 °C with good capacity retention. This work introduces an effective strategy in electrolyte design by utilizing high-valent cation chemistry to unlock durable Zn||I2 batteries for sustainable energy storage.

Results

Effect of cation valence on polyiodides immobilization

The chemical structures of the MVOC and HVOC are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, with the anion consistently fixed as trifluoromethanesulfonate (OTF−). Specifically, OTA(OTF)4 with HVOC (+4) and full name (DAAOTF) with MVOC (+1) were synthesized via nucleophilic substitution and subsequent anion exchange reactions, and their successful preparation was confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Liquid chromatography (LC) analysis indicates the sample is of high purity (Supplementary Fig. 4a). High-resolution mass spectrometry shows a prominent signal at m/z = 124.11 (Supplementary Fig. 4b), in good agreement with the expected mass feature of the OTA4+ cation28. Consistently, N 1s X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) indicates that nitrogen species are predominantly present in the quaternized C–N+ state, suggesting a high degree of quaternization (Supplementary Fig. 5). The synthesis of HVOC is readily scalable to gram-scale batches using standard laboratory procedures (Supplementary Fig. 6). To elucidate their distinct interactions with iodine species, theoretical calculations were conducted to compare the electronic properties of HVOC and MVOC. As shown in the molecular electrostatic potential (MESP) maps in Fig. 2a, HVOC exhibits a significantly higher electrostatic potential compared to MVOC. Notably, a concentrated positive region is formed within the HVOC macrocyclic cavity, providing a strong coulombic center capable of effectively confining negatively charged polyiodides (Supplementary Fig. 7). Frontier molecular orbital analysis further supports these findings. HVOC displays a lower lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level than MVOC, indicating enhanced electrophilicity and a higher propensity for binding with electron-rich polyiodide anions (Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover, the energy gap between the LUMO of HVOC and the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) levels of iodine redox species is smaller than that of MVOC (Supplementary Fig. 9), suggesting better orbital overlap and thus stronger complexation32. Binding energies between cations and iodine species were further analyzed (Fig. 2b). HVOC shows substantially more negative adsorption energies with I−, I3−, I5−, and I2 than MVOC, indicating stronger interactions. The higher affinity of HVOC for polyiodides over molecular iodine suggests that the dominant interaction arises from the electrostatic attraction, which effectively inhibits the shuttle and dissolution of polyiodide species. Charge density difference calculations provide further insight into the interaction mechanism (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 10)33–35. Green regions near iodine atoms indicate charge accumulation, while blue regions around nitrogen atoms in HVOC indicate charge depletion, identifying the electrostatic center as the primary binding site. Due to steric hindrance within the macrocycle, polyiodide ions remain outside the ring, forming stable complexes.

To visually confirm the polyiodide capture capability, HVOC and MVOC were introduced into Zn(I3)2 solutions (Fig. 2d). The solution of bare electrolyte of 3 m Zn(OTF)2 (donated as BE) remained dark brown due to the high solubility of polyiodides. Upon addition of MVOC, even in quadruple molar quantities (4MVOC) to match the charge density of HVOC, partial precipitation occurred, but a significant brown coloration persisted after 12 h. In contrast, the HVOC-containing solution exhibited rapid phase separation and a visibly lighter supernatant, indicative of efficient polyiodide confinement. UV–vis spectroscopy of the supernatants further supports this observation. In BE, the absorption intensity of I3− absorption bands at 287.9 and 351.4 nm7 remained essentially unchanged, indicating its high solubility (Supplementary Fig. 11). When MVOC and 4MVOC were introduced, the absorbance decreased to approximately 24.3% and 17.3% of its original value, respectively. By contrast, the HVOC-containing electrolyte nearly eliminated the I3− absorption bands (Fig. 2e), affirming its effective capability for polyiodide capture and confinement. XPS analysis further shows that the N 1s in HVOC&@ I3− shifts to lower binding energy (Supplementary Fig. 12), attributable to partial charge transfer from iodide to the nitrogen-containing sites7. This behavior further supports strong electrostatic interaction between HVOC and polyiodide species at the iodine electrode interface.

HVOC-driven modulation of interfacial electric double layer

To further elucidate the interfacial role of HVOC during electrochemical processes, the electric double layer (EDL) structure at the electrode/electrolyte interface was investigated using MD simulations. A full-cell MD model was constructed in which both electrodes were polarized simultaneously, enabling quantitative analysis of ion and solvent distributions under the coupled electric fields of discharge and charge36. As shown in Fig. 3a, the BE electrolyte forms a water-rich interfacial layer at the iodine electrode during discharge. These interfacial water molecules are highly reactive and facilitate the dissolution of polyiodide species and I2, giving rise to pronounced shuttle effects and rapid capacity decay. In contrast, MVOC and HVOC-containing electrolytes exhibit distinct interfacial structures under the same conditions. Driven by the applied electric field, MVOC or HVOC molecules migrate toward the positive electrode, forming cation-enriched and water-deficient interfacial layers (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 13). The corresponding ion distribution profiles corroborate this interfacial enrichment (Fig. 3c), while the water density profiles further show that HVOC achieves the most pronounced suppression of interfacial water (Fig. 3d). For the charging state at the Zn electrode, in BE, the absence of cationic species leads to persistently water-rich interfaces at both the positive and negative electrode (Fig. 3a). In the MVOC system, the lower charge density of MVOC results in substantial field-driven migration toward the I2 electrode, leaving negligible interfacial coverage at the Zn electrode (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 13). In contrast, HVOC maintains a measurable interfacial presence at the Zn electrode (Fig. 3b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 14), which increases the separation between water molecules and the electrode compared with BE and MVOC.

To further analyze the evolution of the interfaces across different electrochemical states, we reversed the field polarity to simulate the transition from discharge to charge (Supplementary Fig. 15). In BE, the interfacial environment remains water-rich at both electrodes. For MVOC and HVOC, the simulations reveal that HVOC remains partially accumulated at the iodine electrode during charging, thereby suppressing undesired side reactions. At the same time, both MVOC and HVOC migrate toward the Zn electrode and form cation-enriched layers that increase the separation between water molecules and the electrode surface. Importantly, theoretical calculations revealed that HVOC possess a substantially lower LUMO energy level and a narrower HOMO–LUMO gap than MVOC (Supplementary Fig. 16), indicating that HVOC is thermodynamically more prone to decomposition37. This electrochemical preference enables the in situ formation of a stable, protective cationic layer that persists even under alternating electric fields or during cell resting conditions, offering long-term interfacial protection.

To further confirm the interfacial adsorption behavior, in situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to track the C–H stretching vibration band at ~2953.4 cm−1 for HVOC (Supplementary Fig. 17) in real time28. On the I2 electrode, the C–H stretching vibration increases in intensity during the discharge, and slightly diminishes during charging but remains detectable, indicating persistent cation adsorption at the interface (Fig. 3e). In contrast, the Zn electrode exhibits an opposite trend, where the C–H peak increases during charging and decreases during discharging, consistent with the MD simulations (Fig. 3f). Zeta potential measurements on both electrodes retrieved at discharged (0.6 V, D) and charged (1.8 V, C) states further support this behavior. Electrodes cycled in the presence of HVOC exhibit more positive zeta potential than those in BE or MVOC (Fig. 3g, h). Although the zeta potential of the charged I2 electrode and discharged Zn electrode are lower than those of their corresponding counter-states (Supplementary Fig. 18), both remain higher than the initial electrode values, and the HVOC-based electrodes consistently show the highest zeta potential among the three systems, indicating the formation of a cationic layer. Additionally, the octanol–water partition coefficients (logP)19 of HVOC and MVOC were calculated (Supplementary Fig. 19). HVOC exhibits a significantly higher logP value of 7.36 compared to 1.57 for MVOC, demonstrating its stronger hydrophobicity. This property contributes to the formation of a more robust hydrophobic environment at the electrode surface, which is beneficial for suppressing the dissolution of polyiodides and water-induced side reactions at the Zn electrode.

In addition to modulating interfacial water within the inner Helmholtz layer, the solvation environment of Zn2+ in the outer Helmholtz layer also affects the formation and composition of the SEI at the Zn electrode38. MD simulations revealed that compared with BE, the average coordination number of water in the primary solvation shell decreases from 5.0 to 4.6, accompanied by an increase in OTF− average coordination from 1.0 to 1.4 (Fig. 3i). Beyond the average coordination statistics, HVOC also redistributes the detailed solvation structures, as summarized schematically and quantified in Supplementary Fig. 20. This enhanced Zn2+–OTF coordination originates from the combined effects of ionic size, steric hindrance, and interactions between HVOC and water (Supplementary Fig. 21). Spectroscopic analyses further validate this solvation structure reconfiguration. Both 1H and 19F NMR spectra exhibit significant upfield shifts upon increasing the HVOC content (Supplementary Fig. 22), indicating altered hydrogen-bonding environments and coordination states of H2O and OTF− in the electrolyte39. Raman spectroscopy shows a progressive shift of the ν-SO3 band toward higher wavenumbers together with an increased population of contact ion pairs and aggregate (AGG) species (Supplementary Fig. 23), reflecting more Zn2+–OTF− coordination40. Complementary theoretical calculations show that OTF− coordinated to Zn2+ exhibits an elevated reduction potential relative to free OTF− (Supplementary Fig. 24), indicating that Zn2+–OTF− structures are more prone to reductive decomposition at the interface41, thus contributing to a ZnFx and ZnSx-rich SEI that protects the Zn surface and promotes ion transport. This structural transformation is further validated by X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis. Zn k-edge XANES results show that the near-edge absorption energy of Zn foil is the lowest and corresponds to its zero-valent state (Fig. 3j)42. Compared to BE, the absorption edge of Zn in the HVOC system shifts toward lower energy, suggesting a decreased average charge density around Zn2+ due to enhanced OTF− coordination43. Furthermore, extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra in R space (Supplementary Fig. 25) and the corresponding wavelet transform reveal a shortened Zn–O bond length in HVOC (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 26), further confirming the reconfigured solvation environment44,45. Additionally, low-field 1H NMR (LF–NMR) measurements show a continuous decrease in the T2 relaxation time of water protons with the increasing HVOC concentration, signifying a pronounced reduction in water activity and mobility (Supplementary Fig. 27)46. These results are consistent with the MD simulations. Theoretical calculations combined with spectroscopic analyses indicate that HVOC has the potential to facilitate the formation of robust positive electrode electrolyte interface (PEI) and SEI layers on the electrode surface, thereby enhancing sustained interfacial protection.

Surface chemistry–interphase composition correlation with HVOC

To experimentally validate the interfacial stabilization effects of HVOC and correlate interfacial properties with interphase composition, XPS and time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) were conducted to characterize the PEI and SEI layers formed in different electrolytes. For the XPS results, the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV was used as the reference for calibration (Supplementary Fig. 28). Figure 4a, b present the compositional evolution of organic and inorganic species on the I2 electrode and Zn electrode after cycling. With HVOC, the N 1s signal at 401.1 eV is mainly attributed to the HVOC, which exhibits a pronounced surface enrichment and gradually decreases with sputtering time, confirming the formation of a cation-derived organic-rich interphase on both electrodes. Deconvolution of the F 1s and S 2p regions provided further insight into the inorganic constituents of the SEI at the Zn electrode. Specifically, the F 1s peaks at 688.8 and 686.5 eV corresponded to CF* and ZnFx, respectively, while the S 2p signals were assigned to SO3 (S 2p3/2 at 169.3 eV and S 2p1/2 at 170.5 eV) and ZnSx (S 2p3/2 at 162.2 eV and S 2p1/2 at 163.6 eV) species47,48. The normalized intensities of ZnFx and ZnSx increased significantly with sputtering depth, suggesting their effective and continuous accumulation in the inner SEI region. In contrast, electrodes cycled with BE exhibited negligible N 1s signals and substantially lower levels of ZnFx and ZnSx (Fig. 4b–d). This pronounced difference originates from the enhanced formation of Zn2+–OTF− ion pairs in the HVOC-containing electrolyte, which contributes to the formation of a more rich ZnFx and ZnSx interfacial layer. This interphase exhibits higher ionic conductivity, indicating unobstructed Zn2+ transport across the inorganic-rich SEI (Supplementary Fig. 29a, b). The Zn–ZnF2 and Zn–ZnS binding energies (−1.24 and −4.09 eV, respectively) are significantly more negative than that of Zn–Zn metal (−0.44 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 29c–e). Such strong interfacial interactions facilitate the removal of water molecules from the Zn2+ solvation shell, thereby accelerating Zn2+ desolvation and enhancing ion transport kinetics49,50. Meanwhile, the ZnFx and ZnS-rich SEI serves as an electronically insulating (Supplementary Fig. 30a, b) and mechanically robust interphase (3.7 Gpa for BE and 10.5 Gpa for HVOC, Supplementary Fig. 30c, d), effectively accommodating volume changes and relieving interfacial stress during repeated Zn plating/stripping51.

To further resolve the spatial evolution of these interfacial species, TOF-SIMS was conducted employing C2H4N+, ZnF−, and ZnS− as diagnostic fragments representative of the decomposition products of HVOC and OTF−52. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32, the C2H4N+ signal was predominantly localized near the electrode surface and declined with depth, indicating that the organic interphase derived from HVOC decomposition is located at the outermost region. In parallel, at the Zn electrode, the ZnF− and ZnS− signals in the HVOC system were markedly enhanced compared to those in BE, verifying the formation of inorganic-rich inner layers (Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34). The very weak N-related signal observed in the TOF-SIMS mapping of the BE-cycled electrode is attributed to trace surface contaminants introduced by conductive tape during sample handling. Three-dimensional (3D) ion mapping (Fig. 4e) and the corresponding two-dimensional (2D) element distribution mapping (Supplementary Figs. 35 and 36) further revealed distinct vertical concentration gradients and uniform lateral distribution of these species across the electrode surfaces.

Taken together, these findings substantiate that the HVOC additive induces distinct interphase architectures on the I2 electrode and Zn electrode. On the positive electrode side, a hydrophobic organic-rich PEI layer is formed, which acts as a durable barrier to effectively suppress polyiodide shuttling and water-triggered side reactions, thereby contributing to long-term positive electrode stability. Meanwhile, the Zn electrode develops a dual-layered interphase composed of an organic-rich outer layer and an inorganic-rich inner framework dominated by ZnFx and ZnSx species, which collectively optimize Zn deposition behavior and suppress dendrite formation and parasitic reactions. These interfacial architectures align well with the EDL and solvation structure modulations discussed above (Fig. 3), highlighting the multifaceted role of HVOC in enabling sustained interfacial stability through controlled molecular interactions and decomposition pathways.

Visualizing shuttle suppression and redox kinetics acceleration by HVOC

To directly visualize the suppression of the polyiodide shuttle during operation of Zn||I2 batteries, in situ UV–vis and Raman spectroscopy were conducted. In the BE system, pronounced absorption peaks for I3− are observed during the discharge, confirming the formation and diffusion of polyiodide ions (Fig. 5a). With the introduction of MVOC, the I3− signals are partially attenuated, indicating a moderate suppression effect (Fig. 5b). In sharp contrast, the HVOC-based system exhibits almost no detectable I3− signal throughout the discharge process, demonstrating the strong confinement of polyiodide species by the HVOC induced cationic layer (Fig. 5c). To further validate this confinement effect, in situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to monitor the evolution of I3− and I5− species (at ~106.2 and ~169.2 cm−1, respectively) over three continuous discharge–charge cycles53. In BE, the intensities of both peaks increase progressively with cycling, indicating the accumulation of soluble polyiodides (Fig. 5d). Although MVOC reduces the initial signal intensity, the peaks also grow over time, suggesting that shuttle suppression is insufficient (Fig. 5e). Remarkably, in the HVOC system, the signals of both I3− and I5− decline with each cycle and are nearly undetectable after the third cycle (Fig. 5f), confirming that HVOC significantly inhibits the dissolution and migration of polyiodide species during repeated cycling.

Beyond suppressing the shuttle effect, HVOC also promotes the redox kinetics of iodine species. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that the stronger interaction between HVOC and I3− perturbs the internal bonding structure of polyiodides. Specifically, the I–I bond length increases from 3.04 Å in BE to 3.26 Å in HVOC, with the bond order decreasing from 0.25 (BE) to 0.12 (HVOC) (Fig. 5g), indicating a weakened bond strength and enhanced dissociation. Moreover, the Gibbs free energy (ΔG) for each step in the sequential reduction of I2 was calculated (Fig. 5h). The overall reaction I2 → I5− is exergonic (ΔG < 0) in both MVOC and HVOC, confirming the spontaneity of iodine reduction. Furthermore, the ΔG for the I5− → I− transformation is significantly lower in the HVOC system, indicating an accelerated reaction rate7. Electrochemical characterization further confirms this improvement. Cyclic voltammetry measurements were conducted at varying scan rates on Zn||I2 batteries (Supplementary Fig. 37), and the R1 peak was analysed using the Randles–Sevcik relationship54. The reduction peak current density of R1 exhibited a linear correlation with the square root of the scan rate (v1/2), yielding a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.997, indicating that the R1 process in HVOC most closely follows Randles–Sevcik behaviour and thus exhibits more reversible kinetics14 (Fig. 5i). The results of galvanostatic intermittent titration technique revealed that the HVOC based electrolyte exhibited the highest Zn2+ diffusion coefficient, further confirming its enhanced ion transport kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 38). Spectroscopic characterization, theoretical calculations, and electrochemical analysis collectively demonstrate that HVOC suppresses polyiodide dissolution and shuttle by strengthening interactions with iodine species and reducing reaction energy barriers, thereby further enhancing the reaction kinetics.

HVOC-regulated Zn nucleation and deposition behavior

In Zn-based battery systems, the Zn nucleation and deposition behavior plays a critical role in determining negative electrode lifespan55. In situ optical microscopy was employed to visualize Zn deposition under different electrolyte conditions. As Fig. 6a shows, in the BE, severe dendrite formation was observed after only 40 min of plating at a current density of 5 mA cm−2, accompanied by the appearance of gas bubbles at extended plating times. With MVOC, hydrogen evolution was alleviated, but the deposited Zn layer remained relatively thick and uneven (Fig. 6b). In sharp contrast, the HVOC electrolyte enabled uniform Zn deposition without dendrite formation or gas evolution, even after continuous plating for 120 min (Fig. 6c), highlighting the beneficial regulation of Zn deposition by the added cations. The interfacial environment, which critically influences Zn nucleation and parasitic reactions, was further examined by in situ Raman spectroscopy. During Zn plating, the 2952 cm−1 (HVOC-related peak) peak gradually increases, while the O–H band at 3000–3700 cm−1 corresponds to the O–H of water decreases (Supplementary Fig. 39a). This trend indicates field-induced adsorption of HVOC onto the Zn surface, which reduces the number of water molecules in close to the electrode and consequently suppresses possible water decomposition. In contrast, the BE electrolyte maintains a strong O–H stretching signal, suggesting a persistently water-rich interface (Supplementary Fig. 39b). To further elucidate the interfacial water structure, the O–H stretching envelope was further deconvoluted within the 3000–3800 cm−1 region16. In the HVOC electrolyte, the fraction of weak hydrogen bonds (W–H) decreases during plating (Supplementary Figs. 40 and 42). This evolution indicates that HVOC adsorption suppresses the presence of activated water molecules that are capable of coordinating Zn2+ or participating in hydrogen evolution56. By lowering interfacial water activity, HVOC helps to stabilize Zn deposition and suppress parasitic reactions. In contrast, the BE system retains a high proportion of W–H water (Supplementary Figs. 41 and 42), consistent with abundant Zn-coordinated water and the higher susceptibility to side reactions.

The interfacial evolution of OTF− during Zn plating was also monitored by in-situ Raman spectroscopy. The bands at 1035.5 and 1229.5 cm−1 are assigned to the ν-SO3 and ν-CF3 vibrations of OTF−, respectively57. During plating in the HVOC-containing electrolyte, the intensities of both peaks gradually decrease (Supplementary Fig. 43a), whereas only a slight decrease is observed in BE (Supplementary Fig. 43b). This suggests that more extensive OTF− decomposition occurs at the interface in the HVOC system, contributing to the formation of an inorganic-rich interphase (Fig. 4).

Electrochemical stability window (ESW) measurements further confirmed the impact of the electrolyte composition. HVOC exhibits the ESW (Supplementary Fig. 44) and the lowest hydrogen evolution reaction potential (Fig. 6d), indicating enhanced suppression of side reactions. Tafel analysis shows that the HVOC electrolyte exhibits a larger corrosion potential and lower corrosion current than BE and MVOC, indicating a reduced corrosion tendency and slower corrosion rate58 (Supplementary Fig. 45). The nucleation and growth behavior of Zn was further investigated via chronoamperometry (CA) (Fig. 6e). In BE, Zn nucleation undergoes a prolonged 2D diffusion-controlled regime (97 s) (Supplementary Fig. 46a), indicating a low nucleation density and the formation of relatively large Zn nuclei, which is unfavorable for uniform growth and promotes the formation of dendritic morphologies59. In the MVOC electrolyte, the duration of the 2D region is shortened (67 s) (Supplementary Fig. 46b), suggesting a moderate improvement in nucleation behavior. In contrast, the HVOC electrolyte exhibits an obvious shorter 2D diffusion regime (21 s) and transitions rapidly into the 3D growth region (Supplementary Fig. 46c). This accelerated transition corresponds to a higher nucleation density and enables more uniform Zn deposition, thereby suppressing dendrite formation. SEM observations are consistent with the CA results, showing more uniformly distributed Zn nuclei in the HVOC electrolyte than that in BE (Supplementary Fig. 47). In situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis was further conducted to probe interfacial stability during Zn deposition. For HVOC, the interfacial resistance initially increased due to in situ adsorbed cationic layer, then gradually decreased and stabilized as ZnFx and ZnFx-rich SEI formed, facilitating ion transport and lowering charge-transfer resistance (Rct) (Supplementary Fig. 48). Further activation energy (Ea) calculations revealed that HVOC significantly reduces the desolvation barrier for Zn2+, promoting favorable nucleation and deposition behavior58 (Supplementary Fig. 49).

To assess Zn reversibility, asymmetrical Zn||Cu cells were tested at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 with a capacity of 5 mA h cm−2 (38.9% depth of discharge (DOD)), revealing that the optimal HVOC concentration is about 0.15 m (Supplementary Fig. 50). The effect of Zn(OTF)2 concentration was further evaluated, showing that electrolytes containing 2–3 m Zn(OTF)2 provide balanced Zn reversibility and cycling stability, with 3 m exhibiting slightly higher performance (Supplementary Fig. 51). Accordingly, an electrolyte formulation of 3 m Zn(OTF)2 with 0.15 m HVOC was adopted in all subsequent tests unless otherwise specified. At 1 mA cm−2,1 mA h cm–2 or 5 mA cm−2, 5 mA h cm−2, Zn||Cu cells with HVOC demonstrated stable cycling over 1100 (>2200 h) and 70 (>140 h) cycles (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 52). In Zn||Zn symmetrical cells, the lifetime exceeded 1200 h at 1 mA cm−2,1 mA h cm−2 (7.77% DOD, Supplementary Fig. 53), 600 h at 1 mA cm−2, 5 mA h cm−2 (38.9% DOD, Supplementary Fig. 54). Even at 1 mA cm−2, 10 mA h cm−2 (77.7% DOD), a cycling lifespan exceeding 150 h was achieved with HVOC, surpassing the performance of both BE (2 h) and MVOC (107 h) (Fig. 6g). The increased overpotential observed in HVOC is associated with interfacial electrostatic shielding or competitive adsorption induced by high-valent cations at the Zn surface30,31. To simulate practical operation conditions, intermittent cycling tests were conducted with rest intervals of 24, 48, 96, and 192 h. Even under these stringent conditions, HVOC-based Zn||Zn cells maintained stable operation beyond 2530 h (Fig. 6h), demonstrating effective suppression of interfacial side reactions and highlighting its promise for real-world applications.

Electrochemical performance of Zn||I2 batteries across a wide temperatures

Benefiting from the dual roles of HVOC in optimizing electrode–electrolyte interfaces and promoting the redox kinetics of iodine species, the performance of Zn||I2 batteries assembled with HVOC electrolyte was systematically evaluated across a wide temperature range. The rate performance of Zn||I2 batteries was performed. The HVOC-based battery delivered higher capacities and good rate capabilities, retaining 74.6% of the capacity at 15 A g−1 relative to that at 0.2 A g−1, with a reversible capacity of 171.7 mA h g−1 and the nearly symmetric charge–discharge profiles reflected accelerated redox kinetics and enhanced reversibility (Fig. 7a). The observed capacity exceeding the theoretical value of 211 mA h g−1 for the I0/I− redox couple is attributed to the additional contribution from the YP50 carbon host, which delivers a capacity of 31.0 mA h g−1 at 0.2 A g−1 (corresponds to ~97.7 mA h g−1 based on I2 loading)14 (Supplementary Fig. 55). Long-term cycling performance was also investigated. At a low specific current density of 0.2 A g−1, batteries using BE and MVOC-based electrolytes exhibited rapid capacity fading with capacity retention of 51.3% and 61.6% after 400 cycles, respectively. In stark contrast, batteries using HVOC-based electrolyte maintained a highly stable cycling performance with 85.0% capacity retention over 400 cycles, confirming its effective suppression of undesired side reactions even at a lower specific current density of 0.2 A g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 56). At 1 A g−1, stable cycling over 10,000 cycles was achieved (Supplementary Fig. 57). Notably, at a high specific current density of 5 A g−1, the HVOC-based battery demonstrated good cycling durability, outperforming that with both BE and MVOC electrolytes by sustaining over 70,000 cycles with a high average CE of 99.8% (Fig. 7b). Moreover, after 3300 cycles at 10 A g−1, the battery maintained stable capacity stability even with resting intervals ranging from 12 to 72 h (Supplementary Fig. 58), verifying effective inhibition of polyiodide shuttling and iodine dissolution. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 59) reveal severe corrosion on the Zn electrode after 50 and 500 cycles in the baseline electrolyte, due to the polyiodide shuttle. In the MVOC system, corrosion is negligible at 50 cycles but becomes evident after 500 cycles. In contrast, the HVOC electrolyte maintains a smooth Zn surface even after 500 cycles, confirming its effective suppression of polyiodide shuttling.

For practical considerations, cells with high iodine loading (7.5 mg cm−2) and a low N/P ratio of 2.43 (corresponding to a Zn DOD of 40.7%) were evaluated. At 0.4 A g−1, a capacity of 1.51 mA h cm−2 can be achieved, with 89.4% retention after 1000 cycles and an average CE of 99.6% (Fig. 7c). Owing to interfacial regulation and facilitated redox kinetics, a high iodine utilization of 95.4% was achieved. Even under a high iodine loading of 22 mg cm−2, the HVOC-based battery maintained 100% capacity retention and a reversible capacity of 190.1 mA g−1 after 250 cycles at 0.35 A g−1 (Fig. 7d). The effectiveness of HVOC at this fixed concentration under high iodine loading conditions arises from the high charge density of the tetravalent cation, which enables strong electrostatic association with polyiodide species and the iodine electrode surface. Simultaneously, the selected concentration (0.15 m) of HVOC preserves Zn deposition reversibility and favorable electrolyte transport properties (Supplementary Figs. 60 and 61). The temperature adaptability of the system was further explored. Compared with room temperature (25 °C), the cell retained 89.2%, 86.0%, 81.3%, 72.0%, and 55.8% of its room temperature capacity at 0, −10, −20, −30, and −40 °C, respectively (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 62). Despite the exacerbation of interfacial side reactions at elevated temperatures, the HVOC-based cell demonstrated stable thermal performance, retaining 85.3% capacity retention and average CE of 99.8% after 100 cycles at 60 °C, with an iodine mass loading of 17.1 mg cm−2 at 3 A g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 63). To validate practical applicability, pouch cells were fabricated and tested (Supplementary Table 1). A two-layer pouch cell exhibited a capacity of 0.14 Ah after 60 cycles, with an average CE of 99.2% (Fig. 7f), confirming its promise for real-world energy storage applications. Notably, in terms of both iodine loading and long-term capacity retention, HVOC-based Zn||I2 batteries show competitive performance compared with recent reports10,20,24,27,60–63 (Fig. 7g and Supplementary Table 2), highlighting their significant potential for next-generation wide-temperature aqueous battery technologies.

The effectiveness of the electrolyte regulation strategy is retained in more cost-effective ZnSO4-based electrolytes, where HVOC continues to improve Zn reversibility and Zn||I2 full-cell stability, indicating that the approach is not restricted to a specific salt chemistry (Supplementary Fig. 64). Enlarged analyses of both the initial and long-term cycling regions reveal that HVOC-containing system exhibits more stable cycling behavior with consistently high CE than BE and MVOC, highlighting the role of HVOC in improving the cycling performance. Compared with ZnSO4, Zn(OTF)2-based electrolytes offer additional benefits, including enhanced antifreezing capability (Supplementary Fig. 65) and a greater tendency to form ZnFx and ZnSx-rich interphases that favor interfacial stabilization. Moreover, systematic comparisons across structurally related organic cations with different valence states reveal that higher cation valence consistently affords stronger interfacial regulation than monovalent analogues (Supplementary Figs. 66–68). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the proposed HVOC–based strategy possesses broad applicability and conceptual generality for interfacial regulation in aqueous Zn||I2 batteries.

Discussion

In summary, this study introduces an HVOC approach that effectively addresses the longstanding challenge of interfacial instability in aqueous Zn||I2 batteries through synergistic stabilization of interfaces at both electrodes. The tetravalent macrocyclic cations facilitate the formation of a resilient hydrophobic cationic layer at the iodine electrode, significantly restricting polyiodide migration and maintaining redox reaction efficiency even under dynamic cycling conditions. Concurrently, the HVOC-driven hybrid organic–inorganic solid electrolyte interphase at the Zn electrode, featuring cation-ZnFx/ZnSx architectures that ensure homogeneous Zn deposition and suppress detrimental side reactions. This dual interfacial engineering strategy remarkably improves the I2 and Zn utilization rate, enhances areal capacity (0.14 Ah), and prolongs cycle life under practically relevant operating parameters. Specifically, the Zn||I2 battery (N/P ratio = 2.43) delivers a high iodine utilization of 95.4%, Zn utilization of 40.7%, achieves a capacity of 201.4 mA h g−1 at 22 mg cm−2 iodine loading, and sustains stable cycling over 1000 cycles with only 0.016% capacity decay per cycle, as well as long cycling beyond 70,000 cycles at 5 A g−1. Overall, the demonstrated HVOC framework offers a way for rational electrolyte design that couples high-valence ion chemistry with robust interphase formation for advancing next-generation energy storage devices.

Methods

Materials

1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane (≥97%), potassium carbonate (K2CO3, 99%), anhydrous acetonitrile (99.8%), lithium trifluoromethanesulfonate (LiOTF, 99.5%), silver(I) trifluoromethanesulfonate (AgOTf, 97%), iodine, allyl bromide (98%) and activated carbon (surface area ∼1800 m2 g−1) were purchased from Aladdin. Diallyldimethylammonium chloride (DAACl) solution (60 wt% in water) was supplied by Adamas. Zinc trifluoromethanesulfonate (Zn(OTF)2) was procured from Macklin, Zinc sulfate heptahydrate (ZnSO4·7H2O) was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, and zinc foil was supplied by Qingyuan Metal Co., Ltd.

Preparation of OTA(OTF)4

The synthesis of OTA(OTF)4 was carried out in two steps. First, OTABr4 was prepared following a previously reported procedure28. Subsequently, an anion exchange reaction was performed to obtain OTA(OTF)4. In a typical synthesis, 1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane (5 mmol), allyl bromide (5 mmol), and K2CO3 (20 mmol) were sequentially added to anhydrous acetonitrile (60 mL). The mixture was stirred and refluxed at 80 °C for 4 days to promote a nucleophilic substitution reaction. After completion, the resulting pale-yellow suspension was washed three times with anhydrous diethyl ether and centrifuged after each wash. The precipitate was then dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 24 h to afford OTABr4. For the anion exchange, OTABr4 (0.01 mol) was dissolved in anhydrous acetonitrile (10 mL) and stirred at room temperature for 20 min. LiOTF (0.041 mol) was then added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed under continuous stirring at room temperature for 24 h. Upon completion, the resulting turbid mixture was filtered to remove any insoluble by-products. The pale-yellow filtrate was concentrated by rotary evaporation under reduced pressure at 50 °C, and the residue was dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 24 h to yield the final product of OTA(OTF)4.

Preparation of DAAOTF

An aqueous solution of DAACl (10 mL, 1 mol L−1) was mixed with an aqueous solution of AgOTF (1 mL, 1 mol L−1) under vigorous stirring. The ion-exchange reaction was conducted at room temperature for 12 h. After completion, the resulting turbid mixture was filtered to remove the precipitated silver chloride. The clear, colorless filtrate was collected as the desired product of DAAOTF.

Preparation of BE, MVOC, and HVOC electrolytes

Initially, 1.09 g of Zn(OTF)2 was dissolved in 1 g of deionized water to prepare the Zn(OTF)2 electrolyte, which served as the bare electrolyte (BE). To obtain the HVOC-based electrolytes, OTA(OTF)4 was subsequently added to the BE at concentrations of 0.05 m (m: mol kg−1), 0.15, 0.25, and 0.35 m, followed by magnetic stirring in a glass vial under ambient conditions (23 ± 2 °C) until a homogeneous solution was formed. The MVOC electrolytes were prepared using the same procedure, except that DAAOTF was used in place of OTA(OTF)4. For ZnSO4-based electrolytes, a 2 m ZnSO4 aqueous solution was first prepared, after which HVOC and MVOC were individually introduced at a concentration of 0.05 m, followed by stirring to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneity. All electrolyte solutions were stored in sealed glass vials under ambient conditions prior to use.

Preparation of I2 electrode materials

The I2 electrode materials were prepared via a conventional melt-diffusion method64. Unless otherwise specified, all procedures were carried out under ambient conditions (23 ± 2 °C). YP50 (Canrd Technology Co., Ltd., pore size: 0.8–0.9 nm, micropore volume: 0.7 mL g−1), acetylene black (Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd., purity: 99.9%), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd., purity: 99.5%, molecular weight: 1,200,000) were mixed in a weight ratio of 8:1:1 using N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd., purity: 99.5%) as the solvent. The slurry was homogenized using a centrifugal stirring defoaming machine (TMAX-XXH-1500S) at 800 rpm for 2 h. The resulting slurry was cast onto hydrophilic carbon cloth (Ce-Tech Co., Ltd., model: W0S1011, thickness: 0.35 mm) using a doctor blade and subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C. Prior to electrode fabrication, the carbon cloth was rendered hydrophilic by treatment with concentrated nitric acid (Oriental Chemicals & Lab Supplies Ltd.) at 80 °C for 12 h. The dried electrodes were then sealed with elemental iodine in a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 135 °C for 12 h to enable iodine infusion, yielding the I2 electrode materials. The obtained electrodes (thickness: ~0.35 mm) were cut into 1 cm × 1 cm squares using a paper cutter (Deli Group Co., Ltd., model 8016) for cell assembly and stored in glass vials under ambient conditions prior to use.

Preparation of I2 electrode materials with high mass loading

I2, activated carbon, and Ketjen black (Canrd Technology Co., Ltd., specific surface area: 1400 m2 g−1) were mixed in a mass ratio of 8:1:1 and ground uniformly using a mortar and pestle under ambient conditions (23 ± 2 °C). PTFE emulsion (Canrd Technology Co., Ltd., molecular weight: 50,000, viscosity: 26 mPa s) was then added (final mass ratio: I2: activated carbon: Ketjen black: PTFE = 8:1:1:1), followed by the addition of isopropanol (Aladdin, ≥99.7%) to form a malleable paste. The mixture was pressed onto a titanium mesh (100 mesh, purity ≥99.5%) and dried under vacuum at 45 °C for 12 h. The obtained electrodes (thickness: ~0.55 mm) were cut into the required dimensions using a paper cutter for cell assembly.

Characterizations

Unless otherwise specified, all sample preparation for testing was conducted under ambient laboratory conditions (23 ± 2 °C) without the use of an inert atmosphere. UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded on a Lambda 950 spectrophotometer to monitor the I3− signal, allowing evaluation of the capability of HVOC to capture polyiodide species. In situ Raman spectroscopy was carried out using a Thermo Fisher Scientific DXR2xi spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm laser to monitor the water, BE, and HVOC at 0.15, 0.25, 0.35 m concentrations and polyiodide species on the Zn/I2 electrode surface during charge-discharge processes. The electrolytes for in situ UV–vis and Raman measurements were 3 m Zn(OTF)2 (BE), 0.15 m MVOC, and 0.15 m HVOC. 1H and 19F NMR spectra of water, BE, and HVOC with concentrations of 0.05, 0.15, 0.25, and 0.35 m were acquired using a Bruker 300 MHz spectrometer. Low-field (LF) 1H NMR and 2D LF 1H T1-T2 mapping of water and 0.15 m HVOC electrolytes were performed on a VTMR20-010 V-I NMR analyzer (Niumag Corporation, Suzhou). The surface morphology of Zn electrodes was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU4800). XPS was conducted on a PHI Model 5802 system to analyze the composition of the positive electrode electrolyte interphase (PEI) and SEI layers. All XPS data were corrected using the C 1s signal from surface adventitious carbon, which was set to 284.8 eV as an internal standard. TOF-SIMS analyses were performed on an IONTOF M6 instrument (Germany) using a Cs+ ion beam over a scanned area of 50 μm × 50 μm.

Electrochemical measurements

Symmetrical Zn||Zn, asymmetrical Zn||Cu, and full Zn||I2 batteries were assembled in CR2016-type coin cells (stainless steel) using one piece of glass fiber A separator (GF/A, Whatman, diameter: 19 mm, thickness: 260 μm, average pore size: 1.6 μm, porosity: >90%) and a stainless steel spring (diameter: 15.4 mm, thickness: 1.2 mm). Zn foil (Qingyuan Metal Co., Ltd., purity >99.99%, thickness: 22 μm) and Cu foil (Canrd Technology Co., Ltd., purity >99.99%, thickness: 9 μm) were used as the electrodes. Before use, the Zn and Cu electrodes were cleaned three times with ethanol, naturally dried, and stored in glass vials under ambient conditions (23 ± 2 °C). Unless otherwise specified for pouch-cell tests, all electrodes were cut into 1 cm × 1 cm squares using a paper cutter (Deli Group Co., Ltd., model 8016). For cell assembly, 90 μL electrolyte was dispensed into each cell using a calibrated micropipette equipped with polypropylene tips. Following cell fabrication at 23 ± 2 °C, the assembled cells were mechanically sealed with a hydraulic crimping device and subsequently equilibrated at the designated testing temperature for 4 h before electrochemical characterization. All galvanostatic cycling measurements were performed under constant-current mode using specific current (A g−1) or current density (mA cm−2), as appropriate. Unless otherwise stated, the Zn||I2 batteries were operated within a cut-off voltage range of 0.6–1.8 V, while Zn||Cu cells were tested with an upper cut-off potential of 0.5 V. The CE was calculated as the ratio of discharge capacity divided by the charge capacity in the preceding charge cycle.

For Zn||I2 pouch battery, the detailed parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The pouch cells were assembled with a stacking sequence of negative electrode–separator–positive electrode–separator–negative electrode–separator–positive electrode–separator–negative electrode, where Zn foil and Ti mesh were used as the negative and positive current collectors, respectively. The cells were first top-sealed and side-sealed using an MSK-115A-MS vacuum sealing machine (Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology Co., Ltd.), followed by electrolyte injection and vacuum evacuation for 150 s prior to final sealing. The sealed cells were then rested for 12 h before testing. During electrochemical measurements, an external pressure of approximately 50 kPa was applied using a mold.

Galvanostatic charge–discharge tests were conducted using a Neware battery testing system (CT-4008Tn-5V10mA-HWX, Shenzhen, China) and a LAND battery testing system (CT2001A). Unless otherwise specified, electrochemical measurements at 25 and 60 °C were carried out in a temperature-controlled test chamber (YDC300HT). Electrochemical tests at temperatures below 0 °C and at 60 °C were performed using a high- and low-temperature test chamber (Labtech, TH1200G). The cycling data were obtained from at least three independently assembled cells tested in parallel. The ESW of the electrolytes was determined by linear sweep voltammetry using a three-electrode configuration, where Zn foil, glassy carbon, and Ag/AgCl served as the working, counter, and reference electrodes, respectively, at a scan rate of 1 mV s−1. The ionic conductivity of the electrolytes was measured by EIS on a CHI660E electrochemical workstation using two stainless steel blocking electrodes with dimensions of 0.5 × 0.5 cm. Before measurement, the cells were stabilized at open-circuit conditions for 30 min. EIS spectra were then collected using a 5 mV potentiostatic perturbation within a frequency range of 10–100,000 Hz, with approximately 60 data points collected across the entire frequency window. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

To elucidate the structural and dynamic behavior of the electrode-electrolyte interface during battery operation, MD simulations were conducted using the Forcite module in Materials Studio. The detailed compositions of the simulated electrolyte systems are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. The optimized coordinates and structural configurations associated with the DFT calculations are provided in Supplementary Data 4, 5, and 13. The COMPASSIII force field was employed to model interatomic interactions, with existing force field types and charges retained without reassignment. Simulations were carried out in the canonical (NVT) ensemble at 273 K, with temperature regulation achieved via the Nosé–Hoover–Langevin thermostat. An external electric field with a strength of 1.5 V/−1.5 V was applied along the z-axis, while the x and y components were set to zero. Electrostatic and van der Waals interactions were computed using the particle-particle particle-mesh and Ewald summation schemes, respectively. A total of 1,500,000 MD steps were executed with a time step of 1 fs, resulting in a total simulation time of 1.5 ns. Based on this equilibrated trajectory, the charging process was subsequently simulated by applying a reversed electric field under identical parameter settings, with the total simulation time extended to 3 ns. Complementary MD simulations were also performed using GROMACS to probe the structural features of the electrolyte systems70. The molecules were mixed in a cubic box with periodic boundary conditions by using the PACKMOL package71. The AMBER99 force field was applied to model biomolecular species, and the General AMBER Force Field was employed for small organic components to ensure compatibility and accurate representation of interactions72–74. The simulation protocol began with energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm to relieve unfavorable contacts and relax the system’s initial structure. This step was followed by equilibration under NVT and NPT ensembles, each spanning 1000 ps with a 2 fs timestep. During equilibration, the system temperature was maintained at 298.15 K via the velocity-rescaling thermostat, and pressure was regulated at 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat. A subsequent production simulation was performed under NPT conditions for 5 ns, with system snapshots saved every 2 ps for further structural analysis. Long-range electrostatics were handled using the Particle Mesh Ewald method, and bond constraints were implemented using the LINCS algorithm75,76. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all spatial directions to mimic an infinite system. Post-simulation, Visualization and further analysis were facilitated by the visual molecular dynamics software77.
