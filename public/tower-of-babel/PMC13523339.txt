3D-printed metamaterial femoral prostheses via scalar field fusion and directional porous structure regulation
Authors: Guoqing Zhang, Xiaoyu Zhou, Junxin Li, Yongsheng Zhou, Juanjuan Xie, Aibing Huang, Yuchao Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study integrates topology optimization, scalar field fusion (a method to realize smooth transition between solid and porous regions through implicit function interpolation), and directional porous structure control to develop a design method for customized metamaterial femoral prostheses, addressing key problems of conventional prostheses including poor implant compatibility, excessive bone resection, and insufficient long-term stability.ResultsWe initially completed the personalized design of femoral prostheses using a combination of retrograde and antegrade methods. This was followed by integrating topology optimization with porous structure filling to design the metamaterial. The prostheses were directly manufactured using 3D printing technology, and their performance was thoroughly evaluated. The results showed that the femoral prosthesis experienced the highest stress at 70° knee flexion (half-squat position), with a maximum stress of 1.269 × 4 MPa and a maximum displacement of 2.77 mm. Following topology optimization, although stress concentration and displacement slightly increased, the stress distribution became more uniform, resulting in a weight reduction of approximately 38.19%. Prostheses treated through scalar field fusion and adjustment of the filling direction of the porous structure exhibited metamaterial properties. The 3D-printed femoral prostheses and their matching components maintained high surface quality with no significant warping or deformation. Moreover, the porous structure displayed a well-defined pore configuration and connectivity. The prostheses and their components achieved a close fit, with dimensional accuracy and compatibility that generally met the requirements.ConclusionsAfter post-processing, the developed prototype serves as a valid proof-of-concept, thus establishing a foundation for the subsequent development and practical application of high-performance personalized biofix femoral prostheses.

Background

The knee joint, the most complex joint in the human body, is essential for supporting body weight and facilitating lower limb movements. Conditions such as osteoarthritis, neoplastic lesions, and bone trauma can cause symptoms including pain, swelling, and limited range of motion (LOM) in the knee, necessitating timely treatment. In severe cases, joint replacement may be required to prevent impaired mobility. There is a significant positive correlation between aging and the prevalence of orthopedic conditions. A 2020 global multicenter registry study covering Sweden, Australia, and the United States (2003–2017) showed that the annual knee replacement surgery volumes in the three countries increased by 79%, 127%, and 384% respectively compared with 2003; the 2024 SICOT Global Arthroplasty Registry White Paper further confirmed that the global demand for joint replacement continues to show a growing trend [1]. As the initial cohort of baby boomers in China enters the 60 + age group, the population at high risk for orthopedic diseases is expected to expand significantly. Statistics from 2019 indicate that the number of artificial hip and knee replacement surgeries in China surpassed 900,000, with an annual growth rate of nearly 20% [2]. In 2018, 73.27% of bone joint replacements in China were imported, with only 26.73% produced domestically. Specifically, the proportion of imported knee joints was notably high at 83.56%, while domestic knee joint products constituted less than 20%. A large proportion of joint replacement prostheses are supplied by a limited number of manufacturers, and local production capacity for such prosthetic devices remains constrained in many regions. Furthermore, the poor compatibility of commercially available knee joint prostheses with diverse patient anatomical characteristics often necessitates substantial bone resection during implantation. The intricate curved surface of the femoral prosthesis presents the greatest challenge in the design of knee joint prostheses, and consequently, there is an urgent need to develop metamaterial femoral prostheses that are better suited to match individual patient morphological features.

In recent years, 3D printing technology has developed rapidly, providing a path for the R&D of metamaterial femoral prostheses. The manufacturing principle of 3D printing technology is “layer-by-layer manufacturing, layer-upon-layer overlapping”, that is, the designed model was first sliced, layered and routed with specialized software and then imported into a 3D printer for the purpose of “layer-by-layer manufacturing”. Since the 3D printer processes a picture at a time, it can process complex parts [3–4]. Selective Laser Melting (SLM) is a 3D printing technology which melts metal powder with laser [5].

Li Jiayi et al. [6] designed a biomimetic microstructural femoral prosthesis using biomimetic structures (cubic hexahedron, face-centered cubic, and body-centered cubic). They conducted finite element simulations to mimic compression tests and analyzed deformation, yield stress, and elastic modulus of the structures. It was found that porous biomimetic microstructures could decrease the elastic modulus, reduce stress shielding, and enhance bone growth. Wang Anmin et al. [7] explored the design of personalized knee joint prostheses using 3D printing and reverse engineering methods, assessing the design’s validity through simulation and implantation experiments. The results demonstrated that personalized prostheses significantly improved the fit to the osteotomy plane and contour over commercial alternatives. Song Chunyan et al. [8] investigated the effects of femoral flexion axis positioning and geometric parameters on the contact mechanics of knee joint prostheses, exploring the trade-off between contact area and stress among these parameters. Liu Hongwei et al. [9] designed a personalized femoral prosthesis tailored to the specific morphology of the femoral medullary cavity, which offered advantages such as minimal bone filing, shorter surgery duration, and reduced blood loss. Ding Shisong [10] explored the efficacy of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) based on 3D printing technology in patients with knee osteoarthritis and found that TKA based on 3D printing technology has a good application effect in KOA patients, which is conducive to improving perioperative indicators, promoting knee joint function, range of motion, and lower limb line of force recovery, and has a low incidence of complications. In our previous work, we developed a personalized biofix femoral implant using parametric modeling and manufactured it with 3D printing. The design aligned well with surrounding bone tissues, required minimal bone resection, and the SLM-formed implant showed pillar overlap and pore connectivity [11]. Patil Shantanu et al. [12] performed experiments on cadaver specimens, implanting personalized and standard prostheses. They simulated varus and valgus laxity under conditions of deep flexion and passive states, finding that the personalized prosthesis was more kinematically similar to the native knee joint than the standard prosthesis. Ismail Hakki Korkmaz et al. [13] created an anatomical prosthesis that closely matched the knee joint’s shape, reducing stress and strain, and produced it using 3D printing. This design demonstrated benefits in terms of articular surface wear, aseptic loosening, and maintaining normal joint biomechanics. Using computed tomography (CT) scanning, M Wang et al. [14] extracted internal and external femur contours, created femoral and prosthesis models using three-dimensional reconstruction software, and developed optimized structures based on biomechanical analysis. C.S. Chui et al. [15] measured CT images of 52 Chinese male and female knees, developed a femoral prosthesis, conducted surface matching, bone resection analysis, and cadaver tests, and compared the results with commercial products to validate the proposed design.

Manoharan et al. [16] studied the biocompatibility and bone integration potential of functionally graded lattice structures and found that femoral prostheses with gradient lattice structures have mechanical stability, reduced stress shielding, and enhanced compatibility.

Currently, 3D printed femoral prosthesis research has advanced but focuses only on morphological matching and macroscopic mechanical strength, neglecting the effects of micropore structure and surface morphology on osteointegration, which causes poor long-term stability. Accordingly, this study aims to develop a novel design and manufacturing method for personalized metamaterial femoral prostheses by integrating topology optimization, scalar field fusion, and directional porous structure regulation; to achieve precise anatomical matching, minimal bone resection, optimized mechanical performance, and improved biocompatibility; and to verify the feasibility and performance of the designed prostheses via finite element simulation and 3D printing experiments.

Materials and methods

Design constraints

3D printing technology has been employed to manufacture implants, which must adhere to specific manufacturing constraints. This study delineates the design constraints into three primary categories, based on the geometric requirements for SLM-formed parts, the biocompatibility of porous structures, and the practical application of femoral prostheses and their corresponding parts [17–19]:

(1) Sharp corner and thin wall constraints: The limited focus size of the laser spot used in SLM technology prevents the manufacturing of parts with sharp corners and thin walls that are smaller than the spot diameter. Similarly, the accuracy of parts produced by FDM printers is limited by the nozzle diameter, requiring that the wall thickness of the processed parts be greater than this diameter.

(2) Hole features: In SLM technology, the heat-affected zone in the laser-melted powder causes the melting path to be larger than intended, which can lead to small holes becoming obstructed. With FDM printing, if the holes are too small, molten material of the upper and lower layers may fuse together, also obstructing the hole.

(3) Biocompatibility requirements: The ideal pore size for promoting bone cell growth ranges from 100 ~ 1000 μm, and the optimal porosity to emulate cancellous bone structure is between 50%~90%. A rough surface enhances the implant’s contact area with bone tissues, facilitating tissue adhesion and thus making the maximization of the implant’s surface area a critical factor.

Forming materials and methods

To evaluate the compatibility of the designed femoral prosthesis with the femur post-bone resection and to reduce manufacturing costs, the prosthesis can be produced using a plastic 3D printer. For this purpose, we employed the Z-603 S, an industrial high-precision desktop 3D printer manufactured by JG Maker Co., Ltd., utilizing PLA as the forming material. The printing parameters were set as follows: layer thickness of 0.15 mm, filling density of 18%, printing temperature of 203 °C, and wall thickness of 0.6 mm.

The material used for SLM femoral prosthesis is CoCrMo alloy powder generated by SANDVIK osprey in the UK, which meets the ASTM F75 standard. The powder is prepared by atomization method, and its composition comparison is shown in Table 1. The particle size distribution is narrowly concentrated, with D50 at 22 μm, and D90 at 28.5 μm.

The forming equipment adopts the metal 3D printing equipment GYD 150 produced by Guangdong Hanbang Laser Technology Co., Ltd. In the SLM process, nitrogen was used as the protective gas; oxygen content was kept below 0.03%; the processing laser power was 170 W; the scanning speed was 500 mm/s; the scanning spacing was 80 μm; the processing layer thickness was 30 μm; the XY interlayer staggered scanning strategy was used.

Analysis method

The femur model utilized in this study was the built-in standard femur template provided in Materialise Mimics 2020 software, which is a generic anatomical demonstration model for research and simulation purposes. No external dataset accession number or identification code is applicable for this internal pre-installed model, as it does not derive from clinical patient data or a public research database. The femur was reverse - engineered using Mimics 2020, developed by Materialise, and the surface of the reconstructed femur was processed using Geomagic Studio 2020, developed by Geomagic. The simulation of bone cutting and the parametric design of the femoral prosthesis were carried out using Rhino 6 software, developed by Robert McNeel. The finite element analysis software Inspire 2022 developed by Altair was used for finite element analysis and topology optimization design of femoral prostheses, and OptiStruct was used as the solver. Surface texturing of metamaterial structures was achieved through secondary programming in the 3D modeling software Rhino 6.

Using a high-definition electron microscope from BOCHENG, we examined the surface morphology of the 3D-printed femoral prosthesis, and refined the 3D printing process parameters to optimize the parts. The 3D-printed femoral prosthesis and femur underwent several post-printing treatments: support removal, sandblasting, rough polishing with sandpaper, and final polishing with a polishing cloth.

Results and discussion

Analysis of problems with the use of femoral prostheses

Currently, the majority of femoral prostheses utilized in domestic surgeries are imported series products, tailored for different age groups, and exhibit low compatibility with the Chinese population. The standard bone cutting technique for femoral prosthesis replacement, known as the “5-cut method,” involves 5 specific cuts: along the posterior lateral femoral condyle, posterior medial femoral condyle, distal femur, anterior lateral femoral condyle, and anterior medial femoral condyle. These cuts are determined by the dimensions of the femoral prosthesis, as shown in Fig. 1(a). When using the “5-cut method” to cut bones, it is essential to maintain the continuity of the various bone-cutting surfaces to ensure compatibility between the human bone and the standard femoral prosthesis. However, this requirement may lead to unintended resection at non-affected sites. Observations from Fig. 1(b) reveal that the anterior lateral femoral condyle of the prosthesis is often partially embedded into the femur, creating an interference fit that frequently requires increased bone resection, thus leading to unnecessary bone loss. Similarly, Fig. 1(c) shows an interference fit between the intercondylar fossa and the convex of the femoral platform, often necessitating resection of the intercondylar fossa, which may result in bone removal at non-affected areas. Additionally, Fig. 1(d) illustrates that when foreign products are assembled with the femur of a Chinese individual, areas lacking contact do not support load, thereby not only failing to contribute to load bearing but also increasing the prosthesis’s weight, imposing an extra burden to the patient. Given that bone is exceedingly valuable—often considered more precious than gold—the design of personalized high-performing femoral prostheses should prioritize minimizing bone resection while ensuring compatibility.

Femoral reconstruction and design of personalized femoral prosthesis

One challenge in designing femoral prostheses is achieving a precise fit between the prosthesis and affected site. To address this, we utilized CT to scan the affected site, captured images, and imported them into Mimics for threshold segmentation. Subsequently, the disconnected segmented areas on the initially segmented mask were divided into subgroups to create new masks. Minor irregularities along the edges of the segmented masks were eliminated through an open operation (first erosion, then dilation), and the femur was reconstructed three-dimensional using Calculate 3D Models. It was then imported into Geomagic Studio for mesh repair, denoising, smoothing, and solidification, as depicted in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b).

From an established bone database, femoral prostheses similar in size to the femur were selected. Both the prostheses and the femur were imported into Rhino 6 for assembly, with positions adjusted as needed. The “5-cut method” was then used for bone resection, striving to preserve as much bone as possible. This method involved making cuts along the posterior lateral femoral condyle, posterior medial femoral condyle, distal femur, anterior lateral femoral condyle, and anterior medial femoral condyle, as shown in Fig. 2(c). Post-resection, the femur is shown in Fig. 2(d). The design principle for the femoral prosthesis emphasized preserving non-affected bone tissue and minimizing prosthesis weight. If the femoral prosthesis from the database was not compatible with the patient’s femur, additional resection of the anterior lateral femoral condyle and intercondylar fossa was deemed necessary. An uncontacted section of the posterior lateral femoral condyle, which deviated from the design principles, is highlighted in Fig. 2(e). Thus, we employed Boolean operations (subtracting the femoral prosthesis from the patient’s femur) to redesign the femoral prosthesis, as shown in Fig. 2(f). This redesign aimed to preserve the anterior lateral femoral condyle and intercondylar fossa to the greatest extent possible, reducing bone resection. The removal of the prosthesis section lacking contact with the femur was planned for subsequent topological optimization.

Motional stress analysis of the femoral prosthesis

Simulation parameter setting of the femoral prosthesis

The femoral prosthesis, femur, and supporting tibial insert, following a Boolean operation, were saved as an STP file and imported into Altair Inspire 2022 software to configure the simulation parameters. To preserve the integrity of the outer surface of the femoral prosthesis and the inner convex of the femoral condyle, segmentation was necessary. We utilized the segmentation command to define the thickness of the outer surface of the femoral prosthesis and the inner convex of the femoral condyle at 2 mm, setting the units for the segmented parts to millimeters and aligning the tibial insert with the horizontal plane, as illustrated in Fig. 3(a). Material parameter setting: The femoral prosthesis was specified as CoCr alloy (Elastic modulus 2.1 × 10⁵ MPa, Poisson’s ratio 0.3), the femur as cortical bone (Elastic modulus 1.7 × 10⁴ MPa, Poisson’s ratio 0.3), and the tibial insert as polyethylene (Elastic modulus 6.85 × 10² MPa, Poisson’s ratio 0.3). A mesh convergence study was carried out to ensure numerical accuracy. Tetrahedral solid elements were used for meshing, with an appropriate element size to balance computational efficiency and accuracy. A vertical load corresponding to a half-squat posture was applied on the articular surface, and the distal femoral condyle was set as a fixed support. Frictionless contact was defined at the interface between the prosthesis and the tibial insert. Rigid body group setting: To ensure no positional movement of the segmented femur during motion, the outer surface of the femoral prosthesis, the inner femoral prosthesis, and the inner convex of the femoral condyles were grouped as a rigid body. Contact mode setting: To prevent relative movement between the contact surfaces of the femur and the femoral prosthesis, the articulation mode was set to locked articulation. To allow rotation between the femoral prosthesis and tibial insert, the articulation mode was set to free articulation, as depicted in Fig. 3(b). The femur-prosthesis interface was set to locked articulation for a preliminary simplification, which allowed us to focus on the intrinsic mechanical performance of the metamaterial prosthesis design (the core objective of this study) by eliminating interface motion interference. Additionally, to simulate the rotation of the femur, a rotational motor was added at the center of the femoral epicondyle, with the rotation angle set to 40° and the initial state vertical. Load Condition: The load conditions on the femur within the human body are complex, influenced by factors such as pre-tightening force of screws, frictional forces, and the forces exerted by muscles, ligaments, and bone fracture plates. These generally manifest in three forms: compression, bending, and torsion. When subjected to compression, the femoral head naturally experiences bending and torsional forces, thus eliminating the need to apply bending and twisting forces separately. Taking a 70 kg adult as the research subject, a single-leg compression load F of 1150 N (about twice the body weight) was applied to the femoral cross-section, as illustrated in Fig. 3(c). Motion simulation parameter setting: The end time of the motion simulation was set to 0.5s, and the output rate to 30 Hz. The simulation considered the gravity of the part. After the motion simulation is completed, select the femoral prosthesis, set the element size to 2 mm, and use tetrahedral mesh for force analysis.

Motional stress analysis of the femoral prosthesis

Observations from the stress distribution of the knee joint prosthesis at different motion positions, as shown in Fig. 4(a), reveal that at a 90° standing angle, the stress on the femoral prosthesis was primarily concentrated at the distal femur, reaching a maximum of 5.537 × 10− 1MPa. The stress distribution was moderate and relatively uniform at the distal end. In Fig. 4(b), the displacement cloud map indicates that the maximum displacement, occurring at the anterior lateral femoral condyle, was minimal at 8.113 × 10− 5mm. As shown in Fig. 4(c), at 70° knee flexion (half-squat position), the stress concentration on the femoral prosthesis peaked at 1.269 × 104 MPa. This peak value represents a highly localized numerical stress concentration at the finite element mesh node rather than the macroscopic effective stress of the bulk material. No unit scaling or post-processing manipulation was applied; all stress values are raw output from the finite element solver in absolute megapascals (MPa). The stress distribution remained relatively uniform in the main load-bearing regions. The displacement cloud map in Fig. 4(d) shows that the maximum displacement, located on the one side of the posterior lateral femoral condyle, measured 2.77 mm, significantly higher than at a 90° standing position but still relatively small overall. When squatting at 50°, as depicted in Fig. 4(e), the stress on the femoral prosthesis decreased to a maximum of 1.036 × 104 MPa, shifting gradually towards the other side of the posterior lateral femoral condyle, with a corresponding decrease in stress concentration. The displacement cloud map in Fig. 4(f) shows that the maximum displacement, occurring on both sides of the posterior lateral femoral condyle, was reduced to 1.266 mm, significantly lower compared to the 70° semi-squat position. A detailed comparison of the stress contour plots (Fig. 4(c) and Fig. 4(e)) further confirms the stress distribution characteristics: the high-stress region (above 1.0 × 104 MPa) at 70° knee flexion is relatively extensive, while at 50° squatting, this region is obviously reduced, and the stress gradient (the difference between maximum and minimum stress in adjacent regions) is significantly decreased, indicating a more gradual stress transition and reduced local stress concentration. This trend is consistent with the design goal of improving stress uniformity through structural optimization. From these 3 motion states of the femoral prosthesis, it is evident that despite some stress concentration when under load, the overall stress concentration was low and the safety margin was high. This suggests that there is potential for further topological optimization to achieve a more uniform stress distribution.

Topological optimization design of the femoral prosthesis

Simulation parameter setting for the topology optimization of the femoral prosthesis

Initially, simulation parameters identical to those of the solid femoral prosthesis, including material, load, and constraint, were configured in Inspire software. To ensure the integrity of the outer contour of the contact area between the femoral prosthesis and the tibial insert after topology optimization, it was necessary to segment the outer contour of the femoral prosthesis. Additionally, the inner convex of the femoral condyle of the femoral prosthesis needed to be preserved due to its structural importance. Consequently, we segmented both the outer contour of the femoral prosthesis and the inner convex of the femoral condyle to a thickness of 2 mm in Inspire software, as illustrated in Fig. 5(a). Subsequently, the outer contour of the femoral prosthesis and the inner convex of the femoral condyle were designated as the design space, with the remainder of the femoral prosthesis classified as the non-design space, as shown in Fig. 5(b). Considering the approximately symmetric structure of the femoral prosthesis, unilateral symmetric constraints were applied, as depicted in Fig. 5(c). The optimization objective was to maximize stiffness, setting the mass target at 30% of the total volume of the design space. The maximum thickness constraint was set at 8 mm, and the minimum thickness constraint at 4 mm. The mass of the femoral prosthesis prior to topological optimization was recorded at 144 g. The constraints and loads applied to the femoral prosthesis are demonstrated in Fig. 5.

Topology optimization design of femoral prosthesis

The results of the topology optimization for the femoral prosthesis are displayed in Fig. 6(a). Observations from this figure reveal that a mass reduction target of 30% led to effective topology optimization. Sections of the prosthesis that did not contact the femur or were subjected to minimal force were eliminated. However, the optimization process left fine structures within the framework, including numerous sharp corners that posed challenges for subsequent processing and analysis. To resolve these issues, adjustments were made to the inner framework of the femoral prosthesis by increasing the smoothing value and enhancing details. This approach effectively removed most sharp corners and connected fractured parts of the structure, as shown in Fig. 6(b) and 6(c). Following topological optimization, the weight of the femoral prosthesis framework was reduced to 89 g, representing a weight reduction of approximately 38.19% and meeting the weight reduction target. The outer contour and inner framework of the femur, post-smoothing, were separately exported in STL format.

Post-processing of the topological optimization model

The outer contour and inner framework of the femur in STL format were imported into Rhino 6 software and converted into implicit models, as shown in Fig. 7(a). Observations from Fig. 7(a) indicate that the outer contour was well converted, whereas the inner framework displayed sharp corners and some chaotic parts post-conversion, likely due to the relatively poor mesh quality. To address this issue, both the outer contour and inner framework were imported into Materialise Magics 22.0 software for re-segmentation and mesh quality optimization. The improved meshing effect is depicted in Fig. 7(b), where the re-segmented mesh units appeared uniformly distributed, and at sharp corners and areas with significant undulations, the meshes became dense and of higher quality. These components, after remeshing, were reimported into Rhino 6 software and converted again into implicit models. A Boolean operation was then performed. The part post-fusion is shown in Fig. 7(c). Observations from Fig. 7(c) reveal that the implicit model converted from the remeshed outer contour and inner framework had a positive effect, but there was an uneven transition between the fused sections. Consequently, we refused the outer contour and inner framework of the femur by programming the scalar field, with the effect shown in Fig. 7(d). Observations from Fig. 7(d) indicate a smooth transition between the outer contour and inner framework, eliminating the staircase effect at the contact points of the two parts and laying a foundation for subsequent finite element simulation analysis.

Finite element analysis of the framework of the femoral prosthesis after topology optimization

After the femoral prosthesis was filled with a porous structure, the large data size required for finite element simulation meshing presented challenges. Therefore, we conducted stress analysis on the topology optimized framework before it was filled with a porous structure. Furthermore, given the maximum stress and displacement observed at a semi-squat angle of 70°, we focused our analysis on the femoral prosthesis framework at this specific angle. For this analysis, we set the simulation parameters in Altair Inspire 2022 software identical to those of the filled prosthesis. At a semi-squat angle of 70°, the maximum stress of the femoral prosthesis increased slightly from 1.269 × 104 MPa to 1.284 × 104 MPa and the maximum displacement rose from 2.77 mm to 5.79 mm after topological optimization, with the stress distribution becoming more uniform and the prosthesis weight reduced by approximately 38.19%. The stress-strain cloud map of the femoral prosthesis framework at a semi-squat angle of 70°is shown in Fig. 8(a). Observations from this figure indicate that at a semi-squat angle of 70°, the maximum stress concentration on the framework was 1.284 × 104 MPa. This was an increase compared to pre-optimization levels and was located at the anterior medial femoral condyle, but was more uniformly distributed, suggesting that the topologically optimized framework experienced more uniform stress. Additionally, the displacement cloud map in Fig. 8(b) reveals that the maximum displacement was 5.79 mm, located at the anterior lateral condyle. Although this displacement slightly increased from pre-optimization measurements, it remained within an acceptable range. These finite element analysis results of the topologically optimized framework indicate that despite some stress concentration and increased displacement under load, these factors were generally minimal. Subsequent filling with the porous structure is expected to further reduce stress concentration and displacement, thereby meeting the requirements for mechanical properties. The mechanical reliability of the adopted metamaterial porous structure has been experimentally verified by compression tests on standard specimens in our previous work, which supports the accuracy of the finite element simulation results in this study.

A 38.19% weight reduction and more uniform stress distribution of the topology optimized femoral prosthesis framework align with the findings of Cheng et al. [20] (2022), who validated the efficacy of topology optimization for femoral prosthesis lightweighting and stress field optimization via a homogenization method. Unlike the pure macroscopic optimization in that work, scalar field fusion post-processing is applied herein to mitigate sharp corners and uneven transition defects of conventional optimization results, improving 3D printing manufacturability.

Implementation of the functionality of the femoral prosthesis metamaterial

Implementation of the mechanical metamaterial

The fused outer contour and inner framework of the femur were imported into Rhino 6 software and filled with a porous structure. Given the requirement for the femoral prosthesis to have sufficient load-bearing capacity, we opted to fill it with a Diamond porous unit, known for its mechanical properties. After the addition of the Diamond porous unit and fusing through a scalar field, the resulting femoral prosthesis is depicted in Fig. 9(a). Observations from Fig. 9(a) show that the porous structure of the femoral prosthesis transitioned smoothly into the solid part, achieving an effective integration. Additionally, the solid part of the femoral prosthesis, fused by setting the scalar field, exhibited uneven ripples that could facilitate cell adhesion. Upon examining the filling direction of the porous structure in the main load-bearing area of the distal femur, it was apparent that the orientation of the porous structure was disordered, with pores facing non-load-bearing directions, potentially weakening the load-bearing capacity of the distal femur. To rectify this, we programmed the filling direction of the distal femur in Rhino 6 to face upward and then performed fusion using the scalar field setting. The outcome of this fusion is shown in Fig. 9(b). From Fig. 9(b), it is evident that the pores of the porous structure in the distal femur were oriented consistently upwards, significantly enhancing the load-bearing capacity of the femoral prosthesis. The integration of topological optimization design and adjustment of the pore orientation within the porous structure enabled the femoral prosthesis to exhibit mechanical metamaterial properties.

Implementation of metamaterial with biological functions

The surface morphology of the porous structure significantly influences the biological functions of the implant, with studies suggesting that rough surfaces with a large surface area enhance cell adhesion and promote biological functions [21]. To investigate this further, we conducted research on the surface texturing mechanism of the porous structure. Using the 3D modeling software Rhino 6, we implemented surface texturing of the Diamond metamaterial structure through secondary programming. The specific parameters included a peak roughness of 0.18 mm, a roughness frequency of 10,000,000, and 1,000 seeds. The resulting surface texturing effect is illustrated in Fig. 10. Observing Fig. 10, it is found that the textured porous structure has many small protrusions on the surface, which improves the surface roughness and surface area to volume ratio of the porous structure, increases the opportunity for contact with bone tissue, and is conducive to bone tissue growth, which is theoretically expected to improve the biocompatibility of the femoral prosthesis based on existing research evidence.

3D printing and manufacturing processes optimization for the femoral prosthesis

Data processing of 3d-printed parts

In 3D printing technology, ensuring the quality of the formed parts requires adjusting the placement of the parts and adding support to overhanging sections. When printing the matching part of the femur with a plastic 3D printer, to avoid excessively long scan lines, the matching part is inclined at 15° to the forming plane, with the intercondylar fossa facing and inclined at 45° upwards. Additionally, to prevent warping and deformation due to significant temperature differences between the femur’s matching part and the forming substrate, the substrate is preheated to 50°, as shown in Fig. 11(a). Observations from Fig. 11(a) reveal that supports were added to the overhanging sections perpendicular to the substrate at an angle of less than 45°, and no supports were added to the matching surface, the inner convex of the femoral condyle, or the interior of the fixed hole, which enhance the quality of these surfaces. For the 3D printing and forming of the femoral prosthesis, informed by prior research on support addition and orientation for SLM-formed parts [22], a vertical layout with the articular surface facing upward was utilized. This layout avoids adding supports within the porous structure and prevents excessively long scan lines on the solid parts of the femoral prosthesis. Owing to benefits such as minimal support addition, a stronger connection between the parts and the printing platform, and easier removal without excessive damage to overhanging surfaces, conical supports are widely used in metal 3D printing.

The parameters for addition conical supports were as follows: the minimum distance between cones was 1 mm, the minimum gap was 1 mm, and the maximum distance was 2 mm. The diameter of the cone contact part ranged from 0.1 to 5 mm, and the diameter of the cone contact platform ranged from 0.1 to 0.8 mm, as shown in Fig. 11(b). Observations from Fig. 11(b) indicate that the supports of the femoral prosthesis were primarily located at the bottom and along the beam, oriented perpendicular to the outer surface, with no supports added inside the porous structure. The supports were evenly distributed, minimally added, and demonstrated a favorable removal outcome.

The femoral prosthesis experiences the greatest stress at 70° knee flexion (half-squat position), with a maximum stress of 1.269 × 104 MPa and a maximum displacement of 2.77 mm. The highest stress concentration and displacement occur at the lateral femoral condyle. After topology optimization, the femoral prosthesis framework demonstrates more uniform stress distribution. The femoral prosthesis has a metallic texture, low roughness, and no noticeable dripping residues or deformation. Subsequent research will conduct targeted experimental validations for the prosthesis design, including mechanical performance tests (compression, bending, fatigue), interface fixation tests, and preliminary in vitro/cadaveric evaluations. Further exploration of porous structure variable density control and surface area-to-volume ratio enhancement will be carried out, combined with experimental data to optimize the design system, which will lay a more solid foundation for potential translational development of 3D-printed high-performance femoral prostheses. This study only validates design and manufacturing feasibility under simplified simulation assumptions; clinical readiness, long-term stability, and biological performance remain to be verified through bench testing, animal studies, and clinical trials. This study did not consider frictional wear or interface micromotion at the femur-prosthesis interface (idealized as fully locked in simulations), which is a limitation to be addressed in future research by establishing a frictional contact model that better simulates clinical actuality. Given the criticality of fatigue behavior for knee implants under cyclic in vivo loading, subsequent research will also focus on the fatigue performance characterization and life estimation of the metamaterial femoral prosthesis to complement the static mechanical performance analysis in this study.
