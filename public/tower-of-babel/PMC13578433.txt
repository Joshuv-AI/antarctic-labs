The comparison of two pendrin inhibitors, YS-01 and PDSinh-C01, in lipopolysaccharide-induced acute lung injury
Authors: Hyeon Kyu Choi, Ji Young Son, Mi Hwa Shin, Jinhong Park, Doona Song, Young-Min Hyun, Ji-Hwan Ryu, Je Kyung Seong, Gyoonhee Han, Wan Namkung, Chul Hoon Kim, Jae Young Choi, Moo Suk Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a life-threatening condition without specific treatments, relying on mechanical ventilation and supportive care. Excessive inflammation and increased vascular permeability drive ARDS pathogenesis, with bacterial pneumonia being the most common cause. Pendrin is upregulated in inflammatory lung diseases, and previous studies have demonstrated that its inhibition reduces lung injury. To develop pendrin inhibitors as potential therapeutic agents for ARDS, it is essential to identify the most effective candidate with minimal side effects. In this study, we compared the efficacy of two pendrin inhibitors, YS-01 and PDSinh-C01, in an LPS-induced acute lung injury (ALI) model. Wild-type mice were treated with LPS (10 mg/kg) intranasally to induce lung injury. YS-01 (10 mg/kg) and PDSinh-C01 (10 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally before or after LPS exposure. Lung injury was assessed based on bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cell counts, protein levels, cytology, pathology, and lung injury scores. Pro-inflammatory cytokines and pendrin mRNA levels were measured in lung lysates. Additionally, pendrin-mediated Cl⁻/SCN⁻ exchange activity and cell viability were analyzed following treatment with the two inhibitors. Pendrin expression was elevated in LPS-induced lung injury but reduced by both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 treatment. Both inhibitors mitigated lung injury by decreasing BALF cell counts, protein levels, and pro-inflammatory cytokine production in pre- and post-treatment groups. The two inhibitors showed compound-specific patterns across in vivo endpoints, with YS-01 producing lower lung injury scores in the pre-treatment paradigm and PDSinh-C01 producing lower scores in the post-treatment paradigm. In vitro, YS-01 demonstrated greater inhibitory potency against pendrin-mediated Cl⁻/SCN⁻ exchange activity and a more favorable cell viability profile under the tested conditions. Both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 were active in this LPS-induced ALI model. The two inhibitors showed timing-dependent differences in histologic outcomes in vivo, with YS-01 favored in pre-treatment and PDSinh-C01 favored in post-treatment. Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a severe form of respiratory failure characterized by the acute onset of noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, impaired gas exchange, and the frequent need for mechanical ventilation. It commonly develops in response to severe infections, systemic inflammation, aspiration, or major trauma, and continues to be associated with high mortality despite advances in supportive care1–4. Excessive inflammation and disruption of alveolar-capillary barrier integrity are central features of ARDS pathogenesis, leading to vascular leakage, alveolar injury, pulmonary edema, and ventilation-perfusion mismatch5,6. Because bacterial pneumonia is the most common cause of ARDS, infection-driven inflammation is considered a major contributor to disease progression. In addition, epithelial dysfunction has been increasingly recognized as an important component of ARDS pathogenesis7. However, treatment remains largely supportive, underscoring the need for new therapeutic targets for inflammatory lung injury.

Pendrin (SLC26A4) is an apical transmembrane anion exchanger expressed in several epithelial tissues, including the thyroid, kidney, inner ear, and airway epithelium. In the respiratory system, pendrin mediates the exchange of Cl− with HCO3− and thiocyanate (SCN−) at the airway surface6,8. Although pendrin expression is negligible in normal airway epithelium, it is markedly upregulated in inflammatory airway diseases such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and allergic rhinitis9,10. Pendrin expression is also induced in primary airway epithelial cells by interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-13, and IL-17A11,12. Collectively, these findings support a role for pendrin in inflammatory epithelial responses in the respiratory tract and suggest that pendrin inhibition may represent a potential therapeutic approach in inflammatory lung disease. In support of this concept, Verkman et al. identified the pendrin inhibitor PDSinh-C01 through high-throughput screening and demonstrated the potential utility of pendrin inhibition in airway disease models13.

Our previous studies further established the pathogenic and therapeutic relevance of pendrin in inflammatory lung injury. We found that pendrin expression was increased in LPS-treated mouse lungs, whereas LPS-induced lung injury was attenuated in pendrin-null mice, suggesting that pendrin contributes to the pathogenesis of acute lung injury14. In addition, we identified YS-01 as a potent and selective pendrin inhibitor through screening of more than 54,400 small molecules. YS-01 showed therapeutic efficacy in an ovalbumin-induced allergic asthma model by suppressing pendrin/OSCN⁻/NF-κB-mediated airway inflammation and also attenuated lung injury in an LPS-induced ALI model, with reductions in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cell counts, protein concentration, NF-κB activation, and pro-inflammatory cytokine production15,16. Together, these studies established proof-of-concept for pendrin inhibition as a therapeutic strategy in inflammatory airway disease and acute lung injury.

However, although pendrin inhibition has shown therapeutic promise, the relative performance of different pendrin inhibitors has not been directly evaluated under the same acute lung injury conditions. For further translational development of pendrin-targeting therapy, it is important to determine which inhibitor demonstrates a more favorable overall profile under standardized experimental conditions. Therefore, in the present study, we performed a direct head-to-head comparison of YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 in an LPS-induced acute lung injury model and complementary in vitro assays to evaluate their relative therapeutic efficacy and pharmacologic characteristics.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yonsei University College of Medicine (Seoul, Korea) and conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Wild-type male C57BL/6J mice, aged 8 weeks and weighing 20–25 g, were obtained from Orient Bio (Sungnam, Republic of Korea). All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yonsei University College of Medicine (approval number: 2019 − 0324). This study was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

In the pre-treatment model, group sizes were: vehicle (n = 18), LPS (n = 14), YS-01 + LPS (n = 10), and PDSinh-C01 + LPS (n = 13). In the post-treatment model, group sizes were: vehicle (n = 10), LPS (n = 8), LPS + YS-01 (n = 11), and LPS+PDSinh-C01 (n = 13), for a total of 97 mice. Sample size was guided by prior ALI studies and pilot experiments; no formal power calculation was performed. Mice were included if healthy, within ± 10% of age-matched body weight, and if intranasal administration was successful. Exclusion criteria were limited to technical failures or anesthesia-related deaths. Exact n values are provided in figure legends.

Cell culture

Human pulmonary alveolar epithelial cells (HPAEpiC; ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Catalog No. 3200) were used in this study. According to the supplier, HPAEpiC are primary human pulmonary alveolar epithelial cells isolated from human lung tissue and cryopreserved at an early passage. Cells were cultured in Alveolar Epithelial Cell Medium (AEpiCM; ScienCell Research Laboratories, #3201), consisting of 500 mL basal medium supplemented with Epithelial Cell Growth Supplement (EpiCGS, #4152) and 5 mL penicillin/streptomycin solution (P/S, #0503), at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

LPS induced ALI model

The LPS-induced acute lung injury model and subsequent biological analyses were performed based on methodologies established in our previous work (Lee et al., 2020), with modifications in dosing, time-points and outcome measurements as detailed below. Mice were anesthetized with 1.5–2.5% isoflurane in 100% oxygen for induction and maintained with 1–2% isoflurane throughout the procedure. LPS (Escherichia coli, O111: B4 strain-TLR4 ligand, Catalog #tlrl-eblps, InvivoGen) was prepared at a concentration of 10 mg/kg in 50 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and administered via intranasal (i.n.) instillation. Animals were randomly assigned using random number generator. The control group received 50 µL of sterile PBS intranasally.

In the pre-treatment model, YS-01 (10 mg/kg) and PDSinh-C01 (10 mg/kg) in 50 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) 1 h before LPS inhalation. In the post-treatment model, two doses of YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 (10 mg/kg) were administered at 6 and 12 h after LPS inhalation to evaluate therapeutic effects after injury induction and to maintain drug exposure during the acute inflammatory phase before the 24 h endpoint. This schedule was based on our previously established LPS-induced acute lung injury model. Mice in the pre-treatment group were euthanized, and lung tissue was harvested 48 h after LPS inhalation, while in the post-treatment group, euthanasia and sample collection were performed 24 h after LPS administration. The total study duration per animal was 48 h in the pre-treatment model and 24 h in the post-treatment model, measured from LPS instillation to euthanasia. The control group received PBS following the same administration schedule. YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 were synthesized in the laboratories of Professors Gyoonhee Han and Wan Namkung (College of Pharmacy and Yonsei Institute of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Yonsei University, Incheon, Republic of Korea).

Isolation of bronchoalveolar lavage

All mice were euthanized with a lethal overdose of ketamine. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected by tracheal cannulation using 1 mL of sterile saline. The BALF was centrifuged at 4 °C, 3000 rpm, for 10 min, and the supernatant was stored at − 80 °C for further analysis. The cell pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of PBS and used for cell counts and cytospin preparations. Total cell numbers in each sample were determined with a hemocytometer (Marienfield) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

A 90 µL aliquot of each sample was transferred to slide chambers, which were then loaded into a cytospin with the slide facing outward. The slides were centrifuged at 800 rpm for 3 min, then removed and air-dried before staining. Cytospins were prepared using a Shandon Cytospin 4 cytocentrifuge (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stained with the Diff-Quik Stain Set (Dade Behring, Newark, DE, USA) to assess inflammation.

Protein concentrations in the BALF supernatant were measured using a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Two microliters of each sample and 198 µL of working reagent were added to a microplate well and mixed thoroughly on a plate shaker for 30 s. After a 30-minute incubation at 37 °C, the plate was cooled, and absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Lung tissue harvest and histological examination

The right lung was isolated and stored at − 80 °C for protein extraction after flushing the pulmonary vasculature with saline under low pressure. The left lung was inflated via tracheotomy with low-melting-point agarose (4%) in PBS, excised, and fixed overnight in 10% formaldehyde in PBS. The tissue was then embedded in paraffin for 5 μm sectioning. Sections of the left lung were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and evaluated under light microscopy.

Histopathological evaluation was performed in a blinded manner by two independent investigators using the standardized semi-quantitative lung injury scoring system recommended in the official American Thoracic Society (ATS) workshop report on experimental models of acute lung injury17. Briefly, each lung section was assessed for five established histologic features of ALI: (A) neutrophils in the alveolar space, (B) neutrophils in the interstitial space, (C) hyaline membranes, (D) proteinaceous debris filling the airspaces, and (E) alveolar septal thickening, with the relative weighting of each component followed as described in the ATS guideline. Inter-rater reliability between the two independent scorers was assessed, and the scores from the two investigators showed close agreement, after which the final scores were aggregated.

Western blotting

Lung tissues were harvested and lysed in PRO-PREP™ protein extraction solution (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 x g for 30 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations in the supernatant were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of protein (50 µg per lane) were mixed with sample buffer, denatured, separated on NuPAGE™ 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, NP0336BOX) and transferred onto 0.45-µm PVDF membranes (Immobilon®-P, Merck Millipore, IPVH00010).

Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBS-T (TBS, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA, #170–6435; 0.1% Tween-20, Bio-Rad Laboratories, #170–6531) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 5% skim milk in TBS-T. After washing with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (LumiFlash Ultima Chemiluminescent Substrate, Energenesis Biomedical Co., Ltd., #LF08-500), exposed to X-ray film, and developed using a CP1000 film processor (AGFA). Exposure conditions were adjusted to avoid signal saturation and to ensure quantification within the linear detection range.

The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-SLC26A4/pendrin (Abcam, ab98091), mouse anti-phospho-IκB⍺ (Cell Signaling Technology, #9246), rabbit anti-IκB⍺ (Cell Signaling Technology, #4814), and mouse anti-β-actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-47778). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to β-actin.

Real-time RT-PCR analysis

Total messenger RNA (mRNA) was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and reverse-transcribed with random hexamer primers, an oligo(dT) primer, and SuperScript® III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using Thunderbird SYBR qPCR mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). The thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 10 s in a 96-well reaction plate.

The primer sequences for quantitative RT-PCR were as follows: mouse pendrin (94 bp) – forward: 5’-CAT CTG CAG AAC CAG GTC AA-3’, reverse: 5’-GCA TTC ATC TCT GCC TCC AT-3’; and mouse β-actin (165 bp) – forward: 5’-TGT TAC CAA CTG GGA CGA CA-3’, reverse: 5’-GGG GTG TTG AAG GTC TCA AA-3’.

ELISA

Levels of inflammatory proteins—MIP-2, IL-1ß, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α)—in lung lysates from ALI mice were measured using ELISA kits (Magnetic Luminex Screening Assay Mouse Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit, LXSAMSM, Millipore) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Measurement of pendrin-mediated Cl−/SCN− exchange activity

CHO-K1 cells expressing human pendrin and YFP-F46L/H148Q/I152L were seeded in 96-well plates (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated for 48 h. Each well was washed twice with 200 µL of PBS, followed by the addition of 50 µL of HEPES-buffered solution. To evaluate the effects of YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 on pendrin-mediated Cl–/SCN– exchange activity, cells were pretreated with varying concentrations of YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 for 10 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence measurements of YFP were then conducted using a FLUOstar Omega microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany), preheated to 37 °C.

A NaSCN-substituted HEPES-buffered solution (50 µL) was added at 1 s, and YFP fluorescence was recorded for 6 s (400 ms intervals per point). The initial SCN– influx rate was calculated from the initial slope of YFP fluorescence using nonlinear regression.

Cell viability assay

Human Pulmonary Alveolar Epithelial Cells (HPAEpiC; ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Catalog No. 3200) were seeded in 96-well plates (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Following this, the cells were treated with various concentrations of YS-01 or PDSinh-C01 and incubated for an additional 24 h. An equivalent amount of DMSO was added to all control wells. Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The formazan produced by MTS was quantified by measuring absorbance at 490 nm with an Infinite M200 microplate reader (Tecan, Grödig, Austria).

Solutions

The HEPES-buffered solution was prepared with the following composition (in mM): 140 NaCl, 5 KCl, 1 MgCl₂, 1 CaCl₂, 10 D-glucose, and 10 HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4. The NaSCN-substituted HEPES-buffered solution was created by replacing NaCl with NaSCN in the original HEPES-buffered solution.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software). Group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test, or by Welch’s ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s T3 or Games–Howell’s multiple-comparisons tests when variance homogeneity was not met. Body weight time-course data were analyzed by two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. Cell differentiation data with multiple cell types as subgroups were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using the Brown–Forsythe test. When normality or variance homogeneity assumptions were not met, data were log-transformed prior to analysis (e.g., cytokine data) or analyzed using Welch-type approaches as indicated in the figure legends. To control for multiple testing, family-wise error rate was controlled by Tukey’s, Dunnett’s T3, or Games–Howell’s post-hoc tests as appropriate. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The specific test applied to each panel is indicated in the corresponding figure legend. All in vivo and in vitro experiments were repeated at least three independent times.

Results

YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 attenuated LPS-induced acute lung injury in the pre-treatment model

To evaluate the effects of prophylactic treatment, YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 were administered intraperitoneally 1 h before LPS instillation, and mice were euthanized 48 h after LPS exposure (Fig. 1A). Body weight was significantly reduced in the LPS group as well as in both inhibitor-treated groups compared with the vehicle group; however, no significant difference in body weight was observed between the LPS group and either inhibitor-treated group (Fig. 1B). LPS markedly increased BALF total cell counts and protein concentration, both of which were significantly reduced by YS-01 and PDSinh-C01, with no significant difference between the two inhibitors (Fig. 1C and D). Among BALF inflammatory cell parameters, total cell counts and neutrophil counts were significantly reduced by both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 (Fig. 1E and F). Lung injury scores were significantly lower in both inhibitor-treated groups than in the LPS group; furthermore, YS-01-treated mice showed significantly lower lung injury scores than PDSinh-C01-treated mice (Fig. 1G and H). Western blot analysis showed that LPS increased pendrin and phospho-IκB expression in lung tissue (Fig. 1I). Densitometric analysis demonstrated that both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 significantly reduced pendrin expression compared with LPS alone, whereas no significant difference was observed between the two inhibitors. In addition, LPS significantly increased phospho-IκB expression, and this increase was significantly reduced by both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 (Fig. 1I and J).

YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 post-treatment suppressed the LPS-induced acute lung injury

To determine the effectiveness of YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 post-treatment after the onset of LPS-induced injury, the inhibitors were administered intraperitoneally 6 and 12 h following LPS treatment, and mice were euthanized 24 h post-LPS administration (Fig. 2A). Body weight was significantly reduced at 24 h compared with baseline in the LPS, LPS + YS-01, and LPS+PDSinh-C01 groups, with no significant difference in the degree of weight loss among the three groups (Fig. 2B). In the LPS group, the total cell counts and protein concentration in BALF were markedly elevated, whereas both were significantly reduced in YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 post-treated groups, with no significant difference between the two inhibitors (Fig. 2C and D). LPS-induced increases in BALF total cell, neutrophil and macrophage counts were significantly inhibited in the YS-01 and PDSinh-C01, with no significant difference between the two inhibitor-treated groups (Fig. 2E and F). Lung injury scores were significantly reduced in both the YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 groups compared with the LPS group; furthermore, PDSinh-C01-treated mice showed significantly lower lung injury scores than YS-01-treated mice (Fig. 2G and H). Western blot analysis of lung tissue lysates from the post-treatment groups showed increased pendrin and phospho-IκB following LPS treatment, and phospho-IκB expression was reduced in the YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 groups (Fig. 2I). Densitometric quantification revealed that LPS significantly increased pendrin expression compared with the vehicle group; both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 significantly reduced pendrin expression compared with the LPS group, with no significant difference observed between the two inhibitor-treated groups. Similarly, LPS significantly increased phospho-IκB expression, and this increase was significantly reduced by both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01, with no significant difference between the two inhibitor-treated groups (Fig. 2J).

YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 reduced proinflammatory cytokine levels in LPS-induced acute lung injury

Proinflammatory cytokine levels, including IL-1β, MIP-2, IL-6, and TNF-α, were measured by ELISA in lung tissue protein lysates. In the pre-treatment model, LPS significantly increased all four cytokines, and both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 significantly reduced these increases. Among the inhibitor-treated groups, YS-01 produced significantly lower IL-1β, MIP-2, and IL-6 levels than PDSinh-C01, whereas TNF-α levels did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 3A). In the post-treatment model, LPS significantly increased IL-1β, MIP-2, IL-6, and TNF-α levels compared with the vehicle group, and both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 significantly reduced all four cytokines compared with the LPS group. Between the two inhibitor-treated groups, YS-01 showed significantly lower IL-6 levels than PDSinh-C01, whereas IL-1β, MIP-2, and TNF-α levels did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 3B).

Relative pendrin mRNA levels were decreased in the YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 treatment groups

Pendrin mRNA levels in lung tissue were analyzed using real-time quantitative PCR. LPS exposure significantly increased pendrin mRNA levels, which were markedly decreased in both the YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 pre-treatment groups, with no significant difference observed between the two treatments (Fig. 4A). Similarly, pendrin mRNA levels were significantly reduced in the YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 post-treatment groups, again showing no significant difference between the two drugs (Fig. 4B).

Pendrin expression increased upon LPS exposure and was suppressed by YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 pre-treatment in HPAEpiC

To evaluate the effects of pendrin inhibitors on LPS-induced protein expression in human pulmonary alveolar epithelial cells, HPAEpiC were pre-treated with YS-01 or PDSinh-C01 for 1 h prior to LPS stimulation. Western blot analysis demonstrated that LPS exposure significantly increased the levels of pendrin and phospho-IκB, while pre-treatment with YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 reduced the levels of both proteins (Fig. 5A). Densitometric analysis confirmed that LPS significantly increased both pendrin/β-actin and phospho-IκB/total IκB ratios, and both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 significantly reduced these increases. No significant difference was observed between the two inhibitor-treated groups (Fig. 5B).

YS-01 exhibited a more favorable inhibitory profile on pendrin-mediated Cl−/SCN− exchange activity compared to PDSinh-C01

Pendrin mediated Cl−/SCN− exchange activity was measured in CHO-K1 cells expressing human wild-type pendrin and a halide sensor YFP (Fig. 6A and B). Cells were pretreated with the indicated concentrations of YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 for 10 min. Discussion

In the present study, we performed a direct head-to-head comparison of two pendrin inhibitors, YS-01 and PDSinh-C01, in a mouse model of LPS-induced lung injury and complementary in vitro assays. Our results demonstrate that both inhibitors significantly reduced lung injury in both pre-treatment and post-treatment settings, decreasing BALF cell counts, protein concentrations, and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Histopathological analysis showed a reduction in lung injury severity with both compounds. In the pre-treatment model, both inhibitors significantly reduced lung injury scores compared with the LPS group, with YS-01-treated mice showing significantly lower scores than PDSinh-C01-treated mice. In the post-treatment model, both inhibitors also reduced lung injury scores, with PDSinh-C01 showing a significantly lower score than YS-01. These findings align with previous reports showing pendrin upregulation in LPS-induced lung injury and its attenuation by pendrin inhibitors16,18. Taken together, the present findings indicate that both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 are active in this LPS-induced acute lung injury model, while the comparative differences between the two compounds depend on the specific endpoint and experimental setting.

We also observed that both inhibitors reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in LPS-induced lung injury. Among these cytokines, IL-1β is a well-established mediator of lung inflammation, contributing to disease progression via αvβ6 integrin-dependent mechanisms19,20. MIP-2 plays a crucial role in neutrophil recruitment to the lungs, exacerbating tissue damage, while studies have shown that neutralizing MIP-2 can mitigate lung injury21. Additionally, IL-6, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine elevated in critically ill patients with sepsis, has been associated with an increased risk of lung injury and has been proposed as a biomarker for disease severity22. Furthermore, TNF-α knockdown has been shown to alleviate lung injury in animal models, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target23. Both pendrin inhibitors significantly reduced inflammatory cytokine levels, although the comparative pattern differed between the pre-treatment and post-treatment settings. In the pre-treatment model, YS-01 showed significantly greater reductions than PDSinh-C01 in IL-1β, MIP-2, and IL-6, whereas in the post-treatment model YS-01 showed a significantly greater reduction only in IL-6. These findings are consistent with the view that YS-01 may show modest advantages in selected inflammatory readouts, but not uniform superiority across all endpoints.

At the molecular level, both inhibitors significantly reduced PDS (SLC26A4) mRNA levels compared to the LPS group, restoring them to levels comparable to the vehicle group, indicating effective inhibition of pendrin at the transcriptional level. However, the downstream mechanistic interpretation of these findings should be made cautiously. Based on our prior work, pendrin inhibition may reduce SCN⁻ transport and thereby attenuate OSCN⁻/NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling, but this pathway was not directly tested in the present comparative study. In addition, NF-κB transcriptional activity was not assessed directly, and the mechanistic conclusions of this work therefore remain correlative rather than definitive. NF-κB, a key transcription factor in inflammatory responses, plays a critical role in lung injury pathogenesis, and its inhibition has been shown to attenuate inflammation in vivo24–27. Accordingly, the present findings are best interpreted as showing that pendrin inhibition was associated with reduced inflammatory cytokine production and, in some settings, decreased NF-κB-related signaling markers. Western blot analyses in the current study should therefore be interpreted as supportive of this proposed mechanism rather than as definitive proof that lung protection occurred directly via NF-κB suppression. Direct mechanistic verification of the proposed pendrin–SCN⁻/OSCN⁻/NF-κB axis—including measurement of NF-κB nuclear translocation, transcriptional reporter activity, or rescue and pathway-perturbation experiments—was not performed in the present comparative study. The current evidence is therefore associative rather than mechanistic, and the proposed pathway should be regarded as hypothesis-generating until further validated in dedicated mechanistic studies. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of pendrin inhibitors in managing inflammatory lung conditions, although further mechanistic studies will be required to define the signaling pathway more directly.

We demonstrated that pendrin expression was upregulated following LPS exposure and was effectively suppressed by YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 pre-treatment in human pulmonary alveolar epithelial cells (HPAEpiC). These in vitro data support the biological relevance of pendrin inhibition in an epithelial context; however, they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of clinical efficacy. While direct evidence of pendrin inhibition in ARDS patients is currently lacking, these findings support further evaluation of pendrin inhibition in inflammatory lung disease.

In the present comparative study, we directly evaluated the inhibitory effects of YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 on pendrin-mediated Cl⁻/SCN⁻ exchange activity. The IC50 values were 3.21 µM for YS-01 and 5.29 µM for PDSinh-C01, indicating that YS-01 has a lower IC50 and a more favorable inhibitory profile in this in vitro assay (Fig. 6). However, the present study was not designed to determine whether YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 bind to different sites on pendrin or differ in inhibitory kinetics, pharmacokinetic behavior, or off-target effects. PDSinh-C01 has been characterized as a pyrazole-thiophenesulfonamide compound with reversible, noncompetitive inhibition of pendrin-mediated anion exchange and a likely intracellular site of action13,28, whereas YS-01 was identified through an independent screen by our group and has been characterized primarily in the context of airway inflammation15,16. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as comparative pharmacologic observations rather than as a definitive mechanistic distinction between the two inhibitors.

In addition to efficacy, drug safety and cytotoxicity are critical factors in drug development. Cell viability assays are widely used to assess the impact of compounds on cell proliferation and toxicity29. In our study, YS-01 showed no significant effect on cell viability up to 30 µM, whereas PDSinh-C01 significantly reduced cell viability at concentrations of 10 µM and 30 µM (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7). These results indicate that YS-01 showed a more favorable in vitro cell viability profile than PDSinh-C01 under the tested conditions. However, this finding is based on a short-term MTS assay in a single cell type and should not be overinterpreted as evidence of systemic or translational safety.

Despite advances in critical care, acute lung injury and ARDS remains a major clinical challenge, with high mortality rates and limited treatment options beyond mechanical ventilation and supportive care. Pneumonia-induced ARDS, in particular, is one of the most common and severe forms of ARDS, as pulmonary infection triggers excessive inflammation, alveolar damage, and vascular leakage, leading to impaired gas exchange. The present study used a single-dose LPS-induced acute lung injury model as an experimentally tractable platform for comparative evaluation of two pendrin inhibitors. However, this model does not fully recapitulate the biological complexity of pneumonia-induced ARDS, including pathogen–host interactions, temporal heterogeneity, and later physiological consequences of injury. Given that pendrin inhibitors significantly reduce key inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, they may warrant further investigation as novel therapeutics for pneumonia-induced ARDS. Similarly, the observed changes in NF-κB-related markers are consistent with a possible anti-inflammatory effect, but they do not by themselves establish clinical relevance in pneumonia-associated ARDS. These findings suggest that pendrin inhibition may represent a candidate adjunctive strategy for further preclinical study, and further studies are warranted to validate their efficacy in clinically relevant pneumonia-induced ARDS models.

In this study, we demonstrated that both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 effectively mitigated lung injury, with YS-01 showing a lower IC50 for pendrin-mediated Cl⁻/SCN⁻ exchange activity and demonstrating a more favorable cell viability profile. Taken together, the present data support the view that YS-01 may have a more favorable overall profile in selected in vitro and inflammatory readouts. However, because the comparative differences between YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 were not consistent across all in vivo endpoints, the current findings should be interpreted as comparative pharmacologic observations rather than definitive evidence of overall superiority.

However, several challenges must be addressed before YS-01 can be translated into clinical use. The potential systemic effects of pendrin inhibition in organs where pendrin is expressed should be thoroughly investigated. Further studies should focus on optimizing YS-01 through detailed structure-activity relationship analyses to improve its specificity and pharmacokinetic properties. In addition, no pharmacokinetic, biodistribution, or organ-specific safety studies were performed in the present work, and potential effects on the kidney, thyroid, and inner ear remain to be explored. The study also has several additional limitations. No formal a priori power calculation was performed, multiple endpoints were assessed across the study, and the pre-treatment and post-treatment models used different endpoint timings (48 h and 24 h, respectively), reflecting different biological aims but limiting direct comparison between the two models. Importantly, apparent differences in compound performance between the pre-treatment and post-treatment paradigms may, in part, reflect these distinct assessment windows rather than true differences in compound efficacy, and direct quantitative comparison between the two paradigms is therefore not warranted. Moreover, body weight changes did not always parallel histologic improvement, which may reflect systemic effects of LPS exposure such as sickness behavior and reduced intake rather than lung injury alone. Finally, survival and direct pulmonary edema/permeability measures, such as wet-to-dry lung weight ratio or Evans blue extravasation, were not included. Despite these challenges, YS-01 remains a reasonable candidate for further comparative preclinical development, particularly if future studies clarify its mechanism, pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and organ-level safety.

Conclusion

In conclusion, both YS-01 and PDSinh-C01 attenuated LPS-induced ALI and reduced inflammatory responses in this comparative preclinical study. Across in vivo endpoints, the two inhibitors performed comparably overall, with timing-dependent differences in histologic outcomes: YS-01 produced significantly lower lung injury scores in the pre-treatment paradigm, whereas PDSinh-C01 produced significantly lower scores in the post-treatment paradigm. In contrast, YS-01 showed a clearer in vitro advantage, with a lower IC50 for pendrin-mediated Cl⁻/SCN⁻ exchange inhibition and a more favorable cell viability profile under the tested conditions. These findings support further preclinical evaluation of pendrin inhibition in inflammatory lung injury, while highlighting the need for additional mechanistic, pharmacokinetic, and safety studies.
