A 26-year-old South Asian woman presented to the emergency department with a painful violaceous edematous plaque with central ulceration and purulent discharge on her left calf following a cat scratch sustained several weeks earlier (Figure 1A). She denied systemic symptoms and had a family history of Crohn disease. She had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus but no autoimmune conditions. Before presenting to the emergency department, she had seen her family physician, who diagnosed folliculitis secondary to a cat scratch and prescribed oral doxycycline (100 mg twice daily) for 4 weeks. After no improvement, she presented to the emergency department with an expanding ulcer. The working diagnosis was cellulitis, and she received 1 dose of intravenous ceftriaxone and topical mupirocin. She was discharged with oral amoxicillin–clavulanate (875 mg, twice daily).

Progression showed exacerbation (pathergy) and eventual healing. At initial presentation, the ulcer showed a violaceous border and purulent discharge. The progressed ulcer after 2 months of treatment showed well-demarcated, hyperpigmented-to-black borders, and some central cribriform scarring. After 3 months of treatment, the healing stage showed grey–brown borders, cribriform scarring, and residual erythema.

The following day, the patient returned to the emergency department with another painful lesion on the right lower back (Figure 2A). On examination, a fluctuant, tender, skin-coloured pustule with surrounding erythema was noted. The lesion had been present for several years but was recently aggravated by friction from undergarments. She reported severe localized pain. Incision and drainage of the area were performed, yielding purulent material. After consultation with an infectious disease specialist, she was discharged with continued amoxicillin–clavulanate and analgesics. One week later, she was reassessed in the infectious disease clinic and referred to a dermatologist for suspected pyoderma gangrenosum or vasculitis.

Progression of an ulcer caused by pyoderma gangrenosum on the back of a 26-year-old woman with Fitzpatrick type IV skin tone at (A) initial presentation at emergency department visit, (B) dermatology follow-up after 2 months of treatment, and (C) dermatology follow-up after 3 months of treatment. Progression showed an initial pustule-like lesion, exacerbation (pathergy) following surgical débridement, and eventual healing. At initial presentation, the ulcer was an edematous pustule with surrounding erythema. At 2-month follow-up, it was a well-demarcated ulcer with brown–violaceous undermined borders. After 3 months of treatment, the healing stage showed a violaceous plaque with cribriform scarring, residual erythema, and brown–violet and hyperpigmented borders.

We saw the patient in our dermatology clinic 2 months later, and the drained ulcer had worsened. Our differential diagnosis included pyoderma gangrenosum, cellulitis, vasculitis, and fixed drug eruption. We performed a punch biopsy of the active ulcer’s edge and collected wound samples for culture. Pathology revealed neutrophil-predominant inflammation, resembling a neutrophilic dermatosis such as pyoderma gangrenosum. No underlying vasculitis was identified. The culture grew methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and Corynebacterium striatum, indicating a superimposed infection. We referred her to a gastroenterologist for assessment of underlying systemic disease, given her family history and the known association between pyoderma gangrenosum and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).1 Colonoscopy revealed no evidence of IBD.

Given the wound’s poor response to antibiotics, the neutrophilic infiltration, and the clinical picture of painful, progressive ulcers with inflamed undermined borders, we diagnosed pyoderma gangrenosum. The presence of pathergy, defined as lesion worsening following minor skin trauma (cat scratch, clothing friction, surgical débridement), helped support the diagnosis.

At the patient’s 2-week follow-up visit, we observed several new lesions on the patient’s arms and legs. Given the disease extent and severity, we initiated systemic therapy of oral cyclosporine (150 mg daily). We managed acute flares with short courses of oral prednisone (0.5 mg/kg/d) and oral doxycycline (100 mg); prednisone was tapered over several weeks. Oral contraception (3 mg drospirenone, 14.2 mg estetrol) was initiated, as she regularly experienced premenstrual flares, with increased inflammation, bleeding, pain, and ulcer reopening. Every other day, wound care nurses administered adjunctive pyoderma gangrenosum–specific wound care of tacrolimus 0.1% ointment and nonadherent lipidocolloid contact layer dressing. Clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment was applied around the lesions. Wound care also emphasized very careful, slow, and gentle dressing changes every 3 days, as previous daily dressing changes contributed to skin injury and exacerbated her ulcers. Extremely gentle débridement of loose necrotized skin during dressing changes was also beneficial.

We followed up with this patient every 6 to 8 weeks to monitor her progress. Over several months, she showed improvement with cyclosporine, topical tacrolimus, and specialized wound care (Figure 1 and Figure 2). The ulcers progressively healed, although substantial cribriform scarring and pigmentary changes persisted (Figure 3). Eighteen months after the initial emergency department visit, she had no new lesions and was on maintenance therapy of cyclosporine (100 mg), topical tacrolimus, and adjunctive wound care.

Discussion

Pyoderma gangrenosum is a rare, debilitating autoinflammatory neutrophilic dermatosis that begins as a tender pustule and rapidly progresses into a painful ulcer with irregular undermined violaceous borders and a purulent base, eventually healing with atrophic cribriform scarring and dyspigmentation.1 Pyoderma gangrenosum most commonly involves the lower extremities but may occur anywhere on the body.1 The worldwide incidence is 3 to 10 cases per million people per year.2 Its clinical features overlap with infectious and vascular diseases, making diagnosis challenging. It may mimic soft-tissue infections in early, nonulcerative stages, and vascular conditions (e.g., venous leg ulcers, vasculitis) in later, ulcerative stages.3 Thus, mimicking diseases must be excluded.4

Three validated tools exist to help with diagnostic clarity: criteria from Su and colleagues, Delphi consensus criteria, and the PARACELSUS score.4 The PARACELSUS score had the highest sensitivity and utilizes a scoring system of 10 or more points to diagnose pyoderma gangrenosum.4 In this system, progressive disease, exclusion of other diseases, and a red–violet wound border are scored 3 points each. Amelioration with immunosuppressants, an irregularly-shaped border, pathergy, and substantial pain are scored 2 points each.4 Suppurative inflammation on histopathology, an undermined wound edge, and associated systemic disease are scored 1 point each.1,4 In our patient, all but the last criteria were met; although she had type 2 diabetes mellitus, she lacked a systemic disease commonly associated with pyoderma gangrenosum. Approximately 50% of patients with pyoderma gangrenosum have an associated systemic disease, most commonly IBD.1 Additional conditions include other autoimmune disorders (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, Sjogren syndrome), hematologic disease (e.g., leukemia, lymphoma), neutrophilic disorder, autoinflammatory disorder, or malignancy.1 Pathergy, although not present in all patients, can be a helpful diagnostic clue.5 In our patient, multiple episodes of pathergy supported the diagnosis of pyoderma gangrenosum, in addition to the progression of ulceration and failure of antibiotic therapy. Neutrophilic infiltration on pathology was observed but is not specific to pyoderma gangrenosum, as it occurs in other neutrophilic dermatoses (e.g., Sweet syndrome);3 thus, clinical correlation is required.

The 3 diagnostic tools differ slightly in their major and minor criteria. However, all incorporate colour-dependent features (e.g., the Su criteria include violaceous borders, the Delphi consensus criteria include ulcers with peripheral erythema),4 which may make diagnosis in people with darker skin more difficult. In patients with darker skin, inflammation may appear hyperpigmented, grey, dusky, brown, or black rather than erythematous or red–violet,6,7 potentially resulting in lower scores on these diagnostic tools. These differences in presentation, coupled with the underrepresentation of people with darker skin in medical education and literature, contribute to the diagnostic challenges in this population.6–8 Delay in diagnosis of pyoderma gangrenosum can result in prolonged disease course or hospitalization. Black people have higher rates of hospitalization for pyoderma gangrenosum,9 underscoring the importance of recognizing the clinical presentation of pyoderma gangrenosum in people with Fitzpatrick IV to VI phototype classification.

Currently, pyoderma gangrenosum has no gold-standard treatment, and high-quality evidence is limited.3 Treatment decisions should be individualized, considering lesion location, severity, associated systemic disease, treatment cost, and patient comorbidities and preferences.3 First-line therapy is high-dose corticosteroids, usually oral prednisone (0.5 to 1 mg/kg/d) or intravenous pulse corticosteroid (1000 mg/d).3 Cyclosporine (2.5 to 5 mg/kg/d) could also be considered as a steroid-sparing first-line therapy.1 Emerging therapies such as tumour necrosis factor-α inhibitors have demonstrated benefit as second-line options.1,3 In limited disease (e.g., superficial ulceration), intralesional triamcinolone (40 mg/mL), topical tacrolimus 0.1% ointment, or steroid cream (e.g., clobetasol propionate 0.05%) may be sufficient.3 Pyoderma gangrenosum–specific adjunctive wound care emphasizing gentle handling and appropriate dressings (e.g., nonadherent lipocolloid) is helpful.1 Gentle conservative débridement of loosely adhered necrotized tissue may be beneficial.1 Secondary infections can be treated with antibiotics. Pain management is essential for treating pyoderma gangrenosum to minimize the psychological burden on patients.3,10 Interestingly, our patient had premenstrual pyoderma gangrenosum flares, which were effectively managed with drospirenone–estetrol. Menstrual cycle–related pyoderma gangrenosum was reported and managed similarly with oral contraceptives in one other published case,11 which could indicate a possible hormonal component in the pathophysiology of pyoderma gangrenosum.

Pyoderma gangrenosum requires early immunomodulatory therapy to limit progression and improve outcomes. Given its rarity and complexity, timely diagnosis is a recurring challenge across published case reports of pyoderma gangrenosum.5 The diagnostic delay can impose a substantial psychological burden and reduce quality of life because of severe pain, diagnostic uncertainty, anxiety or depression, and associated physical and social limitations.10 If pyoderma gangrenosum is suspected, early initiation of steroid therapy, biopsy of the active ulcer edges, and screening for underlying systemic diseases (commonly, IBD, autoimmune arthritis, or hematologic disorders) would be beneficial, alongside a dermatology referral to minimize disease duration, given long specialist wait times.1,3,4 Clinical suspicion for an underlying disease should prompt further investigations (e.g., rheumatoid factor, anticyclic citrullinated peptide for suspected rheumatoid arthritis; colonoscopy for suspected IBD). Surgical débridement is contraindicated to avoid pathergy.1 Finally, long-term dyspigmentation and cribriform scarring may be more prominent and disfiguring in patients with darker skin; scar-directed therapies could be considered in remission.

Pyoderma gangrenosum is a rare, debilitating neutrophilic dermatosis that can mimic infectious and vascular conditions, making diagnosis challenging.4 Morphology may vary with skin type, and pyoderma gangrenosum should be considered as a differential in expanding ulcers.1 This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of pyoderma gangrenosum and aims to increase the clinical awareness of this rare dermatosis, especially in people with darker skin.
