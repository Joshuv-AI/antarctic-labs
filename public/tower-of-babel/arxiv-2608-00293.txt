A Phase-Field Method for Curvature Flow of Networks with Triple Junction Drag
Yuchuan Yang, Selim Esedoḡlu

Abstract
We develop a new phase-field (diffuse interface) approximation of multiphase curvature motion with triple junction drag – an important sharp interface model for the evolution of microstructure in polycrystalline materials during heat treatment. This sharp interface model arises as gradient flow for the total length of the interfacial network with respect to a certain metric. Accordingly, we derive our diffuse interface approximation – a coupled system of partial differential equations that is a variant of the Allen-Cahn system – from a variational perspective, in the style of minimizing movements: starting from a discrete in time approximation that entails a convex optimization problem to advance from one time step to the next. In the process, we propose a simple integral expression that counts the number of junctions using the order parameter that appears to be new even for the standard multiphase Allen-Cahn system. The convergence of the resulting flow to the desired sharp interface limit is then verified via the method of matched asymptotic expansions. Numerical convergence studies against both known exact solutions as well as highly accurate benchmark solutions obtained via front tracking provide clear further evidence for this convergence. Moreover, the method retains the most desirable feature of diffuse interface methods: Automatic handling of topological changes in the network of interfaces.



1
Introduction

Multiphase mean curvature motion of interfacial networks plays a central role in many applications.
For example, in materials science, it gets used since the work of Mullins
[
30
]
in 1950’s to model the evolution of grain boundaries in polycrystalline materials during annealing.
One of the challenging aspects of this dynamics is the presence of free boundaries known as junctions along which three or more interfaces meet.
The motion of junctions is part of the unknown in the problem; it is uniquely determined by the (mean) curvature motion of the interfaces away from the junctions, and free boundary conditions that are imposed along the junctions.
The most well known boundary condition along junctions is that of
Herring

[
19
]
which, in physical terms, stipulates that surface tension forces applied by the interfaces on a triple junction must vanish at all times.
The force balance can equivalently be expressed in terms of the angles formed between the interfaces intersecting along a given triple junction, resulting in what is known as the
Herring angle condition
.
During the evolution, interfaces and junctions may collide, pinch off, or altogether disappear, and new junctions may be nucleated.
To deal with these inevitable topological events, implicit interface methods such as phase-field (see e.g.
[
8
,
9
,
15
]
), level-set (see e.g.
[
31
,
10
,
6
,
11
,
35
]
), and threshold dynamics (see e.g.
[
26
,
14
,
12
,
32
,
33
]
) have been developed and employed.
There have been numerous large scale simulations of microstructural evolution reported with this model and its variants in the literature, with varying degrees of success in reproducing certain statistics, such as the grain size distribution or the misorientation distribution function, that are of interest to materials scientists.

Recently, detailed non-destructive experimental measurements of grain boundary motion made possible by high energy diffraction microscopy and diffraction contrast tomography have spurred intense activity in comparing simulation results against individual grain boundaries in 4D data
[
25
,
32
,
33
]
.
Significant differences have been noted, leading to speculation that Mullins’ traditional model may be inadequate or require substantial modification.
One possible modification that has been proposed is
triple junction drag

[
17
,
5
,
13
,
37
]
: The traditional Herring angle condition is replaced by a relaxed version which, in physical terms, no longer requires instantaneous force balance.
Instead, triple junctions move with finite speed to restore the force balance.

There is a shortage of numerical methods in the literature suitable for large scale simulations of grain boundary motion with triple junction drag that can seamlessly handle topological changes in two or three dimensions.
The major exception is
[
21
]
that proposes a phase-field method; see also the very recent follow-ups
[
27
,
28
]
that carry out large scale simulations using closely related methods.
The primary goal of
[
21
,
27
,
28
]
appear to be capturing some
qualitative
implications of junction drag, without claiming convergence to the sharp interface description of the evolution (no careful convergence study is offered).
That level of qualitative approximation may well suffice to explore how junction drag influences statistical measures, such as size distribution, of grain networks.
However, as we demonstrate in Section 3, the approach of
[
21
]
does not always converge
quantitatively
to the solution of the desired system of partial differential equations (PDEs) that describe curvature motion with triple junction drag; see equation (
6
) in Section 2 below for a precise statement of this system.
Recent impetus in the materials science community towards comparing the evolution of
individual
interfaces in simulations against experimental measurements goes far beyond statistics, and calls for numerical methods that are guaranteed to converge to the intended PDE model in the quantitative, traditional numerical analysis sense.
The purpose of the present study is to address the lack of such methods in existing literature.

Our task in this work is thus to follow up on works such as
[
21
,
27
]
by taking a further step towards developing phase-field methods that are equally robust (in handling topological changes) and practical (suitable for simulations with many grains in two or three dimensions), but also
convergent
to the well-known, precise sharp interface limit (
6
) of curvature motion with triple junction drag.
To that end, we propose a novel phase-field method for triple junction drag, substantiate its consistency by formal matched asymptotic analysis, and investigate its convergence with careful numerical experiments against exact solutions as well as highly accurate benchmarks obtained via front tracking. We also demonstrate how topological changes are automatically handled, as is expected from any practical phase-field method.

2
Sharp-interface formulation

In this section, we give a precise description of
multiphase curvature motion with triple junction drag
that appears in models of microstructural evolution.
It constitutes the precise dynamics, described by a PDE system, that existing numerical methods such as
[
21
,
27
,
28
]
as well as the new one proposed in this study aim to approximate.
As the situation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is already interesting, we focus on planar networks in the remainder of the paper, but always with an eye towards seamless extension to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(a hallmark of phase-field methods), which will be taken up in subsequent work.

We fix
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
as our domain and let
\Sigma_{i}(t)\subset\Omega
,

i=1,2,3
, be the (evolving) region occupied by the
i-
th grain. The sets
\Sigma_{i}
form a partition of
\Omega
so that
\Omega=\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}\cup\Sigma_{3}
and
|\Sigma_{i}\cap\Sigma_{j}|=0
whenever
i\neq j
.
(We write
|S|
to denote the area of a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
).
Let
\Gamma_{i}(t)
denote the boundary between
\Sigma_{i-1}(t)
and
\Sigma_{i}(t)
(Figure
1
).
We assume that the curves
\Gamma_{i}(t)
meet at a triple junction
p(t)
for the entire duration of the evolution considered.
Motion by curvature with triple junction drag
refers to the evolution of the curves
\Gamma_{i}(t)
governed by the law:

\displaystyle v_{i}=\sigma_{i}m_{GB}^{(i)}\kappa_{i},\qquad\text{along }\Gamma_{i}

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}p(t)=m_{TJ}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i},\qquad\text{at the triple junction}

(2)

Here,
\sigma_{i}
is the surface tension coefficient associated with the interface
\Gamma_{i}
,
m_{GB}
is the mobility of the grain boundary,
m_{TJ}
is the mobility of the triple junction,
v_{i}
is the normal velocity of
\Gamma_{i}
,
\kappa_{i}
is the curvature of
\Gamma_{i}
and
\tau_{i}
is the unit tangent vector of
\Gamma_{i}
at the triple junction
p(t)
, pointing away from it.

Figure 1:
A network with a single triple junction

The system (
1
) & (
2
) has an underlying variational principle (see
[
37
]
), which will be the basis of our phase-field model.
In words, (
1
) & (
2
) is a formal gradient flow of the total length functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sigma_{i}\text{Length}(\Gamma_{i})

(3)

with respect to a metric comprising

1.

the
L^{2}
norm of the perturbation to the curves in the normal direction and

2.

the Euclidean norm of the perturbation to the triple junction’s location.

To be precise, let
\gamma_{i}(\cdot,t)
be parametrizations of the curves
\Gamma_{i}(t)
so that
\gamma_{i}(0,t)=p(t)
, the location of the triple junction.
Then (
1
) & (
2
) is formally gradient flow for the energy

\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sigma_{i}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}\gamma_{i}|\,dx

(4)

with respect to the following inner product (modulo degeneracy in the tangential direction) defined on a pair of triplets of vector fields
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\{\xi^{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}:=\{\eta^{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
:

\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{m_{GB}^{(i)}}\int_{0}^{1}\big(\xi^{i}(x)\cdot\nu_{i}(x)\big)\big(\eta^{i}(x)\cdot\nu_{i}(x)\big)|\partial_{x}\gamma_{i}(x,t)|\;dx+\frac{1}{m_{TJ}}\xi^{1}(0)\cdot\eta^{1}(0).

(5)

where
\xi^{i}\,,\,\eta^{i}\,:\Gamma_{i}(t)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
obey
\xi^{i}(0)=\xi^{j}(0)
and
\eta^{i}(0)=\eta^{j}(0)
for any
i\not=j
, and
\nu_{i}(x)
denotes the unit normal to
\Gamma_{i}(t)
at
x\in\Gamma_{i}
.
We note that, formally, in the limit
m_{TJ}\to\infty
, the pointwise term in (
5
) drops out and one recovers the standard variational formulation of curvature motion subject to Herring angle conditions.

The geometric evolution problem (
1
) & (
2
) can then be described by a system of PDEs (initial-boundary value problem) in terms of the parametrizations
\gamma_{i}(x,t)
of the curves
\Gamma_{i}(t)
as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma_{it}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i}m_{GB}^{(i)}\frac{\gamma_{ixx}(x,t)}{|\gamma_{ix}(x,t)|^{2}},

\displaystyle(x,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,T],\qquad i\in\{1,2,3\}

(6)

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(0,t)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j}(0,t),

\displaystyle t\in[0,T],\quad i,j\in\{1,2,3\}

\displaystyle\gamma_{1t}(0,t)

\displaystyle=m_{TJ}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sigma_{j}\frac{\gamma_{jx}(0,t)}{|\gamma_{jx}(0,t)|},

\displaystyle t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(1,t)

\displaystyle\equiv\gamma_{i}^{0}(1),

\displaystyle t\in[0,T]\qquad i\in\{1,2,3\}

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(x,0)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{i}^{0}(x),

\displaystyle x\in[0,1]\qquad i\in\{1,2,3\}.

Short time well-posedness of (
6
) was established in
[
37
]
, verifying that the triple junction drag condition (
2
) is a valid boundary condition that uniquely determines the evolution of the network. One may immediately observe that while (
1
) only specifies the normal velocity of the curves, (
6
) further specifies the tangential velocity of the curves as well (this follows
[
7
]
).
It is easy to check that any solution of (
6
) is a solution of (
1
) & (
2
). Conversely, it is proven in
[
37
]
that given any three sufficiently regular curves satisfying (
1
) & (
2
), one can always find parametrizations for them that satisfy (
6
). We note that there is an extensive literature on curvature motion of networks (e.g.
[
7
,
24
,
18
,
23
]
) formulated as such, in terms of parametrized curves.

Before the formation of any singularities or topological changes in the network, such descriptions of the network evolution in terms of explicitly parametrized curves, beyond being of fundamental theoretical interest, are also physically relevant, and naturally suggest
front tracking
as a highly efficient and accurate numerical method.
However, in simulations of relevance to materials scientists, who are often keenly interested in the coarsening dynamics of the network as many grains shrink and disappear, topological changes are inevitable.
While the treatment of topological changes in a front tracking implementation might be feasible in two dimensions under very special circumstances (e.g. equal surface tensions and mobilities, which in the Herring angle case allows a classification of possible transitions in the network into a small number of cases, see e.g.
[
24
,
23
]
), computational exploration of these models for a broad range of parameters, and certainly in three dimensions, strongly suggests implicit interface methods as the preferred numerical method.
We believe this to be even more so in the presence of triple junction drag, which makes additional topological changes possible even under the simplest of conditions (i.e. two dimensions, equal surface tensions and mobilities); see
[
37
]
.

In this paper, we choose to focus on the phase-field approach as an implicit interface method for network dynamics (
1
) & (
2
) that incorporates junction drag, as there is already existing work in this direction
[
21
,
27
]
that we build up on, and phase-field more broadly has a long track record in materials literature; other approaches e.g. threshold dynamics
[
26
,
14
]
are an enticing possibility left for future investigation.
Since it is already well understood (as part of a very long history) how phase-field methods can be generalized to accommodate different surface tensions and mobilities, we focus on junction drag as the main novelty and thus take
\sigma_{i}=m_{GB}^{(i)}=1
for all
i
for the rest of the paper.

Previous work

In
[
21
]
, the authors propose a phase-field method for triple junction drag which in the case of three grains can be described by the order parameters
\eta_{i}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
i=1,2,3
that solve the following system of PDEs:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}=L(\eta)(\Delta\eta-\nabla f_{0}(\eta)),

(7)

where
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3})
and

\displaystyle f_{0}(\eta)=\mu\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\big(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\eta_{i}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}\eta_{i}^{4}\big)+\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j\neq i}\eta_{i}^{2}\eta_{j}^{2}\bigg).

\displaystyle L(\eta)\coloneqq L_{GB}-\exp\bigg(-\Phi_{width}\big(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}-\Phi_{min}\big)^{2}\bigg)(L_{GB}-L_{TJ}).

where the parameters
L_{GB}
and

The expression (

the sum of the order parameters, i.e.
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}

\displaystyle\eta_{1}(x)+\eta_{2}(x)+\eta_{3}(x)\approx\begin{cases}1.0,&\text{ inside grains }\\
1.0,&\text{ along grain boundaries }\\
1.08,&\text{ at triple junctions}\end{cases}

for the particular potential

To discover the relation between the parameters

4
New phase-field approximation for triple junction drag

Inspired by
[
21
]
, our goal is to develop a phase-field approximation to (
1
) & (
2
) that can effectively slow down the motion of triple junctions. The challenge is to design a mobility factor
M
so that the motion of any triple junction is retarded by a factor that is
independent
of the junction profile (i.e. the junction angles, which may change in time) as well as the direction of its motion (in relation to the interfaces forming the junction).

To explain our proposed phase-field method in the simplest setting, let us assume that there are
N=3
phases in our spatial domain that is a square
\Omega=[-L,L]^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions. The evolution of our system will be described, in the style of Baldo
[
4
]
, by a vectorial order parameter
u:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
W:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a triple-well potential defined by

\displaystyle W(x)\coloneqq C_{\alpha}|x-\alpha_{1}|^{2}|x-\alpha_{2}|^{2}|x-\alpha_{3}|^{2}

(10)

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
are the three wells. To ensure symmetry, we choose the wells to be the cube roots of unity, i.e.
\alpha_{1}=(1,0)
,
\alpha_{2}=(-1/2,\sqrt{3}/2)
,
\alpha_{3}=(-1/2,-\sqrt{3}/2)
. The normalization constant
C_{\alpha}\approx 1.837^{-2}
ensures that the
geodesic distance

[
4
]
between each pair of wells (and hence the surface tension between each pair of neighboring grains) is equal to one.
This geodesic distance is defined for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

\displaystyle d(\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\inf_{c}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{2W(c(s))}|\dot{c}(s)|\;ds\;:\;c\in C^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{2}),\;c(0)=\alpha,\;c(1)=\beta\bigg\}.

(11)

The surface tension coefficients
\sigma_{ij}
are given by the geodesic distances between the wells of
W
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}=d(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}).

(12)

We remark that for certain choices of potential
W
(such as (
10
)), the geodesics between each pair of the three wells and hence the
\sigma_{ij}
can be explicitly determined
[
1
]
.
For other choices of potential
W
where no explicit formulas are available, one can compute the geodesic distances numerically.

Finally, our proposed phase-field method for curvature motion with triple junction drag is given by the PDE system

u_{t}(x,t)=M(\nabla u(x,t))\bigg(\Delta u(x,t)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla W(u(x,t))\bigg)

(13)

where

M(\nabla u)\coloneqq I_{2\times 2}-\frac{1}{A_{W}}J_{u}\nabla u\bigg[\varepsilon m_{TJ}\bigg(\nabla u^{\top}\nabla u+I_{2\times 2}\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{A_{W}}J_{u}\nabla u^{\top}\nabla u\bigg]^{-1}\nabla u^{\top}

(14)

is a matrix-valued mobility factor. Here,
J_{u}
is the Jacobian determinant

J_{u}=\sqrt{\det(\nabla u(x,t)^{\top}\nabla u(x,t))}.

(15)

The constant
A_{W}\approx 0.86
is completely determined by the choice of potential
W
, and will be defined and explained in the next section.

We can use the Woodbury formula to rewrite the mobility factor (
14
) as

M(\nabla u)^{-1}=I_{2\times 2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon m_{TJ}}\frac{J_{u}}{A_{W}}\nabla u\bigg(\nabla u^{\top}\nabla u+I_{2\times 2}\bigg)^{-2}\nabla u^{\top}.

(16)

which makes it apparent that the symmetric matrix
M(\nabla u)
is positive definite.
Therefore, our method (i.e. (
13
) & (
14
)) satisfies the energy dissipation identity

\frac{d}{dt}E_{\varepsilon}(u)=-\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\Delta u-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla W(u)\bigg)^{\top}M(\nabla u)\bigg(\Delta u-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla W(u)\bigg)\;dx\leq 0

(17)

where the energy
E_{\varepsilon}
is the well-known Modica-Mortola
[
29
]
approximation to perimeter:

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(u)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(u(x))\;dx,\qquad u\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}).

(18)

We highlight two main differences between our mobility factor (
14
) and those of
[
21
,
27
]
.
First, (
14
) depends on
\nabla u(x,t)
vs.
u(x,t)
.
Second, (
14
) is matrix-valued, whereas those of
[
21
,
27
]
are scalar-valued.
These differences are essential in enabling our method to achieve the correct triple junction dynamics for
all
triple junction configurations.

5
The Jacobian determinant

A distinctive feature of our method (
13
) & (
14
) is its reliance on the Jacobian determinant
J_{u}
(see (
15
)) of the order parameter
u(x,t)
. The purpose of this section is to explain its geometric meaning and the role that it plays in influencing triple junction dynamics.

To fix ideas, let
u(x,t)
be a solution of the usual vectorial Allen-Cahn equation (with constant mobility)

\displaystyle u_{t}=\Delta u-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla W(u).

(19)

Assume that
u
is of ‘triple-junction type’: it divides the domain
\Omega
into three regions in each of which

,
\alpha_{2}
or
\alpha_{3}
, and the (diffuse) grain boundaries meet at a small neighborhood
B_{\varepsilon}
of the triple junction.
Away from the triple junction, the diffuse layer quickly settles into an optimal one-dimensional profile as the solution transitions from
u\approx\alpha_{i}
to
u\approx\alpha_{j}
, with
i\not=j

This optimal profile is given by a particular parametrization of the geodesic connecting
\alpha_{i}
to
\alpha_{j}
(
[

]
Lemma 1).
Hence, roughly speaking,
u(\cdot,t):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
maps the interior of each grain to the corresponding well
\alpha_{i}
, and the diffuse grain boundaries (away from the junction) to the geodesics between each pair of wells
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
.
Therefore, we expect
J_{u}
to be concentrated in a neighborhood
B_{\varepsilon}
of triple junctions just like in Figure

, and
u(\cdot,t)
to map
\Omega
onto the
geodesic triangle

\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is the region bounded by the geodesics connecting the wells
\alpha_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
(see Figure

).

Moreover, the change-of-variables formula tells us that

\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}J_{u}(x)\;dx\approx\mbox{Area}\big(u(B_{\varepsilon},t)\big)\approx\mbox{Area}(\mathcal{T})\eqqcolon A_{W},

(20)

which defines the constant
A_{W}
solely in terms of the potential
W
as promised, and as a byproduct gives us a nifty new formula for the total number of junctions

: At any time

during the evolution when there are no critical events occurring (e.g. a collision among triple junctions), we can compute the number of triple junctions

by

\displaystyle\#\text{Triple junctions}:=N_{TJ}(t)\approx\frac{1}{A_{W}}\int_{\Omega}J_{u}(x)\;dx

(21)

(see Figure

). Therefore, the function
\frac{1}{A_{W}}J_{u}
acts as a normalized (unit mass) bump (approximate delta) function at each triple junction and can be readily used to locate and track them during the evolution (see Figures

&

Furthermore, the topological nature of the argument given above justifying (

) with nontrivial mobilities, where the local

) & (

) below.

6
Variational derivation of (
13
)

The mobility factor (
14
) can be derived from a variational perspective. Recall from (
5
) that triple junction drag arises as a consequence of the triple junction’s contribution to the metric. In this vein, we would like to modify the gradient flow structure of the Allen-Cahn equation by introducing an additional penalty term that approximates the movement of the triple junction. Before that, we first review the basic gradient flow structure of the Allen-Cahn equation.

For an energy
E(\cdot)
defined on a metric space
(X,d)
, there is a natural time-discretization of its gradient flow via the
minimizing-movement scheme

[
16
]

\begin{split}x_{n+1}&\in\argmin_{x}\bigg\{E(x)+\frac{1}{2\delta t}d^{2}(x,x_{n})\bigg\}\\
x_{0}&=x^{0},\qquad\text{(initial data)}\end{split}

(22)

with time step size
\delta t
. When we choose the energy to be the Allen-Cahn (or Modica-Mortola
[
29
]
) approximation to perimeter given by (
18
),
and the metric to be the (rescaled)
L^{2}
norm
\big(\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}|u(x)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}\;dx\big)^{1/2}
, the minimizing-movement scheme (
22
) generates a discrete-in-time approximation of the gradient flow

\displaystyle u_{t}=-\frac{\delta E_{\varepsilon}}{\delta u}=\Delta u-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla W(u)

(23)

which is the (vectorial) Allen-Cahn equation
[
3
]
whose sharp-interface limit (as
\varepsilon\to 0
) is multiphase motion by mean curvature with the Herring angle condition satisfied at triple junctions
[
7
,
22
]
. The connection between the Allen-Cahn equation and motion by mean curvature can be motivated by the fact that the energies
E_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)

\Gamma-
converge (in the
L^{1}
topology) to
E(\cdot)
, which generalizes the total length functional to partitions of the domain
\Omega
via

\displaystyle E(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\sigma_{ij}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial^{*}\Sigma_{i}\cap\partial^{*}\Sigma_{j})

(24)

for
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}\cup\Sigma_{3}=\Omega
and
|\Sigma_{i}\cap\Sigma_{j}|=0
whenever
i\neq j

[
4
]
. Here,
\partial^{*}\Sigma_{i}
refers to the reduced boundary of
\Sigma_{i}
in
\Omega
. (Recall that we have normalized the potential
W
so that all surface tension coefficients
\sigma_{ij}
are equal to one.)

Based on our discussion above, the minimizing-movement scheme for the Allen-Cahn equation (
23
) can be written as

\begin{split}u_{n+1}&\in\argmin_{u}\bigg\{E_{\varepsilon}(u)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\delta t}\int_{\Omega}|u(x)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}\;dx\bigg\}\\
u_{0}&=u^{0}.\end{split}

(25)

Following the gradient flow interpretation of (
1
) & (
2
), we will add to the metric term
\frac{\varepsilon}{2\delta t}\int_{\Omega}|u(x)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}\;dx
in (
25
) a penalty for the perturbation of triple junctions.
To demonstrate this, suppose for simplicity that at the
n-
th time step,
u_{n}
approximates a partition of
\Omega
into three grains, separated by three grain boundaries meeting at a triple junction
p_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Suppose also that
u
(very close to
u_{n}
) also approximates such a partition, with a triple junction at
p
that is close to
p_{n}
.
Our scheme will be an approximation to

\displaystyle u_{n+1}\in\argmin_{u}\bigg\{E_{\varepsilon}(u)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\delta t}\int_{\Omega}|u(x)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}\;dx+\bigg(\frac{1}{m_{TJ}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{1}{2\delta t}\bigg)|p-p_{n}|^{2}\bigg\}.

(26)

In order to make scheme (
26
) practical, we need to approximate, using the order parameters
u
and
u_{n}
, the penalty
|p-p_{n}|^{2}
on deviations from the triple junction location
p_{n}
at the
n
-th time step. To do so, suppose

\begin{split}u(x)&=\mathcal{U}\bigg(\frac{x-p}{\varepsilon}\bigg),\\
u_{n}(x)&=\mathcal{U}\bigg(\frac{x-p_{n}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\end{split}

(27)

where
\mathcal{U}
is a smooth profile (cf.
u^{0}
in (
33
)). Taking a Taylor expansion,

u(x)-u_{n}(x)\approx\nabla\mathcal{U}\bigg(\frac{x-p_{n}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{p-p_{n}}{\varepsilon}\bigg),\qquad\bigg|\frac{p-p_{n}}{\varepsilon}\bigg|\ll 1.

(28)

Note that this approximation makes sense since we often take the time step size
\delta t
to be asymptotically smaller than the width of the diffuse layer
\varepsilon
. Then,

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-u_{n}(x))^{\top}\frac{J_{u_{n}}(x)}{A_{W}}\nabla u_{n}(x)\big(\nabla u_{n}(x)^{\top}\nabla u_{n}(x)+I_{2\times 2}\big)^{-2}\nabla u_{n}(x)^{\top}(u(x)-u_{n}(x))\;dx\\
&\approx\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(p_{n}-p)^{\top}\frac{J_{\mathcal{U}}}{A_{W}}(\nabla\mathcal{U})^{\top}(\nabla\mathcal{U})\bigg((\nabla\mathcal{U})^{\top}(\nabla\mathcal{U})+\varepsilon^{2}I_{2\times 2}\bigg)^{-2}(\nabla\mathcal{U})^{\top}(\nabla\mathcal{U})(p_{n}-p)\;dx.\end{split}

(29)

where all terms in the integrand involving
\mathcal{U}
are evaluated at
(x-p_{n})/\varepsilon
. Here, the (normalized) Jacobian determinant
J_{u_{n}}(x)/A_{W}
acts as an approximate delta function at the triple junction
p_{n}
while the identity matrix
I_{2\times 2}
acts as a regularization wherever
\nabla u_{n}
may not be rank 2 (i.e. away from the triple junction).

Making a change of variables
y=(x-p_{n})/\varepsilon
, the last integral is equal to

\begin{split}\int_{(\Omega-p_{n})/\varepsilon}(p_{n}-p)^{\top}&\frac{J_{\mathcal{U}}}{A_{W}}(\nabla\mathcal{U})^{\top}(\nabla\mathcal{U})\bigg((\nabla\mathcal{U})^{\top}(\nabla\mathcal{U})+\varepsilon^{2}I_{2\times 2}\bigg)^{-2}(\nabla\mathcal{U})^{\top}(\nabla\mathcal{U})(p_{n}-p)\;dy\\
&=|p-p_{n}|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon|p-p_{n}|^{2})\end{split}

(30)

where the last equality will be justified in the next section (Lemma
1
). This means that we can incorporate the integral on the first line of (
29
) as an approximation to the triple junction perturbation
|p-p_{n}|^{2}
into scheme (
26
), rewriting it as

\begin{split}u_{n+1}&\in\argmin_{u}\bigg\{E_{\varepsilon}(u)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\delta t}\int_{\Omega}|u(x)-u_{n}(x)|^{2}\;dx+\\
&\bigg(\frac{1}{m_{TJ}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{1}{2\delta t}\bigg)\int_{\Omega}(u(x)-u_{n}(x))^{\top}\frac{J_{u_{n}}(x)}{A_{W}}\nabla u_{n}(x)\big(\nabla u_{n}(x)^{\top}\nabla u_{n}(x)+I_{2\times 2}\big)^{-2}\nabla u_{n}(x)^{\top}(u(x)-u_{n}(x))\;dx\bigg\},\end{split}

(31)

whose formal limit as
\delta t\to 0
is (
13
).

7
Formal asymptotic analysis

The preceding section derives equation (
13
) from a minimizing-movement perspective, where we designed a proxy for the triple junction perturbation
|p-p_{n}|^{2}
. In this section, we further justify equation (
13
) via formal asymptotic analysis.

The analysis of the solution to (
13
) away from triple junctions (i.e. motion by curvature of the interfaces) follows those of
[
34
,
7
]
closely. Thus, we focus on the behavior near a triple junctions. Let
p(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the position of the triple junction at time
t
. Introducing the stretched variables

y=\dfrac{x-p(t)}{\varepsilon},

(32)

we assume an expansion

u(x,t)=u^{0}(y,t)+\varepsilon u^{1}(y,t)+\ldots.

(33)

Expanding the various terms in (
13
), we get

\begin{split}u_{t}(x,t)&=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{0}(y,t)\dot{p}(t)+u_{t}^{0}(y,t)+\ldots\\
\Delta u&-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla W(u)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bigg(\Delta u^{0}-\nabla W(u^{0})\bigg)+\ldots.\end{split}

(34)

Multiplying both sides of (
13
) by
(\nabla u)^{\top}M(\nabla u)^{-1}
, we get

(\nabla u)^{\top}M(\nabla u)^{-1}u_{t}=(\nabla u)^{\top}\bigg(\Delta u-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla W(u)\bigg).

(35)

Plugging in the expansion for
u(x,t)
into the
expression (
16
) for the inverse mobility
M(\nabla u)^{-1}
gives

M(\nabla u)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon m_{TJ}}\frac{J_{u^{0}}}{A_{W}}\nabla u^{0}\big((\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\nabla u^{0}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{2\times 2}\big)^{-2}(\nabla u^{0})^{\top}+I_{2\times 2}+\ldots.

(36)

The leading order term on the left-hand side of (
35
) is

\begin{split}\bigg(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\bigg)&\bigg(\frac{1}{\varepsilon m_{TJ}}\frac{J_{u^{0}}}{A_{W}}\nabla u^{0}\big((\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\nabla u^{0}+\varepsilon^{2}I_{2\times 2}\big)^{-2}(\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\bigg)\bigg(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla u^{0}(y,t)\dot{p}(t)\bigg)\\
=&\frac{-J_{u^{0}}}{\varepsilon^{3}m_{TJ}A_{W}}B(B+\varepsilon^{2}I_{2\times 2})^{-2}B\dot{p}(t)\end{split}

(37)

where
B\coloneqq(\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\nabla u^{0}
is a positive semidefinite matrix.

Lemma 1
.

J_{u^{0}}B(B+\varepsilon^{2}I_{2\times 2})^{-2}B=J_{u^{0}}I_{2\times 2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)

(38)

where the constant appearing in the error term depends only on the largest singular value of
\nabla u^{0}
.

Proof.

We first orthogonally diagonalize

B=Q\Lambda Q^{\top},\qquad\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\geq 0

(39)

and note that
J_{u^{0}}=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}
. Then,

J_{u^{0}}B(B+\varepsilon^{2}I_{2\times 2})^{-2}B=Q\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}(\varepsilon)&0\\
0&\mu_{2}(\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix}Q^{\top}

(40)

where

\mu_{i}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}}{(\lambda_{i}+\varepsilon^{2})^{2}},\qquad i=1,2.

(41)

Our goal is to show that

|\mu_{i}(\varepsilon)-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}|\leq C\varepsilon

(42)

for some constant
C
. WLOG, we can just consider the case when
i=1
.

\begin{split}|\mu_{i}(\varepsilon)-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}|&=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\bigg|1-\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{(\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon^{2})^{2}}\bigg|\\
&=\varepsilon\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}f(\varepsilon),\end{split}

(43)

where

f(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\bigg(\frac{2\lambda_{1}\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{3}}{(\lambda_{1}+\varepsilon^{2})^{2}}\bigg)\geq 0.

(44)

We claim that

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}f(\varepsilon)\leq 1.

(45)

Indeed, setting
z=\varepsilon/\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
, we get

f(\varepsilon)=\frac{z(2+z^{2})}{(1+z^{2})^{2}}\leq\frac{2z}{1+z^{2}}\leq 1.

(46)

Thus,

|\mu_{i}(\varepsilon)-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}|\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}\varepsilon\leq C\varepsilon

(47)

where

C\coloneqq\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}((\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\nabla u^{0})}.

(48)

∎

By (
37
), the leading order term on the LHS of (
35
) is

\frac{-J_{u^{0}}}{\varepsilon^{3}m_{TJ}A_{W}}\dot{p}(t)

(49)

while the the leading order term on the RHS of (
35
) is

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{3}}(\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\bigg(\Delta u^{0}-\nabla W(u^{0})\bigg).

(50)

Thus, the leading order profile
u^{0}
solves

(\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\bigg(\Delta u^{0}-\nabla W(u^{0})\bigg)=\frac{-J_{u^{0}}}{m_{TJ}A_{W}}\dot{p}(t),

(51)

subject to the same matching conditions as
[
7
]
equation (18) (see also
[
2
]
Definition 3.2). In words, as
|y|\to\infty
along each of the three directions tangent to an interface (i.e.
\tau_{i}
), the solution
u^{0}
converges to the 1-dimensional optimal profile (geodesic) connecting two adjacent wells of
W
.

Figure 12:
The domain of integration
T_{R}
in (
52
), same as the one used in
[
7
]
for standard Allen-Cahn.

Following
[
7
]
, we integrate (
51
) over a large triangular domain
T_{R}
with base length
R
(Figure
12
):

-\frac{1}{m_{TJ}}\frac{1}{A_{W}}\bigg(\int_{T_{R}}J_{u^{0}}(y)\;dy\bigg)\dot{p}(t)=\int_{T_{R}}(\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\big(\Delta u^{0}-\nabla W(u^{0})\big)\;dy.

(52)

The integrand on the RHS is the divergence of a tensor
T
defined by

T_{ij}=u^{0}_{y_{i}}\cdot u^{0}_{y_{j}}-\delta_{ij}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u^{0}|^{2}+W(u^{0})\bigg),\qquad i,j\in\{1,2\}.

(53)

It was shown in
[
7
]
(see also
[
2
]
Chapter 3) that

\begin{split}\int_{T_{R}}(\nabla u^{0})^{\top}\big(\Delta u^{0}-\nabla W(u^{0})\big)\;dy&=\int_{T_{R}}\nabla\cdot T\;dy\\
&=\int_{\partial T_{R}}T\nu\;dS\\
&\to-(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3}),\qquad\text{ as }R\to\infty.\end{split}

(54)

Recalling the change-of-variables formula from the previous section and the matching condition satisfied by
u^{0}
at infinity, we have

\int_{T_{R}}J_{u^{0}}(y)\;dy\to A_{W},\text{ as }R\to\infty.

(55)

Thus, as
R\to\infty
, we deduce from (
52
) the triple junction drag condition

\dot{p}(t)=m_{TJ}(\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\tau_{3}).

(56)

Remark 2
.

With an eye toward efficiency of numerical implementation and scalability, we have intentionally written our mobility factor
M(\nabla u)
in the form of (
14
) to emphasize that we only need to invert a
2\times 2
matrix. This facilitates the generalization of our model to the case where there may be
N\geq 4
phases and the order parameter
u(x,t)
takes values in
R^{N-1}
(see Section
9
). However, if we are only concerned with the case where there are
N=3
phases and
\nabla u\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, then the mobility factor
M(\nabla u)
in (
14
) can be substituted for a simplified version, namely

M(\nabla u)=\bigg(I_{2\times 2}+\dfrac{J_{u}}{A_{W}\varepsilon m_{TJ}}\big(\nabla u\nabla u^{\top}+I_{2\times 2}\big)^{-1}\bigg)^{-1},

(57)

which yields the same leading order equation (
51
).

8
Numerical simulations

We test convergence of our phase-field method (
13
) & (
14
) to the sharp interface description (
1
) & (
2
) under refinement of discretization size and the diffuse interface thickness
\varepsilon
. Periodic boundary conditions are used on the computational domain that is a square, which is discretized into
n=256,512,1024
and
2048
grid points along each dimension.
The following finite differences discretization of equation (
13
) was used on a uniform grid:

\frac{u_{n+1}-u_{n}}{\delta t}=M(\nabla_{\delta x}u_{n})\bigg(\Delta_{\delta x}u_{n}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla W(u_{n})\bigg).

(58)

where
\Delta_{\delta x}
denotes the standard five point discretization of the Laplacian on a uniform grid, and
\nabla_{\delta x}
uses centered differences for first derivatives.
The discretization of (
58
) was kept fully explicit and as simple as possible, as our focus here is on verifying convergence of (
13
) & (
14
) rather than finding the most efficient implementation.

In practice, we observe that with the typical choice
\varepsilon=6\delta x
for the phase field parameter
\varepsilon
, the scheme (
58
) is stable under the restriction

\delta t\lesssim(\delta x)^{2}.

(59)

This is essentially the same CFL condition as that of explicit Euler scheme for the standard (constant mobility) Allen-Cahn equation.
The benign effect of the nontrivial mobility factor
M(\nabla u)
on stability properties of scheme (
58
) can be easily explained rigorously for a potential
W
that has bounded Hessian (e.g. obtained by modifying the natural choice (
10
) to have quadratic growth at
\infty
).

Indeed, as the second term in (
16
) is positive, we see that the symmetric positive definite inverse mobility matrix
M(\nabla_{\delta x}u_{n})^{-1}
has minimum eigenvalue bounded below by one.
We’ll show energy stability of (
58
), with the Lyapunov function

E_{\delta x,\varepsilon}(u)=\sum\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon|\nabla_{\delta x}u|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(u)

(60)

where the summation is over all grid points;
this is a natural discrete version of Modica-Mortola energy (
18
).
To that end, first note that scheme (
58
) has the variational formulation

\begin{split}u_{n+1}=\argmin_{u}\Big\{G_{n}(u):=E_{\delta x,\varepsilon}(u_{n})+&\sum-\varepsilon(u-u_{n})\Delta_{\delta x}u_{n}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla W(u_{n})\cdot(u-u_{n})\\
+&\sum\frac{\varepsilon}{2\delta t}\langle M(\nabla_{\delta x}u_{n})^{-1}(u-u_{n})\,,\,(u-u_{n})\rangle\Big\}\end{split}

(61)

Observe that the first three terms in
G_{n}(u)
of (
61
) are the linearization of (
60
) at
u=u_{n}
, and the last term is a strongly convex proximal (movement limiting) term.
Hence, first of all,

G_{n}(u_{n})=E_{\delta x,\varepsilon}(u_{n}).

Moreover, since by assumption
\sup_{\xi}\|D^{2}W(\xi)\|
is bounded, there is a constant
C>0
so that whenever
\delta t<C\min\{\delta x^{2}\,,\,\varepsilon^{2}\}
we have

E_{\delta x,\varepsilon}(u)\leq G_{n}(u)\mbox{ for all }u.

That means

E_{\delta x,\varepsilon}(u_{n+1})\leq G_{n}(u_{n+1})\leq G_{n}(u_{n})=E_{\delta x,\varepsilon}(u_{n})

establishing energy stability under the advertised CFL condition.

9
Generalization to
N\geq 4
phases

Our PDE (
13
) & (
14
) extends to
N\geq 4
phases naturally.
In this case, as in
[
4
]
, the order parameter
u(x,t)
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
and the potential
W:\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\to\mathbb{R}
has
N
wells located at vertices of a regular
(N-1)-
simplex.
The mobility factor (
14
) – the novelty of our method – becomes

M(\nabla u)\coloneqq I_{(N-1)\times(N-1)}-\frac{1}{A_{W}}J_{u}\nabla u\bigg[\varepsilon m_{TJ}\bigg(\nabla u^{\top}\nabla u+I_{2\times 2}\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{A_{W}}J_{u}\nabla u^{\top}\nabla u\bigg]^{-1}\nabla u^{\top}.

(67)

We give an example with
N=4
phases:

\begin{split}W(x)&=C_{\beta}|x-\beta_{1}|^{2}||x-\beta_{2}|^{2}|x-\beta_{3}|^{2}|x-\beta_{4}|^{2}\\
\beta_{1}&=\bigg(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}},0,-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\bigg)\\
\beta_{2}&=\bigg(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},1,-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\bigg)\\
\beta_{3}&=\bigg(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}},-1,-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\bigg)\\
\beta_{4}&=\bigg(0,0,\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\bigg)\\
C_{\beta}&\approx 0.0322.\end{split}

(68)

As before, the constant
C_{\beta}
was computed numerically to ensure that the surface tensions induced by
W
according to (
11
) are all equal to one.

A key question in the
N\geq 4
case is the validity of identity (
20
), since (in general) there could be infinitely many 2-dimensional curved surfaces that share the same boundary (geodesics between any three wells of
W
).
It is conceivable that this surface, namely
u(B_{\varepsilon},t)
, changes with the profile of the order parameter
u(\cdot,t)
in the vicinity
B_{\varepsilon}
of a junction the angles of which evolves in time.
This raises the question of whether the quantity
\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}J_{u}(x)\;dx
remains a constant depending only on
W
, and if so, what that constant is.

Our numerical simulations clearly indicate that the quantity
\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}J_{u}(x)\;dx
is indeed very nearly constant during the evolution by (
13
) & (
67
).
Moreover, we find that the image
u(B_{\varepsilon},t)
of
B_{\varepsilon}
(a small neighborhood containing a triple junction where phases
i,j,k
meet) under
u(\cdot,t)
very closely matches the surface
\mathcal{S}_{ijk}:=u_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, where
u_{*}
solves the equilibrium problem

\Delta u_{*}(x)=\nabla W(u_{*}(x)),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(69)

subject to far field conditions given by equations 16-18 of
[
7
]
(with wells
\beta_{i},\beta_{j},\beta_{k}
), i.e.

u(B_{\varepsilon},t)\approx u_{*}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\mathcal{S}_{ijk}

(70)

regardless of the local geometry of the junction; see Figures
26
and
27
.

phases, with

).

The natural extension of
A_{W}
to the setting of
N\geq 4
phases is then

A_{W}\coloneqq\text{Area}(\mathcal{S}_{ijk})\approx 1.026

(71)

which does not depend on the choice of
i,j,k\in\{1,2,3,4\}
due to the symmetry of
W
with respect to its four wells.

At this stage, we only provide numerical evidence that supports (
70
), leaving an analysis of its validity to future work.

10
Conclusion

We have proposed a new phase-field method for curvature motion of networks with triple junction drag, supported by both formal asymptotic analysis and strong numerical evidence of convergence to the correct sharp-interface limit.
Along the way, we obtained a new formula that expresses the number of triple junctions as an integral of the order parameter that applies to a variety of vectorial Allen-Cahn systems. Future work will include extension to three dimensions, and more efficient numerical methods such as semi-implicit schemes for (
58
) with low per time step computational cost.
