Improving quality of life in older dialysis patients: the case for broader functional assessment
Authors: Timothy Moreels, Dominique Van de Velde, Eline Van Samang, Karsten Vanden Wyngaert, Valerie Neirynck, Ingrid Vaerendonck, Elien Mahieu, Sigrid De Smet-Van Damme, Sunny Eloot, Tamara Leune, Wim Van Biesen, Patricia De Vriendt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe incidence of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is rising, particularly among adults aged 65 and older, who account for over half of new cases in Europe. While older adults consistently prioritize quality of life (QoL), it is rarely a primary endpoint in clinical trials and remains underemphasized in clinical decision making. Health-related QoL (HRQoL) captures only part of the broader QoL concept, which may contribute to inconsistencies across studies. We examined determinants of overall QoL and its relationship to HRQoL using comprehensive assessments.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of adults ≥65 receiving in-center hemodialysis at four hospitals in Flanders, Belgium. Data included sociodemographic and treatment-specific information, a comprehensive geriatric assessment, Groningen Frailty Indicator, Brussels Integrated Activities of Daily Living Inventory, and HRQoL measures [EuroQol 5-Dimension 3-Level Questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L), Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 (QLQ-C30), and Quality of Life Questionnaire-Myeloma Module]. Overall QoL was assessed with the Anamnestic Comparative Self-Assessment (ACSA). We conducted descriptive, univariate, multivariate, and cluster analyses.ResultsWe included 111 patients (64.9% male; mean age 78.1 ± 7.4 years; median dialysis vintage 36.0 months [interquartile range 11.0–65.8]). Overall QoL was moderately positive (ACSA mean 1.97 ± 2.05; range −5 to 5). The EQ-5D utility index was not correlated with the ACSA (ρ = −0.052, P = .591). Multivariate analysis identified “cognitive functioning” (QLQ-C30), “usual activities” (EQ-5D-3L), hospital admissions, “subjective health status” (EQ-5D-3L), and “social functioning” (QLQ-C30) as independent predictors, explaining 31% of the variance in the ACSA. Cluster analysis identified three subgroups: a relatively resilient subgroup (n = 59), a functioning-impaired subgroup (n = 36), and a psychologically frail subgroup (n = 12).ConclusionsFocusing on cognition, usual activities, and social functioning may improve outcome relevance, identify vulnerable subgroups, and support more effective resource allocation for older adults with ESKD.Keywords: cognition, frailty, hemodialysis, older adults, quality of life

What was known:

Quality of life (QoL) is a key priority for older adults with end-stage kidney disease receiving hemodialysis. However, it remains underemphasized in clinical decision making and research, which has largely focused on medical outcomes or on health-related QoL (HRQoL), not fully capturing patient-relevant outcomes.

This study adds:

“Cognitive functioning,” “usual activities,” and “social functioning” were most strongly associated with overall QoL, and may represent potential targets for intervention. The EuroQol 5-Dimension utility index, often used to guide policy and funding, was not correlated with overall QoL. Many patients reported impairments overlooked by standard assessments, showing the need for more comprehensive assessment.

Potential impact:

Incorporating broader, patient-centered QoL measures and targeting cognitive, functional, and social domains can improve care planning and resource allocation for older adults on hemodialysis. HRQoL indices have limited policy and funding value if they fail to reflect patient-meaningful outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Adults aged 65 and older are the fastest-growing age group with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), comprising 52% of all incident cases in Europe [1]. While life-saving, maintenance hemodialysis imposes a significant treatment burden that, when combined with the already substantial symptom burden of ESKD, can place considerable strain on patients’ overall well-being [2]. Compared to younger populations, older adults additionally face elevated risks of frailty, diminished psychosocial well-being, and multimorbidity, alongside the general challenges of adhering to strict treatment regimens [2, 3].

Older adults with ESKD consistently prioritize quality of life (QoL) over other outcomes [4, 5]. Research indicates their willingness to forgo life expectancy, in some cases by as much as 9 years, to reduce the burdens and restrictions associated with dialysis [6, 7]. Despite QoL being a primary concern for patients, it has long remained an underrecognized outcome, with only 9% of randomized trials in hemodialysis patients including it as an outcome measure and a mere 2% considering it as a primary outcome [8]. Although recent decades have brought advancements in screening, diagnostic, and treatment strategies, these innovations have not resulted in significant improvements in QoL for hemodialysis patients [2, 8, 9]. In response, there have been increasing calls for a deeper investigation of QoL and its underlying factors, with the aim of guiding the development of effective interventions [2, 10].

The World Health Organization defines QoL as an individual’s perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns [11]. In healthcare, QoL is typically evaluated by focusing on specific subdomains that can be directly influenced by medical interventions, referred to as health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [12]. In nephrology research, evidence on HRQoL suggests it may better predict mortality and hospitalization than traditional clinical markers, such as serum albumin levels [13]. Evidence from the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS) shows that lower HRQoL scores independently predict mortality and first hospitalization, even after adjustment for demographic factors, comorbidities, and treatment-related factors. In some cases, HRQoL may also provide better prognostic information than serum albumin levels; a 10-point lower physical component score was associated with larger increases in the adjusted risks of death and hospitalization than a 1 g/dl lower serum albumin level [13]. However, it is unclear to what extent HRQoL captures the full complexity of overall QoL, and whether existing instruments include domains that truly reflect what matters most to patients [14–18]. HRQoL instruments also often face challenges in capturing changes over time, particularly in patients undergoing major life transitions, such as initiating hemodialysis [14–17]. These transitions may result in a shift in patients’ frame of reference, from comparisons with their own prior health state to those of peers in similar circumstances [15, 16].

As different HRQoL instruments emphasize different domains, a patient’s HRQoL score can vary significantly depending on the tool used [19]. This variability can become particularly problematic when an instrument is used to calculate quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), typically used to guide policy or funding decisions [19, 20]. Because QALYs are derived from population-based preference-weighted utility scores that may not align with patients’ own assessments of QoL, they risk overlooking outcomes that matter most to patients. For adults receiving hemodialysis for example, if the utility score does not sufficiently reflect life participation or fatigue-related outcomes, the score may fail to represent what QoL truly means to them [8, 19–21]. This mismatch can lead to misallocated funding, inefficient resource use, and inaccurate assessments of effectiveness in clinical trials [8, 19, 20].

To date, research has largely treated HRQoL as a patient-centered outcome because it is self-reported [8, 22, 23]. However, neither self-report nor population-based preference weights guarantee alignment with patients’ priorities, as generic HRQoL instruments may fail to capture the broader complexity of their overall QoL [24]. True patient-centered outcomes are those “explicitly prioritized by patients as important and relevant for their wellbeing” [25]. To design and evaluate interventions that effectively support older adults on hemodialysis, this study aimed to identify key factors influencing their overall QoL, and examine its relationship with HRQoL. Toward this aim, we explored a comprehensive range of measures, including sociodemographic, dialysis-specific, biopsychosocial, and activity-related variables, along with three HRQoL scales [18, 26, 27].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design

We conducted a cross-sectional observational study, reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [28]. ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT07129083.

Setting, participants, and sampling method

We recruited a sample of adults aged 65 and older receiving in-center hemodialysis (ICHD) from four hospitals in Flanders, Belgium, between January and April 2018. The hospitals were selected to represent regional, rural, urban, and academic settings. Patients were excluded if they were undergoing ICHD due to acute hospital admission, had a formal diagnosis of psychiatric or severe cognitive disabilities (e.g. dementia) as documented in medical records, or were not able to understand and adequately respond to questions in Dutch.

Outcome measures

We employed a diverse set of outcome measures to capture a comprehensive range of variables (n = 95), guided by the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health. All measures had validated Dutch translations available, and each has been previously used in Belgian research settings. An overview of the measures is presented in Fig. 1, with further details provided in Supplemental File 1.

Overview of measurements used in the study. STRATIFY = St. Thomas’s Risk Assessment Tool in Falling Elderly Inpatients; GDS-4 = Geriatric Depression Scale–4-Item version; NRS-2002 = Nutrional Risk Screening 2002; BIA = Brussels Integrated Activities of Daily Living Inventory[36].

The assessment set was administered face-to-face over one to three consecutive ICHD sessions, depending on the participant’s condition, and took ∼45 minutes in total to complete. To reduce the risk of socially desirable responses, the questionnaires were administered by independent researchers with no affiliation to the hospital, unit, or study participants.

Dependent variable

The Anamnestic Comparative Self-Assessment (ACSA) is a self-anchoring QoL rating scale, originally developed for use with cancer patients. It has been used in various QoL studies, including older adults, and has demonstrated greater reliability and sensitivity than other overall QoL scales [16, 29, 30]. The ACSA assesses overall QoL through a structured interview in which participants identify the most positive (+5) and most negative (−5) periods of their lives. Using this personalized reference frame, they then rate their QoL over the past 2 weeks on the same −5 to +5 scale.

The ACSA has shown strong known-groups validity, with extremely low scores among critically ill pretransplant patients, as well as responsiveness, with larger post-transplant improvements than those captured by standard single-item QoL measures [16, 30–33]. Moreover, ACSA ratings appear to be less influenced by stable sociodemographic traits than conventional instruments, as demonstrated in large hospital- and nursing-home based studies [16, 29–33]. As a self-anchoring global measure, without fixed anchors beyond personal biographical reference points, the ACSA does not have a universally established minimal clinically important difference (MCID). Interpretation focuses on individual-level meaning, and is grounded in a patient’s subjective experience and personal context [32].

Independent variables

Independent variables included sociodemographic and treatment-related data extracted from patient medical records; results from a comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA), a multidimensional evaluation of an older adult’s medical, functional, psychological, and social status [34]; a multidimensional frailty measure [35]; a multidomain assessment of basic, instrumental, and advanced activities of daily living (ADLs) [36]; and three HRQoL scales: the EuroQol 5-Dimension 3-Level questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L), the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30), and the EORTC Quality of Life Questionnaire-Myeloma Module 20 (EORTC MY-20) [18, 26, 27]. The measures used to assess HRQoL are described below; detailed descriptions of all measures are provided in Supplementary File 1.

EQ-5D-3L

The EQ-5D-3L is a validated and widely adopted generic HRQoL instrument, consisting of five dimensions (“mobility,” “self-care,” “usual activities,” “pain/discomfort,” and “anxiety/depression”), each with three severity levels (“no problems,” “some problems,” and “extreme problems”). Results from these five dimensions lead to a utility index, which ranges from 0 (worst possible health) to 1 (best possible health). In addition, respondents are asked to score their health on a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), which ranges from 0 to 100, corresponding to the worst to the best imaginable health state. The EQ-5D-3L health states were converted to utility index values using the Belgian EQ-5D-3L value set, based on social preferences elicited from the general Flemish public in Belgium [37]. The EQ-5D-3L has shown solid construct validity and satisfactory internal consistency, and has demonstrated short-interval test–retest reliability in people with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [38]. For frail older adults or those on dialysis, an MCID may start from as little as ∼0.03 on the utility score [39–41].

EORTC QLQ-C30

The QLQ-C30 is a comprehensive and widely validated HRQoL questionnaire, designed to assess multiple dimensions of QoL across 15 domains. It includes functional scales (physical, role, emotional, cognitive, and social functioning) and symptom scales (fatigue, nausea/vomiting, and pain), as well as single-item measures (dyspnea, insomnia, appetite loss, constipation, diarrhea, and financial difficulties). Higher scores indicate better functioning on functional and global health scales, and more severe problems on symptom scales. In addition, the QLQ-C30 also incorporates a global health status scale. Although originally oncology-focused, it has been applied successfully in dialysis cohorts to profile functioning and symptom burden [42]. MCID change varies by subscale and direction (i.e. improvement or deterioration), typically ranging from ∼2 to 7 points for most functioning and symptom domains, and up to around 14 points for pain [43].

EORTC QLQ-MY20

The EORTC Quality of Life Questionnaire-Myeloma Module (QLQ-MY20; hereafter MY-20) is a disease-specific HRQoL module originally designed to supplement the QLQ-C30 by capturing additional aspects of QoL. It comprises four domains: future perspective, disease symptoms, side effects of treatment, and body image. Higher scores indicate better outcomes on the functional scale (future perspective) and greater symptom burden or problems on the symptom scales. The MY-20 has demonstrated strong psychometric properties and cross-cultural reproducibility in patients with myeloma-related complications such as kidney failure, including those receiving dialysis [44]. For the MY-20 MCID differences range between 9 and 13, depending on the domain and direction [45].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.2.0). Pairwise deletion was used for missing item-level data, excluding only analyses involving the missing variable while retaining other available data. Analyses proceeded in four stages. First, descriptive statistics were conducted and normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Second, nonparametric univariate tests assessed associations with the ACSA score: dichotomous variables with the Mann–Whitney U test, variables with three or more nominal categories with the Kruskal–Wallis H test, and ordinal or continuous variables with Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ). Third, variables significant at P < .05 in the univariate analyses were entered into stepwise multiple linear regression to determine independent predictors of the ACSA score. Assumptions of linear regression (normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity) were checked prior to model interpretation. Finally, cluster analysis was used to explore natural groupings within the data. We first performed hierarchical clustering using Ward’s method and Euclidean distance on ACSA scores and the independent predictors identified in the univariate analyses. Hierarchical clustering using Ward’s method is a statistical technique that groups participants based on similarity across selected variables, while minimizing differences within each cluster [46–48]. This method produces a visual representation (a dendrogram), which is visually inspected to determine the optimal number of clusters (Supplemental File 6). Following this, k-means clustering was used to assign participants to the final clusters. The resulting clusters were subsequently characterized by comparing all variables across groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Welch’s ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis tests, or chi-square tests, as appropriate for variable type and distribution. A comprehensive summary of all statistical analyses and results is provided in Supplementary File 6.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Ghent University Hospital (B670201837264) and was carried out in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The participants received verbal and written information about the purpose and methods of the study and provided written and verbal informed consent.

Associations with HRQoL

Using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients, overall QoL (ACSA) showed a small-to-moderate correlation with the health status VAS measures of the EQ-5D (ρ = 0.288, P = .002) and the QLQ-C30 (ρ = 0.233, P = .015) (Table 3). Cluster 2 (n = 36), a functioning-impaired group, showed reduced QoL, modestly higher hospital admissions, higher levels of depression and anxiety, greater impact on usual activities, poorer overall functioning, higher frailty, and lower self-rated health than Cluster 1 (Table 6). Additionally, Cluster 2 was characterized by increased rates of falls and incontinence and the highest dependency in basic and instrumental ADLs, the most pronounced limitations in role functioning, and the highest levels of fatigue compared to the other clusters. Nearly all participants in Cluster 2 were identified as frail (Table 7).

Cluster 3 (n = 12), a psychologically frail group, included participants who were all at risk for depression. This cluster demonstrated the overall lowest QoL, highest number of hospital admissions, most severe depression and anxiety, poorest social, and particularly emotional, functioning, the highest frailty scores, and lowest self-rated health status (Table 6). Cluster 3 also had the highest proportion of female participants, more frequent falls than Cluster 1 (though similar to Cluster 2), and the greatest dependency in advanced ADLs (e.g. social activities). As with Cluster 2, nearly all participants in Cluster 3 were identified as frail using a multidimensional frailty assessment (Table 7). The k-means bar chart is shown in Fig. 3. DISCUSSION

With this study, we aimed to contribute to the development of interventions that truly improve relevant aspects of QoL for older adults with ESKD undergoing ICHD. Specifically, we sought to identify key factors influencing QoL across a broad range of independent variables, including sociodemographic, dialysis-specific, biopsychosocial, and activity-related measures, as well as multidimensional HRQoL measures. We also examined the relationship between overall QoL as measured by the ACSA and HRQoL as assessed by the EQ-5D-3L and EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-MY30 instruments.

Overall, the participants reported a moderately positive ACSA score, comparable to that of older adults without ESKD residing in nursing homes [30]. One possible explanation for the similar score is that people receiving hemodialysis may adapt to their chronic health limitations and, as a result, place greater weight on nonhealth domains (e.g. social or cognitive functioning, resilience, and the impact on family) when evaluating their overall QoL [4, 21]. HRQoL, as measured by the EQ-5D utility index, was consistent with previous findings in ESKD and within the range reported for other chronic conditions, though still significantly lower than in the general population [49]. The multivariable model explained ∼31% of the variance in ACSA scores, indicating that a significant portion remains unaccounted for despite the inclusion of a broad range of independent variables. This may reflect the inherently subjective nature of QoL, as well as the potential influence of variables beyond those included. Nevertheless, it showed specific domains that may represent potential targets for intervention or supportive care: “cognitive functioning,” “usual activities,” and “social functioning.” Although hospital admissions and “health status” were also included in the model, it seems reasonable to accept these are consequences rather than causes and may therefore be less amenable to direct intervention.

The first potential intervention target, “cognitive functioning,” was measured by the QLQ-C30 using two questions referring to the past week: one on concentration (“Have you had difficulty concentrating on things, like reading a newspaper or watching television?”) and one on memory (“Have you had difficulty remembering things?”). Cognitive functioning has been consistently highlighted as particularly critical for older adults with ESKD undergoing hemodialysis [50]. Cognitive decline tends to accelerate with hemodialysis, with patients facing an approximately three-fold higher risk of impairment compared with age-matched controls [51]. Only ∼13% of patients on hemodialysis are estimated to retain normal cognition, and many already exhibit deficits at treatment initiation, showing the potential value of early support [51, 52]. Early impairments typically involve executive function, language, and attention, with memory decline emerging later [53]. Cognitive impairment is associated with poor medication adherence, increased hospitalization rates, higher mortality, and reduced ability to follow dialysis schedules and adhere to fluid and dietary restrictions [54, 55]. Cognitive training and exercise may help reduce cognitive decline by supporting executive function, verbal reasoning, abstraction, and inhibition, with benefits potentially extending to the performance of usual activities and persisting for up to 10 years [56–58].

The second potential intervention target was the ability to perform “usual activities,” assessed using the EQ-5D item: “Do you have problems with your usual activities (e.g. work, study, housework, family, or leisure activities)?”. In recent years, life participation, or the ability to engage in meaningful daily activities, has been recognized as a critical outcome in the ESKD population [10]. Declines in everyday activity performance often begin around dialysis initiation and may become permanent if not addressed early [59]. These declines typically start with higher-order, participation-level activities (e.g. attending art exhibitions, participating in hobby groups) that require coordination, planning, energy, and both cognitive and physical capacity [36]. Because such activities are rarely captured in standard assessments, early deterioration often goes unnoticed, yet it frequently signals a broader decline that eventually affects basic (e.g. self-care) and instrumental (e.g. cooking) ADLs [59, 60]. Exercise training and tailored physical rehabilitation, delivered during dialysis or at home, have been associated with improvements in physical performance, strength, and walking capacity [61]. Occupational therapy and self-management support can also play a critical role in helping patients maintain routines, adapt their activities, and use assistive devices effectively [62]. Additionally, psychosocial interventions incorporating goal-setting, motivational interviewing, and social support can help address cognitive and emotional barriers to life participation [62]. To maximize effectiveness, practical problem-solving and activity adaptation should be prioritized over managing emotional responses to decline [63].

The third potential intervention target included in the model was “social functioning,” assessed using two QLQ-C30 items referring to the past week: “Has your physical condition or medical treatment interfered with your family life?” and “Has your physical condition or medical treatment interfered with your social activities?”. Strong social support is associated with better kidney disease outcomes, including reduced CKD progression risk, even after adjusting for competing risks (e.g. death) and confounders such as financial stress, education, and smoking [64]. Conversely, determinants of social isolation, such as being unmarried, living alone, or having low participation in social activities, may independently predict faster renal decline [65]. A systematic review found that both group-based and individual interventions improve at least one social or psychosocial outcome in older adults [66]. Effective strategies include home-visiting programs, structured social and physical activities, self-management workshops, counseling, and facilitated group discussions [66, 67]. Notably, those most likely to benefit from social support (e.g. patients with cognitive impairment, low QoL, or frailty) are often least likely to have access to it, showing the need for early identification and comprehensive assessment [67].

While the interventions focusing on the potential intervention targets identified in this study show promise in improving those specific aspects of QoL, it should not be assumed that they will directly lead to measurable improvements in overall QoL for all patients. Moreover, the inherently subjective and individual nature of QoL can make it difficult to assess using domain-specific measures alone. Longitudinal research incorporating comprehensive and subjective QoL assessments will be necessary to determine the extent to which improvements in specific domains translate into better overall QoL over time.

Results from the cluster analysis showed the importance of comprehensive and sufficiently sensitive assessment. Even among a predominantly frail sample, three distinct subgroups emerged: a relatively resilient group (55%), a group with significant disruptions in their daily functioning (34%), and a smaller group characterized by significant psychosocial disruptions and the lowest overall QoL (11%). Although direct comparison with previous studies is limited by differences in clustering variables, and demographic and treatment-related factors, the observed proportions are broadly compatible with the heterogeneity reported in other dialysis cohorts. Other person-centered studies have also identified lower-burden or resilient profiles alongside more vulnerable subgroups with physical-functional, psychological, or combined psychosocial difficulties, including a recent latent class analysis of 354 patients receiving maintenance dialysis that identified low symptom burden (37.8%), moderate physical-low psychological symptom burden (29.7%), and moderate physical-high psychological symptom burden (32.5%) groups, with the latter having the poorest QoL outcomes [68]. Similarly, a recent psychosocial profile analysis of 221 HD patients identified a resilient group (37.6%) and two higher-risk groups, characterized mainly by loneliness or broader psychosocial difficulties [69]. The size of our functionally impaired cluster is also consistent with the high burden of functional limitation reported in HD populations. One recent cohort reported ADL disability in 43.5% of patients, while in the international DOPPS cohort of 7226 HD patients, only 36% could perform all 13 assessed basic and instrumental ADLs without assistance, and 14% had high functional dependence [70, 71]. Finally, the presence of a smaller but clinically distinct psychologically frail subgroup is also consistent with evidence that psychological difficulties are common in dialysis, although prevalence estimates can vary by assessment method, with depression affecting ∼23% of dialysis patients when assessed by clinical interview and ∼39% when assessed using symptom rating scales [72]. However, our psychologically frail cluster also represents a more specific high-risk subgroup characterized by combined low QoL, depression risk, anxiety, poorer emotional, cognitive, and social functioning, higher hospitalization burden, and multidimensional frailty. Taken together, these results show that standard assessments may need to more explicitly capture daily functioning and psychosocial factors to ensure sufficient sensitivity to identify patients who may appear relatively well, yet are already experiencing significant disruptions in their daily life [36, 60]. Without timely identification and support, these difficulties may worsen over time, potentially leading to cascading negative outcomes, including premature nursing home placement or death [73].

Previous research has shown that among older adults initiating hemodialysis, HRQoL typically declines in the year preceding initiation and then appears to stabilize thereafter [17]. However, this apparent stabilization may mask substantial deterioration in functional status. Dialysis initiation often significantly affects function. Among nursing home residents, only 39% maintained their functional status after 3 months; by 12 months, 58% had died and just 13% retained their predialysis function [59]. Similar trends are seen in community-dwelling older adults, with nearly half experiencing functional decline or death within 6 months, with the highest risk among older and more frail patients [59, 74]

In our study, the EQ-5D utility index was not correlated with overall QoL, raising concerns about the capacity of generic HRQoL indices to adequately capture outcomes most relevant to older adults with ESKD. Utility indices, such as those from the EQ-5D, are derived from population-level valuations of predefined health states. As such, they may not fully reflect someone’s individual experience and can represent an incomplete proxy for overall QoL, particularly for people with a chronic condition who may adapt to certain health limitations but experience significant debilitations in other domains not captured by these indices [15]. In recent years, there have been increasing calls to add a cognitive domain to the EQ-5D for example, with a number of authors developing “bolt-on” modules to make the EQ-5D more comprehensive [75]. Debates in health economics have also focused more and more on finding the appropriate informational basis for evaluating “value.” Traditionally, this has involved the use of QALYs, where the value of a life year is weighted by a utility score. However, authors such as Amartya Sen have argued that utility is an overly narrow concept for such assessments, and that a person’s capabilities and actual functioning should be equally considered [76]. One key concern is that people often adapt to adverse conditions, leading them to report higher levels of subjective wellbeing than their objective circumstances might warrant [76]. In our study, we found that for older adults with ESKD, relying solely on generic utility indices may underestimate outcomes that they consider meaningful. While HRQoL instruments are valuable for monitoring health status and treatment effects, broader, more sensitive, and appropriately weighted measures may be needed to ensure that clinical trials, policy development, and resource allocation reflect the aspects of QoL that matter most to patients [21]. Recently, the Standardized Outcomes in Nephrology initiative, through a systematic process involving over 9000 patients, caregivers, and professionals, identified the outcomes most important to people with ESKD, already leading to the development of two patient-centered instruments to measure the core outcomes of fatigue and life participation [77, 78].

Clinically, truly addressing what matters to patients will require a shift from disease-centered care to person-centered care, which emphasizes individual preferences, values, and needs within the context of patients’ lives, and focuses on collaboratively defined goals, rather than relying solely on predefined clinical targets [79]. For example, while pain is often included in HRQoL assessments and reducing it can provide relief, this alone may not restore a person’s ability to (re-)engage in their meaningful activities, such as picking up their grandchildren from school or caring for a loved one. In recent years, the concept of goal-oriented care has gained momentum as a way to align treatment plans with what patients value most [80]. It emphasizes understanding “what matters to the patient,” rather than “what is the matter with the patient,” and uses shared decision-making to work toward mutually agreed-upon life goals [80]. Tools such as the ZENN-tool, developed specifically for people with ESKD and centered around meaningful patient-reported life domains, could play a valuable role in supporting these conversations [81]. To more systematically identify patients at risk due to functional or psychosocial difficulties, the systematic administration of a comprehensive nephro-geriatric assessment could also be highly valuable [82].

This study has several limitations. First, as it was conducted in Belgium, the findings may not be generalizable, although we included hospitals with varying profiles and a diverse participant sample. While the exclusion of people with severe cognitive impairment may be a limitation, given the strong association between cognitive impairment and QoL, only those patients were excluded that could not complete the questionnaires due to their condition. As demonstrated by the Mini-Cog scores, 32.4% of the participants in our study were at risk for dementia, indicating that these patients were at least partly represented in our sample. Additionally, this study was conducted within a publicly funded healthcare system in which dialysis is provided at no direct cost to patients, though they often have indirect costs that are not fully reimbursed (e.g. transportation). As QoL and utility assessment is highly sensitive to contextual factors, such as financial burden and access to care, our findings may not be directly transferable to jurisdictions where patients bear substantial out-of-pocket costs for dialysis. Second, the observational and cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Third, although we used a broad range of validated tools, the choice of measures may have influenced the results, as different instruments can yield varying outcomes. A large number of variables relied on self-reported measures, which are inherently influenced by individual expectations, adaptation, mood, and contextual factors [83]. In general, these measures can be subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and response shift, particularly in populations with chronic illness [83]. However, conversely, more objective or performance-based assessments, while less vulnerable to this subjective bias, may fail to capture the personal meaning or perceived impact of impairments on patients’ daily life [21]. By combining both types of measures, and including a dependent variable that is less sensitive to response shift, we aimed to account for this complexity in measuring QoL-related constructs [21, 84, 85]. Fourth, the assessment of a large number of variables may have increased the risk of response fatigue; however, we sought to mitigate this by conducting interviews across multiple sessions. While we used listwise deletion to handle missing values, the overall proportion of missing data was very low across variables, making bias unlikely. Future research should use longitudinal designs to validate and extend these findings.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we identified “cognitive functioning,” performance in “usual activities,” and “social functioning” as potential intervention targets most strongly associated with overall QoL in older adults with ESKD undergoing hemodialysis. Additionally, cluster analysis revealed a sizeable subgroup with markedly impaired functioning despite reporting only modest symptoms in other domains, showing the need for more comprehensive and sensitive assessments to ensure evaluations accurately reflect patients’ lived experiences. The absence of a significant correlation between the EQ-5D utility index and overall QoL further suggests that commonly used HRQoL instruments may not fully capture outcomes most relevant to this population. Incorporating more nuanced, domain-specific measures into both clinical assessment and research may improve the accuracy of outcome evaluation and inform more patient-centered care planning and resource allocation.
