Recurrent Dystrophic Calcification and Squamous Cell Carcinoma in a Cultured Keratinocyte Scar 35 Years After a 60% Total Body Surface Area Flame Burn
Authors: Aurora Carnio, Alfio Luca Costa, Vincenzo Vindigni, Franco Bassetto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Dystrophic calcification of burn scars is a rare but disabling late complication that can result in chronic ulceration and increase the risk of squamous cell carcinoma. A male patient sustained burns covering 60% of his total body surface in 1989 and received a combination of split-thickness autografts, paternal homografts, and cultured keratinocyte sheet grafts. The first ulcer appeared in 2004 and recurrent lower limbs ulcerations continued intermittently over the following 2 decades. Imaging and histopathology revealed subcutaneous calcium deposits and 3 distinct well-differentiated squamous cell carcinomas. The carcinoma developed in areas of chronic ulceration, consistent with the pathogenesis of Marjolin ulcer. Over the subsequent years, he underwent 25 surgical procedures for excision of calcific nodules and malignancies, followed by reconstruction of resultant defects. Throughout follow-up, serum calcium, phosphate, and renal function remained within normal limits. Calcinosis cutis may manifest decades after deep burns, even in areas resurfaced with cultured epithelial grafts. Complete surgical excision of calcific deposits resolves ulceration, whereas delayed recognition can hinder healing and permit malignant transformation. Persistent chronic ulceration may predispose to squamous cell carcinoma, highlighting the importance of early recognition and biopsy. Lifelong surveillance of extensive burn scars is therefore recommended.

Late complications of extensive burns include contracture, pruritus, heterotopic ossification, and malignancy. Far less common is dystrophic calcinosis cutis, caused by the precipitation of calcium salts in damaged tissue despite normal mineral metabolism. The first well-documented case appeared in 2001 in a burn scar with malignant degeneration.1 Subsequent single-patient reports and short series confirmed the phenomenon, usually occurring decades after injury and predominantly affecting the lower limbs.2–4 Lee et al reported 2 leg ulcers presenting 28 and 34 years after scald wounds; both healed following excision of chalk-like deposits.3 Ebrahim et al reviewed 16 patients with heterotopic calcification in old burn scars, emphasizing that diligent debridement was necessary for wound closure.2 A systematic review in 2022 identified only 15 cases, with a mean latency of 37.5 years.4 The pathogenesis is attributed to chronic inflammation, microtrauma, and local hypoxia in avascular scar tissue. The influence of modern tissue-engineering techniques on this process remains unknown. We describe a 35-year evolution of calcinosis and recurrent squamous cell carcinoma in a scar partially covered with cultured keratinocyte autograft, providing long-term insight into this rare entity.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 21-year-old man sustained a 60% total body surface area flame burn on March 10, 1989, when solvent ignited during an industrial accident. Forty-five percent of the burn was full thickness, on both lower limbs and dorsal upper limbs. Acute management at Padua Burn Centre involved sequential escarectomy, coverage with cultured autologous keratinocytes, paternal homograft, and meshed autograft. Two septic episodes required prolonged intensive care. Over the next 10 years, the patient underwent Z-plasties and graft revisions for scar contracture.

In 2004, the patient developed a pretibial ulcer, which was found to be invasive well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma; radical excision and grafting achieved primary healing. Between 2005 and 2012, progressive nodularity and recurrent bilateral leg ulcers emerged. Biopsies were negative for malignancy, but radiographs demonstrated clustered subcutaneous opacities; excision confirmed dystrophic calcification (Figs. 1, 2).

In September 2024, cellulitis of the right calf prompted further biopsies that showed invasive carcinoma within a calcified scar. Serial operations from November 2024 to July 2025 removed 3 carcinomas and more than 20 calcific plaques; reconstruction used split-thickness skin grafts, amniotic membrane, dermal substitutes, and a fascia lata flap (Figs. 3, 4). Serum calcium and phosphate were normal. At last review in July 2025, the grafts were stable, and no new nodules were palpable. The patient consented in writing to publication.

DISCUSSION

All reported cases of late burn scar calcification have occurred in the presence of normal systemic mineral balance and are therefore classified as dystrophic rather than metastatic.2–5 Their distribution corresponds to areas of greatest mechanical stress, mainly around the ankle and knee, where tight, contracted scars are prone to repeated minor trauma. Róbert et al found that 12 of 15 cases involved the lower limbs.4 Our patient followed this pattern; every calcified nodule developed below the knee. Lee et al suggested that local tissue necrosis and protein denaturation in avascular scars create nucleation sites for calcium deposition.3 Persistent inflammation may further increase tissue alkalinity, favoring calcium precipitation. Lockwood and Oliphant emphasized the foreign-body effect, describing rapid ulcer healing once friable calcium deposits were removed.5 The present case supports their observation: each ulcer healed only after meticulous curettage.

The carcinoma in this patient developed in chronic, ulcerated burn scars, consistent with the pathogenesis of a Marjolin ulcer, where long-standing inflammation, repetitive trauma, and hypovascular scar tissue predispose to malignant transformation.

This case is unique in demonstrating dystrophic calcification and subsequent squamous cell carcinoma within areas resurfaced with cultured autologous keratinocyte sheets—a phenomenon not previously reported. Most reported cases of burn scar calcification involve conventional grafts or healing by secondary intention.2–4 Although the absence of case reports does not imply that cultured keratinocyte sheets are immune to causing dystrophic calcinosis or squamous cell carcinoma, it reinforces the likelihood that these late sequelae are driven primarily by the intrinsic biology of the deep scar rather than by the resurfacing technique. In this patient, calcinosis developed in areas covered with both cultured keratinocytes and split-thickness grafts, supporting this hypothesis.

This stands in contrast to the observations of Copcu et al, who reported that Marjolin ulcers typically arise in ungrafted or inadequately grafted wounds.6 Our case demonstrates that early epithelialization does not eliminate long-term oncologic risk. This interpretation is further supported by Sinha et al, who demonstrated that mature burn scars retain persistent defects in extracellular matrix turnover and exhibit features of epithelial–mesenchymal transition even after grafting.7

Calcification alone is benign; however, chronic ulceration over a scar carries a 2%–3% lifetime risk of squamous cell carcinoma. Xiao et al studied 31 scalp Marjolin ulcers with a mean latency of 42.9 years.8 The coexistence of calcium deposits and carcinoma in our patient echoes the seminal report by Lee et al.1 Calcific fragments may perpetuate ulceration, prolong epithelial regeneration, and increase mutational risk. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold for biopsy in any persistent ulcer within a calcified scar.

Optimal treatment consists of complete surgical removal of dystrophic deposits.3–5 Our patient required progressively wider dissections, ultimately including underlying fascia and muscle to obtain clear margins after carcinoma excision. Postoperative recurrence of calcification has not been documented when excision is thorough. Adjuvant medical therapies used in connective tissue disease calcinosis, such as bisphosphonates or diltiazem, remain experimental in burn scars.

CONCLUSIONS

Dystrophic calcinosis cutis should be suspected when a long-healed burn scar becomes nodular, painful, or chronically ulcerated many years after injury. Radiography or bedside ultrasound can confirm the presence of calcium deposits. Early curettage prevents prolonged ulceration and may theoretically reduce the risk of malignant transformation. Burn survivors with extensive full-thickness scars require lifelong clinical surveillance, and any nonhealing ulcer should be biopsied.
