Comparative effects of organic and inorganic fertilizers on photosynthetic pigments, oxidative metabolism, and yield in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) under semi-arid field conditions
Authors: Cevher İlhan Cevheri, Nimet Kilinçoğlu, Vedat Beyyavas, Erdal Sakin, Ahmet Çelik, Suat Cun, Zemzem Firat, Hamza Yalçin, Emrah Ramazanoglu, Ahmet Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study was conducted under field conditions at Harran University’s Osmanbey Campus. Experimental plots were established using a randomized block design. Parameters such as chlorophyll a and b, carotenoids, total soluble protein, carbohydrate content, and antioxidant components were evaluated across different fertilizer applications.The results indicated that groups treated with 300 kg ha⁻¹ DAP (Diammonium phosphate) fertilizer showed the highest values for chlorophyll and protein synthesis, whereas groups treated with 100 t ha⁻¹ farmyard manure achieved the highest results for carbohydrate and antioxidant production. While chemical fertilizers enriched in nitrogen positively influenced photosynthesis and protein biosynthesis, farmyard manure was found to enhance antioxidant defence mechanisms due to its organic component richness. Lipid peroxidation (MDA) levels remained relatively stable across treatments, though higher values were noted under intensive organic fertilization. The findings suggest that integrating organic amendments with strategic mineral fertilization optimizes cotton physiological health and productivity, offering a sustainable alternative to purely chemical regimes.

Introduction

The cotton plant, with its seed, fiber, seed cake, and meal, is one of the indispensable agricultural products for the industrial sector. Cotton fibers are the most important raw material of the textile industry [1]. Cotton products are utilized in over 80 countries worldwide [2–4]. Consequently, farmers increasingly invest in both organic and chemical fertilizers for cotton production [5, 6]. The use of both organic and inorganic fertilizers in low-fertility soils is vital for crop development and improving yield. However, soils treated with chemical fertilizers tend to become barren over time. Additionally, chemical fertilizers contribute to pesticide and water resource contamination [7]. The widespread use of chemical fertilizers in agriculture is not only critical for agricultural sustainability but also for global climate change. These fertilizers accelerate global warming by increasing the greenhouse effect in the atmosphere [8, 9]. Conversely, organic fertilization methods play an essential role in combating climate change by reducing carbon emissions through their environmentally friendly nature and organic compound content [10–13].

Farmyard manure, compost, green manure, and seed cake, as examples of organic waste, enhance soil fertility [7, 14, 15]. These organic materials, through their various acidic components, can alter the effectiveness of other nutrient elements [16]. As a result, nutrient-mediated physiological processes in plants are modified through improved macro- and micronutrient supply and soil–root interactions. Enriching soil with organic compounds increases plant tolerance to abiotic stress factors [17]. The transport of these organic compounds via active and passive mechanisms induces metabolic changes in plants [9]. The active role of these components results in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in tissues. In response to ROS, plants generate enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant substances. These antioxidants aim to mitigate damage to metabolic processes such as protein and carbohydrate mechanisms and photosynthetic reactions [18]. This defense mechanism, which arises from environmental changes, is a result of plants’ physicochemical processes [19].

Global climate change is recognized as a major factor affecting agricultural productivity worldwide. Rising temperatures and irregular precipitation patterns are among the key environmental challenges influencing crop performance. However, in the present study, no explicit abiotic stress treatments (such as drought, heat, or salinity) were applied; all plants were grown under field conditions without controlled stress imposition. Therefore, references to “climate stress” in the context of experimental design should be interpreted as background environmental variability rather than a directly imposed stress factor. Under such field conditions, yield and physiological performance of cotton may still be indirectly influenced by seasonal climatic fluctuations [20].

Fertilizing soil is one of the methods used both to increase plants’ tolerance to abiotic stress and to rehabilitate soil [21–23]. Particularly, pre-sowing soil fertilization is known to influence the growth and physiological development of cotton plants [24–26].

Although the dynamic coordination of photosynthetic performance and nitrogen metabolism in field crops is responsive to nutrient management, how varying levels and combinations of farmyard manure and mineral fertilizers temporally modulate antioxidant compounds and carbohydrate allocation in cotton under semi-arid environment remains poorly understood. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the temporal dynamics (from 45 to 120 days after sowing) of photosynthetic pigments, oxidative markers, carbohydrate accumulation, and fiber yield in cotton (Candia) subjected to nine distinct organic and inorganic fertilizer combinations under the field conditions of the Harran Plain.

Materials and methods

Experimental field and conditions

The field experiment was conducted for one growing season (2021) at the research plots of Harran University. Although the study spanned a single season due to climatic and project time constraints, the experiment design and data collection schedule ensured comprehensive temporal and physiological evaluation of cotton responses to treatments.

The field experiment was conducted at the research plots of the Faculty of Agriculture, Harran University, located on the Osmanbey Campus in Şanlıurfa, Turkey. The region is characterized by a continental climate, with hot and dry summers and mild, wet winters (Table 1) [27].

Plant material and experimental design

The field experiment was conducted under field conditions on May 1, 2021. The plant material used in the study was the Candia cotton cultivar cotton, which is registered and supplied by BASF Agricultural Solutions Turkey. The Candia cultivar has a high yield potential and a medium-late growing season. Candia is widely used by both producers and the cotton processing industry due to its performance in terms of yield and fiber technological quality criteria (BASF Agricultural Solutions Turkey). A randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications was employed to ensure statistical reliability. Each experimental plot consisted of seven rows, each measuring 10 m in length. The row spacing was set at 75 cm, while the intra-row spacing ranged between 10 and 12 cm. A buffer zone of 3 m was maintained between plots and blocks to minimize edge effects, resulting in a total of 27 experimental plots.

Soil preparation and fertilization

The experimental field was plowed in the autumn and subsequently tilled to a medium depth using a cultivator in early spring. Prior to sowing, a pre-emergence herbicide containing 480 g L⁻¹ pendimethalin was applied to the soil at a rate of 300 g ha⁻¹. The soil was then incorporated and homogenized using a disk harrow, followed by levelling with a harrow to prepare the seedbed. Fertilizers were evenly incorporated into the soil using a rake after plot preparation. Sowing was performed with a pneumatic seeder under optimal meteorological conditions. Irrigation was carried out via a drip irrigation system utilizing water from a nearby deep well. Both the timing and quantity of irrigation were optimized according to the developmental stages of the crop. After preparing the field, the seeds were planted with a pneumatic planter under suitable weather conditions. Cotton seedlings were thinned in 2–4 true leaves period. Hoeing was carried out when the plants had 5–6 leaves to control weeds, aerate the soil and break the surface compacted layer. Insecticides were applied against aphid (Aphis gossypii), leafhopper (Empoasca ssp.), whitefly (Bemisia tabaci Genn) and green worm (Heliothis armigera Hübn.) considering the economic damage thresholds.

Sampling design

Photosynthetic pigments in leaf tissues

Carotenoids, chlorophyll a, and chlorophyll b contents were determined spectrophotometrically using the method of [28]. Fresh plant leaves were treated with 80% (v/v) methanol for 10 days, and the extracts were filtered. Filtrate absorbances were measured at 480 nm for carotenoids, 663 nm for chlorophyll a, and 645 nm for chlorophyll b. Anthocyanins and flavonoids were measured following the method of [29], with absorbances recorded at 535 nm and 650 nm. Total phenolic content was determined at 725 nm using a gallic acid standard curve [30]. Total soluble carbohydrate content was measured at 490 nm using the phenol-sulfuric method [31].

Lipid peroxidation (MDA) was assessed using the TBA test, with absorbances read at 532 nm and 600 nm [32]. Free proline content was analysed at 520 nm [33]. Total protein content was determined using the [34] at 595 nm, with BSA as a standard. All spectrophotometric measurements were performed using a Biotek Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer.

Statistical analysis

Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests were conducted to assess normality and homogeneity of variances across experimental units (threshold p > 0.05).

Before performing the primary analyses, data were subjected to the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances (p > 0.05). To account for the repeated measurements taken from the same experimental plots over six different growth stages (45, 60, 75, 90, 105, and 120 days after sowing), data were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) to handle the chronological within-plot temporal dependency. In this statistical framework, fertilizer treatments, sampling time, and their interaction (Treatment × Time) were specified as fixed effects, while blocks and individual plot IDs were designated as random factors. In addition, to comply with conventional agricultural reporting, a global analysis of variance (ANOVA) for a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with repeated measures was carried out. Treatment means were compared using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test at a significance threshold of p < 0.05. All statistical computing and modeling were executed in R (version 4.4.2) using the agricolae and lme4 packages [35].

Results

Fiber yield (kg ha− 1)

Statistical differences at the 5% level were observed among the fertilizer applications in terms of fiber yields (Fig. 1). When the effects of different fertilizer applications on fiber yield were evaluated in terms of percentage change relative to the control group, the highest increase was observed with the application of T7, resulting in a 46.1% increase. This was followed by T8 of farmyard manure with a 32.3% increase, T4 with a 28.8% increase, and T5 with a 19.8% increase. Among the chemical fertilizers, urea application led to a 17.2% increase in fiber yield, while T4, T2, and T3 application resulted in yield increases of 14.6%, 7.9%, and 6.2%, respectively.

Effect of fertilizer type and application rate on photosynthetic pigment content at different sampling periods

This study evaluated the temporal effects of different fertilizer types diammonium phosphate (DAP), farmyard manure, and urea and their varying application rates on the chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) content of cotton plants. The results revealed significant variations in Chl-a levels depending on both fertilizer type and application rate.

Compared to the control group, the highest Chl-a content a 140% increase was observed on day 45 with the application of T4. During the same period, the lowest increase (48%) occurred with urea application. By day 60, the maximum increase (103%) was again achieved with T4, while the lowest increase (32%) was recorded for urea. On day 75, chlorophyll-a content remained statistically uniform across all experimental groups (p > 0.05), indicating a temporary stabilization of pigment concentration among the fertilizer treatments during this specific developmental stage.

On day 90, the maximum increase (80%) was again observed under the T4 treatment, whereas the minimum increase (18.3%) occurred with T5. By day 105, the highest Chl-a content a 71.8% increase was recorded with the application of T8, while the Chl-a value for urea dropped slightly below the control level (− 1.3%). Finally, on day 120, the highest Chl-a content (a 45.3% increase) was again obtained with T8, and the lowest increase (3.2%) was observed with T1 application (Fig. 2).

When compared to the control group, the Chl-b content showed the highest increase (80.6%) on day 45 with the application of T8. In contrast, a 4.2% decrease was recorded under the T2 treatment during the same period. On day 60, the highest increase (217.4%) was observed with the application of T3, while the lowest increase (8.7%) occurred with urea.

Discussion

Consistent with our initial hypothesis, the application of organic and inorganic fertilizers modulated the oxidative stress response and physiological stability of cotton differently throughout the growth stages. The superior performance of treatment T7 in terms of fiber yield can be attributed to the synergistic effect of manure-derived organic matter and mineral nutrients, which enhanced the plant’s antioxidant pool and photosynthetic capacity. Unlike purely chemical fertilization (DAP), which provided a rapid but transient boost, organic amendments ensured a sustained increase in total phenolic compounds and flavonoids, thereby mitigating potential oxidative damage. Ahmad et al. [36] found that the use of only cattle manure (2 t ha− 1) resulted in a decrease in cotton fiber yield compared to the application of synthetic fertilizer and the mixture of cattle manure + synthetic fertilizer. Similarly, other researchers have obtained higher values with the synthetic + farm manure combinations compared to only farm manure, synthetic fertilizer, and control treatments. Our current study showed that farmyard manure (organic amendments) significantly improved the growth, morphological, and yield-related parameters of cotton plants. Various other studies have reported improvements in these parameters with the combined use of poultry manure, farmyard manure, and biochar with synthetic fertilizers [12, 37]. The co-application of manure and synthetic fertilizers to the soil, as suggested by [38] and [36], has been reported to enhance soil porosity, water and nutrient holding capacity, and the concentrations of macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium) and micronutrients (Zn, Fe, Mn, and Cu) necessary for plant growth.

These results clearly demonstrate that both DAP and farmyard manure (FYM) have positive effects on the biosynthesis of chlorophyll a (Chl-a) and chlorophyll b (Chl-b). In particular, the application of T4 and T8 significantly enhanced chlorophyll content by increasing the photosynthetic capacity of the cotton plants. In contrast, the effect of urea diminished over time and, in some cases, resulted in lower chlorophyll concentrations than those observed in the control group. These findings suggest that DAP, due to its high phosphorus content, and farmyard manure, rich in organic matter, are more effective in stimulating chlorophyll biosynthesis in cotton compared to urea.

Fertilizer applications mitigate nutrient deficiency-related stress effects in plants [39]. Deficiencies in various organic and inorganic nutrients impair plant growth and physiology, leading to reduced crop yields [40, 41]. Fertilization increases nutrient availability and improves yield [42–44]. The increased organic compounds in soil due to fertilization initially alter soil water capacity [10]. Changes in water availability stimulate various metabolic signalling pathways in plants [39, 41, 45]. Altered water levels often impact the concentration of photosynthetic pigments, affecting the energy production mechanism of photosynthesis [46].

The superior performance of T4 and T8 in enhancing chlorophyll pigments and soluble protein concentrations could be attributed to its high availability of free nitrogen compounds. Plants require more nitrogen compounds to synthesize chlorophyll pigments and protein derivatives. Utilizing this fertilizer, the plants may have increased their chlorophyll pigment and protein levels [46, 47]. The increase in nitrogen molecules highlights their role as key regulators of structural integrity and enzymatic activities within the antioxidant system, underscoring the importance of nitrogen in promoting plant growth and photosynthesis [42, 47].

These findings indicate that mineral fertilizers particularly DAP rapidly stimulate pigment production during early growth stages, whereas farmyard manure becomes more effective at later stages. The results suggest that the influence of fertilizers on carotenoid metabolism depends not only on their nutrient composition but also on the timing of nutrient uptake by the plant. It has been emphasized in the literature that phosphorus-rich fertilizers such as DAP enhance carotenoid accumulation by promoting the synthesis of photosynthetic pigments [48]. On the other hand, the delayed effectiveness of organic fertilizers likely reflects the gradual release of nutrients due to soil biological processes and microbial activity [49]. These fertilizers may have oxidized cell membranes to varying degrees, triggering the production of diverse antioxidant compounds (e.g., carotenoids), which in turn may have led to similar levels of lipid peroxidation damage. The literature also indicates that intracellular antioxidants are synthesized in response to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) to maintain redox balance [50–52].

The involvement of organic and inorganic compounds in metabolic reactions like photosynthesis increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within cells. These ROS degrade polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes, resulting in MDA production [53]. In this study, all three fertilizer types equally contributed to MDA production. These fertilizers may have oxidized cell membranes at varying rates while producing diverse antioxidant substances (secondary metabolites, carotenoids, total phenols, anthocyanins, flavonoids), leading to similar levels of lipid peroxidation damage. Literature suggests that antioxidants within cells are synthesized to counterbalance ROS increases [50–52]. These antioxidants detoxify accumulated ROS within cells, functioning in both chloroplasts and cytoplasm [54, 55]. The superior impact of T8 on carotenoid, total phenol, anthocyanin, and flavonoid levels compared to other fertilizers may be due to its higher organic and inorganic compound content. In this study, MDA levels were found to increase over time, with the magnitude of this increase varying depending on the type and dose of fertilizer applied. Notably, the application of T8 resulted in the highest MDA accumulation during the later stages (days 90–120), suggesting that high organic matter inputs may enhance microbial activity and oxidative stress. During the mineralization of organic matter, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated, which can promote lipid peroxidation in cellular membranes and consequently elevate MDA levels [56, 57]. In contrast, the relatively modest increase in MDA observed under urea application, particularly in later stages, may be attributed to the rapid plant uptake of mineral nitrogen and reduced microbial interactions. Similarly, high-dose DAP treatments induced marked increases in early stages, but their effects were less pronounced over time. These findings indicate that fertilizer-induced oxidative stress is influenced not only by chemical composition but also by the rate and pathway of nutrient mineralization.

The temporal increase in protein content observed in this study indicates that both fertilizer type and application rate significantly influence nitrogen assimilation in cotton. Notably, the application of T8 and T4 consistently resulted in the highest protein concentrations at later stages, particularly at 105 and 120 days. These findings suggest that high doses of both organic and mineral fertilizers enhance nitrogen availability and uptake, which are critical for protein biosynthesis [58]. Farmyard manure likely contributes to a slow but sustained release of nitrogen, supporting prolonged protein accumulation, while DAP provides readily available nitrogen and phosphorus that stimulate early metabolic activity. In contrast, the lowest increases were generally recorded with lower doses of organic fertilizer, indicating limited nitrogen supply or delayed mineralization. The statistically significant differences (LSD < 0.01) at later stages further emphasize the importance of fertilizer strategy in managing crop quality. These results align with previous findings that link nutrient management with protein dynamics in field crops [59].

These findings demonstrate that the application of T4 and T8 exerts a pronounced and temporally dynamic effect on proline metabolism. High-dose DAP treatments markedly accelerated proline accumulation during the early growth stages, whereas the impact of high-dose organic amendments became more prominent at later developmental phases. Conversely, lower doses of farmyard manure and urea generally failed to enhance proline accumulation, occasionally resulting in concentrations below those observed in the control treatments. These results underscore that, in addition to fertilizer type and dosage, the timing of their physiological efficacy plays a decisive role in the accumulation of osmoregulatory compounds associated with stress responses.

Proline accumulation is frequently used as a physiological indicator of metabolic adjustment in plants. In the present study, variations in proline concentration likely reflected differences in nutrient availability and metabolic activity induced by fertilizer treatments rather than responses to externally imposed abiotic stress [60, 61]. Nevertheless, excessive accumulation of proline can impose metabolic burdens on the plant system, necessitating its maintenance within an optimal range [62].

Furthermore, these findings indicate that high-dose DAP and farmyard manure applications progressively promoted protein accumulation over time. Specifically, treatments involving T8 and T4 significantly stimulated protein biosynthesis during both early and late stages of plant development. In contrast, lower doses of organic fertilizers (notably 200 ton ha− 1) and reduced mineral inputs resulted in inconsistent and, in certain periods, insufficient increases in protein content. This phenomenon may be attributable to differential solubility and mineralization dynamics inherent to organic and mineral fertilizers.

The accumulation of phenolic compounds in plants constitutes a key indicator of defense responses against environmental stressors. Elevated levels of phenolics reflect an enhanced capacity to mitigate oxidative damage and the activation of pathogen defense mechanisms, whereas diminished phenolic concentrations are typically associated with either non-stressful conditions or suppressed defense pathways [63, 64].

The observed fluctuations in the flavonoid content of the plants across treatments indicate that the applied fertilization regimes exerted discernible effects on secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Flavonoids function as effective ultraviolet (UV) filters and potent antioxidants capable of neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby safeguarding cellular membranes and genomic integrity. Notably, it is well established that specific fertilizer dosages can modulate flavonoid synthesis by regulating the phenylpropanoid pathway. Consequently, variations in flavonoid concentrations should be interpreted in the context of not only the nutritional status of the plants but also complex interactions involving soil chemistry, microbial dynamics, and environmental stress factors. Flavonoids are central to plant defense mechanisms against environmental stressors, enhancing resilience to UV radiation, drought, and pathogen attacks [65, 66]. In this study, the observation of reduced flavonoid levels in certain treatments relative to the control suggests either inadequate activation of stress response pathways or metabolic suppression. Conversely, elevated flavonoid concentrations under particular conditions reflect an effective antioxidative defense under oxidative stress. These findings collectively demonstrate that flavonoid accumulation is dynamically modulated by the fertilizer regime and environmental variables through regulation of the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway.

The data obtained further revealed that different fertilization treatments induced temporal variations in anthocyanin content when compared with the control group. Anthocyanin levels were statistically significant at days 45, 60, and 90, whereas no significant differences were detected at days 75, 105, and 120. At day 45, the highest anthocyanin concentration was observed with the application of T4, representing a 23.3% increase, while the lowest was recorded under the T5 treatment, which exhibited a 44.4% reduction (p < 0.05). At day 60, the T4 application again yielded the highest content with a 40.2% increase, whereas a T2 treatment resulted in the lowest content, with a 5.4% reduction (p < 0.05).

At day 75, the maximal increase was 19.75% under the T4 treatment, while the lowest was a 15.1% decrease with T5, though this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). On day 90, the T4 treatment induced a 24.7% increase in anthocyanin content, and the T5 application resulted in a marginal 1.46% reduction (p < 0.05). At day 105, the highest increase (20.5%) was again recorded with T4, whereas urea application caused the lowest content, with a 14.61% decrease (p > 0.05). Finally, at day 120, the maximal increase was 30.8% under T4, and the lowest was a modest 4.74% increase with T1 treatment (p > 0.05).

The experimental outcomes demonstrated that the application of T4 treatment significantly enhanced anthocyanin accumulation, whereas low-dose farmyard manure appeared to suppress the phenylpropanoid pathway, thereby diminishing its efficacy. The phosphorus and ammonium components of DAP may activate signalling cascades that promote anthocyanin biosynthesis [67]. Conversely, organic biofertilizers derived from farmyard manure have been reported to exert inhibitory effects on phenylpropanoid secondary metabolism [68]. Moreover, excessive phosphorus application has also been documented to stimulate anthocyanin accumulation [69]. Collectively, these findings underscore that both fertilizer type and dosage are critical determinants in the regulation of metabolic pathways governing secondary metabolite production.

The increase in soluble carbohydrate content observed under farmyard manure treatments may be associated with enhanced carbon assimilation and improved nutrient availability. Such responses are commonly linked to increased metabolic activity and biomass production [70]. In this study, farmyard manure treatments significantly increased the total soluble carbohydrate (TSC) content, thereby enhancing metabolic activity, stress tolerance, and growth potential. Notably, an increase of up to 38% under the T8 treatment reflects an enrichment of carbohydrate reserves and improved osmotic balance. The superior performance of T8 in proline and soluble carbohydrate production may be attributed to its role in mitigating water deficiency within cells [71, 72].

This biochemical response constitutes an essential foundation for sustainable productivity. Furthermore, in the context of circular economy practices, the application of farmyard manure reduces external dependency by establishing a self-sustaining nutrient cycle within the production system. Such practices promote economically viable production alongside the preservation of environmental quality, soil fertility, and plant health.

Water transfer is crucial for metabolic reactions. Water plays a vital role in physiological processes. When plants face water stress or accumulate excess organic compounds, they synthesize substances like proline to counter ROS production. These synthesized organic substances act as osmotic balancers, aiding in water transport [73]. Consequently, they protect chlorophyll pigments, cell membrane integrity, photosynthesis mechanisms, and the structural integrity of enzymes [74]. Soluble sugars also play a critical role in regulating water availability in plants grown in low-water environments [75]. Soluble sugars act as osmolytes, enhancing plant stress tolerance by signalling carbohydrate metabolism and interacting with other sugar types. The superior performance of T8 in proline and soluble carbohydrate production may be attributed to its higher content of osmolyte like compounds compared to other fertilizers, indicating its role in mitigating water deficiency within cells.

In terms of physiological parameters (except chlorophyll a, b, carotenoid and total soluble protein content) and some antioxidant parameters, T8 came to the forefront compared to other fertilizer types and doses. However, the highest yield parameter result was observed in T7. This result showed us that the most suitable fertilization dose for cotton plant is T7. When T8 of fertilization is applied, we can say that stress signals in the plant increase even more and changes in metabolic mechanisms begin. In the literature, it has been reported that excessive application of farm manure salinizes the soil [76] and causes osmotic imbalance in the plant, causing carbohydrate and proline accumulation [51]. While excess organic fertilizer causes excess N and P, accelerates growth and causes a decrease in photosynthetic pigments; the deterioration of the C / N ratio causes deterioration in amino acid and carbohydrate balance and limits yield [77].

Conclusion

This study provides a comparative evaluation of organic and inorganic fertilizer strategies and demonstrates that fertilizer type and application rate significantly influence physiological traits and fiber yield in cotton grown under semi-arid field conditions.Based on the findings of this study, applying T7 to cotton plants is recommended for improving yield compared to other organic and inorganic fertilizers. In cases where farmyard manure is unavailable, applying T4 is a viable alternative. Urea fertilizer demonstrated comparatively lower effectiveness.

Given the current conditions of global climate change, further physiological, molecular, and genetic studies on cotton plants are necessary to explore the effects of these fertilizers either individually or in combination. Fertilizer usage strategies can be developed to address the specific needs of soils and plants. The impact of this study on soil, carbon cycles, and carbon footprints should also be investigated to strengthen sustainable agriculture.

In conclusion, the integrated use of cattle manure and mineral fertilizers (T7) provides the best results for sustainable cotton production. While this study highlights the physiological benefits, further research is needed to explore the long-term impacts on soil microbial diversity and carbon sequestration.
