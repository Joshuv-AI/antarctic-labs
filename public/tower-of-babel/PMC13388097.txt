Modulation of TH17 cell activity by REV–ERB agonists: path toward novel treatments for canine meningoencephalitis of unknown origin
Authors: Ashton Bailey, Ryan Gibson, Kristine Griffett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeningoencephalitis of unknown origin (MUO) encompasses a heterogeneous group of non-infectious, presumed autoimmune, central nervous system diseases in dogs and remains a major therapeutic challenge in veterinary neurology. Mounting evidence from both experimental and clinical studies has highlighted the pivotal role of T helper 17 (Th17) cells and their proinflammatory cytokines, especially interleukin-17A (IL-17A), in mediating neuroinflammation similar to that seen in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) and multiple sclerosis (MS) models. REV–ERBs are nuclear receptors that act as transcriptional repressors and regulate immune responses, circadian rhythm, and metabolism. Synthetic REV–ERB agonists, such as SR9009, SR9011, and SR12418, have demonstrated selective suppression of Th17 differentiation and function, reduction of disease severity, and improved safety profiles in preclinical autoimmune models. In addition to Th17 differentiation, REV–ERB’s role in other components of the immunomodulating system is ever-growing. Preclinical and translational data support the further exploration of REV–ERB agonists as a potential addition to the targeted immunomodulatory therapies for canine MUO. By continuing to investigate REV–ERB compounds as a component of MUO therapy, researchers can assess their potential for improved efficacy and reduce the side effects associated with traditional immunosuppressive regimens. This review integrates mechanistic insights from immunology, circadian biology, and experimental models, and outlines future directions for clinical translation, highlighting the relevance of REV–ERB agonists in advancing veterinary neuroimmunology and in informing comparative approaches to human neuroautoimmune disease. Subject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

REV–ERBs (REV–ERBα and REV–ERBβ) are nuclear receptors within the superfamily of transcription factors and perform essential roles as transcriptional repressors by regulating their direct target genes1–3. Unique among nuclear receptors, REV–ERBs lack the carboxy-terminal activation function-2 region (helix 12) in their ligand binding domains, which prevents recognition of coactivators and thereby defines their action as transcriptional repressors rather than activators1,4–6. In contrast, retinoic acid-related orphan receptors (RORs) function as transcriptional activators and directly counterbalance the effects of REV–ERBs (Fig. 1)7–9. The dynamic interplay between REV–ERBs and RORs is central to fine-tuning the activity of T helper 17 (Th17) cells, a subset of T lymphocytes that produce IL-17A and other key proinflammatory cytokines and are vital mediators in the development and progression of several autoimmune disorders across species6,7,10–12. Additional studies have provided support for REV–ERB’s effect on other immune cell types and additional cytokines such as IL-21, IL-4, and IL-31, all of which may provide potential mechanistic pathways for use as a therapeutic either alone or as an adjuvant therapy. This review will evaluate the therapeutic potential of REV–ERB-specific synthetic agonists for meningoencephalitis of unknown origin (MUO), comparing mechanistic insights derived from models of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) and multiple sclerosis (MS).

Light entrainment and molecular architecture of the mammalian circadian clock.

In mammals, circadian rhythms are primarily entrained by the environmental light–dark cycle, with light information detected by retinal photoreceptors and transmitted to the brain via the retinohypothalamic tract. This signal converges on the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus, which functions as the central circadian pacemaker coordinating daily rhythms in behavior, physiology, and metabolism. At the cellular level, circadian timing is generated by an interconnected transcriptional–translational feedback loop (TTFL) composed of core clock transcription factors, including Bmal1 and Clock, which heterodimerize to activate expression of the Period (Per1–3) and Cryptochrome (Cry1–2) genes. Accumulating Per and Cry proteins subsequently inhibit Bmal1/Clock activity, thereby establishing a ~24-h oscillation. This core loop is reinforced and fine-tuned by the nuclear receptors REV–ERB (REV–ERBα/β) and ROR (RORα/β/γ), which compete for binding at ROR response elements to repress or activate Bmal1 transcription, respectively. Together, light-driven SCN entrainment and this nuclear receptor–integrated molecular network ensure robust circadian oscillations across central and peripheral tissues.

REV–ERB and circadian biology

REV–ERBs are best known for their integral role in maintaining the circadian rhythm in mammals by controlling core feedback loops within the suprachiasmatic nucleus and peripheral tissues (Fig. 1). Beyond circadian homeostasis, REV–ERBs regulate multiple physiological pathways, including lipid and glucose metabolism, mitochondrial function, and autophagy, underscoring their importance in both health and disease4. Crucially, the regulation of immune responses is intimately linked to the circadian rhythm. Disruption in the biological clock has been shown to exacerbate a variety of metabolic and inflammatory disorders, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), MS, and EAE13,14. Chronic perturbations in circadian signaling can influence immune cell trafficking, cytokine production, and metabolic profiles, strengthening the rationale for targeting REV–ERBs in immune-mediated diseases15–17.

While the intracellular molecular clock relies on a complex, interlocking transcription-translation feedback loop driven by Clock and Bmal1, the translational relevance for neuroinflammation lies in how these core clock components directly gate immune behavior. Rather than operating as static molecular timers, these intracellular loops dictate the daily temporal rhythms of leukocyte bone marrow egress, rhythmic blood–brain barrier permeability, and the basal activation thresholds of microglia. By focusing on the downstream immunological outputs of the clock, specifically through nuclear receptors like REV–ERB, rather than the redundant complexities of the auxiliary clock machinery, we can better appreciate how circadian disruption directly fuels non-resolving neuroinflammatory states.

REV–ERB agonists in immune modulation and autoimmunity models

Mounting evidence supports the role of REV–ERBs as key modulators of immune responses, particularly through the suppression of Th17 cell proliferation and function, as well as innate immune mechanisms (Fig. 2a)10,18. Synthetic agonists of REV–ERB, such as SR9009, SR9011, and, more recently, SR12418, have been extensively tested in both in vitro and in vivo systems. These agents exhibit selective cytotoxicity toward pathological cell populations (e.g., cancer cells) while sparing normal tissues, attesting to their safety as therapeutic candidates19–21. Mechanistically, REV–ERB agonists counterbalance RORγt, a principal driver of Th17 differentiation, thereby suppressing IL-17A and IL-17F cytokine production and preventing the expansion of Th17 cells that perpetuate proinflammatory states (Fig. 2b). Additionally, support for activity against Th2 cytokine activity has been reported in its role for allergic-based lung disease22. In murine models of EAE, treatment with synthetic REV–ERB agonists delays disease onset, mitigates clinical severity, and reduces histopathological hallmarks of CNS demyelination and neuroinflammation6,11,23.

REV–ERB-mediated transcriptional repression and suppression of Th17 cell differentiation.

a Schematic illustrating ligand-dependent repression by the nuclear receptor REV–ERB. In the presence of a REV–ERB agonist, REV–ERB binds to ROR response elements (ROREs) within target gene regulatory regions and recruits the nuclear receptor co-repressor (NCoR) complex and histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3). This repressive chromatin complex suppresses transcription of proinflammatory genes, including Il6, Il1b, Tgfb, Il17, and Il31ra, thereby limiting inflammatory gene expression programs. b Regulation of Th17 cell differentiation by REV–ERB. Under inflammatory conditions, dendritic cell–derived cytokines (IL-6 and TGFβ) promote activation of STAT3 and induction of the lineage-defining transcription factor RORγt, driving differentiation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells into Th17 cells and production of IL-17. Activation of REV–ERB inhibits this pathway by suppressing cytokine-driven STAT3 signaling and/or antagonizing RORγt-dependent transcription, resulting in reduced Th17 differentiation and diminished IL-17 expression.

While initial studies relied on REV–ERB pan-agonists such as SR9009 and SR9011, research has evolved to produce second-generation compounds, including SR12418, which possess improved pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties. SR12418 shows improved bioavailability, tissue penetration, and target specificity compared to older molecules, such as the RORα/γ inverse agonist SR100110. Notably, SR12418 effectively lowers the numbers of pathogenic CD3+ CD4+ T cells and RORγt+ cells at peak disease, without disrupting circadian rhythms or causing toxicity across organ systems10. Dual targeting of REV–ERBα/β receptors enhances the therapeutic impact, possibly by more robustly repressing proinflammatory transcriptional programs and further restraining Th17 activity at multiple molecular nodes. Additionally, Makhija et al.16 described a novel pan-REV–ERB agonist, STL1267, that was successful in targeting immune-mediated pain conditions; however, additional testing in T-cell-mediated disease models is warranted.

Although the suppression of the Th17/IL-17 axis represents a primary therapeutic mechanism of REV–ERB agonists, the pathogenesis of neuroinflammatory diseases like MUO involves widespread dysregulation across both adaptive and innate immune compartments. Beyond its direct repression of RORγt in CD4+ T cells, REV–ERB serves as a critical multi-lineage immunological regulator. In the innate compartment, REV–ERB activation powerfully suppresses the transcription of proinflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) in macrophages and central nervous system-resident microglia, largely through the tethering and repression of NF-κB and AP-1 binding sites16,24–26. By promoting an anti-inflammatory, pro-resolving myeloid phenotype, REV–ERB agonists can mitigate the severe perivascular cuffing and subsequent parenchymal necrosis that characterizes MUO pathology. Furthermore, this suppression of innate signaling directly impacts adaptive crosstalk; decreased myeloid-derived IL-6 production limits subsequent STAT3 activation, altering the polarization of naive T cells6,25,27. Consequently, this shifts the immunological balance away from pathogenic Th17 phenotypes and indirectly favors the maintenance and stability of Foxp3-expressing regulatory T cells (Tregs). Integrating these multi-pronged innate and adaptive mechanisms establishes a more comprehensive framework for utilizing REV–ERB targets to restore overall immune homeostasis in the central nervous system.

While IL-17A serves as the canonical output of the Th17 lineage, recent advances in neuroimmunology demonstrate that IL-17A expression alone is insufficient to drive severe central nervous system pathology. Instead, the true encephalitogenic potential of helper T cells is dictated by a broader, coordinated cytokine network that includes granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), IL-21, IL-22, and the IL-23/IL-23 receptor (IL-23R) signaling axis. GM-CSF is increasingly recognized as a mandatory executioner of neuroinflammation, acting downstream of T-cell activation to license and activate pathogenic, tissue-destructive myeloid subsets within the CNS parenchyma. Crucially, the expression of both GM-CSF and IL-23R is directly dependent on the master transcription factor RORγt. By directly repressing RORγt, REV–ERB agonists fundamentally dismantle this entire pathogenic cassette, concurrently downregulating IL-17A, GM-CSF, and IL-23R expression on developing T cells.

Additionally, REV–ERB signaling exerts upstream control over the external cytokine cues required to stabilize and mature these pathogenic subsets. Under homeostatic conditions, IL-21 acts in an autocrine loop to amplify early Th17 differentiation, while myeloid-derived IL-23 acts via IL-23R to lock in the aggressive, non-resolving phenotype characteristic of diseases like MS, EAE, and MUO. Pharmacological activation of REV–ERB breaks this amplification loop on two fronts. First, it suppresses the innate production of IL-6 and IL-23 by antigen-presenting cells, depriving naive T cells of the initial polarizing and subsequent sustaining signals. Second, the reduction in baseline STAT3 activation limits downstream IL-21 and IL-23R transcription3,6,11,23,28. While the precise direct effects of REV–ERB on IL-22 kinetics remain an active area of investigation, owing to IL-22’s dual protective/inflammatory nature depending on epithelial barrier contexts, the overarching pharmacological effect of REV–ERB activation is a comprehensive collapse of the RORγt-driven pathogenic network, offering a distinct therapeutic advantage over therapies that target single cytokine targets alone.

Immunometabolic regulation of T-cell fitness by REV–ERB

Beyond its direct transcriptional repression of lineage-specific transcription factors, REV–ERB acts as a master regulator at the critical nexus of circadian rhythmicity, cellular metabolism, and T-cell immunometabolic fitness. In autoimmune and neuroinflammatory diseases, the balance between pathogenic effector T cells (Th17/Th1) and protective regulatory T cells (Tregs) is dictated by distinct metabolic programming. Pathogenic Th17 cells heavily rely on accelerated glycolytic pathways and hyperactivated mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling to support their proliferation and inflammatory effector functions. Conversely, Treg stability and suppressive capacity depend on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and lipid oxidation, a balance that is severely compromised under chronic neuroinflammatory conditions where mTOR overactivation drives Treg dysfunction29–32.

REV–ERB agonists directly counter this pathogenic immunometabolic programming through several coordinated mechanisms. Pharmacological activation of REV–ERB downregulates key rate-limiting enzymes in the glycolytic cascade, effectively starving highly glycolytic pathogenic effector T cells of their primary energy source and shifting the metabolic landscape. Concurrently, REV–ERB activation modulates mitochondrial metabolism and limits the stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α). Because HIF-1α is a primary driver of the glycolytic machinery and a direct promoter of Th17 differentiation, its suppression via the REV–ERB-mitochondrial axis breaks the positive feedback loop that sustains autoimmune T-cell survival31–33. Furthermore, by attenuating chronic, low-grade metabolic stress and suppressing upstream innate inflammatory cues like IL-6, REV–ERB agonists mitigate the persistent nutrient-sensing signals that hyperactivate mTOR in autoimmune states. This metabolic restraint not only curtails the encephalitogenic potential of Th17 cells but also alleviates the metabolic constraints that drive Treg oscillatory failure, establishing a more resilient, homeostatic immunometabolic environment within the central nervous system.

EAE as a translational model for MS and MUO

Experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) is widely accepted as an animal model for the human condition of MS34–36. EAE recapitulates essential aspects of MS pathogenesis. This includes CNS inflammation, demyelination, axonal loss, gliosis, and immune cell infiltration, making it a benchmark for understanding autoimmune neuroinflammatory mechanisms (Fig. 3). In both MS and EAE, Th17 cells play a pivotal role in disease induction and progression. These cells infiltrate the CNS, secreting IL-17A, upregulating chemokine receptors like CCR6, and driving the breakdown of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), as well as direct neuronal damage through immune-neuronal synapse formation and calcium signaling dysregulation37. Studies show that depletion or modulation of Th17 cells or their signature cytokines correlates with improved clinical outcomes and reduced tissue injury, spotlighting them as attractive targets for novel therapeutic strategies.

CD4⁺ T cell-driven pathogenesis of inflammatory demyelinating disease in human multiple sclerosis (MS), mouse experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), and canine meningoencephalitis of unknown origin (MUO).

Peripheral immune activation leads to priming and expansion of autoreactive CD4⁺ T cells within secondary lymphoid organs following antigen presentation by professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs). Differentiation into pathogenic T helper subsets, including Th1 and Th17 cells, is accompanied by production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IFNγ, IL-17, IL-21, IL-2, and IL-31. Activated CD4⁺ T cells traffic through the circulation, cross meningeal and blood–brain barrier interfaces, and accumulate within the subarachnoid space and central nervous system (CNS) parenchyma, where barrier disruption is associated with increased albumin and immunoglobulin extravasation. Within the CNS, infiltrating CD4⁺ T cells undergo reactivation by local APCs, including microglia and infiltrating macrophages, amplifying neuroinflammation through cytokine secretion and cell–cell interactions. This inflammatory milieu promotes microglial activation, macrophage recruitment, reactive astrogliosis, and neuronal injury, ultimately contributing to demyelination and neurodegeneration. Across MS, EAE, and MUO, elevated proinflammatory cytokines, increased intrathecal antibodies (including anti-GFAP IgG), and sustained CD4⁺ T cell-mediated immune responses represent conserved pathogenic features underlying disease progression across species.

Meningoencephalitis of unknown origin (MUO) is a collective term for a group of non-infectious, presumed autoimmune, inflammatory CNS diseases in dogs38,39. The recognized clinical and pathological subtypes of MUO include granulomatous meningoencephalomyelitis (GME) and necrotizing encephalitis (NE), the latter encompassing necrotizing meningoencephalitis (NME) and necrotizing leukoencephalitis (NLE)40. MUO predominantly affects young- to middle-aged, small-breed dogs, though age and breed predilections vary across subtypes. The etiology remains ambiguous, with suspected interplay between genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and an immune-mediated breakdown of CNS tolerance38,41,42. Molecular investigations highlight putative associations between specific dog leukocyte antigen (DLA) class II genotypes and risk of NME, suggesting a role for genetic testing in future disease prevention efforts.

Immunohistochemical and molecular studies of canine MUO, particularly GME, demonstrate lesions rich in CD3+ T cells, MHC class II antigen-positive macrophages, and a relative paucity of B cells, implicating a T cell-dominated hypersensitivity response40,43,44. Further analysis of peripheral blood and CNS tissue from affected MUO dogs reveals increased numbers of CD4+ IL-17-producing Th17 cells relative to healthy controls, paralleling findings in EAE and MS (Fig. 3)43. These data support the hypothesis that Th17-driven immune responses are central to MUO pathogenesis, and that modulating Th17 activity may hold promise for disease mitigation39. Additional sites of action have also been identified beyond Th17. In dogs with GME, a T-helper 2-dominated immune response has been observed with the production of IL-21, IL-17, and IL-445. The mRNA and protein expression levels of interferon gamma (IFNγ) were significantly higher in NME, while IL-17 were higher with GME46. IL-17 is upregulated locally in the CNS; alterations in peripheral and CNS-derived cytokine signatures suggest dysregulated immune compartmentalization in MUO (Fig. 3)43. IL-31, a proinflammatory cytokine produced by Th2 cells, has recently been identified as a potential marker for diagnosis and/or therapy, though further correlation is required39. In one study, there was an association with elevated IL-31 and neurological autoimmune disorders in dogs47. ChIP data also show direct evidence of IL-31 alpha receptor (murine) with indirect support for REV–ERB’s potential to affect IL-3148,49.

While EAE serves as the canonical model for autoimmune-mediated, inflammatory demyelination in the central nervous system, its pathological presentation exhibits distinct features when contrasted with MUO. In EAE and MS, demyelination is classically characterized by focal, plaque-like lesions primarily driven by white matter infiltration. In contrast, the pathology of MUO is often distinguished by more diffuse, widespread inflammation with a high propensity for severe necrosis and massive perivascular cuffing that spans both white and gray matter compartments. Rather than lacking a demyelinating component, MUO differs from classic EAE in the structural distribution, intensity, and necrotizing nature of its neuroinflammatory lesions43. However, immune phenotyping across species demonstrates overlapping mechanisms: Th17 cells are implicated in barrier breakdown, tissue infiltration, and neurotoxicity in both canine and human CNS autoimmunity50,51. Although experimentally induced, canine EAE mimics fulminant forms of MS observed in humans, reinforcing the value of cross-species translational research and the utility of EAE for screening novel immunomodulatory therapies. Insights gained from EAE models, regarding cytokine profiling, immune cell trafficking, and the impact of molecular interventions, directly inform the tactical approach to improving outcomes in MUO patients52.

While cross-species comparisons between murine EAE, canine MUO, and human MS offer powerful mechanistic insights, important limitations must be acknowledged when extrapolating data from rodent models to spontaneous large-animal diseases. Induced murine EAE relies on targeted immunization with specific myelin peptides within a genetically uniform population, occurring under highly controlled pathogen-free conditions. In contrast, canine MUO manifests spontaneously in a genetically diverse population, capturing the complex interplay of environmental triggers, variable host genetics, and heterogeneous immune responses that define real-world neuroinflammation. Furthermore, distinct species-specific variations exist in leukocyte biology, cytokine signaling cascades, and blood–brain barrier kinetics. Rather than viewing these differences as an experimental barrier, recognizing the limitations of murine models highlights the critical evolutionary value of veterinary patients. Spontaneous canine MUO represents a vital intermediate translational bridge, offering a more clinically relevant, heterogeneous environment to validate therapeutic strategies before human clinical deployment.

Current treatment approaches and challenges in MUO

The therapeutic landscape for canine MUO is dominated by long-term immunosuppression, primarily with corticosteroids, often augmented by additional agents such as cytarabine, azathioprine, or cyclosporine44,53. Despite transient improvements, these regimens are plagued by significant side effects, incomplete remission rates, and disease relapse. The adverse consequences, including immunosuppression, opportunistic infections, and endocrine disturbances, restrict quality of life and often necessitate ongoing dosage adjustments or therapy substitutions42. Breed and age influence clinical presentation and treatment response; for example, pugs with NME demonstrate highly aggressive disease courses with a poor prognosis. As such, the development of safer, more targeted therapeutics that address underlying immunopathology or allow for a reduction in total steroid dosage, thereby minimizing side effects, remains a top priority in veterinary medicine.

Mechanisms of REV–ERB agonists: translating from bench to bedside

REV–ERB synthetic agonists offer unique advantages in targeting Th17 cell-driven CNS autoimmunity. Through antagonism of RORγt and direct repression of IL-17a, these agents disrupt the proinflammatory feedback loop central to EAE, MS, and MUO. Importantly, this targeted immunomodulation avoids the broad immune suppression characteristic of conventional drug therapies. In addition, the stability of circadian and metabolic processes under REV–ERB agonist regimens, as observed with SR12418, further distinguishes this approach10. In vivo preclinical studies reveal disease attenuation, reduction of clinical scores, and decreased demyelination in murine EAE following prolonged exposure to REV–ERB agonists10. These results suggest that selective immune modulation, particularly temporally aligned with circadian biology, may minimize off-target toxicities, optimize efficacy, and contribute to sustained remission in Th17-mediated CNS disorders.

Based on the mechanistic, experimental, and clinical evidence summarized above, REV–ERB-specific agonists represent compelling candidates for continued investigation of these compounds in targeting MUO and related neuroinflammatory conditions in veterinary medicine. Key endpoints for future trials should include safety/pharmacokinetic profiles, the impact on peripheral and CNS immune markers, clinical and neurologic outcomes, translational relevance to comparative medicine, and long-term treatment sustainability. Given the lack of reliable ante-mortem diagnostic and prognostic markers for MUO, integrated trial designs utilizing advanced immunophenotyping, MRI, and biomarker analysis are warranted. Cross-disciplinary collaboration between veterinary neurologists, immunologists, and translational scientists will be essential for successful implementation.

Current limitations of REV–ERB agonists

Targeting REV–ERB nuclear receptors represents a conceptually innovative approach to the treatment of autoimmune disorders of the central nervous system (CNS), including multiple sclerosis (MS) and canine meningoencephalomyelitis of unknown origin (MUO). By directly coupling circadian transcriptional control to immune regulation, REV–ERB agonists offer a mechanism distinct from conventional immunosuppressive therapies, with the potential to selectively dampen pathogenic Th17 responses while reshaping innate immune activation. Preclinical studies demonstrating robust suppression of neuroinflammatory pathology, together with favorable short-term safety profiles, position REV–ERB modulation as a promising therapeutic axis. Nevertheless, several biological and translational challenges must be addressed before this strategy can be fully realized in clinical settings.

A primary limitation lies in achieving effective CNS exposure. Many existing REV–ERB agonists were originally developed for peripheral metabolic or inflammatory indications and exhibit limited penetration of the blood–brain barrier. Given that autoimmune CNS diseases encompass both peripheral immune dysregulation and intraparenchymal inflammatory processes, it remains unresolved whether peripheral immune modulation alone is sufficient or whether sustained receptor engagement within the CNS is required for durable therapeutic benefit. This issue is particularly salient in MUO, where blood–brain barrier integrity is heterogeneous, and disease progression is often rapid, potentially constraining therapeutic windows.

An additional layer of complexity arises from the central role of REV–ERBs in circadian biology. These receptors coordinate rhythmic transcriptional programs across immune, metabolic, and neural tissues, raising the possibility that chronic or mistimed pharmacological activation could disrupt circadian homeostasis. While available data suggest that synthetic REV–ERB agonists can exert anti-inflammatory effects without overt circadian destabilization, long-term consequences of sustained REV–ERB activation, particularly within the CNS, remain insufficiently characterized. The extent to which therapeutic efficacy depends on temporally restricted dosing, or conversely induces circadian desynchrony across tissues, represents an important area for future investigation.

REV–ERB agonism also carries the inherent risk of on-target but unintended immunosuppression. Although attenuation of Th17-driven pathology is desirable in MS and MUO, REV–ERBs broadly regulate myeloid and lymphoid immune programs. Prolonged or systemic activation could therefore impair host defense mechanisms or alter immune surveillance within the CNS. Determining whether REV–ERB activation can achieve durable immune recalibration without compromising protective immunity will be critical for chronic disease applications, particularly in veterinary contexts where long-term safety thresholds are stringent.

Translational uncertainty is compounded by species-specific differences in immune architecture, REV–ERB expression patterns, and pharmacokinetic profiles. While murine models have provided compelling proof-of-principle, extrapolation to canine MUO and human MS is nontrivial. The lack of validated pharmacodynamic biomarkers that report REV–ERB target engagement in peripheral immune cells or within the CNS further complicates dose optimization and clinical interpretation. Development of such biomarkers will be essential to distinguish pharmacological insufficiency from biological resistance in early-phase studies.

Despite these limitations, the therapeutic potential of REV–ERB agonists remains substantial. Their ability to simultaneously modulate adaptive and innate immune pathways, coupled with mechanistic links to circadian regulation, suggests that they may complement or surpass existing immunomodulatory strategies. Notably, the parallel relevance of these pathways in human and veterinary neuroinflammatory disease highlights the potential for bidirectional translational insight, positioning canine MUO as a valuable comparative model for autoimmune CNS pathology.

While selective immune modulation via REV–ERB activation offers a promising alternative to broad immunosuppression, the long-term impact of chronic activation on protective immunity and baseline immune homeostasis requires careful evaluation. Because REV–ERB receptors natively operate within a dynamic diurnal framework, continuous, around-the-clock pharmacological activation risks disrupting the natural oscillations necessary for robust immune surveillance, cell trafficking, and pathogen clearance. Translating these findings to clinical settings will likely necessitate pulsatile or time-dependent dosing strategies. By restricting targeting receptor activation to specific temporal windows rather than maintaining chronic, static engagement, it may be possible to suppress pathological inflammation while preserving the host’s underlying protective immune architecture.

To successfully translate selective immune-modulating therapies like REV–ERB agonists into veterinary clinical trials, establishing robust, minimally invasive immunological biomarkers is critical for assessing target engagement and therapeutic efficacy. Future veterinary studies should utilize a longitudinal approach combining systemic, cellular, and molecular readouts. At the systemic level, serial profiling of key proinflammatory cytokines natively suppressed by REV–ERB activation, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, alongside species-specific acute-phase proteins (e.g., C-reactive protein in dogs or serum amyloid A in cats and horses) can provide real-time metrics of anti-inflammatory efficacy. At the cellular level, peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) immunophenotyping via flow cytometry allows researchers to monitor shifts in leukocyte populations, tracking whether therapy successfully limits the activation or trafficking of pathogenic myeloid and T-cell subsets. Finally, direct target engagement can be verified by monitoring the expression kinetics of core clock genes and downstream metabolic transcripts within circulating immune cells. Implementing this multi-tiered biomarker framework will be essential for optimizing dosing schedules, identifying therapeutic windows, and predicting long-term clinical outcomes in companion animals.

Conclusion

In summary, REV–ERB-specific synthetic agonists, by tempering the activity of Th17 cells and modulating both innate and adaptive immunity, hold potential as innovative therapeutics for canine MUO and analogous autoimmune CNS diseases. Their success in suppressing pathologic immune responses in animal models, most notably EAE, combined with favorable safety and circadian stability profiles in other species, sets the stage for continued investigation for use in veterinary immune-mediated disease. The potential for such therapies to inform strategies applicable to both human and veterinary neurology further emphasizes their relevance across medical disciplines. Future work should concentrate on comprehensive preclinical to clinical translation, with the goal of improved outcomes and quality of life for affected patients.
