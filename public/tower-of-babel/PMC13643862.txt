Socioeconomic mobility and the prevalence and incidence of difficulties in daily living in US Hispanic/Latino adults
Authors: Wenyan Ma, Paola Filigrana, Robert C Kaplan, Ryung S Kim, Christina Cordero, Martha L Daviglus, Linda C Gallo, Pablo Martinez Amezcua, Kunihiro Matsushita, Amber Pirzada, Daniela Sotres-Alvarez, Jennifer A Schrack, Carmen R Isasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:The objective of the study is to examine the prevalence and incidence of difficulties in daily living and examine their relationship with socioeconomic mobility, defined as transitions across different levels of socioeconomic position (SEP) across the life course, in a diverse group of US Hispanics/Latinos.Methods:We analyzed data from Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL) collected at Visit 2 (2014–2017, N = 11,446) and Visit 3 (2020–2024, N = 5568). Childhood and adulthood SEP indices were combined into four socioeconomic mobility categories: enduring adversity, downward mobility, upward mobility, and enduring advantage. Difficulties in daily living were categorized into four domains (sensory, cognition, mobility, independent living) with overall difficulties defined as having any difficulty.Results:Compared to those in the enduring advantage group, those in the enduring adversity group (OR=1.75 95% CI: 1.40–2.18) and the downward mobility group (OR=1.40; 95% CI: 1.07–1.84) were associated with greater odds of overall difficulties in daily living. Prospectively, those in the downward mobility group were associated with a higher rate of incident difficulties over 6 years (IRR=1.05, 95% CI: 1.00–1.11) compared to those in the enduring advantage group.Conclusions:Socioeconomic mobility is related to difficulties in daily living in US Hispanics/Latinos cross-sectionally and prospectively.Keywords: Life course, Healthy aging, Social determinants

Introduction

Disability is often conceptualized as a limitation or an inability to perform an activity, arising from the interaction between functional limitations (e.g., being unable to walk) and an unaccommodating environment (e.g., a lack of ramps for wheelchair access) [1,2]. In the US, up to 27% of adults have some type of disability [3]. Previously reported from the 2016 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), the prevalence of having any disability reported in Hispanic/Latino adults was 17.6% among 18–44 year olds, 35.5% in 45–64 year olds, and 50.5% among those aged 65 years or older [4]. Compared with non-Hispanic Whites, Hispanic/Latino adults report greater inability to perform activities of daily living and cognitive disabilities [5,6].

Socioeconomic status (SES) is an important determinant of disability. Studies showed that adults with higher SES (measured by education, occupation, and income) are less likely to experience disability, with lower education associated with poorer physical and cognitive function [7–9]. Furthermore, those reporting childhood adversity, low adult SES, or both are more likely to have work disability [10]. However, few studies have examined how socioeconomic mobility across the life course is associated with disability among US Hispanic/Latino adults. Moreover, most previous studies have relied on cross-sectional measures of SES and have not accounted for socioeconomic transitions across the lifespan. Life course epidemiology offers a unique framework to study the timing, accumulation, and interrelation between exposures across the lifespan and later-life health outcomes [11,12]. Examining socioeconomic mobility across the life course may provide a more comprehensive understanding of how accumulated and changing socioeconomic position (SEP) shape difficulties in daily living among US Hispanic/Latino adults and help identify populations at increased risk for disability.

This study from the Hispanic Community Health Survey/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL) aims to enhance our understanding of disability, defined here as difficulties in daily living, among adults from diverse Hispanic/Latino backgrounds. We use 'difficulties in daily living' to emphasize perceived functional challenges rather than a formal diagnosis or performance-based measures to better reflect individuals’ lived experiences. This functional perspective aligns with the World Health Organization’s move towards assessing the impact of health status on participation in everyday life instead of relying solely on clinical diagnosis [13]. Evaluating how life course socioeconomic mobility shape difficulties in daily living will offer actionable evidence to design targeted public policies, such as economic stability initiatives across the lifespan, that mitigate the risk of functional deficits and promote healthy aging among diverse US Hispanic/Latino adults.

Methods

Study population

HCHS/SOL is a longitudinal population-based cohort study with 16,415 participants aged 18–74 recruited from four field centers (Bronx, Miami, Chicago, and San Diego). Through 2024, the study completed three clinical examination visits (baseline visit: 2008–2011; Visit 2: 2014–2017, completed by 11,623 participants, and Visit 3: 2020–2024, completed by 9090 participants). This represents an attrition rate of 29% from baseline to Visit 2, and a further 22% attrition of the remaining Visit 2 participants by Visit 3. HCHS/SOL participants were selected using a probability sampling design within these four areas to provide a representative sample of the target population [14]. Participants’ difficulty status was assessed at Visit 2 and Visit 3 (difficulty status was not assessed at baseline). The prevalent analysis focuses on those who filled out the Participant Disability Screening form at Visit 2 (N = 11,446). For incidence analysis, we focused on those without any difficulty in daily living at Visit 2 who returned for an in-person Visit 3 (N = 5568). The study was reviewed and approved by HCHS/SOL single IRB, and each participating institution's review board and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measuring difficulties in daily living

The HCHS/SOL administered the Participant Disability Screening form at Visit 2 and 3, and most questions in the form are identical to the BRFSS disability questions [15]. Eight questions related to difficulties in daily living (e.g., hearing, vision, cognition, mobility) were included (Supplemental Table 1). We constructed a binary variable of overall difficulties in daily living. In addition, four domains related to sensory, mobility, independent living, and cognition were also derived, similar to the disability types used in the BRFSS and the American Community Survey [15,16]. The specific domains will shed light on how different difficulties are associated with socioeconomic mobility.

Measuring life course socioeconomic position (SEP) and socioeconomic mobility

Childhood SEP

SEP at the two time points, childhood and adulthood, was constructed individually. The participanťs highest parental education attainment reported at baseline was used as a proxy for childhood SEP, since other indicators such as household income during childhood were not collected. Those whose parents’ educational attainment was less than high school were considered to have low childhood SEP, while participants whose parents’ highest education level was high school or greater were considered to have high childhood SEP.

Adulthood SEP

Adulthood SEP was constructed as a composite index that considers participants’ self-reported educational attainment (less than high school vs. high school or above), annual household income (<$30,000 vs. $30,000 or more), home ownership (rent/reside without payment vs. own home with/without a mortgage), employment status (not currently employed vs. employed part-time, full-time, or retired), and long-term occupation type (the longest held occupational category classified as non-skilled, service, or other labor vs. skilled/professional labor) at baseline. The sum of these 5 binary indicators comprised the index of adulthood SEP with a range of 0–5 (median 2; interquartile range (IQR) 1–3). The index was further dichotomized to low adult SEP with an index score of 0–1 and high adult SEP with an index score of 2–5 [17–21].

Socioeconomic mobility

Socioeconomic mobility captured the transition of SEP between childhood and adulthood, and four combinations were created: enduring adversity (those with low SEP in childhood and adulthood), downward mobility (high childhood SEP and low adult SEP), upward mobility (low childhood SEP and high adult SEP), and enduring advantage (high SEP in childhood and adulthood) [17–21].

Covariates

Confounders were identified based on our conceptual framework linking life course SEP and difficulties in daily living, defined as variables influencing both exposure and outcome but not on the causal pathway. For childhood SEP, we included age (continuous), sex (male or female), Hispanic/Latino background (Mexican, Cuban, Puerto Rican, Dominican, Central American, South American, or more than one heritage/Other), and US nativity (born in the 50 US states/DC or elsewhere). For adult SEP, confounders included field center (Bronx, Chicago, Miami, or San Diego), marital status (married/living with partner, single, separated/divorced/widowed), and health insurance (yes/no). We also adjusted for behavioral factors at Visit 2 that may influence adult SEP and are associated with difficulties: cigarette use (never, former, or current), alcohol use (no current use, low use, or high use based on gender-specific weekly cutoffs), physical activity (low, moderate, or high based on self-reported intensity and duration), baseline Alternative Healthy Eating Index 2010 (AHEI2010), and depressive symptoms (CESD10).

The final model was additionally adjusted for health conditions linked to both socioeconomic mobility and difficulty status. These included hypertension (systolic or diastolic blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg or use of antihypertensive medication), diabetes (self-report, use of antidiabetic medication, or meeting American Diabetes Association laboratory criteria) [22], cardiovascular disease (CVD; history of heart attack, stroke, revascularization procedures, or prevalent CVD at HCHS/SOL baseline), class III obesity (BMI ≥40), low lung function (FEV1/FVC <0.70), chronic kidney disease (eGFR <60 or albumin-creatinine ratio ≥30), and self-reported use of medication for leg pain or peripheral artery disease.

Statistical analysis

We described the sociodemographic characteristics at Visit 2, as well as the prevalence of overall and domain-specific difficulties in daily living. Logistic regression assessed the cross-sectional relationship between socioeconomic mobility and overall difficulties in daily living. To explore potential within-group heterogeneity, we tested the interaction between Hispanic/Latino background and socioeconomic mobility in the model for prevalent overall difficulties in daily living. Stratified analyses were conducted when evidence of interaction was observed. For incident analyses among those free of difficulty at Visit 2 and who completed Visit 3, we used Poisson regression with robust standard errors. The log of years elapsed between Visit 2 and 3 was included as the offset term to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRR) of difficulties in daily living.

Given missing data on parental education (10%) and annual household income (6%), we conducted multiple imputation using sequential imputation with chained equations [23,24], including predictor variables of the sampling design, sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics, and the health factors of difficulties in daily living to generate 50 complete imputed datasets [25]. We fitted the regression models using imputed datasets and obtained pooled summary estimates and 95% CIs to estimate the associations accounting for the within-and-between-imputation variability.

All analyses accounted for HCHS/SOL’s complex survey sampling design. Cross-sectional analyses used Visit 2 sampling weights, whereas incidence analyses used Visit 3 sampling weights to account for the different response rates by visit. Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R/R Studio version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

For socioeconomic mobility, 16% experienced enduring adversity, 11% downward mobility, 32% upward mobility, and 41% enduring advantage.

At Visit 2 of HCHS/SOL, 31% of participants reported any difficulty in daily living, with mobility being the most common domain (21%) (Table 1). Females had a higher prevalence of overall and domain-specific difficulties. By Hispanic/Latino background groups, people of Puerto Rican descent had the highest prevalence of overall difficulties in daily living (49%), followed by Dominican individuals (38%), while people of Mexican descent (23%) and those identified as more than one heritage/Other (23%) had a lower prevalence of difficulties. Individuals born outside the US 50 states/DC experienced more overall difficulties. The prevalence of experiencing overall difficulties was higher among those with household income < $30,000 (41%), less than a high school education (43%), or separated/divorced/widowed status (45%). Among the insured, 36% reported difficulty compared to 20% of the uninsured. Over half of retired individuals reported overall difficulties.

In the multivariable logistic regression model (Fig. 1), compared to individuals in the enduring advantage group, those in the enduring adversity group had 1.75 times the odds of experiencing overall difficulties (95% CI: 1.40–2.18), and those in the downward mobility group also had elevated odds (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.07–1.84). Better diet quality, as measured by the AHEI2010, was inversely associated with overall difficulties (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.996). Depression symptoms, as measured by the CESD10 score, were positively associated with the odds of overall difficulties (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.07–1.10). Several health conditions were related to overall difficulties in daily living, including hypertension (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.07–1.51), diabetes (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.09–1.46), class III obesity (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.61–2.81), taking medication for leg pain (OR = 4.08, 95% CI: 2.93–5.70), and peripheral artery disease (OR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.36–3.95).

Socioeconomic mobility was associated with each of the four difficulty domains. Those in the enduring adversity or downward mobility groups had higher odds of experiencing difficulties in these domains compared to those in the enduring advantage group. Elevated depressive symptoms were consistently associated with higher odds of all four difficulty domains.

The cross-sectional associations between socioeconomic mobility and overall difficulties in daily living varied across Hispanic/Latino background groups (pinteraction = 0.052; Fig. 2). Among people of Cuban (OR = 2.48, 95% CI: 1.53–4.03) and Puerto Rican (OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.23–3.84), participants with downward mobility had higher odds of overall difficulty compared to those with enduring advantage, whereas among Mexicans, enduring adversity was associated with higher odds of difficulty (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.34–2.78).

Among the 5568 HCHS/SOL participants free of any difficulty at Visit 2, 1476 (21%, weighted) developed some type of difficulty between Visit 2 and Visit 3. The average follow-up time between visits was 6.4 years (standard deviation = 0.7), ranging from 3.7 to 9.2 years (median: 6.3; IQR: 5.9–6.8). In multivariable Poisson regression models estimating the IRR for incident difficulties (Fig. 3), individuals in the downward mobility group had a 5% higher rate of overall difficulties compared to those in the enduring advantage group (IRR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.00–1.11). The enduring adversity group had a 4% higher rate of difficulty in cognition (IRR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.07), while the downward mobility group also showed a 5% higher rate of mobility difficulty (IRR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.00–1.09), both relative to the enduring advantage group. As depression scores increased, the rate of overall difficulties and difficulties across most domains also increased. Comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and class III obesity were positively associated with experiencing overall difficulties after 6 years of follow-up.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to examine the prevalence and incidence of difficulties in daily living among a diverse group of Hispanic/Latino adults living in the US. Socioeconomic mobility over the life course was associated cross-sectionally with overall difficulties as well as the four domains of difficulty. Those in the enduring adversity group had higher odds of overall difficulties in daily living compared to those in the enduring advantage group. The cross-sectional association between life course socioeconomic mobility and overall difficulties in daily living is not uniform across all Hispanic/Latino subgroups. Incidence results showed that downward mobility was related to developing overall difficulties in daily living after the follow-up. The current study identified some behavioral and health factors associated with overall difficulties in daily living, including depression, diet quality, hypertension, diabetes, and class III obesity.

Previous HCHS/SOL studies revealed the close relationship between socioeconomic mobility with cardiovascular and brain health, and mortality, where those in the enduring advantage mobility group had a higher level of ideal cardiovascular health and cognitive function and bigger brain volume compared to those in the enduring adversity group while those in the enduring adversity and downward mobility groups faced increased hazard of mortality [17–21]. The current study found that those in the enduring adversity group had the highest odds of overall difficulties. Moreover, prospective analyses showed that the enduring adversity group was associated with higher rates of developing difficulty in cognition after 6 years of follow-up. Our results support an accumulation risk model [11,12], showing that persistent socioeconomic disadvantage from childhood to adulthood can have lasting health impacts. Hispanic/Latino individuals with consistently low SEP may face early-life exposures that contribute to lower education, limited economic resources, and increased psychological distress. We also found that those with upward mobility had higher odds of overall difficulties than those with enduring advantage, suggesting that moving away from a familiar disadvantaged socioeconomic origin may entail lasting psychological and physical effects even after achieving higher SEP in adulthood [26,27].

Overall, the association found in the current study for socioeconomic mobility and overall difficulties in daily living is consistent with previous studies. A Finnish study found that individuals with both childhood adversity and low adult SES had the highest risk of work disability, highlighting the lasting impact of cumulative adversity on health [10]. Similarly, analyses from the US Health and Retirement Study showed that disadvantaged childhood health and socioeconomic conditions were linked to greater functional limitations and steeper declines later in life, even after accounting for adult socioeconomic status [28]. Study using parental education status as the main exposure concluded that low parental education was associated with an increased risk of disability retirement due to any cause later in life [29]. Moreover, the prevalence of overall difficulties in daily living by age group found in the current study aligned with the disability prevalence of Hispanics from a study using 2016 BRFSS data [4].

In addition to socioeconomic mobility, the current study identified several behavioral and health factors that were associated with overall difficulties in daily living. Consistent with prior studies [30–33], we found that higher depressive symptoms were linked to both prevalent and incident difficulties in daily living, particularly with difficulties in cognition, aligning with its role as a diagnostic criterion for major depressive disorder [34]. Diet quality emerged as a protective factor, where individuals with higher AHEI2010 scores had lower odds of overall difficulties, similar to national data showing poorer diet quality among adults with disabilities [35,36]. Moreover, hypertension [37,38], diabetes [39–41], and class III obesity [42–44] were associated with greater odds of difficulties in daily living, consistent with prior evidence linking these chronic conditions to disability. These findings underscore the importance of addressing comorbidities and modifiable risk factors such as depressive symptoms, nutrition, and weight management to reduce disability burden in US Hispanics/Latinos through culturally appropriate strategies.

There are limitations in the analysis. We used parental education as a proxy for childhood SEP since other indicators, such as household income during childhood, were not collected. Parental education was collected retrospectively, and the participant’s difficulty status was self-reported, both of which may introduce recall bias. The difficulty-related questions in the current study primarily capture basic functional difficulties and it may not fully capture milder instrumental activities of daily living or performance-based limitations. Because HCHS/SOL participants were recruited from four large metropolitan areas, our findings may not be generalizable to Hispanics/Latinos residing in rural or non-urban communities, where the prevalence of disability has been reported to be higher [45]. In addition, although our analyses adjusted for health insurance status, we were unable to evaluate the influence of state-level differences in healthcare access and other contextual factors because HCHS/SOL represents four selected urban communities rather than entire states [14]. Future studies should examine how these broader structural factors, as well as chronic health conditions, mediate or modify the relationship between socioeconomic mobility and functional health. Lastly, since the HCHS/SOL cohort includes only individuals of Hispanic/Latino heritage which precludes direct comparisons with other racial or ethnic groups, future comparative research across diverse cohorts is warranted.

Despite these limitations, our study extends the literature on socioeconomic determinants of functional health by adopting a life-course perspective to examine healthy aging among US Hispanic/Latino adults. Although associations between conventional measures of SES and disability have been established, fewer studies have evaluated how socioeconomic challenges accumulated across the life course and socioeconomic mobility relate to later-life functional health in this population. To characterize adulthood SEP, we used a multidimensional composite measure incorporating house ownership, household income, occupational type, education, and employment status, providing a broader assessment of socioeconomic circumstances than a single indicator. By evaluating socioeconomic mobility and the differences across Hispanic/Latino background groups, this study highlights the importance of considering life course socioeconomic trajectories when investigating disparities in heathy aging among US Hispanic/Latino adults.

Conclusion

Socioeconomic mobility across the life course was associated with difficulties in daily living in the US Hispanic/Latino population. Those in the enduring adversity group had higher odds of overall difficulties in daily living. During the 6 years of follow-up, downward mobility was associated with a higher rate of overall difficulties. We identified several risk factors for overall difficulties, such as depression, hypertension, diabetes, and class III obesity. By demonstrating that difficulties in daily living are shaped by lifetime socioeconomic trajectories, this study underscores the importance of policies that target educational equity, employment stability, and income security to promote healthy aging among US Hispanic/Latino adults.
