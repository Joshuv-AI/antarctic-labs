A case of endemic Kaposi sarcoma mimicking morphea
Authors: Dikra Suleyman George, Bizuayehu Goshime Haile, Edelawit Yitna Mengesha, Abenezer Sherbo Hayato, Hala Idris, Sana Kamboj, Hayley Braun, Aklilu Melaku Gebremariam, Tizita Yosef Kidane
Published: 2026

Introduction

Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is among the most common cancers in sub-Saharan Africa and is most represented in people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. It has been organized into 5 epidemiologic subtypes: classic, endemic, iatrogenic, epidemic (HIV-associated), and nonepidemic KS in HIV-negative men who have sex with men.1 In 2018, there were an estimated 42,000 new cases of KS and 20,000 deaths worldwide.2 Treatment of KS depends on the type and includes surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, biologic, and targeted therapy.2

Endemic KS largely affects children and male adults in sub-Saharan Africa, where human herpesvirus-8 is endemic.3 The age of onset in endemic KS is lower than that of classical KS, typically arising in the third or fourth decades of life.4 It is classified into benign nodular, aggressive, florid, and lymphadenopathic subtypes; however, a validated staging system has not yet been established.3,4 The benign nodular variant resembles classic KS in course and appearance; the localized aggressive form can involve fungating masses and invasion of deeper structures, such as soft tissue and bone, and the florid form presents with disseminated mucocutaneous and visceral involvement. These forms occur in African adults with a 17:1 male-to-female ratio in patients between the ages of 25 and 40 years.5 The most aggressive form is lymphadenopathic, which is often fatal within 2 years despite aggressive treatment. It typically affects children aged between 1 and 15 years and young adults with a 3:1 male-to-female ratio.5 Not accounted for in this classification is an additional presentation of pediatric endemic KS described in Malawi, which presents with lymphadenopathy and woody edema, commonly of a limb.6

We present an unusual case of truncal KS mimicking morphea in an adult patient in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. We have found no previous description of morphea-like KS in an individual who is HIV-negative.

Case report

A 28-year-old man who is HIV-negative presented with bilateral inguinal skin hardening of 4 months’ duration. Initially, the lesions were asymptomatic and small but later caused sensations of burning, tightness, and restricted movement. The patient also complained of 2 months of penile and scrotal swelling with a sensation of heaviness. The patient’s clinical history and physical examination suggested a differential diagnosis that included mycosis fungoides, morphea, and scleromyxedema. A 6-mm punch biopsy of the plaques on the right and left inguinal areas was performed. Histopathology demonstrated aggregates of neoformed blood vessels and irregular slit-like vascular spaces with variable caliber interstitially between collagen bundles through the reticular dermis associated with aggregates (nodules) of spindle cells at several loci. The vascular spaces contained red blood cells and were lined with spindle-shaped endothelial cells. A promontory sign was appreciated. Hallmark features of the clinically suspected differential of mycosis fungoides, morphea, and scleromyxedema, such as epidermotrophic infiltrate of abnormal T cells, diminished vasculature and adnexal structures, or mucin deposition, respectively, were not noted. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of KS was made (Fig 2). Laboratory investigation was unremarkable, with normal complete blood cell count and comprehensive metabolic panel. The patient was confirmed HIV-negative with 3 separate tests. Abdominopelvic ultrasound and chest x-ray were unremarkable. No occult blood was detected in the stool.

The patient was referred to oncology and received 19 cycles of paclitaxel every 3 weeks, with significant improvement of the symptoms, color, and induration of the KS, as seen in Fig 3.Fig 3Improvement in indurated plaques at 12 (left) and 14 (right) months of paclitaxel treatment.

Discussion

Our case presents an unusual variation of endemic KS: a morphea-like phenotype in an individual who is HIV-negative.

Our patient’s presentation of morphea-like changes, lymphadenopathy, HIV negativity, and good response to treatment does not fit neatly within any of the traditional categories of endemic KS. While pseudosclerosis secondary to lymphedema was considered, the significant response of sclerosis and scrotal edema to paclitaxel suggests that the lesion represented a primary KS malignancy. If the observed changes were due to pseudosclerotic infiltration secondary to lymphedema, we would have anticipated a much less robust response to chemotherapy. Interestingly, prior reports have described the pediatric variant of endemic KS in children who are HIV-negative as presenting with lymphadenopathy and woody edema, which may appear similar to our patient’s scrotal edema and inguinal woody induration, although our patient’s larger morphea-like plaques in a truncal distribution appear to be rare.6 It is further challenging to objectively compare the description of “woody edema.” In addition, the pediatric population in which this was reported is clinically distinct. The average age was 4 years old, 62% of these children had severe anemia, and woody edema was commonly found on extremities.

We further investigated overlapping sclerosing disorders and KS. Two cases of atypical paraneoplastic scleroderma in KS have been reported in the literature, although in these cases, the sclerosis was seen in addition to nodules of KS.7 Alternatively, histologic variants of KS characterized by increased fibrous tissue have been described in a case of KS mimicking nephrogenic systemic fibrosis.8 In systemic scleroderma, transforming growth factor-β and vascular endothelial growth factor overproduction stimulate fibroblast synthesis of excess collagen, leading to fibrosis.9 We suggest that the proangiogenic and inflammatory cascade from human herpesvirus-8 infection of fibroblasts and endothelial cells may mimic this process, leading to the morphea-like presentation of KS in our patient. Future studies are needed to shed light on this theory.
