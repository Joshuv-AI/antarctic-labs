Association of Perioperative Cannabis Use With Postoperative Opioid Consumption in Ankle Fracture Patients
Authors: Harrison Volaski, Ofir Horovitz, Jennifer Jiang, Mohammed Bashier, Vishali Sehgal, Leila Mehraban Alvandi, Pamela Zuckerman, Yungtai Lo, Lauren Crocco, Huai M. Phen, Mani Kahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perioperative cannabis use was not an independent predictor for postoperative complications or opioid use duration in patients undergoing open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) ankle surgery.

Management of acute postoperative pain has become a focus for the orthopaedic community in recent years, with nearly 20% of patients self-reporting severe pain within the first 24 hours after their surgical procedures and less than half of orthopaedic surgical patients reporting adequate postoperative pain relief.1 Studies have correlated poorly controlled postoperative pain with increased postoperative morbidity, functional impairment, delayed recovery, and higher overall healthcare cost.2 Although opioids remain commonplace for postoperative pain management of orthopaedic procedures, concerns about opioid-related complications have drawn notable attention.2 Notably, opioid analgesia in opioid-naïve patients carries a risk of persistent use and dependence.3,4

The nationwide opioid abuse epidemic has prompted increased efforts to identify alternative pain management strategies that minimize opioid reliance. Cannabis has been proposed as one such strategy. Over the past several decades, medicinal and recreational cannabis has become more accessible. Preinjury cannabinoid use may influence postoperative opioid consumption and overall recovery. There is conflicting literature regarding cannabinoid usage and perioperative pain management. Several studies have found that patients with reported marijuana usage require increased opioid analgesic medications in the perioperative period.5,6 Other studies indicate a reduction in chronic pain and decreased use of opiates among cannabis users.7 The goal of this study was to conduct a comprehensive comparison of patient characteristics and postoperative outcomes between those who self-report perioperative cannabis use and those who do not, specifically focusing on postoperative pain and opioid demand after ankle open reduction and internal fixation.

Methods

Adult patients who underwent open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of traumatic ankle fractures from 2016 to 2022 by five orthopaedic surgeons at one academic institution were included in this study. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval (2022-1445) was obtained before study initiation. Patients were contacted sequentially by the telephone numbers listed in their electronic medical records, and IRB-approved, oral informed consent was obtained for enrollment. Phone interviews were conducted between December 2023 and May 2024. Patients who consented were interviewed on whether they consumed cannabis in any form during the perioperative period. Cannabis use was self-reported and collected retrospectively during the phone interview. The interview did not incorporate structured questions regarding frequency, dosage, formulation, method of administration, or duration of preoperative use. No distinction was made between recreational and medicinal use.

Patients younger than age 18 years at the time of surgery and those with speech or intellectual disabilities that precluded their ability to give informed oral consent were excluded from this study. No patients were excluded based on cannabis usage patterns or concurrent substance use because these data were not systematically collected.

Demographic variables of interest included patient sex, age at the time of injury, body mass index (BMI) at the time of injury, insurance type, education level, employment status, perioperative tobacco use, and mental health history. Comorbidities other than diabetes and BMI, as well as concurrent medication use, were not captured in this data set. To account for systemic factors affecting patient health, we included the Distressed Community Index (DCI), which considers unemployment, education, poverty rates, median income, business growth, and housing vacancies by zip code (Figure 1). Patients' DCI scores, ranging from 0 (no distress) to 100 (severe distress), were obtained from the DCI database based on their ZIP code. Clinical characteristics such as surgery setting and hospital length of stay were also recorded.

Diagram illustrating the components of the Distressed Communities Index (DCI). The diagram outlines the key factors including economic, educational, and social indicators, which are combined to form the overall index score.

Postoperative outcomes of interest included infections, delayed unions, unplanned emergency department (ED) visits, and unplanned returns to the operating room (OR). Postoperative pain assessments included Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores at first postoperative office visits, opioid use duration, daily peak morphine milligram equivalent (MME) doses, need for pain management referrals, and incidence of chronic pain syndrome. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (for VAS scores) as appropriate. Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. Group comparisons used chi-square or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. A generalized linear model (GLM) with a log-link function and Gaussian distribution was used to examine the association of cannabis use with postoperative opioid duration adjusting for preoperative smoking, age, and sex. Statistical analysis was done using SPSS software. Statistically significant data were defined as P ≤ 0.05.

Results

In total, 561 patients were contacted by phone, and of those, 259 patients consented to phone interviews and were sequentially enrolled in this study (Figure 2). Of them, 180 (69.50%) denied cannabis use during the perioperative period, while 79 (30.50%) self-reported cannabis use.

Diagram showing patient enrollment through phone interview.

When comparing the two cohorts, we found significant differences in several demographics (Table 1). Cannabis users were more likely to be male compared with nonusers (57.0% vs 36.1%, P = 0.003) and were predominantly younger (P < 0.001). A greater proportion of cannabis users identified as Black or African American (45.2%) relative to nonusers (26.9%), while the proportion of Hispanic individuals was similar between groups (P = 0.012). Although approximately half of the patients in both groups had not completed high school, cannabis users were less likely to hold a college degree (13.2% vs 29.8%) (P = 0.011). Cannabis users were also more likely to reside in highly distressed communities, with 69.6% living in a zip code with a DCI score greater than 75 compared with 52.5% of nonusers (P = 0.010). Perioperative tobacco use was more common among cannabis users (35.4%) than nonusers (16.1%) (P = <0.001). We found no statistically significant differences for medical interpreter needs, employment status, insurance type, BMI at the time of injury, diabetes, anxiety, and depression (Table 1). Our findings also showed no significant differences in surgery setting or hospital length of stay (Table 2).

Discussion

The primary purpose of this study was to assess postoperative pain management and complications of participants who underwent ankle fracture ORIF based on self-reported perioperative cannabis use. Our study found notable differences in the duration of opioid use, with cannabis users experiencing a longer postoperative average. However, there were no notable differences in other pain management factors, nor postoperative outcomes, including infections, delayed unions, unplanned ED visits, and unplanned revision surgeries.

Importantly, after adjusting for confounders, we found that cannabis use was not independently associated with prolonged opioid use. By contrast, we found that tobacco use was a notable predictor. Our findings suggest that although cannabis use may be associated with certain demographic and behavioral patterns, it was not an independent predictor of postoperative pain or surgical outcomes in this cohort.

Demographics

Our findings demonstrated that perioperative cannabis use was associated with younger age, male sex, certain races/ethnicities, decreased college degree attainment, residing in zip codes with higher DCI scores, and higher rates of concurrent tobacco use. Age may play a role in pain perception because younger patients may have different coping mechanisms for pain or may be more inclined toward self-reported cannabis use for pain relief. In addition, socioeconomic factors associated with higher DCI scores could influence both cannabis use and access to postoperative care. Anatomic, physiologic, and hormonal factors, along with cultural norms, may affect pain perception because male and female individuals have been shown to experience and report pain differently.8 Although these demographic factors were markedly associated with cannabis use, they may reflect broader patterns of substance use and social determinants of health rather than causal pathways. Our adjusted analyses demonstrate that these differences do not explain the postoperative outcome disparities.

Postoperative Pain Management

In recent years, medical cannabis has emerged as an alternative approach to postoperative pain management, especially in the chronic setting. In the United States, 10% of cannabis users report using it for the management of a medical condition.9 A multicenter questionnaire-based prospective cohort study by Aviram et al7 showed a 42% decrease in daily opioid demand among chronic pain patients treated with medical cannabis, as well as a notable decline in pain intensity among those same patients. In contrast to these results, our study found that general perioperative cannabis use was associated with longer mean durations of opioid use compared with nonusers, despite no notable differences in reported pain scores. However, after adjusting for confounders, we found that cannabis use was not independently associated with postoperative opioid use duration. This suggests that the relationship between cannabis use and opioid consumption may be influenced by other patient-level factors rather than a direct analgesic effect of cannabis.

Other studies have reported that opioid-naïve surgical patients who use cannabis have a higher risk of opioid dependence and consumption after orthopaedic surgeries compared with nonusers.5,10 Interestingly, one of those studies also found that cannabis use was associated with increased time-weighted average postoperative pain scores.5 However, these findings are not universally consistent in the literature. Interstudy variations may reflect variability in self-reported pain and cannabis use, which are subject to individual perceptions and placebo effects, making it hard to isolate a true drug effect.5 Institutional prescribing practices, variations in cannabis dosing between medical and recreational use, and differences in the surgical procedures studied may also contribute to this.

Recent studies have also suggested that the analgesic potential of cannabis depends heavily on its formulation and dosing. A scoping review of 14 experimental orthopaedic studies found that combined usage of tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD) demonstrated modest opioid-sparing effects with limited adverse effects.11 However, CBD-only interventions showed mixed results, and the single THC-only study that reported increased opioid use and hospital stays was influenced by confounding.11 Another systematic review of 33 studies in orthopaedic cannabis pain management found that noncontrolled studies generally reported positive outcomes, while controlled studies yielded mixed results.12 They also noted that higher-dose cannabis interventions were more likely to report analgesic efficacy but carried increased adverse effect risks.12 This heterogeneity in cannabis composition, dose, and study design limits cross-study comparability, underscoring the need for additional investigation on the influence of specific formulation and dose-response relationships on orthopaedic recovery.

Interestingly, only 29.1% of cannabis users in our study reported that cannabis was helpful for their postoperative pain relief. The subjectivity of this reporting must be considered because self-reported pain relief can be influenced by placebo effects, pain thresholds, and individual perceptions and expectations of cannabis pain management efficacy. Our patient interviews, which were conducted by phone, may have also introduced reporting bias because patients with neutral or negative experiences may have underreported. Notably, a survey of 500 orthopaedic patients with traumatic musculoskeletal injuries by Bhashyam et al13 found marijuana use during recovery to correlate with an increase in both the postoperative opioid amount and duration of use. This finding contrasted with the surveyed patients' perceptions that their use of cannabis had reduced their pain levels. Bhashyam et al13 results underscore the notable variability in individual responses to cannabis and highlight a potential critical disconnect between patient perception and objective outcomes. Since the adverse psychotropic effects of cannabis have limited its capacities as a pharmacologic agent, additional studies are warranted in characterizing the role of cannabinoids in pain management.14 Collectively, our findings challenge the notion that perioperative cannabis use is universally beneficial for postoperative recovery and emphasize the need for more personalized and evidence-based approaches to pain management in surgical patients.

Postoperative Complications

In contrast to the differences observed in opioid use, our study found no notable associations between cannabis consumption and rates of adverse postoperative events, including unplanned ED visits, infections, delayed unions, or unplanned revision surgeries. Our findings are echoed by several recent studies on this topic. For example, Jennings et al15 found that cannabis users undergoing total knee arthroplasty experienced neither adverse nor beneficial outcomes compared with nonusers.

On the other hand, a retrospective cohort analysis of a nationwide database by Chiu et al16 found cannabis-using spine surgery patients to experience higher rates of perioperative thromboembolic and neurologic complications. They also found higher rates of postoperative respiratory complications and sepsis, as well as increased lengths of hospital stay and increased rates of unfavorable discharge disposition.16 The inconsistency in findings across studies may be due to differences in the surgical procedures assessed. Certain surgeries may be more invasive, have greater risks of complications, or require longer recovery periods, all of which could influence patients' postoperative outcomes. Hence, based on our data, perioperative cannabis use may not be associated with delayed wound healing or surgical complications after ankle fracture ORIF, at least in the short term.

Other studies have focused more specifically on cannabis' potential associations with fracture healing. A population study by Heath et al17 found cannabis-using patients to have lower bone mineral densities, be at increased risk for fractures, and have higher risks for delayed fracture healing. Despite these physiologic findings, a national survey of more than 4000 US adults by Bourne et al18 found no associations between self-reported cannabis use and bone mineral density. Although our study did not directly assess fracture healing in postoperative outcomes, other than no difference in delayed union rates between the two groups, the conflicting literature highlights a need for additional research on cannabis's potential influence on postoperative patient outcomes for orthopedic trauma patients.

Given the rising prevalence of cannabis use, understanding its influences on patients' recovery is crucial.5 A recent anonymous survey of Orthopedic Trauma Association (OTA) members found that most of the orthopaedic traumatologists (73%) felt cannabis plays a role in surgical patients' postoperative pain relief.19 However, most of them (88%) did not feel themselves to be knowledgeable about the pharmacologic actions of cannabinoids.19 This further highlights the need for additional research, education, and policy development on cannabis use in postoperative settings. Our study contributes to this growing body of knowledge by offering insights into perioperative cannabis use among orthopedic trauma patients undergoing ankle fracture ORIF.

Cannabis and Tobacco Use

Despite increasing cannabis use in the general population, the effects of cannabis consumption on tobacco smokers remain poorly understood. Our findings demonstrated that perioperative cannabis use was associated with increased rates of concurrent tobacco use. More importantly, the GLM revealed that tobacco use, not cannabis use, was independently associated with a longer postoperative opioid duration even after adjusting for sex and age. This suggests that tobacco use may be a more potent and direct risk factor for adverse postoperative outcomes than cannabis use alone.

This pattern aligns with current literature. For example, Albelo et al20 found that among 1170 patients undergoing orthopaedic surgery, recreational cannabis use was associated with worse mental health scores, decreased pain relief, and lower satisfaction. However, similar to our findings, cannabis use was not predictive of these outcomes after adjusting for variables including smoking.20 Similarly, Dhodapkar et al21 examined adverse events in patients undergoing ankle fracture ORIF. Their study demonstrated that tobacco use (alone or with cannabis) was associated with markedly higher odds of 90-day adverse events such as readmissions and urinary tract infections, whereas cannabis-only users were not.21 Although their study did not assess postoperative pain management, their use of multivariate logistic regression reinforces our finding that tobacco is an independent predictor.

In addition, a large retrospective database study of more than 40,000 elective lumbar fusion patients by Barkay et al22 identified a negative synergistic effect between cannabis and tobacco use in bone fusion rates and higher odds of postoperative complications (such as venous thromboembolism and myocardial infarction) among dual users. Although our study did not find differences in delayed union between our cohorts, nor did we directly assess the effect of concurrent tobacco smoking on fusion, these findings underscore the potential for combined substance use to amplify surgical risk. Hence, these results reinforce the importance of distinguishing between cannabis and tobacco use in perioperative risk assessments. Tobacco use seems to be the more consistent independent predictor of prolonged opioid use. Additional studies should explore the substance-specific interactions between cannabis, tobacco, and other medications to better understand their effect on postoperative recovery in orthopedic trauma patients.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of the study and the reliance on self-reported cannabis use introduce potential recall and social desirability biases, which could influence the accuracy of reported cannabis usage. Selection bias may also be present because only those patients who could be contacted by phone and consented were included, potentially excluding others who underwent ankle fracture ORIF at this institution or sought surgical care elsewhere. This selection bias could limit the generalizability of the findings. The study may also be subject to response bias because patients with greater postoperative pain or complications could have been more likely to participate. In addition, the short-term nature of this study's follow-up limits our ability to assess the effects of cannabis on chronic pain or long-term outcomes.

In addition, our study is powered to detect only moderate-to-large differences and may be underpowered for smaller differences. This could explain borderline P-values for variables such as delayed union (≈11-18 percentage points, depending on baseline risk). Our multivariate analyses may also be affected by residual confounding because of unmeasured confounders such as polypharmacy and other comorbidities, and small sample sizes in categorical groups, resulting in wide confidence intervals and potential sparse-data bias.

Finally, cannabis use was not standardized across our patient population, with variability in dose, frequency, duration, formulation, and method of administration. This heterogeneity may have influenced postoperative pain outcomes. These factors, combined with differences in study design and patient populations in the literature, may contribute to inconsistencies observed across previous studies. As such, our results should be interpreted as exploratory rather than conclusive.

Conclusion

Although perioperative pain continues to be a notable topic of discussion within the orthopaedic community, there seems to be conflicting evidence regarding the growing use of cannabinoids and their effects on perioperative pain in the orthopaedic literature. This retrospective cohort study compares postoperative outcomes of ankle fracture patients based on their self-reported cannabis use during their perioperative periods. Overall, we find no notable associations between perioperative cannabis use and postoperative complications. Although there was a statistically significant association between cannabis use and postoperative opioid use length, cannabis was not independently associated after adjusting for confounding. Our findings do not support cannabis as an effective standalone analgesic in this population, given the lack of independent association with pain outcomes and our patients' limited self-reported benefit. With the increasing popularity of cannabis use among our orthopedic trauma patients, additional investigations are warranted for better postoperative management and improved postsurgical outcomes.
