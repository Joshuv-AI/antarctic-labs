Neural network parametrized level sets for image segmentation
Otmar Scherzer otmar.scherzer@univie.ac.at, Cong Shi cong.shi@univie.ac.at, Thi Lan Nhi Vu thi.lan.nhi.vu@univie.ac.at

Abstract
The Chan-Vese functionals have proven to by a first-class method for segmentation and classification. Previously they have been implemented with level-set methods based on a pixel-wise representation of the level-sets. Later parametrized level-set approximations, such as splines, have been studied. In this paper we consider neural networks as parametrized approximations of level-set functions. We show in particular, that parametrized two-layer networks are most efficient to approximate polyhedral segments and classes. We also prove the efficiency for segmentation and classification.

1. Introduction

Image segmentation
and
classification
aims to partition an image into regions of desired properties. Accurate segmentation and classification plays a crucial role in many areas of applications such as object recognition, medical diagnosis, autonomous driving, and scene understanding, to name but a few.

Basis for this work are the variational Chan-Vese
segmentation

[
4
]
and

classification

[
14
]
methods. In these approaches, piecewise constant functions are computed that minimize an energy functional. The regions of constant intensity are the segments and classes to be detected. The theoretical foundations of the Chan-Vese model date back to the seminal work of Mumford & Shah
[
10
]
and Morel & Solimini
[
9
]
. In computational practice, the Chan-Vese functionals are often minimized via a level-set approach, where the segments and classes are represented by the level-sets. level-set methods, originally introduced by Osher & Sethian
[
11
]
, have proven a powerful approach to solve image segmentation and classification tasks, because the implicit representation of segments and classes allows to find objects of complex topologies, such as, for instance, segments consisting of multiple connected domains. There exist two categories of level-set algorithms (see
Figure 1
):

Unparametrized
level-set algorithms consider the input images and the level-set functions, from which segments and classes are detected, pixel-wise. There, the data are represented as a vector of pixel values.

Parametrized
algorithms (see, for instance,
[
15
,
7
,
2
,
12
]
) use ansatz-functions (such as splines or finite elements) to represent the level-sets and input images. This means, in particular, that the segments and classes are specified
double implicitly
: as the zero level-sets of the representing functions (first), which in turn are represented via the parametrization (second).

The different concepts can be compared with
finite difference
and
finite element
methods for solving differential equations: The latter represents the solution of the differential equation implicitly by finite element parametrizations, wheareas the former are given explicitly as function values on a grid. In fact,
hybrid
level-set algorithms combining the advantages of both parametrized and unparametrized strategies have also been developed (see
[
8
]
).

Figure 1
:
Commutative diagram illustrating the relationships among the classical, parametrized, and smooth Chan–Vese models. The classical (unparametrized) model can be approximated by the parametrized non-smooth model, which can then be approximated by the smooth variant.

The achievements of this paper are as follows:

(i)

We propose a
parametrized
Chan-Vese method for image segmentation and classification, where we use
deep neural networks
for parametrization of the level-set functions. The efficiency of neural networks is unquestioned nowadays, but there remains a lack of transparency regarding why this is the case and how they should be parametrized (that means, which networks one should implement)? Such a motivation is the first goal of this paper: We give illustrative examples that demonstrates why (deep) two-hidden-layer networks efficiently represent segments and classes (see
Section 2
) defined from level-sets. This result puts the mathematical foundations of level-set methods and neural networks in perspective (see
Section 3
).

(ii)

We use these insights to provide an efficient neural network parametrized Chan-Vese algorithm for segmentation and classification (see
Section 4
). We note that by classification, we mean determining which object a pixel in the image belongs to. This differs from the terminology commonly used in the machine learning community, where classification usually refers to assigning a category label to an entire image.

We begin by reviewing the Chan–Vese segmentation and classification models (see

We present a unified formulation of Chan-Vese segmentation and classification:
Let
m\in\mathds{N}
be given and let
\Omega\subseteq\mathds{R}^{2}
, which is partitioned into at most

\left\{\Xi_{\imath}:\imath\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m}\right\}\;,

such that the intersection of any two subregions

either has positive Lebesgue measure or they are disjoint components, that is, for all
\imath\neq\imath^{\prime}
we have

\mu(\Xi_{\imath}\cap\Xi_{\imath^{\prime}})>0\quad\text{or}\quad\Xi_{\imath}\cap\Xi_{\imath^{\prime}}=\emptyset.

When

\left\{-1,1\right\}

corresponds to a segment and
\Xi_{-1}
corresponds to the region outside the segment. Analogously, when

\left\{(1,1),(1,-1),(-1,1),(-1,-1)\right\}

The Chan-Vese model is designed to approximate a given function
{\tt f}:\Omega\to\mathds{R}

by a multiphase function

{\tt m}({\vec{x}})=\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}c_{\imath}\chi_{\Xi_{\imath}}({\vec{x}})\;.

(3.1)

This is achieved by minimizing the Chan-Vese functional

\mathrm{CV}_{\tt f}(\{(c_{\imath},\Xi_{\imath})\}_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}})=\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}\int_{\Xi_{\imath}}\left({\tt m}-{\tt f}\right)^{2}d{\vec{x}}+\mu\hskip-10.34958pt\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Xi_{\imath})+\nu\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\bigcup_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}\Xi_{\imath}\right).

(3.2)

3.1
.

Since the one-dimensional measures of all
\partial\Xi_{\imath}
are penalized, each
\Xi_{\imath}
must be a set of finite perimeter (a Caccioppoli set), meaning that
\partial\Xi_{\imath}
is measurable with finite one-dimensional measure. Moreover, the main theorem of
[
9
, Chapter 5]
guarantees the existence of a segmentation
K:=\{\partial\Xi_{\imath}\}_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}
that minimizes the functional
\mathrm{CV}_{\tt f}
. The minimizer is not unique (see
[
9
, Chapter 15]
). Furthermore, the theorem states that each
\partial\Xi_{\imath}
of this minimizer is either a regular
C^{1}
curve, or a piecewise regular
C^{1}
curve with singular points consisting of triple junctions, where three branches meet at
120^{o}
angles, or boundary points where
K
meets
\partial\Omega
at
90^{o}
angles. This suggests that the problem can be studied within a (piecewise)
C^{1}
framework, which is convenient for variational approximation results such as
\Gamma
-convergence, ensuring the convergence of minimizers of approximate energies used in numerical applications (see
3.8
).

3.1. Numerical minimization of the Chan-Vese functional

can be represented as a

(see

3.2
Multiphase level-set function.

Let
m\in\mathds{N}
(see the example of triangle

For every

{\ell}_{k}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
be a
level-set function
, which means that it satisfies

\displaystyle H_{k}^{1}=\left\{{\vec{x}}\in\Omega:{\ell}_{k}({\vec{x}})>0\right\}\text{ with }\mathcal{H}^{2}(H_{k}^{1})<\infty\text{ and }

\displaystyle H_{k}^{-1}=\left\{{\vec{x}}\in\Omega:{\ell}_{k}({\vec{x}})<0\right\}\text{ and }

(3.3)

\displaystyle\partial H_{k}^{1}=\{{\vec{x}}\in\Omega:\ell_{k}({\vec{x}})=0\}\text{ with }\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial H_{k}^{1})<\infty.

H_{k}^{\pm 1}
represents two classes. In the language of level-set methods,
item
means that the
0
-level-set is not fat. We emphasize that a distance function of a domain is a typical example of a level-set function.

A
multiphase level-set function of degree
2^{m}
is a
L^{2}(\Omega)
function of the form

\displaystyle{\tt m}({\vec{x}})=\hskip-10.34958pt\sum_{\imath=(\imath_{1},\ldots,\imath_{m})\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}\hskip-6.90147ptc_{\imath}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{k}{\ell}_{k}({\vec{x}}))\;.

(3.4)

We use the same notation as in
2.3
, but note that here, the level-set function
{\ell}
is an arbitrary function, while in
2.3
the level-set function is
{\tt a}
, which is affine linear. Since we assumed that
\Omega
is bounded,
{\tt m}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is piecewise constant and attains values
c_{\imath}\in\mathds{R}
on the interior of at most
2^{m}
regions
S_{\imath}
,
\imath\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m}
, respectively. That is,

{\tt m}({\vec{x}})=\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}c_{\imath}\chi_{S_{\imath}}({\vec{x}})\text{ for almost all }{\vec{x}}\in\Omega\,,

where

\displaystyle S_{\imath}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}\left\{{\vec{x}}\in\Omega:\imath_{k}{\ell}_{k}({\vec{x}})>0\right\},\quad\quad\bigcup_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}\partial S_{\imath}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\{{\vec{x}}\in\Omega:\ell_{k}({\vec{x}})=0\}.

(3.5)

3.3
.

According to
Definition 3.2
, if we aim to identify
s

\lceil\log_{2}s\rceil
level-set functions, where
\lceil\cdot\rceil
denotes the smallest integer greater than or equal to its argument.

The Chan-Vese functional, defined in
Equation 3.2
, for minimizing piecewise constant functions
{\tt m}
, can be reformulated in terms of multiphase level-set functions (see
Definition 3.2
) as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}((c_{\imath})_{\imath\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m}},({\ell}_{k})_{k=1}^{m})

(3.6)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}\int_{\Omega}\left(c_{\imath}-{\tt f}({\vec{x}})\right)^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{k}{\ell}_{k}({\vec{x}}))\ d{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle+\mu\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\ell}_{k}=0\})+\nu\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\{{\ell}_{k}>0\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}\int_{\Omega}\left(c_{\imath}-{\tt f}({\vec{x}})\right)^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{k}{\ell}_{k}({\vec{x}}))\ d{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle+\mu\sum_{k=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\delta({\ell}_{k}({\vec{x}}))\left|\nabla{\ell}_{k}({\vec{x}})\right|\ d{\vec{x}}+\nu\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}(-1)^{k+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,m\}\\
\left|A\right|=k\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\prod_{k\in A}\sigma({\ell}_{k}({\vec{x}}))\ d{\vec{x}}\right).

Note that the length term
\delta({\ell}_{k})\left|\nabla{\ell}_{k}\right|
is understood in a distributional sense: it defines a one-dimensional measure supported on the zero level-set of
{\ell}_{k}
, integrating along the normal direction to the curve. Here, the one-dimensional Dirac mass
\delta
is the distributional derivative of the Heaviside function
\sigma
.

3.4
.

For
m=1
and
m=2
, the Chan-Vese functional looks as follows:

(i)

m=1
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}(c_{1},c_{-1},{\ell})=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}({\tt f}({\vec{x}})-c_{1})^{2}\sigma({\ell}({\vec{x}}))d{\vec{x}}+\int_{\Omega}({\tt f}({\vec{x}})-c_{-1})^{2}(1-\sigma({\ell}({\vec{x}})))d{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle+\mu\int_{\Omega}\delta({\ell})\left|\nabla{\ell}\right|d{\vec{x}}+\nu\int_{\Omega}\sigma({\ell}({\vec{x}}))d{\vec{x}}.

This is used for segmentation applications. In practice, the coefficients are customized to
c_{1}=1
and
c_{-1}=-1
.

(ii)

m=2
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}((c_{\imath}),{\ell}_{1},{\ell}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}({\tt f}({\vec{x}})-c_{1,1})^{2}\sigma({\ell}_{1}({\vec{x}}))\sigma({\ell}_{2}({\vec{x}}))d{\vec{x}}+\int_{\Omega}({\tt f}({\vec{x}})-c_{1,-1})^{2}\sigma({\ell}_{1}({\vec{x}}))(1-\sigma({\ell}_{2}({\vec{x}})))d{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}({\tt f}({\vec{x}})-c_{-1,1})^{2}(1-\sigma({\ell}_{1}({\vec{x}})))\sigma({\ell}_{2}({\vec{x}}))d{\vec{x}}+\int_{\Omega}({\tt f}({\vec{x}})-c_{-1,-1})^{2}(1-\sigma({\ell}_{1}({\vec{x}})))(1-\sigma({\ell}_{2}({\vec{x}})))d{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mu\int_{\Omega}\delta({\ell}_{1})\left|\nabla{\ell}_{1}\right|+\delta({\ell}_{2})\left|\nabla{\ell}_{2}\right|d{\vec{x}}+\nu\int_{\Omega}\sigma({\ell}_{1}({\vec{x}}))+\sigma({\ell}_{2}({\vec{x}}))-\sigma({\ell}_{1}({\vec{x}}))\sigma({\ell}_{2}({\vec{x}}))d{\vec{x}}\;.

Note that we have
\sigma(-x)=1-\sigma(x)
for all
x\in\mathds{R}
, therefore
(1-\sigma({\ell}_{i}))=\sigma(-{\ell}_{i})
for
i=1,2
.

Vese & Chan
[
14
]
suggested an evolution process, in which
({\ell}_{k}(t,{\vec{x}}))_{k=1}^{m}
and
(c_{\imath}(t))_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}
evolve with time

and, as

, approximate the minimizers
({\ell}_{k})_{k=1}^{m}
of
\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}
defined in
Equation 3.6
and the corresponding constants
(c_{\imath})_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}
. Concretely, starting from some initial level-sets
\left\{{\ell}_{k,0}=0:k=1,\ldots,m\right\}
and from the initial constant values
c_{\imath}(0)
on the level-sets, satisfying,

{\ell}_{k}(0,{\vec{x}})={\ell}_{k,0}({\vec{x}})\text{ for }{\vec{x}}\in\Omega\text{ and }c_{\imath}(0)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}{\tt f}({\vec{x}})\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{k}{\ell}_{k,0}({\vec{x}}))d{\vec{x}}}{\int_{\Omega}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{k}{\ell}_{k,0}({\vec{x}}))d{\vec{x}}}\,,

the evolution process is governed by the system

\begin{cases}\hskip 14.22636pt\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial{\ell}_{k}}{\partial t}(t,{\vec{x}})=\delta({\ell}_{k})(t,{\vec{x}})&\Biggl[\mu\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\nabla{\ell}_{k}}{\left|\nabla{\ell}_{k}\right|}\right)(t,{\vec{x}})\Biggr.\\
&-\nu\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,m\}\\
\left|A\right|=j\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in A\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\sigma({\ell}_{i})(t,{\vec{x}})\\
&\Biggl.-\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}\imath_{k}(c_{\imath}(t)-{\tt f}({\vec{x}}))^{2}\Biggr]\text{ for }(t,{\vec{x}})\in(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
&\hskip-99.58464pt\text{ together with boundary conditions}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\delta({\ell}_{k})(t,{\vec{x}})}{\left|\nabla{\ell}_{k}(t,{\vec{x}})\right|}\frac{\partial{\ell}_{k}}{\partial\vec{n}}(t,{\vec{x}})&=0\ \text{ for }(t,{\vec{x}})\in(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega,\\
&\hskip-99.58464pt\text{ and the evolution for }c_{\imath}:\\
c_{\imath}(t)&=\frac{\int_{\Omega}{\tt f}({\vec{x}})\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{k}{\ell}_{k}(t,{\vec{x}}))d{\vec{x}}}{\int_{\Omega}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{k}{\ell}_{k}(t,{\vec{x}}))d{\vec{x}}}\text{ for }t\in(0,\infty)\;.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(3.7)

Here, each
\ell_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,m
, evolves in the direction normal to its level-lines.

In contrast to the standard pixel-based formulation (unparametrized) of the Chan-Vese functional, see
Equation 3.6
,
we consider now a

Chan–Vese model, where neural networks are used to represent the piecewise constant segmentation functions

.

3.2. Neural networks parametrized Vese-Chan classification

We consider the Vese-Chan classification, where the level-set functions
\left\{{\ell}_{k}:k=1,\ldots,m\right\}
(see
Equation 3.4
) are approximated by one-layer Heaviside networks:

{\vec{x}}\in\Omega\subseteq\mathds{R}^{2}\mapsto{\tt m}_{\tt s}({\vec{x}}):=\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}c_{\imath}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{k}{\tt s}_{k}({\vec{x}})).\;

(3.8)

In comparison with
Equation 3.4
, we now have
\sigma
composed with
{\tt s}_{k}
instead of
{\ell}_{k}
,
where

{\tt s}_{k}:={\tt s}[\vec{a}_{k},{\bf W}_{k},\vec{b}_{k}]:=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}(\vec{a}_{k})_{j}\sigma(({\bf W}_{k})_{j}^{T}{\vec{x}}+(\vec{b}_{k})_{j})

is defined in
Equation 2.3
with
(\vec{a}_{k})_{j},(\vec{b}_{k})_{j}\in\mathds{R}
denoting the
j
-th elements of the vectors
\vec{a}_{k},\vec{b}_{k}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}}
, respectively, and
({\bf W}_{k})_{j}\in\mathds{R}^{2}
is the
j
-th column of the matrix
{\bf W}_{k}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}\times 2}
.

In this case, the corresponding regions
S_{\iota}
formed by
{\tt m}_{\tt s}
, as defined in
item
, are polygonal sets. Hence, we denote them by
P_{\iota}
:

{\tt m}_{\tt s}=\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}c_{\imath}\chi_{P_{\imath}}({\vec{x}}).

3.5
.

The one-layer network parametrized Chan-Vese functional is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}((c_{\imath})_{\imath},(\vec{a}_{k},{\bf W}_{k},\vec{b}_{k})_{k=1}^{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}\int_{\Omega}\left(c_{\imath}-{\tt f}({\vec{x}})\right)^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{k}{\tt s}_{k}({\vec{x}}))\ d{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle+\mu\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{{\tt s}_{k}=0\})+\nu\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\{{\tt s}_{k}>0\}\right).

(3.9)

The one-layer network parametrized Chan-Vese functional can be used for classification and segmentation: Instead of finding arbitrary level-set functions, which determine the classes of the function
{\tt f}
, by minimizing
\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}
from
Equation 3.6
, we constrain the optimization of the functional to polyhedral classes.

We recall that the maximum number of objects to be segmented or classified is
2^{m}
. The numbers of neurons
n_{1}\in\mathds{N}
corresponds to the number of polygonal edges in the polygonal approximation of a single object: larger values of
n_{1}
yield more accurate approximations (see
2.3
). Although
n_{1}
can vary between objects, for notational simplicity we assume, that all objects are approximated by polygons with the same number of edges
n_{1}
.

Moreover, the function
{\tt m}_{\tt s}
from
Equation 3.8
coincides pointwise with a two-layer Heaviside network
{\tt t}_{\tt m}
as defined in
Equation 2.5
:

3.6
.

Let
m\in\mathds{N}
be fixed. Then, every
{\tt m}_{\tt s}
from
Equation 3.8
can be represented as

\displaystyle{\tt t}_{{\tt m}}({\vec{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}c_{\imath}\chi_{P_{\imath}}({\vec{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}c_{\imath}\sigma\left(\kappa+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{j}{\tt a}_{j}({\vec{x}}))\right)\in{\tt T}\;,

(3.10)

for a fixed constant
\kappa\in\mathds{R}
satisfying
-m<\kappa<-m+1/2
.

The proof follows from the fact that each term
\sigma(\imath_{j}{\tt a}_{j}({\vec{x}}))
takes values in
\{0,1/2,1\},
and therefore, the sum
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{j}{\tt a}_{j}({\vec{x}}))
takes values in
\{0,1/2,1,3/2,\cdots,m\}
. If all terms equal
1
, then the sum equals
m
. Otherwise, the maximal possible value is
m-1/2
.
As a result, for constants
\kappa
satisfying
-m<\kappa<-m+1/2
, the expression
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{j}{\tt a}_{j}({\vec{x}}))+\kappa
is positive if and only if
\sigma(\imath_{j}{\tt a}_{j}({\vec{x}}))=1
for all
j=1,\cdots,m
, which means that

\chi_{S_{\imath}}({\vec{x}})=\sigma\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma(\imath_{j}{\tt a}_{j}({\vec{x}}))+\kappa\right)={\tt t}_{c}\in{\tt T}

(3.11)

pointwise in
\Omega
. For concreteness, we can choose
\kappa=-m+1/3
.

Equation 3.10
shows that the multiphase piecewise-constant Chan–Vese classification model (
Equation 3.4
) can be approximated by two-layer Heaviside networks as defined in
Equation 2.5
. We note that
{\tt t}_{\tt m}
is a weakly customized network with some fixed parameters (
a_{ij}=1
,
d_{ij}=\kappa
). Moreover,
Equation 3.11
corresponds to a heavily customized two-layer network
{\tt t}_{c}
as defined in
Equation 2.7
, which by construction is equivalent to a one-layer network followed by a single Heaviside activation.

Together, these findings establish a structural link between the Chan–Vese model and neural networks (see
Figure 7
). In particular, the classical level-set formulation can be expressed in a neural-network approximation, facilitating numerical implementation using established architectures and optimization algorithms. The analysis also identifies that a minimal depth of two layers is sufficient for image segmentation and classification.

3.3. Regularized Heaviside networks

For numerical implementation, the Heaviside function in the parametrized Chan-Vese functional
\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}
(see
Equation 3.8
) is approximated by a sigmoid function
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
. With this approximation, we obtain the functional
\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f,\varepsilon}
. Accordingly, if we use
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
instead of
\sigma
in the definition of one-layer networks
{\tt s}
in
{\tt N}
(see
Equation 2.3
) and
{\tt t}
in
{\tt T}
(see
Equation 2.5
), we get respectively
{\tt s}_{\varepsilon}
in
{\tt N}_{\varepsilon}
and
{\tt t}_{\varepsilon}
in
{\tt T}_{\varepsilon}
. In the following, we justify the approximation
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
of
\sigma
by the following lemma:

3.7
Properties of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, the sigmoid function
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
is Lipschitz continuous, and the family
\{\sigma_{\varepsilon}:\varepsilon>0\}
approximates the Heaviside function
\sigma
in
L^{1}(\mathds{R}),L^{2}(\mathds{R})
, and pointwise in
\mathds{R}
.

3.8
\Gamma
-convergence under certain regularity assumptions.

We can expect that minimizing the Chan-Vese functional
\mathrm{CV}_{\tt f}(\{(c_{\imath},\Xi_{\imath})\}_{\imath\in\{-1,1\}^{m}})
(see
Equation 3.2
) and the Chan–Vese level-set functional
Equation 3.6
provide the same minimizers under certain regularity assumptions on the classes to be identified.
Then the results of
[
9
]
for
Equation 3.2
(cf.
3.1
) carry over to the minimizers of
Equation 3.6
: Indeed, if each boundary
\partial\Xi_{\imath}
, which is a segment of the function
{\tt f}
, is a regular
C^{1}
curve, then it is a one-dimensional
C^{1}
embedded manifold. Hence, there exist a
C^{1}
function whose zero level-set coincides with
\partial\Xi_{\imath}
. Conversely, given a
C^{1}
function whose zero level-set is regular, this set is a one-dimensional manifold and can be parametrized by finite number of closed regular
C^{1}
curves. Under these regularity assumptions, the regularized Chan–Vese functional
\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f,\varepsilon}
(i.e., the functional
\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}
in
Equation 3.6
with sigmoid
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
replacing Heaviside
\sigma
)
\Gamma
-converges (see for instance
[
5
]
) to the functional
\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}
. If, for each level-set function
{\ell}_{k}
in
C^{1}
, there exists an one-layer sigmoid network
{\tt s}_{k,\varepsilon}:={\tt s}_{\varepsilon}[\vec{a}_{k},\mathbf{W}_{k},\vec{b}_{k}]\in{\tt N}_{\varepsilon}
that approximates
{\ell}_{k}
in
C^{1}
, and for each
\varepsilon>0
, the functional
\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\mathrm{f},\varepsilon}
attains a minimizer
(c_{\imath}^{\varepsilon},{\ell}_{k,\varepsilon})\in\mathds{R}\times C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
, then, there exists a sequence of one-layer sigmoid networks
({\tt s}_{k,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\subseteq{\tt N}_{\varepsilon}
such that a subsequence, together with the corresponding constant
c_{\imath}^{\varepsilon}
, converges to a minimizer
(c_{\imath},{\ell}_{k})\in\mathds{R}\times C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
of the functional
\mathrm{CV}^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\tt f}
, if exists.

4. Numerical results

To verify our theoretical results, we run the parametrized Chan–Vese algorithm with
m=4
(allowing at most
2^{4}=16
distinct segments) using both randomly initialized parameters and pre-trained parameters for initialization, and then compare the results.

Our algorithm (
algorithm 2
) requires an initialization of the level-set functions, which is computed from training samples. For training the initialization, we used
algorithm 1
. The datasets for training consist of
2000
images of size
50\times 50
pixels, each containing circles with variations in size and position. The datasets are split into training and validation sets using a 90–10 ratio. Each network, corresponding to a level-set function, is a linear neural network consisting of a single hidden layer with
8\times 50\times 50
neurons. It uses the sigmoid activation function with
\varepsilon=0.5
and is trained using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of
3.10^{-5}
and a weight decay rate of
10^{-3}
. The model is trained for 100 epochs, where each epoch represents one full pass through the training dataset. The hyperparameters were chosen through iterative testing to best match the characteristics of our dataset and achieve stable training performance.

For parametrized Chan-Vese functional optimization (
algorithm 2
), the regularization parameters are chosen as
\mu=0.5,\nu=0
. Smooth approximation of Heaviside and Dirac-delta functions are used with
\varepsilon=0.5
. The gradient of the Chan-Vese functional with respect to the parameters is calculated using automatic differentiation (autograd), which relies on the analytic gradient calculation using the chain rule. The parameters are then optimized using AdamW.

Visualization results

We evaluate performance qualitatively through visual inspection of the segmentation boundaries and compare the convergence behavior of the Chan–Vese functional across different conditions: using trained versus randomly initialized networks, varying

From

However, the boundaries obtained with random initialization are less clean and more irregular compared to those obtained with the pre-trained model. Some of these edge irregularities can be attributed to the inherent stochasticity of mini-batch optimizers. By operating on subsets of the data, these optimizers reduce memory and computational cost per iteration but introduce variability in the optimization path. This can result in isolated noisy pixels and minor differences in the final solution, even when starting from identical initializations and datasets, leading to slightly less consistent outcomes.

There is an inherent trade-off between precision and stability on one hand and speed and scalability on the other. Stochastic optimizers generally converge faster and require less memory per iteration compared to deterministic methods, which typically rely on full-gradient information. They are also less sensitive to initialization. In contrast, quasi-Newton methods, such as L-BFGS, can diverge depending on the starting point, making random initialization suboptimal. Overall, stochastic optimizers are faster, whereas L-BFGS with a good initialization tends to achieve higher precision. The choice of optimizer should be guided by the desired balance between computational efficiency and accuracy.

Furthermore, from the plot of the parametrized Chan-Vese energy functional, we observe that initially, the Chan-Vese energy value for the pre-trained initialization is lower than that of the randomized initialization, indicating that the pre-trained contours are initialized closer to the object. During the first 20 iterations, however, the randomly initialized parameters appear to converge faster than the pre-trained initialization. This occurs because the pre-trained contours start near the object, resulting in smaller gradients and slower early evolution, whereas randomly initialized contours, starting farther from the object, adjust coarse shapes more quickly during the initial steps. Notably, after approximately 22 iterations, the neural network prior parametrized version rapidly surpasses the random initialization, demonstrating that the prior improves both convergence speed and final performance.

5. Approximation results (Appendix)

Section 4
presented a reliable implementation of the numerical algorithms introduced in
algorithm 1
and
algorithm 2
. In this section, we establish an approximation result showing that any construction involving the Heaviside activation function can be approximated by replacing it with a sigmoid function. Specifically, we quantify the difference between the original and smoothed problems introduced in
Section 3.3
and establish convergence as
\varepsilon\to 0
, thereby justifying the numerical implementation presented in
Section 3.4
and
Section 4
. The result is summarized by the following approximation chain:

{\tt m}_{\varepsilon}\approx{\tt m}\approx{\tt t}\approx{\tt t}_{\varepsilon}.

The approximation procedure is summarized in four steps:

(i)

Step 1 (Parametrization):
We reformulate the minimization problem by replacing the level-set function
{\ell}_{k}
(defined in
Definition 3.2
) with a one-layer Heaviside neural network
{\tt s}
(defined in
Equation 2.3
). This parametrization is established in
Section 3
.

(ii)

Step 2 (Polygonal to general regions):
The parametrization initially yields segmentations consisting of polygonal regions, captured by the multi-phase level-set functions
{\tt m}_{\tt s}
(see
Equation 3.8
). As shown in
Lemma 3.6
, these functions can be represented pointwise in
\Omega
by a weakly customized two-layer Heaviside network
{\tt t}_{\tt m}
. The main goal of this step is to extend this result to approximate arbitrary multi-phase functions
{\tt m}
whose regions have finite perimeter; that is, to establish
{\tt m}\approx{\tt t}
, as stated in
Theorem 5.2
.

(iii)

Step 3 (Heaviside to sigmoid):
We show that a one-layer Heaviside neural network
{\tt s}
can be approximated by a one-layer sigmoid neural network
{\tt s}_{\varepsilon}
. The validity of that is established in
Lemma 5.3
. A similar result applies to two-layer networks.

(iv)

Step 4 (Heaviside to sigmoid - multi-phase):
We extend the approximation to show that the smoothed multiphase level-set functions
{\tt m}_{\varepsilon}
(where
\sigma
is replaced by
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
) can be approximated by two-layer sigmoid neural networks
{\tt t}_{\varepsilon}
. This final result is proven in
Theorem 5.5
.

In what follows, we provide rigorous proofs for Step 2 (see
Section 5.1
) and for Steps 3 and 4 (see
Section 5.2

5.2. Neural network approximation using sigmoid activation

In this section, we show that a sigmoid network can approximate a Heaviside network, and then extend this result to demonstrate that the smoothed multiphase level-set function can be approximated by two-layer sigmoid network:

Theorem 5.5

(Neural networks approximation of multiphase level-set function)

Proof.

Let

Combine the two estimates, we obtain the desired result.
