Oxygen homeostasis: new insight of gut microbiota-host interactions
Authors: Zhipeng Yang, Juan Yang, Jian Peng, Hongkui Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxygen homeostasis is a key ecological factor shaping microbial composition, epithelial function, and immune balance in the gut. Spatial and developmental oxygen gradients drive the transition from facultative to obligate anaerobes. Gut hypoxia is maintained by epithelial oxygen consumption and microbial metabolism, forming a positive feedback loop that supports barrier integrity and immune homeostasis. Disruption by inflammation, antibiotics, or diet elevates oxygen availability, promoting dysbiosis, pathogen expansion, and disease risk.Subject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Microbiology, Physiology

Introduction

The gastrointestinal tract is a metabolically active organ system in which steep oxygen gradients fundamentally shape microbial ecology, epithelial physiology, and mucosal immunity1. Although the gut is traditionally described as an anaerobic environment, accumulating evidence reveals a highly heterogeneous distribution of oxygen availability. This heterogeneity occurs across spatial dimensions, including the radial mucosa-lumen axis and the proximal-distal intestinal tract, as well as across temporal contexts such as postnatal development and inflammatory states2. These oxygen landscapes are not constant physical properties, but dynamic, tightly regulated by microvascular oxygen delivery, epithelial metabolism, and microbial respiration3. Understanding this physiological oxygen dynamic change has become essential for deciphering how the microbiome establishes, matures, and maintains intestinal homeostasis.

In early life, the relatively oxygenated neonatal gut supports the initial colonization of facultative anaerobes, which rapidly consume luminal oxygen and create the conditions necessary for the succession of obligate anaerobes that define the mature microbiome4. Across the lifespan, this microbe-oxygen interplay forms a feedback loop: obligate anaerobes generate short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), fueling colonocyte β-oxidation and deepening mucosal hypoxia, which in turn preserves anaerobic niches and restricts facultative anaerobe5. These mechanisms highlight oxygen level as a central ecological core driving microbiome assembly and function.

Oxygen environment also interfaces directly with host immune function. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), particularly HIF-1α, broadly regulate the innate and adaptive immune function, for example, epithelial barrier6, innate lymphocyte cells (ILCs)7, and intraepithelial lymphocyte cells (IECs)8. Further, microbial metabolites such as butyrate further modulate these pathways by enhancing epithelial oxygen consumption and stabilizing HIF-1α9, thereby linking microbial fermentation to immune homeostasis.

Disruption of physiological hypoxia is a key driver in various intestinal diseases. Inflammatory, dietary, and antibiotic that impair epithelial mitochondrial respiration increase mucosal oxygenation, promoting the aerobic expansion such as Enterobacteriaceae and inducing dysbiosis10,11. Further, this hyperoxia-driven dysbiosis not only destabilizes the microbial community but also boosts the growth of pathogens12. Thus, the breakdown of physiological hypoxia is increasingly recognized not simply because of disease but as a causal driver of microbial imbalance, epithelial dysfunction, and impaired colonization resistance.

Taken together, growing evidence shows that intestinal oxygen availability is a central ecological factor shaping host-microbe interactions, from microbial succession and epithelial metabolism to immune regulation and disease susceptibility. In this review, we integrate current knowledge of how spatial and temporal oxygen gradients are established in the gut, how microbial and host physiological processes cooperatively maintain physiological hypoxia, and how this oxygen landscape supports barrier function and immune homeostasis. We further examine how disruptions in oxygen balance contribute to dysbiosis, pathogen expansion, and inflammatory disease, and highlight emerging methods for measuring gut oxygenation and modeling oxygen-dependent host-microbiota interactions. Finally, we discuss microbial and dietary interventions, including probiotics, prebiotics, and postbiotics, which can modulate oxygen tension to promote gut health. By integrating these concepts, this review provides a comprehensive overview of how intestinal oxygen dynamics shape microbial ecology, epithelial physiology, and immune regulation, and highlights emerging opportunities to target oxygen homeostasis for improving gut health.

Physiological oxygen landscapes in the gut

Spatial heterogeneity

The mammalian gastrointestinal tract displays pronounced longitudinal heterogeneity in oxygen tension, which shapes microbial community composition along its length. Oxygen diffuses from the mucosal capillaries into the lumen, creating a gradient that typically decreases from the proximal small intestine to the distal colon. This gradient determines the relative abundance of aerobic, facultative anaerobic, and obligate anaerobic taxa in each segment. The Po2 of the small intestinal lumen is 5–30 mmHg, whereas that of the colon is 0.5–3 mmHg (∼0.4% O2)13,14. The distal colonic lumen has such low oxygen levels that it is often considered anoxic.

The duodenum is the most oxygenated intestinal segment, although levels remain lower than atmospheric and are classified as microaerobic13. Rapid flow of chyme, together with host secretions, limits overall bacterial abundance. Microbial communities are dominated by facultative anaerobes and microaerotolerant taxa, including Streptococcus, Neisseria, Veillonella alongside smaller proportions of aerotolerant Prevotella15–17. Oxygen levels decline in the jejunum, but remain sufficient to sustain mucosa-associated facultative anaerobes such as Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Enterococcus18,19, which are positioned advantageously to metabolize soluble fibers and oligosaccharides diffusing from the lumen. Oxygen levels are markedly lower in the ileum as transit slows and microbial oxygen consumption intensifies. The reduced-oxygen environment favors greater proportions of obligate anaerobes including SCFAs-producing Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides compared with upstream segments and represents the physiological transition zone toward the anaerobic conditions of the colon20.

The colon is characterized by physiological hypoxia, with the lumen approaching strict anaerobiosis. Mucosal surfaces remain slightly more oxygenated due to epithelial supply, supporting small populations of facultative anaerobes in the inner mucus layer21. While the lumen is dominated by obligate anaerobes (Bacteroides, Clostridium, Eubacterium, Bifidobacterium)22 that ferment complex polysaccharides into SCFAs, which in turn modulate host epithelial oxygen metabolism via β-oxidation and HIF-1α activation23.

From the oxygenated duodenum to the anaerobic colon, the longitudinal oxygen gradient drives a progressive shift in dominant taxa: facultative species in proximal segments give way to mixed communities in the mid gut, and finally to obligate anaerobes in the distal gut. Within each segment, mucosa-lumen oxygen differences create layered niches, with facultative taxa persisting near the mucosa even in overall anaerobic environments, and strict anaerobes dominating the luminal core (Fig. 1).

Temporal dynamics

The postnatal development of the gut microbiota follows a highly conserved trajectory across mammals. The change characterized not only by a progressive shift from facultative anaerobes to obligate anaerobes in parallel with declining intestinal oxygen tension24, but also by changes in their capacity to utilize dietary components. This temporal succession can be divided into three key developmental phases: neonatal colonization, weaning transition, and adult stabilization, each stage marked by distinct colonization niches, metabolic activities, and impacts on gut health.

Small intestine

Detailed postnatal developmental data for the human small intestinal mucosa are rare due to the challenges of obtaining healthy samples from human and conducting longitudinal studies. Most available human datasets come from surgical or post-mortem sample in adults, reflecting the mature state rather than developmental change.

In neonatal mice, the small intestinal lumen supports aerobes such as Enterococcus, Staphylococcus, Streptococcus thriving in the relatively oxygenated environment25. Due to enhanced epithelial respiration and microbial activity, oxygen levels decline progressively over the first 2–3 postnatal weeks. This decline triggers the expansion of facultative anaerobes and anaerobes such as Lactobacillus, Veillonella and Lachnospiraceae, which can utilize the more complex dietary glycans introduced at weaning25. By day 28–56, the microbiota reaches a more stable, lower-oxygen configuration, with reduced facultative abundance and increased anaerobic fermentation capacity25.

Large intestine

In humans, the neonatal large intestine harbors a microbiota dominated by facultative anaerobes and microaerophiles, including Enterobacteriaceae and Streptococcus26. These pioneer commensals are well adapted to the relatively oxygenated gut immediately after birth and rapidly consume residual luminal oxygen through aerobic respiration. Next, the Bifidobacterium begin colonizing where they metabolize available milk derived oligosaccharides and other simple carbohydrates. As oxygen tension declines, obligate anaerobes such as Bacteroides, Prevotella and Faecalibacterium expand4. This developmental trajectory culminates in a marked shift toward an adult-like microbial configuration during weaning, when the introduction of solid foods increases the availability of complex dietary substrates and drives microbial diversification and functional maturation27,28.

By approximately 2 years of age, the human colonic microbiota changes to a stable, highly anaerobic adult-like configuration27. Similar trends occur in other mammals: in mice and wild primates, an oxygen tolerant neonatal phase transitions to a state enriched with obligate anaerobe and SCFAs28,29. Pig studies reveal comparable patterns, with Escherichia, Shigella, Streptococcus, and Lactobacillus prevalent early on, followed post-weaning by the rise of fiber-fermenting genera such as Prevotella, Bacteroides, Ruminococcus, Treponema, Roseburia, Faecalibacterium, and Lachnospiraceae30. Across species, this conserved oxygen-driven shift support the maturation of the colon into a strictly anaerobic ecosystem.

Across mammals, the postnatal assembly of the gut microbiota proceeds through a conserved, oxygen-dependent maturation program that spans from birth to adulthood. Immediately after delivery, both the small intestine and large intestine are relatively oxygenated, favoring the expansion of facultative anaerobes and microaerophiles that initiate microbial colonization and drive the first wave of oxygen depletion. As epithelial metabolic activity increases and microbial communities diversify, luminal and mucosal oxygen tensions progressively decline, enabling the successive rise of obligate anaerobes with greater fermentative capacity. By adulthood, these processes converge toward a stable, strongly anaerobic ecosystem characterized by high microbial diversity, and mature fermentation capacity. Despite anatomical and dietary differences across species, the development trend reflects that oxygen availability acts as a central ecological driver shaping gut homeostasis (Fig. 1).

Mechanisms underlying physiological hypoxia in the gut

Microbial contributions

Facultative anaerobes play a pivotal role in establishing and maintaining the physiological hypoxia of the mammalian gut, particularly during early life and in oxygenated niches near the mucosal surface27. Oxygen diffuses continuously from the intestinal vasculature into the epithelial layer and lumen, creating a gradient of decreasing oxygen tension31. This residual oxygen can be consumed by facultative anaerobic bacteria, which have the metabolic flexibility to perform aerobic respiration when oxygen is available and switch to anaerobic fermentation or anaerobic respiration (e.g., nitrate reduction) in anoxic conditions10.

In the neonatal gut, taxa such as Escherichia coli, Streptococcus, Enterococcus, and Lactobacillus often dominate mucosal sites and the lumen immediately after birth, reflecting a relatively oxygenated environment24. Through aerobic respiration, these organisms consume oxygen diffusing from host tissues, thereby accelerating the decline in local pO2 and creating niches favorable to obligate anaerobes such as Clostridiales32. Experimental evidence supports the central role of microbial respiration in luminal oxygen depletion. Comparisons between conventional and germ-free mice show that oxygen consumption rates are significantly higher in the presence of facultative anaerobes, The introduction of E. coli into germ-free cecal contents reproduces this rapid depletion pattern33. Furthermore, microbiota-derived succinate has been reported to reduce intestinal oxygen levels and promote Clostridiales colonization in the large intestine of neonatal mice, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

SCFAs originate from the microbial fermentation of dietary carbohydrates in the large intestinal microbiota. Butyrate serves as the primary energy source for colonocytes. Its β-oxidation to acetyl-CoA fuels the TCA cycle and subsequent electron transport chain, a process that consumes oxygen and generates ATP. This intense epithelial oxygen depletion creates a hypoxic mucosal environment, which in turn helps maintain a niche for obligate anaerobic microbes5,34.

Moreover, butyrate can act as a natural ligand and activator of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ)35. Multiple studies demonstrate that butyrate-producing commensals drive PPARγ-dependent transcriptional programs in colonocytes, linking microbial fermentation to host metabolic homeostasis23,35,36. Activation of PPARγ shifts epithelial energy metabolism toward mitochondrial β-oxidation, a process that sharply increases epithelial oxygen consumption and thereby reinforces physiological hypoxia at the mucosal surface23,36. Pharmacological activation of PPARγ with 5-aminosalicylic acid can mimic the effects of butyrate, restore mitochondrial activity and suppressing dysbiotic E. coli expansion 12,37. Together, these findings highlight butyrate-PPARγ signaling as a central axis by which the microbiota constrains epithelial oxygenation and maintains gut anaerobiosis (Fig. 2).

Host physiological contributions

Host physiology also plays a decisive role in maintaining gut hypoxia. Oxygen is delivered through the mucosal microvasculature, reaching epithelial cells at villus tips and crypt bases, where it is rapidly consumed by mitochondrial respiration5. Because of the high epithelial metabolic demand, most oxygen is depleted before reaching the lumen, thus enforcing a low-oxygen environment that favors obligate anaerobes.

The intestinal lumen exhibits microbially independent oxygen consumption beyond microbial respiration. Germ-free mouse studies reveal that endogenous luminal components undergo chemical oxidation, particularly via the spontaneous oxidation of unsaturated phospholipids33. Although slower than microbe-driven O2 depletion, this chemical process establishes a basal anaerobic foundation that is later amplified by microbial metabolism. These mechanisms reveal that gut hypoxia is not only a microbial by-product but an emergent property of epithelial physiology and luminal chemistry (Fig. 2).

Interdependence: a positive feedback loop

A reinforcing feedback loop links diet, microbial metabolism, and host oxygen physiology. Microbially produced SCFAs, especially butyrate, stimulate epithelial oxygen consumption through mitochondrial β-oxidation, deepening mucosal hypoxia. This hypoxic state promotes the dominance of obligate anaerobes, which in turn generate more SCFAs through fermentation of dietary fibers and other food components (Fig. 2).

Physiological hypoxia and intestinal immune homeostasis

In the healthy intestine, physiological hypoxia results from high epithelial oxygen consumption, dense colonization by obligate anaerobes, and restricted oxygen diffusion from the submucosa to the lumen. This hypoxia state sustains mucosal immune homeostasis via two interconnected pathways: host hypoxia-activated transcriptional programs and microbial metabolite-mediated modulation (Fig. 3).

HIF‑1α mediated adaptation to intestinal hypoxia

The intestinal epithelium exists in a state of chronic, physiological hypoxia and therefore relies on highly coordinated transcriptional programs to maintain cellular and tissue function. Central to these programs are the hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), among which HIF-1α represents the principal oxygen-sensitive subunit. Under reduced oxygen tension, prolyl hydroxylase activity is inhibited, preventing HIF-1α degradation and enabling the transcriptional activation of hypoxia responsive genes38. In contrast, HIF-2α exhibits partial independence from oxygen availability in the small intestine (Duodenum), with its activity regulated by microbiota-derived metabolites such as 1,3-diaminopropane and reuterin through heterodimer modulationn39. This functional distinction highlights HIF-1α as the dominant molecular axis linking environmental oxygen sensing to epithelial barrier regulation.

Maintain epithelial barrier

HIF-1α, activates key barrier-protective genes in IECs, including tight-junction components claudin-1, occludin and antimicrobial peptides LL-37/CRAMP6,40,41. These responses collectively reduce epithelial permeability and limit pathogen translocation. Beyond strengthening physical barriers, HIF-1α promotes xenophagy, facilitating intracellular clearance of pathogens such as Salmonella Typhimurium42. Further, HIF-1α also suppresses necroptosis by repressing RIPK3, thereby protecting IECs from programmed cell death and preventing secondary systemic inflammation43.

In IBD, HIF-1α directly drives IL-33 expression through promoter engagement and TNF-dependent co-activation, contributing to epithelial-immune communication44. Additionally, in Crohn’s disease, HIF-1α-dependent autophagy restrains inflammation by suppressing mTOR/NLRP3 signaling45. Together, these mechanisms illustrate HIF-1α’s overarching influence on epithelial barrier.

Support intestinal stem cell function

HIF-1α is equally indispensable for maintaining the intestinal stem cell niche, particularly during injury and repair. It accelerates mucosal healing following ischemic or radiation-induced injury46,47. Culturing human organoid under physiological hypoxia (2% O2) more accurately reflects the in vivo environment, enhancing epithelial growth, differentiation, and immune-related responses compared with conventional high-oxygen culture48. Conversely, loss of HIF-1α results in severe crypt injury, Paneth cell depletion, and aberrant epithelial proliferation49. Elevated HIF-1α also drives metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis, promoting epithelial proliferative capacity50.

Regulate mucus barrier integrity

Although HIF-1α does not directly control goblet cell differentiation, it critically regulates goblet cell secretory function and mucus barrier quality. HIF-1α induces expression of MUC3 and its associated intestinal trefoil factor, thereby reinforcing the stability of mucus layer 51,52. Epithelial specific HIF-1α deficiency leads to structurally disordered mucin granules and a thinner mucus layer, whereas pharmacological stabilization of HIF-1α restores mucus organization53.

Coordination of innate and adaptive immune responses

Beyond its epithelial functions, HIF-1α shapes mucosal immune cell activity. In group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s), hypoxia-induced HIF-1α stabilization enhances IL-22 production, promoting epithelial proliferation and antimicrobial defense38. In B cells, HIF-1α governs metabolic programming and IgA production via acetyl-CoA-driven H3K27 acetylation, with deficiency exacerbating intestinal inflammation54. HIF-1α also preserves intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) composition and microbiota balance; its absence disrupts CD8αα+ and TCRγδ+ IELs subsets and increases susceptibility to injury and colitis8.

SCFAs as microbial regulators of oxygen metabolism and HIF stabilization

SCFAs serve not only as primary energy substrates for IECs but also as key indicators and regulators of intestinal health. SCFA signaling through GPR41/GPR43 and histone deacetylases has been extensively reviewed55, thus, this section focuses on a comparatively underexplored but increasingly recognized mechanism: SCFAs-mediated modulation of epithelial oxygen metabolism and HIF stabilization.

SCFAs influence HIF-1α through two major pathways. First, SCFAs indirectly promote HIF-1α activation by stimulating epithelial β-oxidation, thereby increasing oxygen consumption and deepening physiological hypoxia5. Second, SCFAs act as endogenous inhibitors of HIF prolyl hydroxylases, directly stabilizing HIF-1α56. Together, these mechanisms offer new insight into how microbiota-derived metabolites support barrier integrity, immune regulation, and pathogen resistance under varying oxygen conditions.

SCFA-HIF signaling in epithelial barrier defense

Among SCFAs, butyrate plays a dominant protective role. By enhancing oxygen consumption and stabilizing HIF-1α, butyrate upregulates barrier components such as ZO-1 and occludin, enhancing mucosal integrity and reducing pathogen translocation5,40,57. Butyrate also ameliorates DSS-induced colitis by promoting HIF-1α-dependent autophagy, which preserves microbial composition and maintains barrier function9.

SCFA-HIF signaling in immune modulation

Microbiota-derived pentanoate promotes intestinal Treg differentiation via iron-dependent HIF-2α-c-Maf signaling, restoring tolerance in colitis58. SCFAs also enhance IL-22 production by CD4+ T cells and ILCs through a GPR41-mediated, HDAC-inhibited HIF-1α/AhR pathway, contributing to immune protection and homeostasis59. Conversely, hyperbaric oxygen reduces microbiota-derived SCFAs levels, disrupts the HIF-1α-IL-22 axis in ILC3s, and increases susceptibility to Clostridioides difficile infection7. Collectively, these findings underscore the SCFA-HIF axis as a central metabolic pathway in maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis and preventing pathogen invasion.

Loss of physiological hypoxia and intestinal disease

Metabolic drivers of mucosal hyperoxia and dysbiosis

Under physiological conditions, epithelial mitochondria maintain a hypoxic mucosal interface by consuming oxygen at high rates. However, during inflammatory injury such as colitis, this homeostatic hypoxic barrier collapses due to impaired epithelial metabolism. For instance, DSS induced colitis disrupts mitochondrial respiration, lowering epithelial oxygen consumption and consequently increasing mucosal oxygenation37. This shift generates a pathological hyperoxic environment that selectively favors the growth of facultatively anaerobic pathobionts, most notably Escherichia coli and other Enterobacteriaceae37.

Inflamed mucosa generates high levels of NOX1-derived hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Bacterial catalases convert H2O2 into molecular oxygen, creating a local source of O2 that fuels aerobic respiration. Pathobionts such as E. coli, which possess high-affinity oxidases like cytochrome bd-II, are particularly efficient at using this ROS-derived oxygen to expand in the inflamed gut 60,61. Hyperoxia does not merely disrupt microbial ecology but may also directly aggravate tumorigenic disease. In colitis-associated colorectal cancer models, inflammation-induced increases in epithelial oxygenation promote the aerobic expansion of colibactin-producing E. coli, enhancing tumorigenic potential62. This underscores pathological hyperoxia as an active driver of disease progression rather than a secondary inflammatory consequence. Nevertheless, host also response to the elevated luminal oxygen, higher luminal oxygen promotes riboflavin-producing bacteria that generate MAIT-cell ligands, thereby promoting tissue repair and enhancing barrier protection during colitis63.

Diet represents another major determinant of epithelial oxygen homeostasis. High-fat diet impairs colonocyte mitochondrial function, diminishing oxygen consumption and increasing luminal O2 and nitrate availability36. This weaken of physiological hypoxia establishes a hyperoxic niche that encourages the expansion of Enterobacteriaceae and promotes low-grade inflammation, characterize early IBD64. Further, hyperoxia-driven Proteobacteria enrichment further elevates LPS release, activating IDO1-dependent kynurenine metabolism, which has been implicated in cognitive impairment65,66. This metabolic landscape additionally enhances E. coli-mediated choline catabolism to produce the cardiovascular risk factor TMAO11,67. High-fat feeding combined with prior antibiotic exposure can further impair microbial sorbitol catabolism, worsening mucosal oxygen dysregulation and driving diarrheal phenotypes36 (Fig. 4).

Oxygen niches and pathogen colonization resistance

Oxygen availability is a central ecological determinant shaping pathogen fitness in both early life and adult intestinal environments. In neonates, high luminal oxygen tension results from immature epithelial metabolism and incomplete colonization by obligate anaerobes. This creates a permissive niche for oxygen-respiring pathogens such as Salmonella68, K. pneumoniae69, Citrobacter rodentium32, which can rapidly expand before anaerobic communities become established. Microbial interactions modulate this niche: commensal Enterobacteriaceae and Lactobacillus can outcompete pathogens for oxygen, reinforces colonization resistance68,70.

In adults, disturbances such as inflammation, antibiotic treatment, or dietary imbalance deplete butyrate-producing anaerobes (e.g., Clostridia), reducing epithelial β-oxidation and weakening hypoxia maintenance71. Elevated luminal O2 then removes ecological constraints on facultative anaerobes, facilitating expansion of Salmonella71, Candida albicans12 and Citrobacter rodentium72,73.

Many pathogens actively remodel oxygen landscapes to favor their own proliferation. For example, Citrobacter rodentium’s type III secretion system enhances epithelial proliferation and increases oxygen leakage into the lumen72. Salmonella initially relies on aerobic respiration before causing major microbiota disruption. In later stages, inflammation-driven nutrient depletion and diminished SCFAs levels maintain a state of luminal hyperoxia74. Shigella flexneri depends on oxygen for early invasion but subsequently consumes it through respiration to generate localized hypoxic niches that support persistent colonization75. Moreover, E. coli’s appBCX operon enables utilization of catalase-liberated O2 derived from host NOX1-generated ROS, directly coupling host antimicrobial activity to pathogen metabolism60.

Overall, intestinal oxygen availability governs pathogen behavior across developmental and disease contexts. In early life, elevated luminal oxygen establishes a transient window of vulnerability in which oxygen-respiring pathogens gain a competitive advantage, whereas the progressive establishment of anaerobic commensals gradually restores colonization resistance. In adults, inflammatory or antibiotic-induced decrease of butyrate-producing anaerobes reverses this protective hypoxia, enabling facultative pathogens to colonization. Together, these insights underscore oxygen as a central battleground in pathogen-host interactions (Fig. 4).

Invasive methods

Invasive techniques are primarily suited for laboratory and preclinical studies, offering high temporal and spatial resolution for quantifying tissue oxygen tension (PtO2) or mucosal oxygen saturation. Electrochemical sensors, such as miniaturized oxygen sensors and Clark-type microelectrodes, provide real-time, continuous data on serosal, mucosal, or luminal PtO2. These tools have been validated in anesthetized rats to capture dynamic, reversible oxygen fluctuations during vascular occlusion or altered inspired O2 concentrations76. Similarly, tissue tonometers allow for the direct quantification of oxygen and carbon dioxide in intestinal tissues, as demonstrated in porcine models77. While direct intraluminal measurements using fast-response Clark’s polarographic microelectrodes offer exceptional temporal precision and spatially resolved PaO2 profiles, their application is limited by technical complexity and physiological disturbances caused by surgery and anesthesia78.

In murine models, histological and gene-reporter techniques provide complementary means to visualize hypoxia with cellular resolution. The pimonidazole (Hypoxyprobe-1) method labels cells with pO2 < 10 mmHg, enabling the immunostaining of hypoxic regions79. Furthermore, the ODD-Luciferase reporter mouse links the oxygen-dependent degradation domain of HIF-1α to luciferase, allows for in vivo bioluminescent imaging of hypoxia79. Concomitant anti-luciferase immunostaining in these models facilitates the precise microscopic localization of hypoxic microdomains.

Non-invasive methods

Non-invasive approaches are more applicable to clinical and translational research, utilizing optical, spectroscopic, and imaging-based modalities. Near-infrared spectroscopy enables the continuous assessment of regional splanchnic oxygenation, correlating well with portal venous oxygen saturation in neonatal models of graded hypoxia80. Photoacoustic microscopy combines optical absorption with ultrasound detection to provide label-free, high-resolution in vivo imaging of intestinal blood flow and oxygen saturation81. In clinical settings, endoscopic reflectance spectrophotometry has emerged as a promising tool. For instance, advanced endoscopic systems integrating reflectance spectrophotometry can quantify mucosal oxygen saturation in real time across gastrointestinal regions, enabling the detection of regional hypoxia patterns that may predict mucosal injury82. Additionally, electron paramagnetic resonance imaging enables the non-invasive visualization of hypoxic microdomains and spatiotemporal oxygen gradients, offering unique insights into microenvironmental heterogeneity83.

A representative approach involves microfluidic and organoid-on-chip systems. For example, intestinal organoid chips engineered with embedded microvessels and pH-responsive scaffolds can efficiently recapitulate rapid hypoxia-reoxygenation cycles. This platform has facilitated the discovery of hypoxia-responsive genes, such as Olfm4, and their protective roles in ischemia/reperfusion injury78. Similarly, bioengineered 3D scaffolds derived from silk or alginate are designed to sustain both longitudinal and radial oxygen gradients, supporting polarized human intestinal epithelia that maintain barrier integrity and physiological metabolism84. Beyond engineered constructs, ex vivo techniques also provide functional insights. For instance, the using chamber technique has been adapted to simulate hypoxia in the jejunal epithelium, revealing that glucose transport in lagomorphs is modulated by AMP-activated protein kinase under hypoxic conditions85.

Models for host-microbiota interactions under controlled oxygen conditions

Recent findings in piglet models demonstrate that imposing strict anaerobiosis in ileal bioreactors distorts microbial profiles, overriding inoculum effects86. Consequently, next-generation models are incorporating host epithelial cells and physiological oxygen gradients to ensure accurate replication of the in vivo microenvironment (Table 2).

Key examples include the intestinal organoid physoxic co-culture system, which enables human intestinal organoids to coexist with anaerobes like Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron or Blautia species. This model successfully reproduces the contrasting oxygen conditions of the in vivo epithelium, promoting barrier integrity and immunomodulatory gene expression87. Likewise, apical-out intestinal organoids cultured under hypoxic conditions provide direct microbial access to the apical surface, supporting colonization by Lactobacillus casei and Bifidobacterium longum while preserving tissue architecture88.

Microfluidic advancements further enhance this integration. Standalone microfluidic devices can create partitioned oxygen environments without external anaerobic chambers, allowing the co-culture of epithelial cells with both facultative and obligate anaerobes under stable gradients89. In parallel, precision-cut intestinal slice chips maintain native tissue organization, enabling the real-time monitoring of neurochemical signals (e.g., serotonin) while preserving physiological oxygen gradients90.

Collectively, these platforms reproduce the spatial oxygen heterogeneity and bidirectional metabolic interactions defining the gut ecosystem. While organoid-based models best capture human cellular responses, microfluidic and slice systems offer superior temporal control. These tools are invaluable for dissecting how diet-induced shifts in oxygen tension and microbial metabolism shape epithelial physiology.

Microbial interventions that target oxygen homeostasis

Growing evidence highlights intestinal oxygen tension as a powerful ecological determinant governing microbiota assembly, immune development, infectious susceptibility, and inflammatory disease progression. Consequently, strategies that modulate oxygen availability-particularly those leveraging microbial metabolism-are emerging as promising interventions in multiple physiological and pathological contexts. Conceptually, microbial modulation of mucosal hypoxia may be applied to: (i) promote microbiome maturation and immune development in early life by lowering intestinal oxygen levels; (ii) restore microbial-epithelial homeostasis in intestinal inflammation by re-establishing physiological hypoxia; (iii) enhance colonization resistance of intestine by using oxygen as a competitive battlefield during pathogen invasion. Below, we explore key microbial strategies that are capable of tuning intestinal oxygen landscapes and discuss their mechanistic and translational potential.

Probiotic

One major avenue of intervention involves identifying microorganisms capable of rapid oxygen depletion to re-establish intestinal hypoxia. For example, Lactococcus lactis utilizes active aerobic respiration to markedly reduce intestinal oxygen tension, thereby restoring mucosal hypoxia70. Similarly, Saccharomyces boulardii, fungi with strong aerobic capacity, effectively decreases intestinal oxygen levels in neonatal rats, accelerating microbiota colonization and promoting type 3 immune development24.

Building on individual strains, synthetic microbial communities that pair oxygen-scavenging facultative anaerobes with obligate anaerobes emerged as a rational strategy to reshape oxygen gradients and establish more stable microbial ecosystems. In a mixed culture in vitro, aerobic non-degraders like E. coli enable anaerobic degraders by creating an oxygen-free environment and supplying nutrients, leading to efficient biopolymer conversion91. Further, early-life fecal microbiota transplantation together with S. boulardii reduced community dispersion, enriched beneficial taxa, and suppressed opportunistic pathogens in pig model92.

Prebiotics

As fermentable substrates for gut bacteria, prebiotics provide essential metabolic inputs for SCFAs production and thereby influence intestinal oxygen dynamics. Because the availability and complexity of dietary carbohydrates strongly shape microbial fermentation patterns, prebiotic strategies must consider the developmental stage of the host. Notably, the intestinal oxygen environment and microbiota composition differ markedly between infants and adults, leading to distinct carbohydrate utilization capacities. Infants predominantly metabolize structurally simple oligosaccharides93, whereas adults efficiently utilize more complex polysaccharides94. These age-dependent metabolic preferences underscore the need for tailored prebiotic formulations that can optimally support beneficial microbial communities and modulate oxygen tension across life stages.

In early life, combining structurally diverse oligosaccharides has proven more effective than single substrates in promoting infant-type Bifidobacterium communities characteristic of breastfed microbiota. Studies comparing lactulose, raffinose, galactooligosaccharides, and various fructooligosaccharides demonstrate that no single oligosaccharide supports all infant-type strains, whereas specific mixtures more consistently enhance Bifidobacterium growth and SCFAs production, thereby fostering a lower oxygen environment95.

In adults, recent work shows that rationally designed fiber mixtures outperform individual fibers by more enriching beneficial bacteria and increasing SCFAs production. These mixtures exhibit synergistic effects not observed with their single components, including more consistent stimulation of butyrate-producing Clostridium96.

Strategies of prebiotics and probiotics combinations further refine these effects. For example, calcium-magnesium tablets combined with Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron or Akkermansia muciniphila capable of alleviating colitis. Such synergy underscores the potential of precision-designed prebiotic-probiotic interventions to modulate mucosal physiology, including oxygen homeostasis97.

Collectively, emerging evidence reveals that prebiotic effects are profoundly shaped by substrate complexity, microbial metabolic capacities, and host developmental stage. Rational design of oligosaccharide blends, synergistic fiber mixtures, and targeted synbiotic combinations offers a powerful framework for modulating microbial fermentation, enhancing SCFA-driven hypoxia, and ultimately shaping intestinal ecological and immune maturation.

Postbiotics

Postbiotics, defined as non-viable microbial cells, their structural components, and microbial-derived metabolites98, have emerged as an attractive strategy to modulate intestinal oxygen homeostasis without the risks associated with administering live organisms. Because many postbiotic molecules act directly on epithelial metabolism or innate immune pathways, they offer precise and stable tools for reshaping mucosal oxygen landscapes.

Microbial metabolites such as SCFAs remain the most well-established postbiotics influencing oxygen tension. SCFAs, particularly butyrate, enhance epithelial mitochondrial respiration, thereby increasing oxygen consumption and reinforcing physiological hypoxia that supports anaerobic microbial communities5,12.

Emerging evidence also highlights structural postbiotics as modulators of mucosal oxygen status. Extracellular membrane vesicles (EMVs) secreted by respiring Escherichia coli and carried with cytochromes, can reduce epithelial oxygenation in inflamed colons, shift communities toward obligate anaerobes, and attenuate inflammatory markers99. This finding position EMVs as next-generation tools capable of reinstating hypoxic niches disrupted in diseases such as IBD.

Overall, postbiotics provide a versatile and safe platform for influencing intestinal oxygen dynamics through targeted modulation of epithelial metabolism, immune signaling, and microbial ecological structure. As mechanistic insights deepen, postbiotic-based interventions may offer scalable therapeutic solutions for restoring oxygen balance across developmental and disease contexts.

Conclusions and future perspectives

The oxygen environment in the gut is created through continuous cooperation between host tissues and the intestinal microbiota. Epithelial oxygen consumption and microbial metabolism together maintain a stable low-oxygen state that supports a healthy barrier, balanced immunity, and the growth of beneficial anaerobic bacteria. This stability depends on a positive cycle in which host and microbes reinforce each other. When either side is damaged, the cycle breaks. Antibiotic treatment that reduces microbial oxygen use, or inflammation that disrupts epithelial metabolism, can increase oxygen levels in the gut. Such changes disturb the microbial community, weaken colonization resistance, and create conditions that allow pathogens to expand. Long-term disturbances in gut oxygen balance may also contribute to metabolic and cardiovascular diseases.

Understanding these processes highlights the value of microbiome-based strategies that help restore oxygen balance. Probiotics that consume oxygen efficiently, as well as diets that support SCFAs producing bacteria, may help recover the low-oxygen state of the intestine. These approaches improve barrier function, stabilize the microbial community, and support immune homeostasis.

However, our current understanding is still limited. Important questions remain unanswered. For example, we still do not clearly understand how the oxygen levels in the mucosa and in the lumen change during intestinal inflammation or how these two environments influence each other. In addition, the effects of other microbial metabolites, such as succinate, on intestinal oxygen conditions are only beginning to be explored.

Technologies such as ingestible sensors (smart capsule), combined with large population studies and multi-omics analyses, have the potential to record diet, microbiota changes, and intestinal physiology at the same time. These integrated datasets will help reveal how commensals, food components, and the host work together to maintain intestinal oxygen homeostasis. They may also guide the development of personalized nutritional strategies and targeted microbial therapies.

Maintaining a stable oxygen environment in the gut is likely to become an important goal in future interventions that use probiotics and dietary components to promote intestinal and systemic health. Continued collaboration across microbiology, nutrition, immunology, and biomedical engineering will be essential for turning this concept into effective clinical applications.
