Reconstruction from multi-planar MRI with foundation models for uterine fibroid analysis
Authors: Junhao Wang, Jinbo Wang, Hanxiao Zhang, Junyang Wu, Minghui Zhang, Yun Gu, Jin Qiu, Guang-Zhong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUterine fibroids represent one of the most common gynecological diseases and accurate 3D reconstruction is a prerequisite for clinical diagnosis, surgical planning, and treatment evaluation. Routine clinical protocols typically involve a set of orthogonal sagittal, coronal, and transverse MRI scans to assess morphology. These images usually have anisotropic voxels with sparse 3D coverage and existing assessment schemes are often limited to different 2D views based on planar segmentation. Moreover, models trained on data from individual centers often fail to generalize to wider datasets. Accurate and consistent 3D reconstruction for quantitative uterine fibroid analysis, particularly with unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA), is an unmet clinical need. This paper proposes the Foundation Model-Guided Adaptive Segmentation (FGAS) framework for automatic annotation-free multi-planar uterine fibroid MRI segmentation. FGAS uses anatomical priors for pseudo-label optimization, and integrates multi-view consistency constraints and connected component control to reduce planar dependency and suppress false positives. Extensive experiments on clinical datasets demonstrate the superior performance of FGAS, improving the Dice similarity coefficient from 42.8% of baseline models to 70.6%, outperforming existing state-of-the-art UDA and multi-plane methods. These results indicate that FGAS can achieve robust, high-accuracy automated reconstruction for annotation-free multi-view and cross-domain image analysis.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

The incidence of uterine fibroids is approximately 50% to 60% globally, rising to 70% by the age of 50, and they are the most common benign tumors in the female reproductive system1. The use of multi-planar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including sagittal, coronal, and transverse views2, has become a standardized protocol for the clinical assessment of uterine fibroids3. By jointly examining orthogonal planes, radiologists can comprehensively evaluate fibroid morphology, location, and its spatial relationship with the uterine wall and cavity4–6. Such multi-view acquisition is indispensable in routine diagnosis, treatment selection, and longitudinal follow-up. Beyond visual inspection, three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction from these images provides direct clinical utility. It offers intuitive stereoscopic guidance for surgical planning, such as myomectomy7 and high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) therapy8, enables accurate volumetric monitoring of fibroid growth and treatment response9, and supports patient-specific education and communication. However, the clinical reliability of 3D reconstruction critically depends on accurate and consistent segmentation across multiple imaging planes. Accurate and robust segmentation across multiple planes is essential for lesion localization, volumetric quantification, and morphological analysis, guiding clinical decisions and therapeutic evaluation10,11. The practical use of these images are often faced with a number of difficulties. First, manual segmentation across sparsely covered multiple planes is time-consuming and error prone12,13. Experienced radiologists must maintain annotation consistency across different views, resulting in substantially higher annotation burden. Second, insufficient cross-plane generalization remains a core bottleneck of existing models. Most automatic segmentation models are trained on single-plane data, particularly T2-weighted sagittal images14. Some uterine fibroid segmentation studies3,15, demonstrate strong performance when applied to single-planar MRI. However, these methods are typically developed and evaluated under a single-plane setting, and their performance degrades substantially when extended to multi-planar data. For instance, a model pre-trained on sagittal data has Dice scores of only 36.5% and 32.5%, respectively, on coronal and transverse views (Fig. 1a, c). In addition, although recent studies have explored multi-planar uterine fibroid segmentation16, their performance remains sensitive to variations in scanners, acquisition protocols, and image quality, and segmentation accuracy deteriorates noticeably when applied to data acquired under different clinical conditions. In real-world clinical deployment, such domain shifts introduce substantial distribution gaps between the training (source domain)17 and testing (target domain) data18, severely limiting the robustness and generalizability of segmentation models (Fig. 2b, c).

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA)19 has been proposed to mitigate domain discrepancies without requiring target-domain annotations. Meanwhile, recent advances in segmentation foundation models, such as MedSAM220, SAMMed3D21, and nnInteractive22, have demonstrated remarkable robustness under weak supervision, cross-device variability, and low-quality annotations23,24. These models, pre-trained on large-scale, cross-domain datasets, demonstrate strong robustness under weakly annotated or cross-device conditions, offering a promising solution for addressing data scarcity and domain shift in medical image analysis. Nevertheless, their application to multi-planar MRI segmentation remains limited. First, existing models typically treat images acquired from different planes as independent samples. As a result, they cannot exploit the inherent structural consistency across views, leading to notable deficiencies in cross-plane generalization when applied to different imaging orientations25. Second, the outputs of foundation models often suffer from over-expanded boundaries, blurred contours, or connectivity defects(as shown in Fig. 1b), failing to meet the precision requirements for anatomical analysis26.

To overcome the above limitations, we propose a foundation model-guided, unsupervised domain adaptive segmentation framework (FGAS) aimed at simultaneously reducing manual annotation efforts and enhancing cross-domain, cross-plane generalization for multi-planar MRI segmentation(as shown in Fig. 2a). The proposed framework establishes an adaptive training workflow that integrates pseudo-label generation, foundation model-based structural correction, and iterative target-model optimization. Through this closed-loop mechanism, the cross-domain representational capacity of the foundation model is incorporated into the training process. To ensure cross-plane generalization, we introduce a cross-view consistency loss function that enforces learning of shared anatomical representations across orthogonal planes, substantially improving the robustness across all views. In addition, to mitigate the issue of over-expansion of the boundaries and connected false positives27,28, we propose a structure connectivity regularization. This constraint effectively suppresses noisy regions while preserving anatomical continuity and completeness, yielding segmentation results that better adhere to true anatomical morphology. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on offline pseudo-labels or single-plane training, the proposed framework enables the model to learn shared uterine structural features from multi-planar MRI while avoiding boundary drift and spurious connectivity caused by accumulated pseudo-label errors. This design leads to a more stable, interpretable, robust, and automated cross-plane uterine fibroid segmentation.

Results

Dataset details

1) Public Dataset: We used an open uterine fibroid MRI dataset containing 300 sagittal T2-weighted sequences29. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set with 270 cases and a source-domain test set of 30 cases. This training set is used for the cross-domain pre-training of the nnUNet backbone network30. The source-domain test set was used to evaluate the baseline performance of the model under its original data distribution.

2) Private Clinical Dataset: This dataset was collected from Tongren Hospital from 2019 to 2024 (Ethical Approval: Tongren Lun Shen K2024-052-01), including 620 patients with uterine fibroids, with a total of 1853 multi-planar T2-weighted MRI scans (sagittal, coronal, and transverse). All scans were resampled using linear interpolation to an isotropic resolution of 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3, during which the discrete voxel grids of the sagittal, coronal, and transverse planes were precisely aligned to the inherently unified world coordinate system of clinical 3D volumetric MRI acquisitions, which serves as the common spatial reference for all orthogonal planes reconstructed from the same volumetric scan. This preprocessing step ensures a consistent voxel spatial correspondence across the sagittal, coronal, and transverse planes, establishing a rigorous spatial foundation for the subsequent voxel-wise multi-view consistency constraint calculation. In addition, due to patient positioning, equipment, and other clinical scanning factors, some patients had missing imaging planes. To construct an independent validation set, 100 patients were randomly selected from the cohort, yielding 300 multi-planar scans. These scans were annotated on a pixel-wise basis in a double-blind fashion by two radiologists with over five years of clinical imaging annotation experience. The annotated anatomical structures comprised the uterine wall, uterine cavity, uterine fibroids, and Nabothian cysts. All 300 multi-planar MRI scans were used exclusively as an independent test set for final performance evaluation.

Comparison with state-of-the-art UDA methods

To further validate the superiority of the FGAS framework in addressing the challenges of domain-cross and multi-planar uterine fibroid MRI segmentation, this section compares FGAS with two baseline settings (source-only and target-only) and four representative unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) methods, including PseudoSeg31, entropy minimization adaptation (TENT)32, FixMatch33, and SAM-guided iterative pseudo-label correction (IPLC)34.

The source-only setting refers to a model trained exclusively on source domain data without any adaptation to the target domain, serving as a baseline for evaluating the severity of domain shift. The target-only setting, in contrast, represents a performance when the model is trained directly on fully labeled target domain data (using ground truth annotations), providing a reference for the maximum achievable performance under ideal conditions. The selected comparison methods cover four mainstream UDA paradigms. This not only enables a comprehensive evaluation of FGAS’s performance advantages across different UDA technical routes but also highlights its targeted optimization value in multi-planar medical image segmentation scenarios through comparison with medical-specific foundation models.

The four representative UDA methods exhibit a gradient of performance improvement yet each has inherent limitations. PseudoSeg31 improves segmentation performance through structured pseudo-label design, but it lacks geometric augmentations and employs a complex pseudo-label fusion strategy, which can reinforce errors under accumulated target-domain noise. TENT32 performs channel-wise adaptation via online entropy minimization without requiring source data, but its effectiveness is limited under significant domain shifts, its false positive rate remains high, and it does not explicitly address inter-plane structural differences. FixMatch33 and IPLC34 improve performance via pseudo-label optimization. FixMatch generates high-confidence pseudo-labels from weak augmentations and trains on strong augmentations, validating semi-supervised learning, but it was designed for classification and lacks direct adaptation for structured segmentation. IPLC leverages the SAM model with multi-sampling and entropy-weighted estimation, combined with iterative correction and regularization, further boosting DSC and demonstrating the advantage of medical foundation models. Nonetheless, both methods still exhibit high false positives and cross-plane inconsistency, as they rely on label optimization or single-plane strategies, failing to fully address multi-planar structural and anatomical variations.

The proposed FGAS method in this paper has achieved the best performance across all evaluation metrics, with three core advantages: First, leading overall performance—an average DSC of 70.6%, which is 2.3% higher than that of IPLC and 30.8% higher than that of the Source-only model. It is only 2.9% away from the manually labeled Target-only model, achieving near-supervised training accuracy without target domain annotations. Second, excellent cross-plane consistency—the DSC values across the transverse (70.2%), sagittal (71.1%), and coronal (70.6%) views are highly balanced, with a minimal fluctuation of 0.9%, far lower than that of comparative methods. This effectively addresses the plane dependency issue of traditional methods, providing stable and reliable segmentation results for multi-view clinical analysis. Third, it achieves a well-balanced performance in noise suppression and segmentation accuracy, with a false positive rate (FPR) of 4.8%, both metrics significantly lower than those of other UDA methods. This not only effectively suppresses false-positive regions but also ensures high spatial precision of segmentation contours by minimizing the maximum geometric deviation between predicted and ground-truth masks.

Comparison with state-of-the-art multi-planar methods

For the cross-plane alignment challenge, conventional methods typically rely on registration techniques. Transformation matrices are derived from MRI scans of the three orthogonal planes, and the segmentation mask with superior quality is then registered to the other two planes via these matrices. In this experiment, the pseudo-labels with superior segmentation performance derived from the sagittal plane were registered to the other two orthogonal planes using the mutual information-based registration method35. In addition to registration-based methods, we further conducted a systematic comparison between FGAS and two other representative multi-planar fully supervised segmentation approaches (MPUNet36, and tUNet37). Furthermore, we also compared three mainstream medical large model-based segmentation methods (MedSAM220, SAM-Med3D21, and nnInteractive22). To ensure a fair evaluation, for the three multi-plane methods, we selected 300 high-quality pseudo-labels generated by a pretrained model (corresponding to 100 patients × 3 views) as standardized full-supervision masks; for the three foundation model methods, the same data and these 300 masks were used as unified prompt inputs. Meanwhile, to validate the quality of these pseudo-labels, we also trained and tested a fully supervised model based on these 300 cases. This unified experimental design minimizes variations in prompt sources and supervision strength, enabling a more objective and fair assessment of FGAS’s performance advantages and applicability compared with both traditional multi-plane methods and medical foundation-model baselines.

First, the overall performance of the three multi-plane methods is limited by the quality of the pseudo-labels, with mean Dice scores between 53 and 65, and even lower for small structures such as Nabothian cysts. This is closely related to the technical characteristics of each method.

While the registration method yields improved segmentation results for the other two orthogonal planes, involuntary motions (e.g., breathing) during MRI acquisition may cause misalignment among the three planes under the same world coordinate system. This leads to imprecise registration outcomes, which exert a significant adverse impact on the preservation of fine-grained labels. Consequently, noise is introduced during model training, ultimately compromising the overall segmentation performance.

The MPUNet36, with multi-planar sampling and 2D U-Net extension by majority-voting as its core design concept, lacks anatomy-aware constraints. Consequently, it can only learn features through planar resampling at the statistical level, which renders it prone to segmentation deviations when undertaking segmentation tasks on small structures with complex anatomical configurations or sparse annotations.

Lindeijer et al.37 used a triple shared-weight encoder and single decoder framework, based on the UNet architecture. The three encoders process the annotated transverse view, unannotated sagittal view, and unannotated coronal view, respectively. Through the bottleneck layer, the feature dimensions of multi-view data are unified without relying on an explicit fusion mechanism. Meanwhile, a contrastive learning loss is introduced to constrain the consistency of feature representations for the same region of the same patient across different views, thereby exploiting complementary multi-view information. However, this method is essentially a supervised training paradigm, exhibiting a strong dependence on the annotation accuracy of the transverse view. When facing scenarios with missing annotations or the adoption of pseudolabels, its efficiency in utilizing information from sagittal and coronal views is limited, and the corresponding performance improvement effect is not sufficiently significant.

Second, methods based on the foundation model also show limited performance under unified pseudo-label prompts. MedSAM2 and SAM-Med3D achieve low Dice scores, especially failing to detect small structures, while nnInteractive improves somewhat but still falls far behind FGAS. Furthermore, due to the ambiguity of boundaries, the false positive phenomenon is pronounced. This indicates that even using the better 300 pseudo-labels, existing fully supervised or foundation-model methods struggle to fully leverage pseudo-label information in the absence of ground truth.

In contrast, the proposed FGAS has outperformed all methods used for comparison, achieving a mean Dice of 70.6, with the best performance across all four structures and three planes. Its advantages stem from the integrated design of pseudo-label optimization, multi-view consistency, and differentiable connectedness control. Cross-view consistency resolves the common conflicts in traditional multi-plane approaches, while differentiable connectedness control ensures reliable optimization of the foundation model. Notably, FGAS requires no ground truth (GT) and still achieves high-precision segmentation under pseudo-label limitations, highlighting its cost-effective and efficient potential for real clinical scenarios.

Ablation studies

To systematically assess the contribution of each module in FGAS, a stepwise ablation study was conducted. Starting from the pre-trained baseline, we gradually incorporated three key components: nnInteractive-based pseudo-label correction, multi-view consistency loss, and connected component control loss. All variants shared the same initialization (pre-trained nnUNet in Scene I) and training hyperparameters (30 training epochs), with the only difference being the inclusion of these modules. Experiments were performed in a cross-domain adaptation scenario, where the source domain was a single planar public dataset, and the target domain consisted of in-house multi-view MRI scans (n = 50 for testing). Quantitative evaluation included the average Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), DSC across sagittal (Sag), transverse (Tra), and coronal (Cor) views, and the false positive rate (FPR).

The baseline configuration was used directly to fine-tune the pre-trained model using raw pseudo-labels without any additional constraints. As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 5, this setting yielded limited improvement (average DSC 36.7%), with particularly poor results on non-source views (Tra 29.8%, Cor 37.9%). These findings highlight two critical limitations of raw pseudo-labels: (1) severe label noise impairs effective supervision, and (2) strong planar dependence of the pre-trained model prevents generalization across views.

Introducing nnInteractive-based correction improved pseudo-label quality by refining boundaries through foundation model-guided segmentation. This variant raised the average DSC from 36.7 to 49.9% (Tra 43.1%, Cor 49.5%) but increased the FPR to 12.0% due to over-correction in low-resolution or ambiguous regions. Moreover, the substantial gap between Sag and Tra (12.5%) indicated that pseudo-label refinement alone was insufficient to overcome planar domain shifts.

To enhance cross-view generalization, we incorporated a multi-view consistency loss that enforced alignment of segmentation predictions across sagittal, transverse, and coronal planes of the same patient. This addition produced a marked improvement, boosting the average DSC to 65.7% and reducing inter-view performance gaps (Sag 67.2%, Tra 63.5%). These results confirm that multi-view consistency effectively mitigates planar bias by promoting view-invariant anatomical representations.

Further, adding connected component control encouraged coherent and anatomically plausible predictions by penalizing fragmented outputs. This control reduced FPR from 12.0% to 4.3% and further increased the average DSC to 65.8%, validating its role in enforcing structural continuity and reducing false positives.

Finally, integrating all modules, including nnInteractive correction, multi-view consistency loss, and connected component control loss, yielded the full FGAS framework. This configuration achieved the best overall performance (average DSC 70.6%, Sag 71.1%, Tra 70.2%, Cor 70.6%, FPR 4.8%). Collectively, the modules provide complementary benefits: nnInteractive improves boundary precision, multi-view consistency enhances cross-plane generalization, and connected component control ensures anatomical coherence. Together, they form a synergistic pipeline that effectively addresses pseudo-label noise, planar dependence, and false positives advancing cross-domain multi-view segmentation toward fully supervised performance.

Discussion

Unlike conventional large model fine-tuning or direct transfer, FGAS does not use the foundation model as a final predictor, but designs it as a dynamic inference supervisor to provide structural priors and pseudo-label guidance. Despite strong structural perception capabilities, foundation models suffer from insufficient task-specific optimization, leading to poor generalization and false-positive expansion. To mitigate these issues, FGAS takes the high confidence segmentation results of the foundation model as pseudo-label references to fine-tune the task-specific model, enabling its adaptive adjustment to target domain features without any annotations. This design bridges task-agnostic large models and medical segmentation task-specific models, and leverages their complementary advantages to efficiently accomplish unsupervised domain adaptation tasks.

Furthermore, the proposed multi-view consistency constraint implicitly embeds anatomical topological knowledge into the optimization process. Unlike traditional consistency regularization, this multi-plane mechanism enforces anatomical invariance in sagittal, coronal, and transverse plane predictions, effectively mitigating feature shifts caused by different imaging planes. Experiments show this constraint markedly boosts cross-plane consistency (with an inter-plane Dice fluctuation of merely 0.9 points) and the model’s generalization to unseen views, delivering robust performance especially for blurred uterine wall boundaries or complex fibroid morphologies. Additionally, the connected component constraint loss further enhances the spatial continuity and anatomical integrity of predictions, effectively suppressing fragmented artifacts and false positives generated by traditional segmentation models at boundaries or low-signal regions, thus improving the clinical interpretability and reliability of segmentation results.

Our analysis reveals that while FGAS demonstrates strong overall performance, its efficacy is influenced by lesion size. Among the 100 annotated patients (300 scans), 26 patients (78 scans) presented with very small fibroids (<5 mm diameter); the average Dice for fibroids across all scans was 79.7%, yet performance on this challenging subset declined to 67.8%, representing an 11.9 point reduction. Notably, despite this attenuation, FGAS outperformed all compared methods on this subset and achieved performance close to that of the fully-supervised nnUNet model (69.3%). The performance drop primarily stems from the extremely limited voxel representation of minute lesions (typically < 200 voxels), which constrains fine-grained feature extraction against dominant background tissue. However, FGAS maintained clinically acceptable segmentations without complete misses, demonstrating robustness even in challenging small-object scenarios. In addition, we also conducted a dedicated analysis to investigate whether noise in pseudo-labels affects convergence and final segmentation accuracy. Experimental results in the supplementary material (Supplementary Experiment 1) demonstrate that our method is insensitive to errors in anatomical priors, and that the inconsistency of pseudo-labels mainly influences the intermediate optimization process rather than model convergence and final segmentation accuracy.

In addition to this robustness analysis, we further evaluated the clinical significance and statistical significance of the proposed model: based on our segmentation results, clinically relevant indicators such as the maximum diameter and number of uterine fibroids can be accurately measured, and in the 100 patients included in our test set, a total of 270 fibroids were identified, with the maximum diameter reaching 160.04 mm, such quantitative information is highly valuable for preoperative surgical planning and clinical decision-making. Additionally, statistical analysis was performed on the entire test set using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, a commonly used non-parametric method in medical image analysis, and the results demonstrate that the performance improvements achieved by our method are statistically significant (p < 0.05) across all evaluation metrics.

Despite these merits, the method can be enhanced by considering the following aspects. First, pseudo-label generation depends on the initial performance of the pre-trained nnUNet, with early prediction errors potentially propagating as noise during optimization. Second, while effective under standard imaging conditions, performance degrades in challenging scenarios including ultra-small fibroids (<5 mm), and severe motion artifacts. These limitations call for targeted enhancements in future work. Third, the current multi-label design follows a manually defined anatomical sequence; future research could explore dynamic label interaction or foundation model-guided prompt learning for more flexible segmentation. Additionally, though FGAS approaches fully supervised performance in our experiments, its robustness for extremely low-quality or small-sample target domains needs further validation. Computational efficiency could also be improved in future iterations by exploring lightweight interactive models or selective online refinement, to better support real-time clinical workflows.

Methods

Study design

The overall workflow of this study is illustrated in Fig. 6. The proposed FGAS framework consists of pseudo-label optimization, multi-view collaboration, and anatomical constraints, enabling end-to-end optimization for accurate 3D reconstruction of the uterus. The FGAS framework is built upon a base model pre-trained on single-planar MRI data from public dataset, then this pre-trained model takes cross-domain multi-planar data as input and performs fine-tuning through collaborative multi-module processing, ultimately producing high-quality segmentation of four structures (uterine wall, uterine cavity, uterine fibroids, and Nabothian cysts).

Overview of our method.

The yellow dashed box represents the pre-trained model obtained on the labeled public dataset (it can also represent the pre-trained model for the low-quality annotation scenario). The red dashed box indicates that in the Fine-tuning phase, multi-planar unlabeled data (tra, sag, cor) are input. The encoder and decoder share the weights from the pre-training phase to generate initial segmentation results. Combined with multiple modules, the segmentation results of multiple labels (Label 1–4) are optimized and used as pseudo-GT to fine-tune the model, ultimately achieving cross-center domain and cross-plane domain transfer.

First, the pre-trained model establishes the foundational segmentation capability. However, due to cross-domain distribution shifts, cross-plane differences, and weak information in small structures, these initial masks often exhibit blurred boundaries, shown in (Fig. 1b, c). To address this, the nnInteractive model22 is employed to enhance the initial masks, using a dual guidance strategy of bounding boxes and point prompts to improve pseudo-label quality: the bounding box of the initial mask defines the spatial extent of the structure, while key points sampled in boundary ambiguous regions (e.g., the interface between uterine wall and fibroids) and small structures (e.g., Nabothian cysts) specify class and boundary information, thereby significantly improving the accuracy of pseudo-labeling.

Next, supervision reliability is enhanced through filtering and multi-view consistency. The interactive-optimized masks undergo a two-step filtering process: first, masks with Dice similarity below 0.5 relative to the initial mask are discarded; second, the largest connected component is extracted to remove isolated noise regions, resulting in better pseudo-labels used for Dice loss supervision. In addition, a multi-view consistency loss is introduced to constrain the anatomical consistency across transverse, sagittal, and coronal planes, effectively mitigating the limitations of single-view predictions.

Finally, the proposed FGAS incorporates anatomical constraints to enhance the medical plausibility of the segmentation results. To mitigate region overlap and anatomical conflicts in multi-class segmentation, a sequential segmentation strategy is adopted: the uterine wall, fibroid, uterine cavity, and cyst are segmented in sequence, with each preceding structure providing spatial constraints for the subsequent one. In addition, a connected-component constraint loss function penalizes disconnected predictions to ensure structural integrity and continuity. Consequently, the model produces anatomically coherent and clinically reliable segmentation results.

During the fine-tuning phase of the model, we used 201 multi-planar MRI scans from 67 patients as the target domain data, and the fine-tuning process was completed within 20 epochs to achieve the optimal segmentation performance. At test time, the fine-tuned model runs independently of nnInteractive, with the segmentation of a single uterine fibroid MRI case completed in less than 30 seconds.

Algorithm: prompt-driven multi-view pseudo-label refinement

For each anatomical label, we first identify the slice that contains the most informative content and generate a bounding box (bbox)38 prompt based on that slice. For Labels 1 (uterine wall) and 2 (cavity), we extract two points from the largest connected component to serve as point prompts. For Label 3 (fibroids), since multiple lesions may exist, we randomly extract two points for each lesion as point prompts. Label 4 (cysts), which typically appear as small point-like structures, only uses point prompts. The final prompt for each label is a combination of the bbox and point prompts, except for Label 4, which relies solely on point prompts. For point prompt generation, we employ a hybrid strategy of deterministic connected component centroid extraction and random supplementary sampling. Notably, no negative prompts are used in the entire prompt design strategy, and the segmentation guidance of the nnInteractive model is solely realized through positive point prompts and bbox prompts.

These refined pseudo-labels better conform to anatomical structures and provide more reliable supervision for subsequent model training. To ensure training data reliability, we adopt dual-consistency filtering and largest connected component selection:

Consistency Filtering: Compare refined pseudo-label Prefined with initial pseudo-label Pinit, and discard if Dice coefficient < τ = 0.5.

Largest Connected Component (LCC) Selection: Remove isolated fragments and retain the largest connected component.
