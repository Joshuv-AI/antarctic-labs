Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
The DEMO-FTES project sought to demonstrate the thermal efficiency of fracture thermal
energy storage (FTES) through numerical simulations, laboratory and meso-scale field tests. A
detailed dimensional and scaling analysis was performed to identify key parameters and how
they can be most effectively scaled to the laboratory and decameter scale. Numerical models
were developed and used for three purposes: 1. Before field experiments, numerical modelling
can be used to estimate fracture properties based on previous data from the EGS Collab
experiment and then predict thermal hydrological behaviors of the fracture system with hot
water injection/withdrawal, therefore, help to design the experiments (e.g., to decide the
duration of the cycles based on the flow rate the pump can provide, and the estimated fracture
properties); 2. After the field experiment, to estimate the system properties during the
experiment (as the size and shape of a fracture could change over time), and understand why
system performance is different than what has been predicted, i.e., to help understand the
meso-scale test; and, 3. To model the lab experiments and estimate fracture properties and
storage efficiency. Ultimately, the experiment and numerical models could shed light on the
processes and uncertainty happening during fracture activation and help understand the scaling
between lab and field test, and finally, the design and optimization of potential fracture thermal
energy storage systems

Summary of Research Results:
The objective of the DEMO-FTES project is to determine the feasibility of fracture thermal energy
storage (FTES) through numerical, laboratory, and small-scale field tests. While aquifer thermal
energy storage (ATES) provides a solution for long duration energy storage, it requires suitable
conditions to exist naturally. The FTES technology has the potential to make the benefits of
subsurface energy thermal storage available to a much larger population living in areas without
aquifers suitable or available for ATES. FTES involves creating high-permeability flow paths
between a set of wells in otherwise low-permeability formations by creating hydraulic fractures
between the wells, and circulating hot or cold fluids between the wells for storing and
recovering thermal energy.

The project uses dimensional analysis and existing state-of-the-art numerical simulators to
design carefully scaled laboratory and 10-meter-scale field tests of the thermal efficiency of
FTES. The existing advanced laboratory and intermediate-scale field testbeds that will be used

for this project will allow for detailed monitoring of the system performance during the test and
of how the performance changes across time and length scales. These results will determine the
feasibility and highlight challenges of full-scale FTES systems.

Specifically, LBNL was tasked to use numerical simulations for: 1. helping with the design of the
field experiments; 2. Analyzing the field experiments; and 3 analyzing the lab experiments.
Details of the three tasks can be found in the “DEMO-FTES Final Report”, which will become
available at the end of the project. A summary is provided here.

Task 1 – help design the field experiment.

The field test took use of the fracture network created during the EGS Collab experiment, i.e.,
where the EGS Collab Experiment 3 thermal circulation test took place. As a result, we
calibrated our model and fracture parameters using the EGS experiment data collected
between June 17 and August 4, 2022. Using the calibrated model, a number of scenarios with
different length of loading/unloading periods were investigated. The idea was to use existing
infrastructure (e.g., wells) for this project. The examined scenarios could have take any existing
wells but no new wells. Among those, the scenario consisting of 50 days loading and 50 days
unloading using well TU as injection well has the best thermal performance. Figure 1 shows the
calibrated fracture shape and permeability (left), and temperature prediction for the preferred
storage scenario at different times.

Task 2 – Model the field experiment.

Model calibration was mainly focused on the Phase I test, in which hot water was injected
through TC and produced out of TN and TL from approximately 12/13/2024, 20:00:00 to
02/23/2025.

Figure 2. History match for the field test between 12/14/2024, 00:00:00 to 02/23/2025,
11:00:00.

Figure 2 shows the history matching of the field test between 12/14/2024, 00:00:00 to
02/23/2025, 11:00:00. During this period, hot water was injected through TC and flew out of TN
and TL. The pressure was prescribed at TC, and flow at TC was used as an observation to be
matched to the actual flow. Injected hot water gradually heat up the rock around it, causing its
thermal expansion, and as a result, reducing fracture spacing and therefore, fracture
permeability. In the test, the injection rate at TC was decreased after approximately 30 days to
mitigate a substantial rise in injection pressure. We have suspected a shift in stress field, and
fracture (center) location and shape. Even though we have modified the iTOUGH code to reflect
potential narrowing of the fracture and reduction of fracture permeability due to thermal
expansion of the rock, the mechanical effect on stress field was not captured. Inaccurate flow
rates in the model results in mismatch in temperature as well. The field test clearly
demonstrated the complex coupled processes in a fractured system, and importance of
understanding them, including them in the predictive models.

Task 3 – Model the lab experiment.

For the lab experiments, we focused on two of the single fracture experiments.

The experiment setup can be found at Mathey et al. (2026)1. The main advantage of a lab
experiment is that it is well controlled, and the exact fracture location can be measured. A 3D
model using the actual sample size and fracture location was built for history matching effort. It
was only after a few iterations when the model honors all the details of the experiment setup
(i.e., boundary conditions, well setup etc.), was the model able to match the measured data, as
shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3. History match for the single fracture lab experiment performed on 4/16/2025.

The history match was realized using the modified iTOUGH2 code, in which the aperture of the
fracture is reduced as temperature increases, and permeability as a function of the aperture
(power law), also reduced. As a result the pressure change is matched well. The temperature
can only be matched well with the accurate boundary conditions. For the experiment
performed on 4/16/2025, it is estimated that the aperture has a 30% reduction after hot water
injection and permeability had about a reduction of 60%.

Figure 4. Energy balance for the 4/16/2025 experiment.

When examing the energy balance for this experiment (Figure 4), most of the injected heat flow
goes to the rock through fracture. There is also a significant portion goes to the rock through
injection well. For this particular experiment, the total energy change in the rock shows that
most of the energy injected is not recovered. We also modeled an experiment in which energy
is stored and recovered during a cycle using the same method (i.e., fracture aperture is a
function of temperature, fracture permeability is a function of aperture).

Conclusions: The lab experiment provides high quality data for understanding fracture thermal
energy storage. By incorporating detailed experimental setup and fracture aperture reduction
due to thermal expansion from hot water injection, we were able to model the lab experiment
relatively well. This illustrates that it is important to use the targetted temperature injection to
understand the actual system injectivity. It also illustrates that the change in fracture properties
can be estimated, although it is not as easy in the field as in the lab.
