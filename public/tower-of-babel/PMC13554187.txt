Medicine ball throw distance weighted with height-to-mass ratio aligns with other physical fitness tasks
Authors: Gerald Jarnig, Reinhold Kliegl, Reinhold Kerbl, Mireille NM van Poppel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe traditional Medicine Ball Throw (MBTT) for assessing upper-extremity explosive strength inherently favours heavier and taller participants. We designed three alternative indices to adjust for this anthropometric bias: (1) multiplying MBTT by the height-to-mass ratio (MBTA1), (2) dividing MBTT by the product of mass and height (MBTA2), and (3) dividing MBTT by the body mass index (MBTA3). Using age- and sex-adjusted LMS-based z-scores from two independent cohorts (821 children, aged 8.3 ± 0.7 years; 354 adolescents, aged 14.4 ± 2.2 years), we compared the variants’ alignment with the concave BMI-profiles of five standard fitness tests (6-minute run, standing long jump, jump sideways, 4 × 10 m shuttle run, push-ups). The three anthropometrically enriched variants yielded significantly better goodness of fit than MBTT with MBTA1 performing best overall. Furthermore, when deployed as independent covariates, the enriched indices demonstrated systematically higher predictive power for standard fitness tasks than the unadjusted distance. Introduction

Globally, the prevalence of obesity and extreme obesity has increased alarmingly in recent years1. Elevated Body Mass Index (BMI) values, waist circumference, and body fat percentage positively correlate with reduced fitness and a higher risk of metabolic syndrome later in life2. Physical fitness in youth is a critical health indicator3; conversely, paediatric obesity strongly associates with adult cardiovascular risk factors4and coronary heart disease5. Consequently, school-based physical activity interventions are deployed worldwide to counter the obesity pandemic6,7. Monitoring the efficacy of these strategies requires national and international fitness screening systems8using test batteries that are reliable, valid, cost-effective, and easy to administer.9

Beyond basic anthropometrics, comprehensive fitness monitoring in children and adolescents typically targets a latent construct of motor tasks comprising endurance, coordination, flexibility, speed, and muscle power10,11. The parameter “muscle power” distinguishes between explosive strength and strength endurance, static and dynamic strength, and lower and upper body muscular strength12. There are a variety of field tests that can be used to assess upper body muscular strength12,13. While field tests for lower-body power (e.g., jumping) are easily implemented, many traditional upper-body assessments are poorly suited for large-scale school screenings due to prolonged administration times (e.g., timed hangs), equipment costs (e.g., handgrip dynamometers costing > €250), or structural barriers (e.g., pull-up bars).14

The medicine ball throw (MBT) circumvents these limitations as a very easy-to-administer and low-cost field test, yet its traditional assessment via throwing distance (MBTT) fundamentally misaligns with other standard motor tasks, specifically tasks taxing cardiorespiratory endurance, lower body muscular strength, body coordination, action speed, and upper body muscular endurance10. While tasks like running and jumping display concave relationships where high body mass acts as a severe biomechanical penalty, MBTTcorrelates positively with height, mass, and BMI15,16. This divergence occurs because MBTTinherently favours overweight children and adolescents due to their typically higher absolute fat-free mass and longer structural lever arms. Furthermore, it is the only common power task that does not require an individual to accelerate their own body mass against gravity15. Additionally, a reliable correlation exists between a participant’s body mass and their strength performance17. Failing to adjust for these growth metrics results in a systematic misjudgement of true explosive strength18. Because upper-body power remains a vital indicator of overall physical fitness, a method to preserve the MBT’s operational simplicity while neutralizing its structural size bias is highly desirable.

To address this, we propose enriching the traditional throwing distance (MBTT) with anthropometric data, yielding three alternative indices. We evaluate whether these adjusted variants align more accurately with the biological BMI profiles of standard fitness tasks and assess their efficiency as performance predictors. The generalizability of these modelling approaches is validated with cross-sectional cohorts from primary, secondary, and high schools.

Methods

Study participants

The participants of this secondary analysis were selected from two different study populations. Study Population 1 (SP1)19 comprised a primary school cohort of 821 children (419 girls, 402 boys) in grades 2 and 3, with a mean age of 8.3 ± 0.7 years. Study Population 2 (SP2)20 consisted of 354 adolescents (157 boys, 197 girls) from a secondary school (SS, n = 178) and a high school (HS, n = 176), with a mean age of 14.4 ± 2.2 years (see Supplement Figure S1). Due to strict time constraints the push-up test was only conducted in the secondary school cohort (n = 178). Both studies were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Graz, Styria, Austria. Active informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants or, from the adolescents themselves if they were older than 14 years. Demographic data including age, sex, and school type were recorded at enrolment.

Procedure

Anthropometric measurements and physical-fitness testing were conducted by trained research team members at the participating schools during regular physical education classes. All assessments took place in indoor sport halls. Participants wore standard sports attire and were tested barefoot, except for the 6-minute run (6MR), which required athletic sneakers.

Anthropometrics

Body height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a mobile stadiometer (SECA 213, Hamburg, Germany), and body mass was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using an electronic floor scale (BOSCH PPW4202/01, Nuremberg, Germany). BMI was calculated as body mass divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).

Physical fitness assessments (SP1)

Cardiorespiratory endurance was assessed via the 6-minute run (6MR), lower body strength via standing long jump (SLJ), body coordination via jumping sideways test (JS), and action speed via a 4 × 10-meter shuttle run (4 × 10SHR). The action speed (m/s) was scored by dividing the total distance run (i.e., 40 m) by the elapsed running time (4 × 10SHR) in seconds. Upper body strength was measured using the traditional standing 1-kg medicine ball chest throw (MBTT). All fitness tests followed the established field methodology described by Jarnig et al. (2022).19

Physical fitness assessments (SP2)

In the adolescent cohort (SP2), secondary school students (grades: 5 to 8) and high school students (grades: 9 to 12; maximum age: 18 years) completed the medicine ball overhead throw (MBTSOT−2 kg). From a stationary standing position at the starting line, participants held a 2 kg medicine with both hands behind their heads and threw it forward overhead as far as possible. The shortest distance from the starting line to the initial impact point was recorded measured to the nearest centimetre using a tape measure. The maximum distance from two valid attempts was used for analysis.

All SP2 participants completed the SLJ following identical protocols to SP1. Additionally, the secondary school subgroup (n= 178) completed the push-up test (PU) according to the German Motor Test guidelines21. From a prone starting position, participants touched their hands together behind their lower back, placed their hands adjacent to their shoulders, and pushed up into a fully extended plank position. At the peak of extension, they lifted one hand to touch the back of the opposing hand before returning to the starting position in a controlled manner. The score reflected the number of correctly executed repetitions completed within 40 s.

Anthropometrically enriched measures of MBT

Traditional MBTT performance assessment relies on the absolute throwing distance, which is conventionally adjusted for age and sex. However, raw performance is heavily confounded by HB and MBdue to biomechanical advantages.16,22 To mitigate this systemic bias, we designed three alternative indices that explicitly integrate individual growth metrics to isolate true upper-body explosive capacity:

MBTA1. This index multiplies the absolute throwing distance by the height-to-mass ratio (HMR):

The resulting physical unit is square centimetres per kilogram cm²/kg (or cm²kg− 1).

MBTA2. This index divides the absolute throwing distance by the product of body height and body mass:

Because length units (cm) cancel out within the fraction, the resulting physical unit is inverse kilograms (1/kg or kg− 1).

MBTA3. This index scales the absolute throwing distance relative to the Body Mass Index (BMI).

The resulting physical unit is cubic meters per kilogram: m³/kg (or: m³kg− 1).

Standardization and classification

To avoid structural distortions when mixing external reference populations, all anthropometric, fitness, and MBTA variables were standardized using internal cohort metrics. Age- and sex-adjusted z-scores were calculated independently within each cohort (n = 821 for SP1; n = 354 for SP2; PU: n= 178) utilizing the generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) framework to execute the LMS method.23 Smooth reference centiles were generated modelling the skewness (L), median (M), and coefficient of variation (S) using the Box-Cox Cole-Green (BCCG) family distribution.24 For descriptive baseline purposes, raw BMI parameters were also mapped onto the international 5-level International Obesity Taskforce (IOTF) weight classification system (thinness, normal weight, overweight, obesity, and morbid obesity).25

Statistical inference with linear mixed models (LMMs)

Primary analyses targeted the primary school cohort (SP1), while secondary and high school datasets (SP2) evaluated the generalizability of the modelling structures to adolescents.

The first set of LMMs assessed the alignment of the three anthropometrically enriched MBTA variants compared with the traditional MBTT across standard performance profiles. We specified four independent models where a five-level repeated-measures factor (task) represented the dependent variables: the four physical-fitness tasks plus either MBTT or one of the 3 MBTA variants. Fixed effects evaluated quadratic BMI and school grade. Random factors accounted for child- and school-level nesting, providing variance components (VCs) and correlation parameters (CPs) across the five repeated motor metrics.

The second set of LMMs evaluated the comparative predictive efficiency of the traditional and alternative metrics. Rather than acting as a dependent level of the task factor, each MBT index was repositioned as a fixed-effect covariate (independent predictor) for the remaining physical-fitness tasks. Control covariates and random-effect nesting structures were identical to the first set of LMMs.

Because all four MBT indices and standard fitness metrics were converted into unitless, internally standardized z-scores prior to LMM estimation, the dependent variables occupied an identical metric scale. This statistical standardization isolates model comparisons from the arbitrary physical dimensions of the raw scores, enabling Likelihood Ratio Tests (LRT), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to directly reflect true relative goodness-of-fit. Since competitive model alternatives shared identical parameter dimensions and observations, delta values across LRT, AIC, and BIC converged identically.

Model optimization and selection proceeded by comparing LMM configurations with varying complexities in their fixed-effects structures. Final model selection was adjudicated using Likelihood Ratio Tests (LRT) and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), adopting the strict parsimony criterion where a more complex model alternative required an AIC reduction of more than five units to be selected over a simpler variant.26

For the adolescent cohort (SP2), an identical analysis strategy was deployed using the standing long jump and push-up as comparison fitness tasks. Given that these data were collected within only two schools, the random factor school was omitted from the variance components. Consequently, the fixed effect of grade was replaced with a binary fixed-effect factor representing school type (secondary vs. high school).

Statistical computations and visualization were implemented using the tidyverse (version 2.0.0)27 and easystats (version 0.7.5)28 ecologies in the R statistical environment (version 4.5.2).29 Cohort-specific LMS z-scores were computed via the gamlss package (version 5.5-0).24 LMM fitting and estimation were executed utilizing MixedModels.jl (version 5.1.0)30 within the Julia language framework (version 1.12.5)31 to optimize computational efficiency.

Use of artificial intelligence

AI assistants (Gemini Pro, Claude Sonnet 4.6) were consulted for brainstorming and technical language polishing. The authors cross-examined all outputs and retain complete ownership and responsibility for the integrity of the published content.

Among the MBTA options, MBTA1 demonstrated the strongest association with other physical-fitness tasks, but, except for PU (A1: 0.69, A2: 0.52, A3: 0.43), differences between the alternative indices remained below 0.10.

Variance components and correlation parameters

Variance components (VCs) and correlation parameters (CPs) of the 16 LMMs are documented in Supplement Table S7. Within the primary school sample (SP1), all child-related CPs were significantly positive. On average, they were numerically slightly larger with MBTA1 (r =.60) than with MBTA2 (r =.57) or MBTA3 (r =.58) for LMM1 and numerically slightly smaller for LMM2 (MBTA1: r =.49, MBTA2: r =.51, MBTA3: r =.53). Thus, when included as an indicator, MBTA1 yielded a slightly better-defined latent construct of physical fitness than did MBTA2 or MBTA3. Reciprocally, these individual variances contracted and shifted into the fixed-effect expressions when the enriched metrics were deployed directly as predictive covariates. Of course, these small differences are not significant. Residual correlation parameters across school-level nesting structures tracked with less systematic variance, presumably because there were only twelve schools for children and only three CPs for adolescents.

Discussion

Traditional and anthropometrically enriched MBTA

The medicine ball throw (MBT) is a straightforward, low-cost field test that enables rapid group testing. It has been used since 1930 to assess upper-body explosive strength32. Despite its practical advantages, it is rarely incorporated into international health-related fitness batteries33. A key reason for its omission is that throwing distance is directly confounded by body mass, reducing its validity for health-related screenings and systematically biasing performance rankings and talent identification protocols.16,]34

Three fundamental differences separate MBT from weight-bearing physical-fitness tasks. First, high body mass confers an inertial advantage in projectile tasks (i.e., force = mass × acceleration), whereas excess mass acts as a significant ballast performance cost in the other five physical-fitness tasks (6MR, SLJ, JS, 4 × 10 SHR, PU). Second, the MBT does not require participants to move their own body weight, whereas the other physical-fitness tasks require displacing body mass over a target time or distance.

Third, heavier children and adolescents often exhibit functional adaptations to habitual weight-bearing—effectively ‘training’ absolute muscular strength through daily activities—which benefits throwing distance but hinders performance in weight-dependent motor tasks.35[,36 Consequently, the observed divergence between MBTT and the rest of the fitness battery is biomechanically expected.

All three MBTA variants transform the assessment by penalizing excess body mass, neutralizing the structural advantage heavier children and adolescents possess in projectile tasks. This correction effectively shifts the raw metric from a proxy of absolute force into a measure of relative work efficiency, aligning the throw with running and jumping tasks. However, the indices differ in their biomechanical treatment of stature. The first formula (MBTA1) treats height as a functional asset. Conversely, in the second formula (MBTA2), applies height as a secondary performance penalty, while the third formula (MBTA3) weights the height asset even more heavily. Scaling positively for height in the first and third indices is physically justified: taller participants benefit from a higher release point and longer skeletal levers (arm span), which extend the force-application path and increase release velocity.

While both MBTA1 (HMR multiplication) and MBTA3 (BMI division) treat height as an asset, the theoretical preference for MBTA1 rests on the classical principles of allometric scaling. Biologically, muscle strength scales with muscle cross-sectional area (L2), whereas body mass scales with volume (L3)37. MBTA1 accounts for this dimensional discrepancy by scaling the absolute throw (a proxy for force, L2) by the height-to-mass ratio (L/L3 = L− 2), resulting in a scale-invariant metric (L2 L− 2 = 1). Although this assumption of isometric geometric similarity may not capture nuanced allometric shifts in body proportions during puberty, it provides a mathematically orthogonal measure of relative strength that isolates performance from absolute size. In contrast, MBTA3 normalizes by BMI (M/L2), which scales linearly with height (L). Consequently, MBTA3 fails to fully eliminate the size advantage, leaving a residual bias that artificially favours taller participants (L2/L = L). This theoretical preference for the height-to-mass ratio is supported by independent evidence showing that HMR-based models yield a superior fit compared to BMI models when predicting physical fitness38.

Empirically, all three MBTA variants achieved the intended qualitative alignment with weight-bearing physical-fitness tasks and outperformed the traditional MBTT. MBTA1 provided the best goodness of fit in three of the four LMM comparisons, though the differences in goodness-of-fit between the variants were minor. The single exception occurred in the primary-school cohort (6–9 years) where MBTA2 performed marginally better. This shift may reflect a developmental “coordination lag” in younger children, for whom greater height does not yet confer a biomechanical advantage because they cannot yet control longer limbs effectively. For young children, size acts primarily as “bulk”; during adolescence, it matures into functional “leverage.” This matches evidence indicating that anthropometric growth and motor-coordination development contribute independently to throwing mechanics across childhood.39–41

The empirical performance of MBTA1 aligns with the theoretical preference for a scale-invariant metric derived from the square-cube law (see above). The minor exception in the child cohort may simply represent a statistical miss for MBTA1 or a statistical false positive for MBTA2. Furthermore, because MBTA1 and MBTA2 correlate exceptionally high (r=.92 across both cohorts; see Supplement Table S2), the choice between them will rarely alter practical classifications. For reasons of theoretical consistency and parsimony, we recommend MBTA1 (throwing distance scaled by the height-to-mass ratio) as the standard method for youth fitness batteries, hereafter refer to as hmrMBT (height-to-mass ratio medicine ball throw).

Implications for participants physical-fitness assessment and sports training in general

It is standard practice to take participants’ sex and age into account when assessing their physical fitness. This increases the validity because performance is interpreted relative to developmental baselines, facilitating the tracking of training gains or developmental change. Trivially, all else being equal, taller participants run faster and jump further than smaller peers. For the same reason that performance is evaluated relative to age and sex, accounting for anthropometrics provides a more accurate, differentiated motor profile. For example, hmrMBT mitigates the confounding influence of rapid growth spurts or delayed physical maturation on physical-fitness tracking.

In athletic training, macro-, meso-, and micro-cycles focus on distinct physical objectives. The hmrMBTmay prove valuable in strength-and-conditioning programs where a secondary goal is reducing body mass over a targeted micro cycle42. Increasing assessment precision via indices like hmrMBT allows practitioners to track specific goals more effectively. Weight loss or gain due to illness, injury or training adaptations can be directly accounted for upon returning to training, making is easier to isolate true performance gains.

Compared with competing with peers, transparency and objective performance assessments have been shown to increase athletes’ personal motivation and self-assessment accuracy.43[,44 Furthermore, external and intrinsic motivation levels are directly tied to sports retention; as motivation rises, dropout rates decrease45.

In summary, more precise assessments lead to clearer communication of physical performance, ultimately increasing participant acceptance and motivation. Utilizing the hmrMBTmay (1) reduce anthropometric bias in youth competitions, (2) increase athletes’ understanding of coaching and educational decisions, and (3) improve the acceptance of performance feedback among athletes and their legal guardians46.

Limitations

As with any novel performance index, arguments for anthropometrically enriched carry the risk of misapplication. First, our modelling is restricted to body mass and height. In principle, other parameters could also be integrated; for instance, sitting height is relevant for calculating peak-height velocity (PHV) as a proxy of biological maturation during puberty. While hmrMBT effectively mitigates the impact of growth spurts on performance scoring, future research should incorporate maturation offsets to further refine these metrics, as omitting biological growth stages limits evaluation accuracy in paediatric populations.

Second, a mathematical limitation of the current modelling approach is the structural overlap between the MBTA variants and the covariates. Because MBTA1 (i.e., hmrMBT) incorporates the height-to-mass ratio (HMR) directly within the dependent variable, it is mathematically confounded with BMI (= m/h²) when the latter is used as a covariate. This structural overlap likely inflates the negative linear component of the relationship compared to other fitness tasks. However, the primary goal of this modelling was to achieve qualitative alignment with the established youth fitness profiles. All MBTA variants successfully replicated the significant inverted U-shape (negative quadratic) relationship with BMI observed in running and jumping tasks, successfully correcting the linear inertial bias found in traditional assessments. While the absolute magnitude of the BMI coefficients should not be compared directly across different task types due to these structural differences, the resulting quadratic profile provides a more realistic assessment of physical fitness.

Third, a single index may not suit all physical-fitness tasks. For example, a high body mass is an asset in American football, whereas a low body mass is required in endurance-specific disciplines. Some sports may benefit while others are negatively affected if specific demands are ignored. Moreover, for certain motor tests, anthropometric adjustments are unnecessary; for instance, anthropometrics can be safely ignored in reaction-time tasks, where nerve conduction speeds render minor variations in body size irrelevant.

Fourth, the most appropriate index may change across developmental stages. In our study, the small advantage of MBTA2 over hmrMBT in children may relate to a temporary lag in motor coordination development. Complementarily, during later development, adolescent growth spurts involve non-isometric changes in lever-arm mechanics and rigid-body proportions, which may introduce residual variance not captured by the scale-invariant metric. While we prefer hmrMBT for reasons of theoretical und practical parsimony, designing an index sensitive to developmental change remains a valuable topic for future research.

Fifth, measurement protocols for body height and body mass must be executed with high precision to minimize errors. Because these anthropometric values scale or divide the raw throwing distance, measurement errors can have compounding negative effects, substantially distorting individual assessment scores across multiple tests.

Sixth, increased data complexity often leads to unfamiliar data formats. The alternative MBTA variants introduce unusual physical units such as kg− 1, cm²kg− 1 and m³kg− 1. Clear explanations and targeted training will likely be necessary to achieve practical acceptance among physical education teachers, coaches, and administrators responsible for fitness testing.

Finally, a further limitation of this study is that these alternative indices have not yet been validated over longer periods within a longitudinal design. Additionally, field tests such as push-ups, jumping, and running tasks cannot provide the same level of kinetic precision as laboratory measurements.

Conclusion

The anthropometrically enriched hmrMBT index has the potential to optimize youth physical-fitness tracking by providing a fairer, closer monitoring of performance. This methodology could be extended to other tests within fitness batteries, such as the 6-minute run or the 4 × 10-meter shuttle run. Enhancing measurement validity in this manner can improve test acceptance and motivation among students and athletes, as well as the instructors and coaches evaluating them. Ultimately, this approach may support the implementation of large-scale or nationwide physical-fitness monitoring systems, an evaluation strategy that is urgently needed in the era of rising paediatric obesity.
