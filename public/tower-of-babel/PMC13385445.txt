Cloud-connected pluripotent stem cell platform enhances scientific identity in underrepresented students
Authors: Samira Vera-Choqqueccota, Drew Ehrlich, Vladimir Luna-Gomez, Sebastian Hernandez, Jesus Gonzalez-Ferrer, Hunter E. Schweiger, Kateryna Voitiuk, Yohei Rosen, Kivilcim Doganyigit, Isabel Cline, Rebecca Ward, Erika Yeh, Karen H. Miga, Barbara Des Rochers, Sri Kurniawan, David Haussler, Kristian López Vargas, Mircea Teodorescu, Mohammed A. Mostajo-Radji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryStem cell research offers unique opportunities for authentic scientific engagement, yet infrastructure requirements have confined participation to elite institutions, perpetuating workforce disparities. We developed an integrated framework combining stem cell engineering, cloud-connected microscopy, and psychometric assessment. The framework integrates a doxycycline-inducible NGN2 mouse embryonic stem cell (mESC) system, low-cost cloud microscopy, and the Stem Cell Research Identity Scale (SCRIS). Implementation across a high school and a community college demonstrated significant increases in scientific identity. Students using differentiating PSCs showed broader science identity gains than those using neuroblastoma cells, particularly in competence, research readiness, and recognition. High school students showed enhanced research competence gains compared to community college students despite equivalent intervention duration. Demographic analyses revealed enhanced effectiveness for Hispanic and first-generation college students. This framework provides a scalable model for broadening participation in biomedical research.

Introduction

Pluripotent stem cell (PSC) technologies have redefined our capacity to model human development and disease, yet participation remains geographically and socioeconomically constrained. The specialized infrastructure required for PSC research has confined the field to well-resourced institutions (Xu et al., 2024), creating persistent participation gaps that disproportionately exclude students from community colleges, minority-serving institutions, and under-resourced secondary schools (Estrada et al., 2016). While workforce development initiatives have expanded access to training, addressing both pedagogical and infrastructure barriers remains critical for broadening participation at scale (Carosso et al., 2019; Mostajo-Radji, 2025). Programs supported by the California Institute for Regenerative Medicine (CIRM), including Bridges and COMPASS, have made important progress by increasing opportunities for students at community colleges and non-research-intensive universities. However, extending these gains to underserved regions globally will require complementary approaches that can overcome the geographic and infrastructural limitations inherent to PSC research.

Project-based learning (PBL) improves engagement, scientific reasoning, and learning outcomes in biotechnology, particularly for students from underrepresented backgrounds (Hmelo-Silver, 2004; Ferreira et al., 2019). A key outcome of these approaches is the development of scientific identity, defined as the extent to which students see themselves as scientists and envision scientific careers, which predicts persistence in Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM) fields (Carlone and Johnson, 2007; Chemers et al., 2011; Hazari et al., 2010). While validated instruments exist to measure science identity broadly (Estrada et al., 2011), stem cell biology lacks tools that capture its specific technical and conceptual context.

Delivering such PBL experiences in PSC systems, however, remains challenging. Prior cloud-enabled implementations have relied on transformed cell lines lacking developmental relevance, and PSC differentiation protocols require weeks of maintenance incompatible with academic timelines (Baudin et al., 2022; Hossain et al., 2016; Vera-Choqqueccota et al., 2025; Sano et al., 2024). Here, we present an integrated framework combining engineered biology, cloud-connected microscopy, and quantitative assessment. We developed a doxycycline-inducible NGN2 mouse embryonic stem cell (mESC) line enabling rapid neuronal specification, and deployed a low-cost cloud microscopy platform for distributed real-time observation. To quantify educational impact, we developed and validated the Stem Cell Research Identity Scale (SCRIS), a multidimensional instrument measuring scientific identity in regenerative medicine contexts. Implementation across a Title I high school and an urban community college demonstrates that authentic stem cell research can be conducted outside traditional laboratory environments with measurable gains in scientific identity.

Results

A cloud-integrated platform enables rapid neuronal specification and distributed experimentation

To enable stem cell research within constrained academic timelines, we engineered a mESC line with doxycycline-inducible expression of the proneural transcription factor NGN2 (Figure 1) (Zhang et al., 2013). The genetic architecture comprises a human-codon optimized NGN2 transgene under TetO control integrated via PiggyBac transposition, alongside a constitutively expressed TagBFP reporter and puromycin resistance gene (Figure 1A). Following antibiotic selection, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) enriched for high and uniform blue fluorescent protein (BFP) expression, ensuring homogeneous starting populations (Figure S1). We optimized a differentiation protocol using Wnt and Tgf-β inhibition to promote rapid neural commitment in a 2D format (Figure 1C) (Hernandez et al., 2026). While cells in maintenance media showed no neural features, the combination of small molecule inhibitors and NGN2 induction produced synchronous differentiation within 48 h of doxycycline administration (Figure S2). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed progressive neuronal differentiation. By day 3, Pax6+ progenitors and early Map2+ neurons were observed, with expanded Map2+ populations by day 5 (Figure 1D). Cortical layer identity was confirmed by Brn2 (intermediate progenitors and callosal projection neurons) and Tle4 (corticofugal projection neurons) expression by day 5. By day 10, Tuj1 staining indicated mature neuronal morphology (Figure 1E). This five-day timeline enables complete observation of pluripotency-to-neuron transitions and cortical specification within standard academic schedules, without compromising developmental authenticity.

To enable remote observation across bandwidth-limited environments, we developed a low-cost cloud-connected microscopy platform optimized for continuous operation in standard tissue culture incubators (Figures 1F and 1G). This system builds on a modular in-incubator imaging architecture described in detail elsewhere (Ehrlich et al., 2026), with full assembly instructions provided in Note S1. The platform enables continuous micrometer-scale imaging using widely accessible streaming infrastructure, supporting real-time observation of stem cell differentiation in distributed educational settings.

The SCRIS provides validated measurement of scientific identity in stem cell education

To assess how participation in stem cell research influences scientific identity, we developed the SCRIS, a multidimensional instrument grounded in established science identity frameworks and adapted for the unique demands of regenerative medicine education (Carlone and Johnson, 2007; Hazari et al., 2010). The design process was informed by theoretical and empirical studies on science identity, with five initial dimensions identified: performance, competence, recognition, interest, and professional role models, comprising a total of 24 items (Table S1). Items were adapted from existing validated instruments and modified to reflect stem cell research context (Beghetto, 2007; Chen and Wei, 2022; Childers and Jones, 2017; Wulff et al., 2018).

The first validation step involved exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using data from 133 students enrolled in the University of California, Santa Cruz course BME80G: Bioethics in the 21st Century: Science, Business, and Society (Table S2), a population selected for its disciplinary diversity and exposure to stem cell concepts without specialized training. EFA was conducted using principal axis factoring with Promax rotation and Kaiser normalization. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ = 3540.659, p < 0.001), and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure was 0.890, exceeding recommended thresholds. Analysis revealed a four-factor model explaining 73.9% of total variance (Figures 2A and S3; Tables S3 and S4). Factor 1 (3.9% of the variance) was defined as “Performance and Self-Efficacy”. Factor 2 (18.7% of the variance) corresponded to “Competence and Research Readiness”. Factor 3 (8.2% of the variance) represented “Recognition”. Validation of the Stem Cell Research Identity Scale (SCRIS)

To confirm this structure in our target populations, we performed confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on 95 students from diverse educational settings, including AP Biology classes at Alisal High School, a Title I school serving a predominantly agricultural community in Salinas, CA; and the Biotechnology Program at Berkeley City College, an urban community college with significant populations of adult learners and first-generation immigrants (Figure 2B; Tables S5–S9). Items with low factor loadings or substantial cross-loadings were removed, yielding a final 16-item model (Table S10). Interfactor correlation analysis showed low to moderate correlations between factors (all factor correlations <0.80; Table S11), indicating that constructs are related yet distinct. Convergent validity was supported by average variance extracted (AVE) values exceeding 0.50 for all factors (Table S12), while discriminant validity was confirmed by heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratios below 0.85 (Table S13).

To examine whether a general science identity construct underlies the four dimensions, a higher-order CFA model was tested in which the four first-order factors loaded onto a general science identity construct. This model demonstrated acceptable fit (comparative fit index [CFI] = 0.964, Tucker-Lewis index [TLI] = 0.957, root-mean-square error of approximation [RMSEA] = 0.073, and standardized root-mean-square residual [SRMR] = 0.068). However, the original correlated four-factor model showed superior fit (CFI = 0.975, TLI = 0.970, RMSEA = 0.058, and SRMR = 0.059) and was therefore retained as the primary representation of the data (Table S14). All standardized factor loadings exceeded 0.50 and were statistically significant (p < 0.001).

To assess whether the instrument performs equivalently across educational contexts, measurement invariance was tested using multi-group CFA (Table S15). Changes in fit indices were evaluated using recommended thresholds (ΔCFI ≤0.01, ΔRMSEA ≤0.015, and ΔSRMR ≤0.03 for metric and ≤0.01 for scalar invariance; Chen, 2007). Metric invariance was supported (ΔCFI = −0.003, ΔRMSEA = −0.001, ΔSRMR = 0.014), indicating that factor loadings are equivalent across groups and that the relationships between latent constructs and their observed indicators are comparable. Scalar invariance was also supported (ΔCFI = −0.003, ΔRMSEA = 0.001, ΔSRMR = 0.000), indicating that item intercepts are equivalent across groups and that observed scores can be meaningfully compared. Together, these results suggest that the measurement model operates equivalently across high school and community college cohorts.

Cronbach’s alpha coefficients indicated strong internal consistency for each factor: Performance and Self-Efficacy (α = 0.903), Competence and Research Readiness (α = 0.888), Recognition (α = 0.954), and Career Interest and Role Models (α = 0.888), with high overall instrument reliability (α = 0.940). The final SCRIS thus provides a psychometrically robust and contextually specific instrument for assessing scientific identity in stem cell education settings.

Five-phase framework delivers authentic stem cell research across distributed sites

We deployed the SCRIS within a five-phase PBL framework across Alisal High School and Berkeley City College, both utilizing the NGN2 differentiation platform (Figure 3A). The curriculum progressed from remote instructional preparation and student-driven experimental design to collaborative cloud-based execution (Figure 3B), open-ended data analysis, and peer presentation with pre- and post-intervention SCRIS assessment (for details see STAR Methods). Pluripotent stem cells enhance research identity compared to neuroblastoma cells

To examine whether cell type influences science identity development, we built on our previous work showing that a neuroblastoma differentiation platform enhances STEM identity (Baudin et al., 2022) and collected SCRIS data from a new neuroblastoma cohort at Alisal High School (2024, n = 21) and the NGN2 cohort (2025, n = 32). Both cohorts used comparable cloud-based frameworks with the same instructor, though cohorts were separated by one academic year. Pre-intervention SCRIS scores were statistically equivalent between cohorts across all four dimensions (Figure 4A, all p > 0.05), although small to moderate effect sizes were observed (Cohen’s d = 0.29–0.66; Table S16). Both cohorts improved in Performance and Self-Efficacy and Competence and Research Readiness, but the NGN2 platform produced broader gains. Recognition and Career Interest and Role Models showed significant improvements only in the NGN2 cohort (Recognition p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.83; Career Interest and Role Models p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.44; (Figures 4B, 4C and S4; Tables S16 and S17). These findings suggest that use of authentic developmental systems with lineage specification and technical complexity may be associated with broader gains across multiple facets of scientific identity, particularly in dimensions related to research capability, professional recognition, and career aspiration, though cohort differences preclude definitive conclusions.

Platform produces differential science identity gains across institutional contexts

To assess whether educational setting influences science identity development, we compared outcomes across both institutions. Both Alisal High School (AP Biology, n = 32) and Berkeley City College (Biotechnology Program, n = 42), using the NGN2 platform with identical intervention duration, showed equivalent pre-intervention scores (p > 0.05; Cohen’s d = 0.06–0.32; Figure 4D; Table S16) and significant gains across all four dimensions post-intervention (p < 0.05; Cohen’s d = 0.44–1.28; Figures 4C and 4E; Table S16).

Post-intervention analysis revealed institutional differences in one specific dimension (Figure 4F). Alisal High School students showed significantly higher Competence and Research Readiness compared to Berkeley City College students (p < 0.05; Cohen’s d = 0.38; Table S16). This likely reflects differences in academic preparation: AP Biology students had recent biology coursework, while many Berkeley students were adult learners returning to biology after years away, despite entering with equivalent baseline identity scores. Performance and Self-Efficacy, Recognition, and Career Interest and Role Models were equivalent between institutions (all p > 0.05; Cohen’s d = 0.03–0.45; Table S16).

First-generation and hispanic students show enhanced science identity development

To understand whether science identity gains varied across student demographics, we examined outcomes by parental education, ethnicity, gender, and English language proficiency. First-generation college students showed significant increases across all four dimensions (all p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.45–1.13; Figures 4G–4J; Table S18). Continuing-generation students showed significant gains only in Recognition and Career Interest and Role Models (both p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.59 and 0.64, respectively; Table S18), with no changes in Performance and Self-Efficacy or Competence and Research Readiness.

Hispanic students demonstrated significant increases in Performance and Self-Efficacy (p < 0.05; Figure 4K; Table S18), Competence and Research Readiness (p < 0.001; Figure 4L; Table S18), and Recognition (p < 0.01; Figure 4M; Table S18). In contrast, students identifying as Asian or Asian-American, White or European, and Bi-racial or Multi-racial showed no significant pre-to-post changes in any dimension (all p > 0.05; Figures 4K–4N; Table S18). Career Interest and Role Models did not change significantly in any ethnic group (Figure 4N; Table S18).

Gender and English language proficiency, two factors known to influence STEM engagement and persistence (Preuss et al., 2020; Wulff et al., 2018), showed significant increases across most identity dimensions in both groups within each category, indicating broad platform effectiveness (Figure S5; Table S19).

Discussion

The NGN2-inducible mESC line enables five-day pluripotency-to-neuron transitions with cortical specification, addressing academic calendar constraints, while the cloud-connected platform enables participation across schools with varying network capabilities while reducing costs to a few dollars per student at scale (Mostajo-Radji, 2023). The SCRIS provides validated measurement with strong psychometrics, filling a gap in regenerative medicine education assessment across diverse settings. Biological authenticity, institutional context, and demographic factors each shaped science identity development, with significant gains across all dimensions in both institutional cohorts.

The SCRIS complements existing science identity instruments ranging from general measures (Estrada et al., 2011; Paul et al., 2020) to discipline-specific tools (Chen and Wei, 2022; Dou and Cian, 2022; Hazari et al., 2010; Tripp and Liu, 2026) by capturing identity formation specific to stem cell research contexts and underrepresented student populations.

The EFA suggested four distinct dimensions in the SCRIS. Among these, the Career Interest and Role Models factor accounted for a substantial proportion of variance. This aligns with meta-analytic evidence that STEM identity predicts STEM career intentions and varies across student groups (Jiang et al., 2025), highlighting the importance of aspirational engagement. Notably, the EFA sample was drawn from UCSC, a Hispanic-Serving Institution (HSI), and research at HSIs shows that Hispanic students have distinct cultural preferences in their role model selection (Estrada et al., 2018; Preuss et al., 2020), which may explain this factor’s prominence. While role models are often conceptualized as external predictors of science identity, we observed that role model impact items loaded strongly with career interest, suggesting that in stem cell research contexts, exposure to exemplars may function as an intrinsic component of identity formation rather than merely an outside influence (Chemers et al., 2011).

The prominence of this factor suggests direct implications for curriculum design and delivery. If aspirational and role model-driven components are salient in underrepresented populations, curricula may be more effective when they integrate diverse scientific role models and connect experimental work to real-world career pathways (Carosso et al., 2019). In contrast, in cohorts with stronger baseline preparation, emphasizing technical skill development and experimental autonomy may yield greater gains in competence-related identity dimensions.

By comparison, the final Performance and Self-Efficacy items reflect coursework self-efficacy rather than laboratory task confidence as originally theorized; subsequent SCRIS iterations could incorporate items more directly tied to laboratory tasks, test measurement invariance across educational contexts, and examine whether a higher-order construct underlies the four dimensions.

The neuroblastoma comparison used year-over-year cohorts; concurrent randomization would strengthen causal claims (Bloom et al., 1999). Because surveys were administered anonymously, pre- and post-intervention responses could not be paired, limiting interpretation to group-level shifts rather than individual-level change. Higher competence gains in AP Biology students likely reflect differences in prior preparation rather than institutional resources. Hispanic and first-generation students showed the strongest science identity and competence gains, suggesting that accessible, well-designed platforms can particularly benefit underrepresented populations by leveraging their unique community cultural wealth (Ackert et al., 2021; Yosso, 2005). Findings should be replicated across additional sites and larger cohorts, and longitudinal tracking would assess sustained career impact. Future implementations could include gamified virtual laboratory training and extension to human iPSCs or 3D organoid models to enhance translational relevance while addressing complexity and cost (Cvetkovic et al., 2024; Elliott et al., 2023; Ly et al., 2024).

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics statement and institutional oversight

This study was reviewed and approved by the University of California Santa Cruz Office of Research Compliance Administration and Institutional Review Board under protocol number HS-FY2023-264 and HS-FS2025-233.

mESCs

All experiments utilized an adapted BRUCE-4 mESC line derived from a male of the C57BL/6J strain (RRID: CVCL_K037; Millipore Sigma, Cat#SF-CMTI-2). Cell line identity was authenticated by the vendor prior to purchase. Cells were used between passages 22 and 30. Pluripotency was confirmed using Alkaline Phosphatase Live Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#A14353). Mycoplasma testing confirmed the lack of contamination (Universal Mycoplasma Detection Kit; ATCC, Cat#30-1012K). Sterility was confirmed by routine visual inspection throughout all experiments. Karyotypic stability of the BRUCE-4 line is documented by the vendor and available upon request.

Neuroblastoma cells

Neuro-2a neuroblastoma cells (RRID: CVCL_0470; ATCC, Cat#CCL-131), derived from a male albino mouse (A/Jax strain), were used for comparison experiments. Cell line identity was authenticated by the vendor prior to purchase. The cell line was routinely inspected for sterility.

Human participants

Three student cohorts participated in this study. Cohorts were drawn from UC Santa Cruz, Alisal High School (Salinas, CA), and Berkeley City College (Berkeley, CA). Implementation cohorts included AP Biology students at Alisal High School and Introductory Biotechnology students at Berkeley City College. Complete demographic information for all cohorts is available in Tables S2–S7. All participants provided informed consent.

Method details

mESC maintenance and Culture

Cells were maintained on Recombinant Human Vitronectin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#A14700)-coated plates. Plates were coated with Vitronectin (0.5 μg/mL in PBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#70011044) for 30 min. Maintenance utilized mESC media containing Glasgow Minimum Essential Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#11710035), Embryonic Stem Cell Qualified Fetal Bovine Serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#10439001), 0.1 mM MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#11140050), 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate (Millipore Sigma, Cat#S8636), 2 mM Glutamax supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#35050061), 0.1 mM 2-Mercaptoethanol (Millipore Sigma, Cat#M3148), and 0.05 mg/mL Primocin (InvivoGen, Cat#ant-pm-05). Media was supplemented with 1,000 units/mL of ESGRO Recombinant Mouse Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (Millipore Sigma, Cat#ESG1107). The media was changed daily. Dissociation and passages were performed using ReLeSR passaging reagent (Stem Cell Technologies, Cat#05872). Cell freezing used mFreSR cryopreservation medium (Stem Cell Technologies, Cat#05855).

Genetic engineering of doxycycline-inducible NGN2 mESC line

mESCs were co-transfected with a PiggyBac transposon vector encoding TetO-NGN2-T2A-rtTA and EF1α-PuroR-NLS-TagBFP (PB-TO-oNGN2; Addgene, Cat#198397; RRID: Addgene_198397), with a Super piggyBac Transposase expression plasmid (System Biosciences, Cat#PB210PA-1), using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#L3000015) per manufacturer’s instructions. The NGN2 sequence is human codon optimized. After 48 h recovery, Puromycin Dihydrochloride selection (1 μg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#A1113803) was applied for 5 days. BFP-positive cells were enriched via FACS on a BD FACSAria II, gating for the top 11% of fluorescence intensity.

Real-time qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat#74104) and reverse transcribed using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#18080051). Real-time qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#A28137) using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#4444557). Expression levels of NGN2 were normalized to Gapdh using the ΔΔCt method.

Neuronal differentiation

To generate NGN2 neurons, mESCs were dissociated using TrypLE Express Enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#12604021) for 7 min at 37°C and seeded at a density of 300,000 cells in 2 mL of maintenance media per well in 6-well plates pre-coated with Poly-L-ornithine (0.01%; Millipore Sigma, Cat#P4957) and Recombinant Human Vitronectin for 1 h, washed three times with PBS, then coated with Mouse Laminin (15 μg/mL; Corning, Cat#354232) and Human Fibronectin (5 μg/mL; Corning, Cat#354008). Twenty-four hours prior to differentiation induction (day −1), media was supplemented with Rho Kinase Inhibitor Y-27632 (10 μM, Tocris Cat#1254).

On day 0, differentiation was initiated by replacing the media with neural induction medium containing DMEM/F12 Medium with GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#10565018), 0.1 mM MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids, 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate, 1X N-2 Supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#17502048), 1X B-27 Supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#12587010), 1X Chemically Defined Lipid Concentrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#11905031) and 0.05 mg/mL primocin. This medium was supplemented with 2–4 μg/mL Doxycycline Hyclate (Millipore Sigma, Cat#D9891) to induce NGN2 expression, 10 μM Rho Kinase Inhibitor Y-27632, 5 μM Wnt inhibitor XAV939 (Tocris, Cat#3748), and 10 μM Tgf-β inhibitor SB431542 (Tocris, Cat#1614). Doxycycline Hyclate, XAV939, and SB431542 were maintained from days 0–2, with daily media changes.

On day 2, media was replaced with neuronal differentiation media containing Neurobasal-A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#10888022), BrainPhys Neuronal Medium (Stem Cell Technologies, Cat#05790), 2 mM GlutaMAX supplement, 1X N-2 Supplement, 1X B-27 Supplement, 1X Chemically Defined Lipid Concentrate, 2 μg/mL Doxycycline Hyclate (until day 4), and 0.05 mg/mL primocin. Beginning on day 4, L-ascorbic acid (200 μM; Millipore Sigma Cat#A92902) was added.

On day 5 and onward, cultures were transitioned to maturation media containing BrainPhys Neuronal Medium, 1X N-2 Supplement, 1X Chemically Defined Lipid Concentrate, 1X B-27 Supplement, 0.5% (v/v) Matrigel GFR Basement Membrane Matrix (LDEV-free) (Corning, Cat#354230), 0.05 mg/mL primocin, L-ascorbic acid (200 μM) and 5 μg/mL Heparin sodium salt (from porcine intestinal mucosa) (Millipore Sigma, Cat#H3149). Media was changed every other day from day 5 onward.

Distributed cloud-connected microscopy architecture

Imaging data were acquired using an open hardware in-incubator microscope adapted from published designs (Baudin et al., 2022; Ehrlich, 2025; Elliott et al., 2023). The optical system utilized a Raspberry Pi High Quality Camera module (12.3 megapixels, Sony IMX477 CMOS sensor) connected to an Arducam IMX477 UVC adapter board (ArduCam, Cat#B0278). A 20× magnification LM Plan achromatic microscope objective (Boli Optics) with 8.8 mm working distance was mounted using an RMS to C-Mount adapter (Thorlabs, Cat#RMSA5) and a 50 mm C-Mount lens tube (Thorlabs, Cat#CML50). Illumination was provided by a white LED ring light positioned beneath the culture vessel. Assembly details can be found in Note S1.

Axial positioning used a NEMA11 stepper motor-driven linear rail with 150 mm travel distance, a 6 mm leadscrew, and a Pololu Tic T825 stepper motor driver with 24 V power supply, achieving 5 μm positioning precision. Camera control utilized the OpenCV Python library (Version 4.5.4; Intel; RRID: SCR_015526) while motor positioning was managed via the Pololu ticcmd command-line utility. Captured images were displayed on an HTML webpage and live-streamed to YouTube using Open Broadcaster Software (OBS Studio version 27.2) with RTMP protocol and H.264 encoding.

The assembly operated continuously at 37°C and 5% CO2, acquiring images every 90 to 120 s for 3 to 5 days. Optical resolution was validated using a USAF 1951 resolution test target, confirming 2 to 3 μm resolution at 20× magnification.

Design and psychometric validation of the SCRIS

The SCRIS was developed in two stages. The first stage identified core dimensions based on established science identity frameworks (Carlone and Johnson, 2007; Hazari et al., 2010), including performance, competence, recognition, interest, and role models. Performance items focused on academic self-efficacy in stem cell biology coursework while competence measured self-perceived understanding of biological concepts (Beghetto, 2007). Recognition evaluated self-perception and external recognition as members of the scientific field. Interest gauged enthusiasm and career aspirations, and role models explored the impact of mentorship (Carlone and Johnson, 2007).

The second stage involved developing 24 items adapted from validated STEM identity measures (Beghetto, 2007; Chen and Wei, 2022; Childers and Jones, 2017; Wulff et al., 2018) and modified to reflect stem cell and neuroscience contexts. The questionnaire utilized a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

EFA was conducted using IBM SPSS software with principal axis factoring and Promax rotation (kappa = 4). Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity assessed data suitability for factor analysis. Items with factor loadings less than 0.40 or cross-loadings exceeding 0.32 were eliminated. The EFA sample comprised 133 students enrolled in a University of California Santa Cruz bioethics course (BME80G: Bioethics in the 21st Century). Complete demographic information for this cohort is available in Table S2.

CFA was performed using JASP statistical software with maximum likelihood estimation. The CFA sample included 95 students from AP Biology classes at Alisal High School (Salinas, CA) and the Biotechnology Program at Berkeley City College (Berkeley, CA). Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (CMIN/DF), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Root-Mean-Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root-Mean-Square Residual (SRMR). Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for each subscale and the overall instrument. The final SCRIS retained 16 items across four factors. Complete demographic information for these cohorts is available in Tables S5–S9.

Educational implementation and SCRIS administration

Implementation followed a five-phase PBL framework over 2 to 3 weeks at Alisal High School (Salinas, CA; Title I, 95% Hispanic enrollment, AP Biology) and Berkeley City College (Berkeley, CA; Introductory Biotechnology, first semester of a three-semester program).

Phase 1 - Instructional Preparation: Students received remote instruction via Zoom covering stem cell biology fundamentals, pluripotency, neuronal differentiation, the NGN2 transcription factor system, and experimental design principles. The pre-intervention SCRIS was administered electronically via Google Forms.

Phase 2 - Experimental Design: Students conducted literature reviews and brainstormed experimental conditions for testing effects on differentiating neurons. Proposals included culturally relevant compounds (traditional remedies, herbs, vitamins, foods consumed locally) and environmental factors of local concern (agricultural pesticides, air pollutants, heavy metals). The class discussed proposals collectively via Zoom and selected 2–3 conditions by majority vote for parallel testing.

Phase 3 - Experimental Execution: Student-selected compounds were prepared at physiologically relevant concentrations and added to parallel culture wells alongside vehicle controls. Experiments were initiated during a live Zoom session allowing students to observe cell plating, compound addition, and microscope setup. Students monitored differentiation in real-time via the YouTube live stream for 2–3 days, with phase contrast images captured automatically at 90 to 120 s intervals.

Phase 4 - Data Analysis: Students downloaded time-lapse image sequences and performed analyses using self-selected metrics and freely available software (ImageJ, Excel). Analytical approaches included cell density quantification, morphological classification, neurite outgrowth measurement, cell migration tracking, and temporal dynamics of differentiation markers.

Phase 5 - Reporting: Students prepared final reports or oral presentations for peers describing their experimental question, methodology, results, and biological interpretation. Post-intervention SCRIS was administered electronically.

For the neuroblastoma comparison cohort, a similar five-phase framework was implemented at Alisal High School in 2024 using Neuro-2a neuroblastoma cells.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using multiple software platforms. EFA was performed using IBM SPSS software (Version 31; IBM; RRID: SCR_016479). CFA was conducted using JASP statistical software (Version 0.95.4; University of Amsterdam; RRID: SCR_015823) with maximum likelihood estimation. Comparisons between pre- and post-intervention outcomes were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software; SCR_002798). Cohen’s values were determined using IBM SPSS software.

To maintain participant anonymity, surveys were administered without individual identifiers, preventing pairing of pre- and post-intervention responses. This represents a meaningful constraint: independent t-tests cannot account for individual baseline differences or within-person change, so results should be interpreted as group-level differences. Independent two-tailed t-tests compared pre-to post-intervention group means within cohorts and between cohorts at single timepoints.

Demographic analyses examined whether intervention effects differed across student populations. For each demographic category, independent t-tests compared pre-intervention to post-intervention group means within each subgroup. Subgroups with fewer than three participants or no representation at one institution were excluded.

Normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. The majority of distributions met parametric assumptions or had sufficiently large sample sizes to invoke the central limit theorem; for comparisons that violated normality assumptions, including ethnicity subgroups where small sample sizes precluded reliable normality assessment, nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were applied. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 for all analyses. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied given the exploratory nature of demographic subgroup analyses. Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. Sample sizes are reported for each analysis.
