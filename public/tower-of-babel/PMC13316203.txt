Enhancing bread functionality through partial wheat flour replacement with chickpea starch: impacts on dough characteristics, bread quality, and digestive responses
Authors: Saddam Mustafa, Sundas Hina, Shahid Mahmood, Haiam O. Elkatry, Hossam S. El-Beltagi, Khaled M.A. Ramadan, Abdelrahman Ragab Ahmed, Xianfeng Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effects of substituting 5–15% wheat flour with chickpea starch (CS) in bread formulations, focusing on its impact on the physicochemical properties of the flour matrix, dough rheology, quality attributes, and glycemic index (GI) of the bread. CS incorporation improved the hydration capacity of the flour blend, promoted starch granule swelling and gelatinization through enhanced water availability, and improved dough viscoelasticity. FTIR analysis revealed that CS substitution induced a shift in protein secondary structure from disordered conformations (β-turn and random coil) to more ordered structures (β-sheet and α-helix), indicating enhanced structural stability of the dough protein matrix. However, higher CS substitution levels weakened the gluten matrix, resulting in increased bread firmness and decreased specific volume, baking loss, resilience, and springiness (p < 0.05). CS also positively affected the bread's color and flavor, likely due to enhanced Maillard reactions during baking. Sensory analysis showed that bread with 5% CS substitution had the highest acceptability. Additionally, CS substitution significantly reduced the estimated GI and in-vitro digestibility, which may be associated with structural changes in starch and an increased proportion of indigestible starch. In vivo digestion tests in mice indicated that the GI of CS-fortified bread was 49.33, classifying it as a low-GI food. These findings demonstrate the potential of CS as an ingredient for producing bread with a reduced glycemic response and improved quality, making it suitable for slow-digesting meal replacement products.

Introduction

Bread is a staple food worldwide, especially in developing countries, composed primarily of flour, water, salt, and yeast (Mancebo, Martínez, Merino, de la Hera, & Gómez, 2017). Breads are thermodynamically unstable porous structures, consisting of a crust and crumb interior, with starch granules dispersed within a continuous protein network (Arp, Correa, & Ferrero, 2018). Despite wheat-based bread being the most widely consumed bread variety worldwide, there is growing interest in improving its nutritional quality through the incorporation of functional ingredients. Such strategies aim to enhance the nutritional value and physiological benefits of bread while maintaining desirable quality attributes. In particular, increasing consumer de-mand for low glycemic index (GI) foods is driven by the rising prevalence of lifestyle related diseases, including type 2 diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disorders. High GI foods, such as refined grain products, are rapidly digested and absorbed, resulting in sharp postprandial increases in blood glucose levels. Consequently, reducing the glycemic response of staple foods such as bread has emerged as an important approach for promoting healthier dietary patterns and mitigating metabolic health risks (Al-Sheraji et al., 2013; Bar-El Dadon, Abbo, & Reifen, 2017).

In recent years, considerable research has focused on replacing wheat flour with nutrient-dense pulse flours, as well as adding minerals, vitamins, prebiotics, bran, and other functional ingredients (Lovegrove et al., 2023). Gkountenoudi-Eskitzi et al. (Gkountenoudi-Eskitzi et al., 2023), incorporated 30% corn flour into a bread formulation, enhancing its antioxidant properties and reducing its GI. Additionally, the incorporation of high amylose starch into bread resulted in a six-fold increase in resistant starch (RS) content, accompanied by an 80% reduction in digestibility compared to traditional bread (Li, Dhital, & Gidley, 2022). Despite these advantages, such bread varieties have not gained widespread popularity due to limited consumer acceptance and insufficient market penetration. Therefore, further investigation into innovative strategies for developing commercially viable, high-quality, lower-GI bread is essential.

Chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.) seeds contain approximately 65% carbohydrates, with starch being the primary component, accounting for 37.5% to 50.8% of the total (Miao, Zhang, & Jiang, 2009). CS is a C-type starch characterized by both ordered and disordered regions, containing small, oval, and spherical starch granules. The ordered regions of CS exhibit greater resistance to α-amylase degradation than the disordered regions (Sun et al., 2015). Additionally, smaller linear molecules (degree of polymerization, DP < 15) are more easily hydrolyzed by α-amylase than larger molecules (DP >40) (Sun et al., 2018). Due to its high amylose content, CS has the potential to lower the GI by resisting enzymatic breakdown (Fredriksson et al., 2000; Sandhu & Lim, 2008). Previous research has shown that bread with up to 20% high-amylose maize starch substitution exhibited improvements in crumb structure, specific volume, and textural properties (Arp et al., 2018; Šárka et al., 2017). Studies have also demonstrated that retrograded starch fractions contribute significantly to the formation of RS, which supports metabolic benefits and gut health (Mohammed, Ahmed, & Senge, 2014; Yadav, Yadav, & Dhull, 2012). CS, with its naturally high amylose content (approximately 25–30%), promotes enhanced starch retrogradation upon cooling. During the cooling process, amylose molecules realign into ordered double helical and crystalline structures, leading to a compact matrix that increased RS formation and reduced enzymatic digestibility (Arp et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2015). The physicochemical properties of CS, including its unique gelatinization temperature and water absorption capacity, influence dough behavior and bread texture (Vinod, Asrey, Rudra, Urhe, & Mishra, 2023).

Much of the research on CS has been theoretical, focusing primarily on its molecular and physicochemical properties, such as crystallinity, amylose content, and interactions with other food components. These studies have provided valuable insights into the structure and digestibility of CS, suggesting its potential to reduce GI by forming RS (Jagannadham & Parimalavalli, 2015; Polesi, Sarmento, & Anjos, 2011). Based on these findings, we hypothesize that replacing wheat flour with CS can help adjust the glycemic response to bread following consumption. Therefore, the current study aims to develop a low-GI bread by examining the impact of CS molecular structure on mixed flour indices, dough characteristics, as well as in-vitro digestion and in-vivo analysis of bread. This study seeks to provide a clearer understanding of how CS can contribute to the development of healthier, low-GI baked goods by evaluating the bread quality attributes, baking properties, sensory characteristics, and both in-vitro and in-vivo analysis.

Material and methods

Materials

The primary ingredient utilized in the bread production process was soft wheat flour, sourced from a local supplier in the Anhui region of China. Chickpeas (Desi variety, grown in 2022) were sourced from the Ayub Agricultural Research Institute, located in the Punjab Province of Pakistan. Other essential ingredients, including butter, wheat flour, dry yeast, soft sugar, and various additives, were procured from local markets in China. All chemicals and reagents used in the study were of analytical-grade quality, sourced from the Sigma-Aldrich Company (USA), including NaOH, HCl, NaCl, C₂H₅OH, amyloglucosidase, porcine pancreatic α-amylase, distilled water and other standard laboratory reagents.

Isolation and characterization of chickpea starch

For starch isolation, we implemented a modified approach following the approach outlined by Singh et al. (Singh, Kaur, Sandhu, & Guraya, 2004). A total of 500 g of chickpea seeds were soaked in 3 l of deionized water at 25 °C overnight. After soaking, the seed coats were manually removed, and the hulled seeds were ground for 5 min using a Waring Commercial Blender (Model CB15). The resulting flour was suspended in 2 l of 0.16% NaHSO3 solution at 25 °C for 18 h. The resulting slurry was filtered through 100 μm nylon mesh to remove larger debris and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min using an Eppendorf 5804R centrifuge. This step was repeated three times, removing the supernatant and the upper grey layer each time. The remaining white precipitate was washed three times with 1 l of purified water to neutralize the pH. The starch was then dried at 40 °C for 12 h in a Thermo Fisher Scientific convection oven. After drying, the starch was finely crushed and passed through an 80-mesh filter. The purified chickpea starch was sealed in bags and stored for further analysis.

Particle size distribution and molecular weight analysis

The particle size of the starch sample was determined using a laser particle sizer (Malvern Mastersizer 2000, UK) with a flow-through reservoir (1000 mL). The starch sample was loaded into the reservoir and allowed to disperse completely in deionized water. The obscurity was adjusted from 10 to 12%. The refractive indices of the starch samples and the dispersant deionized water were 1.52 and 1.33, respectively. The software recoded the particle size in the range of 0.02 to 2000 μm, respectively. The molecular weight of starch was determined using a GPC-MALLS-RI (Gel-permeation chromatography multi-angle laser light scattering-refractive index, U3000, Thermo, USA) detector by following previous method (Deng et al., 2023).

Bread formulation and preparation

A mixture comprising 135 g of wheat flour, 1.4% high-activity dry yeast, 1% sodium chloride, 4% skimmed milk powder, 6% margarine, 12% refined white sucrose, and 50% water was combined using a dough kneader, producing approximately 240 g of dough. The dough underwent initial fermentation at 28 °C, was subsequently molded, and transferred to a 10 × 10 × 10 cm cubic mold for additional leavening at 38 °C. After leavening, the dough was transferred to a preheated oven and baked for 30 min at 180 °C, utilizing both top and bottom heat. The upper heat was then increased to 200 °C for an additional 8 min. After the bread reached room temperature, all evaluations were performed. The samples with 5%, 10%, and 15% CS replacing wheat flour were labeled as CSB-5, CSB-10, and CSB-15, respectively, while the wheat flour sample was identified as WF.

Characterization of composite flours

Chemical composition analysis

The chemical composition of the chickpea starch and flour blend (wheat flour + CS) was determined following established guidelines by AOAC International for plant-based matrices (Horwitz & Latimer, 2000). All determinations were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Moisture content was measured using AOAC method 923.03, crude protein content was determined by the Kjeldahl method (AOAC 920.39) using a Kjeldahl Nitrogen Determination System (e.g., Foss Kjeltec 8100 or similar model), and ash content was assessed following AOAC 984.13 using a muffle furnace. The protein content was calculated by multiplying the total nitrogen by the standard conversion factor of 6.25. Total starch content in the flour blend was measured using the Megazyme Total Starch Assay Kit, following the procedure outlined by Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2015). However, apparent amylose was determined using the iodine colorimetric method, and phosphorus contents were analyzed according to the molybdate-vanadate method (Hardisson et al., 2001; Jung et al., 2022). These analyses were conducted using a variety of instruments, including a Kjeldahl system for protein analysis and a spectrophotometer for starch and amylose determinations.

Hydration properties

The swelling power (SP), water absorption index (WAI), and water solubility index (WSI) of the flour were determined following the procedure outlined by Cornejo and Rosell (Cornejo & Rosell, 2015). A 1.000 ± 0.001 g sample of the flour was accurately weighed using an analytical balance (e.g., Mettler Toledo, XS Series) and homogenously suspended in 10 mL of distilled water. The suspension was heated in a laboratory water bath (e.g., Thermo Fisher Scientific, Precision Series) at 95 °C for 15 min to induce gelatinization. After the heating process, the sample was allowed to cool to ambient temperature. The solution was then subjected to centrifugation at 5400 ×g for 10 min using a centrifuge (e.g., Eppendorf, 5424R). The liquid fraction was decanted, and its weight was recorded as m2 after being evaporated to a constant weight in a vacuum oven (e.g., Binder, VD23). The wet residue's weight was recorded as m1. Pasting properties

The pasting characteristics of the flour mixture were assessed using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA - Tec Master, Perten Instruments, Stockholm, Sweden), adhering to the methodology outlined by Gunaratne et al. (Gunaratne et al., 2018). The parameters, including peak time (Ptime), setback (SB), breakdown (BD), final viscosity (FV), pasting temperature (PT), trough viscosity (TV), and peak viscosity (PV), were automatically analyzed using a data analysis system.

Dough characterization

Dough texture profile analysis

The TA-XT Plus Texture Analyzer (TA. XT PLUS/50, Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK) was used to assess the dough's texture in texture profile analysis mode (Qin et al., 2021). To form uniform pellets, 30 g of yeast-free dough samples were shaped and then compressed twice using a 36-mm aluminum probe (P/36R), applying a 40% strain. The compression was carried out with a speed of 1 mm/s and an applied force of 5 g.

Mixing properties

The blending characteristics of the mixed flour were evaluated using the Mixolab 2 device (Chopin Technologies, Paris, France), in accordance with the standard CHOPIN+ protocol. This method assesses the properties of starch and protein, while also evaluating the dough's response against temperature variations and kneading. The findings were presented as stability time (TS, minutes), water absorption percentage (WA) and C1 (N·m), C2 (N·m), C3 (N·m), alongside C4 (N·m) and development time (TF, minutes) (Sun, Bu, Qiao, Drawbridge, & Fang, 2023).

Dynamic rheological properties

The viscoelastic properties of the composite dough were analyzed using a rotational rheometer (DHR-1, Waters Corporation, Milford, United States of America), following a modified procedure reported by Zhao et al. (Zhao et al., 2024). In brief, 5 g portion of dough, prepared without the addition of yeast, was placed on a surface, allowed to stand for 10 min, and subsequently its edges were trimmed. To minimize moisture loss, a thin layer of mineral oil was carefully applied to the surface of the dough sample. The test temperature of 25 °C, frequency range from 0.1 to 20 Hz, and holding duration of 120 s were set for the experiment. A 40 mm plate with a 1000 μm gap and a strain of 0.5% were utilized for the analysis.

Protein secondary structure analysis

The structural characteristics of bread samples with different levels of CS substitution were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). After baking and cooling, the crumb portions of WF, CS-5, CS-10, and CS-15 were freeze-dried and ground into fine powder. Approximately 1 mg of each powdered sample was mixed with 100 mg of KBr and compressed into pellets. FTIR spectra were recorded over the range of 4000–400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 with 64 scans. The spectra were used to identify characteristic functional groups and to examine structural changes in the bread matrix induced by CS substitution.

Microstructural analysis

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM-450, FEI Company, Hillsboro, USA) was used to analyze the microstructure of the dough. To begin with, the dough samples were subjected to compression, forming thin slices. These slices were then freeze-dried before being coated with gold. At a magnification of 500×, cross-sectional images were captured.

Bread texture profile analysis

From the center of each freshly baked loaf, cubes measuring 15 × 15 × 15 mm were separated. The texture for the dough was then assessed adhering to the method detailed in Section 2.5.3. The attributes evaluated for texture included chewiness, cohesiveness, springiness, hardness, gumminess, and resilience.

Crumb and crust color analysis

The color attributes (L*, a*, b*, ΔE) of both the crumb and crust of the bread were evaluated with a colorimeter (CS-820, Caipu Technology, Hangzhou, China) in reflection mode, based on the methodology by Cui et al. (Cui, Fei, & Zhu, 2022).

Sensory evaluation

A panel of twenty volunteers evaluated the bread samples using a structured nine-point hedonic scale, assessing attributes including overall acceptability, taste, texture, flavor, color, and appearance. The scale ranged from 1 (“dislike very much”) to 9 (“like very much”), with 5 representing a neutral response (“neither like nor dislike”) (Atudorei, Mironeasa, & Codină, 2022). Each bread sample was evaluated in triplicate to minimize errors and enable calculation of mean scores. Prior to participation, panelists were briefed on the study objectives, and their personal data were handled in accordance with privacy standards. Informed consent was obtained through a detailed form outlining the voluntary nature of involvement, the right to withdraw at any point without penalty, and assurance of confidentiality. Data recording, management, and analysis were conducted using Origin 2021 software.

In vitro starch digestibility

In-vitro digestion of the bread sample was conducted following the methodology of Englyst et al. (Englyst, Kingman, & Cummings, 1992) with slight modifications. For each sample, 200 mg of finely ground bread was accurately weighed using an analytical balance (e.g., Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). The sample was then placed in a 50 mL test tube (e.g., Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and 15 mL of phosphate-buffered solution (pH 5.2) was added, along with five glass beads for homogenization. The mixture was then incubated in a thermostat-controlled water bath (e.g., Grant Instruments, UK) at 37 °C for 5 min to equilibrate. After incubation, a mixed enzyme solution consisting of 5 mL, containing 50 U/mL of amyloglucosidase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 290 U/mg of porcine pancreatic α-amylase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), was added to the sample. The digestion process was carried out at 37 °C for 120 min in the water bath, with the temperature maintained using a thermostat. During the digestion, 0.5 mL aliquots were withdrawn at 20 and 120 min and immediately mixed with 4.5 mL of absolute ethanol to halt the enzymatic reaction. The sample was then centrifuged at 3400 rpm for 10 min using a refrigerated centrifuge (e.g., Eppendorf Centrifuge 5810R, Germany) to separate the supernatant from the remaining starch residue. The glucose content of the supernatant was determined using the dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method. Animal feeding and in vivo digestion study

Healthy male C57BL/6 J mice (6 weeks old, weighing 12–13 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Centre at the University of Sargodha, Pakistan. All animal procedures were performed in strict accordance with relevant national and international laws and regulations, including Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes (sections L 276/72 and L 276/73). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Sargodha (Approval No. UOS/ORIC/2022/69, dated November 30, 2022). Mice were housed individually under controlled environmental conditions (temperature 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 40%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle) with unrestricted access to standard chow and water. Following a 3-day acclimatization period, the animals were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 18 per group) and fed experimental bread diets containing 0%, 5%, 10%, or 15% chickpea starch for a duration of 8 weeks. The diets were formulated to be isocaloric and isonitrogenous, according to the protocol described by Yin et al. (Yin et al., 2023) and were prepared by Anhui Kuibu Shuyu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Throughout the study, body weight and food intake were recorded weekly. Behavioral assessments and fecal sample collections were conducted during the final experimental week. At the conclusion of the feeding period, animals were fasted for 12 h prior to experimentation. For the in-vivo digestion experiment, bread samples (24 g each) were finely crushed and dispersed in 100 mL of water to prepare suspensions. Subsequently, mice with comparable body weights were orally gavaged with either 2.4 g/kg of the bread sample suspension or 2.0 g/kg glucose solution as a control. Blood samples were collected via tail vein puncture at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120-min post-gavage, and blood glucose concentrations were measured using a validated glucose meter. The area under the glucose tolerance curve (AUC) over 0-120 min was calculated using the trapezoidal rule. The GI of the bread samples was determined by dividing the AUC of each bread group by the AUC of the glucose control. At study termination, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane to minimize distress and euthanized by cervical dislocation in accordance with Directive 2010/63/EU guidelines. This method ensured a humane and rapid death, thereby minimizing pain and suffering. Tissue samples were immediately collected and stored appropriately for downstream analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA), incorporating both correlation analysis and Duncan's test. Using Origin 9.1 (OriginLab, Southampton, USA) and Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Redmond, USA), graphs were created. The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with each test conducted in triplicate.

Result and discussion

Chemical composition of chickpea starch and mixed flours

The chemical composition of the isolated CS is presented in Table 1. CS contained 11.1 ± 0.04% moisture, 0.29 ± 0.05% ash, 0.77 ± 0.12% protein, and 0.32 ± 0.14% fat. The starch purity and amylose content were 81.3 ± 0.70% and 20.67 ± 0.40%, respectively, indicating that starch was the major component of the isolated fraction, while protein and lipid contents remained low. The incorporation of CS significantly altered the composition of the composite flours. Compared with wheat flour, protein content decreased progressively as the substitution level increased, whereas total starch content increased (p < 0.05). The reduction in protein content is attributable to the replacement of protein-rich wheat flour with starch-rich CS. In contrast, total starch content increased from 41.35% in wheat flour to 62.30% in the CS15% formulation. Amylose content also increased significantly following CS incorporation, reaching 25.31% and 24.14% in the CS10% and CS15% formulations, respectively. In contrast, water solubility index (WSI) decreased markedly with increasing CS substitution, declining from 12.33% in WF to 4.05% in the CS15% formulation. The lower WSI values suggest reduced starch solubilization within the composite systems. This behavior may be attributed to the increased amylose proportion introduced by CS incorporation. Amylose-rich starches generally exhibit stronger intermolecular associations and partial helical arrangements, which limit molecular dispersion in water and consequently reduce starch solubility (Jia et al., 2023).

A progressive increase in swelling power (SP) was also observed, reaching 7.64 g/g in the CS15% formulation compared with 3.94 g/g in WF. Notably, native CS exhibited the highest SP value (8.15 g/g), indicating an inherently greater swelling capacity than wheat flour. Previous studies have shown that legume starches often possess higher swelling capacities than cereal starches owing to differences in granule architecture, amylose–amylopectin organization, and hydration behavior (Singh, Kaur, & McCarthy, 2007). Although higher amylose contents may reduce starch solubility, they can also contribute to water retention within swollen starch networks under appropriate hydration conditions (Chibbar, Ambigaipalan, & Hoover, 2010). Similar trends have been reported for chickpea-based systems (Manickavasagan, Mathew, Al-Attabi, & Al-Zakwani, 2013). Overall, CS incorporation enhanced the water absorption and swelling characteristics of the composite flours while reducing starch solubility, indicating a substantial modification of flour-water interactions.

Pasting properties

CS incorporation significantly altered the pasting behavior of the composite flours (Table 2). Both pasting temperature and peak time decreased progressively with increasing substitution level. Pasting temperature declined from 88.83 °C in WF to 80.96 °C in the CS15% formulation, while peak time decreased from 5.89 to 5.77 min. These changes are likely associated with the reduction in protein content following CS incorporation. Proteins can interact with starch granules and restrict their swelling during heating; therefore, the lower protein content of the composite flours may have reduced this constraint, facilitating starch gelatinization at lower temperatures (Li, 2022; Sun et al., 2023; Wang, Zhang, Wang, Ai, & Xiong, 2020).

In contrast, peak viscosity and final viscosity increased significantly with increasing CS substitution. Peak viscosity increased from 1489.66 cP in WF to 1578.66 cP in the CS15% formulation, whereas final viscosity increased from 2069.33 to 2214.66 cP. This behavior is consistent with the higher starch content of the composite flours and their enhanced hydration characteristics, which promoted greater granule swelling and viscosity development during heating. Similar increases in viscosity have been reported in starch-enriched wheat flour systems (Singh, Sandhu, & Kaur, 2004; Thakaeng, Boonloom, & Rawdkuen, 2021). Breakdown viscosity also increased following CS incorporation, suggesting reduced hot-paste stability and a greater susceptibility of swollen starch granules to disintegration under thermal and mechanical stress. Furthermore, setback viscosity increased progressively from 1035.33 cP in WF to 1188.00 cP in the CS15% formulation. Since setback viscosity is commonly associated with starch molecular reassociation during cooling, the higher setback values indicate a greater tendency toward retrogradation in the CS-containing formulations. Collectively, the pasting results demonstrate that CS incorporation modified both the gelatinization behavior and reassociation characteristics of the starch system, reflecting substantial changes in flour functionality following starch substitution.

Dough properties

Dough textural attributes

Dough textural properties were significantly affected by CS incorporation (Table 3). Hardness increased progressively from 107.32 N in the control dough to 182.46 N in the CS15% formulation (p < 0.05). This increase is consistent with the compositional changes induced by CS substitution, particularly the reduction in protein content and the concomitant increase in starch content. Similar increases in dough firmness have been reported in starch-enriched composite flour systems, where gluten dilution and changes in water distribution contribute to the development of a more compact dough matrix (Atudorei, Atudorei, & Codină, 2022; Tian et al., 2024; Zafar, Aldughpassi, Al-Mussallam, & Al-Othman, 2020). Cohesiveness also increased following CS incorporation, reaching its highest value in the CS15% formulation. The increase in cohesiveness suggests that the dough matrix maintained greater structural integrity during deformation despite the reduction in gluten content. Resilience values were generally higher in the CS-containing doughs than in the control, indicating a modest improvement in elastic recovery after compression. In contrast, adhesiveness became progressively more negative with increasing CS substitution, indicating greater dough stickiness. This behavior may be related to the enhanced water-binding capacity of the composite flours observed in the hydration analysis, which can alter the distribution of free and bound water within the dough matrix. Mixing properties

Mixolab analysis revealed substantial changes in dough development and thermal behavior following CS incorporation (Fig. 1A and Table 3). While C1 values remained relatively constant among formulations, progressive reductions were observed in C2, TF, and TS as the level of CS substitution increased. C2 decreased from 0.47 N·m in WF to 0.21 N·m in CS15%, whereas TF and TS declined from 4.95 to 2.92 min and from 7.60 to 3.60 min, respectively. These results indicate a progressive reduction in dough development and stability, which is primarily attributable to the dilution of gluten-forming proteins resulting from starch substitution. Consistently, the C1–C2 difference increased from 0.65 to 0.96 N·m, suggesting greater weakening of the protein network during mixing and heating. The modification of the protein network was closely associated with dough texture. A significant positive correlation was observed between dough hardness and C1–C2 values (r = 0.81, p < 0.01), indicating that the weakening of the gluten network coincided with the formation of a firmer dough structure. Similar behavior has been reported in starch-enriched composite dough systems, where replacement of wheat flour reduced gluten continuity and altered dough development characteristics (Pérez-Rodríguez, Ibarra-Herrera, & Pérez-Carrillo, 2023). In contrast, C3 and C4 increased progressively with increasing CS substitution. C3 increased from 1.62 to 1.91 N·m, while C4 increased from 1.77 to 2.01 N·m. These changes are consistent with the RVA results (Table 2), where higher peak and final viscosities were observed following CS incorporation. The increase in C3 suggests enhanced starch gelatinization during heating, whereas the higher C4 values indicate greater torque retention during the holding stage, reflecting the increased contribution of starch to dough behavior. Dynamic rheological characteristics

The frequency sweep results revealed that CS incorporation significantly influenced the viscoelastic behavior of the dough systems (Fig. 1B). Across the entire frequency range, both the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′) increased with increasing levels of CS substitution. The increase in G′ was more pronounced than that of G′′, indicating a greater enhancement of the elastic component of the dough matrix. Similar trends have been reported in composite dough systems enriched with chickpea-derived ingredients (Atudorei et al., 2022). For all formulations, G′ remained higher than G′′ throughout the tested frequency range, demonstrating the predominance of elastic behavior over viscous behavior. In addition, both moduli increased with frequency, suggesting the formation of a mechanically stronger network capable of resisting deformation under dynamic loading. The higher modulus values observed in the CS-containing doughs are consistent with the increased dough hardness and cohesiveness discussed previously (Table 3), indicating the development of a more rigid and structured matrix following CS substitution.

The enhanced viscoelastic response may be associated with changes in the relative contribution of starch and gluten within the dough system. As CS substitution increased, the starch fraction increased while protein content decreased (Table 1), leading to modifications in dough organization and intermolecular interactions. Similar increases in viscoelastic moduli have been reported in starch-enriched wheat doughs, where alterations in matrix structure resulted in greater resistance to deformation (de la Hera, Talegón, Caballero, & Gómez, 2013; Khoozani et al., 2020). The loss tangent (tan δ) remained below unity for all samples (Fig. 1C), confirming that elastic behavior dominated over viscous flow irrespective of substitution level. Therefore, although CS significantly strengthened the viscoelastic response of the dough, the fundamental solid-like character of the system was preserved.

Microstructural analysis

The impact of WF substitution with CS on dough microstructure was analyzed using SEM (Fig. 2A-E). In the WF control sample (Fig. 2A), the gluten network appeared dense and continuous, with starch granules embedded within the protein matrix. Few pores of varying sizes were observed, as marked by the red circles, which is consistent with previous studies on wheat-based dough microstructure (Sun et al., 2023; Wang, Lao, Bao, Guan, & Li, 2021). At 5% CS substitution (Fig. 2B), the gluten network began to show partial disruption, and the matrix became less compact, as indicated by the yellow circle. Compared with the WF control, starch granules remained visible, but their boundaries appeared less sharply defined in some regions, possibly due to greater embedding or partial coverage by the surrounding protein-starch matrix. In addition, the number of pores increased, although their size appeared smaller. This suggests that CS competes with gluten for water and binding sites, forming new interactions with the protein matrix. These microstructural changes may be associated with the altered rheological behavior of the dough. Bread quality characteristics

Baking properties

The baking characteristics of breads prepared with CS are presented in Table 4. Incorporation of CS significantly affected loaf development, resulting in progressive reductions in both bread height and specific volume. Bread height decreased from 7.95 cm in the WF bread to 7.66 cm in the CS15% formulation, while specific volume declined from 4.49 to 3.21 mL/g. These changes were also reflected in the loaf appearance (Fig. 3A–B), where CS-containing breads exhibited a denser crumb structure characterized by smaller and more uniformly distributed gas cells. Similar reductions in specific volume have been reported in breads enriched with legume-derived ingredients, including green pea, lupine, and soy flours (Moreno-Araiza, Boukid, Suo, Wang, & Vittadini, 2023; Bhat & Karim, 2009; Doxastakis, Zafiriadis, Irakli, Marlani, & Tananaki, 2002). The reduction in loaf expansion is consistent with the dough properties observed in the present study. Mixolab analysis demonstrated progressive weakening of dough development and stability following CS incorporation, while SEM observations revealed increasing disruption of the gluten network. Bread texture profile

CS incorporation significantly altered the textural properties of the bread samples (Table 4). Bread hardness increased progressively from 174.65 g in the WF bread to 214.76, 265.56, and 282.22 g in the CS5%, CS10%, and CS15% formulations, respectively (p < 0.05). The increase in hardness is consistent with the reduction in specific volume and the development of a denser crumb structure observed in the baking analysis. Furthermore, the weakening of the gluten network identified through Mixolab and SEM analyses likely restricted gas cell expansion during baking, resulting in a more compact bread matrix and consequently greater firmness. Similar increases in bread hardness have been reported in composite flour systems containing mango peel powder ingredients (Chen et al., 2019). Gumminess and chewiness also increased significantly with increasing CS substitution. Gumminess increased from 131.12 g in WF bread to 209.46 g in the CS15% formulation, while chewiness increased from 126.07 to 202.29 g. These changes are consistent with the increase in hardness and reflect the formation of a more compact crumb structure requiring greater deformation energy during mastication. In contrast, springiness, cohesiveness, and resilience showed gradual reductions as the level of CS increased. Cohesiveness decreased from 0.75 in WF bread to 0.68 in CS15%, while resilience decreased from 0.36 to 0.29. The lower cohesiveness and resilience values indicate a reduced ability of the bread matrix to recover following deformation, suggesting that excessive CS substitution weakened structural continuity within the crumb. These changes are consistent with the disruption of the gluten network observed in the dough microstructure and are likely responsible for the increased tendency of the CS15% bread to fracture under compression. The close relationship between dough and bread texture suggests that the structural modifications introduced during dough development were retained throughout baking and played a major role in determining the final textural characteristics of the breads.

Crumb and crust color characteristics

Bread color plays a crucial role in consumer perception, providing an immediate visual impression. The incorporation of CS significantly altered both the crumb and crust color compared to WF bread. CS substitution lightened the top crust while darkening the bottom. At 5% CS substitution, the specific volume (SV) remained stable, with minimal impact on thermal behavior and surface browning of the top crust, resulting in a uniform color. At 10% CS substitution, the bread color became lighter and more uneven due to a decrease in SV and irregular contact between the bread surface and the baking mold. The coloration of the bread during baking is primarily due to caramelization and Maillard reactions. The light-yellow color of CS and the higher levels of damaged starch, which release more reducing sugars, contributed to the darker bottom color (Li et al., 2020). These sugars participate in caramelization and Maillard reactions, leading to browning (Li, Tilley, Chen, Siliveru, & Li, 2023). As CS substitution increased, the bread crumb exhibited higher b, C, and h values, and lower L* values (Fig. 3 and Table 5). This color change may enhance the bread's visual appeal (Kelanne, Yang, & Laaksonen, 2024). Overall, CS had a more significant impact on the crust color than the crumb, as reflected by the total color difference (ΔE) values. Sensory characteristics

The sensory attributes of bread made with CS were evaluated. No significant differences were observed in texture, color, appearance, or mouthfeel between CS and WF breads (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3B). However, substituting 5–10% wheat flour with CS imparted a distinct ‘beany’ and ‘earthy’ flavor, which significantly improved the overall flavor score compared to WF bread (p < 0.05). At 15% CS substitution, the chickpea flavor became more pronounced, overshadowing the characteristic wheat flavor. Sensory evaluation results showed a significant reduction in the flavor score, from 6.2 for WF to 4.6 for CS15%. The texture of CS15% bread was rated 5.1 ± 0.21, significantly lower (p < 0.05), mainly due to increased hardness and crumbliness, which were consistent with the decrease in cohesiveness and increase in hardness observed at this substitution level. Overall, flavor, appearance, color, and acceptability decreased significantly (p < 0.05) at the 15% CS substitution level, primarily due to uneven color distribution, increased crumbliness, and decreased specific volume, all negatively affecting the sensory qualities. In contrast, CS5% bread achieved the highest acceptability score (7.1 points), maintaining the original appearance, texture, and flavor of wheat bread while adding a unique beany aroma and appealing light-yellow color. This resulted in unanimous positive feedback, highlighting the balance between traditional qualities and subtle enhancements from CS substitution.

Starch digestibility and Glycemic response

In vitro starch digestibility

As shown in Fig. 4A, CS incorporation significantly reduced starch hydrolysis in a con-centration-dependent manner, with the 15% CS bread exhibiting the lowest hydrolysis rate throughout the digestion period. Consistently, the proportion of resistant starch (RS) in-creased, whereas rapidly digestible starch (RDS) decreased with increasing CS substitution (Fig. 4B). The 15% CS formulation exhibited the highest RS content (53%) and the lowest estimated glycemic index (eGI, 67.10), indicating a substantial reduction in starch digestibility compared with the control bread. The reduced digestibility may be partially attributed to the compositional and structural modifications induced by CS incorporation. The increase in amylose content observed in the composite flours (Table 1) may have contributed to greater resistance to enzymatic hydrolysis, as amylose-rich starches generally exhibit enhanced molecular reassociation and lower digestibility than amylopectin-rich systems (Sajilata, Singhal, & Kulkarni, 2006; Zhang, Ao, & Hamaker, 2006).This interpretation is further supported by the progressive increase in setback viscosity with increasing CS substitution (Table 2), suggesting an enhanced tendency for starch retrogradation during cooling. Similar relationships between starch retrogradation and resistant starch formation have been reported in cereal and legume-based systems (Li, 2024). Moreover, SEM analysis revealed substantial alterations in dough microstructure following CS incorporation, characterized by progressive disruption of the gluten network and redistribution of starch granules within the dough matrix. These microstructural changes were accompanied by increases in dough viscoelasticity, as evidenced by the higher storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′) values. Previous studies have demonstrated that modifications in food matrix organization can restrict enzyme accessibility to starch substrates and thereby reduce starch hydrolysis during digestion (Xiaoxuan Wang et al., 2021). Therefore, the higher RS content observed in CS enriched breads is likely associated with the combined effects of increased amylose content, greater retrogradation tendency, and matrix level structural modifications that may have reduced enzyme accessibility during digestion.Fig. 4(A) Starch hydrolysis curve and (B) the contents of RDS, SDS and RS were simulated in-vitro.Fig. 4

As shown in Table 5, the first-order kinetic model adequately described starch hydrolysis in all bread samples, with R2 values exceeding 0.90, indicating a good fit to the experimental data. Furthermore, the estimated glycemic index decreased progressively from 94.61 in the control bread to 67.10 in the 15% CS bread. These findings demonstrate that CS incorporation effectively reduced starch digestibility and glycemic potential. Similar observations have been reported in quinoa-enriched bread systems, where modifications in starch composition and matrix structure contributed to reduced enzymatic digestibility and improved nutritional functionality (Wang et al., 2021).

In-vivo digestion experiment in mice

According to the glycemic index (GI) classification, foods are categorized as high-GI (GI > 70), medium-GI (56 < GI < 69), and low-GI (GI < 55). The GI of wheat bread was 86.13, with the highest peak observed in the blood glucose curve among all four samples (Fig. 5A-B). This is attributed to the rapid digestion of starch in wheat bread, leading to its swift breakdown into glucose and subsequent absorption. Upon incorporating 5% CS into the bread, the GI significantly decreased to 66.44%, shifting the bread from a high-GI to a medium-GI food. This outcome aligns with the findings of Bajka et al. (Bajka et al., 2021) who demonstrated in an in-vivo digestion study that replacing wheat flour with chickpea powder resulted in a 40% reduction in glycemic response, lowering the GI of wheat bread to a medium-GI level. Further substitution with 10% CS resulted in a GI reduction to 53.78%, and at 15% CS, the GI dropped to 49.33. These results indicate that the addition of CS substantially reduces the digestibility of the bread. This effect is likely due to the higher amylose content in chickpea starch, which inhibits amylase activity (Nestel, Cehun, & Chronopoulos, 2004), as well as the increased crystallinity of CS. Edwards et al. (Edwards, Veerabahu, Mason, Butterworth, & Ellis, 2021), suggested that the crystallinity of CS significantly influences the blood glucose response during digestion. It is plausible that starch from chickpeas is digested and absorbed more slowly in the small intestine compared to wheat starch. Conclusion

This study assessed the incorporation of 5%–15% CS into bread formulations, highlighting its effects on dough microstructure, rheological properties, and bread quality. The incorporation of CS improved water retention and reduced the WSI, from 12.33 for WF to 4.05 for CS15%. CS substitution also lowered the pasting temperature, indicating reduced thermal stability and processing energy requirements. FTIR analysis demonstrated that CS induced a transition in protein secondary structure from disordered conformations (β-turn and random coil) to more ordered structures (β-sheet and α-helix), suggesting enhanced structural stability of the dough protein matrix. At higher CS levels, the gluten network was disrupted, leading to a decrease in baking loss and specific volume, reflecting improved processing efficiency. The Maillard reaction contributed to enhanced flavor and color, with higher CS levels resulting in a more pronounced beany flavor and yellowish crumb hue. Sensory evaluation revealed that 5% CS substitution provided the best texture and flavor, while higher substitution levels led to a stronger flavor but reduced cohesiveness. Additionally, CS incorporation reduced the GI of the bread, shifting it from high-GI to medium-GI. The resistant starch content increased significantly, while RDS decreased, indicating the potential of CS as a functional ingredient for low-GI, clean-label bread. In-vivo digestion tests confirmed that CS-fortified bread had a lower GI compared to wheat bread. These findings provide valuable insights into the application of chickpea starch for improving bread quality and reducing starch digestibility. Nevertheless, further studies employing advanced structural characterization techniques, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy, and solid-state NMR, together with long-term storage evaluations, are required to elucidate the structural basis of resistant starch formation and assess its stability in CS-enriched bread systems.
