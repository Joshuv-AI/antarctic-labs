Cognitive and Demographic Associations of Dementia‐Related Behavioural Changes in Adults With an Intellectual Disability: Findings From the IDS‐TILDA Study
Authors: Marie‐Joelle Beaudoin, Pavithra Pavithra, Mary McCarron, Philip McCallion, Antoinette O'Connnor, Sean Kennelly, Shoumitro Deb, Eimear McGlinchey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIndividuals with intellectual disabilities (ID) are at an increased risk of developing dementia. Early detection is essential for providing appropriate support, yet identifying dementia‐related changes is challenging due to pre‐existing cognitive impairments. Informant‐based adapted tools have been developed to support dementia screening in individuals with ID. Among those, the Dementia Screening Questionnaire for Individuals with Intellectual Disabilities (DSQIID) aims to assess behavioural and functional changes related to dementia. This study aimed to examine the associations between the DSQIID total scores, cognitive performance and demographic characteristics of adults with ID.MethodThis cross‐sectional study included 322 individuals with ID (n Down syndrome [DS] = 55; n non‐DS ID = 267). Behavioural and functional changes were assessed using the DSQIID, whereas cognitive functioning was measured with the Test for Severe Impairment (TSI), Brief Praxis Test (BPT) and a semantic verbal fluency task. Negative binomial regression analyses were conducted to explore associations between DSQIID total scores and selected demographic and cognitive variables.ResultsAge was significantly associated with behavioural dementia‐related changes, as assessed by the DSQIID, whereas ID severity was not associated. Results further showed that participants with DS had significantly higher scores on the DSQIID compared to participants with ID without DS. Higher DSQIID scores were significantly associated with higher semantic verbal fluency performance.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that caregivers may more readily detect behavioural and functional changes in adults with ID who exhibit relatively good verbal capacities. This study underscores the elevated risk of dementia in adults with DS and highlights the importance of supporting caregivers in recognizing early signs of dementia in adults with ID who exhibit lower verbal capacities. The findings underscore the importance of monitoring behavioural, functional and cognitive changes over time. Further research is needed to explore the associations between reported dementia‐related changes and the severity of ID.

Background

Over recent decades, the life expectancy of individuals with intellectual disabilities (ID) has increased significantly, with a substantial proportion now living beyond 60 years of age (Dolan et al. 2021). Despite this progress, individuals with ID still die approximately 20 years earlier than those in the general population (O'Leary et al. 2018). As they age, people with ID are at heightened risk of co‐occurring health conditions, including earlier onset of neurodegenerative diseases, compared to their peers without ID (Rubenstein et al. 2024; Takenoshita et al. 2023).

Individuals with Down syndrome (DS) exhibit a genetically determined form of Alzheimer's disease (ad), attributable to the triplication of amyloid precursor protein on chromosome 21, with near universal prevalence of amyloid pathology by age 40. Cognitive decline is almost inevitable given the 88% risk of developing dementia by age 65, with the average onset occurring at 53.4 years (Fortea et al. 2021; Iulita et al. 2022; McCarron et al. 2017).

Fewer studies have examined dementia prevalence in individuals with ID without DS (non‐DS ID). Strydom et al. (2013) found that adults with non‐DS ID are five times more likely to develop dementia compared to the general population with an average age of symptom onset at 65 years old. Prevalence of dementia in individuals with non‐DS ID ranged between 4.2% and 18.3%, depending on the age of groups with higher rates in older adults (Takenoshita et al. 2020b).

In individuals with DS, early‐stage dementia presentation has been characterized by changes in behaviours, personality and cognitive functioning (Aschenbrenner et al. 2021; Ball et al. 2006; Dekker et al. 2015; Vaughan et al. 2016). The most common symptoms reported by caregivers were the loss of previously acquired skills, confusion, forgetfulness and geographical disorientation, whereas personality changes were reported less frequently (Vaughan et al. 2016). Aschenbrenner et al. (2021) found early decline related to ad in individuals with DS in specific neuropsychological tasks assessing memory, language/executive functioning (EF), attention and praxis.

Although fewer studies have examined early dementia presentation in non‐DS ID, existing research suggests that the first signs are typically behavioural and emotional changes accompanied by a general decline in functioning, whereas memory and other cognitive impairments are less commonly reported (Strydom et al. 2007). In a more recent study, early signs of dementia included fatigue, reductions in daily living activities (particularly self‐care skills), forgetfulness and diurnal mood variations (Arvio and Bjelogrlic‐Laakso 2021).

Timely detection of dementia is essential to provide appropriate care and support for individuals with ID (Vaughan et al. 2016), facilitating informed design of individual treatment and care plans (Aschenbrenner et al. 2021). Guidelines from the National Task Group on Intellectual Disabilities and Dementia Practices recommend early and regular dementia screening for ageing adults with ID (Moran et al. 2013) using both objective and informant‐based questionnaires adapted for this population as well as tracking changes with a personalized baseline comparison rather than population norms (Dodd et al. 2018; McCarron et al. 2018; Moran et al. 2013).

A number of cognitive tests are considered sensitive to early decline associated with dementia onset in ID (Aschenbrenner et al. 2021; McCarron et al. 2014, 2017; Wallace et al. 2021). The verbal fluency task, the Test for Severe Impairment (TSI) and the Brief Praxis Test (BPT) can be used to assess cognitive decline in people with ID (McCarron et al. 2017; Mgaieth et al. 2023; Wallace et al. 2021). The TSI is especially useful in ageing adults with more severe ID. McCarron et al. (2017) reported a decline in TSI performance in individuals with DS 1 year prior to the diagnosis of dementia. In the general population, impaired performance at the verbal fluency task, requiring EF abilities, is associated with cognitive decline and ad onset (McDonnell et al. 2020). The findings of Mgaieth et al. (2023) showed that individuals with DS follow the same pattern, although the number of correct words listed decreases in older participants with ad‐related cognitive decline.

Dementia screening, as opposed to an in‐depth assessment, serves as a first step to identify changes related to a decline in functioning, indicating if a comprehensive evaluation is needed. Many proxy questionnaires have been developed for dementia screening in individuals with ID (Zeilinger et al. 2022), including the Dementia Screening Questionnaire for Individuals with Intellectual Disabilities (DSQIID) (Deb et al. 2007). The DSQIID assesses behavioural and functional changes compared to a previous level of functioning. Its conceptual rationale is grounded in the known early presentation of dementia in individuals with ID (e.g., behavioural changes), as well as in widely accepted diagnostic criteria requiring evidence of decline relative to baseline functioning. Deb et al. (2007) recommended that the DSQIID be completed by informants who have known the individual for at least 6 months to ensure an accurate understanding of their prior level of functioning.

So far, studies have identified age, type of ID and changes in behaviour, personality and cognitive functioning as reliable factors that can help practitioners make an informed decision on the aetiology of observed functioning changes in ageing individuals with ID (Aschenbrenner et al. 2021; Dekker et al. 2015; McCarron et al. 2014, 2017; Mgaieth et al. 2023; Strydom et al. 2010; Wallace et al. 2021). The aim of this study is to evaluate whether dementia‐related behavioural changes, as measured by the DSQIID, are associated with cognitive performance and demographic measures (e.g., age, type and severity of ID).

Method

This study analysed data from the Wave 5 Intellectual Disability Supplement to the Irish Longitudinal Study on Ageing (IDS‐TILDA). IDS‐TILDA is a longitudinal study on ageing in people with an intellectual disability in Ireland, with a representative sample of those over the age of 40. Wave 5 of the IDS‐TILDA study included 762 participants who completed both a Pre‐Interview Questionnaire (PIQ) and a Computer‐Assisted Personal Interview (CAPI). The PIQ gathered information on medication use, health service utilization and frequency, free time activities and reported challenging behaviours. The CAPI covered topics such as age, gender, type and severity of ID, health status, social activities, family circumstances, quality of life and interpersonal relationships. The CAPI was conducted face‐to‐face in which the participants are given the choice of opting for either self‐report, self‐report assisted by proxy or proxy interview. The PIQ was completed by the participants 1 week prior to the face‐to‐face interview. This lets participants gather information and seek support if required. Cognitive tests were carried out by a research assistant in person. A paper version of the DSQIID questionnaires was sent to carers by mail and returned to the research institute. Caregivers included both family members and staff caregivers who knew the participants for over a year. Additionally, 506 participants attended a Health Fair, where they engaged in at least one of 27 objective assessments spanning neuropsychological, cardiovascular, mobility, musculoskeletal, anthropometric, oral health, dietary, sensory and audiological domains.

Sample

Of the 762 adults with ID of Wave 5, we included only participants who completed the CAPI, the PIQ and at least one cognitive test from the Health Fair (n = 506). Participants were excluded if the DSQIID was not completed, resulting in an initial sample of 422 participants. An additional 88 participants were excluded due to missing data. Participants with dementia were subsequently excluded, yielding a final sample of 322 adults with ID (see Figure 1). Ethical approval was granted from the Faculty of Health Sciences, Research Ethics Committee at Trinity College Dublin, and ethics committees at 138 service providers involved in the study.

Measures

Participants' demographic characteristics were obtained from the CAPI and PIQ data. Neuropsychological measures included the DSQIID, the verbal fluency semantic task, the TSI and the BPT. The DSQIID is a 53‐item informant questionnaire for dementia screening developed specifically for individuals with ID (Deb et al. 2007). This tool captures functional and behavioural changes in four principal domains: memory and confusion, feelings of insecurity, sleep problems and behavioural problems. In the original English validated study, Deb et al. (2007) proposed a score of 20 as reliable dementia screening cut‐offs in adults with DS and among adults with non‐DS ID, reporting a sensitivity of 0.92 and a specificity of 0.97. Validation studies in German, Japanese and French have shown acceptable sensitivity and specificity at lower cut‐off scores. Two German studies reported sensitivity of 0.94 and specificity of 0.76 for a cut‐off of 7, compared to 0.94 and 0.72 for a cut‐off of 9 (Nuebling et al. 2024; Tarasova et al. 2024). The Japanese study found sensitivity of 1.0 and specificity of 0.96 at a cut‐off of 10–11 (S. Takenoshita et al. 2020a). The French study reported both sensitivity and specificity of 0.94 at a cut‐off of 19 (Rebillat et al. 2021). A dementia screening cut‐off score of 20 was used in the present study, as participants were English‐speaking and the English version validated by Deb et al. (2007) was applied.

The semantic verbal fluency task assesses executive functioning and is widely used in dementia screening (Olmos‐Villaseñor et al. 2023). Participants have been asked to list as many animals as possible in 60 s. The total number of animals named was recorded. The TSI is an instrument developed to assess cognitive functioning (e.g., motor skills, language, memory, general knowledge and conceptualization) in individuals with severe cognitive dysfunction. A low score indicates a possible cognitive decline due to dementia (Albert and Cohen 1992). The BPT is a 20‐item instrument to evaluate the ability to perform meaningful gestures and motor actions on command or by imitation in adults with ID. A lower total score indicates severe apraxia, which is a clinical symptom related to dementia in individuals with DS (Anderson‐Mooney et al. 2016; Dalton 2009; Wallace et al. 2021).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive and frequency analyses were conducted to summarize participants' characteristics (e.g., age, diagnosis) (see Table 1). Normality was assessed for numeric and count variables using graphical methods and skewness coefficients. The distributions of the DSQIID total score, TSI scores and BPT scores were left‐skewed, with a high frequency of zero values. Given the skewness and kurtosis of the latest scores, negative binomial regression was used to model the data. This approach was preferred over a Poisson regression based on the chi‐square/df ratio, which met the overdispersion threshold of 1.2 recommended by Payne et al. (2018). Negative binomial regression models were conducted with DSQIID total score as the dependent variable. First, the type of ID was entered as the independent variable in the full sample, controlling for age. Subsequent models examined the effects of levels of severity, age and cognitive performance (TSI, BPT and verbal fluency) in the full sample and separately in DS and non‐DS subgroups. Age was included as a covariate in all models, except those examining age. Separate models were run for each cognitive variable to address multicollinearity, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29.0.0.

Missing data were handled on a per‐instrument basis. For all cognitive tests, no imputation was performed; participants with missing data were excluded from the corresponding negative binomial regression (see Table 4). Imputation was applied only on the DSQIID to calculate the total score in participants who had completed most but not all items. Missing data on the DSQIID questionnaire occurred when one or more of the 53 items were left unanswered, which prevented the calculation of a total score and resulted in substantial missingness. Missing data on the DSQIID questionnaire occurred when one or more of the 53 items were left unanswered, preventing the calculation of a total score. To address this issue, a mode‐based imputation procedure for binary categorical data was applied (Memon et al. 2023). Following a conservative proportion threshold of 5% missing data, imputation was limited to questionnaires with at least 95% of items completed to maximize the precision (Memon et al. 2023). Among the 248 questionnaires with one or more missing items, 160 met this criterion. Data could not be imputed for the remaining 88 participants, who exceeded the 5% missing data threshold and were therefore excluded from the study. Given that DSQIID items are scored binary (0 or 1), we followed a systematic mode imputation approach to ensure that the imputed values reflected participants' response patterns. Prior to imputation, a principal components analysis was conducted (see Supporting Information). This step allowed for mode imputation to be applied within groups of correlated items, such that missing values were replaced with the most frequently occurring response (0 or 1) within each identified component. This approach aimed to preserve the internal structure of the questionnaire and minimize potential bias introduced by missing data.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Table 1 shows participants' characteristics. Participants with ID included in the study were aged from 36 to 87 years old (M = 56.46; SD = 10.56). Of the 322 participants, 55 were adults with DS (17.08%), and 267 (82.91%) were adults with non‐DS ID. From the total sample, most of the participants (40.06%) had a moderate level of ID, and about half were living in community group homes (50%).

Across the full sample, the effect of participants' age on the DSQIID total score was positive and significant (B = 0.03; SE = 0.006, IRR = 1.03, χ2 = 27.62, p < 0.001). Each additional year of age increased the DSQIID total scores by 3%. In participants with DS, age was positively but not significantly associated with the DSQIID total score (B = 0.04; SE = 0.02, IRR = 1.04, χ2(1) = 3.49, p = 0.06). In contrast, age was positively and significantly associated with the DSQIID total score in participants with non‐DS ID (B = 0.04; SE = 0.006, IRR = 1.03, χ2(1) = 29.91, p < 0.001), with a 3% increase in the DSQIID total score for each year of age.

In the total sample, the level of ID was not significantly associated with the DSQIID total score, χ2(2) = 1.49, p = 0.47. Compared to participants with severe/profound ID (reference category), participants with mild (B = −0.10, SE = 0.18, IRR = 0.91, χ2(1) = 0.32, p = 0.57) and moderate ID (B = 0.09, SE = 0.16, IRR = 1.10, χ2(1) = 0.34, p = 0.56) did not differ significantly in expected DSQIID scores. No significant association was found between the level of ID and the DSQIID total score when the DS subgroup was analysed separately, χ2(2) = 3.19, p = 0.20. In the DS subgroup, participants with mild (B = −0.14, SE = 0.65, IRR = 0.87, χ2(1) = 0.05, p = 0.83) and moderate ID (B = 0.49, SE = 0.60, IRR = 1.63, χ2(1) = 0.65, p = 0.42) did not differ significantly in the expected DSQIID score compared to severe/profound ID. The same pattern of results was found in the non‐DS ID subgroup, where the level of ID and the DSQIID total were not significantly associated, χ2(2) = 0.22, p = 0.90. Again, in this subgroup, participants with mild (B = −0.08, SE = 0.19, IRR = 0.92, χ2(1) = 0.18, p = 0.67) and moderate ID (B = −0.07, SE = 0.17, IRR = 0.94, χ2(1) = 0.15, p = 0.70) did not differ from the ones with severe/profound ID in the expected DSIIQ total score.

Relation Between DSQIID Total Score and Cognitive Functioning

In the total sample, the negative binomial regression analyses indicated that TSI (B = −0.01, SE = 0.01, IRR = 0.99, χ2(1) = 1.50, p = 0.22) and BPT (B = 0.001, SE = 0.005, IRR = 1.00, χ2(1) = 0.02, p = 0.88) were not significantly associated with the DSQIID total score. In contrast, verbal fluency total correct answers were positively and significantly associated with DSQIID scores (B = 0.05, SE = 0.02, IRR = 1.05, χ2(1) = 6.52, p = 0.01). Based on the IRR, each one‐unit increase in verbal fluency total correct answers was associated with an expected 5% increase in DSQIID total score.

In the DS subgroup, TSI was not associated with the DSQIID total scores (B = −0.02, SE = 0.03, IRR = 0.98, χ2(1) = 0.30, p = 0.58). In contrast, verbal fluency total correct answers were significantly associated with DSQIID scores (B = 0.14, SE = 0.05, IRR = 1.15, χ2(1) = 6.43, p = 0.01), whereas BPT showed a marginal positive association (B = 0.04, SE = 0.02, IRR = 1.04, χ2(1) = 3.91, p = 0.05). Based on the IRR, each one‐unit increase in verbal fluency total correct answers was associated with an expected 15% increase in DSQIID total score.

In the non‐DS ID subgroup, verbal fluency total correct answer showed a marginal positive association with DSQIID (B = 0.04, SE = 0.02, IRR = 1.04, χ2(1) = 3.72, p = 0.05), whereas TSI (B = −0.01, SE = 0.01, IRR = 0.99, χ2(1) = 1.50, p = 0.22) and BPT (B = −0.004, SE = 0.006, IRR = 1.00, χ2 = 0.40, p = 0.53) were not significant. Based on the IRR, each one‐unit increase in verbal fluency performance was associated with an expected 4% increase in DSQIID total score. Discussion

This study aimed to examine the association between age, type and severity of ID and cognitive abilities and behavioural changes indicative of dementia, as measured by DSQIID total scores. Our findings suggest that adults with DS exhibit higher levels of functional and behavioural changes related to dementia compared with adults with ID without DS. Across the full sample, increasing age was associated with greater levels of functional and behavioural change. In addition, verbal fluency performance was associated with DSQIID total scores. However, these last associations were not consistently significant across subgroups (DS and non‐DS ID).

Epidemiological studies have previously identified age‐related trends in the onset and prevalence of dementia, with the disease beginning at a younger age for most individuals with DS compared to individuals with non‐DS ID (McCarron et al. 2014, 2017; Rubenstein et al. 2024; Strydom et al. 2009, 2010, 2013; Takenoshita et al. 2023). Although our study used a cross‐sectional design, the DSQIID distribution scores by age group in our sample generally reflected patterns reported in epidemiological studies (Larsen et al. 2024; McCarron et al. 2017; Strydom et al. 2009, 2010, 2013). Among participants with non‐DS ID, those who met the DSQIID dementia screening cut‐off score of 20 (Deb et al. 2007) were predominantly aged 65 years and older. This finding is consistent with Strydom et al. (2009) and Strydom et al. (2013), who reported elevated dementia risk in adults with non‐DS ID from the age of 65 onwards. In contrast, all participants with DS who reached the dementia screening cut‐off score were 50 years old or younger, consistent with McCarron et al. (2017), who observed early signs of behavioural and functional decline in this population before the average age of dementia diagnosis at 55 years.

Our findings indicated that, across the full sample, age was positively associated with DSQIID total scores, suggesting that older adults with ID tend to exhibit greater functional and behavioural changes. However, this association was not observed when participants with DS were analysed separately. These results contrast with previous studies reporting higher DSQIID total scores among older adults with DS (Deb et al. 2007; Ghezzo et al. 2014; Washington et al. 2024). This discrepancy may be due to lower statistical power in our study compared with Deb et al. (2007), which included a larger sample. Additionally, Ghezzo et al. (2014) and Washington et al. (2024) analysed age as a categorical variable, whereas in our study, age was treated as a continuous variable. Consistent with our findings, Shinomoto et al. (2024) also found no significant association between age and DSQIID total scores among adults with DS when age was analysed continuously in a sample of comparable size.

Differences in the presentation of dementia symptoms between individuals with DS and non‐DS ID have been reported in previous studies (Dekker et al. 2015; Strydom et al. 2010; Takenoshita et al. 2020b) and are supported here. Significant differences were found in the reported rates of dementia‐related behavioural changes between the DS and non‐DS ID subgroups. DSQIID total scores were 55% higher in participants with DS compared to those with non‐DS ID. These results suggest that individuals with DS are at a greater risk of exhibiting dementia‐related behavioural symptoms compared to individuals with non‐DS ID. These results are in line with prior studies that found a higher prevalence of dementia‐related symptoms in individuals with DS (Iulita et al. 2022; Larsen et al. 2024; McCarron et al. 2017; Strydom et al. 2013; Takenoshita et al. 2020b, 2023).

No significant relationships between ID severity and DSQIID total scores in any of the subgroups, and in the total sample, were found. Although these results align with previous research investigating dementia presentation in individuals with ID (McCarron et al. 2017; Strydom et al. 2013), the unequal sample sizes within the DS subgroup in the present study may have affected the statistical power of our analysis. Prior studies investigating specifically the relation between DSQIID scores and ID severity have reported inconsistent findings. Takenoshita et al. (2023) found a significant effect of ID severity on dementia prevalence in individuals with non‐DS ID, using (among other measures) the Japanese version of the DSQIID. However, Lin et al. (2014) found no differences in DSQIID total scores across different levels of ID severity among 460 Taiwanese adults with ID aged 45 years and older. Deb et al. (2023) suggested that although ID severity may not directly impact dementia onset, it could influence the clinical presentation of dementia symptoms in individuals with DS. In their systematic review, Anderson et al. (2020) concluded that there is insufficient evidence to confirm that ID severity influences dementia onset in individuals with DS.

No significant association was observed between TSI scores and DSQIID total scores. In contrast, a marginal positive association was found between BPT performances and DSQIID total scores in the DS subgroup, and a positive association was observed between verbal fluency performance and DSQIID total scores in the full sample and the DS subgroup. These results suggest that higher levels of behavioural and functional changes reported by caregivers on the DSQIID were associated with higher verbal abilities in adults with ID, particularly among adults with DS. This pattern may reflect greater observability of behavioural and functional changes in adults with ID who have higher verbal capacities, whereas such changes may be more subtle in individuals with lower verbal abilities. These findings align with previously reported challenges in dementia screening in this population, particularly caregivers' difficulties in recognizing subtle dementia‐related changes (Holingue et al. 2022; Holst et al. 2018). Given that caregivers are often the first to notice changes, our findings underscore the importance of providing caregivers with appropriate education and support to facilitate early detection and the importance of quality caregiving relationships in dementia screening (Acton et al. 2023; Holst et al. 2018; Vaughan et al. 2016).

Informant‐based questionnaires are essential tools for dementia screening in adults with ID, as they provide real‐life information on functional, behavioural and cognitive changes (Zeilinger et al. 2022). Guidelines recommend their use in combination with cognitive testing and longitudinal monitoring (Dodd et al. 2025). Our findings support the combined use of the DSQIID and cognitive measures to screen for dementia in adults with ID, including those without DS. Previous longitudinal studies have demonstrated the sensitivity of semantic verbal fluency tasks in detecting early dementia‐related changes in adults with DS (Firth et al. 2018; Mgaieth et al. 2023). Unlike our cross‐sectional study, these investigations examined the temporal sequence of cognitive and functional changes. For example, Firth et al. (2018) reported that declines in semantic verbal fluency preceded informant‐reported dementia‐related changes, suggesting that cognitive tasks may detect prodromal signs earlier than informant‐based measures. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the relationship between DSQIID scores and cognitive performance over time.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged in our study. First, the DS and non‐DS ID subgroups were imbalanced, which may have reduced statistical power, particularly in the negative binomial regression analysis examining the relationship between DSQIID total scores and ID severity. Given that previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding this relationship, additional research may support more definitive conclusions. In addition, associations between DSQIID total scores and cognitive task performance should be interpreted with caution, as baseline cognitive functioning was not available and single‐time‐point cognitive assessments may not capture cognitive changes. Nevertheless, unlike most research in this area, this study also includes both individuals with DS and non‐DS. This broader inclusion is particularly valuable, as the dementia trajectory in non‐DS populations remains less well understood. Our findings also suggest that the DSQIID may be sensitive to detect differences in dementia‐related behavioural changes between individuals with DS and those with non‐DS ID, highlighting its potential as a valuable tool for capturing variations in dementia experiences across the ID population.

Conclusion

This study highlights the relationships between informant‐reported dementia‐related behavioural changes, as measured by the DSQIID, and key factors associated with dementia onset in individuals with DS and non‐DS ID. Across the full sample, age was significantly associated with the DSQIID total scores, whereas ID severity did not appear to be related to functional and behavioural dementia‐related changes, as assessed by the DSQIID. Results also suggest that individuals with DS are at a higher risk of developing dementia‐related behavioural symptoms compared to those with non‐DS ID. Significant positive associations were observed between the DSQIID total scores and performance on the semantic verbal fluency task, and a marginal association was found in BPT performances. This study highlights the increased risk of dementia in adults with DS. It also highlights the need for caregivers to recognize early dementia‐related changes in adults with ID, particularly those with lower verbal abilities. Our findings support the usefulness of DSQIID for dementia screening among adults with ID and underscore the importance of monitoring behavioural, functional and cognitive changes over time. Longitudinal research is needed to clarify the relationship between DSQIID scores and cognitive performance over time, and larger samples of adults with DS should be included to further examine associations with ID severity.
