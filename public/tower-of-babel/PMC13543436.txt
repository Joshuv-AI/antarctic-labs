Single-cell RNA sequencing in ovarian cancer: decoding the tumor microenvironment for personalized therapy
Authors: Usamah Sayed, Shaker Al-Hasnaawei, Hayjaa Mohaisen-Mousa, Renuka Jyothi-S, Priya Priyadarshini-Nayak, Bethanney Janney-J, Gurjant Singh, Ashish Singh-Chauhan, Manoj Kumar-Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian cancer (OC) remains a leading cause of mortality among gynecological malignancies, largely due to profound inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity and the critical influence of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Comprising immune, stromal, endothelial, and extracellular matrix components, the TME orchestrates tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized the study of OC by providing high-resolution insights into rare cellular subpopulations, dynamic transcriptional programs, and intercellular communication networks. These advances have facilitated the discovery of prognostic biomarkers, immune signatures, and novel therapeutic targets. Moreover, integration of scRNA-seq with spatial transcriptomics, multi-omics platforms, and artificial intelligence has expanded its potential to capture cellular complexity and refine patient stratification. Despite current challenges, including underrepresentation of specific cell types, technical variability, and high cost, scRNA-seq continues to drive progress in precision oncology. Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) represents a tumor type that holds significant promise for the application of personalized medicine approaches. Advances in both diagnostic and therapeutic modalities have gradually improved the overall survival rates for patients with OC, rising from approximately 37% to nearly 50%. This progress reflects ongoing innovation in the medical and scientific understanding of the disease [1, 2]. The underlying causes of ovarian cancer are multifaceted, involving a complex interplay of genetic predispositions, environmental exposures, and behavioral or lifestyle influences. As our comprehension of tumor biology continues to evolve, it has become increasingly evident that both the unique characteristics of cancer cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME) in which they exist play critical roles in disease behavior and therapeutic response. These factors are being increasingly recognized as essential targets for individualized treatment strategies [3].

The TME is a dynamic and heterogeneous milieu that encompasses tumor cells along with a diverse array of surrounding cellular and non-cellular components. It is generally categorized into several key elements: tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TIICs), stromal cells, endothelial cells (ECs), and non-cellular structures such as the extracellular matrix and various signaling molecules [4]. Together, these components create a supportive and interactive environment that influences tumor progression, local invasion, metastatic potential, and resistance to therapies. Specifically, they facilitate tumor proliferation, enable immune system evasion, promote the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis), and contribute to resistance against chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapies. Due to the complexity and functional relevance of the TME, research emphasis has increasingly shifted from focusing solely on tumor cells to examining tumor cells and their surrounding microenvironment as an integrated unit [4–6].

It is also important to highlight that immune cells are frequently present within the stromal compartment of the TME. While the term “immune-infiltrating cells” specifically refers to immune cells that penetrate and act within tumor tissues, the concept of immune cells in the matrix focuses more on their spatial distribution and localization within the tumor stroma [7]. However, in practical research settings, this distinction is often blurred, and the primary scientific interest lies in the dynamic interactions between immune cells and other components within the TME. For the purposes of this discussion, all immune cells present in the TME will be collectively classified as part of the stromal compartment. Both immune and non-immune stromal cells, including fibroblasts, mesenchymal cells, and endothelial cells, constitute a major area of investigation, especially in the context of tumor immunotherapy [7, 8]. The phenotypic characteristics and functional behaviors of these stromal components are influenced by a variety of intrinsic and extrinsic factors, as demonstrated by numerous studies [9, 10]. However, the high degree of heterogeneity and variability within the TME continues to challenge conventional research methodologies, underscoring the need for innovative investigative tools [1, 9–11].

Recent advancements in genomic technologies have paved the way for several powerful tools such as bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell DNA and RNA sequencing, single-cell proteomics, and single-cell metabolomics [12, 13]. These approaches enable a much more nuanced and granular exploration of complex diseases, including cancer, thereby offering new avenues for the development of more effective and precisely targeted therapies. In this context, the application of single-cell technologies is proving to be especially valuable in unraveling the intricate features of the TME in ovarian cancer. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of how single-cell analysis is currently being utilized to investigate the stromal components of the TME in OC, as well as to highlight the latest findings and emerging trends in this rapidly advancing field [12–14].

scRNA-seq technology overview

Direct sequencing of RNA from single cells is technically unfeasible. Therefore, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) requires an initial conversion of RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA), followed by amplification through either polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or in vitro transcription (IVT) before the sequencing process can occur [15]. This workflow presents two primary technical challenges: minimizing RNA degradation during reverse transcription and ensuring sufficient amplification of DNA to produce high-quality sequencing data, while also mitigating the influence of background noise not associated with true single-cell signals. To overcome these limitations, successive iterations of scRNA-seq technology have been developed and refined, expanding the potential applications and improving performance. Each version offers specific strengths and areas of focus in molecular detection (Fig. 1) [15–17].

Cell isolation techniques

The standard workflow for scRNA-seq encompasses four main phases: isolating individual cells, performing reverse transcription, amplifying the resulting cDNA, and constructing a sequencing-ready library [18]. Single-cell isolation methods include manual selection, dilution or random seeding, laser capture microdissection, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), and more recent approaches using microfluidics or microplate formats. Microfluidic-based systems, as employed in methods like Drop-seq, encapsulate single cells into individual droplets containing oligonucleotide primers, DNA barcodes, and molecular tags [19]. This significantly enhances the throughput and enables parallel processing of thousands of cells, which is a key advantage for large-scale transcriptomic studies [23–25].

Variants of scRNA-seq protocols

Numerous scRNA-seq protocols have been developed, each offering varying strengths. The earliest, known as the Tang method, utilized micromanipulation for cell isolation and was characterized by relatively low sensitivity and accuracy [20, 21]. Smart-seq, which uses Moloney murine leukemia virus (MMLV) reverse transcriptase, allows for full-length cDNA generation with improved sensitivity and precision. Variants such as STRT-seq and STRT/C1-seq incorporate unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and biotin-labeled primers to enhance transcript quantification, though they demonstrate a bias toward the 5′ end of transcripts. CEL-seq and its improved version, CEL-seq2, employ IVT to focus on the 3′ ends, achieving high sensitivity but reduced accuracy and a strong 3′ bias. Drop-seq, while enabling the sequencing of a large number of individual cells, exhibits lower sensitivity in detecting low-abundance transcripts [26–28].

Reverse transcription and cDNA amplification

Reverse transcription and cDNA amplification are critical steps that determine the overall accuracy and sensitivity of scRNA-seq. Most approaches use oligodT primers for reverse transcription, which primarily target polyadenylated transcripts and can therefore exclude non-coding RNAs such as long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs [23]. Depending on how reverse transcription and amplification are executed, scRNA-seq techniques can be broadly categorized into three types: poly(A) tail addition followed by PCR, IVT-based methods, and template-switching approaches [29, 30]. In the poly(A) tail method, exemplified by the Tang protocol, synthetic poly(A) sequences are added to the 3′ end of RNA, allowing nearly full-length transcript amplification. Although this technique facilitates the detection of previously uncharacterized transcripts, it suffers from a 3′ bias and lower enzymatic efficiency, which compromises sensitivity and the detection of low-expressing genes. IVT methods utilize primers with a T7 promoter, and following second-strand synthesis, IVT is employed for amplification [26]. This approach is prone to substantial 3′ bias due to its directional nature. Template-switching, used in Smart-seq, Smart-seq2, and STRT-seq, employs MMLV reverse transcriptase to add non-templated nucleotides to the 3′ end of cDNA, which serves as a binding site for a template-switching oligonucleotide (TSO). This mechanism allows the capture of full-length transcripts, effectively minimizing 3′ bias and enhancing transcriptome coverage, see Table 2 [31–33].

Barcoding and unique molecular identifiers

Before sequencing, barcoding is applied to label individual cells uniquely, enabling multiple samples to be processed in a single sequencing run without confusion [29]. This strategy streamlines workflow and reduces reagent consumption by allowing early pooling of samples, followed by a single amplification step. To further address PCR bias, UMIs (short nucleotide sequences that uniquely tag individual RNA molecules) are incorporated during reverse transcription [6, 30]. These tags help distinguish between genuine transcripts and PCR duplicates, thereby improving the reliability of quantification [31]. Techniques such as CEL-seq, CEL-seq2, STRT-seq, STRT/C1-seq, and Drop-seq utilize UMI-based tagging to enhance accuracy. Notably, STRT-seq employs streptavidin-coated beads to simplify downstream library preparation and quality control processes [35–38].

Data analysis is a vital component of scRNA-seq due to the inherently low RNA input from individual cells, which necessitates amplification and introduces technical variability [33]. A frequent consequence of this is “dropout” events, where transcripts go undetected despite being present, resulting in numerous zero values in gene expression matrices [17, 34]. To address this, specialized normalization and analysis tools have been developed. Among the most widely used are Seurat, Monocle, Scanpy, and Linnorm. These platforms support various analytical steps including quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and downstream analyses such as cell type annotation, differential expression, and pseudotime trajectory inference [39–41]. Seurat is a comprehensive toolkit designed to manage and interpret transcriptomic heterogeneity across cells. It facilitates subgroup classification, identification of marker genes, and integration of multi-modal data. In contrast, Monocle provides an unsupervised framework to arrange cells along differentiation trajectories, thereby revealing dynamic gene regulation patterns over time [35]. Scanpy, a Python-based platform, is optimized for processing large-scale datasets and supports numerous analytical functions such as visualization, trajectory inference, and gene regulatory network construction. Additional tools like Linnorm, SCnorm, scran, DESeq, and others offer further analytical flexibility depending on experimental needs [42, 43].

Advances in commercial platforms for scRNA-seq

Currently, leading commercial platforms for scRNA-seq include 10X Genomics and BD Rhapsody. These systems provide integrated workflows that complete cell processing, library construction, sequencing, and preliminary data analysis within a relatively short time frame, typically under 30 min [44]. Recent innovations in single-cell transcriptomics have led to integration with complementary technologies, resulting in highly adaptable platforms. CITE-seq (Cellular Indexing of Transcriptomes and Epitopes by sequencing) merges transcriptome analysis with cell surface protein profiling by tagging antibodies with oligonucleotide barcodes [32]. This allows simultaneous measurement of mRNA expression and protein abundance from the same single cell, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional scRNA-seq, which lacks protein-level information. The barcoded antibodies bind to specific cell surface markers, and their sequences are captured alongside the transcriptome during sequencing, enabling parallel yet distinct analysis of both molecular layers [45, 46]. Another advancement, CROP-seq (CRISPR droplet sequencing), integrates genome editing with scRNA-seq to functionally screen genes at single-cell resolution. This platform involves the delivery of guide RNAs (gRNAs) that are traceable within transcriptomic data, high-throughput single-cell sequencing, and a computational pipeline that links transcriptome profiles to specific genetic perturbations. By directly associating gRNA presence with changes in gene expression, CROP-seq enables precise functional dissection of regulatory networks and heterogeneous cell responses [47, 48]. Investigating tumor heterogeneity and progression using scRNA-seq

Cancer is increasingly understood as a multifaceted disease shaped by various biological and environmental influences. The prevailing genetic model posits that carcinogenesis arises from the gradual build-up of somatic mutations through evolutionary processes [43]. These mutations, occurring unpredictably across the genome, can sometimes drive the malignant transformation of normal cells. Such somatic alterations include diverse forms of genetic heterogeneity, such as point mutations, insertions, and deletions, as well as broader genomic instabilities like chromosomal rearrangements, copy number variations, and microsatellite instability. With the advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS), links between specific oncogenic mutations and tumor types, including breast, liver, and lung cancers, have been firmly established [53–55].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), as an extension of NGS, enables the dissection of the molecular landscape at unprecedented resolution and is instrumental in characterizing tumor evolution from early lesions to advanced and metastatic disease [8, 45]. For instance, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDA) _a malignancy marked by a protracted preclinical phase, scRNA-seq of epithelial cells with pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) allows for the identification of transcriptional changes associated with proliferation, invasion, and metastatic potential. This approach enables early detection of malignant transformation risk and informs therapeutic strategies. Additionally, transcriptional profiling of pancreatic cancer cells can support subtype classification, contributing to more precise targeted interventions (Fig. 2) [56, 57].

Applications of single-cell sequencing

In animal models of PDA, researchers have used scRNA-seq to trace the progression from precancerous stages, including acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM), to invasive cancer. These studies revealed distinct gene expression patterns and cellular heterogeneity, leading to the identification of novel molecular targets for therapy [28, 47, 48]. Tumor metastasis _responsible for the majority of cancer-related mortality_ is characterized by cellular dissemination, survival in the circulatory system, and colonization of distant organs. scRNA-seq has been used to compare primary tumors with their metastatic counterparts, uncovering both genetic and transcriptional divergence. In breast cancer, scRNA-seq identified specific genes involved in cell migration and revealed unique subpopulations in transitional epithelial-mesenchymal states, highlighting potential targets for limiting metastatic spread [58, 59].

In clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), transcriptional heterogeneity related to metastatic potential has been explored using scRNA-seq. Loss of function in the VHL tumor suppressor gene, common event in ccRCC, has been linked to enhanced invasiveness and altered metabolism. Analysis of metastatic tumor cells revealed upregulation of EGFR and Src, suggesting these as viable targets for combinatorial therapy. These findings have been validated through both in vitro assays and in vivo models [60]. The utility of scRNA-seq in tumor heterogeneity extends beyond developmental pathways to include tumor classification, identification of unique malignant cell states, and assessment of microenvironmental and epigenetic influences [51]. For example, in glioblastoma, scRNA-seq has uncovered a range of transcriptionally distinct cell populations, including neural progenitor-like, oligodendrocyte-like, astrocyte-like, and mesenchymal phenotypes, shaped in part by the cell cycle and local niche conditions. Similarly, in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), single-cell sequencing has been used to trace hematopoietic differentiation, identify malignant clones through mutational profiling, and classify these into biologically meaningful subtypes, laying the groundwork for precision medicine approaches [61, 62].

While much attention has historically focused on genomic changes in cancer, recent insights emphasize the role of epigenetic regulation in driving tumor heterogeneity. Environmental and metabolic cues can destabilize chromatin structure, promoting carcinogenesis in precancerous cells or facilitating adaptation in malignant ones. scRNA-seq, when integrated with epigenetic profiling, enables detailed mapping of chromatin dynamics at single-cell resolution [60]. Techniques such as single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (scATAC-seq) have made it possible to measure chromatin accessibility in individual cells, offering insights into the regulatory landscape underlying cell-to-cell variability. For example, DNA accessibility maps generated from lymphoblastoid cells revealed associations between chromatin architecture and transcription factor activity [53]. Other multi-omics approaches, like scNMT-seq, allow for simultaneous assessment of chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, and gene expression in the same cell, facilitating a more comprehensive understanding of the epigenome-transcriptome relationship during cellular transitions [55, 63].

Single-cell transcriptomics also lends strong support to the cancer stem cell (CSC) model, which posits that a subpopulation of self-renewing cells within a tumor drives heterogeneity, metastasis, and therapy resistance. CSCs give rise to various non-tumorigenic lineages within the tumor microenvironment, making them critical targets for therapeutic intervention. scRNA-seq has been instrumental in identifying these populations and uncovering signaling pathways, such as Wnt and Notch, active in CSCs. By combining multiple model systems, including 3D organoids and patient-derived xenografts (PDX), researchers have demonstrated that inhibition of these pathways can suppress CSC-driven tumor growth [64, 65]. Furthermore, pseudotemporal trajectory analysis using single-cell data has revealed the dynamic differentiation paths of CSCs into diverse tumor subtypes. In collecting duct renal cell carcinoma (CDRCC), scRNA-seq identified CSCs as a central node in tumor progression, with high expression of malignancy-associated genes like BIRC5 and CDKN3. These markers were correlated with poor clinical outcomes, and targeted therapies directed at CSC-specific pathways were proposed as promising treatment strategies [66].

Despite its advantages, scRNA-seq is not without limitations. Technical challenges include biased transcript capture (3′ or 5′ end), limited sensitivity for low-abundance transcripts, sequencing depth constraints, and elevated noise levels. To address these issues, novel methodologies have been developed [57]. For example, Genotyping of Transcriptomes (GoT) integrates somatic mutation detection with transcriptomic profiling in thousands of individual tumor cells, overcoming the problem of low gene expression or transcript distance from capture sites. Similarly, tools like inferCNV enable detection of copy number alterations by comparing gene expression patterns between tumor and reference cells, thereby enhancing interpretation of large-scale genomic events [67, 68]. In summary, while challenges remain, single-cell RNA sequencing represents a transformative approach in the study of cancer heterogeneity. It provides unprecedented resolution for understanding the molecular basis of tumor development, metastatic potential, treatment resistance, and epigenetic regulation. Continued refinement of scRNA-seq technologies and integrative multi-omics platforms will likely play a pivotal role in the evolution of personalized cancer therapy.

scRNA-seq in immune cell profiling and tumor immune heterogeneity

A key component of tumor heterogeneity lies in the diversity of immune responses within the tumor microenvironment, a factor that significantly impacts both therapeutic resistance and the outcomes of immunotherapy. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revealed that tumors are infiltrated by a complex array of immune cells [59]. These include elements of the innate immune system such as dendritic cells (DCs), which exist in both immature and activated states, as well as eosinophils, neutrophils, mast cells, macrophages, and various subsets of natural killer (NK) cells, including those characterized by CD56^dim and CD56^bright phenotypes. In parallel, cells of the adaptive immune system are also present, including a diverse array of T lymphocytes such as T helper 1 (Th1) and T helper 2 (Th2) cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), cytotoxic CD8 + T cells, central memory T cells (Tcm), effector memory T cells (Tem), follicular helper T cells (Tfh), and γδ T cells [25, 69].

This immune diversity within tumors, known as immune heterogeneity, manifests not only in the variety of immune cell subtypes but also in the level and distribution of immune infiltration, which can differ markedly among patients depending on tumor type and disease progression stage. For instance, certain cancers such as melanoma and non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) exhibit robust infiltration by T cells. In contrast, immune cell infiltration is sparse in tumors typically resistant to immunotherapy, including ovarian and prostate cancers [58, 59]. Multiple studies have linked the density of tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes (TILs) to both the patient’s overall prognosis and their responsiveness to immune checkpoint therapies. Elevated infiltration by CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes is often predictive of prolonged disease-free survival and improved treatment outcomes. Conversely, increased presence of Treg cells within the tumor microenvironment has been correlated with diminished response to immunotherapeutic strategies, as these cells contribute to immunosuppressive mechanisms that hinder anti-tumor immunity [17, 64, 70].

The application of scRNA-seq has advanced the understanding of these immune dynamics. In a study analyzing breast cancer specimens from multiple patients, it was shown that TIL populations predominantly consisted of T cells, B cells, and macrophages. Among these, a majority of the T cells exhibited signs of functional exhaustion, while the macrophage populations predominantly assumed an M2-like, pro-tumorigenic phenotype. These findings underscored the utility of scRNA-seq in identifying immunosuppressive cellular subsets that could serve as potential therapeutic targets, especially in malignancies such as triple-negative breast cancer that show limited responsiveness to conventional immunotherapies [23, 70]. Owing to its high resolution, scRNA-seq is particularly effective in classifying immune cells with precision, allowing for the differentiation of helper T cells, cytotoxic T cells, and other immune subtypes based on unique transcriptional profiles [44, 60]. In a comprehensive analysis involving over 11,000 T cells from colorectal cancer patients, researchers used scRNA-seq to trace their spatial distribution across tissues, investigate patterns of clonal expansion, and chart their developmental pathways. By examining genes with high expression levels and specific T cell receptor (TCR) repertoires, it was possible to further stratify these T cell subsets and construct a detailed immunological map of the tumor microenvironment. This kind of in-depth analysis facilitates the development of more nuanced and personalized immunotherapeutic approaches [46, 54, 71].

Additionally, scRNA-seq has proven invaluable in monitoring the impact of immune-targeted treatments. In an experiment evaluating the efficacy of anti-CD47 therapy, single-cell analysis revealed an increase in CD4 + and CD8 + T cells and a concurrent reduction in Treg cell populations within the tumor. These changes correlated with enhanced immune activation and greater tumor sensitivity to immunotherapeutic intervention. Unlike bulk sequencing, which aggregates cellular signals and masks subtle changes, scRNA-seq provided high-resolution insight into the remodeling of the immune landscape induced by therapy. The data also indicated a decrease in immunosuppressive macrophages, suggesting that the therapy effectively modulated both innate and adaptive arms of the immune system to facilitate a more potent anti-tumor response [72, 73]. In summary, scRNA-seq enables detailed immune profiling within the tumor microenvironment, revealing the extensive heterogeneity in immune cell populations and their functional states. This high-resolution approach aids in uncovering mechanisms underlying resistance to immunotherapy, identifying key immunoregulatory cell subsets, and guiding the design of tailored therapeutic strategies that target the immune composition of individual tumors. As immunotherapies continue to evolve, scRNA-seq is poised to remain a critical tool for enhancing their precision and effectiveness across diverse cancer types.

Dissecting tumor intercellular communication using scRNA-seq

Tumors are dynamic ecosystems collected of diverse cell populations, including malignant, stromal elements and immune cells [74]. The specific cellular composition and the complicated interactions among these cells significantly contribute to cancer progress and development [74]. While immune heterogeneity is serious, variability among non-immune cell types complicates our considerate of tumor biology [75]. These cellular interactions occur over numerous signaling mechanisms such as ligand-receptor interactions and paracrine and autocrine signaling which impact key cellular behaviors like proliferation rates, morphological changes, invasive potential, and metastatic capacity [75]. These interactions shape the tumor microenvironment and affect treatment responsiveness and clinical results.

Evolving therapeutic approaches

Recent years have seen a shift from conventional tumor-centric therapeutic models to integrated methods that acknowledge the tumor microenvironment as a critical driver of malignant behavior [75, 76]. This recognition has transformed the focus towards targeting interactions among tumor cells and their surrounding stroma and immune cells to improve therapeutic outcomes. scRNA-seq allows for complete profiling of these diverse cell populations, easing detailed analysis of functional states, cell abundance, and intercellular communication networks [75, 76]. This high-resolution tool supports inter-patient comparisons and microenvironmental studies, offering predictive visions into tumor progression and treatment efficiency.

Insights from tumor heterogeneity research

Wide studies have shown that cancer development is heavily influenced by communication between cancer cells and surrounding stromal cells [76]. For instance, tumor cells secrete cytokines, such as interleukins, that recruit immune cells like macrophages [60]. Moreover, VEGF released by tumor cells promotes endothelial cell proliferation and migratory behavior, enabling new blood vessel formation to support tumor growth [60].

One prominent study applied scRNA-seq to examine the expression profiles of over 2,500 ligand-receptor pairs from melanoma samples, surrounding various cell types, including tumor cells, T and B lymphocytes, macrophages, natural killer cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and endothelial cells [74]. This investigate revealed a dense communication network driven by ligand-receptor interactions involving matrix proteins, chemokines, and growth factors, which were concerned in processes such as tumor development and immune regulation [74].

In glioma studies leveraging scRNA-seq, significant crosstalk has been identified between tumor-associated glioma stem-like cells and macrophages. Raised expression of several ligand-receptor pairs, such as Neuroligin-3 (Nlgn3), Notch1, Dll1, and Glial Cell-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (GDNF) has been linked to tumor features like invasion and proliferation [77]. Additionally, a prognostic model based on ligand-receptor expression patterns has effectively stratified patient outcomes, highlighting the clinical relevance of these interactions [77].

Computational modeling and systematic analysis

In other research, computational modeling of scRNA-seq data has allowed for systematic analysis of intercellular communication in tumor models and human melanoma tissues [78]. One analytical context recognized key ligand-receptor interactions, show high interaction scores for certain chemokine receptor-ligand pairs such as CCR1, CCR2, and CCR5. These interactions highlight the critical role of macrophages in arranging tumor microenvironmental communication and inducing immunological dynamics [78].

The application of scRNA-seq in the ovarian cancer ME has discovered essential insights [79]. The TME consists of tumor cells, diverse supporting cellular populations, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells, as well as non-cellular components like the ECM and soluble factors [79]. Important heterogeneity within tumor cells correlates with distinct gene expression profiles indicative of various tumor expansion stages. This heterogeneity complicates treatment but also permits for targeted therapies aimed at specific subtypes [79].

The prosperity of immune cell types within the TME has become apparent, counting various T cell subsets, macrophages, and dendritic cells [80]. Results suggest that certain immune populations are linked to enhanced anti-tumor responses, underscoring the potential for immunotherapeutic strategies [80]. The analysis also emphasized the role of CAFs, which showed gene signatures related with ECM remodeling and the secretion of growth factors, possibly contributing to tumor aggressiveness [81].

Variations in endothelial cell expression patterns indicated an angiogenic phenotype, highlighting the need to target angiogenesis in therapeutic plans. Moreover, cytokine profiling revealed a network of signaling pathways driving immune evasion and forming a tumor-promoting environment.

Investigating tumor heterogeneity and progression using scRNA-seq

Analysis using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has delivered critical insights into the diverse cellular and non-cellular compartments of the OV cancer TME [82]. The TME comprises not only tumor cells but also a variety of supporting cellular populations, including fibroblasts, immune cells, and endothelial cells, alongside non-cellular components such as the ECM and soluble factors [79]. Data show important heterogeneity among the tumor cells, considered by distinct gene expression profiles that correlate with various stages of tumor development [83]. This heterogeneity underscores the complication of ovarian cancer and presents prospects for targeted therapies aimed at specific tumor subtypes [84]. A rich diversity of immune cell types within the TME has been recognized, including numerous subsets of T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. The differential expression of immune checkpoint molecules proposes that certain immune populations may be coupled with enhance anti-tumor responses, highlighting the potential for immunotherapeutic plans [85]. The scRNA-seq analysis indicated an important role for CAFs in modulating tumor dynamics. Activated CAFs showed gene signatures associated with ECM remodeling and secretion of growth factors, which could contribute to tumor aggressiveness and metabolic support [85]. Endothelial cells within the TME showed altered expression patterns revealing of an angiogenic phenotype. This result supports the ongoing employment of blood vessels to sustain tumor growth, highlighting the importance of targeting angiogenesis within therapeutic frameworks [86]. The examination also extended to non-cellular elements in the TME. The incidence of various ECM components and soluble factors was noted, representing a complex interplay that facilitates tumor progression [86]. Changes in ECM-related gene expression propose that the tumor environment is actively modified, promoting invasion and metastasis as a response to the tumor’s metabolic needs [87]. Profiling of cytokines and growth factors discovered a network of signaling pathways that may drive immune evasion and contribute to a tumor-promoting milieu. The secretion of immunosuppressive factors highlights potential therapeutic targets to enhance anti-tumor immunity. Combining these findings into ongoing research enhances the understanding of ovarian cancers TME [87]. Insights gained from scRNA-seq not only clarify the complex interactions among various components but also open avenues for groundbreaking therapeutic plans aimed at disrupting these critical cellular and molecular networks. This complicated method is essential for the progression of effective treatment modalities for ovarian cancer patients.

Specialized cells in the ovarian cancer tumor microenvironment characterized by scRNA-seq

Immune-infiltrating cells (IICs) represent essential components of the ovarian cancer tumor microenvironment, playing dual and sometimes contradictory roles. They contribute to antitumor defense through immune surveillance mechanisms, such as the cytotoxic activities exerted by natural killer (NK) cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). However, IICs are also implicated in tumor progression, promoting proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Particularly, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) have emerged as key contributors by secreting pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic mediators (such as interleukins, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)) that foster a tumor-supportive environment [88, 89].

More recently, attention has shifted toward the role of IICs in shaping responses to immunotherapies. Their functions and plasticity make them potential therapeutic targets, especially within the context of immune checkpoint inhibitors and CAR-T cell-based strategies. The phenotypic and functional heterogeneity of IICs, which is shaped by intercellular signaling with tumor and stromal cells, further underlines their complex role in the TME (Fig. 3) [90].

The tumor microenvironment and scRNA-seq

Single-cell sequencing technologies, such as scRNA-seq, have enabled the dissection of immune cell diversity in the OC microenvironment. Early studies, including one by scientists have identified critical immune cell populations, such as TAMs, T-cell subsets, and dendritic cells associated with tumor cells, each exhibiting distinct transcriptional and phenotypic profiles [91, 92]. In addition to IICs, CAFs, as non-immune components of the TME, also significantly influence immune dynamics. Recent scRNA-seq analyses have increasingly focused on this cell type, further highlighting the complexity of the immune-regulatory network in OC [25, 93]. Within the immune compartment of the TME, tumor-infiltrating macrophages (TIMs) and TAMs are particularly notable. While both cell types arise from circulating monocytes, TIMs typically refer to recently recruited macrophages whose activity is dynamically shaped by local environmental cues, including cytokine gradients and metabolic changes. TAMs, in contrast, tend to adopt a more defined M2-like phenotype that supports tumor growth, matrix remodeling, and immune suppression. Despite some functional overlap, each subtype demonstrates unique regulatory features and contributions to disease progression [94, 95].

The traditional polarization model classifies macrophages into M1 and M2 types. M1 macrophages are known for their pro-inflammatory, tumor-suppressing activity, while M2 macrophages contribute to tumor advancement through immunosuppressive and pro-tumoral effects. Quantitative tools have been developed using scRNA-seq data to evaluate the M1/M2 ratio, offering a means to identify immune subtypes with prognostic significance. In OC, M2-dominant macrophage populations have been linked with poor outcomes and resistance to chemotherapy agents like cisplatin [96]. Genes such as SLAMF7 and GNAS have been implicated in this resistance, further highlighting potential molecular targets [97, 98]. Additionally, autophagy has been identified as a distinguishing feature of M2 macrophages in OC [99]. Highly autophagic M2 cells correlate with chemoresistance, suggesting that modulation of autophagy could enhance treatment response [100]. The omentum, a common site of OC metastasis, harbors immune cell aggregates resembling lymphoid structures (80). Drop-seq studies have verified notable variations in macrophage activation states between primary and metastatic TMEs in ovarian cancer [101]. Specifically, activated macrophages in primary TMEs generally exhibit a more pro-inflammatory profile, characterized by elevated levels of cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which are crucial for anti-tumor immunity [102]. In contrast, macrophages in metastatic TMEs tend to adopt a more immunosuppressive phenotype, often driven by factors such as TGF-β and IL-10 [103]. This shift leads to a reduced capacity for phagocytosis and a diminished ability to present antigens, ultimately facilitating tumor progression and immune evasion [104]. Also, emerging evidence highlights the role of different macrophage subsets, such as M1 and M2 macrophages. While M1 macrophages are typically associated with anti-tumor responses in primary tumors, M2 macrophages, which are more prevalent in metastatic settings, promote tissue remodeling and tumor metastasis [105]. Understanding these dynamics can provide critical insights into therapeutic strategies targeting the immune microenvironment in ovarian cancer [106]. In immune-active environments, M1-like macrophages predominate but may still exhibit non-classical markers such as CD274. Furthermore, these populations often display transcriptional signatures similar to myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), suggesting the presence of transitional or hybrid states that complicate the M1/M2 paradigm [107, 108].

These observations underscore the limitations of binary macrophage classification and point to a spectrum of phenotypic states within the TME. Advances in single-cell technology have made it possible to delineate this diversity more precisely. For instance, in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), scRNA-seq profiling has revealed at least ten macrophage subpopulations, see Table 5 [109].

Macrophage Subpopulations and their key characteristics

Early-stage macrophages demonstrate immunostimulatory properties, while more differentiated subsets exhibit enhanced expression of growth-promoting genes and reduced ability to recruit effector immune cells [90]. The proportional representation of these subtypes significantly impacts patient outcomes [129, 130]. Another central feature of macrophages is their remarkable plasticity [131]. Various factors, including cytokines like CCL2, CSF-1, CCL18, TNF-α, and metabolic conditions within the TME, can drive transitions between phenotypes. Non-coding RNAs have also been shown to influence this plasticity [132]. Newer findings indicate that macrophages can express unique surface receptors like C5aR1, which impair T cell cytotoxicity and promote angiogenesis [133]. Moreover, cytokine-mediated remodeling is evident in interactions involving oncostatin M (OSM) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [134]. Under IL-6 stimulation, macrophages release OSM, which activates tumor cells through the OSMR-IL6ST receptor complex and STAT3 signaling, thus enhancing invasive behavior [134]. IL-6, already known for its involvement in TAM development, is further contextualized by scRNA-seq data, which continues to expand our understanding of its role in OC progression [89, 93].

In summary, single-cell technologies have significantly advanced the understanding of immune and stromal cell behavior in the OC TME. The insights gained from these studies reveal a highly dynamic and heterogeneous cellular ecosystem, where macrophages, due to their phenotypic plasticity and regulatory influence, play a pivotal role in tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. Ongoing research into these diverse cell populations is essential for the refinement of current treatment strategies and the identification of novel therapeutic targets.

T cells

T cells play a multifaceted and indispensable role within the tumor microenvironment (TME), especially in ovarian cancer (OC). While not all T-cell subsets are inherently tumoricidal, T cell–centered immunotherapeutic approaches remain at the forefront of cancer treatment strategies [83]. In OC, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) mainly consist of CD8 + cytotoxic T cells, CD4 + helper T cells, and Tregs [135]. Studies have shown that elevated TIL density coupled with reduced Treg presence is typically associated with improved patient outcomes [106]. However, this broad categorization has limited utility in explaining the frequent failure of immunotherapy in OC [136, 137]. One proposed explanation is that suppressive macrophages within the TME might inhibit TILs from executing effective tumor cell lysis. Moreover, a substantial fraction of CD8 + TILs, particularly in high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSC), appear non-reactive to autologous tumor cells or tumor-associated antigens, indicating that only specific T-cell subsets possess true anti-tumor functionality [138]. Discriminating these functionally potent T-cell subtypes is vital for refining T cell–based immunotherapy [139, 140].

Research led by Céline Laumont identified three surface markers (CD39, CD103, and PD-1) on CD8 + T cells in HGSOC [141]. Cells co-expressing these markers demonstrated superior cytotoxicity and antigen specificity, correlating with better clinical outcomes. Additionally, scientists investigation emphasized how the extent of T-cell infiltration helps define immune subtypes in OC [142]. Infiltration-rich TMEs exhibited TOX+ CD8 + and GNLY+ CD4 + phenotypes, while GNLY expression in low-infiltration cases was confined to CD8 + cells. Plasma cells expressing PRDM1 and SDC1, along with IFNG+ plasmablasts, were also prominent in high-infiltration environments, suggesting a nuanced immune cell composition relevant for personalized therapy [143, 144].

Further expanding on T-cell heterogeneity, one study used single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to characterize nine distinct CD8 + T-cell subpopulations in HGSOC [145]. Among these, tissue-resident memory CD8 + T cells (CD8 + TRM), identified by the CD8-C1-IFIT3 signature, were enriched in tumor tissues. Simultaneously, a large fraction of CD8 + T cells expressing exhaustion markers (CTLA4, PDCD1, TIGIT, LAG3, HAVCR2, SIRPA) was also present, with their abundance diminishing as the tumor progressed. These exhausted cells (CD8 + TEX) likely arise from CD8 + TRM and central memory cells (CD8 + TCM) during early tumorigenesis. Notably, CD8 + TRM cells exhibited high levels of IL15, IL17, and NOTCH ligands, which contribute to localized immune defense but may also drive T-cell exhaustion over time. Parallel findings have implicated CD47 in similar immunosuppressive pathways [144, 146]. Xu’s research also shed light on macrophage–T-cell interactions. Macrophages can secrete chemokines that attract CD8 + TRM cells, possibly supporting immune evasion by trapping them within non-cytolytic zones of the tumor. These findings collectively underscore the complexity of T-cell dynamics and point toward novel therapeutic avenues for optimizing T-cell–based therapies such as CAR-T in OC [109].

Cancer-Associated Fibroblasts (CAFs)

CAFs represent the predominant stromal component of the TME and arise mainly from mesenchymal origins, although transdifferentiation from epithelial or endothelial cells is also documented [147, 148]. CAFs actively contribute to immune suppression, tumor angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis by secreting growth-promoting molecules and recruiting immunosuppressive cell populations [148, 149]. Their metabolic alterations can reshape the TME’s biochemical environment, influencing immune and tumor cell interactions. Accordingly, elevated CAF presence is increasingly considered a negative prognostic indicator in OC [150, 151]. High-resolution single-cell analyses have enabled the subclassification of CAFs, revealing diverse phenotypic states. Siel Olbrecht’s research classified stromal cells in serous ovarian and fallopian tube cancers into mesothelial (FB_CALB2), myofibroblastic (FB_MYH11), and TGF-β-driven CAFs (FB_COMP) [148]. The mesothelial subtype was noted for pro-fibrotic gene expression, while the latter two secreted IL-6, a cytokine promoting tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, migration, and chemoresistance, factors associated with poor prognosis [152]. Similarly, Tongtong Kan’s work linked high expression of RGS5 in specific CAF subsets to distant metastasis and recurrence in OC [153]. Further studies by researcher identified CAF-specific gene signatures associated with inflammatory and hypoxic signaling pathways, including NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin, which influence drug responsiveness [154].

Spatial transcriptomics has proven instrumental in understanding CAF roles within OC tissues. Although the cell types were not fully annotated, their proximity and functional association suggest a role in tumor aggressiveness [155]. Another study demonstrated that the absence of certain CAF subtypes correlates with longer survival in advanced HGSOC. CAFs linked to poor prognosis were found to form physical barriers that impede immune cell infiltration, thereby limiting anti-tumor immunity. In contrast, CAF clusters associated with better outcomes lacked classic markers like FAP and PDGFR, indicating potential therapeutic targets within CAF heterogeneity [156, 157].

Dendritic Cells (DCs)

Dendritic cells (DCs), central to antigen presentation and immune activation, play dual roles in the OC TME. While mature DCs can trigger effective T cell–mediated anti-tumor responses, tumor-derived signals often subvert these cells to promote immune evasion [158]. The OC microenvironment is rich in immunomodulatory cytokines such as TGF-β, IL-10, and CXCL-12. These attract plasmacytoid DC precursors which, upon infiltration, release IL-10, suppressing cytotoxic T-cell functions [159, 160]. Additionally, bone marrow–derived DCs in OC have been observed to overexpress immunosuppressive molecules like PD-L1 and Fgl2 [161]. This contributes to increased Treg populations and reduced T cell proliferation, reinforcing the immune-tolerant state of the TME. Similar immunosuppressive DC populations are also found in ascitic fluid from OC patients with peritoneal metastases, typically exhibiting immature plasmacytoid characteristics and promoting angiogenesis. These are broadly classified as tumor-associated dendritic cells (tDCs) [162, 163].

However, more recent findings by Tang-Huau have identified a subset of mo-DCs within the ascites. These mo-DCs are capable of initiating cytotoxic CD8 + T cell differentiation, highlighting their potential as pro-immunogenic agents [164]. Enhancing the function of these DCs, especially their antigen cross-presentation capabilities, could serve as a valuable direction in OC immunotherapy [46, 159] (Table 6).

Pericytes

Pericytes are contractile cells placed along the walls of capillaries and venules, playing a critical role in vascular stability and angiogenesis. In OV cancer, pericytes are involved in regulating blood flow and maintaining the integrity of the endothelium [165, 166]. They also communicate with tumor cells and stimulus the infiltration of immune cells, impacting tumor development. Studies have revealed that pericytes can secrete chemokines that recruit endothelial cells, thereby promoting tumor-associated angiogenesis [165, 166]. Their role in the TME also comprises the modulation of immune responses, where they can influence the polarization of macrophages, directing them towards a more pro-tumoral phenotype.

Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs)

MSCs contribute to the TME by providing a pool of cells that can differentiate into several stromal components, as well as, fibroblasts and adipocytes. In ovarian cancer, MSCs have been involved in promoting tumor growth and metastasis through the secretion of growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular matrix proteins [90]. They can also enable a pro-inflammatory environment that supports tumor progression by employing and activating immune cells [167]. Also, MSCs can undergo significant variations in response to the local microenvironment, adjusting their functions to promote tumor survival.

Adipocytes

Adipocytes, or fat cells, have expanded recognition for their role in the TME, mainly in obesity-related ovarian cancer. They secrete a variety of adipokines that can influence tumor behavior, promote inflammation, and enhance angiogenesis [81]. The connections between adipocytes and tumor cells can create a supportive niche for cancer development by altering metabolic pathways and increasing the availability of nutrients. Moreover, the inflammatory cytokines released by adipocytes can affect the immune landscape, contributing to a tumor-promoting environment [81] (Table 6).

Future clinical directions

The therapeutic efficacy of current immunotherapy is significantly hindered by the immunosuppressive characteristics of TME, particularly in ovarian cancer (OC). This immunosuppression largely stems from the presence of Tregs, TAMs, CAFs, and the functional decline or exhaustion of cytotoxic T cells. Therefore, a key strategy in advancing immunotherapy involves suppressing these inhibitory cells to enhance the activity of immune effector cells. Single-cell sequencing has emerged as a pivotal technology in addressing these challenges, offering high-resolution insights into the cellular diversity within the TME. This technique has enabled the identification of therapeutic opportunities by revealing distinct immune cell populations and their interactions with stromal components. For example, interventions targeting TAMs have focused on blocking their recruitment, promoting their depletion, or reprogramming them toward an immune-activating phenotype. Similarly, the development of dendritic cell (DC)-based vaccines has gained momentum, as single-cell data allow for personalized modification of DCs to elicit targeted anti-tumor immunity.

Beyond therapeutic innovation, single-cell analysis has transformed prognostic assessment in oncology. Traditional approaches based on bulk RNA sequencing are being complemented or replaced by cellular diversity indices derived from single-cell data, such as ecosystem diversity or Shannon diversity indices [174]. These tools have proven valuable in stratifying patient risk and are now being extended to OC, supporting a more nuanced classification beyond traditional immune subtypes [175]. Furthermore, the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process and specific prognostic markers, including those related to the NOTCH1 pathway, have been linked to clinical outcomes through these refined analytic methods. Looking forward, the integration of single-cell sequencing with artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) offers a powerful direction for personalized oncology. These computational techniques can enhance the interpretation of complex cellular datasets, enabling more accurate treatment planning and facilitating the design of novel cell-based therapies. As these platforms mature, they are expected to significantly influence both the development of curative treatments and early intervention strategies in OC.

Conclusion

The conceptual framework of cancer biology has evolved dramatically over the past decades, shifting from a focus solely on genetic abnormalities in tumor cells to a broader appreciation of the immune landscape. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is now recognized as a dynamic ecosystem comprising diverse cellular components, including T cells, macrophages, B cells, NK cells, fibroblasts, and dendritic cells. These cells interact in complex ways that influence disease progression and response to therapy.

Traditional research tools have been limited in capturing the full complexity of the TME, but single-cell sequencing has enabled unprecedented granularity in profiling cellular phenotypes and functions. This review highlighted the current understanding of OC’s TME based on single-cell studies, emphasizing stromal cells and their roles in immunoregulation and tumor support. Despite its promise, the application of single-cell sequencing in OC research remains limited. Certain immune cell types, such as neutrophils, are underrepresented in existing datasets, partly due to technical challenges like low RNA integrity and cell viability during preparation. The financial burden of single-cell methods compared to alternatives like spatial transcriptomics also restricts broader adoption.

Moreover, current single-cell analysis workflows require significant advancement. Integrative multi-omics approaches and algorithmic innovation are needed to fully harness the potential of these data. Techniques such as scATAC-Seq, CITE-Seq, and ASAP-Seq offer deeper insight into chromatin accessibility, protein expression, and antigen specificity, respectively. When combined with AI, especially ML and deep learning, these platforms can uncover previously inaccessible biological patterns and support precision oncology. Finally, single-cell sequencing is a transformative tool in OC research. It enables detailed mapping of the TME, facilitates the discovery of new therapeutic targets, and enhances predictive modeling for patient outcomes. As this field evolves, its integration into clinical research will be essential for achieving personalized and effective treatments for ovarian cancer.
