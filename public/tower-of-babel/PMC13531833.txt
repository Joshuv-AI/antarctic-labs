Alcaligenes faecalis BUMCn01: a new bacterial isolate larvicide against filarial vector Culex quinquefasciatus
Authors: Nandita Chowdhury, Indranil Bhattacharjee, Goutam Chandra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMosquito-borne diseases continue to pose major public health challenges, necessitating the development of environmentally safe biological control agents. Although several bacterial larvicides are currently used, novel gut-associated bacteria with high specificity and low non-target toxicity remain under-explored. This study (This is the first report demonstrating mosquito larvicidal activity of Alcaligenes faecalis against Culex quinquefasciatus larvae.) reports, for the first time, the mosquito larvicidal potential of Alcaligenes faecalis against Culex quinquefasciatus larvae.MethodsA bacterial (This is the first report demonstrating mosquito larvicidal activity of Alcaligenes faecalisagainst Culex quinquefasciatus larvae.) isolate, obtained from the midgut of naturally dead and moribund Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae, was identified as Alcaligenes faecalis strain BUMCn01 (GenBank accession: PX526002) based on morphological, biochemical, physiological, and 16S rRNA gene analyses. Larvicidal bioassays using A. faecalis strain BUMCn01 formulation were performed against all four larval instars. Bacterial localization and aggregation in the larval midgut following exposure to A. faecalis strain BUMCn01 were examined by scanning electron microscopy. Field efficacy was evaluated in sewage drains of different depths. Laboratory evaluations of the A. faecalis strain BUMCn01 formulation was also conducted on non-target aquatic organisms (Chironomus circumdatus larvae, Daphnia spp., Poecilia reticulata, and tadpoles of Bufo spp.). Furthermore, bacterial proteins were isolated and assessed for larvicidal efficacy against 3rd instar larvae, to identify the bacterial component responsible for the bioactivity.ResultsThe isolate was identified as Alcaligenes faecalis strain BUMCn01 using a polyphasic approach including morphological, biochemical, physiological and 16S rRNA gene sequence analysis (GenBank accession: PX526002).The Alcaligenes faecalis BUMCn01 formulation resulted in clear dose–response relationship. 100% mortality was recorded in 1st instar larvae after 24 h and in 2nd and 3rd instars Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae after 48–72 h at 2.0 mg/mL. 24 h LC₅₀ values ranged from 395 µg/mL to 490 µg/mL across all larval instars, with 1st instar larvae being the most susceptible. Scanning electron microscopy revealed bacterial proliferation and dense aggregation witin the treated larval midgut, comparison with control indicating oral ingestion and possible gut mediated toxicity. Field trials demonstrated complete larval elimination within 7–9 days at an application rate of 100 ml/m2, with moderate residual activity. In non-target aquatic organisms, the A. faecalis BUMCn01 formulation exhibited no mortality in tadpoles of Bufo spp. and only mild effects on C. circumdatus, Daphnia spp. and guppy (P. reticulata). The isolated proteins were found to possess potent larvicidal activity (24 h LC50 of 491 µg/mL) against 3rd instar larvae.ConclusionsThis study provides the first evidence that (The isolate was identified as Alcaligenes faecalis strain BUMCn01 using a polyphasic approach including morphological, biochemical, physiological, and 16S rRNA gene sequence analysis (GenBank accession: PX526002).) Alcaligenes faecalis strain BUMCn01 exhibits significant larvicidal activity against Culex quinquefasciatus. Bacterial proteins appear to be the primary toxic agent. The Alcaligenes faecalis BUMCn01 formulation showed significant efficacy in laboratory and field trials with minimal non-target toxicity, supporting its potential use as a microbial larvicide in integrated mosquito management.

Introduction

Mosquitoes not only cause nuisance through their bites but also act as vectors for several life-threatening diseases, such as malaria, filariasis, yellow fever, dengue, and Japanese encephalitis, thereby contributing significantly to poverty and social debility in tropical countries [37]. Lymphatic filariasis (LF) is a major vector-borne parasitic disease caused by three lymphatic nematodes: Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, and Brugia timori. Infection with these parasites leads to severe chronic manifestations, particularly in the lower limbs (elephantiasis) and the male genitalia (hydrocele) [46, 47]. Globally, about 1.3 billion people are at risk of LF, and approximately 120 million individuals across 83 countries are affected [50]. Of these, nearly 45.5 million people live in the Indian subcontinent and approximately 40 million in sub-Saharan Africa [47]. In India, surveys indicate that 22 states and union territories are endemic for filariasis; nine of these, including the present study area of West Bengal, account for nearly 95% of the national filarial burden [2, 36]. Transmission of W. bancrofti in India occurs primarily by the ubiquitous mosquito Culex quinquefasciatus Say, 1823 [10]. In rural regions of West Bengal, favorable climatic conditions combined with water logging after rainfall further enhance mosquito breeding, increasing vulnerability to filarial transmission. Since mosquito larvae remain confined to aquatic habitats, control at the larval stage is comparatively easier and more efficient. Culex quinquefasciatus breeds extensively in polluted water bodies,and therefore, anti larval operations targeting this species have traditionally received primary emphasis in urban vector control programs [48, 43]. In recent years, increasing concern over the ecological and health impacts of chemical insecticides has led to growing interest in safer and more effective biological control agents, among which mosquitocidal bacteria represent an eco-friendly alternative [15, 13, 30]. A biopesticide should be easy to apply, low-cost, rapid in action, exhibit minimal toxicity toward non-target organisms, and effectively control pests prior to reaching damaging levels. Although no single agent fulfills all these criteria, several Bacillus strains have been developed as microbial pesticides that closely approximate these attributes [35]. Notable examples of mosquitocidal bacteria include Bacillus thuringiensis, B. sphaericus brevi, B. laterosporus and Clostridium bifermentans, all of which have demonstrated potent larvicidal activity [13, 28, 30, 41]. Luxananil et al. [21] reported the presence of Bacillus spp. in the gut of mosquito larvae of Aedes aegypti and Cx. quinquefasciatus, collected from natural breeding habitats. Their findings suggests that mosquito gut microbiota may serve as an important source of novel biocontrol agents. Understanding the diversity of natural mosquito microbial pathogens is essential for developing improved vector control strategies [40].

Some studies have documented pathogenic effects of Alcaligenes spp. on insect hosts. Osborn et al. [29] reported approximately 10% mortality in Hylesia metabus larvae treated with Alcaligenes faecalis under laboratory conditions. Alcaligenes faecalis strain MOR02 demonstrated pathogenicity to Galleria mellonella larvae [34]. However, despite these observations, A. faecalis has not previously been reported to possess larvicidal activity against mosquito larvae.

The objective of the present investigation was to isolate a bacterial strain (subsequently identified as Alcaligenes faecalis BUMCn01) from the gut of naturally dead and moribund Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae and to evaluate its larvicidal efficacy against this principal vector of filariasis under both laboratory and field conditions. Bacterial proteins were isolated from the bacterial culture supernatant to determine the toxic compounds implicated in larval mortality. In addition, the effects of the strain on non-target aquatic organisms (Daphnia, Chironomus circumdatus, Poecilia reticulata, and tadpoles of Bufospp.) were also assessed.

Methodology

Collection of 1larvae

Culex quinquefasciatus larvae (early: 1 st and 2nd instars, and late: 3rd and 4th instars) were reared in the Mosquito, Microbiology and Nanotechnology Research Unit, Parasitology Laboratory, Department of Zoology, University of Burdwan, Golapbagh, West Bengal, India, following the standard WHO protocol [43]. Rearing was carried out under controlled laboratory conditions at 27 ± 1 °C, 85% relative humidity (RH), and a 14L:10D photoperiod.

Isolation of bacteria

Dead and moribund Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae (late instars) were collected from a rainwater pit at a construction site in Burdwan, West Bengal, India. Larvae were surface-sterilized using 70% ethanol and rinsed with sterile distilled water. Gut contents from the moribund and dead larvae were extracted separately in 5 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) to make four gut suspensions (two moribund and two dead, n = 5 larvae per suspension). Each suspension was filtered twice through two layers of sterile cheesecloth to remove debris particles [32]. The pooled filtrates were combined and then serially diluted following standard procedures [8],0.1 ml sample from10−8 dilution was inoculated onto nutrient agar plate (composition per litre: peptone 5 g, beef extract 3 g, agar 15 g; pH 7) by spread plate technique. Plates were incubated at 37 ± 10 C for 24 h. Following overnight incubation, bacterial colonies were counted and isolated based on their morphology, pigmentation, and distinct colony types. Subsequent repeat cultures were then obtained through pure cultures using streaking method. Pure culture containing mosquito-pathogenic bacteria were isolated and maintained by regular subculturing.

Phenotypic characterization of isolated bacteria

2Morphological, physiological, and biochemical characteristics of the isolated mosquito-pathogenic bacteria were studied following standard procedures [9, 18, 31]. Antibiotic sensitivity of the bacterial isolate was assessed using the standard disc diffusion method [3, 27]. The assay was performed on Mueller–Hinton agar (BD) using high-potency antibiotic discs procured from HiMedia (Mumbai, India). After lawn culture preparation, antibiotic discs were placed aseptically on the agar surface and plates were incubated at room temperature for 24 h. Following incubation, the diameters of the inhibition zones around each disc were measured to the nearest millimeter using an antibiotic zone scale and zone sizes were interpreted as indicators of the susceptibility of the isolate to each antibiotic.

16S rDNA sequence determination

Species level identification of the selected bacterial isolate was confirmed using 16S rDNA sequencing. 3Genomic DNA of the bacterial isolate was extracted using the Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (GeNei™, Elpho Kit, Category #107070, Bengaluru, India), following the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR amplification of the 16S rDNA region was performed by conventional method [20] using a GeneAmp 2400 thermal cycler (Perkin Elmer, Chicago, IL, USA). A 100 μL PCR reaction mixture was prepared containing: PCR buffer, 0.2 mM deoxynucleoside triphosphate (dNTP), 1.0 mM MgCl₂, 20 pmol of bacterial primers: 8 F (5′-AGA GTT TGA TCC TGG CTC AG-3′) 1492R (5′-GGT TAC CTT GTT ACG ACT T-3′) [20]. The mixture was used to denature 0.1 μL of fresh bacterial genomic DNA at 95 °C for 4 min, after which 1 unit of Taq DNA polymerase was added to the reaction. PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 30 cycles of Denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s Annealing at 48 °C for 30 s and further extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The Final cycle was extended at 72 °C for 2 min.

PCR products were purified using the GeneiPure™ Quick PCR Purification Kit (Category #117309, Bengaluru, India). The purified amplicons were sequenced using an ABI 3100 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) at Bangalore Genei, Bengaluru, India. Species identification was confirmed by BLAST analysis of the 16S rDNA sequence.

Preparation of A. faecalis strain BUMCn01 formulation

The isolated strain was inoculated into glucose peptone salt (GPS) medium containing glucose (10.0 g/L), peptone (10.0 g/L), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (0.1 M), and peanut oil (1.0 mL/L), adjusted to pH 7. The inoculated medium was incubated on a rotary shaker at 37 °C and 180 rpm for 48 h for laboratory bioassay preparation. After incubation, the culture density was adjusted to OD₅₄₀ = 1.09, corresponding to a cell concentration of approximately 6 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. Colony forming units per milliliter were calculated using the formula: CFU (x) = (Number of colonies × Dilution factor) × 1/Volume plated.

The bacterial cell mass was harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, the resulting supernatant containing residual growth medium components was discarded. Collected cell pellet was lyophilized, and stored at –80 °C until further use.

The Alcaligenes faecalis BUMCn01 formulation was prepared by suspending 10 g of lyophilized bacterial cell mass in sterile distilled water containing 5% (v/v) glycerine, and the final volume was adjusted to 100 mL to obtain a concentration of 100 g/L, following the method of Prabakaran et al. (2003). The term “A. faecalis BUMCn01 formulation” refers to the lyophilized bacterial cell mass mixed with glycerine and sterile water for bioassay.

Bioassay (pathogenicity)

All larvicidal bioassays were performed following the standard WHO test protocols (WHO, 2005) with slight modifications. Mortality was recorded after 24, 48, and 72 h of continuous exposure, as this observation period is sufficient to capture both immediate and delayed toxic effects under controlled laboratory conditions, in accordance with WHO guidelines for mosquito larvicidal bioassays (WHO, 2005), consistent with earlier standardized larval susceptibility protocols [44, 45]. The tests were conducted at room temperature (27–30 °C). The A. faecalis strain BUMCn01 formulation was added to 500 mL glass beakers to obtain four test concentrations: 2.0, 1.5, 0.5, and 0.3 mg/mL. The final volume in each beaker was adjusted to 250 mL with chlorine free water. For each concentration, 50 laboratory reared healthy larvae from early stages of each instar (1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th instar) were introduced separately. Chlorine free water (without test solution) containing 25% glycerol served as the control. Each treatment and control group was tested in triplicate. Larval mortality was recorded after 24, 48, and 72 h of continuous exposure. Mortality percentages were corrected using Abbott’s formula [1]. Molting was observed. The mean percent mortality for each concentration was calculated from the three replicates.

Biostatistics

Lethal concentration values (LC₅₀ and LC₉₀) along with their lower confidence limit (LCL), upper confidence limit (UCL), and slope values were estimated following Finney’s probit analysis method [14]. A three-way factorial ANOVA was performed to assess the main and interaction effects of larval instars, dose and exposure time on mortality, with percent larval mortality (%) was treated as the dependent variable and larval and instar, dose and exposure time as independent variables (fixed factor).

Larval mortality data were analyzed using two-way factorial ANOVA to evaluate the effects of dose and exposure time, as well as their interaction effect. To specifically evaluate differences between treated groups and the control, mortality data at each exposure time were further analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s HSD test where appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses of variance (one-way, two-way, and three-way factorial ANOVA) and post hoc comparisons were conducted following the biostatistical procedures described by Zar [51]. Student’s t-test was performed to compare mortality in non-target organisms with their corresponding control groups. Statistical significance was determined at a level of p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 11.0.

Sample preparation for scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to examine the midgut of dead Culex quinquefasciatus larvae to evaluate ingestion and to assess bacterial localization and accumulation within the gut following exposure to A. faecalis strain BUMCn01. Freshly dead Culex quinquefasciatus larvae were collected from the test solution containing the A. faecalis BUMCn01 formulation and and healthy larvae were collected from the untreated control group. The larval gut were then dissected out under a binocular stereomicroscope using sterile needles and fine forceps. Each dissected gut was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h. After fixation, the gut was torn open to expose the inner gut wall. The tissue was then subjected to dehydration through graded alcohols: 50% alcohol (5 min), 70% alcohol (30 min, two changes), 90% alcohol (30 min, two changes), and absolute alcohol (30 min, two changes). The samples were subsequently transferred sequentially into three different ratios of absolute alcohol and amyl acetate with three changes: 3:1 (30 min), 2:2 (30 min), and 1:3 (30 min). Finally, the tissues were subjected to critical point drying (CPD) to preserve ultrastructural integrity before SEM observation. A corresponding untreated (normal) mosquito larva was processed in parallel for SEM analysis.

4Isolated proteins for toxicity assay

Proteins present in 400 mL of culture from A. faecalis BUMCn01 were precipitated using cold acetone (4:1) by precipitation method [11] and incubated at − 20 °C overnight. Following incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and the resulting proteins pellets were collected. The pellets were then washed twice with cold 5acetone (1.5–2 ml each tube) to remove residual impurities. The pellets were then allowed to air dry until all residual acetone had evaporated. Dried proteins were then used for bioassay to evaluate the toxicity of the extracted proteins. It was mixed with distilled water to make volume of 100 ml to obtain four test concentrations (2 µg/µL, 1.5 µg/µL, 0.5 µg/µL and 0.3 µg/µL) and were added to 250 mL size glass beakers. Late instars are physiologically stable and actively feeding, therefore, 3rd instar larvae were selected to obtain standardized toxicity estimates while avoiding interference from pupation. Each dose was bioassayed against laboratory reared 3rd instar larvae following the standard WHO test protocols [44, 45] with slight modifications. Fifteen larvae were introduced separately into the beakers. Sterile distilled water (without test solution) containing 25% glycerol served as control. Experiments were conducted at room temperature (27–30 °C) using three independent biological replicates. Mortality rates were recorded after 24 h, 48 h and 72 h ofcontinuous exposures (Fig. 4). Mortality percentages were corrected using Abbott’s formula [1]. Mean percent mortality values were calculated for each concentration.

Effect of A. faecalis BUMCn01 formulation on 6non-target aquatic organisms

To evaluate the potential ecological risks, four non-target aquatic organisms (Daphnia spp., Chironomus circumdatus, Poecilia reticulata and tadpoles of Bufo spp.) were selected for toxicity assessment. All organisms were collected from the water of the submerged field at the farm, The University of Burdwan, Golapbagh, Burdwan, West Bengal, India, and were acclimatized to laboratory conditions in containers simulating their natural habitat prior to experimentation.

Following the protocol of Suwannee et al. [39], non-target organisms were exposed to the sublethal dose, LC₅₀ (at 24 h for 4th instar Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae) of the A. faecalis BUMCn01 formulation. Separate untreated controls for each organisms were maintained in parallel. Mortality was recorded after 24, 48, and 72 h of continuous exposure, and percentage mortality was corrected using Abbott’s formula [1],all experiments, including controls, were performed in triplicate and the mean mortality values were recoded.

Field application

Field trials were conducted during late summer of 2024 in rural area of Purba Bardhaman District, West Bengal, India (24°28´ N, 86°56´ E). Three narrow, stagnant and shallow drains were selected for the field trial: Drain 1 (surface area ≈ 10 m2; water depth 35–40 cm), Drain 2 (surface area ≈ 6–7 m2; water depth 15–35 cm), and Drain 3 (surface area ≈ 3–4 m2; water depth 5–25 cm). Study sites naturally contained mixed instars of Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae of different larval densities.

For field application, A. faecalis BUMCn01 culture was incubated in nutrient broth at 37 ± 1 °C for 48 h, centrifuged, and the resulting pellet was lyophilized. A total of 0.9 g of lyophilized bacterial cell mass was suspended in sterile distilled water containing 5% (v/v) glycerine, and the final volume was adjusted to 100 mL (9,000 μg/mL). It was then applied one time using a 200 mL stainless steel manual sprayer over a 1 m2 water surface in natural breeding habitats (sewage drains) of Cx. quinquefasciatus. A control drain (surface area ≈ 8–10 m2, water depth 35–40 cm) was maintained untreated. In natural breeding sites, due to continuous egg hatching and environmental variability, the toxic experiment in field can last up to 7 days unlike laboratory experiments.

Following field application, all drains were monitored daily to assess changes in the mosquito larval density. Larval sampling was performed using a standard 250 mL mosquito dipper, with 10 dips per drain (three dips from each side of the drain and four dips from the center), from Day-1 to Day-17. Collected immatures were classified into two categories: early instars (1st and 2nd) and late instars (3rd and 4th) instars. All larvae were counted, identified to the genus level, classified by developmental stage, and then returned to their respective habitats. The percentage reduction in larval density was calculated using the formula of [24].

Results

Phenotypic identification of the isolated bacteria

The bacterial isolate obtained from naturally dead and moribund Culex quinquefasciatus larvae produced creamy-colored, non-spore-forming colonies. Microscopic examination revealed Gram-negative, motile, coccobacilli with an average cell size of approximately 1–2 µm × ~ 1 µm. The isolate exhibited optimal growth at 37 °C and pH 7.6. and tolerated NaCl concentrations up to 5–6%. The organism did not produce acid or gas from tested carbon sources.

In the present study, the bacterial isolate obtained from the midgut of Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae were initially identified phenotypically as Alcaligenes faecalis based on their morphological, physiological, and biochemical characteristics and subsequently the identification was confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

7Phylogenetic identification

A representative phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1) was constructed using 17 taxa reference 16S rRNA sequences belonging to the genus Alcaligenes (A. faecalis, A. aquatilis, Alcaligenes like environmental isolates) and Achromobacter xylosoxidans (outgroup). Alcaligenes faecalis BUMCn01 is in a tight clade with Alcaligenes faecalis strain L16 (KU315421). The node joining BUMCn01 + L16 has a bootstrap value ≈ 94, which is very strong support. This pair (BUMCn01 + L16) then clusters with other A. faecalis strains (e.g. KT371789, MH587485, LJ3, LJ4, etc.) with bootstrap values mostly > 70–90. So BUMCn01 clearly falls inside the A. faecalis cluster, not with the A. aquatilis group and far from the Achromobacter xylosoxidans outgroup. BUMCn01 formed a sub cluster with A. faecalis L16 (KU315421) (bootstrap 94%), confirming species level identification.

The isolate was therefore designated as Alcaligenes faecalis strain BUMCn01, and its 16S rRNA gene sequence has been deposited in GenBank under the accession number PX526002.

Larvicidal efficacy of A. faecalis BUMCn01 formulation

In the 72 h bioassay, the formulation demonstrated significant larvicidal activity against all four stages of Cx. quinquefasciatus. No morphological abnormalities, swelling, or visible tissue damage were observed; the larvae remained straight and appeared immobile. For 1 st instar larvae, 100% mortality was observed at the highest test concentration (2.0 mg/mL) within 24 h (Fig. 2a). No molting was observed at any of the test concentrations. In control, 5% molting and less than 5% mortality were observed across all of the exposure durations.

For 2nd instar larvae, 100% mortality was recorded at 2.0 mg/mL within 48 h of exposure (Fig. 2b). Corresponding mortality values after 24 h at lower concentrations (1.5, 0.5, and 0.3 mg/mL) were 79.71%, 65.22% and 40.58% respectively (Fig. 2b). No molting was observed in any of the test concentrations. 10% molting and less than 5% mortality were recorded in the control across all exposure durations.

For 3rd instar larvae, at 2.0 mg/mL, mortality reached at 91.06% within 24 h and increased to 100% mortality by 72 h. Lowest mortality was recorded at 0.3 mg/mL. In control, mortality remained negligible throughout the experimental period (≤ 2.49%) (Fig. 2c). No molting was recorded in any of the test concentrations, while 10% molting was recoded in the control.

For 4th instar larvae, 91.08% mortality was recoded after 24 h, at 2.0 mg/mL (Fig. 2d). Less than 5% larval mortality was recoded across all exposure durations in control. 4th instar larvae exhibited minimal pupation after 48 h of continuous exposure across all test concentrations. Pupation rates ranged from 2.86–5.71% after 48–72 h. No pupal death or adult emergence was recorded in any of the test concentrations. Whereas in control, pupation was recorded 20% after 48 h and 75% after 72 h of continuous exposure and 60% adult emergence recorded after 72 h of exposure.

Probit analysis (Table 2) of the bioassay data showed a progressive decrease in LC₅₀ and LC₉₀ values with increasing exposure time, indicating enhanced larvicidal activity of the A. faecalis BUMCn01 8formulation.1st instar was the most susceptible, with 24 h LC₅₀ of 0.395 mg/mL (Table 2). Discussion

The global search for environmentally safe and effective biological control agents has intensified in recent years due to growing concerns over the ecological and health impacts of the chemical insecticides. In this context, the present study examined gut-associated bacteria of Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae and identified the isolate as A. faecalis based on morphological, physiological, and biochemical characteristics, which were confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. While previous studies reported limited larval mortality in non-mosquito species [29, 34], this study provides the first evidence of the larvicidal efficacy of A. faecalis against Cx. quinquefasciatus.

Gram-negative bacteria have been reported to cause mortality in mosquito larvae as well as other insect groups [22]. Toxic metabolites from Pseudomonas spp. have been widely reported as larvicidal agents against mosquitoes and certain lepidopteran pests [16, 23, 26]. The dose-dependent mortality rates observed in the present study, significantly exceeding the control mortality, provide evidence for the efficacy of the bacteria. However, consistent with earlier reports on Gram-negative bacteria P. fluorescens against Cx. quinquefasciatus [33, 42], our results also indicate that Gram-negative bacteria are capable of inducing significant mortality in mosquito larvae. Some toxins, such as those from P. aeruginosa, are capable of penetrating the insect cuticle and acting directly on haemolymph proteins [17]. Metabolites of P. fluorescens have also demonstrated strong activity against larvae and pupae of mosquito vectors [33]. Larval ingestion related toxicity has also been well documented for bacterial larvicides like Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis, which exerts toxicity through the larval midgut [7, 19]. In the present study, there was no obvious morphological abnormalities, swelling, or visible tissue damage were observed in the dead larvae may suggesting that toxicity was unlikely to result from external tissue destruction. Interestingly, no pupal mortality was observed in the present study, suggesting that the toxin is unlikely to be efficiently involve respiratory toxicity. As pupae are non-feeding developmental stages and therefore have limited exposure to ingested toxicants compared to actively feeding larvae, likely consistent with 12oral ingestion being the primary route of action. The SEM observations further support this interpretation. While the untreated control exhibited only sparse indigenous bacteria [12], the treated group was characterized by significant bacterial proliferation. This indicates that the A. faecalis BUMCn01 formulation was orally ingested by Cx. quinquefasciatus larva and effectively bypasses early digestive barriers leading to a dense population within the midgut. Consistent with previous studies on bacterial larvicide, Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis [7, 19], the findings of the present study may suggests a possible gut-mediated toxicity following oral ingestion of A. Faecalis strain BUMCn01. However, the precise mechanism within the gut underlying larval death requires further investigation.

Field trials demonstrated larvicidal efficacy of the A. faecalis formulation at 100 mL/m2. Complete larval reduction (100%) was observed in shallow drains (Day 7–9) than in deeper drain (Day 9–11). Larval recurrence was observed earlier in deeper sewage drains (Day 11) than in shallower drains (Day 13). The variation in the timing of larval reduction and recurrence between different field sites suggests that while Alcaligenes faecalis BUMCn01 is significantly effective initially, its persistence under field conditions may be influenced by environmental factors, as documented in earlier studies [5, 49, 19]. Becker et al. [4] previously demonstrated that certain strains of B. sphaericus can sporulate and possess strong recycling potential within the larval gut, contributing to sustained reduction after a single application. In contrast, the present study found that A. faecalis BUMCn01 exhibited low residual larvicidal activity, implying limited recycling potential inside the larval gut. This reduced persistence is likely due to the non-spore-forming nature of A. faecalis, which limits its 13environmental stability and reduces its ability to recycle, consistent with the findings by Subratty et al., [38]. Another notable field observation was that initial larval density also influenced treatment efficacy, with habitats harboring higher larval densities showing slightly reduced larval reduction. This may be attributed to reduced toxin intake per larva when population density is high, leading to suboptimal exposure at the individual level, as reported in earlier studies of density-dependent efficacy of Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis [6, 25]. Overall, field evaluations demonstrated that a low application dose (100 mL/m2) applied at 10-day intervals demonstrated significant larvicidal efficacy, resulting in > 90% reduction of Culex larval densities across drains of varying depth.

A. faecalis exhibited no observable toxicity in tadpoles and only negligible effect on other non-target aquatic organisms, even after 72 h of exposure, compared with the untreated control. Only minimal toxicity was observed in C. circumdatus larvae after 24 h, suggesting that A. faecalis strain BUMCn01 is safe for non-target aquatic organisms.

The bioactivity of A. faecalis BUMCn01 observed in this study was associated with the isolated bacterial proteins, suggesting that protein components are likely responsible for the larvicidal activity. However, the isolated bacteria exhibited a lower 24 h LC₅₀ value (430 µg/mL) than the bacterial proteins (491 µg/mL) against third instar larvae. This result indicating that while protein components play a major role, the enhanced efficacy of the isolated bacteria may be attributed to synergistic interactions with other metabolites of A. faecalis strain BUMCn01, consistent with previous reports on synergistic effects in microbial bioactive systems [7, 19].

Further studies focusing on the molecular characterization of the toxic protein/proteins and elucidation of their detail mode of action inside the gut would be necessary for the development of A. faecalis BUMCn01 as a larvicide. In addition, pending field-based evaluations of its effects on non-target organisms would further strengthen and validate to use its potential as a environment friendly biopesticide.

14Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first report demonstrating the larvicidal activity of A. faecalis strain BUMCn01 against Cx. quinquefasciatus under both laboratory and field conditions. The findings indicate that bacterial protein metabolites contribute to its efficacy, with minimal impact on non-target organisms, also supporting a favorable environmental safety profile. Although reduced residual persistence was observed under field conditions, the low effective dose and environmental compatibility support its potential as a biocontrol agent. Despite these limitations, our findings provide strong preliminary evidence supporting the larvicidal potential of A. faecalis BUMCn01.
