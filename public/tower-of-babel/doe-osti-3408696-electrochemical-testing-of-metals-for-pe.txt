Electrochemical testing of metals for petroleum
wastewater monitoring
G. M. Carson, E. L. Kendall

Abstract

The purpose of this project was to determine the feasibility of ion-selective electrode (ISE) as
compared to x-ray ﬂuorescence (XRF) for determining the concentrations of metal ions in
petroleum wastewater. ISEs for silver and cadmium were tested in a laboratory under
increasingly realistic conditions, then deployed in the sewer wastewater monitoring complex to
simulate a petroleum wastewater system. By verifying the ISE calibration after wastewater
exposure and comparing its measurements to the XRF systems within the wastewater
monitoring system, we determined that the ISE is more sensitive but less feasible for use in live
wastewater monitoring due to fouling concerns.

Introduction
Background

For every ton of crude oil produced, up to 5m3 of wastewater are generated [1]. The type and
concentration of contaminants can vary signiﬁcantly based on the age and location of the well,
depth of the formation being tapped into, and the reﬁning method/time, which make it diﬃcult
to treat and reuse the water [2] [3]. In the event that produced wastewater is accidentally or
intentionally released into the environment, heavy metals can begin to bioaccumulate and
reach humans through the food chain, increasing the risk of cancers and other health issues [3]
[4]. Figure 1 shows some of the most common and dangerous metals accumulating in an
aquatic environment, with the resulting human health impacts.

This large supply of wastewater could instead become a valuable resource, given the correct
treatment. However, the contaminants vary signiﬁcantly based on the age and location of the oil
well, depth of the formation being tapped into, and the production method and time for wastes
generated in the reﬁning stage [2] [3]. Some instances of petroleum wastewater contain
relatively small traces of metals with only a couple that are above the legal concentration limit
for certain re-use applications, such as irrigation for non-food crops or washing vehicles [3] [5].
Additionally, being able to identify hazardous metals for removal opens up the possibility for
safer re-injection as part of the enhanced oil recovery (EOR) process, and removing said metals
also reduces friction pressure losses, scaling, and build-up, which increases eﬃciency [5-7].
Water is also required for the drilling and reﬁning stages of crude oil processing, and life cycle
analyses have indicated that higher percentages of water reuse compared to fresh water use
can provide cost beneﬁts for production [7]. Quick and eﬀective testing and treatment of reused
water to meet the regulatory standards for reinjection can also create opportunities for drilling
in areas that lack large supplies of fresh water [7]. Because of the high variance in contaminants,
fast in-line measurements would be beneﬁcial for identifying metals. X-ray ﬂuorescence (XRF) is

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                                     3

a common choice in general wastewater testing [8] [9], and Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory uses three within its sewer monitoring complex to detect heavy metals that are also
present in petroleum wastewater. However, the system has its shortcomings, prompting us to
investigate if an ion-selective electrode (ISE), an electrochemical testing method, can perform
better than the XRF. This project is intended as a feasibility study for the use of an ISE
compared to XRF testing to detect metal ion concentrations in a ﬂowing wastewater system.
The sewer wastewater monitoring complex was selected as a simulated wastewater loop
because its wide range of contaminants—including signiﬁcant amounts of organic waste,
measurable amounts of metals, and other solids content—resembles the expected conditions of
produced wastewater. The system is also closely monitored for variations in factors such as pH
and metal concentrations, with an extensive log of data that can be used as a comparison for
the ISE measurements. The wastewater monitoring system currently uses XRF to detect silver
(Ag) and cadmium (Cd) concentrations in ﬂowing water. There are three XRFs in total, which are
necessary to detect more ions because the characteristic x-ray peaks of some ions overlap with
others. XRF systems have previously been used for contaminated wastewater testing, but these
often require removing samples from the wastewater stream or ﬁltering the water and
measuring the remaining particles [8] [9]. The XRFs at the sewer wastewater monitoring
complex can take live in-line measurements by sending x-rays through a thin ﬁlm ﬂow cell,
which will be discussed in more detail later. Importantly, this system requires frequent
maintenance to prevent fouling, and its imperfect measurement conditions combined with its
noisy calibration data make it diﬃcult to reliably measure low (but still potentially harmful)
concentrations of metals.

Ion-selective electrodes are a promising alternative because of their faster measurement time
and wide detection range, extending from 10-7 M to 0.1 M [10] [11]. While this would
theoretically allow them to detect the low metal concentrations that the XRF cannot, this
speciﬁcity is achieved through a very controlled lab environment under the following
conditions:

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                                 4

 1. Ionic strength adjuster (ISA) solution must be added to calibration standards and
 samples at a 1:50 ratio

 2. Samples should be stationary, with several minutes to equilibrate

 [11]

None of these conditions are achievable while taking in-line measurements of a ﬂowing
petroleum wastewater stream. Thus, the limits of the ISEs needed to be tested within a lab
environment before the electrodes could be deployed in the wastewater monitoring complex.

Methods
This project used the Orion Star A214 Benchtop pH/ISE meter to calibrate and collect
measurements from two ISE probes: the Orion Combination Silver/Sulﬁde Electrode and Orion
Combination Cadmium Electrode. Data was recorded and exported from Orion Star Com
software.

Using deionized (DI) water and 1000ppm metal solutions, three calibration standards were
created for each metal via serial dilution with electronic pipettes. 0.200ppm, 2.00ppm, and
20.0ppm were selected for these standards to encompass the expected concentrations within
the sewer wastewater stream. Serial dilution was also used to create 0.5ppm and 5ppm
solutions for each metal, which would be used to check the accuracy of the calibrations. Each
solution was 25mL total, of which 10mL were set aside and mixed with 0.2mL of ionic-strength
adjuster (ISA) solution, which increases the background ionic strength to provide a stable
baseline for measuring varying concentrations of the target ion [10]. Stir plates with magnetic
stir bars were used to help simulate ﬂowing water. This laboratory setup is shown in Figure 2.

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                                 5

 Figure 2. Laboratory setup of ISE in cadmium standard.

Each calibration measurement was taken with at least 5 minutes to reach a steady value.
Laboratory measurements were taken every 5 seconds, and readings that occurred while
cleaning the electrode or within the ﬁrst seconds of switching to a new solution were omitted as
they deviated signiﬁcantly from the remaining readings. Because the intended use for the ISEs is
to be constantly submerged in a ﬂowing wastewater stream, these omitted measurements
would not occur in real-world use and their removal from the data analysis helps the laboratory
tests more accurately reﬂect these conditions.

Results
ISE with vs. without ISA solution in sample and standard

According to the instruction manuals, “ISA must be added to all standards and samples” [11]. I
would either need to design a system that could constantly add ISA to the wastewater ﬂow at a
ﬁxed rate, which would not only be putting trace chemicals in the sewers but would also quickly
get expensive with 24/7 measurements, or ﬁnd a way to take measurements without it.
However, the ISEs are known for being extremely sensitive and can detect concentrations as low
as 0.01ppm of silver or cadmium, which is far below the requirements for this application.

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                                6

Therefore, even if the ISEs measurements were several times less precise without ISA, they
could still yield decent measurements for the concentrations I would be measuring.

For each metal, I calibrated the probes and took measurements of 0.5 and 5ppm solutions using
ISA in both the calibration and solutions as intended, then calibrating with ISA and measuring
solutions without ISA, and with no ISA at all. The concentrations of each are shown in Figure 3.

As I had expected, calibrating with ISA and measuring solutions that did not contain it led to the
least accurate results. However, using no ISA was fairly close to using ISA correctly, although
there was a higher percent error at lower concentrations. For the Ag tests, which I had been

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                                  7

advised tend to be inconsistent, the attempts using no ISA were the closest to the correct
concentration but also the least linear. This aligns with the information provided in the
instruction manual, which states that ISA is used to amplify the background signals and provide
a consistent baseline for the target ion’s concentration to be measured against [10] [11].

This was the ﬁrst sign of a trend I found throughout this project: the ISEs can function in much
poorer conditions than described in their manual, if the calibration also occurs in poor
conditions. Since our goal was to detect relatively high levels of contaminants in wastewater,
the readings without ISA were acceptable.

Extended testing in stirred DI water

The ISE calibration is supposed to be re-veriﬁed every 2 hours and not run for long periods of
time, so this was a way to test if it could give reasonable readings for hours/days and to what
extent it retained its calibration, or could still work and be re-calibrated. The DI water was also
stirred using a magnetic stir bar to simulate the moving water that the ISE would be submerged
in at the wastewater monitoring facility. Figure 4 shows the voltage measurements for the Cd
ISE undergoing this test.

 Figure 4. Voltage readings for Cd ISE in stirred DI water.

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                                     8

The cadmium concentration readings leveled out to 0.002ppm, which could have been caused
by cross-contamination following the calibration. However, the probe was thoroughly rinsed to
prevent this, so this may instead be a slight misreading of the 0ppm DI water. Following this
test, the 0.2 ppm and 2 ppm standards were measured as 0.1 ppm and 1ppm respectively, while
the 20 ppm standard was measured as exactly 20 ppm. This follows the ﬁndings from the ISA
tests, in which the higher concentration solutions had a lower percent error than the less
concentrated solutions.

Lab ﬁndings for ISE, responses to spikes/changing concentration

With both probes still functional following the DI water tests, the next step was to run a more
realistic test. The ISEs were each left in DI water for an extended period of time, then switched
to a 2ppm and a 5ppm metal solution for 15-20 minutes each, with several minutes to
equilibrate in DI water in between. The purpose of this experiment was to determine not only if
the probes could return to a steady baseline after having sensed a spike in metal concentration,
but if they could reach a steady and accurate measurement in the relatively short time that we
would expect to see these spikes within the wastewater stream. The results for silver and
cadmium are shown in ﬁgures 5 and 6, respectively.

The design process was fairly straightforward; I just needed an input and output that would
maintain the correct ﬂow rate while also leaving enough water to submerge the end of the
probe, which would be poking through a hole in the top and held in place by a washer, as shown
in Figure 7. We set everything up for a test during a workday, followed by a weekend test, and
the readings are shown in Figure 8. It is important to note that the concentration readings are
not expected to be accurate; the voltages are far below that of the 0.2 ppm calibration standard
because the measurements are in wastewater rather than DI water, so the meter is only
measuring 0 ppm silver. Factors such as the temperature, ﬂow rate, contaminants, and the
presence of other ions within the wastewater contribute to this shift in the voltage readings [10]
[11]. However, the voltages themselves could be useful, as a major deviation from the baseline
(such as the peak between 25-30 hours) would still indicate that the ionic content of the water
was changing. It was promising that the voltage did appear to be approaching an equilibrium,
and additional time may have allowed it to fully settle and provide this solid baseline.

As show in Figure 10, the cadmium wastewater curve has a tall peak similar to the silver test,
but its readings do not appear to plateau. While this may have been due to the fact that this
test was much shorter than the last, the new measurements for the calibration standards
indicate that something went wrong. The concentrations were not graphed because the
majority of them were displayed as “9999 ppm” error messages; additionally, the voltage
readings are all approximately 110 mV higher than their original calibrated values, with the
relative voltage diﬀerences between each solution remaining the same. The concentration
readings are expected to ﬂuctuate as the ISEs drift slightly out of calibration over time, but the

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                                15

voltages should not be aﬀected by calibration, indicating that another issue such as fouling may
have occurred. Further testing is required to determine what caused such a signiﬁcant voltage
shift and how it can be ﬁxed, if at all. If these issues could occur for any probe, especially within
only 24 hours, this would severely limit their feasibility for wastewater monitoring.

While these ﬁndings do indicate possible fouling, it is interesting that the performance is so
diﬀerent from the silver electrode, which was also in the sewers for a full workday but left over
the weekend as well. The amount of organic contaminants may have been higher for these tests
as more employees are in the lab in the middle of the week rather than on a Friday or weekend.

The other issue for the ISEs is that the sewer measurements contained several voltage spikes
that, if calibrated within the wastewater itself, could have indicated very large concentrations of
Ag or Cd. However, these readings were not corroborated by the XRF measurements during this
time period, so they are likely invalid. This may indicate that there are other ions that can
conﬂict with the readings; this is true for the bromide ISE from the same manufacturer, but no
interfering ions are listed in the instructions for either ISE that I tested. Further research would
be required to identify any conﬂicting ions or other factors that could have caused voltage
ﬂuctuations, as well as to determine the noise of the ISE measurements within the wastewater.

XRF Data

 Figure 11. XRF spectra for multiple metal concentrations. Within the box, the left peak
 represents silver and the right is cadmium.
When an x-ray hits an atom with suﬃcient energy, an electron from one of the inner orbitals is
ejected. An electron in a higher energy state drops down to take its place, and releases its

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                                  16

excess energy in the form of an x-ray that can then be detected by the XRF [12]. The amount of
x-rays that return are measured as “counts” on the y-axis, and a 5-minute “scan” of these values
is collected, resulting in a spectrum like the ones in Figure 11. Each atom has its own unique
combination of peaks; however, some peaks overlap with others, so multiple XRFs are needed
to detect a wider array of metals. Silver and cadmium can both be detected by the same XRF,
which was part of the reason they were selected for this project.

The ratio of counts in speciﬁc parts of the spectrum to the background signal (the broad peak
on the right) is measured repeatedly using a 0 ppm solution to determine the standard
deviation. This allows us to set a limit of detection, which in this case means any reading under
the limit is indistinguishable from 0 ppm assuming a normal distribution. This x-ray has the
lowest standard deviation of the three currently in use at the wastewater monitoring facility at
the lab, but its readings are still noisy which limits its accuracy at low concentrations.

Also of note for the XRF are its maintenance needs. Literature indicates that many XRF systems
that measure contamination are not in-line, meaning they take samples from the wastewater
ﬂow rather than measuring the water directly [8] [9]. The XRF systems in the wastewater
monitoring complex can achieve the latter by using clear thin ﬁlms with a small pocket between
them, allowing water to ﬂow through and creating a window for the x-rays to travel through
mostly uninterrupted. However, these ﬂow cells require regular upkeep, as they tend to warp or
bulge with changes in pressure.

Conclusion
The ISE was capable of operating in a wider range of conditions than what was stated in the
instructions, maintaining accurate readings and remaining calibrated after hours of laboratory
testing in DI water. While the readings of the wastewater were inaccurate, this is reasonable
considering the necessary diﬀerences between the calibration standards and the wastewater
stream. The fact that the calibration remained mostly intact after these tests indicates potential
for the ISEs to take live wastewater measurements if calibrated correctly.

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                                17

However, multiple days of wastewater testing were enough to reveal some potential ﬂaws in the
ISEs. Their ﬁlling solutions depleted quickly and would ideally need to be replenished daily,
which is a higher maintenance requirement than the XRF ﬂow cells. Additionally, while the ISEs
avoided fouling in the two wastewater tests, these were much shorter term than the ﬁnal goal
for these devices, which would be taking continuous measurements for weeks or months.
Having a system in which the detector itself is submerged in wastewater carries a far greater risk
of fouling than an XRF system that measures from a distance.

Future Work
The next step for this project would be to deploy the ISEs in the wastewater stream for several
days or weeks to determine their service lifetimes and maintenance requirements. From the

  LLNL-TR-2022496                                                                               18

ISEs’ continued performance following testing in the sewers and their consistent voltage
readings within the wastewater, it may be possible to obtain accurate concentration readings if
the calibrations can use real wastewater as opposed to DI water; however, this is currently not
possible for hazardous chemicals such as ionic silver and cadmium, as this poses a risk of
creating hazardous mixed waste or contaminating the sewerage system. Future work could
focus on developing a closed system to calibrate the ISEs in wastewater without connecting to
the sewers, and/or calibrating in the sewers with an ISE that detects a non-hazardous ion, such
as bromide. Bromine is a common component in cleaning products, and daily spikes in bromine
concentration have been detected by the XRF systems. Calibrating a bromide ISE in the
wastewater system and measuring these real-world events could provide insight into how
accurate both the XRF and ISE readings are for silver and cadmium. The ideal ﬁnal step for this
project would be to deploy an ISE and XRF in a real petroleum wastewater system to verify the
laboratory and sewer test results.
