Determinants of food safety practices among Ethiopian households: a multilevel mixed effects linear regression analysis
Authors: Aderajew Mekonnen Girmay, Kirubel Tesfaye Teklu, Ermias Alemayehu Adugna, Daniel Abera Dinssa, Abel Weldetinsae, Zinabu Assefa Alemu, Sisay Derso Mengesha, Mesaye Getachew Weldegebriel, Melaku Gizaw Serte, Bedasa Wagari, Abdi Keba, Endale Amare, Masresha Tessema, Getachew Tollera, Mesay Hailu, Hung Nguyen-Viet, Vivian Hoffmann, Eleni Papadopoulou, Delia Grace, Kebede Amenu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The total weighted sample included in this analysis was 62,432,197. Model with the lowest DIC, AIC and BIC values was selected as the best-fitting model. Safe food handling practice among households was 42.4%. Being completed primary school, secondary/vocational education, and university-level education had higher safe food handling practices by 0.21 (95% CI: 0.10, 0.32), 0.49 (95% CI: 0.36, 0.63), and 0.84 (95% CI: 0.64, 1.05), respectively. Working in government, the private sector, self-employed, and retired had significantly higher safe food handling practices by 0.28 (95% CI: 0.05, 0.51), 0.45 (95% CI: 0.17, 0.72), 0.34 (95% CI: 0.14, 0.54), and 0.35 (95% CI: 0.07, 0.64), respectively. Being poorer, middle, richer, and richest wealth index had significantly higher safe food handling practices by 0.33 (95% CI: 0.16, 0.50), 0.52 (95% CI: 0.33, 0.71), 0.84 (95% CI: 0.60, 1.08), and 1.37 (95% CI: 1.09, 1.65), respectively. Being married, female respondents, households having access to basic hygiene services, residing in urban areas, and being exposed to health information were significantly associated with the outcome. There is a significant gap in safe food handling practices. Core household and community-level factors that influence safe food handling practices were identified. Policymakers should implement targeted interventions.

Introduction

Food safety is a critical public health issue that directly affects the well-being of individuals and communities1. The problem of unsafe food remains a significant health risk in developing nations2–4. According the World Health Organization (WHO)5, unsafe food contaminated with harmful bacteria, viruses, parasites, or chemical substances causes more than 200 disease entities, ranging from diarrhoea to cancers. The WHO also conservatively estimated around 600 million people fall ill each year due to contaminated food, leading to about 420,000 deaths and a loss of 33 million disability-adjusted life years5. Children under five years old account for 40% of the burden of foodborne diseases, resulting in 125,000 deaths annually6,7. In low-income countries such as Ethiopia, public health problems associated with poor food handling practices are often much more serious due to poverty and environmental related risks8–10. Poor food handling practices from along the food supply chains, and poor hygiene conditions of households are the major determinants of the occurrence of food and waterborne diseases such as diarrhea, including Cholera, Giardiasis, and Cryptosporidiosis, Salmonellosis, Escherichia coli infection, and Listeriosis11–15. Safe food handling practices in households play a significant role in reducing foodborne diseases and are vital for public health16, as they influence individuals across all ages, races, genders, and income brackets worldwide. A recent study stated that approximately 33.3% of foodborne disease outbreaks occur in private households16. This study also stated that 39.1% of foodborne outbreaks occurred in domestic settings16. In particular, the health problem of unsafe food handling practices is a significant threat in low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia17,18. Therefore, to prevent illness and death, it is essential to conduct national representative study on factors determining the food handling practices at household level and such evidence can provide effective intervention strategies across the food safety system, particularly in safe food handling practices. Ethiopia with a population exceeding 115 million, is characterized by diverse dietary habits, and varying levels of food handling practice, and unequal access to relevant education and resources19,20. More than 80% of its people depend on agriculture for their livelihood and are believed to have unsafe food handling practices21,22. Rural households often face different challenges to have safe food handling practice compared to urban23, including limited access to clean water, basic hygiene facilities, and lower awareness regarding safe food handling practices. Moreover, cultural norms and traditional food preparation methods, which are common in rural areas, can lead to unsafe food handling practices24–26. In many Ethiopian households, food preparation often involves traditional practices such as preparing multiple dishes in the same kitchen space with limited separation between preparation stages27. Raw ingredients such as meat and vegetables may be handled sequentially using the same utensils or surfaces, especially in resource-limited settings28. In addition, traditional cooking methods and shared household kitchens may not emphasize strict hygiene separation between raw and cooked foods, partly due to limited access to dedicated kitchen equipment and food safety training29,30. In contrast, in developed countries such as Italy, food culture places strong emphasis on hygiene and structured meal preparation31. There is widespread awareness of food safety principles, including the separation of raw and cooked foods, influenced by formal education, public health campaigns, and regulatory standards32. Additionally, modern kitchen infrastructure and routine use of separate cutting boards, storage systems, and refrigeration support safer food handling practices33.

A recent study indicated that the impact of foodborne diseases in Ethiopia is significant, with an estimated 89.9 Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) per 100,000 individuals each year. Furthermore, this study revealed that the economic burden is notable, averaging 278.98 Ethiopian Birr (ETB) per household annually34. Additionally, another study found that three foodborne pathogens namely, non-typhoidal Salmonella enterica, Campylobacter species, and enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli are estimated to cost Ethiopia about $723 million, or 0.9% of its gross national income35. Although foodborne diseases pose a significant burden in Ethiopia, there is insufficient focus on domestic food handling practices. Previous research has highlighted the role of education, socioeconomic status, and access to hygiene resources in influencing food safety behaviors36–38. However, there is a gap in research regarding the contribution of household and community-level factors to safe food handling practices. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the associations between safe food handling practices and these factors among Ethiopian households using a multilevel linear regression analysis. This approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of food safety practices compared to previous studies in Ethiopia that relied on single-level analyses. Additionally, the use of nationally representative data enhances the generalizability of the findings and offers updated evidence to inform policy and interventions.

Methods

Study area

This study is based on the National Hygiene and Environmental Health Survey (NHEHS) 2022, which was conducted in nine of the 11 regional states and two city administrations in Ethiopia. The nine regional states included are: Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Somali, Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (SNNPR), Gambella, Sidama, Southwestern Region, and Harari. The two administrative cities are Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa. Because of security concerns, the regions Benishangul Gumuz and Tigray regions were not included. In the recent administrative reorganization (after implementation of the study), the region formerly known as SNNPR was divided into two regional states: South Ethiopia and Central Ethiopia. Therefore, currently, Ethiopia is divided into 13 regional states and two city administrations.

Study design and period

A population-based cross-sectional study was conducted nationwide in Ethiopia from February to May 2022.

Inclusion criteria

The study included households that prepared their food at home, with participants aged eighteen years and older. People with mental and severe illnesses were excluded from the study.

Sampling procedure

The sampling frame for this survey was the complete list of all census enumeration areas (EAs) prepared by the Ethiopian Statistics Service for the 2018 Population and Housing Census. It contained information about the EA location, type of residence (urban or rural), estimated number of residential households, and a sketch map delineating the EA’s geographic boundaries. A two-stage stratified cluster sampling procedure was applied to select the study areas and the study subject. At the first stage, a total of 811 EAs were selected from the sampling frame, consisting of 339 urban and 412 rural enumeration areas, and allocated across regional states and city administrations. In the second stage, each selected enumeration area was delineated using Q-field software, which enabled the precise definition of boundaries and the demarcation of specific areas for sampling and data collection. After delineation, fresh lists of households were collected from each area. A systematic random sampling technique was then applied to select 28 households from each household list, starting with one household identified through a lottery approach, followed by the inclusion of additional households based on a predefined sampling interval. This process resulted in 9,492 households from the 339 urban enumeration areas and 11,536 households from the 412 rural enumeration areas, amounting to a total of 22,708 households for the study.

Data collection and quality control

For data collection, NHEHS employed 75 first degree graduates in environmental health, public health, and food science and technology as enumerators, along with 25 supervisors holding master’s degrees, selected based on experience and qualifications. Additionally, 12 regional coordinators oversaw the data collection. To ensure data quality, a five-day training program was provided for the enumerators, supervisors and coordinators. The survey tool was adapted from the WHO’s “Five Keys to Safer Food” manual questionnaire4, the WHO/UNICEF core questions for water, sanitation, and hygiene39, as well as from previous studies40–44. The questionnaire was translated into Amharic, Afan Oromo, and Somali languages. The developed data collection tool had sections covering household socioeconomic and demographic characteristics, food handling practices, housing conditions, waste disposal practices, energy use patterns, and water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) services such as having adequate water, sanitation and basic hygiene infrastructure. Prior to the actual study, the questionnaire was tested for clarity Based on the results of the pretest; some adjustments were made to improve the clarity, completeness, and consistency of the tool. Cronbach’s alpha (α) was used to evaluate the tool’s internal consistency and validity, resulting in a value of “α” was 0.77 indicating the questionnaire is reliable and valid45. Data were gathered using the Open Data Kit (ODK) software. The data collection process involved face-to-face interviews with heads of households or designated respondents responsible for food handling practices, such as mothers or housemaid, along with observational assessments.

Operational definitions

Potential household and community-level determinants

The assessed determinants included the wealth index, the presence of basic hygiene services, and respondents’ exposure to hygiene and environmental health information, as described below.

Wealth Index: The household wealth index was created using household asset data analyzed through principal components analysis. The types of assets and the methodology for construction were derived from the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey37.

Basic hygiene service: Presence of a handwashing facility with soap and water at home38,39.

Exposure to hygiene and environmental health information: This was evaluated by asking six questions: whether respondents attended awareness creation sessions on hygiene and environmental health practices; if they were exposed to WASH-related information through banners, posters, and leaflets; their participation in food safety education initiatives; involvement in community and school discussions about WASH; whether they listened to hygiene and environmental health education broadcasts on local radio; and if they heard any food and water safety-related announcements at religious venues at least once in the past year.

Statistical analysis

Safe food handling practices: A questionnaire comprising of 25 questions, representing 25 food handling practices, was used to assess and define the safe food handling practices score. Each question provided at least two response options. A score of 1 was assigned for safe practices, while a score of 0 was assigned for unsafe practices. Then, all safe practices were summed to obtain total score, which was then used to calculate the mean. Percentage of all those with a score lower than this average score in low score and percentage of all those with a score equal to or higher than this average scores. For ease of interpretation we transformed the mean score to a mean percentage score using the following formula: Percentage means score = mean score x 100. The mean score was subsequently used in further statistical analysis.

Standard models may underestimate the standard errors of effect sizes due to the hierarchical structure of the data, where households are nested within primary sampling units (EA), which are organized within strata. In this setup, households within the same EA tend to be more similar to each other so multilevel modeling, which integrates both fixed effects (household and community-level factors) and random effects (variations among clusters), was used to obtain more accurate analysis results. The data were verified for completeness and imported into Stata V.16 for analysis. Descriptive analyses, including frequencies and percentages were conducted. We tested the 5 assumptions of multilevel mixed effect linear regression. These are linearity, normality, heteroscedasticity, multicollinearity, and outliers. In this study, we assessed linearity and found that, despite all independent variables being categorical, the food safety mean score showed a linear relationship with minimal variation across categories. The normality test results indicated that the data is normally distributed, as evidenced by a Pr(Skewness) value of 0, showing no skewness, and a Pr(Kurtosis) value of 0, indicating no kurtosis. Although specific values for adj chi2(2) and Prob> chi2 are not provided, the absence of significant skewness and kurtosis supports the conclusion that the residuals are likely normally distributed. Additionally, the large sample size (n = 62,432,197) further reduces concerns about normality46. In the third assumption test, heteroscedasticity was detected. To address this, we employed robust standard errors to account for the heteroscedasticity in the analysis. This approach adjusts the standard errors of the coefficient estimates without altering the model itself47. To the fourth assumption, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)48, and model fit was evaluated using various statistical metrics, including Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC), and Deviance Information Criterion (DIC)49–51. In addition, in the fifth assumption test for the outliers, the scatter plot of Cook’s D values against linear predictions reveals the influence of individual observations on the model. Most points have low Cook’s D values, clustering around zero, indicating minimal influence on the regression results. While a few points have higher Cook’s D values, they are sparse compared to the overall dataset, suggesting the absence of significant outliers. Overall, the plot indicates no widespread influential leverage points affecting the model significantly52. Data were weighted to ensure that the sample was representative of the country’s actual population. Multilevel mixed effect linear regression models were applied to assess the effects of factors influencing safe food handling practice. Four models were developed: Model 1 (unconditional model) was analyzed without any covariates, estimating the random intercept at the cluster level (EA) and assessing the variation in safe food handling practice score across communities. Household-level characteristics only were included in Model 2, community-level factors only were included in Model 3, and in Model 4 both household and community-level factors. These models account for the intraclass correlation of the outcome variable53, ensuring valid standard error estimates. Results

Of the 62,432,197 weighted samples included in the study 58,374,104 were interviewed, resulting in a response rate of (93.5%). Table 1 presents some characteristics of the household respondents. In this investigation, 52.5% of participants were female. Many (42.7%) were not literate and just under 10% had tertiary level education. Farming was the most common occupation with 52.8% of respondents; the next most common categories (self-employed, government workers and merchants) each had around 10% of respondents. Most (79.6%) were married and 56.3% of households had four or fewer members. According to the household wealth index around 40% were in “poor categories” with around one fifth middle-income, and 40% “rich”. Around half (50.8%) of respondents usually ate prepared meals freshly, while the rest (49.2% consumed the prepared food stale. Almost all households (90.1%) reported having no access to basic hygiene services. Just over half (51.6%) of respondents were rural and 48.4% urban. The majority of the community (76.8%) had no exposure to hygiene and environmental health information, whereas 23.2% reported having some exposure (Table 1).

Food safety practices of respondents

In this study, the mean percentage score of safe food handling practice among households was found to be 42.4% and the mean crude score was 10.4 (SD = 3.1). None of the participants reached the maximum score, indicating that none was practicing all the 25 practices. The most commonly reported safe practices were “storing raw and cooked foods inside a covered shelf” (95%), “washing hands before eating” (93%), and “avoiding storage of foods on uncovered shelves” (91%) or “directly on the ground” (86.%). Handwashing and utensil hygiene practices before food preparation were reported by 74.7% of respondents, and 58.7% reported cooking food thoroughly. Practices related to hand hygiene varied, with 60% reporting washing hands after toilet use and 58.9% before preparing foods. In contrast, practices such as avoiding contact between raw and cooked foods (14.8%), using safe water (13.6%), and protecting foods from pests (11.5%) were less frequently reported. The least commonly practiced behaviors included storing food in a refrigerator (5.4%), regular handwashing before breastfeeding (3.5%), and handwashing after changing diapers (4.1%). In all, 80% of participants replied that they were practicing 12 or more safe food handling practices, and 72% replied practicing more than 20 safe practices (Table 2).

Random effects analysis

Table 4 depicts four consecutive models based on multilevel mixed effects linear regression. Among the four models, model 4 provided the best overall model fit, as indicated by its lowest values in key fit statistics, Bayesian information criteria (BIC) (100099.6), Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) (99892.6), and Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) (−2 log-likelihood = 99840.6). These fit indices, together with the variance estimates, confirm the robustness of Model 4. Therefore, all interpretations and conclusions were drawn based on the results of this final model. Significant differences in safe food handling practices score between communities were found by the random effects analysis. The measures of variation presented in Table 4 highlight significant differences in the variability of safe food handling practices across different models. Various households and community-level characteristics were associated with the safe food handling practices score, as seen in the fully adjusted model (Model 4). Both household and community level variables were found to be significantly associated with safe food handling practices. In the multilevel linear regression model: from the household level factors, educational status, occupation, marital status, sex, wealth index, access to basic hygiene services were significantly associated with safe food handling practice. Similarly, from the community level factors, type of residence, and exposure to hygiene and environmental health information were significantly associated (P-value < 0.05) with safe food handling practice. Compared to household heads/respondents who could not read and write, those who completed primary school, secondary/vocational education, and university-level education had significantly higher safe food handling practice scores by 0.21 (95% CI: 0.10, 0.32), 0.49 (95% CI: 0.36, 0.63), and 0.84 (95% CI: 0.64, 1.05) respectively. In terms of occupational status, household heads working in government, the private sector, self-employed, and retired had significantly higher scores compared to farmers, with coefficients of 0.28 (95% CI: 0.05, 0.51), 0.45 (95% CI: 0.17, 0.72), 0.34 (95% CI: 0.14, 0.54), and 0.35 (95% CI: 0.07, 0.64), respectively. Married household heads had significantly higher outcome scores (B = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.21, 0.58) than those who were single. Female respondents also scored significantly higher safe food handling practice (B = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.26, 0.44) than male participants. Furthermore, wealth index of households showed a strong positive association, where poorer, middle, richer, and richest households had significantly higher scores than the poorest by 0.33 (95% CI: 0.16, 0.50), 0.52 (95% CI: 0.33, 0.71), 0.84 (95% CI: 0.60, 1.08), and 1.37 (95% CI: 1.09, 1.65) units, respectively. Households with access to basic hygiene service had significantly higher safe food handling practice score (β = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.67, 1.15) than those without. At the community level, residing in urban areas and being exposed to hygiene and environmental health information were both positively associated with the outcome, with coefficients of 0.34 (95% CI: 0.08, 0.61) and 0.29 (95% CI: 0.12, 0.46), respectively (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the associations between various household and community-level factors and safe food handling practices among Ethiopian households. The findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to improve food safety practices, particularly in rural areas. The study also underscores the importance of integrating multilevel approaches in future research and program design. Our findings indicated that the mean percentage score of safe food handling practice is 42.4%. This figure is in the same range and a bit lower than pooled prevalence reported in two systematic reviews and meta-analyses: 47.14% in one review54 and 50.5% in another55, both of which focused on Ethiopian safe food handling practices, as well as compared to a similar community-based cross-sectional study conducted among Southern Ethiopian households, which reported a prevalence of 51.1%14. Possible reasons for these differences could include variations in geographical coverage, differences in data collection tools, sample size variations, and demographic factors such as education level or socioeconomic status, along with local food safety regulations and cultural practices. Furthermore, cultural shifts, environmental changes, and a decline in community health initiatives may have compromised safe food handling practices, highlighting the need for targeted interventions, although this national study may still provide a more accurate representation of the country’s safe food handling practices than previous studies. We found that only 14.8% of households reported avoiding contact between raw and cooked foods. This is lower compared to a study conducted in Southern Italy, where 71.2% of Italian households reported always keeping raw and cooked foods separate56. The lack of proper separation between cooked and raw food can result in cross-contamination, increasing the risk of foodborne illnesses, while the disparity in safe food handling practices between Italian and Ethiopian households may stem from cultural attitudes that prioritize hygiene, along with higher levels of education and training in Italy regarding food safety. Additionally, stricter food safety regulations, differences in socioeconomic conditions, and access to resources like clean water and sanitation may further influence the effectiveness of safe handling practices, along with variations in study periods. However, the current study’s finding is nearly consistent with a similar cross-sectional study where 78.7% of participants affirmed using the same cutting board for raw and cooked foods57. Our findings show that 80% of participants practice more than 12 safe food handling behaviors, and 72% follow more than 20 (out of the 25 assessed practices). Notably, 20% of individuals reported fewer than half of the assessed practices, raising concerns about their risk for foodborne illnesses. This underscores the need for targeted educational interventions to enhance overall food safety awareness and practices58.

The random effects analysis indicates that the crude model (Model 1) had the highest variance estimate (3.4), reflecting greater variability at this stage. In contrast, the subsequent models show lower variances: model 2 has a variance of 2.4, model 3 has 2.8, and model 4 has 2.3. These findings demonstrated that the inclusion of additional factors in the models may account for some of the variability in the data. The p-values for all models are statistically significant (< 0.001), indicating that the differences observed are unlikely to be due to random chance. This reinforces the importance of the factors included in each model and their contribution to the explanation of variability. Furthermore, a substantial amount of the variation in safe food handling practices was explained by household variations, as seen by the null model’s (Model 1) Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) values of 35.9%. This value indicated that over one-third of the total variance was attributable to community-level factors. The ICC values (35.9%) in Model 1, decreasing to 25.1% in Model 4) reflect the proportion of total variance attributed to differences between household safe food handling practices. The higher ICC suggests that a significant portion of the variability in safe food handling practices is explained by the household level rather than community differences. Also, the decrease in ICC across models suggests that community-level factors also contribute to this variability. Similarly, the Proportional Change in Variance (PCV) from the null model (Model 1) to the fully adjusted model (Model 4) was 31.4%, indicating that nearly one-third of the between-community variability in food safety practice was explained by the included household and community-level covariates.

As shown in Table 4, the study on the associations of food safety practices with household and community-level factors among Ethiopian household respondents provides critical insights into the determinants influencing food safety behaviors. The multilevel mixed effects linear regression analysis revealed significant variability in food safety practices across households and communities. A substantial portion of the variability could be attributed to differences between households, indicating that household-level characteristics played a significant role in shaping food safety behaviors. The study identified concerning gaps in food safety practices, such as a significant percentage of households lacking clean water, basic hygiene service, and not engaging in essential behaviors like proper food storage and hand hygiene. In this study both household and community level variables were associated with safe food handling practice. The household level factors that associated with safe food handling practice were: education, occupation, marital status, sex of respondents, wealth index, and having basic hygiene service. Furthermore, residence type and exposure to hygiene and environmental health information were the community level factors that were associated with safe food handling practice. The analysis underscores the complex interplay of education, occupation, marital status, sex, wealth, having basic hygiene service, and community level characteristics in influencing food safety behaviors.

At the household level, educational status emerged as a critical determinant of food safety practices. Respondents with higher education levels demonstrated markedly better food handling behaviors compared to those with lower education. This association aligned with existing literature59–63, suggesting that education enhanced awareness of food safety protocols, thereby improving practices. Similarly, occupational status played a crucial role, with individuals in private sector jobs, government roles, and self-employment showing significantly better food handling practices than farmers. This suggests that job-related training and familiarity with food safety standards may be more widespread in specific occupations, underscoring the necessity for focused educational programs in food safety, though further research is required. In addition, this study revealed that married respondents were more likely to engage in safe food handling practice compared to single respondents. This could be attributed to shared responsibilities in food preparation and a greater emphasis on family health and safety. This is supported by a study done in 2019, where single food handlers were 64% less likely to practice food safety than married participants64. This could be attributed to factors such as greater household responsibilities, access to information, and a stronger emphasis on long-term health considerations in a family context. However, this needs further research. Additionally, the household wealth index revealed a strong association between economic status and food safety practices. Households with higher wealth levels exhibited progressively better food safety behaviors, indicating how economic resources impacted access to safe food storage, education, and hygiene services. This is supported by similar cross sectional studies conducted among Chinese, Ethiopian and Ghanaian households14,60,65. Besides, the current investigation showed that households with access to basic hygiene services significantly enhanced their food safety practices and were more likely to engage in safe behaviors compared to those without access. This is also supported by a similar study conducted in Gauteng, South Africa65. The current study’s finding also revealed that the community level factors were associated with safe food handling practice. Being resident in urban areas and having exposure to hygiene and environmental health information were associated with higher safe food handling practice. In this study, the type of residence had a significant impact on food safety practices, as urban respondents were more likely to practice safe food handling behaviors compared to their rural counterparts. The current result is also in line with previous study conducted in Bangladesh66,67. The difference may be attributed to several contextual factors. Urban settings generally provide better access to health information, media, and food safety education, which enhances awareness and promotes safer food handling behaviors24,68,69. In addition, urban households are more likely to benefit from improved infrastructure, such as access to clean water, sanitation facilities, and refrigeration, all of which support safer practices70. Stronger regulatory oversight and greater exposure to food safety campaigns in urban areas may further reinforce appropriate behaviors2. In contrast, rural settings often face constraints, including limited access to safe water and health education, as well as reliance on traditional practices that may not align with recommended food safety standards71. Providing access to cost-effective and accessible media, including mobile applications and other digital interventions, while considering rural constraints such as limited infrastructure and lower educational levels, may serve as essential tools for improving awareness. The difference could be from having better access to resources, education, and hygiene services in urban areas24,72. Furthermore, communities’ exposure to hygiene and environmental health information was linked to safe food handling practice, suggesting that public health campaigns and educational outreach effectively enhanced food safety practices, particularly in areas with low awareness. This idea is supported by a similar cross sectional study conducted in Malawi73.

Limitation of the study

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Second, as most data were collected through observation, the findings may be subject to observer bias and the Hawthorne effect, where individuals may alter their behavior when being observed. Third, some potentially relevant variables, such as cultural practices or detailed environmental factors, may not have been captured.

Conclusion

In this study, the mean percentage score of safe food handling practice among Ethiopian households is low, indicating a significant gap. This study identified core household and community-level factors that influence safe food handling practice. These findings emphasize the need to address the multifaceted nature of food safety in Ethiopia. Therefore, to improve food safety practices and minimize the risk of foodborne diseases, policymakers should implement targeted educational initiatives for individuals with lower literacy levels, establish socioeconomic support programs to offer resources for low-income households, assist vulnerable households with access to basic hygiene services,, and adopt gender-sensitive strategies. In addition, policymakers should create awareness campaigns for rural households that utilize mobile applications or online platforms to provide easy access to guidelines and tips for safe food handling.
