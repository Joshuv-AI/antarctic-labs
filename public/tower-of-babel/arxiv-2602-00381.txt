Modeling Image-Caption Rating from Comparative Judgments
Kezia Minni, Qiang Zhang, Monoshiz Mahbub Khan, Zhe Yu

Abstract
Rating the accuracy of captions in describing images is time-consuming and subjective for humans. In contrast, it is often easier for people to compare two captions and decide which one better matches a given image. In this work, we propose a machine learning framework that models such comparative judgments instead of direct ratings. The model can then be applied to rank unseen image-caption pairs in the same way as a regression model trained on direct ratings. Using the VICR dataset 10 , we extract visual features with ResNet-50 and text features with MiniLM, then train both a regression model and a comparative learning model. While the regression model achieves better performance (Pearson’s ρ \rho : 0.7609 and Spearman’s r s r_{s} : 0.7089), the comparative learning model steadily improves with more data and approaches the regression baseline. In addition, a small-scale human evaluation study comparing absolute rating, pairwise comparison, and same-image comparison shows that comparative annotation yields faster results and has greater agreement among human annotators. These results suggest that comparative learning can effectively model human preferences while significantly reducing the cost of human annotations.

Introduction

Humans often engage in subjective decision-making, such as evaluating whether a caption accurately describes an image or whether a piece of artwork is good. Such assessments are inherently subjective and heavily rely on individual interpretation
1
. Automating such judgments can reduce human time and effort in large-scale evaluation tasks. Traditional numeric rating methods (e.g., assigning scores from 1 to 5) are often inefficient, time-consuming and not ideal, because individuals may interpret scales differently or inconsistently apply ratings, leading to noisy labels and poor inter-rater reliability
17
;
19
.

To address these limitations, researchers have looked at comparative judgment, which involves raters choosing between two options relative to each other rather than independently assigning scores. This method, based on Thurstone’s Law of Comparative Judgment
16
;
14
, is well-suited for subjective attributes like originality or relevance, where there is no objective ground truth. This method reduces individual bias and simplifies decision-making by focusing on relative quality.

In this work, we apply comparative learning to model image-caption rating—i.e., assessing how well a caption matches a given image. While regression models are trained on direct human ratings which are expensive and noisy, comparative learning models are trained on comparative judgments such as image-caption pair A better matches each other than image-caption pair B. In addition, we explore whether the comparative judgments between image-caption pairs of the same image (which of the two captions better matches a given image) are easier to acquire and more reliable than the comparative judgments between image-caption pairs of different images. To do so, the following research questions are explored in this work.

RQ1:

Can a model trained on comparative judgments perform as well as a regression model trained on direct ratings in predicting how well a caption matches an image?

RQ2:

Can we correctly model which caption better matches a given image?

RQ3:

Can we validate the advantages of comparative judgments over direct ratings?

To investigate these research questions, we use multimodal modeling to compare the traditional regression model with the comparative learning model. While the regression model learns from direct human ratings to predict a single relevant value, the comparative learning model is trained to capture which image-caption pair better matches each other. Because the comparative model requires only binary decisions rather than numerical values, the training data labels can be collected more efficiently with less variation among human annotators
16
;
14
.

Our findings show that comparative learning is an effective substitute for absolute scoring in assessing and evaluating image–caption pairs. Although the regression model provides stronger performance, the comparative learning model quickly approaches this level using simpler and sparser feedback. Moreover, comparative learning performs well on the same-image caption ranking task, even when the difference between captions is subtle.

A small-scale human subjects experiment was conducted with eight participating human raters annotating both direct ratings and comparative judgments. The results of the experiment validated the advantage of comparative annotation – comparative judgments have shorter annotation time in average and higher inter-rater agreements than direct ratings.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

Background and Related Work

2.1
Image-Text Matching

Image-text matching is a multimodal training task
2
;
18
. Traditional methods usually rely on absolute manual ratings and use regression models to directly predict the matching quality. The advantages of these methods are intuitive and easy to train, but they also have the disadvantage that they are ”often susceptible to subjectivity and inconsistent annotations”
17
.

A representative example is Narins et al.
10
, who applied linear regression on the VICR dataset. They used the ViLBERTmodel pre-trained model to decompose the image and text into vectors to predict the average manual rating. Although this method is simple, its performance is often poor when the rating noise is large or the dataset is small.

Pre-training methods such as CLIP
12
and UNITER
4
also use contrastive learning or multi-task learning to deal with the image-text alignment problem. However, these studies only discuss the implementation ideas of contrastive methods and do not analyze the differences between regression models and contrastive learning models.

In summary, while regression models and contrastive models provide reliable baselines, they often lack robustness in capturing the subtleties of human judgment. Our work aims to address this issue by leveraging pairwise preference learning to better model relative correlations and enhance generalization capabilities.

2.2
Learning from Comparative Judgments

Learning from comparative judgments provides an alternative to absolute rating methods. Instead of giving scores directly, users compare pairs of items, which helps to obtain more consistent and detailed feedback. Comparative judgments have shown effectiveness in recommendation, information retrieval, and subjective evaluation tasks
5
.

7
.

Early ranking learning models, such as RankNet and LambdaRank
3
, use neural networks and pairwise loss functions (such as hinge loss or cross entropy loss) to learn pairwise preferences. These methods have shown excellent performance in retrieval applications, but have not been applied to some multimodal problems.

Fürnkranz and Hüllermeier
7
formalized the pairwise preference learning framework by modeling ranking as a binary classification problem of a series of item pairs - the basis of our method.

Building on this, Ghosh et al.
8
proposed an adaptive pairwise comparison method based on active sampling. Their method jointly optimizes regression and ranking objectives, reducing annotation costs while improving preference modeling. However, their work is limited to the pure text domain and does not explore the image-text space.

Khan et al.
9
propose a comparative learning approach to agile story point estimation. This work formats data on backlog items’ texts and their direct ratings into a pairwise format to include two items’ texts and a comparative label denoting which item has the higher direct rating. This data is used on a single dense layer based encoder structure with Hinge loss to train on the reformatted data for the task of story point estimation to predict an order of the backlog items.

In contrast to previous work, our work extends pairwise comparison to the multimodal domain. We propose a dual encoder structure comprised of multiple dense layers, ReLU activation functions, batch normalization, dropout regularization and L2 penalties. This proposed structure is trained with a pairwise hinge loss and show its superior ability to model human preferences and handle subjective variability. We also compare the traditional direct scoring regression model with the new contrastive learning model.

2.3
Image-text embeddings

11
presented a dual encoder model that trains image and text representations that are compatible via contrastive learning on massive web data. The model adopts separate encoders for images and captions, enabling strong zero-shot performance. We adopt a similar dual encoder structure using ResNet for images and MiniLM for text, applying it to comparative caption evaluation tasks.

6
improves visual-semantic embedding models by introducing hard negative mining in a dual encoder setup for image-text retrieval. Instead of averaging over randomly sampled negatives, it selects the hardest mismatched image or caption during training, encouraging the model to make finer-grained distinctions. Like our comparative model, VSE++ uses a hinge loss objective and a dual encoder architecture, supporting our approach to learning relative caption quality.

13
modifies BERT into a Siamese network to generate semantically meaningful sentence embeddings that can be compared using cosine similarity. It enables efficient semantic search, clustering, and similarity scoring by encoding each sentence independently. MiniLM from SentenceTransformers framework was employed by our model to encode captions. It utilizes the speed and semantic accuracy of MiniLM for a comparative evaluation of the captions.

Methodology

This section discusses the end-to-end approach used to stimulate a model that predicts human preferences for image-caption pairs using both regression and comparative learning strategies. The pipeline includes data preprocessing, multimodal embedding generation, model training (regression and comparative), and evaluation.

3.3
Model Architecture

We developed two learning frameworks: the first is a regression-based model that predicts the quality of the caption as a continuous scalar value (as shown in Fig.
2
), and the second is a comparative model that learns to estimate the caption quality in terms of pairwise rank order between image-caption pairs
(as shown in Fig.
2
)

Both models use the same dual-encoder architecture for multimodal feature learning. ResNet-50 is used for the image encoding, which yields a 2048-dimensional feature vector for each image. The caption encoder is the MiniLM-L6-v2 SentenceTransformers configuration, which renders a 384-dimensional embedding for each caption. Both embeddings are concatenated to create a 2432-dimensional joint representation. In the regression model (Fig.
2
), we processed the representation through a fully connected feedforward neural network with four dense layers using ReLU activations, batch normalization, and dropout. In the comparative learning model (Fig.
2
), two such representations were produced independently for each image-caption pair and passed through a shared scoring function. The outputs were optimized through the hinge-loss function, which encouraged alignment with human preference judgments.

3.4
Regression Modeling

We train a fully connected neural network to predict normalized human ratings from the multimodal embeddings. The architecture consists of dense layers with ReLU activation, batch normalization, dropout regularization, and L2 penalties. To jointly optimize for score fidelity and ordinal consistency, we use a custom ranking-penalized Mean Absolute Error (MAE) loss during training. Although MAE is the primary loss, we also evaluate the model using Mean Squared Error (MSE), in addition to Pearson (
\rho
) and Spearman (
r_{s}
) correlation metrics. The model is trained using the Adam optimizer with cosine decay learning rate scheduling and early stopping.

3.5
Comparative Learning Modeling

To model comparative judgment, we train the same scoring function used in the regression setting, but with pairwise supervision instead of absolute ratings. The model takes two multimodal embeddings as input
(x_{i},x_{j})
and produces scalar utility scores for each. These scores are subtracted to compute a preference difference:

C_{ij}=f(x_{i})-f(x_{j})

The model is optimized using a margin-based hinge loss:

\mathcal{L}(O_{ij},C_{ij})=\max(0,1-O_{ij}\cdot C_{ij})

Where
O_{ij}
denotes the comparative preference label between the two image-caption pair inputs
(x_{i},x_{j})
:

O_{ij}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1,&\text{when }&x_{i}\text{ better matches each other than }x_{j}\\
&-1,&\text{when }&x_{j}\text{ better matches each other than }x_{i}.\end{aligned}\right.

This comparative approach closely mimics how humans make subjective decisions is areas such as caption evaluation. It is often more easier for a rater to judge which caption better suits the image than to assign an exact numeric score. Therefore, comparative learning serves as a super vision signal more in line with human nature for more in line with subjective quality estimation. Because the model is trained to learn relative preferences, it avoids issues related to scale inconsistency that is often seen in absolute ratings. This makes pairwise data collection much faster and less noisy while still conveying fine grain preference information.

As shown in Fig.
2
, the comparative model utilizes two feed forward neural networks (same as the regression model) with shared weights to process each image-caption pair independently and output a single scalar utility score. Each encoder first concatenates a 2048-dimensional ResNet-50 image embedding and a 384-dimensional MiniLM text embedding to form a 2432-dimensional vector. These vectors pass through dense layers with Relu activations, batch normalization, dropout and L2 regularization to produce the final score. During the training phase, a pair of embeddings
(x_{i},x_{j})
is fed in parallel to get the scores
f(x_{i})
and
f(x_{j})
. Their difference,

C_{ij}=f(x_{i})-f(x_{j})
, is compared with the ground-truth label

O_{ij}=\mathrm{sgn}(y_{i}-y_{j})\in\{+1,-1\}
using the hinge-ranking loss. This loss encourages the preferred caption to have a higher score by a margin
m=1.5
, penalizing the model only when the score difference contradicts the human preference or falls within the margin.

We choose comparative learning because it aligns with human decision behavior and perform well for subjective, ranking-oriented tasks. Unlike the regression baseline from the VICR framework, which minimizes the numeric distance between predicted and actual scores, our model focuses on the relative order of caption quality. While regression models require the same rating scales and can be biased by annotation noise, the comparative approach requires only pairwise order information and is therefore robust to inter-rater variability. Empirically, the formulation allows the model to capture local ranking structure effectively, approaching the regression baseline in correlation metrics while requiring far fewer annotated labels.

Overall, the comparative model provides an interpretable utility-based framework that learns humanlike preference ordering and generalizes well to new image-caption pairs.

4.2
RQ1: Regression VS. Comparative Learning

For RQ1, we train a regression model on absolute ratings and a comparative model on pairwise preferences derived from the same dataset. The regression model is evaluated on an 80/20 split, where the last 20% of image–caption pairs are held out for testing. The comparative model is trained and evaluated on pairwise data constructed from the full set of items, using an 80/20 split at the pair level. Although the two models are not evaluated on exactly the same instances, both follow a consistent 80/20 protocol on the same underlying dataset, and we compare them using comparable correlation-based metrics.

Hypotheses

In RQ3, we focus on testing the following two hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1:

The average annotation time of comparative judgments (Task 2 and 3) is shorter than the average annotation time of direct ratings (Task 1).

Hypothesis 2:

The inter-rater agreement level of comparative judgments (Task 2 and 3) is higher than that of direct ratings (Task 1).

Hypothesis 1: Annotation Time.

Table
10
presents the average decision time of the eight participants. Such an experiment made it very clear that comparative tasks take substantially less time per judgment. The annotation time reported in Table
10
also includes the reading time of captions, which takes a big part of the annotation time (given that the average annotation time is around 10 seconds) and the comparative judgments (Task 2 and 3) require about double the reading time. So, for the real decision-making time, the difference should be even larger. Therefore,
Hypothesis 1
is accepted. Just as the law of comparative judgment indicated, it is easier to make comparative judgments than providing the direct ratings. The difference could be larger in tasks where the decisions are more difficult to make.

Besides the faster annotation time, participants also expressed that comparative choices were more natural and less ambiguous. The assignment of numeric ratings often required repetition of the reading of the caption to calibrate the scale of 1 to 5, while pairwise decisions could be made immediately based on visual alignment and caption accuracy. Therefore, comparative labeling has the potential to significantly lower annotation costs and increase the consistency of subjective evaluation tasks. These discoveries are in line with the utilization of comparative learning frameworks to model caption quality on a large scale since they are both time-efficient and have higher inter-rater reliability than scalar rating methods.

Discussion

The experimental results reveal that comparative methods are more suitable for modeling human subjective judgment. The regression model is marginally better than the comparative model in terms of absolute correlation, but the difference becomes very small very quickly as the number of training samples is increased; hence, the comparative model is scalable and data-efficient. The comparative model is close to the regression baseline even though the amount of supervision is limited, which is a strong argument for the relative nature of the learning process rather than the use of explicit numeric scores.

One of the advantages of comparative learning is the efficiency of data annotation. It is often easier for humans to judge which of two samples is better than to assign precise scores to each sample. This makes pairwise comparisons not only cheaper to annotate but also more consistent, because they are less susceptible to rating scale bias and subjective fluctuations
16
. Our human evaluation results are in line with this idea: pairwise comparisons led to almost perfect inter-rater agreement, whereas absolute ratings showed lower consistency even though they correlated quite well with the ground-truth trend. These results indicate that comparative judgments help to lower the cognitive load and also lessen the problem of rating-scale inconsistencies, thus they are a more appropriate choice for subjective caption evaluation.

The results from the same-image comparison lead to the same conclusion. Although the inter-rater reliability in Task 3 was a bit lower than that in pairwise comparisons across different images, this was anticipated due to the dataset’s nature. The majority of same-image caption pairs had very close ground-truth ratings (e.g., 4 vs. 5), which inherently made the differences more subtle. In spite of this, participants managed to achieve a high level of agreement (90%), and the comparative model trained in this setting was able to maintain strong pairwise accuracy and stable correlation across runs. This is an indication that comparative supervision is still effective when the differences between captions are at a fine-grained level.

The comparison-based approach works well in ranking captions, showing stable accuracy and alignment with human judgment over several tests. Its reliability matches earlier findings supporting ranked scales for evaluations where clear metrics are hard to set up.

Still, some drawbacks exist. Creating paired comparisons for big data uses high computing power, since pair counts rise fast with size. To reduce load, our method applies random picks; yet, smarter ways like focused selection could help when scaling up. Also, because similar images had only slight caption changes, the training signals lacked variation. Creating datasets that include more varied caption quality per image might improve comparison-based learning later on. Lastly, although the model picks up consistent usefulness scores for ordering items, turning these into accurate number-based forecasts could need extra stages when exact ratings matter.

Overall, these results suggest that comparative learning can serve as a practical, steady, and expandable option instead of absolute scoring in multimodal evaluation - because it matches how people make choices more closely. Since this method reflects real judgment patterns, it opens a useful path toward better accuracy and trustworthiness when evaluating image-caption pairs.

7
Conclusion

This study investigates the effectiveness of comparative learning in modeling image-caption correlation. While traditional regression models based on absolute scores have a slight advantage in correlation, our comparative model achieves stable and scalable performance relying only on pairwise preference data.

The model achieves a maximum Spearman (
r_{s}
) of 0.69 and Pearson (
\rho
) correlation of 0.67 with limited training samples, while maintaining high accuracy in the subtitle ranking task. In addition, the comparative model performs strongly on the same-image caption comparison task, showing that it can reliably capture fine-grained preference differences even when captions are similar. These results show that, compared with absolute scores, comparative supervision has lower annotation cost, higher consistency, and stronger robustness in subjective evaluation tasks.

The human evaluation further supports this finding. Pairwise and same-image comparisons were completed more quickly and with higher inter-rater agreement than absolute ratings, indicating that comparative labeling imposes less cognitive load and produces more reliable judgments. This provides practical evidence that comparative learning is not only effective as a modeling approach but also easier and more efficient for human annotators.

In the future, we plan to extend this framework to multimodal matching tasks such as video-text and audio-text. At the same time, we will also explore collaborative human-machine annotation interfaces and active learning strategies to further reduce annotation costs and improve model performance.
