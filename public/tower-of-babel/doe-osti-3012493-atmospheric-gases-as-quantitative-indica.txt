 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The urban heat island (UHI) effect is a significant phenomenon characterized by higher surface
temperatures in cities compared to surrounding rural areas. This change in the Earth's energy
balance presents considerable challenges for urban populations, increasing heat exposure, straining
energy infrastructure, and exacerbating health vulnerabilities. Quantifying UHI intensity is
challenging due to blurred distinctions between urban and rural areas as land-use patterns evolve.
To address this challenge, the present study builds on the framework established by Salvador et al.
(2025) and investigates the use of atmospheric constituents, specifically volatile organic
compounds (VOCs) and inorganic gases, as tracers of urbanization levels. A key improvement in
this approach is the inclusion of a designated true rural site for comparison. Field measurements
were conducted at two locations in the Southeastern United States during the summer of 2025.
Inorganic gases were monitored continuously, while VOC samples were collected biweekly and
analyzed using a proton transfer reaction time-of-flight mass spectrometer. The urban site
exhibited elevated concentrations of anthropogenic inorganic gases, with carbon monoxide and
nitrogen dioxide averaging 878 ppb and 17.99 ppb, respectively. In contrast, the rural site showed
lower levels of these pollutants, 796 ppb for carbon monoxide and 12.46 ppb for nitrogen dioxide,
while displaying higher concentrations of biogenic VOCs, including isoprene and monoterpenes
at 1.24 ppb and 0.08 ppb, respectively. Extended mass spectral analyses revealed distinct chemical
differences between the two sites, supporting the observed variability of major gases. Overall,
these findings are consistent with our previous research and highlight the value of atmospheric
constituents as effective indicators of urbanization, thereby improving our understanding of UHI
dynamics.

I. INTRODUCTION

 The urban heat island (UHI) effect refers to the phenomenon by which the Earth’s surface
energy balance differs markedly between urban and non-urban areas, primarily due to land surface
modifications associated with urbanization [1]. A key driver of the UHI effect is the widespread
use of man-made materials, such as asphalt, to create impervious surfaces in metropolitan areas,
which dominate the landscape and significantly reduce natural vegetation. Unlike vegetated areas,
these surfaces possess low albedos and high volumetric heat capacities [2], enabling them to absorb
and retain greater amounts of incoming solar radiation. This, combined with the loss of vegetation
and the resulting decrease in evapotranspiration, alters the Earth’s surface energy balance by
reducing latent heat flux and enhancing heat storage. Consequently, urban areas tend to cool at a
slower rate than their rural counterparts. Recent studies have quantified the magnitude of the UHI
effect ranging from 5 to 15 °C [2] relative to surrounding rural environments. Prolonged exposure
to these elevated temperatures has direct implications for human health. Heat-related illnesses can
range from mild heat exhaustion to severe and potentially fatal heat stroke [3], though heat stroke
does not act as the sole cause of death, but instead contributes to death and morbidity by worsening
pre-existing health conditions [4]. Given that urban populations constitute a substantial and
continually growing proportion of the global population, developing a comprehensive
understanding of the UHI phenomenon is critical.

 Despite growing understanding of the UHI phenomenon, there remains significant
knowledge gaps, particularly in defining and characterizing what constitutes an urban region. Such
characterization is critical in the calculation of UHI intensity (UHII), which is commonly defined
as the temperature difference between urban and rural areas [5]. Historically, ground-based
measurements (i.e. population density, urban land use [6]) or remote sensing techniques (i.e. land
cover data [7]) have been used as reference frameworks for this purpose. However, characterizing
urban environments has become increasingly complex in recent years. Cities often incorporate
heterogeneous land uses, including the integration of green spaces and mixed-density
developments, that generate highly variable microclimates [8]. These spatial variations can reduce
the effectiveness of conventional metrics of characterization, making it difficult to delineate the
boundaries of urban areas. Consequently, there is a growing need for a more robust tracer of
urbanization that can accurately represent the spatial structure of the urban environment.

 The atmosphere contains particulate and gaseous species that often play detrimental roles
in air quality, climate, and human and environmental health [9]. Among the typical atmospheric
components are inorganic gases such as carbon monoxide (CO), nitric oxide (NO), nitrogen
dioxide (NO₂), and ozone (O₃). Natural sources of these gases include processes such as lightning-
induced nitrogen fixation, volcanic degassing, and soil microbial activity [10, 11], while
anthropogenic emissions primarily stem from fossil fuel combustion, intensive agricultural
practices, and urban-industrial activities [12]. Although inorganic gases such as CO and NO x have
historically served as indicators of urbanization [13-15], improvements in vehicle efficiency have

 6

reduced their emissions, weakening their effectiveness as distinct urban tracers. Given this reduced
diagnostic power, it is necessary to consider additional atmospheric constituents when
characterizing urban influence. In this context, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) offer particular
promise.

 VOCs are an important class of atmospheric constituents, defined as organic chemicals that
readily vaporize into the atmosphere due to their high volatility [16]. Once emitted, VOCs act as
precursors of ozone and secondary aerosols through reactions with atmospheric oxidants [17].
VOCs can be broadly categorized into two groups: biogenic VOCs (BVOCs) and anthropogenic
VOCs (AVOCs). BVOCs are emitted naturally from vegetation and dominate global VOC
emissions. Among them, isoprene (C5H8) and monoterpenes (C10H16) are the most significant.
Isoprene is a well-established driver of tropospheric ozone formation [18], while monoterpenes
comprising compounds such as α-pinene, β-pinene, limonene, and δ-carene serve as key precursors
to aerosol formation events. In contrast, AVOCs originate from human activities such as vehicle
exhaust, industrial operations, and biomass burning. Benzene (C 6H6) and toluene (C7H8) are often
used as indicators of AVOC concentrations. Once emitted, AVOCs react with atmospheric
nitrogen oxides under the presence of sunlight to form ground-level ozone.

 The diverse origin of inorganic gases and VOCs present a unique opportunity to decompose
atmospheric composition into anthropogenic and biogenic components, thereby providing a
framework for quantifying urbanization. While previous studies have demonstrated the utility of
inorganic gases as urbanization tracers [19, 20], the identification of optimal VOC species for this
purpose remains underexplored. This study builds upon the experimental design of Salvador et al.
(2025), which employed the variability of such atmospheric components as direct tracers of
urbanization in ground-based measurements. A limitation of that prior work, however, was the
absence of a true rural reference site, which restricted the ability to capture atmospheric signatures
representative of non-urbanized environments. By incorporating such a site, the present study aims
to address this knowledge gap, enabling the identification of which inorganic gases and VOC
species that most effectively distinguish urbanized from nonurbanized environments. This study
also integrated the comprehensive characterization of the meteorological conditions of the two
sites, which differed substantially and consequently impacted the emission and transformation of
atmospheric components and the variability of UHII.

A. SITE SAMPLING DETAILS

This work is part of a larger study investigating the effect of soil moisture and plant
evapotranspiration on ambient temperature and relative humidity in several city parks in
Knoxville, Tennessee, USA. Particularly, the research presented herein is built upon the work done
in Salvador et al. (2025), in which we substitute the previously designated “less urbanized site”
(Cumberland Estate Park, CEP) with a true rural location. The urban site, the Socially Equal

Energy Efficient Development (SEEED) site, remains unchanged. This new rural site, termed as
Gamble-Gregg (GG) is a farm site in south Knoxville. GG (latitude: 35.470, longitude: 83.511,
elevation: 304m) consists of open grasslands and forest, with sparse manmade structures
surrounding its immediate vicinity. Instruments at GG were collocated in one of the grass fields
next to a forested area. SEEED (latitude: 35.971, longitude: -83.903, elevation: 323 m) is a
nongovernmental organization in proximity to downtown Knoxville that experiences high levels
of transportation activity (Fig. 1 for close-up geographical locations of GG and SEEED).
Instruments deployed in the SEEED site was placed in the sparse vegetative area just behind the
SEEED building. The distance between the SEEED and GG sites is approximately 20 km.

B. INSTRUMENTATION

 Measurements were taken from May 30th to July 18th, 2025. Inorganic gas concentrations
were continuously monitored using low-cost sensor units equipped with solar power modules.
MODULAIR (QuantAQ, Inc. Massachusetts, USA) units were deployed to measure CO, NO,
NO2, and O3. A detailed description of the characterization of source signatures and temporal
variability of these gases is provided in Salvador et al. (2025). Meteorological parameters,
including air temperature, atmospheric pressure, and solar radiation, were simultaneously collected
using a METER ATMOS 41 All-in-One Weather Station (Pullman, Washington, USA).

 The assessment of AVOC and BVOC concentrations of the two sites was carried out using
a combination of offline collection using stainless steel canisters and measurement using the high-
resolution proton transfer reaction time-of-flight mass spectrometer (PTR-ToF-MS) (6000X2,

 8

IONICON, Innsbruck, Austria). A miniature air sampling canister (400 cc electropolished stainless
steel with quick-connect stem fitting) equipped with a quick-connect stem fitting with 0.0016"
orifice was used to sample gases at a flow rate of 4-8 mL/min. In such a configuration, the canister
collected ambient air with one hour sampling time. Canisters were prepared by a repeated series
of vacuuming at -29 inHg, purging of zero air, and heating canisters at 120°C. Samples were
collected twice a week on Mondays and Thursdays for 5 weeks with two replicates per site.

transfer in the drift tube of the PTR-ToF-MS. To be measured by the PTR-ToF-MS, the target

III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

A. COMPARISON OF METEOROLOGICAL CONDITIONS

 Meteorologic parameters, including but not limited to, atmospheric pressure, vapor
pressure, and wind speed exert a controlling influence on the transport, dilution, and retention of
pollutants within the lower atmosphere. These mechanisms are largely regulated by boundary-
layer dynamics, where the depth and internal structure of the atmospheric boundary layer (BL)
determine vertical mixing and consequently near-surface pollutant concentrations. In urban
settings, BL becomes markedly more complex, as the presence of building roughness,
anthropogenic heat fluxes, and mechanically generated turbulence led to enhanced spatial and
temporal variability in pollutant fate [18, 21]. Additionally, the UHI effect further modifies these

 9

dynamics by altering the structure of the BL [22]. During the daytime, elevated surface
temperatures deepen the BL and enhance turbulent mixing, facilitating the dispersion of locally
emitted pollutants. After sunset, however, persistent urban heat delays surface cooling and
promotes the formation of shallow, stably stratified nocturnal BLs. This suppression of mixing
limits ventilation and allows heat, moisture, and pollutants to accumulate near the surface.
Therefore, contextualizing the meteorological conditions present during the study period is
essential for interpreting the observed variability in inorganic gas and VOC concentrations.
 The average atmospheric pressures measured at GG and SEEED sites were 98.20 ± 0.261
kPa and 97.56 ± 0.268 kPa, respectively, with a calculated significant difference (p < 0.05). At
GG, the maximum and minimum values recorded for the duration of measurements were 98.85
and 97.61 kPa; at SEEED they were 98.10 and 96.84 kPa. The higher average pressure at the urban
site may promote more stable BL conditions which in turn reduce vertical mixing, potentially
enhancing pollutant accumulation near the surface. However, this effect must be interpreted within
the context of broader heat-island dynamics, which can partially counteract stability during
daytime hours.

 Vapor pressure exhibited a clear diurnal cycle at both sites, with mean early-morning
minima (± standard deviation) of 2.26 ± 0.21 kPa at GG and 2.36 ± 0.26 kPa at SEEED, followed
by a pronounced mid-morning rise to mean maxima of 2.77 ± 0.18 kPa at GG and 2.88 ± 0.23 kPa
at SEEED. Because saturation vapor pressure increases exponentially with temperature (Clausius-
Clapeyron ≈ 7% °C-1), the warmer nocturnal air associated with the UHI effect can sustain a higher
moisture capacity [23]. Consequently, when even modest evapotranspirative fluxes add water
vapor to this already warm air mass, actual vapor pressure can increase disproportionately in urban
environments relative to their rural counterparts. When combined with anthropogenic moisture
inputs (i.e. cooling towers, vehicle emissions), this leads to greater latent moisture content in the
urban BL. At night, the BL over urban surfaces tend to become less efficient at ventilating this
accumulated moisture due to reduced turbulence, further exacerbating moisture retention. Local
hydrologic context, including SEEED’s proximity to the Tennessee River (approximately 0.84
km), likely reinforced these humidity contrasts. Statistical testing (Table S1) indicated that vapor
pressure differences were significant between 00:00-07:00 and 11:00-19:00 . However, during
08:00 to 10:00 , vapor pressure differences between GG and SEEED were not statistically
significant, suggesting convergence in near-surface humidity. This period coincided with
enhanced vertical mixing [22] that redistributes moisture more uniformly between the sites,
temporarily diminishing contrasts. After 19:00 , the two sites again show limited statistical
difference as GG’s atmosphere stabilizes and re-moistens through radiative cooling and surface
dew formation [24].

 VPD, defined as the difference between the moisture the air can hold at a given temperature
and the actual moisture present, remains low at night and then increases rapidly after sunrise in
both sites. Both sites experienced low early morning values, reaching a minimum of 0.03 ± 0.05
kPa at GG and 0.10 ± 0.10 kPa at 06:00 SEEED. By late afternoon, peaks reached maximums of
2.06 ± 0.47 kPa at GG and 2.13 ± 0.52 kPa at SEEED, corresponding to the maximum of solar
heating and BL growth. Between 00:00 and 08:00 and again from 19:00 to 23:00 , SEEED
exhibited significantly higher VPD than GG. These periods corresponded to strong UHI influence,
where elevated urban temperatures raise saturation vapor pressure without a proportional increase
in actual vapor pressure, widening the deficit [25]. Between 09:00 and 16:00 , GG briefly overtook
SEEED in VPD. This reversal reflected the rural surface’s low thermal inertia that allows rapid
sensible heat to gain once solar radiation increases [26], temporarily raising saturation vapor
pressure faster than actual vapor pressure. By late afternoon, continued solar heating combined
with retained heat from SEEED’s impervious surfaces drove VPD back above GG values.

 Wind speed plays a critical role in moderating the UHI effect and influencing pollutant
concentration by controlling the efficiency of BL ventilation and turbulent mixing. Figure 3
provides context for these processes by illustrating the diurnal evolution of wind speed at both
sites. Mean wind speeds remained low overnight, with minima of 0.32 m/s at GG and 0.41 m/s at
SEEED reached at 05:00 . Wind speeds increased sharply after sunrise, with a maximum of 0.82
m/s at GG and 0.83 m/s at SEEED reached at 12:00 and 14:00 , respectively, before gradually
declining through the evening. Statistical testing (Table S3) indicates that most hourly differences
between the sites are not significant, with the only meaningful differences occurring intermittently
during early morning (01:00-03:00) and late-afternoon hours (16:00-17:00). The pronounced
overlap of the SEM enveloped throughout most of the day further supports this result. The two
sites display highly similar wind speed cycles, suggesting that large-scale atmospheric forcing
largely dominated, while local surface characteristics at SEEED and GG exerted only minor
influence on wind magnitude. Overall, the similarity in wind speed cycles suggests that both GG
and SEEED experienced comparable BL ventilation, implying that differences in UHI intensity
and pollutant buildup were likely driven more by thermal and surface processes than wind
variability.

 Radiative parameters provide insight for diagnosing the magnitude and spatial variability
of the UHI effect. Because emitted longwave radiation and near-surface air temperature both

 12

directly reflect surface energy balance processes [2, 27], they serve as effective indicators of urban
thermal modification. A pronounced UHI is typically characterized by persistently higher daytime
emitted longwave radiation and elevated air temperatures, reflecting the enhanced absorption and
re-emission of solar energy by impervious urban surfaces. Consequently, concurrent analysis of
these two variables provides a robust means of assessing the strength and temporal persistence of
the UHI and offers evidence to support the distinction of the urban-rural disparity between the two
sites.

 This radiative behavior closely parallels the co-evolving air temperature patterns. SEEED
maintained consistently higher temperatures throughout the day, with warmer conditions
persisting overnight and into the early morning hours when GG’s temperatures reached their
minimum. The mean temperature at SEEED (26.61 °C) exceeded that at GG (25.50 °C), a
difference that was statistically meaningful (Table S4) and indicative of sustained thermal
enhancement. Similar to emitted longwave radiation, temperature differences were significant for
nearly all hours except the period of 09:00-13:00 , corresponding again to the morning-to-midday

warmer at night with a minimum of 21.84 ± 1.37 °C and reached a higher daytime maximum of

demonstrates that SEEED and GG more accurately represent the intended urban and rural
conditions, thereby validating their suitability as comparative reference sites.

B. VARIATION OF INORGANIC GASES

 Inorganic gases serve as indicators of combustion processes, atmospheric oxidation
capacity, and photochemical processes that differ across urban and rural environments. CO,
predominantly emitted from incomplete combustion, provides a proxy for primary emissions. NO
and NO2, collectively referred to as NOx, are key regulators of photochemical O3 and largely
modulate radical chemistry in the lower atmosphere. Concentrations of these gases are expected
to differ between urban areas, influenced by dense traffic and industrial activity, and rural sites,
where background emissions dominate. These gases were continuously monitored on both rural
(GG) and urban (SEEED) sites and their diurnal cycles.

 Mean CO concentrations are consistently higher at SEEED, averaging 878.28 ppb relative
to GG, which averages 796.11 ppb. This pattern is consistent with enhanced anthropogenic activity
in urban environments. Both sites display a characteristic daytime decrease in CO, followed by a
nocturnal recovery. This structure reflects daytime photochemical losses, primarily through
oxidation with hydroxyl (OH) radicals [30], coupled with enhanced turbulent mixing within a
deeper daytime BL that dilutes concentrations. At night, the BL collapses and this mixing weakens,
enabling local emissions to accumulate near the surface.

 Mean NO concentrations are identical at both sites, 2.04 ppb, though the timing and
magnitude of peak values differ slightly. GG reaches a peak of 3.25 ppb in the late morning
(10:00), whereas SEEED peaks a bit lower at 3.21 ppb in the mid-afternoon (15:00). These
variations may reflect contrasting source timing (i.e. agricultural equipment in rural regions versus
urban traffic congestion during midday) as well as advection of NO-rich plumes from upwind
corridors, where air carries NO emitted from urban areas toward GG’s measurement site. The
slower decline of NO at GG additionally suggests weaker photochemical processing relative to
SEEED, where higher levels of atmospheric oxidants in the urban environment facilitate more
efficient conversion of NO to NO2 [31].

 16

 Mean NO2 concentrations are significantly higher at SEEED, averaging at 17.99 ppb,
whereas GG averages at 12.6 ppb. Both sites display a pronounced afternoon maximum around
15:00 , consistent with strong photochemical production under peak solar irradiance. In addition
to this afternoon maximum, SEEED exhibits a secondary early morning peak at 06:00 . This pre-
dawn enhancement likely arises from a combination of early morning traffic and the rapid titration
of NO with residual ozone in the absence of sunlight, which elevates nocturnal NO2 concentrations
[32]. Following sunrise, the growth of the BL enhances vertical mixing, rapidly diluting these
concentrations and contributing to the sharp decline after 06:00 .

 The mean O3 concentrations are highly similar between sites, with GG averaging 30.47
ppb and SEEED averaging 30.56 ppb. Additionally, the site’s diurnal profiles show only modest
differences. Because ozone is not directly emitted but rather produced photochemically via
oxidation of NOx and VOCs [33], its spatial distribution may reflect regional atmospheric
chemistry rather than local emissions strength [34]. Furthermore, the expansion of the BL during
daytime and subsequent enhanced transport service to redistribute O3 [35], leading to weaker
spatial contacts between environments compared to primary pollutants.

 Although NO and O3 showed less contrast across the urban-rural gradient, the pronounced
urban signatures of CO and NO2 confirm that inorganic gases remain valuable for characterizing
urbanization.

rural gradient. The observed differences in major BVOCs and AVOCs at the GG and SEEED sites

 The GG site exhibited higher concentrations in BVOCs, with isoprene averaging 0.25 ppb

intuitive pattern. While benzene and toluene are primarily associated with fuel combustion and
expected to be higher at more urbanized sites such as SEEED, benzene concentrations were higher
at GG (0.56 ppb) than SEEED (0.45 ppb) whereas toluene followed the expected trend with
slightly elevated concentrations at SEEED (0.055 ppb versus 0.050 ppb, respectively). Unlike
BVOCs, AVOCs such as benzene and toluene have longer atmospheric lifetimes of approximately
two days [37] due to their aromatic stability, allowing them to be transported over greater distances
and partially decoupling their concentrations from immediate land-use characteristics.

D. ANALYSIS OF THE EXTENDED LIST OF VOCS

 A comprehensive analysis of the full mass spectra for samples collected at GG and SEEED
identified 1,056 peaks ranging from 30 to 205.95 m/z. The peak list was first reduced using a t-
test to identify compounds whose concentrations differed significantly (p < 0.05) between the
blank and the GG and SEEED samples. This analysis revealed 802 and 816 compounds in GG and
SEEED, respectively, with concentrations higher than those in the blank.

 18

 To further refine the list, an additional t-test was performed to identify compounds that
differed significantly between the GG and SEEED sites. Only compounds with concentrations
greater than 5.0 parts per trillion, which is the typical detection limit of the PTR-ToF-MS, were
retained in the final peak list. In total, 243 compounds met these criteria, with 213 and 30
compounds being higher at GG and SEEED, respectively.

 The total VOC concentrations at GG and SEEED were 155.41 ppb and 131.84 ppb,
respectively. Compounds more abundant at GG primarily consisted of hydrocarbons (C xHy) and
oxygenated hydrocarbons (CxHyOz), while compounds elevated at the SEEED site were mostly
nitrogenated species (CxHyOzNw). This pattern is consistent with our previous study (Salvador et
al., 2025), which showed that nitrogenated compounds persist in urban environments and are likely
produced through secondary reactions between NOx and hydrocarbons. These compounds, in
addition to the typical BVOCs and AVOCs, can be further used to differentiate sites with distinct
microclimates.

IV. CONCLUSIONS

 Urban heat islands constitute a critical public health challenge, contributing to increased
heat stress, elevated mortality rates, and deteriorating air quality. Rigorous assessment of UHI
impacts requires establishing a clear delineation between urban and nonurban environments.
During summer 2025, air quality sensors and integrated gas collectors were deployed at two
southeastern U.S. sites to characterize atmospheric composition across a rural-urban gradient
encompassing rural and urban sites. Prior to analyzing atmospheric composition, meteorological
and radiative parameters were examined to verify the existence and magnitude of the gradient
between sites. Vapor pressure, vapor pressure deficit, emitted longwave radiation, air temperature,
and UHI intensity were consistently elevated at SEEED relative to GG, while wind speeds
exhibited comparable magnitudes between GG and SEEED. These systematic differences in
meteorological context confirm that GG serves as a substantially more appropriate rural reference
than CEP site, as originally used in Salvador et al. (2025). Establishing this strong rural-urban
contrast is essential for subsequent interpretation of atmospheric compositions and confident
diagnosis of the urbanization gradient.

 Inorganic gases, notably CO and NO2, exhibited clear spatial stratification with
concentrations significantly elevated at SEEED relative to GG. As a primary pollutant with well-
characterized anthropogenic sources, CO serves as a direct tracer of urban emissions intensity.
However, despite its classification as a secondary pollutant, NO2 similarly demonstrated
pronounced urban enhancement. The diagnostic utility of NO 2 stems from elevated precursors,
thus meriting the utilization of such inorganic gases as a differentiation for urban and rural areas.

 19

 Besides NO2 and CO, PTR-ToF-MS measurements revealed a clear anthropogenic signal
at SEEED and strong biogenic influence at GG. Toluene, emitted predominantly from vehicular
exhaust, industrial solvent use, and fuel evaporation, was distinctly higher at SEEED, reinforcing
its role as a robust AVOC tracer. BVOCs, both isoprene and monoterpene, were elevated at GG
and proved effective at differentiating rural from urban areas. Expanded analysis of all the VOCs
measured at the two sites revealed several compounds, with urban environments recording more
nitrogenated compounds. Accordingly, future work should prioritize detailed characterization of
anthropogenic VOCs, particularly nitrogenated species, which were consistently enhanced in the
urban environment and may provide a more sensitive indication of urban influence than traditional
VOC metrics alone. Importantly, the revised analysis incorporating a true rural reference site
resolved a sharper pollutant gradient and a substantially stronger urban heat island intensity signal,
demonstrating that atmospheric species do provide a robust framework for tracing urbanization
and, in turn, quantifying the urban heat island effect.
