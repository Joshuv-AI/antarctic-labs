The impact of community water fluoridation cessation on health equity and service utilization: a global scoping review
Authors: Man Hung, Corban Ward, Jacob Marx, Megan Nelson, Hanna Chriss, Zachary Smit, Aayushi Shah, Jacob Newman, Alicia Parry, Amir Mohajeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCommunity water fluoridation (CWF) is a widely used population-level intervention for preventing dental caries. However, CWF discontinuation is increasingly debated globally, often without a comprehensive synthesis of the subsequent clinical, service-related, economic, and equity implications. We aimed to systematically map and synthesize empirical evidence regarding dental health and service-based outcomes following CWF cessation.MethodsFor this scoping review, we searched 17 bibliographic databases and grey literature from inception through Oct 31, 2025. Eligible studies reported empirical dental outcomes, including caries indices, service utilization, and treatment costs, before and after CWF cessation or compared outcomes in ceased communities with those in appropriate control communities. Data were extracted independently by multiple reviewers, and overlapping cohorts were linked into evidence clusters to avoid double-counting findings.ResultsNineteen studies (1970–2025) conducted in Europe, North America, Australia, and Israel were included. Findings were heterogeneous across settings and study designs. Jurisdictions without active fluoride replacement programs more frequently reported increases in caries measures, including up to a 75% increase in mean DMFT, and increased treatment utilization, particularly restorative procedures. By contrast, settings with alternative fluoride strategies or broader preventive infrastructure often reported stable outcomes or continued improvements over time. Several studies also reported greater treatment burden and costs, including a 32% increase in caries-related expenditures, among younger children and selected vulnerable populations, including socioeconomically disadvantaged groups and publicly insured children.ConclusionsOral health outcomes following CWF cessation varied across settings and populations. Little change or continued improvement was more commonly observed in settings with broader preventive supports or alternative fluoride strategies, whereas greater treatment burden and less favorable outcomes were more frequently reported among younger or vulnerable populations and in settings with limited preventive supports. More rigorous comparative research is needed to clarify these relationships and inform policy decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09007-w.Keywords: Community water fluoridation, Fluoridation cessation, Dental caries, Health equity, Service utilization

Background

Dental caries is a critical yet often overlooked component of the global non-communicable disease burden, imposing substantial health and economic costs across the life course [1, 2]. Although largely preventable, untreated caries is associated with systemic sequelae, including pain, infection, impaired nutrition, reduced quality of life, and avoidable healthcare expenditures that strain public health budgets [3–5]. The burden of caries is strongly socially patterned; structural inequities contribute to disproportionate disease prevalence and severity among children and families experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage [6]. Reducing oral health inequities therefore requires coordinated strategies across individual, clinical, community, and population levels, with population-based preventive interventions serving as an important complement to clinical care.

Community water fluoridation (CWF), the controlled adjustment of fluoride concentration in public drinking water, is a widely used population-level strategy for preventing dental caries. CWF has been associated with reductions in caries burden, although a recent systematic review suggested that the magnitude of effect may be smaller than estimates reported in earlier studies [7]. Because CWF is delivered through public water systems, it provides a population-level preventive exposure that does not require direct engagement with dental services or adherence to individual treatment regimens. Its effects, however, occur within broader social and behavioral contexts, including patterns of water consumption and dietary factors that influence caries risk. Evidence suggests that CWF may help reduce socioeconomic disparities in dental caries by providing a preventive measure that is broadly accessible across populations [8–10]. Major international health organizations, including the World Health Organization (WHO) and the FDI World Dental Federation, have endorsed CWF as a population-level public health strategy for caries prevention [11, 12]. Although evidence syntheses suggest that CWF is associated with reductions in dental caries and may improve oral health outcomes across population groups [7], evidence specifically examining its impact on oral health inequities remains comparatively limited.

Despite these longstanding endorsements, CWF remains the subject of ongoing public, political, and scientific debate in several jurisdictions [13]. Decisions about whether to continue or discontinue CWF have often been shaped by competing perspectives on public health ethics, autonomy, perceived health risks, costs, and public acceptability. Common concerns include individual autonomy and informed consent, as well as potential adverse health effects; however, evidence reviews and public health organizations have generally concluded that CWF is safe at recommended levels and can provide population-level oral health benefits [14, 15]. Supporters of CWF emphasize its role as a broadly accessible preventive strategy intended to reduce caries burden and improve equity in access to prevention [8, 16, 17]. At the same time, the widespread adoption of fluoride toothpaste and other fluoride delivery methods has coincided with substantial declines in caries prevalence [18], leading some stakeholders to question the relative contribution of CWF within contemporary prevention systems. These policy decisions are important because replacement strategies differ in cost, accessibility, reach, and reliance on individual adherence, often in the absence of a comprehensive evidence base on the broader clinical and economic consequences of cessation. Communities undergoing CWF cessation therefore provide natural experiments, but interpretation of post-cessation outcomes is complex. Oral health is influenced by dietary patterns, access to professional services, background fluoride exposure, and broader social determinants of health. Existing evidence suggests that the consequences of fluoridation cessation may not be uniformly distributed across populations and could disproportionately affect groups facing greater barriers to preventive care [10]. Emerging evidence from historical and contemporary post-cessation settings further suggests that outcomes may vary by age, preventive environment, fluoride exposure, and socioeconomic context [19–21].

Although substantial research has evaluated fluoridation initiation, synthesized evidence regarding the multidimensional impacts of CWF withdrawal remains limited [10, 17]. Clinical measures such as DMFT and DMFS remain central indicators of caries burden and may be sensitive to changes in disease prevalence, whereas service-based outcomes provide complementary information on healthcare utilization, treatment intensity, and downstream system burden. In recent years, administrative datasets and electronic health records have increasingly enabled evaluation of restorative procedures, emergency visits, treatment under general anesthesia (GA), and treatment-related costs, offering an additional perspective on the broader consequences of fluoridation cessation [22]. Much of the relevant evidence also remains in grey literature and government surveillance reports, making comprehensive synthesis and interpretation challenging.

To address these gaps, we systematically mapped the global evidence base on CWF cessation across clinical, economic, health-system, and equity domains. By integrating findings from traditional clinical studies and contemporary administrative datasets, the review identifies patterns, contextual factors, and evidence gaps related to fluoridation withdrawal. The primary objective was to synthesize the post-cessation evidence landscape and inform policymakers and public health stakeholders about the potential implications of fluoridation discontinuation across settings.

Methods

This scoping review followed Joanna Briggs Institute methodological guidance for scoping reviews and was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The protocol was established a priori to ensure methodological transparency. The review was structured according to the Population–Concept–Context framework. The population of interest included individuals of any age living in communities that had previously implemented CWF and subsequently discontinued it. The concept of interest was CWF cessation as a population-level public health exposure, including its clinical, service-related, economic, and equity-related consequences. The context encompassed any geographic location or jurisdiction where CWF had been discontinued, regardless of the reason for cessation or the duration of prior fluoridation.

Studies were eligible if they presented empirical data comparing dental health outcomes following CWF cessation. Eligible studies compared outcomes before and after cessation within the same community over time or compared ceased communities with appropriate controls. All study designs were considered, including longitudinal cohorts, ecological analyses, cross-sectional surveys, time-series analyses, and retrospective analyses of administrative health records. Grey literature sources, including technical government reports and public health surveillance summaries, were included. Studies focusing solely on attitudes, chemical water analysis without health outcomes, or non-CWF fluoride exposures were excluded. Studies reporting only intermediate or mechanistic outcomes (e.g., microbiological or biochemical measures) without clinical or service-based dental health outcomes were also excluded. The full eligibility criteria, including specific inclusion and exclusion rationales for study design and publication type, are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

A comprehensive search strategy was developed in collaboration with a health sciences librarian to maximize sensitivity. Electronic searches were conducted in 17 bibliographic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and CINAHL. Search terms combined controlled vocabulary, including MeSH and Emtree, and free-text terms related to “water fluoridation,” “cessation,” and “dental health outcomes,” using Boolean operators, truncation, and proximity operators. No language or date restrictions were imposed to support global evidence mapping. The full reproducible search strategy for all 17 databases is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Where feasible, non-English records were screened using translated titles and abstracts, and full texts were translated as needed for inclusion. Final searches were completed in October 2025.

To capture evidence outside traditional academic channels, grey literature searches included targeted queries in Google Scholar, OpenGrey, and relevant government and public health websites. International, national, and regional agencies were prioritized, including the WHO, FDI World Dental Federation, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Public Health Agency of Canada, and equivalent bodies in jurisdictions with known cessation events. Conference abstracts from organizations such as the International Association for Dental Research were also screened. All searches were documented by date, source, exact search terms, and number of records retrieved.

Search results were imported into EndNote reference management software, and duplicates were removed. To minimize bias, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers selected from a pool of five reviewers, followed by independent full-text screening by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. The study selection process was documented using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram. Before screening, reviewers pilot-tested the eligibility criteria on a sample of records to ensure consistent interpretation and application. Data were extracted using a standardized form that captured bibliographic details, study characteristics, population demographics, CWF exposure history, reasons for cessation, comparators, dental health outcomes, direction and magnitude of changes, concurrent interventions or confounders, key findings, and reported limitations. To ensure data integrity, extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using the standardized form, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. When multiple publications reported on the same cessation event and study population, records were linked and treated as a single evidence cluster to prevent double-counting. These overlapping cohorts and linked publications are documented in Supplementary Table 3.

Findings were synthesized descriptively and presented in narrative and tabular formats. Studies were grouped by geographic region, population age group, follow-up period, and outcome type. The direction of change in dental health outcomes was summarized, and, where available, the magnitude of observed changes was reported to provide context for interpretation. Contextual factors that could explain variability were noted, and study limitations were summarized. Consistent with scoping review methodology, formal risk-of-bias assessment was not conducted. However, methodological characteristics and key limitations reported in the included studies were systematically charted to support interpretation of the synthesized evidence. The review protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework (DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/PQFWB).

Results

Study selection

Systematic searches of 17 bibliographic databases and grey literature sources identified 9,585 records before deduplication. After duplicate removal, 5,857 unique records underwent title and abstract screening, from which 5,565 were excluded. Full-text assessment was conducted for 292 records, and 276 were excluded, primarily because they represented duplicate publications (n = 204) or did not evaluate CWF cessation (n = 55) (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the 19 included studies published between 1970 and 2025, representing evidence from Europe, North America, Australia, and Israel [19–21, 23–38]. Cessation events occurred between 1960 and 2014 and reflected diverse administrative, legal, policy, and infrastructure-related circumstances [28, 31, 37]. The evidence base included both early post-cessation investigations and more recent administrative and population-based studies [33, 34, 37], reflecting changes in available data sources and study approaches over time. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across study designs and data sources, including repeated cross-sectional clinical surveys, longitudinal cohort studies, ecological analyses, retrospective administrative claims analyses, and electronic health record-based investigations. More recent studies increasingly incorporated administrative datasets and claims-based analyses alongside traditional clinical examinations. Study populations were predominantly pediatric, with particular focus on children and socially or structurally vulnerable populations, including Medicaid-enrolled cohorts, Indigenous communities, and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. Because these studies focused largely on specific pediatric and vulnerable populations, findings may not be generalizable to broader populations. Post-cessation follow-up periods ranged from approximately 2 to 15 years [28, 38].

Direction of effects following CWF cessation

Table 4 summarizes the reported direction and magnitude of changes following CWF cessation. Findings were heterogeneous across settings and study designs. Several studies reported increases in measures of dental disease or treatment utilization following cessation. In Scotland, repeated survey studies documented increases in caries measures among children, including a rise in mean DMFT among 10-year-olds from 2.0 to 3.5 (+75%) approximately three years after cessation [24]. Similarly, early observations from Wisconsin and later investigations from Calgary reported increases in caries measures, particularly for primary dentition outcomes among younger children [19–21]. Administrative analyses from Israel and Alaska also identified increases in treatment utilization, including approximately 13% to 18% increases in restorative procedures and a 32% increase in annual caries-related costs among Medicaid-enrolled children [32, 33, 37].

Primary findings and direction of dental health outcomes following community water fluoridation cessation

Other studies reported stable findings or continued declines in caries measures following cessation. Repeated survey studies from Finland and the Netherlands reported stable findings or continued decreases in selected caries outcomes over time, whereas studies from Germany and Cuba reported low caries levels after cessation in settings where alternative fluoride programs had been implemented [28, 30, 31, 35, 38].

Additional findings extended beyond caries measures. In British Columbia, fluorosis prevalence decreased following cessation, with the proportion of children exhibiting dental fluorosis declining from 58% to 24% among examined exposure cohorts [27]. Lower fluorosis prevalence was also reported in Calgary following cessation compared with a continuously fluoridated comparison community [21]. In contrast, a temporary 11-month interruption of fluoridation in North Carolina was not associated with measurable differences in caries outcomes [26]. Given heterogeneity in study designs, follow-up duration, populations, and preventive contexts, findings should be interpreted in light of individual study characteristics and reported limitations.

Equity and vulnerability-related findings

Table 5 summarizes findings stratified by vulnerability indicators. Evidence on differences across population groups was identified across several study designs and settings. Age-related patterns were reported in several studies, with adverse changes more frequently observed among younger children and cohorts whose teeth erupted after cessation [23–25]. Socioeconomic differences were also reported, with claims-based analyses from Israel and Alaska identifying greater treatment burden among publicly insured populations [32, 33, 37]. Repeated cross-sectional studies from Calgary further suggested that adverse post-cessation outcomes may be more pronounced among children from materially or socially disadvantaged communities [21].

Equity and vulnerability-related findings following discontinuation of community water fluoridation (CWF)

Evidence from remote and Indigenous communities further underscored equity concerns. In Australia, a rapid rebound in dental decay was documented in a remote Indigenous community following infrastructure failure. Caries levels stabilized only after the introduction of active preventive interventions, including selective fissure sealants and annual applications of povidone-iodine and fluoride varnish [29].

Discussion

Principal findings

This scoping review mapped evidence from 19 empirical studies examining dental health outcomes following CWF discontinuation across diverse geographic and policy contexts. Across heterogeneous study designs, populations, and post-cessation settings, several studies reported increases in caries burden, treatment utilization, and healthcare costs following cessation, whereas others reported stable outcomes or continued declines in caries measures. Variation in findings appeared to align with differences in preventive environments, population characteristics, and broader healthcare contexts, although causal interpretation remains limited.

Contextual factors and replacement strategies

The reviewed studies demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in post-cessation outcomes, ranging from worsening clinical indicators to stable outcomes across settings. Studies reporting stable findings frequently occurred in settings characterized by alternative fluoride delivery mechanisms or comprehensive preventive infrastructure, including fluoridated salt programs, school-based prevention initiatives, and broader public dental services. Because the included evidence was primarily observational and varied substantially in design, these observations should be interpreted as contextual patterns rather than causal relationships. By contrast, in settings where no active, universal replacement strategies were implemented, studies from Scotland in the 1980s and 1990s reported increases in disease prevalence and treatment intensity following CWF cessation [23–25]. Similar patterns were observed in a remote Australian Indigenous community during the 2000s and 2010s, where caries measures increased after cessation and before active preventive interventions were introduced [29]. These observations suggest that post-cessation outcomes may be shaped by broader preventive contexts and health-system factors.

Life-course patterns and vulnerability

Age-related patterns emerged across the post-cessation literature, with younger children and cohorts whose teeth erupted after cessation more often represented among populations experiencing adverse outcomes. Findings from Calgary suggested that these changes may be particularly apparent for primary dentition outcomes among younger children. Administrative data from Alaska showed increases in mean caries-related procedures among Medicaid-enrolled children from 2.02 to 2.35 per child overall and from 2.01 to 2.68 among children younger than 7 years following cessation [33]. Similarly, data from Israel demonstrated an approximately two-fold increase in treatment burden, including restorations and stainless-steel crowns, among children aged 3–5 years receiving treatment under GA or deep sedation after fluoridation cessation, with larger increases among younger age groups without early-life CWF exposure [34]. However, the available evidence cannot determine the extent to which these observations reflect fluoridation cessation itself, broader secular trends, or concurrent changes in preventive environments.

Equity-related findings

Equity-related outcomes were examined in a smaller and methodologically diverse subset of studies. Across these studies, increases in treatment burden or disease measures were more frequently reported among vulnerable populations, including lower socioeconomic groups, publicly insured children, and Indigenous communities. However, evidence on equity-related outcomes was limited and heterogeneous in design, measurement, and context. The available findings suggest that CWF cessation may have differential implications across population groups, but further research using robust comparative designs is required to better characterize these relationships.

Potential trade-offs following cessation

Evidence on fluorosis outcomes was limited but suggested that fluorosis prevalence may decrease following cessation in some settings. More recent investigations also reported lower fluorosis prevalence in post-cessation settings while simultaneously observing heterogeneous caries-related findings. The broader implications of these findings remain uncertain because few studies examined fluorosis outcomes, and direct comparisons between potential benefits and harms were not consistently evaluated.

Implications for research and policy

Important gaps remain in the current evidence base. Included studies varied substantially in design, outcomes assessed, follow-up duration, and contextual characteristics, limiting direct comparison across settings. Future research would benefit from standardized approaches to evaluating post-cessation outcomes, including both clinical and service-related measures, explicit assessment of equity-related outcomes, and careful measurement of contextual factors that may influence observed patterns.

From a policy perspective, the mapped evidence suggests that fluoridation cessation decisions occur within broader health-system and preventive environments. Policymakers should consider whether accessible, effective, and sustainable replacement strategies are in place, particularly for children and populations facing barriers to preventive dental care. These contextual factors are essential for interpreting findings across jurisdictions and evaluating future policy decisions.

Limitations and strengths

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Most included studies were observational, limiting causal inference and leaving findings vulnerable to residual confounding from secular trends, concurrent policy shifts, and changes in background fluoride exposure or preventive behaviors. Heterogeneity in outcome measures, surveillance intensity, and analytic approaches across time periods further constrained direct comparability. In addition, evidence from low- and middle-income countries remains sparse, limiting external validity in settings with different baseline disease burdens and health-system capacities.

As a scoping review, this study was designed to map the available evidence rather than formally evaluate effectiveness or establish causal relationships. Formal risk-of-bias assessment was not conducted, consistent with scoping review methodology. Nevertheless, the review provides a broad synthesis of evidence across clinical, service-based, economic, and equity-related domains and identifies important gaps for future investigation.

Conclusions

Findings following CWF cessation varied across settings and populations and appeared to be shaped by broader preventive and healthcare contexts. Communities with established preventive systems or alternative fluoride strategies more often reported stable outcomes or continued improvement, whereas adverse changes and increased treatment burden were more frequently observed in settings lacking such supports and among younger or vulnerable populations. Substantial heterogeneity across studies limits causal interpretation and underscores the need for rigorous comparative research across diverse settings and populations.
