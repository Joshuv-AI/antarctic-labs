L-DNA calibrators for PCR amplicon characterization
Authors: Nicholas Spurlock, Frederick R Haselton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is powerful on its own, its utility is markedly improved by post-PCR techniques like melt analysis and target quantification. We have previously utilized L-DNA stereoisomers matching the sequences of the reaction primers and target amplicon to increase PCR sensitivity and specificity in the presence of interferents. In this report we describe using L-DNA sequences as internal calibrators to confirm amplicon specificity and estimate initial target concentration. An S. mitis reaction with high analytical sensitivity was used to collect real-time PCR and melt data. In addition, melt data for end-labeled L-DNA and amplicon intercalator signal were acquired continuously during the PCR reaction and reinterpreted in post-PCR analysis. For melt analysis, melt curves were analyzed to produce a unitless melt signature via comparison to the L-DNA calibrator strands. These melt signatures were then converted to temperature using the different but known melt temperatures of the L-DNA calibrators. The S. mitis initial target concentration was estimated in three steps. First, the amplitudes of the melt peaks corresponding to the amplicon and the L-DNA high temperature calibrator were extracted from the melt data in each cycle. Second, the L-DNA melt derivative peak height was set as a known-concentration standard to convert intercalator fluorescence from the amplicon intercalator melt peak into amplicon concentration. Third, a sigmoidal model of efficiency decay estimated reaction efficiencies and initial S. mitis target concentrations from the amplicon melt derivative peak heights at later cycles. The S. mitis amplicon melt signature was found to be a consistent 0.8143 ± 0.0058 (mean ± std, n = 16) with a coefficient of variation of 0.7%. After translation to temperature, these values were equivalent to 84.96 ± 0.10 °C. Initial target concentration estimates were accurate above 102 copies/µL, with a coefficient of variation of 120% for 102 and a relatively constant 22% for the other concentrations. These proof-of-concept results suggest that the addition of L-DNA strands can serve as internal calibrators for single-sample characterization of a PCR amplicon.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44331-026-00022-w.Keywords: Amplicon characterization, Quantitative PCR, Melt analysis, L-DNA stereoisomers, Hybridization control

Introduction

Post-PCR melt analysis and analysis of the time course of fluorescence have made PCR markedly more powerful. Post-PCR melt analysis in many workflows now confirms amplicon identity as a means to distinguish specific products from spurious ones by their characteristic Tm profiles. Additionally, quantitative PCR estimates the initial target concentration from the cycle threshold (Cq) by comparing the end-cycle PCR fluorescence values of an unknown sample to known standards in parallel reactions. However, both of these analysis methods require specialized equipment or comparator samples, preventing their use in single-sample applications.

We have previously used L-DNA’s combination of thermodynamic fidelity and biological orthogonality in a prototype instrument design as a real-time hybridization surrogate to control PCR cycling [1–3], and as an internal comparator for single-sample melt analysis on a standard PCR instrument [4, 5]. Both of these applications are possible because the hybridization thermodynamics of D-DNA reagents and targets are effectively mirrored by L-DNA stereoisomers, while biological interactions are avoided. That is, the addition of L-DNA acts as an internal, reagent-based standard that reports on DNA hybridization or melting within the same tube as the analyte but without interfering with amplification or target binding [1, 4, 5].

Most recently, we reported a new modification of a prototype instrument incorporating a 1370 nm laser diode as a PCR cycling heat source and simultaneous fluorescence channel measurement capabilities [3]. As part of the development of this prototype, we increased sampling frequency for the fluorescence channels tenfold to improve the fluorescence-based control algorithm. Unexpectedly, we observed that even though the cycle control L-DNA melt peak remains constant in all cycles, the amplitude of the melt peak corresponding to the D-DNA amplicon increased with PCR cycle, and appeared to be proportional to the amplicon concentration within the reaction.

In this proof-of-concept report, the L-DNA initially developed for cycling control was used to characterize the amplicon melt temperature and follow up on this unexpected observation as a novel means to estimate the initial copies in a single reaction.

PCR reaction protocols

All S. mitis PCR reactions were performed using 1X Luna master mix (Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix, New England Biolabs Catalog #M3003S), and prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions in standard 0.2 mL PCR tubes (ThermoFisher Scientific #AB-0620, Waltham, MA, USA). Both primers were added at a final concentration of 400 nM each (all sequences are listed in Table 1). L-DNA calibrators were added at final concentrations of 100 nM for each fluorescently labeled sequence and 300 nM for each quencher sequence. These values were chosen as they were the best guarantee of matching primer annealing behavior while maintaining high fluorescence signal [2]. This sets the maximum double-stranded L-DNA concentration used as a standard in the quantification experiments at 100 nM. Serial dilutions of the synthetic S. mitis target were prepared at concentrations from 103 to 106 copies/µL, with 1 µM (dT)21(sequences consisting of 21 consecutive thymine bases) added to each serial dilution to mitigate potential binding events with the tube walls [7]. These dilutions were added to the 20 µL reaction volume to create known initial sample concentrations of 102 to 105copies/µL (n = 4 per concentration). NTCs were prepared with molecular grade water instead of adding target. A layer of 10 µL of mineral oil was added to each sample to prevent evaporation, and all samples were run for 40 PCR cycles, using the L-DNA calibrator strands to establish switch points as in our previous publications [2, 3]. At the end of this cycling, one additional cycle using the same L-DNA control was carried out to obtain the melt temperature of the final product.

Real-time acquisition and storage of end-label and intercalator fluorescence measurements for analysis

All PCR assays were conducted using the previously described 1370 nm prototype PCR system [3]. L-DNA hybridization control for both annealing and melting was accomplished by measuring the fluorescence of the end-labeled L-DNA sequences listed in Table 1 using the detector’s “orange” channel, which has an excitation wavelength of 565 nm and a detection wavelength of 625 nm. SYBR Green intercalating signal was collected using the detector’s “green” channel, which has an excitation wavelength of 470 nm and a detection wavelength of 520 nm, along with the UNIX timestamp at which this data is acquired. These measurements were taken continuously during each cycle, with the Texas Red signal acquisition beginning when the PCR reaction was initiated, while the SYBR Green signal was acquired from cycle 11 onward. On average, this results in around 36 thousand end-label and 26 thousand intercalator data points, for a total average file size of 865 kilobytes for each sample. Because this prototype instrument has simultaneous signal detection hardware, real-time PCR fluorescence of the amplicon was obtained using the SYBR intercalating signal, measured at the melt peak of the L-DNA low temperature calibrator instead of when the L-DNA low temperature calibrator was fully annealed as in previous reports [3]. The PCR fluorescence measurements were background subtracted using the NTCs, and normalized to a scale of 0 to 100. The prototype PCR instrument collects a melt curve of the end-labeled L-DNA sequences every cycle, but to collect the intercalating signal and the end-label signal simultaneously, minor software modifications were necessary. Critically, the same confocal detector was used to collect both the end-label and intercalator fluorescence to ensure simultaneous detection. Therefore, the first of these modifications was changing the fluorescence collection protocol for the detector to collect both signals simultaneously. To ensure reliable cycling control during melting phases, one other software modification was made to the control system. Because the laser heating method has been previously established to have remarkable consistency across heating and cooling cycles [3], a simplified control method based on this observation was used in this study. The hybridization control algorithm was modified to use set heating and cooling times after the initial cycling characteristics were determined. The instrument stores the heating and cooling times for each cycle, and the heating and cooling times for cycles 6–10 were averaged and used to set the duration of heating and cooling for the remaining 30 cycles instead of continuing to use the L-DNA hybridization control over all cycles, as described in the previous report utilizing this instrument [3].

Post-PCR analysis of cycling data to calculate amplicon melt

As shown in the second row of Fig. 1, to show consistency of L-DNA calibrated melt analysis in samples with the same target at different initial target concentrations, melt analysis was performed for each sample in the standard curve using the L-DNA calibrators.

Extraction of L-DNA end-label and amplicon fluorescence data for each cycle

The two L-DNA calibrator peaks in each melt were determined by calculating the derivative of the Texas Red end-label fluorescence using a finite difference method with a five-point stencil [8], and a function consisting of two Gaussians and a linear offset was fitted to the resulting values. The melt peaks for the amplicon were extracted in a similar fashion, using the SYBR Green intercalating dye rather than the end-label signal. However, due to the higher baseline resulting from the high background of the SYBR dye, a background derivative value for the SYBR fluorescence was calculated by averaging the fluorescence derivative in the first 10 s of each melt, and this background value was subtracted from the derivative signal before fitting the two Gaussian functions. Because the intercalator is not specific, multiple intercalator peaks are detected during melt. As the S. mitis target sequence was predicted to have a lower melting temperature than the L-DNA high temperature calibrator, the lower temperature SYBR melt peak was identified as that of the amplicon sequence. Similarly, the specific end-label signal from the high temperature L-DNA calibrator was used instead of its intercalator signal for peak position during melt analysis.

Calculation of characteristic amplicon melt signature

Since this instrument does not measure temperature, the melt time for the amplicon in each cycle was standardized to a “melt signature” using the L-DNA calibrator peaks. The melt signature was calculated by subtracting the melt time for the low L-DNA calibrator peak from the melt time from the target peak, and dividing the resulting number by the time difference between the high and low L-DNA calibrator peaks. Thus, the melt signature value is expressed as a decimal value between 0 and 1, where 0 would have a melt time equivalent to the low temperature L-DNA calibrator, and 1 would have a melt time equivalent to the high temperature L-DNA calibrator. Because intercalator signal increases with increasing temperature while end-label signal decreases, comparisons are made by reporting the absolute value of their derivatives. To guarantee the best resolution of the amplicon melt peak, amplicon melt signature was calculated from the average amplicon melt signature of the last five PCR cycles.

Thermocouple calibration

Although the prototype PCR instrument does not measure temperature, in this report the melt signatures derived from L-DNA hybridization measurements were also translated into melt temperatures to allow comparison with existing temperature-based approaches. This translation required measurement of the melt temperature of the L-DNA calibrators. Melt temperatures were measured by inserting a K-Type thermocouple wire into the center of a post-PCR no-template control (NTC) sample using a 3D printed holder, and placing the sample in the 1370 nm prototype PCR system for 5 melts at a laser power of 1.35 W. L-DNA melt times for each double-stranded L-DNA sequence were extracted using the same Gaussian fitting method described above. The L-DNA melt temperature for each sequence was defined as the average of the temperature values measured within 40 ms of the respective Gaussian peak. This segment of data usually contained 5 or 6 temperature values for each peak. These temperatures were used as the endpoints for the melt signature measurement described above, assuming a relatively linear ramp rate between each L-DNA peak. The melt temperature was calculated by multiplying the melt signature by the difference between the two endpoint temperatures and adding the lower endpoint temperature. The thermocouple itself was calibrated with an ice-water slurry that was heated until boiling. Synchronized temperature data was acquired using an ADS1115 analog-to-digital converter (Product #1085, Adafruit, New York City, NY, USA).

The thermocouple-derived melt temperatures were also used to calculate the average heating rate of the instrument by subtracting the melt temperature of the low temperature calibrator from the melt temperature of the high temperature calibrator, and dividing by the time difference between them.

Post-PCR analysis of cycling data to estimate initial target concentration

As depicted in the third row of Fig. 1, estimating the initial S. mitis target concentration was done in three steps.

Extraction of amplicon fluorescence data and calculation of amplicon peak amplitude

First, a constant double-stranded concentration of the L-DNA high temperature calibrator was added to every reaction as described in the PCR reaction methods. Using the SYBR Green melt signal, the derivative peak obtained for this L-DNA calibrator was used as a known copy number standard to convert the amplicon intercalator fluorescence into amplicon concentration. Because the amplicon and L-DNA intercalator melt peaks overlap at later cycles, the L-DNA intercalator melt peak amplitude from earlier cycles was used. Therefore, the derivative amplitude values from cycles 11–15 were chosen to ensure that the PCR target peak was not yet present. These are the first five cycles where melts are recorded by the software, as active cycle control cannot be done at the same time as melt analysis. A single Gaussian with a linear offset was fit to the derivative intercalator fluorescence data in each of these five cycles, and the resulting amplitude values were averaged to reduce cycle-to-cycle variation in the amplitude measurement. Once the PCR amplicon peak appeared, a fit focusing on the amplicon peak itself, rather than the merged peaks, was used to measure the amplitude of the amplicon derivative peak.

Estimation of amplicon concentration at each cycle

Second, to convert amplicon intercalator fluorescence to concentration, the SYBR amplicon derivative peak amplitude was divided by the L-DNA high temperature calibrator derivative amplitude obtained in cycles 11–15. The proportionality of the two derivative peaks was multiplied by the known L-DNA double-stranded concentration of 100 nM to obtain a concentration estimate at that cycle. This could be performed at every cycle, but to improve consistency of the estimate, early cycles were avoided because of low signal to noise ratio, and later cycles were avoided to decrease the potential impact of intercalator saturation. Therefore, the first five cycles with fit-determinable amplicon melt peaks were chosen. These five concentration estimates were divided by their respective normalized PCR fluorescence values and averaged to produce a conversion between concentration and fluorescence. Amplicon concentration for each detectable cycle outside of the chosen calibration region was estimated by multiplying the PCR fluorescence value by its constant sample-specific conversion factor.

Calculation of initial amplicon concentration

In the third step, initial copies in a sample were estimated from the copies at later cycles via a simplistic sigmoidal decay model. The model used assumed that amplicon concentration at each cycle is a function of pre-PCR concentration (C0) and reaction efficiency (E), and that PCR efficiency decreases from a maximum of 2 as PCR products accumulate [9, 10]. With these assumptions, the concentration at cycle X can be estimated from an initial concentration C0 using Eq. 1 if the efficiency E at every preceding cycle n is known.

Solving for C0 by dividing both sides by the cumulative efficiency product gives an estimate of initial concentration. The efficiency at each cycle was estimated using Eq. 2. This relationship describes a reaction that has a first-cycle efficiency of 2 that decays to 1 as the reaction proceeds.

In this equation, n is the cycle number, and B is a parameter that describes the decay rate of the efficiency. Here the simplest case is assumed, namely that B is constant and positive. The latter assumption bounds the resulting efficiency between 2 and 1 across all cycles. With the exception of the C0 values, which ranged from 102 to 105copies/µL, all PCR reactions were set up in the same way. The efficiency is reduced over cycles by the interactions of all reaction components. To avoid baseline noise and simultaneously avoid potential intercalator saturation at later cycles and higher concentrations, the reaction characteristics for a single amplicon concentration range is required [11, 12]. Based on the concentration estimate from the second step, B was estimated for each sample by finding the efficiency of the reaction when the product was estimated to be 50 nM and solving Eq. 2 for B.

Discussion

The example S. mitis PCR reaction produced the expected real-time PCR results with clear Cq separation as a function of initial target concentration and a single amplicon melt peak. However, the major focus of this report is on post-PCR analysis. The analysis of these experiments suggests that L-DNA sequences can be used as internal calibrators to enhance post-PCR analysis, enabling both target validation and estimation of initial target copy number.

Post-PCR melt analysis displayed a high degree of consistency, both in replicate melt runs of the same sample and across multiple samples of the same target. Replicate melts of a single S. mitis sample were closely packed at a melt signature value of 0.8061 ± 0.0033 and calibrated temperatures of 84.80 °C ± 0.06 °C (Fig. 3). The melt signature measurements across all 16 positive PCR samples are also tightly packed at 0.8143 ± 0.0058, and 84.96 ± 0.10 °C after translation to temperature. Three key factors enable the rapid, intra-cycle melt-analysis protocol without losing accuracy: high homogeneity of the L-DNA strands, rapid homogenous photothermal heating, and continuous fluorescence monitoring. First, the L-DNA strands are uniformly distributed within the reaction matrix. In bulk, the fluorescence response of these L-DNA strands is a more precise measurement of the thermal state of the sample volume than the external or estimated measurements of traditional PCR systems. The linear interpolation of the melt peaks from these constant concentration standards is used to control for run-to-run variation in ramp rate and temperature start point. Secondly, this heating method overcomes the distortion of peaks due to intrasample thermal gradients previously reported by others [13]. The infrared heating method in this instrument heats the sample internally and more uniformly to reduce these thermal gradients [3]. Finally, the continuous fluorescence monitoring embedded in the L-DNA controlled PCR system enables melt analysis simultaneous to PCR cycling instead of requiring an additional end-reaction melt step. This provided multiple melt curves that were combined into one analysis without meaningfully changing runtime.

In post-PCR initial copy estimation, the L-DNA control strands are shown to serve as concentration calibrators, accurately estimating initial concentration in samples down to 103 copies/µL, using only data collected for each sample. As observed in the initial experiments, while the increasing target concentration did not noticeably affect the horizontal location of the product melt peak, it did affect the amplitude. The increase in derivative peak amplitude of the target melt peak combined with the presence of the constant L-DNA high temperature calibrator melt peak (Fig. 4) enabled estimation of amplicon concentration for each cycle (Fig. 5, upper panel). In this usage, the L-DNA derivative peak is a constant indicator of the amount of fluorescence produced by a known quantity of double-stranded DNA within the sample vial, controlling for fluorescence gain and variations in sample opacity. In these experiments, this known quantity was 100 nM of complementary double-stranded L-DNA. Conversion of fluorescence to concentration preserved the tightly bunched replicates of the fluorescence measurements (Fig. 2) while diminishing variation in plateau height, reflecting the expected similar yields from the same reaction once it reached completion (Fig. 5, upper panel). Interestingly, the plateaus themselves were around 150 nM, significantly below the 400 nM that would be reached if the sample reached primer exhaustion. This reduced yield could be due to several factors, including polymerase or dye saturation, SYBR Green inhibition, or the decreased annealing time of the rapid L-DNA PCR instrument. Some dye saturation was observed, at least with the derivative melt peaks, as the ratio between melt peak amplitude and PCR fluorescence began to decrease as the target concentration increased. However, this was compensated for by using values early in the exponential phase to calculate the conversion factor, which was made possible by the melt fluorescence available in every cycle, rather than as an end-point analysis. A potential avenue of improving the accuracy of these concentration estimates could be achieved by adding a secondary long L-DNA double strand at a different concentration. This change would enable a two-point calibration, in which the melt peak amplitude of the unknown target could be interpolated between the two known bookends, as opposed to the simplistic single point calibration currently performed. Two-point calibration would diminish cycle-to-cycle variation and possibly help with dye saturation effects.

These concentration estimates from the height of the amplicon melt derivative peak were then used with the sigmoidal model of efficiency decay to estimate initial target concentration, returning estimates within half an order of magnitude for all sample groups with initial concentrations of 103 copies/µL or greater. However, these estimates exhibited a high degree of variation in the 102 copies/µL samples (Fig. 5, lower panel), with the coefficient of variation increasing from around 22% for the 3 higher concentrations to 120% for 102 copies/µL. This variance at low concentrations may be due to errors in efficiency estimation. The calculation model used in these experiments has two primary advantages. First, it requires no foreknowledge or previous experiments, only assuming that efficiency begins at 2 and decreases over PCR cycling. Second, it accounts for decaying efficiency over cycles, enabling a choice of threshold far enough above the baseline to avoid noise. However, efficiency estimates from this model rely on two specific features of the PCR curve and will lose accuracy if either is compromised. The reaction used in these experiments has an efficiency of 1.98, close to the perfect efficiency of 2 that was assumed in Eq. 2 for cycle 1 and is thus unlikely to impact accuracy. A less efficient reaction would increase error in the initial copy estimate, as the cycle 1 efficiency would start further from 2 and thus decrease the accuracy of the efficiency calculations. The calibration point chosen during amplification is also vulnerable to noise in fluorescence measurements, which can introduce error in the calculation of the efficiency decay rate B. Thus, it is imperative that a point with a large absolute change be chosen to reduce the impact of any noise on the efficiency calculation. More advanced methods that rely on fewer assumptions would require techniques like nonlinear fitting that have their own vulnerabilities, such as poor initial guesses or improper weighting. In all cases, difficulty estimating efficiency is compounded by the high degree of sensitivity seen in the measurement. Since Eq. 1 contains the product of the efficiencies at every cycle, the error compounds exponentially. A change as small as 10% in the efficiency estimate, from 1.50 to 1.55, can cause a threefold underestimation of pre-PCR target concentration if the minimum detectable concentration occurs at cycle 35. Many nonlinear fitting methods have wider confidence intervals than this range on their coefficients, especially when few reaction properties are available to confine the other factors in the fitted equation. This sensitivity is likely one of the causes of the difficulties with the 102 copies/µL samples: noise errors compounded across a large number of cycles. Because of the exponential effect of the efficiency estimate, an improvement in efficiency calculations remains critical for increasing the accuracy of initial concentration estimates.

Beyond mathematical challenges and shortcomings, there are some practical limitations on this methodology. First, the fluorescence of a known concentration of SYBR is a strong function of temperature. This was compensated for by making PCR fluorescence measurements at the constant L-DNA low temperature calibrator peak. Second, continually exciting the SYBR Green fluorophore leads to photobleaching and overall fluorescence decay. This was compensated for by not measuring melt fluorescence during the first 10 cycles, preserving fluorophore functionality until later cycles. Third, the initial target quantification process assumes that the intercalator fluorescence produced by each double stranded amplicon and the intercalator fluorescence produced by each double stranded L-DNA calibrator are the same, but if there is a significant difference in length, they could produce different amounts of intercalator fluorescence. In these experiments, the sequences were chosen to be close to the same length (83 base pairs for the amplicon and 77 base pairs for the L-DNA calibrator) implying they would fluoresce similarly. Matching L-DNA calibrator and amplicon length could potentially be bypassed by calibrating a hydrolysis probe signal rather than an intercalating dye signal. One way to do this would be to match a hydrolysis probe to the end-label fluorophores on the L-DNA, as it is reasonable to assume that a molecule of a given fluorophore will produce the same fluorescence as another molecule of the same fluorophore. This hydrolysis probe technique assumes that the amount of hydrolysis probe triggered is equal to the copies in the reaction, but it uses a far brighter, less temperature sensitive signal.

For both melt analysis and initial target quantification, the presence of the L-DNA calibrators within the reaction provides important advantages over the current available technology, particularly in single-sample analysis. Melt analysis is frequently used to reduce false positives resulting from nonspecific amplification, and has further utility in detection of small sequence variations that can confer drug resistance [4, 5, 14]. However, melt analysis typically requires slow, precise manipulation of temperatures and comparator samples to give accurate results, neither of which is typically practical with a single-sample workflow. Similarly, while target quantification has implications for patient prognosis, contagiousness, and can inform the effectiveness of ongoing therapy [15, 16], traditional quantification requires a large number of known control samples to be run simultaneously to accurately quantify the initial target concentration. Evolving methods of quantification, such as digital PCR, still require specialized equipment and alternate workflows [17, 18]. By comparison, the L-DNA are present within the single sample tube with the unknown PCR reaction itself, and do not require external knowledge or additional control samples.

Although this was developed using a prototype instrument, the L-DNA calibrators could also be used with any standard PCR instrument. The prototype instrument described here is ideally suited to taking the high volume of fluorescence measurements required for these methods. If the PCR reaction is set up to include L-DNA on a standard instrument, the L-DNA calibrators could also be used to characterize the PCR amplicon with any PCR control system as long as the instrument has the capability to simultaneously and continuously collect fluorescence signals at a high rate during each PCR cycle. If this data file can be collected, these same post-analysis methods could be applied.

Conclusion

The incorporation of L-DNA strands as internal calibrators provides another method for characterizing the PCR amplicon in single-sample diagnostic applications.
