Weighted Holm Procedures: Theory, Properties, and Recommendations
Beibei Li, Wenge Guo

Abstract
In many statistical applications, particularly in clinical studies, hypotheses may carry different levels of importance, motivating the use of weighted multiple testing procedures (wMTPs) to control the familywise error rate (FWER). Among these approaches, two weighted Holm procedures are commonly used: the weighted Holm procedure (WHP), which is based on ordered weighted p p -values, and the weighted alternative Holm procedure (WAP), which relies on ordered raw p p -values. This paper provides a systematic comparison of these two procedures, along with practical recommendations for their use. We first examine their corresponding closed testing procedures (CTPs) and show that WHP is uniformly more powerful than WAP. We further investigate their structural properties, demonstrating that WAP, while consonant, lacks monotonicity. To facilitate communication with non-statisticians, we introduce graphical representations of both procedures using a common initial graph and distinct updating strategies. In addition, we derive adjusted p p -values and adjusted weighted p p -values for both methods. Finally, we establish an optimality result: WHP cannot be improved by enlarging any of its critical values without violating FWER control, whereas WAP is optimal only under specific conditions. Simulation studies support these theoretical findings and highlight the superior FWER control and average power of WHP.

Introduction

In many clinical applications, hypotheses differ in importance, this motivates the use of weighted multiple testing procedures (wMTPs). Several such methods have been developed, including weighted Bonferroni, Simes, and resampling-based approaches
(
25
;
17
;
2
;
27
;
31
)
. A weighted Bonferroni procedure, for example, can increase power for more important hypotheses. Among stepwise methods, two weighted Holm procedures are widely used: the weighted Holm procedure (WHP;
17
,
17
), which is based on ordered weighted
p
-values, and the weighted alternative Holm procedure (WAP;
2
,
2
), which relies on ordered raw
p
-values. WAP is often viewed as more objective since its ordering is independent of weights, but this comes at the cost of losing monotonicity, a key property in stepwise testing. Prior studies have examined weighted procedures under pre-ordered hypotheses
(
23
;
33
;
32
)
and weighted Simes-type approaches
(
27
)
, but the relative merits of ordering by raw versus weighted
p
-values remain unresolved. This motivates a careful comparison of WHP and WAP to clarify their statistical properties, evaluate their performance in terms of familywise error rate (FWER) control and power, and provide practical guidance. We show that WHP is uniformly more powerful than WAP, establish new optimality results, and demonstrate the advantages of WHP through theory and simulation.

Bonferroni-type weighted procedures can also be formulated as closed testing procedures (CTPs), which offer a rigorous framework for FWER control
(
22
;
3
;
16

. However, as the number of hypotheses grows, the number of intersection tests increases rapidly, making CTPs difficult to apply in practice. Graphical methods provide a more accessible alternative: they can represent a wide range of multiple testing procedures (MTPs), are easier to communicate to clinical teams than lengthy decision tables, and reduce programming effort. They are now widely used in clinical research
(
4
;

5

. Weighted procedures also underpin key gatekeeping strategies, including serial, parallel, and mixture approaches

(

;
29
;

. Developing simple and powerful weighted procedures is therefore essential for advancing these frameworks. Moreover, their optimality—whether they can be improved without sacrificing error control—remains largely unexplored, especially for WHP and WAP.

Although this paper does not address the choice of weights, their importance should be emphasized. Weights may be assigned based on the a priori importance of hypotheses or prior knowledge
(
29
)
, or chosen adaptively from data to improve power while preserving significance levels. Several approaches have been proposed for data-dependent weighting that still maintain familywise or generalized familywise error control
(

In summary, we compare WHP and WAP through their closed testing formulations, graphical representations, adjusted

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce notation and describe the two weighted Holm procedures studied in this paper.

2.1
Basic Notation

We consider the simultaneous testing of
m
hypotheses
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
with corresponding
p
-values
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
and positive weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
. The weighted
p
-values are defined as

\tilde{P}_{i}=\frac{P_{i}}{w_{i}},\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

Let
P_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq P_{(m)}
denote the ordered raw
p
-values with associated hypotheses
H_{(1)},\ldots,H_{(m)}
and weights
w_{(1)},\ldots,w_{(m)}
. Similarly, let
\tilde{P}_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\tilde{P}_{(m)}
denote the ordered weighted
p
-values, with corresponding hypotheses
H^{*}_{(1)},\ldots,H^{*}_{(m)}
and weights
w^{*}_{(1)},\ldots,w^{*}_{(m)}
.

Suppose that
m_{0}
of the hypotheses are true nulls and
m_{1}=m-m_{0}
are false nulls, and let
I_{0}
be the index set of the true null hypotheses. Denote by
R
the total number of rejections and by
V
the number of true nulls rejected. The FWER is defined as

\text{FWER}=\Pr(V\geq 1),

i.e., the probability of making at least one type I error
(
26
)
. Our goal is to control the FWER at a pre-specified significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Throughout, we assume that for each
i\in I_{0}
, the corresponding
p
-value satisfies the standard super-uniform condition

\Pr(P_{i}\leq x)\leq x,\quad\forall\,x\in(0,1),

without imposing any restrictions on their joint distribution.

2.2
Two weighted Holm procedures

We now describe two weighted Holm procedures that are widely used in practice. The key difference lies in how the hypotheses are ordered: WHP orders by weighted
p
-values, while WAP orders by raw
p
-values. This choice affects both their properties and performance.

Procedure 2.1
.

(
17
)
.

WHP.
Reject
H^{*}_{(i)}
if

\tilde{P}_{(j)}\leq\frac{\alpha}{\sum_{k=j}^{m}w^{*}_{(k)}},\quad j=1,\ldots,i.

(1)

Here, the hypotheses are ordered according to the weighted
p
-values. By incorporating weights into the ordering, WHP gives priority to hypotheses deemed more important, extending the idea of the weighted Bonferroni test.

Procedure 2.2
.

(
2
)
.

WAP.
Reject
H_{(i)}
if

P_{(j)}\leq\frac{w_{(j)}}{\sum_{k=j}^{m}w_{(k)}}\alpha,\quad j=1,\ldots,i.

(2)

In contrast, WAP orders hypotheses by their raw
p
-values, regardless of their assigned weights. This makes the procedure appear more objective, but as we will see, it also leads to a loss of certain monotonicity properties.

Remark 2.1
.

It is worth noting that when the weights exhibit highly uneven magnitudes (e.g., diverging weights such that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}=1
,
w_{1}\to 1
, and
w_{k+1}/w_{k}\to 0
for
k=1,\ldots,m-1
), WHP and WAP display markedly different behavior. Under such configurations, ordering hypotheses by weighted
p
-values causes WHP to effectively reduce to a fixed-sequence testing procedure, with the testing order largely determined by the weights
(
29
)
. In contrast, WAP continues to order hypotheses according to their raw
p
-values, while its rejection thresholds depend on the weights. This decoupling of ordering and stopping rules highlights an important conceptual distinction between the two procedures.

To compare WHP and WAP, we focus on four key aspects:
(i) their CTPs, emphasizing monotonicity and consonance;
(ii) graphical representations that offer intuitive visualization;
(iii) adjusted
p
-values and adjusted weighted
p
-values; and
(iv) optimality, which characterizes the decision rules and their relation to the global significance level
\alpha
.

3
Underlying CTPs and theoretical results

In this section, we study the underlying CTPs of the weighted Holm procedures. This perspective clarifies their statistical properties and enables direct comparison. We also examine their power, monotonicity, and consonance, and illustrate the differences through examples.

3.1
CTP representations of WAP and WHP

We first show that both WAP and WHP can be expressed as CTPs. This provides a unified framework for studying their properties.

For any intersection hypothesis
H_{I}=\bigcap_{i\in I}H_{i}
, with
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
, let

P_{(1)}^{I}=\min_{i\in I}P_{i}

denote the smallest
p
-value in
I
, and let
w_{(1)}^{I}
be its associated weight.

The local test for WAP rejects
H_{I}
if

P_{(1)}^{I}\leq\frac{w_{(1)}^{I}}{\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}}\alpha.

(3)

The type I error rate of this test satisfies

\Pr\!\left(P_{(1)}^{I}\leq\tfrac{w_{(1)}^{I}}{\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}}\alpha\right)\leq\sum_{i\in I}\Pr\!\left(P_{i}\leq\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}}\alpha\right)\leq\alpha,

so by the closure principle, the resulting procedure controls the FWER.

Proposition 3.1
.

The CTP with local tests defined in (
3
) is equivalent to the WAP of
2
.

For WHP, each intersection hypothesis
H_{I}
is tested using a weighted Bonferroni test, which rejects
H_{I}
if

P_{i}\leq\frac{w_{i}}{\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}}\alpha\quad\text{for some }i\in I.

(4)

This test is valid since

\Pr\!\left(\cup_{i\in I}\{P_{i}\leq\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}}\alpha\}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in I}\Pr\!\left(P_{i}\leq\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}}\alpha\right)\leq\alpha.

Proposition 3.2
.

(
29
)
.
The CTP with local tests defined in (
4
), that is, the weighted Bonferroni test, is equivalent to
17
’s WHP.

3.2
Power, monotonicity, and consonance

By comparing the local tests of WAP and WHP, we can establish a power dominance result.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any hypothesis
H_{i}
, if it is rejected by WAP, it is also rejected by WHP. Thus, WHP is uniformly more powerful than WAP under arbitrary dependence.

Proof
.
For each intersection hypothesis
H_{I}=\cap_{i\in I}H_{i}
, the rejection event

\{P_{(1)}^{I}\leq\tfrac{w_{(1)}^{I}}{\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}}\alpha\}

of WAP’s local test implies

\bigcup_{i\in I}\{P_{i}\leq\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{i\in I}w_{i}}\alpha\},

the rejection event of WHP’s local test. Hence, by the closure principle and Propositions 3.1 and 3.2, any
H_{i}
rejected by WAP must also be rejected by WHP.
\square

Notably, the converse does not hold in general; that is, there exist configurations in which WHP rejects certain hypotheses that WAP fails to reject. This phenomenon is illustrated in Example
3.2
in Section
3
.

Remark 3.1
.

The critical values of WAP lack the monotone property
(
2
)
, which causes power loss. If the weights do not alter the ordering of
p
-values, i.e.,
\tilde{P}_{(i)}=P_{(i)}/w_{(i)}
, then WHP and WAP coincide since

\tilde{P}_{(i)}\leq\frac{\alpha}{\sum_{k=i}^{m}w_{(k)}^{*}}\iff P_{(i)}\leq\frac{w_{(i)}}{\sum_{k=i}^{m}w_{(k)}}\alpha.

We now formalize two notions of monotonicity.

Definition 3.1

(Monotone property;
8
,
8
)
.

A
p
-value–based MTP is said to be
p
-value monotone if lowering one or more
p
-values never decreases the number of rejections.

Definition 3.2

(Monotonicity condition;
18
,
18
)
.

For a CTP, let
\alpha_{i}(I)
,
i\in I\subseteq M=\{1,\ldots,m\}
, denote the intersection-specific critical values, which satisfy

\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}(I)\leq\alpha.

The procedure is said to satisfy the
monotonicity condition
if

\alpha_{i}(I)\leq\alpha_{i}(J)\quad\text{for all }i\in J\subset I\subseteq M.

Remark 3.2
.

The quantities
\alpha_{i}(I)
, for
i\in I\subseteq M=\{1,\ldots,m\}
, in Definition
3.2
denote intersection-specific critical values assigned to the component hypotheses within the intersection, where
I
is the index set defining the intersection. These quantities arise from the closed testing procedure and depend on the particular intersection hypothesis
H_{I}
.

In contrast, the quantities
\alpha_{i}
, for
i\in I\subseteq M=\{1,\ldots,m\}
, introduced in Section
4
, denote local significance levels assigned to individual hypotheses in the initial graph (Step 1) and in the updated graphs (Step 4) of the graphical algorithm. In this context,
I
represents the set of remaining (unrejected) hypotheses at the corresponding stage of the procedure.

The WHP is
p
-value monotone, and its CTP representation satisfies the monotonicity condition, making it consonant. By contrast, WAP is neither
p
-value monotone nor monotonic in its CTP representation. Nevertheless, its closed testing representation remains consonant—a somewhat surprising property.

Proposition 3.3
.

The CTP corresponding to WAP is consonant, despite WAP itself not being monotone under Definitions
3.1
or
3.2
.

4
Graphical representations of WHP and WAP

In the graphical approach
(
4
)
, each hypothesis is represented by a node, and the global significance level
\alpha
is initially distributed across the nodes. The relationships among hypotheses are encoded through transition coefficients, which specify how local significance levels are reallocated when a hypothesis is rejected. Formally, let the initial local levels be
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\leq\alpha\in(0,1)
. Define
M=\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
G=(g_{ij})_{m\times m}
, where
g_{ij}
is the fraction of
\alpha_{i}
transferred to
H_{j}
if
H_{i}
is rejected, subject to
0\leq g_{ij}\leq 1
,
g_{ii}=0
, and
\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{ij}\leq 1
for all
i
. The pair
(G,\bm{\alpha})
, together with the updating algorithm, defines a MTP.

A convenient choice of the initial transition matrix is

\bm{G}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\tfrac{w_{2}}{\sum_{j=2}^{m}w_{j}}&\cdots&\tfrac{w_{m}}{\sum_{j=2}^{m}w_{j}}\\
\tfrac{w_{1}}{\sum_{j\neq 2}^{m}w_{j}}&0&\cdots&\tfrac{w_{m}}{\sum_{j\neq 2}^{m}w_{j}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tfrac{w_{1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}w_{j}}&\tfrac{w_{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}w_{j}}&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix},

with initial local levels
\alpha_{i}=\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}}\alpha
for
i=1,\ldots,m
, which guarantees
\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{ij}=1
for all
i
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=\alpha
. Here, the initial local significance levels
\alpha_{i},i=1,\ldots,m
, are specified for the graphical approaches underlying both the WHP and WAP procedures, which are described below.

Graphical Algorithms

WHP

1.

Initialize
I=M
.

2.

Let
j=\arg\min_{i\in I}\tilde{p}_{i}
.

3.

If
p_{j}\leq\alpha_{j}
, reject
H_{j}
; otherwise stop.

4.

Update:

I\leftarrow I\setminus\{j\},\quad\alpha_{l}\leftarrow\alpha_{l}+\alpha_{j}g_{jl}\;(l\in I),

g_{lk}\leftarrow\frac{g_{lk}+g_{lj}g_{jk}}{1-g_{lj}g_{jl}}\;(l,k\in I,l\neq k).

5.

If
I\neq\varnothing
, return to Step 2; otherwise stop.

WAP

1.

Initialize
I=M
.

2.

Let
j=\arg\min_{i\in I}p_{i}
.

3.

If
p_{j}\leq\alpha_{j}
, reject
H_{j}
; otherwise stop.

4.

Update the graph as in Step 4 of WHP.

5.

If
I\neq\varnothing
, return to Step 2; otherwise stop.

The graphical representations of WHP and WAP are displayed side by side above.
Their
essential distinction
lies in that WHP operates on weighted
p
-values
(\tilde{p}_{i})
, whereas WAP is based on the raw
p
-values
(p_{i})
(see Step 2 of the graphical algorithms for both procedures).

Proposition 4.1
.

The WHP (
17
,
17
)
and the WAP (
2
,
2
)
are equivalent to the graphical algorithms described above,
in the sense that they yield identical rejection decisions for any configuration of
p
-values.

Graphical formulations of WHP have previously been considered in
1
and
15
, though only for a small number of hypotheses (e.g.,
m=3
). Here, we extend this representation to arbitrary

. In addition, we introduce a graphical representation for WAP based on the raw

-values, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously developed in the literature. Presenting these representations within a common graphical setup enables a direct comparison between WHP and WAP, thereby clarifying their structural differences and connections.

Remark 4.1
.

The graphical approach underlying WAP lies beyond the original framework of
4

because its local tests are not weighted Bonferroni tests and thus need not satisfy the usual monotonicity conditions.
This highlights the need for a broader graphical framework capable of encompassing WAP and related procedures.

5
Adjusted
p
-values

Adjusted
p
-values provide an alternative implementation of MTPs.
Instead of comparing raw or weighted
p
-values to stepwise thresholds, one can compare adjusted
p
-values directly with the global significance level
\alpha
.
This leads to decisions identical to those of the original procedures.
In this section, we derive the adjusted weighted
p
-values for WHP and the adjusted
p
-values for WAP.

For WHP, the adjusted weighted
p
-values
\tilde{P}_{(i)}^{\,adj}
corresponding to the ordered hypotheses
H_{(i)}^{*}
are

\tilde{P}_{(i)}^{\,adj}=\begin{cases}\min\!\left\{\tilde{P}_{(1)}\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{(k)}^{*},\,1\right\},&i=1,\\[6.0pt]
\max\!\left\{\tilde{P}_{(i)}\sum_{k=i}^{m}w_{(k)}^{*},\,\tilde{P}_{(i-1)}^{\,adj}\right\},&i=2,\ldots,m.\end{cases}

For WAP, the adjusted
p
-values
P_{(i)}^{\,adj}
corresponding to the ordered hypotheses
H_{(i)}
are

P_{(i)}^{\,adj}=\begin{cases}\min\!\left\{\dfrac{P_{(1)}}{w_{(1)}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{(k)},\,1\right\},&i=1,\\[6.0pt]
\max\!\left\{\dfrac{P_{(i)}}{w_{(i)}}\sum_{k=i}^{m}w_{(k)},\,P_{(i-1)}^{\,adj}\right\},&i=2,\ldots,m.\end{cases}

Proposition 5.1
.

For every hypothesis
H_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, the adjusted weighted
p
-value under WHP is less than or equal to the corresponding adjusted
p
-value under WAP.
Consequently, any hypothesis rejected by WAP will also be rejected by WHP.

Proposition
5.1
confirms Theorem
3.1
: every rejection made by WAP is also made by WHP. Thus, WHP uniformly dominates WAP while maintaining strong FWER control, and in terms of adjusted
p
-values, it constitutes a uniformly more refined procedure.

Example 5.1
.

Consider the setting of Example

From the formulas above we obtain

P_{1}^{\,adj}=P_{2}^{\,adj}=0.06,\qquad P_{3}^{\,adj}=0.3,

\tilde{P}_{1}^{\,adj}=\tilde{P}_{2}^{\,adj}=0.042,\qquad\tilde{P}_{3}^{\,adj}=0.3.

With
\alpha=0.05
, WHP rejects
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, while WAP rejects none.
This outcome is consistent with Example
3.2
and further illustrates the stronger performance of WHP.

6
Optimality of the WHP and WAP

A central question in small-scale multiple testing is whether a given procedure is
optimal
,
in the sense that it cannot be improved without compromising control of the FWER
(
21
;
14
;
20
)
.
To the best of our knowledge, no previous work has examined the optimality of WHP or WAP.
In this section, we establish such results for both procedures.
Throughout, we assume that the weights
w_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, are fixed in advance
and common to all weighted
p
-value–based procedures under consideration.

6.1
Optimality of WHP

We first show that WHP is optimal in a broad sense.
By constructing suitable joint distributions for the
p
-values
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
under arbitrary dependence,
one can verify that the FWER bound of
\alpha
is sharp.

Theorem 6.1
.

The WHP (
17
,
17
) is optimal in the sense that no critical value can be increased without violating FWER control.

A stronger form of optimality arises when we restrict attention to weighted
p
-value step-down procedures.
Let
\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{m}
be a nondecreasing sequence of critical values.
A weighted
p
-value step-down procedure rejects
H_{(i)}^{*}
if and only if

\tilde{P}_{(j)}\leq\alpha_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,i,

where
\tilde{P}_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\tilde{P}_{(m)}
are the ordered weighted
p
-values
\tilde{P}_{i}=P_{i}/w_{i}
.

Definition 6.1

(Dominance)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
be two weighted
p
-value–based procedures, with rejection sets
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{\tilde{P}})
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\mathbf{\tilde{P}})
for a vector of weighted
p
-values
\mathbf{\tilde{P}}
.
We say that
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
dominates
\mathcal{M}
, written
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\succeq\mathcal{M}
, if

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\mathbf{\tilde{P}})\supseteq\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{\tilde{P}})\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{\tilde{P}}.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{w}
be any weighted
p
-value step-down procedure with
\mathrm{FWER}\leq\alpha
.
Then
\mathcal{M}^{w}\preceq\mathrm{WHP}
.

Proposition
6.1
demonstrates that, within the class of weighted
p
-value step-down procedures, WHP is uniformly most powerful.

6.2
Optimality of WAP

We now turn to WAP.
In contrast to WHP, WAP is not uniformly optimal; rather, it attains optimality only under specific restrictions on the weight ratios.

Proposition 6.2
.

The WAP (
2
,
2
) is optimal in the sense that no critical value can be increased without violating FWER control, provided that

\frac{\min_{i}w_{i}}{\max_{i}w_{i}}\,\geq\,\alpha.

Remark 6.1
.

The condition
\min_{i}w_{i}/\max_{i}w_{i}\geq\alpha
is typically not restrictive in applications.
For instance, when
\alpha=0.05
, it merely requires that the largest weight is at most
20
times the smallest weight.
Such a balance is common in practice, since weights are usually assigned to reflect relative importance of hypotheses rather than to induce extreme disparities.
If the condition is violated, however, the WAP may fail to retain its optimality.

In summary, WHP enjoys unconditional optimality, whereas WAP is only conditionally optimal, depending on the relative magnitudes of the weights.

9
Conclusion

The wMTPs, such as the weighted Bonferroni, weighted Holm, and weighted Hochberg methods, are widely used for controlling the FWER. However, it has not always been clear which procedure is most preferable and straightforward to apply in practice, particularly in clinical trials. In this paper, we examined two variants of the weighted Holm procedure: WHP, which orders weighted
p
-values, and WAP, which orders raw
p
-values. We compared these procedures from four perspectives: (i) representation as CTPs, (ii) graphical visualization, (iii) adjusted
p
-values, and (iv) optimality.

Our theoretical results show that WHP uniformly dominates WAP in terms of power under arbitrary dependence. Furthermore, we established optimality properties: WHP is optimal among all step-down procedures based on weighted
p
-values that control the FWER, whereas WAP is optimal only under restrictive conditions on weight ratios. Simulation studies corroborated these findings, demonstrating that WHP achieves superior power while maintaining strong FWER control.

Beyond power and optimality, we investigated structural properties. WHP is monotone in
p
-values and also satisfies monotonicity in its closed testing representation, which ensures consonance and enables efficient shortcut implementations. In contrast, WAP fails monotonicity under both definitions. Nevertheless, its closed testing representation remains consonant, permitting shortcut formulations. This contrast is notable: WAP does not belong to the class of CTPs with weighted Bonferroni-type local tests and lacks monotonicity, yet it still enjoys consonance.

Overall, our results suggest that WHP should be the method of choice in practice. It combines stronger theoretical guarantees, more reliable empirical performance, and favorable structural properties. While future advances in generalized weighted graphical frameworks may broaden these insights, WHP currently offers the most transparent and powerful option for real-world applications.

A promising direction for future research is to extend weighted Holm procedures to more complex settings in which
p
-values arise from structured hypotheses, such as one- or two-way groupings. Such extensions would further enhance the applicability of weighted procedures and open new opportunities for integrating structure into multiple testing while preserving rigorous error control.

I_{0}=\{1,\ldots,m\}

\displaystyle g_{lk}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{k}}{\sum_{r\in I_{i-1}\setminus\{l\}}w_{r}},\quad g_{lj_{i}}=\frac{w_{j_{i}}}{\sum_{r\in I_{i-1}\setminus\{l\}}w_{r}},

Proof of Proposition
5.1

\tilde{P}_{(r)}=\frac{P_{(i)}}{w_{(i)}},\qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.

\tilde{P}_{(r)}^{\,\text{adj}}\leq P_{(i)}^{\,\text{adj}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,m.

- If

\mathcal{M}^{w}
be a weighted step-down procedure with nondecreasing critical values
\alpha_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{m}
that controls the FWER at level
\alpha
. Suppose it rejects
H_{(1)}^{*},\ldots,H_{(r)}^{*}
. Define

l=\sum_{k=r}^{m}w_{(k)}^{*},\qquad\tau=\min\{\alpha_{r},1/l\},\qquad\beta=l\tau.

Consider the least favorable configuration where all false null
p
-values are zero and all true null
p
-values are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
. Suppose
m_{1}
hypotheses are false; without loss of generality, let these be the first
m_{1}
hypotheses. Then, for
r=m_{1}+1
, we obtain
l=\sum_{k=r}^{m}w_{k}
.

Generate the weighted
p
-values
\tilde{q}_{r},\ldots,\tilde{q}_{m}
for the true null hypotheses
H_{r},\ldots,H_{m}
as follows:

•

Choose
j\in\{r,\ldots,m,0\}
with probabilities
w_{r}\tau,\ldots,w_{m}\tau,\,1-\beta
.

•

If
j\neq 0
, let
\tilde{q}_{j}\sim U[0,\tau]
and
\tilde{q}_{i}\sim U[\tau,\,1/w_{i}]
for
i\neq j
.

•

If
j=0
, let all
\tilde{q}_{i}\sim U[\tau,\,1/w_{i}]
for
i=r,\ldots,m
.

In either case, the induced raw
p
-values
q_{i}=w_{i}\tilde{q}_{i}
,
i=r,\ldots,m
, are uniform on
[0,1]
. Moreover, if
j\neq 0
, then

\tilde{q}_{(r)}=\min_{r\leq i\leq m}\tilde{q}_{i}\leq\tau\leq\alpha_{r}.

(5)

For indices
1,\ldots,r-1
, we have
\tilde{q}_{(j)}=0
. Thus, with probability at least
\beta
,
\mathcal{M}^{w}
rejects at least one true null. Consequently,

\mathrm{FWER}\;\geq\;\beta.

Since
\mathcal{M}^{w}
controls FWER at
\alpha
, it follows that
\beta\leq\alpha
, i.e.,

l\tau\leq\alpha.

Since
\tau=\min\{\alpha_{r},1/l\}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it follows that

\tau\leq\frac{\alpha}{l}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\alpha_{r}\leq\frac{\alpha}{\sum_{k=r}^{m}w_{k}}=\frac{\alpha}{\sum_{k=r}^{m}w_{(k)}^{*}}.

This upper bound coincides with the rejection threshold of the WHP. Since
m_{1}
is arbitrary, the inequality holds for all
r=1,\ldots,m
. Therefore,

\mathcal{M}^{w}\preceq\mathrm{WHP}.

\square

10.7
Proof of Proposition
6.2

The argument parallels the proof of Theorem
6.1
, relying on the same least favorable configuration (LFC) and joint distribution of
p
-values.

Let

j^{*}:=\arg\min_{1\leq i\leq m_{0}}w_{i},\qquad w_{j^{*}}=\min_{1\leq i\leq m_{0}}w_{i}

denote the index and value of the smallest weight among the true null hypotheses. Under the LFC, exactly one true null hypothesis
H_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,m_{0}\}
, is selected with probability
\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}
, and its
p
-value follows

P_{i}\sim\text{Unif}\!\left(0,\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}\right).

For each non-selected true null, the
p
-value is distributed as

P_{j}\sim\text{Unif}\!\left(\tfrac{w_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}},1\right),\quad j\in I_{0}\setminus\{i\}.

Denote the collection of true null
p
-values by
\mathbb{P}_{0}=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{m_{0}}\}
.

Consider first the case where
H_{j^{*}}
is selected (with probability
\tfrac{w_{j^{*}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}
). Then the probability of erroneously rejecting it under WAP is

\Pr\!\left(P_{j^{*}}\leq\tfrac{w_{j^{*}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}\alpha,\;P_{j^{*}}\leq P_{i}\;\;\forall P_{i}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}\setminus\{P_{j^{*}}\}\right)=\tfrac{w_{j^{*}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}\,\alpha.

Next, suppose that some other
H_{i}
,
i\in I_{0}\setminus\{j^{*}\}
, is selected. Then

\Pr\!\left(P_{i}\leq\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}\alpha,\;P_{i}\leq P_{l}\;\;\forall P_{l}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}\setminus\{P_{i}\}\right)=\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}\,\alpha,

provided that the WAP rejection threshold for
H_{i}
does not exceed the upper bound associated with
P_{j^{*}}
, i.e.,

\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}\alpha\;\leq\;\tfrac{w_{j^{*}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}\quad\iff\quad\tfrac{w_{j^{*}}}{w_{i}}\geq\alpha.

Thus, the condition
\tfrac{w_{j^{*}}}{w_{i}}\geq\alpha
for all
i\in I_{0}\setminus\{j^{*}\}
guarantees that the probability of a Type I error is bounded appropriately. Consequently,

\text{FWER}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}}\Pr\!\left(P_{i}\leq\tfrac{w_{i}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}w_{k}}\alpha,\;P_{i}\leq P_{l}\;\;\forall P_{l}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}\setminus\{P_{i}\}\right)\;=\;\alpha.

Therefore, whenever

\frac{\min_{i}w_{i}}{\max_{i}w_{i}}\,\geq\,\alpha,

the WAP attains equality
\text{FWER}=\alpha
and is thus optimal in the sense that no critical value can be increased without violating FWER control.
\square

Intuition.
The smallest weight
w_{j^{*}}
acts as a bottleneck: if it is not too small relative to the largest weight, then every rejection threshold remains consistent with the uniform distribution of true null
p
-values. This balance ensures that the FWER is pushed exactly to the boundary
\alpha
, establishing the optimality of WAP.
