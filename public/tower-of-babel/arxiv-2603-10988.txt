Sharp propagation of chaos for mean field Langevin dynamics, control, and games
Manuel Arnese and Daniel Lacker

Abstract
. We establish the sharp rate of propagation of chaos for McKean-Vlasov equations with coefficients that are non-linear in the measure argument, i.e., not necessarily given by pairwise interactions. Results are given both on bounded time horizon and uniform in time. As applications, we deduce the sharp rate of propagation of chaos for the convergence problem in mean field games and control, and for mean field Langevin dynamics, the latter being uniform in time in the strongly displacement convex regime. Our arguments combine the BBGKY hierarchy with techniques from the literature on weak propagation of chaos.



1.
Introduction

We study a system of particles

(1.1)

\begin{split}&dY_{t}^{i}=V(m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{i})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma\,dB_{t}^{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,\\
&Y_{0}^{i}\sim\text{ iid }\mu_{0},\qquad m_{t}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{t}^{i}},\end{split}

that interact through the empirical measure
m_{t}^{n}
. Each particle
Y_{t}^{i}
takes values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
B_{t}^{1},\dots,B_{t}^{n}
are independent Wiener processes. It is moreover important for our results that
\sigma
is non-zero. Systems of this type have numerous applications in physics, economics, and machine learning, to name but a few, and we refer to
[
12
,
13
]
for a thorough recent survey.
The large-
n
limit of the system is typically expected to be described by the following McKean-Vlasov equation:

(1.2)

dX_{t}=V(\mu_{t},X_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma\,dB_{t},\ \ \mathcal{L}(X_{0})=\mu_{0},\ \ \mu_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(X_{t}).

There is by now a large body of work studying the convergence of (
1.1
) to (
1.2
), for a wide variety of specializations of
V
. There are two equivalent ways to formulate the large-
n
limit:

(1)

Mean-field limit: As
n\to\infty
,
m^{n}_{t}
converges to
\mu_{t}
(weakly in probability).

(2)

Propagation of chaos: For each fixed
k
, as
n\to\infty
, the joint law of
(Y_{t}^{1},\dots,Y_{t}^{k})
converges to the product measure
\mu_{t}^{\otimes k}
.

Although it is classical that these two formulations are equivalent
qualitatively
, they turn out to be quite different
quantitatively
, and it remains unclear how to transfer quantitative global bounds into local bounds in a sharp way, or vice versa.
Moreover, these two formulations are somewhat different in spirit: The first (1) is a
global
property that involves the entire configuration
(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{n})
, while the second (2) is more
local
as it involves low-dimensional marginals.

The majority of the literature focuses on systems with
pairwise interactions
, where
V
takes the form

(1.3)

V(\mu,x)=\int\phi(x,y)\,d\mu(y),\qquad\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

Pairwise interactions are especially prominent in applications in physics, where the relevant interaction forces in many-body systems (e.g., electrostatic or gravitational) naturally act pairwise.
Major challenges arise when the interaction function
\phi
is singular, and for many such models it remains an active topic of research to justify (1) or (2) even just qualitatively
[
41
]
.

In this paper we focus on non-pairwise interactions, where
V
is a fairly general nonlinear functional of its measure argument, assumed smooth in a sense we make precise later. This setting arises naturally in the contexts of mean field Langevin dynamics, mean field games, and mean field control, all of which will be discussed in more detail below.
Our main goal is to prove sharp estimates of propagation of chaos.

There are two main quantitative techniques used in prior literature for general interactions, which are both global in nature.
First is the synchronous coupling approach, popularized by Sznitman
[
61
]
. If
V(\mu,x)
is jointly Lipschitz, with the 2-Wasserstein distance
\mathbb{W}_{2}
used for
\mu
(though any
p\neq 2
works analogously), then one can prove a global bound of

\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\pi^{n}_{t},\mu^{\otimes n}_{t})=O(1),

where
\pi^{n}_{t}
is the joint law of
(Y^{1}_{t},\ldots,Y^{n}_{t})
. By a subadditivity inequality, this implies

(1.4)

\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\pi^{k}_{t},\mu^{\otimes k}_{t})=O(k/n),\quad k\leq n,

where
\pi^{k}_{t}
is the joint law of
(Y^{1}_{t},\ldots,Y^{k}_{t})
.
The second and more recent approach, known as
weak propagation of chaos

[
15
,
27
]
, is based on the analysis of the Kolmogorov backward equation on the space of measures associated with the dynamics of
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. For appropriately smooth functions
f:\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
this yields the sharp estimate

(1.5)

|{\mathbb{E}}[f(m_{t}^{n})-f(\mu_{t})]|=O(1/n).

In fact, these techniques can yield precise expansions of
{\mathbb{E}}f(m_{t}^{n})
around
f(\mu_{t})
in powers of
1/n
, with coefficients determined by derivatives of
f
.

The second author recently introduced in
[
47
]
a purely
local
method for studying propagation of chaos, based on the BBGKY hierarchy.
For pairwise interactions (
1.3
), with
\phi
being Lipschitz or bounded, it was shown that

(1.6)

\mathbb{H}(\pi_{t}^{k}\,\|\,\mu_{t}^{\otimes k})=O(k^{2}/n^{2}),

where
\mathbb{H}
is relative entropy. When
\phi
is Lipschitz, applying a Talagrand inequality leads to a notable improvement of (
1.4
) to
O(k^{2}/n^{2})
. On the other hand, (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) are hard to compare directly, except that the latter easily implies the former when
f
is a regular enough U-statistic.

In this paper we will prove (
1.6
) for a broad class of general (non-pairwise) interactions
V
, both on bounded time horizons and uniform in time, the latter when
V
is displacement monotone (see Assumption
Assumption (UiT)
). A first step toward this appeared in
[
47
, Section 2D]
, but with a slower rate and with unnatural and difficult-to-check smoothness assumptions on
V
.

Our proof builds on the BBGKY approach of
[
47
]
. In the pairwise interaction case, the time evolution of the marginal laws
(\pi_{t}^{k})_{k=1}^{n}
follows the BBGKY hierarchy; in particular, the evolution of the
k
-th marginal
\pi_{t}^{k}
depends only on the conditional law of
Y_{t}^{k+1}
given
(Y_{t}^{1},\dots,Y_{t}^{k})
. The approach of
[
47
]
was to analyze the dynamics of the relative entropy at each level of the hierarchy. This led to a system of differential inequalities,

(1.7)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}_{t})\lesssim\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}+k\Big(\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k+1}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes(k+1)}_{t})-\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}_{t})\Big),\quad k<n,

which can then be used to derive the desired estimate (
1.6
).
Subsequent work extended this method and result to be uniform in time
[
48
,
52
]
, to obtain higher-order (cluster) expansions of
\pi^{k}_{t}
around
\mu^{\otimes k}_{t}

[
38
]
, to non-exchangeable interactions
[
49
]
, to hold in Fisher information
[
37
]
, to interactions through the diffusion coefficient
[
36
]
, and even to certain singular interactions
[
64
]
, but always in the setting of pairwise interactions.

To handle non-pairwise interactions, we adapt the BBGKY approach by performing a Taylor expansion of
V(m^{n}_{t},\cdot)
around
V(\mu_{t},\cdot)
at an appropriate moment. The first-order term produced takes the form of a pairwise interaction which can be analyzed using prior methods. This ultimately leads to a system of differential inequalities

(1.8)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}_{t})\lesssim\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}+k\Big(\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k+1}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes(k+1)}_{t})-\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}_{t})\Big)+{\mathbb{E}}[R_{t}(m^{n}_{t})],

for some non-linear “remainder” term
R
. The problem then reduces to understanding the rate at which this remainder vanishes. To do this, we use ideas from the literature on weak propagation of chaos mentioned above, in particular using tools from
[
15
]
and
[
3
]
. However, the off-the-shelf estimate in (
1.5
) yields only
1/n
instead of our desired rate of
1/n^{2}
, and we must instead carefully exploit the fact that the remainder
R_{t}(\cdot)
vanishes at first order at
\mu_{t}
.

It is worth stressing that some smoothness of
V(\mu,x)
is necessary. Mere Wasserstein-Lipschitz continuity suffices for the non-sharp bound (
1.6
) but not for the sharp bound (
1.6
). This fact, explained in Example
2.10
, lies in contrast with the pairwise interaction case (
1.3
), where Lipschitz
\phi
was good enough for the sharp bound (
1.6
).

For simplicity, we focus on the case of iid initializations, but see Remark
5.7
for some discussion of how to go beyond this case.
It should be possible to obtain (
1.6
) without assuming iid initial conditions, but rather just assuming
\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}_{0}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}_{0})=O(k^{2}/n^{2})
.
Indeed, this was shown in prior work in the case of pairwise interactions, starting in
[
48
]
. Many kinds of mean field Gibbs measures
\pi^{n}_{0}
are known to satisfy this condition for suitable
\mu_{0}
, as was shown in
[
46
,
58
]
.

2.
Results

2.1.
Analysis on the space of measures

For a random variable
X
we write
\mathcal{L}(X)
for its law.
For a separable metric space
(E,d)
we write
\mathcal{P}(E)
for the set of Borel probability measures on
E
. For
p\geq 1
we let
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
denote the subset of
m\in\mathcal{P}(E)
for which
\int d^{p}(x_{0},\cdot)\,dm<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in E
. Define the Wasserstein distance
\mathbb{W}_{p}
as usual by

\mathbb{W}_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi}\int_{E\times E}d^{p}(x,y)\,\pi(dx,dy),

where the infimum is over couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
. The relative entropy is defined by

\mathbb{H}(\nu\,\|\,\mu)=\begin{cases}\int_{E}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\log\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\,d\mu&\text{if }\nu\ll\mu\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We will make heavy use of differentiability concepts on the space of probability measures; for an introduction, see
[
2
]
(for an optimal transport perspective) or
[
10
, Chapter 5]
(for a more probabilistic approach). Consider a function
f:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
. A
flat derivative
of
f
is a function
\delta_{m}f:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that satisfies, for every
\nu,\eta\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

\int\delta_{m}f(\nu,y)\,d(\nu-\eta)(y)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f((1-h)\nu+h\eta)-f(\nu)}{h},\quad\delta_{m}f(\nu,\cdot)\in L^{2}(\nu)\cap L^{2}(\eta).

If it exists, the function
x\mapsto\delta_{m}f(\nu,x)
is unique up to an additive factor that is allowed to depend on
\nu
; this is because the constant gets erased by integrating against a zero mass measure. For concreteness, for each
\nu
we choose the unique normalization that guarantees that
\delta_{m}f(\nu,0)=0
.

Notice that flat derivatives acquire an extra space argument compared to the original function
f
. We can iterate the operation of taking a flat derivative by keeping the extra space argument fixed, obtaining the
k
-flat derivative
\delta_{m}^{k}f:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
The Wasserstein (or Lions) derivative
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}f:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is defined as

\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}f(\nu,y)=\nabla_{y}\delta_{m}f(\nu,y).

To obtain the
k
-Wasserstein derivative, we apply this operation
k
times, resulting in a function
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}f:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k}\to({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\otimes k}
,

\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}f(\nu,\bm{y})=\nabla_{y_{k}}\delta_{m}\dots\nabla_{y_{1}}\delta_{m}f(\nu,y_{1},\dots,y_{k}).

Under standard regularity assumptions (e.g.,
[
15
, Theorem 2.6]
or
[
3
, Lemma 5.4]
), space and flat derivatives commute, and so we might also apply
\delta_{m}^{k}
and then
k
gradient operations, one in each variable, to obtain the same function
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}f
.

Definition 2.1
.

Pick two non negative integers
\mathsf{w},b\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and a vector of
\mathsf{w}
non negative integers
\bm{c}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{\mathsf{w}}
. We say that
(\mathsf{w},\bm{i})=(\mathsf{w},b,\bm{c})
is a multi-index of size
\mathsf{w}+|\bm{i}|:=\mathsf{w}+b+\sum_{j=1}^{\mathsf{w}}c_{j}
. For a function
f=f(\nu,x)
on
\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
taking values in a Euclidean space, we denote

D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}f(\nu,x,\bm{y})=\nabla_{y_{1}}^{c_{1}}\dots\nabla_{y_{\mathsf{w}}}^{c_{\mathsf{w}}}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{\mathsf{w}}\nabla_{x}^{b}f(\nu,x,y_{1},\dots y_{\mathsf{w}}).

Let us explain this piece of notation in words: the first component of a multi index is a number
\mathsf{w}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
that denotes how many Wasserstein derivatives in
\nu
we are taking, while the second counts the number of derivatives in the spatial argument
x
. The third element is a vector
\bm{c}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathsf{w}}_{0}
that counts the number of derivatives taken in each of the new space arguments that arise due to the
\mathsf{w}
Wasserstein gradients. Notice that for a fixed
\nu
,
D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}f(\nu,\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{d(\mathsf{w}+1)}\to({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\mathsf{w}+|\bm{i}|}
, and we equip
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\mathsf{w}+|\bm{i}|}
with the Euclidean norm.

Definition 2.2
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\textup{bd}}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the set of functions
f:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that for every multi-index
(\mathsf{w},\bm{i})=(\mathsf{w},b,\bm{c})
, with
0<\mathsf{w}+|\bm{i}|\leq k
, the derivative
D^{\bm{\mathsf{w},i}}f
exists and is bounded and Lipschitz with respect to the
\mathbb{W}_{2}
and Euclidean metrics. We similarly write
C^{k}_{\textup{bd}}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
for the subset of
C^{k}_{\textup{bd}}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
consisting of those functions which depend only on the measure argument, and the notation
D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}f
for such a function will be defined only for a multi-index of the form
(\mathsf{w},\bm{i})=(\mathsf{w},0,c)
, i.e., with
b=0
.

We stress that the strict inequality
0<\mathsf{w}+|\bm{i}|
means that
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{bd}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
contains unbounded functions (of linear growth).
Examples include functions of the form
f(\nu,x)=g(x,\int h(x,y)\,d\nu(y))
, where
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
h:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
each have
k
bounded space derivatives.

2.2.
Assumptions and main results

We work with two sets of assumptions. The first is for bounded time horizon, and the second is for uniform-in-time results.

Assumption (
A
)
.

(1)

The function
V
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bd}}^{6}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

(2)

The initial condition
\mu_{0}
satisfies a T
1
transport inequality: there exists a constant
C_{\textup{T}_{1}}(0)>0
such that for every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

\mathbb{W}_{1}^{2}(\mu_{0},\nu)\leq 2C_{\textup{T}_{1}}(0)\,\mathbb{H}(\nu\,\|\,\mu_{0}).

(3)

The noise is non-degenerate:
\sigma>0
.

Let us note that Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
(2) is equivalent to
\mu_{0}
being
C_{\textup{T}_{1}}(0)
-subgaussian, in the sense that

(2.1)

\log\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{cf(x)}d\mu(x)\leq\lambda\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\,d\mu+C_{\textup{T}_{1}}(0)\frac{c^{2}}{2},

for all
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
and all 1-Lipschitz functions
f
. This equivalence is due to Bobkov-Götze
[
63
, Theorem 4.8]
.

Under Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
, the stochastic differential equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) both admit pathwise unique strong solutions. Let us write
\pi^{n}\in\mathcal{P}(C({\mathbb{R}}_{+};({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}))
for the law of the process
(Y^{1},\ldots,Y^{n})
and
\pi^{k}
for the marginal of
(Y^{1},\ldots,Y^{k})
, for
1\leq k\leq n
.
In what follows, for
t\geq 0
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(C({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{m}))
, we write
\nu[t]
for its restriction to the time interval
[0,t]
, i.e., the pushforward by the restriction map
C({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{m})\ni x\mapsto x|_{[0,t]}\in C([0,t];{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
.
We also write
\nu_{t}
for the time-
t
marginal, i.e., the pushforward by
C({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{R}}^{m})\ni x\mapsto x_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

Our first main result is the following propagation of chaos bound on a finite time horizon.

Theorem 2.3
.

Under assumption
Assumption (

A

)
, for every
k\leq n
and every fixed
t>0
, we have

(2.2)

\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[t])=O(k^{2}/n^{2}).

The hidden constant depends on
t
,
C_{\textup{T}_{1}}(0)
,
\mu_{0}
, and the derivative bounds on
V
.

This rate cannot be improved under the present assumptions, as explained by the Gaussian example in
[
47
]
.
Theorem
2.3
implies bounds in total variation by Pinsker’s inequality, and a coupling argument yields control in Wasserstein distance.

Corollary 2.4
.

Under assumption
Assumption (

A

)
, for every
k\leq n
and every fixed
t>0
,

\|\pi^{k}[t]-\mu^{\otimes k}[t]\|^{2}_{\textup{TV}}+\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\pi^{k}[t],\mu^{\otimes k}[t])=O(k^{2}/n^{2})

To be clear, in Corollary
2.4
,
\pi^{k}[t]
and
\mu^{\otimes k}[t]
are measures on the path space
C([0,t];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k}\cong C([0,t];{\mathbb{R}}^{dk})

Remark 2.5
.

When the particles take values in dimension
d=1
, Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
(1) can be relaxed to require only two bounded Wasserstein derivatives, instead of six in all variables, and the proof is much simpler, particularly in the analysis of the remainder term in (

). See Remark

for details.

Remark 2.6
.

This bound on
\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}
does not invoke a
T_{2}
(Talagrand) inequality, as one might guess. The path measure
\mu^{\otimes k}[t]
need not satisfy a (dimension-free)

inequality because we assumed only that
\mu_{0}
satisfies the significantly weaker

inequality.

Remark 2.7
.

We have stated our result in a time homogeneous setting, but the same applies without change to the case that
V
is a time dependent function; we explain how to adapt the proof in Remark
3.6
.

In the above results, the constants hidden by the big-O notation grow exponentially in

. Under stronger assumption, we obtain similar sharp bounds uniformly in time, at the level of the time-marginals instead of path measures. The extra requirements are displacement monotonicity of the drift (in the sense of optimal transport) and a smallness condition of the interaction.

Assumption (UiT)
.

(1)

Assumption

A

)

(2)

such that for any square-integrable

.

2.3.
Comments on the assumptions

The assumption that
V
has bounded Wasserstein and space gradient is important; in the pairwise case
V(\mu,x)=\int\phi(x,y)\,d\mu(y)
this means that
\phi
is Lipschitz, the original assumption of
[
47
]
. The boundedness of higher order derivatives is not essential and could most likely be relaxed with an appropriate approximation procedure. While the existence of
6
derivatives is a demanding assumption, it is satisfied in many applications of interest; moreover, in low dimension fewer than
6
derivatives are needed.
The other significant assumption is that
\mu_{0}
concentrates well enough to satisfy a T
1
inequality, a useful property that leads the particles
Y_{t}^{i}
to also concentrate well.

We do not think that the requirement for existence of
6
derivatives is sharp, but a simple example shows that having at least one derivative is necessary: Lipschitzianity is not enough to guarantee dimension-free convergence rates.

Example 2.10
.

Let
\gamma
be the law of a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion, so that
\gamma_{t}
is a Gaussian measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with mean 0 and variance
tI
. Define the function
V_{t}(\nu)=\mathbb{W}_{1}(\nu,\gamma_{t})
and consider the particle system

\displaystyle dY_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=V_{t}(m_{t}^{n})\cdot\mathbf{1}\,dt+dB_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle Y_{0}^{i}

\displaystyle=0\,\,,\,\,i=1,\dots,n.

where
\mathbf{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a vector of all ones. The function
V_{t}
is Lipschitz (in the measure argument, and trivially in space), but does not have Wasserstein derivatives. The corresponding McKean Vlasov process
X_{t}
is simply a Brownian motion: indeed Brownian motion solves the SDE, and the solution is unique. Then, using the representation of
\pi^{1}[T]
given by Lemma
3.3
along with a standard Girsanov calculation of relative entropy,

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\pi^{1}[T]\,\|\,\mu[T])=\mathbb{H}(\pi^{1}[T]\,\|\,\gamma[T])

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbb{W}_{1}(m_{t}^{n},\gamma_{t})\mid Y^{1}[t]\big]^{2}\Big]\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d}{2}\inf_{q}\mathbb{W}_{1}^{2}(q,\gamma_{t}),

where the infimum is taken over all atomic probability measures
q
with
n
atoms. A well-known quantization lower bound (see, e.g.,
[
55
, Theorem 2.1]
) yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{2/d}\inf_{|Q|=n}\mathbb{W}_{1}^{2}(Q,\gamma_{t})\geq C_{d}>0

and thus it is impossible to obtain the good
1/n^{2}
rate.

Assumption
Assumption (UiT)
(2) is natural from a couple of perspectives. In the case where
V=-\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi
for some smooth functional
\Psi
on Wasserstein space, it is equivalent to the
\lambda
-displacement convexity of
\Psi
in the sense of optimal transport
[
34
, Lemma 3.6]
. In the pairwise case
V(\mu,x)=-\nabla U(x)-\int\nabla W(x-y)\,d\mu(y)
this holds when
U
is strongly convex and
W
is convex, which is a standard regime for uniform-in-time propagation of chaos ever since the work
[
51
]
. Assumption
Assumption (UiT)
(3) is a smallness condition, constraining the strength of the interaction relative to the strength of monotonicity
\lambda
. Restrictions of this kind are common in the literature on uniform-in-time propagation of chaos
[
31
,
27
,
48
]
, particularly for going beyond the aforementioned convex regime. It seems unlikely assumption
Assumption (UiT)
(3) is necessary for the sharp rate
(k/n)^{2}
, given that we are imposing the convexity assumption
Assumption (UiT)
(2), but it remains an open question if the sharp rate can be obtained without such a smallness condition even in the pairwise case treated in
[
48
]
. This was recently achieved in the stationary (equilibrium) regime in
[
58
]
.

Sharpening previous work on sharp uniform-in-time propagation of chaos
[
48
,
52
]
, we do not directly assume a log-Sobolev inequality (LSI) for the initial measure
\mu_{0}
or its dynamical counterpart
\mu_{t}
. Instead, we assume merely the T
1
inequality, and we exploit the regularizing effects of the dynamics to prove that
\mu_{t}
satisfies a LSI for large enough time, with constant uniformly bounded for
t\geq t_{0}
; this is an application of a recent result of
[
18
]
, which shows that the LSI constant “comes down from infinity” for subgaussian initial conditions. We then apply the short-time result from Theorem
2.3
to control the time interval
[0,t_{0}]
.

Remark 2.11
.

Factor of
3
in Assumption
Assumption (UiT)
(3) agrees with the smallness assumption of
[
48
, Theorem 2.1(1)]
, after correcting a minor error therein: In their proof of Theorem 2.1(1) on page 468 it is required that
\alpha>3
, which means that
\sigma^{4}>12\gamma\eta
in their notation is required for their proof of the sharp uniform in time rate
(k/n)^{2}
. To map their notation to ours, note that their
\sigma
is our
\sqrt{2}\sigma
, their log-Sobolev constant
\eta
is our
\sigma^{2}/2\lambda
, and their
\gamma
is our
(2\sigma^{2}/\lambda)\||\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V|^{2}_{\text{op}}\|_{\infty}
. Hence, their condition
\sigma^{4}>12\gamma\eta
is equivalent to our
\||\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V|^{2}_{\text{op}}\|_{\infty}<\lambda^{2}/3
.

2.4.
Application: Mean Field Langevin Dynamics

Motivated by models of scaling limits of neural networks and connections to optimization problems over the space of probability measures, the paper
[
39
]
introduced the mean field Langevin dynamics (MFLD)

(2.3)

dX_{t}=-\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi(\mathcal{L}(X_{t}),X_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma dB_{t},

where we recall that
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})
denotes the law of
X_{t}
.
Equation (
2.3
) (or rather, the associated nonlinear Fokker-Planck PDE) can be interpreted as the Wasserstein gradient flow of the functional

(2.4)

\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\ni\mu\mapsto\Psi(\mu)+\sigma^{2}\int\mu\log\mu,

with the integral (differential entropy) interpreted as
+\infty
if
\mu
does not possess a sufficiently integrable density.
A key property of MFLD is that
\mu_{t}
converges to the minimizer of (
2.4
) as
t\to\infty
, and does so very quickly under the correct assumptions (see for instance
[
20
]
). This makes simulating MFLD a viable approach to sampling from the measure which minimizes (
2.4
). Two popular and distinct sets of assumptions are known to lead to fast convergence: the flat convexity regime, where
\Psi
is taken to be convex in the usual sense, and the displacement convexity regime where the functional
\Psi
is convex along displacement interpolations, i.e., geodesics in the Wasserstein space
(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\mathbb{W}_{2})
.

The most natural way to simulate MFLD is via the particle approximation

(2.5)

dY_{t}^{i}=-\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi(m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{i})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma dB_{t}^{i}

The quality of this particle approximation to (
2.3
) is precisely a question of quantitative propagation of chaos.
This question has been tackled by
[
17
]
and
[
60
]
(see also
[
16
]
for the related kinetic version). The article
[
17
]
proves a strong propagation of chaos result with global methods, deducing from subadditivity that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{H}(\pi_{t}^{k}\,\|\,\mu_{t}^{\otimes k})=O\Big(e^{-at}+\frac{k}{n}\Big).

On the other hand
[
60
]
proves a weak propagation of chaos result for a specific function
V
with a strong (superquadratic) regularization: for every
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{bd}}^{2}(\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})),

\sup_{t\geq 0}{\mathbb{E}}[|f(m_{t}^{n})-f(\mu_{t})|]^{2}=O(1/n).

The more recent paper
[
53
]
improved the dependence (hidden here) on the log-Sobolev constants. See also the recent work
[
19
,
65
]
with improved uniform-in-
n
bounds on the LSI constant of the
n
-particle system, which lead quickly to uniform-in-time propagation of chaos when combined with finite-horizon bounds.
These results were all obtained in the flat convexity regime, which arises naturally in models of two-layer neural networks.

The natural question of
sharp
uniform-in-time propagation of chaos for MFLD has not yet been addressed.
An application of Theorem
2.8
yields such a result in the displacement convex regime:

Corollary 2.12
.

Take
\lambda>0
. Let the functional
\Psi
be
\lambda
-convex, in the sense that for any square-integrable
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued random variable
X
and
Y
defined on any probability space, and any
t\in(0,1)
,

(2.6)

\Psi(\mathcal{L}(tX+(1-t)Y))\leq t\Psi(\mathcal{L}(X))+(1-t)\Psi(\mathcal{L}(Y))-\frac{1}{2}\lambda t(1-t){\mathbb{E}}|X-Y|^{2}.

Assume
V=-\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi
exists and satisfies Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
as well as
\|\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}\Psi\|_{\text{op}}\|^{2}_{\infty}<\lambda^{2}/3
. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{H}(\pi_{t}^{k}\,\|\,\mu_{t}^{\otimes k})=O(k^{2}/n^{2}).

Proof.

Apply Theorem
2.8
after noting that the convexity assumption (
2.6
) implies (in fact, is equivalent to) the monotonicity condition
Assumption (UiT)
(2) for
V=-\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi
; see Lemma
B.1
.
∎

Corollary
2.12
imposes stronger assumptions than previous work on MFLD, namely higher order smoothness and the weak interaction condition, but it improves the rate of convergence to the sharp
k^{2}/n^{2}
.

There are two natural followup problems which we do not address here and would likely require different methods. The first, as mentioned before, is removing the likely unnecessary smallness condition
\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}\Psi\|^{2}_{\infty}<\lambda^{2}/3
. The second is to address the flat-convex instead of displacement convex regime, in light of its relevance to neural network models. We note that (
2.6
) is equivalent to the usual notion of
\lambda
-displacement convexity from the theory of optimal transport, as explained in
[
34
, Lemma 3.6]
; it is also equivalent to the
(\lambda/n)
-convexity of the function
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}\ni(y^{1},\ldots,y^{n})\mapsto\Psi(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{y^{i}})
.

2.5.
Application: Mean Field Games

In this section we introduce the mean field game convergence problem and explain how Theorem
2.3
leads to a new sharp propagation of chaos estimate.
Fix a time horizon
T>0
, and consider cost functions
f_{0},g:\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
f_{1}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Precise assumptions on these functions will be given in the theorem statements below. Consider the following game played by
i=1,\dots,n
players: each player controls a private state process

dY_{t}^{i}=\alpha^{i}(t,Y_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma dB_{t}^{i},\qquad Y_{0}^{i}\sim\text{ iid }\mu_{0},

and wishes to minimize the cost functional

\displaystyle J_{n}(\alpha^{1},\ldots,\alpha^{n})={\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}f_{0}(m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{i})+f_{1}(Y_{t}^{i},\alpha^{i}(t,Y_{t}))\,dt+g(m_{T}^{n},Y_{T}^{i}).\bigg]

where
m_{t}^{n}
is the empirical measure associated to
Y_{t}^{1},\dots,Y_{t}^{n}
. Here the choice variable
\alpha_{i}
is a Markovian (closed-loop) control, chosen to be a measurable function of
Y_{t}=(Y^{1}_{t},\ldots,Y^{n}_{t})
of linear growth, so that the SDE for
Y
has a unique in law solution. The associated Hamiltonian
H
is

H(x,y)=-\inf_{a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\{a\cdot y+f_{1}(x,a)\}.

We will always assume that
H
is a continuous function with a derivative in the
y
argument so that the following expressions make sense. A Nash equilibrium is a profile of controls
(\alpha^{i})_{i=1}^{n}
such that for each
i
,

\alpha^{i}\in\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\beta^{i}}J_{n}(\beta^{i},(\alpha^{j})_{j\neq i}).

Under some regularity assumptions (see for instance
[
11
, Proposition 6.26]
) a Nash equilibrium exists and takes the form

(2.7)

\alpha^{i}(t,x)=-\nabla_{y}H(x_{i},\nabla_{x_{i}}u^{n,i}(t,x)),\quad x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n},

where
(u^{n,i})_{i=1}^{n}:[0,T]\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the solution to the so-called Nash system of PDEs:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}u_{t}^{n,i}(x)&-H(x^{i},\nabla_{x^{i}}u^{n,i}_{t}(x))+\sigma^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta_{x^{j}}u_{t}^{n,i}(x)+f_{0}(m_{x}^{n},x^{i})\\
&-\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\nabla_{y}H(x^{j},\nabla_{x^{j}}u^{n,j}_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla_{x^{j}}u_{t}^{n,i}(x)=0,\\
u_{T}^{n,i}(x)&=g(m_{x}^{n},x^{i}),\qquad m^{n}_{x}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{x^{j}}.\end{split}

The equilibrium trajectories follow the dynamics

(2.9)

\displaystyle dY_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{y}H(Y_{t}^{i},\nabla_{x_{i}}u^{n,i}(t,Y_{t}))\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma\,dB_{t}^{i}.

Viewing
Y^{i}
as a random element of
C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, let
\pi^{k}=\mathcal{L}(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{k})
for
1\leq k\leq n
.

The large-
n
behavior of the above
n
-player game is expected to be captured by the following mean field game, in which the large finite population is replaced by a single representative player interacting with a mean field.
The representative player controls the state process
dX_{t}=\alpha_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma dB_{t}
. Assuming the distribution of the other players’ states at time
t
is given by
\mu_{t}
, the representative player seeks to choose
\alpha
to minimize the cost functional

\displaystyle J(\alpha,\mu)={\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}f_{0}(\mu_{t},X_{t})+f_{1}(X_{t},\alpha_{t})\,dt+g(\mu_{T},X_{T})\bigg].

A mean field equilibrium (MFE) is a couple
(\mu,\alpha)
such that
\alpha\in\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}J(\cdot,\mu)
and
\mu=\mathcal{L}(X)
. Associated to this mean field game is the so-called
master equation
, a PDE for a function
U:[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
which plays the role of the value function:

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}U_{t}(\nu,x)&-H(x,\nabla_{x}U_{t}(\nu,x))+\sigma^{2}\Delta_{x}U_{t}(\nu,x)+f_{0}(\nu,x)\\
&+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big(\sigma^{2}\text{Tr}(\nabla_{y}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U_{t}(\nu,x,y))-\nabla_{y}H(y,\nabla_{x}U_{t}(\nu,y))\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U_{t}(\nu,x,y)\Big)\,d\nu(y)=0\\
U_{T}(\nu,x)&=g(\nu,x).\end{split}

When the master equation admits a suitably smooth solution, the unique MFE control is given by

(2.11)

\alpha(t,\nu,x)=-\nabla_{y}H(x,\nabla_{x}U_{t}(\nu,x))

so that the equilibrium state process is

(2.12)

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{y}H(X_{t},\nabla_{x}U_{t}(\mathcal{L}(X_{t}),X_{t}))\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma\,dB_{t}.

Let
\mu={\mathcal{L}}(X)
denote the law (on path space) of this process. We refer to
[
6
]
and
[
10
]
for additional background on mean field games.

The
convergence problem
in mean field game theory is the problem of rigorously showing that the
n
-player Nash equilibria converge in some sense to the MFE. This is known to hold qualitatively in great generality
[
45
,
28
]
. The earliest quantitative results go through the master equation
[
6
]
, but more recent progress has developed non-asymptotic viewpoints based on an analysis of the PDEs
[
22
,
21
]
or forward-backward SDEs
[
43
,
57
]
which characterize the
n
-player equilibria.
Comparing the
n
-player equilibrium states to the state process of the MFE, these papers obtain estimates like

(2.13)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{W}^{2}_{1}(\mu^{\otimes k}_{t},\pi^{k}_{t})=O(k/n).

The following theorem improves this to
(k/n)^{2}
, when the master equation admits a smooth enough solution.

Theorem 2.13
.

Assume that:

(1)

The functions
f_{0},f_{1},g
are measurable and subpolynomial in the following sense: There exists
C\geq 0
such that for each
x,a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

|f_{0}(\nu,x)|+|f_{1}(x,a)|+|g(\nu,x)|\leq C\Big(1+|x|^{2}+|a|^{2}+\int|y|^{2}\,d\nu(y)\Big).

(2)

There exists a classical solution
(u^{i,n})_{i=1}^{n}
to the Nash system (
2.8
) that has space derivatives with linear growth, and a corresponding Nash equilibrium given by (
2.7
) with a well posed optimal state process
(
2.9
)
.

(3)

There exists a classical solution
U
to the master equation (
2.10
) with
\nabla_{x}U,\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U,\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U
bounded and Lipschitz uniformly in time and a corresponding Mean Field Equilibrium
(\mu,\alpha)
where
\alpha
is given by (
2.11
) and
\mu
is the law on path space of the equilibrium state process given by (
2.12
).

Assume further that

(a)

The function
\nabla_{x}U_{t}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bd}}^{6}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
uniformly in
t
:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leq T}\sup_{0<\mathsf{w}+|\bm{i}|\leq 6}|D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}\nabla_{x}U_{t}|\leq C<\infty.

(b)

The initial condition
\mu_{0}
satisfies the
T_{1}
inequality of assumption
Assumption (

A

)
(2).

(c)

The function
(x,y)\mapsto\nabla_{y}H(x,y)
has 6 bounded derivatives.

Then

\mathbb{W}^{2}_{1}(\mu^{\otimes k},\pi^{k})=O(k^{2}/n^{2}).

Proof.

The proof combines Theorem
2.3
with a known estimate from the mean field game literature.
Note that Theorem
2.3
does not apply directly, because the drift of
Y^{i}
in (
2.9
) varies with
n
due to the dependence on
n
of
u^{n,i}
.
We follow the idea of
[
6
]
of introducing an intermediate process
(Z^{i})_{i=1}^{n}
defined by

(2.14)

\displaystyle dZ_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{y}H(Z_{t}^{i},\nabla_{x}U(t,m_{t}^{n},Z_{t}^{i}))\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma dB_{t}^{i},\qquad m_{t}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{t}^{j}}.

This system, unlike (
2.9
), does fit into the setting of our Theorem
2.3
, by setting

V(t,\nu,x)=-\nabla_{y}H(x,\nabla_{x}U(t,\nu,x)).

(See Remark
2.7
regarding time dependence.) Thanks to the assumed regularity of
U
and
H
, we can apply our Corollary
2.4
to obtain a sharp estimate on the propagation of chaos,

(2.15)

\mathbb{W}_{1}\big({\mathcal{L}}(Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{k}),\,\mu^{\otimes k}\big)=O(k/n).

This was the piece missing in prior work on the mean field game convergence problem.
It is known from prior work that

{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|Y^{1}_{t}-Z^{1}_{t}|\bigg]\leq C/n,

for some constant
C
independent of
n
.
This was shown in
[
6
, Theorem 6.6]
under smoothness assumptions for the master equation implied by ours, when the state space is the torus, and we refer to
[
11
, Theorem 6.32]
or
[
26
, Theorem 4.2]
for analogous estimates on the state space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Using exchangeability, this implies

\mathbb{W}_{1}\big({\mathcal{L}}(Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{k}),\,\pi^{k}\big)\leq{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big|Y_{t}^{i}-Z_{t}^{i}\big|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg]\leq k{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big|Y_{t}^{1}-Z_{t}^{1}\big|\Big]\leq\frac{Ck}{n}.

Combine this with (
2.15
) and the triangle inequality to complete the proof.
∎

Note that Theorem
2.13
requires the solution of the master equation
U
to be very regular. Regularity of the (first order) master equation is well studied, for instance in
[
14
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
11
, Section 5]
. In particular the latter three references provide conditions under which the master equation has two bounded Wasserstein derivatives. The following lemma gives some sufficient conditions for
U
to satisfy the assumption of Theorem
2.13
on short time horizons. We expect the result to be valid for arbitrary time horizon under a monotonicity condition, such as that of Lasry-Lions
[
50
]
or displacement monotonicity
[
33
]
, but we do not pursue this here.

Lemma 2.14
.

Assume that

(1)

\nabla_{x}f_{0},\nabla_{x}g
are of class
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bd}}^{6}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
;

(2)

\nabla_{a}^{2}f_{1}(x,a)\geq I\lambda
for
\lambda>0
;

(3)

\nabla_{x}f_{1},\nabla_{y}H
are six times continuously differentiable, with derivatives of order 1–6 being bounded.

Then, for
T
sufficiently small,
U
satisfies assumption (1) of Theorem
2.13
on
[0,T]
.

We omit the proof, as it is essentially a long continuation of the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 5.49]
. The idea is to consider the BSDE representation of
\nabla_{x}U
given by the Master field (
[
11
, Section 4]
, which allows to prove regularity for
\nabla_{x}U
by proving regularity for the driving BSDE system. This is done by differentiating the BSDE informally, checking that the candidate solution is uniquely solvable, and taking limits. This program is carried out in detail in
[
14
]
and
[
11
, Section 5]
for the first two derivatives; higher order derivatives work in a similar way, as shown in the case of forward SDEs by
[
15
]
.

2.6.
Application: Mean Field Control

In this section we consider the convergence problem for mean field control, which can be seen as a variant of mean field games in which players cooperate instead of competing. The mathematical analysis is considerably simpler, as optimization is simpler than Nash equilibrium, and as a result much more is known.

Consider cost functions
F,G:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
L:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Consider the
n
-particle cooperative control problem

(2.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\alpha}\,\,&{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{T}L(Y_{t}^{i},\alpha_{t}^{i})+F(m_{Y_{t}}^{n})\,dt+G(m_{Y_{T}}^{n})\bigg],\text{ where for }x\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n},m^{n}_{x}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{x^{j}},\\
\text{s.t.\,\,}dY_{t}^{i}&=\alpha_{t}^{i}\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma\,dB_{t}^{i}\qquad Y_{0}^{i}\sim\text{ iid }\mu_{0},\ \ i=1,\dots,n.\end{split}

Classical results yield that under regularity assumptions on
F,G
and
L
, there exist optimal feedback controls which can be expressed in terms of the value function
v(t,x)
of the control problem and the Hamiltonian

H(x,y)=-\inf_{a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\{a\cdot y+L(x,a)\}.

where we recall that the value function
v:[0,T]\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
solves an
n
-dimensional HJB equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{t}(x)

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\Delta v_{t}(x)+F(m_{x}^{n})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H(x^{i},n\nabla_{x_{i}}v_{t}(x))=0

\displaystyle v_{T}(x)

\displaystyle=G(m_{x}^{n})

The optimal trajectories are given by the SDE

(2.17)

\displaystyle dY_{t}^{i}=-\nabla_{y}H(Y_{t}^{i},n\nabla_{i}v_{t}(Y_{t}))\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma\,dB_{t}^{i}

Viewing each
Y^{i}
as a random element of
C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, let
\pi^{k}=\mathcal{L}(Y^{1},\dots,Y^{k})
for
1\leq k\leq n
.

The large
n
limit of the cooperative problem is described by the mean field control problem,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\alpha}\ \ &\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}[L(X_{t},\alpha_{t})]+F({\mathcal{L}}(X_{t}))\,dt+G({\mathcal{L}}(X_{T}))\\
\text{s.t.\,\,}dX_{t}&=\alpha_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma\,dB_{t},\quad X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}.\end{split}

Under appropriate smoothness assumption on
F
,
G
, and
L
, the optimal trajectory is given by

(2.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}dX_{t}&=-\nabla_{y}H(X_{t},\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U_{t}(\mu_{t},X_{t}))\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,\sigma dB_{t},\qquad\mu=\mathcal{L}(X),\end{split}

where
U:[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a suitably defined value function of the problem, which solves the infinite dimensional HJB equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U_{t}(\nu)

\displaystyle+F(\nu)+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big(\sigma^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\big(\nabla_{y}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U_{t}(\nu,y)\big)-H(y,\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U_{t}(\nu,y))\Big)d\nu(y)=0

\displaystyle U_{T}(\nu)

\displaystyle=G(\nu).

The convergence problem is again about relating the finite-
n
control problem to the mean field problem. Qualitative results are known in general situations similar to mean field games
[
44
,
29
]
, but the quantitative picture of mean field control is now understood in much finer detail due to the sequence of papers
[
5
,
8
,
25
,
7
]
. These papers, especially
[
25
]
, clarify how convergence rate of the value functions depends on the smoothness of the cost functions. As with mean field games, these sharp convergence rates for the value functions do not translate directly to sharp convergence rates (propagation of chaos) for the state processes.

We address this gap in the literature in the nicest regime where the master equation is smooth.

Theorem 2.15
.

Assume that

(1)

The functions
F,L,G
are measurable and subpolynomial in the following sense: there exists
C\geq 0
such that for each
x,a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

|F(\nu)|+|L(x,a)|+|G(\nu)|\leq C\Big(1+|x|^{2}+|a^{2}|+\int|y|^{2}\,d\nu(y)\Big).

(2)

There exists a classical solution
v
to the
n
-HJB such that the optimal state process given by
(
2.17
)
is well posed.

(3)

There exists a classical solution
U
to the master equation such that the optimal state given by (
2.19
) is well posed. Moreover
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U,\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U
are Lipschitz uniformly in
t
.

Assume further that

(a)

The function
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U
belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{bd}^{6}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
uniformly in time:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\leq T}\sup_{0<\mathsf{w}+|\bm{i}|\leq 6}|D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}U_{t}|\leq C<\infty.

(b)

The initial condition
\mu_{0}
satisfies the
T_{1}
inequality of assumption
Assumption (

A

)
(2).

(c)

The function
(x,y)\mapsto\nabla_{y}H(x,y)
has 6 bounded derivatives.

Then

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{W}^{2}_{1}(\mu_{t}^{\otimes k},\pi_{t}^{k})=O(k^{2}/n^{2}).

We give the proof in Appendix
C
, and it closely parallels the argument for Theorem
2.13
. The assumed smoothness of the value function
U
can be checked along similar lines to Lemma
2.14
, but more easily so we omit the details.

2.7.
Related literature

The closest result to our Theorem
2.3
in prior literature, for non-pairwise
V
(and not uniform in time), is in previous work of the second author
[
47
, Theorem 2.17, Corollary 2.18]
. There it was shown that
\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[t]))=O(k^{3-\epsilon}/n^{2-\epsilon})
for arbitrary
\epsilon>0
. This was not so satisfactory, both due to the worse exponent on
n
and especially
k
, and more importantly due to restrictive assumptions on
V
. Indeed, there it was required that
V
admits a power series expansion around
\mu_{t}
,

V(\nu,x)=V(\mu_{t},x)+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{p}}\delta_{m}^{p}V(\mu_{t},x,\bm{y})d(\nu-\mu_{t})^{\otimes p}(\bm{y})

with a strong decay condition on the flat derivatives:
\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\|\delta_{m}^{p}V\|_{\infty}e^{cp}/p!<\infty
for all
c>0
. This already excludes functions like
V(\mu,x)=f(\int y\,d\mu(y))
for smooth
f
of compact support, which have unbounded flat derivatives but bounded Wasserstein derivatives. And it is prohibitively difficult to check in the setting of mean field games and control described above.

Let us highlight also the result of
[
62
, Theorem 4.2]
, which also studied non-pairwise interactions
V
, giving a (global) bound on the rate of convergence of the empirical measure. For
\overline{Y}^{i}\sim\mu
denoting iid solutions of the McKean-Vlasov equation, coupled with
(Y^{1},\ldots,Y^{n})
synchronously (i.e., using the same Brownian motions and initial conditions, they show that

(2.20)

{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|Y^{i}_{t}-\overline{Y}^{i}_{t}|^{2}\bigg]=O(1/n).

In the case of pairwise interaction, where
V
is given as in
1.3
with
\phi
Lipschitz, this bound was already implicit in
[
61
]
. And it was already well known that a suboptimal (dimension-dependent) bound can be shown for non-pairwise
V
under a
\mathbb{W}_{2}
-Lipschitz assumption by using the same synchronous coupling approach
[
9
, Theorem 1.20]
.
The contribution of
[
62
, Theorem 4.2]
was to show how smoothness of
V
leads to the sharper bound in (
2.20
) even in the non-pairwise case.
By exchangeability, the global bound (
2.20
) implies the local bound

(2.21)

\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu^{\otimes k}_{t},\pi_{t}^{k})=O(k/n).

The main assumption in
[
62
, Theorem 4.2]
is that
V
has 3 bounded Wasserstein derivatives. Our Theorem
2.3
improves (
2.21
) to
O(k^{2}/n^{2})
, at the price of requiring more smoothness of
V
. We employ similar methods of
weak propagation of chaos
as in
[
62
]
, but implemented in a new way within the BBGKY approach of
[
47
]
.

Lastly, it is worth mentioning that relative entropy and related modulated energy methods have been popular recently not only due to their ability to obtain sharp rates of propagation of chaos, but also (arguably more so) because they are able to handle singular (pairwise) interactions which arise in many physically relevant models. The work of Jabin and Z. Wang
[
40
,
42
]
was particularly influential in this direction, and S. Wang recently showed in
[
64
]
how to combine the ideas of
[
42
]
with the BBGKY approach of
[
47
]
to achieve sharp rates for certain singular interactions. For additional references and discussion of the active line of research on mean field limits for singular interactions, especially for Coulomb and Riesz gases, we refer to the recent survey
[
59
]
.
Quantitative propagation of chaos for models without noise is not as well understood; sharp rates are known in weaker metrics
[
56
,
30
]
.
For further references and methods in the vast literature on propagation of chaos, we refer to the thorough review articles
[
12
,
13
]
.

(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})

\mathcal{F}
-Brownian motion
B_{t}

Y_{t}=(Y_{t}^{1},\dots,Y_{t}^{n})

1.1
). We will use the letter

3.1.
Hierarchies of linear differential inequalities

As a first preparation, we present a Gronwall-type lemma for a type of linear systems of differential inequality that arises in BBGKY-approaches to entropic propagation of chaos. Initially, in
[
47
]
, it was analyzed by a direct iteration and somewhat tedious manipulations of iterated integrals. A simpler inductive argument is given in
[
38
]
. Here we give a short proof following the strategy of
[
49
, Section 1.4]
.

Consider functions
R=R_{t}:[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
f=f_{t}(k):[0,T]\times[n]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, and scalars
a,b,c\geq 0,p\geq 1
. We will deal with the following system of differential inequalities: For each
1\leq k\leq n
,

(3.1)

\frac{d}{dt}f_{t}(k)\leq ak(f_{t}(k+1)-f_{t}(k))1_{k<n}+\frac{b}{n^{2}}k^{p}+kR_{t}-cf_{t}(k).

Hierarchies of this type are a cornerstone of sharp approaches to propagation of chaos.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
f:[0,T]\times[n]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a non-decreasing function in
k
that satisfies (
3.1
) for
p\in\{2,3\}
. Assume that there exists a constant
C_{0}\geq 0
such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle f_{s}(k)\leq C_{0}\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}},\quad 1\leq k\leq n.

Then, for
0\leq s\leq T
and
1\leq k\leq n

f_{T}(k)\leq 2C_{0}\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}}e^{(2a-c)(T-s)}+\frac{8bk^{p}}{n^{2}}\int_{s}^{T}e^{(ap-c)(t-s)}\,dt+k\int_{s}^{T}e^{(a-c)(t-s)}R_{t}\,dt.

Proof.

We will use a semigroup interpretation of (
3.1
) to organize the analysis.
Consider the
n\times n
matrix
\mathcal{G}
which acts on a function
\varphi:[n]\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

\mathcal{G}\varphi(k)=ak(\varphi(k+1)-\varphi(k))\mathbf{1}_{k<n}.

Each row of this matrix sums to zero (i.e.,
\mathcal{G}
maps constant functions to zero), and every off-diagonal entry is nonnegative. Hence,
\mathcal{G}
is the infinitesimal generator of a continuous-time Markov process on state space
[n]
; in fact, this process goes by the name of the
Yule process
stopped at level
n
, and we denote it by
(\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. The matrix exponential is the semigroup of this process and admits the representation
e^{t\mathcal{G}}\varphi(k)={\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(\mathcal{Y}_{t})\,|\,\mathcal{Y}_{0}=k]
. We will not use this stochastic interpretation except to deduce a monotonicity property, namely that
e^{t\mathcal{G}}\varphi\geq e^{t\mathcal{G}}\psi
pointwise whenever
\varphi\geq\psi
pointwise.
With this notation we can rewrite (
3.1
) as a coordinatewise inequality between vectors,

\frac{d}{dt}f_{t}\leq(\mathcal{G}-cI)f_{t}+\frac{b}{n^{2}}m_{p}+R_{t}m_{1},

where
I
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix, and
m_{q}(k):=k^{q}
for
q>0
.
Using the aforementioned monotonicity, we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big[e^{-ct}e^{t\mathcal{G}}f_{T-t}\Big]

\displaystyle=e^{-ct}e^{t\mathcal{G}}\Big[(\mathcal{G}-cI)f_{T-t}-\frac{d}{ds}f_{s}\big|_{s=T-t}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq-e^{-ct}e^{t\mathcal{G}}\big(\frac{b}{n^{2}}m_{p}+R_{t}m_{1}\big).

Integrate to get the coordinatewise inequality

(3.3)

\displaystyle f_{T}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-c(T-s)}e^{(T-s)\mathcal{G}}f_{s}+\int_{s}^{T}e^{-c(t-s)}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{G}}\Big(\frac{b}{n^{2}}m_{p}+R_{t}m_{1}\Big)\,dt.

Now we estimate
e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{q}
for
q=1,2,3
. This could be done by using the known fact that the Yule process follows a negative binomial distribution, but we instead present a short self-contained argument.

•

q=1
: Use
\mathcal{G}m_{1}(k)=ak(m_{1}(k+1)-m_{1}(k))=ak
to get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{1}=e^{t\mathcal{G}}\mathcal{G}m_{1}=ae^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{1},

and thus
e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{1}=e^{at}m_{1}
.

•

q=2
: We have
\mathcal{G}m_{2}(k)=ak\big((k+1)^{2}-k^{2}\big)=2am_{2}(k)+am_{1}(k)
, and thus

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{2}

\displaystyle=2ae^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{2}+ae^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{1}.

Multiply by
e^{-2at}
and integrate to get

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{2}

\displaystyle=e^{2at}m_{2}+ae^{2at}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2as}e^{s\mathcal{G}}m_{1}\,ds.

Using
e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{1}=e^{at}m_{1}
, we get the coordinatewise inequality

e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{2}=e^{2at}m_{2}+e^{2at}(1-e^{-at})m_{1}\leq 2e^{2at}m_{2}.

•

q=3
: We have
\mathcal{G}m_{3}(k)=ak\big((k+1)^{3}-k^{3}\big)=3am_{3}(k)+3am_{2}(k)+am_{1}(k)
, and thus

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{3}

\displaystyle=3ae^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{3}+3ae^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{2}+ae^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{1}.

Multiply by
e^{-3at}
and integrate to get

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{3}

\displaystyle=e^{3at}m_{3}+ae^{3at}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-3as}\big(3e^{s\mathcal{G}}m_{2}+e^{s\mathcal{G}}m_{1}\big)\,ds.

Use
e^{s\mathcal{G}}m_{1}\leq e^{2as}m_{3}
and
e^{s\mathcal{G}}m_{2}\leq 2e^{2as}m_{3}
to get

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg[e^{3at}+7ae^{3at}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-as}\,ds\bigg]m_{3}\leq 8e^{3at}m_{3}.

Using the
q=2
case and the time-
s
assumption, we have
e^{(T-s)\mathcal{G}}f_{s}(k)\leq 2C_{0}e^{2a(T-s)}k^{2}/n^{2}
.
We can summarize all cases
q=1,2,3
as
e^{t\mathcal{G}}m_{q}\leq 8e^{qat}m_{q}
.
Combining these bounds with (
3.3
) yields

\displaystyle f_{T}(k)\leq 2C_{0}e^{(2a-c)(T-s)}\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}}+\int_{s}^{T}e^{-c(t-s)}\Big(\frac{8bk^{p}}{n^{2}}e^{pa(t-s)}+R_{t}ke^{a(t-s)}\Big)\,dt.

∎

3.2.
Short time Analysis

In this section we prove the differential inequality
1.8
, and perform the iteration procedure to tackle the pairwise term; we leave the analysis of the remainder for the next sections. First note that Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
(1) implies that
V
is
L
-Lipschitz for some

, in the sense that

|V(\nu,x)-V(\nu^{\prime},x^{\prime})|\leq L(|x-x^{\prime}|+\mathbb{W}_{2}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})).

We begin with a fairly standard fact about T
1
inequalities propagating along Lipschitz SDE solutions, but we include the short proof in Appendix
A
as we could not locate a precise reference with explicit constants.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
holds. Define

C_{\textup{T}_{1}}(t):=e^{2Lt}\max(2C_{\textup{T}_{1}}(0),4\sigma^{2}t).

Let
0\leq t\leq T
. Then
\mu[t]
satisfies the T
1
inequality

\mathbb{W}_{1}^{2}(\mu[t],\nu[t])\leq 2C_{\textup{T}_{1}}(t)\,\mathbb{H}(\nu[t]\,\|\,\mu[t]),\quad\text{ for all }\nu[t]\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,t];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})).

Moreover, for every
p\geq 1
we have

\sup_{t\leq T}{\mathbb{E}}\big[|X_{t}|^{p}\big]+\sup_{t\leq T}{\mathbb{E}}\big[|Y^{1}_{t}|^{p}\big]\leq C_{p,T}<\infty.

where the constant
C_{p,T}
does not depend on
n
.

The first main ingredient of the argument is a probabilistic representation of the law
\pi^{k}[T]
, provided by a well known projection lemma (see
[
47
, Lemma 4.1]
and references therein).
Let
Y^{i}[t]=(Y^{i}_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
denote the trajectory up to time
t
of
Y^{i}
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, and similarly write
Y^{[k]}[t]=(Y^{1}[t],\dots,Y^{k}[t])
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
holds.
There exists a standard Brownian motion
W=(W^{i})_{i=1}^{k}
, with respect to the filtration generated by
Y^{[k]}
, such that

(3.4)

dY_{t}^{i}={\mathbb{E}}\big[V(m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{i})\mid Y^{[k]}[t]\big]\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma dW^{i}_{t},\ \ i=1,\dots,k.

This can be seen as a form of the BBGKY hierarchy, and we use it to develop entropy estimates. Because of the non-linearity of
V
, there will be a remainder term
\mathcal{R}(t)
that is new here compared to prior work on sharp propagation of chaos.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
holds.

(1)

There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
n
and
k
such that

(3.5)

\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[T]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[T])\leq C\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}+C\int_{0}^{T}k\mathcal{R}(t)\,dt,

where we define

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}^{2}V(sm_{t}^{n}+(1-s)\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},y,z)\,d(m_{t}^{n}-\mu_{t})^{\otimes 2}(y,z)\Big|^{2}\bigg]\,\,ds.

(2)

If we further assume that
\mathbb{H}(\pi^{3}[T]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes 3}[T])\leq M/n^{2}
for some constant
M
independent of
n
and
k
, then

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[T]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[T])\leq C\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}}+C\int_{0}^{T}k\mathcal{R}(t)\,dt,

where again
C
is a constant that does not depend on
n
or
k
.

Proof.

We begin with
(1)
. Let
1\leq k<n
. By Lemma
3.3
,
\pi^{k}[T]
is the law of a weak solution of the SDE (
3.4
). Using a well known formula for the path entropy (e.g.,
[
47
, Lemma 4.4]
) and exchangeability of
Y^{1},\dots,Y^{k}
we have the following formula for the path entropy between
\pi^{k}
and
\mu^{\otimes k}
:

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[t])&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big|V(\mu_{s},Y_{s}^{i})-{\mathbb{E}}\big[V(m_{s}^{n},Y_{s}^{i})\mid Y^{[k]}[s]\big]\Big|^{2}\bigg]\,ds\\
&=\frac{k}{4\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big|V(\mu_{s},Y_{s}^{1})-{\mathbb{E}}\big[V(m_{s}^{n},Y_{s}^{1})\mid Y^{[k]}[s]\big]\Big|^{2}\bigg]\,ds.\end{split}

In particular,
\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[t])
is absolutely continuous in
t
, and it will be convenient to take derivatives. First, we expand
V
around
\mu_{t}
in flat derivatives up to second order:

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1})-{\mathbb{E}}\Big[V(m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{1})\mid Y^{[k]}[t]\Big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},y)d(m_{t}^{n}-\mu_{t})(y)\,\Big|\,Y^{[k]}[t]\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{1}(1-s){\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\mu+(1-s)m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{1},y,z)\,d(m_{t}^{n}-\mu_{t})^{\otimes 2}(y,z)\,\Big|\,Y^{[k]}[t]\Big]\,ds.

This is nothing but a Taylor expansion with remainder of
f(1)
around
f(0)
for the scalar function
f(s)=V(s\mu_{t}+(1-s)m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{1})
; for details see, e.g.,
[
15
, Lemma 2.2]
. Break up the square and use Jensen’s inequality to eliminate the conditional expectation from the second term,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[t])

\displaystyle\leq\text{Term}_{1}+\frac{k}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathcal{R}(t),

where
\mathcal{R}(t)
was defined in the statement of the proposition, and

\displaystyle\text{Term}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{k}{2\sigma^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big|{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},Y_{t}^{i})-\int\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},z)\,d\mu_{t}(z)\,\Big|\,Y^{[k]}[t]\Big]\Big|^{2}\bigg].

Term
1
appears also in the previous arguments from
[
47
]
: observe that it features a pairwise interaction structure. The second term
\mathcal{R}(t)
is a remainder that is due to the higher-order interactions embodied in
V
(and indeed
\mathcal{R}(t)=0
for pairwise interactions
V
).

We first analyze Term
1
, along similar lines to
[
47
]
.
Break up the sum to find

(3.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Term}_{1}&\leq\frac{k}{\sigma^{2}n^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},Y_{t}^{i})-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},z)\,d\mu_{t}(z)\Big|^{2}\bigg]\\
&+\frac{k}{\sigma^{2}n^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\bigg|\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\bigg({\mathbb{E}}\Big[\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},Y_{t}^{i})\,\Big|\,Y^{[k]}[t]\Big]-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},z)\,d\mu_{t}(z)\bigg)\bigg|^{2}\bigg].\end{split}

Fix a continuous function
y:[0,T]\to({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k}
and
i>k
. By Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
(3), the map
z\mapsto\delta_{m}V(\nu,x,z)
is Lipschitz uniformly in
\nu,x
since
\nabla_{z}\delta_{m}V(m,x,z)=\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(m,x,z)
is bounded. Define
\pi^{k+1\mid k}_{[t]\mid[t]}(\cdot\mid Y^{[k]}[t])
to be the conditional law of
Y^{k+1}[t]
given
Y^{[k]}[t]
. By Kantorovich duality,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bigg|{\mathbb{E}}&\bigg[\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},Y_{t}^{i})\,\Big|\,Y^{[k]}[t]\bigg]-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},z)\,d\mu_{t}(z)\bigg|\\
&=\bigg|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},z_{t})\,d\Big(\pi_{[t]\mid[t]}^{i\mid k}\,(\cdot\mid Y^{[k]}[t])-\mu[t]\Big)(z)\bigg|\\
&\leq\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}\mathbb{W}_{1}\big(\pi_{[t]\mid[t]}^{i\mid k}(\cdot\mid Y^{[k]}[t]),\mu[t]\big).\end{split}

Plug
3.11

\displaystyle\text{Term}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma^{2}n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big|\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},Y_{t}^{1})-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}V(\mu_{t},Y_{t}^{1},z)\,d\mu_{t}(z)\Big|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\frac{k(n-k)}{\sigma^{2}n^{2}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbb{W}_{1}^{2}\big(\pi_{[t]\mid[t]}^{i\mid k}(\cdot\mid Y^{[k]}[t]),\mu_{t}\big)\Big].

Use again the fact that

is Lipschitz and the the square-integrability of

from Lemma

to bound the first term by

. For the second term, the T

\mathbb{W}_{1}^{2}\big(\pi_{[t]\mid[t]}^{i\mid k}(\cdot\mid Y^{[k]}[t]),\mu[t]\big)\leq 2C_{\text{T}_{1}}(t)\mathbb{H}\Big(\pi_{[t]\mid[t]}^{k+1\mid k}(\cdot\mid Y^{[k]}[t])\,\big\|\,\mu[t]\Big).

\displaystyle\text{Term}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}+\frac{2k}{\sigma^{2}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}C_{\text{T}_{1}}(t){\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\mathbb{H}\Big(\pi_{[t]\mid[t]}^{k+1\mid k}(\cdot\mid Y^{[k]}[t])\,\big\|\,\mu[t]\Big)\bigg].

(3.14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Term}_{1}&\leq C\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}+\frac{2k}{\sigma^{2}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}C_{\text{T}_{1}}(t)\bigg(\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k+1}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k+1}[t])-\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[t])\bigg).\end{split}

) to finally obtain the differential inequality (

) from the introduction:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[t])&\leq C\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}+Ck\bigg(\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k+1}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k+1}[t])-\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}[t]\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}[t])\bigg)+\frac{k}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathcal{R}(t).\end{split}

We then apply Lemma
3.1
to the functions

, with

, and absorb the exponentials into the constant

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{C_{\text{LS}}}\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}_{t})+C\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}+Ck\mathcal{R}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{kC_{\text{LS}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}\sigma^{2}}\bigg(\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k+1}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k+1}_{t})-\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}_{t})\bigg)

This was valid for
1\leq k<n
, and for
k=n
we have the same bound except that the last term vanishes.
By Theorem
2.3
, for each
1\leq k\leq n
, we have
\mathbb{H}_{\overline{T}}(k)\leq C\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}}
for a constant that depends on
\overline{T}
only.
Use the estimate from Lemma
3.1
with
s=\overline{T}
to find, for
T\geq\overline{T}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{T}(k)

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}}\exp\Big\{(T-\overline{T})\cdot\big(\frac{2C_{\text{LS}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}\sigma^{2}}-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{C_{\text{LS}}}\big)\Big\}

\displaystyle\quad+C\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}\int_{\overline{T}}^{T}\exp\Big\{(t-\overline{T})\cdot\big(\frac{3C_{\text{LS}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}\sigma^{2}}-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{C_{\text{LS}}}\big)\Big\}\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{\overline{T}}^{T}\exp\Big\{(t-\overline{T})\cdot\big(\frac{C_{\text{LS}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}\sigma^{2}}-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{C_{\text{LS}}}\big)\Big\}\Big(\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}+k\mathcal{R}(t)\Big)\,dt

Now, we claim that
\delta>0,\epsilon\in(0,1)
may be chosen so that

a:=\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{C_{\text{LS}}}-\frac{3C_{\text{LS}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}\sigma^{2}}>0.

Recalling that
C_{\textup{LS}}\leq 2(1+\delta)\epsilon^{2}\sigma^{2}/\lambda
, it suffices to have

\frac{(1+\delta)^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\frac{\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}<\frac{1}{3}.

By Assumption
Assumption (UiT)
, we have
\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V\|_{\infty}^{2}<\lambda^{2}/3
, and thus we may indeed take
\delta
small and
\epsilon
close to 1 make
a>0
.
We have thus obtained

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\pi_{T}^{k}\,\|\,\mu_{T}^{\otimes k})\leq C\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}}+C\int_{\overline{T}}^{T}e^{-a(t-\overline{T})}\Big(\frac{k^{3}}{n^{2}}+k\mathcal{R}(t)\Big)\,dt.

To prove (2) under the additional assumption on
\mathbb{H}(\pi_{t}^{3}\,\|\,\mu_{t}^{\otimes 3})
, we adapt the proof of Proposition
3.4
(2) similarly; we omit the details.
∎

Similarly to the short time analysis, we need to estimate the remainder in order to conclude the proof. The difference is that we require a uniform in time bound.

Proposition 3.11
.

If we assume
Assumption (UiT)
, then there exists a constant
C
such that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{R}(t)\leq\frac{C}{n^{2}}.

The proof of Proposition
3.11
is given in Section
5
. Taking it for granted, we can now finish the proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Use Proposition
3.11
along with the inequality from Proposition
3.10
(1) to obtain
\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}_{t})\leq Ck^{3}/n^{2}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
,
where
C
does not depend on
k
or
n
. As a result,
\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{H}(\pi^{3}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes 3}_{t})\leq C/n^{2}
, and thus Proposition
3.10
(2) applies. Combine Proposition
3.10
(2) with Proposition
3.11
to get
\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{H}(\pi^{k}_{t}\,\|\,\mu^{\otimes k}_{t})\leq Ck^{2}/n^{2}
for all

, which completes the proof.
∎

We check that our uniform in time estimates imply the same rates in Wasserstein distance.

Proof of Corollary
2.9
.

The total variation bound follows immediately by applying Pinsker’s inequality. Let
\overline{T}
be the threshold time from Proposition
3.8
. Up to
\overline{T}
we can invoke Corollary
2.4
since
\sup_{t\leq\overline{T}}\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\pi^{k}_{t},\mu_{t}^{\otimes k})\leq\mathbb{W}_{2,\overline{T}}^{2}(\pi^{k}\left[\overline{T}\right],\mu^{\otimes k}\left[\overline{T}\right])=O(k^{2}/n^{2})
. For every
t>\overline{T}
,
\mu_{t}
satisfies the LSI by Proposition
3.8
, and in particular
\mu_{t}^{\otimes k}
satisfies the LSI with the same constant. By the Otto-Villani theorem
[
54
]
,
\mu_{t}^{\otimes k}
satisfies the transport cost inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\pi_{t}^{k},\mu_{t}^{\otimes k})\leq C_{\text{LS}}\mathbb{H}(\pi_{t}^{k}\,\|\,\mu_{t}^{\otimes k}).

Now the uniform in time bound follows by Theorem
2.8
.
∎

4.
Flows and semigroups over
\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

In this section we begin our analysis of the remainder
\mathcal{R}(t)
defined in (
3.6
). Our approach will use techniques developed in
[
3
]
and
[
15
]
.
The strategy can be roughly summarized as follows. We show that the desired estimate
\mathcal{R}(T)=O(1/n^{2})
holds when
m^{n}_{T}
is replaced by
m^{n}_{0}
, which is an empirical measure of iid random variables. This exploits the smoothness of
V
and the dependence of
\mathcal{R}(t)
on the quadratic form
(m^{n}_{t}-\mu_{t})^{\otimes 2}
. Then, we use a semigroup-type analysis (rather than directly differentiating
\mathcal{R}(t)
in
t
) to show that this
1/n^{2}
estimate propagates over time. Special care is needed to exploit dissipativity for the uniform in time case.

It is worth noting that in dimension
d=1
, the machinery of this section is not needed, and a much simpler argument is possible; see Remark
5.1
.

We begin by recalling some results of
[
3
]
on the differentiability of the solution of the McKean-Vlasov equation (
1.2
) with respect to its space and measure arguments. We work with a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
that we assume is rich enough to support a random variable with law
\nu
for every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
; we denote such random variable by
[\nu]
. Consider the decoupled system of SDEs from
[
3
]
:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}X^{s,\nu}_{T}&=[\nu]+\int_{s}^{T}V(\mathcal{L}(X^{s,\nu}_{t}),X^{s,\nu}_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma(W_{T}-W_{s})\,;\\
X^{x,s,\nu}_{T}&=x+\int_{s}^{T}V(\mathcal{L}(X^{s,\nu}_{t}),X^{x,s,\nu}_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\sigma(B_{T}-B_{s}),\end{split}

where
B,W
are independent Brownian motions.
As
V
is Lipschitz, these SDEs have unique strong solutions. Denote
\mu_{t}^{s,\nu}=\mathcal{L}(X^{s,\nu}_{t})
. We are interested in studying the SDE
X^{0,\mu_{0}}
and thus we use the shorthand
\mu_{t}=\mu_{t}^{0,\mu_{0}}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}^{0,\mu_{0}})
where
\mu_{0}
is the original law of
X_{0}
from (
1.2
). Notice that while the value of
X_{t}^{s,\nu}
depends on the random variable
[\nu]
, its law
\mu_{t}^{s,\nu}
does not. Denote also

\mu_{t}^{x,s,\nu}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}).

We will make some use of the time-homogeneity property,

(4.2)

\mu^{s,\nu}_{t}=\mu^{0,\nu}_{t-s},\qquad\mu^{x,s,\nu}_{t}=\mu^{x,0,\nu}_{t-s},\quad t\geq s\geq 0,

as well as the flow property, implied by uniqueness of the dynamics:

(4.3)

\mu^{s,\mu^{r,\nu}_{s}}_{t}=\mu^{r,\nu}_{t},\quad t\geq s\geq r\geq 0.

We record some classical moment bounds, whose proof is omitted.

Lemma 4.1
.

For each
p\geq 1
and
T\geq t\geq s\geq 0
, there exists constant
C
depending on
p,T,s
and
L
such that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,d\mu_{t}^{y,s,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|y|^{p});

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,d\mu^{s,\nu}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C+C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,d\nu(x).

In what follows we will need multiple independent copies of the processes
X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
and
X_{t}^{[\nu],s,\nu}
; we will denote them by
X_{t,(i)}^{x,s,\nu}
and
X_{t,(i)}^{[\nu],s,\nu}
for
i=1,\dots,6
. To be clear, we assume that these copies are started from independent initial conditions and driven by independent Brownian motions. We will denote by
{\mathbb{E}}_{(k)}
the operation of taking expectation with respect to the first
1,\dots,k
copies only (and not with respect to the original process). Since
V
is Lipschitz and differentiable in space, it is classical that the map
x\mapsto X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
admits a derivative with dynamics

(4.4)

\displaystyle d\nabla_{x}X^{x,s,\nu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}V(\mu^{s,\nu}_{t},X^{x,s,\nu}_{t})\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}\,dt,\quad\nabla_{x}X_{s}^{x,s,\nu}=I.

One of the contributions of
[
3
]
, specifically Theorem 4.1 and Remark 4.5 therein, is that the map
L^{2}({\mathbb{P}};\mathcal{F}_{s})\ni\xi\mapsto X_{t}^{x,s,\mathcal{L}(\xi)}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{P}})
(where
L^{2}({\mathbb{P}};\mathcal{F}_{s})
is the space of square-integrable
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable random variables) also admits a Fréchet derivative of the form

\displaystyle D_{\xi}X_{t}^{x,s,\mathcal{L}(\xi)}(\eta)={\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}\big[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\mathcal{L}(\xi)}(\xi)\cdot\eta_{(1)}\big]

where for each
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)
is the unique solution to the SDE

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X^{x,s,\nu}_{t}(y)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X^{s,x,\nu}_{t})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)+{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}\Big[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu}\Big]

(4.5)

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}\Big[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu}(y)\Big],\quad\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{s}^{x,s,\nu}=0.

and
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}
is given by the dynamics

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X^{s,\nu}_{t}(y)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X^{s,\nu}_{t})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X^{s,\nu}_{t}(y)+{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}\Big[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu}\bigg]

(4.6)

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}\bigg[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu}(y)\bigg],\ \ \ \nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{s}^{s,\nu}=0.

In particular, like
[
3
]
we consider
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
the derivative over
\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
of the map
\nu\mapsto X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
.
It is important to note that
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)=\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)\mid_{x=[\nu]}
almost surely by strong uniqueness.
We will differentiate (
4.5
) again with respect to both the measure and space variables given our assumptions on the smoothness of
V
.
Recall from Definition
2.1
the notation for multi-indices
(\mathsf{w},\bm{i})
, which we now begin to use.
To shorten notation, we define

(4.7)

\|X^{s}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}=\sup_{1\leq\mathsf{w}+|\bm{j}|\leq|4|}\sup_{\bm{y},x,\nu}\|D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{j}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(\bm{y})\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{P}})},\qquad t>s\geq 0.

The supremum can be taken to be plus infinity if the process does not admit a derivative.
We will make implicit use of the time-homogeneity property, that
\|X^{s}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}
depends on
t
and
s
only through
t-s
:

\|X^{s}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}=\|X^{0}_{t-s}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}},\qquad t>s\geq 0.

We record some differentiability results in the following lemma, taken from
[
15
, Theorem 3.4]
and
[
24
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The map
(x,\nu)\mapsto X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
is
6
times differentiable in its space and measure arguments. Moreover, there exist constants
C,b>0
such that

\|X_{t}^{0}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}\leq Ce^{bt}.

A key object of the analysis will be the operator
P_{t}
defined as follows for an arbitrary function
f:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
:

P_{t}f(\nu)=f(\mu_{t}^{0,\nu}).

This is the semigroup associated with the McKean-Vlasov equation.
We collect a simple but necessary estimate.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be in
C_{\textup{bd}}^{4}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
and let
(\mathsf{w},\bm{i})
be a multi-index with size
\mathsf{w}+|\bm{i}|=k\leq 4
. Assume that for some constants
A\geq 0
and
p\geq 1
,

|D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}f(\nu,\bm{y})|\leq A\Big(1+|\bm{y}|^{p}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|z|^{p}\,d\nu(z)\Big).

Under Assumption
Assumption (

A

)
there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
A,p
such that

\displaystyle|D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}P_{t}f(\nu,\bm{x})|

\displaystyle\leq\|X^{0}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}C\big(1\vee\|X^{0}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}\big)^{k-1}\Big(1+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|z|^{p}\,d\mu_{t}^{0,\nu}(z)+\sum_{j=1}^{\bm{\mathsf{w}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|z|^{p}\,d\mu_{t}^{x^{j},0,\nu}(z)\Big)

Proof.

For
k=1
, by the derivation rules established in
[
3
, Lemma 6.1]
, Jensen inequality and the growth assumption,

\displaystyle|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}P_{t}f(\nu,y)|

\displaystyle=\Big|{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}f(\mu_{t}^{0,\nu},X_{t}^{y,0,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{y,0,\nu}+\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}f(\mu_{t}^{0,\nu},X_{t}^{0,\nu})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{0,\nu}(y)]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\|X^{0}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}f(\mu_{t}^{0,\nu},X_{t}^{y,0,\nu})|+|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}f(\mu_{t}^{0,\nu},X_{t}^{0,\nu})|\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\|X^{0}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}A{\mathbb{E}}\Big[1+|X_{t}^{y,0,\nu}|^{p}+|X_{t}^{0,\nu}|^{p}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|z|^{p}\,d\mu_{t}^{0,\nu}(z)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 4\|X^{0}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}A\Big(1+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|z|^{p}\,d(\mu_{t}^{0,\nu}+\mu_{t}^{y,0,\nu})(z)\Big)

For higher order terms, we simply repeat this argument after observing that differentiating again leads to second order derivatives of
X_{t}
, namely

\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{\cdot,s,\nu},\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{\cdot,s,\nu}(\cdot),\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{\cdot,s,y}(\cdot),\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}X_{t}^{\cdot,s,\nu}(\cdot,\cdot)

multiplied by the first and second derivatives of
f
.
∎

The point of Lemma
4.3
is that the Wasserstein semigroup (and its derivatives) preserve polynomial growth estimates, quantitatively. The lemma is stated for functions with bounded derivatives, so the polynomial growth bound assumed for
D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}f
is redundant in some sense; the point, however, is that the constant
C
depends only on
A
and not on any uniform bounds of the derivatives of
f
. This will be used within an approximation argument later, when an unbounded function is approximated by bounded ones, and it will be crucial that this
C
does not blow up as we pass to the limit.

The remainder of this section is devoted to uniform-in-time estimates of the derivatives of the process
X_{t}
, which are much more involved. This will be used to obtain uniform in time estimates for the semigroup. As opposed to the PDE approach of
[
27
]
, we adopt a more probabilistic perspective, leveraging the notion of displacement monotonicity.

Lemma 4.4
.

Under Assumption
Assumption (UiT)
, for square-integrable random variables
X
and
Y
,

(4.8)

{\mathbb{E}}[Y^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mathcal{L}(X),X,X_{(1)})Y_{(1)}+Y^{\top}\nabla_{x}V(\mathcal{L}(X),X)Y]\leq-\lambda{\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{2}],

where
(X_{(1)},Y_{(1)})
are an independent copy of
(X,Y)
.

Proof.

Starting from the definition of monotonicity from Assumption
Assumption (UiT)

{\mathbb{E}}[(V(\mathcal{L}(X),X)-V(\mathcal{L}(Z),Z))\cdot(X-Z)]\leq-\lambda{\mathbb{E}}[|X-Z|^{2}]

take
Z=X+hY
where
Y\in L^{2}(\Omega)
to obtain

\frac{1}{h}{\mathbb{E}}[(V(\mathcal{L}(X+hY),X+hY)-V(\mathcal{L}(X),X))\cdot Y]\leq-\lambda{\mathbb{E}}[|Y|^{2}]

Now
[
10
, Proposition 5.85]
asserts the existence of the derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dh}V(\mathcal{L}(X+hY),X+hY)\Big|_{h=0}={\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mathcal{L}(X),X,X_{(1)})Y_{(1)}]+\nabla_{x}V(\mathcal{L}(X),X)Y,

and we use dominated convergence theorem to conclude the proof.
∎

Using the dissipativity from Remark
3.16
, we can deduce the following standard uniform-in-time moment bounds. We omit the proof.

Lemma 4.5
.

Under assumption
Assumption (UiT)
, for each
p\geq 1
there exists a constant
C
such that, for every
s\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,d\mu_{t}^{y,s,\nu}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C+C|y|^{p};

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,d\mu_{t}^{s,\nu}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,d\nu(x).

The following proposition now gives the key uniform-in-time analog of Lemma
4.2
.

Proposition 4.6
.

There exist constants
C,b>0
such that

\|X_{t}^{s}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}\leq Ce^{-b(t-s)}
for all
t\geq s\geq 0
, where
\|X_{t}^{s}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}
was defined in (
4.7
) .

Proof.

We write the proof for
d=1
to simplify notation, so that all of the
\nabla_{x}
symbols below are really just univariate derivatives. Differentiability of the map
\nu\mapsto X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
and
x\mapsto\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
was discussed above.
In what follows, we take
b
and
C
to be positive constants that can change from line to line but do not depend on time or the space and measure variables.

The proof goes by induction on the number of derivatives. We begin by estimating
\nabla_{x}^{k}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
for
k=1
, which we recall satisfies the ODE (
4.4
). Use the chain rule and the fact that
\nabla_{x}V
is negative definite by Remark
3.7
:

\frac{d}{dt}|\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}|^{2}=2\nabla_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})(\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})^{2}\leq-2\lambda|\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}|^{2},

which by Gronwall yields
|\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}|^{2}\leq e^{-2\lambda(t-s)}
. For the second derivative, by differentiating again we find

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\nabla_{x}^{2}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}^{2}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}+\nabla^{2}_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})(\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})^{2};

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}^{2}X_{s}^{x,s,\nu}

\displaystyle=0.

The second term decays exponentially, by the preceding estimate on
\nabla_{x}^{k}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
and the boundedness of
\nabla_{x}^{2}V
. The first term provides dissipativity as in the
k=1
case; applying the chain rule to compute
d|\nabla_{x}^{2}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}|^{2}
and then using Gronwall yields again
|\nabla_{x}^{2}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}|\leq Ce^{-b(t-s)}
.
The higher order derivatives
\nabla_{x}^{k}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
are estimated inductively following a similar pattern: the highest order term will hit the function
\nabla_{x}V
which provides dissipativity, and the higher derivatives of
V
appear only multiplied by lower order derivatives of
(\nabla_{x}^{j}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})_{j<k}
.

The reasoning for the Wasserstein derivatives is similar, but more elaborate: First consider
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)
, recalling the dynamics from (
4.6
). Check that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)|^{2}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu^{s,\nu}_{t},X_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu}(y))\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu}(y)]\

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)\nabla_{x}V(\mu^{s,\nu}_{t},X_{t}^{s,\nu})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)]

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{[\nu]},X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu}]

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\lambda}{2}{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)|^{2}]+\frac{1}{2\lambda}{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu}|^{2}].

where we use Lemma
4.4
to control the first two terms and Young’s inequality to control the third. Our previous argument shows that
|\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}|\leq e^{-2\lambda(t-s)}
, and we deduce from Gronwall and boundedness of
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V
that
{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)|^{2}]\leq Ce^{-b(t-s)}
.
Next, recalling the dynamics (
4.5
) for the linearized process
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)
, we obtain a similar bound for every
x
after computing

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y){\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu}(y)]

\displaystyle\quad+\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)\nabla_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)

\displaystyle\quad+\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y){\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{x,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu}]

\displaystyle\leq C{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)|^{2}]-\frac{\lambda}{2}|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)|^{2}+C{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{y,s,\nu}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-\lambda(t-s)/2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)|^{2}+Ce^{-(t-s)\lambda}.

Here we used
\nabla_{x}V\leq-\lambda
from Remark
3.7
along with Young’s inequality, boundedness of
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V
, and the bound on
{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)|^{2}]
just established. We deduce that
|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)|^{2}\leq Ce^{-b(t-s)}
for some
b>0
for every
x
; notice that this pathwise estimate holds almost surely.

We continue to higher order derivatives. Recall that
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)=\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)
. The dynamics of
\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)
are given by (see
[
3
, Lemma 5.3]
):

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)=\

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}[\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,{(1)}}^{s,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu}(y)]

\displaystyle+\nabla_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)

\displaystyle+\nabla_{x}^{2}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}[\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu}\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}].

Notice that
\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)
appears on the right-hand side only in the
\nabla_{x}V
term. The remaining terms all contain higher derivatives of
V
, which are uniformly bounded by assumption, multiplied by lower-order derivatives of
X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu}(y)
and
X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)
which we have already shown to decay exponentially in time. Thus, using Young’s inequality and these lower-order bounds, we get

\frac{d}{dt}|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)|^{2}\leq-\frac{\lambda}{2}|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)|^{2}+Ce^{-b(t-s)},

which yields
|\nabla_{x}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)|^{2}\leq Ce^{-b(t-s)}
.
The higher orders can be checked inductively following the same scheme that we now sketch: Differentiating again leads us to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y,z)=\

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{x,s,\nu})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y,z)]

\displaystyle+\nabla_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y,z)

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}\bigg[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\bigg(\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{x,s,\nu})\bigg)(z)\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)\bigg]

\displaystyle+\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\bigg(\nabla_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})\bigg)(z)\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)

\displaystyle+\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu})\nabla_{x}X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu}].

Notice that we are not fully developing the derivatives in the last three lines.
The first two terms provide dissipativity by using Lemma
4.4
after computing
(d/dt)|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y,z)|^{2}
, similarly to before, while the remaining terms involve lower order derivatives of
X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
, which we have already shown to be decaying exponentially,
multiplied by derivatives of
V
which are bounded by assumption. This yields
|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y,z)|\leq Ce^{-b(t-s)}
. Let us give one more example of the pattern, with the cross derivatives like
\nabla_{y}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\nabla_{y}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)=\

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{(1)}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu})\nabla_{y}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t,(1)}^{s,\nu}(y)]

\displaystyle+\nabla_{x}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu})\nabla_{y}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}(y)

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{2}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu})|\nabla_{x}X_{(1)}^{y,s,\nu}|^{2}]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}V(\mu_{t}^{s,\nu},X_{t}^{x,s,\nu},X_{t,(1)}^{y,s,\nu})\nabla^{2}_{x}X_{(1)}^{y,s,\nu}]

since the last two terms decay exponentially, we can use the same calculations as above, using first
x=[\nu]
and Lemma
4.4
displacement convexity of
V
to find
{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla_{y}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}X_{t}^{s,\nu}(y)|^{2}]\leq ce^{-b(t-s)}
. In summary, derivatives of
X_{t}^{s,\nu}
with respect to the measure or additional variables are controlled by applying Lemma
4.4
to the leading order term, whereas derivatives of
X_{t}^{x,s,\nu}
are then controlled using
\nabla_{x}V\leq-\lambda
from Remark
3.7
.
∎

5.
Analysis of the remainder

Fix a time
T>0
and define
R_{T}:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to[0,\infty)
by

(5.1)

R_{T}(\nu)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,y,z)\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})^{\otimes 2}(y,z)\Big|^{2}\,d\nu(x)\,ds.

Recall that given the vector
Y_{t}=(Y_{t}^{1},\dots,Y_{t}^{n})
solution to
1.1
, we define the empirical measure of the particle system

m_{t}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{t}^{i}}.

By exchangeability, the remainder
\mathcal{R}(t)
defined in (
3.6
) can be written as

\mathcal{R}(t)={\mathbb{E}}[R_{t}(m^{n}_{t})].

For this reason, the goal of this section is to show that
{\mathbb{E}}[R_{T}(m_{T}^{n})]=O(1/n^{2})
, in order to prove Propositions
3.5
and
3.11
. It will be convenient to define the constant

(5.2)

\Theta(t):=\|X^{0}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}(1\vee\|X^{0}_{t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}^{3})

We will often use the flow property to identify
\|X^{t}_{T}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}(1\vee\|X^{t}_{T}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{4}}^{3})=\Theta(T-t)
.

Remark 5.1
.

In dimension
d=1
the analysis is particularly simple. Using Kantorovich duality twice and the definition of Wasserstein gradient,

\displaystyle\Big|

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,y,z)\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})(y)\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})(z)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{W}_{1}(\nu,\mu_{T})\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\nabla_{z}\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,y,z)\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})(y)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{W}_{1}^{2}(\nu,\mu_{T})\sup_{y,z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big|\nabla_{y}\nabla_{z}\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,y,z)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{W}_{1}^{2}(\nu,\mu_{T})\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}V\|_{\infty}.

As a result,
{\mathbb{E}}[R_{T}(m_{T}^{n})]\leq C{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbb{W}_{1}^{4}(m_{T}^{n},\mu_{T})\big]
. This quantity is well studied both in the short and long time regimes; focusing on the case of a bounded time horizon, a classical coupling argument shows that
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbb{W}_{1}^{4}(m_{T}^{n},\mu_{T})\big]\leq C{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbb{W}_{1}^{4}(L_{T}^{n},\mu_{T})\big]
, where
L_{T}^{n}
is the empirical measure associated to
n
independent copies of the McKean–Vlasov law
\mu_{T}
. This term in turn can be estimated by
[
32
, Theorem 2]
to obtain

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbb{W}_{1}^{4}(m_{T}^{n},\mu_{T})\big]\leq C/n^{2},

which allows us to close the estimate; notice that we are making use only of the first two Wasserstein derivatives. The same argument does not work in higher dimensions since the speed of convergence of empirical measures in Wasserstein distance deteriorates (see
[
32
, Theorem 1]
). This approach can be salvaged in higher dimension, by performing a higher-order Taylor expansion in the measure argument to introduce three-way interactions (or four-way, etc.) which can be estimated hierarchically as we did with the pairwise interactions from the first-order expansion. But for
d>6
this Taylor expansion requires more regularity than our argument based on weak propagation of chaos.

We will wish to appeal to results from
[
3
]
and
[
15
]
that apply to functions in
\mathcal{C}_{\text{bd}}^{4}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
. However,
R_{T}
itself does not belong to
\mathcal{C}_{\text{bd}}^{4}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, because
\delta_{m}^{2}V(m,x,y,z)
may be unbounded in
(y,z)
. We thus perform an easy approximation procedure and pass to the limit at the end of the section. Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{3d})
be an approximation of the indicator function of the unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3d}
; specifically,
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
with
\varphi\equiv 1
on the unit ball and
\varphi\equiv 0
outside the ball of radius
2
. Let
\varphi^{N}(x)=\varphi(x/N)
for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Define

r^{N}_{T}(s,\nu,x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi^{N}(x,y,z)\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,y,z)\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})^{\otimes 2}(y,z)

and set
R_{T}^{N}(\nu)=\int_{0}^{1}\int|r^{N}_{T}(s,\nu,x)|^{2}\,d\nu(x)\,ds
.
We begin with a useful lemma, which is essentially
[
15
, Lemma 2.7]
:

Lemma 5.2
.

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bd}}^{k}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
it holds

\displaystyle\delta_{m}^{k}f(\mu,x,\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}\int_{[0,1]^{k}}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}f(\mu,x,q_{1}y_{1},\dots,q_{k}y_{k})\,dq_{1},\dots,dq_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}|y_{i}|\int_{[0,1]^{k}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}f(\mu,x,y_{1}t_{1},\dots,y_{k}t_{k})|\,dt,

where we treat the
k
-tensor
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}f
as a
d^{k}
-dimensional vector, and the norm is the Euclidean one.

Proof.

By choice of normalization,
\delta_{m}^{k}V(\mu,x,\bm{y})=0
whenever at least one coordinate of
\bm{y}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k}
vanishes. The first identity follows immediately.
∎

We summarize the quartic properties of
R_{T}^{N}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The following holds:

(1)

For every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the function
R_{T}^{N}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{4}_{bd}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
.

(2)

R_{T}^{N}(\mu_{T})=\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}R_{T}^{N}(\mu_{T},y)=\nabla_{y}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}R_{T}^{N}(\mu_{T},y)=\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}R_{T}^{N}(\mu_{T},y,z)=0
for all
y,z
,

(3)

There exist constants
C,p>0
independent of
N
such that for each multi-index
(\mathsf{w},\bm{i})
with size
0<k\leq 4
we have

|D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}R_{T}^{N}(\nu,\bm{y})|\leq C\bigg(1+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,d(\nu+\mu_{T})(x)+|\bm{y}|^{p}\bigg).

(4)

There exist constants
C,p>0
independent of
N
such that for each multi-index
(\mathsf{w},\bm{i})
of size
k\leq 4
,

\displaystyle D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}P_{t}(R_{T}^{N},\nu,\bm{x})\leq C\Theta(t)\bigg(1+\int|z|^{p}\,d(\mu_{t}^{0,\nu}+\mu_{T})(z)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int|z|^{p}\,d\mu_{t}^{x^{i},0,\nu}(z)\bigg)

Proof.

(1)

By Lemma
5.2
we have
|\delta_{m}^{2}V(\nu,x,y,z)|\leq\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}V\|_{\infty}^{2}|y||z|
. Since
\varphi^{N}
is supported on the ball of radius
2N
, we deduce
|\varphi^{N}\delta_{m}^{2}V|\leq 4N^{2}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}V\|_{\infty}^{2}
. Similarly if we consider
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}\delta_{m}^{2}V(\nu,x,y,z,(u^{1},\ldots,u^{k}),y,z)
for some fixed
k\in\{1,2,3,4\}
and
(x,u^{1},\ldots,u^{k})
, we have
|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}\delta_{m}^{2}V(\nu,y,z,(u^{1},\dots,u^{k}))|\leq\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k+2}V\|_{\infty}^{2}|y||z|
, and we deduce that
\varphi^{N}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{k}\delta_{m}^{2}V
is bounded. The
x
derivatives of
\varphi^{N}\delta_{m}^{2}V
are bounded similarly, and the claim follows readily.

(2)

It is obvious that
R_{T}(\mu_{T})=0
. Compute the Wasserstein gradient,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}r_{T}^{N}(s,\nu,x,u)&=s\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\varphi^{N}(x,y,z)\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,y,z,u)\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})^{\otimes 2}(y,z)\\
&+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\nabla_{y}\big[\varphi^{N}(x,u,z)\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,u,z)\big]\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})(z)\\
&+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\nabla_{z}\big[\varphi^{N}(x,y,u)\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,y,u)\big]\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})(y).\end{split}

At
\nu=\mu_{T}
, this derivative vanishes. Then we can check that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}R_{T}^{N}(\nu,y)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{x}r_{T}^{N}(s,\nu,y)r^{N}_{T}(s,\nu,y)\,ds+2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}r_{T}^{N}(s,\nu,x,y)r_{T}^{N}(s,\nu,x)\,d\nu(x)\,ds.

Note that
r_{T}^{N}(s,\nu,x)
,
\nabla_{x}r_{T}^{N}(s,\nu,x)
, and
\nabla_{x}^{2}r_{T}^{N}(s,\nu,x)
vanish at
\nu=\mu_{T}
. We thus deduce that
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}R_{T}^{N}(\mu_{T},\cdot)=0
, and differentiating the above shows also that
\nabla_{y}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}R_{T}^{N}(\mu_{T},y)=0
. Similar considerations show that
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}R_{T}^{N}(\mu_{T},\cdot,\cdot)=0
.

(3)

We check that
r_{T}^{N}
satisfies a similar property. Indeed by using Lemma
5.2
, boundedness of
\varphi^{N}
and
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}V
and Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle r_{T}^{N}(s,\nu,x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}V\|_{\infty}|y||z|\,d(\nu+\mu_{T})^{\otimes 2}(y,z)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int|y|\,d\mu_{T}(y)\Big)^{2}+C\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|y|\,d\nu(y)\bigg)^{2}

Similarly if we consider the first term of (
5.3
), by Lemma
5.2
and boundedness of
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{3}V,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bigg|s\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}&\varphi^{N}(x,y,z)\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,y,z,u)\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})^{\otimes 2}(y,z)\bigg|\\
&\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{3}V\|_{\infty}|y||z|\,d(\nu+\mu_{T})^{\otimes 2}(y,z)\\
&\leq C\Big(\int|y|\,d\mu_{T}(y)\Big)^{2}+C\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|y|\,d\nu(y)\bigg)^{2}\end{split}

Considering instead the second term of (
5.3
) and using again Lemma
5.2
and Young’s inequality, we get

(5.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bigg|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}&\nabla_{y}\varphi^{N}(x,u,z)\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,u,z)\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})(z)\bigg|\\
&+\bigg|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi^{N}(x,u,z)\nabla_{y}\delta_{m}^{2}V(s\nu+(1-s)\mu_{T},x,u,z)\,d(\nu-\mu_{T})(z)\bigg|\\
&\leq C|u|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}V\|_{\infty}|z|\,d(\nu+\mu_{T})(z)+C\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}V\|_{\infty}|z|\,d(\nu+\mu_{T})(z)\\
&\leq C+|u|^{2}+C\Big(\int|y|\,d\mu_{T}(y)\Big)^{2}+C\bigg(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|y|\,d\nu(y)\bigg)^{2}.\end{split}

Repeating the same reasoning yields that for every multi index
|\bm{i}|\leq 4
, for some
p\geq 1
and universal constant
C
,

\displaystyle|D^{\mathsf{w},\bm{i}}r_{T}^{N}(s,\nu,x,\bm{y})|\leq C\bigg[1+\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|z|\,d\nu(z)\Big)^{p}+\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|z|\,d\mu_{T}(z)\Big)^{p}+|\bm{y}|^{p}\bigg]

now the result follows by computing the derivatives of
R^{N}_{T}
and using the bounds on
r^{N}_{T}
and Jensen’s inequality.

(4)

Apply Lemma
4.3
.

∎

5.1.
A semigroup interpolation of the remainder

We next make use of the weak error expansion that is the main contribution of
[
15
]
.

Proposition 5.4
.

The function
P_{t}R^{N}_{T}
is differentiable in time and belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{4}_{\textup{bd}}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
. Moreover, we have the following formula:

(5.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[R^{N}_{T}(m_{T}^{n})]&={\mathbb{E}}[P_{T}R^{N}(m_{0}^{n})]\\
&+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\int_{0}^{T}\bigg({\mathbb{E}}[P_{t}g_{t}(m_{0}^{n})]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{Tr}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}P_{t-s}g_{t}(m_{s}^{n},Y_{s}^{i},Y_{s}^{i})]\,ds\bigg)\,dt\end{split}

where we define
g_{t}:\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

g_{t}(\nu)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{Tr}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(\nu,x,x)\,d\nu(x).

Proof.

Regularity of
P_{t}R_{T}^{N}
comes from
[
15
, Theorem 2.18]
since
R_{T}^{N}
and
V
both have
4
derivatives in space and measure. Fix
T>0
; by
[
3
, Theorem 7.2]
,
P_{T-t}R^{N}_{T}
satisfies (in the classical sense)

(5.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(\nu)+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big(\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(\nu,x)\cdot V(\nu,x)+\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(\nu,x)\Big)\,d\nu(x)

\displaystyle=0,

for
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
t\in(0,T)
.
By the same reasoning,
s\mapsto P_{T-s}P_{T-t}f
solves the same PDE on
[0,t]
(with the appropriate terminal condition).
We follow the steps of
[
15
, Lemma 2.11(ii)]
to obtain a second order expansion of
f
. Observe that
P_{T-t}f(m_{t}^{n})
is a function of the vector
Y_{t}
. Use Ito’s rule on
P_{T-t}f(m_{t}^{n})
to see

\displaystyle dP_{T-t}

\displaystyle R_{T}^{N}(m_{t}^{n})=\partial_{t}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(m_{t}^{n})\,dt+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(m_{t}^{n},x)\cdot V(m_{t}^{n},x)\,dm_{t}^{n}(x)\,dt

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(m_{t}^{n},x)\,dm_{t}^{n}(x)\,dt

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma\sqrt{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{i})\cdot dW^{i}_{t}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Tr}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{i},Y_{t}^{i})\,dt.

The first three
dt
terms combine to zero, using the PDE (
5.7
). Notice that the stochastic integral is a true martingale since
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}P_{t}R_{T}^{N}
is bounded uniformly in
t
. Integrate and take expectations to get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[R_{T}^{N}(m_{T}^{n})]={\mathbb{E}}[P_{T}R_{T}^{N}(m_{0}^{n})]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{Tr}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}P_{T-t}R_{T}^{N}(m_{t}^{n},Y_{t}^{i},Y_{t}^{i})]\,dt

Notice that the integrand in the last term is precisely
{\mathbb{E}}[g_{t}(m_{t}^{n})]
.
We can thus use Ito’s rule again on the flow
s\mapsto P_{t-s}g_{t}(m_{s}^{n})
to find

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[g_{t}(m_{t}^{n})]={\mathbb{E}}[P_{t}g_{t}(m_{0}^{n})]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{Tr}\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}^{2}P_{t-s}g_{t}(m_{s}^{n},Y_{s}^{i},Y_{s}^{i})]\,ds.

Combine the above two equations to complete the proof.
∎

We will need some regularity estimates on the functions
g_{t}
and
P_{s}g_{t}
, which easily follow from Lemma
4.3
:

Corollary 5.5
.

There exist

Remark 5.6

and we have

\displaystyle|X_{s}-Z_{s}|

where we used that

Appendix B
Convexity implies monotonicity

Lemma B.1
.

Assume that
\Psi
is in
\mathcal{C}_{bd}^{6}(\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and assume it is
\lambda
-strongly displacement convex. Then for every random variable
X
with law
\mu
and every random variable
Y
with law
\nu
,

(B.1)

{\mathbb{E}}[(Y-X)\cdot(\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi(\mu,X)-\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi(Y,\nu))]\geq\lambda{\mathbb{E}}[|Y-X|^{2}]

Proof.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},{\mathbb{P}})
be a probability space rich enough such that for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, there exists a random variable on
\Omega
with law
\mu
. Let
\psi:L^{2}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the Lions lift of
\Psi
:

\displaystyle\psi(X)=\Psi(\mathcal{L}(X)).

The differentiability assumed of
\Psi
implies that
\psi
is Frechèt differentiable, and the Frechèt derivative
D\psi(X)
can be represented as
\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi(\mathcal{L}(X),X)
; see the appendix of
[
6
]
, or
[
35
]
. Hence for every two random variables
X,Z
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
,

(B.2)

\psi(Z)-\psi(X)\geq{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi(\mathcal{L}(X),X)\cdot(Z-X)]+\frac{\lambda}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\big[|Z-X|^{2}\big].

Swap the position of
Z,X
in (
B.2
), and add the two inequalities to obtain

{\mathbb{E}}\big[(\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi(\mathcal{L}(Z),Z)-\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi(\mathcal{L}(X),X))\cdot(Z-X)\big]\geq\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\big[|Z-X|^{2}\big].

This is precisely the monotonicity condition of Assumption
Assumption (UiT)
(2) for
V=-\nabla_{\mathbb{W}}\Psi
.
∎
