Causal Tongue-Tie: LLMs Can Encode Causal Direction, But Their Yes/No Outputs Fail to Express
Ziyi Ding, Xiao-Ping Zhang

Abstract
We find a mismatch between what large language models encode about a causal question and what they answer. On anti-commonsense CLadder ( 15 ) items, a fixed linear probe recovers the evidence-supported answer from the model’s hidden state (accuracy ≈ 0.97 {\approx}0.97 ), while the spoken Yes/No reverts to the commonsense one (accuracy ≈ 0.5 {\approx}0.5 ). We call this Δ ≈ + 0.5 \Delta{\approx}{+}0.5 gap Causal Tongue-Tie : a wrong Yes/No decomposes into two separable failure modes—no internal signal vs. a signal the verbal interface cannot say. The implication cuts both ways for output-only causal benchmarks: a benchmark “correct” need not mean the model has understood, and a benchmark “wrong” need not mean it cannot. Sweeping claims about whether LLMs can do causal reasoning, drawn from a single accuracy number, deserve a second look.

3.1
Setting and notation

To make Causal Tongue-Tie precise, we need a single forward pass on
which we can read two things side by side: what the hidden state
contains, and what the model says out loud. We therefore work with a
frozen instruction-tuned transformer decoder
f_{\theta}
with
L
residual
blocks. For each prompt
x_{i}
we read the activation

\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
after block
\ell
at the

last prompt token
position, and on the same pass we read the
next-token
\textsf{Yes}/\textsf{No}
probabilities through the
model’s own unembedding

\texttt{lm\_head}\!:\!\mathbb{R}^{d}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
. Our linear
probe (defined below) reads
\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}
, and
lm_head

reads
\bm{h}_{i}^{(L)}
, both at the same token position, so on every
item we can compare directly what is internally readable with what is
actually said (
Appendix

A
gives a self-contained refresher
on the decoder modules and residual stream).

The items we audit are causal Yes/No questions, each with a gold
answer. We write the dataset as

\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i},c_{i},e_{i},b_{i},r_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},

where
x_{i}
is the prompt,
y_{i}\in\{\textsf{Yes},\textsf{No}\}
is
the gold answer,
(c_{i},e_{i})
is the candidate cause–effect pair, and

r_{i}
marks the evidence type. The subset tag

b_{i}\in\{\text{cs},\text{anti-CS},\text{ns}\}
splits the data into three flavours that
will drive the rest of the paper:
commonsense-aligned
items
(
cs
, e.g.
smoking causes lung cancer
), where the prompt
evidence agrees with everyday commonsense;
anti-commonsense

items (
anti-CS
), where the in-prompt evidence is constructed to point
the opposite way from the commonsense prior; and
nonsense

controls (
ns
), whose prompts use placeholder entity tokens with no
real-world content and which serve as a contamination-free baseline.

To isolate the direction of the answer inside the hidden state, we
compare each prompt
x_{i}
with a
counterfactual minimal pair

x_{i}^{\mathrm{cf}}
that swaps only the words that flip the
answer—e.g.
vaccinated
vs.
not vaccinated
, or

cause
vs.
prevent
—so the evidence-supported answer
flips while the surface form is otherwise identical

(
6
;
18
;
19
;
12
)
.
The hidden-state difference at layer
\ell
,

\Delta\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}=\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}-\bm{h}_{i}^{(\mathrm{cf},\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1)

is the answer-direction signal we will read in two complementary
ways. First, a
linear readout (frozen)
is an

\ell_{2}
-regularised logistic regression

g_{\mathbf{w}}(\bm{h})=\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\bm{h}+b)
, fit

once
on hidden states from
cs
items only and then frozen
for every reported number; the language model itself is never
updated. Second, the same idea can be expressed as a direction in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
: stacking the
cs
paired differences into a matrix

\mathbf{D}_{\text{cs}}^{(\ell)}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{cs}}\times d}
(rows

\Delta\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}
,
i\!\in\!\text{cs}
) and taking its truncated SVD,

\mathbf{D}_{\text{cs}}^{(\ell)}=U\Sigma V^{\!\top},\qquad V_{\mathrm{cs}}=V_{:,1:k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k},

(2)

defines the
counterfactual subspace

V_{\mathrm{cs}}
as the top-
k

right singular vectors of that matrix

(
22
;
21
;
4
;
18
)
.

Both the readout and
V_{\mathrm{cs}}
are fit on
cs
alone and reused frozen
everywhere else;
k
is small, with
k\!\in\!\{1,2,4\}
and the
simple mean of
\Delta\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}
giving the same qualitative
gap on
cs
and
anti-CS
(
Appendix

E
).

A readout pipeline of any reasonable shape might still appear to
pick up a direction by accident, so we rule out the most obvious
non-causal alternatives with two same-shape controls. The

sham subspace
rebuilds the entire fit-then-freeze pipeline on
the
ns
subset (whose prompts have no real-world content), and the

random-direction control
replaces
V_{\mathrm{cs}}
with Haar-random
vectors of the same rank. Both controls share the readout’s shape
but should be uninformative, and we use them to set the chance
baseline in the definition of
encode
below.

3.2
What “encode” means here

With those pieces in place, we say precisely what we mean when we
claim the hidden state
encodes
the evidence-supported answer
on an item: on the same frozen forward pass,
(i)
the
linear readout recovers that answer;
(ii)
on the
counterfactual minimal pair the same readout recovers the opposite
direction (so it tracks the causal direction, not the wording);
and
(iii)
both same-shape controls (sham and random
direction) sit at chance on (i)–(ii), so the readout is specific
to the
cs
causal direction, not anything a 7B hidden state
happens to support. The claim is deliberately
narrow—this is a hidden-state-vs.-output gap, not human-style
understanding—and we use it throughout the paper.

3.3
How we measure the gap

We quantify the gap with two accuracies on the same item.

\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{probe}}(\ell)
is the rate at which the frozen linear
readout from
\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}
recovers the evidence-supported
direction;
\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{out}}
is the rate at which the model’s
ordinary
Yes
/
No
probabilities pick the same answer.
The empirical gap is

\Delta=\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{probe}}(\ell^{\star})-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{out}},

(3)

where
\ell^{\star}
is the best probing layer chosen on commonsense
items only (
Section

4
; hyperparameters in

Appendix

E
); anti-commonsense and the hard-negative
families are held out from this choice. A large positive
\Delta
is
the numerical form of Causal Tongue-Tie: the hidden-state readout
matches the evidence-supported answer much more often than the
spoken Yes/No does. A small or negative
\Delta
says the mismatch
is absent under this test.

3.4
The empirical claims

The rest of the paper tests three empirical claims, each tied to a specific
subsection of the evidence.

Claim 1.

\Delta
is largest when the prompt evidence points
against commonsense: the hidden-state readout still matches the
evidence-supported answer while the Yes/No output falls back to a weak or
misleading response (checked in
Section

5.1
).

Claim 2.
The readout direction
V_{\mathrm{cs}}
is not random variation:
same-shape sham, random-direction, and pair controls all support it
as a stable, readable direction, even though it is not itself a switch
that flips the final answer (checked in
Section

5.2
).

Claim 3.

\Delta
is not explained away by visible causal words
or candidate-token surface cues alone; those cues can matter, but they
do not account for when the readout direction is recoverable and when
the Yes/No answer expresses it (checked in
Sections

6.1
and
6.2
).

Roadmap.
Claims 1–3 map to
Sections

5.1
,
5.2
,
6.1
and
6.2
; a final scope check (
Section

6.3
) localises the failure to the verbal interface rather than to one particular Yes/No surface form.

Around the readout direction

7
Discussion and Conclusion

We introduced
Causal Tongue-Tie
: hidden states often carry an
evidence-aligned causal direction that the ordinary Yes/No does not
say (
Sections

5.1
and
5.2
; controls in
Sections

6.2
and
6.3
), and
full-state injection flips over half the wrong Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
items (
Appendix

N
). A wrong Yes/No thus decomposes into
two failure modes—no internal signal vs. an unexpressed one—not
a single “failed” verdict.

Implication for benchmark-based claims.

The gap warns against output-only evaluation: hidden-state and Yes/No
verdicts can differ by
\Delta{\approx}{+}0.5
, while prompt form also
matters (
Appendix

O
). Single-number claims conflate all three.

Limitations

Scope of the claim.

Causal Tongue-Tie is an empirical diagnostic of a mismatch between
hidden-state evidence and the final Yes/No answer. Following the
operational definition in
Section

3
, we claim that the model
encodes causal direction only in that narrow sense, not human-like
understanding, complete causal reasoning, or a causal circuit; the
direction is causally sufficient on
{>}50\%
but not all items the
ordinary interface gets wrong (
Appendix

N
).

Scope of the finding.

Our gap is a hidden-state-vs-output mismatch on
textual
causal
questions (a short story plus a Yes/No probe). It is
not
a
finding about causal discovery from raw observational data such as
physical sensor readings, time-series, or experimental measurements;
those tasks have very different inputs and evaluation, and lie outside
our scope. A local contamination heuristic
(
Table

18
) reports
anti-CS
marker rate
=0.000
and

ns
/
CounterBench
nonsense-entity coverage
=1.000
, so the
audited items are unlikely to be verbatim pretraining text; a
rigorous
n
-gram contamination audit is left to future work.

Direction-label confounding and wording sensitivity.

The original direction label in
Section

5.1
is partly confounded with question
format, so the controlled hard-negative diagnostic—not the
Section

5.1
probe
alone—anchors the readout claim. Even there, the multi-template run
exposes a brittle family, so the recovered direction should still be
read as tied to the tested wording and syntax. The obs-matched
hard-negative families are template-generated; 5 hand-written templates
(
Table

11
, including a Chinese paraphrase, passive, and
indirect phrasings) all reproduce the qualitative gap (per-template
probe
\mathrm{Acc}

{\geq}\,0.95
, gap
{\geq}\,+0.26
), but cross-template
probe transfer is fragile (
Table

12
, off-diagonal
mean
0.672
); the recovered direction is template-conditioned in its
parameterization. Full human-style paraphrases beyond these 5 templates
remain future work.

Limited interface repair and partial sufficiency.

Prompting narrows but does not close the interface gap. The scalar

V_{\mathrm{cs}}
swap (
Section

5.2
) is a finite-power null on full Yes/No
control; full-state activation patching (
Appendix

N
) is
causally sufficient on
{>}50\%
but not all of the items the ordinary
interface gets wrong, so we demonstrate a partially exploited internal
direction, not a complete causal control path. The diagnostic is
single-direction, single-layer, and scalar; manifold or multi-mode
geometries, norm-preserving multi-layer lesions, and low-rank or
cone-shaped interventions could close the remaining sufficiency gap.
Forced-choice decoding is not standardized across surface forms, but
the Yes/No vs. True/False vs. Chinese (
Shi
/
Fou
)
vs. A/B-reframe comparison on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(
Appendix

O
) keeps
\Delta\!\geq\!+0.595
across all four;
a fully multi-token-calibrated protocol over the entire model suite is
still left to future work.

Coverage of models and controls.

The evidence is limited by the models and tests we could run. The full
diagnostic suite is centered on Qwen2.5-Instruct and
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3; DeepSeek-LLM-7B-Chat supports the basic
probe–answer gap but not the full
Sections

5.2
,
6.1
,
6.2
and
6.3
analysis. These checks bound
the claim but do not exhaust other model families, layers, prompts,
interventions, or lexical shortcuts. Several controls use smaller

Sections

6.2

N{=}24

widening their CIs relative to the headline anti-CS gap on
N{=}80

All eight backbones are instruction-tuned; auditing base checkpoints
would help separate interface failure from RLHF answer policy.

Probe training set and cross-dataset transfer.

The probe in the main text is always fit on CLadder commonsense items.
We report a preliminary cross-dataset transfer evaluation in

Appendix

Ethical Considerations

Data and model provenance.

We use two public causal-reasoning benchmarks, CLadder and CounterBench, both MIT-licensed and free of personal or sensitive information. The eight instruction-tuned checkpoints we probe—Qwen 2.5 (0.5B–72B), DeepSeek-LLM-7B, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3—are publicly released models used only for research under their licenses. Prompts, hidden-state artifacts, per-item probe logits, and Yes/No probabilities accompany the public release.

Intended use and dual-use considerations.

This work diagnoses an alignment failure between a model’s internal representation and its Yes/No output; it provides no deployable fix or jailbreak path. Hidden-state read-out applies only to open-weight checkpoints—adding no attack surface against closed-API models—and our structured prompts modify only the input, not the weights. The reported hidden-state-vs-output gap is a measurement statement and should not be cited as evidence that a model truly understands causality.

Compute and environmental cost.

All probing and interface experiments ran on a mixed-GPU node of consumer RTX 4090 cards, with NF4-quantized offload to H20 GPUs for the
\geq 32
B checkpoints; we performed no fine-tuning or gradient updates, so total compute is far below training a comparable model. Aggregated GPU-hours, per-sample inference latency, and an energy/carbon estimate are reported in
Sections

E.1
and
L
.

Use of AI writing assistance.

Experimental design, claim formation, result analysis, and final editorial decisions were made by the authors. For wording polish, sentence-level rewrites, and draft figure and table captions, the authors used large language models as writing aids, consistent with ACL’s policy on Generative Assistance in Authorship. All cited references were verified manually.

Appendix A
Transformer decoder and the hidden state we read

This subsection is a minimal, self-contained refresher on the part of the
transformer decoder that our probe touches; readers familiar with the
residual stream can skip it.

Stack and residual stream.

An instruction-tuned transformer decoder
f_{\theta}
embeds an input token
sequence into a tensor of activations and updates that tensor through
L

identical
decoder blocks
. Block
\ell
takes the activation

\bm{h}^{(\ell-1)}
from the previous block, applies a causal multi-head
self-attention sublayer and a position-wise MLP sublayer with residual
connections. Writing
\bm{a}^{(\ell)}\,:=\,\mathrm{Attn}_{\ell}\!\bigl(\bm{h}^{(\ell-1)}\bigr)
for the attention sublayer output and
\bm{u}^{(\ell)}\,:=\,\bm{h}^{(\ell-1)}+\bm{a}^{(\ell)}
for the post-attention residual,

\bm{h}^{(\ell)}\;=\;\bm{u}^{(\ell)}\;+\;\mathrm{MLP}\!\bigl(\bm{u}^{(\ell)}\bigr),

and produces the next activation
\bm{h}^{(\ell)}
. Following the
standard
residual-stream
view of a decoder,
\bm{h}^{(\ell)}
is
the running sum of all sublayer contributions up to block
\ell
.

The state we read.

At the final, autoregressively visible token of the prompt

x_{i}
—which we call the
last prompt token
—the decoder has
been exposed (via causal attention) to every prior token, so its
activation
\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unique state from which the
model can produce the next token. The model’s unembedding map

\texttt{lm\_head}\!:\mathbb{R}^{d}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
takes exactly this

\bm{h}_{i}^{(L)}
and returns next-token logits, from which we read the

\textsf{Yes}/\textsf{No}
probabilities. Our linear probe reads the

same
activation

\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}
at the same position, only at a possibly earlier
block
\ell\!\leq\!L
; nothing else about
f_{\theta}
is touched.

What “frozen” means here.

Across the entire paper, no parameter of
f_{\theta}
ever changes: all
attention, MLP, layernorm, embedding, and unembedding weights stay at
their released values. The only learned object is the per-layer
logistic probe (
Section

3
); the readout direction
V_{\mathrm{cs}}
in

Equation

2
is the result of an SVD on stored
\Delta\bm{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}

vectors, not a gradient step on
f_{\theta}
. There is no fine-tuning, PEFT,
or weight editing.

Appendix M
Token-Level Entropy: Why A/B Saturates While Yes/No Fails

Setup.

To address why the A/B-edge interface achieves near-perfect accuracy on
anti-CS
items while ordinary Yes/No fails at chance, we compute the
selected-token binary entropy
from the stored per-item logits in the readout-sweep pilots.
For each item
i
and interface, we extract the log-probabilities
\ell_{0}^{(i)}
and
\ell_{1}^{(i)}
of the two candidate tokens, normalise to obtain probabilities
(p_{0}^{(i)},p_{1}^{(i)})
, and compute the binary interface entropy

H^{(i)}=-p_{0}^{(i)}\log p_{0}^{(i)}-p_{1}^{(i)}\log p_{1}^{(i)},

where the base is
e
(nats) and
\ln 2\approx 0.693
is the maximum.
For the
Yes/No interface
(
cause_fwd_yesno
), token
0
is the anti-CS-correct Yes answer and token
1
is No; the gold label is always
0
.
For the
A/B-edge interface
(
ab_choose_causal_direction
), token
0
is option A (the correct direction) and token
1
is option B.

The low Yes/No entropy in instruction-tuned models also implies a multi-token commitment effect: once the first generated token is No (committed with
