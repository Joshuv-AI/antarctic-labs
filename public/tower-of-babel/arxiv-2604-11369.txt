Introduction

Writing correct concurrent programs is a notoriously challenging task, as interprocess communication is inherently non-deterministic, encompassing unanticipated scheduling patterns that often escape the programmers’ mental model.
For the same reason, concurrency bugs are difficult to reproduce during debugging, and are commonly referred to as
heisenbugs

(
34
)
.

One fundamental building block in the mental model of programmers is
atomicity
, where the whole program consists of individual blocks that can be considered to execute atomically.
Although this is not actually the case due to the scheduler, atomicity allows one to reason about programs in terms of atomic blocks, meaning that their interleaving cannot produce new behaviors.
However, violations of atomicity are common in practice and are the source of many real-world concurrency bugs

We remark that atomicity violations are different from data races; they occur when concurrent accesses from different threads interleave in an unexpected way, even, e.g., if such accesses are synchronizing, and can thus occur even on race-free programs.

The programming languages community has responded with techniques for detecting atomicity violations automatically.
One standard approach is via dynamic analyses that report warnings based on violations of
conflict-serializability
.
The analysis observes executions
\sigma
of the concurrent program which are annotated with intended atomic blocks, called transactions.
Annotations are external to the analysis —they might come explicitly from the programmer
(
17
)
, or be inferred automatically
(
27
)
.
Although transactions may interleave in
\sigma
, the analysis must decide whether there is an equivalent execution
\sigma^{\prime}
, free of transaction interleavings, obtainable from
\sigma
by successive swaps of non-conflicting events.
If not,
\sigma
witnesses an atomicity violation.
The size of
\sigma
is usually huge, which puts pressure on the analysis to be as fast as possible.
In the following, consider that
\sigma
contains
n
events,
k
threads,
v
locations, and
\ell
locks.

In high level, conflict-serializability violations can be detected by creating a conflict graph of all transactions, whereby two transactions are ordered
T_{1}\to T_{2}
iff each
T_{i}
contains an event
e_{i}
such that
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
conflict (both access the same shared resource and at least one modifies it) and
e_{1}
executes before
e_{2}
in
\sigma
.
Then, a violation corresponds to a cycle
T_{1}\to\dots\to T_{m}
, representing the fact that these
m
transactions cannot execute serially without breaking the conflict order of the events in
\sigma
(see, e.g., the cycle
T_{1}\to T_{2}\to T_{1}
in
Fig.

1
).
Constructing the conflict graph and checking for cycles can be easily performed in
O(n)
time, but this approach has two notable disadvantages.
First, the space usage is
\Theta(n)
, which can be prohibitive in practice.
Second, practical applications pose an online setting, whereby
\sigma
is processed in a streaming style, and a violation is reported as early as it occurs.
Consequently, there have been several efforts in designing atomicity-checking algorithms that operate in this online fashion and are as efficient as possible (see
Table

1
).

One of the first techniques for dynamic atomicity testing was developed in
(
2
)
.
That algorithm, however, employed aggressive pruning of its data structures in order to keep its memory footprint small, which lead to incorrect results.
This was reported in
(
12

, which proposed an automata-based algorithm (which we refer to as
\mathtt{FM}
) with running time
O(nk(v+\ell))
.
At the same time, the graph-based algorithm
\mathtt{Velodrome}
was developed
(
14
)
, and has become a standard reference in atomicity testing.

\mathtt{Velodrome}
maintains explicitly a conflict graph that can grow as large as
\Theta(n)
, with cycles representing atomicity violations.
It has
O(n^{2})
time and
O(n)
space complexity, which are prohibitive in theory, but is made performant in practice via graph-pruning heuristics.

\mathtt{DoubleChecker}

(
5
)
further improved of
\mathtt{Velodrome}
by observing that precise cycle detection is required infrequently.

\mathtt{Aerodrome}
was recently proposed as the first algorithm utilizing vector clocks as its main data structure
(
32
)
, which are efficient and common in dynamic concurrency analyses.

\mathtt{Aerodrome}
has time and space complexity
O(nk(k+v+\ell))
and
O(k(k+v+\ell))
, respectively.
The latest development in this sequence is
\mathtt{RegionTrack}
, which also utilizes vector clocks, towards time and space complexity
O(nk^{3})
and
O(k(kv+\ell))
, respectively
(
28
)

The running time of
\mathtt{RegionTrack}
is incomparable to
\mathtt{Aerodrome}
in general, but it becomes faster for reasonable input parameters (i.e., typically
k<<v
).
However,
\mathtt{RegionTrack}
has increased space usage by a term
vk^{2}
.
Finally, when deployed in a runtime monitoring setting, where various threads access the components of the algorithm concurrently, all existing testers require each thread to acquire a global lock.
This incurs congestion and leads to noticeable monitoring overhead, beyond what is considered acceptable in an industrial scale (e.g.,
\mathtt{TSAN}

\times

The long study of atomicity checking, as witnessed by the above sequence of improvements, highlights the need for methods that are as performant as possible.

Is there an atomicity checker with even better complexity?
Does it also lead to practical improvements?
Is atomicity monitorable at overheads acceptable by industrial practice?
Is the complexity increase of online monitoring necessary, compared to the
O(n)
cost for offline detection?

This paper answers these questions in positive, by making the following contributions.

1. AtomSanitizer for conflict-serializability.

We develop
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
, a new algorithm for testing conflict-serializability.
For a trace
\sigma
of
n
events,
k
threads,
v
locations, and
\ell
locks,
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
uses
O(nk^{2})
time and
O(k(k+v)+\ell)
space.
This is better than all existing algorithms in both time and space (see
Table

1
), for reasonable input parameters.
In particular,
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
is faster when
k<<v
,
while its space complexity is smaller by a term
k\ell
.

We further remark that the space complexity of
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
is also bounded by
O(n+k^{2})
, but the aforementioned space bound is better as long as
kv<n
, which is typically the case.
Moreover, the term
kv
can be refined to
\sum_{x}k_{x}
, where
k_{x}
is the number of threads reading on variable
x
.
Although
\sum_{x}k_{x}=O(kv)
in the worst-case, in practice most variables are accessed by only a few threads, reducing the space complexity to
O(k^{2}+v+\ell)
.
In contrast, existing algorithms do not enjoy this property.
This makes
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
the first algorithm with a memory footprint small enough to be employed in a runtime setting.

2. AtomSanitizer for increasing-cycle violations.

We present a version of
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
focused on increasing-cycle violations, which are specific conflict-serializability violations in which the root cause can be localized to a single offending transaction, and has been utilized in prior work
(
14
;
5
;
28
)
.

\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
detects increasing-cycle violations in time
O(nk)
and space
O(k(k+v)+\ell)
.
Although this does not improve the time complexity of the state-of-the-art (in particular
\mathtt{RegionTrack}

(
28
)
), it improves the space complexity by a term
k\ell
, and in practice achieves a memory footprint of
O(k^{2}+v+\ell)
, by a similar analysis as above.

3. Lower bounds.

We complement our improved upper bounds with two interesting lower bounds that characterize fundamental computational resources required to detect conflict-serializability violations in an streaming fashion.
First, recall that, if we relinquish the practical requirement that a violation is reported in an online fashion as soon as it occurs (or shortly after, e.g., when the latest offending transaction completes), the problem can be trivially solved in
O(n)
time.

Is it possible to achieve linear time also in the online setting?

We prove that this is unlikely, in the sense that the online problem must take
\Omega(n^{3/2-\epsilon})
time, for any fixed
\epsilon>0
, under the popular Online Matrix-Vector hypothesis
(
21
)
.
Second, note that the space usage of
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
is practically sublinear in
n
, but can become linear in
n
when there are
v=\Omega(n)
locations.

Is a sublinear space bound always possible?

We prove that this is not the case, in the sense that any streaming algorithm for conflict-serializability (or even increasing-path) violations must use
\Omega(n)
space when
v=\Omega(n)
, in the worst case.

4. Implementation and experiments.

We implement
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
inside the
\mathtt{RAPID}
framework of dynamic analyses
(
33
)
.
Our tool processes a trace
\sigma
and reports the first transaction which completes a cycle in the underlying conflict graph, as per standard.
We compare the performance of our tool to existing algorithms in the literature, on standard benchmarks.

\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
is faster on each benchmark, achieving an average speedup of
2.0\times
,
4.5\times
, and
12\times
over
\mathtt{RegionTrack}
,
\mathtt{Aerodrome}
, and
\mathtt{Velodrome}
, respectively.
Moreover,
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
is always faster than
\mathtt{RegionTrack}
in detecting increasing-cycle violations (this is the only baseline that makes sound and complete reports of increasing-cycles), with an average speedup of
1.7\times
.

We also implement
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
inside the widely used LLVM
\mathtt{TSAN}
framework
(
43
)
for runtime analysis.
The core data structure of
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
is designed to use little locking in a runtime setting, and our implementation exploits this feature to avoid expensive synchronization between threads most of the time.
We perform scalability experiments on a standard set of benchmarks, and our results show that
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
incurs only a marginal slowdown and memory cost over the data-race detector engine of
\mathtt{TSAN}
(
1.49\times
and
1.06\times
, respectively, on average).
Relative to the original execution, the average time and memory overhead is
4.63\times
and
3.83\times
, respectively,
which generally aligns with industrial expectations
(
43
)
.
To our knowledge, this is the first case of an atomicity tester that achieves such a small overhead, making it now possible to monitor atomicity at overheads that are industry standard.

Preliminaries

We begin with general notation on concurrent executions and the atomicity notions we consider.

2.1.
Concurrent Execution Model

Events and traces.

A trace is a sequence of events
\sigma=e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
, encapsulating the execution of a concurrent program.
Given an event
e
of
\sigma
, we write
\sigma[{:}e]
to denote the prefix of
\sigma
up to (including)
e
.
In turn, an event is a tuple
e=\langle t,i,op\rangle
, where
t
is an identifier of the thread executing
e
,
i
is an incremental id for thread
t
(i.e. it denotes the
i
-th event of
t
), and
op
is the operation performed by
e
,
which is one of the following.

1.

\mathtt{begin}
, denoting that
e
is the beginning of a transaction.

2.

\mathtt{end}
, denoting that
e
is the end of a transaction.

3.

\mathtt{r}(x)
, denoting that
e
reads global variable
x
.

4.

\mathtt{w}(x)
, denoting that
e
writes to global variable
x
.

5.

\mathtt{acq}(\ell)
, denoting that
e
acquires the lock
\ell
.

6.

\mathtt{rel}(\ell)
, denoting that
e
releases the lock
\ell
.

We often denote the
\mathtt{begin}
and
\mathtt{end}
events as
\triangleright
and
\triangleleft
, respectively.
We use
\mathtt{tid}(e)
,
\mathtt{op}(e)
, and
\operatorname{id}(e)
, to denote the thread identifier, the operation, and identifier of
e
, respectively.
If
e
accesses a variable
v
or a lock
\ell
, we let
\mathtt{var}(e)
denote
v
or
\ell
, respectively.
We often denote an event
e
as
\langle t,op\rangle
, omitting its identifier.
Note that we ignore the values read/written, as these are not important in our setting.
We also don’t explicitly consider fork/join events, though these can be handled naturally.

Traces have to be well-formed: each
\mathtt{acq}(\ell)
is paired with a corresponding
\mathtt{rel}(\ell)
in the same thread, and no lock is held by more than one thread simultaneously.
Moreover, for any thread
t

\mathtt{begin}
and
\mathtt{end}

\mathtt{begin}

\mathtt{begin}
and
\mathtt{end}

\mathtt{end}

Transactions.

Given a trace

We make frequent use of partial orders over events

A (strict) partial order

We let

Given two events

The trace

\sigma

e_{1}<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{TR}}e_{2}

2.2.
Atomicity as Serializability

Serial traces.

As a natural consequence of concurrency, different transactions may overlap in a trace
\sigma
.
We call
\sigma

serial
if there is no such overlap, i.e., for any two transaction begin events
e_{1},e_{2}
such that
e_{1}<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{TR}}e_{2}
,
we have
e_{3}<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{TR}}e_{2}

e_{3}
is the matching end event of
e_{1}

Serializability
captures the fact that, although
\sigma
might not be serial, its behavior is equivalent to a serial trace
\sigma^{\prime}
that can be obtained by permuting adjacent non-conflicting events in
\sigma

If such a transformation exists,
\sigma
is deemed
serializable

Otherwise,
\sigma
witnesses an atomicity violation.
In the following, we make these concepts formal.

\sigma

\mathsf{Events}_{\sigma}=\mathsf{Events}_{\sigma^{\prime}}

<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{CHB}}=<_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{\mathtt{CHB}}

\sigma

Detecting Atomicity Violations

In this section we present

We start with some basic lemmas that capture serializability (

3.1.
Serializability Conditions

Transaction-happens-before.

Given a trace
\sigma
, the
transaction-happens-before
relation
<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
is the smallest transitive relation over the transactions of
\sigma
with the property that, for any two distinct transactions
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, if there exist
e_{1}\in T_{1}
and
e_{2}\in T_{2}
such that
e_{1}<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{CHB}}e_{2}
, then
T_{1}<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}T_{2}
.
Conceptually, if
<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
is a partial order, we can arrive at a serial trace
\sigma^{\prime}
witnessing the serializability of
\sigma
by linearizing
<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
.
This is formally captured in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1 (
(
14
)
).

A trace
\sigma
is conflict-serializable iff
<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
is irreflexive.

For example, the trace
\sigma_{3}
(
Fig.

3(a)
) yields a cyclic
<_{\sigma_{3}}^{\mathtt{THB}}
due to the orderings
T_{1}\to T_{2}\to T_{3}\to T_{1}
.
It is easy to see that
<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
satisfies the following monotonicity property.

Remark 1.

For any event
e
of
\sigma
, we have
<_{\sigma[{:}e]}^{\mathtt{THB}}\subseteq<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
.

Monotonicity implies that there is a smallest prefix of
\sigma
when a conflict-serializability violation occurs (i.e.,
<_{\sigma[:e]}^{\mathtt{THB}}
becomes cyclic).
Different atomicity testers employ different techniques for detecting cycles in
<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
by performing a single pass (i.e., streaming-style) of
\sigma
and checking whether
<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
is irreflexive at each step.

\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
is based on the notions of latest remote and earliest local transactions, that we develop below.

Latest remote transactions and earliest local transactions.

For a trace
\sigma
and two threads
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
, we define the
latest remote transaction
of
t_{2}
known to
t_{1}
,
\operatorname{LRT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})
, as follows.

•

If
t_{1}
is not executed in
\sigma
, then
\operatorname{LRT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})=\bot
.
Otherwise, let
T_{1}
be the latest transaction of
t_{1}
in
\sigma
.

•

If
t_{2}
has executed a transaction
T_{2}
such that
T_{2}<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}T_{1}
, then
\operatorname{LRT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})
is the latest such transaction
T_{2}
, otherwise
\operatorname{LRT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})=\bot
.

If
\operatorname{LRT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})\neq\bot

earliest local transaction
in
t_{1}
for
t_{2}
as
\operatorname{ELT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})=T^{\prime}_{1}

T^{\prime}_{1}
is the earliest transaction in

such that
\operatorname{LRT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}T^{\prime}_{1}

Note that such a transaction must exist, being either
T_{1}
or some earlier transaction.
On the other hand, if
\operatorname{LRT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})=\bot

\operatorname{ELT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})=\bot
.
In words,
\operatorname{LRT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})
is the latest transaction of thread

that

is aware of,
while
\operatorname{ELT}_{\sigma}^{t_{1}}(t_{2})
is the earliest transaction of
t_{1}
that was made aware of that transaction of
t_{2}
.

3.4.
Lower Bounds

Finally, in this section we establish two fundamental lower bounds on the time and space complexity of monitoring conflict-serializability.

A super-linear online time lower bound.

Recall that, if we relinquish the practical requirement that a violation is reported in an online fashion as soon as it occurs, conflict-serializability violations can be detected in
O(n)
time, by simply recording the direct
<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
orderings and checking for the existence of a cycle.

\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
operates in an online setting, and although it also achieves linear running time when
k=O(1)
, its complexity is generally superlinear.
Here we prove that such an overhead for the online problem is likely unavoidable, using techniques from fine-grained complexity theory.
In particular, the Online Matrix-Vector multiplication (OMv) problem is stated as follows.
Given a
m\times m
matrix
M
and an online sequence of vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
,
after a possibly polynomial-time preprocessing of
M
, the task is to compute each product
Mv_{i}
before
v_{i+1}
is revealed.
The corresponding OMv hypothesis
(
21
)

states that any algorithm for OMv requires
\Omega(m^{3-\epsilon})
time, for any fixed
\epsilon>0
.
We establish the following super-linear lower bound based on OMv, which states that the detection on conflict-serializability violations in an online manner is a fundamentally more difficult problem.

Lemma 3.7.

Any algorithm for conflict-serializability violations over traces
\sigma
that reports a violation when it occurs (in particular, at the first event
e
for which
<_{\sigma[:e]}^{\mathtt{THB}}
is cyclic, or even when
\mathtt{txn}(e)

\epsilon>0
,
under the OMv hypothesis.

We further remark that the lower bound of

In this case,
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}

O(n^{2})

A linear space lower bound.

Note that the space usage of

5.
Concurrent Runtime Setting

We now turn our attention to the online setting, where atomicity must be monitored during program execution.
This poses the challenge of sharing the data structure
\mathtt{DS}
among threads, and accessing/updating it concurrently.
To avoid data races (which can lead to wrong results), such accesses must be appropriately locked.
Existing atomicity checkers address this challenge by forcing each thread to acquire a global lock at each step, which can cause considerable congestion.
Instead, the data structure
\mathtt{DS}
of
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
optimizes for the case when the transaction of the thread executing the current step is not known to any other thread, which is by far the most common case.
In such cases, updates on
\mathtt{DS}
can be performed concurrently via a combination of read and write locks, while completely avoiding the use of a global lock, and therefore reducing congestion.

provides an illustration.

Correctness.

We now outline the correctness for
Algorithm

5
, by focusing on three key aspects.

Deadlock-freeness.

First, note that the algorithm cannot deadlock:
\mathtt{reachable}
does not have nested locking, whereas in all other cases, acquiring a thread lock of the form
\mathbb{L}_{t_{i}}
(in lines
5
,
5
,
5
) is preceded by accessing the global lock
\mathbb{L}
(in lines
5
,
5
).
Note, however, that the hot path is in lines
5
to
5
in
\mathtt{insertEdgeBackwards}
, where
\mathbb{L}
is read-acquired, thus allows the concurrent execution of multiple threads, avoiding congestion.
If multiple readers hold
\mathbb{L}
, deadlocks are avoided due to the use of
\mathtt{tryWLock}(\mathbb{L}_{t_{2}})
at line
5
.
This can cause a livelock, which in practice resolves quickly.

Mutual exclusion.

Second, the algorithm guarantees mutual exclusion: every time a full transitive closure is started by a thread, the data structure is protected by
\mathbb{L}
(line
5
) so that no other thread can modify it.
Moreover, the locks
\mathbb{L}_{t}
of all threads
t
have been read-acquired (line
5
), which also avoids write-read races.
All other data structure updates are such that thread
t_{2}
only writes on the
\operatorname{\mathsf{From}}_{t_{2}}
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{To}}_{t_{2}}
clocks at line

, which avoids write-write races.
Moreover, write-read races are avoided because whenever thread
t_{2}
accesses the vector clocks

and
\operatorname{\mathsf{To}}_{t_{1}}
of some other thread

\mathbb{L}_{t_{1}}
(line

\mathbb{L}_{t_{2}}
(line

A benign race condition.

Finally, notice a subtlety with regards to the accesses of the flag
\mathtt{hasOutEdge}_{t_{1}}

Here, a race condition may cause some thread

and

In practice, however, this occurs rarely to warrant the overhead of avoiding this race condition via locking.

Benchmarks.

Our benchmark set consists of 13 popular open-source projects known for high concurrency, also used in related works (e.g.,
(

).

(

, and has been provided the specifications of prior work (e.g.,
(

).
In high level, this process starts with a fine-grained specification which marks each function as atomic.

\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
and
\mathtt{TSAN}

Related Work

Detecting atomicity vulnerabilities automatically has been an active research topic and targeted by a range of methods.
Atomizer
(
16
)
was a precursor of Velodrome, based on Lipton’s reduction theory
(
25
)
.
Techniques like AtomFuzzer
(
37
)
and PENELOPE
(
45
)
aim at generating schedules that expose atomicity violations, while others focus on synthesizing tests for the same task
(
41
)
.
Algorithms like
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
can speed up such approaches, by efficiently testing whether the generated execution indeed constitutes an atomicity violation.
Since atomicity invariants are not always explicit, several approaches aim at inferring them automatically
(
27
;
38
)
.
Finally, atomicity has also been targeted by static analyses, most prominently using types and effects
(
17
;
42
;
13
)
.

View serializability is another atomicity notion, which is more general than conflict-serializability.
Intuitively, the serial witness trace
\sigma^{*}
can now be obtained from the observed trace
\sigma
by swapping conflicting events, as long every read observes the same write in
\sigma
and
\sigma^{*}
.
However, testing view serializability is much harder: it is NP-complete in general
(
36
)
, and even hard when parameterized by the number of threads
(
30
)
.

Dynamic analyses are a standard approach to concurrency bugs.
Besides atomicity violations, they have been targeting
data races
(
15
;
39
;
20
;
24
)
,
deadlocks
(
48
)
,
memory vulnerabilities
(
8
)
, and
linearizability violations
(
19
;
11
;
1
;
23
)
.
Their efficiency has been an active pursuit in both theory and practice
(
29
;
46
;
22
;
40
;
7
)
.

8.
Conclusion

We have introduced
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
, a new algorithm for dynamically testing for atomicity violations.

\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
is theoretically faster than all previous algorithms for the problem, while it also has small space complexity.
Our experimental results show that the theoretical advantage of
\mathtt{AtomSanitizer}
is also realized in practice, while its lightweight time and memory footprint make it suitable for a runtime monitoring setting.
Interesting future directions include transferring the insights of this work towards monitoring transactional consistency in databases, against a range of popular isolation levels
(
4
;
9
;
35
)
.

Appendix E
Experiments on DoubleChecker

We have also implemented the first phase of
\mathtt{DoubleChecker}
, which performs the
Imprecise cycle detection
(ICD).
In particular, it uses
\mathtt{Octet}

(
7
)
, a software based concurrency control mechanism, to maintain the imprecise happens-before relations.
With this technique, the ICD phase does not record the last write or read event of each memory location.
This allows it to process each event faster, while maintaining a conflict graph that overapproximates
<_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{THB}}
.
If a cycle is detected in this graph,
\mathtt{DoubleChecker}
runs
\mathtt{Velodrome}
from scratch, to try and verify the cycle.
Since
\mathtt{DoubleChecker}
does not make precise violation reports, we have not used in our main experimental setting.
Here we report how the ICD phase
\mathtt{DoubleChecker}
performs in our benchmark set.

The results are shown in
Table

5
.
We see that ICD is overall considerably faster than
\mathtt{Velodrome}
.
For some benchmarks,
\mathtt{DoubleChecker}
stops earlier than other tools, because it will conservatively insert unnecessary edges into the transactional happens-before graph, which may introduce cycles.
Note, however, that ICD reports violations on all benchmarks except two (
philo
and
fop
), and has a high false positive rate.
Rerunning
\mathtt{Velodrome}
on all the positive reports of ICD clearly results in running times that are far larger than
