Biomechanical evaluation of a laxity-minimizing suture for rotator cuff repair
Authors: Maxim Vanderstappen, Renaud Debras, Thomas Louwagie, Heleen Fehervary, Philippe Debeer, Filip Verhaegen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoss of approximation force can impair healing following rotator cuff repair. This study investigates the biomechanical properties of a high-strength, laxity-minimizing suture designed to enhance postrepair approximation force.MethodsA comparative biomechanical model was employed to evaluate eight matched pairs of fresh-frozen ovine infraspinatus tendons. Tendons were repaired using either a laxity-minimizing suture (Dynacord group) or a reference suture (Permacord group) in a double-row configuration with bone anchors. Quasi-static and cyclic tensile loading were applied in a phosphate buffered saline bath at 37 °C to simulate physiological conditions. Tendon-bone displacement was quantified under different conditions using digital image correlation.ResultsFollowing pretensioning at 25 N, relaxation forces at 2 hours were 7.68 N for Dynacord and 5.50 N Permacord, and 6.45 N vs. 3.58 N at 16 hours, respectively. A significant difference in approximation force was observed between groups (2.18 ± 0.67 N, P = .01). Under cyclic loading, all Dynacord-repaired tendons exhibited gap formation below the critical 3 mm threshold at loads up to 105 N, with two samples sustaining up to 155 N. In contrast, only seven Permacord samples withstood 105 N and only 1 reached 155 N. While Dynacord showed lower creep values under loaded and unloaded conditions, the difference did not reach statistical significance.ConclusionDynacord suture could have superior approximation force retention compared to Permacord under low tensile forces. However, under cyclic loading conditions, Dynacord did not show statistically significant biomechanical advantages in this dynamic cadaveric model.

Rotator cuff tears are a common pathology, with reported prevalence ranging from 5% to over 35% in the general population.25 These injuries often lead to shoulder pain and disability.24 Surgical repair can be performed if conservative treatment fails; however, nonhealing rates remain high, ranging from 11% to 94%, with most failures occurring at the suture-tendon interface.7,16 Several biological factors, such as proximal humeral bone quality, the rotator cuff tendon quality, and vascularity, are known to influence healing after rotator cuff repair. Additionally, mechanical aspects and the properties of materials used in the repair, including sutures, anchors, and surgical knots, also play a significant role in the outcome of the repair.1,6,13,15,26

Prior research has indicated that the contact force at the interface between the reattached tendon and the proximal humeral footprint is substantially reduced following repair. This reduction can lead to gap formation, decreased tendon-bone, hereby potentially compromising normal healing. One of the main contributors to this loss of footprint compression is suture laxity. Therefore, understanding and optimizing the mechanical performance of the sutures is essential to decrease this phenomenon.4 To obtain this goal, an ideal suture should exhibit high ultimate tensile strength, low creep (progressive elongation under constant load), and minimal relaxed elongation (permanent elongation after sustained tension).13 Considerable research has been devoted to the evaluation of suture viscoelasticity, focusing on properties such as stiffness, initial elongation, static and dynamic creep, and plastic deformation.13,19 High-strength tapes have shown superior biomechanical performance in both mechanical and cadaveric models, including increased tendon-footprint contact pressure and enhanced suture-tissue pullout strength relative to round high-strength sutures; however, the extent to which these findings translate to improved clinical outcomes is still uncertain.3,9,22

Recently, attention has turned toward novel suture designs such as Dynacord (DePuy Mitek, Raynham, MA, USA), a laxity-minimizing high-strength round suture, developed to reduce suture laxity, helping to maintain tendon-to-bone approximation and preserve footprint compression during the healing phase.2,10,18,21 Dynacord is a high-strength suture with a silicone core embedded with 12% salt crystals (NaCl). This salt-impregnated silicone technology should enable hydration and radial expansion in an aqueous environment. This expansion, combined with a flexible braided outer sheath made of ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene and an inner polyester sheath, allows the core to expand in diameter and shorten the suture by up to 7.1% of its original length thereby reducing laxity.2 This design presents several potential biomechanical advantages as follows9,20: (1) suture shortening may minimize suture creep, reduce the risk of 'cheese-wiring' damage to the tendon, and prevent failure at the knot-tendon interface. (2) Radial expansion could enhance the tendon-suture contact, reducing local pressure exerted on the tendon surface and risk of local tissue necrosis while promoting a larger healing interface at the footprint. (3) The assumed reversible plastic deformation may enable the suture to absorb varying tensile forces applied to the repaired tendon.18 Biomechanical testing has demonstrated that Dynacord reduces tendon cut-through compared to other sutures.20 Additionally, studies have reported improved knot and loop security with Dynacord sutures.8,9,14

This study aimed to evaluate the biomechanical properties of a high strength, laxity-minimizing suture (Dynacord, DePuy Mitek) under dynamic physiological loading, using an ovine infraspinatus tendon-bone interface model. Specifically, we compared the biomechanical performance of Dynacord with a non–laxity-minimizing high-strength suture (Permacord, DePuy Mitek, Raynham, MA, USA). We hypothesized that Dynacord will demonstrate superior biomechanical properties compared to Permacord, while minimizing gap formation at the tendon-bone interface.

Material and methods

Specimen preparation

Eight matched pairs of ovine humeri with intact infraspinatus tendons and no macroscopic signs of tearing were harvested from fresh cadavers by a single orthopedic surgeon (R.D.).23 Specimens were stored at −20 °C and thawed at 4 °C 24 hours prior to preparation and mounting. After sharply detaching the infraspinatus from its humeral insertion, a double-row repair with bone anchors was performed using a standardized medial mattress-suture technique by a single orthopedic surgeon (M.V.). For all specimens, two 4.5 mm Healix medial row anchors and two 4.5 mm Healix Advance lateral row anchors (DePuy Mitek, Raynham, MA, USA) were used (Fig. 1). The medial anchors were single-loaded with either a #2 laxity-minimizing suture (Dynacord group) or a #2 non–laxity-minimizing suture (Permacord group) with suture type randomized for each shoulder. Both suture types were tested in each pair and sutures were placed at equal distances from the myotendinous junction to ensure consistency. The distal humerus was secured in a custom aluminum mold using a two-component resin. To enable digital image correlation (DIC), graphite powder was applied to the tendon and bone surfaces to create a speckle pattern, allowing for optical tracking of their relative motion during testing (Fig. 2).Figure 1Schematic representation of the double row repair at the tendon-bone interface using single-loaded suture anchors for the medial row.Figure 2Tendon-bone repair construct: (a) covered with graphite speckles for visual tracking.

Sample mounting

Specimens were mounted on a dynamic tensile test bench (3330 Electroforce, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA). The aluminum mold, containing the cemented humerus, was secured with a bracket at one end, while the infraspinatus tendon was clamped at the other. Testing was conducted in a physiological environment by submerging the specimen in a bio bath filled with phosphate buffered saline, maintained at 37 °C ensuring optimal hydration of the sutures as core hydration is a key factor for suture performance (Fig. 3). A high-resolution camera (Grasshopper 3, FLIR Systems, Wilsonville, Oregon, USA) and a LED light source (Profilux LED1000,Hedler Systemlicht GmbH, Runkel, Germany) were positioned to capture images of the bone and tendon surfaces during the test. Images were recorded using VicSnap software (Correlated Solutions, Inc., Columbia, South Carolina, USA), integrated by isi-sys (isi-sys GmbH, Kassel, Germany).Figure 3Overview of the dynamic tensile test bench setup: (a) the distal end of the repaired tendon is secured in a static clamp, while the humerus bone is moved vertically to apply the desired tension. Testing

To eliminate tendon slack, a 5 N preload was applied to each tendon, followed by a preconditioning loading cycle consisting of 20 repetitions between 5 N and 15 N.

Relaxation test

Next, a relaxation test was performed to evaluate the tension-retaining capacity of the tendon-suture complex. A tensile load of 25 N was applied to the tendon, after which it gradually relaxed under a constant strain. The displacement on the test bench was maintained for 16 hours, during which force relaxation was measured. Force and time data were captured at a sampling rate of 20 Hz, while camera images were captured at 1 Hz.

Cyclic test

Cyclic loading was performed to simulate the mechanical properties under dynamic physiological loading conditions, using a stepwise increase in maximum loads. After applying a preload of 25 N at a rate of 1 N/s, each specimen underwent 50 cycles between 25 N and a maximum load at a constant rate. The maximum loads applied were as follows: 55 N, 80 N, 105 N, 155 N, 205 N, and 255 N. Force and time data were recorded at a rate of 100 Hz, while images were captured at a rate of 20 Hz.

Data processing

Data processing was performed using MATLAB (MathWorks R2018b, Natick, Massachusetts, USA), DIC analysis was performed using VIC 2D software (Correlated Solutions, Inc., Columbia, South Carolina, USA) to measure any tendon-bone displacement. Gap formation between bone and tendon

Two surfaces were defined as follows: one representing the bone surface adjacent to the anchoring points and the other corresponding to the tendon around the knot locations. The displacement of these surfaces was tracked over time using DIC. The positions of the bone and tendon were determined by averaging two and three points, respectively, within each surface. The relative displacement between the bone and tendon was quantified as the difference in their vertical positions (Fig. 4).Figure 4Gap formation is measured as the distance between paired reference points on either side of the sutured construct.

Gap formation is measured as the distance between paired reference points on either side of the sutured construct.

Dynamic creep

Dynamic creep was defined as the permanent deformation resulting from repeated cyclic loading. It was extracted for both the loaded and unloaded states by monitoring the relative displacement between the bone and tendon at each peak and valley during cyclic testing. The loaded (c50, L) and unloaded (c50, U) dynamic creep values after 50 cycles were calculated by averaging the dynamic creep of the last five peaks and valleys for each loading step.

Statistical analysis

A mixed models analysis was conducted using a commercially available version of R Statistics (Shortstop Beagles version 4.2.3, Vienna, Austria) to compare the biomechanical properties of both suture types. The homogeneity of variances was confirmed with a Levene test. For post hoc comparisons, false discovery rate correction was applied. The level of statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Cyclic testing

All specimens successfully completed three full cyclic loading steps reaching up to 105 N. The primary mode of failure in all samples was tendon cut-through. Three specimens failed during the fourth loading step (up to 155 N), occurring bilaterally in both the Dynacord and Permacord groups. The remaining five bilateral samples withstood the cyclic loading up to 155 N before failing at loads between 205 N and 355 N. All Dynacord-sutured specimens demonstrated gap formation of less than the critical 3 mm threshold at loads up to 105 N, with two specimens maintaining this threshold up to 155 N. In contrast, seven Permacord specimens met the <3 mm threshold up to 105 N and only 1 maintained it at 155 N. Similar trends were observed during the unloaded phase: all eight Dynacord samples rested at < 3 mm after loading at 105 N and four remained < 3 mm after 155 N. All Permacord samples rested at <3 mm after 105 N and two did so after 155 N. Detailed results are provided in Tables IV and V and illustrated in Figures 6 and 7. Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the creep behavior and mechanical properties of a laxity-minimizing suture under dynamic physiological loading using an ovine infraspinatus tendon-bone interface model. Our testing protocol was designed to assess the viscoelastic behavior of the suture-tendon interface, recognized as the weakest link in rotator cuff repair constructs. Our study hypothesis was partially supported, as Dynacord demonstrated improved approximation force retention under static conditions, but no statistically significant advantage was observed under cyclic loading.

The relaxation test revealed a substantial reduction in absolute tension of the suture construct within the first 2 h postrepair, consistent with prior reports of rapid contact pressure loss following rotator cuff repair.4 After this initial phase, the relaxation process stabilized. Notably, a consistent 2.18 N difference in tension was observed between Dynacord and Permacord from the second hour to the end of the experiment (16 hours), with Dynacord maintaining higher tension approximation force under static conditions and low tensile loading. While this absolute difference may appear modest, it represents approximately a 9% increase in approximation force. However, the small absolute magnitude could limit clinical relevance and higher initial loads might have increased the discriminatory ability of our study. The relaxation forces measured in this study were approximately 10-15 N lower than those reported previously for Dynacord.17 In the study by Algeri et al., Dynacord and a non–laxity-minimizing suture were compared under static and cyclic loading. Dynacord retained significantly higher approximation force under both conditions (19 N vs. 8 N and 6 N, respectively). Furthermore, they also reported an initial 17N drop in approximation force followed by a rebound that exceeding the initial load attributed to Dynacord's biomechanical properties, such as stiffness and extension, which are directly related to gapping under load. The rebound observed by Algeri et al was linked to the radial expansion Dynacord's silicone core containing water-attracting salt. Brassart et al4 similarly reported an immediate loss of approximately 60% in contact pressure after suture tying, with further losses over the following hour.

In contrast, our data did not confirm the relaxation dynamics found by Algeri et al. Both of the tested sutures demonstrated marked tension loss within the first 2 h, consistent with the near-instantaneous contact pressure reduction described for conventional sutures. Furthermore, we did not observe the expected rebound in Dynacord. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. Unlike Algeri et al, we employed a bone-tendon interface rather than a purely mechanical setup. This model seems to be more physiological since it introduces clinically relevant factors such as cheese-wiring and tendon slack, which may reduce final approximation force after rotator cuff repair. Whether these factors alone account for the divergent relaxation patterns remains uncertain.

In our dynamic test testing phase, we used a phosphate buffered saline environment to ensure hydration and optimize the potential advantages of Dynacord. The cyclic loading protocol involved stepwise increases in maximum load to simulate physiological loading conditions. For rotator cuff repair constructs, displacement ≤3 mm at the tendon-bone interface under a 60 N load is commonly regarded as an arbitrary threshold for acceptable gap formation and optimal healing.5,12 Three specimens failed during the fourth loading step (up to 155 N), occurring bilaterally in both groups, suggesting that the early failure was likely due to specimen variability, rather than suture material. All remaining samples, except 1 repaired with Permacord, maintained displacement below 3 mm at 105 N. Although a favorable trend toward reduced displacement was noted with Dynacord in loaded dynamic creep, this difference did not reach statistical significance. However, the absence of significant group differences under cyclic loading may reflect insufficient power due to failure of the 3 specimens at lower loading, rather than true equivalence.

Tendon cut-through, is considered a key mechanism of failure both in dynamic testing and after rotator cuff repair. Owens et al20 reported significantly less cut through with Dynacord comparted to a non–laxity-minimizing suture in an ovine cadaveric model. However, the semiwet testing conditions in their study (prehydration followed by dry testing) may limit generalizability, and thus their results should be interpreted with caution. In our study, cut-through distance was not specifically measured. Instead, we evaluated overall displacement of the sutured tendon from its original fixation under both loaded and unloaded conditions. This approach recognizes that factors such as bite width and anchor configuration may have an equal or greater impact on repair strength than cheese-wiring alone.11

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, paired tendons were used to reduce interspecimen variability but tendon width and thickness were not measured. These anatomical differences may have contributed to early failures in some samples. These early failure during cycling testing of three specimens may further contribute to a possible type 2 error in this study. Although we conducted the rotator cuff repair in a standardized fashion, the exact position of the sutures in the tendon, distance from the myotendinous junction and exposed suture length were not quantified, which may introduce variability in local stress distribution and construct stiffness. Second, differences in experimental setup from other studies limit direct comparison. Relaxation testing was capped at 16 hours, as we anticipated Dynacord's salt-impregnated silicone core to shorten and expand radially within the first 2 h postknot tying, rendering prolonged observation unnecessary. Indeed, approximation forces stabilized after 6-8 hours for both sutures, with curves approaching an asymptote, making later changes unlikely. Third, our starting load was relatively low (25 N), decreasing to mean relaxation forces of 7.68 N (Dynacord) and 5.50 N (Permacord) after 2 h, with a plateau thereafter. These forces are lower than those potentially encountered in vivo during healing. Fourth, using Permacord as a reference introduces challenges in contextualizing results, as its viscoelastic properties have not been previously reported in biomechanical literature. Thus, this study is mostly a comparison of the biomechanics of two different sutures rather than an assessment of the biomechanical effectiveness of Dynacord.

Fifth, during cyclic testing, minimal relaxation time was allowed between load steps, meaning that our unloaded dynamic creep values likely represent approximations rather than true gap formation. Finally, our study uses suture-tendon construct elongation and relaxation as an indirect measure of postrepair approximation force rather than directly quantifying forces at the tendon-bone interface. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting relative suture behavior rather than direct approximation force.

Conclusion

This study assessed the creep behavior and mechanical properties of Dynacord in a tendon-bone interface model under physiological loading. Dynacord could have superior approximation force retention compared to Permacord under low static loads and initial fixation. However, under cyclic loading, no statistically significant biomechanical advantage was observed in this dynamic cadaveric model.
