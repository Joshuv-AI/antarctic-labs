Human length telomeres restrict the regenerative potential of hematopoietic stem cells in mice
Authors: Melissa M. Rowe, Joanna Tober, Vivian Ortiz, Riham Smoom, Yehuda Tzfati, Nancy Speck, Klaus H. Kaestner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Telomere biology disorders (TBDs), including dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome (HHS), demonstrate that critically short telomeres cause bone marrow failure. Mutations in RTEL1, encoding a telomere-associated helicase, are among the genetic causes of DC and HHS. However, whether telomere length variation within the physiological human range affects hematopoietic function remains poorly understood. We investigated this question using the "Telomouse" model, which harbors a single amino acid substitution in Rtel1 (Rtel1M492K/M492K) that results in human-length telomeres rather than the considerably longer telomeres characteristic of wild-type Mus musculus. Although Telomice maintain normal steady-state hematopoiesis, proliferative stress induced by serial 5-fluorouracil treatment or bone marrow transplantation into lethally irradiated recipients revealed significant depletion of bone marrow progenitor cells compared to wild-type controls. Nanopore sequencing and fluorescence in situ hybridization demonstrated an elevated frequency of critically short telomeres in stressed Telomouse hematopoietic cells, accompanied by increased DNA damage (γH2AX foci) and apoptotic signaling (cleaved caspase-3). These findings establish that telomere length within the normal human range represents a critical determinant of hematopoietic reserve capacity under proliferative stress, with implications for understanding individual variation in bone marrow resilience and TBD pathophysiology.

Telomeres are repeated sequences of DNA complexed with shelterin proteins at the ends of the chromosomes, which protect genome integrity and function (1). The importance of telomere length in highly regenerative tissues is emphasized in humans with genetic telomere biology disorders (TBDs) such as dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson Syndrome (HHS). The predominant symptom and main cause of mortality in DC and HHS is bone marrow failure, underscoring the impact of dysfunctional telomeres on replicative cell niches such as the hematopoietic system (2). Mutations in multiple genes can cause DC and HHS, including TERT, encoding telomerase (3, 4, 5), TINF2 (6, 7, 8), encoding a shelterin complex component, and RTEL1 (9, 10, 11), encoding regulator of telomerase elongation helicase 1, among others.

Telomere biology disorders manifest predominantly in the bone marrow, due to the high proliferation rate of the hematopoietic system. Thus, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) replicate every 25 to 50 weeks in humans, with the descendent progenitor cells cycling much faster (12). Labeling of HSCs in transgenic mice via inducible expression of a histone 2B-green fluorescence fusion protein and analysis of the kinetics of label loss suggested that in mice, HSCs have non-uniform proliferation rates. Thus, 20% of HSCs were found to turn over at a rate of 0.8 to 1.8% per day, while 80% of HSCs replicated at a much faster rate (13). Based on labeling of replicating cells with thymidine analogues, it has been estimated that 30 to 31 mitotic events separate HSCs from mature circulating blood cells in mice (14). This self-renewal capability is dependent on the enzyme telomerase, which adds telomeric repeats to compensate for the shortening that occurs during each cycle of DNA replication. Human telomerase is active predominantly in germ, stem and progenitor cells (15).

Unfortunately, human telomere biology is not well recapitulated by the common Mus musculus lab strains, which have telomere repeat arrays that are about 5 times longer than those present in humans (16). As a result, M. musculus cells can tolerate many cycles of replication even in the absence of telomerase activity before telomeres become critically short. Even mice null for the telomerase RNA component mTERC gene, lacking all telomerase activity, are viable until the sixth generation of intercrossing (17); in contrast, complete Telomerase deficiency is incompatible with life in humans (3). To bridge this gap between human and mouse biology, we recently derived a new M. musculus C57BL/6 mouse strain with generationally stable human-length telomere repeat arrays, the ‘Telomouse’ (16). This was accomplished by introducing a point mutation into the Rtel1 gene that changes the methionine at position 492 of the RTEL1 helicase to a lysine (M492K), a mutation that caused progressive telomere loss across generations until F15. While these mice have telomeres within the healthy human range, they are much shorter than the norm for their species. Importantly, starting in F15, the telomere length has stabilized and shows no evidence of significant reduction. Previous work shows that these mice have a blunted proliferative response to injury in both the colon and liver (16, 18). Here, we tested the hypothesis that the shortened telomeres present in the Telomouse bone marrow might also limit the regenerative capacity of the hematopoietic system.

Results

In order to evaluate if proliferative capacity is limited in late-generation Telomice (Rtel1M492K/M492K; between F11 and F17), we first determined bone marrow function at baseline under homeostatic conditions compared to otherwise isogenic M. musculus C57BL/6 controls. We isolated bone marrow from young (2 months) and aged (12 months) mice and determined the proportion of various stem and progenitor lineages using flow cytometry (Fig. 1, A and B; see Fig. S1 for flow cytometry gating strategy, and Table S1 for a list of antibodies employed). We employed antibodies to the SLAM (signaling lymphocyte activation molecule) family proteins CD150 and CD48, first described by Kiel and colleagues for the definition of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell lineages (19). While aged mice showed a relative increase in phenotypic long-term repopulating HSCs (Lineage- Sca-1+ c-Kit+ (LSK)150+48-) and a relative decrease in lymphoid-biased progenitor (LSK150-48+) cells compared to young mice, there were no differences by genotype (Fig. 1, A and B). Having established that there are no significant differences in bone marrow composition between Telomice and WT mice at baseline homeostatic conditions, we next sought to test the ability of bone marrow cells to regenerate under replicative stress. To this end, Telomice and WT mice were dosed with 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU), a cancer-treatment drug known to deplete bone marrow cells (20). 5-FU was administered to groups of Telomice and WT mice on days 0, 7, and 14, and bone marrow collected from all mice on day 21. Peripheral blood was also harvested at 2- to 3-day intervals over the course of the entire experiment (Fig. 2A). Throughout the experimental timeline, some mice had to be euthanized for humane reasons. This occurred significantly more frequently in Telomice than in control mice (Fig. 2B). Notably, humanely euthanized mice had pale extremities upon harvesting. Over the course of the experiment, the percentage of c-Kit+/CD45+ progenitor cells relative to total CD45+ cells increased in the peripheral blood in both groups, indicative of the 5-FU effect, with no significant differences between WT and Telomice (Fig. 2C; see Fig. S2 for flow cytometry gating strategy). However, when we analyzed the bone marrow on day 21 of the study, we noted significantly lower percentages of lineage-negative (Lin-), Sca1+ c-Kit+ (LSK) hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells in Telomice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2D). Of these, in particular, LSK CD150+ CD48-, i.e. long-term repopulating hematopoietic stem cells (19), and transient multilineage progenitor LSK CD150- CD48-populations were reduced in frequency in the Telomice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2D). It should be noted that due to increased early mortality in the Telomice, survivorship bias is present in these experiments. Therefore, the difference in bone marrow resilience and recovery between control mice and Telomice is likely understated by these findings. To directly compare the regenerative ability of Telomouse bone marrow to that of wild type M. musculus C57BL/6 mice, we performed a competitive bone marrow repopulation study (Fig. 3A). Bone marrow was harvested from 8–10-week-old CD45.2+ Telomice and 8–10-week-old CD45.1+/CD45.2+ WT mice, and a mixture of one million cells each of the two populations was administered to lethally irradiated CD45.1+ hosts via retro-orbital injection. Peripheral blood and bone marrow were collected 16 weeks post-transplant. Cells were stained for common immune and hematopoietic stem cell markers and analyzed based on positivity for CD45.1 and CD45.2 to differentiate the donor and competitor contributions. In the peripheral blood, there was a slight reduction in the contribution of bone marrow from Telomouse donors to T-cells, macrophages, and total CD45+ cells compared to WT donors (Fig. 3B; see Fig. S3 for flow cytometry gating strategy). In the bone marrow, there was a higher contribution by Telomouse donors to CD45+ cells due to a large contribution to the granulocyte population (Fig. 3C). When analyzing the stem cell compartment, we noted nearly equal contribution of the two donor groups to the various stem and progenitor populations, though the Telomice provided significantly fewer LSK CD150+ CD48+ cells to the reconstituted bone marrow (Fig. 3D). LSK CD150+ CD48+ cells are considered to be lineage-restricted progenitors (21). Next, we sought to challenge the regenerative potential of the Telomouse-derived hematopoietic stem cells even further by forcing the cells to undergo additional rounds of cell division. To this end, bone marrow from five of the original transplant recipient mice that had a higher percentage of Telomouse donor-derived cells than wild-type competitor cells in the CD45+ population of PB and BM, and multilineage donor cell engraftment, was transplanted into two irradiated CD45.1+ host mice in secondary transplants (Fig. 3A). Remarkably, stem and progenitor populations in the BM of the secondary recipient exhibited reductions in the Telomouse contribution to the hematopoietic stem and multipotent progenitor cell pools (Fig. 3E). Thus, by forcing the hematopoietic stem cell compartment of Telomice to cycle multiple times, we uncovered a regenerative limitation in these mice, as compared with the WT mice with longer telomeres.

We sought to apply a second model to determine if this effect was also present in aged mice. We repeated the competitive repopulation assay using bone marrow from aged mice (Fig. 4A). Bone marrow was harvested from 1-year-old CD45.2+ WT or Telomice, and from 8–10-week-old CD45.1+/CD45.2+ WT mice, and an equal mixture of the two combinations of young and old (WT or Telomouse) populations was administered to irradiated CD45.1+ hosts via retro-orbital injection. Peripheral blood and bone marrow were collected 16 weeks post-transplant. Cells were stained and sorted as described above (Fig. 4B; see Fig. S3 for flow cytometry gating strategy). Aged Telomouse donors were significantly less robust in contributing to peripheral blood populations, including B-cells, T-cells, macrophages, and granulocytes, than aged wild-type donors (Fig. 4B). When comparing the regenerative functionality of the two aged bone marrow populations, there were significantly fewer LSK CD150+ CD48-long-term repopulating hematopoietic stem cells from aged Telomouse donors than from aged WT donors present in their respective hosts (Fig. 4C). Genome integrity and replicative health of a cell are determined by the shortest telomeres, as below a certain length the protective shelterin complex cannot suppress the DNA damage response at the DNA end, which limits further cell proliferation (22). We sought to determine if the loss of functional telomere length in some of a cell’s chromosomes accounted for the mechanism by which stressed Telomouse bone marrow cells failed to regenerate appropriately. We analyzed metaphase spreads from untreated WT and Telomouse bone marrow cells. Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed to probe G-strand telomeres (TTAGGG). This allowed us to determine the number of chromosomes ends without detectable telomeric signal (signal-free ends) in bone marrow cells from both genotypes. Typically, four telomeres should be detectable per chromosome. We found that even at homeostasis, Telomouse bone marrow cell chromosomes displayed a significantly decreased proportion of intact telomeres (Fig. 5, A and B). We have shown before that signal-free telomere ends in the Telomouse blood represent short but functional telomeres (23). This suggests that while these cells may not be in crisis yet, they are already close since some of their telomeres are close to the critical length. Interestingly, no metaphase spreads could be generated from Telomouse bone marrow from 5-FU treated mice, which we suspect is due to cells being unable to successfully progress through mitotic checkpoints within the experimental timeframe (data not shown). This idea is supported by telomere length sequencing performed on saline- and 5FU-treated WT mice and Telomice. As expected, saline-treated Telomice had a significantly shorter median telomere length (5,182 bp) compared to saline-treated WT mice (37,865 bp). Interestingly, Telomice treated with 5-FU had significantly shorter telomeres (3,831 bp) than saline-treated Telomice. A reduction was also seen in the telomeres of 5FU-treated WT mice (21,201 bp) compared to saline-treated WT mice, although the median telomere length in 5FU-treated WT mice remained well above that from either Telomouse group. In addition to telomere integrity, we examined the effect of 5FU on WT mice and Telomice with regards to cellular damage. Whole bone marrow cells from saline- or 5FU-treated control or Telomice were stained for γH2AX and cleaved caspase 3 to investigate DNA damage and apoptosis, respectively. Slides were also stained for c-Kit as a marker of stem and progenitor cells (Fig. 6A). 5FU treatment caused increased DNA damage in WT mice and increased apoptosis in Telomice (Fig. 6B). 5FU treatment also created trends towards increased DNA damage in Telomice and increased apoptosis in WT mice, although neither of these differences were significant (Fig. 6B). In addition, saline-treated Telomouse c-Kit+ bone marrow had significantly increased γH2AX signal compared to c-Kit+ bone marrow from saline-treated WT mice (Fig. 6B). The proportion of c-Kit+ cells showing both γH2AX and cleaved caspase 3 was significantly greater in 5FU-treated Telomice than in saline-treated Telomice and showed a non-significant increase over 5FU-treated WT mice (Fig. 6B). Discussion

This study demonstrates that telomere length within the physiological human range may represent a critical determinant of hematopoietic regenerative capacity under proliferative stress. Using the Telomouse model, which carries a single amino acid substitution in Rtel1 (Rtel1M492K/M492K) resulting in human-length telomeres, we show that while steady-state hematopoiesis remains intact, the ability to respond to replicative challenges is significantly compromised. These findings have important implications for understanding individual variation in bone marrow resilience.

The most striking finding of our study is the divergence between homeostatic and stress-induced phenotypes in Telomice. Under baseline conditions, both young and aged Telomice maintain normal bone marrow architecture with appropriate proportions of long-term HSCs, multipotent progenitors, and lineage-restricted progenitors. The 5-fluorouracil challenge and serial transplantation experiments suggest that human-length telomeres may impose a reserve capacity limitation that only becomes apparent when HSCs are forced to undergo multiple rounds of division. The results from the 5-FU challenge are even more striking when the increased mortality of the Telomice is considered, as the Telomice showed reduced stem and progenitor cell recovery even with the survivorship bias caused by analyzing mice that survived until the end of treatment. The kinetics of human HSC replication, approximately once every 40 weeks compared to once every 2.5 weeks in mice (12, 13), suggest that humans naturally operate closer to their replicative limits. Our data support a model in which telomere length acts as a "buffer" that determines how many additional cell divisions can be tolerated before critical telomere erosion triggers cell cycle arrest or apoptosis. In Telomice, this buffer is substantially reduced compared to wild-type mice with ∼35 kb telomeres, making them more vulnerable to exhaustion under demand.

The inability to generate metaphase spreads from 5-FU-treated Telomouse bone marrow, combined with the significant increase in cleaved caspase 3 signal, suggests that these cells are either undergoing apoptosis or arresting at cell cycle checkpoints. This is consistent with the known mechanisms by which dysfunctional telomeres activate p53-dependent cell cycle arrest and programmed cell death (24). The increased mortality and pale extremities observed in 5-FU-treated Telomice recapitulate key features of bone marrow failure seen in patients with severe TBDs.

The aged Telomouse experiments demonstrate that the combination of shortened telomeres and physiological aging creates additive vulnerability. Aged Telomice showed more pronounced deficits in contributing to all peripheral blood populations compared to aged wild-type donors. This mirrors the phenomenon of genetic anticipation observed in families with autosomal dominant DC caused by TERT and TERC mutations, where disease severity increases, and age of onset decreases in successive generations due to inherited telomere shortening (3, 25). Human HSCs undergo approximately 30 to 31 mitotic events during normal hematopoiesis (26), and each replicative cycle shortens telomeric DNA. Individuals starting with shorter telomeres—whether due to genetic factors, inheritance patterns, or environmental exposures—will reach critical thresholds earlier in life.

A fundamental limitation of using standard laboratory mice to model human telomere biology has been the ∼5- to 10-fold difference in telomere length. Wild-type M. musculus rarely exhibit spontaneous telomere-related pathology, and telomerase-deficient mice require multiple generations of breeding to manifest phenotypes (17). The Telomouse addresses this gap by providing a model with human-length telomeres in a single generation. Our results validate the Telomouse as a more physiologically relevant system for studying how telomere dynamics influence hematopoietic function, bone marrow failure, and potentially other age-related pathologies where telomere attrition plays a role.

Our findings have several clinical implications. First, they support the use of telomere length measurement as a biomarker for hematopoietic reserve capacity, particularly in patients with unexplained cytopenias or in those being considered for bone marrow transplantation. Second, they suggest that interventions aimed at reducing replicative demand on HSCs, such as prophylactic antimicrobials to prevent infection-induced proliferative stress, may be beneficial in TBD patients or individuals with shortened telomeres. Third, they provide a mechanistic rationale for the observation that TBD patients often deteriorate following chemotherapy or immunosuppressive therapy (27, 28), highlighting the need for modified treatment regimens that minimize proliferative stress.

Future studies using the Telomouse model could investigate whether pharmacological approaches to reduce oxidative stress, enhance DNA repair, or modulate cell cycle checkpoints can preserve HSC function in the context of shortened telomeres. Additionally, the Telomouse provides a platform for testing telomerase activators or alternative lengthening mechanisms as therapeutic strategies. The model may also prove valuable for studying other age-related conditions where telomere attrition contributes to tissue dysfunction, including pulmonary fibrosis, liver disease, and cardiovascular pathology—all of which are increasingly recognized as manifestations of TBDs (24, 29, 30). Several limitations of our study should be noted. First, the RTEL1 mutation may have additional phenotypic impacts that are independent of telomere length. There is evidence that the RTEL1 helicase antagonizes homologous recombination and crossover events, functions which may also be impacted by the Telomouse mutation (16). While determining non-telomeric roles of RTEL1 in hematopoesis is beyond the scope of this work, future experiments will seek to gain clarity regarding any non-telomeric effects the Telomouse mutation may exert. Second, the Telomouse model represents shortened but functional telomeres throughout development, which differs from the progressive telomere attrition seen in human aging or in late-generation telomerase-deficient mice. Third, our experiments were conducted in relatively young mice (8–10 weeks to 1 year), and longer-term studies will be necessary to fully characterize age-dependent phenotypes. Finally, the 5-FU and transplantation models represent acute severe stress; additional studies using chronic low-level stressors may better model physiological aging and inflammation-driven hematopoietic demand.

In summary, we demonstrate that reduced telomere length in mice caused by a mutation in RTEL1 limits hematopoietic regenerative capacity under proliferative stress, despite having no impact on steady-state hematopoiesis. The Telomouse model presents a valuable tool to study the dynamics of human-length telomeres in a laboratory model, as the telomeres in this mouse model are within the human physiological range. Lastly, this work provides a framework for understanding individual variation in bone marrow reserve capacity and highlights the importance of considering telomere status in clinical decision-making for patients at risk of hematopoietic stress.

Experimental procedures

Mice

Telomice were generated and maintained as described previously (16). The Telomice analyzed here were between F11 and F17. Wild-type C57BL/6J mice were initially purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664) to establish a breeding colony for the Kaestner lab. Control wild-type mice were derived from this “in-house” breeding colony. B6.SJL-PtprcaPepcb/BoyJ (CD45.1) used for transplantation experiments were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (RRID:IMSR_JAX:002014). CD45.1/45.2 mice used as transplant competitors were generated by crossing B6.SJL-PtprcaPepcb/BoyJ with C57BL/6J mice.

All animals were maintained on 12-h dark/light cycles with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and water. The use of animals in this study followed the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, Laboratory Animal Ordinances, and the Animal Welfare Act.

Software

Telomere length analysis was performed using version 1.1.0-beta of Telomere Analyzer. Telomere Analyzer is publicly available, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.8379362 Dan Lichtental, N.E., Yehuda Tzfati. (2023), Zenodo.

CellProfiler 4.2.8 was used to quantify nuclei and puncta in bone marrow immunofluorescence images. Full pipeline and original images attached as supplementary data file.

Bone marrow collection

Femurs and tibia were harvested from mice, their ends removed, and bone marrow cells extracted by spinning under submersion in 5% FBS/PBS (5% Fetal Bovine Serum in PBS). Cells were then filtered three times using a FACS tube with cell strainer cap (Falcon 352235) and resuspended in 5% FBS/PBS.

Flow cytometry

Peripheral blood analysis was performed at 8- and 15- to 16-week post-transplant. Approximately 75 ml of blood was collected from the retroorbital venous sinus of the mouse, treated with ACK (Ammonium-Chloride-Potassium) Lysing Buffer two times for 10 min, then washed and filtered using a 70 μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences). For analysis of 5-FU-treated mice, bone marrow cells were isolated as above, and a lineage depletion was performed by incubating cells in biotinylated B220, CD3e, CD11b, Gr1, Ter119 (eBiosciences) and streptavidin-microbeads (Miltenyi Biotech). Lineage-negative cells were run through MS columns on a MACS magnetic separator (Miltenyi Biotech).

Cells were washed with 5% FBS/PBS and resuspended in 100 ml antibody cocktail (Table S1) for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were then diluted in PBS and strained through a 70 μm filter and stained with 0.5 mg/ml DAPI in FBS. Flow cytometry was performed on a FACSAria instrument, and data were analyzed using Fowjo 9.0.1 (Tree Star, Inc). Gating strategies are shown in Figs. S1–S3v.

5-FU treatment. Seven-to nine-week-old mice were injected intraperitoneally with suspended 5-fluorouracil (150 mg/kg body weight) every 7 days for 3 weeks.

Bone marrow transplantation

B6.SJL-PtprcaPepcb/BoyJ (CD45.1) mice were administered 900 cGy of irradiation using GammaCell 40 137Cs irradiator in a split dose separated by 3 to 4 h 2 × 106 total bone marrow cells resuspended 0.2 ml 5% FBS/PBS were injected retro-orbitally while mice were under anesthesia. Mice were monitored for signs of dehydration throughout treatment and given subcutaneous injections of 0.2 ml 0.9% normal saline for relief. Mice were also maintained on antibiotic diet food containing sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim (Lab Diet, p/n 5053) for 2 weeks post-transplantation.

Metaphase spreads/telomere FISH

Bone marrow cells were collected as outlined above and were plated in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Bone marrow cells were incubated for 2 h before media was supplemented with Colcemid (0.1 μg/ml). Colcemid-treated cells were incubated for 30 min to allow dividing cells to arrest in metaphase, then cells were removed using trypsin and collected by gentle centrifugation (5 min, 100 g). Cell pellets were gradually resuspended in hypotonic media (0.075 M KCl) warmed to 37 °C. Additional hypotonic media was added until the volume was at least ten times that of the cell pellet. Samples were then incubated for 25 min at 37 °C to promote cell swelling. Following the incubation, samples were spun down and cell pellets were slowly resuspended in fixative (3:1 methanol: glacial acetic acid). Cells were washed twice and were then resuspended in fixative. These samples were then dropped from a height of 0.7 m onto a glass slide that had been pre-treated with L-proline and steamed. Slides were dried at 65 °C and subsequently stored at room temperature until hybridization.

To perform telomeric FISH hybridization, slides were first dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (70%, 95%, 100%) then air-dried. Sample areas were then covered with hybridization mix, which consisted of 14 μl deionized formamide, 2 μl blocking solution (Roche, prepared according to manufacturer recommendations), 1 μl 1M Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 μl MgCl2 buffer (82 mM Na2HPO4, 9 mM citric acid, 25 mM MgCl2), 1 μl telomere probe (Panagene F1002-5), and 1 μl molecular biology-grade water per slide. Slides were heated at 80 °C for 10 min in the dark, then hybridized at room temperature in the dark overnight. After hybridization, slides were washed twice with a 70% formamide, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 solution, then thrice with a 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.08% Tween-20 solution at room temperature. Slides were submerged in deionized water to remove excess salt and then dried in the dark at room temperature. Dry slides were coated with Vectashield + DAPI and a coverslip, followed by curing in the dark at room temperature overnight.

Nanopore sequencing of telomeres (NanoTelSeq)

DNA from bone marrow cells was extracted using the Monarch HMW DNA Extraction Kit for Cells & Blood using the manufacturer’s protocol for blood and using minimum recommended shaking speeds to generate ultra-high molecular weight DNA. This DNA was processed and sequenced on an Oxford Nanopore PromethION machine as described previously in (16, 18). Briefly, telomere ends from processed ultra-high molecular weight DNA were ligated to the telorette:teltail oligo scaffolding system (sequences above under Reagents) using 100,000 units of high-concentration T4 ligase (NEB) per microliter of reaction volume overnight at room temperature. The ligation reactions were stopped by adding 20 μM EDTA, and the samples were combined. The samples were then processed using Oxford Nanopore Technologies’ Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (SQK-NBD114.24) protocol, beginning after barcode ligation and EDTA inactivation (step 11 under 4. Native barcode ligation). Libraries were sequenced on a Nanopore R10.4.1 flow cell for PromethION using Library Beads (LIB) and following manufacturer’s recommendations. Flow cells were washed and reloaded with the sequencing library as needed for optimal throughput. Base-calling was performed with the Nanopore Guppy application in super accuracy mode. The sequences (FastQ files) were filtered and analyzed by a dedicated application termed Telomere Analyzer (16, 18). Read lengths under 10,000 bp were discarded unless the sequenced subtelomeric region was at least 1000 bp in length to reduce bias in the data caused by sequenced telomere fragments.

Immunofluorescence

Bone marrow cells from saline- or 5FU-treated WT mice or Telomice were collected as described above. Cell resuspensions were pipetted onto Superfrost Plus Adhesion Microscope Slides and dried at 37 °C for 1 h before being fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Slides were rinsed with PBS then dehydrated using a graded ethanol series and stored in 100% ethanol at −20 °C. For staining, slides were rehydrated using a graded ethanol series then permeabilized using 0.2% Triton-X. Heat-based antigen retrieval was performed in citrate (pH 6.0) buffer. Slides were blocked in Cas block for 1 h then incubated overnight at 4 °C with c-Kit (eBioscience 14-1171-85), γH2AX (Sigma-Aldrich 05-636-I), and cleaved caspase 3 (Cell Signaling Technology 9664) primary antibodies (detailed antibody information in Table S1). The next day, slides were washed with PBS and blocked again for 1 h using Cas block. Slides were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature then washed with PBS. Vectashield with DAPI was used to mount glass coverslips and stain nuclei. Images were taken on a Keyence microscope and processed using Cellprofiler, as described under Software.
