Nipah virus infects the brain and triggers neuronal and glial damage in a hamster model
Authors: Karl Valerdi, Takeshi Saito, Ruchi Paroha, Kirsten Littlefield, Jessica Plante, Kenneth Plante, Silvana Valdebenito, Junki Maruyama, Eliseo Eugenin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nipah virus (NiV) is distributed in South-East Asia and the Western Pacific, with currently active outbreak sites. NiV causes an acute febrile illness with high mortality (40–70%), encephalitis, and long-term disability among survivors. Currently, there are no effective therapeutic or preventive interventions, and its potential for explosive spread resulted in its designation as a pathogen of international epidemiological concern. However, the pathophysiology of NiV-induced encephalitis remains unclear. Using a hamster model of NiV infection, we demonstrated that NiV targets several brain areas, such as the cortex and the hippocampus, infecting neurons, astrocytes, and blood-brain barrier cells. NiV infection also induced glial activation, including both microglia and astrocytes, leading to vascular and neuronal compromise and parenchymal lesions. Overall, NiV demonstrates a marked tropism for the central nervous system, resulting in major damage and death.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03901-4.

Introduction

The Nipah virus (NiV) is a highly virulent pathogen that poses an urgent and ongoing threat to public health due to its recently accelerated geographical spread, which has the potential to cause mass outbreaks. Further enhancing an urgent attention for proper diagnostic, treatment, or vaccination programs (see details in https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/nipah-virus). Clinical features range from mild respiratory distress to seizures, coma, and severe encephalitis, reaching a fatality rate of 40–70% and long-term sequelae in surviving individuals [1–4].

Human autopsy studies from NiV-infected individuals show viral antigen detection in most tissues, including the central nervous system (CNS), with strong evidence of endothelial and smooth muscle infection resulting in vascular compromise, thrombosis, ischemia, and microinfarctation [5–7]. Also, neuronal infection and cell death have been described in association with ischemia and microinfarction, suggesting that oxygen deprivation and thrombosis are key components of the CNS-related symptoms observed in most of the infected individuals [8].

NiV-infected survivors develop long-term neurological consequences such as cerebral atrophy, paralysis, weakness, dystonia, negative changes in behavior, and even recurring encephalitis years after primary infection [9, 10]. However, the mechanisms of long-term damage triggered by NiV infection are not fully understood, and here animal studies are essential to address this gap in knowledge. Currently, NiV infection models have been developed in foxes, pigs, ferrets, monkeys, cats, mice, and others, most with limited or no encephalitis [11–14]. In contrast, the golden Syrian hamster model develops respiratory and neurological disease even with low viral doses, with similar histopathology to humans, including systemic vasculitis [12, 13], but a deep characterization of CNS invasion and compromise has not been performed. The three main limitations of all studies, human and animal models, are: first, human data had significant time after death and limited numbers of cases [1, 5, 15]; second, as described, the variability of the animal models and their representation of the human disease, including brain compromise [11–13, 15–18]; third, due to the Nipah biosafety classification as a Biosafety Level 4 (BSL4) pathogen, the protocols of inactivation are extreme to prevent any accidental release, mostly extended periods in formalin [19]. These protocols compromise most protein antigens, resulting in the limited detection of abundant antigens by immunohistochemistry [12, 20–23]. Our laboratory has developed techniques to uncrosslink and recover weak antigens such as neuronal and glial markers, and even perform 3D reconstructions of the tissue analyzed [24, 25].

Here, we demonstrate that NiV peripheral infection in hamsters results in a deadly disease with significant systemic and central nervous system (CNS) complications, including paralysis. Our analysis mainly focused on the cortex, the hippocampus, and the dentate gyrus (DG), due to the significant accumulation of NiV antigens and their correlation with cognitive and motor compromise observed in survivors [8, 10, 26]. Presence of the NiV nucleoprotein (N) in neurons was observed in specific areas of the hippocampus and cortex, with significant differences in viral antigen accumulation according to the area analyzed. NiV N was also detected in lower amounts in microglia/macrophages and around blood vessels (astrocytes and blood-brain barrier, BBB, cells), supporting BBB compromise. The NiV brain infection resulted in neuronal loss, axonal/dendritic reduction, and glial activation (microglia/astrocytes)- all critical components of cognitive and motor decline as observed in people who perished as well as survivors of NiV infection.

Materials and methods

Institutional review board statement

Experiments involving NiV were conducted in BSL-4 containment at the Galveston National Laboratories (GNL), Galveston, Texas, US. Animal studies were conducted in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the UTMB Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (2401009). The GNL is fully accredited by AAALAC International.

Cells and viruses

African green monkey kidney Vero cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Corning, Corning, NY) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. NiV 199,902,916 Malaysia was received from the World Reference Center for Emerging Viruses and Arboviruses at the University of Texas Medical Branch (UTMB) and propagated in Vero cells with DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. Cell culture supernatants were stored at − 80 °C until use. All work with infectious NiV was approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee at UTMB and performed in BSL-4 facilities at UTMB in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Animal model

Six- to seven-week-old female Syrian golden hamsters were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. All animals were housed in animal biosafety level 2 (ABSL-2) and ABSL-4 facilities at the Galveston National Laboratory (GNL) at UTMB, Galveston, Texas. Body weights were measured using an Ohaus CX1201 portable scale, and animal identification was performed using subcutaneously implanted BMDS IPTT-1000 transponders and a DAS-8037 transponder reader (Avidity Science, Waterford, WI). Ten hamsters were intraperitoneally inoculated with 104 PFU of NiV diluted in 100 µL of sterile PBS. Another n = 10 hamster with equal characteristic were used as controls. The body weight and temperature (data not shown) of animals were measured daily after the virus infection throughout the study for 8 days. Animals were humanely euthanized once they showed neurological symptoms such as paralysis or were unable to access their food or water, and/or had lost more than 20% of their body weight, based on their baseline measurement at the time of virus inoculation. A subset of three of the ten animals were euthanized at 5 days post-infection (dpi) for scheduled sample collection. Blood, serum, and organs (liver, spleen, kidney, lung, and brain) were collected at scheduled sampling or at the time of euthanasia. Clinical scoring was performed using the following criteria: 0 = normal; 1 = one clinical sign of the disease; 2 = two or more clinical signs of the disease; 3 = a score of two plus one additional clinical sign and/or > 15% weight loss; 4 = > 20% weight loss, labored breathing, partial paralysis, paralysis, or bleeding. Animals that met euthanasia criteria or died naturally were both assigned a final score of 4.

Virus titration

Virus titers were determined through plaque assay. Tissue samples collected from NiV-infected hamsters were homogenized with DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. Confluent monolayers of Vero cells in 12-well plates were inoculated with 100 µL of 10-fold serially diluted cell culture supernatants of NiV, tissue homogenates, or serum samples, and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After removing the inoculum, each well was overlaid with a minimum essential medium containing 2% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 0.6% tragacanth (Sigma, St Louis, MO). Following a 3-day incubation, cells were fixed with 10% formalin and stained with crystal violet. Viral titers were represented as plaque-forming units (PFU).

Complete blood count

Whole-blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture at 5 dpi or the time of euthanasia from hamsters infected with NiV. Samples were drawn into EDTA tubes (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ), and complete blood count analysis was performed using the VETSCAN HM5 (Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ) following the manufacture’s instructions.

Immunostaining

The whole brain and lumbar spinal cord were fixed in formalin for viral inactivation as required [27]. Tissues remain in formalin for several months. To recover the antigens, especially the neuronal and glial, we designed and published [24, 28]. The whole brain was sectioned into 10 μm serial sections. Antibodies correspond to Nipah virus against nucleocapsid monoclonal (MA5-47044, Thermofisher), anti-Glial Fibrillar Acid Protein (GFAP) Polyclonal Antibody (PA1-10004, Invitrogen), anti-Laminin Polyclonal Antibody, Biotin (PA5-22900, Invitrogen), anti-Iba1 antibody - Microglia marker (ab5076, Abcam), and anti- microtubule-associated protein-2 (MAP-2) chicken (ab5543, Millipore). For secondaries, Goat anti-Chicken IgY (H + L) Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 647 (A21449, Invitrogen), Invitrogen™ Streptavidin, Alexa Fluor™ 647 Conjugate (S32357, Invitrogen), Donkey anti-Chicken IgY (H + L) Highly Cross Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 568 (A78950, Invitrogen), Donkey anti-Goat IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 546 (A11056, Invitrogen), Donkey anti-Chicken IgY (H + L) Highly Cross Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 647 (A78952, Invitrogen), and Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (A21206, invitrogen). We selected Nipah nucleoprotein due to its interaction with the Viral RNA, MAP-2, due to its expression in dendrites, playing a key role in dendrite synaptic plasticity as well as axonal transport [29], GFAP, due to its expression in astroglia and in the end feet of the blood-brain barrier [30], and laminin, due to its localization at the BBB [31]. For immunofluoresce and confocal analysis, the entire brain was analyzed, and each animal was examined.

Confocal microscopy imaging

Formalin-fixed brain and spinal cords harvested from Syrian hamsters were paraffin-embedded and sectioned at 10 μm onto glass slides at the Albert Einstein College of Medicine’s department of Comparative Histology, then subjected to ethanol-based rehydration prior to permeabilization. The sections were sequentially incubated in graded ethanol (30%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 90%, 95%) for 5 min each, followed by two back-to-back incubations in 100% xylene (10 min each), rehydration in water, then a reverse ethanol series back to 30%. The sections were then washed once with PBS for 5 min, followed by 10 min of incubation in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. The sections were then washed 3 times in PBS for 5 min each. The sections were then incubated in preheated reversal buffer for 30 min (SDS, 0.175 M; 0.5 EDTA pH = 8.0, 2.0 mM; Tris base, 0.5 M, and NaCl, 0.15 M), then transferred to preheated antigen retrieval buffer (Sodium Citrate dihydrate, 10 mM and Immuno Saver, 1:200 dilution) for an additional 30 min at 95 °C, followed by a 20 min cool down period. The sections were then blocked in super blocking buffer (SBS) for 16 h at 4 °C. Primary antibodies were diluted in SBS and applied to the sections for 2 h at room temperature, followed by 16 h at 4 °C, followed by 4 h at room temperature. The sections were then washed 5 times in PBS, 5 min each, and species-specific Alexa Fluor conjugated antibodies diluted in SBS were applied to the sections, and incubated for 2 h. After secondary incubation, sections were washed 5 times for 5 min each, and then incubated in 0.5 µM DAPI in PBS, rocking for 10 min. The samples were then mounted with Invitrogen ProLong™ Gold Antifade reagent for imaging. Fluorescent micrographs were taken with a Nikon A1 confocal with spectral detection (Nikon, Japan) with 15–20 optical slides to perform 3D deconstruction and deconvolution.

Data acquisition and analysis

Three-dimensional reconstruction and deconvolution of regions of interest (ROI) were performed from 15 to 20 optical sections obtained at 0.25–0.55 μm intervals in large pieces of tissue. Upon identifying NiV antigens in the whole brain, the tissue coordinates are selected and rescreened at better resolution, 12 to 25 optical sections obtained at 0.250–0.10 μm intervals. However, we have to note that most of the brain tissue was negative for NiV antigens. Thus, the figures presented are the selected positive areas, especially in the hippocampus, cortex, and D.G. To analyze and quantify the abundance and colocalization of the used antibodies, the number of positive pixels and their intensity in microglia/macrophages, neurons, and astrocytes as well as BBB dells were measured in specific ROI, as well as the Pearson’s and Mander’s colocalization index. Colocalization and numbers of positive pixels for each color were quantified using NIS Elements’s imaging software (Nikon, Japan).

Statistics and reproducibility

All data were analyzed and graphically represented using Origin Lab (version 2022) and/or GraphPad Prism (v9.4.1). All results were reported as mean ± S.D., except Fig. 1, mean ± S.E.M. Comparisons between groups were performed using ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test as post hoc analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05 after Bonferroni correction. Each animal was considered a different n, with 4–5 serial sections analyzed to obtain a mean ± S.D. for each animal. Each experiment analyzed n = 10 different animals. Pilot experiments and subsequent statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size; no data were excluded from analysis. Calculations were performed by our Department of Biostatistics at UTMB. Finally, the data were blinded, acquired, and analyzed.

Results

Pathogenicity of NiV in hamsters

Ten Syrian golden hamsters were inoculated intraperitoneally with 104 PFU of NiV and monitored for signs of illness and body weight change (Fig. 1A and B). All hamsters exhibited weight loss at 4–8 dpi. At 5 dpi, 1 of 10 hamsters had died, whereas the remaining animals showed no additional clinical signs. At 6 dpi, an additional animal died. Among the six animals still under observation, three exhibited a ruffled coat and labored breathing, with one of them also developing partial paralysis and was humanely euthanized (Fig. 1B). At 7 dpi, three of the four remaining animals presented with a scruffy coat, labored breathing, and partial paralysis and were subsequently euthanized (Fig. 1B). At 8 dpi, the final surviving animal developed labored breathing but did not show neurological symptoms and was euthanized. Among the euthanized animals, neurological symptoms characterized by partial paralysis and tremors were observed in 4 of 5 animals, representing 80%.

Viral dissemination analysis revealed widespread NiV in animals sampled at 5 dpi, with detectable virus across all major organs. Although viral titer in the liver was limited (2.16 log10 PFU/g), the spleen and lungs contained significant titers of 3.01 log10 PFU/g and 2.96 log10 PFU/g, respectively (Fig. 1C). Infectious virus was also detected in the brain and serum at mean levels of 2.51 log10 PFU/g and 1.23 log10 PFU/mL, respectively, (Fig. 1C). In contrast, in animals sampled at the time of euthanasia (EU), little to no infectious virus was detected in the liver, spleen, lungs, or serum; however, the kidneys and brains of these animals consistently harbored infectious virus at mean titers of 2.77 log10 PFU/g and 2.06 log10 PFU/g, respectively, supporting active replication (Fig. 1C).

NiV accumulates in the hippocampus and cortex

The consensus of the field based on post-mortem tissues indicates that the major target of NiV infection is neurons [8, 9, 11, 13]; however, the data were mostly obtained in a limited number of post-mortem tissues [5, 6, 9, 32]. Here, using serial coronal brain sections of uninfected and Nipah-infected animals, staining for nuclei (DAPI), NiV nucleoprotein (N), neurons (MAP-2, a neuronal marker), and microglia/macrophages (ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1, Iba-1) was performed and analyzed by confocal microscopy and 3D reconstructions. We focused on Sect. 29/30/31/32/33 (Bregma ∼2.3 mm as described in A stereotaxic Atlas of the Golden hamster Brain, Academic Press), due to the main localization of N protein staining. No significant differences between animals sacrificed at days 5–8 were identified; thus, we pooled the data according to the comparison of the analysis of the n = 10 animals per group.

The analysis of uninfected animals shows no signal for Nipah N antigen, as expected (Fig. 1A, Control). The distribution of MAP-2 positive cells, neurons, was in the cell body of the neurons in the Cornus Ammonis 1 to 3 (CA1-3, see DAPI positive layers, labeled Hippocampus), as well as axons and dendrites extending below this cellular layer (see Fig. 1A, control, MAP-2, a white arrow denotes them). Iba-1 positive cells, microglia/macrophages, are widely distributed without processes as expected in control conditions (Fig. 1A, Iba-1).

In contrast, the animals that were infected with NiV, by an intraperitoneal route, show extensive but localized NiV N staining. Not all neurons were infected, and there was a significant difference in NiV N detection depending on the brain area analyzed (Fig. 2B, Nipah, representative 3D reconstructions of hippocampal infected areas). Quantification of the positive cells for NiV N indicates that in the hippocampus, CA1 neurons are more susceptible to infection or subsequent replication than neurons in CA2 and CA3 hippocampal regions (Fig. 2C). Also, we observed “patchy” but consistent infection in neurons in the dentate gyrus (DG) and the cortex (frontal and occipital) of all animals (Fig. 2C).

MAP-2 is a neuronal marker, but more importantly is a neuronal-specific protein that stabilizes microtubules in the dendrites, synapses, and axons [29]. Quantification of MAP-2 total pixels per area indicates that NiV infection reduced the total amount of MAP-2 in the hippocampus, D.G., and cortex, supporting a strong neuronal function dysregulation (Fig. 2D, *p<0.05 compared to uninfected brains). Also, areas with neuronal infection show fewer neuronal extensions, axonal or dendrites (Fig. 2B compared to Fig. 2A. See arrows for axons, *p<0.05. See Supplemental Fig. 1 for lower magnifications). Also, the use of Nikon Spatial Array Confocal (NSPARC) for higher resolution shows a significant microglia activation (Iba-1 staining, Supplemental Fig. 2) and neuronal infection, as well as the presence of NiV antigens in their processes (MAP-2 staining, Supplemental Fig. 2).

The analysis of the hamster brains identified that 20–35% of Iba-1 positive cells, microglia/macrophages, were positive to NiV N (Fig. 2E, *p<0.05 compared to 6 uninfected brains). But also, microglia/macrophage activation, measured by elevated expression and increased numbers of Iba-1 positive cells, was elevated due to NiV infection (Fig. 2A, control, *p<0.05 compared to 2B, NiV-infected brains). Quantification of Iba-1 positive pixels indicates a significant increase in Iba-1 staining, supporting immune activation in areas containing NiV antigens (Fig. 2F, p<0.05 compared to uninfected brains, and Supplemental Fig. 2). These data indicate that NiV not only targets neurons but also a small population of activated macrophage/microglia, probably contributing to the reduction in MAP-2 expression in the hippocampus and the cortex.

NiV infection induces low levels of astrocyte infection and NiV N accumulation at the BBB

To identify whether NiV targets astrocytes and the Blood Brain Barrier (BBB), staining for NiV N, astrocytes (GFAP-positive cells), and laminin (to label the Blood-brain barrier) was utilized. In uninfected animals, GFAP and laminin expression and distribution were as expected, mainly in astrocyte-like cells and around the BBB (Fig. 3A, Control). In contrast, in NiV-infected animals, a small population of astrocytes and the BBB cells were positive for NiV N (Fig. 3B, Nipah). The use of NSPARC microscopy, to enhance resolution and detection, also showed a significant degree of infection (NiV) and increased GFAP staining or gliosis compared to control uninfected conditions (Supplemental Fig. 3, control and Nipah). Overall, the frequency of astrocytes that express N proteins was spatially dependent, with higher rates of infection in the Hippocampus (Fig. 3C, *p<0.05 compared to uninfected). No astrocyte infection was detected in the D.G. (Fig. 3C, D.G.). Quantification of the positive pixels for GFAP staining indicates that the degree of infection (Fig. 3C) is associated with an increase in GFAP expression (or gliosis), in the Hippocampus and the cortex (Fig. 3D, *p<0.05 compared to uninfected).

Laminin (α4β1γ1 or α5β1γ1) expression in uninfected conditions was highly localized in long structures or blood vessels (Fig. 3A, control). In NiV-infected animals, laminin staining was disorganized, especially in the hippocampus and cortex (Fig. 3B, Nipah). Quantification of NiV N within laminin-positive cells indicates, like astrocytes, a regional specificity in blood vessels of the hippocampus and the Cortex, but no infection was detected in the D.G. (Fig. 3E, *p<0.05 compared to uninfected). In contrast, the quantification of laminin-positive pixels in NiV-infected brains indicates an increase in the hippocampus and cortex compared to uninfected brains (Fig. 3F, *p<0.05 compared to uninfected brains). The increase in laminin-positive pixels was not correlated with blood vessel-like structures; instead, expression was disorganized (Fig. 3F, *p<0.05 compared to uninfected). A similar assessment was observed using NSPARC microscopy, indicating the loss and fragmentation of the laminin layer at the BBB and accumulation of the N NiV staining within the blood vessels (Supplemental Fig. 3, compare control versus Nipah). The dysregulation of the BBB was associated with NiV infection, due to the fact that no significant changes in laminin were observed in the D.G. (Fig. 3F). Overall, NiV infects a small population of astrocytes and BBB cells, resulting in hippocampal and cortex compromise.

NiV infection results in inflammatory lesions and lose of synaptic integrity

A critical point in NiV-infected individuals and animal models is the development of different brain lesions [5, 6, 9]. To determine alterations in neuronal, microglia/macrophage, and astrocyte markers, as well as changes in the brain structure, staining for Iba-1, MAP-2, GFAP, and laminin was performed, as well as 3D reconstruction/deconvulution to identify lesions as described in Fig. 4. Discussion

Here, we further demonstrated that peripheral NiV infection is devastating within the CNS, including the infection of multiple brain areas and cell types, leading to glial and neuronal compromise, microglia/astrocyte activation, and lesion formation. The hamster model replicated several aspects of the human disease, including its deadly nature, encephalitis, and the potential mechanisms of long-term damage observed in survivors [1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 15, 20, 23, 32]. Acute NiV-mediated encephalitis has been associated with ischemia, microinfarction, and neuronal infection. All histological human studies correspond to a few, but important, samples with extended post-mortem time, compromising the interpretation of some of the findings [5, 33–35]. Most of the CNS damage and high mortality are associated with high replication, including in the CSF and neuronal-related necrosis [1, 2, 4, 9, 13]. Our study in hamsters allows us to examine earlier time points that are not available in studies involving humans, addressing the critical point of early damage. In agrement of ealy damage, we did not observe any difference in infectivity and microglia/astrocyte activation or neuronal infection between hamsters sacrificed after 5 or 8 days, suggesting that this phenotype is developed early on. Here, we observed several overlapping mechanisms of damage; first, neuronal infection and lesion formation, with significant axonal and dendrite decrease, further contributing to the cognitive impairment observed in NiV-infected individuals; Second, limited glial infection, including astrocytes in the parenchyma and at the BBB; third, limited infection and immune activation in microglia/macrophages; and lastly, BBB and microinfartation in a spatial manner. Thus, pathology induced by NiV infection is different depending on the area analyzed. However, human data [1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 23, 32, 36] and our hamster model resulted in significant neuronal/glial damage that certainly will remain for long periods if not resolved or for the life of the individual, including tissue destruction and blood vessel occlusion resulting in mini-strokes. Currently, two routes of brain NIV infection have been proposed in humans: a hematologic route and a retrograde route through the olfactory nerve [15, 16, 18], both associated with BBB destruction and expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α that further contribute to neuronal/glial compromise [37]. Our data show that NiV infection resulted in intravascular coagulation and gliosis, which is in agreement with the literature that at least 40% of all small blood vessels in humans and animal models are compromised [5, 17, 38, 39]. Both conditions are well described as major contributors to neurodegeneration and stroke-related cognitive decline [40, 41].

We have to note that our antigen uncrosslinking and recovery enable us to examine large pieces of tissue, as no one has before, performing immunohistochemistry, in BSL4 formalin-fixed tissues, recovering lost neuronal and viral antigenicity, as well as enhancing GFAP and Iba-1 detection. The level of detection by confocal and NSPARC is extraordinary and provided the tools to develop this manuscript, especially to detect low levels of viral antigens, but also to examine their distribution and, more importantly, their spatial contribution to generate lesions as described in Fig. 4 and Supplemental Fig. 4. These lesions corresponds not only to altered staining, but also to aberrant 3D structures within the brains of the infected animals. The use of these techniques in human tissues could provide the breakthroughs to prevent or treat the devastating consequences of NiV.

Further, NiV infection was directly correlated with lower amounts of MAP-2, suggesting neuronal structural and functional compromise. In contrast, microglia activation was evenly distributed in CA1, CA2, CA3, D.G., and the cortex, suggesting that microglia/macrophage activation was systemic. In contrast, astrocyte and BBB infection and activation were spatially distributed with higher infection in the hippocampus (no differences in the area analyzed), and the cortex, but interestingly, no infection of astrocytes and BBB cells was detected in the D.G, again suggesting that NiV infection is specific for some areas of the brain. Unfortunately, autopsy data are extremely limited or unavailable, and most data come from non-human primates [1, 17, 35, 36, 38]. However, in these models, brain lesions are well described. The loss of MAP-2 and increase in GFAP and Iba-1 staining clearly indicate a compromised neuronal communication and an amplified inflammation resulting in gliosis and microglia activation as observed in multiple neurodegenerative diseases [42–46]. Our data and others indicates the necessity to examine the brain tropism of Nipah and the mechanisms of brain invasion and infection to prevent death or lifelong damage.

Our data shows the evolution of brain damage triggered by peripheral NiV infection, including critical systemic features, such as the specific organ viral clearing, coagulation issues, neuronal/astrocytes and microglia infection/activation, synaptic/axonal compromise, and lesion formation. NiV is an aggressive virus that targets the CNS, resulting in high mortality and long-term damage. More concerning is the ongoing outbreaks and geographical expansion of NiV, which reveals the urgency and necessity of research on these emerging viruses to mitigate NiV infection and deaths worldwide.
