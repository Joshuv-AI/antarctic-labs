The adjoint state method for parametric definable optimization without smoothness or uniqueness
Jérôme BolteEdouard Pauwels, Cheik Traoré

Abstract
Definable parametric optimization problems with possibly nonsmooth objectives, inequality constraints, and non-unique primal and dual solutions admit an adjoint state formula under a mere qualification condition. The adjoint construction yields a selection of a conservative field for the value function, providing a computable first-order object without requiring differentiation of the solution mapping. Through examples, we show that even in smooth problems, the formal adjoint construction fails without conservativity or definability, illustrating the relevance of these concepts to grasp theoretical aspects of the method. This work provides a tool which can be directly combined with existing primal-dual solvers for a wide range of parametric optimization problems.



Key words and phrases.

Adjoint state method, conservative fields, nonsmooth optimization, o-minimal geometry, parametric optimization, variational analysis.

Introduction

Parametric optimization and the adjoint method.

Parametric optimization problems are central in optimization
[
9
]
, control
[
31
]
, and engineering
[
10
]
. They also arise naturally in modern machine learning, where one seeks to tune hyperparameters, architectures, regularization levels, or embedded optimization layers through the optimization of an induced value function; see
[
35
,
4
]
. In these situations, the quantity of interest often has the parametric form

\displaystyle f(\theta)\coloneq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{F(x,\theta):G(x,\theta)\leq 0,H(x,\theta)=0\right\},

(1.1)

where the parameter
\theta
ranges in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz, the constraint functions
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
are continuously differentiable, and the inequality is understood coordinate-wise. A central difficulty in optimizing the value function
f
lies in obtaining useful first-order information at a reasonable computational cost. In principle, if one denotes by

\displaystyle x^{*}(\theta)\in\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{F(x,\theta):G(x,\theta)\leq 0,H(x,\theta)=0\right\},

a selection of minimizers, the value writes

f(\theta)=F(x^{*}(\theta),\theta)
, thus,
if the mapping
\theta\mapsto x^{*}(\theta)
were differentiable, the chain rule would give

\nabla f(\theta)=\nabla_{\!x}F(x^{*}(\theta),\theta)^{\!\mathsf{T}}\frac{dx^{*}(\theta)}{d\theta}+\nabla_{\!\theta}F(x^{*}(\theta),\theta).

However, this approach raises both computational and conceptual difficulties. On the computational side, differentiating through a solver is generally expensive, while conceptually it relies on strong regularity assumptions, typically requiring an application of the implicit function theorem, which is often restrictive in practice.

The adjoint state method, introduced by Céa
[
11
]
, provides a way to bypass the computation of the derivative of the solution mapping. It also avoids relying on the duality framework typically associated with convexity assumptions
[
37
,
21
]
. Denoting
(x^{*}(\theta),\lambda^{*}(\theta),\mu^{*}(\theta))
a solution of the KKT system of (
1.1
), the adjoint formula writes (see
[
11
]
for a formal derivation under equality constraints)

\nabla f(\theta)=\nabla_{\!\theta}F(x^{*}(\theta),\theta)+\jac_{\theta}\!G(x^{*}(\theta),\theta)^{\!\mathsf{T}}\lambda^{*}(\theta)+\jac_{\theta}\!H(x^{*}(\theta),\theta)^{\!\mathsf{T}}\mu^{*}(\theta).

(1.2)

Remarkably, at a negligible additional cost—see Remark
2.8
()
—associated with computing Lagrange multipliers, this formula does not involve the derivative of the primal-dual solution mapping. Yet, the theoretical validity of the analysis of Céa requires solutions of the KKT system to be unique and differentiable.

The adjoint formula in the definable world.

The objective of this work is to analyze the adjoint formula, under simple and versatile assumptions, that applies to a broad class of nonsmooth optimization problems with inequality constraints and possibly nonsmooth or non-unique primal and dual solutions. To this end, we show that the expression (
1.2
) carries strong variational information whenever the Mangasarian–Fromovitz constraint qualification holds and all the mappings
F,G,H
belong to a common o-minimal structure (for example, are semialgebraic). We also discuss the computational cost of our approach, comparing it with less sophisticated methods and connecting it to automatic differentiation.

Our approach relies on two structural ingredients: definability and conservative calculus. Definability provides a natural framework that encompasses most optimization and machine learning models
[
8
,
15
]
, while offering powerful stability and regularity properties that are essential for our analysis. Conservative calculus, on the other hand, supplies generalized differential objects suited to nonsmooth calculus and has already led to strong applications in optimization and learning; see
[
8
,
15
,
34
]
. The use of conservative fields for the value function is essential here: the formal application of the adjoint formula in (
1.2
) produces artifacts, even for smooth data
F,G,H
resulting in a smooth value function
f
, see the very simple Example
2.7
()
. This is very common in nonsmooth differential calculus. Nonetheless, the outputs of the adjoint formula (
1.2
) form a conservative field, in particular, artifacts only have a minor impact. We also point out that the natural connections between conservativity, differentiable programming, and algorithmic differentiation make our approach amenable to modern computational frameworks
[
7
,
8
]
.

Although primarily theoretical, this work provides a rigorous foundation for the practical use of the adjoint state method in settings where smoothness or uniqueness of solutions cannot be guaranteed. Such situations arise in a wide range of applications, including robust optimization
[
5
]
, uncertainty quantification
[
1
]
, optimal design of structures and systems
[
1
]
, parametric decomposition
[
24
,
20
]
, and discretized neural ODEs
[
12
]
. A detailed investigation of these applications is beyond the scope of the present paper.

To conclude, we emphasize that the definable assumption is quite sharp. Indeed, in the absence of definability, the adjoint formula may fail to carry variational meaning for the value function, even for locally Lipschitz, path differentiable and (point-wise) differentiable objective function with smooth constraints. We provide an example showing that some structural assumption is required, justifying the o-minimal (definable) framework to ensure the validity of the adjoint formula. This framework is broad enough to cover most models that arise in applications. For example, most relevant neural networks are definable in some o-minimal structure
[
8
,
15
]
. Beyond machine learning, many engineering and numerical models, when discretized, involve solely polynomials or definable functions.

Related work.

Let us briefly describe some related literature. In the continuous, infinite dimensional setting, extensions of the adjoint method to nonsmooth or non-unique solutions can be found in

In nonlinear programming, related sensitivity results based on Lagrange multipliers likewise provide only upper and lower bounds on directional derivatives and entail similar optimization procedures over both primal and dual solution sets; see the work of Gauvin

established that the Clarke subdifferential of the value function is included in a convex set constructed with the subdifferential of the Lagrangian; see the discussion in Section

, this inclusion is strict in general, further confirming that subdifferentials are not the best paradigms to analyze the adjoint state method with multiple solutions. A limitation that this paper overcomes through conservative gradients.

2
The adjoint state method: smooth case

This section studies the adjoint state method in the setting where the objective function
F
is continuously differentiable, in the presence of equality and inequality constraints and possibly a continuum of minimizers. We focus here on the smooth case for simplicity and will expand on nonsmooth objectives in Section
4
.

2.1
Preliminaries

We consider the following parametric nonlinear programming problem:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \minimize_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad&F(x,\theta)\\
\mathrm{subject\text{ }to}\quad&G(x,\theta)\leq 0,\\
&H(x,\theta)=0,\end{aligned}

(2.1)

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
. The objective function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz, and the constraint mappings

G(x,\theta)=\big(g_{1}(x,\theta),\ldots,g_{m}(x,\theta)\big)^{\!\mathsf{T}},\qquad H(x,\theta)=\big(h_{1}(x,\theta),\ldots,h_{p}(x,\theta)\big)^{\!\mathsf{T}}

are continuously differentiable, the inequality being understood coordinate-wise. For any
(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
, let
I(x,\theta)\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}
be the set of active (inequality) constraints, i.e., for all
i\in I(x,\theta)
,
g_{i}(x,\theta)=0
. The
Lagrangian
of Problem (
2.1
),
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
, writes

\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\mu,\theta)\coloneq F(x,\theta)+\lambda^{\!\mathsf{T}}G(x,\theta)+\mu^{\!\mathsf{T}}H(x,\theta).

Definition 2.1

(
Mangasarian-Fromovitz constraint qualification
)
.

Let
(x,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that
G(x,\theta)\leq 0,H(x,\theta)=0
. We say that the
MFCQ holds
at
(x,\theta)
, if the set

\left\{\nabla_{\!x}h_{j}(x,\theta),j=1,\ldots,p\right\}

is linearly independent and there exists a direction
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\nabla_{\!x}g_{i}(x,\theta)^{\!\mathsf{T}}d<0\text{ for all }i\in I(x,\theta)\text{ and }\jac_{x}\!H(x,\theta)d=0.

The MFCQ has an equivalent dual formulation. Indeed, the MFCQ is satisfied at
(x,\theta)
if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I(x,\theta)}\lambda_{i}\nabla_{\!x}g_{i}(x,\theta)+\jac_{x}\!H(x,\theta)^{\!\mathsf{T}}\mu=0,\text{ and }\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\forall\,i\in I(x,\theta)

\displaystyle\implies\mu=0,\lambda_{i}=0,\forall\,i\in I(x,\theta).

(2.2)

Remark 2.2
.

If the MFCQ is satisfied at
(x,\theta)
, then the following holds for the joint Jacobians in
x,\theta
,

\displaystyle\jac\!G(x,\theta)^{\!\mathsf{T}}\lambda+\jac\!H(x,\theta)^{\!\mathsf{T}}\mu=0,\text{ and }\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\forall\,i\in I(x,\theta)

\displaystyle\implies\mu=0,\lambda_{i}=0,\forall\,i\in I(x,\theta).

(2.3)

In other words, the MFCQ holds taking into account the joint dependence in
x,\theta
, not just the partial dependence in
x

Remark 2.5

(On conservative fields)
.

The result in Theorem

()
allows to invoke generic properties of conservative fields. Among them, let us highlight the following.

(i)

Conservative fields represent a notion of generalized gradients
[
8
]
. Theorem
2.4
()
provides a rigorous mathematical framework and analysis that justify the use of the adjoint state method without any uniqueness or differentiability assumptions on the primal/state and dual/adjoint solutions. The standard notions of subdifferential from nonsmooth analysis

are insufficient to describe the variational properties of the adjoint method under those conditions; see Example

. This justify the relevance of the notion of conservative field in this context.

(ii)

For general locally Lipschitz semialgebraic functions, like

, selections of conservative fields can be used for optimization. They carry sufficient variational information to serve as oracle for first-order methods with qualitative guaranties; see
[

,
42
]
. This fact enables, for instance, parameter optimization for (

at each iteration.

(iii)

Conservative fields are compatible with differentiable programming paradigms such as algorithmic differentiation

,
7
]
. This means that, in practice,

()
can be combined with further operations in a larger numerical program, in a way which is compatible with algorithmic differentiation as implemented for example in Python libraries such as Pytorch.

(iv)

The combination of

This is reminiscent of the sensitivity analysis performed, for example, in

2.3
Comparison to alternative methods

Throughout this section
\bar{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
denotes a fixed set of parameters. We compare
ASM
()
with the following two widely used approaches that are based on differentiating the solution to get the sensitivities of
f
. The first approach, which we will simply call
automatic differentiation
, uses reverse-mode algorithmic differentiation to obtain the derivative of the solution
x(\bar{\theta})
by differentiating through
\mathrm{solver_{p}}
. The second is
implicit differentiation
, where the implicit function theorem is applied to the critical condition
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}(\bar{\theta}))=0
, with
\mathcal{L}_{1}\colon(x,\lambda,\mu)\mapsto\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\mu,\bar{\theta})
where
(x,\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\bar{\theta}
is fixed.

In terms of assumptions, it is immediately apparent that both methods require the uniqueness and differentiability of the solutions, while
ASM
()
does not. For the purpose of comparing computational costs, let us consider the favorable settings ensuring the validity of both methods.

A straightforward way to implement
ASM
()
, after getting a primal-dual solution from
\mathrm{solver_{pd}}
, is for the user to just compute
u=\texttt{autodiff}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\bar{\theta})
, with
\mathcal{L}_{2}\colon\theta\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}(\bar{\theta}),\theta)
, where
\bar{\theta}
is fixed, treated as a constant in the differentiation process. We use the notation
Cost
to denote the computational cost of implementing operations, which can be identified with computational time. According to the cheap gradient principle
[
26
]
,

\text{Cost}\big(\texttt{autodiff}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\bar{\theta})\big)\leq\omega\text{Cost}\big({\mathcal{L}_{2}}\big)=\omega\text{Cost}\big(\mathcal{L}\big),

where
\omega>0
is an absolute constant,
\text{Cost}\big(\texttt{autodiff}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\bar{\theta})\big)
is the computational cost to calculate the derivative of
\mathcal{L}_{2}
through reverse-mode automatic differentiation, and
\text{Cost}\big(\mathcal{L}_{2}\big)
and
\text{Cost}\big(\mathcal{L}\big)
the computational cost to evaluate the value of the functions
\mathcal{L}_{2}
and
\mathcal{L}
, respectively. Therefore, the total cost of the adjoint state method
ASM
()
is upper bounded as follows:

\text{Cost}\big(\text{ASM}\big)\leq\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}\big)+\omega\text{Cost}\big(\mathcal{L}\big).

(2.8)

Typically, the term
\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}\big)
dominates on the right hand side.

Remark 2.8
.

In most nonlinear programming solvers, such as SNOPT
[
25
]
and IPOPT
[
40
]
, dual variables are already computed internally as part of satisfying the KKT optimality conditions, or because of the primal-dual nature of the underlying algorithms. Consequently, in such a situation, getting the dual solution in addition to the primal solution, requires little to no additional computational effort,
\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}\big)
is the same as
\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{p}}\big)
and the adjoint state formula
ASM
is readily implementable with minimal code extension.

Both vanilla automatic differentiation and implicit differentiation are respectively based on the following chain rules,

\displaystyle\nabla f(\bar{\theta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\!x}F\big(\mathrm{solver_{p}}(\bar{\theta}),\bar{\theta}\big)^{\!\mathsf{T}}\jac\big(\mathrm{solver_{p}}(\bar{\theta})\big)+\nabla_{\!\theta}F\big(\mathrm{solver_{p}}(\bar{\theta}),\bar{\theta}\big)

\displaystyle=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{1}\big(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}(\bar{\theta})\big)^{\!\mathsf{T}}\jac\big(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}(\bar{\theta})\big)+\nabla\mathcal{L}_{2}(\bar{\theta}),

The computational cost of automatic differentiation can be roughly bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Cost}\big(\text{AD}\big)

\displaystyle=\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{p}}\big)+\text{Cost}\big(\texttt{autodiff}F(\mathrm{solver_{p}}(\bar{\theta}),\bar{\theta})\big)+\text{Cost}\big(\texttt{autodiff}\,\mathrm{solver_{p}}(\bar{\theta})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{p}}\big)+\omega\text{Cost}\big(F\big)+\omega\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{p}}\big).

(2.9)

Again, in typical situations the terms
\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{p}}\big)
dominates.
Finally, assuming all regularity conditions for its application
[
2
]
, implicit differentiation has the following approximate bound on its computational cost:

\displaystyle\text{Cost}\big(\text{ID}\big)

\displaystyle=\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}\big)+\text{Cost}\big(\texttt{autodiff}\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}(\bar{\theta}),\bar{\theta})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\text{Cost}\Big(\texttt{autodiff}\big(\texttt{autodiff}\mathcal{L}\big)(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}(\bar{\theta}),\bar{\theta})\Big)+\text{Cost}\big(\text{LE}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\text{Cost}\big(\mathrm{solver_{pd}}\big)+\text{Cost}\big(\text{LE}\big)+\omega(n+q+1)\text{Cost}\big(\mathcal{L}\big),

(2.10)

where
Cost
(
LE
)
is the computational cost of inverting a linear system.

The choice of automatic differentiation and implicit differentiations in (
2.3
) and (
2.3
) depends on the number of variables and number of constraints, and possibly further characteristics of the problem. Depending on the setting, one solution may be preferable to the other, both in terms of worst case upper bounds and in practice. Note that this is a high level analysis and the actual performance may depend on additional computational factors, such as how second order derivatives are computed, how linear equations are solved, and potential approximation and parallelization strategies. Nonetheless this provides a reasonable upper bound on the computational burden for both automatic differentiation and implicit differentiation. Let us also mention that automatic differentiation requires to have access to the code of
\mathrm{solver_{p}}
, which may not be available for closed-source software.

Comparing with Equations (
2.8
), it is obvious that the worst case computational cost of
ASM
()
is lower, and possibly much lower, than either automatic differentiation or implicit differentiation, making it a strong competitor for the purpose of differentiating the value function of nonlinear programs.

3
Elements of nonsmooth analysis and o-minimality

In full generality, our study is conducted in a setting where the objective function
F
is merely locally Lipschitz. Accordingly, we begin by introducing tools required to handle nonsmoothness.

3.1
Conservative set-valued fields

A
set-valued map
or
multifunction

D\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{s}
is a mapping from
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
to the set of all subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
. The
graph
of
D
is given by
\graph D:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}:y\in D(x)\}
.
D
is
locally bounded
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
if there exist a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
x
and
r>0
such that
\displaystyle\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{N}}D(z)\subset\mathcal{B}_{c}(0,r)
.
D
is
graph closed
if
\graph D
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Equivalently,
D
is graph closed if for all
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and all
(y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
such that
x_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}x
,
y_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}y
and
y_{k}\in D(x_{k})
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows that
y\in D(x)
.

Definition 3.1

(Clarke generalized gradients
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz function. By Rademacher’s theorem,
\varphi
is differentiable on a full measure subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, say
\Omega
. Then, the
Clarke subdifferential
of
\varphi
is the set-valued map
\partial^{c}\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
defined as

\displaystyle x\mapsto\conv\,\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:\exists\,(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\Omega,u_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}x\text{ and }\nabla\varphi(u_{k})\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}u\}.

The Clarke subdifferential of
\varphi
, like for any other locally Lipschitz real-valued function, has nonempty, convex and compact values, and is closed graph and locally bounded.

Given
(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, the Clarke partial subdifferential
\partial^{c}_{y}\varphi(y,z)
of
\varphi
at
(y,z)
with respect to
y
is the Clarke subdifferential of the function
\varphi(\cdot,z)
at
y
.

Our analysis revolves around the notion of conservative gradients that we present in the next definition. Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a nonempty interval and
u\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be a
curve
. The curve
u
is said to be
absolutely continuous
if
u
is differentiable Lebesgue-almost everywhere on
I
, its derivative
\dot{u}
is Lebesgue integrable on
I
, and
\displaystyle u(b)-u(a)=\int_{a}^{b}\dot{u}(t)dt
, for all
a,b\in I
.

Definition 3.2

(Conservative fields
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz function and
D_{\varphi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be a locally bounded and graph closed set-valued map with nonempty values. We say that
D_{\varphi}
is a conservative field for
\varphi
if for any absolutely continuous curve
u\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
,

\frac{d}{dt}\varphi(u(t))=\langle{\dot{u}(t),v}\rangle\quad\forall\,v\in D_{\varphi}(u(t)),

(3.1)

for almost all
t\in[0,1]
. Any such
\varphi
is called
path differentiable
.

3.2
Definition of o-minimality

We briefly recall the concepts of tame geometry relevant to the class of mappings (
definable mappings
) considered in the present paper. Important works on this topic include
[
39
,
14
]
.

Definition 3.3

(o-minimal structures)
.

An
o-minimal structure
on
(\mathbb{R},+,\cdot)
is a collection of families

\mathcal{O}=(\mathcal{O}_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
, where for each

\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
\mathcal{O}_{\ell}
is a family of subsets of

\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, satisfying the following properties:

(i)

\mathcal{O}_{\ell}
is closed under complementation, finite unions, and finite intersections.

(ii)

If
A\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell}
, then both

A\times\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}\times A
belong to

\mathcal{O}_{\ell+1}
.

(iii)

Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be the canonical projection.
If
A\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell+1}
, then
\pi(A)\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell}
.

(iv)

The family
\mathcal{O}_{\ell}
contains all real algebraic subsets of

\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, which are sets of the form

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:\phi(x)=0\},

where
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
is a polynomial function.

(v)

The sets in
\mathcal{O}_{1}
are precisely the finite unions of points and intervals.

Definition 3.4

(Definable sets and definable mappings)
.

A subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
that belongs to an o-minimal structure

\mathcal{O}
is said to be
definable
in
\mathcal{O}
.
A function or a set-valued map is definable in
\mathcal{O}
if its graph is definable in
\mathcal{O}
.

Example 3.5
.

The simplest o-minimal structure is given by the class of
real semialgebraic sets
.
Recall that a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
is called
semialgebraic

if it is a finite union of sets of the form

\bigcap_{k=1}^{K}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:\phi_{k}(x)<0,\;\varphi_{k}(x)=0\},

where
K\geq 1
and
\phi_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
are polynomial functions for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.
Therefore, the semialgebraic functions and semialgebraic set-valued maps are functions and set-valued maps, respectively, whose graphs are semialgebraic sets.

From now on, we fix an o-minimal structure

\mathcal{O}
. And unless stated otherwise, all definable sets and definable mappings are understood to be definable in
\mathcal{O}
.

4.1

Proposition 4.4
.

The set-valued map of primal-dual solutions

has nonempty values and is locally bounded.

For all

and for every

()

4.4
Failure of the method without the definability assumption

Let us illustrate the importance of the definability assumptions. For this we demonstrate that the local Lipschitz continuity, path differentiability and even differentiability of the objective function

Lemma A.1

The set-valued map of feasible points

,
\lVert{{c_{s}}-{w_{\ell,s}}}\rVert<1/\ell\leq 1/L
. Suppose that there exist
\ell\geq L
and
s\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
such that
\lVert{{w_{\ell,s}}-{\bar{z}}}\rVert<1/L
. It follows that

2/L<\dist(\bar{z},A)\leq\lVert{{\bar{z}}-{c_{s}}}\rVert\leq\lVert{{\bar{z}}-{w_{\ell,s}}}\rVert+\lVert{{w_{\ell,s}}-{c_{s}}}\rVert<1/L+1/L=2/L.

This is a contradiction. So for every
\ell\geq L
,
\lVert{{\bar{z}}-{w_{\ell,s}}}\rVert\geq 1/L
for all
s\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
. That means that there exists
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
k\geq K
,
\lVert{{z_{k}}-{\bar{z}}}\rVert\geq 1/L
. Thus
\bar{z}
is not an accumulation point of
(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.

We can conclude that the accumulation points of
(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are exactly
A
.
∎

Claim A.7
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be closed with empty interior. Then, there exists a differentiable and locally Lipschitz function
\bar{g}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\bar{g}(z)=0
for every
z\in C
, and
\bar{g}(z)>0
for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\setminus C
. Moreover
\bar{g}
is continuously differentiable everywhere except on
C
with
\mathcal{B}_{c}(0,1)\subset\partial^{c}\bar{g}(z)
for any
z\in C
. In particular, if
C
is defined as in Section
4.4
()
,
\bar{g}
is also path differentiable.

Consider the bump function
\bar{b}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\bar{b}(x)\coloneq\begin{cases}e^{-1/(1-x^{2})}&\text{if }|x|<1,\\
0&\text{if }|x|\geq 1.\end{cases}

Let
b(x)=\left(\bar{b}(x)\right)^{2}
. The derivative
b^{{}^{\prime}}
is odd and
b^{{}^{\prime}}(1/2)<0
. Define
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{-b^{{}^{\prime}}(1/2)}b(x)
.
The function
\varphi
is infinitely differentiable and is support is
[-1,1]
. In addition,
\varphi^{\prime}(1/2)=-1
and
\varphi^{\prime}(-1/2)=-\varphi^{\prime}(1/2)=1
.

By Whitney theorem
[
30
, Theorem 2.29]
,
C
is the zero of an infinitely differentiable and non-negative function
\bar{f}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
and, for any
z\in C
,
\nabla\bar{f}(z)=0
. Thanks to Lemma
A.6
()
, there exists a sequence
(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus C
such that its set of accumulation points is
C
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, consider
\mathcal{B}(z_{k},r_{k})
such that
r_{k}>0
,
r_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0
, and
\mathcal{B}(z_{k},r_{k})\cap\mathcal{B}(z_{s},r_{s})=\varnothing
for
s\neq k
. Such a choice is possible, because for each
k
,
z_{k}
is at positive distance of
z_{i}
for each
i\neq k
and at positive distance of
C
the set of accumulation points.
Define
\bar{g}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
as

z\mapsto\bar{f}(z)+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi\left(\frac{\lVert{z-z_{k}}\rVert}{r_{k}}\right)r_{k}.

Then,
\bar{g}(z)=0
for every
z\in C
, and
\bar{g}(z)>0
for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\setminus C
. Moreover
\bar{g}
is differentiable and locally Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. It is continuously differentiable everywhere except on
C
because
\mathcal{B}_{c}(0,1)\subset\partial^{c}\bar{g}(z)
for any
z\in C
. Indeed, let
\bar{z}\in C
and, without loss of generality, we assume that
z_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\bar{z}
. Let
v\in\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:\lVert{w}\rVert=1\}
. Consider
(\bar{z}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\bar{z}_{k}=z_{k}-\frac{r_{k}}{2}v
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we have, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\nabla\bar{g}(\bar{z}_{k})=\nabla\bar{f}(\bar{z_{k}})+v
. So,
\bar{z}_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\bar{z}
and
\nabla\bar{g}(\bar{z}_{k})\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}v
. It follows that
v\in\partial^{c}\bar{g}(\bar{z})
. Since
v
is arbitrary in
\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:\lVert{w}\rVert=1\}
, and by Definition
3.1
()
,
we have

\conv\,\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}:\lVert{w}\rVert=1\}=\mathcal{B}_{c}(0,1)\subset\partial^{c}\bar{g}(z).

Now let us assume that
C
is defined as in Section
4.4
()
. Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be an absolutely continuous curve. Given that
\bar{g}
is locally Lipschitz,
F\circ\gamma
is absolutely continuous. Let
\Omega\subset[0,1]
be the full measure set where
F\circ\gamma
and
\gamma
are differentiable.
Consider the following three sets:

\displaystyle S=\{t\in\Omega:\gamma(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\setminus C\},

\displaystyle S_{1}=\{t\in\Omega:\gamma(t)\in C\text{ and }\dot{\gamma}(t)=0\},

\displaystyle S_{2}=\{t\in\Omega:\gamma(t)\in C\text{ and }\dot{\gamma}(t)\neq 0\}.

We have
\Omega=S\cup S_{1}\cup S_{2}
. Since
\bar{g}
is continuously differentiable outside
C
, the chain rule holds on
S
, i.e., for any
t\in S
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bar{g}(\gamma(t))=\langle{u,\dot{\gamma}(t)}\rangle\quad\forall\,u\in\partial^{c}\bar{g}(\gamma(t))=\{\nabla\bar{g}(\gamma(t))\}.

Thanks to
[
34
, Lemma 6]
, we know that
S_{2}
has zero measure. As
\bar{g}
is locally Lipschitz and
\gamma(t+h)=\gamma(t)+o(h)
for all
(t+h,t)\in\Omega\times S_{1}
with
|h|
small enough, the chain rule holds in
S_{1}
.
In conclusion, the chain rule holds for almost all
t\in[0,1]
and
\bar{g}
is path differentiable.
∎
