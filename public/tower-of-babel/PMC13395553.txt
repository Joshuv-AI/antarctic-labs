Holodorsal Nondysraphic Intramedullary Lipoma in a 5-Year-Old Male
Authors: Rebecca Jean DeCarlo, Matthew McCoy Stowe, Erin Miller, Scott Wait, Mark Van Poppel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A review of the literature reveals that fewer than two dozen pediatric cases of nondysraphic intramedullary lipoma have been described, with most lesions spanning limited spinal segments. Extensive or holodorsal involvement has been reported only rarely, including isolated cases involving long cervicothoracic segments or multilevel disease. The present case is notable not only for the patient’s age, but also for the exceptional size and extent of the lesion, exceeding that of most previously reported pediatric nondysraphic intramedullary lipomas. This distinction is clinically relevant, as lesion size and longitudinal extent may increase operative complexity and the risk of postoperative neurological deficit and spinal deformity. Because of their rarity, optimal management strategies for nondysraphic intramedullary lipomas remain incompletely defined. Complete resection is often precluded by the absence of a clear dissection plane between the lipoma and normal neural tissue, and subtotal debulking is generally favored to minimize neurological morbidity. Despite these challenges, timely surgical intervention in symptomatic patients can halt neurological deterioration and allow for meaningful functional recovery. We present a case of a nondysraphic intramedullary spinal cord lipoma that, on review of literature, represents one of the largest reported nondysraphic intramedullary lipomas in a pediatric patient. This case adds to the limited body of literature on this rare disease process and underscores important considerations regarding diagnosis, surgical strategy, and long-term management.

Case Information

No patient consent was necessary according to institution guidelines for anonymized case reports. The patient was a 5-year-old male at the time of presentation. He was born at 37 weeks’ gestation with otherwise normal early development and no cutaneous stigmata of occult dysraphic conditions. He presented with a change in gait characterized by inversion of the right foot. He was first evaluated by orthopedic surgery and was reasonably treated with bracing, although he had no clinical improvement. Over subsequent months, he developed worsening thoracolumbar back pain with burning radicular pain radiating into the right lower extremity, intermittent left leg pain, severe constipation, and progressive right foot and ankle contractures.

On initial neurosurgical evaluation, physical examination demonstrated normal bulk and tone in the left lower extremity. The right lower extremity exhibited mild distal muscle atrophy, increased tone, ankle contracture, and weakness with right ankle dorsi and plantarflexion. Given concerns for a central neurological process, spinal imaging was obtained.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the spine revealed a large, intradural intramedullary lipomatous mass extending from the cervicothoracic junction through the lumbar spine (C7–L3), causing significant spinal cord expansion and distortion of normal neural architecture ( Fig. 1 ). The lesion demonstrated imaging characteristics consistent with adipose tissue, including T1 hyperintensity and signal suppression on fat-saturated sequences. No associated spinal dysraphism was identified on MRI or subsequent computed tomography imaging ( Fig. 2 ).

The patient was referred to urology for evaluation preoperatively. Screening renal ultrasound was normal except for a small amount of dependent debris, which is nonspecific and can be related to dysfunctional elimination in general. Formal urodynamic testing was considered, but ultimately deferred after discussion with his parents, given his young age and need for surgery, regardless of the findings with his progressive contractures and bowel difficulty.

The patient underwent C7 to L3 laminoplasty with subtotal tumor resection ( Fig. 3 ). Intraoperative neuromonitoring was utilized for the duration of the procedure with spontaneous and stimulated needle electromyography. Intraoperatively, the lesion was noted to be extensively infiltrative and inseparable from surrounding neural tissue, consistent with previously described intramedullary lipomas. Lipoma was noted to be exophytic along a large portion of the lesion. We sharply incised the pia over long segments of the lesion and debulked using the ultrasonic aspirator. Subtotal resection was performed to achieve decompression while minimizing neurological risk. The pia was loosely reapproximated, the dura tightly closed, and the lamina secured with cranial plating hardware. Subsequent histopathologic analysis demonstrated a well-differentiated adipocytic lesion consistent with lipoma.

C7 to L3 laminectomy was performed and is shown in this picture. Lamina was reaffixed with the cranial plating system at the end of the case.

Postoperatively, the patient required a short inpatient stay of 6 days followed by inpatient rehabilitation for 25 days. He experienced transient urinary retention and an increase in dysesthetic pain in his legs. He had stable right leg rigidity and stable strength in both legs bilaterally. At his 3-month postoperative follow-up, he was continent, ambulating with minimal assistance, and reporting improved pain control with gabapentin. Subsequent outpatient rehabilitation evaluations demonstrated continued functional improvement with the use of a custom right ankle–foot orthosis and shoe lift. At his 6-month postoperative follow-up, his pain was largely controlled, and he was ambulating independently.

Discussion

The lesion described here is notable for its exceptional size and extent, spanning from the cervicothoracic junction to the lumbar spine (C7–L3). On review of literature, this represents one of the largest nondysraphic intramedullary lipomas reported in a pediatric patient. Previously published pediatric cases describe significantly more limited craniocaudal involvement, most often confined to a single spinal region. 1 There are only two other cases of similarly sized lesions. Nguyen and Lew describe a 2-month-old infant with a C6 to L5 intramedullary lipoma who underwent multilevel split laminotomy for debulking with a postoperative course complicated by kyphoscoliosis. 2 Understandably, patients who undergo long-segment laminotomy, laminectomy, or laminoplasty, as in the present case study, remain at risk of developing postoperative spinal deformity. In another case, Kumar et al report a 14-month-old child who underwent a C7 to L1 laminotomy for an intramedullary lipoma with reported gross total resection. 3 The patient had excellent results and recovered neurological function, but unfortunately, no long-term follow-up information was reported. 3 This case is unusual as gross total resection is not frequently possible.

Intramedullary spinal lipomas not associated with spinal dysraphism are rare, and their development is incompletely understood. The prevailing theory suggests that premature dysjunction of the cutaneous ectoderm and neuroectoderm allows mesenchymal tissue to migrate into the developing neural tube, where it subsequently differentiates into adipose tissue. 1 4 5 These masses have therefore been described by some as a hamartoma, but the propensity to recur after resection does still suggest a more neoplastic nature. 1 5 6 The slow growth of the lipoma often delays symptom onset, leading to insidious presentations characterized by progressive myelopathy, gait disturbance, pain, and bowel or bladder dysfunction. 6 7 8 This patient’s gradual symptom evolution over several months is consistent with other patient presentations in the literature. 6 7 8

Surgical intervention for nondysraphic intramedullary lipomas is generally reserved for patients with progressive neurological symptoms. 7 9 Prophylactic surgery in asymptomatic patients is not routinely recommended due to the substantial risk of iatrogenic spinal cord injury and postoperative spinal deformity in the long term. 1 9 Current consensus favors maximum safe subtotal resection of the lipoma to halt neurological decline and potentially improve function. 4 5 7 8 9 10 Gross total resection is typically not possible due to the interdigitation of the lipoma with normal neural structures and most authors recommend against its pursuit. 1 4 8 9 It is also important to note that surgical intervention may lead to neurological deterioration in the short term due to the extent of neural manipulation required. 1 5 Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, ultrasonic aspiration, and meticulous microsurgical technique are critical to maximizing decompression while preserving neurological function. 4 5 8

Postoperative outcomes in pediatric patients are variable and depend on preoperative neurological status, duration of symptoms, and extent of decompression achieved. Early surgical intervention before the development of fixed deficits appears to be associated with improved functional outcomes. 7 9 The favorable postoperative course observed in this patient, including recovery of bladder function and improved ambulation, supports the role of timely decompressive surgery even in extensive lesions.

Long-term clinical and radiographic surveillance is imperative, especially with subtotal resection, given the risks of regrowth and development of spinal deformity. 4 5 11 The patient described in this case report has only been followed for 6 months following resection, so the long-term effect of his extensive laminoplasty and debulking remains to be seen and is a limitation of this report. A retrospective analysis of 66 pediatric patients treated at John Hopkins University suggests that fusion rates after laminoplasty remain high. 11 A total of 83% of patients in their cohort developed postoperative spinal deformities with 17% ultimately requiring fusion procedures. 11 In sum, repeat surgery may be required in some cases but should be approached cautiously given cumulative surgical risk. 5

This case expands the existing literature by documenting one of the largest reported nondysraphic intramedullary lipomas in a child, highlighting the broad spectrum of disease presentation and reinforcing key principles of management. Awareness of this rare entity is critical to avoid diagnostic delay and to facilitate early referral for neurosurgical evaluation when progressive neurological symptoms arise in pediatric patients.

Conclusion

Nondysraphic intramedullary lipomas are incredibly rare, accounting for less than 1% of all spinal neoplasms. This case expands the current understanding of pediatric nondysraphic intramedullary lipomas by documenting one of the largest reported lesions in this population. It reinforces existing evidence that early recognition and carefully planned surgical intervention can stabilize neurological function even in extreme presentations of this rare entity.
