Association between integrated specialty pharmacy care model and pharmaceutical access and costs
Authors: Alexandra G Hames, Geronimo Bejarano, Insiya B Poonawalla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealth care consolidation in the United States has yielded mixed outcomes for patients; however, little is known about the impact of integration between insurers and specialty pharmacies. Through improved data and clinical integration, integrated specialty pharmacy care models may improve health care access and reduce costs for beneficiaries. However, this type of vertical integration may allow increased market power, which could lead to higher costs for beneficiaries.MethodsUsing Humana prescription claims, our cross-sectional study used adjusted regression analyses evaluating the association between the use of an integrated specialty pharmacy care model with pharmaceutical access and costs for beneficiaries managing complex specialty conditions in 2024.ResultsAfter adjusting for demographic and clinical characteristics, the integrated specialty pharmacy care model was associated with fewer days from prescription to fill (absolute difference: −5.7 days; 95% CI: −6.7 to −4.7 days) and, reduced yearly beneficiary pharmacy costs (−$106; 95% CI: −$163 to −$49) and yearly beneficiary costs for the specialty drug (−$77; 95% CI: −$129 to −$27) compared with nonintegrated specialty pharmacies. We found additional benefits for beneficiaries who also used integrated primary care services.ConclusionIntegrated specialty pharmacy care models may deliver faster and less costly pharmaceutical care for beneficiaries.

Vertical integration between insurers and specialty pharmacies, especially when aligned with integrated primary care, was associated with substantially faster access to specialty medications and lower beneficiary costs, suggesting that coordinated payer–pharmacy models may enhance both affordability and timely care for Medicare Advantage beneficiaries.

Introduction

Health care in the United States has undergone increasing consolidation over the past 2 decades.1-4 Much of this has occurred through horizontal integration, where organizations operating at the same level of the supply chain merge their operations (eg, hospital–hospital mergers). Such mergers are associated with higher prices and no improvements in health outcomes,5-7 leading to greater scrutiny by regulators.8 Considerably less attention has been given to other types of consolidation in health care, notably vertical integration between payers and providers (ie, hospitals, physician groups, and pharmacies), that operate at different levels of the supply chain. This type of integration has grown in recent years and, to our knowledge, has not been assessed thoroughly.9-11

Within vertical integration, most studies have evaluated provider consolidation that does not include payers,12-14 with mixed findings. For example, integration between health systems and physician groups led to higher spending and more patient steering (ie, health care entities directing patients to their own providers and hospitals) without subsequent improvement in hospital admissions or emergency department visits.13 On the other hand, integration between hospitals or physician organizations and pharmacies has been associated with lower prices for high-cost medications and increased cost savings for patients who used the integrated pharmacies.15,16 Integrations that include payers (eg, insurance companies) may better align both clinical and financial incentives. Aligning incentives between insurers and providers (eg, pharmacies) may reduce care fragmentation and lead to improved access to care and better patient experiences and health outcomes.17-19. Policymakers raise concerns that such consolidation between insurers and providers may result in higher costs and care access barriers without health outcome improvements, similar to the effects of horizontal integration.4 Recent evidence found that prices increased after Optum, which is vertically integrated with an insurer, acquired physician practices.20 Emerging evidence in payer–provider integration focuses on Medicare Advantage (MA) models, suggesting that MA–hospital integration may better coordinate care and improve health outcomes.21,22 Increasingly, some MA models also integrate pharmacy care (ie, retail, mail order, or specialty pharmacies), which has garnered substantial recent interest from policymakers and antitrust organizations. Pharmacies owned by the largest MA insurers—CVS (Aetna), OptumRx (United Healthcare), CenterWell (Humana), and Express Scripts/Accredo (Cigna)—accounted for over one-third of Medicare pharmacy spending in 2021.23 Recent research suggests that MA insurers’ own enrollees are more likely to use the integrated pharmacy than enrollees in other insurers' plans. Policymakers and antitrust regulators have expressed concern that insurer–pharmacy vertical integration could reduce competition, leading to higher prices and foreclosure of nonintegrated pharmacies and insurers.24 However, there is a lack of peer-reviewed research assessing the impact of such payer–pharmacy vertical integration.25,26

Within this space, specialty pharmacy care models play a unique and growing role. Unlike traditional retail pharmacies, specialty pharmacies focus on managing patients who use high-cost, high-complexity medications, including biologics and injectables that often require close monitoring, patient education, and coordination between prescribers, payers, and manufacturers. In fact, over half of total prescription drug spending is on specialty prescriptions, even though it represents a smaller proportion of prescriptions than retail pharmacies.27 Because of their specialized functions, specialty pharmacies sit at a critical intersection between clinical care and benefits management, making them a distinct area of interest for studying vertical integration between insurers and pharmacies. Recent evidence suggests that patients at vertically integrated specialty pharmacies have greater medication needs compared with those at nonintegrated pharmacies, which further supports the need to better understand vertical integration in the specialty pharmacy market.28 Among the top 10 specialty pharmacies by market share, 6 are owned by parent companies that are also insurers.29 Integrated specialty pharmacies may allow insurers and specialty pharmacies to create operational efficiencies, such as faster navigation of the prior authorization process, that potentially lead to faster initiation of therapy, shared data systems, and coordinated support programs that can enhance access to medications. This close coordination also provides specialty pharmacies with more detailed patient-level data that enables better patient identification and monitoring—allowing for timely interventions to prevent nonadherence or care delays. The integrated pharmacy may also increase patient access to clinical pharmacist support (often 24/7)—improving side effect and polypharmacy management—and assistance with financial optimization. These factors may particularly benefit populations who face increased barriers to managing their complex specialty pharmacy needs, such as older adults who are more likely to have physical or cognitive limitations and take multiple medications.30 There is an immediate need to rigorously study integrated specialty pharmacy care models to inform health policy.

In this study, we assessed the association of an integrated specialty pharmacy care model with medication access and cost among MA beneficiaries. Additionally, we assessed whether an added encounter for individuals seeking care at an integrated primary care and specialty pharmacy was associated with medication access and cost.

Data and methods

This study was reported in concordance with the STROBE reporting guidelines.31 The Humana Healthcare Research Human Subject Protection Office used US Department of Health and Human Services regulations 45 CFR 46 and the Office of Human Research Protections Guidance on Coded Private Information or Specimens Use in Research, Guidance (2008), to determine that this study did not constitute human subjects research and was therefore exempt.

Data and sample

In this study, our data source was the Humana Research Database, which includes enrollment information and medical and pharmacy claims for Medicare Advantage Prescription Drug (MAPD) beneficiaries enrolled in a large, national health plan. To define our sample, we selected 20 specialty medications with the highest claims volume dispensed by specialty pharmacies in 2024. Since some medications are exclusive to a particular specialty pharmacy, we included medications that were dispensed by both the integrated specialty pharmacy and other specialty pharmacies. These specialty medications included drug classes of HIV, neurology, rheumatoid arthritis, dermatology, respiratory, osteoporosis, and mental health (Table S1). We excluded oncology-related medications.

Our population included MAPD plan beneficiaries who were continuously enrolled during the study period of January 1, 2023, through December 31, 2024, and who filled at least 2 prescriptions for 1 of the top 20 specialty medications between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024. The specialty medication filled by the beneficiary was considered the index medication. Multiple observations per beneficiary were included for those who met inclusion criteria for more than 1 index specialty medication.

Variables

We included beneficiary-level demographic and clinical characteristics and plan-level covariates. Using the plan administrative data, we assessed age, sex, race, geographic region, Medicaid–Medicare dual enrollment, Medicare Part D low-income subsidy enrollment, and disability as original reason for Medicare entitlement. Using clinical and pharmacy claims during the 12-month pre-index period (January 1 to December 31, 2023), we calculated the Rx-Risk-V and Elixhauser comorbidity indices. The Rx-Risk-V score is a pharmacy-based comorbidity index based on the identification of 45 distinct medical condition categories via National Drug Code and/or Generic Product Identifier codes for their associated medication treatments. The Elixhauser comorbidity index is based on the identification of 31 categories of distinct medical conditions via International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes on an inpatient hospitalization or 2 or more outpatient claims at least 30 days apart. For both indices, a higher score indicates a greater comorbidity burden. At the plan level, we included the 2024 plan value from Milliman's Medicare Advantage Competitive Value Added Tool (MACVAT). The MACVAT accounts for plan premiums and the value of plan benefits to determine the plan's overall value added for the beneficiary. We also included the plan's specialty tier co-insurance percentage.

The exposure variable was an indicator of whether a patient selected to use the integrated specialty pharmacy or a nonintegrated specialty pharmacy to fill their prescriptions for the index medication. Following the Pharmacy Quality Alliance pharmacy attribution method, we considered beneficiaries to be users of the integrated specialty pharmacy if at least 51% of their fills for the index specialty medication were at a specialty pharmacy owned by their MAPD insurer.32 Beneficiaries who filled their index specialty medications at other specialty pharmacies were considered users of nonintegrated specialty pharmacies. Most (>86%) patients in both the groups used only 1 pharmacy to fill their index medications (Table S2). Additionally, plan benefit designs did not have a cost-sharing differential between different pharmacies for the specialty medication tier that incentivized use of a particular specialty pharmacy.

Outcomes

We had 2 primary outcomes. The first outcome was the number of days from the first prescription date for the index specialty medication to the fill date, which was assessed as the difference between the date the prescription was written and the fill date when the medication was billed to the payer. This outcome was calculated at the fill-level, for only fills that were indicated as non-refills, and averaged to the beneficiary level if a beneficiary had multiple non-refills. The second outcome was yearly beneficiary pharmacy costs, assessed as the total beneficiary payments for Part D drugs in 2024. As secondary outcomes, we assessed the yearly beneficiary costs attributed to the index specialty drug and the total number of fills for the index specialty medication in 2024.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were used to describe demographic and clinical characteristics by use of the integrated specialty pharmacy. In order to assess the association between use of the integrated specialty pharmacy with the outcomes, we used linear regression adjusting for demographic variables (age, sex, race, dual eligibility, region, Medicaid–Medicare dual eligibility, Part D low-income subsidy (LIS), rurality, and Area Deprivation Index), health characteristics (disability as the original reason for Medicare eligibility, Rx-Risk-V score, and Elixhauser comorbidity index), MACVAT value of their MAPD plan, and specialty co-insurance. All models included drug class fixed effects to control for unobserved, time-invariant drug class differences. Since these fixed effects are included, the results can be interpreted as the difference in outcome between groups within the same drug class. Standard errors were clustered at the pharmacy level. We conducted a subgroup analysis of beneficiaries using the integrated pharmacy who also were attributed to a primary care provider from a clinic integrated with the insurer to assess the potential effects of additional levels of integration. We also conducted several sensitivity analyses. Since integrated MA primary care providers have been associated with higher coding intensity, we removed the Elixhauser comorbidity index from our model to reduce any potential upward bias.33 We also conducted the analysis with plan-level fixed effects to assess potential bias from differential selection into integrated pharmacies by plan and a model with propensity-score matching as a robustness check.

Results

Our study population included 8783 beneficiaries using nonintegrated pharmacies and 14 458 beneficiaries using integrated pharmacies to fill prescriptions for the 20 most common specialty medications, including 1063 who were also using integrated primary care services (Table 1). Overall, the most common drug classes were osteoporosis medications (53%), rheumatoid arthritis therapies (14%), HIV agents (8%), and dermatology agents (7%) (Table S1). Compared with nonintegrated beneficiaries, beneficiaries using integrated specialty pharmacies were slightly older (74.3 vs 73.6 years), more likely to be female (82.2% vs 75.8%) or White (75.4% vs 67.2%), and less likely to be disabled (25.5% vs 28.6%) or dually enrolled in Medicaid and Medicare (32.9% vs 40.0%) or the Part D LIS only (7.3% vs 8.8%) (Table 1). The prevalence of comorbidities was similar between the groups (Rx-Risk-V score: 6.9 vs 7.0; Elixhauser score: 3.4 vs 3.5). The subsample of beneficiaries with integrated specialty pharmacy and primary care was slightly older (75.8 years) and more likely to be female (86.0%) or White (75.6%) compared with the full integrated specialty pharmacy sample and the nonintegrated sample.

Integrated specialty pharmacy beneficiaries spent an average of $1644 yearly on total pharmacy costs, which was $106 lower than nonintegrated beneficiaries using specialty medications in the same class (95% CI: −$163 to −$49), a 6% relative difference (Table 2). Spending towards index specialty medications was also 6% lower for integrated beneficiaries (−$77; 95% CI: −$129 to −$27), comprising most of beneficiaries’ total pharmacy costs (mean: $1266). Lower out-of-pocket spending was not driven by differences in adherence, as beneficiaries in integrated specialty pharmacy care had 3% more fills for the index specialty medication (0.21; 95% CI: 0.10-0.31).

The subsample of integrated specialty pharmacy with integrated primary care beneficiaries had similarly faster access to care and lower costs, with larger relative differences, compared with nonintegrated beneficiaries. Beneficiaries in an integrated specialty pharmacy with integrated primary care had 40% fewer days between prescription and fill (−6.3 days; 95% CI: −7.8 to −4.9 days) vs nonintegrated beneficiaries who filled prescriptions for the same drug class. For pharmacy costs, integrated specialty pharmacy and integrated primary care beneficiaries spent a mean of $1584 in total pharmacy costs, which was $165 less than nonintegrated beneficiaries (9% relative difference; 95% CI: −$224 to −$106). There was no significant difference in number of fills for the index specialty medication between beneficiaries receiving integrated specialty pharmacy with integrated primary care vs beneficiaries in nonintegrated care delivery settings (−0.01 fills; 95% CI: −0.12 to 0.11 fills). However, integrated beneficiaries spent 7% less on out-of-pocket costs for the index specialty medication (−$89; 95% CI: $143 to −$37).

Sensitivity analyses without the Elixhauser comorbidity index (Table S3) and with plan-level fixed effects (Table S4) were consistent. Results were also consistent in a propensity score–matched model (Table S5), although the difference between integrated and nonintegrated beneficiaries in count of specialty medication fills was not statistically significant.

Discussion

Our study found that use of a specialty pharmacy integrated with an MA plan was associated with significantly faster access to the top 20 specialty medications and lower out-of-pocket costs compared with use of a nonintegrated specialty pharmacy. Compared with nonintegrated beneficiaries, we found combined benefits for those who sought care at the integrated specialty pharmacy and an integrated primary care clinic also owned by their insurer. With an average 35% faster access to prescriptions and 6% lower pharmacy costs compared with nonintegrated beneficiaries, the magnitude of these results is likely to be clinically meaningful for beneficiaries receiving care within the integrated framework. This suggests that alignment between payer, primary care, and pharmacy operations may improve access to medications and cost efficiency. It may also reflect the lower rates of prior authorization rates by vertically integrated insurers allowing for faster access to care, which has been previously reported.34

Prior studies that have assessed vertical integration between providers and insurers have shown mixed results, but few have examined access to care outcomes. For example, 1 analysis found that MA beneficiaries enrolled in vertically integrated hospital plans had less inpatient care utilization, fewer surgical complications, better patient experience, and higher plan quality.21,22,35-38 However, other studies have reported that vertically integrated provider–insurer models are associated with higher premiums and enroll fewer dual-eligible beneficiaries.10,36 Our study adds to the current body of literature by examining integration of specialty pharmacy within an MA care model. Similarly, vertical integration between an insurer and pharmacy benefit manager has led to increases in premiums for nonaffiliated insurers without subsequent increases in benefits to beneficiaries.25 These divergent findings may reflect differences in the nature and goals of integration, including how incentives are aligned, the extent of data sharing, and the degree of clinical coordination achieved across these organizations.

While potential mechanisms are out of the scope of this study, there are several ways that insurer–pharmacy as well as insurer–pharmacy–provider integration may translate into improved access to care and health system performance. For patients, closer alignment between payers and pharmacies can streamline care coordination, reduce operational barriers, and enhance access to needed medications. The ability for these entities to comprehensively use patient data creates ample opportunity for specialty pharmacists to provide proactive support and improved care coordination such as timely refills and adherence outreach.39 For providers, integration can substantially simplify utilization management processes such as prior authorization to improve care coordination and health care access, allowing clinicians to focus more on patient care. Providers within integrated networks may have access to more detailed patient information, including medication histories, clinical information, and claims data, facilitating more informed and timely clinical decisions.17,18,40 Our results also show lower out-of-pocket costs for beneficiaries using integrated pharmacies, coupled with a greater number of fills. While the role of potential mechanisms such as lower drug prices is unclear, this finding was not explained by differences in benefit design (ie, there was no preferred cost-sharing structure for the specialty medication tier at the integrated specialty pharmacies). As integration models continue to evolve, health policy must balance incentivizing the benefits that come from improved coordination while minimizing anticompetitive risks from increased market consolidation.

There have been high levels of interest in insurer–pharmacy vertical integration. For example, Arkansas became the first state to pass a law limiting insurer–pharmacy vertical integration.24 Our study findings suggest that there may be benefits for patients with more efficient access to medications and lower out-of-pocket costs. Other evidence suggests that more socially vulnerable patients are more likely to use vertically integrated pharmacies.28 Policy should consider the unintended consequences of broad limitation of vertical integration and should be focused on improving competition while incentivizing benefits from vertical integration. For policymakers, it is important to understand how integration affects coordination, cost, and access. Future studies should explore mechanisms through which vertical integration achieves these outcomes and identify conditions that maximize value while preserving competition and patient choice.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of observational administrative claims data introduces the possibility of misclassification due to coding inaccuracies, missing data, or incomplete clinical documentation, which may affect the reliability of certain measures. Although we controlled for multiple demographic and clinical characteristics and used drug class fixed effects, residual confounding from unmeasured factors and selection into vertically integrated specialty pharmacies may still bias the results upward. However, recent evidence suggests that patients with greater medication needs and higher social vulnerability receive medications from vertically integrated pharmacies, which suggests that selection bias may be towards the null.28 Selection into the integrated pharmacy can also differ by medication or medication class, which may reflect factors such as prescribing behaviors; our sample had a large imbalance between integrated and nonintegrated users for some medications (eg, Botox). Second, some patients in the control group may have received care from specialty pharmacies that are vertically integrated with other insurers. This may potentially dilute observed differences between groups towards the null if some of the benefits spilled over to nonintegrated beneficiaries. This type of spillover has been found when the share of MA patients increases, the differentiation in care spills over to traditional Medicare beneficiaries.41 Third, although we chose the specialty medications that were most common, it is possible that our results are not generalizable to other specialty medications. Fourth, our sample included only Humana MA Part D beneficiaries, which may differ from beneficiaries covered by other insurers and may make it difficult to generalize the findings to broader MA populations. Future research should consider using multi-insurer data, such as MA Part D data, merged with vertical integration datasets to determine if there is heterogeneity between insurer–pharmacy vertical integration led by different insurers.

Conclusion

Our study found that the integrated specialty pharmacy care model is associated with faster access to medications and lower costs compared with nonintegrated beneficiaries. As vertical integration continues to increase, our findings have important implications for the possibility of benefits for patients from aligned incentives between insurers and specialty pharmacies.
