5′-end uridine-derived base modification for siRNAs potency reinforcement
Authors: Yuqi Yao, Xinming Qi, Ruimin Huang, Tianfu Guo, Jin Ren, Jinbiao Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall interfering RNAs (siRNAs), bound by Argonaute, efficiently cleave target mRNAs through full complementary base pairing, achieving potent gene silencing. While chemical modifications are widely employed to improve siRNA stability and binding affinity, modifying the 5′-end nucleotide of the guide strand remains a primary strategy for enhancing potency. In this study, we performed a structural analysis of the 5′-end binding pocket of the RISC complex to design a novel series of uridine-derived modifications and screen a library including 001U-018U and vs30U as candidates. We further tested the 001 ~ 003 μm-modified siRNA binding activity via in vitro RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) loading in HepG2 cells and in vivo RISC loading in mice. These function valiation results indicated a significant improvement in gene silencing activity for the modified siRNAs compared to unmodified control. Additionally, preliminarily safety evaluations demonstrated the modified nucleoside carry no detectable cytotoxicity or genomic insertion risks. Our findings expand the chemical space of siRNA modification and provide new insights into the design of high-potency siRNA drugs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01586-z.Keywords: siRNA, 5′-end nucleotide modification, Argonaute, Uridine, RNA silencing, Drug safety

Introduction

Small silencing RNAs are a class of RNAs that are 20–30 nucleotides in length and regulate gene expression via incorporation with the Argonaute (AGO) family of proteins [1]. As a class of small silencing RNAs, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) that induce RNA interference (RNAi) via their complementary sequence execute selective cleavage together with the RNA nuclease III argonaute 2 (AGO2) [2–5]. Recently, siRNAs have served as an RNAi tool in current RNA therapeutic trends. To date, six siRNA therapeutics have been approved for clinical use, all of which target hepatocytes. These siRNAs are characterized by their long-acting efficacy and require administration once every six months [6–11]. Compared with small-molecule drugs, siRNA therapeutics have significantly superior therapeutic outcomes [6].

Two primary factors underlie the remarkable features of these marketed siRNAs. First, chemical modifications such as 2′-O-methyl, 2′-deoxy-2′-fluoro, and phosphorothioates increase the resistance of siRNAs to degradation by various RNases [12–17]; these modifications improve siRNA stability while preserving the gene-silencing activity of the antisense strand. Second, the use of cleavable linkers to conjugate N-acetyl galactosamine (GalNAc) to the 3′-end of the siRNA sense strand enables highly specific binding to the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR), which is predominantly expressed on the surface of hepatocytes. This design achieves hepatocyte targeting, with over 60% of siRNA molecules delivered to the liver, ensuring sufficient intracellular siRNA concentrations [18–20]. As a result, GalNAc-conjugated, fully chemically modified siRNAs exhibit superior hepatic pharmacokinetics, intracellular stability, and gene-silencing potency, enabling efficient gene silencing for over six months with a single administration [21].

Currently, the extrahepatic application of siRNAs is constrained by the lack of a delivery system as efficient as GalNAc conjugation [6–11]. Alternative strategies, including lipid chain modifications, lipid nanoparticle (LNP) encapsulation, and localized administration, such as intrathecal injection, can extend the gene-silencing effect to approximately three months [22]. However, these approaches often require higher doses and are usually associated with increased side effects. For extrahepatic applications, further modification of the 5′-end of the siRNA antisense strand, such as the incorporation of a 5′-E-VP moiety, enhances the binding affinity to AGO2, thereby improving the gene-silencing efficiency and stability of the siRNA [18]. Nevertheless, in diseases such as cancer and autoimmune disorders, where targeting the liver or localized delivery is ineffective, the combination of lipid modifications and 5′-E-VP enhancements fails to achieve robust gene silencing or long-term efficacy [23, 24].

To overcome these challenges, further optimization of the interaction between the 5′-end nucleotide and the AGO2 protein [25, 26], alongside the development of more efficient extrahepatic delivery systems, holds promise for expanding the therapeutic potential of siRNAs in extrahepatic diseases. For instance, ANGPTL3 (Angiopoietin-like Protein 3) is a liver-secreted protein that plays a key role in lipid metabolism by inhibiting lipoprotein lipase (LPL) and endothelial lipase (EL), enzymes responsible for breaking down fats in the blood. Genetic studies show that people with non-functional ANGPTL3 have lower levels of LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, and a reduced risk of cardiovascular disease. This makes ANGPTL3 a target for new lipid-lowering therapies, including monoclonal antibodies, antisense oligonucleotides and siRNAs [27].

Since there remains significat potential to optomize 5′-end manipulation of uridine-based siRNA guide strands, we anticipate that rational structure-based designs will substantially improve the performance of future siRNA therapeutics. Here, we present our track of modification devising by analyzing existing structures of the human AGO2 MID domain (hAGO2-MID) in complex with uridine. Subsequent series screening and reverse verification by NMR and X-ray crystallography, respectively, were performed to identify the accurate efficacy of these uridine-derived monophosphates. To evaluate functional potency, we selected ANGPTL3 as the target, combined with a GalNAc-modified delivery system, and tested the gene silencing efficiency of siRNAs containing new uridine modification. Additionally, we performe preliminary safety evaluation which showed no evidence of cytotoxicity or genome insertion risks. Our findings provide a new chemcal space for siRNA 5′-end uridine modification and provide a robust framwork for the development of next-generation siRNA drugs.

Results

Rational design of UMP analog modification and selection

To determine whether these analogs are capable of better binding with hAGO2, we envisaged potential enhancement methods for uridine-derived analogs. One is through the consensus amino acid Y529, which is conserved among the AGO proteins in the binding pocket, always helping the base plain dock into position through a nonspecific aromatic ring stacking effect (Fig. 1A). We assumed that once we extended the uridine base in a certain plain, in our trial, specifically to be a heterocyclic modification, it would help the base plain gain stronger stacking effects with Y529. Moreover, considering the hydrophilic space among the phosphate, ribose and base, a charged atom should be considered in the heterocyclic modification. Thus, a series were designed and numbered 001 ~ 003.

Another approach involves potential hydrophilic amino acids in the periphery, such as K525. We assumed that an aliphatic acid extension might provide a flexible probability to reach these peripheral amino acids, which also have a flexible side chain, thus generating electrostatic interactions. This approach was intended to stabilize the chamber. These alternative designs were subsequently numbered 017, 018, and vs30 (Fig. 1D). Next, we designed and synthesized these compounds. Considering the synthetic feasibility of these uridine-derived analogs, we selected six candidates, namely, 001/002/003/017/018/vs30, for further research (Fig. 1C, D).

Increased affinity to hAGO2-MID of the modified uridines

To assess the potency of these designed structures, we conducted 1‒15 N heteronuclear single quantum coherence (1‒15 N HSQC) NMR titration experiments to quantify the binding affinity of the nucleoside monophosphate to the hAGO2-MID, which was performed as previously described [25, 28]. The difference could be clearly observed in some of the chemical shifts in the superimposed spectra (Fig. 2A, B), and the chemical shifts are indicated via arrows. Next, we investigated the differences in the chemical shifts of these monophosphates. During analysis, each of the selected amino acid-represented dots could be clearly isolated from other signals in each titration for further dissociation constant (Kd) determination (Fig. S1). The Kd value of UMP in our trial was 320 ± 69 µM, which deviates by a certain magnitude from that reported in a previous study (~ 120 µM) but is credible for use as an indicator in our study [25, 28].

Notably, in the case of the three thiophene heterocycle-modified isomers on UMP, 003-UMP seems to move the farthest, and 002-UMP moves the shortest. The Kd value of 003-UMP was 172 ± 26 µM, suggesting that this specific modification increases the affinity of UMP by almost 2-fold compared with that of UMP. Interestingly, the Kd values of 001-UMP and 002-UMP are 250 ± 28 µM and 460 ± 112 µM, respectively, suggesting that the position of the sulfur atom on the heterocycle of modified uridine might affect the binding affinity to a certain degree, although they share a similar chemical structure, resulting in a discrepancy in which two of the designs enhance the affinity, whereas the other designs exhibit affinity even worse than that of regular uridine.

The Kd values of 017-UMP, 018-UMP and vs30-UMP were 195 ± 19 µM, 380 ± 8 µM and 90 ± 21 µM, respectively, which suggests that after the aromatic ring, a shorter aliphatic acid modification might enhance the binding affinity, whereas a longer aliphatic acid modification might slightly hinder the binding affinity. Moreover, compared with that of the regular UMP, the chemical shift of the vs30-UMP was nearly 4-fold stronger than that of the unmodified one, which was most dramatic compared with those of the other three aliphatic acid-modified UMPs and motivated us to explore the causal relationships between the improvements and our rational designs.

Structural basis of the modified uridine analogs binding to MID domain of hAGO2

To unravel how the monophosphates might dock into the binding pocket and reflect the differences in their binding affinities, we applied X-ray diffraction to the crystals of these complexes, which was followed by the previous analysis of hAGO2-MID and naturally existing monophosphates. The initial model used protein molecules extracted from the original hAGO2-MID in complex with UMP (PDB ID: 3LUJ) [28]. Consequently, six sets of molecular structures were defined through the electronic densities, namely, hAGO2-MID in complex with 001-UMP (PDB ID: 9LSN), 002-UMP (PDB ID: 9LSO), 003-UMP (PDB ID: 9LMZ), 017-UMP (PDB ID: 9VLC), 018-UMP (PDB ID: 9VLD) and vs30-UMP (PDB ID: 9VLE).

All of the constructs possessed a well-defined electron density in the active region of all three symmetric units, whereas the models of the newly designed monophosphates fit well into the chambers (Fig. S2). The patterns of these monophosphates docking into the pocket are almost identical to those of regular UMP, especially the positions of the phosphate and ribose atoms among the complexes (Fig. 3A, F). The bases from the structures located in the same plain interact with Y529. Unsurprisingly, all of the modifications had a greater stacking effect on tyrosine (Fig. 3B–E). In contrast, slight distinctions might lead to differences in binding affinity. Using thiophene-modified UMPs as examples, the distance between N3 from the uridine ring and the carbonyl of G524 varies between different nucleotides. This specific hydrogen bond in 003-UMP was 2.8 Å in distance, which was the shortest, possibly leading to its high binding affinity according to our NMR results (Figs. 2 and 3E). Moreover, the hydrogen bonds in distance between UMP and 001-UMP was identical, but in 001-UMP, there was more base stacking between Y529 and 001-UMP than between Y529 and UMP (Fig. 3B, C).

In addition, the peripheral water molecule network acting as a bridge between the nucleic acid and the protein, also effect the stabilization of the 5′-end nucleotide recognition of hAGO2 (Figs. 3A, S2A–C). In the 001-UMP/003-UMP complex structures, the unique water molecules interact with the hydroxyl group from Y529, forming 2.8/3.1 Å hydrogen bonds, respectively, and seem to stabilize tyrosine residu and enhance aromatic ring stacking (Fig. 3C, E). Moreover, the unique water molecules in the 002-UMP complex formed a hydrogen bond with the sulfur atom from the thiophene ring, which is 3.1 Å in length and might affect the stability of the base plain, thus impeding stacking enhancement (Fig. 3D).

In the case of the aliphatic-acid-group-modified UMPs, according to the electronic density, almost all of the modified groups showed a high-level flexibility. However, vs30-UMP formed a 2.7 Å hydrogen bond via its terminal carboxyl group with the amino group provided by K525 (Fig. 3G). K525 used to be too flexible to wobble around the periphery of the chamber; in all our other X-ray diffraction results, the side chain of K525 was always difficult to define because of its flexibility. In the case of the vs30-UMP complex with the hAGO2-MID, K525 could be clearly identified from the main chain to the terminal amino group, although the phenyl group of vs30-UMP could not be visualized. These findings align with our NMR data (Fig. 2C), which is likely the reason why vs30-UMP possessed such a potent binding affinity.

The 5′-thiophene-ring-modified UMP is more potent than the 5′-unmodified UMP counterparts at the 5′-end of the guide strand

To further evaluate whether these modified uridine analogs are powerful enough to execute their potency, we first compared the differences in the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) loading efficiency of the antisense strands of 001U~003U-modified siRNAs in HepG2 cells. Here, we introduced a methoxy modification at the 2′-hydroxyl group from the ribose, which is now widely applied in RNA medication as a consideration of RNA stability [12]. The methylated uridine monophosphates together with their analogs are abbreviated as Um and 001 ~ 003 μm in this article. Consistent with the dissociation constant data from the previous HSQC experiments, 003 μm exhibited the strongest affinity for the hAGO2, followed by 001 μm, whereas 002 μm showed the weakest affinity compared with Um (Fig. 4A).

Next, we assessed the gene-silencing efficacy of 001 μm~003 μm-modified siRNAs under liposomal transfection conditions. Consistent with the HSQC dissociation constant and RISC loading data, 003 μm-modified siRNA exhibited the greatest ability to suppress ANGPTL3 mRNA, with an IC50 value of only 20% of that of Um. The siRNA modified with 001 μm displayed intermediate efficacy, while the 002 μm-modified siRNA had the weakest silencing ability, comparable to that of the Um-modified siRNA (Fig. 4B).

To this end, we conjugated a GalNAc group to the 3′-end of the sense strands of both Um- and 003 μm-modified siRNAs and compared their silencing efficacy against ANGPTL3 mRNA in primary cynomolgus monkey hepatocytes under free uptake conditions. The results demonstrated that 003 μm-modified siRNA significantly outperformed Um-modified siRNA in suppressing ANGPTL3 mRNA in monkey hepatocytes (Fig. 4C).

Finally, we compared the in vivo gene-silencing efficacy of 003 μm- and Um-modified siRNAs in mice. We used intravenous injection of AAV vectors to express human ANGPTL3 mRNA in mouse livers. Fourteen days post-AAV injection, subcutaneous administration of 003 μm- and Um-modified siRNAs was performed. At doses of 0.3, 1.0, and 3.0 mg/kg, 003 μm-modified siRNA exhibited significantly greater in vivo gene-silencing potency than Um-modified siRNA did (Fig. 4D).

Cytotoxicity assay of artificially synthesized nucleosides ensures application safety

GalNAc-conjugated siRNAs, when administered subcutaneously, predominantly localize to the liver and kidneys. In rats, up to 60% of the administered siRNA accumulates in the liver, whereas in monkeys, this proportion reaches 87%. Conversely, up to 17% of the siRNA is distributed to the kidneys in both species [29].

To evaluate the effects of thiophene-modified nucleotides on cell viability and mitochondrial function, four human cell lines derived from mitochondria-rich tissues (liver, muscle, kidney, and intestine) were selected. The cells were cultured in glucose-containing media and glucose-free galactose media to induce a metabolic shift from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation, thereby increasing the sensitivity to mitochondrial dysfunction.

Long-term exposure assays involving four doses over an 11 day period were conducted to account for the delayed onset of mitochondrial effects due to the initial abundance of mitochondria and mitochondrial DNA. The highest tested concentration, 250 µM, was approximately 12-times the projected maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of the 2′-fluoro-modified monomers in humans for revusiran.

Cytotoxicity was assessed on day 11 via a sulforhodamine B-based assay to measure total protein biomass (Table 1). Most cell lines (liver, muscle, kidney, and intestine) exhibited similar sensitivities to thiophene-modified nucleotide exposure in both glucose and galactose media, suggesting that the effects on cell viability were not predominantly due to mitochondrial toxicity. However, the intestinal cell line COLO205 demonstrated a 10-fold increase in sensitivity to 003-UMP in galactose media compared with that in glucose media, as reflected by a significantly lower IC50. This observation indicates a potential contribution of mitochondria to the reduced viability of this cell line.

Evaluation of the risks of genomic insertion from thiophene-ring-modified nucleosides

Similar to antiviral drugs, nucleoside or nucleotide metabolites of siRNAs can be stepwise phosphorylated at the ribose 5′-end position by intracellular kinases, forming triphosphate nucleotides (NTPs). These NTPs derived from our new nucleosides or nucleotides may be recognized by DNA polymerase or RNA polymerase as substrates during DNA replication or RNA transcription, potentially integrating into genomic DNA and RNA.

To assess the genomic incorporation risk of our newly designed nucleoside and nucleotides accurately, we administered the most promising modified nucleotide, 003 μm, into siRNA alongside the classical genotoxic agent aristolochic acid I (AAI), a positive control, to mice expressing human ANGPTL3. Liver tissues were collected on days 7, 14, 28, 56, and 84 postdosing. Genomic DNA and total RNA were extracted from the liver and enzymatically hydrolyzed into single nucleotides. The levels of the AA-DNA adducts (dA-AL1) and 003 μm in the genomic DNA and total RNA were quantified via LC‒MS/MS.

The results revealed that the level of dA-AL1 displayed an obvious decreasing trend in the liver genome from day 7 to day 84, indicating an effective DNA repair process to remove DNA adducts (Fig. 5A). In contrast, 003 μm was consistently undetectable in the genomic DNA throughout the study (Fig. 5B). 003 μm-modified siRNA had a knockdown effect on the target gene human ANGPTL3 on day 7, with the levels of human ANGPTL3 mRNA increasing over time (Fig. 5C).

Additionally, the total RNA from the liver presented 003 μm levels of less than 0.4 per million nucleotides (Fig. 5D), which correlated with the intracellular distribution of 003 μm-modified siRNA in liver cells. These findings suggest that the 003 μm detected in liver RNA likely originated entirely from siRNA entering hepatocyte lysosomes following administration rather than from de novo incorporation into newly synthesized RNA.

Discussion

The extrahepatic application of siRNAs faces significant challenges due to the absence of a delivery system as efficient as GalNAc conjugation for liver targeting. The addition of the 5′-E-VP moiety, lipid modifications, LNPs, and localized delivery methods increased the potency of the siRNAs in vivo but did not achieve robust or sustained gene silencing in cancer. Optimizing the interaction between the base of the 5′-end nucleotide and the hAGO2 may unlock the full therapeutic potential of siRNAs for most challenging disease settings.

In our study, we followed previous methods for siRNA amelioration and developed our own devising and screening strategy, including rational design, NMR affinity determination, and crystallized complex structure visualization. With the help of the solid workflow, we constructed a series of heterocyclic thiophene modifications between C2 and N3 of the uridine or an aliphatic acid group extension on C2 that might affect the recognition between the RNA and hAGO2, especially 003-UMP and vs30-UMP (Fig. 3). In contrast with regular UMP, 003-UMP has an almost 2-fold increase in binding affinity to the hAGO2-MID, and vs30-UMP has an almost 4-fold increase in binding affinity, even though UMP already has a high binding affinity in nature (Fig. 2).

During the initial design phase, we also conducted a series of trials on AMP-derived modifications. Several of these variants exhibited significantly improved binding affinity, notably vs37-AMP (Fig. S3A), which displayed a Kd of 102.95 ± 39 µM—more than 6-fold stronger than that of the natural AMP (652.05 µM in our assay as control; Fig. S3B). However, preliminary atomic modeling of vs37-AMP in complex with hAGO2-MID revealed no discernible differences in the binding pocket compared to the unmodified AMP (data not shown). Nonetheless, these results suggest promising directions for future design efforts.

We also compared our designs with previously published, highly optimized AMP-derived analogs 8-Br AMP and 6-mCeph-purine [25] by evaluating 003-UMP and vs30-UMP alongside them (Fig. S3C, D). Although the measured Kd values indicated somewhat weaker binding affinity for our constructs, this may be attributable to systematic variation, as the control nucleotides (regular UMP and AMP) also showed reduced affinity under the same conditions.

In the superimposed structural model, both 003-UMP and 8-Br AMP maintained the canonical recognition pattern with the rigid GKT loop. Additionally, both analogs exhibited enhanced stacking interactions with Y529 due to their extended modifications (Fig. S3C). Among the aliphatic acid-extended designs, both vs30-UMP and 6-mCeph-purine oriented their side groups toward the rigid loop. Owing to its more flexible amide linker, vs30-UMP was able to reach the side chain of K525 and form a stable ionic bond, whereas 6-mCeph-purine only established a weak interaction (> 4 Å) with P527 (Fig. S3D). Despite these differences, all modified compounds exhibited a significant enhancement in binding affinity.

To prevent artificial impacts from only the monophosphate domain and the single domain of hAGO2, we conducted RISC loading experiments in the HepG2 cell line (Fig. 4A), and the loading efficiency was consistent with our results from the NMR titration, as 003-UMP worked the best; the results of the gene-silencing experiments under liposomal transfection proved the conclusion more practically (Fig. 4B). Compared with those of regular uridine-treated hepatocytes, 003-uridine-treated primary cynomolgus monkey hepatocytes in vitro and mouse hepatocytes in vivo presented a significant increase in gene silencing (Fig. 4C, D).

We also preliminarily assessed the safety of the above nonnatural nucleosides from two perspectives (Fig. 5). First, since mitochondrial DNA polymerase is more sensitive to nucleoside analogs, we used cell lines with high mitochondrial content to compare the differences in cytotoxicity under two culture conditions: glucose-based media and galactose-based media. The cells were continuously exposed to nonnatural nucleosides for 10 days. We found that only the small intestine-derived cell line COLO205 exhibited sensitivity to 003U under galactose-based culture conditions. However, the IC50 exceeded 20 µM, which is 10 times greater than the estimated hepatic concentration under clinical dosing of approximately 800 mg. Second, we investigated the potential incorporation of 003 m into genomic DNA and total RNA following a single administration in mice. Even on day 7 postadministration, corresponding to the peak time for RISC-loaded siRNA in hepatocytes [21], we failed to detect the presence of 003 μm in genomic DNA from liver cells. The 003 μm detected in total RNA was more likely derived from the siRNA itself bound to hAGO2. We hypothesize that (1) the ribose 2′-OMe modification of 003 μm enhances its resistance to RNase degradation in hepatocytes, (2) its association with the hAGO2 may provide additional protection, and (3) the low dosing and single subcutaneous administration may result in minimal release of free 003 μm from the 5′-end of the siRNA antisense strand, rendering incorporation into genomic DNA undetectable or below our detection limits. In summary, our preliminary evaluations of in vitro safety and in vivo genomic incorporation risks suggest that the nonnatural nucleosides of this study possess favorable safety profiles and hold significant potential for clinical application. However, further studies involving higher doses and prolonged administration in animal models are needed to comprehensively assess their safety characteristics.

Our study presents a robust workflow for the de novo screening of innovative nucleotide modifications. However, there is still a gap between screening and efficiency validation. In our cases, we fortunately identified chemically modified structures that yielded highly concordant results from different experiments. However, modification at the 5′-end nucleotide of guide strand may affect not only the 5′-end recognition domain MID but also the intact hAGO2 protein. Therefore, further analysis using full-length siRNAs and full-lenth hAGO2 will provide more convincing insights, particularly as the molecular design is more complicated. The significant improvement of siRNA-mediated gene silencing via 5′-end nucleotide modification observed here encourages the continued development of high-potency RNAi-based drugs.

Methods

Preparation of thiophene ring-modified nucleosides and nucleotides

Thiophene ring-modified monophosphate nucleotides with 2′-hydroxyls of ribose (001 ~ 003-UMP) and nucleosides with 2′-methoxyls of ribose (001 ~ 003Um) were synthesized following the protocol of 003-UMP and 003Um in Supplementary Fig. 1. Compound 1 (5 g) and compound 2 (1.2 eq) were dissolved in acetonitrile (200 mL), trimethylsilyl trifluoromethanesulfonate (2 eq) was added, and the mixture was stirred at 40°C for 180 minutes. After the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, Compound 3 was purified via a silica column (11.2 g, yield: 93%). Compound 3 (10 g) was dissolved in an ammonia methanol solution (200 mL), and the mixture was stirred at 37°C for 1 hour to deprotect benzonate (Bz). After the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, Compound 4 was purified via a silica column (9.2 g, yield: 91%). Compound 4 (3.6 g) and diphenyl carbonate (DphC, 1.2 eq) were dissolved in acetonitrile (100 mL), and the mixture was stirred at 130°C for 75 minutes to remove the 2′-hydroxyl group of ribose (Compound 5, 2.6 g, yield: 72%). Compound 5 (2 g) was dissolved in 50 mL of magnesium dimethanolate (6–10 wt%) and stirred at 70 °C for 3 days. After the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, Compound 6 (003 μm) was purified via a silica column (1.6 g, yield: 79%). Compound 4 (1 g) was phosphorylated with phosphoryl chloride (POCl3) in trimethyl phosphate (TMP) at 5 °C overnight. Compound 7 (003-UMP) was purified on a silica column (0.9 g, yield: 90%) and further analyzed via 1-15 N HSQC and crystallization. Compound 6 was used to replace the 5′-end of the antisense strand of the siRNA.

Protein preparation

The hAGO2-MID (439–578 a.a.) was expressed and purified as previously described [28]. In brief, the protein sequence was cloned and inserted into the EcoRI and XhoI sites of a pET58a vector, which included a 6×His-Sumo tag. The protein was then expressed in a bacterial system and purified via a standard protein purification protocol, followed by Ni-affinity chromatography, tag cleavage, re-Ni-affinity chromatography and size exclusion chromatography via a Superdex 75 column in 50 mM Tris buffer, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 3 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) for crystallization. The 15N isotope-labeled hAGO2-MID was expressed in M9 medium supplemented with 15NH4Cl and then purified via the same procedure as the standard protein, after which it was stored in 25 mM MES buffer, pH 6.5, 200 mM NaCl, and 3 mM DTT for NMR.

NMR experiments

All the 1-15 N HSQC NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker 600 MHz NMR spectrometers equipped with TCI cryogenically cooled probes at 25 °C. For Kd determination, certain amounts of modified UMPs were added to the 15N-labeled hAGO2-MID (50 µM). Kd was calculated from the titration data using selected residues, which show chemical shift perturbations upon modified UMP titrations, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. All of the 1-15 N-HSQC NMR spectra were processed by Topspin 3.5 and analyzed by Sparky [11].

Crystallization, data collection and structure determination

The purified protein was mixed with modified UMPs at a ratio of 1:10 and then crystallized as previously described [28]. X-ray diffraction data were collected at beamlines BL02U1 and BL10U2 at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) and beamlines BL18U1 and BL19U1 of the National Facility for Protein Science Shanghai (NFPS). Diffraction data were indexed, integrated, and scaled via the HKL3000 program (HKL Research, Inc.) or the xia2_3dii. The structure was solved by molecular replacement via the protein chain of the hAGO2-MID with the UMP structure as an initial model (PDB ID: 3LUJ) via phaser_MR in the CCP4 package [30] and further refined via the Refmac5 program of the CCP4 suite. The final refinement of all the structures was performed via the phenix.refine program [31]. The data collection, processing statistics and structural refinement statistics are summarized in Table S1.

Synthesis of siRNA and conjugation of GalNAc

Oligonucleotide synthesis was conducted on an RNA synthesizer at scales ranging from 1 to 10 µmol, employing 5′-O-(4,4′-dimethoxytrityl)-2′-deoxy-2′-fluoro and 5′-O-(4,4′-dimethoxytrityl)-2′-O-methyl-3′-O-(2-cyanoethyl-N, N-diisopropyl) phosphoramidite monomers of uridine, cytidine, adenosine, and guanosine, following standard solid-phase synthesis protocols. For the antisense strand, thiophene-modified phosphoramidites of 001 ~ 003 μm were site-specifically incorporated at the 5′ terminus of the solid-phase-bound oligonucleotide. These modifications were achieved under standard synthesis conditions using a 0.25 M solution of 5-(ethylthio)-1 H-tetrazole in acetonitrile as the phosphoramidite activator. The phosphitetriester intermediates were subsequently converted into phosphorothioate linkages via thiolation reactions. For the GalNAc-conjugated siRNA, the GalNAc ligand was conjugated to the 3′ terminus of the sense strand of the siRNA through a phosphoramidite linker (L96).

In vitro gene silencing experiments

Transfection was carried out by adding 7.4 µL of Opti-MEM (Gibco) and 0.1 µL of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher) to 20 µL of siRNA solution per well in a 24-well plate, followed by incubation at room temperature for 20 min to facilitate complex formation. Five hundred microliters of DMEM (HyClone, Takara) containing approximately 5 × 10⁵ HepG2 cells was added to the siRNA-lipid complex. The cells were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C prior to RNA isolation. mRNA isolation was performed via the use of TRIzol (Takara). cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of total RNA per sample via the Hifair AdvanceFast 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeason, Shanghai, China). Real-time qPCR was performed via SuperReal Probe Master Mix (FP206, Tiangen, Beijing, China) on an ABI7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System. The following qPCR program was used: preincubation for 5 min at 95 °C, 40 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C, and 30 s at 60 °C. The relative abundance of human ANGPTL3 mRNA was determined via comparison with the internal reference gene glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (human GAPDH) via the ddCt method. IC50 values and dose‒response curves were generated via GraphPad Prism software, with the data presented as relative expression levels normalized to those of the untreated control condition. All the primers used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

In vivo gene silencing experiments

C57BL/6 male mice aged 6–7 weeks were infected by intravenous administration of 1 × 1011 viral particles of an adeno-associated virus 8 (AAV8) vector encoding the human ANGPTL3 gene (coding region) driven by the liver-specific thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG) promoter (AAV8-TBG-ANGPTL3). After 2 weeks of infection, the mice (n = 4 per group) were administered 0.1 mL/10 g body weight subcutaneously. siRNAs were administered at three doses, 0.5, 1.5 and 5 mg/kg, for all the compounds. The control group was treated with PBS (n = 4). Blood samples were collected before and 120 h after treatment. Liver tissues were collected at 120 h postdosing and cryopreserved at -80 °C. Frozen mouse livers (20 mg) were ground and resuspended in 1 mL of TRIzol (Takara Bio, Japan). Chloroform (100 mL) was added to each sample, which was mixed and then incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 g at 4 °C for 15 min. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, followed by the addition of 1.5 vol. of 100% isopropanol. The RNA was purified via an EZ-10 spin column (Sangon, Shanghai, China). The RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of total RNA per sample via the Hifair AdvanceFast 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeason, Shanghai, China). Real-time qPCR was performed via SuperReal Probe Master Mix (FP206, Tiangen, Beijing, China) on an ABI7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System. The following qPCR program was used: preincubation for 5 min at 95 °C, 40 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C, and 30 s at 60 °C. The relative abundance of human ANGPTL3 mRNA was determined via comparison with the internal reference gene glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (gapdh) from mice via the ddCt method. All the primers used for qPCR are listed in supplementary Table 2.

Evaluation of the incorporation risks of thiophene ring-modified nucleosides

C57BL/6 male mice aged 6‒7 weeks were infected by intravenous administration of 2 × 1011 viral particles of an adeno-associated virus 8 (AAV8) vector encoding the human ANGPTL3 gene (coding region) driven by the liver-specific thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG) promoter (AAV8-TBG-ANGPTL3). After 2 weeks of infection, the mice (n = 4 per group) were administered 0.1 mL/10 g body weight. siRNA was subcutaneously administered at 3 mg/kg. Aristolochic acid I was dissolved in 1% NaHCO3 and intraperitoneally administered at 3 mg/kg. The control group was treated with PBS (n = 4). Liver tissues were collected at 7, 14, 28, 56 and 84 days postdosing and cryopreserved at -80 °C. Genomic DNA and total RNA were isolated for enzymatic digestion and detection of dA-AL1 and 003 μm.

Cytotoxicity assay

HepG2, SkMC, Caki-1, and COLO205 cells were grown in either glucose- or galactose-containing media for 3 weeks, after which they were seeded into 96-well plates for nucleotide treatment. Nucleotides in DMSO were dosed at concentrations up to 250 mM, with every medium changed on days 0, 3, 7 and 10 for a total of four doses. On day 11, cytotoxicity was assessed via a sulforhodamine B-based assay (Cat# TOX6, Sigma‒Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

DNA isolation, digestion, and protein precipitation

DNA was isolated via a silica spin column following Zymo Research’s protocol with minor modifications. Fifty-five mg of purified DNA (48 mL) was used for DNA digestion. DNase I (1.5 µL, 2542 U/mL in 0.15 mol/L NaCl; 254.2 U/mg DNA; Sigma) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Next, 1 µL of nuclease P1 (100 U/mL in 1 mmol/L ZnCl2; 4 U/mg DNA; Sigma) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. A total of 1.5 µL alkaline phosphatase (24 U/mL in 1 mmol/L MgCl2; 2 U/mg DNA; Sigma) and 8 µL phosphodiesterase I (1.7 U/mL in 110 mmol/L Tris-HCl at pH 8.9 containing 110 mmol/L NaCl, 15 mmol/L MgCl2, and 50% glycerol; 0.0714 U/mg DNA; Sigma) were added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. After incubation, 60 µL internal standard (IS) solution was added to 60 µL above the mixture for protein precipitation and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. A total of 110 µL supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate for LC–MS/MS analysis.

RNA isolation, digestion, and protein precipitation

Total RNA was isolated from mouse livers for digestion via the same protocol used for in vivo gene silencing experiments. Total RNA from the liver was digested into ribonucleosides following the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 30 mg of RNA was digested in 300 µL of digestion buffer (60 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5, 1.2 mM ZnSO4, 10 units of RNase A, 2 units of nuclease P1, and 9 units of alkaline phosphatase) at 37 °C for 12 h. The digested RNA samples were subjected to vacuum centrifugation to remove the buffer and methanol extraction, after which the methanol was also removed via vacuum centrifugation according to the DNA extraction procedure. After incubation, 60 µL of internal standard (IS) solution was added to 60 µL of the above mixture for protein precipitation and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. A total of 110 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate for LC–MS/MS analysis.

Quantitative analysis of dA-ALI and 003 μm

dA-ALI, 003 μm in genomic DNA, and 003 μm in total RNA were analyzed with an ultraperformance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (LC-30, SHIMAZU, Tokyo, Japan) connected to a Triple Quad 6500 + System (UPLC–MS/MS, AB Sciex LLC, MA, USA) with electrospray ionization. The separation was carried out on an ACE C18 column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 5 μm) maintained at 40 °C. A gradient program was conducted using an aqueous mobile phase A of 0.2% acetic acid in water and an organic mobile phase B of acetonitrile. The flow rate was set at 0.5 mL/min throughout each injection. The injection volume was 15 µL, and the retention times of dA-ALI (Analyte), mtU03 (Analyte) and AAII (IS) were 1.09 min, 1.31 min and 1.52 min, respectively. The gradient program used followed a previous report. Detection of the ion pairs was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode fitted with an electrospray ionization (ESI) probe and operated in positive ion mode. The MRM ion pairs for dA-ALI and mtU03 were 543.2/427.1 and 674.8/378.9, respectively. The optimized conditions were as follows: curtain gas, 40 psi; collision gas, 10 psi; ion spray voltage, 5000 V; temperature, 550 V; ion source gas 1, 50 psi; ion source gas 2, 50 psi; entrance potential, 10 V; and collision cell exit potential, 15 V. Data were acquired and analyzed by Analyst 1.6.3. For the qualification of dA-ALI, the following criteria were used: the signal-to-noise ratio should be ≥ 3, and the relative abundance variation between two ion pairs should be ≤ 50% (when the production content is ≤ 10% of that of another product ion in the total ion current). For the quantification of dA-ALI, a limit of detection of 5 pg/mL was selected, and its accuracy, stability, and precision were validated. For the quantification of mtU03, a limit of detection of 10 pg/mL was selected, and its accuracy, stability, and precision were validated.
