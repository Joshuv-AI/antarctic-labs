The tumor-microenvironment-associated prognostic gene TIGIT promotes malignant phenotypes in esophageal carcinoma
Authors: Wenjing Wang, Lisha Ye, Jiamin Sheng, Linchun Wen, Huihui Li, Wei Hong, Yongling Ji, Xiaoling Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•TIGIT is highly expressed in ESCC tumor cells, not only immune cells.•High TIGIT correlates with shorter DFS and OS in resectable ESCC.•TIGIT knockdown reduces ESCC cell migration, invasion, and proliferation.•RNA-seq implicates cytokine-receptor and JAK-STAT as candidate pathways.•Dual targeting of immune and tumor-intrinsic TIGIT is proposed.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) ranks as the seventh most commonly diagnosed cancer and the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide as of 2020[1]. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), which constitutes approximately 87% of EC cases globally, remains a prevalent subtype in Asia, notably in China, despite a general decline in its incidence[2]. Despite various treatment options, the long-term prognosis for EC patients is poor, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 40%, even for resectable cancers[3]. Consequently, new therapies are urgently needed to improve the prognosis of this disease.

Recently, immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment of several malignancies, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma[4]. Immunotherapy has also shown promising antitumor activity in EC with acceptable toxicity, based on numerous ongoing clinical trials worldwide[[5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, challenges remain in the application of immunotherapy to EC, particularly in identifying potential biomarkers to predict prognosis.

Several studies have suggested that the expression of tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) markers is closely associated with tumor prognosis[[9], [10], [11]]. Among immune checkpoint molecules, CD8, lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG3), PD-L1, T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain 3 (TIM3), CD163, and T-cell Ig and ITIM domain (TIGIT) have garnered significant interest. These molecules are considered crucial regulators of immune responses in esophageal cancer. Patients with high PD-L1 expression in tumor-infiltrating immune cells have shown better responses to immunotherapy and improved prognoses[12]. Therefore, the up-regulation of PD-L1 expression in the TIME not only reflects tumor cell immunosuppression but is also related to the body's antitumor immune activity[13]. LAG3, expressed on activated T cells, negatively regulates T cell activation, proliferation, and homeostasis, promoting tumor immune evasion[14]. Additionally, TIM3, an inhibitory molecule on Th1 cell surfaces, triggers Th1 cell death, suppresses autoimmunity, and induces peripheral tolerance through its interaction with Galectin-9[15,16]. As a marker on M2 macrophages, CD163 may indicate poor prognosis in EC[17,18]. CD8+ T lymphocytes can recognize tumor-associated antigens and kill tumor cells, thus playing a critical role in antitumor immunity and correlating with favorable survival in various cancers[19,20]. Another immune checkpoint molecule that has attracted attention is TIGIT, an inhibitory receptor expressed by activated T cells and NK cells[21]. Preliminary studies indicate that TIGIT plays an immunosuppressive role by enhancing interleukin 10 production in dendritic cells, thereby inhibiting T cell activation[22]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated beneficial therapeutic outcomes by blocking TIGIT’s binding to CD155 or PD-1[23,24].

The expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as CD8, TIM3, PD-L1, LAG3, CD163, and TIGIT can help predict tumor prognosis[25,26]. However, few studies have explored the prognostic value of these biomarkers in patients with resectable ESCC. To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective study to examine the relationship between the expression levels of CD8, LAG3, PD-L1, TIM3, CD163, and TIGIT and the clinicopathological features in patients with resectable ESCC. Additionally, we identified a distinctive expression profile of TIGIT in ESCC and investigated its functional roles in ESCC cell lines.

Methods

Patients and treatment

Between January 2008 and July 2010, 187 patients who underwent esophageal cancer resection at Zhejiang Cancer Hospital were initially identified. Among these, 145 patients had formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues available for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis using tissue microarrays (TMA). From these, 112 samples were deemed of sufficient quality for further analysis. Subsequently, 89 patients were selected based on the availability of complete clinical data and histopathological confirmation of ESCC. Ultimately, 69 patients with detailed postoperative follow-up information were included in this study (Fig. S6). Preoperative clinical examinations, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, computed tomography scans of the chest and abdomen, and whole-body bone scans were conducted to exclude patients unsuitable for complete resection. Patients were excluded if they had previous or concurrent cancer, received neoadjuvant therapy before surgery, or died within 30 days post-operation. Patient medical records were reviewed to obtain tumor staging, pathology, and survival information. All patients were followed for at least five years unless lost to follow-up or deceased, with a median follow-up period of 47.4 months. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital, and informed consent was obtained from all patients (Ethics Approval Number: IRB-2021-49).

Triple-color and multiplex immunofluorescence histochemistry

To validate TIGIT expression in tumor cells, we employed triple immunofluorescence staining on paraffin-embedded ESCC tissue samples. Triple-color immunofluorescence histochemistry was performed on 3 µm sections using Opal Multiplex IHC Kits & Reagents (#NEL811001KT, Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA) following the manufacturer's protocol. After deparaffinization and rehydration, tissue sections underwent heat-induced epitope retrieval in citric acid (0.01 mol/L, pH 6.0) at 100°C for 15 minutes. The sections were then blocked with 2.5% normal goat serum for 30 minutes before overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used included anti-CD8 (clone: C8_144B, #70736, diluted 1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), anti-CD163 (clone: D6U1J, #93498, diluted 1:400, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-LAG3 (clone: D2G4O, #15372, diluted 1:200, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-PD-L1 (clone: E1L3N, #13684, diluted 1:400, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-TIGIT (clone: TIGIT/3106, #ab243903, diluted 1:100, Abcam), and anti-TIM3 (clone: D5D5R, #45208, diluted 1:100, Cell Signaling Technology). Following washing, sections were incubated with suitable secondary antibodies conjugated to fluorophores and counterstained with 100 µL DAPI (AR1177, Boster Bio).

Multiplex immunofluorescence histochemistry was conducted according to the manufacturer's protocol using the Opal Multiplex Immunofluorescence Staining Kit and antigen retrieval buffers at pH 6.0 (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA). Four µm thick sections were prepared from ESCC tumor specimens. Briefly, after heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER), sections were incubated with a primary antibody followed by an HRP-conjugated sheep anti-pika secondary antibody. Subsequently, the appropriate Opal fluorophore-conjugated TSA (1:100 dilution) was added. Sections were washed with TBST after each step. HIER was employed to remove primary and secondary antibodies, repeating the above procedures until all six markers were labeled. Finally, DAPI at a 1:100 dilution was used for nuclear staining. Akoya Mantra/Vectra/Polaris imaging equipment was used to capture images according to dye spectrum conditions, and stained tissue data were interpreted and analyzed under a fluorescence microscope. The fluorophore and marker channel order was as follows: Opal 520 (anti-CD8), Opal 540 (anti-CD163), Opal 560 (anti-LAG3), Opal 580 (anti-PD-L1), Opal 620 (anti-TIGIT), and Opal 650 (anti-TIM3).

For orthogonal validation of tumor cell TIGIT expression, serial 3 μm sections were stained with TIGIT/PAN-CK/DAPI (triple immunofluorescence) as previously described. Tumor cell regions positive for PAN-CK were segmented using CaseViewer software, and the mean fluorescence intensity of TIGIT within these regions was normalized to the PAN-CK area. This was reported as the tumor-cell-specific TIGIT signal.

Essential control experiments, including single-channel staining controls for each primary antibody and isotype controls, were conducted to ensure staining specificity. The spectral unmixing process was validated using reference spectra for each fluorophore, with software tools provided by Akoya Biosciences. Imaging was performed using an Akoya Mantra Imaging System with a 20x objective. Exposure settings were optimized for each fluorophore, and images were analyzed using CaseViewer Software. Staining specificity was confirmed by the absence of signal in isotype controls. Equipment used in the experiment included a tissue dehydrator (Excelsior ES, Thermo), a tissue embedding machine (EC350, Thermo), a microtome (HM325, Thermo), and a microscope (Axio.Scope A1, Carl Zeiss AG).

Tumor cell expression assessment

Marker expression levels on tumor cells, including CD8, TIM3, PD-L1, LAG-3, CD163, and TIGIT, were semi-quantitatively evaluated using the formula: (Number of marker-positive tumor cells)/(Sum of DAPI-positive total tumor cells and marker-positive tumor cells) × 100. This method provides a relative measure of marker expression within the tumor cell population. Tumor nests were first identified by their characteristic cohesive epithelial architecture—polygonal cell morphology, high nuclear density, and strong PAN-CK positivity in serial sections—and were distinguished from the surrounding loose stromal tissue containing scattered immune infiltrates. The segmentation algorithms utilized in the Akoya platform were employed to distinguish tumor cells from other cell types using a combination of morphological and intensity-based features. Immune cells closely apposed to tumor nest boundaries were actively excluded from tumor cell quantification to prevent contamination by peritumoral infiltrating lymphocytes. A clear classification protocol was established for cells exhibiting ambiguous phenotypes (e.g., PAN-CK⁺/immune-marker⁺ cells). These cells were classified based on their primary localization and expression intensity: cells primarily localized within the cohesive epithelial cluster and exhibiting strong PAN-CK expression were classified as tumor cells, whereas cells located in the intervening stroma with strong immune marker expression were classified as immune cells. This protocol ensures consistent and accurate cell type classification. Optimal cut-off values for biomarkers were determined using the Area Under the Curve (AUC) from Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis, allowing for the selection of thresholds that maximized separation between outcome groups and provided a robust measure for analysis. Cut-off values for high tumor cell expression were determined as follows: CD8 (≥0.10), TIM3 (≥3.49), PD-L1 (≥20.08), LAG3 (≥8.88), CD163 (≥30.83), and TIGIT (≥38.40).

Cell culture and treatment

KYSE-30 and KYSE-50 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Hyclone, Cat. No. SH30809.18), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Procell, No. 164210-500) and 100 U/mL penicillin (Hyclone, Cat. No. SH30010) at 37°C with 5% CO₂. TIGIT-homo-67, TIGIT-homo-504, and TIGIT-homo-309 small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were synthesized by GenePharma. When cell confluence reached approximately 70%, each well was transfected with 7.5 µL Lipofectamine 3000 (#L3000015, Invitrogen, CA, USA) and 75 pmol of siRNA, along with corresponding negative control sequences. The stock concentration of siRNA was 4.55 µg/µL. Lipofectamine 3000 and siRNA were diluted separately in Opti-MEM I Reduced Serum Medium, using 125 µL of Opti-MEM (#PM150708, Procell, Wuhan) to achieve their respective working concentrations. The diluted Lipofectamine 3000 and siRNA were mixed at a 5:1 volume ratio and incubated for 20 minutes at room temperature to form siRNA-Lipofectamine complexes. After incubation, the complexes were added to the wells containing the cells. The cells were then incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ for 4-6 hours to facilitate uptake of the complexes. Following incubation, the medium was replaced with complete RPMI-1640 medium (#PM150110, Procell, Wuhan) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin, and the cells were further cultured for an additional 24 hours to assess transfection efficiency.

The sequence of TIGIT-homo-67 siRNA was as follows: 5’-GACAGGCACAAUAGAAACATT-3’

The sequence of TIGIT-homo-504 siRNA was as follows: 5’-CCCUCAGAAUCCAUUCUGUTT-3’

The sequence of TIGIT-homo-309 siRNA was as follows: 5’-GGGAGUACUUCUGCAUCUATT-3’

Western blot analysis

We conducted a Western blot analysis of protein extracts from KYSE-30 cells and primary tumor samples to confirm the presence of TIGIT in tumor cells. Tumor cells were enriched from these samples using cell sorting techniques, followed by validation of TIGIT expression through immunofluorescence and Western blot analyses.

Total protein was extracted from cells and tissues using RIPA buffer (#PC102, EpiZyme, Shanghai), which contained both protease and phosphatase inhibitors to prevent protein degradation. Protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay (#P0012, Beyotime), and 20 μg of protein per lane was subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (#P0015, Beyotime) on a 10% gel under standard conditions (constant voltage). The proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (#IPVH00010, Millipore, Bedford, MA) using a wet transfer method at 100 V for 1 hour. Prior to transfer, the PVDF membranes were pre-activated with methanol as per the manufacturer's instructions. The membranes were then blocked with 5% skimmed milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline, 0.05% Tween 20) (#BL602A, Biosharp) for 2 hours and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The primary antibody used was TIGIT (E5Y1W, XP® Rabbit mAb #99567, diluted 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology). Following three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. β-actin served as a loading control, and protein bands were normalized to β-actin levels. Proteins were analyzed using Quantity One software (Version 4.5.2, Bio-Rad, USA). Protein bands were detected with an ECL substrate (#34579, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and images were captured using a Chemi-Doc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, USA).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was utilized to analyze mRNA expression of TIGIT in an ESCC cell line. Transfected cells were collected, the supernatant was removed, and they were washed twice with PBS. Total RNA was extracted from the cells according to the reagent kit's instructions and subsequently reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA). The resulting cDNA was used as a template to prepare a 20 µL qPCR reaction mixture comprising 0.2 µL of cDNA, 0.8 µL of forward and reverse primers, 10 µL of 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Mix, and adjusted to 20 µL with ddH2O. The Applied Biosystems 7500 real-time PCR System (ABI, CA, USA) was employed to measure levels of KESY-30-GAPDH and KESY-30-TIGIT in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. All results were standardized using KESY-30-GAPDH as an internal reference, and the data were normalized employing the 2-∆∆Ct method. Transwell migration and invasion assays

Transwell migration and invasion assays were performed using 24-well Transwell permeable inserts (#353097, BD). For the invasion assay, Transwell chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel matrix (#354234, Corning, USA). Transfected cells (20,000 cells per chamber) were collected, starved for 24 hours, and then inoculated into the upper chamber with 200 µL of serum-free medium. Subsequently, 500 µL of complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) was added to the lower chamber. Following a 48-hour incubation period, the chamber insert was removed, and non-migrated cells were wiped from the upper side. The chamber insert was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (600 µL) for 25 minutes and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (600 µL, WB0026, Dawen Biotec) for 10 minutes. The migrated and invasive cells were observed and counted using an Olympus inverted microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Flow cytometric analysis

In accordance with the cell cycle analysis kit instructions (MULTI SCIENCES, CCS012), 5 × 10^5 transfected cells were collected and treated with 1 mL of DNA staining solution and 10 µL of permeabilization solution after washing. The mixture was vortexed for 5 seconds to ensure thorough mixing and then incubated for 30 minutes before immediate analysis on a flow cytometer. The flow cytometer used was a SONY SP6800Z, and 100,000 events were acquired for each sample. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software. For apoptosis detection, as directed by the apoptosis detection kit (P-CA-201, Pricella), transfected cells were washed and counted, and 2.5 × 10^5 resuspended cells were subjected to an additional wash. Cells were then resuspended in 500 µL of diluted 1 × Annexin V Binding Buffer, and 5 µL each of Annexin V-FITC staining solution and PI staining solution were added. The mixture was vortexed to mix thoroughly and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 minutes, followed by immediate analysis using FlowJoVaX software (BD Biosciences).

RNA sequencing and analysis

RNA sequencing and analysis were conducted using the Illumina platform by Berry Genomics. Three RNA samples were collected from each group. Single cDNA libraries were generated from these RNA samples and sequenced using the Illumina platform. Genes with a P-value ≤ 0.05 and a fold change (|FC|) ≥ 1.2 in the 150 bp paired-end reads were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). These DEGs were then subjected to KEGG pathway analysis.

Cell authentication and mycoplasma contamination statement

The KYSE-30 and KYSE-50 cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and matched to the JCRB (Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources) database entries. Mycoplasma testing was performed using the MycoAlert™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) within 3 months prior to experimentation, and both lines tested negative.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed in standardized formats: continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while survival data and proportions are shown as medians with interquartile ranges or as counts with percentages, as applicable. Analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 26, GraphPad Prism 8.0, and R 4.4.2 software. Optimal cutoff values were determined based on median or mean values, classifying the expression levels of CD8, TIM3, PD-L1, LAG3, CD163, and TIGIT into two groups. Pearson’s chi-squared test was employed to analyze the relationship between the expression of CD8, TIM3, PD-L1, LAG3, CD163, and TIGIT and clinicopathologic characteristics. Correlations between the expression of these markers were also analyzed using chi-squared tests. Disease-free survival (DFS) was measured from the date of surgery to tumor recurrence, progression, death, or the last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined from the date of surgery to death or last follow-up. Both DFS and OS were evaluated to present the prognostic value of marker expression and clinicopathologic characteristics using the Kaplan-Meier method and Cox proportional hazards analysis. To address potential overfitting in multivariate models, variables were selected based on their significance in univariate analyses (p < 0.10) and clinical relevance. Interaction terms were included based on biological plausibility and prior research. The robustness and predictive accuracy of the Cox models were evaluated using the validate function from the rms package, which employs bootstrap resampling (B = 200) to correct for overfitting and provides a concordance index (C-index) to measure the model's ability to discriminate between patients with different survival outcomes. The C-index ranges from 0.5 (no discrimination) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination), with higher values indicating better predictive performance. A heatmap was utilized to visualize the correlation of variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The proportional hazards assumption was formally tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed by calculating the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with values < 5.0 considered acceptable.

Significant associations were identified between immune marker expression and clinicopathological characteristics. High PD-L1 expression correlated with increased tumor invasion depth and advanced clinical stage (both p = 0.028). Elevated LAG3 expression was linked to vascular carcinomatosis (p = 0.027). Although high TIM3 expression was significantly associated with perineural invasion (p = 0.006), high PD-L1 (p = 0.060) and LAG3 (p = 0.090) expression showed only non-significant trends in the same direction.

Inter-marker correlations are illustrated in Fig. 1J-K. CD8 expression significantly correlated with TIM3 (p < 0.001), PD-L1 (p = 0.010), and LAG3 (p = 0.038), but not with CD163 (p = 0.125) or TIGIT (p = 0.146). TIM3 exhibited strong positive associations with PD-L1, LAG3, and CD163 (all p < 0.001), but had a weaker, non-significant correlation with TIGIT (p = 0.345). PD-L1 was strongly linked to LAG3 and CD163 (both p < 0.001), but not to TIGIT (p = 0.690). LAG3 correlated with CD163 (p < 0.001), but not with TIGIT (p = 0.786), and CD163 showed no significant association with TIGIT (p = 0.713). Collectively, these findings reveal a complex network of immune biomarkers within the tumor microenvironment.

Analysis of biomarker levels in the tumor microenvironment and their impact on prognosis

Significant associations were identified between specific biomarkers and survival outcomes. Patients with elevated PD-L1 levels had a significantly shorter median disease-free survival (mDFS) compared to those with lower levels (mDFS: 14.1 months vs. 19.8 months, P = 0.049) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, higher CD163 levels were associated with reduced mDFS (11.1 months vs. 19.8 months, P = 0.028) (Fig. 2B), as were higher TIGIT levels (13.8 months vs. 19.3 months, P = 0.047) (Fig. 2C). To assess the expression of various markers in the immune microenvironment, we quantified the relative expression levels of each marker in CD8-positive cells (cytotoxic T cells) and analyzed the correlations between DFS and OS, these relative expression levels, and other clinical characteristics (Table S1). Univariate analysis revealed that DFS was significantly associated with tumor location, differentiation, and TIGIT expression (HR, 2.9; 95% CI, 1.3 - 6.7; P = 0.010) (Fig. S2). There was also a slight correlation with clinical stage, vascular tumor thrombus, and CD8+LAG3+, PD-L1+TIGIT+ expression. However, in the univariate analysis of OS, high tumor differentiation and low CD163 expression emerged as adverse prognostic factors for OS. In multivariate analysis, differentiation, lymph node metastasis, CD163 expression, and CD163 and TIGIT co-expression levels were confirmed as independent prognostic factors for OS (TABLE S2, Fig. S3). The C-index for the DFS model was 0.701 and the C-index for the OS model was 0.647, indicating moderate predictive accuracy. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for the OS model (global Schoenfeld test p = 0.55) and marginally significant for the DFS model (p = 0.021), with no substantial time-dependent trends observed in individual plots (Fig. S7-10). Multicollinearity was negligible in the OS model (all VIF < 2.0). In the DFS full model, interaction terms showed elevated VIF due to structural correlation with main effects (e.g., CD163 × TIGIT: VIF = 14.8); a main-effects-only model confirmed that core predictors were free from harmful multicollinearity (all VIF < 3.0). Bootstrap resampling demonstrated acceptable stability (OS corrected C-index = 0.627; DFS corrected C-index = 0.569).

Validation of the TIGIT/PanCK ratio as a prognostic biomarker

To further validate the prognostic utility of tumor-cell-intrinsic TIGIT expression, we examined the TIGIT-to-PANCK ratio. Patients with higher TIGIT-to-PANCK ratios exhibited significantly shorter mDFS compared to those with lower ratios (mDFS: 10.7 months vs. 19.8 months, P=0.002) (Fig. S4A). Similarly, elevated TIGIT-to-PANCK ratios were associated with inferior OS (mOS: 15.0 months vs. 31.1 months, P<0.001 (Fig. S4B).

Analysis of TIGIT expression in cytotoxic T cells and its prognostic implications

In our comprehensive analysis of the immune microenvironment within cytotoxic T cells, we identified several key biomarkers that significantly influence patient prognosis. In the cytotoxic T-cell compartment, higher TIGIT expression was also associated with significantly shorter mDFS (13.3 months vs. 25.2 months, P=0.007) (Fig. S5A). This finding underscores the critical potential of TIGIT as a biomarker for predicting disease recurrence and progression.

Furthermore, the co-expression of PD-L1 and TIGIT (PD-L1+TIGIT+) emerged as a significant predictor of improved disease-free survival, with patients exhibiting higher levels of this co-expression experiencing an mDFS of 19.8 months compared to 39.1 months for those with lower levels (P = 0.050) (Fig. S5B). This suggests that the combined assessment of TIGIT and PD-L1 expression in cytotoxic T cells could enhance the accuracy of prognostic predictions and may identify patients more likely to benefit from targeted immunotherapies.

Conversely, higher CD163 expression levels were associated with extended overall survival (mOS: 45.3 months vs. 29.7 months, P = 0.033) (FIG. S5C), indicating a potential protective role of this biomarker in patient outcomes.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the relationship between the expression of CD8, TIM3, PD-L1, LAG3, CD163, and TIGIT and the clinicopathological features of resectable ESCC patients. We found that elevated expression of PD-L1, CD163, and TIGIT significantly correlated with reduced DFS, and high TIGIT expression was associated with decreased OS within the tumor microenvironment (TME). These findings suggest that PD-L1, CD163, and TIGIT may serve as valuable markers for the tumor immune microenvironment, with TIGIT emerging as a potential prognostic biomarker and functional regulator in the ESCC TME. Our study provides novel insights into the immune landscape of ESCC, although further validation is required to establish causal relationships.

Consistent with established epidemiological features of ESCC[27,28], the majority of our patients were male (69.6%) and had histories of smoking (47.5%) and alcohol consumption (73.9%). Notably, PD-L1 overexpression correlated with advanced tumor invasion depth and clinical stage, while LAG3 was associated with vascular carcinomatosis and TIM3 with perineural invasion. These findings suggest that specific immune checkpoints drive distinct invasive phenotypes, collectively fostering an immunosuppressive TME[29]. The strong inter-marker correlations (TIM3-PD-L1-CD163 network, p<0.001) further indicate coordinated immune evasion mechanisms in ESCC.

The association between PD-L1 expression and prognosis in ESCC remains controversial. While meta-analyses associate high PD-L1 with inferior survival[[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]], our data revealed that high PD-L1 expression correlated with shortened DFS, though not with OS. This aligns with its dual identity as both an adaptive resistance marker and a factor associated with tumor progression[36]. Importantly, while high PD-L1 expression showed a trend approaching statistical significance for adverse prognosis in univariate DFS analysis, it did not retain independent prognostic value in the multivariate model, underscoring its complex interplay within the broader immunosuppressive network.

High tumor-associated CD163 correlated with reduced DFS, consistent with the M2-TAM promotion of angiogenesis and metastasis[37,38]. Paradoxically, elevated CD163 within cytotoxic T-cells was associated with improved OS. This may reflect distinct biological contexts: tumor-infiltrating CD163+ macrophages typically signify immunosuppression, while CD163 expression on activated T-cells might indicate phagocytic activity or engagement with hemoglobin-haptoglobin complexes, potentially conferring cytoprotective effects under oxidative stress.

TIGIT, an inhibitory immune checkpoint receptor, plays a critical role in immune regulation and is a potential target for novel immunotherapies[39,40]. Interestingly, unlike previous studies, TIGIT is not only expressed on T cells and NK cells but is also highly expressed in ESCC tumor cells and cell lines. TIGIT overexpression has been observed in various cancers, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), melanoma, and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC)[23,39,41]. TIGIT+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in HNSCC were highly correlated with other immune checkpoint markers, especially PD-1[42], and similar results have been found in melanoma and glioblastoma[23,43]. Accumulation of TIGIT+ TILs in colorectal cancer is associated with poor prognosis and recurrence[44]. Our findings suggest that TIGIT acts as both a prognostic biomarker and a functional regulator within the TME. This finding aligns with prior research identifying AIF1 as a novel prognostic gene in the TME and a potential regulator of immune cell infiltration correlating with TIGIT expression in esophageal cancer[45]. Elevated TIGIT expression predicted inferior OS in our analysis, and the TIGIT/PANCK ratio provided additional prognostic stratification, validating its robustness. Functionally, high TIGIT expression within the CD8+ T-cell compartment was linked to reduced DFS, confirming its role in promoting T-cell exhaustion. Additionally, the PD-L1+TIGIT+ co-expression signature in CD8+ T-cells predicted reduced DFS, highlighting synergistic immune evasion. This provides strong rationale for dual checkpoint blockade, supported by the recent SKYSCRAPER-01 trial, where anti-TIGIT/anti-PD-L1 therapy improved outcomes in solid tumors[46]. Furthermore, the latest results from the SKYSCRAPER-08 trial indicate that tiragolumab (anti-TIGIT antibody) combined with atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1 antibody) and chemotherapy as first-line therapy benefits patients with unresectable ESCC, consistent with our findings[47]. Importantly, if TIGIT possesses tumor-cell-intrinsic pro-tumorigenic functions beyond its classical immune checkpoint activity, anti-TIGIT antibodies such as tiragolumab may achieve therapeutic efficacy through dual mechanisms: (i) reactivating anti-tumor immunity via checkpoint blockade, and (ii) directly disrupting tumor-cell-intrinsic TIGIT signaling. This "double-hit" hypothesis provides a rationale for stratifying patients based on tumor-cell TIGIT expression in future clinical trials.

Beyond its established role in T-cell exhaustion, we identified tumor-autonomous pro-metastatic functions of TIGIT. For the first time, through siRNA-mediated knockdown of TIGIT, we demonstrated its key roles in regulating cell migration, invasion, proliferation, and cell cycle progression in ESCC. TIGIT knockdown significantly inhibited the migration, invasion, and proliferation abilities of ESCC cells.

RNA sequencing implicated cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions and the JAK-STAT signaling axis as potentially involved pathways. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require direct biochemical validation (e.g., phosphorylated STAT1/3 immunoblotting, JAK inhibitor assays) before mechanistic conclusions can be drawn. As an immune checkpoint molecule, previous research has mainly focused on TIGIT's role in mediating immune escape by inhibiting T-cell activity[48,49] . However, this study found that TIGIT directly regulates malignant phenotypes in ESCC cells, suggesting a potential non-immune-dependent pro-carcinogenic role. For example, the significant inhibition of cell migration and invasion following TIGIT knockdown resembles mechanisms reported by Liu et al., where TIGIT regulates tumor cell metastasis through integrin signaling by binding to its ligand CD155, thereby affecting the integrin/FAK pathways and promoting metastasis[50,51]. Notably, single-cell atlases of brain metastases confirm that TIGIT+ cancer cells drive invasive niches via CD155-PVR interactions[52]. Regarding the cell cycle phenotype, the decrease in the G0/G1 phase proportion and the concurrent rise in S and G2/M phases following TIGIT knockdown[53] may reflect several biological possibilities: TIGIT knockdown accelerates cell cycle processes entry into S phase and subsequent progression to G2/M; DNA replication stress or repair defects causing cells to stall at S or G2/M checkpoints[54,55]. Moreover, glucose-restricted tumor microenvironments may amplify TIGIT signaling through exosomal factors like TRAIL, reshaping pre-metastatic microenvironments[56]. We acknowledge that distinguishing between these possibilities requires complementary analyses such as EdU incorporation kinetics, γH2AX staining for replication stress, and Cyclin/CDK profiling, which were not performed in this study.

Our study had limitations. Firstly, it was a retrospective, single-center study. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to accurately quantify the expression and evaluate the prognostic value of TIME-related markers, including TIGIT in resectable ESCC. Secondly, our functional experiments were limited to siRNA-mediated knockdown in two ESCC cell lines without complementary rescue experiments, gain-of-function studies, or in vivo validation. Therefore, while our data suggest that TIGIT modulates tumor-associated cellular behaviors, we cannot conclude that TIGIT is an oncogenic driver. Future research should include TIGIT overexpression rescue assays, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout, and xenograft or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models to validate these findings comprehensively. Thirdly, the mechanistic basis of TIGIT-mediated effects remains incompletely elucidated. Our RNA-sequencing data implicated cytokine-receptor interactions and the JAK-STAT signaling axis, but these observations are hypothesis-generating. Direct biochemical validation—including phosphorylated STAT1/3 analysis, JAK inhibitor rescue experiments, CD155 blocking studies, and reporter assays—is required to establish causal mechanistic links. Additionally, the specific effector molecules within the "cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction" pathway regulated by TIGIT remain to be identified. Furthermore, whether TIGIT synergizes with other immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PD-1 or CD96) to affect ESCC progression requires further investigation. Finally, the biological function of TIME-related markers in ESCC, particularly the CD155/TIGIT pathway, has not been fully elucidated and warrants dedicated mechanistic studies.

Conclusion

In this study, we identify TIGIT, PD-L1, and CD163 expression as significant adverse prognostic factors for DFS, with TIGIT emerging as a key independent predictor for OS. Notably, TIGIT was expressed not only in immune cells but also in ESCC tumor cells, underscoring its potential as both a biomarker and therapeutic target in ESCC. Functional assays further revealed that TIGIT regulates tumor cell migration, invasion, and proliferation, and alters cell cycle distribution, suggesting potential non-immune-dependent roles in ESCC biology that warrant further mechanistic validation.
