Why Zeroth-Order Adaptation May Forget Less: A Randomized Shaping Theory
Yao Shu, Jian Mu, Zhongxiang Dai

Abstract
Continual learning requires new-task adaptation without damaging previously acquired capabilities. Recent forward-pass and zeroth-order (ZO) results show that low-query adaptation may retain better than first-order (FO) descent, but the usual view of ZO as noisy FO estimation does not explain why. We give a local randomized gradient-shaping analysis: finite differences expose a raw shape that is mean-aligned with FO, while the norm-matched comparator fixes the expected squared adaptation norm. Under this controlled comparison, forgetting is controlled by how the adaptation shape exposes retention curvature. For norm-matched ZO, the expected shaped retention curvature obeys an exact identity that preserves the isotropic retention floor while contracting only the anisotropic component. Projecting this identity onto the incoming gradient yields the observable FO–ZO quadratic forgetting gap: ZO improves mean forgetting precisely when the FO direction has above-average retention curvature, by a query-dependent fraction of that curvature excess. A practical finite-query accounting separates the mean mechanism from one-batch sampling and smoothing perturbations. This mechanism also yields an algorithmic transfer: RISE ( Retention-Aware Isotropic Shaping for Exact-Gradient Adaptation ) applies the calibrated ZO shape to exact FO gradients inside parameter blocks. The resulting stability–plasticity tradeoff is explicit: randomized shaping may reduce the retention exposure paid by FO, exact gradients remove finite-smoothing bias from finite-difference ZO, and blockwise sampling supplies many local shaping directions after one gradient computation. The blockwise analysis separates mean-step damage from centered random exposure, showing how block-diagonal curvature, cross-block coupling, and local shaping scores indicate where this exact-gradient transfer is most likely to be visible.

Introduction

Continual learning asks a model to absorb new tasks without erasing the
capabilities learned from previous ones. This tension is acute when large models
are repeatedly adapted across domains, users, and deployment conditions: even an
accurate new-task gradient can move parameters through locally fragile
historical-loss directions. Classical methods address this risk with explicit
retention channels: distillation in Learning without Forgetting

[
16
]
, Fisher-weighted regularization in EWC

[
13
]
, exemplar replay in iCaRL

[
22
]
, and gradient constraints in GEM and A-GEM

[
18
,
2
]
. Recent forward-pass and
zeroth-order (ZO) continual-learning work changes the baseline: ZeroFlow

[
6
]
and forward-only adaptation

[
3
]
show that forward passes can mitigate forgetting,
while ZO-FC reports a ZO/FO stability–plasticity tradeoff in adapter/head
training
[
30
]
. MeZO establishes forward-pass language-model
fine-tuning
[
19
]
, and the ZO-LLM benchmark broadens the
large-model evidence for memory-efficient ZO adaptation

[

]
.
These results make the empirical phenomenon visible, but they do not identify
the scale-controlled local mechanism. This paper studies that mechanism after
the incoming signal and adaptation scale are matched; Appendix
A

gives the detailed positioning.

Viewing ZO only as noisy FO estimation misses the object seen by forgetting.
Estimation accuracy concerns the incoming gradient; forgetting is governed by
the induced displacement and the retention curvature it exposes. We therefore
compare FO and ZO through
randomized gradient shaping
: a random operator
acts on the incoming gradient before local adaptation. Sec.
3

separates the practical finite-difference estimator into raw mean alignment,
smoothing error, norm matching, and the mean shrinkage induced by matching
adaptation scale. With these confounders separated, Sec.
4
shows
that calibrated ZO preserves the isotropic part of retention curvature while
contracting the anisotropic part. Projecting this contraction onto the incoming
gradient gives a sign-sensitive gap: ZO reduces mean quadratic forgetting
exactly when the FO direction crosses above-average retention curvature, and it
can hurt in the opposite regime. The theory therefore describes a
query-controlled move toward isotropic exposure, not a uniform ZO-dominates-FO
result.

RISE (
Retention-Aware Isotropic Shaping for Exact-Gradient Adaptation
) transfers this mechanism to exact-gradient adaptation, as
developed in Sec.
5
. It computes the FO gradient by
backpropagation, removing finite-difference smoothing bias; applies the
calibrated norm-matched ZO shape, importing the retention-curvature mixing that
may lower FO forgetting in favorable curvature-gap regimes; and applies the
shape inside parameter blocks, turning a small number of random directions per
block into many local shaping samples after one gradient computation. These
pieces define a stability–plasticity tradeoff: RISE keeps the exact-gradient
plasticity signal missing from raw finite-difference ZO, while norm matching
still shrinks the mean direction relative to plain FO. The same controls shape
the experiments in Sec.
6
: retention gains from
exact-gradient shape transfer must be separated from smaller steps, mean
scaling, generic covariance noise, and matched-budget optimization effects.

This paper makes four contributions. First, it formulates local forgetting as
shaped adaptation: after the incoming direction is fixed, FO and ZO can be
compared by the quadratic forgetting damage of their induced displacements under
matched expected squared adaptation norm. Second, it calibrates finite-difference
ZO for this comparison by separating raw mean alignment, finite-smoothing
residuals, raw norm inflation, norm-matched scaling, and the resulting mean
shrinkage. Third, it proves the main mechanism: norm-matched ZO preserves the
isotropic retention floor, contracts anisotropic retention curvature, and yields
an exact FO–ZO quadratic-forgetting gap governed by the directional
retention-curvature excess. Fourth, it transfers the calibrated shape to exact
gradients through RISE, and evaluates the resulting stability–plasticity
tradeoff with controlled mechanism tests and end-to-end continual-learning
benchmarks. The first step is therefore to make precise what historical loss
sees during a local adaptation.

2
Local Forgetting as Shaped Adaptation

The mechanism above needs a comparison object that is independent of how an
incoming direction was estimated. Historical loss observes the displacement
caused by adaptation, not whether that displacement came from backpropagation or
finite differences. We therefore fix one adaptation stage and compare FO and ZO
at the level where forgetting is measured: the local adaptation displacement.
This local view removes trajectory confounders such as data order, optimizer
state, higher-order drift, and learning-rate schedules. The displacement may be
one optimizer step or a short net adaptation, as long as it stays in the Taylor
neighborhood of the historical loss. At that level, forgetting reduces to a
quadratic damage functional, and FO–ZO comparison becomes a question about the
retention curvature exposed by each induced displacement.

2.1
From Historical Loss to Quadratic Damage

Fix an adaptation stage and suppress its index. Let
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

d\geq 2
be the parameter before adapting to the incoming loss
f
, and let

\mathcal{L}
be the historical loss over past data or tasks. For a local
adaptation displacement
{\bm{\Delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, write

{\bm{\theta}}^{+}={\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\Delta}}
and define induced forgetting as

\mathfrak{F}({\bm{\Delta}})\triangleq\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}^{+})-\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\Delta}})-\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}).

(1)

This definition does not specify the optimizer. It keeps only what the
historical loss observes: the displacement and the retention curvature exposed
by that displacement. In the
approximately consolidated regime, assume

\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})\right\|\leq\varepsilon
for small

\varepsilon\geq 0
. When the quadratic object is the true Taylor term, write

{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}\triangleq\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}})
and assume the
historical-loss Hessian is locally Lipschitz in the Taylor neighborhood. Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}({\bm{\Delta}})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{\Delta}}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\Delta}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}{\bm{\Delta}}+\mathcal{O}(\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|^{3})

(2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\Delta}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}{\bm{\Delta}}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|+\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\right\|^{3}).

Thus, up to lower-order terms, the true Taylor contribution is governed by the
historical-loss Hessian along the adaptation direction. The main theory writes

{\mathbf{H}}
for the retention curvature used in the quadratic analysis: it may be

{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}
in the true-Hessian reading, or a fixed symmetric
retention proxy such as a Fisher, Gauss–Newton, or empirical sensitivity matrix.
In the proxy reading, the next display is a surrogate damage functional rather
than the Taylor equality in (
2
). The resulting
comparison object is

\mathcal{Q}({\bm{\Delta}})\triangleq\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\Delta}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\bm{\Delta}}.

(3)

Introducing
\mathcal{Q}
does not turn continual learning into a
retention-only objective. Standard methods also separate stability from
plasticity by protecting past knowledge with regularizers such as EWC

[
13
]
, distillation as in Learning without
Forgetting
[
16
]
, replay as in iCaRL

[
22
]
, or gradient constraints as in GEM and A-GEM

[
18
,
2
]
, while another term drives
the incoming task.
The present analysis is narrower: once the incoming-loss direction is fixed, the
local question is whether the resulting adaptation shape lowers retention cost.
This scope avoids the trivial zero-displacement solution of minimizing

\mathcal{Q}({\bm{\Delta}})
over arbitrary displacements. Plasticity enters as a
control: the setup fixes the incoming direction, and
Section
3
separates the raw ZO first-order signal from
the norm-matched squared adaptation scale.

2.2
Adaptation Shape as the Comparison Object

Once forgetting is reduced to
\mathcal{Q}({\bm{\Delta}})
, the object to compare is
the displacement placed inside this quadratic form, not the procedure that
produced the direction. Let

{\bm{g}}\triangleq\nabla f({\bm{\theta}})\neq{\bm{0}}
be the incoming-loss gradient. A
shape operator
{\mathbf{P}}
transforms this gradient before it becomes a local
adaptation. With learning rate
\eta>0
,

{\bm{\Delta}}=-\eta{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}},\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{{\mathbf{P}}}\triangleq\mathcal{Q}({\bm{\Delta}})=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}{\bm{g}}^{\top}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}.

(4)

Here
\mathcal{Q}_{{\mathbf{P}}}
is the forgetting damage induced by
{\mathbf{P}}
, and

{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}
is the retention curvature seen by the shaped incoming
gradient. We call this operator-level transformation
shaping
, and call it

randomized shaping
when
{\mathbf{P}}
is random. At this stage
{\mathbf{P}}={\mathbf{I}}

gives the FO reference; ZO enters in Section
3
as a
calibrated randomized shaping distribution.

The local comparison also removes scale as an explanation. Because

\mathcal{Q}
is quadratic, a method could reduce forgetting merely by producing
a smaller displacement. We therefore match expected squared adaptation norm, but
do not solve an unrestricted minimization over arbitrary curvature-aware
conditioners. If
{\mathbf{P}}
could be chosen with full knowledge of

{\mathbf{H}}
, the best rule would steer
{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}
toward low-curvature eigenspaces,
which is not the ZO family calibrated in Sec.
3
.
Instead, for a specified curvature-agnostic family
\mathcal{C}
of shaping
rules, the theory compares

\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{{\mathbf{P}}}\right]:{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathcal{C},\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\right]=\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\right\}.

(5)

For this paper,
\mathcal{C}
contains the FO reference and the ZO shaping
rules calibrated in Section
3
. The expectation is over
shaping randomness and is vacuous for deterministic FO. The norm constraint
prevents smaller expected squared adaptation from being mistaken for a retention
mechanism. ZO therefore needs a rule with two properties: a raw form that aligns
the incoming-loss signal and a normalized form that fits the norm-matched
comparison. Sec.
3
provides that calibration.

Assumption 2.1

(Local comparison)
.

The local comparison in (
5
) relies on three
conditions: (i)
{\bm{g}}\neq{\bm{0}}
;
(ii) the stage is in the consolidated local regime, so the first-order and
higher-order terms in (
2
) are lower-order at the
local displacement scale considered; and (iii)
{\mathbf{H}}
is a fixed symmetric
retention curvature or proxy used in
\mathcal{Q}
. The Gaussian moment
identities in Section
4
require only this symmetry. Whenever

\mathcal{Q}
is interpreted as nonnegative forgetting damage, and in the
curvature-agnostic benchmark and finite-query deviation bound, we additionally
assume
{\mathbf{H}}\succeq 0
and
\tr({\mathbf{H}})>0
.

This assumption turns forgetting into a displacement-level comparison, but
(
5
) still needs a ZO rule that supplies both the
incoming signal and the matched scale. Finite differences provide that rule:
their raw shape carries the leading first-order signal, and their normalized
shape fits the norm-matched comparison.

3
Calibrating the ZO Shape

The shaped-adaptation comparison in (
5
) uses a
common local form

-\eta{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}
, but practical ZO arrives through finite differences rather than
an explicit shape operator. The two-point estimator admits the needed
separation: a raw ZO shape carrying the leading first-order signal, a
finite-smoothing residual that remains a controlled perturbation, and a
norm-matched shape whose expected squared adaptation norm is comparable with FO.

3.1
From Finite Differences to Raw ZO Shape

The random directions used by the two-point ZO estimator define the raw shape.
For query count
q
, draw

{\mathbf{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{z}}_{q}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\mathbf{I}})

independently of
{\bm{g}}
and
{\mathbf{H}}
; all expectations below are over these
directions. With smoothing radius
\mu>0
, the practical estimator and its
zero-smoothing raw shape are

\widehat{{\bm{g}}}_{\mu}\triangleq q^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{q}\frac{f({\bm{\theta}}+\mu{\mathbf{z}}_{r})-f({\bm{\theta}}-\mu{\mathbf{z}}_{r})}{2\mu}\,{\mathbf{z}}_{r},\qquad{\mathbf{Z}}\triangleq q^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{q}{\mathbf{z}}_{r}{\mathbf{z}}_{r}^{\top}.

(6)

The matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}
is the leading zero-smoothing shape induced by the sampled
directions. Taylor expansion along the queried line segments separates the
practical estimator into this raw shape applied to the incoming gradient plus a
finite-smoothing residual. Because the same estimator will later be inserted
into a quadratic damage functional, the residual is controlled at both first and
second moments: the first moment justifies the estimator-level reduction to

{\mathbf{Z}}{\bm{g}}
, and the second moment controls its contribution to forgetting
damage.

Lemma 3.1

(Finite-Smoothing Bridge)
.

Let
f
be
L
-smooth on the queried line segments. Then there exists a
remainder vector

{\bm{r}}_{\mu}
such that

\widehat{{\bm{g}}}_{\mu}={\mathbf{Z}}{\bm{g}}+{\bm{r}}_{\mu},\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\bm{r}}_{\mu}\right\|\right]=\mathcal{O}(L\mu d^{3/2}),\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\bm{r}}_{\mu}\right\|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(L^{2}\mu^{2}d^{3}).

(7)

Lemma
3.1
identifies
{\mathbf{Z}}{\bm{g}}
as the leading shaped
incoming direction and keeps finite smoothing outside the zero-smoothing
curvature mechanism. The raw shape still requires two calibrations: preservation
of the incoming signal in expectation and a squared adaptation norm comparable
with FO.

3.2
From Raw Alignment to a Norm-Matched Comparator

Raw ZO preserves the leading mean but inflates the quadratic scale. The fair
forgetting comparator is therefore not the raw adaptation
{\mathbf{Z}}{\bm{g}}
itself, but
the normalized shape
{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}=\kappa^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z}}{\bm{g}}
. This normalization matches
the expected squared adaptation norm with FO and makes the induced mean
shrinkage explicit.

Lemma 3.2

(Raw Alignment, Norm Inflation, and Norm Matching)
.

For the raw ZO shape
{\mathbf{Z}}
in (
6
),

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{Z}}\right]={\mathbf{I}},\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{Z}}{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\right]=\kappa\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2},\qquad\kappa=\frac{q+d+1}{q}.

(8)

For the norm-matched shape
{\mathbf{P}}=\kappa^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z}}
,

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}]=\sqrt{\frac{q}{q+d+1}}\,{\bm{g}},\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\right]=\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}.

(9)

Lemma
3.2
separates the two calibration roles. The identity

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{Z}}]={\mathbf{I}}
says that raw ZO preserves the FO incoming signal in the
zero-smoothing leading term. The inflation factor

\kappa=(q+d+1)/q
comes from the Wishart-type fourth moment

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{Z}}^{2}]=\kappa{\mathbf{I}}
, so it is not a tunable scale choice. Because raw ZO
inflates
\mathbb{E}[\left\|{\mathbf{Z}}{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}]
, it cannot be the fair quadratic-forgetting
comparator. The comparator is instead
{\mathbf{P}}=\kappa^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z}}
, which matches
the FO squared norm exactly but shrinks the mean to

\sqrt{q/(q+d+1)}\,{\bm{g}}
. This asymmetry is unavoidable: for any random shaped
direction
{\mathbf{x}}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}\right]=\left\|\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}]\right\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}]\right\|^{2}\right]
, so exact mean alignment and exact
expected squared-norm matching force zero shaping variance. Scaled-FO and
covariance-matched noise controls are therefore needed before attributing a
retention change to curvature shaping.

After this calibration, the curvature theory compares only two shape operators:

{\mathbf{P}}={\mathbf{I}}\quad\text{(FO)},\qquad{\mathbf{P}}=\kappa^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z}}\quad\text{(ZO)}.

(10)

Here
{\mathbf{Z}}
justifies the raw first-order signal, while

{\mathbf{P}}=\kappa^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z}}
is the norm-matched ZO shape used in the retention
comparison. The finite-smoothing residual remains outside the zero-smoothing
curvature mechanism and reappears as the additive perturbation in
(
19
) when the mean gap is translated to a
practical finite-difference batch. With raw alignment, norm inflation,
norm-matched mean shrinkage, and expected squared norm separated, the
zero-smoothing retention mechanism reduces to the expected shaped retention
curvature defined in (
11
).

4
Why ZO May Forget Less: Randomized Gradient Shaping

The calibration in Sec.
3
leaves FO and norm-matched ZO
on a common local scale:
raw ZO carries the first-order signal, finite smoothing is separated as a
remainder, and norm matching fixes the expected squared adaptation norm while
making mean shrinkage explicit. The remaining zero-smoothing difference is the
retention curvature exposed by the shaped adaptation.

4.1
Expected Shaped Retention Curvature

For a shaped adaptation
-\eta{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}
, the historical loss sees

{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}
rather than
{\mathbf{H}}
. The expectation of this induced
curvature matrix is the central object:

\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}_{q}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}\right],\qquad\bar{\lambda}\triangleq\frac{\tr({\mathbf{H}})}{d}.

(11)

Here
\bar{\lambda}
is the average retention curvature, and
q
is the number of ZO
directions used by the norm-matched shape in (
10
). If the
retention curvature were known and positive definite, a whitening-like reference

{\mathbf{P}}_{\star}=\sqrt{\bar{\lambda}}\,{\mathbf{H}}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{O}}
with
{\mathbf{O}}^{\top}{\mathbf{O}}={\mathbf{I}}
would
realize
{\mathbf{P}}_{\star}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}_{\star}=\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}
. This full-information
reference only names the isotropic target; the actual ZO shape has no access to
the eigensystem of
{\mathbf{H}}
. Finite-query ZO can now be evaluated by whether it
moves the exposed retention curvature toward this isotropic target without
knowing
{\mathbf{H}}
.

Theorem 4.1

(Expected Curvature Identity for Norm-Matched ZO)
.

For the norm-matched ZO shape in (
10
), let

\tau=d/(q+d+1)
. For any fixed symmetric
{\mathbf{H}}
,

\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}_{q}=(1-\tau){\mathbf{H}}+\tau\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}.

(12)

Theorem
4.1
is the mechanism backbone. It says that
norm-matched ZO does not uniformly shrink retention curvature. Instead, it
leaves the isotropic component
\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}
unchanged and multiplies only the
traceless anisotropic component
{\mathbf{H}}-\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}
by
1-\tau
. The standard
normal direction distribution supplies this closed-form moment identity; no
symmetry of the neural network, loss landscape, or optimizer state is assumed.
The query
count enters only through
\tau=d/(q+d+1)
: smaller
q
gives stronger
mixing toward isotropic curvature, while larger
q
approaches the FO curvature.

Corollary 4.2

(Spectral Equalization)
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
be the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{H}}
, and let

\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{d}
be the eigenvalues of

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}\right]
. For the norm-matched ZO shape in
(
10
) and any fixed symmetric
{\mathbf{H}}
,

\lambda_{i}-\lambda^{\prime}_{i}=\tau\left(\lambda_{i}-\bar{\lambda}\right),\qquad\lambda^{\prime}_{i}=(1-\tau)\lambda_{i}+\tau\bar{\lambda},\qquad i=1,\ldots,d.

(13)

Consequently, the mean eigenvalue is fixed and every eigen-deviation from

\bar{\lambda}
is contracted by the factor
1-\tau
.

Corollary
4.2
gives the eigen-level interpretation of the same
mechanism. Every eigenvalue sheds a
\tau
fraction of its signed deviation from
the mean. Large retention-curvature eigenvalues move down, small ones move up,
and the average is preserved, so the spectrum becomes less anisotropic without
changing its trace.

4.2
Quantifying the FO–ZO Forgetting Reduction

The expected-curvature identity in Theorem
4.1
explains how
norm-matched ZO reshapes retention curvature. Its forgetting effect is the
projection of that shaped curvature onto the fixed incoming gradient. Define the
directional retention curvature

\lambda\triangleq\frac{{\bm{g}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\bm{g}}}{\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}},\qquad\bar{\lambda}=\frac{\tr({\mathbf{H}})}{d}.

(14)

The FO baseline quadratic damage and the norm-matched ZO quadratic damage are

\mathcal{Q}^{\text{FO}}\triangleq\mathcal{Q}(-\eta{\bm{g}})=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\lambda,\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\triangleq\mathcal{Q}(-\eta{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}})=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}{\bm{g}}^{\top}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}.

(15)

These quantities use the same incoming gradient before shaping and matched
expected squared adaptation norm after shaping. Thus the comparison removes
smaller-step explanations, while the explicit mean shrinkage in
(
9
) is handled by scaled-FO controls. Any
remaining mean forgetting reduction comes from the shaped retention curvature in
Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.3

(FO–ZO Forgetting-Reduction Identity)
.

For the norm-matched ZO shape in (
10
), let

\tau=d/(q+d+1)
. For any fixed symmetric
{\mathbf{H}}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\text{FO}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right]=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\tau\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda-\bar{\lambda}\right),\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right]=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\left((1-\tau)\lambda+\tau\bar{\lambda}\right).

(16)

If
\lambda>0
, the corresponding relative reduction is

\frac{\mathcal{Q}^{\text{FO}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right]}{\mathcal{Q}^{\text{FO}}}=\tau\left(1-\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{\lambda}\right).

(17)

When
{\mathbf{H}}\succeq 0
and
\mathcal{Q}
is read as forgetting damage,
Theorem
4.3
gives the promised quantitative comparison:
norm-matched ZO reduces mean quadratic forgetting exactly when the current
gradient points through above-average retention curvature,
\lambda>\bar{\lambda}
.
The amount saved is
(\eta^{2}/2)\tau\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}(\lambda-\bar{\lambda})
, and the
fraction of FO damage removed is
\tau(1-\bar{\lambda}/\lambda)
whenever

\lambda>0
. Equivalently,
\bar{\lambda}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}
is the isotropic forgetting
floor, while

\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}(\lambda-\bar{\lambda})={\bm{g}}^{\top}({\mathbf{H}}-\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}){\bm{g}}
is the signed
directional excess above that floor; ZO shaping leaves the floor unchanged and
attenuates only this excess by the factor
1-\tau
. If
\lambda<\bar{\lambda}
, the
same expression is negative and norm-matched ZO increases the mean local damage,
so the mechanism controls high-curvature exposure by giving up the lucky case in
which FO already moves through below-average retention curvature. The result is
therefore a geometry-dependent risk-control statement, not a uniform dominance
theorem.

Practical finite-query ZO.

Theorem
4.3
is the mean zero-smoothing gap. A practical
finite-difference ZO batch under the same norm-matched calibration adds two
separate perturbations. With

\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mu}\triangleq\kappa^{-1/2}\widehat{{\bm{g}}}_{\mu}
and

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{q,\mu}^{\text{ZO}}\triangleq\mathcal{Q}(-\eta\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mu})
, the accounting is

\displaystyle G_{q}

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathcal{Q}^{\text{FO}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right]=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\tau\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda-\bar{\lambda}\right),

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\text{FO}}-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{q,\mu}^{\text{ZO}}

\displaystyle=G_{q}-\left(\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right]\right)-\left(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{q,\mu}^{\text{ZO}}-\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right).

(19)

Thus the practical ZO gap has the same mechanism signal as
Theorem
4.3
. The two parentheses in
(
19
) are the centered
finite-query deviation and the finite-smoothing perturbation, respectively; the
zero-smoothing case drops the last parenthesis. A realized benefit requires

G_{q}
to dominate both perturbations. Proposition
B.1

and Corollary
B.2
in
Appendix
B
give the corresponding high-probability
one-batch deviation bound and expected finite-smoothing damage perturbation.

4.3
When Curvature Is Unknown: ZO Moves Toward the Blind-Optimal Isotropic Exposure

The fixed-
{\mathbf{H}}
results in
Secs.
4.1
–
4.2
explain the actual FO–ZO
forgetting gap for a given local retention geometry. A complementary scope
question arises when curvature information is absent. Scalar norm matching fixes
only the total squared norm; the full exposure matrix matters once unknown
curvature can choose directions. The benchmark below is retention-only and acts
on second moments, not a joint plasticity–retention optimum under exact mean
constraints. Let
{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}}
,
{\bm{\Delta}}=-\eta{\mathbf{x}}
, and

{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right]
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}({\bm{\Delta}})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\tr\!\left({\mathbf{H}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{\Delta}}{\bm{\Delta}}^{\top}\right]\right)=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\tr({\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{M}}).

(20)

This blind benchmark intentionally ignores higher distributional details and
compares only how a fixed norm budget is exposed to unknown curvature.

To define the curvature-agnostic benchmark, compare exposures under a fixed trace
scale for retention curvature:

\mathcal{H}_{\bar{\lambda}}\triangleq\left\{{\mathbf{H}}\succeq 0:\tr({\mathbf{H}})=d\bar{\lambda}\right\},\qquad\mathcal{M}\triangleq\left\{{\mathbf{M}}\succeq 0:\tr({\mathbf{M}})=\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\right\}.

(21)

Here
\bar{\lambda}
denotes the unknown average curvature scale shared by the
benchmark class, and
\mathcal{M}
is the norm-matched exposure budget

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}\right]=\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}
. If
{\mathbf{H}}
is otherwise
unknown, the worst case places its curvature trace on the most exposed direction
of
{\mathbf{M}}
, so the safest target is the exposure with no preferred direction.

Theorem 4.4

(Curvature-Agnostic Isotropic Benchmark)
.

Fix
\bar{\lambda}>0
and
{\bm{g}}\neq{\bm{0}}
. For any

{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathcal{M}
, let
\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{M}})
denote its largest eigenvalue.
Then

\displaystyle\sup_{{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\bar{\lambda}}}\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\tr({\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{M}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\,d\bar{\lambda}\,\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{M}}),\hskip 18.49988pt

\displaystyle\inf_{{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathcal{M}}\ \sup_{{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\bar{\lambda}}}\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\tr({\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{M}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\bar{\lambda}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}.

(22)

The unique second moment attaining the second value is

{\mathbf{M}}^{\star}=(\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}/d){\mathbf{I}}
.

Theorem
4.4
identifies the retention-only endpoint.
Under no curvature information, the minimax exposure is isotropic. This target
benchmarks exposure after the incoming direction has been fixed; it does not
prescribe a new plasticity objective. Norm-matched ZO then sits between FO’s
rank-one exposure and this isotropic endpoint.

Corollary 4.5

(Norm-Matched ZO Exposure Interpolation)
.

Let
\tau=d/(q+d+1)
,

{\mathbf{M}}^{\text{FO}}\triangleq{\bm{g}}{\bm{g}}^{\top}
, and

{\mathbf{M}}^{\star}=(\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}/d){\mathbf{I}}
. For the norm-matched ZO shape in
(
10
),

{\mathbf{M}}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[({\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}})({\mathbf{P}}{\bm{g}})^{\top}\right]=(1-\tau){\bm{g}}{\bm{g}}^{\top}+\tau\frac{\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}}{d}{\mathbf{I}}=(1-\tau){\mathbf{M}}^{\text{FO}}+\tau{\mathbf{M}}^{\star}.

(23)

Moreover, with

\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\lambda}}({\mathbf{M}})\triangleq\sup_{{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\bar{\lambda}}}\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\tr({\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{M}})
,

\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\lambda}}\!\left({\mathbf{M}}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right)-\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\lambda}}\!\left({\mathbf{M}}^{\star}\right)=(1-\tau)\left[\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\lambda}}\!\left({\mathbf{M}}^{\text{FO}}\right)-\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\lambda}}\!\left({\mathbf{M}}^{\star}\right)\right].

(24)

Corollary
4.5
supplies the curvature-agnostic
interpretation. FO has rank-one exposure, the blind-optimal benchmark is
isotropic, and norm-matched ZO closes exactly a
\tau
-fraction of the
FO-to-optimum exposure gap, leaving

1-\tau=(q+1)/(q+d+1)
. This benchmark explains why randomized shaping is
principled when
{\mathbf{H}}
is unknown, but it does not replace the fixed-
{\mathbf{H}}

forgetting identity in Theorem
4.3
, which governs the actual
FO–ZO gap for a given retention geometry. Figure
1

visualizes the same mechanism in the equivalent shaped-curvature contour view.

5
From Theory to RISE: Exact-Gradient Randomized Shaping

The theory isolates the transferable part of low-query ZO: after the calibration
in Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
, the
retention-relevant operation is a randomized shape applied to a fixed incoming
direction. RISE (
Retention-Aware Isotropic Shaping for Exact-Gradient Adaptation
) transfers this operation to exact-gradient
adaptation. It computes the incoming FO gradient
{\bm{g}}
, replaces it by a
calibrated shaped vector
\widetilde{{\bm{g}}}
, and lets the same base optimizer
execute the adaptation. Thus RISE sits between FO and practical
finite-difference ZO. Relative to FO, it changes second-moment exposure as in
(
23
) under a matched expected squared norm.
Relative to finite-difference ZO, it keeps the exact incoming signal, removes
finite-
\mu
bias from (
7
), and replaces raw norm
inflation with explicit norm matching from (
9
).

RISE uses the same shape blockwise because global low-query shaping has
dimension-dependent deviation (Proposition
B.1
).
For blocks of size
d_{b}
,
q
directions per block give
Bq
local shaping
samples after one backward pass and no function-value queries. The block theory
keeps cross-block curvature in the mean term and treats omitted couplings as a
perturbation when small (Theorem
C.3
;
Proposition
C.4
). Thus RISE acts as a
stability–plasticity wrapper, importing ZO-style curvature mixing into
exact-gradient adaptation at a norm-matched mean-shrinkage cost relative to FO.

For a block partition
{\bm{g}}=({\bm{g}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{g}}_{B})
, draw

{\mathbf{z}}_{b,1},\ldots,{\mathbf{z}}_{b,q}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\mathbf{I}}_{b})

and define, for each
b
,

{\mathbf{Z}}_{b}\triangleq\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}{\mathbf{z}}_{b,i}{\mathbf{z}}_{b,i}^{\top},\qquad\kappa_{b}\triangleq\frac{q+d_{b}+1}{q},\qquad{\mathbf{P}}_{b}\triangleq\kappa_{b}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z}}_{b},\qquad\widetilde{{\bm{g}}}_{b}\triangleq{\mathbf{P}}_{b}{\bm{g}}_{b}.

(25)

By Lemma
3.2
, each block is raw-aligned before normalization and
norm-matched after normalization:

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{Z}}_{b}{\bm{g}}_{b}]={\bm{g}}_{b}
,

\mathbb{E}[\left\|{\mathbf{P}}_{b}{\bm{g}}_{b}\right\|^{2}]=\left\|{\bm{g}}_{b}\right\|^{2}
, and

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{P}}_{b}{\bm{g}}_{b}]=\kappa_{b}^{-1/2}{\bm{g}}_{b}
. The shaped blocks replace the raw
gradient inside the same FO optimizer. Blocks may be tensors, layers, or optimizer groups; Appendix
C

defines the blockwise scores used to interpret ablations.

Input:

Exact gradient
{\bm{g}}
, block partition
\{{\bm{g}}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
, directions per block
q
, base optimizer
Opt

for

each block
b=1,\ldots,B

do

1

Draw
{\mathbf{z}}_{b,1},\ldots,{\mathbf{z}}_{b,q}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\mathbf{I}}_{b})

2

{\mathbf{Z}}_{b}\leftarrow q^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q}{\mathbf{z}}_{b,i}{\mathbf{z}}_{b,i}^{\top}
and
\kappa_{b}\leftarrow(q+d_{b}+1)/q

3

\widetilde{{\bm{g}}}_{b}\leftarrow\kappa_{b}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z}}_{b}{\bm{g}}_{b}

4

Concatenate
\widetilde{{\bm{g}}}\leftarrow(\widetilde{{\bm{g}}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{{\bm{g}}}_{B})

5

Pass
\widetilde{{\bm{g}}}
to
Opt

7
Conclusion

Low-query ZO retention is calibrated randomized shaping: raw ZO supplies the
mean-aligned signal, norm matching fixes squared norm with explicit mean
shrinkage, and the FO–ZO damage gap becomes a query-dependent fraction of
directional curvature excess. RISE transfers this shape to exact gradients as
a blockwise wrapper, yielding a local second-moment stability–plasticity
tradeoff whose global trajectory and selection questions are handled by the
appendix controls and blockwise analyses.

Appendix A
Related Work and Positioning

Continual Learning and Retention Geometry.

Continual-learning methods have long controlled forgetting by restricting how a
new-task adaptation may move the model. Learning without Forgetting preserves
old behavior through distillation
[
16
]
; EWC penalizes movement
in Fisher-important parameters
[
13
]
; iCaRL stores
exemplars for replay
[
22
]
; GEM and A-GEM impose
past-gradient constraints
[
18
,
2
]
;
and OGD and GPM project updates away from past-task subspaces

[
5
,
24
]
. More recent geometric work
makes the same stability concern curvature-aware: AlterSGD searches for flat
continual-learning minima
[
12
]
, SAM penalizes sharp
solutions
[
8
]
, C-Flat adapts flatness control to
continual learning
[
1
]
, and Hessian-eigenvalue analysis links
forgetting to high-curvature directions
[
14
]
. These
methods use explicit retention information to change the objective, gradient, or
training rule. Our analysis isolates a smaller local object that exists before
such retention channels are added: once the incoming-loss direction is fixed,
historical loss only sees the induced adaptation displacement and the retention
curvature exposed by that displacement.

Pretrained-Model and PEFT Continual Adaptation.

The empirical baselines in this paper sit on a newer line that adapts
pretrained representations rather than training continual learners from
scratch. Unified classifier rebalancing is an early class-incremental
classifier baseline
[
10
]
. LoRA supplies the low-rank adapter
parameterization used by many language-model continual-learning pipelines

[
11
]
, while O-LoRA adds orthogonal subspace constraints for
language-model continual learning
[
29
]
. For vision
continual learning with pretrained models, EASE expands subspace ensembles

[
34
]
, and APER emphasizes adaptive pretrained-model
class-incremental learning
[
33
]
. These methods define
strong host settings and baseline rows, but they do not by themselves explain
why a ZO-shaped adaptation should expose less retention curvature than an
ordinary FO displacement under the same norm budget.

Zeroth-Order Optimization and Forward-Pass Adaptation.

Classical ZO optimization estimates descent directions from function values,
from bandit finite differences
[
7
]
to randomized
gradient-free smoothing
[
20
]
. In modern language-model
fine-tuning, MeZO shows that large models can be adapted with forward passes
alone
[
19
]
, and the ZO-LLM benchmark broadens this evidence
across optimizers, model families, and tuning schemes
[
32
]
.
Subsequent variants reduce the estimation burden by selecting sparse parameter
subsets
[
17
]
, using low-rank ZO structures

[
4
]
, adapting layerwise ZO update scales

[
26
]
, or combining ZO and FO optimization

[
25
]
. These works establish the feasibility and systems
value of forward-pass fine-tuning. Their main object, however, is still gradient
estimation or memory-efficient optimization, not the retention curvature exposed
after an incoming gradient has already been fixed.

Forward-Pass and ZO Continual Learning.

The closest empirical continual-learning work moves from memory-efficient ZO to
forgetting itself. ZeroFlow benchmarks gradient-free algorithms for continual
learning and shows that forward passes can be sufficient in several forgetting
settings
[
6
]
; Forward-Only Continual Learning explores
gradient-free adaptation for pretrained models
[
3
]
; and
the ZO-FC study shows that ZO optimization can reduce forgetting beyond memory
savings, identifies a stability–plasticity tradeoff, and optimizes an adapter
with ZO while keeping the classifier/head under FO gradients
[
30
]
.
These papers establish the phenomenon that motivates this work. The open
mechanistic question is what remains after finite-smoothing error, raw-ZO mean
alignment, raw norm inflation, norm-matched mean shrinkage, and expected squared
adaptation norm are separated. We answer that question with a direct
norm-matched FO–ZO forgetting-gap identity, and RISE then transfers the same
shape mechanism to exact gradients.

Appendix B
Practical Finite-Query ZO Gap

The mean zero-smoothing mechanism leaves one implementation question: whether a
single finite-difference batch realizes the same sign and scale. The resulting
realization-level account keeps the two perturbations separate.
Finite-query randomness is a centered sampling deviation around the mean gap,
while finite smoothing is an estimator remainder that perturbs the quadratic
damage.

Proposition B.1

(One-Batch Deviation around the Mean Gap)
.

Let
{\mathbf{Z}}=q^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{q}{\mathbf{z}}_{r}{\mathbf{z}}_{r}^{\top}
with

{\mathbf{z}}_{r}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\mathbf{I}})
,

{\mathbf{P}}=\kappa^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z}}
,
\kappa=(q+d+1)/q
, and
\tau=d/(q+d+1)
.
Assume
{\mathbf{H}}\succeq 0
and let
\lambda_{\max}
denote its largest eigenvalue.
Let

G_{q}=(\eta^{2}/2)\tau\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}(\lambda-\bar{\lambda})
be the mean
forgetting-reduction signal in (
18
), where

\lambda={\bm{g}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\bm{g}}/\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}
and

\bar{\lambda}=\tr({\mathbf{H}})/d
. For

\delta\in(0,1)
, define

\psi_{q}(\delta)\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{d+\log(1/\delta)}{q}}+\frac{d+\log(1/\delta)+1}{q}.

(26)

There is a universal constant
C>0
such that, with probability at least

1-\delta
,

\left|\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\text{FO}}-\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right)-G_{q}\right|=\left|\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right]\right|\leq C\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\lambda_{\max}\left(\psi_{q}(\delta)+\psi_{q}(\delta)^{2}\right).

(27)

Proposition
B.1
turns a sampled ZO batch into a
signal–deviation comparison. The deterministic signal is
G_{q}
, while the
right-hand side of (
27
) is the local
sample-covariance scale multiplied by the largest retention curvature. A
positive mean benefit is visible in one batch only when the signal dominates
this deviation; a negative mean gap is visible by the same logic. The query
count therefore has two roles: smaller
q
increases the mean mixing weight

\tau
, but also increases the realization scale
\psi_{q}(\delta)
.

Corollary B.2

(Finite-Smoothing Perturbation to the Realized Gap)
.

Assume the conditions of Lemma
3.1
. Also assume

{\mathbf{H}}\succeq 0
and let
\lambda_{\max}
be its largest eigenvalue. Define
the norm-matched finite-difference direction

\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mu}\triangleq\kappa^{-1/2}\widehat{{\bm{g}}}_{\mu}
and its
quadratic damage

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{q,\mu}^{\text{ZO}}\triangleq\mathcal{Q}(-\eta\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mu})
. Then the realized-gap
accounting in (
19
) holds. Moreover, the
same finite-smoothing residual controls both the damage perturbation and the
small mismatch from exact zero-smoothing norm matching. For a universal constant

C>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{q,\mu}^{\text{ZO}}-\mathcal{Q}_{q}^{\text{ZO}}\right|\right]\leq C\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\lambda_{\max}\left[\kappa^{-1/2}L\mu d^{3/2}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|+\kappa^{-1}L^{2}\mu^{2}d^{3}\right].

(28)

The expected squared norm obeys

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mu}\right\|^{2}\right]-\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\right|\leq C\left[\kappa^{-1/2}L\mu d^{3/2}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|+\kappa^{-1}L^{2}\mu^{2}d^{3}\right].

(29)

The finite-difference residual enters the quadratic damage in two ways: through
a cross term with the zero-smoothing shaped direction and through the residual
quadratic term. The expected-norm bound in Lemma
3.1

justifies the estimator-level reduction, while the second-moment bound

\mathbb{E}[\left\|{\bm{r}}_{\mu}\right\|^{2}]
controls the damage-level perturbation through
Cauchy–Schwarz and the residual quadratic term. Thus
(
19
) realizes the main-text mean mechanism
when
G_{q}
dominates both the finite-query deviation and the finite-smoothing
perturbation at the relevant scale. Equation (
29
)
also makes explicit that norm matching is exact for the zero-smoothing shape and
perturbed, at the same order, for a finite-difference batch.

Corollary B.3

(Realized Gap Sign Certificate)
.

Under the conditions of Proposition
B.1
and
Corollary
B.2
, define

\displaystyle B_{q}(\delta)

\displaystyle\triangleq C\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\lambda_{\max}\left(\psi_{q}(\delta)+\psi_{q}(\delta)^{2}\right),

(30)

\displaystyle B_{\mu}

\displaystyle\triangleq C\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\lambda_{\max}\left[\kappa^{-1/2}L\mu d^{3/2}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|+\kappa^{-1}L^{2}\mu^{2}d^{3}\right],

(31)

where
C
is a universal constant large enough for the two preceding results.
For any
\delta_{q},\delta_{\mu}\in(0,1)
, with probability at least

1-\delta_{q}-\delta_{\mu}
,

\left|\left(\mathcal{Q}^{\text{FO}}-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{q,\mu}^{\text{ZO}}\right)-G_{q}\right|\leq B_{q}(\delta_{q})+\frac{B_{\mu}}{\delta_{\mu}}.

(32)

Consequently, if

|G_{q}|>B_{q}(\delta_{q})+B_{\mu}/\delta_{\mu}
, the practical realized gap has the same
sign as the mean signal
G_{q}
. In the zero-smoothing setting, set
B_{\mu}=0
.

If the two function values are observed with additive zero-mean scalar noise,
the estimator gains an additional residual. For independent noises with
conditional variance at most
\sigma_{f}^{2}
per function value, the residual

{\bm{n}}_{\mu}=q^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{q}((\xi_{r,+}-\xi_{r,-})/(2\mu)){\mathbf{z}}_{r}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\left\|{\bm{n}}_{\mu}\right\|^{2}]\leq\sigma_{f}^{2}d/(2\mu^{2}q)
. The same perturbation
calculation applies after replacing
{\bm{r}}_{\mu}
by

{\bm{r}}_{\mu}+{\bm{n}}_{\mu}
. This is the reason practical ZO experiments either use
common mini-batches/common random numbers for the two function values or report
the additional variance induced by stochastic function-value observations.

Appendix C
Blockwise Support Theory for RISE

RISE applies the calibrated ZO shape independently inside parameter blocks,
so the global identity from Sec.
4
is localized rather than
reused verbatim. The blockwise support does not claim that vanilla ZO is
intrinsically blockwise. A full-
{\mathbf{H}}
oracle could exploit intra-block and
inter-block curvature simultaneously; a blockwise informed rule would keep the
within-block matrices
{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}
and ignore cross-block couplings. RISE uses
weaker information by shaping natural parameter blocks independently. The local
calculation identifies how much of the global identity survives this
localization and which term records the cost of ignoring cross-block curvature.

Partition the parameter into
B
blocks and write the retention curvature in
blocks
{\mathbf{H}}_{bc}
. Let block
b
have dimension
d_{b}
and identity
{\mathbf{I}}_{b}
.
For each block, draw

{\mathbf{z}}_{b,1},\ldots,{\mathbf{z}}_{b,q_{b}}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},{\mathbf{I}}_{b})
independently across blocks and define

{\mathbf{Z}}_{b}\triangleq\frac{1}{q_{b}}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{z}}_{b,i}{\mathbf{z}}_{b,i}^{\top},\qquad\kappa_{b}\triangleq\frac{q_{b}+d_{b}+1}{q_{b}},\qquad{\mathbf{P}}_{b}\triangleq\kappa_{b}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z}}_{b}.

(33)

Let
{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{blk}}\triangleq\operatorname{blkdiag}({\mathbf{P}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{P}}_{B})
,

a_{b}\triangleq\kappa_{b}^{-1/2}
, and

\tau_{b}\triangleq d_{b}/(q_{b}+d_{b}+1)
.

Theorem C.1

(Blockwise Expected Curvature and Gap for RISE)
.

For the blockwise RISE shape
{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{blk}}
in
(
33
), any fixed symmetric retention curvature

{\mathbf{H}}
satisfies

\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{blk}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{blk}}\right]\right)_{bb}=(1-\tau_{b}){\mathbf{H}}_{bb}+\tau_{b}\bar{\lambda}_{b}{\mathbf{I}}_{b},\qquad\left(\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{blk}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{blk}}\right]\right)_{bc}=a_{b}a_{c}{\mathbf{H}}_{bc}\quad(b\neq c),

(34)

where
\bar{\lambda}_{b}\triangleq\tr({\mathbf{H}}_{bb})/d_{b}
. Consequently, with

\mathcal{Q}^{\text{RISE}}_{\text{blk}}\triangleq\mathcal{Q}(-\eta{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{blk}}{\bm{g}})
,

\mathcal{Q}^{\text{FO}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}^{\text{RISE}}_{\text{blk}}\right]=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left[\sum_{b=1}^{B}\tau_{b}\,{\bm{g}}_{b}^{\top}({\mathbf{H}}_{bb}-\bar{\lambda}_{b}{\mathbf{I}}_{b}){\bm{g}}_{b}+2\sum_{b<c}(1-a_{b}a_{c}){\bm{g}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}_{bc}{\bm{g}}_{c}\right].

(35)

Theorem
C.1
is the formal connection between the
global mechanism and the blockwise wrapper. If
{\mathbf{H}}
is block diagonal, the
gap is exactly the sum of the global FO–ZO identity applied inside each block.
If cross-block curvature is present, the second term in
(
35
) is the explicit price of blockwise localization:
it may help or hurt depending on the signed cross-block interaction

{\bm{g}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}_{bc}{\bm{g}}_{c}
. This is why the blockwise evaluation uses the
blockwise mean-matched FO control
{\mathbf{A}}{\bm{g}}
and the covariance-matched control
in (
46
); otherwise shrinkage of cross-block mean
terms could be mistaken for a shaping effect.

Proposition C.2

(Blockwise One-Batch Deviation)
.

Assume the setting of Theorem
C.1
. For

\delta_{b}\in(0,1)
define

\psi_{b}(\delta_{b})\triangleq\sqrt{\frac{d_{b}+\log(1/\delta_{b})}{q_{b}}}+\frac{d_{b}+\log(1/\delta_{b})+1}{q_{b}}.

(36)

There is a universal constant
C>0
such that, with probability at least

1-\sum_{b=1}^{B}\delta_{b}
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{Q}^{\text{RISE}}_{\text{blk}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}^{\text{RISE}}_{\text{blk}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{B}C\left\|{\bm{g}}_{b}\right\|^{2}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}\right\|_{\text{op}}\left(\psi_{b}(\delta_{b})+\psi_{b}(\delta_{b})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\eta^{2}\sum_{b<c}a_{b}a_{c}\left\|{\bm{g}}_{b}\right\|\left\|{\bm{g}}_{c}\right\|\left\|{\mathbf{H}}_{bc}\right\|_{\text{op}}\left(\psi_{b}(\delta_{b})+\psi_{c}(\delta_{c})+\psi_{b}(\delta_{b})\psi_{c}(\delta_{c})\right).

(37)

Proposition
C.2
explains the finite-query
advantage and its limitation. The within-block sampling scale depends on

d_{b}
, not the full ambient dimension, so moderate blocks can make the
mean-curvature signal easier to realize with low
q_{b}
. The cross-block term
does not vanish automatically; it is controlled by the off-diagonal curvature
and by the shaped block norms. Thus blockwise shaping is a stable realization
of the global mechanism only when the within-block anisotropic signal is large
relative to both within-block sampling fluctuation and cross-block coupling.

The same blockwise object can be rewritten as a mean adaptation plus centered
random residual. This decomposition is useful for interpretation because it
separates deterministic mean-step damage from the expected cost of randomized
exposure.

Theorem C.3

(Expected Forgetting Decomposition for Blockwise Randomized Shaping)
.

Under the local quadratic forgetting model in (
3
),
let a randomized adaptation decompose as
{\bm{\Delta}}={\bm{m}}+{\bm{\epsilon}}
, where

{\bm{m}}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\Delta}}]
and
{\bm{\epsilon}}=({\bm{\epsilon}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\epsilon}}_{B})
is partitioned by
blocks. Assume

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\epsilon}}_{b}]={\bm{0}}
,

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\epsilon}}_{b}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{c}^{\top}]={\bm{0}}
for
b\neq c
, and

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\epsilon}}_{b}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{b}^{\top}]={\bm{\Sigma}}_{b}
. With

{\bm{\Sigma}}=\operatorname{blkdiag}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\Sigma}}_{B})
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}({\bm{\Delta}})\right]=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{m}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\bm{m}}+\frac{1}{2}\tr({\mathbf{H}}{\bm{\Sigma}})=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{m}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\bm{m}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\tr({\mathbf{H}}_{bb}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{b}).

(38)

Theorem
C.3
is the expectation identity that matches
the blockwise wrapper. The first term is the damage of the mean adaptation and
may involve the full curvature
{\mathbf{H}}
, including cross-block couplings. This
term is why the empirical setup includes mean-scaled FO controls. After the
mean adaptation is controlled, the expected damage introduced by the centered
blockwise random residual depends only on
{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}
and the block covariances

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{b}
. Cross-block curvature is not irrelevant; it enters the stability
of one sampled realization. For a zero-mean blockwise residual,

\mathcal{Q}({\bm{\epsilon}})-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}({\bm{\epsilon}})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left({\bm{\epsilon}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{b}-\tr({\mathbf{H}}_{bb}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{b})\right)+\sum_{b<c}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}_{bc}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{c}.

(39)

Thus the mean randomized damage is block-diagonal, while inter-block curvature
controls run-to-run fluctuation and the reliability of a single sampled shaped
adaptation.

The same decomposition gives a trace-level measure when a block is shaped
isotropically. If
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{b}=\alpha_{b}{\mathbf{I}}_{b}
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}({\bm{\epsilon}})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\alpha_{b}\tr({\mathbf{H}}_{bb}),\qquad D_{b}\triangleq\frac{1}{d_{b}}\tr({\mathbf{H}}_{bb}).

(40)

The damage density
D_{b}
is the mean forgetting cost per unit isotropic
randomized exposure in block
b
. It is milder than a largest-eigenvalue score
and matches the average behavior of randomized shaping. Directional block
geometry gives a complementary score. For blocks with positive definite or
damped curvature, define

R_{b}(\rho)\triangleq{\bm{g}}_{b}^{\top}({\mathbf{H}}_{bb}+\rho{\mathbf{I}}_{b})^{-1}{\bm{g}}_{b},\qquad\rho\geq 0.

(41)

Large
R_{b}(\rho)
means the incoming signal in block
b
can be carried through
directions that are flat for the historical loss. A block is therefore
forgetting-resistant not merely because
D_{b}
is small, but because it combines
low average retention curvature, signal alignment with flat directions, and
weak coupling to the rest of the network. A shape-aware coupling score is

C_{b}\triangleq\sum_{c\neq b}\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{b}^{1/2}{\mathbf{H}}_{bc}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c}^{1/2}\right\|_{\text{F}},

(42)

with the raw alternative

\sum_{c\neq b}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}_{bc}\right\|_{\text{F}}
when the block covariance is not yet
specified. Blocks with modest
D_{b}
but large
C_{b}
can look safe on average
while showing unstable realized forgetting; these are fragile bridge blocks
rather than uniformly robust layers.

The distance from a full-
{\mathbf{H}}
oracle is controlled by how large the omitted
couplings are relative to the block diagonal. Let

{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}\triangleq\operatorname{blkdiag}({\mathbf{H}}_{11},\ldots,{\mathbf{H}}_{BB})
,

{\mathbf{E}}\triangleq{\mathbf{H}}-{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}
, and

\epsilon_{\text{blk}}\triangleq\left\|{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{E}}{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}^{-1/2}\right\|_{\text{op}}.

(43)

Proposition C.4

(Block-Coupling Perturbation)
.

Let
{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}=\operatorname{blkdiag}({\mathbf{H}}_{11},\ldots,{\mathbf{H}}_{BB})
,

{\mathbf{E}}={\mathbf{H}}-{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}
, and

\epsilon_{\text{blk}}=\left\|{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{E}}{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}^{-1/2}\right\|_{\text{op}}
.
If
{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}\succ 0
and
\epsilon_{\text{blk}}<1
, then

(1+\epsilon_{\text{blk}})^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}^{-1}\preceq{\mathbf{H}}^{-1}\preceq(1-\epsilon_{\text{blk}})^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}_{\text{blk}}^{-1}.

(44)

Proposition
C.4
gives the intended status of the
blockwise relaxation. When inter-block coupling is weak in the normalized sense
of (
43
), blockwise curvature information is
close to the full-
{\mathbf{H}}
oracle up to the displayed multiplicative factors.
When the coupling is large, the blockwise wrapper is not invalid; it gives up
coordinated use of off-diagonal curvature in exchange for a cheaper and more
stable randomization rule. Finally, the standard norm-matched ZO
forgetting-gap identity can still be read inside each block as a local shaping
score,

\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\text{RISE}}\triangleq\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\tau_{b}\left\|{\bm{g}}_{b}\right\|^{2}\left(\lambda_{b}-\bar{\lambda}_{b}\right),\quad\lambda_{b}\triangleq\frac{{\bm{g}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}{\bm{g}}_{b}}{\left\|{\bm{g}}_{b}\right\|^{2}},\quad\bar{\lambda}_{b}\triangleq\frac{\tr({\mathbf{H}}_{bb})}{d_{b}},\quad\tau_{b}\triangleq\frac{d_{b}}{q_{b}+d_{b}+1}.

(45)

This score is not a pointwise decomposition of the full quadratic form. It is a
block-local prediction of where the RISE wrapper has positive within-block
anisotropic excess to attenuate after the mean step and scale have been
controlled. These quantities have distinct roles:
\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\text{RISE}}

tracks the within-block anisotropic benefit predicted by
Theorem
C.1
;
D_{b}
tracks the average cost of
isotropic randomized exposure;
C_{b}
tracks sensitivity to omitted cross-block
coupling and one-batch instability; and
R_{b}(\rho)
is an oracle-style
flat-signal proxy for whether the incoming block signal lies in low-retention
directions. None of these quantities is an unconditional selection threshold
on its own. Their selection value is assessed through the completed
query-count sweep and by additional scale controls when those controls are
reported, as discussed in Appendix
D.3
.

D.3
End-to-End Settings and Ablations

The end-to-end experiments treat RISE as an exact-gradient transfer of the ZO
shape, not as a generic ZO method, replay method, or memory-saving
finite-difference optimizer. They are benchmark-transfer tests that complement
the quadratic-sandbox evidence in Fig.
2
. The reported
vision table inserts RISE into ZO-FC and therefore tests the paired
ZO-FC to ZO-FC+RISE change. The language table uses SeqLoRA and O-LoRA as
additional hosts, illustrating that the same wrapper can be attached beyond
ZO-FC when exact block gradients are available.

For visual continual learning, the central comparison is ZO-FC versus
ZO-FC+RISE. Both methods keep the classifier head optimized by FO gradients,
so the head remains a fast-plastic component. The difference is on the
representation side: ZO-FC uses finite-difference ZO adaptation, while
ZO-FC+RISE applies the exact-gradient shaping rule in
Eq. (
25
).
The classifier-only, FO-optimization, and ZO-optimization rows in
Table
1
provide context for this paired comparison.
They are not used to claim generic dominance over all continual-learning
methods.

For language continual learning, Table
2

compares FO, finite-difference ZO, and RISE variants under the same task
orders and training settings for SeqLoRA and O-LoRA. This design tests the
specific confound raised by recent ZO continual-learning results: raw
finite-difference ZO can reduce forgetting while losing plasticity. The reported
comparison tests whether exact-gradient transfer recovers much of that
plasticity while retaining some of the stability benefit associated with ZO
shaping. These rows support the attachable-wrapper view, but the magnitude of
the gain remains host-method dependent and is not inferred for methods without a
reported RISE variant.

Across both domains, the reported metrics are Avg, Last, and Fgt. Avg measures
overall continual-learning performance, Last serves as the plasticity-sensitive
readout, and Fgt measures retention. Efficiency breakdowns and memory overhead
are useful system-level measurements, but they are not part of the reported
evidence package.

E.4
A General Isotropic-Moment Lemma

Lemma E.1

(General Isotropic-Moment Form)
.

Let
{\mathbf{P}}
be a random shape operator with finite second moments whose distribution is
isotropic in a selected comparison space, meaning

{\mathbf{O}}^{\top}{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{O}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{P}}
for every orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{O}}
,
where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution. Define

{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{H}})\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}\right]
and

\bar{\lambda}=\tr({\mathbf{H}})/d
. Then there exist scalars
\alpha
and
\rho
, depending
on the shape-operator distribution but not on
{\mathbf{H}}
, such that for every
symmetric
{\mathbf{H}}
,

{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{H}})=\alpha{\mathbf{H}}+\rho\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}.

(63)

Equivalently, if
{\mathbf{H}}=\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}+{\mathbf{R}}
with
\tr({\mathbf{R}})=0
, where
{\mathbf{R}}
is
the traceless anisotropic component of retention curvature, then

{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{H}})=(\alpha+\rho)\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}+\alpha{\mathbf{R}}.

(64)

The isotropic-moment assumption in Lemma
E.1
implies that
the linear map
{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{H}})=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}\right]
is orthogonally equivariant:

{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{O}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{O}})={\mathbf{O}}^{\top}{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{H}}){\mathbf{O}}
for every orthogonal
{\mathbf{O}}
.
The space of symmetric matrices decomposes into the direct sum of the identity
span and the traceless symmetric subspace. These two subspaces are invariant
under orthogonal conjugation. By the standard irreducibility of the traceless
symmetric representation, an orthogonally equivariant linear map acts as a
scalar on each component. Hence there exist scalars
\omega
and
\alpha
such that

{\mathcal{T}}(\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}+{\mathbf{R}})=\omega\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}+\alpha{\mathbf{R}},\qquad\tr({\mathbf{R}})=0.

(65)

Writing
\rho=\omega-\alpha
gives

{\mathcal{T}}({\mathbf{H}})=\alpha{\mathbf{H}}+\rho\bar{\lambda}{\mathbf{I}}
, which is
(
63
); the equivalent traceless form follows immediately.

{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha}^{\star}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}}{d}{\mathbf{I}},&\alpha^{2}\leq 1/d,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\alpha^{2}{\bm{g}}{\bm{g}}^{\top}+\frac{(1-\alpha^{2})\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}}{d-1}\Pi_{\perp},&\alpha^{2}>1/d.\end{cases}

(95)

The set
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
contains the second moments of all random
directions
{\mathbf{x}}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}]=\alpha{\bm{g}}
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}\right]=\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}
. The theorem records the aligned
curvature-agnostic characterization that the main text uses only as positioning.

Proof.

The adversarial-curvature calculation from
Theorem
4.4
gives

\sup_{{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\bar{\lambda}}}\tr({\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{M}})=d\bar{\lambda}\,\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{M}}).

(96)

It remains to minimize
\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{M}})
over

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
. Every feasible
{\mathbf{M}}
satisfies both

\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{M}})\geq\frac{\tr({\mathbf{M}})}{d}=\frac{\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}}{d}

(97)

and, by applying the constraint

{\mathbf{M}}\succeq\alpha^{2}{\bm{g}}{\bm{g}}^{\top}
to the unit vector

{\bm{g}}/\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|
,

\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{M}})\geq\alpha^{2}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}.

(98)

Thus

\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{M}})\geq\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}\max\left\{\alpha^{2},\frac{1}{d}\right\}.

(99)

When
\alpha^{2}\leq 1/d
, the isotropic moment

(\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}/d){\mathbf{I}}
is feasible because

(\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}/d){\mathbf{I}}-\alpha^{2}{\bm{g}}{\bm{g}}^{\top}\succeq 0
, and it attains this lower
bound. When
\alpha^{2}>1/d
, the moment in
(
95
) has trace
\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}
and satisfies

{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha}^{\star}-\alpha^{2}{\bm{g}}{\bm{g}}^{\top}=((1-\alpha^{2})\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}/(d-1))\Pi_{\perp}\succeq 0
. Its eigenvalue along

{\bm{g}}
is
\alpha^{2}\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2}
, and all perpendicular eigenvalues equal

((1-\alpha^{2})\left\|{\bm{g}}\right\|^{2})/(d-1)
, which is no larger because

\alpha^{2}>1/d
. It therefore attains the lower bound. Multiplying the optimal
largest eigenvalue by
d\bar{\lambda}\eta^{2}/2
gives
(
94
).

Finally, the condition
{\mathbf{M}}\succeq\alpha^{2}{\bm{g}}{\bm{g}}^{\top}
is the second-moment form
of preserving the aligned mean. If
{\mathbf{M}}
satisfies it, set

{\mathbf{C}}={\mathbf{M}}-\alpha^{2}{\bm{g}}{\bm{g}}^{\top}\succeq 0
and take any zero-mean random vector

{\mathbf{\epsilon}}
with covariance
{\mathbf{C}}
; then
{\mathbf{x}}=\alpha{\bm{g}}+{\mathbf{\epsilon}}

\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}]=\alpha{\bm{g}}
and
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}]={\mathbf{M}}

. Applying the same Gaussian

Increasing

{\mathbf{P}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}{\mathbf{P}}_{b}-\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{P}}_{b}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}{\mathbf{P}}_{b}]=\kappa_{b}^{-1}\left[{\mathbf{D}}_{b}{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}+{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}{\mathbf{D}}_{b}+{\mathbf{D}}_{b}{\mathbf{H}}_{bb}{\mathbf{D}}_{b}-\frac{1}{q_{b}}\left({\mathbf{H}}_{bb}+\tr({\mathbf{H}}_{bb}){\mathbf{I}}_{b}\right)\right].
