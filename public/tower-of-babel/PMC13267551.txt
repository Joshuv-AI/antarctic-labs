Postviral encephalitis in a child with bilateral basal ganglia involvement: MRI findings and diagnostic considerations
Authors: Saleck Choumad, Hind Qajia, Ahmed Ebedda, Aichetou Mohamed El Hacen, Lina Belkouchi, Nazik Allali, Latifa Chat, Siham El Haddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostinfectious encephalitis in children may occur after viral illness and present with nonspecific neurological symptoms. We report a 7-year-old child who developed behavioral disturbances 2 weeks after a flu-like syndrome. Brain MRI demonstrated bilateral asymmetric hyperintensity of the deep gray nuclei on T2 and FLAIR sequences. In this clinical context, imaging findings were consistent with postviral encephalitis, highlighting the key role of MRI in diagnosis.Keywords: Postviral encephalitis, Child, MRI, Basal ganglia, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

Postinfectious encephalitis is an immune-mediated inflammatory disorder occurring days to weeks after a viral illness and is more frequent in children [1]. Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and may include behavioral disturbances, altered consciousness, or seizures. Cerebrospinal fluid findings can be normal, making neuroimaging essential for diagnosis and etiological orientation. MRI plays a central role in detecting inflammatory lesions and differentiating postinfectious encephalitis from infectious, metabolic, or autoimmune conditions. We report a case of postviral encephalitis in a child presenting with bilateral basal ganglia involvement on MRI.

Case report

A 7-year-old child with no significant past medical history was admitted for neurological symptoms occurring 2 weeks after a flu-like illness. The clinical course was marked by progressive vomiting, asthenia, and behavioral disturbances.

Brain MRI was requested due to suspicion of encephalitis.

Lumbar puncture showed no abnormalities. Serologic testing revealed positive HSV-1/2 IgM, suggesting a recent viral infection.

Although serologic testing revealed positive HSV-1/2 IgM suggesting recent viral infection, no confirmatory PCR testing was performed, which represents a limitation in identifying the exact viral agent.

Discussion

Postviral encephalitis results from immune-mediated neuroinflammation rather than direct viral cytotoxicity. This mechanism explains the frequent discrepancy between clinical symptoms and normal CSF findings [1]. MRI is therefore crucial for suggesting the diagnosis and guiding management.

Postviral encephalitis has been described following several viral infections, including influenza, measles, and more recently SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19). These infections are thought to trigger immune-mediated neuroinflammatory responses rather than direct viral cytotoxicity [2,3].

In postinfectious encephalitis, MRI typically demonstrates T2/FLAIR hyperintensity reflecting inflammatory edema. Deep gray nuclei involvement may be bilateral or asymmetric and usually occurs without hemorrhage or necrosis [4]. When basal ganglia abnormalities are present, several differential diagnoses must be considered.

Herpes simplex encephalitis is the most urgent differential because it requires prompt antiviral therapy. It usually involves the medial temporal lobes and limbic structures and may show hemorrhage or mass effect, features that are uncommon in postviral inflammatory forms [5].

Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis and MOG-associated disease are common pediatric postinfectious inflammatory disorders. They typically show multifocal white-matter lesions, often associated with deep gray nuclei involvement. The presence of extensive white-matter abnormalities favors these diagnoses over isolated basal ganglia inflammation [6].

Autoimmune encephalitis may also mimic postinfectious encephalitis. MRI can be normal or show cortical or limbic hyperintensities. Diagnosis relies primarily on clinical features and antibody testing rather than imaging alone [7].

Acute necrotizing encephalopathy of childhood is another important differential, especially after influenza infection. It is characterized by symmetric thalamic lesions, often with hemorrhage or necrosis, which were absent in our case [8].

Metabolic disorders such as hypoglycemia, mitochondrial disease, or Wilson disease may also affect the basal ganglia. However, these entities typically present with specific clinical or biological abnormalities and characteristic symmetric patterns on MRI [9].

In our patient, the temporal relationship with a preceding viral illness, normal CSF analysis, absence of necrosis or hemorrhage, and favorable evolution supported the diagnosis of postviral encephalitis.

Outcome is generally favorable with early treatment, although neurological sequelae may occur in severe cases. Early recognition and imaging are therefore crucial.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the value of MRI in diagnosing postviral encephalitis in children. Bilateral basal ganglia involvement should prompt consideration of an inflammatory postinfectious process, especially when CSF findings are normal. Prompt diagnosis enables early treatment and improves prognosis.
