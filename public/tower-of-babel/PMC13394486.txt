A Three‐Dimensional Culture–Drug Sensitivity Test Predicts MDM2 Inhibitor–Sensitivity in SMARCB1/INI1‐Deficient Tumors
Authors: Hiroaki Goto, Chiyoko Nishime, Takashi Ohtsu, Mieko Ito, Maiko Sagisaka, Takuya Naruto, Norihiko Kitagawa, Mio Tanaka, Masakatsu Yanagimachi, Yukihiko Hiroshima, Junko Takita, Yasuhide Hayashi, Yohei Miyagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLoss of SMARCB1/INI1 expression is a common feature of various types of tumors including malignant rhabdoid tumor, an aggressive cancer of children. Because SMARCB1/INI1 deficiency leads to genome‐wide transcriptional dysregulation, identifying a specific therapeutic target that acts on a wide range of SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors has been challenging. This study aimed to establish if the personalized selection of effective drugs against SMARCB1/INI‐deficient tumors is possible by in vitro drug sensitivity profiling. The in vitro drug sensitivity profiles of tumors from SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumor cell line–derived mouse xenograft (CDX) models were evaluated by a short‐term collagen gel–embedded three‐dimensional culture–drug sensitivity test (3D‐DST). With the 3D‐DST, molecular targeting drugs, including inhibitors of aurora B kinase, mammalian target of rapamycin, mitogen‐activated protein kinase, WNT/β‐catenin, or mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2), were selected as therapeutic drug candidates in SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors. A CDX tumor, which was resistant to MDM2 inhibitors in the 3D‐DST, expressed lower TP53 compared to an MDM2 inhibitor–sensitive tumor. In cultured cell lines, exposure to MI‐773, a MDM2 inhibitor, disturbed the expression of a significant number of genes and induced p21 protein expression in MDM2 inhibitor–sensitive cells. However, such changes were not observed in resistant cells. These results suggest that 3D‐DST can predict biologically relevant drug sensitivity profiles in SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors.Keywords: in vitro drug sensitivity test, INI1, MDM2 inhibitor, SMARCB1, three‐dimensional culture

Introduction

Malignant rhabdoid tumor (MRT) is a rare aggressive tumor that develops mostly in children before 3 years of age [1, 2]. Such tumors are highly resistant to conventional chemotherapy drugs, and the prognosis of patients with unresectable or metastatic disease is dismal [1, 2].

Biallelic inactivation of the tumor suppressor gene, SMARCB1 (also known as INI1) or, rarely, SMARCA4, is a characteristic feature of MRT [3, 4]. Loss of SMARCB/INI1 expression is a hallmark in the pathological diagnosis of MRT, although it is not exclusive to this tumor. Epithelioid sarcoma, pancreatic undifferentiated rhabdoid tumor, or other tumors are also SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors. SMARCB1/INI1 is a core subunit of the SWItch/Sucrose Non‐Fermentable (SWI/SNF) ATP‐dependent chromatin remodeling complex, which has a central role in regulating nucleosome remodeling. SWI/SNF complex function that is disrupted due to SMARCB1/INI1 deficiency leads to genome‐wide transcriptional deregulation, some of which is presumed to be essential in oncogenesis. Because of diverse oncologic signaling dysregulation resulting from SMARCB1/INI1 deficiency, identification of a specific molecular target that acts in a wide range of SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors has been unsuccessful. EZH2 is a subunit of the polycomb repressive complex (PRC2) that represses gene transcription in an antagonistic manner to the SWI/SNF complex [4, 5]. Suppression of the PRC2 complex by EZH2 inhibitors can counteract effects resulting from the loss of the SWI/SNF complex function in theory. However, clinical responses to the EZH2 inhibitor were not consistent among patients with SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors [6].

Personalized matched targeted therapy is a possible strategy to overcome refractory diseases and to reduce the risk of unnecessary host damage by using ineffective toxic drugs. Several trials to incorporate in vitro drug sensitivity profiling data into personalized therapy have been performed in patients with various malignant diseases [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. However, as for MRT or other SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors, whether in vitro drug sensitivity profiling can provide clinically relevant results has not been well established.

In this study, we performed in vitro drug sensitivity testing (DST) using a collagen gel–embedded culture method in cell line–derived xenograft (CDX) models of SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors. The results of the DST were compared to their molecular backgrounds to determine if the DST can predict biologically relevant drug sensitivity profiles of SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines and Cell Line–Derived Xenograft Models

RTK(J)‐4 N and TTN45 cell lines were described previously [12]. KCS1 and KCS3 are newly established cell lines derived from resected tumors of patients with an unclassified SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumor or MRT, respectively. The characteristics of patients who provided tumor samples for tissue culture are summarized in Table S1. For the establishment of cell lines, each surgically resected tumor was minced and enzymatically dispersed. After enzyme treatment, each single‐cell suspension was washed, resuspended in DMEM medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific), seeded in a 25‐cm2 culture flask, and incubated in an atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37°C. When cells growing in monolayer reached confluence in a flask, cells were detached using TrypLE Express Enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 20%–30% of cells were transferred to a new flask. Passaging was repeated every 1–2 weeks and cells were used for experiments more than 6 months after the start of culture. For authentication of newly established cell lines, genomic PCR using 10 short tandem repeat (STR) loci‐specific primer sets was performed and the sizes of PCR products were analyzed on a genetic analyzer using GeneMapper software 6 (Applied Biosystems Inc., Foster City, CA, USA). The STR data were compared to their primary cultured tissues from which cell lines were derived.

The absence of mycoplasma contamination was confirmed in all cell lines using MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

To establish a CDX model, 0.3–1.5 × 107 cells were subcutaneously injected into NOD.Cg‐Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ mice (The Jackson Laboratory Japan, INC., Kanagawa, Japan). When each tumor size reached approximately 15 mm in diameter, mice were euthanized and subcutaneous tumors were resected.

In Vitro Drug Sensitivity Test

A short‐term collagen gel–embedded three‐dimensional–culture drug sensitivity test (3D‐DST) was performed as previously reported [13]. Briefly, tumor tissues resected from CDXs were minced using a sterile scalpel then enzymatically dispersed. The tissues were resuspended in DMEM medium with 10% FBS and filtered through 300, 100, and 40 μm pore‐nylon meshes, in that order. A collagen gel–coated flask was made in advance by applying 3 mL of a type‐I collagen gel (Nitta Gelatin, Osaka, Japan) to a 25‐cm2 cell culture flask (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA). The cells were suspended in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and seeded into a collagen gel–coated flask. After one day pre‐culture in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere at 37°C, living tumor cells were enzymatically detached from the collagen gel. Cells were resuspended in collagen gel–containing F‐12 medium (collagen gel culture kit, Nitta Gelatin) at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL, and 10 μL of the gel was seeded into each well of a 384‐well plate. PCM‐2 medium (KURABO, Osaka, Japan) samples containing one of 60 drugs to be tested in the DST, and their 5−1, 5−2, and 5−3 serial dilutions, were prepared in a 384‐well plate (drug stock plate) in advance and then stored at −20°C until use. For the assay, 10 μL of drug‐containing PCM‐2 medium was transferred from a drug stock plate into wells of a collagen gel–seeded 384‐well plate using an epMotion96 semi‐automated pipetting system (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). In control wells, 10 μL of drug‐free PCM‐2 medium was added. The control wells were prepared as six replicate wells to evaluate any deviation in cell density among wells in each assay. After 4 days incubation in a humidified environment at 37°C and 5% CO2, the cell viability of each well was measured using a CellTiter‐Glo luminescent assay and Glo‐Max plate reader (Promega Co., Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. An illustrated overview of 3D‐DST and this study is shown in Figure 1.

Illustrated overview of the study. This study aimed to determine whether personalized selection of effective drugs for SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors is feasible using in vitro drug sensitivity profiling. Drug sensitivity was assessed using a short‐term collagen gel–embedded three‐dimensional drug sensitivity test (3D‐DST) in tumors derived from SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumor cell line xenograft models. The resulting drug sensitivity profiles were compared with the molecular characteristics of the tumors.

A list of drugs and the final concentrations used in the DST are shown in Table S2. Cell survival rates in drug‐containing wells were expressed as ratios of luminescence compared to the mean luminescence value of six drug‐free control wells. Normal adult dermal fibroblasts (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) were also tested in the 3D‐DST as a reference. A tumor was considered to be sensitive to a drug when the cell survival rate at each drug concentration was significantly lower than that of fibroblasts in a 2‐way ANOVA test.

To compare drug sensitivities between tumor samples, a drug effect score (DES) was used as previously reported [13, 14, 15]. The DES was calculated as follows: DES = ([100 − % survival at 5−3 dilution] * ln(125) + [100 − % survival at 5−2 dilution] * ln(25) + [100 − % survival at 5−1 dilution] * ln(5) + [100 − % survival at no dilution])/[ln(125) + ln(25) + ln(5) + 1]. A DES of 100 indicates a total cell kill at all tested concentrations, whereas a DES of 0 indicates no effect of a drug. For each drug, the average and standard deviation of DESs among four tumor samples were calculated, and then a Z‐score for each drug in each respective tumor was calculated.

RNA Sequencing

The mRNA samples were prepared using an Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) from total RNA extracted from xenograft tumor tissues. In brief, after eliminating DNA contamination using DNase, mRNA with a poly‐A tail was purified. The RNA fragments were transcribed into first strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase and random primers. The cDNA was synthesized to second strand cDNA using DNA polymerase I and RNase H. Adapters were ligated onto both ends of the cDNA fragments. After amplification of cDNA fragments using PCR, fragments with an insert sized between 200 and 400 bp were selected. Both ends of the cDNA fragments were sequenced by read length using a NovaSeq X Plus sequencing system (Illumina Inc.) in paired‐end mode. Overall reads' quality, total bases, total reads, GC (%), and basic statistics were calculated by FastQC v0.11.7 (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). After artifacts, such as low‐quality reads, adaptor sequence, contaminated DNA, or PCR duplicates, were removed in order to reduce bias analysis, host (mouse) and graft (human) reads were deconvolved, respectively, using BAMCMP (https://hpc.nih.gov/apps/bamcmp.html). After classifying all reads to “host only”, “graft only”, “ambiguous”, or “unmapped” reads categories, reads only on the graft genome were mapped to a GRCh38 reference genome with HISAT2, a slicer‐aware aligner (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml). Assembling transcripts by StringTie (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/) with aligned reads, normalization values that were calculated based on transcript length and depth of coverage, were provided as Transcript Per Kilobase Million (TPM).

Differentially Expressed Gene Analysis

The read count values of known genes obtained through an ‐e option of the StringTie were used as the original raw data. During data pre‐processing, low quality transcripts were filtered. Afterwards, Trimmed Mean of M‐values Normalization was performed. Statistical analysis was performed using a Fold Change (fc) exactTest and edgeR per comparison pair. A significant difference was defined as conditions of |fc| > = 2 and an exactTest raw p‐value < 0.05. Gene‐set enrichment analysis was performed based on gene ontology (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/) or a Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). p53 target genes (n = 21) were identified by KEGG database. The expression levels of p53 target genes were compared among samples by a Z‐score of TPM values.

Whole Exome Sequencing

Genomic DNA from xenograft tumors was processed using a SureSelect Human All Exon V5 + UTRs Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Captured genomic DNA was sequenced on a HiSeq2500 sequencer (Illumina Inc.) with 101‐bp paired‐end reads. Quality‐controlled reads were aligned to a human reference genome (UCSC hg19, NCBI build 37.1) using a Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA, version 0.7.10) (http://bio‐bwa.sourceforge.net/index.shtml). PCR duplications were removed using Picard (version 1.118) (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Variants, including single‐nucleotide variants, and small insertions and deletions, were called using a Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, version 3.2–2) (The UnifiedGenotyper and the HaplotypeCaller algorithms) (https://software.broadinstitute.org/gatk/) and were annotated using ANNOVAR (22 March 2015) (http://annovar.openbioinformatics.org/en/latest/). Out of all called variants within exons, or ±10 bp from exon–intron boundaries, those registered in the NHLBI GO Exome Sequencing Project 6500, the 1000 Genomes Project, dbSNP138, the Human Genetic Variation Database, and our in‐house Japanese exome data (57 individuals) were removed. Variants that passed all quality filters (FILTER = “PASS”) and were annotated as having a “MODERATE” or “HIGH” impact by SnpEff (https://pcingola.github.io/SnpEff/) were extracted.

Genome‐Wide Analysis of Copy Number Variants

Genomic DNA integrity was assessed using Genomic DNA ScreenTape on the TapeStation 2200 system (Agilent Technologies), and DNA concentration was measured using the QuantiFluor dsDNA System (Promega Co.) with the ARVO X2 multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Whole‐genome sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq DNA PCR‐Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina Inc.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 1 μg of genomic DNA was fragmented using Covaris adaptive focused acoustics, followed by end repair, size selection, A‐tailing, and adapter ligation. Libraries were quantified by qPCR using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit and assessed using a High Sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent Technologies). Paired‐end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc.) by Macrogen. FASTQ files were generated using bcl2fastq2 (v2.20.0), and reads were aligned to the reference genome using ISAAC Aligner (v04.18.11.09). Structural variants were identified using Manta (v1.5.0) with default parameters. Copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed using Control‐FREEC (v11.5) with a 10‐kb window size and GC‐content normalization. CNV status was determined based on a diploid baseline (ploidy = 2), with values > 2 defined as copy number gain and values < 2 defined as copy number loss.

RNA or Protein Expression in Cultured Cells

RTK(J)‐4 N and KCS3 cells in three‐dimensional (3D) culture were exposed to MI‐773. RTK(J)‐4 N (2.0 × 106) or KCS3 (1.0 × 106) cells were embedded in 4 mL of a collagen gel and seeded into a 25‐cm2 flask. PCM‐2 was used as the culture medium. After 24 h, the medium was changed using fresh PCM‐2, with or without MI‐773 at 1 μM. Cells were harvested after 24 or 48 h and cellular proteins were extracted in radioimmunoprecipitation buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Protein (25 μg/well) was loaded onto 4%–12% SDS‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels, electrophoresed, and then blotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane using an iBlot 2 Dry Blotting System (ThermoFisher Scientific). Blots were blocked and probed with antibodies against mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2), p21, or p53 (1:500 dilution; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or with antibodies against β‐actin (1:10000 dilution; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The blots were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies and detected using an iBind Western System (ThermoFisher Scientific). Finally, protein bands were scanned with a gel imaging instrument (KURABO, Osaka, Japan). To validate the results of Western blotting, cellular lysates were collected after 24 or 48 h of culture with or without 1 μM MI‐773, and p53 and p21 expression levels were quantified using Human p53 and p21 ELISA kits (Abcam) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cellular lysates from KCS1 and TTN45 cells were also analyzed by ELISA.

Total RNA was extracted from RTK(J)‐4 N or KCS3 cells cultured as above. The mRNA samples were prepared and used for RNA sequencing (RNAseq).

Results

Establishment of MRT CDX Models

Morphological features of cultured cell lines are shown in Figure 2A. RTK(J)‐4 N cells, which originate from a CNS tumor, grew as aggregations of small round cells whereas other cell lines derived from extracranial tumors grew as monolayers of spindle or polygonal cells. STR data of KCS1, KCS3, and their primary cultured tissues are provided in Table S3. Genome‐wide CNV analysis revealed a biallelic deletion of chromosome 22q encompassing SMARCB1 (chr22: 23,570,000–24,290,000 in KCS1, 23,570,000–24,300,000 in KCS1‐primary tumor, 23,160,000–23,890,000 in KCS3 and KCS3‐primary tumor, respectively, GRCh38.p14, Figures S1 and S2). The four SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumor cell lines were subcutaneously implanted in mice. The mice CDX tumors were resected when their diameters reached 15 mm; this took 21 to 30 days after implantation. Histologically, CDX tumors were composed of sheets of large polygonal cells with eccentric vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm (Figure 2B). By immunohistochemical analysis, tumors were negative for SMARCB1/INI1 expression (Figure 2C); thus, SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumor‐derived CDXs were successfully established. It was confirmed that CDX tumors contained 85.6%–91.8% of graft (human) cells by deconvolving RNAseq data using BAMCMP.

In Vitro Drug Sensitivity Profiling

An in vitro drug sensitivity profile of CDX tumors by 3D‐DST was highly diverse as indicated by a heat map (Figure 3A, Table S2), which represents Z‐scores of DES in respective drugs among four CDX tumors. [Correction added on 23 July 2026, after first online publication: Table S2 citation has been added in this version.] The drug panel used in this study included 18 cytotoxic chemotherapy drugs and 42 molecular targeting drugs. To explore common therapeutic targets in SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors, molecular targeting drugs, which were effective in multiple CDX tumors, were identified (Figure 3B); such candidates included barasertib (aurora B kinase inhibitor), rapamycin (mammalian target of rapamycin: mTOR inhibitor), trametinib (mitogen‐activated protein kinase: MAPK inhibitor), CCT251545 (WNT/β‐catenin inhibitor), and MI‐773 (MDM2 inhibitor). Thus, cell‐cycle, mTOR, MAPK, or p53 signaling pathways were suggested as potential therapeutic targets in SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors. However, responses to identify common targets for these molecular targeting drugs were highly variable in CDX tumors. As an exception, the dose‐responses for MI‐773 were similar in CDX tumors, except for KCS3. After 4 days exposure to MI‐773 at 1 μM, cell survival rates were 6%, 26%, or 51% compared to untreated cells in CDX tumors derived from RTK(J)‐4 N, KCS1, or TTN45 cells, respectively. Another MDM2 inhibitor, RG‐7112, was included in the drug panel. A statistical analysis of a comparison with normal fibroblasts failed to reveal RG‐7112 sensitivity in CDX tumors; however, responses to RG‐7112 were similar to those to MI‐773 among the CDX tumors. The KCS3‐derived CDX tumor was unresponsive to both MI‐773 and RG‐7112.

Comparison of Expression Profiles Among CDX Models

The expression levels of mRNAs in the RTK(J)‐4 N–derived CDX were compared to those of other tumors since the RTK(J)‐4 N‐CDX tended to be more sensitive to drugs compared to other cell lines. By gene set enrichment analysis, several pathways involved in tumor progression, such as phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)‐AKT, MAPK, RAS, and WNT signaling pathways, were differentially expressed between CDX tumors (Figure 4A, S3). These findings suggest important signals in tumor progression might be varied among SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors, consistent with the results of in vitro drug sensitivity profiles. Expression Characteristics in MDM2 Inhibitor–Sensitive and –Resistant Tumors

MI‐773 and RG‐7112 were supposed to suppress tumor growth by activating p53 signaling. To explore sensitive or resistant mechanisms, TP53 status was investigated in CDX tumors. The fold change of TP53 mRNA expression was −8.1 when the expression level in KCS3‐CDX was compared to the MI‐773 sensitive RTK(J)‐4 N‐CDX tumor (TPM values of 6.1 vs. 51.8, p < 0.001; Figure 4B). Thus, the suppressed expression of TP53 was speculated to confer a different response to MDM2 inhibitors. The expression levels of 21 downstream genes in the p53 signaling pathway (CDKN2A, SFN, RPRM, GADD45A, GADD45B, GADD45G, GTSE1, ZNF385A, FAS, PIDD1, BAX, SIVA1, TP53I3, SHISA5, AIFM2, IGFBP3, SERPINE1, DDB2, PTEN, STEAP3, MDM2) did not differ among CDX tumors (Figure 4C), regardless of TP53 expression levels.

To verify findings in CDX tumors, RTK(J)‐4 N or KCS3 cells were cultured in three‐dimensional culture, treated with MI‐773, and their responses observed. After 48‐h treatment, significant changes in gene expression were observed in RTK(J)‐4 N cells. Affected signaling pathways included the cell‐cycle and p53 signaling pathways. In contrast, the expression of a few genes was altered in KCS3 cells (Figure 5A,B, S4). Protein expression of p53 and p21 was enhanced after 24‐ or 48‐h treatment in RTK(J)‐4 N, but such changes were not observed in KCS3 (Figure 5C). The same results in p53 and p21 expression were observed by ELISA; enhanced expressions of p53 and p21 were observed after 24‐ or 48‐h MI‐773 treatment in RTK(J)‐4 N, KCS1, and TTN45, but not in KCS3 cells (Figure 5D). The expression of p53 or p21 could not be detected in KCS3 cells by ELISA at any time points. Discussion

Trials of functional precision medicine, which combine cancer genome data with in vitro drug sensitivity profiles, have been reported [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Drug sensitivity–guided therapy might be a promising approach especially for patients in whom cancer genome profiling solely failed to identify actionable molecular targets. One of the challenges in applying functional precision medicine for the clinic is the lack of a standardized method of DST, especially for solid tumors. A collagen gel droplet–embedded culture drug sensitivity test (CD‐DST) has yielded predictive values of clinical drug responses for several cancers [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Among in vitro drug sensitivity profiling methods, such as organoid and spheroid cultures, collagen‐embedded culture offers advantages in terms of higher success rates of primary tissue culture and better reproducibility of culture conditions [21, 22]. We previously utilized CD‐DST in hepatoblastoma, a pediatric malignant liver tumor, to explore novel effective drugs [23]. This study demonstrated growth‐inhibitory effects of irinotecan and sorafenib, which are not included in the standard chemotherapy regimens for hepatoblastoma. In a pilot clinical study, combination therapy with irinotecan and sorafenib showed clinical responses in patients with disease refractory to standard chemotherapy [24]. Taking advantage of a high success rate of primary tumor culture using a collagen‐embedded method, we modified the original CD‐DST method to increase the number of tested drugs in a single assay [13]. In this study, we examined if our method could identify therapeutic targets in MRT and other SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors.

3D‐DST, as well as the original CD‐DST, utilizes a 3D culture method. Several studies have suggested that 3D culture systems can better recapitulate physiological drug responses compared to conventional two‐dimensional (2D) culture systems [25]. We compared the drug sensitivities of RTK(J)‐4 N cells in collagen‐embedded 3D culture with those in conventional 2D culture. As a result, sensitivity to an MDM2 inhibitor, a MEK inhibitor, and a PI3K/mTOR inhibitor was enhanced in 3D culture (Figure S5). Because cell lines are routinely maintained under 2D culture conditions without an extracellular matrix, the results may have been influenced by cellular adaptation to 2D conditions. Although this study does not determine whether 3D culture more accurately predicts physiological drug responses than 2D culture in SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors, the collagen‐embedded culture method offers an advantage in terms of a higher success rate of primary culture [21, 22], which is critical for in vitro drug profiling using clinical tumor specimens. SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors comprise a heterogeneous group of clinically distinct diseases. Intensive chemotherapy and radiotherapy have improved the prognosis of atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor, an intracranial SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumor [26]. In contrast, no promising treatment has been established for MRT, particularly in advanced disease [1, 2]. Despite this clinical heterogeneity, SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors are genetically quiet [27]. Aside from biallelic inactivation of SMARCB1/INI1, no other recurrent driver gene alterations are typically identified, suggesting that epigenetic regulation may underlie intertumoral heterogeneity [28]. Consistent with the heterogeneous clinical features, mRNA expression profiles among CDX tumors were variable in this study. Because the identification of effective therapies based solely on cancer genome sequencing is challenging, functional precision medicine using in vitro drug sensitivity profiling may contribute to improve outcomes in patients with SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors.

The 3D‐DST, when used in CDX models, highlighted several candidates for therapeutic molecular targets in SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors. Of these candidates, MDM2 inhibitors suppressed tumor growth effectively in three of four tumors. The expression level of TP53 in the KCS3‐CDX tumor, which did not respond to MDM2 inhibition, was significantly lower compared to those in the sensitive tumors. The expression of p53 protein was not detected in cultured KCS3 cells, and p21 induction, a downstream event of p53 signaling, was not observed after MDM2 inhibition. These results suggest p53 inactivation via interaction with MDM2 or other mechanisms has a pivotal role in the survival of SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors. In an INI1‐deficient zebrafish model, mutant p53 was reported to work cooperatively in the development of epithelioid sarcoma or other tumors [29]. The essential role of p53 inactivation in MRT has also been reported by another study using cell lines [30]. Our findings are in line with such studies by other groups. Mutated TP53 is an infrequent event in SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors [27], suggesting MDM2 inhibition as a possible therapeutic strategy for these tumors.

The TP53 variant observed in MDM2 inhibitor–resistant KCS3 cells (NM_000546.6: c.672G>T, NP_000537.3: p.Glu224Asp) has been recurrently identified in several cancers [31, 32]. This variant has been suggested to cause a splicing defect resulting in retention of the intron between exons 6 and 7, which may affect DNA binding or other protein functions (The TP53 Database; https://tp53.cancer.gov/). The copy number alteration identified in KCS3 may partially account for the reduced TP53 expression, and additional mechanisms, including nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay [33] triggered by intron 6 retention, may further contribute to TP53 downregulation. Such effects are difficult to predict from exome sequencing alone. These findings suggest that the 3D‐DST may provide insights into molecular characteristics of tumors that cannot be fully explained by exome sequencing alone.

MDM2 inhibition has been reported to enhance the effects of aurora kinase inhibitors [34] and mTOR inhibitors [35] in other tumor types. As these inhibitors have also shown potential therapeutic efficacy in some tumors in this study, their combination effects in SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors warrant further investigation.

Following deficient SMARCB1/INI1 expression, multiple cellular events related to tumor proliferation and progression have been shown to be induced, including p16‐Rb, WNT/β‐catenin, or Sonic Hedgehog signaling pathways [3, 4]. The PI3K‐AKT or RAS signaling pathways have also been suggested to be associated with the proliferation of SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumors [26, 36, 37]. In 3D‐DST, inhibitors involved in these downstream pathways suppressed survival of CDX tumor cells to various extents. The results of 3D‐DST and expression profiles suggested that dependency on these pathways varied according to individual cases, although precise mechanisms underlying different sensitivities to molecular target drugs could not be analyzed in this study.

In conclusion, the use of 3D‐DST in SMARCB1/INI1‐deficient tumor‐CDX models revealed differential sensitivities to MDM2 inhibitors. An association between p53 expression levels and sensitivity to MDM2 inhibitors was suggested; however, other potential mechanisms underlying resistance to MDM2 inhibitors, such as the upregulation of MAPK or NF‐kB signaling pathways [38], were not thoroughly investigated in this study. Other therapeutic candidates, such as inhibitors of aurora kinase, mTOR, MAPK kinase, or WNT/β‐catenin, were identified by 3D‐DST although responses to these inhibitors varied among tumors. Further studies are required to prove if in vitro drug sensitivity profiling by 3D‐DST correlates with clinical drug effects in MRT or other SMARCB1/INI‐deficient tumors.
