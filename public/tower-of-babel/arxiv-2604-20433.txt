ON REWARD-BALANCING METHODS FOR REINFORCEMENT LEARNING
Simone Baroncini, Bahman Gharesifard, Giuseppe Notarstefano

Abstract
. This paper investigates the so-called reward-balancing methods, a novel class of algorithms for solving discounted-return reinforcement learning (RL) problems. These methods consist of iteratively adjusting the reward function to transform the RL problem into an equivalent one in which the optimal policies are greedy. For this procedure, referred to as normalization process, we provide a theoretical analysis of the involved transformations, emphasizing their algebraic structure. Then, we introduce a control-theoretic reformulation, recasting the reward-balancing procedure into an optimal control framework. The approach is further extended to address model uncertainty through stochastic model sampling, yielding normalization guarantees and probabilistic bounds on stochastic fluctuations. Using the proposed optimal control framework within a scenario model predictive control (MPC) setting, we demonstrate, through simulation studies, performance improvements over the current state-of-the-art.



1. Introduction

Most reinforcement learning algorithms developed within the Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) framework rely on a reward function that assigns values to the actions available in each state. Specifically, the role of rewards is to compare actions in the long term, either in terms of average or discounted return. Consequently, many MDPs can induce similar, if not identical, pairwise action comparisons, yet exhibit different performance under a given algorithm. Therefore, the task can be viewed from a different perspective, i.e., modify the reward function in a way to either gain better performance or simplify the problem. This idea has been explored in the literature in different contexts. The concept of modifying rewards without altering the set of optimal policies was first explored by Ng et al.
[
1
]
, where potential functions were used to define policy-invariant reward transformations. This method, commonly referred to as
reward shaping
, has then become a foundational tool for improving sample efficiency and convergence speed in reinforcement learning. Subsequent works have extended these ideas to more sophisticated shaping functions and learning-based shaping mechanisms, including techniques for automatic reward-balancing
[
2
,
3
]
and methods that learn
shaping potentials
[
5
,
4
]
. The reward transformations treated in this paper were recently introduced by Mustafin and collaborators
[
6
]
, where they are termed
reward-balancing methods
. Unlike traditional RL algorithms, which iteratively improve the policy while keeping the reward function fixed, reward-balancing methods take an alternative approach, called
normalization
, which consists of fixing the policy as greedy with respect to the current reward function and adjusting the latter to make that policy optimal. Interest in reward balancing is driven by the fact that the method proposed in
[
6
, Section 8]
matches the state-of-the-art sample complexity of
Q
-learning, while being
fully parallelizable, thus representing a significant improvement over the current state-of-the-art
[
7
]
.

Statement of Contributions.

The main contribution of this paper is threefold. First, we provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis of the group of transformations underlying reward-balancing methods, which allows for the iterative reward adjustment process not to alter the original problem. Specifically, we study the action of this group on the space of reward functions, providing insight into the mathematical foundations of this approach, with particular focus on its underlying geometry. Second, we introduce a control-theoretic reformulation, which offers a structured and systematic approach for the design and analysis of reward-balancing methods. From this perspective, the problem is recast as an optimal control problem, which facilitates the design of novel methods through the introduction of constraints and penalty terms. Third, we extend the framework to settings with model uncertainty, adopting a stochastic model-sampling paradigm. In this context, we establish rigorous normalization guarantees and derive probabilistic bounds on the deviation from the average update, offering a quantitative measure of the stochastic fluctuations. These results ensure that reward-balancing methods remain well-posed and stable under stochastic model sampling. Finally, simulation results demonstrate that the proposed optimal control framework, implemented in a scenario receding-horizon setting, outperforms the current state-of-the-art method in terms of policy optimality.

Organization.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we summarize some notation and mathematical preliminaries we need to present the problem under study. In Section
3
, we introduce the discounted-return reinforcement learning framework. In Section
4
, we introduce reward-balancing methods in the framework where the exact MDP model is known, providing their characterization as a group and studying their action on the space of reward functions. In Section
5
, we provide a control-theoretic reformulation of these methods. Finally, in Section
6
, we extend the framework to handle the case of uncertain model, and we provide tools for the design of robust reward-balancing laws.

The objective of discounted-return reinforcement learning is to find a DSP maximizing the so-called value function
v_{\pi,r}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
which assigns to each state the expected long-run cumulative reward associated with the given policy, and is formally defined as

\displaystyle v_{\pi,r}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{\pi}(x_{t})\biggr\rvert\begin{array}[]{l}x_{0}=x\\
x_{t+1}\sim f_{\pi}(\cdot,x_{t})\end{array}\right],

for some discount factor

\displaystyle v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}(x)\geq v_{\pi,r}(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\forall\pi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}).

\displaystyle v(x)=r_{\pi}(x)+\gamma\sum_{z\in\mathcal{X}}f_{\pi}(z,x)v(z),\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

\displaystyle\text{adv}_{\pi,r}\circ\pi=0.

The last ingredient which we require is the notion of symmetry for MDPs, as we outline next.

MDP Symmetry.

In many cases, the MDP of interest exhibits some form of symmetry, meaning that certain states and actions are interchangeable. For example, in the game of tic-tac-toe, an empty board with a cross in the top-right corner is equivalent, from the point of view of the game dynamics, to an empty board with the cross in any other corner, provided that the available actions are rotated accordingly. More generally, a symmetry in an MDP implies that there exists a group of transformations acting simultaneously on the state and action spaces, under which the transition and reward functions remain invariant. 4. Exact-Model Reward-Balancing Methods

In this section, we focus on model-coupled MDPs and examine the reward transformations involved in reward-balancing methods. We highlight their main properties and provide the mathematical foundations of the control-theoretic framework developed later in the paper.

4.1. Problem Statement

In
[
6
]
, the authors propose a class of methods for modifying the reward function of an MDP to transform it into a
normal MDP
. We recall this definition.

Definition 4

(
Normal MDPs
)
.

We say that an MDP is in normal form iff its optimal value function is zero.

Relation (
9
) implies that, in a normal MDP, every state has (at least) one action with zero reward in its fiber, which lies in the image of an optimal policy. Thus, the key advantage of these MDPs is that to find an optimal policy it is sufficient to select, for each state, an action with zero reward. Now, the challenge is to “normalize” a given MDP without altering the problem. That is, the transformation should preserve the relative position of the value functions, which induce a preorder over the policy space. We now state the problem addressed in this paper:

(a)

Given a class of MDPs sharing the same
known
model:

\rhd

characterize the set of normal MDPs and its relationship to the reward transformations involved in reward-balancing methods (cf. Section
4.2
), and

\rhd

identify a subset of these transformations that “improve” the reward function (cf. Section
4.3
);

(b)

Cast the normalization procedure as an optimal control problem in which the state and the input are the reward function and the transformation, respectively (cf. Section
5
);

(c)

In realistic settings where the MDP is
unknown
but can be sampled, leverage this control-theoretic formulation to design reward-balancing strategies outperforming the existing one in the literature (cf. Section
6
).

4.2. Advantage-Preserving Reward Transformations

The set of reward transformations introduced in
[
6
]
is a subgroup of the affine group of
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, consisting of all transformations of the form

(12)

\displaystyle\ell_{\delta}:r\mapsto r+b(\delta),

parametrized by the real-valued functions
\delta:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
on the state space through the injective linear operator
b:\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})\to\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
defined by

\displaystyle b(\delta)(u)=\delta(s(u))\bigl(\gamma f(s(u),u)-1\bigr)+\gamma\!\!\!\sum_{y\neq s(u)}\!\!\delta(y)f(y,u),

where
u\in\mathcal{U}
. Denoting by
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the vector representation of
\delta
, according to the notation introduced in the previous section, we can write the reward update as

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}:R\mapsto R+\left(\gamma F-S\right)\Delta,

where
S
is defined as in (
8
). It is interesting to observe that this subgroup of transformations is the result of a faithful group action of the additive group
\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
on
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. More precisely, the map
\ell:\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})\times\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})\to\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
, satisfies:

\displaystyle\ell_{0}=\text{id}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle\ell_{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}=\ell_{\delta_{1}}\circ\ell_{\delta_{2}}

and, since
b
is injective, for every distinct pair
\delta_{1}\neq\delta_{2}
there correspond distinct transformations
\ell_{\delta_{1}}\neq\ell_{\delta_{2}}
. It immediately follows that the “reward space”
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
can be partitioned by the orbits of this group action (henceforth referred to as
\ell
-orbits), which are affine
n
-dimensional subspaces. This allows us to align our treatment with
[
6
]
and define an equivalence relation between model-coupled MDPs.

Definition 5
.

We consider the reward functions
r_{1},r_{2}
of two model-coupled MDPs to be equivalent, and we write
r_{1}\sim_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\ell}r_{2}
, iff they belong to the same
\ell
-orbit. In symbols

\displaystyle r_{1}\sim_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\ell}r_{2}\penalty\ \Longleftrightarrow\penalty\ \exists\delta\in\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}):r_{2}=\ell_{\delta}(r_{1}).

Two model-coupled MDPs with equivalent reward functions are said to be affine-equivalent.

Remark 6
.

For model-coupled MDPs, each MDP is uniquely determined by its reward function. Thus, in this context, we will use “MDP” and “reward function” interchangeably.

Next, we recall, without proof, the result provided in
[
6
]
showing that this group action preserves the advantage functions.

Proposition 7

(
[
6
, Theorem 3.5]
)
.

If
r\sim_{\scriptscriptstyle\!\ell}r^{\prime}
are two equivalent reward functions, then:

\displaystyle\text{adv}_{\pi,r^{\prime}}=\text{adv}_{\pi,r},\quad\forall\pi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}).

Moreover, the corresponding value functions are related by:

\displaystyle v_{\pi,r^{\prime}}=v_{\pi,r}-\delta,\quad\forall\pi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U}).

In view of the previous proposition, we will refer to the transformations (
12
) as
advantage-preserving
. Another useful property shown in
[
6
]
is recalled in the following lemma.

Lemma 8
.

For any reward function
r
and deterministic policy
\pi
, it holds

\displaystyle\ell_{\scalebox{0.7}{$v_{\pi,r}$}}(r)=\text{adv}_{\pi,r}.

In summary, if we set
\delta
as the value function associated to a given policy and reward function, the corresponding transformation maps the reward function into its advantage function relative to that policy. The above results lead to the following corollary, which follows from the properties of the advantage function
\text{adv}_{\pi^{*}\!,r}
relative to an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
.

Corollary 9
.

For any reward function
r
, if
v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}
is the optimal value function, then the transformed reward function
\ell_{\scalebox{0.7}{$v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}$}}\!(r)
is nonpositive and the corresponding MDP is in normal form.

Leveraging these observations, we provide the following result, which is a consequence of the faithfulness of the action
\ell
.

Theorem 10

(
Characterization of normal MDPs
)
.

Consider a class
\mathfrak{M}\left(\cdot,\gamma f\right)
of model-coupled MDPs. Then:

(a)

In every
\ell
-orbit there exists a unique normal MDP.

(b)

An MDP
\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma f\right)
is in normal form if and only if its reward function satisfies

(14)

\displaystyle\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{x}}\,r(u)=0,\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

Proof.

(a)
The existence follows directly from Corollary
9
. To show uniqueness assume
\pi^{*}
is an optimal policy for the MDP and assume the latter to be in normal form. In particular, by (
9
), this implies that
r_{\pi^{*}}=0
. Now, consider any normal reward function
r^{\prime}
in the same orbit, i.e., such that
r_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime}=0
and
\ell_{\delta}(r)=r^{\prime}
for some
\delta\in\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
. From the latter, written in vector form, we have that

\displaystyle R_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}R\bigr)_{\pi^{*}}

\displaystyle=\bigl(R+\left(\gamma F-S\right)\Delta\bigr)_{\pi^{*}}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\underbrace{\Pi^{*}R}_{=0}+\,\Pi^{*}\!\left(\gamma F-S\right)\Delta\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi^{*}}\bigr)\Delta

and thus, from the normality of
R^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle 0=R_{\pi^{*}}^{\prime}=-\bigl(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi^{*}}\bigr)\Delta\penalty\ \Longleftrightarrow\penalty\ \Delta=0,

since
(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi^{*}})\in GL(n,\mathbb{R})
. Hence,
r^{\prime}=\ell_{0}(r)=r
, which proves the uniqueness.

(b)
By putting together Corollary
9
and Theorem
10-(a)
it follows that the reward function of a normal MDP is nonpositive and maps to zero at least one action in the fiber of each state, implying (
14
). Conversely, if we assume (
14
), the reward function is nonpositive and there exists a policy
\hat{\pi}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U})
satisfying
\hat{\pi}(x)\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{x}}r(u)
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, such that
v_{\hat{\pi},r}=0
, see (
9
). Now, by Lemma
8
,

\displaystyle\text{adv}_{\hat{\pi},r}=\ell_{v_{\hat{\pi},r}}(r)=\ell_{0}(r)=r\leq 0,

meaning that any action differing from the one selected by
\hat{\pi}
in the same fiber yields a nonpositive advantage. Therefore,
\hat{\pi}
is optimal and, since its value function is zero, the MDP is in normal form.

\Box

Based on this result, we give the following definition.

Definition 11

(
Normal set
)
.

We define the normal set for a class of comparable MDPs as the subset
\mathcal{N}\subset\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
consisting of all the reward functions satisfying condition (
14
):

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\left\{r\in\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R}):\penalty\ \max_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{x}}\,r(u)=0,\penalty\ \forall x\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

Theorem
10-(b)
motivates the term “normal” set, as it is exactly the set of all reward functions in normal form. The following result characterizes the structure of this set and shows that the reward space
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
has a fiber bundle structure.

Theorem 12

(
Characterization of the normal set
)
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be the normal set of a class
\mathfrak{M}\left(\cdot,\gamma f\right)
of model-coupled MDPs, equipped with the relative topology
3
3

3

The sets
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
and
\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
are endowed with the topologies induced by the vector identification introduced in the previous section.
. Then:

(a)

The normal set
\mathcal{N}
admits a decomposition as a union

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=\!\!\bigcup_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U})}\!\!\mathcal{N}_{\pi},

where
\mathcal{N}_{\pi}
, defined as

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\pi}\coloneqq\{r\in\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R}):r\leq 0,r_{\pi}=0\},

is the set of normal rewards for which
\pi
is optimal.

(b)

The reward space
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
is homeomorphic to the product
\mathcal{N}\times\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
and the map

(18a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}:\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{N}

(18b)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\mapsto\ell_{v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}}(r),

is a continuous projection which makes it the total space of a principal bundle over
\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

(a)
The decomposition naturally follows from the definition of
\mathcal{N}
, while the policy
\pi
is optimal for any
r\in\mathcal{N}_{\pi}
since it satisfies
r_{\pi}=0
and has zero value function.

(b)
Since the action is faithful, each
\ell
-orbit is isomorphic to the group
\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
and also intersects the normal set in just one point by Theorem
10-(a)
. Moreover, by Corollary
9
and Theorem
10-(a)
, the map
\mathcal{P}
defined above maps each
\ell
-orbit in its unique intersection point with
\mathcal{N}
.
Furthermore, it is surjective and idempotent, since for each
r\in\mathcal{N}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(r)=\ell_{v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}}(r)=\ell_{0}(r)=r,

and thus,
\mathcal{P}\rvert_{\mathcal{N}}=\text{id}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}}
. Therefore, we can identify
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
with the product
\mathcal{N}\times\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
through the mapping
r\mapsto\left(\mathcal{P}(r),v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}\right)
whose inverse is continuous and given by
(r_{n},\delta)\mapsto r_{n}-b(\delta)
, where
b
is the linear transform characterizing the
\ell
-action, see (
12
). It only remains to prove that
\mathcal{P}
is continuous. Consider the quotient space
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})/_{\sim_{\ell}}\coloneqq\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})/\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
with quotient map
q
, whose restriction
q\rvert_{\mathcal{N}}
is continuous and bijective by construction. The projection
\mathcal{P}
can be expressed as
\mathcal{P}=(q\rvert_{\mathcal{N}})^{-1}\circ q
, and it is continuous if and only if
(q\rvert_{\mathcal{N}})^{-1}
is continuous. If
U\subseteq\mathcal{N}
is an arbitrary
\mathcal{N}
-closed set it is also
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
-closed, since
\mathcal{N}
is
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
-closed. Furthermore,

\displaystyle q^{-1}(q(U))=\{r:r=r_{n}-b(\delta),r_{n}\in U,\delta\in\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})\},

and it is therefore
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
-closed, implying that
q(U)
is
(\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})/_{\sim_{\ell}})
-closed, being the preimage of a closed set through a quotient map. This further implies that
q
is also a closed map and its restriction
q\rvert_{\mathcal{N}}
is a homeomorphism.

\Box

The previous theorem has two main consequences:

Part (a), which holds for all comparable MDPs, implies that a policy
\pi
is optimal for a given MDP if and only if the corresponding
\ell
-orbit intersects
\mathcal{N}_{\pi}
. Note that the subsets
\mathcal{N}_{\pi}
are not disjoint. Their intersections correspond precisely to normal rewards that admit multiple optimal policies. In particular, if the
\ell
-orbits of two comparable MDPs intersect the normal set in
\mathcal{N}_{\pi^{*}}\setminus\bigcup_{\pi\neq\pi^{*}}\mathcal{N}_{\pi}
, then the two MDPs share the same unique optimal policy
\pi^{*}
. Hence, they can be regarded as equivalent with respect to the normalization process: replacing one with the other yields, after the normalization process, a greedy policy that is optimal for both. On the other hand, suppose the
\ell
-orbit of one MDP intersects
\mathcal{N}_{\pi^{*}}\setminus\bigcup_{\pi\neq\pi^{*}}\mathcal{N}_{\pi}
, while the
\ell
-orbit of the other intersects
\mathcal{N}_{\pi^{*}}\cap\mathcal{N}_{\pi}
, for some other
\pi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U})
. Then they cannot be considered equivalent. In fact, normalizing the first still yields the unique optimal policy
\pi^{*}
, which remains optimal for the second (although the other optimal policy
\pi
would be ignored). However, normalizing the second would yield two optimal policies, only one of which is optimal for the first. A tie-breaking rule could then select a policy that is suboptimal for the first. These considerations should be taken into account when dealing with uncertainty in the model used to normalize an unknown or partially known MDP (see next section).

Part (b) implies that any reward function
r
is uniquely determined by the pair consisting of the corresponding normal reward function
r_{n}
and optimal value function
v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}
, which leads to a natural identification:

(19)

\displaystyle r\leftrightarrow(r_{n},v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}).

MDP Symmetry and Reward Transformations.

We conclude this subsection by briefly characterizing reward transformations that, when the original MDP is acted upon by a group
G
, preserve the symmetry. Recalling Definition
3
and (
11
), we obtain the following result.

Lemma 13
.

If
(\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma f\right),G)
is a
G
-invariant MDP, then:

(20)

\displaystyle\bigl[\ell_{\delta}(r)\bigr]\circ a^{\mathrm{u}}_{g}=\ell_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\delta\circ a^{\mathrm{x}}_{g}$}}(r),

for all
\delta\in\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
, or, in vector representation:

(21)

\displaystyle A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}R\right)=\mathcal{L}^{A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}\Delta}R.

Proof.

Let us apply an arbitrary action
A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}
to both sides of the update rule (
13
):

\displaystyle A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}R\right)

\displaystyle=A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}R+A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}\left(\gamma F-S\right)\Delta

\displaystyle\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(a)}}}}{{=}}R+\left(\gamma FA_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}-A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}S\right)\Delta

\displaystyle=R+\left(\gamma F-A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}S\bigl(A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}\bigr)^{\top}\right)A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}\Delta

\displaystyle\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(b)}}}}{{=}}R+\left(\gamma F-S\right)A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}\Delta,

where (a) follows from the invariance properties (
11
) together with the fact that each permutation matrix is an orthogonal matrix, implying
A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}F=FA_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}
. Identity (b) follows from the structure of
A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}
. On one hand it permutes the row blocks corresponding to the states. On the other hand it permutes the rows within each block, which correspond to the actions in the corresponding fiber. The latter permutation has no effect on the block-diagonal matrix
S=\diag\{\mathds{1}_{m_{i}}\!:\,i=1,\ldots,n\}
, since each block contains identical rows. Hence, the effect of
A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}
on this matrix is to permute the state blocks. This can be formalized by thinking of it as a diagonal matrix
D
, where each row corresponds to a block. In this case:

\displaystyle A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}D(A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}})^{\top}

remains diagonal, with its diagonal elements permuted according to the action
a^{\mathrm{x}}_{g}
. Finally, the group action
a^{\mathrm{x}}_{g}
permutes states with the same number of actions, and then a diagonal block
\mathds{1}_{m_{i}}
may only be replaced by a block
\mathds{1}_{m_{j}}
with the same size
(m_{i}=m_{j})
, ensuring that the block-diagonal structure is preserved under the group action.

\Box

Lemma
13
finally implies the following characterization.

Proposition 14

(
\boldsymbol{G}
-invariance and reward transformations
)
.

Let
(\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma f\right),G)
be a
G
-invariant MDP. A reward transformation
\ell_{\delta}
preserves
G
-invariance if and only if
\delta
is
G
-invariant, i.e.
\delta\circ a^{\mathrm{x}}_{g}=\delta
for all
g\in G
.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow):
If
\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}R
is invariant,
A_{g}^{\mathrm{u}}(\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}R)=\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}R
for all
g\in G
. Then, by Lemma
13
, we have:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(\gamma F-S\right)}_{=B}\left(A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}-I_{n}\right)\Delta=0,\quad\forall g\in G,

and, since
B
is injective:

\displaystyle(A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}-I_{n})\Delta=0,\quad\forall g\in G.

Therefore:

\displaystyle A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}\Delta=\Delta\penalty\ \Longleftrightarrow\penalty\ \delta\circ a^{\mathrm{x}}_{g}=\delta.

(\Leftarrow):
Follows directly from Lemma
13
.

\Box

4.3. The Set of Admissible Transformations

So far, we have introduced and analyzed the group of advantage-preserving transformations and its action on
\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
. However, not every such transformation is useful for the purpose of (iteratively) normalizing the MDP. For instance, if the MDP is already in normal form, the only feasible transformation is the identity, corresponding to
\delta=0
, by Theorem
10-(a)
. In particular, we are interested in those transformations which, when applied to a given reward function, shift the corresponding optimal value function closer to zero. By Proposition
7
, this requirement on
\ell_{\delta}
can be expressed by asking for the corresponding (unique) group element
\delta
to belong to the following set:

(22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{*}(r)\coloneqq\left\{\delta:|v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}(x)-\delta(x)|\leq|v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}(x)|,\penalty\ \forall x\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

However, as we will see in the following section, it is convenient to further restrict our attention to nonpositive reward functions. Among other benefits, this restriction allows us to reformulate the normalization of the MDP as an optimal control problem using condition (
14
). Unsurprisingly, it can always be assumed, without loss of generality, that the initial reward function is nonpositive, as justified by the following proposition, which we include for completeness.

Proposition 15
.

Given any reward function
r
, the transformation

\displaystyle r(u)\mapsto r(u)-\max_{a\in\mathcal{U}}r(a)\leq 0

is advantage-preserving.

Proof.

In vector notation, the transformation corresponds to

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{\max_{j}R^{j}}{1-\gamma}\mathds{1}_{n},

since

\displaystyle R^{\prime}

\displaystyle=R+\frac{\max_{j}R^{j}}{1-\gamma}(\gamma F-S)\mathds{1}_{n}

\displaystyle=R-\bigl(\max\nolimits_{j}R^{j}\bigr)\mathds{1}_{n}

where the last identity follows by the row-stochasticity of the
F
matrix.

\Box

It is therefore reasonable to require admissible transformations to also preserve nonpositivity, namely to satisfy

(23)

\displaystyle r\leq 0\penalty\ \implies\penalty\ \ell_{\delta}(r)\leq 0,

which is equivalent to requiring
\delta
to solve the following system of
m
linear inequalities

(24)

\displaystyle b(\delta)(u)\leq-r(u),\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U}.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{u}(r)
the closed half-space defined by each of these inequalities and by
\mathcal{C}(r)
the set

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(r)=\bigcap_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{C}_{u}(r),

corresponding to the solutions of the system (
24
).

Remark 16
.

Each action can be identified with the boundary hyperplane of the corresponding half space
\mathcal{C}_{u}(r)
through the map

\displaystyle u\mapsto\partial\mathcal{C}_{u}(r)=\{\delta:b(\delta)(u)=-r(u)\}.

The coefficients of the defining linear-affine function
r(u)+b(\cdot)(u)
for
\partial\mathcal{C}_{u}(r)
determine the entries of the action vector corresponding to
u
, as introduced in
[
6
]
. Consequently, the actions of the MDP define an arrangement of hyperplanes in the “value space”
\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, for any
\pi
it holds

(26)

\displaystyle\{v_{\pi,r}\}=\bigcap_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\partial\mathcal{C}_{\pi(x)}(r).

The discussion above motivates the following definition.

Definition 17

(
The admissible set
)
.

For any nonpositive reward function
r
, we define the admissible set of transformations for the normalization problem as

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathcal{C}(r)\cap\mathcal{B}_{*}(r).

We summarize the key properties of the admissible set in the following proposition.

Proposition 18

(
Properties of the admissible set
)
.

For any nonpositive reward function
r
, the admissible set
\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(r)
is nonempty, compact, and convex.

Proof.

The admissible set is nonempty since both the zero function and, by Corollary
9
, the optimal value function belong to this set. The fact that it is compact and convex follows immediately from the fact that it is the intersection between two closed convex sets, one of which,
\mathcal{B}_{*}(r)
, is compact.

\Box

Observe that the nonpositivity of the reward function implies the nonpositivity of each value function, see (
3
), including the optimal one. Consequently, the set
\mathcal{B}_{*}(r)
is a compact subset of the nonpositive orthant. Together with the following proposition, this provides a clear geometric interpretation of each function
\delta
belonging to this set.

Proposition 19

(
Admissible inputs as overestimates of the optimal value function
)
.

For any nonpositive reward function
r
, every function in
\mathcal{C}(r)
is an overestimate of the corresponding optimal value function. Formally:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(r)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{*}(r),

where

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{*}(r)\coloneqq\{\delta\in\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}):\delta\geq v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}\}.

Proof.

If
\delta\in\mathcal{C}(r)
then

(29)

\displaystyle r_{\pi}(x)+(\gamma\text{$f_{\pi}(x,x)$}-1)\delta(x)+\sum_{y\neq x}\gamma f_{\pi}(y,x)\delta(y)\leq 0,

for every state
x\in\mathcal{X}
and any fixed policy
\pi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U})
. Since
v_{\pi\!,r}
satisfies (
29
) with equality (by the Bellman equation (
4
)), we can rewrite (
29
) as

(30)

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma f_{\pi}(y,x)\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}(y)\leq\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}(x),

where we have introduced the shorthand notation
\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}\coloneqq\delta-v_{\pi\!,r}
. Since the left-hand side of (
30
) is a convex combination scaled by
\gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}(z)\leq\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma f_{\pi}(y,x)\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}(y)\leq\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}(x).

Now, since this holds for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\gamma\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}(z)\leq\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}(x),

or

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\min_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}(z)\geq 0,

which, since
\gamma<1
, implies that
\tilde{\delta}_{\pi,r}(x)=\delta(x)-v_{\pi\!,r}(x)\geq 0,\forall x
. Since the policy
\pi
is arbitrary, this holds for the optimal one as well, and this concludes the proof.

\Box

We can therefore interpret the admissible set as the collection of all nonpositive overestimates of the optimal value function
v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}
, whose corresponding transformations preserve the nonpositivity of the reward function. This finally yields the following characterization.

Proposition 20

(
Characterization of the admissible set via linear inequalities
)
.

The admissible set of any nonpositive reward function
r
can be expressed in terms of linear inequalities as follows:

(31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(r)=\left\{\delta\in\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}):\delta\leq 0,\penalty\ \ell_{\delta}(r)\leq 0\right\},

where
\ell_{\delta}:\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})\to\text{Map}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R})
is defined as in (
12
).

Proof.

If
\mathcal{C}_{*}(r)
denotes the set of overestimates of the optimal value function as in (
28
), and
\mathcal{O}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leq}
denotes the nonpositive orthant of
\text{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{*}(r)\cap\mathcal{B}_{*}(r)=\mathcal{C}_{*}(r)\cap\mathcal{O}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leq},

since
\mathcal{B}_{*}(r)\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leq}
trivially implies
\mathcal{C}_{*}(r)\cap\mathcal{B}_{*}(r)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{*}(r)\cap\mathcal{O}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leq}
, while:

\displaystyle\delta\in\mathcal{C}_{*}(r)\cap\mathcal{O}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leq}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow 0\geq\delta(x)\geq v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}(x),\penalty\ \forall x\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow-v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}(x)\geq\delta(x)-v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}(x)\geq 0,\penalty\ \forall x\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\delta\in\mathcal{B}_{*}(r),

or, equivalently,
\mathcal{C}_{*}(r)\cap\mathcal{O}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leq}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{*}(r)\cap\mathcal{B}_{*}(r)
. This implies, by Proposition
19
, that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(r)\cap\mathcal{B}_{*}(r)=\mathcal{C}(r)\cap\mathcal{O}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leq}.

\Box

5. Control-Theoretic Reformulation

In this section, we provide a control-theoretic reformulation of the normalization problem. Specifically, in Subsection
5.1
we formulate this control problem, while in Subsection
5.2
we characterize control laws achieving asymptotic normalization, and provide some examples thereof. Finally, in Subsection
5.3
, we reformulate the normalization problem as an optimal control problem and provide sufficient conditions on its feasibility and on the existence of solutions in the infinite-horizon case.

5.1. Normalization as an Output Stabilization Problem

In the previous section, we introduced the reward-dependent set of transformations to be used in the design of a normalizing algorithm for a given MDP. Now, we reformulate this task within a system-theoretic framework, with the idea of restating it as a control problem. To this end, consider the vector representation (
13
) of a general advantage-preserving reward transformation and let us rewrite it as an iterative law

(32)

\displaystyle R_{t+1}=R_{t}+B\Delta_{t}

where

(33)

\displaystyle B\coloneqq\gamma F-S,

and
t\in\mathbb{N}
. In doing so, we are considering the reward vector
R_{t}
as the state of a linear time-invariant system and
\Delta_{t}
as a control input. Hence, our problem boils down to searching for a feedback control law
R\mapsto\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{FB}}(R)
able to steer the reward
R
, initialized in the nonpositive orthant, towards the normal set. The normalization problem can be interpreted as an output stabilization problem, with output map given by

(34)

\displaystyle R\mapsto\underbrace{(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi^{*}})^{-1}\Pi^{*}R}_{=V_{\pi^{*}\!,r}}

where
\pi^{*}
is any optimal policy for the MDP. However, this output map is impractical, since computing it explicitly is essentially equivalent to solving the very problem the normalization process is meant to address. Therefore, we leverage Theorem
10-(b)
and replace (
34
) with a measurable nonlinear output map
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with components defined by

(35)

\displaystyle h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}^{i}(R)=\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R^{j},\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

Consequently, we consider the dynamical system

(36a)

\displaystyle R_{t+1}

\displaystyle=R_{t}+B\Delta_{t},

(36b)

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R_{t}),

and seek a sequence of admissible control inputs
\{\Delta_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(R_{t})\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
. We will refer to such a sequence as a
normalizing control law
.

5.2. Normalizing Control Laws

Before providing examples of normalizing control laws, we first present a characterization thereof.

Proposition 21

(
Characterization of normalizing controls
)
.

A control law
t\mapsto\Delta_{t}
for the dynamics (
32
) is normalizing if and only if it satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\Delta_{t}=V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}.

Proof.

By Theorem
12-(b)
, every reward vector
R
can be uniquely identified with a tuple
(\bar{R},V_{\pi^{*}\!,r})
, where
\bar{R}
is its normal representative and
V_{\pi^{*}\!,r}
is its optimal value function. In particular, it can be expressed as

\displaystyle R=\bar{R}-BV_{\pi^{*}\!,r}.

We can exploit this fact to easily put the dynamics in error coordinates with respect to the unique normal representative. In particular, we have:

\displaystyle R_{t+1}

\displaystyle=R_{t}+B\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle=R_{0}+B\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\bar{R}-BV_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}+B\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau},

and thus, by introducing
\tilde{R}\coloneqq R-\bar{R}
, we finally obtain:

(37)

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{t+1}=-B\left(V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}\right).

The statement then follows from the injectivity of
B
.

\Box

In other words, in order to normalize an MDP with reward function
r
, a control law
\{\Delta_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
must constitute a partition of the corresponding optimal value function
v_{\pi^{*}\!,r}
. We now present three examples of normalizing control laws. Two of them were originally introduced in
[
6
]
as normalization algorithms: here, we recast them as control laws within our system-theoretic framework and establish additional structural properties.

I) Ideal Normalizing Control.

The first law we present is impractical, yet it serves as the prototype for all normalizing control laws.

Definition 22
.

We define the ideal output-feedback control law by setting

(38)

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}=V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{t}}.

This control law is characterized by the following result, which is an immediate consequence of Corollary
9
and Proposition
21
.

Proposition 23
.

The ideal output-feedback control law is admissible and normalizing. In particular, it solves the problem in one step.

It follows that the corresponding input sequence is given by:

(39)

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}=\begin{cases}(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi^{*}})^{-1}\Pi^{*}R_{0},&t=0\\
0,&t>0\end{cases}

II) Full-Output Feedback.

The second law we consider is the one used in
[
6
, Algorithm 2]
, where it was originally applied to an approximate-model setting.

Definition 24
.

The full-output feedback reward-balancing method is the control law obtained by setting

(40)

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}=y_{t}.

We then provide the following result, which not only establishes its normalizing properties but also offers a clear interpretation linking this method to standard dynamic programming.

Proposition 25
.

The full-output feedback reward-balancing control law is admissible, normalizing, and equivalent to value iteration
4
4

4

For details about value iteration see, e.g.,
[
8
, Chapter 6.3]

with initial estimate
\hat{V}_{0}=h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R_{0})

Proof.

The control law is feasible by Proposition
20

R_{t}\leq 0
then clearly, for every

\Delta_{t}^{i}=\max_{l\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R^{l}_{t}\leq 0
, and, for every

(41)

\displaystyle R^{j}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=R_{t}^{j}-\Delta^{i}_{t}+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k}^{j}\Delta_{t}^{k}

(42)

\displaystyle=R_{t}^{j}-\max_{l\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R^{l}_{t}+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k}^{j}\max_{l\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}R_{t}^{l}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq 0.

Furthermore:

\displaystyle\smash{\underbrace{\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R^{j}_{t+1}}_{=\Delta_{t+1}^{i}}}

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{R^{j}_{t}-\Delta^{i}_{t}+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k}^{j}\Delta_{t}^{k}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{R_{0}^{j}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}^{i}+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k}^{j}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}^{k}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{R_{0}^{j}+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k}^{j}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}^{k}\right\}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}^{i},

which yields

(44)

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=0}^{t+1}\Delta_{\tau}^{i}

\displaystyle=\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{R_{0}^{j}+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k}^{j}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}^{k}\right\}.

By setting
\hat{V}_{t}^{i}\coloneqq\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}^{i}

(45)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{t+1}=\mathcal{T}\bigl(\hat{V}_{t}\bigr),

where
\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the well known optimal Bellman operator, which is a
\gamma
-contraction with respect to
\infty
-norm (see, e.g.,
[
8

). Consequently, (
44
) is precisely the value iteration update on
\hat{V}_{t}

\gamma
-contraction
\mathcal{T}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{t}\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}-\Delta_{0}\right\|_{\infty}

(46)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{t}\max_{i}\left|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}^{i}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R_{0}^{j}\right|,

and thus, by Proposition
21
, the control law is normalizing.

\Box

III) Safe Reward-Balancing

Definition 26
.

The safe reward-balancing (RB-S) method is the nonlinear state-feedback control law obtained by setting

\displaystyle\Delta^{i}_{t}=\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\frac{R^{j}}{1-\gamma F_{i}^{j}},\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

The properties of this control law were already established in the analysis in the aforementioned paper. We nevertheless provide them here for completeness.

Proposition 27
.

The safe reward-balancing control law is admissible and normalizing.

Proof.

The control law is admissible by Proposition

since clearly if
R_{t}\leq 0
, then
\Delta_{t}\leq 0
and, by
[

,
R_{t+1}\leq 0
. Moreover, as shown in the proof of
[

, the RB-S control law satisfies

(48)

\displaystyle\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\Delta_{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{i}\left\{-y_{0}^{i}\right\}\frac{\gamma^{t}}{1-\gamma},

which in turn implies by Proposition

that it is normalizing.

\Box

Proposition 28
.

For every nonpositive

Consider the

5.3. Normalization as an Optimal Control Problem

In this subsection, we recast the normalization problem as an optimal control problem, which can be used to design normalizing control laws in either a finite-horizon setting (where normalization can be achieved approximately, or exactly via model predictive control) or in an infinite-horizon setting. This formulation also allows for incorporating a broader variety of constraints and possibly additional penalty terms in the cost.
Let us consider the following Optimal Control Problem (OCP)

(52a)

\displaystyle\min_{\scriptscriptstyle\{(R_{t},\Delta_{t})\}}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R_{t}^{j}

subj.to:

(52b)

\displaystyle\qquad R_{0}=\bar{R}\leq 0

(52c)

\displaystyle\qquad R_{t+1}=R_{t}+B\Delta_{t},

(52d)

\displaystyle\qquad PR_{t}+Q\Delta_{t}\leq 0,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all }t\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}

where
T\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
and
P\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m},Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
satisfy

(53)

\displaystyle\emptyset\neq\mathcal{F}(R)\coloneqq\{\Delta:PR+Q\Delta\leq 0\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(R),

for all
R\leq 0
. That is, we allow for restrictions to a subset of the admissible set. In particular, if we choose:

(54)

\displaystyle P=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
I_{m}\end{bmatrix},\quad Q=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}\\
B\end{bmatrix},

with
d=m+n
, we retrieve the admissible set by Proposition
20
. In this case, the optimal solution is trivial as shown by the following result.

Proposition 29
.

If
P
and
Q
are chosen as in (
54
), then the input sequence (
39
) is the unique optimal solution of Problem (
52
), for every
T\geq 1
.

Proof.

The input sequence (
39
) normalizes the MDP in a single step, and the corresponding
stage cost

(55)

\displaystyle c_{t}\coloneqq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R_{t}^{j}

is thus zero for all
t\geq 1
. Since the reward is constrained to remain nonpositive, the cost is lower-bounded by zero and this input sequence is therefore optimal. Any other optimal solution would also need to normalize the MDP in a single step as well (by Theorem
10-(b)
). However, Proposition
21
shows that there is no
\Delta_{0}
other than
V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}
capable of achieving this.

\Box

Although known in closed form, the previous solution is impractical, as already discussed. Moreover, in the presence of model uncertainties, more conservative solutions become particularly valuable. For instance, one could make the solution more robust by restricting the feasible set or by introducing additional penalty terms in the cost. Furthermore, if the MDP exhibits symmetries, it may be desirable to enforce the input to satisfy Proposition
14
, which can be expressed as a finite set of constraints of the type
\pm(A_{g}^{\mathrm{x}}-I_{n})\Delta\leq 0
, compatible with the structure of (
52d
). For general choices of
P
and
Q
, the problem admits no solution in closed form, and exact normalization of the MDP can be achieved only asymptotically in the infinite-horizon case. In this setting, we provide two sufficient conditions on
P
and
Q
that guarantee feasibility of the problem and existence of optimal solutions.

Theorem 30

(
Feasibility of the infinite-horizon OCP
)
.

Problem (
52
) with
T=\infty
is feasible if, for every
R\leq 0
, at least one of the following conditions holds:

(a)

There exists
\alpha\in[0,1)
such that:

(56)

\displaystyle\min_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta:PR+Q\Delta\leq 0$}}\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r}-\Delta\right\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r}\right\|_{\infty}.

(b)

The output map (
35
) satisfies

(57)

\displaystyle PR+Qh_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R)\leq 0.

Moreover, under either condition, an optimal solution exists and has finite cost.

Proof.

(a) If we choose a feasible control law satisfying

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\Delta:PR_{t}+Q\Delta\leq 0$}}\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{t}}-\Delta\right\|_{\infty},

we have

\displaystyle\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{t}}-\Delta_{t}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{t}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{t-1}}-\Delta_{t-1}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{t+1}\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}\right\|_{\infty}

where we iteratively used the relation

\displaystyle V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{t}}=V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{t-1}}-\Delta_{t-1}.

It follows that the stage cost (
55
) is geometrically bounded, since

\displaystyle-\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R_{t+1}^{j}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}^{i}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{R_{t}^{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma F_{k}^{j}\Delta_{t}^{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(1)}}}}{{=}}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}^{i}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{R_{0}^{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma F_{k}^{j}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}^{k}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}^{i}-\left[\mathcal{T}\left(\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}\right)\right]^{i}-\underbrace{\left[V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}-\mathcal{T}\left(V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}\right)\right]^{i}}_{=0}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}\hskip-7.11317pt+\left\|\mathcal{T}\left(V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}\right)-\mathcal{T}\left(\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}\right)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\gamma)\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Delta_{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\gamma)\alpha^{t+1}\left\|V_{\pi^{*}\!,r_{0}}\right\|_{\infty},

where in (1) we used

\displaystyle R_{t}^{j}=R_{0}^{j}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\left(\Delta_{\tau}^{i}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma F_{k}^{j}\Delta_{\tau}^{k}\right),

and thus the summation
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}c_{t}
converges.

(b) If the output map
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}
, as introduced in (
35
), satisfies (
57
), then the feedback law
\Delta_{t}=y_{t}
is feasible for the optimal control problem. It follows from (
5.2
) and an argument analogous to that used in the previous part, that the stage cost (
55
) is geometrically bounded.

J

t+1
is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}(\bar{R})=\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{S}_{t}(\bar{R})),

where
\mathcal{L}:(R,\Delta)\mapsto R+B\Delta
, and is therefore compact by continuity of
\mathcal{L}
. Consider, without loss of generality, a sequence
(R_{k},\Delta_{k})\in\mathfrak{S}_{t+1}(\bar{R})
such that
R_{k}\to\hat{R}\in\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}(\bar{R})
as
k\to\infty
. Since

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(R_{k})\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(R_{k})\subset\mathcal{B}_{*}(R_{k})\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{*}(\bar{R})

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, see (
22
), there exists a subsequence
(R_{k^{\prime}},\Delta_{k^{\prime}})
converging to some
\bigl(\hat{R},\hat{\Delta}\bigr)\in\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}(\bar{R})\times\mathcal{B}_{*}(\bar{R})
. Since
\Delta_{k^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{F}(R_{k^{\prime}})
for every
k^{\prime}
, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}P_{i}^{l}R_{k^{\prime}}^{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}^{l}\Delta_{k^{\prime}}^{j}\leq 0,\quad\forall l\in\{1,\ldots,d\}.

Passing to the limit preserves the inequalities, and it follows by continuity that
\hat{\Delta}\in\mathcal{F}(\hat{R})
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{S}_{t+1}(\bar{R})
is sequentially compact, and thus compact. By induction
\mathfrak{S}_{t}(\bar{R})
is compact for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, and thus the space of feasible sequences, given by
\prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{S}_{t}(\bar{R})
, is compact in the product topology by Tychonoff theorem (see, e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 13]
). Furthermore, the cost is lower-semicontinuous, since it is the supremum over
\mathbb{N}
of its partial sums, which are continuous on this space. Now, under either assumption
(a)
or
(b)
there exists a feasible solution with finite cost
\bar{J}
. Hence, the sublevel set
J^{-1}((-\infty,\bar{J}])=J^{-1}([0,\bar{J}])
is nonempty. By lower-semicontinuity this set is closed and thus compact as a closed subset of a compact topological space. By the extended Weierstrass theorem for lower-semicontinuous functions,
J
attains its minimum on this set.

6. Approximate-Model Reward-Balancing Methods

In this section, we examine the consequences of performing a reward-balancing transformation when only an approximate model is available. Specifically, in Subsection
6.1
, we characterize the effect of an advantage-preserving transformation on the entire class of reward-coupled MDPs. Furthermore, in Subsection
6.2
we discuss how a sample-based normalization strategy can be used to increase the likelihood of achieving optimality in the absence of an exact model. We also propose a principled framework for the design of robust normalizing laws based on a scenario MPC approach, extending the optimal control formulation introduced in the previous section. Finally, in Subsection
6.3
we provide numerical simulations comparing the scenario MPC and the full-output feedback first introduced in
[
6
]
and recalled in the previous section.

6.1. Reward Transformations with Approximate Model

Let
\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma f\right)
be the MDP modeling the problem we aim to solve, and suppose that we have access to an approximation
\hat{f}
of the transition function, which could be obtained, for example, from experimental data. Consequently, our reference MDP is actually
\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma\hat{f}\right)
and we can normalize its reward function using one of the methods discussed in the previous section. However, at this point, a question naturally arises: what happens to the original MDP? This question is difficult to answer a priori, as it depends heavily on the specific model
\hat{f}
. Nevertheless, we can reasonably expect that the advantage functions, together with the relative positions of the value functions of
\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma f\right)
will be altered during the normalization procedure. This is because we are shifting the reward function of two reward-coupled MDPs along the orbit of one, thus moving it away from the orbit of the other. Clearly if
\hat{f}
is a good approximation of
f
, we might still expect that, in the end, the greedy policy will be optimal for
\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma f\right)
. This occurs in the case where the orbit of the approximate MDP intersects the same sections of the normal set as
\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma f\right)
, as already discussed. This being said, the normalized reward will inevitably differ from the unique normalization of the original MDP. The next proposition addresses part of the problem, by capturing the effect of an advantage-preserving reward transformation on any reward-coupled MDP.

Proposition 31
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma\hat{f}\right)
and
\mathfrak{M}\left(r,\gamma f\right)
be two reward-coupled MDPs, and suppose that the reward function is updated with an advantage-preserving transformation of the first MDP:

\displaystyle\hat{R}=R+\bigl(\gamma\hat{F}-S\bigr)\Delta.

Then, the value functions of the second MDP change according to the following relations:

(58)

\displaystyle V_{\pi,\hat{r}}=V_{\pi,r}-\Delta+\gamma\bigl(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(\hat{F}_{\pi}-F_{\pi}\bigr)\Delta,

for all
\pi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U})
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary policy
\pi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U})
. Both the value functions corresponding to
\hat{r}
and
r
, respectively, satisfy the corresponding Bellman equation. In vector notation:

(59)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=R_{\pi}-(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi})V_{\pi,r}

(60)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\underbrace{R_{\pi}-(I_{n}-\gamma\hat{F}_{\pi})\Delta}_{\eqqcolon\hat{R}_{\pi}}-(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi})V_{\pi,\hat{r}}.

The comparison between (
59
) and (
60
) leads to

(61)

\displaystyle V_{\pi,\hat{r}}=V_{\pi,r}+(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi})^{-1}\bigl(\gamma\hat{F}_{\pi}-I_{n}\bigr)\Delta.

Expanding the second term on the right-hand side,

\displaystyle(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi})^{-1}\bigl(\gamma\hat{F}_{\pi}-I_{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}(F_{\pi})^{t}\right)\bigl(\gamma\hat{F}_{\pi}-I_{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\gamma(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi})^{-1}\hat{F}_{\pi}-I_{n}-\gamma\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}(F_{\pi})^{t}\right)F_{\pi}

\displaystyle=-I_{n}+\gamma(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi})^{-1}\bigl(\hat{F}_{\pi}-F_{\pi}\bigr),

from which the proposition follows.

The following proposition shows that the additional term in (
58
) can be bounded by a function of the model mismatch and the input amplitude.

Proposition 32
.

The deviation of the perturbed update (
58
) from the exact-model update admits the following upper bound:

(62)

\displaystyle\left\|V_{\pi,\hat{r}}-(V_{\pi,r}-\Delta)\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\|\Delta\|_{2},

where
n
is the cardinality of the state space,
\gamma
is the discount factor and
\varepsilon
is the operator 2-norm of the model error:

\displaystyle\varepsilon\coloneqq\bigl\|\hat{F}-F\bigr\|_{2}.

Proof.

Let
\pi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U})
be any policy. From Proposition
31
, it immediately follows that

(63)

\displaystyle\left\|V_{\pi,\hat{r}}-(V_{\pi,r}-\Delta)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma}{\sigma^{\min}_{\pi,\gamma}}\left\|\hat{F}_{\pi}-F_{\pi}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}

(64)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma}{\sigma^{\min}_{\pi,\gamma}}\left\|\Pi\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\hat{F}-F\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}

(65)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\frac{\gamma}{\sigma^{\min}_{\pi,\gamma}}\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2},

where
\sigma^{\min}_{\pi,\gamma}
denotes the minimum singular value of
A\coloneqq(I_{n}-\gamma F_{\pi})
. Now, the matrix
A
is always diagonally dominant, regardless of the policy and the discount factor, since for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have

\displaystyle\left|A_{i}^{i}\right|=1-\gamma[F_{\pi}]_{i}^{i}>\gamma\left(1-[F_{\pi}]_{i}^{i}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(a)}}}}{{=}}\sum_{j\neq i}\gamma[F_{\pi}]_{j}^{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\left|A_{j}^{i}\right|,

where (a) follows from the row-stochasticity of
F_{\pi}
. Therefore, by
[
11
, Theorem 1]
, it satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|A^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{\alpha}

with

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{i}\left\{\left|A_{i}^{i}\right|-\sum_{j\neq i}\left|A_{j}^{i}\right|\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{i}\left\{1-\gamma[F_{\pi}]_{i}^{i}-\sum_{j\neq i}\gamma[F_{\pi}]_{j}^{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{i}\left\{1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma[F_{\pi}]_{j}^{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{i}\left\{1-\gamma\right\}

\displaystyle=1-\gamma.

Finally, norm equivalence implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{\min}_{\pi,\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\left\|A^{-1}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\left\|A^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}<\frac{\sqrt{n}}{1-\gamma}

which, used in (
63
), concludes the proof.

6.2. Reward-Balancing and Model Sampling

The previous results characterize the structural relationship between a perturbed, advantage-preserving update and the value functions of the true model. We now turn to analyzing the long term behavior of the normalization process under model uncertainties. As already discussed, choosing a single approximate model completely determines the outcome of the normalization process, and no choice of input can change this outcome. Consequently, in situations where a certain level of model accuracy cannot be guaranteed, a different approach is required. Suppose we have access to random realizations
\hat{F}_{t}
of the true model
F
. Then the reward can be updated as

(66a)

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\hat{R}_{t}+\hat{B}_{t}\Delta_{t}

(66b)

\displaystyle=\hat{R}_{t}+B\Delta_{t}+\bigl(\hat{B}_{t}-B\bigr)\Delta_{t},

where

(67)

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{t}\coloneqq\gamma\hat{F}_{t}-S,

which falls into the standard framework of stochastic systems with multiplicative noise (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
13
]
). In this setting, the outcome of a normalization process is not predetermined by a single fixed model. A first drawback of this approach is that, in general, the admissible set changes at every model realization. It is therefore convenient to identify a model-invariant subset common to
every
admissible set. For example, the set
\{\Delta:h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R)\leq\Delta\leq 0\}
is a subset of
\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(R)
for all
f
and is therefore independent of the particular model choice. In general, however, this set is not the largest subset of
\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(R)
with this invariance property, as established by the following theorem.

Theorem 33

(
The largest model-invariant admissible set
)
.

For any nonpositive reward function
r
and discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
the set

(68)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle I}(R)\coloneqq\bigcap_{i,k=1}^{n}\bigl\{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:0\geq\Delta^{i}\geq\gamma\Delta^{k}+R^{j},\penalty\ j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}\bigr\}

satisfies (
53
) for any model
f
. In particular, it is the maximal model-independent feasible set in the following sense:

(69)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle I}(R)=\bigcap_{f}\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(R).

Furthermore, it satisfies condition (b) of Theorem
30
, i.e.:

(70)

\displaystyle h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R)\in\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle I}(R),\quad\forall R\leq 0.

Proof.

Consider any
\Delta\in\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle I}(R)
and choose any model
\hat{F}
. We need to show that
\Delta\in\mathcal{A}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$\,\gamma\!f$}}(R)
, i.e., by (
31
), that
\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}(R)\leq 0
(the other condition,
\Delta\leq 0
, is satisfied by definition). Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
be any action index and
i=s(j)
be the corresponding state index. Then, we have:

\displaystyle\bigl[\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}(R)\bigr]^{j}

\displaystyle=R^{j}+(\gamma\hat{F}_{i}^{j}-1)\Delta^{i}+\sum_{k\neq i}\gamma\hat{F}_{k}^{j}\Delta^{k}

\displaystyle=R^{j}-\Delta^{i}+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n}\hat{F}_{k}^{j}\Delta^{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(a)}}}}{{=}}-\Delta^{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\hat{F}_{k}^{j}\bigl(\gamma\Delta^{k}+R^{j}\bigr)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(b)}}}}{{\leq}}-\Delta^{i}+\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\hat{F}_{k}^{j}}_{=1}\Delta^{i}

\displaystyle=0,

where
(a)
follows from the row-stochasticity of
\hat{F}
, whereas
(b)
follows from the definition (
68
). Since
j
is arbitrary, this shows that
\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}(R)\leq 0
, thus concluding the first part of the proof. In general, some of the linear inequalities defining the set
\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle I}(R)
could be redundant: we say that a triple
(i,j,k)
is active if the corresponding inequality
\Delta^{i}\geq\gamma\Delta^{k}+R^{j}
is non-redundant, i.e., we cannot remove it without changing the set. Observe that the inequality corresponding to any active triple
(i,j,k)
coincides with the inequality
[\mathcal{L}_{\Delta}(R)]^{j}\leq 0
, for a model
\hat{F}
such that
\hat{F}_{k}^{j}=1
and
\hat{F}_{l}^{j}=0
for all
l\neq k
. Indeed:

\displaystyle 0\geq R^{j}-\Delta^{i}+\sum_{l=1}^{n}\gamma\hat{F}_{l}^{j}\Delta^{l}=R^{j}-\Delta^{i}+\gamma\Delta^{k},

which shows that the inequality corresponding to the active triple
(i,j,k)
is indeed part of the definition of the feasible set for this specific model, and this concludes the proof of the second part. Finally, if
R\leq 0
, for all index tuples
(i,j,k)
, with
j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}
, it holds:

\displaystyle 0\geq\max\nolimits_{l\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R^{l}\geq R^{j}\geq R^{j}+\underbrace{\gamma\max\nolimits_{l\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}R^{l}}_{\leq 0},

and thus
h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R)\in\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle I}(R)
, which is precisely condition (b) of Theorem
30
.

\Box

Theorem
33
guarantees that, if we have an initial reward
\hat{R}_{0}\leq 0
and we apply (
66
) with a control input
\Delta_{t}\in\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle I}(\hat{R}_{t})
, then
\hat{R}_{t}
remains nonpositive for all
t
, regardless of the specific model realizations
\{\hat{B}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
. However, we have no guarantee that the reward vector can be asymptotically normalized via (
66
). The following theorem addresses this issue by providing a sufficient condition for a control law to preserve the nonpositivity of the reward vector and to make
\hat{R}_{t}
converge to the normal set, regardless of the sequence of models used for the update.

Theorem 34

(
Sufficient condition for robust normalization
)
.

If an input control law
h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies

(71a)

\displaystyle h(R)\in\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle I}(R)

(71b)

\displaystyle h^{i}(R)+\gamma\left\|h(R)\right\|_{\infty}\leq h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}^{i}(R)+\alpha\left\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R)\right\|_{\infty},

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
R\leq 0
and for some
\alpha\in[0,1)
, then
\Delta_{t}=h(\hat{R}_{t})
is geometrically normalizing for the stochastic update (
66
), regardless of the realization
\{\hat{B}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and the initial condition
\hat{R}_{0}\leq 0
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{t}=h(\hat{R}_{t})
satisfy (
71
). Then, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have:

\displaystyle\left|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}^{i}\bigl(\hat{R}_{t+1}\bigr)\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(a)}}}}{{=}}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\hat{R}_{t+1}^{j}

\displaystyle=-\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{\hat{R}_{t}^{j}+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl[\hat{F}_{t}\bigr]_{k}^{j}\Delta_{t}^{k}-\Delta_{t}^{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}^{i}+\min_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{-\hat{R}_{t}^{j}+\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl[\hat{F}_{t}\bigr]_{k}^{j}\left(-\Delta_{t}^{k}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(b)}}}}{{\leq}}\Delta_{t}^{i}+\min_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{-\hat{R}_{t}^{j}+\gamma\max_{k}\left\{-\Delta_{t}^{k}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}^{i}+\min_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{-\hat{R}_{t}^{j}\right\}+\gamma\max_{k}\left\{-\Delta_{t}^{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(c)}}}}{{=}}\Delta_{t}^{i}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\left\{\hat{R}_{t}^{j}\right\}+\gamma\max_{k}\left\{\bigl|\Delta_{t}^{k}\bigr|\right\}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}^{i}-h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}^{i}\bigl(\hat{R}_{t}\bigr)+\gamma\left\|\Delta_{t}\right\|_{\infty}

(72)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(d)}}}}{{\leq}}\alpha\left\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}\bigl(\hat{R}_{t}\bigr)\right\|_{\infty},

where (a) holds since (
71a
) ensures sign preservation of the reward and thus
\hat{R}_{0}\leq 0\implies h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(\hat{R}_{t})\leq 0
for all
t
, (b) holds since any realization
\hat{F}_{t}
is row-stochastic, (c) holds since (
71a
) implies
\Delta_{t}\leq 0
, and (d) follows from (
71b
). Since (
72
) holds for all
i
, we have:

(73)

\displaystyle\left\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}\bigl(\hat{R}_{t+1}\bigr)\right\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha\left\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}\bigl(\hat{R}_{t}\bigr)\right\|_{\infty},

and thus
\hat{R}_{t}
asymptotically converges to the normal set by Theorem
10-(b)
.

\Box

be the filtration

(80)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma\bigl(\hat{F}_{0},\ldots,\hat{F}_{t-1}\bigr).

Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\bigl(\hat{F}_{\tau}-F\bigr)\Delta_{\tau}\mid\mathfrak{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\bigl(\hat{F}_{\tau}-F\bigr)\Delta_{\tau}+\gamma\bigl(\hat{F}_{t}-F\bigr)\Delta_{t}\mid\mathfrak{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}+\gamma\bigl(\hat{F}_{t}-F\bigr)\Delta_{t}\mid\mathfrak{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(a)}}}}{{=}}M_{t}+\gamma\mathbb{E}\left[\bigl(\hat{F}_{t}-F\bigr)\right]\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle=M_{t},

where
(a)
follows from the fact that
\Delta_{t}
is independent of
\hat{F}_{t}
and is thus
\mathfrak{F}_{t}
-measurable. Therefore,
M_{t}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued martingale, whose martingale increments are bounded as follows

(81)

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt\left\|M_{t}-M_{t-1}\right\|_{\infty}=\gamma\left\|\bigl(\hat{F}_{t-1}-F\bigr)\Delta_{t-1}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mu\gamma\left\|\Delta_{t-1}\right\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, square-summability of
\{\Delta_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
implies

(82)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\left\|M_{t}-M_{t-1}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq\mu^{2}\gamma^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\left\|\Delta_{t-1}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}<\infty.

Finally, as a Hilbert space under the Euclidean inner product,
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2})
is a
(2,D)
-smooth separable Banach space with
D=1
, which, together with (
82
), allows us to apply the maximal inequality for 2-smooth Banach spaces
[
14
, Theorem 3.5]
, yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\mu^{2}\gamma^{2}\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\left\|\Delta_{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}\right),

for all
\varepsilon>0
. Since, by norm equivalence,
\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{2}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{2}.

This implies the following event inclusion

\displaystyle\left\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon\right\}\subseteq\left\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right\},

which leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right).

\Box

Theorem 40

(

)
.

Let
\{\hat{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random realizations of

such that

and
\left\|\hat{F}_{t}-F\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mu

and for all

and

(83)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|\hat{R}_{t}-R_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-c\frac{(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{min}})^{2}}{\left\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R_{0})\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}\right),

for all
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{min}}

(84a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{min}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2\frac{1+\gamma}{(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)}\left\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R_{0})\right\|_{\infty}

(84b)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{(1-\alpha^{2})(1-\gamma)^{2}}{2\mu^{2}\gamma^{2}}.

Proof.

Leveraging (
75
), we have, for all
t>0
:

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{R}_{t}-R_{t}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\bigl[\hat{B}_{\tau}-B\bigr]h\bigl(\hat{R}_{\tau}\bigr)+B\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\bigl[h\bigl(\hat{R}_{\tau}\bigr)-h(R_{\tau})\bigr]\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(1)}}}}{{\leq}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}+(1+\gamma)\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\left\|h\bigl(\hat{R}_{\tau}\bigr)-h(R_{\tau})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}+(1+\gamma)\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\left(\left\|h\bigl(\hat{R}_{\tau}\bigr)\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|h(R_{\tau})\right\|_{\infty}\right)

(85)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{(2)}}}}{{\leq}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}+2\frac{1+\gamma}{1-\gamma}\left\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R_{0})\right\|_{\infty}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\alpha^{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}+\underbrace{2\frac{1+\gamma}{(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)}\left\|h_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{max}}(R_{0})\right\|_{\infty}}_{=\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{min}}},

where in (1) we used (
77

\displaystyle\left\|B\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{j}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl|B_{k}^{j}\bigr|\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{j}\left\{\bigl(1-\gamma F_{\scalebox{0.6}{$s(j)$}}^{j}\bigr)+\gamma\bigl(1-F_{\scalebox{0.6}{$s(j)$}}^{j}\bigr)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{j}\left\{1+\gamma-2\gamma F_{\scalebox{0.6}{$s(j)$}}^{j}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\gamma,

while (2) follows from Lemma
36
. Hence, since for all

(86)

\displaystyle\left\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|\hat{R}_{t}-R_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon\right\}\subseteq\left\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|M_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{min}}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|\hat{R}_{t}-R_{t}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{min}})^{2}}{2\mu^{2}\gamma^{2}\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\left\|h(\hat{R}_{\tau})\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}\right).

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\left\|h(\hat{R}_{\tau})\right\|_{\infty}^{2}

We conclude the section by leveraging the optimal control framework introduced in Subsection
5.3
to design a normalizing feedback control law. This framework allows us to exploit both constraints and the cost function as degrees of freedom for the design of a control law for the stochastic dynamics (
66
). Ideally, we aim to design a robust normalizing control law, for instance, one satisfying the conditions of Theorem
34
. Condition (
71a
) can be directly enforced as a constraint, since it can be expressed as a finite set of linear inequalities of the form (
52d
). On the other hand, instead of explicitly enforcing (
71b
), we adopt a scenario-based approach. Specifically, we consider a finite number of realizations (scenarios) of the stochastic dynamics over a sufficiently long prediction horizon, and we seek a given control input that minimizes the average cumulative output defined in (
35
), in analogy with Problem
52
. This leads to a scenario-based MPC formulation, as described in
[
15
, Section 3.2]
. Given a reward vector
R_{t}
at time
t
, we consider the following optimal control problem

(87a)

\displaystyle\min_{\scriptscriptstyle\{(R_{\sigma,\tau},\Delta_{\tau})\}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\sigma=1}^{N}\sum_{\tau=t}^{t+T-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\max_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}R_{\sigma,\tau+1}^{j}\right)+\epsilon\left\|\Delta_{\tau}\right\|_{2}^{2}

subj.to:

(87b)

\displaystyle\qquad R_{\sigma,t}=R_{t}\leq 0,

(87c)

\displaystyle\qquad R_{\sigma,\tau+1}=R_{\sigma,\tau}+\hat{B}_{\sigma,\tau}\Delta_{\tau},

(87d)

\displaystyle\qquad\Delta_{\tau}\in\mathcal{F}^{\scriptscriptstyle I}(R_{\sigma,\tau}),

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all }\tau\in\{t,\ldots,t+T-1\},\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

Here,
N
is the number of sampled scenarios, while the regularization term
\epsilon\left\|\Delta_{\tau}\right\|_{2}^{2}
promotes square-summability of the control input. At each iteration
t
, we sample
N
independent realizations and solve problem (
87
). The first input solution
\Delta_{t}
, which depends on the current reward
R_{t}
, is then used to update the reward with newly sampled model
\hat{B}_{t}
, and the process is repeated in a receding horizon fashion. This approach yields a feasible, model-invariant feedback control law, which can be compared with the full-output feedback strategy of Definition
24
. The latter has been selected because it is the only control law introduced in Section
5.2
that satisfies the conditions of Theorem
34
(cf. Corollary
35
). In the following subsection, we present a numerical example in which the MPC feedback law derived from (
87
) outperforms this strategy, originally proposed in
[
6
]
.
