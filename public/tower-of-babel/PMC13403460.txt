Therapeutic intensification in post-traumatic stress disorder worldwide: where do we stand?
Authors: Arnaud Leroy, Eric Bui, Emmanuelle Verkest, Frédérique Warembourg, Ad de Jongh, Guillaume Vaiva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: First-line treatments for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are trauma-focused psychotherapies. To overcome the limitations of these approaches, intensive and massed programmes have shown promising outcomes.Objective: To provide a structured narrative literature review on recent advances in intensive and massed programmes for psychological trauma over the past five years.Methods: All clinical studies investigating massed or intensive therapies were included, including qualitative research.Results: Two main approaches of intensive or massed programmes have emerged, mainly in North America and Europe. These two models differ in their modalities (individual vs. group-based interventions) and in the extent of validation across diverse populations. Across the two approaches, both therapeutic alliance and trauma-related negative cognitions predict treatment response.Conclusion: Current evidence supports the continued use of intensive and massed programmes and underscores the need to further develop and refine them to address existing limitations.KEYWORDS: PTSD, therapy, intensive, massed, review

Introduction

Trauma-related disorders, due to both their prevalence and individual impact, have long represented a major public health concern as well as a significant societal issue. Especially, trauma exposure can contribute to the development of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms, including intrusion, avoidance of situations or thoughts reminiscent of the trauma, hypervigilance, and negative alterations in mood and cognition. The prevalence of trauma-related disorders, including PTSD, but also comorbid disorders such as depression and anxiety disorders, is significant. The 12-month prevalence of PTSD ranges from 1 to 8.8% (Alonso et al., 2004; Burback et al., 2024; Kessler et al., 1995; Koenen et al., 2017; Vaiva et al., 2008; Wittchen et al., 2012). Further, psychiatric comorbidities including mood disorders, anxiety disorders, psychotic symptoms and substance use disorders (Kessler et al., 2005; Sareen et al., 2007) are also frequent. Finally, it has been recently reported that exposure to traumatic events may be a risk factor for suicide, regardless of diagnosis (DeVylder et al., 2018).

There are several barriers to the implementation of specialized trauma-focused psychotherapies, including prolonged exposure (PE), cognitive processing therapy (CPT) and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) therapy, which are first-line recommendations for PTSD treatment in most international guidelines (Hamblen et al., 2019). Limited availability of care remains a major concern. Even when therapists are accessible and available, they are not necessarily trained in evidence-based psychotherapies (Ehlers et al., 2013; Richards et al., 2017). It is estimated that fewer than two out of ten patients with PTSD actually receive an evidence-based therapy (Watts et al., 2013). Logistical barriers represent another major challenge. Most recommended PTSD therapies involve an average of 12 weekly sessions. Attending weekly sessions regularly can be difficult due to environmental stressors, family obligations, work schedule changes, transportation difficulties, or a loss of motivation, all of which may interfere with treatment compliance. Some surveys indicate that work-related conflicts or the need to travel to specialized clinics may further prevent patients from engaging consistently in treatment (Lankila et al., 2022). Such logistical barriers contribute to premature treatment discontinuation and therapeutic failure. Clinical barriers related to individual patient characteristics including symptoms must also be considered. Especially, only a few studies have specifically addressed the treatment of complex PTSD (CPTSD) (Karatzias & Cloitre, 2019), Moreover, CPTSD is not assessed with DSM-5 that is used in most randomized controlled trials in the US. Current recommendations suggest initiating psychotherapy with a stabilization phase (phase 1) aimed at ensuring safety through improved self-regulation and enhancement of emotional, social, and psychological skills. A second phase (phase 2) then focuses on trauma processing and the treatment of traumatic memories, while a third phase (phase 3) consolidates therapeutic gains and supports reintegration into daily life (Cloitre et al., 2011). However, randomized controlled studies suggest that the utility of a preliminary stabilization phase is limited and unnecessary, since direct trauma-focused interventions (phase 2 without phase 1) have demonstrated efficacy in several trials (De Jongh et al., 2016; Oprel et al., 2024; Van Vliet et al. 2021). Moreover, there is no clear clinical recommendation for resistant PTSD (ie. treatment non-response) (Fonzo et al., 2020).

In recent years, several massed and intensive programmes have been developed for the treatment of PTSD (Johnson et al., 1996; van Woudenberg et al., 2023). Terminology in the PTSD literature remains inconsistent, with the terms massed, intensive, and accelerated often used interchangeably across studies, to describe trauma-focused therapies delivered more frequently than typical weekly sessions, either with or without adjunctive interventions.

Massed therapies generally refer to a single type of therapeutic approach delivered at high frequency, whereas intensive therapies refer to a collection of therapeutic approaches delivered over a short period of time. Although these concepts are distinct, they share a common feature, namely the provision of a greater amount of individual or group psychotherapy within a condensed timeframe. Thus, despite these differences, both modalities explore the same dimension of care, with heterogeneity across programmes that warrants clarification. Even though a few studies compared massed versus intensive approaches without finding significant results (Peterson et al., 2023), the diversity of approaches led us to group these studies by emphasizing a dimensional aspect. In most cases, these programmes are embedded within a broader multidisciplinary and integrative care programme, which facilitates patient acceptance of and tolerance for trauma-focused therapy. These programmes typically combine both individual and group sessions. Trauma-focused psychotherapies are generally delivered in individual sessions, while psychoeducation, and physical activities are provided in group settings. Programme duration usually ranges from 2 to 4 weeks, or several days (Bongaerts et al., 2022). Massed and intensive treatment programmes present several advantages: (a) they reduce systemic barriers to PTSD treatment by providing comprehensive care in a single setting and within a condensed timeframe; and (b) they reduce individual barriers by integrating adjunctive therapies aimed at enhancing emotional regulation and tolerance of trauma-focused work, while group settings foster therapeutic alliance and engagement. Moreover, because the full treatment protocol is delivered over a shorter period than standard treatment, this format offers the potential for more rapid symptom relief, faster improvements in global functioning, and earlier resumption of meaningful daily activities. By delivering the same ‘dose of effective psychotherapy’ over a shorter duration, attrition rates are also reduced (Zalta et al., 2018; Tanev et al., 2019; Woudenberg et al., 2023; Zepeda Méndez et al., 2018). Overall, for comparable therapeutic efficacy (Johnson et al., 1996; Bryan et al., 2018; Foa et al., 2018; Woudenberg et al., 2023; Zalta et al., 2018; Zepeda Méndez et al., 2018), these approaches enable treatment of a greater number of patients with improved acceptability, thereby representing a significant public health benefit.

Over the past decade, a few publications have reported the results of massed and intensive PTSD programmes, consistently demonstrating substantial effectiveness and low dropout rates (2.3% versus 20% for traditionally delivered psychotherapies) (Harvey et al., 2019; Van Woudenberg et al., 2018). It has been summarized in recent reviews reporting 0–13,6% dropout rate (Sciarrino et al., 2020). Importantly, these effects appear to be sustained over time, with benefits maintained up to 12 months after treatment (Hendriks et al., 2018), with large effect size on PTSD symptoms (Cohen’s d > 1.2) and depression (Cohen’s d > 1).

Despite substantial effects on PTSD and depression symptoms, the number of studies remains limited, and models of care remain insufficiently characterized, with their active components and determinants of outcome still poorly understood. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have proposed various forms of massed or intensive therapy, targeting different population subtypes (age, trauma index and target group). Especially, programme modalities vary in terms of treatment frequency, type of therapeutic approach, and care setting (e.g. outpatient vs. inpatient), and format (such as telehealth). To account for recent advances in the field and clarify the recent amount of data, we propose a five-year structured narrative literature review aimed at clarifying the different models used, their effectiveness, recent innovations and key theoretical concepts, as well as the factors contributing to effectiveness, from both the caregivers’ and the patients’ perspectives. The objective is therefore to provide initial avenues for defining the strengths and limitation of these models, with the aim of informing clinical applications in the coming years.

Methods

Search strategy and methodology

We conducted a structured narrative review following a structured and comprehensive literature search of three online bibliographic databases: PubMed, MEDLINE, and Embase, covering all available publication from 1 January 2020 to 24 November 2025. This review provides a structured, theory-informed overview of emerging intensive PTSD treatment formats rather than a systematic quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis. An empirical approach was applied to construct a primary search strategy aimed at identifying relevant studies. The search terms used were: (‘PTSD’ OR ‘post-traumatic stress disorder’ OR ‘post-traumatic stress disorder’) AND (‘intensive’ OR ‘massed’) AND (‘psychotherapy’ OR ‘therapy’). Systematic reviews and protocols were excluded from the analysis. Titles and abstracts were independently screened for relevance by two authors. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Inclusion criteria were clinical studies involving PTSD massed or intensive psychotherapeutic treatment (we defined as more than two trauma-focused psychotherapy sessions per week (Holliday et al., 2025b), that is the usual frequency of sessions in ‘as usual’ psychotherapeutic programmes). Full texts of potentially eligible studies were then reviewed in detail. In addition, we performed manual screening of the reference lists of all articles to identify any additional studies not captured in the initial search. Furthermore, relevant studies known to the authors but not identified through database searches were also considered for inclusion. Data extraction was conducted in a structured, nonstandardized manner, consistent with the narrative and theory-driven aims of this review. Key methodological and clinical characteristics were summarized for each study, including design, sample characteristics, PTSD diagnostic criteria, treatment modality and format, intervention type, treatment intensity and duration, care setting, and outcomes. Given the heterogeneity of included studies, no formal extraction instrument, quantitative harmonization, or risk-of-bias assessment was applied. Studies were synthesized qualitatively and grouped by structural and conceptual characteristics to support an interpretive review of the literature.

This review also used a participatory approach, incorporating lived-experience expertise throughout study selection and interpretation. Qualitative studies were included to capture participants’ experiences in intensive treatment programmes. Integrating experiential perspectives with clinical outcomes was intended to provide a more balanced understanding of perceived benefits and limitations and to enhance ecological validity.

Importantly, this review was conducted within a fully participatory framework, integrating lived-experience expert perspectives at all stages of the research process, including study selection and interpretation. Particular attention was paid to the inclusion of qualitative research in order to capture in-depth, contextualized accounts of participants’ experiences, all of whom were engaged in intensive therapeutic programmes. This approach aims to ensure a comprehensive and balanced understanding of these interventions by systematically considering both their perceived benefits and limitations from the perspective of those directly involved. The integration of experiential knowledge aims to complement clinical outcomes and strengthen the ecological validity and relevance of the findings.

Results

Study selection

After adjusting for duplicates, the searches provided a total of 527 citations. Of these, 370 studies were discarded (on the basis of title and abstract) because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. After examination of the full texts of the remaining 134 articles, eight additional articles were excluded. Ultimately, 126 studies were included in the structured narrative review (Figure 1). Overlapping cohorts from the same programmes were taken into account, but not counted multiple times in the total number of patients. The review was written as a structured narrative review, to provide an organization of the literature according to themes, mechanisms, and models, while ensuring a clear argumentative framework and transparency in methodological choices. This choice was made to preserve a conceptual and theory-driven perspective, allowing for a structured synthesis of mechanisms and models rather than a purely systematic aggregation of findings. Thus, although some PRISMA criteria were applied, they were not implemented strictly. In particular, duplicates were retained, and a formal quality check was not performed due to the large number of studies with a low level of evidence (mainly observational studies or non-randomized studies) in order to allow for a theory-driven model to be argued.

Study characteristics

These studies involved 36,042 patients in 126 studies (5 RCTs, 17 comparative studies, 91 observational studies, and 6 case series or case report studies, 1 retrospective study and 6 qualitative studies), after removing duplicates (Supplementary Figure 1).

Models of care

Main different models

Over the past five years, several intensive or massed therapy models have been studied, of which two were most frequently reported (Table 1). Terminology in the PTSD literature remains inconsistent, with the terms massed, intensive, and accelerated often used interchangeably across studies. The categorizations proposed in this review are therefore intended as pragmatic and descriptive organizational tools rather than formal or standardized classifications. Across the manuscript, for the sake of clarity, and as it is a structured narrative review, we choose to separate these two main approaches based on their main therapeutic approach from a dimensional perspective: Single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes and multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes. The first one has been studied nearly exclusively in North America, and the second one (Woudenberg et al., 2023), has been developed in the Netherlands by the team of PSYTREC mental health centre, and has so far been reported exclusively in European studies. Although some geographical patterns may be observed, the present article does not aim to reduce any given model to a specific geographic region. While we acknowledge that certain programmes are more frequently reported in particular geographic areas, this oversimplified dichotomy may not accurately reflect the diversity within each category. Moreover, given the dimensional nature of intensive trauma-focused treatments, some overlap between programme categories is expected, and programmes should be interpreted as existing along a continuum of treatment structure and intensity rather than within strictly separated categories.

Population differences and philosophy of care

Single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes are extensively studied, with recent literature reporting more than 31,000 patients, primarily veterans or active-duty military personnel, and occasionally suicide-bereaved military widows (Ohye et al., 2022). Patients are generally treated in outpatient units, often accommodated in nearby hotels, and rarely via telehealth (Verdi et al., 2025). Programme durations range from 1 (Held, Kovacevic, et al., 2022) to 3 weeks (Kovacevic, Bravo, et al., 2023), with follow-up periods up to 12 months (Ptak et al., 2025a), during which effects are maintained. Treatment augmentation has also been proposed, with promising results (Bryan et al., 2025).

Multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes have also been widely published, involving over 4,000 patients in recent years. Numerous populations have been studied, with positive outcomes across all groups, including: adolescents and young adults (Ooms-Evers et al., 2021; Rentinck et al., 2025; van Pelt et al., 2021), severe/resistant PTSD (Klaeth et al., 2024; van Pelt et al., 2021; Zoet et al., 2021), complex PTSD/dissociative subtype (Bongaerts et al., 2021, 2022; Gahnfelt et al., 2025; Matthijssen et al., 2024; Paridaen et al., 2023; van der Linde et al., 2023; Voorendonk et al., 2020), somatoform dissociation (Zoet et al., 2021), mild intellectual disability (Ooms-Evers et al., 2021), dissociative identity disorder (single case) (van Minnen & Tibben, 2021); war trauma (Reij et al., 2025), borderline personality disorder (A. De Jongh et al., 2020; Kolthof et al., 2022), and refugees (Tinghög et al., 2024). Care settings are diverse: inpatient (Kolthof et al., 2022; Voorendonk et al., 2020), outpatient (day-hospital format) (Jamil et al., 2026; Matthijssen et al., 2024), and telehealth (Bongaerts et al., 2021, 2022; Ellenbroek et al., 2024). Treatment duration is typically two weeks, with follow-up showing maintained effects up to 12 months (Klaeth et al., 2024; Kolthof et al., 2022).

Other models

In addition to these two major models, Other approaches have been proposed in a more limited number of studies with follow-up periods up to six months (Miller et al., 2025), delivered in outpatient (Cabrera et al., 2020), inpatient (Mevissen et al., 2020; Strijk et al., 2025; Zepeda Méndez et al., 2025), or weekend retreat formats (for couple therapy) (Fredman et al., 2020, 2021, 2024, 2025). These models vary widely in duration, ranging from 2 days (Fredman et al., 2020, 2021, 2024, 2025) to 1 month (Oprel et al., 2024), and are heterogeneous in nature. They offer tailored interventions for specific populations, including couples (Fredman et al., 2020, 2021, 2024, 2025), families (Mevissen et al., 2020), individuals with mild intellectual disability (Mevissen et al., 2020; Versluis et al., 2025), and highly traumatized (Miller et al., 2025) or military populations (Fredman et al., 2020, 2021, 2024, 2025).

Therapeutic modalities

Although the therapeutic modalities within the care programmes differ considerably between the two models, both employ psychotherapeutic methods validated for PTSD. In single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes, individual therapies are usually delivered as monotherapy, generally Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT) or Prolonged Exposure (PE) (Table 2). These are sometimes combined with ‘booster’ sessions, in vivo exposure, or complementary methods such as the stellate ganglion block. The number of sessions per day is typically one or two. Multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes usually employ bitherapy (PE and EMDR therapy), with two sessions per day. Modalities are more variable in other models, including EMDR therapy, Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET), PE, or Trauma-Sensitive Yoga. They are usually delivered as monotherapy, ranging from three sessions per week to two sessions per day.

Group modalities (Table 2) are extensive in single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes and highly varied, including in vivo exposure, PE, CPT, DBT, ACT, CGT, cognitive restructuring, interpersonal skills training, coping skills, mindfulness, resilience therapy, art therapy, physical activity, trauma-sensitive yoga, case management, psychoeducation, sleep hygiene, recreational activities, nutrition and healthy lifestyle, spiritual guidance, wellness programmes, family involvement, and military-related challenges. In multimodal intensive treatment programmes, group activities do not use specific or personalized psychotherapeutic modalities; they primarily consist of physical activity and psychoeducation. In other models, group activities are adapted to the specific programme, such as the KINGS Programme (parental skills) (Mevissen et al., 2020) for families or Cognitive Behavioral Couple Therapy (CBCT) for couples (Fredman et al., 2020, 2021, 2024, 2025). Other modalities, including physical activity, art therapy, and trauma-sensitive yoga, are variably implemented.

Therapist rotation is rare in single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes (Straud et al., 2025), but is constant in multimodal intensive treatment programmes, with multiple trauma targets addressed throughout the programme (Alting van Geusau et al., 2025; Kolthof et al., 2022; Matthijssen et al., 2024; Paridaen et al., 2023; Reij et al., 2025; Thoresen et al., 2022; Tinghög et al., 2024; Wesseling et al., 2025). Importantly, it is also explicitly described in Straud et al. (2025), where groups are facilitated by two therapists with daily rotation of therapists. However, unlike the other studies, this intervention did not involve therapist rotation as defined by Van Minnen et al. Instead, the rotation concerned group leaders responsible for psychoeducation, treatment rationale, and planning of in vivo exposure. This distinction should be considered when interpreting the findings, as these two approaches may be perceived differently by both patients and clinicians.

Clinical outcomes

Efficacy on PTSD and depression

Over the past five years, throughout all population studies, both models have shown within group pre–post large effect sizes for post-traumatic symptomatology (d = 0.8–2.34) (Harward et al., 2024; Paridaen et al., 2023;) and for depression (d = 0.69–1.49) (Baez et al., 2026; Held et al., 2023; Held, Kovacevic, et al., 2022) (Table 3). Effect sizes are more heterogeneous for other models and are difficult to compare due to the variability of the care models used (d = 0.71–1.37 for PTSD) (Fredman et al., 2021, 2025).

Comparative studies

In single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes, regarding symptoms severity, no difference was found regarding type of individual therapy (no difference between PE and CPT) (Goetter et al., 2021; Yamokoski et al., 2023), programme duration (2 weeks versus 3 weeks) (Dell, Sbisa, Forbes, O’Donnell, Bryant, Hodson, Morton, Battersby, Tuerk, Elliott, et al., 2023; Dell, Sbisa, Forbes, O’Donnell, Bryant, Hodson, Morton, Battersby, Tuerk, Wallace, et al., 2023; Held et al., 2023, 2024; Schlesselmann et al., 2025), amount of group therapy (massed vs. intensive formats) (Peterson et al., 2023), veteran versus active military service member status (Murphy, Smith, et al., 2026). Moreover, no difference was found between standard and massed PE (Galovski et al., 2022) Crisis response planning was superior to self-guided safety planning in reducing suicidal ideation (Bryan et al., 2024).

In multimodal intensive treatment programmes, regarding symptom severity, there was no difference between veteran versus non-veteran status (Reij et al., 2025), overreporting (van Minnen et al., 2020), age (Gielkens et al., 2021),. The programme was superior to treatment as usual (traditionally spaced treatment) a lower dropout rate (4.3 vs 24.1%).

Dropout rates

Dropout rates are low and below those typically reported in ‘as usual’ PTSD care in the literature (Lewis et al., 2020), in both models. Although no direct comparison between the two models is available and that differences needs to be proven, dropout rates appear to differ slightly, at around 7% in single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes (Held et al., 2020; Peterson et al., 2023; Tanev et al., 2022; Tirone et al., 2021) and 3% in multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes (Alting van Geusau et al., 2025; Gahnfelt et al., 2025; Gielkens et al., 2021; Kolthof et al., 2022; Paridaen et al., 2023; Reij et al., 2025; Rentinck et al., 2025; van der Linde et al., 2023; van Woudenberg et al., 2023). Other models show greater variability in dropout rates, ranging from 0 to 18.8% (Oprel et al., 2024).

Additional outcomes

Beyond these primary symptoms, additional outcomes have been studied and all improved. In single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes, improvements have been observed in pain intensity (Held et al., 2023; Kovacevic et al., 2024), sleep (none to moderate effect size) (Held, Szoke, et al., 2025; Sherrill, Patton, et al., 2022; Zalta et al., 2020), substance use disorder (Held, Steigerwald, et al., 2021; Schubert et al., 2026; Stefanovics & Rosenheck, 2020; Watkins et al., 2024), post-concussion symptoms (medium to large effect size) (Harward et al., 2024; Scimeca et al., 2025), quality of life (Aosved et al., 2025; Dell, Sbisa, Forbes, O’Donnell, Bryant, Hodson, Morton, Battersby, Tuerk, Elliott, et al., 2023), satisfaction with mental health care (Aosved et al., 2025), employment (Stevenson et al., 2021), lengths of stay in the programme (Holliday et al., 2025a) and social functioning (Rauch et al., 2021), suicidal ideation (Bryan et al., 2024; Murphy, Smith, et al., 2026), complicated grief (Ohye et al., 2022), and neurological symptoms (Rauch et al., 2021).

In multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes, general psychiatric symptoms (Ellenbroek et al., 2024), borderline personality disorder symptoms (A. De Jongh et al., 2020; Kolthof et al., 2022), physical functioning (Zoet et al., 2021), well-being (Klaeth et al., 2024), interpersonal functioning (Klaeth et al., 2024; Ooms-Evers et al., 2021), behavioural problems (Ooms-Evers et al., 2021), emotion regulation difficulties (van Toorenburg et al., 2020), and sexual functioning (van Woudenberg et al., 2023) have been investigated with positive results. In the other models, anxiety and anger (Fredman et al., 2020) have been studied.

Specific variables have also been examined in couple interventions, including couple communication, dyadic coping, and partner accommodation of PTSD symptoms (Fredman et al., 2020).

Prognostic factors, predictors and residual symptoms

Prognostic factors

A number of prognostic factors have been investigated across the three models (Table 4). Notably, several elements appear to have no significant impact on therapeutic outcome: baseline emotion regulation (van Toorenburg et al., 2020), type of traumatic event (Held, Klassen, et al., 2021), the impact of a ‘moral injurious event’ (Held, Klassen, et al., 2021), the level of sexual revictimization prior to treatment (Tirone et al., 2021), time elapsed since the traumatic event (Splaine et al., 2023), the addition of physical activity (Voorendonk et al., 2023), dissociation-related beliefs (van der Linde et al., 2023), and somatoform dissociation (Zoet et al., 2021).

Predictors

Conversely, several factors seem to influence outcomes. These include the presence of a dedicated intake team (associated with reduced dropout) (Coleman et al., 2025), the implementation of a crisis response plan (associated with lower suicidal ideation) (Bryan et al., 2024), perceived working alliance and pre-treatment relationship satisfaction (Held, Meade, et al., 2022), baseline general self-efficacy (Murphy et al., 2025), agreement between clinician-rated and patient-reported PTSD (Touponse et al., 2025), pre-treatment PTSD and depression severity (Smith et al., 2024; Zalta et al., 2020), personality traits (neuroticism and higher extraversion) (Hyatt et al., 2025), participation in disability separation (Hoyt & Staley Shumaker, 2021), gender (Stefanovics & Rosenheck, 2020), pre-treatment resilience (Ptak et al., 2025b) and cortisol response to the trauma-potentiated startle paradigm (Maples-Keller et al., 2022). Specifically, post-treatment depressive relapse may be explained by the presence of complex PTSD at baseline and the level of insomnia (Held, Boland, et al., 2025).

Potential treatment mechanisms

What are the psychopathological mechanisms underlying treatment response? This question has been investigated by examining which factors are associated with a reduction in symptoms during treatment. A central finding is the decrease in negative post-traumatic cognitions (particularly the level of individualized stuck points and guilt-related cognitions) (Held, Kaysen, et al. 2022; Limdi et al., 2025; Murphy, Warren, et al., 2026) as well as changes in cognitive emotion regulation strategies (Kovacevic et al., 2025; Murphy, Warren, et al., 2026; Murphy, Walker, et al., 2026), both of which are correlated with reductions in post-traumatic symptomatology during care.

Other predictors of treatment response have also been identified, including decreased substance use (Held, Steigerwald, et al., 2021; Straud et al., 2025), hope (Kuijpers-Van Ommen et al., 2026), coping self-efficacy (Murphy et al., 2025; Patton et al., 2025), changes in event centrality, reduction in pain, sudden gains (Christ et al., 2023), fear extinction (Maples-Keller et al., 2022), improvements in insomnia (Zalta et al., 2020), reduced emotional intensity of traumatic memories (Alting van Geusau et al., 2025), and decreases in somatoform dissociation (Zoet et al., 2021). Importantly, cannabis use was not found to have an impact on treatment response (Schubert et al., 2026).

Residual symptoms

Despite highly positive therapeutic outcomes, recent years have also highlighted certain limitations of these treatments, particularly the persistence of residual symptoms at the end of programmes, which may contribute to relapse. In the single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes, persistent symptoms most often include sleep disturbances (Tanev et al., 2022), depression (associated with higher relapse rates) (Kovacevic, Bravo, et al., 2023), and PTSD symptoms (especially hyperarousal) (Tanev et al., 2022). In contrast, multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes emphasizes the persistence of negative beliefs, negative emotions, intrusive memories, and avoidance of thoughts and feelings (Wesseling et al., 2025). Overall, however, the rate of symptom exacerbation at the end of treatment remains very low (Szoke et al., 2025).

Qualitative data

In addition to these quantitative data, several qualitative studies have examined the effects, the determinants of effectiveness, and the challenges reported from the perspective of individuals who participated in these programmes. In the trauma-focused protocol-based intensive programme context, participant reactions to massed Prolonged Exposure (PE) were predominantly positive, with 51.27% reporting beneficial experiences compared to 17.77% expressing negative reactions (Sherrill, Maples-Keller, et al., 2022). Concerning drawbacks, participants noted that massed PE can induce short-term discomfort and is demanding in terms of effort and time, which aligns with the clinical theory of PE and the justification for its intensive delivery. Participants highlighted the acquisition of tangible skills, the feasibility of the programme, the therapeutic process, symptom presentation, and treatment expectations (Kovacevic, Tharaud, et al., 2023). These findings indicate that although the massed or intensive format poses immediate challenges, its structured and rapid approach supports meaningful engagement and early therapeutic gains in the single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes context. Providers and administrators identified barriers to implementation, including presumed negative veteran and provider attitudes to massed treatment, increased pressure on providers, lack of staff time and other resources, and limits created by current systems and performance metrics. Providers and administrators also identified implementation facilitators, including high expectations for effectiveness, engagement, and acceptability; increased resources, time, and flexibility over scheduling; access to knowledge and training; a heightened need for adjunctive and coordinated care; and thoughtful planning, communication, and support from leadership (Wells et al., 2026)

In multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes, participants’ experiences emphasized both individual and collective aspects of therapy. They reported short-term discomfort and high demands, yet recognized substantial benefits extending beyond PTSD symptom reduction. The interplay between individual attributes and social support emerged as a key factor shaping treatment experiences. Participants described learning through shared experiences (Vaage-Kowalzik et al., 2025), encountering a new form of trauma therapy that changed attitudes and clinical praxis, and expressing doubts about intensive trauma treatment. Participants identified benefits largely consistent with the rationale for the programme intensity, namely that the structured format limits distractions and avoidance, and that rapid symptom gains enhance motivation and engagement (Thoresen et al., 2022). Overall, patients perceived the programme as highly demanding but worthwhile for symptom reduction. The intensity of the treatment was considered instrumental to its effectiveness, while the sense of unity with other participants (Bragesjö et al., 2025), engagement in physical activity, and the rotation of therapists were highlighted as critical components facilitating treatment completion and enhancing overall efficacy.

Discussion

Over the past five years, a growing number of studies, mostly observational, have suggested that massed or intensive therapies can be effective and well tolerated for trauma-related disorders, with low dropout rates and follow-up periods extending up to one year. We therefore chose not to conduct a meta-analysis of these therapeutic studies, as their level of evidence remains insufficient (Leroy et al., 2018). Two main models have emerged, multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes and trauma-focused, protocol-based intensive programmes, each with distinct strengths and limitations. Single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes represent comprehensive approaches that address a wide range of clinical needs. They provide truly comprehensive mental health care, extending beyond psychiatric treatment to include rehabilitation, social, and spiritual workshops. This model combines evidence-based psychotherapeutic methods for trauma, primarily trauma-focused cognitive–behavioural therapies, with interventions typically used during the stabilization phase, such as dialectical behaviour therapy and skills-based coping work. Care also encompasses medication management, case management, and specific modules for addiction treatment. The programme is highly flexible, with additional modules or booster sessions frequently offered. Inpatient and outpatient settings may exist across both formats and are not defining characteristics. Its main limitation is that it is almost exclusively focused on veterans and active military personnel, a population known to have specific characteristics in PTSD. Another limitation is that the broad range of care and substantial social support provided alongside therapy may limit the model's generalizability to other settings.

In contrast, multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes follow a highly structured protocol without stabilization phases (Ad De Jongh et al., 2016), with a strong emphasis on physical activity, which is valued by patients (Thoresen et al., 2022), although its efficacy has not been clearly demonstrated (Voorendonk et al., 2023). This model has been tested across diverse populations, including adolescents, patients with borderline personality disorder, and individuals with mild intellectual disabilities. Its highly protocolized and multi-population approach favours easier generalization, although some of the tools used in single-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes might be relevant for addressing residual symptoms. Several major innovations in multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes have significant impacts on patients, as highlighted by qualitative studies. For example, therapist rotation has been shown to improve perceived quality of care for both patients and clinicians (Van Minnen et al., 2018). Patients preferred the programme structure at its conclusion, with the therapeutic alliance maintained throughout. They benefited from the unique expertise of each therapist, which provided potential psychotherapeutic advantages, such as addressing guilt-related cognitions. For therapists, rotation allowed shared responsibility, reduced individual burden, increased clinical experience within the programme, minimized protocol deviations, facilitated peer learning, and improved team cohesion. This approach appears valuable from both the clinician’s and the patient’s perspectives, and warrants further studies based on these experiences to determine whether this model should be generalized. Finally, the ability to address multiple trauma targets within a single programme is a major strength in multi-approach trauma-focused intensive programmes approach, particularly given that complex or treatment-resistant PTSD often involves multiple traumatic events.

Beyond these two primary models, other innovative approaches have focused on family – or couple-centered care, recognizing that involving the patient’s social environment is an important prognostic factor in PTSD treatment (Ozer et al., 2003). These models need further development regarding the growing literature in the field (Harvey et al., 2017). Overall, all validated psychotherapeutic methods for PTSD can be relevant in massed or intensive programmes, but individualized tailoring of interventions may optimize outcomes for each patient. Specific interventions, such as physical activity, require further investigation, although different modules appear to benefit patients according to their clinical profiles. Future research should examine the contribution of each module and potential synergistic effects. Dual pathology, such as substance use disorder associated with PTSD and comorbid psychotic disorders, often requires specialized care, including enhanced treatment adherence, hospitalization when necessary, and coordination among professionals. Massed or intensive therapy approaches may represent a particularly relevant avenue for these populations, and consideration of pharmacological treatment within such models could further inform future research. Moreover, a key limitation of this review is that the included studies were conducted exclusively in Europe or North America, the latter often involving populations exposed to war-related trauma. This restricts the generalizability of the findings to other cultural, socio-economic, and ethnic contexts, as well as to populations with different trauma profiles and a broader range of comorbid conditions. It may also reflect the current concentration of research on these therapeutic models in this specific context. Further research examining their applicability and effectiveness in more diverse settings is warranted. Most studies include mixed populations, and the effects appear to be consistent across genders. Nevertheless, no study has directly compared men and women in the context of massed or intensive therapy. This requires confirmation, as differences in effectiveness favouring better outcomes in women are often reported in the literature (Khan et al., 2020). Furthermore, the modalities and constraints associated with massed or intensive therapies may indeed be experienced differently depending on social roles and related responsibilities, likely influencing accessibility, engagement in care, and acceptability. Paying particular attention to the specificities of women's care pathways, which are often characterized by increased organizational and social constraints, would provide a better understanding of the scope and limitations of these therapeutic approaches. For instance, the constraints faced by a single woman with a child may limit her access to massed or intensive therapies. Moreover, to our knowledge, the subjective perception of emotional exposure and the associated feelings of security have not been specifically examined in previous studies, which may be an interesting avenue for future research.

This review also highlights factors that require particular attention to optimize treatment outcomes. Maintaining a strong therapeutic alliance is critical, consistent with existing literature (Howard et al., 2022). In contrast, trauma type, time since trauma, basal emotion regulation difficulties and dissociation level appear to have little impact on efficacy. Studying the mechanisms underlying treatment efficacy and residual symptoms provides further insight into programme improvements. Post-traumatic cognitions, changes in event centrality (Berntsen & Rubin, 2006) (extent to which a traumatic or stressful event becomes central to a person’s identity and life story), sudden gains (Tang & DeRubeis, 1999) (large and stable improvement in symptom severity that occurs between two consecutive therapy sessions), and interactions with pain are particularly important and warrant further development. Most of the response mechanisms identified in this review are indeed well-established factors known to play a significant role in treatment outcomes. In particular, the literature highlights the role of post-traumatic cognitions (Alpert et al., 2023), emotion regulation strategies (McLean et al., 2019), and coping strategies (Cieslak et al., 2008). Other mediators are supported by lower levels of evidence, such as cannabis use (Alpert et al., 2023) and sudden gains (Sloan et al., 2022). Overall, although several of these factors still require further confirmation, there is currently no clear evidence for mechanisms that are specific to massed or intensive therapy formats. This may support the argument that increasing treatment intensity does not fundamentally alter the underlying mechanisms of action of psychotherapeutic interventions. However, many of the cited predictors derive from single-programme observational studies, often within the same institutional settings. These findings reflect programme-specific exploratory analyses rather than broadly replicated predictors across intensive treatment formats. Therefore, these results still require replication.

Residual symptoms remain a key limitation, with sleep disturbances representing a major factor in treatment resistance. Targeted interventions for insomnia, particularly cognitive–behavioural therapies (van der Zweerde et al., 2019) and frequent sleep monitoring, should be considered at the end of treatment, although data are currently limited. Depressive symptoms are associated with higher relapse risk, underscoring the importance of post-programme follow-up, particularly for long-term outcomes. Residual PTSD symptoms are also common, particularly negative cognitions and emotions, which could benefit from specific CBT tools or supportive interventions to prevent relapse. The persistence of intrusive symptoms raises the question of complementary desensitization methods, including body-focused approaches (Guépin et al., 2025; van der Kolk et al., 2014; Zaccari et al., 2023), which remain under development and require further validation. Overall, these limitations underscore the importance of developing consolidation or reintegration programmes to follow massed or intensive trauma-focused psychotherapies. Such approaches may contribute to the maintenance of therapeutic gains and promote a more effective reintegration into everyday life and society, even in the presence of residual symptoms.

In conclusion, over the past five years, numerous observational studies have provided extensive information on the effectiveness and acceptability of massed or intensive trauma-focused care, which appears to offer several advantages over conventional treatments, including lower dropout rates. Findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered primarily descriptive and exploratory, particularly given the predominance of observational studies and the limited number of randomized controlled trials. There is also a potential publication bias associated with this study. Indeed, it is not possible to rule out the existence of unpublished negative studies. These data should be taken into account when interpreting the very large effect sizes reported in this review. However, the exact mechanisms of action, the value of personalized intervention content, and the limits of this approach require continued investigation, as does the integration of these programmes with broader healthcare and social support systems. We included qualitative studies in order to reflect the perspectives of those affected in this review. However, additional studies are needed to systematically involve individuals with lived experience in future research. Comparisons between the different models to determine whether one model demonstrates superiority on a given outcome, as well as evaluations of the various modules, are still needed. Moreover, the overall level of evidence remains limited despite large effect sizes, with few randomized controlled trials validating these interventions. Additional challenges are also associated with the assessment of programme outcomes. Given the rapid succession of sessions, measuring session-to-session changes using existing instruments can at times be difficult. Furthermore, the cost of these models has, to date, been insufficiently investigated in comparison with traditional outpatient care. Although these approaches are highly promising, it appears necessary to examine whether the reduced duration of treatment offsets the increased resources required for this type of programme. Nevertheless, current data suggest that massed or intensive programmes are not inferior to standard care, and a two-week format is most commonly implemented, as comparative studies do not show superiority of a three-week programme, potentially due to cognitive fatigue in patients. Overall, these findings underscore the relevance of massed or intensive care models, which provide a dynamic framework to address the therapeutic challenges encountered by patients with PTSD.
