Early Prosthetic Thrombosis After Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Replacement
Authors: Francesco Trinca, Nassim Bousmaha, Jean Porterie, Siamak Mohammadi, Eric Dumont, Benoît M-Labbé, Kim O'Connor, Anthony Poulin, Josep Rodès-Cabau, Dimitri Kalavrouziotis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeterminants of early prosthetic thrombosis following transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) are unknown.Case SummaryA 70-year-old man with severe tricuspid regurgitation underwent an uneventful TTVR using the EVOQUE system (Edwards Lifesciences). Intravenous heparin was temporarily suspended a few days after the procedure to insert a permanent pacemaker. Sudden clinical deterioration occurred in the following days. Surgical TTVR explantation may damage the native tricuspid leaflets and subvalvular apparatus, reducing the likelihood of valve repair.Key Words: thrombosis, tricuspid valve, valve replacement

History of Presentation

A 70-year-old man was admitted to our institution with severe symptomatic tricuspid regurgitation (TR). Elective transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) using a 56-mm EVOQUE prosthesis (Edwards Lifesciences) was successfully carried out without complications. Procedural steps were previously described.1 Periprocedural transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed good prosthesis positioning and function, with only trace periprosthetic regurgitation adjacent to the septal leaflet.

Pre-TTVR, the patient was chronically anticoagulated with apixaban 5 mg twice daily for permanent atrial fibrillation. Apixaban was discontinued 48 hours before TTVR. During the procedure, intravenous unfractionated heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time >300 seconds. Six hours post-TTVR, a continuous intravenous heparin infusion was initiated at 18 U/kg/h without a bolus and titrated to maintain an activated partial thromboplastin time above the institutional therapeutic target of 45 seconds.

On postprocedural day 3, the patient developed complete heart block. A Micra leadless pacemaker (Medtronic Inc) was implanted on postprocedural day 5. The heparin infusion was discontinued 4 hours before pacemaker implantation and resumed 12 hours after the procedure with an 80 U/kg bolus followed by continuous infusion. The patient remained without anticoagulation for approximately 16 hours surrounding the pacemaker implantation. Antiplatelet therapy consisting of acetylsalicylic acid 81 mg daily was never interrupted, both for the TTVR and the pacemaker implantation.

Three days later, the patient deteriorated clinically, with marked tachypnea and increasing oxygen requirements, and developed cardiogenic shock requiring a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated severe hypoxemia with a partial pressure of oxygen of 62 mm Hg. At the time of diagnosis, therapeutic anticoagulation had already been re-established, with serial activated partial thromboplastin time values consistently maintained above the institutional target of 45 seconds.

Past Medical History

The patient had previously undergone an attempted tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair 6 months earlier. However, the procedure was aborted because of inadequate leaflet grasp. He has a history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes, permanent atrial fibrillation with left bundle branch block, obstructive sleep apnea, chronic kidney disease, and portal hypertension secondary to alcohol-related liver disease. The patient's TRI-SCORE2 was 6, indicative of a high-risk profile, with a predicted in-hospital mortality of 22% after isolated tricuspid valve (TV) surgery.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of severe hypoxemia and cardiogenic shock in a patient early post-TTVR included pulmonary embolism, device migration, device thrombosis, tamponade, right ventricular (RV) failure, sepsis, and myocardial ischemia.

Investigations

Baseline transthoracic echocardiography before TTVR showed severe central TR with a vena contracta of 13 mm, mild RV dysfunction, and moderate RV dilatation; no patent foramen ovale (PFO) was observed. Post-TTVR echocardiography showed trace TR, and mean transprosthetic gradient was 2 mm Hg. Transthoracic echocardiography on day 8 post-TTVR showed markedly diminished prosthetic leaflet excursion associated with leaflet thrombus. No migration of the TTVR was observed. The RV was underfilled with mild systolic dysfunction, eliminating a primary RV etiology, and not supporting a diagnosis of massive pulmonary embolism. There was no pericardial effusion thus eliminating a diagnosis of tamponade. Myocardial ischemia was unlikely as markers of myocardial injury were normal; sepsis also was considered unlikely. TEE revealed significant thrombotic burden surrounding the perivalvular fabric skirt of the device (Videos 1A and 1B). The thrombus also covered the prosthetic leaflets with associated prosthetic valve stenosis. Continuous Doppler confirmed the stenotic effect with a mean transvalvular gradient of 6 mm Hg (Video 2). Elevated right atrial pressures resulted in a significant right-to-left interatrial shunt through a PFO, as demonstrated with Doppler on TEE, and a microbubble study on transthoracic echocardiography (Video 3), explaining the patient's severe hypoxemia. The patient required 10 L of oxygen on a ventimask; he had a normal venous lactate measurement.

Management

Anticoagulation therapy (intravenous heparin) and antiplatelet therapy were administered but failed to improve both valve thrombotic burden and the clinical condition of the patient. The patient was reevaluated by the multidisciplinary heart team. Intravenous or catheter-delivered thrombolysis was felt to be of limited benefit because of the size and extent of the thrombotic burden. An off-label transcatheter valve-in-valve intervention using the S3 TAVR prosthesis (Edwards Lifesciences) to relieve obstruction of the TV and reduce right-to-left shunting, was felt to be associated with a high risk of thrombus embolization. Conventional salvage surgery was chosen as the best therapeutic option for this patient.

Vasoactive medications (10 mg of midodrine 3 times a day and 0.17 μg/kg/min norepinephrine infusion) were given for 2 days to increase systemic blood pressure and decrease shunting but were met with limited clinical improvement.

The patient was taken to the operating room on a semiurgent basis. After median sternotomy and bicaval cannulation with snares surrounding the 2 cavae, cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated. The aorta was not cross-clamped, and the heart was not arrested. The caval snares were tightened and the right atrium was opened. The prosthesis was encased in extensive thrombus (Figure 1, Video 4). Removal of the TTVR prosthesis proved to be challenging, even though it was deployed only 10 days before open surgery. To disengage the 9 ventricular anchors, incising the native tricuspid leaflets and subvalvular apparatus was necessary, including dividing multiple chordae tendineae and papillary muscles (Video 5). For this reason, TV repair was not possible following extirpation of the prosthesis in this patient, and the native TV was replaced without sparing the subvalvular apparatus, using a 33-mm Epic Plus bioprosthesis (Abbott). The PFO was also identified and closed primarily. Examination of the removed TTVR prosthesis confirmed the presence of thrombus involving both the nitinol stent frame and the prosthetic leaflets (Figures 2A and 2B). The patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass on a moderate dose of epinephrine for mild-moderate RV dysfunction. Perioperative TEE showed a normally functioning TV prosthesis without periprosthetic regurgitation.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was uneventful. Thrombus was confirmed on histopathological analysis and no microorganisms were observed. The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 8 on oral diuretics. Discharge transthoracic echocardiography showed normal left ventricular function, mild-to-moderate RV dysfunction, and a mean TV prosthetic gradient of 3 mm Hg. The patient was discharged on coumadin following intravenous heparin bridging and a therapeutic international normalized ratio (between 2.0 and 3.0), for a period of 3 months, after which he was changed to his usual dose of apixaban for long-term management of his permanent atrial fibrillation. At 3 months postdischarge, the patient was in NYHA functional class I, and echocardiographic parameters remained unchanged except for a mild improvement in RV systolic function.

Discussion

The safety and efficacy of TTVR have been demonstrated in a recent randomized controlled trial comparing TTVR with medical therapy alone in patients with severe symptomatic TR.3 Clinical benefit was mainly driven by improvements in symptoms and quality of life in the TTVR group. Need for a permanent pacemaker (17.4% vs 2.3%, P < 0.001) and severe bleeding (15.4% vs 5.3%, P = 0.003) at 1 year were the 2 most common adverse events; both were significantly higher in the TTVR group compared with medical treatment. Higher bleeding risk in the TTVR group was primarily observed early (≤30 days), with similar rates of bleeding after 30 days. The authors attributed this early bleeding risk to the protocol-mandated anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy among TTVR patients. Conversely, risk of thrombosis was low, with individual sites reporting a total of 10 cases of TTVR thrombosis at 1 year post procedure (3.9%).

Current peer-reviewed guidelines are uninformative regarding optimal management of anticoagulation periprocedurally and long-term following TTVR, and practices vary widely between institutions. A recent single-center report of 28 patients receiving a variety of TTVR prostheses suggested that anticoagulation may be safely stopped preprocedurally (5 days before TTVR for coumadin, and 48 hours before for a direct oral anticoagulant) for patients with atrial fibrillation, and subsequently restarted 24 to 48 hours post-TTVR without the need for heparin bridging, thus mitigating bleeding risk without increasing the risk of TTVR thrombosis.4 The case illustrated in our report suggests that these recommendations should be interpreted with caution, as this case raises concern that even brief interruption of anticoagulation early after TTVR may be associated with clinically significant valve thrombosis in selected high-risk patients. Although thrombosis occurred after a brief interruption of anticoagulation, causality cannot be established. Other contributing factors may have included permanent atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, portal hypertension, right-sided low-flow conditions, the early postprocedural inflammatory state, and purported thrombogenic potential of the prosthetic fabric skirt. Our case illustrates the need for a multidisciplinary assessment of the optimal management of patients with severe TR. Notably, consideration of bleeding vs thrombosis risk should be included as a parameter in the decision-making process for selecting the ideal therapeutic modality in these patients. The appropriateness of TTVR compared with medical therapy or high-risk conventional surgery should be individualized to each patient's unique bleeding/thrombosis risk profile.

Our case suggests that feasibility of surgical TV repair following TTVR may be unlikely, as extensive anchoring of the TTVR prosthesis compromises the integrity of the native TV leaflets and subvalvular apparatus after its removal. Conversely, whether tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair may be compatible with subsequent surgical TV repair in the event of failure of transcatheter edge-to-edge repair is currently unknown. The mitral literature suggests that repair is feasible following failures of mitral-transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, although the great majority of patients (>80%) will require mitral valve replacement.5

Conclusions

In summary, extensive device thrombosis can occur early post-TTVR after a brief cessation of anticoagulation, in patients with multiple risk factors for increased thrombogenicity. Further research is required to determine the optimal antithrombotic regimen in patients undergoing TTVR. The likelihood of surgical TV repair following TTVR may be decreased
