Improved bounds for the double cap conjecture
Domonkos Czifra, Ákos Dúcz, Máté Matolcsi, Dániel Varga, Pál Zsámboki

Abstract
. In 1974, Witsenhausen asked for the maximum possible density α n \alpha_{n} of a measurable subset A A of the unit sphere 𝕊 n − 1 ⊂ ℝ n \mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} such that A A contains no pair of orthogonal vectors. For n = 3 n=3 , the best known lower bound is 1 − 1 / 2 = 0.29289 ​ … 1-1/\sqrt{2}=0.29289\dots , obtained from the natural “double cap” construction of two opposite spherical caps. This construction was conjectured by Gil Kalai [ 9 ] to be optimal for all n n . In this paper, we use a novel approach to establish the upper bound α 3 ≤ 0.2953 \alpha_{3}\leq 0.2953 , improving on the previous best known bound of 0.2977 0.2977 due to Bekker et al. [ 2 ] . Our approach combines arguments from harmonic analysis with the concept of the geometric fractional chromatic number of finite graphs, introduced recently by Ambrus et al. [ 1 ] . Within this framework, any finite subset of the sphere yields an upper bound for α n \alpha_{n} ; we obtain our bound by identifying a suitable 33-element point set through a large-scale computer search. The same method can also be used in higher dimensions to yield potential improvements of the best known bounds.



1.
Introduction

A measurable subset
A
of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \|x\|=1\}
is said to
avoid orthogonality
if
\langle a,a^{\prime}\rangle\neq 0
for all
a,a^{\prime}\in A
. Witsenhausen
[
16
]
posed the problem of finding the maximum possible density of such a set
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. That is, we aim to determine the value of

(1.1)

\alpha_{n}=\sup\{\omega(A)/\omega_{n}\ :\ A\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\ \textrm{is \ measurable \ and \ avoids \ orthogonality}\},

where
\omega
is the surface measure of the sphere, and
\omega_{n}
is the total measure of the sphere.

The best known lower bound for
\alpha_{n}
comes from the natural construction of two open antipodal spherical caps of spherical radius
\pi/4
. This
“double cap” construction is conjectured to be optimal for all
n
by Gil Kalai
[
9
]
. The conjecture is open for all
n\geq 3
. If true, it would imply that
\alpha_{n}=(\sqrt{2}+o(1))^{-n}
.

As for upper bounds, Witsenhausen
[
16
]
observed the trivial bound
\alpha_{n}\leq 1/n
by considering an orthonormal system of
n
unit vectors, and an averaging argument over the sphere. The first exponentially decreasing bound,
\alpha_{n}\leq(1+o(1))1.13^{-n}
was given by Frankl and Wilson
[
7
]
, which was later improved to
\alpha_{n}\leq(1+o(1))1.225^{-n}
by Raigorodskii
[
12
]
.

Besides progress on the asymptotics of
\alpha_{n}
, there have been several improvements on upper bounds in low dimensions. For
n=3
, the first improvement on the trivial bound
\alpha_{3}\leq 1/3
was obtained by a harmonic analytic version of Delsarte’s linear programming bound combined with some combinatorial constraints by DeCorte and Pikhurko
[
5
]
, who proved
\alpha_{3}\leq 0.313
. The bound was later improved to
\alpha_{3}\leq 0.30153
by DeCorte, Oliveira and Vallentin
[
4
]
by incorporating complete positivity constraints, and most recently to
\alpha_{3}\leq 0.2977
by Bekker, Oliveira, Kuryatnikova and Vera
[
2
]
by similar methods. The main result of this note is to further improve the upper bound on
\alpha_{3}
by a novel approach of combining harmonic analytic arguments with the notion of the geometric fractional chromatic number of finite graphs introduced by Ambrus et al.
[
1
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For the Witsenhausen problem in dimension 3 we have
\alpha_{3}\leq 0.2953
.

The structure of the paper is the following. In Section
2
, we describe how the geometric fractional chromatic number (GFCN) of a finite set
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
fits into the framework of upper bounding
\alpha_{n}
. In Section
3
, we link up the notion of GFCN with the harmonic analytic version of Delsarte’s linear programming bound, and show how weak duality can produce a verifiable dual witness to our upper bound. This gives the method of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Finally, the Appendix contains essential auxiliary ingredients, such as the description of the computer search which resulted in an appropriate finite set
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
consisting of 33 antipodal pairs, and the steps of the symbolic verification of our dual witness.

2.
The geometric fractional chromatic number

The notion of the geometric fractional chromatic number, introduced in
[
1
]
, is a very effective tool in several problems with underlying symmetries. In this section, we describe how this concept fits into the framework of Witsenhausen’s problem.

We first make a technical remark, and introduce some terminology. If
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
avoids orthogonality, then so does the symmetrized set
A\cup-A
, of possibly larger measure. As such, we will assume without loss of generality, throughout the paper, that
A
is symmetric,
A=-A
. Similarly, in the forthcoming arguments, whenever we consider a finite subset
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, we always assume that
X
is symmetric,
X=-X
. As such,
X
can be written as a union of antipodal pairs,
X=\cup_{i=1}^{N}\{x_{i},-x_{i}\}
. (That is,
X
is essentially a subset of the projective plane, but still, we would rather think of it as a collection of antipodal pairs on the surface of the sphere.) To such a set
X
, we will associate a corresponding
orthogonality graph

G_{X}
in the following way: any vertex
v_{i}
of
G_{X}
is a pair
\{x_{i},-x_{i}\}
, and we join two vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
by an edge if and only if
\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle=0
. For an orthogonal transformation
T\in O(n)
we say that
T(v_{i})=v_{j}
if
T(x_{i})=x_{j}
or
T(x_{i})=-x_{j}
. We will call two sets of vertices
Y=\{v_{i_{1}},\dots,v_{i_{k}}\}
and
Z=\{v_{j_{1}},\dots,v_{j_{k}}\}

geometrically congruent
, in notation
Y\cong Z
, if there exists an orthogonal transformation
T
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
T(Y)=Z
.

After this preparation, let a symmetric finite set
X=\cup_{i=1}^{N}\{x_{i},-x_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
|X|=2N
, and a symmetric, orthogonality avoiding set
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
with density
\delta(A)=\frac{\omega(A)}{\omega_{n}}
be given, and let
\mu
denote the normalized Haar measure on the orthogonal group
O(n)
. Notice that if
A
avoids orthogonality and
T
is an orthogonal transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the intersection
T(A)\cap X=\cup_{j=1}^{k}\{x_{i_{j}},-x_{i_{j}}\}
is necessarily a collection of antipodal pairs, which corresponds to an independent set
I=\{v_{i_{1}},\dots,v_{i_{k}}\}
in
G_{X}
, i.e. a subset of vertices without any edges among them. With a slight abuse of notation we will denote this by
T(A)\cap G_{X}=I
. As such, it is natural to introduce a probability measure on the collection of independent sets of
G_{X}
in the following way. For any independent set of vertices
I\subset G_{X}
, we define the
atomic density

(2.1)

a_{X}(I)=\mu\Big(T\in O(n):T(A)\cap G_{X}=I\Big).

We have the following trivial properties of the atomic densities
a_{X}(I)
:

(2.2)

a_{X}(I)\geq 0\ \ {\textrm{for all \ }}I

(2.3)

\sum_{I}a_{X}(I)=1,

i.e., the atomic densities indeed constitute a probability measure.

Also, for any fixed index
i
, the density of
A
can be expressed as the
\mu
-probability that
v_{i}\in T(A)
, that is
\mu(T\in O(n):v_{i}\in T(A))
. This, in turn, is the sum of atomic densities for independent sets
I
containing
v_{i}
. Therefore we conclude

(2.4)

\sum_{v_{i}\in I}a_{X}(I)=\delta(A)\ \ {\textrm{for all \ }}i=1,2,\dots N.

In particular, the value of the sum above is the same value for any vertex:

(2.5)

\sum_{v_{i}\in I}a_{X}(I)=\sum_{v_{j}\in I}a_{X}(I)\ \ {\textrm{for all \ }}1\leq i,j\leq N.

There is one more crucial property of the atomic densities
a_{X}(I)
, corresponding to the underlying geometric invariance of the problem. Namely, if two sets of vertices
Y,Z\subset G_{X}
are geometrically congruent, then the shift-invariance of the Haar measure ensures that
\mu\Big(T\in O(n):Y\subset T(A)\Big)=\mu\Big(T\in O(n):Z\subset T(A)\Big)
. This can be expressed via the atomic densities
a_{X}(I)
as follows:

(2.6)

\sum_{I\supset Y}a_{X}(I)-\sum_{I\supset Z}a_{X}(I)=0\ \ {\textrm{whenever \ }}Y\cong Z.

Note that equation (
2.5
) is a special case of (
2.6
), with the choice
Y=\{v_{i}\},Z=\{v_{j}\}
.

Having equations (
2.2
), (
2.3
), (
2.4
), (
2.6
) at hand, we can give an upper bound on
\alpha_{n}
by the following linear program:

(2.7)

{\textrm{maximize}}\sum_{v_{1}\in I}a_{X}(I)\ {\textrm{subject to}}

a_{X}(I)\geq 0\ \ {\textrm{for all \ }}I,

\sum_{I}a_{X}(I)=1,

\sum_{I\supset Y}a_{X}(I)-\sum_{I\supset Z}a_{X}(I)=0\ \ {\textrm{whenever \ }}Y\cong Z\ {\textrm{in}}\ G_{X}.

Note that we did not include equation (
2.5
) among the constraints because it is formally included in (
2.6
).

Let
\Gamma(G_{X})
denote the objective value of the linear program above. In analogy with the terminology in
[
1
]
we define the geometric fractional chromatic number
\chi_{gf}(G_{X})
of the graph
G_{X}
as
\chi_{gf}(G_{X})=1/\Gamma(G_{X})
.

By the discussion above, we have the following upper bound on
\alpha_{n}
:

(2.8)

\alpha_{n}\leq\inf\{\Gamma(G_{X})\ :\ X\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\ X\ {\textrm{is finite, symmetric}}\}.

It would be interesting to know whether equality holds in (
2.8
).

It is worth mentioning (cf. a similar discussion in
[
11
]
) that
\Gamma(G_{X})
is monotonically decreasing with respect to inclusion, i.e.

(2.9)

\Gamma(G_{X_{1}})\leq\Gamma(G_{X_{2}})\ {\textrm{for all}}\ G_{X_{1}}\supset G_{X_{2}},

although we will not use this property in this note.

In practice, we only have heuristic guidance as to what properties the set
X
should possess in order to produce a low value of
\Gamma(G_{X})
. First,
X
should contain many geometric congruences, so as to induce several constraints of the type (
2.6
). Second,
X
should contain many orthogonal pairs
\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle=0
, so that the number of independent sets (i.e. the number of variables in the linear program) remains as low as possible.

Using a computer search described in Subsection
5.1
, we have found a 21-point set
X
that achieves the bound
\alpha_{3}\leq\Gamma(G_{X})=32/107\approx 0.2991
. See Table
3
.

3.
Harmonic analysis and weak duality

In this section, we will link up the linear program (
2.7
) with the harmonic analytic version of Delsarte’s linear programming bound to produce a sharper upper bound on
\alpha_{n}
.

Let
t\in[-1,1]
, and
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be given such that
\langle x,y\rangle=t
. For any measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
define the function
f_{A}:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, as

(3.1)

f_{A}(t)=\mu\{T\in O(n):\ Tx\in A\ {\textrm{and}}\ Ty\in A\}

It is clear that the value
f_{A}(t)
does not depend on the particular choice of
x
and
y
. Also, it is easy to see (e.g.
[
5
, Lemma 4]
) that
f_{A}
is continuous and
positive definite
. As such, Schoenberg’s theorem (
[
13
]
,
[
6
, Theorem 14.3.3]
) applies, and
f_{A}
can be written in the form

(3.2)

f_{A}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}P_{k}(t),

where
c_{k}\geq 0
, and
P_{k}
are the Gegenbauer polynomials
C^{(n-2)/2}_{k}
of index
(n-2)/2
and degree
k
, with the normalization
P_{k}(1)=1
. In particular, for
n=3
,
P_{k}
are the Legendre polynomials.

Assume now that
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is symmetric, avoids orthogonality and has density
\delta(A)
. In this case,
f_{A}
has the following essential properties:

(3.3)

f_{A}(t)\geq 0\ {\textrm{for all}}\ t\in[-1,1]

(3.4)

c_{k}\geq 0\ {\textrm{in}}\ \eqref{pdf}

(3.5)

f_{A}(t)=f_{A}(-t)\ {\textrm{due to}}\ A=-A

(3.6)

c_{k}=0\ {\textrm{for all odd}}\ k\ {\textrm{due to }}\ \eqref{fsym}

(3.7)

\sum_{2|k,k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}=\delta(A)\ {\textrm{due to}}\ t=1\ {\textrm{in}}\ \eqref{pdf}

(3.8)

\sum_{2|k,k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}P_{k}(0)=0\ {\textrm{due to}}\ t=0\ {\textrm{in}}\ \eqref{pdf},

the last equation being true because
A
avoids orthogonality.

Furthermore, if
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is a finite, symmetric set, and
G_{X}
is the corresponding graph introduced in Section
2
, then the atomic densities
a_{X}(I)
and the coefficients
c_{k}
are linked via the following relation:

(3.9)

\sum_{2|k,k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)-\sum_{v_{i},v_{j}\in I}a_{X}(I)=0\ {\textrm{for all}}\ 1\leq i\leq j\leq N.

This last equation needs a little clarification. Recall that
v_{i}=\{x_{i},-x_{i}\}
, and hence the value of
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle
is ambiguous. Nevertheless, the value
P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)
is well-defined, because the polynomial
P_{k}
is an even function for even values of
k
. Thus, the first sum above is the
\mu
-probability of
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
being contained in
T(A)
. The second sum is the
\mu
-probability of
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}=\{x_{i},-x_{i},x_{j},-x_{j}\}
being contained in
T(A)
. Due to the symmetry of
X
and
A
these two events are the same.

Note that equality
i=j
is allowed in (
3.9
), in which case
P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{i}\rangle)=1
, and thus (
3.9
) reduces to (
3.7
) via (
2.4
) .

In summary, we obtain an upper bound on
\alpha_{n}
by the following (infinite dimensional) linear program:

(3.10)

{\textrm{maximize}}\ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\ {\textrm{subject to}}\ \eqref{fpos},\eqref{cpos},\eqref{codd},\eqref{t0},\eqref{axck},\eqref{iep},\eqref{iet},\eqref{iec}.

Let
\Delta(G_{X})
denote the objective value (supremum) of the linear program above. By the discussion above, we have the following upper bound on
\alpha_{n}
:

(3.11)

\alpha_{n}\leq\inf\{\Delta(G_{X})\ :\ X\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\ X\ {\textrm{is finite, symmetric}}\}.

We conjecture that equality holds in (
3.11
). Similar convergence results have been proved in
[
2
]
, but it is not clear whether those results apply to our setting without modification.

Finally, we apply weak duality to the linear program (
3.10
) in order to obtain a verifiable witness for an upper bound on
\alpha_{n}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
X=\{x_{1},-x_{1},\dots,x_{N},-x_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is a finite, symmetric subset of the sphere,
v_{i}=\{x_{i},-x_{i}\}
,
G_{X}
is the orthogonality graph corresponding to
X
, and real parameters
q_{1}
,
s_{Y,Z}
(for subsets
Y
and
Z
of
G_{X}
, where
Y\cong Z
),
w_{0}
and
w_{i,j}
(for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq N
) are given such that the inequalities

(3.12)

w_{0}P_{k}(0)+\sum_{i\leq j}w_{i,j}P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)\geq 1\ {\textrm{for all}}\ k\geq 0,\ k\ {\textrm{is even}}

(3.13)

q_{1}-\sum_{v_{i},v_{j}\in I}w_{i,j}+\sum_{I\subset Y}s_{Y,Z}-\sum_{I\subset Z}s_{Y,Z}\geq 0\ {\textrm{for all}}\ I\subset X,I\ {\textrm{is independent}}

are satisfied. Then
\alpha_{n}\leq q_{1}
.

Proof.

The proof is a direct application of linear duality, as follows. Let us use the notation

\beta_{k}=w_{0}P_{k}(0)+\sum_{i\leq j}w_{i,j}P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)
, and
\gamma_{I}=q_{1}-\sum_{v_{i},v_{j}\in I}w_{i,j}+\sum_{I\subset Y}s_{Y,Z}-\sum_{I\subset Z}s_{Y,Z}
. Consider the weighted sum of equations (
2.3
), (
2.6
), (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) with respective weights
q_{1}
,
s_{Y,Z}
,
w_{0}
and
w_{i,j}
. This weighted sum gives us
\sum_{2|k,k=0}^{\infty}\beta_{k}c_{k}+\sum_{I\subset X}\gamma_{I}a_{X}(I)=q_{1}
. Therefore, using the assumptions
\beta_{k}\geq 1,\gamma_{I}\geq 0
, equation (
3.7
), and the nonnegativity of the variables
c_{k}
and
a_{X}(I)
we obtain

(3.14)

\delta(A)=\sum_{2|k,k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\leq\sum_{2|k,k=0}^{\infty}\beta_{k}c_{k}+\sum_{I\subset X}\gamma_{I}a_{X}(I)=q_{1}.

∎

The reader might notice that we have not used the constraint (
3.3
) in the formulation and proof of Theorem
3.1
. It is simply because, in our experience, this constraint is always satisfied automatically in practice, and therefore does not carry any value in sharpening the bound on
\alpha_{n}
.

While the proof of Theorem
3.1
is straightforward, it is far from trivial to make use of it in practice. The difficulty is twofold. First and foremost, one needs to construct a finite, symmetric set
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
which has the potential to give a sharp upper bound on
\alpha_{n}
via Theorem
3.1
. We achieve this by a large-scale computer search governed by some heuristics. Details of this search are described in the Appendix.

Second, once
X
is fixed, we need to find the parameters
q_{1}
,
s_{Y,Z}
,
w_{0}
and
w_{i,j}
as in Theorem
3.1
, and verify that the constraints (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) are satisfied. The numerical values of the parameters are found by a linear programming solver, and then rounded to rational numbers for purposes of rigorous verification. The verification of (
3.13
) is fairly easy as there are only finitely many inequalities, and therefore any minor violation of an inequality can be compensated by increasing the value of
q_{1}
slightly. However, (
3.12
) is a collection of infinitely many constraints, and hence the verification procedure is more involved in this case. Notice that as
k\to\infty
, we have
P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)\to 0
for all
i\neq j
, while
P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{i}\rangle)=P_{k}(1)=1
for all
i
and
k
. Therefore, as
k\to\infty
, the left hand side of (
3.12
) converges to
\sum_{i}w_{ii}
. If this sum is larger than 1, then inequality (
3.12
) is satisfied automatically above a threshold
k>k_{0}
. In order to determine the threshold
k_{0}
, we use standard estimates for the absolute value of Jacobi polynomials (see e.g
[
4
]
):

(3.15)

|P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)|=|P_{k}(\cos\theta)|\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(1-\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\phi)^{k/2}d\phi.

Still, for any
k<k_{0}
the verification of (
3.12
) requires the evaluation of
P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)
to a high precision. This task is non-trivial in itself, as
k
can range up to several thousands. The way out is to use the recursion formula (see
[
15
]
)

(3.16)

P_{k}(u)=\frac{2k-1}{k}uP_{k-1}(u)-\frac{k-1}{k}P_{k-2}(u).

The details of the rigorous verification process are given in the Appendix.

Finally, we provide here the finite set
X
which is used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.
The upper bound
\alpha_{3}\leq 0.2953
is witnessed by an appropriate finite, symmetric set
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, geometric congruences
Y\cong Z
among some subsets of
G_{X}
, and corresponding parameters
q_{1},s_{Y,Z},w_{0}
and
w_{i,j}
satisfying inequalities (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) as described in Theorem
3.1
. The witness
X
consists of 33 antipodal pairs, and hence
|G_{X}|=33
. By definition, the vectors in
X
are normalized,
\|x_{i}\|=1
. However, in order to reduce clutter, we present the coordinates of
x_{i}
without normalization: all coordinates are of the form
a+\sqrt{5}b:a,b\in Z
. We provide the coordinates of one point from each antipodal pair in Table

. As for the congruences
Y\cong Z
, and the coefficients parameters
q_{1},s_{Y,Z},w_{0}

5.
Appendix

5.1.
Computer search

Below we give a high level overview of our graph search.

The symmetric witness set
X
, for which the value
\Delta(G_{X})
provides the upper bound for
\alpha_{3}
as described in Section
2
, is obtained by a heuristic search algorithm. Throughout the search we restrict our attention to symmetric sets
X
, and consider any antipodal pair
\{x_{i},-x_{i}\}
as a single vertex
v_{i}
in the orthogonality graph
G_{X}
, as explained in Section
2
.

We start from a promising configuration
G_{X_{0}}
of small cardinality, and iteratively add and remove vertices until we obtain a sufficiently low value of
\Delta(G_{X})
. Below, we refer to candidate sets
X
as
nodes
, as in nodes of a search tree, not to be confused with
vertices
, which term is reserved for the elements of
G_{X}
.

Computing
\Delta(G_{X})
is challenging: writing up the linear program requires finding all the independent sets of
G_{X}
and all the isometries among them. We work in the space of antipodal pairs, and adapt the isometry checks accordingly. The size of the LP seems to grow exponentially in
|G_{X}|
, so computing
\Delta(G_{X})
for large graphs is not feasible. We employed the GUROBI solver
[
8
]
on a computer with 1TB of RAM for our graph search, which limited us to circa
|G_{X}|\leq 33
.

The design of the search algorithm was guided by the following considerations:

•

monotonicity:
\Delta(G_{X})
is monotone w.r.t. set inclusion.

•

evaluation time: evaluating
\Delta(G_{X})
is roughly exponential in
|G_{X}|
.

5.1.1.
Beam-constrained Monte Carlo Tree Search

In standard MCTS setups typical in game-tree search
[
3
,
10
,
14
]
, inner nodes have similar evaluation times rather than depending heavily on tree depth.
Hence, we modify the standard MCTS algorithm with a beam search aspect: we extend a node only in the case when its value is at least the k-th best among nodes of the same size.
This trade-off may exclude some promising nodes, but significantly reduces computational demands.

The search starts from a carefully chosen 6-vertex graph
G_{X_{0}}
(corresponding to 6 antipodal pairs), see Table
2
. We only add vertices that are orthogonal to at least two vertices already present. Consequently, there are
O(n^{2})
possible expansions, which can be computed efficiently by taking cross products of vertex pairs.
Adding new vertices can make others redundant. Therefore, node expansion involves removing vertices as well as adding them. These reduced nodes are inserted into the selection path: they become the parent of the expanded node and the (virtual) child of its grandparent.

Node selection is performed according to the UCB bound
[
10
]
.
As our target is a simple maximization task and not the standard 2-player min/max-setup, we are looking for the best descendant of the root.
Therefore, during the expansion step, we evaluate all child nodes and backpropagate the maximum value observed among them.
We empirically normalize and rescale target values to the range
[0,1]
.

In the search that found
X
, we use exploration rate 0.1 and beam size 5. The total computational budget is roughly 14 CPU core-years on AMD EPYC 7643 processors.

5.2.
Symbolic verification

The vertices of
G_{X}
are given by closed algebraic expressions, and hence orthogonal pairs and geometric congruences of subsets
Y,Z\subset G_{X}
can be determined unambiguously.

After declaring rational values of the parameters
q_{1},s_{Y,Z},w_{0}
and
w_{i,j}
(which we find by linear programming, and slight modifications to account for numerical errors), we must verify that (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) are fulfilled.

Necessary tricks for checking (
3.12
):

First we find a threshold index
k_{0}
. For this we use the trivial estimate

|w_{0}P_{k}(0)+\sum_{i<j}w_{i,j}P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)|\leq|w_{0}P_{k}(0)|+\sum_{i<j}|w_{i,j}||P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)|,

and for each term we use the estimate

|P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)|=|P_{k}(\cos\theta_{i,j})|\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(1-\sin^{2}\theta_{i,j}\cos^{2}\phi)^{k/2}d\phi.

For each fixed
\theta
the integral is monotonically decreasing in
k
, so we first aim to find a particular
k_{0}
such that

(5.1)

\frac{2|w_{0}|}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(1-\cos^{2}\phi)^{k_{0}/2}d\phi+\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{i<j}|w_{i,j}|\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(1-\sin^{2}\theta_{i,j}\cos^{2}\phi)^{k_{0}/2}d\phi\leq\sum_{i}w_{ii}-1.

In order to find such a
k_{0}
, we observe that for each fixed
\theta
and
k
, the function
(1-\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\phi)^{k/2}
is monotonically increasing (as a function of
\phi
) on
[0,\pi/2]
, so the integral is easy to estimate by a Riemannian sum. With this, we find the threshold
k_{0}
. Finally, for any
k\geq k_{0}
we automatically have

w_{0}P_{k}(0)+\sum_{i\leq j}w_{i,j}P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)=\sum_{i}w_{i,i}+w_{0}P_{k}(0)+\sum_{i<j}w_{i,j}P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)\geq\\
\sum_{i}w_{i,i}-\left(|w_{0}P_{k}(0)|+\sum_{i<j}|w_{i,j}||P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)|\right)\geq\\
\sum_{i}w_{i,i}-\left(\frac{2|w_{0}|}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(1-\cos^{2}\phi)^{k_{0}/2}d\phi+\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{i<j}|w_{i,j}|\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(1-\sin^{2}\theta_{i,j}\cos^{2}\phi)^{k_{0}/2}d\phi\right)\geq 1

by equation (
5.1
), and hence (
3.12
) is satisfied for all
k\geq k_{0}
.

Next, we must check (
3.12
) for all
k<k_{0}
. This requires high-precision values of
P_{k}(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle)
. We obtain these using interval arithmetic: we compute
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle
to precision
10^{-7000}
and then propagate intervals through the recursion (
3.16
).
