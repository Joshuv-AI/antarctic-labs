A Novel Machine Learning Signature Incorporating Lipid and Inflammatory Biomarkers to Predict Pulsed Radiofrequency Efficacy in Zoster-Associated Pain
Authors: Lei Zhang, Hongfei Chen, Haoying Chen, Wenjing Hu, Junkai Zhang, Zhibing Pi, Yuan Jin, Yu Peng, Linchao Li, Hai Lin, Wujun Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPulsed radiofrequency (PRF) is a pivotal neuromodulation strategy for zoster-associated pain (ZAP); however, clinical outcomes exhibit significant interindividual heterogeneity. This study aimed to identify robust predictors and develop a transparent machine learning (ML) framework to forecast treatment response, thereby facilitating individualized pain management.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of a large-scale cohort comprising 1773 patients with ZAP treated with PRF. Patients were stratified into “responders” and “nonresponders” on the basis of clinical outcomes at a 3-month follow-up. To handle an initial pool of 47 multidimensional clinical and laboratory variables, a tripartite feature selection pipeline—incorporating least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), Boruta, and multivariable logistic regression—was implemented. We benchmarked eight ML architectures. The optimal model was interpreted using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) to ensure biological transparency and subsequently deployed as an interactive point-of-care tool.ResultsThe favorable clinical response rate for PRF was 68.0%. A parsimonious set of five core predictors was identified: age, baseline Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) score, preoperative opioid use, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMR). Among the candidate algorithms, the CatBoost architecture was selected for its robust performance, achieving the highest F1 score (0.853) and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.834. SHAP analysis revealed that preoperative opioid use was the most potent determinant of suboptimal response, followed by advanced age and high baseline pain intensity. Notably, elevated ApoB levels emerged as a novel metabolic indicator of favorable prognosis, whereas an increased NMR suggested a systemic pro-inflammatory state associated with diminished PRF efficacy.ConclusionsWe developed and internally validated a high-performance CatBoost-based model for predicting PRF outcomes in ZAP. By integrating novel metabolic (ApoB) and immunoinflammatory (NMR) biomarkers with established clinical metrics, this model provides a granular approach to risk stratification. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? While pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) is a pivotal neuromodulation strategy for zoster-associated pain (ZAP), existing prognostic models largely overlook the synergistic roles of lipid metabolism (apolipoprotein B, ApoB) and systemic neuroinflammation (neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio [NMR]) in predicting its highly heterogeneous therapeutic responses. What did the study ask? Can an interpretable machine learning framework integrating novel metabolic and immunoinflammatory biomarkers reliably predict individualized PRF treatment outcomes and facilitate personalized pain management? What was learned from the study? Leveraging a cohort of 1773 patients, we developed a high-performing CatBoost model (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC] = 0.834) revealing that preoperative opioid dependency, advanced age, severe pain, and elevated NMR drive PRF failure, whereas elevated ApoB acts as a novel protective biomarker.Through Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)-derived transparent clinical logic, this model has been successfully deployed as an open-access Streamlit web application, providing clinicians with a real-time risk-stratification tool to guide early intervention.

Introduction

Zoster-associated pain (ZAP), a neuropathic pain syndrome resulting from varicella-zoster virus reactivation, affects approximately 65% of patients with herpes zoster (HZ) [1]. Up to 30% of these individuals develop postherpetic neuralgia (PHN)—a chronic, refractory condition that significantly impairs quality of life, sleep, and mental health [2]. Pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) has emerged as a key interventional strategy for ZAP, with demonstrated efficacy in reducing pain intensity and preventing PHN [3].

However, clinical responses to PRF exhibit considerable variability: while some patients achieve sustained pain relief, others experience partial or no improvement. Given the narrow therapeutic window for preventing PHN, failure to accurately identify patients who were PRF-refractory early not only subjects them to ineffective invasive procedures but also delays alternative aggressive neuromodulation therapies. This accelerates the transition from acute viral neuropathy to highly refractory central sensitization, underscoring the urgent need for personalized prediction of treatment efficacy [4].

Current clinical practice relies heavily on subjective assessments and traditional statistical models, which are limited by three major constraints. First, they fail to capture the complex, synergistic interactions among multifaceted predictors—such as baseline immune status and concurrent medication use. Second, they frequently exclude high-dimensional data (e.g., laboratory indices) that profoundly affect treatment response. For instance, emerging pathophysiological evidence increasingly implicates systemic immune–inflammatory dysregulation and lipid metabolism imbalances as critical determinants of viral-induced neural injury and repair [5–8]; nevertheless, specific blood biomarkers reflecting these pathways are largely overlooked in standard PRF prognostic evaluations. Third, existing studies are often hampered by underpowered sample sizes and an overreliance on conventional linear multivariable regression. These linear models struggle to decode the high-dimensional, heterogeneous nature of real-world clinical data, thereby compromising both predictive discrimination and clinical interpretability [4, 9, 10].

Machine learning (ML) offers a robust methodological paradigm to overcome these limitations. Tree-based ensemble algorithms, particularly gradient-boosting architectures such as categorical boosting (CatBoost), have consistently demonstrated superior efficacy in complex clinical prediction tasks by efficiently modeling multidimensional and nonlinear relationships [11, 12]. Furthermore, recent advancements in explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), such as Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), resolve the historical “black-box” critique of ML by providing transparent, patient-level feature attribution [13, 14]. Despite these algorithmic advances, a persistent “bench-to-bedside” translational gap remains: the computational complexity of these models typically confines them to offline research, rendering them impractical for real-time, point-of-care clinical workflows.

To address the methodological limitations of prior studies and bridge this translational barrier, this study leveraged a large retrospective cohort (N = 1773) to develop, benchmark, and internally validate a suite of ML architectures for predicting PRF outcomes in patients with ZAP. By integrating novel systemic inflammatory and lipid metabolic biomarkers with routine clinical features, we aimed to optimize predictive discrimination. Specifically, we hypothesized that the inclusion of lipid metabolic (apolipoprotein B [ApoB]) and systemic neuroinflammatory (neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio [NMR]) indices would significantly enhance the model’s ability to identify patients at high risk of PRF failure compared with traditional clinical models alone. Beyond evaluating standard performance metrics, we used SHAP to anatomize the optimal model, thereby establishing the biological and clinical plausibility of key therapeutic determinants. Ultimately, we translated the final CatBoost model into an open-access, interactive web application, delivering a pragmatic clinical decision-support system (CDSS) to guide personalized ZAP management.

Methods

Study Design and Ethical Considerations

This retrospective, single-center cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines (Table S1 in the electronic supplementary material). The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No.: KY2025-R192). Given the retrospective, observational nature of the study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. All data were strictly de-identified prior to computational analysis.

Patients with zoster-associated pain (ZAP) who underwent pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) therapy between January 2018 and June 2025 were initially identified from the electronic medical records (EMR). Inclusion criteria were (1) confirmed clinical diagnosis of ZAP with a documented history of herpes zoster rash, encompassing the full continuous spectrum of the disease from acute/subacute phases to postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) and (2) inadequate response to at least 2 weeks of conventional pharmacotherapy prior to PRF. Exclusion criteria included (1) concurrent chronic pain disorders unrelated to herpes zoster, (2) substantial missing baseline data or loss to follow-up, (3) severe neurological/psychiatric disorders confounding pain assessment, and (4) severe systemic comorbidities (e.g., active malignancies). After applying these criteria, 414 of the initial 2187 patients were excluded (Fig. 1), yielding a final analytical cohort of 1773 patients. For machine learning modeling, outcomes were dichotomized into clinical responders (positive class, n = 1206) and nonresponders (negative class, n = 567).

Data Collection and Preprocessing

Clinical data were extracted by two independent researchers who were blinded to the final PRF treatment outcomes, comprising demographics, clinical indicators, and laboratory indices, yielding an initial pool of 47 candidate variables. According to standard preoperative protocols, all peripheral blood samples were collected within 72 h prior to the PRF procedure. To strictly minimize dietary confounding, particularly for lipid metabolic markers such as ApoB, blood draws were uniformly performed in the early morning under standard overnight fasting conditions (at least 8 h of fasting). Specifically, “preoperative opioid use” was defined as any documented administration or prescription of opioid analgesics (including records from both our institution and external hospitals) within 1 week prior to the PRF procedure. Variables missing in > 20% of cases (D-dimer and C-reactive protein) were excluded. For variables with ≤ 20% missing data, the multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) framework was implemented. To prevent logical inconsistencies between base features and their mathematical derivatives, a rigorous “passive imputation” strategy was employed. Highly skewed absolute indices (e.g., neutrophil and monocyte counts) were log-transformed, imputed using the random forest algorithm, and subsequently exponentially back-transformed. The pre-imputation missing data landscape for all retained features (≤ 20% missing data) is visually detailed in Supplementary Fig. S1. Finally, continuous variables underwent z-score standardization, and categorical variables were encoded prior to training (Supplementary Table S2).

Outcome Definition

Based on the core recommendations of the Initiative on Methods, Measurement, and Pain Assessment in Clinical Trials (IMMPACT) [15], “treatment failure” (nonresponder) at the 3-month follow-up was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: (1) moderate to severe residual pain (NRS score ≥ 4); (2) pain-induced sleep disturbances, (3) pain-related emotional disorders;, or (4) the requirement for additional interventional pain management. Patients meeting none of these criteria were classified as responders. Because validated scales (e.g., Insomnia Severity Index [ISI], Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [HADS]) were not routinely administered in retrospective real-world outpatient settings, sleep and emotional disturbances were extracted as binary variables based on explicit clinical documentation in the electronic medical records. To minimize subjective assessor bias and ensure reliability, the clinical outcomes at the 3-month follow-up were independently extracted by two researchers blinded to the baseline predictors. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus with a third senior physician.

Feature Selection

The dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%) via stratified sampling. To mitigate multicollinearity and isolate robust predictors, a tripartite feature selection pipeline was executed: (1) least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression for initial L1-regularized shrinkage, (2) the Boruta algorithm for rigorous isolation of highly important features against shadow variables, and (3) multivariable logistic regression to ensure independent statistical significance (P < 0.05). The intersection of these methods yielded a parsimonious five-feature signature: preoperative opioid use, baseline NRS, age, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMR).

Model Development, Validation, and Interpretation

Using the isolated features, eight machine learning algorithms—logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), CatBoost, LightGBM, linear discriminant analysis (LDA), and quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA)—were trained using tenfold crossvalidation. Hyperparameters were optimized via grid search maximizing the training area under the curve (AUC) (Supplementary Table S4). While logistic regression served as a linear benchmark, tree-based algorithms were prioritized due to their capacity to capture nonlinear thresholds and synergistic interactions inherent in complex clinico-serological microenvironments (e.g., the interplay between ApoB and NMR).

Model performance was evaluated on the independent testing set using discriminative metrics (AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score). Calibration was assessed via calibration curves and Brier scores, while clinical utility was quantified using decision curve analysis (DCA). The optimal model (CatBoost) was subsequently decoupled using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) to visualize the magnitude and directionality of each feature’s impact, providing patient-level biological interpretability.

Clinical Translation Via Streamlit Web Application

To facilitate real-time clinical application, the optimal model was deployed into a dynamic, web-based interactive application using the Streamlit framework (freely available at: https://zoster-pain-predictor.streamlit.app/). Unlike traditional static nomograms, this calculator allows clinicians to input patient-specific parameters—such as baseline NRS, age, and ApoB levels—to instantly generate personalized probability scores indicating the risk of PRF treatment failure, thereby seamlessly integrating data-driven risk stratification into the point-of-care clinical workflow.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics and traditional statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Machine learning pipeline development, model validation, and advanced data processing were executed using R software (version 4.5.2) and Python (version 3.11.9). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on their data distribution. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Intergroup comparisons were analyzed via the independent Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Wilcoxon rank–sum test (for non-normally distributed data). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and assessed using the Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bootstrapping with 1000 resamples was conducted to calculate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for model performance metrics and rigorously validate model stability. Sensitivity analyses—including alternative imputation methods and outlier exclusion—were performed to confirm the robustness of the findings.

Results

Patient Characteristics

This retrospective study included a total of 1773 eligible participants. Among them, 1206 (68.0%) patients achieved satisfactory pain relief post-PRF (responders), whereas 567 (32.0%) exhibited a poor treatment response (nonresponders). The dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (n = 1241) and a testing set (n = 532). Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics were excellently balanced between the training and testing cohorts. Crucially, no statistically significant differences were observed across all 47 baseline variables between the two groups (all P > 0.05). This highly balanced data partition eliminates potential baseline confounding and provides a rigorous, reliable foundation for the development and validation of downstream machine learning models. Comparative analyses of baseline characteristics revealed significant intergroup disparities across multiple demographic, clinical, and laboratory dimensions (Table 1). Regarding demographic and clinical profiles, nonresponders exhibited significantly older age, higher baseline numerical rating scale (NRS) scores, and distinct body mass index (BMI) distributions compared with responders (all P < 0.05). Furthermore, significant differences were observed in lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol consumption), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia), and pharmacological histories, notably the use of lipid-lowering drugs and preoperative opioids (all P < 0.05). In contrast, variables including sex, pain duration, lesion laterality, lesion location, history of malignancy or surgery, and the use of immunosuppressants did not differ significantly between the two groups (all P > 0.05).

Univariate analyses of laboratory indices revealed profound differences in the systemic immune–inflammatory and metabolic microenvironments between the groups. Specifically, absolute hematological counts (WBC, NEUT, LYMPH, PLT), classical inflammatory markers (ESR, IgM), and systemic immune–inflammatory ratios (NLR, NHR, PNR, NMR, SIRI, SII) were significantly distinct (all P < 0.05). Concurrently, variables reflecting the lipid profile and nutritional status—including ApoAI, ApoB, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), TCHO, TG, albumin, TP, AG, and HGB—also demonstrated robust statistical significance (all P < 0.05). Other specific biomarkers, such as eosinophils (EOX), monocytes (MONO), NSE, lipoprotein, HDL, globulin, ferritin, IgA, and IgG, remained comparable between the cohorts (all P > 0.05).

Feature Selection

To identify the most robust predictors associated with pain relief following pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) treatment, a comprehensive tripartite feature selection strategy was employed, incorporating the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), the Boruta algorithm, and multivariable logistic regression analysis.

First, the LASSO model, using optimized penalty parameters, minimized model deviance to isolate a subset of critical variables with nonzero coefficients. Concurrently, the Boruta algorithm—a robust random forest-based wrapper method—rigorously evaluated predictive importance, screening out high-value features by comparing original attributes against randomized shadow features (Fig. 2). Finally, multivariable logistic regression confirmed the independent statistical association of these candidate variables with the binary outcome of PRF efficacy (P < 0.05; Supplementary Table S3).

The intersection of these three distinct selection methodologies converged on a highly stable and clinically meaningful five-feature signature: preoperative opioid use, baseline NRS score, age, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMR). By consistently surviving LASSO, Boruta, and multivariable regression screening, these five variables represent the robust core predictors for subsequent machine learning modeling.

We benchmarked eight distinct machine learning architectures to predict PRF efficacy. Following rigorous hyperparameter optimization via grid search and tenfold crossvalidation (parameters detailed in Supplementary Table S4), the CatBoost model emerged as the optimal algorithm for final deployment (Table 2, Fig. 3).

The discriminative performance of the models was primarily evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall (PR) curves. As illustrated in Fig. 3A and Table 2, the CatBoost model demonstrated robust discriminative capacity on the test set, achieving an AUROC of 0.834 (95% CI 0.792–0.871). While logistic regression (LR), quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA), and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) yielded marginally higher AUROCs (0.842, 0.837, and 0.836, respectively), CatBoost was prioritized due to its superior overall balance across diverse clinimetrics. Notably, CatBoost achieved the highest overall accuracy (0.793), Kappa statistic (0.504), and Youden’s index (0.484) among all evaluated models. It delivered a highly stable and clinically pragmatic balance of precision (0.824), sensitivity (0.884), and specificity (0.600), culminating in the highest F1 score of 0.853. Furthermore, the PR curve (Fig. 3B) substantiated CatBoost’s excellent capability in managing this precision-recall trade-off, yielding a high PR-AUC of 0.898.

Beyond discriminative accuracy, model reliability and clinical utility were rigorously quantified. The calibration curve (Fig. 3C) revealed a tight alignment between the probabilities predicted by the CatBoost model and the actual observed positive rates, achieving a Brier score of 0.148. This indicates excellent goodness-of-fit and minimal prediction error. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was executed to assess real-world clinical applicability (Fig. 3D). The DCA demonstrated that the CatBoost model provided the highest net clinical benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities, markedly outperforming both the default “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies, as well as conventional statistical models (e.g., LR, LDA, and QDA).

Consequently, integrating its optimal comprehensive performance across discriminative power, balanced precision-recall trade-off, calibration reliability, and superior clinical net benefit, CatBoost was finalized as the underlying algorithmic engine for the web-based Streamlit calculator.

Global Feature Importance and Directional Effects

The directional impacts of specific feature values on PRF outcomes were visualized using a SHAP beeswarm plot (Fig. 4A). High feature values (depicted in red)—specifically preoperative opioid use (value = 1), advanced age, severe baseline pain (high NRS), and elevated systemic inflammation (high NMR)—consistently clustered on the negative side of the SHAP axis (SHAP value < 0). This distribution strongly indicates that these factors act as critical risk drivers, shifting the prediction toward treatment failure. Conversely, elevated ApoB levels (red points) were robustly clustered on the positive side (SHAP value > 0), confirming its role as a potent, protective metabolic factor driving favorable therapeutic responses.

Furthermore, the global feature importance ranking (Fig. 4B) revealed a distinct clinical hierarchy: preoperative opioid use exerted the highest average impact on the model’s output magnitude (mean |SHAP|), establishing it as the paramount predictor in the CatBoost algorithm. This was closely followed by age, baseline NRS, ApoB, and NMR.

Instance-Level Explanations of Treatment Outcomes

To illustrate the model’s clinical utility for individualized risk assessment, we used SHAP waterfall and force plots to visualize how individual prognostic scores (fx) deviate from the cohort’s expected base value (Efx) based on raw clinical inputs. For clinicians unfamiliar with machine learning, the “base value” represents the average baseline prognostic score across the entire cohort. A final predicted score (f(x)) greater than zero explicitly translates to a high clinical probability of being a “responder” (favorable outcome), whereas a negative f(x) value intuitively indicates a high risk of being a “nonresponder” (treatment failure).

Figure 4C deconstructs the step-by-step predictive logic for a severely refractory patient using a SHAP waterfall plot. Starting from the cohort’s expected baseline score E[f(x)]=1.385, each clinical feature cumulatively shifts the prediction. In this high-risk instance, extreme advanced age (90.0 years) and preoperative opioid use (value = 1) were the dominant risk factors, drastically penalizing the prognostic score by −3.06 and −1.0, respectively. Combined with a depleted ApoB level (0.78), severe baseline pain (NRS = 5.0), and elevated systemic neuroinflammation (NMR = 12.83), the final score was driven deep into the negative zone (f(x) = −4.204), indicating an extreme risk of treatment failure.

Complementarily, Fig. 4D employs SHAP force plots to contrast two distinct clinical phenotypes. The top plot depicts a highly favorable responder (f(x) = 2.81), where the absence of preoperative opioids (0.0), mild baseline pain (NRS = 2.0), robust lipid metabolic reserve (ApoB = 1.29), lower systemic inflammatory burden (NMR = 5.73), and relatively younger age (60.0 years) synergistically pushed the prediction toward a successful outcome. Conversely, the bottom plot illustrates a typical nonresponder (f(x) = −2.08), where the collective negative pull of severe systemic inflammation (NMR = 15.47), severe acute pain (NRS = 7.0), advanced age (80.0 years), depleted ApoB (0.6), and opioid use (1.0) completely neutralized any potential therapeutic benefit of PRF.

Clinical Translation Via Streamlit Web Application

To facilitate the practical clinical application of our predictive model, we developed and deployed an interactive, web-based platform using the Streamlit framework (freely accessible at: https://zoster-pain-predictor.streamlit.app/). Furthermore, to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility, the underlying source code has been deposited in a public GitHub repository. This platform serves as an intuitive point-of-care tool for clinicians to evaluate the probability of PRF treatment outcomes in patients with ZAP. By directly inputting the five-feature clinical signature (age, baseline NRS, preoperative opioid use, ApoB, and NMR), practitioners can instantly generate personalized risk stratifications. This initiative effectively bridges the translational gap between complex algorithmic models and real-time clinical decision-making.

Discussion

This large-scale retrospective study analyzed 1773 eligible patients with zoster-associated pain (ZAP) undergoing pulsed radiofrequency (PRF) treatment, aiming to identify robust predictive factors for PRF responsiveness and develop a high-performance, interpretable machine learning model. The overall effective rate of PRF for ZAP in our cohort was 68.0%, consistent with therapeutic efficacies reported in prior clinical literature [4]. Baseline comparative analyses revealed that nonresponders to PRF were characterized by older age, higher pain intensity, a greater prevalence of metabolic comorbidities, increased preoperative opioid reliance, and distinct derangements in systemic lipid and inflammatory profiles. Through a rigorous tripartite feature selection pipeline, a parsimonious five-feature signature—preoperative opioid use, age, baseline NRS, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMR)—was isolated as the core predictive foundation. Among the benchmarked machine learning architectures, CatBoost algorithm demonstrated the most balanced and clinically applicable discriminative performance (AUROC = 0.834, F1 score = 0.853) and superior clinical net benefit. Subsequent SHAP analysis unpacked this “black box,” providing a highly transparent and mechanistic basis for individualized clinical decision-making.

The five core predictors identified in this study carry profound pathophysiological and clinical implications. Strikingly, our global SHAP importance ranking revealed preoperative opioid use as the paramount determinant of PRF failure, surpassing traditional demographic metrics such as age. Mechanistically, this negative impact likely reflects two distinct but interrelated processes: opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH) and advanced central sensitization. Long-term or high-dose opioid exposure can paradoxically sensitize the nociceptive system, lowering the pain threshold via NMDA receptor activation and spinal dynorphin upregulation [16, 17]. In this profoundly sensitized state, the neuromodulatory mechanisms of PRF—which aim to stabilize neuronal membranes and suppress spinal microglial activation—may be overwhelmed by the overarching pro-nociceptive signaling driven by OIH [18]. Furthermore, preoperative opioid use serves as a high-fidelity surrogate marker for a “refractory pain phenotype,” signaling that maladaptive neuroplasticity and extensive nerve injury have already rendered the patient less receptive to interventional neuromodulation.

Advanced age emerged as the second most significant risk factor, likely attributable to age-related immunosenescence, diminished peripheral nerve repair capacity, and heightened neural vulnerability to viral-induced damage [19, 20]. Higher baseline NRS scores closely followed as a major negative predictor [20–22]. Rather than implying a direct causal detrimental effect, severe acute pain reflects a highly refractory baseline state, accelerating the transition from acute viral neuropathy to central sensitization. Together with opioid use, these findings support a “narrow therapeutic window” hypothesis: by the time a patient’s pain escalates to a level necessitating systemic opioids or reaches extreme intensity, the optimal window for achieving PRF-induced neuromodulation has begun to close [23].

Intriguingly, elevated ApoB—which strongly correlates with low-density lipoprotein (LDL)—emerged as a novel protective biomarker for PRF responsiveness. As the primary structural protein for lipid transport, ApoB provides the essential substrate for remyelinating virus-damaged peripheral nerves, with LDL supplying crucial cholesterol to Schwann cells [24, 25]. The functional integrity of peripheral nociceptors is intricately linked to this lipid homeostasis. Recent molecular evidence indicates that key pain-sensing ion channels, predominantly TRPV1 and TRPA1, are functionally anchored within cholesterol- and sphingolipid-rich membrane microdomains known as lipid rafts [23, 24]. While the acute, targeted depletion of local membrane cholesterol can inhibit nociceptor activation and produce analgesia, the long-term recovery from zoster-induced severe neuropathy inherently demands massive structural rebuilding. Therefore, higher baseline systemic lipid availability (reflected by elevated ApoB) likely provides the indispensable material basis for membrane remodeling and myelin regeneration during the critical repair phase.

Furthermore, the inclusion of NMR underscores the critical role of the systemic immune–inflammatory axis. An elevated NMR reflects a neutrophil-driven pro-inflammatory state coupled with a relative depletion of monocytes [26]. During neuropathic pain pathogenesis, circulating neutrophils are rapidly recruited to sites of nerve injury and sensory ganglia. Upon activation, they release a barrage of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β), reactive oxygen species, and chemokines, which directly lower nociceptor firing thresholds and profoundly drive peripheral and central sensitization [5, 6]. Conversely, monocytes are vital precursors for anti-inflammatory macrophages, which are indispensable for phagocytosing myelin debris and orchestrating nerve repair [27, 28]. Thus, a high baseline NMR predisposes patients to a prolonged state of systemic neuroinflammation. While PRF successfully modulates local neuroimmune responses in the spinal dorsal horn [18, 29, 30], a persistent systemic inflammatory “storm” indicated by a high NMR may override these localized effects, culminating in a blunted therapeutic response [31, 32].

Notably, in contrast to numerous prior studies [33], we did not observe a significant association between disease duration and PRF treatment outcomes. As chronological disease duration alone may not fully capture the clinically meaningful pathophysiological differences between distinct pain stages (e.g., acute inflammatory phase versus chronic central sensitization phase), it failed to serve as a robust predictor. Instead, the true biological pain stage—and its subsequent impact on PRF efficacy—was more accurately captured by dynamic circulating biomarkers in our model, such as NMR for systemic inflammation and ApoB for metabolic repair capacity. Furthermore, this implies a unique central neuromodulatory mechanism for PRF that differentiates it from purely peripheral-targeted modalities. Preclinical evidence demonstrates that PRF actively inhibits microglial hyperactivation and attenuates abnormal synaptic transmission [18]. This capacity to mitigate central sensitization regardless of disease chronicity highlights the distinct advantage of PRF for patients suffering from long-standing ZAP.

Similarly, we found no significant association between anatomical lesion location and PRF treatment efficacy (P = 0.101). This indicates that the fundamental neuromodulatory mechanisms of PRF—specifically the stabilization of neuronal membranes and the suppression of neuroinflammation at the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) level—are broadly robust across different dermatomes (e.g., trigeminal, cervical, thoracic, and lumbosacral regions). While the lesion location dictates the technical procedural approach, it does not inherently limit the biological responsiveness of the injured nerve to PRF, further underscoring the versatility of this interventional modality.

Algorithmically, while several machine learning frameworks exhibited comparable discriminatory power—and traditional logistic regression achieved a marginally higher AUROC (0.842 versus 0.834)—CatBoost was ultimately selected as the final predictive signature. This decision was driven by CatBoost’s superior overall performance across multiple holistic metrics, including the highest accuracy (0.793), F1 score (0.853), Kappa statistic (0.504), and Youden’s index (0.484). These metrics indicate that CatBoost provides a more balanced and clinically robust decision boundary, optimizing the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity without relying heavily on linear assumptions. Furthermore, this superiority is largely attributed to CatBoost’s innovative “ordered boosting” architecture, which effectively prevents target leakage, and its reliance on symmetric decision trees to mitigate overfitting in high-dimensional clinical datasets [34]. The integration of SHAP analysis elevated the model’s clinical utility by quantifying feature attributions, dynamically visualizing the individualized driving forces behind real-time predictions [35, 36].

From a clinical perspective, these findings strongly advocate for a paradigm shift toward “opioid-sparing interventional strategies.” Rather than escalating to systemic opioids when conventional pharmacotherapy fails, clinicians should prioritize integrating PRF earlier in the treatment algorithm. By intervening before the onset of opioid dependence and irreversible neural remodeling, the nervous system’s receptivity to neuromodulation can be preserved. The deployment of this validated CatBoost model as an open-access Streamlit web application directly translates these complex algorithmic insights into an accessible, bedside risk calculator, empowering clinicians with personalized, real-time risk stratification. While the application outputs a continuous probability score, a predicted failure probability exceeding the model’s default classification threshold (50%) serves as a practical “watershed” to flag highly refractory patients. For these high-risk individuals, clinicians should carefully weigh the utility of standard PRF and proactively consider escalating to higher-tier neuromodulation therapies (e.g., spinal cord stimulation) or intensified multidisciplinary management. This threshold-driven approach aims to prevent delayed interventions and minimize unnecessary procedural burdens.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the single-center, retrospective design inherently introduces selection bias and limits broader external generalizability. Second, although ZAP encompasses a continuous spectrum from acute herpes zoster to chronic PHN, we developed a universal predictive model for the entire cohort rather than performing subgroup analyses based on distinct pain stages. While this maximizes sample size and provides a generalized tool for real-world screening, we acknowledge that specific inflammatory and metabolic microenvironments may dynamically evolve across different disease phases, warranting stage-specific model validation in future studies. Third, granular long-term outcome trajectories (e.g., 1-year follow-up) were not captured. Fourth, the variable influence of antiviral therapy (e.g., discrepancies in viral load and replication control) remains an unaccounted confounder, exacerbated by incomplete pharmacological histories transferred from external institutions. Additionally, due to the constraints of retrospective data extraction, we were unable to accurately calculate the cumulative morphine milligram equivalent (MME) for patients who used opioids. Future prospective studies are warranted to rigorously quantify the dose–response relationship between preoperative opioid dosage and PRF efficacy. Fifth, specific PRF procedural parameters (e.g., precise radiofrequency duration, voltage, and temperature) and the choice of specific radiofrequency modalities were not incorporated into the model. Recent evidence highlights that optimizing RF parameters (such as using high-voltage or prolonged duration) or employing radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RF-TC) can significantly impact recurrence rates and long-term therapeutic outcomes in ZAP [37, 38]. Future predictive models should aim to integrate these procedural technicalities to further refine prognostic accuracy.

Conclusions

We engineered and validated an interpretable, CatBoost-driven machine learning framework that accurately predicts PRF outcomes in patients with ZAP based on a parsimonious five-feature clinico-biological signature. The identification of preoperative opioid use as the paramount risk determinant, alongside the discovery of novel lipid (ApoB) and inflammatory (NMR) prognostic biomarkers, offers profound new insights into ZAP pathophysiology and management. Deployed as an interactive point-of-care web application, this model bridges the translational gap, providing clinicians with a robust, transparent decision-support tool to individualize risk stratification and optimize early interventional strategies.
