Reasoning Resides in Layers: Restoring Temporal Reasoning in Video-Language Models with Layer-Selective Merging
Zihang Fu, Haonan Wang, Jian Kang, Kenji Kawaguchi, Jiaying Wu

Abstract
Multimodal adaptation equips large language models (LLMs) with perceptual capabilities, but often weakens the reasoning ability inherited from language-only pretraining. This trade-off is especially pronounced in video-language models (VLMs), where visual alignment can impair temporal reasoning (TR) over sequential events. We propose MERIT , a training-free, task-driven model merging framework for restoring TR in VLMs. MERIT searches over layer-wise self-attention merging recipes between a VLM and its paired text-only backbone using an objective that improves TR while penalizing degradation in temporal perception (TP). Across three representative VLMs and multiple challenging video benchmarks, MERIT consistently improves TR, preserves or improves TP, and generalizes beyond the search set to four distinct benchmarks. It also outperforms uniform full-model merging and random layer selection, showing that effective recovery depends on selecting the right layers. Interventional masking and frame-level attribution further show that the selected layers are disproportionately important for reasoning and shift model decisions toward temporally and causally relevant evidence. These results show that targeted, perception-aware model merging can effectively restore TR in VLMs without retraining.



1
Introduction

Reasoning abilities acquired during language-only pretraining are often expected to transfer naturally to multimodal models. In practice, however, multimodal adaptation can weaken the very capabilities that make LLMs effective reasoners
(
17
)
. A standard recipe for building video-language models (VLMs) is to attach a visual encoder to a pretrained LLM, as in models such as LongVA
(
41
)
. While this design yields strong perceptual performance, it can
substantially compromise
the reasoning ability inherited from the language backbone. As illustrated in Figure
1
, this degradation appears in two forms. First, multimodal adaptation can disrupt the model’s intrinsic text-based temporal reasoning, causing the adapted VLM to fail on temporal logic problems that the original LLM solves correctly. Second, even when the VLM correctly identifies salient objects and visual cues in a video, it may still fail to infer the temporal-causal structure of the event sequence. We attribute these failures to a
mismatch in adaptation objectives
: existing multimodal training pipelines are dominated by spatial perception and object-level alignment, while providing much weaker supervision for temporal abstraction and causal inference
(
25
)
. As a result, multimodal adaptation can erode internal representations that support higher-level reasoning, leaving the model with strong perceptual competence but limited temporal understanding. This tension is especially pronounced for temporal reasoning (TR), which requires tracking event order, causality, and state changes across time
(
6
;
33
)
. For VLMs, such reasoning is indispensable: understanding a video requires not only perceiving objects and actions, but also relating events over time to infer how they unfold and causally interact
(
3
;
9
;
21
;
5
)
.

A base LLM correctly answers a text-only TR question, whereas the corresponding VLM fails on the identical text input after multimodal adaptation.
Bottom:
On a video task, while the VLM correctly perceives salient visual entities, it fails to infer the causal-temporal structure of the event sequence, exposing a gap between visual perception and TR.

Existing approaches to recovering these lost capabilities remain unsatisfactory. Training-based methods, such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
17
)
, can improve reasoning, but they require additional supervision and substantial computation. Training-free model merging is more efficient, but existing approaches
(
4
)
typically merge the VLM and its paired text backbone uniformly at the full-model level, treating the language backbone as a homogeneous block. This coarse-grained strategy may recover some reasoning ability, but does so unreliably and often at the cost of visual performance. At the same time, recent work suggests that reasoning-related behavior in large models is not uniformly distributed, but can depend disproportionately on specific layers or subnetworks
(
28
;
29
;
15
)
. We therefore argue that
the trade-off between reasoning recovery and perceptual preservation in multimodal models is not fundamental.
Instead, TR in VLMs may depend disproportionately on
a localized subset of layers
, such that selectively restoring these layers can recover reasoning more effectively than uniform full-model merging while minimizing interference with temporal perception.

Motivated by this, we propose
MERIT
, a training-free, task-driven model merging framework for restoring TR in VLMs through selective layer-level intervention. Instead of uniformly averaging parameters across the full model,
MERIT
searches over layer-wise self-attention merging recipes between a VLM and its paired text-only backbone. The search is driven by an explicit objective that rewards TR gains while penalizing degradation in temporal perception (TP), allowing
MERIT
to recover temporal reasoning under perceptual constraints. As this objective is evaluated on a targeted temporal subset,
MERIT
identifies effective layer-wise recipes without requiring full-benchmark search or retraining. In this way,
MERIT
replaces coarse full-model merging with targeted capability recovery.

Extensive experiments across three representative VLMs show that
MERIT
consistently improves TR while preserving, and in many cases improving, TP and overall performance. These gains transfer well beyond the targeted Video-MME
(
11
)
search subset of 55 TP examples and 177 TR examples to four additional popular video benchmarks, indicating that the discovered recipes recover reasoning behavior that generalizes across datasets. Uniform full-model merging and random layer selection both perform worse, showing that effective recovery depends critically on selecting the right layers. Interventional masking further shows that the layers selected by
MERIT
are disproportionately important for reasoning, while frame-level attribution reveals a shift away from locally salient cues and toward temporally and causally relevant evidence. These findings suggest that targeted, perception-aware model merging may provide a scalable path for restoring TR and, more broadly, other specialized reasoning capabilities in multimodal systems without costly retraining.

2
Related Work

Video Temporal Reasoning.

Temporal reasoning has long been a central challenge in video understanding. Early benchmarks such as CLEVRER
(
38
)
and NExT-QA
(
35
)
explicitly evaluate causal and temporal relations, but strong performance on these datasets does not necessarily indicate robust temporal understanding in modern Video-LLMs. Recent diagnostic benchmarks reveal substantial gaps. TempCompass
(
19
)
shows that current models struggle with fine-grained temporal perception, while analyses on TOMATO
(
27
)
and TVBench
(
7
)
show that many existing benchmarks remain vulnerable to static biases and shortcut exploitation, allowing models to answer correctly without genuine temporal reasoning. More controlled evaluations, including Vinoground
(
39
)
and LongVideoBench
(
34
)
, further highlight persistent weaknesses in tracking event dependencies and reasoning over long video contexts. Recent work also suggests that these failures are not purely perceptual. Although visual encoders may capture relevant temporal cues, the adapted language backbone often fails to transform those cues into coherent temporal reasoning
(
16
)
. This perspective is central to our work: rather than treating temporal failure as solely a representation problem, we target the reasoning bottleneck in the language backbone itself.

Enhancing Reasoning in Video-Language Models.

Existing approaches to improving reasoning in VLMs can be broadly divided into training-based and training-free methods. Training-based methods typically introduce time-aware architectural components, instruction tuning
(
26
;
14
)
, or explicit supervision on intermediate reasoning traces through self-training or distillation
(
30
;
23
;
10
)
. In a related direction,
16
show that targeted text-only supervision can improve temporal reasoning, further suggesting that the language backbone is a primary bottleneck. While effective, these approaches require additional data, supervision, and computation. Training-free approaches instead aim to improve reasoning either at inference time, for example through modular prompting and task decomposition
(
20
;
18
)
, constrained decoding that enforces temporal consistency
(
22
)
, or more effective use of reasoning demonstrations at test time
(
31
)
, or at the parameter level through model merging
(
4
)
. Among these, model merging is a particularly promising direction because it modifies model behavior directly at the parameter level without retraining.
4
show that merging a reasoning-specialized LLM into a vision-language model can recover logical deduction, and suggest that perception and reasoning may reside in different parts of the network. However, existing merging methods remain coarse-grained: they focus primarily on static image settings and apply uniform parameter averaging across the model. In contrast, we study the video setting, where temporal reasoning is essential, and propose a task-driven, layer-selective merging framework that restores reasoning while explicitly preserving temporal perception.

Task-Driven Layer-Selective Merging for Temporal Reasoning

Uniformly merging a VLM with its paired text-only backbone treats the language stack as a homogeneous component and often forces a trade-off between perceptual competence and reasoning ability. We instead propose
MERIT
(
M
erging for
E
nhanced
R
easoning
I
n
T
emporal Tasks
), a task-driven framework for selective layer-level merging. As illustrated in Figure
2
,
MERIT
searches for a layer-wise merging recipe that restores temporal reasoning while preserving temporal perception, enabling targeted capability recovery within the VLM’s language backbone.

3.1
Problem Formulation and Objective

Let
M
denote a VLM and
N
its paired text-only LLM backbone. Our goal is to construct a merged model that recovers temporal reasoning from
N
without disrupting the temporal perception already acquired by
M
. To this end,
MERIT
defines a family of layer-wise merging recipes parameterized by
g
, where each recipe specifies how the self-attention parameters of
N
are incorporated into the corresponding layers of
M
.

We evaluate each merged model on a temporal task set
\mathcal{T}=\{\mathrm{TP},\mathrm{TR}\}
, where
\mathrm{TP}
denotes Temporal Perception and
\mathrm{TR}
denotes Temporal Reasoning. Let
\mathrm{Acc}_{t}(g)
denote the accuracy of the merged model produced by recipe
g
on task
t\in\mathcal{T}
. To preserve perceptual competence, we measure the degradation in temporal perception relative to the original VLM
M
. Let
\mathrm{Acc}^{\mathrm{base}}_{\mathrm{TP}}
denote the TP accuracy of
M
, and define the TP degradation under recipe
g
as

D_{\mathrm{TP}}(g)=\max\big(0,\mathrm{Acc}^{\mathrm{base}}_{\mathrm{TP}}-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{TP}}(g)\big).

(1)

We then optimize for TR under an explicit penalty on perceptual degradation:

\displaystyle g^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{g}\ \mathcal{F}(g),

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(g)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{TR}}(g)-\lambda\cdot D_{\mathrm{TP}}(g),

where
\lambda
controls the strength of the TP penalty. This objective favors recipes that improve temporal reasoning while preserving temporal perception, thereby operationalizing our central design principle of reasoning recovery under perceptual constraints.

The remaining challenge is to optimize this objective efficiently over a large space of layer-wise merging recipes, which we address with evolutionary search.

3.2
Evolutionary Search with Interpolated Self-Attention Merging

The objective in Eq.
2
defines a combinatorial search problem over layer-wise merging recipes. This is challenging for two reasons. First, for a VLM with
L
self-attention layers, the space of possible layer-level structures grows exponentially with
L
. Second, the fitness of a recipe is defined by downstream evaluation on temporal reasoning and temporal perception, making
\mathcal{F}(g)
non-differentiable and expensive to optimize directly. We therefore adopt the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES)
(
13
)
, which is well suited to black-box optimization over structured, non-differentiable objectives.

To make the search both flexible and tractable, we parameterize each candidate recipe by a continuous gating vector
g\in[0,1]^{L}
, where each dimension corresponds to one self-attention layer. During evaluation, the continuous gates are thresholded to produce a discrete layer-wise structure:

\hat{g}_{\ell}=\begin{cases}1,&g_{\ell}\geq 0.5,\\
0,&g_{\ell}<0.5.\end{cases}

(3)

This design allows CMA-ES to explore the recipe space continuously while still yielding a concrete merged model for evaluation.

Given a discrete recipe
\hat{g}
, we merge the self-attention parameters at layer
\ell
using directional interpolation:

\theta_{\ell}=\begin{cases}\alpha\,\theta^{M}_{\ell}+(1-\alpha)\,\theta^{N}_{\ell},&\hat{g}_{\ell}=1,\\
(1-\alpha)\,\theta^{M}_{\ell}+\alpha\,\theta^{N}_{\ell},&\hat{g}_{\ell}=0,\end{cases}

(4)

where
\theta^{M}_{\ell}
and
\theta^{N}_{\ell}
denote the self-attention parameters of layer
\ell
in
M
and
N
, respectively, and
\alpha\in[0.5,1]
controls the interpolation strength. Intuitively,
\hat{g}_{\ell}
determines which model dominates the merged parameters at layer
\ell
, enabling MERIT to bias different layers toward either perceptual grounding from the VLM or reasoning priors from the text backbone.

CMA-ES iteratively samples a population of gating vectors, converts them to discrete recipes, instantiates the corresponding merged models, evaluates them using
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
, and updates the search distribution toward higher-performing regions. To keep the search tractable, we do not jointly optimize
\alpha
with
g
. Instead, we restrict
\alpha
to a small discrete set
\{1.0,0.9,0.8,0.7,0.6,0.5\}
and run independent evolutionary searches for each value. The final recipe is selected as the one that achieves the highest objective score across all runs.

The result is a task-specific merged VLM whose layer-wise configuration is optimized to recover temporal reasoning while preserving temporal perception.

4.2
MERIT Recovers Temporal Reasoning While Preserving Perception

Tables
1
and
2
summarize the performance of
MERIT
and the baselines; full
Random-k
results are provided in Appendix
C.4
. We highlight four main observations.

Temporal Reasoning Improves Consistently.

MERIT
improves TR performance across all evaluated architectures on Video-MME (Table
1
), with relative gains of +23.9% for LongVA-7B, +10.8% for InternVL3-8B, and +3.8% for Qwen3-VL-4B. These improvements also transfer beyond the search benchmark to LongVideoBench, LVBench, and MMBench-Video, indicating that the discovered merging recipes generalize across datasets.

Perception is Preserved.

Although
MERIT
is optimized for TR, it maintains temporal perception and overall performance well. On Video-MME (Table
1
), TP is preserved across all three backbones, and performance on the broader benchmark suite remains stable, with occasional improvements. This is consistent with the objective in §
3
, which explicitly penalizes perceptual degradation during search.

Selective Merging is Necessary.

Both
Base
and
Random-k
consistently underperform
MERIT
. Uniformly merging all layers yields inconsistent gains and is more likely to coincide with drops in perception or overall accuracy, suggesting that full-model merging is too coarse-grained to recover reasoning reliably. Random layer selection performs substantially worse than
MERIT
and exhibits high variance across runs, indicating that recovery depends not only on how many layers are merged, but also on which layers are selected.

MERIT Generalizes Beyond the Search Set.

The merging recipes are identified using only a small subset of Video-MME, yet they generalize well to four additional video benchmarks (Table
2
). This suggests that
MERIT
is not simply fitting the search set, but is recovering reasoning behavior that transfers across datasets and evaluation settings. Complete layer configurations and interpolation weights are provided in Appendix
C.2
.

4.3
MERIT Identifies Reasoning-Critical Layers

To test whether the layers selected by
MERIT
are especially important for temporal reasoning, we perform layer-masking interventions on the
unmodified
base VLMs. For each selected self-attention layer, we scale its output by a coefficient
\kappa\in[0,1]
, where
\kappa=1
leaves the model unchanged and
\kappa=0
fully suppresses the layer output while preserving the residual connection. We evaluate the masked models on LVBench using
\kappa\in\{1.0,0.8,0.6,0.4,0.2,0.0\}
and define relative degradation as

\Delta(\kappa)=\frac{\mathrm{Score}(\kappa=1.0)-\mathrm{Score}(\kappa)}{\mathrm{Score}(\kappa=1.0)}.

(5)

We track three metrics:
Reasoning
,
Overall
, and
Others
, where
Others
denotes the mean of non-reasoning sub-metrics. Figure
3
shows that masking the selected layers degrades
Reasoning
substantially faster than either
Overall
or
Others
. For LongVA-7B at
\kappa=0.4
,
Reasoning
drops by 35.1%, compared with 16.8% for
Overall
and 2.7% for
Others
. At
\kappa=0
, the
Reasoning
drop reaches 51.7%. InternVL3-8B shows a similar pattern: at
\kappa=0
,
Reasoning
drops by 58.2%, compared with 44.2% for
Overall
and 35.2% for
Others
. These results provide interventional evidence that the layers selected by
MERIT
are disproportionately important for temporal reasoning.

4.4
MERIT
Shifts Temporal Grounding Toward Causally Relevant Evidence

To better understand how
MERIT
improves temporal reasoning, we analyze behavioral changes on temporal-reasoning questions from suspense short films in the Video-Holmes benchmark. We use InternVL3-8B as a representative case and examine two complementary views: (1) frame-level visual attribution at the decision step, and (2) the corresponding generated reasoning traces for the base and merged models.

Attributing the Final Answer Decision.

For Video-Holmes, we compute attribution at the
answer token

t
to isolate the visual evidence underlying the final choice. Given an input
x
and generated output
y
formatted as
<think>...</think>\n<answer>...</answer>
, we extract the prefix
y_{<t}
ending immediately before the answer token. Applying teacher forcing to
(x,y_{<t})
yields next-token logits
z_{t}
. Restricting
z_{t}
to the candidate set
\mathcal{C}
of six options, we define the attribution target as the logit margin between the selected option
c
and its strongest alternative:

s=z_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}(c)-\max_{c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{c\}}z_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime}).

(6)

This score isolates the evidence that most directly supports the model’s final decision.

Measuring Frame-Level Evidence for the Final Decision.

Suppose the input contains
T
uniformly sampled frames. Each frame is encoded into
K
visual tokens and projected into the LLM embedding space, producing projected embeddings
V_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}
for frame
t
. We define frame importance by aggregating gradient-activation products with respect to the decision score
s
:

I_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\frac{\partial s}{\partial V_{t}[k,j]}\cdot V_{t}[k,j]\right|.

(7)

The scores
\{I_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are min-max normalized to
[0,1]
. Because attribution is computed at the answer step, it highlights the temporal evidence prioritized when the model commits to a decision.

Qualitative Evidence of Improved Temporal Grounding.

Figures
4
and
5
present two representative examples. Because evaluation is performed over 64 frames whereas each figure displays 12 key frames, we first smooth attribution scores over a local temporal window of 5 neighboring frames, and then select frames based on both attribution ranking and consistency with the generated reasoning trace. In Figure

, the base model answers incorrectly by focusing on a salient but misleading local event, specifically the male lead on the phone, and concluding that the victim died because help did not arrive in time. In contrast,
MERIT
emphasizes two temporally separated but causally informative moments, specifically the victim being restrained and the later scene in which the victim is found lying motionless on the ground. Based on these frames,
MERIT
infers the correct causal account that the victim was killed by the murderer, even though the killing act itself is not explicitly shown. In Figure

MERIT

MERIT
is less likely to rely on misleading local evidence and more likely to ground its predictions in temporally structured event relations. Additional examples are provided in Appendix

5
Conclusion

We present MERIT, a training-free, task-driven model merging framework for restoring temporal reasoning in VLMs. By formulating merging as an optimization problem over layer-wise self-attention interventions, MERIT identifies configurations that improve temporal reasoning while preserving temporal perception. Across diverse VLMs and multiple challenging benchmarks, MERIT yields consistent gains in reasoning, generalizes beyond the search set, and is supported by interventional and attribution analyses that localize reasoning-critical layers and reveal shifts toward temporally grounded evidence. These findings show that reasoning deficits introduced during multimodal adaptation can be addressed through targeted architectural intervention, pointing toward a scalable path for recovering specialized capabilities in future multimodal systems.

Appendix A
Limitations and Future Work

Our results show that targeted, perception-aware model merging can effectively restore temporal reasoning in VLMs without retraining. At the same time, they open several promising directions for future work. First,
MERIT currently relies on benchmark-defined evaluation signals for temporal perception and temporal reasoning
, so the discovered recipes may depend on how these capabilities are operationalized. Extending the framework to settings with weaker, noisier, or less structured supervision would broaden its applicability. Second,
the current formulation reflects a practical balance between expressivity and tractability.
In principle, one could jointly optimize a continuous
L
-dimensional parameterization that unifies gating variables and interpolation weights, yielding a richer search space than our discretized formulation. Developing more efficient optimization methods for such continuous merging objectives is a promising next step. Finally,
the optimal recipes discovered by MERIT vary across models, suggesting that layer-selective recovery is currently model-specific.
Understanding whether there exist more transferable principles for layer selection, or even reusable recipes across related architectures, is important for future exploration.

Appendix D
Additional Case Studies on Frame-Level Attribution

We present additional attribution-based case studies that further illustrate how
MERIT
changes temporal grounding behavior. As in the main paper, we compare the base model and
MERIT
using frame-level attribution and generated reasoning traces, focusing on whether the model relies on isolated local cues or integrates temporally distributed evidence.

Figures
6
and
7
provide two additional case studies that complement the examples in the main paper. Figure
6
highlights a failure case in which the base model anchors on a salient but ultimately non-causal event, whereas
MERIT
integrates a longer sequence of temporally related clues to recover the correct explanation. Figure
7
shows a case where both models answer correctly, but
MERIT
exhibits stronger temporal abstraction by linking repeated events into a coherent cyclic pattern rather than relying on a single observed transition. Together, these examples further illustrate that
MERIT
improves temporal grounding not only by correcting errors, but also by inducing more structured event-level reasoning when the final prediction is unchanged.

Figure 6:
Case study (Video-Holmes, Question ID: 565).
The base model attributes high importance to the moment when the murderer enters through the unlocked door, and consequently identifies “Unlocked door” as the direct cause of death. This reflects a static interpretation based on a single salient event. In contrast, MERIT focuses on a sequence of temporally related events: an initial staged scare, a first scream followed by a non-lethal encounter, and a second scream that is ignored due to the earlier false alarm. By integrating these events, MERIT correctly infers that ignoring the second scream is the decisive factor leading to the victim’s death. This example suggests that MERIT is better at reasoning over multi-stage temporal dependencies rather than anchoring on isolated frames.

Figure 7:
Case study (Video-Holmes, Question ID: 527).
Both models predict the correct answer, but differ in reasoning quality. The base model focuses on a single instance of the event (waking up, finding the gun, and firing it), and attributes the phenomenon to this isolated transition. In contrast, MERIT links multiple occurrences of the same event pattern and explicitly captures their repetition. By relating the first and second wake-up sequences, MERIT infers a recurring structure where the gunshot consistently triggers a shift in reality. This example suggests that MERIT better captures cyclic temporal patterns, while the base model relies on a single observed transition.
