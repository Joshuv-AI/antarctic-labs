Executive Summary
The primary objective of this project was to design, construct, and operate a first-of-a-kind
demonstration facility capable of producing rare earth elements (REEs) and select critical minerals (CMs)
from domestic feedstocks. The facility described herein was designed to produce 1 to 3 metric tons per
day of REE product and is anticipated to provide net environmental benefits through the use of
premium feedstocks derived primarily from acid mine drainage as well as other deleterious mining
wastes. Once operational, the facility will separate mixed rare earth oxides into individual and binary
REE components prior to refining those elements into high purity REE metals. As envisaged, the facility
will separate, refine, and purify REE/CM products at a single site to be located in the Central or Northern
Appalachian coal mining region. The REE/CM products from the facility will focus on magnet and
battery critical materials (i.e. Nd, Pr, Dy, Tb, Co, Li, Mn, Ni) to support domestic manufacturing and clean
energy technologies. The overall project as well as the demonstration facility will also provide
community benefits, both locally, and nationally, through education and training opportunities,
economic development, and environmental cleanup.

The project activities were to be carried out in two phases. Phase 1 included the activities needed to
complete a Front-End Engineering Design study according to the American Association of Cost Engineers
Class 3 guidelines. The scope of work for Phase 1 included: project management; ongoing community
engagement; preparation of National Environmental Policy Act documentation; preparation and
submittal of all permit applications required for construction and operation; the completion of an AACE
Class 3 FEED Study; the completion of all bench-scale and pilot-scale test work needed to optimize and
integrate the REE/CM separation and refining process; and the development and execution of various
operating contracts and plans.

Subsequently, Phase 2 was to include the detailed engineering, procurement, construction, and
operation of the separation and refining facility over a period of four years.

Due to several administrative and financial challenges, the project did not successfully move to Phase 2,
and as such, the findings in this report are framed around the envisaged facility and the lessons learned
from the Phase 1 activities.

Background
Since 2016, the project team led by WVU and supported by Virginia Tech (VT), L3 Process Development
and numerous private sector partners has pioneered a method to extract rare earth elements (REEs)
and critical minerals (CMs) from AMD. Known as AMDREE (US Patent 10,954,582 B2), the technology
resulting from this multifaceted research, development and deployment effort has been supported by
almost $30 million in federal funds and $8 million in non-Federal cost share. Figure 1 illustrates the
technology progress pace at which efforts in this program have ranged from our initial exploratory and
bench-scale studies (DE-FE0026927) to regional sampling and resource estimation (DE-FE0026444) to
integrated technology development (DE-FE0031524). Recently completed and current efforts in the
program include pilot-scale production of mixed rare earth oxides (DE-FE0031834), advanced process
development for separation and refining (DE-FE003120), regional economic development,
infrastructure, and technology assessment (DE-FE0032054), the pre-feasibility study for a vertically
integrated 1-3 ton per day (tpd) separation and refining facility (Contract Number:
89243320CFE000059), and a Class 3 FEED study (submitted as part of this report). Figure 1 illustrates the
progression of our technology development process to the current technology readiness level (TRL) 6.

REE/CM enriched feedstocks based on AMD or other deleterious mineral processing wastes have several
community and environmental advantages. Most come from already permitted mine sites requiring no
further mining which leads to minimal site commissioning delays. Our technique does not produce
radioactive byproducts like most conventional rare earth mines and treatment will result in cleaner
lands and waterways. Finally, low-pH AMD has similar properties and REE distribution regardless of its
location garnering a more predictable and economical process. AMD has 45%+ distribution of the
desirable heavy rare earth (HREE) elements most frequently utilized in green energy and defense
technologies, a number far surpassing that of MP Minerals’ Mine (CA, USA) or Bayan Obo (China). This
approach will not only create valuable, new jobs for coal workers displaced by the energy transition but

 6

will build upon previously established relationships for strong community engagement, outreach, and
workforce development.

Altogether, these prior efforts have culminated in both a commercial-ready technology package suitable
for deployment as well as an innovative ecosystem and research infrastructure that will support further
refinement, optimization and is poised to make a disruptive impact on critical materials supply chains in
the future. Our approach has been commercially focused since our initial exploratory studies and has
been well aligned with the USDOE and the National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) goals to
secure domestic supply chains for REEs from diverse sources. Techno-economic assessment, contained
value analysis, market readiness analysis, and process scalability have been longstanding tools and
constraints in our technology development efforts. Notably, our initial decision to focus efforts on AMD-
based feedstocks was based on market factors, such as the high fraction of magnet-critical REEs (i.e. Nd,
Pr, Tb, Dy), high contained value, and amenability to conventional (and thus scalable) processing
routes. Thus far, we have partnered with the West Virginia Department of Environmental Protection
(WVDEP) to construct two AMDREE pilot plants near Mt. Storm, and Morgantown, WV (Figure 2). These
facilities can treat up to 1500 gallons of AMD per minute from adjacent coal properties while producing
about 2 tons per year (tpy) of REE plus other CMs in the form of mixed oxides.

Figure 2. The A34 AMDREE facility near Mt. Storm WV (l) and the new Richard AMDREE plant (r) near Morgantown WV.

The facilities produce compliant discharge water and hydraulic preconcentrate (HPC). HPC is then
treated to yield pregnant leach solution (PLS) containing mixed rare earth oxides (MREO) at the A34
site. Our prior success in technology development and scaleup, as well as the positive technical and
economic indicators, identified in our AACE Class 3 cost estimate and front-end engineering design
(FEED) study indicated a sound strategic focus.

Introduction
Every REE facility needs to know how their costs compare to those of China with the understanding that
China subsidizes their REE production at every stage. These Chinese subsidies take the form of tax
breaks, rebates, grants, discounted energy costs, discounted chemical costs, lower cost or requirements
of ESG standards, discounted labor, and many others. Because of these subsidies, any facility outside of
China is at a market disadvantage. China has methodically and strategically built their REE supply chain
over the last four decades to gain market share and leverage the REE industry to their advantage.

 7

It will take many years to re-establish a rare earth supply chain outside of China. During this time, the US
Government may need to supply subsidies in many forms—tax breaks, purchase agreements, grants,
loans, etc.—to support rebuilding the supply chain here. REEs supply critical materials across our
economy and should be supported not just for military and DOD purposes, but also to ensure that our
commercial sector has access to cutting edge materials for future applications.

Building a demonstration facility such as the one proposed will provide value far beyond the REE
materials produced. The ﬁrst priority was to produce essential baseline material for the defense sector,
key industry players (such as semiconductors), and the research & development eﬀorts of commercial
players, national labs, and academic institutions. In addition to this, it was proposed to provide a training
environment for much needed future REE expertise and a proving ground for potential new
technologies. As the industry grows in the US, commercial players will need experts that are ready to
immediately enter the market with an appropriate skillset. The Chinese have over 30 State Key labs that
train REE workers—this is part of the reason that they have such a labor advantage. We need workers
that are ready to engage immediately throughout the supply chain.

WVU’s commitment to this project and the underlying strategic mission is unquestioned. WVU
understands the importance of securing a domestic supply of rare earth elements and other critical
minerals for our county's national and economic security. We continued to engage with our advisory
committee and potential investors relative to how best to move forward to deliver on this important
mission.

Project Goal
This project seeked to develop a vertically integrated, domestic REE supply chain based on AMD and
mineral tailings feedstocks. In this project, we have designed a demonstration facility capable of
producing REEs as well as selected critical materials including lithium, cobalt, zinc, nickel and manganese.
Processing operations are designed to be ﬂexible with market demand for select critical materials, thus
intermediate products will be processed to either high-purity oxides, salts or metals on an element-by-
element basis. Speciﬁc process goals include a conﬁguration capable of producing at least 1 tpd of
individually separated, high purity (ISHP) REEs at the baseline and objective targets shown in Table 1.

Technical Description, Innovation, and Impact
The AMDREE Central Refinery envisaged in this project would build upon our existing patented process
as illustrated in Figure 3. First, we will use a network-based sourcing strategy for AMD and mine waste
feedstocks.

Figure 3. WVU's A34 pilot plant near Mt. Storm WV produces high grade light and heavy REO.

Our patented preconcentration steps were installed on-site at existing and newly treated AMD waste
streams to produce preconcentrate (PC, ~0.5-2% total rare earth oxide, (TREO)) that were shipped to a
regional concentrator and ultimately to the central refinery for processing. Figure 4 illustrates the
performance of a recently constructed AMDREE facility at Richard, WV. Note that the field unit (500
gpm AMD feed) generates a higher purity HPC than was produced in the laboratory (2.0% vs. 0.6%
TREE). This is also true with our other HPC production unit at A34. This is likely due to longer residence
times and high clarifier performance under full-scale operations. Primary extraction, separation, and
reduction to metals of Appalachia generated PC were performed at the central refining facility along

with MREO concentrates supplied from other regional concentrators and industrial waste reprocessing.
The overall system processed the equivalent of 1-3 tpd of MREO concentrate.

The technology in the AMDREE demonstration facility is divided into four major components, described
in detail below:
 (1) AMD preconcentration and Production of MREO Concentrates
 (2) Production of individually separated high purity oxides (ISHP)

Efficient capture of this resource requires not only selective hydroxide precipitation of the REE/CM ions
but physical separation of solid and aqueous phases. The former represents the valuable product while
the latter represents treated water suitable for discharge under the Clean Water Act. Our patented
approach (US Patent No. 10,954,582B), uses two-stage, independent pH control combined with passive
dewatering to produce an enriched rare earth pre-concentrate. In the process, AMD is first brought to
a pH of ~4.5 to selectively precipitate nearly all the iron, most of the aluminum, as well as other
impurities, and in the second step, the AMD is brought to a pH of ~8.0 to selectively capture the REEs,

 11

Co, Mn, and other critical elements. This second precipitate is passively dewatered and prepared for
shipment. Figure 5 shows a schematic description of this process as deployed at the AMDREE facility
near Mt. Storm WV (DE FE 0031834). This facility has demonstrated that the approach can be
successfully deployed at commercial scales, thus the same approach for AMD preconcentration will be
taken with the feedstocks flowing into the AMDREE pilot facility.

Figure 5. WVU's patented process (US105458282B2) simultaneously treats polluting acid mine drainage while recovering REE/CM
as hydraulic preconcentrate (HPC). The process is scalable to large or small AMD discharges.

Montana Resources’ Berkeley Pit copper mine in Butte, MT. The Berkeley Pit complex alone could
generate about 45 tpy of MREO. Its composition is similar to coal-based AMD (Figure 6) and will be
amenable to a common processing scheme. We also evaluated AMD from Rio Tinto’s Kennecott copper
mine in Utah and from the Oceana’s Haile Gold’s mine in South Carolina with similar, favorable results.

Previous projects have demonstrated that AMD treatment systems can be modified with our patented
approach to achieve necessary preconcentration steps and that this process allows for flexibility in
impurity profiles without significant impact on the downstream processing steps. A hub-and-spoke
model as articulated by DOE in the FOA and envisioned by our research team is privileged, based on an
economic optimization between transportation and local pre-concentration. Up to six pre-concentration
hubs as depicted in Figure 7 are currently envisioned in the supply chain. Each AMD preconcentration
hub will include a MREO Regional Concentrator (RC) that utilizes our patent pending approach (PCT
Patent Application WO2023220072A1) to efficiently produce LREO and HREO concentrates from a
pregnant leach solution (PLS). It should be noted that the actual product of the RC process will usually
be a dried oxalate solid, but the process can also produce oxide products if the driers are set at a
calcination temperature. For simplification, these oxalate products will be referred to as oxide
equivalents. The simplified flow diagram of the regional concentrator process is presented as Figure 8.

The RC process is tailored to the specific profile of local feedstock and AMD pre-concentrates to
accommodate variation in impurity profiles. It was also demonstrated that this technology could be
implemented in the reprocessing of deleterious industrial waste to selectively recover light rare earth
oxides (LREO) and heavy rare earth oxides (HREO) concentrates of similar distributions as the AMD
derived LREO and HREO.

The RC process as applied to AMD pre-concentrates also produces two critical mineral products: (1) a
sulfide concentrate composed of zinc, cobalt and nickel, and (2) a manganese oxide product.

Separation of the REO Concentrates. In addition to our network-based feedstock sourcing strategy,
other keys to the technical approach include a modular central separation and refining facility with
readily configurable solvent extraction (SX) modules for production of target individually separated high
purity REO products. The innovative design and model-based operational optimization approach not
only reduces the capital costs to a fraction of that of conventional systems, but they also provide the
adaptability and process robustness needed to accommodate feedstock variability. Our life cycle
assessment has shown that the embodied CO2 emissions of our final REE products are at least 50% less
than those of conventionally produced products. Figure 9 shows a simplified block flow diagram of the
separation process.

Figure 9. Simplified diagram showing the integrated refining process.

In the FEED study, the light rare earth oxalate concentrate is initially calcined at elevated temperatures
to convert all rare earth oxalates to rare earth oxides and to effectuate a phase transition on the cerium
(III) oxide to cerium (IV) oxide. The resulting LREO solids are subjected to a counter-current hydrochloric
acid leach process where a high pH (> 3.0) pregnant leach solution (PLS) composed of light rare earth
chlorides (LREE PLS) is produced while leaving cerium (IV) as a residue. This process configuration uses
feed material to reduce acidity and thus eliminate the need for a neutralization step. However, since the
FEED study was completed, it was determined that the phase transition is incomplete and this unit
should be removed from the design.

The leach unit should be operated to leach all LREOs. The LREE PLS is then processed in 2 batteries of
mixer settlers. The first one uses Cyanex 572 in a typical extraction (EXT), Scrub (SCB), Strip (STR)
configuration removes any MREE and HREE from the LREE PLS and sends them to the yttrium circuit. The
second battery uses 2-ethylhexyl phosphoric acid mono-2-ethylhexyl ester (EHEHPA) to separate the
NdPr from the LREE PLS. In lieu of a conventional stripping circuit, a prestripitation process identical to
the one used in the LREOx precipitation process is used to recover a NdPr oxalate solid, which is calcined
to an oxide product.

The heavy rare earth oxalate concentrate is first reacted with a sodium carbonate solution in a
metathesis reaction train to produce a heavy rare earth carbonate solids and a sodium oxalate solution
which will be used to precipitate rare earths in the separation process. The heavy rare earth carbonates

 15

are then leached with an acidic solution comprising the MREE/HREE strip solution from area 100, the
scrub solution from the Yttrium recovery circuit and fresh hydrochloric acid to generate the yttrium-rich
PLS (Y PLS). The Y PLS is then processed in two identical batteries of mixer settlers. The first one uses
sec-octyl phenoxyacetic acid (CA-12) in a typical extraction (EXT), Scrub (SCB), Strip (STR) configuration
to extract all MREE and HREE with an atomic weight lower than holmium, with the exception of yttrium
from the Y PLS and precipitates them as a MREE/HREE oxalate solid, enriched in terbium and
dysprosium while being fully depleted of yttrium. The raffinate from the Y recovery circuit is processed
in the second battery using a synergistic mixture of CA-12 and EHEHPA to separate all REE from the
yttrium raffinate. The HREE are precipitated as oxalates and calcined to produce a HREO product
enriched in very heavy elements. The yttrium is precipitated as an oxalate from the raffinate solution
and calcined to produce a yttrium oxide product.

While the NdPr and yttrium circuits utilize conventional SX circuitry, the advanced separation process
(ASP) has been uniquely designed to accommodate full elemental separation of medium to heavy REEs
using a single SX battery operated in semi-batch campaigns.

The ASP is designed to produce four sequential products within a feed group (i.e. a light product, a
medium-light product, a medium-heavy product and a heavy product – LE, ME1, ME2, and HE,
respectively), and it can be operated in semi-batch campaigns with the SEG/Tb/Dy/Ho+ configuration
being operated as the base case scenario.

The unit is designed such that no more than 3 different campaign configurations are operated per year
to minimize the startup effort associated with a configuration change. The process will be described
generally for the four products, LE/ME1/ME2/HE, configuration, as it is the most economically
profitable, but the same unit operations can be operated using other configurations. For example,
adjacent elements can be grouped into mixed products to allow the plant to respond to market
demand.

The ASP is further designed to accommodate upgraded separation technologies currently under
development such as the use of ionic liquids in lieu of organo-phosphorus extractants. The use of ionic
liquids is favorable due to lower volatility, lower environmental footprint, and to reduce reliance on
foreign chemical vendors which typically sell conventional extractants. Our team recently demonstrated
the feasibility of replacing several of the conventional extractants with ionic liquids, with one ionic liquid
boasting 13-27% greater performance compared to traditional extractants and better separation factors
for the HREE.

Reduction to Metal of the Individual REO. The baseline project includes a Reduction to Metal unit
based on the AMES process (Larochelle) an individually separated rare earth oxide is first reacted with
ammonium bifluoride to produce a rare earth fluoride in a rotary furnace, and the rare earth fluoride is
reduced to rare earth metal using calcium metal in a vacuum induction furnace. The rare earth metal is
then remelted with calcium chloride to increase purity and cast as ingot. This process has been
commercially and successfully used to produce commercial volumes of rare earth metals, especially for
rarer and more expensive heavy rare earths.

 Process Modeling and Digital Twin. Advanced process modeling is an enabling technology that is
instrumental in facilitating the proposed plant design and operation. While the proposed system relies
heavily on commercial technology components to minimize risk and time to production, it includes
significant innovations such as a modular plant design concept and distributed feedstock sourcing – both

 16

of which are new paradigms for the REE industry. To facilitate process robustness, the design team will
use METSIM, integrated with custom modules for SX simulation (Stickland, Noble and Larochelle) This
simulation tool will allow for the same circuit to be converted to various feed compositions and will
allow for significant variance in feed material compositions to be supplied to the separation plant. In
addition, the advanced separation process will be designed in modules that will be flexible in their
interconnections, allowing for additional operational flexibility.

This modularization also allows for a small plant footprint, using the same equipment for processing
various materials in stages. It is important to highlight that the reconfigurability of the proposed concept
is not inherent to rare earth solvent extraction circuits. It is derived from the simulation tool allowing for
the rapid optimization of circuits across mineral sectors. The advanced modeling capabilities will also be
used to create a digital twin for the process operation, allowing employees to evaluate the impact of any
modification to the process operating parameters prior to their implementation and for the
simultaneous maximization of on-spec production with reagent and energy minimization.

Finally, the advanced modeling capabilities will allow for a fast start-up and ramp-up, with the pre-
seeding of the circuit with synthetic solutions, organics and recycle streams with near equilibrium
composition. It is expected that the attainment of equilibrium conditions time could be more than
halved using this technology. Our proposed demonstration facility and accompanying waste-sourced
supply chain concept directly coincide with several agency, programmatic, and FOA-specific goals. Most
apparently, the facility, once fully operational, will meet the statutory requirements of the Bipartisan
Infrastructure Law (Section 40205), including the establishment of a rare earth demonstration facility led
by an academic partner that provides net environmental impacts, utilizes acid mine drainage and mine
waste feedstocks, and separates REEs and refines RE metals at a single site.

More broadly, the project will provide a pathway to onshore a significant fraction of domestic REE
consumption. While the facility will be designed and constructed at the demonstration scale, production
volumes of specific elements (notably including Dy and Tb) are anticipated to be significant fractions of
global production (5.4% to 7.3% for Tb and Dy, respectively). Moreover, at a maximum production
capacity of 3 tpd, the overall annual MREO production is anticipated to be commensurate with
Department of Defense needs.

The facility and operational concept will directly and indirectly support broader community and social
goals prescribed by DOE. Notably, the incentivization of waste cleanup that proceeds from the upstream
sourcing strategy will support local communities as well as the Biden Administration’s Justice40
Initiative, as described in our Community Benefits Plan. The design, construction, and operation of the
demonstration facility will provide opportunities for applied workforce development and advanced
technical training. By partnering with major research universities as well as local trade schools, the team
will support efforts to build the knowledge and skill base needed to support a sustainable domestic REE
production industry.

Technology Components

The project strategy consists of three major components: Upstream AMD treatment and preconcentrate
production which would ship to Midstream Regional Concentrators that will produce MREO for
shipment to a single Central Refinery. The latter will undertake elemental separation and reduction as
needed to produce marketable products. The technology supporting the upstream and midstream
operations has been demonstrated in continuous operations. The 800 gpm AMDREE facility,
incorporating AMD treatment with REE/CM recovery (DE FE 0031834) is now operating near Mt. Storm
WV (Figure 2). It is producing HPC within our target grade and solids content criteria 2% TREE, 2.4%
solids. The onsite, MREO facility is a downscaled version of a midstream, regional concentrator and has
thus far produced REO grades between 90 and 95%, which already exceeds the FOA requirements for
intermediate mixed REO products.

General Discussion

Our AMDREE technology was originally designed around the recovery of REE from coal AMD. Both the
upstream preconcentration and downstream refining unit operations have been extensively
investigated at both the bench and at pilot scales, and these studies have been augmented by advanced
process modeling efforts. More recent studies with hard rock AMD have indicated that this strategy not
only recovers high grade REE but also separates hard rock’s abundant transition metals as a separate
process stream. Other preliminary investigations have also indicated the applicability of this approach
to bauxite tailings.

The AMDREE technology has been successfully reduced to practice and is currently deployed at an 800
gpm operating facility. As described above, the process uses known and well-understood chemical
reactions to remove iron and aluminum while producing an REE-CM preconcentrate nominally assaying
1% REE. Extensive laboratory, pilot, and commercial-scale testing has shown that the process is
extremely robust to variation in the raw AMD concentration and quality and is readily scalable and
deployable.

Owing to the high overall REE grade and the elevated content of Tb, Dy, Pr, Nd, and Y the
preconcentrate produced in our staged AMD precipitation process has been shown to have a contained
value superior to that of conventional REE ore resources. In this context, contained value represents the
total recoverable value of one ton of feedstock before losses and costs. A study of more than 40
conventional REE deposits (Noble, 2023) showed that the contained value of mined ores typically ranges

 38

from $100 to $350/ton of ore, while our data shows that the total contained value of preconcentrate
approaches $2,000/ton.

Under DE-FE0031834, the team, in conjunction with WVDEP built and operates two full scale AMDREE
plants (A34 and Richard Mine). A34 includes an onsite leaching and solvent extraction unit. In
production since 2022, it routinely produces MREO products exceeding 90% purity. The proposed
separation and reﬁning plant is based on conventional technologies using proven chemistry. Given this
understanding as well as the proven feasibility of the upstream processes, the technical risk associated
with the project lies primarily in the integration and optimization of the process. Innovations are
primarily focused on the recovery manganese and other CMs and are not central to the production of
REEs. To ensure technical feasibility, we employed simulation-based design software to optimize
processes (METSIM). Standard pilot tests show that the model has been successfully validated against
experimental data (Larochelle and Kasaini, Predictive modeling of rare earth element separation by
solvent extraction using metsim.), proving the method’s utility in expediting the design process.

Leaching

Methods and Materials

Leaching tests were conducted to evaluate how the extent of dewatering impacts HPC leaching
eﬃciency. For each experiment, the HPC and deionized water were added to a beaker to achieve a 2%
solids mixture. The mixture was agitated at 100 RPM with a standing paddle mixer. The pH was adjusted
every 15 minutes with 6 M HCl to maintain the necessary pH. After the allotted 2 hours, the mixture was
vacuum ﬁltered using a Buchner ﬁlter. The volume of the ﬁltrate and the mass of solids after drying was
recorded. The solids were digested in a diluted nitric acid solution using a microwave digester to dissolve
the solids for ICP-MS analysis. The digestion was set for a ramp time of 10 minutes with a hold at 180°C
for 15 minutes. The ﬁltrate and digested solids solutions were analyzed using ICP-MS. Recovery is
determined by the mass of an element in the leachate from the combined masses of the element in the
leachate and in the residual solids. Each experimental test was conducted in triplicate. Elemental
recovery is deﬁned as the mass of the element in the leachate divided by the total mass of the element
in the leachate and residual solids.

The material tested in these experiments was HPC generated from AMD processing plant A34 in Mt.
Storm, WV. Fischer Chemical Certiﬁed ACS Plus hydrochloric acid was selected for the leaching processes
to determine recovery. The downstream processes are conducted in a chloride matrix, thus necessitating
HCl. The raw HPC generated from A34 is approximately 2% solids, as the solids content is low, the HPC
was mixed vigorously for at least four hours to suspend the solids before extracting a sample for
experimentation. Acid-leaching experiments were conducted at diﬀering stages of HPC dewatering. For
the ﬁltered HPC tests, the raw HPC was mixed vigorously as stated before, and then pumped through a
ﬁlter press to achieve a sludge of approximately 16% solids as shown in Figure 16. HPC was also
dewatered at the A34 plant, utilizing passive dewatering to yield 22% to 62% solids for the HPC.

Solvent Extraction

Methods and Materials

Solvent extraction equilibrium testing was carried out to scope various extractants for the process. The
equilibrium constants can be used to model how the extraction will perform in a solvent extraction
system. The models can then used to ﬁnd the best separation scheme to meet the desired outcomes for
the circuit. Extraction percent, distribution ratio, and separation factors are also calculated to compare
extractants directly.

Each extractant was tested using a lab prepared pregnant leach solution (PLS). The extractant and PLS
were mixed in a beaker and then separated in a separatory funnel where the organic and PLS phases
separate. Four diﬀerent organic to aqueous ratios were used and each set of 4 was completed in
triplicate for data redundancy. Total PLS concentrations were also altered with a 1.50 g/L, 15.0 g/L, and
150 g/L tested. Both the aqueous and organic phases were analyzed. A mass balance was used to ensure
the collected data was suﬃcient for the calculation. A reconciliation process was performed on the ﬁnal
data sets before the equilibrium calculation was performed.

Fundamentally, solvent extraction can be described as a reversible chemical reaction involving the
exchange of cations between phases of varying miscibility. Generally:

where Ln refers to any general REE in and aqueous phase and RH refers to an extractant in an organic

Background

A metathesis process was selected and tested to improve process economics and material intensity for
rare earth element (REE) recovery from acid mine drainage (AMD). In previous steps of the process,

 48

solvent extraction and precipitation was performed to separate and recover diﬀerent REEs and other
critical material species. Once LREO (light rare earth species) and HREO (heavy rare earth species) are
recovered in the modular concentrator, they then move on to further reﬁning. After initial roasting to
convert LREO oxalates to oxides, CeO2 and NdPr metal are removed. The remaining LREO oxides are then
fed in with HREO oxalates for the metathesis process. This process involves the exchange of the oxalate
anion with a carbonate anion, to increase leachability in acid and ease processing. Normally, roasting to
convert oxalates to oxides, followed by acid leaching is performed, which is energy and resource
intensive. Metathesis oﬀers an elegant workaround to avoid these process steps while still maintaining
process separation goals.

oxalate species roasting and leaching. REE processing requires some form of leaching to dissolve solid
species into solution. Standard techniques to separate chemically similar REEs such as solvent extraction,
ion exchange, and selective precipitation all require liquid processing. Easing the conversion of insoluble
REE species to liquid soluble ones through metathesis rather than roasting would be an improvement to
existing processing methodology. The metathesis reaction creates a more easily leachable version of
REEs in solution, making extreme roasting and leaching unnecessary. A follow-on eﬀect to the realized
process improvements is a decrease in environmental impacts from lower acid use and minimizing waste
products through reagent recirculation.

Experimental Parameters and Data

To assess the viability of the metathesis conversion synthetic oxalates of lanthanum or yttrium were
prepared by leaching rare earth elements into deionized water with hydrochloric acid (HCl) followed by
precipitation with oxalic acid to form rare earth oxalate. Deionized water was prepared to the
appropriate temperature (ambient-120°C) and rare earth oxalate and sodium carbonate was added to
the mixture at varying percent solids (5-40%) at various stoichiometric excess of sodium carbonate
(100%-200%) and mixed for an allotted time (7.5-120 minutes). The slurry was then either immediately
vacuum ﬁltered or allowed to cool and then vacuum ﬁltered. Solids were then rinsed with deionized
water at the appropriate temperature (ambient-120°C). The precipitated carbonates were then leached
at pH 2.5 with HCl in deionized water. Samples were taken at each step for mass balancing and
quantitative determination by ICP-MS.

Parameter Eﬀects

 49

Mixing time was tested between 7.5 to 120 minutes. It was found that contingent on successful chemical
reactions, mixing time was irrelevant and did not aﬀect results or precent recovery. While metathesis
was successful at 7.5 minute mixing time, 30 minute mixing time was reported as the most consistent
and reliable mixing time. Temperature of water during the process was tested at temperatures between
ambient (21°C) and 120°C. It was found that temperature does play a signiﬁcant role in test success. 90°C
was reported as the most optimal temperature for the metathesis reaction. Temperatures below 80°C
and above 100°C were unsuccessful. The solid/liquid ratio was also varied to determine the most reliable
conversion environment. After multiple tests from 5-40% solids ratio, 10% was found to be the most
eﬀective for successful dissolution and formation of carbonate. The most important factor was the
stoichiometric ratio of rare earth oxalate to sodium carbonate in solution. Without excess sodium
carbonate, the driving force for the metathesis reaction was insuﬃcient to convert the rare earth
oxalates present in solution. Stoichiometric excesses from 100%-200% were tested with 200% showing
the best results for metathesis product formation. Overall the ideal reaction was found to be 30 minutes
of rare earth oxalate mixing at 80 °C with a 200% stoichiometric excess of sodium carbonate with a 10%
solid to liquid mixing ratio.

While the described process was successful in converting rare earth oxalates to carbonates, conﬁrmed by
their leachability in acid, the economics of conversion did not prove suitable for the current process
design. Future work to increase viability of this process should include oxalate recycle in the process ﬂow
to improve economics and feedstock assessment for metathesis conversion of diﬀerent rare earth
species.

1.0 INTRODUCTION

 1.1 Purpose of the Project

The primary objective of the project is to design, construct, and operate a first-of-a-kind

demonstration facility capable of producing rare earth elements (REEs) and select critical

minerals (CMs) from domestic feedstocks. The facility proposed herein will be designed to

produce 1 to 3 metric tons per day of REE product and will provide net environmental benefits

using premium feedstocks derived primarily from acid mine drainage as well as other

deleterious mining wastes. Once operational, the facility will separate mixed rare earth oxides

into individual and binary REE components prior to refining those elements into high purity REE

metals. As envisaged, the facility will separate, refine, and purify REE/CM products at a single

site to be in the Central or Northern Appalachian coal mining region. The REE/CM products

from the facility will focus on magnet and battery critical materials (i.e., Nd, Pr, Dy, Tb, Co, Zn,

Mn, Ni) to support domestic manufacturing and clean energy technologies. The overall project

as well as the demonstration facility will also provide community benefits, both locally, and

nationally, through education and training opportunities, economic development, and

environmental cleanup. These benefits will particularly benefit disadvantaged and

underrepresented groups in mine-impacted communities.

Coincident with the requirements of the FOA, the starting Technology readiness level of the

proposed technology is TRL 6, and during Phase 1, the anticipated TRL is anticipated to remain

at TRL 6. The efforts in Phase 1 are to provide the engineering, optimization, and permitting

activities needed to achieve TRL 8 in the subsequent project phase.

AMD Preconcentration. Our patented approach (US Patent No. 10,954,582B) uses two-stage,

independent pH control combined with passive dewatering to produce an enriched rare earth

pre-concentrate nominally assaying 2% to 5% TREE. In the process, AMD is first brought to a pH

of ~4.5 to selectively precipitate all the iron, most of the aluminum, as well as other impurities,

and in the second step, the AMD is brought to a pH of ~8.0 to selectively capture the REEs, Co,

Mn, and other critical elements. This second precipitate is passively dewatered and prepared

for shipment, while the aqueous overflow can be discharged as treated water. Critical

components of this TRA system include the staged precipitation process, the solids removal and

handling system, and the solids dewatering step.

MREO Production. The MREO production process uses acid leaching to selectively extract

critical minerals and minimize the solubilization of silica. The pregnant leach solution (PLS) is

then partially neutralized to remove most of the gangue minerals, and the resulting PLS is

processed in either a single solvent extraction circuit (using D2EHPA + TBP or alternatively

EHEHPA). Prestripitation (direct precipitation of RE-oxalates in the organic phase) and acid

stripping are used conjointly to produce a partially separated light REE, a zinc concentrate and a

HREE product. The partially separated LREE-oxalates are then calcined to produce a mixed light

rare earth oxide concentrates and the oxalate is used as an intermediate product.

REE and CM Separations. The separation processes use a series of solvent extraction and

further oxalate precipitation circuits to separate and purify individual REEs and CM such as

cobalt and nickel. Solvent extraction is conducted in a series of modular batteries with carefully

selected commercial extractants, including D2EHPA, EHEHPA, CA-12, Cyanex 272, and Cyanex

572. While the primary yttrium, and mischmetal recovery circuits utilize conventional SX

 4

circuitry, the advanced separation process unit (ASPU) has been uniquely designed to

accommodate full elemental separation of medium to heavy REEs using a single SX battery

operated in batch campaigns. In addition, the solvent extraction processes are augmented by a

novel ammonium chloride leaching process that selectively removes calcium and zinc and a

metathesis process that converts the REE oxalates to carbonates, allowing for low acid leach

prior to the next separation circuit, avoiding the need for calcination and releach.

REE Metal Reduction. The proposed technology for the reduction has been developed by Hela

Novel Metals (patent pending US20200047256A1) and uses hydrogen to reduce metal oxalates

to metal, yielding a micron size powder. This carboxylate reduction process emulates the

advantages of molten salt electrolysis in its simplicity and continuous nature while having none

of the disadvantages.

3.0 REFERENCE MATERIAL

 3.1 Definition of TRA System

NETL’s interpretation (Section 3.2) of the DOE TRL definitions (Section 3.3) is based on a view of

technology maturation in which “components” are integrated into a “system” that is being

assessed for its technology readiness. To clearly and consistently apply the DOE TRL definitions,

one must first precisely identify what “system” is being assessed, defined herein as the

“Technology Readiness Assessment (TRA) System.” Since most technologies can be viewed as

 10

subsystems within larger systems, multiple choices are available for defining the TRA System.

However, note that the choice of the “level” of the TRA System affects how TRLs are assessed:

 • A TRL 3 is achieved for the specified TRA System when analytical performance
 predictions for each of the TRA System’s critical 1 components have been validated in
 separate experiments (i.e., without integration across components). Accordingly, the
 table in Section 3.2 shows the required scope of TRL 3 as “single component” and the
 required integration of TRL 3 as “none.”
 • A TRL 4 or 5 is achieved for a given TRA System when the targeted performance
 requirements for each of its critical, multi-component subsystems (or the entire TRA
 system) have been validated in a laboratory environment (TRL 4) or relevant
 environment (TRL 5) with integration of some or all components.
 • Achieving TRLs 6 to 9 requires testing of the entire, fully integrated, TRL system.

To further clarify, consider, for example, a fuel cell stack. Its critical components are multiple,

identical fuel cells. In turn, the critical components of each fuel cell are an anode, cathode, and

electrolyte. If one wished to assess the technology readiness of the fuel cell stack, the TRA

System would be defined as an integrated system of multiple fuel cell subsystems, and a TRL 6

could only be achieved by successfully testing an entire stack of integrated fuel cells. However,

if one instead wished to assess the technology readiness of only the fuel cell, the TRA System

would be defined as an integrated system of cathode, anode, and electrolyte components, and

a TRL 6 could be achieved by successfully testing just a single, integrated fuel cell. In both cases,

achievement of TRL 6 could be claimed, but only in the context of the properly specified TRA

System.

1 Executive Summary
1.1 Disclaimer

The current report is a draft issue of the FEED study and should be used as such. The project
team asses that all technical sections with the exception of the infrastructure and building design
have been completed and meet the requirements for an AACE Class 3 estimate with the
disclaimers described below. Some of the FEED study deliverables that support the process
design are still being finalized and thus are not included in this draft issue. No material change to
the process design will be resulting from the completion of those process deliverables.

The project team was not able to complete the required de-risking and optimization test work
prior to the finalization of this process report. The current design uses a combination of literature
and experimentally derived data to support the process design. The process team believes that the
current design is conservative and that stages of mixer-settlers will likely be removed from the
design at the detailed engineering stage following the integration of the test work data.
Depending on data availability, it is possible that the process design will be updated to reflect the
de-risking and optimization test work prior to issuing the final report. However, it is our opinion
that insufficient solvent extraction test work has been accomplished and that the current process
design inherently contains technical risks that shall be addressed prior to detailed engineering.

Notwithstanding the foregoing, the project team believes that the updated capital and operating
cost estimates resulting from the final FEED Study report will not materially differ from the
estimates presented herein, and as such the conclusion and recommendations presented in this
report will be maintained.

1.2 Introduction

As of the proposal date, WVU has not received the firm financial commitments needed to fully
fund the project. The advisory committee has engaged several private investment firms but has
had difficult securing commitments due to the depressed REE pricing, Chinese control of the
REE market, and significant degree of overall project risk associated with the first-of-a-kind pre-
commercial REE demonstration plant.

In addition, the timing of project has proved challenging, and detailed discussion on financing
prior to the completion of the Class 3 FEED study are premature. In order to properly engage
 9

potential financing partners, we needed to first have our costs established at a reasonable level of
accuracy, which we now have with the completion of the FEED study. However, because we
have only recently ascertained these costs, we have not yet been able to solicit funding from
potential partners.

Every REE facility needs to know how their costs compare to those of China with the
understanding that China subsidizes their REE production at every stage. These Chinese
subsidies take the form of tax breaks, rebates, grants, discounted energy costs, discounted
chemical costs, lower cost or requirements of ESG standards, discounted labor, and many others.
Because of these subsidies, any facility outside of China is at a market disadvantage. China has
methodically and strategically built their REE industry over the last four decades in order to gain
market share and leverage the REE industry to their advantage.

It will take many years to re-establish a rare earth supply chain outside of China. During this
time, the US Government may need to supply subsidies in many forms—tax breaks, purchase
agreements, grants, loans, etc.—in order to support rebuilding the supply chain here. REEs
supply critical materials across our economy, and should be supported not just for military and
DOD purposes, but also to ensure that our commercial sector has access to cutting edge materials
for future applications.

Building a demonstration facility such as the one we propose may provide value far beyond the
REE materials produced. The first priority will be to produce essential baseline material for the
defense sector, key industry players (such as semiconductors), and the research & development
efforts of commercial players, national labs, and academic institutions. In addition to this, we
will be able to provide a training environment for much needed future REE expertise and a
proving ground for potential technologies. As the industry grows in the US, commercial players
will need experts that are ready to immediately enter the market with an appropriate skillset. The
Chinese have over 30 State Key labs that train REE workers—this is part of the reason that they
have such an advantage. We need workers that are ready to engage immediately throughout the
supply chain.

WVU’s commitment to this project and the underlying strategic mission is unquestioned. WVU
understands the importance of securing a domestic supply of rare earth elements and other
critical minerals for our county's national and economic security. We will continue to engage
 10

with our advisory committee and potential investors relative to how best to move forward to
deliver on this important mission. In addition, we recommend that DOE, DOD and WVU
establish a national center focused on developing REE/CM policy, capabilities, and supply chain.
WVU is eager to engage in a discussion with the DOE on next steps to accomplish these
objectives.

1.5 Sensitivity Analysis

To further analyze the conditions that would lead to economic viability, the team analyzed the
two integrated project scenarios assuming two market/government controls: subsidized capital
costs (expressed as a percent of the total CAPEX) and price support (expressed as a percent of
the long-term price scenario). Figure 1 shows this sensitivity analysis for the base case plant
scenario.

Several key conclusions can be taken from this analysis. First, the data show that neither
scenario is economically viable without significant government/market intervention. Given that
the current project envisions a 50% cost share, the 50% CAPEX subsidized line reaches a
breakeven NPV between 120% and 125% price support. This suggests that a modest price
support structure may yield a sustainable venture, and higher levels of support can yield
attractive returns. Lastly, this data shows that in both cases, the 100% CAPEX supported line
yield a slightly positive NPV, even at no price support beyond the long term anticipated price.

sustainable solution for meeting national security needs.

2 Introduction

Since 2016, the project team led by WVU and supported by Virginia Tech (VT), L3 Process
Development and numerous private sector partners has pioneered a method to extract rare earth
elements (REEs) and critical minerals (CMs) from AMD. Known as AMDREE (US Patent
10,954,582 B2), the technology resulting from this multifaceted research, development and
deployment effort has been supported by almost $30 million in federal funds and $8 million in
non-Federal cost share. Efforts in this program have ranged from our initial exploratory and
bench-scale studies (DE-FE0026927) to regional sampling and resource estimation (DE-
FE0026444) to integrated technology development (DE-FE0031524). Recently completed and
current efforts in the program include pilot-scale production of mixed rare earth oxides (DE-
FE0031834), advanced process development for separation and refining (DE-FE003120),
regional economic development, infrastructure, and technology assessment (DE-FE0032054),
the pre-feasibility study for a vertically integrated 1-3 ton per day (tpd) separation and refining
facility (Contract Number: 89243320CFE000059), and a Class 3 FEED study (submitted as part
of this report). Figure 2 illustrates the progression of our technology development process to the
current TRL 6.

REE/CM enriched feedstocks based on AMD or other deleterious mineral processing wastes
have several community and environmental advantages. Most come from already permitted mine
sites requiring no further mining which leads to minimal site commissioning delays. Our
technique does not produce radioactive byproducts like most conventional rare earth mines and
treatment will result in cleaner lands and waterways. Finally, low-pH AMD has similar
properties and REE distribution regardless of its location garnering a more predictable and
economical process. AMD has 45%+ composition of the desirable heavy rare earth (HREE)
elements most frequently utilized in green energy and defense technologies, a number far
surpassing that of the 12% HREE present in MP Minerals Mine (CA, USA) or Bayan Obo
(China). This project will not only create valuable, new jobs for coal workers displaced by the
energy transition but will build upon previously established relationships for strong community
engagement, outreach, and workforce development. Altogether, these prior efforts have
culminated in both a commercial-ready technology package suitable for deployment as well as
an innovation ecosystem and research infrastructure that will support further refinement,
optimization and is poised to make a disruptive impact on critical materials supply chains in the
future. Our approach has been commercially focused since our initial exploratory studies and has
been well aligned with the USDOE and the National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL)
goals to secure domestic supply chains for REEs from diverse sources. Techno-economic
assessment, contained value analysis, market readiness analysis, and process scalability have
been longstanding tools and constraints in our technology development efforts. Notably, our
initial decision to focus efforts on AMD-based feedstocks was based on market factors, such as
the high fraction of magnet-critical REEs (i.e. Nd, Pr, Tb, Dy), high contained value, and
amenability to conventional (and thus scalable) processing routes. Thus far, we have partnered
with the West Virginia Department of Environmental Protection (WVDEP) to construct two
AMDREE pilot plant near Mt. Storm, and Morgantown, WV (Figure 3). These facilities can treat
up to 1500 gallons of AMD per minute from adjacent coal properties while producing about 2
tons per year (tpy) of REE plus other CMs in the form of mixed oxides.

 16

 Figure 3: The A34 AMDREE facility near Mt. Storm WV (l) and the new Richard AMDREE plant (r) near

The facilities produce compliant discharge water and hydraulic preconcentrate (HPC). HPC is
then treated to yield pregnant leach solution (PLS) and then mixed rare earth oxides (MREO) at
the A34 site. Our prior success in technology development and scaleup as well as the positive
technical and economic indicators identified in our AACE Class 3 cost estimate and front-end
engineering design (FEED) study indicate a sound strategic focus. This effort will culminate in
the design, construction, and operation of a 1-3 tpd demonstration scale Central Refinery for the
separation and refining REE/CM. The team has selected a candidate site for this plant and has
also initiated engagement with a broad range of stakeholders including but not limited to:
upstream feedstock suppliers, downstream off takers, state regulatory bodies, state and local
legislative bodies, environmental and community-based economic development groups, AMD-
treatment operators, trade unions, technology providers, OEMs, and select academic institutions.

3.1 Scope of Facilities

3.1.1 West Virginia Regional Concentrator Plant

The West Virginia Regional Concentrator Plant (WVRCP) will aggregate Hydraulic Pre-
Concentrate material from Appalachia and produce the following rare earth intermediates and
critical materials products:

Rare Earth Intermediates

 • Mixed Light Rare Earth Oxalates (LREOx)
 • Mixed Heavy Rare Earth Oxalates (HREOx)

Critical Materials Products

 • • Mixed Sulfide Material (Zn, Ni, Co)
 • • Manganese Concentrate

The WVRCP will be integrated into the Central Processing Refinery Site and will share all
utilities and infrastructure.

3.1.2 Central Processing Refinery

The Central Processing Refinery (CPR) will aggregate LREOx and HREOx intermediate
products from the following sources and separate its marketable rare earth constituent as
individual products.

Supply of LREOx and HREOx:

 • West Virginia Regional Concentrator Plant
 • HsB Regional Concentrator (Montana Resources and Kennecott Utah Copper)

 • ElementUS Red Mud reprocessing facility at Gramercy, LA
 • Chemours Rare Earth Extraction Unit
 • Northump Grunman Spent Crystals

The CPR facility will be composed of all infrastructure and utilities required to operate as an
industrial site. Specific infrastructure include:

 • Administrative Facilities
 • Maintenance Facilities
 • Laboratory Facilities
 • Employee Change Room Facilities
 • Fire Protection
 • Site Security Facilities
 • Parking

The operating factor is defined as:


Where Up-time is the number of operating hours per year is initially set at 2,920. As the feed
stock supply increases, the CPR facility up-time will be increased to 8,760 hours per year. Up-
time incorporates both planned and unplanned maintenance and hours lost producing off-spec
product. Capacity utilization is the average annual percentage of design capacity achieved when
operating. Capacity utilization accounts for lower than nameplate production during ramp-up and
ramp-down around shutdowns and limitations on one area caused by dependency on adjacent
areas.

Because the solvent extraction process design for the CPR facility is designed to be safely
stopped whenever an operating controller is non-responsive, it is expected that its capacity
utilization will be above 90%. The capacity utilization was selected at 90.4%, or 330 days of full
operational capacity per calendar year.

The Operating Factor is equivalent to the actual annual production of saleable product divided by
theoretical annual production of the plant operating at design rate for 2,920 h (8760 h).

3.3.4 Buffer Capacities

Buffer capacities were established for raw materials that are to be imported or exported to the
site and also between process areas. Buffer capacities for raw materials and storage capacity for
by-products for export are nominally set at total capacity of 14 operational days.

It is assumed that buffers will operate never lower than 50% capacity. This provides an available
buffer capacity of at least 7 operational days. Buffer capacities between process areas cannot be
sized for similar durations due to the quantities involved. They are sized to accommodate a
single shift (8 h) interruption of an upstream or downstream process.

Hours lost to the production of off-spec products are accounted for within the Operating Factor.

4.1 AMD Treatment

4.1.1 Background

Under conventional AMD treatment, raw AMD is continually produced and treated at mine
discharge points. Treatment consists of acid neutralization followed by oxidation to precipitate
regulated metals from solution. The precipitates are separated from clean water, which can then
be discharged. While these precipitates are enriched in REE/CM, we have found that significant
gains can be made by first precipitating the bulk of gangue metals while REEs are still in
solution then precipitating REEs at a higher pH to yield a high-grade pre-concentrate. The result
is an AMD treatment strategy that meets the statutory CWA discharge limits while optimizing
recovery and purity of an REE pre-concentrate. The study team demonstrated under DE FE
0031524 that two stage AMD treatment first removes the bulk of gangue metals then the second
produces an enriched pre-concentrate of 0.5 to 5% MREO. This and supporting technology are
the subject of U.S. Patent No. 10,954,582, awarded to members of the study team in March
2021. A 500 gpm demonstration plant using this technology is in operation. Prior testing of this
process shows that the technology can effectively concentrate REE/CMs from raw AMD, with
overall enrichment factors ranging from 13,000x to 15,000x. More importantly, the process has
proven to be extremely robust, as testing on different AMD feedstocks representing a variety of
geochemical settings has consistently produced similar results with respect to final REE purity.
When properly optimized, the system has consistently achieved REE/CM recovery >96% and
generated products with REE/CM grade close to 2wt%.

4.1.2 AMD Treatment Unit

The AMD Treatment Unit is used to treat AMD streams and produce a critical material pre-
concentrate product. It will consist of many units located at various AMD streams sites. While
the specific of each unit has to be tailored to its specific site, the general process flowsheet is the
same.

In this process, raw AMD is first neutralized to a pH of 4.8 with Calcium Oxide, precipitating
most base metals. The precipitate is dewatered in a thickener or in a settling pond and the
 26

resulting partially treated water is subsequently neutralized to a pH of 8. This second
neutralization step induces the precipitation of all REE, zinc, nickel and cobalt with a significant
portion of manganese. This pre-concentrate material is then dewatered in a clarifier or in a
settling pond. The decant water will meet regulatory discharge limits and would be discharged
to a receiving stream. The pre-concentrate material is then further dewatered in geo tubes and the
dewatered material is periodically collected and transported to the central refinery for the
extraction, separation and refining of its constituent critical materials.

4.2 Area 000 – West Virginia Regional Concentrator

The West Virginia Regional Concentrator (WVRC) is the first area of the REE Demonstration
Facility. It is used to extract REE and CM from AMD-Derived pre-concentrate material
aggregated from Appalachia. Other similar regional concentrators are expected to join the supply
chain such as the Berkely Pit Regional Concentrator and the Utah Regional Concentrator. The
block flow diagram of Area 000 is presented Figure 4 and is available as Appendix A – Block
Flow Diagrams.

 Figure 4: Block flow diagram of Area 000, the regional concentrator.
 27

The aggregated pre-concentrate is dropped into a bin, diluted with PLS and fed to parallel series
of four co-current agitated leach vessels where it is contacted with 93% sulfuric acid to maintain
a leach pH of 4. After leaching, the solid residuals are filtered, washed and disposed off on the
site solids tailings area while the PLS is sent to the neutralization circuit. In the neutralization
circuit, sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide are added to maintain a pH of 4.25 and a sulfide
concentration slightly below the solubility level of H2S gas. The sulfide reacts with zinc, nickel,
and cobalt and the Zn/Ni/Co are precipitated as a critical material sulfide concentrate. The
increased pH from the sodium hydroxide precipitates the minor aluminum impurity along with
some the of dissolved silica. The Zn/Ni/Co sulfide concentrate is then dried, packaged and sold
while the neutralized PLS is sent to the extraction circuit. The extraction circuit uses Cyanex 801
diluted in kerosene in a series of mixer-settlers to selectively extract REEs from the neutralized
PLS while leaving manganese in the aqueous phase. The REE-loaded organic goes through a
prestripping circuit where a combination of sodium oxalate and oxalic acid is used to recover the
LREE as oxalates (LREOx) directly from the loaded organic. The LREOx product is then dried,
packaged and store before being sent the the NdPr Recovery Area (Area 100). The
MREE/HREEY rich organic is then stripped in a system of mixer-settlers using concentrated
hydrochloric acid and the barren organic is recycled back to the extraction circuit. The strip
liquor is reacted with oxalic acid to precipitate the REE as oxalates. The resulting slurry is
dewatered and dried to create a HREE/Y oxalate product to be stored prior to being processed in
the Yttrium Recovery Unit (Area 200).

The raffinate from the solvent extraction process is rich in Mn. A sodium hypochlorite oxidation
reaction is performed to change the Mn2+ to Mn4+ which is then easily precipitated out with an
increased pH. The resulting slurry is filtered using candle filters with the solids being further
dried with a rotary dryer and packaged for storage or market.

4.3 Area 100 – NdPr Recovery

The NdPr Recovery Area is used to recover didymium (Pr, Nd) oxide from the LREOx produced
in the WVRC. The didymium oxide is produced along with a HRE strip solution and a Ce oxide
product. The block flow diagram of Area 100 is presented in Figure 5 and is available as
Appendix A – Block Flow Diagrams.
 28

 Figure 5: Block flow diagram of Area 100, the NdPr recovery unit.
4.3.1 LREO Calcining

The initial step of the NdPr Recovery Area is a high temperature calcination of the LREOx solids
to LREO. In addition to converting the oxalates into oxides, this unit is used to oxidize cerium
(III) to cerium (IV), a refractory compound which presents a low reactivity with hydrochloric
acid.

4.3.2 LREO Leaching

The LREO solids are then leached using concentrated hydrochloric acid in a counter current
circuit. The LREO solids are first mixed with a recycled LREE PLS where the fresh oxides react
and uses up residual hydrochloric acid and are dewatered to generate a PLS with a pH of 3.5-4.0,
suitable for direct extraction without requiring neutralization.

The partially leached solids are reacted with concentrated hydrochloric acid to leach all REE
with minimal ceria. The resulting slurry is then dewatered and the solids are washed, and dried to
generate a ceria product. The acidic PLS is sent to the initial leach stage.

4.3.3 MREE Recovery

LREE PLS from the LREO Leaching circuit is contacted with EHEHPA diluted in kerosene in a
series of mixer-settlers. In this circuit, all of the rare earth elements heavier than neodymium are
extracted in the organic phase leaving the majority of the La, Pr, Nd, and any remaining Ce in

 29

the aqueous phase. To control the pH of the reaction, ammonium hydroxide is added to the
second and fourth mixer settler in the battery.

The raffinate from the primary solvent extraction circuit is sent to the didymium recovery circuit
while the loaded organic continues on to a scrubbing battery. In the scrubbing unit, the loaded
organic is combined with a diluted HCl solution in a mixer-settler bank. The scrubbing process
will remove extracted La, Ce, Pr, and Nd from the loaded organic and recycle it back to the
extraction battery. This process operation is referred to as “reflux”.

Loaded organic from the scrubbing battery continues to a stripping battery where the loaded
organic is contacted with a high concentration hydrochloric acid solution. The strip liquor
containing all REE heavier than samarium with minimal amounts of neodymium is sent to the
leach circuit of Area 200 for further recovery of the REE products.

4.3.4 Didymium Recovery Circuit

The raffinate from the MREE Recovery circuit is contacted with EHEHPA diluted in kerosene in
a series of mixer-settlers. In this circuit, all of the praseodymium and neodymium are extracted
in the organic phase leaving the majority of the lanthanum cerium Ce in the aqueous phase. To
control the pH of the reaction, ammonium hydroxide is added to the second and fourth mixer
settler in the battery.

The raffinate from the extraction circuit is sent to wastewater treatment while the loaded organic
continues on to a scrubbing battery. In the scrubbing unit, the loaded organic is combined with a
diluted HCl solution in a mixer-settler bank. The scrubbing process will remove extracted La and
Ce from the loaded organic and reflux it back to the extraction battery.

The didymium-loaded organic is then reacted with a mixture of sodium oxalate and oxalic acid
solution in a series of agitated tanks to precipitate the didymium as an oxalate. The reactor
discharge is segregated into two phases in a clarifier with the organic phase being recovered in
the overflow and both the aqueous and solids phase being recovered in the underflow. The
underflow solids are dewatered using plate and frame filters. Finally, the didymium product is
calcined in a rotary calciner prior to being packaged for storage. Any remaining water from the
process is sent to the wastewater treatment unit for proper disposal.

4.4 Area 200 - Yttrium Recovery

The Yttrium Recovery Unit is used to recover yttrium oxide from the HREE oxalate solids along
with a light HREO product do be further refined in Area 300 and a heavy HREO product to be
sold as a product.

 Figure 6: Block flow diagram of Area 200, the yttrium recovery unit.
4.4.1 Y Recovery Leaching

HREE oxalates are first reacted with a sodium carbonate solution at elevated temperature (80-
90°C) to generate a heavy rare earth carbonate slurry and a sodium oxalate solution. The solids
are filtered using a plate and frame press before being sent to a leach circuit. The leach circuit
uses a cascade of agitated reactors to leach the REE carbonates with the MREE strip liquor from
Area 100, supplemented with fresh hydrochloric acid. Unreacted solids are dewatered and
reprocessed in the metathesis step.

The filtered PLS is sent on to the yttrium recovery circuit.

 31

4.4.2 Yttrium Recovery Circuit

PLS from the Y Leaching circuit is contacted with sec-Octyl Phenoxy Acetic Acid (CA-12)
diluted in kerosene and modified with tri-butyl phosphate (TBP) in a series of mixer-settlers. In
this circuit, all of the rare earth elements lighter than holmium are extracted in the organic phase
leaving the majority of the Ho, Er and Y in the aqueous phase. To control the pH of the reaction,
ammonium hydroxide is added to the second and fourth mixer settler in the battery.

The raffinate from the Y Recovery solvent extraction circuit is sent to the Y Purification circuit
while the loaded organic continues on to a scrubbing battery. In the scrubbing unit, the loaded
organic is combined with a diluted HCl solution in a mixer-settler bank. The scrubbing process
will remove all extracted Y from the loaded organic along with some heavier elements and reflux
them back to the extraction battery.

Loaded organic from the scrubbing battery continues to a stripping battery where the loaded
organic is contacted with a high concentration hydrochloric acid solution. The strip liquor can be
sent directly to Area 300, or precipitated using a sodium oxalate solution.

If the operation decides to precipitate the strip liquor, it is mixed with a sodium oxalate solution
in cascading mixing reactors to precipitate the HREE as oxalates. Following the precipitation the
slurry is then filtered with a plate and frame press to dewater the HRE oxalates. Residual water
from the filtering is sent to the wastewater treatment plant while the filtered solids continue on in
the process. The solids are then dried in a rotary dryer before they are packaged in supersacks
and sent to storage.

4.4.3 Yttrium Purification Circuit

Raffinate from the Y Recovery circuit is contacted with a synergistic mixture of sec-Octyl
Phenoxy Acetic Acid (CA-12), EHEHPA and TBP diluted in kerosene in a series of mixer-
settlers. In this circuit, all of the rare earth elements other than yttrium are extracted in the
organic phase leaving the majority of the Y in the aqueous phase. To control the pH of the
reaction, ammonium hydroxide is added to the second and fourth mixer settler in the battery.

The raffinate from the Y Purification circuit is sent to Yttrium Oxide Recovery while the loaded
organic continues on to a scrubbing battery. In the scrubbing unit, the loaded organic is
combined with a diluted HCl solution in a mixer-settler bank. The scrubbing process will remove
 32

all extracted Y from the loaded organic along with some heavier elements and reflux them back
to the extraction battery.

Loaded organic from the scrubbing battery continues to a stripping battery where the loaded
organic is contacted with a high concentration hydrochloric acid solution. The strip liquor can be
sent directly to Area 300, or precipitated using a sodium oxalate solution.

If the operation decides to precipitate the strip liquor, it is mixed with a sodium oxalate solution
in cascading mixing reactors to precipitate the HREE as oxalates. Following the precipitation the

4.4.4 Yttrium Oxide Recovery

The yttrium purification raffinate is reacted with a sodium oxalate solution in a cascade of
agitated tanks to precipitate yttrium as an oxalate. The reactor discharge is dewatered using a
press filter and the yttrium oxalate solids are calcined in a rotary calciner to make yttrium oxide.
The yttrium oxide is then packaged for storage and sold as a product.

4.5 Area 300 - Advanced Separation Process

The Advanced Separation Process Unit (ASP) is used to separate SEG, Dy, Tb, and Ho+ into
individual element groups. The ASP unit is designed to accept any oxalate product or PLS
solution. Separation of the groups will happen over a series of solvent extraction, scrubbing, and
stripping steps after the RE oxalates are resolubilized through the metathesis process. The pH
control of the scrubbing and stripping circuits are very important for proper separation of the
products.

In regular operation, the metathesis and leach unit will be bypassed and strip solution from the Y
Recovery unit will be fed directly to the extraction battery in lieu of PLS generated in the ASP
Leach.

For simplicity, only the base case configuration will be described herein. In this configuration,
LE represents Sm, Eu and Gd (SEG), ME1 is Tb, ME2 is Dy and HE is any element heavier than
Ho.

4.6 Area 400 - Reduction to Metal Unit

Dysprosium oxide from the Advanced Separation Processing Unit is brought in and mixed with

surge silo prior to being mixed with calcium metal pellets in a pug mill. The calcium pellets and
dysprosium fluoride mixture is loaded into a vacuum reduction furnace. The mixture is heated to
the melting point of its constituents and dysprosium metal is generated from the calciothermic
reduction reaction. The metal is casted and remelted with calcium chloride then re-casted in
ingots that are sealed and packaged before being sold as a product.

Compressed air is provided by compressors followed by heated desiccant dryers and an air
receiver. All air is produced as dry specification (-40 C DP) avoiding the need for parallel plant
and instrument air piping systems. The compressed air plant is capable of meeting peak air
volume demand with two units in operation and the third as standby.
5.7 Industrial Gases

Bulk supply of industrial gases is installed near the point of use. These facilities will be provided
on lease contract with only the foundations and piping representing capital investment costs.
5.8 Waste Treatment and Emission Control Facilities

Emissions from the various facilities can be classified in three categories: solids, liquid and
gaseous. Strategies for emission control and reduction are discussed below for each category.

5.8.1 Solid Emissions

Solids emissions consist of two waste streams: the AMD neutralization solids and the Pre-
Concentrate Leach Residue. The former are classified as non-hazardous, falling under RCRA
subtitle D industrial wastes and are disposed as backfill at the AMD treatment site. The latter
will be evaluated according to USEPA method 1311 and classified accordingly. After pH
adjustment, this waste is expected to be similarly classified under RCRA subtitle D.

5.8.2 Liquid emissions

Liquid emissions consist of 2 liquid streams: the treated AMD discharge and the refining plant
liquid discharge. Our process incorporates AMD treatment with REE/CM recovery and the
former would occur under the site’s Clean Water Act (CWA) s ec.402 discharge permit which is
issued and enforced by the primacy state agency

 40

The refinery being sited on the A34 AMD treatment site, acidic reject streams will be minor
relative to the main treatment stream and will simply be injected into the incoming, raw water
stream for neutralization and metal removal.

5.8.3 Gaseous emissions

Gaseous emissions will be of three types: volatile organic emissions, acidic emissions and carbon
dioxide emissions. The volatile organic emissions will be caused by the extraction system diluent
(kerosene), modifier (TBP) and extractants (EHEHPA, D2EHPA, CA-12, Cyanex C-272, C-301
and C-572). Of these organic substances, kerosene will be the greatest contributor to VOCs. The
sections of the refinery where these emissions will occur will include negative pressure areas.
Discharged air will be processed through a water scrubber containing amphiphilic block
copolymers to ensure environmental compliance. Where acid emissions are present, the areas
will be designed using slightly negative pressure with the discharged air being processed through
a caustic scrubber to ensure environmental compliance.

The project team has followed a gated approach based on the front-end loading framework with
increasing project definition as the project progressed. This approach has been demonstrated to
increase the likelihood of success and minimize the risks associated with the realization of
complex multi-disciplinary projects. In this framework, successive phases increase the project
definition from all perspectives. These various perspectives include the process development and
design itself, but they also include engineering and permitting activities, stakeholders’
engagement, as well as financial and commercial activities.

The project execution phase involves the assembly of an owner’s team (OT) to manage and
oversee all activities that will lead to the construction, startup and initial operation of the REE
Demonstration Facility. The project execution phase is divided into two phases, an engineering,
procurement and construction phase and a start-up and operation phase, both running parallel
with the owner’s team management, community, and regulatory activities.

The project execution phase will be conducted in 2 budget periods:

Budget Period 1 (24 months): Detailed Engineering, Procurement and Construction: This
budget period will include: detailed design of the facility’s infrastructure, buildings and process
modules; permitting and procurement activities, and facility construction and shakedown.

Budget Period 2 (24 months): Facility Operation: This budget period will include the
commissioning, ramp up, and operation of the facility.

6.2 Feedstock and Product Disposition Management

A strategic approach to sourcing raw materials for the rare earth elements (REE) processing
facility in a decentralized manner to mirror the distribution of AMD producing regions will be
executed. The management strategy will involve identifying potential suppliers, negotiating
contracts that ensure consistent quality and supply, supporting them in the design, procurement
and construction of the pre-concentrate and regional concentration processes. Additionally, this
task will involve setting up logistics for efficient transportation and implementing quality control
measures at the point of origin to streamline the receiving process, and on developing
relationships with suppliers that can adapt to the facility's fluctuating needs. This decentralized
sourcing model will enhance supply security and ensure the REE Demonstration facility can be
operated properly.

The OT will execute on a targeted approach, focusing on securing the supply chain for a select
group of key players in the United States. This strategy involves maintaining long-term
partnerships with defense contractors and critical technology companies that rely on REEs for
specific applications for which no substitutes exist. The goal is to become a reliable supplier of
high-purity rare earths, ensuring that these strategic partners have a secure supply, insulated from
market volatility. Specifically, the OT will negotiate to set up strategic supply agreements,
implement a customization of the products offerings to meet specific customer requirements, and
possibly to co-develop products to better serve niche applications. By concentrating sales efforts
on these key players, the OT will not only ensure a stable revenue stream but will also position
the project as a vital component in the supply chains of high-value industries, promoting mutual
dependency and long-term business stability while promoting our national security.

9 Economic Analysis

9.1 Economic Scenario Assumptions

The cost estimation data described in the preceding sections was used to support a life-cycle
economic analysis to assess the overall project profitability with respect to common project
indicators including net present value, rate of return, and discounted payback period. In addition,
the economic analysis was used to determine the minimum product selling price needed to
achieve a positive net present value.

This analysis was performed for the following scenarios:

The first three scenarios involve the units composing the CPR Complex individually at their
respective design capacities, for their initial product suite and using NETL supplied pricing:

 1) West Virginia Regional Concentrator

 2) Central Processing Facility

 3) Reduction to Metal Unit.

In this initial analysis, each unit was assumed to operate as independent business units so as to
ascertain the standalone economic merit of the process operation. To account for feedstock sales
and acquisition, a discount factor of 40% was applied to the West Virginia Regional
Concentrator products’ value and to all mixed element products purchased from third parties.
This is consistent with L3’s market experience.

The analysis was also performed at the initial and maximal production capacity of the CPR and
for an alternative product suite where all heavy REE are produced. It should be noted that at the
3 mtpd equivalent MREO feed capacity, additional capital cost was added to the project to allow
for the full heavies’ separation.

Following the assessment of the individual units, two integrated scenarios were also analyzed:

 1) Complete Project (Base Case)

 2) Optimized Case (No Y, No RDM)

These scenarios included the West Virginia Regional Concentrator, commercial case CPR, and
the RDM unit, as appropriate, operating in an integrated manner. Intermediate products were
 54

assumed to be transferred to the downstream unit with no internal costs. These scenarios, thus,
provide an assessment of the fully integrated operation from AMD to oxide and metal products.
The final, optimized case, was established based on a close assessment of the primary cost and
revenue drivers. In particular, an assessment of the cost data showed that the yttrium circuit and
the RDM unit did not yield a net economic benefit, and removing these circuits prompted a 37%
reduction in capital costs, a 12% reduction in operating costs, but only a 3% reduction in
revenue.

Finaly, a price-based economic analysis was performed on the selected project scenario using the
four alternative price decks as described in Section 5.2 – Product Pricing.

 1) NETL Provided Pricing

 2) Current Market Pricing

   3) 2022 Pricing

 4) L3 Forecast for Long Term Pricing

Many of the economic assumptions, including those regarding the financing structure, escalation
rates, tax calculations, and operating period were supplied by National Energy Technology
Laboratory (NETL) in the RFP for this project. Key economic assumptions utilized in this study
include:

 • All amounts are in US dollars.

 • The total operational period for the plant is 20 years.

 • Inflation was applied to sales revenue and operating costs using a fixed rate of 3% per
 year.

 • The initial configuration capital costs are spread over a period of three years, and the
 allocation between those three years is 65.5%, 20.5% and 14% respectively. Thus, the
 total analysis period (capital purchase plus operating) is set at 23 years.

 • During the capital expenditure period, capital costs escalate at a constant rate of 3.6% per
 year.

 55

 • The project is debt financed for 50% of the total overnight capital requirement; the
 remaining 50% is financed by equity.

 • The debt repayment terms include: 6% interest rate, 10-year loan period, and no grace
 period on debt repayment. The repayment uses a standard amortization schedule with
 constant payments throughout the payoff period.

 • Working capital is not included in this estimate and will instead be borne by the operating
 entity at no cost to the project.

 • The combined federal and local tax rate is fixed at 26%.

 • All capital is depreciable, using a 150% declining balance depreciation schedule over 20
 years. The depreciation method was not changed to straight line when conditions favored
 the switch.

 • The mineral depletion rate for REEs is 14%. Depletion is charged at the appropriate rate
 times the net sales revenue after deducting royalties and any severance tax, provided that
 the total amount calculated by depletion rates does not exceed 50% of the taxable income
 before depletion.

 • The plant is part of a larger commercial entity with sufficient revenue to offset negative
 taxable income. Thus, losses are not carried forward and are instead calculated as a
 “negative tax” that indicates the reduction in tax burden required for overall entity.

 • No royalties are charged for the productions of REEs, as this cost is assumed to be borne
 with the feedstock acquisition costs.

 • Lastly, the data presented in this section provide a summary of the most salient outputs
 needed to interpret the findings. A more detailed listing of model outputs, assumptions,

11 Technology Gap Analysis

 (Refer to Appendix D – Technology Gap Analysis)

The proposed supply chain concept addresses many of the most important challenges and
limitations of the current state-of-the-art processes on which it is based. By adopting an
innovative approach to traditional technologies, the project team is confident that this proposed
supply chain concept will overcome the main limitations of current processes.

11.1 Pre-Concentrate Generation, Leaching and two-stage Oxalate Precipitation

No significant shortcomings are present in the pre-concentrate generations as the proposed
system will not increase reagent consumption nor impose significant additional burdens on the
current AMD neutralization processes. In fact, the transition to a two-stage neutralization process
is likely to decrease the operation cost of many sites.

The low acid proposed leaching process will use significantly less acid than traditional processes
and the use of concentrated acid along with a PLS recycle will minimize the amount of
wastewater generation. The inclusion of a sulfide precipitation stage prior to the oxalate
precipitation stages will greatly reduce the oxalate and acid consumption and rejection to the
environment.

11.2 Solvent Extraction

While the proposed system heavily relies on current state of the art technologies to minimize risk
and time to production, it includes significant innovations such as a modular concept, the
utilization of prestripitation (integrated precipitation and solvent stripping), and novel modeling
techniques. The modeling relies on a simulation tool allowing for the predictive modeling of
REE separation circuits using a combination of METSIM, a commercial simulation software, in
conjunction with SEASE, L3’s proprietary solvent extraction simulator. The current design and
the simulation tool will allow for the transition to an ionic liquid extraction system from the
current organic extraction system once such a system is ready for implementation. The transition
to ionic liquid extraction systems is likely to greatly reduce reagent consumption and wastewater
generation while eliminating any organic emissions.

 64

The modularization of the SX circuits in combination with the simulation tool will allow for the
same circuit to be converted to various feed compositions and will allow for significant variance
in feed material compositions to be supplied to the separation plant. In addition, the advanced
separation process will be designed in modules that will be flexible in their interconnections,
allowing for additional operational flexibility.

This modularization also allows for a small plant footprint, using the same equipment for
processing various materials in stages. It is important to highlight that the reconfigurability of the
proposed concept is not inherent to rare earth solvent extraction circuits. It is derived from the
novel simulation tool allowing for the rapid optimization of circuits.

11.3 Reduction to Metal

The proposed technology for the reduction of rare earths is the installation of a metallothermic
reduction furnace. While the technology is known and understood, it is expected that a
significant effort will be required to achieve market specifications and that is it unlikely to be
economically viable.

11.4 Assessed Technology Gaps and R&D Needed for Demonstration

No critical technology gaps have been identified in the current proposed supply chain.

Research and development activities required to execute the project are considered standard
technology development activities and are all related to optimizing the process flowsheet rather
than developing new technologies.

The current flowsheet is based on data retrieved from various literature sources for similar
applications and processes and from bench and pilot scale test work performed by West Virginia
University and Virginia Tech.

The de-risking and optimization test work campaign is on-going. The bench scale test work
related to leach, metathesis, and precipitation units is completed. The test work related to the
derivation of fundamental equilibrium parameters underpinning the modeling software for the
predictive simulation of REE separation circuit by solvent extraction is on-going and is expected
to be completed before the end of the Definitive Feasibility Study phase. The equilibrium data

and resulting circuit modeling output will be included in the final plant design. The current

Equipment-based technology research and development is not required as part of the project as

2 Understanding the Market

2.1 Pricing in the Rare Earth Industry

Prices in the rare earth industry are controlled by China. In general, the industry standard
regarding pricing for contracts is https://www.asianmetal.com/, a subscription service. Their
pricing method includes regularly canvassing buyers and sellers in China to establish a price for
a given product. These prices are used as a starting point for negotiating, as many customers do
not use the exact specification of the given element or they have other circumstances to consider
when pricing, such as form, purity, deleterious elements, volumes, etc.

Because the Chinese REE industry currently controls about 90% of mined REE material in the
world and 95% of processing, they have a stranglehold on REE pricing. When the Chinese
government wants lower REE prices, they use various mechanisms such as raising mining and
smelting quotas (placing more material into the market), as well as directives to the industry
inside China (ordering them to lower prices). If they want to raise prices, they can stockpile
material or cut quotas.

The major players outside China include Myanmar (100% of their material is controlled by
China, and is priced by China), Lynas (which has customers including the Japanese, Chinese and
others, but is subject to the influence of China pricing), and MP Materials (which sells the bulk
of its mineral concentrate to China and is subject to Chinese pricing).

Until a substantial rare earth element market is established outside of China, the Chinese pricing
model will continue to dominate the industry.

Our goal will be to seek out customers that are willing to pay a premium price to the Chinese
price, and we are in conversations with some of those customers now. There are a number of
customers that are willing to pay a premium to Chinese pricing in order to have a secure
alternative supply outside of China. These include customers that:

 • Have high margins
 • Are required by US government programs to source outside of China
 • Are required to have traceable material and supply chains
 • Are expanding their current supply chains to include alternatives to China
 • Are required to have ESG-compliant material for all or a portion of their materials

2.3 China Risk

There is a growing understanding throughout the rare earth market outside of China that
dependence on China for REEs presents an increasing risk. This risk mainly takes three forms:
geopolitical risk, domestic market expansion, and environmental/humanitarian risk.

 6

The geopolitical risk comes from the recent history of China cutting off Japan from REEs in
2010 over a territorial dispute. China began threatening to cut off the US in 2019 as a result of
the trade war. Over the last 12 months, the Chinese have restricted the export of REE equipment
and technology, as well as the critical minerals gallium, germanium, graphite and antimony in
response to trade issues with chips and other issues.

Chinese domestic market expansion risk comes from the fact that Chinese manufacturing has
been moving further downstream in recent years. In the very near future, Chinese domestic
demand for REEs in robotics, EVs, wind turbines, catalysts, motors, and other applications could
quite possibly absorb all of their domestic production capacity—leaving nothing for the export
market.

The environmental and humanitarian risk comes from the damaging methods China uses for
extraction in Africa as well Myanmar and Malaysia and other countries. Downstream, there is
also risk that manufacturing is taking place in Xinjiang where the Uygur population has been
documented to be oppressed.

Many potential customers are now looking for ways to mitigate the above risks and are turning to
alternative supply chains for some or all of their future supplies.

Our project is positioned for a key role in providing an environmentally clean alternative
(cleaning up acid mine drainage) in a safe jurisdiction (the United States), with a product that is
needed by the market in North America (oxides).

3 Customer Acquisition

The first step is to speak with various potential customers to let them know what elements will
be available, as well as volumes, forms, purity levels and potential specifications. It is critical to
be cognizant of customer timelines for product design cycles, qualification, purchasing, etc. We
have relationships with multiple companies that have immediate need for the elements we will
produce. Several of the customers that we have relationships with are in higher-margin, critical
industries that are directly related to national security and may therefore be able to pay a
premium over the standard market pricing.

We are fully aware of the complexities of onboarding potential customers—from negotiating
offtake agreements, to the qualification process, to meeting ongoing customer specifications.
Offtake agreements often begin as non-binding memorandums of understanding and may take
months to negotiate. From there, binding agreements can be reached as certain benchmarks are
achieved. The qualification process can take anywhere from several months to several years,
depending on the nature of the application and the specifications of the product. The
specifications may include varied criteria such as purity, deleterious elements, particle size and
shape, and others. We have the ability to not only create unique products to meet customer
specifications, but also the lab equipment to test to ensure that specifications are properly
achieved.

5 Financing Plan

In order to properly engage potential financing partners, we needed to first have our costs
established, which we now have. However, because we have only recently ascertained these
costs, we have not yet been able to solicit funding from potential partners.

Every REE facility needs to know how their costs compare to those of China with the
understanding that China subsidizes their REE production at every stage. These Chinese
subsidies take the form of tax breaks, rebates, grants, discounted energy costs, discounted
chemical costs, lower cost or requirements of ESG standards, discounted labor, and many others.
Because of these subsidies, any facility outside of China is at a market disadvantage. China has
methodically and strategically built their REE industry over the last four decades in order to gain
market share and leverage the REE industry to their advantage.

It will take many years to re-establish a rare earth supply chain outside of China. During this
time, the US Government may need to supply subsidies in many forms—tax breaks, purchase
agreements, grants, loans, etc.—in order to support rebuilding the supply chain here. REEs
supply critical materials across our economy and should be supported not just for military and
DOD purposes, but also to ensure that our commercial sector has access to cutting edge materials
for future applications.

Building a demonstration facility such as the one we propose may provide value far beyond the
REE materials produced. The first priority will be to produce essential baseline material for the
defense sector, key industry players (such as semiconductors), and the research & development
efforts of commercial players, national labs, and academic institutions. In addition to this, we
will be able to provide a training environment for much needed future REE expertise and a
proving ground for potential technologies. As the industry grows in the US, commercial players
will need experts that are ready to immediately enter the market with an appropriate skillset. The
Chinese have over 30 State Key labs that train REE workers—this is part of the reason that they
have such an advantage. We need workers that are ready to engage immediately throughout the
supply chain.

6 Waste Disposition

Emissions from the various facilities can be classified in three categories: solids, liquid and
gaseous. Strategies for emission control and reduction are discussed below for disposal of liquid
and solid effluents.

6.1 Solid Effluents

Solids emissions consist of two waste streams: the AMD neutralization solids and the Pre-
Concentrate Leach Residue. The former are classified as non-hazardous, falling under RCRA
subtitle D industrial wastes and are disposed as backfill at the AMD treatment site. The latter
will be evaluated according to USEPA method 1311 and classified accordingly. After pH
adjustment, this waste is expected to be similarly classified under RCRA subtitle D. Both solids
will be combined with the AMD Neutralization Thickener underflow 1 and stockpiled in the
existing permitted tailings storage facility.

6.2 Liquid Effluents

Liquid emissions consist of 2 liquid streams: the treated AMD discharge and the refining plant
liquid discharge. Our process incorporates AMD treatment with REE/CM recovery and the
former would occur under the site’s Clean Water Act (CWA) s ec.402 discharge permit which is
issued and enforced by the primacy state agency

The refinery being sited on the A34 AMD treatment site, acidic reject streams will be minor
relative to the main treatment stream and will simply be injected into the incoming, raw water
stream for neutralization and metal removal.
