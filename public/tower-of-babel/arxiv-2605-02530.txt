A partial dictionary between universal central extensions and orthogonal polynomials in the superelliptic Krichever–Novikov setting
Felipe Albino dos Santos

Abstract
. Let A = ℂ [ x ± 1 , u ∣ u m = P ( x ) ] A=\mathbb{C}[x^{\pm 1},\,u\mid u^{m}=P(x)] be the coordinate ring of a superelliptic curve in the Krichever–Novikov two-point framework. We establish a systematic dictionary between the cocycle data of universal central extensions — both of Der ⁡ ( A ) \mathrm{Der}(A) and of 𝔤 ⊗ A \mathfrak{g}\otimes A — and families of orthogonal polynomials in the parameter a a encoding the branch locus of P P . The dictionary has three canonical entries: (1) the reduction relations in the center A / ∂ A A/\partial A of Der ⁡ ( A ) ^ \widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)} are exactly the three-term recurrence of the associated orthogonal polynomial family; (2) the generating function of the center satisfies the Sturm–Liouville ODE of that family; (3) the cross-cocycle ψ ⁡ ( e 1 , f s ) \psi(e_{1},f_{s}) equals the Legendre antiderivative ( P n − 1 ​ ( a ) − P n + 1 ​ ( a ) ) / ( 2 ​ n + 1 ) (P_{n-1}(a)-P_{n+1}(a))/(2n+1) in the quadratic case. We prove the dictionary completely for P ⁡ ( x ) = x 2 − 2 ​ a ​ x + 1 P(x)=x^{2}-2ax+1 (Legendre polynomials) and establish it for the quartic symmetric case P ⁡ ( x ) = x 4 − 2 ​ a ​ x 2 + 1 P(x)=x^{4}-2ax^{2}+1 . In the quadratic case, palindromic symmetry forces the unique sector recurrence to be the Legendre three-term recurrence. In the quartic case, the odd-positive sector is governed by the Legendre family, while the even sector satisfies a two-component recurrence with palindromic (symmetric-in-degree) coefficients. We conjecture that this structural pattern — palindromic symmetry forcing symmetric recurrence coefficients — persists for all palindromic P P of even degree. The superelliptic polynomial families governing the Kähler side Ω A 1 / d ​ A \Omega^{1}_{A}/dA fit the same dictionary via the Rescaling Lemma, reducing all sectors to the sector- 1 1 family at parameter m / l m/l . The dictionary is canonical in the recurrence and the ODE; it is partially choice-dependent in the mixed (cross-sector) cocycle direction.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The phenomenon

The universal central extensions (UCEs) of two infinite-dimensional
Lie algebras arise naturally from a superelliptic curve

u^{m}=P(x)
: the derivation algebra
\mathrm{Der}(A)
and the current algebra

\mathfrak{g}\otimes A
, where
A=\mathbb{C}[x^{\pm 1},u\mid u^{m}=P(x)]
.
For the simplest case — the loop algebra
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathbb{C}[x^{\pm 1}]

corresponding to the genus-zero curve — the center of each UCE is
one-dimensional and no recurrence arises.
Once the curve has positive genus or the base ring is replaced by a
superelliptic ring, the centers become multi-dimensional and the
reduction relations in those centers carry rich structure.

Concretely, the well known dimension formulas
[
3
,
7
]
yield:

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}A/\partial A

\displaystyle=1+n(m-1),

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA

\displaystyle=r(m-1)+1,

where
n=\deg P
and
r
is a structural parameter of
P
.
Each basis element
[x^{k}]
in
A/\partial A
satisfies a linear recurrence
derived from the identity
\partial(x^{k}u^{m-1})=0
in the quotient.
The question we address is: what mathematical objects do these
recurrences describe?

1.2.
Prior work

Universal central extensions of infinite-dimensional Lie algebras were
studied systematically by Kassel and Loday
[
4
]
;
the framework extends naturally to the Krichever–Novikov algebras
introduced in
[
5
]
and developed by
Schlichenmaier
[
9
]
.
In the superelliptic setting, the algebras
\mathrm{Der}(A)
and
\mathfrak{g}\otimes A

and their UCEs were studied by Cox–Guo–Lu–Zhao
[
2
]

and Cox–Zhao
[
3
]
, who established the dimension formulas
above and computed explicit cocycle bases.
The connection between the UCE center and orthogonal polynomial families
was first observed by Cox, Futorny, and Tirao
[
1
]

for DJKM algebras, and then developed in the superelliptic current algebra
setting in
[
7
]
;
a sequel
[
8
]
extends these results to

m\geq 3
, identifying the relevant families as ultraspherical polynomials.
The derivation algebra side — the
\mathrm{Der}(A)
half of the dictionary —
had not been treated systematically before the present paper.

1.3.
The answer

The reduction relations in
A/\partial A
are exactly the three-term
recurrence of a classical or non-classical orthogonal polynomial
family.

For the quadratic case

P(x)=x^{2}-2ax+1
(any
a\neq\pm 1
), the recurrence

(1)

(r+1)[x^{r+1}]-a(2r+1)[x^{r}]+r[x^{r-1}]=0\quad\text{in }A/\partial A

is term-for-term the Legendre three-term recurrence

(n+1)P_{n+1}(a)=(2n+1)a\,P_{n}(a)-n\,P_{n-1}(a).

The initial conditions
[x^{0}]=\omega_{2}
,
[x^{1}]=a\omega_{2}
match

P_{0}(a)=1
,
P_{1}(a)=a
.
By uniqueness of orthogonal polynomial sequences,

[x^{n}]=P_{n}(a)\,\omega_{2}
for all
n\geq 0
.

This identification has three further consequences that we prove:

(1)

The generating function of
\{[x^{k}]\}_{k\geq 0}
is

(1-2az+z^{2})^{-1/2}
, which satisfies the ODE

(1-2az+z^{2})F^{\prime}+(z-a)F=0
— the same equation that defines
the Legendre generating function.

(2)

The canonical 2-cocycle cross-term
\psi(e_{1},f_{s})
equals

\psi(e_{1},f_{s})=\frac{P_{n-1}(a)-P_{n+1}(a)}{2n+1}\,\omega_{2},\qquad n=s+1,

which equals the definite integral

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{1}P_{n}(t)\,dt
of the
n
-th Legendre polynomial.
Indeed, the standard identity
P_{n+1}^{\prime}(a)-P_{n-1}^{\prime}(a)=(2n+1)P_{n}(a)

gives
\frac{d}{da}\bigl[(P_{n-1}(a)-P_{n+1}(a))/(2n+1)\bigr]=-P_{n}(a)
,
so
(P_{n-1}(a)-P_{n+1}(a))/(2n+1)=\int_{a}^{1}P_{n}(t)\,dt
.
In other words,
the cross-cocycle is the integrated tail of the
Legendre polynomial
, a classical integral evaluated algebraically.

(3)

On the Kähler side, the superelliptic polynomial families
P^{(l,j)}_{k}(c;m,r)
governing
\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA
satisfy the
same Legendre recurrence in sector
l=1
, and the Rescaling Lemma
(proved in
[
10
]
) identifies all sectors via

P^{(l,j)}_{k}(c;m,r)=P^{(1,j)}_{k}(c;m/l,r)
.

The main contribution of this paper is the explicit, two-sided
dictionary (Table
1
) between the UCE cocycle data and
orthogonal polynomial data, including the new identification of the
cross-cocycle as the Legendre antiderivative
(Theorem
5.2
).

1.4.
Canonical versus choice-dependent parts

The dictionary has a
canonical
part and a
choice-dependent

part.
The
canonical
part consists of the data intrinsic to
P(x)
and
m
:
the three-term recurrence (
1
) satisfied by the basis elements
of
A/\partial A
, the ODE governing the generating function
F(z)
, and the
functional form of the cross-cocycle formula
\psi(e_{1},f_{s})
.
These objects are determined entirely by
P(x)
and
m
, independently
of how one labels or normalises the basis generators.

The
choice-dependent
part is the concrete presentation in terms
of explicit basis generators
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
:
identifying which generator is the positive-sector class and which is the
negative-sector class, and fixing the overall normalisation of the cocycle.
Concretely, the Legendre reduction
[x^{k}]=P_{k}(a)\,\omega_{2}
requires
fixing
\omega_{2}=[1]
; the parallel formula
[x^{-k}]=P_{k-1}(a)\,\omega_{1}

requires
\omega_{1}=[x^{-1}]
.
Both choices are natural (they are the unique monic choices compatible
with the
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-grading), and the symmetry
x\mapsto x^{-1}
exchanges
them without changing the underlying mathematics.

1.5.
Why it is structural

The identification is not a computational accident.
We believe the key structural point is:
the map
P(x)\mapsto\{A/\partial A,\text{recurrence}\}
is naturally mediated by the geometry
of the branch locus of the superelliptic curve
.
The branch locus of
P(x)=x^{2}-2ax+1
consists of two points

\alpha,\beta
(the roots), and the parameter
a=(\alpha+\beta)/2

is the “position parameter” of the Legendre family.
The derivation
\partial=u\,d/dx
acts on
A
by differentiation, and
the quotient
A/\partial A
kills all exact forms; the surviving classes
are constrained by the residue data at the branch points.
In the quadratic case, this constraint is precisely captured by
the Legendre recurrence.
A general proof that the recurrence for arbitrary
P
is governed
by the branch-locus geometry in this sense remains an open problem.

Remark 1.1

(Heuristic motivation)
.

Informally, the Legendre weight
w(x)=1
on
[-1,1]

can be thought of as the flat measure on the branch interval
between the two roots of
P
, up to scaling.
This heuristic motivates the appearance of the Legendre family,
but a precise identification of the weight measure with the
branch-locus data would require further analysis beyond the scope
of this paper.

For palindromic
P
(i.e.,
P(x)=x^{n}P(1/x)
), the symmetry
forces the recurrence coefficients in
A/\partial A
to be symmetric in the
index.
In the quadratic and quartic cases, we prove that this yields
the Legendre family (and a two-component Legendre recurrence,
respectively); we conjecture that the general palindromic case
produces an orthogonal family with symmetric recurrence coefficients.
For non-palindromic
P
, a non-classical family (of the type studied in

[
1
]
) arises.
We prove these criteria for
m=2
in Sections
4

and
9
.

2.
Setup and notation

2.1.
Superelliptic algebras and the KN two-point framework

Definition 2.1
.

Let
m\geq 2
and
P(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
be a polynomial with simple roots.
The
superelliptic algebra
is

A=\mathbb{C}[x^{\pm 1},\,u\mid u^{m}=P(x)].

The associated
superelliptic curve
is

\mathscr{C}\colon u^{m}=P(x)
.
The algebra
A
carries a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-grading

A=\bigoplus_{l=0}^{m-1}A^{l}
with
A^{l}=\mathbb{C}[x^{\pm 1}]\cdot u^{l}
.

In the Krichever–Novikov (KN) two-point framework

[
5
,
9
]
,
the algebra
A
plays the role of the algebra of meromorphic
functions on
\mathscr{C}
that are regular everywhere except
possibly at the two points over
x=0
and
x=\infty
.
The algebras
\mathrm{Der}(A)
and
\mathfrak{g}\otimes A
are the KN
vector-field algebra and the KN current algebra, respectively.

Definition 2.2

(Distinguished derivation)
.

The
distinguished derivation
of
A
is

\partial\coloneqq u^{m-1}\frac{d}{dx}\in\mathrm{Der}(A).

One verifies
\partial(u^{m}-P(x))=0
, so
\partial
is well-defined on
A
.
For
m=2
:
\partial=u\,d/dx
.

Theorem 2.3

(Cox–Guo–Lu–Zhao
[
2
]
)
.

\mathrm{Der}(A)=A\,\partial
as an
A
-module.
Every derivation has the form
f\partial
for a unique
f\in A
.

Notation 2.4
.

For
m=2
, set
e_{r}\coloneqq x^{r}\partial
and
f_{r}\coloneqq x^{r}u\partial

for
r\in\mathbb{Z}
.
These span
\mathrm{Der}(A)
as a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space.

2.2.
The two universal central extensions

2.2.1.
UCE of the derivation algebra

Theorem 2.5

(Cox–Zhao
[
3
]
)
.

With
P(x)=x^{l}(x-a_{1})\cdots(x-a_{n})
for pairwise distinct

a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
and
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
:

(1)

The UCE of
\mathrm{Der}(A)
is

\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}=\mathrm{Der}(A)\oplus(A/\partial A)
with bracket

[f\partial,g\partial]=(f\partial(g)-g\partial(f))\partial+\overline{\partial(f)\partial^{2}(g)}.

(2)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}A/\partial A=1+n(m-1)
.

The canonical 2-cocycle is
\psi:\mathrm{Der}(A)\times\mathrm{Der}(A)\to A/\partial A
,

\psi(f\partial,g\partial)=\overline{\partial(f)\,\partial^{2}(g)}.

2.2.2.
UCE of the current algebra

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra with
invariant bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Theorem 2.6

(Kassel–Loday
[
4
]
)
.

\mathfrak{g}\otimes A
is perfect and its UCE is

\widehat{\mathfrak{g}\otimes A}=(\mathfrak{g}\otimes A)\oplus(\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA)
with bracket

[X\otimes f,\,Y\otimes g]=[X,Y]\otimes fg+(X,Y)\,\overline{f\,dg},

where
\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA
denotes the Kähler differentials modulo
exact forms.

Theorem 2.7

(Dimension and basis
[
7
]
)
.

For
A=\mathbb{C}[x^{\pm 1},u\mid u^{m}=P(x)]
with
P(x)=1-2cx^{r}+x^{2r}

and
m,r\geq 2
:

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA=r(m-1)+1.

A basis is provided by

\bigl\{x^{-1}dx,\;x^{-j}u^{l}\,dx\mid 1\leq l\leq m-1,\;1\leq j\leq r\bigr\}
.
The UCE has structure constants forming polynomial families

P^{(l,j)}_{k}(c;m,r)
in the parameter
c
.

2.3.
Comparison of centers

The two centers have different dimensions:

\dim A/\partial A=1+n(m-1)
while
\dim\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA=r(m-1)+1

(with
n=\deg P=2r
in our palindromic setup,
m,r\geq 2

Their difference is
(n-r)(m-1)=r(m-1)\geq 2
, so
A/\partial A

is strictly larger.
Concretely,
A/\partial A
carries an additional
r(m-1)
generators
not seen by
\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA
; in the quadratic case (
r=1
,
n=2
) this
is one extra generator, the “Virasoro-type” class
\omega_{0}=[x^{-1}u]
.
Table
3
in Section

makes
this precise.

\mathrm{Der}(A)
and the analytic data of a classical orthogonal

Concretely: the reduction relations in
A/\partial A
— derived from the

\partial(x^{r}u)=0

the ODE, and the cross-cocycle.

3.2.
The mechanism

The dictionary is not an analogy; it is a precise algebraic identity.
We explain the mechanism in three steps.

Step 1: The center as a polynomial space.

By Theorem
2.5
,
\dim A/\partial A=1+n(m-1)
.
For
m=2
,
P(x)=x^{2}-2ax+1
(so
n=2
):
\dim=3
.
A basis is
\omega_{0}=[x^{-1}u]
,
\omega_{1}=[x^{-1}]
,

\omega_{2}=[1]
(proved in Proposition
4.3
).
The classes
[x^{k}]
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
are all expressible in terms of
these three generators.

Step 2: The recurrence is the three-term recurrence.

The identity
\partial(x^{r}u)=0
in
A/\partial A
expands to the
linear relation (Lemma
4.1
):

(r+1)[x^{r+1}]-a(2r+1)[x^{r}]+r[x^{r-1}]=0.

Setting
p_{k}\coloneqq[x^{k}]
and dividing by
\omega_{2}
(so

p_{k}=c_{k}\omega_{2}
), the scalars
c_{k}
satisfy

(r+1)c_{r+1}-a(2r+1)c_{r}+rc_{r-1}=0

with
c_{0}=1
,
c_{1}=a
.
The unique sequence with these coefficients and initial conditions
is the Legendre sequence:
c_{k}=P_{k}(a)
.

Step 3: Uniqueness is classical.

A sequence
\{c_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfying a three-term recurrence

(r+1)c_{r+1}=a(2r+1)c_{r}-rc_{r-1}
with
c_{0}=1
,
c_{1}=a

is unique, and equals
P_{k}(a)
by the uniqueness theorem for
orthogonal polynomial sequences with given recurrence coefficients
(see, e.g.,
[
11
]
).

The same mechanism works on the
\Omega^{1}_{A}/dA
side (Section
6
).

3.3.
Dimension counting and the “OP sector count”

For general
m
and
P
with
n
roots, the dimension

1+n(m-1)
counts:

•

1
odd-sector generator (
\omega_{0}=[x^{-1}u^{m-1}]
);

•

n(m-1)/2
independent OP sectors in the positive
x
-direction;

•

n(m-1)/2
independent OP sectors in the negative
x
-direction.

For
m=2
,
n=2
:
1+2(1)=3=1+1+1
,
matching
\omega_{0}
(odd),
\omega_{2}
(positive),
\omega_{1}
(negative).
For
m=2
,
n=4
(quartic):
1+4(1)=5=1+2+2
,
matching
\omega_{0}
(odd) plus two independent positive and two
independent negative generators.
This counting corresponds to the fact that the OP family has

n(m-1)/2
“degrees of freedom” in the bracket structure,
one per residue constraint of
\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}
beyond the Virasoro central charge.

4.
The quadratic entry: Legendre polynomials

We now prove the
\mathrm{Der}(A)
-side dictionary entry completely for

P(x)=x^{2}-2ax+1
.

4.1.
The derivation action on monomials

The next two lemmas compute the action of
\partial
on monomials

x^{r}u
and
\partial^{2}(x^{s}u)
; these computations are the engine of all
subsequent reductions in
A/\partial A
and of the cocycle formulas.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
r\in\mathbb{Z}
:

(2)

\partial(x^{r}u)=(r+1)x^{r+1}-a(2r+1)x^{r}+rx^{r-1}.

Proof.

Since
\partial=u\,d/dx
, we compute

\displaystyle\partial(x^{r}u)

\displaystyle=u\frac{d}{dx}(x^{r}u)=u(rx^{r-1}u+x^{r}u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=rx^{r-1}u^{2}+x^{r}(uu^{\prime}).

Using
u^{2}=P(x)=x^{2}-2ax+1
and

uu^{\prime}=u\cdot(u^{\prime})=\tfrac{P^{\prime}(x)}{2}=x-a
:

\displaystyle\partial(x^{r}u)

\displaystyle=rx^{r-1}(x^{2}-2ax+1)+x^{r}(x-a)

\displaystyle=(r+1)x^{r+1}-a(2r+1)x^{r}+rx^{r-1}.\qed

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
s\in\mathbb{Z}
:

(3)

\partial^{2}(x^{s}u)=(s+1)^{2}x^{s}u-as(2s+1)x^{s-1}u+s(s-1)x^{s-2}u.

Proof.

Apply
\partial
to each monomial in
\partial(x^{s}u)=(s+1)x^{s+1}-a(2s+1)x^{s}+sx^{s-1}

(Lemma
4.1
), using
\partial(x^{k})=kx^{k-1}u
:

\displaystyle\partial((s+1)x^{s+1})

\displaystyle=(s+1)^{2}x^{s}u,

\displaystyle\partial(-a(2s+1)x^{s})

\displaystyle=-as(2s+1)x^{s-1}u,

\displaystyle\partial(sx^{s-1})

\displaystyle=s(s-1)x^{s-2}u.

Summing gives (
3
).
∎

4.2.
The basis of
A/\partial A

Proposition 4.3
.

For
P(x)=x^{2}-2ax+1
with
a\neq\pm 1
:

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}A/\partial A=3,\qquad\text{basis: }\omega_{0}=[x^{-1}u],\;\omega_{1}=[x^{-1}],\;\omega_{2}=[1].

Proof.

Dimension 3 follows from Theorem
2.5
with
n=2
,
m=2
.
Setting
\partial(x^{r}u)=0
in
A/\partial A
via (
2
) gives:

(4)

(r+1)[x^{r+1}]=a(2r+1)[x^{r}]-r[x^{r-1}].

Positive powers.

For
r=0
:
[x]=a[1]
.
For
r=1
:
2[x^{2}]=3a[x]-[1]=3a^{2}[1]-[1]
, so
[x^{2}]=\tfrac{3a^{2}-1}{2}[1]
.
By induction on
r\geq 0
: (
4
) expresses
[x^{r+1}]
as a
scalar multiple of
[1]
, so all
[x^{k}]
(
k\geq 0
) lie in
\mathbb{C}[1]=\mathbb{C}\omega_{2}
.

Negative powers.

For
r=-1
: (
4
) gives
0=-a[x^{-1}]+[x^{-2}]
,
so
[x^{-2}]=a[x^{-1}]
.
For
r=-2
:
(-1)[x^{-1}]=a(-3)[x^{-2}]-(-2)[x^{-3}]
,
so
[x^{-3}]=\tfrac{1+3a^{2}}{2}[x^{-1}]
.
By induction, all
[x^{-k}]
(
k\geq 1
) lie in
\mathbb{C}[x^{-1}]=\mathbb{C}\omega_{1}
.

Odd-sector powers.

Applying (
2
) to
x^{k}
(not
x^{k}u
) gives

\partial(x^{k})=kx^{k-1}u
, so
[x^{k}u]=[\partial(x^{k})/k]=0
for
k\neq 0
.
For
k=0
:
\partial(u^{2}-P(x))=0
in
A
, but
[u]=0
since
\partial(1)=0
and

\partial(x/2)=x^{0}u=u
, so
[u]=[\partial(x/2)]=0
in
A/\partial A
.
One checks by dimension counting (dim
=3
) that the three classes

\omega_{0}=[x^{-1}u]
,
\omega_{1}=[x^{-1}]
,
\omega_{2}=[1]
are linearly
independent, completing the basis.
∎

4.3.
The Legendre identification

With the basis of
A/\partial A
in hand, we can now state the main
identification: the scalars
c_{k}
defined by
[x^{k}]=c_{k}\,\omega_{2}

satisfy the Legendre recurrence with Legendre initial conditions,
so they
are
the Legendre polynomials.

Theorem 4.4

(Legendre reduction)
.

For
P(x)=x^{2}-2ax+1
, the following identities hold in
A/\partial A
:

(5)

[x^{k}]=P_{k}(a)\,\omega_{2}\quad(k\geq 0),\qquad[x^{-k}]=P_{k-1}(a)\,\omega_{1}\quad(k\geq 1),

where
P_{k}(a)
denotes the
k
-th Legendre polynomial evaluated at
a
.

Proof.

Define scalars
c_{k}
by
[x^{k}]=c_{k}\,\omega_{2}
for
k\geq 0
.
From (
4
), the sequence
\{c_{k}\}
satisfies

(r+1)c_{r+1}=a(2r+1)c_{r}-rc_{r-1},\qquad r\geq 0,

with
c_{0}=1
(
[x^{0}]=[1]=\omega_{2}
) and
c_{1}=a
(from
r=0
).
Rewriting:
(r+1)c_{r+1}-a(2r+1)c_{r}+rc_{r-1}=0
.
The Legendre polynomials satisfy
(n+1)P_{n+1}(a)=(2n+1)aP_{n}(a)-nP_{n-1}(a)

with
P_{0}(a)=1
,
P_{1}(a)=a
.
These are the same recurrence and initial conditions.
By uniqueness of the sequence satisfying a three-term recurrence
with given initial conditions
[
11
]
,
c_{k}=P_{k}(a)
.

For
k\leq-1
: set
c_{-k}=b_{k}
with
[x^{-k}]=b_{k}\,\omega_{1}
.
Setting
r=-(k+1)
in (
4
):

(-k)[x^{-k}]=a(-2k-1)[x^{-k-1}]+(k+1)[x^{-k-2}]
,
which gives
(-k)b_{k}=a(-2k-1)b_{k+1}+(k+1)b_{k+2}
.
Reindexing with
j=k-1
:
(j+1)b_{j}=a(2j+1)b_{j+1}-jb_{j+2}
.
With
b_{1}=c_{1}|_{x\mapsto x^{-1}}=1
(since
[x^{-1}]=\omega_{1}
)
and
b_{2}=a
(from
[x^{-2}]=a\omega_{1}
), this is again the
Legendre recurrence with initial conditions
P_{0}(a)=1
,
P_{1}(a)=a
.
Hence
b_{k}=P_{k-1}(a)
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The identification is canonical in the following sense.
The assignment
P_{k}(a)\mapsto[x^{k}]_{A/\partial A}/\omega_{2}
defines
a graded linear bijection between the sequence of Legendre polynomials

(P_{k}(a))_{k\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{C}[a]
and the basis
([x^{k}])_{k\geq 0}

of
A/\partial A
.
This bijection intertwines the Legendre three-term recurrence with
the algebraic reduction (
4
), showing the two sequences
are
equivalent as solutions of the same recurrence with the same
initial conditions
; the uniqueness theorem for three-term recurrences
then forces their equality.
(The map is not a ring homomorphism in general, since
[x^{j}][x^{k}]

in
A/\partial A
is not
P_{j}(a)P_{k}(a)
but involves a product reduction.)

4.4.
Generating function and the ODE

Theorem 4.6

(Legendre generating function)
.

Let
F(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}[x^{k}]\,z^{k}
be the generating function
in
A/\partial A
.

(1)

F(z)=(1-2az+z^{2})^{-1/2}\cdot\omega_{2}
.

(2)

The scalar part satisfies the first-order ODE

(6)

(1-2az+z^{2})F^{\prime}(z)+(z-a)F(z)=0.

(3)

The initial condition
F(0)=\omega_{2}
uniquely determines
F
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.4
,
F(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P_{k}(a)z^{k}\cdot\omega_{2}
.
The classical Legendre generating function is

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P_{k}(a)z^{k}=(1-2az+z^{2})^{-1/2}

(see
[
11
]
, Theorem 3.3.1).
This gives (1).

For (2): multiply the recurrence
(r+1)P_{r+1}=(2r+1)aP_{r}-rP_{r-1}

by
z^{r}
and sum over
r\geq 0
:

\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}(r+1)P_{r+1}z^{r}-a\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}(2r+1)P_{r}z^{r}+\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}rP_{r-1}z^{r}=0.

The first sum equals
F^{\prime}(z)
.
The second equals
2zF^{\prime}(z)+F(z)
.
The third equals
z^{2}F^{\prime}(z)+zF(z)
(reindex).
Collecting:
(1-2az+z^{2})F^{\prime}(z)-(a-z)F(z)=0
,
which is (
6
).
Separability gives
F(z)=(1-2az+z^{2})^{-1/2}
as the unique
solution with
F(0)=1
.
∎

5.
The cross-cocycle and the Legendre antiderivative

We now turn to the mixed (cross-sector) cocycle
\psi(e_{r},f_{s})
,
which provides the deepest entry in the dictionary: the cross-cocycle
equals the definite integral of a Legendre polynomial.

5.1.
The general cross-cocycle formula

Recall from Notation
2.4
that
e_{r}=x^{r}\partial
and

f_{s}=x^{s}u\partial
.
The cross-cocycle is
\psi(e_{r},f_{s})\in\mathbb{C}\omega_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}\omega_{2}
.

Theorem 5.1

(General cross-cocycle)
.

For all
r,s\in\mathbb{Z}
with
r\neq 0
, setting
n=r+s
:

(7)

\psi(e_{r},f_{s})=r\!\sum_{k=-3}^{1}C_{k}(s,a)\,[x^{n+k}]_{A/\partial A},

where

\displaystyle C_{1}(s,a)

\displaystyle=(s+1)^{2},

\displaystyle C_{0}(s,a)

\displaystyle=-a(4s^{2}+5s+2),

\displaystyle C_{-1}(s,a)

\displaystyle=(2s^{2}+s+1)+2a^{2}s(2s+1),

\displaystyle C_{-2}(s,a)

\displaystyle=-as(4s-1),

\displaystyle C_{-3}(s,a)

\displaystyle=s(s-1).

Applying the Legendre reduction (
5
) expresses
this as a linear combination of Legendre polynomials in

\mathbb{C}\omega_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}\omega_{2}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.1
:
\partial(x^{r})=rx^{r-1}u
.
From Lemma
4.2
:

\partial^{2}(x^{s}u)=(s+1)^{2}x^{s}u-as(2s+1)x^{s-1}u+s(s-1)x^{s-2}u
.
The cocycle is
\psi(e_{r},f_{s})=\overline{\partial(x^{r})\cdot\partial^{2}(x^{s}u)}
,
i.e. the class of
rx^{r-1}u\cdot[(s+1)^{2}x^{s}u-as(2s+1)x^{s-1}u+s(s-1)x^{s-2}u]
.
Multiplying: each term
x^{r-1}u\cdot x^{s+j}u=x^{r+s+j-1}u^{2}=x^{r+s+j-1}(x^{2}-2ax+1)
.
Expanding each product
x^{n+j-1}(x^{2}-2ax+1)=x^{n+j+1}-2ax^{n+j}+x^{n+j-1}

and collecting over
k=-3,-2,-1,0,1
(shift from
n
) yields
the five-term formula (
7
).
∎

5.2.
The
r=1
closed form: Legendre antiderivative

Theorem 5.2

(Legendre antiderivative)
.

For
r=1
and
n=1+s\geq 1
:

(8)

\psi(e_{1},f_{s})=\frac{P_{n-1}(a)-P_{n+1}(a)}{2n+1}\,\omega_{2}.

Moreover:

(9)

\frac{P_{n-1}(a)-P_{n+1}(a)}{2n+1}=\int_{a}^{1}P_{n}(t)\,dt.

Thus the cross-cocycle
\psi(e_{1},f_{s})
equals the definite integral
of the
n
-th Legendre polynomial from
a
to
1
, with

n=s+1
.

Proof.

We first establish (
9
) as an independent
classical identity, then use it to complete the proof of (
8
).

Equation (
9
).

Set
F(a)\coloneqq(P_{n-1}(a)-P_{n+1}(a))/(2n+1)
.
The standard Legendre derivative identity

P_{n+1}^{\prime}(a)-P_{n-1}^{\prime}(a)=(2n+1)P_{n}(a)

(differentiate the three-term recurrence; see
[
11
]
)
gives

F^{\prime}(a)=\frac{P_{n-1}^{\prime}(a)-P_{n+1}^{\prime}(a)}{2n+1}=-P_{n}(a).

Evaluating at
a=1
: using
P_{k}(1)=1
for all
k
,

F(1)=(1-1)/(2n+1)=0
.
By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

F(a)=F(1)+\int_{1}^{a}F^{\prime}(t)\,dt=\int_{1}^{a}(-P_{n}(t))\,dt=\int_{a}^{1}P_{n}(t)\,dt.

This proves (
9
) without any reference to the
cross-cocycle.

Equation (
8
).

Substitute
r=1
(so
s=n-1
) in (
7
).
After applying the Legendre reduction (
5
),
the cross-cocycle becomes the linear combination

(10)

g_{n}(a)\coloneqq\sum_{k=-3}^{1}C_{k}(n-1,a)\,P_{n+k}(a).

We must show
g_{n}(a)=(P_{n-1}(a)-P_{n+1}(a))/(2n+1)
for all
n\geq 1
.

Base cases
n=1,2,3
.

For
n=1
:
C_{1}=1
,
C_{0}=-2a
,
C_{-1}=1
,
C_{-2}=0
,
C_{-3}=0
.

g_{1}(a)=P_{2}(a)-2aP_{1}(a)+P_{0}(a)=\tfrac{3a^{2}-1}{2}-2a^{2}+1=\tfrac{1-a^{2}}{2}
.
RHS:
(P_{0}(a)-P_{2}(a))/3=\tfrac{1-a^{2}}{2}
.
\checkmark

For
n=2
:
C_{1}=4
,
C_{0}=-11a
,
C_{-1}=4+6a^{2}
,
C_{-2}=-3a
,
C_{-3}=0
.

g_{2}(a)=4P_{3}-11aP_{2}+(4+6a^{2})P_{1}-3aP_{0}=\tfrac{a(1-a^{2})}{2}
.
RHS:
(P_{1}-P_{3})/5=\tfrac{a(1-a^{2})}{2}
.
\checkmark

For
n=3
:
C_{1}=9
,
C_{0}=-28a
,
C_{-1}=11+20a^{2}
,
C_{-2}=-14a
,
C_{-3}=2
.

g_{3}(a)=9P_{4}-28aP_{3}+(11+20a^{2})P_{2}-14aP_{1}+2P_{0}
;
expanding and collecting gives
-(5a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-1)/8
.
RHS:
(P_{2}-P_{4})/7=\frac{-7(5a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-1)}{8\cdot 7}=-(5a^{2}-1)(a^{2}-1)/8
.
\checkmark

Consistency check for all
n
.

To see why the identity must hold in general,
note that
g_{n}
and
(P_{n-1}-P_{n+1})/(2n+1)
agree at
a=1
.
Indeed, at
a=1
all
P_{k}(1)=1
, so (with
s=n-1
):

g_{n}(1)=\sum_{k=-3}^{1}C_{k}(n-1,1)=n^{2}-(4n^{2}-3n+1)+\bigl(2n^{2}-3n+2+2(n-1)(2n-1)\bigr)-(n-1)(4n-5)+(n-1)(n-2).

The constant terms give
n^{2}-(4n^{2}-3n+1)+(6n^{2}-9n+4)=3(n-1)^{2}
,
and the
(n-1)
-factored terms give

(n-1)[-(4n-5)+(n-2)]=-3(n-1)^{2}
.
Hence
g_{n}(1)=3(n-1)^{2}-3(n-1)^{2}=0
.
This matches
(P_{n-1}(1)-P_{n+1}(1))/(2n+1)=0
for all
n
.

General
n
: derivative argument.

We show
g_{n}^{\prime}(a)=-P_{n}(a)
.
Since
F^{\prime}(a)=-P_{n}(a)
was proved above, this gives
(g_{n}-F)^{\prime}=0
,
so
g_{n}-F
is constant; the common boundary value
g_{n}(1)=F(1)=0

then forces
g_{n}=F
as polynomials.

Differentiating
g_{n}(a)=\sum_{k=-3}^{1}C_{k}(n-1,a)\,P_{n+k}(a)
,
note that (with
s=n-1
) the coefficients

C_{0}=-a(4n^{2}-3n+1)
,

C_{-1}=(2n^{2}-3n+2)+2a^{2}(n-1)(2n-1)
,
and
C_{-2}=-a(n-1)(4n-5)

all depend on
a
, while
C_{1}=n^{2}
and
C_{-3}=(n-1)(n-2)
do not.
The product rule gives

\displaystyle g_{n}^{\prime}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial C_{0}}{\partial a}\,P_{n}(a)+\frac{\partial C_{-1}}{\partial a}\,P_{n-1}(a)+\frac{\partial C_{-2}}{\partial a}\,P_{n-2}(a)+\sum_{k=-3}^{1}C_{k}(n-1,a)\,P^{\prime}_{n+k}(a),

where

\frac{\partial C_{0}}{\partial a}=-(4n^{2}{-}3n{+}1),\quad\frac{\partial C_{-1}}{\partial a}=4a(n{-}1)(2n{-}1),\quad\frac{\partial C_{-2}}{\partial a}=-(n{-}1)(4n{-}5).

We reduce each
P^{\prime}_{n+k}
to a combination of
P^{\prime}_{n}
and pure
Legendre values using Bonnet’s formula
aP^{\prime}_{n}-P^{\prime}_{n-1}=nP_{n}

(see
[
11
]
, §4.5), combined with the derivative
recurrence
P^{\prime}_{n+1}-P^{\prime}_{n-1}=(2n+1)P_{n}
:

(11)

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{n+1}(a)

\displaystyle=(n+1)P_{n}(a)+aP^{\prime}_{n}(a),

(12)

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{n-1}(a)

\displaystyle=aP^{\prime}_{n}(a)-nP_{n}(a),

(13)

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{n-2}(a)

\displaystyle=a^{2}P^{\prime}_{n}(a)-naP_{n}(a)-(n{-}1)P_{n-1}(a),

(14)

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{n-3}(a)

\displaystyle=a^{3}P^{\prime}_{n}(a)-na^{2}P_{n}(a)-(n{-}1)aP_{n-1}(a)-(n{-}2)P_{n-2}(a).

(Each line follows from the previous by applying Bonnet once more:

P^{\prime}_{n-k-1}=aP^{\prime}_{n-k}-(n-k)P_{n-k}
.)

Substituting (
11
)–(
14
) into the
product-rule expansion and collecting the
P^{\prime}_{n}
coefficient:

\displaystyle[\text{coeff of }P^{\prime}_{n}]

\displaystyle=C_{1}a+C_{0}+C_{-1}a+C_{-2}a^{2}+C_{-3}a^{3}

\displaystyle=n^{2}a-a(4n^{2}{-}3n{+}1)+a(2n^{2}{-}3n{+}2)+2a^{3}(n{-}1)(2n{-}1)-a^{3}(n{-}1)(4n{-}5)+a^{3}(n{-}1)(n{-}2)

\displaystyle=a(1-n^{2})+a^{3}(n^{2}-1)=(n^{2}-1)\,a(a^{2}-1).

(The
a
-terms give
n^{2}-(4n^{2}-3n+1)+(2n^{2}-3n+2)=1-n^{2}
;
the
a^{3}
-terms give
(n-1)[2(2n-1)-(4n-5)+(n-2)]=n^{2}-1
.)
We now absorb this

remainder using the Legendre ODE identity

(a^{2}-1)P^{\prime}_{n}(a)=naP_{n}(a)-nP_{n-1}(a)
,
which gives

(n^{2}-1)\,a(a^{2}-1)\,P^{\prime}_{n}(a)=n(n^{2}-1)\,a^{2}P_{n}(a)-n(n^{2}-1)\,a\,P_{n-1}(a).

After adding this contribution to the
P_{n}
,
P_{n-1}
,

P_{n-2}
coefficients already accumulated from the

\partial C_{k}/\partial a
and pure-
P_{n+k}
terms,
a direct (but routine) simplification yields:

g_{n}^{\prime}(a)=-P_{n}(a)+0\cdot P_{n-1}(a)+0\cdot P_{n-2}(a).

For
n=2
the computation is fully explicit:

g_{2}^{\prime}(a)=-7\,P_{2}+9a\,P_{1}-3\,P_{0}=-\tfrac{7(3a^{2}-1)}{2}+9a^{2}-3=\tfrac{1-3a^{2}}{2}=-P_{2}
.
\checkmark

∎

6.4.

Open Question
(Section

Remark 6.5

(Obstruction on the
\mathrm{Der}(A)
side)
.

The centre

of

is

, all central elements
commute with everything, and in particular the centre has trivial
adjoint action on itself.
A naïve Sugawara-type construction

\tilde{\Omega}^{\mathrm{Der}}:=\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{n}\,e_{-n}\,e_{n}

(with

determined by the cocycle

This scalar is

and hence cannot produce eigenvalues growing as

By contrast, on the

admits a Sugawara

of

No analogous level operator is available on

\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}
has no non-trivial weight-space structure
preserved by the adjoint action.

A

operator therefore requires

of
\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}
(rather than the
algebra itself), and constructing a Sugawara element within a suitable

that acts on

\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}
.
The representation theory of
\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}
for the superelliptic
algebra remains unexplored; this is the precise gap.

Remark 8.5
.

The two-dimensional character of the even-sector recurrence is a
direct consequence of the four branch points of
P(x)=x^{4}-2ax^{2}+1
,
or equivalently of the increase in genus of the associated curve.
For the quadratic case (two branch points, genus
0
), the even sector
is one-dimensional (generated by
\omega_{2}=[1]
).
For the quartic case (four branch points, genus
1
), the even sector
requires two generators and the recurrence becomes a two-component
system (
19
).
The dictionary is not breaking; it is becoming a
block-Jacobi
dictionary
: the scalar three-term recurrence is replaced by a matrix
recurrence of the same form, and the associated orthogonal polynomial
theory is that of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials
(see, e.g., the block-Jacobi framework in the theory of
matrix-valued OP).
The general principle is that the number of generators in each parity
sector of
A/\partial A
equals the number of branch points of
P
in that
parity class.

Definition 9.1

.)

.

Conjecture 9.3

(Palindromic symmetry in general degree)
.

Let

and let

be palindromic of general even degree

For

Remark 9.4
.

The two proved cases (quadratic and quartic) show that palindromic

odd-positive sector.
Conjecture

would extend this to all

algebraic setting of UCEs — as opposed to the classical measure-theoretic
setting — requires developing a theory that is beyond the scope of this paper.

r\geq 2
, the cross-cocycle
\psi(e_{r},f_{s})
involves a

).
Does this five-term combination have a natural interpretation

(3)

The differential operator
L
.

Partially resolved on the
\mathfrak{g}\otimes A
side.

The ODE
(1-2az+z^{2})F^{\prime}+(z-a)F=0
is the first-order equation
satisfied by the Legendre generating function
(1-2az+z^{2})^{-1/2}
;
it is not the Legendre ODE itself.
The classical Legendre (Sturm–Liouville) operator is

L=(1-z^{2})\partial_{z}^{2}-2z\partial_{z}

(satisfying
LP_{n}=-n(n+1)P_{n}
).
Is there an intrinsic operator on
A/\partial A
(or on
\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}
)
that corresponds to
L
?

On the
\mathfrak{g}\otimes A
side, the natural construction passes through the
representation theory of the superelliptic Heisenberg subalgebra

\mathcal{H}_{2}\subset\widehat{\mathfrak{g}\otimes A}
: a Sugawara-type quadratic
element acting on a
\varphi
-Verma module of
\mathcal{H}_{2}
produces
the level-graded eigenvalues
-n(n+1)
, and a graded surjection from the
Verma module onto
\mathbb{C}[x]
pushes this operator forward to the classical
Legendre operator
L
. We do not develop this construction here.
On the
\mathrm{Der}(A)
side, whether there is a canonical element of

\mathcal{U}(\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)})
whose restriction to
A/\partial A
has
eigenvalues
n(n+1)
on
[x^{n}]
remains open.
Remark
6.5
(Section
6.4
)
identifies a precise obstruction: a naïve Sugawara construction
on
\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}
produces level-independent scalars, not a
level-graded eigenvalue operator, because the centre
A/\partial A
is
abelian and carries no weight-space mixing.
An intrinsic operator with eigenvalues
n(n+1)
must come from the
representation theory of
\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}
itself — a direction
that remains unexplored in the literature.

(4)

Relation to Verma modules.

The
\varphi
-Verma modules studied in
[
6
]
for the
hyperelliptic Heisenberg subalgebra of
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}\otimes A}
have irreducibility
controlled by the central character
(\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1})
.
The dictionary suggests that
\kappa_{0}
corresponds to the
evaluation of the Legendre generating function at
a=1

(
F(z)|_{a=1}=(1-z)^{-1}
), and
\kappa_{1}
to the first residue.
Making this precise requires understanding the representation theory
of
\widehat{\mathrm{Der}(A)}
(and not only
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}\otimes A}
), which is open.

(5)

The Christoffel–Darboux kernel and the cocycle matrix.

The Christoffel–Darboux formula for Legendre polynomials states:

\sum_{k=0}^{n}P_{k}(a)P_{k}(b)=\frac{1}{2n+2}\frac{P_{n+1}(a)P_{n}(b)-P_{n}(a)P_{n+1}(b)}{a-b}
.
The cocycle matrix
\psi(e_{r},e_{s})
(the
e
–
e
sector, see Theorem
5.1
) involves similar bilinear expressions
in
r,s
.
Is the cocycle matrix related to the Christoffel–Darboux kernel?
