Overview
The December 2025 roadshow gathered rapid feedback from community members on the
proof of concept AmSC/HPDF data lakehouse and catalog solution1. This provided the
opportunity to demonstrate our current state of progress and gather input on workflows
and AI agents. We recognize that the proofs of concept interfaces (OpenMetadata and Goose)
are not intended for our end users so we have captured feedback to keep in mind as additional
technical and conceptual design work is undertaken.

Five conversations were held with individuals spanning SLAC (three participants), The
Materials Project, DESI, LHC-ATLAS, and GRETA. Individuals lead or support data
infrastructure and releases.

General Data Lakehouse & Catalog Feedback
 ●​ Interest in registering public datasets was fairly strong. Some participants proposed
 using specific datasets to test functionality.
 ●​ Requests for information on data providers’ responsibilities and how they should
 expect to interface with AmSC and HPDF infrastructure. Users need to know what
 format and documentation requirements there are to register data in the
 lakehouse.
 ○​ This is at least partially addressed via a January 2026 AmSC document2.
 ●​ Some potential collaborators are concerned they do not have the disk space to
 reformat and store lakehouse-ready data (e.g., large amounts of parquet files). To
 onboard as many projects as possible, space should be dedicated to address these
 needs.
 ●​ Uncertainty around who will provide AmSC API. We need this information to share
 publicly as it defines how data is put in and pulled out.

Evaluation Summary
The HPDF Requirements and Workflow Readiness team identified and categorized usability
issues in the November/December 2025 proof of concept data lakehouse and catalog. The
team applied Jakob Nielsen's 10 heuristics for interaction design, comparing the proof of
concept interface to the heuristic ideals in a structured evaluation. Nielsen’s heuristics
indicate best practices for user interfaces. Adhering to their ideals reduces cognitive load
for users and simplifies interaction with a system. Conducting a heuristic evaluation allows
you to systematically identify usability issues in your product. By adopting this evaluation
framework, reviewer bias can be reduced and attention is directed toward identifying and
addressing common usability issues (see Appendix: Understanding Heuristic Evaluations
for more). HPDF’s heuristic evaluations identify opportunities to better adhere to the
heuristic ideals, improving the usability of the lakehouse catalog.

Both a user interface (UI) constructed with OpenMetadata and an agentic interface using
Goose were evaluated. It was assumed that data stewards are likely to be the primary
users of these interfaces. We also considered that scientific end-users focused on data
discovery may also interact with each UI (Openmetadata and Goose). Thus, we have
captured feedback to keep in mind as additional technical/conceptual design work is
undertaken for both user groups. We also recognize that this was a rapid proof of concept
and not intended for substantial end user access. We summarize the usability challenges
identified and recommendations for HPDF to improve user experience as the lakehouse
infrastructure is further developed—either via a purpose-built interface or customization of
the UIs evaluated. Following this summary, we include detailed notes from the heuristic
evaluations of both interfaces.
Summary of Challenges & Opportunities
Two key value propositions that HPDF can offer in the context of data catalog and
lakehouse users are to 1) reduce effort and complexity for teams delivering services and
end users building workflows and 2) provide resources for enabling increased data
stewardship within and among DOE communities. These value propositions speak to
providing usable experiences for end-users and infrastructure providers, as well as offering
new infrastructure and best practices to improve data stewardship efforts. Our heuristic
evaluations of the proof of concept data lakehouse catalog and agentic interface primarily
identify high priority issues relevant to these value propositions. By addressing these
issues, HPDF can offer data stewards simple, easy to use interfaces that allow them to
streamline existing workflows. Below we summarize top challenges and key
recommendations for consideration; further detail and examples are available in Heuristic

Challenges and Recommendations for the Lakehouse Data Catalog with
OpenMetadata UI
 -​ Ambiguous Nested Entities: Ambiguous naming of entities and their properties,
 combined with their complex modeling in OpenMetadata, causes confusion about
 what entities are and complicates navigation. Entity names are non-obvious (e.g.,
 “glue,” “table,” “children”) and distinct entities have the same or very similar names
 (e.g., edx_catalog and edx_catalog and EDX Data Catalog)
 -​ Recommendation: HPDF should leverage early partnerships to develop a more
 intuitive and simplified view model for displaying registered entities in
 OpenMetadata.
 -​ Recommendation: HPDF can mitigate differences among domain specific
 schemas by offering role or domain specific views of the lakehouse interface since
 an intuitive universal schema is likely to be too high level.
 -​ Impeded Discoverability and Navigation: The current interface makes it difficult
 for users to find what they need. For instance, related resources are not directly
 linked, forcing users to perform new searches to find connections. Key navigational
 aids like a clear "home page" for a catalog are missing.
 -​ Recommendation: The HPDF catalog should enable users to quickly find their
 most valued resources—catalog/repository home pages, recently viewed
 resources, and suggested searches or datasets.
 -​ Cluttered Layout: The interface often displays irrelevant or empty metadata fields,
 cluttering the view with non-essential information, increasing cognitive load. There
 are also frequent layout issues, such as confusingly located information panels and
 improper text wrapping, that detract from the user experience. Visual noise makes
 the interface harder to interpret at a glance.
 -​ Recommendation: The HPDF catalog should offer an uncluttered interface which
 will improve users’ ability to quickly interpret information. The portal should show
 no irrelevant metadata panels and ensure relevant ones are near the objects they
 describe.

Challenges and Recommendations for an Agentic AI Interface
 -​ Low Visibility into the System Status: The Goose agent interface refers to queries
 ambiguously, sometimes fails without immediate notification, and hides its actions
 behind generic "thinking" messages, making it hard for the user to understand what

 9

 is happening. Increased transparency can help users verify that the expected query
 is being conducted, an especially important consideration when users have different
 access permissions.
 -​ Recommendation: HPDF should better highlight its existing transparency features
 by expanding the collapsable Tool Details information the first time it appears,
 highlighting its availability.
 -​ Recommendation: HPDF can use colored text to alert users to emergent issues;
 red text should indicate an issue has been discovered.
 -​ Recommendation: Because queries can take long periods of time to complete,
 HPDF can offer users a picture-in-picture window to monitor progress while
 completing other tasks.
-​ Low Visibility into Capabilities: The agent’s capabilities are hidden—when
 beginning a session. A user needs cues for what kinds of queries they could perform
 using the system.
 -​ Recommendation: HPDF should offer example queries at session start and
 suggested follow up queries when a task is completed.
-​ Limited User Control: The user's freedom to pursue new lines of inquiry is limited.
 There is no way to easily edit a query the agent has constructed or to fork the
 conversation to explore an alternative path. Users need to start an entirely new
 query instead.
 -​ Recommendation: Investigate users’ need for editing queries. Anticipate the need
 to provide an option to stop the agent’s thinking and directly edit a query the
 agent constructed (forking the conversation). A copy button next to the query
 may ensure users do not have to type it out themselves.
 -​ Recommendation: HPDF should enable users to fork a conversation, retaining its
 history up until that point but allowing new prompts after that.
-​ Inherited Schema/Terminology Problems: The agent uses some of the same
 confusing terms found in the catalog UI and uses some key terms ("data," "dataset,"
 "container") inconsistently, perpetuating that core usability issue.
 -​ Recommendation: Establish intuitive names for lakehouse entities and develop
 clear definitions for these. Ensure agents do not use distinct terms
 interchangeably.
-​ Disconnection from the Data Catalog: When the agent returns information about
 a dataset, it doesn't provide a direct hyperlink, preventing the user from easily
 validating the response or exploring the data further in the catalog UI.
 -​ Recommendation: When an agent points users to a specific dataset, include a
 hyperlink.

Approach
The HPDF Requirements and Workflow Readiness team used the technical team’s recorded
demos to identify interface, language, and workflow elements that could better align with
Jakob Nielsen's 10 heuristics for interaction design. It was assumed that data stewards are
likely to be the primary users of these interfaces, but we considered that scientific
end-users may also interact with each UI. Each opportunity for improvement was assigned
to the heuristic (i.e., design principle) that it was most relevant to, though some
opportunities relate to multiple heuristics. For each opportunity, an issue and
recommendation is described. Screenshots and examples were included to aid
interpretation of results. Issues were each assigned a color to indicate the analyst’s
perception of how disruptive they are:

 Red cells indicate high priority, very disruptive usability issues

 Orange cells indicate moderate priority, somewhat disruptive
 usability issues

 Yellow cells indicate moderate priority, lightly disruptive
 usability issues

 White cells indicate a low priority, very lightly disruptive usability
 issue

Appendix: Understanding Heuristic Evaluations

Nielsen’s 10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design indicate best practices for user
interfaces; adhering to their ideals reduces cognitive load1 for users and simplifies
interaction with a system. By adopting this evaluation framework, reviewer bias can be
reduced and attention is directed toward systematically identifying and addressing
common usability issues. Heuristic evaluations focus on how features are delivered,
ensuring their easy adoption and utility to novice and expert users alike. Heuristic
evaluations do not assess if a given feature is desirable by a user, nor do they comment on
aesthetic choices that are unlikely to affect usability (though issues like layout or color
scheme can impact usability and may be commented on in a heuristic evaluation if they
negatively affect cognitive load). Heuristic evaluations can be applied to graphical user
interfaces, but are appropriate for other interfaces as well (e.g., command line or API).

Trained reviewers can complete an evaluation, noting occasions where the guidelines are
violated. These issues are understood as possible occasions where usability can be
hindered, though real-world users may not always recognize these issues themselves
(Bailey, Allen, & Raiello, 1992). Nevertheless, because heuristic evaluations are a quick way
to evaluate usability and do not require special data collection or analysis tools—only the
system to be evaluated and a place to take notes—they are a useful way to rapidly identify
needed improvements to a user experience.

The HPDF team uses the STRUDEL project’s Heuristic Evaluation Template.

1
 Cognitive load is commonly understood as “the extent to which cognitive resources are taken up by
activities that facilitate learning” (Quiroga, Crosby, & Iding, 2004); reducing this load by removing
unnecessary complication or content is thought to improve learning and work.
