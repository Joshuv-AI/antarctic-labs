Fragmented Movements, Connected Opponents: Analyzing the Interconnectivity of Firms and Environmental Justice Organizations in Global Socio-Environmental Conflicts
Dario Cottafava, José R. Nicolás-Carlock, Marcel Llavero-Pasquina

Abstract
This study investigates the interconnectivity of firms and Environmental Justice Organizations (EJOs) involved in socio-environmental conflicts worldwide, using data from the Environmental Justice Atlas (EJAtlas). By constructing a multilayer network that links firms, conflicts, and EJOs, the research applies social network analysis to evaluate the simultaneous involvement of these actors across multiple disputes. Both projected networks of firms and EJOs have been analysed by aggregating nodes by categories and countries to reveal structural differences between global networks of actors. Findings reveal a stark contrast between the interconnectedness of firms and EJOs. Multinational corporations form a cohesive global network, enabling them to coordinate strategies and exert influence across regions. Conversely, EJOs are fragmented, often operating in isolated clusters with limited interconnection but forming a robust, decentralized and self-organized global network. Firms network present a strong dependence on pertaining conflict category while EJOs network does not depend on conflict category. This structural difference suggests a risk of systemic and structural coordination for firms towards exploitative expansion while EJOs dynamics seems to be led by a white blood cells defense-like mechanism. While fragmentation may represents a critical challenge for social movements, decentralization and self-organization show a more diffuse global networks supported by a limited number of central hub able to build stronger global alliances to effectively counter the power dynamics of transnational corporations. By providing robust evidence of these asymmetrical networks, this research contributes to discuss how structural differences in global coordination for companies and EJOs directly derives as an emergent properties depending on the purpose of the network itself, sectorial expansion for firms while ecosystem preservation for EJOs.

Starting form the EJAtlas grandfather question ”is there a global EJ movement?”
(

, in this study we aim to answer:
(A) What are the main properties of the global network both for EJOs and firms? and (B) What are the main structural differences between the those networks at global level and across sectors?

To analyse the global network of firms and EJOs involved in socio-environmental conflicts, in this study, we analysed the projected networks - i.e, the projection starting from the bi-partite networks, conflicts-company and conflicts-EJOs - of company and EJOs. Through the use of common centrality degrees
18

The projected networks have been obtained starting from the hypothesis of co-engagement of actors in socio-environmental conflicts documented in the EJAtlas. Specifically, we draw an edge between two actors whenever those two actors co-occurred in the same conflicts.

Findings reveal how the two networks are structurally different. The company network is more densely connected with many companies with a high degree, i.e., a high number of conflicts where they are involved and consequently a high number of co-occurrence with other companies, while the EJOs network is composed by EJOs engaged in fewer number of conflicts with some evident central hubs represented by a limited number of transnational EJOs. Despite this difference, the largest connected component (LCC) in the EJOs network connect more than the 80% of the whole network, while in the corporations network the giant is composed by only the 60% of all the nodes, revealing how a environmental justice global movement exists although with structural differences with respect to the corporations network.

Methodology

In this study, we apply a network perspective to examine the co-engagement of actors in socio-environmental conflicts
(
5
)
documented in the EJAtlas
(
44
)
. Specifically, we focus on two key categories of actors: companies and environmental justice organizations. Companies frequently appear as project developers, investors, or operators in environmentally contested activities, particularly in sectors such as mining, fossil fuels, and large-scale infrastructure. Environmental justice organizations, by contrast, represent networks of civil society actors—including NGOs, grassroots organizations, and transnational advocacy groups—that mobilize to defend environmental rights, local livelihoods, and ecological integrity

44
;
58

By constructing and analyzing projected networks of corporation–corporation and EJO–EJO co-participation in environmental conflicts, this paper investigates the structural properties of these two actor groups and compares their patterns of connectivity, clustering, and community formation. Through this analysis, we seek to understand whether corporate actors and environmental justice organizations exhibit different forms of network organization within global environmental conflicts.

4
Discussion

4.1
Is there a global environmental justice movement?

The question of whether a global environmental justice movement exists has been central to the EJAtlas literature from its own conception
(
43
)
. Our findings provide a nuanced answer to support the existence of a global robust, resilient and emergent EJ movement. Moreover, while a global EJ movement does emerge structurally, it exhibits a fundamentally different network organization compared to corporate actors.

From a network perspective, the EJOs network is characterized by a high level of fragmentation combined with the presence of a large connected component encompassing more than 80% of all the EJOs. This result suggests the coexistence of two seemingly contradictory dynamics: on the one hand, EJOs are predominantly embedded in localized clusters, reflecting the place-based nature of environmental conflicts; on the other hand, a limited number of highly central organizations act as bridges connecting otherwise disconnected regions. These actors, often transnational or international EJOs (see Fig.
8
), could play a crucial role in sustaining the global coherence of the movement by facilitating information flows and coordination across geographically distant struggles. A stress and robustness test should be done in future studies to verify the effective role of these actors in connecting different disconnected components and clusters of EJOs
(
9
)
.

This structure is consistent with the conceptualization that local struggles are globally interconnected through shared narratives, and common struggles in an emergent way
(
39
;
60
)
. In this sense, the global EJ movement does not resemble neither a coordinated structure nor a highly clustered core group as, instead, it emerges in the company network (see Fig.
5
and
6
), but rather it presents an emergent, decentralized system shaped by relational dynamics across conflicts.

In contrast, the corporations network displays a more cohesive and densely interconnected structure, with multiple actors exhibiting high degree and forming tightly clustered communities (see Fig.
5
). This reflects the systemic nature of global production networks and transnational corporate structures
(
61
;
17
)
, where firms operate across multiple regions and sectors, often participating in several conflicts simultaneously. Such structural cohesion suggests a higher capacity for coordination, and strategic alignment among corporate actors with a strong risk to reproduce worst and illicit practices across different regions and countries. Further investigation and analysis, both in depth through case studies
(
47
)
, sectorial analysis
(
4
)
or via structured risk and corruption indicators
(
51
;
50
;
42
;
19
;
64
)
are needed.

The comparison between the two networks highlights a clear asymmetry of structural power. While corporations form a relatively integrated and highly clustered global system (see Fig.
5
) according to the belonging sector (see Fig.
6
), EJOs’ clusters remain more independent by the belonging sector. This asymmetry may reflect broader dynamics of ecological distribution conflicts
(
45
)
and unequal exchange
(
34
)
, where economic power is concentrated in transnational corporate networks, whereas resistance is often rooted in localized, and place-based struggles that can be derived and reacted from the widely known NIMBY (Not In My Backyard) phenomenon or from more structural social phenomena as described by
56
as
enlightening process
, i.e., a context-specific dynamics depending on different types of capital, namely social, knowledge, patrimonial and political. In this sense, it is crucial in future to conduct case by case analyses to identify the emergence of shared values and common cultures across the EJ global movements, i.e., a
social movement scene
,
a common identity
or a common set of belief, values and norms
(
25
)
.

At the same time, the presence of a large connected component in the EJO network provides empirical support to the hypothesis of a global EJ movement. However, this movement should be understood as an emergent property of a complex adaptive system
(
37
;
26
)
, rather than as a formally organized global actor. Its structure, indeed, is shaped by co-engagement in conflicts, and shared environmental injustice episodes. These structural differences could be direct consequences of a deep difference in nature between corporations’ and EJOs’ inner and intrinsic purpose. The former acts based on their exploitative and sectorial nature, while the latter reacts based on shared values and common believes (so, not on a specific sector).

Finally, the country- and region-level aggregations further reinforce this interpretation. While corporate networks reproduce patterns of ecologically unequal exchange
(
34
)
and global North–South dependencies
(
41
;
15
)
, EJOs tend to remain more territorially embedded, with limited transnational connectivity. In this sense, the role of international EJOs becomes therefore critical in enabling the emergence of a global movement.

An emergent and distributed global movement.

While international EJOs play a key role in connecting geographically distant conflicts, our findings suggest that they are not the sole drivers of global connectivity within the EJ network. Rather, the global environmental justice movement emerges as a distributed and self-organizing system. Local EJOs, even when primarily engaged in place-based struggles, contribute to the global structure through shared participation in conflicts, and the diffusion of knowledge, and narratives across cases. This results in a form of emergent
globality
, where the overall coherence of the network does not depend exclusively on centralized or transnational actors, but arises from the accumulation of local interactions and relational ties. This consideration is supported by the high inter sectorial edge frequency sign of the emergent property in the EJO–EJO network to connect different sectors. In addition, the EJO–EJO network shows a more robust and resilient LCC and structure even within the same cluster (see Fig.
7
). Such a configuration is consistent with theories of complex adaptive systems, where global patterns emerge from decentralized interactions without requiring hierarchical coordination
(
37
;
26
)
. In this sense, international EJOs act as important accelerators or amplifiers of connectivity, but the underlying structure of the movement remains fundamentally distributed, and rooted in local struggles.

Divergent actor logics and structural organization.

The structural differences observed between corporate actors and EJOs can be further interpreted in light of their fundamentally different organizational logics and objectives. Corporations are primarily oriented toward the development and expansion of infrastructure and production systems within specific sectors, such as mining, energy, or transport. This sectoral specialization is reflected in the strong clustering patterns observed in Fig.
7
, where companies tend to co-occur within similar categories of conflicts, indicating coordinated or parallel involvement in projects characterized by comparable technological and economic logics. Such a structure suggests a tendency toward increasing global integration, where corporate actors expand their activities across regions while maintaining sectoral coherence, reinforcing patterns of coordination and accumulation at global scale.

In contrast, EJOs operate according to a fundamentally different rationale. Their primary objective is not expansion, but the protection of specific territories, and communities affected by environmental harm. As a result, their emergence is often reactive and context-dependent, triggered by localized instances of environmental injustice. This gives rise to a network structure that resembles a
distributed response system
, where EJOs appear and mobilize in specific locations without central coordination. In this sense, EJOs can be metaphorically understood as
white blood cells of socio-ecological systems
, acting as decentralized guardians that respond to threats as they arise. This analogy highlights both the strengths and limitations of the movement: while it enables rapid and context-sensitive responses, it also contributes to fragmentation and limits the capacity for coordinated action at global scale. The coexistence of these two logics—expansive and coordinated for corporations, reactive and distributed for EJOs—ultimately shapes the asymmetric structure of the global environmental conflict network.

4.2
Policy and Managerial Implications

The structural differences identified between corporate and EJO networks have important implications both for policy-making and corporate risk management as well as for environmental justice movement coordination. Therefore, this study may support ethical practices not only for policy and decision-makers but also for corporations who want to act ethically and avoid to be involved in scandal and conflicts.

Systemic corruption.

First and foremost, beyond the discussed structural asymmetries, observed patterns point toward the existence of systemic forms of corruption embedded within global networks of environmental conflicts. Rather than being reducible to isolated illegal practices, corruption can be understood as an emergent property of interconnected systems of actors, and institutions

4.3
Limitations and Future Research

This study presents several limitations that open avenues for future research. While the EJAtlas provides a uniquely rich empirical basis for analyzing ecological distribution conflicts
(
45
)
, and unequal exchange at the global scale
(
41
)
, it also presents important constraints that must be acknowledged.

First, the database relies on a crowdmapping approach, which may introduce geographical and informational biases, with stronger representation in regions where research networks and activist documentation are more developed, particularly in Latin America, Europe, and North America
(
45
)
. Second, the presence of actors within the same conflict reflects co-involvement in a contested socio-environmental process rather than formal, or legal relationships, limiting the possibility to infer a real coordination, or power dynamics
(
5
)
. As a consequence, the network structures analyzed in this study capture patterns of co-occurrence rather than explicit relational ties. Third, the EJAtlas encompasses a wide spectrum of conflicts—from cases involving violence and repression to legal disputes and public mobilizations—making it difficult to assume homogeneity in the nature or intensity of interactions across cases
(
43
;
58
)
. Finally, the aggregation and classification procedures adopted in this study (e.g., country attribution, or network projections) inevitably simplify the heterogeneity of actors (e.g. no difference in size has been study) and their involvement in conflict (e.g. legal dispute or active social movements) and may hide or underrepresent real dynamics.

5
Conclusion

This study aimed to explore the global structure of environmental justice conflicts by adopting a network perspective on the EJAtlas database, with the objective of identifying patterns of co-occurrence and structural organization among actors to compare and identify similarities and structural differences between the company and the environmental justice organization network.

Methodologically, we combined a bipartite network approach with successive projections (co-occurrence in conflicts) and aggregation procedures (belonging to same category or country), allowing for a multi-level quantitative analysis of the global networks. The results reveal highly skewed and unequal network structures with structural differences between the company and the environmental justice organization network. Specifically, the company network reveal high densely and sectorial clusters of companies with many shared conflicts and high degree, suggesting for a systemic and structured coordination. On the contrary EJOs network reveal to be global and distributed in nature with a limited number of hubs with a high degree and acting at a transnational level. Moreover, the EJOs network suggest a more globalized and connected network with more than 80% of total EJOs belonging to the largest connected components (against the 59% of the companies).

Therefore, to answer to the EJAtlas’s father question: is there a global EJ movement? According to findings from this study, yes there exist a global self-organized, distributed global movement. Concluding, these quantitative findings are further complemented by qualitative insights, which highlight the coexistence of two fundamentally different logics: a coordinated, expansion-driven corporate network operating at a global scale, and a decentralized, place-based, and reactive network of EJOs emerging locally in response to environmental conflicts.
