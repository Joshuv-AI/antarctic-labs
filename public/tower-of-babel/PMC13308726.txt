Bolus versus Continuous Infusion of Esketamine for Prevention of Postpartum Depression After Caesarean Delivery: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Controlled Trial
Authors: Jie Yang, Qiqi Yan, Ting Zhang, Yaoyue Hu, Fangliang Peng, Ruihan Zhao, Meiling Deng, Li Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsketamine is increasingly being applied for preventing postpartum depression (PPD). However, clinical protocols for this application are inconsistent, particularly concerning the route of administration. The aim was to determine whether esketamine bolus administration differed from continuous infusion in terms of preventing PPD.MethodsA total of 503 subjects without prenatal depression undergoing caesarean delivery were included in this superiority trial and randomly allocated to three groups: the esketamine bolus group received a bolus injection of 0.25 mg/kg esketamine, the esketamine infusion group was administered a continuous infusion of 0.25 mg/kg esketamine, while the control group was given an equivalent volume of normal saline. Primary outcome was set as the incidence of PPD at 6 weeks postpartum. The positive screening of PPD was defined as Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) with scores of more than 10. Incidence of PPD at 1 and 4 weeks postpartum, pain scores and all adverse events were also assessed.ResultsEsketamine bolus administration significantly reduced the incidence of PPD compared with control group (9.15% vs 19.33%; RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.25–0.91). However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the bolus group and the infusion group (9.15% vs 11.54%; RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.40–1.57). There were no significant differences in PPD incidence at 1 and 4 weeks postpartum among the three groups. Similarly, the analgesic effects were comparable across the three groups. Regarding adverse events, less dizziness and nausea/vomiting during surgery was revealed with esketamine bolus administration. Overall, subjects receiving esketamine bolus administration also experienced fewer intraoperative adverse events than those in the infusion group (45.75% vs 65.38%, RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.56–0.95).ConclusionFor patients without prenatal depression undergoing caesarean delivery, esketamine bolus administration or continuous infusion demonstrated comparable efficacy for preventing PPD, bolus administration may be the preferred route due to a lower incidence of adverse events.

Introduction

Postpartum depression (PPD), a moderate-to-severe depressive episode with onset typically within six weeks of delivery,1 affects an estimated 19.18% of new mothers, with reported rates ranging from 3% to 44%.2 PPD is a major contributor to maternal morbidity and mortality3 and can lead to adverse outcomes including maternal social dysfunction, strained family relationships, and negative impacts on infant development.4 It has been confirmed that caesarean delivery is a risk factor for PPD.5 Consequently, this population is currently receiving increased attention regarding PPD prevention.

Ketamine or esketamine (dextro-isomer of ketamine) has been shown to exert a rapid antidepressant effect.6,7 Consequently, an increasing number of researchers are beginning to explore the potential of ketamine or esketamine in the prevention of postpartum depression (PPD). To date, 16 randomized clinical trials (RCTs) addressing this topic have been published, but the conclusions drawn from these trials are inconsistent. Beyond differences in the enrolled populations and implementation of research protocols, a critical methodological inconsistency lies in the route of esketamine administration, which may directly influence its efficacy in preventing PPD.8 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first published article on the antidepressant effects of ketamine indicated that it was infused intravenously over a period of 40 minutes.9,10 Thus, this approach has also been adopted and applied to the prevention of PPD in several subsequent studies.11 Meanwhile, some researchers have begun to explore alternative administration routes for ketamine/esketamine, such as intravenous bolus administration,12 continuous infusion13 or its use in patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA).14 However, the optimal administration routes of esketamine for the prevention of PPD have received little systematic study. This knowledge gap limits evidence-based clinical decision-making regarding the optimal route.

In light of the potential risk of ketamine/esketamine exposure to infants via breast milk,15 the continuous PCIA route was restricted although serious adverse events with PCIA usage have not been reported in published articles.16,17 Pharmacologically, intravenous bolus rapidly achieves peak plasma concentrations, whereas continuous infusion yields slower onset and more stable levels. These differences may lead to divergent antidepressant efficacy, analgesic effects, and adverse event profiles. In a previous animal experiment, a significant difference was observed in the analgesic effects and side effects when ketamine was administered via bolus injection compared to infusion.18 However, no study has directly compared bolus versus continuous infusion of esketamine for PPD prevention. Accordingly, the optimal administration route remains unknown, and high-quality comparative data are urgently needed to guide clinical practice.

To address this concern, we conducted a randomized controlled trial to investigate the efficacy and tolerability of esketamine administrated by bolus injection versus infusion for preventing PPD among women with caesarean delivery. We hypothesized that esketamine administered as an intravenous bolus might be associated with a lower incidence of PPD, as measured by the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), compared with intravenous infusion.

Materials and Methods

The single-centered, randomized, double-blinded, three-arm trial was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between April 2024 and April 2025. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Number: 2024–111-03, date: 9 April 2024, president: Peng Zheng). Eligible patients were informed about the study and provided with informed consent before surgery. The study was registered at the Clinical Trial Registry (No. ChiCTR2400082919). All procedures were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and CONSORT statement.

Subjects

Full-term pregnant women aged 20–45 years with singleton pregnancies who were admitted for caesarean deliveries under spinal anesthesia were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria were: (1) conditions contraindicating esketamine, such as severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases, severe gestational hypertension, preeclampsia or eclampsia, intragastric, intraocular, or intracranial hypertension, and uncontrolled hyperthyroidism; (2) a history of mental illness, including a positive screen for prenatal depression (EPDS score ≥10); (3) allergy to local anesthetics; (4) a history of drug abuse; (5) intellectual disability; (6) severe obstetric complications.

Randomization and Blinding

Eligible patients were randomly assigned to the following groups using computer-generated block randomisation with a block size of 6: esketamine administrated by bolus injection or continuous infusion or saline. Group assignments were sealed in opaque envelopes.

Before anesthesia, an assistant, unblinded to the protocol, opened the envelopes according to the recruitment sequence, and the agent was prepared either 0.25 mg/kg esketamine diluted in 20 mL saline (for bolus or infusion groups) or 20 mL saline alone, using a 20-mL syringe for each patient. The assistant would not be involved in participant recruitment, eligibility assessment, anesthesia management, postoperative follow-up, outcome assessment, or statistical analysis. Outcomes assessors were blinded to the group assessments. The anesthesiologists and healthcare team members performing the caesarean delivery would be blinded to treatment allocation and were not involved in the follow-up surveys.

Perioperative Care and Group Assignment

All the subjects received standard maternal care upon hospital admission, including routine preoperative preparation and education. Anesthesiologists conducted a preoperative visit one day before surgery. Intraspinal anesthesia was conducted by a senior anesthesiologist for each patient. Upon admission to the operating room, electrocardiography, blood pressure, and pulse oxygen saturation were monitored, and low-flow oxygen (3 L/min) was administered to all patients.

Patients were placed in the lateral position, and the L3-L4 interspace was chosen for puncture. A dose of 15–20 mg 1% ropivacaine was administrated, targeting a sensory block level of T6 to T4. A senior obstetrician performed the surgery via a transverse lower uterine segment incision. Furthermore, other surgical treatment, such as B-lynch suture or uterine balloon tamping or forceps, was performed by the obstetrician when necessary. Uterotonic was injected into the uterus to enhance uterine contractility. If necessary, vasopressors or volume therapy were used for hemodynamic stabilization. Immediately after delivery and umbilical cord clamping, patients in the bolus group received 0.25 mg/kg of esketamine via intravenous bolus in 20 mL over 20 seconds, followed by a 20 mL intravenous infusion of 0.9% saline over 20 minutes. Patients in the infusion group received 20 mL of 0.9% saline via intravenous bolus over 20 seconds, followed by an intravenous infusion of 0.25 mg/kg of esketamine in a total volume of 20 mL over 20 minutes. Subjects in control group received 20 mL of 0.9% saline via intravenous injection over 20 seconds, followed by a 20 mL intravenous infusion of 0.9% saline over 20 minutes. At the end of surgery, all patients received an ultrasound-guided transversus abdominis plane block. Additionally, the patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) was provided to each patient at the end of surgery. The PCIA solution contained 1 mg fentanyl and 10 mg dexamethasone, diluted with saline to a total volume of 100 mL. The PCIA was programmed for a continuous infusion at 1.5 mL/h, with a 2 mL bolus dose and a 15-minute lockout interval. The acute pain service team adjusted PCIA parameters based on the patient’s pain intensity, and the PCIA was discontinued within 48 hours postoperatively.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was the incidence of PPD at 6 weeks postpartum. The positive screening of PPD was defined as EPDS scores more than 10. The incidence of PPD was calculated as the number of patients who were screened positive PPD divided by total number of patients.

Secondary Outcomes

The secondary outcomes were the incidence of PPD at 1 and 4 weeks postpartum. EPDS scores at different times were also assessed in this study. Additionally, the pain intensity assessed by numerical rating scale (NRS) at 1, 2, 3 days postpartum was also recorded in this study. Pain at rest was defined as pain experienced while lying in a supine position. Pain with movement was defined as pain experienced during coughing or walking on level ground. PCIA usage was also recorded.

The changes of vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate and pulse oxygen saturation, at 1, 5, 10 min after delivery and at the end of surgery were collected. Neonatal variables, including sex, body weight, and Apgar scores at 1, 5, and 10 minutes after delivery, were recorded. In addition, any discomfort reported by each subject was documented at the end of surgery through an open-ended question, and the adverse events were defined based on these reported concerns. The definition of common adverse event was provided in the Supplementary Material e-Table 1 Furthermore, any complaints from the subjects were documented during visits within 6 weeks postpartum.

Sample Size

A power analysis was performed based on our previous study and pilot study.19,20 We hypothesized that the incidence of PPD at 6 weeks postpartum was 23% and that esketamine infusion or bolus would result in a superiority incidence of PPD of 15% or 10%, respectively. An omnibus test was used to assess differences between the three groups. To achieve a statistical power of 80% at a significant level of 0.05, a total of 452 patients were required. Considering a 10% dropout rate, the final sample size was set at 503 patients. The sample size was calculated using Chi-Square Tests in PASS 15.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as either the mean ± standard deviation (for normally distributed data) or median (interquartile range) (for non-normally distributed data). Categorical variables were expressed as the number (percentage). To compare continuous variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed data, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non-normally distributed data. The chi-square test was used to analyze categorical variables. In the event of a significant result, pairwise comparisons between groups were performed with Bonferroni correction. Differences between groups were expressed as relative risk (RR) or median difference (MD) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Absolute differences between groups were also calculated in this study. Repeated-measures ANOVA was used to analyze repeated-measure data, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure and heart rate. In addition, a linear mixed effects model was established to assess trends in EPDS scores over time.

Per-protocol analysis was primarily performed, and intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was also conducted using worst-case scenario imputation to address missing data. A predefined subgroup analysis (primipara, age ≥ 35 years old, medium of EPDS scores) was also performed in this study. Additionally, although a cut-off value of ≥10 was the most commonly used to screen for PPD, various cut-off values have been used in previously published studies.21–23 Thus, a sensitivity analysis was also performed based on different EPDS cut-off values for screening PPD. Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS (version 17.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, III) and R (reversion 4.5.0, http://www.R-project.org). P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Intraoperative Variables

A total of 607 pregnant women were screened for participation. Of these, 104 patients were excluded: 47 screened positive for prenatal depression (baseline EPDS≥10), 3 were diagnosed as preeclampsia, 43 were treated with gestational hypertension, and 11 declined to participate. Consequently, 503 patients were randomly assigned to one of the three groups: control group (n=167), esketamine bolus (n=168) or infusion group (n=168). Within 6 weeks postpartum, 17 patients in control group, 15 bolus group and 12 in infusion group were lost to follow-up. Finally, data from 150 patients in control group, 153 in esketamine bolus group and 156 in esketamine infusion group were included in the final data analysis (Figure 1). Efficacy Outcomes

PPD

In the analysis of the primary outcome, a significant difference was observed in the incidence of positive PPD screening at 6 weeks postpartum among the three groups (χ2=7.445, P=0.024) (Table 2). Pairwise comparison indicated that bolus esketamine administration did not provide advantage in reducing the incidence of PPD compared with continuous infusion (9.15% vs 11.54%, RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.40–1.57). Further ITT analysis showed consistent results between the infusion and bolus groups (17.85% vs 17.26%, RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.54–1.68). However, compared with the control group, patients in esketamine bolus group rather than the infusion group exhibited a lower incidence of PPD (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.25–0.91). In the terms of EPDS scores at 6 weeks postpartum, no difference was noted between the esketamine bolus group and the infusion group (MD −0.23, 95% CI −1.34 to 0.87). When compared with control group, both esketamine bolus (MD −1.18, 95% CI −2.30 to −0.07) and infusion (MD −1.42, 95% CI −2.54 to −0.30) administration were effective in reducing EPDS scores. At 1 and 4 weeks postpartum, no significant differences were observed among three groups in either the incidence of PPD or the EPDS scores. At 6 weeks postpartum, no significant difference was observed between the two esketamine administration routes in reducing the incidence of PPD, regardless of whether the cut-off values were set as 11 (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.27–1.68) or 12 (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.31–2.07). EPDS scores in both esketamine bolus and infusion administration demonstrated a similar pattern as the incidence of PPD when compared with control group, albeit with minor discrepancies in some detailed findings. At 1 and 4 weeks postpartum, no statistical difference was noted among three groups in either the incidence of PPD or EDPS scores.

In the subgroup analysis, there was no significant interaction between the potential factors (age≥35 years old, primipara and baseline EPDS≥5) and esketamine administration routes, indicating that the effect of different routes of esketamine was consistent across the subgroups (Supplementary Material e-Table 5).

Pain Control

The analgesic effect was also assessed in this study. Results suggested that there were no significant differences in NRS pain scores at rest on postoperative day 1 (3 (2–3) vs 3 (2–3) vs 3 3 (2–3), H=0.209, P=0.901), day 2 (1 (0–2) vs 1 (0–2) vs 1 (0–2), H=0.576, P=0.750) and day 3 (0 (0–1) vs 0 (0–0) vs 0 (0–0), H=0.888, P=0.641) among the three groups (Figure 3a). Similarly, the NRS scores with movement on postoperative day 1 (4 (3–5) vs 4 (4–5) vs 4 (4–5), H=0.662, P=0.718), day2 (3 (3–4) vs 3 (3–4) vs 3 (3–4), H=0.763, P=0.683) and day 3 (1 (1–2) vs 1 (1–2) vs 1 (1–2), H=0.553, P=0.758) were also comparable (Figure 3b). Adverse Events

All the adverse events are shown in Table 3. During surgery, the incidence of adverse events exhibited significant difference among three groups (χ2=58.348, P<0.001). Pairwise comparison indicated that patients in bolus esketamine group exhibited lower incidence of adverse event compared with esketamine infusion group (45.75% vs 65.38%, RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.56–0.95). In terms of adverse events in neuropsychiatric system, esketamine bolus administration provided advantage in reducing incidence of dizziness compared with continuous infusion (14.37% vs 30.12%, RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.30–0.75). Conversely, although more cases with hallucination were reported in esketamine bolus group, no statistical difference was found between esketamine bolus group and infusion group (RR 2.65, 95% CI 0.97–7.26). However, absolute difference between the two groups exhibited a statistical significance (5.29, 95% CI 0.08–10.50) (Supplementary Material e-Table 6). Discussion

This clinical trial demonstrated that, among women without prenatal depression undergoing cesarean delivery, esketamine administered by intravenous bolus or continuous infusion provided comparable efficacy in preventing PPD at 6 weeks postpartum. Postoperative analgesic effects were also similar between groups. However, patients receiving esketamine via intravenous infusion exhibited more adverse events. The optimal route of esketamine administration for preventing PPD warrants further investigation.

It is well established that the administration route can significantly alter drug pharmacological effect and clinical outcome.24,25 From a classic pharmacokinetic perspective, bolus treatment would be expected to have higher maximum concentration (Cmax) than infusion administration, which typically results in different efficacy and tolerability as usually understood. Yet the present study observed comparable efficacy between the two routes. There may be several reasonable explanations for this finding. First, esketamine was administrated prophylactically, and the primary outcome was set as PPD incidence at 6 weeks postpartum. Such a delayed effect may result in substantial bias,26 which may partly account for the inconsistency with conventional pharmacokinetic findings. Second, it is widely accepted that there are some changes in the pharmacokinetics of many drugs during pregnancy and the postpartum period,27,28 including for ketamine/esketamine. This topic has drawn widespread attention, and relevant pharmacokinetic alterations await clarification pending the release of the PREPAR-2 findings (Clinical Trial: NCT06767566). Third, emerging evidence indicated that rapid antidepressant action of ketamine/esketamine partly depended on sustained AMPA receptor signaling and synaptogenesis.29 It was reasonable to speculate that the total drug exposure (AUC)—rather than Cmax—appears dominant in driving sustained antidepressant response. This implied that dosage may be a more critical determinant of antidepressant efficacy. Previous study has demonstrated that anesthetic doses of ketamine (twice the subanesthetic dose) also exert an antidepressant effect.30 Our results extended these findings by showing that the mode of administration exerts minimal influence on preventive efficacy when an effective dose is reliably delivered. In terms of efficacy, the two routes of esketamine administration have comparable preventive effects on PPD. Considering the differences in tolerability and the convenience of clinical administration, intravenous bolus administration appears to be the preferred route. However, this argument necessitates validation through large-scale, high-quality studies.

In this study, esketamine administrated via continuous infusion did not exhibit any advantage in reducing PPD compared to saline, which is inconsistent with the results of our previous study.20 A comparative analysis of the two articles revealed that the primary difference stems from the variation in study populations—specifically, patients with antenatal depression were excluded in this article but included in the prior one. As far as we know, three articles reporting negative results for ketamine/esketamine in PPD prevention have been published to date. Interestingly, one study only included patients without prenatal depression,31 and two other studies suggested that most of the included patients did not experience prenatal depression.32,33 In our previous study20 and Wu’s study,34 results consistently suggested that patients with prenatal depression or higher baseline EPDS scores showed a higher response to esketamine treatment. Therefore, we speculated that the preoperative EPDS scores may exert an impact on the efficacy of esketamine. This hypothesis was verified in a predictive model-based study; however, the sensitivity and specificity of this model were relatively low, at 0.651 and 0.815, respectively.35 Although the results may be influenced by antenatal depression, from a clinical significance, it is difficult to deny its effect due to the marginal statistical difference. There is a growing consensus that ketamine/esketamine can effectively prevent PPD, largely driven by positive findings from recently published meta-analyses.8,36–38 However, it seems premature to generally believed that esketamine can be effectively used to prevent PPD. The eligible population, administration route, and dosage are all potential factors. Unfortunately, the present study failed to demonstrate the effects of enrolled population, administration route and their interaction. Given that esketamine still carries some adverse reactions, optimized application for eligible populations remains a key focus for future exploration.

This study has several limitations. First, while EPDS is an optimal screening tool for PPD in China,39 and a conversion between EPDS scores and 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale has been constructed,40 it is primarily used for screening rather than diagnosis. PPD was evaluated at 6 weeks postpartum; however, the long-term effect of the treatment beyond this specified period remains uncertain. Second, subjects with a history of mental illness were excluded from this study due to the potential influence on the outcome. Compared with other studies, our inclusion criteria were more rigorous, and individuals with positive screening results for prenatal depression were also excluded. However, this may have inadvertently excluded the population that is more sensitive to esketamine, potentially affecting the results. Third, the PPD incidence in control group is lower than the assumed rate, this lower-than-expected incidence may have reduced the statistical power of the analysis. In addition, similar to a previous study, an omnibus test between groups was performed in a three-armed clinical trial.41 This method helps reduce type I error with a smaller sample size; however, large-scale trials are still needed to confirm the conclusions of this trial. Fourth, pharmacokinetic parameters were not available in this study, given that bolus and infusion of the same total dose produce substantially different peak plasma concentration and concentration-time curve may influence both treatment efficacy and tolerability. Again, the lack of these data precludes a pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic interpretation of the differences in this study.

Conclusion

This clinical trial demonstrated that intravenous bolus and infusion of esketamine provide comparable efficacy in preventing PPD at 6 weeks postpartum among women without prenatal depression undergoing cesarean delivery. Neither route offered an advantage in postoperative pain control. Notably, esketamine bolus exhibited a lower incidence of adverse events, all of which were mild and self-limiting, Given its better tolerability, greater clinical convenience, and equivalent preventive efficacy, intravenous bolus administration appears preferable in clinical practice. The optimal route of esketamine for PPD prevention warrants further validation in large-scale, multicenter studies.
