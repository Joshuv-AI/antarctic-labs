A genetic algorithm–optimized EWMA control chart integrating machine learning-based risk prediction for multistage thyroid cancer treatment monitoring

Mohammad Rasouli, Elham Rahimi, Arshia Ilaty

ABSTRACT
Effective monitoring of thyroid cancer treatment requires timely detection of clinically meaningful changes across complex, multistage care pathways. Traditional monitoring approaches often rely on static thresholds and periodic assessments, limiting their sensitivity to subtle yet important process deviations and potentially delaying clinical intervention. This study aimed to develop and evaluate an integrated, data-driven framework that combines deep learning–based risk prediction with genetic algorithm–optimized statistical process monitoring to enhance thyroid cancer treatment surveillance. Informative clinical variables were first identified using the Information Functions algorithm, and a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural network was then employed to predict patient-specific treatment risk, capturing nonlinear relationships within longitudinal electronic medical record data. The resulting risk estimates were sequentially incorporated into Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) control charts to enable continuous process monitoring. To further improve chart performance, a genetic algorithm was used to optimize EWMA parameters, enhancing accuracy and sensitivity while maintaining chart stability. The results demonstrate that the MLP model outperforms conventional machine learning approaches in risk prediction across multiple evaluation metrics. Genetic algorithm–optimized EWMA charts exhibit smoother trajectories, improved stability, and more reliable detection of clinically meaningful deviations compared with non-optimized charts. The consistency of these improvements across multiple features suggests that GA-based optimization enhances overall control chart performance rather than feature-specific behavior. This hybrid framework demonstrates the feasibility of integrating machine learning–derived risk prediction with optimized statistical process control for longitudinal treatment monitoring. The proposed approach supports proactive treatment surveillance, risk-stratified follow-up, timely clinical intervention, and data-driven decision-making in thyroid cancer care. Furthermore, the framework has the potential to enable continuous and adaptive monitoring across complex multi-stage treatment pathways if integrated with electronic medical record (EMR) systems in future implementations.

1. Introduction Thyroid cancer ranks seventh among all malignancies worldwide and exhibits a more rapidly increasing incidence rate than any other cancer type, accompanied by a slowly rising mortality rate [ 1 , 2 ]. Once considered a relatively rare malignancy, thyroid cancer has become the most common cancer of the endocrine system [ 3 ], and its treatment presents significant clinical challenges due to disease heterogeneity and variability in patient response [ 4 – 6 ]. The treatment process often involves repeated imaging, fine-needle aspiration biopsy, thyroidectomy, radioactive iodine therapy, and lifelong thyroid hormone replacement therapy [ 3 ]. In the current clinical workflow, monitoring treatment progress, predicting patient outcomes, and adjusting treatment plans rely heavily on clinical indicators such as thyroglobulin levels, tumor size, and administered radioactive iodine dose [ 3 ]. However, routine clinical decision-making based on periodic assessments and static thresholds may fail to capture early or subtle changes in disease status, resulting in delayed interventions, increased exposure of low-risk patients to invasive procedures and radiation, and substantial financial burden on patients and healthcare systems [ 3 ]. Moreover, the growing volume and complexity of clinical data may limit clinicians’ ability to fully leverage available information [ 7 ], further increasing the risk of suboptimal outcomes [ 8 ]. These challenges highlight the need for more precise, data-driven treatment process monitoring and risk stratification approaches that support timely decision-making while balancing the risks of overtreatment and undertreatment, with the goal of reducing morbidity and mortality while avoiding unnecessary interventions in low-risk patients. Statistical process control (SPC) methods have been increasingly explored for monitoring healthcare and treatment processes due to their ability to detect deviations in system behavior over time [ 9 – 12 ]. In particular, Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) control charts, designed to assign greater weight to recent observations, have been applied in healthcare settings to monitor surgical outcomes and multistage treatment processes, demonstrating improved sensitivity to small and gradual shifts compared with traditional control charts [ 13 , 14 ]. Rasouli et al. introduced a risk-based framework using multivariate EWMA group control charts to monitor multistage treatment processes, highlighting the potential of SPC techniques for healthcare process monitoring [ 15 – 17 ]. In parallel, machine learning and deep learning models have been widely adopted in healthcare for risk prediction and outcome modeling, leveraging large-scale clinical data to capture complex and nonlinear relationships. Consequently, statistical and machine learning approaches have increasingly been incorporated into healthcare process modeling to better represent system dynamics and patient heterogeneity. Yeganeh et al. proposed a machine learning–based framework for monitoring multistage healthcare processes, demonstrating the value of predictive modeling in guiding process-level decision-making [ 18 , 19 ]. More recently, risk-adjusted EWMA control charts have been extended using statistical learning and machine learning models to further enhance monitoring performance in clinical applications. Noor-ul-Amin et al. proposed a risk-adjusted EWMA control chart based on support vector machines for monitoring cardiac surgery outcomes [ 14 ], while Abbas et al. developed a machine learning–based adaptive EWMA framework to dynamically improve control chart performance [ 20 ]. Ahmadini et al. further advanced this line of research by integrating artificial neural networks into risk-adjusted EWMA control charts, demonstrating enhanced sensitivity and robustness in healthcare monitoring scenarios [ 21 ]. Despite these advances, existing statistical methodologies often rely on linear risk-adjustment or static control chart parameters, which may fail to capture the high-dimensional, non-linear dynamics inherent in longitudinal patient data. While hybrid SPC-ML models have shown promise, current frameworks frequently overlook the optimization of control chart parameters, potentially leading to increased false-alarm rates or delayed detection of clinical shifts. The performance of EWMA control charts is highly sensitive to the choice of the smoothing parameter (λ) and the control-limit width (L), which are conventionally fixed or selected from tabulated average run length (ARL) values derived under the assumptions of known process parameters and normally distributed, independent observations [ 22 ]. These assumptions are difficult to justify when the monitored quantity is a model-derived risk score with an unknown and potentially non-normal distribution. Furthermore, when in-control parameters must be estimated rather than assumed to be known, EWMA run-length performance can deteriorate, increasing false alarms while reducing sensitivity [ 23 ]. Manual or grid-based tuning also becomes impractical when monitoring is extended across multiple features and treatment stages, as the parameter space expands. Therefore, a single automated and reproducible tuning procedure is preferable to manual, per-chart parameter selection. Consequently, there is a lack of unified frameworks that integrate dynamic deep learning–based risk prediction with automated optimization for monitoring multistage treatment pathways. This is particularly critical in thyroid cancer care, where patient heterogeneity and complex trajectories demand adaptive surveillance tools capable of detecting subtle deviations to prevent adverse clinical outcomes. This gap motivates the development of an integrated framework that combines machine learning–driven risk estimation with genetic algorithm–optimized EWMA control charts, enabling more sensitive, adaptive, and clinically meaningful monitoring of treatment processes. Informative clinical variables are first identified using the Information Functions algorithm, which enhances the accuracy and efficiency of the risk prediction model by ensuring that only the most relevant data are used in predictive modeling. Risk prediction is then performed using a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model capable of capturing complex, nonlinear relationships in treatment-related data. The resulting risk estimates are subsequently embedded within EWMA control charts to enable continuous and dynamic monitoring of treatment trajectories. As these risk-score statistics have an unknown, potentially non-normal distribution and are monitored across multiple features and treatment stages, a distribution-free, derivative-free optimization strategy is preferable to fixed or tabulated parameter selection. Genetic algorithms, as a population-based, derivative-free approach, have been applied successfully to the optimal design of univariate and multivariate EWMA charts [ 24 ]. We therefore employ a genetic algorithm to optimize the EWMA parameters, providing a principled and extensible basis for producing more conservative charts that maintain chart stability while reducing unnecessary alarms. The rationale for this approach is clinical rather than purely statistical. In longitudinal patient surveillance, an overly sensitive chart generates frequent non-actionable alarms, which can exceed 70% of all alarms and contribute to alarm fatigue and clinician desensitization [ 25 , 26 ], as well as to unnecessary clinical follow-up and intervention which, in thyroid cancer, are associated with avoidable complications and considerable financial burden [ 27 ]. By optimizing the control-chart parameters accordingly, the framework prioritizes specificity and lowers the burden of unnecessary alarms and follow-up. Collectively, this framework advances precision monitoring in thyroid cancer treatment by bridging predictive modeling and adaptive statistical process control, enabling earlier detection of treatment deviations, supporting timely clinical intervention, and improving patient-centered decision-making. 2. Methods 2.1. Methodological framework The proposed model framework consists of four main stages: Data preprocessing: The dataset from patients with thyroid cancer was preprocessed to ensure consistency and standardization. This step included data cleaning, handling missing values, and normalization of variables. Risk prediction modeling: Relevant features of the preprocessed data were selected using the Information Functions algorithm to ensure that only informative and high-importance variables were included in the modeling process. A MLP neural network was then designed and implemented as a risk prediction model for thyroid cancer patients. Control chart design and implementation: The prediction outputs produced by the MLP model were used as inputs for constructing control charts. These charts were designed to monitor the treatment process over time and detect significant deviations. For this purpose, an EWMA control chart was employed to identify shifts and trends in the treatment process. Genetic algorithm–based optimization of the EWMA control chart: The EWMA control chart parameters, including the smoothing parameter (λ) and control limits (L), were optimized using a genetic algorithm (GA). The GA simulates the process of natural selection to identify optimal parameter values that enhance the performance of the control chart. Overall, this integrated framework combines deep learning–based predictive modeling with genetic algorithm–optimized statistical process monitoring, providing a comprehensive and efficient approach to improving treatment monitoring and patient outcomes. Dataset description In this study, secondary data analysis was conducted using data extracted by Rasouli et al. [ 17 ] from the electronic medical records (EMRs) of a hospital located in Tehran, Iran, between 2009 and 2013. The dataset consists of 80 patients who underwent two-stage thyroid cancer surgery and includes de-identified demographic information, medical history, test results, surgical reports, and treatment stages. All extracted medical record data were verified for completeness and accuracy by medical experts. In addition, lifestyle habits, such as smoking status and alcohol consumption, collected through questionnaires and patient interviews, were extracted from the electronic medical records. 2.2.1. Ethics statement. This study involved secondary analysis of de-identified electronic medical record data from Shariati Hospital, Tehran, Iran. The dataset was fully de-identified prior to access, and no direct or indirect patient identifiers were available to the research team. No new data collection or patient contact was performed. In accordance with the regulations of the Shariati Hospital Ethics Committee, secondary use of de-identified data from the hospital’s data center is exempt from ethics-committee review when conducted under the institution’s data use agreement; the present study was carried out entirely under that agreement and therefore qualified for this exemption. The data use agreement authorized only designated members of the approved research team to access the de-identified data, and solely through the hospital’s physical server, without any capability to transfer or download the underlying data; only analytical results may be extracted and published. Informed consent was waived given the retrospective, fully de-identified nature of the analysis. 2.3. Data preprocessing A multi-step data preprocessing pipeline was implemented to ensure data quality and consistency. First, data cleaning was performed. Records with incorrect values, either due to entry errors or discrepancies, were identified and corrected by medical experts. If a correction was deemed invalid by medical expert opinion, the data were removed. Missing values were identified and, when possible, imputed using the mean of the corresponding feature. Next, feature normalization was applied using the Min–Max scaling method to maintain consistent scales across all variables, reducing scale variations and preventing any feature from disproportionately influencing the modeling process. Finally, standardization was performed using the Z-score method so that each feature had a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one, ensuring uniform scaling across the dataset and improving the performance of the machine learning models. 2.4. Risk prediction modeling In this stage, we applied feature selection and advanced machine learning techniques to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of risk prediction models for thyroid cancer patients. The Information Functions algorithm was used to identify the most informative variables, which were then fed into a MLP model. Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics, including accuracy, 5-fold cross-validation accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and F1 score. The predictive performance of several additional machine learning models, Linear Regression, Random Forest, Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Deep Neural Network (DNN), was also assessed and compared with that of the MLP, facilitating the selection of the model with optimal predictive performance. 2.4.1. Feature selection based on information functions. After data preprocessing, the Information Functions algorithm was employed to identify the features with the greatest impact on prediction. Feature selection using information functions is a robust and effective approach for identifying the most relevant variables associated with the target outcome in a dataset. This method leverages concepts from information theory, including entropy and mutual information, to quantify the dependency and relationships between each feature and the target variable. In this study, the effect of MI-based feature selection on treatment-process monitoring is realized through the risk-prediction model. By retaining the most informative features, the selection improves the quality of the predicted risk scores, which are the quantities monitored by the EWMA control charts. As these scores drive the monitoring stage, any improvement in predictive performance is reflected in the resulting control-chart signals. Design and implementation of the control chart Control charts are statistical tools used to assess whether a process is operating within a state of statistical control. In this study, an EWMA control chart was designed and implemented with the primary objectives of monitoring and evaluating the thyroid cancer treatment process and detecting significant deviations from expected performance, which may indicate potential issues requiring intervention. The construction of the control chart follows the standard two-phase framework of statistical process control: a Phase I calibration step, in which the in-control mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ) of each feature are estimated, and a Phase II step, in which the resulting limits are applied to monitor patient trajectories. Due to the limited size of the available single-institution dataset, an independent Phase I calibration cohort was not available; the in-control parameters were therefore estimated from the study dataset and applied to monitor the same patients’ trajectories. The following steps outline the design and implementation of the control chart: The EWMA statistic for each variable X at time t is calculated as shown in Eq. (8) , where z t is the EWMA at time t , x t is the observed value at time t , and z t − 1 is the EWMA at time t − 1 . The initial value, z 0 , is typically set to the mean of the historical data. z t = λ x t + ( 1 − λ ) z t − 1 (8) The control limits for the EWMA chart are calculated as shown in Eqs. (9) and (10) . In these equations, U C L t is the upper control limit at time t , L C L t is the lower control limit at time t , μ is the mean of the historical data, σ is the standard deviation of the historical data, and L is the control limit multiplier, typically set to 3 for 3-sigma control limits. U C L t = μ + L σ λ 2 − λ ( 1 − ( 1 − λ ) 2 t ) (9) L C L t = μ − L σ λ 2 − λ ( 1 − ( 1 − λ ) 2 t ) (10) To implement the control chart, the key features of the thyroid cancer patients identified during the design phase were selected from the preprocessed data and used as inputs for the chart. Next, the mean ( μ ) and standard deviation ( σ ) for each selected variable were calculated using historical data. For each time point t , the EWMA statistic ( Z T ) for each variable was calculated. The upper control limit ( U C L t ) and lower control limit ( L C L t ) at each time point t were also calculated using Eqs. (9) and (10) . Finally, the EWMA statistics, along with the control limits, were plotted to visualize process performance, and any points outside the control limits were identified as potential signals of process changes that require further investigation. Genetic algorithm–based optimization of the EWMA control chart Although the EWMA control chart is a powerful statistical tool for monitoring process performance over time and detecting significant deviations that may indicate potential issues, optimizing its parameters can substantially enhance its sensitivity and specificity, leading to improved process control and enabling more timely interventions. Therefore, a GA was employed to optimize the parameters of the EWMA control chart, specifically the smoothing parameter ( λ ) and control limits ( L ) . Although the EWMA chart involves only two design parameters, their selection is not trivial in this setting. Conventional selection relies on tabulated values derived under the assumptions of normality and known in-control parameters; here, the monitored quantity is a model-derived risk score with an unknown, potentially non-normal distribution, and its in-control parameters must be estimated from limited data, which is known to degrade run-length performance [ 23 ]. Moreover, monitoring is performed across multiple features and treatment stages, so a single automated and reproducible tuning procedure is preferable to manual, per-chart selection. Genetic algorithms provide such a procedure and have been applied successfully to the optimal design of univariate and multivariate EWMA charts [ 24 ]; we therefore adopt a GA as a general-purpose, distribution-free optimizer within this framework. The genetic algorithm is an optimization technique inspired by the principles of natural evolution and selection. This algorithm operates through a process that involves generating an initial population, evaluating fitness, selecting superior solutions, and inducing crossover and mutation to preserve diversity, iterating these steps until a satisfactory level of fitness is achieved. The GA was initialized by generating a population of candidate solutions (chromosomes), each representing a set of EWMA control chart parameters, λ a n d L . The initial population was generated randomly within predefined bounds to ensure a diverse set of potential solutions. The fitness of each chromosome was evaluated using a fitness function that assesses control chart performance, defined as the number of observations falling outside the control limits. A lower number of out-of-control observations corresponds to a better fitness score, indicating that the selected parameters are more effective in maintaining process stability. The fittest chromosomes were selected as parents for the next generation using selection methods such as tournament selection or roulette wheel selection, ensuring that higher-performing solutions had a greater probability of passing their genes to the next generation. New offspring were generated through crossover and mutation to maintain genetic diversity. Crossover involved combining segments of two parent chromosomes to produce new offspring, while mutation introduced random alterations to selected genes, enabling exploration of new regions of the solution space and reducing the risk of convergence to local optima. The optimization procedure was iterated for a specified number of generations or until a satisfactory fitness level was achieved. Throughout these iterations, the GA progressively improved the overall fitness of the population, converging toward an optimal set of parameters. The final outcome was an optimized pair of λ and L values that minimized the number of out-of-control observations, thereby prioritizing the specificity of the EWMA control chart in detecting process variations and reducing unnecessary alarms, with an acknowledged trade-off in sensitivity. 3. Discussion This study presents a framework that integrates deep learning–based risk prediction with genetic algorithm-optimized statistical process monitoring to enhance the monitoring of thyroid cancer treatment processes. By jointly leveraging machine learning, information-theoretic feature selection, and optimized EWMA control charts, the proposed approach addresses key limitations of traditional monitoring methods, which often lack sensitivity to subtle yet clinically meaningful changes in patient status. The results demonstrate that the MLP model outperformed several conventional machine learning models in predicting patient risk, highlighting the importance of nonlinear modeling for capturing the complex relationships inherent in clinical and treatment-related data. The identification of thyroglobulin level at different treatment stages as the most informative predictor aligns with its clinical use as a key biomarker in thyroid cancer management [ 28 , 29 ]. Beyond prediction, a major contribution of this study is the application of EWMA control charts to machine learning-derived risk prediction scores rather than raw clinical measurements, thereby enabling proactive process monitoring by translating predictive insights into actionable monitoring signals. Genetic algorithm–based optimization of EWMA parameters improved chart performance, as evidenced by smoother EWMA trajectories, reduced false alarms, and preserved sensitivity to meaningful process changes across all monitored features. The consistent improvement across features suggests that GA-based optimization enhances overall chart behavior rather than tailoring performance to individual variables. In particular, the higher smoothing parameter (λ) assigns greater weight to recent observations, enhancing the chart’s responsiveness to emerging changes while maintaining stability. From a clinical and operational perspective, the framework could be integrated into hospital EMR systems as a clinical decision-support tool to enable dynamic, sequential monitoring of treatment processes. At each clinical update, such as new laboratory results, surgical procedures, or follow-up visits, the MLP model will generate updated patient-specific risk estimates, which will be sequentially incorporated into the EWMA monitoring component and will evaluate each patient’s treatment trajectory relative to expected performance over time, enabling continuous longitudinal assessment rather than reliance on isolated cross-sectional evaluations. When the EWMA statistics exceed predefined control limits, the system generates an automated alert notifying the clinical team that the patient may require additional evaluation or follow-up. In practice, this approach enables risk-stratified monitoring, whereby patients with stable or low predicted risk continue routine surveillance, while those demonstrating elevated or worsening risk profiles are prioritized for targeted diagnostic testing or intervention. This potential workflow may improve the allocation of clinical resources, minimize alarm fatigue, and ultimately improve patient outcomes while reducing complications and healthcare costs. Beyond thyroid cancer, the framework may provide a methodological template for monitoring other diseases involving longitudinal treatment data and complex patient trajectories [ 30 ] and may support data-driven quality assurance and performance evaluation. The framework used in this study is related to, but conceptually distinct from, the established family of risk-adjusted control charts used in healthcare surveillance. Risk-adjusted cumulative sum (CUSUM) charts [ 31 , 32 ] and risk-adjusted EWMA charts [ 33 ] were developed to monitor clinical outcomes [ 34 ], most commonly binary events such as surgical mortality, while accounting for patients’ preoperative risk [ 14 , 35 ]. These methods typically use logistic regression for risk adjustment and are designed to evaluate aggregate performance at the institutional level. In contrast, our approach serves as a longitudinal, patient-level risk surveillance rather than case-mix-adjusted performance monitoring and differs in both its monitoring target and methodology. More recent studies have integrated neural networks with risk-adjusted outcome monitoring, including evolutionary artificial neural network (ANN)-based control charts evaluated using the average run length criterion [ 36 – 39 ]. Our contribution differs from these approaches by focusing on continuous risk-score trajectories rather than binary clinical outcomes. A direct empirical comparison with conventional risk-adjusted CUSUM and EWMA charts would help clarify the relative strengths and limitations of each approach and represents an important direction for future research. Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. The dataset was relatively modest in size and derived from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. This constraint is inherently linked to the nature of thyroid cancer as a relatively rare malignancy, which presents significant challenges in data acquisition. However, the adequacy of this sample size for monitoring purposes was previously validated through simulation analyses on the same cohort [ 15 ]. Also, an independent Phase I calibration cohort was not available, and the in-control parameters were therefore estimated from the same dataset used for Phase II monitoring. This is a recognized limitation, as the use of estimated rather than independently specified in-control parameters can affect EWMA run-length performance [ 23 ]. While limited training data frequently hinders the development of predictive models, a key contribution of this work is the achievement of high predictive accuracy and efficiency despite these constraints. This demonstrates the framework’s robustness in rare disease contexts where large-scale datasets are unavailable. The monitored risk score is derived from short per-patient trajectories with an unknown distribution. Under these conditions, reliable estimation of average run length (ARL) would require assumptions that are not sufficiently supported by the available data. A formal ARL evaluation using an independent calibration cohort and a larger, multi-institutional dataset therefore remains an important area for future investigation. Additionally, the genetic algorithm objective function used in this study minimizes the frequency of out-of-control signals and therefore emphasizes specificity. As it does not distinguish between true and false alarms, it does not simultaneously optimize sensitivity. A joint sensitivity–specificity formulation based on average run length could address this limitation by constraining the in-control ARL (ARL₀) to control the false-alarm rate while minimizing the out-of-control ARL (ARL 1 ) for clinically meaningful shifts. Given the relatively low-dimensional search space, alternative metaheuristic approaches such as particle swarm optimization or grey wolf optimization may yield comparable or potentially improved results [ 40 ]. Therefore, future research should compare these optimization strategies and evaluate adaptive or personalized control limits, real-time data processing, and advanced machine learning architectures using larger, multicenter datasets. Extending this approach to other cancer types and chronic disease management scenarios represents a promising direction for future research.
