Modelling the impact of climate variability on malaria morbidity in the Tamale Metropolitan Area: a time series analysis
Authors: Abdul-Ganiu Zakaria, Shamsu-Deen Ziblim, Yakubu Amadu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains endemic in northern Ghana, with seasonal outbreaks placing significant strain on health facilities in the Tamale Metropolitan Area. Although climatic variability is known to influence malaria transmission, the specific short- and long-run relationships between temperature, rainfall, relative humidity, and malaria outcomes in this setting remain poorly understood. This study models the impact of climate variability on population-adjusted malaria morbidity in the Tamale Metropolitan Area using a time series approach.MethodsMonthly time series data on laboratory-confirmed malaria morbidity (converted to rates per 1,000 population), as well as temperature, rainfall, and relative humidity, were obtained from the District Health Information Management System and the Ghana Meteorological Agency for the period January 2014 to December 2020 (84 observations). The Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) bounds testing approach was selected for its ability to handle variables with mixed orders of integration (I(0) and I(1)) and to simultaneously estimate short- and long-run dynamics. No logarithmic transformation was applied, preserving direct interpretation of coefficients as changes in malaria rates per 1,000 population.ResultsThe ARDL cointegration test confirmed a long-run equilibrium relationship between each climatic variable and malaria morbidity. In the short run, increases in relative humidity, temperature, and rainfall were significantly associated with higher malaria morbidity (p < 0.05 to p < 0.001). In the long run, however, temperature showed a significant inverse relationship: a one-unit (1 °C) increase in temperature was associated with a 0.37 case per 1,000 population decrease in malaria morbidity (p = 0.038). Model diagnostic tests (Ljung-Box and ARCH-LM) indicated that residuals were white noise, supporting model validity.ConclusionClimatic variables, particularly temperature, play a significant but complex role in malaria transmission in the Tamale Metropolitan Area, with opposing short- and long-run effects. These findings support the development of climate-informed early warning systems tailored to northern Ghana. To strengthen local malaria control and climate adaptation strategies, future efforts should integrate intervention coverage and health system data, improve surveillance, and apply advanced time-series methods to better capture non-linear and delayed climate effects.Clinical trialNot applicable.

Introduction

Malaria remains one of the most significant global public health threats. Although global malaria morbidity and mortality declined steadily over the past three decades, recent disruptions, notably the COVID-19 pandemic, have reversed some of these gains, with morbidity rising from 56.3 to 59.0 cases per 1,000 population at risk between 2019 and 2020 [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate burden, accounting for approximately 95% of cases and 96% of deaths globally, with 80% of all regional deaths occurring among children under five years of age [2]. This high transmission intensity is largely driven by ecological and climatic conditions that favor the proliferation of Anopheles mosquitoes.

In Ghana, malaria is endemic and persistent, with marked seasonal fluctuations. The northern region experiences a single rainy season from May to September (peaking in August), resulting in a three- to seven-month transmission season and a maximum number of cases between July and November. Ghana accounts for 2.1% of global malaria morbidity and 1.9% of global mortality, and despite considerable control efforts, malaria imposes a heavy socioeconomic burden: malaria expenditure accounts for 34% of the income of impoverished households compared with only 1% among wealthy households [3–5]. Pregnant women and children under five remain at increased risk of malaria mortality [6].

The growth and maturity of the Plasmodium parasite and the ecology of mosquito vectors are highly sensitive to climatic factors. Temperature influences parasite development rates and mosquito survival [7, 8]; rainfall creates breeding sites, although extreme events may diminish them [9, 10]; and relative humidity affects vector desiccation and the extrinsic incubation period [11, 12]. Empirical evidence has consistently shown that climatic variability can either support or undermine malaria control efforts [13].

Despite this established nexus, current research investigating the climate–malaria relationship through time-series methodologies remains limited, particularly in highly endemic regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa [6, 14, 15]. Where studies exist, they often rely on conventional ARIMA-type models, which, while effective for capturing seasonal patterns, inadequately account for covariance among meteorological variables, thereby limiting explanatory power for complex climate-disease interactions [14, 16].

More dynamic approaches, such as ARDL cointegration, dynamic regression, and machine learning, have been employed elsewhere [6, 17–19], but Ghana remains largely underrepresented in this predictive modelling literature. Specifically, no study has systematically modelled both the short- and long-run effects of climate variability on malaria morbidity in the Tamale Metropolitan Area of northern Ghana.

This knowledge gap is actionable. Tamale shares socioeconomic and climatic peculiarities with much of northern Ghana: a single intense rainy season, a fragile health system, and recurrent inundation of under-resourced facilities with malaria cases during peak transmission months. Understanding how rainfall, temperature, and relative humidity drive malaria outcomes in this setting is essential for developing climate-informed early warning systems and relevant preventive and coping strategies.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to model the short-run and long-run effects of climate variability (temperature, rainfall, and relative humidity) on laboratory-confirmed malaria morbidity in the Tamale Metropolitan Area from January 2014 to December 2020, using the Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) bounds testing approach to cointegration. The findings aim to inform climate-resilient prevention and adaptive measures against malaria in northern Ghana.

Methods

This section highlights the different methods and techniques adopted in this study.

Study area

The study was conducted in the Tamale Metropolitan Area, the administrative capital of northern Ghana (latitude 9°23′ N, longitude 0°50′ W; approximately 750 km²). This location was selected due to the endemic nature of malaria and the availability of reliable health and climatic data.

Data sources

Monthly time series data spanning January 2014 to December 2020 (84 observations) were obtained from two sources:

Health data

Laboratory-confirmed malaria morbidity records were sourced from the District Health Information Management System (DHIMS) of the Ghana Ministry of Health (accessed August 23, 2021). Monthly malaria morbidity counts were converted to incidence rates per 1,000 population using annual population estimates for the Tamale Metropolitan Area. Intercensal estimates were interpolated linearly between the 2010 Ghana Population and Housing Census (371,351) and the 2020 projected population (494,000).

Climate data

Monthly measurements for temperature (°C), rainfall (mm), and relative humidity (%) were obtained from the Ghana Meteorological Agency (accessed August 30, 2021).

Analytical framework

Pre-processing and software

All analyses were performed in R version 4.2.2 using the packages dynlm (dynamic linear models), tseries (KPSS test), vars (VAR lag selection), lmtest (diagnostics), and strucchange (structural break tests). Data were checked for missing values (none present).

Variable transformation and functional form

All variables (malaria morbidity rate, temperature, rainfall, and relative humidity) were analyzed in their original level forms (non-logged). This choice preserves direct interpretation of coefficients as marginal effects (change in malaria cases per 1,000 population per one-unit change in a climate variable) and avoids the elasticity interpretation that would be required in a log-log specification.

Trend analysis

To identify underlying temporal patterns and guide the inclusion of a deterministic trend, three models (linear, quadratic, and log-linear) were estimated for each variable. Model selection was based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Stationarity and unit root testing

The stationarity properties of all series were examined using the Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin (KPSS) test to determine the order of integration and avoid spurious regression.

Cointegration and long-run analysis

Given the mixed orders of integration among the variables, conventional cointegration tests (e.g., Johansen) were inappropriate. The Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) bounds testing approach [20] was therefore selected because it (i) accommodates regressors that are I(0), I (1), or mutually cointegrated; (ii) simultaneously estimates short- and long-run parameters; and (iii) provides valid inference with small sample sizes (n = 84).

Lag selection

Optimal lag lengths (p, q) were selected using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) with a maximum lag of 8 (to capture up to two full seasonal cycles). The final model [7, 8] represents the AIC-minimizing specification.

Multicollinearity assessment

Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated for all independent variables; all VIF values were below 5, indicating no problematic multicollinearity.

Model diagnostics

Model diagnostics: Post‑estimation diagnostic tests were conducted to validate model adequacy: the Ljung‑Box test for serial correlation and the ARCH‑LM test for heteroscedasticity. Residuals were considered white noise if p‑values exceeded 0.05.

Ethical approval

This study received ethical approval from the Committee on Human Research, Publication, and Ethics (CHRPE) of the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (reference number CHRPE/AP/442/21). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The analysis used aggregated, anonymized data with no personal identifiers; therefore, individual consent was not required. This anonymization protocol supports reproducibility while adhering to ethical standards for secondary analysis of routine health data.

Short run effects

In the short run, all three climate variables were positively and significantly associated with malaria morbidity in Tamale (Table 8). Specifically:

A one-unit (1%) increase in relative humidity was associated with an increase of 1.08 cases per 1,000 population in malaria morbidity (p < 0.001).

A one-unit (1 °C) increase in temperature was associated with an increase of 0.97 cases per 1,000 population in morbidity (p < 0.001).

A one-unit (1 mm) increase in rainfall was associated with an increase of 0.31 cases per 1,000 population in morbidity (p < 0.05).

All error correction terms (ECT) were negative and statistically significant (ranging from − 0.101 to − 0.254), confirming rapid adjustment back to long‑run equilibrium after a short‑run shock. For instance, the ECT of − 0.254 for the relative humidity–morbidity model implies that approximately 25% of any disequilibrium is corrected within one month.

Model diagnosis

For all malaria morbidity models, the Ljung‑Box test (p > 0.05) and ARCH‑LM test (p > 0.01) indicated that residuals were white noise, free from serial correlation and heteroscedasticity (Table 10). This supports the validity of the morbidity model estimates. Variance inflation factors (VIF) for all independent variables were below 5 (range: 1.24–3.87), indicating no problematic multicollinearity.

Discussion

This study provides the first ARDL‑based evidence from the Tamale Metropolitan Area that climatic variability exerts significant short‑run effects on malaria morbidity, with temperature showing opposing long‑run associations. These findings extend the climate–malaria literature by demonstrating how the same climate variable can have opposite short‑ and long‑run effects in a distinct seasonal setting like northern Ghana. The consistent seasonal pattern of relative humidity observed in Tamale contrasts with reports from Manga, Upper East Ghana, where humidity fluctuated erratically [21]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in geographical scope and microclimate rather than methodological variation. Temperature in Tamale showed a gradual upward trend without structural breaks, consistent with regional West African warming patterns [22]. Rainfall exhibited pronounced seasonality but no long‑term trend, contrasting with studies that report declining rainfall elsewhere in Ghana [23, 24]. This spatial heterogeneity underscores the importance of local‑level climate data for malaria modelling—a key justification for the Tamale case study.

Our analysis confirms that rainfall, temperature, and relative humidity serve as significant short-run predictors of malaria morbidity, though long-run effects are limited to temperature. The sensitivity of malaria to rainfall aligns with findings from the Democratic Republic of Congo [25], South Africa [26], China [27], Rwanda and Uganda [28], and Ghana [29]. The positive short‑run associations we observed are biologically plausible. In Tamale, rainfall creates transient breeding pools for Anopheles mosquitoes [30]. Relative humidity above 60% (typical in Tamale’s wet season) reduces vector desiccation and supports Plasmodium sporogony. Temperature operates as a thermodynamic regulator: the short‑run positive effect (0.97 cases per 1,000 population per 1 °C) aligns with optimal ranges of 25–30 °C, which accelerate parasite development and mosquito biting rates [7, 8]. Not all studies agree. Some report non‑significant [32] or negative [31, 34] climate–malaria associations, including research from Ghana’s forest zone where only humidity was predictive [33]. These discrepancies likely reflect geographical heterogeneity in vector species, baseline immunity, health system capacity, and non‑linear threshold effects.

Importantly, climate represents only one component of malaria transmission dynamics. Socioeconomic determinants, including poverty, healthcare access, sanitation, and population density, interact with climatic factors [35, 36]. Intervention coverage (insecticide-treated nets, seasonal malaria chemoprevention, indoor residual spraying) and human mobility further modulate transmission. Effective vector control and emerging vaccination programs may substantially mitigate climate-related transmission risks [37], emphasizing the need for integrated, multi-sectoral approaches.

Four main limitations affect interpretation. Omitted variable bias: low R² values for exploratory models indicate missing predictors, notably intervention coverage, health system capacity, and population mobility. These factors were unavailable for inclusion. Causality and generalisability: temporal precedence supports association, but causality cannot be established without quasi‑experimental designs. The single‑region focus limits extrapolation to other ecological zones in Ghana. Overfitting risk: with 84 observations and up to 8 lags, parsimony was prioritized; the final morbidity model uses fewer lags [7, 8]. Readers should interpret coefficients as associations only. Structural breaks: while no significant breaks were detected in the morbidity series, unmeasured breaks (e.g., policy changes, outbreak events) could affect parameter stability.

Future studies in Tamale and similar settings should collect and incorporate intervention coverage and health system metrics, apply distributed lag non‑linear models (DLNMs) to capture delayed and threshold effects, use regime‑switching or GARCH models to address volatility clustering, and extend the study period to include post-2020 data to assess pandemic-related disruptions. Despite limitations, three priority actions emerge. Climate‑informed early warning should integrate seasonal climate forecasts (temperature, rainfall) with routine surveillance to trigger pre‑emptive interventions (e.g., vector control, stockpiling) 1–2 months before peak transmission in Tamale’s single rainy season. Health system strengthening is essential because climate adaptation alone is insufficient; investments in universal health coverage, peripheral facility staffing, and malaria commodity security are critical. Finally, improved surveillance with disaggregated data (age, sex, socioeconomic status, treatment delay) and reliable morbidity registration are needed to validate future models.

Conclusion

Climatic variables significantly influence malaria transmission in the Tamale Metropolitan Area, with differential short-run versus long-run effects. Climate represents an important, but not deterministic, component of malaria transmission ecology. Effective control requires integrating climate adaptation with robust health systems, socioeconomic development, and targeted interventions, while investing in enhanced surveillance and analytical frameworks to better quantify and respond to climate-health linkages.
