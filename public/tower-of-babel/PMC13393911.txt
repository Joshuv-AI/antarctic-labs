Incivility in nursing: implications for work-related quality of life and turnover intention in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Khulud Almutairi, Abdualrahman Saeed Alshehry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWorkplace incivility is a pervasive challenge in nursing, yet its sources, correlates, and consequences remain underexplored in Saudi Arabia. Saudi Arabia’s nursing workforce is among the most internationally diverse in the world and operates within a rapidly evolving healthcare system marked by hierarchical structures and high turnover rates. This study examined the level of workplace incivility experienced by nurses in Saudi Arabia, identified the demographic and professional factors associated with exposure to incivility, and investigated the associations among incivility, work-related quality of life, and turnover intentions.MethodsA quantitative cross-sectional survey design was employed. A convenience sample of 278 nurses working in hospital settings across Saudi Arabia was recruited through online platforms between September 30, 2025 and January 6, 2026. Data were collected using three validated instruments: the Nursing Incivility Scale, the Work-Related Quality of Life scale, and the Anticipated Turnover Scale. Data analysis included descriptive statistics, independent-samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA with post hoc comparisons, Pearson correlation analysis, and hierarchical multiple regression.ResultsThe majority of participants (84%) reported moderate to high levels of workplace incivility; given recruitment through convenience sampling, this figure is best interpreted as an upper-bound estimate within a self-selected sample. The highest incivility scores were observed for patient and family sources, followed by nurse colleague sources. Younger nurses and those with fewer years of experience reported significantly higher levels of incivility. Nationality was a significant differentiator, with Saudi nurses reporting higher incivility than Arab nurses. Married nurses reported lower levels of incivility than single nurses. Workplace incivility was significantly and negatively associated with work-related quality of life and positively associated with turnover intentions. In the hierarchical regression analysis, work-related quality of life explained substantial additional variance in turnover intentions beyond incivility alone, after which the direct effect of incivility was no longer significant.ConclusionsWorkplace incivility is prevalent among nurses in Saudi Arabia and is associated with poorer work-related quality of life and higher turnover intentions. The findings suggest that work-related quality of life may account for the relationship between incivility and turnover. Multilevel interventions targeting patient and family incivility, peer dynamics, and organizational culture are needed to protect nurse well-being and support workforce retention in the Saudi healthcare system.Keywords: Nursing, Saudi Arabia, Turnover intention, Work-related quality of life, Workplace incivility

Background

Workplace incivility in nursing is a complex, multisource phenomenon that encompasses a range of low-intensity yet harmful behaviors directed at nurses by colleagues, supervisors, physicians, and patients and their families [1]. Although incivility has been extensively studied in Western healthcare contexts, the experiences of nurses working in Saudi Arabia have received comparatively limited systematic investigation. The Saudi nursing workforce operates within a distinctive sociocultural environment characterized by substantial workforce diversity, hierarchical professional structures, and ongoing healthcare transformation. These factors may shape both the prevalence and perception of uncivil behavior in ways that existing international evidence does not fully capture [2–4].

Existing studies conducted in Saudi Arabia have documented moderate to poor quality of working life among nurses and notable rates of turnover intention, yet few have examined workplace incivility as a contributing factor to these outcomes [5–7]. This gap is consequential because, without a clear understanding of the extent to which nurses in Saudi Arabia are exposed to incivility, the sources from which it most frequently originates, and the demographic and professional characteristics associated with greater vulnerability, healthcare organizations lack the empirical foundation required to intervene effectively. Furthermore, the perceived reasons nurses attribute to their experiences of incivility, including factors such as nationality, gender, religion, and professional role, remain largely unexplored in this context, thereby limiting the development of culturally informed workplace policies.

Saudi Arabia’s nursing workforce is highly diverse, comprising nurses from a wide range of national, cultural, and linguistic backgrounds who practice within a rapidly evolving healthcare system undergoing significant transformation under Vision 2030. In 2018, the Kingdom had a total of 184,565 nurses, of whom only approximately 38% were Saudi citizens. Foreign nurses were predominantly from India, the Philippines, and Malaysia, reflecting a workforce that is among the most internationally diverse in the world [8]. High turnover rates among nurses in Saudi Arabia, estimated at between 17% and 60% per year, have been driven in part by perceptions of unfavorable working conditions, low pay, and low social status. This trend underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the organizational and interpersonal factors that undermine nurse well-being and retention [3, 9].

Accordingly, this study sought to address several interrelated gaps in the literature. Specifically, it aimed to determine the level of workplace incivility experienced by nurses in Saudi Arabia, identify the demographic and professional factors associated with these experiences, and examine the extent to which incivility is associated with nurses’ work-related quality of life and intention to leave their current positions. The significance of this study is further underscored by the well-documented relationship between workplace incivility and quality of working life. Poor work-related quality of life has been associated with decreased organizational commitment, increased stress, impaired professional functioning, and a greater intention to leave [10, 11]. Nurse turnover carries substantial financial and operational costs for healthcare organizations and ultimately threatens the continuity and quality of patient care [12, 13].

Materials and methods

Study design

A quantitative, cross-sectional survey design was employed to assess the level of workplace incivility among nurses in Saudi Arabia and examine its relationships with work-related quality of life and turnover intentions. This design is appropriate for estimating the prevalence of workplace incivility and exploring associations among variables within a single time frame. Furthermore, it facilitates the identification of demographic and professional factors associated with nurses’ experiences of incivility [14].

Setting and sample

The study was conducted among nurses working in hospital settings across Saudi Arabia. A convenience sampling strategy was used to recruit participants. Inclusion criteria were being a registered nurse employed in a hospital setting in Saudi Arabia, the ability to read and understand the survey language, and willingness to provide informed consent. Nurses who did not meet these criteria or who declined participation were excluded from the study.

Data collection procedure

Data collection took place between September 30, 2025, and January 6, 2026, after Institutional Review Board approval was obtained. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling using a combination of online and informal strategies, including the dissemination of a survey link and QR code via social media platforms (e.g., Twitter and LinkedIn), word-of-mouth referrals, and the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties. An introductory section outlined the study’s purpose, procedures, and confidentiality assurances. Participants who agreed to take part completed an electronic questionnaire that included informed consent, demographic information, and validated study instruments. The survey required approximately 10–15 min to complete. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Instruments

Demographic questionnaire

Participants provided information across several personal and professional domains relevant to the study’s objectives. Continuous variables included age, length of employment in Saudi Arabia, and total years of nursing practice. Categorical variables included gender, nationality, and marital status. Educational background was assessed by asking participants to identify their highest completed nursing degree. Participants were also asked to indicate their proficiency in Arabic on a four-point scale ranging from not well at all to very well. This measure was included to characterize the linguistic diversity of the nursing workforce in Saudi Arabia. Participants identified their healthcare setting and nursing role from predefined lists of options. Screening criteria included current employment as a nurse in a hospital setting in Saudi Arabia and English language fluency sufficient to provide informed consent.

Nursing incivility scale

Workplace incivility was assessed using the Nursing Incivility Scale (NIS), an occupation-specific instrument designed to assess hospital nurses’ experiences of incivility across multiple sources: hospital administration, nurse colleagues, supervisors, physicians, and patients and their families [1]. The NIS consists of 43 items organized into five subscales: general incivility/hospital administration (9 items), nurse colleague incivility (10 items), supervisor incivility (7 items), physician incivility (7 items), and patient and family incivility (10 items). All items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree), with higher scores indicating greater levels of incivility from each respective source. In the original validation study, Cronbach’s alpha values ranged from 0.81 to 0.94 [1]. The NIS has been applied in the Saudi Arabian nursing context, supporting its cultural applicability [15].

Work-related quality of life scale

The Work-Related Quality of Life (WRQoL) scale is a 23-item instrument developed by Van Laar et al. [10] to measure quality of working life among healthcare workers. The six subscales are General Well-Being, Home-Work Interface, Job and Career Satisfaction, Control at Work, Working Conditions, and Stress at Work. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale, with items 7, 9, and 19 reverses scored. Subscale scores are computed by summing the relevant item scores, and an overall WRQoL score is derived by summing the totals of all six subscales [16]. Validation studies have consistently reported Cronbach’s alpha values greater than 0.70 across subscales [17]. The WRQoL has been used in nursing research in Saudi Arabia, with findings indicating moderate overall quality of working life scores [11, 18].

Anticipated turnover scale

The Anticipated Turnover Scale (ATS) was developed by Hinshaw and Atwood [12] to evaluate nurses’ intention to voluntarily leave their current position. The ATS is a 12-item self-administered questionnaire scored on a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 7 (Strongly Agree), with six items reverse scored. Higher scores indicate a greater intention to leave. A meta-analysis of 12 reliability studies involving 2,442 registered nurses reported an overall mean weighted reliability coefficient of 0.89 and a mean validity coefficient of − 0.53 for the correlation between the ATS and job satisfaction measures [13]. The ATS has been used in Saudi Arabian nursing contexts [7].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29). Participants’ nationality was collected as an open-ended response and subsequently categorized into three groups for analysis: (1) Saudi (Saudi Arabian nationals), (2) Arabs (nurses from other Arab countries, including Egypt, Jordan, Mauritania, Palestine, Sudan, Syria, Lebanon, and Yemen), and (3) Non-Arabs (nurses from non-Arab countries, including Croatia, Eritrea, India, Malaysia, Nigeria, Pakistan, the Philippines, and South Africa). This categorization was used to facilitate meaningful comparisons across regional and cultural backgrounds. Prior to analysis, the data were screened for missing values, outliers, and potential violations of statistical assumptions. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were calculated to summarize sample characteristics and key study variables. The internal consistency of the study instruments was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients.

Independent-samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to examine differences in workplace incivility across demographic and professional characteristics, including gender, nationality, and marital status. When appropriate, post hoc analyses were performed. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to assess the relationships among workplace incivility, work-related quality of life, and turnover intentions, as well as selected continuous demographic variables. Simple linear regression was used to examine the effect of workplace incivility on work-related quality of life. Hierarchical multiple regression was used to examine the sequential contribution of workplace incivility and work-related quality of life to turnover intentions, with changes in explained variance (ΔR²) used to assess model improvement.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the ethical standards established by the Institutional Review Board of King Saud University. All procedures performed in this study were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained prior to data collection (Approval No. KSU-HE-25-920). Before beginning the survey, participants reviewed an electronic informed consent statement describing the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, voluntary nature of participation, and data handling practices; only those who actively indicated agreement proceeded to the questionnaire. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants in the study. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to decline or withdraw at any time without penalty. No personally identifying information (e.g., names, contact details, or institutional identifiers) was collected, and responses were treated as anonymous. To further protect confidentiality, all data were securely stored on a restricted-access OneDrive account accessible only to the principal investigator, with all electronic files protected by passwords.

Results

Participants description

A total of 278 nurses participated in this study. The majority were female (n = 186, 67.1%) and Saudi nationals (n = 198, 71.2%). The remaining participants were Arab nurses from other countries (n = 50, 18%) and Non-Arab nurses (n = 30, 10.8%). Within the Arab group, the largest subgroups were Egyptians (n = 21) and Sudanese (n = 11), with smaller numbers of Jordanians (n = 7), Lebanese (n = 4), Yemenis (n = 3), Palestinians (n = 2), Mauritanians (n = 1), and Syrians (n = 1). Within the Non-Arab group, the largest subgroups were Filipinos (n = 10), Indians (n = 7), and Pakistanis (n = 7), with smaller numbers of South Africans (n = 2), Croatians (n = 1), Eritreans (n = 1), Malaysians (n = 1), and Nigerians (n = 1).

Participants ranged in age from 22 to 50 years (M = 29.51, SD = 5.60). Most participants held a bachelor’s degree in nursing (n = 219, 78.8%), and the majority worked in hospital settings (n = 248, 89.2%). The largest proportion held the role of Registered Nurse (n = 190, 68.3%), with a mean of 6.15 years of nursing experience (SD = 5.29). More than half of the participants (59.4%) reported being single. The scales used in this study demonstrated good to excellent reliability. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were as follows: Nursing Incivility Scale α = 0.96, Work-Related Quality of Life α = 0.94, and Anticipated Turnover Scale α = 0.73. Overall, the mean total incivility score was 118.16 (SD = 33.61) out of a possible maximum of 196. Examination of individual subscales showed that incivility from patient and family sources was highest (M = 27.98, SD = 9.80), followed by nurse colleague sources (M = 27.56, SD = 9.44) and hospital administration sources (M = 26.01, SD = 7.48). Incivility from physicians (M = 19.36, SD = 7.82) and supervisors (M = 17.30, SD = 7.81) was comparatively lower.

To facilitate interpretation, incivility levels were categorized using the sample mean and standard deviation as reference points. In the absence of universally validated cut-off scores for the Nursing Incivility Scale, this approach represents a sample-relative descriptive convention rather than a standardized classification. Specifically, low incivility was defined as scores below 84 (mean − 1 SD), moderate incivility as scores between 84 and 152 (within ± 1 SD of the mean), and high incivility as scores above 152 (mean + 1 SD). Based on this categorization, 16% of participants were classified as low, 66.7% as moderate, and 17.3% as high. This approach is consistent with prior studies that have used study-specific thresholds or treated NIS scores as continuous variables for analysis (e.g., Alsadaan et al. [15]; Martin and Zadinsky [19]).

Factors associated with workplace incivility

This section examines which demographic and professional factors were associated with nurses’ exposure to workplace incivility. Several significant associations were identified.

Age and years of experience

Pearson correlation analyses revealed that nurse age was significantly and negatively associated with total incivility (r = − .23, p < .001), indicating that younger nurses reported higher levels of workplace incivility. Similarly, years of nursing experience was negatively correlated with incivility (r = − .23, p < .001), suggesting that less experienced nurses were exposed to higher incivility incidents.

Years working in Saudi Arabia

A Pearson correlation was conducted among expatriate nurses only (n = 77) to examine the relationship between years working in Saudi Arabia and total workplace incivility exposure. Results indicated no statistically significant association (r = .158, p = .104), although the direction of the correlation suggested a weak positive trend. This analysis was limited to the expatriate subsample, and the small sample size substantially reduced statistical power.

Arabic language proficiency and workplace incivility

A Pearson correlation was conducted to examine whether self-reported Arabic language proficiency was associated with total workplace incivility exposure. Results indicated no statistically significant relationship (r = .04, p = .261).

Gender

An independent samples t-test was conducted to examine whether incivility differed between male and female nurses. Results indicated no statistically significant difference in mean incivility scores between male nurses (M = 115.36, SD = 34.16) and female nurses (M = 119.51, SD = 32.98), t(255) = − 0.94, p = .175 (one-tailed). Levene’s test supported the assumption of equal variances, F = 0.23, p = .633.

Nationality

A one-way ANOVA examined whether incivility levels differed across nationality groups (Saudi, Arab, and Non-Arab). The overall model was statistically significant, F(2, 240) = 5.40, p = .005, η² = 0.043 (see Table 3). Post hoc comparisons using the Games-Howell procedure (employed due to unequal variances, as indicated by Levene’s test, p < .001) revealed that Saudi nurses reported significantly higher levels of incivility than Arab nurses (mean difference = 17.87, SE = 6.72, p = .028, 95% CI [1.63, 34.09]). No significant differences were found between Saudi and Non-Arab nurses (p = .35) or between Arab and Non-Arab nurses (p = .65).

Association between incivility and work-related quality of life

This section examines whether workplace incivility was associated with nurses’ work-related quality of life. Bivariate correlations indicated a significant negative association between total incivility and work-related quality of life (r = − .48, p < .001).

A simple linear regression was conducted, with workplace incivility as the predictor and work-related quality of life as the outcome variable. The model was significant, F(1, 211) = 58.59, p < .001, and explained approximately 21.7% of the variance in work-related quality of life (R² = 0.217, Adjusted R² = 0.214). Workplace incivility was a significant negative predictor (B = − 0.24, SE = 0.03, β = −0.47, t = − 7.65, p < .001), indicating that for each one-unit increase in total incivility, work-related quality of life decreased by 0.24 points.

This pattern suggests that work-related quality of life may account for the relationship between workplace incivility and turnover intentions, such that incivility is associated with lower work-related quality of life, which in turn is associated with higher turnover intentions. Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined the relationships among workplace incivility, work-related quality of life, and turnover intentions among nurses working in Saudi Arabia. The findings demonstrate that incivility is associated with both quality of working life and turnover intentions, reflecting the multifaceted consequences of uncivil workplace experiences. In the present sample, 84% of participants were classified as experiencing moderate to high levels of incivility, a strikingly high prevalence that signals not an isolated interpersonal problem but a pervasive feature of the contemporary nursing work environment in Saudi Arabia, with implications for workforce well-being, retention, and ultimately patient care. More importantly, fostering respectful work environments may strengthen teamwork, support ethical practice, and contribute to safer patient outcomes [15].

Alsadaan et al. [15] reported that 69% of participants experienced moderate to high incivility in a sample collected from four public hospitals in northern Saudi Arabia. The higher proportion observed in the present study may reflect the broader geographic and demographic representation of the sample, which includes nurses from multiple regions and nationality groups across Saudi Arabia. More importantly, the use of convenience sampling via social media means the sample may over-represent nurses who actively engage with online professional communities and who feel strongly enough about workplace conditions to respond; the 84% figure should be interpreted as an upper-bound estimate within a self-selected sample rather than a population prevalence. Notably, Alsadaan et al. did not report incivility by source, which limits direct comparison and underscores the added value of the present study’s subscale-level findings.

The highest incivility subscale scores were observed for patient and family sources (M = 27.98), followed by nurse colleague sources (M = 27.56). This finding aligns with Alshehry et al. [20, 21], who identified patient and visitor incivility as a predominant source of uncivil behaviors among nurses in Saudi Arabia. Nurse colleague incivility scores were also notably high in this sample, consistent with the concept of horizontal violence and the phenomenon described in the nursing literature as experienced nurses displaying hostile or dismissive behaviors toward less experienced peers [22]. This interpretation is further supported by the finding that younger, less experienced nurses reported significantly higher levels of incivility.

While these findings highlight significant challenges, Saudi Arabia has increasingly strengthened protections for healthcare providers through national workplace safety and professional conduct regulations. The Ministry of Health has implemented penalties for violence against healthcare workers and established workplace violence reporting systems, while the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties has expanded training initiatives and electronic support services that enable healthcare practitioners to report workplace violence and seek professional support [3, 23–25].

Turning to demographic and professional correlates, age and years of nursing experience showed significant negative associations with incivility, consistent with findings suggesting that more experienced nurses may report different exposure or tolerance patterns compared with less experienced staff [26]. In contrast, one study from Tehran reported a positive correlation between age and incivility [27], suggesting that contextual factors may moderate this relationship across settings.

Arabic language proficiency was not significantly associated with incivility in this sample, which may reflect the high proportion of participants (87.7%) who reported speaking Arabic very well. Gender was not significantly associated with incivility, consistent with findings from Tehran (Nazari et al. [27]); however, other studies have reported higher civility scores among male nurses [28].

Nationality emerged as a significant differentiating factor, with Saudi nurses reporting higher incivility than Arab nurses. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence from Saudi Arabia, where Saudi national healthcare workers were significantly more exposed to bullying than their non-Saudi counterparts [29]. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to examine nurses’ incivility quantitatively across these nationality groupings within Saudi Arabia. The Saudi nursing workforce is exceptionally multicultural, drawing nurses from across the wider Arab world, South and Southeast Asia, and beyond, who work alongside a growing cohort of Saudi nationals as the workforce undergoes accelerated Saudization [3, 8]. Within this context, the higher incivility reported by Saudi nurses may reflect intersecting workforce and cultural dynamics. Saudization policies have gradually shifted the composition of clinical teams, altering traditional workplace hierarchies and interactions between Saudi and expatriate staff [3, 4, 8]. In addition, cultural and communication differences within multicultural healthcare environments may contribute to misunderstandings, interpersonal tension, and differing perceptions of uncivil behaviors [19–21]. Expatriate nurses, by contrast, may have stronger incentives to under-report given the dependency of their residency status on continued employment [5, 6, 9]. Under-reporting may also be compounded by limited awareness of formal reporting mechanisms: a study reported 31.4% of staff were unaware of any specific system for reporting violent incidents and 68.6% had received no training on such systems [30].

This pattern underscores that incivility in highly diverse workforces is shaped not only by individual interactions but also by macro-level workforce policy. Marital status was also a significant factor, with married nurses reporting lower levels of incivility than single nurses. This pattern may reflect differences in social support or coping resources, consistent with the notion that such resources influence the appraisal of incivility in the workplace. This interpretation is also broadly consistent with findings on the role of marital status in nursing burnout research [27, 31].

A central finding is the significant negative relationship between incivility and work-related quality of life, such that higher exposure to incivility was associated with poorer quality of working life. This finding aligns with the broader nursing literature, which indicates that uncivil environments erode psychological well-being, job satisfaction, and perceived quality of working life [15, 19–21, 32]. The positive association between incivility and turnover intentions is also well-documented, as uncivil workplace climates increase stress and reduce engagement, thereby contributing to turnover risk [33, 34]. It is important to recognize that turnover intentions among nurses are shaped by factors beyond workplace incivility and work-related quality of life, including compensation and benefits, family circumstances, opportunities for career advancement, scheduling and workload, and, for expatriate nurses, visa and residency considerations. The present analyses focused on a specific pathway from incivility to turnover via work-related quality of life and should not be read as implying that this pathway exhausts the determinants of turnover.

The hierarchical regression findings indicate that work-related quality of life explained substantial additional variance in turnover intentions beyond workplace incivility alone. After accounting for work-related quality of life, the association between incivility and turnover intentions was no longer statistically significant, suggesting that the relationship between incivility and turnover may operate largely through its effect on nurses’ quality of working life. This pattern is consistent with theoretical frameworks proposing that work environment conditions influence retention through well-being and satisfaction pathways. In the Saudi context, prior studies have shown that negative workplace interactions affect nurse outcomes through well-being pathways, supporting the conceptual role of work-related quality of life as an explanatory variable linking incivility to turnover-related outcomes [15, 20, 21]. Longitudinal research is needed to establish the directionality of these associations.

These findings carry direct implications for practice and policy. Given that patients and their families constitute a major source of incivility, hospitals should implement zero-tolerance policies, reinforce civil communication norms, and provide clear channels for reporting uncivil acts by patients or visitors, with appropriate protection for staff and timely remediation [15, 20, 21]. It should be noted that, while national legislation in Saudi Arabia prohibits assault on healthcare providers, behaviors such as microaggressions and dismissive communication may not be addressed by these protections; therefore, institutional-level policies remain essential.

Interventions such as cognitive rehearsal, civility training, and empowerment strategies have shown promise in reducing perceived incivility and improving work-related quality of life across various contexts, including randomized trials and quasi-experimental studies [32, 35]. Structural empowerment and supportive leadership are consistently associated with lower levels of incivility, higher work-related quality of life, and improved retention. Policies that enhance autonomy, access to resources, mentorship, and inclusive decision-making may reduce uncivil experiences and their downstream consequences [36–38]. A positive organizational culture and climate emerged as critical determinants of nurse retention and quality of care. Cultural change initiatives targeting civility should be embedded within broader quality and safety strategies, with explicit leadership accountability, training, and feedback mechanisms [26, 32, 39]. Although nationality, age, and marital status showed differential associations with incivility in the Saudi context, policy and practice should avoid stereotyping and instead tailor supportive measures to ensure equitable access to resources, social support, language access, and culturally competent management practices [20, 21]. At the system level, workplace civility aligns with Saudi Arabia’s broader occupational health and patient-safety agenda under Vision 2030 and healthcare transformation initiatives. National bodies such as the Ministry of Health, the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties, the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development (MHRSD), and the Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions have emphasized healthcare worker protection, professional conduct, violence prevention, and safe workplace environments through regulatory standards, accreditation requirements, workforce wellbeing initiatives, and professional ethics frameworks [25, 40–43]. In addition, the MHRSD issued the Professional Code of Ethics Guide to support organizations in establishing ethical principles and professional conduct standards aimed at creating positive, transparent, fair, and respectful work environments free from corruption and misconduct [42].

Finally, because patients and their families emerged as the largest source of incivility in this sample, hospital-level efforts should be complemented by public education campaigns that reinforce respectful interactions with healthcare workers as a shared social norm; such campaigns have been deployed in other countries facing similar pressures on frontline staff and could be adapted to the Saudi context through the Ministry of Health and professional-association channels [44, 45].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings, alongside directions for future research. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. The observed associations among incivility, work-related quality of life, and turnover intentions are correlational, and reverse pathways are probable, for example, nurses already considering leaving their positions may perceive and report workplace interactions more negatively, and lower work-related quality of life may itself sensitize nurses to uncivil behavior. Longitudinal studies are therefore needed to delineate temporal sequences and to test moderation and mediation models (e.g., job demands–resources framework) in Saudi settings [19, 32]. Recruitment relied on convenience sampling via social media, which introduces self-selection bias: nurses who are more affected by incivility, more engaged with online professional communities, or strongly motivated to comment on workplace conditions may have been more likely to respond. The response bias may inflate prevalence estimates and skew the demographic profile of the sample relative to the underlying workforce. Self-reported data are also subject to recall and social-desirability effects, particularly on items concerning sensitive interpersonal experiences. Finally, a limitation of this study is that data on hospital type (e.g., public, private, or military), specialty, size, and bed capacity were not collected, which may have limited the ability to account for institutional factors influencing workplace culture and experiences of incivility.

Future research would benefit from the use of mixed or qualitative methods, including in-depth interviews and focus groups with nurses of different nationalities, career stages, and healthcare settings (e.g., public, private, and military hospitals), as well as varying hospital sizes and bed capacities. These approaches would help to better capture the lived experience of incivility, the meanings nurses attach to specific behaviors, and the contextual factors that quantitative scales cannot capture. Multisite and multicountry comparisons would help distinguish Saudi-specific patterns from generalizable phenomena, particularly regarding the roles of expatriate status, language, and cultural expectations. Additional mediators and moderators warrant further exploration, including organizational support, psychological capital, burnout, and compassionate leadership [15, 20, 21].

Conclusion

This study’s findings indicate that workplace incivility in Saudi nursing contexts is associated with work-related quality of life and turnover intentions and that work-related quality of life may account for the relationship between incivility and turnover. Demographic and professional factors, including age, experience, nationality, and marital status, influence exposure to incivility, underscoring the need for targeted, culturally sensitive interventions. The convergence of Saudi-specific data with the broader international literature supports the central claim that fostering civil workplace environments through comprehensive, multilevel strategies based on leadership engagement, empowerment, and policies addressing incivility from patients, families, and colleagues can improve nurse well-being, care quality, and retention.
